Pain in the claustrum
Authors: Christian Faig, Chun Yin Liu, Jesse Jackson, Brian N. Mathur, David A. Seminowicz, Anna M.W. Taylor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pain arises from coordinated activity across distributed brain networks. The claustrum, a thin subcortical nucleus with extensive bidirectional connectivity with the neocortex, has recently emerged as a potential hub in this network. Although historically difficult to isolate due to its thin anatomical structure and proximity to other pain-associated regions, recent advances in neuroimaging and circuit-specific manipulations have revealed new insights into its role in pain. This review synthesizes the evidence from human and animal studies and evaluates the evidential strength of current approaches, ranging from incidental reporting in whole-brain neuroimaging studies to claustrum-targeted imaging, electrophysiology and projection-specific manipulations. The literature suggests that the claustrum contributes to pain not as a primary sensory relay, but as a modulatory and integrative structure influencing various facets of cognition. We have organized these findings into three theory-guided frameworks: (1) sensory filtering of salient stimuli, (2) cognitive network engagement, and (3) offline consolidation of pain-related memory during rest and sleep. In chronic pain, claustrocortical function is altered in ways that map onto each of these frameworks, with rodent models pointing to depressed claustrocingulate output and loss of inhibitory control, and human imaging linking claustrum signals to catastrophizing, expectancy, and altered network recruitment. Taken together, the evidence highlights the claustrum as a promising but incompletely understood node in pain circuitry and underscores the need for more anatomically precise and mechanistically decisive studies to define its contribution to normal and pathological pain states.

Introduction

Pain is a complex, cognitively demanding sensory and affective experience that emerges from a distributed network of brain regions (Coghill, 2020). How this distributed network encodes nociceptive information and transforms it into the multidimensional experience of pain remains unclear. Mounting evidence from rodent and human studies implicates the subcortical region, the claustrum, as an essential top-down modulator of pain networks.

The claustrum is a narrow subcortical brain region located between the striatum and the insular cortex (insula). This structure is highly conserved across vertebrate species, including reptiles (Norimoto et al., 2020), birds (Zaremba et al., 2025), mice (Wang et al., 2023), and both non-human and human primates (Lei et al., 2025; Torgerson et al., 2015). The claustrum maintains extensive reciprocal connections with the neocortex that are topographically and functionally organized into projection motifs targeting specific cortical territories (Chia et al., 2020; Lei et al., 2025; Qadir et al., 2022; White and Mathur, 2018). This unique and conserved anatomy suggests that the claustrum plays a fundamental role in network coordination and cortical function.

One of the challenges of claustrum research is that it has been historically difficult to isolate. While the primate claustrum is encased by the external and extreme capsules, non-primate mammals lack an extreme capsule, making it challenging to distinguish a claustrum-insula border. Even in human imaging studies, the thin sheet of claustrum cells is often thinner than a single voxel. This means that much of the early work in claustrum research lacked the spatial resolution to isolate activity from the neighbouring insular cortex—a region deeply associated with interoception and pain (Craig, 2009). This unique anatomy and orientation next to the insula has rendered it difficult to study in the context of pain.

With recent advances in precision circuit manipulation and increases in resolution in neuroimaging tools, it is now possible to isolate claustrum activity and function from its neighbouring regions. In this review, we begin with a brief description of claustrum anatomy and physiology that make the claustrum relevant to pain, followed by a review of the state of knowledge linking the claustrum to pain across species. We then present and appraise three theory-guided frameworks, namely sensory filtering, cognitive network engagement, and offline consolidation, to explain the claustrum's role in physiological and pathological pain. Throughout, we distinguish between weaker forms of evidence, such as incidental claustrum peaks reported in whole-brain imaging studies, and stronger forms of evidence, such as claustrum-targeted analyses and projection-specific manipulations. The purpose of this review is to not only to provide an update on the progress of work regarding the claustrum's role in pain but also provide some clarity and guidance in interpreting this growing, and sometimes incongruous, body of literature.

Claustrum anatomy and physiology

The cellular organization of the claustrum resembles that of the neocortex, with excitatory projection neurons and several interneuron populations defined by their peptide expression, including vasoactive intestinal peptide, parvalbumin, somatostatin, and neuropeptide Y (Graf et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2016; Lei et al., 2025; Real et al., 2003). Claustrum projection neurons densely innervate frontal midline and temporal cortical regions, including the prelimbic, anterior cingulate, retrosplenial, and entorhinal cortices (Wang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2017; White et al., 2017). Projections to sensory and motor areas of the cortex are, by comparison, much weaker.

In the rodent, claustrum projection neurons synapse onto both excitatory pyramidal cells and inhibitory interneurons in the rodent ipsilateral cortex (Jackson et al., 2018; McBride et al., 2023; White and Mathur, 2018). Because synaptic inputs are stronger onto cortical inhibitory cells, claustrum activation drives interneuron spiking and feedforward inhibition of the cortical network (da Costa et al., 2010; De La Torre-Martínez et al., 2025; Jackson et al., 2018; McBride et al., 2023). As such, the predominant effect of claustrum activation is a highly synchronized cortical ‘down state’ that is characterized by periods of reduced neuronal activity and slow wave oscillations observed most commonly during sleep or quiet rest (Atlan et al., 2024; De La Torre-Martínez et al., 2025; Jackson et al., 2018; Marriott et al., 2024; McBride et al., 2023; Narikiyo et al., 2020).

Cortical projections form the predominant input into the claustrum, though their functional impact remains incompletely understood. Some evidence suggests that anterior cingulate cortical inputs activate contralateral claustrum neurons, while ipsilateral cingulate projections drive feedforward inhibition (Koga et al., 2024; Shaker et al., 2026; White and Mathur, 2018). Whether this relationship extends to inputs from other cortical regions remains to be determined. The claustrum also receives excitatory inputs from subcortical regions, such as the associative nuclei of the thalamus, basolateral amygdala and dorsal raphe nucleus. Notably, the claustrum has relatively minor input from sensory nuclei, including sensory cortices or sensory thalamic nuclei, with the notable exception of the medial and posterior insular cortex (Gehrlach et al., 2020; Qadir et al., 2018). From these data, a picture begins to emerge of how the claustrum may be involved in pain processing. The claustrum's dense reciprocal connectivity with frontal and associative cortical regions, rather than sensory ones, positions it to shape the integration of nociceptive information rather than the encoding of stimulus properties. “Encoding” here refers to the faithful representation of stimulus features (e.g., intensity, location, modality) typically achieved via topographically organized input from primary sensory pathways; “integration” refers to the contextual modulation of that afferent signal by attentional, cognitive, or affective state. This possibility is especially relevant given the claustrum's strong connectivity with regions such as the anterior cingulate, prelimbic, and retrosplenial cortices, which have established roles in salience, attention, decision-making, and pain-related affect (Barrière et al., 2019; Hao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2021; Rainville et al., 1997; Zhou et al., 2018).

Evaluating evidence for claustrum involvement in pain

Not all evidence for claustrum involvement in pain carries the same inferential weight. At the weakest end are incidental reports of claustrum activation peaks or clusters in whole-brain fMRI studies, especially when the claustrum is not the primary target of the analysis. This is because the reported signal may reflect partial volume contamination from the adjacent insula or putamen (Atilgan et al., 2022; Duerden and Albanese, 2013, Fallon et al., 2020). Stronger human evidence comes from studies that use claustrum-focused regions of interest, higher-resolution imaging, or analysis methods designed to mitigate signal contamination, such as small region confound correction (SRCC) (Stewart et al., 2024). However, improved anatomical precision does not by itself establish a causal role for the claustrum in pain. Controlled imaging paradigms can demonstrate that pain-related manipulations alter claustrum activity, but they cannot establish that claustrum activity causes, is necessary for, or mediates pain perception or pain-related behaviour. Associations between claustrum activity, connectivity, and pain-related measures may instead reflect downstream recruitment by other pain-related systems or shared modulation by broader cognitive and affective processes. Human lesion and stimulation studies can provide complementary causal clues, but their anatomical specificity is also limited by the claustrum's thin shape and close relationship to surrounding white matter and insular cortex (Atilgan et al., 2022). Mechanistic claims therefore require convergent evidence from perturbational approaches that can test necessity, sufficiency, or directed circuit influence.

In animal models, the evidential gradient is different. cFOS mapping and in vivo calcium measurements provide improved anatomical and temporal precision relative to most human imaging studies, whereas projection-specific electrophysiological, optogenetic, and chemogenetic approaches provide the strongest causal leverage because they can isolate defined claustrocortical pathways (Faig et al., 2024; Koga et al., 2024; Ntamati et al., 2023). This hierarchy does not invalidate broader human imaging findings; rather, it provides a framework for weighing them appropriately. Incidental human imaging reports can identify plausible loci for further study, but the strongest mechanistic inferences currently come from claustrum-targeted human analyses and circuit-specific animal work.

Animal studies

Some of the earliest investigations of claustrum activity and sensory responses involved in vivo recordings in monkeys and cats. These studies found unique regions of the claustrum that were activated by auditory, visual and tactile stimuli, though to a lesser extent than the associated primary sensory cortex (Olson and Graybiel, 1980; Remedios et al., 2010). With respect to pain, early studies in rats described nociceptive evoked activation of the claustrum. While these studies are a valuable early contribution to the field, they lacked anatomical precision and cell-type specific identification (at the molecular level) of claustrum neurons that are available currently.

Recent animal models offer more precise measurements of neuronal activity in subpopulations of both inhibitory and excitatory claustrum cells. Two studies found that repetitive painful stimulation increases cFOS expression – an indirect marker of neuronal activation – in claustrum projection neurons (Faig et al., 2024; Koga et al., 2024; Persinger et al., 1997; Słoniewski et al., 1995). One study followed this with in vivo measurements of activity using fiber photometry providing a more precise description of the temporal pattern of claustrum activation following a nociceptive stimulus (Faig et al., 2024). This study found that claustrum activity was relatively low during painful stimulation but exhibited a gradual increase of activity during the minutes following the stimulus when pain was resolved or resolving. This is in line with another study that found that claustrum neurons were rarely modulated by tactile sensory input, but rather by subsequent conditioned motor response following the sensory stimulus (Chevée et al., 2022). In contrast, another study using two photon calcium imaging found a subset (∼20%) of neurons that were active following painful foot shock, but not innocuous stimulation (Ntamati et al., 2023). This suggests there may be a small subset of pain-responsive cells in the claustrum, although it remains to be determined whether this applies to other, more naturalistic, pain modalities.

Human studies

The most direct neuroimaging evidence in humans for the involvement of the claustrum in pain comes from a small number of studies that explicitly attempted to isolate claustral signal by study design and analysis choices. A recent study employed a region-of-interest approach with small region confound correction to minimize signal contamination from nearby structures (Stewart et al., 2024). The authors reported significant claustral activation at the onset of painful stimuli and in response to pain-predictive cues in both healthy participants and migraine patients, together with altered interactions between the right claustrum and the posterior dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (piDLPFC), a region implicated in top-down pain modulation. In another study that treated the right claustrum as a region-of-interest, (Paul et al., 2021) found comparable activation to both painful and non-painful stimuli, calling into question whether the claustrum carries pain-specific information. In contrast, a large-scale meta-analysis of 200 fMRI studies of experimentally induced acute pain in healthy volunteers did not identify the claustrum as significantly activated (Xu et al., 2020). Taken together, these findings suggest that pain-evoked claustrum responses are not a robust, canonical feature of acute pain neuroimaging in healthy volunteers, but may become detectable under more targeted acquisition and analysis conditions.

The studies reviewed above provide the more direct human neuroimaging evidence for claustrum involvement in pain, either by explicitly assessing the claustrum or by evaluating its involvement across a large-scale meta-analysis. A second tier of evidence comes from exploratory, whole-brain, or secondary analyses in which the claustrum was implicated but was not the primary target of investigation. These should be interpreted more cautiously than claustrum-targeted work, as the study was not primarily designed to adjudicate claustrum function. More broadly, the difficulty of resolving the claustrum from the adjacent insula and putamen in conventional human imaging means that positive findings in this category are better taken as suggestive evidence of claustrum-related involvement than as definitive demonstrations of a pain-specific claustrum signal. Many other imaging studies reviewed below fall into this broader category, and have ascribed a function for claustrum activation in pain expectation and modulation rather than sensory perception per se.

In a study using a tonic pain stimulus, functional connectivity between the right amygdala and the right claustrum during pain-free rest was positively correlated with subjective pain ratings (Meeker et al., 2022). This is potentially informative because it links claustrum-related circuitry to individual differences in pain experience, suggesting that claustrum-related circuitry may be linked to the affective and predictive aspects of pain. In a meta-analysis of brain activation patterns during pain anticipation, reduced claustrum activity was observed before the onset of painful stimuli, suggesting a role in expectancy-driven pain modulation (Palermo et al., 2015). Direct manipulations of pain expectancy have also been reported to influence claustrum activity: placebo analgesia reduced pain-evoked claustrum activity (Amanzio et al., 2013), whereas nocebo-like enhancement of pain expectation increased claustrum activity (Kong et al., 2008). Several imaging studies of acupuncture have also reported claustral activation during verum needle stimulation compared with sham conditions, although it remains unclear whether this reflects nociceptive processing, expectancy, interoception, or salience (Chae et al., 2009; Chen et al., 2012; Huang et al., 2012, Yeo et al., 2010). In contrast, a meta-analysis of cerebral blood flow changes associated with pain relief through invasive motor cortex stimulation found increased claustral CBF following device activation (Volkers et al., 2020). These studies are informative because they repeatedly implicate the claustrum in contexts where pain is anticipated, modulated, or relieved; however, they do not by themselves establish that the claustrum directly encodes nociceptive input.

Overall, the results remain nuanced about whether sensory stimuli, such as nociception, meaningfully change claustrum activity levels. A fair conclusion from the human literature is a limited but non-trivial one. The most defensible claim is not that the claustrum has been established as a core human pain structure, but that a small number of claustrum-focused studies support its recruitment during pain onset and pain prediction, while a broader and methodologically weaker literature associates claustrum-related signals with pain modulation, expectancy, and analgesia. The negative evidence from large-scale meta-analysis remains important as it demonstrates that claustrum activation is not consistently recovered in conventional acute pain imaging. Even where pain-related manipulations reliably alter claustrum signals, these findings do not establish that the claustrum causally mediates pain perception or modulation. This, combined with the weak anatomical connections to sensory nuclei, make it unlikely the claustrum serves bottom-up nociception in the traditional sense. Rather, the evidence collected across species points to a higher-order, context-dependent role that is mediated through control of cortical regions involved in cognitive processing rather than direct nociceptive encoding. It is also possible that the claustrum exerts tonic, non-phasic control over cortical states that influences sensory processing without being strongly driven by sensory inputs, a possibility supported by circuit manipulations in animal models showing that claustrocortical pathways can shape behavioural responses to sensory stimuli even when population activity is relatively low (Faig et al., 2024; Ntamati et al., 2023). Thus, the human imaging literature supports recruitment of the claustrum during pain-related states, whereas stronger claims about its causal function will depend on convergent evidence from circuit-level perturbation studies.

Theories for claustrum function in pain

Here, we provide three conceptual frameworks that can be used to guide future research investigating the claustrum's role in nociception and pain: sensory filtering of salient stimuli, cognitive network engagement, and offline consolidation of pain-related memory. These frameworks are not necessarily exclusive. Rather, they emphasize different levels of explanation: moment-to-moment salience control, coordination of distributed cortical processing, and delayed updating of pain-related memory and expectation.

Filtering of salient stimuli

This theory proposes that the claustrum serves to integrate diverse sensory and limbic information from various cortical and subcortical regions to direct and sustain attention towards salient, behaviourally relevant stimuli. Indeed, the general anatomy of the claustrum providing feedforward inhibition onto frontal cortical regions gives some face validity to this theory. Moreover, human lesion and stimulation studies also provide some evidence that loss of claustrum function increases nociceptive gain. Painful sensations have been reported in many cases involving claustrum lesions (Atilgan et al., 2022; Barcikowska, 1979; Biemond, 1956; Couto et al., 2013; Obrador et al., 1957), although their characteristics and severity vary considerably, and in most cases the lesions extended well outside of the claustrum. However, one patient with more selective ischemic stroke of the left claustrum reported persistent paraesthesia in left half of the head (Maximov et al., 2018). Conversely, bilateral stimulation of the dorsal portion of the claustrum in awake neurosurgical patients produced only modest and inconsistent changes in self-reported sensation, with pain being reported in only 1 out of 5 patients (Bickel and Parvizi, 2019). Overall, these results suggest that loss of claustrum function appears to enhance pain sensations, yet activation of the claustrum does not produce notable changes in sensory sensitivity.

Evidence from animal models provide more direct evidence for the contribution of the claustrum to sensory saliency. In mice, inhibiting the claustrum during a visual cue response task increased the distractibility of auditory stimuli (Atlan et al., 2018) and lower claustrum activity was associated with impulsive responses in a cue selection task (Atlan et al., 2024). Conversely, high claustrum activity increased the rate of missed responses to sensory cues, suggesting the claustrum can modulate engagement by shifting the cortex towards on “offline” state. Frontal cortical structures, including the anterior cingulate and prelimbic cortices provide top-down modulation of sensory gain and multi-modal sensory integration (Chen et al., 2018; Dale et al., 2018; Hopfinger et al., 2000; Nakajima et al., 2019). Therefore, claustrum inputs to prefrontal regions may modulate sensory processing indirectly, by controlling activity in these cortical regions, rather than through direct inputs to sensory cortex. While this suggests broader involvement of the claustrum in indirect, state-dependent sensory modulation, investigation of a wider range of sensory modalities is needed.

With respect to pain, ablation and chemogenetic inhibition of claustrum projections targeting the anterior cingulate cortex enhances behavioural withdrawal responses to normally innocuous stimuli (Faig et al., 2024). Like in humans, bilateral activation of claustrocingulate neurons has no impact on sensory thresholds (Faig et al., 2024). However, another study found that claustrum neurons targeting the anterior cingulate cortex preferentially respond to contralateral mechanical sensory stimulation (Koga et al., 2024). Unilateral activation of these claustrum projections reduced sensory withdrawal responses on contralateral hind paw. This suggests that, in rodents, the claustrum participates in a cross-hemispheric cortical circuit to restrict cortical activity on the non-stimulated hemisphere. These studies support the idea that the claustrum serves as a gate to restrict nociceptive processing, which leverages the noted feed-forward inhibition of frontal cortical structures.

Of the three frameworks, sensory filtering currently receives the strongest causal support from rodent circuit studies. What it does not yet explain well is why human claustrum-related signals also track pain expectation, catastrophizing, and cognitively demanding conditions. A strong test of this theory would be to show that selective disruption of claustrocortical pathways reliably increases sensory gain or distractibility without necessarily changing pain-related memory or broader network coordination.

Cognitive network engagement

Pain is a cognitively demanding experience, which can be modulated by prior experience, expectation, and emotion (Bushnell et al., 2013; Seminowicz et al., 2004; Seminowicz and Davis, 2007). This experience emerges from synchronized activity across a widely distributed neural network. The Network Instantiation in Cognitive Control (NICC) theory puts forward a model that proposes the claustrum plays a central role in this network synchronization: the claustrum receives a signal from the frontal cortices to prompt the emergence of a specific network, transforms this signal, and broadcasts the transformed signal through return projections to frontal cortices and projections to posterior sensory and association cortices, promoting synchrony between these network nodes (Madden et al., 2022). Several lines of evidence support this theory. First, the unique claustrum anatomy with its wide and reciprocal projections to most of the neocortex position the claustrum to be a central network node. Second, the slow oscillating ‘down states’ produced in the cortex by claustrum activation is well suited to facilitate network synchrony. Third, recent human neuroimaging studies indicate the claustrum is engaged during cognitively demanding conditions (Krimmel et al., 2019; Stewart et al., 2025). The transient claustrum activation at the onset of cognitive tasks (White et al., 2020) and pain (Stewart et al., 2024) support this proposed role of the claustrum in initiating network activation. This model fits well within the previous salience filtering theory. The claustrum could act to filter salient information and activate only once a threshold required for cortical network engagement is required. Alternatively, the claustrum may set broader cortical network states that are required for filtering salient information.

Stewart et al. (2024) provides the strongest human support for this framework in the context of pain. Significant claustral activation was observed at the onset of painful stimuli and pain-predictive cues. Dynamic causal modelling further identified bidirectional excitatory effective connectivity between the right claustrum and the posterior dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (piDLPFC), a region implicated in top-down pain modulation. Migraine patients showed increased bidirectional claustrum–piDLPFC effective connectivity during difficult cognitive task processing, although no significant group difference was detected at acute pain onset. The pain-related activation and cognitive-task connectivity findings fits more naturally with a role for the claustrum in coordinating higher-order pain-related processing mediated through cognitive control, suggesting a potential mechanism through which the claustrum integrates affective and sensory aspects of pain, rather than with a purely sensory role. At the same time, NICC is a broad theory of cognitive control rather than a pain-specific model, so the crucial question is whether pain engages claustrum-dependent network instantiation in a distinctive way or simply recruits a more general cognitive control mechanism. A strong test of this framework would be to show that claustrum perturbation disrupts the coordination of distributed pain-related networks, especially at state transitions such as cue onset, pain onset, or task switching, more than it alters sensory thresholds alone.

Offline consolidation of pain-related memory during rest and sleep

One of the main limitations of both the sensory filtering and cortical network engagement theories is that claustrum activity is relatively low during high arousal states when sensory processing and cognition are most likely to occur. While pain can evoke claustrum activity, this activation is relatively modest, which may explain some of the conflicting data in both human and animal model literature, reviewed above. The highest claustrum activity occurs during offline states – quiet rest or slow wave sleep (Atlan et al., 2024; Lamsam et al., 2024; Marriott et al., 2024; Narikiyo et al., 2020; Norimoto et al., 2020). Moreover, the ability of the claustrum to evoke a cortical down-state is state-dependent and most efficient during quiet rest or sleep (Marriott et al., 2024; Narikiyo et al., 2020). Taken together, these data suggest that the claustrum contributes to pain not primarily during online nociceptive processing, but during offline states when the consolidation of pain-related memories occur. Pain is not only a sensory event, but also a powerful driver of associative learning, shaping future behaviour through memories of threat, avoidance, and expectation. The claustrum's ability to induce cortical down-states and synchronize distributed cortical activity may provide a mechanism for integrating these pain-related experiences into longer-term representations.

Evidence from other behavioural domains supports this interpretation, as claustrum manipulation can impact memory acquisition (Reus-García et al., 2021; Terem et al., 2020), consolidation (Marriott et al., 2024; Medina et al., 2024), and reversal/reinstatement (Grasby and Talk, 2013; Negishi et al., 2025). With respect to pain, while inhibition of claustrocingulate pathways increases pain sensitivity, it interferes with pain memory as assessed through the conditioned place avoidance assay (Faig et al., 2024). This suggests that inhibiting claustrocortical projections impairs pain memory encoding, specifically by disrupting how the affective and motivational components of pain experience are consolidated, with downstream effects on subsequent pain-related behaviour. This offline processing could work in tandem with the other two theories by updating expectations and memories that are recruited when processing pain. A test of this framework would be to show that manipulating claustrum activity during rest or sleep selectively alters later pain expectation, avoidance, or conditioned responses more than immediate pain thresholds.

These three frameworks need not be treated as competitors. Sensory filtering may shape immediate salience, cognitive network engagement may coordinate the distributed cortical response to pain, and offline processing may update the expectations and memories that influence future perception. One possibility is that different claustrum projection motifs contribute differently across cortical states and timescales. Another is that the same claustrocortical circuit serves different functions depending on whether the brain is engaged online by salient stimuli or offline during consolidation. Thus, these three theories of the claustrum in pain could each explain different aspects of the complex chronic pain experience.

Claustrum and chronic pain

Evidence linking the claustrum to chronic pain remains limited, but chronic pain is nevertheless a useful test case because it asks whether the mechanisms proposed above—sensory filtering, cognitive network engagement, and offline consolidation—become altered under persistent nociceptive, affective, or cognitive load.

Studies from the human literature suggest that claustrum-related signals are associated with chronic pain phenotypes, but they do not yet establish the claustrum as an independent generator of chronic pain states. In fibromyalgia, claustrum activity correlates with pain catastrophizing scores, pointing more strongly to pain appraisal and emotional processing than to sensory encoding (Gracely et al., 2004). In chronic low back pain, a systematic review found bilateral claustrum activation during nociceptive stimulation(Hazra et al., 2022), while in migraine, a meta-analysis reported heightened claustrum activation to painful stimulation and an association between reduced right claustrum volume, reduced right anterior cingulate volume, and greater headache frequency (Jia and Yu, 2017). Stewart et al. (2024) further reported stronger claustrum-related signals in migraine patients during acute pain and pain-predictive cues. Taken together, these findings suggest that in humans the claustrum is more consistently implicated when chronic pain is accompanied by altered salience, expectancy, appraisal, or broader network engagement. They are less persuasive as evidence that the claustrum itself serves as a primary chronic pain generator.

Data from animal models suggest a more pathway-specific role. Mice injected with complete Freund's adjuvant in the hind-paw showed a selective reduction of cFos expression in claustrocingulate projections at 14 days post injection but not 3 days post injection (Faig et al., 2024). Persistent pain also depressed spontaneous (Ntamati et al., 2023) or stimulus-evoked (Faig et al., 2024) calcium fluorescence in claustrocortical projection cells. Whole-cell patch clamping revealed weakened glutamatergic activation of pyramidal cells by optogenetic stimulation of claustrocingulate axons (Ntamati et al., 2023). These results highlight largely dysfunctional communication from the claustrum to the anterior cingulate cortex in chronic pain states. Considering the anterior cingulate cortex is well established to be pro-nociceptive and hyperexcitable in chronic pain (Acuña et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2018; Lançon et al., 2025), this suggests that chronic pain can involve a loss of claustrocingulate inhibitory control and claustrocingulate depression could be important for the early stages of central hypersensitivity. Supporting this, experimental inhibition of the claustrum in chronic pain provided minor (Ntamati et al., 2023) or no (Faig et al., 2024) improvements to mechanical allodynia, while chemogenetic activation completely recovered nociceptive thresholds (Faig et al., 2024). Additionally, following injection of formalin into the hind-paw, unilateral inhibition of claustrocingulate projections induced hypersensitivity in the contralateral hind-paw (Koga et al., 2024). Contrasting these findings, one study found that optogenetic stimulation of claustrum projections induced visceral hypersensitivity (Xu et al., 2022). However, viral transfection in these experiments was not sufficiently targeted to the claustrum leading to significant transfection of the surrounding insula, which is known to enhance nociception (Labrakakis, 2023; Ostrowsky et al., 2002).

Viewed through the three frameworks above, the apparently non-convergent chronic pain literature becomes more interpretable. The rodent data most strongly support the sensory filtering model: chronic pain is associated with depressed claustrocingulate function, and restoring activity in this pathway can normalize pain thresholds, which is exactly what would be expected if claustrum output normally helps constrain nociceptive gain. The human imaging data are more consistent with the cognitive network engagement model, because claustrum-related signals are observed alongside catastrophizing, migraine, cue processing, and other features that load heavily on appraisal, expectancy, and distributed cortical recruitment. The offline consolidation model is the least directly tested in chronic pain, but it remains relevant because chronic pain is maintained in part by learned expectations, avoidance behaviours, and persistent negative appraisals. The available conditioned avoidance data suggest that claustrocortical circuits can influence this domain, even if chronic pain-specific sleep or consolidation studies are still lacking.

One clear discrepancy exists between the human and animal models, with the human imaging literature reporting increased claustrum activation signals in chronic pain, whereas the animal literature report loss of function of claustrum projection cells. While the source of this discrepancy is not fully resolved, it may reflect differences in measurement rather than a simple contradiction. Human neuroimaging likely captures the net output of multiple claustrum populations and neighbouring tissues across large-scale networks, whereas rodent circuit studies isolate specific projections, particularly those linking the claustrum to the anterior cingulate cortex. Under this view, chronic pain may simultaneously involve loss of function in one claustrocortical pathway and compensatory or maladaptive recruitment in others. At present, the available data do not allow these possibilities to be cleanly disentangled, and more research in both animal models and human patient populations will be required to resolve this question.

Conclusions & future directions

The decades of accumulating evidence are beginning to suggest a complex yet pivotal role for the claustrum in pain processing. The current literature does not support viewing the claustrum as a primary sensory relay or a processor of bottom-up sensory information. Its relatively weak sensory inputs, strong reciprocal connections to frontal and midline associative cortical regions and its ability to shape cortical state are more consistent with functions related to saliency filtering, cognitive network engagement, and pain-related memory consolidation. It is possible that the claustrum serves all these functions across cortical states, with higher ‘phasic like’ claustrum activity associated with offline sensory encoding and sustained ‘tonic like’ claustrum activity supporting online sensory processing or cognition.

Despite the very clear anatomical relationship of the claustrum to the rest of the brain, there remains considerable disagreement between studies on even some of the most basic questions around how the claustrum responds to and affects pain. Some of these discrepancies are likely due to differences in techniques used to measure claustrum activity across species and the inherent difficulties in isolating claustrum activity from neighbouring regions. Specific challenges include: 1) selectively isolating and manipulating claustrum circuits without influencing the insula and other neighbouring regions, 2) disruptions to claustrum axons when implanting in vivo imaging tools through cortical white matter tracts, and 3) problems with measuring activity signals in sub-voxel compartments. Progress in the field will depend on the availability of higher-resolution human imaging, better anatomical segmentation, pathway-aware stimulation or lesion approaches, and cross-species paradigms that can test shared predictions of the three frameworks.

Differences in results may also be due to variability in activity and function of subpopulations of claustrum projection cells. Several subpopulations of excitatory claustrum cells have been identified based on their topographical orientation (Ham and Augustine, 2022; Marriott et al., 2021; White et al., 2017), projection target (Wang et al., 2023), neurochemical identity (Erwin et al., 2021; Fodoulian et al., 2025; Lei et al., 2025) and functional identity (Graf et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2016; White and Mathur, 2018). It is possible that these various subpopulations behave differently in response to pain, expectation, cognitive demand, or offline consolidation, and that at least some of the disagreement in the literature reflects this internal diversity rather than a single uniform claustrum function. More precise analysis of circuit specific changes to claustrum activity in pain is needed to reconcile these differences. Finally, more research about how the claustrum controls pain processes across the entire sleep-wake cycle of an organism may also be important to elucidate the full function of the claustrum in pain.

The claustrum remains one of the most enigmatic and poorly understood regions of the brain. Despite rapid advancements in our understanding of claustrum dynamics and function, a cohesive view about the role the claustrum plays in brain function or behaviour remains elusive. Pain is an especially useful lens through which to study it because pain reliably engages sensory, affective, cognitive, and mnemonic processes that can be measured in both humans and animal models. As the precise relationship between the claustrum and pain is revealed it will clarify when claustrum activity reflects nociception itself, when it reflects network coordination or salience, and when it reflects the consolidation of pain-related memory. This is positioned to advance pain neuroscience and deepen our understanding of claustrum function.
