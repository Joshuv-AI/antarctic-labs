Sacrococcygeal teratoma with severe gluteal deformation in a young woman: A case report
Authors: Ghodratollah Maddah, Seyed Ramin Dabiri, Seyed Masoud Hoseini, Saba Chalak, Ahmad Jawed Razaie, Mahmoud Reza Kalantari, Ali Mehri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Sacrococcygeal teratoma (SCT) is a germ cell tumor derived from primitive germ layers. While SCTs are a common neoplasm in infants and children, they are very rare in adults.Case presentation:We present a 24-year-old Afghan woman with a severe gluteal deformity, who suffered from pelvic pain and difficulty in sitting and walking.Clinical discussion:SCT is an important differential diagnosis for a presacral mass that can be identified through performing both magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography scans. When the lesion grows posteriorly to the coccyx, complete removal and coccygectomy via a trans-sacral approach are associated with favorable surgical and cosmetic outcomes.Conclusions:It is important to be aware of SCT as a possible diagnosis for a presacral mass.

Introduction

Sacrococcygeal teratoma (SCT) is a germ cell tumor composed of elements derived from all three primitive germ layers: ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm[1]. The most accepted theory of its origin attributes this tumor to totipotent stem cells that persist in Hensen’s node and gonads. Since Hensen’s node lies anterior to the sacrococcygeal region during embryogenesis, this area is the most common extragonadal site for the development of teratoma[2,3].

SCTs typically present during the fetal or neonatal period due to their developmental origin. They are most often identified and surgically excised in early life. Adult cases are generally limited to tumors that remain asymptomatic or untreated since childhood. We present a case of SCT in an adult woman with a severe gluteal deformity. Such reports contribute to the limited clinical evidence available and help in developing standardized guidelines for diagnosing and managing SCTs in adults. Case presentation

A 24-year-old immigrant woman from an underserved population was referred to the surgical clinic due to a severe deformity of the gluteal region and posterior perineum (Fig. 1A). She reported constipation, pelvic pain, and difficulty sitting and walking. Her past medical history revealed that she has had this lesion since childhood, but her parents did not seek medical care due to financial constraints. At age 15, the lesion became enlarged and painful. Therefore, her parents attempted to drain a white and cloudy liquid from the lesion using a needle. They repeated this procedure every few months to relieve the pain and reduce the size of the lesion. Apart from this, her past medical, family, and medication history were unremarkable. Physical examination revealed a mass measuring approximately 10 × 8 cm, with heterogeneous consistency in the intergluteal region. Digital rectal examination demonstrated a firm mass causing intrarectal bulging. Laboratory results revealed C-reactive protein: 8.7 mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (1 h): 23 mm, white blood cell count: 5.4 × 103/μL (polymorphonuclear neutrophils: 51.4%, Lymph: 43.0%), Hb: 11.9 g/dL, and platelet count: 302 × 103/μL. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) displayed a mass with cystic and solid structures posterior to the coccyx. The mass also involved the pelvic floor muscles and pelvic cavity on the right side, resulting in displacement of the rectum to the left (Fig. 2). In the spiral computed tomography (CT) scan of the pelvis with oral and intravenous contrast, the compressive effect of the lesion on the rectum and uterus was observed (Fig. 3). Based on the patient’s presentation and imaging findings, a differential diagnosis including SCT, congenital mesenteric tumors, fibrosarcoma, neurofibroma, and dermoid cyst was considered. Informed consent was obtained, and the surgery was planned. After the induction of general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the prone-jackknife position, and a longitudinal elliptical incision was made in the lower sacrum and coccyx area up to the anus. Afterward, the anococcygeal ligament was cut. The mass adhered to the rectal wall. By preserving the external sphincter, the presacral mass and deformed skin were freed from the surrounding tissue and completely excised. The coccyx was also removed. Bilateral gluteus maximus myocutaneous flaps, based on the superior gluteal arteries, were used to cover the defect and reconstruct the gluteal region.

The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery with excellent wound healing (Fig. 1B). One month after surgery, she reported relief from pain and was able to sit and walk comfortably, expressing satisfaction with the surgical outcome (Fig. 1C). At the 1-year follow-up, she experienced only brief episodes of gas incontinence. Digital rectal examination showed only a slight decrease in the anal sphincter’s tonicity, and there were no signs of recurrence or other complications.

Pathologic study of the resected mass confirmed the diagnosis of mature teratoma. The macroscopic view revealed an epidermal surface, cystic components, areas with fibrosis, and fat accumulation. In the microscopic examination, the cystic components were coated with squamous and respiratory epithelium, and digestive glands were also seen. Discussion

Our report highlights key barriers to achieving health equity among vulnerable populations. This patient, an immigrant woman with low socioeconomic status, suffered years of pain, limited mobility, and body image distress, which caused her embarrassment and social withdrawal. Repeated attempts by her parents to drain the lesion also increased the risk of infection and tumor spread. The main reasons that restricted this patient’s access to surgical care included financial constraints, limited education of her parents, and cultural beliefs. As emphasized by the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery, disparities in surgical care remain a critical global health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries[5]. Such inequities are most pronounced among marginalized groups. This case indicates the urgent need for global health initiatives to strengthen surgical capacity, promote health education, and ensure equitable access to care.

SCTs are a group of congenital tumors with an extremely rare incidence in adults. Histologically, teratomas can be divided into mature and immature types. The definite differentiation is based on the presence of mature tissues from ectoderm (such as cortical glands, hair, and follicles), mesoderm (such as muscle tissue), or endoderm (such as gastrointestinal tissue)[2,6]. Most teratomas have a benign nature with a low chance of malignant transformation (1–2%). However, this chance increases as patients get older[1]. Altman et al also defined another classification of SCTs based on location: type 1 is totally outside of the pelvis, type 2 is mostly outside of the pelvis, type 3 is mostly inside of the pelvis, and type 4 is totally inside of the pelvis[7].

Adult SCTs usually extend into the pelvic cavity and do not cause any symptoms or external deformities[8,9]. Therefore, they are often discovered incidentally during investigation for another pathology in the abdominopelvic cavity or when they grow enough to cause a compressive effect on adjacent organs. The compressive effect, depending on the involved structure, might vary from slight and vague pelvic pain to constipation, bladder dysfunction, venous congestion, and neurological symptoms[2]. When SCT extends outside the pelvic cavity, it usually causes gluteal swelling, and the overlying skin may be puckered and discolored[1,9–11]. Our patient, however, presented with a severe deformity in the skin, possibly due to the recurrent drainage of the lesion by the parents.

MRI and CT scans are the recommended imaging options for detecting SCTs. Although MRI has higher specificity and sensitivity for assessing the soft tissues of teratomas, CT is more accurate for detecting calcifications and evaluating hard tissues[2]. In our patient, using both MRI and CT scans helped evaluate the lesion’s size, location, structure, and impact on nearby organs. This precise assessment played a vital role in determining the surgical strategy.

Shatnawi et al believe that fine-needle aspiration of the mass could be informative[9]. In contrast, others recommend avoiding biopsy and drainage of SCTs because of the various tissues inside teratomas and the difficulty in assessing results. Furthermore, when malignancy is suspected, a biopsy can spread tumoral cells, increasing the risk of recurrence and metastasis[2,12]. In this case, we avoided performing a biopsy and made the diagnosis based solely on physical examination and imaging findings. However, repeated attempts by the parents to drain the lesion increased the risk of infection and potential tumor dissemination. This emphasizes the importance of educating patients and caregivers to avoid manipulating congenital or long-standing skin lesions to prevent potential adverse outcomes.

The best treatment option for SCTs is total surgical excision. A literature review on managing SCT cases reported three surgical techniques: transabdominal, transsacral, and combined abdominosacral[2]. The transsacral approach is appropriate when the tumor is confined below the S3 level, there is sacral involvement, malignancy is not suspected, and the lesion is relatively small[13,14]. In our case, using this technique provided direct access to the lesion and the coccyx, avoided a large abdominal incision, and preserved the sphincter and surrounding tissues, resulting in favorable surgical and cosmetic outcomes. Creating bilateral gluteus maximus myocutaneous flaps based on the superior gluteal arteries also helped achieve a satisfactory esthetic result. This technique provides durable coverage and preserves muscle function, making it a good choice for reconstructing posterior and presacral defects after tumor resection[15]. The robust vascularity of the flaps also ensures good perfusion and prevents secondary deformity[16]. Performing a coccygectomy helped eliminate pluripotent stem cell remnants and reduced the risk of tumor recurrence[13].

Complications of surgery include fistula/abscess formation, bleeding, and urinary/fecal incontinence[14]. When the coccyx is removed, there is a risk of injury to the S2 or S3 nerve roots. These nerves are responsible for the voluntary control of the external anal sphincter; hence, checking for fecal/gas incontinence during the follow-up period is necessary.

This report provides clinical insight into the diagnosis and surgical management of SCT in adults; however, it is limited by the presentation of a single case. As with most case reports, the findings may not be generalizable to all patients. Further studies and multicenter case series are needed to establish standardized guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of adult SCT.

Conclusion

SCT should be considered in the differential diagnosis of a presacral mass with gluteal deformity. MRI and CT scans can be complementary in the accurate assessment of the SCT. Complete removal of the tumor and coccyx prevents recurrence and provides favorable outcomes.
