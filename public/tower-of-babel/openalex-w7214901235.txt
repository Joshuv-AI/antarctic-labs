Small-scale plasticity in oxide ceramics at room temperature:
Recent progress and future opportunities
Hiroshi Masuda1,³

This review synthesizes recent advances in nanomechanical testing and atomistic modeling of oxide ceramics,
with particular emphasis on the relationship between room-temperature plasticity and microstructure at small
length scales. Although the limited plasticity and resultant brittleness of ceramics have long inhibited their
structural applications, recent developments in nanomechanical testing, such as nanoindentation and micropillar
compression, have enabled the characterization of dislocation-mediated plasticity prior to crack initiation, even
at room temperature. Particular attention is given to the relationship between small-scale ductility and the
activation of crystallographic slip systems, the resistance of individual grain boundaries to dislocation transmission, and atomistic mechanisms governing dislocation dynamics revealed through molecular dynamics simulations. Emerging microstructural design strategies for enhancing small-scale plasticity and fracture toughness,
including dislocation engineering and interface-induced plasticity, are also discussed. These ﬁndings highlight
new opportunities for tailoring microstructures of ceramics through advanced processing routes, including
mechanical treatment and solidiﬁcation processes, in addition to conventional sintering approaches. By
integrating insights from nanomechanical characterization, atomistic simulations, and microstructural design
strategies, this review proposes potential pathways toward intrinsic toughening and macroscopic strengthening
of engineering ceramics.
Keywords: Ceramics, Plasticity, Dislocation, Nanoindentation, Micropillar compression

1. Background
Inherent brittleness of ceramics has inhibited their application as structural and functional materials. Ceramics are
prone to fracture under external loading particularly at
room temperature, as stress concentration at the crack tips
is insuﬃciently dissipated through plastic deformation.1)
Although the mechanical behavior of ceramics has long
been studied, macroscopic plasticity can be observed only
at elevated temperatures for most ceramics, typically
above 500 °C.2,3) The limited plasticity (or substantial
resistance to dislocation activity) in ceramics arises from
strong ionic and/or covalent bonding and the complex
crystal structures with the small number of slip systems
and long Burgers vectors.4)
Recent advances in nanomechanical testing techniques
such as nanoindentation and micropillar compression have
enabled the characterization of plastic deformation in ceramics prior to crack initiation, even at room temperature.
Early nanoindentation studies reported that below the critical indentation depth, plastic deformation dominates with

Plasticity of single crystals

Earlier nanomechanical testing on ceramics primarily
focused on strength aspects, paying limited attention to
ductility (plastic strain to fracture). Nanoindentation can
precisely measure hardness, but is inherently unsuitable
for ductility evaluations because it generates self-similar
strain gradients depending on the indenter tip geometry.13)
Although micropillar compression can evaluate the intrinsic deformability of materials under nearly uniaxial stress,
earlier studies mostly focused on the yielding behaviors
of single crystals, rarely exploring their larger deformation beyond ³10 % strain.8–11) The limited scope of these
studies was possibly due to the technical shortcomings of
conventional load-controlled nanoindentation systems,
where stable testing is sustained only when the micropillars undergo strain hardening. Recent nanomechanical
testing facilities have increasingly incorporated displacement-control modes, enabling stable measurements of
deformation even with strain softening and/or serrated
ﬂow. With this displacement control, micropillar compression can now track the deformation behavior to higher
strain levels, and is increasingly adopted as a practical
approach for evaluating the ductility of ceramics at room
temperature.24–27)
Figure 2(a) shows representative stress–strain curves
during micropillar compression of 9.8 mol % Y2O3-stabilized cubic ZrO2 (YSZ) single crystals with various orientations.22) Micropillars with diameters of ³1 ¯m fabricated
via focused ion beam (FIB) were compressed at room
temperature under an initial strain rate of 1 © 10¹3 s¹1.
Whereas a bulk single crystal of cubic zirconia exhibits
brittle fracture under a compressive stress of ³2 GPa,28) all
micropillars were plastically deformed with yield stresses
exceeding 7 GPa. Especially, the [111] pillar showed
remarkable plasticity, deforming to nearly 40 % nominal
strain without crack initiation. Slip traces appeared along
speciﬁc crystallographic planes on the side surfaces of the
deformed micropillars [Figs. 2(b)–2(e)].
As shown in Figs. 2(b) and 2(c), the [111] and [112]
pillars were both deformed through {001}©110ª slip systems, the primary one of cubic zirconia,3) whereas they
exhibited markedly diﬀerent ductility behaviors. The [111]
pillar deformed via multiple slips with simultaneous activation of three {001}©110ª slip systems having identical
Schmid factors. In contrast, the [112] pillar slipped only
along a (001)[110] slip system, possessing the highest
Schmid factor. Consequently, the [111] pillar deformed
more uniformly with ﬁne and dense slip traces, whereas
deformation of the [112] pillar was localized near the top
region, resulting in a premature fracture. These results
suggest that the plasticity of YSZ pillars is enhanced by
moderate forest hardening among dislocations on multiple
slip systems. This hypothesis was further supported by
crystal plasticity ﬁnite element simulations, which demonstrated that uniform deformation can be promoted via
multiple slips and resultant strain hardening.

the accessible length scales of molecular dynamics (MD)
simulations are approaching those relevant to realistic
nanomechanical testing. In particular, nanoindentation is
narrowing the gap between atomistic modeling and experimental observations. Furthermore, atomistic-scale dislocation dynamics obtained from MD simulations can complement TEM observations and advance our understanding
of room-temperature plasticity in ceramics.59–63)

4. Designs for intrinsic toughening
Nanomechanical testing and atomistic modeling have
successfully characterized room-temperature plasticity in
ceramics, providing insights for microstructural designs
enhancing plasticity and fracture toughness. This research
strategy has attracted growing attention, particularly over
the past ﬁve years. This section reviews the development
in this ﬁeld, particularly emphasizing the author’s own
works and discussing future opportunities for intrinsic
toughening and macroscopic strengthening in ceramics.

4.1

Dislocation engineering

Most ceramics are fabricated via sintering at elevated
temperatures over long durations, which typically yields
fully annealed microstructures with low dislocation densities. Substantial stresses are required for dislocation
nucleation in such defect-free crystals, leading to limited
plasticity and toughness, particularly at macroscopic
scales. However, if containing a high density of preexisting dislocations, plasticity and toughness may also be
enhanced in ceramics. This concept was experimentally
demonstrated by Moon and Saka (2000)71) and in their
subsequent studies combining shot peening and crack
healing.72,73) Although shot peening techniques are widely
used for introducing surface residual stresses, the role of
dislocations associated with shot peening has received
limited attention in ceramics.74–76)
Since the report by Porz et al. (2021),77) dislocation
engineering in ceramics has attracted renewed attention.
They introduced dislocations into SrTiO3 crystals through
mechanical polishing and signiﬁcantly suppressed crack
propagation from Vickers imprints. The enhanced toughness was attributed to the plasticity promoted by the
preexisting dislocations. Subsequently, dislocations have
been introduced into various oxide ceramics (e.g., SrTiO3,
KNbO3, MgO, and Ga2O3) through diﬀerent mechanical
treatments, such as cyclic indentation,78–81) scratching,36,37,82–84) and ball rolling.85) The author revisited the
shot peening technique as an accumulative indentation
process, and attempted to introduce dislocations into
harder oxide ceramics such as YSZ and Al2O3.23) To
suppress crack initiation during shot peening, the author
employed microparticle shot media with an average
diameter of ³20 ¯m, roughly one order of magnitude
smaller than those used in conventional shot peening.

Interface-induced plasticity

Heterointerfaces can also promote room-temperature
plasticity in ceramics. Figure 10(a) shows the stress–strain

10 GPa. Figures 10(b) and 10(c) show TEM micrographs

To the author’s knowledge, plasticity arising from a
combination of two brittle phases is extremely rare. In
addition, basal dislocations in Al2O3 have traditionally
been considered as inactive at temperatures below 600 °C.2)
Nucleation of such dislocations from eutectic interfaces, as
potential dislocation sources, may enhance the small-scale
plasticity at room temperature. The Hall–Petch relationship
supposes that interfaces in crystalline solids inhibit dislocation motion. However, at micro- to nanoscopic scales,
where conventional dislocation sources are scarce, interfaces can serve as alternative sources for dislocation nucleation. This view is supported by nanoindentation studies,
which revealed easier pop-in events in the vicinity of grain
boundaries than within grain interiors.41,50) Controlling
such active interfaces through solidiﬁcation processes can
potentially overcome the inherent brittleness of ceramics.
Interface-induced plasticity in ceramics has attracted
growing interest and is also reported in several other
studies. Increasing the density of coherent ¡/¢ interfaces
can enhance both the strength and ductility in nanopillar
compression of Si3N4 ceramics.90) Generation of borrowed
dislocations from heterophase interfaces with metallic
molybdenum has imparted plasticity to La2O3 ceramics at
nanoscales.91) Furthermore, engineering of negative stacking fault energies has improved the plasticity of TiN/TaN
multilayered ceramics under micropillar compression.92)

4.3

Other approaches

Various other approaches have also been reported to
promote dislocation-mediated plasticity in ceramics at
small length scales. Oxygen vacancies, primarily introduced through heat treatment,93) aliovalent-cation doping94,95) or redistribution under electric ﬁelds,96) can reduce
the stress required for nanoindentation pop-in. Meanwhile,
the introduction of nonstoichiometry was reported to
reduce the yield stress and enhance plasticity of nonoxide
ceramics.97,98) Photoindentation studies have demonstrated
that light irradiation can tune the hardness of ceramics.99,100) Electron-beam irradiation has also induced obvious softening in ceramics.101,102)

4.4

Toward toughening and strengthening

Various microstructural designs for enhancing the plasticity of ceramics have been proposed, but such small-scale
plasticity does not directly translate into macroscopic deformation. The yield stress of ceramics remains substantial,
requiring extremely small ﬂaw sizes for realizing macroscopic plasticity, as shown in Fig. 1. Nevertheless, promoting small-scale plasticity may enhance the fracture toughness and improve the macroscopic strength of ceramics.

Crack propagation in brittle fracture is driven by stress
concentration at the crack tip. In metallic materials with
substantial ductility and toughness, the stress concentration is dissipated through a plastic zone developing around
the crack tip. Dislocation emission from crack tips and
localized plasticity can also occur in ceramics,103,104) as
shown in Fig. 11. However, the improvement of fracture
toughness is restrained by such a limited number of
dislocations, which cannot provide suﬃcient plastic strain
and crack-tip shielding. Consequently, ceramics have
historically been toughened by other mechanisms such
as phase transformation16) and crack bridging.17)
As revealed in recent nanomechanical testing, dislocation-mediated plasticity in ceramics can be potentially
enhanced at small scales under various microstructural
conditions. More extensive screening of small-scale plasticity and subsequent optimization of crack-tip microstructure might enlarge the plastic zone and improve the
fracture toughness (Fig. 12). This concept has been partially demonstrated by the improved resistance to indentation fracture, as shown in Fig. 9. However, indentationbased approaches have inherent limitations in the direct
evaluation of fracture toughness and crack growth behavior, necessitating fracture experiments using precracked
specimens. One promising approach is microcantilever
bending,105,106) which employs a small specimen fabricated via FIB milling with well-deﬁned precracks and can be
applied to localized regions, such as subsurface zones
containing a high density of dislocations (Fig. 8). Moreover, by removing the surrounding material during specimen fabrication, the inﬂuence of residual stress ﬁelds on
fracture behavior may also be minimized.

5. Outlook
Nanomechanical testing techniques have enabled observations of room-temperature plasticity in ceramics and
investigations of microstructural impacts. It is now being
established that brittleness is not an invariant property of
ceramics. Instead, dislocation-mediated plasticity can be
activated through various microstructural designs. The
