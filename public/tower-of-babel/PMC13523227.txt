Discrepancy between centric relation and therapeutic position in TMJ disc displacement: a case report
Authors: Natalia Savychuk, Vasil Pekhno, Olena Doroshenko, Stanislav Riebienkov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTemporomandibular disorders often involve internal derangements such as anterior disc displacement, which can alter the centric relation (CR). Clinicians frequently rely on a splint-guided therapeutic position (ThP), without imaging confirmation of the condyle–disc relationship before for fabrication and adjustment of a repositioning splint. This case report presents magnetic resonance imaging across two mandibular positions-centric occlusion (CO) and ThP-and describes a mismatch between disc position and condylar seating. It suggests that partial disc recapture observed in a ThP may not coincide with a CR in long-standing, structurally advanced joint pathology.Case presentationA 30-year-old female reported over seven years of nocturnal clenching and persistent right-side clicking. Her history included fixed orthodontic treatment (2014–2016) followed by retention splints. A nightguard fabricated in 2018 did not alleviate symptoms. Examination revealed a reproducible reciprocal click on the right. CR was established using Dawson’s manoeuvre after muscle deprogramming and recorded with addition silicone. Magnetic resonance imaging in CO, CR, ThP, and maximal opening demonstrated advanced degenerative changes and anterior disc displacement without reduction on the right, with no recapture in any position; the left joint showed anterior displacement with reduction. In the ThP, partial recapture of the left disc occurred, but the condyle still failed to reach a CR. A maxillary repositioning splint with retrusive control and anterior guidance was fabricated from the constructive bite. With nocturnal wear and 10–14-day follow-ups over three months, joint clicking diminished and function improved, with all effects remaining reversible.ConclusionsA ThP may facilitate partial disc recapture and symptomatic relief in chronic temporomandibular disorder, yet the condyle may remain non‑physiologic position. Magnetic resonance imaging in CO, CR, and ThP may help identify discrepancies between disc position and condylar seating before selecting a mandibular position for prolonged splint therapy or irreversible prosthodontic or orthodontic treatment. The ThP can be considered a reversible, intermediate stage within a controlled splint protocol rather than a definitive target. Background

Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) are frequently associated with intra-articular alterations, including disc displacement, which may affect the mandibular reference position used for diagnosis and treatment planning [1]. Centric relation (CR) is commonly described as a jaw relationship independent of tooth contact, characterized by an anterosuperior condylar position against the posterior slope of the articular eminence, which allows a reproducible hinge movement for clinical registration [2]. Because of this, CR is widely used as a reference in restorative dentistry, TMD management, and orthodontic workflows [3, 4]. In complex rehabilitation—particularly in patients with a history of TMD—treatment planning often requires attention to TMJ-related constraints and maintaining a stable mandibular position during restorative phases [4].

In the present report, the splint-guided therapeutic position (ThP) refers to the constructive-bite mandibular position recorded for fabrication and adjustment of a repositioning splint. This position is treatment-driven and reversible, selected to improve symptoms and/or optimize the disc–condyle relationship on MRI, and it may differ from physiologic CR [5].

Because CR registration depends on neuromuscular deprogramming and the clinician’s guidance technique, numerous approaches have been proposed since the mid-20th century and compared in clinical studies [6–8]. Contemporary work addresses both repeatability (how consistently CR can be registered) and the relationship between the recorded jaw position and the resulting condylar seating as assessed by imaging or jaw-tracking methods [9–12]. Common clinical strategies combine short-term deprogramming (e.g., anterior jig or leaf gauge) with guided mandibular positioning maneuvers (e.g., bimanual manipulation, chin-point guidance, or Dawson’s manoeuvre) [13].

Disc recapture protocols aim to improve the disc–condyle relationship and stabilize joint function using occlusal appliances. Depending on appliance design and clinical protocol, repositioning may be achieved by active chairside mandibular guidance or by creating conditions that facilitate adaptive changes through neuromuscular modulation [12, 14].

The aim of this case report is to describe MRI findings in centric occlusion (CO), centric relation (CR), and ThP, and to highlight that these positions may differ in chronic TMD, potentially leading to discrepancies between condylar seating and disc position.

Case presentation

A female patient born in 1994 presented with complaints of clicking in the right temporomandibular joint and severe nocturnal teeth clenching that had been ongoing for more than 7 years. Her dental history included orthodontic treatment from 2014 to 2016, after which retention splints were fabricated for both the upper and lower jaws. In 2018, the patient reported increased clenching and jaw clicking, for which a nightguard was fabricated, but without satisfactory symptom relief. In January 2025, the patient presented to a dental clinic with persistent complaints. Intraoral photos are presented in Fig. 1.

On clinical examination, a disc displacement with reduction click was noted in both temporomandibular joints. During the visit, CR was determined using Dawson’s manoeuvre, and after muscle deprogramming according to our previously described protocol [13], CR was recorded with an addition-cured silicone (A-silicone) material. MRI of the temporomandibular joints revealed the following common features on both sides: articular congruency was preserved and no joint effusion was present (Figs. 2, 3 and 4).

Right TMJ. The mandibular condylar head is deformed in its apical regions, with deep erosions and marked subchondral sclerosis; the condylar outline appears sharpened. The configuration of the glenoid fossa and articular eminence is generally preserved, although subchondral sclerosis of the posterior slope of the articular eminence is present. The collateral ligaments are markedly altered, with fibre disorganisation and damage to the lateral disc–capsular attachment. The bilaminar zone shows severe degenerative change, with pronounced myxoid degeneration and rupture of a substantial proportion of the disc–condylar and disc–temporal ligament fibres. In the retrodiscal space, oedema and small fluid collections are visualised. The articular disc is completely displaced anteriorly, to the level of the tip of the articular eminence; in the frontal plane, a partial medial displacement is evident. In the ThP, the disc remains located near the tip of the eminence, while the condylar head does not recapture. On mouth opening, the condylar head approaches the level of the eminence apex, pushing the disc even further anteriorly; however, disc reposition (recapture) does not occur. The structure of the posterior portion of the disc is moderately altered, with mild degenerative changes. Within the muscular complex, oedema of individual fibres of the lateral pterygoid muscle is observed, as well as oedema of the joint capsule. Joint mobility was reduced, and the disc was observed to shift its position during movement.

Left TMJ. The mandibular condylar head maintains a typical overall shape, although mild flattening of the anterosuperior surface is present in the lateral segments. The configuration of the glenoid fossa and articular eminence is preserved, without obvious structural alterations. In the collateral ligaments, fibre disorganisation and degenerative changes of the medial disc–capsular attachment are visualised. The bilaminar zone shows reduced structural integrity: fibre orientation is irregular and fragmented, with a subtotal tear on the background of widespread degeneration. Signs of oedema are present in the retrodiscal space. The articular disc is deformed and flattened, being compressed laterally between the condylar head and the glenoid fossa. In the lateral portions, the disc is completely displaced anteriorly relative to the condylar head, with rotational components. In the medial part of the disc, a complete oblique–horizontal tear is observed on the background of diffuse degeneration and surface erosion. In the ThP, the disc becomes slightly unfolded at the lateral pole; the condylar head approaches the posterior thickened portion of the disc but does not fully recapture. On mouth opening, the medial part of the disc remains fixed to the posterior slope of the articular eminence, while the lateral portions of the disc shift anteriorly relative to the condylar head. Within the muscular complex, oedema of individual fibres of the lateral pterygoid muscle is visualised at their insertion into the disc–capsular complex. Joint mobility is moderately reduced; the disc appears fragmented and partially changes its position during movement.

According to the international classification, the diagnosis in this patient is anterior disc displacement without reduction (ADDwoR) of the right TMJ and anterior disc displacement with reduction (ADDwR) of the left TMJ [13]. In the constructive bite position, we identified a ThP (ThP), as a portion of the disc in the left TMJ showed partial recapture.

In this clinical case, in our opinion, the ThP can be regarded only as an intermediate stage in splint therapy for chronic TMD, because the anatomical position of the mandibular condyle does not correspond to the generally accepted CR; however, according to the TMJ MRI findings, the position of the articular disc relative to the condylar head in the left TMJ is closer to the CR.

The next step was digital scanning in CO and in the established constructive bite position, followed by virtual design of the joint splint (Fig. 5). In this clinical case, a maxillary repositioning splint with retrusive control and anterior guidance was fabricated. During 3-month follow-up with nocturnal wear and 10–14-day reviews [14], the patient reported diminished joint clicking, which was also less evident on clinical examination. As a next step, mandibular repositioning may be adjusted under TMJ MRI guidance, if clinically indicated, with the aim of improving condylar seating.

Discussion

In the presented chronic TMD case, MRI assessment in CO, CR, and in the ThP provided useful descriptive information on the disc–condyle relationship. MRI-documented observations in this case included persistent anterior disc displacement without reduction on the right across positions, and partial disc recapture on the left in ThP. Clinically, improvement was assessed by patient report and clinical examination (reduced clicking over 3 months). The interpretation proposed below is hypothesis-generating and limited to this single case.

Although CR is widely used as a reference position for diagnosing internal derangements, the mandibular ThP achieved with a repositioning splint may differ substantially from the CR [15]. A ThP selected to reduce joint sounds does not necessarily correspond to a biomechanically stable condylar seating; therefore, MRI confirmation of both disc recapture and an acceptable condylar position may be valuable when planning prolonged splint therapy and especially before considering irreversible treatment steps [16]. In the present patient, MRI demonstrated only partial disc recapture in the ThP on one side (an anterior disc displacement with reduction), while the condyle could not be fully seated in CR. These findings reflect a discrepancy observed in this single patient and do not demonstrate that CR is systematically unreliable in patients with chronic disc displacement.

This finding suggests the need for imaging-based verification of the intended jaw position before prolonged splint therapy is prescribed. Splint therapy remains a core element of conservative TMD management, but different appliances exert distinct biomechanical effects. Flat stabilization splints, typically adjusted at or near CR, mainly reduce pain by joint decompression and muscle relaxation, and available evidence indicates limited and inconsistent capacity to achieve reliable anatomical disc repositioning in chronic closed-lock situations [17].

Nonetheless, a recent meta-analysis reported that stabilization splints can produce slight forward–downward condylar displacement and increased joint space, which may facilitate disc recapture in a subset of cases with reducible anterior disc displacement [18]. Anterior repositioning splints (ARS), which advance the mandible to recapture the disc, are particularly effective in anterior disc displacement with reduction: an MRI-based investigation found approximately 99% immediate disc recapture and marked reductions in disc displacement following ARS therapy, with excellent clinical outcomes in about 60% of treated joints [19]. In the present case, symptom change was assessed clinically and by patient report; no quantitative outcome measures were applied.

By contrast, in chronic anterior disc displacement without reduction (closed lock), ARS usually fails to restore normal disc–condyle alignment and functions predominantly as a pain-relieving device, whereas dynamic distraction appliances such as the RA.DI.CA. splint have been associated with higher rates of functional improvement and clinically relevant disc recapture [17].

Importantly, even when full anatomical disc reduction is not obtained, re-establishing a functional coordination between the condyle and disc, with improved mobility and reduced pain, may result in a stable, asymptomatic condition [17]. Overall, this case illustrates that a forward mandibular repositioning splint can provide partial disc recapture (ADDwR) and symptom relief; however, the empirically determined ThP should not be assumed equivalent to an orthopedically sound CR. MRI verification of both condylar relationship and disc position may be warranted before this position is adopted as the basis for long-term therapy [16]. In this case we observed that, in chronic TMD with long-standing disc displacement and degenerative changes of the TMJ, a ThP may provide partial disc recapture and symptomatic improvement while the condylar position remains distant from a CR. MRI assessment in CO, CR and ThP is may be useful to identify potentially clinically relevant discrepancies between disc position and condylar seating before using any mandibular position as the basis for long-term splint therapy or definitive prosthodontic or orthodontic rehabilitation. In the present case, the ThP was used as a reversible, intermediate stage within a controlled splint protocol rather than a definitive target position. Further clinical studies are needed to clarify prognostic factors that distinguish joints in which disc recapture is achievable from those with predominantly degenerative, non-reducible changes.

Limitations

This report describes a single patient; therefore, causality cannot be inferred and generalizability is limited. Clinical outcomes were not quantified using validated symptom scales, and follow-up was limited to 3 months. In addition, imaging findings reflect the time points presented and do not establish long-term stability or systematic differences between mandibular positions.

Conclusions

In the presented case, MRI comparison of centric occlusion, centric relation, and a splint-guided therapeutic position showed that a symptom-oriented therapeutic position may not coincide with physiologic centric relation. Therefore, when planning prolonged splint therapy in chronic TMJ disc displacement, MRI-based verification of disc position and condylar seating may support selection of the mandibular position used for treatment.
