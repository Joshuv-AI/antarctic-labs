An interpretable machine learning model for diabetic foot risk classification in patients with diabetes
Authors: Xiaohua Hu, Huijun Xi, Zibo Fan, Leixia Zhu, Jia Shi, Shuangshuang Qian, Jing Qiu, Yingzhi Li, Hong Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic foot is a severe chronic complication of diabetes, mainly resulting from peripheral neuropathy and vasculopathy, and may progress to ulcers, infections, and amputation if not treated in a timely manner. Early identification of patients who already present a high-risk diabetic foot profile at clinical evaluation is therefore critical for guiding preventive interventions. This study aimed to develop an interpretable machine learning–based risk classification platform for classifying current diabetic foot risk status in patients with diabetes and supporting clinical risk stratification and personalized care. The model was not designed to predict future ulcer occurrence, amputation, or wound healing outcomes. In this retrospective study, data from 1938 diabetic patients at Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University were used for model development, and an independent external validation cohort of 695 diabetic patients from Shanghai Changhai Hospital was used to assess generalizability. Fifty clinical features covering demographic, metabolic, vascular, neurological, inflammatory, renal, and diabetes-related factors were included. Ten machine learning models were developed and compared. Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) was applied to the top-performing models for feature selection, and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) was used to interpret model predictions and construct a diabetic foot risk classification platform. Using sixteen selected features, the CatBoost model achieved the best performance on the internal test set, with an AUC of 0.935 ± 0.016 and accuracy, precision, and recall of 0.88. In the external validation cohort, the model maintained stable performance, with an AUC of 0.922 ± 0.006 and accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score all equal to 0.88, demonstrating good generalizability. We developed a CatBoost-based diabetic foot risk classification model incorporating sixteen clinically accessible features. The model showed stable and reliable performance across internal and external cohorts and remained robust under data noise and class imbalance, supporting its potential utility for real-world clinical risk classification and early preventive care.

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcers are primarily induced by repetitive mechanical stress in regions subjected to elevated vertical or shear forces, particularly in individuals with peripheral neuropathy. The presence of peripheral arterial disease further exacerbates the risk of ulcer formation1–3. Patients with diabetic feet often pursue further diagnostic evaluation only after the onset of overt symptoms or significant changes in clinical indicators, which frequently results in delayed intervention. Recent studies have predicted the probability of diabetic foot risk and analyzed influencing factors using statistical and traditional machine learning methods. However, these approaches often neglect targeted optimization and model interpretation, which can compromise both accuracy and interpretability4–9.

In recent years, the integration of machine learning (ML) methods into clinical practice has gained significant attention, particularly with the growing use of EMR. EMR systems have greatly facilitated the collection of healthcare data, making it more accessible and accurate, which has, in turn, enabled clinicians to apply machine learning techniques more effectively10. In the field of diabetes care, early identification of high-risk individuals and the implementation of targeted interventions are critical for reducing the incidence of diabetic foot. Data routinely collected by nursing staff—such as foot examination findings (e.g., abnormal skin temperature, callus formation), patient self-management behavior records (e.g., adherence to foot care, frequency of blood glucose monitoring), and dynamic physiological indicators (e.g., frequency of intermittent claudication episodes)—can serve as valuable features for training machine learning models. By deeply integrating longitudinal data generated from nursing practice with electronic medical record systems, the model’s ability to dynamically capture disease progression can be significantly enhanced. Moreover, such integration empowers nursing teams to construct a precision care pathway—comprising screening, assessment, and intervention—based on model-derived risk stratification results. Unlike traditional statistical methods, which often impose strict data assumptions, machine learning algorithms are capable of handling complex, high-dimensional datasets, making them ideal for modeling intricate medical conditions. As a result, ML techniques have seen widespread application in various areas of healthcare, driving advancements in predictive modeling and decision-making processes11. Various ML models have been developed to predict a wide range of medical conditions. For example, a random forest model has been employed to predict the probability of acute kidney injury in pediatric patients12, Chen et al. developed Bayesian models for predicting complications like cesarean scar ectopic pregnancies13. In the field of diabetic foot, recent studies have made significant progress in applying machine learning models for disease risk prediction. For example, Amith et al. utilized machine learning algorithms for early diabetes detection based on thermographic images14, while Nora et al. classified diabetic foot ulcers using mapped binary patterns in conjunction with convolutional neural networks15, and other studies have employed techniques like LASSO to address issues such as data imbalance16. Despite the significant advantages of ML models, a major challenge remains in their interpretability. The complexity of these models often results in them being considered “black box” models17, where it is difficult to understand how specific features contribute to the final predictions. Although numerous studies have attempted to analyze feature importance in diabetic foot classification models18–20, traditional methods often fall short in providing a comprehensive understanding of how different variables influence the model’s output. These methods generally focus on global feature importance, which can provide a high-level view of the model’s performance but fail to offer insight into the micro-level interactions between features. Additionally, these approaches lack sufficient visualizations to make the model’s inner workings more transparent to clinicians. To address these challenges, recent advancements have incorporated the SHAP method, which offers a unified framework for interpreting machine learning model outputs. The SHAP method has been increasingly used to explain complex models and visualize individual variable contributions, providing more detailed insights into how each feature affects the classification21. This approach is particularly valuable in the context of diabetic foot, as it allows clinicians to better understand the underlying factors influencing risk predictions, ultimately improving model transparency and aiding clinical decision-making. Through the integration of SHAP with machine learning, the interpretability of models can be enhanced, helping bridge the gap between model complexity and clinical application.

This study aimed to compare multiple machine learning models for classifying the current diabetic foot risk status of patients with diabetes using routinely collected clinical features. The endpoint of the model was the presence versus absence of clinically diagnosed diabetic foot or a high-risk diabetic foot profile at the time of evaluation, rather than future incident ulceration or amputation. In this regard, the proposed model differs from ulcer-healing, ulcer-recurrence, or amputation-risk models because it focuses on screening and risk stratification at the clinical encounter. Furthermore, we sought to identify the relative contribution of different features to model output and to establish a simplified, interpretable risk classification platform based on the optimal machine learning model, thereby enhancing its applicability and value in clinical practice.

Method and materials

Patients

This retrospective cohort study was conducted among patients with diabetic foot in China, aiming to develop and validate a predictive model. The derivation cohort consisted of diabetic foot inpatients admitted to Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2023. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (Approval No. NFEC-2024-149).

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) diagnosis of diabetes mellitus in accordance with the 2024 American Diabetes Association (ADA) diagnostic criteria; (3) presence of diabetic neuropathy and vasculopathy without prior amputation; (4) the patient or caregiver was fully conscious, with no cognitive impairment or communication barriers; (5) the patient or legal guardian provided written informed consent to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with concomitant malignant tumors; (2) patients with lower limb varicose vein ulcers; (3) patients with sepsis, osteomyelitis, or other severe comorbid malignant diseases; (4) patients with depression, hematological disorders, psychiatric illnesses, or other acute infectious diseases. The target outcome was a binary classification label indicating diabetic foot risk status at the time of data collection. Patients clinically diagnosed with diabetic foot or presenting a high-risk diabetic foot profile were labeled as the diabetic foot/risk-positive group, whereas patients with diabetes without diabetic foot were labeled as the non-diabetic foot/risk-negative group. Therefore, the model should be interpreted as a cross-sectional risk classification and screening-support tool, not as a longitudinal prognostic model for future ulcer onset, amputation, or wound-healing outcomes.

Power calculation

As this was a retrospective study, the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients available during the study period. To evaluate whether the available sample size was sufficient for model development and validation, we performed a post hoc power calculation based on ROC AUC analysis. With a two-sided significance level of 0.05, a null AUC of 0.80 representing the minimum acceptable discriminative performance, an expected AUC of 0.90, and the observed class distribution in the derivation cohort, the available sample size of 1938 patients, including 578 risk-positive cases and 1360 risk-negative cases, provided more than 99% statistical power. Therefore, the study cohort was considered adequate for developing and evaluating the proposed diabetic foot risk classification model.

Statistical method

Data balancing

Since the outcome classes were imbalanced, we designed a probability-stratified boundary oversampling (PSBO) scheme to enhance minority representation specifically in clinically ambiguous regions of the decision space. Unlike conventional SMOTE, which generates synthetic minority samples mainly according to distance-based nearest-neighbor interpolation in the original feature space, PSBO first uses an initial classifier to estimate each sample’s predicted probability and then restricts interpolation to probability bins around the decision boundary. This design aims to reinforce minority cases that are most informative for classification while reducing unnecessary synthesis of easily separable or low-confidence minority samples. Variable screening and feature analysis

Features and samples with missing values exceeding 20% in the dataset were removed, and the remaining missing values were imputed using the median. One-hot encoding was applied to the discrete variables. After model training, RFE was employed to select the optimal feature combination. Following feature selection, the SHAP algorithm was used to analyze the feature importance and the global and local impact of each feature.

Machine learning models

In the study, ten distinct machine learning models were employed for training and testing, including Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), CatBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest (RF), AdaBoost, Decision Tree (DT), Logistic Regression, Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Support Vector Machine (SVM) and XGBoost.

GBM is an ensemble learning algorithm that builds models sequentially, where each model corrects the errors of the previous one22,23. CatBoost is a gradient boosting library designed to handle categorical features without requiring explicit encoding24,25. LightGBM is another gradient boosting framework that is optimized for speed and memory efficiency26,27. RF is an ensemble method based on decision trees. It builds multiple decision trees and aggregates their results to improve predictive accuracy and reduce overfitting28–30. AdaBoost is an ensemble method that combines weak classifiers to create a strong classifier31,32. DT is a simple, interpretable model that splits the data into subsets based on feature values33–35. Logistic Regression is a linear model used for binary classification problems36,37. MLP is a type of neural network that consists of multiple layers of neurons38,39. SVM is a supervised learning model that finds the hyperplane that best separates data points of different classes38,40–42. XGBoost is an optimized gradient boosting framework designed for speed and performance43–45.

Selection of machine learning models

The dataset was split into a 7:3 ratio, with 70% used for model training and 30% for testing. During training, fivefold cross-validation was applied to all ten models to optimize hyperparameters and compute AUC values, ensuring the robustness and accuracy of the results. Subsequently, the top five performing models were subjected to feature selection using the RFE method. The model with the highest average performance was selected as the final predictive model. Evaluation metrics included the AUC, confusion matrix, and accuracy. To further interpret the model, the SHAP method was employed to visualize the contribution of each feature to the final prediction, both globally and locally. Finally, the model was deployed as a web-based application for clinical use.

Additionally statistical techniques

All statistical analyses were conducted using Python. Because no directional superiority hypothesis was pre-specified for baseline clinical characteristics or model-performance comparisons, all hypothesis tests were performed as two-sided tests with a predefined significance level of 0.05, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated where applicable. Continuous variables were reported as mean (standard deviation) for normally distributed data and as median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were summarized as frequency (percentage). For comparisons of continuous variables between two groups, Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data with homogeneous variance. Welch’s t-test was applied when normality was met but variance was unequal. The Mann–Whitney U-test was used for non-normally distributed data.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (NFEC-2024-149), and the Medical Ethics Committee of Shanghai Changhai Hospital (CHEC2025-129). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to participation in the study.

Variable screening

Feature selection was performed using RFE in a training-only manner to avoid information leakage. Specifically, after cohort construction, the dataset was first split into a development set and an independent test set, and the external cohort was kept fully untouched for external validation. All preprocessing steps and feature selection procedures were conducted exclusively on the development set.

Within the development set, we adopted a five-fold cross-validation framework. In each CV training fold, we applied RFE separately using the five best-performing candidate models as reported in Tables 2 and 3 as the base estimators. For each base model, RFE produced a ranked subset of features in that fold. To obtain a stable and reproducible feature set, we aggregated the fold-wise selections and retained features that appeared in at least three out of five folds. This stability criterion was applied within each base model. Finally, the selected features were defined as the intersection of the stable feature sets obtained from the five base models, resulting in the final feature subset used for model training as shown in Fig. 2. After feature selection, the final model was refit on the entire development set using only the selected features, and its performance was evaluated on the independent internal test set and further validated on the external cohort. To address class imbalance between diabetic foot and non-diabetic foot cases, we implemented a probability-guided oversampling strategy in a training-only manner. Specifically, within each CV training fold, an initial classifier was trained to estimate predicted probabilities. Minority samples located in predefined boundary probability bins were then used to generate synthetic samples by local interpolation with minority neighbors from the same bin. This differs from standard random oversampling and conventional SMOTE because sampling was guided by model-estimated boundary uncertainty rather than only by class membership or feature-space distance. The oversampling ratio was controlled by a global cap to avoid excessive synthetic sample generation. After oversampling, the final model was trained on the resampled training fold and evaluated on the untouched validation fold. This fold-wise design ensured that oversampling was confined to training data and minimized information leakage or optimistic bias in performance estimation.

Model development and performance comparison

Based on the selected feature subset, the five best-performing models were retrained and evaluated. The results were comparable to those obtained prior to feature selection, with slight performance improvements observed in certain models, indicating the effectiveness of the feature selection process. In addition, for the CatBoost model, we conducted hyperparameter tuning using a grid search strategy combined with stratified cross-validation. The tuning process explored multiple combinations of iterations, learning rate, and tree depth, and the configuration that achieved the highest mean AUC ± standard deviation derived from cross-validation across folds was selected. The final settings were: iterations = 40, learning rate = 0.2, depth = 2, with all remaining parameters kept at their default values. Under this optimized configuration, the CatBoost model demonstrated the best overall performance, achieving an accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score of 0.88, with a fivefold cross-validated AUC of 0.935 ± 0.016. Comparison with baseline models

To comprehensively evaluate the predictive capability of the proposed model, we compared it with two of the most authoritative and widely used diabetic foot screening systems, IWGDF and IDF. All methods were applied to the same test set and underwent identical data preprocessing procedures to ensure a fair comparison. For both IWGDF and IDF, patients with a risk rating of 0 were categorized as non-diabetic foot, whereas those with ratings of 1–3 (ranging from moderate to very high risk) were classified as diabetic foot for binary evaluation. As shown in Table 5, the proposed CatBoost model substantially outperformed both IWGDF and IDF across all evaluation metrics. The CatBoost model achieved the highest accuracy (0.92), precision (0.92), recall (0.92), and F1-score (0.92), demonstrating excellent discriminative ability for identifying diabetic foot. In contrast, both IWGDF and IDF exhibited lower precision (0.57) and F1-scores (0.72), despite achieving perfect recall (1.0), indicating that these rule-based screening systems tend to classify a larger proportion of patients as high-risk, resulting in higher false-positive rates. Notably, CatBoost also showed a substantially higher AUC, whereas AUC values were not applicable to IWGDF and IDF due to their deterministic, non-probabilistic classification nature. Multicenter external validation

To further evaluate the generalizability and clinical robustness of the proposed model, an independent external validation cohort comprising 695 individuals was collected from Shanghai Changhai Hospital. Although this cohort was independent from the derivation dataset, it was obtained from a comparable tertiary-care clinical environment with similar diagnostic workflows and data acquisition practices. Therefore, this external validation primarily assesses the reproducibility and robustness of the model in a similar real-world clinical setting, rather than demonstrating broad generalizability across substantially different healthcare systems or populations. All external data were processed using the same preprocessing pipeline as the derivation cohort, including variable standardization, missing value imputation, and feature scaling. No model retraining or recalibration was performed during the external validation phase. The final CatBoost model was directly applied to the external validation cohort to generate predictions. As shown in Fig. 6, the CatBoost model achieved AUC values above 0.90 in both five-fold and ten-fold cross-validation. The model demonstrated stable predictive performance, with accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score all reaching 0.88, which were consistent with the results observed in the internal test set. These findings indicate that the proposed model maintains strong performance and robustness when applied to an independent cohort collected from a comparable clinical environment. Model explanation

The SHAP algorithm was utilized to interpret the CatBoost model and quantify each feature’s contribution to the model output, helping clinicians better understand and apply the model. Both global and individual case analyses were performed. Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a machine learning–based diabetic foot risk classification model that is designed not merely to achieve high statistical performance, but to address a clinically meaningful problem: identifying patients who, at the time of clinical evaluation, present a diabetic foot or high-risk diabetic foot profile and may benefit from intensified preventive care. The model should therefore be interpreted as a cross-sectional screening-support and risk stratification tool, rather than a model for predicting future ulcer occurrence, amputation, or wound-healing outcomes. Using routinely available clinical and biochemical variables, the proposed model provides an interpretable and practical tool that can be integrated into real-world clinical and nursing workflows under clinician supervision. From a clinical perspective, the primary value of this model lies in its ability to support risk stratification and decision-making at the point of care, rather than in its numerical performance alone.

Diabetic foot is a multifactorial complication that often progresses silently before overt ulceration occurs, and traditional screening strategies rely heavily on rule-based criteria that may overestimate risk in some patients while failing to identify others who already have substantial underlying risk at the time of assessment. By synthesizing demographic, metabolic, vascular, neurological, and inflammatory information, the proposed model offers a more individualized assessment of risk status, enabling clinicians to distinguish patients who require immediate preventive attention from those who may be managed with standard care.

Importantly, the predictors selected by the model are well aligned with established clinical understanding of diabetic foot pathophysiology, which enhances its clinical credibility and acceptability. Markers of vascular impairment, neuropathy, metabolic dysregulation, and systemic inflammation collectively reflect the core mechanisms driving tissue ischemia, loss of protective sensation, impaired wound healing, and chronic inflammation. Rather than introducing opaque or non-actionable predictors, the model reinforces known risk pathways and translates them into a quantitative, patient-specific risk estimate that can inform targeted preventive strategies at the current clinical encounter.

A key strength of the proposed approach is its explainability, which is essential for clinical adoption. Through SHAP-based interpretations, clinicians and nurses can visualize how individual factors contribute to a patient’s estimated risk at evaluation. This transparency allows the model to function not as a “black box,” but as a clinical decision-support aid that complements professional judgment. In practice, such explanations can be used to guide clinician–patient communication, helping patients understand how modifiable factors—such as glycemic control, lipid levels, or smoking—contribute to their risk profile, thereby improving engagement and adherence to preventive recommendations.

From the perspective of diabetes nursing practice, the model has substantial potential to enhance preventive care pathways. Risk stratification outputs can be directly linked to tiered nursing interventions. Patients identified as high risk at assessment may benefit from intensified foot care education, more frequent foot examinations, closer monitoring of vascular and neurological status, and early referral to multidisciplinary teams involving vascular surgery or neurology. Conversely, patients at lower risk can be managed through community-based nursing programs, routine screenings, and remote monitoring, optimizing the allocation of limited healthcare resources. Embedding the model into electronic health record (EHR) systems could further enable automated alerts and standardized care pathways, supporting a shift from reactive treatment of ulcers to proactive prevention based on real-time risk classification.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the current model is based on static measurements obtained at a single time point and therefore provides a cross-sectional risk classification rather than a true longitudinal classification of future incidence. Because diabetic foot risk evolves dynamically over time, incorporating longitudinal data and temporal modeling approaches may further improve the model’s ability to support continuous risk monitoring and early warning in future work. Second, although class imbalance was addressed during model development, the unequal distribution between diabetic foot and non-diabetic foot cases may still influence the learned decision boundaries and classification stability. Future studies may benefit from exploring more advanced resampling strategies, cost-sensitive learning frameworks, or synthetic data augmentation to further enhance robustness. Third, although the model relies on routinely collected variables, integrating additional modalities—such as imaging or wearable sensor data—may further enhance early risk identification and clinical utility in future iterations. Fourth, the web application developed in this study is currently a research prototype for demonstrating model deployment, clinician-supervised screening selection, and explainable risk communication. It has not yet undergone prospective workflow testing, usability evaluation, regulatory assessment, or clinical-impact evaluation. Therefore, it should be used only as a decision-support aid in research or supervised clinical settings until further prospective validation confirms its safety, usability, and effect on patient outcomes.

In conclusion, this study presents an interpretable and clinically oriented machine learning model for diabetic foot risk stratification at the time of clinical evaluation. Beyond demonstrating strong discriminative capability, the proposed approach emphasizes clinical usability, explainability, and integration into nursing and preventive care workflows. By enabling individualized risk classification and supporting timely preventive intervention, this model has the potential to improve preventive care delivery and reduce the burden of diabetic foot. Future work should focus on prospective validation, workflow integration, and evaluation of real-world clinical impact.
