Activation Steering for Synthetic Data Generation: The Role of Diversity in Downstream Safety Detection
Vijeta Deshpande, Tootiya Giyahchi, Veena Padmanabhan, Leman Akoglu, Anna Rumshisky

Abstract
Safety detection models require examples of HHH (Helpful, Harmless, Honest)-violating outputs for robust generalization, however such examples are scarce. Activation Steering (AS) has emerged as a data-efficient method for generating target-concept-aligned responses. We investigate whether AS can generate high-quality training datasets for downstream classifiers, a question that remains untested. We present a two-fold study with intrinsic and extrinsic evaluation across 4 4 concepts × 2 \times\,2 models × 4 \times\,4 steering methods. Intrinsically , beyond the field-standard rubric of steering success (concept alignment) and coherence, we introduce sample- and set-level diversity as a quality axis previously absent from the literature, and find that increasing steering strength reduces response diversity. Extrinsically , we replace HHH-violating examples in the available training data with steered generations and fine-tune detection classifiers. AS-generated data results in a better classifier than the prompting-generated data on 3 3 of 4 4 concepts. However, only 41 41 of 136 136 AS configurations outperform prompting, indicating that downstream utility lies in a narrow regime that jointly satisfies success, coherence, and diversity. The harmonic mean of these three axes correlates with downstream AUROC more consistently across concepts than success and coherence alone, providing a practical heuristic target for practitioners tuning AS hyperparameters. Together, our results highlight the potential of AS in synthetic data generation for improving safety detection and identify diversity as a critical, previously overlooked axis for tuning AS.

Preliminaries

Activation steering rests on the linear representation hypothesis: high-level concepts such as honesty or toxicity correspond to approximately linear directions in the latent activation space of large language models (LLMs)
(
37
)
. The framework comprises two stages. First, a
steering vector

\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e., target concept direction, is estimated using a paired dataset
\mathcal{D}
of contrastive examples and an extraction method
f:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbf{v}
. Second, at inference time, the LLM’s hidden states (
\mathbf{h}
) are modified through an intervention
\mathbf{h}\leftarrow g(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{v})
to align LLM generations with the target concept.

Activation steering methods therefore differ along three axes: the construction of
\mathcal{D}
, the extraction map
f(\cdot)
, and the intervention
g(\cdot)
. To isolate the effect of the extraction function, we fix
\mathcal{D}
across models and methods, and fix the intervention to scaled addition,
g(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{v};\lambda)=\mathbf{h}+\lambda\mathbf{v}
, where the scalar
\lambda
controls steering strength and is a hyperparameter. A positive
\lambda
steers the model toward the target concept, while a negative
\lambda
steers it away. The addition of the
\lambda\mathbf{v}
is performed on the last token and repeated for each forward pass until the generation ends.

Steering Vector Extraction.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a transformer-based LLM with
L
layers, indexed by
l\in\{1,\dots,L\}
, and let
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i}^{+},x_{i}^{-})\}_{i=1}^{N}
be a dataset of
N
contrastive pairs, where
x_{i}^{+}
exhibits the target concept (e.g., hallucination) and
x_{i}^{-}
the opposing behaviour (e.g., factual adherence). For each input
x
and layer
l
, we extract the hidden state
\mathbf{h}^{(l)}(x)=\mathcal{M}(x)^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
at the final input token, taken from the residual stream immediately after the
l
-th transformer block. Flattening pairs into individual labelled examples yields
\{(x_{j},y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{2N}
with
y_{j}\in\{+1,-1\}
, used by methods that operate on labelled instances rather than pairs. We study four extraction methods
f(\cdot)
: Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA)
(
41
)
, a normalised CAA variant, Recursive Feature Machines (RFM)
(
5
)
, and Logistic Regression
(
5
;
58
;
30
)
, defined as follows.

•

CAA.
The steering vector is the mean difference between positive and negative activations at layer
l

(
41
)
:

\mathbf{v}^{(l)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{h}^{(l)}(x_{i}^{+})-\mathbf{h}^{(l)}(x_{i}^{-})\right).

•

Normalised CAA.
Identical to CAA, but the vector is unit-normalised before use:
\bar{\mathbf{v}}^{(l)}=\mathbf{v}^{(l)}/\lVert\mathbf{v}^{(l)}\rVert
. This decouples the direction from its magnitude, so
\lambda
alone controls steering strength.

•

RFM.
We adopt the Recursive Feature Machines for calculating steering vectors as proposed by
5
. We adopt the implementation from the official repository and refer readers to
(
5
)
for method details. (refer to Appendix
B

•

Logistic Regression.
An
\ell_{2}
-regularised logistic classifier is fit on the hidden states, and its weight vector is taken as the steering direction:
We use the default
scikit-learn
implementation (refer to Appendix

The activation-steering setup introduces a large design space. We catalogue

Experimental Setup

We assess the utility of activation steering as a data-generation tool for safety-detection models. The pipeline has three stages: (i) curate contrastive pairs for each target concept, (ii) extract steering vectors per (concept, method, model, layer) combination, and (iii) use the steered generations in two downstream investigations. 6
Conclusion

We evaluated activation steering as a generator of rare HHH-violating data for safety detection, focusing on two areas not investigated in the literature:
diversity
and
downstream utility
. Intrinsically, steering success rises with steering strength while coherence and
diversity degrade. Extrinsically, detectors fine-tuned on the best steered configurations beat a prompting baseline on
3
of
4
concepts, but downstream utility lives in a narrow regime where success, coherence, and diversity are jointly satisfied. The harmonic mean of these three axes correlates with downstream AUROC consistently enough to serve as a practical tuning target. Practitioners can navigate the vast hyperparameter space of AS more effectively. Overall, our results provide a grounded heuristic for AS hyperparameter tuning and highlight the potential of AS as a synthetic data generator for specific target concepts.

Limitations

Our study has several scope limitations.
(i) Hyperparameter scope:
AS admits many hyperparameters (Appendix A), and we fixed most of the hyperparameters to practically justifiable values. Given the sensitivity of hyperparameters in AS
(
58
;
25
;
11
;
12
;
48
;
34
;
52
)
, changes to fixed values may affect our findings, and the extent of impact is not measured in our experiments.

(ii) Small sample size:

112
fine-tuning runs reduce to only
28
extrinsic measurements per dataset. Hence, the correlation between
intrinsic
and
extrinsic
metrics has limited generalizability. However, the consistency of the positive correlation between our heuristic and AUROC across concepts and ablations is therefore a stronger argument than any single coefficient.
(iii) Length bias:
compression ratio and
n
-gram diversity are length-sensitive; truncation reduces but does not eliminate this bias.

(iv) Effect of model scale:
We primarily focused on only one model family (Olmo) with two checkpoints (
7
and
32
B). Hence, the better steering outcomes for smaller model can only be interpreted for Olmo-
7
B.

Broader Impacts

The overarching goal of our study is to explore and investigate existing methods to improve safety detection models. However, we acknowledge the potential risks of misuse of our work. To mitigate the negative impact of the misuse, we propose to release code but not the steered generations, leaving a compute barrier and audit trail for reproduction. We provide a more detailed discussion in Appendix
A
.

Appendix A
Broader Impacts and Ethics Statement

Intent and scope.

The goal of this work is to strengthen safety-detection classifiers that flag harmful outputs from language models, addressing the chronic scarcity of HHH-violating training examples. We do not repurpose activation steering as a tool for producing harmful content; we evaluate whether it can strengthen safety detection. The steering methods we evaluate are established in prior literature
[
41
,
5
,
58
]
; we contribute analysis, not new techniques.

Positive impact.

Safety detectors gate LLM deployment but require harmful examples that are scarce and ethically fraught to source. Our work characterises when activation steering can supply such examples synthetically. The proposed heuristic (harmonic mean of success, coherence, and diversity) helps practitioners discard low-utility configurations during validation, reducing wasted compute and the risk of training detectors on poor data.

Negative impact.

The same machinery can produce harmful content for non-defensive use.
(i) Misuse:
A motivated actor could apply these methods to open-weight models to generate toxic, sycophantic, hallucinated, or unfaithful text at scale; our narrow-regime finding provides partial information about which configurations are most effective.
(ii) False security:
Detectors trained on steered data may overfit to the artefacts we document (length drift, diversity collapse) and fail on real-world content, which is concerning if such detectors are treated as hard safety gates.
(iii) Asymmetric error costs:
High false-positive rates could disproportionately suppress speech from groups whose language patterns differ from the model’s training distribution; we do not audit this property.

Mitigations.

(1) Gated release:
we release code and configurations but not the steered generations, leaving a compute barrier and audit trail for reproduction.
(2) Defence in depth:
AS-trained detectors should be one component of a layered safety system, not a single gate.
(3) Real-world validation:
detectors must be validated on an independent corpus of real harmful content before deployment, not on held-out synthetic data alone.
(4) Red-team asymmetry:
defensive use of our heuristic is operationally simpler than offensive use, which additionally requires evading deployed safety systems. Our central finding — that downstream utility lies in a narrow regime jointly satisfying success, coherence, and diversity — indicates that reliable misuse via steering is more constrained than commonly assumed.

LLM usage disclosure.

All prose in this paper was drafted by the human authors; large language models (LLMs) were used only to edit and tighten already-written text, never to generate drafts of any section. We additionally used LLMs to convert tabular data from CSV into LaTeX format and to assist with visualisation. LLM-based coding assistants supported routine data-processing tasks and the implementation of well-defined statistical procedures such as Pearson correlations and paired
t
-tests.

All of our fine-tuning experiments are conducted with a replacement protocol, i.e., steered or prompting generations replace the true positives in the available training data. Our preference for the replacement protocol over augmentation was based on two key reasons. First, replacement controls the dataset size, lowering compute cost. This collapse erases the signal needed to differentiate bad, moderate, and good steering configurations. Replacement instead spreads configurations from near-random to near-ceiling, preserving the dynamic range needed for our analysis.

F.4
Prompt Optimization for the Prompting Baseline

For the adversarial prompts used for the prompting baseline (Section
4.1
); we iteratively optimised them over
4
–
6
rounds before freezing them for the main sweep. For each concept, we started from a first version, generated a small sample of responses, and inspected whether the outputs (i) exhibited the target concept at a high rate and (ii) were coherent and on-topic. We then added components such as a data-generation framing, explicit definitions of the target concept, format constraints (sentence limits, no reasoning), and output-start markers. The final templates in Section
F
reflect this tuning and should be treated as engineered prompting performance; naive or unoptimised prompts would likely produce weaker baselines and inflate the apparent margin of AS over prompting.
