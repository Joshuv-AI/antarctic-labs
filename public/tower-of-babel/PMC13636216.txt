Mechanical‐medical convergence in heart failure: Artificial intelligence, finite‐element modeling, and 3D printing for diagnosis and prognosis
Authors: Quazi Noor E Sabrina, Quazi Md Zobaer Shah, Quazi Noor E Sohela, Md Mahabub Hasan Mousum, Md. Moyeen Uddin Chisty, Quazi Md. Akbar Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeart failure remains a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, yet routine clinical indices often miss the regional biomechanical disturbances that drive progression and shape treatment response. This State‐of‐the‐Art review examines how finite‐element (FE) modeling, additive manufacturing, and artificial intelligence (AI) are converging to improve the diagnosis, phenotyping, procedural planning, and prognostic assessment of heart failure (HF). Although these technologies have matured in structural heart disease and transcatheter intervention research, their greatest translational potential may lie in HF, where patient‐specific ventricular remodeling, myocardial stress–strain heterogeneity, valve‐ventricular coupling, and device‐tissue interaction are incompletely captured by conventional clinical indices. We synthesize translational and clinical literature published from 2015 to 2026 on imaging‐derived FE models, multimaterial 3D‐printed cardiovascular phantoms, and AI‐based analytic pipelines relevant to HF populations or HF‐related decision pathways. FE approaches provide mechanistic estimates of regional myocardial deformation, wall stress, and remodeling trajectories; 3D‐printed phantoms enable bench‐top validation, device rehearsal, and hemodynamic replication; and AI supports segmentation, phenotyping, multimodal integration, and risk prediction. Across all three domains, however, the evidence base remains dominated by retrospective studies, bench validation, and small translational cohorts, with limited prospective outcome validation and inconsistent reporting of reproducibility. We therefore propose an evidence‐aware, HF‐centered framework for integrating computational mechanics, physical phantoms, and data‐driven models into digital‐physical twin workflows. From a clinical perspective, the near‐term opportunity is not autonomous decision‐making, but better mechanistic phenotyping, more transparent procedural planning, and more rigorous testing of patient‐specific hypotheses before intervention. Standardized reporting, external validation, careful materials characterization, and multicenter endpoint‐linked studies will be essential for clinical translation.

Translational Impact StatementIntegrating imaging‐derived finite‐element cardiac mechanics, multimaterial 3D‐printed patient phantoms, and data‐driven models enables a translational digital–physical twin approach that links mechanistic insight with experimental validation and predictive analytics; this framework can improve patient‐specific diagnosis, procedural planning, and risk stratification, streamline device testing, and accelerate clinical translation of precision heart‐failure interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Heart failure (HF) remains a major global health challenge, affecting more than 64 million individuals worldwide and contributing to substantial mortality, frequent hospitalizations, and escalating socioeconomic burden. 1 , 2 Despite advances in pharmacological therapy, device‐based interventions, and surgery, clinical outcomes in HF remain highly variable. This variability reflects the complex, patient‐specific interactions among myocardial remodeling, valvular dysfunction, and ventricular–vascular coupling that govern disease progression and response to therapy. 3 , 4 , 5 Conventional diagnostic approaches, which rely largely on global indices such as left ventricular ejection fraction, chamber dimensions, and surrogate pressure‐volume metrics, often fail to capture the underlying biomechanical abnormalities that precede irreversible dysfunction or determine therapeutic efficacy. 6

At its core, HF is a disease of disturbed cardiac mechanics. Alterations in myocardial material properties, wall stress distribution, fiber architecture, and ventricular geometry drive maladaptive remodeling and progressive functional decline. 7 Valvular heart disease (VHD) frequently coexists with HF, either as a primary driver through chronic pressure or volume overload or as a secondary consequence of ventricular dilation and annular deformation. 8 Although anatomical imaging and Doppler‐based hemodynamic assessment remain central to clinical practice, these methods provide limited insight into regional stress–strain behavior, energetic inefficiency, and early biomechanical derangements that may precede overt functional deterioration. 9

Finite‐element (FE) modeling has emerged as a powerful framework for quantifying cardiac mechanics by integrating imaging‐derived geometry, constitutive myocardial behavior, and physiologically relevant boundary conditions. 10 Early studies demonstrated the ability of FE models to estimate regional myocardial stress and predict remodeling under pressure‐ and volume‐overload states, establishing a mechanistic basis for understanding HF progression. 6 Advances in cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) and three‐dimensional (3D) echocardiography have further enabled increasingly patient‐specific FE models, narrowing the translational gap between computational biomechanics and clinical cardiology. 6

In parallel, 3D printing has gained prominence in cardiovascular medicine, initially for anatomical visualization and procedural planning. 11 In VHD, patient‐specific printed phantoms have been widely used to simulate transcatheter valve deployment, assess paravalvular leakage, and support training in anatomically complex scenarios. 12 More recently, the integration of FE‐derived stress and deformation fields with 3D printing has enabled the fabrication of functional cardiac phantoms that reproduce both anatomy and mechanical behavior, allowing experimental validation of computational predictions through in vitro testing. 13

Recent cardiovascular artificial intelligence (AI) literature highlights the value of integrating data‐driven learning with physics‐based digital twins to improve interpretability and scalability, while also emphasizing that most current approaches remain retrospectively validated and insufficiently mature for unsupervised clinical decision support. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 However, most AI‐driven HF applications remain largely phenomenological and insufficiently linked to underlying myocardial and valvular mechanics, raising concerns regarding interpretability, generalizability, and clinical trust. 19 Recent conceptual advances therefore emphasize the convergence of FE modeling, 3D printing, and AI into integrated “digital‐physical twin” paradigms, in which mechanistic modeling, experimental validation, and data‐driven prediction inform patient‐specific care. 10

Although promising, most studies to date are retrospective, include modest sample sizes, and rely on surrogate endpoints, underscoring the need for outcome‐linked validation. Despite their promise, adoption of these technologies in routine HF management is constrained by nonstandardized workflows, limited validation against longitudinal clinical outcomes, a relative scarcity of HF‐focused studies compared with valve‐centric applications, and unresolved regulatory and ethical challenges related to AI‐enabled decision support and advanced manufacturing. 14 , 19 , 20 A comparison of recent review articles highlights this gap and underscores the distinct HF‐centric focus of the present work (Table 1).

This review was conducted as a narrative, targeted literature synthesis rather than a formal systematic review. We searched PubMed and Scopus for peer‐reviewed publications from January 2015 through February 2026 and supplemented database searching with backward citation tracking of key original articles and relevant review papers. Search terms combined controlled vocabulary and free‐text keywords related to HF and the enabling technologies discussed in this review, including “HF,” “cardiac remodeling,” “FE,” “biomechanics,” “3D printing,” “additive manufacturing,” “AI,” “machine learning (ML),” “digital twin,” “ventricular assist device (VAD),” “transcatheter,” and “patient‐specific model.” Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance to HF pathophysiology, HF‐related procedural planning, patient‐specific cardiovascular modeling, and translational validation. We prioritized studies that directly addressed HF populations, informed HF‐related decision pathways, or provided enabling methods later applied to HF‐relevant structural heart applications. We excluded noncardiovascular applications, purely technical engineering studies without cardiovascular interpretive relevance, duplicate records, and superseded reports. Additional seminal and widely cited studies were included where necessary to provide historical context and conceptual completeness.

CARDIAC BIOMECHANICS AND FE MODELING

In HF, FE modeling is particularly relevant for quantifying regional stress patterns that drive ventricular remodeling and functional decline. While many technical advances in FE and structural heart modeling originate in valve and device research, this review focuses on how these computational and physical modeling techniques inform HF care. Mechanically driven processes—including regional wall stress, adverse remodeling, valve–ventricular coupling, and device–tissue interaction—are central to HF progression and therapeutic response. Accordingly, the following sections interpret each methodology in terms of its relevance to HF phenotyping, clinical decision‐making, and level of outcome validation. Within this HF‐centered framework, bioprosthetic valve mechanics, and structural valve degeneration are considered primarily in terms of their impact on ventricular loading conditions, remodeling trajectories, and overall ventricular–vascular coupling.

Core HF pathophysiology as the translational anchor

HF is fundamentally driven by multiscale alterations spanning myocardial structure, mechanics, and systemic hemodynamics. Central to this process is ventricular remodeling, characterized by changes in chamber geometry, wall stress distribution, and contractile efficiency, often accompanied by myocardial fibrosis that alters tissue stiffness and electromechanical coupling. In parallel, electrical and mechanical dyssynchrony further impairs coordinated contraction, reducing pump efficiency and promoting adverse remodeling. Distinct HF phenotypes, particularly heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), are increasingly recognized as disorders of diastolic dysfunction, microvascular impairment, and ventricular–vascular uncoupling rather than isolated systolic failure. Importantly, ventricular–vascular coupling governs the interaction between cardiac contractility and arterial load, serving as a critical determinant of energetic efficiency and clinical progression. These interconnected mechanisms provide the physiological foundation upon which computational modeling, data‐driven approaches, and experimental platforms must be interpreted to ensure translational relevance. Conditions including obesity, diabetes mellitus, metabolic syndrome, and hypertension contribute to a chronic systemic inflammatory state marked by increased circulating cytokine levels, dysregulated adipokine profiles, and endothelial activation. At the cellular level, dysregulation of calcium handling contributes to divergent phenotypes of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and HFpEF, influencing both systolic contractility and diastolic relaxation dynamics. A most recent review, as shown in Figure 1 further highlights those alterations in calcium‐regulating proteins—including SERCA2a, RyR2, and the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger—lead to impaired calcium cycling, thereby linking molecular dysfunction to macroscopic features such as ventricular remodeling, stiffness, and impaired relaxation. These findings reinforce the multiscale nature of HF pathophysiology and support the integration of cellular mechanisms into computational and translational modeling frameworks.

Because this review is centered on HF, the valve‐focused examples below are included only where they alter ventricular loading conditions, contribute to HF‐relevant structural deterioration, or provide transferable computational and experimental methods for mechanistic phenotyping, intervention planning, and device–tissue interaction.

FE modeling of valve mechanics and HF–relevant biomechanics

Bioprosthetic heart valves (BHVs) remain widely used because they provide favorable hemodynamics, no audible mechanical noise, and less need for lifelong anticoagulation than mechanical valves. 26 , 27 Their major limitation is reduced durability, as structural valve deterioration arises from both biologically mediated tissue degeneration and calcification and mechanically driven fatigue under repetitive cyclic loading. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 These processes often interact, making valve longevity and failure patterns difficult to predict. 26 , 27 , 33 , 34

Under cyclic loading, BHV leaflets undergo progressive geometric and microstructural remodeling. After approximately 50 million cycles, corresponding to roughly 2 years of in vivo function, leaflets show permanent deformation, especially in the central belly region, as illustrated in Figure 2 (left). 26 This remodeling is accompanied by collagen redistribution rather than overt fiber rupture, and collagen orientation metrics remain relatively stable despite substantial macroscopic deformation. 35 , 36 Accordingly, BHV fatigue is often viewed as a staged process in which early geometric remodeling precedes stress redistribution, microstructural injury, and eventual leaflet failure. 37

Representative deformation patterns of BHV leaflets following prolonged cyclic loading demonstrate permanent geometric remodeling localized primarily within the leaflet belly region. Concurrently, fatigue‐associated plastic deformation leads to progressive alterations in leaflet curvature and deformation metrics over repeated loading cycles. Together, these observations illustrate the spatial and temporal evolution of leaflet mechanics during fatigue progression. Adapted from 26 (with permission, License Number 6238580974023).

Time‐dependent FE frameworks have therefore been developed to model this long‐term biomechanical evolution. 26 , 37 These approaches use constitutive laws for exogenously crosslinked soft tissues to simulate changing leaflet geometry, material properties, and collagen architecture under prolonged cyclic loading. A typical workflow includes quasi‐static simulation of the initial valve, updating of material parameters to account for plastic deformation, and iterative geometric remeshing across loading cycles. Compared with uncycled valves, valves subjected to 50 million cycles show reduced circumferential and radial stretch, reflecting progressive stiffening and more homogeneous deformation patterns. 26 These changes, illustrated in Figure 2 (right), demonstrate the spatial and temporal evolution of stretch, shear, and curvature during fatigue.

Collagen fiber architecture plays a central role in governing the mechanical response and fatigue behavior of BHVs. Comparative FE simulations incorporating isotropic fiber distributions, bovine pericardial fiber architectures, and native porcine aortic valve fiber architectures demonstrate pronounced differences in strain heterogeneity and deformation patterns. 26 , 38

As shown in Figure 3, valves with isotropic or exogenously crosslinked pericardial fiber architectures exhibit relatively uniform deformation under loading, whereas valves incorporating native porcine aortic valve fiber architecture display pronounced spatial heterogeneity, particularly within the leaflet belly region. The fully pressure‐loaded FE results are quantified using maximum in‐plane Green–Lagrange strain (MIPE). The porcine valve (Figure 3c) demonstrates marked heterogeneity in deformation, in contrast to the more homogeneous patterns observed in the pericardial (Figure 3b) and isotropic (Figure 3a) models, especially in the top‐down view. These latter two configurations show comparable deformation magnitudes, with MIPE values of approximately 0.2. Collectively, these findings suggest that aligned collagen fibers can modulate deformation through fiber straightening, highlighting the importance of accurately representing collagen architecture for reliable prediction of BHV fatigue behavior and valve design optimization.

Experimental validation and fluid–structure interaction

Robust validation of FE‐based BHV models necessitates integration with experimental platforms capable of reproducing physiological flow conditions. To this end, high‐fidelity fluid–structure interaction (FSI) models based on the immersed boundary method have been developed to simulate BHV dynamics within pulse‐duplicator systems (Figure 5). 35 These models employ anatomically realistic geometries and incorporate reduced‐order Windkessel representations to replicate physiological pressure and flow boundary conditions.

Model predictions demonstrate good qualitative agreement with experimental measurements of leaflet kinematics, including sequential leaflet closure and large‐scale flow features, as shown in Figure 6. 35 Remaining discrepancies between simulations and experiments are attributed to factors such as inter‐leaflet geometric variability, differences in collagen fiber architecture, and unresolved turbulent flow effects at physiological Reynolds numbers on the order of 20,000. 26 , 40

ADDITIVE MANUFACTURING (3D PRINTING) IN CARDIOLOGY

Rationale and evolution of 3D printing in cardiovascular medicine

Additive manufacturing, more commonly known as 3D printing, has emerged as a transformative tool in cardiovascular medicine by enabling the fabrication of patient‐specific anatomical and functional models directly derived from clinical imaging datasets. The growing anatomical complexity of both congenital and acquired heart disease, together with the increasing demand for individualized structural interventions, has driven the widespread adoption of 3D printing for anatomical visualization, procedural planning, medical education, and device evaluation. 43 , 44 A broad spectrum of cardiovascular pathologies has been modeled using this approach, including intracardiac defects, vascular malformations, and airway abnormalities, as illustrated in Figure 8. 43

Three‐dimensional (3D) printed models of complex cardiovascular anatomy. 3D printed models of complex intracardiac and extracardiac heart disease across pediatric and adult patients, demonstrating their role in surgical planning, education, and anatomical visualization. 43 (With permission from, 43 Order No. 6238600310574).

Initial cardiovascular applications of 3D printing focused primarily on static anatomical replication based on CT imaging datasets. With subsequent advances in CMR, three‐dimensional transesophageal echocardiography (3D TEE), and increasingly sophisticated image segmentation methodologies, the scope of 3D printing has expanded to encompass a wider range of structural and functional cardiac pathologies. 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 These developments have positioned 3D printing as a critical translational interface linking medical imaging, computational modeling, and hands‐on clinical decision‐making.

Clinical experience has demonstrated particular value in pediatric and congenital heart disease (CHD), where complex 3D relationships among cardiac chambers, great vessels, and airways are often difficult to fully appreciate using conventional two‐dimensional imaging alone. Patient‐specific printed models have enabled precise preoperative planning in infants with vascular rings, tetralogy of Fallot with pulmonary atresia, total anomalous pulmonary venous return, and other complex intracardiac and extracardiac anomalies. By physically reproducing patient anatomy, these models have facilitated improved surgical strategy selection, enhanced interdisciplinary communication, and more effective education of trainees and caregivers, as shown in Figure 8.

Image acquisition and segmentation for patient‐specific modeling

Image segmentation constitutes the foundational step in the 3D printing workflow, enabling the transformation of volumetric medical imaging datasets into patient‐specific digital anatomical models. Segmentation pipelines typically begin with the export of imaging data in Digital Imaging and Communication in Medicine (DICOM) format, followed by intensity‐based thresholding and iterative manual refinement to accurately isolate target cardiovascular structures. 44

Historically, segmentation for cardiovascular 3D printing has relied predominantly on CT imaging due to its high spatial resolution and robust depiction of calcified and vascular structures. 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 More recently, CMR imaging has been increasingly employed to reconstruct congenital heart defects and systemic vascular anatomy with high geometric fidelity, particularly in younger patients and those requiring serial imaging without ionizing radiation exposure. 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 , 61 In parallel, the feasibility of reconstructing mitral valve leaflets and annular geometry from 3D TEE datasets has been demonstrated by multiple groups. 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 Hybrid segmentation strategies that combine CT‐derived structural detail with echocardiography‐based leaflet morphology have further improved geometric accuracy and functional representation. 49 , 51

Once segmentation is completed, the resulting masks are converted into stereolithography (STL) files to enable fabrication. These digital models often undergo additional modification using computer‐aided design (CAD) software to tailor the geometry for specific clinical or experimental objectives, including integration into flow‐loop systems, color‐coding of anatomical regions, or assignment of region‐specific material properties. 49 The overall workflow from image acquisition to fabrication of a multimaterial patient‐specific model is summarized in Figure 9.

Workflow for patient‐specific three‐dimensional (3D) printing. 3D printed modeling of patient‐specific anatomy illustrating the sequential workflow from computed tomography (CT) image acquisition and segmentation to digital model refinement and multimaterial fabrication. 44 (With permission from, 44 Order No. 6238610307736).

Cardiovascular 3D printing commonly employs material jetting (PolyJet), SLA, and fused deposition modeling (FDM), each with distinct implications for geometric fidelity, mechanical realism, and reproducibility. PolyJet is particularly suited to multimaterial fabrication because it can combine compliant and rigid regions within a single model, making it useful for patient‐specific cardiovascular phantoms that require both anatomical accuracy and regionally differentiated mechanics. SLA offers high spatial resolution and smooth surface finish, which is advantageous for reproducing fine anatomical detail and thin‐walled structures, although resin selection and postcuring conditions strongly influence the final mechanical response. 62 FDM is more accessible and cost‐effective, but its output is generally more anisotropic and less anatomically refined because model quality depends on layer height, nozzle temperature, raster orientation, infill density, and cooling conditions. 63 Across these platforms, key technical parameters that should be reported include imaging source, segmentation workflow, file conversion steps, layer thickness, print orientation, support strategy, curing or postprocessing conditions, and the elastic properties of the selected material. For HF‐related applications, geometric fidelity alone is insufficient; mechanical fidelity must also be characterized under use‐specific loading conditions so that the printed model can be interpreted appropriately as an anatomic, procedural, or biomechanical surrogate. 64

Educational, communication, and training applications

Patient‐specific 3D‐printed cardiac models have demonstrated substantial value in medical education, surgical rehearsal, and patient‐physician communication. Physical models representing complex CHD, valvular abnormalities, and vascular malformations have been widely used to enhance anatomical understanding among trainees, nurses, and allied healthcare professionals, while also improving procedural confidence and informed consent discussions with patients and families. 65 , 66 , 67

Beyond professional training, 3D‐printed models provide a tangible medium for patient education by enabling visualization of otherwise abstract anatomical and pathophysiological concepts. Multimaterial and multicolored heart models can be selectively simplified or intentionally exaggerated to illustrate disease progression and long‐term hemodynamic consequences of untreated defects. For example, progressive right atrial and right ventricular dilation associated with atrial septal defect (ASD) has been effectively demonstrated using sequential models that depict increasing chamber enlargement over time, as shown in Figure 10. 68

Beyond static anatomical representation, 3D printing has enabled the development of functional cardiac phantoms capable of replicating patient‐specific hemodynamics when integrated with pulsatile flow‐loop systems. CLINICAL APPLICATIONS: DIAGNOSIS AND PROGNOSIS USING AI AND ML

Conceptual foundations of AI in cardiovascular medicine

AI broadly refers to computational systems designed to perform tasks that traditionally require human intelligence, including pattern recognition, classification, and decision‐making. ML, a major subset of AI, originated from computer science and statistical learning theory and relies on large datasets to identify complex patterns and relationships without explicit rule‐based programming. 76 , 77 A further subset, deep learning (DL), employs artificial neural networks (ANNs) composed of multiple interconnected computational layers inspired by biological neural systems, enabling hierarchical feature extraction and nonlinear decision boundaries, as illustrated in Figure 15.

SVM = support vector machines; RF = random forests; KNN = k‐nearest neighbor; PCA = principal components analysis; DNN = deep neural network; CNN = convolutional neural network; RNN = recurrent neural network; GAN = generative adversarial network; VAE = variational autoencoder. 76 (With permission from, 76 Order No. 6238611355483).

In cardiovascular applications, ML workflows typically divide available data into training, validation, and testing subsets. Training datasets are used to estimate model parameters, validation datasets to optimize hyperparameters and prevent overfitting, and independent testing datasets to evaluate generalizability. Strict separation of testing data from model development is essential to avoid information leakage and artificial inflation of predictive performance. 76

Natural language processing and longitudinal disease surveillance

Natural language processing (NLP), when combined with rule‐based engines, has enabled large‐scale identification and longitudinal surveillance of VHD using both structured and unstructured echocardiographic report data. 82 Significant discrepancies were identified between quantitative measurements—such as aortic valve area—and narrative report conclusions, underscoring the value of AI‐assisted reconciliation of imaging data.

EVIDENCE MATURITY AND TRANSLATIONAL READINESS

The translational maturity of FE modeling, AI, and 3D‐printed cardiac phantoms remains heterogeneous. FE approaches are predominantly validated in retrospective or simulation‐based studies, with limited prospective clinical validation. 3D printed phantoms are widely used for in vitro testing and procedural rehearsal; however, direct evidence linking their use to improved patient outcomes remains sparse. In contrast, AI‐based approaches have achieved broader clinical integration, particularly in imaging and risk stratification, although many models rely on retrospective datasets and lack robust external validation. Importantly, differences in study design—including retrospective analyses, preclinical experimentation, and early‐phase clinical studies—complicate direct comparison of predictive performance and reproducibility. Establishing standardized validation pipelines, including prospective trials and multi‐center reproducibility assessments, will be essential for translating these approaches into routine HF care. Direct quantitative comparison of predictive performance across FE models, 3D‐printed phantoms, and AI systems is not currently appropriate because these studies differ substantially in endpoints, datasets, validation standards, and intended clinical use. FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND CONCLUSIONS

The integration of FE modeling, ML, and patient‐specific 3D platforms offers a powerful framework to interrogate core HF mechanisms across scales. Computational models enable quantification of ventricular stress–strain distributions associated with remodeling and fibrosis, while data‐driven approaches can identify phenotypic patterns such as HFpEF heterogeneity and dyssynchrony‐related dysfunction. Similarly, 3D‐printed cardiac phantoms and in vitro systems provide controlled environments to evaluate ventricular–vascular interactions and device–tissue coupling under physiologically relevant conditions. Anchoring these approaches within established HF pathophysiology strengthens mechanistic interpretation and improves their relevance for diagnosis, risk stratification, and therapeutic planning.

Limitations and future directions

Despite substantial progress in data‐driven phenotyping, translation of cardiac mechanics into clinical practice remains limited by nonstandardized definitions, heterogeneous modeling assumptions, and incomplete validation. FE‐derived descriptors are sensitive to imaging quality, segmentation, constitutive modeling, boundary conditions, and mesh resolution, while experimental and phantom studies often lack harmonized reporting. These factors currently impede reproducibility and multicenter comparison.

To address this gap, drawing on prior FE, imaging, and phantom studies, 6 , 26 , 44 , 84 , 85 , 86 we propose a concise set of candidate mechanical descriptors and minimum reporting elements (Table 3). Future work should prioritize reproducible, integrated workflows linking imaging, FE modeling, AI analysis, and experimental validation. Such workflows may support the development of patient‐specific digital‐physical twins for hypothesis testing, therapeutic planning, and risk stratification. Prospective, multicenter studies will be essential to establish reproducibility, clinical relevance, and regulatory confidence. Parallel advances in bioprinting, materials science, and AI‐based surrogate modeling may further enhance physiologic fidelity while reducing computational burden. Ethical considerations, including data governance, transparency, and algorithmic bias, must be addressed to ensure responsible clinical adoption.

Taken together, recent studies indicate a transition from isolated modeling or fabrication approaches toward integrated translational platforms in which 3D printing, FE modeling, and machine‐learning‐based phenotyping are jointly optimized. This convergence is particularly relevant for myocardial fibrosis, ventricular remodeling, and personalized HF management, where patient‐specific constructs, computational simulation, and digital‐twin frameworks may enhance risk stratification, procedural planning, and prediction of therapeutic response. 87 , 88 , 89 , 90 , 91 , 92 , 93 , 94 , 95 , 96 , 97 , 98 , 99 Moreover, emerging work extending these approaches to electrophysiological modeling, vascular remodeling, and device‐based interventions highlights their broader potential for integrated cardiovascular decision support. 100 , 101 , 102 , 103 , 104 , 105

Conclusions

HF is a mechanically complex syndrome that cannot be fully characterized by conventional clinical metrics alone. FE modeling, additive manufacturing, and AI offer complementary tools to quantify cardiac mechanics, experimentally validate computational predictions, and extract clinically meaningful patterns from high‐dimensional data. This review highlights how their integration provides a coherent framework for mechanistic phenotyping and precision HF research. With standardized reporting, rigorous validation, and interdisciplinary collaboration, integrated digital‐physical modeling approaches have the potential to advance HF management toward more predictive, personalized, and mechanistically grounded care. At present, these technologies are best viewed not as standalone clinical tools, but as complementary components of a developing translational ecosystem, where their greatest impact will depend on rigorous validation, integration, and HF‐specific clinical targeting.
