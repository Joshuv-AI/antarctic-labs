Deep learning identifies water bodies from low-cost drone images for mosquito larval habitat mapping
Authors: Micanaldo Ernesto Francisco, Andri Ruliansyah, Firda Yanuar Pradani, Gaku Masuda, Lia Faridah, Kozo Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mosquito larval source management remains a critical strategy for malaria control, but traditional methods for mapping breeding sites are labor-intensive and slow. While drones offer a promising tool, their application is often hindered by reliance on costly orthomosaic generation (stitching drone images into precise, georeferenced maps) and multispectral sensors, creating significant barriers for resource-limited health programs. This study developed an alternative workflow using a deep learning approach for water body segmentation. We utilized the DeepLabV3 + architecture with an EfficientNetV2 backbone on still RGB and Grayscale drone imagery, bypassing the need for orthomosaics by directly georeferencing individual images using GPS metadata. The model was trained and evaluated on a dataset of over 4,400 images from Pangandaran Regency, Indonesia, with performance assessed via the Intersection over Union (IoU) metric. Predictions were validated through field inspections to confirm water presence and larval abundance. The model achieved a mean IoU of 0.86 on RGB images and 0.80 on Grayscale data, demonstrating robust performance even with minimal spectral information. Field validation of 47 predicted sites confirmed water presence in all cases (100% confirmation of water presence), with 31.9% found to contain mosquito larvae, including the primary local vectors Anopheles vagus and An. sundaicus. The entire workflow, from cloud-based processing to field localization, was executed without specialized hardware or software. Our findings demonstrate that a deep learning-based analysis of still drone imagery provides a scalable, cost-effective, and rapid alternative to multispectral and orthomosaic-dependent methods for larval habitat mapping. This approach democratizes advanced surveillance technology, significantly shortens the delay between data collection and intervention, and holds great promise for enhancing the efficiency of vector control programs in malaria-endemic regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58240-4.

Introduction

Malaria poses a significant global health burden, with 249 million cases in 2022, a 5 million increase from 20211. Effective surveillance and vector control through larval source management (LSM) are critical to prevent malaria re-emergence2,3. This is particularly relevant in regions like Indonesia, which aims for malaria-free status by 20304, although struggling to contain infections in remote areas5. In specific areas like Pangandaran Regency, remains vulnerable to imported malaria despite subnational elimination certification2,6. Effective surveillance and vector control through LSM are critical to prevent malaria re-emergence.

Drone technology has become an asset in tackling the limitations of traditional ground-based surveillance methods for mosquito control in LSM. While ground surveys can be effective, they are typically labor-intensive, time-consuming, and challenging in expansive or hard-to-access areas7. Drones are capable of capturing high-resolution images across wide regions, enhancing the efficiency of mapping potential mosquito breeding water bodies. However, detecting water in drone imagery is complicated by the inherent complexity of water’s appearance. Variations in lighting, exposure, turbidity, and depth during drone flights can lead to substantial differences in the color and spectral reflectance of water bodies in images8,9. For instance, flooded areas display distinct colors compared to non-flooded ones or those containing grass, making consistent automated detection a significant challenge.

One common approach for mapping water bodies with drones is manual digitization using Geographic Information Systems (GIS) software, which leverages the human ability to easily identify water. This method involves identifying and delineating water bodies by hand on drone images10. Although effective for small areas, this technique becomes increasingly time-consuming and prone to human error and underdetection when applied to larger, unfamiliar, or complex environments, making it less feasible for large-scale operations7,11.

To address the limitations of manual digitization, more advanced techniques such as machine learning have been employed. However, these models face challenges when using standard RGB imagery due to the data heterogeneity described earlier. Since machine learning classifiers depend on consistent data patterns, they can yield inaccurate results when faced with the high variability in the spectral responses of water bodies, which can change markedly depending on illumination conditions, water depth, turbidity, surface roughness, and the presence of submerged or emergent vegetation12. To deal with the limitations of RGB data, multispectral imaging is often recommended to enhance classification accuracy by capturing a broader range of spectral information, including near infrared light12,13. Nevertheless, multispectral imaging necessitates specialized drones and camera sensors, driving up costs and limiting use in malaria endemic- developing countries. Moreover, although both commercial and open-source photogrammetry software can generate from drone imagery, these workflows typically require substantial computational resources, technical expertise, and processing power and time, creating obstacles to rapid deployment in resource-limited settings14.

A major bottleneck in the current workflow is the time-consuming process of constructing orthomosaics — highprecision-georeferenced maps assembled by stitching many overlapping drone images after correcting for lens, perspective, and terrain distortions — prior water body detection. This is typically achieved using Structure-from-Motion photogrammetry coupled with multi-view stereo, where algorithms identify and match features across overlapping images to estimate camera positions and reconstruct dense three-dimensional point clouds before generating orthomosaic outputs15. This process involves georeferencing images and stitching them to form a cohesive map, which requires specialized photogrammetry software and powerful computing resources16. While highly effective at producing detailed spatial products, Structure-from-Motion and multi-view stereo processing requires considerable computational resources and careful parameterisation, contributing significantly to overall processing time prior to water body detection17. The lengthy duration of these analyses can impede the agility needed for timely detection and monitoring of mosquito habitats, raising concerns about the feasibility of using drones for mapping when orthomosaic creation is required18.

Several open‑source photogrammetry platforms, most notably Web Open Drone Map (WebODM), provide accessible pipelines for generating orthomosaics from RGB drone imagery and enable the derivation of vegetation and water‑related indices without multispectral sensors. These tools significantly lower software barriers compared to commercial photogrammetry suites and have proven valuable for high‑resolution environmental mapping. However, even cloud‑enabled or desktop open‑source workflows still rely on Structure‑from‑Motion and multi‑view stereo reconstruction, requiring dense image overlap, extended processing times, and a level of photogrammetric expertise that may limit their operational use in time‑sensitive vector‑control contexts. Moreover, the generation of orthomosaics remains computationally intensive regardless of licensing model, and can introduce delays between image acquisition and field intervention.

Recent advancements in deep learning have greatly enhanced the ability to detect and map water bodies through satellite19 and drone imagery20. Techniques such as convolutional neural networks and U-Net architectures show high accuracy in distinguishing water features within complex landscapes21,22. These models can analyse large amounts of high-resolution images, facilitating efficient and automated identification of water bodies, even in difficult conditions. The strengths of deep learning in water body detection lie in its capacity to learn complex spatial patterns, recognize colours, shapes and boundaries, and address variations in water appearance caused by environmental elements like lighting, turbidity, and vegetation cover23. A critical gap in previous studies is the lack of field confirmation connecting models’ predictions of water presence with actual larval presence. The assumption that detected water bodies are suitable for breeding is not always correct, as factors such as water quality and habitat conditions can prevent some water bodies from supporting mosquito larvae. This disconnect presents a notable shortcoming, where effective models for water detection fail to connect with actual breeding site validation.

To address these challenges, our study proposes a task‑oriented alternative to conventional orthomosaic‑based drone workflows by applying deep learning directly to individually georeferenced still RGB drone images for rapid water‑body localisation, aimed at enhancing LSM programs. Our research presents a technique that removes the reliance on orthomosaics and multispectral data, thereby simplifying the process and reducing the required time and computing resources. To demonstrate the robustness of our deep learning model, we further show that it achieves high accuracy even when using grayscale images, proving its capability to detect water with minimal color information. We suggest georeferencing every single image by utilizing the coordinates of its center and scaling the image size to correspond with its ground coverage. Rather than producing cartographically optimised spatial products, the proposed workflow is intentionally designed to prioritise speed, minimal computational overhead, and direct usability for field‑based LSM. Because no spatially explicit baseline data on mosquito breeding habitats were available for the study area, this study initially assumed that any surface water body could represent a potential larval habitat. Accordingly, our objective was to comprehensively detect and localise all water bodies using drone imagery, followed by field inspections to discriminate between inert water bodies and those actively supporting mosquito larvae. This two‑stage approach reflects a pragmatic surveillance strategy for settings where prior ecological stratification is unavailable.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted within a 3 km2 area located in the Pangandaran Regency of Jawa Barat province, Indonesia, with a central point at latitude 7° 40′ 47″ S and longitude 108° 34′ 41″ E. The site spans the adjacent villages of Cibenda and Sukaresik, situated in a low-lying coastal zone (Fig. 1). This region is characterized by a predominance of natural and artificial water bodies, and the survey area was selected randomly to capture this diversity. The terrain is characterized by low topographic relief, with minimal variation in elevation across the entire site. In terms of landscape composition, the area presents a mosaic of dense forests, residential zones, and extensive agricultural plots. A key feature is the high density and variety of water bodies, including flooded rice fields, rivers, and numerous aquaculture ponds. These ponds, both active and abandoned, are distributed throughout the landscape and are frequently found in close proximity to housing, within forested patches, and interspersed among rice fields. The combination of this heterogeneous landscape, the abundance of permanent and temporary water sources, and the region’s humid coastal climate, establishes a highly favourable environment for diverse mosquito genera, including the malaria vector, Anopheles.

Until 2021, Pangandaran was one of the Regencies with worrying malaria transmission rates in West Java region where the main malaria vectors were An. Sundaicus and An. Vagus24. However, Pangandaran has been reporting very low numbers of malaria cases and in May 2022, Pangandaran met the requirements and received malaria subnational malaria elimination certification2. This certification is given when a Regency or City indigenous malaria cases are absent for at least 36 months in a row.

Equipment and flight plan

Aerial imagery was captured using a DJI Mavic 3 Classic drone with DJI RC controller (DJI, Shenzhen, China), selected for its high-resolution photographic capabilities and relatively affordable if compared to professional-grade drones. The aircraft is equipped with a Hasselblad L2D-20c camera featuring a 20-megapixel, 4/3-inch CMOS sensor and a 24 mm equivalent lens (Fig. 2).

Fig. 2DJI Mavic 3 Classic aircraft used to capture RGB images in Pangandaran.

DJI Mavic 3 Classic aircraft used to capture RGB images in Pangandaran.

All flights were conducted at an altitude of 100 m, a setting deliberately chosen to operate well within the 120-meter ceiling prescribed by Indonesian national regulations. A consistent flight speed of 2.5 m/s was maintained to facilitate systematic image capture, with the camera triggered automatically at five-second intervals. Flight operations were managed via the DJI RC controller and DJI Fly App. A significant constraint of this controller is its inability to install other third-part flight control applications to create complex, multi-waypoint grids for automated area coverage. Consequently, a linear flight path strategy was adopted. For each transect, a simple path was defined between only two waypoints, a southern starting point (Point 1) and a northern ending point (Point 2). This ensured a consistent south-to-north trajectory, allowing the camera to capture north-facing photographs and minimize sun glare to avoid processing artifacts. To ensure geometric consistency, the map on the DJI RC was oriented to true north using the software’s orientation function. The two waypoints for each flight line were then aligned parallel to the map’s north-south gridlines using the screen edges as a visual guide, with precise placement assisted by a Bluetooth-connected mouse. Both points were set at the predefined 100-meter altitude. The total survey area of 3 km2 was subdivided into three sequential 1 km2 blocks. For each block, the home point for take-off and landing was established near its geometric centre. This strategy ensured the drone operator maintained a clear line of sight with the aircraft and minimized potential interference from physical obstacles, as required for safe operation. Due to the limited programmability of the DJI Fly App, it was not possible to predefine a fixed distance between adjacent flight paths. As a result, the number of flight lines required to cover each 1 km2 block varied based on manual placement (Supplementary file 1). The flights took place between November 15 and 18, 2024 (4 days). These flights were carried out under different weather conditions as shown in Table 125. The flights were carried out by a pilot duly licensed by the Indonesian authorities as required in the country.

Individual drone images georeferencing

The images captured by the DJI Mavic 3 Classic include embedded GPS coordinates for each image centre and flight altitude. To bypass the orthomosaic generation, each image was independently georeferenced using this metadata and empirically derived ground footprint. This approach utilizes the image centre latitude and longitude coordinates of each photograph, along with its calculated ground footprint dimensions (width and height). To determine the precise ground coverage of a single image, a calibration flight was conducted. A photograph was taken at the standard operational altitude of 100 m over a flat, feature-rich area easily identifiable in Google Satellite imagery. The camera was oriented at −90° and set to a 4:3 aspect ratio without digital zoom. This calibration image was then manually georeferenced in QGIS using Google Satellite as a basemap reference layer. The Helmert transformation algorithm was selected for this process, as it provided the highest geometric accuracy by performing a simple scale and rotation. The analysis confirmed that a single image from the DJI Mavic 3 Classic under these specific conditions covers a ground area of approximately 150 by 108 m. This footprint estimation assumes relatively uniform flight altitude above ground and low terrain variability, conditions satisfied by the study area. With these established footprint dimensions and the inherent GPS coordinates, every image in the dataset was programmatically georeferenced as a distinct, spatially located polygon, creating a spatially referenced image layer that could be directly used for model prediction and field deployment. These images were aimed for localisation‑oriented analysis rather than high‑precision spatial mapping. This procedure was carried out in R version 4.1.0, using the “raster”, “rgdal” and “exiftoolr” packages.

Deep learning model approach for semantic segmentation

For water body segmentation, this study employed the DeepLabV3 + architecture, selected for its strong performance in multi‑scale semantic segmentation and proven robustness in heterogeneous remote‑sensing environments8,26,27. We utilized an EfficientNetV2 backbone, which provides an effective balance between computational efficiency and feature extraction accuracy. The model leveraged pre-trained weights from the ImageNet dataset to initialize the backbone, thereby accelerating convergence and improving performance through transfer learning. The architecture integrates several key components: the EfficientNetV2 backbone for initial feature extraction, an Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling module to capture multi-scale contextual information at various receptive fields, and a decoder that refines the segmentation output by combining high-level semantic features with low-level spatial details from the encoder28. The final layer was configured for binary classification, outputting a pixel-wise mask distinguishing water from non-water. The model was trained setting the hyperparameters to an input image size of 640 × 640 pixels, a batch size of 16, a learning rate of 1e−4, and for 100 epochs. All other parameters were set to their default values.

Model performance was quantitatively evaluated using the Intersection over Union (IoU) metric and qualitatively evaluated via field inspections. The overall segmentation accuracy is reported as the Mean IoU, which is the average IoU score across all images in the validation dataset. All detected water bodies were considered as suspected habitats. Ground inspections on the suspected habitats were used to finally evaluate the model accuracy.

From the full dataset of 4416 JPG images acquired across all blocks, an initial subset of 220 images (≈ 5% of the full dataset) were randomly selected from block B for potential annotation. However, due to a high degree of forward and lateral overlap, the dataset contained many duplicate scenes. Furthermore, a vast majority of images contained no water bodies at all. Therefore, to avoid annotating redundant or irrelevant data, binary segmentation masks were created for images that captured the representative diversity of water-body types present in the study area. Furthermore, to control spatial redundancy and avoid data leakage, we employed a spatial screening to ensure non‑overlapping image footprints (Supplementary file 2). Using the CVAT platform (https://www.cvat.ai), binary segmentation masks were manually created for the 98 training and 5 validation images, marking relevant water bodies as the sole class and excluding non-breeding environments (e.g. sea or active aquaculture ponds). All masks were exported in PNG format as “Segmentation mask 1.1.”. Validation data was not used for backpropagation during the training process but were evaluated at the end of each epoch to monitor model performance. Training was conducted for a fixed number of 100 epochs, and final model weights were selected based on maximum validation IoU. Lastly, predictions from a held-out test set of 4,196 images (not used in training or validation) were used for field inspection. To improve the model’s generalization and robustness, a pipeline of augmentation techniques was applied using “keras_cv” to incorporate (1) random flipping which introduced variability by flipping images horizontally or vertically; (2) random brightness and contrast adjustments which altered pixel intensity to simulate diverse lighting conditions; (3) random translation and zooming which simulated drone camera shifts and varying focal lengths; (4) cropping, which ensured consistent input size of 640 × 640 pixels. Although our data was obtained from diverse environmental conditions, the augmentation enhanced further the model’s ability to perform well under diverse environmental conditions and perspectives. We generated two types of models, the RGB and Grayscale with data split as described earlier for training, validation and testing. All configurations were set to the same in the RGB and Grayscale models. The R package “magick” was used to convert RGB images to Grayscale securing original metadata information.

The training environment was configured to utilize high-performance computing resources. A Google Compute Engine backend (https://colab.research.google.com/) using L4 GPU runtime with Python 3 support ensured efficient model training. The system specifications included 22.5 GB of GPU RAM, 53 GB of system RAM, and a 112.6 GB disk space. All computations were completed with 100 Compute Units purchased at approximately USD 7,75. Key libraries, including “keras-cv”, were installed for model implementation. To ensure reproducibility, a random seed value (seed_value = 42) was set using Python’s “random”, “NumPy”, and “TensorFlow” libraries. The entire workflow, including the dataset and model outputs, was managed on Google Drive.

Collection of mosquito larvae and morphological identification

In the absence of baseline ecological data for Anopheles habitat distribution in the study area, this work intentionally adopted an inclusive detection strategy, mapping all water bodies as potentially suitable habitats. Field inspections then served as the discriminative step, separating merely aquatic features from biologically productive breeding sites. While effective as an initial surveillance framework, this approach inevitably generated more candidate sites than are operationally actionable. The detected water bodies using the RGB model were used for a series of field visits to perform ground-truthing. The objective of this validation was twofold: first, to confirm the physical presence of water, and second, to inspect for the presence of mosquito larvae. Given the large number of water masks generated by the model, a strategic sampling approach was necessary. Field inspections were prioritized for detections that met the following criteria: (1) Small or ambiguous features—predicted water bodies with a very small surface area, where the model’s prediction was less certain and not obviously a traditional water body and (2) Spectral responses – detections where the spectral signature suggested distinct water conditions, such as the presence of algae or moss, which can complicate automated segmentation. For selecting the sites, we loaded all the 4196 georeferenced predictions into QGIS. Most of these images contained overlapping predictions. Using the above explained criteria, a subset of a total of 50 representative locations was manually selected from overlapping predictions to support field inspection. The geographical coordinates of these points were digitized in QGIS as point vector shapefile and exported as a Keyhole Markup Language (KML) file. Afterwards, the KML file was imported into Google My Maps to create a practical navigation guide for efficient field tracking and data collection. (https://www.google.com/maps/d/). The map hosted on Google My Maps was then accessed using a smartphone via the Google Maps app, which allowed each point to be traced following the route suggested by Google Maps. Mosquito larvae were collected using a dipper with an extendable cable of about 2 m.

It is important to note that field activities were primarily designed to confirm water presence and collect mosquito larvae for taxonomic identification. Systematic documentation of environmental characteristics of each water body, such as water permanence, flow condition, proximity to human dwellings, vegetation structure, or organic matter was not conducted. As a result, although larval presence or absence was determined, the dataset does not support inferential modelling of habitat suitability or risk stratification in the present study.

Larval sampling was conducted in accordance with WHO guidelines. At each suspected water body, ten dips were taken using a standard larval dipper systematically distributed along accessible margins and where mosquito larvae are most likely to occur. This strategy aligns with established larval ecology by targeting productive microhabitats (margins and vegeted edges), improving detection of larval presence. Since most of residents communicate in a local language other than English, a guide and translator from the Pangandaran Public Health Laboratory (Labkesmas) were made available during the field visits. It should also be noted that in cases where the suspected area was within unattended private property, it was decided not to carry out the inspections in this case. The larvae collected in the field were stored in plastic bottles and then transported to Labkesmas for further identification. A senior Labkesmas expert carried out the morphological identification of the larvae to genus level and the emerging adults were identified to species level. The larvae that did not survive were not identified to species level. The framework applied in this study is presented in Fig. 3.

Results

Imagery and overlaps

The number of flight paths varied across the three experimental blocks, with 19, 17, and 13 paths for blocks A, B, and C, respectively. This variation in flight line density directly influenced the lateral image overlap between adjacent paths. The resulting image counts were 1838 for block A, 1448 for block B, and 1130 for block C (Supplementary File 1). The average width of the overlapping areas between images from consecutive flight paths (in the east-west direction) was 88 m for block A, 89 m for block B, and 73 m for block C (Supplementary File 3). Along the flight path (south-north direction), the camera trigger interval of five seconds at a flight speed of 2.5 m/s resulted in a consistent longitudinal spacing of approximately 12 m between consecutive image centres. Given that a single image covers a ground area of 150 × 108 m, this configuration ensured a substantial forward overlap, with each point on the ground being captured in a minimum of 12 consecutive images along the flight line.

Due to the varying weather conditions experienced during the flight campaigns, the captured images exhibit considerable variability in exposure, illumination, and shadow patterns (Fig. 4). This resulting diversity in spectral responses could contribute to improved model robustness by enabling the learning algorithm to generalize across a wider range of radiometric conditions, which can be beneficial for reliable water body detection under heterogeneous environmental settings.

Model predictions and accuracy assessment

Quantitatively, the RGB model achieved a high mean Intersection over Union (IoU) of 0.86, demonstrating strong overall segmentation accuracy. The Grayscale model also performed competently, with a mean IoU of 0.80, indicating that the deep learning approach remains effective even with minimal colour information, although with a measurable decrease in performance. However, these accuracy measures should be interpreted in the light of the limited data size, which nevertheless captured the dominant and recurrent water-body patterns in the study area. A qualitative analysis of predictions on five validation images revealed that the models generally localized water bodies effectively, with most predicted areas coinciding closely with the ground truth (GT) masks. However, this analysis also uncovered important nuances. In several instances, such as Spot 1 in Fig. 5A and Spot 2 in Fig. 5B, the model correctly identified genuine water bodies that had been omitted during manual annotation, highlighting the challenges and potential inconsistencies of manual annotation in complex landscapes. Conversely, Fig. 5C highlighted a key challenge with inconsistent predictions in homogeneous areas; the model correctly predicted Spot 3 over an active aquaculture pond but failed to detect a nearly identical pond at Location 4, indicating a sensitivity to subtle contextual or spectral variations. Further inconsistencies were observed in Fig. 5E, where the model fully predicted one GT mask at Spot 6 but only partially captured another, and correctly identified an unannotated water at Spot 7 while failing to detect a similar feature at Spot 8. These inconsistencies were similar on the Grayscale model (Fig. 6).

Field visits for water body and mosquito larvae inspection

A final field validation was conducted at 50 purposely mapped sites (Supplementary files 4 and 5), with 47 successfully inspected. The remaining three were inaccessible on private property. Of the 47 visited sites, 45 were confirmed as persistent water bodies, while two were transient puddles that form after heavy rainfall. Furthermore, 9 sites (4, 12, 14, 18, 19, 20, 23, 24, and 26) were originated from block B, which their prediction images contained overlapping features from train or validation data. These locations were intentionally included to ensure complete epidemiological coverage of Block B. Nonetheless, water bodies detections were 100% accurate in blocks A and C, validating the model’s ability to detect water bodies in unseen images, even with limited training and validation data. This confirms that all inspected predicted sites corresponded to actual water bodies during field visits. More importantly, these inspections were conducted to assess ecological relevance rather than to provide an independent evaluation of the model predictive accuracy. Mosquito larvae were found in 15 (31.9%) of the inspected sites, reflecting the expected distinction of between water presence and breeding suitability (Fig. 7). Taxonomic identification revealed that 10.6% of the positive sites contained Anopheles genus larvae, specifically the malaria vectors An. Vagus and An. Sundaicus, while 21.3% contained Culex genus larvae. A comprehensive breakdown of the larval findings is provided in Table 2.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the DeepLabV3 + model can effectively detect and accurately map water bodies from individually georeferenced, still RGB and Grayscale drone images. These findings validate that this method provides a sufficient basis for predicting, locating, and intervening in mosquito breeding sites, offering an alternative to multispectral and orthomosaic-based approaches.

Water bodies segmentation accuracy with RGB imagery in deep learning

Our study demonstrates that the DeepLabV3 + architecture is highly effective for detecting water bodies in drone imagery, achieving robust performance even when using standard RGB data under challenging conditions. This capability stems from the model’s core components: convolutional layers that excel at identifying fundamental spatial patterns, and the atrous spatial pyramid pooling module that captures multi-scale contextual information29. Crucially, this foundational strength in pattern recognition also explains the model’s notable performance with grayscale imagery. Notably, the model’s strong performance on grayscale imagery provides direct evidence that segmentation relies primarily on learned spatial structure, texture, and shape cues rather than colour information alone. The model leverages visual cues (surface homogeneity, edge continuity) that also correspond to ecological characteristics of mosquito‑utilisable aquatic habitats. It detects the characteristically smooth, homogeneous texture of water surfaces, which strongly contrasts with the complex textures of vegetation, soil, or cocreate. Furthermore, it leverages the consistent tonal signature of water, which often appears as a contiguous dark region due to light absorption. Finally, the model discerns the cohesive geometrical shape of water bodies, such as the connected, curvilinear form of a river or a spatially contiguous pond feature, which binds these textural and tonal features into a coherent object. By relying on these fundamental visual cues, textural contrasts, brightness, and geometrical shapes, the model maintains significant accuracy even when color information is reduced, a finding consistent with other studies that have successfully used such features for water classification23,30.

This finding demonstrates that robust water body detection can be achieved using standard RGB imagery with off-the-shelf deep learning architectures, eliminating the operational dependency on costly multispectral or infrared sensors. This approach presents an alternative solution to conventional methods, which often rely on multispectral data and computationally intensive orthomosaic processing14,18. For the purpose of purely localizing breeding sites, our methodology simplifies the entire pipeline, from data acquisition to analysis. By utilizing affordable, consumer-grade drones and operating with simple, linear flight paths, our approach has a potential cost benefit because as eases the use of photogrammetry software which demands strong processing power to generate orthomosaics. This makes sophisticated larval habitat mapping accessible to local health authorities in resource-limited settings. Our method, however, is less applicable in contexts demanding high planimetric or altimetric precision, an area where orthomosaics excel.

While the model showed high proficiency with the Pangandaran landscape—characterized by high inter-image similarity, some inconsistencies were observed, particularly in segmenting shallow or turbid water. We attribute these errors primarily to a limited training dataset and a lack of extensive hyperparameter optimization. From a public‑health perspective, the primary value of this workflow lies in enabling rapid identification of aquatic habitats that warrant ecological inspection and intervention. Future work should expand training data across contrasting landscapes, as model robustness and generalization beyond Pangandaran cannot be assumed without additional representative image samples.

Computational resources

Furthermore, the reliance on cloud computing platforms, specifically Google Colab, was instrumental in overcoming the barrier of high computational costs. This approach allowed all model development and data processing to be executed on a powerful remote backend. Crucially, this backend was accessed through a standard notebook, a MacBook Pro with an Intel Core i5 processor and 8GB of RAM, which functioned solely as a terminal. By demonstrating that such advanced deep learning is feasible without expensive local hardware or specialized software, this strategy democratizes access to these capabilities. It presents a scalable and affordable solution for health authorities in resource-limited settings, effectively reducing the financial and technical barriers to implementing drone-based mosquito surveillance31. This cloud-based paradigm significantly enhances the feasibility and potential impact of such technologies in the global fight against malaria.

From suspected water bodies to confirmed mosquito breeding sites

This study successfully bridged the gap between remote detection and on-the-ground validation, confirming that all inspected sites predicted by the RGB model were genuine water bodies. This integration enables rapid localization of aquatic habitats, while field inspection remains essential to distinguish productive breeding sites from non-productive water bodies. Although image inference and georeferencing are fully automated, consolidation of overlapping predictions required manual intervention in this study, and therefore the workflow should be considered semi‑automated at the deployment stage. It should be noted that while the flight plan was designed for full coverage (as shown in Supplementary files 1 and 3), the study design prioritized verification of predicted water bodies and did not explicitly quantify the occurrence of undetected sites (false negatives). However, false negatives (under-detected water bodies) were not assessed, limiting estimation of detection sensitivity and to estimate the proportion of water bodies that may have remained unreported. From an epidemiological perspective, the absence of false‑negative assessment represents a more critical operational gap than false positives. As this workflow is intended as a first‑pass environmental screening tool, high recall (sensitivity) is prioritised to minimise the risk of missing viable breeding habitats. In practice, false positives can be mitigated through rapid visual review of model outputs against the original drone imagery before dispatching field teams, whereas false negatives incur no such safeguard and result in undetected and untreated habitats. Accordingly, the proposed workflow is designed as a human‑in‑the‑loop system, where automated detections support analyst triage and prioritisation prior to targeted field inspection rather than fully autonomous deployment. Future work should therefore incorporate systematic ground surveys or independent reference datasets to evaluate missed detections and provide a more comprehensive assessment of model performance across both precision and recall dimensions.

The entomological findings highlight that only specific aquatic environments among those detected contribute to malaria transmission risk. Larval detections reflected edge‑associated microhabitats consistent with known mosquito ecology, although detailed microhabitat characterisation was beyond the scope of this study32. A preliminary observation from this limited dataset, indicated that mosquito larvae were more frequently encountered in water bodies with floating moss. The ecological mechanism behind this association, however, remains unclear and warrants further investigation. The specific habitat profile of Anopheles in our study area supports a targeted intervention strategy. Previous research in Pangandaran has suggested that distributing fish seeds to ponds where Anopheles larvae are present is an effective control measure2,33. Our findings strongly support this approach, as no mosquito larvae were detected in the active fish farming ponds inspected during this study. Utilizing fish as biological control agents presents a sustainable and environmentally friendly method for larval source management, directly leveraging the ecological dynamics of the local environment to reduce vector populations. The relatively low larval counts observed in Table 2 should be interpreted in light of the sampling strategy employed. This study adopted a systematic dipping approach to ensure spatial consistency and reproducibility across sampling sites. However, unlike purposive dipping, which targets visually favourable micro-habitats such as shaded margins or areas with visible organic matter, systematic sampling may underestimate larval abundance by including less productive micro-sites34,35. As a result, larval densities reported here likely represent conservative estimates rather than true population maxima. Future studies could benefit from integrating purposive and systematic sampling approaches to better capture both spatial representativeness and peak larval productivity. Inferential statistical modelling (e.g., regression analysis) was not applied to these observations due to limited sample size, low larval counts, purposive site selection, and the absence of systematically recorded environmental covariates. Under these conditions, formal modelling would lack statistical power and risk spurious inference. Consequently, ecological observations reported here should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis‑generating. The inability to record detailed habitat characteristics during field inspections limited further stratification of breeding risk. Future studies should systematically register environmental and spatial attributes of every inspected water body to support the development of inferential or machine‑learning models capable of categorising habitats into high, medium, and low breeding‑risk classes. Such risk‑based filtering would allow vector control programmes to prioritise intervention sites more efficiently, shifting the workflow from comprehensive detection toward targeted surveillance.

Potential use of still RGB images as alternative to orthomosaics for vector control purposes

Operationally, the workflow proceeds from drone image acquisition, to per‑image georeferencing, deep‑learning‑based water detection, and direct navigation to predicted sites using standard mapping applications like Google Maps. This pipeline proved to being valid alternative to orthomosaics for localizing potential larval habitats in vector control contexts. Open‑source platforms such as WebODM have substantially improved access to drone‑based photogrammetry, allowing users to generate orthomosaics and extract spectral indices from RGB imagery alone. While these tools reduce licensing costs over commercial softwares, they do not eliminate the fundamental dependence on Structure‑from‑Motion pipelines, dense image overlap, and orthorectification. In contrast, the workflow proposed in this study deliberately avoids photogrammetric reconstruction altogether. By operating directly on still images and leveraging embedded GPS metadata solely for localisation purposes, our approach removes the most time‑consuming and computationally expensive stage of drone data processing36,37. By georeferencing still images directly and individually, we eliminate the lengthy stitching and post-processing steps, significantly reducing the lag between data collection and intervention. Our approach is critical for operational malaria control, where the primary objective is not the production of spatially continuous, metrically precise maps, but the rapid identification and navigation to potential larval habitats for inspection and intervention. Nevertheless, this simplified approach entails several limitations. Because images are georeferenced using a single pair of geographic coordinates without additional photogrammetric corrections, geometric distortions related to camera perspective, lens effects, and terrain relief may persist, particularly toward the edges of each image. Such distortions can introduce localized spatial offsets between predicted and true water-body locations, which may affect navigational precision but remain acceptable for rapid field localization. These effects are expected to be more pronounced in areas with greater topographic variability or when operating at lower flight altitudes. Furthermore, the absence of multi-image alignment and orthorectification precludes correction of cumulative positional errors that are typically mitigated through Structure-from-Motion workflows. As a result, while the proposed method is adequate for rapid localisation and field navigation, it is not intended for applications requiring precise planimetric or altimetric measurements, detailed spatial analyses, or accurate area and distance estimations, for which orthomosaic-based products remain the more appropriate option.

An under-appreciated operational advantage of using individual images emerged in densely vegetated areas. The model predicted small water patches that, upon field inspection, were revealed to be larger water bodies concealed beneath the forest canopy. This was possible because the drone captured multiple images of the same area from different angles during its flight, increasing the likelihood of a clear line of sight through the vegetation in at least one frame. The multi-perspective inspection is a general capability of drone-based workflows, as overlapping imagery enables feature detection from multiple viewing angles, either through direct examination of raw images or through three-dimensional products generated by SfM processing. Our field observations underscore the importance of actively leveraging these multi‑angle perspectives, regardless of processing pipeline, when conducting mosquito habitat surveillance, as partially occluded or overhung water bodies may be visible in oblique views but not in single nadir representations. We therefore recommend that practitioners using any drone‑based workflow explicitly examine raw image sequences or 3D products to enhance detection of concealed aquatic habitats. It is important to note, however, that our method is best suited for detection and localization tasks where precise planimetric or altimetric measurements are not required, and its accuracy may be compromised in steep terrain where significant elevation variations occur within a single image’s footprint.

Limitations and future directions

While the proposed method shows strong potential for rapid vector surveillance, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, spatial positioning relied exclusively on on‑board GPS metadata without photogrammetric correction or ground control points, resulting in horizontal positional uncertainty on the order of approximately 2–5 m under favourable conditions, and potentially exceeding 10 m in less controlled settings. This level of accuracy is adequate for rapid field localisation but constrains applicability in complex terrain with pronounced elevation gradients or in applications requiring precise planimetric or altimetric measurements. In topographically diverse regions, maintaining localization accuracy would require altitude-adaptive footprint estimation that accounts for variations in terrain elevation and flight altitude rather than a fixed image ground coverage.

Second, because individual images are georeferenced independently, the method is not suitable for high‑precision mapping tasks such as accurate boundary delineation, area estimation, volumetric analysis, or detailed spatial modelling. In such cases, orthomosaic‑based workflows derived from Structure‑from‑Motion processing remain more appropriate. Relatedly, spatial distortions induced by perspective effects, terrain relief, and platform motion are not corrected through multi‑view optimisation.

Third, flight planning was constrained by the limited programmability of the native drone control software, which restricted systematic grid design and fixed inter‑track spacing. Although this did not prevent effective water detection in the present study, it may influence image overlap, coverage uniformity, and detection consistency in other operational contexts.

Fourth, the annotated training dataset was relatively small, and although it captured the dominant and recurrent water‑body types in the Pangandaran landscape, detection inconsistencies were observed, particularly for shallow or turbid water bodies. Model generalisation beyond this environmental setting cannot be assumed and would require additional representative training data from contrasting ecological and geomorphological contexts. Also, the limited number of inspected sites and conservative larval sampling further precluded robust inferential statistical modelling of environmental predictors of larval presence.

Fifth, although the workflow is intended to maximise recall as a first‑pass screening tool, the evaluation framework did not assess false negatives, preventing estimation of detection sensitivity and representing a key operational limitation. As a result, detection sensitivity could not be quantified, limiting full operational performance assessment. Future work should incorporate systematic ground surveys or independent reference datasets to enable balanced evaluation across both precision and recall.

Finally, the workflow intentionally avoided reliance on ancillary geospatial datasets (e.g., land‑use/land‑cover, hydrography, surface‑water layers, or proximity to settlements) in order to preserve minimal data requirements and operational simplicity. While this design choice enhances deployability in data‑poor settings, integrating secondary geospatial information could further reduce false positives and enable risk‑based prioritisation of breeding habitats. Such integration represents an important direction for future research aimed at improving decision‑making efficiency in resource‑limited vector control programs.

Based on the findings and limitations identified in this study, several recommendations emerge for improving operational applicability. Future work should prioritise expansion and diversification of the training dataset across contrasting ecological and geomorphological settings to enhance model robustness and generalisability. Incorporating environmental and spatial covariates, such as land‑use/land‑cover, hydrography, vegetation structure, and proximity to human settlements, would enable risk‑based classification of water bodies rather than binary water detection alone. Evaluation frameworks should be strengthened by systematically assessing false negatives in addition to confirmed detections, allowing balanced estimation of both precision and recall. Deeper integration with existing geospatial datasets could further reduce false positives and improve contextual decision‑making. Resolution of overlapping predictions could be addressed in future iterations through computational aggregation methods. Finally, closer coupling of deep learning outputs with ecological interpretation and public‑health objectives would strengthen the interdisciplinary relevance of drone‑based surveillance for practical larval source management.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and validated a semi-automated workflow for identifying mosquito breeding sites using drone-captured RGB imagery. We demonstrated that rapid water body detection, when coupled with targeted ecological inspection can support actionable larval source management. The core methodological innovation lies not merely in bypassing orthomosaic creation, but in reframing drone analysis around a minimal‑input, localisation‑focused workflow that is explicitly optimised for rapid vector‑control decision‑making. Field inspections demonstrated that only a subset of detected water bodies constituted active mosquito breeding sites. The entire process, from image analysis to field validation, was designed for accessibility, utilizing consumer-grade drones and cloud computing, thus democratizing advanced deep learning capabilities for regions with limited resources. While the approach may be less suited for steep terrain and currently identifies water generically rather than specifically targeting breeding sites, it represents a significant leap forward for larval source management in endemic areas with flat topography. By shortening the timeline between surveillance and intervention, this method provides a practical and scalable tool for health authorities. Future work will focus on enriching the model with contextual and secondary geospatial data to distinguish high-risk breeding habitats and on conducting larger-scale validation to fully assess sensitivity and specificity. By design, the current workflow prioritises exhaustive detection followed by field‑based discrimination. Incorporating risk‑based habitat classification represents a logical and necessary next step for improving operational efficiency in resource‑limited vector control programmes.
