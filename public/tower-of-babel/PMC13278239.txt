Clinic-based Treatment of Type B Ulnar Polydactyly Saves Costs Compared With Treatment in the Operating Room
Authors: Nikhi P. Singh, EJun Yun, Khoa Tran, Craig Brooks, Joshua M. Adkinson, Gregory H. Borschel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:One in 500 children in the United States is born with polydactyly. Type B ulnar polydactyly excision is often performed in the operating room (OR) but can also be conducted in an office setting. This study compares costs for patients/families and healthcare systems between these settings.Methods:A retrospective review from January 2019 to December 2023 included patients who underwent type B ulnar polydactyly excision. Patients were categorized into in-office or OR groups. Patient demographics and financial data were analyzed using independent-samples t tests, χ2 tests, and analysis of variance (significance: P < 0.05).Results:Of 114 patients, 64 underwent in-office excision and 50 underwent OR excision. Total charges to the payer were significantly lower for in-office excision compared with OR ($2130 versus $10,731; P < 0.001). The average cost per digit removed was also less for in-office procedures ($1218 versus $5937; P < 0.001). Health system costs were lower for in-office excision overall ($845 versus $2349; P < 0.001) and per digit ($472 versus $1272; P < 0.001).Conclusions:In-office excision of type B ulnar polydactyly significantly reduces costs for patients, families, and healthcare systems. Shifting care to the office can alleviate OR demand and decrease financial burdens. These cost differences should inform care planning and resource allocation.

Takeaways

Question: Can in-office excision of type B ulnar polydactyly reduce healthcare costs compared to operating room (OR) excision without compromising care?

Findings: In this retrospective analysis of 114 patients, in-office excision was significantly less expensive than OR-based excision across all cost categories. Total charges to payers ($2130 versus $10,731) and health system costs ($845 versus $2349) were markedly lower (P < 0.001), with no difference in operating margin.

Meaning: In-office excision is a safe, efficient, and substantially more cost-effective approach that can reduce financial burden and OR demand, supporting its broader adoption for minor procedures.

INTRODUCTION

Polydactyly is a common congenital anomaly.1 Temtamy and McKusick2 define type A as well-developed, functional digits with a bony connection, whereas type B are underdeveloped, nonfunctional digits connected by skin and a neurovascular bundle. Within the United States, type B ulnar polydactyly is more common than type A, with an incidence of 1 in 1500 to 3000 births among White populations and 1 in 100 to 300 births among African American populations.3,4 In African American children, ulnar polydactyly is often isolated, inherited in an autosomal dominant fashion, and presents bilaterally in 70% of cases.4

Most patients are referred in infancy for treatment. However, there is a lack of consensus recommendations in the literature regarding age-specific polydactyly management. As such, polydactyly is managed at various ages depending on the type of polydactyly and the preferences of the physician and the patient’s family/caregivers.1 Uncorrected polydactyly may lead to functional impairment in addition to psychosocial effects resulting from cosmetic differences compared with peers.5,6 Treatment of polydactyly involves removal of the accessory digit, and type B digits can be managed with either excision or suture ligation.1

Traditionally, surgical excision is performed in the operating room (OR) under anesthesia.7 However, in-office excision has demonstrated safety and efficacy.1,8,9 As healthcare systems face increasing cost pressures and OR constraints, identifying safe, lower-cost alternatives for minor procedures is essential for improving efficiency and resource allocation. Here, we compare the costs of in-office excision with those of excision in the OR. We hypothesize that in-office excision of type B ulnar polydactyly reduces costs for families and the healthcare system.

METHODS

This institutional review board–approved retrospective review was performed for all patients treated for type B ulnar polydactyly. Patients who underwent procedures between January 2019 and December 2023 at Riley Children’s Hospital were identified using current procedural terminology codes 11200, 26587, and 64774.

Demographic information, including age, gender, race, and insurance status, and billing and financial data were obtained from Indiana University Hospital financial databases. The number of digits removed per procedure was obtained from chart review. Patients were stratified into those who underwent excisions performed in the OR (OR group) or those who underwent excisions performed in the office (office group). Inclusion criteria constituted procedures performed in either ORs or offices that only involved excisions of type B polydactyly. Exclusion criteria included procedures that involved any surgery other than excision of type B ulnar polydactyly and procedures for patients aged 18 years or older at the time of excision. Procedures for patients who underwent additional surgery at the time of polydactyly excision were excluded. Average total charge to patients, average charge per digit removed per visit, and average operating margin per visit were the primary outcomes. Secondary outcomes included average reimbursement, average cost to hospitals, and insurance classes of patients. System costs reflected institutional cost accounting data and included procedural staffing, supplies, and associated overhead. Of note, this analysis does not include facility fees, as facility fee data were not available for the in-office group.

Statistical analysis was performed using independent-samples t tests and analysis of variance for quantitative measures and χ2 test for categorical variable analysis. Post hoc analysis was performed with Tukey test and the Bonferroni correction method. Analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel (Seattle, WA) and IBM SPSS Statistics (Armonk, NY). Statistical significance was determined as a P value of less than 0.05.

Office Procedures

Patients fasted for 3 hours before the procedure. Parents held the patient in their lap while the clinic team prepared and draped the patient’s hand(s), and 1 mL of bupivacaine 0.25% with epinephrine 1:200,000 was injected. Patients were distracted at the time of anesthetic injection by concurrently feeding them. A scalpel was used to incise the skin, and thermal cauterization was used to ligate the neurovascular bundle, which was allowed to retract under the skin after cauterization. Rapid-absorbing 4-0 Vicryl Rapide (Ethicon) sutures were used to close the wound, and Dermabond (Johnson & Johnson) was applied.

Operating Room

Patients fasted at midnight before the procedure. Induction of monitored anesthesia care or general anesthesia was determined by consensus between the surgeon and anesthesiologist. Patients were prepared and draped in the usual fashion. Local anesthetic injection and excision were performed in a similar manner as office procedures, except that electrocautery was used instead of thermal cautery.

RESULTS

Of the 114 patients who underwent excision, 64 were treated in-office, and 50 in the OR. Patient demographics are indicated in Table 1. Those who underwent in-office excision were treated at an earlier age (1.5 ± 0.6 y; median, 1 y) compared with those treated in the OR (7.62 ± 2.3 y; median, 7 y) (P < 0.001). A difference existed between the groups of payment methods (Fig. 1, Table 2, P = 0.01). There was no difference in the number of digits removed between in-office and OR cohorts (P = 0.12).

Overall, across all cost categories, in-office excision costs were less than excision in the OR. On average, total charges to payers were less for the in-office group compared with the OR group ($2130 versus $10,731; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Total cost to the health system was lower for in-office excision compared with OR excision ($845 versus $2349; P < 0.001). Health system reimbursement was higher for the OR group compared with the in-office group ($1714 versus $777; P = 0.03). There was no difference in operating margin between groups (P = 0.29) (Table 2).

Cost comparison of total and per-digit charge between in-office and OR excision. Error bars represent the SD for each subgroup.

Similar outcomes were seen when stratified by per-digit charge, as 207 digits were excised across the 114 patients. Average charge to payers per digit removed in the in-office group was less than that of the OR group ($1218 versus $5937; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Total cost to the health system per digit was lower for the in-office group compared with the OR group ($472 versus $1272; P < 0.001). Health system reimbursement was higher in total and per digit for OR excision compared with in-office excision (total: $1714 versus $777; P = 0.03) (per digit: $964 versus $426; P = 0.03). When compared on a per-digit charge, there was no difference in operating margin between groups (P = 0.38) (Table 2).

When stratified by payer type (commercial, Medicaid, uninsured, or self-pay), there were no differences in total or per-digit charge, cost, reimbursement, or operating margin for the in-office group. No difference in total or per-digit charge or cost was observed for the OR cohort. Differences within the OR group were observed by payer type for total and per-digit reimbursement and operating margin (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

We observed that in-office excision of type B ulnar polydactyly greatly decreases costs across multiple categories compared with OR excision. This practice reduces charges to patients and their families, as well as costs incurred by the healthcare system. The total and per-digit charge, cost, and reimbursement were all lower when in-office excision was performed. There was no difference observed in operating margin, which may be due to a complex financial interplay that is beyond the scope of this article. Charge to payers per digit was 503% higher in the OR than in an in-office setting. Cost to the healthcare system per digit was 278% higher in the OR. Reimbursement was greater for OR excisions ($538 per digit). This heightened reimbursement is likely secondary to performing excision in the more complex OR setting described herein.

Excision of polydactylous digits in an office setting likely reduces cost through substitution of the OR, which typically has higher usage cost than a clinic setting. This also decreases staffing costs, as personnel such as anesthesiologists, anesthesia assistants, circulating nurses, scrub nurses, turnover services, and OR administration are not needed for an office excision.10,11 Additionally, equipment costs are decreased, as full surgical trays are not used, standard operating sterility and draping protocols are not used, and the depreciation of OR equipment is a nonfactor. A detailed analysis of such factors may reveal marked differences in certain care categories; however, this may vary between institutions, and such institutions may not share such financial information. Other specialties, such as otolaryngology and vascular surgery, have pioneered in-office procedures for procedures traditionally performed in the OR.12,13 Importantly, selection of procedural setting should occur within a shared decision-making framework that incorporates clinical judgment, family preferences, and institutional policies.

It is also notable that the in-office excisions were performed in a timelier manner compared with the excisions done in the OR. The exact times involved in each surgery were not compared. However, the in-office excisions took a similar amount of time to excisions done in the OR, not including OR turnover amount of time. Furthermore, the starve-feed method with concurrent local anesthesia was always performed successfully, with no patients unable to complete the in-office procedure and therefore requiring subsequent OR scheduling.

Additionally, decreased OR utilization may enable greater OR use for other cases. Such additional cases may be more complex and lengthier, thereby producing a more favorable economic balance for the institution. At the very least, we suspect there is an increase in OR volume with a subsequent decrease in the time from scheduling a case to performing the operation. This may result in increased patient retention and satisfaction, which may increase patients’ and families’ perceptions of a healthcare system. Doing so could increase further use of such a healthcare system for future care. Hospital leaders should take note of this potential positive “snowball” effect. This could be translated to other procedures and other surgical specialties in which an in-office procedure is possible but traditionally performed in the OR (Fig. 3).

It is notable that patients who underwent excision in the OR were older than those who underwent in-office excision. This was likely attributable to a generalized age cutoff for the in-office group to allow for successful distraction with feeding. Additionally, the OR offers a more controlled environment when dissecting neurovascular bundles in an older patient. Distraction feeding has been previously described in newborn circumcision and childhood vaccination.14,15

A difference was noted in the payer distribution between in-office and OR cohorts, possibly explained by the lower cost incurred by those undergoing in-office excision. Understandably, the 1 patient who was self-pay underwent excision in the office. If they were to undergo excision in the OR, our analysis indicates a charge of $4719 more per digit.

Interestingly, when patients who underwent excision in the OR were stratified by payer, those with commercial insurance had a higher total and per-digit reimbursement than those with Medicaid and those without insurance. This also translated into operating margin, as those with commercial insurance had a net positive operating margin and those without insurance had a net negative operating margin. Table 3 demonstrates the differences between groups. Although those with commercial insurance produce a positive operating margin, it is critical not to discriminate in the treatment of patients based of financial means. As such, it may be beneficial to adopt a one-size-fits-all approach, as in-office excision reduces costs.

This study has limitations, including its retrospective design and being conducted at a single center. Additionally, analyses performed at the digit level may violate assumptions of independence when multiple digits are treated in the same patient. A limitation of this study includes inaccuracies in the realized payment amounts that patients incur. It is well known that charges do not equate to patient costs, and this is dependent on complex and multifactorial financial considerations. Thus, the charges documented in this study are not indicative of true costs to patients. Additionally, we did not measure patient outcomes in this study, as excision of type B polydactyly is a low-risk procedure. Future studies may examine such outcomes in addition to patient/family satisfaction of in-office excision compared with OR excision. There was a significant age difference between the office and OR cohorts, which may represent an important confounder. Younger age likely facilitated office-based excision through the use of distraction and feeding techniques, whereas older patients more often required OR-based anesthesia. As such, differences in age, anesthetic approach, and procedural setting are inherently interrelated and may contribute to observed cost differences independent of treatment location. Facility fees were not included in the analysis because these data were unavailable for the in-office cohort, which may limit full characterization of system-level costs. Although parametric analyses were used, cost data may be skewed, which represents a limitation and may be better addressed with nonparametric approaches in larger future studies.

CONCLUSIONS

Excision of type B ulnar polydactyly in an office setting results in lower charges to payers and lower costs to the healthcare system compared with traditional excision in the OR. Using the OR for cases other than type B polydactyly excision may serve to further increase hospital system revenue.
