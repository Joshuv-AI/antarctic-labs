Ectopic ACTH syndrome caused by pheochromocytoma: diagnostic challenges and surgical outcomes in two cases
Authors: Zexuan Lv, Yi Wang, Chuan Zhang, Yansheng Xu, Yubo Zhao, Xiyou Wang, Weihao Chen, Yipeng Jin, Zhuomin Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEctopic adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) syndrome is a rare form of ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome, most commonly caused by neuroendocrine tumors. Pheochromocytoma is a rare source of ectopic ACTH secretion. Its clinical presentation is often heterogeneous and may lack the typical features of Cushing syndrome, which can lead to delayed diagnosis or misdiagnosis. Increased awareness of this rare entity is essential for early diagnosis and appropriate management.Case presentationWe report two cases of ectopic ACTH syndrome caused by pheochromocytoma. Both patients presented with refractory hypertension and hypokalemia, and one patient developed symptoms during pregnancy with atypical clinical manifestations. Imaging studies demonstrated adrenal masses, while pituitary imaging and investigations for other potential ectopic ACTH sources were unremarkable. Both patients underwent robot-assisted laparoscopic adrenalectomy. Introduction

Ectopic adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) syndrome is an uncommon cause of ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome, accounting for approximately 10%–20% of all cases. The most frequent sources include pulmonary neuroendocrine tumors, thymic tumors, and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors [1, 2].

Pheochromocytoma is a neuroendocrine tumor originating from the adrenal medulla and is classically characterized by paroxysmal hypertension, headache, palpitations, and diaphoresis. However, ACTH secretion by pheochromocytoma leading to ectopic ACTH syndrome is exceedingly rare [3]. Patients with this condition often exhibit simultaneous hypercortisolism and catecholamine excess, resulting in a complex and heterogeneous clinical presentation that may mimic primary hypertension or other endocrine disorders, thereby increasing the risk of misdiagnosis [4].

Ectopic ACTH syndrome caused by adrenal pheochromocytoma is exceptionally rare. In this report, we describe two cases of ectopic ACTH syndrome secondary to pheochromocytoma, both of which presented with atypical clinical features, including one case with disease onset during pregnancy. Through systematic endocrine evaluation, precise imaging localization, and pathological confirmation, the diagnoses were established, and both patients achieved favorable endocrinological and clinical remission following surgical tumor resection. This report aims to enhance clinicians’ awareness of this rare entity in order to reduce misdiagnosis and optimize diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Case report

Case 1

A 53-year-old woman of Han ethnicity (height, 163 cm; weight, 55 kg; body mass index [BMI], 20.7 kg/m²) was admitted in October 2025 with a 4-month history of facial and lower-extremity edema and hypokalemia identified one month prior to admission. In June 2025, she developed numbness of the head without an apparent precipitating factor. By late August 2025, she noticed progressive facial hyperpigmentation and recurrent episodes of paroxysmal limb weakness without overt paralysis. These episodes were accompanied by a single episode of paroxysmal hypertension, as well as diaphoresis and hand tremors. She was evaluated at a local hospital, where hypokalemia was detected, with a lowest serum potassium level of 2.87 mmol/L. Arterial blood gas analysis revealed metabolic alkalosis (pH 7.56).

Initial endocrine testing suggested ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism. Physical examination revealed nailfold hemorrhages in both hands, scattered petechiae over the abdomen and lower extremities, and pitting edema of the lower limbs. No typical cushingoid features, such as central obesity, moon face, buffalo hump, or violaceous striae, were observed.

Laboratory examinations

Endocrine evaluation revealed a markedly elevated ACTH level with loss of diurnal variation (08:00, 272.0 pg/mL; 16:00, 277.0 pg/mL; 00:00, 251.0 pg/mL; reference range, 7.2–63.3 pg/mL) and a significantly increased 24-hour urinary free cortisol level (2314.8 µg/24 h [6386.4 nmol/24 h]; reference range, 10–55 µg/24 h [27.6–151.7 nmol/24 h]). Serum cortisol levels were markedly elevated with loss of the normal circadian rhythm (08:00, 227.5 µg/dL [6275.9 nmol/L]; 16:00, 197.9 µg/dL [5458.9 nmol/L]; 00:00, 171.6 µg/dL [4734.6 nmol/L]). Outside-hospital endocrine records documented lack of cortisol suppression after dexamethasone administration. Serum cortisol remained above the upper reportable limit before and after dexamethasone testing: baseline, > 75 µg/dL (> 2069 nmol/L); after low-dose dexamethasone, > 75 µg/dL (> 2069 nmol/L); and after high-dose dexamethasone, > 75 µg/dL (> 2069 nmol/L). The 24-hour urinary free cortisol level was markedly elevated at baseline, 2314.8 µg/24 h, and remained markedly elevated after low-dose dexamethasone, 1768.5 µg/24 h. Because the serum cortisol values exceeded the upper reportable range, the exact percentage of suppression could not be calculated; however, the available results were consistent with absent suppression. Dexamethasone suppression tests were not repeated at our institution. Persistent hypokalemia was observed, with a poor response to potassium supplementation.

Catecholamine-related examinations demonstrated pronounced abnormalities, including elevated plasma norepinephrine (624.2 pg/mL; reference < 95 pg/mL), plasma epinephrine (430.5 pg/mL; reference < 62 pg/mL), plasma dopamine (294.1 pg/mL; reference < 20 pg/mL), and plasma normetanephrine (303.4 pg/mL; reference < 145 pg/mL). In addition, 24-hour urinary free norepinephrine was elevated to 182.2 µg/24 h (reference 0–90 µg/24 h), and urinary free epinephrine was elevated to 173.4 µg/24 h (reference 0–20 µg/24 h). Arterial blood gas analysis indicated metabolic alkalosis.

Imaging studies

Contrast-enhanced adrenal computed tomography (CT) (Fig. 1) revealed an irregular nodular enlargement of the left adrenal gland, measuring approximately 3.2 × 2.5 cm at its maximum dimensions. The lesion showed a non-contrast attenuation of approximately 36 Hounsfield units (HU) and demonstrated moderate enhancement on contrast-enhanced imaging. The right adrenal gland was mildly enlarged, with a small round hypodense lesion measuring approximately 0.6 × 0.3 cm in the medial limb.

Preoperative contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the adrenal gland in Case 1. Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT revealed a well-defined mass in the left adrenal region (red arrows), measuring approximately 36.6 mm × 25.6 mm in maximum dimensions, consistent with an adrenal-origin tumor

68Ga-DOTATATE positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) demonstrated high somatostatin receptor expression in the left adrenal lesion. ^68Ga-Pentixafor PET-CT revealed marked CXCR4 receptor expression in bilateral adrenal lesions. Contrast-enhanced pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) did not identify a definitive ACTH-secreting adenoma; a Rathke’s cleft cyst in the posterior pituitary was considered.

Genetic testing was not performed in either case, as the patients and their families declined further testing because of financial constraints.

Surgical procedure and postoperative pathology

Based on the above findings, a preliminary diagnosis of ectopic ACTH syndrome originating from the adrenal gland was made, with a strong suspicion of pheochromocytoma. The patient underwent robot-assisted laparoscopic left adrenal tumor resection under general anesthesia on November 3, 2025. Intraoperatively, a tumor was identified in the left adrenal region, measuring approximately 3 × 3 cm, with dense adhesion to surrounding tissues. The tumor and adrenal tissue were completely excised. The patient’s vital signs remained stable throughout the procedure, and intraoperative blood loss was approximately 100 mL.

Perioperative glucocorticoid coverage was provided with intravenous hydrocortisone 200 mg on the day of surgery. On postoperative day 1, intravenous hydrocortisone was reduced to 100 mg, followed by gradual tapering according to the patient’s clinical symptoms and serial hormone levels. The regimen was subsequently transitioned to oral hydrocortisone, and the dose had been reduced to 10 mg daily by the time of discharge. The patient was discharged with continued glucocorticoid replacement therapy.

Postoperative pathology revealed a left adrenal pheochromocytoma measuring approximately 3.5 × 3.3 × 2.5 cm, with focal hemorrhage. No tumor necrosis or adrenal capsular invasion was identified, and only occasional mitotic figures were observed. Immunohistochemical staining showed positivity for chromogranin A (CgA), synaptophysin (Syn), CD56, and GATA3, weak positivity for SDHB, and negativity for CKpan, Vimentin, S100, CD10, Inhibin-α, Melan-A, and PAX-8. P53 was weakly positive in occasional cells, and the Ki-67 labeling index was approximately 2%. Additional ACTH immunohistochemistry showed partial positivity in tumor cells, supporting the diagnosis of an ACTH-secreting pheochromocytoma.

Postoperative follow-up

Early postoperative endocrine reassessment showed marked biochemical improvement after unilateral adrenalectomy. ACTH decreased from 272.0 pg/mL preoperatively to 40.2 pg/mL on postoperative day 1. On postoperative day 3, the 08:00 ACTH level was 40.4 pg/mL, whereas the serum cortisol level was 11.98 µg/dL [330.5 nmol/L] while the patient was still receiving perioperative hydrocortisone replacement. Therefore, this postoperative cortisol value was interpreted cautiously and was not used as evidence of persistent endogenous hypercortisolism. On postoperative day 8, the 08:00 ACTH level remained within the normal range at 59.9 pg/mL. Serum potassium gradually normalized and reached 3.86 mmol/L during postoperative follow-up. Follow-up abdominal CT (Fig. 2) showed expected postoperative changes in the left adrenal region, while the contralateral right adrenal enlargement and medial low-density nodule were similar to the preoperative CT findings without obvious progression. Clinically, the patient’s symptoms improved substantially compared with the preoperative status.

Follow-up computed tomography (CT) one month after surgery in Case 1. Follow-up abdominal CT performed one month after surgery showed no residual mass or recurrent lesion in the left adrenal region, indicating complete tumor resection

Case 2

A 26-year-old woman of Han ethnicity (height, 157 cm; weight, 65 kg; body mass index [BMI], 26.4 kg/m²) was admitted in October 2023 with a 1-year history of elevated blood pressure and a 1-month history of a left adrenal mass. She first noted an increase in blood pressure at 20 weeks of gestation in August 2022, reaching a maximum of 170/110 mmHg, accompanied by proteinuria (++) and intermittent episodes of paroxysmal hypertension. During these episodes, her blood pressure would rise to 200/130 mmHg, associated with discomfort below the xiphoid process, but without significant dizziness, headache, chest tightness, chest pain, visual disturbances, or limb weakness. Each episode lasted approximately 2–3 min, with blood pressure spontaneously returning to normal levels. During her pregnancy, she was treated with labetalol and nifedipine to control her blood pressure, which fluctuated between 130–150/90–100 mmHg. After delivery, her blood pressure remained elevated, and treatment was adjusted to include sacubitril/valsartan, bisoprolol, and hydrochlorothiazide; however, blood pressure control remained suboptimal, reaching a maximum of 150/100 mmHg.

In September 2023, abdominal CT performed at a local hospital revealed a round hypodense mass in the left adrenal gland, approximately 3.2 cm in size. She was subsequently transferred to our hospital for further evaluation, including enhanced adrenal MRI and hormone testing, where a functional adrenal tumor was suspected. Over the past year, her mental state, appetite, sleep, and urinary output have remained normal, with no significant changes in body weight. She has a history of gestational diabetes mellitus and is currently receiving subcutaneous insulin therapy. She also has a history of hyperlipidemia. She denies any history of coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, or infectious diseases such as hepatitis B or tuberculosis. She underwent a cesarean section in 2022. She has no known food or drug allergies. On physical examination, no obvious central obesity, moon face, buffalo hump, or purple striae were observed. No significant skin ecchymosis or hyperpigmentation was noted, and there was no noticeable edema in both lower extremities. The rest of the examination was unremarkable.

Laboratory examinations

Serum potassium was markedly decreased, with a minimum level of 2.36 mmol/L (reference range, 3.5–5.3 mmol/L), accompanied by elevated carbon dioxide combining power up to 31.9 mmol/L (reference range, 22–29 mmol/L), consistent with metabolic alkalosis. Alanine aminotransferase was elevated (ALT, 63.8 U/L; reference range, 7–40 U/L), whereas aspartate aminotransferase remained within the normal range (AST, 22.1 U/L). Serum cortisol levels were significantly elevated with loss of circadian rhythm (08:00, 34.41 µg/dL [949.2 nmol/L]; 16:00, 39.5 µg/dL [1089.6 nmol/L]; 00:00, 51.77 µg/dL [1428.1 nmol/L]). ACTH was markedly elevated on multiple measurements (08:00, 80.33 pg/mL; 16:00, 104.10 pg/mL; 00:00, 121.10 pg/mL; reference range, 7.2–63.3 pg/mL). Dexamethasone suppression testing, 24-hour urinary free cortisol, and late-night salivary cortisol measurements were not available for Case 2. Therefore, although the elevated ACTH levels and loss of serum cortisol circadian rhythm suggested ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism, endogenous hypercortisolism could not be fully established according to standard screening criteria before surgery. Direct renin, aldosterone, and the aldosterone-renin ratio (ARR) were within the measured reference ranges (supine renin, 14.11 µIU/mL [2.8–39.9]; supine aldosterone, 5.0 ng/dL [3.0–23.6]; standing renin, 22.46 µIU/mL [4.4–46.1]; standing aldosterone, 5.1 ng/dL [3.0–35.3]; ARR, 0.23 [0–5.7]). However, interpretation was limited by concurrent treatment with sacubitril/valsartan, bisoprolol, and hydrochlorothiazide. Therefore, these findings were not considered sufficient to definitively exclude primary aldosteronism, although PA was considered less likely in the context of a non-suppressed renin level, non-elevated aldosterone, a non-elevated ARR, and adrenal imaging not suggestive of a typical aldosterone-producing adenoma.

Catecholamine-related examinations showed pronounced abnormalities, including plasma metanephrine of 1965.4 pmol/L (reference range, ≤ 500 pmol/L), normetanephrine of 1856.0 pmol/L (reference range, ≤ 900 pmol/L), norepinephrine of 5613.6 pmol/L (reference range, 510–3260 pmol/L), and dopamine of 615.1 pmol/L (reference range, 70–680 pmol/L).

Imaging studies

Adrenal CT showed a well-defined, round hypodense lesion in the left adrenal gland, measuring approximately 3.2 cm in size, with a CT value of approximately 37 Hounsfield units (HU). Adrenal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Fig. 3) revealed a nodular lesion at the left adrenal junction, with heterogeneous signal intensity on both T1- and T2-weighted images. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) showed hyperintensity, and contrast-enhanced imaging demonstrated uneven enhancement. The contralateral right adrenal gland was normal in size, morphology, and signal intensity, without evidence of a focal lesion. Chest CT did not reveal any obvious ectopic ACTH sources, and pituitary imaging showed no evidence of an adenoma.

Genetic testing was not performed in either case, as the patients and their families declined further testing because of financial constraints.

Surgical procedure and postoperative pathology

Given the patient’s refractory hypertension, hypokalemia, markedly elevated ACTH and cortisol levels with loss of circadian rhythm, along with significantly elevated catecholamine levels and a left adrenal mass, a clinical diagnosis of left pheochromocytoma with probable ectopic ACTH secretion was considered. After adequate preoperative preparation, the patient underwent robot-assisted laparoscopic left adrenal tumor resection under general anesthesia on October 19, 2023. Intraoperatively, a mass was identified in the left adrenal region, measuring approximately 3 × 3 cm, with dense adhesion to surrounding tissues and rich blood supply. The tumor and left adrenal gland were completely excised. The patient’s vital signs remained stable during the procedure, with an estimated blood loss of approximately 100 mL, and no blood transfusion was required.

Perioperative glucocorticoid coverage was provided with intravenous hydrocortisone 200 mg on the day of surgery. On postoperative day 1, intravenous hydrocortisone was reduced to 100 mg, followed by gradual tapering according to the patient’s clinical symptoms and serial hormone levels. The regimen was subsequently transitioned to oral hydrocortisone, and the dose had been reduced to 10 mg daily by the time of discharge. The patient was discharged with continued glucocorticoid replacement therapy.

Postoperative pathology revealed a left adrenal pheochromocytoma measuring approximately 3 × 3 × 1.7 cm. The tumor cells showed moderate atypia, with occasional mitotic figures, but no hemorrhage, necrosis, or definite invasive growth was identified. The interface between the tumor and the adjacent adrenal cortex was focally indistinct, and adrenal cortical hyperplasia was also noted. Immunohistochemical staining showed positivity for CD56 (+++), chromogranin A (CgA, ++), and synaptophysin (Syn, ++), focal positivity for S-100, and negativity for Vimentin, CKAE1/AE3, Melan-A, HMB45, and PHH3. P53 was positive in occasional cells, and the Ki-67 labeling index was approximately 2–3%. Additional ACTH immunohistochemistry showed partial weak positivity in tumor cells, supporting the diagnosis of an ACTH-producing pheochromocytoma.

Postoperative follow-up

Early postoperative follow-up showed clinical and biochemical improvement. Serum potassium increased from a preoperative nadir of 2.36 mmol/L to 3.52 mmol/L on postoperative day 3 and 4.22 mmol/L on postoperative day 4. During endocrine reassessment in the first postoperative week, ACTH decreased markedly compared with the preoperative levels, with values of < 1.00 pg/mL at midnight and 2.33 pg/mL at 08:00. Because the patient was receiving perioperative hydrocortisone replacement during this period, postoperative serum cortisol values were interpreted cautiously and were not used to assess endogenous cortisol secretion or circadian recovery. At approximately one week after surgery, blood pressure was 132/110 mmHg, showing improvement compared with the preoperative paroxysmal hypertensive episodes, although diastolic hypertension persisted. Imaging follow-up revealed no residual tumor or recurrence (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Ectopic ACTH syndrome is a rare cause of ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome. Its clinical features are often atypical, with refractory hypertension, hypokalemia, and metabolic disturbances as the main manifestations, while lacking the typical physical changes seen in Cushing syndrome, thereby complicating the diagnostic process [2, 5]. Among the causes of ectopic ACTH syndrome, pheochromocytoma is exceedingly rare. However, due to its ability to secrete both ACTH and catecholamines, it can lead to a complex and variable clinical phenotype, making it more prone to misdiagnosis [4].

In 1995, Chen et al. proposed diagnostic criteria for pheochromocytoma (PHEO) secreting ectopic ACTH: (1) clinical and laboratory evidence of hypercortisolism; (2) significantly elevated ACTH levels in the blood; (3) elevated catecholamines in the blood and urine, or the presence of an adrenal mass with high signal intensity on T2-weighted imaging on adrenal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), providing evidence for the existence of PHEO; (4) resolution of Cushing syndrome and PHEO symptoms after tumor resection; and (5) a rapid decrease or normalization of elevated ACTH levels following tumor resection [6]. All patients in the study underwent unilateral adrenalectomy, and postoperatively, their plasma ACTH, urinary metanephrine, and urinary free cortisol levels normalized, with symptom resolution. In the present report, both patients exhibited significantly elevated ACTH and cortisol levels, accompanied by loss of circadian rhythm, as well as hypokalemia and hypertension. One case began during pregnancy and lacked the typical pheochromocytoma triad, while the other presented primarily with metabolic abnormalities and electrolyte disturbances. These findings suggest that when faced with ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome with negative pituitary imaging, clinicians should consider pheochromocytoma as a potential differential diagnosis. A combined evaluation of the cortisol axis and catecholamine system is crucial for accurate diagnosis and identifying the underlying etiology.

Compared with the recently published structured review (Table 1) of 94 ACTH-producing pheochromocytomas/paragangliomas, our 2 patients shared several common features, including female sex, hypertension, marked ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism, and biochemical evidence of catecholamine excess [7]. However, unlike the review population, in which a cushingoid appearance was reported in 82% of cases, neither of our patients had overt classic cushingoid features [8]. This is consistent with occasional published cases lacking pigmentation or cushingoid appearance despite marked ACTH/cortisol excess [9]. In addition, the pregnancy-associated onset in Case 2 resembles earlier reports showing that gestation may further obscure diagnosis and delay recognition [10]. Surgical resection led to marked biochemical and clinical improvement in both of our cases, which is consistent with the high remission rate reported in the literature [8].

Inferior petrosal sinus sampling (IPSS) was considered but not performed in either case. Although IPSS is the gold standard for distinguishing pituitary from ectopic ACTH secretion, it is invasive and is usually reserved for diagnostically equivocal cases after noninvasive evaluation [11]. In our patients, catecholamine excess, adrenal lesions suspicious for pheochromocytoma, negative pituitary imaging, and the absence of convincing alternative ectopic ACTH sources made an adrenal ectopic ACTH source more likely. In Case 1, outside-hospital records documented absent suppression after dexamethasone testing, with serum cortisol remaining above the upper reportable limit (> 75 µg/dL [> 2069 nmol/L]) after both low- and high-dose dexamethasone administration; the 24-hour urinary free cortisol level was 2314.8 µg/24 h [6386.4 nmol/24 h] at baseline and remained elevated at 1768.5 µg/24 h [4879.2 nmol/24 h] after low-dose dexamethasone. The marked postoperative hormonal improvement and final histopathological findings further supported this interpretation.

A limitation of Case 2 is that standard confirmatory tests for endogenous hypercortisolism, including dexamethasone suppression testing, 24-hour urinary free cortisol, and late-night salivary cortisol, were not available. Therefore, Case 2 should be interpreted as probable ectopic ACTH secretion or probable ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism associated with an ACTH-positive pheochromocytoma, rather than a fully biochemically confirmed case of endogenous hypercortisolism. This interpretation was supported by elevated ACTH levels, loss of serum cortisol circadian rhythm, catecholamine excess, an adrenal pheochromocytoma with partial weak ACTH immunopositivity, postoperative normalization of hypokalemia, and a marked postoperative decrease in ACTH. However, postoperative serum cortisol values were not used as definitive evidence of remission because they may have been affected by perioperative hydrocortisone replacement.

Published cases show substantial variability in tumor size, and the structured review suggests that clinically significant ACTH secretion can occur across a broad size spectrum [8, 12, 13]. In our report, both tumors were approximately 3 cm in diameter. These findings indicate that functional activity should not be inferred from size alone and that combined endocrine and catecholamine testing remains more informative than tumor size itself when evaluating suspected ACTH-secreting pheochromocytoma [4, 8].

In the diagnosis of ectopic ACTH syndrome, rapid and accurate localization of the ACTH-secreting source is crucial for determining the treatment strategy. In recent years, the value of functional imaging techniques in such cases has gained increasing attention. Notably, 68Ga-DOTATATE PET-CT and 68Ga-Pentixafor PET-CT, which reflect the expression of somatostatin receptors and CXCR4 receptors, respectively, have shown high sensitivity and specificity in localizing neuroendocrine tumors [14, 15]. In our report, functional imaging was performed in Case 1, in which ^68Ga-DOTATATE PET-CT demonstrated high somatostatin receptor expression in the left adrenal lesion and ^68Ga-Pentixafor PET-CT showed marked CXCR4 receptor expression in bilateral adrenal lesions. These findings supported the adrenal origin of the lesion and helped exclude other potential ectopic ACTH sources. In Case 2, localization mainly relied on conventional cross-sectional imaging, including adrenal CT and MRI, together with negative chest CT and pituitary imaging. These findings suggest that functional imaging may be particularly helpful in selected cases when ectopic ACTH syndrome is strongly suspected and conventional imaging is inconclusive.

Surgical resection of the tumor is considered the preferred and definitive treatment for pheochromocytoma-induced ectopic ACTH syndrome. However, it is important to note that patients with pheochromocytoma-induced ectopic ACTH syndrome often present with severe hypokalemia, refractory hypertension, and metabolic disturbances at the time of diagnosis, which theoretically increases perioperative risks. Some previous studies have suggested that surgery should only be performed after complete correction of endocrine abnormalities. However, in clinical practice, achieving this goal is often difficult to accomplish in a short period of time [11, 16]. Both previous studies and the present case report show that, with adequate preoperative preparation and multidisciplinary management, even when patients have not achieved an “ideal” endocrine state, surgical resection of the tumor remains a key measure for controlling disease progression and reversing hypercortisolemia [7, 17, 18]. In this study, although both patients had varying degrees of hypokalemia and poor blood pressure control, they successfully underwent robot-assisted laparoscopic adrenal tumor resection after systematic evaluation and thorough preparation. The perioperative course was uneventful, and postoperative ACTH and cortisol levels decreased rapidly, with significant clinical improvement. These findings suggest that proactive and cautious surgical intervention is both feasible and necessary in such cases.

In conclusion, although pheochromocytoma is a rare cause of ectopic ACTH syndrome, it should be considered in patients presenting with refractory hypertension, hypokalemia, and ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome. Preoperative endocrine evaluation and accurate imaging localization are essential for clinical suspicion and surgical planning, while the definitive diagnosis is established by postoperative histopathological examination and immunohistochemistry. Surgical resection remains the definitive treatment and can lead to marked improvement in hormonal abnormalities and clinical symptoms. Given the small sample size of this report, these observations should be interpreted cautiously.
