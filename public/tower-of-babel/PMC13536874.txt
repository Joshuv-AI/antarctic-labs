Pathways associated with hypertension self-management behaviors among older adults: a PRECEDE–PROCEED theory-guided path analysis
Authors: Jin Li, Jin Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension self-management is essential for reducing complications and maintaining functional well-being in later life, yet the multi-level factors associated with self-management among older adults remain insufficiently understood. This study investigated pathways linking predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors to hypertension self-management behaviors (HSMB) among older adults using a PRECEDE–PROCEED theory-guided model.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from late March to early April 2026 among 264 older adults with hypertension in Chongqing, China. Data were collected through interviewer-administered structured questionnaires. Pearson correlation analysis and path analysis were used to examine direct and indirect associations among hypertension-related knowledge, self-efficacy, health education, healthcare resources and access, family support, and HSMB.ResultsSelf-efficacy and hypertension-related knowledge showed significant direct associations with HSMB. Healthcare resources and access demonstrated both direct and indirect associations with HSMB through knowledge and self-efficacy. Family support was associated with HSMB both directly and indirectly through healthcare resources and access, knowledge, and self-efficacy. Health education showed a negative indirect association with HSMB through knowledge, suggesting that the content, timing, or targeting of education may warrant closer examination.ConclusionsHSMB among older adults was associated with multi-level cognitive, environmental, and social factors. Self-efficacy, healthcare resources and access, and family support appeared to be particularly important correlates. Interventions for older adults with hypertension may benefit from combining skills-building, accessible services, and family-engaged support while improving the relevance and comprehensibility of health education.Keywords: Hypertension, Self-management behaviors, Older adults, PRECEDE–PROCEED model, Path analysis

Introduction

Hypertension remains a major public health challenge worldwide and disproportionately affects older adults, who account for a rapidly growing share of the global population [1–3]. In China, more than half of older adults have hypertension, and the prevalence has been reported to approach 90% among those aged 80 years or older [4]. With advancing age, vascular stiffness, multimorbidity, functional decline, and cognitive changes may complicate long-term blood pressure management and increase the burden of daily self-care [5, 6]. As a result, maintaining adequate hypertension control in older adults requires more than pharmacological treatment alone; it also depends on sustained engagement in self-management behaviors.

Hypertension self-management behaviors (HSMB), including regular medication use, home blood pressure monitoring, dietary modification, physical activity, follow-up visits, and risk-factor control, are essential for preventing cardiovascular complications and preserving functional health [7–11]. However, these behaviors are not determined by knowledge alone. In later life, self-management may be shaped by personal capability, confidence, access to healthcare resources, family involvement, and the extent to which health information is communicated and understood.

Self-management is commonly defined as an individual’s ability to manage symptoms, treatment, lifestyle adjustment, and psychosocial consequences associated with chronic illness [12, 13]. In older adults with hypertension, self-management is particularly complex because behavioral performance may be influenced by disease-related knowledge and beliefs, self-efficacy, family support, healthcare access, and age-related differences in health literacy, functional capacity, and care dependency [14–18]. A theory-informed framework is therefore useful for clarifying how these determinants may operate together.

Existing studies have examined factors related to hypertension self-management, but evidence focused specifically on older adults remains limited and fragmented. Many studies have considered isolated predictors, whereas fewer have explored how cognitive, enabling, and reinforcing factors may be interrelated. In addition, findings from general adult populations may not be directly transferable to older adults because of age-related differences in health literacy, functional capacity, care dependency, and family context. These gaps suggest the need for a more integrated analytic approach to determinants of HSMB in later life.

The PRECEDE–PROCEED model provides a useful framework for organizing determinants of health-related behaviors into predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors [19]. In the context of hypertension self-management, predisposing factors may include hypertension-related knowledge and self-efficacy, enabling factors may include health education and healthcare resources and access, and reinforcing factors may include family support. This framework allows self-management to be examined not only as an individual behavior, but also as a behavior shaped by service conditions and social support.

To address these gaps, this study examined HSMB among older adults with hypertension using a PRECEDE–PROCEED theory-guided path model. Specifically, we assessed the associations of predisposing factors, enabling factors, and reinforcing factors with HSMB, and tested theoretically informed pathways among these variables.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted from from late March to early April 2026 in Chongqing, a municipality directly under the central government in western China. Chongqing is one of the largest metropolitan areas in China and is characterized by a pronounced urban-rural dual structure and substantial heterogeneity in healthcare resource distribution. With rapid population aging and a high burden of chronic non-communicable diseases, particularly hypertension, Chongqing represents a typical setting for examining self-management behaviors among older adults within China’s healthcare system [20].

The study was carried out in the geriatric department of a tertiary general hospital located in Fuling District, Chongqing, which serves both urban and surrounding rural populations. This institution was selected because it provides standardized hypertension-related services and receives older patients with diverse socioeconomic backgrounds [21, 22]. However, because participants were recruited from a single tertiary hospital, the sample should be interpreted as a clinical sample rather than as representative of all community-dwelling older adults with hypertension in Chongqing.

Participants and sampling

Participants were recruited using a consecutive sampling method during the study period. Older adults attending outpatient clinics or admitted to the geriatric department were screened for eligibility.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 60 years; (2) a prior diagnosis of hypertension according to the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension (2024 Revision) or equivalent clinical records; and (3) ability to communicate and complete the interview with or without minimal assistance. Participants were excluded if they (1) had severe cognitive impairment or diagnosed psychiatric disorders, (2) were in an acute or clinically unstable condition at the time of investigation, or (3) were unwilling to participate.

Eligible participants were informed of the study objectives and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection.

Because no universally accepted prevalence estimate or cutoff for adequate hypertension self-management behaviors among older adults was available, and because the present study used a study-specific operationalization of HSMB, the prevalence-based calculation could not provide a precise disease-specific estimate.

For the path analysis, the model used observed composite variables rather than latent constructs and included a limited number of theoretically specified pathways among six observed variables. The final sample of 264 participants met the commonly cited minimum of approximately 200 cases for structural path models and provided an acceptable case-to-parameter ratio for the present observed-variable, exploratory, theory-guided path analysis. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the sample size remains moderate for testing multiple indirect pathways, and the findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger multicenter samples.

Theoretical framework

This study was guided by the PRECEDE–PROCEED model [19], in which determinants of self-management behaviors can be grouped into predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors that jointly shape health-related practices. In this framework, predisposing factors influence motivation and capability, enabling factors reflect practical and organizational conditions that facilitate behavior, and reinforcing factors capture social support and feedback that sustain behavior over time.

Based on this framework, the study hypothesized that:

Predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors are associated with HSMB;

Predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors are associated with HSMB;

Enabling factors may be associated with HSMB indirectly through predisposing factors;

Enabling factors may be associated with HSMB indirectly through predisposing factors;

Data collection and measures

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire administered by trained investigators through face-to-face interviews in designated clinic rooms. Interviews were used to reduce missing data and to improve participation among older adults with varying literacy levels.

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics

Information on age, sex, marital status, education level, residence (urban/rural), living arrangement, former occupation, duration of hypertension, comorbidities, and health insurance status was collected using a standardized form.

HSMB

HSMB were assessed using a study-specific behavioral index developed with reference to the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension [24]. The six items covered key recommended self-management behaviors: regular medication use, home blood pressure monitoring, regular follow-up or medical visits, salt-reduction diet practice, regular physical activity or weight control, and avoidance of smoking and excessive alcohol consumption. Each item was rated on a five-point frequency scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”). Total scores ranged from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating more frequent engagement in hypertension self-management behaviors. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this six-item index was 0.735. Because the index was designed to capture multiple guideline-recommended behaviors rather than a single narrow psychological construct, it was treated as a content-based behavioral index.

Hypertension-related knowledge

Hypertension-related knowledge was assessed using a six-item guideline-informed checklist focusing on essential management knowledge emphasized in national guidelines [24]. The items assessed awareness of the chronic nature of hypertension, the importance of regular medication use even when blood pressure is controlled, potential complications of uncontrolled hypertension, benefits of salt restriction, the need for blood pressure monitoring, and the value of regular follow-up. Each correct answer received 1 point, yielding a total score of 0 to 6. Higher scores indicated greater hypertension-related management knowledge.

Self-efficacy for HSMB

Self-efficacy for HSMB was measured using a four-item study-specific measure assessing participants’ confidence in performing key self-management behaviors, including long-term medication adherence, dietary modification, regular physical activity, and blood pressure monitoring. These items were aligned with the core behavioral domains assessed in the HSMB index. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not confident at all”) to 5 (“very confident”). Item scores were summed, with higher scores indicating stronger self-efficacy for hypertension self-management. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this four-item measure was 0.713.

Health education

Health education was assessed using a study-specific exposure index covering hypertension-related education received from healthcare workers. The nine items were developed with reference to guideline-recommended hypertension management content and included education on the importance of long-term treatment, correct medication use, management of missed doses, recognition and management of medication side effects, home blood pressure monitoring, regular follow-up visits, healthy diet guidance, exercise advice, and smoking/alcohol control. Each reported health education item received was scored as 1 point, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 9, with higher scores indicating greater reported exposure to hypertension-related health education topics. This index measured exposure to education content rather than the quality, tailoring, comprehension, or behavioral effectiveness of health education.

Healthcare resources and access

Healthcare resources and access were assessed using a six-item study-specific composite index reflecting enabling conditions for hypertension self-management within the PRECEDE–PROCEED framework. The items included accessibility of healthcare services, availability of regular follow-up or chronic disease management services, continuous access to antihypertensive medications, affordability of hypertension-related care, convenience of blood pressure monitoring, and access to professional advice when needed. Each favorable condition was scored as 1 and each unfavorable condition as 0, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 6. Higher scores indicated more favorable enabling conditions for self-management.

Family support

Family support was assessed using a four-item study-specific measure covering medical-related support, daily life support, emotional support, and financial support. These items were designed to capture reinforcing support from family members for hypertension self-management within the PRECEDE–PROCEED framework. Responses were rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“none”) to 5 (“very high”). Total scores ranged from 4 to 20, with higher scores reflecting stronger reinforcing support for hypertension self-management. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this four-item measure was 0.726.

Quality control

Before the formal survey, the questionnaire was pilot-tested among 15 older adults with hypertension to assess its clarity, comprehensibility, interview feasibility, and completion time. Based on participant feedback, minor revisions were made to improve item wording and readability. All investigators received standardized training on interview techniques and study procedures before data collection.

Completed questionnaires were checked daily for completeness and logical consistency. A random subset of questionnaires was re-examined to verify data accuracy, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion among the research team.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered using EpiData 3.0 (The EpiData Association, Odense, Denmark) and analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were examined for distributional characteristics using Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests and visual inspection of histograms and normal probability plots. As the study variables showed acceptable distributional characteristics, they were treated as approximately continuous variables and are presented as means and standard deviations or as scores with observed ranges. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for descriptive and correlation analyses.

Pearson correlation analysis was then performed to examine bivariate linear associations among HSMB and the predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors.

Variables showing statistically significant correlations were subsequently considered for inclusion in the path analysis together with the theoretical framework.

Path analysis was conducted using AMOS version 21.0 to estimate direct and indirect associations among predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors and HSMB [25]. Model parameters were estimated using the maximum likelihood method. Standardized path coefficients (β) were reported to facilitate interpretation of relative association strength. Model fit was evaluated using multiple fit indices, including χ²/df, comparative fit index (CFI), normed fit index (NFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), relative fit index (RFI), and incremental fit index (IFI). Model adequacy was interpreted based on the overall pattern of fit indices rather than on any single index alone.

Indirect effects were tested using bias-corrected bootstrap procedures with 500 resamples and 95% confidence intervals. Because the path model was used to estimate structural associations within a theory-informed framework, findings were interpreted cautiously and without causal claims.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Chongqing Medical and Pharmaceutical College (KYLLSC20260326084). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 264 older adults with hypertension were included in the final analysis. As shown in Table 1, more than half of the participants were female (55.3%, n = 146), and nearly half were aged 60–69 years (48.5%, n = 128). Participants aged 70–79 years and ≥ 80 years accounted for 34.8% (n = 92) and 16.7% (n = 44) of the sample, respectively. Most participants resided in urban areas (59.1%, n = 156), while 40.9% (n = 108) lived in rural areas. Approximately 61.4% (n = 162) of participants were married. In terms of educational attainment, 46.2% (n = 122) had completed primary school or below, and 21.2% (n = 56) had attained a high school education or above.

Correlation between the predisposing, enabling, reinforcing factors and HSMB

Hypertension-related knowledge was positively correlated with self-efficacy (r = 0.671, P < 0.01), healthcare resources and access (r = 0.531, P < 0.01), family support (r = 0.147, P < 0.05), and HSMB (r = 0.752, P < 0.01). Discussion

This study examined structural associations among predisposing, enabling, and reinforcing factors and HSMB in older adults within a PRECEDE–PROCEED framework. Overall, the findings suggest that hypertension self-management in later life is shaped by interrelated cognitive, service-related, and family factors rather than by any single determinant. The path model showed an overall acceptable fit and supported the theoretical organization of these factors, with self-efficacy, hypertension-related knowledge, healthcare resources and access, and family support emerging as important correlates of HSMB. Although the RFI value was slightly below the conventional threshold of 0.90, most model fit indices supported the adequacy of the model. Therefore, the model should be interpreted as an exploratory, theory-guided representation of observed associations rather than as a fully confirmed causal structure.

Among the predisposing factors, self-efficacy was positively associated with HSMB. This finding is consistent with behavior-change theory and prior research showing that confidence in one’s ability to perform disease-management tasks is closely related to treatment adherence and lifestyle regulation [26]. Older adults who feel capable of maintaining medication use, dietary modification, physical activity, and blood pressure monitoring may be more likely to translate intentions into daily practice. From a practical perspective, this result supports the inclusion of confidence-building strategies, such as stepwise goal setting, tailored counseling, and repeated skills reinforcement, in future interventions.

Hypertension-related knowledge also showed a significant direct association with HSMB and an indirect association through self-efficacy. This pattern suggests that knowledge may not only inform patients about what should be done, but may also strengthen confidence in carrying out self-management behaviors. For older adults, clear understanding of long-term treatment needs, potential complications, and behavior-specific recommendations may reduce uncertainty and support more consistent self-care. At the same time, the magnitude of the knowledge-related association indicates that informational content alone is unlikely to be sufficient unless it is accompanied by motivational and contextual support.

Healthcare resources and access demonstrated both direct and indirect associations with HSMB, highlighting the importance of enabling conditions for self-management. Adequate access to services, follow-up, medicines, monitoring tools, and professional advice may reduce practical barriers and make recommended behaviors easier to sustain [27]. The indirect pathways through knowledge and self-efficacy further suggest that favorable service environments may strengthen self-management not only by providing resources, but also by improving patients’ understanding and confidence. This finding is particularly relevant for older adults, whose behavioral capacity may depend heavily on continuity of care and the accessibility of chronic disease services.

An unexpected finding was the negative association between reported health education exposure and hypertension-related knowledge, which contributed to a negative indirect association with HSMB. This finding should be interpreted with particular caution and should not be understood as evidence that health education is ineffective or harmful. In this study, health education was measured as the number of hypertension-related education topics that participants reported receiving, rather than as the quality, intensity, tailoring, comprehension, or behavioral uptake of education. The variable therefore captured exposure to education content rather than the effectiveness of education.

One possible explanation is reverse targeting. Older adults with poorer knowledge, lower self-management capacity, or more complex management difficulties may have been more likely to receive repeated education from healthcare providers. Another possibility is that routine education may not have been sufficiently tailored to older adults’ health literacy, cognitive capacity, beliefs, or practical barriers [28–30]. In such circumstances, greater exposure to education content may indicate greater need rather than better educational effect. Therefore, this finding points less to a failure of health education itself than to the need to examine how education is targeted, delivered, understood, and translated into daily self-management among older adults.

Because the present study was cross-sectional and did not include qualitative assessment of educational content or patient comprehension, these explanations remain tentative. Future longitudinal, intervention-based, and qualitative studies are needed to evaluate the timing, content, communication quality, and patient-perceived usefulness of hypertension education in older populations [31–33].

Family support played a significant reinforcing role in hypertension self-management, showing both direct and indirect associations with HSMB. Emotional encouragement, assistance with daily routines, reminders about medication, accompaniment to clinical visits, and financial support may all help older adults adhere to recommended care [34, 35]. The indirect associations observed through healthcare resources and access, knowledge, and self-efficacy further suggest that family involvement may operate by facilitating service use, improving understanding, and reinforcing confidence. In settings where older adults rely heavily on household members, family-centered approaches may therefore strengthen chronic disease management.

Overall, the findings support the usefulness of the PRECEDE–PROCEED framework for organizing correlates of HSMB in older adults. Predisposing factors were associated with HSMB directly, enabling factors showed both direct and indirect associations, and reinforcing factors contributed through both direct support and pathway-based mechanisms. This pattern may help guide future intervention design by highlighting where educational, service, and family-based strategies can be combined.

A key strength of this study is its theory-driven use of the PRECEDE–PROCEED framework and path analysis to examine multiple interrelated factors within a single model. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the observed pathways should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Second, the health education variable measured reported exposure to education topics rather than education quality, comprehension, or effectiveness; therefore, the unexpected negative association should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Third, the moderate sample size and single-tertiary-hospital setting may limit model stability and generalizability to community or primary-care populations. Finally, several variables were assessed using study-specific checklist-based or composite indices, which require further validation in future studies.

Conclusion

This study identified theory-informed pathways associated with HSMB among older adults with hypertension. Self-efficacy, hypertension-related knowledge, healthcare resources and access, and family support were significant correlates of HSMB, while the pattern observed for health education suggests that educational delivery for older adults may require refinement. These findings provide a basis for developing geriatric hypertension self-management interventions that are more accessible, family-engaged, and responsive to the needs of older adults.
