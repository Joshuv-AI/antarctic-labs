Immigration enforcement in hospitals: a framework for health systems
Authors: Joseph V Sakran, Karey McDonough, Zakk Arciaga, Bellal Joseph
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHospitals are intended to be spaces of diagnosis, treatment, and healing, yet health systems are increasingly confronting immigration enforcement activity in and around clinical settings, including emergency departments, inpatient units, and other restricted care areas. These encounters may introduce fear, operational uncertainty, and ethical tension for patients, families, clinicians, and institutional leaders. In high-acuity environments such as trauma bays, perioperative areas, and emergency departments, the presence of enforcement personnel may be particularly disruptive to rapid clinical decision-making, patient trust, and the delivery of uninterrupted care.This perspective article proposes a practical, nonpartisan framework to guide health system responses to immigration enforcement encounters in clinical spaces. The framework is grounded in core legal and ethical obligations, including protection of patient confidentiality, compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, adherence to the Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act, and maintenance of clinical boundaries between public and non-public hospital areas. We outline a three-phase approach: preparation before an encounter occurs, a stepwise real-time response, and post-event support for patients and staff.Key recommendations include establishing a clear written policy, designating a 24/7 legal or administrative point of contact, training frontline staff not to make legal determinations, aligning electronic health record practices to minimize unnecessary documentation of immigration status, preserving clinical care during enforcement encounters, and using structured debriefings after events. A trauma-informed approach is essential, recognizing that enforcement activity may reinforce fear, institutional mistrust, and prior experiences of violence or coercion. By standardizing protocols and centering patient care, hospitals can protect confidentiality, support clinicians, reduce operational confusion, and preserve their fundamental mission as places of healing without fear or favor.

Introduction

Hospitals exist to diagnose, treat, and heal. They are spaces where people seek care in moments of vulnerability, often at the most fragile points in their lives. Yet clinicians and health system leaders across the USA are increasingly reporting encounters with immigration enforcement agents in and around healthcare facilities, including emergency departments and inpatient units. Recent policy changes, including the 2025 rollback of U.S. Department of Homeland Security guidance protecting sensitive locations, have heightened these risks by removing clear limits on enforcement actions in hospitals.1

Such encounters introduce uncertainty, fear, and operational confusion. They place frontline staff in difficult positions and threaten to erode public trust in healthcare institutions. Major physician organizations have warned that immigration enforcement activity in or around hospitals and emergency departments undermines patient trust and deters people from seeking urgently needed care. In a recent statement, the American Medical Association (AMA) expressed deep concern that such actions compromise the safety of patients and clinicians and threaten the foundational principle that hospitals must remain places of refuge for anyone in medical distress.2

This article offers a practical, nonpartisan framework to help hospitals respond when immigration enforcement appears in clinical spaces, grounded in law, ethics, and medicine’s core mission: to care for patients. What is at stake extends beyond regulatory compliance. When individuals fear seeking medical attention because of potential enforcement activity, preventable morbidity and mortality rise. The AMA has emphasized that such fear “threatens their health, impedes the ability of physicians to render care, and ultimately undermines basic trust in our healthcare institutions.”3 Health systems therefore have both an ethical obligation and a public health imperative to establish clear, standardized approaches that safeguard patient care, confidentiality, and safety.

Guiding principles

Institutional responses should be anchored in several fundamental principles: patient care is the top priority; confidentiality is legally and ethically protected; frontline staff should never make legal determinations; and protocols must be clear and rehearsed. These principles apply regardless of local politics or federal enforcement priorities.

These principles should also be delivered through a trauma-informed lens that recognizes how fear of enforcement may mirror prior experiences of violence, coercion, or institutional mistrust. For some patients, the presence of enforcement in clinical spaces may be perceived as an extension of government or policing systems, potentially undermining trust and willingness to engage in care.

Legal foundations

Hospitals and clinicians operate within established legal frameworks that remain unchanged by the presence of immigration enforcement.

Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act

Under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, protected health information cannot be disclosed without patient authorization unless a specific legal exception applies, such as a valid warrant, court order, or defined public safety threat.

Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act

The Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act requires hospitals to provide emergency evaluation and stabilization for all patients, regardless of immigration status or ability to pay.

Access limitations

Law enforcement officers are permitted to access public areas of a hospital or clinic, but are not automatically granted access to nonpublic areas.

These legal baselines support a simple institutional stance: hospitals provide care; legal departments handle law enforcement requests.

Preparation before an encounter occurs

Preparation is the most effective protection. Health systems should not wait for an unplanned confrontation to define their approach.

Establish a clear written policy

Every hospital should maintain a concise, easily accessible policy that specifies whom to contact if an enforcement agent arrives, who is authorized to communicate with agents, how legal documents including warrants and court orders are to be verified, and what information may or may not be shared.

Designate a single point of contact

A 24/7 legal or administrative contact—such as the office of public safety, legal department, administrator on call, or incident command lead—should be identified. Frontline clinicians should be instructed to call this contact immediately and not engage substantively with government officials.

Train the workforce

Training should be included during onboarding and refreshed periodically using brief, scenario-based modules. Staff should understand that they are not required to answer questions from enforcement agents, should not disclose patient information, and must contact designated leadership. Memory aids or pocket cards with scripts can reduce anxiety and variability.

Align electronic health record practices

Immigration status should not be documented in the electronic health record unless required for a specific legal, regulatory, or administrative purpose defined by institutional policy or applicable law. Screens should autolock when unattended, and visible displays of patient identifiers in public spaces should be minimized. Understanding the frequency and context of law enforcement presence may help health systems identify operational vulnerabilities, improve staff preparedness, and better support populations at higher risk of disengagement from care.4

Clarify public versus nonpublic spaces

Hospitals should explicitly delineate which areas are public (lobbies, waiting rooms, cafeterias) and which are restricted. Signage and badge access systems reinforce these boundaries.

Real-time response: a stepwise framework

These considerations may be particularly relevant in trauma bays, perioperative environments, and emergency departments where rapid clinical decision-making, restricted access areas, and high-acuity care create additional operational complexity. Health systems should clearly define how hospital security, clinical leadership, and legal counsel interface with external law enforcement personnel in these settings to minimize disruption to emergent patient care.

Patient-centered actions

Enforcement activity in or near the hospital can be deeply distressing for patients and families, regardless of immigration status. Clinicians and staff should reassure patients that their care will continue uninterrupted and explain that their health information is legally protected. Whenever possible, hospitals should offer private spaces for discussions or procedures to preserve dignity and safety. Involving social work, case management, and patient advocates can help address emotional and logistical needs, whereas qualified interpreters ensure that patients fully understand what is occurring. Evidence from emergency care settings suggests that the presence of law enforcement may affect patient trust, perceptions of safety, and engagement in care, particularly among individuals with prior exposure to violence or institutional mistrust.5 In this context, clear communication, defined roles, and preservation of clinical boundaries are critical to maintaining a patient-centered environment.

Supporting the workforce

Such encounters also create moral and emotional strain for clinicians whose primary obligation is to patient welfare. Institutions must offer explicit support for staff who adhere to established policy during enforcement events. Clear scripts, pocket cards, and visible leadership presence help maintain a calm, coordinated response. After any such event, structured debriefings provide opportunities to address both operational lessons and emotional impact. Staff should be confident that they will not face retaliation or disciplinary action for prioritizing patient care and confidentiality.

Practices to avoid

Hospitals should take care to prevent well-intentioned but potentially harmful practices. Clinicians should not ask patients about immigration status unless the laws in their state require it, nor should clinicians volunteer information to enforcement agents. Clinicians should not attempt to obstruct or physically stop law enforcement activity. Bedside questioning should never occur without prior legal review, and individual staff members should not attempt to judge the validity of a warrant or court order. Confrontational behavior can heighten risk and confusion. A consistent, policy-driven approach protects patients, staff, and the integrity of the institution.

Ethical rationale

Medical ethics provide a steady compass amid the uncertainty of enforcement activity. The principle of beneficence requires acting in the patient’s best interests, whereas non-maleficence obliges clinicians to avoid harm, including harm that results when fear deters care. Justice demands equitable treatment of all patients, irrespective of citizenship or status. Professional integrity reminds clinicians of medicine’s social contract: to serve as a sanctuary for healing. Hospitals, as vital civic institutions, depend on the public’s confidence that care remains available and safe. A trauma-informed approach reinforces these ethical commitments by recognizing the cumulative impact of fear, violence exposure, and structural vulnerability on patient decision-making and health outcomes.

Conclusion

Immigration enforcement may occur in communities, but hospitals must remain spaces devoted to healing. Through clear protocols, legal grounding, and patient-centered values, health systems can navigate these challenging situations without politicization. By preparing in advance, standardizing responses, and centering care, hospitals can protect patients, support clinicians, and preserve medicine’s fundamental mission—to heal without fear or favor.
