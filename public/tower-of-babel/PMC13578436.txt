Juglone induces GSDME axis pyroptosis in osteosarcoma cells via the JNK/P38 MAPK pathway
Authors: Jierui Zhao, Xingyu Zhao, Yue Lu, He Wang, Yuhan Zhang, Yi Wang, LiChun Xing, Yumiao Guo, Peng Peng, Wei Zhang, Qibiao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe MAPK signaling pathway plays a considerable role in cancer inhibition, and pyroptosis is a type of programmed cell death (PCD); thus, it is essential for cellular activity and cancer progression. Juglone can inhibit the viability and motility of cancer cells, but it is unclear whether it can induce pyroptosis in cancer cells, and the corresponding regulatory mechanisms are not well understood. This study explored the in vitro effects of juglone on OS cells, further revealing its ability to induce pyroptosis via the caspase-3/GSDME pathway. Moreover, juglone can regulate the GSDME pathway to induce both pyroptosis and apoptosis through the JNK/p38 pathway. These findings suggest that juglone has potential as a therapeutic agent for osteosarcoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58482-2.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Cancer, Cell biology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) is a highly aggressive and metastatic malignant bone tumor that most commonly affects children and adolescents. It is characterized by the presence of malignant mesenchymal cells that produce osteoid and immature bone; thus, patients with metastatic OS have extremely low five-year survival rates, even less than 20%1. The 5-year survival rate for primary osteosarcoma patients using traditional chemotherapy.has not been significantly improved.However, when tumors metastasize to other organs (most commonly the lungs), the 5-year survival rate decreases significantly to less than 30%2,3. Moreover, metastatic osteosarcoma patients do not respond well to conventional chemotherapy4,5, which makes finding new treatment methods to improve the treatment of osteosarcoma patients an urgent priority.

Pyroptosis is one of the ways of programmed cell death. It is distinguished from necrosis and apoptosis mainly by the cell death process mediated by the cysteine aspartate protease (caspase family)5. Studies have shown that pyroptosis can be initiated via different pathways. In original studies pyroptosis was thought to be activated by caspase-1, which subsequently cleaves GSDMD at the N-terminus, ultimately leading to pores in the plasma membrane6,7. As research continues to progress, it has been found that most members of the Gsdermin family can induce pyroptosis.Except for members of the Gsdermin family, some factors can induce pyroptosis, such as LPS in the cytosol of the pathogen, which triggers the cleavage of caspase-11, leading to the release of intracellular ATP8. Also as the main way, cleavage by activated Caspase-3 at the N-terminus of GSDME, which can lead to ultimately pyroptosis9,10.The characteristics of pyroptosis include cell membrane swelling and rupture and nuclear shrinkage, after which inflammatory factors such as IL-1 and IL-1β can be released into the extracellular space11.

Juglone (5-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone), which is isolated from the fresh root bark, stem bark, and green fruit peel of walnut, is a hydroxyquinone compound known for its cytotoxic properties12,13. At present, many studies have shown that juglone has very promising anticancer effects on many cancers, such as cervical cancer, lung cancer hepatocellular carcinoma and colon cancer14–16. However, whether juglone can induce pyroptosis in OS cells remains unknown.

In this study, we demonstrated that juglone has the potential to treat osteosarcoma cells in vitro. Further exploration of the underlying mechanisms revealed that juglone can activate the JNK/P38 signaling pathway to regulate pyroptosis in OS via the GSDME pathway and simultaneously induce the apoptosis of OS cells. Overall, our study aimed to demonstrate that juglone is a promising drug for the treatment of osteosarcoma.

Results

Juglone reduces viability and motility in U2OS and Mg63 cells

To investigate the effects of juglone on OS cell proliferation, invasion, and migration, the U2OS and Mg63 cell lines were cultured with different concentrations of juglone for 24 h. After treatment, the viability of U2OS and Mg63 cells decreased in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1B). Additionally, we assessed apoptosis rates via flow cytometry. Compared with the control group, the juglone-treated group presented a significant increase in the apoptosis rate (Fig. 1C, D). Juglone treatment also significantly upregulated the expression of the proapoptotic protein Bax and activated Parp while significantly downregulating the antiapoptotic protein Bcl-2 in both U2OS and Mg63 cells (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was measured via flow cytometry to confirm the involvement of mitochondria in juglone-induced apoptosis. The data demonstrated a sharp decline in the MMP following juglone treatment (Fig. 1F).

(C, D) The percentage of apoptotic OS cells following 24 h of juglone treatment (5, 10 µM) was assessed via Annexin V/PI double-staining. (E) Western blot images showing Bax, Bcl-2, and Parp protein levels. (F) Mitochondrial membrane potential in U2OS cells after 24 h of juglone treatment, as measured by JC-1 staining and flow cytometry. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 vs. control; means=‾X土S.

To determine whether juglone inhibits osteosarcoma cell invasion and migration, we observed that, compared with those in the control group, the scratch rate (Fig. 2A) and the number of invasive cells (Fig. 2B) were significantly reduced in a dose-dependent manner, with Juglone resulting in a scratch in the Transwell assay. Furthermore, the expression of invasion- and migration-related proteins in cells was analyzed by Western blotting. As shown in Fig. 2C, the expression of N-cadherin and vimentin was significantly reduced, whereas the expression of E-cadherin was significantly increased. These data revealed that juglone suppressed cell viability, migration and invasion in vitro in osteosarcoma.

Juglone induces pyroptosis in OS cells

Cell swelling accompanied by plasma membrane rupture is a characteristic morphological feature of pyroptosis. To evaluate whether juglone induced pyroptosis-associated morphological changes in OS cells, U2OS and MG-63 cells were examined after juglone treatment. Morphological analysis showed that juglone-treated cells displayed obvious cell swelling and large bubble-like protrusions emerging from the plasma membrane, resembling pyroptotic morphology (Fig. 3A). Scanning electron microscopy further revealed plasma membrane disruption, cell swelling, and cell lysis in both cell lines. In addition, juglone treatment significantly increased LDH release in OS cells (Fig. 3C), indicating impaired plasma membrane integrity and cytolytic cell death. These findings suggest that juglone induces pyroptosis-like morphological alterations and plasma membrane rupture in OS cells, which prompted us to further investigate the underlying gasdermin-mediated mechanism.

Juglone induces pyroptosis in OS cells via the GSDME pathway

As mentioned in the introduction, pyroptosis can be mediated by various pathways, such as the classical GSDMD pathway17, in which the N-terminus is cleaved by active caspase-1, and the GSDME pathway, in which the N-terminus is cleaved by active caspase-36. To elucidate the pathway by which juglone induces pyroptosis, we detected the key proteins of these two pathways. Our results revealed that the levels of caspase-1 and cleaved GSDMD were not elevated in the juglone-treated U2OS and Mg63 cells. However, the protein levels of caspase-3 and GSDME-N were increased significantly in both U2OS and Mg63 cells (Fig. 4A). These findings suggest that GSDME, rather than GSDMD, is involved in juglone-induced pyroptosis in OS cells.

(B) Western blot images of GSDME, GSDME-N, Caspase1 and Caspase3 in U2OS and Mg63 cells treated with juglone, with or without Z-YVAD-FMK or Z-DEVD-FMK. (C) LDH levels in the cell supernatant. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 vs. control; ‾X土S.

To further verify the above results, we pretreated U2OS and Mg63 cells with Z-YVAD-FMK and Z-DEVD-FMK, which are inhibitors of caspase-1 and caspase-3, respectively, to reduce the expression of these two proteins in the cells. The results showed that the cleavage of GSDME and the release of LDH induced by juglone were not influenced by low expression of Capase-1. In contrast, the N-terminal cleavage of GSDME and the release of LDH were significantly reduced by the downregulation of Caspase-3 expression (Fig. 4B and C).

Juglone activates the JNK/P38 pathway, promoting pyroptosis and apoptosis

Previous studies have shown that MAPK signaling pathways play a critical role in the response of tumor cells to anticancer agents18,19, JNK and p38 MAPK as its branches are involved in this process. Thus, to verify whether juglone also has such a mechanism of action in OS cells, we evaluated these proteins via western blot analysis. Both the expression of JNK and P38 phosphorylation increased following juglone treatment (Fig. 5A).

(C, D) Cell viability was determined by a CCK-8 assay. (E) LDH levels in the cell supernatant. (F) Western blot images of Bax, N-cadherin, and vimentin proteins. (G) Western blot images of cleaved caspase-3, GSDME-F and GSDME-N. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 vs. control; ‾X土S.

We subsequently treated U2OS and Mg63 cells with SP600125 or SB2035580, which are specific inhibitors of JNK and P38, respectively, with or without juglone. Western blot analysis revealed that the inhibitors significantly suppressed P-JNK and P-P38 expression (Fig. 5B). The results of the CCK-8 assay indicated that, when the cells were treated with the inhibitors, their viability increased and that their viability decreased (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, the expression levels of apoptosis- and pyroptosis-related proteins also decreased significantly (Fig. 5D and E).

Knocking out GSDME switches juglone-induced cell death from pyroptosis to apoptosis in vitro

These results demonstrate that the pyroptosis of OS cells induced by juglone is due to the cleavage of GSDME. further confirmed that GSDME plays a crucial role in juglone-induced pyroptosis in OS cells, the gsdme gene was knocked out in two osteosarcoma cell lines. Western blot analysis revealed that caspase-3 activation was not influenced by gsdme knockout, indicating that GSDME is downstream of activated caspase-3 in juglone-mediated pyroptosis (Fig. 6A).

Effects of juglone treatment on U2OS and MG63 cells with or without GSDME knockout. (A) Western blot analysis showing the protein levels of GSDME-F, GSDME-N, and cleaved caspase-3 (C-Caspase3). (B, C) Percentages of Annexin V and 7-AAD double-positive cells detected via flow cytometry. (D) Cell viability was measured via the CCK-8 assay. (E) LDH levels in the cell supernatants. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 vs. control; ‾X土S.

However, flow cytometry analysis confirmed that GSDME knockout decreased the number of Annexin V and 7-AAD double-positive cells but increased the number of Annexin V single-positive U2OS and Mg63 cells after treatment withjuglone. This indicates that GSDME knockout reduces the pyroptosis-related cell pore-breaking characteristics, thereby shifting to early apoptosis.Moreover, when treated with juglone, the viability of U2OS and Mg63cells was not affected by GSDME knockout (Fig. 6B, C,D), although LDH release was reduced (Fig. 6E). These findings indicate that the cell death induced by juglone is shifted from pyroptosis to apoptosis upon GSDME knockout. In summary, GSDME is crucial for juglone-induced pyroptosis in OS cells, whereas the knockout of GSDME should transform juglone-induced pyroptosis into apoptosis.

Discussion

Juglone, a hydroxy-naphthoquinone compound isolated from the fresh root bark20, stem bark, and green fruit peel of walnuts, has demonstrated significant anticancer effects in various studies. However, limited research has been conducted on the effect of juglone on osteosarcoma, particularly in relation to pyroptosis-related mechanisms. In this study, we treated U2OS and MG63 osteosarcoma cells with varying concentrations of juglone and observed a dose-dependent inhibition of cell proliferation, suggesting that juglone can effectively suppress osteosarcoma cell growth. Nevertheless, further research is needed to explore the relevant mechanism.

As one of the mechanism of programmed cell death.Cellular pyroptosis is distinct from apoptosis21,22. Previously, pyroptosis was recognized as caspase-1-induced monocyte death. Research has shown that Caspase-1 not only promotes the production and release of inflammatory markers such as interleukins but also cleaves GMDMD to trigger pyroptosis. Therefore, this process is also referred to as the inflammatory pyroptosis pathway23. Research has revealed that Caspase-3 not only triggers apoptosis but also cleaves GSDME to trigger pyroptosis24. However, unlike the Caspase-1-mediated pyroptosis pathway, Caspase-3-mediated pyroptosis does not promote the production of interleukins, which provides very important insight into antitumor mechanisms through pyroptosis. In recent studies, overexpression of GSDME gradually shifted apoptosis to pyroptosis, while the absence of GSDME reversed this effect. Nevertheless, some anticancer drugs have also been shown to induce both apoptosis and pyroptosis24. Additionally, certain anticancer drugs have been found to induce both apoptosis and pyroptosis simultaneously. However, whether juglone can induce pyroptosis and the relevant anticancer mechanisms in osteosarcoma remain unknown. In this study, juglone induced apoptosis in both U2OS and MG63 cells and further mediated pyroptosis through the caspase-3/GSDME pathway rather than the caspase-1/GSDMD pathway.

After GSDME knockout, programmed death gradually progresses from pyroptosis to apoptosis. These results indicate that pyroptosis and apoptosis can be converted into each other and suggest that juglone has a unique anticancer mechanism. However, the specific regulatory mechanisms involved have not been fully elucidated, and further research on potential mechanisms is needed.

MAPK plays important roles in apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, proliferation, and differentiation regulation. The p38 and JNK pathways can respond to various extracellular stimuli, including chemotherapeutic drugs. When p38 MAPK is activated, Bax will be responded to and transferred to mitochondrial plasma membrane and to regulate mitochondrial apoptosis19. The induction of apoptosis in cancer cells by various anticancer drugs occurs through the activation of MAPK25, and the P38 signaling pathway is also involved in the expression of EMT-related proteins26. In our study, the expression of phosphorylated JNK and P38 was increased by juglone. However, after the addition of inhibitors, the effects of juglone on inhibiting proliferation and invasion and promoting pyroptosis in both U2OS and Mg63 cells were reversed, which indicated that juglone could regulate the growth of osteosarcoma cells through the JNK/P8 pathway; moreover, the JNK and P38 pathways also participate together in the regulation of mitochondrial apoptosis.

In summary, our study demonstrated that juglone inhibits the growth of osteosarcoma cells in vitro. We further showed for the first time that juglone induces pyroptosis in osteosarcoma cells through the caspase-3/GSDME pathway rather than the caspase-1/GSDMD pathway. In addition, juglone-induced pyroptosis and apoptosis were mediated, at least in part, by the JNK/p38 signaling pathway. GSDME played an important role in the transition from apoptosis to pyroptosis induced by juglone. However, this study has several limitations. First, no in vivo experiments were performed; therefore, the antitumor efficacy and systemic safety of juglone remain to be further validated in animal models. Second, the present study did not evaluate the potential cytotoxic effects of juglone on normal cells, which limits the assessment of its safety. However, given its promising antitumor activity in previous preclinical studies, juglone may still hold potential for future translational development. Third, the precise upstream regulatory mechanisms responsible for activation of the caspase-3/GSDME pathway by juglone remain incompletely understood and require further investigation.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cultures

U2OS and Mg63 cells (purchased from Sigma) were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in McCoy’s 5 A medium and DMEM, respectively, containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The caspase-3-specific inhibitor Z-DEVD-FMK (20 µM, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA), the caspase-1-specific inhibitor Z-YVAD-FMK (10 µM, Abcam, Cambridge, USA), the JNK inhibitor SP600125 (40 µM, Abcam, Cambridge, USA), and the P38 inhibitor SB203580 (30 µM, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA) were purchased from Servicebio Company (Servicebio, Wuhan, China).

Generation of GSDME-knockout cells

GSDME knockout cells were established using a CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing strategy. Two sgRNAs targeting human GSDME were designed: sgRNA-1 (5′-AGCTGCCACCACCATTGCCT-3′) and sgRNA-2 (5′-GAGTTATACGTGAAACTGGA-3′). The corresponding oligonucleotides were cloned into a CRISPR/Cas9 expression vector, while a non-targeting sgRNA served as the negative control. Cells were transfected with 2 µg of plasmid DNA using Lipofectamine™ 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After selection, single-cell clones were isolated and expanded. Successful knockout of GSDME was verified by Western blot analysis and sequencing of the genomic target site.

Cell viability assay

U2OS and Mg63 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 3000 cells/well and treated with different doses of juglone for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed via the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Microscopy imaging

To examine the morphology of apoptotic and pyroptotic cells, the cells were first seeded into 35-mm culture dishes. Static bright-field images were captured using a Leica XSP-8CA microscope. Lobaplatin-induced apoptosis and pyroptosis were further examined via inverted microscopy and scanning electron microscopy (TEM) to observe pore formation activity.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay

U2OS and Mg63 cells were seeded at 3 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After the medium was removed, different drugs were added, and according to the instructions of the LDH release assay kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), the cell culture supernatants were collected for LDH release measurement.

Cell migration and invasion assays

U2OS and Mg63 cells were seeded at 2 × 108 cells/well in 6-well plates. Juglone was added to the culture medium, and the cells were cultured for 24 h. The extent of cell migration at the edge of the scratch was observed and photographed. The number of cells that crossed the scratch boundary was recorded as an indicator of cell migration ability and compared to that of the control group. This experiment was repeated at least three times.

Matrigel (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was thawed at 4 °C, and a small amount was added to the Transwell chambers for precoating. The cells were seeded in the upper chamber and cultured in serum-free medium for 24 h. Uninvaded cells in the upper layer of the filter membrane were wiped away with a cotton swab, and the invading cells in the lower layer were stained with hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) and observed under an inverted microscope.

Flow cytometry for apoptosis detection

U2OS and Mg63 cells were seeded at 5 × 104 cells/ml in 6-well plates and treated with different doses of juglone. Cells that were or were not transfected with the GSDME-KO plasmid were used for flow cytometry detection. The cells were labeled with Annexin V-FITC/PI (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and analyzed via flow cytometry to detect apoptosis.

Western blot analysis for apoptosis, invasion, migration, and pyroptosis-related proteins

The cells were lysed with PMSF (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to extract total protein. The protein concentration was measured via a BCA assay (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Proteins were separated by SDS‒PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting BAX, BCL-2, active PARP, caspase-3, cleaved-caspase-3, E-cadherin, N-cadherin, Vimentin, GSDME-F, GSDME-N, JNK, P-JNK, P38, P-P38, Cyto-C, and GAPDH. After being washed with PBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. After further washing, the proteins were detected via an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and imaged. ImageJ software was used for analysis. The antibodies used were from ZEN-BIOSCIENCE (Chengdu, China), Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA), and Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed via GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) of three independent experiments. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among multiple groups, and the least significant difference (LSD) test was used for pairwise comparisons. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
