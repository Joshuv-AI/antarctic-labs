Surgical Management and Outcomes of Carotid Body Tumors in a High-Altitude Region of Yemen: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Nabeel Yahya Al-Madhwahi, Ahmed Mohammed Sabbar, Haitham Mohammed Jowah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCarotid body tumors (CBTs) are more prevalent in high-altitude populations, possibly owing to chronic hypoxia-induced carotid body hyperplasia. This study evaluated the clinical characteristics and surgical outcomes of CBTs at a tertiary center in Sana’a, Yemen, located approximately 2,250 m above sea level.Materials and MethodsThis prospective observational study included 24 patients who underwent CBT resection between October 2023 and November 2025 at our institution. We analyzed patient demographics, tumor volume, Shamblin classification, operative details, and surgical outcomes. Factors associated with cranial nerve injury (CNI) were evaluated using Fisher exact and non-parametric tests.ResultsThe cohort had a mean age of 37.8±11.3 years, with marked female predominance (87.5%) and a high prevalence of blood group O (83.3%). Most tumors were advanced, with Shamblin class II or III tumors accounting for 91.7% of cases. External carotid artery (ECA) management, including permanent ligation in three patients and temporary clamping in one patient, was required in 16.7% of cases. The overall transient CNI rate was 29.2% (7/24), and no permanent neurological deficits or perioperative stroke occurred. Transient CNI was numerically more frequent in patients with Shamblin class III tumors than in those with Class I/II tumors (45.5% vs. 15.4%; P=0.182). Intraoperative ECA management was significantly associated with transient CNI (100.0% vs. 15.0%; P=0.003), whereas tumor volume was not significantly associated with CNI (P=0.820).ConclusionIn this cohort, surgical resection of CBTs in a high-altitude region of Yemen was feasible without preoperative embolization, with no permanent neurologic deficits or perioperative stroke. The observed high prevalence of blood group O may suggest a distinctive demographic feature in this population, although this finding requires further investigation. Shamblin class III tumors showed a numerically higher rate of transient CNI, and the need for ECA management appeared to reflect greater surgical complexity. Long-term follow-up and larger studies are needed to validate these findings.Keywords: Carotid body tumor, Paraganglioma, Altitude, Cranial nerve injuries, Yemen

INTRODUCTION

Carotid body tumors (CBTs) are rare paragangliomas that arise from the chemoreceptor cells of the carotid body at the carotid bifurcation. These benign but locally aggressive neoplasms account for less than 0.5% of head and neck tumors, with an estimated incidence of 1-2 cases per 100,000 individuals at sea level [1]. Their epidemiology shows a 2- to 4-fold female predominance, with a peak incidence in the fourth and fifth decades of life.

The development of CBTs has been closely linked to chronic hypoxia, which stimulates carotid body hyperplasia and subsequent neoplastic transformation. This pathophysiological relationship explains the markedly increased incidence of CBTs in high-altitude populations, where chronic hypoxic exposure is associated with CBT rates up to 10-fold higher than those in sea-level communities [2,3]. Populations residing at elevations above 2,000 m are therefore at increased risk, making CBTs an important clinical concern in mountainous regions.

Surgical resection remains the definitive treatment for CBTs; however, this procedure is associated with a significant risk of cranial nerve injury (CNI), particularly involving the hypoglossal (CN XII), vagus (CN X), and glossopharyngeal (CN IX) nerves [4]. Reported complication rates vary widely in the literature and range from 10% to 40%, depending on the tumor characteristics and surgical experience [5].

Despite the increased incidence of CBTs in populations residing at high altitudes, data on CBT management in high-altitude regions of developing countries remain limited. Yemen, with its high-altitude capital, Sana’a, located approximately 2,250 m above sea level, provides a relevant setting for studying the characteristics and surgical outcomes of CBTs in chronically hypoxic population. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics, surgical management strategies, and early postoperative outcomes of CBTs at a major tertiary vascular surgery center in Yemen.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1) Study design and setting

This prospective observational cohort study was conducted at the Department of Vascular Surgery, Al-Thawra Modern General Hospital, Sana’a, Yemen. Sana’a, the capital city of Yemen, is located at an altitude of approximately 2,250 m (7,380 ft) above sea level. This study enrolled consecutive patients between October 2023 and November 2025.

2) Ethical considerations

The study protocol adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Al-Thawra Modern General Hospital, Sana’a, Yemen (General Institutional Approval; IRB No. 2023-10-15). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for the surgical procedure and the use of their clinical data for research purposes.

3) Patient selection

Patients diagnosed with CBTs by Doppler ultrasonography and computed tomography (CT) angiography and scheduled for surgical resection were prospectively enrolled. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) radiologically confirmed CBT, (2) suitability for general anesthesia, and (3) consent to participate in the study. Patients with catecholamine-secreting tumors (functional paragangliomas), those managed non-surgically with radiotherapy or observation, and those with incomplete follow-up data were excluded. Catecholamine-secreting tumors were excluded to ensure a homogenous surgical cohort focused on anatomical dissection challenges and to avoid confounding by hemodynamic instability requiring preoperative alpha blockade. During the study period, no patients evaluated for CBTs at our center presented with clinically functional tumors.

4) Surgical technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation in the supine position. Preoperative embolization was not performed in any patient. A standard longitudinal cervical incision was made along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle to provide adequate exposure of the carotid triangle. The carotid sheath was opened, and the common, internal, and external carotid arteries (ECAs) were identified. Dissection was performed in the subadventitial (Gordon–Taylor) plane using bipolar cautery and fine suture ligation to minimize thermal injury to adjacent cranial nerves and separate the tumor from the carotid vessel wall while preserving arterial integrity [4,5].

Particular attention was paid to the identification and preservation of the hypoglossal (CN XII), vagus (CN X), and spinal accessory (CN XI) nerves during dissection. For advanced Shamblin class III tumors with intimate vascular encasement, specific management of the ECA was performed when necessary to facilitate safe tumor removal and protect the internal carotid artery (ICA). Given the dense adherence and vascular encasement in these advanced cases, ECA ligation was performed when standard vessel control was not feasible, with priority given to ICA and nerve preservation. In our cohort, permanent ECA ligation was performed in three cases (12.5%), and temporary ECA clamping was performed in one case (4.2%). In all cases, the ICA was preserved to maintain cerebral perfusion without shunts or bypasses.

5) Data collection and variable definitions

Data on patient demographics, including age and sex, residential altitude, clinical presentation, comorbidities, and family history were collected. Tumors were classified intraoperatively according to the Shamblin classification system as class I, localized tumors with minimal vascular attachment; class II, tumors partially surrounding the carotid vessels; and class III, tumors intimately encasing the carotid vessels [6].

Tumor volume was calculated from three-dimensional measurements obtained from preoperative CT images using the ellipsoid formula: volume=0.523×A×B×C, where A, B, and C represented the anteroposterior, transverse, and craniocaudal diameters, respectively. Postoperative outcomes included CNI, stroke, mortality, and local recurrence. CNI was classified as transient if a new postoperative deficit resolved during follow-up and as permanent if it remained at the last available follow-up.

6) Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Co.). Continuous variables, including age and tumor volume, were expressed as mean±standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on the distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Owing to the relatively small sample size, Fisher exact test was used to analyze association between categorical variables, such as Shamblin class and CNI. Intergroup differences in continuous variables (e.g., tumor volume) were assessed using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U-test or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

RESULTS

1) Patient demographics

Twenty-four patients underwent surgical resection of CBTs during the study period (Table 1). The cohort showed a marked female predominance, with 21 females (87.5%) and three males (12.5%). Most patients had blood group O (20 patients, 83.3%). Patient age ranged from 18 to 65 years, and all patients resided at a high altitude, with a mean residential elevation of 2,270±199 m above sea level.

The duration of symptoms before presentation was highly variable, with a mean of 25.0±24.9 months (median, 18 months; range, 2-132 months). The comorbidity burden was low, with hypertension in two patients (8.3%), diabetes mellitus in two patients (8.3%), and thyroid disease in five patients (20.8%). A family history of CBTs was rare and identified in one patient (4.2%).

2) Tumor characteristics

Tumor laterality was right-sided in 12 patients (50.0%), left-sided in 10 patients (41.7%), and bilateral in two patients (8.3%). The Shamblin classification showed a predominance of advanced tumors. Two patients (8.3%) had class I tumors, whereas 11 patients each had class II and class III tumors (45.8% each). Overall, class II and III tumors accounted for 91.7% of the cohort. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of paraganglioma with the characteristic Zellballen pattern in all resected specimens. No evidence of regional lymph node metastasis was observed on preoperative CT or during surgical exploration.

Tumor volume varied across Shamblin classes. The overall mean volume was 30.21±24.51 cm3 (range: 3.42-91.63 cm3). The mean tumor volume was 4.96 cm3 for class I tumors, 19.12 cm3 for class II tumors, and 45.90 cm3 for class III tumors. The Kruskal–Wallis test showed a statistically significant difference in tumor volume among the Shamblin classes (P<0.05). Adventitial invasion was identified in 19 patients (79.2%). No significant correlation was observed between altitude of residence and tumor volume (r=–0.175, P>0.05) (Table 2).

3) Surgical outcomes

Vascular management of the ECA to facilitate safe tumor excision from the carotid bifurcation was required in four patients (16.7%) with advanced Shamblin class II or III tumors. Specifically, permanent ECA ligation was performed in three patients (12.5%), and temporary ECA clamping was performed in one patient (4.2%).

CNI occurred in seven patients (29.2%). All injuries were transient, and the patients showed early postoperative functional improvement. Hypoglossal nerve (CN XII) dysfunction occurred in seven patients, including one patient who also had transient vagus nerve (CN X) dysfunction. No perioperative stroke occurred (Table 3).

A comparison of tumor volume between patients with and without CNI revealed no significant differences. The mean tumor volume was 28.23±21.76 cm3 in patients with CNI and 31.03±26.08 cm3 in those without CNI (P=0.820, Mann–Whitney U-test).

ECA ligation or control was significantly associated with transient CNI. Among the four patients who required ECA ligation or temporary clamping, CNI occurred in all four patients (100.0%). Among the 20 patients who did not require this maneuver, CNI occurred in three patients (15.0%). This difference was statistically significant (P=0.003, Fisher exact test).

4) Short-term postoperative outcomes

The mean follow-up duration from surgery to the final study assessment was 16.2±8.1 months (range, 0.7-25.4 months). During this period, seven patients (29.2%) developed early postoperative cranial nerve dysfunction; however, all patients showed functional improvement by discharge or at their first follow-up visit. No permanent neurological deficits, stroke, mortality, or local tumor recurrence were observed during the follow-up period.

DISCUSSION

This prospective study provides clinical insights into the surgical management of CBTs in Sana’a, Yemen, a high-altitude region located approximately 2,250 m above sea level. Our findings are consistent with the established pathophysiological link between chronic hypoxia and carotid body hyperplasia. Chronic hypoxic stimulation at high altitude promotes carotid body enlargement and has been associated with an increased incidence of CBTs, a phenomenon first described by Arias–Stella and Valcarcel and later reported in other high-altitude populations [2,7]. This biological relationship is further supported by experimental evidence showing that hypoxia-inducible factor 2α (HIF-2α), encoded by EPAS1, plays an essential role in carotid body development and function [8]. The high-altitude environment may have contributed to the advanced tumor characteristics observed in our cohort, in which most tumors were classified as advanced Shamblin classification (class II or III). These findings are consistent with reports from other high-altitude cohorts in the Andes and Mexico, which reported larger tumor volumes and higher proportions of advanced Shamblin classes than sea-level series [9,10].

We also observed marked female predominance, consistent with previous findings from a study conducted in Yemen in 2023 [11]. Furthermore, we identified a notably high prevalence of blood group O in our patient cohort. The 83.3% prevalence observed in our patients was higher than the reported baseline prevalence of approximately 48.0% in the general Yemeni population [12]. Although this finding is notable, it should be interpreted cautiously because of the small sample size and the absence of a matched control group. The predominance of blood group O may represent a demographic feature of this cohort or suggest a possible serological susceptibility that warrants further investigation.

A clinically important observation in this study was the relationship between tumor complexity and postoperative CNI. Transient CNI occurred more frequently in patients with Shamblin class III tumors than in those with class I/II tumors, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, intraoperative ECA management was significantly associated with transient CNI. This finding likely reflects the greater operative complexity of advanced tumors requiring ECA ligation or temporary clamping, rather than a direct causal effect of ECA management itself. All observed nerve injuries were transient, and the patients showed early signs of functional recovery during the short-term postoperative period. These findings suggest that meticulous subadventitial dissection and careful vascular control may help minimize permanent morbidity, even in advanced CBTs.

Transient hypoglossal nerve dysfunction (CN XII) was the most common neurological complication in our study. This finding is consistent with the broader CBT surgery literature, in which hypoglossal and vagus nerve dysfunction are commonly reported after CBT resection, with overall CNI rates of approximately 25%-27% and permanent deficits in 11%-14% of cases [13,14]. In our cohort, the predominant CN XII neurapraxia may be related to the cranial extension of advanced tumors and the traction required during mobilization of the superior tumor pole near the skull base, which can make the hypoglossal nerve vulnerable to temporary injury.

The diagnosis and preoperative planning of CBTs rely heavily on imaging. A comprehensive preoperative imaging work-up is recommended, including Doppler ultrasonography for initial non-invasive screening, followed by CT or magnetic resonance (MR) angiography for precise anatomical mapping and surgical planning. CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) typically demonstrate a hypervascular mass at the carotid bifurcation, with splaying of the ICA and ECA, known as the “Lyre sign”. MRI may also show a characteristic “salt-and-pepper” appearance due to flow voids [15,16]. In our practice, CT angiography was the preferred imaging modality because it provided sufficient anatomic information regarding the carotid bifurcation, tumor extent, and feeding vessels necessary for safe resection.

Recent studies have suggested that the tumor size and distance to the base of skull (DTBOS) may be important predictors of cranial nerve complications [17,18]. However, in our cohort, although tumor volume was substantial, it was not significantly associated with CNI. This finding may reflect the limited sample size and the fact that tumor volume alone does not fully represent the degree of vascular encasement or cranial extension. In the authors’ clinical practice, a large exophytic tumor is often safer to resect than a small tumor that intimately encases the carotid bifurcation. Therefore, the Shamblin classification remains a practical tool for describing operative complexity, particularly in resource-limited settings where advanced three-dimensional workstation measurements may not be routinely available [19,20].

Preoperative embolization remains controversial in the surgical management of CBTs. While some studies have advocated embolization to reduce intraoperative blood loss [21], recent meta-analyses have suggested that it does not clearly reduce CNI or perioperative stroke [22-24]. In the present cohort, acceptable early surgical outcomes were achieved without preoperative embolization, with no perioperative stroke, mortality, or persistent CNI. However, this study did not include a comparison group treated with embolization; therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence against embolization in all cases. Rather, they suggest that surgical resection without preoperative embolization can be feasible in selected patients when meticulous dissection and appropriate vascular control are performed.

The primary limitation of this study was the small sample size, which is typical for this rare pathology but limited the statistical power to detect associations between tumor characteristics and postoperative CNI. Second, this was a single-center study from a high-altitude region without a matched low-altitude control group; therefore, the effects of altitude on tumor characteristics and surgical outcomes could not be directly assessed. Third, the high prevalence of blood group O should be interpreted cautiously because no matched population-based control group was included. Fourth, DTBOS was not measured, although it has been identified as an important predictor of CNI [25]. Fifth, routine genetic testing, including SDH mutation analysis, was unavailable; therefore, hereditary or multifocal disease may have been underrecognized. Finally, advanced functional imaging (e.g., 68Ga-DOTATOC positron emission tomography-CT) was not available for screening occult multifocal or metastatic disease.

Although no persistent CNI, recurrence, or mortality was observed during follow-up, the follow-up duration remains insufficient to fully assess long-term recurrence. Langerman et al. [26] reported that the natural history of cervical paraganglioma is generally slow, whereas recent high-altitude cohort data suggest that CBTs may show faster growth than those in sea-level populations [27]. Future prospective studies in Yemen should incorporate standardized measurements, including DTBOS, longer follow-up, genetic testing, and population-based comparisons to better define the clinical behavior of CBTs in high-altitude populations.

CONCLUSION

Surgical resection of CBTs in this high-altitude region of Yemen was feasible without preoperative embolization, with no perioperative stroke, mortality or persistent CNI observed during follow-up. Patients in this Yemeni highland cohort showed a distinctive clinical profile characterized by marked female predominance and a high prevalence of blood group O. Transient CNI occurred numerically more frequently in patients with Shamblin class III tumors, and ECA management was significantly associated with transient CNI, likely reflecting the greater surgical complexity of advanced tumors. Long-term follow-up and larger population-based studies are required to validate these findings, assess late recurrence, and clarify the clinical significance of blood group O predominance in this population.
