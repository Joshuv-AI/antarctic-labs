ABSTRACT
Dehydration of biomass-derived alcohols is the rate-limiting step in alcohol-to-jet fuel production,
and competition between E1 and E2 elimination mechanisms in water determines which alkenes form
downstream. Ab initio molecular dynamics in high-temperature water is prohibitively expensive,
leaving the dominant mechanism under process conditions unresolved. We combined machine-
learned interatomic potentials with metadynamics to compute free energy surfaces of alcohol
dehydration in water. After validating the approach on ethanol and on formamide dissociation against
published results, we adopted the pretrained MACE model, which outperformed our task-specific
potential, and studied 2-butanol at 500–700 K and 0.4–1.0 g/mL along the full pathway and as six
elementary steps. The protonated alcohol intermediate is short-lived at all conditions, reaction occurs
only at the lowest density, and but-1-ene is favored at 500 K versus but-2-ene at 700 K. Solvent-
mediated E2 elimination appears favorable at 0.4 g/mL, and general-purpose potentials show promise
for modeling solution-phase reactivity.

1. INTRODUCTION
Sustainable energy technologies are developed to provide reliable and affordable energy options that
strengthen national security, support economic growth, and reduce environmental impacts. One
important piece in such energy technologies is the production of sustainable hydrocarbon fuels from
biomass. Early in the alcohol-to-jet process alcohols derived from biological sources need to be
dehydrated [1-3], a very energy-intensive process. Alcohol dehydration in biomass-relevant aqueous
media is thought to proceed via several mechanisms which lead to different process outcomes
influencing the fuel production process downstream. The competition between these different
mechanisms depends on alcohol structure, solvent details, and operating conditions.
In order to have a better understanding and thus a better control, ab initio computational studies are a
promising avenue to study the underlying reactions and their rates. Recent advances in computational
and machine learning (ML) methods have provided many of the tools needed to enable detailed
modeling studies of these systems, yet they remain computationally challenging, and dependent on
approximations. This report focuses on applying ab initio computational approaches to characterize
competing alcohol dehydration pathways in biomass-relevant aqueous environments, with the goal of
identifying the molecular factors that control reaction rates and downstream process outcomes. We
performed an ML study to advance the understanding of dehydration of bioalcohols in aqueous media.
Our goal was to improve understanding of relevant pathways under different operating conditions.
We addressed the computational challenge of ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) by using a machine
learned interatomic potential (MLIP) trained to density functional theory (DFT) data. We used the
semi-empirical method AM1 and DFT computations to explore the potential energy surface (PES)
for the alcohol and water molecules, to provide data for MLIP training. We used neural network (NN)
interatomic potentials (IAPs) based on our neural network potential energy surface (NNPES) [4, 5]
work as well as the pretrained equivariant model MACE [6-8] to run metadynamic simulation. We
used LAMMPS [9] for molecular dynamics (MD) computations, allowing coupling to our IAPs. With
this construction, we studied bioalcohol dehydration in aqueous media, starting with ethanol as a
model molecule. To sample the geometry space, we used AM1 and our own trained MLIP to run MD
simulations around the reaction pathway. Sampled structures were stored in a database and used to
train our MLIP. We used the construction to run large-scale simulations in water at various
temperatures and pressures to examine dominant dehydration pathways relevant in 2-butanol.

1.1. Mechanism of alcohol dehydration
Alcohol dehydration under aqueous conditions can occur via two different reaction mechanisms: E1
or E2. E1 is a unimolecular elimination, while E2 is a bimolecular elimination; somewhat
counterintuitively, the E1 mechanism has two (unimolecular) steps, while the E2 mechanism has just
one (bimolecular) one. Both mechanisms start with the protonation of the -OH moiety of the alcohol
from the surrounding water. The E1 mechanism forms a stabilized carbocation intermediate, which
then in a second unimolecular step loses a proton from a carbon atom that is adjacent to the one
where the OH was eliminated, leaving behind a double bond overall. The E2 mechanism differs in
that it proceeds without a stabilized intermediate, the loss of OH and H (or O(H)H and H+ from two
adjacent carbon atoms) happens in a single reaction step over a single free energy barrier. The
competition between the two mechanisms can be understood only by considering the combined
impact of entropic and enthalpic effects, and the dominance of either depends on alcohol structure,
solvent composition, and operating conditions. Importantly, the two pathways may lead to different
alkenes, influencing the fuel production process downstream. For instance, if the E1 mechanism

 9

dominates, the product branching and their cis-trans conformer ratio are correlated with the stability
of the carbocation. If the E2 mechanism is operational, then perhaps steric effects are more important.
Furthermore, which mechanism dominates is likely to be dependent on the temperature and pressure
under which the reaction takes place.

1.2. Previous theoretical studies on alcohol dehydration
Alcohol dehydration under both conventional ambient-acid and hydrothermal conditions has been
investigated using model molecules such as cyclohexanol and 4-tert-butylcyclohexanol [10-24]. Studies
in the aqueous phase [10, 13] have reported experimental measurements in a high-temperature water
(HTW) reactor, as well as estimated reaction mechanisms and kinetic rates. A complicating factor in
modeling these processes, especially under HTW conditions, is that the pressure and temperature are
near critical, where the properties of water are distinctly different from those under ambient
conditions. Predictions for these conditions are possible using theoretical techniques but are more
challenging [25]. Akiya and Savage [10] showed that for cyclohexanol, the E1 mechanism is favored
at higher temperatures and water densities. Bockisch et al. [13] showed that for secondary alcohols,
the E1 mechanism should dominate under most conditions and that chain length has no systematic
effect on the reaction rate constant. Further, dehydration processes are often intensified via catalysis.
Catalytic dehydration of alcohols has been the subject of intense study, employing combinations of
kinetic measurements, DFT computations, and AIMD [12-28].
It is clear that improved understanding of the dehydration of alcohols in bulk solvents, confined
spaces, or under catalytic conditions is a challenge that continues to motivate detailed study on
multiple fronts. In particular, detailed computational studies remain intractable without
approximations to address the cost of ab initio quantum mechanical (QM) computations of reactive
processes in liquids. Thus, AIMD is only feasible for small systems and short time scales. Examples
include QM/Molecular Mechanics and DFT/MD approaches with approximations of the coupling

 10

between the two landscapes. Alternatively, one can use (classical) MD with an IAP, typically of some
parametric form. These IAPs can be pretrained using DFT/QM data, often enriched with active
learning. Aside from their reliance on geometric descriptors, choosing a specific IAP parametric form
carries the risk of limited ability to fit a general function. More generally, one can use an NNPES, as
the IAP in MD. Such potentials can be more expensive to train given the NN construction, whose
energy/force-prediction is significantly more expensive than simpler IAPs, but it is still cost-effective
relative to AIMD, and potentially feasible for appropriately sized systems.

1.3. Goals
In this work we used MLIPs to study the dehydration reaction of 2-butanol in HTW. We used
metadynamics simulations to generate the FES of the reaction at a variety of densities for both the E1
and E2 reaction pathways to uncover which pathway is favored at the tested densities. We did this by
training an MLIP to the system in question and by using a pretrained MACE potential. We tested our
pipeline by first training a model on the ethanol dehydration reaction then created a new dataset for
2-butanol.

2. METHODS
To understand the kinetics of the dehydration reaction we first need a potential with which we can
run simulations. To this end, we tried two methods. We first trained an MLIP using the ANI method
[29] as implemented in our in-house software pykinml coupled with AEVmod [30]. We also used a
pretrained MACE potential [8]. To train our own model, we built a dataset consisting of our solute
molecule at various points along the reaction pathway submerged in water, computed the DFT
energies and forces for these structures, and used them to build our training set. This was done first
with the dehydration reaction of ethanol as a proof of concept and then using 2-butanol. To generate
the structures along the naïve, chemically inspired reaction pathway (e.g., a bond breaking is a linear
motion along a bond) we first used MD simulations with constraints to forcibly make and break the
covalent bonds needed for the reaction to occur. This was done with the semi-empirical method AM1
[31] using ORCA [32]. Once these initial simulations were run the steps from them were used to
generate an initial training set for our MLIP. Then, we began an iterative training process where we
used our trained model to run MD simulations with metadynamics to generate additional structures
for training. The MD simulations were run using LAMMPS [9] with the PLUMED plugin [33-35].

2.1. Metadynamics
To sample diverse regions of the FES, including high energy regions such as transition states, we
employed metadynamics, a method to increase the sampling of low probability events [36]. Using
conventional MD simulations, sampling these low probability events is unfeasible due to the length
of time such a simulation would need to run before having a significant chance of crossing the free
energy barriers necessary to reach unlikely structures, in some cases much longer than a human
lifespan even when using fast potential evaluations. Metadynamics biases the simulation by
sequentially placing Gaussian pseudopotentials along the free energy surface (FES). The probability P
of visiting a point 𝛿 on the FES 𝐴 is related to the free energy at that point by

                                     𝑃(𝛿) ∝ exp(−𝐴 (𝛿)⁄𝑘! 𝑇)

where kB is the Boltzmann constant and T is the temperature. This means that as we place these
pseudopotentials, we increase the free energy at that point in chemical space and make it less likely for
that point to be visited again during the simulation. Eventually, enough pseudopotentials are placed
to fill up the wells in the FES and the simulation is able to explore regions of the surface that belong
to rare events otherwise.

2.1.1. Collective Variable
In order to perform metadynamics, we needed to define a set of coordinates to track our reaction. We
defined two path collective variables, S and Z, using the method developed by Branduardi et al. [37].
S measures the progress along the reaction pathway and Z measures the distance (deviation) from the
prescribed reaction pathway.
For our choice of reaction coordinate we followed the framework of Pietrucci and Saitta [38]. This
method, implemented in the PLUMED plugin, allows us to define our reaction coordinate based on
the bonding pattern of specific atoms in the structure with all other atoms. Two matrices were created,
one for the product and one for the reactant. The rows of the matrix pertain to specific atoms within
the system. The columns describe all atoms in the system. The entry Cij in the matrix is the
coordination number between the atom in row i with all atoms of the atom type in column j. An
example matrix for the heavy atoms of an ethanol molecule is given in Table 1.

2.2. Building a dataset
Our plan to construct a dataset on which our model would be trained consisted of two main phases.
First, we built an initial dataset to train our model. This phase involved sampling from MD simulations
run using a high speed but low fidelity method. Second, we performed an iterative process of MD
sampling where we ran metadynamics simulations using our trained model, adding to our dataset and
retraining the model each iteration. All sampled structures had their energies and forces computed
using DFT at the ωB97M-V/def2-TZVPD level.

2.2.1. Initial dataset
To build our initial training set we used MD sampling to generate structures. This was first done with
ethanol then with 2-butanol as the solute. To construct an initial dataset for the dehydration of ethanol,
15 simulations were run in ORCA using AM1 with reflective boundaries at 500 K with a Berendsen
thermostat inside a box 14.4 Å on each side. Each box initially contained a single ethanol molecule,
29 water molecules, a single OH and a single H fragment, essentially a dissociated water molecule.
While point charges cannot be defined in a system like this, these fragments were meant to represent
an OH- and an H+. The H+ was placed in the vicinity of the alcohol group (~1 Å). The density of
water was chosen to be 0.3 g/mL, representative of HTW conditions. Each simulation was run for
2000 steps with a timestep of 0.25 femtoseconds. The simulations were run in three stages. First, the
positions of each of the carbons, as well as the OH group and the extra H ion were frozen for 500
steps while the rest of the atoms in the box were allowed to move freely. This was done to allow the
water molecules to relax around the ethanol. Then these constraints were removed and two new
constraints were added instead. A constraint was placed to ramp the distance between the oxygen
atom in the ethanol's alcohol group and the carbon to which it is bonded up to 2.5 times its initial
distance in order to break the C–O bond. For the second constraint, we first chose a hydrogen atom
to be "eliminated" from the ethanol. The three candidates are the hydrogens bonded to the carbon

 14

adjacent to the α-carbon.𝛼 As 15 simulations were run, each hydrogen was chosen several times. Then
a constraint was placed to shrink the distance between the OH- ion and the chosen hydrogen from its
initial distance to 1.1 Å. The simulation was then run for 1000 steps with these constraints in effect.
Then, all constraints were removed from the box and the simulation was run for 500 more steps.
Once the simulation was complete, we took the last 1500 steps, used DFT to compute their energies
and forces, and added them to our dataset. The first 500 steps were discarded.
To construct our initial dataset for the dehydration of 2-butanol we used a similar method. We used a
single 2-butanol molecule in a box with 79 waters, 1 H+, and 1 OH-. This time, 20 simulations were
run with densities randomly chosen between 0.1 and 0.9 g/mL. 10 were run with 2-butanol initially in
a cis conformation and 10 in a trans conformation. We once again used a temperature of 500 K with
a timestep of 0.25 fs and a Berendsen thermostat. The simulations were first run for 500 steps with
the 4 carbon atoms frozen in place. These constraints were then removed and a new constraint was
added to break the C-O bond of the 2-butanol. Then, one of the hydrogen atoms of the 2-butanol
was chosen to be eliminated. The chosen hydrogen was attached to either carbon 1 or carbon 3. With
5 hydrogens to choose from, each was chosen 4 times, twice for the simulations starting in cis
conformation and twice for the simulations starting in trans. A constraint was added to increase the
distance between the chosen hydrogen and the carbon it is bonded to and a second constraint to bring
the oxygen of the OH ion to a distance of 1.1 Å of the chosen hydrogen. The simulation was then run
for 1000 steps. Finally, all constraints were removed and the simulation was run for another 500 steps,
for a total of 2000 steps. The first 500 steps were again discarded and every 5th step had its energies
and forces computed using DFT and was added to our dataset.

2.2.2. Iterative sampling
For both the ethanol dehydration and 2-butanol dehydration reactions, once our first model was
trained to the DFT data, we ran metadynamic MD simulations using our trained model as the
potential. Steps from these simulations were taken and added to our dataset to retrain and improve
our model. These simulations were run using LAMMPS with the PLUMED plugin, with further details
discussed below. To track the reaction, we constructed our collective variables using the method
described in section 2.1.1. This method, implemented in the PLUMED plugin, allows us to define our
reaction coordinate based on the bonding pattern of specific atoms in the structure with all other
atoms. During sampling, only the heavy atom (not hydrogen) matrix entries whose values changed
between the two matrices were used in the calculation of S and Z.

2.2.2.1. Metadynamics sampling of ethanol
For simulations with ethanol, we generated a water box with 32 waters and a single ethanol molecule.
The simulations were run for densities between 0.3 and 0.8 g/mL in 0.1 increments. Each round of
sampling included 6 simulations, 1 at each density. To construct our path collective variables, S and
Z, two matrices were created, one for the reactant and one for the product, shown in Tables 2 and 3.
The simulations were run for 100,000 steps with a timestep of 0.25 fs. Once the simulation was
completed, we sampled from the resulting structures according to their S value in the simulation.
Because the S values were bounded between 1.0 and 2.0 by construction, we divided the steps into 10
groups, 1.0-1.1, 1.1-1.2, etc. up to 1.9-2.0. From each group we selected 12 samples chosen at random
and performed DFT calculations (see details below) on them to compute their energies and forces. A
new MLIP was then trained to the augmented dataset and we repeated the process.

2.2.2.2. Metadynamics sampling of 2-butanol
For these simulations, we generated a water box with 80 waters and a single 2-butanol molecule. The
simulations were run with a density between 0.3 and 0.8 g/mL in 0.1 increments. For each round of
sampling, we ran 24 simulations, 4 at each density with 2 simulations starting with 2-butanol in the cis
conformation and 2 starting in the trans conformation. This reaction has two possible products with
different bonding patterns, but-1-ene and but-2-ene. At each density and conformation, one
simulation used as its product matrix the matrix for but-1-ene and the other but-2-ene, shown in
Tables 5 and 6 respectively. For each simulation, only the matrix elements that change were used in
the calculation of S and Z.
From here, we followed the same sampling method used with ethanol with the same timestep and run
length. The steps were again separated by S value at the same intervals with 12 samples chosen at
random per interval. Our final dataset consisted of 8718 structures.

2.3. Code we used
To train our MLIPs, we used an in-house software we developed called pykinml, available
on GitHub (https://github.com/sandialabs/pykinml). To better accommodate the needs of this
project, we made some changes to pykinml. Firstly, the code now utilizes PyTorch’s Distributed Data
Parallel [39] feature to allow for training across multiple nodes and multiple GPUs per node. We also
implemented a lazy loading feature that greatly decreases memory usage during training to allow for
training to larger systems. We partnered pykinml with another in-house software called AEVmod

 17

(https://github.com/sandialabs/aevmod). AEVmod is a C++ code which generates the input
vectors, called atomic environment vectors (AEVs), and their Jacobians based on the parameters set
in pykinml. To better accommodate the large systems we trained our model to, we optimized
AEVmod to speed up the calculation of the AEV and its Jacobian and to allow the code to run across
multiple CPUs. In addition to training our own MLIP, we also used a pretrained MACE model to run
MD simulations with 2-butanol. MACE is an equivariant, graph neural network that uses message
passing to help capture interactions between atoms. We used the MACE-omol-0-extra-large-1024
model with the omol head. This model was trained on the OMol25 dataset at the ωB97M-V/def2-
TZVPD level.
All DFT calculations, as well as the simulation described in section 2.2.1 were performed using ORCA.
The DFT calculations were performed at the ωB97M-V/def2-TZVPD level. All metadynamic
simulations were performed using LAMMPS with the PLUMED plugin. All geometry optimizations
done by an MLIP were performed using Sella [40] and those done with DFT were done using ORCA.

3. ETHANOL
As a proof of concept, we first trained our pykinml implementation of an ANI model to simulate the
ethanol dehydration reaction. We placed a single ethanol molecule in a box with 64 water molecules
with periodic boundaries.

3.1. Free Energy Surface
To run our metadynamic MD simulations we used the method described in section 2.1. The matrices
used for our reactant (ethanol) and product (ethene) are given by Tables 2 and 3 respectively in section
2.2.2.1. For these matrices we did not include the hydrogen atoms in the rows as we did not want to
bias the simulation to remove one over the other. This was an NVT simulation run at 500 K and a
density of 0.3 g/mL. Before the metadynamics portion of the simulation we ran for 5000 steps without
metadynamics to allow the system to relax. Including these 5000 steps, the simulation was run for
105000 steps with a time step of 0.25 fs for a total of 26.25 ps. For our Gaussian pseudopotentials we
used a height of 2 kcal/mol, σs=0.02, and σz=0.06. We tested values for σs and σz with these values
reversed and with the two parameters equal at 0.03 and found that this combination allowed the best
exploration of the Z axis without compromising exploration along the reaction pathway.

The only coordination numbers included in the calculation of the collective variables during the
metadynamics simulation were the heavy atoms whose bonding patterns changed between the reactant
and the product. Figure 6 shows the FES from the simulation. Figure 7 shows the S and Z values vs
time and Figure 8 shows the values of the 4 coordination numbers that are used to calculate S vs time.
We can see that there is a deep well at approximately S=2 that corresponds to our product, ethene.
This is confirmed by Figures 7 and 8 where we see that the coordination numbers shift to the values
of our product at the same time the value of S shifts to 2. There is a smaller, shallower, well at
approximately S=1.0, Z=0.6 that corresponds to our reactant. The simulation also finds another well
at approximately S=1.7. This well corresponds to CH2CH2OH. The alcohol group left the solute first,
and one of the hydrogens attached to C1 was lost about 2 ps later, giving ethene and water. The water
molecule that formed in this reaction eventually returned to the solute to form the CH2CH2OH after
losing a hydrogen to the solvent.

 Figure 6. Free energy surface for ethanol dehydration reaction. Values in kcal/mol.

 20

Figure 8 further shows that the two bonds do not break at the same time. The C–O coordination
numbers fall to their product values at approximately 10.5 to 11 ps, whereas the C1–H coordination
number remains at its reactant value until approximately 12.7 ps. For roughly 2 ps the solute has
therefore lost its hydroxyl group while retaining all three hydrogens on C1. The reaction in this
trajectory is thus stepwise rather than concerted, following the E1 pattern described in section 1.1,
with loss of the hydroxyl group preceding loss of a proton from the adjacent carbon. Two
qualifications are important. First, the interval between the two events is measured along a biased
trajectory and is not a physical lifetime, since the metadynamics bias is actively driving the system
along S. Second, this is a single trajectory at 500 K and 0.3 g/mL, so it shows that our collective
variables can resolve a stepwise pathway rather than that E1 dominates at these conditions. The
intermediate does not appear as a separate basin in Figure 6, because S was constructed only from the
coordination numbers that change between ethanol and ethene.
The minima in Figure 6 do not lie near Z = 0, as they would if the reference frames matched the
equilibrium structures; the reactant basin sits near Z = 0.6. The reason is visible in Figure 8. The
reference matrices specify idealized integer coordination numbers, but the switching function returns
smaller values for real bonds: in the reactant region the C–H coordination number fluctuates around
2.5 rather than 3, and the two C–O entries around 0.6 and 0.3 rather than 1. The reference points
therefore correspond to configurations the system cannot reach, and both endpoints of the path lie a
finite distance from their own reference frames. This inflates Z throughout the surface and means the
path is anchored at points that are not the true minima. Measuring the reference values from short
equilibrated simulations of the reactant and product, rather than assigning integers, would remove this
offset, and we recommend it for future work.

 Figure 7. S and Z (Y axis) vs Time (X axis) for the metadynamics simulation of ethanol.

Figure 7 provides a further diagnostic. During the first 10 ps, S fluctuates across almost the whole
range from 1.0 to 1.9, while Figure 8 shows the solute to be unchanged over the same interval, with
the C–H, C–O and O–H coordination numbers all at their reactant values until approximately 10.5
ps. Large excursions in S therefore occur without any chemical change. The most likely cause is that

 21

the coordination numbers count every atom of the relevant species within the switching range,
including solvent, so that a hydrogen-bonded water contributes to the O–H entry and the arrival or
departure of water in the first solvation shell moves S even when no bond is made or broken. After
the product forms, S remains at 2.0 between approximately 12.7 and 18 ps, then returns to the range
1.0 to 1.5 once water adds back to give CH2CH2OH. Two consequences follow: bias deposited during
the early excursions is spent on solvent fluctuations rather than on reaction progress, which slows the
exploration, and the value of S alone is not sufficient to identify a structure.

4. 2-BUTANOL
Section 2 outlines the steps we took to prepare and train an MLIP to the 2-butanol PES. However,
the rise of high-quality, general-purpose foundation models, in particular the MACE models, in
combination with the large computational cost associated with constructing our own training set, has
caused us to reconsider this approach. We instead opted to use MACE-omol-0 model. The MACE
architecture has been shown to be viable for bond dissociation energy [6] as well as aqueous phase
solutions [41]. We first tested MACE on a subset of our sampled data from section 2.2. Then, to verify
the integrity of both our method and the MACE potential, we tested the potential by attempting to
recreate the work performed by Pietrucci and Saitta in which they describe the method we discussed
in section 2.1.1. We then used metadynamics to compute the FES of the 2-butanol dehydration
reaction. We looked at the FES in the gas phase then with the alcohol submerged in water at various
temperatures and densities. We also broke the full reaction into elementary steps to examine each
individually.

4.1. Verifying MACE PES
We tested MACE on a test set consisting of 10% of our dataset constructed using the method
described in section 2.2. Figures 9 and 10 compare the MACE model, shown on the left of each figure,
with our trained model on the right. MACE has a mean absolute error of 0.04 kcal/mol/atom vs 0.06
kcal/mol/atom for our trained model. For forces MACE has a mean absolute error of 0.51
kcal/mol/Å compared to 2.97 kcal/mol/Å for our trained model. For both energies and forces, the
pretrained MACE model outperformed our model. The comparison is, if anything, set up in favour
of our own model, since the test set is a 10% split of the data that model was trained on and is therefore
drawn from the same distribution, whereas MACE had seen none of these structures. That a
pretrained model still gives lower errors on both energies and forces is a stronger result than the raw
numbers alone suggest. It is likely that with additional training data we could improve our model’s
accuracy but this comes at great computational cost so we decided instead to use the pretrained model.

4.1.1. Formamide Reactions
To assess the validity of our overall methodological choice using MACE and metadynamics, we first
wanted to validate it against published results. We did this by running a metadynamics simulation using
MACE on the formamide dissociation reaction where formamide dissociates into carbon monoxide
and ammonia. This reaction was simulated by Pietrucci and Saitta in the work where they introduced
the collective variable method described in section 2.1.1. We used the same hyperparameters described
by Pietrucci and Saitta as much as we could reproduce them in LAMMPS with the PLUMED plugin.
We ran the simulation first in the gas phase then in the aqueous phase. The reaction can be written as
HC(=O)NH2 → CO + NH3

4.1.1.1. Gas-phase decomposition of formamide
We first ran a simulation of the formamide dissociation reaction in the gas phase using MACE. The
simulation started as a single formamide molecule. This structure is seen as the well on the left side of
the FES. The deepest part of the well is at approximately Z=0.1, which represents only slight
deviations from the equilibrium structure. Structures from larger Z values in this well displayed slightly
greater distortions until the molecule eventually split into NH3 and CO. The barrier of 87 kcal/mol
differs slightly from that found by Pietrucci and Saitta, who found a barrier of 79 kcal/mol. This is
likely due to the different underlying potentials used in the study. Our simulation followed the same
mechanism found by Pietrucci and Saitta where a short-lived HCONH3+ intermediate is found.

4.1.1.2. Aqueous phase decomposition of formamide
We also tested the MACE PES on the formamide dissociation reaction in the aqueous phase. The
reaction was run with a single formamide molecule and 62 water molecules. In the simulation run by
Pietrucci and Saitta, 2 different product minima were found, one containing carbon monoxide and

product minimum, containing carbon monoxide and ammonia. The well contains ammonia and

from the simulation run by Pietrucci and Saitta. As with the simulation in the gas phase, such
disagreement in the barrier is minor and is not unexpected and stems from the differences in the
underlying potential on which the simulations were run. Pietrucci and Saitta used the Perdew–Burke–
Ernzerhof exchange-correlation functional with Grimme's dispersion corrections. With this
calculation we consider our LAMMPS-MACE framework to be operational and likely valid. As with
the simulation in the gas phase, we found the same intermediate as in the study by Pietrucci and Saitta
(HCONH3+).

4.2. 2-Butanol Dehydration
Once we verified that our method with the MACE potential was able to properly model the
formamide dissociation reaction in the gas phase and find the primary product in the aqueous phase,
we then proceeded to model the 2-butanol dehydration reaction. As with formamide, we first modeled
the reaction in the gas phase. Then we ran a series of simulations to model the reaction in the aqueous
phase at a variety of temperatures and densities. Finally, we broke the reaction down into a set of
proposed elementary steps, each representing a different step along the reaction pathway to form
butene.
All metadynamics simulation in this section were run with σs=σz=0.03. We used a timestep of 0.125
fs and placed a pseudopotential with a height of 1 kcal/mol every 200 steps. We also included an
upper wall at Z=2.7 with KAPPA=400.0 EXP=2, EPS=1. We reduced the value of σz compared to
our ethanol simulation as larger values caused the simulation to be more influenced by the upper wall.
We chose 1 kcal/mol for the pseudopotential height because this smaller height helps the simulation
better imitate the true reaction pathway.

 26

4.2.1. Gas-phase dehydration reaction of 2-butanol
We first examined the reaction barrier of the dehydration reaction in the gas phase. We performed a
transition state optimization using Sella [40] for the reaction that eliminates water from 2-butanol and
forms each of but-1-ene and but-2-ene. We then computed the vibrational frequencies of the two
transition states using DFT with ωB97M-V/def2-TZVPD. The DFT calculations each had a single
imaginary frequency, confirming that each was a valid saddle point. MACE predicts a free energy
barrier for the but-1-ene reaction pathway of 55.3 kcal/mol and a barrier of 56.3 kcal/mol for the but-
2-ene reaction pathway at 500 K in the gas phase. We computed the same barriers with DFT and
found a barrier of 62.7 kcal/mol for the but-1-ene reaction and 63.6 kcal/mol for the but-2-ene
reaction. MACE underestimates each barrier but predicts the difference between the two barriers is
1.0 kcal/mol compared to 0.9 kcal/mol from DFT. The barriers each include zero-point energy
corrections. The difference between the MACE and DFT results is caused almost entirely by
differences in the prediction of entropy of the transition state. MACE and DFT disagree on the free
energy of 2-butanol by only 0.05 kcal/mol and the barrier heights when looking at only the enthalpy
are 62.1 and 64.7 for DFT for the 1-ene and 2-ene transitions respectively and 63.9 and 65.3 for
MACE.

After the transition state optimization, we ran a metadynamics simulation in the gas phase. The FES
from this simulation is shown in Figure 14. We included in our calculation of S and Z the C2O, OH,
and OC coordination numbers. The simulation explored the FES before finding the product, but-2-
ene. The barrier for this reaction was 48.0 kcal/mol, lower than the barrier from the transition state

4.3. Conclusions
Our results indicate that the protonated alcohol that serves as the first step when following either E1
or E2 mechanism is a short-lived species, making it difficult to model. The barrier from the search
targeting but-1-ene decreased substantially with increased temperature, from 39.1 to 33.5 kcal/mol,
while the search targeting but-2-ene showed an increase, from 32.3 to 34.5 kcal/mol. These are barriers
for a targeted search rather than for a single transformation: at 700 K the search targeting but-1-ene
in fact produced but-2-ene, so the two entries in that column do not describe the same reaction. The
search for either product had the same barrier at both temperatures but found different products, but-
1-ene at the lower temperature and but-2-ene at the higher.
When we searched for the full reaction pathway, none of the simulations included water as a reactant.
Instead, the aqueous conditions greatly reduced the reaction barrier allowing the product to form via
the same mechanism used in the gas phase, where the hydrogen used to trigger the release of the
alcohol group comes from the 2-butanol itself. Crucially, the protonated alcohol, seen in box two of
Figure 18, is not stable at any of the conditions tested. When simulating the full reaction pathway, no
product formed at 0.7 g/mL or 1.0 g/mL within the simulation time, although we cannot say whether
this reflects a genuinely slower reaction at high density or a loss of driving efficiency in the collective
variable. In the searches that did not target a specific product, but-1-ene formed at 500 K and but-2-
ene at 700 K. We base the selectivity on those runs alone, because each targeted search uses a different
collective variable and the resulting barriers are not directly comparable: at 500 K the search targeting
but-2-ene gave a lower barrier than the one targeting but-1-ene, even though the untargeted search at
that temperature produced but-1-ene.
When we examined the hypothesized elementary steps, we found that in two instances the product
was formed by a reaction with the water solvent: reaction 1 at 0.4 g/mL and reaction 3 at 0.7 g/mL.
Reaction 3, however, started with a hydrogen in close proximity to the solute. Although the overall
system was neutral because of an extra OH- ion, this hydrogen starting near the solute simulated an
acidic local environment for the solute. This simulation proceeded via an E1 mechanism to form but-
2-ene. However, at a density of 0.4 g/mL the reaction followed the gas phase mechanism. The barriers
for reaction 3 were similar to one another despite these different mechanisms. In reaction 1, the
reaction proceeded via the E2 mechanism to form but-1-ene. The barrier for reaction 1 was lower
than for reaction 3 at either density, suggesting that the E2 mechanism may be favorable at a density
of 0.4 g/mL and 500 K, but further study is needed to confirm this. The comparison is weakened by
the fact that the two reactions were run along different collective variables, and that the coordinate
used for reaction 1 does not separate the protonated alcohol from the eliminated product.
Further work remains to be done for a task specific MLIP on this system, but we believe the sampling
method we demonstrated can be used to construct an adequate data set for such a task. Still, MACE
was able to demonstrate qualitatively similar results to published results on the formamide system and
closely matched the difference between the barrier heights predicted by DFT for the gas phase 2-
butanol dehydration reaction, lending credence to general purpose MLIPs for studying alcohol
dehydration. The barriers taken from the metadynamics surfaces are best understood as the bias
required for a first escape from the reactant basin, rather than as converged free energies of activation.
Under our conditions the dehydration products are strongly favoured, so the reverse reaction is rare,
the barrier region is visited once, and continued deposition of bias eventually drives the system out
along an unintended coordinate. We observed the latter twice, in reaction 4 at 0.4 g/mL, where all C–
C bonds broke, and at 0.7 g/mL and 700 K, where the bias drove the solute apart into CH3CH2OH
and CH2CHOH rather than to an alkene. Barriers obtained this way are unconverged and can err in
either direction: for the gas-phase dehydration, metadynamics gave 48.0 kcal/mol for the formation

 37

of but-2-ene where transition state optimization on the same MACE surface gives 56.3 kcal/mol,
whereas for gas-phase formamide our value of 87 kcal/mol lies above the 79 kcal/mol of Pietrucci
and Saitta, who in turn reproduce the 80.5 kcal/mol of a zero-temperature CCSD(T)/CBS calculation.
The contrast with that study is instructive. Formamide and CO + NH3 differ in stability by less than
1 kcal/mol, so both basins are populated and recrossing is frequent: a single 170 ps gas-phase
trajectory there explored seven forward and seven backward reactions, and the resulting surface
reproduces the CCSD(T) barrier to within 1.5 kcal/mol. That work also used 300 K and liquid density
in cells of 96 to 192 atoms, repeated every simulation at least three times with different Gaussian sizes
and deposition rates, and validated transition states by committor analysis, quoting a precision of ±2
kcal/mol. Our dehydration reactions are strongly downhill at 500 to 700 K and were run once per
condition, which is why comparable convergence was not reached.
The results appear to be sensitive to the choice of collective variable, particularly which coordination
numbers are included in the S and Z calculations. Three cases in the elementary-step simulations
illustrate the cost of a reduced coordinate. In reaction 1 the only included entry, the O–H coordination
number, takes the same value for the protonated alcohol and for the water released alongside but-1-
ene, so the intended endpoint and the final product are indistinguishable. In reaction 2 the entries that
separate 1,3-butadiene from but-2-ene, C1–H and C4–H, were not included, and a structure that
matched the product in the reduced coordinate was not the intended product. In reaction 6 the contact
map is defined on specific atom indices and is not invariant to the symmetry of the solute, so forming
the double bond at the equivalent end of the chain displaced the minimum in Z even though the
molecule was unchanged. The choice of entries therefore determines not only how efficiently the
surface is explored but also which structures the simulation can tell apart, and we recommend that
future work confirm the identity of every minimum directly from the trajectory rather than from the
value of S alone. Moving forward, we believe further study of the individual reaction steps can help
lend insight to the true reaction mechanism, especially regarding reaction 1.
Rate coefficients follow from the gas-phase barriers through conventional transition state theory, k =
(kBT/h)·exp(−ΔG‡/RT), with the universal Eyring prefactor kBT/h equal to 1.0 × 1013 s−1 at 500 K.
For the formation of but-1-ene in the gas phase this gives 4 × 10−15 s−1 from the DFT barrier of 62.7
kcal/mol and 7 × 10−12 s−1 from the MACE barrier of 55.3 kcal/mol. The 7.4 kcal/mol difference
between the two potentials is therefore a factor of about 1700 in the rate, and at this temperature every
1 kcal/mol of error in ΔG‡ corresponds to a factor of 2.7. This sets the accuracy a potential must
reach before it can be used for kinetics rather than for mechanism. We do not quote rate coefficients
from the aqueous simulations, because the barriers obtained there are first-escape values rather than
converged free energies of activation. Obtaining them would require a method built for one-way
reactions, such as infrequent metadynamics or a weighted-ensemble treatment. We note also that steps
involving the solvent, such as reaction 1, would yield pseudo-first-order coefficients that fold in the
water density and are not directly comparable with the unimolecular steps.
