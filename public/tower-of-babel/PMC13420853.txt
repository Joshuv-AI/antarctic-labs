Online learning resource preferences across learning stages: a cognitive load theory perspective in UK medical students in early years
Authors: Yizhen Wang, Xiaomeng Zhang, Chao Wang, Xiaoming Xing, Vrinda Nayak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOnline learning resources (OLRs) have become an important part of the education for students in the early years of the medicine programme (Years 1–2) in the UK. There are a variety of OLRs. However, an excessive amount of resources can impose a significant cognitive load. Students selectively use OLRs to avoid cognitive load, forming their preferences for OLRs. When students use OLRs before and after class, as well as before exams, these distinct learning stages involve different learning objectives and challenges. Thus, students’ preferences for OLRs may also differ. This study aims to identify how OLRs preferences change across different learning stages (pre-class, post-class, and pre-exam), enabling educators to provide more targeted OLRs support to learners based on an understanding of their cognitive needs. MethodsThis cross-sectional study used a self-administered questionnaire. Participants were early-year medicine undergraduates (Years 1–2) at the University of Exeter. The questionnaire included Likert scales and open-ended questions. To examine undergraduates’ OLRs preferences and attitudes, one- and two-way repeated-measures ANOVA analyses were conducted. Qualitative analysis of open-ended responses utilized a framework combining Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis with Krippendorff’s principles of content validity.ResultsThe most preferred OLRs vary across different learning stages. The highest-scoring OLRs were interactive videos (3.16 ± 0.86, out of 4) in the pre-class stage, optional practice questions (3.65 ± 0.71, out of 4) in the post-class stage, and online mock exams (3.89 ± 0.38, out of 4) in the pre-exam stage. Personal study habits, content value, time cost, and resource delivery are crucial factors that influence learners’ preferences.ConclusionsLearners’ preferences for OLRs vary across different stages of the learning process. Understanding these patterns may help educators better align the design and delivery of OLRs with students’ perceived needs. For example, providing interactive, vivid, and clearly structured materials during early learning stages and incorporating practice questions or self-assessment opportunities after classes and before examinations may better support learners’ preparation and consolidation. While these findings reflect learners’ reported preferences rather than objective measures of learning effectiveness, they offer insights that may inform the development of stage-appropriate OLRs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09694-7.Keywords: Online learning resources, Preference, Cognitive load theory, Learning stage, Early-Year medical undergraduates

Background

In the UK, students in the early years of the medicine programme (Years 1–2) mainly focus on learning essential knowledge. Because clinical medicine is a knowledge-intensive programme, it covers much content that needs to be understood, memorized, and applied [1]. Therefore, courses delivering content only face-to-face not only easily lead to cognitive overload but also cannot adapt to students’ personalized learning needs [2]. As an essential supplement to face-to-face teaching, online learning resources (OLRs) can provide students with continuous learning support [3]. Medical students use multiple digital resources for study and exam preparation [4, 5]. Common OLRs include videos, slides, readings, question banks, and self-testing tools [6–10]. In educational practice, the curation of course-related OLRs remains predominantly instructor-led. However, studies indicate that learners’ resource selection is highly dependent on peer recommendations, far more than on instructor recommendations [7, 10]. Therefore, understanding learners’ actual OLR preferences is necessary for course designers to incorporate learner perspectives and deliver knowledge according to their cognitive needs.

Cognitive load theory (CLT) is a psychological framework that examines the patterns of human learning processes and the characteristics of cognitive structures [11]. When acquiring new knowledge, all incoming information must pass through working memory (WM), which has a highly limited capacity [12], before it can be understood and encoded into long-term memory. Once the amount of information exceeds this capacity, the WM becomes overloaded, ultimately impairing learning efficiency [13]. An excessive number of OLRs may impose substantial strain on WM leading to cognitive overload. When instructor-recommended OLRs do not align with learners’ needs, students are likely to perceive them as additional tasks that increase cognitive load. CLT distinguishes three types of load: intrinsic load (IL), determined by content complexity; extraneous load (EL), generated by poor instructional design; and germane load (GL), devoted to schema construction and deep processing [14]. IL reflects the inherent complexity of the content [14]. EL arises from how information is presented—poorly structured slides, for example, or unclear navigation [14]. GL is the cognitive effort actually invested in learning [14]. Ideally, OLRs should reduce EL, keep IL manageable, and free up capacity for GL.

CLT provides a solid framework for designing OLRs. However, most existing research on learner resource selection treats learning as a holistic process, neglecting its phased characteristics. This is particularly crucial in learning contexts that are inherently phased, such as preclinical medical education. As students progress through stages such as pre-class (knowledge acquisition), post-class (consolidation), and pre-exam (preparation), their prior knowledge and cognitive goals change, posing different cognitive challenges [13]. Therefore, their strategies for managing cognitive load - as well as their preferences for different OLRs (such as videos, slides, texts, or quizzes) - are likely to vary across stages. However, empirical evidence linking these dynamic preferences to specific learning stages is still limited.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the stage-specific OLRs preferences of preclinical medical students and to explain these preferences through the lens of CLT. The research findings are expected to provide a more precise understanding of preclinical learners’ online learning behavior, offering evidence-based guidance for curriculum designers to provide appropriate OLRs support at different learning stages. These findings also have reference value for international colleagues committed to improving educational practice.

Methods

Study design

This is a cross-sectional study. The research employed a quantitative data collection method using a questionnaire. Data collection was conducted through Microsoft Forms. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Exeter (Research Ethics Committee approval reference: 148/24/04/04). Ethical compliance was ensured through instructor-approved pre/post-session briefings and QR-based anonymized data collection. All participants provided informed consent electronically prior to accessing the questionnaire. The full questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Questionnaire design, distribution, and collection

To ensure the validity and reliability of the questionnaire, six medicine undergraduates from years 3 to 5 were recruited to complete the pilot stage. All participants gave positive feedback on the content and design. The supervisor and the ethics team reviewed the content validity of the questionnaire. The formally released questionnaire was modified according to the opinions of the supervisor and the ethics team. The entire research process strictly adhered to the requirements of ethical approval.

There were three types of questions. The first part was the demographics section. The second part of the questionnaire consisted of items using two types of Likert scales. The first type assessed learners’ attitudes towards OLRs at different learning stages, using a 5-point scale ranging from very useful [5] to not at all useful [1], with a midpoint of 3 representing a neutral response. The second type assessed learners’ preferences for different OLRs, using a scale ranging from most preferred to not at all preferred. To minimise bias from unfamiliar resources, an additional “not used” option was included. For analysis, only the four preference levels were scored from 1 to 4. The final part consisted of 4 open-ended questions exploring reasons for not preferring certain OLRs and inviting participants to recommend additional resources. These responses supplemented the resource types included in the Likert-scale items.

Participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at a health sciences campus of the University of Exeter in the UK, from May 9 to June 9, 2024. This period corresponded to the late teaching and revision phase of the academic year, shortly before end-of-year examinations. The participants were students in the early years of the medicine programme (Year 1 and Year 2), which follows an integrated, systems-based curriculum with multiple subjects delivered across the academic year. The survey referred to students’ overall use of OLRs across their courses.

Data analysis

Quantitative data from the Likert scale were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29) software (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). Figures were created using GraphPad Prism (Version 10, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were analysed using parametric statistical methods. The characteristics of the participants were summarised and described using descriptive statistics. Frequencies and percentages were used to describe categorical variables. Mean and standard deviation (SD) are used to describe continuous variables. One- and two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with post-hoc pairwise comparisons was used to determine whether there were significant differences between groups. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The qualitative data from open-ended questions were examined using a combined thematic and content analysis framework, guided by established Braun and Clarke’s reflective thematic analysis and Krippendorff’s principles of content validity [15–17]. A hybrid manual and Excel-based approach was employed to facilitate in-depth engagement with the focused dataset. Coding followed a deductive-inductive strategy, beginning with researcher-informed categories while remaining open to emergent themes. To ensure reliability, both researchers independently coded a 20% sample, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion and reference to the original text to refine the coding framework. The first author then completed the coding of the remaining data, with ongoing consultation on ambiguous cases. Related codes were subsequently clustered into core themes, which were iteratively reviewed and refined against the full dataset to ensure interpretive coherence.

Results

Demographics

This study was conducted among 452 Year 1 and Year 2 medicine undergraduate students at the University of Exeter. A total of 139 responses were received, yielding a response rate of 30.8%. By year group, the response rate was 39.4% (91 out of 231) for Year 1 and 21.7% (48 out of 221) for Year 2. Of these, 91 (65.5%) were Year 1 students and 48 (34.5%) were Year 2 students. In terms of age at BMBS medicine porgramme enrolment, 112 participants (80.6%) were under 21 years old, while 27 (19.4%) were aged 21 or above.

Participant attitudes towards OLRs

Participants were surveyed on their perceptions of the usefulness of the OLRs provided by the course (Supplementary File 1, Q4). A one-way ANOVA was conducted to compare mean scores across the three learning stages. The results showed that the mean score (out of 5) for pre-class preparation, post-class revision stage and the pre-exam preparation stage were 3.17 ± 1.05, 3.55 ± 1.09 and 3.65 ± 1.23. As shown in Fig. 1A, participants found the OLRs more useful after class and before exams than before class (pre-class vs. post-class, p = 0.0012; pre-class vs. pre-exam, p = 0.0138). As shown in Fig. 1B, participants’ intention to use their preferred OLRs was high across all three stages (pre-class: 3.95 ± 1.07; post-class: 4.33 ± 0.83; pre-exam: 4.74,±0.54). This indicates that participants agreed to use their preferred OLRs for learning, with the strongest agreement observed in the pre-exam stage. At each learning stage, participants’ intention to use their preferred OLRs score was significantly higher than their perceived usefulness of the course-provided OLRs.

Preferences for OLRs at different learning stages

This study investigated the differences in participants’ preferences for different types of OLRs at three different learning stages.

Pre-class preparation stage

As shown in Table 1, during the pre-class preparation stage, the OLRs that scored higher were interactive videos (3.16 ± 0.86), slides (3.07 ± 0.85), and animated videos (3.05 ± 0.82). Interactive videos had the highest mean, while the mean for the slides and animated videos, were not statistically different from those of interactive videos. This suggests that participants preferred these three resources at this stage. Participants held a neutral attitude toward multiple short lecture videos. The remaining resources received low scores, suggesting that participants did not favor them. In the open-ended question (Supplementary File 1, Q10), 22 (15.8%) mentioned the ‘video’ resource (Supplementary File 2, Fig. 1). Additionally, 18 (12.9%) mentioned the ‘question to test their knowledge’ resource during the pre-class stage (Supplementary File 2, Fig. 1). Participants recommended some specific websites or apps for all learning stage (Table 2). A brief introduction to these resources is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Post-class revision stage

During the post-class revision stage (Table 1), participants preferred optional practice questions (3.65 ± 0.71) and slides (3.32 ± 0.80). By contrast, extra reading material, reading lists, and discussion platforms were rated lower, and the remaining resources showed neutral preference. Among these participants, some recommended specific websites or apps for testing their knowledge, including PassMedicine, Anki Flashcard, Quesmed, and Quizzes (Table 2). In addition, among those who recommended this type of OLRs, 7.2% (10 of 139) recommended mock exam questions (Supplementary File 2, Fig. 2).

Pre-exam preparation stage

As shown in Table 1, during the pre-exam preparation stage, online mock exams (3.89 ± 0.38) and optional practice questions (3.80 ± 0.56) were the higher-scoring resources. Extra reading material (2.02 ± 0.82) and reading lists (2.01 ± 0.90) received the lower scores. All remaining resources, including slides (3.28 ± 0.84), interactive videos (3.04 ± 0.87), animated videos (2.90 ± 0.96), multiple short lecture videos (2.66 ± 0.89), e-textbooks (2.34 ± 0.88), discussion platforms (2.24 ± 0.95), and full-length lecture videos (2.41 ± 0.92), showed intermediate preferences. In the open-ended questions (Supplementary File 1, Q12), most participants still mentioned the ‘Question to test their knowledge’ resource, accounting for 53.2% (Supplementary File 2, Fig. 3). Participants directly recommended specific websites or apps for testing their knowledge, including PassMedicine, Anki Flashcards, Quesmed and Applied Medical Knowledge (Table 2).

Compared to the pre-class preparation stage, slides, e-textbooks, and reading lists showed statistically significant improvements in the post-class revision stage. In the pre-exam preparation stage, animated and interactive videos declined, whereas slides continued to increase relative to pre-class. Extra reading material increased from pre-class to post-class but declined in the pre-exam stage. Full-length lecture videos, e-textbooks, extra reading material, reading lists, and discussion platforms received consistently low scores across all three stages. There were no statistically significant differences in scores for other resources across the three learning stages.

Discussion

This study revealed that early-year medical students at the University of Exeter demonstrated dynamic preferences for OLRs across different learning stages. Specifically, interactive video was most preferred in the pre-class stage, optional practice questions in the post-class stage, and online mock exams in the pre-exam stage, while several resources (e.g., reading list, extra reading material, discussion platforms) received consistently low ratings across all stages. These findings suggest that students adapt their OLRs choices to stage-specific learning demands. To interpret this pattern, we draw on CLT as a theoretical lens.

Preferences for OLRs in different learning stages

Pre-class preparation stage – reduce external cognitive load and build initial knowledge connections

This study found that learners preferred three types of resources in the pre-class stage: interactive videos, slides and animated videos. This finding is similar to those of Sharmin et al. and Boyatzis et al., who found that those OLRs reduce distractions and help novice learners establish initial connections between concepts [18–20]. This aligns with CLT, which states that beginners have limited WM to process new information. Reducing EL from unnecessary details and focusing on GL that supports learning can help prevent cognitive overload [21].

Interactive videos can effectively reduce EL and support the formation of GL. According to Mayer and Moreno’s dual-channel hypothesis, using both visual and verbal channels for learning can prevent overload in a single channel, ultimately reducing the overall EL [22]. Regarding multiple short lecture videos or full lecture videos, although these resources also involve visual and verbal channels, they differ from highly preferred video resources in other important dimensions such as segmentation granularity, interactivity, and visual information density [23]. Previous research suggests that interactive videos create a self-regulated learning environment where learners feel less anxious and can absorb new content more easily [24]. Unlike passive video watching, interactive videos engage learners actively, with embedded questions prompting deeper thinking and helping them build connections between concepts. Learner preference ratings confirm that interactive videos are the most favored resource in the pre-class stage, highlighting their role in fostering GL. Animated videos help medical students better understand complex and dynamic medical concepts by visually illustrating processes and relationships, thereby improving comprehension and learning outcomes [25]. Although the slides lacked an audio track, they still received high ratings at the time. This suggests that factors such as clear structure, concise presentation, and learner-controlled pacing may play an important role for beginners in the pre-lesson stage [26].

Post-class review stage - focus on knowledge integration and avoid redundant information

In the post-class review stage, learners’ cognitive load is mainly driven by knowledge integration, not by acquiring new information. IL is stable, while GL becomes more important [27]. To support integration, strategies should minimize EL by focusing on core concepts and relationships. The goal is to enhance “schema automation” [14]. Practice questions, clear slides, and concise videos help organize complex knowledge, reducing the burden on WM and improving retrieval [28].

Learners rated two resources highly in the post-class stage. The most preferred resource was optional practice questions. Medical knowledge often involves many independent points and causal relationships, and learners need to integrate this knowledge into long-term memory [24, 29]. Studies show that exercises aid long-term retention more effectively than simply rereading information [29]. Therefore, optional practical questions help learners practice and deepen their knowledge integration. Slides ranked second in preference. They clearly outline course content and serve two purposes in post-class review: aiding recall and connecting knowledge points through the instructor’s logic. CLT suggests that meaningful knowledge units reduce cognitive load and improve comprehension [2]. Thus, the review stage should organize fragmented knowledge into a coherent structure, reducing WM load and improving retrieval efficiency.

Pre-exam preparation stage - optimizing cognitive strategies and simulating stressful situations

During the pre-exam preparation stage, online mock exams and optional practice questions were rated very highly, aligning with their recommendations. Both resources fall under “questions that test knowledge”, playing a crucial role in the test effect [4, 30, 31]. Studies show that the test effect enhances long-term memory [32], helping learners consolidate knowledge and assess their review progress. Mock exams not only reinforce knowledge but also help learners adjust to exam pressure.

Optimizing cognitive strategies is essential for effective exam preparation. Active recall and test practice significantly improve knowledge retrieval, outperforming simple rereading [33]. Additionally, a meta-analysis by Baker et al. of 48 studies found that slides help focus attention, improve understanding, and support exam review [34]. This suggests that combining test exercises with structured resources, such as slides, enhances review efficiency.

Significant factors influencing the preferences of OLRs

Four factors affect learners’ non-use of OLRs: personal study habits, content value, time cost, and resource delivery. These factors were identified through coding, content analysis, and thematic analysis of survey responses. In terms of personal study habits, our results indicate that learners’ resource usage habits and knowledge acquisition practices affect their preferences for OLRs. In the post-class review stage and the pre-exam stage, many participants recommended the PassMedicine website. The study by Deng et al. found that the habit of using online learning resources will be relatively stable after it is formed [35]. In the knowledge acquisition aspect, different OLRs transmit information differently. Some OLRs transmit information in a one-way manner, like lecture videos, while others engage learners interactively, such as through interactive videos and optional practice questions, encouraging active participation and providing instant feedback. From the perspective of constructivism, learners actively construct their knowledge system by combining their knowledge and experience [36]. The results indicate that learners prefer OLRs that support active learning. This aligns with Brandt’s view that constructivism should be the foundation of online learning [37]. Building on these perspectives, providing OLRs that promote active thinking can effectively support learners in constructing their knowledge systems.

In addition to learners’ learning habits, the content value of OLRs also affect learners’ preferences. Learners are only inclined to use this type of OLRs if the relevance is high. Students’ resource selection and use are driven much more by assessment needs than by exploring for understanding [6]. When learners perceive that OLRs are not closely related to the course content or formal assessments, they view these resources as low-yield and not meeting their immediate learning needs. This may also help explain why slides received relatively high ratings across all three learning stages, as they were closely aligned with the course content. And the time required to complete the resource is also a critical consideration. On the one hand, learners have limited free time at their disposal. On the other hand, learners have varying attention spans for different resources. Learners can maintain their attention on video resources for six minutes [38]. Moreover, adding interactive elements to videos can increase learners’ engagement time by at least 20% [39]. Therefore, providing different content lengths for various types of resources is also a factor that must be considered.The final factor to consider is resource delivery: clear and straightforward presentation of course materials encourages learner engagement, which may be one reason why slides were consistently well rated. Addressing this during course design can minimize technical barriers and ensure that OLRs better align with learners’ preferences and needs.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the findings primarily reflect the characteristics of Year 1 students—a majority of participants of this study. In addition, non-response bias cannot be excluded, as students with greater engagement in OLR use may have been more likely to participate in the survey. However, participant responses showed varying levels of OLR use and preference across different learning stages. Second, this research examined preferences rather than their direct link to academic performance. Understanding stage-specific preferences is valuable in its own right, as it enables curriculum designers to select resources that students are more likely to engage with, thereby avoiding investment in well-designed but underutilised tools. However, preference does not necessarily equate to pedagogical effectiveness. Future research should build on these findings by exploring whether preference-aligned OLRs contribute to measurable improvements in learning outcomes. Finally, the questionnaire’s predefined categories did not capture all resources mentioned qualitatively—a limitation that was addressed by incorporating findings from the open-ended questions.

Conclusion

This study reveals that pre-clinical medicine undergraduates’ OLRs preferences vary across three learning stages. Learners most preferred OLRs are different at each stage. Additionally, individual learning strategies, content value, time efficiency, and resource accessibility are crucial factors influencing their choices. This research provides evidence and practical recommendations for optimising OLRs for pre-clinical medicine undergraduates in the UK. Teachers can provide targeted support for the OLRs that students most want at each stage while also considering those factors that influence preferences to a certain extent. This finding offers valuable insights for international readers committed to improving educational practice.
