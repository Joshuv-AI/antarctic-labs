Universal dualities for Wilson loops in lattice Yang–Mills
Thibaut Lemoine

Abstract
We identify a universal finite- N N structure underlying Wilson loop expectations in lattice Yang–Mills, in any dimension d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , for gauge group U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) , and for arbitrary smooth central plaquette actions. The starting point is a state-sum expansion in plaquette labels by irreducible representations, in which each term factorizes into an action-dependent spectral weight and an action-independent topological coefficient. We then analyze these coefficients in three exact ways: as a gauge/string expansion over decorated spanning surfaces, as a local spin-foam/channel model on the dual incidence graph, and as a universal finite- N N master loop equation that closes on the coefficient side. As a consequence, several recent Wilson-action results are recovered as specializations of our broader action-agnostic framework.

The main contribution of this paper is to identify a

The starting point is a spectral/topological splitting: after Fourier expansion of the plaquette action, Wilson loop expectations become sums over plaquette labels, with action-dependent spectral weights and action-independent topological coefficients. We then show that these coefficients unravel two complementary kinds of dualities: a gauge/string duality on a global geometric side, and a spin-foam duality on a local representation-theoretic side. We will also prove that they satisfy a kind of Schwinger–Dyson equation, the

In this way, surface sums, spin-foam-type dual models, and master loop equations appear as three manifestations of a single universal formalism that was previously understood for the Wilson action only. The conceptual point is the following: once the spectral/topological splitting is isolated, surface expansions, local dual models, and loop equations all emerge from the same action-independent topological coefficients, and the Wilson-action existing results are just a specialization of this universal formalism.

1.2
A state-sum expansion

The starting point of this framework is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(State-sum representation)
.

Let
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
be a finite lattice, with oriented edge set
E(\Lambda)
and oriented plaquette set
P(\Lambda)
. Let
U=(U_{e})_{e\in E(\Lambda)}
be a random configuration with distribution
\mu_{\Lambda,Q}
. Assume that
(Q_{t})_{t>0}
is a family of smooth central actions. For any family
{\mathcal{L}}=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{k})
in the 1-skeleton of
\Lambda
, we have

(3)

\mathbb{E}\left[W_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(U)\right]=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{G}}\kappa_{\Lambda,Q}(\alpha)\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha),

where

(4)

\kappa_{\Lambda,Q}(\alpha)=\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\langle Q_{\beta_{p}},\chi_{\alpha_{p}}\rangle,\qquad\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)=\int_{G^{E(\Lambda)}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})\right)\left(\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{p})\right)dU.

Simply stated, all Wilson loop expectations are sums over
plaquette decorations

\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{G}
, with a product of two terms:

–

A
spectral coefficient

\kappa_{\Lambda,Q}(\alpha)
, which depends on the action and the area/coupling but not on the loops,

–

A
topological coefficient

\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
that depends on the loops but not on the area/coupling.

This result is so simple that we prove it right now.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We start with

\mathbb{E}[W_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(U)]=\frac{1}{Z}\int_{G^{E(\Lambda)}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})\right)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}Q_{\beta_{p}}(U_{p})dU,

assuming that
G
is a compact subgroup of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
for some integer

Q_{\beta_{p}}(x)=\sum_{\alpha_{p}\in\widehat{G}}\langle Q_{\beta_{p}},\chi_{\alpha_{p}}\rangle\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(x).

Note that, because
Q_{\beta_{p}}
is smooth, this expansion is absolutely and uniformly convergent, and we have

\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{G}}d_{\lambda}\big|\langle Q_{\beta_{p}},\chi_{\lambda}\rangle\big|<\infty,

where

is the dimension of the irreducible representation of
G

\lambda

\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})\leq N
for all

\sum_{\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{G}}\left|W_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(U)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\langle Q_{\beta_{p}},\chi_{\alpha_{p}}\rangle\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})\right|\leq N^{k}\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{G}}d_{\lambda}\big|\langle Q_{\beta_{p}},\chi_{\lambda}\rangle\big|,

so that we can exchange the sum and integral, giving

\mathbb{E}[W_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(U)]=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{G}}\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\langle Q_{\beta_{p}},\chi_{\alpha_{p}}\rangle\int_{G^{E(\Lambda)}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})\right)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{p})dU.

The theorem follows from a simple identification of the coefficients.

1.3
Gauge/string duality

The first consequence of Theorem
1.1
is that the Wilson loop expectations can be rewritten in terms of surface-sums.

Theorem 1.2

(Topological expansion)
.

Let
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
be a finite lattice, with oriented edge set
E(\Lambda)
and oriented plaquette set
P(\Lambda)
. Let
U
be a random configuration with distribution
\mu_{\Lambda,Q}
. For any family of loops
{\mathcal{L}}=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{k})
in the 1-skeleton of
\Lambda

(6)

\mathbb{E}[W_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(U)]=\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda,Q}\left[\sum_{\Sigma\in S_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)}\Omega_{N}(\Sigma;\alpha)N^{\chi(\Sigma)-h(\Sigma)}\right],

where
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
is a set of spanning surfaces decorated by
\alpha

{\mathcal{L}}
,
\chi(\Sigma)
is its Euler characteristic,
h(\Sigma)\geq 0
is a local defect term, and
\Omega_{N}(\Sigma;\alpha)
is an explicit coefficient.

This theorem will follow from a rewriting of all topological coefficients
\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
as sums over decorated surfaces in the spirit of Weingarten calculus. It can be interpreted as a topological expansion, or an exact gauge/string duality, understood in the broad sense initiated by ’t Hooft’s genus expansion
[

and made geometrically explicit in two-dimensional Yang–Mills by Gross and Taylor, who interpreted the large-

[

The particular feature of our expansion is that the surfaces live in a random plaquette-decorated environment, and it is a quite complicated task to exchange the sum and the expectation in order to absorb the random environment inside the surface-sum. It is nonetheless possible for the Wilson action, and we will show how to recover a surface-sum formula by Cao–Park–Sheffield
[
3
]
in this specialization. Let us mention that other exact or convergent expansions were obtained in two dimensions by Lévy
[
43
]
and Novak
[
55
]
via Schur–Weyl duality and ramified coverings, and asymptotic expansions by the author together with Maïda
[
35
,
36
]
while in higher-dimensional lattice gauge theory the strong-coupling large-

works of Chatterjee
[
6
]
and Jafarov
[

]
provided different realizations of the same gauge/string philosophy. Theorem
1.2
should be viewed as an exact finite-

N

It is worth emphasizing that this theorem is actually the lattice Yang–Mills specialization of two more general results of independent interest, namely Theorem

and Proposition

{\mathrm{U}}(N)

In this sense, the gauge/string expansion of lattice Yang–Mills derived here should be viewed as a target-lattice incarnation of a broader topological-expansion phenomenon for unitary integrals, initiated by the works of Magee–Puder

1.4
Spin-foam duality

A second main theme of the paper is that Wilson loop expectations admit, besides the global surface expansion of Theorem
1.2
, a genuinely local combinatorial description. The guiding idea is that once the plaquette action has been expanded in irreducible characters, each plaquette carries a highest-weight label, and the remaining problem is to understand how these local representation-theoretic degrees of freedom interact through the edge integrations. This naturally suggests a dual formulation in which plaquettes are viewed as local representation
variables and edges as local gluing constraints. The outcome is an exact local model for Wilson loop expectations, in which the observable is computed by summing over plaquette labels together with additional finite-dimensional channel data living on plaquette-edge incidences.

This point of view is close in spirit to the spin-foam and duality formulations of lattice gauge theory initiated by Oeckl–Pfeiffer
[
56
]
and later developed by Oeckl
[
57
]
, in which non-Abelian lattice gauge theory is rewritten as a local state sum of representation data. It is also related to later work on dual computations and spin-foam methods
[
8
]
. The novelty of the present framework is that the local dual model is extracted after the universal spectral/topological splitting of Theorem
1.1
. As a consequence, the action dependence is entirely carried by the spectral weights of the plaquette labels, while the topological coefficients themselves admit an exact finite-range representation in terms of local channel variables. In particular, Wilson loop expectations become ratios of two partition functions of the same local background model, differing only through finitely many edge factors determined by the loop family.

This local formulation is complementary to the surface expansion: the latter is global and geometric, and it organizes the coefficients in terms of spanning surfaces and makes the gauge/string aspect of the theory transparent. By contrast, the spin-foam representation keeps track only of local representation-theoretic compatibility data, and is therefore better adapted to locality questions.

Theorem 1.3

(Spin-foam representation)
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a finite connected lattice, let
T\subset E(\Lambda)
be a spanning tree, and let
{\mathcal{L}}=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k})
be a finite family of loops in the
1
-skeleton of
\Lambda
. For every plaquette decoration
\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)},
there exists a finite set
\mathcal{C}(\alpha)
of admissible local channel fields
\Gamma=(\Gamma_{p,e})_{(p,e):e\in\partial^{T}p},
together with local plaquette weights
A_{p}
and local edge kernels
K_{e}
, such that

(7)

\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}(\alpha)}\Bigl(\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}A_{p}(\Gamma_{\partial p};\alpha_{p})\Bigr)\Bigl(\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}K_{e}(\Gamma_{e};\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})\Bigr).

Moreover:

(i)

the plaquette amplitude
A_{p}
depends only on the single plaquette label
\alpha_{p}
and on the channel tuple around
p
;

(ii)

the edge kernel
K_{e}
depends only on the local neighborhood of the edge-vertex
e
in
D_{T}(\Lambda)
;

(iii)

the loop family enters only through the edge kernels, never through the plaquette amplitudes.

Equivalently, topological coefficients are computed by a finite-range local model on the pair of fields
(\alpha_{p})_{p\in P(\Lambda)}
and
(\Gamma_{p,e})_{(p,e):e\in\partial^{T}p},
living respectively on the plaquette vertices and incidence edges of the dual incidence graph. Define the
defect partition function

(8)

\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda,Q}^{({\mathcal{L}})}:=\sum_{\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}}\kappa_{\Lambda,Q}(\alpha)\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}(\alpha)}\Bigl(\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}A_{p}(\Gamma_{\partial p};\alpha_{p})\Bigr)\Bigl(\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}K_{e}(\Gamma_{e};\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})\Bigr),

and the defect support

D({\mathcal{L}}):=\{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T:\text{ some loop of }{\mathcal{L}}\text{ traverses }e\}.

Wilson loop expectations can then be expressed as ratios of such partition functions.

Theorem 1.4

(Exact defect-ratio representation)
.

For any finite loop family
{\mathcal{L}}
, the partition functions
\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda,Q}^{({\mathcal{L}})}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda,Q}^{(0)}
differ only through the edge factors in
D({\mathcal{L}})
, and we have

(9)

\mathbb{E}[W_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(U)]=\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda,Q}^{({\mathcal{L}})}}{\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda,Q}^{(0)}}.

Theorem
1.4
may be compared with the worldsheet/background decompositions considered in the spin-foam literature on confinement, notably by Cherrington and collaborators
[
8
]
. There, Wilson loop observables are organized as sums over worldsheets interacting with vacuum spin-foam backgrounds, typically with non-trivial degeneracy issues. In contrast, the present defect-ratio formula is an exact identity at finite
N
: the observable is represented as the ratio of two partition functions in the same local channel model, differing only through the finitely many edge factors supported on the defect set
D({\mathcal{L}})
. This avoids any non-canonical decomposition of charged configurations into background plus spanning surface, while retaining a fully local dual description.

Together, Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
answer a question of Cao–Park–Sheffield
[
3
, §7, (16)]
: compared to the surface-sum expansion, the spin-foam representation captures the
local properties
of lattice Yang–Mills, which is not possible in the topological expansion. Such locality might be naturally relevant for infinite-volume limits or scaling limits, and it would be interesting to explore these directions.

1.5
Master loop equation

Master loop equations occupy a central place in lattice Yang–Mills theory because they are the basic exact recursions satisfied by Wilson observables. In the Wilson-action setting, such equations lie behind the strong-coupling and large-
N
analyses of Chatterjee and Jafarov
[
6
,
32
]
, and were recently rederived at finite
N
for
{\mathrm{SO}}(N)
,
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
and
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
by Shen–Smith–Zhu
[
60
]
through a Langevin-dynamic approach. These results
show that the Wilson action admits a remarkably rigid cut-and-join/deformation recursion directly at the level of Wilson loop expectations. From that point of view, the classical finite-
N
master loop equation is already one of the fundamental exact identities of the subject.

Theorem
1.1
reveals these equations as instances of a broader topological and algebraic recursion. Rather than starting from a particular action and working directly with Wilson expectations, we first pass to the coefficient side of the state-sum expansion and prove a universal integration-by-parts identity for the topological coefficients
\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
. The result is a coefficientwise master loop equation that closes on the family

\bigl\{\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha):{\mathcal{L}}\text{ loop family},\ \alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}\bigr\}.

It involves two local operators
\mathscr{L}_{e}
and
\mathscr{B}_{e,p}
that we will describe in Section
4
; the former is the standard cut-and-join operator and acts on loops directly, and the latter is a purely spectral operator that acts on the topological coefficients.

Theorem 1.5

(Universal coefficientwise master loop equation)
.

For every oriented edge
e\in E(\Lambda)
, every loop sequence
{\mathcal{L}}
, and every plaquette decoration

\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}
, one has

(10)

(\mathscr{L}_{e}\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}})(\alpha)+\sum_{p\ni e}(\mathscr{B}_{e,p}\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}})(\alpha)=0.

In particular, it closes on all topological coefficients.

Although it seems more natural to us to state this master loop equation at the level of topological coefficients, it also admits a resummed form. Using (
5
), the coefficientwise identity (
10
) implies

(11)

\mathscr{L}_{e}\mathbb{E}[W_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(U)]+\sum_{p\ni e}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda,Q}\left[(\mathscr{B}_{e,p}\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}})(\alpha)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda,Q}\left[\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,\varnothing}(\alpha)\right]}=0.

We see that there are both a loop part, applied on Wilson loop expectation
W_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(U)
, and a Fourier part, applied to the topological coefficients
\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
. The former involves the familiar cut-and-join operator, and the latter involves a local recoupling operator on the active Fourier label
\alpha_{p}
.

This point of view is important for two reasons. First, it shows that the mechanism behind the master loop equation is more universal than the classical Wilson-action formulas suggest: what is truly fundamental is a hybrid position/Fourier recursion, in which the loop sector stays geometric while the plaquette sector is transferred to representation space. Second, it opens the door to non-Wilson specializations. For the Wilson action, the local recoupling operator collapses after resummation to the usual deformation terms; for the heat kernel action, by contrast, it remains visible as an explicit spectral transfer operator on the active plaquette label. In this sense, Theorem
1.5
should be viewed as a common finite-
N
ancestor of the usual lattice master loop equations and of the more spectral Makeenko–Migdal-type identities that arise in two-dimensional Yang–Mills
[
61
]
.

{\mathrm{SU}}(N),{\mathrm{SO}}(N),{\mathrm{Sp}}(N)

for a guideline on how to adapt our formalism to these other groups.

Another part of the theory has been left untouched in the present paper, which is the large-

regime. As the formalism imported from the two-dimensional setting in this paper has led to the construction of the master field in the plane and in compact surfaces
[
45
,
18
,
17
,
20
]
, it is natural to wonder whether the same holds in higher-dimensional lattices. We will apply the finite-

results of the present universal formalism in a companion paper
[

]
, which will also rely on recent advances in asymptotic spectral theory of compact classical groups
[

, obtained by the author together with Maïda.

2
Topological expansion

In this section, we introduce the combinatorial toolbox required to prove Theorem
1.2
. We will start with a general procedure, consisting in the integration of products of characters of rational irreducible representations of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
, and which is a natural generalization of the Weingarten calculus that computes integrals of products of traces of tensor powers of the fundamental and contragredient representation. An important part of the arguments presented here are already present in the recent works
[
52
,
53
,
20
]
but not stated in full generality, and we will extend them to a fairly general setting, before applying them to Wilson loop expectations. More precisely, Magee showed how some particular integrals of products of rational representations of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
can be rewritten as
N^{\chi}
-weighted sums over surfaces with boundary, whose topology can then be controlled using geometric arguments on words in surface groups. On the other hand, Dahlqvist recast this strategy in the language of mixed Schur–Weyl duality, traceless mixed tensors, and walled Brauer algebras, obtaining a surface-sum formalism similar to the one of Magee, but with a slightly different control.

Our goal is to show that the arguments of
[
53
,
20
]
can be naturally extended to
any product
of irreducible representations of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
and
any words
, thereby yielding Theorem
2.11
and Proposition
2.16
. Then, we will apply these techniques to the particular setting of the present paper, which is the study of topological coefficients (
4
). It will lead us to Theorem
2.18
, and finally to the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

2.1
Irreducible representations of
\mathrm{U}(N)

The irreducible representations of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
are in bijection with
highest weights
(or
signatures
)

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N},\quad\text{such that}\quad\lambda_{1}\geq\ldots\lambda_{N}.

We will therefore identify canonically
\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}
with the set of highest weights. Given any highest weight
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)
, we will denote by
(\rho_{\lambda},V_{\lambda})
the corresponding irreducible representation
\rho_{\lambda}\in\mathrm{Hom}(G,{\mathrm{End}}(V_{\lambda}))
, which has finite dimension
d_{\lambda}=\dim V_{\lambda}
. We will also denote by
\chi_{\lambda}:G\to\mathbb{C}
the corresponding
character
:

(12)

\chi_{\lambda}(U)=\mathrm{Tr}_{V_{\lambda}}(\rho_{\lambda}(U)),\qquad\forall U\in{\mathrm{U}}(N).

Let
n\geq 0
be an integer. A
partition of size
n
is a finite nonincreasing family of positive integers
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
such that
|\alpha|:=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=n
(by convention, the empty partition
\varnothing
is the only partition of size 0). We call
|\alpha|
its
size
and
\ell(\alpha):=r
its
length
. As much as highest weights label irreducible representations of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
, the partitions of size
n
label irreducible representations of the symmetric group
S_{n}
. We shall denote by
\chi^{\alpha}
the irreducible characters of
S_{n}
, with an exponent instead of a subscript to distinguish with the characters of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
. Given two integer partitions
\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-}
, with respective sizes
n^{+}
and
n^{-}
(not necessarily equal), one can always construct a highest weight

(13)

[\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-}]_{N}=(\lambda_{1}^{+},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell(\lambda^{+})}^{+},0,\ldots,0,-\lambda_{\ell(\lambda^{-})}^{-},\ldots,-\lambda_{1}^{-}),

for any
N\geq\ell(\lambda^{+})+\ell(\lambda^{-})
. All such highest weights are particular cases of irreducible representations of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
, but also correspond to
rational representations
(or
stable representations
) of
{\mathrm{GL}}(N,\mathbb{C})

[
49
,
48
,
64
]
. The interplay between rational representations and general irreducible representations of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
have been investigated in the large-
N
regime by the author
[
37
,
38
]
together with Dahlqvist
[
16
]
and Maïda
[
35
,
36
]
, but also by Magee
[
52
,
53
,
50
]
and Dahlqvist
[
20
]
alone. We will not elaborate on the large-
N
implication in the present paper, but mention that every irreducible representation of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
can be seen as a rational representation, by splitting the highest weight
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}
into a positive part
\lambda^{+}
and a negative part
\lambda^{-}
and forgetting the eventual zero coefficients. By construction, in this case, we always have
\ell(\lambda^{+})+\ell(\lambda^{-})\leq N
. We will see in the next subsection that the study of rational representations of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
is actually a manifestation of Schur–Weyl duality.

2.2
Mixed Schur–Weyl duality and walled Brauer diagrams

Let
V=\mathbb{C}^{N}
be the standard/fundamental representation of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
and
V^{*}=\mathbb{C}^{N}
its dual. For any
n,m\geq 1
, we define the space of
mixed tensors

T_{n,m}=V^{\otimes n}\otimes{(V^{*})}^{\otimes m}
. There are two actions, one of the unitary group
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
and one of the product
S_{n,m}=S_{n}\times S_{m}
of symmetric groups, which are given by

U\cdot(v_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes v_{n}\otimes v_{1}^{*}\otimes\ldots\otimes v_{m}^{*})=(Uv_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes Uv_{n}\otimes v_{1}^{*}(U^{-1})\otimes\ldots\otimes v_{m}^{*}(U^{-1})),

(\alpha,\beta)\cdot(v_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes v_{n}\otimes v_{1}^{*}\otimes\ldots\otimes v_{m}^{*})=(v_{\alpha^{-1}(1)}\otimes\ldots\otimes v_{\alpha^{-1}(n)}\otimes v_{\beta^{-1}(1)}\otimes\ldots\otimes v_{\beta^{-1}(m)}).

We will denote respectively by
\rho_{n,m}:{\mathrm{U}}(N)\to{\mathrm{End}}(T_{n,m})
and
\rho_{N}:S_{n,m}\to{\mathrm{End}}(T_{n,m})
the representations of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
and
S_{n,m}
on
T_{n,m}
corresponding to these actions.

A convenient way to describe the decomposition of mixed tensors is through the
walled Brauer algebra
, which is a subalgebra of the standard Brauer algebra. We briefly recall them, keeping things short because we only use them here as finite diagrammatic bases for equivariant endomorphisms.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
r\geq 1
. A
Brauer diagram on
r
strands
is a perfect matching of the
2r
vertices
\{1,\dots,r\}\times\{+,-\},
which we picture as
r

top
vertices
(1,+),\dots,(r,+)
and
r

bottom
vertices
(1,-),\dots,(r,-)
, joined by
r
pairwise disjoint edges. Equivalently, a Brauer diagram is an involution without fixed point on
\{1,\dots,r\}\times\{+,-\}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{B}_{r}
the finite set of Brauer diagrams on
r
strands. An edge joining a top vertex to a bottom vertex is called
vertical
, while an edge joining two top vertices or two bottom vertices is called
horizontal
.

Definition 2.2
.

Fix a parameter
\delta\in\mathbb{C}
. The
Brauer algebra

{\mathfrak{B}}_{r}(\delta)
is the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space with basis
\mathcal{B}_{r}
, endowed with the multiplication defined as follows.

For
D_{1},D_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{r}
, place
D_{1}
above
D_{2}
and identify the bottom row of
D_{1}
with the top row of
D_{2}
. This produces:

–

a new Brauer diagram
D_{1}\circ D_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{r}
, obtained by reading the connections between the top row of
D_{1}
and the bottom row of
D_{2}
;

–

a certain number
\ell(D_{1},D_{2})\geq 0
of closed loops created in the middle row.

One then sets

D_{1}D_{2}:=\delta^{\ell(D_{1},D_{2})}(D_{1}\circ D_{2}),

and extends this multiplication bilinearly to all of
{\mathfrak{B}}_{r}(\delta)
.

Figure 1:
A concatenation of two Brauer diagrams (on the left), and the resulting Brauer diagram after simplification (on the right). Since one loop is erased, the concatenation produces a factor
\delta
.

Remark 2.3
.

The symmetric group
S_{r}
embeds into
{\mathfrak{B}}_{r}(\delta)
as the subset of Brauer diagrams having only vertical strands: a permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{r}
is identified with the diagram joining
(i,+)
to
(\sigma(i),-)
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
n,m\geq 0
, not both zero. A
walled Brauer diagram of type
(n,m)
is a perfect matching of the
2(n+m)
vertices

\{-m,\dots,-1,1,\dots,n\}\times\{-1,1\},

subject to the rule that no edge crosses the wall separating the negative labels from the positive labels.

Equivalently, it is an involution
\pi
without fixed point on

\{-m,\dots,-1,1,\dots,n\}\times\{-1,1\}

such that, whenever
\pi(v,\eta)=(v^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})
, one has

\operatorname{sign}(v^{\prime})\eta^{\prime}=-\operatorname{sign}(v)\eta.

We denote by
\mathcal{{\mathcal{B}}}_{n,m}
the set of such diagrams.

Figure 2:
A walled Brauer diagram of type
(4,3)
.

As before, strands joining one top vertex to one bottom vertex are called
vertical
, and strands joining two top vertices or two bottom vertices are called
horizontal
.

Remark 2.5
.

The condition

\operatorname{sign}(v^{\prime})\eta^{\prime}=-\operatorname{sign}(v)\eta

means exactly that vertical strands stay on the same side of the wall, whereas horizontal strands necessarily connect one positive label and one negative label. This is the mixed-tensor diagrammatics appropriate to
V^{\otimes n}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes m}
.

Definition 2.6
.

Fix
\delta\in\mathbb{C}
. The
walled Brauer algebra of type
(n,m)
is the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space

{\mathfrak{B}}_{n,m}(\delta):={\mathrm{Span}}_{\mathbb{C}}\big(\mathcal{{\mathcal{B}}}_{n,m}\big),

with multiplication defined by the same concatenation rule as for the ordinary Brauer algebra.

More precisely, if
D_{1},D_{2}\in\mathcal{{\mathcal{B}}}_{n,m}
, stacking
D_{1}
above
D_{2}
and identifying the intermediate row produces a new walled Brauer diagram
D_{1}\circ D_{2}
together with a number
\ell(D_{1},D_{2})
of closed loops in
the middle row, and one sets

D_{1}D_{2}:=\delta^{\ell(D_{1},D_{2})}(D_{1}\circ D_{2}).

This extends bilinearly to
{\mathfrak{B}}_{n,m}(\delta)
.

By default, we shall work with
\delta:=N
, and we will denote by
{\mathfrak{B}}_{n,m}(N)
the corresponding Brauer algebra.

Remark 2.7
.

The subgroup
S_{n,m}=S_{n}\times S_{m}
identifies with the subset of walled Brauer diagrams with no horizontal strands: the factor
S_{n}
permutes the positive labels
\{1,\dots,n\}
, and the factor
S_{m}
permutes the negative labels

\{-m,\dots,-1\}
. In particular,
{\mathfrak{B}}_{n,m}(N)
acts on the mixed tensor space
T_{n,m}
and provides the standard diagrammatic model for the commutant of the
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-action.

There are two important operations that are not really endomorphisms of
T_{n,m}
. For any
1\leq i\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq m
, the
contraction

c_{i,j}:T_{n,m}\to T_{n-1,m-1}
is the pairing of the
i
th
V
-slot with the
j
th
V^{*}
-slot, and corresponds to an edge between the top vertices
(i,+)
and
(j,+)
, and the
coevaluation

\iota_{i,j}:T_{n-1,m-1}\to T_{n,m}
inserts the canonical tensor
\sum e_{a}\otimes e_{a}^{*}
into the
i
th
V
-slot and the
j
th
V^{*}
-slot, and corresponds to an edge between the bottom vertices
(i,-)
and
(j,-)
. Combining these yields a walled Brauer diagram
e_{i,j}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n,m}
that we will call
Weyl contraction
. It is known (see e.g.
[
31
]
) that
{\mathfrak{B}}_{n,m}(N)
is generated by transpositions and Weyl contractions. We shall also call
covariant permutations
the permutations
(\sigma,\mathrm{Id})\in S_{n,m}\subset{\mathcal{B}}_{n,m}
and
contravariant permutations
the permutations
(\mathrm{Id},\tau)\in S_{n,m}\subset{\mathcal{B}}_{n,m}
. From all Weyl contractions, we can build an element
A_{n,m}\in{\mathfrak{B}}_{n,m}(N)
of the algebra, defined by

(14)

A_{n,m}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}e_{i,j}.

It is the image of the
total contraction-coevaluation operator

A=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\iota_{i,j}c_{i,j}
by the natural representation of
{\mathfrak{B}}_{n,m}(N)
on
T_{n,m}
. This operator is studied by Goncharov
[
25
]
, who proved that
{\mathrm{Spec}}(A_{n,m})\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
and that
A_{n,m}
is diagonalizable in
T_{n,m}
.

The
mixed Schur–Weyl duality
, discovered by Koike
[
34
]
, states that the subspace

{\mathring{T}}_{n,m}=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq n\\
1\leq j\leq m\end{subarray}}\ker(c_{i,j}),

of
traceless tensors
decomposes in terms of rational representations of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
and mixed representations of
S_{n,m}
:

(15)

{\mathring{T}}_{n,m}=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash n,\mu\vdash m\\
\ell(\lambda)+\ell(\mu)\leq N\end{subarray}}V^{[\lambda,\mu]}\otimes V_{[\lambda,\mu]_{N}}.

We will consider three types of projectors:

–

The
{\mathrm{U}}(N)\times S_{n,m}
-equivariant projector
P_{N}^{n,m}
onto the space
{\mathring{T}}_{n,m}
of traceless mixed tensors.

–

The isotypic projector
P_{N}^{[\lambda,\mu]}
onto the summand associated to
(\lambda,\mu)
in the decomposition (
15
).

–

The symmetric group central idempotent
\Pi_{\lambda,\mu}\in{\mathrm{End}}(T_{n,m})
defined by

\Pi_{\lambda,\mu}=\frac{\chi^{\lambda}(1)\chi^{\mu}(1)}{n!m!}\sum_{(\sigma,\tau)\in S_{n,m}}\chi^{\lambda}(\sigma^{-1})\chi^{\mu}(\tau^{-1})\rho_{N}(\sigma,\tau),

which is the projector on
T_{n,m}
onto the
(\lambda,\mu)
-isotypic part inside the symmetric group side.

Because of the
S_{n,m}
-equivariance of
P_{N}^{n,m}
, we have in particular

(16)

P_{N}^{[\lambda,\mu]}=P_{N}^{n,m}\Pi_{\lambda,\mu}=\Pi_{\lambda,\mu}P_{N}^{n,m}.

It follows that the character of the rational representation of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
associated to
(\lambda,\mu)
is given by

(17)

\chi_{[\lambda,\mu]_{N}}(U)=\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{n,m}}\left(P_{N}^{[\lambda,\mu]}\rho_{n,m}(U)\right)=\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{n,m}}\left(P_{N}^{n,m}\Pi_{\lambda,\mu}\rho_{n,m}(U)\right),\quad\forall U\in{\mathrm{U}}(N).

The last result we need before going further is the following, which is due to Goncharov
[
25
]
.

Proposition 2.8

(
[
25
]
)
.

For any
\lambda\vdash n,\mu\vdash m
, write
W_{\lambda,\mu}=\Pi_{\lambda,\mu}T_{n,m}
, and let

I_{\lambda,\mu}={\mathrm{Spec}}(A_{n,m}|_{W_{\lambda,\mu}})\setminus\{0\}.

Then

(18)

P_{N}^{[\lambda,\mu]}=\Pi_{\lambda,\mu}\prod_{a\in I_{\lambda,\mu}(N)}\left(1-\frac{1}{a}A_{n,m}\right).

In fact, Goncharov proves a stronger statement, where
I_{\lambda,\mu}
can be refined into a smaller and more precise subset of the spectrum of
A_{n,m}
, but we will not need it here. What matters to us is that it leads to an expansion of the projector
P_{N}^{[\lambda,\mu]}
.

Corollary 2.9
.

For any
\lambda\vdash n,\mu\vdash m
, there is a family
(c_{N}^{[\lambda,\mu]}(\tau))_{\tau\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n,m}}
such that

(19)

P_{N}^{[\lambda,\mu]}=\sum_{\tau\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n,m}}c_{N}^{[\lambda,\mu]}(\tau)\rho_{N}(\tau).

Proof.

By Proposition
2.8
,
P_{N}^{[\lambda,\mu]}
belongs to the image of
{\mathfrak{B}}_{n,m}(N)
in
T_{n,m}
, which is spanned by the set
{\mathcal{B}}_{n,m}
of Brauer diagrams. In the stable range
N\geq n+m
,
{\mathcal{B}}_{n,m}
is a basis therefore there is a unique decomposition of the projector. Otherwise,
{\mathcal{B}}_{n,m}
gives only a generating family, so that there exist several decompositions, and we just need to choose one.
∎

A similar statement, which holds directly for
P_{N}^{n,m}
can be found in
[
20
, Lemma 4.1]
, but only in the stable range
N\geq n+m
, which is enough for a large-
N
analysis but not a finite-
N
expansion. The advantage of Dahlqvist’s version is to provide a precise bound of the coefficients of the decomposition, which we do not have here.

2.3
Weingarten expansion of the integral

We will now perform a refined Weingarten expansion of all integrals of products of rational characters of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
. The basic point, already emphasized by Magee
[
53
]
, is that a coarse expansion performed using the classical Weingarten calculus may hide large cancellations and may even exhibit apparently “catastrophic” contributions whose size is much too large to be compatible with the expected large-
N
behavior. In Magee’s approach, this is resolved by refining the Weingarten calculus through a projector onto the relevant traceless mixed-tensor component. Although we remain in the finite-
N
regime here, we believe it is still the right strategy to follow. The first combinatorial object, popularized by Collins
[
13
]
and later by Collins–Sniady
[
12
]
, is the Weingarten function, whose definition is recalled below.

Definition 2.10
.

For
n\geq 0
, let
G_{N}^{(n)}
be the matrix indexed by
S_{n}\times S_{n}
defined by

G_{N}^{(n)}(\sigma,\tau):=N^{\#(\sigma^{-1}\tau)},\qquad\sigma,\tau\in S_{n},

where
\#(\pi)
denotes the number of cycles of the permutation
\pi
. The
unitary Weingarten function
at rank
n
is the central function

\mathrm{Wg}_{N}^{(n)}:S_{n}\to\mathbb{C}

whose matrix

\bigl(\mathrm{Wg}_{N}^{(n)}(\sigma^{-1}\tau)\bigr)_{\sigma,\tau\in S_{n}}

is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of
G_{N}^{(n)}
. When there is no ambiguity on
n
, we simply write
\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\pi)
for
\mathrm{Wg}_{N}^{(n)}(\pi)
,
\pi\in S_{n}
.
In the stable range
n\leq N
, the matrix
G_{N}^{(n)}
is invertible, so
\mathrm{Wg}_{N}^{(n)}
is characterized by

\sum_{\tau\in S_{n}}N^{\#(\sigma^{-1}\tau)}\,\mathrm{Wg}_{N}^{(n)}(\tau^{-1}\rho)=\delta_{\sigma,\rho}.

We will now introduce the main objects involved in the procedure:

–

A finite family of words
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{m}
in the free group on generators
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
. We always assume them in their
reduced form
, so that their combinatorial length
|w_{i}|
is minimal.

–

A finite family
(\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{i}^{-})_{1\leq i\leq k}
of pairs of partitions, denoting by
n_{i}^{\pm}=|\lambda_{i}^{\pm}|
their size.

Given this data, we define
s=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(n_{i}^{+}+n_{i}^{-})|w_{i}|
, and three maps on
[s]=\{1,\ldots,s\}
:

(i)

An
orientation map

\varepsilon:[s]\to\{+,-\}

(ii)

A
letter map

a:[s]\to\{1,\ldots,m\}
,

(iii)

A
sign map

\delta:[s]\to\{-1,1\}
.

For each
\mathcal{I}
, let
T_{i}:=T_{n_{i}^{+},n_{i}^{-}}
be the space of mixed tensors,
\rho_{i}=\rho_{n_{i}^{+},n_{i}^{-}}:{\mathrm{U}}(N)\to{\mathrm{End}}(T_{n_{i}^{+},n_{i}^{-}})
be the natural mixed tensor representation, and

P_{i}:=P_{N}^{[\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{i}^{-}]}=P_{N}^{n_{i}^{+},n_{i}^{-}}\Pi_{\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{i}^{-}}

be the isotypic projector involved in the mixed Schur–Weyl decomposition (
15
). We have

(20)

\chi_{[\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{i}^{-}]_{N}}(g)=\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{i}}(P_{i}\rho_{i}(g)).

The maps
\varepsilon,a,\delta
are here to describe the decomposition of
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\rho_{i}(w_{i}(U))
as a tensor product of copies of the matrices
U_{1},\ldots,U_{m},\bar{U_{1}},\ldots,\bar{U_{m}}
in the mixed tensor space
T_{\varepsilon}=V^{\varepsilon_{1}}\otimes\ldots V^{\varepsilon_{s}}
, where
V^{+}:=V
and
V^{-}:=V^{*}
:

\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}\rho_{i}(w_{i}(U))=U_{a}^{\otimes\delta}:=U_{a(1)}^{\delta(1)}\otimes\ldots U_{a(s)}^{\delta(s)}\in{\mathrm{End}}(T_{\varepsilon}).

Hence

(21)

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\chi_{[\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{i}^{-}]_{N}}(w_{i}(U))=\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{\varepsilon}}\Bigl(PU_{a}^{\otimes\delta}\Bigr),

where
P=P_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes P_{k}
.

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, write each word
w_{i}
in an explicit form

w_{i}=x_{a_{i,1}}^{\varepsilon_{i,1}}\cdots x_{a_{i,\ell_{i}}}^{\varepsilon_{i,\ell_{i}}},\qquad\ell_{i}:=|w_{i}|,\qquad r_{i}:=n_{i}^{+}+n_{i}^{-},

and let

T_{i}:=T_{n_{i}^{+},n_{i}^{-}}=V^{\otimes n_{i}^{+}}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes n_{i}^{-}},\qquad\chi_{[\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{i}^{-}]_{N}}(g)=\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{i}}\bigl(P_{i}\rho_{i}(g)\bigr).

Recall that by Corollary
2.9
we have the projector decomposition

P_{i}=\sum_{\tau\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n_{i}^{+},n_{i}^{-}}}c_{i,N}(\tau)\rho_{N}(\tau),

where
c_{i,N}(\tau):=c_{N}^{[\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{i}^{-}]}(\tau)
. Define the local Brauer index set
\mathcal{I}:=\prod_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{B}}_{n_{i}^{+},n_{i}^{-}}^{\ell_{i}}.
For
\tau=(\tau_{i,r})_{1\leq i\leq k,\ 1\leq r\leq\ell_{i}}\in\mathcal{I}
, set

c_{N}(\tau):=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{r=1}^{\ell_{i}}c_{i,N}(\tau_{i,r}).

Although it does not appear in the notation, note that
c_{N}(\tau)
depends on all
\lambda_{i}^{\pm}
. Let
\mathcal{S}:=\{(i,r,u):1\leq i\leq k,\ 1\leq r\leq\ell_{i},\ 1\leq u\leq r_{i}\}.
For
x=(i,r,u)\in\mathcal{S}
, define its letter by
a(x):=a_{i,r},
and its matrix-entry sign by

\delta(x):=\begin{cases}+1,&\text{if }(u\leq n_{i}^{+}\text{ and }\varepsilon_{i,r}=+1)\text{ or }(u>n_{i}^{+}\text{ and }\varepsilon_{i,r}=-1),\\
-1,&\text{if }(u\leq n_{i}^{+}\text{ and }\varepsilon_{i,r}=-1)\text{ or }(u>n_{i}^{+}\text{ and }\varepsilon_{i,r}=+1).\end{cases}

For each letter
a\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, set

\mathcal{S}_{a}^{\pm}:=\{x\in\mathcal{S}:\ a(x)=a,\ \delta(x)=\pm 1\}.

Theorem 2.11
.

If for some
a
one has
|\mathcal{S}_{a}^{+}|\neq|\mathcal{S}_{a}^{-}|
, then

\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\chi_{[\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{i}^{-}]_{N}}(w_{i}(U))dU=0.

Otherwise, define
|\mathcal{S}_{a}^{+}|=|\mathcal{S}_{a}^{-}|=:p_{a}
for every
a
. Choose orderings
\mathcal{S}_{a}^{+}=(x_{a,1}^{+},\dots,x_{a,p_{a}}^{+}),\ \mathcal{S}_{a}^{-}=(x_{a,1}^{-},\dots,x_{a,p_{a}}^{-}),
and define
\mathcal{J}:=\prod_{a=1}^{m}(S_{p_{a}}\times S_{p_{a}}).
For fixed
\tau\in\mathcal{I}
, introduce cyclic multi-indices

I_{i,r},J_{i,r}\in[N]^{r_{i}},\qquad 1\leq i\leq k,\ 1\leq r\leq\ell_{i},

with the convention
I_{i,\ell_{i}+1}=I_{i,1}
. For
x=(i,r,u)\in\mathcal{S}
, set

j_{x}:=(J_{i,r})_{u},\qquad i_{x}:=(I_{i,r+1})_{u}.

For
\sigma=((\alpha_{a},\beta_{a}))_{a=1}^{m}\in\mathcal{J}
, define

\mathcal{K}_{N}(\tau,\sigma):=\sum_{\{I_{i,r},J_{i,r}\}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{r=1}^{\ell_{i}}(\rho_{N}(\tau_{i,r}))_{I_{i,r},J_{i,r}}\right)\prod_{a=1}^{m}\prod_{t=1}^{p_{a}}\delta^{\mathrm{K}}_{j_{x_{a,t}^{+}},i_{x_{a,\alpha_{a}(t)}^{-}}}\delta^{\mathrm{K}}_{i_{x_{a,t}^{+}},j_{x_{a,\beta_{a}(t)}^{-}}},

where
\delta^{\mathrm{K}}
denotes the Kronecker delta. Then

(22)

\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\chi_{[\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{i}^{-}]_{N}}(w_{i}(U))dU=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{I}}c_{N}(\tau)\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{J}}\left(\prod_{a=1}^{m}{\mathrm{Wg}}_{N}(\alpha_{a}^{-1}\beta_{a})\right)\mathcal{K}_{N}(\tau,\sigma).

Proof.

For each
1\leq i\leq k
and any
1\leq r\leq\ell_{i}
, set
R_{i,r}(U):=\rho_{i}\bigl(U_{a_{i,r}}^{\varepsilon_{i,r}}\bigr),
Since
P_{i}
is
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-equivariant, it commutes with every
R_{i,r}(U)
, and since
P_{i}
is a projector,
P_{i}^{2}=P_{i}
. Hence

\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{i}}\bigl(P_{i}R_{i,1}(U)\cdots R_{i,\ell_{i}}(U)\bigr)=\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{i}}\bigl(P_{i}R_{i,1}(U)P_{i}R_{i,2}(U)\cdots P_{i}R_{i,\ell_{i}}(U)\bigr).

Expanding each copy of
P_{i}
using (
19
), we obtain

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\chi_{[\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{i}^{-}]_{N}}(w_{i}(U))=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{I}}c_{N}(\tau)\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{i}}\Bigl(\prod_{r=1}^{\ell_{i}}\rho_{N}(\tau_{i,r})R_{i,r}(U)\Bigr).

Now, fix
\tau
. Expanding each trace in the standard basis of
T_{i}
, we get

\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{i}}\Bigl(\prod_{r=1}^{\ell_{i}}\rho_{N}(\tau_{i,r})R_{i,r}(U)\Bigr)=\sum_{\{I_{i,r},J_{i,r}\}}\prod_{r=1}^{\ell_{i}}(\rho_{N}(\tau_{i,r}))_{I_{i,r},J_{i,r}}(R_{i,r}(U))_{J_{i,r},I_{i,r+1}},

with the cyclic convention
I_{i,\ell_{i}+1}=I_{i,1}
. Now
R_{i,r}(U)
acts diagonally on the
r_{i}
mixed tensor slots, so each matrix coefficient
(R_{i,r}(U))_{J_{i,r},I_{i,r+1}}
factorizes into a product of
r_{i}
matrix entries of the single unitary
U_{a_{i,r}}
, one for each slot
u\in\{1,\dots,r_{i}\}
. By definition of
\delta(i,r,u)
, the occurrence
x=(i,r,u)
contributes

(U_{a(x)})_{j_{x},i_{x}}\quad\text{if }\delta(x)=+1,\qquad(\overline{U_{a(x)}})_{j_{x},i_{x}}=(U_{a(x)})_{i_{x},j_{x}}\quad\text{if }\delta(x)=-1.

Grouping together all factors involving the same letter
a
, the fixed-
\tau
integrand becomes

\sum_{\{I_{i,r},J_{i,r}\}}\left(\prod_{i,r}(\rho_{N}(\tau_{i,r}))_{I_{i,r},J_{i,r}}\right)\prod_{a=1}^{m}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{|\mathcal{S}_{a}^{+}|}(U_{a})_{j_{x_{a,t}^{+}},i_{x_{a,t}^{+}}}\right)\left(\prod_{t=1}^{|\mathcal{S}_{a}^{-}|}(U_{a})_{i_{x_{a,t}^{-}},j_{x_{a,t}^{-}}}\right).

If for some
a
one has
|\mathcal{S}_{a}^{+}|\neq|\mathcal{S}_{a}^{-}|
, then the integral over
U_{a}
vanishes by Haar invariance, so the whole integral is zero, which yields the first assertion.

Assume now that
|\mathcal{S}_{a}^{+}|=|\mathcal{S}_{a}^{-}|=:p_{a}
for every
a
. For each
a
, applying the ordinary unitary Weingarten formula gives

\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}\prod_{t=1}^{p_{a}}(U_{a})_{j_{x_{a,t}^{+}},i_{x_{a,t}^{+}}}\prod_{t=1}^{p_{a}}(U_{a})_{i_{x_{a,t}^{-}},j_{x_{a,t}^{-}}}dU_{a}=\sum_{\alpha_{a},\beta_{a}\in S_{p_{a}}}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{p_{a}}\delta^{\mathrm{K}}_{j_{x_{a,t}^{+}},i_{x_{a,\alpha_{a}(t)}^{-}}}\delta^{\mathrm{K}}_{i_{x_{a,t}^{+}},j_{x_{a,\beta_{a}(t)}^{-}}}\right)\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\alpha_{a}^{-1}\beta_{a}).

Since the variables
U_{1},\dots,U_{m}
are independent, multiplying these formulas over
a=1,\dots,m
and interchanging the finite sums yields

\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\chi_{[\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{i}^{-}]_{N}}(w_{i}(U))dU=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{I}}c_{N}(\tau)\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{J}}\left(\prod_{a=1}^{m}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\alpha_{a}^{-1}\beta_{a})\right)\mathcal{K}_{N}(\tau,\sigma),

which is the desired formula.
∎

One expects Theorem
2.11
to admit further vanishing criteria beyond the ordinary balance condition

|\mathcal{S}_{a}^{+}|=|\mathcal{S}_{a}^{-}|\qquad(a=1,\dots,m).

For instance, in a similar setting, Magee
[
53
]
proves that in his refined calculus certain classical Weingarten matchings vanish identically because the relevant projector acts on the traceless mixed-tensor space: when a local pairing forces a mixed contraction incompatible with tracelessness, the corresponding contribution is zero. This is summarized in his “forbidden matching property.” The same mechanism holds here.

Proposition 2.12
.

Keep the notation of Theorem
2.11
. For
\sigma=((\alpha_{a},\beta_{a}))_{a=1}^{m}\in\mathcal{J}
,
define

\widetilde{K}_{N}(\sigma):=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{I}}c_{N}(\tau)K_{N}(\tau,\sigma).

Suppose that there exist a letter
a\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and an index
t\in\{1,\dots,p_{a}\}
such that, writing

x_{a,t}^{+}=(i,r,u)\in\mathcal{S}_{a}^{+},\qquad x_{a,\alpha_{a}(t)}^{-}=x_{a,\beta_{a}(t)}^{-}=(i,r,v)\in\mathcal{S}_{a}^{-},

the positive occurrence
x_{a,t}^{+}
and the two negative occurrences paired with it by
\alpha_{a}
and
\beta_{a}
all come from the same local occurrence
(i,r)
. Then
\widetilde{K}_{N}(\sigma)=0.

Proof.

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
,
the image of
P_{i}=\Pi_{i}P_{N}^{n_{i}^{+},n_{i}^{-}}
is contained in
{\mathring{T}}_{n_{i}^{+},n_{i}^{-}}
because
P_{N}^{n_{i}^{+},n_{i}^{-}}
is the projector onto
{\mathring{T}}_{n_{i}^{+},n_{i}^{-}}
. Hence, for every covariant slot
p
and contravariant slot
q
,
c_{p,q}P_{i}=0.
By self-adjointness, this also gives
P_{i}\iota_{p,q}=0,
where
\iota_{p,q}
denotes the corresponding coevaluation operator.

Undoing the Brauer expansion in the proof of Theorem
2.11
, the quantity
\widetilde{K}_{N}(\sigma)
is obtained by keeping the factors
P_{i}
unexpanded. If the configuration above occurs, then the two Kronecker deltas corresponding to the chosen pair
(a,t)
identify the indices in such a way that, at the local occurrence
(i,r)
, one inserts either a mixed contraction
c_{u,v}
or its adjoint coevaluation
\iota_{u,v}
between two consecutive copies of
P_{i}
. After summing over the intermediate indices, the corresponding local factor is therefore either
P_{i}c_{u,v}P_{i}
or
P_{i}\iota_{u,v}P_{i},
and both vanish by the relations above. Hence every summand is zero, so
\widetilde{K}_{N}(\sigma)=0
.
∎

This gives a first rigorous forbidden-matching criterion. A systematic exploitation of such vanishing mechanisms, together with the corresponding large-
N
estimates, will be developed in the companion paper
[
39
]
.

Remark 2.13
.

As pointed out in
[
53
, Section 3]
, Theorem
2.11
contains the ordinary unitary Weingarten calculus as a special case. Indeed, if there is no nontrivial mixed-tensor projector insertion, then the Brauer side becomes trivial: only the identity contribution survives in each local Brauer factor, the sum over
\tau
collapses, and the formula reduces to a pure sum over the permutation-pair data
\sigma=((\alpha_{a},\beta_{a}))_{a=1}^{m}
with weights
\prod_{a=1}^{m}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\alpha_{a}^{-1}\beta_{a}),
which is exactly the classical unitary Weingarten expansion for expectations of traces of words in independent Haar unitaries.

2.4
Construction of spanning surfaces

In this subsection we reinterpret the Weingarten expansion of Theorem
2.11
in geometric terms. Let us recall a few notations: for each
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, write

w_{i}=x_{a_{i,1}}^{\varepsilon_{i,1}}\cdots x_{a_{i,\ell_{i}}}^{\varepsilon_{i,\ell_{i}}},\qquad\ell_{i}:=|w_{i}|,\qquad r_{i}:=n_{i}^{+}+n_{i}^{-},

and
\mathcal{I}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}{\mathcal{B}}_{n_{i}^{+},n_{i}^{-}}^{\ell_{i}},

\mathcal{J}=\prod_{a=1}^{m}S_{p_{a},p_{a}}.
We write

\tau=(\tau_{i,r})_{1\leq i\leq k,\ 1\leq r\leq\ell_{i}}\in\mathcal{I},\qquad\sigma=((\alpha_{a},\beta_{a}))_{a=1}^{m}\in\mathcal{J}.

From this data we will construct polygons associated to each word
w_{i}
and each tensor slot
u\in\{1,\ldots,r_{i}\}
, as well as “Brauer gadgets” associated to
\tau
that attach ribbons to the polygons according to rules dictated by Theorem
2.11
. Then, these combinations of polygons and Brauer gadgets are glued together by means of
\sigma
, following Haar integration over all remaining edge variables.

In order to illustrate the procedure, we will describe a simple example in full details:

Example 2.14
.

Consider the integral

\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{2}}\chi_{[(1),(1)]_{N}}(xyx^{-1}y^{-1})dxdy.

We will describe, for each step of the construction, how it works for this specific example.

Boundary polygons.
For each
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
and each slot
u\in\{1,\dots,r_{i}\}
, let
P_{i,u}
be an

\ell_{i}
sides

e_{i,u,1},\dots,e_{i,u,\ell_{i}},

read in cyclic order according to the word
w_{i}
. The side
e_{i,u,r}
is labelled by the
r
-th letter
x_{a_{i,r}}^{\varepsilon_{i,r}}
of
w_{i}
, together with the slot index
u
. We denote by
P:=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{k}\bigsqcup_{u=1}^{r_{i}}P_{i,u}
the resulting disjoint union of slot-polygons. Thus the set of boundary edges of

is naturally indexed by

\mathcal{S}=\{(i,r,u):1\leq i\leq k,\ 1\leq r\leq\ell_{i},\ 1\leq u\leq r_{i}\},

Construction of the surface.
Let us now detail the construction of the surface. It consists in three steps: we first glue the Brauer gadgets to boundary polygons, then we glue edges by performing Haar integration, and finally we cap the surface into a closed surface.

Step 1. Brauer gluings.
Fix
\tau\in\mathcal{I}
. For every
i,r,u
, glue the incoming boundary interval
p^{\mathrm{in}}_{i,r,u}\subset\partial G_{i,r}(\tau_{i,r})

P(\tau)
the resulting oriented surface with boundary. The opposite interval

remains exposed after the gluing; we denote the corresponding exposed boundary edge by

\widetilde{e}_{i,u,r}\subset\partial P(\tau).

Step 2. Haar patches.
Fix
\sigma=((\alpha_{a},\beta_{a}))_{a=1}^{m}\in\mathcal{J}
. For each letter
a\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, let

\mathcal{S}_{a}^{+}=\{x_{a,1}^{+},\dots,x_{a,p_{a}}^{+}\},\qquad\mathcal{S}_{a}^{-}=\{x_{a,1}^{-},\dots,x_{a,p_{a}}^{-}\}

be the ordered positive and negative occurrences of

2.11
. We define

and whose internal identifications encode the Kronecker constraints

\delta^{K}_{j_{x_{a,t}^{+}},i_{x_{a,\alpha_{a}(t)}^{-}}},\qquad\delta^{K}_{i_{x_{a,t}^{+}},j_{x_{a,\beta_{a}(t)}^{-}}}\qquad(1\leq t\leq p_{a})

Remark 2.15
.

The capping construction induces a canonical bijection

\mathrm{cap}:S\longrightarrow\widehat{S},

because the capped-decorated-isomorphism class remembers the capping faces

, and removing their interiors recovers the uncapped decorated surface and map.

For
(\tau,\sigma)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}
, define

\Theta_{N}(\tau,\sigma):=N^{-\chi(\Sigma(\tau,\sigma))+h(\tau)}K_{N}(\tau,\sigma),

where
K_{N}(\tau,\sigma)
is the index-contraction factor of Theorem

. Equivalently, since

(23)

\chi(\widehat{\Sigma}(\tau,\sigma))=\chi(\Sigma(\tau,\sigma))+b(\tau,\sigma),

we also have the tautological identity

K_{N}(\tau,\sigma)=\Theta_{N}(\tau,\sigma)N^{\chi(\widehat{\Sigma}(\tau,\sigma))-b(\tau,\sigma)-h(\tau)}.

For any
\Xi=[({\mathcal{M}}(\tau,\sigma),\Sigma(\tau,\sigma))]
, we define

\chi(\Xi):=\chi(\Sigma(\tau,\sigma)),\qquad h(\Xi):=h(\tau),

which are invariants of the decorated-isomorphism class, and the local coefficient

\Omega_{N}(\Xi):=\sum_{(\tau,\sigma)}c_{N}(\tau)\left(\prod_{a=1}^{m}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\alpha_{a}^{-1}\beta_{a})\right)\Theta_{N}(\tau,\sigma),

where the sum is performed over all
(\tau,\sigma)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}
such that
\Xi=[({\mathcal{M}}(\tau,\sigma),\Sigma(\tau,\sigma))]
.

Proposition 2.16
.

With the notation of Theorem
2.11
,

\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\chi_{[\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{i}^{-}]_{N}}(w_{i}(U))dU=\sum_{\Xi\in S}\Omega_{N}(\Xi)N^{\chi(\Xi)-h(\Xi)}.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.11
,

\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\chi_{[\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{i}^{-}]_{N}}(w_{i}(U))dU=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{J}}c_{N}(\tau)\left(\prod_{a=1}^{m}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\alpha_{a}^{-1}\beta_{a})\right)K_{N}(\tau,\sigma).

Using the definition of
\Theta_{N}(\tau,\sigma)
, each summand can be rewritten as

c_{N}(\tau)\left(\prod_{a=1}^{m}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\alpha_{a}^{-1}\beta_{a})\right)\Theta_{N}(\tau,\sigma)N^{\chi(\Sigma(\tau,\sigma))-h(\tau)}.

Grouping together all pairs
(\tau,\sigma)
that produce the same uncapped decorated-isomorphism class
\Xi\in S
yields the formula.
∎

At this stage, the construction seems to carry too much data to be seen as purely topological. It is nonetheless useful to get a better control in the large-
N
, as shown in
[
53
,
20
]
. We can turn Proposition
2.16
into a coarser, more natural topological expansion as follows. Let
\pi:S\longrightarrow S_{\mathrm{coarse}}
be the forgetful map defined as follows. Starting from a refined pair
({\mathcal{M}}(\tau,\sigma),\Sigma(\tau,\sigma)),
forget:

–

the decomposition into local Brauer gadgets
G_{i,r}(\tau_{i,r})
,

–

the positionwise labels
r
on the Brauer layer,

–

the internal subdivision data that distinguish different local realizations of the same surface,

while retaining:

–

the underlying oriented surface with boundary,

–

the associated topological map,

–

the decomposition of the faces into the images of
P
and
H
,

–

the total defect

h(\tau)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{r=1}^{\ell_{i}}h(\tau_{i,r}).

The image is denoted by
S_{\mathrm{coarse}}
.

Corollary 2.17
.

With the notation above,

(24)

\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\chi_{[\lambda_{i}^{+},\lambda_{i}^{-}]_{N}}(w_{i}(U))dU=\sum_{\Sigma\in S_{\mathrm{coarse}}}\Omega_{N}(\Sigma)N^{\chi(\Sigma)-h(\Sigma)},

where

\Omega_{N}(\Sigma):=\sum_{\Xi\in\pi^{-1}(\Sigma)}\Omega_{N}(\Xi).

Proof.

Apply Proposition
2.16
and regroup the sum according to the forgetful map
\pi:S\to S_{\mathrm{coarse}}
. By construction,
\pi
forgets only the local realization data and preserves the underlying surface, the topological map, and the total number of horizontal Brauer bands. Hence
\chi
,
b
, and
h
are constant on the fibres of
\pi
, which yields the stated coarse formula.
∎

2.5
Proof of the topological expansion

We now apply the general surface expansion of Proposition
2.16
to the topological coefficients
\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
. The point is that the expansion applies to the family of plaquette words
(\partial p)_{p\in P(\Lambda)}
together with the loop words
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}
. For the plaquette factors, the highest weights are
\alpha_{p}=[\lambda_{p}^{+},\lambda_{p}^{-}]_{N},
whereas for the loop factors we use the fundamental representation
\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})=\chi_{[(1),\varnothing]_{N}}(U_{\ell_{i}}).
Accordingly, each plaquette

|\lambda_{p}^{+}|+|\lambda_{p}^{-}|

\ell_{i}
contributes exactly one distinguished loop-polygon

.

Theorem 2.18
.

Fix a plaquette-decoration
\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}
, and note
\alpha_{p}=[\lambda_{p}^{+},\lambda_{p}^{-}]_{N}
for all
p\in P(\Lambda)
. There exists a finite set

of decorated oriented spanning surfaces with boundary, such that each
\Sigma\in S_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
is equipped with a coefficient
\Omega_{N}(\Sigma;\alpha)\in\mathbb{C}
and an integer

, and such that

(25)

\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)=\sum_{\Sigma\in S_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)}\Omega_{N}(\Sigma;\alpha)N^{\chi(\Sigma)-h(\Sigma)}.

Moreover, every
\Sigma\in S_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
comes equipped with:

–

a cellular map
\varphi_{\Sigma}:\Sigma\to\Lambda^{(2)}
;

–

k
ordered marked boundary components
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
;

–

for each

\varphi_{\Sigma}|_{C_{i}}
has cyclic boundary word
\ell_{i}
;

–

a plaquette-sheet labeling compatible with
\alpha
;

–

a record of the local walled Brauer and Haar-pairing data.

Proof.

Apply Proposition
2.16
to the family of words

(\partial p)_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\cup(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}),

with highest weights
\alpha_{p}=[\lambda_{p}^{+},\lambda_{p}^{-}]_{N}
on the plaquette factors and

((1),\varnothing)
on the loop factors. Since
\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})=\chi_{[(1),\varnothing]_{N}}(U_{\ell_{i}}),
this yields a finite expansion indexed by pairs
(\tau,\sigma)
, where
\tau
is the local walled Brauer datum
\tau=(\tau_{i,r})_{i,r},
and
\sigma
is the letterwise Haar-pairing datum of Theorem
2.11
. For each such pair, Section
2.4
and Remark
2.15
produce a capped decorated pair
({\mathcal{M}}(\tau,\sigma),\widehat{\Sigma}(\tau,\sigma)),
where
\Sigma(\tau,\sigma)
is a compact oriented surface with boundary and
{\mathcal{M}}(\tau,\sigma)
is a topological map embedded in
\Sigma(\tau,\sigma)
. Because the loop factors are fundamental, each loop
\ell_{i}
contributes exactly one distinguished
loop-polygon
P_{\ell_{i}}
among the faces of
{\mathcal{M}}(\tau,\sigma)
.

We now define the canonical cellular map

\widehat{\varphi}_{\tau,\sigma}:\widehat{\Sigma}(\tau,\sigma)\longrightarrow\Lambda^{(2)}.

It is specified on the faces of the completed map
{\mathcal{M}}(\tau,\sigma)
as follows:

–

each plaquette slot-polygon corresponding to a plaquette word
\partial p
is sent homeomorphically onto the plaquette
p
, respecting the cyclic boundary word
\partial p
;

–

each distinguished loop-polygon
P_{\ell_{i}}
is sent to the loop
\ell_{i}
in the
1
-skeleton of
\Lambda
, with boundary word
\ell_{i}
;

–

each edge of the map labelled by an oriented lattice edge

is sent homeomorphically onto the corresponding oriented edge of
\Lambda
;

–

each Brauer face and each Haar face is collapsed onto the corresponding lower-dimensional cell determined by its edge labels.

This defines a cellular map from the completed surface to
\Lambda^{(2)}
.

We then define the punctured spanning surface associated with
(\tau,\sigma)
by removing the interiors of the distinguished loop-faces:

\Sigma^{\partial}(\tau,\sigma):=\widehat{\Sigma}(\tau,\sigma)\setminus\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{k}\operatorname{int}(P_{\ell_{i}}).

Its boundary contains
k
distinguished components

C_{i}:=\partial P_{\ell_{i}},\qquad i=1,\dots,k.

Restricting
\widehat{\varphi}_{\tau,\sigma}
gives a cellular map

\varphi_{\tau,\sigma}:\Sigma^{\partial}(\tau,\sigma)\to\Lambda^{(2)}.

By construction, each plaquette slot-polygon associated with a plaquette word
\partial p
is mapped homeomorphically onto the plaquette
p

is exactly the loop
\ell_{i}
. The restriction

The contribution of
(\tau,\sigma)
in Proposition
2.16
is

c_{N}(\tau)\Bigl(\prod_{a}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\alpha_{a}^{-1}\beta_{a})\Bigr)\Theta_{N}(\tau,\sigma)N^{\chi(\Sigma(\tau,\sigma))-h(\tau)},

where
\Sigma(\tau,\sigma)
is the uncapped surface of Section
2.4
. Recall that, by (

),

\chi(\widehat{\Sigma}(\tau,\sigma))=\chi(\Sigma(\tau,\sigma))+b(\tau,\sigma).

Moreover, since
\Sigma^{\partial}(\tau,\sigma)
is obtained from
\widehat{\Sigma}(\tau,\sigma)
by removing exactly the
k
distinguished loop-faces, we also have

\chi(\Sigma^{\partial}(\tau,\sigma))=\chi(\widehat{\Sigma}(\tau,\sigma))-k.

Hence

\chi(\Sigma(\tau,\sigma))=\chi(\Sigma^{\partial}(\tau,\sigma))-b(\tau,\sigma)+k.

Therefore

c_{N}(\tau)\Bigl(\prod_{a}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\alpha_{a}^{-1}\beta_{a})\Bigr)\Theta_{N}(\tau,\sigma)N^{\chi(\Sigma(\tau,\sigma))-h(\tau)}=\widetilde{\Omega}_{N}(\tau,\sigma;\alpha)N^{\chi(\Sigma^{\partial}(\tau,\sigma))-h(\tau)},

where

\widetilde{\Omega}_{N}(\tau,\sigma;\alpha):=c_{N}(\tau)\Bigl(\prod_{a}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\alpha_{a}^{-1}\beta_{a})\Bigr)\Theta_{N}(\tau,\sigma)N^{k-b(\tau,\sigma)}.

We now define
S_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
to be the finite set of all decorated punctured spanning surfaces
\Sigma=\Sigma^{\partial}(\tau,\sigma)
arising in this way, modulo orientation-preserving homeomorphisms preserving the map to
\Lambda^{(2)}

\Omega_{N}(\Sigma;\alpha):=\sum_{(\tau,\sigma):\Sigma^{\partial}(\tau,\sigma)=\Sigma}\widetilde{\Omega}_{N}(\tau,\sigma;\alpha),

and let

which is well-defined because the decoration remembers the local walled Brauer data. Grouping the finite expansion of Proposition

according to the punctured spanning surface produced yields

\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)=\sum_{\Sigma\in S_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)}\Omega_{N}(\Sigma;\alpha)N^{\chi(\Sigma)-h(\Sigma)}.

This proves the result.
∎

We can now prove the topological expansion of Wilson loop expectations.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We combine Theorem
1.1
that gives an expression of the Wilson loop expectation as a sum over plaquette decorations, and Theorem
2.18
that gives a finite expansion of topological coefficients, which yields

\mathbb{E}[W_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(U)]=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\alpha}\kappa_{\Lambda,Q}(\alpha)\sum_{\Sigma^{\prime}\in S_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)}\Omega_{N}(\Sigma^{\prime};\alpha)N^{\chi(\Sigma^{\prime})-h(\Sigma^{\prime})}.

The result follows from an identification of the spectral measure
\widehat{\mu}_{\Lambda,Q}
.
∎

To conclude this section, let us mention that Theorem
1.2
should be understood as an exact topological expansion at the level of moments. As such, it naturally mixes the connected components carrying the marked loop boundaries with closed vacuum components. Indeed, for
\Sigma\in S_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
, one may decompose

\Sigma=\Sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}\sqcup\Sigma_{\mathrm{vac}},

where
\Sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}
is the union of the connected components meeting at least one of the marked boundary components
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
, and
\Sigma_{\mathrm{vac}}
is the union of the remaining connected components. The latter are closed connected components, namely vacuum bubbles. Thus the exact expansion of Theorem
1.2
naturally mixes two kinds of contributions: the components carrying the loop boundaries, and the closed vacuum components independent of the loops. This is why the coarse topological expansion is exact but not yet the optimal form for large-
N
analysis. A connected version could be obtained by refining the decorated surface classes so as to remember the connected-component decomposition prior to quotienting, and then applying the standard exponential formula for vacuum components together with Möbius inversion on set partitions for Wilson loop cumulants. We do not develop this refinement here, since for the applications of the present paper the more useful reorganization is the local defect-ratio formalism of the next section, which absorbs the vacuum bubbles into the background partition function.

3
Local channel model and defect partition functions

Section
2
produced two complementary geometric descriptions of the coefficients
\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha).
However, they are not yet the most convenient form for the later developments of the paper. Even in the refined formulation of Theorem
2.18
, the local building blocks are assembled into a global spanning surface, so that formulas still depends on global connectedness, boundary components, capping operations, and possible vacuum bubbles. For the purposes of defect partition functions and of the later master-loop analysis, one would instead like a genuinely finite-range description in which the coefficients are written directly in terms of local data attached to plaquettes, non-tree edges, and their incidences.

The goal of the present section is to extract exactly this local content from the refined geometric picture of Section
2
. After gauge fixing along a spanning tree, each plaquette insertion can be resolved into finitely many local mixed Schur–Weyl channels, and the remaining Haar integrations can then be compressed edgewise into explicit local kernels. This yields a finite-range channel model on the dual incidence graph: the global surface topology is forgotten, while the local representation-theoretic data responsible for the coefficients
\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
are retained in a form adapted to locality. This local form is the key input for the exact defect-ratio representation and for the locality statement of the master loop equation proved later.

In contrast with many previous dual formulations of lattice gauge theory in terms of spin foams
[
56
,
57
,
14
,
8
]
, we do not dualize the full Yang–Mills measure at once. Instead, we work after the universal spectral/topological splitting of Theorem
1.1
: the dependence on the plaquette action is entirely carried by the spectral weights
\kappa_{\Lambda,Q}(\alpha)
, whereas the present section reconstructs the topological coefficients
\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
as a local channel partition function. After summing against the spectral measure, this yields an exact defect-ratio formula for Wilson loop expectations.

3.1
The dual incidence graph

Fix a finite connected lattice
\Lambda
, and let
T\subset E(\Lambda)
be a spanning tree. We define the
dual incidence graph

D_{T}(\Lambda)
to be the bipartite graph whose vertex set is

V\left(D_{T}(\Lambda)\right)=P(\Lambda)\sqcup\bigl(E(\Lambda)\setminus T\bigr),

and with one incidence edge
(p,e)
between a plaquette vertex
p\in P(\Lambda)
and an edge vertex
e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T

whenever
e\subset\partial p
. Hence, the plaquette vertices of
D_{T}(\Lambda)
record the face variables, whereas the edge vertices record the non-tree holonomies that remain after gauge fixing. The graph is bipartite: plaquette vertices only connect to non-tree edge vertices, and an incidence edge simply records that the corresponding non-tree edge appears in the boundary of the plaquette. An example in two dimensions is given in Figure
8
.

Figure 8:
The dual incidence graph of a finite square lattice in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
for a given spanning tree (in bold). Plaquette vertices are red disks, edge vertices are grey squares, incidence edges are in blue.

This graph is the natural local carrier for the coefficient

\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
for the following reason: after gauge fixing along
T
, every plaquette holonomy
U_{\partial p}
can be rewritten as a word involving only the non-tree edge variables. Accordingly, the tensor associated with the plaquette label
\alpha_{p}
has external legs only at the non-tree incidences
(p,e)
with
e\subset\partial p
,
e\notin T
. On the other hand, after all plaquettes have been locally resolved, the remaining Haar integrations are performed independently over the non-tree edge variables
U_{e}
, and therefore couple only the plaquette tensors adjacent to the same non-tree edge
e
, together with the possible traversals of the loop family
{\mathcal{L}}
through
e
. In other words:

–

the
plaquette side
of the model is localized at the plaquette vertices
p
of

D_{T}(\Lambda)
;

–

the
edgewise Haar couplings
are localized at the edge vertices
e
of

D_{T}(\Lambda)
;

–

the
channel variables
live naturally on the incidence edges
(p,e)
of

D_{T}(\Lambda)
.

From this viewpoint, the global surface expansion of Section
2
should be understood as a first, geometric organization of the coefficients, while the present section extracts from it the local representation-theoretic data that remain visible after forgetting the global topology of the surface. The dual incidence graph is precisely the combinatorial skeleton on which this local data lives.

It is worth emphasizing that this graph does not arise ad hoc. It is already implicit in the gauge-fixed form of lattice Yang–Mills. Indeed, once a spanning tree is chosen, the independent group variables are the non-tree edge holonomies, and each plaquette contributes a local tensor depending only on the subset of those variables appearing in its gauge-fixed boundary word. Thus the pair

\{\text{plaquettes}\}\longleftrightarrow\{\text{non-tree edges}\}

together with their incidences is the minimal combinatorial structure needed to record which plaquette tensors can interact through Haar integration. In this sense,
D_{T}(\Lambda)
is the natural higher-dimensional analogue of the factor graph underlying a tensor-network or spin-foam description: plaquette labels play the role of face variables, the incidence edges carry local intertwining/channel data, and the edge vertices implement the local contraction constraints.

This interpretation also explains why the dual incidence graph is the correct framework for the later defect-ratio and master-loop analysis. A loop family
{\mathcal{L}}
enters the gauge-fixed integral only through the non-tree edges it traverses, therefore its effect is localized on the finite set of edge vertices

D({\mathcal{L}}):=\{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T:\text{ some loop of }{\mathcal{L}}\text{ traverses }e\},

which we shall call the defect support. The observable is thus represented inside one and the same local background model on
D_{T}(\Lambda)
, with the loop family modifying only finitely many edge factors.

3.2
Gauge-fixed local resolutions

The starting point will be a local gauge-fixed version of Theorem
2.11
. For a plaquette
p\in P(\Lambda)
, let
\partial^{T}p:=\{(p,e):e\subset\partial p,\ e\notin T\}
be the set of incidences of
p
with the non-tree edges. In terms of the dual incidence graph, it corresponds to all edges incident to the plaquette
p
, seen as a vertex of
D_{T}(\Lambda)
. Let
\alpha_{p},\ n_{p}^{\pm},\ r_{p}
be defined as in Section
2
. Write
T_{p}:=V^{\otimes n_{p}^{+}}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes n_{p}^{-}}
, and let

be the projector onto the isotypic component associated to
(\lambda_{p}^{+},\lambda_{p}^{-})
of the mixed Schur–Weyl duality. Recall that by (
20
), the rational character is expressed by means of
P_{p}

(26)

\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(g)=\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{p}}\big(P_{p}\rho_{p}(g)\big),\qquad g\in{\mathrm{U}}(N),

where
\rho_{p}=\rho_{n_{p}^{+},n_{p}^{-}}
denotes the natural mixed-tensor representation. We now fix the spanning tree
T\subset E(\Lambda)
and gauge-fix along

{\mathrm{U}}(N)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a finite connected lattice, let
T\subset E(\Lambda)
be a spanning tree, and let
\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}
be a plaquette decoration. Then the coefficient

\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)=\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}W_{{\mathcal{L}}}(U)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})dU

is unchanged if one performs a rooted-tree gauge fixing sending all tree-edge holonomies to the identity. In particular, after such a gauge fixing, each plaquette character depends only on the non-tree edges contained in its gauge-fixed boundary word.

In this gauge, the boundary holonomy of
p
is represented by a cyclic word

w_{p}^{T}=x_{p,1}^{\varepsilon_{p,1}}\cdots x_{p,m_{p}}^{\varepsilon_{p,m_{p}}},

where each
x_{p,r}\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T
is a non-tree edge adjacent to
p
,
\varepsilon_{p,r}\in\{\pm 1\}
, and
m_{p}
is the number of non-tree edge occurrences in the gauge-fixed boundary word of
p
. Equivalently,

(27)

U_{\partial p}=U_{x_{p,1}}^{\varepsilon_{p,1}}\cdots U_{x_{p,m_{p}}}^{\varepsilon_{p,m_{p}}}.

For
1\leq r\leq m_{p}
, set

R_{p,r}(U):=\rho_{p}\big(U_{x_{p,r}}^{\varepsilon_{p,r}}\big)\in{\mathrm{End}}(T_{p}).

Then (
26
) and (
27
)
give

(28)

\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})=\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{p}}\Big(P_{p}R_{p,1}(U)\cdots R_{p,m_{p}}(U)\Big).

Since
P_{p}
commutes with the
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-action on
T_{p}
, it commutes with every operator
R_{p,r}(U)
. Using also
P_{p}^{2}=P_{p}
, we may insert a copy of
P_{p}
between consecutive letters:

(29)

\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})=\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{p}}\Big(P_{p}R_{p,1}(U)P_{p}R_{p,2}(U)\cdots P_{p}R_{p,m_{p}}(U)\Big).

We now expand each copy of
P_{p}
using Corollary
2.9
. We have

P_{p}=\sum_{\tau\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n_{p}^{+},n_{p}^{-}}}c_{p,N}(\tau)\rho_{N}(\tau),

and the sum is finite. It yields

(30)

\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})=\sum_{(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m_{p}})\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n_{p}^{+},n_{p}^{-}}^{m_{p}}}\Big(\prod_{r=1}^{m_{p}}c_{p,N}(\tau_{r})\Big)\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{p}}\Big(\rho_{N}(\tau_{1})R_{p,1}(U)\cdots\rho_{N}(\tau_{m_{p}})R_{p,m_{p}}(U)\Big).

Fix a tuple
\tau=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m_{p}})\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n_{p}^{+},n_{p}^{-}}^{m_{p}}.
If we use cyclic multi-indices
I_{r},J_{r}\in[N]^{r_{p}},\ 1\leq r\leq m_{p},
with the convention
I_{m_{p}+1}=I_{1}
, then we can rewrite

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{p}}\Big(\rho_{N}(\tau_{1})R_{p,1}(U)\cdots\rho_{N}(\tau_{m_{p}})R_{p,m_{p}}(U)\Big)

(31)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{I_{\bullet},J_{\bullet}}\prod_{r=1}^{m_{p}}\big(\rho_{N}(\tau_{r})\big)_{I_{r},J_{r}}\big(R_{p,r}(U)\big)_{J_{r},I_{r+1}}.

Now each factor
\big(R_{p,r}(U)\big)_{J_{r},I_{r+1}}
splits into a product of
r_{p}
matrix entries of the single unitary
U_{x_{p,r}}
: for each slot
u\in\{1,\ldots,r_{p}\}
, it contributes one entry of
U_{x_{p,r}}
or of
\overline{U_{x_{p,r}}}
, according to

–

whether the slot
u
lies in the covariant part
V^{\otimes n_{p}^{+}}

or the contravariant part
(V^{*})^{\otimes n_{p}^{-}}
, and

–

whether
\varepsilon_{p,r}=+1
or
\varepsilon_{p,r}=-1
.

Therefore, before any Haar integration over the non-tree edge variables is performed, the expression in (
31

(p,e)\in\partial^{T}p

\mathcal{H}_{p,e}

or

(r,u)
such that

Edgewise Haar pairings and compression

\alpha

For a plaquette decoration
\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}

compatible channel field

\Gamma=(\Gamma_{p,e})_{(p,e):e\in\partial^{T}p}

p
one has

\Gamma_{\partial p}:=(\Gamma_{p,e})_{(p,e)\in\partial^{T}p}\in\mathcal{C}_{p}(\alpha_{p}).

We denote by
\mathcal{C}(\alpha)
the set of such channel fields. If
{\mathcal{L}}=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{k})
is a loop family and
e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T
is a non-tree edge, the local channel datum at
e
is written
\Gamma_{e}:=(\Gamma_{p,e})_{p\ni e}
. Given a global channel field
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}(\alpha)
, let
{\mathcal{L}}_{e}^{+}(\Gamma,\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{e}^{-}(\Gamma,\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})
be the ordered collections of positive and negative local legs at
e
, obtained by combining:

–

the external legs produced by the adjacent plaquette resolutions;

–

the loop traversals of
{\mathcal{L}}
through
e
.

If
|L_{e}^{+}|\neq|L_{e}^{-}|
, set
K_{e}(\Gamma_{e};\alpha,{\mathcal{L}}):=0
.

Otherwise, let
\mathcal{P}_{e}(\Gamma,\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})
be the finite set of local Weingarten pairings
between
{\mathcal{L}}_{e}^{+}
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{e}^{-}
. Then we define the
local edge kernel
by

K_{e}(\Gamma_{e};\alpha,{\mathcal{L}}):=\sum_{\sigma_{e}\in\mathcal{P}_{e}(\Gamma,\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\sigma_{e})\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\sigma_{e},\Gamma,\alpha,{\mathcal{L}}),

where the indicator records the Kronecker-delta compatibility imposed by the local tensor legs. The construction of the local edge kernel
K_{e}(\Gamma_{e};\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})
is illustrated in Figures

and
11
, respectively in the cases without and with loop insertions.

Lemma 3.5
.

Fix a plaquette decoration
\alpha
and a global local-resolution field
r=(r_{p})_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\in R(\alpha):=\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}R_{p}(\alpha_{p}).
After gauge fixing along
T
and substituting all plaquette resolutions, the integrand defining

\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
factorizes over the non-tree edges:

W_{{\mathcal{L}}}^{T}(U)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\Phi_{p,r_{p}}=\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}\Psi_{e}(U_{e};r_{\partial e},\alpha,{\mathcal{L}}),

where
r_{\partial e}:=(r_{p})_{p\ni e}
and each factor
\Psi_{e}
depends only on:

–

the single Haar variable
U_{e}
,

–

the local resolutions adjacent to
e
,

–

the traversals of the loop family
{\mathcal{L}}
through
e
.

In particular, no variable
U_{e}
interacts with any variable
U_{f}
for
e\neq f
.

Proof.

Fix a plaquette decoration
\alpha
and a global local-resolution field

r=(r_{p})_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\in R(\alpha)=\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}R_{p}(\alpha_{p}).

After gauge fixing along the spanning tree
T
, all tree-edge holonomies are equal to the identity, so the only remaining group variables are the non-tree edge variables

(U_{e})_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}.

For each plaquette
p
, the elementary tensor
\Phi_{p,r_{p}}
is obtained from the gauge-fixed plaquette word

w_{p}^{T}=x_{p,1}^{\varepsilon_{p,1}}\cdots x_{p,m_{p}}^{\varepsilon_{p,m_{p}}}

by the construction of Section
3.2
. In particular, in the index expansion (
31
), every factor
(R_{p,r}(U))_{J_{r},I_{r+1}}
is a product of matrix entries of the
single
unitary
U_{x_{p,r}}
, while the coefficients coming from
(\rho_{N}(\tau_{r}))_{I_{r},J_{r}}
do not involve any group variable. Hence every monomial appearing in
\Phi_{p,r_{p}}
is a product of factors, each of which depends on exactly one non-tree edge variable
U_{e}
.

Moreover, by construction of the local resolutions, all internal contractions have already been performed inside the plaquette tensor, and the only free legs of
\Phi_{p,r_{p}}
are the external legs attached to incidences

(p,e)\in\partial^{T}p.

Therefore
\Phi_{p,r_{p}}
may be written as a tensor product over the non-tree edges adjacent to
p
:

\Phi_{p,r_{p}}=\bigotimes_{e\subset\partial p,\ e\notin T}\Phi^{(e)}_{p,r_{p}}(U_{e}),

where
\Phi^{(e)}_{p,r_{p}}(U_{e})
collects exactly the matrix-entry factors of
\Phi_{p,r_{p}}
whose edge label is
e
. If
e\not\subset\partial p
, we set
\Phi^{(e)}_{p,r_{p}}(U_{e}):=1
.

The same remark applies to the gauge-fixed loop insertion tensor
W_{{\mathcal{L}}}^{T}(U)
: after gauge fixing, each traversal of a loop through a non-tree edge
e
contributes a matrix entry of
U_{e}
or
\overline{U_{e}}
, and different traversals through different non-tree edges involve different
group variables. Hence

W_{{\mathcal{L}}}^{T}(U)=\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}W_{{\mathcal{L}},e}^{T}(U_{e}),

where
W_{{\mathcal{L}},e}^{T}(U_{e})
is the product of the loop matrix-entry factors coming from the traversals of
{\mathcal{L}}
through
e
, and is equal to
1
if no loop traverses
e
.

Combining these factorizations gives

W_{{\mathcal{L}}}^{T}(U)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\Phi_{p,r_{p}}=\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}\left(W_{{\mathcal{L}},e}^{T}(U_{e})\prod_{p\ni e}\Phi^{(e)}_{p,r_{p}}(U_{e})\right).

Define

\Psi_{e}(U_{e};r_{\partial e},\alpha,{\mathcal{L}}):=W_{{\mathcal{L}},e}^{T}(U_{e})\prod_{p\ni e}\Phi^{(e)}_{p,r_{p}}(U_{e}),\qquad r_{\partial e}:=(r_{p})_{p\ni e}.

By construction,
\Psi_{e}
depends only on:

–

the single Haar variable
U_{e}
,

–

the local resolutions adjacent to
e
,

–

the traversals of the loop family
{\mathcal{L}}
through
e
.

This yields the factorization

W_{{\mathcal{L}}}^{T}(U)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\Phi_{p,r_{p}}=\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}\Psi_{e}(U_{e};r_{\partial e},\alpha,{\mathcal{L}}).

In particular, each factor in the product depends on a single coordinate
U_{e}
, so no variable

U_{e}
interacts with any variable
U_{f}
for
e\neq f
.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Fix a plaquette decoration
\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}
. By Lemma
3.1
, after gauge fixing along the spanning tree
T
, the coefficient
\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
may be written
as

\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)=\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)\setminus T}}W_{\mathcal{L}}^{T}(U)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}dU_{e},

where
W_{\mathcal{L}}^{T}(U)
is the gauge-fixed loop insertion tensor and
\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})
is the gauge-fixed plaquette tensor attached to
p
.

For each plaquette
p
, Proposition
3.3
gives the finite local resolution

\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})=\sum_{r_{p}\in R_{p}(\alpha_{p})}a_{p}^{N}(r_{p};\alpha_{p})\Phi_{p,r_{p}}.

Since the lattice is finite, the product

R(\alpha):=\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}R_{p}(\alpha_{p})

is finite. Expanding the product of the plaquette resolutions, we obtain

\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)=\sum_{r\in R(\alpha)}\left(\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}a_{p}^{N}(r_{p};\alpha_{p})\right)I(r),

where

I(r):=\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)\setminus T}}W_{\mathcal{L}}^{T}(U)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\Phi_{p,r_{p}}\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}dU_{e}.

Now fix
r=(r_{p})_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\in R(\alpha)
. By Lemma
3.5
, the integrand factors over the non-tree edges:

W_{\mathcal{L}}^{T}(U)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\Phi_{p,r_{p}}=\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}\Psi_{e}(U_{e};r_{\partial e},\alpha,{\mathcal{L}}),

with
r_{\partial e}:=(r_{p})_{p\ni e}
. Since the Haar measure is a product measure, Fubini’s theorem gives

I(r)=\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}\Psi_{e}(U_{e};r_{\partial e},\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})dU_{e}.

Fix a non-tree edge
e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T
. By construction,
\Psi_{e}(U_{e};r_{\partial e},\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})
is a finite linear combination of monomials in matrix entries of
U_{e}
and
\overline{U_{e}}
, or equivalently a local mixed tensor with some number of positive
e
-legs and some number of negative
e
-legs. If these two numbers do not coincide,
the integral over
U_{e}
vanishes by the standard balance criterion for Haar integration on
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
. Otherwise, the ordinary unitary Weingarten formula applies and yields a finite sum over local pairings of the positive and negative
e
-legs. Therefore

\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}\Psi_{e}(U_{e};r_{\partial e},\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})dU_{e}=\sum_{\sigma_{e}}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\sigma_{e})\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\sigma_{e},r_{\partial e},\alpha,{\mathcal{L}}),

where
\sigma_{e}
ranges over the local edgewise Weingarten pairings at
e
, and the indicator encodes the Kronecker-delta compatibility imposed by the local tensor legs.

Substituting this into the previous formula, we obtain

\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)=\sum_{r\in R(\alpha)}\left(\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}a_{p}^{N}(r_{p};\alpha_{p})\right)\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}\left(\sum_{\sigma_{e}}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\sigma_{e})\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\sigma_{e},r_{\partial e},\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})\right).

Expanding the product over the non-tree edges gives a finite sum over pairs
(r,\sigma)
, where
\sigma=(\sigma_{e})_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}
:

\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)=\sum_{r\in R(\alpha)}\sum_{\sigma}\left(\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}a_{p}^{N}(r_{p};\alpha_{p})\right)\left(\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\sigma_{e})\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\sigma_{e},r_{\partial e},\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})\right).

For each plaquette
p
, the local resolution
r_{p}
determines a boundary-channel tuple

\Gamma_{\partial p}(r_{p};\alpha_{p})\in C_{p}(\alpha_{p}).

Hence every global local-resolution field
r\in R(\alpha)
determines a compatible channel field

\Gamma(r)=\bigl(\Gamma_{p,e}(r_{p};\alpha_{p})\bigr)_{(p,e):e\in\partial^{T}p}\in\mathcal{C}(\alpha).

We now regroup the finite sum according to the value of
\Gamma(r)
:

\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)=\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}(\alpha)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in R(\alpha)\\
\Gamma(r)=\Gamma\end{subarray}}\sum_{\sigma}\left(\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}a_{p}^{N}(r_{p};\alpha_{p})\right)\left(\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\sigma_{e})\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\sigma_{e},r_{\partial e},\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})\right).

Fix such a channel field
\Gamma
. Since the condition
\Gamma(r)=\Gamma
is plaquettewise, the sum over the plaquette resolutions factorizes:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in R(\alpha)\\
\Gamma(r)=\Gamma\end{subarray}}\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}a_{p}^{N}(r_{p};\alpha_{p})=\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{p}\in R_{p}(\alpha_{p})\\
\Gamma_{\partial p}(r_{p};\alpha_{p})=\Gamma_{\partial p}\end{subarray}}a_{p}^{N}(r_{p};\alpha_{p}).

The inner sum is exactly
A_{p}(\Gamma_{\partial p};\alpha_{p}),
so the plaquette contribution becomes

\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}A_{p}(\Gamma_{\partial p};\alpha_{p}).

Likewise, once the local channel datum
\Gamma_{e}:=(\Gamma_{p,e})_{p\ni e}
is fixed at a non-tree edge
e
, the remaining sum over local edgewise pairing data depends only on the local positive and negative legs at
e
, hence only on
\Gamma_{e}
,
\alpha
, and the loop traversals through
e
. By Definition of the compressed edge kernel, this contribution is precisely
K_{e}(\Gamma_{e};\alpha,{\mathcal{L}}).
Therefore the total edge contribution factorizes as

\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}K_{e}(\Gamma_{e};\alpha,{\mathcal{L}}).

Combining the plaquette and edge factorizations yields the expected formula.

Finally, assertions (i)–(iii) are immediate from the construction:

–

A_{p}
depends only on the single label
\alpha_{p}
and the channel tuple around
p
by Definition
3.4
;

–

K_{e}
depends only on the local data adjacent to the edge-vertex
e
in the dual incidence graph, namely the channel data at the incidences
(p,e)
, the adjacent plaquette labels, and the loop traversals through
e
;

–

the loop family enters only through the factors
K_{e}
, never through the plaquette amplitudes
A_{p}
.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.4
Defect partition functions

In this subsection, we combine the previous statements, in particular Theorem
1.3
, into the defect partition function of the dual incidence graph. Let us first recall the definition of the defect partition function from (
8
):

\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda,Q}^{({\mathcal{L}})}:=\sum_{\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}}\kappa_{\Lambda,Q}(\alpha)\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}(\alpha)}\Bigl(\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}A_{p}(\Gamma_{\partial p};\alpha_{p})\Bigr)\Bigl(\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)\setminus T}K_{e}(\Gamma_{e};\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})\Bigr).

The only remaining part is the following.

Proposition 3.6
.

For every compatible pair
(\alpha,\Gamma)
and every non-tree edge
e\notin D({\mathcal{L}})
, one has

K_{e}(\Gamma_{e};\alpha,{\mathcal{L}})=K_{e}(\Gamma_{e};\alpha,\varnothing).

Equivalently, the loop family modifies the compressed weight only through the finitely many edge kernels indexed by the defect support
D({\mathcal{L}})
.

Proof.

The only dependence on
{\mathcal{L}}
in the local Haar integral at
e
comes from the loop-insertion space carried by the traversals of
{\mathcal{L}}
through
e
. If
e\notin D({\mathcal{L}})
, that insertion space is trivial, exactly as in the empty-loop case. Hence the local kernel is unchanged.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

By Theorem
1.1
,

\mathbb{E}[W_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(U)]=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}}\kappa_{\Lambda,Q}(\alpha)\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha).

Substituting the compressed expansion of Theorem
1.3
identifies the numerator with
\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda,Q}^{({\mathcal{L}})}
.

When
{\mathcal{L}}=\varnothing
, the observable is identically equal to
1
, so the same identity gives

1=\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda,Q}^{(0)}}{Z}.

Hence
\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda,Q}^{(0)}=Z
, and therefore

\mathbb{E}[W_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(U)]=\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda,Q}^{({\mathcal{L}})}}{\mathcal{Z}_{\Lambda,Q}^{(0)}}.

The fact that both partition functions differ from a finite number of local factors is a consequence of Proposition
3.6
.
∎

4
The master loop equation

In this section we derive the universal coefficientwise master loop equation and make its closure on the topological coefficients completely explicit. Starting from a Haar integration-by-parts identity, we will identify its two main contributions: the classical cut-and-join loop term, and a new Fourier-side local recoupling term. We will then prove Theorem
1.5
.

4.1
Edge derivatives and integration by parts

Let
\Lambda
be a finite connected oriented lattice, with oriented edge set
E(\Lambda)
and oriented plaquette set
P(\Lambda)
. For a configuration
U=(U_{e})_{e\in E(\Lambda)}\in{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)},
and an oriented path
\gamma=e_{1}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots e_{m}^{\varepsilon_{m}}
,
\varepsilon_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
, write
U_{\gamma}:=U_{e_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots U_{e_{m}}^{\varepsilon_{m}}.
For a loop family
{\mathcal{L}}=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{r})
, set
W_{{\mathcal{L}}}(U):=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}}).
Let us endow the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}(N)=\{X\in M_{N}(\mathbb{C}):\ X^{*}=-X\}
with the invariant inner product
\langle X,Y\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(XY^{*}),
which corresponds to the Hilbert–Schmidt norm on
M_{N}(\mathbb{C})
. Let
(X_{a})_{a=1}^{N^{2}}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathfrak{u}(N)
. For this inner product, one has the so-called
magic formulas

(35)

\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}X_{a}^{2}=-NI_{N},\qquad\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}X_{a}AX_{a}=-\mathrm{Tr}(A)I_{N},\qquad\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(X_{a}A)\mathrm{Tr}(X_{a}B)=-\mathrm{Tr}(AB).

The name “magic formulas” is due to Driver–Hall–Kemp
[
23
]
, but they were already known before by Sengupta
[
58
]
. Mind that we use a different normalization for the inner product on
\mathfrak{u}(N)
, which yields a different scaling in
N
.

For
e\in E(\Lambda)
and
F\in\mathscr{C}^{1}({\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)})
, define

(\partial_{e,a}F)(U):=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}F\bigl((U_{e^{\prime}})_{e^{\prime}\neq e},e^{tX_{a}}U_{e}\bigr).

For
F\in\mathscr{C}^{2}({\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)})
, define by
\Delta_{e}F:=\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}\partial_{e,a}^{2}F
the local Laplacian acting on
U_{e}
. For a plaquette decoration
\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}
, define

F_{{\mathcal{L}},\alpha}(U):=W_{{\mathcal{L}}}(U)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p}).

Proposition 4.1
.

For every oriented edge
e\in E(\Lambda)
, every loop family
{\mathcal{L}}
, and every plaquette decoration

\alpha:P(\Lambda)\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}
, one has

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}\partial_{e,a}\Bigl((\partial_{e,a}W_{{\mathcal{L}}})\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})\Bigr)dU

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}(\Delta_{e}W_{{\mathcal{L}}})\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})dU

(36)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{p\ni e}\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}(\partial_{e,a}W_{{\mathcal{L}}})\partial_{e,a}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})\prod_{q\neq p}\chi_{\alpha_{q}}(U_{\partial q})dU.

Proof.

Fix
e\in E(\Lambda)
and
a\in\{1,\dots,N^{2}\}
. For any smooth function
G\in C^{1}({\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)})
, Haar invariance on the copy of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
attached to the edge
e
implies

\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}G\bigl((U_{e^{\prime}})_{e^{\prime}\neq e},e^{tX_{a}}U_{e}\bigr)dU=\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}G(U)dU\qquad(t\in\mathbb{R}),

because the product Haar measure is invariant under left translation in each coordinate. Differentiating at
t=0
gives

\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}\partial_{e,a}G(U)dU=0.

Apply this identity to

G(U):=(\partial_{e,a}W_{{\mathcal{L}}})(U)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p}).

We obtain

0=\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}\partial_{e,a}\left((\partial_{e,a}W_{{\mathcal{L}}})(U)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})\right)dU.

Summing over
a=1,\dots,N^{2}
yields the first displayed identity in the statement.

We now expand the derivative by the Leibniz rule. Since

\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}\partial_{e,a}^{2}W_{{\mathcal{L}}}=\Delta_{e}W_{{\mathcal{L}}},

the contribution in which the derivative falls twice on
W_{{\mathcal{L}}}
is

\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}(\Delta_{e}W_{{\mathcal{L}}})(U)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})dU.

For the derivative of the plaquette part, note that
\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})
depends on

U_{e}
if and only if the edge
e
lies on the boundary of
p
. Therefore

\partial_{e,a}\left(\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})\right)=\sum_{p\ni e}\bigl(\partial_{e,a}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})\bigr)\prod_{q\neq p}\chi_{\alpha_{q}}(U_{\partial q}).

Substituting this into the previous identity gives

0=\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}(\Delta_{e}W_{{\mathcal{L}}})(U)\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})dU+\sum_{p\ni e}\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}(\partial_{e,a}W_{{\mathcal{L}}})\partial_{e,a}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})\prod_{q\neq p}\chi_{\alpha_{q}}(U_{\partial q})dU,

which is exactly the second displayed identity.
∎

Proposition
4.1
contains the essential information for the master loop equation, and it remains to find the right interpretation. Equation (
36
) can be rewritten as a relationship between two terms:

(37)

I_{\Delta}+I_{\mathrm{mix}}=0

where

I_{\Delta}=\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}(\Delta_{e}W_{{\mathcal{L}}})\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})dU,

and

I_{\mathrm{mix}}=\sum_{p\ni e}\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}(\partial_{e,a}W_{{\mathcal{L}}})\partial_{e,a}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})\prod_{q\neq p}\chi_{\alpha_{q}}(U_{\partial q})dU.

We will provide interpretations of these terms in separate subsections.

4.2
Cut-and-join term

Let us start with
I_{\Delta}
. Its interpretation will involve standard local loop surgeries, that can be found in
[
6
,
32
,
60
,
62
]
for instance. In particular, all surgeries we present here are conveniently illustrated in the aforementioned papers and we advise the reader to look at them for a better understanding. The main result will be Proposition
4.6
. Before we state it, recall a bit of terminology.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{k})
be a finite family of loops. In order to describe local surgeries at an active edge, we choose for each loop
\ell_{i}
a cyclic word representative

w_{\ell_{i}}=e_{i,1}^{\varepsilon_{i,1}}\cdots e_{i,m_{i}}^{\varepsilon_{i,m_{i}}},\qquad\varepsilon_{i,r}\in\{\pm 1\},

in oriented edges of
\Lambda
. We write
|\ell_{i}|:=m_{i},

|{\mathcal{L}}|:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\ell_{i}|.
All constructions below are independent of the choice of cyclic representative, up to cyclic rotation of the resulting loops. When needed, we denote by
[w]
the equivalence class of the word
w
for backtracking equivalence. We denote by
\widehat{D}_{i}({\mathcal{L}})
the set of all edges
e\in E(\Lambda)
such that
e
or
e^{-1}
is contained in
\ell_{i}
, and by
D_{i}({\mathcal{L}})
a fixed subset of
\widehat{D}_{i}({\mathcal{L}})
keeping only one oriented edge among
e
and
e^{-1}
. For
e\in D_{i}({\mathcal{L}})
, define

A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}):=\{r\in\{1,\dots,m_{i}\}:e_{i,r}^{\varepsilon_{i,r}}=e\},\qquad B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}):=\{r\in\{1,\dots,m_{i}\}:e_{i,r}^{\varepsilon_{i,r}}=e^{-1}\},

and

C_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}):=A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})\cup B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}),\qquad m_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}):=|C_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})|.

For
j\neq i
, define similarly
A_{j}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
,
B_{j}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
and
C_{j}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
.

Definition 4.2

(Splittings)
.

Let us fix
e\in D_{i}({\mathcal{L}})
, and

\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)=\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}F_{{\mathcal{L}},\alpha}(U)dU.

Let
e\in E(\Lambda)
be an oriented edge. For a loop family
{\mathcal{L}}
and a plaquette decoration
\alpha
, define

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{e}\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=-N\sum_{i:e\in D_{i}}m_{i}(e)\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i:e\in D_{i}}\ \sum_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\in S^{-}_{i,e}({\mathcal{L}})}\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}(\alpha)-\sum_{i:e\in D_{i}}\ \sum_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\in S^{+}_{i,e}({\mathcal{L}})}\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i:e\in D_{i}}\ \sum_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\in M^{-}_{U,i,e}({\mathcal{L}})}\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}(\alpha)-\sum_{i:e\in D_{i}}\ \sum_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\in M^{+}_{U,i,e}({\mathcal{L}})}\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}(\alpha).

This operator
\mathscr{L}_{e}
can be described as follows: it turns the topological coefficient associated to the loop family
{\mathcal{L}}
into a linear combination, with coefficients
\pm N^{\pm 1}
, of topological coefficients associated to loop families
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
obtained from
{\mathcal{L}}
by splitting and merger. The next proposition shows that it encodes the behavior of the Laplacian acting on the loop part of the coefficient, namely
I_{\Delta}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

For every oriented edge
e
, loop sequence
{\mathcal{L}}
, and plaquette decoration
\alpha
, one has

I_{\Delta}:=\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}(\Delta_{e}W_{{\mathcal{L}}}(U))\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})dU=(\mathscr{L}_{e}\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}})(\alpha).

Proof.

We prove first a pointwise identity for
\Delta_{e}W_{{\mathcal{L}}}
, and then integrate against the undifferentiated plaquette factors
\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})
. Fix an oriented edge
e\in E(\Lambda)
. For each
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, choose the cyclic representative

w_{\ell_{i}}=e_{i,1}^{\varepsilon_{i,1}}\cdots e_{i,m_{i}}^{\varepsilon_{i,m_{i}}},\qquad\varepsilon_{i,r}\in\{\pm 1\}.

If
x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
, write
w_{\ell_{i}}=a_{x}eb_{x},
and if
x\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
, write
w_{\ell_{i}}=a_{x}e^{-1}b_{x}.
By differentiation, we have

\partial_{e,a}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})=\sum_{x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})}\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(U_{a_{x}}X_{a}U_{e}U_{b_{x}}\bigr)-\sum_{x\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})}\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(U_{a_{x}}U_{e}^{-1}X_{a}U_{b_{x}}\bigr).

Using cyclicity of the trace, this may be rewritten as

(38)

\partial_{e,a}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})=\sum_{x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})}\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(X_{a}U_{eb_{x}a_{x}}\bigr)-\sum_{x\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})}\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(X_{a}U_{b_{x}a_{x}e^{-1}}\bigr).

Since
W_{{\mathcal{L}}}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})
, the first derivative is

\partial_{e,a}W_{{\mathcal{L}}}=\sum_{i:e\in D_{i}({\mathcal{L}})}\left(\prod_{j\neq i}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{j}})\right)\partial_{e,a}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}}),

and therefore, by differentiating once more and summing over
a
,

\Delta_{e}W_{{\mathcal{L}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\prod_{j\neq i}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{j}})\right)\Delta_{e}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})+\sum_{i\neq j}\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}\left(\prod_{m\neq i,j}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{m}})\right)\bigl(\partial_{e,a}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})\bigr)\bigl(\partial_{e,a}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{j}})\bigr).

We analyze separately the two terms on the right-hand side and use the magic formulas (
35
).

Step 1: second derivatives inside a single loop.

Fix
i
with
e\in D_{i}({\mathcal{L}})
. Differentiating (
38
) once more, the two derivatives may hit either the same active occurrence or two distinct active occurrences.

–

If they hit the same active occurrence
x\in C_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
, then one gets a factor
\sum_{a}X_{a}^{2}
, hence, with the normalization matching the definition of
{\mathcal{L}}_{e}
,

\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}\partial_{e,a}^{2}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})\Big|_{\text{same }x}=-N\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}}).

Summing over the
m_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})=|C_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})|
active occurrences gives the contribution

-Nm_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}}).

–

If they hit two distinct active occurrences
x\neq y\in C_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
, we have to distinguish between four cases.

(i)

If
x,y\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
, write
w_{\ell_{i}}=aebec
. Then the contribution is

\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{a}X_{a}U_{e}U_{b}X_{a}U_{e}U_{c}).

By cyclicity of the trace and the standard
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
contraction identity in the chosen
normalization,

\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(AX_{a}BX_{a})=-\mathrm{Tr}(A)\mathrm{Tr}(B),

this equals
-\mathrm{Tr}(U_{aec})\mathrm{Tr}(U_{be}),
which is exactly the Wilson weight associated with the positive splitting
(\times^{1}_{x,y}\ell_{i},\times^{2}_{x,y}\ell_{i})
.

(ii)

If
x,y\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
, writing
w_{\ell_{i}}=ae^{-1}be^{-1}c
, the same computation gives
-\mathrm{Tr}(U_{aec})\mathrm{Tr}(U_{be^{-1}}),

i.e. the positive splitting contribution again.

(iii)

If
x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
and
y\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
, write
w_{\ell_{i}}=aebe^{-1}c
. Then the second
derivative carries one minus sign from differentiating the inverse occurrence, and one gets

-\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{a}X_{a}U_{e}U_{b}U_{e}^{-1}X_{a}U_{c}).

Using cyclicity and the same contraction identity, this becomes
\mathrm{Tr}(U_{ac})\mathrm{Tr}(U_{b}),
which is exactly the contribution of the negative splitting
(\times^{1}_{x,y}\ell_{i},\times^{2}_{x,y}\ell_{i})
.

(iv)

If
x\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
and
y\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
, the case is identical to (iii) and gives the other half of

S^{-}_{i,e}({\mathcal{L}})
.

Collecting all same-loop contributions, we obtain

(39)

\displaystyle\Delta_{e}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})

\displaystyle=-Nm_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})+\sum_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\in S^{-}_{i,e}({\mathcal{L}})}W_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}^{(i)}-\sum_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\in S^{+}_{i,e}({\mathcal{L}})}W_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}^{(i)},

where
W_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}^{(i)}
means the product of traces obtained from
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
after reinserting the undifferentiated loops
\ell_{j}
,
j\neq i
.

Step 2: cross terms between two different loops.

Fix
i\neq j
with
e\in D_{i}({\mathcal{L}})\cap D_{j}({\mathcal{L}})
. By (
38
), the cross term is a sum over pairs of active occurrences
x\in C_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
,
y\in C_{j}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
.

If
x,y
have the same orientation, say
x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
,
y\in A_{j}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
, write
w_{\ell_{i}}=aeb
and
w_{\ell_{j}}=ced
. Then

\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{a}X_{a}U_{e}U_{b})\mathrm{Tr}(U_{c}X_{a}U_{e}U_{d}).

Using the contraction identity

\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(X_{a}A)\mathrm{Tr}(X_{a}B)=-\mathrm{Tr}(AB)

in the normalization matching
{\mathcal{L}}_{e}
, this becomes

-\mathrm{Tr}(U_{aedceb}),

namely the positive
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-merger
\ell_{i}\oplus_{x,y}\ell_{j}
.

If
x,y\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})\times B_{j}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
, one obtains similarly the other half of
M^{+}_{U,i,e}({\mathcal{L}})
.

If
x,y
have opposite orientations, say
x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
,
y\in B_{j}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
, there is one minus sign coming from differentiating the inverse occurrence in
\ell_{j}
, and the same contraction identity yields

\mathrm{Tr}(U_{adcb}),

which is the negative
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-merger
\ell_{i}\ominus_{x,y}\ell_{j}
. The case
x\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
,
y\in A_{j}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
is identical.

Therefore the total cross-term contribution is

(40)

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}\bigl(\partial_{e,a}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{i}})\bigr)\bigl(\partial_{e,a}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\ell_{j}})\bigr)=\sum_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\in M^{-}_{U,i,e}({\mathcal{L}};j)}W_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}-\sum_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\in M^{+}_{U,i,e}({\mathcal{L}};j)}W_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}},

where
M^{\pm}_{U,i,e}({\mathcal{L}};j)
denotes the part of the merger multiset involving the pair

(i,j)
.

Step 3: conclusion.

Substituting (
39
) and (
40
) into the product-rule expansion of
\Delta_{e}W_{{\mathcal{L}}}
, and summing over all active loops and all active pairs of loops, we obtain the pointwise identity

\Delta_{e}W_{{\mathcal{L}}}(U)=-N\sum_{i:e\in D_{i}({\mathcal{L}})}m_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})W_{{\mathcal{L}}}(U)+\sum_{i:e\in D_{i}({\mathcal{L}})}\sum_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\in S^{-}_{i,e}({\mathcal{L}})}W_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}(U)-\sum_{i:e\in D_{i}({\mathcal{L}})}\sum_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\in S^{+}_{i,e}({\mathcal{L}})}W_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}(U)

\hskip 85.35826pt+\sum_{i:e\in D_{i}({\mathcal{L}})}\sum_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\in M^{-}_{U,i,e}({\mathcal{L}})}W_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}(U)-\sum_{i:e\in D_{i}({\mathcal{L}})}\sum_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\in M^{+}_{U,i,e}({\mathcal{L}})}W_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}(U).

Multiplying this identity by
\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})
and
integrating over
{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}
gives the expected formula.
∎

4.3
Mixed terms

The next proposition isolates the genuinely new part of the coefficientwise master loop equation. Indeed, Proposition
4.6
treats the loop-Laplacian term, and this is still governed by the classical cut-and-join calculus on loop families. Its proof is therefore very close to previous proofs obtained for the Wilson action
[
6
,
32
,
60
]
. By contrast, the mixed term

\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}(\partial_{e,a}W_{{\mathcal{L}}})\partial_{e,a}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})

is the place where the Fourier expansion in plaquette variables interacts with the geometric loop sector. We will see in Proposition
4.8
that this interaction remains local: after differentiating at a fixed incidence
(e,p)
, one does not generate any nonlocal reorganization of the whole plaquette decoration, but only a finite list of local reconnection types at the active incidence together with a finite local recoupling of the active plaquette label
\alpha_{p}
. This is the mechanism behind the closure of the master loop equation on the family of topological coefficients
\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
. The resulting operator acts on the topological coefficients
\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha)
while the loop family is still kept in geometric position space.

Definition 4.7

(loop-plaquette mergers)
.

Fix an oriented incidence
(e,p)
, and choose a cyclic representative of the plaquette boundary
\partial p=aeb.
Let

\gamma_{p,u}:=\begin{cases}aeb=\partial p,&1\leq u\leq n_{p}^{+},\\[2.84526pt]
b^{-1}e^{-1}a^{-1}=(\partial p)^{-1},&n_{p}^{+}<u\leq r_{p},\end{cases}

so that covariant slots carry the positively oriented plaquette word and contravariant slots carry the oppositely oriented plaquette word.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k})
be a loop family, and let
x
be an active occurrence of
e
or
e^{-1}
in the loop
\ell_{i}
. Write

w_{\ell_{i}}=\begin{cases}ced,&x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}),\\
ce^{-1}d,&x\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}),\end{cases}

where the displayed occurrence of
e
or
e^{-1}
is the active one corresponding to
x
. The
loop-plaquette merger

\tau_{x,u}({\mathcal{L}})
is the loop family obtained from
{\mathcal{L}}
by replacing
\ell_{i}
with the loop produced by merging
\ell_{i}
and
\gamma_{p,u}
at their active occurrences. More precisely, if
y_{u}
denotes the active occurrence in the slot-word
\gamma_{p,u}
, then

\tau_{x,u}({\mathcal{L}})=\begin{cases}\text{replace }\ell_{i}\text{ by }\ell_{i}\oplus_{x,y_{u}}\gamma_{p,u},&\text{if }x\text{ and }y_{u}\text{ have the same orientation},\\
\text{replace }\ell_{i}\text{ by }\ell_{i}\ominus_{x,y_{u}}\gamma_{p,u},&\text{if }x\text{ and }y_{u}\text{ have opposite orientations}.\end{cases}

Given a loop-plaquette merger
\tau_{x,u}({\mathcal{L}})
as above, we define the auxiliary mixed tensor space

T^{\mathrm{aux}}_{x,u}:=\begin{cases}V^{\otimes(n_{p}^{+}-1)}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes n_{p}^{-}},&x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}),\ 1\leq u\leq n_{p}^{+},\\[2.84526pt]
V^{\otimes(n_{p}^{+}+1)}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes n_{p}^{-}},&x\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}),\ 1\leq u\leq n_{p}^{+},\\[2.84526pt]
V^{\otimes n_{p}^{+}}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes(n_{p}^{-}+1)},&x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}),\ n_{p}^{+}<u\leq r_{p},\\[2.84526pt]
V^{\otimes n_{p}^{+}}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes(n_{p}^{-}-1)},&x\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}),\ n_{p}^{+}<u\leq r_{p}.\end{cases}

These correspond to the following four local cases

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}\text{local type of }(x,u)&\tau_{x,u}({\mathcal{L}})&\text{plaquette operation}\\
\hline\cr x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}),\ u\leq n_{p}^{+}&\ell_{i}\oplus_{x,y_{u}}\gamma_{p,u}&\text{covariant contraction}\\
x\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}),\ u\leq n_{p}^{+}&\ell_{i}\ominus_{x,y_{u}}\gamma_{p,u}&\text{covariant coevaluation}\\
x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}),\ u>n_{p}^{+}&\ell_{i}\ominus_{x,y_{u}}\gamma_{p,u}&\text{contravariant coevaluation}\\
x\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}),\ u>n_{p}^{+}&\ell_{i}\oplus_{x,y_{u}}\gamma_{p,u}&\text{contravariant contraction}\end{array}

where
i=i(x)
denotes the index of the loop containing
x
, and
y_{u}
denotes the active occurrence in the slot-word
\gamma_{p,u}
. The next result translates the mixed term of the master loop equation in terms of these loop-plaquette mergers.

Proposition 4.8
.

Fix an oriented incidence
(e,p)
, and choose a cyclic representative
\partial p=aeb
. Write
\alpha_{p}=[\lambda_{p}^{+},\lambda_{p}^{-}]_{N},\ n_{p}^{+}:=|\lambda_{p}^{+}|,\ n_{p}^{-}:=|\lambda_{p}^{-}|,\ r_{p}:=n_{p}^{+}+n_{p}^{-},
and let

T_{p}:=V^{\otimes n_{p}^{+}}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes n_{p}^{-}},\qquad P_{p}:=P^{[\lambda_{p}^{+},\lambda_{p}^{-}]}_{N}.

Then, for each pair
(x,u)
, there exists a
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-equivariant endomorphism
R_{x,u}(U)\in\mathrm{End}(T^{\mathrm{aux}}_{x,u})
such that

\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}(\partial_{e,a}W_{L})(U)\,\partial_{e,a}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})=\sum_{x}\sum_{u=1}^{r_{p}}W_{\tau_{x,u}({\mathcal{L}})}(U)\,\mathrm{Tr}_{T^{\mathrm{aux}}_{x,u}}\!\bigl(R_{x,u}(U)\bigr),

where the outer sum runs over all active occurrences
x
of
e
or
e^{-1}
in
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Proof.

We start from the mixed-tensor expression

\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})=\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{p}}\!\bigl(P_{p}\,\rho_{p}(U_{a})\rho_{p}(U_{e})\rho_{p}(U_{b})\bigr).

For the loop part, if
x
is an active occurrence of
e
or
e^{-1}
in the loop
\ell_{i}
, choose a cyclic representative of
\ell_{i}
of the form

w_{\ell_{i}}=\begin{cases}cxe\,d_{x},&x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}),\\
cxe^{-1}d_{x},&x\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}),\end{cases}

and write

\Xi_{x,a}(U):=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{c_{x}}X_{a}U_{e}U_{d_{x}}),&x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}),\\
-\mathrm{Tr}(U_{c_{x}}U_{e}^{-1}X_{a}U_{d_{x}}),&x\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}}).\end{cases}

Then

\partial_{e,a}W_{L}(U)=\sum_{x}W_{{\mathcal{L}}\setminus x}(U)\,\Xi_{x,a}(U),

where
W_{{\mathcal{L}}\setminus x}(U)
denotes the product of the loop traces not differentiated at the occurrence
x
.

For the plaquette part, differentiating only the active factor
\rho_{p}(U_{e})
gives

\partial_{e,a}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})=\sum_{u=1}^{r_{p}}\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{p}}\!\bigl(P_{p}\,\rho_{p}(U_{a})\,D_{u,a}\,\rho_{p}(U_{b})\bigr),

where
D_{u,a}
is the infinitesimal action on the
u
-th slot. Thus

\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}(\partial_{e,a}W_{L})\,\partial_{e,a}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})=\sum_{x}\sum_{u=1}^{r_{p}}W_{{\mathcal{L}}\setminus x}(U)\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}\Xi_{x,a}(U)\,\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{p}}\!\bigl(P_{p}\,\rho_{p}(U_{a})\,D_{u,a}\,\rho_{p}(U_{b})\bigr).

Thus, it is enough to analyze a fixed pair
(x,u)
.

For such a pair, the index
a
appears exactly once in the loop factor and exactly once in the active plaquette slot. Summing over
a
therefore performs the unique local Casimir contraction at the incidence
(e,p)
. There are four cases.

If
x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
and
u\leq n_{p}^{+}
, the active loop occurrence and the active slot occurrence
y_{u}
in
\gamma_{p,u}=\partial p
have the same orientation. The Casimir contraction reconnects the loop strand with the active plaquette strand into the positive loop-plaquette merger
\tau_{x,u}({\mathcal{L}})=\ell_{i}\oplus_{x,y_{u}}\gamma_{p,u}
, while on the plaquette side it removes the active covariant slot. Hence the remaining plaquette factor is the trace of a
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-equivariant endomorphism of

V^{\otimes(n_{p}^{+}-1)}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes n_{p}^{-}}.

If
x\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
and
u\leq n_{p}^{+}
, the active loop occurrence and
y_{u}
have opposite orientations. The Casimir contraction yields the negative loop-plaquette merger
\tau_{x,u}({\mathcal{L}})=\ell_{i}\ominus_{x,y_{u}}\gamma_{p,u}
, while on the plaquette side it inserts one additional covariant slot, i.e. a covariant coevaluation. The resulting plaquette factor is therefore the trace of a
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-equivariant endomorphism of

V^{\otimes(n_{p}^{+}+1)}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes n_{p}^{-}}.

If
x\in A_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
and
u>n_{p}^{+}
, then
\gamma_{p,u}=(\partial p)^{-1}
, so the active loop occurrence and
y_{u}
have opposite orientations. The loop part is the negative loop-plaquette merger
\tau_{x,u}({\mathcal{L}})=\ell_{i}\ominus_{x,y_{u}}\gamma_{p,u}
, while the plaquette side acquires one additional contravariant slot, i.e. a contravariant coevaluation. Thus the remaining plaquette
factor is the trace of a
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-equivariant endomorphism of

V^{\otimes n_{p}^{+}}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes(n_{p}^{-}+1)}.

Finally, if
x\in B_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})
and
u>n_{p}^{+}
, the active loop occurrence and
y_{u}
have the same orientation. The loop part is the positive loop-plaquette merger
\tau_{x,u}({\mathcal{L}})=\ell_{i}\oplus_{x,y_{u}}\gamma_{p,u}
, while the plaquette side loses one contravariant slot, i.e. undergoes a contravariant contraction. Hence the remaining plaquette
factor is the trace of a
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-equivariant endomorphism of

V^{\otimes n_{p}^{+}}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes(n_{p}^{-}-1)}.

In each case, all non-active loop and plaquette letters remain unchanged, so the contribution of the fixed pair
(x,u)
has the form

W_{\tau_{x,u}({\mathcal{L}})}(U)\,\mathrm{Tr}_{T^{\mathrm{aux}}_{x,u}}\!\bigl(R_{x,u}(U)\bigr)

for some
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-equivariant endomorphism
R_{x,u}(U)\in\mathrm{End}(T^{\mathrm{aux}}_{x,u})
. Summing over all active occurrences
x
and all slots
u
gives the stated identity.
∎

Proposition 4.9
.

In the setting of Proposition
4.8
, for each pair
(x,u)
, the
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-equivariant tensor trace
\mathrm{Tr}_{T^{\mathrm{aux}}_{x,u}}\!\bigl(R_{x,u}(U)\bigr)
admits a finite decomposition into irreducible rational characters:

\mathrm{Tr}_{T^{\mathrm{aux}}_{x,u}}\!\bigl(R_{x,u}(U)\bigr)=\sum_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{x,u}(\alpha_{p})}c_{x,u}(\alpha_{p},\beta;N)\,\chi_{\beta}(U_{\partial p}),

where
{\mathcal{B}}_{x,u}(\alpha_{p})\subset\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}
is finite. Moreover, each set
{\mathcal{B}}_{x,u}(\alpha_{p})
is contained in the set of irreducible summands of the auxiliary mixed tensor space
T^{\mathrm{aux}}_{x,u}
.

Proof.

Fix a pair
(x,u)
as in Proposition
4.8
, and write
T^{\mathrm{aux}}_{x,u}=T_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}:=V^{\otimes n^{\prime}}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes m^{\prime}}.
The pair
(n^{\prime},m^{\prime})
is one of the following four possibilities:

(n_{p}^{+}-1,n_{p}^{-}),\qquad(n_{p}^{+}+1,n_{p}^{-}),\qquad(n_{p}^{+},n_{p}^{-}+1),\qquad(n_{p}^{+},n_{p}^{-}-1),

according to the local type of
(x,u)
. By construction in Proposition
4.8
, the contribution indexed by
(x,u)
is obtained from the mixed-tensor formula for
\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})
by performing exactly one local contraction or one local coevaluation at the active incidence, while leaving all other plaquette letters unchanged. Therefore there exists a
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-equivariant endomorphism
A_{x,u;N}\in{\mathrm{End}}(T_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}})
such that

R_{x,u}(U)=A_{x,u;N}\,\rho_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(U_{\partial p}),

where
\rho_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}
denotes the natural mixed tensor representation on
T_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}
. Indeed, all dependence on the gauge variables is still carried by the plaquette holonomy
U_{\partial p}
, whereas the local contraction/coevaluation and the projector/idempotent data produce a
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-equivariant operator independent of
U
.

Since
T_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}
is a finite-dimensional rational
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-module, it is completely reducible. Hence there exists a finite set
{\mathcal{B}}_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}
of highest weights and multiplicity spaces
M_{\beta}
such that

T_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\simeq\bigoplus_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}}V^{[\beta]}\otimes M_{\beta}

as a
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-module. In this decomposition,

\rho_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(g)=\bigoplus_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}}\rho_{\beta}(g)\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{M_{\beta}},\qquad g\in{\mathrm{U}}(N),

where
\rho_{\beta}
is the irreducible rational representation of highest weight
\beta
. Because
A_{x,u;N}
commutes with the
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
-action, Schur’s lemma yields

A_{x,u;N}=\bigoplus_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}}\mathrm{Id}_{V^{[\beta]}}\otimes A_{x,u;\beta}(N)

for certain endomorphisms
A_{x,u;\beta}(N)\in{\mathrm{End}}(M_{\beta})
. Therefore, for every

g\in{\mathrm{U}}(N)
,

\mathrm{Tr}_{T^{\mathrm{aux}}_{x,u}}\!\bigl(R_{x,u}(g)\bigr)=\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}}\!\bigl(A_{x,u;N}\rho_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(g)\bigr)=\sum_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}}\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl(A_{x,u;\beta}(N)\bigr)\,\chi_{\beta}(g).

Applying this with
g=U_{\partial p}
gives

\mathrm{Tr}_{T^{\mathrm{aux}}_{x,u}}\!\bigl(R_{x,u}(U)\bigr)=\sum_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}}c_{x,u}(\alpha_{p},\beta;N)\,\chi_{\beta}(U_{\partial p}),

where

c_{x,u}(\alpha_{p},\beta;N):=\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl(A_{x,u;\beta}(N)\bigr).

If we then set

{\mathcal{B}}_{x,u}(\alpha_{p}):=\{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}:c_{x,u}(\alpha_{p},\beta;N)\neq 0\},

the desired finite decomposition follows.
∎

A direct consequence of Proposition
4.9
is that

\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}(\partial_{e,a}W_{{\mathcal{L}}})\partial_{e,a}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})\prod_{q\neq p}\chi_{\alpha_{q}}(U_{\partial q})dU=\sum_{x,u}\sum_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{x,u}(\alpha_{p})}c_{x,u}(\alpha_{p},\beta;N)\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,\tau_{x,u}({\mathcal{L}})}(\alpha^{p\to\beta}).

Grouping the finitely many elementary contributions indexed by the pairs
(x,u)
according to the resulting loop family and the resulting recoupled plaquette label, we obtain the following packaged form.

Let

I_{e,p}({\mathcal{L}}):=\{(x,u):x\text{ is an active occurrence of }e\text{ or }e^{-1}\text{ in }{\mathcal{L}},\ 1\leq u\leq r_{p}\}.

Let

\mathcal{T}_{e,p}({\mathcal{L}}):=\{\tau_{x,u}({\mathcal{L}}):(x,u)\in I_{e,p}({\mathcal{L}})\}

be the finite set of loop families obtained from the elementary local surgeries of Proposition
4.8
.

For each
\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{e,p}({\mathcal{L}})
, define

{\mathcal{B}}^{\tau}_{e,p}(\alpha_{p}):=\Bigl\{\beta\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}(N)}:\exists(x,u)\in I_{e,p}({\mathcal{L}})\text{ such that }\tau_{x,u}({\mathcal{L}})=\tau,\ \beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{x,u}(\alpha_{p})\Bigr\}.

For
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}^{\tau}_{e,p}(\alpha_{p})
, define

b^{\tau}_{e,p}(\alpha_{p},\beta;N):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,u)\in I_{e,p}({\mathcal{L}})\\
\tau_{x,u}({\mathcal{L}})=\tau\\
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{x,u}(\alpha_{p})\end{subarray}}c_{x,u}(\alpha_{p},\beta;N).

Then

\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}\sum_{a=1}^{N^{2}}(\partial_{e,a}W_{\mathcal{L}})\partial_{e,a}\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})\prod_{q\neq p}\chi_{\alpha_{q}}(U_{\partial q})dU=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{e,p}({\mathcal{L}})}\sum_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}^{\tau}_{e,p}(\alpha_{p})}b^{\tau}_{e,p}(\alpha_{p},\beta;N)\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,\tau}(\alpha^{p\to\beta}).

Before packaging the previous results into the operator
\mathscr{B}_{e,p}
, let us spell out its conceptual content. We have seen that the mixed incidence term is controlled by only three kinds of local data:

(i)

the choice of an active occurrence of
e
or
e^{-1}
in the loop family;

(ii)

the choice of an active tensor slot in the mixed-tensor realization of

\chi_{\alpha_{p}}(U_{\partial p})
;

(iii)

the local contraction pattern determined by the invariant pairing

\sum_{a}X_{a}\otimes X_{a}
at the incidence
(e,p)
.

Since each of these choices is finite for fixed
({\mathcal{L}},\alpha_{p})
, the whole mixed term reduces to a finite linear combination of new decorated coefficients. The loop part changes only through a local surgery at
(e,p)
, while the plaquette part changes only through a finite recoupling of the single active label
\alpha_{p}
. In particular, no other plaquette labels are touched. This is the coefficientwise locality statement that replaces the classical Wilson-action deformation term.

From this point of view, Propositions
4.8
and
4.9
are best understood as hybrid position/Fourier statements. On the loop side, it still looks like a local surgery rule in the one-skeleton of the lattice. On the plaquette side, however, it is already a representation-theoretic transfer rule: the active character
\chi_{\alpha_{p}}
is replaced by a finite combination of characters
\chi_{\beta}
, with coefficients depending only on the local incidence type. This is why the operator
\mathscr{B}_{e,p}
is generally not diagonal in the plaquette label, and also why the subsequent specializations behave differently: for the Wilson action, resummation collapses this local transfer rule back to the usual deformation terms, whereas for the heat-kernel action it remains visible as a genuinely spectral local operator on the active plaquette label.

This local-recoupling viewpoint is also closer in spirit to recent representation-theoretic formulas in two-dimensional Yang–Mills. For example, in Lévy’s recent combinatorial formula on compact surfaces
[
47
]
, Wilson loop expectations are expressed as sums over highest-weight assignments to the connected components of the complement of the curves, with local factors attached to intersection points. The mechanism is of course different here, but the philosophy is similar: once one has passed to the spectral side, the interaction of Wilson loops with the gauge field is encoded by local transfer rules between nearby representation labels.

We are now in position to define the second operator involved in the master loop equation.

Definition 4.10
.

For an incidence
(e,p)
, define the operator
\mathscr{B}_{e,p}
on the decorated coefficients by

(\mathscr{B}_{e,p}\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}})(\alpha):=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{e,p}({\mathcal{L}})}\ \sum_{\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}^{\tau}_{e,p}(\alpha_{p})}b^{\tau}_{e,p}(\alpha_{p},\beta;N)\widehat{W}_{\Lambda,\tau}(\alpha^{p\to\beta}),

where the grouped local surgeries
\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{e,p}({\mathcal{L}})
, the finite sets
{\mathcal{B}}^{\tau}_{e,p}(\alpha_{p})
, and the coefficients
b^{\tau}_{e,p}(\alpha_{p},\beta;N)
are those obtained at the end of Proposition
4.9
.

4.4
Proof of the master loop equation

We can now prove the universal coefficientwise master loop equation.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Start from Proposition
4.1
. The first term is identified with the universal loop operator by Proposition
4.6
. Each local incidence term is identified with the corresponding operator
\mathscr{B}_{e,p}
by the grouped form obtained at the end of Proposition
4.9
. Summing over the incident plaquettes gives the result.
∎

5.1
Surface-sum expansion

The global surface expansion admits a more geometric specialization than the general coarse topological expansion of Theorem
1.2
. The natural objects are no longer arbitrary decorated spanning surfaces, but edge-plaquette embeddings. However, we will recover the surface-sum from an intermediary step of Section
2
.

Let us first prove a correspondence that recasts the coefficients in Proposition
2.16
in terms of Cao–Park–Sheffield’s
dual bipartite maps
(DBM). We refer to
[
3
]
for a precise description and definition.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\Gamma=(\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{k})
be a balanced collection of words on the alphabet
\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{L}\}
. The following holds:

(i)

For every
i
, one has
r_{i}=1
, the Brauer index set
\mathcal{I}
is a singleton, and
h(\tau)=0
. Consequently, the surface construction of Section
2.4
involves no Brauer gadgets: it consists only of one polygon for each word
\Gamma_{i}
and one Haar patch for each letter
\lambda_{\ell}
.

(ii)

The data of a pair
(\tau,\sigma)
occurring in Proposition
2.16
are therefore equivalent to the data of a collection of pairs of bijections

(\alpha_{\ell},\beta_{\ell})\in S_{n_{\ell},n_{\ell}},\qquad\ell\in[L],

where
n_{\ell}
is the number of occurrences of
\lambda_{\ell}
in
\Gamma
. The corresponding coarse surface class is canonically identified with an element
M\in DBM(\Gamma)
in the sense of
[
3
]
.

(iii)

Under this identification, the partition
\mu_{\ell}(M)
is the cycle type of
\alpha_{\ell}^{-1}\beta_{\ell}
, and

\Omega_{N}(\Xi)N^{\chi(\Xi)}=\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\mu_{\ell}(M))\right)N^{V(M)},

where
\Xi
is the coarse surface class corresponding to
M
, and
V(M)
denotes the number of vertices of
M
.

Proof.

Since
\chi_{[(1),\varnothing]_{N}}(g)=\mathrm{Tr}(g)
, the mixed tensor space attached to each word is just
V
, hence
r_{i}=1
for every
i
. Therefore the local Brauer index set is trivial, there is no nontrivial projector expansion, and
h(\tau)=0
. The construction of Section
2.4
reduces to the following: one starts with one polygon for each word
\Gamma_{i}
, and for each letter
\lambda_{\ell}
one glues in a Haar patch determined by a pair of bijections
(\alpha_{\ell},\beta_{\ell})\in S_{n_{\ell},n_{\ell}}
. This is exactly the construction of a map from the strand diagram of
\Gamma
: the word-polygons are the yellow faces, and the Haar patches are the blue faces. Hence the resulting coarse surface classes are canonically identified
with the elements of
DBM(\Gamma)
.

By
[
3
, Definition 3.5]
, for
M\in DBM(\Gamma)
and each
\ell\in[L]
, the partition
\mu_{\ell}(M)
is given by the half-degrees of the blue faces glued into the strand diagram of
\lambda_{\ell}
. Equivalently,
\mu_{\ell}(M)
is the cycle type of
\alpha_{\ell}^{-1}\beta_{\ell}
. Thus the Weingarten factor attached to
(\tau,\sigma)
is exactly

\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\mu_{\ell}(M)).

Moreover, by
[
3
, Lemma 3.4]
, the number of connected components of the strand diagram associated with the bijection data
(\alpha_{\ell},\beta_{\ell})_{\ell\in[L]}
is exactly the number
V(M)
of vertices of the corresponding map. In the present trace-only specialization, the index-contraction factor
K_{N}(\tau,\sigma)
is therefore equal to
N^{V(M)}
. Since

\Omega_{N}(\Xi)N^{\chi(\Xi)}

is the total contribution of the corresponding pair
(\tau,\sigma)
, it follows that

\Omega_{N}(\Xi)N^{\chi(\Xi)}=\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\mu_{\ell}(M))\right)N^{V(M)},

as claimed.
∎

Corollary 5.2

(
[
3
]
, Proposition 3.8)
.

Let
\Gamma=(\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{k})
be a balanced collection of words on the alphabet
\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{{\mathcal{L}}}\}
. For each
\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}
, let
n_{\ell}
be the number of occurrences of
\lambda_{\ell}
in
\Gamma
. For
n\geq 0
, denote by
\mathrm{Wg}_{N}:S_{n}\to\mathbb{C}
the ordinary unitary Weingarten function. If
\mu\vdash n
, write
\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\mu)
for its common value on permutations of cycle type
\mu
. If one defines

\widetilde{\mathrm{Wg}}_{N}(\pi):=N^{n+|\pi|}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\pi),\qquad|\pi|:=n-\#\mathrm{cycles}(\pi),

then

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{Tr}(\Gamma_{i}(U))\right]=\sum_{M\in\mathrm{DBM}(\Gamma)}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\widetilde{\mathrm{Wg}}_{N}(\mu_{\ell}(M))\right)N^{\chi(M)-k}.

Proof.

Apply Proposition
2.16
in the trace-only specialization described in Lemma
5.1
. By that lemma, the sum over coarse surface
classes may be rewritten as a sum over
M\in DBM(\Gamma)
, and one obtains

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{Tr}(\Gamma_{i}(U))\right]=\sum_{M\in DBM(\Gamma)}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\mu_{\ell}(M))\right)N^{V(M)}.

This is already exactly the CPS map expansion before normalization of the Weingarten function.

Now recall the identities stated in
[
3
]
:

E(M)=2\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}n_{\ell},\qquad F(M)=k+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\ell(\mu_{\ell}(M)),

where
\ell(\mu)
denotes the number of parts of the partition
\mu
. If
\pi_{\ell}\in S_{n_{\ell}}
has cycle type
\mu_{\ell}(M)
, then

|\pi_{\ell}|=n_{\ell}-\ell(\mu_{\ell}(M)),

hence

\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\bigl(n_{\ell}+|\pi_{\ell}|\bigr)=2\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}n_{\ell}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\ell(\mu_{\ell}(M))=E(M)-F(M)+k.

Therefore, by the definition

\widetilde{\mathrm{Wg}}_{N}(\pi):=N^{n+|\pi|}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\pi),

we get

\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\widetilde{\mathrm{Wg}}_{N}(\mu_{\ell}(M))=\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\mu_{\ell}(M))\right)N^{E(M)-F(M)+k}.

Using Euler’s identity

\chi(M)=V(M)-E(M)+F(M),

we conclude that

\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\widetilde{\mathrm{Wg}}_{N}(\mu_{\ell}(M))\right)N^{\chi(M)-k}=\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathrm{Wg}_{N}(\mu_{\ell}(M))\right)N^{V(M)}.

Substituting this into the previous formula yields the desired identity.
∎

For two multiplicity fields

K^{+},K^{-}:P(\Lambda)\to\mathbb{N},

define the associated collection of words

\Gamma_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(K^{+},K^{-}):=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k})\sqcup\bigsqcup_{p\in P(\Lambda)}(\partial p)^{\sqcup K^{+}(p)}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\bigl((\partial p)^{-1}\bigr)^{\sqcup K^{-}(p)}.

We write

|K|:=\sum_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\bigl(K^{+}(p)+K^{-}(p)\bigr),\qquad(K^{+},K^{-})!:=\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}K^{+}(p)!K^{-}(p)!,

and

\beta^{K^{+},K^{-}}:=\prod_{p\in P(\Lambda)}\left(\frac{\beta_{p}}{2}\right)^{K^{+}(p)+K^{-}(p)}.

We denote by
{\mathrm{EPE}}_{\Lambda}({\mathcal{L}};K^{+},K^{-})
the set of edge-plaquette embeddings
(M,\phi)
with boundary
{\mathcal{L}}=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k})
and oriented plaquette counts
(K^{+},K^{-})
, meaning that:

–

M
is a compact oriented surface with
k
ordered boundary components;

–

\phi:M\to\Lambda^{(2)}
is cellular;

–

the
i
-th boundary component maps to the loop
\ell_{i}
;

–

for each plaquette
p
, exactly
K^{+}(p)
plaquette-faces of
M
are mapped homeomorphically onto
p
with the chosen orientation, and exactly
K^{-}(p)
plaquette-faces are mapped homeomorphically onto
p
with the opposite orientation.

For
(M,\phi)\in{\mathrm{EPE}}_{\Lambda}({\mathcal{L}};K^{+},K^{-})
and
e\in E(\Lambda)
, let
\mu_{e}(\phi)
be the partition whose parts are half the boundary-degrees of the edge-faces mapped to
e
.

Corollary 5.3
.

For the Wilson action,

Z\mathbb{E}[W_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(U)]=\sum_{K^{+},K^{-}:P(\Lambda)\to\mathbb{N}}\frac{\beta^{K^{+},K^{-}}}{(K^{+},K^{-})!}\sum_{(M,\phi)\in{\mathrm{EPE}}_{\Lambda}({\mathcal{L}};K^{+},K^{-})}\left(\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)}\widetilde{\mathrm{Wg}}_{N}(\mu_{e}(\phi))\right)N^{\chi(M)-k},

where
k
is the number of loops in the family
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Proof.

Expand each Wilson plaquette weight into its trace power series:

Q_{\beta_{p}}^{\mathrm{W}}(U_{\partial p})=\sum_{a,b\geq 0}\frac{(\beta_{p}/2)^{a+b}}{a!b!}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\partial p})^{a}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{\partial p}^{-1})^{b}.

Multiplying over all plaquettes gives

Z\mathbb{E}[W_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(U)]=\sum_{K^{+},K^{-}:P(\Lambda)\to\mathbb{N}}\frac{\beta^{K^{+},K^{-}}}{(K^{+},K^{-})!}\int_{{\mathrm{U}}(N)^{E(\Lambda)}}\prod_{w\in\Gamma_{\Lambda,{\mathcal{L}}}(K^{+},K^{-})}\mathrm{Tr}(U_{w})dU.

Fix a pair
(K^{+},K^{-})
. The integrand is now a product of traces only, so we are in the trace-only specialization of the surface construction of Section
2
.

In this specialization, every word is carried by the fundamental representation or its dual. Hence the local mixed-tensor rank is
1
at every insertion, the local Brauer index set is trivial, and no horizontal Brauer bands appear. In particular, the defect term vanishes. The refined surface construction of Theorem
2.18
therefore reduces to polygons for the loop and plaquette words together with Haar patches, and the resulting surfaces are precisely compact oriented surfaces with ordered boundary
{\mathcal{L}}
, endowed with a cellular map to
\Lambda^{(2)}
whose plaquette-faces map homeomorphically onto plaquettes with multiplicities prescribed by
(K^{+},K^{-})
. In other words, the resulting objects are exactly the edge-plaquette embeddings
(M,\phi)\in{\mathrm{EPE}}_{\Lambda}({\mathcal{L}};K^{+},K^{-})
.

Moreover, in the same trace-only specialization, Corollary
5.2
identifies the local Haar contribution with the product of renormalized unitary Weingarten weights

\prod_{e\in E(\Lambda)}\widetilde{\mathrm{Wg}}_{N}(\mu_{e}(\phi)),

and the global power of
N
is
N^{\chi(M)-k}
, where
k=\#{\mathcal{L}}
.

Substituting this trace-only surface expansion into the Wilson trace series yields the expected formula.
∎

5.2
Master loop equation

Let us now turn to the master loop equation. For

D^{+,p}_{i,e}({\mathcal{L}}):=\{(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{i-1},\ell_{i}\oplus_{x}p,\ell_{i+1},\dots,\ell_{k}):x\in C_{i}(e;{\mathcal{L}})\},

Appendix A
Adaptation to other classical groups

In this appendix we explain more concretely how the strategy of the paper extends to the other compact classical groups. Our purpose is not to reprove every statement in full detail, but to isolate the finite list of representation-theoretic inputs on which Sections
2
–
4
depend, and to indicate how these inputs are replaced for
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
,
{\mathrm{SO}}(N)
and
{\mathrm{Sp}}(N)
.

At a structural level, the proofs of the unitary case only use the following ingredients.

(I) Fourier expansion of the plaquette action.

For every compact group
G
, every central square-integrable function admits a character expansion by Peter–Weyl. This yields the analogue of Theorem
1.1
verbatim.

(II) Tensor realization of irreducible characters.

For each irreducible label
\lambda
of
G
, one needs a finite-dimensional tensor space
T_{\lambda}
, a representation
\rho_{\lambda}
of
G
on
T_{\lambda}
, and a
G
-equivariant projector
P_{\lambda}
such that

\chi_{\lambda}(g)=\mathrm{Tr}_{T_{\lambda}}\!\bigl(P_{\lambda}\rho_{\lambda}(g)\bigr).

(III) Diagrammatic expansion of the projector.

One needs a finite diagrammatic basis of the commutant algebra of the relevant tensor power, together with an expansion

P_{\lambda}=\sum_{\tau}c_{\lambda,N}(\tau)\,\rho(\tau)

in this basis. In the unitary case this basis is given by walled Brauer diagrams.

(IV) Haar integration in the same diagrammatic language.

One needs a Weingarten-type expansion for Haar integrals of matrix coefficients, expressed in terms of the same diagrammatic basis. Once (III) and (IV) are available, the refined expansions of Section
2
follow by exactly the same pattern: one inserts the projector expansion, integrates
letter by letter, and regroups the resulting finite sums.

(V) Local Lie-algebra contraction identities.

To derive the master loop equation, one only needs the analogue of the magic formulas (
35
) for the defining representation of the group under consideration. Once these are known, the proof of the loop part is the usual group-dependent cut-and-join computation, while the mixed term is again a local incidence computation on one loop strand and one plaquette strand.

Once these ingredients are granted, the remainder of the paper is formal:
Section
2
produces a topological expansion from (II)–(IV), Section
3
only uses the resulting
local resolutions and the factorization of Haar integration edge by edge, and Section
4
only
uses (V) together with the same locality mechanism. Thus the problem of adapting the whole
formalism to another compact classical group reduces to identifying the correct replacements of
the tensor model, of the commutant diagrams, and of the Weingarten kernels.

A.1
The special unitary group

The case of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
is the closest to the unitary theory. Irreducible representations of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
are still realized inside tensor constructions built from the defining representation
V=\mathbb{C}^{N}
and its dual
V^{*}
, and the relevant commutant is still governed by the mixed Schur–Weyl formalism. Concretely, one may choose for each dominant weight of
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
a highest-weight representative for
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
, for instance with last coordinate equal to
0
, and realize the corresponding
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
-module inside a mixed tensor space
T_{n,m}=V^{\otimes n}\otimes(V^{*})^{\otimes m}.
The associated isotypic projector is then described by the same kind of mixed-tensor representation-theoretic data as in Section
2
.

There is, however, one point that should be stated explicitly: compared with
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
, the commutant for
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
is enlarged by the possible presence of determinant-type intertwiners coming from the invariant volume tensor. Equivalently, in addition to the usual walled Brauer contractions, one may have local
\varepsilon
-tensor insertions when the total covariant and
contravariant degrees are not balanced in the same way as in the unitary case. Thus the correct diagrammatic basis for
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
should be viewed as the unitary walled-Brauer basis, possibly enlarged by finitely many determinant channels.

After making this enlargement, the strategy of Sections
2
–
4
goes through unchanged.
Indeed:

–

the state-sum expansion is still obtained by Peter–Weyl on
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
;

–

the topological coefficients are still integrals of products of irreducible characters realized inside mixed tensor spaces;

–

the local projector expansions are still finite and local, now in the enlarged
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
commutant basis;

–

Haar integration still acts letterwise and produces local Weingarten coefficients attached to the same local channels;

–

the defect-ratio formalism of Section
3
is unaffected, since it only uses the existence of finite local resolutions and edgewise compression;

–

the master loop equation of Section
4
again reduces to the usual
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
cut-and-join operator together with a local spectral recoupling term at the active plaquette.

In particular, the analogues of Theorems
1.1
–
1.5
remain valid for lattice Yang–Mills with gauge group
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
after replacing
{\mathrm{U}}(\widehat{N})
by
\widehat{{\mathrm{SU}}(N)}
in the plaquette summation and replacing the unitary local channels by the corresponding
{\mathrm{SU}}(N)
channels. The only genuinely new feature is the possible appearance of determinant-type local channels, but these are still finite-dimensional and local, so they do not affect the architecture of the arguments.

A.2
The orthogonal and symplectic groups

For
{\mathrm{SO}}(N)
and
{\mathrm{Sp}}(N)
, the required replacements are different but equally standard. The defining representation is self-dual, so the covariant/contravariant distinction disappears. As a result, the mixed tensor spaces of the unitary case are replaced by ordinary tensor powers
V^{\otimes r},
and the walled Brauer algebra is replaced by the ordinary Brauer algebra.

More precisely, if
G_{N}
denotes either
{\mathrm{SO}}(N)
or
{\mathrm{Sp}}(N)
, the commutant of the
G_{N}
-action on
V^{\otimes r}
is described by Brauer diagrams. The analogue of the unitary traceless mixed projector is the projector onto the appropriate harmonic/traceless summand with respect to the invariant bilinear form preserved by
G_{N}
. Thus the role of Section
2.2
is played by the usual orthogonal/symplectic Schur–Weyl duality: irreducible characters are realized as traces of
G_{N}
-equivariant projectors on tensor powers, and these projectors admit finite expansions in the Brauer basis.

Once this replacement is made, the rest of the machinery is parallel:

–

in the Weingarten expansion, ordinary Brauer diagrams replace walled Brauer diagrams;

–

the local contractions and coevaluations are taken with respect to the orthogonal or symplectic invariant form;

–

the edgewise Haar integrals are governed by the orthogonal or symplectic Weingarten kernels;

–

the local channel data of Section
3
now live on ordinary tensor legs, without a wall separating covariant and contravariant sectors;

–

the coefficientwise master loop equation again follows from the corresponding Lie-algebra contraction formulas, with the cut-and-join part replaced by the orthogonal/symplectic one.

Thus the analogues of the local resolution theorem, of the defect-ratio formula, and of the coefficientwise master loop equation remain valid for
{\mathrm{SO}}(N)
and
{\mathrm{Sp}}(N)
after replacing the unitary mixed-tensor input by the orthogonal/symplectic tensor input.

On the geometric side, the surface expansion should be understood through the thickening of ordinary Brauer diagrams rather than walled Brauer diagrams. This yields the exact analogue of Section
2
, with the same finite local defect bookkeeping. If one wishes to formulate the resulting topological expansion in an explicitly geometric language, one should use the natural orthogonal/symplectic version of the Brauer-surface model rather than force the unitary oriented one; the precise topological packaging is secondary here, since the local channel and master-equation parts depend only on the underlying finite diagrammatic calculus. It is worth mentioning that, as usually seen in topological expansions involving the orthogonal and symplectic groups, the surfaces obtained through this procedure might not be orientable, in contrast to those obtained for unitary groups.

In summary, for all compact classical groups the adaptation problem is local and representation-theoretic: one replaces the tensor model, the diagrammatic commutant basis, the corresponding Weingarten kernel, and the Lie-algebra contraction formulas. Once these inputs are substituted, the state-sum, topological expansion, local channel model, defect-ratio representation, and coefficientwise master loop equation follow by the same formal mechanism as in the unitary case.
