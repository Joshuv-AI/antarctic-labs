Experimental optimization of nanofiltration with a novel two-stage periodic washing mechanism for bioactive compound concentration in tomato serum
Authors: Behnam Alaei, Reza Amiri Chayjan, Mohammad Ali Zolfigol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study optimized nanofiltration incorporating periodic washing in two successive stages for tomato serum concentration and evaluation of Lycopene content and Vitamin C content. Response surface methodology and desirability optimization identified 120.9 s and 22.3 °C as the selected conditions. The confirmation experiment yielded 3.87 × 10−7 m3 m−2 s−1 for Permeate flux, 0.309 kWh per standardized batch concentration run for Energy consumed, 8.14 mg 100 g−1 for Lycopene content, and 46.41 mg 100 g−1 for Vitamin C content. Concentration factors were 3.36 for lycopene and 3.51 for vitamin C. The overall liquid mass balance closed at 100%, and apparent rejection values for the integrated process were 98.3% and 96.4%, respectively. In five replicate controls without periodic washing at 7 bar and 22.3 °C, complete cessation of flow occurred after a mean of 4.3 min; periodic washing allowed processing to continue to 14°Brix.Keywords: Tomato serum, Nanofiltration, Periodic washing, Membrane fouling, Apparent rejection, Lycopene, Vitamin C

Introduction

Tomato serum is a clarified liquid fraction obtained during tomato juice processing and represents a promising food matrix for the concentration of selected bioactive compounds. Lycopene is the principal carotenoid responsible for the characteristic red colour of tomato products and contributes to their antioxidant properties, whereas vitamin C is an important nutritional constituent that is susceptible to processing conditions. These compounds are therefore relevant indicators when evaluating concentration processes intended to produce tomato derived liquid ingredients with increased contents of valuable constituents (Trifunschi et al., 2022; Tufail et al., 2024). In previous work, clarification of tomato juice by a pressure and vacuum assisted microfiltration process produced a serum fraction containing measurable amounts of vitamin C and lycopene while removing larger suspended solids (Alaei et al., 2021).

Concentration of fruit and vegetable liquid fractions is commonly applied to reduce volume and obtain products with increased contents of desirable constituents. Conventional thermal concentration may expose sensitive food compounds to conditions that promote degradation, whereas membrane operations can achieve water removal under comparatively mild temperature conditions. Membrane processes have therefore been investigated for the clarification and concentration of fruit juices and for the recovery or retention of compounds associated with nutritional and functional quality (Conidi et al., 2018; Conidi et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2024). Nanofiltration is of particular interest in this context because it has been applied to fruit juice matrices for the concentration of bioactive constituents. Arriola et al. (2014), for example, reported the application of nanofiltration to watermelon juice and demonstrated substantial retention of lycopene in the concentrated fraction.

A major limitation in the membrane concentration of fruit derived liquid matrices is fouling caused by the deposition of soluble, colloidal, and suspended constituents on or near the membrane surface. This phenomenon can increase hydraulic resistance, reduce permeate flux, and restrict continued operation of the process. Such effects are especially relevant for tomato serum because, even after clarification, the feed may retain fine colloidal structures capable of contributing to concentration polarization and cake layer formation. Studies on membrane processing of juices have emphasized that feed composition, membrane characteristics, and operating conditions strongly influence fouling development and process performance (Lu et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2024). Therefore, an effective approach for controlling fouling during operation is required if nanofiltration is to be applied for the concentration of tomato serum.

In addition to the operational challenge of fouling, interpretation of vitamin C and lycopene retention requires particular care. Retention in nanofiltration cannot be inferred solely from a comparison between nominal membrane pore size and estimated molecular dimensions. It may also be affected by membrane characteristics, solution composition, solute polarity and ionic state, hydration or solvation, interactions between the membrane and solutes, concentration polarization, and fouling behaviour. This issue is particularly relevant to the present food matrix because vitamin C is highly soluble in water, whereas lycopene is hydrophobic and may be associated with fine colloidal structures remaining in clarified tomato serum. Consequently, the behaviour of these compounds must be evaluated experimentally from their distribution between the initial serum, concentrated fraction, and permeate, rather than predicted only from nominal pore size considerations (Alonso et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2021; Roth et al., 2024).

Accordingly, the present study aimed to develop and optimize a nanofiltration process equipped with a novel periodic washing mechanism comprising two successive stages, namely membrane surface flushing and reverse flow from the permeate side, for the concentration of tomato serum and retention of selected bioactive compounds. It was hypothesized that periodic application of these washing stages during nanofiltration would reduce the operational limitation caused by rapid fouling and allow the concentration process to proceed while retaining vitamin C and lycopene in the concentrated fraction. To test this hypothesis, the process was evaluated in terms of vitamin C and lycopene contents, recovery, concentration factor, apparent rejection, permeate flux, energy consumption, and fouling resistance.

Materials and methods

Preparation of tomato serum

Tomatoes were sourced from a local greenhouse in Hamedan, Iran, and thoroughly washed to remove surface contaminants. To preserve freshness and prevent dehydration, the tomatoes were stored in polyethylene bags under refrigeration at 4 °C until processing. Prior to each experiment, the tomatoes were equilibrated at 20 °C for 1 h to standardize their temperature and improve dewatering efficiency during juice extraction, as this temperature optimizes liquid release without compromising bioactive compounds (Silva et al., 2019). Juice extraction was carried out using a Cowy CJ-01 electric extractor employing a cold pressing technique, which minimizes thermal degradation and preserves key bioactive components such as vitamin C and lycopene. The extracted juice was subsequently filtered through a 1 mm mesh sieve to remove residual skin and seeds, following the method described by Prestes et al. (2023). Particle size analysis of the resulting juice indicated a high pulp content, with particles ranging from 100 to 1000 μm. A custom spiral press filter was employed to isolate the pulp fraction. This filter allows adjustment of fluid temperature and filtration pressure. Optimal separation of particles larger than 100 μm was achieved at a fluid temperature of 45 °C and a pressure of 6 bar, as determined through prior optimization studies (Alaei et al., 2024). Detailed specifications and optimization data for the spiral press filter are provided in the referenced study. Tomato serum was then obtained by removing insoluble solids from the juice using a bespoke microfiltration system operating under combined pressure and vacuum conditions. Using this system, particles larger than 0.2 μm were effectively removed under the previously optimized conditions of 26 °C fluid temperature, 5 kPa vacuum pressure, and 2 bar fluid pressure, as reported by Alaei et al. (2021). Tomato serum prepared using this procedure was previously characterized by a pH of 4.44 and residual particle sizes ranging from 4.01 to 198.78 nm (Alaei et al., 2021). The resulting microfiltered serum was used as the feed material for the nanofiltration experiments in the present study.

Nanofiltration membrane selection and basis for evaluating solute retention

A KLN-NF60 nanofiltration membrane was used for concentrating the microfiltered tomato serum prepared as described in Section 2.1. The available technical specifications of the membrane module are summarized in Table 1. The nominal pore size of the membrane was 0.5 nm. In the present study, this value was considered a technical specification of the selected membrane and was not used alone to predict the retention of vitamin C or lycopene. The tomato serum used as the feed contained dissolved compounds together with residual colloidal structures, as previously established for serum prepared using the same clarification procedure. Consequently, the behaviour of the target compounds during nanofiltration could not be interpreted solely through a comparison between molecular dimensions and nominal membrane pore size. Retention in nanofiltration may also be affected by membrane characteristics, solute polarity and ionic state, hydration or solvation, solution composition, interactions between the membrane and solutes, concentration polarization, and fouling behaviour. These factors are particularly relevant in fruit juice matrices, in which dissolved and colloidal constituents may influence both solute retention and membrane performance during concentration operations (Alonso et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2021; Roth et al., 2024). Accordingly, the retention behaviour of vitamin C and lycopene was evaluated experimentally using their measured contents in the initial tomato serum, collected concentrate, and permeate reservoir samples. The use of feed, concentrate, and permeate fractions for evaluating the retention and concentration of bioactive compounds has previously been reported in nanofiltration studies involving fruit juice matrices, including lycopene concentration from watermelon juice (Arriola et al., 2014). In the present study, the measured content values were used to calculate component mass balance, recovery, concentration factor, and apparent rejection, as described in Section 2.4. Since molecular weight cutoff, surface charge, and specific interactions between the applied membrane and the target compounds were not experimentally determined for the membrane module used in this study, the apparent rejection values were interpreted as indicators of the performance of the integrated nanofiltration and periodic washing process rather than as intrinsic selectivity coefficients of the membrane.

Nanofiltration with integrated two-stage periodic washing

To concentrate tomato serum while retaining vitamin C and lycopene, a nanofiltration system incorporating a two-stage periodic washing mechanism was constructed as illustrated in Fig. 1. The washing mechanism consisted of flow parallel to the membrane surface followed by reverse flow of permeate from the permeate side through the membrane, and was designed to limit deposit accumulation during operation. The nanofiltration process utilized a KLN-NF60 membrane with a nominal pore size of 0.5 nm, housed within a spiral module (Fig. 1, No. 1). Tomato serum was pumped from a storage tank (Fig. 1, No. 15) through a thermal chamber (Fig. 1, No. 11) into the filtration unit. The permeate was collected in a designated tank (Fig. 1, No. 18) and continuously weighed using a digital scale (Fig. 1, No. 19, AND model, 0.01 g accuracy). At the same time, the retentate was recycled back to the feed tank (Fig. 1, No. 15). Fluid flow was regulated at 1 L min−1 by a primary flow limiter (Fig. 1, No. 13) and at 0.3 L min−1 by secondary limiters (Fig. 1, Nos. 16 and 17), with operating pressure maintained at 7 bar using a pressure regulator (Fig. 1, No. 4) and a four-channel diaphragm (Fig. 1, No. 5). The periodic washing mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 2. Each washing sequence consisted of a 10 s flush of tomato serum parallel to the membrane surface (Fig. 2b), followed by a 5 s reverse flow of permeate from the permeate side through the membrane (Fig. 2c). The durations of these two washing stages were kept constant throughout the experiments. The filtration period preceding each washing sequence, denoted as Xi , was evaluated at five levels: 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 s, based on preliminary trials. These washing cycles were synchronized and controlled by three digital timers (Fig. 1, Nos. 21–23), each with an accuracy of 0.1 s. Timer 1 (Fig. 1, No. 21) controlled serum delivery during the filtration and surface washing portions of each cycle, activating for Xi + 10 s and deactivating for 5 s, while valves 6 and 7 were opened to pump serum (Stream A) through the membrane. This operation resulted in permeate collection (Stream B) in the permeate tank (Fig. 1, No. 18) and retentate recirculation (Stream C) to the feed tank (Fig. 1, No. 15). Timer 2 (Fig. 1, No. 22) governed the surface washing stage, activating for 10 s and pausing for Xi + 5 s, while valve 8 was opened to redirect serum (Stream E) across the membrane surface. Timer 3 (Fig. 1, No. 23) controlled the backflow washing stage, activating for 5 s and pausing for Xi + 10 s, while valves 9 and 10 were opened to pump permeate (Stream G) through the membrane in the reverse direction, with serum leaving through Stream D and returning to the feed tank (Fig. 1, No. 15). Synchronization was achieved by initiating Timer 1 first, followed by Timers 2 and 3 after a 5 s delay, as detailed in the timing diagram (Fig. 3).

Overall and component mass balance, recovery distribution, and apparent rejection

Overall and component mass balance calculations were performed using the process fractions obtained from a confirmation experiment conducted at the operating conditions selected through the optimization procedure. These calculations were used to quantify the distribution, recovery, and concentration of vitamin C and lycopene during the integrated nanofiltration and washing process, following the general approach used for evaluating bioactive compound recovery and concentration performance in fruit juices processed by membrane operations (Cassano et al., 2008; Ma et al., 2024; Rodrigues et al., 2004).

At the beginning of the confirmation experiment, the feed reservoir was loaded with 1500 g of tomato serum. Energy consumed

Electrical energy consumption was measured throughout each experimental run using an energy meter (Efergy, EMS-EU, UK). The recorded energy included the electrical demand of the fluid pumps, digital timers, solenoid valves, temperature control unit, and heating element during nanofiltration and periodic washing. All experiments were conducted using the same initial amount of tomato serum with the same initial soluble solids content, and processing was continued until the concentrated serum reached 14°Brix. Therefore, energy consumed, expressed as kWh per standardized batch concentration run, was used as the response variable for comparison of the experimental treatments. This basis avoided uncertainty associated with normalizing energy to permeate or concentrate mass in a system containing fixed internal liquid hold up volumes and a permeate reservoir involved in the backflow washing operation.

Determination of lycopene content

Lycopene content in the initial tomato serum, permeate, and retentate samples was determined using the spectrophotometric extraction procedure described by Fish et al. (2002), as previously applied to tomato serum by Alaei et al. (2021). For each measurement, 0.50 g of sample was mixed with 5 mL of ethanol (95%, v/v), 5 mL of acetone, and 10 mL of hexane. The mixture was agitated at 180 rpm for 15 min. Distilled water (3 mL) was then added, and agitation was continued for a further 5 min. After standing at laboratory temperature for 5 min, the phases were separated, and an aliquot of the upper hexane phase was collected. Determination of vitamin C content

Vitamin C content, expressed as total ascorbic acid, was determined using the spectrophotometric procedure based on reaction with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH), as reported by Kapur et al. (2012) and previously applied to tomato serum by Alaei et al. (2021). The samples were maintained under chilled conditions until analysis. A 500 μL aliquot of each sample was mixed with 500 μL of 3% metaphosphoric acid solution and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min. Subsequently, 280 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a reaction tube and mixed with 14 μL of bromine solution at a concentration of 0.05 mol L−1. Thereafter, 14 μL of 4% thiourea solution was added to remove excess bromine, followed by the addition of 70 μL of 2% DNPH solution. The reaction mixture was incubated at 35 ± 2 °C for 3 h. After incubation, the samples were cooled on ice, and 350 μL of cold sulfuric acid (85%, v/v) was added. After 5 min, 80 μL of the prepared solution was transferred to a microplate, and its absorbance was measured at 520 nm using a microplate reader.

Freshly prepared ascorbic acid solutions in phosphate buffered saline at pH 7.4, without calcium chloride and magnesium chloride, were used as analytical standards. Vitamin C content was determined from the standard response and expressed as mg 100 g−1 of sample.

The determination of lycopene and vitamin C was performed by a specialized analytical chemistry laboratory according to the procedures described above. The measured content values obtained for the initial tomato serum, permeate, and retentate samples were subsequently used for the calculation of component mass balance, recovery, concentration factor, and apparent rejection.

Experimental design and statistical analysis

This study aimed to develop and optimize a nanofiltration system with integrated two-stage periodic washing for the concentration of tomato serum while maintaining vitamin C and lycopene in the concentrated fraction. Two independent variables were investigated: filtration time and fluid temperature. Filtration time was evaluated at five levels of 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 s, while fluid temperature was varied at five levels of 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 °C. The durations of the surface flushing and backflow washing stages were fixed at 10 and 5 s, respectively. Accordingly, filtration time represented the operating interval between successive fixed washing sequences, and variation in this factor altered the frequency of periodic washing during tomato serum concentration. The response variables were vitamin C content and lycopene content in the concentrated tomato serum, expressed as mg 100 g−1, energy consumed, expressed as kWh per standardized batch concentration run, and permeate flux, expressed as m3 m−2 s−1. A central composite design within the response surface methodology framework was employed to evaluate the individual and interactive effects of filtration time and fluid temperature on these responses. The experimental design comprised 13 runs, including five observations at the central condition, consisting of one central run and four replicates, to estimate pure error and support assessment of model adequacy. Statistical analysis and numerical optimization were performed using Design Expert software, version 12. The optimum operating conditions predicted by the model were subsequently examined in a confirmation experiment. The fractions obtained from this experiment were used for determination of component mass balance, recovery, concentration factor, and apparent rejection, as described in Section 2.4. To examine the operational consequence of omitting periodic washing, five replicate control runs were conducted using tomato serum in the same nanofiltration system. Except for deactivation of both periodic washing stages, the operating conditions were maintained as selected by the numerical optimization procedure, including a transmembrane pressure of 7 bar and a fluid temperature of 22.3 °C. In the control runs, filtration was continued from process initiation until complete cessation of flow during the filtration process. This endpoint was defined as operational blockage of the membrane module, and the elapsed time to blockage was recorded.

Chemical cleaning of the membrane after each experiment

After each experiment, the membrane module was chemically cleaned to remove material deposited during tomato serum nanofiltration. Chemical cleaning was conducted for 60 min at 40 °C, 100 kPa, and a flow rate of 0.003 m3 min−1 using a 0.05% (w/w) NaOH solution at pH 12 and a 0.1% (w/w) Deteacid solution at pH 2.4. The hydraulic permeability measurements associated with the cleaning procedure and their use in determining the resistance components are described in Section 2.11. The chemical cleaning procedure was adapted from Cassano et al. (2007).

Results and discussion

Characteristics of tomato serum and concentrated serum

The physicochemical characteristics of the initial and concentrated tomato serum were evaluated after nanofiltration with integrated two-stage periodic washing. The pH changed only slightly from 4.44 in the initial serum to 4.42 in the concentrated serum. The concentrated tomato serum reached 14°Brix, confirming concentration of the serum during processing. Correspondingly, viscosity increased from 18.80 × 10−3 to 45.96 × 10−3 Pa s, while density increased from 1.02 to 1.07 g cm−3. These changes are consistent with the increased soluble solids content obtained during nanofiltration concentration of a food liquid matrix (Nath et al., 2018).

Statistical adequacy of the fitted models

The adequacy statistics of the quadratic models fitted to Permeate flux, Energy consumed, Lycopene content, and Vitamin C content are presented in Table 2, while the corresponding analysis of variance is provided in Table 3. All quadratic models were significant (p < 0.0001), and no significant lack of fit was detected at the 5% significance level for any response. The models showed high coefficients of determination, with R2 values ranging from 0.9880 to 0.9989. The adjusted and predicted R2 values were in acceptable agreement for all responses, with a maximum difference of 0.0811. Adeq Precision values ranged from 39.6127 to 128.2383, indicating an adequate signal for process optimization. The lack of fit values for Energy consumed (p = 0.0501) and Vitamin C content (p = 0.0619) were close to the significance threshold and were therefore interpreted with caution. These statistical criteria were used to assess model adequacy within the response surface methodology framework (Selvamuthu & Das, 2024).

The maximum value of Permeate flux was obtained at a filtration time of 120 s and a fluid temperature of 60 °C, reaching 4.95 × 10−7 m3 m−2 s−1. The model adequacy statistics for this response are presented in Table 2. According to the ANOVA results in Table 3, filtration time, fluid temperature, their interaction, and the quadratic effect of filtration time significantly affected Permeate flux (p < 0.05), whereas the quadratic effect of fluid temperature was not significant (p = 0.5865). The combined effects of filtration time and fluid temperature are illustrated in Fig. 5(a). Because the durations of surface washing and backflow washing were fixed at 10 and 5 s, respectively, increasing the filtration interval up to 120 s increased the net permeate retained during each operating cycle. However, longer filtration intervals reduced Permeate flux, indicating that the benefit of increasing the filtration period was limited by progressive fouling during concentration. The operational relevance of periodic washing was evaluated directly through five replicate control runs conducted without activation of either washing stage, while maintaining a transmembrane pressure of 7 bar and a fluid temperature of 22.3 °C. In these control runs, complete cessation of flow during filtration occurred after a mean time of 4.3 min. This observation indicates that omission of periodic washing led to rapid operational blockage of the membrane module under the selected operating condition. In contrast, application of periodic washing allowed tomato serum concentration to proceed until the target endpoint of 14°Brix was reached. This behaviour is consistent with reports describing rapid flux decline and blocking phenomena during nanofiltration of complex fruit juice matrices. Lai et al. (2023) identified complete blocking as the dominant fouling mechanism during nanofiltration of cranberry juice and showed that removal of macromolecular and insoluble constituents by pretreatment increased permeate flux and reduced fouling. Similarly, Gaglianò et al. (2022) reported rapid permeate flux decline during nanofiltration concentration of clarified apple juice, which was attributed to concentration polarization and fouling phenomena. In the present study, the tomato serum feed had already undergone microfiltration, but residual colloidal structures remained in the feed matrix, and the serum was further concentrated to 14°Brix. Therefore, the rapid blockage observed without periodic washing supports the need for an in process fouling control strategy during tomato serum concentration. The present process was operated at a transmembrane pressure of 7 bar, which is lower than the pressures applied in recent nanofiltration studies on clarified apple juice and cranberry juice, where pressures of 25 and 40 bar were used, respectively (Gaglianò et al., 2022; Lai et al., 2023). This comparison does not establish superior separation performance, because the feed matrices, membrane properties and operating configurations differed among the studies. Nevertheless, it indicates that the developed periodic washing system enabled concentration of a tomato serum matrix with a pronounced tendency to foul the membrane under a relatively mild pressure condition. This is relevant for a food concentration process in which process operation must be considered together with the retention of selected compounds, as subsequently evaluated through Lycopene content and Vitamin C content.

Energy consumed

Among the experimental design runs, the lowest value of Energy consumed was obtained at a filtration time of 120 s and a fluid temperature of 20 °C, reaching 0.314 kWh per standardized batch concentration run. The model adequacy statistics for this response are presented in Table 2. According to the ANOVA results in Table 3, filtration time, fluid temperature, their interaction, and both quadratic terms significantly affected Energy consumed (p < 0.05). The combined effects of filtration time and fluid temperature on Energy consumed are illustrated in Fig. 5(b). The Energy consumed response included the electrical requirements of the fluid pumps, digital timers, solenoid valves, temperature control unit, and heating element during nanofiltration with periodic washing. All experimental design runs were conducted using the same initial amount of tomato serum and were continued until the concentrated serum reached 14°Brix. Therefore, Energy consumed provided a consistent basis for comparing the operating conditions evaluated in the present study. The markedly larger F value obtained for fluid temperature than for filtration time in the ANOVA results indicates that temperature was the dominant model factor affecting Energy consumed. This finding is consistent with the minimum value observed at 20 °C. The increase in Energy consumed at filtration intervals longer than 120 s may also be partly associated with the reduction in Permeate flux observed beyond this interval, which would increase the processing time required to reach the specified concentration endpoint. Membrane concentration processes are of interest in fruit juice processing because their comparatively mild operating conditions can reduce thermal exposure and energy demand relative to conventional concentration approaches (Conidi et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2024). Direct numerical comparison of the Energy consumed value obtained in the present study with values reported for other membrane concentration systems was not considered appropriate because energy demand depends strongly on processing scale, feed matrix, concentration endpoint, equipment configuration, operating conditions, and the basis used for normalization. In the present study, the minimum Energy consumed value occurred within the low temperature operating region that also favoured Lycopene content and Vitamin C content in the concentrated tomato serum.

Lycopene content

Among the experimental design runs, the highest Lycopene content in the concentrated tomato serum was obtained at a filtration time of 120 s and a fluid temperature of 20 °C, reaching 8.25 mg 100 g−1. The model adequacy statistics for this response are presented in Table 2. According to the ANOVA results in Table 3, filtration time, fluid temperature, their interaction, and both quadratic terms significantly affected Lycopene content (p < 0.05). Fluid temperature was the dominant model factor affecting Lycopene content, as indicated by its markedly higher F value (F = 906.90, p < 0.0001). The response surface shown in Fig. 5(c) indicates that Lycopene content decreased as fluid temperature increased. Although lycopene is susceptible to thermal deterioration, the reduction observed in the concentrated fraction cannot be attributed solely to chemical degradation because degradation kinetics were not independently determined in the present study. Nevertheless, the temperature related trend is consistent with studies reporting increased degradation of lycopene at elevated temperatures in tomato and other food matrices rich in carotenoids, while recognising that those studies were conducted at higher temperatures than the present process (Abdullahi et al., 2020; Syawalluddin et al., 2024). A relevant comparison based on membrane processing is provided by Arriola et al. (2014), who applied nanofiltration to watermelon juice and reported that Lycopene content in the concentrate increased with increasing volume reduction factor, with the highest content obtained at a volume reduction factor of three. In the present study, the higher Lycopene content obtained in the low temperature region supports the selection of mild operating conditions for tomato serum concentration. The contribution of the periodic washing mechanism was primarily operational, as it enabled concentration of a tomato serum matrix with a pronounced tendency to foul the membrane. The extent of lycopene retention is evaluated separately through concentration factor, recovery distribution, and apparent rejection in Section 3.8.

Vitamin C content

The response surface model identified higher Vitamin C content in the low temperature region and at a filtration interval close to 120 s. Under the operating conditions selected by numerical optimization, namely a filtration time of 120.9 s and a fluid temperature of 22.3 °C, the predicted Vitamin C content in the concentrated tomato serum was 46.32 mg 100 g−1. The corresponding value measured in the confirmation experiment was 46.41 mg 100 g−1. The model adequacy statistics for this response are presented in Table 2. According to the ANOVA results in Table 3, filtration time, fluid temperature, their interaction, and both quadratic terms significantly affected Vitamin C content (p < 0.05). Fluid temperature was the dominant model factor, as indicated by its markedly higher F value (F = 543.17, p < 0.0001). The response surface shown in Fig. 5(d) indicates that Vitamin C content decreased as fluid temperature increased. The lower Vitamin C content observed at higher temperatures supports selection of the low temperature operating region for tomato serum concentration. However, this trend should not be interpreted solely as chemical degradation, because degradation kinetics were not independently determined and Vitamin C content in the concentrated fraction may also reflect its distribution during processing. Rodrigues et al. (2020) reported that reverse osmosis concentration of a camu camu extract produced a concentrate with Vitamin C content seven times higher than that of the initial extract. Although direct numerical comparison is not appropriate because the food matrices and processing sequences differed, that study supports the use of membrane based concentration for producing fruit derived concentrates with increased Vitamin C content. In the present study, periodic washing primarily enabled continued nanofiltration of tomato serum despite its pronounced fouling tendency. Therefore, the retention behaviour of vitamin C is evaluated separately through concentration factor, recovery distribution, and apparent rejection in Section 3.8. More broadly, membrane based concentration remains relevant for fruit juice processing because it can limit thermal exposure during concentration, while fouling control remains essential for maintaining process performance (Ma et al., 2024).

Optimization and experimental validation of tomato serum nanofiltration

Following assessment of model adequacy and analysis of variance, simultaneous numerical optimization of the four measured responses was performed using the desirability function. The optimization goals, response ranges, and assigned weights for Permeate flux, Energy consumed, Lycopene content, and Vitamin C content are presented in Table 4. Equal weights were assigned to the four responses to identify operating conditions that balanced process operation with the contents of the selected bioactive compounds in the concentrated tomato serum. The selected operating conditions were a filtration time of 120.9 s and a fluid temperature of 22.3 °C, with an overall desirability value of 0.987. As presented in Table 5, the predicted values at these conditions were 3.99 × 10−7 m3 m−2 s−1 for Permeate flux, 0.308 kWh for Energy consumed, 8.25 mg 100 g−1 for Lycopene content, and 46.32 mg 100 g−1 for Vitamin C content. A confirmation experiment conducted at the selected operating conditions yielded 3.87 × 10−7 m3 m−2 s−1 for Permeate flux, 0.309 kWh for Energy consumed, 8.14 mg 100 g−1 for Lycopene content, and 46.41 mg 100 g−1 for Vitamin C content. The absolute relative deviations between the predicted and confirmation values were 3.01%, 0.32%, 1.33%, and 0.19% for Permeate flux, Energy consumed, Lycopene content, and Vitamin C content, respectively. These low deviations indicate close agreement between the model predictions and the measured responses obtained in the confirmation experiment, supporting the suitability of the desirability based optimization approach for selecting the operating conditions in the present process. Similar use of a confirmation experiment to evaluate agreement between predicted and experimental responses after RSM and desirability based optimization has been reported in food process optimization (Cruz et al., 2010). The selected conditions therefore represented a balanced operating point for the four target responses in tomato serum concentration. The fractions obtained from the confirmation experiment were used to calculate mass balance, recovery distribution, concentration factor, and apparent rejection for vitamin C and lycopene.

Mass balance, recovery distribution, and apparent rejection

The optimum operating conditions identified by the optimization procedure were verified through a confirmation experiment, and the resulting fractions were used for mass balance calculations. As shown in Table 6, the total liquid mass accounted for at the end of the experiment was equal to the initial liquid mass of 3500.0 g, confirming complete closure of the overall liquid balance. The initial tomato serum contained 13.21 mg 100 g−1 vitamin C and 2.42 mg 100 g−1 lycopene. In the collected concentrate, these concentrations increased to 46.41 and 8.14 mg 100 g−1, corresponding to concentration factors of 3.51 and 3.36, respectively. Recovery in the collected concentrate was 38.7% for vitamin C and 37.0% for lycopene. When the estimated compound masses in the liquid retained within the retentate circuit were included, the estimated total recoveries increased to 92.8% and 88.9%, respectively. This difference indicates that a substantial fraction of the concentrated compounds remained within the internal liquid volume of the laboratory apparatus. The final masses accounted for in the permeate circuit were 7.14 mg for vitamin C and 0.62 mg for lycopene, representing 3.6% and 1.7% of their initial masses. Accordingly, the apparent rejection values were 96.4% for vitamin C and 98.3% for lycopene. The estimated component mass balance closure was 96.4% for vitamin C and 90.6% for lycopene, leaving unaccounted fractions of 3.6% and 9.4%, respectively. The unaccounted vitamin C fraction was close to the 3% ascorbic acid loss reported during osmotic evaporation of camu camu juice and lower than the 8% loss reported for reverse osmosis concentration at 60 bar and 22 °C (Rodrigues et al., 2004). Conversely, Cassano et al. (2008) reported rejection values of 0 to 4.3% for ascorbic acid and other soluble acids during ultrafiltration of kiwifruit juice. Thus, the higher apparent rejection obtained in the present study should not be attributed solely to molecular sieving. For lycopene, Chaparro et al. (2016) reported complete retention during microfiltration of watermelon juice and increases in concentration by factors of 11 in the retentate and 41 in the final extract. The lower concentration factor obtained in the present study, 3.36, is consistent with a process intended to concentrate tomato serum while retaining both vitamin C and lycopene, rather than to produce a purified carotenoid extract. Overall, the apparent rejection values describe the performance of the integrated nanofiltration and washing process and should not be interpreted as intrinsic rejection coefficients of the membrane alone (Lu et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2024).

Membrane resistance and fouling analysis

The reversible and irreversible fouling resistances were 6.22 × 1014 and 7.78 × 1012 m−1, corresponding to 13.5% and 0.17% of the total fouling resistance, respectively (Table 7). The predominance of cake resistance indicates that accumulation of tomato serum constituents on the membrane surface was the principal contributor to the hydraulic resistance observed after filtration. The small irreversible resistance fraction indicates that only a limited proportion of the fouling resistance remained after chemical cleaning. These findings distinguish the functions of washing and cleaning in the developed process. Periodic washing was applied during nanofiltration to limit rapid deposit accumulation and allow tomato serum concentration to proceed, whereas cleaning was performed after completion of each experiment to recover membrane permeability and determine the remaining resistance components. The predominance of cake resistance indicates that retained material on the membrane surface may have contributed to the apparent rejection values obtained for vitamin C and lycopene. Because fouling and concentration polarization can influence observed retention during membrane processing of juice matrices, the apparent rejection values should be interpreted as indicators of the performance of the integrated nanofiltration and periodic washing process rather than as evidence of intrinsic membrane selectivity (Lu et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2024).

Conclusion

This study developed and optimized a nanofiltration process integrated with a two-stage periodic washing mechanism for tomato serum concentration and evaluation of selected bioactive compounds in the concentrated fraction. Numerical optimization within the evaluated design space identified a filtration time of 120.9 s and a fluid temperature of 22.3 °C as the selected operating conditions. In the confirmation experiment conducted under these conditions, the measured responses were Permeate flux of 3.87 × 10−7 m3 m−2 s−1, Energy consumed of 0.309 kWh per standardized batch concentration run, Lycopene content of 8.14 mg 100 g−1, and Vitamin C content of 46.41 mg 100 g−1. The measured Lycopene content and Vitamin C content corresponded to concentration factors of 3.36 and 3.51, respectively. The integrated process yielded apparent rejection values of 98.3% for lycopene and 96.4% for vitamin C, while the overall liquid mass balance was fully closed. These apparent rejection values describe the performance of the integrated nanofiltration and periodic washing process and should not be interpreted as intrinsic selectivity coefficients of the membrane. In the absence of periodic washing, the membrane module became blocked after 4.3 min, whereas application of the washing mechanism allowed tomato serum concentration to proceed until 14°Brix was reached. Resistance analysis showed that cake resistance represented 86.3% of the total fouling resistance, indicating that surface deposit accumulation was the principal contributor to the hydraulic resistance observed after filtration. Therefore, the principal contribution of the developed periodic washing mechanism was its ability to overcome the operational limitation caused by rapid fouling during nanofiltration of clarified tomato serum at a low fluid temperature. Further research should evaluate scale up performance, membrane behaviour during repeated operation, cleaning requirements, and process economics before the industrial applicability of the developed process is assessed.
