The Power of Power Codes: New Classes of Easy Instances for the Linear Equivalence Problem
Michele Battagliola, Anna-Lena Horlemann, Abhinaba Mazumder, Rocco Mora, Paolo Santini, Michael Schaller, Violetta Weger

Abstract
Given two linear codes, the Linear Equivalence Problem (LEP) asks to find (if it exists) a linear isometry between them; as a special case, we have the Permutation Equivalence Problem (PEP), in which isometries must be permutations. LEP and PEP have recently gained renewed interest as the security foundations for several post-quantum schemes, including LESS. A recent paper has introduced the use of the Schur product to solve PEP, identifying many new easy-to-solve instances. In this paper, we extend this result to LEP. In particular, we generalize the approach and rely on the more general notion of power codes. Combining it with Frobenius automorphisms and Hermitian hulls, we identify many classes of easy LEP instances. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work exploiting algebraic weaknesses for LEP. Finally we show an improved reduction to PEP whenever the coefficients of the monomial matrix are in a subgroup of the multiplicative group of the finite field.

Introduction

The Code Equivalence Problem (CEP) asks whether there exists a Hamming-metric isometry mapping one linear code to another.
Whenever the isometry is required to be a permutation, we speak of the Permutation Equivalence Problem (PEP); when instead we consider the more general case of monomials (permutations that can also scale coordinates), then we speak of the Linear Equivalence Problem (LEP).

Our contribution

In this paper, we identify several previously unknown weak instances. At the basis of our techniques lies the use of
power codes
, exploiting the property that the powers of equivalent codes remain equivalent.
This approach, combined with the concepts of the Frobenius automorphism and the (Hermitian) hull, allows us to derive new easy LEP instances based on the length and dimension of the code.

Remarkably, prior to this paper, power codes in the context of CEP have only been used to attack PEP

Here, we show how power codes can be used to attack (certain instances of) LEP.
Looking ahead, the classes of weak instances we identify, differ significantly from the ones that were already known, which required only

Indeed, our attacks have a strong algebraic flavor and show that, for any finite field, weak instances with non-trivial parameters (i.e., with dimension that grows as a power of

We further improve the attack for non-prime fields.

Let

Let

Definition 4

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an

2.3
The Code Equivalence Problem

Definition 5

(Linear Equivalence Problem (LEP))
.

Given as input two generator matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
of two linear codes
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, the Linear Equivalence Problem asks to find, if there exists, an invertible matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathrm{GL}_{k}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, a diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{d})
with
\mathbf{d}\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\star})^{n}
, and a permutation matrix
\mathbf{P}\in S_{n}
such that

\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}.

If
\mathbf{D}
is required to be the identity matrix we call this problem the
Permutation Equivalence Problem (PEP)
.

Whenever the dimension
h
of the hull of the input code is small enough, then PEP can be solved on average in polynomial time relying on SSA
[
20
]
or in worst case quasi-polynomial time by the reduction to the Graph Isomorphism Problem (GIP)
[
3
]
.

The complexity of both algorithms grows as
\widetilde{O}(n^{h})
.
Hence, whenever
h=O\big(\log(n)\big)
, we obtain a quasi-polynomial time solver for PEP.

Moreover,
[
17
]
shows how to transform any LEP instance into a PEP instance, using the closure.

Theorem 6

(Reducing LEP to PEP)
.

[
17
, Theorem 2]

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
be two
[n,k]_{q}
linear codes. Then
\mathcal{A}
is linearly equivalent to
\mathcal{B}
if and only if

\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{A}
is permutation equivalent to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{B}
.

The above transformation is, however, not very useful from a cryptanalytic standpoint, due to the following fact.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an
[n,k]_{q}
linear code, and let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{A}
be its closure.
Then, if
q\geq 4
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{A}\subset(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{A})^{\bot}
, hence the hull of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{A}
has dimension
k
.

If
k
is too small (say,
k=O\big(\log(n)\big)
, then the resulting PEP instance can be solved easily.
However, whenever the dimension is larger, we obtain a hard instance for PEP.

Power Codes

Let
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
be two
[n,k]_{q}

In

This definition does not depend on the choice of generator matrix and for
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{B}

[
3
]
.

Given

, respectively, we show a deterministic construction of four

Let

Simple solver for LEP

Proposition 8
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}

Let

be with generator matrices
\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2},\mathbf{B}_{1},\mathbf{B}_{2}

•

\mathcal{A}_{1}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\bot}=\mathcal{B}_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{2}^{\bot}=\{\mathbf{0}\}.

If Algorithm
1

Proof.

We prove the proposition by contraposition, assuming that
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
are linearly equivalent.
By hypothesis, we have
\mathbf{B}_{1}=\mathbf{S}_{1}\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{D}^{i}\mathbf{P},\;\mathbf{B}_{2}=\mathbf{S}_{2}\mathbf{A}_{2}\mathbf{D}^{j}\mathbf{P}

(q-1)c=(i+j)

Recall that

, since

. Hence,
\textsf{Adj}(\mathbf{B}_{1},\mathbf{B}_{2})
corresponds to

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\top}(\mathbf{B}_{1}\mathbf{B}_{2}^{\top})^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{S}_{2}\mathbf{A}_{2}\mathbf{D}^{j}\mathbf{P})^{\top}((\mathbf{S}_{1}\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{D}^{i}\mathbf{P})(\mathbf{S}_{2}\mathbf{A}_{2}\mathbf{D}^{j}\mathbf{P})^{\top})^{-1}(\mathbf{S}_{1}\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{D}^{i}\mathbf{P})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{j}\textsf{Adj}(\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2})\mathbf{D}^{i}\mathbf{P}.

Given a

In particular, the elements on the diagonal remain unchanged by the transformation:

d_{u,u}^{j}d_{u,u}^{i}m_{u,u}=d_{u,u}^{c(q-1)}m_{u,u}=m_{u,u}.

Applying
\mathbf{P}^{\top}

on the right implies an identical swap of column and row indices. Therefore, the elements on the diagonal are simply permuted among them. It follows that

In other words, whenever Algorithm

\mathcal{A}

∎

Frobenius automorphisms for non-prime fields

By letting the field size

The rate range loss can be mitigated by means of the Frobenius automorphisms.

Given

\mathcal{A}^{[p^{i}]}=\{\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\mid\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{A}\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},

2

2

The code

Theorem 9

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}

\mathbf{B}^{[p^{j}]}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}^{[p^{j}]}\mathbf{D}^{p^{j}}\mathbf{P}.

Thus, using the Frobenius automorphism we have the following construction: let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

Then, we pick

\mathcal{A}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}^{(p-1)}\star\dots\star(\mathcal{A}^{(p-1)})^{[p^{m-1}]}

We have:

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}((\mathcal{A}^{(p-1}))^{[p^{i}]})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathcal{A}^{(p-1)})\leq\binom{k+p-2}{p-1}^{m}.

\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(p-1)p^{i}=(p-1)\frac{p^{m}-1}{p-1}=q-1.

Since

k<\sqrt[(p-1)m]{(p-1)!^{m}\cdot n}\cdot(1+o(1)).

This is the first case where
(q-1)\mid(i+j)
, but
q-1\neq i+j
. It is not a coincidence that, differently from the previous constructions, this choice also allows to attack instances defined over fields of characteristic 2.

On the other hand, for odd field size, the factor 2 in
i+j=2(q-1)
can be removed, extending the range of weak parameters.
Indeed, let
q=p^{m}
,
p=2r+1
and

\mathcal{A}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}^{(r)}\star\dots\star(\mathcal{A}^{(r)})^{[p^{m-1}]},

\mathcal{B}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{B}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{B}^{(r)}\star\dots\star(\mathcal{B}^{(r)})^{[p^{m-1}]}.

Similarly as before, the dimension of
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\mathcal{A}_{2}
(and analogously of
\mathcal{B}_{1}=\mathcal{B}_{2}
) can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}((\mathcal{A}^{(r)})^{[p^{i}]})\leq\binom{k+r-1}{r}^{m}.

As before, we have that Algorithm
1
provides a PPT algorithm for the average LEP with high probability of success, for
q=(2r+1)^{m}
and whenever
\binom{k+r-1}{r}^{m}<n
, which for fixed
q
asymptotically becomes

k<\sqrt[rm]{r!^{m}\cdot n}(1+o(1)).

3.3
Hermitian hulls and further

The analysis shown before by means of the Frobenius automorphism can be further improved for fields that are even-degree extensions of prime fields, as we show next.

Let
q=p^{2\ell}
and

\mathcal{A}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}^{(p-1)}\star\dots\star(\mathcal{A}^{(p-1)})^{[p^{\ell-1}]},\quad\mathcal{A}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}_{1}^{[p^{\ell}]}

\mathcal{B}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{B}^{(p-1)}\star\dots\star(\mathcal{B}^{(p-1)})^{[p^{\ell-1}]},\quad\mathcal{B}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{B}_{1}^{[p^{\ell}]}.

With the same argument as before, the dimension of code

\mathcal{A}_{1}
(and analogously of
\mathcal{A}_{2},\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2}
) can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}((\mathcal{A}^{(p-1)})^{[p^{i}]})\leq\binom{k+p-2}{p-1}^{\ell}.

Differently from the previous constructions, this time we set
\mathcal{A}_{1}\neq\mathcal{A}_{2},\mathcal{B}_{1}\neq\mathcal{B}_{2}
.
However, the target intersections can be reinterpreted as Hermitian hulls:
\mathcal{A}_{1}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\perp}=\mathcal{H}_{H}(\mathcal{A}_{1}),\mathcal{B}_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{2}^{\perp}=\mathcal{H}_{H}(\mathcal{B}_{1})
.
Such Hermitian hulls are expected to be trivial with high probability as well. Let us define

t=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}(p-1)p^{i}=(p-1)\frac{p^{\ell}-1}{p-1}.

Since

q-1=(p-1)\frac{p^{2l}-1}{p-1}=\sum_{i=0}^{2l-1}(p-1)p^{i}=(t+p^{\ell}t),

which clearly divides
(t+p^{\ell}t)=i+j,

and since
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
, Proposition
8
thus implies that Algorithm
1
provides a PPT algorithm for the average LEP with high probability of success, for
q=p^{2\ell}
and whenever
\binom{k+p-2}{p-1}^{\ell}<n
.
For
q
fixed this asymptotically becomes

k<\sqrt[(p-1)\ell]{(p-1)!^{\ell}\cdot n}\cdot(1+o(1)).

3.4
Odd degree extensions

Finally, we show an additional improvement, applicable to odd degree extensions of odd characteristic fields, which cannot be interpreted as a hull (neither Euclidean nor Hermitian). Our framework is here applied in its most general form.

Let
q=p^{2\ell+1}
,
p=2r+1
and

\mathcal{A}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}^{(p-1)}\star\dots\star(\mathcal{A}^{(p-1)})^{[p^{\ell-1}]}\star(\mathcal{A}^{(r)})^{[p^{\ell}]},

\mathcal{A}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(\mathcal{A}^{(r)})^{[p^{\ell}]}\star(\mathcal{A}^{(p-1)})^{[p^{\ell+1}]}\star\dots\star(\mathcal{A}^{(p-1)})^{[p^{2\ell}]},

\mathcal{B}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{B}^{(p-1)}\star\dots\star(\mathcal{B}^{(p-1)})^{[p^{\ell-1}]}\star(\mathcal{B}^{(r)})^{[p^{\ell}]},

\mathcal{B}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(\mathcal{B}^{(r)})^{[p^{\ell}]}\star(\mathcal{B}^{(p-1)})^{[p^{\ell+1}]}\star\dots\star(\mathcal{B}^{(p-1)})^{[p^{2\ell}]}.

The dimension of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
(and analogously of
\mathcal{A}_{2},\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2}
) can be upper bounded in the following way

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}((\mathcal{A}^{(r)})^{[p^{\ell}]})\cdot\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}((\mathcal{A}^{(p-1)})^{[p^{i}]})

\displaystyle\leq\binom{k+p-2}{p-1}^{\ell}\cdot\binom{k+r-1}{r}.

If
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}
, then
\mathbf{B}_{1}=\mathbf{S}_{1}\mathbf{A}_{1}\mathbf{D}^{i}\mathbf{P}
,
\mathbf{B}_{2}=\mathbf{S}_{2}\mathbf{A}_{2}\mathbf{D}^{j}\mathbf{P}
, where
i=(p-1)+(p-1)p+\dots+(p-1)p^{\ell-1}+rp^{\ell}
and
j=rp^{\ell}+(p-1)p^{\ell+1}+\dots+(p-1)p^{2\ell}
. As usual, we can check that
q-1=(i+j)\mid(i+j)
and that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathcal{A}_{1})=\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathcal{A}_{2})
.
Experiments reported in Table
2
indicate that we get with good probability
\mathcal{A}_{1}\cap\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\bot}=\mathcal{B}_{1}\cap\mathcal{B}_{2}^{\bot}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
Similar to
[
6
]
, such probabilities could be further improved by applying a refinement as in the Support Splitting Algorithm
[
20
]
.
Thus, Proposition
8
implies that Algorithm
1
provides a PPT algorithm for the average LEP with high probability of success, for
q=(2r+1)^{2\ell+1}
and whenever
\binom{k+p-2}{p-1}^{\ell}\cdot\binom{k+r-1}{r}=\binom{k+2r-1}{2r}^{\ell}\cdot\binom{k+r-1}{r}<n
, which asymptotically relaxes the constraint on
k
to

k<\sqrt[2rl+r]{(2r)!^{l}r!\cdot n}\cdot(1+o(1)).

4
Partial Closure

A natural question to ask is if taking power codes on the hull of a code can increase the range of the attacks in Section
3
.
However, the answer to this question is negative.
Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{A}
denote the code generated by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathbf{A}
.
Then we have
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{A})^{(\ell)}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{[\ell]}\otimes\mathcal{A}^{(\ell)}
where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{[\ell]}
is the component-wise
\ell
-th power of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{A}^{(\ell)}
is the
\ell
-th power code.

Instead, we might use the power code together with a new construction which we call
partial closure
to get a reduction to PEP.
This reduction will usually still give a self-orthogonal code, but of shorter length.

Definition 10
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\star}
be a primitive element, and let
\mathcal{A}
be a
[n,k]_{q}
linear code generated by
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
.
Let
r\mid(q-1)
and define

\boldsymbol{\alpha}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(1,\alpha^{(q-1)/r},\alpha^{2(q-1)/r},\ldots,\alpha^{(r-1)(q-1)/r}).

The
r
-th
partial closure
is defined as the span of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathbf{A}
and denoted by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{A}
.

Remark 11
.

The special case
r=q-1
is the usual closure and the case
r=1
does not change the code at all.

Let
U=\langle\alpha^{(q-1)/r}\rangle
be the subgroup of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\star}
generated by
\alpha^{(q-1)/r}
.
Note that for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
as above, we still have
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}=\sum_{\beta\in U}\beta^{2}=0
as long as
|U|=r>2
by orthogonality of characters since there exists an element
\beta
such that
\beta^{2}\neq 1
and since the map
x\to\beta x
is a permutation of
U
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be two linearly equivalent codes with generator matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
.
Let
\mathbf{D}
be a diagonal matrix with entries
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
and
\mathbf{P}
be a permutation matrix such that
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}
.
Then, we have for the partial closure

\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathbf{A})\tilde{\mathbf{D}}(\mathbf{I}_{r}\otimes\mathbf{P})

for some monomial matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}
with special properties which we will describe next.
Let
d_{i}=\alpha^{j_{i}}
with
j_{i}=s_{i}\frac{q-1}{r}+t_{i}

with
0\leq t_{i}<\frac{q-1}{r}
.
The matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}
can be chosen to be a block diagonal matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{M}_{n}).

Here
\mathbf{M}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{r\times r}
can be chosen to be of the form
\alpha^{t_{i}}
multiplied by a permutation matrix that gives a shift by
s_{i}
.
This means that
\mathbf{M}_{i}
is a monomial matrix that has entries only from the fixed set of coset representatives
1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{(q-1)/r-1}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\star}/\langle\alpha^{(q-1)/r}\rangle
.
This is only a permutation if
d_{i}=\alpha^{j(q-1)/r}
, i.e., if
d_{i}\in U
.
In general such matrices
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}
do not form a group.

They trivially form a group if
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\in

S_{rn}
.
That is, if
t_{i}=0
for all
i
, or equivalently, in the case where the diagonal entries are in the subgroup
U
generated by
\alpha^{\frac{q-1}{r}}
.

Note that if the rate is small enough this property can be achieved using power codes as we saw in Section
3
.
Therefore, in the following, we restrict to this case.

Definition 12
.

The (search version of the) code equivalence problem with entries in the subgroup
U\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\star}
(written LEP(
U
)) asks to find, given two codes
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
with generator matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
, if there exists, an invertible matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathrm{GL}_{k}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, a diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{d})
with
\mathbf{d}\in U^{n}
, and a permutation matrix
\mathbf{P}\in S_{n}
such that
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}
.

LEP, PEP and signed permutation equivalence problem fall naturally in LEP(
U
) with
U=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\star},U=\{1\}
and
U=\{\pm 1\}
.

Now we can apply the partial closure.
Note, that while we still end up with a self-orthogonal code, the length is now smaller and we get a better reduction to PEP.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
be two
[n,k]_{q}
linear codes and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
as in Definition
10
.
Then
\mathcal{A}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{B}
with entries in the subgroup
U
generated by
\alpha^{\frac{q-1}{r}}
if and only if

\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{A}
is permutation equivalent to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{B}
.

The proof for the reduction is essentially the same as for Theorem
6
in
[
17
, Theorem 1]
and thus omitted.
