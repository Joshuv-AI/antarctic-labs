Low-temperature hyperbolic phonon polaritonics with suppressed phononic scattering
Authors: Gang Zhong, Lu Liu, Wenqi Bi, Zongyuan Xie, Zhe Cui, Hao Yang, Jingda Wu, Qintao Sun, Ying Xiao, Qiaoxia Xing, Yingjie Wu, Tao Cheng, Hugen Yan, Bo Peng, Qiaoliang Bao, Chong Wang, Zhigao Dai, Qingdong Ou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyperbolic phonon polaritons (PhPs) enable unparalleled light manipulation due to their subwavelength confinement and anisotropic dispersions but suffer from substantial phononic scattering losses at room temperature. Recent cryogenic nanoimaging has demonstrated extended hyperbolic PhP propagation at low temperatures; however, the fundamental dissipation mechanisms remain elusive. Here, we unveil the intrinsic loss channels and scattering rates of hyperbolic PhPs in van der Waals alpha–molybdenum trioxide (α-MoO3) and bulk yttrium orthovanadate (YVO4) through integrated structural, vibrational, and polaritonic characterizations at cryogenic temperatures, complemented by anharmonic lattice dynamics analysis. We reveal that three-phonon interactions dominate dissipation within the entire hyperbolic frequency range; four-phonon processes become significantly inhibited at low temperature. Notably, suppressed phonon decay extends the PhP lifetime, leading to enhanced quality factors by ~97.8% in α-MoO3–based polaritonic crystals. This phonon suppression-induced prolongation of polariton lifetime also applies to YVO4, and temperature-dependent analysis of various PhP materials further confirms its universality. These insights elucidate the dissipation physics of hyperbolic PhPs and provide a foundation for designing high-performance polaritonic devices for low-temperature applications.

INTRODUCTION

Polaritonics (1–5), leveraging the strong coupling between light and matter, catalyzes transformative progress in twistronics (6–8), nanoimaging (9–12), sensing (13–15), energy conversion (16–18), and ultrafast and quantum optics (19–23). However, elevated temperature-induced polariton losses represent a significant constraint limiting its further applications. Cryogenic polaritons serve as an effective approach to mitigate this limitation, exhibiting lower losses and extended lifetimes. For instance, electron-electron and electron-phonon scattering are suppressed in plasmon polaritons (PPs) at low temperatures (24–26), leading to a pronounced temperature dependence of PPs properties, as has been observed in hyperbolic WTe2 (27, 28). The suppression of intrinsic losses enables long-distance propagation exceeding 13 μm with an intrinsic 12-ps lifetime of PPs in graphene (25). It even facilitates the gating of programmable Bloch modes in graphene-based plasmonic crystals (29, 30).

Unlike PPs, phonon polaritons (PhPs) result from the photon-phonon coupling, where phonons represent quantized lattice vibrations that are highly temperature-sensitive due to their origin in thermal motions of atoms. Moreover, the intrinsic wide bandgap of PhP dielectric materials with fewer defects renders electron scattering negligible. This makes phonon-phonon scattering the dominant pathway for PhP losses. Therefore, unveiling the phonon scattering and decay channels through crystal structure and chemical bond vibrations is key to understanding the PhP loss mechanisms. Early studies have explored the low-temperature dissipation of PhPs in both hBN and α-MoO3 (31–33). Recently, the prolonged PhP propagation at the bulk YVO4 crystal surface has also been observed via cryogenic nanoimaging (34). All the observed extensions in propagation serve as evidence for a smaller polariton damping rate and reduced losses. However, these current findings rely on apparent distance measurements, while the fundamental loss mechanisms governing PhPs remain incompletely elucidated, impeding progress in polariton manipulation and the development of advanced polaritonic devices.

Here, we reveal the phonon scattering mechanisms and decay channels in two representative hyperbolic crystals, van der Waals (vdW) α-MoO3 and bulk YVO4, through cryogenic structural and vibrational characterization combined with anharmonic scattering analysis. It is found that phonon dissipation at room temperature is primarily governed by the coexistence of three-phonon and four-phonon processes, while the extended phonon lifetime at low temperatures results from the suppression of scattering. At 140 K, the four-phonon scattering process even vanishes, doubling the quality (Q) factor in α-MoO3–based hyperbolic polaritonic crystals (PoCs; consisting of a thin α-MoO3 slab on a patterned Au disk lattice) with prolonged polariton lifetime. In addition, the analysis of V─O bond vibrations demonstrates that this mechanism of phonon scattering suppression is equally applicable to YVO4. We further verify its applicability in other PhP platforms through temperature-dependent analysis. Our work reveals cryogenic loss mechanisms in hyperbolic PhPs, enabling low-loss devices and advanced quantum and nanophotonic applications.

RESULTS

Principle of low-temperature enhancement for PhPs

PhPs originate from the strong coupling between infrared photons and optical phonons. Being bosons, phonons have a thermal population that strictly follows Bose-Einstein statistics. It implies that higher temperatures lead to an increase in the number of thermally excited phonons, thereby intensifying phonon-phonon scattering processes governed by the Planck distribution. For the Raman shift induced by anharmonic phonon scattering (35, 36), the contributions from three-phonon and four-phonon processes can be evaluated separately byω(T)=ω0+γω3ph[1+2ex−1]+γω4ph[1+3ey−1+3(ey−1)2](1)where x=ħω02kBT, and y=ħω03kBT. γω3ph and γω4ph are fitted constants of temperature-dependent Raman shift in per centimeter, named anharmonic scattering coefficient (γ). Thus, according to the simplified relationship (eqs. S1 and S2), we obtain that γω3ph ∝ T, whereas γω4ph ∝ T2.

At high temperatures, the lattice vibrations become more intense and disordered, making four-phonon processes particularly significant. As temperature decreases, both scattering processes are suppressed, with the four-phonon contribution diminishing more rapidly, leaving three-phonon scattering processes to dominate in the ordered crystal lattice (Fig. 1A). This suppression of phonon scattering and decay channels leads to an increase in phonon lifetime.

The prolonged phonon lifetime at lower temperatures directly reduces the loss and prolongs the lifetime of PhPs, resulting in significantly extended propagation distances in the near field (Fig. 1, B and C). When used in polaritonic structures, such as PoCs, these long-lived PhPs enable stronger resonant responses and higher Q factors (Fig. 1D). This fundamental property highlights the crucial role of temperature control in manipulating PhPs and offers a pathway for advancing high-performance polaritonic devices.

Correlation between anisotropic lattice changes and chemical bond vibrations in α-MoO3

The lifetimes of phonons and PhPs are intrinsically linked to lattice vibrations. Investigating lattice dynamics, the collective behavior of atoms, is essential for understanding the loss mechanisms of polaritons, and low temperatures can provide better experimental conditions by avoiding the introduction of additional interference. In orthorhombic α-MoO3 (Pnma space group) (37), each Mo atom is coordinated by six oxygen atoms, forming three inequivalent types: one O1, two O2, and three O3 atoms (Fig. 2A). The Mo─O1 bond, along with one Mo─O3 bond and the vdW gap, defines the b axis ([010] direction). The O2─Mo─O2 bonds form the a axis ([100] direction), while the three Mo─O3 bonds create a chain-like network along the c axis ([001] direction). To investigate these structural changes at low temperatures, we performed temperature-dependent x-ray diffraction (XRD) on α-MoO3 from 623 down to 153 K. The XRD pattern at 298 K exhibited three strong characteristic peaks alongside several weaker ones, matching the reference pattern [Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS), no. 05-0508] (fig. S1A). Upon cooling, the (040) diffraction peak shifted from 25.21° to 25.77° (Fig. 2B), indicating a contraction of the interplanar spacing. This shift corresponds to a reduction in the lattice parameter b from 14.08 to 13.78 Å. The values of a and c were also obtained (Fig. 2C; fig. S1, B and C; and eqs. S3 and S4). While a and b decreased with cooling, c increased, leading to an overall reduction in unit cell volume (fig. S1D). This anisotropic thermal expansion of the lattice agrees well with previous works (38, 39), as illustrated in Fig. 2D.

Then, the Raman spectroscopy was used to probe the vibrational modes of Mo─O bonds. Typically, peaks in the range of 0 to 200 cm−1 correspond to lattice modes, 200 to 600 cm−1 to deformation, and 600 to 1000 cm−1 to bond stretching (Fig. 2E) (40–42). We selected three peaks that represent the anisotropic phonon vibrational modes in α-MoO3, and their variation along the respective crystallographic directions corresponds to the RBs of its anisotropic PhPs: 158 cm−1 (translational motion of Mo─O3 chains, along [001], RB1), 820 cm−1 (asymmetric stretching of Mo─O2 bonds, along [100], RB2), and 996 cm−1 (symmetric stretching of Mo─O bonds, along [010], RB3) (fig. S2).

All peaks exhibited temperature-dependent shifts (fig. S3): The mode at 158 cm−1 (RB1) exhibited a pronounced blueshift of 5.03 cm−1, indicating shortening of the Mo─O3 bond (Fig. 2F). The peak at 820 cm−1 showed a red shift of 1.51 cm−1, suggesting O2─Mo─O2 bond stretching (Fig. 2G). The discrepancy between the Raman trends and the expansion of the a- and c-axis lattice parameters observed by XRD is attributed to temperature-induced changes in bond angles (39). The concerted effects of bond length and bond angle variations lead to anisotropic thermal expansion. The peak at 996 cm−1 displayed an anomalously small blueshift of 0.35 cm−1, implying Mo─O1 bond elongation (Fig. 2H), which is also inconsistent with the XRD results. This divergence is attributed to the dominant influence of the contraction of the vdW gap at low temperature. In general, the peak shifts in XRD and Raman spectra manifest different Mo─O bonds in the collective lattice behavior of α-MoO3, representing the sources of phonons and phonon scattering.

Anharmonic phonon scattering and decay channels in α-MoO3

Based on the understanding of the correlation between anisotropic lattice changes and bond vibrations, we further investigate the phonon scattering mechanisms of α-MoO3. Phonon scattering typically arises from phonon-electron interactions, phonon-phonon interactions, impurities, and grain boundaries. For the custom-grown, high-quality, and single-crystal intrinsic α-MoO3 with a wide bandgap of 3.0 eV used in our experiments (42), the phonon-electron scattering, as well as the effects of impurities and grain boundaries, can be neglected. Thus, the decay model used here primarily considers three-phonon and four-phonon scattering processes.

The three-phonon and four-phonon decay mechanisms are illustrated in Fig. 3 (A and B) (40), where arrows indicate possible phonon decay channels (eqs. S5 to S7). Alternatively, the reverse process occurs when two low-energy phonons A1g(Г) and TA(Y) combine to generate one high-energy phonon TO3(Y), indicated by red arrows. The purple arrows indicate that the four-phonon process involves the scattering of one high-energy phonon A1g(Г) into one low-energy optical phonon B3g(Г) and two acoustic phonons TA(Г−Y) and ZA(Г−Y). The contributions of phonon decay channels to scattering are quantifiable. Stronger resonances and larger quality factors in α-MoO3 PoC

The excitation of hyperbolic PhPs in this study is based on a PoC that consists of a thin α-MoO3 slab atop a two-dimensional (2D) Au disk lattice (Fig. 4A and fig. S4A). PoCs introduce artificially designed periodic variations of permittivity into materials, establishing Bragg scattering effects for manipulating polaritons. One method involves constructing a periodic dielectric constant directly on the material (44–47), while the other involves creating a periodic dielectric environment on substrates (48–50). PoCs based on α-MoO3 exhibit anisotropic Bloch modes and distinct optical responses for different RBs, bridging the far-field spectra and near-field electromagnetic distributions. The low-temperature performance of the hyperbolic PhPs in our PoCs was characterized using cryogenic far-field Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), supplemented by near-field images.

Room-temperature FTIR measurements with varying periodicities (P) revealed previously unidentified resonance peaks at 984 cm−1. These peaks were most pronounced at P = 2 μm under polarized light (fig. S4, B and C) and were absent in pure α-MoO3 and Au disks lattice alone (fig. S4D), confirming the successful construction of the PoCs. Further analysis focused on the PoC with P = 2 μm to investigate the low-temperature behavior of the in-plane hyperbolic PhPs. The resonance peaks can be assigned to specific hyperbolic RBs: 879 and 919 cm−1 to RB2 ([100] polarization) and 693 and 796 cm−1 to RB1 ([001] polarization) (Fig. 4, A and B, and fig. S2). These findings demonstrate the sensitivity of hyperbolic PoCs to both frequency and RB characteristics of α-MoO3. Cryogenic FTIR measurements at 12 K revealed a red shift in all resonance peaks, further confirming the coupling between α-MoO3 and Au lattice.

Near-field imaging at specific frequencies in Fig. 4 (D to H) confirmed the excitation of Bloch modes in hyperbolic PoCs associated with RB3 (984 cm−1, fig. S5), RB2 (919 and 879 cm−1), and RB1 (769 and 693 cm−1). The arrangement of these peaks is determined by the anisotropic variation of the dielectric constants of α-MoO3 along the [001] (RB1), [100] (RB2), and [010] (RB3) directions. Bloch modes are generated only when the incident light matches the resonant frequency, while no obvious resonance is observed at other frequencies (fig. S6). The polaritonic patterns of our hyperbolic PoCs in RB1 exhibited collective canalization, a previously unreported phenomenon attributed to Au lattice and α-MoO3 coupling. This novel phenomenon is further confirmed by the simulated electromagnetic distribution (fig. S7). We define it as canalization induced by resonance in artificial structures with periodically varying dielectric constants. Fourier transforms of near-field images and isofrequency contours (IFCs) in momentum space further revealed the selective mode excitations in hyperbolic PoCs at different frequencies (Fig. 4, I to M). Furthermore, a connection has been established among the dispersion relations, the IFCs, and the far-field resonance peaks (fig. S8).

Low-temperature analysis of representative peaks (RB3, 984 cm−1; RB2, 919 cm−1; and RB1, 693 cm−1) corresponding to the three RBs showed a nonmonotonic temperature-dependent shift: an initial red shift, followed by a blueshift, ultimately resulting in a net red shift (Fig. 4N). The specific values of the peak red shift are 3.84 cm−1 for RB1 and 2.94 cm−1 for RB2, both of which are larger than 1.02 cm−1 for RB3. The blueshift values are 1.28, 1.02, and 1.02 cm−1, which are closer to each other. This disparity suggests that the resonance within the in-plane hyperbolic band (RB1 and RB2) is more sensitive to temperature variations, thereby enhancing the frequency sensitivity of the Bloch mode. All the resonance peaks show the same shift tendency, even at P = 3 μm (fig. S9). Note that this behavior contrasts with the intrinsic peak blueshift or red shift observed in pure α-MoO3 (fig. S10). We deduce that the observed peak shift arises from a temperature-induced mismatch between the IFC evolution and the Bragg lattice, driven by alterations of the phonon modes (51–53).

The FWHM of the resonance peaks was fitted using a Lorentz function and was found to decrease with temperature (fig. S11 and Fig. 4O). These resonance peaks originate from the interaction between the periodic dielectric environment in the PoC and the polaritons, with their peak width (i.e., the FWHM) directly reflecting the loss characteristics of the system. For the in-plane hyperbolic bands (RB1 and RB2), the magnitude of the reduction in FWHM differs by an order of magnitude compared to that of the out-of-plane hyperbolic RB3. This linewidth narrowing leads to a significant increase in the quality factor Q at lower temperatures (Figs. 1D and 4P). The Q = ω0/Γ (where ω0 is the resonance frequency and Γ is the FWHM) is a key parameter for evaluating the energy retention capability of a mode in a system (54). An increase in its value explicitly indicates a reduction of loss, thereby demonstrating an extension of the PhP lifetime.

Notably, the Q factor of RB2 exhibited the most significant improvement, increasing by ~97.8% from 41.3 to 81.7 at low temperatures, compared to a 44.9% increase for RB1 and an 8% increase for RB3. This indicates that RB2 is the hyperbolic band most significantly impacted by temperature variations. These peak shift results confirm that hyperbolic PhPs in hyperbolic PoCs are different from those in pure α-MoO3 and result in strong sensitivity of Bloch modes to frequency and temperature. Furthermore, the higher Q at low temperatures implies enhanced polariton lifetimes, demonstrating strong coherence and reduced damping.

Anharmonic phonon scattering and enhanced polariton propagation in YVO4

The suppression of phonon scattering and decay channels results in extended lifetimes for both phonons and hyperbolic PhPs, a mechanism that applies not only to vdW materials but also to 3D bulk crystals. In the unit cell of the YVO4 crystal, each oxygen atom bonds with two Y atoms and one V atom. Due to the oxygen coordination numbers of 8 and 4 for Y and V, respectively, their atomic ratio is effectively 1:1 (Fig. 5A and fig. S12A) (34). Possible three-phonon and four-phonon scattering channels are indicated by arrows in the phonon spectrum (Fig. 5B and eqs. S10 and S11). The red arrows indicate that a typical four-phonon process involves the scattering of one high-energy phonon A1g(Г) into one low-energy optical phonon B2g(Г) and two acoustic phonons TA(Г−M) and ZA(Г−M).

To further investigate anharmonic phonon scattering, we performed temperature-dependent Raman spectroscopy on the peak at 890 cm−1, which originates from the symmetric stretching of the V─O bond (Fig. 5C and fig. S12B) (55, 56).

The changes in the peak position and width of the highlighted peak, as well as the corresponding changes in phonon lifetime, are presented in Fig. 5 (D and E). As shown in Fig. 5D, the vibration of the V─O bond exhibits decreased FWHM from 5.15 to 3.85 cm−1 as the temperature decreased from 300 to 80 K. Meanwhile, the blueshift from 890.78 to 894.36 cm−1 indicates an increase in energy and a shortening of the bond length. This phenomenon is consistent with the trend observed for the peak at 158 cm−1 of α-MoO3. Consequently, the phonon lifetime, calculated using the damping constant, increased from 1.03 to 1.38 ps (Fig. 5E). We fitted the data using an anharmonic model, and the obtained γ3ph and γ4ph indicate the coexistence of three-phonon and four-phonon processes at room temperature (table S5).

As the temperature decreases, phonon scattering is suppressed, and the four-phonon process exhibits greater sensitivity to temperature within our measured range due to its higher-order contribution, according to the results of α-MoO3. Therefore, we can regard the four-phonon scattering process as a hallmark of phonon scattering suppression. As shown in Fig. 5F, the absolute values of γω4ph and γГ4ph decrease from 0.0128 and 0.005 at 140 K to 0.033 and 0.0158 at 300 K, respectively. This indicates that, although YVO4 is a 3D bulk crystal different from layered α-MoO3, both materials exhibit the common behavior of four-phonon process suppression at low temperatures. As a result of the strong suppression of phonon scattering at low temperatures, the polariton propagation distance can be significantly enhanced. This conclusion is confirmed by recent near-field images of YVO4 with the propagation is confirmed by recent near-field images of YVO4 with the propagation distance extending from less than 30 μm at 300 K to 59 μm at 150 K (Fig. 1, B and C) (34).

Overall, the suppression of phonon scattering, both with the three-phonon and four-phonon scattering, is the primary reason for the increased lifetime of PhPs, with four-phonon scattering serving as a key indicator of this suppression. This conclusion, backed by comprehensive data extraction and fitting from numerous previous studies, demonstrates a similar trend across diverse PhP platforms (Fig. 5G, fig. S13, and tables S6 to S8). Comparative analysis between high and low temperature conditions reveals that four-phonon processes become particularly significant at elevated temperatures. As the temperature decreases, the four-phonon scattering channels are strongly suppressed, signifying the overall suppression of phonon scattering and the prolongation of phonon lifetime. This mechanism enables PhPs to achieve extended longevity while simultaneously yielding higher Q in related polaritonic devices. These findings provide valuable insights for macroscopic understanding of polariton enhancement through phonon scattering suppression, opening possibilities for developing low-loss PhP devices and advancing cryogenic quantum photonics.

DISCUSSION

In summary, using multiple cryogenic characterization techniques, we reveal the phonon scattering–dominated loss mechanism of hyperbolic PhPs. The anharmonic scattering model, combining temperature-induced anisotropic lattice contractions and bond vibrations, confirms that both three-phonon and four-phonon scattering contribute to polariton losses. The suppressed phonon scattering with dominated three-phonon scattering and nearly eliminated four-phonon scattering at reduced temperatures are identified as the origin of the enhanced polaritons in hyperbolic PoCs based on α-MoO3. Cryogenic FTIR spectroscopy demonstrates a significant increase in the Q factors within the hyperbolic bands. This mechanism of low-temperature phonon scattering suppression is also applicable to YVO4 and other PhP systems. These findings provide fundamental insights into the low-temperature loss mechanism of polariton dynamics, highlighting its potential for cryogenically optimized polaritonic devices (57). Emerging phenomena such as hyperbolic exciton polaritons (58), Dirac magnetoexcitons (59), and temperature-dependent PhPs (60–62) could be further investigated and combined with loss-reduced techniques like complex-frequency waves (11, 63), unlocking possibilities in nanophotonics and quantum optical technologies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample fabrication

The Au disks were fabricated on the pure silicon substrates by direct laser writing lithography to define the patterns. Electron-beam evaporation was subsequently used to deposit 35-nm-thick Au in a vacuum with a chamber pressure of <6 × 10−6 torr. α-MoO3 bulk crystals were grown by the chemical vapor transport method. In brief, 0.1 g of α-MoO3 particle (Alfa Aesar) was placed in the middle of a quartz tube [2 inches (5.08 cm) in diameter], where the temperature was 780°C. Ar [200 standard cubic centimeters per minute (SCCM)] and O2 (50 SCCM) were used as carrier gases to reduce the oxygen vacancies. α-MoO3 bulk crystals were collected at the downstream position where the temperature was ~550°C. α-MoO3 flakes (160 nm) were mechanically exfoliated and then transferred onto the Au disk lattice.

Optical measurements

Real-space imaging experiments were performed using the commercially available scattering-type scanning near-field optical microscopy (s-SNOM; Neaspec) with a basic setup based on a tapping-mode atomic force microscope (AFM). The incident light is a p-polarized laser beam with tunable mid-infrared wavelengths, which illuminates the AFM tip (NanoWorld) with oscillating frequencies of ~285 kHz and an amplitude of ~70 nm, respectively. The nanoscale hotspot is then formed at the tip to map the near field of the sample. Last, the pseudoheterodyne interferometer is used to record the tip scattering field in the far field, and the near-field image is generated by demodulation of the tip oscillation harmonics.

Characterizations

A Bruker FTIR spectrometer (Vertex 80v) integrated with a Hyperion 2000 microscope and a mid-infrared polarizer (ZnSe) was used in the measurements. The Mercury cadmium telluride was used as a detector with a cryostat (Janis ST-300). The XRD test was measured by Bruker D8 Advance. Raman tests were conducted using a 532-nm laser coupled to a confocal microscope system (WITec, alpha 300R) with a helium optical cryostat.
