Approximation properties of double complexes
Daniel F. Holmen 1,*, Jan M. Nordbotten 1,2, Jon E. Vatne 3

Abstract
. We consider the simplicial de Rham complex and the Čech-de Rham complex, two bigraded Hilbert complexes whose Hodge-Laplace problems govern spatially coupled problems in mixed dimension and homogeneous dimension, respectively. The former complex can be realized as a subcomplex of the latter. In this paper, we quantify how close these complexes are to each other by constructing bounded cochain complexes between them, and thus we quantify how close a mixed-dimensional formulation of a problem is to an equidimensionally coupled formulation of the same problem. From this construction, we derive a priori- and a posteriori error estimates between the associated Hodge-Laplace problems on the two complexes. These estimates represent the error which is introduced by treating a spatially coupled problem as mixed-dimensional, rather than an equidimensional problem with thin overlaps.



1.
Introduction

Hilbert complexes are obtained by combining the familiar Hilbert spaces from functional analysis with cochain complexes from homological algebra, providing a rich mathematical structure for modeling and analysis of elliptical PDEs. The prototypical example of such a cochain complex is the de Rham complex, and the associated Hodge-Laplace problem on the de Rham complex confines both scalar and vector-Laplace problems with corresponding boundary conditions.

In the framework of finite element exterior calculus (FEEC)
[
1
,
2
]
, a bounded cochain projection is constructed from infinite-dimensional Hilbert complexes to their finite-dimensional counterparts. These projections lead to several important results related to the consistency, stability and convergence of mixed finite element methods.

Using the same concepts and similar techniques, we construct a bounded cochain projection between two infinite-dimensional Hilbert complexes; the Čech-de Rham complex
[
6
,
4
]
and the embedded mixed-dimensional de Rham complex
[
5
,
7
]
.

Concretely, we obtain approximation properties between the Hodge-Laplace problems on the simplicial de Rham complex and the Čech-de Rham complex, which have been shown to correspond to two different modeling paradigms for spatially coupled problems. Such model approximations for elliptic equations appear in e.g.
[
9
,
11
]
.

This article is structured as follows:
section

1
gives a overview over the theory of Hilbert complexes, as well as introducing the specific Hilbert complexes of interest: the simplicial de Rham complex and the Čech-de Rham complex.
Section

2
discusses Hilbert subcomplexes and bounded cochain projections. In
section

3
, we derive a priori error estimates based on the abstract Hilbert complex theory from
[
1
]
. We also derive functional a posteriori error estimates based on
[
14
]
. Finally, in
section

4
we consider a simple coupled problem as an example: two one-dimensional elastically joined rods.

1.1.
Hilbert complexes

We begin by establishing basic definitions and notations for Hilbert complexes. We refer to
[
1
,
2
]
for a more comprehensive study on the topic.

A
Hilbert complex

(A^{\bullet},d_{A})
is a cochain complex where each object is a Hilbert space and each differential
d_{A}^{k}:A^{k}\to A^{k+1}
is a closed densely-defined unbounded linear operator:

(1.1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{...}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A^{k-1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A^{k}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A^{k+1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{...}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d^{k-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d^{k}}

We frequently omit the subscript indicating the domain of the differential operator whenever convenient. Moreover, all Hilbert complexes we are working with are closed (i.e. a Hilbert complex where
\mathrm{im}\;d_{A}^{k}
is closed in
A^{k+1}
for every integer
k
), and we therefore assume throughout this section that
(A^{\bullet},d_{A})
is a closed Hilbert complex.

Since the differentials
d_{A}
are not necessarily defined everywhere on
A^{k}
, there is an associated subcomplex of
A^{k}
which we refer to as the
domain complex
, defined by the domains of the differential operators
d_{A}
(we write
\mathrm{dom}\;d^{k}_{A}=\mathcal{A}^{k}
):

(1.2)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{...}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{A}^{k-1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{A}^{k}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{A}^{k+1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{...}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d^{k-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d^{k}}

Throughout the text we will employ the convention of using capital letters (e.g.
A,B,C
) for Hilbert complexes and calligraphic letters (e.g.
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}
) for their respective domain complexes. For the subspaces of
A^{k}
, we use Fraktur letters to denote the image
\mathfrak{B}^{k}=\mathrm{im}\;d^{k-1}\subset A^{k}
and the kernel
\mathfrak{Z}^{k}=\ker d^{k}\subset A^{k}
. Since
d^{2}=0
, we have that
\mathfrak{B}^{k}\subset\mathfrak{Z}^{k}
, and we write
\mathfrak{H}^{k}
for the cohomology
\mathfrak{Z}^{k}/\mathfrak{B}^{k}
. When it is not immediately clear which Hilbert complex the subspaces belong to, we will write
\mathfrak{B}^{k}(A)
,
\mathfrak{Z}^{k}(A)
and
\mathfrak{H}^{k}(A)
for emphasis.

Since each
A^{k}
is a Hilbert space, there is an associated inner product
\langle u,v\rangle_{A^{k}}
for
u,v\in A^{k}
which defines a norm
\|v\|_{A^{k}}=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle_{A^{k}}}
. In addition to the inner product and the norm on the full Hilbert complex, we also have a
graph inner product
and a
graph norm
associated to the domain complex
(\mathcal{A}^{\bullet},d)
:

(1.3)

\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathcal{A}^{k}}=\langle u,v\rangle_{A^{k}}+\langle du,dv\rangle_{A^{k+1}},\qquad\|v\|_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}=\|v\|_{A^{k}}^{2}+\|dv\|_{A^{k+1}}^{2}.

Since we have an inner product, we can define the adjoint of the differential operator
d
:

(1.4)

\langle du,v\rangle_{A^{k+1}}=\langle u,d^{*}v\rangle_{A^{k}},\qquad u\in A^{k},v\in A^{k+1}.

The adjoint operator
d^{*}
forms a chain complex called the
adjoint complex
which also has an associated domain complex:

(1.5)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{...}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{A}^{*}_{k-1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{A}_{k}^{*}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{A}^{*}_{k+1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{...}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d^{*}_{k}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d^{*}_{k+1}}

We denote subspaces of the adjoint complexes by
\mathfrak{B}^{*}_{k}=\mathrm{im}\;d^{*}_{k+1}
and
\mathfrak{Z}_{k}^{*}=\ker d^{*}_{k}
. By slight abuse of notation, we use the same symbol for the space of harmonic forms
\mathfrak{H}^{k}=\mathfrak{Z}^{k}\cap\mathfrak{Z}^{*}_{k}
, as it is isomorphic to the cohomology space
\mathfrak{Z}^{k}/\mathfrak{B}^{k}
.

In general, we work with an inner product that can be weighted by a bijective, symmetric, bounded linear operator
w_{k}
:

(1.6)

\langle u,v\rangle_{A^{k}_{w}}=\langle w_{k}u,v\rangle_{A^{k}}.

The weighted inner product can be used to account for physical properties such as material law, or a length scale
\epsilon
for mixed-dimensional representations (where
\epsilon
is the physical length in the collapsed dimension).

Note that we in principle have different weights at different degrees of our cochain complex. The differential operator for a weighted Hilbert complex remains the same as in the case of unit weights. The adjoint on the other hand is defined through the inner product and will therefore depend on the choice of weights
w_{k}
.

We emphasize the weight in the adjoint by writing
d^{*,w}
. The weighted adjoint can be expressed in terms of the unit weighted adjoint through the following relationship:

(1.7)

d^{*,w}_{k}=w^{-1}_{k-1}d^{*,1}_{k}w_{k},

where
d^{*,1}_{k}
is the adjoint with respect to the unweighted inner product.
The differential and the codifferential together define a generalization of the usual Laplace operator in this abstract Hilbert complex setting, called the
Hodge-Laplace operator
or
Hodge-Laplacian
:

(1.8)

\Delta_{d,w}^{k}=d^{k-1}d^{*,w}_{k}+d_{k+1}^{*,w}d^{k}.

The Hodge-Laplace operator in turn defines the problem of interest, the
Hodge-Laplace problem
: given
f\perp\ker(\Delta_{d,w}^{k})
find
v\in\mathrm{dom}(\Delta_{d,w}^{k})
such that

(1.9)

\Delta_{d,w}^{k}v=f,\qquad v\perp\mathfrak{H}^{k}.

The following orthogonal decomposition of
A^{k}
is valid for each integer
k
:

(1.10)

\displaystyle A^{k}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{im\;}d^{k-1}\oplus\ker\Delta_{d,w}^{k}\oplus\mathrm{im\;}d^{*,w}_{k+1}=\mathfrak{B}^{k}\oplus\mathfrak{H}^{k}\oplus\mathfrak{B}^{*,w}_{k}.

The sub/superscript indicating that the adjoint differential and the Hodge-Laplacian are weighted will regularly be suppressed. A similar decomposition can be achieved for non-closed Hilbert complexes by taking the closure of
\mathfrak{B}^{k}
and
\mathfrak{B}^{*,w}_{k}
. The Hodge decomposition allows for defining projection operators on
A^{k}
, for each of the three orthogonal subspaces. We write
P_{\mathfrak{B}}^{k}
,
P_{\mathfrak{H}}^{k}
and
P_{\mathfrak{B}^{*}}^{k}
for projections from
A^{k}
onto,
\mathfrak{B}^{k}
,
\mathfrak{H}^{k}
and
\mathfrak{B}^{*,w}_{k}
, respectively.

By replacing the right-hand side of
eq.

1.9
with
f-P_{\mathfrak{H}}f
, we can formulate the Hodge-Laplace problem for initial data
f\in A^{k}
, without explicitly requiring orthogonality to
\ker(\Delta_{d,w}^{k})
.

Finally, we have a Poincaré inequality for each integer
k
, which tells us that there exists a constant
C_{\mathcal{A},k}
(called the Poincaré constant) such that

(1.11)

\|v\|_{\mathcal{A}^{k}}\leq C_{\mathcal{A},k}\|dv\|,\qquad\forall v\in\mathcal{A}^{k}\cap\mathfrak{Z}^{k,\perp},

where
\mathfrak{Z}^{k,\perp}
is the orthogonal complement of
\mathfrak{Z}^{k}
in
A^{k}
. From the Hodge decomposition
eq.

1.10
, we can identify
\mathfrak{Z}^{k,\perp}
with
\mathfrak{B}^{*,w}_{k}
. The inequality
eq.

1.11
relies on
(A^{\bullet},d)
being a closed Hilbert complex.

1.2.
Double Hilbert complexes

Ultimately, we are interested in Hilbert complexes defined on a hierarchy of domains rather than a single domain. We therefore consider products of Hilbert spaces for each domain in the hierarchy, which leads to Hilbert complexes which are bigraded.

A
double cochain complex
is a lattice of bigraded objects
(A^{p,q},d_{h},d_{v})
with a horizontal and vertical differential,

(1.12a)

\displaystyle d_{h}:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{p,q}\subset A^{p,q}\to A^{p+1,q},

(1.12b)

\displaystyle d_{v}:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{p,q}\subset A^{p,q}\to A^{p,q+1},

where each row
A^{\bullet,q}
is a cochain complex, each column
A^{p,\bullet}
is a cochain complex and the two differentials anti-commute:

(1.13a)

\displaystyle d_{h}d_{h}

\displaystyle=0,

(1.13b)

\displaystyle d_{v}d_{v}

\displaystyle=0,

(1.13c)

\displaystyle d_{v}d_{h}

\displaystyle=-d_{h}d_{v}.

We assume the double complex to be in the first quadrant, i.e. both
p\geq 0
and
q\geq 0
. Moreover, we will work with double complexes that are bounded with respect to both the degree
p
and
q
, meaning that
A^{p,q}=0
if either
p>p_{max}
or
q>q_{max}
.

Given a double complex
A^{p,q}
, we define the
total complex
of
A^{p,q}
as the single-graded cochain complex

(1.14)

(\mathrm{Tot}A^{p,q})^{k}=A^{k}=\bigoplus_{k=p+q}A^{p,q},\qquad D=d_{h}+d_{v}.

Since we assume that the bigraded double complex is bounded with respect to both degree
p
and
q
, the total complex is also necessarily bounded.

In our case, we are interested in double complexes where each
A^{p,q}
is a Hilbert spaces, and both the horizontal differential
d_{h}
and vertical differential
d_{v}
are closed densely-defined unbounded linear operators. Moreover, we require
d_{v}d_{h}
and
d_{h}d_{v}
to be well-defined. Hence, we require that
\mathrm{im}(d_{v}^{p,q})\subset\mathrm{dom}(d_{h}^{p,q+1})
and
\mathrm{im}(d_{h}^{p,q})\subset\mathrm{dom}(d_{v}^{p+1,q})
.

Each of the Hilbert spaces
A^{p,q}
has an inner product, which allows us to define an inner product on the total complex
A^{k}
:

(1.15)

\langle u,v\rangle_{A^{k}}=\sum_{p+q=k}\langle u_{p,q},v_{p,q}\rangle_{A^{p,q}},\qquad u_{p,q},v_{p,q}\in A^{p,q}.

In general, the domain of
D=d_{h}+d_{v}
is the intersection
\mathrm{dom}(d_{h})\cap\mathrm{dom}(d_{v})
, which may not be dense. For two unbounded operators
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
, a sufficient condition for
\mathrm{dom}(d_{1}+d_{2})
to be dense is given in
[
10
]
. Similarly, a sufficient condition for
d_{1}+d_{2}
to be closed is presented in
[
8
, Chapter 4, Theorem 1.1]
. In practice, these conditions are satisfied for the Hilbert complexes of interest in this paper.

Under the assumption that
\mathrm{dom}(d_{h})\cap\mathrm{dom}(d_{v})
is dense and
D=d_{h}+d_{v}
is closed, the total complex
(A^{\bullet},D)
is a Hilbert complex. The domain complex
\mathcal{A}^{k}\subset A^{k}
is equipped with the graph norm
\|v\|^{2}_{\mathcal{A}^{k}}=\|v\|^{2}_{A^{k}}+\|Dv\|^{2}_{A^{k+1}}
.

1.3.
Čech-de Rham complex and simplicial de Rham complex

The Hilbert complex of interest to us is the de Rham complex with square-integrable function components. The exterior derivative is not defined everywhere on this complex, but it is defined on a dense subcomplex. We refer to this complex as the
L^{2}
de Rham complex, and write
(L^{2}\Lambda^{\bullet},d)
. The domain of the exterior derivative defines a subcomplex which we refer to as the
domain complex
, for which we write
(H\Lambda^{\bullet},d)
. For more details on the de Rham complex, see
[
3
, Chapter 6]
.

We consider a hierarchy of manifolds that are overlapping, each equipped with a de Rham complex. In the case of the Čech-de Rham complex we have overlapping manifolds, and in the case of the mixed-dimensional de Rham complex each lower-dimensional manifold of dimension
m
is contained in the boundary of some manifold with dimension
m+1
. By defining a difference operator and a jump operator on these hierarchies we get two double complexes.

Let
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
be a finite open cover of a manifold
\mathbf{\Omega}
indexed by an ordered set
\mathcal{I}
. We write
i\in\mathcal{I}^{p}
for an increasing multi-index
(i_{0},...,i_{p})
where each
i_{j}\in\mathcal{I}
, and write
U_{i}
with
i\in\mathcal{I}^{p}
as short-hand for the intersection
U_{i_{0}}\cap...\cap U_{i_{p}}
. We form a product space by taking the de Rham complex of each intersection
U_{i}
, for each increasing multi-index
i\in\mathcal{I}^{p}
:

(1.16)

C^{p,q}=C^{p}(\mathcal{U},L^{2}\Lambda^{q})=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{p}}L^{2}\Lambda^{q}(U_{i}).

The spaces
C^{p,q}
make up the bigraded Čech-de Rham complex. This product admits a differential operator in the exterior derivative by having it act on each de Rham complex. Moreover, we can define a
difference operator
by taking alternating sums restricted to intersections of degree
p+1
:

(1.17)

\displaystyle(\delta v)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{p+1}(-1)^{k+j}\;v_{i\setminus j}|_{U_{i}},

\displaystyle\forall i\in\mathcal{I}^{p+1},

where
k=p+q
and
i\setminus j
is short-hand for
i_{0},...,i_{j-1},i_{j+1},...,i_{p+1}
, i.e. the multi-index
i
with the
j
-th entry removed. We write
\mathcal{C}^{p,q}=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{p}}H\Lambda^{q}(U_{i})
for the domain complex. The difference operator and the exterior derivative together form a double complex:

(1.18)

\displaystyle\delta^{p}:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{p,q}\to\mathcal{C}^{p+1,q}

(1.19)

\displaystyle d^{q}:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{p,q}\to\mathcal{C}^{p,q+1}

We are able to form a total complex
\mathcal{C}^{k}=\bigoplus_{p+q=k}\mathcal{C}^{p,q}
with total differential
D^{k}=\bigoplus_{p+q=k}d^{q}+\delta^{p}
.

The simplicial de Rham complex shares a lot of similarities to the Čech-de Rham complex. We consider a partition of
\mathbf{\Omega}
in a collection of non-overlapping
n
-manifolds
\{\Omega_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
indexed by the same index set
\mathcal{I}
, such that
\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\overline{\Omega_{i}}=\overline{\mathbf{\Omega}}
, and such that the common boundary of two
n
-manifolds
\Omega_{i_{0}}
and
\Omega_{i_{1}}
is a new manifold
\Omega_{i}
,
i=(i_{0},i_{1})\in\mathcal{I}^{1}
. More generally, a manifold of dimension
(n-p)
is then represented by some multi-index
i\in\mathcal{I}^{p}
. Details regarding the geometrical description is found in
[
7
]
(see also
[
5
]
for a more general mixed-dimensional Hilbert complex).

The choices we make in this work ensure that the open cover
\mathcal{U}
for the Čech-de Rham complex is not just an open cover of a manifold
\mathbf{\Omega}
, it also satisfies that each
U_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
covers
\Omega_{i}\in\{\Omega_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
. More generally, any
U_{j}
covers
\Omega_{j}
, for the same multi-index
j\in\mathcal{I}^{p}
. Moreover, in
proposition

3.3
we consider a family of open covers
\{\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{M})}
indexed by a non-negative parameter
\epsilon
which denotes the maximal diameter for overlaps
U_{i}
, where
i\in\mathcal{I}^{p}
,
p\neq 0
.
For some sections, we consider a weighted inner product for the Čech-de Rham complex, where we use the parameter
\epsilon
as a weight, with exponent matching the degree of overlap (which corresponds to codimension in the mixed-dimensional setting):

(1.20)

\langle u_{p,q},v_{p,q}\rangle_{C^{p,q}_{\epsilon}}=\langle\epsilon^{-p}u_{p,q},v_{p,q}\rangle_{C^{p,q}},\qquad\langle u,v\rangle_{C^{k}_{\epsilon}}=\sum_{p+q=k}\langle u_{p,q},v_{p,q}\rangle_{C^{p,q}_{\epsilon}}.

In the same vein as for the Čech-de Rham complex in
eq.

1.16
, we define a bigraded product space for the mixed-dimensional hierarchy
\{\Omega_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
:

(1.21)

S^{p,q}=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{p}}L^{2}\Lambda^{q}(\Omega_{i}).

The
jump operator
is defined similarly to the difference operator from the Čech-de Rham complex, but in this case we take the trace of differential forms onto boundaries rather than restricting it onto a subset.

(1.22)

\displaystyle(\delta v)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{p+1}(-1)^{k+j}\;\mathrm{tr}\;v_{i\setminus j},

\displaystyle\forall i\in\mathcal{I}^{p+1},

Here, the trace is understood as a mapping of differential forms on
\Omega_{i\setminus j}
to differential forms on
\Omega_{i}
. For the domain complex, we want the composition of the two differentials to be well-defined. However, the trace of an element in
H\Lambda^{q}(\Omega)
is not necessarily in
H\Lambda^{q}(\partial\Omega)
. To account for this, we introduce recursively defined Sobolev spaces:

(1.23)

H\Lambda^{q}(\Omega_{i},\mathrm{tr})=\{v\in H\Lambda^{q}(\Omega_{i}):\mathrm{tr}_{j}\;v\in H\Lambda^{q}(\Omega_{j},\mathrm{tr})\}.

These spaces ensure that the trace of a differential form is in the domain of the exterior derivative
d
, as well as allowing for iterative application of the trace operator.

We define the simplicial de Rham complex to be the bigraded cochain complex consisting of the Sobolev spaces from
eq.

1.23
:

(1.24)

\mathcal{S}^{p,q}=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{p}}H\Lambda^{q}(\Omega_{i},\mathrm{tr}),\qquad\mathcal{S}^{k}=\bigoplus_{p+q=k}\mathcal{S}^{p,q}.

In previous work, we have constructed a bounded injective cochain map between the two aforementioned double Hilbert complexes
[
7
]
. That is, a bigraded cochain map

(1.25)

\Xi^{p,q}:\mathcal{S}^{p,q}\to\mathcal{C}^{p,q},

which induces a cochain map between the total complexes:

(1.26)

\Xi^{k}:\mathcal{S}^{k}\to\mathcal{C}^{k}.

Moreover, we prove that the cochain map is bounded from below and above (see
[
7
, Section 4.1]
): for each pair
(p,q)
satisfying
0\leq p+q\leq n
, there exists constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that

(1.27)

C_{1}\|v\|_{\mathcal{S}}\leq\|\Xi^{p,q}(v)\|_{\mathcal{C}}\leq C_{2}\|v\|_{\mathcal{S}},\qquad\forall v\in\mathcal{S}^{p,q}.

The boundedness of the cochain map means that we have an isomorphism from
\mathcal{S}
onto its image by
\Xi^{k}
, and we therefore have a subcomplex
\mathcal{E}^{\bullet}=\Xi^{\bullet}(\mathcal{S})
. The Čech-de Rham complex together with this subcomplex becomes an appropriate setting for an approximation theory between equidimensional- and mixed-dimensional models.

2.
Subcomplexes and bounded cochain projections

In this section, we will look at two of the fundamental properties in the theory of Hilbert complexes; subcomplexes and the existence of bounded cochain projections. In the theory of FEEC, the existence of bounded cochain projections onto Galerkin subspaces implies stability of the associated finite element method. Analogously, bounded cochain projections from the Čech-de Rham complex
\mathcal{C}^{\bullet}
to the subcomplex
\mathcal{E}^{\bullet}
will provide an approximation theory between solutions of equidimensional problems and solutions of mixed-dimensional problems.

We will review the definitions of bounded cochain projections, show sufficient conditions for existence of bounded cochain projections and review the error estimates that are derived from Hilbert subcomplexes.

2.1.
Bounded cochain projections

Let
(A^{\bullet},d)
be a Hilbert complex,
(\mathcal{A}^{\bullet},d)
be its associated domain complex and suppose that
(\mathcal{B}^{\bullet},d|_{\mathcal{B}})
is a subcomplex of
(\mathcal{A}^{\bullet},d)
. That is, for each
k
we have
\mathcal{B}^{k}\subset\mathcal{A}^{k}
and
d\mathcal{B}^{k}\subset\mathcal{B}^{k+1}
.

The differential operator on
\mathcal{B}
is just the restriction
d|_{\mathcal{B}}
, so we can simply write
d
for both complexes. Similarly, the inner product on
\mathcal{B}^{k}
is then just restriction of the inner product on
\mathcal{A}^{k}
, so we do not distinguish between
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{B}}
. Note that the adjoint
d^{*}_{\mathcal{B}}
on the subcomplex is not generally equal to the restriction
d^{*}|_{\mathcal{B}}
.

A
bounded cochain projection
is a collection of linear maps
\pi^{k}:\mathcal{A}^{k}\to\mathcal{B}^{k}
which restricts to the identity on
\mathcal{B}^{k}
, commutes with the differential operators (meaning that
\pi^{k+1}\circ d=d\circ\pi^{k}
) and satisfies the following inequality: there exists a positive constant
\kappa_{k}
such that

(2.1)

\|\pi^{k}v\|_{\mathcal{B}^{k}}\leq\kappa_{k}\|v\|_{\mathcal{A}^{k}},\qquad\forall v\in\mathcal{A}^{k}.

Here, the inequality can be in either the full norm (
\|\cdot\|_{A^{k}}
) or the graph norm (
eq.

1.3
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Boundedness in the full norm
\|\cdot\|_{A^{k}}
implies boundedness in the graph norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{A}^{k}}
.

Proof.

We assume that for every
k
,

(2.2)

\|\pi^{k}v\|\leq\kappa_{k}\|v\|,\qquad\forall v\in\mathcal{A}^{k}.

Using the commutative property of the cochain projection, we have

(2.3)

\|\pi^{k}v\|^{2}_{\mathcal{A}^{k}}=\|\pi^{k}v\|^{2}+\|d\pi^{k}v\|^{2}=\|\pi^{k}v\|^{2}+\|\pi^{k+1}dv\|^{2}.

Using the boundedness assumption, we get

(2.4)

\|\pi^{k}v\|^{2}+\|\pi^{k+1}dv\|^{2}\leq\kappa^{2}_{k}\|v\|^{2}+\kappa^{2}_{k+1}\|dv\|^{2}\leq\max\{\kappa_{k}^{2},\kappa_{k+1}^{2}\}\|v\|^{2}_{\mathcal{A}^{k}}.

Hence we have boundedness in the graph norm.
∎

A bounded cochain projection gives rise to a Poincaré inequality between the two complexes, meaning that there exists a constant
c_{\mathcal{B},k}
such that

(2.5)

\|v\|_{\mathcal{A}^{k}}\leq c_{\mathcal{B},k}\|\pi^{k}\|\|dv\|,\qquad v\in\mathcal{B}^{k}\cap\mathfrak{Z}^{k,\perp},

where
\|\pi^{k}\|
is the operator norm of the bounded cochain projection
\pi^{k}
.

2.2.
Different formulations of the Hodge-Laplace problem

Herein we briefly summarize the many different formulations of the Hodge-Laplace problem: the primal strong formulation, the primal weak formulation and the mixed weak formulation. In addition, we may also formulate the Hodge-Laplacian as a system of first order equations, which is later used for deriving a posteriori error estimates. We also consider the mixed weak formulation of the Hodge-Laplace problem on the subcomplex
\mathcal{B}^{\bullet}\subset\mathcal{A}^{\bullet}
.

We refer to
eq.

1.9
as the
primal strong formulation
of the Hodge-Laplace problem. Rather than insisting that
f\perp\ker(\Delta_{d,w}^{k})
, we can write
eq.

1.9
as

(2.6)

\Delta_{d,w}^{k}v=f-P_{\mathfrak{H}}f,\qquad v\perp\mathfrak{H}^{k}.

where
P_{\mathfrak{H}}
denotes the projection associated with the Hodge decomposition in
eq.

1.10
.

In certain applications, the initial data
f
is sometimes entirely in the space
\mathfrak{B}^{k}
. In this case, the Hodge-Laplace problem is formulated as follows: given
f\in\mathfrak{B}^{k}
, find
v\in\mathfrak{B}^{k}
such that

(2.7)

D^{k-1}D^{*}_{k}v=f,\qquad v\perp\mathfrak{H}^{k}.

Likewise, given
g\in\mathfrak{B}^{*}_{k}
, the Hodge-Laplace problem becomes to find
v\in\mathfrak{B}^{*}_{k}
such that
D^{*}_{k+1}D^{k}v=g
. We refer to these two special cases as
\mathfrak{B}
-problems and
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
-problems, respectively. Note that the Hodge-Laplace problems for
k=n
and
k=0
are
\mathfrak{B}
-problems and
\mathfrak{B}^{*}-
problems, respectively.

To write the Hodge-Laplacian in a weak formulation, we integrate with respect to a test-differential form
v^{\prime}
which vanishes on the boundary of the domain:

(2.8)

\langle(d^{k-1}d^{*,w}_{k}+d_{k+1}^{*,w}d^{k})v,v^{\prime}\rangle=\langle f-P_{\mathfrak{H}}f,v^{\prime}\rangle,\qquad v\perp\mathfrak{H}^{k}.

Since we assume
v^{\prime}
to vanish on the boundary, we can use linearity of the inner product and integration by parts to write the equation in the
primal weak formulation
:

(2.9)

\langle dv,dv^{\prime}\rangle+\langle d^{*}v,d^{*}v^{\prime}\rangle=\langle f-P_{\mathfrak{H}}f,v^{\prime}\rangle,\qquad v\perp\mathfrak{H}^{k}.

We introduce the following variables:
u=d^{*}v
and
q=P_{\mathfrak{H}}f
, and obtain the
mixed weak formulation
of the Hodge-Laplace problem: find
(u,v,q)\in\mathcal{A}^{k-1}\times\mathcal{A}^{k}\times\mathfrak{H}^{k}
such that the following set of equations hold:

(2.10a)

\displaystyle\langle u,u^{\prime}\rangle-\langle v,du^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{k-1},

(2.10b)

\displaystyle\langle du,v^{\prime}\rangle+\langle dv,dv^{\prime}\rangle+\langle q,v^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f,v^{\prime}\rangle,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{k},

(2.10c)

\displaystyle\langle v,q^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall q^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{H}^{k}(\mathcal{A}).

The three different formulations of the Hodge-Laplace problem
eq.

2.6
,
eq.

2.9
and
eq.

2.10
are all equivalent and well-posed
[
3
, Theorem 4.7 and 4.8]
.

In addition to the three main formulations of the Hodge-Laplace problem, we can also formulate a subcomplex mixed formulation of the Hodge-Laplace problem. We write
\mathfrak{Z}^{k}(\mathcal{B})
and
\mathfrak{B}^{k}(\mathcal{B})
for the subspace kernel and subcomplex image, respectively. Note that
\mathfrak{Z}^{k}(\mathcal{B})\subset\mathfrak{Z}^{k}(\mathcal{A})
and likewise
\mathfrak{B}^{k}(\mathcal{B})\subset\mathfrak{B}^{k}(\mathcal{A})
. We define the subspaces of harmonic forms on
\mathcal{B}^{\bullet}
by

(2.11)

\mathfrak{H}^{k}(\mathcal{B})=\{v\in\mathfrak{Z}^{k}(\mathcal{B}):v\perp\mathfrak{B}^{k}(\mathcal{B})\}.

In general,
\mathfrak{H}^{k}(\mathcal{B})\not\subset\mathfrak{H}^{k}(\mathcal{A})
. The mixed formulation of the subcomplex Hodge-Laplace problem is as follows: given
f\in A^{k}
, find
(u_{\mathcal{B}},v_{\mathcal{B}},q_{\mathcal{B}})\in\mathcal{B}^{k-1}\times\mathcal{B}^{k}\times\mathfrak{H}^{k}(\mathcal{B})
such that

(2.12a)

\displaystyle\langle u_{\mathcal{B}},u^{\prime}\rangle-\langle v_{\mathcal{B}},du^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}^{k-1},

(2.12b)

\displaystyle\langle du_{\mathcal{B}},v^{\prime}\rangle+\langle dv_{\mathcal{B}},dv^{\prime}\rangle+\langle q_{\mathcal{B}},v^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f,v^{\prime}\rangle,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}^{k},

(2.12c)

\displaystyle\langle v_{\mathcal{B}},q^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall q^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{H}^{k}(\mathcal{B}).

2.3.
Existence of bounded cochain projections

In this section we will prove that there exists bounded cochain projections from the Čech-de Rham complex to the subcomplexes given by
eq.

1.26
.

We have the following two assumptions for the subcomplex
(\mathcal{B},d)
: there exists positive constants
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
such that

(2.13a)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{1}\|dv\|_{\mathcal{A}},\qquad

\displaystyle v\in\mathfrak{Z}^{k,\perp}(\mathcal{B}),

(2.13b)

\displaystyle\|q\|_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{2}\|P_{\mathfrak{H}(\mathcal{A})}q\|_{\mathcal{A}},\qquad

\displaystyle q\in\mathfrak{H}^{k}(\mathcal{B}).

Under the assumptions from
eq.

2.13a
and
eq.

2.13b
, the existence of bounded cochain projections is guaranteed, as shown in
[
2
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose the assumptions in
eq.

2.13
hold. Then there exists bounded cochain projections
\pi^{k}:\mathcal{A}^{k}\to\mathcal{B}^{k}
, where
\|\pi^{k}\|
is bounded by the constants
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
:

(2.14)

\|\pi^{k}_{\mathcal{B}}v\|_{\mathcal{A}}\leq(1+\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})\|v\|_{\mathcal{A}},\qquad\forall v\in\mathcal{A}^{k}.

Proof.

The first assumption implies that there exists an operator

(2.15)

Q_{\mathcal{B}}:\mathcal{A}^{k}\to\mathfrak{Z}^{k,\perp}(\mathcal{B}),\qquad dQ_{\mathcal{B}}v=P_{\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{B})}dv.

It follows from
eq.

2.13a
that
Q_{\mathcal{B}}
is bounded:

(2.16)

\|Q_{\mathcal{B}}v\|_{\mathcal{A}}\leq\kappa_{1}\|dQ_{\mathcal{B}}v\|=\kappa_{1}\|P_{\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{B})}dv\|\leq\kappa_{1}\|v\|_{\mathcal{A}}.

Next, we consider the orthogonal projection
P_{\mathfrak{H}}:\mathcal{A}^{k}\to\mathfrak{H}^{k}
. When restricting the domain of the projection to
\mathfrak{H}(\mathcal{B})
, it defines a map
P_{\mathfrak{H}}|_{\mathfrak{H}(\mathcal{B})}:\mathfrak{H}^{k}(\mathcal{B})\to\mathfrak{H}^{k}
. We denote the inverse of this map by
R_{\mathcal{B}}:\mathfrak{H}\to\mathfrak{H}(\mathcal{B})
, which exists and is bounded because of
eq.

2.13b
.

We now consider the following map:

(2.17)

\pi_{\mathcal{B}}^{k}=P_{\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{B})}+R_{\mathcal{B}}P_{\mathfrak{H}}(I-Q_{\mathcal{B}})+Q_{\mathcal{B}}.

We claim that
eq.

2.17
defines a bounded cochain projection. For this to be true, we need to verify the following three properties for
eq.

2.17
:

(1)

\pi_{\mathcal{B}}^{k}
is a cochain map

(2)

\pi_{\mathcal{B}}^{k}
is bounded

(3)

\pi_{\mathcal{B}}^{k}
is a projection

Firstly, for
\pi^{k}_{\mathcal{B}}
to be a cochain map it satisfies
d\pi^{k}_{\mathcal{B}}v=\pi^{k+1}_{\mathcal{B}}dv
. Using the Hodge decomposition, we can decompose
v=du+q+d^{*}w
.

On one hand,

(2.18)

d\pi_{\mathcal{B}}^{k+1}v=dP_{\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{B}}}v+d(R_{\mathcal{B}}P_{\mathfrak{H}}(I-Q_{\mathcal{B}})v)+dQ_{\mathcal{B}}v.

The first two terms are zero, since
P_{\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{B}}}v=du_{\mathcal{B}}
and thus
dP_{\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{B}}}v=ddu_{\mathcal{B}}=0
, and similarly
dq_{\mathcal{B}}=0
.

On the other hand,

(2.19)

\pi_{\mathcal{B}}^{k}dv=P_{\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{B}}}dv+(R_{\mathcal{B}}P_{\mathfrak{H}}(I-Q_{\mathcal{B}})dv)+Q_{\mathcal{B}}dv.

In this expression, the second term and the third term are zero. The second term is zero since
P_{\mathfrak{H}}dv=0
, while the last term is zero since
Q_{\mathcal{B}}:\mathcal{A}^{k}\to\mathfrak{Z}^{k,\perp}(\mathcal{B})
, but
dv\in\mathfrak{B}^{k}\subset\mathfrak{Z}^{k}
, hence
Q_{h}dv=0
. By definition of
Q_{\mathcal{B}}
,
dQ_{\mathcal{B}}v=P_{\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{B}}}dv
. The only non-zero terms coincide, and thus we conclude that
\pi_{\mathcal{B}}^{k+1}dv=d\pi^{k}_{\mathcal{B}}v
.

Secondly,
\pi^{k}_{\mathcal{B}}
is bounded because both
Q_{\mathcal{B}}
and
R_{\mathcal{B}}
are bounded, and each orthogonal projection
P_{K}
is bounded with bound 1.

(2.20a)

\displaystyle\|\pi_{\mathcal{B}}^{k}v\|_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=\|(P_{\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{B})}+R_{\mathcal{B}}P_{\mathfrak{H}}(I-Q_{\mathcal{B}})+Q_{\mathcal{B}})v\|_{\mathcal{A}}

(2.20b)

\displaystyle\leq\|P_{\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{B})}v\|_{\mathcal{A}}+\|R_{\mathcal{B}}P_{\mathfrak{H}}(I-Q_{\mathcal{B}})v\|_{\mathcal{A}}+\|Q_{\mathcal{B}}v\|_{\mathcal{A}}

(2.20c)

\displaystyle\leq\|v\|_{\mathcal{A}}+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}\|v\|_{\mathcal{A}}+\kappa_{1}\|v\|_{\mathcal{A}}=(1+\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})\|v\|_{\mathcal{A}}.

If
v_{\mathcal{B}}=du_{\mathcal{B}}+q_{\mathcal{B}}+d^{*}_{\mathcal{B}}w_{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}
, then
Q_{\mathcal{B}}v_{\mathcal{B}}=P_{\mathfrak{B}^{*}(\mathcal{B})}v_{\mathcal{B}}
and
R_{\mathcal{B}}P_{\mathfrak{H}}v_{\mathcal{B}}=P_{\mathfrak{H}(\mathcal{B})}v_{\mathcal{B}}
. From this we see that the map
\pi_{\mathcal{B}}^{k}
is invariant on
v_{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathcal{B}^{k}
:

(2.21)

\pi_{\mathcal{B}}^{k}v_{\mathcal{B}}=du_{\mathcal{B}}+q_{\mathcal{B}}+d^{*}_{\mathcal{B}}w_{\mathcal{B}}=v_{\mathcal{B}}.

This invariance proves the final claim that
\pi^{k}
is a projection. We can therefore conclude that
\pi^{k}
as defined in
eq.

2.17
is a bounded cochain projection.
∎

Theorem

2.2
guarantees the existence of bounded cochain projections, given the assumptions
eq.

2.13a
and
eq.

2.13b
.

3.
Error estimates

Now that we have established both the subcomplex property and the existence of bounded cochain projections, we proceed by formulating
a priori
error estimates and
a posteriori
error estimates between solutions in the full Čech-de Rham complex and the subspace which is the embedded mixed-dimensional de Rham complex. These error estimates compare the solution to Hodge-Laplace problems on mixed-dimensional geometries to the solution of an equivalent Hodge-Laplace problem on an equidimensional (overlapping) geometry.

3.1.
Poincaré constants for mixed-dimensional de Rham complex

The error estimates we produce are dependent on Poincaré constants of the different Hilbert complexes. In this section we will review and discuss the different Poincaré constants that arise in this setting.

The classical Poincaré inequality is usually stated in terms of gradient, and allows us to bound a function by its derivative:

(3.1)

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq c_{P}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\qquad\forall u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

The classical Poincaré inequality can be generalized in the following way: any closed Hilbert complex
(\mathcal{A}^{\bullet},d)
permits a Poincaré-type inequality for each
k
;

(3.2)

\|u\|\leq c_{\mathcal{A},k}\|du\|,\qquad u\in\mathcal{A}^{k}\cap\mathfrak{Z}^{k,\perp}.

Under the assumption that the Hilbert complex is closed, the subspace
\mathfrak{B}^{k}
is a Hilbert space for each
k
, and
d^{k}
defines a bounded linear isomorphism
d^{k}:\mathfrak{Z}^{k,\perp}\to\mathfrak{B}^{k}
, which admits a bounded inverse. The Poincaré constant
c_{\mathcal{A},k}
is the bound for the inverse of
d^{k}
.

The Poincaré constant can be described by the following infimum:

(3.3)

\frac{1}{c_{\mathcal{A},k}}=\inf_{u\perp\ker D^{k}}\frac{\|D^{k}u\|}{\|u\|}.

We can also consider the Poincaré constant we get when considering a subspace (or more generally a subcomplex)
V^{k}\subset\mathcal{A}^{k}
:

(3.4)

\frac{1}{c_{V,k}}=\inf_{u\in V^{k}\perp\ker D^{k}}\frac{\|D^{k}u\|}{\|u\|}.

A classical result
[
15
]
shows that the Poincaré constant of the gradient operator is bounded by
\pi^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)
for any convex domain
\Omega
. The generalization of this result tells us that all the Poincaré constants in the de Rham complex have the bound
c_{k}\leq\pi^{-1}\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)

[
13
]
.

We also make use of the following result which equates the Poincaré constant of a differential operator
D^{k}
with the Poincaré constant associated with its adjoint
D^{*}_{k+1}
:

Proposition 3.1
.

(3.5)

\frac{1}{c_{\mathcal{A},k}}=\inf_{u\perp\ker D^{k}}\frac{\|D^{k}u\|}{\|u\|}=\frac{1}{c_{\mathcal{A},k+1}^{*}}=\inf_{v\perp\ker D^{*}}\frac{\|D^{*}_{k+1}u\|}{\|u\|}.

Proof.

See
[
14
, Lemma 2.2]
.
∎

Another type of Poincaré inequality exists when dealing with functions that are not necessarily vanishing on the boundary (and more generally, differential forms that are not orthogonal to the kernel
\mathfrak{Z}^{k}
).

We write
u_{\Omega}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}u
for the average value of
u\in H\Lambda^{k}(\Omega)
. Then, we have a Poincaré inequality for any
u\in H\Lambda^{k}(\Omega)
:

(3.6)

\|u-u_{\Omega}\|\leq c_{k,\Omega}\|d^{k}u\|.

The Čech-de Rham complex, the simplicial de Rham complex and the embedded simplicial de Rham complex all permit a global Poincaré-type inequality as
eq.

3.2
. As shown earlier, the Poincaré constant for the subcomplex
\mathcal{E}^{\bullet}
, can be expressed in terms of the Poincaré constant for the simplicial de Rham complex
\mathcal{S}^{\bullet}
:

(3.7)

c_{\mathcal{E},k}=\frac{C_{2}}{C_{1}}c_{\mathcal{S},k},

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are bounds on the embedding map
eq.

1.26
. Since the Hilbert complexes we consider are product spaces of the de Rham complex, there are also local Poincaré-type inequalities for each
\Omega_{i}
and
U_{i}
.

3.2.
A priori error estimates

Now that we have established the embedded simplicial de Rham complex
(\mathcal{E}^{\bullet},D)
as a subcomplex of the Čech-de Rham complex through the embedding
\Xi^{\bullet}:\mathcal{S}^{\bullet}\to\mathcal{C}^{\bullet}
, and the existence of bounded cochain projections from the full Čech-de Rham complex onto the subcomplex, we can establish general a priori error estimates as presented in
[
3
]
.

Theorem 3.2

(Error estimates for Hodge-Laplace problems)
.

Let
(u,v,q)\in\mathcal{C}^{k-1}\times\mathcal{C}^{k}\times\mathfrak{H}^{k}(\mathcal{C})
solve the mixed weak formulation of the Hodge-Laplace problem on
(\mathcal{C}^{\bullet},D)
and let
(u_{\mathcal{E}},v_{\mathcal{E}},q_{\mathcal{E}})\in\mathcal{E}^{k-1}\times\mathcal{E}^{k}\times\mathfrak{H}^{k}(\mathcal{E})
solve the mixed weak formulation of the Hodge-Laplace problem on
(\mathcal{E}^{\bullet},D_{\mathcal{E}})
. Then

(3.8a)

\displaystyle\|u-u_{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle+\|v-v_{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\mathcal{C}}+\|q-q_{\mathcal{E}}\|\leq

(3.8b)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}\Big(\inf_{\hat{u}\in\mathcal{E}^{k-1}}\|u-\hat{u}\|_{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle+\inf_{\hat{v}\in\mathcal{E}^{k}}\|v-\hat{v}\|_{\mathcal{C}}+\inf_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{E}^{k}}\|q-\hat{q}\|_{\mathcal{C}}+M_{k}\inf_{\hat{v}\in\mathcal{E}^{k}}\|P_{\mathfrak{B}}^{k}v-\hat{v}\|_{\mathcal{C}}\Big),

where
\mathfrak{c}
depends on the Poincaré constant
c_{\mathcal{C},k}
and
\|\pi^{k}\|
(where
\pi^{k}:\mathcal{C}^{k}\to\mathcal{E}^{k}
denotes the bounded cochain projection), while the other constant is given by

(3.9)

M_{k}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hat{q}\in\mathfrak{H}^{k}(\mathcal{C}),\\
\|\hat{q}\|=1\end{subarray}}\|(\mathrm{id}-\pi^{k})\hat{q}\|.

We refer to
[
3
, Section 5.2]
for the proof, where the theorem is proven for an arbitrary closed Hilbert complex
(\mathcal{A}^{\bullet},d)
, a subcomplex
\mathcal{B}^{\bullet}\subset\mathcal{A}^{\bullet}
, and a bounded cochain projection
\pi^{\bullet}:\mathcal{A}^{\bullet}\to\mathcal{B}^{\bullet}
. One can obtain improved error estimates if we have a cochain projection that is bounded in
L^{2}
norm instead of the graph norm. This is shown in
[
1
]
.

For a simply connected domain, the cohomology
\mathfrak{H}^{k}(\mathcal{C})
is zero for
k>0
, and thus the constants
M_{k}
, as well as the terms
\|q-q_{\mathcal{E}}\|
and
\inf_{\hat{q}\in\mathcal{E}^{k}}\|q-\hat{q}\|_{\mathcal{A}}
vanish. Thus,
eq.

3.8
for a
k\neq 0
Hodge-Laplacian on a simply-connected domain takes a much simpler expression:

(3.10)

\|u-u_{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\mathcal{C}}+\|v-v_{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\mathcal{C}}\leq c\Big(\inf_{\hat{u}\in\mathcal{E}^{k-1}}\|u-\hat{u}\|_{\mathcal{C}}+\inf_{\hat{v}\in\mathcal{E}^{k}}\|v-\hat{v}\|_{\mathcal{C}}\Big).

In finite element exterior calculus, one obtains an approximation property based on the mesh size
h
for the triangulation of the domain. In particular, one aims to show that in the limit as
h\to 0
, the infimum tends towards zero:
\inf\limits_{u_{h}\in V_{h}}\|u-u_{h}\|_{V}\to 0
.

We want to express a similar type of approximation property, but instead of considering the limit as the mesh size approaches zero, we consider a family of open covers
\{\mathcal{U}^{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{M})}
and take the limit as the maximal diameter of overlaps goes towards zero, as illustrated in
fig.

1
.

Figure 1
.
Three left domains: A simple open cover consisting of two open sets, with diameter in the transversal direction equal to a parameter
\epsilon
. Rightmost domain: When
\epsilon\to 0
, we get a mixed-dimensional geometry.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}
be a family of Čech cover associated to a simplicial geometry, where the subscript
\epsilon
denotes the maximal diameter in the transversal direction.

Given
u\in\mathcal{C}^{k}
,

(3.11)

\inf\limits_{u_{\mathcal{E}}\in\mathcal{E}^{k}}\|u-u_{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\mathcal{C}}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

Proof.

We consider general case when the inner products for the Čech-de Rham complex are weighted by the parameter
\epsilon
, as in
eq.

1.20
. Unweighted inner products for the Čech-de Rham complex can be considered a special case, and the result for unweighted inner products follows immediately.

For the
\epsilon
-weighted inner products, the expression in
eq.

3.11
becomes the following:

(3.12)

\|u-u_{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\mathcal{C}}=\sum_{p+q=k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{p}}\epsilon^{-p/2}\|u_{i}-u_{\mathcal{E},i}\|_{D}.

The recursive norm for a differential form of degree
(p,q)
will give contributions to subdomains corresponding to
(p+1,q)
. Thus, some domains will have contributions multiple (but finitely many) times. We will show the desired result for a fixed
(p,q)
, and ignore the recursive norm.

For
p=0
, we have unit weights (
\epsilon^{-0}=1
), and since the subcomplex imposes no restriction on the differential forms on the non-overlapping parts of
U_{i}
, the difference

u_{i}-u_{\mathcal{E},i}
vanishes on
\tilde{U}_{i}
. Moreover, the difference on the overlapping parts of
U_{i}
goes towards zero in the limit, since the measure of the overlaps tends towards zero with order
\epsilon^{p/2}
.

For
p\neq 0
, we have a weight
\epsilon^{-p/2}
, and we are considering domains
U_{i}
,
i\in\mathcal{I}^{p}
with
p
transversal directions of length
\epsilon
. We can set
u_{\mathcal{E}}
to be equal to the mean value of
u
:

(3.13)

u_{\mathcal{E},i}=\frac{1}{|U_{i}|}\int_{U_{i}}u_{i}.

Using the Poincaré inequality,

(3.14)

\epsilon^{-p/2}\|u_{i}-u_{\mathcal{E},i}\|_{D}\leq\epsilon^{-p/2}c_{\mathcal{A},k}\|du_{i}\|.

Since the difference
u_{i}-u_{\mathcal{E},i}
is bounded in the tangential direction, the Poincaré constant is proportional to
\epsilon
(and
c_{\mathcal{A},k}\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}\epsilon}{\pi}
). Each
u_{i}\in H\Lambda^{q}(U_{i})
, hence the terms
\|du_{i}\|
are finite, and of order
\epsilon^{p/2}
.

The Poincaré constant together with the term
\|du_{i}\|
has order
\epsilon^{p/2+1}
, and accounting for the weight
\epsilon^{-p/2}
, we still have a right-hand side in
eq.

3.14
that is of order
\epsilon
. We therefore conclude that

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\inf\limits_{u_{\mathcal{E}}\in\mathcal{E}^{k}}\|u-u_{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\mathcal{C}}=0
with order
\epsilon
.
∎

A posteriori error estimates

In

\displaystyle D^{k}u

\displaystyle=f,

(3.15b)

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}^{k}
be an exact solution to the Hodge-Laplace
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
-problem on the Čech-de Rham complex, and let
(u_{\mathcal{E}},v_{\mathcal{E}})
be an approximation of
(u,v)
derived from the Hodge-Laplace
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
-problem on
\mathcal{S}^{k}
.

The Hodge-Laplace
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
-problem can be identified with a first order system

(3.20a)

\displaystyle D^{k}u=v,\qquad D^{k+1}v=0,

(3.20b)

\displaystyle D^{*}_{k}u=0,\qquad D_{k+1}^{*}v=f,

(3.20c)

\displaystyle\pi^{k}_{\mathfrak{H}}u=0,\qquad\pi^{k+1}_{\mathfrak{H}}v=0.

Then the errors
e^{u}=u-u_{\mathcal{E}}
and
e^{v}=v-v_{\mathcal{E}}
decompose according to the Hodge decomposition:

(3.21)

\displaystyle e^{u}

\displaystyle=e^{u}_{\mathfrak{B}}+e^{u}_{\mathfrak{H}}+e^{u}_{\mathfrak{B}^{*}}\in\mathfrak{B}^{k}\oplus\mathfrak{H}^{k}\oplus\mathfrak{B}^{*}_{k},

(3.22)

\displaystyle\|e^{u}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}

\displaystyle=\|e^{u}_{\mathfrak{B}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}+\|e^{u}_{\mathfrak{H}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}+\|e^{u}_{\mathfrak{B}^{*}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k}},

and

(3.23)

\displaystyle e^{v}

\displaystyle=e^{v}_{\mathfrak{B}}+e^{v}_{\mathfrak{H}}+e^{v}_{\mathfrak{B}^{*}}\in\mathfrak{B}^{k+1}\oplus\mathfrak{H}^{k+1}\oplus\mathfrak{B}^{*}_{k+1},

(3.24)

\displaystyle\|e^{v}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\|e^{v}_{\mathfrak{B}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k+1}}+\|e^{v}_{\mathfrak{H}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k+1}}+\|e^{v}_{\mathfrak{B}^{*}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k+1}},

The following a posteriori error estimates for the respective error parts hold:

(1)

(3.25)

\|e^{u}_{\mathfrak{B}^{*}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\leq\left(c_{k}^{2}\|D^{*}_{k+1}D^{k}u_{\mathcal{E}}-f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\right)^{2}.

(2)

(3.26)

\|e^{u}_{\mathfrak{H}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}=\min_{\varphi\in\mathrm{dom}(D^{k-1})}\min_{\phi\in\mathrm{dom}(D^{*}_{k+1})}\|D^{k-1}\varphi+D^{*}_{k+1}\phi-u_{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}^{2}.

(3)

(3.27)

\|e^{v}_{\mathfrak{B}}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k+1}}^{2}\leq\left(c_{k}\|D^{*}_{k+1}v_{\mathcal{E}}-f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\right)^{2}.

For the
\mathfrak{B}^{*}
-problem the error

since

.
Moreover, for any Hodge-Laplace problem,

and

are zero because

, hence orthogonal to both
\mathfrak{B}^{*}_{k+1}
and
\mathfrak{H}^{k+1}

The error in harmonic form can be estimated by the gap

(3.28)

\mathrm{gap}(\mathfrak{H}^{k}(A),\mathfrak{H}^{k}(B))\leq M_{k},

where

is the constant which appears in

Remark 3.5
.

Note that the expressions depend on the Poincaré constant
c_{k}
. Since the Poincaré constant is derived as the infimum over the function space, if we restrict to a smaller subcomplex
V^{k}\subset A^{k}
, we have that
\frac{1}{c_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{c_{V,k}}
, and therefore
c_{V,k}\leq c_{k}
. We observe that a smaller subspace (or more generally subcomplex) will yield a smaller (hence better) Poincaré constant, and in turn better error estimates. Such subspaces are discussed in
[
16

; where the authors consider three cases: no conservation (full complex), subdomain mass conservation (which allows for local Poincaré constants for domains
U_{i}
) and exact mass conservation (where any constant is permissible).

4.1.
One-dimensional elastically joined rods

In this section we consider two elastic rods that are joined together. We simplify the example considerably by treating the rods as one-dimensional. We consider two different geometrical descriptions for the mathematical models: an equidimensional model where the overlap of the two rods have non-zero length, and the mixed-dimensional model wherein the overlap is represented by a zero-dimensional interface. Such models for one-dimensional elasticity appear in literature, see e.g.

4.2.
A posteriori error estimates for the example

Now that we have both the solution to the Hodge-Laplace problem on the Čech-de Rham complex and the embedded solution of the simplicial de Rham- Hodge-Laplacian, we can compare the two solutions by relying on the theory presented in
section

3.3
.

The Poincaré constant can be expressed in terms of the Rayleigh quotient,

(4.16)

\inf_{u\perp\ker D}R(u)=\frac{1}{C_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}}.

where the Rayleigh quotient of
D
is defined by

(4.17)

R_{D}(u)=\frac{\|Du\|_{\mathcal{C}^{1}}^{2}}{\|u\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}^{2}}=\frac{\|u_{0}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{0,1}}+\|u_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0,1}}^{2}+\|u_{1}-u_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{1,0}}}{\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{0,0}}+\|u_{1}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0,0}}^{2}}.

We define the Rayleigh quotient associated to the independent operators
d
and
\delta
:

(4.18a)

\displaystyle R_{d}(u)=\frac{\|du\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0,1}}^{2}}{\|u\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0,0}}^{2}}=\frac{\|u_{0}^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0,1}}^{2}+\|u_{1}^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0,1}}^{2}}{\|u_{0}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0,0}}^{2}+\|u_{1}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{0,0}}},

(4.18b)

\displaystyle R_{\delta}(u)=\frac{\|\delta u\|_{\mathcal{C}^{1,0}}^{2}}{\|u\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0,0}}^{2}}=\frac{\|u_{1}-u_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{1,0}}}{\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{0,0}}+\|u_{1}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{0,0}}}.

The infimum of the first Rayleigh quotient is realized by functions with zero global mean value, while the infimum of the second Rayleigh quotient is realized by constant pairs
(c,-c)
. Standard Poincaré inequality tells us that
1/C^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{\mathrm{diam}(U)^{2}}
. Since
\ker D
are the global constant functions, the infimum is realized by the global diameter, i.e. the diameter of the union
U_{0}\cup U_{1}
:

(4.19)

\inf_{u\perp\ker D}\frac{\|du\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0,1}}^{2}}{\|u\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0,0}}^{2}}=\frac{w\pi^{2}}{2^{2}}.

On the other hand, the constant pair
(c,-c)
gives the following quotient:

(4.20)

\frac{\|\delta(c,-c)\|_{\mathcal{C}^{1,0}}^{2}}{\|(c,-c)\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0,0}}^{2}}=\frac{\frac{w_{0,1}}{\epsilon}(2c)^{2}|U_{0,1}|}{|U_{0}|^{2}c^{2}+|U_{1}|^{2}c^{2}}=\frac{8c^{2}}{2(1+\epsilon)c^{2}}=\frac{4w_{0,1}}{(1+\epsilon)}.

An upper bound for the Poincaré constant can be determined based on which expression dominates:
\frac{1}{C^{2}}\leq\min\{R_{d},R_{\delta}\}
. In general, the relative size of the quotients
R_{d}
and
R_{\delta}
depends on the values of
w
and
w_{0,1}
. If
w/w_{0,1}\approx 1
, then the Rayleigh quotient
R_{d}
will be greater, and we can therefore estimate the Poincaré constant by

(4.21)

C_{\mathcal{A}}\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{w}\pi}.

The error estimates for
e^{u}
and
e^{\sigma}
decompose in accordance with the Hodge decomposition:

(4.22a)

\displaystyle u-u_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=e^{u}=e^{u}_{\mathfrak{B}}+e^{u}_{\mathfrak{B}^{*}}+e^{u}_{\mathfrak{H}},

(4.22b)

\displaystyle\|e^{u}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}

\displaystyle=\|e^{u}_{\mathfrak{B}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}+\|e^{u}_{\mathfrak{B}^{*}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}+\|e^{u}_{\mathfrak{H}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k}},

(4.22c)

\displaystyle\sigma-\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=e^{\sigma}=e^{\sigma}_{\mathfrak{B}}+e^{\sigma}_{\mathfrak{B}^{*}}+e^{\sigma}_{\mathfrak{H}},

(4.22d)

\displaystyle\|e^{\sigma}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\|e^{\sigma}_{\mathfrak{B}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k+1}}+\|e^{\sigma}_{\mathfrak{B}^{*}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k+1}}+\|e^{\sigma}_{\mathfrak{H}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k+1}}.

Since
u
and
u_{\mathcal{E}}
are in
\mathcal{A}^{0}
, then
e^{u}_{\mathfrak{B}}=0
. Similarly,
\sigma
and
\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}
are at the end of the complex, so
\|e^{\sigma}_{\mathfrak{B}^{*}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k+1}}
and
\|e^{\sigma}_{\mathfrak{H}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{C}^{k+1}}
are both zero.

The error in the harmonic part of
u
is zero. In the Čech-de Rham Hodge-Laplacian, we seek a solution which satisfies
u\perp\mathfrak{H}^{0}(\mathcal{C})
, hence
u_{\mathfrak{H}}=0
.
u_{\mathcal{S}}
also satisfies
u_{\mathcal{S}}\perp\mathfrak{H}^{0}(\mathcal{S})
and since
u_{\mathcal{E},0}|_{U_{0,1}}=-u_{\mathcal{E},1}|_{U_{0,1}}
, it follows that
u_{\mathcal{E}}\perp\mathfrak{H}^{0}(\mathcal{C})
, and therefore we have that
\|e^{u}_{\mathfrak{H}}\|=0
.

The only non-zero parts of the errors are then
e^{u}=e^{u}_{\mathfrak{B}^{*}}
and
e^{\sigma}=e^{\sigma}_{\mathfrak{B}}
. The error estimates that are obtained in
theorem

3.4
tell us

(4.23a)

\displaystyle\|e^{u}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|e^{u}_{\mathfrak{B}^{*}}\|^{2}\leq\left(c_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}\|D^{*}Du_{\mathcal{E}}-f\|\right)^{2},

(4.23b)

\displaystyle\|e^{\sigma}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|e^{\sigma}_{\mathfrak{B}}\|^{2}\leq\left(c_{\mathcal{A}}\|D^{*}\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}-f\|\right)^{2}=\left(c_{\mathcal{A}}\|D^{*}Du_{\mathcal{E}}-f\|\right)^{2}.

In order to get reasonable values for the right-hand side of these inequalities, we consider a smoothed solution
\hat{\sigma}
. If we were to use the embedded solution
\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}
, then on the overlap, the residuals read

(4.24)

\displaystyle D^{*}\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}-f=d^{*}\sigma_{\mathcal{E},i}+\delta^{*}\sigma_{\mathcal{E},(0,1)}-0.

However, the embedded stress is constant (zero) on the overlap, so the first term vanishes. Because we have inner products weighted by
\epsilon^{-1}
in
\mathcal{A}^{1,0}
, the operator
\delta^{*}
absorbs that weight and we are left with a term which blows up as
\epsilon\to 0
.

To avoid this blow-up, we define a smoother stress variable which is non-constant on the overlap (and stays the same as
\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}
on the non-overlapping region), such that the term
d^{*}\sigma_{i}
and
\delta^{*}\sigma_{0,1}
can balance each other. Since
\sigma_{0,1}
is a constant value, we write
\hat{\lambda}=\frac{w_{0,1}}{w\epsilon}\hat{\sigma}_{0,1}
, and define
\hat{\sigma}_{i}
on the overlap by:

(4.25a)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\mathcal{E},0}(-\epsilon)-\lambda(x+\epsilon),\qquad x\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon),

(4.25b)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\mathcal{E},1}(\epsilon)+\lambda(x-\epsilon),\qquad x\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon).

When we apply the codifferential to
\hat{\sigma}_{i}
, we get

(4.26)

d^{*}\hat{\sigma}_{0}=w\hat{\lambda}=\frac{w_{0,1}}{\epsilon}\hat{\sigma}_{0,1}=(\delta^{*}\sigma_{0,1})_{0}.

For
\hat{\sigma}=(\hat{\sigma}_{0},\hat{\sigma}_{1},\sigma_{\mathcal{E},(0,1)})
, we get
D^{*}\hat{\sigma}=0
exactly on the overlap.

Since
\hat{\sigma}=\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}
on
\tilde{U}_{i}
, we have that
D^{*}\hat{\sigma}=d^{*}\hat{\sigma}=\pm(1-\epsilon)r
. Together, the residual is then evaluated to

(4.27)

\|D^{*}\sigma_{\mathcal{E}}-f\|=\|\mathbbm{1}_{\tilde{U}_{i}}\left((1-\epsilon)\pm r\right)-\mathbbm{1}(\pm r)\|=\|\mathbbm{1}_{\tilde{U}_{i}}(\pm\epsilon r)\|=2\epsilon r\sqrt{1-\epsilon}.

The residual is
O(\epsilon)
, and using
C_{\mathcal{A}}\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{w}\pi}
, we get a reliable and robust estimate for the error of the displacement.

The same smoothed solution
\hat{\sigma}
does not give a reliable upper bound for the error
\|e^{\sigma}\|
, because the error is
O(\sqrt{\epsilon})
, while the residual of
\hat{\sigma}
is
O(\epsilon)
. Instead of considering a smoothed solution which balances
d^{*}
and
\delta^{*}
exactly on the overlap, we seek a smoothed solution
\tilde{\sigma}=(\tilde{\sigma}_{0},\tilde{\sigma}_{1},\sigma_{\mathcal{E},(0,1)})
which whose residual is constant on the overlap
(-\epsilon,\epsilon)
,

Similarly to
\hat{\sigma}
, we let
\tilde{\lambda}=\frac{1}{w}(\frac{w_{0,1}}{\epsilon}\hat{\sigma}_{0,1}+1)
, and define

(4.28a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\mathcal{E},0}(-\epsilon)-\tilde{\lambda}(x+\epsilon),\qquad x\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon),

(4.28b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\mathcal{E},1}(\epsilon)+\tilde{\lambda}(x-\epsilon),\qquad x\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon).

Then
D^{*}\tilde{\sigma}-f=\pm 1
exactly on the overlap, and equal to
\pm\epsilon r
outside the overlap. The constant value
1
on the overlap ensures robustness for the error estimates, since the
O(\epsilon^{1/2})
residual matches the asymptotics of the error
e^{\sigma}
.

Explicit solution to equations (
4.10a
)

We make use of two assumptions which simplify the equations considerably: we assume that
w_{0}=w_{1}=w
and
w_{0,1}
are constant functions (i.e. the same uniformly distributed material property for the two rods, with a possibly different material property on the overlap) and we assume that

.

We can use the expression
\sigma_{0,1}=u_{1}|_{U_{0,1}}-u_{0}|_{U_{0,1}}
to eliminate one equation and variable. We are left with the following system:

(A.1a)

\displaystyle\frac{du_{0}}{dx}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0},

(A.1b)

\displaystyle\frac{du_{1}}{dx}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1},

(A.1c)

\displaystyle-w\frac{d\sigma_{0}}{dx}+\frac{w_{0,1}}{\epsilon}\mathbbm{1}_{U_{0,1}}(u_{1}-u_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\tilde{U}_{0}}r,

(A.1d)

\displaystyle-w\frac{d\sigma_{1}}{dx}-\frac{w_{0,1}}{\epsilon}\mathbbm{1}_{U_{0,1}}(u_{1}-u_{0})

\displaystyle=-\mathbbm{1}_{\tilde{U}_{1}}r,

The characteristic function
\mathbbm{1}
is equal to zero outside of
U_{0,1}
and equal to
1
on
U_{0,1}
. We can therefore separate the problem into two cases: outside the overlap and on the overlap.

When we are outside
U_{0,1}
, the right-hand side of
eq.

and
eq.

A.1d
are equal to zero. The system of equations reduce to

(A.2a)

\displaystyle\frac{du_{0}}{dx}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{0},

(A.2b)

\displaystyle\frac{du_{1}}{dx}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1},

\displaystyle-w\frac{d\sigma_{1}}{dx}

(A.3a)

\displaystyle u_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{r}{2w}x^{2}+A_{0}x+B_{0},\qquad

\displaystyle x\in\tilde{U}_{0},

(A.3b)

\displaystyle u_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{2w}x^{2}+A_{1}x+B_{1},\qquad

\displaystyle x\in\tilde{U}_{1},

(A.3c)

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{r}{w}x+A_{0},\qquad

\displaystyle x\in\tilde{U}_{0},

(A.3d)

\displaystyle\frac{du_{0}}{dx}

(A.5b)

\displaystyle\frac{du_{1}}{dx}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1},
