Thinking and feeling matter: a second-order meta-analysis of student characteristics across developmental domains and their effect on academic outcomes
Authors: Cahit Erdem, Metin Kaya, Mustafa Polat, Eray Eğmir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Student characteristics are widely recognized as significant correlates of academic achievement. Among these, attributes related to developmental domains—such as cognitive abilities, psychomotor skills, positive emotions, and positive personality traits—have been the focus of numerous meta-analyses. This study employed a second-order meta-analytic approach to synthesize findings from 29 first-order meta-analyses selected based on predefined quality criteria using the R-AMSTAR checklist, yielding a total of 71 effect sizes. Results revealed a medium-level overall relationship between student characteristics and academic achievement. Effect sizes varied significantly by educational level, with the strongest correlations observed in kindergarten, and moderate associations found at the elementary and secondary levels. Among the domains, cognitive abilities demonstrated the highest correlation with academic achievement, followed by positive personality traits and positive emotions, both showing moderate effects. The findings are discussed in light of existing literature, and practical implications for educational policy and practice are presented.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-05035-x.

Introduction

Understanding the variations in students’ academic achievement across different educational levels has long been a central concern in educational research. A considerable body of work has sought to identify the key variables that influence academic performance and to determine the strength and direction of these relationships. This expansive and diverse literature is characterized by a complex network of interrelated factors, each contributing to the academic outcomes of students in varying degrees. Theoretical frameworks in the literature classify these factors into broad categories based on their focal points. While some studies emphasize psychological variables intrinsic to students, others adopt a more systemic approach by incorporating correlates related to schools [1], teachers [2], and student backgrounds [3]. However, a closer examination of recent empirical and meta-analytic studies suggests that student-related characteristics remain among the variables most consistently associated with academic achievement. These characteristics—such as cognitive abilities, emotional or psychological correlates, personality traits, and psychomotor abilities—can be categorized under the three developmental domains: cognitive, affective, and psychomotor [4]. This tripartite classification aligns with widely accepted educational taxonomies and developmental psychology frameworks.

In recent years, student-related factors have attracted attention from researchers across disciplines, including educational psychology, developmental science, and neuroscience. Scholars such as Quilez-Robres [5] have emphasized the crucial role of motivational, emotional, and social components in academic performance. However, the literature has historically placed disproportionate emphasis on cognitive abilities, particularly constructs like general intelligence [6], complex information processing, divergent thinking [7], and visual-spatial reasoning [8]. Cognitive ability is consistently reported as one of the characteristics most strongly associated with academic achievement [9, 10].

Nonetheless, growing empirical evidence highlights the importance of non-cognitive variables. Studies have shown that personality traits [11, 12], psychological characteristics [13], and emotional factors—including both positive [14, 15] and negative emotions [16, 17]—can exert significant effects on academic outcomes. Similarly, psychomotor abilities [18] have demonstrated relevance, especially in early and skill-based learning. Additional influential variables include socio-economic status [19], socio-cultural background [20], school climate [21], teacher quality [22], and parental involvement [23, 24].

Given the multifaceted nature of student development, it is essential to consider factors across the cognitive, affective, and psychomotor domains holistically. Despite the growing number of meta-analyses examining the relationship between specific student characteristics and academic achievement, findings across these syntheses are often inconsistent in magnitude and scope, vary by developmental domain and educational level, and remain largely fragmented. As a result, the field lacks an integrative synthesis that systematically compares and consolidates evidence across cognitive, affective, and psychomotor domains. This fragmentation limits the ability to draw generalizable conclusions regarding the relative importance of different student characteristics for academic achievement. The accumulation of such first-order meta-analysis studies also provides fertile ground for a second-order meta-analysis, which can offer more robust and generalizable conclusions by aggregating findings from multiple meta-analyses.

The present study addresses this need by conducting a second-order meta-analysis to investigate the relationship between academic achievement and student characteristics across developmental domains. The current second order meta-analysis study combined the results of 29 independent meta-analysis studies with a comparative perspective in terms of school levels. The data set for the student characteristics in the current study include the variables of positive emotions, positive personality traits (affective domain), cognitive abilities (cognitive domain), and psychomotor abilities (psychomotor domain). The relationships were also examined in terms of various moderator variables. In sum, using the second order meta-analysis method, the current study aimed to examine the relationship between students’ cognitive abilities, positive emotions, positive personality traits, and psychomotor abilities and their academic achievement. The following research questions were sought to answer:

Conceptual framework

Student characteristics across developmental domains

Studies dedicated to investigating the multidimensional structure of student characteristics on academic achievement focus on a great number of different correlates. While some are directly related to a specific domain or a variable, others are based on several interdependent factors. The impacts of student characteristics, which predict academic achievement, have attracted the attention of scholars, especially since the beginning of the 21st century, with the understanding of the importance of these differences in education [25].

The literature encompasses distinctive student characteristics affecting their learning and academic success. On the other hand, divergent tendencies regarding the classification of these variables and the lack of consensus on the domains in which these variables are thematized have led to the emergence of a complicated structure. The current study examines the relationship between student characteristics in developmental domains and their academic achievement levels through a second order meta-analysis method to build a comprehensive and integrated picture of the links between academic achievement and correlates. Thus, after the selection of related meta-analyses in consideration of the R-AMSTAR checklist, we adopted a reasonable approach to categorize the variables in the dataset. First, all the traits were listed based on the reported findings of the research. Then, these variables were gathered under subthemes in accordance with their theoretical framework to build a comprehensive and integrated picture of the links between academic achievement and correlates in this study. Student characteristics in the developmental domains in this study are composed of four themes: positive emotions, positive personality traits, cognitive abilities, and psychomotor abilities. This study deliberately focuses on positive emotional and personality characteristics (e.g., well-being, motivation, grit, conscientiousness) rather than negative attributes, in line with the positive psychology perspective and because the first-order meta-analyses included in the dataset predominantly examined positive traits. Negative emotional factors (e.g., anxiety, neuroticism) were therefore considered beyond the scope of the present synthesis.

Positive emotions

Positive psychology regards positive emotions as a measurable and teachable domain [26] in relation to affective development. Fredrickson [27], and Fredrickson and Branigan [28] propose that positive emotions broaden individuals’ attention and thought–action repertoires, enabling engagement in a wider range of thoughts and behaviors than under neutral conditions. Over time, these broadened repertoires contribute to the development of personal, social, intellectual, and psychological resources that are directly relevant to learning and academic achievement. Variables such as well-being [14], motivation [5], enjoyment [16], grit [29], emotional intelligence [30], self-regulation [31], and self-confidence [32] are among the positive emotions focused in previous meta-analysis studies in the dataset of this research.

Positive personality traits

Positive personality traits constitute the second component of student characteristics in this research. Seligman and Csikszentmihalyi [33] mention the concept of positive personality as one of the three pillars while outlining a framework for positive psychology. Although numerous characteristics such as empathy, optimism, tolerance, mindfulness, satisfaction with life, gratitude, and forgiveness are listed under the title of positive personality traits in different studies [34, 35], there is no consensus on the scope of the correlates under this theme due to the nature of the concept. Various research shows the impacts of positive personality in different contexts and the links among other variables. For example, Rehman et al. [36] claim that positive personality characteristics could support individuals to earn the required confidence to perform well in a different environment. Kunanitthaworn et al. [37] found that positive personality characteristics are correlated with intrinsic motivation, particularly perseverance and determination. Also, positive personality-related characteristics may help students to deal with their psycho-social problems [38], protect individuals from the possible negative impacts of stressors in an educational context, and play facilitator roles that enhance their resilience capability [39]. Finally, studies reported that students with high positive personality characteristics such as agreeableness, conscientiousness, and openness would be predicted to deal with stressors in their education life better and struggle against difficulties, and they are regarded as characteristics consistently associated with academic achievement [40–42]. In this respect, openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and emotional stability [15, 43] are the variables which constitute the category of positive personality traits in the dataset of this research.

Cognitive abilities

The third theme, called cognitive abilities, related to cognitive domain, refers to a number of notions related to intelligence and individuals’ cognitive process and skills such as computational thinking, creativity, fluid reasoning, comprehension, and so on. Since Binet and Simon’s attempts to reveal the relationships between the cognitive capacities of children and their performance in educational context, cognition-based factors have attracted enormous attention of researchers. Beyond their well-established predictive strength, cognitive abilities can also be understood through several theoretical frameworks. Cognitive abilities, including intelligence and higher-order thinking skills, are relatively stable characteristics and have consistently been shown to play a central role in academic achievement. This relationship is supported by major theoretical frameworks such as Investment Theory [44], which emphasizes the role of cognitive resources in learning, and Dual-Process Theory [45], which highlights interactions between automatic and reflective cognitive processes. Studies devoted to scrutinizing the multifaceted structure of cognitive abilities show that intelligence and cognitive abilities, which are more stable than psychological and personality-related ones, are overarching and play facilitator roles in students’ academic performance [9, 46–50]. Peng and Kievit [51], who look from a new perspective, see that the relationship as bidirectional, which is in line with the theory of mutualism [52].

Psychomotor abilities

The last theme under student characteristics related to psychomotor domain is psychomotor abilities which refer to the ability to operate physical motor movements such as moving limbs, balance, coordination, and so on. Pioneering research in this field [53–55] has demonstrated the link between psychomotor skills, learning, and cognitive development. Also, findings from empirical studies have supported the conceptual framework showing the bonds between psychomotor abilities and cognitive related domains [56–58]. Besides, previous research has shed light on the correlation between psychomotor abilities and academic achievement in different subject areas [59, 60]. In this research, psychomotor skills were limited to visual perception and motor functioning as a matter of the limitation of studies in the dataset.

Method

This study aimed to examine the relationship between student characteristics and academic achievement. To this end, we employed a second-order meta-analysis, which statistically synthesizes the effect sizes from multiple meta-analyses (as opposed to first-order meta-analysis, which synthesizes primary studies). This approach has been recommended when sufficient meta-analytic studies on a topic exist [61–63]. We preferred this method since a substantial number of meta-analysis studies were accumulated probing the relationship between various student characteristics and academic achievement. Thanks to this employed method, we sought to examine how certain student characteristics are associated with their academic achievement in the general sense.

Data collection

We obtained this study’s data from the main academic databases including Web of Science, Scopus, PsycNet, and ERIC. The keywords used in the search in the databases were achievement or success or academic performance or academic gap or student outcomes or school outcomes or educational outcome and meta-analysis or meta analytic or systematic review. The search was conducted across Web of Science, Scopus, PsycNet, and ERIC using the specified keywords, which were searched in the titles of studies. Title-based searching was employed to increase specificity and ensure the inclusion of studies with a primary and explicit focus on academic achievement and meta-analytic synthesis, thereby reducing the retrieval of tangential or methodologically heterogeneous records. Overlapping

To minimize the risk of dependency among effect sizes, several precautionary strategies were employed in the current study. Regarding the overlapping meta-analysis studies, the one involving the highest number of primary studies and the most current one is included in this second order meta-analysis. Ones with fewer primary studies and older ones are excluded. We developed this criterion because the overlapping meta-analysis studies included the same primary studies. Tan et al. [63] suggest that using overlapping meta-analysis studies in second order meta-analysis studies will result in an erroneous calculation of true effect sizes because the same primary study would be included at least twice in the effect size calculation. This should be considered while coding the studies [65]. We employed several strategies to tackle the overlapping problem. We checked whether the span of scanning, cultural context, research output, and keyword used in the search differed among the meta-analysis studies involving the same or similar predictor variables. In the meta-analysis studies covering the same or similar year intervals, we checked if the predictor variables, research output, cultural context and the keywords differed. As well as these and other similar strategies, we selected the most current meta-analysis study among the ones using the same predictor and output variables. Two researchers independently reviewed and discussed each entry until consensus was reached. Thus, the included and excluded articles due to the reasons above are presented in Table 1.

Included and excluded articles due to overlapping problem

Furthermore, in instances where a meta-analysis reported effect sizes across multiple school levels (e.g., kindergarten, elementary, secondary), these effect sizes were treated as independent analytical units. This was due to the fact that they were derived from non-overlapping subsamples and represented distinct educational populations. In the course of the analysis, no multiple effect sizes drawn from the same meta-analysis and the same school level were included simultaneously.

To further preserve the independence of the analytical units, each effect size included in the second-order meta-analysis represented a unique combination of meta-analysis and school level. Accordingly, multiple effect sizes derived from the same first-order meta-analysis were included only when they referred to distinct, non-overlapping educational populations (e.g., kindergarten, elementary, or secondary students). Effect sizes representing the same population within a single meta-analysis were not simultaneously entered into the analyses. This procedure was adopted to minimize statistical dependency while retaining information relevant to moderator analyses by school level. Through the implementation of these procedures, the potential violation of the independence assumption was mitigated to an acceptable level, consistent with methodological practices observed in prior second-order meta-analyses.

Quality assessment

We used the R-AMSTAR scale, revised by Kung et al. [71], to assess the quality of the included meta-analysis studies. The scores in the R-AMSTAR were evaluated as follows: 0–11 = inadequate, 12–22 = low, 23–33 = medium-level and 34–44 = high [72]. The R-AMSTAR consists of 11 criteria, and each criterion has a score of four point. 8 C and 8D items of the eight criteria are in accordance with clinical studies. Therefore, these items were not scored in the quality assessment procedure. 8 A and 8B items were given two points each in this study. The quality scores of the studies comprising the dataset for this study range from a minimum of 25 to a maximum of 41. The average quality score is 33.72 with a standard deviation of 3.99. The overall quality of the dataset is high, and the quality level for each study is provided in Appendix 1. Besides, two studies were excluded from the analysis as they were below the quality level.

Coding

We created a coding form reflecting the characteristics of meta-analysis studies. This form included the codes of authors of the study, publication year, academic achievement type, types of students’ individual characteristics, cultural context of the study, types of primary studies, and school grade level. The codes are explained as follows.

Publication date

the year when the meta-analysis study was published was coded.

Cultural context

The study was coded as mixed context/global context if it involved data from multiple countries, and it was coded as country context/local context if it involved data from a single country.

Types of primary studies

If the meta-analysis study involved at least two types of studies among articles, doctoral dissertations and others, it was coded as a mixed group; if it included only one type of study, it was coded with that type of study, such as article group.

School grade level

Meta-analysis studies mostly include effect sizes in terms of school level. Since this study also aimed to examine academic achievement and individual characteristics in terms of school levels, the effect sizes for school levels in the meta-analysis studies were used. School grade levels were coded as kindergarten, elementary, and secondary.

Publication bias status

If the meta-analysis study reported publication bias, it was coded as publication bias is extant; if it did not report anything on publication bias, it was coded as unknown; and if it reported lack of publication bias, it was coded as no publication bias.

Academic area

If the meta-analysis study involved multiple academic areas (e.g., mathematics, language, and others), it was coded as general domain; if it involved a single academic area, it was coded as single domain, such as mathematics domain.

Types of student characteristics

The variables examined in the meta-analysis studies were grouped. Various studies were referenced during this coding: personality characteristics [73], cognitive abilities [74], emotional characteristics [75], and psycho-motor skills [18]. Besides, regarding student characteristics, if the k value was lower than three in the meta-analysis studies (k ≤ 3), it was not coded. The characteristics of the FOMs in the data set are tabulated in Appendix-1, in which the sub-category variables of student characteristics are presented.

All included meta-analyses were independently coded in full by two researchers. To achieve inter-rater agreement, inter-rater reliability was assessed across all coding categories using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient. This coefficient was 0.88 in this study. In instances where such discrepancies could not be resolved through discussion, expert opinion was sought. The expert who was consulted was a senior academic with extensive experience in meta-analysis and educational research. The role of experts was confined to the adjudication of cases that were ambiguous in nature, particularly with regard to the classification of student characteristics, moderator variables, and the management of overlapping meta-analyses. The expert did not modify the extracted effect sizes or influence the statistical analyses. The final coding decisions were made through a consensus process, ensuring both methodological rigour and transparency.

Data analysis

This study aimed to examine the relationships between student characteristics in the developmental domains, and academic achievement in line with school grade levels. The appropriate analysis unit is the independent effect sizes in the meta-analysis studies to examine this relationship in terms of school levels. In other words, the effect sizes regarding the independent school levels in the meta-analysis studies were used in this study. In sum, the analysis unit of this study is the independent effect sizes in the meta-analysis studies in the data sets. Some first-order meta-analyses contributed more than one effect size because they reported separate estimates for different school levels. These effect sizes were retained only when they represented mutually exclusive educational populations and were therefore treated as independent analytical units. Although effect sizes originating from the same meta-analysis may share certain methodological characteristics, this approach minimized dependency while allowing school level to be examined as a moderator.

The included meta-analysis studies used different effect size indices (e.g., Pearson’s correlations (r), Fisher’s z (Fz), and Cohen’s (d). Most of them used the r value as the effect size index (n = 30; k = 92). All other effect size indices were transformed to r. It is the most common index used in meta-analysis studies [76]. Therefore, r index effect sizes were used while reporting the results of this study. While interpreting the effect sizes, the value intervals suggested by Funder and Ozer [76] were used as a reference. The effect sizes were calculated through weighting by variance in the FOMs. The analysis was performed on CMA 2.0 package program.

It is recommended to use the random effect model if the studies are from different samples and the characteristics of the studies are diverse [77]. The procedures for mean effect size, heterogeneity analysis, and moderator analyses were conducted with the random effect model. Q statistics technique was used to test the heterogeneity of the effect sizes. The results are presented with between groups Q(b) values. Additionally, I2 value was used to identify the heterogeneity level. The Egger’s test and Duval and Tweedie Trim and Fill test were used in the publication bias analysis regarding the data set [78]. Besides, when the FOM studies with the largest and smallest effect sizes were excluded, no significant change was observed in the total variance Q and I2 values. This situation was interpreted as indicating that there was no effect size in the data set that could be interpreted as an outlier.

Findings

The data set included the relationship between certain student characteristics and academic achievement. The descriptive statistics, heterogeneity analysis, publication bias, and moderator analysis for the data set are presented in this section.

Student characteristics and academic achievement

The data set included 71 effect sizes. The effect sizes ranged between r = 0.46 and r = 0.01. The mean effect size was r = 0.26 (LL = 0.238 UL = 0.288). That is, there was a medium-level relationship between student characteristics (across cognitive, affective, and psychomotor domains) characteristics and academic achievement. The total heterogeneity amount of the data set was Q ((70) = 1434,70; p = < 0.01). The total heterogeneity level of the data set was I2= 95.12. Based on these values, the data set was heterogenous. The main findings presented in Table 2 are discussed as follows. The distribution of effect sizes in terms of school grade levels differed significantly (Qb = 8.00; p = 0.04). At the kindergarten level, there was a high-level relationship between students’ characteristics and academic achievement (r = 0.37). The mean effect sizes differed significantly in terms of student characteristics type (Q = 14.56; p < 0.01). Discussion and conclusion

This second-order meta-analysis study revealed a medium-level relationship between students’ characteristics in affective, cognitive and psychomotor domains of development and academic achievement. The relationship between students’ cognitive abilities and academic achievement was moderate and represented the strongest observed association among the student characteristics examined, accounting for approximately 8.4% of the variance in academic achievement. That it had the highest association with academic achievement is reasonable given the bidirectional relationship between cognitive abilities and academic achievement [51] and the emphasis on cognitive abilities in schools [79]. The finding is clearly in line with the previous research that highlights the importance of cognitive abilities in students’ academic achievement [6, 80, 81]. A meta-analysis, for example, revealed that cognitive abilities were a greater predictor of academic achievement than personality characteristics [15].

Students’ positive emotions had a medium level relationship with academic achievement, explaining 6.3% of the variance. Positive emotions include variables such as well-being, motivation, grit, emotional intelligence, self-regulation, and so on. Extant meta-analysis studies on these variables provided mixed results. For example, the relationship between well-being and academic achievement was reported as small [69] or small to moderate [14], or the association between motivational factors and academic achievement was reported as moderate [5] or small [82]. This second-order meta-analysis study synthesized the results and provided a moderate relationship. In support of this finding, the control-value theory, which presents a holistic framework for investigating the origins and impacts of emotions, encountered in academic achievement, points out the hypothesis that individuals’ judgments of control and values by individuals are situated in the central of achievement emotions which influence their academic achievement [83].

Students’ positive personality traits had a medium-level relationship with academic achievement, explaining 4% of the variance. Positive personality traits include openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and emotional stability. Individuals whose personality is portrayed by openness, extraversion, conscientiousness, agreeableness and emotional stability employ a deeper approach to learning which stimulates their understanding [84]. These traits may facilitate students’ confidence in academic endeavours [36], and it is also related to motivation [37]. It serves as protective factor for students in stressful environments [39] and helps cope with problems [38, 41]. These consistent relations between positive personality traits and academic achievement have been put forth in various studies in the literature [11, 13, 85]. Although cognitive abilities have a higher level of relationship with academic achievement and explain more variance, the values suggest that emotional and personality traits of students also matter a lot with respect to academic achievement. Besides, affective characteristics such as positive emotions have been suggested to be positively associated with the development of cognitive abilities [28]. The observed large heterogeneity may also reflect the inherently multifaceted nature of academic achievement, which emerges from dynamic interactions among cognitive, emotional, personality-related, and contextual factors. Consequently, the moderate overall associations identified in this study should be viewed as broad developmental patterns rather than deterministic relationships, highlighting the importance of considering individual and contextual differences when interpreting student characteristics and academic outcomes.

Additionally, despite the relatively large effect size observed in relation to psychomotor abilities, it should be noted that this finding is based on evidence synthesised from a single first-order meta-analysis. Consequently, in contrast to the well-established empirical foundations of the cognitive and affective domains, the psychomotor domain remains insufficiently substantiated by empirical evidence to support strong generalisations. Consequently, the observed association should be regarded as indicative rather than conclusive, underscoring the necessity for additional meta-analytic research in this domain. Therefore, comparisons between the psychomotor domain and the cognitive or affective domains should be interpreted with caution, as the underlying evidence base differs substantially in both quantity and methodological breadth.

As well as the components of student characteristics, the variables of academic area, cultural context, publication bias status, research quality, research report type, school grade level, and publication year variables were examined as moderators. Among these moderator variables, the relationship between student characteristics and academic achievement differed only in terms of school levels. There is a strong relationship between student characteristics and academic achievement at the kindergarten level, explaining 13.7% of the variance in academic achievement. On the other hand, this relationship is at medium-level at the elementary level, explaining 7.3% of the variance, and at a low-level at the secondary level, explaining 4.8% of the variance.

Research revealed that the relationship between parental involvement and academic achievement declines as children grow up [86]. Support from home affects not only academic achievement but also positive personality characteristics positively, and personality characteristics are more related to home factors in early school years [87]. Students receive more support from home at earlier ages, contributing to both positive personality characteristics and academic achievement. On the other hand, this moderation of school levels is also observed in the literature, for instance, in the relationship between personality types and academic achievement [11]. Vedel and Popopat [85] related this to the measurement of personality. They argued that desirability bias might be involved in self-rated measurement, and more educated individuals tend to overstate their levels in self-reports [88].

In summary, this second-order meta-analysis provides consistent evidence that cognitive, affective (emotional), and personality characteristics are meaningfully associated with K–12 students’ academic achievement, with cognitive abilities demonstrating the strongest observed association. Importantly, the observed associations between these student characteristics and academic achievement are stronger in earlier grades than in later stages of schooling. These findings offer a comprehensive evidence base that student thinking and feeling both matter for success in school.

Implications

This study revealed the fact that, as well as cognitive abilities, emotional characteristics are also really significant for students’ academic achievement. These two variables are inseparably linked, as emphasized in Vygotsky’s sociocultural theory of mind, which brings together cognition and emotions [89]. Since individuals’ academic achievement has a multifactorial and complex structure, it is not possible to evaluate it from a single-oriented perspective [90]. Earlier research has already indicated that emotions play a crucial role in cognitive processes [91]. From this point of view, considering only cognitive factors and ignoring other factors while examining the success or failure of individuals may lead to incomplete results [5, 92].

Conradi et al. [93] identified the motivational elements predicting academic achievement. These elements overlap with students’ emotional characteristics. Emotions have a critical impact on cognitive processes such as attention, perception, memory, and so on which are intimately linked with the learning process [94]. Positive emotions are consistently associated with higher levels of intrinsic motivation for learning [95]. Some studies also report the moderating effects of emotions in the relationship between acquiring knowledge and cognitive skills [96]. Therefore, emotional characteristics are critical for the learning process. Despite its importance, the affective dimension of teaching has received less attention in research and academic studies than the cognitive dimension [89]. Additionally, the limited number of instruments developed to identify students’ emotional characteristics and the insufficient number of qualitative studies on the subject are noteworthy. Therefore, the present findings suggest that supporting students’ motivation and emotional capacities may be beneficial for learning environments and academic development. In particular, in-service training programs to enhance teachers’ knowledge and skills regarding students’ motivation may prove fruitful. Several empirical researches have demonstrated that teacher support is significantly associated with positive emotions and significantly negatively correlated with negative emotions in an educational context [97]. Accordingly, learning and making use of students’ cognitive and emotional states in the class is pivotal for teaching [98]. Given that student characteristics were most strongly linked with achievement in early childhood, it is especially crucial to foster cognitive, social, and emotional development in the early years (e.g., preschool and kindergarten programs that develop self-regulation and positive learning attitudes) when they can have the greatest impact. Meanwhile, sustaining support through elementary and secondary school remains important, even as the relative influence of these traits slightly diminishes.

In terms of practical implications, we underline the importance of encouraging students to maintain the positive emotions they experience in the learning environment through the creation of safe and supportive learning environments and through both parental and teacher support. At the level of curriculum and policy, the findings also suggest the importance of systematically integrating social-emotional learning (SEL) into school programs. In light of the demonstrated moderate relationship between positive emotions and personality characteristics and academic achievement, educational policymakers may consider the implementation of structured SEL curricula in conjunction with traditional academic content. A considerable number of educational systems around the globe have recently started incorporating SEL frameworks with the aim of fostering well-being, resilience, and self-regulation. The present findings offer empirical validation for these initiatives. The intentional incorporation of cognitive and socio-emotional competencies into curricular design may support a more holistic approach to promoting academic success, consistent with the pattern of associations identified in the present second-order meta-analysis.

Limitations and further research

This study has several limitations. First, the studies involved in the current study are limited to meta-analysis studies published in English. Further research may include studies in other languages. Second, this study is limited to published articles. Further research may include unpublished meta-analysis studies. Third, this study focuses on the relationship between students’ certain characteristics and academic achievement. Further second-order meta-analysis studies may explore the relationship between academic achievement and other psychological factors or environmental factors such as socio-economic status and cultural factors. It should also be noted that examining such complex psychosocial or contextual variables may require different methodological approaches, including primary longitudinal research or first-order meta-analyses specifically targeting relational and environmental constructs, as these factors are not yet consistently synthesized in second-order meta-analytic form. Fourth, this study does not involve complicated psycho-social behaviours such as student-teacher relationship, social acceptance engagement, and violence. Other studies may examine the relationship between students’ psycho-social behaviours and academic achievement.

Fifth, the evidence base for the psychomotor domain was limited, as only one first-order meta-analysis satisfied the inclusion criteria. As a result, the estimated effect size for psychomotor abilities should be regarded as preliminary and interpreted with caution. Unlike the cognitive and affective domains, which were supported by multiple independent meta-analyses, the psychomotor findings could not be evaluated in terms of consistency, robustness, or potential moderator effects. Future first-order and second-order meta-analyses are needed to establish a more reliable and comprehensive understanding of the relationship between psychomotor abilities and academic achievement. Sixth, another limitation of the present study is its exclusive focus on positive emotions. Although the literature indicates that both positive and negative emotions are related to academic outcomes, negative emotions were not included in the current synthesis. Future second-order meta-analyses examining the effects of negative emotions on academic achievement are needed to provide a more comprehensive picture of academic success. Seventh, further studies may independently analyse students’ cognitive, emotional, personality and psychomotor characteristics. Thus, we can reveal which cognitive abilities, for example, have stronger associations with academic achievement, or which affective characteristics predict academic achievement to a greater extent. Because we aggregated traits into broad domains, we could be masking differences between specific traits (e.g., is grit more predictive than well-being, or working memory vs. processing speed within cognition? ). Future meta-analyses might perform a more granular second-order analysis focusing on sub-dimensions within these domains.

Eighth, the substantial heterogeneity among effect sizes (I² ≈ 95%) indicates that the magnitude of the relationship between student characteristics and academic achievement varies considerably across studies and educational contexts. Consequently, the overall effect size reported in the present study should be interpreted as an average tendency rather than a universal estimate applicable to all settings. Although several moderator analyses were conducted, a substantial proportion of between-study variance remained unexplained, suggesting that additional contextual, methodological, or measurement-related factors may contribute to this variability. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing the findings across different educational populations and research contexts. Finally, because this is a second-order meta-analysis, it inherits limitations of the included meta-analyses. We attempted to minimize this by including only studies of adequate quality (R-AMSTAR ≥ 22) and avoiding overlapping datasets. Nevertheless, issues such as publication bias or measurement differences in the original studies could influence our results.
