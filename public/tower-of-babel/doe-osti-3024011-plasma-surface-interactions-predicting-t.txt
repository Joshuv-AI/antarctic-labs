Overview

This project focused on the development and integration of high-performance simulation tools to predict
the operating behavior of Plasma-Facing Components (PFCs) in magnetic confinement fusion (MCF)
systems. A key objective at Illinois was to assess the impact of the dynamic interplay between the evolving
material surface and the magnetized plasma sheath, and characterize the impact of tungsten-based PFCs on
plasma contamination, including phenomena such as surface erosion (sputtering), dynamic recycling
(reflection and subsequent ionization and redeposition) of fuel species, and tritium retention (implantation),
which are critical for the success of future magnetic fusion devices.

Establishing a fundamental physical understanding and developing predictive capabilities of plasma-
surface interactions (PSI) requires simultaneously addressing complex multi-scale multi- physics
phenomena occurring over a wide range of length (Angstroms to meters) and time (femtoseconds to years)
scales, as well as integrating extensive physical processes across the plasma-surface interface. This requires
development of not only detailed physics models and computational strategies at each scale, but also
algorithms and methods to couple them effectively in a way that can be robustly validated. Deploying these
tools requires the continued development and coupling of leadership-scale computational codes to describe
the boundary plasma (plasma sheath) and the evolving PFC surface (PSI tools), as well as a host of
simulations that bridge disparate scales to address complex physical and computational issues at the plasma-
surface interface in multi-component materials systems for magnetic fusion energy development beyond
ITER.

The project required a unique combination of three activities: (1) code development activities, equally
divided between the development of a plasma-surface interaction module and a plasma sheath module; (2)
development of coupling methodologies between plasma and surface models, in order to capture dynamic
surface evolution during plasma irradiation; and (3) physics investigation and exploration, where the
innovative tools mentioned above were exercised on cases of experimental interest for MCF. The
deployment of these integrated tools required the continued evolution and synergistic coupling of
leadership-scale computational codes, methodologies, and verification/validation platforms. The high-

(1) Code Development Activities

Development of Plasma-Surface Interaction Modules

 ● Development Phase 1: Development of the Fractal-TRIDYN code
 ● Development Phase 2: Development of the RustBCA code

Fractal TRIDYN (F-TRIDYN) is a modified version of the widely used Monte Carlo, Binary Collision
Approximation code TRIDYN that includes an explicit model of surface roughness and additional output
modes for coupling to plasma edge and material codes. Surface roughness plays an important role in ion
irradiation processes such as sputtering; roughness can significantly increase the angle of maximum
sputtering and change the maximum observed sputtering yield by a factor of 2 or more. The complete effect
of surface roughness on sputtering and other ion irradiation phenomena is not completely understood. Many
rough surfaces can be consistently and realistically modeled by fractals, using the fractal dimension and
fractal length scale as the sole input parameters. Fractals provide a compelling path toward a complete and
concise understanding of the effect that surface geometry plays on the behavior of plasma-facing materials.
In Fractal-TRIDYN we included a robust fractal surface algorithm that is more computationally efficient
than those in previous fractal codes and which reproduces available experimental sputtering data from
rough surfaces. F-TRIDYN serves as a flexible tool for simulating ion-solid interactions and coupling with
other codes. Related Publication: Drobny, A. Hayes, D. Curreli, D. N. Ruzic, F-TRIDYN: A Binary
Collision Approximation Code for Simulating Ion Interactions with Rough Surfaces, Journal of Nuclear
Materials, Vol. 494, 278-283 (2017).

In order to overcome some of the limitations of Fractal-TRIDYN, we developed RustBCA, a new Binary
Collision Approximation (BCA) Monte Carlo code with modern software architecture. RustBCA is a new
high-performance, general-purpose BCA code for ion-material interactions, prioritizing scientific
flexibility and ease of use. Its features include support for electronic stopping formulations in both low (up
to 25 keV/nucleon) and high (up to 1 GeV/nucleon) energy regimes; a selection of interatomic potentials
such as Kr-C, ZBL, Moliere, and Lenz-Jensen screened Coulomb, along with Lennard-Jones and Morse
attractive-repulsive potentials; and the unique capability to use multiple interatomic potentials within a
single simulation. RustBCA also allows for determining the scattering angle using either Gaussian
quadrature or the approximate MAGIC algorithm. It provides full trajectory tracking for both ions and
material atoms, including local nuclear and electronic energy losses, and supports multiple geometry types.
The code is configured via an easily readable file and provides comprehensive 6D output for all particles
exiting the simulation via sputtering or reflection. Related Publication: J. Drobny, D. Curreli, RustBCA: A
High-Performance Binary Collision Approximation Code for Ion-Material Interactions, Journal of Open
Source Software, 6 (64), 3298, (2021).

Development of Magnetized Plasma Sheath Modules

 ● Development Phase 1. hPIC (MPI/OpenMP particle-in-cell code)
 ● Development Phase 2. hPIC2 (GPU-accelerated version of hPIC)

The hPIC code is a fully-kinetic electrostatic Particle-in-Cell application targeted at large-scale simulations
of magnetized plasma sheaths and Plasma–Material Interactions. The code can simulate multi-component
strongly-magnetized plasmas in a region close to the wall, including the magnetic sheath and presheath,
plus the first surface layers which release material impurities. The Poisson solver is based on PETSc
conjugate gradient with BoomerAMG algebraic multigrid preconditioners. Scaling tests on the Blue Waters
supercomputer demonstrated excellent weak-scaling (tested up to 65,536 cores) and good strong-scaling up
to 262,144 cores. The code was used for the kinetic characterization of the ion energy-angle distribution
(IEAD) function of the plasma species impacting on the material wall after being accelerated by the
magnetized plasma sheath. Related publications: R. Khaziev, D. Curreli, Ion Energy-Angle Distribution
Functions at the Plasma-Material Interface in Oblique Magnetic Fields, Physics of Plasmas, Vol. 22, Is.
4, 043503 (2015); R. Khaziev, D. Curreli, hPIC: A Scalable Electrostatic Particle-in-Cell for Plasma-
Material Interactions, Computer Physics Communications, Vol. 229, 87-98 (2018).

The development of the hybrid plasma simulation code, hPIC2, was motivated by the critical challenges
posed by the forthcoming exascale era of high performance computing and by the need of GPU acceleration
of the hPIC code. While exascale computing promised to dramatically advance computational plasma
physics toward accurate, self-consistent multiscale modeling, this power was fundamentally tied to
heterogeneous hardware, specifically, dedicated accelerators like GPUs from different manufacturers
(NVIDIA, AMD, Intel). This architectural diversity necessitated distinct parallel programming models
(e.g., CUDA, HIP, oneAPI), leading to a substantial and ever-increasing code maintenance burden for
applications seeking performance portability. To directly address the issue of architectural diversity and
code maintenance, hPIC2 was developed using the Kokkos performance portability framework developed
at SANDIA. This architectural choice was central to hPIC2 design, as Kokkos enabled the code to maintain
a single codebase while efficiently targeting the diverse architectures projected to drive exascale computing,
thereby ensuring long-term viability and performance across platforms. Furthermore, the design of hPIC2
centered on creating a truly multiscale simulation capability. hPIC2 was designed to be uniquely positioned
as a hybrid code able to investigate complex physical phenomena, by enabling the simultaneous use of
various plasma models, such as Particle-in-Cell (PIC), fluid, and kinetic descriptions, on the same
computational domain. This flexible, multiscale approach is essential for accurately capturing phenomena
that span vastly different spatial and temporal scales, particularly those found near material boundaries. A
crucial development step for hPIC2 was the integration of specialized physics for plasma-material
interaction. This was achieved through an in-memory coupling of hPIC2 to RustBCA, the plasma-surface
interaction module described above. This coupling enabled the hPIC2-RustBCA coupled codes to
accurately model dynamic ion-material interactions. With this addition, hPIC2 moved beyond simple
plasma descriptions to accurately account for the physics of energetic particle impingement, reflection,
sputtering, and implantation into solid surfaces. The development of hPIC2 successfully combined a
performance-portable architecture with a multiscale hybrid modeling approach and specialized ion-material
interaction physics. This integrated design allows hPIC2 to achieve scalable performance across a variety
of modern and next-generation computing architectures, establishing it as an indispensable tool for

hardware-accelerated, hybrid particle-in-cell code for dynamic plasma-material interactions, Computer
Physics Communications, Volume 283, 108569, (2023).

(2) Development of Coupling Methodologies between Plasma and Surface Models

A key research focus at Illinois was on the development of efficient coupling methodologies between the
models describing the magnetized plasma sheath and plasma-surface interactions. To this end, we
introduced an innovative scheme to couple solvers of the Boltzmann equation (such as Particle-in-Cell,
Vlasov Continuum codes, etc.) to Binary Collision Approximation (BCA) methods, for simulating the near-
wall plasma dynamics in the presence of a material-releasing wall. This method combined a full-f
multispecies Boltzmann solver for the plasma (charged and neutral species) with a modification of the
classical BCA. Both the plasma ions and the impurities are treated as Boltzmann kinetic species, ensuring
high resolution across disparate densities, particle fluxes, drift velocities, and energy fluxes. From the
resulting distribution functions, all fluid moments (density, heat flux, etc.) and the net and gross erosion
rates can be derived. Related publication: S. Keniley, D. Curreli, Dynamic Coupling of Boltzmann Plasma
Model to Surface Erosion Model for Kinetic Treatment of Plasma-Material Interactions, Fusion Science
and Technology, Vol. 71, N. 1, 93-102 (2017).

The work was further extended to analyze two classes of Density-Estimation techniques which can be used
to consistently couple different kinetic models of the plasma-material interface, intended as the region of
plasma immediately interacting with the first surface layers of a material wall. In particular, we handled the
general problem of interfacing a continuum multi-species Vlasov-Poisson-BGK plasma model to discrete
surface erosion models, such as Monte Carlo/BCA. The continuum model solves for the energy-angle
distributions of the particles striking the surface, which are then driving the surface response. A
modification to the classical Binary-Collision Approximation (BCA) method was utilized as a prototype
discrete model of the surface, to provide boundary conditions and impurity distributions representative of
the material behavior during plasma irradiation. The same coupling techniques could also be utilized for
other discrete material models such as Molecular Dynamics in place of the BCA. The numerical tests
revealed the superior convergence properties of Gaussian Mixture Models over Kernel Density Estimation
methods, with Gaussian Mixtures and Epanechnikov-KDEs both being up to two orders of magnitude faster
than Gaussian-KDEs. The methodology developed allowed a self-consistent treatment of the plasma-
material interface in MCF devices, including both the near-surface plasma (plasma sheath and presheath)
in magnetized conditions, and surface effects such as sputtering, back-scattering, and ion implantation.
Related publication: S. Keniley, D. Curreli, Density Estimation Techniques for Multiscale Coupling of
Kinetic Models of the Plasma Material Interface, Journal of Computational Physics, Vol. 400, 108965
(2020).

Early work on data-driven approaches was also performed for effective construction of a surrogate model
in high-dimensional parameter space for the ion energy-angle distribution (IEAD) output of hPIC
simulations of plasma-surface interactions. The methodology was based on a bin-by-bin least-squares
fitting of the IEAD in the parameter space. The fitting was performed in a transformed coordinate system
to normalize the IEAD, and it employed sparse grids for sampling the parameter space to overcome

 4

sampling challenges in high dimensions. The surrogate model we obtained is significantly cheaper
computationally than direct hPIC simulations yet maintains high fidelity to them, providing a fast emulator
for hPIC simulations. Sensitivity analysis based on the surrogate model was utilized to characterize how
physical parameters influence the moments of the ion impact angle and energy. Related publication: P.
Seleson, M. Mustafa, D. Curreli, C. D. Hauck, M. Stoyanov, D. Bernholdt, Data-driven surrogate modeling
of hPIC ion energy-angle distributions for high-dimensional sensitivity analysis of plasma parameters
uncertainty, Computer Physics Communications, 279, 108436 (2022).

Historically, Binary Collision Approximation (BCA) codes for ion-material interactions, such as SRIM,
Tridyn, F-TRIDYN, and SDtrimSP, have been restricted to screened Coulomb potentials, even at low
energies. This limitation stems from the difficulty in numerically solving the Distance of Closest Approach
(DOCA) problem for more complex attractive-repulsive potentials. Traditional methods, including direct
n-body simulation or modifications to Newton’s method, are either computationally expensive or lack
guaranteed convergence for all potential types. We developed a solution to this problem using advanced
rootfinding techniques, specifically proxy rootfinding based on Chebyshev interpolation. While companion
matrix solvers offer a powerful approach, they are often inapplicable directly to attractive-repulsive
potentials that cannot be expressed in a monomial basis. Thanks to proxy rootfinding we addressed this by
using the zeros of a best-fit polynomial approximant as close approximations to the function’s zeros.
Utilizing the Chebyshev basis and grid provided significant advantages, including guarantees regarding
approximation quality, faster convergence, and the avoidance of Runge’s phenomenon. This allowed the
zeros of any real function on a bounded domain to be accurately found using the Chebyshev-Frobenius
companion matrix. Specifically, we demonstrated that an Adaptive Chebyshev Proxy Rootfinder with
Automatic Subdivision (ACPRAS), when combined with appropriate scaling functions, can successfully
overcome the numerical difficulties, particularly those related to scale, presented by attractive-repulsive
potentials. Our findings confirmed that these techniques make it possible to incorporate any physically
reasonable interatomic potential into any BCA code, guaranteeing the correctness of the resulting scattering
angle calculations, and effectively extending the range of validity of BCA codes to much much lower
energy (< 10 eV’s). Related publication: J. Drobny, D. Curreli, Enabling Attractive-Repulsive Potentials in
Binary-Collision-Approximation Monte-Carlo Codes for Ion-Surface Interactions, Materials Research
Express, 10, 126513 (2023).

As an extension of the geometrical and meshing capabilities of hPIC2, we developed a block-structured
nonuniform meshing technique enabling multi-scale plasma simulations of plasma sheaths and scrape-off
layers. The technique was easily extendable to implementation in hybrid Particle-in-Cell (PIC) schemes
that consider kinetic ions and Boltzmann electrons. The meshing scheme is designed to support large-scale
fusion plasma domains (spanning tens of meters) with a substantially reduced number of degrees-of-
freedom (DOF) compared to simulations employing a uniform mesh. We showed that the solution derived
at low DOFs maintains the same level of accuracy as solutions obtained from highly refined uniform
meshes, still maintaining particle noise under control. The meshing scheme could be equally applied to both
1D, 2D, and 3D plasma domains. This reduction in DOFs leads to a significant reduction in PIC
computational cost while keeping total count of computational particles the same for corresponding cases,
making it a valuable tool for cost-effective, multi-scale fusion plasma simulations. As part of this study we
demonstrated the feasibility to simulate full-SOL connection lengths (10-100’s of meters) and still retain
PIC exponential sampling at the boundaries using Particle-in-Cells. Related publication: M.F. Huq, V.V.

(3) Physics Investigations, Integrated PSI Workflow, and Validation Activities

A key enhancement introduced in F-TRIDYN was the inclusion of an explicit model for surface roughness
based on surface fractal dimension. We showed that surface roughness can significantly alter sputtering
yields and other ion-surface interaction quantities such as reflection/backscattering and implantation
profiles. The unique surface roughness model in F-TRIDYN allowed us to investigate the effects of
morphology on tungsten self-sputtering, a phenomenon of interest and concern in future MCF systems.
Related Publication: J. Drobny, D. Curreli, F-TRIDYN Simulations of Tungsten Self-Sputtering and
Applications to Coupling Plasma and Material Codes, Computational Materials Science, Vol. 149, Is.
15, 301306 (2018).

Developing a comprehensive integrated PSI workflow involves a wide range of multi-physics phenomena.
In collaboration with the PSI-2 team, we coupled our newly-developed coding tools with other codes
developed by the team in order to expand the multi-physics capabilities and include all PSI processes
relevant to MCF: (a) the gas implantation and its dynamic evolution below the divertor surface; (b) erosion
of wall material; (c) transport and re-deposition of the eroded impurities; and (d) the scrape-off layer plasma
including fuel ions and extrinsic impurities. These components were successfully integrated together to
predict changes in surface morphology and fuel recycling, and the effect of material erosion and re-
deposition in fuel retention. Integrated simulations for ITER-like parameters in a helium plasma
environment were obtained, focused on the response of the tungsten divertor. The model was also applied
to predicting the response of the tungsten surface pre-damaged by He plasma, to burning plasma operations.
This case demonstrated the capability to model the effect of sub-surface helium dynamics, which include
helium nucleation, clustering and the bursting of over-pressurized bubbles, its impact on fuel recycling as
well as the effect of sputtering on the surface evolution. Related Publications: A. Lasa, J. M. Canik, S.
Blondel, T. R. Younkin, D. Curreli, J. Drobny, P. Roth, M. Cianciosa, W. Elwasif, D. L. Green and B. D.
Wirth, Multi-physics modeling of the long-term evolution of helium plasma exposed surfaces, Physica
Scripta, T171, 014041 (2020); A. Lasa, S. Blondel, D. Bernholdt, J. Canik, M. Cianciosa, W. Elwasif, D.
Green, P. Roth, T. Younkin, D. Curreli, J. Drobny, B. Wirth, Integrated model predictions on the impact of
substrate damage on gas dynamics during ITER burning-plasma operations, Nuclear Fusion, Volume 61,
Number 11, (2021).

The integrated PSI workflow was then upgraded to incorporate time-dependence and two-way information
flow. This extension allowed the model to address dynamic recycling in transient scenarios, specifically,
the evolution of pre-damaged tungsten samples exposed to ELMy H-mode plasmas in the DIII-D DiMES.
Initial simulations explored the effect of ELM frequency; subsequent analyses investigated the impact of
the code-coupling frequency. These simulations started with SOLPS solutions converged to the inter-ELM
state, utilized ion impact energy and angles calculated by hPIC2, and incorporated an improved heat transfer
description in Xolotl. The model predicted that increasing the coupling time-step resulted in a 10%-20%
increase in particle fluxes and a corresponding decrease in heat fluxes. Compared to the first set of
simulations, the shallower impact angle led to lower reflection rates and significant deuterium implantation.

The higher and deeper fraction of implanted flux, particularly during ELMs, contributed to an increased
accumulation of D content in the W near-surface region. Related Publication: A. Lasa, J.-S. Park, J. Lore,
S. Blondel, D.E. Bernholdt, J.M. Canik, M. Cianciosa, J. Coburn, D. Curreli, W. Elwasif, J. Guterl, J.
Hoffman, J.M. Park, G. Sinclair and B.D. Wirth, Exploring the effect of ELM and code-coupling frequencies

An initial experimental validation of the magnetic presheath model was performed using 3D LIF (Laser
Induced Fluorescence) experimental data from the HELIX linear plasma device, a facility operated at West
Virginia University. The three-dimensional diagnostic was composed of three LIF beam lines along three
perpendicular directions, and was able to probe the magnetic presheath in its fully three-dimensional
structure and observe how it was modified by ion-neutral collisions. The velocity distributions of both ions
and neutrals showed that cross-field ion drifts do not result from entrainment of ions in a flowing neutral
background. Ion flows parallel to ExB arise and accelerate to as much as 0.2 Cs (Cs, Bohm acoustic speed)
within several ion gyroradii of the boundary surface, as predicted by the hPIC code run under the same
experimental conditions. Within measurement resolution, the onset of the ExB aligned flow occurs at the
same distance to the surface that ions begin to deflect from travel along magnetic field lines. hPIC particle-
in-cell simulations of the boundary region were compared to the experimental measurements. We found
that, in contrast to the classical collisionless Chodura model, collisional effects between the ions and the
non-flowing neutral population are essential to quantitatively predict the observed ion drift velocities. No
momentum coupling between ions and neutrals, separable from noise and other effects, was observed in
either signals. Related publication. D. S. Thompson, R. Khaziev, M. F. Henriquez, S. Keniley, E. E. Scime,
D. Curreli, Three-dimensional cross-field flows at the plasma-material interface in an oblique magnetic
field, Physics of Plasmas, 27 (7), 073511 (2020). The publication was selected as “Featured Article” in
Physics of Plasmas.
