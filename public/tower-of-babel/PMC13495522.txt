Exogenous melatonin mitigates vanadium toxicity in Brassica napus L.: a transcriptomic perspective
Authors: Latif Ullah Khan, Sana Basharat, Muhammad Umer, Enerand Mackon, Pingwu Liu, Muhammad Waseem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrassica napus L. is one of the world’s most important edible oil crops, and its productivity is severely affected by abiotic stresses, including heavy metal contamination. Vanadium (V) toxicity disrupts plant growth, impairs photosynthesis, and induces excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation. Melatonin (MT) is an emerging plant regulatory molecule known to enhance stress tolerance; however, its physiological and transcriptomic roles in mitigating V-induced toxicity in B. napus remain poorly understood.ResultsIn this study, B. napus seedlings were subjected to four treatments: control (CK), MT (100 µM), V (100 mg L⁻¹), and MT + V (MTV). V stress significantly inhibited seedling growth, biomass accumulation, chlorophyll content, chlorophyll fluorescence (Fv/Fm), and water status, while increasing oxidative damage and ROS accumulation. MT supplementation markedly alleviated V-induced phytotoxicity by improving growth performance, chlorophyll accumulation, antioxidant enzyme activities (POD, CAT, and APX), and ROS scavenging capacity. MT treatment also reduced overall V accumulation in seedlings under V stress conditions. Transcriptomic analysis identified numerous differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with stress responses, antioxidant regulation, photosynthesis, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis. Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) identified four key co-expression modules (turquoise, blue, brown, and yellow) strongly associated with MT-mediated stress responses. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses revealed significant enrichment of genes involved in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, antioxidant defense, and transporter-related pathways.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that MT alleviates V-induced toxicity in B. napus through coordinated physiological and transcriptomic regulation involving antioxidant defense, photosynthetic protection, and stress-responsive metabolic pathways. This study provides new insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying MT-mediated heavy metal stress tolerance and highlights the potential application of MT for improving crop resilience under vanadium-contaminated conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09251-w.

Introduction

Brassica napus L., commonly referred to as rapeseed, is a significant crop within the Brassicaceae family. In China, B. napus serves as the primary oilseed crop, contributing 20% to global production and covering an area of 7.5 million hectares. Despite this, the self-sufficiency rate for edible oil remains below 40% [1, 2]. Recently, various abiotic stresses, including heat, cold, drought, and heavy metal contamination, have negatively impacted B. napus production [3, 4]. Among these, heavy metal pollution is a predominant agricultural and environmental challenge of the 21st century [5, 6]. Vanadium (V), in particular, is a toxic heavy metal that poses a significant threat to both plant and human health [7]. Vanadium ranks as the fifth most abundant trace element in the Earth’s crust among transitional elements, with notable concentrations in the USA, South Africa, and China [8]. Globally, V production accounts for 57%, with China being the largest producer and consumer, encompassing approximately 26.5% of vanadium-contaminated soil in Southwest China [9, 10]. It is reported that V concentrations in the Earth’s crust range from 10 to 220 mg kg− 1; however, these levels are elevated in soils utilized by humans, necessitating reduction or elimination to prevent further transfer to plants and food chains consumed by humans and animals [11, 12]. Vanadium accumulation in various plant tissues induces permanent alterations, including reduced photosynthetic activity, stomatal closure, suppressed enzymatic activities, leaf necrosis, and cellular structural damage, ultimately leading to decreased crop yield [13, 14]. At concentrations of 35 mg L− 1 V in rice and 40 mg L− 1 in tomato, there is a noticeable increase in malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) accumulation, which subsequently impairs rice growth [8, 13]. An elevated V accumulation beyond the threshold level (40-55.8 mg L− 1) diminishes the production of essential nutrients in plants, thereby adversely affecting crop yield [4, 15]. High concentrations of V impede the physiological, phenotypic, and biochemical activities of plants, primarily due to the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), alterations in enzymatic activities, and changes in gene expression [11, 12]. Exogenous V application (40 and 80 mg L-1) significantly modifies root architecture and reduces root and stem fresh weight as well as fruit production [11, 12]. Similar findings were reported in chickpea (Cicer arietinum L.) at V 25 mg L− 1 [7]. However, given that it has been documented that vanadium can substantially inhibit the uptake of phosphorus by plants. Additionally, V exposure in humans can lead to symptoms such as weakness, diarrhea, nosebleeds, weight loss, and vomiting [15]. Consequently, managing V toxicity and accumulation in plants is crucial for promoting healthier plant growth and ensuring human safety.

Melatonin (MT) referred to as N-acetyl-5-methoxy-tryptamine, a low molecular weight organic compound ubiquitously found in a wide range of living organisms, including animals, bacteria, and plants [16–18]. Initially identified in 1958 within the pineal glands of cows [19], MT was later discovered in plants in 1995. Subsequent research has documented its presence in various crops, such as tomatoes, rice, and pepper, and in different plant parts, including leaves, roots, flowers, stems, fruits, and seeds, with concentrations ranging from approximately 5 to 14,500 pg g− 1 based on fresh weight [20–23]. MT significantly influences the physiological and biochemical properties of plants, affecting gene and enzyme regulation related to growth initiation, germination, sucrose biosynthesis, and crop production [20, 24]. It plays a crucial role in regulating numerous processes, including plant growth, flowering, circadian rhythms, fruit ripening, mitotic spindle formation, lateral root formation, and root regeneration [12, 20, 24]. Additionally, MT enhances the total chlorophyll and carotenoid content in plants and prevents chlorophyll degradation, thereby augmenting photosynthesis and regulating sugar and nitrogen metabolism [20, 25]. MT further improves photosynthetic efficiency by modulating the expression of genes such as PsaK, PsaG, PsaO, PsaH, PsaA, and PsaF in photosystem I, and PsbZ, PsbP, Psb28, PsbQ, PsbY, PsbE, and PsbO in photosystem II [26–28]. Moreover, MT serves as a significant anti-stress regulator and plant growth bio-stimulator, particularly under adverse environmental conditions, including ultraviolet radiation and various stressors such as heavy metals, salinity, water scarcity, cold, acid rain, drought, and heat stress [24–26, 28, 29].

Melatonin-mediated alleviation of heavy metal stress has been widely reported in several plant species under cadmium, aluminum, nickel, and other abiotic stress conditions. However, studies investigating the physiological and transcriptomic mechanisms underlying melatonin-mediated responses to V-toxicity in Brassica napus remain limited. In particular, the integration of antioxidant responses, WGCNA-based co-expression networks, and phenylpropanoid pathway regulation under MT-mediated V stress mitigation has not been comprehensively investigated in B. napus. Therefore, the present study aimed to explore the physiological, biochemical, and transcriptomic mechanisms associated with MT-mediated alleviation of V toxicity in B. napus seedlings.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

In this study, the B. napus genotype XiZiYuan (XZY) was used from our germplasm resources at the Sanya Nanfan Research Institute of Hainan University, Haikou, China. Prior to sowing, the seeds were surface sterilized by immersion in sodium hypochlorite [0.5% (v/v)] for 15 min, followed by five thorough rinses with deionized water. Approximately 30 seeds per treatment were placed in Petri dishes containing wet filter paper and deionized water to maintain moisture levels for germination. The dishes were then transferred to a growth chamber with a controlled environment maintained as follows: day/night cycle (16 h light/8 h dark), relative humidity 75% ± 1%, temperature (23–28 ± 1 °C), and the photon flux density was maintained at 820 µmol m−2s− 1.

After five days of germination, uniform seedlings were transferred to black hydroponic plastic pots (8.5 cm height, 10 cm top diameter, and 7 cm bottom diameter) enriched with Hoagland Solution (pH 5.6 ± 0.1). The nutrient solution was replenished every five days until plants reached the four-leaf stage (approximately 20 days after transfer to black pots), allowing adequate acclimation to the hydroponic system (Fig. S1).

Preliminary assays were conducted using vanadium (V) concentrations (0-200 mg L⁻¹) to determine the threshold causing growth inhibition in B. napus. Based on these results, 100 mg L⁻¹ V was selected, as it reduced fresh and dry weights by ~ 50% (Fig. S2a). Similarly, different melatonin (MT) concentrations (0–200 µM L⁻¹) were tested under constant V (100 mg L⁻¹) stress. A concentration of 100 µM L⁻¹ MT was selected due to its significant improvement in seedling growth under V stress (Fig. S2b). All preliminary experiments were conducted under identical growth conditions.

Seven days after transfer to Hoagland solution, seedlings were divided into two sets: one maintained in half-strength Hoagland solution (control), and the other exposed to 100 mg L⁻¹ V for 21 days. After 7 days of V exposure, stress symptoms became evident. Subsequently, seedlings were assigned to four treatments: (i) control (CK), (ii) melatonin (MT), (iii) vanadium stress (V), and (iv) melatonin + vanadium (MT + V = MTV). Melatonin (100 µmol L⁻¹) was prepared by dissolving in ethanol followed by dilution with distilled water. Following seven days of treatment, the phenotypic traits and chlorophyll pigments were recorded, and for further analyses of transcriptomic, molecular, and biochemical activities, the roots and shoots were separately stored at -80℃. All samples were collected with three independent biological replicates, and each include five technical replicates per biological replicate.

Measurement of vanadium accumulation

Plant leaves were washed, oven-dried at 60 °C, ground, and dry-ashed. Subcellular fractions obtained by differential centrifugation were dried on a hot plate. All samples were digested with concentrated H₂O₂ and HNO₃ (1:5, v/v) until clear. The digests were diluted to 10 mL with deionized water, and vanadium (V) content was quantified using graphite furnace atomic absorption spectrophotometry (GFAAS-GTA 120) at a wavelength of 240.8 nm. Method accuracy was validated using spike recovery tests, with recovery rates ranging from 92.3% to 96.7%, consistent with standard protocols for trace metal analysis. Analyses was performed in triplicate (with three biological replicates, each with five technical replications).

Plant biomass and relative water content (RWC) parameters

Plant biomass, defined as the whole plant’s fresh weight, along with the fresh and dry weights of roots and shoots, was measured using three uniformly growing seedlings for each treatment. Following the determination of fresh weight, the roots and shoots were immediately placed in an oven for 20 min at 105 °C. Root and shoot lengths were measured using a Vernier caliper. Chlorophyll fluorescence measurements were conducted on fully expanded upper leaves of B. napus. Quantification of oxidative stress markers: MDA, H2O2, and proline

Fresh leaves, approximately 0.1 g, from B. napus seedlings of each MT, V, MTV, and CK were utilized to estimate oxidative stress markers, including MDA, H2O2, and proline. The leaves were ground into a fine powder using a mortar and pestle with liquid nitrogen. The stress markers were quantified from the supernatant containing total soluble proteins, extracted using 100 mM PBS buffer (900 µL, pH 7.4), following the guidelines provided in the kits from Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China, for MDA (Cat # A003-3-1), H2O2 (Cat # A064-5-1), and proline (Cat # A107-1-1). Optical density absorption values were recorded at wavelengths of 405 nm, 560 nm, and 520 nm using a Lambda 25 UV/VIS Spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, USA) and calculated according to the formulas provided in the kits.

Determination of antioxidant enzyme accumulation

Leaves approximately 1 g, crushed with liquid nitrogen from MT, V, MTV, and CK (three biological replicates), were separately homogenized in PBS buffer (1 mL, pH 7.4) and centrifuged for 15 min at 10,000 rpm and 4 °C [160]. The supernatant was subsequently used for further analysis. The accumulation of SOD (CAT # A001-4-1), POD (CAT # A084-3-1), CAT (CAT # A007-1-1), and APX (CAT # A123-1-1) in B. napus leaves was assessed following the methodologies outlined in their commercial kits by, often provided by Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute at wavelengths of 550, 420, 405, and 290 nm, respectively, using a Lambda 25 UV/VIS Spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, USA), and calculated according to the formulas provided in the kits.

RNA extraction, library construction

Total RNA was extracted from leaves of MT, V, MTV, and CK using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (TIANGEN) kit. The purity and quality of the extracted RNA were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and a Nanodrop® spectrophotometer (Implen, CA, USA). Genomic DNA contamination was removed with DNase I, and cDNA was prepared following manufacturer’s protocol (QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit). The RNA sequencing libraries were generated using NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA; Cat. No. E7530L) following manufacturer’s recommendations and the Novogene personalized protocols. cDNA fragments of 200–300 bp were selected for library construction. The qualified libraries were pooled for RNA-Seq using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 kit components (Beijing Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd.). China.

Transcriptome analysis and assembly preparation

The reliability and quality of the sequenced data were assessed using fastp (version 0.23.1) [34], in accordance with the recommended methodology of Novogene transcriptome assembly. The B. napus reference genome assembly was retrieved from BnIR, Brassica napus multi-omics database (http://yanglab.hzau.edu.cn/BnIR/) [35]. Subsequently, the clean reads were aligned with B. napus reference genome by HISAT2 (2.0.5) software [36]. StringTie (https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie/) was used to prepare the assembly from the mapped libraries. The final assembly and annotation were generated using the StringTie merger gffcompare against the reference annotation of B. napus. The expression levels of mRNAs considered to identify significantly differentially expressed gene (DEGs) were predicted in terms of FPKM using the DESeq R package with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2(fold change) | ≥ 1. Raw read counts were generated using the featureCount program (https://rnnh.github.io/bioinfo-notebook/docs/featureCounts).

Gene functional annotation, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses of all putative mRNAs were performed using Eggnog-Mapper (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/). GO analysis results were classified into three groups: (i) molecular function (MF), (ii) cellular component (CC), and (iii) biological process (BP). Simultaneously, KEGG pathways were determined based on the adjusted P < 0.05 cutoff criteria. Plots were generated using ggplots in R.

Genes co-expression network analysis by WGCNA

The weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed at R (version 4.5.1) [37]. The whole genome genes with read counts were used for WGCNA analysis. Based on gene expression, the Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated. The soft threshold power was set at β = 14 and the scale-free topology index customized at R2 = 0.9. For module trait relationship analysis, the Pearson’s correlation coefficients between the modules and traits were assessed using the ‘cor’ function in R4.5.1 (http://www.r-project.org/) software based on FPKM values, and a correlation graph was drawn using the ggplot2 R package.

Expression validation by quantitative real-time PCR analysis

The cDNA synthesis was performed using the Vazyme HiScript II Q RT SuperMix Kit (Vazyme, China). RT-qPCR was conducted on 96-well plates following the methodology described by [38], and the kit protocol ChamQTM SYBR RT-qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co, Ltd, China), with readings taken at Lightcycler 96/Lightcycler480 Real-time System (Roche Diagnostics, UK). The primers utilized in the present study are listed in Table S1. The 2−ΔΔCT method by KJ Livak and TD Schmittgen [39], was employed to determine relative expression. Moreover, the correlation analysis was performed between RNA-seq and RT-qPCR expression data to validate the reliability and consistency of transcriptomic data.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, and the treatment means were compared using the least significant difference (LSD) post hoc test (p ≤ 0.05) using SPSS v22.0. Prior to ANOVA, homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test, and all datasets satisfied the assumptions required for parametric analyses. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8 and SRplot (Science and Research Online Plot). All experiments were conducted with three independent biological replicates, and each measurement (RT-qPCR, antioxidant enzyme assays, and pigment analysis) included five technical replicates per biological replicate.

Results

B. napus phenotype attributes under V stress and MT supplementation

B. napus seedlings were individually subjected to treatment with MT (100 µM) and V (100 mg L− 1). It was observed that MT enhanced the phenotypic growth of the seedlings, whereas V significantly inhibited growth, affecting root and shoot development as well as leaf number (Fig. 1a). Moreover, when MT was administered concurrently with V, it mitigated the phytotoxic effects induced by V and enhanced both phenotypic growth and seedling biomass (whole plant weight) compared to seedlings exposed solely to V stress (Fig. 1a-b). The whole-plant V content in the MTV group was significantly reduced by 66.79% compared to the V group (P < 0.001). The reduction was most pronounced in roots (72.3%), followed by stems (58.6%) and leaves (49.2%) (Fig. 1c). This suggests that MT can inhibit V uptake by roots and leaves, thereby promoting plant growth and development.

Analyses of B. napus seedlings phenotypic attributes

The application of V dose resulted in significant alterations in the vegetative growth and development of B. napus seedlings. It was observed that V treatment substantially decreased the seedling shoot length (SL; 93.2 mm), shoot fresh weight (SFW; 1.02 g), shoot dry weight (SDW; 0.34 g), root length (RL; 185.4 mm), root fresh weight (RFW; 1.12 g), root dry weight (RDW; 0.45 g), root relative moisture content (RRMC; 0.013%), shoot relative moisture content (SRMC; 0.011%), and leaf relative water content (LRWC; 30.326%) in comparison with the CK treatment (Fig. 2a–i). Conversely, MT supplementation significantly improved seedling growth and moisture-related parameters under V stress. Compared with uniformly V-treated seedlings, the MTV treatment enhanced SL (127.5 mm), SFW (1.57 g), SDW (0.78 g), RL (222.9 mm), RFW (1.67 g), RDW (0.78 g), RRMC (0.028%), SRMC (0.018%), and LRWC (52.831%) (Fig. 2a–i). Additional phenotypic parameters, including root, shoot, and leaf widths, as well as leaf length analyses under V stress and MT supplementation, are presented in (Fig. S3). The application of MT significantly alleviated the toxic effects of V, thereby promoting the growth-related attributes of Brassica seedlings (Fig. 1a-i).

SPAD index, photosynthesis, and leaf gas exchange parameters

The supplementation of MT resulted in a significant increase in the accumulation of chlorophyll contents, specifically Chl a and b, as well as carotenoid contents, by 159.67%, 140.67%, and 150.81%, respectively (Fig. 3a-c), in V-induced seedlings. In contrast, these pigment contents were reduced by V exposure by 59.23%, 61.00%, and 62.22%, respectively, compared to CK (Fig. 3a-c). MT effectively mitigated the toxic effects of V on plant chlorophyll pigments and enhanced chlorophyll content. Additionally, MT supplementation improved the SPAD index by 220.33%, which was reduced by V exposure by 52.57% (Fig. 3d). Moreover, V stress led to a reduction in leaf gas exchange parameters, including Pn, Ci, Gs, and Tr, by 51.89%, 55.08%, 42.86%, and 59.74%, respectively, compared to CK. However, MT supplementation supported these parameters by 45.47%, 41.97%, 47.88%, and 63.46%, respectively, compared to V-induced stressed seedlings (Fig. 3f-h). Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, such as Fo, Fv, Fm, Fv/Fm, and Fv/Fo, were quantified in MT, V, MTV-treated seedlings, and CK (Fig. S4). The results indicated that the maximum quantum efficiency of PSII, Fv/Fm, was increased by MT supplementation by 145.81% compared to V-stressed seedlings, whereas it was reduced by V exposure by 61.20% compared to CK (Fig. 3i).

Vanadium induced oxidative stress and proline accumulation

Following a seven-day exposure to V stress, the oxidative stress markers hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and malondialdehyde (MDA) were measured in the fresh leaves of B. napus seedlings. Compared to the CK group, H₂O₂ and MDA contents in the V group increased significantly by 9.2-fold and 6.51-fold, respectively (p < 0.001). Compared to the V group, these levels in the MTV group decreased significantly by 5.3-fold and 5.2-fold, respectively (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4a, b). Proline accumulation, which is involved in the scavenging of free radicals or reactive oxygen species (ROS), was significantly elevated under V stress by 15.11-fold compared to CK. However, the MT amendment decreased the proline content by 5.2-fold relative to seedlings treated with both MT and V (Fig. 4c), suggesting that MT plays a crucial role in mitigating oxidative stress and scavenging free radicals.

Analyses of antioxidant enzymes and genes activities

The accumulation of antioxidant enzymes in the leaves of B. napus seedlings was assessed spectrophotometrically to determine the potential role of MT in the antioxidant defense system against V. The levels of antioxidant enzymes, specifically SOD, POD, CAT, and APX, were significantly reduced by V, with decreases of 49.65%, 59.07%, 63.86%, and 55.34%, respectively, compared to CK. In contrast, MT supplementation markedly increased the accumulation of these enzymes by 329.16%, 266.69%, 247.21%, and 217.69%, respectively, relative to seedlings subjected solely to V stress (Fig. 5a-c).

Transcriptome assembly and data analyses under V stress and MT supplementation

To elucidate the mechanism of action and the underlying transcriptional dynamics of MT amendment in response to V stress, transcriptome sequencing was performed using leaf tissues collected from CK, MT, V, and MTV-treated seedlings. In total, 12 cDNA libraries were constructed and pooled for sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, resulting in approximately 649.353 million raw reads. Following the removal of sequencing errors, ambiguous nucleotides, and false sequences, and after assessing the GC content distribution, a total of approximately 639.217994 million clean reads were retained. On average, the clean reads achieved 97.65% (Q20) and 93.72% (Q30) scores, with a GC content of 40%, indicating the high quality of the sequencing data (Table S2). The filtered clean reads (~ 639 million reads) were mapped to the B. napus reference genome, and subsequent alignment allowed for the measurement of gene expression to obtain raw counts for all genes across different samples.

Analyses of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) under V stress and MT supplementation

Principal component analysis revealed clear separation among treatments, with PC1 (24.1%) distinguishing treatment effects (Fig. S5). V-stressed samples clustered distinctly, indicating strong transcriptomic perturbation. MT treatment alone formed a separate cluster, reflecting its regulatory role in gene expression. Notably, MTV resulted in a unique cluster, distinct from both control and stress groups, suggesting that melatonin induces an adaptive transcriptional reprogramming rather than fully restoring the control state.

To elucidate the mechanism by which MT mediates defense against V-induced stress in B. napus, DEGs were analyzed across MT, V, and MTV treatments in comparison to CK (Fig. 6). A total of 1767 DEGs (|log2FoldChange| ≥1 and P < 0.05) were identified. Among them, 741, 789, and 237 DEGs were found in the CK vs. MT, CK vs. V, and CK vs. MTV. There were 126 overlapping DEGs across comparisons (Fig. 6a), resulting in 1641 unique DEGs. A substantial number of DEGs were expressed in V-stressed plants compared to CK, followed by MT, with MTV exhibiting the fewest DEGs. This suggests that M activates numerous genes to counteract V stress. Conversely, the reduced levels of gene expression in MT alone and in combination with V (MTV) indicate that MT significantly enhances seedling immunity against V stress and mitigates V toxic effects by maintaining gene expression levels akin to those of CK. The total number of downregulated DEGs (1002 DEGs) across all combinations exceeded that of upregulated genes (765 DEGs) (Fig. 6a). The aforementioned DEGs expression among MT, V, and MTV compared to CK is also depicted in an expression heatmap (Fig. 6b) and a volcanic plot (Fig. 6c).

Hierarchical clustering with prominent topological overlapping genes was employed to identify the top four expression network modules: turquoise, blue, brown, and yellow. Genes involved in phenylpropanoid pathway

The phenylpropanoid pathway in plants serves as a critical metabolic route for the transformation of the amino acid phenylalanine into various essential secondary metabolites, including flavonoids, lignins, and sinapines. These secondary metabolites are integral to the plant’s defense mechanisms against a range of biotic and abiotic stresses, thereby playing a vital role in plant growth and development. A total of 24 DEGs within the phenylpropanoid pathway were predominantly involved in the three metabolic pathways (flavonoids, lignins, and sinapines) and exhibited significant upregulation in response to MT, V, or MTV (Fig. 9). Among the 24 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), four are implicated in the biosynthesis of flavanols, specifically quercetin, kaempferol, and myricetin. Among these four DEGs, namely PAL, PTAL, CYP73A, and FLS, exhibited significantly elevated expression levels in seedlings subjected to M supplementation in conjunction with V stress. This observation suggests that MT plays a crucial role in upregulating these genes to mitigate reactive oxygen species (ROS) production induced by V stress (Fig. 9).

The correlation analysis between RNA-seq and RT-qPCR expression data was performed to validate the reliability and consistency of transcriptomic results. Linear regression analysis demonstrated a positive correlation between RNA-seq and RT-qPCR datasets across the selected differentially expressed genes under MT, V, and MTV treatments. Discussion

The present study sought to investigate the melatonin (MT)-mediated defense mechanism in B. napus against vanadium (V) toxicity. Plants inherently mitigate reactive oxygen species (ROS) damage induced by heavy metal stress by enhancing their antioxidant systems; however, when this system is compromised, the application of exogenous compounds with substantial antioxidant capabilities can bolster the plant defense system under various stress conditions [16]. MT is an exogenous compound with notable antioxidant properties that enhances plant immunity, augments stress resistance, functions as a signaling molecule, and upregulates stress-resistant genes and endogenous ME levels in stress environments [19]. Furthermore, recent studies have indicated that MT plays a significant protective role against abiotic stresses in various plants, such as kiwifruit kiwifruit [40], rubber tree [41], and tomato [27]. In this study, WGCNA identified the turquoise, blue, brown, and yellow modules, which were significantly enriched for genes involved in phenylpropanoid synthesis (e.g., PAL, CYP73A), antioxidant enzymes (e.g., POD, CAT), and heavy metal transport (Fig. 7). This aligns with the physiological findings that MT enhances antioxidant capacity and inhibits V uptake, indicating that MT mitigates V toxicity through a coordinated ‘transcriptional regulation - physiological response’ network.

Preventing V uptake by roots and its translocation to aerial parts is essential to safeguard crops from V-induced toxicity. Our findings demonstrated that V accumulation severely impaired brassica growth, as evidenced by reduced plant biomass (roots, shoots, and leaves), whereas MT supplementation significantly enhanced brassica growth and development (Figs. 1b and 3a-f). Most published research suggests that heavy metals, such as Ni, Cd, and V, stress in tomato and rice seedlings can inhibit growth and biomass (root and shoot) production, whereas MT supplementation can reinforce these growth traits [5, 12, 28].

Heavy metal toxicity leads to a reduction in photosynthetic activity, directly damages the system [42], lowers the CO2 exchange [43, 44], and can cause stomatal closure [45, 46]. Plant growth is positively correlated with photosynthetic activity; a higher photosynthesis rate corresponds to improved plant growth and vice versa. Photosynthesis is directly correlated with chlorophyll and carotenoid accumulation [47]. The high accumulation of V in B. napus significantly decreased chlorophyll a, b, carotenoid, SPAD index, Pn, intercellular CO2 concentration, Gs, Tr, maximum quantum of PSII, and Fv/Fm levels in the present investigation. Notably, MT supplementation significantly improved the content of these photosynthetic pigments and parameters in B. napus seedlings (Fig. 3a-i). Our results are consistent with findings in various crops, including rice, mustard, and chickpea [6, 7, 11, 48], where V accumulation decreased and MT significantly increased the photosynthetic pigments and parameters. The results revealed that MT effectively mitigated V toxicity by supporting the photosynthetic machinery, thereby enhancing B. napus seedling growth (Fig. 1b). This is consistent with the physiological observations of increased Fv/Fm ratio (Fig. 3i) and inhibited chlorophyll degradation, suggesting that MT maintains photosynthetic efficiency by regulating the expression of photosynthesis system genes.

Plants exposed to heavy metal stress may encounter various metabolic disorders, leading to the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress, which can damage cellular membranes and components such as DNA, proteins, and lipids, ultimately resulting in cell death [11, 49, 50]. In the current study, V exposure significantly increased oxidative stress markers, including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), malondialdehyde (MDA), and proline (Fig. 4a-c). This elevated accumulation of chemicals induced oxidative damage in B. napus seedlings, consistent with observations in mustard and rice [6, 11]. Conversely, MT markedly reduced the accumulation of these stress markers (Fig. 4a-c). Our findings align with previous studies demonstrating MT-mediated reductions in ROS, MDA, and proline accumulation in C. cathayensis [51], Moringa oleifera [52], Peach fruit [53], Citrullus lanatus [54], Cucumis sativus [55], and Stevia rebaudiana [56] under various environmental conditions. Thus, our results indicate that MT alleviates oxidative damage, stabilizes membrane integrity, and mitigates ROS production induced by V toxicity.

Following the overproduction of ROS due to heavy abiotic stress, plants activate antioxidant enzymes to neutralize these free radicals. MT is recognized as a universal antioxidant due to its ability to enhance the plant’s antioxidant defense system and detoxify ROS accumulation [19, 57]. In this study, MT significantly increased the accumulation of antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX), along with their relative gene expression (Figs. 5A-H). Our results are consistent with previous research on various plants, where MT effectively upregulates antioxidant enzyme activity under abiotic stress [51, 58]. Together, these results demonstrate that MT provides a dual defense against oxidative damage: directly neutralizing ROS and stimulating enzymatic detoxification pathways.

To investigate the potential mechanism of MT action against V toxicity in B. napus, we conducted RNA sequencing analyses on CK, MT, V, and MTV treated seedlings. Our results demonstrated that MT significantly mitigated V toxicity by modulating gene expression. The analysis of DEGs using a Venn diagram (Fig. 6d) identified five common genes across CK, MT, V, and MTV treatments, with two DEGs significantly upregulated by MT (Fig. 6e). In the WGCNA analysis, DEGs were categorized into four significant modules: turquoise, blue, brown, and yellow. Notably, the majority of DEGs in these modules were uniformly upregulated by MT, both alone and in combination with V stress, while they were downregulated by V toxicity (Fig. 7d-g). These findings suggest that MT mitigates V toxicity at the molecular level. Gene ontology (GO) analyses revealed that stress-responsive genes were predominantly upregulated by MT (Fig. 8) (Table S3 – S4).

In the KEGG analyses, it was observed that the DEGs within the phenylpropanoid pathways were significantly upregulated by MT (Fig. 9). Flavonoids play crucial roles in various processes, including plant–microbe interactions, pigmentation, development, redox and UV protection, and the regulation of auxin transport [24–26, 59]. Notably, flavonoids serve as essential antioxidants capable of scavenging stress-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) in plants [60]. This is consistent with the observed higher accumulation of H2O2 in the V-treatment and its reduced accumulation in the M treatment. The expression of four genes involved in flavonoid synthesis, specifically PAL, CYP73A, and FLS, was significantly upregulated by MT and downregulated by V, as significantly enriched in the KEGG pathway (Fig. 9). Our findings suggest that MT may alleviate V toxicity in B. napus seedlings by reducing H2O2 concentration and ROS production through the regulation of genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis. MT upregulates genes like PAL and CYP73A to promote flavonoid synthesis, which may alleviate toxicity by chelating intracellular V ions to reduce damage to organelles and by scavenging ROS to mitigate oxidative stress. These genes are involved in lignin and related secondary metabolite biosynthesis pathways that may contribute to stress adaptation and antioxidant responses. However, because lignin, sinapine, and related phenolic metabolites were not directly quantified in the present study, the functional consequences of these transcriptional changes remain speculative and require further metabolomic validation.

Concurrently, enhanced lignin synthesis (upregulated CAD, POD genes) may strengthen the cell wall barrier, reducing the efficiency of V transmembrane transport (Fig. 9). These processes likely act synergistically to enhance plant V tolerance. Additionally, many DEGs associated with lignin and sinapine biosynthesis were significantly upregulated by MT supplementation and downregulated by V toxicity (Fig. 9). The coordinated regulation of antioxidant defense, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and photosynthesis-related pathways by MT suggests the involvement of interconnected stress-responsive signaling networks rather than isolated pathway regulation. MT may function through modulation of ROS signaling, transcription factor activity, and hormonal crosstalk to orchestrate broad transcriptional reprogramming under V stress. However, the identification of specific upstream master regulators controlling these pathways requires further functional and molecular investigations.

In conclusion, V stress can induce oxidative stress, increase ROS production, and decrease the accumulation of photosynthetic pigments, whereas MT supports plant growth and mitigates the toxic effects of V. The results should be discussed in relation to previous studies and working hypotheses, considering their implications in the broadest possible context. Future research directions are also highlighted. Despite the significant protective effects of MT against V-induced toxicity observed in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the present investigation was conducted only at the seedling stage of B. napus; therefore, the effectiveness of MT in alleviating V toxicity during reproductive growth stages and its impact on yield-related traits require further validation under greenhouse and field conditions. Second, although transcriptomic analyses identified several stress-responsive pathways and transporter-related genes, the expression patterns and functional roles of V transporter families, including ABC and NRAMP transporters, were not comprehensively characterized. Although MT treatment reduced overall vanadium accumulation and alleviated oxidative damage in B. napus seedlings, the present study did not specifically distinguish whether MT-mediated protection resulted from reduced vanadium uptake, altered root-to-shoot translocation, intracellular sequestration, or enhanced physiological tolerance. Future studies integrating subcellular localization, protein–protein interaction analyses, and yeast two-hybrid assays may provide deeper mechanistic insight into MT-mediated regulation of V transport and detoxification. Finally, the practical agricultural application of MT requires optimization of application methods, including foliar spray and soil drench approaches, as well as determination of optimal dosages under field environments. These future investigations will further strengthen the understanding and practical utilization of MT for improving heavy metal stress tolerance in crops.

Conclusions

MT has the capacity to inhibit V uptake, thereby controlling its accumulation and preventing it from reaching toxic levels. Our findings indicate that MT substantially reduce ROS production induced by V toxicity and notably enhances plant biomass, photosynthetic systems, antioxidant enzyme activity, and gene expression, thereby counteracting V-phytotoxic effects. Transcriptomic analyses revealed that exogenous MT supplementation safeguarded the antioxidant system by regulating genes involved in ROS metabolism, photosynthesis, and the biosynthesis of phenylalanine, flavonoids, lignin, and sinapine. The WGCNA identified key DEGs within various modules, whose expression was significantly up-regulated by MT in response to V toxicity. These results suggest that MT can enhance V stress tolerance either directly or indirectly by scavenging H2O2 accumulation in B. napus plants. Our findings are instrumental not only in elucidating the role of MT in V stress toxicity but also in providing new insights into the potential application of MT against abiotic stresses in B. napus.
