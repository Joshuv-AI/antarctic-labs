Intraoperative Tissue Oxygenation and Postoperative Creatinine Rise: Interpreting the Microcirculatory Context
Authors: Bedirhan B. Bayıcı, Fatih Kızılyel
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the recent article by Banerjee et al. entitled “A Prospective Observational Study on Role of Intraoperative Renal & Muscle Oxygen Saturation on Post-Operative Serum Creatinine Level in Patients Undergoing Cardiac Surgery on Cardiopulmonary Bypass” [1]. The authors should be commended for investigating the potential role of intraoperative near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS)-derived renal (SrO2) and thenar muscle (SmO2) oxygen saturation measurements in predicting postoperative renal function changes following cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB).

In this prospective observational study of 55 patients undergoing elective cardiac surgery with CPB, a decrease in SrO2 greater than 20% from baseline was found to significantly predict postoperative rise in serum creatinine (ΔsCr), with an AUROC of 0.921. Additionally, a 15% decrease in SmO2 from baseline demonstrated predictive value, and a postoperative rise in serum creatinine occurred in 43.6% of patients [1]. These findings contribute to the growing body of literature exploring real-time intraoperative markers of renal perfusion.

However, several methodological considerations may influence the interpretation of these results. The study endpoint was defined as a postoperative rise in serum creatinine of at least 0–0.3 mg/dL [1]. While even minimal creatinine changes have been associated with adverse outcomes in prior studies [2], serum creatinine kinetics in the immediate postoperative period following CPB are influenced by hemodilution, altered distribution volume, and systemic inflammatory response [3]. As such, early postoperative ΔsCr may reflect transient volume and perfusion shifts rather than established structural renal injury, particularly in the absence of standardized AKI classifications such as KDIGO criteria [4].

In addition, patient selection based on a skin-to-kidney depth of less than 4 cm, as required for reliable NIRS signal acquisition, may introduce selection bias by excluding individuals with increased tissue depth [1]. This is clinically relevant, as patients with obesity, diabetes mellitus, or other cardiometabolic risk factors–who are inherently at greater risk of CPB-associated AKI–may have been underrepresented. Consequently, the reported predictive performance of SrO2 and SmO2 may not be generalizable to higher-risk surgical cohorts.

Furthermore, intraoperative tissue oxygen desaturation may not exclusively represent regional renal ischemia. SmO2 reflects peripheral microcirculatory oxygen delivery and extraction dynamics that are influenced by multiple systemic variables including hemoglobin concentration, mean arterial pressure, cardiac output, systemic vascular resistance, and CPB flow characteristics. In the absence of adjustment for indexed oxygen delivery (DO2) or perfusion pressure, it remains unclear whether NIRS-detected desaturation reflects organ-specific hypoxia or broader alterations in systemic microvascular perfusion [5,6].

In this context, the observed association between SmO2 decline and postoperative creatinine rise may potentially represent CPB-associated microcirculatory dysfunction rather than isolated renal ischemia. Peripheral NIRS-derived desaturation may therefore serve as a surrogate marker of systemic microvascular uncoupling between oxygen delivery and utilization during CPB [5,6]. Future investigations incorporating standardized AKI definitions alongside oxygen delivery–indexed perfusion parameters may help clarify whether intraoperative NIRS signals reflect kidney-specific hypoxic insult or global microcirculatory impairment.

We congratulate the authors for their valuable contribution and believe that further research in this area may enhance our understanding of the relationship between intraoperative tissue oxygenation and postoperative renal outcomes in cardiac surgery.
