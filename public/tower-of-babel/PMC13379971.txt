Putative buffering roles of two-way social support and psychological resilience in the association between nurse-patient conflict and situational emotional response: a cross-sectional correlational study among Chinese nursing interns
Authors: Rui Guan, Yasha Zhao, Jiamin Liu, Zhaoxia Yan, Panpan Li, Fangli Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNurse-patient conflict acts as a common stressor for nursing interns during clinical training. Existing research fails to empirically verify the synergistic buffering pathways of two-way social support and psychological resilience on interns’ situational emotional responses in Chinese clinical settings. This study aimed to describe interns’ emotional response status under nurse-patient conflicts and explore relevant associated factors.MethodsA cross-sectional correlational design was adopted. A total of 423 eligible nursing interns with conflict experience were recruited from six Henan hospitals via convenience sampling between August and October 2025. Three validated scales (ERES, Brief Two-Way Social Support Scale, CD-RISC) were adopted for data collection, with Pearson correlation, multiple linear regression and SEM used for statistical analysis.ResultsThe average situational emotional response score was 124.49 ± 21.73; situational emotional perception obtained the lowest subscore. Two-way social support (r = 0.682, p < 0.01) and psychological resilience (r = 0.512, p < 0.01) were positively correlated with emotional response. Conflict frequency, two-way social support and psychological resilience served as independent associated predictors, and the SEM confirmed their positive associative pathways.ConclusionNursing interns showed moderately high situational emotional response but weak emotional perception. Frequent nurse-patient conflicts were negatively correlated with emotional functioning, whereas two-way social support and psychological resilience served as protective correlates. Based on cross-sectional data, we recommend targeted emotion perception training and regular mutual support channels for interns; systematic intervention schemes need subsequent verification.Clinical trialNot applicable.

Introduction

Clinical internship is not only a pivotal nexus bridging nursing education and clinical practice but also a core developmental stage for nursing interns. During this period, interns internalize classroom-based theoretical knowledge into hands-on clinical competencies, while simultaneously cultivating professional identity and nurturing ethical literacy [1]. Unlike standardized, low-risk simulation training conducted in academic settings, authentic clinical environments are characterized by diverse patient needs, dynamically evolving and complex clinical conditions, and a high-intensity, time-pressured caregiving rhythm. Compounded by inherent limitations—such as limited clinical experience, underdeveloped communication skills, and unclear professional role identification—nursing interns are especially vulnerable to nurse–patient conflict when confronted with unexpected situations in clinical interactions [2].

In this study, nurse–patient conflict is defined as any antagonistic interaction between nursing interns and patients or their family members, including verbal aggression, emotional confrontation, and physical violence [3]. Some prior research indicates that up to 90% of nursing interns experience violent incidents during their internships, with patients and their families being the primary perpetrators [4, 5]. Such conflicts are closely linked to interns’ acute psychological reactions, increased job burnout and reduced professional identity [6]. Notably, within the collectivist cultural context of Asia, nurses—including interns—often suppress the expression of negative emotions to preserve interpersonal harmony and organizational stability [7, 8]. While this culturally embedded norm serves an adaptive social function, it may impair the timeliness of emotional awareness and regulation in resource-constrained, high-stress clinical environments—thereby heightening interns’ sensitivity to conflict and increasing the risk of escalation.

The situational emotional response ability, as defined in this study, refers to the capacity of nursing students to rapidly perceive emotions, rationally regulate emotional responses, and express emotions constructively within typical nurse–patient conflict scenarios. Fundamentally, it is a context-specific competence essential for effective conflict resolution and delivery of high-quality nursing care [9, 10]. Substantial evidence shows that strong situational emotional response ability is associated with maintained cognitive stability during high-pressure nurse–patient conflicts and a lower likelihood of emotionally driven confrontations. Additionally, this competence correlates with improved empathy for patients’ emotional needs and de-escalated hostile atmospheres, as well as reduced risks of violent conflict escalation [11–13]. Therefore, improving nursing interns’ situational emotional response ability plays an important role in alleviating nurse-patient conflicts and optimizing clinical nursing practice.

However, the current research has notable limitations. First, the assessment tools predominantly rely on general emotional intelligence scales [14], lacking specialized scales tailored to the high-stress, high-time-pressure, and high-role-tension context of nurse-patient conflicts.

This deficiency constrains both the accuracy and ecological validity of existing measurements. Second, the putative underlying mechanisms of action remain inadequately explored. While existing studies indicate that nurse-patient conflict constitutes a core stressor during the clinical practicum, that social support is associated with emotional coping efficacy [15], that psychological resilience is linked to relief from conflict–induced psychological injury [16], and that social support systems are positively related to the development of psychological resilience [17], the interactive associations of these three factors on the situational emotional response in the setting of nurse-patient conflict—particularly the interplay between the hypothesized stress-buffering pathway and the resource-gain pathway—have not yet been empirically examined among nursing interns [18–20].

The Chinese version of the Situational Emotional Response Scale (ERES), a context-specific instrument, has been validated to exhibit satisfactory reliability and validity among nursing interns [21], thus addressing certain limitations inherent in general emotional intelligence scales. Building upon this foundation, the present study employed a cross-sectional correlational design to address gaps in the empirical evidence, with the following objectives: (1) to delineate the current status and dimensional distribution characteristics of the situational emotional response among nursing interns exposed to nurse-patient conflicts; and (2) to explore relevant associative patterns between the core study variables. The findings of this study are expected to identify deficiencies in situational emotional response ability training for nursing interns and to provide evidence-based guidance for reforming clinical nursing education and constructing targeted support systems for clinical practice.

Methods

Aim

This cross-sectional correlational study aimed to: (1) characterize the current status of situational emotional response among nursing interns following nurse-patient conflicts; and (2) identify the independent and synergistic factors associated with situational emotional response through statistical analysis.

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional correlational design.

Participants

From August to October 2025, participants were recruited from six hospitals in Henan Province. Eligible subjects were nursing interns who had encountered nurse-patient conflicts within the previous month, with standardized objective inclusion and exclusion criteria formulated as follows: (1) Full-time nursing students receiving formal clinical internship training; (2) Had completed no less than one month of ward rotation in the surveyed hospital and maintained routine clinical practice throughout data collection; (3) Had encountered verbal disputes, emotional confrontations or physical altercations with patients or their families in the previous month, consistent with the definition of nurse-patient conflict defined in this study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Nursing trainees with cognitive or emotional dysfunction incapable of independently understanding and completing electronic questionnaires; (2) Administrative nursing interns and offline trainees without direct ward clinical practice; (3) Nursing students who had finished complete full-cycle clinical rotations in other medical facilities before participating in this survey.

Sample

The eligible participants of this study were nursing interns who had experienced nurse-patient conflicts within the past month, representing a subgroup population with clearly defined inclusion criteria. A unified roster of eligible individuals was not established across the participating institutions. Furthermore, due to the frequent ward rotations and highly dynamic scheduling of nursing interns, none of the participating hospitals maintained a real-time, comprehensive directory of all interns. Consequently, the conditions required for a complete sampling frame in probability sampling could not be met.

Considering the characteristics of the target population and the practical constraints inherent in multi-center clinical research, this study employed a convenience sampling method. Study participants were recruited from six hospitals in Henan Province, each of which had a dedicated internship management department.

Prior to the initiation of the study, the nursing departments of the six participating hospitals estimated that the total number of nursing interns expected to be on duty during the study period was approximately 1,520.

Given that the inclusion criterion for this study was “having experienced nurse-patient conflicts within the past month”—a condition that dynamically changes over time—a dedicated screening question was embedded in the electronic questionnaire. All on-duty interns completed self-assessment to report whether they had encountered the nurse-patient conflict defined in this study. Eligibility screening was conducted weekly by the internship coordinators at each hospital among the on-duty interns throughout the entire study period. A total of 460 interns who met the inclusion criteria were identified. Among them, 20 interns could not be contacted due to objective reasons, such as completion of their internship or leave of absence. Ultimately, questionnaires were distributed to the 440 eligible interns, and all invitees agreed to participate in the study.

Following the collection of the questionnaires, 17 invalid responses were excluded according to the aforementioned criteria. A total of 423 valid questionnaires were finally included, yielding a valid response rate of 96.14%.

The sample size was calculated using the formula n = Z²P(1-P)/d² [22], where n is the sample size, Z is the statistic corresponding to the confidence level, P is the expected proportion, and d is the permissible sampling error. Referring to the study by Sun Xiaoxiong et al. [21], a “total score ≥ 120 points” was used as the criterion for meeting the standard of situational emotional response ability. Based on the normal distribution of scores, the estimated attainment rate was approximately 47.2%. To ensure an adequate sample size, a conservative estimate was adopted by setting the expected proportion P = 45%. A 95% confidence level, which is common in medical research, was used, corresponding to Z = 1.96. Following the precision selection principle recommended by Naing et al., d was set at ± 5%. An anticipated sample attrition rate of 5% was considered. The calculated minimum required sample size was 408.42, which was rounded up to 409. The final sample size met the requirements of the study design.

Data collection

This study was led by the Director of the Nursing Department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University in collaboration with six other hospitals within Henan Province. First, the inclusion and exclusion criteria for nursing interns were defined, the content of the electronic questionnaire was designed, and a unified completion protocol was established. A standardized training session was conducted for the designated coordinators from each participating hospital to clarify the specific requirements for questionnaire distribution, nursing intern screening, and completion guidance. Following the training, the electronic questionnaire was distributed via the “Wenjuanxing” (an online questionnaire survey platform) to the hospital coordinators, who then guided the nursing interns at their respective institutions to complete the questionnaire according to the established standards. To ensure data quality, the questionnaire was configured to allow only one submission per IP address and was designed to be fully anonymous throughout the process to protect nursing interns’ privacy. Upon data collection, two researchers holding master’s degrees performed cross-verification of the questionnaires. After confirmation of accuracy, the data were entered. Concurrently, invalid questionnaires—specifically those with logical inconsistencies in responses or a completion time of less than 120 s—were excluded to ensure the validity of the data for subsequent analysis.

Measures

Collection of general information

Given that the factors associated with nursing interns’ situational emotional response ability in nurse–patient conflict remain unclear, this study incorporated demographic and internship background variables to control for potential confounders and explore underlying determinants—thereby providing a foundation for future research and clinical interventions. The questionnaire used in this study was self-developed based on a literature review and group discussion. It included the following items: gender, age (years), educational background, whether the participant was an only child, place of residence, type of educational institution, number of systematic emotion management and communication training sessions received during schooling, whether the participant had engaged in simulated nurse-patient conflict training prior to the internship, total duration of the internship, average weekly internship hours, frequency of nurse-patient conflicts experienced in the past month, number of times the clinical instructor provided targeted feedback on the interns’ emotion management, whether the assigned department had established guidelines or protocols for handling nurse-patient conflicts, whether the participant had volunteer service or part-time work experience outside of the internship period, and subjective perception of current clinical internship stress.

Situational emotional response scale

The scale was originally developed by Mayor-Silva et al. [10] and later translated into Chinese and revised by Sun Xiaoxiong et al. [21]. It is primarily used to assess the emotional skill performance of nursing interns in conflict situations with patients during clinical practice. Different from the general emotional traits measured by conventional emotional intelligence scales [14], this scale evaluates scenario-specific emotional responses of nursing interns facing nurse-patient conflicts [21]. The scale consists of 29 items across four dimensions: situational use of emotion, situational expression of emotion, situational regulation of emotion, and situational perception of emotion. A 5‑point Likert scale is used for scoring, with total scores ranging from 29 to 145. Higher scores indicate better emotional response. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.960.

Brief two-way social support scale

The scale was developed by Obst et al. [23] and subsequently translated and culturally adapted into Chinese by Cui et al. [24]. It comprises four dimensions: receiving emotional support, providing emotional support, receiving instrumental support, and providing instrumental support, with a total of 12 items. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “completely disagree” (1 point) to “completely agree” (5 points) for each item, resulting in a total possible score ranging from 12 to 60. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.929.

Connor-Davidson resilience scale

Developed by Connor and Davidson [25] and subsequently translated and culturally adapted into Chinese by Yu [26], this scale was used to measure an individual’s ability to cope with stressors. It comprises three dimensions: optimism, strength, and tenacity, with a total of 25 items. Responses are recorded using a 5–point Likert scale (0 = never, 4 = always), yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 100. A higher score indicates a greater level of psychological resilience. The total score was categorized as follows: 0–56 indicates a low level, 57–70 indicates a moderate level, and 71–100 indicates a high level. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.910.

Data analysis

This study employed a stepwise analytical strategy: First, univariate analyses (including t-tests, one-way ANOVA, and chi-square tests) were conducted to screen for factors significantly associated with the outcome; second, Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine bivariate associations among key variables; third, multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors; finally, a SEM was constructed—grounded in both the stress-buffering model and the resource-gain theory—to test hypothesized pathways and the synergistic associative pattern among nurse–patient conflict exposure, two-way social support, psychological resilience, and situational emotional response. All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS 26.0 and AMOS software, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University (Approval No. 2025-03-089) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Scores of the two-way social support scale and Connor-Davidson resilience scale among nursing interns

The total score of the Two-Way Social Support Scale was (48.85 ± 9.67). Univariate analysis of situational emotional response among nursing interns

The results of this study indicated that gender, the number of systematic emotional management and communication training sessions received during schooling, whether the participant had engaged in simulated nurse-patient conflict training prior to the internship, the frequency of nurse-patient conflicts experienced in the past month, whether the assigned department had established guidelines or protocols for handling nurse-patient conflicts, and the subjective perception of current clinical internship stress were significantly associated with the situational emotional response total score of nursing interns (p < 0.05). Details are presented in Table 1.

Pearson correlation analysis of two-way social support, psychological resilience, and situational emotional response among nursing interns

Pearson correlation analysis indicated that situational emotional response total score was strongly positively correlated with the total score of two-way social support (r = 0.682, p < 0.01), and moderately positively correlated with the total score of psychological resilience (r = 0.512, p < 0.01).

Multiple linear regression analysis of factors associated with situational emotional response among nursing interns

The total situational emotional response score was entered as the dependent variable, and variables showing statistical significance in both univariate analysis and Pearson correlation analysis were included in the multiple linear regression model (α-entry = 0.05, α-removal = 0.10). Variable coding is detailed in Table 2, and the full regression results are presented in Table 3.

The final model was statistically significant (F = 152.877, P < 0.001) and explained 50.8% of the total variance in situational emotional response (adjusted R² = 0.499). No evidence of autocorrelation was detected (Durbin–Watson = 2.055), and multicollinearity was not a concern (all Variance Inflation Factors [VIF] < 2).

Three core independent predictors were identified: two-way social support total score emerged as the strongest positive predictor (β = 0.566, P < 0.001); Psychological resilience total score was also a significant positive predictor (β = 0.143, P = 0.001); Frequency of nurse–patient conflicts experienced in the past month was a significant negative predictor (β = −0.126, P < 0.001).

All other covariates included in the model failed to reach statistical significance (P > 0.05).

Model diagnostics confirmed normality of residuals (Fig. 1) and satisfactory homoscedasticity (Fig. 2). Influence diagnostics further supported model stability: removal of extreme cases (e.g., high-leverage or high-Cook’s-distance observations) did not meaningfully alter the core findings—thus, all valid cases were retained in the final analysis.

Structural equation model of factors associated with situational emotional response among nursing interns

Based on the stress-buffering model and the resource-gain theory, this study constructed an SEM with two-way social support and psychological resilience as latent variables and the total situational emotional response score as the outcome variable. The initial model was revised according to modification indices (MI > 10), permitting correlations only between error terms of indicators belonging to the same latent variable—and thus within the same validated scale—to control for common method bias. The final modified model demonstrated excellent model fit indices: GFI = 0.964, IFI = 0.991, CFI = 0.991, TLI = 0.986, CMIN/DF = 2.724, and RMSEA = 0.064. Standardized factor loadings revealed robust associations between all observed variables and their respective latent constructs—all standardized loadings exceeded 0.6 (p < 0.001). Path analysis revealed significant positive associative links between two-way social support and situational emotional response (β = 0.629, p < 0.001), as well as between two-way social support and psychological resilience (β = 0.625, p < 0.001). Psychological resilience also showed a weaker positive associative link with situational emotional response (β = 0.159, p < 0.001). Discussion

This study explores a common issue in nursing education: the mismatch between nursing interns’ operational skills and emotional awareness in high-pressure interpersonal scenarios. The reciprocal nature of two-way social support is a core component of professional nursing ethics across different cultural contexts.

This study explored the synergistic associative pattern of two-way social support and psychological resilience among nursing interns facing nurse-patient conflicts

The results showed that the total score for situational emotional response among nursing interns was 124.49 ± 21.73, with the situational perception dimension scoring the lowest and the situational use dimension scoring the highest. Those with insufficient situational perception struggle to identify patients’ inner feelings, and their nurse-patient interactions are more likely to become tense. This imbalance, characterized by “high output but low perception,” may reflect that nursing interns tend to adopt experience-based and routinized emotional coping strategies to maintain basic interpersonal interaction.

This insufficient situational perception is closely related to the weak statistical association between psychological resilience and situational emotions response (β = 0.159) observed in the structural equation model of this study. Within the tiered clinical care system, nursing interns occupy a position of power asymmetry in nurse-patient interactions, characterized by limited clinical decision-making authority, insufficient practical experience, and ambiguous professional role boundaries [27, 28]. When confronted with sudden, high-stress conflicts, these nursing interns struggle to rapidly translate their intrinsic psychological resilience into actionable emotional coping behaviors, rendering its potential protective role difficult to manifest independently and stably. The translation of resilience into effective emotional coping behaviors is highly dependent on two-way social support [29–31]. The data from this study further corroborate this finding: in the SEM, the magnitude of the association between two-way social support and situational emotional responses was approximately four times that of psychological resilience (β = 0.629 vs. β = 0.159). Furthermore, a significant positive association was identified between two-way social support and psychological resilience (β = 0.625, P < 0.001). This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that in the absence of stable two-way social support, the association between psychological resilience and emotional responses appears attenuated. Additionally, this study found that when nursing interns experienced two or more nurse-patient conflicts within a month, their level of situational emotional response significantly decreased. This pattern further suggests that relying solely on individual psychological resilience cannot mitigate the negative impacts related to nurse-patient conflict, and two-way social support is a crucial foundation for psychological resilience to function effectively. The SEM further validated the above findings and revealed their important clinical implications for nursing education and practice. Two-way social support, a foundational resource linked to nursing interns’ emotional adaptation, shows direct and indirect associative links with stronger emotional use and regulation, as well as greater emotional stability via its connection to psychological resilience. In contrast, the direct effect of psychological resilience alone on situational emotional response is relatively weak, suggesting that resilience training delivered in isolation—devoid of supportive relational contexts—may yield limited effectiveness. In clinical practice, even a single empathic feedback session from a clinical instructor, or a structured peer debriefing meeting following a conflict episode, can serve as concrete, scalable, and clinically feasible entry points for emotional empowerment.

From the perspective of clinical education practice, existing training predominantly focuses on post-conflict management and intervention for nurse-patient conflicts [32], with insufficient attention devoted to the preemptive cultivation of situational perception of emotion prior to conflicts. This deficiency leads to an imbalanced competency structure among nursing interns, which is reflected in stronger situational emotional use but weaker situational perception of emotion. Consequently, there remains considerable room for optimization in relevant systematic training programs. To address this gap, the present study constructed and tested a structural equation model grounded in the stress-buffering model and resource-gain theory, to explore the combined associations of two-way social support and psychological resilience with situational emotional response. The model demonstrated two significant and independent positive hypothesized pathways linking the two core predictors to interns’ total situational emotional response scores, which aligns with the theoretical expectations of this study. Accordingly, clinical nursing education may combine targeted cultivation of two-way social support and psychological resilience to remedy this weak dimension of emotional competence.

Two-way social support is positively associated with nursing interns’ situational emotional response

Multiple linear regression analysis showed that after controlling for covariates including nurse-patient conflict frequency, demographic characteristics and priortraining experience, the total score of two-way social support had the strongest independent positive association with situational emotional response. This observation may reflect that clinical nursing environments are not limited to one-sided resource reception, but form an interactive system of giving and receiving assistance. This hypothesized system can alleviate acute stress via emotional and instrumental support, and strengthen interns’ confidence in emotional communication during interpersonal interactions. Structural equation modeling further demonstrated stable independent latent associations for this hypothesized pathway. While psychological resilience also exhibited significant positive correlations, its function may serve as a fundamental, cumulative individual trait reserve that requires long-term adaptation and cannot be rapidly transformed into instant coping behaviors amid sudden conflicts. In this sample, 36.4% of interns had volunteer or part-time experience. Such experience provided repeated opportunities to practice mutual support, which may partially account for their higher scores on situational emotional response. This observation suggests that the capacity for two-way social support can be acquired and applied across various clinical contexts.

Frequency of nurse-patient conflicts is negatively associated with situational emotional response

Consistent with Pearson correlation and multiple linear regression results, the frequency of nurse-patient conflicts served as a significant negative independent correlate of situational emotional response, and interns’ scores decreased gradually as conflict exposure increased. This phenomenon may be associated with cumulative stressors that continuously deplete individuals’ emotional regulatory reserves and further impair emotional functioning [33]. Furthermore, univariate analyses indicated that nursing interns from departments with standardized nurse-patient conflict handling protocols and supporting training achieved higher total situational emotional response scores. Nevertheless, such institutional factors showed no statistically meaningful independent associations in multivariate regression models. This observation may imply that the strength of correlation between departmental management measures and interns’ emotional performance varies according to individual reserves of two-way social support and psychological resilience: when interns possess sufficient personal protective resources, institutional protocols show weak correlations with emotional status; when personal coping reserves are insufficient, departmental support correlates more strongly with emotional outcomes. Accordingly, integrated interventions combining departmental institutional arrangements and individualized emotional competency training may deliver more balanced clinical effects.

Theoretical contribution and clinical translation: advancing from generic skills to situational emotional response

Based on the observed variable association patterns, this study yields two core theoretical insights. First, unlike universal emotional intelligence measurements, the Situational Emotional Response Scale (ERES) is tailored exclusively to nurse–patient conflict contexts and partitions emotional competence into four discrete dimensions. Our data revealed situational perception of emotion as the lowest-scoring subdimension among interns, a shortcoming seldom captured by general emotional assessment tools that ignore conflict-specific scenarios. Second, this work provides empirical evidence of both direct and indirect correlational links between two-way social support, psychological resilience and situational emotional response, filling gaps in domestic research examining their synergistic relational patterns within Chinese nursing intern populations.

Regression and SEM outputs further indicated that two-way social support bore substantially stronger correlations with interns’ emotional functioning than personal psychological resilience, which serves as a correlated protective trait under clinical internship stress. Constrained by the cross-sectional design, this paper only puts forward mild directional suggestions for nursing education rather than complete standardized intervention systems. Educators may prioritize training targeting interns’ emotion identification deficits; clinical wards could set up routine communication channels to foster mutual support among trainees. Comprehensive training protocols and fixed coping frameworks cannot be validated based on current cross-sectional data and await testing in subsequent interventional trials.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

This study adopted the conflict-specific Situational Emotional Response Scale instead of general emotional intelligence scales for outcome measurement, which improves the pertinence of evaluation targeting nurse–patient conflict scenarios. Structural equation modeling was applied to verify the synergistic associative relationship between two-way social support and psychological resilience, a topic with limited domestic empirical evidence. In addition, this multi-center investigation covered six hospitals across Henan Province with a sufficient valid sample size, which enhances the regional representativeness of the findings for local nursing intern cohorts.

Limitations

This study has several inherent limitations. First, it employed a cross-sectional correlation design, which precludes the establishment of causal relationships among the variables. Although the well-fitted structural equation model identified hypothesized pathways, further validation through longitudinal studies or cluster randomized controlled trials is required to confirm these theoretical mechanisms. All buffering associations identified in this study should be interpreted as patterns consistent with the stress-buffering and resource-gain theory, rather than as confirmed causal effects.

Second, all data were collected using self-report questionnaires. Within the context of a collectivist culture and hierarchical ward management in China, participants’ responses may be susceptible to social desirability bias. Additionally, the questionnaires were distributed with the assistance of internship coordinators from each hospital, which may have introduced a slight selection bias.

Third, this study employed convenience sampling and only recruited nursing interns from six hospitals in Henan Province. This sampling method limits the cross-regional generalizability of the findings. Future studies may adopt probability sampling and conduct nationwide multi-center surveys to replicate the present results.

Fourth, the mean score of participants on the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) was 90.39, with most scores concentrated in the high range of the scale. This distribution suggests a potential ceiling effect, which may have compressed the variability in resilience scores. Future research may mitigate such measurement bias by expanding sample diversity and incorporating longitudinal designs.

Conclusion

Nursing interns in this sample demonstrated a moderately high overall level of situational emotional response, with notably weaker performance in the situational perception of emotion dimension. This imbalance may represent a vulnerability factor associated with an increased risk of conflict escalation in clinical settings.

The frequency of nurse-patient conflict exposure was negatively associated with situational emotional response, whereas two-way social support and psychological resilience showed patterns consistent with modifiable putative protective resources. Given the cross-sectional correlational design of this study, all observed associations should be interpreted as correlational patterns rather than confirmed causal relationships.

These findings provide a rationale for nurse educators and clinical managers to move beyond a sole focus on individual emotion-regulation training, and toward co-constructing relational support infrastructures that foster both reciprocal support capacities and psychological resilience development. Feasible entry points include structured peer debriefing circles and standardized preceptor feedback protocols that cultivate both support-giving and support-receiving competencies among interns.

Further longitudinal and interventional studies are required to verify the direction of these associations and to evaluate the effectiveness of targeted support programs.
