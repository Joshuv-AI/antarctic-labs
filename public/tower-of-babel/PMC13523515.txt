The mediating role of mental fatigue awareness in the relationship between perceived stress and psychological well-being among combat athletes
Authors: Taner Yılmaz, Faik Öz, Oğuzhan Gül, Suzan Dal, Tayfun Kara, Orkun Akkoç, Hamza Küçük, Patricia Roxana Forț, Robert Çitozi, Mustafa Nurullah Kadi, Vlad Adrian Geantă, Viorel Petru Ardelean, Coşkun Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCombat athletes face unique psychological demands, and stress may influence both their cognitive experiences and psychological well-being. This study aimed to examine the mediating role of mental fatigue awareness in the relationship between perceived stress and psychological well-being among combat athletes, thereby contributing to a clearer understanding of a potential cognitive mechanism linking stress to well-being in this population.MethodsA total of 303 combat athletes voluntarily participated. Participants completed the Perceived Stress Scale, Mental Fatigue Awareness Scale, and Psychological Well-being Scale. Statistical analyses assessed the direct effects of perceived stress on psychological well-being, the effect of stress on mental fatigue awareness, and the potential mediating role of mental fatigue awareness.ResultsPerceived stress significantly increased mental fatigue awareness (b = 0.732, SE = 0.064, β = 0.549, p = 0.001) and directly decreased psychological well-being (b = − 0.488, SE = 0.166, β = − 0.191, p = 0.004). Mental fatigue awareness negatively affected psychological well-being (b = − 0.374, SE = 0.124, β = − 0.195, p = 0.003) and partially mediated the relationship between stress and well-being (indirect effect: b = − 0.274, SE = 0.115, β = − 0.107, p = 0.003; 95% CI = − 0.504 to − 0.049).ConclusionPerceived stress was associated with psychological well-being both directly and indirectly through increased mental fatigue awareness. These findings suggest that mental fatigue awareness may represent a relevant cognitive pathway linking stress perceptions to well-being. Accordingly, stress management and strategies aimed at regulating mental fatigue may be considered as potentially beneficial components in efforts to support psychological well-being among combat athletes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-04960-1.Keywords: Perceived stress, Mental fatigue awareness, Psychological well-being, Combat athletes, Mediation model

Introduction

Perceived stress is a subjective stress experience that arises when combat athletes evaluate the demands they encounter during competition preparation and competition as threatening or challenging situations, comparing them with their own coping capacities [1, 2]. Prolonged exposure to this subjective stress load can deplete the athlete’s cognitive resources, paving the way for mental fatigue. Mental fatigue is a cognitive dysfunction that reduces decision-making speed and accuracy in athletes, negatively affects technical performance, and weakens concentration processes [3]. Importantly, the present study focuses on mental fatigue awareness rather than mental fatigue itself. Mental fatigue awareness reflects athletes’ awareness of fatigue-related symptoms and experiences, whereas mental fatigue refers to the actual state of cognitive exhaustion. The chronic persistence of mental fatigue can have a direct negative impact on psychological well-being. Psychological well-being is an important indicator of athletes’ psychological functioning, reflecting the extent to which individuals experience positive psychological functioning, purpose, and emotional balance under the demands of training and competition [4, 5]. In combat sports, where athletes are frequently exposed to performance pressure, uncertainty, and intense preparation demands, understanding the factors associated with psychological well-being is particularly important.

The current literature suggests that perceived stress is a significant determinant of psychological health indicators and that high stress levels are associated with reduced well-being [6–9]. However, stress can exacerbate mental fatigue by leading to the overexertion of cognitive resources and the onset of symptoms of mental exhaustion [10]. Mental fatigue contributes to impaired psychological well-being through effects such as weakened attention processes, difficulty regulating emotions, and loss of motivation [11]. These findings suggest that the impact of perceived stress on psychological well-being may be indirectly mediated by a cognitive mechanism, such as mental fatigue awareness. The fact that these relationships have not been tested in a comprehensive model forms the theoretical starting point of the study.

The limited number of studies examining the relationship between stress, mental fatigue, and psychological well-being in combat sports within the same model makes the present study significant in terms of both the literature and the applied field. In the literature, these variables are primarily examined independently; however, comprehensive models explaining the cognitive mechanisms through which perceived stress may be associated with psychological well-being have not been sufficiently developed. The extreme scarcity of studies evaluating the mediating role of mental fatigue awareness points to a significant theoretical gap in combat sports. Despite extensive research on perceived stress and psychological well-being in athletes, the mechanisms linking these variables remain insufficiently clarified. In particular, mental fatigue awareness has rarely been examined as a mediating construct in this relationship.

This study investigates the mediating role of mental fatigue awareness in the relationship between perceived stress and psychological well-being among combat athletes. By focusing on this mechanism, the study aims to provide a clearer understanding of how stress-related cognitive processes are associated with psychological well-being, while also offering practical implications for athlete support.

Materials and methods

Participants

This cross-sectional observational study included 303 combat athletes who voluntarily participated. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling approach through sports clubs, university sport networks, and direct contact with coaches.

Inclusion criteria were: (a) being 18 years of age or older, (b) actively training in a combat sport discipline (e.g., boxing, taekwondo, judo, kickboxing), and (c) having at least one year of regular training experience. Exclusion criteria included: (a) self-reported diagnosis of a chronic psychological or neurological disorder and (b) incomplete questionnaire responses. Surveys with more than 10% missing data were excluded from analysis.

A total of 327 athletes initially accessed the survey; after screening for eligibility and data completeness, 303 valid responses were retained (response rate: 92.7%).

Prior to participation, individuals were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, and written informed consent was obtained electronically. Ethical approval was granted by the Ondokuz Mayıs University Social and Human Sciences Research Ethics Committee (Decision No. 2025 − 648, 25 April 2025) before data collection commenced.

Data were collected between June 15, 2025, and July 25, 2025, via a secure online survey platform. Participants completed the questionnaire individually and anonymously under voluntary conditions. To reduce potential response bias, no identifying information was collected, and participants were allowed to withdraw at any time without consequence.

The survey included demographic questions followed by validated instruments assessing perceived stress, mental fatigue awareness, and psychological well-being. Completion time was approximately 10–12 min.

Although previous studies have independently examined perceived stress, mental fatigue, and psychological well-being in athletes, the underlying cognitive mechanisms linking these constructs remain insufficiently understood, particularly in combat sport populations. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to examine mental fatigue awareness as a mediating cognitive mechanism between perceived stress and psychological well-being in combat athletes using a conditional process modeling framework. The study is further distinguished by focusing on combat athletes, a population exposed to unique psychological demands such as rapid decision-making, repeated exposure to competitive pressure, weight-regulation practices, and high cognitive load. In addition to testing a mediation model, the inclusion of moderated mediation analyses across gender, national athlete status, weight-loss category, and sport experience provides a more comprehensive understanding of the structural stability of these relationships across athlete subgroups. Therefore, the present study contributes to the sport psychology literature not only by identifying a potential cognitive pathway linking stress and well-being, but also by extending mental fatigue awareness research into the context of combat sports.

Measurement tools

Perceived stress

The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) was developed to assess the extent to which individuals perceive situations in their lives as stressful [12]. Its Turkish adaptation was carried out in a Turkish validation study [13]. The scale consists of a total of 14 items, and participants respond to each item on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from “Never (0)” to “Very often (4)”. Seven positively worded items (items 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, and 13) are reverse-scored so that higher scores indicate greater perceived stress. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.84 in the Turkish validation study [13]. Although the total PSS score was used in the mediation, moderation, and correlation analyses, the two-factor structure identified in the Turkish adaptation study was additionally considered for descriptive subgroup comparisons presented in Table 8. The first factor, Insufficient Self-Efficacy Perception, comprises items 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, and 13 and reflects difficulties in coping with life demands and perceived inadequacy in managing personal challenges. The second factor, Perception of Stress/Discomfort, includes items 1, 2, 3, 8, 11, 12, and 14 and reflects feelings of stress, discomfort, and loss of control in response to daily life events [13].

Mental fatigue awareness

This scale was developed to determine the level of awareness of mental fatigue experienced by athletes who actively participate in sports after training or competition [14]. The scale consists of 25 items, a single sub-dimension, and a 5-point Likert-type response format. The Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient of the scale was reported as 0.96 [14]. According to confirmatory factor analysis results, the model demonstrated acceptable fit indices (χ²(287) = 1307.60; χ²/df = 4.55; RMSEA = 0.079; NFI = 0.96; NNFI = 0.96; CFI = 0.96) [13].

Psychological well-being

The Psychological Well-Being Scale consists of 8 items and is rated on a 7-point Likert-type scale [15]. During the Turkish adaptation process, exploratory factor analysis was conducted, and although the items were technically separated into six factors, they were evaluated under a single dimension in terms of content [16]. The total variance explained was 68%, and factor loadings ranged from 0.30 to 0.94. Confirmatory factor analysis indicated acceptable model fit (RMSEA = 0.07; NFI = 0.97; CFI = 0.98; GFI = 0.93; sRMR = 0.06) [16].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 28 and Hayes’ PROCESS macro (v4) [17]. Descriptive statistics, normality diagnostics (skewness and kurtosis), and independent samples t-tests were computed to examine distributional properties and group differences (Table 8). Skewness and kurtosis values were within ± 1.5, supporting the use of regression-based analyses.

The mediation model was tested using PROCESS Model 4 [17]. Perceived Stress (X) was specified as the independent variable, Mental Fatigue Awareness (M) as the mediator, and Psychological Well-Being (Y) as the dependent variable. Indirect effects were estimated using ordinary least squares regression with 5000 bootstrap samples, generating bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals for the indirect effect without assuming normality [17].

To examine conditional process effects, moderated mediation analyses were conducted using PROCESS Model 7 [17] (Tables 4, 5, 6 and 7). In separate models, gender, national athlete status, pre-competition weight loss category, and sport age (years) were entered as first-stage moderators of the relationship between perceived stress and mental fatigue awareness. The index of moderated mediation and bootstrapped confidence intervals (5000 samples) were used to determine whether indirect effects varied across moderator levels [17].

These variables were treated as theoretically relevant moderators rather than covariates, as the aim was to evaluate effect heterogeneity across meaningful subgroups rather than to estimate covariate-adjusted average effects. Additional participant characteristics were reported descriptively to characterize the sample but were not entered into the regression models.

The mediation model estimated several effects corresponding to the study hypotheses. Specifically, the effect of perceived stress on mental fatigue awareness (pathway a; H2), the effect of mental fatigue awareness on psychological well-being while controlling for perceived stress (pathway b; H3), the direct effect of perceived stress on psychological well-being (pathway c’; H1), the total effect of perceived stress on psychological well-being (pathway c; H1 total effect), and the indirect effect of perceived stress on psychological well-being via mental fatigue awareness (a × b pathway; H4) were calculated. The significance of the indirect effect was assessed using 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, with intervals not including zero indicating significant mediation. For all pathways, unstandardized coefficients (b), standard errors (SE), standardized coefficients (β), t-values, p-values, and bootstrap confidence intervals were reported.

Hypotheses of the study

Based on prior research and theoretical frameworks on stress, mental fatigue, and psychological well-being, the study tested the following hypotheses. It was expected that higher perceived stress would negatively impact psychological well-being, while mental fatigue awareness would act as a mechanism explaining part of this relationship.

H1: Perceived stress has a negative direct effect on psychological well-being. Individuals experiencing higher stress are likely to report lower levels of well-being.

H3: Mental fatigue awareness has a negative effect on psychological well-being. Higher awareness of mental fatigue is anticipated to be associated with lower psychological well-being.

Theoretical framework

Perceived stress reflects an individual’s subjective evaluation of their capacity to cope with environmental demands and has a direct influence on psychological functioning. The literature indicates that increased levels of perceived stress are associated with reduced psychological well-being and adverse outcomes such as anxiety, depression, and burnout [9, 18–20]. Accordingly, perceived stress is expected to have a negative effect on psychological well-being (H1).

Perceived stress emerges when individuals evaluate environmental demands as exceeding their coping capacity, and elevated stress levels increase cognitive load, which may enhance awareness of mental fatigue symptoms. When stress disrupts executive functions, such as attention, decision-making, and cognitive control, individuals may experience declines in cognitive performance. As a result, higher stress levels may lead individuals to become more attentive to internal indicators of mental fatigue [21–23]. Based on this theoretical rationale, perceived stress is expected to have a positive and significant effect on mental fatigue awareness (H2).

Mental fatigue awareness refers to an individual’s subjective recognition and evaluation of mental fatigue-related experiences, rather than the fatigue itself. Importantly, mental fatigue awareness should be distinguished from mental fatigue. While mental fatigue refers to a psychobiological state characterized by cognitive exhaustion and reduced cognitive performance, mental fatigue awareness reflects the extent to which individuals perceive, recognize, and interpret fatigue-related symptoms and experiences. Therefore, athletes may experience similar levels of mental fatigue but differ in their awareness and appraisal of these experiences. Mental fatigue awareness has been associated with psychological distress such as burnout, stress, and emotional strain [24, 25]. Therefore, as mental fatigue awareness increases, psychological well-being is expected to decrease (H3).

Although previous research has examined the relationships between perceived stress, cognitive strain, and psychological well-being, the mediating role of mental fatigue awareness in this association has not been sufficiently investigated in the literature. Increased levels of perceived stress may heighten individuals’ sensitivity to mental fatigue-related experiences, and this heightened awareness may, in turn, be associated with psychological well-being. Accordingly, mental fatigue awareness is proposed as a potential explanatory mechanism in the relationship between perceived stress and psychological well-being (H4).

In addition to the proposed mediation model, several athlete-related characteristics were considered as potential moderators of the relationship between perceived stress and mental fatigue awareness. Previous research suggests that psychological responses to stress may vary according to demographic and sport-related factors. Gender has been associated with differences in stress perception and psychological well-being, while national-level athletes may experience distinct competitive demands and performance pressures. Similarly, pre-competition weight-loss practices, which are common in combat sports, have been linked to increased psychological strain and cognitive burden. Furthermore, sport experience may influence athletes’ coping resources, stress adaptation, and responses to fatigue-related experiences. Therefore, gender, national athlete status, pre-competition weight loss, and sport age were included as theoretically relevant moderators to examine whether the association between perceived stress and mental fatigue awareness varied across meaningful athlete subgroups.

The mediation model developed within the scope of this study examined the effect of perceived stress on individuals’ psychological well-being and the mediating role of mental fatigue awareness in this relationship. The model was tested using PROCESS Model 4, and all direct and indirect paths were found to be statistically significant, supporting a partial mediation structure. In addition, conditional process analyses were conducted using PROCESS Model 7 to examine whether this mediation mechanism varied across gender, national athlete status, pre-competition weight loss, and sport age (Tables 4, 5, 6 and 7). Although specific subgroup effects emerged in isolated comparisons, the overall moderation indices were not statistically significant. These findings indicate that the indirect effect of perceived stress on psychological well-being through mental fatigue awareness was largely consistent across demographic and sport-related subgroups (Fig. 1).

The mediation model illustrating the role of mental fatigue awareness in the relationship between perceived stress and psychological well-being

The direct effect of perceived stress on psychological well-being was found to be significant and negative (b = − 0.488 ± 0.166, β = −0.191, p = 0.004). Perceived stress was found to predict mental fatigue awareness in a positive direction at a significant level (b = 0.732 ± 0.064, β = 0.549, p < 0.001), and mental fatigue awareness was found to have a negative effect on psychological well-being (b = − 0.374 ± 0.124, β = −0.195, p = 0.003). Furthermore, the indirect effect of perceived stress on psychological well-being is significant (b = − 0.274 ± 0.115, β = −0.107, p = 0.003; 95% CI [− 0.504, − 0.049]), indicating that mental fatigue awareness plays a partial mediating role. The fact that the bootstrap 95% CI values for the indirect effect do not include zero supports the validity of the mediating effect.

The moderation analysis indicated that gender did not significantly moderate the relationship between perceived stress and mental fatigue awareness (B = − 0.050, SE = 0.105, 95% CI [− 0.256, 0.156], ΔR² = 0.0003). In line with this, the index of moderated mediation was also non-significant (Index = 0.019, 95% Boot CI [− 0.058, 0.122]), suggesting that the indirect effect did not differ as a function of gender.

Although the conditional indirect effects were statistically significant for both male (B = − 0.281, BootSE = 0.123, 95% CI [− 0.532, − 0.048]) and female athletes (B = − 0.262, BootSE = 0.110, 95% CI [− 0.479, − 0.046]), these effects were comparable in magnitude. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as evidence of a consistent indirect association across gender groups rather than as indicating meaningful subgroup differentiation.

The moderation analysis indicated that national athlete status did not significantly moderate the relationship between perceived stress and mental fatigue awareness (B = − 0.164, SE = 0.111, 95% CI [− 0.383, 0.055], ΔR² = 0.0033). Consistent with this finding, the index of moderated mediation was non-significant (Index = 0.061, 95% Boot CI [− 0.019, 0.181]), indicating that the indirect effect did not vary as a function of national athlete status.

Although the conditional indirect effects were statistically significant for both non-national athletes (B = − 0.294, BootSE = 0.125, 95% CI [− 0.542, − 0.053]) and national athletes (B = − 0.233, BootSE = 0.103, 95% CI [− 0.445, − 0.042]), these effects were similar in magnitude. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as reflecting a consistent indirect association across groups rather than evidence of meaningful subgroup differences.

The moderated mediation analysis indicated that weight loss category did not significantly moderate the indirect effect of perceived stress on psychological well-being via mental fatigue awareness. The overall interaction was non-significant (ΔR² = 0.0044), and neither the 2–5% vs. <2% comparison (B = 0.143, SE = 0.122, 95% CI [− 0.097, 0.383]) nor the > 5% vs. <2% comparison (B = 0.265, SE = 0.175, 95% CI [− 0.080, 0.610]) reached statistical significance. In addition, the indices of moderated mediation were non-significant, as their bootstrapped confidence intervals included zero.

Although the conditional indirect effects were statistically significant across all weight loss groups (< 2%: B = − 0.240, BootSE = 0.113, 95% CI [− 0.479, − 0.032]; 2–5%: B = − 0.294, BootSE = 0.124, 95% CI [− 0.538, − 0.044]; >5%: B = − 0.339, BootSE = 0.156, 95% CI [− 0.657, − 0.047]), these effects were broadly similar in magnitude. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as indicating a consistent indirect association across weight loss categories rather than evidence of meaningful differences between groups.

The moderated mediation analysis indicated that sport age did not significantly moderate the indirect effect of perceived stress on psychological well-being via mental fatigue awareness at the overall model level (ΔR² = 0.0096, p = 0.066).

When examining specific interaction terms, only the comparison between 8 and 11 years and 1–3 years reached statistical significance (B = − 0.301, SE = 0.125, 95% CI [− 0.546, − 0.055]), with a corresponding significant index of moderated mediation (Index = 0.113, 95% Boot CI [0.006, 0.267]). However, this isolated finding should be interpreted with caution, as the overall interaction effect was not supported and the remaining comparisons (4–7 years and 12 + years) were non-significant, with confidence intervals including zero.

Despite the lack of consistent moderation, conditional indirect effects were statistically significant across all sport age groups (1–3 years: B = − 0.326, 95% CI [− 0.603, − 0.063]; 4–7 years: B = − 0.321, 95% CI [− 0.606, − 0.064]; 8–11 years: B = − 0.214, 95% CI [− 0.423, − 0.041]; 12 + years: B = − 0.241, 95% CI [− 0.496, − 0.036]). These effects were generally comparable in magnitude.

As presented in Table 8, skewness (γ₁) and kurtosis (γ₂) values indicated acceptable distributional characteristics for all variables. Although psychological well-being (PWB) showed a skewness value of − 1.12, all skewness and kurtosis coefficients remained within ± 2, supporting the use of parametric analyses.

Independent samples t-tests indicated that gender differences were generally limited. A statistically significant difference emerged only for psychological well-being, t(301) = − 3.22, p = 0.001, with a medium effect size (d = 0.41), suggesting higher PWB scores among female athletes. However, no significant gender differences were observed for Insufficient Self-Efficacy Perception (PS_ISEP), Perception of Stress/Discomfort (PS_PSD), or mental fatigue awareness (MFA) (all p > 0.05), and the associated effect sizes were small. This overall pattern suggests a largely consistent distribution of these variables across gender (Table 9).

Regarding national team status, a statistically significant difference was found for psychological well-being under unequal variance assumptions, t(166.94) = − 1.99, p = 0.049, with a small effect size (d = 0.25), indicating slightly higher PWB scores among national team athletes. Nevertheless, no significant differences were identified for PS_ISEP, PS_PSD, or MFA (all p > 0.05), with trivial to small effect sizes.

Taken together, these findings indicate that subgroup differences (i.e., gender and national team status) were generally limited and not consistently supported across variables. Therefore, the results should be interpreted cautiously, emphasizing the overall similarity of patterns across subgroups rather than suggesting robust differentiation.

Pearson correlation analysis indicated that perceived stress was positively associated with mental fatigue awareness (r = 0.549, p < 0.01) and negatively associated with psychological well-being (r = − 0.298, p < 0.01). Mental fatigue awareness was also negatively related to psychological well-being (r = − 0.300, p < 0.01), supporting the assumptions required for mediation analysis.

Discussion

The positive association between perceived stress and mental fatigue awareness represents one of the most consistent findings of the present study. The results indicate that higher levels of perceived stress are associated with greater awareness of mental fatigue symptoms among combat athletes (b = 0.732, β = 0.549, p = 0.001). This finding aligns with research in the sports psychology literature suggesting that stress is linked to increased cognitive load. Di Fronso et al. reported that athletes become more sensitive to bodily sensations and internal signals in stressful situations, with heightened interoceptive awareness contributing to a more pronounced perception of fatigue signals [26]. Similarly, Gerber et al. demonstrated that elevated stress levels are associated with greater cognitive exhaustion, which may facilitate the recognition of mental fatigue symptoms [27]. Taken together, these findings suggest that perceived stress is closely related to athletes’ awareness of mental fatigue, supporting the interpretation that cognitive load plays an important role in how fatigue signals are experienced and interpreted.

The negative association between mental fatigue awareness and psychological well-being represents another important finding of the present study. The results indicate that higher levels of mental fatigue awareness are associated with lower levels of psychological well-being (b = − 0.374, β = − 0.195, p = 0.003). Previous research suggests that heightened awareness of mental fatigue may be linked to reduced well-being, particularly when fatigue-related experiences are perceived as difficult to manage. Kara et al. reported that increases in fatigue awareness are accompanied by weakened psychological recovery processes, reflected in decreased positive affect and increased negative emotional states [28]. Similarly, Zhang et al. highlighted that when mental fatigue is appraised as threatening, it may be associated with adverse outcomes for psychological well-being [29]. In addition, evidence showing parallel increases in anxiety and psychological tension during periods of elevated mental load, such as rapid weight loss phases, supports the view that mental exhaustion is closely associated with diminished well-being in athletes [30]. Taken together, these findings indicate that mental fatigue awareness is not inherently protective and, under conditions of elevated stress, may be associated with reduced psychological well-being.

The negative association between perceived stress and psychological well-being (b = − 0.488, β = − 0.191, p = 0.004) is one of the findings of the present study that closely aligns with existing literature. A substantial body of research involving athletes, student-athletes, and coaches consistently suggests that elevated stress levels are associated with poorer psychological outcomes and reduced well-being. Previous studies have shown that stress may negatively affect quality of life, motivation, sleep quality, and emotional functioning, while also contributing to burnout and fatigue-related experiences [31–34]. Furthermore, evidence suggests that protective factors such as social support and healthy lifestyle behaviors may help buffer some of the adverse consequences of stress on psychological well-being [31, 35]. Collectively, these findings support the view that perceived stress represents an important challenge to athlete well-being and provide additional support for the negative association observed in the present study.

One of the key contributions of the present study is the demonstration that the association between perceived stress and psychological well-being is partially explained through mental fatigue awareness. The significant indirect effect identified in the analyses (b = − 0.274, β = − 0.107, p = 0.003) indicates that perceived stress is associated with psychological well-being both directly and indirectly via mental fatigue awareness. Previous research has consistently suggested that stress-related cognitive and emotional processes may contribute to poorer well-being outcomes through indirect psychological pathways. Studies have shown that elevated stress levels are associated with mental exhaustion, reduced psychological resilience, and increased burnout, all of which have been linked to diminished well-being [27, 33]. Related evidence further indicates that stress-related cognitive processes may intensify individuals’ subjective experiences of mental strain, thereby contributing to mechanisms that connect stress and psychological functioning [36]. Collectively, these findings support the interpretation that mental fatigue awareness may represent an important cognitive mechanism through which perceived stress is associated with psychological well-being. Nevertheless, the underlying processes remain complex and should be examined further in future longitudinal and experimental research [37, 38]. In addition, previous research has highlighted the importance of using psychometrically validated psychological instruments when examining stress-related cognitive and well-being processes in athlete populations, supporting the interpretability of mediation-based findings in sport psychology research [39].

Evidence indicating that sustained mental load is associated with lower levels of psychological well-being further supports the findings of the present study. Russell et al. reported that increased mental load is linked to impairments in emotional regulation, heightened sensitivity to environmental stimuli, and a greater likelihood of errors in attention-demanding tasks [40]. Similarly, Sun et al. demonstrated that mental fatigue is associated with disruptive effects across multiple performance domains, including motor skills and perceptual–cognitive processes [3]. In addition, findings from elite wrestlers undergoing rapid weight loss indicate concurrent increases in anxiety and psychological stress responses during competition periods, highlighting the cumulative nature of mental load in athletic contexts [30]. Taken together, these studies support the view that mental load–related cognitive and behavioral processes are closely associated with psychological well-being.

The findings of the present study are consistent with previous research indicating a close association between stress, burnout, and psychological well-being across different athletic and coaching populations. Bíró and Balogh reported that higher stress levels among coaches are associated with increased burnout, while this association appears to vary depending on individual levels of mental resilience [41]. Similarly, Chyi et al. demonstrated that life stress is positively associated with burnout in athletes, with the relationship being particularly pronounced through the perceived distress component [42]. In addition, Potts et al. observed that exposure to intense stressors among sports coaches is associated with lower levels of multiple well-being dimensions, including autonomy, environmental mastery, relationships, and self-acceptance [43]. Reinboth and Duda reported that higher performance pressure within an ego-focused motivational climate is associated with lower self-esteem, increased burnout, and reduced psychological well-being [44]. Consistent with this perspective, the present finding regarding the negative association between perceived stress and psychological well-being aligns with the study by Toktaş and Zambak, who identified psychological resilience as a significant predictor of burnout and well-being among football coaches [45]. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal relationships, and future longitudinal or experimental research is needed to clarify temporal and causal mechanisms. Previous studies also suggest that athlete populations may exhibit distinct psychological profiles when assessed using validated instruments, which may influence observed associations between stress-related constructs and well-being [46].

Limitations

The characteristics of the sample should be considered when interpreting the findings. The sample was largely composed of young athletes (19–21 years) and included a higher proportion of female participants, which may limit generalizability. In addition, athletes from different combat sports were analyzed as a single group, and the relatively high representation of taekwondo athletes may have influenced the results. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the relationships among perceived stress, mental fatigue awareness, and psychological well-being. Second, all variables were assessed using self-report instruments administered at a single time point. Therefore, the observed associations may have been influenced by common method variance, which has the potential to inflate relationships among variables measured using the same method. In addition, self-report measures may be affected by response tendencies such as social desirability, recall bias, and participants’ subjective interpretations of questionnaire items. Although validated instruments were used and anonymous participation was ensured, the potential influence of common method variance cannot be completely ruled out. Future studies may benefit from incorporating multiple data sources, longitudinal designs, or objective indicators to reduce the likelihood of method-related bias. Third, potential confounding variables (e.g., training load and competitive level) were not included as control variables, which may have influenced the observed associations. Finally, as the study was conducted within a single cultural context, future cross-cultural research is warranted to examine the generalizability of the findings.

Although the present study was conducted within a specific cultural context, the findings may have broader relevance. Combat and weight-class sports share similar structural and psychological demands across countries, suggesting that the observed associations may extend to comparable athlete populations. From an applied perspective, monitoring perceived stress and mental fatigue awareness may support athlete well-being across different sport systems.

Although the present study was conducted within a specific cultural and sport context, certain aspects of the findings may extend beyond the current sample. Combat and weight-class sports share several common psychological and physiological demands across countries and disciplines, including performance pressure, rapid decision-making, cognitive load, and weight-management practices. Therefore, the observed associations between perceived stress, mental fatigue awareness, and psychological well-being may also be relevant to athletes participating in other combat or weight-class sports contexts. Nevertheless, caution is warranted when generalizing the findings, as cultural background, competition level, training systems, and sport-specific characteristics may influence psychological responses and stress-related experiences. Future cross-cultural and sport-specific studies are needed to determine the extent to which the present findings can be generalized across diverse athletic populations.

Strengths

This study addresses a notable gap in the literature, being among the few to examine perceived stress, mental fatigue awareness, and psychological well-being within a single model. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the first to evaluate mental fatigue awareness as a mediating construct linking perceived stress and psychological well-being in combat athletes. The sample of 303 active combat athletes, representing multiple disciplines and experience levels, enhances the generalizability of the results within the athlete population. Beyond theoretical contributions, the findings provide practical guidance for coaches and sports psychologists, informing stress management, mental load regulation, and the design of psychological support programs for athletes.

Recommendations

Given the observed negative association between perceived stress and psychological well-being, stress management strategies may be considered as potentially beneficial components of athlete support systems in combat sports contexts. Monitoring perceived stress levels during intensive training and competition periods could help identify athletes who may be at greater risk of reduced well-being. In such cases, individualized psychological support and structured recovery periods may represent supportive practices.

Considering that higher perceived stress was associated with increased mental fatigue awareness, balanced cognitive and psychological regulation strategies could be explored. Psychoeducational sessions aimed at enhancing athletes’ understanding of stress–fatigue dynamics, as well as mental recovery practices integrated into training routines, may contribute to better self-regulation. Providing informational resources that clarify the relationship between stress, mental fatigue, and well-being may further assist athletes and coaching staff in recognizing early warning signs.

Regular psychological monitoring may facilitate the early detection of substantial increases in stress or mental fatigue, particularly among athletes exposed to additional demands (e.g., competition-related weight management). Access to psychological counseling services before and after competition periods may be considered as a supportive measure. Furthermore, educating coaches to recognize behavioral and cognitive indicators of mental fatigue could enhance preventive strategies. Integrating stress-awareness components into broader resilience development frameworks at the club and national team levels may offer a structured approach to supporting athletes’ psychological health.

Conclusions

The mediation analyses indicated that perceived stress was negatively associated with psychological well-being and positively associated with mental fatigue awareness in combat athletes. Perceived stress significantly predicted psychological well-being (B = − 0.488) and mental fatigue awareness (B = 0.732), while mental fatigue awareness was negatively associated with psychological well-being (B = − 0.374). The indirect effect was statistically significant (B = − 0.274), supporting a partial mediation model. These findings suggest that the association between perceived stress and psychological well-being may be partially linked to increased awareness of mental fatigue, without indicating a causal or temporal relationship.

Moderated mediation analyses showed that gender, national athlete status, and pre-competition weight loss did not significantly moderate the first-stage path, and the indices of moderated mediation were non-significant, indicating structural stability of the mediation mechanism across subgroups. Conditional indirect effects remained significant across all levels of these moderators. A partial differentiation emerged for sport age, with a significant interaction observed for athletes with 8–11 years of experience compared to those with 1–3 years; however, indirect effects were significant across all experience levels. Group comparisons (Table 8) revealed higher psychological well-being among female and national athletes, whereas perceived stress and mental fatigue awareness did not significantly differ across these groups. Overall, the findings indicate that perceived stress is associated with lower psychological well-being both directly and indirectly through mental fatigue awareness. Rather than pointing to a broader resilience framework, the present results specifically support the relevance of a potential cognitive association between perceived stress and psychological well-being involving mental fatigue awareness. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, these findings should be interpreted as associations within a statistical mediation model rather than evidence of causal or temporal relationships.
