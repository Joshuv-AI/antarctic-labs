Risk of carbon emission leakage through international trade of fossil-based e-fuels under regionally differentiated climate policies
Authors: Ken Oshiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryElectrofuels (e-fuels) produced from captured CO2 and hydrogen are expected to replace fossil fuels in hard-to-abate sectors. However, when the carbon originates from fossil-based sources, international trade of these fuels risks transferring CO2 emissions across borders in the form of carbon leakage that undermines the climate goals. This study employed a global techno-economic energy system model to assess the leakage risk under various climate policy scenarios. The results indicate that approximately 0.5 Gt CO2, accounting for 17% of OECD (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development) country emissions by 2050, can be transferred to non-OECD through fossil-based e-fuels under heterogeneous climate policies. Including the carbon footprint of e-fuels in consumer-side emission accounting with effective carbon pricing largely eliminates this leakage and instead promotes carbon-neutral e-fuels. Harmonized global climate policies are similarly effective. These findings underscore the importance of a well-defined policy framework, such as carbon border adjustments for e-fuel trade, similar to those in place for hydrogen in the European Union.

Introduction

Many countries and companies have pledged to reduce CO2 emissions to address climate change, including setting net-zero emission goals by mid-century. The recent deployment and cost reduction of renewable energy technologies are expected to promote both direct and indirect electrification and associated emission reductions, using hydrogen and its derivatives.1,2,3 Among the hydrogen derivatives, carbon capture and utilization (CCU), specifically electrofuels (e-fuels), which encompass synthetic liquefied and gaseous fuels using renewable electricity as a hydrogen source, is emerging as an emission reduction option.4,5,6 If e-fuels are derived from carbon-neutral sources, such as biomass or direct air capture (DAC), they can effectively reduce residual CO2 emissions by replacing fossil-based liquid or gaseous fuels due to their carbon-neutral footprint. Recent scenario studies assessing the role of e-fuels derived from carbon-neutral sources indicate that they can substantially reduce residual emissions in hard-to-abate energy demand sectors.7,8,9 While the national-level decarbonization scenario studies that included CCU options have been mainly developed for Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries,10,11 some studies have indicated the role of CCU in the long-term decarbonization scenarios in Asian countries.12 Given this background, the large emitters in Asia, such as China, have positioned CCU as a key mitigation option in their national energy plans.13

Synthetic fuels have recently received attention as a means of indirect utilization of renewable electricity. Historically, they were produced by the conversion of fossil fuels directly into other energy carriers via liquefaction or gasification. Their deployment has been anticipated in future scenarios without climate change mitigation, such as business-as-usual projections.14,15,16,17 Even within low-emission energy scenarios, the CO2 used for e-fuel generation can theoretically originate from fossil-based sources. The International Energy Agency (IEA) has suggested that small amounts of fossil-based carbon were utilized in the “net-zero” scenario, accounting for 0.05 Gt of CO2 per year, which is remarkably lower than those derived from carbon-neutral sources, accounting for 0.9 Gt CO2 per year.18 While recent studies have considered the carbon footprint and emission attributions of pure hydrogen and categorized it by its “color” depending on its primary energy sources,19,20 there has been little discussion of these issues for fossil-based e-fuels.

Regionally heterogeneous stringency in climate policies may pose a risk of unintended emissions transfer across regions if the carbon footprint of fossil-based e-fuels is not adequately accounted for. Several carbon emission leakage channels have been identified, including energy and non-energy markets, international trade, and the associated relocation of industrial production, primarily from non-OECD to OECD countries.21 A number of studies have assessed the historical carbon emission transfer associated with international trade.22,23 Furthermore, the risk of future carbon leakage under regionally fragmented climate policies has been assessed using simulation models.24,25,26 These studies have also evaluated the impact of policy measures designed to avoid emission leakages, such as the carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM).27,28

Given its high risk of carbon leakage, the European Union (EU) included pure hydrogen as a CBAM-covered good during its transition period in 2023–2025, alongside other carbon-intensive products such as iron, steel, aluminum, and cement. While ammonia is also included in CBAM as a form of fertilizer, other hydrogen derivatives, such as e-fuels, are not yet included, despite the European Hydrogen Observatory recognizing their potential leakage risk.29 Recent scenario studies have considered the international trade of e-fuels; however, they consider the e-fuel trade derived from carbon-neutral sources.8,10,30 Consequently, little is known about the carbon leakage risks of fossil-based e-fuels, and the effectiveness of policy measures for avoiding leakage thorough e-fuel trade has not been explored.

Given this background, the present study aimed to clarify the risks of unintended emission leakage through fossil-based e-fuels globally and to explore policy recommendations to avoid such effects. To this end, a global techno-economic energy system model representing various e-fuel production routes, e-fuels, and their international trade was used for future scenario simulation.7 In this model, carbon sources for e-fuel production include fossil-based (including fossil fuels and industrial process emissions) and carbon-neutral sources, namely biomass and DAC. While the original model attributed CO2 emissions from fossil-based e-fuel combustion to the production phase, this study introduced an additional accounting option, in which the carbon footprint of e-fuels is attributed to the consumption phase. This approach corresponds to a condition where the carbon footprint of fossil-based e-fuels is included in the CO2 emissions of the importing country, and carbon prices are imposed accordingly. Under either method, CO2 used for e-fuel production is accounted for in the emission inventories of either the producers or the consumers within the same year. This approach differs from the life-cycle CO2 assessment, as it does not account for the carry-over of e-fuel reserves across different years. Furthermore, captured CO2 is managed through underground storage (carbon capture and storage [CCS]) or CCU for e-fuel conversion. In case of CCU, CO2 is eventually released into the atmosphere and is accounted for in the emissions for either the producer or the consumer, as described above. In this study, emission leakage is defined as the emission transfer that occurs when e-fuels are traded under conditions where their carbon footprints are attributed to the producers, while carbon prices are not imposed for the consumers.

Several climate policy conditions were prepared to comprehensively assess the risk of emission leakage (Table 1). Regarding the emission-accounting method of fossil-based e-fuels, in the production-based accounting case (Prod), CO2 emission was allocated to the producers of e-fuels. Consequently, importers can use these fuels as carbon-free energy carriers. In the consumption-based accounting case (Cons), the carbon footprint of e-fuels was attributed to the consumer, similar to other conventional fossil fuels. In terms of emission scenarios, various pathways were prepared based on cumulative CO2 emissions from 500 to 1400 Gt CO2 (500C–1400C). Constraints on global CO2 emissions from energy and industrial processes were imposed for each scenario. Furthermore, the impact of regionally harmonized or heterogeneous international climate policy was assessed in this study. Regionally differentiated climate policy (DiffCP) scenarios considered near-term emission reduction targets included in the Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) in each region, alongside the global emissions constraints, and the carbon price differences among regions were extended to 2050. Scenario design

In addition to these default technology assumptions (“default”), sensitivity scenarios (AdvTech) were prepared to analyze the impact of different assumptions regarding key technologies on e-fuel production, trade, and consumption. Following previous studies,3,7 the AdvTech case assumes a substantial cost reduction in solar power, wind power, and DAC. Results

Emission trajectories and energy system transformations

Regionally heterogeneous climate policy results in differentiated mitigation efforts across regions. Figures 1A and 1B compare CO2 emissions and carbon prices between the OECD and non-OECD countries. Compared with the HarmCP cases, the DiffCP scenarios are characterized by lower CO2 emissions and higher carbon prices in the OECD countries. The difference in CO2 emissions between the HarmCP and DiffCP cases is the largest in the 1400C scenario, accounting for approximately 2.8 Gt of CO2 by 2050 (Figures 1A, S2, and S3). Under more stringent mitigation scenarios, such as the 500C scenario, the emission difference narrows to around 0.4 Gt CO2 by 2050, as stringent emission constraints are also imposed on non-OECD countries. Conversely, CO2 emissions in non-OECD countries in the DiffCP scenarios are greater than in the HarmCP scenarios. The regionally differentiated mitigation effort is reflected in the carbon price level among regions, which is estimated to range from 240 to 310 US$ per t-CO2 in the OECD countries by 2050 (Figures 1B and S4). In the DiffCP scenarios, the drivers of additional emission reductions in the OECD countries are mainly low-carbon energy upscaling (Figures 1C, S5, and S6) and end-use electrification (Figures 1D, S7, and S8). While these energy transformations are observed across all mitigation scenarios for both OECD and non-OECD countries, the share of renewable energy sources in primary energy and the end-use electrification rate increase in OECD countries in the DiffCP scenarios relative to the HarmCP scenarios. The estimated differences in the renewable share and electrification rate by 2050 account for approximately 10 and 7 percentages points, respectively, in the 1400C scenario. In contrast, renewable expansion and electrification in the DiffCP scenarios are moderate in the non-OECD countries compared with those in the HarmCP scenarios.

Additionally, an increase in e-fuel use is observed in OECD countries, contributing to a reduction in direct fossil fuel consumption in the final energy sectors (Figure 1E). In the 1400C-DiffCP scenario, e-fuels accounts for 4.6% of the final energy demand in OECD countries, whereas in non-OECD countries, e-fuel deployment is limited to less than 0.7% across all scenarios. Under the HarmCP scenarios, e-fuel consumption remained limited in both OECD and non-OECD countries.

Carbon leakages associated with fossil-based e-fuels

While e-fuel consumption increases in the DiffCP scenarios, their carbon sources differ among the emission-accounting schemes, namely the Prod and Cons cases. In the DiffCP-Prod scenarios where the carbon footprint of fossil-based e-fuel is included in the producer’s emissions, most of the carbon utilized for e-fuels is derived from fossil-based sources, especially in the 1400C and 1000C scenarios (Figure 2A). In contrast, in the DiffCP-Cons cases where the carbon footprint is accounted for by the consumer, carbon converted to e-fuels is mainly derived from carbon-neutral sources, including biomass and DAC, while the fossil-based e-fuel supply is minimal. In the 500C scenario, captured carbon for e-fuel production is mainly derived from Latin America, where bioenergy-based, carbon-neutral e-fuel potential is abundant (Figure S9). Figure 2B summarizes the carbon footprint balances of fossil-based e-fuels between the OECD and non-OECD countries in the DiffCP-Prod cases. Combustion of fossil-based e-fuels and the direct release of CO2 mainly occur in the OECD countries, which are the major consumers of e-fuels, as shown in Figure 1E. However, because importers can use e-fuels as emission-free energy carriers in the DiffCP-Prod case, their carbon footprint is attributed to the non-OECD countries. Through such e-fuel trade and emission attribution, about 0.51 Gt CO2 would be virtually transferred to non-OECD countries from OECD countries by 2050 in the 1400C scenario. At a more granular regional level, the majority of this emission transfer is associated with Asian countries, followed by Middle East and Africa (bottom image in Figures 2B and S10). In particular, the emission transfer attributed to China would reach 0.32 Gt CO2 by 2050 under the same scenario. This carbon emission leakage from non-OECD to OECD countries is smaller in the more stringent mitigation scenarios, accounting for about 0.14 Gt CO2 in the 1000C scenario in 2050. In contrast, in the DiffCP-Cons scenarios, this emissions transfer is constrained because the carbon footprint is attributed to the consumption of e-fuels. Alternatively, e-fuels are mostly derived from carbon-neutral sources in these scenarios. According to the sensitivity scenarios in this study, the emission transfer could reach 0.61 Gt CO2 per year in the 1400C scenario under the AdvTech conditions (Figure S11). The regional trends in other cases, specifically those where emission transfers from the non-OECD to OECD countries are significant, are similar to those in the “default” technology assumption cases.

Approximately 0.51 Gt CO2 emission leakage in the 1400C-DiffCP-Prod scenario is equivalent to approximately 17.3% of the total CO2 emissions in the OECD countries (Figure 2C). In the 1000C and 700C scenarios, this carbon leakage rate is approximately 4.5% and 2.2% of total OECD emissions, respectively. Under the AdvTech assumption, the leakage rate increases relative to the “default” technology case, reaching 17.8% in the 1400C scenario. In the HarmCP conditions, the leakage rate is approximately 1% or less (Figure S12). In addition to CCU implementation as a form of e-fuel supply, carbon capture and storage (CCS) plays a role in reducing emissions (Figure S13). It is used in both the HarmCP and DiffCP scenarios, while CCU is mainly used in the DiffCP scenarios.

e-fuel production, consumption, and trade

The policy design regarding e-fuel emission accounting also affects the energy demand, supply, and international trade. While e-fuels are estimated to be traded across all mitigation scenarios by 2050, regionally DiffCP scenarios particularly promote the global trade of fossil-based e-fuels alongside emission transfer (Figures 3A, S14, and S15). Under the DiffCP-Prod cases, OECD countries would import approximately 6.9 EJ and 1.8 EJ per year of fossil-based e-fuels from non-OECD countries by 2050 in the 1400C and 1000C scenarios, respectively. In stringent mitigation scenarios, such as 700C and 500C, and in all DiffCP-Cons scenarios, international trade in carbon-neutral e-fuels is estimated to increase by 2050, while fossil-based e-fuel trade is projected to decline. e-fuels are consumed mainly in the transport sector followed by the industry sector, which is consistent with a previous study of global e-fuel potential7 (Figure 3B). These sectors are characterized by long-distance transport and high-temperature heating demand, where decarbonization by electrification is relatively difficult. e-fuels can replace the direct use of fossil fuels in these sectors and contribute to reducing residual emissions in the e-fuel-importing countries.

On the energy supply side, the expansion of renewable energy sources is observed across all mitigation scenarios, specifically solar photovoltaic (PV) and wind power, both at the global scale and in the major e-fuel exporting countries like China (Figures S16, S17, and S18). Moreover, hydrogen is produced using surplus electricity from these sources (Figure S19). As a result, hydrogen generation is effectively decarbonized, given that it is primarily derived from low-carbon electricity (Figures 3C and S20). Thus, hydrogen used for e-fuel production is mostly produced in low-emission pathways, whereas the carbon source varies between the Prod and Cons cases. Because the upscaling of low-carbon electricity generation and direct use of hydrogen also contribute to additional emission reductions in OECD countries in the DiffCP scenarios, global hydrogen production is the largest in the 500C scenario.

Due to the increased demand for fossil-based e-fuel production in the DiffCP-Prod cases, the primary energy supply of fossil fuels with carbon capture increases in the non-OECD countries compared with the DiffCP-Cons cases (Figure 3D). In these scenarios, fossil-based carbon is mainly captured from coal in the power generation and industry sectors, followed by natural gas. While the coal and gas supply with carbon capture in the Diff-Prod is greater in the 1400C and 1000C scenarios, the difference between Prod and Cons case is limited in the 700C and 500C scenarios. In these stringent mitigation scenarios, the fossil-based e-fuel production is limited, and captured carbon is predominantly stored underground via CCS.

Driver of fossil-based e-fuel deployment

Increases in fossil-based e-fuel and associated emission transfers across regions are mainly due to energy cost conditions for fuel importers. The average cost of e-fuel production is generally high, as demonstrated in a previous study,7 with costs in this study reaching about 40 US$ per gigajoule (GJ), even though their carbon sources are fossil based, whereas the cost of oil-based liquid fuels is approximately 20 US$ per GJ (Figure 4). In cases where high carbon prices, such as those imposed in OECD countries (Figure 1B), are applied to oil products, fossil-based e-fuels become competitive with oil, as the total cost of oil, including emission costs, increases to about 40 US$ per GJ. However, when the carbon footprint of fossil-based e-fuels is attributed to the consumer, their cost increases to approximately 60 US$ per GJ, rendering them no longer competitive. Consequently, fossil-based e-fuel use is limited under the Cons cases, whereas carbon-neutral e-fuels can still play a role because they have no carbon footprint.Figure 4Estimated average energy production cost of fossil-based e-fuels and oil products by 2040 and 2050Emission costs (ECs) are based on the carbon content of each fuel and carbon price taken from each scenario’s result.

Discussion

The scenario analysis in this study indicates that up to 0.5 Gt CO2 per year could be transferred from OECD countries to non-OECD countries through the international trade of fossil-based e-fuels under specific conditions, which is equivalent to about 17% of total CO2 emissions in the OECD countries. This represents a potential new channel of emission transfer that has not been addressed in previous studies.21

This study clarifies the conditions under which emission leakage occurs through e-fuel trade. First, regionally heterogeneous international climate policy emerged as a major driver of leakage, which is consistent with the channels established in existing studies. However, this study demonstrated that new technologies, such as CCU and e-fuels, pose emission leakage risks unless the emission attribution of their carbon footprint is strictly managed. Although it was agreed to transition away from fossil fuel use in the United Nations Conference of the Parties (COP28), there are still gaps in international climate policy. Therefore, careful policy design is required when promoting e-fuel production and deployment.

Second, the stringency of the global emission reduction level is a key determinant of emission leakage associated with e-fuels. Emission leakage was the greatest in the 1400C and 1000C scenarios, reaching 0.5 Gt CO2. Conversely, in the 700C and 500C scenarios, emission leakage is substantially reduced, even when the carbon footprint of e-fuels is attributed to the producers. In these stringent mitigation scenarios, fossil-based e-fuel production becomes an economically unattractive option due to the tight emission constraints imposed on producers.

Under a globally HarmCP design, where carbon prices are uniformly imposed across regions, emission leakage is effectively reduced. This is because the direct utilization of fossil fuels is a more cost-effective option than converting them into e-fuels. As discussed in a previous study,7 e-fuel production involves significant energy losses and associated cost increases, which render them less competitive than other low-carbon fuels. Moreover, the result indicated that carbon-neutral e-fuel consumption is also limited because direct electrification or the use of hydrogen and biomass remains more cost effective, even in the aviation and shipping sectors. In this regard, careful policy design is essential for promoting e-fuels, whether derived from fossil-based or carbon-neutral sources, as these fuels inherently involve additional costs.

While stringent and harmonized international climate policy with global uniform carbon pricing would effectively reduce emission leakage, recent studies have shown that imposing such carbon prices on developing countries could exacerbate poverty and income inequality.31,32,33 Furthermore, the current national emission reduction goals, including the current NDCs and the long-term strategies, are still heterogeneous. Nevertheless, the findings of this study suggest that even under the moderate mitigation level with regionally differentiated polices, unintended carbon leakage can be avoided if the carbon footprint attribution of e-fuels is well designed. In these contexts, rather than relying solely on the realization of a globally uniform carbon price imposition, the prioritized policy recommendation should be the robust monitoring, verification, and certification of the e-fuel carbon footprint.

It is also important to consider the estimated leakage potential of 0.5 Gt CO2 per year across the OECD and non-OECD countries. Compared with other emission leakage channels, Peters et al. estimated that historical net emission transfer through international goods trade is about 0.4–1.6 Gt CO2 per year.22 While the potential leakage through e-fuels would not surpass these numbers, it could be a critical factor for climate change mitigation. Additionally, although this study assumed regionally DiffCP implementation between OECD and non-OECD countries as a representative example, similar emission leakage theoretically exists among other regions or sectors with heterogeneous emission reduction stringency. Furthermore, corporate emission-accounting frameworks must consider such leakage risks to ensure that e-fuel use does not result in unintended environmental consequences. To mitigate these risks, the cross-sectoral Emissions Trading Systems (ETS), such as those already implemented in the EU and China,34 should incorporate the trading of e-fuels.

This study highlights the need for careful policy design concerning the emission attribution of the carbon footprint of e-fuels. A robust monitoring, reporting, and verification framework for e-fuels is also essential, especially because the CBAM implemented in the EU today has covered only hydrogen and ammonia. As implemented in the EU, the classification and verification of e-fuels, such as renewable fuel of non-biological origin (RFNBO),35 coupled with an adequate carbon pricing mechanism, would be effective. Policy focus should extend beyond the production of renewable-based electricity and hydrogen for e-fuel supply to include commitments on conversion pathways and supply chains, with particular attention given to their carbon sources.

Limitations of this study

There are several limitations and caveats to consider when interpreting the findings of this study. First, this analysis did not consider other traditional channels of emission leakage, such as the relocation of carbon-intensive industries. If these channels were included in the analysis, the impact of emission transfer using e-fuels might change. Second, because the model used for the assessment is a partial equilibrium model, some geopolitical and economic factors that affect the international energy trade are not accounted for. These factors might change the trade volumes and alter the scale of emission leakage. Therefore, to develop more concrete policy designs for the e-fuel carbon footprint standard and associated carbon pricing mechanisms, the integration of economic models and comprehensive scenario analyses is essential for future research.

Method details

Energy system model

This study employed the global energy system model AIM-Technology for numerical scenario simulation. This model is based on a bottom-up approach incorporating energy technologies across both energy demand and supply sectors, and industrial processes.3,36 Energy technologies were selected to minimize total energy system costs, which included capital, operation and management (O&M), and energy costs. The energy efficiency and cost parameters of each technology, energy service demands, and associated constraints, such as energy resource potentials, were provided as exogenous parameters. Details of the model version used in this study, including its mathematical equations and technological parameter assumptions, are summarized in the model description document.37

The AIM-Technology model represents various energy technologies across multiple sectors. On the energy demand side, the industry, buildings, and transport sectors are disaggregated into various industrial products, building energy services, and transport modes. The energy supply side includes primary energy extraction of fossil fuels and biomass, alongside transformation processes such as electricity and hydrogen generation. The model includes synthetic liquids and gases as CCU-based e-fuels, including their international trade.7 Hydrogen production pathways include conversion from fossil fuels (coal, gas and oil) or biomass, as well as electrolysis. The CO2 sources for e-fuel production include fossil-based sources (including fossil and industrial process CO2 emissions), and carbon-neutral sources that are derived from atmospheric carbon, including biomass and DAC. Both energy and CO2 sources for e-fuel production are determined endogenously through cost optimization. On the energy demand side, e-fuels can substitute for conventional liquid fuels and natural gas usage in the industry, transport, and buildings sectors. In the original model configuration, CO2 emissions from e-fuel combustion are accounted for at the production phase if they are derived from fossil-based CO2. In this study, an additional emission accounting method was introduced whereby the carbon footprint of e-fuels was included in the consumer’s emissions. E-fuels generated using carbon-neutral sources have no associated carbon footprint; thus, their CO2 emissions were not counted by either producers or consumers. For clarity, e-fuels were defined here as hydrocarbons synthesized from CO2 and hydrogen; other hydrogen derivatives, such as ammonia, were excluded from this category.

The AIM-Technology model explicitly incorporates international trade in coal, crude oil, natural gas, oil products, bioenergy and hydrogen derivatives (ammonia and e-fuels). While energy carriers are primarily transported via maritime shipping, gases and crude oil can also be traded by pipeline where infrastructure is currently available. A schematic overview of the energy trade flows is provided in Figure S21, where EXC and PES represent energy carriers used for interregional trade and primary domestic energy supply, respectively. Additionally, DOM and IMP indicate domestic energy supply and imports, respectively. Through this representation, the model assumes bilateral trade among regions. The trade balance is determined based on global cost minimization, subject to regional energy demand, energy production costs, and transport costs. Regarding transport costs, the initial investment and operating costs for energy transport facilities are derived from the literature,38,39 as summarized in Table S1.

Scenario assumptions

To assess the risks of emission leakage, several climate and technological scenarios were examined (Table 1). Regarding the accounting of fossil-based e-fuel’s carbon footprint, two options were compared in this study: based on production- or consumption-based methods. In the production-based accounting method (Prod), which was originally modeled in AIM-Technology, the carbon footprint of fossil-based e-fuels was attributed to CO2 emissions in the producing country. Consequently, if e-fuel derived from fossil-based carbon was traded internationally, the importing country could treat these fuels as carbon-free energy carriers. In contrast, the consumption-based accounting (Cons) method, which was introduced in this study, assigned the carbon footprint of fossil-based e-fuels to the consuming country’s emissions. Under this approach, carbon pricing in the importing country applies to imported fossil-based e-fuels, which is analogous to the direct use of fossil fuels.

In terms of emission scenarios, various pathways were examined based on the cumulative CO2 emissions. The 500C, 700C, 1000C, and 1400C scenarios corresponded to the cumulative global CO2 emissions by 2100 of 500, 700, 1000 and 1400 Gt-CO2, respectively, which aligned with the scenario design of the previous study.36,41 Under this framework, the 500C scenario corresponded to the 1.5°C goal of the Paris Agreement, where global energy-related CO2 emissions reach nearly zero by 2050. This constraint was applied annually to the global energy and industrial process CO2 emissions. Furthermore, impact of harmonized or heterogeneous international climate policy was assessed in this study. To this end, regionally different climate policy (DiffCP) scenarios were prepared based on the NDCs and their extensions to 2050. The near-term emission level according to the NDCs was taken from an existing study.42 The mitigation effort extension approach was based on the assumption that regional differences in carbon price for high ambition regions remained by 2050, which aligned with the existing study’s approach. In the harmonized climate policy cases (HarmCP), each region reduced emissions under uniform global carbon prices to meet the global emission pathways in each scenario. The socioeconomic conditions were based on the Shared Socio-economic Pathways 2 (SSP2)43 in all scenarios.

In the sensitivity scenario analyses, the effect of different assumptions on key technologies associated with e-fuel production have been assessed. This includes solar and wind power generation and DAC. The costs of solar and wind power in the AdvTech case were assumed to decrease by 2050 in line with International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) estimates,44,45 whereas the assumptions of the Default case were based on the IEA World Energy Outlook.46 Technical parameter values for DAC were based on the existing literature from multi-model assessment of DAC.47 Values for the Default scenario used the high estimate, whereas the AdvTech scenarios used the low estimates of parameter values. Parameter assumptions for these technologies in the sensitivity cases are summarized in Figure S1.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The energy system model was formulated and solved in GAMS 50.3.0 using the CPLEX solver 22.1.2.0. As this study employs a deterministic optimization framework rather than stochastic analysis, no statistical testing was performed. Instead, the robustness of the results was assessed through sensitivity analysis, where cost assumptions on renewables and carbon capture technologies were varied as summarized in Figure S1.
