Beyond the JAK2 mutation: The inflammasome, clonal stability, and the thrombotic niche in myeloproliferative neoplasms
Authors: Amr Ali Mohamed Abdelgawwad El-Sehrawy, Mutaz Jamal Al-khreisat, Makhfirat Kibriyeva, Islamova Dilbar, Fotima Axmedova, Jbar Abady Mohammed, Swati Mishra, Navin Kumar Tailor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Philadelphia-negative myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) carry a disproportionate thrombotic burden that cannot be explained by conventional cardiovascular risk factors or blood count parameters alone. This review synthesizes emerging evidence positioning MPN-associated thrombosis as a distinct pathobiologic entity, clonal thrombo-inflammation, driven by the convergence of somatic mutations and innate immune activation. We examine the continuum from clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) to overt MPN, highlighting how Janus kinase 2 (JAK2)V617F and other driver mutations reprogram myeloid cells toward hyperinflammatory phenotypes. A recurring mechanistic theme is NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation and interleukin-1 family signaling, which may create a feed-forward loop in which mutant clones amplify inflammatory circuits that, in turn, may enhance clonal fitness and contribute to thrombogenicity across multiple cellular compartments. We propose the ‘thrombotic niche’ as a conceptual, multi-compartment model encompassing mutant hematopoietic stem cells, hyperinflammatory myeloid effectors, hyperreactive platelets, platelet-leukocyte aggregates, and activated endothelium, but it remains a hypothesis-generating framework that lacks direct prospective clinical validation. Current cytoreductive strategies inadequately address this underlying biology, leaving substantial residual vascular risk. Emerging anti-inflammatory and anti-clonal strategies targeting interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) (canakinumab), mutant-selective JAK2 inhibition, NLRP3 inflammasome blockade, and P-selectin-mediated adhesion are biologically plausible, but their ability to reduce thrombotic events in MPN remains unproven and should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than established clinical benefit. We conclude by outlining a translational research agenda integrating inflammation-aware risk stratification, niche-directed imaging, and spatial multi-omics to guide precision anti-inflammatory interventions in MPN.Graphical abstractThe clonal thrombo‑inflammation cycle in myeloproliferative neoplasms. Introduction: The convergence of hemato-oncology and cardio-inflammation

The clinical landscape of Philadelphia negative myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) reveals a paradox in which patients sustain a strikingly high burden of both arterial and venous thrombosis that is not fully explained by conventional cardiovascular risk factors and frequently predates formal hematologic diagnosis [1]. The epidemiology of these events shows that thrombotic episodes may be the presenting manifestation in a substantial minority of cases, and that the excess risk persists despite guideline-directed antithrombotic and cytoreductive strategies [1, 2]. Molecular analyses have clarified that the Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) V617F mutation in particular confers an independent thrombotic propensity, with higher variant allele frequencies correlating with greater event rates, yet reduction of blood counts with agents such as hydroxyurea does not consistently eradicate this vascular vulnerability [3, 4]. These clinical findings point to mechanisms beyond simple hemorheology or platelet number that anchor thrombosis to clonal and inflammatory biology rather than to traditional cardiometabolic risk alone [5]. However, this picture is heterogeneous: age, prior thrombosis, hematocrit control, leukocytosis, and mutation-specific biology still determine absolute risk, so the inflammatory model should be presented as complementary to, not a replacement for, traditional risk assessment [6–9].

Concurrently, the emergence of clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) has recast our understanding of the temporal and mechanistic links between somatic hematopoietic mutations and cardiovascular disease [10, 11]. Landmark human studies show that CHIP is associated with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, while murine models provide mechanistic evidence that mutant myeloid cells can promote atherogenesis through proinflammatory myeloid cell phenotypes, establishing CHIP itself as an independent and mechanistically plausible risk factor for cardiovascular events [11, 12]. Subsequent studies have shown that canonical MPN driver mutations, including JAK2 V617F, can be detected long before overt MPN and therefore occupy a continuum in which CHIP may seed later clonal expansion into frank myeloproliferative disease [13, 14]. Recognizing this CHIP to MPN evolutionary axis is critical because it reframes thrombosis as not only a complication of established malignancy but also as a potential downstream consequence of mutationally reprogrammed innate immune effectors emerging years prior to hematologic diagnosis [10, 11].

Mechanistic advances implicate inflammasome biology and interleukin-1 family signaling as important mediators that may link mutant hematopoietic clones to the vascular wall and thrombotic niche [15, 16]. Experimental deletion or pharmacologic blockade of interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) in JAK2 V617F models attenuates clonal expansion, marrow remodeling, and downstream prothrombotic phenotypes, supporting a model in which mutant clones amplify innate immune circuits that, in turn, foster thromboinflammation [16, 17]. Recent work implicates NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation within JAK2 mutant hematopoietic compartments as a driver of excessive IL-1β and IL-18 release with direct effects on platelet production and myeloid skewing, thereby mechanistically linking intrinsic clonal signals to peripheral thrombotic biology [17, 18].

Building on these convergent epidemiologic and mechanistic insights, we propose that MPN-associated thrombosis should be conceptualized as clonal thrombo-inflammation, a discrete pathobiologic entity in which a mutant hematopoietic clone and a self-perpetuating inflammatory microenvironment form a feed-forward loop that sustains vascular risk [4, 16]. Importantly, the ‘thrombotic niche’ is presented here as a conceptual framework rather than a clinically validated entity, and its incremental value over established clinico-molecular risk models remains to be demonstrated [19–21]. This reconceptualization has therapeutic implications because it suggests that controlling cell counts alone may be insufficient; future prevention strategies may need to combine clonal suppression with interruption of inflammasome and interleukin 1 driven circuits [3, 16]. The aim of this review is to synthesize recent human and experimental data that validate the CHIP to MPN continuum, to critically appraise evidence that inflammasome-mediated inflammation is a proximate driver of thrombotic risk, and to advance a translational framework for trials that target both the clone and its inflammatory ecosystem [11, 16].

The spectrum of clonal dominance: From CHIP to overt MPN

CHIP as a cardiovascular risk factor

CHIP is now recognized not merely as an age related hematologic curiosity but as a clinically important driver of cardiovascular disease, with seminal population analyses demonstrating that carriers of somatic mutations in genes such as DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, and JAK2 have substantially higher rates of coronary artery disease and coronary calcification compared with noncarriers [11]. Mutant hematopoietic clones exert outsized cardiovascular effects even when variant allele fractions are low, a finding that has been replicated across diverse cohorts and meta-analyses and that argues for a causal, mutation driven relationship rather than simple confounding by age or comorbidity [22]. Mechanistic interrogation in murine atherosclerosis models supplied the necessary link by showing that marrow reconstitution with Tet2 deficient cells increases lesion size and inflammation, establishing a biologically plausible pathway from mutant myeloid cells to accelerated atherogenesis [23]. At the cellular level the dominant model implicates hyperinflammatory macrophages derived from mutant clones; for example Tet2 loss enhances NLRP3 inflammasome activation and interleukin 1β secretion in macrophages, thereby potentiating vascular inflammation and plaque progression [23]. Translationally this axis is compelling because inflammasome inhibition or IL 1 blockade attenuates atherosclerosis in these preclinical systems and correlates with reduced cardiovascular events in humans, supporting the concept that clonal mutations remodel innate immune function to become a proximal cardiovascular risk factor [11, 23].

The JAK2V617F advantage

Among CHIP driver alleles JAK2 V617F stands out for its disproportionately large effect on thrombotic risk, with recent analyses reporting a markedly higher hazard for arterial and venous thromboembolism in carriers of JAK2 mutant clones compared with carriers of other CHIP variants [22, 24]. This augmented cardiovascular phenotype appears to be more than a simple function of clone size because effect sizes for JAK2 exceed those predicted by variant allele fraction alone in pooled cohorts, implying mutation specific gain of function biology [22]. Mechanistically JAK2 V617F produces distinctive perturbations that plausibly amplify thrombogenicity: cell intrinsic activation of Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) signaling in myeloid progeny favors erythrocytosis and myeloid skewing, platelets from JAK2 mutant backgrounds demonstrate hyperreactivity, and there is emerging evidence for direct cross talk between JAK2 mutant cells and the endothelium that promotes a proadhesive vascular surface [22, 24]. Murine and human data further indicate that JAK2 driven clones elicit stronger inflammasome related cytokine signatures than many other CHIP alleles, offering a mechanistic bridge to the observed thromboinflammatory phenotype [22]. Clinically this constellation helps explain why JAK2 mutant clonal hematopoiesis behaves more like a systemic prothrombotic disorder than a silent precursor state and why JAK2 carriers frequently present with vascular events well before overt hematological disease is diagnosed [22, 24].

Transition to overt MPN: The second hit

Progression from latent clonal hematopoiesis to overt MPN is not a single-step event; it usually reflects a series of second hits acting on a pre-existing driver clone. One important class is inherited susceptibility, including the JAK2 46/1 haplotype and TERT-associated germline variants, which predispose to acquisition or expansion of JAK2-mutant clones [25–27]. A second class is cooperating somatic evolution. MPNs commonly acquire additional mutations over time, and studies show that additional lesions present in the same cells as JAK2V617F can alter cell fate and accelerate progression; mutation order itself also modifies phenotype. Copy-number change is relevant as well, because acquired 9p uniparental disomy can convert JAK2V617F to homozygosity, increasing signaling output and contributing to phenotypic switch and less favorable outcomes [28, 29]. A third class is non-genetic selection pressure from inflammation and marrow niche stress. TNFα promotes clonal dominance of JAK2V617F-positive cells, inflammatory environments favor mutant over wild-type progenitors, and the JAK2V617F-bearing vascular niche can promote clonal expansion; IL-1β also promotes early JAK2-mutant clone expansion in experimental models [30–33]. Together, these hits converge on enhanced JAK/STAT signaling, inflammatory resistance, altered self-renewal, and lineage skewing toward hyperinflammatory myeloid and megakaryocytic progeny, ultimately driving the transition from CHIP to overt PV, ET, or MF [28, 30]. Synthesizing these strands, the CHIP to MPN transition is best considered a multistep process in which germline loci and environmental inflammation cooperate to convert an initially indolent mutant clone into a dominant, clinically manifest disease with attendant thromboinflammatory complications [25, 34]. The inflammatory ecosystem in MPNs: Beyond the blood count

The source of the fire: The malignant clone as an inflammatory organ

The contemporary view of MPNs positions the mutant hematopoietic clone not simply as a proliferative lesion but as a distributed inflammatory organ that continuously secretes cytokines and danger signals capable of remodeling bone marrow, blood and vascular compartments, a concept supported by genetic and interventional studies showing that JAK2 V617F hematopoietic cells are a major source of IL 1β in experimental MPN and that IL 1β blockade reduces marrow fibrosis and systemic inflammation [16]. At the cellular level, committed myeloid progeny of JAK2 mutant clones exhibit an inflammatory transcriptional program with neutrophils and monocytes producing elevated IL 1β, IL 6 and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) relative to non mutant counterparts, and lineage restricted expression of JAK2 V617F in neutrophils alone is sufficient to raise systemic IL 1 levels in murine models [16, 35]. Megakaryocytes and platelets may amplify this milieu by abnormally expressing adhesion molecules such as P-selectin, shedding pro inflammatory microparticles, and forming stable neutrophil platelet aggregates that promote coagulation and local cytokine transfer; these platelet-mediated mechanisms provide a plausible cellular bridge between clonal hematopoiesis and thromboinflammation [36, 37]. Endothelial involvement is increasingly appreciated as a non passive participant because somatic detection of JAK2 V617F in endothelial cells from affected patients and experimental data showing that JAK2 positive extracellular materials provoke endothelial activation indicate that endothelial clonality or clonal crosstalk induces adhesion molecule upregulation and a prothrombotic endothelial surface [38, 39]. Taken together, these lines of evidence argue that the malignant clone operates across multiple cell types to maintain a self reinforcing inflammatory ecosystem that cannot be inferred solely from peripheral blood counts and which directly seeds sites of thrombosis [16, 37].

Key inflammatory pathways and effectors

Convergence of somatic mutation driven signalling and innate immune activation makes the JAK-STAT axis the central hub in which proliferative and inflammatory programs are integrated, such that constitutive JAK2 activity drives STAT transcriptional programs that upregulate cytokines, chemokines and survival signals in both stem and effector compartments; integrated genomic analyses and functional studies across MPN genotypes consistently show JAK-STAT activation as a unifying disease node [40, 41]. Mechanistically, mutant clones prime the inflammasome via NF κB dependent transcriptional upregulation and create permissive conditions for activation through cell intrinsic oxidative stress and extracellular danger signals such as mutant cell derived DNA and exosomes, producing the massive IL 1 family cytokine release that links marrow events to vascular inflammation [16, 39, 43, 44]. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) likely represent a context-dependent effector arm rather than a uniformly established independent driver of thrombosis in MPN. JAK2-mutant neutrophils can be primed for NET formation, and preclinical work supports a prothrombotic role for NETs; however, human MPN data also show elevated NETosis without a consistent association with thrombotic events. The most balanced interpretation is that NETs amplify thrombosis when they cooperate with platelet activation and endothelial injury, rather than acting as a sole causal determinant [45–47]. The sum of these pathways produces a feed forward loop: constitutive JAK-STAT signalling amplifies inflammasome priming and cytokine production, inflammasome activation generates IL 1β and IL 18 that further expand and bias mutant myelopoiesis, and NETs together with platelet microparticles and activated endothelium convert inflammation into localized thrombosis, a network that explains why cytoreduction alone incompletely mitigates vascular risk [23, 41, 45]. Deconstructing the ‘Thrombotic Niche’: A conceptual multi-compartment model

The vicious cycle of clonal expansion and inflammation

MPNs may exemplify a self-sustaining biological circuit in which mutant hematopoietic clones produce inflammatory mediators that both remodel the marrow niche and increase the competitive fitness of the clone, thereby generating a positive feedback loop of clonal expansion and inflammation [16]. Evidence from JAK2 V617F murine models demonstrates that hematopoietic cell derived interleukin 1β is a principal upstream mediator of marrow fibrosis, niche damage and augmented megakaryopoiesis, and that genetic or pharmacologic disruption of IL 1β signaling reduces both inflammation and disease phenotype [16]. Parallel studies of TET2 mutant clonal hematopoiesis reveal a convergent mechanism in which mutant myeloid cells adopt hyperinflammatory programs mediated by NLRP3 inflammasome activation and interleukin 1β secretion, thereby accelerating atherosclerosis and reinforcing inflammatory selection pressures within hematopoiesis [23]. Clinically, patients with MPN maintain elevated circulating cytokines including IL 1β, IL 6 and tumor necrosis factor alpha despite cytoreductive therapy, which helps to explain why reduction of peripheral counts alone incompletely mitigates vascular risk [16, 48]. Mechanistically, inflammatory cytokines and niche destruction selectively suppress wild type progenitors while providing proliferative cues to JAK-STAT driven clones, converting systemic inflammation into a Darwinian selection pressure that stabilizes clonal dominance [16, 23]. This model reframes MPN as an inflammatory organ disorder in which interrupting the self sustaining IL 1β driven loop is a rational translational strategy to both blunt clonal fitness and reduce thromboinflammatory complications [16, 23].

The somatic mutation-inflammation cycle in atherogenesis

Somatic mutant macrophages seeded from clonal hematopoiesis are repeatedly detected within atherosclerotic plaques and exert outsized influence on plaque biology by adopting a hyperinflammatory phenotype that destabilizes lesions [11, 23]. Experimental bone marrow chimeras carrying Tet2 deficiency or Jak2 V617F clones develop larger, more inflamed plaques with expanded necrotic cores in murine atherosclerosis models, linking specific hematopoietic genotypes to plaque vulnerability in preclinical systems [22, 23, 49]. At the cellular level these mutant macrophages display enhanced NLRP3 inflammasome activity and increased interleukin 1β and interleukin 18 release, molecular events that promote local matrix degradation, necrosis and cap thinning, all canonical pathways that raise the risk of plaque rupture and downstream arterial thrombosis [23]. Human cohort analyses corroborate these mechanistic findings by showing that carriers of CHIP, and especially those with JAK2 or TET2 mutations, have higher rates of coronary events and more rapid progression of atherosclerotic disease than noncarriers [11, 22]. Taken together, these data support a somatic mutation to inflammation to plaque instability axis in which clonal myeloid effectors actively remodel vascular lesions rather than merely reflecting a bystander phenomenon [22, 23].

Platelet–leukocyte aggregates as a functional synapse

Platelet–leukocyte aggregates form a mobile, hyperactive thrombotic unit that functionally couples mutant platelets to proinflammatory myeloid effectors and thereby concentrates coagulation and inflammatory mediators at sites of vascular injury [50, 51]. In MPNs, platelets express higher levels of P selectin and shed prothrombotic microparticles that bind P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL-1) on neutrophils and monocytes, stabilizing aggregates that facilitate mutual cellular activation and local thrombin generation [50, 52]. Experimental and clinical studies show that JAK2 driven megakaryocytes produce reticulated, hyperreactive platelets and that platelet specific expression of Jak2 V617F is sufficient to increase platelet activation and accelerate arterial thrombosis in mice, emphasizing the independent contribution of mutant platelets to the thrombotic niche [22, 53]. Moreover, platelet mediated transfer of cytokines, microRNA and extracellular vesicles to adherent leukocytes and endothelium amplifies local inflammasome priming and endothelial activation, converting platelet–leukocyte contacts into a biochemical synapse for thromboinflammation [16, 50]. Clinically measurable elevations in circulating leukocyte–platelet aggregates correlate with prior thrombosis and predict recurrent events in MPN cohorts, supporting the pathophysiologic and prognostic relevance of these complexes [51].

Clonal diversity and its impact on cardiovascular phenotype

Genotype dictates biological behavior in MPN and generates distinct cardio inflammatory phenotypes: JAK2 V617F is classically associated with erythrocytosis, leukocytosis and frequent endothelial involvement, features that together predispose to both arterial and venous thrombosis [22, 48]. In contrast, CALR mutated disease more often manifests as isolated thrombocytosis with a different cytokine milieu and generally lower thrombotic risk in essential thrombocythemia cohorts, a clinical divergence that reflects underlying differences in leukocyte activation and platelet function between genotypes [54, 55]. MPL mutations yield yet another phenotype with variable thrombotic tendencies that overlap but are not identical to JAK2 and CALR driven biology [48]. Beyond driver lesions, high molecular risk co-mutations such as ASXL1 and EZH2 alter epigenetic programs in hematopoietic stem cells and are robustly associated with adverse hematologic outcomes, but their contribution to cardiovascular risk is less well defined and likely mediated through modification of inflammatory transcriptional networks rather than direct thrombogenic mechanisms [56, 57]. Some emerging data suggest that ASXL1 mutated clones present with proinflammatory signatures that could amplify vascular inflammation, yet prospective evidence that these variants worsen cardiovascular outcomes independent of their role in disease progression is currently limited [56, 58]. Therefore, appreciating clonal diversity is essential for precision cardiovascular risk assessment in MPN because genotype and co-mutational context shape the inflammatory architecture of the thrombotic niche and consequently determine arterial versus venous risk patterns and therapeutic responsiveness [22, 48]. At the same time, genotype is only one layer of risk; CALR- and MPL-mutated disease often follows a different thrombotic trajectory than JAK2-mutated disease, and the clinical history of prior thrombosis remains one of the strongest predictors of recurrence [6–8].

Limitations of the thrombotic niche model

The thrombotic niche should be interpreted as a conceptual, hypothesis-generating framework rather than a clinically validated entity. To date, no prospective study has directly tested the full construct in patients, and its incremental value over established clinico-molecular risk models remains unproven [19–21]. In addition, the relative contribution of each compartment likely varies by MPN subtype and disease phase, and the model overlaps with broader thromboinflammation and endothelial dysfunction frameworks. Future validation should therefore use longitudinal cohorts, multimodal imaging, spatial multi-omics, and outcome-driven risk modeling to determine whether the niche adds predictive or therapeutic value beyond existing approaches [19, 21, 59].

Therapeutic horizons: Breaking the cycle

Limitations of current cytoreduction

Current cytoreductive strategies remain the backbone of thrombosis prevention in MPNs, yet they may not fully address the underlying clonal and inflammatory biology implicated in thromboinflammation. Hydroxyurea reliably lowers hematocrit, leukocyte and platelet counts and reduces the incidence of arterial events in large pooled series, but substantial residual thrombotic risk persists, particularly in venous beds such as splanchnic veins and in patients who continue to exhibit inflammatory markers despite count control [3]. Multiple contemporary cohort and mechanistic studies show that hydroxyurea reduces systemic leukocyte indices but does not uniformly normalize proinflammatory cytokines and innate immune activation signatures that originate from the mutant clone, suggesting that cytoreduction addresses a downstream epiphenomenon rather than the source of pathogenic inflammation [60].

Interferon alpha, especially pegylated formulations, has advanced the field by producing durable hematologic responses and by reducing variant allele fractions in a substantial fraction of patients, and in selected series deep molecular remissions are reported. Nonetheless, molecular eradication is incomplete in many treated patients, and interferon does not universally eliminate the inflammatory phenotype or the excess cardiovascular risk; long term data show that reductions in variant allele fraction (VAF) are heterogeneous and that clinical thrombotic events continue to occur in some molecular responders [61]. This partial molecular effect implies that interferon may alter clonal dynamics without fully extinguishing the inflammatory ecosystem propagated by residual mutant progeny and their nonhematopoietic partners.

Ruxolitinib exemplifies a paradigm of JAK-pathway control within MPN. In randomized trials and real world cohorts it improves symptoms, spleen size, and circulating inflammatory markers, and some studies suggest fewer thromboembolic events; however, those findings were not generated by thrombosis-powered studies and the signal is not uniform across indications or analyses. Accordingly, ruxolitinib should be viewed primarily as a disease-control and symptom-directed therapy with a possible but unconfirmed antithrombotic effect [62–65]. In short, existing cytoreductive agents either do not reliably remove the mutant clone or do not sufficiently interrupt the inflammasome-driven circuits that sustain the thrombotic niche, and their immunomodulatory effects have not yet translated into clearly demonstrated cardiovascular protection in MPN populations. Persistent event rates despite contemporary cytoreductive and antithrombotic management indicate that anti-inflammatory treatment should be viewed as additive to classical risk control, not as a substitute for control of hematocrit and other established risk modifiers [1, 2, 8].

Novel anti-inflammatory and targeted strategies

The conceptual reframing of MPN thrombosis as clonal thrombo-inflammation opens multiple therapeutic entry points, but most remain investigational with respect to hard vascular outcomes. Canakinumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting interleukin-1β, has established cardiovascular benefit in atherosclerotic disease, and exploratory analyses suggest that TET2-driven clonal hematopoiesis may identify a subgroup with heightened inflammatory responsiveness. In MPN, however, the available phase II study is designed to evaluate safety, symptoms, spleen volume, fibrosis, cytokines, and variant allele fraction; it does not yet establish thrombotic benefit. A parallel strategy is to develop mutant-selective JAK2 inhibitors that more potently ablate JAK2V617F clones while sparing wild type JAK2 function, but whether such agents translate into lower thrombotic risk remains unknown. Targeting the inflammasome itself is supported by preclinical rationale, yet clinical efficacy in MPN and vascular outcome data are still lacking. Finally, disrupting P-selectin mediated platelet–leukocyte interactions is a rational hypothesis, but direct evidence in MPN is absent. Taken together, these strategies are biologically plausible rather than clinically validated, and the near-term goal should be to test whether they improve symptoms, biomarker profiles, clonal burden, and, critically, prospectively adjudicated thrombotic endpoints [64, 66].

A parallel strategy is to develop mutant-selective JAK2 inhibitors that more potently ablate JAK2V617F clones while sparing wild type JAK2 function. Early clinical stage compounds such as LY2784544 (gandotinib) demonstrated preferential activity against the V617F mutant in phase 1 work, and ongoing medicinal chemistry and structural biology efforts aim to yield allosteric or mutant-biased inhibitors that could reduce allele burden with less hematologic toxicity than pan-JAK blockade. If successful, mutant-selective JAK2 inhibition could directly remove the cellular source of inflammasome priming and microparticle release, offering a disease modifying path to eliminate the root of thromboinflammation [67–69].

Targeting the inflammasome itself represents a complementary tactic. MCC950 is the prototype small molecule NLRP3 inhibitor that potently blocks inflammasome activation and IL-1β release in multiple preclinical models and that has been used extensively to demonstrate proof of concept that inflammasome blockade reduces atherosclerosis and inflammatory organ damage. Although clinical development of MCC950 was previously halted due to safety concerns, next generation NLRP3 antagonists with improved safety and pharmacology are advancing and could directly dismantle the IL-1β/IL-18 axis that fuels clonal selection and vascular inflammation in MPN [70, 71].

Finally, disrupting the cellular mechanics of the thrombotic niche is a promising adjunct strategy. Blocking P-selectin mediated platelet–leukocyte interactions with crizanlizumab proved that adhesion blockade can reduce vaso-occlusive biology in sickle cell disease and has been shown in translational models to lower biomarkers of thrombosis and vascular inflammation. Given the centrality of platelet–leukocyte aggregates and P-selectin biology in MPN thromboinflammation, targeted P-selectin inhibition is a rational repurposing hypothesis that is currently discussed in the literature and is being considered in early translational frameworks for MPN-associated splanchnic and systemic thrombosis [72, 73].

Taken together, these strategies illustrate a bifurcated therapeutic horizon: interventions that more definitively eliminate or suppress the mutant clone plus agents that interrupt the inflammasome and adhesion axes which translate clonal signals into thrombosis. The most plausible path to durable cardiovascular protection in MPN will likely require combination regimens that simultaneously reduce allele burden and neutralize the inflammatory ecosystem that sustains the thrombotic niche. Rigorous biomarker studies and randomized trials that include vascular endpoints are urgently needed to determine whether these approaches can break the cycle of clonal thrombo-inflammation. Future directions and unanswered questions

The need for an integrated cardiovascular risk score for MPNs

Current thrombotic risk stratification schemes in MPNs, including IPSET and its variants, reliably capture classical clinical predictors such as age, prior thrombosis and JAK2 status but systematically underperform when confronted with the residual, inflammation-driven vascular risk carried by many patients. Evidence from large contemporary cohorts indicates that markers of thromboinflammation correlate with incident thrombosis independently of IPSET components, arguing that a next-generation score must incorporate circulating inflammatory biomarkers such as high sensitivity C reactive protein, interleukin 6 and interleukin 1β in addition to measures of genetic complexity including variant allele fraction and co-mutational burden [19, 20]. Several translational studies further show that these biomarkers are mechanistically linked to clonal biology rather than acting as nonspecific correlates of comorbidity, strengthening the case for their formal incorporation into risk models that would reclassify patients who currently appear low risk by count based scores but harbor an active thromboinflammatory phenotype [21]. Any revised score should therefore preserve age and prior thrombosis, incorporate hematocrit control where relevant, and be validated separately across PV, ET, and MF because patient-level heterogeneity limits a one-size-fits-all model [6–9].

The role of the bone marrow niche

The bone marrow microenvironment is not a passive stage on which mutated hematopoietic cells act but an active adjudicator of clonal fitness that both selects for mutant progenitors and imprints inflammatory programs transmitted to mature peripheral effectors. Spatial transcriptomic and single-cell mapping of myelofibrosis and related MPN marrow reveal vascular and stromal niches with stress signatures, inflammatory ligand expression and matrix remodelling that preferentially colocalize with mutant clones, indicating that niche remodeling and inflammation create a selective ecology for clonal dominance [59, 81, 82]. Preclinical work shows that inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor alpha and IL 1β can both promote survival of TET2 or JAK2 mutant progenitors and suppress competing wild type progenitors, a mechanism that converts systemic inflammation into a marrow level selective pressure and that explains how a mutationally altered marrow teaches a proinflammatory phenotype to circulating myeloid cells [23, 83]. Understanding which niche signals are necessary and sufficient for this teaching phenomenon is essential because interrupting niche-derived cues could prevent peripheral thromboinflammatory sequelae without requiring full molecular eradication of the clone.

Imaging the inflammatory niche

Noninvasive imaging offers a means to visualize the biologic substrate of risk rather than relying solely on blood biomarkers. Positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) using tracers such as 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18 F-FDG) for metabolic inflammation and 18 F-sodium fluoride (18 F-NaF) for active microcalcification can identify metabolically hot plaques and vascular beds at risk in patients with atherosclerosis, and these modalities are attractive candidates for stratifying MPN patients in whom inflammation is presumed to drive plaque vulnerability [78, 79]. Although mutation-specific radiotracers are not yet ready for clinical use in this context, dual-tracer strategies that combine inflammation imaging with molecular or cellular markers of myeloid activity could permit direct in vivo visualization of plaques enriched for mutant macrophages or heightened inflammasome activity [77]. Prospectively linking PET indices of plaque inflammation to circulating clonal metrics and hard vascular outcomes in MPN cohorts would be a high yield translational path that could validate imaging as a companion diagnostic for inflammation-directed therapy.

Single-cell and spatial multi-omics

Resolving the cellular choreography that sustains clonal thromboinflammation requires technologies that preserve positional context while providing molecular depth. Single-cell and spatial multi-omic platforms are now capable of mapping cell identity, somatic genotype, transcriptome and epigenome within intact marrow and vascular tissue, and recent applications in myelofibrosis demonstrate how these methods can deconvolute niche cell states and ligand–receptor interactions [59, 80]. Applied to paired bone marrow and atherosclerotic plaque samples, integrated single-cell and spatial analyses could pinpoint where mutant and wild type cells interact, identify the precise mediators of inflammasome priming, and reveal which microenvironments are most responsible for producing prothrombotic myeloid effectors. Such datasets would enable causal inference far beyond bulk correlation, provide biomarker candidates for noninvasive monitoring and suggest tightly targeted interventions that disrupt specific cell-cell dialogues rather than broadly suppressing immunity.

Collectively these four priorities define a research agenda that is simultaneously clinical and mechanistic: build and validate an inflammation-aware MPN cardiovascular score, dissect and manipulate marrow niche selection, develop imaging biomarkers that localize biological risk, and apply single-cell spatial discovery to nominate actionable cellular interactions. Success will require coordinated consortia with harmonized biobanking, standardized imaging protocols and prospective vascular endpoints so that interventions designed to break the clonal thromboinflammatory loop can be evaluated on the most relevant clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

The conceptual framework advanced in this review repositions MPN-associated thrombosis not as a passive consequence of hyperviscosity or elevated blood counts, but as an active, multi-compartment process of clonal thrombo-inflammation. The evidence synthesized here suggests that mutant hematopoietic clones, particularly those driven by JAK2V617F, may function as distributed inflammatory organs, continuously seeding the circulation with hyperinflammatory myeloid effectors, hyperreactive platelets, and prothrombotic extracellular vesicles. The NLRP3 inflammasome and interleukin-1 family cytokines emerge as central nodes that transduce clonal signals into vascular pathology, creating a self-sustaining loop in which inflammation enhances clonal fitness while the expanding clone amplifies systemic thromboinflammation. This paradigm explains the residual thrombotic risk observed despite guideline-directed cytoreduction and argues that effective cardiovascular protection requires parallel strategies: more potent clonal suppression combined with targeted interruption of inflammasome-driven inflammatory circuits. The convergence of CHIP biology, MPN pathogenesis, and vascular inflammation research has illuminated rational therapeutic opportunities, from IL-1β blockade to mutant-selective JAK2 inhibition and P-selectin antagonism. Realizing the promise of these approaches will require prospective trials incorporating vascular endpoints, the development and validation of inflammation-aware risk stratification tools, and mechanistic studies leveraging spatial multi-omics to decode the cellular dialogues sustaining the thrombotic niche. As a conceptual framework, however, the thrombotic niche remains to be prospectively validated, and future work must determine whether it improves prediction or treatment selection beyond existing risk models before it can be considered a clinically actionable construct.
