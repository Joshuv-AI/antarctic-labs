An infinite family of homogeneous discrete equations with the Laurent property
Andrei K. Svinin

Abstract
. We present and investigate a new infinite family of homogeneous equations which possess the Laurent property. The first representative in this family is the well-known Somos-5 recurrence.



Introduction

Recently, considerable interest has been shown in nonlinear equations of the form

t_{n}t_{n+N}=L\left(t_{n+1},\ldots,t_{n+N-1}\right),

(0.1)

which possess the Laurent property
[
1
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
10
]
, where
L
is assumed to be a (Laurent) polynomial.

Definition 0.1
.

Equation (
0.1
) is said to have the Laurent property if
t_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}[t_{0}^{\pm 1},\ldots,t_{N-1}^{\pm 1}],\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

The requirement for nonlinear equation formulated by this definition seems extremely strict, and one is entitled to ask whether such equations even exist, but as it turns out, they do not just exist – there are actually quite a lot of such recurrences. A classic example of such equations represents the class of Gale-Robinson recurrences which are written in the form

t_{n}t_{n+N}=\alpha t_{n+a}t_{n+N-a}+\beta t_{n+c}t_{n+N-c},

(0.2)

where
N\geq 4
and
1\leq a<c\leq\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor
. Note that
\alpha
and
\beta
, in (
0.2
) are assumed to be arbitrary parameters, but in applications, as a rule, they are equal to one.

It is clear that there is something behind the Laurent property. It is currently known that this property is linked to two key developments: the theory of cluster algebras by Fomin and Zelevinsky
[
5
]
and the broader framework of Laurent phenomenon algebras introduced by Lam and Pylyavskyy
[
10
]
.
In particular, Gale-Robinson recurrences provide a concrete example of a dynamical system that is fully described in the language of cluster algebras: it arises from quiver mutations, its Laurent property follows from general theorems of cluster algebras. Fomin and Zelevinsky, in their seminal work
[
5
]
, used precisely the Gale-Robinson recurrence (
0.2
) as one of the main examples to demonstrate and prove this property within the framework of their theory. Thus, cluster algebras provide a universal explanation for the
mysterious
Laurent property for this and similar classes of recurrences.

If the Laurent property is already known for some nonlinear recurrence, then an elementary consequence is the integrality of the terms of the sequence defined by this recurrence under certain conditions. Indeed, if we put
t_{j}=\pm 1
for
j=0,\ldots,N-1
, then we can be sure of the integerness of the corresponding sequence. In some cases, nonlinear recurrences generate integer sequences with a sufficiently large number of applications. Nonlinearity here is not an obstacle, but rather a source of rich structure; integrality is not a coincidence, but a manifestation of deep algebraic and geometric laws.

Allow us to delve a little deeper into the history. In the early 1980s, Michael Somos discovered several nonlinear recurrence relations which, despite their nonlinearity, generate integer sequences for suitable initial data. One of them is the equation

t_{n}t_{n+5}=\alpha t_{n+1}t_{n+4}+\beta t_{n+2}t_{n+3}

(0.3)

known in the literature as Somos-5. It is clear that (
0.3
) is a special case of the Gale-Robinson recurrence (
0.2
) for
\left(N,a,c\right)=\left(5,1,2\right)
.

The classical integer sequence generated by (
0.3
) and listed in the OEIS as A006721, is defined by the initial values
t_{j}=1
for
j=0,\ldots,4
and
\left(\alpha,\beta\right)=(1,1)
. This sequence surprisingly combines combinatorial, arithmetic, and geometric properties about which we would like to say a few words.
For example, in
[
3
]
, one can find the following lemma, which relates A006721 to the elliptic curve
E:y^{2}+xy=x\left(x-1\right)\left(x+2\right)
.

Lemma 0.2
.

The relation between A006721 and the elliptic curve
E
is determined by the relation

Q+nP=\left(\frac{t_{n+2}^{2}-t_{n}t_{n+4}}{t_{n+2}^{2}},\frac{4t_{n}t_{n+2}t_{n+4}-t_{n}^{2}t_{n+6}-t^{3}_{n+2}}{t_{n+2}^{3}}\right),

where a point
P=(2,2)
of infinite order and a point
Q=(0,0)
of order two on the curve.

Thus, the sequence encodes the complex arithmetic of adding points on the elliptic curve. The connection of this elliptic curve with the sequence A006721, as far as we know, was found by Elkies.

In the 1990s, Buchholz and Rathbun, in contrast to Schubert’s erroneous statement
[
4
]
, discovered an infinite family of Heronian triangles with two rational medians
[
2
]
. They noticed that this infinite family is somehow connected to A006721 and some associated sequence. Hone went further and provided explicit formulas expressing the sides of the triangles, the two rational medians, and their area directly in terms of the terms of two intertwined Somos-5 sequences
[
8
]
. The significance of these works is that they established that the parameters of the triangles in this infinite family
live
on corresponding elliptic curve. An infinite sequence of such Heronian triangles correspond exactly to the sequence of points
Q+nP
on the elliptic curve.

The aim of this work is to present and investigate an infinite family of homogeneous equations of the form

t_{n}t_{n+2g+3}=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{g}\alpha_{j}B^{j}_{2g-j}(n+1)}{\prod_{j=3}^{2g}t_{n+j}},\;g\geq 1,

(0.4)

which we denote as
R_{2g+3}
. The coefficients
\alpha_{j}
in (
0.4
) are supposed to be arbitrary.
On the right-hand side of (
0.4
), it is necessary to define the homogeneous discrete polynomials
B^{k}_{s}(n)
. In the next section, we will show how these polynomials are successively defined using a certain recurrence relation, but for now we only note that
B^{j}_{2g-j}(n)
is a homogeneous polynomial in the variables
\left(t_{n},\ldots,t_{n+2g+1}\right)
. The degree of this polynomial is
2g
. Thus, (
0.4
) is supposed to be a homogeneous recurrence relation of degree
2g
for
g\geq 1
. From what we have said, it follows that (
0.4
) is a relation of the form (
0.1
), where the right-hand side is a linear combination of certain Laurent polynomials.
Only in one case, namely
g=1
, should the product on the left-hand side of (
0.4
) be considered equal to one. In this case, (
0.4
) reduces to Somos-5 given by (
0.3
) with
\left(\alpha,\beta\right)=\left(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{0}\right)
.

In what follows, we will show that (
0.4
) are homogeneous recurrences of the form (
0.1
). For illustration, let us write explicitly the recurrence relation (
0.4
) for the case
g=2
. It has the following form:

t_{n}t_{n+7}=\alpha_{0}t_{n+2}t_{n+5}+\alpha_{1}\frac{t_{n+1}t_{n+4}^{2}t_{n+5}+t_{n+2}^{2}t_{n+5}^{2}+t_{n+2}t_{n+3}^{2}t_{n+6}}{t_{n+3}t_{n+4}}+\alpha_{2}t_{n+1}t_{n+6}.

(0.5)

It is obvious that this recurrence is of the form (
0.1
). Let us put
n=0
, so that corresponding
L
becomes a Laurent polynomial in the variables
\left(t_{1},\ldots,t_{6}\right)
. This polynomial exhibits a symmetry:
L(t_{1},\ldots,t_{6})=L(t_{6},\ldots,t_{1})
. In other words, renumbering the arguments in reverse order yields the same polynomial. This example suggest that this symmetry is likely a specific property not only of all recurrences in our family, but also of many other recurrences with the Laurent property found in the literature. Naturally, for this to hold, all the discrete polynomials
B^{k}_{s}(n)
appearing in the definition of the recurrences
R_{2g+3}
must themselves possess this symmetry.
We also observe that under the condition
\alpha_{1}=0
this recurrence reduces to the Gale-Robinson one (
0.2
) for the case
\left(N,a,c\right)=\left(7,1,2\right)
.

The following more general statement holds.

Proposition 0.3
.

Under the condition
\alpha_{j}=0,\;\forall j=1,\ldots,g-1
the recurrence
R_{2g+3}
reduces to the Gale-Robinson one (
0.2
) of type
\left(N,a,c\right)=\left(2g+3,1,2\right)
.

Let us also consider the following question: what is the number of Laurent monomials occurring in
R_{2g+3}
? The answer to this question is quite interesting and given by the following statement.

Proposition 0.4
.

The number of monomials in relation (
0.4
) is equal to
F_{2g+1}+1
, where
F_{k}
denotes the
k
-th Fibonacci number.

Let us formulate another property of recurrences (
0.4
), which is a generalization of our observation for the case
g=2
(and for
g=1
, of course).

Proposition 0.5
.

Setting
n=0
in (
0.4
), the right-hand side of this relation admits the symmetry
L(t_{1},\ldots,t_{2g+2})=L(t_{2g+2},\ldots,t_{1})
.

Also we will show the following simple property for the
R_{2g+3}
.

Proposition 0.6
.

Given a pair of nonzero constants
A
and
B
and any solution of the
R_{2g+3}
. The transformation
t_{n}\mapsto AB^{n}t_{n}
gives another solution to this recurrence.

Let us now formulate the main property of the recurrences
R_{2g+3}
that we are going to prove.

Theorem 0.7
.

Any recurrence
R_{2g+3}
has the Laurent property. More precisely, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
t_{n}
belongs to the ring
\mathbb{Z}[t_{0}^{\pm 1},\ldots,t_{2g+2}^{\pm 1};\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{g}]
.

The plan of the article is as follows. In the following section, we will write down and examine recurrence that is directly related to (
0.4
). It will be defined using some discrete polynomials
T^{k}_{s}(n)
. We will show how our recurrence
R_{2g+3}
under study is related to this associated recurrence by means of a certain substitution. The point of dealing with the associated recurrence, rather than with (
0.4
) directly, is that it is easier to work with. In Section
2
, we construct a Lax representation for associated recurrence. This representation has its own specific features. In particular, it is closely related to a certain continued fraction
St_{n}(x)
. Essentially we need this Lax representation to prepare the ground for the proof of Theorem
0.7
. Section
3
is essentially devoted to the proof of Theorem
0.7
, but not only that.
There we establish a connection between (
0.4
) and the discrete Mumford dynamical system introduced in
[
9
]
. Short Section
4
provides background information about integer sequences generated by (
0.4
) in a some particular case. Finally, Section
5
is devoted to remarks and conjectures.

Remark 0.8
.

We would like to use the two terms discrete equation and recurrence relation together and consider them as synonyms. We also consider the terms sequence and solution of a discrete equation as synonymous.

1.
Associated recurrence

1.1.
Definition

The first thing we want to do is to fully define recurrences of the form (
0.4
).
To this aim, for any integer
g\geq 1
, we define the associated recurrence

\prod_{j=0}^{2g}u_{n+j}=\sum_{j=0}^{g}\alpha_{j}T^{j}_{2g-j}(n+1).

(1.1)

Here it is assumed that
T^{k}_{s}(n)
, for
k,s\geq 1
, is some discrete homogeneous polynomial to be determined. We should immediately explain why we have written this relation here.
We will show that by using the substitution

u_{n}=\frac{t_{n}t_{n+3}}{t_{n+1}t_{n+2}}

(1.2)

in (
1.1
), we can reduce it to (
0.4
).

Remark 1.1
.

Some comments are in order. Note that here we are working backwards, so to speak, compared to
[
7
]
. Having noted the property of the Somos-5 mentioned in Proposition 1, Hone goes on to say that it is natural to define
gauge-invariant
combination
w_{n}=t_{n}t_{n+2}/t^{2}_{n+1}
and work with the recurrence for the sequence
\left(w_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Then, he constructs an invariant and a general solution for this recurrence. Note that one has
u_{n}=w_{n}w_{n+1}
, in (
1.2
). Note that Proposition
0.6
can be regarded as proven simply by the construction of our recurrences (
0.4
). Equation (
1.1
) is, as we intend, a consequence of
R_{2g+3}
for any
g\geq 1
. If Theorem
0.7
holds, then one can say that the substitution (
1.2
) yields a
Laurentification
of (
1.1
).

1.2.
Discrete polynomials
T^{k}_{s}(n)

In what follows, we largely follow the works
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
.
From a practical point of view, a good definition of
T^{k}_{s}(n)
is given by the recurrence relation:

T^{k}_{s}(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{s-k}u_{n+j}T^{k-1}_{s-j-1}(n+j+2),\;\forall s\geq k.

(1.3)

For example, one can start with
T^{0}_{s}(n)=1
and then step by step compute the polynomials
T^{k}_{s}(n)
of increasingly higher degree
k
.

We can also write
T^{k}_{s}(n)
in explicit form, namely as

T^{k}_{s}(n)=\sum_{0\leq\lambda_{1}<\cdots<\lambda_{k}\leq s-1}\prod_{j=1}^{k}u_{n+\lambda_{j}+j-1}.

(1.4)

Expression (
1.4
) represents a summation over a finite set of admissible values of
\lambda_{j}
. Let us denote
D_{k,s}=\left\{\lambda_{j}:0\leq\lambda_{1}<\cdots<\lambda_{k}\leq s-1\right\}
. Note that in the case
s=1,\ldots,k-1
, we have
D_{k,s}=\varnothing
, and consequently
T^{k}_{s}(n)=0
for these values of
s
. In turn, if
s=k
, then the set
D_{k,s}
consists of a single element
\left\{\lambda_{j}=j,\;\;j=0,\ldots,k-1\right\}
, and thus we obtain

T^{k}_{k}(n)=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}u_{n+2j},\;\;\forall k\geq 1.

(1.5)

The drawback of this definition is that (
1.4
) is rather difficult to use in practice compared to (
1.3
); nevertheless, as we will see below, some useful facts can be derived from it. Therefore, it is better to use these definitions together. What can we observe by looking at (
1.4
)? We see that
T^{k}_{s}(n)
is a homogeneous polynomial in the variables
\left(u_{n},\ldots,u_{n+k+s-2}\right)
of degree
k
. Of course, it is not obvious that (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) yield the same infinite class of discrete polynomials; however, as we will show below in the further exposition, we will demonstrate this fact.

1.3.
Discrete polynomials
B^{k}_{s}(n)
and definition of recurrences (
0.4
)

The first thing to investigate is how to go from (
1.1
) to (
0.4
). To do this, we need to define the mapping
T^{k}_{s}(n)\mapsto B^{k}_{s}(n)
. Let us write

B^{k}_{s}(n)=T^{k}_{s}(n)\prod_{j=1}^{k+s}t_{n+j},\;\;\forall s\geq k,\;k\geq 1,

(1.6)

provided that we have made the substitution (
1.2
) into
T^{k}_{s}(n)
. But we must be sure that
B^{k}_{s}(n)
are indeed polynomials. The following lemma is useful for proving this.

Lemma 1.2
.

[
12
]

The two relations

\displaystyle T^{k}_{s}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{k}_{s-1}(n)+u_{n+s+k-2}T^{k-1}_{s-1}(n)

(1.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{k}_{s-1}(n+1)+u_{n}T^{k-1}_{s-1}(n+2)

(1.8)

are identities.

Proof.

The relations (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) arise as a result of a suitable partition of the set
D_{k,s}
into two disjoint sets. For example, one can verify that (
1.7
) corresponds to the partition
D_{k,s}=D^{(1)}_{k,s}\sqcup D^{(2)}_{k,s}
with

D^{(1)}_{k,s}=\left\{\lambda_{j}:\lambda_{1}=0,\;\;1\leq\lambda_{2}<\cdots<\lambda_{k}\leq s-1\right\}

and

D^{(2)}_{k,s}=\left\{\lambda_{j}:1\leq\lambda_{1}<\cdots<\lambda_{k}\leq s-1\right\}.

∎

Note that (
1.3
) can be obtained by successive applications of (
1.8
). From this, in turn, follows the equivalence of the two different definitions (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) for the polynomials
T^{k}_{s}(n)
.

From Lemma
1.2
together with (
1.6
), we obtain the following statement.

Lemma 1.3
.

Discrete polynomials
B^{k}_{s}(n)
can be computed using one of the following two recurrence relations:

\displaystyle B^{k}_{s}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t_{n+k+s}B^{k}_{s-1}(n)+t_{n+k+s-2}t_{n+k+s+1}B^{k-1}_{s-1}(n)

(1.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t_{n+1}B^{k}_{s-1}(n+1)+t_{n}t_{n+3}B^{k-1}_{s-1}(n+2).

(1.10)

However, for these recurrence relations to work, additional data are needed. Taking (
1.5
) into account, we can write:

B^{k}_{k}(n)=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}t_{n+2j}\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}t_{n+2j+3},\;\;\forall k\geq 1.

Fortunately, this polynomial can be computed without using any recurrence relations. We also need to define
B^{0}_{s}(n)=\prod_{j=1}^{s}t_{n+j},\forall s\geq 1
. Note that the latter is consistent with (
1.6
) provided that we set
T^{0}_{s}(n)=1,\forall s\geq 1
. Now we have enough data to use relation (
1.9
) or, if desired, relation (
1.10
) to determine the polynomials
B^{k}_{s}(n)
, first for
k=1
, then for
k=2
, and so on.

Obviously,
\deg\left(B^{0}_{s}\right)=s
and
\deg\left(B^{k}_{k}\right)=2k
. Moreover, the two terms on the right-hand sides of (
1.9
) and (
1.10
) have the same degree, equal to
k+s
. All this proves that a discrete polynomials
B^{k}_{s}(n)
, computed using (
1.9
) (or (
1.10
)), is indeed a homogeneous one of degree
k+s
in the variables
\left(t_{n},\ldots,t_{n+k+s+1}\right)
.

As a complement to Lemma
1.3
, we prove the following statement, which generalizes our previous observation. For convenience, we take
n=0
, so that a discrete polynomial
B^{k}_{s}(n)
turns into a polynomial
B^{k}_{s}(0)
in the variables
\left(t_{0},\ldots,t_{k+s+1}\right)
. For convenience, let us denote this polynomial simply by
B^{k}_{s}
.

Lemma 1.4
.

The polynomial
B^{k}_{s}
exhibits a symmetry:

B^{k}_{s}\left(t_{k+s+1},\ldots,t_{0}\right)=B^{k}_{s}\left(t_{0},\ldots,t_{k+s+1}\right),\;\;\forall k\geq 0,s\geq 1,

that is, renumbering the arguments
\left(t_{0},\ldots,t_{k+s+1}\right)
in reverse order yields the same polynomial.

Proof.

From (
1.9
) it follows the recurrent relation

B^{k}_{s}\left(t_{0},\ldots,t_{k+s+1}\right)=t_{k+s}B^{k}_{s-1}\left(t_{0},\ldots,t_{k+s}\right)+t_{k+s-2}t_{k+s+1}B^{k-1}_{s-1}\left(t_{0},\ldots,t_{k+s-1}\right).

(1.11)

Given any values of
\left(k,s\right)
, we perform the substitution

t_{j}\rightarrow t_{k+s+1-j},\;j=0,\ldots,k+s+1

in (
1.11
). It thereby turns into

B^{k}_{s}\left(t_{k+s+1},\ldots,t_{0}\right)=t_{1}B^{k}_{s-1}\left(t_{k+s+1},\ldots,t_{1}\right)+t_{3}t_{0}B^{k-1}_{s-1}\left(t_{k+s+1},\ldots,t_{2}\right).

Suppose now that the statement of the lemma has already been proved for
B^{k}_{s-1}
and
B^{k-1}_{s-1}
. Then, by virtue of (
1.10
), we obtain this property for
B^{k}_{s}
. It is now clear that the lemma admits a proof by induction. Indeed, since
B^{0}_{1}=t_{1}
and
B^{1}_{1}=t_{0}t_{3}
satisfy the statement of the lemma, it follows by induction that this statement holds for all polynomials
B^{1}_{s},\;s\geq 2
. Next, by induction, the statement of the lemma is proved for all
B^{2}_{s}
, and so on and so forth.
∎

Notice that having proved this lemma, we have thereby proved Proposition
0.5
.

Let us now look at what recurrence relation we obtain as a result of substituting (
1.2
) into (
1.1
). We get:

\prod_{j=3}^{2g}t_{n+j}\cdot t_{n}t_{n+2g+3}=\sum_{j=0}^{g}\alpha_{j}B^{j}_{2g-j}(n+1).

(1.12)

In addition, note that

B^{0}_{2g}(n+1)=\prod_{j=3}^{2g}t_{n+j}\cdot t_{n+2}t_{n+2g+1}\;\mbox{and}\;B^{g}_{g}(n+1)=\prod_{j=3}^{2g}t_{n+j}\cdot t_{n+1}t_{n+2g+2}.

Taking into account these expressions, we can rewrite (
1.12
) as

\prod_{j=3}^{2g}t_{n+j}\cdot\left(t_{n}t_{n+2g+3}-\alpha_{g}t_{n+1}t_{n+2g+2}-\alpha_{0}t_{n+2}t_{n+2g+1}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}\alpha_{j}B^{j}_{2g-j}(n+1)

(1.13)

Rewriting (
1.12
) in the form (
1.13
) gives a proof of Proposition
0.3
.

Lemma 1.5
.

The number of monomials on the right-hand side of relation (
1.1
) is equal to
F_{2g+1}
.

Proof.

Obviously,
\#D_{1,s}=s
. From (
1.3
) and the binomial identity

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}s\\
k\end{array}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{s-k}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}s-j-1\\
k-1\end{array}\right),

by induction we obtain that the number of monomials occurring in
T^{k}_{s}(n)
is equal to the binomial coefficient
\binom{s}{k}
, or in other words,
\#D_{k,s}=\binom{s}{k}
. In turn, using the well-known formula for Fibonacci numbers

F_{2g+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{g}\binom{2g-j}{j},

we obtain that the number of monomials on the right-hand side of (
1.1
) is equal to
F_{2g+1}
.
∎

According to Lemma
1.5
, the number of monomials on the right-hand side of (
1.12
) is equal to
F_{2g+1}
. Thus, the total number of monomials defining (
1.12
) is
F_{2g+1}+1
, and therefore we have proved Proposition
0.4
.

2.
Lax representation for equation (
1.1
)

The purpose of this section is to show the Lax representation for recurrence (
1.1
). Since discrete polynomials
T^{k}_{s}(n)
are involved in the definition of the recurrence (
1.1
), it is natural to expect that they will somehow appear in the Lax representation.

2.1.
Lax pair

In this section, we construct a Lax representation for the associated recurrence (
1.1
). To this end, consider a pair of matrices

L_{n}(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{n}(x)&R_{n}(x)\\
Q_{n}(x)&-P_{n}(x)\end{array}\right)\;\mbox{and}\;\;M_{n}(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&u_{n}x\\
1&0\end{array}\right)

and a relation of the form

L_{n}M_{n}=M_{n}L_{n+1},

(2.1)

where
\left(P_{n},Q_{n},R_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a sequence of triples of polynomials

P_{n}(x)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j,n}x^{j},\;\;Q_{n}(x)=2+\sum_{j=1}^{g}q_{j,n}x^{j}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;R_{n}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{g+1}r_{j,n}x^{j}.

(2.2)

We want to define the coefficients of these polynomials in such a way that relation (
2.1
) becomes generating one for (
1.1
).

Theorem 2.1
.

Equation (
1.1
) admits a Lax representation (
2.1
), with the coefficients of polynomials (
2.2
) of the form

q_{k,n}=2T^{k}_{2g-k}(n+1)+2\sum_{j=1}^{k}T^{k-j}_{2g-k-j}(n+1)\frac{T_{j,g-j}(n+1)}{\prod_{s=0}^{2g-2j}u_{n+s}},

(2.3)

r_{k,n}=2\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}T^{k-j-1}_{2g-k-j+1}(n)\frac{T_{j,g-j}(n)}{\prod_{s=0}^{2g-2j-1}u_{n+s}},

(2.4)

and

\displaystyle p_{k,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{k}_{2g-k+1}(n)+\sum_{j=1}^{k}T^{k-j}_{2g-k-j}(n+1)\frac{T_{j,g-j}(n+1)}{\prod_{s=0}^{2g-2j}u_{n+s}}

(2.5)

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}T^{k-j-1}_{2g-k-j+1}(n)\frac{T_{j,g-j}(n)}{\prod_{s=0}^{2g-2j-1}u_{n+s}}+T^{k-2}_{2g-k}(n+2)\frac{T_{0,g}(n+1)}{\prod_{j=1}^{2g-1}u_{n+j}}

\displaystyle+u_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}T^{k-j-1}_{2g-k-j+1}(n+2)\frac{T_{j,g-j}(n+2)}{\prod_{s=1}^{2g-2j+1}u_{n+s}}.

We will prove Theorem

The formulas in this theorem for the coefficients of the polynomials

look rather cumbersome. To avoid excessive cumbersomeness, we use the following notation:

(2.6)

For example,

T_{s,0}(n)=\alpha_{s},\;\;T_{s,1}(n)=\alpha_{s}+\alpha_{s+1}T^{1}_{1}(n),

One can verify that the coefficients of the polynomials

entering into (

2.2.
Proof of Theorem

The proof of this theorem is technical and and possibly is not of sufficient interest.

First of all we note that our associated recurrence (

(2.8)

Componentwise, they are written as:

\displaystyle r_{k,n+1}

\displaystyle 2T^{k-1}_{2g-k+1}(n+1)\frac{T_{0,g}(n+1)}{\prod_{s=1}^{2g}u_{n+s}}+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}T^{k-j-1}_{2g-k-j+1}(n+1)\frac{T_{j,g-j}(n+1)}{\prod_{s=1}^{2g-2j}u_{n+s}}

Further, we compute the coefficient

\displaystyle+u_{n}T^{k-1}_{2g-k+1}(n+2)+u_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}T^{k-j-1}_{2g-k-j+1}(n+2)\frac{T_{j,g-j}(n+2)}{\prod_{s=1}^{2g-2j+1}u_{n+s}}.

3.
Discrete Mumford dynamical system

3.1.
Definition

Suppose now that the coefficients of the polynomials
\left(P_{n},Q_{n},R_{n}\right)
do not depend on
\left(u_{n},\ldots,u_{n+2g-1}\right)
and
\left(\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{g}\right)
like in Theorem
2.1
and generally do not depend on any dynamical variables. The Lax representation (
2.1
) in such a case can be found in a recent paper
[
9
]
by Hone, Roberts, and Vanhaecke.
In more detail, the matrix relation (
2.1
) can be written in the form

P_{n+1}=Q_{n}-P_{n},\;\;Q_{n+1}=\frac{2P_{n}-Q_{n}+R_{n}}{u_{n}x}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;R_{n+1}=u_{n}xQ_{n}

(3.1)

provided we put

u_{n}=p_{1,n}-\frac{q_{1,n}}{2}+\frac{r_{1,n}}{2}.

(3.2)

Note that the last relation is exactly the same as in Lemma
2.5
. It is also natural that (
2.8
) and (
3.1
) look exactly the same in form; the difference is only that in (
2.8
) it is supposed that the coefficients of the polynomials are discrete rational functions of
\left(u_{n},\ldots,u_{n+2g-1}\right)
, while in (
3.1
) they are not.
Under Lemma
2.5
, relation (
3.2
) is consistent with (
2.3
), (
2.4
), and (
2.5
) and follows from them. However, if coefficients of polynomials
\left(P_{n},Q_{n},R_{n}\right)
are independent of anything, then (
3.2
) is merely a notation for correctly defining the system of discrete equations (
3.1
).

System (
3.1
) defines a discrete dynamical system on the phase space
M_{g}\cong\mathbb{C}^{3g+1}
, whose points are specified by coordinates
\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{g};p_{1},\ldots,p_{g};r_{1},\ldots,r_{g+1}\right)
. Probably, it would be correct to call (
3.1
) a discrete Mumford dynamical system, since its construction is very similar to the construction of continuous Mumford dynamical systems
[
15
]
. By the way, in
[
9
]
, the authors call the dynamical system generated by (
3.1
) a Mumford-like system.

As was shown in
[
9
]
, system (
3.1
) has remarkable integrability properties. The definition of integrability, in
[
9
]
, involves an infinite number of invariants and compatible Poisson structures on
M_{g}
. In conclusion, it is proved in
[
9
]
that, for any
g\geq 1
, the Mumford system (
3.1
) has the property of algebraic integrability.

3.2.
Embedding (
1.1
) into Mumford system (
3.1
)

Observe, that the number of parameters
\left(u_{0},\ldots,u_{2g-1};\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{g}\right)
that need to be determined in order to fully specify the sequence via (
1.1
) is likewise equal to
3g+1
. All this suggests that the two dynamical systems – the first of which is the discrete Mumford dynamical system and the second, which is defined by the associated recurrence (
1.1
) – might be equivalent to each other. At any rate, Theorem
2.1
tells us that any solution of (
1.1
) yields a solution of the system (
3.1
). This embedding and the corresponding tools are sufficient for us to prove Theorem
0.7
.
In what follows, we will talk about some specific features of this Lax representation, namely its connection with a certain continued fraction. Additionally, establishing the relationship between (
1.1
) and the Mumford dynamical system furnishes a foundation for the study of their integrability properties.

3.3.
Invariants for discrete Mumford dynamical system

We do not aim to study the integrability properties of (
1.1
), but for our purposes the following will be needed.
By virtue of (
2.1
), the matrices
L_{n}
are similar to each other for different values of
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore,

f=-\det L_{n}=P_{n}^{2}+Q_{n}R_{n}

depends on
x
but not on
n
. By construction,
f
is a polynomial in
x
of the form
f=1+\sum_{j=1}^{2g+1}f_{j}x^{j}
, with coefficients

f_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}p_{j,n}p_{k-j,n}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}q_{j,n}r_{k-j,n},

(3.3)

where it is supposed that
p_{0,n}=1
and
q_{0,n}=2
. Notice that, in principle, formula (
3.3
) is universal and at first glance does not depend on
g
; however, one must take into account that
p_{j,n}=q_{j,n}=r_{j+1,n}=0
for all
j\geq g+1
.

Let
y(x)
be an algebraic function defined by relation

y^{2}=f(x)=P_{n}^{2}+Q_{n}R_{n}.

(3.4)

Consider its expansion into a power series
y=1+\sum_{j\geq 1}H_{j}x^{j}
. Obviously, the coefficients

are polynomials in
\left(f_{1},\ldots,f_{2g+1}\right)
and are essentially invariants for the system (
3.1
).

It would be interesting, based on the explicit form of the coefficients (
2.3
), (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) of the polynomials appearing in the Lax representation, to find the explicit form of the invariants
\left(H_{1},\ldots,H_{2g+1}\right)
. However, here we encounter technical difficulties. Nevertheless, one can calculate something. Let us compute, for example, an invariant
H_{1}
. For any
g\geq 1
, one has

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{1}}{2}=p_{1,n}+r_{1,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{1}_{2g}(n)+\frac{T_{g-1,1}(n+1)}{\prod_{s=0}^{2g-2}u_{n+s}}+\frac{T_{g,0}(n)}{\prod_{s=0}^{2g-1}u_{n+s}}.

Example 3.1
.

For example, in the case
g=1
,
H_{1}
has the form

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{n}+u_{n+1}+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{u_{n}}+\frac{\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}u_{n}}{u_{n}u_{n+1}}

(3.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t_{n}t_{n+3}}{t_{n+1}t_{n+2}}+\frac{t_{n+1}t_{n+4}}{t_{n+2}t_{n+3}}+\alpha_{1}\left(\frac{t_{n+1}t_{n+2}}{t_{n}t_{n+3}}+\frac{t_{n+2}t_{n+3}}{t_{n+1}t_{n+4}}\right)+\alpha_{0}\frac{t_{n}t_{n+4}}{t_{n+2}t_{n+3}}.

(3.6)

Expression (
3.6
) represents a well-known invariant for Somos-5. Relation (
3.5
) says that
\left(u_{n},u_{n+1}\right)
lies on the curve defined by the equation

\left(X+Y\right)XY-H_{1}XY+\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\left(X+Y\right)=0.

It turns out that the curve defined by this equation is birationally equivalent to an elliptic curve for which the equation can be written in Weierstrass form. This fact, in turn, is the basis for the analytic form of the terms of the sequence defined by the Somos-5 recurrence. For details, see the paper
[
7
]
.

3.4.
Continued fraction

Here, following
[
9
]
, we demonstrate the distinctive feature of the Lax representation (
2.1
) for the Mumford system, namely, its link to a certain continued fraction.
The following lemma is proved by direct computations. We will see below, it is important for proving the Laurent property for our homogeneous recurrences.

Lemma 3.2
.

[
9
]

Let
\left(P_{n},Q_{n},R_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a sequence of triples of polynomials satisfying (
3.1
), where
u_{n}
is given by (
3.2
). Then the relation

\left(y+P_{n}\right)\left(y+P_{n+1}\right)=Q_{n}\left(y+P_{n+1}\right)+u_{n}xQ_{n}Q_{n+1},\;\;\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}

(3.7)

holds.

Proof.

The proof, as we have already said, proceeds by direct computation. Rewrite (
3.7
) as

y^{2}+\left(P_{n}+P_{n+1}-Q_{n}\right)y+P_{n}P_{n+1}-Q_{n}P_{n+1}-u_{n}xQ_{n}Q_{n+1}=0.

By the first relation in (
3.1
) the coefficient of
y
is zero. And now, taking into account (
3.4
), it remains to be verified that relation (
3.7
) is indeed an identity by virtue of the first and second relations in (
3.1
).
∎

Let us denote

F_{n}=\frac{y+P_{n}}{Q_{n}}.

(3.8)

With this notation, we can rewrite (
3.7
) as
F_{n}=1+u_{n}x/F_{n+1}
, which in turn yields the remarkable continued fraction expansion

F_{n}(x)=St_{n}(x),

(3.9)

where

St_{n}(x)=1+\frac{u_{n}x}{1+\displaystyle{\frac{u_{n+1}x}{1+\displaystyle{\frac{u_{n+2}x}{1+\cdots}}}}}.

(3.10)

To be more precise, we talk about an infinite sequence of continued fractions
\left(St_{n}(x)\right)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
defined by (
3.10
). By definition, this sequence satisfies a recurrence

St_{n}St_{n+1}=St_{n+1}+u_{n}x.

(3.11)

3.5.
Birational mapping

Let us expand the continued fraction
St_{n}(x)
into a power series

St_{n}(x)=1+\sum_{j\geq 1}(-1)^{j+1}s_{j,n}x^{j}.

(3.12)

The coefficients
s_{j,n}
turn out to be certain homogeneous polynomials in
u_{n}
.
For example, the first three coefficients
s_{j,n}
are as follows:

s_{1,n}=u_{n},\;s_{2,n}=u_{n}u_{n+1}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;s_{3,n}=u_{n}u_{n+1}\left(u_{n+1}+u_{n+2}\right).

One sees that the coefficient
s_{j,n}
, for any
j\geq 1
, is a polynomial in variables
\left(u_{n},\ldots u_{n+j-1}\right)
, but it
is important to note that it depends linearly on the variable
u_{n+j-1}
. This means that
u_{n+j-1}
, for any
j\geq 1
, can be expressed unambiguously as a rational function of the
variables
\left(s_{1,n},\ldots,s_{j,n}\right)
. And this, in turn, implies that relation (
3.12
) can be interpreted as a generating one for the birational mapping
\left(u_{n},u_{n+1},\ldots\right)\leftrightarrow\left(s_{1,n},s_{2,n},\ldots\right)
.

Let us explain why we need this birational map. If the initial data
\left(u_{0},\ldots,u_{2g-1}\right)
for the associated recurrence are given, then, by (
3.12
), we have a finite set of polynomials
s_{j}=s_{j}\left(u_{0},\ldots,u_{j-1}\right),\;j=1,\ldots,2g
.
Denote

C(x)=St_{0}(x)-1=\sum_{j\geq 1}(-1)^{j+1}s_{j}x^{j}.

Remark 3.3
.

Here and in what follows, for simplicity, we use the notation
s_{j}=s_{j,0}
,
p_{j}=p_{j,0}
,
q_{j}=q_{j,0}
and so on.

Making the substitution
F_{0}\rightarrow St_{0}
, we obtain
y=-P+Q\left(1+C\right)
. Substituting the latter into (
3.4
) yields

QC^{2}+2\left(Q-P\right)C-2P+Q-R=0,

(3.13)

where

P=1+\sum_{j=1}^{g}p_{j}x^{j},\;\;Q=2+\sum_{j=1}^{g}q_{j}x^{j},\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;R=\sum_{j=1}^{g+1}r_{j}x^{j}.

Example 3.4
.

Let us see how relation (
3.13
) works in the case
g=1
. Suppose that the coefficients
\left(q_{1},p_{1},r_{1},r_{2}\right)
are defined as (
2.12
), (
2.13
), (
2.14
) and (
2.15
). For the initial data one has

s_{1}=u_{0}=\frac{t_{0}t_{3}}{t_{1}t_{2}},\;s_{2}=u_{0}u_{1}=\frac{t_{0}t_{4}}{t_{2}^{2}}

(3.14)

and

s_{m}=\left(q_{1}-p_{1}\right)s_{m-1}+\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}s_{j}s_{m-j}-\frac{q_{1}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m-2}s_{j}s_{m-j},\;\;m\geq 3.

(3.15)

The coefficients of (
3.15
) belong to the ring
\mathcal{L}_{5}=\mathbb{Z}[t_{0}^{\pm 1},t_{1}^{\pm 1},t_{2}^{\pm 1},t_{3}^{\pm 1},t_{4}^{\pm 1};\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}]
, while the initial data
\left(s_{1},s_{2}\right)
for the recurrence (
3.15
) also belong to this ring. The right-hand side of (
3.15
) contains only multiplications and summations. This means that
s_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{5}
for all
n\geq 1
.

3.6.
Proof of Theorem
0.7

We present a proof of Theorem
0.7
, essentially following the scheme given in
[
9
]
.
Calculations in Example
3.4
can be generalized for any
g\geq 1
. An finite set of Laurent polynomials

s_{j}=s_{j}\left(u_{0},\ldots,u_{j-1}\right)=s_{j}\left(t_{0}^{\pm 1},\ldots,t_{j+2}^{\pm 1}\right),\;\;j=1,\ldots,g+1

(3.16)

serves as the initial data for the recurrence relation

s_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}(-1)^{j}\left(p_{j}-q_{j}\right)s_{m-j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{g}(-1)^{k}q_{k}\sum_{k=1}^{m-k-1}s_{j}s_{m-j-k},\;\;m\geq g+2

which is derived from (
3.13
). Notice that we put
q_{0}=2
here. An example of the initial data would be (
3.14
). Let us explain once again that the initial data (
3.16
) are determined by the mapping
\left(u_{0},u_{1},\ldots\right)\rightarrow\left(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots\right)
defined by a generating relation

St_{0}(x)=1+\sum_{j\geq 1}(-1)^{j+1}s_{j}x^{j},

and further use of substitution (
1.2
).

According to Lemma
2.4
, the coefficients of this recurrence belong to the ring
\mathcal{L}_{2g+3}
. The initial data also belong to this ring. By induction, we obtain that
s_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{2g+3}
for all
n\geq 1
.

Let us put
\Delta_{-2}=\Delta_{-1}=\Delta_{0}=1
and define the remaining terms of the sequence
\left(\Delta_{n}\right)_{n\geq-2}
as the Hankel determinants:
\Delta_{2k-1}=\det\left(s_{n+m-1}\right)
and
\Delta_{2k}=\det\left(s_{n+m}\right)
,
where it is supposed that
n
and
m
run from
1
to
k
. It is an elementary fact that
\Delta_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{2g+3},\;\forall n\geq 1
provided that
s_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{2g+3},\;\forall n\geq 1
. Now we need to prove that
t_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{2g+3},\;\;\forall n\geq 0
. The following technical lemma will be helpful to this aim.

Lemma 3.5
.

[
9
]

The relations

t_{2k}=t_{0}\left(\frac{t_{2}}{t_{0}}\right)^{k}\Delta_{2k-2}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;t_{2k+1}=\frac{t_{0}t_{1}}{t_{2}}\left(\frac{t_{2}}{t_{0}}\right)^{k+1}\Delta_{2k-1},\;\;\forall k\geq 0

(3.17)

are identities.

Remark 3.6
.

Note that this lemma has no relation to our recurrences; it simply gives some identities by virtue of definition of these quantities.

Relations (
3.17
) show that
t_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{2g+3},\;\;\forall n\geq 0
, but how to prove the same for
n\leq-1
? Symmetry, which holds for our homogeneous recurrences (
0.4
), comes to the rescue – a property stated in Proposition
0.5
. It tells us that the considerations given in support of the proof of the theorem remain valid if we calculate iterations
t_{n}
, by virtue of
R_{2g+3}
in the opposite direction. Thus, we consider the proof of Theorem
0.7
complete.

5.
Remarks and conjectures

Let us summarize what was presented in the paper. We exhibited an infinite family of discrete equations possessing the Laurent property and a Lax representation. The idea of the existence of such a family of homogeneous recurrences possessing the Laurent property was proposed at the end of the paper
[
14
]
, and examples were given there. We began our exposition with recurrence (
1.1
)
which is fully defined using discrete polynomials
T^{k}_{s}(n)
, and it is clear that these discrete polynomials play an important role in the paper.
Now, allow us to spend below some space to make a number of remarks and propose some conjectures. We have gathered here material that is not directly related to the main topic of the article.

5.1.
The relationship between the continued fraction
St_{n}(x)
and the discrete polynomials
T^{k}_{s}(n)

Let us show how the discrete polynomials
T^{k}_{s}(n)
are directly related to the continued fraction
St_{n}(x)
given by (
3.10
).
To this end, consider the corresponding convergent

St_{s,n}(x)=1+\frac{u_{n}x}{1+\displaystyle{\frac{u_{n+1}x}{1+\displaystyle{\frac{\cdots}{1+u_{n+s}x}}}}},\;\forall s\geq 0.

(5.1)

It can be shown, that it is related to the discrete polynomials
T^{k}_{s}(n)
by the relation

St_{s,n}(x)=\frac{P_{s+1,n}(x)}{P_{s,n+1}(x)},

(5.2)

where

P_{s,n}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor(s+1)/2\right\rfloor}T^{j}_{s-j+1}(n)x^{j}.

The following lemma is a simple consequence of Lemma
1.2
.

Lemma 5.1
.

By (
1.7
) and (
1.8
), the sequence of continuants
P_{s,n}(x)
satisfies two recurrence relations

\displaystyle P_{s+2,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{s+1,n}+u_{n+s+1}xP_{s,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{s+1,n+1}+u_{n}xP_{s,n+2}.

simultaneously.

Proof.

Let us prove, for example, the second relation. From (
5.1
) it obviously follows

St_{s+1,n}=1+\frac{u_{n}x}{St_{s,n+1}}.

Substituting (
5.2
) into this relation yields

\frac{P_{s+2,n}}{P_{s+1,n+1}}=1+u_{n}x\frac{P_{s,n+2}}{P_{s+1,n+1}}\;\;\mbox{or}\;\;P_{s+2,n}=P_{s+1,n+1}+u_{n}xP_{s,n+2}.

∎

5.2.
Origin of recurrence (
1.1
)

Let us say a few words about where recurrence (
1.1
) comes from. In
[
12
]
, we considered the equation

u_{n+2g+1}=u_{n}\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{g}c_{j}T^{s-j}_{s+j}(n+2)}{\sum_{j=0}^{g}c_{j}T^{s-j}_{s+j}(n+1)},

(5.3)

where it is assumed that
\left(c_{1},\ldots,c_{g}\right)
are arbitrary parameters, and
c_{0}=1
. One can easily show that (
5.3
) is equivalent to (
1.1
). The number of parameters
\left(u_{0},\ldots,u_{2g-1};\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{g}\right)
needed to uniquely determine the sequence
\left(u_{n}\right)
using (
1.1
) is
3g+1
. At the same time, the number of parameters
\left(u_{0},\ldots,u_{2g};c_{1},\ldots,c_{g}\right)
needed to uniquely determine the sequence defined by (
5.3
) is also
3g+1
, and this is a necessary condition for the equivalence of (
1.1
) and (
5.3
).
It is easy to see that the rational function

K=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{g}c_{j}T^{g-j}_{g+j}(n+1)}{\prod_{j=0}^{2g}u_{n+j}}

is an invariant for (
5.3
).
The relation

\prod_{j=0}^{2g}u_{n+j}=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{g}c_{j}T^{g-j}_{g+j}(n+1)}{K},

where
K
keeping to be an arbitrary constant, actually coincides with (
1.1
).

5.3.
Homogeneous recurrences of even order

As already noted, the homogeneous recurrences (
0.4
) studied in this article can be represented in the form

\prod_{j=3}^{N-3}t_{n+j}\cdot\left(t_{n}t_{n+N}-\alpha_{g}t_{n+1}t_{n+N-1}-\alpha_{0}t_{n+2}t_{n+N-2}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{g-1}\alpha_{j}B^{j}_{N-j-3}(n+1)

(5.4)

with
N=2g+3
. The corresponding discrete polynomials
B^{k}_{s}(n)
were defined in Lemma
1.3
by recurrent relation. From this, in particular, it follows that these recurrences can be reduced to Gale-Robinson recurrences (
0.2
) of type
\left(N,a,c\right)=\left(2g+3,1,2\right),\;\forall g\geq 1
. By the way, one can conjecture that these recurrences represent the maximal possible generalization of the corresponding Gale-Robinson recurrence that preserves the Laurent property.

Note that there is nothing to prevent us from setting
N=2g+2
in (
5.4
). Since all the necessary discrete polynomials
B^{k}_{s}(n)
are defined, we obtain an infinite family of homogeneous recurrences, which we denote by the symbol
R_{2g+2}
. In the single case
g=1
, the right-hand side of (
5.4
) is equal to zero. Furthermore, we consider the product on the left-hand side of the relation to be equal to one. As a result, we obtain the well-known Somos-4 equation

t_{n}t_{n+4}=\alpha_{1}t_{n+1}t_{n+3}+\alpha_{0}t_{n+2}t_{n+2}.

Thus, it is the first representative in the family of even-order recurrences. Let us, for illustration, write down explicitly the recurrence – the second representative in this family. This is how it is written:

t_{n}t_{n+6}=\alpha_{0}t_{n+2}t_{n+4}+\alpha_{1}\frac{t_{n+1}t_{n+4}^{2}+t_{n+2}^{2}t_{n+5}}{t_{n+3}}+\alpha_{2}t_{n+1}t_{n+5}.

The first question that comes to mind is: do these recurrences have the Laurent property? Experimentation with these recurrences suggests that the answer is most likely positive. Proving this fact is therefore an open problem.

5.4.
Conjectures about inclusions

After we have defined the homogeneous recurrences of even order, we can propose some conjectures based on actual computations. Other assumptions are in order.

Conjecture 5.2
.

Any
R_{2g+2}
-sequence, for every
g\geq 1
, satisfies the recurrence
R_{2g+3}
with suitable coefficients.

Notice that, in the case
g=1
, this is not a problem. Indeed, it is a well-known fact that any Somos-4 sequence satisfies the Somos-5 recurrence
[
7
]
.
The converse statement is generally not true. By the way, in
[
13
]
there is a much more general result that any Somos-4 sequence satisfies the Gale-Robinson recurrences (
0.2
) of every type
\left(N,a,c\right)
with suitable coefficients.

The following statement seems to also be true.

Conjecture 5.3
.

Any
R_{2g+3}
-sequence, for every
g\geq 1
, satisfies the recurrence
R_{2g+4}
with suitable coefficients.

If these two conjectures are true, then we have the following chain of inclusions:

\mathcal{M}_{4}\subset\mathcal{M}_{5}\subset\mathcal{M}_{6}\subset\cdots,

where
\mathcal{M}_{N}
denotes the space of all sequences
\left(t_{n}\right)
satisfying the recurrence
R_{N}
.
