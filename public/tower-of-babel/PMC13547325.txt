A 40 Gbps low-power inverter-based CNTFET optical receiver
Authors: Nazanin Zamani, Mehdi Amoon, Zahra Alaie, Mahmoud Daneshvar Farzanegan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper presents a low-power 40 Gbps optical receiver implemented using 32 nm carbon nanotube field-effect transistor (CNTFET) technology. The proposed receiver architecture consists of a modified inverter-based transimpedance amplifier (TIA) followed by cascaded limiting amplifiers (LAs). Using the gm/ID design methodology, the TIA is optimized to achieve a balanced trade-off among gain, bandwidth, noise, and power consumption. Simulation results indicate that the proposed TIA achieves a transimpedance gain of 47.6 dBΩ, a − 3 dB bandwidth of 28.4 GHz, and an input-referred noise current of 15.5 pA/√Hz, while consuming only 183 µW and occupying an area of 3.93 μm². The complete receiver provides an overall gain of 77.8 dBΩ, a − 3 dB bandwidth of 29.5 GHz, and a total power consumption of 1377 µW within a layout area of 49.19 μm². In addition, analytical predictions derived from the gm/ID framework are in close agreement with the simulation results, thereby validating the adopted design methodology. Compared with conventional CMOS-based implementations, the proposed CNTFET-based receiver demonstrates notable improvements in power efficiency, noise performance, and integration density, indicating its potential for next-generation optical interconnects and communication systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-54513-0.

Introduction

The optical communication system illustrated in Fig. 1 consists of three main building blocks: a transmitter, an optical fiber link, and a receiver. In the transmitter, an externally modulated laser (EML) maps the input electrical data onto an optical carrier. The modulated optical signal then propagates through the optical fiber and reaches the receiver, where a photodiode detects the incident optical signal and converts it into a corresponding photocurrent1. This photocurrent is subsequently converted into a voltage signal by a TIA. The resulting voltage is further amplified by a LA to provide the amplitude levels required for subsequent digital signal processing2.

Fig. 1Block diagram of the optical communication system2.

Block diagram of the optical communication system2.

TIAs are critical components in optical receivers, and different TIA architectures offer distinct trade-offs among gain, bandwidth, noise, stability, power consumption, and implementation complexity. Therefore, optimizing the TIA topology is essential for improving the overall performance of high-speed optical receiver systems. This study focuses on the design and optimization of a TIA architecture aimed at enhancing gain and bandwidth while maintaining low power consumption.

A resistive-feedback TIA offers structural simplicity and low-noise operation; however, its high-frequency performance is limited by parasitic capacitances and stability constraints3. A common-gate TIA also provides a relatively simple topology, but its bandwidth and noise performance can be constrained by input impedance requirements and parasitic effects2. Fully differential TIAs can achieve high gain, wide bandwidth, improved linearity, and strong common-mode rejection, although these advantages are typically obtained at the cost of increased power consumption and circuit complexity4. The regulated-cascode (RGC) TIA provides low input impedance and wide output swing while maintaining high output resistance5. Inverter-based TIAs are attractive because of their high effective transconductance and compact implementation; however, their bandwidth may be limited by intrinsic capacitances6.

To mitigate these limitations, cascaded-inverter TIA architectures have been proposed to improve gain, bandwidth, and noise performance, albeit generally at the expense of higher power dissipation7. In this work, a modified inverter-based TIA is introduced as the primary amplification stage, with particular emphasis on bandwidth enhancement and power-efficient operation.

The selection of an appropriate TIA architecture depends on application-specific requirements, including transimpedance gain, bandwidth, noise performance, and power consumption. For reliable operation in high-speed optical receivers, the TIA bandwidth is typically designed to be approximately 70% of the target data rate8. Several bandwidth-enhancement techniques have therefore been developed to address the intrinsic frequency limitations of TIA circuits.

Stagger tuning and T-coil peaking extend the bandwidth through inductive peaking, but they require substantial chip area because of the passive inductive components9,10. The Cherry–Hooper topology improves bandwidth by employing local feedback; however, it typically requires a higher supply voltage2,11. Capacitive degeneration introduces a compensating zero that shifts the dominant output pole to higher frequencies, although this approach can increase thermal noise12. Other techniques, such as fT doublers and negative capacitance, can enhance high-frequency response, but they may lead to increased power consumption or be limited by capacitor-sizing constraints13,14.

Among these techniques, the active-inductor approach is particularly attractive because it emulates inductive behavior and resonates with the load capacitance, thereby extending the bandwidth without requiring large passive inductors15. In this work, this technique is adopted to achieve bandwidth enhancement under low-power operating conditions.

In optical receiver systems, the low-amplitude output voltage generated by the TIA is further amplified by an LA to reach signal levels suitable for digital processing. The LA must provide sufficient gain to suppress the effect of noise contributed by subsequent stages while preserving the bandwidth required for high-speed operation. When multiple LA stages are cascaded, each stage is commonly designed with a bandwidth of approximately 80% of the target data rate to compensate for cumulative bandwidth degradation.

In addition to gain and bandwidth, the input-referred noise of the LA is a key design parameter because a wider bandwidth generally increases the integrated noise. The total input-referred noise current of the cascaded TIA–LA receiver depends on the noise bandwidth and the equivalent input noise contributions of both stages. Consequently, minimizing the LA input-referred noise voltage is essential for reducing the overall receiver noise2.

As electronic devices continue to scale into the nanometer regime, conventional metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor (MOSFET) technology faces several fundamental limitations, including short-channel effects, increased leakage current, high electric fields, lithographic constraints, and quantum-confinement phenomena16. To address these challenges, CNTFETs have emerged as promising alternatives because of the exceptional electrical and structural properties of carbon nanotubes17.

CNTFETs offer several advantages over conventional MOSFETs, including improved electrostatic gate control, high carrier mobility for both n-type and p-type devices, near-ballistic transport, high transconductance, large on/off current ratios, and tunable threshold voltage characteristics18,19–25. Their excellent electrical and thermal conductivities can further improve device- and circuit-level performance25. Despite fabrication-related challenges and sensitivity to structural defects26, CNTFETs can be compatible with standard CMOS-oriented fabrication flows, enabling the adaptation of established MOSFET design methodologies and tools while reducing implementation complexity27,28.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the overall architecture and design methodology of the proposed modified TIA, LA, and complete optical receiver. It also introduces the active-inductor technique for bandwidth enhancement and analyzes the input-referred noise characteristics of the modified TIA using an equivalent noise circuit model. Section 3 describes the gm/ID design methodology and demonstrates its application to the optimization of the gain, bandwidth, and noise performance of the proposed TIA. Analytical formulations and simulation results are then provided to validate the effectiveness of the design approach. Section 4 discusses the simulation results for the amplifiers and the complete optical receiver, including performance comparisons with previously reported 40 Gbps designs. Finally, Sect. 5 concludes the paper.

Modified circuit architecture and design

Conventional inverter-based TIA topology

Recent studies have shown that the inverter, although traditionally regarded as a fundamental digital building block, can also operate effectively in analog applications29,30. When both transistors operate in the saturation region, small variations in the input voltage can induce substantial changes at the output31. Under this condition, the inverter behaves as a voltage amplifier and has therefore been adopted as the core element in a variety of analog circuit implementations32.

Simultaneous conduction of the n-channel and p-channel transistors increases the effective transconductance, thereby extending the bandwidth and enabling high-speed amplification in optical receiver applications6. Inverter-based architectures also offer favorable noise characteristics because the input-referred noise contribution is shared by both transistors33,34,35. In addition, their inherently low input resistance makes them well suited for wideband applications36–38.

Modified transimpedance amplifier

Figure 3 illustrates the proposed modified cascode inverter-based TIA, which consists of CNTFETs CNT1–CNT4. The circuit incorporates cascoding, resistive feedback, diode-connected loading, and active-inductor-based peaking techniques. The cascode configuration increases the inverter output impedance to approximately gm2rds2rds1∥gm3rds3rds4, thereby improving the DC gain compared with a conventional inverter-based TIA while maintaining nearly the same power consumption37. CNT5 operates as a feedback resistor with an equivalent resistance of rds5, which reduces the output resistance and low-frequency gain and consequently extends the bandwidth6,38. CNT6 is implemented as a diode-connected device and behaves as a small-signal resistance of approximately 1/gm6, further lowering the output resistance32.

The active inductor formed by CNT7 and CNT8 introduces controlled inductive peaking by generating an equivalent inductance that resonates with the output capacitance, thereby extending the − 3 dB bandwidth15. Although active inductors generally exhibit higher noise, lower quality factor, and greater sensitivity to bias and process variations than passive inductors, they provide important advantages for this design, including electronic tunability, compact area, and compatibility with fully integrated multi-GHz operation. In particular, the peaking response can be adjusted through transistor sizing and bias optimization, while the use of only two CNTFET devices substantially reduces the chip area compared with an on-chip passive inductor. To minimize power consumption, the TIA is implemented as a single-stage amplifier. The use of active devices also reduces the occupied area and simplifies integration.

In cascaded multi-stage structures, cascading several similar LA stages reduces the overall bandwidth of the signal chain. Therefore, the bandwidth of each stage must be sufficiently large. gm/ID design methodology

The gm/ID methodology provides a systematic framework for optimizing analog circuit performance by using the transconductance-to-drain-current ratio as a key design parameter39. This approach simplifies the design procedure, reduces design-variable interdependence, and enables a trade-off between power efficiency and circuit performance40. Although this methodology is well established for MOSFET-based circuits, its application to CNTFET-based designs introduces additional challenges due to the quantized dimensions of carbon nanotubes41.

Figure 9 illustrates the proposed gm/ID-based design flowchart for the modified TIA, including the steps required to achieve the target transimpedance gain, bandwidth, and input-referred noise. The procedure begins by selecting suitable gm/ID ratios and drain currents for each CNTFET according to the device function and the constraints listed in Table 1. These parameters are then substituted into the analytical expressions for gain, bandwidth, and input-referred noise derived within the gm/ID framework. The design is iteratively refined until the target specifications are satisfied.

Once the design objectives are met, the bias voltages and the number of nanotubes in each CNTFET are determined to realize the required conductivity and drain current. The complete circuit is subsequently simulated to evaluate the transistor operating regions, inductive peaking behavior, eye-diagram quality, and total power consumption. If the simulated performance deviates from the design targets, the parameters are adjusted and the procedure is repeated. The design process is completed when the simulation results satisfy the specified criteria.

To facilitate quantitative performance evaluation, two figures of merit (FoMs) are defined as40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:FOM1=\frac{Gain\:\left(dB\varOmega\:\right)\times\:Bandwidth\:\left(GHz\right)}{Power\:Consumption\:\left(mW\right)\times\:Input\:Referred\:Noise\:\left(pA/\sqrt{Hz}\right)}$$\end{document}41\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:FOM2=\frac{Gain\:\left(dB\varOmega\:\right)\times\:Bandwidth\:\left(GHz\right)\times\:{C}_{PD}\:\left(fF\right)}{Power\:Consumption\:\left(mW\right)\times\:Input\:Referred\:Noise\:\left(pA/\sqrt{Hz}\right)}$$\end{document}

Both FoMs indicate that the proposed modified cascode inverter-based TIA and low-noise optical receiver achieve favorable performance relative to previously reported designs. The observed improvements are primarily attributed to the selected circuit topology, low-voltage operation, and the efficient transport characteristics of CNTFET devices.

The photodiode capacitance, CPD, primarily determines the input pole of the TIA and therefore directly affects the receiver frequency response and bandwidth. In contrast, the DC or low-frequency transimpedance gain is mainly governed by the transconductance of the driver transistors and the effective output resistance. Accordingly, the reported reduction in power consumption should not be interpreted as a direct consequence of a smaller CPD. Rather, it mainly stems from the intrinsic device-level advantages of the CNTFET-based implementation, including higher achievable transconductance at lower bias current, reduced supply-voltage operation, and improved electrostatic gate control.

In this work, the photodiode capacitance is assumed to be 10 fF to model an intrinsic, best-case device-level condition. This choice is consistent with the nanoscale CNTFET technology considered here, in which the intrinsic and parasitic capacitances of circuit nodes are typically on the order of a few femtofarads. Therefore, a value of 10 fF is adopted for both the photodiode capacitance and the capacitive load as the dominant effective capacitance at the input and output nodes. Since this value is approximately one order of magnitude larger than the equivalent parasitic capacitances of CNTFET-based nodes, it provides a reasonable basis for evaluating the intrinsic high-frequency performance of the proposed circuit within the scope of the present analysis.

Accordingly, the selected 10 fF value should be interpreted as an idealized device-level assumption for assessing the intrinsic and high-speed performance of the proposed receiver, rather than as a complete representation of a practical optical receiver including all interconnect and packaging parasitics. Under this low-capacitance loading condition, the chosen value may therefore be regarded as physically plausible for evaluating the proposed design. Since a larger CPD may degrade the bandwidth, input-referred noise, and transient response, the comparison presented in Table 7 should be understood as a design-level benchmark rather than a fully normalized comparison under identical photodiode-capacitance conditions.

Conclusion

This study presented the design and simulation of a 40 Gbps optical receiver implemented in 32 nm CNTFET technology. The receiver architecture consists of two identical modified cascode inverter-based TIAs followed by three cascaded differential LA stages. To improve high-frequency performance, the TIA incorporates resistor-feedback, diode-connected, and active-inductor techniques. All circuit blocks operate under a ± 0.45 V supply.

The modified TIA achieves a transimpedance gain of 47.6 dBΩ, a − 3 dB bandwidth of 28.4 GHz, an input-referred noise current of 15.5 pA/√Hz, and a power consumption of 183 µW within an area of 3.93 μm². In addition, the analytical design based on the gm/ID methodology shows good agreement with the simulation results. The LA stage provides a voltage gain of 10.3 dB, a − 3 dB bandwidth of 32 GHz, and a power consumption of 382 µW, while occupying an area of 11.14 μm².

At the system level, the complete optical receiver achieves a transimpedance gain of 77.8 dBΩ, a − 3 dB bandwidth of 29.5 GHz, and an input-referred noise current of 13.74 pA/√Hz, with a total power consumption of 1377 µW and an area of 49.19 μm². Overall, the simulation results, supported by analytical validation, indicate that the proposed design achieves a favorable trade-off between performance and power consumption. These characteristics suggest that the receiver is a suitable candidate for integration into next-generation energy-efficient optical communication systems.
