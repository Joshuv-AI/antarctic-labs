Rank Distribution and Dynamics of Gram Matrices from Binary m-Sequences with Applications to LCD Codes
Hengfeng Liu, Chunming Tang, Cuiling Fan, Zhengchun Zhou

Abstract
The Gram matrix is a classical object formed from the pairwise inner products of a collection of vectors, with fundamental roles in functional analysis, statistics, combinatorics, and coding theory. In the realm of sequence design, maximum-length sequences (m-sequences) are among the most fundamental classes of sequences, traditionally characterized by their span, decimation, shift-and-add, balance, run, and ideal autocorrelation properties. In this paper, we bridge the two foundational concepts by uncovering novel structural features of m-sequences through the lens of a family of Gram matrices. Specifically, for each 1 ≤ t ≤ 2 n − 1 1\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1 , we extract n n consecutive subsequences of length t t from an m-sequence of period 2 n − 1 2^{n}-1 , construct their corresponding n × n n\times n Gram matrix, and investigate its rank, denoted by r n ​ ( t ) r_{n}(t) . Utilizing semilinear representation of Galois groups and Bézoutian of polynomials, we derive an explicit formula for r n ​ ( t ) r_{n}(t) for all t t , thereby establishing the complete rank distribution of these Gram matrices. Notably, we prove that full rank is attained for approximately half of the admissible values of t t . We further uncover the intricate dynamics of r n ​ ( t ) r_{n}(t) : rank-deficient states are strictly unstable (i.e., r n ​ ( t ) < n r_{n}(t)<n implies r n ​ ( t + 1 ) ≠ r n ​ ( t ) r_{n}(t+1)\neq r_{n}(t) ), whereas the full-rank state exhibits strong persistence, remaining at n n over a nontrivial interval of consecutive values of t t . Altogether, our results fully characterize both the global rank distribution and the local dynamics of rank function, as invariant of m-sequences. As an application, our findings completely determine the hull distribution of the family of punctured cyclic simplex codes.

1.

Span Property:
Each nonzero

occurs exactly once in one period of
\{s_{t}\}

2.

Decimation Property:
For any positive integer

\{s_{dt}\}
is also an m-sequence of period
2^{n}-1
.

3.

Shift-and-Add Property:
For any shift
\tau\not\equiv 0\pmod{2^{n}-1}

\{s_{t}^{\prime}\}
with
s_{t}^{\prime}=s_{t+\tau}+s_{t}
is also an m-sequence of period
2^{n}-1
.

4.

Balance Property:
In one period of
\{s_{t}\}
, the element
1
occurs exactly
2^{n-1}
times, and the zero element occurs
2^{n-1}-1
times.

5.

Run Property:
In one period of
\{s_{t}\}
, exactly
1/2
of the runs have length
1

1/4
have length
2
,
1/8
have length
3
,
1/16
have length
4
, and so on, as long as these fractions give integral numbers of runs. Here, a run of length
k
is defined as a block

such that
s_{i-1}\neq s_{i}=\dots=s_{i+k-1}\neq s_{i+k}
.

6.

Ideal Autocorrelation Property:
The periodic autocorrelation function
C_{s}(\tau)
is given by

C_{s}(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{2^{n}-2}(-1)^{s_{t+\tau}-s_{t}}=\begin{cases}2^{n}-1,&\text{if }\tau\equiv 0\pmod{2^{n}-1},\\
-1,&\text{if }\tau\not\equiv 0\pmod{2^{n}-1}.\end{cases}

In particular, Golomb recognized the importance of the balance, run, and ideal autocorrelation properties in the context of randomness. Binary sequences satisfying these three properties are called
pseudo-noise (PN) sequences
. Due to their good pseudo-random properties, PN sequences have many practical applications in digital communications, including navigation, radar, and spread-spectrum systems

(2)

In this paper, we not only answer the two questions, but also reveal new properties of m-sequences that go beyond the classical ones established by Golomb. More precisely, we derive new properties of

The main results of this paper can be summarized in four structural properties of binary m-sequences. The following theorem gives a global rank distribution of Gram matrices arising from m-sequences.

Theorem 1
(Rank distribution).

Let
n\geq 3
, and let
\{s_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
be an m-sequence of order
n
. For
1\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1
, let
G_{t}
be the observability matrix associated with the m-sequence. Then the rank distribution of the family
\{G_{t}G_{t}^{\top}\mid 1\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1\}
is given by

\#\{\,1\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1\mid\ {\mathrm{rank}}(G_{t}G_{t}^{\top})=k\,\}=\begin{cases}1,&k=0,\\
2,&k=1,\\
2^{k-1},&2\leq k\leq n-1,\\
2^{n-1}-2,&k=n.\end{cases}

(3)

The next three theorems characterize the local rank dynamics.

Theorem 2
(Persistence after reaching full rank).

Let
n\geq 3
, write
r_{n}(t):={\mathrm{rank}}(G_{t}G_{t}^{\top})
, and regard
t
modulo
2^{n}-1
. If
r_{n}(t-1)=n-1
and
r_{n}(t)=n
, then

r_{n}(t+1)=n.

Theorem 3
(Instability of rank-deficient states).

Let
n\geq 3
, if
r_{n}(t)<n
, then

r_{n}(t+1)\neq r_{n}(t).

Theorem 4
(Enumeration of local minima).

A value
t
(modulo
2^{n}-1
) is called a local minimum of the rank function
r_{n}(\cdot)
if

r_{n}(t-1)=r_{n}(t)+1\quad\text{and}\quad r_{n}(t+1)=r_{n}(t)+1.

For
n\geq 3
, the number of local minima of
r_{n}(\cdot)
is

\frac{2^{n-1}-(-1)^{n-1}}{3}.

Preliminaries

In this section, we present some preliminaries and auxiliary results that will be used.

2.1
LFSR sequences and m-sequences

In this subsection, we provide some basic properties of binary LFSR sequences and m-sequences.

Definition 5
.

Let
g(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]
with
g(0)=1
. The order of
g(x)
is defined to be the least positive integer
N
such that
g(x)\mid(x^{N}-1)
.

Definition 6
.

For the field extension
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}
, the trace function

{\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}:\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}

is defined by

{\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(a)=a+a^{2}+\cdots+a^{2^{n-1}}.

Definition 7
.

Let

g(x)=x^{n}+c_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+c_{1}x+c_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x].

A binary sequence
\{s_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is called an LFSR sequence with characteristic polynomial
g(x)
if

s_{t+n}=c_{n-1}s_{t+n-1}+\cdots+c_{1}s_{t+1}+c_{0}s_{t}\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

If
\{s_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
cannot be generated by any LFSR of smaller order, then
g(x)
is called the minimal polynomial of the sequence. The degree of the minimal polynomial of
\{s_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is called its linear complexity.

The minimal polynomial and linear complexity of an LFSR sequence can be determined by the following proposition
[
34
]
.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\{s_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
be an LFSR sequence of period
N
, and let
S^{N}(x)=s_{0}+s_{1}x+\cdots+s_{N-1}x^{N-1}
. Then

1.

the minimal polynomial of
\{s_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is

\frac{x^{N}-1}{\gcd(x^{N}-1,S^{N}(x))};

2.

the linear complexity of
\{s_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is

n-\deg(\gcd(x^{N}-1,S^{N}(x))).

Linear complexity serves as a crucial measure of the strength of keystream sequences in stream cipher systems, and more broadly, as an indicator of the randomness quality of sequences
[
13
]
.

The trace representation and minimal period of LFSR sequences are presented in the following theorem, summarized from
[
19
,
20
]
.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\{s_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
be a binary LFSR sequence with characteristic polynomial
g(x)
.

1.

Suppose that
g(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]
is irreducible of degree
n
, and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
be a root of
g(x)
. Then there exists
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, such that

s_{t}={\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\beta\alpha^{t}).

If
g(x)
is the minimal polynomial of the sequence, then the minimal period of
\{s_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is
{\mathrm{ord}}(g)
.

2.

More generally, suppose that

g(x)=g_{1}(x)g_{2}(x)\cdots g_{e}(x),

where
g_{1}(x),\dots,g_{e}(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]
are distinct irreducible polynomials, with
\deg(g_{i})=d_{i}
, and let
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{d_{i}}}
be a root of
g_{i}(x)
for each
1\leq i\leq e
. Then there exist
\gamma_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{d_{i}}}
such that

s_{t}={\mathrm{Tr}}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{e}\gamma_{i}\alpha_{i}^{t}\right).

where
{\mathrm{Tr}}
denotes the trace function from the splitting field of
g(x)
to
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. If
g(x)
is the minimal polynomial of the sequence, then the minimal period of
\{s_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is

\mathrm{lcm}\bigl({\mathrm{ord}}(g_{1}),\dots,{\mathrm{ord}}(g_{e})\bigr).

If
g(x)
is primitive of degree
n
, then
{\mathrm{ord}}(g)=2^{n}-1
, which is the maximum possible period of a nonzero binary LFSR sequence of order
n
. Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
be a root of
g(x)
. Then such an m-sequence can be written in the form

s_{t}={\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\lambda\alpha^{t}),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}.

(4)

We further recall the generating function representation of LFSR sequences,
a classical result that expresses an LFSR sequence as a rational function. For details, the reader is referred to
[
20
]
.

Proposition 10
.

Let
\{s_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
be a binary LFSR sequence with characteristic polynomial

g(z)\;=\;z^{r}+c_{r-1}z^{r-1}+\cdots+c_{1}z+c_{0}\;\in\;\mathbb{F}_{2}[z],\quad c_{0}=1

and initial state

(s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{r-1})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
. Then we have the representation:

\frac{h(z)}{\widetilde{g}(z)}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}s_{t}z^{t},

where
\widetilde{g}(z)=z^{r}g(z^{-1})
is the reciprocal polynomial of

g(z)
, and

h(z)\;=\;\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{i}c_{\,r-i+j}\,s_{j}\bigg)z^{i}.

Example 11
.

Let
n=5
and take a primitive polynomial

g(z)\;=\;z^{5}+z^{2}+1\;\in\;\mathbb{F}_{2}[z],

so that its reciprocal is
\widetilde{g}(z)=1+z^{3}+z^{5}
. The
coefficients of
g(z)
are

(c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4},c_{5})=(1,0,1,0,0,1)
. With initial state

(s_{0},s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4})=(1,0,0,0,0)
, the linear recurrence

s_{t+5}=s_{t+2}+s_{t}
generates an m-sequence of order
5
; and we have

h(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{4}\bigl(\sum_{j=0}^{i}c_{5-i+j}\,s_{j}\bigr)z^{i}=1+z^{3}.

Then Proposition
10
gives

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}s_{t}\,z^{t}\;=\;\frac{1+z^{3}}{1+z^{3}+z^{5}}.

2.2
Semilinear representation of Galois groups

In this subsection we introduce the semilinear representation of Galois groups, which is needed in Section
3
. Semilinear representation is a natural extension of linear representation, and frequently appears in several branches of modern mathematics, including projective geometry
[
1
]
, Galois cohomology
[
41
]
, and
p
-adic Hodge theory
[
17
]
.

We first give the definitions of semilinear map and semilinear representation.

Definition 12
.

Let
V
and
W
be vector spaces over a field
K
, a semilinear map between them is a function
T:V\to W
satisfying

1.

T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v)
;

2.

there exists a field automorphism
\psi
of
K
such that,
T(\lambda v)=\psi(\lambda)T(v),\forall\lambda\in K.

Then
T
is called
\psi
-semilinear. When
V=W
, we say that
T
is a semilinear transformation on
V
. All invertible semilinear transforms on a vector space
V
forms a group
\Gamma\rm{L}(V)
, called the general semilinear group, extending the general linear group
\rm{GL}(V)
.

Definition 13
.

A semilinear representation of a group
G
on a vector space
V
over a field
K
is a group homomorphism
\rho:G\to\Gamma\rm{L}(V)
from
G
to
\Gamma\rm{L}(V)
given by
g\mapsto\rho_{g}
, such that
\rho_{g_{1}g_{2}}=\rho_{g_{1}}\rho_{g_{2}}
.

We will use the following well-known lemma, which asserts that distinct characters are linear independent.

Lemma 14
(Dedekind’s lemma).

Let
G
be a group,
L
be a field, and let
\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{n}:G\to L^{\times}
be pairwise distinct
L
-characters of
G
. If
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in L
satisfy

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\chi_{i}(g)=0,\quad\forall g\in G,

then
\lambda_{i}=0,\,1\leq i\leq n
.

For a Galois extension
L/K
, if the Galois group
G
admits a semilinear representation
\rho:G\to\Gamma\rm{L}(V)
,
g\mapsto\rho_{g}
, where
V
is an
L
-vector space, then the set of fixed points: i.e.,

V^{G}=\{v\in V\mid\rho_{\sigma}(v)=v,\quad\forall\sigma\in G\},

forms a
K
-subspace of
V
. Using the technique of scalar extension, we can further determine the dimension of
V^{G}
by the following theorem.

Given a field extension
L/K
and
a
K
-vector space
W
, it is well-known that the extension of scalars
W\otimes_{K}L
is naturally an
L
-vector space with
\dim_{L}(W\otimes_{K}L)=\dim_{K}W
.

Theorem 15
.

Let
L/K
be a finite Galois extension with Galois group
G=\mathrm{Gal}(L/K)
, and let

V
be an
L
-vector space equipped with a semilinear representation
\rho:G\to\Gamma\rm{L}(V)
,
g\mapsto\rho_{g}
. Define a
K
-subspace of
V
:

V^{G}=\{v\in V\mid\rho_{\sigma}(v)=v,\,\forall\sigma\in G\}.

Then the
map

\mu\colon V^{G}\otimes_{K}L\longrightarrow V,\quad v\otimes\lambda\longmapsto\lambda v,

is an isomorphism of
L
-vector spaces. In particular,

\dim_{K}V^{G}=\dim_{L}V
.

Proof.

We first prove the injectivity. Suppose, for contradiction, that
\ker(\mu)\neq 0
, and pick a non-zero element

\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{r}v_{i}\otimes\lambda_{i}\in\ker(\mu)

with
r
chosen as the smallest. Clearly
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
for each
i
. Dividing
\omega
by
\lambda_{1}
,
we may further assume
\lambda_{1}=1
. The condition
\mu(\omega)=0

reads

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}v_{i}=0.

(5)

Applying any
\sigma\in G
to Eq. (
5
) and together with
\sigma(v_{i})=v_{i}
, we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma(\lambda_{i})\,v_{i}=0,\quad\forall\,\sigma\in G.

(6)

Subtracting Eq. (
5
) from Eq. (
6
) yields

\sum_{i=2}^{r}\bigl(\sigma(\lambda_{i})-\lambda_{i}\bigr)\,v_{i}=0,\quad\forall\,\sigma\in G.

(7)

Suppose that some
\lambda_{i_{0}}
with

i_{0}\geq 2
does not lie in
K=L^{G}
. Then there exists

\sigma_{0}\in G
with

\sigma_{0}(\lambda_{i_{0}})\neq\lambda_{i_{0}}
. Specializing Eq.
(
7
) to
\sigma=\sigma_{0}
gives that the element

\omega^{\prime}:=\sum_{i=2}^{r}v_{i}\otimes\bigl(\sigma_{0}(\lambda_{i})-\lambda_{i}\bigr)\in V^{G}\otimes_{K}L

satisfies
\mu(\omega^{\prime})=0
and has its
i_{0}
-th coefficient non-zero,
so
\omega^{\prime}\neq 0
; but this contradicts the
minimality of
r
.

Therefore
\lambda_{i}\in K
for every
i
, together with Eq. (
5
), we obtain

\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{r}v_{i}\otimes\lambda_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\lambda_{i}v_{i})\otimes 1=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}v_{i}\Bigr)\otimes 1=0,

contradicting the assumption
\omega\neq 0
. Hence
\ker(\mu)=0
.

Next, we prove the surjectivity of
\mu
. Let
W\subseteq V
be the image of
\mu
, which is the
L
-span of
V^{G}
. We prove that
W=V
.

Observe that
W
is stable under the
G
-action on
V
, i.e.,
\rho_{\sigma}(W)\subseteq W
. Indeed, for any

\sigma\in G

2.3
Bézoutian of polynomials

We briefly recall the definition of the Bézoutian and the rank formula that will be needed. Bézoutians is a classical object invented by Bézout
[
3
,
4
]
, and developed by Sylvester
[
44
]
, Jacobi
[
26
]
and Cayley
[
11
]
, with primary applications in elimination theory. Over the years, Bézoutian has become a useful tool in many fields, such as control theory, and symbolical computing
[
28
]
.

Let
K
be a field, and let
f(x),g(x)\in K[x]
satisfy

\deg(f),\deg(g)\leq n.

The Bézoutian of order
n
associated with
f
and
g
is a symmetric matrix
B_{n}(f,g)=(b_{i,j})_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}
, where the entries
b_{i,j}
are coefficients in the following expansion of a bivariate polynomial:

\frac{f(x)g(y)-g(x)f(y)}{x-y}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}b_{i,j}x^{i}y^{j}.

The following rank formula of Bézoutian is standard.

Theorem 16
.

Let
f(z),g(z)\in K[z]
be nonzero polynomials with

\max\{\deg(f),\deg(g)\}\leq n.

Let
B_{n}(f,g)
be their associated Bézoutian.
Then

{\mathrm{rank}}\bigl(B_{n}(f,g))\bigr)=\max\{\deg(f),\deg(g)\}-\deg(\gcd(f,g)).

Example 17
.

Let
K=\mathbb{F}_{2}
, and let

f(z)=z^{3}+1,\quad g(z)=z^{3}+z^{2}.

Then

\gcd(f,g)=z+1,

\displaystyle\frac{f(x)g(y)-g(x)f(y)}{x-y}

\displaystyle=\frac{(x^{3}+1)(y^{3}+y^{2})-(x^{3}+x^{2})(y^{3}+1)}{x-y}

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{3}y^{2}+y^{3}+y^{2}+x^{2}y^{3}+x^{2}}{x-y}

\displaystyle=x^{2}y^{2}+x^{2}+xy+x+y^{2}+y.

Hence the associated Bézoutian matrix is

B_{3}(f,g)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1\\
1&1&0\\
1&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,

{\mathrm{rank}}\bigl(B_{3}(f,g)\bigr)=2,

in agreement with Theorem
16
.

3
Rank problem of Gram matrices associated with m-sequences

In this section, we explicitly determine the ranks of the singular Gram matrices
G_{t}G_{t}^{\top}
, associated with binary m-sequences over the full period
1\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1
. Via a factorization for the observability matrix
G_{t}
, we first lift the rank problem from the binary field to its extension field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
. Subsequently, for singular Gram matrices, we develop a rank characterization using semilinear representation of Galois groups. Employing a rank argument of polynomial Bézoutian, we then obtain a rank formula for those matrices. This allows us to translate the rank distribution problem into enumeration of certain rational functions.

3.1
Reduction to symmetric matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}[x]

Let
\{s_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the binary
m
-sequence given by

s_{t}={\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\lambda\alpha^{t}),

where
\alpha
is a primitive element of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
. For

1\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1
, recall that the associated observability matrix is

G_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}s_{0}&s_{1}&\cdots&s_{t-1}\\
s_{1}&s_{2}&\cdots&s_{t}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
s_{n-1}&s_{n}&\cdots&s_{n+t-2}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\,n\times t}.

(10)

For vectors
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{t})
and
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{t})
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}
, define the Euclidean inner product of
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
by

\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=\sum_{r=1}^{t}u_{r}v_{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}.

Write

\mathbf{R}_{i}=(s_{i-1},s_{i},\dots,s_{i+t-2})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t},\quad 1\leq i\leq n,

for the
i
-th row of
G_{t}
. Our goal is to determine the rank of the following Gram matrix:

G_{t}G_{t}^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{1}&\mathbf{R}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{2}&\cdots&\mathbf{R}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{n}\\
\mathbf{R}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{1}&\mathbf{R}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{2}&\cdots&\mathbf{R}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{R}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{1}&\mathbf{R}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{2}&\cdots&\mathbf{R}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

One obstacle is that, over the binary field, a direct rank analysis of
G_{t}G_{t}^{\top}
is not tractable. To obtain a more
explicit description, we lift the problem to the extension field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
by the following lemma, where
G_{t}
admits a
Vandermonde-type factorization.

Lemma 18
.

For
1\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1
, let
G_{t}
be the observability matrix associated with a binary
m
-sequence given by

s_{t}={\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\lambda\alpha^{t}).

For
1\leq\ell\leq n
, write
\alpha_{\ell}=\alpha^{2^{\ell-1}}
and
\lambda_{\ell}=\lambda^{2^{\ell-1}}
. Then,

G_{t}=V\Lambda\widetilde{G}_{t},

where
V_{i,\ell}=\alpha_{\ell}^{\,i-1}
for
1\leq i,\ell\leq n
,
\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
, and

\widetilde{G}_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{1}^{2}&\cdots&\alpha_{1}^{t-1}\\
1&\alpha_{2}&\alpha_{2}^{2}&\cdots&\alpha_{2}^{t-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\alpha_{n}&\alpha_{n}^{2}&\cdots&\alpha_{n}^{t-1}\end{pmatrix}.

(11)

Furthermore, one has

1.

{\mathrm{rank}}(G_{t})={\mathrm{rank}}(\widetilde{G}_{t})=\min\{t,n\}
.

2.

Let
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
be a variable, and let
M(x)
be a symmetric matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}[x]
with

M(x)_{i,j}=\frac{1+x^{2^{i-1}+2^{j-1}}}{1+\alpha^{2^{i-1}+2^{j-1}}},\quad 1\leq i,j\leq n,\quad i.e.,

M(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{1+x^{2^{0}+2^{0}}}{1+\alpha^{2^{0}+2^{0}}}&\dfrac{1+x^{2^{0}+2^{1}}}{1+\alpha^{2^{0}+2^{1}}}&\cdots&\dfrac{1+x^{2^{0}+2^{n-1}}}{1+\alpha^{2^{0}+2^{n-1}}}\\[5.16663pt]
\dfrac{1+x^{2^{1}+2^{0}}}{1+\alpha^{2^{1}+2^{0}}}&\dfrac{1+x^{2^{1}+2^{1}}}{1+\alpha^{2^{1}+2^{1}}}&\cdots&\dfrac{1+x^{2^{1}+2^{n-1}}}{1+\alpha^{2^{1}+2^{n-1}}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\dfrac{1+x^{2^{n-1}+2^{0}}}{1+\alpha^{2^{n-1}+2^{0}}}&\dfrac{1+x^{2^{n-1}+2^{1}}}{1+\alpha^{2^{n-1}+2^{1}}}&\cdots&\dfrac{1+x^{2^{n-1}+2^{n-1}}}{1+\alpha^{2^{n-1}+2^{n-1}}}\end{pmatrix}.

(12)

If
x=\alpha^{t}
, where
1\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1
, then,

{\mathrm{rank}}(G_{t}G_{t}^{\top})={\mathrm{rank}}(M(x)).

Proof.

For
1\leq i\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq t
,

(G_{t})_{i,j}=s_{i+j-2}={\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\lambda\alpha^{i+j-2})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(\lambda\alpha^{i+j-2})^{2^{\ell-1}}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\lambda_{\ell}\alpha_{\ell}^{\,i-1}\alpha_{\ell}^{\,j-1},

so
G_{t}=V\Lambda\widetilde{G}_{t}
. Since
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
are pairwise distinct,
V
is
nonsingular; so is
\Lambda
because
\lambda\neq 0
. Hence

{\mathrm{rank}}(G_{t})={\mathrm{rank}}(\widetilde{G}_{t})=\min\{t,n\}
.

Moreover,

G_{t}G_{t}^{\top}=V\Lambda\,\widetilde{G}_{t}\widetilde{G}_{t}^{\top}\,\Lambda V^{\top},

and hence

{\mathrm{rank}}(G_{t}G_{t}^{\top})={\mathrm{rank}}(\widetilde{G}_{t}\widetilde{G}_{t}^{\top}).

Also,

(\widetilde{G}_{t}\widetilde{G}_{t}^{\top})_{i,j}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\widetilde{G}_{t})_{i,k}(\widetilde{G}_{t})_{j,k}=\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}(\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j})^{k}=\frac{1+(\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j})^{t}}{1+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}},\quad 1\leq i,j\leq n.

Since
\alpha_{i}=\alpha^{2^{i-1}}
, writing
x=\alpha^{t}
yields

(\widetilde{G}_{t}\widetilde{G}_{t}^{\top})_{i,j}=\frac{1+x^{2^{i-1}+2^{j-1}}}{1+\alpha^{2^{i-1}+2^{j-1}}}=M(x)_{i,j},\quad 1\leq i,j\leq n.

Thus
\widetilde{G}_{t}\widetilde{G}_{t}^{\top}=M(x)
, and hence

{\mathrm{rank}}(G_{t}G_{t}^{\top})={\mathrm{rank}}(M(x)).

Therefore, determining the rank distribution of

\{G_{t}G_{t}^{\top}\mid 1\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1\}

is reduced to that of

\{M(x)\mid x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}\}.

Accordingly, the remainder of this section is devoted to the rank of
M(x)
.

3.2
Zeros of the determinant polynomials of the associated symmetric matrices

In this subsection, for
M(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n\times n}
defined as E.q (
12
), we study zeros of its determinant polynomial, i.e., the set

\Omega(M):=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}\mid\det(M(x))=0\}.

By Lemma
18
, under the parametrization
x=\alpha^{t}
, the elements of

\Omega(M)
are in one-to-one correspondence with those integers
t\in\{1,2,\dots,2^{n}-1\}
for
which the Gram matrix
G_{t}G_{t}^{\top}
is singular. We characterize the elements in
\Omega(M)
via a special class of rational functions associated with reciprocal polynomials. The following definition will be used.

Definition 19
.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}
denote the vector space of polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
with degree strictly less than
n
.
For any polynomial
f(z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]
, its standard reciprocal polynomial is defined as
\tilde{f}(z)=z^{\deg(f)}f(z^{-1})
. A polynomial is called self-reciprocal if
\tilde{f}(z)=f(z)
.

Furthermore, for any
f\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}
, we define its reciprocal polynomial with respect to degree
n-1
as

f^{*}(z)=z^{n-1}f(z^{-1}).

(13)

The operator
f\mapsto f^{*}
is an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear involution on the vector space
\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}
satisfying
(f^{*})^{*}=f
.

The following theorem is the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 20
.

Let
\alpha
be a primitive element of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, where
n\geq 3
, consider the following two sets:

1.

The set of rational functions

T=\left\{z^{k}\frac{\widetilde{u}(z)}{u(z)}\,\middle|\,u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n},\;u(0)=1,\;\gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1,\;|k|\leq n-1-\deg(u)\right\}.

2.

The set

\Omega(M)=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}\mid\det(M(x))=0\}.

Then the following evaluation is a bijection between them:

z^{k}\frac{\widetilde{u}(z)}{u(z)}\longmapsto\alpha^{k}\frac{\widetilde{u}(\alpha)}{u(\alpha)}.

Furthermore, each rational function
z^{k}\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{u}(z)}{u(z)}
in
T
is uniquely determined by the pair
(k,u(z))
.

We call this bijection a
canonical representation
of elements in
\Omega(M)
. The canonical representation will not only play an important role in the rank characterization, but also help us to obtain more results about rank dynamics in Section
4
.

We first prove the following theorem, from which Theorem
20
will follow.

Theorem 21
.

Let
n\geq 3
be an integer. Elements in

\Omega(M)=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}\mid\det(M(x))=0\}

are exactly generated by the rational mapping

x=\frac{P^{*}(\alpha)}{P(\alpha)},

where
P(z)
ranges over all non-zero polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}
.

The proof of Theorem
21
splits into two parts. We first deduce that any element in
\Omega(M)
must admit the representation
x=P^{*}(\alpha)/P(\alpha)
, and subsequently show that every such element constitutes a root.

Some auxiliary results will be needed. The following proposition characterizes that, within the kernel of
M(x)
, a vanishing determinant guarantees the presence of a vector whose components follow a Frobenius orbit. We achieve this by fixed-point theorem of semilinear representation presented in the previous section.

Proposition 22
.

If
\det(M(x))=0
, then the kernel of
M(x)
contains a non-zero vector
c\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n}
of the form

c=\left(c_{1},c_{1}^{2},c_{1}^{2^{2}},\dots,c_{1}^{2^{n-1}}\right)^{T},

(14)

for some
c_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}
.

Proof.

By assumption, the
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
-vector space
V=\ker M(x)\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n}
is non-trivial. For
1\leq i,j\leq n
, the matrix entries are given by
M(x)_{i,j}=\frac{1+x_{i}x_{j}}{1+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}}
with
x_{i}=x^{2^{i-1}}
and
\alpha_{i}=\alpha^{2^{i-1}}
. Observe that the matrix entries satisfy the relation

(M(x)_{i-1,j-1})^{2}=\frac{1+x_{i-1}^{2}x_{j-1}^{2}}{1+\alpha_{i-1}^{2}\alpha_{j-1}^{2}}=\frac{1+x_{i}x_{j}}{1+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}}=M(x)_{i,j},

with indices taken modulo
n
, where index
0
is identified with
n
.

Define a map
\phi:V\to V
by
\phi(v)_{i}=v_{i-1}^{2}
for
1\leq i\leq n
, i.e.,

\phi\!\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\\
\vdots\\
v_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{n}^{2}\\
v_{1}^{2}\\
\vdots\\
v_{n-1}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Indeed, for any
v\in V
,
\phi(v)
belong to the kernel of
M(x)
, because

\displaystyle(M(x)\phi(v))_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}M(x)_{i,j}\phi(v)_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(M(x)_{i-1,j-1}\right)^{2}v_{j-1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}M(x)_{i-1,j-1}v_{j-1}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left([M(x)v]_{i-1}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=0.

The field extension
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}
is Galois with group
G=\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2})=\langle\sigma\rangle
, where
\sigma(a)=a^{2}
is the Frobenius automorphism. Then there is a semilinear representation
\rho:G\to\Gamma\rm{L}(V)
, defined as
\rho(\sigma^{k})=\phi^{k}
. We now verify that this is indeed a semilinear representation:

1.

additivity:

\phi(v+w)_{i}=(v_{i-1}+w_{i-1})^{2}=v_{i-1}^{2}+w_{i-1}^{2}=\phi(v)_{i}+\phi(w)_{i}
. Thus
\phi
, and consequently all
\phi^{k}
, are additive.

2.

semilinearity:
For any scalar
a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
and
v\in V
, we have
\phi(av)_{i}=(av)_{i-1}^{2}=a^{2}v_{i-1}^{2}=\sigma(a)\phi(v)_{i}
. By induction,
\rho(\sigma^{k})
is semilinear.

3.

identity and composition:
Evaluating
\phi^{n}
on any vector
v\in V
, we get
\phi^{n}(v)_{i}=v_{i-n}^{2^{n}}=v_{i}^{2^{n}}
. Since
v_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
, we have
v_{i}^{2^{n}}=v_{i}
. Therefore,
\rho(\sigma^{n})=\rho(\sigma)=id_{V}
. The composition rule
\rho(\sigma^{k})\circ\rho(\sigma^{l})=\phi^{k+l\pmod{n}}=\rho(\sigma^{k+l})
trivially holds.

This verification confirms that
G
acts semilinearly on
V
. Since
V\neq 0
, Theorem
15
guarantees that the invariant
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-subspace
V^{G}
is non-trivial. Thus, there exists a non-zero vector
c\in V^{G}
.

Being a fixed point implies
\phi(c)=c
. Component-wise, this expands to
c_{i-1}^{2}=c_{i}
for
2\leq i\leq n
, and
c_{n}^{2}=c_{1}
. By induction, this recurrence directly yields
c_{i}=c_{1}^{2^{i-1}}
for
1\leq i\leq n
, which is precisely the desired form for the vector
c
.

The following partial fraction identity will be used.

Lemma 23
.

Let
L(z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]
be a polynomial of degree
n
with
n
distinct roots
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
, and let
L^{\prime}(z)
be its formal derivative. For any polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]
with
\deg(f)<n
, we have

\frac{f(z)}{L(z)}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{f(\alpha_{j})/L^{\prime}(\alpha_{j})}{z+\alpha_{j}}.

(15)

Proof.

By Lagrange interpolation over the
n
distinct roots of
L(z)
, we can write

f(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(\alpha_{j})\prod_{k\neq j}\frac{z-\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{k}}.

Since
L(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(z-\alpha_{i})
, its formal derivative evaluated at
\alpha_{j}
is exactly
L^{\prime}(\alpha_{j})=\prod_{k\neq j}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{k})
. Substituting this into the interpolation formula gives

f(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(\alpha_{j})\frac{L(z)}{(z-\alpha_{j})L^{\prime}(\alpha_{j})}.

Dividing both sides by
L(z)
yields the result.

Proposition 24
.

If
x
is a root of
\det(M(x))=0
, then
x=P^{*}(\alpha)/P(\alpha)
for some non-zero polynomial
P\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}
, where
n\geq 3
.

Proof.

Let
L(z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]
be the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
. By Proposition
22
, assume that
c=\left(c_{1},c_{1}^{2},c_{1}^{2^{2}},\dots,c_{1}^{2^{n-1}}\right)^{T}
is a non-zero vector contained in the kernel of
M(x)
. By polynomial interpolation, there exists a unique
p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}
such that
c_{1}=p(\alpha)/L^{\prime}(\alpha)
. Applying the Frobenius automorphism, we obtain
c_{i}=p(\alpha_{i})/L^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n
.

Let
d_{i}=x_{i}c_{i}
. Since
x_{i}=x_{1}^{2^{i-1}}
and
c_{i}=c_{1}^{2^{i-1}}
, we have
d_{i}=d_{1}^{2^{i-1}}
. Thus, there exists a unique
q\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}
such that
d_{i}=q(\alpha_{i})/L^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})
. Because
c_{i}\neq 0
, it follows that
p(\alpha_{i})\neq 0
, which implies

x_{i}=\frac{q(\alpha_{i})}{p(\alpha_{i})}.

(16)

The condition
M(x)c=0
means that for each row
i
,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1+x_{i}x_{j}}{1+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}}c_{j}=0.

Using
x_{j}c_{j}=d_{j}
and the representations
c_{i}=p(\alpha_{i})/L^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})
and
d_{i}=q(\alpha_{i})/L^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})
, this becomes

\alpha_{i}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{p(\alpha_{j})/L^{\prime}(\alpha_{j})}{\alpha_{i}^{-1}+\alpha_{j}}+x_{i}\alpha_{i}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{q(\alpha_{j})/L^{\prime}(\alpha_{j})}{\alpha_{i}^{-1}+\alpha_{j}}=0.

(17)

By Lemma
23
, the sums in (
17
) correspond to the partial fraction expansions of
p(z)/L(z)
and
q(z)/L(z)
evaluated at
z=\alpha_{i}^{-1}
. Hence, the equation simplifies to

\alpha_{i}^{-1}\frac{p(\alpha_{i}^{-1})}{L(\alpha_{i}^{-1})}+x_{i}\alpha_{i}^{-1}\frac{q(\alpha_{i}^{-1})}{L(\alpha_{i}^{-1})}=0.

Multiplying by
\alpha_{i}L(\alpha_{i}^{-1})
gives
p(\alpha_{i}^{-1})+x_{i}q(\alpha_{i}^{-1})=0
. Substituting
x_{i}=q(\alpha_{i})/p(\alpha_{i})
from (
16
), we get

p(\alpha_{i})p(\alpha_{i}^{-1})+q(\alpha_{i})q(\alpha_{i}^{-1})=0.

Multiplying by
\alpha_{i}^{n-1}
yields

p(\alpha_{i})p^{*}(\alpha_{i})+q(\alpha_{i})q^{*}(\alpha_{i})=0.

Let
H(z)=p(z)p^{*}(z)+q(z)q^{*}(z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]
, then
H(\alpha_{i})=0
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Observe that

\displaystyle H(z^{-1})

\displaystyle=p(z^{-1})p^{*}(z^{-1})+q(z^{-1})q^{*}(z^{-1})

\displaystyle=\big(z^{-(n-1)}p^{*}(z)\big)\big(z^{-(n-1)}p(z)\big)+\big(z^{-(n-1)}q^{*}(z)\big)\big(z^{-(n-1)}q(z)\big)

\displaystyle=z^{-(2n-2)}\big(p^{*}(z)p(z)+q^{*}(z)q(z)\big)

\displaystyle=z^{-(2n-2)}H(z).

Then
z^{2n-2}H(z^{-1})=H(z)
. Evaluating this identity at
z=\alpha_{i}
produces

\alpha_{i}^{2n-2}H(\alpha_{i}^{-1})=H(\alpha_{i})=0.

(18)

Since
\alpha_{i}\neq 0
, this immediately forces
H(\alpha_{i}^{-1})=0
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, providing
n
additional roots. For
n\geq 3
, the elements
\alpha
and
\alpha^{-1}
are not Galois conjugates, meaning the
2n
roots
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n},\alpha_{1}^{-1},\dots,\alpha_{n}^{-1}
are all distinct. Since
H(z)
has degree at most
2n-2
but possesses
2n
distinct roots, it must be the zero polynomial, giving

p(z)p^{*}(z)=q(z)q^{*}(z).

(19)

Let
P(z)=p(z)+q^{*}(z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}
. Then
P^{*}(z)=p^{*}(z)+q(z)
. Using
pp^{*}=qq^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle q(z)P(z)

\displaystyle=q(z)\big(p(z)+q^{*}(z)\big)

\displaystyle=q(z)p(z)+q(z)q^{*}(z)

\displaystyle=p(z)q(z)+p(z)p^{*}(z)

\displaystyle=p(z)\big(q(z)+p^{*}(z)\big)

\displaystyle=p(z)P^{*}(z).

Now the form of
x
can be determined:

•

Case 1:
P(z)\neq 0
.
Since
\deg(P)<n
,
P(\alpha)\neq 0
. Evaluating
qP=pP^{*}
at
z=\alpha
gives

x=\frac{q(\alpha)}{p(\alpha)}=\frac{P^{*}(\alpha)}{P(\alpha)}.

•

Case 2:
P(z)=0
.
Then
p(z)=q^{*}(z)
, which implies
p^{*}(z)=q(z)
. This directly gives

x=\frac{q(\alpha)}{p(\alpha)}=\frac{p^{*}(\alpha)}{p(\alpha)}.

In either case,
x
can be expressed in the required form.

Having established the necessary form of the zeros, we now prove the converse: any element admitting such a representation necessarily forces the matrix
M(x)
to be singular.

Proposition 25
.

For any non-zero polynomial
p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}
, the element
x=\displaystyle\frac{p^{*}(\alpha)}{p(\alpha)}
is a root of the equation
\det(M(x))=0
.

Proof.

Let
x=\displaystyle\frac{p^{*}(\alpha)}{p(\alpha)}
, then the
(i,j)
-th entry of the matrix
M(x)
is given by

M(x)_{i,j}=\frac{1+x_{i}x_{j}}{1+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}}=\frac{p(\alpha_{i})p(\alpha_{j})+p^{*}(\alpha_{i})p^{*}(\alpha_{j})}{p(\alpha_{i})p(\alpha_{j})(1+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j})}.

(20)

Using
p^{*}(z)=z^{n-1}p(z^{-1})
, we have
p^{*}(\alpha_{j})=\alpha_{j}^{n-1}p(\alpha_{j}^{-1})
and
p(\alpha_{j})=\alpha_{j}^{n-1}p^{*}(\alpha_{j}^{-1})
. Substituting these into the numerator yields

\displaystyle p(\alpha_{i})p(\alpha_{j})+p^{*}(\alpha_{i})p^{*}(\alpha_{j})

\displaystyle=p(\alpha_{i})\big(\alpha_{j}^{n-1}p^{*}(\alpha_{j}^{-1})\big)+p^{*}(\alpha_{i})\big(\alpha_{j}^{n-1}p(\alpha_{j}^{-1})\big)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{j}^{n-1}\big(p(\alpha_{i})p^{*}(\alpha_{j}^{-1})+p^{*}(\alpha_{i})p(\alpha_{j}^{-1})\big).

Writing the denominator factor as
1+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{j}(\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}^{-1})
, the matrix entry becomes

M(x)_{i,j}=\frac{1}{p(\alpha_{i})}\cdot\frac{p(\alpha_{i})p^{*}(\alpha_{j}^{-1})+p^{*}(\alpha_{i})p(\alpha_{j}^{-1})}{\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}^{-1}}\cdot\frac{\alpha_{j}^{n-2}}{p(\alpha_{j})}.

(21)

The middle term in (
21
) is precisely the Bézoutian polynomial
B(z,y)
of
p(z)
and
p^{*}(z)
, evaluated at
z=\alpha_{i}
and
y=\alpha_{j}^{-1}
. Since both
p
and
p^{*}
have degrees at most
n-1
, their Bézoutian has a maximum degree of
n-2
in each variable. Let
C=(C_{r,h})
be its
(n-1)\times(n-1)
coefficient matrix, such that

B(z,y)=\sum_{r=0}^{n-2}\sum_{h=0}^{n-2}C_{r,h}z^{r}y^{h}.

(22)

Substituting this expansion back into (
21
) gives:

M(x)_{i,j}=\frac{1}{p(\alpha_{i})}\left(\sum_{r=0}^{n-2}\sum_{h=0}^{n-2}\alpha_{i}^{r}C_{r,h}(\alpha_{j}^{-1})^{h}\right)\frac{\alpha_{j}^{n-2}}{p(\alpha_{j})}.

(23)

This scalar summation is the exact entry-wise definition of the matrix product
M(x)=\Delta_{1}V_{1}CV_{2}^{\top}\Delta_{2}
, where:

•

\Delta_{1}=\mathrm{diag}\big(p(\alpha_{1})^{-1},\dots,p(\alpha_{n})^{-1}\big)
,

•

\Delta_{2}=\mathrm{diag}\big(\alpha_{1}^{n-2}p(\alpha_{1})^{-1},\dots,\alpha_{n}^{n-2}p(\alpha_{n})^{-1}\big)
,

•

V_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n\times(n-1)}
with entries
(V_{1})_{i,r}=\alpha_{i}^{r}
,

•

V_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{n\times(n-1)}
with entries
(V_{2})_{j,h}=(\alpha_{j}^{-1})^{h}
.

Hence,
\mathrm{rank}(M(x))=\mathrm{rank}(C)\leq n-1
. The
n\times n
matrix
M(x)
is therefore singular, concluding the proof.

Combining Proposition
24
and Proposition
25
together, the proof of Theorem
21
is completed. To derive Theorem
20
from Theorem
21
, we need several auxiliary results.

Lemma 26
.

Let
n\geq 3
, and let
p,q\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}\setminus\{0\}
. If
\frac{p^{*}(\alpha)}{p(\alpha)}=\frac{q^{*}(\alpha)}{q(\alpha)}
, then their corresponding rational functions are identical in the function field
\mathbb{F}_{2}(z)
, i.e.,
\frac{p^{*}(z)}{p(z)}=\frac{q^{*}(z)}{q(z)}
.

Proof.

The condition implies
p^{*}(\alpha)q(\alpha)+p(\alpha)q^{*}(\alpha)=0
. Define the polynomial

H(z)=p^{*}(z)q(z)+p(z)q^{*}(z).

(24)

Because
H(z)
has coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
H(\alpha)=0
, it must also vanish at all
n
Galois conjugates of
\alpha
. Thus,
H(\alpha_{i})=0
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Using
p(z^{-1})=z^{-(n-1)}p^{*}(z)
and
q(z^{-1})=z^{-(n-1)}q^{*}(z)
, we have:

\displaystyle H(z^{-1})

\displaystyle=p^{*}(z^{-1})q(z^{-1})+p(z^{-1})q^{*}(z^{-1})

\displaystyle=\big(z^{-(n-1)}p(z)\big)\big(z^{-(n-1)}q^{*}(z)\big)+\big(z^{-(n-1)}p^{*}(z)\big)\big(z^{-(n-1)}q(z)\big)

\displaystyle=z^{-(2n-2)}\big(p(z)q^{*}(z)+p^{*}(z)q(z)\big)

\displaystyle=z^{-(2n-2)}H(z).

Evaluating the identity
z^{2n-2}H(z^{-1})=H(z)
at
z=\alpha_{i}
gives
\alpha_{i}^{2n-2}H(\alpha_{i}^{-1})=H(\alpha_{i})=0
. This yields
n
additional roots
H(\alpha_{i}^{-1})=0
.

For
n\geq 3
, the
2n
roots
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n},\alpha_{1}^{-1},\dots,\alpha_{n}^{-1}
are all distinct. Then
H(z)
must be the zero polynomial. This gives
\frac{p^{*}(z)}{p(z)}=\frac{q^{*}(z)}{q(z)}
.

Lemma 27
.

Let
0\neq f(z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]
. Then there exist a unique integer
r\geq 0
and unique
polynomials
v(z),u(z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]
such that

f(z)=z^{r}v(z)u(z),

where

u(0)=v(0)=1,\quad\widetilde{v}(z)=v(z),\quad\gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1.

Moreover, every self-reciprocal divisor of
f(z)
with nonzero constant term divides
v(z)
.

Proof.

Write
f(z)=z^{r}h(z)
, where
r\geq 0
and
h(0)=1
. Factor
h(z)
into monic irreducible polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Since
h(0)=1
, every
irreducible factor of
h
has nonzero constant term. For any such irreducible polynomial

\phi(z)
, its reciprocal polynomial
\widetilde{\phi}(z)
is again monic and irreducible.
Hence the irreducible factors of
h
are partitioned into two types: self-reciprocal factors

\phi=\widetilde{\phi}
, and reciprocal pairs
\{\psi,\widetilde{\psi}\}
with

\psi\neq\widetilde{\psi}
.

Therefore
h(z)
can be written uniquely in the form

h(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{s}f_{i}(z)^{a_{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{t}g_{j}(z)^{b_{j}}\widetilde{g_{j}}(z)^{c_{j}},

where each
f_{i}(z)
is monic irreducible and self-reciprocal, each pair

g_{j}(z),\widetilde{g_{j}}(z)
consists of distinct monic irreducible polynomials, and all
polynomials occurring above are pairwise distinct.

Define

v(z):=\prod_{i=1}^{s}f_{i}(z)^{a_{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{t}\bigl(g_{j}(z)\widetilde{g_{j}}(z)\bigr)^{\min(b_{j},c_{j})},

and

u(z):=\prod_{j=1}^{t}g_{j}(z)^{\,b_{j}-\min(b_{j},c_{j})}\widetilde{g_{j}}(z)^{\,c_{j}-\min(b_{j},c_{j})}.

Then

f(z)=z^{r}v(z)u(z).

By construction,
u(0)=1
. Also,
\widetilde{v}(z)=v(z)
, since each
f_{i}
is self-reciprocal and
each product
g_{j}\widetilde{g_{j}}
is self-reciprocal. Moreover, for each
j
, at most one of

g_{j}
and
\widetilde{g_{j}}
occurs in
u
, so no irreducible factor can divide both
u
and

\widetilde{u}
. Hence

\gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1.

Let
d(z)
be a self-reciprocal divisor of
f(z)
with
d(0)\neq 0
. Then
d(z)\mid h(z)
.
Since
d(z)=\widetilde{d}(z)
, the exponents of
\psi
and
\widetilde{\psi}
in
d(z)
are equal
for every reciprocal pair
\{\psi,\widetilde{\psi}\}
. Therefore the exponent of each
f_{i}
in

d(z)
is at most
a_{i}
, and the exponent of each of
g_{j}
and
\widetilde{g_{j}}
is at most

\min(b_{j},c_{j})
. It follows that

d(z)\mid v(z).

Thus every self-reciprocal divisor of
f(z)
with nonzero constant term divides
v(z)
. Finally, the integer
r
is uniquely determined by
f(z)
, and the factorization of
h(z)
into monic irreducible polynomials is unique. Hence the above construction uniquely determines
v(z)
and
u(z)
.
This completes the proof.

Proposition 28
.

In the rational function field
\mathbb{F}_{2}(z)
, the following two sets coincide:

S=\left\{\frac{p^{*}(z)}{p(z)}\,\middle|\,p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}\setminus\{0\}\right\},

and

T=\left\{z^{k}\frac{\widetilde{u}(z)}{u(z)}\,\middle|\,u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n},\;u(0)=1,\;\gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1,\;|k|\leq n-1-\deg(u)\right\}.

Moreover, every element of
T
is uniquely determined by the pair
(k,u(z))
.

Proof.

We first prove
S\subseteq T
. Take any

R(z)=\frac{p^{*}(z)}{p(z)}\in S,\quad 0\neq p(z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}.

By Lemma
27
, there exist unique polynomials
v(z),u(z)
such that
p(z)=z^{r}v(z)u(z)
, where

\widetilde{v}=v,\quad u(0)=v(0)=1,\quad\gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1.

Using
\widetilde{v}=v
, we obtain

p^{*}(z)=z^{n-1}p(z^{-1})=z^{n-1-r-\deg(v)-\deg(u)}\,v(z)\widetilde{u}(z).

Therefore

R(z)=\frac{p^{*}(z)}{p(z)}=z^{k}\frac{\widetilde{u}(z)}{u(z)},

where
k=n-1-2r-\deg(v)-\deg(u)
. Now set
s:=n-1-\deg(u)
.

Since
\deg(p)=r+\deg(v)+\deg(u)\leq n-1
, we have

r+\deg(v)\leq s.

It follows that

k=s-(2r+\deg(v))\leq s.

Also,

2r+\deg(v)\leq 2(r+\deg(v))\leq 2s,

so

k=s-(2r+\deg(v))\geq-s.

Thus

|k|\leq s=n-1-\deg(u),

and hence
R(z)\in T
. This proves
S\subseteq T
.

Next we prove
T\subseteq S
. Let

F(z)=z^{k}\frac{\widetilde{u}(z)}{u(z)}\in T,

where

u(0)=1,\quad\gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1,\quad|k|\leq n-1-\deg(u).

Set

r:=\max\{-k,0\},\quad D:=n-1-\deg(u)-k-2r.

Then
r\geq 0
and
D\geq 0
. Indeed, if
k\geq 0
, then
r=0
and

D=n-1-\deg(u)-k\geq 0,

while if
k<0
, then
r=-k
and

D=n-1-\deg(u)+k\geq 0

since
|k|\leq n-1-\deg(u)
.

Choose any self-reciprocal polynomial
v(z)
of degree
D
with
v(0)=1
, and define

p(z):=z^{r}v(z)u(z).

Then

\deg(p)=r+D+\deg(u)=n-1-k-r\leq n-1,

so
p(z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}\setminus\{0\}
.

Since
\widetilde{v}(z)=v(z)
, we have

\frac{p^{*}(z)}{p(z)}=z^{n-1-2r-D-\deg(u)}\frac{\widetilde{u}(z)}{u(z)}=z^{k}\frac{\widetilde{u}(z)}{u(z)}=F(z),

because
D=n-1-\deg(u)-k-2r
. Thus
F\in S
, and hence
T\subseteq S
. Therefore
S=T
.

Finally, we prove the uniqueness of the representation in
T
.
Assume

z^{k}\frac{\widetilde{u}(z)}{u(z)}=z^{\ell}\frac{\widetilde{w}(z)}{w(z)},

where both pairs
(k,u)
and
(\ell,w)
satisfy the defining conditions of
T
.
Without loss of generality, assume
k\geq\ell
. Then

z^{k-\ell}\widetilde{u}(z)w(z)=\widetilde{w}(z)u(z).

Evaluating the above identity at
z=0
gives
k=\ell
. Therefore

\widetilde{u}(z)w(z)=\widetilde{w}(z)u(z).

Since
\gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1
, the polynomial
u
divides
w
. By symmetry,
w
divides
u
. We conclude that
u=w
.

Thus the representation is unique.

Now Theorem
20
is a direct corollary of Proposition
28
and Lemma
26
.

3.3
Rank characterization of the associated symmetric matrices

Based on the established canonical representation of elements in
\Omega(M)
in Theorem
20
, using Bézoutian of polynomials, we prove the following theorem, which is an explicit characterization of
{\mathrm{rank}}(M(x))
.

Theorem 29
.

For
n\geq 3
, let
x\in\Omega(M)=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}\mid\det(M(x))=0\}
, and let

x=\alpha^{k_{0}}\frac{\widetilde{u}(\alpha)}{u(\alpha)}

be the canonical representation of
x
, where

u(0)=1,\quad\gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1,\quad|k_{0}|\leq n-1-\deg(u).

Then

{\mathrm{rank}}(M(x))=\deg(u)+|k_{0}|.

Proof.

Choose
0\neq p(z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}
such that

x=\frac{p^{*}(\alpha)}{p(\alpha)}.

By Proposition
28
and Lemma
27
, suppose

p(z)=z^{t}v(z)u(z),\quad p^{*}(z)=z^{w}v(z)\widetilde{u}(z),

where
\widetilde{v}(z)=v(z)
,
\gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1
, and
k_{0}=w-t
.

By the factorization in Proposition
25
,

M(x)=\Delta_{1}V_{1}CV_{2}^{\top}\Delta_{2},

where
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}
are invertible and
V_{1},V_{2}
have full column rank. Hence

{\mathrm{rank}}(M(x))={\mathrm{rank}}(C),

where
C
is exactly the Bézoutian

B_{n}(p,p*)=\frac{p(z)p^{*}(y)-p^{*}(z)p(y)}{z-y}.

By Theorem
16
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{rank}}(M(x))

\displaystyle=\max\{\deg(p),\deg(p^{*})\}-\deg(\gcd(p,p^{*}))

\displaystyle=\max\{t+\deg(v)+\deg(u),\,w+\deg(v)+\deg(u)\}-\deg\bigl(z^{\min(t,w)}v(z)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\max\{t,w\}+\deg(v)+\deg(u)-\min(t,w)-\deg(v)

\displaystyle=\deg(u)+|w-t|

\displaystyle=\deg(u)+|k_{0}|.

We conclude this section by providing the following example illustrating the canonical representation in Theorem
20
and the enumeration of singular Gram matrices.

Example 30
.

For
n=5
, Theorem
1
gives

\#\{1\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1\mid{\mathrm{rank}}(G_{t}G_{t}^{\top})<n\}=|T|=2^{n-1}+1=17.

A direct Magma computation confirms this. More precisely, one checks that

T=\left\{z^{k}\frac{\widetilde{u}(z)}{u(z)}\,\middle|\,u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<5},\;u(0)=1,\;\gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1,\;|k|\leq 4-\deg(u)\right\}

contains exactly
17
distinct rational functions, shown as follows:

1,\ z,\ z^{2},\ z^{3},\ z^{4},\ z^{-1},\ z^{-2},\ z^{-3},\ z^{-4},

and

\frac{z^{4}+z+1}{z^{4}+z^{3}+1},\quad\frac{z^{3}+z+1}{z^{4}+z^{3}+z},\quad\frac{z^{3}+z+1}{z^{3}+z^{2}+1},\quad\frac{z^{4}+z^{2}+z}{z^{3}+z^{2}+1},

\frac{z^{4}+z^{3}+1}{z^{4}+z+1},\quad\frac{z^{3}+z^{2}+1}{z^{4}+z^{2}+z},\quad\frac{z^{3}+z^{2}+1}{z^{3}+z+1},\quad\frac{z^{4}+z^{3}+z}{z^{3}+z+1}.

4
The proof of main results

In this section, we prove our main results stated in the introduction. We first enumerate polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
with non-zero constant term that are coprime to their reciprocal polynomials. This enumeration not only serves as a key ingredient in deriving the rank distribution, but may also be of independent interest.

Proposition 31
.

For each integer
d\geq 0
, we have the following enumeration:

\#\{u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]\mid\deg(u)=d,\ u(0)=1,\ \gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1\}=\begin{cases}1,&d=0,\\[2.0pt]
0,&d=1,2,\\[2.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{2^{d-1}-2(-1)^{d}}{3},&d\geq 3.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let

A_{d}:=\#\{u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]\mid\deg(u)=d,\ u(0)=1,\ \gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1\}.

Write their generating function as
U(x):=\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}A_{d}x^{d}
, and let
P(x):=\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}p_{d}x^{d}
be the generating function for polynomials
f(z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]
with
f(0)=1
, where
p_{d}

denotes the number of such polynomials of degree
d
. Since
p_{0}=1
, and
p_{d}=2^{d-1}(d\geq 1)
, we have

P(x)=1+\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}2^{d-1}x^{d}=\frac{1-x}{1-2x}.

Let
V(x):=\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}v_{i}x^{i}
be the generating function for self-reciprocal polynomials
v(z)
with
v(0)=1
, where
v_{i}
denotes the number of such polynomials of degree
i
. Since such a polynomial is determined by its coefficients of degrees
1,\dots,\lfloor i/2\rfloor
, one has

v_{i}=2^{\lfloor i/2\rfloor},

and therefore

V(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{\lfloor i/2\rfloor}x^{i}=\frac{1+x}{1-2x^{2}}.

By Lemma
27
, every polynomial
f(z)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]
with
f(0)=1

admits a unique factorization

f(z)=v(z)u(z),

where

v(0)=u(0)=1,\quad\widetilde{v}(z)=v(z),\quad\gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1.

Hence
P(x)=V(x)U(x)
, and thus

U(x)=\frac{P(x)}{V(x)}=\frac{(1-x)(1-2x^{2})}{(1-2x)(1+x)}.

(25)

Then we have

U(x)=1+\frac{2x^{3}}{(1-2x)(1+x)}=1+\frac{2x^{3}}{3}\left(\frac{2}{1-2x}+\frac{1}{1+x}\right),

(26)

expanding each fraction yields

U(x)=1+\frac{2x^{3}}{3}\left(2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(2x)^{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-x)^{j}\right)=1+\frac{2}{3}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(2^{j+1}+(-1)^{j}\right)x^{j+3}.

(27)

Substituting
d=j+3
in Eq. (
27
) gives

U(x)=1+\frac{2}{3}\sum_{d=3}^{\infty}\left(2^{d-2}+(-1)^{d-3}\right)x^{d}=1+\sum_{d=3}^{\infty}\frac{2^{d-1}-2(-1)^{d}}{3}x^{d}.

(28)

Then the coefficients
A_{0}=1
,
A_{1}=A_{2}=0
, and for
d\geq 3
,

A_{d}=\frac{2^{d-1}-2(-1)^{d}}{3}.

This completes the proof.

Combining Proposition
31
with the canonical representation in Theorem
20
and the rank characterization in Theorem
29
, we are now ready to prove Theorem
1
.

The proof of Theorem
1
.

By the canonical representation in Theorem
20
and the rank characterization in Theorem
29
, we have

\displaystyle N_{k}

\displaystyle:=\#\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}\mid{\mathrm{rank}}(M(x))=k\}

\displaystyle=\#\left\{z^{k_{0}}\frac{\widetilde{u}(z)}{u(z)}\,\middle|\,u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n},\ u(0)=1,\ \gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1,\ \deg(u)+|k_{0}|=k\right\}.

Since every rational function in this set is uniquely determined by the pair
(k_{0},u(z))
, for
0\leq k\leq n-1
, we have

N_{k}=A_{k}+2\sum_{d=0}^{k-1}A_{d},

where

A_{d}=\#\{u\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]\mid\deg(u)=d,\ u(0)=1,\ \gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1\}.

Write
N(x):=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}N_{k}x^{k}
and
U(x):=\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}A_{d}x^{d}
. Substituting the definition of
N_{k}
and interchanging the order of summation, we obtain

N(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}N_{k}x^{k}=U(x)+2\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}A_{d}\sum_{k=d+1}^{\infty}x^{k}=U(x)+\frac{2x}{1-x}\,U(x).

(29)

Combining Eq. (
29
) with Eq. (
25
) yields

N(x)=\frac{1+x}{1-x}\,U(x)=\frac{1-2x^{2}}{1-2x}.

(30)

Since

N(x)=(1-2x^{2})\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{j}x^{j}=1+2x+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}2^{k-1}x^{k},

the coefficients are
N_{0}=1
,
N_{1}=2
, and for
2\leq k\leq n-1
,
N_{k}=2^{k-1}
.

It follows that

\#\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}\mid{\mathrm{rank}}(M(x))=k\}=\begin{cases}1,&k=0,\\
2,&k=1,\\
2^{k-1},&2\leq k\leq n-1.\end{cases}

Because

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}N_{k}=1+2+\sum_{k=2}^{n-1}2^{k-1}=2^{n-1}+1,

we have

\#\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}^{*}\mid{\mathrm{rank}}(M(x))=n\}=(2^{n}-1)-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}N_{k}=2^{n-1}-2.

This proves the theorem.

We then prove the three dynamical properties, and begin with the persistence phenomenon after full-rank state.

The proof of Theorem
2
.

Since

r_{n}(t-1)={\mathrm{rank}}(M(\alpha^{t-1}))=n-1,

the matrix
M(\alpha^{t-1})
is singular. By Theorem
20
, the element
\alpha^{t-1}
admits a unique canonical representation

\alpha^{t-1}=\alpha^{k_{0}}\frac{\widetilde{u}(\alpha)}{u(\alpha)},\quad u(0)=1,\quad\gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1,\quad|k_{0}|\leq n-1-\deg(u).

By Theorem
29
,
n-1={\mathrm{rank}}(M(\alpha^{t-1}))=\deg(u)+|k_{0}|
, hence
|k_{0}|=n-1-\deg(u)
. If
k_{0}=-(n-1-\deg(u))
, then

|k_{0}+1|=n-2-\deg(u),

and therefore Theorem
29
gives

{\mathrm{rank}}(M(\alpha^{t}))=\deg(u)+|k_{0}+1|=n-2,

contrary to the assumption that

r_{n}(t)={\mathrm{rank}}(M(\alpha^{t}))=n.

Thus

k_{0}=n-1-\deg(u).

It follows that

\alpha^{t+1}=\alpha^{k_{0}+2}\frac{\widetilde{u}(\alpha)}{u(\alpha)}.

Since

k_{0}+2=n+1-\deg(u)>n-1-\deg(u),

we have

\alpha^{t+1}\notin T.

Hence
M(\alpha^{t+1})
is nonsingular, and therefore

r_{n}(t+1)={\mathrm{rank}}(M(\alpha^{t+1}))=n.

We next show that rank-deficient states are unstable.

The proof of Theorem
3
.

Set
x=\alpha^{t}
. Since

r_{n}(t)={\mathrm{rank}}(M(x))\leq n-1,

the matrix
M(x)
is singular. By Theorem
20
,
x
admits a unique canonical representation

x=\alpha^{k_{0}}\frac{\widetilde{u}(\alpha)}{u(\alpha)},\quad u(0)=1,\quad\gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1,\quad|k_{0}|\leq n-1-\deg(u).

Set
d=\deg(u)
. Then Theorem
29
gives

k=r_{n}(t)={\mathrm{rank}}(M(x))=d+|k_{0}|.

If
|k_{0}+1|\leq n-1-d
, then

\alpha x=\alpha^{k_{0}+1}\frac{\widetilde{u}(\alpha)}{u(\alpha)}

is again the canonical representation of
\alpha x
. Hence

r_{n}(t+1)={\mathrm{rank}}(M(\alpha x))=d+|k_{0}+1|.

Therefore

r_{n}(t+1)-r_{n}(t)=|k_{0}+1|-|k_{0}|\in\{-1,1\},

so

r_{n}(t+1)\in\{k-1,k+1\}.

Now suppose
|k_{0}+1|>n-1-d
. Since
|k_{0}|\leq n-1-d
, this forces

k_{0}=n-1-d.

Hence

k=d+k_{0}=d+(n-1-d)=n-1.

Moreover,

\alpha x=\alpha^{k_{0}+1}\frac{\widetilde{u}(\alpha)}{u(\alpha)}\notin T.

Therefore
M(\alpha x)
is nonsingular, and thus

r_{n}(t+1)={\mathrm{rank}}(M(\alpha x))=n=k+1.

This proves that

r_{n}(t+1)\in\{k-1,k+1\}.

Finally, we characterize the local minima of the rank function and count them.

The proof of Theorem
4
.

Let

\alpha^{t}=\alpha^{k_{0}}\frac{\widetilde{u}(\alpha)}{u(\alpha)}

be the canonical representation of
\alpha^{t}
given by Theorem
20
. By Theorem
29
,

r_{n}(t)={\mathrm{rank}}(M(\alpha^{t}))=\deg(u)+|k_{0}|.

We first determine when the rank increases by one to the right. If
k_{0}<n-1-\deg(u)
, then

r_{n}(t+1)=\deg(u)+|k_{0}+1|.

If
k_{0}=n-1-\deg(u)
, then
\alpha^{t+1}\notin T
, and hence
r_{n}(t+1)=n=\deg(u)+|k_{0}|+1
. Therefore,

r_{n}(t+1)=r_{n}(t)+1\iff k_{0}\geq 0.

We next determine when the rank increases by one to the left. If
k_{0}>-(n-1-\deg(u))
, then

r_{n}(t-1)=\deg(u)+|k_{0}-1|.

If
k_{0}=-(n-1-\deg(u))
, then
\alpha^{t-1}\notin T
, and hence

r_{n}(t-1)=n=\deg(u)+|k_{0}|+1.

Therefore,

r_{n}(t-1)=r_{n}(t)+1\iff k_{0}\leq 0.

Thus
t
is a local minimum if and only if

k_{0}=0.

Hence the local minima are in one-to-one correspondence with the polynomials

u(z)\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}[z]
satisfying

u(0)=1,\quad\gcd(u,\widetilde{u})=1,\quad\deg(u)\leq n-1.

Therefore the number of local minima is

\sum_{d=0}^{n-1}A_{d}.

Consider the generating function

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{d=0}^{m-1}A_{d}\right)x^{m-1}.

We have

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{d=0}^{m-1}A_{d}\right)x^{m-1}=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}A_{d}\sum_{m=d+1}^{\infty}x^{m-1}=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}A_{d}\frac{x^{d}}{1-x}=\frac{U(x)}{1-x}.

Since

U(x)=\frac{(1-x)(1-2x^{2})}{(1-2x)(1+x)},

it follows that

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{d=0}^{m-1}A_{d}\right)x^{m-1}=\frac{1-2x^{2}}{(1-2x)(1+x)}=1+\frac{x}{(1-2x)(1+x)}.

Moreover,

\frac{1}{(1-2x)(1+x)}=\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{1-2x}+\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{1+x}.

Therefore

\sum_{d=0}^{n-1}A_{d}=\frac{2^{n-1}-(-1)^{n-1}}{3}.

This completes the proof.

5
Hull distribution of punctured cyclic simplex code

In this section, we determine the hull distribution of linear codes generated by observability matrices associated with m-sequences, as an application of the established results. In particular, we find that nearly half of them are LCD codes.

Let
\{s_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
be the binary
m
-sequence considered throughout the paper, where
s_{t}={\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\lambda\alpha^{t})
. Recall that the corresponding observability matrix is

G_{t}=[v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{t-1}]=\begin{pmatrix}s_{0}&s_{1}&\cdots&s_{t-1}\\
s_{1}&s_{2}&\cdots&s_{t}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
s_{n-1}&s_{n}&\cdots&s_{n+t-2}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n\times t}.

For
n\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1
, let
\mathcal{C}_{t}
be the
[t,n]
linear code generated by
G_{t}
. We point out that the code family
\{\mathcal{C}_{t}\}
is actually a family of punctured cyclic simplex codes. Indeed, the matrix
G_{2^{n}-1}
has entries

(G_{2^{n}-1})_{i,j}={\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\lambda\alpha^{i+j-2}).

On the other hand, the binary cyclic simplex code of length
2^{n}-1
admits the standard trace representation

\left\{\left({\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\beta),{\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\beta\alpha),\dots,{\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\beta\alpha^{2^{n}-2})\right)\mid\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}\right\}.

Hence
\mathcal{C}_{2^{n}-1}
is exactly the binary cyclic simplex code with parameters
[2^{n}-1,n,2^{n-1}]
, and therefore each
\mathcal{C}_{t}
is a punctured code of this binary cyclic simplex code, obtained by retaining the first
t
coordinates.

Definition 32
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a binary linear code. The hull of
\mathcal{C}
is defined as

\textit{Hull}(\mathcal{C}):=\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\perp}.

The code
\mathcal{C}
is called a linear complementary dual (LCD) code if

\textit{Hull}(\mathcal{C})=\{0\},

and it is called a self-orthogonal code if
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
, i.e.,

\textit{Hull}(\mathcal{C})=\mathcal{C}.

Due to their nice applications, LCD codes and self-orthogonal codes have been extensively studied
[
29
,
30
,
33
,
32
]
. The notion of hull is a natural generalization of LCD codes and self-orthogonal codes. It was first introduced by Assmus and Key
[
2
]
, aiming to classify finite projective planes. Linear codes with various hull dimensions are attractive since they have important applications in quantum coding theory
[
32
,
21
]
, and can be used to determine the complexity of some algorithms
[
38
,
39
]
. The dimension of hull can be determined by the rank of Gram matrix, and the following proposition is well-known
[
15
]
.

Proposition 33
.

Let
q
be a prime power, and let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n}
be a linear code with generator matrix
G
. Then,

\dim(\textit{Hull}(\mathcal{C}))=\dim(\mathcal{C})-{\mathrm{rank}}(GG^{\top}).

By Lemma
18
, when
n\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1
, the rank of
G_{t}
equals
n
, then
\{\mathcal{C}_{t}\mid n\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1\}
is a family of
[t,n]
code. The following characterization of LCD codes is direct by Theorem
21
.

Proposition 34
.

Let
\alpha
be a primitive element of
\mathbb{F}_{{2}^{n}}
, and let
s_{t}={\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\lambda\alpha^{t})
be an m-sequence. Let
\mathcal{C}_{t}
be the punctured simplex code generated by the observability matrix
G_{t}
associated with the m-sequence. Let
n\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1
, then
\mathcal{C}_{t}
is LCD if and only if

\alpha^{t}\notin\left\{\frac{p^{*}(\alpha)}{p(\alpha)}\,\middle|\,p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[z]_{<n}\setminus\{0\}\right\}.

Employing the established results, now we determine hull distribution of the family
\{\mathcal{C}_{t}\mid n\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1\}
.

Proposition 35
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{t}
be the simplex code generated by the observability matrix
G_{t}
associated with an m-sequence of order
n
, where
n\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1
. For each integer
h
with
0\leq h\leq n
, we have

\#\{n\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1\mid\dim(\textit{Hull}(\mathcal{C}_{t}))=h\,\}=\begin{cases}2^{n-1}-2,&h=0,\\[2.84526pt]
2^{n-h-1}-1,&1\leq h\leq n-2,\\[2.84526pt]
1,&h=n-1,\\[2.84526pt]
1,&h=n.\end{cases}

Proof.

Write
B_{h}:=\#\{n\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1\mid\dim(\textit{Hull}(\mathcal{C}_{t}))=h\,\}
. For
n\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1
, by Proposition
33
,

\dim(\textit{Hull}(\mathcal{C}_{t}))=n-{\mathrm{rank}}(G_{t}G_{t}^{\top}),

thus

B_{h}=\#\{n\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1\mid{\mathrm{rank}}(G_{t}G_{t}^{\top})=n-h\}.

Recall that in Theorem
1

we have obtained the following rank distribution for the Gram matrices:

\#\{\,t\in\{1,\dots,2^{n}-1\}\mid{\mathrm{rank}}(G_{t}G_{t}^{\top})=k\,\}=\begin{cases}1,&k=0,\\[2.84526pt]
2,&k=1,\\[2.84526pt]
2^{k-1},&2\leq k\leq n-1,\\[2.84526pt]
2^{n-1}-2,&k=n.\end{cases}

(31)

For
1\leq t\leq n-1
, the canonical representation of
\alpha^{t}
is
\alpha^{t}\cdot\widetilde{1}/1
, Theorem
29
gives

{\mathrm{rank}}(G_{t}G_{t}^{\top})={\mathrm{rank}}(M(\alpha^{t}))=\deg(1)+|t|=t.

Subtracting the contributions of
t=1,\dots,n-1
from Eq. (
31
) , we obtain the hull distribution:

B_{h}=\begin{cases}2^{n-1}-2,&h=0,\\[2.84526pt]
2^{n-h-1}-1,&1\leq h\leq n-2,\\[2.84526pt]
1,&h=n-1,\\[2.84526pt]
1,&h=n.\end{cases}

This completes the proof.

In particular, in the family
\{\mathcal{C}_{t}\mid n\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1\}
, there are exactly
2^{n-1}-2
LCD codes, and only one self-orthogonal code, namely the cyclic simplex code
\mathcal{C}_{2^{n}-1}
.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we studied the family of Gram matrices
\{G_{t}G_{t}^{\top}\mid 1\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1\}
arising from binary
m
-sequences. We characterized all singular matrices in this family by a special class of rational functions over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, determined their ranks explicitly, and obtained the exact rank distribution. We also investigated the rank dynamics along the natural cyclic ordering and counted the local minima of the rank function. As an application, we characterized when the associated codes
C_{t}
are LCD and determined the hull distribution in the family
\{C_{t}\mid n\leq t\leq 2^{n}-1\}
.

Some open problems remain. A natural
q
-ary analogue can be defined similarly. Let
\{s_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
be the
q
-ary m-sequence
s_{j}={\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\lambda\alpha^{j})
, where
\alpha
is a primitive element in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}
. Let
G_{t}^{(q)}
be the corresponding observability matrix, where
1\leq t\leq\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}
. Then
G_{\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}}^{(q)}
exactly generates the
\left[\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1},\,n,\,q^{n-1}\right]
simplex code:

\left\{\left({\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\beta),{\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\beta\alpha),\dots,{\mathrm{Tr}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}/\mathbb{F}_{q}}\!\left(\beta\alpha^{\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}-1}\right)\right)\mid\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{n}}\right\}.

Thus
\{G_{t}^{(q)}\mid n\leq t\leq\frac{q^{n}-1}{q-1}\}
gives rise to a punctured family of
q
-ary simplex codes. By Proposition
33
, the hull distribution of this family is completely determined by the rank distribution of the corresponding Gram matrices. Our extensive Magma computations suggest the following open problems.

Open Problem 36
.

Let
q=3
. Determine the rank distribution of the family

\left\{\,G_{t}^{(3)}(G_{t}^{(3)})^{\top}\;\middle|\;1\leq t\leq\frac{3^{n}-1}{2}\right\}

for ternary
m
-sequences. In particular, prove that for every integer
n\geq 2
, one has

\#\left\{\,1\leq t\leq\frac{3^{n}-1}{2}\;\middle|\;{\mathrm{rank}}\!\bigl(G_{t}^{(3)}(G_{t}^{(3)})^{\top}\bigr)=k\right\}=\begin{cases}1,&k=0,\\[4.30554pt]
\dfrac{3^{k}-(-1)^{k}}{2},&1\leq k\leq n-1,\\[8.61108pt]
\dfrac{3^{n}-(-1)^{n}}{4}-1,&k=n.\end{cases}

Open Problem 37
.

Let
q=5
. Prove that for every integer
n\geq 2
, one has

\#\left\{\,1\leq t\leq\frac{5^{n}-1}{4}\;\middle|\;{\mathrm{rank}}\!\bigl(G_{t}^{(5)}(G_{t}^{(5)})^{\top}\bigr)=k\right\}=\begin{cases}0,&k=0,\\[4.30554pt]
\dfrac{5^{k}-(-1)^{k}}{3},&1\leq k\leq n-1,\\[8.61108pt]
\dfrac{5^{n}-(-1)^{n}}{6},&k=n.\end{cases}

It would be of interest to investigate the rank distribution over general finite fields and to determine whether any a common pattern can be found.
