Mild Thrombocytopenia at Admission to Labor and Delivery: Implications for Postpartum Hemorrhage
Authors: Adrian Hernandez Lopez, Rebecca Post, Joanie Chung, Kiran Magee, Brian Crosland, Judith Chung, Richard Benoit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostpartum hemorrhage remains a leading cause of severe maternal morbidity and mortality. The association between mild thrombocytopenia and postpartum blood loss or red blood cell transfusion has not been well studied.ObjectiveTo evaluate whether mild thrombocytopenia at admission to labor and delivery is associated with increased rates of postpartum hemorrhage and packed red blood cell transfusion.Study DesignThe authors performed a retrospective cohort study of pregnancies delivered within a large health maintenance organization from 2008 to 2020. Maternal characteristics and outcomes of those with mild thrombocytopenia were compared to those with typical platelet levels before delivery. The primary outcome was the rate of red blood cell transfusion. Secondary outcomes included quantity of red blood cell transfusion, postpartum hemorrhage, additional procedures performed, and use of uterotonic medications. Statistical analysis was done using χ2 or Mann-Whitney U test. A multivariable logistic regression was also performed.ResultsData from a total of 305,794 pregnancies were included in this study. Mild thrombocytopenia was associated with a small but statistically significant increase in postpartum red blood cell transfusion compared with typical platelet levels (1.2% vs 0.9%, P < .0001). Mild thrombocytopenia was also associated with a higher rate of 2 or more red blood cell transfusions (0.33% vs 0.19%), postpartum hemorrhage (2.4% vs 2.0%), use of uterotonics, and additional procedures performed (all P values < .05). These outcomes all remained statistically significant after adjusting for confounding variables.ConclusionsMild thrombocytopenia prior to delivery is associated with a small increased chance of red blood cell transfusion, postpartum hemorrhage, uterotonic use, and the need for additional procedures postpartum.Keywords: thrombocytopenia, postpartum hemorrhage, blood transfusion, labor and delivery, maternal morbidity and mortality, methylergonovine, misoprostol, carboprost tromethamine

Introduction

Postpartum hemorrhage is an obstetric emergency and remains a leading cause of peripartum morbidity and mortality worldwide. 1 The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) defines immediate postpartum hemorrhage as a total blood loss of 1000 mL or greater or blood loss with signs or symptoms of hypovolemia during the initial 24 hours postpartum. 2 Hemorrhage after delivery requires careful clinical management and may warrant blood product transfusions and surgical procedures to avoid mortality. Even in the absence of mortality, postpartum hemorrhage can lead to extensive morbidity, such as respiratory distress syndrome, hemorrhagic shock with resultant organ failure, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and long-term infertility. 3

Identifying pregnant persons with the potential to experience postpartum hemorrhage is important for preventing adverse outcomes. Established risk factors include a prolonged labor course, chorioamnionitis or intrapartum infection, prior cesarean delivery or uterine surgery, known coagulopathy, history of postpartum hemorrhage, atypical placentation, and genital tract trauma. 2 Knowing which pregnant individuals have the potential to experience postpartum hemorrhage allows for appropriate preparation, including type and crossmatch for blood products, as well as ensuring availability of a hemorrhage care cart. Moderate and severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 100 k/μl or 70 k/μl, respectively) has previously been shown to increase the chance of postpartum hemorrhage. 4,5 Such low platelet levels are often associated with conditions that independently increase the likelihood of postpartum hemorrhage, including preeclampsia with severe features, Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, and Low Platelets (HELLP) syndrome, and immune thrombocytopenia (ITP).

Mild thrombocytopenia (platelet count 100–149 k/μl) is seen in approximately 10% of uncomplicated pregnancies by the time of delivery, 5–8 yet its association with postpartum blood loss remains poorly understood. Previous studies have shown mild thrombocytopenia to be associated with an increased chance of postpartum hemorrhage and uterotonic use, but not with an increased chance of blood transfusion, in both nulliparous term pregnancies undergoing labor 9 and uncomplicated pregnancies undergoing elective cesarean delivery. 10 However, these studies were not adequately powered to detect a difference in blood transfusion rate, which is a clinically relevant indicator of severe maternal morbidity. 11 More recently, Rottenstreich et al reported an increased likelihood of peripartum hemorrhage among patients with mild thrombocytopenia on admission to labor (17.6% vs 14%), although this analysis was limited by a smaller sample size and lack of population diversity. 12 Therefore, the purpose of this study was to further investigate whether mild thrombocytopenia prior to delivery increases the chance of postpartum hemorrhage and red blood cell transfusion, compared with typical platelet counts (≥ 150 k/μl).

Materials and Methods

STUDY DESIGN AND PARTICIPANTS

The authors performed a retrospective cohort study using data abstracted from electronic medical records (EMR) from the Kaiser Permanente Southern California medical system. All pregnant persons at least 18 years in age who delivered within Kaiser Permanente Southern California from January 2008 to December 2020 (N = 484,182) were screened for study eligibility. This time frame was chosen as the EMR data for this period were available at the time at which this study was performed. Inclusion was also predicated on admission platelet counts 100 k/μl and above. The authors limited this study to pregnant individuals with mild thrombocytopenia (platelet count 100–149 k/μl) and those with typical platelet counts (≥ 150 k/μl) and excluded those with moderate or severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 100 k/μl or < 70 k/μl, respectively) as these platelet counts are associated with conditions that have an increased chance of postpartum hemorrhage and need for blood transfusions, such as HELLP syndrome, ITP, and severe preeclampsia. The authors also excluded pregnant persons with no platelet count data available predelivery, current steroid use, and the presence of other conditions associated with postpartum hemorrhage and blood transfusion (eg, atypical placentation, multifetal gestation, stillbirth, preterm delivery, and a predelivery hemoglobin < 10 g/dL).

Kaiser Permanente Southern California is a large health care system that provides services to over 4.7 million members across southern California. Prior research has shown that Kaiser Permanente Southern California membership is reflective of the socioeconomic diversity of the southern California population. 13 For this study, the authors obtained from the EMR pregnant individuals’ specific data on demographics, medical history, intrapartum factors, obstetrical outcomes, laboratory test results, and medication use during hospitalization. Prior studies have validated the accuracy of variables used in this study. 13–15 The Kaiser Permanente Southern California institutional review board approved this study with exemption of informed consent (study #13062).

Demographic characteristics captured include age, self-reported race or ethnicity, body mass index (BMI) at the time of delivery, parity (defined as 0, 1, 2, 3+), history of cesarean delivery or postpartum hemorrhage, and presence of the following comorbidities: chronic hypertension, gestational hypertension or preeclampsia, preexisting diabetes, and asthma. Additionally, predelivery hemoglobin, platelet count, and mode of delivery (spontaneous vaginal, operative vaginal, or cesarean delivery) were abstracted from the medical record. The authors also evaluated rates of use of intravenous magnesium sulfate in labor, suspected intraamniotic infection, and third- and fourth-degree perineal lacerations, as these are known confounders that may impact postpartum hemorrhage rates.

EXPOSURE

The authors defined the exposure as pregnant persons having mild thrombocytopenia with a platelet count of 100–149 k/μl prior to delivery. The comparison group consisted of pregnant persons with typical platelet counts defined as 150 k/μl or greater. Platelet count (k/μl) and hemoglobin (g/dL) data were abstracted by study team members from the delivery hospitalization and from the last complete blood count obtained prior to delivery.

OUTCOMES

The primary outcome was whether packed red blood cells (pRBCs) were transfused, which is a clinically relevant proxy for adverse maternal outcomes. Secondary outcomes included the quantity of pRBC units transfused (categorically defined as 1 unit vs 2 or more units); presence of postpartum hemorrhage (defined as estimated or quantified blood loss ≥ 1000 mL); nonroutine use of uterotonic medications (including methylergonovine, carboprost tromethamine, or misoprostol); use of tranexamic acid; difference in mean change in hemoglobin (comparing pre- and postdelivery) for pregnant persons who did not receive a blood transfusion; hospital length of stay; rate of intensive care unit (ICU) admission; rate of additional procedures used to mitigate blood loss (ie, peripartum hysterectomy, dilatation and curettage, or uterine artery embolization); and rate of maternal death. Quantitative blood loss was measured using under-buttock drape collection and weighing of surgical sponges and towels.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

This study was powered to detect a red blood cell transfusion rate of 3% in the mild thrombocytopenia group compared with 2% in the typical platelet count group, as informed by a prior study using California Maternal Quality Care Collaborative risk groups. 16 Using α = .05 and β = .80, the authors estimated needing 3825 pregnant persons per study arm (7650 total pregnant persons) for this study.

Categorical variables were described using counts and proportions. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations. Comparison of the distributions of demographic and clinical variables was performed using χ2 tests for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to adjust for potential confounders. All analyses were 2-sided and performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc). P values < .05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Of 484,182 eligible pregnant persons, 305,794 met inclusion criteria for the analyses. Of those included, 34,615 pregnant persons (11.3%) had mild thrombocytopenia, and 271,179 (88.6%) had typical platelet counts (Figure).

Table 1 summarizes the demographics of the 2 study groups. Compared with pregnant persons with typical platelet levels, those with mild thrombocytopenia were slightly older, had a lower BMI, and had a slightly higher representation of Black and White pregnant persons and a slightly lower representation of Hispanic and Asian American pregnant persons. The mean predelivery platelet count for mild thrombocytopenia and typical platelet count groups were 132 k/μl and 220 k/μl, respectively. Rates of prior cesarean delivery, prior postpartum hemorrhage, and select comorbidities (preeclampsia, gestational hypertension, chronic hypertension, and asthma) differed significantly between groups, although the absolute differences were small. Pregnant persons with mild thrombocytopenia had slightly higher use of intravenous magnesium sulfate use (1.4% vs 0.8%; P < .0001) and slightly higher rates of third- or fourth-degree lacerations (2.4% vs 2.0%; P < .0001). Overall, baseline characteristics were relatively comparable between those with mild thrombocytopenia and those with typical platelet levels.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to adjust for potential confounders such as maternal age, race or ethnicity, BMI, parity, preeclampsia or gestational hypertension, chronic hypertension, asthma, hemoglobin, intravenous magnesium sulfate use, and third- or fourth-degree perineal laceration (Table 3). After analysis, pregnant individuals with mild thrombocytopenia were more likely to require a red blood cell transfusion (adjusted odds ratio (aOR), 1.50; 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.33–1.71; P < .001), to require 2 or more units of blood (aOR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.03–1.80), and more likely to be diagnosed with postpartum hemorrhage (aOR, 1.34; 95% CI, 1.23–1.47), although the overall risks were low. The association between mild thrombocytopenia and the use of uterotonics, as well as the use of additional interventions, also remained statistically significant after controlling for baseline characteristics (Table 3).

There was no difference in mode of delivery, hospital length of stay, or rates of ICU admission between pregnant persons with mild thrombocytopenia and those with typical platelet levels. The authors did not find a difference in the change in postdelivery hemoglobin between the study groups when excluding pregnant persons who received a blood transfusion (mean change of −1.9 g/dL for both groups, P = .0287). Two pregnant individuals with mild thrombocytopenia died during this study period (1 developed eclampsia with subsequent intracranial hemorrhage, and 1 developed disseminated intravascular coagulation from suspected amniotic fluid embolism), compared with 1 pregnant person with a typical platelet value (declined all blood products and experienced anoxic brain injury).

Discussion

PRINCIPAL FINDINGS

Among term pregnant persons admitted for delivery, mild thrombocytopenia (platelet count 100–149 k/μl) was independently associated with an increased chance of receiving a red blood cell transfusion in the immediate postpartum period. Mild thrombocytopenia was also associated with a higher chance of needing 2 or more red blood cell units compared with pregnant individuals with typical platelet counts when controlling for confounding variables, although the absolute risk was low. Additionally, mild thrombocytopenia was also associated with a higher rate of postpartum hemorrhage, uterotonic use, and tranexamic acid administration. The authors also found pregnant persons with mild thrombocytopenia had a higher chance of needing additional procedures in the postpartum period, including dilation and curettage, uterine artery embolization (UAE), and peripartum hysterectomy, although the absolute risk was < 1% in both study arms.

RESULTS IN THE CONTEXT OF WHAT IS KNOWN

This study’s findings are consistent with prior studies showing a small but significant association between mild thrombocytopenia and postpartum hemorrhage. Govindappagari et al retrospectively evaluated a group of 2845 nulliparous, term, singleton, vertex pregnancies undergoing labor and delivery at a single center over a 1-year period. 9 They found that pregnant persons with mild thrombocytopenia had a 2-fold greater chance of having blood loss > 1000 mL compared with pregnant individuals with typical platelet levels. However, they found no difference in the rate of blood transfusion, and their study may not have been adequately powered to detect a difference. Carlson et al performed a secondary analysis of a prospective cohort of pregnant persons delivered by cesarean or vaginal birth after a cesarean, and among 54,597 pregnant individuals included, they found that thrombocytopenia was associated with a higher incidence of both clinical (aOR, 1.68; 95% CI, 1.52–1.83) and laboratory (aOR, 1.60; 95% CI, 1.38–1.86) evidence of hemorrhage. 5 The elevated risk the authors of this study observed is more in line with Carlson et al and may differ from Govindappagari et al due to the fact that they evaluated a smaller and more homogeneous cohort when compared with this study’s larger and more diverse study cohort.

Conversely, DiSciullo et al did not find an association between mild thrombocytopenia and postpartum hemorrhage, red blood cell transfusion, wound complications, or postpartum emergency department visits in pregnant individuals undergoing cesarean delivery. 17 However, they had a comparably smaller study cohort of 3133 pregnant persons, focused only on cesarean deliveries, and had data from a single hospital.

The recent study by Rottenstreich et al reported on a smaller, more homogenous population, and also showed an increased chance of peripartum hemorrhage among primiparous women with mild gestational thrombocytopenia on admission to labor. 12 The authors’ study focused on mild thrombocytopenia prior to delivery and reports the largest number of study patients evaluated retrospectively.

Clinical Implications

The authors’ finding of an association between mild thrombocytopenia and red blood cell transfusion is novel, as prior studies were not adequately powered to find this association. 9,17 Red blood cell transfusion is a clinically relevant outcome that can be a marker for increased maternal morbidity and mortality. 11 Red blood cell transfusion, and transfusions in general, have inherent risks, including acute transfusion reaction, hemolytic transfusion reaction, transfusion-related acute lung injury, and transfusion-associated circulatory overload. 18

Although the platelet count threshold for increased chance of postpartum hemorrhage is unclear, ACOG and California Maternal Quality Care Collaborative toolkits use platelet counts < 70 k/μl and 100 k/μl, respectively, to designate pregnant individuals as high risk for postpartum hemorrhage. 19,20 Below these thresholds, the recommendations are to type and crossmatch pregnant individuals, alert the care team of hemorrhage risk, and mobilize resources (such as a hemorrhage cart) in preparation. 19–21 The authors’ study suggests that mild thrombocytopenia (100–149 k/μl) may also be a risk factor for postpartum hemorrhage, and with further study validation, it may be appropriate to add mild thrombocytopenia to these toolkits.

In the authors’ cohort of 305,794 pregnant persons, they found mild thrombocytopenia had an incidence of 11%, which is consistent with prior studies. 5–8 The authors’ postpartum hemorrhage rate was 2%, which is comparable to the current literature. 22,23 Although the authors did not find a decrease in postdelivery hemoglobin between the study groups, this analysis was done after excluding pregnant individuals who had undergone a red blood cell transfusion, as receiving a blood transfusion would have affected hemoglobin levels. The mode of delivery, hospital length of stay, and rates of ICU admission were comparable between the groups. The authors did note a higher rate of intrapartum magnesium use in the mild thrombocytopenia cohort (1.4% vs 0.8%), which could have been related to the higher incidence of preeclampsia (10% vs 8.3%) in this group, although the authors did not specifically look for the incidence of preeclampsia with severe features. The rate of maternal death between the 2 groups was exceedingly rare (2 with mild thrombocytopenia vs 1 with typical platelet levels) and due to rare complications, thus limiting the interpretation of these outcomes.

RESEARCH IMPLICATIONS

The authors’ research suggests that mild thrombocytopenia prior to delivery is associated with increased chance of postpartum hemorrhage. However, more research is needed to evaluate the clinical significance of this risk, particularly as the authors noted only a small absolute increased risk in the primary and secondary outcomes. Additionally, there is currently limited knowledge on platelet count and hemorrhage risk in obstetrics, thus identifying an area for future research. Furthermore, the authors’ understanding of the pathophysiology linking mild thrombocytopenia and postpartum hemorrhage is limited.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

There are several strengths to this study, including the large sample size of 305,794 pregnant individuals, which allowed sufficient power to detect a significant difference in the rate of red blood cell transfusions, a relatively rare but clinically significant medical intervention. This study also had a socioeconomically and racially diverse pregnant population from a multicenter catchment area. This supports the generalizability of these findings to a large, diverse proportion of the pregnant population in southern California, and potentially to other parts of the United States. A methodological strength of this study was adjusting for several potential confounding variables to assess the independent effect of mild thrombocytopenia on the primary and secondary outcomes. Because International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes do not always reliably capture rates or severity of postpartum hemorrhage, the authors utilized documented blood loss in their study to define postpartum hemorrhage. Also, by excluding pregnant persons with moderate or severe thrombocytopenia, the authors are more likely to exclude other major medical conditions that can cause thrombocytopenia, such as preeclampsia with severe features, HELLP syndrome, and ITP. However, the authors recognize that there remains a chance that these conditions are still present despite excluding persons with moderate or severe thrombocytopenia. Interestingly, the authors noted a higher rate of procedures with mild thrombocytopenia, including dilation and curettage, UAE, and hysterectomy (Table 3), all of which are major interventions for obstetric hemorrhage emergencies.

A limitation of the present study is the retrospective approach, with an inability to control for all potential confounding variables. However, given the low rates of gestational thrombocytopenia 5–8 and postpartum hemorrhage, 2,23 and an even more rare occurrence of red blood cell transfusion, 16,23 the study methodology used was a feasible way to investigate the study’s question. Because pregnant persons with mild thrombocytopenia received uterotonics at a higher rate, this may have limited overall blood loss, thus obviating the need for a red blood cell transfusion.

Conclusion

Among term, singleton pregnancies, mild thrombocytopenia prior to delivery was independently associated with a higher likelihood of needing a pRBC transfusion compared with typical platelet levels. Mild thrombocytopenia was also associated with a small statistically significant increased rate of postpartum hemorrhage, transfusion of 2 or more pRBCs, use of any uterotonics as well as tranexamic acid, and additional procedures performed, including peripartum hysterectomy, dilation and curettage, or UAE. Although the absolute increased risk was small, the inclusion of mild thrombocytopenia prior to delivery as a risk factor for postpartum hemorrhage is advised for obstetric care safety guidelines and team preparation.
