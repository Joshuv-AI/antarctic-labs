AAV vector production in suspension cells using PEI transfection and sodium butyrate with orthogonal assessment of function and quality
Authors: Sujan Ravendran, Simon Fammé, Maya Graham Noer, Thomas Wisbech Skov, Nanna Steengaard Mikkelsen, Jessica Lee Schneller, Sofie Rahbek Dorset, Jonas Holst Wolff, Anaïs Marie Julie Møller, Didde Haslund, Anne Louise S Revenfeld, Mette Holm, Trine H Mogensen, Bjarne Kuno Møller, Jacob Giehm Mikkelsen, Rasmus O Bak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors are widely used in gene therapy, yet academic in-house production remains dominated by labor-intensive adherent cell workflows with limited scalability. Here, we describe an AAV vector production platform using suspension cells in orbital shaking Erlenmeyer flasks, based on polyethyleneimine (PEI) transfection and sodium butyrate supplementation. Following systematic evaluation of transfection conditions, this approach yields vectors with performance comparable to a commercial production kit. Vector quality was interrogated using orthogonal methodologies, including two-dimensional ddPCR, mass photometry, and nanopore sequencing, enabling comparative assessment of genome packaging, capsid composition, and vector heterogeneity. Functional validation was performed by in vitro transduction of K562 cells and primary human CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, as well as in vivo gene delivery to mouse liver and heart. Across assays, vectors produced using this protocol demonstrated comparable genome integrity and transgene expression. Comparative purification analysis revealed that iodixanol density gradient purification resulted in higher proportions of full capsids and reduced producer-cell-derived impurities relative to PEG 8000 precipitation. Together, this work establishes a scalable suspension-based AAV production workflow and demonstrates the value of orthogonal quality assessment combined with functional validation for robust vector benchmarking.Keywords: AAV6, AAV9, AAV production, sodium butyrate, suspension cells, adeno-associated virus, Erlenmeyer, scalable, PEI, ddPCR, mass photometry, nanopore

Introduction

Advances in gene therapy have enabled cures for diseases that were previously untreatable. Conventional gene therapy involves the delivery of genetic material to the diseased cells via viral or non-viral vectors.1 Currently, viral vectors constitute the most efficient way of introducing genetic material into cells (the process known as transduction), and most approved gene and cell therapies are based on gene delivery using retroviral or adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors.2,3,4 Particularly for in vivo gene therapies, AAV vectors have favorable features such as low immunogenicity, broad tissue tropism, and high episomal stability that enable persistent transgene expression in non-dividing cells.4,5 These traits have led to several clinically approved gene therapies across various therapeutic areas such as metabolic, ophthalmological, neurological, and hematological diseases.6 These include Glybera for familial lipoprotein lipase deficiency, Luxturna targeting RPE65-associated inherited retinal diseases, Zolgensma for spinal muscular atrophy, Upstaza for aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase deficiency, Roctavian for hemophilia A, Beqvez and Hemgenix for hemophilia B, Elevidys for Duchenne muscular dystrophy, and recently Otarmeni for sensorineural hearing loss caused by otoferlin deficiency.7,8,9,10,11,12 In addition to in vivo gene delivery, AAV vectors have also found use in delivery of repair templates for CRISPR/Cas9-based gene insertion by homology-directed repair (HDR), which has so far been tested clinically for sickle cell disease and CAR-T cell therapy.13,14,15

The most widely employed method for producing AAV vectors is transient transfection of producer cells such as adherent human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293).16,17 AAV production requires the transfection of producer cells with a plasmid containing the vector genome as well as a plasmid encoding the viral proteins Rep and Cap, which are essential for genome replication and viral particle assembly. The natural replication process of AAV requires the presence of a helper virus such as adenovirus, herpesvirus, or vaccinia virus, which provides essential functions that activate AAV gene expression and genome replication.18 Hence, a helper-virus-free AAV system relies on transient delivery of a third plasmid carrying adenoviral helper genes (e.g., E2A, E4, and VA RNA). AAV rep/cap genes and adenoviral helper genes can also be encoded on a single plasmid. Plasmid delivery to packaging cells is often achieved by inexpensive transfection reagents such as polyethyleneimine (PEI) or calcium phosphate [Ca3(PO4)2].17,19,20

Despite the advantages of AAV vectors, their manufacturing process remains complex and presents several challenges that can be broadly divided into upstream and downstream processes.21 The upstream process includes plasmid development, producer cell expansion, transfection, and vector synthesis.21 The downstream process involves vector harvesting, purification, and quality control.21 Thorough optimization of upstream and downstream processes is crucial for clinical application, especially as required doses can reach 3.5E14 vector genomes (vg) per kilogram for systemic delivery.6,22,23,24

Beyond the need for large-scale manufacturing, another critical parameter for AAV vectors is the ratio of bioactive particles carrying full-length vector genomes to non-functional particles, which may be empty or contain truncated, non-functional genomes.25 This ratio directly influences therapeutic potency, immunogenicity, and manufacturing efficiency and may be associated with variable clinical outcomes and immune responses in both clinical trials and approved gene therapies. A notable example involves recent events related to Sarepta’s AAVrh74-based gene therapies (Elevidys and SRP-9004), where three fatal cases of acute liver failure led to clinical holds and revocation of platform designation. These developments underscore the real-world consequences of capsid load and vector purity, highlighting the need for rigorous quality control in AAV manufacturing and proactive risk mitigation in gene therapy development.

Traditionally, adherent cells have been used for clinical-scale AAV manufacturing with one example using more than 400 10-stack culture flasks to generate the required vector amount for treating 100 patients in a hemophilia B clinical trial at a maximum dose of 2E11 vg/kg, i.e., three orders of magnitude lower than used in other trials.26 However, biotechnological advancements have enabled a transition to more scalable production in suspension cell lines,27,28 a method particularly embraced by the private sector. Without the need for large surface areas for cell attachment, these suspension cultures are space-saving, particularly when high cell densities can be employed. In addition, AAV vectors are not typically released from the producer cells, which requires laborious handling to detach cells by scraping or trypsinization prior to cell lysis to extract the AAV vector particles.

Since the discovery of AAV in 1966 and its subsequent vectorization for introducing foreign DNA to mammalian cells in the 1980s, the use of AAV vectors has steadily increased (Figure 1A).29,30,31 To gain insight into current AAV production practices, we conducted a literature screening of research articles published between June 13 and July 1, 2024, that employed AAV vectors. In total, 217 articles using AAV vectors were screened. Of these, 165 were excluded based on criteria such as insufficient methodological detail or the use of externally sourced AAV vectors from commercial vendors or academic core facilities for which the production method was not disclosed. Interestingly, more than 30% of these excluded studies used AAV vectors that were procured from external entities (private vendors or core facilities), reflecting the complex nature of setting up in-house AAV vector production. This left 52 articles from which partial or complete information on the AAV production system could be extracted (detailed descriptions of inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in Table S1). Of the studies producing AAV vectors in-house, 87% used adherent cell culture systems (Figure 1B). HEK293 producer cells were primarily used (74%), with PEI-based transfection being the preferred plasmid delivery method (68%) (Figures 1C and 1D). Downstream processing frequently involved vector purification using iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation (46%) or cesium chloride (CsCl) density gradients (41%) to achieve higher preparation purity. Alternatively, polyethylene glycol 8000 (PEG 8000) precipitation was utilized (21%), either solely for crude vector enrichment or combined with subsequent purification steps (Figure 1E; cumulative percentages exceed 100% as methods were not mutually exclusive). A significant proportion of the analyzed studies (23%) also incorporated supplementary affinity-based purification; however, substantial protocol heterogeneity across systems precluded the classification of these methodologies into distinct groups (see Table S1 for a detailed methodological description). This suggests that the adoption of suspension cell systems in academic settings remains low, potentially due to a lack of need for large-scale production, economic constraints, or limited access to the required equipment and infrastructure.

Commercial AAV production kits are available and claim to ease workloads, increase production efficiency, and provide easy-to-use reagents and straightforward protocols. These kits can include producer cells, culture medium, transfection reagents, production enhancers, and extraction and purification kits. However, their widespread application can be hindered by high costs, particularly for smaller academic laboratories with limited resources and the need to produce large AAV quantities, for example, for in vivo experiments. Furthermore, general increases in laboratory running costs often force researchers to adopt more cost-effective strategies.32 Therefore, an unmet need exists for developing more affordable approaches for large-scale AAV vector production, as well as for investigating potential advantages among different AAV production methods.

Here, we investigate cost-effective parameters for AAV production in suspension cells to ease the transition to larger-scale manufacturing. By serial optimizations, we develop a protocol that is economical and easily implementable for most laboratories with conventional equipment and reagents. We validate the structural integrity and functional attributes of the produced AAV particles using dual and triple transfection, and we demonstrate in vivo functionality by gene delivery to mouse liver and heart. We believe that our findings will facilitate and accelerate the transition to suspension AAV production and that they may be broadly applicable to a wide variety of viral vector production pipelines.

Results

Transitioning to suspension-cell-based AAV vector production

To establish a straightforward and cost-effective suspension cell system for AAV production, we used Erlenmeyer flasks and an affordable rocking platform to be placed inside a standard CO2 cell culture incubator. This setup was combined with single-use, vented Erlenmeyer flasks specifically designed for the suspension culture of human cells. The platform can be fitted with interchangeable adapters to accommodate up to two 2.8L Erlenmeyer flasks, each supporting cell culture volumes of up to 1,000 mL. We acquired a commercially available suspension HEK293F-derived cell line, Viral Production Cells 2.0 (VPCs), stated by the vendor to be specifically suitable for AAV production. Typical of many HEK293-derived suspension cell lines, these cells are optimized for high-density culturing, with a maintenance cell density of 0.3 × 106 to 0.6 × 106 cells/mL and an increase to 3 × 106 cells/mL upon plasmid transfection and vector production. To illustrate the space-saving advantage of such suspension culture over adherent systems, a single 1,000 mL suspension culture yields approximately 3E9 cells at the time of transfection. In contrast, achieving a similar cell number with adherent HEK293 cells would require around 300 15-cm cell culture dishes (total surface area ∼43,500 cm2) or roughly two CellSTACK 40-layer culture chambers (total surface area ∼50,880 cm2).

Transfection reagents in suspension HEK293 cells

The acquired suspension cell line is part of a commercial AAV vector production kit that includes specialized reagents for the workflow leading up to vector purification. In addition to the cell line, this includes a cell culture medium optimized for high-density growth and a transfection kit containing reagents tailored for suspension cells, including a transfection reagent, a transfection booster, and an AAV vector production enhancer. However, as this transfection kit is costly, we set out to investigate if other generic and cheaper reagents could substitute for these. We cultured the cells in the commercially available medium and compared the performance of the commercial transfection kit (AAV-MAX Transfection Kit, hereafter referred to as commercial kit) directly with the commonly used, low-cost alternatives calcium phosphate [Ca3(PO4)2] and polyethyleneimine (PEI), which are readily available from chemical suppliers. We also included PEI MAX, a proprietary linear PEI-based transfection reagent developed and supplied by a single vendor and reported to be more efficient than generic 25 kDa PEI.33,34 We transfected cells with a dual-plasmid AAV6 system: pDGM6 carrying the AAV2 rep and AAV6 cap genes, and adenoviral helper genes along a conventional single-stranded AAV (ssAAV) transfer plasmid containing an expression cassette with GFP expressed from the strong constitutive SFFV promoter (Figures 2A and 2B). The commercial kit was used according to the vendor’s instructions, whereas the protocols for the other three reagents were adapted from previously developed protocols for adherent HEK293 cells as detailed in the Material and Methods section.

Following transfection, AAV production cells were analyzed by flow cytometry at four different time points (24, 48, 72, and 96 h) to investigate the impact on cell viability, cell proliferation (cell count using counting beads), and transfection efficiencies and expression kinetics (GFP fluorescence) (Figures 2C and S1A). For the commercial kit, PEI, and PEI MAX, cell viabilities remained high (>80%). In contrast, Ca3(PO4)2 transfection resulted in excessive toxicity, with viability dropping to ∼6% and no subsequent recovery. This condition was therefore excluded from further studies (Figure 2D). Cell proliferation slightly increased across all conditions up to 48 h post-transfection. Subsequently, the non-transfected-control (NTC) resumed expansion, whereas the groups transfected with PEI, PEI MAX, and the commercial kit exhibited a decline in proliferation (Figure 2E). The commercial kit yielded markedly higher transfection efficiency, reaching 99% GFP+ cells at 96 h post-transfection, compared to 43% and 64% for PEI and PEI MAX, respectively (Figure 2F). Accordingly, the commercial kit also resulted in significantly higher GFP expression levels, as measured by mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the GFP+ population (Figure 2G). PEI MAX was slightly superior to PEI, which may be attributable to its higher degree of deacylation, as previously reported.33 A distinct and sizable subpopulation of high-GFP-expressing cells was observed in cultures transfected with the commercial kit (40.4% of cells), compared to just 3.1% and 5.6% for PEI and PEI MAX, respectively (Figures S1B and S2A). The slight reductions in MFI and viability across all three conditions between 72 and 96 h post-transfection suggest that 72 h is the optimal AAV harvest time point. This is in line with most published AAV production protocols and is also recommended by the commercial supplier of the AAV transfection kit.

To further optimize PEI MAX-based transfection, we investigated the effect of varying the DNA:PEI MAX ratio. Titration from 1:10 to 1:1 revealed, in line with previous articles, that a 1:3 ratio yielded the highest transfection efficiency (51% GFP+ cells) without compromising cell viability (Figures 2H, S2B, and S2C).35,36

Sodium butyrate increases transfection and transgene expression in producer cells

Several small molecules have been identified that improve viral vector production.37 Among these, sodium butyrate (NaBu), a low-cost histone deacetylase inhibitor (HDACi), has been shown to improve production of both lentiviral and AAV vectors in adherent cell cultures, where it increases transfection efficiency and vector yield.24,37,38 NaBu functions by inhibiting histone deacetylases, thereby promoting a more open chromatin structure that enhances DNA accessibility to the transcriptional machinery.39 This mechanism may also be relevant to exogenous plasmids, as the application of NaBu has been shown to both enhance plasmid transcription and increase the uptake of foreign DNA.40 To investigate the impact of NaBu supplementation at the time of transfection and for the duration of AAV production, we harvested producer cells for flow cytometry analysis 72 h after transfection with PEI MAX with and without NaBu (PEI MAX + NaBu or PEI MAX). NaBu supplementation led to a significant increase in transfection efficiency, with 89% GFP+ cells compared to 56% without NaBu (Figure 3A). A small, but non-significant, difference in transfection efficiency was still observed between the commercial kit and PEI MAX + NaBu (Figure 3A). Notably, a marked fluorescence intensity shift in the GFP+ population was observed with NaBu supplementation, leading to a 4.5-fold increase in MFI of the GFP+ population (Figures 3B and 3C). However, the inclusion of NaBu also led to significant decrease in cell viability from 86% viable cells without NaBu to 56% with NaBu (Figure 3D). In summary, supplementing the medium with NaBu significantly enhances transfection efficiency and transgene expression. However, the commercial kit outperformed both in terms of transfection efficiency and cell viability.

Comparison of AAV6 vector preparations produced with different transfection reagents

To investigate the impact of the transfection method in viral yield and purity at a 30 mL production scale, we used two conventional purification methods, namely AAV precipitation using PEG 8000 or iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation (Figure S3). For both methods, AAV6 vectors were harvested only from cells and not the culture medium. The total number of vector genomes in the purified particles was determined based on a previously published protocol employing a two-dimensional droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) assay to detect the ITR and the bGH polyA simultaneously.41 While qPCR remains a widely used method for titer determination, its reliance on standard curves and sensitivity to parameters that affect PCR efficiency can lead to higher analytical variability and overestimation of viral titers.42,43 Two-dimensional ddPCR offers superior precision and absolute quantification capabilities but may report lower viral titers than traditional methods. The two-dimensional nature of the employed ddPCR enables a crude discrimination of truncated vector genomes that have lost either both ITRs or the internal bGH polyA sequence and thus provides a better estimate of the number of intact vector genomes (Figure S4A). The absolute AAV yields following purification by iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation were 1.38E9 vg, 1.99E10 vg, and 1.42E10 vg for the PEI MAX, PEI MAX + NaBu, and the commercial kit, respectively (Figures 4A and S4B). A similar trend was observed following PEG 8000 precipitation where the absolute yields were 1.16E10 vg, 1.509E11 vg, and 1.47E11 vg for PEI MAX, PEI MAX + NaBu, and the commercial kit, respectively (Figures 4A and S4B). Hence, consistent with the transfection analyses, the inclusion of NaBu resulted in increased AAV vector yields compared to PEI MAX alone. Purification with iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation resulted in significantly lower vector yields compared to PEG-8000-mediated precipitation (Figure 4A). Notably, comparable vector yields were observed between the commercial kit and PEI MAX + NaBu. Interestingly, the integrity of the vector genomes as measured by the two-dimensional ddPCR assay was highly compromised in the transfection condition without NaBu, which was apparent for both iodixanol and PEG 8000 purification (Figure 4B). In parallel with the extraction and harvest of AAV vectors from transfected producer cells, we also applied all the same processes to NTCs, which confirmed the absence of any contamination during the process (Figure S4B). Furthermore, these AAV-deficient cell extracts were used to assess potential toxicity coming from PEG 8000 or iodixanol without the influence of AAV particles. The results showed that PEG 8000 purification was associated with higher toxicity than iodixanol when applied to the K562 lymphoblast cell line or primary human CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) (Figures S5A and S5B). However, high vector titers could circumvent the need for using large volumes, thereby mitigating non-AAV-related toxicities.

Vector production discrepancy between adherent and suspension production system

The adherent AAV production platform requires extensive manual handling and results in considerable use of space, reagents, plastic, and labor hours in comparison to the customized AAV production protocol in suspension cells. The inefficiencies associated with adherent cultures due to the reliance on surface attachment limit scalability and contribute to an increased resource consumption.

We next directly compared production efficiencies between adherent and suspension cell platforms (Figure S6). To enable a comparable analysis, we used 9E7 total cells in the adherent production, corresponding to the cell numbers of a 30 mL suspension culture. We adopted a previously published protocol for AAV production in adherent cells with minor modifications.44 Transfection conditions for the adherent cell culture method included both PEI and PEI MAX, with the culture medium supplemented NaBu. For the suspension cell culture, we again included the commercial kit and PEI MAX + NaBu. Out of the four different transfection methods, the commercial kit used in suspension cell culture showed the best transfection efficiency, reaching a total of 98% GFP+ producer cells, followed by PEI-mediated transfection of adherent HEK293 cells (Figure 5A). Interestingly, in adherent cells, PEI + NaBu was superior to PEI MAX + NaBu measured by the fraction of the GFP+ population and the MFI of the GFP+ population (Figures 5A and 5B). GFP MFIs were also higher in adherent cells than in suspension cells (Figure 5B). Viability was highly compromised in adherent cells compared to the suspension cells, which possibly reflects viability loss during the cellular detachment process, as this was also seen in the NTC (Figure 5C). No significant difference in viability was observed between PEI- and PEI MAX-mediated transfection in adherent cells. As previously observed, the PEI MAX + NaBu transfection was associated with higher toxicity than the commercial kit (Figure 5B).

Consistent with our previous findings, we did not observe any significant difference in the total AAV vector yield between the two suspension culture methods after PEG 8000 purification (Figure 5D). The commercial kit showed a non-significant higher yield (1.6E11 vg) compared to the adherent cell cultures, both to PEI MAX + NaBu (1.2E11 vg) and PEI + NaBu (7.7E10 vg) (Figure 5E). The integrity of the vector genomes, as determined by the double-positive droplet method using ddPCR, was similar across the preparations. Values ranged from 63.6% for the PEI production in adherent cells to a maximum of 78.6% detected for the commercial kit in the suspension cells (Figure 5E). The AAV vector transduction efficiencies and related toxicities were tested in K562 cells at an MOI of 20,000 and did not show any significant differences between the AAV vector preps, indicating fully functional AAV particles independent from the production method (Figures 5F and 5G).

Inclusion of NaBu in AAV9 vector production

We hypothesized that the inclusion of NaBu would have a positive effect on vector production of other serotypes. We therefore switched the serotype from AAV6 to AAV9, which was produced by triple plasmid transfection. At the same time, we also investigated increased production scales from 30 mL to 60 mL and 120 mL cell cultures, providing sufficient vector amounts for conducting in vivo experiments. It has been reported that AAV9 production typically results in titers approximately one order of magnitude higher than those obtained with AAV6.28,45,46 This observation is consistent with historical data from our laboratory (data not shown). Here, we used an AAV transfer plasmid encoding eGFP and nanoluciferase expressed from the human ubiquitin C (UBC) promoter. We observed a decrease in transfection efficiency when using PEI MAX + NaBu compared to the commercial kit, and there was also a slight reduction when scaling from 60 to 120 mL (Figure S5E). However, the MFI of the GFP+ populations was comparable across conditions (Figure S5F).

Next, AAV vectors were purified from the 120 mL productions by iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation or PEG 8000 precipitation, and yields were determined using the two-dimensional ddPCR assay. For iodixanol-purified vectors, the commercial kit led to a 2-fold higher vector yield than PEI MAX + NaBu (1.5E13 vg vs. 7.4E12 vg) and for PEG-8000-precipitated vectors a 1.3-fold higher yield was observed for the commercial kit (1.7E13 vg vs. 1.3E13 vg) (Figure 6A). The vector purities assessed by the frequencies of ddPCR droplets positive for both ITR and bGH polyA were 52% and 47.4% for the commercial kit and PEI MAX + NaBu PEG-8000-purified preparations, respectively (Figure S6G). As expected, iodixanol-purified vectors displayed higher purities as determined by the ddPCR assay, reaching >88% with no difference between transfection method (Figure 6B). Despite the difference in production yields, the vectors showed comparable transduction efficacy when tested at four different MOIs on HEK293T cells (Figure 6C). To summarize, a reduction in AAV9 yield was observed for PEI MAX + NaBu compared to the commercial kit, but with no detectable difference in vector purity or functionality.

In vivo comparison of AAV9 vectors

To compare the in vivo efficacy of the produced AAV9 vectors, we targeted the mouse liver and heart, since AAV9 exhibits strong tropism for these tissues following tail vein injection.47,48 Mice were injected with the iodixanol-gradient-purified AAV9 vectors produced either by PEI MAX + NaBu or by the commercial kit (nine mice per group); the mice were analyzed by in vivo bioluminescence imaging at 1 and 4 weeks post-injection (Figure 6D). All treated mice showed strong bioluminescence from the upper compartment of the abdomen (Figure 6E). At both time points, the total bioluminescence intensity was comparable between the two groups (Figures 6F and 6G). To further investigate the biodistribution, mice were euthanized immediately after the last imaging, and livers and hearts were extracted (Figure 6H). These tissues were analyzed for GFP expression using widefield fluorescence microscopy and luciferase expression by in vitro luciferase assay of homogenized tissue lysates. Both analyses showed high gene expression in the two tissues with no significant differences between groups (Figures 6I and 6J). In conclusion, AAV9 vectors from the two production methods showed no functional differences in transducing tissues in vivo.

Comparative characterization of AAV vectors through mass photometry and nanopore sequencing

AAV vector heterogeneity and impurities that occur during AAV production have been recognized as major hurdles to achieve successful clinical translation and regulatory approval, as they can impact both the efficacy and safety profile of the final gene therapy product. To characterize these features, we employed mass photometry and long-read nanopore sequencing to analyze the vector preparations in detail.

Mass photometry is a precise and rapid method for quantifying AAV subpopulations and verifying genomic cargo by measuring the mass of individual particles in solution through light scattering, while requiring only minimal sample volumes.49 We detected a total of 1,700 to 7,659 events from the AAV vector preparations with the lowest number observed in preparations produced using the commercial kit and purified by iodixanol gradient and the highest in those purified using PEG 8000 (Figures S7 and S8A). For iodixanol-gradient-purified AAV9, our analysis showed that approximately 28.9% of all events corresponded to AAV particles for the commercial kit preparations compared to 12.2% for the PEI MAX + NaBu preparations. In contrast, for AAV9 vectors purified by PEG 8000 precipitation, the fraction corresponding to AAV particles constituted only about 0.3%–2.2% of all events (Figure S8B). When analyzing the ratio of full to empty particles, the commercial kit production method and the PEI MAX + NaBu method showed equal ratios, with approximately 30% full particles (Figure 7A). The measured median masses of the empty and full AAV particles were consistent across production methods (Figure S8C).

To investigate the vector genome integrity, we analyzed the AAV vectors prepared by iodixanol gradient purification and PEG 8000 precipitation using long-read nanopore sequencing. Reads were mapped to the human genome (hg38), the transgene plasmid, and the packaging plasmids (pAdDeltaF6 and pAAV2.9) (Figures 7B–7E). More than 90% of reads from PEG 8000-precipitated AAV mapped to the provided sequences. In contrast, the lowest mapping rate was observed for the commercial kit (iodixanol purification), where only 75% of reads aligned to the provided sequences (Figure S8D). The iodixanol-gradient-purified samples yielded the highest percentage of mapping to the transgene plasmid, specifically 74.2% for the commercial kit and 72.51% for the PEI MAX + NaBu preparations (Figure S8E). Conversely, the PEG-8000-precipitated vectors mapped predominantly to hg38 at 69.0% and 89.7% for the commercial kit and the PEI MAX + NaBu production methods, respectively (Figure S8F). Furthermore, contamination with packaging plasmids was observed at levels up to 3% (Figures S8G and S8H). For vector genomes defined as intact (i.e., mapping to the full-length ssAAV9 sequence with no more than 100 bp loss) and single-stranded AAV (ssAAV), iodixanol-gradient-purified samples had the highest proportion, reaching approximately 35.5% and 40.2% for the commercial kit and PEI MAX + NaBu, respectively (Figure 7C). Backbone contamination was also observed at frequencies around 2.8%–6.2% (Figure 7D). The partial ssAAV vector genome frequency did not differ significantly between transfection methods but was lower for iodixanol-gradient-purified AAV9 preps at 45% of reads than for PEG-8000-precipitated samples at around 60% reads (Figure 7E). Furthermore, we detected low levels of impurities in the samples including ITR fragments, full self-complementary AAVS (scAAVs), partial scAAVs, and complex forms, defined as reads with three or more alignments to the transgene, packaging or helper plasmids, or hg38, all of which remained below 15.2% (Figure S8I).

Discussion

The developed suspension-based AAV production platform employing NaBu-supplemented media and PEI MAX-mediated transfection produced high-quality AAV6 and AAV9 vectors. This custom-developed protocol achieved viral yields equivalent to those obtained with a commercial kit, while simultaneously reducing the calculated in-house reagent costs approximately 5-fold, thereby facilitating the adoption of suspension systems. This proposed production platform eliminates reliance on third-party suppliers for AAV vector acquisition, consequently reducing production timelines.

Flow-cytometry-based assays were employed to evaluate transfection efficiency prior to downstream processing using reporter transgene expression (GFP) as a surrogate marker for AAV production. It is acknowledged that persistent transgene expression can potentially reduce viral yield.50 Furthermore, Dash et al. reported that only a subset of producer cells (5%–10%) produced AAV capsids, despite high transgene expression levels.51 This finding is crucial, as limited capsid production reduces viral yield.52Future incorporation of assays targeting cap-producing cells could provide additional insight into parameters influencing production efficiency. Collectively, these findings suggest a limitation of the reporter assay: not all reporter-positive cells may uniformly contribute to virus production, thus potentially overestimating the productive cell fraction. Nonetheless, we observed a positive correlation between the viral yield and both the fraction of reporter-positive cells and their transgene expression intensity. This correlation justifies the use of a flow-based assessment of transfection efficacy as a viable method for the comparative assessment of different AAV production methods within this study.

Literature indicates an average recovery of 73% during iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation, though recovery as low as 22% has been reported.53 Consistent with these findings, losses observed in the present study likely stem from inherent technical variabilities, including inconsistent fraction recovery from the gradient, deviations in the preparation of the discontinuous gradient layers, and ultrafiltration efficiency during final concentration. In addition, the absence of explicit vector genome quantification in the crude cell lysates prior to further purification by iodixanol or PEG 8000 precludes a definitive distinction between upstream productivity and downstream recovery. Analysis of crude viral vector lysates may be hindered by host cell impurities, which can increase background signal and reduce resolution in mass photometry measurements, impair library preparation or sequencing efficiency in nanopore sequencing, and destabilize droplet formation or inhibit amplification in ddPCR. Consequently, the reported data represent the final recovered yield following purification rather than the initial upstream productivity. These considerations highlight that purification-related losses must be taken into account when interpreting overall vector production performance.

The transfection efficiency in both the commercial kit and the PEI MAX + NaBu-based protocol exceeded 90%. This high rate represents a substantial improvement, as earlier studies using transient transfection rarely report more than 60%.51,54 However, viral production is influenced by multiple, interdependent factors. Previous studies have identified several crucial parameters, including the total amount of DNA transfected, producer cell passage number, plasmid ratios, cell density at transfection, transfection volume, and medium supplementation.28,55,56 In the present study, most of these parameters adhered to the commercial kit manufacturer’s recommendations. Only, the plasmid ratios were based on previously published protocols. Notably, the custom suspension-based production system required a lower total amount of plasmid DNA than conventional adherent systems. However, this reduction may not represent the optimal condition, and further investigation is warranted to identify the ideal plasmid amount. In particular, suboptimal ratio of transfected plasmids may lead to an excess of empty vectors or otherwise defective AAV particles, which has been proposed to impair transduction efficacy.57,58,59 Moreover, modifying a single variable at a time may not adequately capture the interplay among the multiple interdependent factors influencing viral production.38 AAV9 outperformed AAV6 in total viral yield. This difference is in line with prior studies reporting serotype-dependent packaging efficiency.45,46 Dual-plasmid strategies can produce comparable or even superior AAV vector yields.19,60 However the specific configuration of our system, where one plasmid carries the ITR-GOI and the second contains the rep, cap, and helper genes, may not be the optimal strategy for maximizing transfection-based viral production.19 Consequently, the differing plasmid architectures and ratios inherent to dual- versus triple-transfection methods make a direct, head-to-head comparison of their absolute packaging efficiencies challenging. However, while this study confirms the benefits of NaBu, additional studies evaluating a wider range of AAV serotypes and production conditions would further clarify the generalizability of these observations.

In this study, 5 mM NaBu was employed based on documented capacity of this concentration to enhance viral yields in different production systems.61,62 The inclusion of NaBu led to a 5-fold increase in the viral yield. NaBu regulates gene expression by inhibiting histone deacetylases, resulting in histone hyperacetylation.63 This mechanism of action subsequently affects protein production.37,64 The impact of NaBu on viral yield remains heavily debated in the literature. While some studies report that NaBu enhances the production of functional viral vectors, others observe minimal or no effect.38,55,65,66,67 In our experiments, NaBu supplementation increased both the fraction of transfected cells and the intensity of reporter gene expression. Unfortunately, NaBu negatively impacted cell viability. This finding supports previous studies demonstrating the association of NaBu with several adverse effects, including impaired cell proliferation, inducing apoptosis, and cell-cycle arrest.55,65,68,69 Interestingly, Chung et al. reported that the innate immune response is upregulated during AAV production in HEK293 cells as early as 12 h post-transfection.70 The inclusion of NaBu may mitigate the immune response upregulation, as it has been reported to significantly downregulate inflammation-related genes.71 However, it remains unclear which, if any, of the diverse functions of NaBu contribute to a favorable environment for viral production. Further research is required to elucidate the precise mechanisms of NaBu and to determine if additives or empirical titration of NaBu could further optimize the balance between cytotoxicity and viral yield. Beyond NaBu evaluated here, previous studies have identified additives capable of boosting viral production. In particular, the inclusion of salts (NaCl or KCl),72 fetal calf serum,73 and small molecules such as Nocodazole, M344, and z-VAD-fmk37 has been reported to enhance viral vector production. Whether these additives could act synergistically with NaBu remains to be investigated. Ultimately, further optimization of critical process parameters could address potential scalability challenges and further optimize cost-effective AAV yields.

This study implements three different methods to interrogate the quality of the produced AAV vectors, including ddPCR, mass photometry, and nanopore long-read sequencing, which have been applied in previous studies.41,74,75 Notably, we believe this study is the first to implement all three modalities to conduct a direct comparative analysis of purified samples using iodixanol density gradient and PEG 8000. The proportion of full particles varied between the methods, with the highest purity estimate being found using the two-dimensional ddPCR assay and the lowest for nanopore sequencing. This likely reflects that the ddPCR assay interrogates the vector genome at only two loci and may miss some truncated forms, whereas Nanopore sequencing detects all DNA fragments and is therefore more sensitive to incomplete packaging. Nonetheless, these data validate that the custom PEI MAX + NaBu production pipeline yields AAV vectors with vector genome quality comparable to that achieved with the commercial kit. Iodixanol purification was particularly effective in reducing impurities. Purity is highly dependent on ensuring accurate and error-free vector genome packing into the viral particles. A previous study, evaluating the integrity of the genome, observed increased truncation with larger cargoes, while sizes over 5.0 kb were almost depleted of full ssAAV vector genomes and resulted in low viral yield.76 Furthermore, small cargo-carrying AAV (<2.5 kb), seems more prone to be overpacked, and ideally 3.0–3.5 kb led to less truncation and less overfilled particles.76 The applied transgene size varied between 2.5 and 2.8 kb, resulting in approximately 29% fully packed AAV particles in iodixanol-purified samples and 15% in PEG-8000-purified samples. Heterogeneous AAV vectors, specifically those with residual producer-cell- or production-associated impurities, present an increased risk of immunotoxicity and genotoxicity. Furthermore, the presence of these impurities is associated with a theoretical risk of undesired gene expression or potential oncogenicity. While mass photometry sensitivity for impurities makes it ideal for investigating product homogeneity, its lack of discrimination between signal sources may lead to an incorrect estimate of the product quality, particularly when the molecular mass of the impurities is similar to that of AAV vectors. Mass photometry revealed no observable differences in fully packed particles. However, PEG-8000-purified samples contained a lower fraction of AAV particles relative to all detected particles, indicating the presence of process-related impurities. Further analysis is required to determine the nature of these impurities. In addition, extensive in-depth analysis is possible but requires more time-consuming and expensive techniques, such as Illumina NGS, PacBio’s single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing, analytical ultracentrifugation, and mass spectrometry.77,78,79 Alternatively, implementation of three-dimensional linkage ddPCR can provide a cost-efficient vector genome integrity assessment.80 These findings suggest that no single standalone method currently exists to simultaneously evaluate process-related and product-related impurities. Furthermore, this emphasizes the necessity of orthogonal approaches to ensure optimal AAV batch quality for research and clinical application.

In addition, the adequacy of traditional iodixanol- and PEG-8000-mediated purification for academic applications is increasingly questioned, as their limited capacity to exclude empty or partially filled capsids may confound interpretation of downstream experimental results. In contrast, more sophisticated methodologies, such as anion exchange chromatography (AEX) and size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), have been shown to enrich fully packaged vector particles while minimizing loss.53,81,82

Scaling up the PEI MAX + NaBu-based production from 30 to 120 mL resulted in a reduction in transfection efficiency. This finding highlights a known challenge in biomanufacturing processes where process complexity and variability can compromise viral yield and quality.83,84 Several AAV manufacturing processes have been developed, including transient dual/triple plasmid transfection in mammalian cells, stable producer cell lines, direct infection of mammalian cells with herpes simplex viruses, or infection of insect cells with recombinant baculovirus.17,85,86,87,88 While yields at the 120 mL scale did not statistically differ between the commercial kit and the PEI MAX + NaBu production pipeline, further optimization of this method and implementation of bioreactors might be required to scale the production for large-scale AAV preparations for preclinical studies and larger animal models.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that a simple, low-cost PEI MAX + NaBu-suspension-based workflow can reliably generate AAV vectors with yield and genome integrity comparable to a commercial production system. By integrating orthogonal quality-control assays, including ddPCR, mass photometry, and nanopore sequencing, we show that the protocol produces vectors suitable for downstream in vitro and in vivo applications. The platform provides an accessible, scalable alternative for academic laboratories and supports the broader adoption of suspension-based AAV manufacturing. In addition, our findings may ultimately facilitate large-scale implementation and direct integration into industrial bioreactors for enhanced production efficiency.

Material and methods

Cell culture

Viral Production Cells 2.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A49784) were cultured in Viral Production Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A4817901) supplemented with 4 mM GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #35050061). These cultures were maintained in single-use, vented Erlenmeyer flasks specifically designed for the suspension culture of human cells in a standard incubator with an installed orbital shaker (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #88881104) at 125 rpm, 37°C, and 8% CO2. K562 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Lonza). CD34+ mobilized HSPCs (mHSPCs) from healthy donors were collected after written consent at the Department of Clinical Immunology, Aarhus University Hospital, under study protocol 1-10-72-144-19, which was approved by the Central Denmark Region Committees on Health Research Ethics. CD34+ mHSPCs were isolated from apheresis products using a CliniMACS Prodigy (Miltenyi Biotec), using the CD34 enrichment standard procedure, following the manufacturer’s protocol, and the cells were cryopreserved. mHSPCs were cultured in StemSpan II (STEMCELL Technologies, #09655) supplemented with Flt3-L (100 ng/mL, PeproTech, #300-19), Human TPO (100 ng/mL, PeproTech, #300-18), SCF-1 (100 ng/mL, PeproTech, #300-07), interleukin-3 (IL-3) (20 ng/mL, PeproTech, #300-03), UM171 (35 nM, STEMCELL Technologies), and StemRegenin 1 (100 ng/mL, STEMCELL Technologies, #72342). K562 and mHSPCs were maintained at 37°C, 5% CO2 in a static incubator.

Production of AAV vectors

AAV production in suspension cells (commercial kit)

AAV vectors were produced using suspension Viral Production Cells 2.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A49784). Regardless of the production method, the producer cells were transfected with a total of 45 μg of plasmid DNA per 30 mL culture. Two distinct plasmid configurations were employed: dual transfection utilized a transgene-to-packaging/helper plasmid ratio of 1:3.5 (10 μg transgene and 35 μg of packaging/helper plasmid), and triple transfection utilized a mass ratio of 1:2.6:1.2 (9.375 μg transgene plasmids, 24.375 μg helper plasmid, and 11.25 μg packaging plasmid). Production of AAV particles was completed using the AAV-MAX Helper-Free AAV Production System Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A51217) strictly according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

AAV production in suspension cells (custom-optimized protocol)

Suspension cells designated for small-scale production were seeded at a density of 90E6 cells in 30 mL of Viral Production Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A4817901) supplemented with 4 mM GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #35050061). Three different transfection reagents were tested: calcium phosphate [Ca3(PO4)2], PEI, and PEI MAX. Cells were transfected with a plasmid DNA mix diluted in OptiMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #31985070). For the calcium phosphate method,: the DNA was first diluted in OptiMEM to a final volume of 450 μL. This mixture was then added to 63.5 μL 2.5 mM CaCl2 and 635 μL HBS buffer (56 mM HEPES, 380 mM NaCl, and 1.5 mM sodium phosphate dibasic dihydrate). The final mixture was vortexed for 15 s and incubated for 15 min at room temperature before adding to the cells. For PEI- or PEI MAX-based transfection, the agent was added to the diluted DNA mixture, vortexed for 15 s, and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Initially, a DNA to PEI-based transfection agent weight ratio of 1:4 was used, which was later optimized to 1:3. Afterward, the transfection mixture was added dropwise to the cells. For cultures supplemented with sodium butyrate, the compound was added to the production medium to achieve a final concentration of 5 mM immediately prior to transfection.

AAV production in adherent cells

Adherent HEK293 cells were employed to produce AAV6 as previously described, with a few modifications.44 Briefly, 1 day before transfection, HEK293T cells were seeded at 10 × 106 cells per 15-cm culture dish in 20 mL DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin-streptomycin, and L-glutamine. For AAV6, one 15-cm dish was transfected using standard PEI or PEI MAX transfection with 6 μg transfer plasmid and 22 μg pDGM6.

Plasmids for AAV production

For AAV6 production, we used the packaging plasmid pDGM6, and for AAV9 production, we used pAdDeltaF6 as helper plasmid and pAAV2/9n as packaging plasmid (pDGM6 was a gift from David Russell [Addgene plasmid #110660], and pAdDeltaF6 and pAAV2/9n were gifts from James M. Wilson [Addgene plasmid #112867 and #112865, respectively]). Transfer plasmids were the following: (1) an SFFV-GFP-bGHpolyA expression plasmid. This plasmid contains the gene cassette flanked by AAV2 ITRs, which is necessary for AAV packaging. The gene cassette also featured 400 bp homology arms, which were originally designed to facilitate homology-directed repair mediated insertion into the DOCK8 gene; in this study, these arms had no relevance. (2) A UbC-eGFP–Nanoluc-bGHpolyA cassette flanked by 400 bp homology arms for CRISPR/Cas HDR-directed integration into the AAVS1 locus.

Flow analysis of producer cells

For flow cytometric analysis, a 10 μL aliquot of the producer cells suspension was extracted and diluted 1:10 in a total volume of 100 μL with FACS buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% FBS, 0.2% EDTA). Additionally, the intercalating live/dead marker propidium iodide (125 ng) and Absolute Counting Beads (5 μL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #C36950) were added. Following an incubation period of 5–10 min, samples were immediately analyzed using a NovoCyte flow cytometer (Agilent) and the data subsequently processed with FlowJo software.

Harvesting cells

Producer cells were harvested by centrifugation at 2,500 rpm at 4°C for 15 min. The supernatant was then discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of AAV cell lysis buffer (ddH2O supplemented with 10 mM Tris, 2 mM MgCl2). After cell harvest, the resuspended cell pellet underwent three freeze-thaw cycles to lyse and release the AAV particles. Each cycle consisted of freezing the sample in a liquid dry-ice/ethanol bath for 10 min, followed by thawing in a 37°C water bath for 15 min. After each thawing, the sample was vortexed to ensure full resuspension. After three cycles, TurboNuclease (Sigma-Aldrich, #T4330-50KU) was added to a final concentration of 200 U/mL. This mixture was incubated for 45 min at 37°C, with the solution vortexed every 15 min. The lysed cell solution was centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 20 min at 4°C to pellet cell debris. The supernatant was transferred to a clean tube and stored at −80°C until further purification. A 7%–10% fraction of the lysate was processed via PEG 8000 precipitation, while the remaining volume was purified using an iodixanol gradient. Total vector yield was subsequently calculated by extrapolating these fractional yields to the original production volume.

PEG 8000 purifications

PEG 8000 (40%, with 2.5M NaCl) was added to the supernatant to a final concentration of 8% PEG 8000, and 500 mM NaCl and mixed, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C. The mix was then centrifuged at 3,200 × g for 30 min at 4°C. The supernatant was then removed, and the pellet was resuspended in PBS supplemented with 0.001% Pluronic F-68.

Iodixanol purification

Iodixanol density gradients were performed as described by Zolotukhin et al. with minor modifications.89 Crude virus lysate (thawed if frozen and adjusted to a total volume of 14 mL with PBS) was loaded into a Beckman Quick-Seal centrifuge tube (Ramcon, #342413). Using a sterile glass Pasteur pipette, iodixanol layers were then sequentially loaded below the virus in the following order 15%, 25%, 40%, and 58%. Phenol red (Sigma-Aldrich, #P0290) was added to the 25% and 58% layers to aid visualization of the layers within the tube. Tubes were loaded into a Beckman Type70Ti rotor and subjected to ultracentrifugation at 48,000 rpm for 2 h at 18°C with maximum acceleration and lowest deceleration settings. Following centrifugation, a 20-gauge needle was inserted into the interface between the 40% and 58% layer, and 3–5 mL of the viral solution was extracted. The extracted virus was mixed with PBS containing Sorbitol (1.372 M) and 0.001% Pluronic F-68 solution to a final volume of 15 mL and loaded onto a 100 kDa Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter unit (Sigma-Aldrich, #UFC9100). The filter was centrifuged at 3,000 × g at room temperature until the vector solution was concentrated. The concentrated vectors were then aliquoted and stored at −80°C until further analysis.

ddPCR titering

The assay included two primer/probe sets targeting either the ITR region of AAV2 or bGH poly A as previously published.90 For viral genome extraction, 5 μL of the AAV preparation was incubated with Ambion DNase I (RNase-free) (Invitrogen, AM2222) and DNase I Buffer (10×) (Invitrogen™, AM8170G) for 30 min at 37°C. DNase activity was subsequently terminated by adding an EDTA to a final concentration of 20 mM. Next, 25 μL of QuickExtract DNA Extraction Solution (Biosearch Technologies, SS00035-D2) was added, and the samples were vortexed for 15 s to facilitate cell lysis. Further, lysis was performed by incubation at 60°C for 6 min, followed by enzyme deactivation by heating to 100°C for 10 min. Samples were further diluted to yield final dilutions of 1:10,000; 1:100,000; or 1:200,000. The ddPCR reaction was assembled by mixing the diluted sample with ddH20, ddPCR Supermix for Probes (No dUTP) (Bio-Rad, #186-3023), and the PrimeTime qPCR Assay primer/probe mix (IDT). Primers were included at a final concentration of 500 nM, and probes were included at a final concentration of 250 nM in the total reaction volume. The ITR primers and probe used for ddPCR were forward primer 5ʹ-GAACCCCTAGTGATGGAGTT-3ʹ; reverse primer 5ʹ-CGGCCTCAGTGAGCGA-3ʹ; ITR probe: 5ʹ-FAM-CACTCCCTCTCTGCGCGCTCG-IBFQ-3ʹ (IDT). The bGH poly A primers used were forward: 5′-GCCAGCCATCTGTTGT-3′; reverse: 5′-GGAGTGGCACCTTCCA-3′; and Probe: 5′-/5HEX/TCCCCCGTG/ZEN/CCTTCCTTGACC/3lABkFQ/-3′ (IDT).

Twenty microliters of each ddPCR reaction mix was loaded into a DG8 Cartridge for QX200/QX100 Droplet Generator (Bio-Rad, #1864008) along with 70 μL of Droplet Generation Oil for Probes (Bio-Rad, #1863005). This cartridge was sealed with DG8 Gaskets for QX200/QX100 Droplet Generator (Bio-Rad, #1863009) and was subsequently placed into the QX200 Droplet Generator to partition the sample into droplets. Following droplet generation, 40 μL of the resulting ddPCR emulsion was transferred to a ddPCR 96-well plate (Bio-Rad, #12001925) and sealed with pierceable PCR foil (Bio-Rad, #1814040) using a PX1 PCR Plate Sealer (Bio-Rad).

Droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) plates were processed on the C1000 Touch thermal cycler thermocycler (Bio-Rad) using the following conditions: an initial incubation at 95 for 10 min, followed by 42 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 1 min, annealing at 60°C for 1 min, and extension at 72°C for 30 s. This was succeeded by an enzyme deactivation step at 98°C for 10 min. Samples were maintained at 4°C and analyzed within 24 h of completion of the PCR reaction. Quantification of AAV preparations was performed via two-dimensional ddPCR (QX-200; Bio-Rad). Subsequent analysis was conducted using QX Manager software.

In vitro transduction experiments

5E4 K562 cells were seeded in 96-well U-bottom plates with 100 μL RPMI. The concentration was kept under 1E6 cells/mL. 1E4 HSPCs were seeded in 96-well flat-bottom plates with 100 μL fresh media. 1E4 HEK293 were seeded in 96-well flat-bottom plates with 100 μL fresh media. Twenty-four hours after transduction, all transduced cells were spun down at 300 × g for 5 min and resuspended in fresh media. Seventy-two hours after transduction, cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS with 2% FBS, 0.2% EDTA) and stained with 125 ng Propidium Iodide.

In vivo biodistribution assessment

Animal experiments were approved by and conducted in accordance with the accepted protocol by the Danish Animal Experiments Inspectorate (2023-15-0201-01458). Six-week-old, white BALB/c mice were procured from Janvier and housed in the animal facilities of the Department of Biomedicine, Aarhus University, Denmark. The mice were fed with standard chow and water ad libitum. After a 2-week acclimatization period, 5E11 AAV vectors encoding a transgene expressing eGFP and luciferase were administered intravenously in 200 μL PBS. Nine mice received AAV vectors produced with PEI MAX + NaBu supplementation and nine received AAV vectors prepared using the commercial kit. Two control mice were injected with PBS without AAV vectors.

For bioluminescence imaging, mice were maintained under isoflurane anesthesia. The Nano-Glo Fluorofurimazine In Vivo Substrate (Promega. #N4100) was administered via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 0.1 pmol per mouse. Five minutes after injection, the mice were placed into the Newton 7.0 BIO imaging system (Vilber) with ophthalmic lubricant applied to the eyes. Image acquisition was performed 7 and 28 days after AAV vector injection. Imaging data were analyzed using Kuant software (quantified as total-bkgd ph/s/sr per mouse). Four weeks after injection, mice were sacrificed, and liver and heart tissues were harvested. GFP expressions were assessed by widefield fluorescence microscopy, after which tissues were homogenized for ex vivo bioluminescence analysis.

Bioluminescence detection

In vitro setup: cells undergoing luciferase assay were resuspended in Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #31985062) and an equal amount of Nano-Glo Live Cell Reagent (Promega, #N2011) was added according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The bioluminescence from the Luciferase assay was quantified using SpectraMax iD3 (Molecular Devices). In vivo Setup: Mouse tissues (heart and lungs) were extracted subsequently after euthanizing and kept in 500 μL cold PBS. The tissue was weighed before adding 500 μL 1× Glo-Lysis buffer (Promega. #E2661). The tissue was then homogenized using TissueLyzer II. Fifty microliters of the homogenized samples were then transferred to a new empty white plate, to which 50 μL Nano-Glo Live Cell Reagent (Promega, #N2011) substrate was added per well and measured.

Mass photometry of the AAV preps

Data acquisition was performed using a mass photometer (TwoMP, Refeyn). AAV samples were initially diluted to a stock concentration of 1E11 particles/mL. Prior to measurement, samples were further diluted to a final concentration appropriate for mass photometry analysis, ensuring optimal detection of individual particles. Thyroglobulin (670 kDa) and BSA (66.5 kDa) were used as calibration standards to ensure accurate mass determination. Samples were prediluted in PBS to minimize background interference. Measurements were conducted following the manufacturer’s guidelines for coverslip preparation and instrument calibration. To streamline the analysis across the samples, events were classified as full particles (4,000–5,500 kDa) or empty particles (2,000–4,000 kDa). This classification utilizes a slightly modified version of previously established criteria.49 The partial and overfilled categories were not separately defined; instead, they were incorporated into either the full or empty AAV vector particle classifications for the purpose of this analysis. In addition, we defined 0–2,000 kDa as events from impurities.

Nanopore sequencing of the AAV preps

At least 2.6E10 AAV vector genomes were treated with 20U DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #EN0521) for 10 min at 37°C to remove non-encapsidated DNA. Two microliters of 500 mM EDTA was added, and the reactions were incubated for 10 min at 72°. For DNA extraction and purification from the AAV preps, the PureLink Viral RNA/DNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #12280050) was used according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. The DNA was eluted in 50 μL. Following purification, the DNA was end-prepped using the NEBNext Ultra II End Repair/dA-tailing module (NEB, #E7546) by the addition of 7 μL reaction buffer and 3 μL enzyme mix to the purified DNA, followed by 5 min of incubation at 20°C and then 65°C for 5 min. The end-prepped DNA was purified using AMPure XP Beads (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, #SQK-NBD114.24) with a 1.8× ratio of beads to sample. Sequencing adapters and barcodes were attached using the Native Barcoding Kit V14 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, #SQK-NBD114.24) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, using a 1.8× bead to sample ratio for purification steps. The library was sequenced using a PromethION flow cell. The analysis was performed using the Oxford Nanopore wf-aav-qc workflow via the EPI2ME desktop application.

Statistical analysis

For all statistical analyses, we used one-way and two-way ANOVA with Turkey multiple comparisons test, unless otherwise noted in the figure legend, and p ≤ 0.05 (∗) was considered significant. Specific significance thresholds are denoted in the figures as following: p ≤ 0.01 (∗∗), p ≤ 0.001 (∗∗∗), and p ≤ 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗). These analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Graphs were made using GraphPad Prism 9. Graphical presentation of the flow data was made using FlowJo v.10 software.
