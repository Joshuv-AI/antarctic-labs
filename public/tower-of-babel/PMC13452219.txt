A Multi-omics Regulated Cell Death Framework Defines Immune Phenotypes and Guides Precision Therapy in Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Fanhe Gao, Jun Xiang, Chunlin Wang, Hao Zhang, Yunxiao Liu, Jiaqi Wang, Hao Jiang, Nana Zhang, Nanfeng Meng, Shuaibin Feng, Zhongming Wang, Guiyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is molecularly and immunologically heterogeneous, contributing to variable treatment response. Because regulated cell death (RCD) intersects with tumor metabolism, immune regulation, and therapeutic susceptibility, we built an RCD-centered framework for CRC stratification. Multi-cohort transcriptomic data were used to infer RCD subtypes with non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) and non-negative least squares (NNLS). Genomic, bulk RNA-seq, single-cell RNA-seq, and spatial transcriptomic datasets were integrated to characterize subtype-associated biology. Machine-learning models were developed for immunotherapy response and survival-risk estimation. Candidate compounds were screened by GDSC2-based drug-sensitivity modeling and molecular docking, and FSTL3 was functionally assessed in vitro. The framework separated CRC samples into two RCD-related phenotypes resembling immune-hot and immune-cold states. RCD1 showed immune activation and higher mutational burden, whereas RCD2 showed immune-suppressed features, intratumoral heterogeneity, and aggressive biology. RCD-associated signatures showed potential for predicting immunotherapy response and survival risk. Dasatinib was prioritized for immune-cold, high-risk tumors, with preliminary evidence supporting its activity in CRC cells, while functional assays suggested a role for FSTL3 in growth, invasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and apoptosis regulation. These findings suggest that RCD-based multi-omics analysis may refine CRC stratification and help generate therapeutic hypotheses.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most frequently diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. Its treatment is complicated by molecular heterogeneity and a complex tumor microenvironment. During tumor evolution, cancer cells may evade immune surveillance through immune checkpoint pathways, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have changed treatment for selected CRC patients [2]. Durable benefit, however, remains restricted to a subset of patients, making response biomarkers urgently needed [3].

Regulated cell death (RCD) comprises cell-death programs that can be genetically or pharmacologically modulated. The 2018 Nomenclature Committee on Cell Death recommendations defined twelve canonical modalities, including necroptosis and immunogenic cell death [4], and later studies have described additional non-canonical forms. RCD pathways participate in immune regulation, inflammation, metabolic reprogramming, and immune escape [5,6]. They may also interact as networks that influence the tumor microenvironment and ICI response [7,8]. A systematic evaluation of these programs in CRC is still limited.

We assembled twenty RCD pathways and used them to construct a CRC stratification framework. Genomic, bulk transcriptomic, single-cell, and spatial transcriptomic data were integrated to examine subtype biology and obtain preliminary mechanistic insight. We also tested whether this framework could help identify patients more likely to benefit from immunotherapy and nominate candidate agents for high-risk tumors. In vitro assays were used to examine a candidate target. Materials and methods

Data collection and preprocessing

We collected 23 primary CRC cohorts from GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), excluding samples with documented preoperative chemoradiotherapy. Tumor transcriptomes from 33 cancer types and CRC normal samples were downloaded from UCSC Xena (https://xena.ucsc.edu/). Nine ICI cohorts with matched expression and clinical data were also included (Supplementary Table S1). Expression matrices were log2-scaled, batch effects were adjusted with ComBat, and outliers were filtered by interquartile range. Paired TCGA-CRC tumor and normal samples formed the TCGA-CRC paired cohort (50 pairs). Twenty RCD gene sets were compiled from MSigDB, KEGG, GeneCards, and published literature (Supplementary Table S2) [9].

Unsupervised clustering

NMF was applied to the RCD pathway expression matrix using the NMF R package. Candidate ranks (K = 2-6) were evaluated by cophenetic correlation and residual sum of squares (RSS). At the optimal rank, the GEO-CRC pathway expression matrixV (pathways × samples) was first normalised to obtain V˜=N(V). Nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF) was then applied: V˜≈WH,W∈R≥0p×K,H∈R≥0K×n, where W denotes the basis matrix (pathways × components) and H the activity matrix (components × samples). Subtype labels were assigned according to component weights in H; for sample j, z^j=argmaxk∈{1,…,K}Hkj [10].

For cross-cohort application, W learned in the training cohort was fixed, and an external cohort matrix V′ was normalized in the same way to yield V˜′=N(V′). The corresponding activity matrix H′ was estimated by projecting V˜′ onto the fixed W using nonnegative least squares (NNLS): H′=argminH′≥0∥V′˜−WH′∥F2. Subtype labels for external samples were assigned analogously: z^′j=argmaxk∈{1,…,K}H′kj, thereby enabling consistent RCD subtyping across datasets [11].

Pathway activity and immune scoring

ssGSEA quantified 28 immune cell signatures from TISIDB (http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/download.php), and GSVA was used for pathway activity estimation. CMS and CRIS labels were assigned with CMSclassifier and CRISclassifier [12,13]. ESTIMATE scores, tumor purity, CIBERSORT, and MCPcounter estimates were generated through IOBR. TIP and TIDE scores were obtained from their respective web portals.

WGCNA and functional enrichment

WGCNA was performed with the WGCNA R package to identify co-expression modules [14]. After sample clustering and soft-threshold selection, the adjacency matrix was converted into a topological overlap matrix. Modules were detected by dynamic tree cutting and merged by eigengene similarity. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed for each hub gene set, with the top 10 terms reported.

Genomic data

Somatic mutations and copy number alterations were analyzed in the genomic layer. Somatic variants were processed with maftools and visualized by oncoplot. Tumor mutational burden was calculated from mutation data, and focal amplifications and deletions were defined with GISTIC 2.0 [15]. Additional features, including SNV neoantigens, mutation rates, and aneuploidy scores, were obtained from published resources [16].

Single-cell RNA-seq

Five scRNA-seq datasets (GSE205506, GSE236698, GSE231559, GSE236581, and GSE245552) were integrated. Cells were filtered using predefined QC criteria, potential doublets were removed with scDblFinder and DoubletFinder, and Harmony was used for batch correction. Clustering and manual marker-based annotation were performed using the top 30 principal components. Scissor mapped bulk RCD subtype information onto tumor cells, and CellChat, CytoTRACE/CytoTRACE2, Monocle2, and scTenifoldKnk were used for cell-cell communication, stemness, pseudotime, and in silico perturbation analyses.

To distinguish malignant epithelial cells, epithelial cells were reclustered into 20 subclusters, and inferCNV was run with T cells, B cells, and myeloid cells as references. Gene-level inferCNV values were converted into copy-number burden scores using the neutral interval estimated from reference cells. Summed scores generated a per-cell CNV burden metric termed totalCNV.

Spatial transcriptomics data analysis

Public CRC spatial transcriptomics data were analyzed [17]. A separate Seurat object was generated for each dataset, and SPOTlight used the single-cell reference to infer spot-level cell-type composition. Inferred cell-type distributions were visualized with SpatialDimPlot.

Machine learning development of predictive signatures

RCD.resp was designed to estimate immunotherapy response in the merged ICI cohort, which was split 7:3 into training and internal validation sets. Candidate predictors were restricted to shared RCD-related genes. Response was modeled as a binary endpoint, with responders as the positive class. Training-set standardization parameters were applied to validation datasets. Seven classifiers were trained: LASSO, Ridge regression, support vector machine, XGBoost, random forest, k-nearest neighbors, and naive Bayes. Hyperparameters were tuned by cross-validation, and models were evaluated by AUC and accuracy in the internal validation set and four independent ICI cohorts. The best internal-validation model was selected as RCD.resp.

RCD.sig was constructed for overall survival prediction. Prognostic candidates were screened from RCD1 and RCD2 hub genes by univariate Cox analysis. GEO-CRC cases were split 7:3 into training and internal validation sets, and TCGA-PanCancer served as external validation. We generated 101 pipelines by combining 12 feature-selection strategies with 13 modeling algorithms, assessed them by C-index, time-dependent AUC, and Kaplan-Meier analysis, and compared the selected model with published signatures [18]. R packages included glmnet, CoxBoost, e1071, caret, kernlab, randomForest, Boruta, spls, xgboost, minet, timeROC, rms, ggDCA, and limma.

Drug screening and molecular docking

GDSC2 drug-response and expression profiles from CancerRxGene (GDSC, https://www.cancerrxgene.org) were used as the training resource. Patient-level IC50 values were inferred with the standard oncoPredict workflow; lower predicted IC50 indicated greater predicted sensitivity. Candidate compound structures were obtained from PubChem, and target-related protein structures from UniProt. Receptors were prepared in PyMOL, and docking was performed with AutoDock Vina [19]. Binding free energy and binding poses were recorded for each ligand.

IHC and TUNEL staining

Human CRC specimens were obtained from patients undergoing surgical resection at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned for staining. For IHC, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval, and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against FSTL3 and CD8. Staining was visualized with DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. TUNEL staining was performed on adjacent sections using a commercial kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were captured under a light microscope, and representative tumor regions were analyzed.

Cell Viability Assays

Cells were seeded into 96-well culture plates and incubated overnight to allow for complete attachment. Subsequently, the old culture medium was removed and replaced with fresh medium containing varying concentrations of dasatinib (specific concentration gradients are indicated in the figure). A vehicle control group containing an equivalent volume of solvent (DMSO, final concentration < 0.1%) and blank wells without cells were also established. Each experimental condition was tested in triplicate, and the cells were continuously treated for 72 h. After the treatment, cell viability relative to the vehicle control group was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Finally, the absorbance (OD value) at a wavelength of 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. The final cell viability percentage was calculated using the following formula: [(OD of experimental well − OD of blank well) / (OD of vehicle control well − OD of blank well)] × 100%.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the CRC cell lines using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). cDNA was synthesized from 2 µg RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (TOYOBO). Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green on an Applied Biosystems 7500 instrument, with 2 µL of cDNA per reaction. Gene expression was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method normalized to GAPDH.

Cell lines and cell culture

CRC cell lines were acquired from the Cell Bank of the Type Culture Collection Committee, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cells were maintained in medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, GIBCO, USA) at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere.

Cell transfection

Lentiviral vectors for FSTL3 overexpression or knockdown were constructed and packaged by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). HT-29 and RKO cells were transduced with recombinant lentivirus for 16-18 hours, followed by medium replacement and continued culture of transduced cells. After cells reached ∼90% confluence, stable cell lines were established through puromycin (2 μg/ml) selection. The efficiency of FSTL3 overexpression and knockdown was verified by Western blot analysis.

Western blotting assay and antibodies

Western blotting was performed according to standard protocols. Briefly, total cellular proteins were extracted and quantified using a BCA assay kit (APPLYGEN, #P1513). Protein samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 2 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Target protein signals were detected using an ECL kit (APPLYGEN, #P1010) and imaged with a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP system. The following primary antibodies were used: FSTL3 (Rabbit pAb, 232761, Abcam), E-cadherin (Rabbit mAb, 3195, CST) and N-cadherin (Rabbit mAb, 13116, CST), PCNA (Rabbit pAb, 10205-2-AP, Proteintech). The original western blot data are provided in Supplementary Data S1.

Cell proliferation and colony formation assays

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Solarbio, #CA1210). Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well and cultured for 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. At each time point, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was then measured to determine cell viability. For the colony formation assay, 1,000 cells were plated into 6-well plates and cultured for 14 days. The resulting colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 2% crystal violet, and then counted under a microscope.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion assays were performed using Transwell chambers (LABSELECT, #14241). For migration assessment, 1×10⁵ cells were seeded into the upper chamber in serum-free medium. For invasion assays, the upper chamber was pre-coated with Matrigel (Corning, #356234) before cell seeding. In both assays, the lower chamber contained medium with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24–48 h of incubation, cells that had traversed the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and counted using ImageJ software.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted in R (v4.3+) [20]. Welch's t test was used for approximately normal continuous variables with similar variance; otherwise, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used. Categorical variables were compared with Fisher's exact test. Correlations were assessed mainly by Spearman's rho and, where appropriate, Pearson's r. Multiple testing was controlled by the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 or FDR < 0.05 considered significant. Survival analyses used survival and survminer; Kaplan-Meier curves were compared by log-rank test, and Cox models estimated hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

Results

Identification of RCD subtypes in CRC

In the TCGA-CRC paired cohort, most RCD pathways exhibited significantly different activities between tumor and matched normal tissues (Supplementary Figure S1A). In addition, principal component analysis of the 20 RCD pathways revealed a pronounced separation between tumor and normal samples, indicating that RCD programs undergo systematic reprogramming during CRC initiation and progression (Supplementary Figure S1B). Using the preprocessing workflow described in Methods, we established three analysis-ready cohorts for downstream analyses: the GEO-CRC cohort (n = 2,940), the TCGA-PanCancer cohort (n = 9,760), and the Merged ICI cohort (n = 633) (Supplementary Figure S2A–C). Applying NMF to these pathway activities in the GEO-CRC cohort resolved two RCD subtypes, with RCD1 showing predominant upregulation of inflammation and immune related RCD modalities and RCD2 showing preferential activation of RCD programs linked to metabolism and cell cycle control (Figure 2A). Clinical features of RCD subtypes

In the GEO-CRC cohort, we assessed associations between RCD subtypes and clinical features (Figure 2C; Supplementary Figure S3A). Age and sex distributions were similar between RCD1 and RCD2 (p > 0.05). By contrast, differences were observed in anatomic site and histologic grade. RCD1 was more frequently associated with left-sided tumors and lower histologic grade, whereas RCD2 was more often observed in right-sided tumors and intermediate-to-high-grade disease. These results suggest that the two subtypes may differ not only in molecular profiles but also in clinical and pathological features.

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed a significant difference in overall survival between the two groups, with more favorable outcomes in patients with RCD1 tumors (P < 0.05; Figure 2D). The two subtypes also showed distinct distribution patterns across CMS and CRIS classifications (Figure 2E). In addition, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses indicated that RCD subtype was associated with overall survival after adjustment for established clinical variables, as well as CMS and CRIS classifications (Figure 2F).

Immune features of RCD subtypes

RCD1 showed higher ESTIMATE scores than RCD2 (Figure 2G). Consistently, RCD1 was associated with higher immune cell infiltration scores (Figure 2H) and greater activity in several steps of the cancer immunity cycle (Figure 2I). Hallmark enrichment analysis suggested that RCD1 was more related to immune and inflammatory programs, whereas RCD2 showed relatively higher activity in cell cycle- and protein synthesis-related pathways (Figure 2J). In addition, TIDE analysis predicted a higher likelihood of immune checkpoint blockade response in RCD1 than in RCD2 (Figure 2K). Together, these results suggest that the two RCD subtypes are associated with distinct immune landscapes and biological programs, which may be relevant to immunotherapy response.

External validation of RCD subtypes

To evaluate whether the RCD framework could be applied beyond the GEO-CRC cohort, we fixed the GEO-CRC-derived basis matrix W and projected TCGA-PanCancer samples onto this reference using NNLS, thereby assigning samples to RCD1 or RCD2 (Figure 3A). RCD pathway activity patterns in TCGA-PanCancer were broadly consistent with those in GEO-CRC, suggesting that the main framework structure was partly preserved across cancer types. In the FGES immune taxonomy, RCD1 was more frequently distributed in the IE and IE/F categories, whereas RCD2 was more often observed in the F and D categories (Figure 3B). Under the Thorsson immune classification, RCD1 showed relatively higher proportions of C2 and C3, while RCD2 was more often associated with C1 and C4 (Figure 3C) [16,21]. In independent CRC cohorts, RCD subtyping also showed prognostic stratification capacity (Figure 3D). Genomic landscape of RCD subtypes

Because comprehensive genomic data were mainly available from TCGA, genomic comparisons were performed in the TCGA-CRC cohort stratified by RCD subtype. The 30 most frequently mutated genes showed subtype-related differences, although common CRC drivers, including APC, TP53, KRAS, PIK3CA, and SMAD4, were present in both groups (Figure 3E). Arm-level GISTIC profiles also differed, with RCD2 showing broader copy number alteration peaks than RCD1 (Figure 3F). RCD1 had higher tumor mutational burden and neoantigen load (Figure 3G, H), whereas RCD2 showed features related to chromosomal instability, including more copy-number segments, a higher fraction of the genome altered, and higher aneuploidy scores (Figure 3I; Supplementary Figure S3B). Together, these results suggest that RCD1 may be relatively neoantigen-rich, while RCD2 may be associated with greater chromosomal instability and intratumoral heterogeneity.

Single-cell characterization of malignant epithelial cells

After quality control and preprocessing, five single-cell datasets were integrated into a unified scRNA-seq atlas (Supplementary Figure S4A). A total of 372,485 cells were annotated into five major cell classes and 28 subtypes based on representative marker genes (Figure 4A, B; Supplementary Figure S4B). Epithelial cells were reclustered into 20 clusters, and malignant epithelial cells were identified using inferCNV-derived totalCNV scores (Supplementary Figure S4C–F). The 95th percentile of totalCNV values in reference cells was used as an empirical cutoff to define the upper boundary of background CNV variation. Statistically, this threshold represents the upper 5% tail of the reference-cell distribution and provides a non-parametric, one-sided criterion for distinguishing clusters with elevated CNV burden. Epithelial clusters with median totalCNV values below this cutoff were classified as normal epithelial clusters. Pseudotime analysis divided tumor cells into five states, with State 1 designated as the root state based on sample type, CytoTRACE, and CytoTRACE2 patterns (Figure 4C, D). Along pseudotime, CytoTRACE and CytoTRACE2 decreased initially and then increased, whereas totalCNV showed the opposite trend (Figure 4E, F; Supplementary Figure S4F). Single-cell and spatial mapping of RCD subtypes in tumor cells

Bulk RCD phenotypes were projected onto tumor cells using Scissor to identify subtype-associated tumor cell populations (Figure 4G). RCD1-associated tumor cells were mainly enriched in primary tumors, whereas RCD2-associated tumor cells were more frequently derived from liver metastases (Figure 4H). Along pseudotime, RCD1-associated tumor cells were primarily distributed in early states, while RCD2-associated tumor cells were enriched in middle-to-late states (Figure 4I, J). Compared with background tumor cells, RCD1-associated tumor cells showed lower CytoTRACE, CytoTRACE2, and totalCNV scores, whereas RCD2-associated tumor cells showed higher values across these metrics (Figure 4K–M), suggesting greater malignant potential in the RCD2-associated population. Cell–cell communication analysis indicated stronger interactions between RCD1-associated tumor cells and immune cells, while RCD2-associated tumor cells showed stronger communication with stromal cells. Spatial transcriptomic analysis further showed that RCD1-associated tumor cells were more spatially aggregated and closer to T cells, whereas RCD2-associated tumor cells were more dispersed and tended to be spatially separated from T cells (Figure 4O–R). Together, these findings suggest that RCD2-associated tumor cells may be linked to increased invasiveness and immune evasion.

Core gene programs of RCD subtypes

Using WGCNA, we identified 12 co-expression modules after sample clustering and soft-threshold selection (β = 9, R² = 0.85) (Supplementary Figure S5A–E). MEcoral and MEdarkolivegreen showed the strongest associations with RCD subtypes (MEcoral: r = 0.67, P < 0.001; MEdarkolivegreen: r = 0.64, P < 0.001) (Figure 5A). Based on |GS| ≥ 0.3 and |MM| ≥ 0.5, 94 and 98 hub genes were identified from these two modules, respectively (Figure 5B; Supplementary Table S3). MEcoral hub genes were mainly upregulated in RCD2, whereas MEdarkolivegreen hub genes were mainly upregulated in RCD1 (Supplementary Figure S5F). Functional enrichment analysis suggested that RCD1-associated hub genes were related to immune functions, while RCD2-associated hub genes were linked to cell cycle and proliferation pathways (Figure 5C). AUCell analysis showed higher RCD1 scores in T and B cells and higher RCD2 scores in stromal and malignant tumor cells (Supplementary Figure S6A, B). Prognostic and immunotherapeutic relevance of core RCD programs

Based on these hub genes, GSVA-derived RCD1 and RCD2 scores were calculated for each sample and dichotomized at the median. The score-defined groups were largely consistent with the original RCD subtypes, suggesting that these gene sets captured key features of the two phenotypes (Figure 5D). Patients with high RCD1 scores or low RCD2 scores showed more favorable survival outcomes (Figure 5E). In pan-cancer analyses, RCD1 scores were positively correlated with immune checkpoint molecules and effector T- and B-cell signatures, whereas RCD2 scores showed weaker associations with immune checkpoints, positive correlations with fibroblast and endothelial signatures, and negative correlations with most immune cell signatures (Figure 5F, G). In the merged ICI cohort, median-based score stratification was associated with overall survival and clinical response: higher RCD1 scores were linked to better outcomes and higher response rates, whereas higher RCD2 scores were associated with poorer outcomes (P < 0.01; Figure 5H, I).

Development of the immunotherapy response model RCD.resp

Candidate features were defined as the union of RCD1 and RCD2 hub genes. In the training cohort, seven machine learning algorithms were used to build models for predicting immunotherapy response, and the best performing model in the validation cohort was selected as RCD.resp and further evaluated in independent ICI cohorts (Figure 6A). In internal validation, the XGBoost model achieved the best performance, with an AUC of 0.775 and an accuracy of 0.733 (Figure 6B, C). Calibration curve and decision curve analysis demonstrated that RCD.resp exhibited favorable predictive calibration and potential predictive value (Figure 6D; Supplementary Figure S7A). This model was subsequently tested in four independent ICI cohorts and maintained favorable AUC and accuracy, indicating good generalizability across datasets (Figure 6E; Supplementary Figure S7B). SHAP analysis further highlighted the major contributors to model prediction (Figure 6F; Supplementary Figure S7C). Development of the prognostic model RCD.sig

To develop a prognostic signature, survival-associated genes shared between GEO-CRC and TCGA-PanCancer Cox analyses were intersected with WGCNA hub genes, yielding 37 candidates (Figure 6G). In the training set, 101 model pipelines were constructed by combining 12 feature-selection methods with 13 modeling algorithms (Figure 6H; Supplementary Figure S8A). Joint evaluation using the C-index and time-dependent AUCs selected Boruta + RSF as the optimal framework, termed RCD.sig (Figure 6I, J; Supplementary Figure S9A, B). Patients were then stratified by the median risk score, and the high-risk group showed poorer survival outcomes (P < 0.001; Figure 6K–M). RCD.sig also maintained prognostic performance in independent validation cohorts and showed higher C-index and AUC values than previously published models (Figure 6N; Supplementary Figure S9C).

Among the eight model genes, STAT4, IL2RB, CD3D, and CCR7 were derived from the RCD1 hub gene set and acted as protective factors, whereas THBS2, SFRP2, FSTL3, and COMP were derived from the RCD2 hub gene set and acted as risk factors (Supplementary Figure S9D). Consistently, RCD2 tumors had higher RCD.sig risk scores than RCD1 tumors (Figure 6O), suggesting that RCD.sig partly reflects the biological distinction between the two subtypes and that higher scores may indicate an immune-cold, high-risk state.

Screening of candidate therapeutics

Candidate agents were screened by correlating predicted IC50 values with RCD.sig risk scores and the expression levels of the four risk genes. Compounds with significant negative correlations were considered potential sensitivity candidates, because lower IC50 values indicate higher predicted drug sensitivity. Intersecting the top five negatively correlated compounds for each feature identified dasatinib as the only shared candidate (Figure 7A). In the TCGA-CRC cohort, validation analysis again identified dasatinib among the candidate agents (Supplementary Figure S10A). Stratification by RCD subtype and risk group showed lower predicted IC50 values for dasatinib in the high-risk group and in RCD2 tumors, with the RCD2 high-risk subset showing the lowest predicted IC50 values (Figure 7B; Supplementary Figure S10B). Molecular docking suggested potential binding between dasatinib and proteins related to the four risk genes, with binding free energies of −8.1, −8.8, −8.4, and −7.6 kcal/mol (Supplementary Figure S10C). FSTL3 mRNA expression was significantly elevated in CRC cell lines compared with the normal colonic epithelial cell line NCM460, with SW480 showing the highest expression (Supplementary Figure S10D). Dasatinib inhibited cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, and the IC50 value was highest in NCM460 cells (8.97 μM) but lower in CRC cell lines, including HCT15 (2.51 μM), HT29 (1.68 μM), RKO (1.33 μM), and SW480 (0.66 μM) (Supplementary Figure S10E). Identification of the key target gene FSTL3

Across 12 feature-selection methods, FSTL3 was repeatedly selected as a prognostic gene (Figure 7C). Correlation analysis showed that FSTL3 expression was associated with multiple RCD pathways, as well as pathways related to targeted therapy and immunotherapy (Figure 7D, E). In both the GEO-CRC and TCGA-CRC cohorts, FSTL3 expression tended to increase with higher AJCC stage and T category (Figure 7F; Supplementary Figure S10F–I). Median-based stratification of FSTL3 expression was associated with overall survival, with higher expression linked to poorer outcomes (Figure 7G; Supplementary Figure S10G). In silico FSTL3 knockout perturbed 87 genes, which were mainly enriched in apoptosis, ferroptosis, MHC class I antigen presentation, cell adhesion, and extracellular matrix remodeling pathways (Figure 7H, I). IHC analysis further showed that FSTL3-high tumors had lower CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and fewer TUNEL-positive cells (Figure 7J). Transcriptomic analysis confirmed higher FSTL3 expression in tumor tissues, consistent with the IHC results (Figure 7K).

In vitro Functional validation of FSTL3

In HT-29 and RKO cells, Western blotting confirmed efficient FSTL3 overexpression and knockdown (Figure 8A, B). Transwell assays showed that FSTL3 overexpression significantly enhanced cell migration and invasion, whereas FSTL3 knockdown markedly suppressed these phenotypes (Figure 8C, D). Colony formation assays further demonstrated increased clonogenic capacity upon FSTL3 overexpression and a clear reduction in colony numbers after knockdown (Figure 8E, F). EMT related protein profiling showed that overexpression increased N-cadherin and PCNA while reducing E-cadherin, with the opposite pattern observed after knockdown (Figure 8G, H). Consistently, CCK-8 assays indicated that FSTL3 overexpression significantly promoted proliferation, whereas knockdown significantly inhibited cell growth (Figure 8I, J). Discussion

CRC is characterized by substantial molecular and microenvironmental heterogeneity, which remains a major challenge for individualized treatment [1,3]. RCD includes multiple biological programs involved in tumor progression and treatment response [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. In this study, we developed an RCD-based framework to stratify CRC patients and explore potential therapeutic implications, particularly in relation to immunotherapy response and candidate drug selection.

Previous studies have often focused on individual RCD modalities, which may not fully capture the coordinated effects of different cell death programs [22,23]. By integrating 20 RCD pathways, we identified two major RCD subtypes with immune-hot and immune-cold features. This subtype-related pattern was also observed in the TCGA-PanCancer cohort, suggesting that RCD activity may reflect a broader immune-related organization of the tumor microenvironment.

Multi-omics analyses further supported the biological distinction between the two subtypes. RCD1 was associated with higher immune infiltration, stronger cancer immunity cycle activity, and positive correlations with immune checkpoint molecules. Genomically, RCD1 tumors showed higher mutational and neoantigen burdens, which may contribute to immune recognition [24]. Single-cell and spatial analyses also suggested that RCD1-associated tumor cells were located closer to T cells. These findings indicate that RCD1 tumors may represent a more immune-active state and may be more likely to respond to immune checkpoint blockade.

In contrast, RCD2 showed lower immune activity and features related to tumor aggressiveness, including greater chromosomal instability, clonal diversity, and stromal interaction. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses further suggested that RCD2-associated tumor cells may be more spatially dispersed and relatively separated from T cells. For this immune-cold, high-risk phenotype, computational drug sensitivity analysis prioritized dasatinib as a candidate agent. Although dasatinib is not a standard therapy for CRC and its clinical efficacy has been variable [25], its predicted sensitivity differed by RCD subtype and risk score, suggesting that the RCD framework may help generate hypotheses for subtype-guided therapeutic selection.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was mainly based on retrospective analyses of publicly available multi-cohort sequencing datasets, with only preliminary validation from in vitro experiments. Therefore, our findings require further confirmation in prospective clinical cohorts. In particular, dasatinib was proposed as a candidate therapeutic agent for immune-cold tumors primarily on the basis of bioinformatic prediction and initial in vitro validation. Although we observed potential antitumor effects of dasatinib in different colorectal cancer cell lines, these results are not sufficient to support its direct clinical application. Future studies should incorporate in vivo models, patient-derived organoids, and prospective clinical investigations to systematically evaluate the therapeutic potential of dasatinib in colorectal cancer patients with specific molecular subtypes.

Second, the available public immunotherapy cohorts remain limited in sample size, and most of them are small-scale studies with imbalanced clinical outcome distributions. Although we integrated multiple immunotherapy cohorts and applied appropriate methods to reduce batch effects, differences and missing information regarding treatment background, therapeutic regimens, cancer types, and clinical characteristics could not be fully eliminated. These factors may partly explain the modest fluctuations in AUC observed during external validation. Therefore, the present study should be viewed primarily as an exploratory effort to assess the potential value of candidate genes for constructing an immunotherapy response prediction model. In future work, we plan to establish a large colorectal cancer cohort treated with immunotherapy and combine systematic transcriptomic profiling, complete clinical treatment information, and experimental validation to further refine the model and develop a more applicable tool for predicting immunotherapy response in colorectal cancer patients.

In addition, although the four analytical dimensions showed generally consistent trends, some patients still exhibited intermediate or discordant classification patterns, indicating that the current framework requires further optimization. Future studies should incorporate more comprehensive clinical and experimental data to better integrate these dimensions and establish a more unified, stable, and clinically interpretable decision-making framework.

Finally, single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses suggested that RCD2-associated tumor cells were more spatially dispersed and might exhibit spatial exclusion from T cells. However, because of the limited resolution of spatial transcriptomics and the absence of in vivo validation, the functional role of FSTL3 in the native tumor immune microenvironment remains to be directly demonstrated. Although our in vitro experiments indicated that FSTL3 regulates CRC cell malignant phenotypes and apoptosis-related cell death, and IHC and transcriptomic analyses suggested a potential association between FSTL3 expression, reduced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, and altered antigen presentation, the current evidence remains insufficient to define FSTL3 as a key actionable target in vivo. Future studies should incorporate multiplex immunofluorescence, mouse models, and mechanistic experiments to validate the spatial relationship between FSTL3-high tumor cells and CD8⁺ T cells and to clarify whether FSTL3 directly contributes to immune evasion in the tumor microenvironment.
