Healthcare utilisation patterns for paediatric functional somatic symptom presentation: a 3-year retrospective analysis
Authors: Dhiya Mahirah, Hong Choon Oh, Jane Lim, Jia Le Lee, Vicknesan Jeyan Marimuttu, Sharon Cohan Sung, Helen Yu Chen, Ngiap Chuan Tan, Angelina Su Yin Ang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren and young persons frequently present to Emergency Departments (EDs) with physical symptoms lacking a clear medical cause, often reflecting underlying psychosocial distress. Such presentations are associated with repeated healthcare use, specialist referrals, and avoidable costs, yet longitudinal evidence on healthcare trajectories and cost patterns across care settings remains limited, particularly in Asian contexts. This study characterised three-year healthcare utilisation and associated costs among children and young persons presenting with possible functional somatic symptoms to a paediatric ED in Singapore, and identified factors associated with recurrent ED use.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using de-identified electronic medical records from a tertiary paediatric hospital. Patients aged 6–15 years who presented to the ED in 2018 with predefined indicators of functional somatic symptom were followed for three years from their index visit (2018 to 2021), with annual tracking of ED, inpatient, and specialist outpatient care (SOC) use. Descriptive analyses summarised healthcare use and costs, and logistic regression examined factors associated with recurrent ED visits beyond Year 1.ResultsAmong 4,964 unique patients, ED re-visits declined by 84.5% and total healthcare costs dropped by 75.6% from Year 1 to Year 3. While most patients in Year 1 were treated and discharged (39.9%), SOC referrals (31.1% in Year 2; 35.6% in Year 3) and inpatient admissions (26.4% in Year 2; 30.7% in Year 3) remained substantial in the following years. Younger age, minority ethnicity, and initial inpatient admission were associated with higher odds of recurrent ED visits beyond Year 1.ConclusionDespite declining ED re-visits over time, persistent specialist outpatient and inpatient use suggests unresolved psychosocial needs in a subset of patients. Younger age, minority ethnicity, and initial inpatient admission identified subgroups at higher risk of recurrent ED use. These findings support the integration of systematic psychosocial screening and culturally responsive referral pathways across care settings to reduce avoidable healthcare utilisation and address underlying distress in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14996-z.Keywords: Functional somatic symptoms, Adolescent health services; Emergency services, Hospital; Electronic health records

Introduction

Children and young persons (CYPs) commonly present to healthcare settings with physical complaints that may lack an identifiable medical cause at the point of care [1]. These presentations are often described as functional somatic symptoms, a broad term encompassing physical complaints like headache, abdominal pain, or chest pain, that may arise from a range of underlying mechanisms, including psychosocial or emotional factors [2, 3].

Functional somatic symptoms are common in paediatric settings, with estimates from international studies suggesting a prevalence of about one third of individuals under 19 [2–4]. While formal diagnostic criteria for somatic symptom and related disorders are well established in DSM and ICD classifications, such diagnoses typically require comprehensive clinical assessment over time. Conversely, defining functional somatic symptoms is challenging due to the absence of universally accepted diagnostic criteria and the heterogeneity of clinical presentations [5, 6]. However, there is growing consensus that these functional somatic symptoms often arise from psychosocial distress [7–9]. In acute care settings such as emergency departments (EDs), where assessments focus on immediate symptom evaluation, these presentations may represent a heterogenous group of patients, including those with underlying psychosocial distress, as well as undiagnosed or transient symptoms. In such circumstances, clinicians often record symptoms as diagnostic labels in the patient’s medical record, but may not explicitly frame the symptoms as “possible functional somatic symptoms” [10].

Such presentations are not only distressing for CYPs and families but also strain the healthcare system. In the absence of a clearly identifiable organic pathology at the point of presentation, these cases may result in repeated ED visits, specialist referrals, and extensive diagnostic investigations, contributing to potentially avoidable healthcare utilisation and costs [5, 11]. In Singapore, a qualitative study found that caregivers frequently choose the paediatric ED due to concerns about symptom severity and convenience, even when symptoms are non-urgent, reflecting complex decision-making influenced by service accessibility and lack of confidence in primary care [6]. Moreover, local community data suggest a rise in functional somatic complaints among CYPs [12], with recent findings indicating that nearly one in three adolescents report such symptoms [8].

Despite increasing recognition of functional somatic symptoms in paediatrics, most studies on their impact on healthcare have been conducted in Western contexts. There remains limited longitudinal data from Asian settings, where differing cultural norms surrounding emotional expression and health-seeking behaviours may influence how psychological distress manifests and is managed [9, 13]. The ED may serve as an initial point of contact for CYPs with undiagnosed or unaddressed mental health issues [14]. In fast-paced emergency settings, psychosocial contributors to physical symptoms may be under-recognised, as clinicians may prioritise medical explanations over psychosomatic interpretations when assessing adolescents presenting with such symptoms [10], likely reflecting time constraints, limited training, and the prioritisation of physical symptom evaluation in acute care environments [15]. These observations highlight the importance of early detection and timely intervention to prevent worsening of underlying mental health issues and ensure appropriate support [10, 16].

Singapore’s integrated public healthcare system, with administrative datasets that can capture patient care across emergency, inpatient and outpatient settings within institutions, provides a unique opportunity to examine these trajectories at the patient level. While frequent attenders in EDs are known to place disproportionate strain on hospital resources, local evidence indicates that this group includes younger individuals with low-acuity complaints [17]. However, little is known about the healthcare trajectories of CYPs with possible functional somatic symptoms, a group that may follow distinct patterns of service use. Gaining insight into their healthcare utilisation across multiple settings could offer valuable guidance for more responsive and coordinated care. In this study, we focus on CYPs presenting with possible functional somatic symptoms identified using administrative diagnostic codes at the point of ED care. These presentations do not necessarily correspond to formally diagnosed somatic symptom or related disorders, but reflect symptom-based encounters in routine clinical practice.

This study aims to characterise the longitudinal healthcare utilisation patterns and associated costs among CYPs presenting to a paediatric emergency department with possible functional somatic symptoms in Singapore. The primary objective of this retrospective cohort study is to describe annual healthcare utilisation across emergency, inpatient, and specialist outpatient settings, and associated costs, among CYPs aged 6 to 15 years who presented to a paediatric ED with possible functional somatic symptoms over a three-year follow-up period. The secondary objective is to identify demographic and clinical factors associated with recurrent ED visits beyond one year following the index presentation.

Methods

Study setting and population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of CYPs who presented to the Emergency Department (ED) at KK Women’s and Children’s Hospital (KKH). The ED, which treats approximately 150,000 patients annually, is the largest tertiary paediatric ED in Singapore that provides emergency care for patients under 18 years of age. It is located within an 830-bedded public hospital specialising in healthcare for women and children in Singapore. This study used de-identified and linked electronic medical records (EMRs) covering the period from 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2021.

Cohort creation

The study cohort comprised CYPs aged 6 to 15 years who were Singapore Citizens or Permanent Residents and had an ED visit in 2018 for possible functional somatic symptoms. This age range allowed observation of healthcare trajectories over three years, with the oldest participants reaching 18 years of age by the end of follow-up. Possible functional somatic symptoms were identified using a pre-defined list of 424 SNOMED diagnostic codes (Supplementary File 1). These codes were refined through a structured survey of paediatric emergency physicians at KKH, who reviewed the diagnostic categories and reached a consensus on which codes reflected presentations without clear organic causes. For each patient, the index visit was defined as the first ED visit between 1 January and 31 December 2018 with a diagnosis code corresponding to this list.

Following this initial selection, an additional screening step was conducted to improve the specificity of the cohort. Patients were excluded if, within 90 days following the index visit, they had a visit to the ED or Specialist Outpatient Clinic (SOC) or were hospitalised with a diagnosis indicative of acute illness, chronic illness, or major trauma. These diagnostic categories were identified through a clinical review process in which clinicians examined the structured data of SNOMED diagnosis codes, problem descriptions, and problem summaries. This step ensured that the final cohort more accurately reflected presentations consistent with functional somatic symptoms, rather than early presentations of serious illnesses or trauma-related conditions. Further details of this clinical evaluation process are provided in Supplementary File 2.

Study variables

ED visit characteristics

Information extracted from each ED visit included the date and time of attendance, along with the SNOMED diagnosis code and description. All diagnosis codes were categorised into one of four mutually exclusive groups: (1) possible functional somatic symptoms (Appendix A), including presentations such as abdominal pain, chest pain, and headache; (2) mild illnesses (Appendix B), such as respiratory or gastrointestinal conditions; (3) mental, behavioural or psychosocial conditions (Appendix C), including mood disorders, anxiety, and self-harm; and (4) other conditions not falling within the previous categories. Appendices A-C are available in Supplementary File 1. Each ED visit was also assigned a final disposition, indicating whether the patient was treated and discharged, admitted, referred to a SOC, referred to a non-SOC (e.g. primary care doctors or other government hospital) or categorised under other outcomes (e.g. absconded or left against medical advice). Demographic variables included age at index visit, sex, and ethnicity.

Healthcare utilisation variables

Healthcare utilisation data were obtained from KKH’s electronic medical records system, which integrates administrative records across emergency department, inpatient, and specialist outpatient services. These records were linked at the patient level using unique patient identifiers, allowing longitudinal tracking of healthcare utilisation across care settings. Healthcare utilisation was assessed by the number of ED attendances, SOC visits, inpatient admissions, and total healthcare costs incurred during each year of the study period. Financial charges were based on the gross bill charged to the patient before any government subsidies or third-party payments (e.g., from insurers or employers), and were presented in Singapore dollars (S$). SOC visits were defined as outpatient specialist consultations within KKH and included internal referrals from the ED, inpatient wards, and other SOC, as well as external referrals from both public and private healthcare organisations. For each patient, healthcare utilisation and cost data were aggregated annually over the three-year follow-up period.

The primary outcome was any ED revisit occurring more than one year after the index visit. Patients were categorised based on the presence or absence of such recurrent ED visits. This threshold was selected to distinguish between short-term re-attendances from longer-term patterns of ED use, which may suggest underlying psychosocial needs that require follow-up beyond acute care settings.

Statistical analysis

Healthcare utilisation and cost data were summarised annually for up to three years following the index ED visit. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise baseline characteristics, healthcare use, and costs. Age was analysed as a continuous variable in regression models. For descriptive purposes and visualisation, age was grouped into 6–9 years and 10–15 years, broadly corresponding to child and adolescent developmental stages. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models identified factors associated with ED revisits beyond one year. Results from regression analyses were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R version (2024.04.0 + 735).

Healthcare utilisation trends and cost analysis

Table 2 summarises healthcare utilisation over the three-year follow-up period. ED use declined substantially after Year 1, with visits decreasing by 85.0% in Year 3. Possible functional somatic symptoms made up the majority of presentations in Year 1, reflecting the inclusion criteria used to define the study cohort, in which all index visits involved such symptoms. In subsequent years, this proportion among CYPs who returned in Year 2 and/or Year 3 declined substantially, with a relative increase in visits for mild illnesses and psychosocial presentations. Although most patients were treated and discharged throughout the years, a larger proportion were referred to SOCs by Year 3. Compared to the inpatient admission rate of 31.1% in Year 1, the inpatient admission rates among CYPs who returned to ED declined to 26.4% in Year 2 but returned to near-initial levels (30.7%) in Year 3.

Inpatient and SOC utilisation decreased over the three-year follow-up period. Inpatient admissions dropped by 85.4%, with a corresponding decrease in unique patients (from 1,910 to 260). Total inpatient length of stay followed a similar trend. Although SOC visits declined, the decrease was less pronounced, with a 45.2% drop by Year 3. Overall, the decline in SOC visits was less pronounced than for inpatient or ED services throughout the follow-up period.

Total healthcare costs decreased substantially after the first year (Fig. 2). In Year 1, the total cost across all service types was approximately $10.99 million, which dropped by 72% to $3.09 million in Year 2, followed by a further 13% reduction to $2.68 million in Year 3. Inpatient costs alone decreased by 77% from Year 1 to Year 2. ED cost also declined markedly (approximately 76%), while SOC costs were more modest (approximately 46%) over the same period. Across all years, inpatient care accounted for the largest proportion of total costs, followed by SOC and ED services.

Patients with recurrent ED visits had higher cumulative healthcare utilisation across all care settings over the 3-year follow-up period (Table 3). They accounted for 6,120 ED visits, compared to 4,726 visits among those with ED use in Year 1 only. While both groups had similar proportions of inpatient admissions from ED (30.5% vs. 30.0%) and discharges (37.9% vs. 40.3%), recurrent users had a higher proportion of psychosocial presentations (2.1% vs. 0.4%). Additionally, recurrent users had more inpatient admissions (1,882 vs. 1,435), longer total length of stay (5,198 vs. 3,011 days), and more SOC visits (9,491 vs. 8,664) over the study period. Average costs were higher across all service types for patients with recurrent ED visits.

As shown in Table 3, patients with recurrent ED use demonstrated higher healthcare utilisation across all service settings, with greater mean ED visits per patient (4.09 vs. 1.36), inpatient admissions per patient (2.17 vs. 1.15), and SOC visits per patient (8.07 vs. 4.97) compared to those with ED use limited to Year 1. Median inpatient length of stay per patient was slightly higher among recurrent users (3 [2–6] days vs. 2 [1–3] days). Correspondingly, the mean costs per patient were also higher across all service types among recurrent users: ED ($1,128 vs. $359), inpatient ($4,336 vs. $1,156), and SOC ($1,149 vs. $467). Inpatient care accounts for the highest cost in both groups, with a higher mean cost per visit observed in recurrent users ($3,446 vs. $2,794).

Factors associated with recurrent ED use

Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with ED revisits beyond Year 1 (Table 5). In the multivariable model, younger age was significantly associated with higher odds of recurrent ED use (adjusted OR [aOR]: 0.90; 95% CI: 0.88–0.92; p < 0.001). Compared to Chinese patients, Malay (aOR: 1.66; 95% CI: 1.42–1.93) and Indian (aOR: 1.75; 95% CI: 1.48–2.07) patients had significantly higher odds of recurrence. Additionally, patients admitted during their index ED visit were more likely to have recurrent visits (aOR: 1.19; 95% CI: 1.03–1.39; p = 0.021), compared to those treated and discharged. Sex and presenting symptom group were not significantly associated with recurrence in the adjusted model. Multicollinearity diagnostics showed no issues, with all variance inflation factors (VIFs) below 1.1.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study examined healthcare utilisation patterns among CYPs presenting to a paediatric ED in Singapore with possible functional somatic symptoms. We found that about one-third of the participants had recurrent ED visits beyond the first year, and these patients incurred higher cumulative healthcare utilisation and costs over three years. Younger age, minority ethnicity, and inpatient admission during the index ED visit were significantly associated with increased odds of recurrent ED use.

Our finding that younger children have higher odds of recurrent ED use contrasts with evidence from Singapore primary care settings, where frequent attendance was associated with older adolescent age groups [19]. This may reflect differences in care-seeking patterns across healthcare settings and developmental stages. Caregivers, as primary decision makers for younger children’s healthcare, may bring them to the ED early during symptom onset, especially when seeking reassurance or when primary care is perceived as less accessible or less capable of managing paediatric cases [6]. Furthermore, the 24-hour accessibility may encourage caregivers to use paediatric EDs as a first point of contact [6, 20]. In contrast, older adolescents have sufficient cognitive maturity to understand when clinicians explain that their symptoms are not medically serious, which may reduce repeated ED visits for similar complaints [21, 22]. Their ability to describe their symptoms more clearly may also lessen caregiver anxiety and shift follow-up to primary care or SOC rather than returning to the ED. However, as we did not have access to healthcare utilisation prior to the index visit, it is not possible to determine whether younger patients represent a distinct subgroup with higher healthcare needs or an earlier stage along a healthcare utilisation trajectory. Nonetheless, whether these patterns among younger children reflect subgroup differences or earlier stages of care engagement, they underscore the importance of timely identification and appropriate care pathways at initial presentation.

In contrast, adolescents aged 16 years and above may attend general EDs instead of paediatric EDs due to convenience, accessibility, and eligibility for adult services. For example, individuals aged 16–25 years accounted for a disproportionately high share of frequent attendances at a general ED in Singapore, often for low acuity conditions [17]. These factors suggest that older adolescents with recurrent functional somatic complaints may be underrepresented in our paediatric ED data due to these alternative care-seeking pathways. Similar age-related utilisation patterns have been reported in other high-income settings, where frequent paediatric ED use is most common among younger children and declines through adolescence [23, 24]. Thus, the lower odds of recurrent ED use among older adolescents in our cohort may reflect a redistribution of healthcare utilisation rather than lower symptom burden. These findings may reflect evolving patterns of care engagement across healthcare settings with age and highlight the importance of tracking service use across the wider healthcare system.

We observed ethnic differences in healthcare utilisation, with Malay and Indian ethnicities associated with higher odds of recurrent ED visits. This contrasts with earlier studies from the same paediatric ED, which found higher 72-hour reattendance rates among Chinese children [25, 26]. However, Goh et al. [25] also reported that Malay children were more likely to be admitted upon reattendance, suggesting differences in illness severity, help-seeking timing, or unmet needs. Our study, in contrast, focused on children presenting with possible functional somatic symptoms and examined recurrent ED use over a longer time period. These differences in study population, outcome definitions, and follow-up period may explain the divergent findings and indicate that ethnic disparities could be more evident over extended timeframes. While ethnicity is not inherently causal, it often reflects broader predisposing and enabling factors that influence healthcare access, perceived need and utilisation patterns [27, 28]. Other studies have also reported disparities in emergency care use among ethnic minority youths [29, 30]. These may stem from systemic factors, including reduced access to culturally responsive care, stigma or differences in referral pathways into the healthcare system, reflecting unmet mental health needs [31, 32]. Although specific characteristics cannot be attributed to each ethnic group, the consistent association between ethnicity and recurrent ED use in our cohort highlights the need to better understand how health and social systems interact with cultural norms and lived experiences to shape long-term care trajectories and support more equitable and responsive service delivery.

High tertiary healthcare utilisation for functional somatic symptoms and recurrent ED use beyond one year, with substantially high cumulative healthcare costs across all care settings over three years, may reflect underlying psychosocial needs rather than physical illness alone. This healthcare utilisation pattern is consistent with evidence that functional somatic symptom presentations in CYPs often reflect unresolved psychosocial distress rather than discrete, self-limiting physical illness [33, 34]. When underlying emotional and social determinants remain unaddressed, care-seeking may shift across settings rather than resolve, resulting in continued system burden without meaningful improvement in the young person’s well-being [11]. This trajectory aligns with the World Health Organization’s position that health is a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being, and that fragmented, disease-focused care systems are less well-equipped to address the complex, interacting determinants that drive persistent health service use among CYPs [35]. A coordinated, biopsychosocial approach — integrating mental health, social support, and physical care across settings — has been associated with reduced healthcare utilisation and improved functional outcomes in young people with functional somatic symptoms [34, 36]. These findings collectively reinforce the need to move beyond episodic, symptom-focused management in the ED toward proactive, multi-disciplinary care models that address psychosocial determinants of health at the individual, family, and system level.

The absence of an explicit aetiological diagnosis may also influence care-seeking behaviour. As this cohort was defined using administrative diagnostic data at the point of presentation, it likely represents a heterogeneous group of patients, including those with underlying psychosocial distress as well as those with low-acuity medical conditions, rather than individuals with formally diagnosed somatic symptom or related disorders, which typically require more comprehensive clinical assessment. In the context of COVID-19 pandemic-related shifts in paediatric emergency care and trends in ED utilisation for functional somatic symptoms, these findings may also reflect a broader pattern of co-occurring functional somatic and psychiatric presentations [8, 11, 37, 38]. This highlights an opportunity to shift from reactive care to more proactive management: identifying and addressing psychosocial distress at the first point of contact may help reduce recurrent ED use and minimise longer-term healthcare burdens [15, 39, 40].

In Singapore, the Youth Well-Being (YWB) questionnaire – introduced in 2020 for use at KKH’s ED – provides a contextually adapted, self-administered tool to support early detection of psychosocial concerns among CYPs who may initially present with functional somatic symptoms [39]. Complementary initiatives within KKH, such as the Resilience in Children and Adolescents with Psychosomatic Symptoms (RECAP) programme [41] and the Youth Connect Associates [42, 43], provide structured assessment and intervention for youths with psychosomatic symptoms or psychosocial needs. However, these programmes remain localised to KKH, and similar approaches are not implemented consistently across the wider healthcare system. Similar efforts have been implemented in other countries, where structured screening tools have shown promise in supporting psychosocial screening, clinical decision-making, and follow-up care in paediatric ED settings [44–46]. Such approaches should also be extended to other healthcare settings, including polyclinics, where older adolescents—who may not return to the ED—often present with recurrent complaints [19]. Screening tools can help detect evolving psychosocial needs and initiate referrals, reducing escalation into repeated healthcare use driven by unresolved psychological distress [47].

Raising awareness of functional somatic symptoms among healthcare providers, caregivers, and young people themselves is crucial to ensure these presentations are recognised and properly managed. In paediatric emergency settings, time pressures, limited training in psychosocial assessments, and absence of formal guidelines can cause providers to overlook emotional distress, especially when it presents as physical complaints [48, 49]. CYPs may also struggle to recognise or express their emotions, due to limited self-awareness, abstract reasoning, or verbal skills [50, 51], while caregivers’ mental health literacy, cultural beliefs, and prior service experience shape how symptoms are interpreted and whether care is sought [52–55]. Without timely recognition, these functional somatic presentations risk being missed, resulting in missed opportunities for early intervention.

To improve care for CYPs with functional somatic symptoms, stronger emphasis is needed on early identification, consistent care pathways and continuity across care settings. Clinical guidelines and structured care pathways, such as those developed for somatic symptoms and related disorders in other paediatric hospital settings, can standardise assessment, reduce unnecessary investigation and promote functional recovery through rehabilitative approaches, while emphasising collaborative input from behavioural health providers and clear communication with families [34, 56–58]. Implementation requires training and capacity building - for providers to develop psychosocial assessment skills, engage families effectively, and make appropriate referrals; and for caregivers to understand and respond to emotional distress that may present physically [34, 56]. Embedding these strategies across settings, including primary care, schools and other touchpoints, ensures responsiveness as CYPs move through developmental stages and service systems [36, 59–61]. Sustained impact will require governance-level support, through policy mandates, institutional quality standards and investments in integrated, youth-centred models of care [62, 63].

Strengths and limitations

This study makes several important contributions to adolescent health services research in Singapore. By utilising KKH’s integrated EMR system, it offers a detailed, patient-level analysis of healthcare utilisation across emergency, inpatient, and outpatient specialist settings over a three-year period. The cohort was carefully defined using clinically reviewed diagnostic codes and exclusion criteria to enhance specificity in identifying possible functional somatic presentations in a paediatric population. To our knowledge, this is the first local study to describe longitudinal care patterns and associated costs among CYPs presenting to ED with possible functional somatic symptoms, addressing a gap in health service planning for the younger population.

Nonetheless, some limitations must be considered. Case identification relied on diagnostic codes rather than clinical assessments, which could have led to potential misclassification despite efforts to improve specificity through code review and exclusion criteria. For example, the clinical relatedness between subsequent presentations within 90 days and the index visit relied solely on clinician comparison of diagnoses from the two healthcare episodes based on the structured format of administrative data, without detailed examination of the full details of each healthcare utilisation episode in the electronic medical records. Since the data was limited to a single tertiary hospital, healthcare utilisation outside KKH was not captured, potentially underestimating the full scope of care received by this population. We also predetermined the recurrent ED use as visits after one year for ease of analysis – it is possible that re-attendances related to psychosocial factors could have been shorter than one year. As a retrospective cohort study, this analysis identifies associations but cannot establish causal relationships between individual or visit-level factors and recurrent ED usage. Furthermore, the lack of pre-index healthcare utilisation data limits our ability to distinguish whether observed patterns of recurrent ED use reflect distinct patient subgroups or different stages along a healthcare utilisation trajectory. Additionally, including the COVID-19 pandemic years (2020–2021) may have influenced healthcare utilisation patterns. While nationwide public health measures were associated with reductions in paediatric ED attendances and admissions at KKH [64], another Singapore study reported increased demand for mental health and social services during the pandemic period [65]. These contextual factors may have influenced both care-seeking behaviour and patterns of recurrent utilisation observed in this study. Future research should extend the study period to examine post-pandemic trends and consider linking datasets across paediatric and adult ED at multiple tertiary hospitals to better track older adolescents who may transition out of paediatric care [66]. These findings should also be interpreted within the context of Singapore’s healthcare system and demographic profile, which may limit generalisability to other health systems organised differently.

Conclusion

This study provides new evidence on the longitudinal healthcare utilisation patterns and associated costs among children and young persons presenting to a paediatric ED with possible functional somatic symptoms in Singapore. Approximately one in three patients had recurrent ED visits beyond the first year, and these patients incurred substantially higher cumulative healthcare costs across all care settings. Younger age, minority ethnicity, and inpatient admission at the index visit were independently associated with recurrent ED use, highlighting subgroups that may benefit from targeted early intervention. Persistent utilisation of tertiary healthcare services over three years suggests that underlying psychosocial needs may remain unaddressed for many. These findings underscore the need for systematic psychosocial screening, culturally responsive care pathways, and stronger cross-setting coordination — from the ED to primary care and community settings — to reduce avoidable healthcare utilisation and support the well-being of this population. Future research should extend these findings beyond a single tertiary centre and examine post-pandemic trends to better inform integrated, youth-centred models of care in Singapore and comparable healthcare systems.
