Greater Japanese horseshoe bats (Rhinolophus nippon) gradually converge their echolocation call frequency to colony members
Authors: Haruhito Matsumoto, Soshi Yoshida, Shizuko Hiryu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In bats using CF-FM echolocation calls, CF component frequencies vary among colonies, across geographic regions, and between sexes. In this study, we investigated frequency changes of the second harmonic of the CF component (CF2), which is the dominant harmonic in the multi-harmonic echolocation calls, that occur following the mixed housing of resident and wild-caught populations of greater Japanese horseshoe bats (Rhinolophus nippon). By focusing on individual-level frequency adjustments in the context of colony mixing, we tracked CF2 frequencies in a total of 101 individuals across 15 capture events over a one-month period. CF2 frequencies initially differed between the resident and wild-caught populations. These intergroup frequency differences gradually decreased after mixing, accompanied by a reduction in overall variability within the colony. This convergence was achieved asymmetrically, such that bats with lower CF2 frequencies adjusted their frequencies upward, while bats with higher CF2 frequencies individuals showed little or no change. No systematic changes were observed when initial frequencies did not differ between two groups. Echolocation in group contexts would be expected to suffer from severe acoustic interference caused by overlapping calls from conspecifics; however, CF bats instead tend to converge on a shared CF frequency. Because this convergence was primarily driven by individuals using lower frequencies, it may be interpreted as a strategy to allow individuals to share a “silent spectral window” above the CF2 frequency, which is critical for Doppler-based detection. In other words, for CF bats, in which the use of Doppler information is fundamentally important, frequency convergence may function as a strategy for avoiding acoustic interference.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00359-026-01821-5.

Introduction

For survival, animals must accurately perceive their external environment. While most species rely primarily on passive sensory modalities, bats employ echolocation as an active sensing strategy: they actively emit ultrasonic signals and process the resulting echoes (Donald 1958). For animals that engage in such active sensing, the nature of the emitted signals is a crucial factor that directly determines the information available to them. The majority of bats emit frequency-modulated (FM) pulses, whereas some groups, Rhinolophidae and Hipposideridae, together with Pteronotus parnellii, employ unique CF–FM pulses composed of both constant-frequency (CF) and FM components (Schnitzler and Kalko 2001; Jones and Teeling 2006). This echolocation strategy, characterized by the presence of CF components and high-duty-cycle vocalizations, is generally thought to represent an adaptation for detecting instantaneous Doppler shifts produced by prey wingbeats during foraging, commonly referred to as glints (Schnitzler and Denzinger 2011; Fenton et al. 2012). In these bats, the auditory system exhibits a specialization known as the acoustic fovea, present across the auditory pathway and characterized by exceptionally high sensitivity to a narrow frequency band centered around the second harmonic CF component (CF2) of the echolocation call (Grinnell 1970; Suga and Jen 1976; Neuweiler 1990). During flight, echoes returning to the bat are shifted upward in frequency due to the Doppler effect. To compensate for this shift, CF bats are known to lower the frequency of their emitted pulses in advance according to their flight speed, thereby maintaining the frequency of the returning echoes at a constant reference value, a behavior known as Doppler shift compensation (DSC) (Schnitzler 1968, 1973; Schnitzler and Denzinger 2011; Hiryu et al. 2016; Schoeppler et al. 2018). By doing so, bats actively control their vocal output such that the CF2 echo consistently falls within the acoustic fovea.

In CF-FM bat echolocation, the CF2 frequency of calls emitted while bats are stationary is defined as the resting frequency. Although the resting CF2 frequency is considered essential for CF bat echolocation, previous studies have reported considerable inter-individual variation within species, as well as differences across regions and colonies. At the level of individual variation, such differences have been discussed in relation to body size and sex, and regional or colony-specific differences have been attributed to genetic and geographic factors, as well as social transmission between mothers and offspring (Suga et al. 1987; Jones et al. 1992; Jones 1999; Guillén et al. 2000; Sun et al. 2013). On the other hand, it has also been reported that CF2 frequency can exhibit gradual yet continuous changes over several months within the same individual, and may further vary across an individual’s lifetime or on a seasonal basis (Suga et al. 1987; Gaioni et al. 1990; Jones and Ransome 1993; Hiryu et al. 2006; Furusawa et al. 2012; Bastian and Jacobs 2015). This fact implies that the CF2 frequency is not fixed at a single frequency within each individual, but rather flexibly adjusted according to the situation. Furthermore, it can be interpreted that each colony uses a different frequency as a result of individual frequency adjustments within each colony.

Therefore, in this study, we investigate individual-level frequency adjustments within colonies by monitoring changes in the resting CF2 frequency over the medium to long term in bats newly introduced into established captive colonies. Previous studies have long documented that CF2 frequencies differ among colonies in CF bats, and potential explanations for this variation have primarily focused on social and genetic factors, typically treating colonies separately and sampling at discrete time points (Yoshino et al. 2008; Sun et al. 2013; Xie et al. 2017). In contrast, studies that have intentionally mixed conspecific populations and examined changes in individual CF2 frequencies under such conditions are scarce, with only a few reports from captive Hipposideros (Hiryu et al. 2006; Chen et al. 2016). This study examines temporal changes in CF2 frequency in both resident and newly introduced individuals during the one-month period after the introduction of wild-caught bats into a captive colony of greater Japanese horseshoe bats, with the aim of elucidating the mechanisms underlying CF2 frequency determination and its functional significance for acoustic sensing.

Methods

Study species and housing conditions

The study species was the greater Japanese horseshoe bats (Rhinolophus nippon), which emits ultrasonic pulses consisting of a CF component followed by short FM components (Fig. 1a). A total of 15 capture events conducted between March 2008 and January 2009, August 2010 and September 2012, and September 2020 and May 2024 were included in the analysis. In this study, we analyzed data from individuals collected from cave colonies in Mikazuki, Minoh, and Inagawa (Hyogo Prefecture), Nose (Osaka Prefecture), and Tsuruga and Sabae (Fukui Prefecture), Japan. During each capture event, 3–11 individuals were captured (Fig. 1b; Table 1).

The bats were housed in a temperature- and humidity-controlled colony room (3 m × 4 m × 2 m) at Doshisha University (Kyoto, Japan). Newly captured individuals were housed in a cage (0.10 m × 0.15 m × 0.15 m) and provided with mealworms and water ad libitum until they were fully acclimated and trained to feed in captivity. The cages were placed inside the colony room, which already contained a resident colony of bats that were not caged and were able to fly freely within the room (The resident bats were also originally captured individuals at earlier time points. As shown in Fig. 1b, capture events were conducted approximately every six months or one year; thus, the resident bats had been maintained in captivity for at least six months). Once acclimation was complete, the newly captured bats were released into the colony room and allowed to join the resident group. After release, they were also able to fly freely and had continuous access to food and water. The room was maintained under a 12 h light : 12 h dark cycle.

All capture and handling procedures were conducted under permits and in accordance with Japanese wildlife protection laws. Permits to capture bats for research were obtained from Osaka, Hyogo, and Fukui Prefectures, where the capture sites were located. A license to maintain bats was obtained from Kyotanabe City, where the university is situated. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee of Doshisha University, and all necessary permissions were secured prior to the start of the study.

Acoustic recordings

Vocalizations of both resident and newly captured bats were recorded over a 30-day period following each capture event. Recordings were conducted every 1–7 days during the first week after capture, and subsequently once every 2–7 days. The number of recording sessions over the 30-day period ranged from 4 to 25 (mean ± SD: 8.6 ± 7.6). During each session, a single bat was placed in an isolated cage, and stationary vocalizations were recorded while the bat was at rest for more than 5 s.

For recordings conducted between 2008 and 2012, a microphone (Type 4939, Brüel & Kjær, Nærum, Denmark) was positioned approximately 10 cm from the bat. The output signal was amplified using a conditioning amplifier (Type 5935 L, Brüel & Kjær, Nærum, Denmark) and passed through a high-pass filter (Model 3625, NF Corporation, Yokohama, Japan; 20 kHz cutoff). The filtered signal was then digitized using a digital audio tape recorder (SIR-1000 W, Sony Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) at 16-bit resolution and a sampling rate of 384 kHz. For recordings conducted between 2020 and 2024, vocalizations were recorded using a microphone (CM16, Avisoft Bioacoustics, Berlin, Germany) positioned approximately 30 cm from the bat. Signals were digitized using an ultrasound recording interface (UltraSoundGate 116, Avisoft Bioacoustics, Berlin, Germany) at a sampling rate of 500 kHz.

To examine whether CF2 frequency could temporarily change due to stress associated with post-capture transport to the laboratory, we measured the CF2 frequency of captured bats inside the cave immediately after capture on the day of capture. Measurements were conducted on two individuals on 26 May 2025 and on six individuals on 23 September 2025 in Sabae City, Fukui Prefecture, Japan. To minimize stress to the bats during measurement, each bat was kept inside a cloth bag, and vocalizations were recorded using a microphone (AudioMoth, Open Acoustic Devices) positioned 50 cm from the bat.

Acoustic analysis

From each daily recording, ten pulses were randomly selected for analysis; if the number of pulses was between five and nine, five pulses were randomly selected. Individuals with fewer than five detectable pulses in a given recording session were excluded from analysis. This threshold was chosen to maximize the number of usable recordings, given that some individuals produced only a limited number of pulses during stationary recordings, while still ensuring reliable estimation of CF frequency. For each selected pulse, the CF2 frequency was measured using a custom-written analysis program in MATLAB 2024a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) with a frequency resolution of 20 Hz. The median CF2 value of the selected pulses was defined as the daily CF2 frequency for that individual. Data from 15 capture events were screened. Individuals for which CF2 frequencies could not be obtained on at least two separate days within one month post-capture, as well as individuals that died during the recording period, were excluded. The final dataset comprised 177 individuals, including 86 captured bats (excluding 7 that died and 2 with incomplete measurements) and 91 resident bats (excluding 14 that died and 8 with incomplete measurements). Because many individuals appeared as both captured and resident bats across different events, the total number of unique individuals was 101.

Statistical analysis

The following statistical analyses were performed using custom-written scripts in MATLAB R2024a. Normality was assessed using the Lilliefors test (lillietest). For each capture event, differences in CF2 frequency between the captured and resident groups on the first and last measurement days were tested using statistical procedures selected according to sample size. When the sample size was fewer than eight individuals, a Wilcoxon rank-sum test (ranksum) was used. For sample sizes of eight or more individuals, Welch’s two-sample t-test was applied when normality was confirmed for both groups; otherwise, a Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used.

Within each group (resident and captured), changes in CF2 frequency between the first and the last measurement days were analyzed using statistical tests selected according to sample size. When the number of individuals was two or fewer, no statistical test was performed. For sample sizes of three to five individuals, a paired permutation test was used. For six or more individuals, either a paired t-test or a Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used, depending on normality. In the resident group, five events were excluded due to insufficient sample size (≤ 2 individuals); the remaining events were analyzed using permutation tests, paired t-tests, or Wilcoxon tests, as appropriate. The same criteria were applied to the captured group.

Results

Among the 15 capture events analyzed, on the day of first measurement, the mean CF2 frequency of the captured group was significantly lower than that of the resident group in seven events (t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for small sample sizes; Fig. S1), whereas no significant difference was detected in eight events (t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for small sample sizes; Fig. S2). There were no events in which the mean CF2 frequency of the captured group was significantly higher than that of the resident group (Table 1).

An example of a capture event in which the mean CF2 frequency of the captured group was significantly lower than that of the resident group on the day of first measurement is shown in Fig. 2a. Comparison of the mean CF2 frequencies between the first measurement day following capture and day 30 revealed a significant increase in CF2 frequency in both the resident and captured groups (paired t-test, p < 0.001 for residents; paired t-test, p < 0.001 for captured individuals). While the mean CF2 frequency of the resident group increased at a rate of approximately 10 Hz per day, the captured group showed a steeper increase of approximately 29 Hz per day, resulting in a larger increase in the captured group. By day 30, the mean CF2 frequencies of the captured and resident groups converged to 68.0 5 ± 0.45 kHz and 68.18 ± 0.14 kHz, respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups (t-test, p = 0.41). Across all seven events in which the captured group initially exhibited a significantly lower mean CF2 frequency, the difference in mean CF2 frequency between captured and resident groups decreased over time. In six of these seven events, no significant difference in mean CF2 frequency was observed between the two groups on the last measurement day (Fig. 2c; Table 1). Together, these results suggest that following capture events, CF2 frequencies within the colony were adjusted in two ways: first, the CF2 frequencies of captured and resident groups converged toward each other, and second, the overall CF2 frequency of the colony shifted toward higher values.

In contrast, an example in which no significant difference in mean CF2 frequency was observed between the captured and resident groups on the day of the first measurement is shown in Fig. 2b. In this case, statistical analysis could not be performed for the resident group due to the small sample size (n = 2). The captured group showed no significant change in CF2 frequency over time (paired permutation test, p = 0.25, not significant), and the rates of change in CF2 frequency were similar between the two groups (8.1 Hz per day for residents and − 6.9 Hz per day for captured individuals). Across all eight events in which no initial difference in mean CF2 frequency was detected, the difference in mean CF2 frequency between the captured and resident groups decreased in four events. However, in all eight events, no significant difference was observed between the two groups on the last measurement day (Fig. 2d; Table 1).

Figure 2e shows the relationship between ΔCF2 and the magnitude of CF2 frequency change over one month of the measurement period. ΔCF2 represents each individual’s deviation from the event-specific mean CF2 (i.e., mean CF2 of bats in that particular cohort) on the first day. Therefore, positive ΔCF2 values indicate individuals with higher CF2 than the cohort mean on the first day, whereas negative ΔCF2 values indicate lower frequency. The analysis showed that cases falling into the second quadrant (negative ΔCF2 and positive one-month change) accounted for 50% of the data, whereas those in the third quadrant (both ΔCF2 and one-month change negative) were comparatively rare (8%). In contrast, observations in the first and fourth quadrants comprised 20% and 22%, respectively, which did not substantially differ from the chance-level expectation of 25%. These results indicate that individuals with initially lower CF2 frequencies tended to shift their frequencies upward, whereas individuals with initially higher CF2 frequencies showed no clear directional tendency. Collectively, these findings suggest that convergence of CF2 frequency within the colony is achieved primarily through upward frequency shifts by individuals with initially lower CF2 frequencies.

There was no significant difference between the mean CF2 frequency of seven individuals measured on the first day of recording and that of eight individuals measured inside the cave immediately after capture (Welch’s t-test, p = 0.65, Fig. 2f). This finding indicates that the observed increase in CF2 frequency was not attributable to recovery from capture-related physiological stress, but rather reflects adjustments driven by inter-individual relationships in CF2 frequency within the colony. To examine whether baseline CF2 frequencies differed across sites, we also analyzed data from capture locations where two or more capture events were conducted. CF2 frequencies measured immediately after capture did not differ significantly among the four sites (one-way ANOVA, F(3,74) = 1.90, p = 0.137; Fig. S3), and no site showed a consistent tendency toward higher or lower values.

Discussion

In this study, we examined temporal changes in CF2 frequencies following the mixing event of resident and wild-caught populations of greater Japanese horseshoe bats. It was observed that when CF2 frequencies differed between the two populations at the time of mixing, the intergroup frequency difference gradually decreased over time. Furthermore, we observed an asymmetric pattern in this convergence, whereby the captured population, which generally exhibited lower frequencies, showed pronounced upward frequency adjustments. No such patterns were observed when CF2 frequencies did not differ between the two groups at the time of mixing.

Given that the captured group tended to exhibit lower CF2 frequencies in many cases, one possible critical interpretation would be that CF2 frequency was transiently reduced by capture-related stress and later increased as physical condition recovered. Nevertheless, no significant difference was detected between CF2 frequencies measured immediately after capture in the wild and those measured after transport to the laboratory. Similarly, studies investigating long-term changes in CF2 frequencies within colonies to explore its determination process have been conducted in other CF–FM bat species, including Hipposideros, with results consistent with those of the present study. Hiryu et al. (2006) investigated how the addition or removal of individuals affects call frequency in captive colonies and demonstrated that colony members adjust their frequencies in a coordinated manner, either upward or downward. That study also showed that individuals with lower frequencies tend to exhibit stronger upward adjustments, and that a reduction in colony size is accompanied by a downward shift in the frequencies of the remaining individuals. Chen et al. (2016) reported that when high- and low-frequency individuals were housed in pairs, frequency alignment occurred not only at the colony level but also at the individual level, with both individuals adjusting their frequencies toward each other. Notably, again the frequency adjustment was asymmetric, with the low-frequency individual exhibiting a larger adjustment.

Previous studies have long documented that CF2 frequencies differ among colonies in CF bats, and potential explanations for this variation have primarily focused on social and genetic factors (Jones and Ransome 1993; Yoshino et al. 2008; Chen et al. 2009; Sun et al. 2013; Xie et al. 2017). Recent psychophysical studies also suggest that task-dependent learning can modulate peak frequency (Ye and Luo 2022). In this study, we demonstrate that CF2 frequencies are actively adjusted to converge within colonies, and that this process exhibits a pronounced asymmetry, with lower-frequency individuals showing stronger frequency adjustments. Based on these findings, we propose a new hypothesis that interprets the determinants of CF2 frequencies from a sensory-ecological perspective, considering their functional significance and utility in echolocation.

In group contexts such as within colonies, echolocation is particularly challenging because calls emitted by conspecifics overlap in frequency, resulting into acoustic interference, which has been recognized as one of the most difficult conditions for sensing in bats (Corcoran and Moss 2017; Chen et al. 2025). In some FM bats, such interference is mitigated by frequency adjustment behaviors in which multiple individuals flying simultaneously shift their call frequencies away from one another, thereby reducing mutual interference between echolocation signals (Ulanovsky et al. 2004; Chili et al. 2009; Hase et al. 2018). This reduction in signal similarity and segregation of frequency bands is thought to facilitate more reliable echolocation in acoustically crowded environments. By contrast, although frequency changes during group flight have also been examined in CF bats, such studies have not reported frequency separation behaviors of this kind (Furusawa et al. 2012; Fawcett et al. 2015; Hase et al. 2022). Instead, results from the present study and related work observed over medium to long timescales indicate a gradual convergence of frequencies among individuals both in the wild and captivity (Hiryu et al. 2006; Yoshino et al. 2008; Furusawa et al. 2012; Chen et al. 2016). We therefore propose a new hypothesis that the previously observed patterns reflect a fundamental difference in signal design between FM and CF echolocation strategies, and that the sharing of a common CF frequency within a colony may itself contribute to interference mitigation in CF bats.

In our recent work, we propose that DSC behavior may function to secure a “silent spectral window” several kilohertz above the CF2 frequency by regulating background echo frequencies below a certain threshold. This, in turn, enables the detection of fluttering prey-derived glints within this band with a high signal-to-noise ratio (Yoshida et al. 2026). Furthermore, because detection of approaching objects—such as in predator avoidance—relies on positive Doppler shift cues (Yoshida et al. 2024), the frequency band above CF2 is particularly important. Taken together, the significance of asymmetric frequency convergence among individuals within CF bat colonies can be explained in a highly coherent and rational manner. When individual frequencies within a colony are widely separated, lower-frequency individuals face a situation in which their “silent spectral window” is disrupted by higher-frequency individuals. Therefore, avoiding such interference requires lower-frequency individuals to increase their own frequencies (Fig. 3). As individual frequencies converge, the “spectral window” can be shared without mutual interference, allowing multiple individuals to maintain high sensory performance simultaneously (Note that bats using higher CF frequencies do not actively adjust their frequencies because they have already secured their own “spectral window” and thus have no need for additional frequency adjustments). Accordingly, framing frequency adjustment behavior within CF bat colonies as a strategy to secure “spectral windows” provides a unified and novel explanation that consistently accounts for both how frequency convergence itself contributes to interference avoidance and why frequency adjustment preferentially occurs in lower-frequency individuals. In addition, although this argument remains speculative, the use of a shared frequency within a group—and the consequent sharing of a “spectral window”—may allow individuals to detect approaching objects or prey-derived glints by exploiting Doppler shift information present in echoes generated by the calls of other conspecifics, even without emitting their own pulses. Essentially, this is a strategy that reduces the costs of echolocation through group sensing while increasing sensing opportunities. However, this hypothesis requires further experimental verification in the future.

This interpretation explains both the convergence of frequencies within colonies and the preferential adjustment by bats using lower frequencies, without excluding the potential contributions of social or genetic factors previously discussed in relation to inter-colony frequency differences. Notably, in most cases examined in this study, resident individuals exhibited higher CF2 frequencies than newly captured individuals. In addition, in Fig. 2a, although the rate of change was greater in the captured group, the resident population also exhibited parallel upward shifts in CF frequency over time. These patterns are not fully explained by our current hypothesis and may reflect adaptation to the captive environment, and such adaptation may not have been fully completed even in the resident population. To examine this possibility, we reviewed the captive history of the resident population in this case and confirmed that, among the 10 individuals, six had been maintained in captivity for two years and the remaining four for one year. This suggests that these individuals were unlikely to be in an unstable post-capture phase in which CF frequency might still be strongly affected by capture-related factors. Nevertheless, our conclusions are based on patterns observed across multiple cases and do not exclude the possibility that, in individual cases, CF frequency may have been influenced by other factors, such as physiological condition or seasonal effects. In particular, how CF frequencies change when wild individuals are introduced into captivity in the absence of resident bats, or when individuals with higher frequencies than resident bats are introduced, remains unknown. Examining these scenarios will be important for further evaluating this interpretation and represents an important direction for future research.

In the present study, we found that CF2 frequencies within the colony converge over time through asymmetric adjustment, primarily driven by increases in individuals that initially exhibited lower frequencies. Together with previous findings, these results suggest that such frequency adjustment represents a functionally important strategy for securing and sharing a “spectral window” above the CF2 frequency, thereby reducing acoustic interference and maintaining high sensory performance. This perspective provides new insight into inter-individual interactions in bat echolocation. This study directly builds upon our previous work on this species that was handled by Professor Peter Narins during his tenure as Editor of the Journal of Comparative Physiology A. Specifically, it is grounded in our investigations of daily emitted frequency variation (Hiryu et al. 2006) and Doppler-shift compensation mechanisms (Hiryu et al. 2008).
