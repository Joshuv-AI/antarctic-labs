ABSTRACT
The method of electron back-scatter diffraction provides an analytic means to identify crystalline
phase and the grain size of structural materials. This analysis is especially important for a consistent
means to evaluate the effects of refined microstructures on physical behaviors such as mechanical
strength. The case for additively manufactured 316L is explored wherein submicron features are
present and properties are often highly anisotropic. The need to utilize imaging parameters
consistent with revealing the contributing microstructural scale is often overlooked through
packaged imaging scripts. It is shown that imaging parameters consistent with a post processing
assessment of low misorientation angles between boundaries are achievable as using pixel sizes on
the order of 101 nm.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The measurement of grain size (ℎ! ) is often used to assess the effect of microstructural refinement
on material properties such as tensile strength. The value computed for ℎ! becomes subjective to
the instrument parameters selected for analysis of the electron back scatter diffraction (EBSD)
patterns. Specifically, it’s found that the ℎ! value computed using the instrument EBSD software
analysis package changes as the misorientation angle (MA) specified to differentiate adjacent grains is
changed. In general, as the MA specified in the analysis is reduced, the computed grain size ℎ!
decreases. This is an important consideration when establishing standards used in data analysis for
samples with anisotropic microstructures, and does expose practical limitations in the software
packages to effectively compute a representative grain size.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 The use of additive manufacturing has increased broadly to directly produce net-shaped engineered
 components by 3-D printing processes. The development of structural alloys for this purpose
 includes both 304L [1] and 316L [2]. Components with small dimensions and high aspect ratios are
 of particular interest in presenting unique challenges to prepare a microstructure with predictable
 mechanical behaviors. Recent modelling efforts [3-4] to formulate the mechanical behavior of such
 materials reveal the commensurate role of both grain size and features to the onset of work
 hardening that can be used to both simulate and predict the plasticity and strength of AM alloys.
 Recent AM synthesis efforts [5] demonstrate the ability to produce compositionally homogenous
 316L AM in thin-wall sections that are only a few hundred microns in thickness. As seen within the
 rapidly solidified 316L AM growth of Figure 1, low-angle misorientation angles are observed when

 The need remains present to appropriately quantify the grain size to the refined microstructure of
 rapidly solidified AM materials such as 316L. The use of scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
 methods to quantify grain size include the use of electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD). This
 approach acquires Kikuchi diffraction patterns from the domains of individual grains in the back
 scatter diffraction mode. The orientation of the zone axis for each coherently scattering domain may
 change as grain boundaries and/or subcellular regions are transited by the scanning electron beam.
 Parameters regarding SEM pixel size and the misorientation angle (MA) between the grains must be

slope of the work hardening curve Θ(𝜎) at the onset of plastic deformation. The effects of
parameterizing the MA and pixel size are now evaluated in assessing commensurate variations in
EBSD measurement of ℎ! for thin-wall 316L AM samples.

2. METHODS
Samples are synthesized using laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) on a 3D Systems, Inc. PROX300
Additive Manufacturing Metal 3D-printer. A roller is used for compacting the 316L powder layer to
promote transfer of laser energy between particles. The composition (wt.%) of the elements in the
316L source powder is 16-18 Cr, 10-14 Ni, 2-3 Mo, < 2 Mn, < 0.75 Si. An argon gas atmosphere
(with <250 ppm of oxygen) is used to avoid oxidation during synthesis. The contours/outline
feature is used for the laser beam raster pattern (instead of a hatch strategy) for synthesizing 400𝜇𝑚-
thick walls along with a lower beam energy and scan speed to enhance laser stability and accuracy.
The laser parameters include a 200 W power, 1000 mm×s-1 scan speed, 50 𝜇𝑚 spot size, and 40 𝜇𝑚
powder layer thickness. Three laser passes are used to fuse the powders across the width of the thin
wall.

The thin wall material is potted in an epoxy resin for metallographic sectioning along the build axis
to reveal the internal structure. The mechanical polishing progresses using: 400, 600, 1200, and 4000
grit silicon carbide (SiC) for intervals of 30-120 s at each step; followed by 6, and 1 𝜇𝑚 diamond for
intervals of 300 s each; colloidal silica for 300 s; and a vibratory polish for 1 h with a colloidal-silica
suspension at a pH ~9. An electrochemical etch is performed using a solution composition by
volume of 55 ml H20, 60 ml 15.8M (70%) HNO3, and 15 ml 0.1M HCl. The sample is the anode in
the electrochemical cell where the etch is performed under a driving potential of 1.15 V for 7 s at a
current density of ~50 mA×mm-2. These conditions are found [5] to produce a smooth surface
suitable for revealing grain structure without adversely affecting the evaluation of composition
across the width of the thin wall.

The electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) patterns are acquired using an FEI Apreo loVac
scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with a Bruker e-Flash FS EBSD detector using
Bruker Esprit version 2.3 software. The incident electron beam voltage is 24.8 KeV and the detector
tilt is 9.67°. The full cross-section to the thin wall can be imaged at an 800x magnification. The
default instrument setting for the pixel size ℎ" of 0.176 𝜇𝑚 provides an equivalent angular
resolution of 5° for a 2 𝜇𝑚-object size. To further the spatial resolution for assessing misorientation
angles (MA) effects that can account for contributions from subcellular microstructures in the 316L
AM material, an EBSD scan is taken at 2000x using 0.030 𝜇𝑚-pixel size. This condition enables and
equivalent resolution of submicron-scale features for grain boundaries with at MA < 2°. The MA

The originating nature to the differences observed in Figure 4 for the reduction of average grain

imaging condition is a result of the ability to resolve smaller grain size (and subcellular features)

In comparison to the 0.176 𝜇𝑚 pixel size for the 800x EBSD scan, the 0.030 𝜇𝑚 pixel size and
2000x magnification imaging condition provides the spatial resolution necessary to more accurately
accommodate the contribution of low-angle grain boundaries for a quantitative assessment of ℎ!
that are accessible for MA values below 7°. An appropriate selection of the pixel size, at a scale fine
enough to resolve contributing boundaries, is needed to appropriately account for grain boundary
effects as is needed in comparing AM conditions and postprocessing treatments for a Hall-Petch
analysis of strengthening effects.

4. CONCLUSIONS
The cross-section of a thin wall 316L AM LPBF is imaged using EBSD to determine grain size ℎ! .
The parameters selected for the imaging condition are varied to accentuate the resolution of
submicron grain size using representative portions of the sample section. A finer pixel size ℎ" is
required to resolve the lower values of misorientation angle between boundaries as associated with
the dendritic subcellular structure of the rapidly solidified material. A reduction in ℎ" from 176 to 30
nm increases the quantitative contribution of low angle boundaries reducing the computed ℎ! value
from >10 to <5 𝜇𝑚. The use of the intercept method to determine ℎ'! (𝜇𝑚) validates a higher
resolution image to determine its value, where accuracy should be enhanced by the EBSD analysis
including all grains rather than a sampling. A consistent set of imaging parameters is required to
enable the resolution of fine scale structure and for use of the grain size metric in evaluating the
effects of synthesis process parameters on mechanical properties such as (Hall-Petch) strength and
useable (Kocks-Mecking) plasticity.
