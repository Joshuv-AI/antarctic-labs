Cardiometabolic benefits of parathyroidectomy in dialysis patients with secondary hyperparathyroidism: preliminary insights from CT radiomics and body composition analysis
Authors: En-Shao Liu, Chien-Liang Chen, Ming-Ting Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSecondary hyperparathyroidism (SHPT) contributes to mineral–bone disorders, chronic inflammation, and adverse cardiometabolic outcomes in patients undergoing dialysis. Radiomics of epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) derived from computed tomography (CT) may serve as an imaging biomarker of cardiometabolic risk; however, the effect of parathyroidectomy (PTX) on EAT remodeling remains unclear. This cohort study enrolled dialysis patients with severe SHPT who underwent PTX and controls who received standard medical therapy. At baseline and 6 months, the participants underwent CT-based EAT radiomics assessment, coronary artery calcification (CAC) scoring, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) for body composition and bone mineral density (BMD), and biochemical evaluation. PTX significantly decreased EAT attenuation (−69.99 ± 8.07 to −72.64 ± 5.68 HU; p = 0.04) and increased EAT volume (p = 0.04), indicating a shift toward less inflamed, lipid-rich adipose tissue. Fat mass and BMD increased (p < 0.01), whereas lean mass and resting metabolic rate decreased (p < 0.01). Controls exhibited stable EAT parameters but showed significant BMD decline (p = 0.02) and CAC progression (p = 0.005). PTX was associated with significantly lower CAC progression according to both the Hokanson and Modified Raggi criteria (p < 0.05). Changes in EAT volume were negatively correlated with changes in attenuation (r = −0.444, p = 0.006). These findings suggest that PTX in dialysis patients with SHPT might modulate cardiometabolic profiles by increasing EAT volume, reducing EAT attenuation, improving bone density, and attenuating CAC progression. Furthermore, CT-derived EAT radiomics may provide complementary biomarkers for cardiometabolic remodeling following PTX.Keywords: Parathyroidectomy, secondary hyperparathyroidism, epicardial adipose tissue, computed tomography

Introduction

Secondary hyperparathyroidism (SHPT), which is characterized by disturbances in mineral metabolism, is a common and serious complication in patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) that significantly contributes to morbidity and mortality [1,2]. Parathyroid hormone (PTH) is an endocrine peptide, and the parathyroid-related proteins are expressed in various tissues and exert diverse biological effects [3–6]. In SHPT, elevated PTH levels can induce increased bone resorption, hypercalcemia, protein-energy wasting, malnutrition, and cachexia [4–6]. SHPT potentially activates cyclic adenosine monophosphate-dependent protein kinase A, which subsequently activates thermogenic genes to increase resting energy expenditure to induce adipose tissue browning and loss of fat and muscle [3,7–9]. Thus, SHPT may be associated with higher resting energy expenditure and could contribute to morbidity and mortality in patients undergoing dialysis. [10]. Furthermore, as hyperparathyroidism treatments may alter body composition, which modifies morbidity and mortality risks, the changes in higher fat composition that were associated with a higher body mass index (BMI) in patients with ESRD on dialysis were associated with improved survival [11].

Current medical management of SHPT includes administration of vitamin D analogs, calcimimetics, and phosphate binders, which are associated with various degrees of vascular calcification [12]. Parathyroidectomy (PTX), which is typically reserved for patients with severe SHPT, can rapidly reduce the circulating PTH levels. Moreover, in patients with ESRD and severe hyperparathyroidism, PTX decreases cardiovascular mortality risk [13,14]. However, whether PTX in patients with SHPT can induce cardiometabolic changes that improve protein-energy wasting remains unclear.

Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), the fat located between the heart and pericardium, cushions the heart, provides energy, and releases cytokines that protect the myocardium and coronary arteries. Because EAT is in direct contact with the heart and shares its blood supply, EAT-associated inflammatory products can readily influence cardiac function and coronary vessel health [15]. Increased EAT volume or altered EAT characteristics have been linked to higher cardiovascular risk. Recent computed tomography (CT) imaging techniques, such as radiomics, enable EAT monitoring and provide diagnostic information for cardiovascular risk stratification [16,17]. CT-derived measurements of EAT, including volume and attenuation (EATAT), may predict coronary artery disease. Elevated EATAT, potentially reflecting inflammatory cell infiltration, may serve as a marker of inflammation associated with morbidity and mortality. Increased EAT volume has been associated with cardiometabolic risk in the general population [17]; however, its prognostic value in patients with ESRD remains unclear. Thus, assessment of EAT, including volume and density parameters, may be a useful tool for monitoring cardiometabolic changes in dialysis patients.

PTX has a mixed effect on overall fat percentage. Some studies showed no significant postoperative change in fat mass or lipid/glucose metabolism, especially in patients with mild primary hyperparathyroidism [18]. However, epicardial fat thickness significantly decreased after PTX in patients with primary hyperparathyroidism, suggesting a potential reduction in cardiovascular risk [19]. Another study showed that PTX reduced inflammatory markers, suggesting a postoperative beneficial shift in the cardiovascular risk profile [20].

In this study, we aimed to investigate post-PTX EAT remodeling, vascular calcification, and body composition changes in patients with SHPT undergoing dialysis and to ascertain their implications for cardiometabolic health.

Materials and methods

Population

The study followed the protocol approved under VGHKS-CT18-CT18-19 [21] and prospectively recruited additional patients, forming a cohort at Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital, a tertiary medical center in Taiwan. Ethical approval was obtained from the hospital’s ethics committee (approval no. KSVGH21-CT7-25). Investigators obtained written informed consent from each subject prior to participation. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and followed the STROBE guidelines for observational studies. Consistent inclusion criteria, exposure definitions, and outcome measures were applied across prospective components to ensure methodological rigor.

Dialysis group (parathyroidectomy and non-parathyroidectomy groups)

Patients with ESRD who had undergone hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis for ≥6 months at Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital between January 2022 and December 2024 were screened for eligibility. Adults aged 18–80 years with SHPT – defined as intact PTH levels >800 pg/mL in the setting of persistent hypercalcemia with normal or non-suppressed PTH concentrations – were eligible for inclusion. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patients underwent serial assessments, including whole-body dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) and cardiac CT, at 6-month intervals. Exclusion criteria included active infection or malignancy. Patients in the PTX group underwent surgical treatment for severe hyperparathyroidism. Postoperative hypocalcemia was prevented with appropriate supplementation of vitamin D, calcium, and dialysate calcium. Patients in the non-PTX group received standard medical therapy.

Biochemical analysis

Venous blood samples were obtained from patients following overnight fasting. All biochemical parameters including calcium, phosphate, and intact PTH concentrations were measured.

Coronary artery calcium scoring

For patients with a heart rate >65 beats/min, 50–100 mg of oral metoprolol was administered prior to CT acquisition. All scans were performed using a 64-detector-row CT scanner (Aquilion 64; Toshiba Medical Systems Corporation, Otawara, Japan). A non-contrast electrocardiography-gated scan was obtained for coronary artery calcium (CAC) quantification. Scanning parameters were as follows: prospective ECG-triggered sequential mode; tube voltage 120 kV; tube current 250–300 mA; gantry rotation time 0.35 s; detector collimation 0.5 mm; and field of view 180 mm. Images were reconstructed at 75% of the R–R interval using a 512 × 512 matrix, medium soft-tissue kernel, and 3.0-mm slice thickness [21–23]. The entire coronary arterial tree was inspected by a CT technologist with 17 years of experience (C.C.C.), who was blinded to subject identification and treatment, under the supervision of a thoracic radiologist with 27 years of experience (M.T.W.). A calcified plaque was defined as an area of three connected voxels with a CT attenuation ≥130 Hounsfield units (HU) by applying the 3D-connectivity criteria. The Agatston score for each calcification was calculated and summed up.

Definition of CAC progression

CAC progression was evaluated using the modified Raggi and modified Hokanson criteria. [21–23].

Modified Raggi criterion:

Baseline CAC = 0 and follow-up CAC > 0; or

Baseline CAC = 0 and follow-up CAC > 0; or

Baseline CAC > 0 and relative increase >10% over 6 months.

Modified Hokanson criterion:

Square root–transformed difference ≥2.5 between follow-up and baseline CAC scores.

Epicardial adipose tissue quantification

Pericardial adipose tissue was divided into EAT and paracardial adipose tissue shown in noncontrast cardiac CT datasets generated using OsiriX MD (https://www.osirix-viewer.com/) according to established definitions (Figure 1) [18,21]. Radiomic analysis was performed using LIFEx software version 6.1. Radiomic analyses were performed using the LIFEx version 6.1 package [21,24,25]. Semi-automated segmentation with a threshold of −190 to −30 HU was used to analyze the radiomics parameters. The shape and volume features, as well as the first-order histogram, including mean, interquartile range [IQR; Q1, Q2 (median), Q3], kurtosis, and skewness, were extracted. The same CT technologist with 22 years of experience (C.C.C) who was blinded to the study groups, repeated the measurements on a random sample of 100 scans. Intra-reader variability in the measurements was <1%.

The EAT appears more yellow (higher attenuation) than ParaAT, suggesting increased inflammatory activity, as demonstrated by the color-coded adipose tissue highlights.

Body composition

The machine DPX-L device (Lunar Corp, Madison, WI)-based DEXA scanning for the assessment of body composition is an excellent reproducible measurement compared with other noninvasive measurements in ESRD, especially for repeated measurements [26]. Body composition variables included lean body mass, total fat mass (FM), fat (% FM), bone mineral density (BMD), and BMI. However, patients need to stay on the table for 6 to 10 min to complete the whole-body scan.

Statistical analysis

Results are presented as mean ± SD for normally distributed data and as median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed continuous variables, with normality assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-group comparisons of parametric data were performed using the independent t-test, while the Mann–Whitney U-test was applied to skewed variables. Categorical data were compared using Fisher’s Exact Test or the Chi-Square Test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. For paired data, the paired-sample t-test and Wilcoxon matched-pairs test were used for parametric and non-parametric variables, respectively. Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between changes in EAT attenuation and EAT volume. In addition, a linear mixed-effects model was employed to evaluate between-group differences across all parameters, including EAT, CAC, and body composition.

Results

We enrolled 51 patients with severe hyperparathyroidism. Of these, 30 initially chose PTX with follow-up CT; however, only 21 underwent surgery within the study period and completed CT and DXA body composition assessments. Nineteen patients opted for conservative medical management, and subsequently, 16 of them completed follow-up CT and DXA within the study period. Eight patients underwent preoperative CT and DXA assessments within one month prior to PTX, while 13 patients completed these evaluations 1–2 months before surgery. Postoperative CT and DXA assessments were performed at approximately six months. Control subjects underwent baseline and follow-up testing at comparable six-month intervals (Figure 2).

Linear mixed model analyses demonstrated significant group × time interactions across all three outcomes (EAT volume, FM percentage, and CAC) after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI. In the non-PTX control group, EAT volume declined over 6 months (121.93 → 110.05 mL), whereas the PTX group showed an increase (94.60 → 102.33 mL), yielding a robust interaction effect (p = 0.031; log-transformed p = 0.013). FM percentage remained stable in the non-PTX control group (32.85% → 32.85%) but increased in the PTX group (28.58% → 31.93%), with a significant interaction in the raw model (p = 0.029) though attenuated after log-transformation (p = 0.060). CAC exhibited marked skewness and required log-transformation; results revealed progression in the non-PTX (6.12 → 6.37, p < 0.001) while the PTX group remained stable (6.92 → 6.90), with a highly significant interaction (p < 0.001). Covariate analyses confirmed BMI and sex as consistent positive predictors of EAT and CAC, whereas FM% was not significantly influenced. Collectively, these findings indicate that PTX modifies longitudinal trajectories of white adipose tissue browning and calcification, with divergent trends between PTX and non-PTX patients with severe SHPT.

Discussion

We evaluated the effect of PTX on EAT and associated cardiometabolic changes in patients with SHPT. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effects of PTX on EAT volume, density, vascular calcification, and body composition in patients with SHPT. We demonstrated that PTX significantly increased EAT volume, reduced EATAT, and retarded the progression of coronary artery disease in patients with SHPT. These findings suggest that PTX contributes to cardiovascular risk reduction, potentially through the modulation of vascular calcification, EAT characteristics, and body composition, with an improvement in microinflammation and cachexia.

To elucidate why PTX significantly increases EAT volume in SHPT, we inferred that EAT volume reflects both the amount of adipose tissue and the number of fat cells surrounding the heart. PTH–related protein is expressed in adipose tissue and exerts endocrine actions that extend beyond calcium and phosphate metabolism, potentially influencing metabolic processes. Parathyroid‑related hormones can induce the conversion of white fat into brown fat, thereby enhancing mitochondrial activity and energy expenditure [7–9]. In patients with renal failure, hyperparathyroidism may contribute to cachexia, reduced body fat, and browning of white adipose tissue [4–6]. The reversal of fat browning following PTX provides evidence for the observed increase in EAT volume. Changes in body composition, including increased adipose tissue, may be attributable to reduced resting energy expenditure after PTX compared with non‑PTX patients (Table 3). These findings are consistent with previous studies [27,28].

To clarify why PTX significantly decreases EATAT in SHPT, we propose that excess PTH may induce inflammation. PTH is essential for maintaining serum calcium homeostasis, but excessive levels exert direct and indirect metabolic effects that influence both inflammation and the cardiovascular system [3,7–9]. Correction of hyperparathyroidism reduces EATAT (more negative Hounsfield units), indicating lipid-rich, less inflamed adipose tissue. In contrast, higher attenuation (less negative HU) suggests inflammation or fibrosis, potentially linked to coronary inflammation. These findings are consistent with prior evidence showing that PTX may be associated with reduced inflammation [20,29].

EAT volume and attenuation represent distinct characteristics that provide complementary clinical information, particularly in cardiovascular imaging. EAT volume quantifies the amount of visceral fat surrounding the heart, whereas EATAT reflects the average radiodensity of EAT as measured by CT. Following PTX, EAT volume increased while attenuation decreased, suggesting a shift toward less inflamed, lipid-rich adipose tissue. This paradox suggests nutritional recovery with reduced inflammation, rather than increased cardiovascular risk. Although overweight and obesity are traditionally associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, numerous studies have shown that patients with higher BMI and better nutritional status experience improved outcomes in ESRD [30–32]. This phenomenon, termed ‘reverse epidemiology,’ has been observed across various cardiovascular conditions, including ESRD [33]. The clinical significance of EAT changes in ESRD may therefore differ from that in the general population. Decreased attenuation with increased volume may indicate reduced inflammation and altered cardiovascular risk in patients without protein-energy wasting, whereas increased attenuation with reduced volume may reflect ongoing wasting and inflammation. Potential mechanisms underlying reverse epidemiology include protein-energy wasting, altered cytokine profiles, sequestration of uremic toxins in adipose tissue, and endotoxin–lipoprotein interactions. In this study, PTX was associated with increased EAT volume, decreased attenuation, and less vascular calcification, consistent with improved nutritional status and reduced inflammation. Taken together, EAT radiomics emerges as a promising exploratory tool for assessing cardiometabolic remodeling in ESRD, though its prognostic value warrants further validation. Nevertheless, these associations remain speculative and require confirmation in larger, longitudinal cohorts.

The strength of our study lies in the use of CT and DEXA scanning to measure EAT and body composition, which are considered gold-standard imaging modalities due to their accuracy. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the small sample size, open-label design, and non-matched control group reduced statistical power. Although descriptive analyses suggested that the treatment group exhibited marked changes from baseline to follow-up and the mixed linear model detected a statistically significant group × time interaction, the study captured only short-term outcomes and did not assess longitudinal endpoints such as cardiovascular events beyond CAC progression. Future studies with larger cohorts or multicenter randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate these findings. However, obtaining a well-matched control group remains challenging due to ethical considerations: patients undergoing PTX are typically more symptomatic with severe hyperparathyroidism, whereas the control group includes fewer symptomatic patients with comparable severity. Despite these limitations, this study contributes novel insights into the impact of PTX on adipose tissue dynamics, including CT- and DEXA-based measurements of body composition, EAT, and CAC in patients receiving maintenance dialysis.

In conclusion, PTX increased EAT volume and reduced attenuation, changes associated with improved BMD and stabilization of coronary calcification in dialysis patients with severe SHPT. These findings suggest that PTX may mitigate cachexia and microinflammation, thereby contributing to cardiovascular risk reduction. CT‑based EAT radiomics emerges as a promising exploratory tool for monitoring cardiometabolic remodeling in ESRD. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and longer follow‑up are warranted to validate these preliminary insights and establish the prognostic utility of EAT radiomics in clinical practice.
