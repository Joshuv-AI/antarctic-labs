In vivo evaluation of a 3D-printable polyurethane-acrylate soft denture liner
Authors: Dong-Joon Lee, Jang-Hyun Park, Jaehan Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEThe purpose of this study was to evaluate the in vivo biological response of a newly developed 3D-printable polyurethane-acrylate-based soft denture liner (SDL) following direct contact with oral mucosa, focusing on cytocompatibility, epithelial integrity, and inflammatory responses.MATERIALS AND METHODSA polyurethane-acrylate SDL formulation optimized for additive manufacturing was fabricated using digital light processing (DLP) 3D printing. Cytotoxicity was assessed in vitro using L929 fibroblasts according to ISO 10993-5 guidelines through microscopic observation and MTT assays. For in vivo evaluation, plate-shaped SDL specimens were applied to the buccal mucosa of Sprague-Dawley rats, with sutured sham controls. Animals were sacrificed at 24 h (day 1) and 72 h (day 3). Histological assessment was performed using hematoxylin and eosin staining, while epithelial integrity and differentiation were evaluated by immunohistochemistry for E-cadherin and pan-keratin. Inflammatory and collagen-related gene expression was analyzed using RT-qPCR.RESULTSThe SDL extracts showed no cytotoxic effects at any concentration, with cell viability exceeding 97% in all groups. Histological analysis revealed no epithelial disruption, inflammatory cell infiltration, or tissue damage in SDL-applied mucosa. Epithelial thickness exhibited a mild increase from day 1 to day 3 in both sham and SDL groups, with no significant intergroup differences. E-cadherin and pan-keratin expression patterns remained comparable between groups at all time points. RT-qPCR demonstrated a transient increase in inflammatory markers at day 1, which resolved by day 3 without sustained inflammation.CONCLUSIONThe newly formulated 3D-printable polyurethane-acrylate SDL demonstrated excellent biocompatibility and preserved oral mucosal integrity in vivo, supporting its potential clinical application as an alternative to conventional soft denture liners.

INTRODUCTION

Soft denture liners (SDLs) are widely used in prosthodontic treatment to improve patient comfort, distribute masticatory loads, and protect compromised oral mucosa.1234 They are particularly beneficial for patients with severe residual ridge resorption, thin or inflamed mucosa, or pronounced anatomical undercuts. Despite these clinical advantages, the long-term use of conventional SDL remains limited due to inherent material-related drawbacks.123

Currently available SDLs are broadly classified as silicone-based or acrylic-based materials.12 Silicone-based liners exhibit relatively stable elasticity and durability; however, their lack of chemical bonding to the denture base often leads to debonding and hygiene-related complications.5 In contrast, acrylic-based liners demonstrate better initial adhesion to denture bases but are prone to plasticizer leaching, resulting in progressive hardening, discoloration, and deterioration of mechanical properties over time.126 In addition, most conventional soft liners require manual mixing and chairside application, making the final material properties highly dependent on operator skill and susceptible to void formation and material inconsistency.7

To overcome these limitations, our research group has focused on developing a new class of SDL materials based on polyurethane chemistry. Polyurethane-based polymers are particularly attractive because their mechanical and viscoelastic properties can be precisely tailored through molecular-level design, and they have a well-established history of use in biomedical applications.89 By controlling the molecular architecture of urethane-acrylate oligomers, our group aimed to simultaneously achieve sufficient mechanical strength and appropriate viscoelastic behavior required for SDL applications.

In parallel, additive manufacturing technologies were incorporated into the material development process. The application of three-dimensional (3D) printing to SDL fabrication offers several advantages, including the elimination of manual mixing procedures, improved material homogeneity, and the ability to produce patient-specific geometries with high precision.1011 This digital workflow represents a promising alternative to conventional chairside relining techniques and may expand the clinical applicability of SDLs.

Through a series of experimental evaluations, the polyurethane-acrylate-based SDL developed by our group demonstrated mechanical and viscoelastic characteristics suitable for intraoral use, as well as acceptable cytocompatibility at the cellular level.11 These findings indicate that the material possesses the fundamental properties required for a SDL and highlight its potential as an alternative to conventional products. However, these results were obtained under controlled in vitro conditions and do not fully reflect the complex biological environment of the oral cavity.

In clinical settings, SDLs are continuously exposed to repetitive masticatory loading, saliva, oral microorganisms, and direct contact with oral mucosal tissues.34 Therefore, favorable in vitro performance alone is insufficient to ensure clinical safety and effectiveness. Evaluation of the biological response of oral tissues following material application is essential to determine whether the material induces inflammation, epithelial disruption, or adverse tissue reactions.

Accordingly, the purpose of the present study was to evaluate the in vivo biological response of a 3D-printable polyurethane-acrylate SDL developed by our research group using an animal model. Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses were performed to assess epithelial integrity, inflammatory response, and tissue compatibility following material application. This in vivo investigation aims to provide critical evidence supporting the biological safety of the developed material and its potential for future prosthodontic applications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Preparation of the 3D-printable polyurethane-acrylate SDL (condensed)

The SDL used in this study was a 3D-printable polyurethane-acrylate-based material developed in-house by our research group for in vivo evaluation. During material screening, a formulation prepared using urethane-acrylate oligomers derived from polyols with average molecular weights of 1 kDa and 3 kDa mixed at a 1 : 1 ratio was selected, as it provided the most favorable balance between mechanical performance and printability within the constraints of additive manufacturing. This formulation was used throughout the animal experiments.

Hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA; Nippon Shokubai, Osaka, Japan) was used for oligomer synthesis, and isodecyl acrylate (IDA; Green Chemical, Seosan, Korea) was incorporated as a reactive diluent to adjust resin viscosity. A phosphine oxide-based photoinitiator (phenyl bis(2,4,6 trimethylbenzoyl)phosphine oxide; Omnirad 819, IGM Resins, Waalwijk, Netherlands) was added to enable visible-light photopolymerization, resulting in a photocurable resin system suitable for 3D printing. Specimens were fabricated using a digital light processing (DLP) 3D printer operating at 405 nm (Uniz Slash 2, Uniz, San Diego, CA, USA) and post-polymerized prior to biological application.

Prior to in vivo evaluation, the mechanical properties of the developed SDL were characterized. The material exhibited a tensile strength of 1.13 ± 0.29 MPa and an elongation at break of 143.3 ± 15.9% (n = 7). The Shore A hardness measured after 1 day was 28.7 ± 1.53.

The cytotoxicity of the test material was evaluated using both qualitative (microscopic observation) and quantitative (MTT assay) methods in cultured cells. This study was conducted in accordance with ISO 10993-5:2009 guidelines. An NCTC clone 929 (L929) cell line was purchased from the Korean Cell Line Bank. The cells were cultured with Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) on a T-75 flask at 37℃, 5% CO2 condition in a humidified incubator. The cubic SDL specimens measuring 10 mm on each side were designed using computer-aided design (CAD) software to achieve a final weight of 2 g and were manufactured using the same 3D printing protocol. Each cube was placed in 10 mL of EMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and incubated at 37℃ for 24 h. Following incubation, the culture medium was collected and defined as the undiluted (100%) extract. This extract was subsequently diluted in EMEM to prepare a series of two-fold dilutions (50%, 25%, 12.5%, and 6.25%). EMEM containing 10% FBS served as the negative control, while aqueous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) solutions at 3%, 6%, and 12% were used as positive controls. Following the pre-incubation step, the culture medium was aspirated, and three wells were allocated to each experimental group. Each well received 100 µL of the appropriate test solution. The plates were then maintained at 37℃ for 24 h under humidified conditions with 5% CO2. After exposure, cellular morphology was observed under a microscope to evaluate cytotoxic responses. Subsequently, 20 µL of MTT solution (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide; 5 mg mL-1 in DPBS) was added to each well, followed by an additional 4 h incubation. The supernatant was removed, and the plates were allowed to dry at room temperature. Formazan crystals were then dissolved by adding 100 µL of DMSO to each well, and optical density was determined at 540 nm using a microplate reader (VERSA max™, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Plate-shaped SDL specimens with a thickness of 0.5 mm were sectioned into 2.5 mm × 1.5 mm pieces and applied to the buccal mucosa of 5-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats (Narabiotech Co., Pyeongtaek, Korea). The trimmed specimens were sutured in place with 6-0 Vicryl, and knot tying was carefully performed to avoid the trajectory of the facial nerve (Fig. 1). Rats in which the specimen was applied were assigned to the SDL group, whereas rats that underwent suturing at the same site without specimen application were designated as the Sham group. Each group consisted of 12 rats; six animals were sacrificed at 24 h (Day 1) and the remaining six at 72 h (Day 3). Of the six rats at each time point, three were used for histological analysis, and the remaining three were subjected to RT-qPCR analysis. All rats were housed in a temperature-controlled room (22℃) under artificial illumination with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and 55% relative humidity. The rats were provided access to food and water ad libitum. All operational procedures were performed under deep anesthesia with ketamine and Rompun® and the protocols involving animals were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Dankook University (Approval no: DKU-25-084).

The tissues were excised and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Staining was performed on 5 mm paraffin-embedded sections. After deparaffinization, the slides were rehydrated and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin. The slides were incubated with Proteinase K (10 mg/mL, AM2546, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 20 min at 37℃. Subsequently, the slides were incubated with antibodies against E-cadherin (1 : 400 dilution, mAb #3195, Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA) and pan-keratin (1 : 200 dilution, mAb #67306, Cell Signaling, United States). The specimens were sequentially incubated with secondary antibodies and streptavidin peroxidase. The results were visualized following staining with a diaminobenzidine (DAB) reagent kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin. All the specimens were observed using a slide imaging scanner (VS200; Evident). The mean thickness of oral epithelium was calculated, measured as the area over basal line length of ROI. The mean intensity of immunohistochemistry image was measured using ImageJ software (ver. 1.38e, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

The total RNA was extracted using TRIzol® reagent (#15596-026, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The extracts were reverse transcribed using Maxime RT PreMix (#25081, iNtRON, Seoul, Korea). RT-qPCR was performed using a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Bio-Systems, Foster City, CA, USA). The amplification program consisted of 40 cycles of denaturation at 95℃ for 15 s and annealing at 62℃ for 30 s. The expression levels of each gene are expressed as normalized ratios against the GAPDH housekeeping gene. Primers are listed in Table 1.

The graphical outputs were expressed as the mean ± SD. A GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used to analyze the data. Comparison of two groups was performed using an unpaired two-tailed t-test. The P value < .05 was considered significant.

RESULTS

Microscopic examination demonstrated no evidence of cellular reactivity at any of the tested extract concentrations (Table 2). In agreement with these observations, the MTT assay revealed high levels of cell viability across all concentrations tested. Specifically, cell viabilities at extract concentrations of 0, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, and 100% were 100%, 113%, 112%, 119%, 108%, and 97%, respectively. All values were markedly above the 70% viability threshold corresponding to 30% cytotoxicity, as defined by the ISO 10993-5:2009 guidelines for quantitative cytotoxicity evaluation. Taken together, both qualitative microscopic findings and quantitative viability data clearly demonstrate that the SDL material does not induce cytotoxic effects under the conditions examined in this study.

To assess tissue-level responses following SDL application, histological evaluation of the oral mucosa was performed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. On day 1, the epithelial thickness in the SDL-applied group remained comparable to that observed in the sham group (Fig. 2A). Quantitative measurements showed epithelial thicknesses of 82.7 ± 3.1 µm in the sham group and 92.9 ± 2.2 µm in the applied group (Fig. 2B). On day 3, epithelial thickness was measured at 95.0 µm in the sham group and 89.0 ± 5.8 µm in the applied group, indicating no substantial alteration in epithelial thickness over time (Fig. 2B). Importantly, throughout the entire observation period, no remarkable inflammatory cell infiltration, epithelial disruption, or tissue damage was observed, supporting the histological safety of the SDL material.

The integrity and differentiation status of the oral epithelial cells following SDL application were further examined by immunohistochemical staining for E-cadherin and pan-keratin on days 1 and 3 (Fig. 3A). E-cadherin expression was consistently localized to intercellular junctions within the epithelial layer in both the sham and applied groups. No significant differences in expression intensity were detected between groups at either time point, indicating that epithelial cell-cell adhesion was preserved following material application (Fig. 3B). Similarly, pan-keratin expression was predominantly observed within the epithelial layer, and no significant differences were found between the sham and applied groups on either day 1 or day 3 (Fig. 3B). These findings suggest that SDL application does not adversely affect epithelial cell survival, differentiation, or junctional integrity.

To further investigate inflammatory responses and collagen-related gene expression in the buccal mucosa following SDL application, RT-qPCR analysis was performed (Fig. 4). On day 1, expression of NF-κB, a well-established regulator of tissue inflammation, was increased by approximately twofold in the SDL-applied group. In addition, other inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and IL-1β, exhibited increasing trends; however, these changes did not reach statistical significance. The expression of Col1a1 in the mucosa was also elevated by approximately 1.7-fold at this time point. By day 3, no significant differences were observed between the sham and the applied groups in the expression levels of NF-κB, IL-6, or IL-1β, and Col1a1 expression likewise showed no statistically significant difference. These results indicate that SDL application induces only a mild and transient molecular response without progression to sustained inflammation or excessive collagen accumulation.

DISCUSSION

Long-term use of complete or removable partial dentures is often associated with patient-reported discomfort, including reduced soft tissue retention and the development of traumatic lesions.121314 In clinical practice, denture relining is commonly performed to alleviate these issues. However, the limited durability and material-related drawbacks of currently available soft denture liners restrict their use as semi-permanent solutions, frequently necessitating replacement with hard liners. This transition can be problematic for patients with maxillofacial defects or prolonged denture use, who often exhibit compromised alveolar support. In addition, individuals with pronounced undercuts may experience discomfort and difficulty when wearing dentures with rigid tissue-contacting surfaces.15 Consequently, there is a clear clinical demand for novel materials that provide improved durability while maintaining the compliance required for long-term intraoral use, beyond the capabilities of conventional soft denture liners.

The present study demonstrates that a newly formulated SDL exhibits excellent biocompatibility in both in vitro and in vivo settings, as evidenced by the absence of cytotoxicity and the preservation of oral mucosal architecture. The baseline mechanical properties of the developed SDL, including tensile strength, elongation at break, and Shore A hardness, were within the range reported for conventional soft denture liners in previous studies.1617 Unlike conventional soft denture liners, which are limited by material degradation and restricted clinical longevity, this novel SDL formulation was designed to maintain tissue compatibility while providing improved stability during oral mucosal contact. SDL application did not induce histological abnormalities, inflammatory cell infiltration, or disruption of epithelial integrity in the rat buccal mucosa. While epithelial thickness showed a slight increase from day 1 to day 3 in both the sham and SDL groups, averaging approximately 3.2 µm, the difference was not statistically significant and was therefore considered biologically negligible. Although a tendency toward increased keratin deposition was noted, this change was considered to be associated with normal masticatory activity rather than a material-induced response.

Histological and immunohistochemical analyses further confirmed the maintenance of epithelial integrity, differentiation, and cell-cell adhesion following SDL application. E-cadherin is a calcium-dependent cell-cell adhesion molecule localized to adherens junctions that plays a central role in maintaining the structural integrity and barrier function of epithelial tissues. Several biomaterial studies have demonstrated that interaction with material surfaces can influence epithelial cell behavior via E-cadherin-mediated mechanisms. Its high expression in normal oral mucosa contributes to strong intercellular adhesion and preservation of epithelial architecture, while reduced expression has been associated with pathological changes such as dysplasia and carcinoma progression.1819 No detectable changes in E-cadherin expression were observed following SDL material application, indicating that epithelial cell-cell adhesion was not altered by material contact. In addition, although H&E staining revealed an increase in the thickness of the stratum corneum, immunohistochemical analysis showed no significant change in the overall expression of keratin. This increase in the stratum corneum is considered a physiological adaptive response commonly associated with denture use rather than a pathological alteration of the oral mucosa.20

Given the typical duration of daily denture wear, the 24- and 72-hour observation periods were considered adequate to assess the short-term biological effects of SDL. Although a transient up-regulation of inflammatory and collagen-related genes was observed at an early time point, these changes resolved by day 3 without progression to sustained inflammation or tissue damage. These findings suggest that the newly developed SDL formulation effectively overcomes key biological limitations associated with existing soft liner materials.

Within the limitations of this study, 3D-printable polyurethane-acrylate SDL demonstrated favorable biological responses without inducing cytotoxicity or adverse tissue reactions, supporting its potential clinical applicability in prosthodontic treatment involving oral mucosal contact.

CONCLUSION

In this study, the biological effects of SDL were systematically evaluated using both in vitro and in vivo models relevant to oral mucosal contact. The SDL material exhibited no cytotoxicity in cell-based assays and did not induce adverse histological changes in the rat buccal mucosa. Epithelial thickness, cellular differentiation, and intercellular adhesion were well preserved following material application, and only a mild and transient molecular response was observed at an early time point without progression to sustained inflammation. Collectively, these findings indicate that SDL demonstrates favorable biocompatibility and may be considered a safe material for prosthodontic applications involving direct contact with oral mucosal tissues.
