Concomitant use of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists in patients with heart failure
Authors: Reza Khademi, Saeed Shoar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure (HF) is a major cardiovascular complication of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) both improve cardiovascular outcomes, but their comparative roles in HF and the potential value of combination therapy remain uncertain.Main BodySGLT2 inhibitors consistently reduce HF hospitalization and cardiovascular death across the spectrum of ejection fraction, with relative risk reductions of approximately 30–37% (Hazard ratio (HR) 0.63–0.70). In contrast, GLP-1 receptor agonists have a more modest effect on HF hospitalization (approximately 11% risk reduction; HR 0.89) while primarily reducing atherosclerotic cardiovascular events and supporting weight and metabolic control. Mechanistically, SGLT2 inhibitors act predominantly through hemodynamic and cardiorenal pathways, whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists exert metabolic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-atherosclerotic effects, providing a rationale for potential complementarity. Emerging data from meta-analyses, subgroup analyses, and observational studies suggest possible additive benefits of combination therapy; however, most evidence is indirect and not derived from dedicated randomized heart failure trials. Safety data indicate no major excess risk with combination therapy, although gastrointestinal effects and class-specific adverse events remain relevant.ConclusionSGLT2 inhibitors are established therapies for reducing HF outcomes, whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists primarily address cardiometabolic risk with uncertain direct HF benefit. Combination therapy is mechanistically plausible but remains insufficiently supported by dedicated randomized evidence in HF populations. Further prospective trials are required to define its clinical role.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) affects an estimated 462 million people worldwide and is strongly associated with cardiovascular disease (CVD), the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in this population [1–3]. Heart failure (HF) is a major cardiovascular complication of T2DM, with affected individuals having an approximately 1.6 to 2-fold higher risk of incident HF than those without diabetes [4]. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) and sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) both provide cardiovascular benefit, although their clinical profiles differ as SGLT2 inhibitors have shown greater reductions in HF hospitalization, whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists appear particularly effective in reducing atherosclerotic events such as stroke [5, 6]. One important unresolved issue is the limited evidence regarding concomitant use of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists, a gap also acknowledged in European Society of Cardiology guidelines and Current guidelines prioritize SGLT2 inhibitors in patients with T2DM who have HF, chronic kidney disease, or elevated cardiovascular risk, while GLP-1 receptor agonists are emphasized for patients with obesity or established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [7]. Given the increasing real-world use of combined therapy and the limited HF-specific evidence base, this review summarizes current data regarding the comparative and combined effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors in HF, with emphasis on efficacy, safety, mechanistic rationale, and evidence gaps.

SGLT2 inhibitors in heart failure

SGLT2 inhibitors, including empagliflozin and dapagliflozin, are well established in reducing cardiovascular death and HF hospitalization in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF; LVEF ≤ 40%) [8, 9]. Similar benefits have also been demonstrated in patients with heart failure with mildly reduced or preserved ejection fraction (HFmrEF/HFpEF) [10, 11]. In pooled analyses, empagliflozin showed clinically meaningful benefit across a broad ejection fraction spectrum (< 25% to < 65%), with attenuation of effect only at very high EF values (≥ 65%) [12]. These findings support the use of SGLT2 inhibitors as foundational therapy across the HF spectrum, with early initiation favored when clinically appropriate [13].

GLP-1 receptor agonists in heart failure

GLP-1 receptor agonists have demonstrated favorable cardiovascular effects in patients with T2DM and obesity as meta-analyses suggest a reduction in hospitalization for incident or new-onset HF of approximately 18%, alongside reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events [6, 14, 15]. In the SELECT trial, semaglutide reduced major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with overweight or obesity and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease without diabetes [16]. A prespecified analysis of SELECT further suggested reductions in composite HF outcomes among participants with and without prior HF, although these findings were secondary analyses rather than HF-dedicated trial endpoints [17]. In contrast, evidence in patients with established HFrEF has been less consistent as small randomized trials reported neutral or potentially unfavorable findings in some patients with reduced ejection fraction [18, 19]. Meta-analyses indicate that although GLP-1 receptor agonists may reduce the risk of new-onset HF, they have not consistently improved outcomes in patients with pre-existing HF, and caution has been suggested in those with LVEF < 40% [20, 21]. Across the HF spectrum, their effects may differ according to phenotype, with potentially greater utility in patients with obesity or HFpEF, whereas their role in established HFrEF remains uncertain and should be individualized pending dedicated outcome trials [22]. Overall, GLP-1 receptor agonists appear most beneficial for cardiometabolic risk reduction, obesity management, and prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular events.

Rationale for combination therapy and comparative overview

SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists provide cardiovascular benefit through distinct clinical profiles. Across randomized cardiovascular outcome trials and meta-analyses, SGLT2 inhibitors consistently reduce hospitalization for heart failure by approximately 30–37% (hazard ratio [HR] 0.63–0.70), with benefits observed regardless of prior atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease status [6, 23]. In contrast, GLP-1 receptor agonists are particularly effective in reducing major adverse cardiovascular events, while their effect on HF hospitalization appears more modest, with relative risk reductions of approximately 11% (HR 0.89) in pooled analyses [6, 14]. These differences support a potentially complementary therapeutic strategy in patients with overlapping HF, metabolic, and atherosclerotic risk.

Evidence supporting combined therapy is emerging but remains limited as a recent meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials reported lower risk of HF hospitalization with concomitant SGLT2 inhibitor and GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy compared with monotherapy or standard care [24].

However, most available randomized data derive from subgroup, post hoc, or exploratory analyses rather than trials specifically designed to evaluate dual therapy in HF populations. For example, exploratory analyses from AMPLITUDE-O found no statistically significant interaction between efpeglenatide treatment effect and background SGLT2 inhibitor use for cardiovascular or HF outcomes [25]. Similarly, post hoc analyses of Harmony Outcomes and DECLARE–TIMI 58 suggested preserved benefit with concomitant therapy, but these studies were not powered to establish additive efficacy of the combination [26, 27].

Observational studies and multinational cohorts have also reported lower risks of HF hospitalization, major cardiovascular events, and all-cause mortality with combined therapy compared with either class alone [28–30]. Nevertheless, such studies remain vulnerable to residual confounding, treatment-selection bias, and heterogeneity in patient populations. Overall, current evidence suggests that combination therapy is biologically plausible and clinically promising, but definitive conclusions regarding incremental benefit in established HF await dedicated randomized outcome trials.

Mechanisms of action

The cardiovascular benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors are likely mediated through multiple complementary pathways relevant to heart failure which include osmotic diuresis and natriuresis, which may reduce cardiac preload; modest blood pressure lowering, which may reduce afterload; and favorable renal hemodynamic effects that lessen cardiorenal stress as demonstrated in Fig. 1 [6, 31, 32]. Additional proposed mechanisms include improved myocardial energetics, reduced inflammation, and attenuation of cardiac remodeling, although the relative contribution of each pathway remains uncertain [33–35]. As illustrated in Fig. 2, GLP-1 receptor agonists appear to exert cardiovascular benefit through mechanisms more closely related to metabolic and vascular risk modification. These agents improve glycemic control, promote weight loss, lower blood pressure, and may reduce systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [36–39]. Such effects are consistent with their established reduction in atherosclerotic cardiovascular events and may be particularly relevant in patients with obesity, insulin resistance, or HFpEF phenotypes, and taken together, these mechanistic differences provide a biologically plausible rationale for combination therapy: SGLT2 inhibitors primarily target HF-related hemodynamic and cardiorenal pathways, whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists may further improve metabolic and vascular risk. However, whether these complementary effects translate into additive clinical benefit in patients with established HF remains to be confirmed in dedicated trials.

Safety considerations

Available evidence suggests that combined therapy with SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists is generally well tolerated, with safety profiles largely consistent with those of each class individually [24, 40–43]. Studies have reported additive benefits in glycemic control, body weight reduction, and blood pressure lowering without major unexpected safety signals [40–43]. The most frequently reported adverse events with combination therapy are gastrointestinal symptoms, including nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, which are primarily attributable to GLP-1 receptor agonists and may occur more often than with SGLT2 inhibitor monotherapy [24, 43]. Gradual dose escalation and patient counseling may improve tolerability, particularly when initiating GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy [38, 43]. SGLT2 inhibitor–related adverse effects, including genital mycotic infections, volume depletion, and rarely ketoacidosis, remain clinically relevant and should be considered when these agents are prescribed as part of combination therapy and importantly, the intrinsic risk of hypoglycemia with combined GLP-1 receptor agonist and SGLT2 inhibitor therapy is low because neither class directly stimulates insulin secretion in a glucose-independent manner and when hypoglycemia occurs, it is more commonly related to concomitant insulin or sulfonylurea use rather than the combination itself [41, 42, 44]. Overall, current data do not suggest a major increase in serious adverse events with dual therapy; however, most studies have been of modest duration and conducted in diabetes populations rather than dedicated HF cohorts as continued pharmacovigilance and longer-term HF-specific safety data are needed.

Clinical implications and practical recommendations

In patients with T2DM and established heart failure, SGLT2 inhibitors currently have the strongest evidence base for improving HF outcomes and should be prioritized when not contraindicated [8, 11, 45]. Their benefits extend across the ejection fraction spectrum and are observed irrespective of diabetes status [8, 10, 11, 46]. GLP-1 receptor agonists may be considered when additional treatment goals include weight reduction, improved glycemic control, chronic kidney disease risk reduction, or lowering atherosclerotic cardiovascular risk, particularly in patients with obesity or established Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD) [7, 16, 47]. However, they should not be viewed as substitutes for evidence-based HF therapies, and their role in established HFrEF remains less certain [18, 19, 22, 48].

Altogether, combination therapy may be reasonable in selected high-risk patients who would benefit from both HF-directed treatment and broader cardiometabolic risk reduction. Decisions should be individualized according to comorbidity burden, renal function, tolerability, cost, route of administration, and patient preference. At present, routine use of dual therapy specifically to improve HF outcomes cannot be recommended because dedicated randomized HF trials are lacking.

Authors’ perspective and future directions

Current evidence supports SGLT2 inhibitors as a core component of contemporary heart failure management because of their consistent reductions in HF hospitalization and cardiovascular death across a broad range of patient populations [8–13, 45, 49]. In contrast, GLP-1 receptor agonists appear to offer important complementary benefits related to weight reduction, glycemic control, renal protection, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular risk reduction, although their direct role in established HF remains less certain [16, 20, 22, 47]. The potential value of combining these therapies lies in addressing overlapping cardiorenal and metabolic risk pathways as available evidence for dual therapy in HF populations remains limited and is derived largely from subgroup analyses, observational studies, and extrapolation from diabetes or obesity trials rather than dedicated HF randomized trials [24, 25, 27, 30, 50].

Future research should prioritize adequately powered randomized controlled trials evaluating combined SGLT2 inhibitor and GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy in patients with HF. Key objectives include determining long-term safety, identifying patient subgroups most likely to benefit, clarifying effects across HF phenotypes, and assessing patient-centered outcomes such as quality of life, functional status, and hospitalization burden.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the current evidence base. First, most data evaluating combined SGLT2 inhibitor and GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy derive from post hoc analyses, subgroup analyses, observational cohorts, or trials primarily designed for glycemic or cardiovascular outcomes rather than dedicated heart failure endpoints. Second, study populations have been heterogeneous with respect to diabetes status, obesity, renal function, baseline cardiovascular disease, and HF phenotype, which may limit generalizability.

Third, HF-specific outcomes such as functional capacity, quality of life, congestion burden, and recurrent hospitalization have been inconsistently reported. Fourth, observational studies remain susceptible to residual confounding and treatment-selection bias despite statistical adjustment. Finally, long-term safety, treatment persistence, cost-effectiveness, and identification of patient subgroups most likely to benefit from dual therapy remain uncertain.

Conclusion

SGLT2 inhibitors have an established role in reducing heart failure hospitalization and cardiovascular death across a broad spectrum of patients with heart failure. GLP-1 receptor agonists provide important cardiometabolic benefits, particularly for weight management, glycemic control, renal protection, and reduction of atherosclerotic cardiovascular risk, although their direct effects in established heart failure remain less certain. The combination of these therapies is mechanistically appealing and may offer incremental benefit in selected patients with overlapping metabolic, renal, and cardiovascular risk; however, current evidence remains limited, heterogeneous, and derived largely from secondary analyses or observational studies rather than dedicated randomized heart failure trials. Further adequately powered prospective studies are required before combined SGLT2 inhibitor and GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy can be routinely recommended, specifically to improve heart failure outcomes.
