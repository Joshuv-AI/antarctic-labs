A three-pronged strategy with minimalist nattokinase nanocomposite eye drops breaks the vicious cycle in ultraviolet-B-induced cataract
Authors: Jingyi Zhao, Jian Gao, Yiming Ma, Hongtao Li, Kunfeng Zhang, Yantong Li, Xinrong Liu, Yanzhi Song, Yihui Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCataract remains the most prevalent cause of visual impairment and irreversible blindness worldwide. It is characterized by lens opacification arising from crystallin denaturation, leading to variable degrees of vision loss and poor quality of life. Although crystallin denaturation is the primary pathological basis of cataract, oxidative stress and inflammation also play significant roles.ResultsIt was first confirmed that nattokinase (NK) could degrade cataractous tissue and alleviate the opacification using human cataract phacoemulsification fluid, thus supporting the in vivo anti-cataract efficacy of NK-based nanocomposites (NNs). Herein, we developed minimalist NNs as eye drops, through electrostatic self-assembly between NK and natural macromolecules. The NNs exert three-pronged therapeutic effects (antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-crystallin denaturation) that disrupt the pathological cycle in cataract. The in vitro cellular studies demonstrated that the NNs were internalized via receptor-mediated endocytosis and micropinocytosis, and efficiently achieved a synergistic scavenging effect of reactive oxygen species (ROS). In ultraviolet B (UVB)-induced cataract mice, the NNs successfully suppressed oxidative stress and inflammation, while protecting crystallins through enhanced intracorneal retention, ultimately ameliorating lens opacity index. Moreover, further analysis revealed that NNs effectively inhibited ROS accumulation by up to 15.78-fold, along with the ROS/NLRP3/pyroptosis axis and the denaturation of crystallins, thereby offering new insights into therapeutic regimens for cataract.ConclusionsConsequently, the NNs alleviated lens opacification by breaking the cataract vicious cycle through a three-pronged inhibition of oxidative stress, inflammation, and crystallin denaturation. Our findings pave the way for utilizing NNs as a novel strategy for reversing cataract, as well as associated ocular disorders.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04648-z.

Introduction

The global population affected by blindness or visual impairment has reached 94 million, among whom cataract is the primary cause of reversible blindness worldwide [1]. With the intensification of population aging and the proliferation of electronic devices, cataract has become increasingly prevalent. Although surgical intervention remains the primary and effective treatment, it is often accompanied by postoperative discomfort and potential complications [2, 3]. Moreover, it also imposes considerable economic and prognostic burdens on patients [4]. To date, numerous attempts have been made to develop non-invasive therapies as potential alternatives. Nevertheless, no pharmaceutical treatment is yet available to prevent or reverse cataract progression, rendering it a persistent public health challenge [3]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to advance the understanding of cataract pathogenesis and explore novel interventions for reversing cataract.

The lens is a transparent, biconvex structure that focuses light onto the retina [2, 5]. Crystallins, the dominant structural protein in the lens, are essential for maintaining the transparency and refractivity [6, 7]. Cataract has been recognized as lens opacification, arising from a combination of local and systemic factors [8, 9]. Aging and oxidative stress are recognized as the leading causes of acquired cataract [1], with additional factors including mutations, trauma, and UVB exposure, all of which contribute to crystallin conformational disorder and aggregation [6, 10–12]. Notably, these risk factors are closely associated with the generation of ROS in the lens, which plays a central role in cataract [1, 13, 14]. UVB-induced ROS overproduction promotes oxidative stress and triggers inflammation cascades, ultimately leading to lens damage and crystallin aggregation [15, 16]. In a reinforcing manner, oxidative stress and inflammation further facilitate ROS accumulation in the lens [17]. Therefore, Inhibition of oxidative stress, inflammation, and crystallin aggregation may serve as a promising strategy for cataract treatment.

Pyroptosis is an inflammasome-mediated form of programmed cell death, characterized by the rapid rupture of the plasma membrane and the subsequent release of cellular contents and proinflammatory cytokines [18–21]. Excess ROS is regarded as a key activator of the NLRP3 inflammasome, promoting its assembly and activation, as well as the cleavage of Cysteinyl aspartate-specific proteinase (Caspase)-1 [22]. This cascade facilitates the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18, induces gasdermin D (GSDMD)-mediated plasma membrane pore formation, and ultimately precipitates pyroptosis [23]. Hyperactive ROS/NLRP3/pyroptosis axis drives a vicious cycle of amplified inflammation and tissue injury, and has been linked to a spectrum of diseases. Emerging evidence highlights its critical role in cataract development, thereby offering new insights into therapeutic regimens for cataract.

Nattokinase (NK) is an alkaline serine protease originally isolated from Japanese natto in 1987 [24]. It exhibits biological activity primarily through its capacity to selectively recognize and hydrolyze cross-linked fibrin without degrading plasma fibrinogen, thereby serving as a safe thrombolytic agent in vivo [25]. Beyond its fibrinolytic properties, NK has demonstrated multiple bioactivities, including anti-inflammatory effects [26], shrinkage of nasal polyp growth [27], and enhancement of retinal health [28]. Inspired by these versatile functions, we selected NK as a candidate therapeutic against cataract. Meanwhile, various natural macromolecules have emerged as ideal carriers for ocular drug delivery due to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and adhesive properties [29–31]. Although eyedrops represent the most patient-friendly administration route for ocular diseases, their efficacy is often limited by poor bioavailability [31–38]. The integration of nanotechnology into ophthalmic formulations has thus garnered considerable interest [39–42], as it can prolong ocular surface retention time [43, 44], and significantly improve treatment outcomes [45, 46].

In this study, we constructed minimalist nanocomposite eyedrops through electrostatic self-assembly of NK with four negatively charged natural macromolecules: polysialic acid (PSA), hyaluronic acid (HA), chondroitin sulfate (CS), and polyglutamic acid (PGA). NK was first confirmed to degrade cataractous tissue and alleviate the opacification using human cataract phacoemulsification fluid. Subsequently, the NNs successfully achieved three-pronged therapeutic effects (antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-crystallin denaturation) in UVB-induced cataract mice through enhanced intracorneal retention, ultimately ameliorating the lens opacity index. Moreover, further analysis revealed that NNs effectively inhibited ROS accumulation by up to 15.78-fold, along with the ROS/NLRP3/pyroptosis axis and the denaturation of crystallins, offering new insights into therapeutic strategies for cataract. Our findings pave the way for utilizing NNs as a novel strategy for reversing cataract, as well as associated ocular disorders.

Materials and methods

Materials

NK was supplied by Shuangjun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shantou, China). PSA (30 kDa) was purchased from Keshun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). HA (40 kDa) was sourced from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). CS (50 kDa) was obtained from Deebiotech Co., Ltd (Deyang, China). PGA (70 kDa) was purchased from Meryer Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was provided by BaiDi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (BDBIO) (Hangzhou, China). DMEM/F12 cell culture medium, DMEM cell culture medium, insulin, recombinant human epidermal growth factor (EGF), penicillin/streptomycin solution (P/S), Phosphate buffered solution (PBS), 1,1’-dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine perchlorate (DiD), 4’,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI), Filipin, 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide (DiR), 4% paraformaldehyde solution, CCK-8 and ROS kits, and Avertin were sourced from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). Chlorpromazine, Amiloride (EIPA), and Methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD) were supplied by Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Quantitative detection kits for total superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), malondialdehyde (MDA), bicinchoninic acid (BCA), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits for interleukin-1beta (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) detection kit were obtained from Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Cells

Human corneal epithelial cells (CECs) were sourced from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). CECs were cultured in DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS (v/v), 5 µg/ml insulin, and 10 ng/ml human EGF, 1% P/S (v/v) under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). Human lens epithelial cells (LECs) were purchased from Ubigene Biosciences Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). LECs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS (v/v) and 1% P/S (v/v) under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2).

Animals and tissues

Male C57BL/6 mice (20 ± 2 g, 6 ~ 8 weeks) were supplied by Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Benxi, China). All animal studies were carried out strictly in compliance with the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals under the supervision of the Animal Welfare Committee of Shenyang Pharmaceutical University (SYPU-2ACUC-S2024-1113-106). Human cataract phacoemulsification fluid was kindly provided by Shenyang Xingqi Eye Hospital (Shenyang, China).

Preparation and characterization of NNs

Preparation and structure confirmation of NNs

The NK@PSA, NK@HA, NK@CS, and NK@PGA nanocomposites were prepared by mixing equal volumes of an NK solution (4.0 mg/ml) with respective PSA/HA/CS/PGA solutions (12.0 mg/ml) (Tab.S1). The mixtures were stirred for 10 min in an ice bath and then filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane. The structure of NNs was characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). In brief, the samples were mixed and tableted with KBr (1:100, w/w) and scanned from 400 to 4000 cm⁻¹ at 25 °C for infrared absorption; The endothermic curve was acquired by sealing samples in airtight aluminum pans and heating from 40 to 200 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min under 40 ml/min N2 flow.

Characterization of NNs

The particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and Zeta potential were determined using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) particle size tester (Nano-ZS ZEN 3700, Malvern Instruments, UK). The pH value of each NN was determined using a standard pH meter (Sartorius, Germany). The enzymatic activity of the NNs was determined using the fibrin-agarose plate method [47, 48]. Furthermore, the morphology of the NNs was imaged by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM1200EX, JEOL, Japan) under 25 °C at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV.

In vitro feasibility studies of NNs

In vitro cytotoxicity of NNs

The in vitro cytotoxicity of NNs against CECs and LECs was evaluated via the CCK-8 assay. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates (1 × 10⁵/ml) and treated with varying concentrations of the NK, PSA, HA, CS, PGA, NK@PSA, NK@HA, NK@CS, and NK@PGA for 24 h at 37 °C. Thereafter, each well was supplemented with 10 µL of CCK-8 solution. Following 4 h of incubation, cell viability was assessed by measuring the optical density (OD) at 450 nm with a Multifunctional Microplate Reader.

In vitro cellular uptake capacity of CECs

DiD-labeled NNs were fabricated employing a post-insertion method [49]. For competitive uptake studies, CECs were seeded in 6-well plates (2 × 10⁵/ml) and pre-treated with PSA/HA/CS/PGA solution (10.0 mg/ml) at 37 °C for 2 h before exposure to DiD-labeled NNs. Subsequently, CECs were incubated with DiD-labeled NK (NKDiD), DiD-labeled NK@PSA (NK@PSADiD), DiD-labeled NK@HA (NK@HADiD), DiD-labeled NK@CS (NK@CSDiD), or DiD-labeled NK@PGA (NK@PGADiD) (each at a DiD-equivalent dose of 0.2 µg/ml) at 37 °C and 4 °C for 2 h, washed with PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Eventually, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of DiD was quantified using Flow Cytometry (FCM, FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences, USA).

To further explore the uptake mechanism of CECs, they were pretreated with 10 µM Chlorpromazine, 2 µM Filipin, 50 µM EIPA, and 5 mM M-βCD at 37 °C for 2 h, followed by incubation with NK@PSADiD, NK@HADiD, NK@CSDiD, and NK@PGADiD (each at a DiD-equivalent dose of 0.2 µg/ml) at 37 °C for 2 h. The MFI of DiD was quantified by FCM.

For qualitative analysis, CECs were implanted in 12-well plates (5 × 104/ml) and treated with DiD-labeled NNs. Following internalization, the cells were fixed, counterstained with DAPI, and imaged under a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, LSM710, ZEISS, Germany).

In vitro ROS inhibition activity of LECs

Intracellular ROS generation was quantified using the cell-permeable probe 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). In brief, LECs (1 × 105/ml) were seeded into 96-well plates and pretreated with 400 µM of H2O2 at 37 °C for 30 min [50]. Afterwards, LECs were incubated with 100 µL of the treatment groups at 37 °C for 12 h. After the incubation, the DCFH-DA probe was added for 30 min. Subsequently, the OD (excitation wavelength = 488 nm, emission wavelength = 525 nm) was determined with a Multifunctional Microplate Reader to evaluate the ROS level.

In vivo corneal retention capacity

To assess the corneal retention effect of NNs in vivo, a single drop (10.0 µL) of DiR-labeled NK (NKDiR), NK@PSA (NK@PSADiR), NK@HA (NK@HADiR), NK@CS (NK@CSDiR), NK@PGA (NK@PGADiR) (each at a DiR-equivalent dose of 10.0 µg/ml) was instilled into the cataract mice conjunctival sac. DiR-labeled NNs were prepared in the same way as DiD-labeled NNs. At pre­determined intervals (5, 15, 60, and 120 min), fluorescence images were captured using the IVIS Lumina Series III imaging system (Perkin Elmer, USA). Following euthanasia by cervical dislocation, mouse eyeballs were harvested, fixed, and subjected to frozen sectioning. The biodistribution of DiR in the ocular tissues was subsequently visualized under a fluorescence microscope.

Analysis of the anti-cataract effect in vitro

Cataract tissue fragments were isolated from 0.5 ml of human cataract phacoemulsification fluid by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The harvested tissue was then incubated with 2.0 mg/ml filter-sterilized NK solution at 37 °C after thorough mixing. The turbidity of the human phacoemulsification fluid was assessed at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 h to evaluate the degrading effect of the NK solution. NK solution, initial human cataract phacoemulsification fluid, and phacoemulsification fluid treated with NK solution were collected and subjected to SDS-PAGE analysis.

Evaluation of anti-cataract efficacy in vivo

Following anesthesia with tribromoethanol, the mice received topical application of tropicamide eye drops to induce mydriasis. They were then subjected to repeated sub-threshold UVB irradiation (wavelength = 302 nm, 330 µW/cm²) for 5 min per day, a total of seven exposures, culminating in a cumulative dose of 2.31 mJ/cm² [51]. The cataract mice were randomly assigned to 11 groups (n = 8): Healthy, Model (treated with 0.9% normal saline), and the treatment groups. The administered doses were as follows: NK at 2.0 mg/ml; PSA/HA/CS/PGA at 6.0 mg/ml; and the NNs containing NK at 2.0 mg/ml and PSA/HA/CS/PGA at 6.0 mg/ml. All groups received topical administration of 5.0 µL of the corresponding preparation twice daily for 14 days post-modeling.

Subsequently, lens opacity was examined and photographed using a slit-lamp biomicroscope. The assessment included the lens opacity grade, the percentage of opaque area, the average gray value of the lens, and the lens opacity index (LO index). Opacity grading was performed according to the Lens Opacity Classification System III (LOCS III) [52, 53]. On day 21, the mice were sacrificed, and the fresh lens tissues were isolated and homogenized in 0.9% normal saline at a 1:9 (w/v) ratio. The supernatant was isolated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The levels of SOD, GSH-Px, MDA, IL-1β, TNF-α, and total protein in the supernatant were determined using commercial detection kits.

Investigation of the underlying mechanisms in vivo

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical assay

The eyeballs were harvested, fixed, and sectioned into 5 μm slices. Afterwards, Dihydroethidium (DHE) and DAPI staining were conducted for the ROS immunofluorescence assay. Meanwhile, primary and secondary antibodies and hematoxylin were incubated sequentially. The section images were observed and obtained by optical microscopy. Quantitative analysis of red fluorescence or brownish-yellow intensity was performed using ImageJ software on the acquired photomicrographs.

Western blot (WB) analysis

Lens tissues were lysed with Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) buffer containing protease inhibitors. After quantifying the total protein concentration with a BCA assay, equal protein amounts were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes, and blocked with skim milk. The membranes were immunoblotted overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, protein bands were visualized via an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system and quantified using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) assay

Following RNA extraction, lens tissue samples were subjected to complementary DNA synthesis with a reverse transcription kit. Specific primers were designed for amplification using a real-time PCR system. The relative mRNA expression levels of each target gene, normalized to that of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), were quantified using the ΔΔCT method.

Analysis of the crystallins

To evaluate αB-crystallin expression in the lens, UVB-exposed mice were randomly divided into 11 groups and treated for 14 days as outlined previously (n = 4). Upon completion of the treatment regimen, lens tissues were collected from all groups for subsequent WB analysis. Additional UVB-exposed mice were randomized into a Model group (0.9% normal saline) and an NK@PSA-treated group, with an additional Healthy group (n = 6). After 14 days of treatment, six lenses from each group were harvested and homogenized to generate one pooled sample for subsequent crystallin structural analysis. Then they were added to PBS (10 mM, pH 7.2 ~ 7.4) at a ratio of 0.5% (w/v). After grinding, the supernatant was isolated by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min [54]. The structure of crystallins was analyzed by a circular dichroism (CD) spectrometer (JASCO J-1500, Applied Photophysics, UK), a fluorescence spectrometer (FL31000, Edinburgh, UK), and a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV1801, RAYLEIGH, China) at 25 °C. In brief, the samples were screened at a scanning speed of 200 nm/min with a 0.5 nm interval, and the ellipticity at 180 ~ 250 nm and 250 ~ 300 nm was recorded for CD analysis; the fluorescence intensity of the samples were recorded at 300 ~ 550 nm upon excitation at 295 nm; the UV-Vis absorbance of the samples were scanned at 200 ~ 500 nm with a 1 nm interval.

Safety evaluation

The body weight of mice was monitored weekly during the experiment, and the percentage of weight change over time was plotted. At the end of the experiment, the eyeballs were collected, fixed, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and TUNEL immunofluorescence. The representative images were examined and captured by an optical microscope and a fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

All values are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). The statistical significance was assessed using GraphPad Prism software 9.5. For the comparison of two groups, Student’s t-test was applied, while differences among multiple groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The statistical significance was determined by a p-value threshold of 0.05.

Results and discussions

Characterization of NNs

In aqueous solutions, positively charged NK and negatively charged PSA/HA/CS/PGA tend to self-assemble into nanocomposites through electrostatic interaction. The definite interaction between NK and PSA/HA/CS/PGA was identified by FT-IR and DSC analysis. As shown in Fig. 1A, the characteristic peaks of NK were identified at 1645.7 cm− 1 (vC=O) and 1072.6 cm− 1 (vC−O−C). Similarly, the distinctive peaks of the macromolecules were observed: PSA at 1640.1 cm− 1 (vC=O) and 1036.4 cm− 1 (vC−O−C); HA at 1646.6 cm− 1 (vC=O), 1591.1 cm− 1 (vNH2), and 1400.9 cm− 1 (vOH), CS at 1637.7 cm− 1 (vC=O), 1615.9 cm− 1 (vOH), and 1038.4 cm− 1, 599.0 cm− 1 (vSO4); PGA at 1636.3 cm− 1 (vC=O) and 1401.1 cm− 1 (vC−N). Critically, the FT-IR spectra of the corresponding physical mixtures exhibited a direct superposition of the characteristic peaks from each constituent. In contrast, the intensity of the characteristic peaks of the NNs was remarkably diminished, which preliminarily suggests that they are not simple physical mixtures. As depicted in Fig. 1B, the endothermic melting peaks for NK, PSA, HA, CS, and PGA were identified at 82.8 °C, 120.9 °C, 110.1 °C, 108.7 °C, and 134.8 °C, respectively. Although the peaks of each NN were close to those of the physical mixture, the melting enthalpies were distinctly different. Specifically, the melting enthalpy values of NK@PSA, NK@HA, NK@CS, and NK@PGA were measured to be -407.8 J/g, -320.5 J/g, -376.1 J/g, and − 347.3 J/g, while those of NK/PSA, NK/HA, NK/CS, and NK/PGA physical mixtures were − 354.8 J/g, -205.9 J/g, -232.3 J/g, and − 251.5 J/g, respectively. Notably, the melting enthalpies of each NN were consistently greater than those of their corresponding physical mixture, further demonstrating the successful construction of NNs.

As illustrated in Fig. 1C and Tab.S1, the particle sizes of NNs ranged from approximately 50 to 80 nm with a relatively narrow size distribution. The Zeta potentials were measured between − 20 and − 10 mV (Fig. 1D and Tab.S2). The pH values fell within the range of 5.5 to 7.0, meeting the quality standards for eye drops (Fig. 1E and Tab.S2). The enzyme activities were determined at 11,000 ~ 13,000 IU·mg− 1 (Fig. 1F and Tab.S2). The TEM images revealed that NNs were composed of uniform spherical or near-spherical nanoparticles (Fig. 1G). Taken together, these results demonstrate the successful fabrication of NNs with favorable physicochemical properties, providing a solid foundation for their potential therapeutic efficacy in vitro and in vivo.

In vitro feasibility study of NNs

In vitro cytotoxicity of NNs

To comprehensively evaluate the in vitro biocompatibility and feasibility of the NNs, both CECs and LECs were employed as representative cellular models, considering their crucial roles in cataract-related drug delivery. The toxicity of NNs towards CECs and LECs was detected by the CCK-8 assay. In comparison to the NK solution, neither PSA/HA/CS/PGA nor NNs exert any inhibitory effect on CECs and LECs, and maintained the favorable proliferation ability (Fig.S1). Only the NK solution exhibited a weak proliferation-inhibitory effect at a relatively high concentration (200.0 IU/ml). These results indicated that PSA/HA/CS/PGA effectively protected CECs and LECs while preserving their normal physiological functions, thus supporting the potential application of the NNs in cataract treatment.

In vitro uptake capacity of CECs

Since the corneal barrier is primarily composed of CECs, they were employed to assess the uptake capacity in vitro. The uptake of DiD-labeled NNs by CECs was analyzed via FCM and CLSM. DiD-labeling did not induce obvious alterations in the physicochemical characteristics of NNs (Tab.S3). As depicted in Fig. 2A, DiD-labeled NNs were efficiently internalized by CECs at 37 °C, with their MFI being significantly higher than NKDiD and following the order: NK@PSADiD > NK@HADiD > NK@CSDiD > NK@PGADiD. In contrast, cellular uptake activity was essentially halted at 4 °C, confirming that the uptake process was an energy-dependent active endocytosis (Fig. 2B).

In the competitive inhibition experiment, the pre-saturated group with PSA solution exhibited notable inhibition of NK@PSADiD uptake by CECs, which was significantly reduced by 72.52% (****p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2C). When pre-saturated with free HA solution, there was no significant difference in the uptake of NK@HADiD by CECs (Fig.S2). In contrast, pre-saturation with CS and PGA solution led to a potent 3.61-fold and 1.26-fold increase in the uptake by CECs (**p < 0.01) (Fig.S2). These results suggested that PSA enables the enhanced uptake efficiency of NK@PSADiD through the interaction with PSA-binding receptors localized on CECs. However, for NK@HADiD, NK@CSDiD, and NK@PGADiD, their cellular uptake by CECs may not be predominantly dependent on corresponding receptor-mediated endocytosis. Instead, other uptake pathways in CECs might exhibit compensatory enhancement following pre-saturation of these receptors.

For further exploration of the potential uptake mechanism, CECs were separately pre-treated with CPZ, Filipin, MβCD, and EIPA. The uptake of NK@HADiD, NK@CSDiD, and NK@PGADiD by CECs was significantly inhibited by EIPA, with the inhibition rate of 76.3% (****p < 0.0001), 69.5% (****p < 0.0001), and 68.9% (****p < 0.0001), respectively (Fig. 2D ~ F). Hence, macropinocytosis also contributed to the cellular internalization of NK@HADiD, NK@CSDiD, and NK@PGADiD by CECs. The following results obtained via CLSM were consistent with those from the FCM analysis (Fig. 3A). Collectively, as evidenced by the above results, the uptake of NK@PSADiD into CECs was predominantly mediated by receptor-dependent endocytosis. In contrast, the cellular internalization of NK@HADiD, NK@CSDiD, and NK@PGADiD by CECs relied not only on receptor-mediated endocytosis but also on macropinocytosis.

This differential internalization is attributed to the distinct physicochemical properties of the carriers and the receptor expression profile on CECs. For NK@HA and NK@CS, low basal CD44 expression on CECs and the moderate, activation-dependent affinity of the HA-CD44 interaction likely preclude efficient receptor-mediated endocytosis under non-inflammatory conditions, thereby rendering macropinocytosis the dominant uptake route [55, 56]. This finding is consistent with prior evidence showing that 50–80 nm nanoparticles preferentially enter CECs via micropinocytosis [57, 58]. For NK@PGA, our results corroborate the findings of Zhao et al., who demonstrated that PGA-modified conjugates are primarily internalized by human CECs through micropinocytosis [59]. In contrast, NK@PSA is taken up via a receptor-mediated mechanism. Although the specific sialic acid-binding receptor on CECs has not yet been molecularly defined, PSA is a recognized natural ligand for this receptor family. Notably, there is a significant inhibition of NK@PSA by free PSA rather than EIPA, which unequivocally supports a receptor-dependent endocytic pathway. These results collectively suggest that NNs are effectively internalized by CECs, a prerequisite for realizing their anti-cataract efficacy in vivo.

In vitro ROS inhibition activity of LECs

UVB irradiation elicits excessive ROS generation in the lens, thereby triggering a self-amplifying inflammatory cascade in cataract [15, 60]. Effective removal of excess ROS may therefore represent a viable strategy to suppress inflammation and interrupt the vicious cycle of cataract. As the primary defensive cells in the lens, LECs play a critical role in maintaining lens transparency [61]. Elevated H₂O₂ levels have been reported in the lenses of cataract patients, contributing to lens damage and opacification [62, 63]. To evaluate the ROS-scavenging capacity of the NNs, intracellular ROS levels in LECs under H₂O₂ (400 µM) stimulation were measured using a DCFH-DA probe [50]. As shown in Fig. 3B, the NK solution suppressed ROS levels in LECs in a concentration-dependent manner. Compared to the Model group, overproduction of ROS in LECs was effectively suppressed in all the treatment groups (each at an NK-equivalent dose of 100.0 IU/ml or at a macromolecule dose of 300.0 IU/ml) (Fig. 3C). The inhibitory efficacy followed the order: NK@PSA > NK@HA ≈ NK@CS > NK@PGA, though no statistically significant differences were observed among these groups. It is anticipated that the combination of NNs could produce a “1 + 1 > 2” synergistic therapeutic effect over prolonged treatment. Furthermore, the sustained-release properties of NNs may further enhance their capacity to inhibit ROS accumulation in LECs. These findings demonstrate that NNs serve as effective ROS scavengers, highlighting their potential to disrupt the vicious cycle underlying cataract progression.

In vivo corneal retention capacity

The precorneal retention capability of NNs was tested in cataract mice using in vivo imaging and an immunofluorescence assay. DiR-labeling did not induce obvious alterations in the physicochemical characteristics of NNs (Tab.S4). As shown in Fig. 4A, the DiR fluorescence signal in all groups exhibited high levels at 5 min, after which they underwent a gradual decrease. The MFI of all the NNs groups remained at high levels even after 60 min. In comparison, the free NK diminished rapidly in 30 min (Fig. 4B). Building on the results of in vivo imaging studies, the ocular surface retention at 60 min post-topical instillation was further investigated using immunofluorescence staining sections of eyeballs. As revealed in Fig. 4C, NK@PSA demonstrated more effective retention in the corneal region. Notably, negligible ocular retention was detected for free NK solution, a finding consistent with the in vivo imaging result. Altogether, these results support the potential of NNs as a topical delivery system for effective in vivo cataract treatment.

Analysis of the anti-cataract effect in vitro

As shown in Fig. 4D, the initial cataract phacoemulsification fluid exhibited abundant suspended particles and displayed a weak Tyndall effect. This phenomenon arises from the abundance of micron-scale particles in the initial fluid, which induce weak Mie scattering and consequently result in a poorly defined light path [64]. Upon treatment with NK, the particle size in the system gradually decreased over 2 ~ 8 h, accompanied by a progressive brightening and widening of the Tyndall light path. During this process, larger particles were degraded into smaller, more heterogeneous fragments, resulting in the coexistence of strong Mie scattering and Rayleigh scattering [64, 65]. This dual-scattering regime led to a continuous increase in both the angular distribution and intensity of the scattered light. After 10 h, the system stabilized, and the light path became brighter and more uniform, likely reflecting the NK-mediated degradation of nanoscale particles into even smaller species, thereby minimizing Rayleigh scattering interference from abundant small particles. Ultimately, a well-defined and intensified Tyndall scattering profile emerged [66]. As shown in Fig. 4E, initial human cataract phacoemulsification fluid displayed multiple protein bands, likely corresponding to native crystallins (~ 20 kDa) and high-molecular-weight aggregates (~ 25 kDa). Following NK treatment, the cataractous aggregates were markedly diminished or disappeared, accompanied by the appearance of new bands below 15 kDa, indicating that NK effectively hydrolyzed the aggregated proteins into smaller fragments. These findings provide direct evidence supporting the potential of NK to degrade crystallin aggregates and reverse cataract progression.

Evaluation of anti-cataract efficacy in vivo

To evaluate the anti-cataract efficacy of the NNs, we employed a well-established UVB-induced cataract mouse model. Although the model does not fully replicate the chronic progression of human age-related cataract, it essentially serves as an accelerated recapitulation of cumulative UVB exposure over years or even decades in the natural environment, and remains a well-validated model for oxidative stress-driven lens opacification. The overall pharmacodynamic scheme was outlined in Fig. 5A. Herein, we assessed the therapeutic effect of NNs on lens opacification using both traditional lens opacity grading and a newly proposed lens opacity (LO) index (Fig.S3). While conventional grading systems focus on morphological classification, the LO index integrates the percentage of opacity area and the average gray value of the lens that quantitatively reflect the extent and density of opacification, respectively. This multi-dimensional metric enables a more comprehensive evaluation of cataract progression and highlights intergroup pharmacodynamic differences. Compared with the healthy group, UVB irradiation resulted in significant lens opacification as observed in slit-lamp images (Fig. 5B). In contrast to the Model group, NNs efficiently reduced the severity of cataracts with no significant differences among them (Fig. 5C and Tab.S5). Notably, the LO index of the NK@PSA, NK@HA, NK@CS, and NK@PGA groups decreased by 45.2%, 39.2%, 40.2%, and 39.1% (****p < 0.0001), respectively, consistent with the lens opacity grade results.

Oxidative stress is a crucial mechanism underlying the development of cataracts. The lens relies on a delicate redox balance to maintain its elastic structure and optical transparency [67, 68]. Persistent oxidative stress disrupts metabolic homeostasis, activates inflammatory pathways, promotes crystallin denaturation, and initiates other pathological cascades that ultimately lead to lens opacification [69]. It is believed that there are antioxidant systems in the lens to counteract damage from ROS or free radicals, including SOD, GSH-Px, catalase (CAT), GSH, and MDA [70–72]. Thus, the lenses in each group were extracted to investigate the levels of oxidative stress and proinflammatory cytokines. The levels of SOD and GSH-Px were considerably increased, while the MDA level was markedly decreased in the NNs groups (Fig. 5D ~ F). Concurrently, both IL-1β and TNF-α were notably reduced in all NNs groups, indicating potent anti-inflammatory activity (Fig. 5G and H). These findings demonstrate that NNs effectively attenuate oxidative damage and help restore redox and inflammatory homeostasis in the lens with no significant differences among them.

Sustained oxidative stress and inflammation promote the conversion of soluble crystallins into insoluble aggregates, resulting in a gradual loss of lens transparency and cataract formation [15, 16]. Subsequent measurements were performed for total protein content in lens tissue. As shown in Fig. 5I, all treatment groups exhibited a notable elevation in total protein concentration, demonstrating that the NNs could potently suppress crystallin denaturation and preserve the normal status and function. Taken together, these results indicated the NNs exhibited antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and crystallin-protective effects with no statistically significant differences.

To investigate the ROS-inhibitory capacity of NNs, immunofluorescence staining with DHE and DAPI was conducted. This assay confirmed that the in vivo therapeutic effect of NNs was linked to ROS scavenging, as evidenced by reduced ROS levels across all the treatment groups (Fig. 6A). Compared with the Model group, the ROS levels were notably reduced (****p < 0.0001). These results indicate that NK@PSA and NK@CS possess the most potent ROS-inhibitory activity, followed by NK@PGA and NK@HA (Fig. 6B).

The ROS/NLRP3/pyroptosis axis has been increasingly implicated in cataract pathogenesis [74–76]. Excessive ROS production can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, which is composed of NLRP3 and apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a Caspase recruitment domain protein (ASC) [76]. Inflammasome assembly promotes the activation of Caspase-1, which in turn catalyzes the maturation and secretion of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, thereby amplifying local inflammation [77]. Upon activation, Caspase-1 cleaves GSDMD to generate its N-terminal fragments. These fragments subsequently oligomerize and form pores in the plasma membrane, thereby driving pyroptosis and exacerbating inflammatory tissue injury [78, 79].

Enlightened by these findings, the relevant key markers in lenses were assessed via immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 6C ~ G). Consistent with previous reports, UVB-induced ROS overproduction activated the NLRP3 inflammasome, upregulating downstream effectors and promoting pyroptosis [80]. The NNs effectively inhibited the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, as evidenced by the marked downregulation of its core components (NLRP3, ASC, Caspase-1) and downstream effectors (IL-1β, GSDMD) (Fig. 6H ~ L). These results suggest that NK interferes with ASC oligomerization and Caspase-1 activation by inhibiting ROS, leading to suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, diminished IL-1β production, inhibition of GSDMD cleavage, and ultimately attenuation of pyroptosis.

Western blot analysis

Given the established role of the NLRP3 signaling pathway in cataract progression [23], WB experiments were conducted to validate the expression levels of previously identified markers. The NNs mediated the downregulation of the key markers to varying degrees, in agreement with the immunohistochemical results. Compared with the Model group, NLRP3, ASC, Caspase-1, IL-1β, and GSDMD expression levels were accordingly decreased (Fig. 7A ~ E). Overall, these results indicate that the NNs significantly suppress the NLRP3 signaling pathway with varying degrees of potency.

Analysis of the crystallins

As a major constituent of crystallins, α-Crystallin accounts for up to 40% of the total crystallins [81]. It consists of αA- and αB-crystallin subunits and functions as a molecular chaperone that suppresses the aggregation of aberrant proteins. This chaperone activity is believed to confer resilience against age-related crystallin degradation, thereby delaying cataract formation [7, 82]. Dysfunction of αB-crystallin due to mutation or reduced expression may increase the accumulation of denatured proteins [7]. Given the critical role, the expression of αB-crystallin was analyzed by WB. Compared with the Model group, αB-crystallin levels were significantly elevated in the NNs groups (****p < 0.0001). These findings suggest that NNs are beneficial for protecting crystallins from UVB-induced damage, thereby contributing to maintaining lens transparency and function (Fig. 8A).

CD spectroscopy was employed to determine the secondary structure of crystallins. As shown in Fig. 8B, a negative peak was observed at ~ 195 nm, indicating the presence of α-helix, β-sheet, and β-turn conformations in the crystallins. A negative shoulder peak at ~ 215 nm suggested the existence of β-sheet and random coil structures, while a negative peak at ~ 225 nm verified the presence of α-helix elements. The Healthy group exhibited a distinct α-helix characteristic peak at ~ 225 nm, accompanied by only weak features at ~ 215 nm, reflecting a high α-helix content and overall structural integrity. In contrast, the Model group also showed a negative peak at ~ 225 nm, but it displayed pronounced tailing and overlap with the ~ 215 nm signal. Together with an intensified negative peak at ~ 195 nm, suggestive of increased random coil content, these spectral changes indicate widespread protein misfolding and aggregation, consistent with a substantial loss of native structure. Notably, the NK@PSA group exhibited spectral features intermediate between the Healthy and Model groups with moderate intensity. These results indicate that NK@PSA treatment effectively mitigated UVB-induced structural disruption and partially preserved the native α-helical architecture of crystallins.

As illustrated in Fig. 8C, the CD spectra exhibited positive peaks at approximately 255 nm and 275 nm, along with a negative peak near 285 nm, characteristic of disulfide bonds and aromatic amino acid residues in crystallins. The Healthy group displayed sharp, high-intensity peaks, indicating a well-ordered spatial conformation and structural stability. In contrast, the Model group showed markedly broader and flatter peaks with reduced intensity, suggesting protein aggregation, conformational disruption, and increased structural disorder. The spectral profile of the NK@PSA group fell between those of the Healthy and Model groups in both peak width and intensity, indicating partial preservation of native crystallin architecture.

Furthermore, the CD spectra were analyzed using the CONTINLL algorithm in CDpro software. The calculated proportions of α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil structures, along with the degree of molecular conformational change, are summarized in Fig. 8D. Quantitative analysis revealed a progressive decrease in α-helix and β-sheet content from the Healthy group to the NK@PSA group and further to the Model group, accompanied by a corresponding increase in random coil content and overall conformational disorder (Fig. 8E). These results confirm that UVB irradiation promotes the loss of ordered secondary structures and increases structural randomness in crystallins, the process was substantially mitigated by NK@PSA treatment. In summary, UVB exposure disrupts the ordered secondary structure of crystallins, leading to protein unfolding and aggregation. NK@PSA treatment effectively counteracts these deleterious changes, demonstrating its potential to preserve crystallin integrity and delay cataract development.

Fluorescence spectroscopy and UV-Vis spectrophotometry were further used to assess the structure of crystallins. As shown in Fig. 8F, the Healthy group displayed the highest fluorescence intensity, followed by the NK@PSA group, with the Model group exhibiting the lowest signal. In UV-Vis absorption measurements, the Model group showed increased absorbance relative to the Healthy group, likely due to enhanced light scattering from UVB-induced crystallin aggregation. The NK@PSA group exhibited intermediate absorbance levels, consistent with a partial preservation of crystallin solubility and structural organization (Fig. 8G).

Subsequently, protein aggregation was quantified using an established aggregation index based on absorbance at 280 nm and 350 nm [83, 84]. The index was increased progressively from the Healthy group to the NK@PSA group and further to the Model group, indicating a gradual rise in disordered crystallin assembly (Fig. 8H). Overall, these results demonstrate that NK@PSA treatment mitigates UVB-induced crystallin aggregation and preserves the structural and functional integrity of crystallins.

Safety evaluation

The body weight changes in mice serve as a valuable indicator of overall health and can indirectly reflect visual function. To identify the safety and potential systemic effects of the NNs, the body weight was monitored throughout the study (Fig. 9A). During the UVB-induced cataract modeling phase, daily anesthesia and localized ocular irritation likely contributed to reduced feeding and drinking, resulting in a slower weight gain rate in the Model and treatment groups. Throughout the subsequent treatment period, all groups exhibited a progressive increase in body weight with parallel trends. Notably, all the NNs groups displayed improved weight gain relative to the Model group, suggesting alleviation of cataract-associated visual decline and its impact on general well-being.

Histopathological evaluation of corneal and lens tissues was conducted at the end of the treatment period. As shown in Fig. 9C, the Healthy group displayed normal corneal morphology, featuring a well-organized non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, an intact Bowman’s layer and basal layer, and regularly arranged collagen fibers in the stroma without inflammatory infiltration. In contrast, the Model group exhibited severe structural alterations, including disorganized epithelial layers, disruption of the Bowman’s and basal layers, stromal edema, vacuolation, and marked inflammatory cell infiltration. Treatment with macromolecules alone partially alleviated the changes, with PSA and PGA groups showing more pronounced pathology consistent with their weaker antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities. All the NNs groups demonstrated near-normal corneal architecture, with only slight irregularity in epithelial cell arrangement. Lens tissues across all groups maintained an intact capsule and regularly aligned lens fibers (Fig. 9D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the NNs exhibit therapeutic potential in alleviating UVB-induced damage while maintaining an excellent safety profile.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed a minimalist NK nanocomposite eye drop to break the vicious cycle in UVB-induced cataract. The NNs effectively achieved three-pronged therapeutic effects, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-crystallin denaturation. It was first confirmed that NK could degrade cataractous tissue and alleviate the opacification using human cataract phacoemulsification fluid. In UVB-induced cataract mice, the NNs potently decreased oxidative stress and proinflammatory cytokine levels, protecting the crystallins through enhanced intracorneal retention and ultimately ameliorating the LO index. Moreover, NNs efficiently inhibited ROS accumulation by up to 15.78-fold, along with the ROS/NLRP3/pyroptosis axis and the denaturation of crystallins, offering new insights into therapeutic strategies for cataract. In conclusion, the NNs alleviated lens opacification by breaking the cataract vicious cycle through a three-pronged inhibition of oxidative stress, inflammation, and crystallin denaturation. Our findings pave the way for utilizing NNs as a novel strategy for reversing cataract, as well as associated ocular disorders.
