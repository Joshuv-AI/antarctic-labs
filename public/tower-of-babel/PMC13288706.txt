How is access to palliative care negotiated and accomplished among Muslim immigrants and their direct descendants in Germany, and what culturally and religiously framed narrative resources shape meaning-making in serious illness and approaching death? A multicentre four-group qualitative study using constructivist Grounded theory and Narrative inquiry
Authors: Florian Bernhardt, Ferya Banaz-Yaşar, María Rosa Salvador Comino, Thomas Altscher, Burkhard Dasch, Michael Terborg, Karin Scheer, Janina Krüger, David Rene Steike, Mitra Tewes, Philipp Lenz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMuslim immigrants and their direct descendants are a large ethnoreligious minority in many regions, yet their access to palliative care remains under-researched and poorly theorised for practice. Existing evidence points to structural, communicative and cultural constraints, while mechanisms at the threshold of access remain unclear.AimWe explored access to palliative care among this population, developed an explanatory model of access dynamics and reconstructed culturally and religiously framed narrative resources.DesignWe conducted a qualitative, multicentre, four-group study using constructivist Grounded theory with complementary Narrative inquiry. Loosely structured interviews followed Kaufmann’s ‘L’entretien compréhensif’ between August 2021 and December 2022.Setting/participantsThis study involved six centres in North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany. Participants were 53 adults from 14 countries in four groups (patients receiving palliative care vs not, relatives, interface stakeholders).ResultsThe Grounded theory SAME SAME BUT DIFFERENT conceptualises a paradox between formal equality and lived constraint at the threshold of access. Six analytical categories operated cyclically and mutually reinforced delayed access: (I) Othering/Belonging, (II) Information/Representation, (III) Role of the family, (IV) Palliative stigma, (V) ‘Language barrier’ as a multidimensional challenge and (VI) Dealing with life crisis, illness and death.Participants identified leverage points within the access dynamics, including native-language information and contact persons, openness to cultural-religious concerns, culture- and religion-sensitive training, Muslim support services and spiritual care and non-denominational environments.Narrative inquiry reconstructed four culturally and religiously framed narrative resources that shaped access decisions.ConclusionsIntersecting structural, communicative and meaning-making dynamics affect access to palliative care. Culturally adaptive, multilingual and community-engaged approaches require evaluation and should be supported by improved routine sociodemographic data.

Introduction

Migration is a defining demographic factor in Europe and many other regions worldwide. Nearly 87 million international migrants reside in Europe, over 40 million of whom originate from non-European countries.1 In Germany alone, 20–22 million people (25.1%–27.2%) have a migration background,2 and 5.3–5.6 million (6.4%–6.7%) are Muslims.3 Therefore, people with a history of migration are increasingly experiencing serious illnesses and end-of-life care within their host country’s healthcare system rather than in their countries of origin.

Palliative care, as defined by the WHO, is an approach that aims to improve the quality of life of patients and their relatives facing problems associated with life-threatening illnesses.4

Globally, access to palliative care remains uneven. Community- or home-based palliative care exists in 43% and primary care-based palliative care in 42% of WHO member states, with both models more common in high-income than in low-income countries.5 Yet even within high-income countries, access is unequally distributed.6 7

In Germany, such gaps affect migrants, especially Muslim immigrants.8 9 Reported barriers include cultural-religious factors,10 11 although their significance remains debated given limited research.12

Existing qualitative research suggests that barriers arise from structural gaps alongside cultural and communicative disjunctions. Muslim patients and families have been shown to navigate conflicting value frameworks and interpersonal expectations at the end of life.13 A qualitative evidence synthesis further links language inflexibility, social isolation and resource constraints to delayed access and increased caregiving burdens.14

However, larger-scale studies focusing specifically on access to palliative care for ethnic and religious minorities remain scarce, which limits understanding of underlying dynamics and potential solutions.15 Therefore, this study specifically focuses on access to palliative care among Muslim immigrants and their direct descendants, one of the largest ethnoreligious minorities in Germany and many other high-income countries.

Our primary, a priori research question is as follows: How is access to palliative care for Muslim immigrants and their direct descendants in Germany negotiated and accomplished between those providing and those receiving care, and what conditions shape actions and interactions at the point of referral and early engagement?

Our secondary, post hoc and complementary research question is as follows: What culturally and religiously shaped narrative resources do participants draw on to make sense of serious illness and approaching death, and how do these resources orient decisions about whether, when and how to engage with palliative care?

Methods

Study design

This is a multicentre, four-group, qualitative study. Recruitment took place between August 2021 and December 2022. The study combines two analytic lenses: (I) constructivist Grounded theory and (II) Narrative inquiry.

Our approach was underpinned by an interpretivist, constructivist stance, assuming that experiences of access are co-constructed in interaction and can be analysed to develop an explanatory, context-sensitive model. This stance is compatible with narrative analytic attention to how participants make meaning through culturally and religiously available storylines.

Setting

This study was conducted in North Rhine-Westphalia, a federal state in Germany with about 18 million inhabitants, around 30% with migration background, mostly Turkish or Syrian.2

Participants were recruited via two university hospitals, two specialised outpatient palliative care providers, a municipal hospital and an outpatient hospice service.

For patients and relatives, eligibility required self-identification as Muslim. We applied a narrow definition of migration, covering only the first and second generations. Further inclusion criteria for patients were legal age, capacity to consent and a WHO-defined palliative care condition. Minors and patients lacking capacity to consent were excluded.

‘Interface stakeholders’ were defined as professionals who frequently influence, initiate or gatekeep access to palliative care for our target population. ‘Relevant areas’ were defined a priori as services or roles involved in referral, information provision, symptom management, care coordination or psychosocial and spiritual support.

All participants provided informed consent. Written study materials were multilingual and explained by interpreters if necessary.

Participants were purposively invited by previously known employees of the collaborating institutions. Sampling followed maximum variation across diverse sociodemographic and migration-related characteristics and was extended through theoretical sampling to refine emerging categories.

Refusals and dropouts were not systematically recorded as recruitment was facilitated by multiple (partly non-academic) clinical and community gatekeepers and contact attempts were made within routine care. Based on informal, iterative feedback, we assume that between three and five patients and relatives were approached per interview conducted.

Data collection

Interviews followed Kaufmann’s ‘L’entretien compréhensif’, a loosely structured, conversational approach designed to elicit participants’ subjective perspectives.16 This framework proved effective in migration-sensitive palliative care research.17

Interview guidelines (online supplemental appendix 1) were developed from the literature, piloted with three participants of varying German proficiency and refined through expert feedback. For interface stakeholders, interview guidelines were developed profession- and field-specific. Additionally, we collected sociodemographic data (online supplemental appendix 2).

All interviews were conducted by a male person (FB, then a university-employed junior researcher, now a medical doctor) with several years of qualitative research experience.

Before inclusion, he established personal contact and disclosed his background in a region with a high Muslim-immigrant population and the study’s relation to his doctoral thesis.

Interviews took place on-site or online, with certified interpreters if needed. Interpreters were briefed on verbatim translation and neutrality and debriefed to capture nuances. Third-party presence was discouraged and accepted only if unavoidable.

All interviews were audio-recorded and verbatim-transcribed.18 Transcripts were not returned, but participants could clarify or revise statements immediately post-interview. Our results were not shared post-analysis, as most patients had died.

Data analysis

Our analysis included two separate stages using the same dataset. Two researchers (FB, PL) independently analysed the transcripts using f4 software and then consolidated their analyses. Any coding discrepancies were resolved collaboratively.

While core analysis proceeded based on constructivist Grounded theory,19 we identified recurring storylines reflecting culturally and religiously framed meaning-making processes. To address these narrative resources systematically, we applied Narrative inquiry20 as a complementary analytic lens after the Grounded theory model had stabilised. This dual strategy enabled both a ‘thick description’ and categorical model of access dynamics and captured culturally and religiously framed narrative resources illustrating how participants embedded experiences in broader cultural-religious frameworks.

Narrative findings are presented as complementary to the Grounded theory model and do not constitute an independent explanatory model or category.

Primary analytic lens (a priori): constructivist Grounded theory

At first, our coding proceeded from initial to focused, with constant comparison and memo-writing. Emerging categories were iteratively discussed, initially categorised case-wise, then reviewed per study group and theoretically integrated into higher-order conceptual categories across all groups. Data collection and analysis continued in parallel until theoretical saturation, defined as the point when no new conceptual categories emerged and the explanatory model was stable across the four participant groups.

Secondary analytic lens (post hoc): Narrative inquiry

Subsequently, we used Narrative inquiry to extract recurrent culturally and religiously framed narrative resources from our dataset, to situate them in context and to reconstruct storylines that shaped orientations towards palliative care access.

Trustworthiness and reflexivity

Trustworthiness was supported through analyst triangulation, audit trail and reflexive team debriefs. Given the emotionally demanding topic and the deterioration or death of many participants, the research process included regular team debriefings that also served to reflect emotional strain and support researcher well-being.

To reduce ethnocentrism, our interdisciplinary team included researchers with and without migration and Muslim backgrounds.

To avoid a deficit framing, and consistent with our constructivist approach, we conceptualised access dynamics (including constraining and enabling conditions) as relational and structural processes that emerge within care pathways and interactions, rather than as shortcomings of individuals or communities. Analytically, we also attended to resources, competencies and adaptive practices mobilised by participants wherever these were evident in the data.

Potential influence of interviewer gender, perceived positionality and differences between interpreter-mediated and non-mediated interviews were considered and reflexively discussed. However, no clear pattern of relevant differences between interpreter-mediated and non-mediated interviews emerged regarding interview acceptance, interview conduct, analytic depth or data interpretation.

German quotes were translated by fluent speakers. Please refer to online supplemental appendix 3 for verbatim German quotes.

Ethics

The study conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethics committees of the Universities of Münster (2021-441-f-S; 18 August 2021) and Duisburg-Essen (21-10403-BO; 11 April 2022). We report according to the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative studies (online supplemental appendix 5).21

Analysis of sociodemographic characteristics

Sociodemographic characteristics were summarised descriptively using SPSS and SAS. No inferential statistics were conducted. The world map was generated using Microsoft PowerPoint for Mac.

Patient and public involvement

Non-academic partners with expertise in clinical care, hospice and palliative care and community-facing work were involved in the study from the outset. They were approached through existing clinical and community networks of the participating centres and contributed to the research question, interview guidelines, study design, recruitment and interpretation of findings. Further stakeholders from relevant care and community contexts were additionally consulted before, during and after the study. Patients and relatives were involved selectively during study development and conduct, particularly to support non-deficit-oriented framing, culturally and religiously sensitive wording and practicable recruitment procedures.

Grounded theory: SAME SAME BUT DIFFERENT

The Grounded theory SAME SAME BUT DIFFERENT integrates the analysis across our four study groups (figure 2). It integrates six analytical categories into an explanatory model of how access is negotiated and accomplished at the threshold of referral and early engagement. Participants perceived palliative care as something that should be equally available, yet still experienced access as uncertain, conditional or delayed. Culture, religion and migration were often characterised as ‘not the issue’ on the surface, while cultural and religious frames were repeatedly used to explain why access felt more difficult.

Although some empirical material touched several categories simultaneously, each category captures a distinct analytic dimension of access dynamics rather than an exclusive thematic area.

The six analytical categories operated cyclically and were mutually reinforcing. We observed them both among participants already receiving palliative care and those not yet admitted, suggesting that many barriers seem to occur primarily at the threshold of access. Where relevant, nuances between groups are indicated directly within the categories.

Participants also articulated five context-sensitive solutions to facilitate and improve access. To aid readability, we mapped these participant-identified leverage points within the category or categories where they emerged most prominently in the data. Yet, these opportunities also depend on each other and operate across the cyclical access dynamics, rather than being confined to a single category.

Othering/Belonging

Othering/Belonging captures the relational and symbolic positioning of Muslim immigrants and their direct descendants within palliative care. Participants moved along a continuum of marginalisation (‘othering’) to recognition and inclusion (‘belonging’), sometimes even within the same care trajectory. These dynamics were not reducible to length of residence or formal markers of integration but emerged in everyday interactions with professionals and institutions.

Othering was described when people felt treated as less entitled to support. One participant stated:

“I have been living in Germany for almost 50 years. […] Nevertheless, I often feel like a second-class citizen because I am denied important support. […] I don’t understand if this is because I’m not a German citizen. I just want to get the same funding and support as German citizens. I need care, but they withhold it from me because I am a Muslim and Turkish woman, although I am entitled to it.” (Interview 14)

Such accounts framed exclusion as structural rather than accidental. Being positioned as ‘second-class’ undermined trust in services and contributed to hesitancy to approach palliative care, as it did not necessarily feel available to ‘people like us’.

Belonging, by contrast, was narrated as recognition and legitimate inclusion. One participant explained:

“[…] We are all equal, but many other Muslims see it differently. They say Muslims should keep to themselves. That’s not an issue for us, we live here in Germany, a beautiful country, and I experienced golden times here in the 70s and 80s. (cries)” (Interview 30)

Feeling entitled to care, and seeing one’s own values reflected in practice, made engagement with palliative care more acceptable. However, identification with both ‘German’ and ‘Muslim’ cultural repertoires could trigger disagreement within families about how illness and dying should be handled.

Participant-identified leverage points to facilitate access

Openness to and addressing of patients’ culturally relevant concerns was indicated as a way to mitigate othering and foster belonging. Recognising cultural identity as a legitimate resource for care—rather than an obstacle—was described as central to equitable access.

Information/Representation

Information/Representation captures how Muslim immigrants and their direct descendants encounter palliative care through limited information and visibility. Participants reported that knowledge about palliative care often does not reach migrant communities and that Muslim people are barely represented within palliative care structures. As a result, participants frequently did not know what palliative care was, whether it applied to them or how to access it. Professionals also reported uncertainty about Islamic views on death and dying, signalling that Muslim perspectives are not reliably included in routine professional knowledge.

Lack of exposure to palliative care before advanced illness was common. One participant stated:

“I’ve heard about palliative care for the first time today and have no thoughts. In 26 years, I’ve only been ill once, had a fever and took Ibuprofen. There are no such concepts in Pakistan because the family system is different over there, so I’ve never heard of it.” (Interview 9)

In such accounts, palliative care appeared unfamiliar and externally defined and therefore not immediately legitimate. This delayed engagement and made early conversations about palliative care less likely.

Participants also described a mutual gap: communities often lack information about palliative care, and services lack strategies to reach these communities or address their needs. As one participant put it:

“When I think of both terms, I see two worlds that know little about each other. Many Muslims are not familiar with palliative care and ask: ‘What is that?’ […] Conversely, palliative care providers often lack knowledge about Muslim migrants, such as how to take their needs into account or how to reach them. I perceive a great distance between both.” (Interview 53)

Participant-identified leverage points to facilitate access

Access to native-language information and contact persons was viewed as critical to reduce informational asymmetries and make palliative care feel approachable.

Role of the family

Role of the family highlights how kinship structures act both as a resource and constraint in engaging with palliative care. Family members frequently mediated between patients and professionals, bridged linguistic and informational gaps and provided care at home for as long as possible. However, concentrating responsibility within the family could overwhelm individuals, postpone contact with palliative care and generate pressure linked to honour, duty and reputation.

Protecting the patient was a recurring pattern, especially around intimate and end-of-life care at home. One participant explained:

“Often, external help is not accepted because of the fear that outsiders will interfere and not understand the cultural concept. Sensitive topics such as intimate care lead relatives to shield their parents from third parties. However, this often results in unnecessary decompensation at home and hospitalisation, which could be avoided with better structures. Also, palliative care as a discipline is still in its infancy in Muslim countries.” (Interview 48)

Relatives thus acted as gatekeepers to preserve dignity and protect cultural or religious norms around the body and soul. This self-protection strategy could have clinical consequences: palliative or supportive input was deferred, care escalated in the home and avoidable hospital admissions occurred.

Families also described a duty to provide all care themselves, even at significant personal cost. One participant said:

“In our family, unlike most Germans, it’s a matter of course that we take care of our relatives, including until they die. When my mother was ill, her doctor suggested a care service, but that’s out of the question for us. I would even pause my education to spend time with her and make sure all her wishes are fulfilled. I feel obliged to do so.” (Interview 35)

Caregiving was framed not as optional but as obligatory. That duty could make professional support feel culturally inappropriate or disloyal and could delay or prevent use of palliative care.

Participant-identified leverage points to facilitate access

Establishment of culture-sensitive and/or Muslim support services and spiritual care was proposed as a way to relieve individual relatives and support families without undermining dignity.

Palliative stigma

Palliative stigma describes moralised and affective associations attached to palliative care. When it was known at all, palliative care was often equated with imminent death, treatment withdrawal or ‘nothing more can be done’. These associations contributed to reluctance to talk about palliative care early. For many participants, entering palliative care felt incompatible with what they saw as their expected responsibilities.

One participant stated:

“I think about death, of course, and also about the burden on the family. I couldn’t imagine burdening my family with the responsibility of having to make such decisions for me. It scares me a little to think that I might end up in this kind of situation where the main thing is to die. Or is it not like that? Palliative care just sounds like death to me, it’s off-putting.” (Interview 1)

Here, palliative care was not perceived as supportive care alongside serious illness. It was perceived as the point at which ‘the main thing is to die’ and as a setting that shifts heavy moral responsibility onto family members. Fear of ‘ending up there’ was described as a reason to avoid or postpone engagement with palliative care services.

Another participant said:

“I associate palliative care with the last breaths of a patient’s life. I think it starts when the doctor says, ‘I’m sorry, there’s nothing more we can do for you, you need palliative care.’ I think the term is primarily perceived as negative.” (Interview 32)

This framing positioned palliative care as something that begins only after curative or life-prolonging efforts are declared over. This made palliative care feel morally and emotionally unacceptable: it is associated with ‘giving up’, abandonment by the health system and direct confrontation with death. Some participants who eventually received palliative care reported relief when these assumptions did not match their actual experience and expressed regret about not having accessed support earlier.

Participant-identified leverage points to facilitate access

Culture- and religion-sensitive training of healthcare professionals was highlighted as essential to counteract stigmatising associations and to communicate palliative care as supportive rather than as surrender.

‘Language barrier’ as a multidimensional challenge

‘Language barrier’ as a multidimensional challenge conceptualises communicative difficulties not only as limited language proficiency but as a complex interactional phenomenon. Communication often relied on relatives or ad hoc interpreters, introducing risks of selective disclosure and misinterpretation. Missing direct translations for ‘palliative care’ reinforced uncertainty about what was being offered. Patients also reported hiding comprehension difficulties out of embarrassment. Exhaustion and language regression further complicated conversations at precisely the point when nuanced discussions about goals of care were required.

One participant explained:

“At the beginning of my hospital stay, it was difficult, not as a Muslim, but as a foreigner. I couldn’t speak German very well, and sometimes my German didn’t flow properly, perhaps because of my medication. Today, for example, I asked a doctor something, but he didn’t really answer. Then I followed up and they told me a bit more. Then they start to speak. But otherwise I often get the feeling that they don’t have any interest in talking to me.” (Interview 24)

This highlights how communication barriers were experienced as relational and status-related, not just linguistic. Limited language skills were also linked with dependence on others. Another participant mentioned:

“There are still major language problems with older migrants, whereas this is less the case with the second and third generations. This is complicated due to the fact that the healthcare system in Germany is not well known and there is a lack of relevant information. If someone is unable to express themselves because they use different wording in their native language, it’s very hard to explain to the doctor what’s wrong with them or what feelings and symptoms they’re experiencing.” (Interview 51)

Language was described not as a simple translation task but as a barrier to being understood medically. If pain, distress or fears cannot be conveyed clearly, healthcare professionals may assume understanding where there is none, and patients and families may not feel able to seek help.

Participant-identified leverage points to facilitate access

Access to native-language information and contact persons was repeatedly emphasised as a strategy to counteract communicative asymmetries and to foster earlier engagement.

Dealing with life crisis, illness and death

Dealing with life crisis, illness and death captures existential orientations, as expressed through collective practices. Participants described efforts to maintain stability and hope and often avoided open discussions of dying to protect the patient and preserve morale. At the same time, emotional and practical processing took place ‘behind the scenes’, including preparation for death and mourning in more private settings. This created a split between what was said in the patient’s presence and what was acknowledged outside. In this sense, existential orientations became visible not as abstract beliefs but through patterned ways of handling prognosis, deterioration and dying.

One participant described this patterned division:

“Openly discussing dying or palliative care is usually difficult, but it is particularly challenging with Muslim patients. On the one hand, it is difficult to discuss this topic openly within the whole family, but on the other hand, there is often a well-prepared tradition of dying, such as death insurance and the repatriation of the corpse. Suffering is not discussed in the patient’s room, where attempts are made to motivate the patient to get up and eat. Outside, however, in one-to-one conversations or with each other, they talk openly about the deteriorating condition and death. This leads to a kind of division: outside the door, relatives talk about the imminent death, while a different attitude is adopted inside the room. This is a kind of ritualized play that is internalized. It is not necessarily an enactment but has functional significance for the family. And surprisingly, after death, things often resolve much more easily than we would have expected during the dramatic days and weeks leading up to it.” (Interview 52)

Another participant described how, within their cultural background, death is often handled in a way that is emotionally intense but verbally suppressed:

“In my native culture, death is handled much more dramatically. It is rarely discussed, rather suppressed, and it often manifests in grief or a sombre mood in the family. I think this is particularly the case in Muslim communities, such as in Western Balkans, Turkey or the Middle East, where death is handled differently.” (Interview 17)

These accounts suggested that dealing with life crises, illness and impending death was not only an individual psychological task but also a collectively enacted practice through which existential orientations became socially organised. Within close relational contexts, what was voiced and what was withheld was actively managed. This protective silence could postpone explicit conversations about deterioration, prognosis and palliative care even when decline was already recognised internally.

Participant-identified leverage points to facilitate access

Structuring hospitals and palliative care as non-denominational environments was suggested as a way to respect diverse existential orientations without privileging particular worldviews.

Narrative inquiry: culturally and religiously framed narrative resources with relevance for access to palliative care

Our Narrative inquiry reconstructed four recurring culturally and religiously framed narratives that shaped how participants made sense of serious illness, decision-making and end-of-life care (figure 3). These narrative resources helped participants to distinguish which responses to serious illness were seen as legitimate and which appeared rather morally difficult.

Narrative inquiry: recurring culturally and religiously framed narrative resources orienting engagement with palliative care.

Their relevance, however, varied greatly and intersected with other structural and relational factors.

Miracles are possible

This narrative situates illness within an open horizon of divine intervention, sustaining hope even in terminal stages. The possibility of a miracle morally justified continuing maximal therapy and postponed consideration of palliative care. One participant said:

“I think you should never give up hope. Doctors may tell me that there is no chance, but I will always hold onto God as my last hope. No matter how many doctors say the same thing, I will hold onto the belief that God can make it happen at any time.” (Interview 9)

Destiny is predetermined

In this narrative, death is framed as divinely fixed and ultimately beyond human control. This created tension between submitting to what is ‘already decided’ and the demand to accept every possible treatment so as not to appear to hasten death. One participant explained:

“Belief in destiny and predestination is deeply rooted in Islamic culture. But many misunderstand destiny and use it to avoid taking responsibility for their actions, saying, ‘It’s destiny, I can’t help it.’ The idea of God’s omnipotence is also particularly important in this context. Regarding palliative care, this often leads to the attitude, ‘We have to do everything because God is almighty and can still save this person.’ Even when doctors say that cure is beyond the bounds of possibility, they continue to pursue maximum therapy because they still believe there is a chance. But this understanding of destiny and God’s omnipotence is completely wrong from a religious perspective.” (Interview 41)

Illness as a test

Here, serious illness is narrated as a spiritual trial in which suffering acquires meaning through patience and endurance. Withdrawing from escalation or prioritising palliative care could be seen as failing that test. As one participant described:

“We are told that God gives dear people illnesses to see how they deal with them and how they react to Him. I have studied the Quran for 40 years and prayed five times a day, but in the last two or three years, when I have gone through many illnesses, I feel that I have drifted away from it. Although I still want to hold onto God, it’s hard for me, especially during this difficult time I’m going through. I often ask myself, ‘Why?’ Maybe God sees this as a test to assess how I deal with it.” (Interview 24)

Fear of committing sins by wrong decisions

In this narrative, medical decision-making is constructed as morally charged. Limiting treatment or ‘allowing death’ was feared as a possible sin for which relatives or the affected person could be held accountable. This uncertainty prolonged life-sustaining measures and shifted responsibility onto healthcare professionals. One participant expressed:

“I am not a doctor and cannot make informed decisions. That’s why I ask doctors, ‘What are the options, what’s best?’ When they recommend radiation or chemotherapy and explain the side effects, I trust them. Everyone has their profession, and I rely on their expertise. I consult with several doctors, and when they all say similar things, I follow their recommendation. That way, I am not responsible to God or anyone else if they ask why I didn’t do something differently later on. For me, it is very important to do as much as I can for my parents, because in our faith, parental happiness is sacred. It is the key to paradise, while their unhappiness could be the reason for going to the fire.” (Interview 4)

Discussion

Main findings/results of the study

We developed the Grounded theory SAME SAME BUT DIFFERENT, which conceptualises access to palliative care for Muslim immigrants and their direct descendants as shaped by a central paradox: palliative care is understood as something that should be equally available, yet participants described multiple potential barriers to accessing it. Six analytical categories capture how symbolic boundaries, epistemic asymmetries, relational negotiations, stigmatised meanings, communicative infrastructures and existential orientations intersect.

In addition, our Narrative inquiry reconstructed four recurring culturally and religiously framed narrative resources that provided culturally and religiously embedded logics of decision-making in serious illness.

Together, these patterns help explain why access to palliative care is currently often delayed or refused and indicate context-specific opportunities for intervention.

What this study adds

Our study shifts attention from experiences within palliative care to the threshold of access. Access is not a single referral event but an accumulative process shaped by interdependent dynamics. Late theoretical saturation after 53 participants reflects this complexity and supports the view of palliative care as a complex adaptive system,22 consistent with previous studies on migrants reporting multifactorial themes.17 23 24

Cultural-religious factors strongly influenced whether palliative care was regarded as acceptable, echoing earlier findings on their relevance during ongoing care.2527 Our data indicate that these factors already operate at the point of first contact, not only once palliative care is already established. It is crucial to emphasise, though, that culturally and religiously motivated rejections partly stem from palliative care-related prejudices that may change with culturally sensitive information. Yet, such prejudices are widespread, and the ‘stigma’ associated with palliative care is not exclusive to Muslim communities.28

An intersectional lens helps interpret our findings without attributing access dynamics to ‘culture’ or ‘religion’ alone. Access to palliative care is shaped by intersecting structural positions and system-level conditions (such as language mediation, socioeconomic resources, legal or administrative precarity and experiences of discrimination) which can co-occur with culturally and religiously shaped determinants. Structural vulnerability frameworks highlight how such conditions constrain health-related options and interactions in clinical care and migration contexts.29 30 Intersectional qualitative work in end-of-life care among migrant groups further illustrates how ethnicity, religion, gender and impairment can jointly shape social position and care experiences.31 In palliative care specifically, reviews show that migrants’ utilisation and access are associated with multilevel factors at patient, provider, system and societal levels. This highlights the need for multilevel responses rather than explanations based on single factors.32 More broadly, evidence also links language and discrimination to unequal healthcare access for migrants, supporting an interpretation of access dynamics as structurally patterned rather than community ‘shortcomings’.33 34

Consistent with previous indications that many Muslims in non-Western countries have limited knowledge about palliative care,35 our findings underline the need for accessible and comprehensible information. Interpreters and multilingual resources were described as essential for equitable access but are still scarce.36

Participants also described deliberate distancing from parts of German society, a form of ‘othering’ rooted in both minority and majority dynamics.37

Furthermore, strong family structures were often cited as making palliative care unnecessary. Epidemiological data confirm that Muslims in Germany are twice as likely as non-Muslims to live with children and have larger households.3 However, studies show that such claims often mask honour-driven caregiving ideals, high caregiver burden and limited support.23 38 This reflects tensions between collectivist caregiving obligations and autonomy-oriented models that underpin much of Western palliative care.35 39 40

Strengths and weaknesses/limitations of the study

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to examine access to palliative care among an ethnoreligious minority in a Western, predominantly Christian context using a multicentre, multigroup qualitative design. A particular strength is the inclusion of participants without prior palliative care experience, who are rarely captured yet central to understanding access dynamics.

A potential limitation is the absence of quantitative data. This was intentional, as origin and religion are scarcely documented in German healthcare, which limits data validity and risks reproducing structural biases (eg, name-based categorisation). We also did not include a non-Muslim, non-migrant comparison group to avoid framing our findings as a deviation from an assumed norm.

Conclusions and implications for research and practice

Lower palliative care utilisation among ethnoreligious minorities emerges from complex, interdependent dynamics.

Equitable access requires culturally adaptive care, specialised training, structural changes and community agency.

From a research perspective, reconsidering the limited collection of sociodemographic data in healthcare is essential, as its absence constrains epidemiological insight and may distort disparity assessments.
