A Large Language Model for Extracting Post-marketing Adverse Drug Events from Clinical Notes in the Electronic Health Record
Authors: Dana Ludwig, Michelle Wang, James Buchanan, Trang Trinh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical notes contain a vast amount of potentially useful information about adverse drug event (ADE) signals that never reach pharmacovigilance databases. Traditional rule-based or sentence-level models often miss subtle causal cues and generate excess false positives.ObjectiveTo build a large language model (LLM) pipeline that reads entire electronic health record (EHR) notes, identifies drug-event pairs with a “reasonable possibility” of causation, and infers other important properties of each ADR, such whether the event is “serious” or “unlabeled”.MethodsWe adopted a two-pass workflow using model “OpenAI o1”: Pass 1 screens each note for ADEs; Pass 2 adds 20 structured fields. A diverse sample of 372 deidentified notes from physicians and pharmacists at the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF), from 31 specialty/setting cells, yielded 191 ADEs. One medical expert reviewed each ADE for validity, seriousness, and label status. Another expert created a gold standard manually curated ADE set on 100 of the 372 ADEs to give us a percent “recall” estimate. A third expert met with the first expert to arrive at a consensus on the validity of LLM ADEs validated by the first expert but not found in the gold standard ADEs, giving us a estimate of “accuracy”.ResultsOf 191 ADEs, 180 were true positives (94.2% precision) with 84.1% recall (F1 = 88.9%). Seriousness was correct in 100% and label status in 93.9% of cases. Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) lowest level term (LLT) coding was correct in 92.5% of valid ADEs; errors were mostly non-existent LLTs. Of all valid ADEs, 12.2% met FDA “serious” criteria, 15.0% were unlabeled, and 8.9% were “failure of efficacy.” On the first pass, 84.9% of notes contained no ADEs, keeping inference costs to USD $0.18 per note and $0.35 per validated ADE. The model inferred some ADEs not mentioned by physicians, e.g., tacrolimus-associated hypomagnesemia.ConclusionsWhile the LLM evaluated in this study is not perfect, it can transform free-text EHR notes into ADEs with 94% accuracy, and such data, when statistically analyzed in aggregate, can lead to new safety signals of potential drug side effects. Integrated with platforms like Sentinel in the USA, or Darwin EU, in the European Union, this approach could rapidly surface rare, serious, and unlabeled ADEs for further regulatory analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40264-026-01682-6.

Key Points

This paper introduces an LLM-based model capable of extracting ADEs from a diverse variety of clinical notes with 94.2% precision and 84.1% recall.If used by regulatory authorities, such a model would substantially increase the availability of drug safety signals beyond what is available today.The cost of the current model, charged by the LLM vendor, averages $0.35 USD per ADE extracted. This cost will be reduced with newer models and open-source LLM models.

Introduction

Adverse drug events (ADEs) are a major threat to patient safety, contributing to avoidable morbidity, prolonged hospitalizations, adherence challenges, early termination of treatments, and substantial costs. Post-marketing safety surveillance is especially challenging because most reporting is voluntary, leading to underreporting of clinically meaningful events [1].

Current real-world data approaches illustrate both opportunities and gaps. For instance, electronic health record (EHR)-based surveillance often relies on coded data (billing or diagnostic codes) as proxies for ADEs, missing important mentions in clinical free text such as progress notes and discharge summaries [2]. Additionally, structured data alone does not usually provide enough information to suggest a “reasonable possibility” of a causal relationship, whereas notes may reflect the provider’s thinking about causality, such as “drug A was stopped out of concern for event B.” Pattern-matching approaches in clinical notes may overlook crucial context, including temporal relationships and physician decisions to discontinue a medication – factors that help determine whether there is a “reasonable possibility” that the drug caused the event.

“Reasonable possibility” is a phrase that is used throughout this document. In a 1997 guidance to industry, the FDA stated that case reports based on solicited information from patients should not be reported unless, among other things, “there is a reasonable possibility that the drug or biologic product caused the adverse experience” [3]. In another document, the FDA defines “reasonable responsibility”: “For the purposes of IND safety reporting, ‘reasonable possibility’ means there is evidence to suggest a causal relationship between the drug and the adverse event” [4]. While this definition is necessarily vague, “reasonable possibility” is an important filter to prevent downstream users of the data from being overwhelmed with events that are unlikely to be drug related, especially in a data source that is not a randomized clinical trial with a control group but is data from patient care that is documented for purposes other than ADE reporting. Using the “reasonable possibility” filter depends on some level of expert human-like judgment in any proposed model.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) allow clinical notes to be interpreted in a way comparable to a clinician reading the note [5]. These tools can identify whether the physician changed a dose because of an event, verify the event’s biological plausibility, and assess whether it was merely an indication for prescribing the drug or a hypothetical possibility as mentioned in patient instructions. Such contextual understanding helps confirm that the drug was likely causing the event, rather than simply co-occurring in the same note.

Once captured, these ADEs can be analyzed with methods such a disproportionality analysis [6] to detect higher than expected event frequencies or confirm that known ADEs are occurring more often than reported in clinical trials. Such signals enable regulatory authorities to prioritize reviews of potentially important safety concerns.

In this study, we develop and evaluate a system using structured prompts with a state-of-the-art LLM (OpenAI o1) to extract ADEs from diverse EHR clinical notes. The approach identifies ADE mentions with a “reasonable possibility” of being drug related and records key attributes (e.g., seriousness, labeling status, and relation to the present illness). We report precision and recall of extracted ADEs, aiming to validate an affordable, scalable tool for governments and drug manufacturers to more rapidly detect signals of drug safety problems to improve patient safety.

Previous Work

Machine learning for ADE detection in clinical text gained prominence with the 2018 n2c2 challenge on ADEs and medication extraction [7]. Teams worked on concept extraction, relation extraction, and end-to-end extraction using 505 discharge summaries from MIMIC-III [8]. The winning team (UT Health) reported an end-to-end F1 of 0.4755 for the ADE-Drug task, noting challenges such as minimal ADEs per note and the presence of events spanning sentences [9]. At that time, neural network-based models were not yet widely used in studies detecting ADEs in clinical notes.

Subsequently, the winning team utilized Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) for a simplified sentence-level ADE→Drug classification, reaching an F1 of 0.8124 [1]. Another group fine-tuned BioBERT to classify whether or not a sentence contained an ADE, using pre-annotated drug and event pairs, reporting precision, recall and F1 of 0.95, 0.99 and 0.97, respectively [10]. While the performance was impressive, the task was simplified to determining whether or not a pre-annotated drug and event pair in a single sentence represented an ADE.

In 2023, researchers tested generalizability between different datasets (2018 n2c2 and immune checkpoint inhibitor notes) and found ClinicalBERT performed best though cross-domain performance dropped by ~10% [11].

Also in 2023, a Microsoft Research team fine-tuned PubMedBERT using GPT-3.5 outputs, producing an end-to-end ADE extraction model with an F1 of 91.99, slightly below the 93.36 F1 obtained via direct gold-standard training [12, 13]. While the F1 scores were impressive, the corpus of free text was sourced from PubMed abstracts of case reports rather than clinical notes, and the size of the test text was limited to a single sentence.

Another group fine-tuned GPT-3.5 for the VAERS flu vaccine reports, achieving a relaxed F1 of 0.816 when detecting Guillain-Barré Syndrome and other neurologic symptoms [14].

In 2024, a study used GPT-3.5 to analyze 10,000 social media posts about a cannabis-derived product, matching human annotators in 94.4% of all ADE cases [15].

A 2024 the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) team (including some authors here) developed a BERT-based end-to-end ADE extraction model for serious adverse events (SAE) leading to hospitalization in patients on biologic therapy for inflammatory bowel disease. Leveraging a hierarchical BERT (H-UCSF-BERT) to handle up to 512 tokens, they achieved a macro F1 of 61% for medication-hospitalization-SAE extraction [16, 17].

In 2025, a Swedish team reported an F1 of 0.70 for ADE relation extraction using a BERT model fine-tuned on Swedish clinical notes, noting only about 15% of notes contained explicit drug-ADE mentions [2, 18].

Methods

This section describes the methods and data that went into building and evaluating the final model.

Source and Diversity of Clinical Notes

All clinical notes were drawn from the UCSF deidentified clinical notes having an encounter date > September 5, 2023, so they would best reflect drugs with current US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) product labels. Only notes with at least 256 characters that were authored by physicians or clinical pharmacists, that were patient care or procedure notes, and not clinical lab, pathology or radiology notes.

This set of notes was further subdivided into 31 classes in an attempt to create the maximum diversity of clinical cases between classes. The validation dataset was drawn from equal numbers of notes from each of the 31 classes and randomly selected within each class. The set of notes was further constrained to contain no more than one note from the same patient, and to exclude all notes that had previously been used for earlier testing. See Fig. 1 for a summary. Also see online resource, Appendix section “Version 4 SQL Queries used to generate the 93 Notes” for the definitions of the 31 classes.

The validation set used for the analysis below randomly selected 12 notes from each of the 31 classes, resulting in 372 notes. The 31 classes were created by finding classes of notes that were most different in content from the other notes on at least one of three dimensions (specialty, note type, in/outpatient). Specifically, the three dimensions were (a) provider specialty group (e.g., surgery, internal medicine, etc.) (b) inpatient versus outpatient encounters and (c) whether the note type was a patient care or procedure encounter. Only combinations of dimension values with at least 50,000 notes were included to indicate that the class was a commonly occurring class. The selection of these dimensions was somewhat arbitrary but motivated by the desire to represent the full range of clinical care at UCSF (Table 1). The model was run to extract ADEs from all of the 372 notes.

Large Language Model Definition

The large language model was OpenAI version o1, “o1-2024-12-17”. The notes were processed in two passes, with the output of the first pass being used in the second pass. The first pass identifies the ADEs that have a reasonable possibility that the drug caused the event. The second pass adds 16 fields to each ADE such as “serious” and “labeled” that may be useful to downstream users in characterizing the ADE.

Processing Pass 1

The first pass was focused on the task of extracting all ADEs from the note for which there was a “reasonable possibility” that the medication caused the adverse event. The prompt used FDA definitions for an event, for a medication (limited to drugs and biologics only), and the concept of “reasonable possibility”. The prompt was fairly specific on what events to include or exclude, but only rarely gave single examples of such cases. The following event identifiers on Table 2 were requested as output in Pass 1. Evaluating LLM Performance

Reviewer 1 – Evaluating Precision – Whether the LLM ADEs were Valid

Our initial thinking was that the model would be used by drug regulatory authorities to screen for safety signals that had been previously undiscovered. In cases of this use, it was important that the model identified events that were not heavily contaminated with false ADEs that might hurt the downstream statistical analysis attempting to find statistically significant and valid signals. For this use, it was not too important how many ADEs the model missed. Therefore, we designed our evaluation to measure precision and not recall. This reduced evaluation was also much less expensive because we did not need to use human experts to create a gold standard dataset. In this design, we had an expert, reviewer #1, go through each LLM generated ADE and determine whether the generated event was valid (true positive) or invalid (false positive). Evaluating Both Recall and Precision with a Gold Standard Set of Notes

After the LLM had been run on the 372 clinical notes and the precision had been calculated, we reconsidered the importance of estimating the recall of the model. For clinical trials, it is important that all ADEs be reported, and there would be a high cost to missing a real ADE in the notes. Our approach to evaluating recall and precision is summarized in Fig. 3.

Revised evaluation of recall and precision. ADE adverse drug event, FN false negative, FP false positive, LLM large language model, TN true negative, TP true positive

To measure recall, we needed a gold standard dataset of true positives and true negatives. Reviewing 372 clinical notes was too labor intensive for our project, so we created a subset of 100 notes for the gold standard. The 100 notes were selected from (a) all of the notes where the model had found at least 1 ADE (58 notes), and (b) a random sample of the notes where the model had found zero ADEs (42 of 314 notes).

Reviewer 2 – Annotating Gold Standard ADEs from 100 Notes

The 100 notes were then annotated by a human expert, Reviewer 2, who had not seen the results of the LLM model, but who shares a common definition of an “ADE” as provided in the LLM model prompt. Reviewer 2 read through each of 100 clinical notes to annotate each of the true ADEs. These annotations gave us the ability to define a preliminary truth table consisting of:

True positives (TP2) – ADEs generated by the LLM and annotated by Reviewer 2.

True positives (TP2) – ADEs generated by the LLM and annotated by Reviewer 2.

False positives (FP2) – ADEs generated by the LLM but not annotated by Reviewer 2.

False positives (FP2) – ADEs generated by the LLM but not annotated by Reviewer 2.

False negatives (FN2) – ADEs not generated by the LLM but found by Reviewer 2.

False negatives (FN2) – ADEs not generated by the LLM but found by Reviewer 2.

True negatives (TN2) – For each note with no ADEs found by the LLM or by the Reviewer 2, we arbitrarily counted one true negative ADE.

True negatives (TN2) – For each note with no ADEs found by the LLM or by the Reviewer 2, we arbitrarily counted one true negative ADE.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to determine whether a state-of-the-art LLM could extract ADEs from full-length clinical notes with enough precision to be useful in downstream statistical analysis to generate drug safety signals of value to regulators and drug manufacturers.

Our results suggest that a newer reasoning model (OpenAI o1) can precisely detect (94.2%) most (84.1%) of a gold standard set of ADEs in clinical notes. In 372 diverse, randomly selected clinical notes, our system identified 191 ADEs of which 180 were judged to be correct; the 11 errors were mostly due to the LLM inappropriately “guessing” the drug or the event or the chronological relationship. To our knowledge, no other system identifies ADEs from clinical notes with such precision.

The FDA’s “Sentinel” project relies on structured data (diagnosis codes, medication orders, problem lists), which often excludes key details like signs, symptoms, and the physician’s rationale for altering therapy. Similarly, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) uses Darwin EU to collect and analyze real-world clinical data from 250 million patients and 40 data partners to detect drug safety signals from structured electronic health record data harmonized through the global OMOP-Common Data Model. Our study indicates that with carefully designed prompts and a state-of-the-art LLM, both labeled and unlabeled AEs can be identified in de-identified clinical text. Using the clinical notes as a source of finding adverse events should greatly increase the ability of the regulators and manufacturers to detect more drug safety signals sooner.

We were encouraged to see that clinicians do mention unlabeled ADEs in their notes, even if such events may be rare. This opens the possibility that previously undetected ADEs may be discovered with a system similar to that presented here.

Limitations of this Study

There were several limitations to this study. Although the test cases were drawn from a variety of specialties, the testing was limited to one institution (UCSF), a tertiary care facility. Performance may or may not generalize to other institutions. The study was limited by the de-identification of the source notes. Some drugs and events were replaced with “*****” and this limited the LLM model’s ability to reliably identify some ADEs. The model had two limitations that are well known in current LLMs. First, the model was seen to “hallucinate” by guessing drug names and event phrases that were actually “*****” in the source text. It also hallucinated 8% of MedDRA LLTs that looked reasonable but were actually undefined in the current MedDRA dictionary. Second, the model tended to limit the output to one to two pages, which we could only overcome in Phase 2 by changing the prompt to process all Phase 1 events. This limitation could be a problem in future notes with a very large number of ADEs per note. Another limitation is that OpenAI has announced that they will be retiring both models used in this study, “GPT4.o” and “OpenAI o1”, and this will make replication of this study impossible at some future time. Another limitation is that this prompt is not universal and would need to be adapted to different use cases. For example, if the use case was to find adverse events against a specific substance (cannabis products) in social medial posts, the prompt would need to be changed. Another limitation is that we could not define, in advance, a final “Gold Standard” against which any model can be evaluated. Instead, we found that the model extracted some ADEs that, while not in the gold standard, appeared to be correct to two additional reviewers. We believe this is due to the complexity of the concept of “reasonable possibility of causality”. We suspect future work on this problem may continue to require this post hoc validation of the model results.

Another limitation is that we did not measure the variability of the performance of the LLM between runs with the same data. We have seen variability and we believe there is no way to eliminate it with current generations of LLMs.

Another limitation is that the results of this study depend on a proprietary closed-source system that charges a fee for the use of their model. Shipping patient data to 3rd party vendors creates privacy risks, and the costs may create a barrier to the scaling up of the use of this model in the future. We address this in “Future Research” below.

Future Research

It would be helpful to validate this model against other benchmark clinical notes datasets. A useful validation would be to run the model against the 2018 n2c2, end-to-end, “ADE-Drug” relationship classification, described in “2018 n2c2 shared task on adverse drug events and medication extraction in EHRs ” [7]. Based on published results for this standard, our model, with an F1 of 88.9% on UCSF data, should perform favorably compared to the best model of the challenge, with an F1 of 47.55, but n2c2 had a different gold standard and that performance should be verified.

An additional important direction would be to test open-source LLMs against our dataset. An open-source model could be run locally at a medical institution or regulatory authority site, thereby decreasing risk to patient privacy. It would also permit the institution to control when and whether the LLM versions change over time.

Another useful direction we hope to explore is adding structured data such as diagnoses, medications and lab results to the narrative. This gives the model more precise dates for establishing the chronology of meds versus events.

Conclusion

Regulatory authorities, including the FDA, and pharmaceutical companies have been actively searching for new ways to discover adverse event “signals” from real-world patient experience with licensed drugs. In particular, they have been considering the possibility that AI methods could be used to find these signals in EHRs.

This project demonstrates that this is feasible today with current LLMs. The model can extract ADEs that are at least 94% true adverse drug events, as documented by expert review of the extracted ADEs. Furthermore, the model can accurately determine whether the events are “serious” or “unlabeled”, and whether the events came from the current illness or the past medical history.

With the 94% accuracy of events extracted in this way, regulatory authorities could implement such a system to analyze all clinical notes from the EHRs of partnering medical facilities. The regulators could then extend their existing statistical analyses (e.g., disproportionality analysis) to ADEs extracted from this much larger set of clinical notes data to find signals of uncommon ADEs that may not be found in the voluntary reports or the currently used structured data used in Sentinel (in the USA) or Darwin EU (in the European Union.

Scaling a system such as this, using OpenAI version o1, would require a cost of about $0.35 per ADE extracted. There are other similar LLMs that are open-source and carry no cost per usage. The open-source models have the additional important advantage that they can protect patient privacy by being run locally on computing resources at a regulatory agency or medical center. This will enable scaling such systems to large populations, permitting regulatory agencies to detect more uncommon ADEs and more effectively protect the public from harm from adverse drug events.
