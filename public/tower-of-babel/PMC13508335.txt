Short-term visual acuity responder rates after full-thickness macular hole surgery stratified by hole size: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Luca Timoceanu, Corina Lang, Scott Tschuppert, Josef Guber, Christophe Valmaggia, Andreas Ebneter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo quantify early real-world visual acuity (VA) responder rates at 25–35 days after idiopathic full-thickness macular hole (FTMH) surgery and evaluate the influence of hole size, gas tamponade, and prescribed duration of face-down positioning.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 193 eyes were analyzed after pars plana vitrectomy with internal limiting membrane peeling and SF6 20% or C3F8 12% gas tamponade. Routine Snellen (decimal) best-corrected VA was extracted from the medical record and converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR). Primary outcomes were mean VA change from the last preoperative assessment to the postoperative visit at 25–35 days and rates of two and three lines VA improvement (≥ 0.2 and ≥ 0.3 logMAR, respectively). Secondary outcomes were baseline-adjusted postoperative VA and anatomical closure.ResultsOverall, 66.8% of eyes improved by ≥ 0.2 logMAR and 52.3% by ≥ 0.3 logMAR. Mean VA improved from 0.774 ± 0.409 to 0.455 ± 0.346 logMAR (mean VA change − 0.318 logMAR; p < 0.001). VA change and responder rates did not differ significantly across International Vitreomacular Traction Study size groups (< 250 μm, 250–400 μm, > 400 μm), and no statistically significant association with gas choice or prescribed face-down positioning duration was detected within the limitations of this retrospective cohort. Baseline-adjusted postoperative VA differed by size, with large holes having worse early postoperative VA than small and medium holes, independent of prescribed face-down positioning duration or vitreomacular traction status. Anatomical closure differed by International Vitreomacular Traction Study size group (small 98.9%, medium 96.7%, large 78.0%; p < 0.001).ConclusionsIn routine care, FTMH surgery results in clinically meaningful early VA improvement at 25–35 days, with approximately two-thirds of eyes gaining ≥ 0.2 logMAR and half gaining ≥ 0.3 logMAR. Although VA improvement was similar across size groups, large holes had lower closure rates and worse early postoperative BCVA. No statistically significant association between gas choice, prescribed face-down positioning duration, and the extent of VA improvement was detected in this retrospective cohort.Keywords: Full-thickness macular hole, Peeling, Visual acuity gain, Vitrectomy, Vitreomacular surgery, Vitreoretinal interface disorder

Introduction

A full-thickness macular hole (FTMH) is a foveal defect that spans from the internal limiting membrane (ILM) to the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) [1]. This condition was first described by Knapp [2] and later staged by Gass [3]. Contemporary optical coherence tomography (OCT)-based definitions and classification according to hole diameter were standardized in 2013 by the International Vitreomacular Traction Study (IVTS) group [1].

A recurring methodological issue is reliance on the final best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) as the main outcome. Final BCVA is strongly influenced by the preoperative visual acuity (VA), macular hole size, and symptom duration [4, 5]. Complementary endpoints include VA gain and categorical VA improvement thresholds (proportion of eyes gaining ≥ 0.2 or ≥ 0.3 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR)). However, these measures are often used inconsistently across IVTS categories and macular hole sizes, which limits comparability and may under-represent functional gains in large holes [6]. A recent cohort has shown that also in large FTMH (≥ 650 μm), many eyes achieve ≥ 0.2 logMAR of improvement even when the final BCVA remains suboptimal [7]. Without responder analyses and continuous change metrics, these benefits can easily be missed. Many studies still focus on the final BCVA, which does not necessarily translate to the true functional gains in groups with worse baseline vision [4, 7, 8].

With this in mind, our retrospective study was conducted with a focus on change-based, patient-relevant outcomes while remaining anchored in the OCT anatomy [1, 9, 10]. In a single-center cohort of idiopathic FTMH treated with pars plana vitrectomy (PPV), we primarily investigated visual improvement using both VA change and categorical VA improvement analyses [11, 12]. We defined VA change as postoperative minus preoperative logMAR, such that negative values indicated improvement, aligning with the categorical VA improvement thresholds reported in a large clinical cohort [11]. Our central clinical question was whether the magnitude of improvement differed significantly by IVTS size, even if the absolute postoperative BCVA remained low, especially in larger defects [4, 11]. Accordingly, we compared VA change across small, medium, and large holes and reported baseline-adjusted early postoperative BCVA to contextualize change-score findings [4].

The secondary objectives were aligned with anatomical mechanisms. Firstly, we compared primary closure across the IVTS size strata, recognizing size and chronicity as key predictors of anatomical success [4, 11, 13]. Second, we explored the associations between vitreous status [1, 4], tamponade selection [12, 14], and prescribed face-down positioning (FDP) duration [15, 16] with both functional and anatomic endpoints. Finally, we summarized the safety signals relevant to perioperative counseling and the decision-making processes.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study at the Cantonal Hospital of St. Gallen, including eyes treated for FTMH between January 2019 and December 2024. Potential cases were identified using a structured query of electronic medical records. All included patients had provided the hospital-wide general consent for the use of collected health-related data in future retrospective research. Because this retrospective study used routinely collected data from patients with documented general consent, the need for additional study-specific informed consent was waived by the local ethics committee and IRB pursuant to Art. 34 of the federal Human Research Act (HRA). Medical records of patients without documented general consent were not assessed. This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethics committee (Ethikkommission Ostschweiz, St. Gallen, Switzerland; Ref. 2025–02161).

Participants and eligibility

A total of 216 patients with documented general consent were screened, and 186 patients (193 eyes) met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analyses. A total of 30 eyes were excluded because they met predefined clinical exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years and a diagnosis of idiopathic FTMH. Specific exclusion criteria were a history of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment, high myopia (axial length > 26.5 mm or spherical equivalent ≥ − 6.00 D), history of ocular trauma, or prior PPV. Analyses were performed at the eye level. If both eyes met the inclusion criteria, they were independently included in the study. Symptom duration was defined as the patient-reported interval between first visual acuity symptoms and the time of FTMH diagnosis, as documented in the first available clinical note or referral letter. This information was retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records. Standardized symptom questioning was not used.

Surgical procedures

All eyes underwent 23-gauge PPV under general anesthesia using a non-contact wide-angle viewing system (Oculus BIOM® or Zeiss RESIGHT®). A 23-gauge platform (Bausch & Lomb Stellaris) was the routine vitreoretinal surgical approach used at our institution during the study period. Core vitrectomy was followed by the induction of posterior vitreous detachment when absent. The ILM was peeled following staining with dual dye containing DDG and Trypan Blue, typically over a 2–3 disc-diameter area around the fovea. In selected cases, an inverted ILM flap technique was used at the discretion of the operating surgeon. After fluid-air exchange, gas tamponade with 20% sulfur hexafluoride (SF6) or 12% perfluoropropane (C3F8) was used. SF6 was the default tamponade in routine practice, whereas C3F8 was used selectively in challenging cases at the discretion of the surgeon. No standardized gas-selection algorithm based on predefined macular hole characteristics was available. Peripheral breaks or lattice degeneration were treated with endolaser or cryopexy as required. The prescribed duration of FDP was not standardized and varied according to individual surgeon judgment rather than a fixed protocol based on hole size or tamponade agent. Posturing recommendations ranged from 0 to 8 days and were recorded in discrete categories (0, 3, 5, 6, 7, and 8 days). Actual adherence was not systematically assessed or objectively monitored. Combined phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation was performed in all phakic eyes.

Imaging

All eyes were imaged using spectral-domain OCT (Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) on a HEYEX 2 platform (version 2.5.4). The standardized acquisition protocol used 6–24 radial B-scans centered on the fovea. High-density macular cubes were included when additional information was required. Image quality was screened for signal strength (adequate signal by grader judgment), foveal centering, and absence of major artifacts. Scans that failed the quality criteria were excluded from the quantitative analyses. FTMH was defined according to the IVTS criteria, with holes stratified by the minimum linear diameter (MLD) as small (< 250 μm), medium (250–400 μm), or large (> 400 μm) [1]. Two independent graders measured the MLD and base diameter using HEYEX calipers (MLD: narrowest full-thickness gap; basal diameter: at the RPE level). No predefined numeric threshold for acceptable inter-grader variability was used. Measurements were performed independently, and discrepancies in IVTS size categorizations, were resolved by joint consensus review.

Visual acuity assessment

Monocular distance VA was retrieved from the electronic medical record for both the final preoperative and the postoperative assessment. VA was routinely assessed by the physician using a Snellen (decimal) distance chart under standardized examination-room conditions. When necessary, pinhole testing and/or manifest refraction were conducted to determine the best-corrected distance VA. VA was recorded in Snellen decimal and converted to logMAR for all analyses using a standard conversion [17]. In this manuscript, we consistently use the term BCVA to denote the best-recorded corrected VA obtained in routine clinical care.

Outcomes

The principal anatomical outcome was primary closure on OCT one month (25–35 days) postoperatively. Primary closure was defined as the restoration of the foveal contour with complete approximation of the neurosensory retina. The primary functional outcome was the VA change recorded at the same visit. Secondary outcomes included closure after reoperation and postoperative adverse events, including intraocular pressure elevation, macular edema, and retinal detachment. Adverse events were identified retrospectively from the electronic medical record at routine postoperative visits. Intraocular pressure elevation was defined as an intraocular pressure above 21 mmHg. The safety assessment was available for all eyes at the 25–35-day postoperative visit. If further visits were documented, then also from later routine follow-up. OCT-based adverse findings beyond failure of anatomical closure, such as ischemic macular changes or outer retinal disruption, were not systematically assessed.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using Jamovi (version 2.6; modules: jmv, GAMLj, and Rj). Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range] (Shapiro–Wilk test). Categorical variables were reported as n/N (%) with Wilson’s 95% confidence interval (CI). Group comparisons were performed using ANOVA (or Kruskal–Wallis for non-normal data) and χ² (or Fisher’s exact test when sparse) as appropriate, with Holm adjustment for multiple pairwise tests. For functional outcomes, we compared the VA change across IVTS sizes and modeled the baseline-adjusted early postoperative BCVA using ANCOVA, with baseline BCVA as a covariate. Primary anatomical closure was assessed using multivariable logistic regression. The dependent variable was primary closure on OCT at 25–35 days after surgery, coded as closed versus primary failure. Covariates were selected based on presumed clinical relevance and included IVTS size group, vitreomacular traction status, gas tamponade, combined phacoemulsification, intraoperative endolaser, intraoperative cryotherapy, and prescribed duration of postoperative FDP. The IVTS size group was entered as a categorical variable with large holes as the reference group. Gas tamponade was coded as SF6 versus C3F8, with C3F8 as the reference. Vitreomacular traction, phacoemulsification, endolaser, and cryotherapy were entered as binary variables. Prescribed FDP duration was entered as a continuous variable in days. Given the low number of primary failures, Firth-penalized logistic regression was performed as a sensitivity analysis in the evaluation of re-do surgeries. All hypothesis tests were two-sided (α = 0.05).

Results

Clinical and demographic data

Of the 223 eyes assessed for eligibility, 193 eyes from 186 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the study sample. Of these, 92 eyes had a small FTMH, 60 eyes had a medium FTMH, and 41 eyes had a large FTMH. The patient demographics and history are summarized in Table 1. The size distribution was typical of routine practice (small 92/193, 47.7%; medium 60/193, 31.1%; large 41/193, 21.2%). The C3F8 group (n = 11) represented harder to treat eyes with worse preoperative BCVA (median 1.3 vs. 0.699 logMAR; Mann–Whitney U = 444.5, p = 0.002) and larger holes (MLD 540 vs. 248 μm; U = 371.0, p < 0.001; basal diameter 1028 vs. 662 μm; U = 368.5, p < 0.001), longer reported symptom duration at diagnosis (120 vs. 60 days; C3F8 n = 6, SF6 n = 84; U = 92.5, p = 0.010), and older age (75.3 vs. 69.5 years; U = 578.5, p = 0.019). The IVTS size group distribution also differed (χ²(2, N = 193) = 13.6, p = 0.001), with 63.6% large holes in the C3F8 group vs. 18.7% in the SF6 group.

Anatomical outcomes

Among the 193 eyes included, 182 received SF6 and 11 received C3F8 as the primary tamponade. Primary closure was higher in the SF6 (95.1%) treated eyes than in C3F8 treated ones (72.7%). In the multivariable closure model, no statistically significant association was detected between gas tamponade and primary closure (SF6 vs. C3F8; ref=C3F8; OR = 2.62, 95% CI 0.41–16.80; p = 0.309). Of note, the study was not powered to detect such associations. An inverted ILM flap technique was used in 17 eyes at the discretion of the operating surgeon, including 12 large and 5 medium macular holes. Primary closure was achieved in 15/17 eyes (88.2%) treated with this technique.

Anatomical closure rates differed by IVTS size (Pearson χ², χ²(2, N = 193) = 22.40, p < 0.001) (large 78.0%, medium 96.7%, small 98.9%) (Fig. 1). Holm-adjusted z-tests for the two proportions showed that small > large (p < 0.001) and medium > large (p = 0.006), with no difference between small and medium (p = 0.330). Binomial logistic regression confirmed size as a significant predictor: medium vs. large (OR = 10.35, 95% CI 1.79–60.04, p = 0.009), and small vs. large (OR = 38.54, 95% CI 3.91–379.45, p = 0.002). Univariate (crude) logistic regression showed medium vs. large OR = 8.16 (95% CI 1.66–40.07, p = 0.010) and small vs. large OR = 25.59 (95% CI 3.12–210.03, p = 0.003). Vitreomacular traction (VMT) (no vs. yes; ref = yes) showed a non-significant trend (OR = 3.70, 95% CI 0.80–17.18, p = 0.094). Adjusted ORs for phacoemulsification (OR = 1.84, 95% CI 0.42–7.98, p = 0.417), endolaser (OR = 3.02, 95% CI 0.53–17.09, p = 0.212), cryotherapy (OR = 1.41, 95% CI 0.25–7.93, p = 0.700), and prescribed postoperative FDP (per day, OR = 1.10, 95% CI 0.82–1.46, p = 0.530) were also non-significant (Table 2). The estimates were directionally consistent in the Firth logistic sensitivity analysis (Table 3). The logistic model showed good discrimination (AUROC = 0.921; additional calibration metrics are shown in Fig. 2 with 95% binomial CIs).

Eyes with persistent macular holes had a significantly larger basal diameter (mean 1049 ± 297 μm) than eyes with closure (mean 662 ± 304 μm) (Welch’s t(12.6) = − 4.3, mean difference = 387 μm (95% CI 195–579), p < 0.001). The basal diameter was positively correlated with postoperative VA (Spearman’s rank correlation, ρ = 0.447, p < 0.001), indicating worse outcomes with larger holes.

Of the 12 primary failures, 8 underwent reoperation after a median of 23.5 days from the first operation (IQR 16.5–34.5). Revision surgery included a free ILM flap technique in all reoperated eyes. SF6 was used in 6 eyes and C3F8 in 2, respectively. No platelet-rich plasma, autologous tissue grafts, or other adjunctive material was used. Closure was achieved in 5/8 eyes (62.5%). Persistent failure after reoperation was associated with a longer reported symptom duration (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.016). Within this small reoperation subgroup, larger MLD and basal diameter showed directionally higher values in eyes with persistent failure after revision surgery, but these exploratory comparisons did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.100 and p = 0.098, respectively). This finding should be interpreted cautiously given that only 8 eyes underwent reoperation. No significant associations were found between reoperation outcomes and macular hole stage, gas choice, presence of vitreomacular traction, or combined phacoemulsification (Fisher’s exact test, p ≥ 0.464). BCVA after reoperation did not significantly improve compared to the situation after the first surgery (paired t-test, t(7) = 0.948, p = 0.375; Shapiro–Wilk test for normality: p = 0.739).

Functional outcomes

The mean preoperative BCVA was 0.774 ± 0.409 logMAR, which improved to 0.455 ± 0.346 logMAR. The mean VA change was − 0.318 logMAR (95% CI -0.372 to -0.264; paired t-test t(192) = 11.50, p < 0.001), corresponding to approximately 3.2 Snellen lines. Categorical VA improvement analyses showed that 129/193 eyes (66.8%, 95% CI 59.7–73.4) achieved an improvement of ≥ 2 Snellen lines, with similar rates across IVTS sizes: 68.5% (63/92) for small holes, 68.3% (41/60) for medium holes, and 61.0% (25/41) for large holes (Pearson χ² test, χ²(2) = 0.81, p = 0.668). For the more stringent threshold of ≥ 3 lines, 101/193 eyes (52.3%, 95% CI 45.0–59.6) met this target, again without a significant size gradient: 45.7% (42/92) in small holes, 58.3% (35/60) in medium holes, and 58.5% (24/41) in large holes (Pearson χ² test, χ²(2) = 3.14, p = 0.208) (Table 4; Fig. 3).

Clinically meaningful early VA worsening, defined as a loss of ≥ 0.2 logMAR at 25–35 days, occurred in 12/193 eyes (6.2%). This was observed in 5/92 small holes (5.4%), 3/60 medium holes (5.0%), and 4/41 large holes (9.8%). Because of the small number of events, no formal multivariable risk-factor model for VA worsening was performed.

After adjusting for baseline BCVA, postoperative BCVA differed according to IVTS size (ANCOVA: F(2,189) = 5.28, p = 0.006, partial η²=0.053). Baseline BCVA was also associated with postoperative BCVA (F(1,189) = 29.50, p < 0.001). The homogeneity of slopes assumption was met (interaction term IVTS size×baseline BCVA, p = 0.615). The EMMeans at the mean baseline BCVA values were as follows: small, 0.402 logMAR (SE 0.034); medium, 0.425 logMAR (SE 0.038) and large, 0.584 logMAR (SE 0.057) (Fig. 4). Holm-adjusted pairwise comparisons of EMMeans showed that the large group had worse BCVA than the small (difference 0.182 logMAR, p = 0.020) and medium (0.159, p = 0.043) groups. The difference between the small and medium group was not significant (p = 0.655). Because Levene’s test indicated heteroscedasticity (p < 0.001), we performed a Welch ANOVA on baseline-adjusted residuals. The omnibus test was non-significant (Welch F(2,87.1) = 2.05, p = 0.135), although the direction of differences was the same (large vs. small 0.150 logMAR, p = 0.118; large vs. medium 0.143, p = 0.159). Overall, after accounting for baseline BCVA, large holes tended to have poorer early postoperative BCVA than small/medium holes by approximately 0.16–0.18 logMAR (approximately 1.6–1.8 Snellen lines).

In contrast, VA change did not differ among the IVTS size groups (Kruskal–Wallis test, H(2) = 4.23, p = 0.121, ε²=0.022) (Fig. 5), and reported symptom duration at diagnosis in days was not significantly correlated with VA change (Spearman’s rank correlation, ρ=−0.025, p = 0.812). No statistically significant association was detected between prescribed postoperative FDP duration and VA change (Kruskal–Wallis test, H(5) = 6.96, p = 0.224, ε²=0.036).

After adjusting for baseline BCVA, no statistically significant difference in postoperative BCVA was detected between gas groups (ANCOVA, F(1,190) = 1.02, p = 0.313). EMMeans were SF6 0.450 logMAR (95% CI 0.406–0.494) and C3F8 0.547 logMAR (95% CI 0.364–0.731) (Δ = 0.097 logMAR). Levene’s test indicated heteroscedasticity (p = 0.007); a Welch t-test on baseline-adjusted residuals likewise detected no statistically significant difference (t(10.5) = 0.662, mean difference = 0.093 logMAR, 95% CI − 0.217 to 0.402, p = 0.522). No statistically significant difference in VA change was detected between gas groups (Kruskal–Wallis H(1) = 0.039, p = 0.843).

In an additional sensitivity analysis, combined phacoemulsification was not associated with early postoperative BCVA after adjustment for preoperative BCVA, IVTS size group, age, and the interaction between phacoemulsification and IVTS size group (ANCOVA: F(1,185) = 0.078, p = 0.780). There was also no evidence that the association between phacoemulsification and postoperative BCVA differed by IVTS size group (interaction: F(2,185) = 0.412, p = 0.663).

Eyes with primary closure showed greater VA improvement than those with primary failure (Mann–Whitney U = 480, p = 0.001; Hodges–Lehmann median difference = 0.398 logMAR, 95% CI 0.204–0.544). As a parametric sensitivity check, the mean difference was 0.299 logMAR (95% CI 0.078–0.521; Student’s t(191) = 2.67, p = 0.008). During routine postoperative follow-up, the rates of postoperative intraocular pressure elevation (SF6, 7.1%; C3F8, 9.1%) and macular edema (SF6, 7.7%; C3F8, 0%) did not differ significantly between the groups (Fisher’s exact test, all p > 0.300). One case of postoperative retinal detachment was observed.

Discussion

In this single-center cohort study of 193 eyes, surgery for idiopathic FTMH resulted in clinically meaningful improvement in vision. The mean BCVA improved from 0.774 ± 0.409 to 0.455 ± 0.346 logMAR, which equates to approximately three Snellen lines and is consistent with large UK series reporting substantial postoperative gains after contemporary vitrectomy with ILM peeling [4, 18]. Although smaller holes finished with better absolute VA, the amount of VA change did not differ significantly across the IVTS size groups, consistent with previous reports [6, 19]. The distribution of hole sizes in our cohort mirrored routine practice, with approximately half of the cases being small holes, one-third being medium, and one fifth accounting for large holes, supporting the generalizability of these findings to the affected population [18].

Categorical VA improvement analyses help translate these averages into individual patient expectations. Overall, 66.8% gained at least two lines of vision (≥ 0.2 logMAR), and 52.3% gained at least three lines (≥ 0.3 logMAR). These proportions were comparable across the IVTS size groups, indicating that clinically meaningful improvement is common even when the holes are larger, provided closure is achieved. This pattern aligns with multicenter and longitudinal cohorts (including a series of very large holes) showing substantial line gains and continued improvement for 1–3 years [7, 20–22]. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as describing early postoperative recovery rather than final visual outcome, because VA may continue to improve beyond the first postoperative month. The similar VA changes across IVTS groups should be interpreted in the context of baseline VA differences. Large holes started with worse VA and therefore had greater potential for line gain, even though their closure rate and absolute postoperative BCVA were less favorable. Thus, similar change scores do not imply equivalent prognosis. They rather indicate that patients with large holes may still experience meaningful early improvement despite worse early postoperative BCVA.

In contrast, the closure probability varied according to the IVTS size group. We observed that closure was higher in the small and medium holes than in the large holes, with no difference between the small and medium holes. This finding is consistent with large prospective cohorts and meta-regression demonstrating decreased closure as the diameter increases, as well as reviews emphasizing tailored strategies for large (≥ 400–500 μm) or chronic holes [4, 23–25]. In practice, air or SF6 can achieve high closure rates in small to medium holes and selected larger holes [26, 27], whereas flap-based techniques may improve anatomical closure in larger holes compared with conventional peeling [28]. In our cohort, the inverted ILM flap technique was used selectively at the discretion of the operating surgeon in 17 eyes, of which 12 were large and 5 were medium holes, with primary closure achieved in 15 cases. Because flap use was not standardized according to a prespecified size threshold, the lower closure rate observed in the large-hole subgroup should be interpreted in the context of a real-world surgical strategy rather than as the outcome of conventional peeling alone.

After adjusting for baseline BCVA, the early postoperative BCVA still differed by IVTS size: eyes with large holes finished about 1.6–1.8 lines worse than small or medium holes, while small and medium holes had similar outcomes. A heteroscedasticity-robust sensitivity check showed the same directional pattern, although with wider uncertainty. Together, these findings mirror large registry data showing that hole size independently predicts both anatomic success and postoperative acuity [4, 6, 18]. Larger holes close less often, and even when they close, their postoperative acuity levels tend to remain lower than those of smaller holes.

Combined procedures with phacoemulsification may have influenced early postoperative VA outcomes, because VA improvement after phacovitrectomy may reflect the combined effects of macular hole repair and cataract removal rather than macular hole repair alone [12, 29]. In our cohort, all phakic eyes underwent combined phacoemulsification, reflecting routine surgical practice at our institution. In a sensitivity analysis, no statistically significant association between combined phacoemulsification and early postoperative BCVA was detected after adjustment for preoperative BCVA, IVTS size group, age, and the interaction between phacoemulsification and IVTS size group. Nevertheless, cataract severity was not systematically graded, and the relative contribution of lens removal versus macular hole repair to early VA improvement cannot be fully separated in this retrospective dataset. Therefore, the functional outcomes should be interpreted as real-world early postoperative results after the overall surgical strategy rather than as the isolated effect of macular hole repair.

Multivariable modeling underscored the central role of the anatomical size and vitreomacular interface. Compared with large holes, medium and small holes had substantially higher odds of primary closure, which is consistent with prior reports [4]. Eyes with VMT showed a non-significant trend toward lower closure when adhesion was present, aligning with the direction expected from the literature, although it did not reach significance in this cohort [4]. In contrast, no statistically significant association was detected between gas type, combined phacoemulsification, intraoperative endolaser or cryotherapy, prescribed duration of postoperative FDP, and closure in this retrospective cohort. However, because FDP adherence was not systematically assessed and recommendations were not standardized across surgeons, these findings should be interpreted as associations with the prescribed duration rather than with actual postoperative positioning behavior. Similarly, because gas selection was non-randomized and C3F8 was used selectively in more complex cases, the tamponade findings should be interpreted cautiously and not be considered as evidence for equivalence between gas tamponades. However, the findings are in accordance with mechanistic and clinical data: vitreomacular traction is a negative anatomic factor, and ILM peeling relieves tangential traction and promotes closure [30, 31]. In contemporary practice with thorough ILM peeling, the role of gas choice is probably secondary [32–34]. The lower closure rate in C3F8-treated eyes in our cohort most likely reflects a selection bias, because C3F8 was preferred for more complex cases, while SF6 was the choice of default.

All procedures in the present cohort were performed using 23-gauge PPV, reflecting routine practice at our institution during the study period. Although 25-gauge systems are widely used in contemporary macular surgery and may reduce wound-related tissue trauma and facilitate early postoperative recovery because of the smaller sclerotomy size, comparative evidence has not shown superiority over 23-gauge surgery in terms of macular hole closure or visual outcomes. In a macular-hole-specific comparative series, 20-, 23-, and 25-gauge PPV achieved similar closure rates and visual outcomes [35], and broader comparative data have not demonstrated a significant difference in early postoperative complication rates between 23- and 25-gauge vitrectomy [36].

Primary failure was uncommon in our cohort (12/193, 6.2%). Among eyes that underwent a reoperation, approximately two-thirds achieved secondary closure, consistent with large series and systematic reviews reporting approximately 70–80% anatomic success after revision for persistent idiopathic FTMH [37–39]. The preferred choice for redo surgery in our cohort was a free ILM flap, while no platelet-rich plasma, autologous tissue grafts, or other adjunctive material were used. Functional gains after reoperation are typically smaller than those after primary repair, and high final BCVA is uncommon, likely reflecting outer retinal damage accrued by chronicity [37, 39]. In our cohort, BCVA after reoperation did not significantly improve compared to BCVA after the primary surgery, but this analysis was limited by the very small number of reoperated eyes. Prior evidence has shown that longer symptom duration is associated with lower closure probability and worse visual outcomes, underscoring the importance of timely diagnosis and repair [13]. In our cohort, longer reported symptom duration at diagnosis was associated with persistent failure after reoperation, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of reoperated eyes and the retrospective, patient-reported nature of this variable.

Safety signals were also favorable. The rates of postoperative intraocular pressure elevation and macular edema were low and did not differ significantly between the gases, although the statistical validity was limited by the small C3F8 subgroup and low event counts. We observed one case of retinal detachment after macular hole surgery.

Limitations

This study was limited by its retrospective design, which implies selection bias and the potential for unaccounted confounding factors. Because eligibility assessment was restricted to patients with documented hospital-wide general consent, patients who had declined consent or for whom consent was not recorded were not screened, and the cohort may therefore not represent a fully consecutive series. Symptom duration was based on retrospective patient-reported history documented during routine care rather than standardized questioning and may therefore be affected by recall bias and variability in documentation.

Surgical management was not fully standardized. Tamponade choice was non-randomized and based on surgeon discretion, with SF6 as the default gas and C3F8 used selectively. Comparisons between tamponades are therefore prone to selection bias and should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence between gas tamponading agents. The small sample size of the C3F8 subgroup also precluded meaningful subgroup analysis. FDP was recorded as the prescribed duration rather than verified adherence and recommendations were not standardized. Therefore, the FDP findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations with prescribed posturing rather than with actual postoperative positioning behavior. Actual compliance was not systematically assessed. The use of inverted ILM flaps was left to surgeon discretion and reflects real-world settings. Because all procedures were performed using 23-gauge PPV, we could not evaluate whether gauge size influenced early recovery or complication rates. Importantly, sclerotomies were routinely sutured with a single interrupted 8 − 0 vicryl suture in all cases. Combined phacoemulsification was performed in all phakic eyes, and cataract severity was not systematically graded. Therefore, confounding of VA data by lens status cannot be excluded and cataract removal-related visual improvement is possible, although baseline lens status was similar across groups.

The outcome assessment was based on routine clinical data. VA was measured using Snellen decimal charts and represented the best-recorded corrected VA rather than standardized ETDRS refraction-based BCVA. Nevertheless, the study data reflect routine clinical practice. The primary postoperative assessment was limited to 25–35 days, and standardized longer-term VA data were not available. Our findings therefore describe early postoperative recovery rather than final long-term visual outcomes. Residual intraocular gas, particularly after C3F8 tamponade, may still have been present and could have influenced early VA measurements.

Although adverse events were reviewed at all available routine postoperative visits, follow-up beyond the 25–35-day visit was not standardized, and the reported safety findings may underestimate delayed complications. OCT-based postoperative adverse findings beyond anatomical closure were not systematically assessed. Overall, adverse event rates were low, the reoperation cohort was small, and multiple comparisons were performed with statistical adjustments where appropriate. Nevertheless, the possibility of type I errors remains.

Conclusion

Framing early outcomes around VA change, supported by categorical VA improvement, provides patients with tangible short-term expectations. Although early postoperative BCVA remained lower in larger holes, VA changes were similar between IVTS groups, indicating that patients with larger macular holes may still experience meaningful early improvement. No statistically significant association between prescribed posturing duration, VA change, or primary closure was detected in this retrospective cohort. However, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence for equivalence between tamponade agents or postoperative positioning strategies given the inherent limitations in the study design. Finally, our data seems to confirm that chronicity compromises anatomical and visual outcomes, underscoring the recommendation for timely repair.
