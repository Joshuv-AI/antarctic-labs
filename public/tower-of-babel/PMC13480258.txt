Prognostic impact of invasive features in completely resected NSCLC stratified by tumor diameter
Authors: Naoki Furukawa, Takashi Sakai, Yujiro Bunno, Marino Yamamoto, Ryohei Miyazaki, Hironobu Okada, Masaya Tamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn node-negative non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), only Pleural invasion (PI) is reflected in the current TNM staging, whereas the prognostic roles of Lymphatic invasion (LI) and Vascular invasion (VI) remain poorly defined. Moreover, the variation in their prognostic impact across tumor size strata is not well understood. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic impact of PI, LI, and VI on recurrence-free survival (RFS) in node-negative NSCLC, stratified by tumor size.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 692 patients with completely resected, node-negative NSCLC. Cases of adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) and minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA) were excluded from the analysis. Patients were categorized into three cohorts: Cohort1 (size: ≦ 2.0 cm), Cohort2 (> 2.0 cm ≦ 3.0 cm), and Cohort3 (> 3.0 cm ≦ 5.0 cm). Multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed to identify independent prognostic factors .ResultsIn Cohort 1, PI (Hazard Ratio [HR]: 2.87, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 1.21–6.78, P = 0.02) was independent prognostic factors for worse RFS. In Cohort 2, only LI was significantly associated with recurrence (HR:3.62, 95% CI: 1.31–5.62, P = 0.01). In Cohort 3, both PI (HR:2.67, 95% CI: 1.24–5.79, P = 0.01) and LI (HR: 6.27, 95% CI: 2.79–14.1, P < 0.001) were independent predictors of poor prognosis, with LI demonstrating a more substantial impact than PI. VI showed limited prognostic value in larger tumors.ConclusionThe prognostic relevance of PI, LI, and VI in NSCLC varies by tumor size. PI plays a critical role in small tumors, while LI becomes increasingly influential with larger tumors. These findings suggest the need for size-specific pathological risk assessment to guide postoperative management and consideration of adjuvant therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-04406-0.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, including in Japan. Among these cases, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% [1]. Recent advances in imaging technologies have enabled earlier detection of lung cancer, leading to an increasing number of surgical resections being performed for early-stage disease.

However, even in patients with early-stage lung cancer who have undergone complete resection, postoperative recurrence can still occur. In fact, it has been reported that up to 10% of patients with pathologic stage IA disease experience recurrence and die following surgery [2].

In NSCLC, pleural invasion (PI) has been identified as an independent poor prognostic factor [3]. Similarly, Vascular invasion (VI) and Lymphatic invasion (LI), like PI, are also considered adverse prognostic indicators in NSCLC [2]. However, the prognostic significance of these invasive features with respect to tumor size remains inadequately elucidated.

In this study, we retrospectively evaluated the impact of PI, VI, and LI on recurrence-free survival (RFS) in node-negative NSCLC. Patients were stratified into three groups based on tumor size: ≦ 2.0 cm, > 2.0 cm ≦ 3.0 cm, and > 3.0 cm ≦ 5.0 cm.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed as a retrospective cohort study. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Ethics Committee of Kochi Medical School and the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of this single-center study.

Histopathologic evaluation

All specimens were fixed in 10% buffered formalin and sectioned for microscopic examination. Pathological assessment was based on an average of three to five representative tumor sections, including the largest cross-section focusing on the maximal tumor diameter. Sections were routinely stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E).

The presence of LI and VI was determined using D2-40 and Elastica-van-Gieson (EvG) staining, respectively. For the operational definitions of lymphovascular invasion, LI was identified by the presence of clear-cut tumor cell clusters within a D2-40-positive endothelial-lined channel, distinguishing it from artificial stromal retraction. Similarly, VI was determined by identifying conspicuous clusters of intravascular cancer cells surrounded by an elastic layer, confirmed by EvG staining to visualize the internal or external elastic lamina. Even in the absence of distinct endothelial cells, a vessel was considered positive for if an elastic fiber envelope was clearly identified around the tumor emboli.

PI was defined as positive if the tumor had invaded beyond the elastic layer into the visceral pleural surface or neighboring organs. EvG stain was utilized to visualize elastic fibers and facilitate the evaluation of PI.

Each sample was independently examined microscopically by two pathologists. Discordant cases were re-evaluated and adjudicated by an expert pulmonary pathologist to reach a consensus. Pathologic staging was performed according to the TNM 8th edition (International Union for Cancer Control [TNM-UICC8]), and histological classification was based on the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of lung tumors.

Patients

The patient selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Between January 2012 and December 2022, a total of 923 patients with NSCLC underwent complete surgical resection at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Kochi Medical School. Complete surgical resection was defined as R0 resection, requiring both macroscopically and microscopically tumor-free margins at the bronchial, vascular, and soft tissue resection lines. Patients were excluded if they had undergone non-radical resection, had pathological N1–N3 disease, tumor diameter > 5.0 cm, received neoadjuvant treatment, histologically classified as adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) or minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), or had incomplete clinicopathological data. After applying these criteria, 692 patients were included in the final analysis. To evaluate the prognostic impact across different clinical stages, these patients were stratified into three cohorts based on the maximum whole tumor diameter. Cohort 1 included patients with tumors ≦ 2.0 cm, following the definition of small-sized peripheral lung cancer used in the JCOG0802/WJOG4607L trial [4]. Cohort 2 included tumors > 2.0 cm ≦ 3.0 cm, and Cohort 3 included tumors > 3.0 cm ≦ 5.0 cm. The 3.0 cm and 5.0 cm cutoffs were selected to align with the T-factor boundaries (T1 vs. T2 and T2 vs. T3, respectively) in the TNM 8th edition (International Union for Cancer Control [TNM-UICC8]). Regarding our surgical policy for nodal evaluation, systematic lymph node dissection (ND2a-1 or higher) was routinely performed for patients undergoing lobectomy. During segmentectomy, hilar lymph node sampling was routinely conducted to confirm nodal negativity. However, for wedge resection, lymph node sampling was not routinely performed.

Data collection and follow-up

Patient data, including age, sex, smoking history, body mass index (BMI), surgical procedure, postoperative adjuvant therapy (e.g., UFT or platinum-based chemotherapy), histological subtype, whole tumor size, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level, consolidation-to-tumor ratio (CTR), and presence of LI, VI, or PI, were retrospectively extracted from pathology reports, electronic medical records, and clinical charts.

Postoperative follow-up consisted of physical examinations, blood tests, and chest radiographs every three months for the first three years, and every six months thereafter. Computed tomography (CT) scans were performed every 6 to 12 months. RFS was defined as the time from the date of surgery to the date of first recurrence or death from any cause. Recurrence was confirmed by radiological imaging, primarily through these CT scans, or by pathological diagnosis. Patients alive and without recurrence were censored at the date of the last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were used to identify potential preoperative predictors of recurrence.

To ensure statistical robustness and avoid potential overfitting associated with data-driven categorization, clinical parameters— age, BMI, serum CEA level, and CTR—were treated as continuous variables in these models.

Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model to identify independent prognostic factors for RFS. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using log-minus-log survival plots, which showed approximately parallel curves for all variables. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Variables included in the univariate analysis were age, sex, BMI, smoking history, surgical procedure, histological subtype, tumor size, CEA level, C/T ratio, and the presence of LI, VI, or PI. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, these variables were assessed postoperatively. Continuous variables are presented as medians and ranges and were compared using the Student’s t-test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro, version 12 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

In the sublobar resection group, especially in patients who underwent wedge resection, the possibility of occult lymph node metastasis cannot be entirely ruled out. To address this potential bias and ensure the reliability of the pN0 classification, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients who underwent lobectomy with systematic lymph node dissection (ND2a-1 or higher), as nodal evaluation was most rigorous in this subgroup.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 692 patients were included in the analysis. Their clinicopathological characteristics, stratified by tumor size, are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 73 years (range, 31–92), with 428 patients (61.8%) having a history of smoking and 410 patients (59.2%) being male. Sublobar resection was performed in 292 patients (42.2%). The majority of patients (78.8%) were diagnosed with adenocarcinoma. Regarding postoperative adjuvant therapy, 607 patients (87.7%) did not receive any adjuvant treatment, while 81 (11.7%) received oral fluoropyrimidine (UFT) and 4 (0.6%) received platinum-based chemotherapy.

Survival analysis according to pleural, lymphatic, and vascular invasion

In Cohort 1, 47 patients (13.1%) had PI, while 312 patients (86.9%) did not. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients with PI had significantly worse RFS than those without (5-year RFS: 59.7% vs. 91.8%, P < 0.0001; Fig. 2A). LI did not significantly affect RFS in Cohort 1 (P = 0.35; Fig. 2B). However, the presence of VI was associated with significantly poorer prognosis (P = 0.02; Fig. 2C).

Prognostic impact of invasive features and other clinicopathological variables

To identify independent prognostic factors for RFS, we applied a univariable screening strategy where all variables with a p-value < 0.05 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model.

In Cohort 1, univariate Cox regression analysis identified sex, smoking status, histological type, CEA level, CTR, VI, and PI as significant prognostic factors. When these variables were entered into the multivariable model, only PI (HR: 2.87, 95% CI: 1.21–6.78, P = 0.017) remained as independent predictors of poor RFS (Table 2).

When stratified by tumor size, PI was identified as the most critical adverse prognostic factor in Cohort 1. However, in Cohort 2, LI demonstrated as a stronger association with poor RFS than PI. In Cohort 3, LI remained a major prognostic factor, while PI exerted a modest but significant impact on RFS.

A sensitivity analysis was performed on a subgroup of 400 patients who underwent lobectomy with systematic lymph node dissection (Cohort 1: n = 155; Cohort 2: n = 131; Cohort 3: n = 114). The results of the multivariate Cox regression analyses in this restricted cohort were remarkably consistent with those observed in the entire study population. Specifically, the prognostic significance of PI and LI remained robust across the respective tumor size categories, independent of the surgical extent or potential nodal understaging (Supplementary Tables 2, 3, and 4).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the impact of PI, LI, and VI on RFS in patients with node-negative NSCLC, stratified by tumor size. Our results demonstrated that, for tumors ≦ 2.0 cm, PI was the strongest prognostic factor. In tumors measuring > 2.0 cm ≦ 3.0 cm, only LI was a significant prognostic indicator. In larger tumors > 3.0 cm ≦ 5.0 cm, both PI and LI were associated with poor prognosis, with LI having a greater impact.

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality both in Japan and globally, with NSCLC accounting for approximately 85% of all cases [1]. Advances in imaging technologies have facilitated early detection, and in Japan, low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening is now strongly recommended (Grade A) for heavy smokers aged 50–74 [2].

Accordingly, the number of early-stage lung cancer cases eligible for surgical treatment is expected to increase. Although many early-stage NSCLC patients with complete resection have favorable outcomes, recurrence still occurs in a subset of cases. Therefore, identifying reliable prognostic factors is recommended for postoperative risk stratification and decision-making regarding adjuvant therapy. Historically, LI, VI, and PI have each been considered independent risk factors for recurrence [3, 5, 6]. In particular, PI has been incorporated into the T factor in the 8th edition of the TNM classification. Tumors with PL1 or PL2 are classified as T2a, even if they are ≦ 3 cm, while PL3 tumors are classified as T3 or T4 depending on the adjacent structure involved [7]. In contrast, LI and VI are not currently considered determinants of T status. However, interest in LI as a prognostic marker has been growing, as several reports suggest that LI-positive tumors may have worse outcomes than PI-positive tumors, even in small lesions [8–12].

A key finding of our study is the size-dependent variation in the prognostic impact of PI, LI, and VI. In tumors ≦ 2 cm, PI was the most significant factor, possibly because smaller tumors are less likely to exhibit LI or VI, and their proximity to the pleura increases the risk of pleural dissemination. As tumor size increases, local invasiveness and the likelihood of micrometastasis via lymphatic or vascular pathways may increase, thereby amplifying the influence of LI and VI. In fact, in tumors measuring > 2.0 cm ≦ 3.0 cm, and > 3.0 cm ≦ 5.0 cm., LI had a greater prognostic impact than PI.

In the present cohort, a substantial proportion of patients underwent sublobar resection, raising concerns regarding the reliability of pN0 classification due to potentially limited lymph node assessment. To address this issue, we conducted a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients who underwent lobectomy with systematic lymph node dissection, in whom adequate N1 and N2 nodal evaluation was ensured. In this subgroup, the prognostic impact of LI, VI, and PI on RFS remained consistent with that observed in the overall cohort. These findings suggest that the associations between invasive pathological features and prognosis in the present study are not primarily driven by nodal understaging related to sublobar resection.

In our cohort, VI was a significant prognostic factor only in tumors ≦ 2.0 cm, and its relative impact declined with increasing tumor size. This contrasts with the findings of Shimada et al., who reported that VI positivity was associated with poor prognosis across all tumor sizes, regardless of nodal involvement [13]. This discrepancy may be explained by the fact that our study focused exclusively on node-negative patients, and in more advanced tumors, the impact of VI may be masked by the stronger effects of LI and PI. Furthermore, our pathological assessment of VI was limited to a binary evaluation and did not account for the severity of invasion, such as the number of affected vessels or the vessel caliber. This lack of qualitative granularity might contribute to the diminished prognostic impact of VI in larger tumors, where the patterns of invasion are more likely to be heterogeneous.

Our results suggest that PI and LI positivity are strongly associated with poor prognosis in Stage I NSCLC. Tsutani et al. also reported that adjuvant chemotherapy significantly improved RFS in patients with pT1c or pT2a tumors, or those with LI positivity, indicating the potential benefit of adjuvant therapy in high-risk groups [14].

According to the current TNM classification, tumors with PI are classified as T2a and staged as IB or higher, even when the tumor diameter is ≦ 3 cm. This aligns with our findings, as PI is expected to substantially affect prognosis, even for small tumors. In contrast, in our study, patients with LI-positive pT1a–b tumors (n = 23) showed RFS comparable to that of patients with LI–negative pT1c (n = 102; 5-year RFS: 82.0% vs. 74.1%, P = 0.91) and LI–negative pT2a tumors (n = 55; 5-year RFS: 82.0% vs. 85.8%, P = 0.53) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, patients with LI–positive pT2a tumors (n = 8) demonstrated significantly worse RFS than those with LI–negative pT2a tumors (5-year RFS: 53.6% vs. 85.8%, P = 0.001). These results suggest that, even among pT2a tumors currently classified as Stage IB, the presence of LI may confer a prognostic disadvantage that outweighs the effect of tumor size alone.

Although these findings raise the possibility that LI–positive T2a tumors could be considered for classification as Stage IIA, this observation is severely constrained by the very small sample size of this subgroup (n = 8). Therefore, the reliability of such a modification lacks sufficient statistical robustness, and the observed differences must be interpreted with extreme caution. These results may be particularly susceptible to statistical instability, the fragility of small-group analysis, or unmeasured confounding factors. Consequently, further validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is mandatory before any modifications to the current TNM classification can be justified.

Furthermore, PI positivity was associated with markedly reduced RFS across all tumor sizes, including tumors ≦ 2.0 cm. This suggests that even patients with small tumors and PI positivity may benefit from adjuvant therapy.

Thus, postoperative risk stratification and treatment planning could be further refined by considering pathological factors in addition to the TNM classification. Furthermore, a comprehensive assessment that integrates tumor size and pathological features such as PI, LI, and VI may provide more detailed prognostic information. Future large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies are warranted to develop more refined prognostic models incorporating these factors and to validate their clinical utility.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective, single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. In particular, the analysis of specific subgroups, such as the LI-positive pT2a cohort, was based on an extremely small number of patients (n = 8). While the results reached statistical significance, the clinical magnitude of these findings must be interpreted with caution due to their inherent fragility and the risk of overfitting. Second, although the results of our sensitivity analysis on the lobectomy subgroup were consistent, a significant proportion of patients (42.2%) underwent sublobar resection. In these cases, especially those involving wedge resection, the extent of lymph node sampling may have been less intensive compared to systematic dissection. Therefore, the possibility of “occult” N1 or N2 disease influencing our RFS data—particularly regarding the impact of lymphatic invasion—cannot be entirely ruled out.

Third, we did not assess the interobserver agreement (e.g., kappa coefficient) for the pathological findings of LI, VI, and PI. Although all specimens were evaluated by experienced pathologists based on standardized criteria, the potential for interobserver variability cannot be entirely excluded. Future studies involving central pathological review or multi-institutional validation would be beneficial to confirm the reproducibility of our findings.

Fourth, pleural lavage cytology was available in only 38% of patients, which may have limited a comprehensive assessment of its association with PI and outcomes. Additionally, while the study focused on NSCLC, the impact of invasive features may differ between histological subtypes (e.g., adenocarcinoma vs. squamous cell carcinoma), and further subgroup-specific validation is warranted. Lastly, large-scale, multi-institutional prospective cohort studies are necessary to confirm the reproducibility of our findings and to validate whether LI should be formally incorporated into the T-category of the TNM staging system.

Conclusion

In this study, we conducted a stratified analysis based on tumor size in patients with surgically resected NSCLC without lymph node metastasis, and examined the impact of PI, LI, and VI on RFS. Our findings revealed that the prognostic significance of these pathological factors varies depending on tumor size. Specifically, PI had a notable impact on prognosis in small tumors, whereas LI became increasingly influential as tumor size increased. These results suggest that an integrated approach considering both tumor size and pathological factors are recommended for postoperative risk stratification and determining indications for adjuvant chemotherapy following surgical resection.
