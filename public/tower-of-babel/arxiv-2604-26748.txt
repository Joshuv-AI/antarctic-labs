On the Complexity of Robust Markov Decision Processes and Bisimulation Metrics
Marnix Suilen, Guillermo A. Pérez

Abstract
Robust Markov decision processes (RMDPs) extend standard Markov decision processes (MDPs) to account for uncertainty in the transition probabilities. RMDPs have an uncertainty set that defines a set of possible transition functions, each of which induces a standard MDP. The natural objective in an RMDP is to optimize the discounted cumulative reward under the worst-case transition function in the uncertainty set. We study the complexity of the associated threshold problem for RMDPs with polytopic uncertainty sets in halfspace representation. Previous results focused on approximating the optimum or restricted attention to specific subclasses of RMDPs, such as interval MDPs or L ∞ L_{\infty} -RMDPs. Our contributions are threefold: (1) For ( s , a ) (s,a) -rectangular RMDPs, we prove that robust policy evaluation is in P via robust linear programming, and that the threshold problem is in NP . As a corollary, robust policy iteration is a polynomial-time algorithm for these RMDPs when the discount factor is fixed. (2) For s s -rectangular RMDPs, we show that the threshold problem is in PSPACE via the first-order theory of the reals. (3) We establish lower bounds by reducing both parity games and bisimulation metrics between MDP states to the RMDP threshold problem. A polynomial-time algorithm for the threshold problem would resolve the long-standing open question of whether parity games can be solved in polynomial time. The reduction from bisimulation metrics also yields a practical benefit: it allows us to apply robust policy iteration as a more efficient alternative to the standard fixed-point iteration, as our empirical evaluation demonstrates.

Introduction

Markov decision processes (MDPs) form the theoretical backbone for many sequential decision-making problems and are foundational in reinforcement learning (RL)
[
31
,
36
]
.
The standard objective in an MDP is for the decision-making agent to compute a policy that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted reward.

MDPs rely on the assumption that their transition probabilities are exactly known; a prohibitive assumption in practice, where probabilities may be inaccurate or derived from a finite amount of samples and thus subject to statistical error
[
4
]
.

Robust MDPs
(RMDPs) overcome this assumption by instead modeling an uncertainty set which contains all probabilities the transition function can attain
[
20
,
34
,
40
]
.
The typical objective in an RMDP is to optimize the policy and value against the
worst-case
instance in the uncertainty set.
RMDPs are particularly well studied from the algorithmic and semantic perspectives.
Under certain structural independence assumptions on the uncertainty set, known as
rectangularity
, the Bellman operator of standard MDPs can be extended to RMDPs to account for the worst (or best) case instance.
Consequently, the classical value and policy iteration algorithms can be extended to RMDPs
[
20
,
28
]
.

For standard MDPs, it is well known that comparing their value against a threshold is
P
-hard and can be solved in polynomial time through linear programming
[
29
]
.
Existing complexity results for RMDPs are less pronounced and follow different directions.
In particular, previous works establish (1) polynomial time complexity based on the number of iterations the robust Bellman operator needs to achieve
\epsilon
-approximate solutions, where the precision
\epsilon
and the discount factor are both considered constant
[
28
,
40
]
; (2) that RMDPs with polytopic uncertainty where the vertices are part of the input (
i.e.
, vertex representation) are in
{\textsc{NP}}\cap{\textsc{coNP}}
by reduction to stochastic games
[
10
]
;
and (3) RMDPs with an
L_{\infty}
-based uncertainty set can be solved in (strongly) polynomial time for a fixed discount factor.
Note that all these results are consistent with each other, but the complexity of exactly solving RMDPs with polytopic uncertainty in the halfspace representation, the input format typically used in practice
[
3
,
27
,
28
,
35
]
, has remained open until now.

Contributions

In this paper, we expand on and generalize previous results regarding the computational complexity of RMDPs. We focus on the decision variant of the robust optimization problem: does there exist a policy such that its value surpasses a given threshold under the worst-case instance of the uncertainty set?
Our specific contributions are as follows

1.

For
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs, we prove that robust policy evaluation is in
P
via robust linear programming (
\Cref
thm:rmdp:policy:eval:in:P), and that the threshold problem is in
NP
(
\Cref
thm:rmdp:sa:threshold:NP).
As a corollary, robust policy iteration is a polynomial-time algorithm for these RMDPs when the discount factor is fixed (
\Cref
thm:rpi:polytime).
We also show how three common subclasses of RMDPs, interval MDPs,
L_{1}
-RMDPs, and
L_{\infty}
-RMDPs can be translated into equivalent polytopic RMDPs of polynomial size.

2.

For the larger class of
s
-rectangular RMDPs, where optimal policies may need randomization, we show that the threshold problem is in
PSPACE
via the first-order theory of the reals (
\Cref
thm:rmdp:s:threshold).

3.

We establish lower bounds by reducing both parity games and bisimulation metrics between MDP states to the threshold problem (
\Cref
prop:lower-bound,thm:bisim:reduces:rmdp).
Consequently, a polynomial-time algorithm for the RMDP threshold problem would resolve the long-standing open question of whether parity games can be solved in polynomial time.

We empirically demonstrate the benefits of using robust policy iteration over the standard fixed-point iteration for computing bisimulation metrics.

Related Work

In addition to the results already mentioned, our work is consistent with and expands on the following existing results.
In
[
30
]
, robust LPs are constructed to compute
optimistic
values and policies for RMDPs with convex uncertainty sets.
That is, policies and their value under the
best-case
in the uncertainty set.

For Markov chains with interval uncertainty,
[
12
]
shows that computing the value can be done in polynomial time via a linear program.
Similarly, bisimulation metrics between Markov chains are also known to be in
P

[
11
]
.
Both results are special cases of ours: that evaluation of a memoryless policy in
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs is in
P
.
For bisimulation metrics between MDP states, it is known that a single step of the fixed-point iteration is in
P

[
9
]
.
In
[
14
]
, it is shown that, for a fixed coupling, the fixed-point operator for the bisimulation metric under that coupling yields an MDP value function.
Consequently, there exists an MDP whose value function equals the optimal bisimulation metric.
We extend their result by showing that the optimal bisimulation metric, characterized by its fixed-point equation, coincides with the robust value function of a specifically constructed RMDP, characterized by its robust Bellman equation. This generalizes their result and allows us to directly apply RMDP algorithms such as robust policy iteration.

RMDPs are closely related to stochastic games
[
10
,
20
,
34
]
.
A similar observation has been made for bisimulation metrics between DTMCs and, consequently, for MDPs (
i.e.
, one-and-a-half-player games), algorithms such as policy iteration have been proposed
[
37
]
. Our work connecting bisimulation metrics between MDP states with RMDPs can be seen as an extension of that work to two-and-a-half-player games.
Finally, in
[
25
]
, the problem of
minimizing
the bisimulation metric under memoryless randomized policies is considered, and shown to be complete for the existential theory of the reals.

Background

We write
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
for the sets of real numbers and non-negative real numbers, respectively.
For a finite set
X
we write
|X|
for the number of elements in
X
.
A discrete probability distribution over
X
is a function
\mu\colon X\to[0,1]\subset\mathbb{R}
with
\sum_{x\in X}\mu(x)=1
.
The set of all distributions over
X
is denoted by
\mathcal{D}(X)
.

Vectors and matrices are denoted in bold,
e.g.
,
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and we write
\boldsymbol{x}(i)
for the
i
-th element of
\boldsymbol{x}
.
The vector
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the element of the standard basis with
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}(i)=1
and zeros elsewhere, and
\boldsymbol{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denotes the identity matrix.
For a finite set
J
, we write
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
for the vector indexed by elements of
J
, implictly assuming a total order on
J
.
This notation extends to Cartesian products of indices,
i.e.
,
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times K}
.
A
convex polytope

is a closed and bounded set of points
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by a linear equation system
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{b}
, with
\boldsymbol{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, which we denote by the tuple
(\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{b})
.
For any set
X
, its
convex hull
, that is the smallest convex polytope that contains
X
, is denoted
\mathsf{CH}(X)
.

A
linear program
(LP) is an optimization problem of the form

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{minimize}&\boldsymbol{c}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{x}\\
\text{subject to}&\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{b}\end{array}

where
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
form the input, and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a vector of
n
optimization variables.
Solving the LP
means finding an assignment of
\boldsymbol{x}
that is feasible,
i.e.
, it satisfies
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{b}
, and optimal,
i.e.

\boldsymbol{c}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\boldsymbol{c}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{y}
for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that are feasible.

For computability matters, we suppose all entries of
\boldsymbol{A}
,
\boldsymbol{b}
, and
\boldsymbol{c}
are rational numbers. In this case, it is known that a solution to the LP, if one exists, is also a vector of rationals. LPs can be solved in
time
(m+n+L)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}

[
24
,
7
,
39
]
. In words, we can solve them in time polynomial in the number
n
of variables, the number
m
of inequalities, and the number
L
of bits used to represent any entry of
\boldsymbol{A}
,
\boldsymbol{b}
, and
\boldsymbol{c}
as a pair of integers written in binary.

2.1
Markov Decision Processes

Definition 1

(MDP)
.

A Markov decision process (MDP) is a tuple
(S,A,P,R,s_{\iota},\gamma)
, where
S
and
A
are finite sets of states and actions;
P\colon S\times A\to\mathcal{D}(S)
, the transition function;
R\colon S\times A\to\mathbb{R}
, the reward function;
s_{\iota}\in S
, the initial state;
\gamma\in(0,1)
, a discount factor.

A
policy
selects actions to resolve the non-determinism in an MDP.
In general, a policy maps paths to distributions over actions:
\pi\colon(SA)^{*}S\to\mathcal{D}(A)
. The set of all policies is denoted by
\Pi
.
A
memoryless randomized
policy is a function
\pi\colon S\to\mathcal{D}(A)
that maps states to distributions over actions; a
memoryless deterministic
policy, a function
\pi\colon S\to A
that maps states to actions.
The set of all memoryless randomized (resp. memoryless deterministic) policies is denoted by
\Pi^{\text{MR}}
(resp.
\Pi^{\text{MD}}
).

An MDP and a policy together induce a classical (possibly infinite) Markov chain.
The probability space of a Markov chain is well (and even uniquely) defined: its events are sets of infinite paths starting from the initial state
s_{\iota}

[
5
,
26
]
.
We can thus talk about the expectation of (Borel-measurable) aggregation functions applied to the transition rewards along runs.

Discounted-reward value.

Given an MDP
M
and a policy
\pi\in\Pi
, its expected discounted cumulative reward value function
V^{\pi}_{M}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
, or simply “value”, is defined as

V^{\pi}_{M}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(s_{t},a_{t})\right],

where
R(s_{t},a_{t})
is the reward obtained from the state-action pair visited at step
t
.
We are interested in maximizing this value,
i.e.
we are looking for the optimal value

V^{*}_{M}=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}V^{\pi}_{M}.

It is well established that for this objective in MDPs, the optimum can be achieved by a memoryless deterministic policy
[
31
]
.
Consequently, the supremum is equal to the maximum over the finite set of all memoryless deterministic policies,
i.e.

V^{*}_{M}=\max_{\pi\in\Pi^{\text{MD}}}V^{\pi}_{M}.

2.1.1
Decision Problem, Encoding and Known Complexity Results

We reformulate the problem of optimizing the expected reward as that of deciding whether a specific threshold can be surpassed or not.

Problem 1

(MDP discounted reward problem)
.

Given an MDP
M
, is there a policy whose value is at least some given threshold
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
,
i.e.
,

\exists\pi\in\Pi^{\text{MD}}\colon V_{M}^{\pi}(s_{\iota})\geq\kappa
?

It is well known that the discounted reward problem for MDPs reduces, in polynomial time, to solving an LP. To facilitate the encoding, the transition and reward functions of the MDP can be interpreted as matrices.
Formally, for each state-action pair
(s,a)\in S\times A
, we define probability vectors

\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}\in[0,1]^{S}
with
\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})=P(s,a)(s^{\prime})
for all states
s^{\prime}\in S
. Similarly, for any memoryless deterministic policy
\pi\in\Pi^{\text{MD}}
, we write
\boldsymbol{P}^{\pi}\in[0,1]^{S\times S}
and
\boldsymbol{R}^{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
to denote the transition matrix and reward vector of the induced Markov chain.

Definition 2

(MDP LP encoding
[
31
]
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
be a vector of
|S|
optimization variables,
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
a vector of
|S|
strictly positive, but otherwise arbitrary, coefficients (
e.g.
, all ones).
For
the (unique)
optimal solution
\boldsymbol{v}
to the following LP, we have that
\boldsymbol{v}(\iota)=V_{M}^{*}
.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{minimize}&\boldsymbol{c}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\text{subject to}&\bigwedge_{s\in S}\bigwedge_{a\in A}(-\boldsymbol{e}_{s}+\gamma\boldsymbol{p}_{sa})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{v}\leq-R(s,a)\end{array}

This LP is polynomial in the size of
M
. Moreover, if the probabilities and rewards of
M
, and the threshold
\kappa
are all rational numbers given as pairs of binary-encoded integers, the same holds for the entries of the matrices and vectors of the LP. (Importantly, the magnitude of the maximal constant in the LP is bounded by that of
M
.)
Since solving LPs is in polynomial time and
V^{*}_{M}=\max_{\pi\in\Pi^{\text{MD}}}V^{\pi}_{M}
, solving
1
is also in polynomial time.

In practice, however, value iteration and policy iteration are more commonly used.
Value iteration, on the one hand, computes the least fixed point of the Bellman equation, which results in the optimal value function
V_{M}^{*}
.
Several variants of value iteration have been developed over time, either focusing on soundness
[
17
,
32
]
or efficiency
[
19
]
.
Policy iteration, on the other hand, computes an optimal policy and its value by iteratively evaluating and improving an arbitrary initial policy.
The evaluation of a memoryless deterministic policy
\pi
,
i.e.
, computing
V^{\pi}_{M}
, can be done by solving the following system of linear equations:

V^{\pi}_{M}=(\boldsymbol{I}-\gamma\boldsymbol{P}^{\pi})^{-1}\boldsymbol{R}^{\pi}.

(1)

The policy iteration algorithm (optimized with a so-called optimality test) is as follows.

Definition 3

(Policy iteration with suboptimality test)
.

Let
\pi\colon S\to A
be an arbitrary policy and
A_{s}^{*}=A
be the set of possibly optimal actions for every state
s
. Repeat the following:

1.

Evaluate the current policy
\pi
by computing
V_{M}^{\pi}
by solving
Equation 1
.

2.

Compute the normalized values

\Delta^{\pi}_{sa}=\left(R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}P(s,a)(s^{\prime})V_{M}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})\right)-V_{M}^{\pi}(s).

3.

Determine the improving actions:

A_{s}^{+}=\{a\in A_{s}^{*}\mid\Delta^{\pi}_{sa}>0\}
.
If
A_{s}^{+}=\emptyset
for all
s\in S
, return
\pi
and
V_{M}^{\pi}
.
Otherwise, update
\pi
such that

\forall s\in S\colon\pi(s)=\max_{a\in A_{s}^{*}}\Delta^{\pi}_{sa}
.

4.

Compute the new set of possibly optimal actions as follows:

A_{s}^{*}=\left\{a\in A_{s}^{*}\,\middle|\,\Delta^{\pi}_{sa}\geq\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\{\Delta^{\pi}_{sa^{\prime}}\}-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\left(\max_{s^{\prime}\in S}\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\{\Delta^{\pi}_{s^{\prime}a^{\prime}}\}-\min_{s^{\prime}\in S}\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\{\Delta^{\pi}_{s^{\prime}a^{\prime}}\}\right)\right\}.

Policy iteration without suboptimality tests is sound as long as the policy evaluation step is precise enough to correctly identify improving actions
[
18
]
.
The variant with suboptimality tests is known to terminate in at most
T(|A|-|S|)
iterations, with
T=\lfloor\nicefrac{{|S|}}{{1-\gamma}}\log(\nicefrac{{|S|^{2}}}{{1-\gamma}})\rfloor+1
, and
\mathcal{O}(|S|^{2}(|A|-|S|))
operations per iteration to compute all values exactly.
Interestingly, it is unknown whether policy iteration runs in polynomial time independent of the discount factor,
i.e.
, when it is part of the input
[
23
]
.

3
Robust Markov Decision Processes

We now introduce robust Markov decision processes (RMDPs).
Intuitively, an RMDP is a set of MDPs that differ in their transition probabilities, and policies for RMDPs must be optimized for the best- or worst-case MDP in the set.

Definition 4

(RMDPs)
.

A
robust MDP
(RMDP) is a tuple
(S,A,\mathcal{U},R,s_{\iota},\gamma)
, where

S,A,R,s_{\iota}
and
\gamma
are as for MDPs, and
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{S\times A\times S}
is a set of non-negative real vectors

\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}

that satisfy

\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{u}(s,a,s^{\prime})=1
, for all
(s,a)\in S\times A
.

The
uncertainty set

\mathcal{U}
of an RMDP induces a family of transition functions
P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,a)(s^{\prime})=\boldsymbol{u}(s,a,s^{\prime})
, one for each
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}
, such that each tuple
(S,A,P_{\boldsymbol{u}},R,s_{\iota},\gamma)
is a classical MDP.

Definition 5

(Rectangularity)
.

The uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
of an RMDP
(S,A,\mathcal{U},R,s_{\iota},\gamma)
is:

(s,a)
-rectangular

if it is composed of sets
\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\{s\}\times\{a\}\times S}
,
i.e.

\mathcal{U}=\bigtimes_{(s,a)\in S\times A}\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}
;

s
-rectangular

if it is composed of sets
\mathcal{U}_{s}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\{s\}\times A\times S}
such that
\mathcal{U}=\bigtimes_{s\in S}\mathcal{U}_{s}
.

Any RMDP that is neither
(s,a)
- or
s

non-rectangular
.

(s,a)
-rectangularity allows nature to choose a probability distribution at each state-action pair independently from all other state-action pairs
Consequently, nature can minimize the agent’s reward by selecting probability distributions
locally
,
i.e.
, at each state-action pair independently and unrestricted from the uncertainty sets at other state-action pairs.
The
(s,a)
-rectangularity assumption is commonly made for both theoretical and algorithmic purposes, as well as in applications such as reinforcement learning
[
21
,
35
]
, abstraction techniques
[
3
]
, and (statistical) model checking
[
2
]
.
Under
s
-rectangularity and, more generally, without rectangularity
assumptions, nature’s choices are more restricted. While this results in
the agent’s
robust discounted reward
(a formal definition follows)
being less conservative compared to
(s,a)
-rectangularity, the associated
problems become harder.

(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs, such as interval MDPs (trivially included in convex polytopes),

-RMDPs (which are a special case of IMDPs), and
L_{1}
-RMDPs, can all be reduced to RMDPs with convex uncertainty sets, as detailed in
Appendix A
.

3.2
Robust Discounted Reward

Given an RMPD
\mathcal{M}
and a fixed policy
\pi\in\Pi
, its
robust value
is defined as

\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}=\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi,P_{\boldsymbol{u}}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(s_{t},a_{t})\right].

In words, this is the worst-case value of
\pi
over all instantiations of the uncertainty. As before, we are interested in maximizing this value,
i.e.
we are looking for the optimal robust value
\underline{V}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}
.

Proposition 6

(Sufficient Policy Classes
[
40
]
)
.

The following policy classes are known to be sufficient for optimality, depending on the rectangularity and uncertainty set assumed.

(s,a)
-rectangular.

Under
(s,a)
-rectangularity, memoryless deterministic policies suffice,
i.e.

\underline{V}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}=\max_{\pi\in\Pi^{\text{MD}}}\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}
.

s
-rectangular and convex.

Under
s
-rectangularity, if each composing uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}_{s}
is convex, memoryless randomized policies suffice,
i.e.

\underline{V}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}=\max_{\pi\in\Pi^{\text{MR}}}\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}
.

To study its complexity, we reformulate the optimization problem as a decision one where we take a threshold as input.

Problem 2

(Robust discounted reward problem)
.

Given an RMDP
\mathcal{M}
, is there a policy whose robust value is at least some given threshold
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
,
i.e.
,

\exists\pi\in\Pi\colon\underline{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\pi}(s_{\iota})\geq\kappa?

Note that we are not restricting
\pi
in this general version of the problem. We will mostly look at particular instances based on rectangularity and properties of the uncertainty set, where further assumptions can be made about the policy.

3.3
Bellman Operator for
(s,a)
-Rectangular RMDPs

For this section, we fix an RMDP
\mathcal{M}
.
Under
(s,a)
-rectangularity, as per
6
, the optimal robust value of
\mathcal{M}
is given as

\underline{V}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}=\max_{\pi\in\Pi^{\text{MD}}}\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi,P_{\boldsymbol{u}}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(s_{t},a_{t})\right].

The robust value function
\underline{V}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
, where we are making the initial state
s_{\iota}
a parameter, admits a
robust version of the standard Bellman equation
. To state this formally, we recall
the robust Bellman operator
\mathfrak{B}\colon(S\to\mathbb{R})\to(S\to\mathbb{R})
:

\mathfrak{B}(\underline{V}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}})(s)=\max_{a\in A}R(s,a)+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}}\left\{\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,a)(s^{\prime})\underline{V}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}(s)\right\}.

Intuitively, under
(s,a)
-rectangularity, nature’s choice for a probability distribution can be inserted directly into the Bellman equation as a
local optimization problem
at the given state
s
and action
a
. The operator behaves just as nicely as that for standard MDPs.

Proposition 7

(
[
20
, Theorem 3.2]
)
.

The operator
\mathfrak{B}
is a contraction mapping w.r.t.
L_{\infty}
and its unique fixed point is
\underline{V}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}
.

4
The Complexity of the Robust Discounted Reward Problem

In this section, we consider the computational complexity of the robust
discounted reward problem for the different kinds of rectangularity
constraints we introduced earlier. First, we make some observations about how
hard the problem is:
P
-hard and at least as hard as finding the winner in parity games, a
problem known to be in
{\textsc{NP}}\cap{\textsc{coNP}}
and
{\textsc{UP}}\cap{\textsc{coUP}}

[
22
]
and even solvable in quasi-polynomial
time
[
8
]
, but not known to be in
P
.

Proposition 8
.

The robust discounted reward problem is
P
-hard, even under rectangularity
and convex uncertainty assumptions. Moreover, determining the winner of a
parity game reduces, in polynomial time, to the robust discounted reward
problem.

Proof sketch.

The problem is
P
-hard since the discounted reward problem for classical
MDPs is
P
-hard
[
29
]
and MDPs are a
special case of (rectangular, convex) RMDPs.

For the second part of the claim, consider an arbitrary
discounted-sum
game
—in contrast to MDPs, here, the successor state after playing
a
from
s
is resolved
antagonistically by an adversary. We can construct an
(s,a)
-rectangular
RMDP with uncertainty sets
\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}
being the simplex of the deterministic distributions over successors of
s
after playing
a
.
By memoryless determinacy of discounted-sum games
[
41
]
, the robust value of the
RMDP can be shown to coincide with the “value” of the discounted-sum
game. Parity-game hardness is then obtained by appealing to the reduction from parity games to
discounted-sum games
[
22
]
.
The full proof can be found in
\Cref
apx:games.
∎

Concerning upper bounds, we establish that for
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs the problem is in
NP
. The crux of the argument relies in proving that the robust value of a (guessed) memoryless deterministic policy can be evaluated in polynomial time. For
s
-rectangular RMDPs, where optimal policies may need randomization, we instead resort to an encoding into the theory of the reals, placing it in
PSPACE
.

To conclude, we leverage our policy evaluation results to derive a robust policy iteration procedure for
(s,a)
-rectangular convex-uncertainty RMDPs to obtain optimal robust policies.

4.1
The Case of
(s,a)
-Rectangularity and Convex Polytopic Uncertainty

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDP with convex uncertainty sets given as convex polytopes
\{\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}=(\boldsymbol{D}_{sa},\boldsymbol{b}_{sa})\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\{s\}\times\{a\}\times S}\mid s\in S,a\in A\}
. For complexity matters, we suppose the entries of all
\boldsymbol{D}_{sa}
and
\boldsymbol{b}_{sa}
are rationals represented as pairs of binary encoded integers. We also suppose we are given a threshold
\kappa\in\mathbb{Q}
. The last part of the input we consider is a
memoryless deterministic policy
\pi\colon S\to A\in\Pi^{\text{MD}}
.

Lemma 9
.

Determining whether
\underline{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\pi}\geq\kappa
holds true, can be done in polynomial time.

We remark that this result generalizes
[
12
, Proposition 3]
. In fact, it can be
proven by adapting the separation-oracle argument from
[
12
]
. Below,
we give an alternative argument using results from
robust linear
optimization

[
15
]
.

Proof.

For all
s\in S
, let
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}
be an arbitrary element of the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}_{s}=\bigtimes_{s\in A}\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}
. We introduce some notation to talk about the (uncertain) probabilities and rewards of the DTMC induced by
\pi
and the
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}
.
Write
\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{u}\pi}_{s}
for the vector from
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{S}
satisfying

\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{u}\pi}_{s}(s^{\prime})=\boldsymbol{u}_{s}(s,\pi(s),s^{\prime})
, for all
s^{\prime}\in S
, and
set
R^{\pi}(s)=R(s,\pi(s))
.

We will be working with the definition of the robust value

\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
where we consider it a function of the
initial state.
Now, by definition of
\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}
and linearity of
the expectation operator, we have the following.

\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}(s)=\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}_{s}\in\mathcal{U}_{s}}R^{\pi}(s)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{u}\pi}_{s}(s^{\prime})\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}(s^{\prime}),\text{ for all }s\in S.

If we write
P_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\pi}
for the matrix such that
P_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\pi}(s)(s^{\prime})=\boldsymbol{p}_{s}^{\boldsymbol{u}\pi}(s^{\prime})
for all
s,s^{\prime}\in S
, then we can rephrase the above in matrix form as
\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}=\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\bigtimes_{s\in S}\mathcal{U}_{s}}R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}
. By definition of
\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}
and
V^{\pi}
we also have that
\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}=\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\bigtimes_{s\in S}\mathcal{U}_{s}}V^{\pi}_{M(\boldsymbol{u})}
where
M(\boldsymbol{u})
is the MDP obtained from
\mathcal{M}
by fixing
\boldsymbol{u}
. Now, from
Equation 1
we get that
V^{\pi}_{M(\boldsymbol{u})}=(\boldsymbol{I}-\gamma P^{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{u}})^{-1}R^{\pi}
. In particular, the matrix
\boldsymbol{I}-\gamma P^{\pi}_{\boldsymbol{u}}
is guaranteed to be nonsingular, thus invertible, for all
\boldsymbol{u}
.

Since each
\mathcal{U}_{s}
is a product of convex polytopes, hence also a convex polytope, we know that the infimum is achieved at some vertex. This means the infimum can be rewritten as a minimum over the (finite) set
\mathrm{vert}(\mathcal{U}_{s})
of vertices of
\mathcal{U}_{s}
.

\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}(s)=\min_{\boldsymbol{u}_{s}\in\mathrm{vert}(\mathcal{U}_{s})}R^{\pi}(s)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{u}\pi}_{s}(s^{\prime})\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}(s^{\prime}),\text{ for
all }s\in S.

The equation above implies the following
inequality. Moreover, we know that for all
s\in S
there is some
\mu(s)\in\mathrm{vert}(\mathcal{U}_{s})
that makes it an equality.

\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}(s)\leq R^{\pi}(s)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{u}\pi}_{s}(s^{\prime})\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}(s^{\prime}),\text{ for
all }s\in S\text{ and all }\boldsymbol{u}_{s}\in\mathcal{U}_{s}.

(2)

Let
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}_{>0}
and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
be a vector of
(optimization) variables. The unique optimal solution
\boldsymbol{v}
to the
following mathematical program is such that
\boldsymbol{v}(s)=\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}(s)
, for all
s\in S
.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{maximize}&\boldsymbol{c}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\text{subject to}&\bigwedge_{s\in S}\bigwedge_{\boldsymbol{u}_{s}\in(\boldsymbol{D}_{s\pi(s)},\boldsymbol{b}_{s\pi(s)})}(\boldsymbol{e}_{s}-\gamma\boldsymbol{u}_{s}(s,\pi(s)))^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{v}\leq R^{\pi}(s)\end{array}

(3)

Indeed,
Equation 2
, tells us
\boldsymbol{v}(s)=\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}(s)
is a feasible assignment. Moreover, any optimal solution must satisfy
(\boldsymbol{I}-\gamma P_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\pi})\boldsymbol{v}\leq R^{\pi}
for all
\boldsymbol{u}\in\bigtimes_{s\in S}\mathcal{U}_{s}
. Then,
\boldsymbol{v}\leq(\boldsymbol{I}-\gamma P_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\pi})^{-1}R^{\pi}
for all
\boldsymbol{u}\in\bigtimes_{s\in S}\mathcal{U}_{s}
, and in particular
\boldsymbol{v}(s)\leq((\boldsymbol{I}-\gamma P_{\mu(s)}^{\pi})^{-1}R^{\pi})(s)=\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}(s)
for all
s\in S
, where the last equality follows from our choice of
\mu
to make
Equation 2
an equality. Based on our choice of
\boldsymbol{c}
, we thus conclude
\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}
is just as good as any optimal solution
\boldsymbol{v}
.

Equation 3
is a
robust LP
with
polytopic uncertainty
, already in standard form
[
15
]
.
Hence, we can directly apply dualization techniques to replace the infinite set of constraints by a small number of constraints, yielding the following standard LP
[
15
, Table 1]
.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{minimize}&-\boldsymbol{c}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\text{subject to}&\displaystyle\bigwedge_{s\in S}\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{e}_{s}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{v}+(\boldsymbol{b}_{s\pi(s)})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{w}_{s}\leq R^{\pi}(s)\\
(-\boldsymbol{D}_{s\pi(s)})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{w}_{s}=\gamma\boldsymbol{v}\\
\boldsymbol{w}_{s}\geq 0\end{cases}\end{array}

(4)

This LP is polynomial in the input, particularly in the convex polytopes given in their halfspace representation as tuples
(\boldsymbol{D}_{s\pi(s)},\boldsymbol{b}_{s\pi(s)})
.
Let
m
be the maximum number of rows of any
\boldsymbol{D}_{s\pi(s)}
and
\boldsymbol{b}_{s\pi(s)}
, for all
s
.
The LP has at most
(m+1)|S|
variables,
\boldsymbol{w}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
k\leq m
for each
s\in S
and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
, and
(2m+1)|S|
constraints.
∎

Now, we can leverage the fact that deterministic policies suffice. This allows us to have a guess-and-check procedure for the robust discounted reward problem.

Theorem 10
.

The robust discounted reward problem (
2
) is in
NP
, assuming
(s,a)
-rectangularity and convex polytopic uncertainty.

Proof.

By
9
we have that robust policy evaluation of a memoryless deterministic policy is in
P
.
We also know that memoryless deterministic policies are sufficient for discounted reward in
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs by
6
.
Since each such policy can be represented using polynomial space, we can thus nondeterministically guess a policy and evaluate it in polynomial time.
We conclude that the problem is in
NP
.
∎

4.2
The Case of
s
-Rectangularity and Convex Polytopic Uncertainty

This time we let
\mathcal{M}
be an
s
-rectangular RMDP with convex uncertainty sets given as convex polytopes
\{\mathcal{U}_{s}=(\boldsymbol{D}_{s},\boldsymbol{b}_{s})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\{s\}\times A\times S}\mid s\in S\}
. Once more we suppose rationals given as pairs of binary-encoded integers, and we take a threshold
\kappa\in\mathbb{Q}
as input.

Theorem 11
.

The robust discounted reward problem (
2
) is in
PSPACE
, assuming
s
-rectangularity and convex polytopic uncertainty.

Proof.

By
6
, it suffices to focus on memoryless randomized policies. Let
\pi\colon S\to\mathcal{D}(A)
be such a policy. Then, by repeating the first part of the proof of
9
for
s
-rectangular uncertainty sets, we establish that the unique optimal solution
\boldsymbol{v}
to the following mathematical program is such that
\boldsymbol{v}(s)=\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}(s)
, for all
s\in S
.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{maximize}&\boldsymbol{c}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\text{subject to}&\bigwedge_{s\in S}\bigwedge_{\boldsymbol{u}_{s}\in(\boldsymbol{D}_{s},\boldsymbol{b}_{s})}(\boldsymbol{e}_{s}^{\intercal}-\gamma\pi(s)^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{u}_{s}(s))\boldsymbol{v}\leq R^{\pi}(s)\end{array}

(5)

Equation 5
is a bit different from
Equation 3
since
\pi
is now memoryless randomized.
Note that
\pi(s)
, interpreted as a vector
\mathbb{R}^{A}
, is right-multiplied by
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}(s)
interpreted as a matrix
\mathbb{R}^{A\times S}
. Also,
R^{\pi}(s)
is now set to
\sum_{a\in A}\pi(s)(a)R(s,a)
.

It follows that the existence of a feasible assignment of
\boldsymbol{v}
such that
\boldsymbol{v}(s_{\iota})\geq\kappa
suffices to conclude
\underline{V}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}(s_{\iota})\geq\kappa
. Recall that
\pi
was chosen to be an arbitrary memoryless randomized policy. Towards encoding this in a logical statement, let
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times A}
be a vector of
|S||A|
real-valued variables. So far we have established that
\underline{V}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}(s_{\iota})\geq\kappa
if and only if there exists an assignment of
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{x}
that satisfies the following.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(s_{\iota})\geq\kappa\land\bigwedge_{s\in S}\left(\sum_{a\in A}\boldsymbol{x}(s)(a)=1\land\bigwedge_{a\in A}\boldsymbol{x}(s)(a)\geq 0\right)\text{ and }

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{u}:\bigwedge_{s\in S}\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{s}\boldsymbol{u}\leq\boldsymbol{b}_{s}\land(\boldsymbol{e}_{s}^{\intercal}-\gamma\boldsymbol{x}(s)^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{u}(s))\boldsymbol{v}\leq\sum_{a\in A}R(s,a)\boldsymbol{x}(s)(a)\right).

This is a sentence in the first-order theory of the reals with order and addition. Given such sentence of fixed quantifier alternation (in prenex form), determining its truth value is known to be in
PSPACE

[
6
,
33
]
. This concludes the proof of the claim, but we note that we even got a sentence from the
\exists\forall
-fragment of the theory, which is rather low in the recently explored hierarchy of complexity classes induced by the theory of the reals
[
33
]
.
∎

4.3
Robust Policy Iteration with Exact Evaluation

Standard policy iteration can be extended to robust policy iteration for
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs with convex polytopic uncertainty by replacing the policy evaluation step from
3
with the solution of the linear program from
Equation 4
.

Definition 12

(Robust policy iteration)
.

Let
\pi\colon S\to A
be an arbitrary policy and
A_{s}^{*}=A
be the set of possibly optimal actions for every state
s
. Repeat the following:

1.

Evaluate the current policy
\pi
by computing its robust value
\underline{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\pi}
by solving
Equation 4
.

2.

Compute the normalized values

\displaystyle\underline{\Delta}^{\pi}_{sa}

\displaystyle=\left(R(s,a)+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,a)(s^{\prime})\underline{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})\right)-\underline{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\pi}(s),

3.

Determine the improving actions:

A_{s}^{+}=\{a\in A_{s}^{*}\mid\underline{\Delta}^{\pi}_{sa}>0\}
.
If
A_{s}^{+}=\emptyset
for all
s\in S
, return
\pi
and
\underline{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\pi}
.
Otherwise, update
\pi
such that

\forall s\in S\colon\pi(s)=\max_{a\in A_{s}^{*}}\underline{\Delta}^{\pi}_{sa}
.

4.

Compute the new set of possibly optimal actions as follows:

A_{s}^{*}=\left\{a\in A_{s}^{*}\,\middle|\,\underline{\Delta}^{\pi}_{sa}\geq\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\left\{\underline{\Delta}^{\pi}_{sa^{\prime}}\right\}-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\left(\max_{s^{\prime}\in S}\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\left\{\underline{\Delta}^{\pi}_{s^{\prime}a^{\prime}}\right\}-\min_{s^{\prime}\in S}\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\left\{\underline{\Delta}^{\pi}_{s^{\prime}a^{\prime}}\right\}\right)\right\}.

Theorem 13
.

Robust policy iteration returns an optimal policy
\pi\in\Pi^{\text{MD}}
in at most
T(|A|-|S|)
iterations, with
T=\lfloor\nicefrac{{|S|}}{{1-\gamma}}\log(\nicefrac{{|S|^{2}}}{{1-\gamma}})\rfloor+1
, and
(m+n+L)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
operations per iteration. In particular, the runtime of a single iteration is dominated by solving two polynomial-sized LPs with
m
constraints and
n
variables.

Note that the algorithm runs in polynomial time if
\gamma
is fixed, just like standard policy iteration for classical MDPs. Critically, it is
not strongly polynomial
, even with
\gamma
being fixed, because we solve an LP in the first steps (that is not known to be doable in strongly polynomial time).
If one can replace the LP from steps 1 and 2 by a strongly polynomial policy evaluation, like was recently done for RMDPs with bounded-
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets
[
1
]
, then the whole algorithm becomes strongly polynomial—again, for a fixed
\gamma
.

The correctness of the optimality test follows from the robust Bellman operator (still) being a contraction mapping, and thus the proof for standard MDPs can be modified, see
\Cref
apx:robust:optimality:test for the full proof.

5
The Relation with Bisimulation Metrics

The preceding discussion on (robust) policy iteration is the perfect transition from RMDPs to
bisimulation metrics
to compare MDPs. In this section, we will argue that computing the
Kantorovich distance
between two given distributions with respect to a given
pseudometric
roughly corresponds to the (internal) LPs solved in the first steps of the policy-iteration procedure described above. Moreover, the fixed-point definition of bisimulation metric we focus on mirrors the iterative nature of the robust policy iteration procedure. After some necessary preliminaries to introduce all the notation, and formalizing the two links just claimed, we will construct an RMDP from two given MDPs so that the robust value of the RMDP corresponds to the bisimulation distance between designated states of the MDPs.

5.1
Notation for Bisimulation Metrics

We recap the standard definitions for bisimulation metrics between (states of) MDPs
[
13
]
.

Definition 14

(Pseudometric)
.

A pseudometric on a set
X
is a mapping
d\colon X\times X\to[0,\infty)
such that for all
x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in X
:

1.

x=x^{\prime}\implies d(x,x^{\prime})=0
;

2.

d(x,x^{\prime})=d(x^{\prime},x)
; and

3.

d(x,x^{\prime\prime})\leq d(x,x^{\prime})+d(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
.

The set of all pseudometrics over
X
is denoted as
\mathfrak{M}_{X}
.

We compare two pseudometrics using the product ordering. In symbols, for any two pseudometrics
d_{1},d_{2}\colon X\times X\to[0,\infty)
, we write
d_{1}\leq d_{2}
if
d_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\leq d_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})
holds true for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
. More interestingly, we use pseudometrics to quantify the distance between distributions over
X
.

Definition 15

(Kantorovich distance)
.

Let
d\in\mathfrak{M}_{X}
be a pseudometric over a finite set
X
. The Kantorovich distance between two distributions
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{D}(X)
, written
\mathfrak{D}_{K}(d)(\mu,\nu)
, is the solution to the following LP:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{minimize}&\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(j,k)d(x_{j},x_{k}),\\
\text{subject to}&\left(\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(j,k)=\mu(x_{j})\right)\land\left(\bigwedge_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(j,k)=\nu(x_{k})\right)\land\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq\boldsymbol{0},\end{array}

where
\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}(j,k)\mid 1\leq j,k\leq n\}
are
n^{2}
optimization variables.
A feasible assignment
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
to this LP is called a
coupling
, and by minimizing the objective function, we find the
minimal coupling
between distributions
\mu
and
\nu
w.r.t. the pseudometric
d
.
We denote the set of couplings between
\mu
and
\nu
by
\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}
.

Note that the set of couplings
\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}
is a convex polytope and
\sum_{j,k}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(j,k)=1
, for all
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Lambda_{\mu,\nu}
.

We can now define a (family of) bisimulation metrics between the states of an MDP

M=(S,A,P,R,s_{\iota},\gamma)
using
[
13
, Theorem 4.5]
.

Definition 16

(Fixed-point bisimulation metrics)
.

Let
0\leq c_{R},c_{T}
and
c_{R}+c_{T}\leq 1
and consider the operator
\mathfrak{F}_{K}\colon\mathfrak{M}_{S}\to\mathfrak{M}_{S}
on pseudometrics over the states
S
.

\mathfrak{F}_{K}(d)(s_{1},s_{2})=\max_{a\in A}c_{R}|R(s_{1},a)-R(s_{2},a)|+c_{T}\mathfrak{D}_{K}(d)(P(s_{1},a),P(s_{2},a))

Then,
\mathfrak{F}_{K}
has a least fixed point
d^{*}
which we call the
(c_{R},c_{T})
-fixed-point bisimulation metric.

The bisimulation metric between two MDPs
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
is defined as the distance between states
d({s_{\iota_{1}}},{s_{\iota_{2}}})
in the disjoint union of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
.
A standard choice for the weights is
c_{T}=\gamma,c_{R}=1-\gamma

[
13
]
. Henceforth, we refer to the
(1-\gamma,\gamma)
-fixed-point bisimulation metric simply as the
bisimulation metric
.

5.2
From Bisimulation Metric to an
(s,a)
-Rectangular RMDP

We show how computing the bisimulation metric between states of an MDP reduces to computing the robust discounted reward value of an
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDP.

Definition 17

(Bisimulation metric RMDP)
.

Let
M=(S,A,P,R,s_{\iota},\gamma)
be an MDP and consider
states
s_{1}^{*},s_{2}^{*}\in S
.
Construct the following
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDP:
\mathcal{M}=(S\times S,A,\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R},(s_{1}^{*},s_{2}^{*}),\gamma)
, where

\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{R}
are the uncertainty set and RMDP reward function defined as follows:

•

\mathcal{U}=\bigtimes_{((s,s^{\prime}),a)\in S\times S\times A}\mathcal{U}_{((s,s^{\prime}),a)}\text{ where }\mathcal{U}_{((s,s^{\prime}),a)}=\Lambda_{P(s,a),P(s^{\prime},a)}
; and

•

\mathcal{R}((s,s^{\prime}),a)=(1-\gamma)|R(s,a)-R(s^{\prime},a)|
.

That is,
\mathcal{U}
is an
(s,a)
-rectangular uncertainty set and the local uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}_{(s,s^{\prime}),a}
is the set of couplings between the distributions
P(s,a)
and
P(s^{\prime},a)
in the MDP transition function.
The reward function is the difference between rewards in the MDP, scaled by
1-\gamma
.

The robust value function of an RMDP is, in general, not a pseudometric.
For the specific bisimulation metric RMDP constructed above, we first show that its robust value function forms a pseudometric. Then, we prove that the robust value precisely coincides with the bisimulation metric of the original MDP.

Lemma 18
.

The robust value function
\underline{V}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
of the RMDP
\mathcal{M}
constructed in
17
is a pseudometric over the set of states
S
.

Proof sketch.

By induction, it follows that each property holds for every iteration of the robust Bellman operator of
\mathcal{M}
. The triangle inequality follows from the fact that the Kantorovich distance is a metric.
The full proof can be found in
Appendix D
.
∎

Theorem 19

(Correctness of RMDP construction)
.

The optimal robust value
\underline{V}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}
of the RMDP
\mathcal{M}
constructed in
17
precisely coincides with the bisimulation metric
d^{*}
between states of the MDP
M
,
i.e.
,
\underline{V}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}=d^{*}
and in particular
\underline{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{*}(s_{1}^{*},s_{2}^{*})=d^{*}(s_{1}^{*},s_{2}^{*})
.

Proof.

Let
M
be an MDP for which we want to compute the bisimulation metric between two states
(s_{1}^{*},s_{2}^{*})
, and let
\mathcal{M}
be the RMDP constructed in
17
.
By
7
, the robust Bellman operator
\mathfrak{B}
has
\underline{V}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}
as unique fixed point.
We show that
\mathfrak{B}
, when used on nonnegative functions over states of
\mathcal{M}
, is equivalent to the operator
\mathfrak{F}_{K}
on pseudometrics over states of the MDP
M
. It will thus follow that
\mathfrak{F}_{K}
is a contraction mapping, it has a unique fixed point
d^{*}
, and that
d^{*}
coincides with
\underline{V}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}
as required.

To ease readability, we omit the brackets around pairs of states when used in functions.
The robust Bellman operator for
\mathcal{M}=(S\times S,A,\mathcal{U},\mathcal{R},(s_{1}^{*},s_{2}^{*}),c_{T})
is defined as follows, where
\underline{V}
is a function
\underline{V}\colon S\times S\to\mathbb{R}
.

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}(\underline{V})(s,s^{\prime})=\max_{a\in A}\mathcal{R}(s,s^{\prime},a)+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,s^{\prime},a)}}\left\{\sum_{(t,t^{\prime})\in S\times S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,s^{\prime},a)(t,t^{\prime})\underline{V}(t,t^{\prime})\right\}.

We unfold the definition of
\mathcal{R}
and rewrite the uncertainty set with the set of couplings:

\displaystyle=\max_{a\in A}(1-\gamma)|R(s,a)-R(s^{\prime},a)|+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\Lambda_{P(s,a),P(s^{\prime},a)}}\left\{\sum_{(t,t^{\prime})\in S\times S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,s^{\prime},a)(t,t^{\prime})\underline{V}(t,t^{\prime})\right\}

Note that by definition of
\mathcal{U}
, we have
P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,s^{\prime},a)(t,t^{\prime})=\boldsymbol{u}(s,s^{\prime},a,t,t^{\prime})
, and thus we obtain

P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,s^{\prime},a)(t,t^{\prime})=\boldsymbol{\lambda}(t,t^{\prime})
:

\displaystyle=\max_{a\in A}(1-\gamma)|R(s,a)-R(s^{\prime},a)|+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Lambda_{P(s,a),P(s^{\prime},a)}}\left\{\sum_{(t,t^{\prime})\in S\times S}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(t,t^{\prime})\underline{V}(t,t^{\prime})\right\}

Since the inner optimization problem is a linear program on which the infimum is attained, and minimizers are guaranteed to exist, we may replace
\inf
by
\min
and obtain precisely the definition of the Kantorovich distance
\mathfrak{D}_{K}
with respect to
\underline{V}
:

\displaystyle=\max_{a\in A}(1-\gamma)|R(s,a)-R(s^{\prime},a)|+\gamma\min_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Lambda_{P(s,a),P(s^{\prime},a)}}\left\{\sum_{(t,t^{\prime})\in S\times S}\boldsymbol{\lambda}(t,t^{\prime})\underline{V}({t,t^{\prime}})\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{a\in A}(1-\gamma)|R(s,a)-R(s^{\prime},a)|+\gamma\mathfrak{D}_{K}(\underline{V})(P(s,a),P(s^{\prime},a))

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{F}_{K}(\underline{V})(s,s^{\prime}).\qed

6
Conclusion

We have clarified the complexity landscape of the broad class of RMDPs with polytopic uncertainty sets.
In particular, we established that these
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs are
P
-hard and in
NP
in general, and that robust policy iteration is a polynomial time algorithm for these RMDPs when the discount factor is fixed.
Yet, the exact complexity of
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs remains open.
A potential direction for future work would be to prove hardness for some level in the hierarchy of the theory of the reals.
For
s
-rectangular RMDPs, we have established the complexity to be in
PSPACE
via the theory of the reals.
Reductions from parity games and bisimulation metrics relate RMDPs to other well-known problems and allow the use of robust policy iteration to compute bisimulation metrics between MDP states, providing a computationally efficient alternative to the standard fixed-point iteration.

We only considered the expected discounted reward as the objective in this paper.
Our results naturally extend to other objectives, such as reachability, as long as the uncertainty set is
graph-preserving
,
i.e.
, all vectors in the uncertainty set must define the same graph structure.
Whether our complexity results can be maintained without this assumption, or whether that makes the RMDP threshold problem harder, is left for future work.

Special RMDP Instances

In this appendix, we show how three commonly used instances of RMDPs, namely interval MDPs (IMDPs), and
(s,a)
-rectangular
L_{1}
and
L_{\infty}
RMDPs, are
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs with polytopic uncertainty sets.

A.1
Interval MDPs

We recap the standard definition of interval MDPs used across the literature
[
35
,
34
]
and explain how they are
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs with convex polytopic uncertainty sets.

Definition 20
.

An interval MDP (IMDP) is a tuple
\mathcal{I}=(S,A,\check{P},\hat{P},R,s_{\iota},\gamma)
where
S,A,R,s_{\iota}
and
\gamma
are as for standard MDPs, and
\check{P},\hat{P}\colon S\times A\times S\to[0,1]
are the lower and upper bound functions on the transition probabilities such that

0\leq\check{P}(s,a,s^{\prime})\leq\hat{P}(s,a,s^{\prime})\leq 1
.

An IMDP is an
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDP with uncertainty set

\mathcal{U}=\bigtimes_{(s,a)\in S\times A}\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}=\left\{\boldsymbol{u}\,\middle|\,\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})=1\wedge_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})\in[\check{P}(s,a,s^{\prime}),\hat{P}(s,a,s^{\prime})]\right\}.

Lemma 21
.

Every state-action uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}
of an IMDP forms a convex polytope.

Proof.

Since all constraints in
\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}
are linear, it follows that the set forms a convex polytope.
We now explicitly construct the matrix
\boldsymbol{D}_{sa}
and vector
\boldsymbol{b}_{sa}
such that
\mathcal{U}_{s,a}=(\boldsymbol{D}_{sa},\boldsymbol{b}_{sa})
.
Assume an ordering over the
k=|S|
successor states and define

\boldsymbol{D}_{sa}=\begin{pmatrix}{-}\boldsymbol{1}_{k}^{\intercal}\\
\boldsymbol{1}_{k}^{\intercal}\\
{-}\boldsymbol{I}_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{I}_{k}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{b}_{sa}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
1\\
-\check{P}(s,a,s_{1})\\
\vdots\\
-\check{P}(s,a,s_{k})\\
\hat{P}(s,a,s_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\hat{P}(s,a,s_{k})\end{pmatrix},

where
\boldsymbol{1}_{k}
is the one vector of size
k
, and
\boldsymbol{I}_{k}
is the
k\times k
identity matrix.
Clearly, constructing
\boldsymbol{D}_{sa}
and
\boldsymbol{b}_{sa}
from an input IMDP is polynomial.
∎

A.2
L_{\infty}
-RMDPs

Next, we recap the standard definition for
L_{\infty}
MDPs, and prove how every
L_{\infty}
MDP can be transformed into an IMDP.

Recall that the
L_{\infty}
distance between two vectors
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as
\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{i}\{|\boldsymbol{x}(i)-\boldsymbol{y}(i)|\}
.

Definition 22

(
L_{\infty}
MDP)
.

An
L_{\infty}
MDP is a tuple
{{\textsc{L}}}_{\infty}=(M,\epsilon)
where
M=(S,A,P,R,s_{\iota},\gamma)
is an MDP, and
\epsilon\colon S\times A\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a state-action distance measure.
The
L_{\infty}
MDP
{\textsc{L}}_{\infty}
is an
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDP with state-action uncertainty sets defined by
\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}=\{\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}-\boldsymbol{u}_{sa}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon(s,a)\}
,
i.e.
, the set of probability vectors within
L_{\infty}
distance
\epsilon(s,a)
to the transition probability
P(s,a)
of
M
.

Lemma 23
.

L_{\infty}
MDPs reduce to IMDPs. That is, for every
L_{\infty}
RMDP, there is a polynomially sized IMDP with precisely the same uncertainty set.

Proof.

Given an
L_{\infty}
MDP
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}=(M,\epsilon)
around MDP
M=(S,A,P,R,s_{\iota},\gamma)
, we construct the IMDP
\mathcal{I}=(S,A,\check{P},\hat{P},R,s_{\iota},\gamma)
.
The interval on each transition
[\check{P}(s,a,s^{\prime}),\hat{P}(s,a,s^{\prime})]
is defined by

\check{P}(s,a,s^{\prime})=\max\{P(s,a)(s^{\prime})-\epsilon(s,a),0\},\quad\hat{P}(s,a,s^{\prime})=\min\{P(s,a)(s^{\prime})+\epsilon(s,a),1\}.

Note that this is a valid IMDP satisfying
0\leq\check{P}(s,a,s^{\prime})\leq\hat{P}(s,a,s^{\prime})\leq 1
for every transition, and the uncertainty sets at each state-action pair are

\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{I}}_{(s,a)}=\left\{\boldsymbol{u}\mid\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})=1\wedge\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})\in[\check{P}(s,a,s^{\prime}),\hat{P}(s,a,s^{\prime})]\right\}.

By the definition of the intervals, we have

\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{I}}_{(s,a)}=\left\{\boldsymbol{u}\,\middle|\,\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})=1\wedge\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})\in[P(s,a)(s^{\prime})-\epsilon(s,a),P(s,a)(s^{\prime})+\epsilon(s,a)]\wedge\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})\in[0,1]\right\}.

We now show
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}_{(s,a)}=\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{I}}_{(s,a)}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}_{(s,a)}
, and as before
\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}=P(s,a)
.
Then
\boldsymbol{u}
by definition satisfies the following constraints

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})\in[0,1]\wedge\sum_{s^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})=1\wedge\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}-\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon(s,a).

The first two constraints also appear in the definition of
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{I}}_{(s,a)}
.
We rewrite the last constraint.

\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}-\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon(s,a)\implies\max_{s^{\prime}}\big\{|\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})|\big\}\leq\epsilon(s,a).

Assume the maximum is attained at some
s^{*}
, then for all
s^{\prime}\neq s^{*}
, we have

|\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})|\leq|\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{*})-\boldsymbol{u}(s^{*})|\leq\epsilon(s,a).

(6)

Additionally, for all
s^{\prime}\in S
, including
s^{*}
, we have for arbitrary
c\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})|\leq c

\displaystyle\implies-c\leq\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})\leq c

\displaystyle\implies-c-\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})\leq-\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})\leq c-\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\implies c+\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})\geq\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})\geq\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})-c

\displaystyle\implies\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})\in[\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})-c,\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})+c]

From
\Cref
eq:maximum:linf:element with
c=\epsilon(s,a)
, it then follows that for all
s^{\prime}\in S
, we have
\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})\in[\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})-\epsilon(s,a),\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon(s,a)]
.
Hence
\forall s\in S,a\in A\colon\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}_{(s,a)}\subseteq\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{I}}_{(s,a)}
.

The other direction follows similarly.
Assume
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{I}}_{(s,a)}
.
By definition,
\boldsymbol{u}
satisfies
\forall s^{\prime}\in S\colon\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})\in[0,1]\wedge\sum_{s^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{u}=1
, hence we only need to show it satisfies the infinity norm constraint.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{I}}_{(s,a)}

\displaystyle\implies\forall s^{\prime}\in S\colon\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})\in[\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})-\epsilon(s,a),\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon(s,a)]

\displaystyle\implies\forall s^{\prime}\in S\colon|\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})|\leq\epsilon(s,a)

\displaystyle\implies\max_{s^{\prime}}\{|\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}(s^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})|\}\leq\epsilon(s,a).

Thus,
\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}-\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\epsilon(s,a)
,
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}_{(s,a)}
, and
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{L}_{\infty}}_{(s,a)}\supseteq\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{I}}_{(s,a)}
.
We conclude that both uncertainty sets contain the same probability distributions, and thus that every
L_{1}
MDP can be reduced to an IMDP.
∎

From
\Cref
lemma:IMDP:is:RMDP follows that every
L_{\infty}
MDP is an
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDP with convex polytopic uncertainty sets.

A.3
L_{1}
-RMDPs

Definition 24

(
L_{1}
MDP)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(M,\epsilon)
where
M=(S,A,P,R,s_{\iota},\gamma)

The

\mathcal{M}

(s,a)

\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}=\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{S}\mid\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})=1\wedge\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}-\boldsymbol{u}_{sa}\rVert_{1}\leq\epsilon(s,a)\}.

That is, it is the set of probability vectors within
L_{1}

\epsilon(s,a)
to the transition probability
P(s,a)
of

.

The uncertainty sets
\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}

|S|
additional variables, but a linear number of constraints.
We give both formulations below.

First, the polytope that remains
|S|
-dimensional but incurs an exponential blow-up in the number of constraints.
The polytope is given by

\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}=\left\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{S}\mid\sum_{s^{\prime}inS}\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})=1\bigwedge_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\{-1,1\}^{S}}\boldsymbol{t}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{u}\leq\epsilon(s,a)\boldsymbol{t}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}\right\}.

\boldsymbol{t}\in\{-1,1\}^{S}
,

,
2^{|S|}
constraints.
It can be represented by the following matrix
\boldsymbol{D}_{sa}
and vector
\boldsymbol{b}_{sa}
such that
\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}=(\boldsymbol{D}_{sa},\boldsymbol{b}_{sa})
:

\boldsymbol{D}_{sa}=\begin{pmatrix}{-}\boldsymbol{1}_{k}^{\intercal}\\
\boldsymbol{1}_{k}^{\intercal}\\
{-}\boldsymbol{T}\end{pmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{b}_{sa}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\

\epsilon(s,a)\boldsymbol{1}_{2^{k}}+\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{p}_{sa}\end{pmatrix},

where

,

Alternatively,
\mathcal{U}_{s,a}

, one with more variables but a linear number of constraints in the input size.

\mathcal{U}_{s,a}^{+}=\left\{(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{t})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2|S|}\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{u}(s^{\prime})=1\wedge\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{t}(s^{\prime})\leq\epsilon(s,a)\\

The lifted polytope is represented by the following matrix and vector.

\boldsymbol{D}_{sa}^{+}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{1}_{k}^{\intercal}&\boldsymbol{0}_{k}\\
{-}\boldsymbol{1}_{k}^{\intercal}&\boldsymbol{0}_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{0}_{k}&\boldsymbol{1}^{\intercal}\\
\boldsymbol{I}_{k}&-\boldsymbol{I}_{k}\\
{-}\boldsymbol{I}_{k}&\boldsymbol{I}_{k}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{b}_{sa}^{+}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-1\\
\epsilon(s,a)\\
\boldsymbol{p}\\
{-}\boldsymbol{p}\end{pmatrix},

where again the subscript

denotes the respective dimensions.
We can rewrite the polytope

We split the matrix
\boldsymbol{D}_{sa}^{+}

{-}\boldsymbol{1}_{k}^{\intercal}\\
\boldsymbol{0}_{k}\\

\boldsymbol{0}_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{1}^{\intercal}\\

\boldsymbol{I}_{k}\end{pmatrix}.

The problem then dualizes into

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{v}}\,

\displaystyle\,{-}\boldsymbol{c}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{v}

Subject to:

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{s\in S}\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{e}_{s}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{v}+(\boldsymbol{b}_{s}^{\pi})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{w}_{s}\leq R^{\pi}(s)\\

(7)

A.4

We close the RMDPs defined in

We now construct the matrix
\boldsymbol{D}_{sta}
and vector

Let
\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathit{row}}=\boldsymbol{I}_{k}\otimes\boldsymbol{1}_{k}^{\intercal}

be the probability vectors at state-action pairs

The polytope is then defined by

\boldsymbol{D}_{sta}=\begin{pmatrix}{-}\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathit{row}}\\
\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathit{row}}\\
{-}\boldsymbol{C}_{\mathit{col}}\\

Parity-game Hardness

Let
\pi
be a memoryless policy. We define the
antagonistic value
of the policy by (adversarially) choosing successors in the RMDP. For a function
\tau:S\times A\to S
, we say it respects the possibilities in the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
, and write
\tau\models\mathcal{U}
if:

P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,a,\tau(s,a))>0,\text{ for all }s\in S,a\in\mathsf{Supp}(\pi(s)).

for some
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}
. If, additionally,

P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,a,\tau(s,a))=1,\text{ for all }s\in S,a\in\mathsf{Supp}(\pi(s)).

for some
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}
, we say
\mathcal{U}
contains the
\tau
-vertex and we write
\tau\in\mathcal{U}
. Now, define:

\mathrm{aVal}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}=\inf_{\tau\models\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(s_{t},a_{t}).\right]

where
s_{t+1}=\tau(s_{t},a_{t})
.

Lemma 25
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be such that, for all
\tau:S\times A\to S
, it contains the
\tau
-vertex. Then, for all policies
\pi
, we have that

\mathrm{aVal}^{\pi}_{\mathcal{M}}\leq\underline{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\pi}.
If, moreover,
\mathcal{U}
is the convex hull of all
\tau
-vertices, then we have equality.

The first part of the statement follows directly from containment of the
\boldsymbol{u}
corresponding to each
\tau
in
\mathcal{U}
. For the second part, we note that the
\tau
-vertices correspond to the vertices of the convex hull. Indeed, recall that the robust discounted reward value of a given policy is achieved at a vertex of the uncertainty polytope.

A
discounted-sum game

[
41
]
(played on an automaton) is a tuple
(S,A,\Delta,R,s_{\iota},\gamma)
where
S
,
A
,
R
,
s_{\iota}
and
\gamma
are as for MDPs and
\Delta:S\times A\to 2^{S}
is a transition relation. A
strategy
of the protagonist is a function
\sigma:S\to A
; while a strategy of the antagonist is a function
\tau:S\times A\to S
such that
(s,a,\tau(s,a))\in\Delta
for all
(s,a)\in S\times A
. We remark that we are restricting ourselves to memoryless deterministic strategies for both players. This is known to be no loss of generality for the value problem (defined below)
[
41
]
. A pair
(\sigma,\tau)
of strategies for both players defines a unique infinite path
\rho_{\sigma\tau}=s_{0}a_{0}s_{1}\dots
from
s_{0}=s_{\iota}
and its value
\mathrm{Val}(\rho_{\sigma\tau})
is
\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\gamma^{t}R(s_{t},a_{t})
. We are usually interested in the value of the game
\mathrm{Val}^{*}=\max_{\sigma}\min_{\tau}\mathrm{Val}(\rho_{\sigma\tau})
. The natural decision problem asks whether this value is at least some given (rational) threshold.

Theorem 26
.

Discounted-sum games reduce (in polynomial-time) to the robust discounted reward problem, and the constructed RMDP is
(s,a)
-rectangular with convex polytopic uncertainty.

Proof.

For any instance of a discounted-sum game, we add as uncertainty sets to it the convex hull of all
\tau
-vertices for
\tau
a strategy of the adversary. Note that this yields an
(s,a)
-rectangular uncertainty set by definition of
\tau
(the successor depends only on
s
and
a
). Moreover, we the uncertainty sets are all convex by definition too. To conclude, it follows from
25
that the value of the game coincides with the robust expected discounted-sum value of the RMDP.
∎

Using the known reduction from parity games to discounted-sum games
[
22
]
, we conclude that if we had a polynomial-time algorithm for the robust expected discounted-sum problem we would have a polynomial-time algorithm for parity games.

Appendix C
Correctness of the Robust Optimality Test

For standard MDPs, it is known that the set of possibly optimal actions is defined as

A_{s}^{*}=\left\{a\in A_{s}^{*}\,\middle|\,\Delta^{\pi}_{sa}\geq\max_{a\in A}\Delta^{\pi}_{sa}-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\Big(\max_{s^{\prime}\in S}\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\Delta^{\pi}_{s^{\prime}a^{\prime}}-\min_{s^{\prime}\in S}\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\Delta^{\pi}_{s^{\prime}a^{\prime}}\Big)\right\}.

In the following, we show that the set of possibly optimal actions in an RMDP is given by

A_{s}^{*}=\left\{a\in A_{s}^{*}\,\middle|\,\underline{\Delta}^{\pi}_{sa}\geq\max_{a\in A}\underline{\Delta}^{\pi}_{sa}-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\Big(\max_{s^{\prime}\in S}\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\underline{\Delta}^{\pi}_{s^{\prime}a^{\prime}}-\min_{s^{\prime}\in S}\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\underline{\Delta}^{\pi}_{s^{\prime}a^{\prime}}\Big)\right\}.

Recall the robust Bellman operator

\mathfrak{B}(\underline{V})(s)=\max_{a\in A}R(s,a)+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,a)(s^{\prime})\underline{V}(s^{\prime}).

The robust Bellman operator is a monotone contraction mapping
[
20
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Theorem 27
.

Given an
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDP
\mathcal{M}=(S,A,\mathcal{U},R,s_{\iota},\gamma)
, an action
a\in A
is suboptimal for state
s\in S
, when for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}

R(s,a)+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,a)(s^{\prime})\boldsymbol{x}(s^{\prime})<\mathfrak{B}(\boldsymbol{x})(s)-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\mathsf{span}(\mathfrak{B}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}),

where
\mathsf{span}(\boldsymbol{y})=\max_{s\in S}\boldsymbol{y}(s)-\min_{s\in S}\boldsymbol{y}(s)
for any
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
.

Proof.

Let
b,c\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
b<c
,
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
, and
\boldsymbol{v}=\underline{V}
the robust value function as a vector.
If

\boldsymbol{x}+b\boldsymbol{1}\leq\boldsymbol{v}\leq\boldsymbol{x}+c\boldsymbol{1}\text{ and }R(s,a)+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}}\sum_{s^{\prime}}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,a)(s^{\prime})\boldsymbol{x}(s^{\prime})<\mathfrak{B}(\boldsymbol{x})(s)-\gamma(c-b),

then action
a
is suboptimal.

For any
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}
, let
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times S}
be the stochastic matrix defined by
\boldsymbol{p}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{a}(s,s^{\prime})=P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,a)(s^{\prime})
.
Then for all
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}

\displaystyle R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{p}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{a}(s,s^{\prime})(\boldsymbol{x}(s^{\prime})+c)=R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{a}(s,s^{\prime})\boldsymbol{x}(s^{\prime})\gamma c,

as
\boldsymbol{p}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{a}
is stochastic.
The above holds in particular for the stochastic matrix
\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}}\boldsymbol{p}_{u}^{a}
, thus we obtain

\displaystyle R(s,a)+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{p}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{a}(s,s^{\prime})(\boldsymbol{x}(s^{\prime})+c)

\displaystyle=R(s,a)+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}}\sum_{s^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{a}(s,s^{\prime})\boldsymbol{x}(s^{\prime})\gamma c

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{B}(\boldsymbol{x})(s)+\gamma b=\mathfrak{B}(\boldsymbol{x}+b\boldsymbol{1})(s).

From the monotonicity of
\mathfrak{B}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{B}(\boldsymbol{v})(s)\geq\mathfrak{B}(\boldsymbol{x}+b\boldsymbol{1})(s)

\displaystyle>R(s,a)+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{p}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{a}(s,s^{\prime})(\boldsymbol{x}(s^{\prime})+c)

\displaystyle\geq R(s,a)+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\boldsymbol{p}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{a}(s,s^{\prime})\boldsymbol{v}(s^{\prime})

Set
b=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\min_{s\in S}(\mathfrak{B}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x})(s)
and
c=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\max_{s\in S}(\mathfrak{B}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x})(s)
, and we obtain

R(s,a)+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,a)(s^{\prime})\boldsymbol{x}(s^{\prime})<\mathfrak{B}(\boldsymbol{x})(s)-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\mathsf{span}(\mathfrak{B}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}),

as desired.
∎

From the result above, with
\boldsymbol{x}=\underline{V}^{\pi}
the value under some policy
\pi
, we note that

(\mathfrak{B}(\underline{V}^{\pi})-\underline{V}^{\pi})(s)=\max_{a\in A}R(s,a)+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,a)}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,a)(s^{\prime})\underline{V}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})-\underline{V}^{\pi}(s)=\max_{a\in A}\underline{\Delta}_{sa}^{\pi}.

Thus, we can reduce the set of improving actions at state
s\in S
to

\left\{a\in A\,\middle|\,\underline{\Delta}_{sa}^{\pi}\geq\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\underline{\Delta}_{sa^{\prime}}^{\pi}-\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}(\max_{s^{\prime}\in S}\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\underline{\Delta}_{s^{\prime}a^{\prime}}^{\pi}-\min_{s^{\prime}\in S}\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\underline{\Delta}_{s^{\prime}a^{\prime}}^{\pi})\right\}.

Appendix D
Proof
\Cref
lemma:robust:bisim:value:is:pseudometric

\Cref

lemma:robust:bisim:value:is:pseudometric

The robust value function
\underline{V}
of the RMDP
\mathcal{M}
constructed in
\Cref
def:bisim:RMDP is a pseudometric over the set of states
S
.

Proof.

By construction of the reward function
\mathcal{R}
, all rewards are positive, hence,
\underline{V}\colon S\times S\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and we can initialize the value function as
\underline{V}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
We now show the properties of a pseudometric hold by induction on the iterations of
\underline{V}^{(n)}
.
For the base case
\underline{V}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{0}
, the properties immediately follow.
As induction hypothesis, assume
\underline{V}^{(n)}
is a pseudometric.
We now show that applying the robust Bellman operator on
\underline{V}^{(n)}
,
i.e.
, computing
\underline{V}^{(n+1)}=\mathfrak{B}(\underline{V}^{(n)})
again satisfies properties 1–3.

1.

Assume
s=s^{\prime}
, we write

\underline{V}^{(n+1)}(s,s^{\prime})=\max_{a}|\mathcal{R}(s,s^{\prime},a)|+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,s^{\prime},a)}}\sum_{(t,t^{\prime})\in S\times S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,s^{\prime},a)(t,t^{\prime})\underline{V}^{(n)}(t,t^{\prime}),

and observe that for all
a\in A
we have
\mathcal{R}(s,s^{\prime},a)=|R(s,a)-R(s^{\prime},a)|=0
.
Additionally, since
s=s^{\prime}
, we have
P(s,a)=P(s^{\prime},a)
and since
\mathcal{U}_{(s,s^{\prime},a)}=\Lambda_{P(s,a),P(s^{\prime},a)}
we get that the minimal coupling weighed by
\underline{V}^{(n)}
has a cost of zero as the distributions are the same, and thus the successor states too,
i.e.
, for all
(t,t^{\prime})\in S\times S
where
P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,s^{\prime},a)(t,t^{\prime})\neq 0
we have
t=t^{\prime}
and thus
\underline{V}^{(n)}(t,t^{\prime})=0
.
Hence,
\underline{V}^{(n+1)}(s,s^{\prime})=0
.

2.

Note that in addition to
\underline{V}^{(n)}(s,s^{\prime})=\underline{V}^{(n)}(s^{\prime},s)
, by definition the reward function and the uncertainty set are symmetrical,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{R}(s,s^{\prime},a)=\mathcal{R}(s^{\prime},s,a)
and
\mathcal{U}_{(s,s^{\prime},a)}=\Lambda_{P(s,a),P(s^{\prime},a)}=\Lambda_{(s^{\prime},s,a)}=\mathcal{U}_{(s^{\prime},s,a)}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\underline{V}^{(n+1)}(s,s^{\prime})=\max_{a\in A}\mathcal{R}(s,s^{\prime},a)+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s,s^{\prime},a)}}\sum_{(t,t^{\prime})\in S\times S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,s^{\prime},a)(t,t^{\prime})\underline{V}^{(n)}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad=\max_{a\in A}\mathcal{R}(s^{\prime},s,a)+\gamma\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s^{\prime},s,a)}}\sum_{(t^{\prime},t)\in S\times S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s^{\prime},s,a)(t^{\prime},t)\underline{V}^{(n)}(t^{\prime},t)=\underline{V}^{(n+1)}(s^{\prime},s).

3.

Using that
\underline{V}^{(n)}
is a pseudometric by assumption, and that
\mathcal{U}_{(s,s^{\prime\prime},a)}
is the set of couplings between
P(s,a)
and
P(s^{\prime\prime},a)
, we observe that the inner minimization problem defines the Kantorovich distance, which is a metric and thus satisfies the triangle inequality.
Thus it follows that

\displaystyle\underline{V}^{(n+1)}(s,s^{\prime\prime})=\max_{a\in A}\mathcal{R}(s,s^{\prime\prime},a)+\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}(s,s^{\prime\prime},a)}\sum_{(t,t^{\prime\prime})\in S\times S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,s^{\prime\prime},a)(t,t^{\prime\prime})\underline{V}^{(n)}(t,t^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{a\in A}\mathcal{R}(s,s^{\prime},a)+\mathcal{R}(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime},a)+\gamma\Bigg(\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}(s,s^{\prime},a)}\left\{\sum_{(t,t^{\prime})\in S\times S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,s^{\prime},a)(t,t^{\prime})\underline{V}^{(n)}(t,t^{\prime})\right\}

\displaystyle+\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime},a)}}\bigg\{\sum_{(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\in S\times S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime},a)(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\underline{V}^{(n)}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\bigg\}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\max_{a\in A}\mathcal{R}(s,s^{\prime},a)+\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}(s,s^{\prime},a)}\sum_{(t,t^{\prime})\in S\times S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s,s^{\prime},a)(t,t^{\prime})\underline{V}^{(n)}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+\max_{a\in A}\mathcal{R}(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime},a)+\inf_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime},a)}\sum_{(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\in S\times S}P_{\boldsymbol{u}}(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime},a)(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\underline{V}^{(n)}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\underline{V}^{(n+1)}(s,s^{\prime})+\underline{V}^{(n+1)}(s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}).

∎
