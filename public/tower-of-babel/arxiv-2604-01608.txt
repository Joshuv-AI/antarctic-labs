From Multi-Agent to Single-Agent: When Is Skill Distillation Beneficial?
Binyan Xu, Dong Fang, Haitao Li, Kehuan Zhang, [4pt] The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, China

Abstract
Multi-agent systems (MAS) tackle complex tasks by distributing expertise, though this often comes at the cost of heavy coordination overhead, context fragmentation, and brittle phase ordering. Distilling a MAS into a single-agent skill can bypass these costs, but this conversion lacks a principled answer for when and what to distill. Instead, the empirical outcome is surprisingly inconsistent: skill lift ranges from a 28% improvement to a 2% degradation across metrics of the exact same task. In this work, we reveal that skill utility is governed not by the task, but by the evaluation metric. We introduce Metric Freedom ( F F ), the first a priori predictor of skill utility. F F measures the topological rigidity of a metric’s scoring landscape by quantifying how output diversity couples with score variance via a Mantel test. Guided by F F , we propose AdaSkill , a two-stage adaptive distillation framework. Stage 1 acts as a selective extraction mechanism, extracting tools and knowledge while discarding restrictive structures on “free” metrics to preserve exploration. Stage 2 applies iterative refinement selectively on free metrics, exploiting their forgiving scoring landscape to safely maximize remaining headroom. Evaluating across 4 tasks, 11 datasets, and 6 metrics, F F strongly predicts skill utility ( r = − 0.85 r{=}{-}0.85 , p < 0.0001 p{<}0.0001 ). Strikingly, identical agent trajectories yield diametrically opposite skill lifts under rigid versus free metrics, demonstrating that skill utility is fundamentally a metric-level property. Driven by this signal, AdaSkill matches or exceeds the original MAS while reducing cost up to 8 × \times and latency by up to 15 × \times .

Introduction

Large Language Model (LLM) agents have demonstrated remarkable proficiency across complex domains, from scientific discovery to software engineering
(
6
;
13
;
44
)
. To tackle increasingly difficult tasks, the field has largely converged on Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), which decompose problems into specialized, interacting sub-agents to capture domain expertise. However, this distributed approach comes at a steep cost: severe coordination overhead, context fragmentation, and brittle phase ordering. Consequently, a compelling alternative has emerged: distilling the expertise of a MAS into a single-agent system equipped with specialized skills
(
38
;
18
)
. While recent work shows these single-agent systems can match MAS performance at a fraction of the cost, empirical results reveal a baffling inconsistency. Skill distillation can yield massive performance gains (
+
28pp) or actively degrade performance (
-
2pp) on the
exact same task
.

Existing literature largely treats this variance as an unpredictable byproduct of task complexity or data scale, proceeding under the assumption that skill augmentation is universally beneficial. Current approaches focus entirely on
how
to distill skills: either through rigid structural conversions that map MAS pipelines into instructions
(
17
;
21
;
41
)
, or through iterative trajectory optimization
(
5
;
39
;
37
;
43
)
. Both paradigms suffer from a critical blind spot. Non-adaptive structural conversions routinely over-constrain agents on simple queries, while iterative methods succumb to trajectory-local overfitting, bloating the skill library until retrieval accuracy collapses
(
17
)
. The fundamental question remains unanswered:
when does distilling a MAS into single-agent skills actually help?

In this work, we reveal that the efficacy of skill distillation is not dictated by the task itself, but by an overlooked property of the
evaluation metric
. We introduce
Metric Freedom
(
F
), the first quantifiable,
a priori
predictor of skill utility.
F
measures the topological rigidity of a metric’s scoring landscape by quantifying how tightly output variation couples with score variation via a Mantel test. On
rigid
metrics (low
F
), structured skill guidance steers the agent through a narrow success corridor and yields large gains. On
free
metrics (high
F
), diverse paths score equally well, so the same constraints restrict exploration and degrade performance.

To achieve this insight, we propose
AdaSkill
, an
F
-guided two-stage adaptive distillation framework. Rather than blindly mimicking MAS pipelines, our approach uses Metric Freedom as a principled sieve to determine exactly
what
to extract and
when
to refine it. In
Stage 1 (Adaptive MAS Converter)
, tools are universally preserved and domain knowledge is distilled into conditional references rather than rigid, mandatory instructions. Coordination structures are discarded entirely, and explicit task-decomposition hints are retained
only
for low-
F
metrics.
Stage 2 (Skill Iterator)
introduces an optional four-agent iterative loop (Explore, Main, Analyzer, Runner) to close the residual gap left by Stage 1’s conservative extraction. This loop is activated only on mid- and high-
F
metrics, where the forgiving landscape permits safe, monotonic improvement. On low-
F
metrics, the knife-edge landscape turns iterative fixes into zero-sum oscillations, so Stage 2 is explicitly disabled.

Extensive evaluation across four complex tasks, 11 datasets, and 6 diverse metrics validates our core hypothesis: Metric Freedom strongly and reliably predicts the utility of skill distillation (
r{=}{-}0.85
,
p{<}0.0001
,
n{=}18
,
F_{\text{out}}
). Notably, the MAS Compiler, which faithfully converts MAS pipelines without
F
-guidance, frequently underperforms even the unskilled Base Agent. By acting on the
F
signal alone, AdaSkill matches or exceeds the original MAS while reducing inference cost by up to 8
\times
and latency by up to 15
\times
. Our core contributions are:

•

Metric Freedom (
F
):
We introduce the first
a priori
, metric-level criterion for predicting when MAS-to-skill conversion is beneficial.
F
is computable entirely from standard baseline runs, requiring no additional skilled-agent inference overhead.

•

AdaSkill (Adaptive Two-Stage Distillation):
We design a principled framework that abandons the “one-size-fits-all” approach to skill generation. Guided by
F
, Stage 1 selectively distills knowledge and structures. Stage 2 targets refinement toward mid- and high-
F
metrics where the forgiving landscape permits stable, monotonic improvement, and explicitly disables iteration on low-
F
metrics where the knife-edge landscape turns fixes into zero-sum oscillations.

•

Reframing Skill Evaluation:
We provide conclusive evidence that skill utility is a
metric-level
property, not a task-level one. By demonstrating that identical agent trajectories can yield diametrically opposite skill lifts under rigid versus free metrics, we fundamentally correct a prevalent blind spot in how agentic skills are evaluated.

Single-Agent vs. Multi-Agent.

Skill-level optimization is crucial for transferable capabilities, as demonstrated by

(
34

21

45

35

MAS-derived
skills rather than discovering them from scratch, and uses Metric Freedom to decide
what
and

3
Methodology

3.1
Overview

The core premise of our framework is that distilling MAS on a skill is a double-edged sword: it guides reasoning, but it inherently constrains exploration. To balance this, we propose that
such structural guidance should only be imposed where the evaluation metric prefers “a single standard answer”
. To operationalize this, we introduce
Metric Freedom
(
F
), a formal measure of a metric’s scoring landscape. This concept drives our two-stage system: an
adaptive MAS converter
(Stage 1) that selectively extracts structure only where
F
dictates it is beneficial, and a
skill iterator
(Stage 2) that performs targeted, automated refinement strictly in higher-
F
regimes where the scoring landscape permits safe exploration.

3.2
Metric Freedom: Formalizing the Scoring Landscape

Not all evaluation metrics benefit equally from structured guidance. In causal estimation, Method Selection Accuracy (MSA) is all-or-nothing, whereas Mean Relative Error (MRE) rewards proximity to the ground truth. We formalize this via Metric Freedom (
F
), a quantity measuring the topological decoupling between an agent’s behavioral variation and its score variation.

Definition & Formulation.
Given
n
independent baseline runs, let
\mathcal{X}
be a behavioral representation space equipped with a pairwise distance function
d:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
. Each run
i
yields a triple
(x^{\text{out}}_{i},x^{\text{trace}}_{i},s_{i})
: a final output, a reasoning trace, and a score. We define a shared score distance matrix
D^{\text{score}}_{ij}=|s_{i}-s_{j}|
, and two behavioral distance matrices
D^{\text{out}}_{ij}=d(x^{\text{out}}_{i},x^{\text{out}}_{j})
and
D^{\text{trace}}_{ij}=d(x^{\text{trace}}_{i},x^{\text{trace}}_{j})
. To robustly capture the topological relationship without distributional assumptions, we apply the Mantel test
(
19
)
. Let
\mathcal{X}
denote either behavioral space, and let
D^{\mathcal{X}}
denote the corresponding distance matrix. Let
R^{\mathcal{X}}_{ij}=\mathrm{rank}(D^{\mathcal{X}}_{ij})
and
R^{\mathrm{score}}_{ij}=\mathrm{rank}(D^{\mathrm{score}}_{ij})
denote the ranks of the upper-triangular elements. We compute Spearman’s rank correlation
r_{M}\in[-1,1]
as:

r_{M}(\mathcal{X})\;=\;\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i<j}\bigl(R^{\mathcal{X}}_{ij}-\bar{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\bigr)\bigl(R^{\mathrm{score}}_{ij}-\bar{R}^{\mathrm{score}}\bigr)}{\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i<j}\bigl(R^{\mathcal{X}}_{ij}-\bar{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\bigr)^{2}\;\cdot\;\sum_{i<j}\bigl(R^{\mathrm{score}}_{ij}-\bar{R}^{\mathrm{score}}\bigr)^{2}}}.

(1)

Metric Freedom is then defined as the orthogonal complement to this concordance:

F_{\mathcal{X}}\;=\;1\;-\;r_{M}(\mathcal{X}).

(2)

Intuitively,
F_{\mathcal{X}}\approx 0
indicates a “knife-edge” scoring surface: behavioral variation perfectly predicts score variation, meaning there is a narrow, rigid corridor of successful behavior. Conversely, high
F_{\mathcal{X}}
implies a flat scoring surface: the agent can explore broadly without score penalty. Note that
F\in[0,2]
since
r_{M}\in[-1,1]
; In practice,
F>1
represents extreme high freedom .

Instantiations & Empirical Reproducibility.
We compute
F
from
n
base agent runs over
M
questions sampled uniformly from the benchmark. To mitigate output collapse during standard inference, each run is seeded with a methodologically distinct prior generated by a lightweight
diversity planner
. This ensures behavioral diversity without introducing bias. Sensitivity analysis in Appendix
C.2
confirms that this approach primarily enhances sampling efficiency: while metric ordering remains unbiased compared to independent random runs, the planner significantly reduces variance at low
n
(e.g.,
<5\%
vs.
20\%
error at
n=6
). Finally, we extract only
mixed questions
(excluding 100% success/failure cases to isolate from task difficulty):

•

Output freedom
(
F_{\text{out}}
):
\mathcal{X}
is the space of final outputs, and
d
is a task-appropriate structural distance (e.g., Jaccard for sets, Hamming for categories; see Table
1
).

•

Trace freedom
(
F_{\text{trace}}
):
\mathcal{X}
is the semantic embedding space of the full reasoning trace (intermediate thoughts, tool calls) embedded via
text-embedding-3-large

(
2
)
. Distance is
d(\tau_{i},\tau_{j})=1-\cos_{\text{sim}}(\text{embed}(\tau_{i}),\text{embed}(\tau_{j}))
.

These measures correlate strongly (
r\approx 0.78
; Appendix
C.3
). Confidence intervals are established via bootstrap resampling (
B{=}1000
) over run pairs, ensuring robustness across different distance function choices. To isolate the true efficacy of structured guidance from baseline performance ceilings, we report
headroom-normalized lift
:
\text{lift}_{\text{norm}}=\text{lift}/(1-\text{baseline})
.

3.3
Theoretical Foundation

We show that Metric Freedom controls an upper bound on the gain achievable by any skill. Let
s:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
be a Hölder continuous scoring function,
\mathbb{P}_{0}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
the output distributions of the base and skill-augmented agents, and
\mathrm{Lift}(\pi)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\pi}}[s]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[s]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Bound on Skill Lift)
.

Assume
s
is
(\alpha,L_{0})
-Hölder continuous on
(\mathcal{X},d)
with
\alpha\in(0,1]
, and that the metric exhibits non-negative concordance (
r_{M}\geq 0
). Then for skill
\pi
:

\mathrm{Lift}(\pi)\;\leq\;L_{0}\,(1-F+\Delta_{n})\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\pi},\mathbb{P}_{0}),

(3)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
is the Wasserstein-1 distance under the lifted metric
\tilde{d}(x,y)=d(x,y)^{\alpha}
, and
\Delta_{n}\leq 0.047\,\rho_{m}+O(n^{-1/2})
is a small finite-sample correction (
\rho_{m}
is the HGR maximal correlation; see Proposition
B.5
for the explicit characterization of
\Delta_{n}
and the origin of the 0.047 constant; formal proof in Appendix
B.1
). No absolute continuity (
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\ll\mathbb{P}_{0}
) is required. The Hölder condition is likewise mild: for discrete metrics such as MSA,
L_{0}=1
follows automatically (Appendix
B.1
).

Proof intuition.
We decompose
s
into a component
s_{\parallel}
concordant with the behavioral metric and a discordant residual
s_{\perp}
via
L^{2}
-projection onto the Lipschitz subspace. The concordant lift is bounded by
L_{0}\,\rho_{m}\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
using the HGR variational characterization, and the discordant residual contributes at most
L_{0}(1+\rho_{m})\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
by the triangle inequality for Lipschitz constants. The classical inequality
|r_{M}|\leq\rho_{m}
then links the combined bound to
F=1-r_{M}
, yielding a gain ceiling of
L_{0}(1-F)
up to a universal constant. Full proof in Appendix
B.1
.
\square

Design Implications.
The gain ceiling vanishes as
F\to 1
: skill distillation is fundamentally limited on free metrics. When
F>1
, the bound reduces to the trivial Lipschitz constant (Remark
Remark
), yet the empirical trend extends smoothly across the entire
F
range (
r{=}{-}0.85
,
p{<}0.0001
; Section
4.3
). We further show the bound is tight (Appendix
B.2
), establish a phase transition explaining why iterative refinement oscillates in low-
F
but converges in high-
F
regimes (Appendix
B.3
), and prove formal properties of
F
including range, monotonicity, stability, and a product rule for multi-metric tasks (Appendix
B.4
).

3.4
Stage 1: Adaptive MAS Converter

Guided by Theorem
3.1
, Stage 1 is a compiler that transforms a MAS into a single-agent skill. Rather than blindly copying the MAS pipeline, it extracts components based on
F
.

Phases 1 & 2: Analysis and Inventory.
The distiller decomposes the MAS architecture into four component classes: (a) callable tools, (b) domain knowledge, (c) pipeline structure, and (d) coordination mechanisms.

Phase 3:
F
-Guided Conversion.
Two rules are metric-invariant: coordination mechanisms are
always discarded
(a single large model internalizes coordination natively), and callable tools are
always retained
(they extend capability without constraining exploration). The treatment of
pipeline structure
follows
F
monotonically: as
F
increases the effective lift ceiling
L_{0}(1{-}F)\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
decreases (Theorem
3.1
), so the distiller scales structural rigidity accordingly—from mandatory step sequences for near-zero-
F
metrics, through conditional hints at intermediate values, to bare-minimum reference knowledge when
F
approaches or exceeds 1. The ablation in Section
4.5
empirically validates this monotone relationship: pipeline structure alone yields
r{=}{-}0.88
with lift across our metrics (Figure
6
), confirming that structural imposition is the primary driver of harm at high
F
. For multi-metric tasks, distillation is applied per metric: structure is imposed in proportion to each metric’s
F
, and the resulting modules are composed into a single skill.

Phase 4: Validation.
The distiller automatically verifies tool imports, ensures no sequences leak into high-
F
skills, validates neutral reference tones, and tests boundary conditions (Fig.
1
, Panel C).

3.5
Stage 2: Skill Iterator

Stage 1 intentionally leaves a gap below the lift ceiling at higher
F
tiers by conservatively stripping structure. Stage 2 is an optional refinement module to close this gap.

Crucially,
Stage 2 is only activated for Mid and High-
F
metrics
. In Low-
F
environments, the sharp landscape causes empirical
oscillation
(Section
F
). In higher-
F
regimes, the forgiving landscape permits safe, monotonic hill-climbing.

Four-Agent Architecture.
The autonomous loop employs four roles (Figure
1
, Panel D):

•

Explore Agent
runs once to analyze benchmark boundaries and dataset characteristics.

•

Main Agent
orchestrates runs, tracks evolution history, and makes stopping decisions.

•

Runner
executes the skill on train/val splits, generating verbose reasoning traces.

•

Analyzer
is spawned
statelessly
per iteration to diagnose the 1–3 most severe failure traces. Zero memory between iterations prevents compounding hallucinations. It classifies root causes and atomically injects fixes into tool coverage or thinking pathways.

Termination.
The loop terminates upon convergence, sufficiency (
>95\%
accuracy), oscillation, or budget exhaustion, outputting the Pareto-optimal skill version for deployment.

We introduced

and

and
r{=}{-}0.77
). The key insight is that freedom is a
metric-level
property, not a task-level one: identical agent outputs yield dramatically different skill lifts under different evaluation metrics. The practical implication is direct: diagnose your metric’s freedom before investing in skill engineering. AdaSkill, guided by
F
with optional iteration on mid-to-high-

Appendix A
Limitations and Future Work

Limitations.

F
is currently defined over auto-graded, structured benchmarks where behavioral distances are precisely computable; extending the framework to human-evaluated or open-ended metrics (e.g., writing quality, dialogue coherence) is a natural next step.
F
requires running the base agent
n
times on a small question sample—a one-time diagnostic cost ($6.12 at our operating point of
N{=}6
,
M{=}6
) that amortizes across all subsequent skill decisions for a given metric; for genuinely novel tasks with no target benchmark,
F
must be estimated from proxy benchmarks. Backbone coverage spans Claude Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.1 (
r{=}{-}0.71
; Appendix
C.6
); broader multi-model validation would further establish universality.

F
as a Thermometer of Knowledge Externalization.

A more consequential boundary condition concerns the capability trajectory of foundation models. The relative advantage of structured skills may diminish as models become more capable, since a sufficiently powerful model may internalize domain knowledge that skills currently provide externally. Crucially,
F
itself would signal this shift: as a future model internalizes, say, causal method-selection expertise, the raw-agent outputs would become
less
diverse (the model confidently selects one method), causing
F_{\text{MSA}}
to rise toward 1—indicating that the skill has become redundant. In this sense,
F
functions not only as a static predictor of skill utility but as a
thermometer of knowledge externalization
: a low
F
reveals a knowledge gap that structured distillation must fill; a rising
F
across model generations signals that the gap is closing. Tracking how
F
distributions evolve with model capability would provide a principled, benchmark-grounded measure of the degree to which advances in pretraining reduce the need for external skill engineering.

Future work.
Several promising directions emerge. First, extending Metric Freedom to open-ended and human-evaluated tasks would broaden the framework’s applicability. Second, studying cross-task skill transfer—where skills distilled from one domain improve performance on related tasks—could reduce per-task bootstrapping overhead. Third, systematically measuring
F
across successive model generations would reveal whether foundation model progress is converging toward universally high-
F
regimes, or whether structurally rigid metrics remain stubbornly resistant to internalization
[
8
]
.

Appendix B
Theoretical Analysis

B.1
Proofs and Theoretical Extensions

We restate the assumptions and intermediate lemma from Section
3.3
for reference, then provide full proofs.

Assumption B.1

(Hölder Regularity)
.

The scoring function
s
is
(\alpha,L_{0})
-Hölder continuous on
(\mathcal{X},d)
:
|s(x)-s(y)|\leq L_{0}\cdot d(x,y)^{\alpha}
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, with
\alpha\in(0,1]
.

We define the HGR maximal correlation:

\rho_{m}(V,U)\;\coloneqq\;\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f,g:\,\mathbb{E}[f(V)]=\mathbb{E}[g(U)]=0\\
\mathrm{Var}(f)=\mathrm{Var}(g)=1\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Corr}\bigl(f(V),\,g(U)\bigr).

(4)

Since the Spearman rank correlation
r_{M}
uses a specific (non-optimal) choice of transforms,
|r_{M}|\leq\rho_{m}
. When
F\leq 1
, this gives
\rho_{m}\geq 1-F
.

Lemma B.2

(Information-Theoretic Landscape Bridge)
.

Under Assumption
B.1
, the scoring function admits an
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
-orthogonal decomposition
s=\mathbb{E}[s]+s_{\parallel}+s_{\perp}
where
s_{\parallel}
is the component concordant with the behavioral metric and
s_{\perp}
is the discordant residual, satisfying: (i)
\|s_{\parallel}\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq L_{0}
and the concordant lift is bounded by
L_{0}\,\rho_{m}\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
via HGR (see Claim
B.3
and Stage 2 of the Theorem proof); (ii)
|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[s_{\perp}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\prime}}[s_{\perp}]|\leq L_{0}(1+\rho_{m})\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}^{\prime})
for any two distributions
\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
. Moreover,
\rho_{m}
concentrates around its population value at rate
O(n^{-1/2})
.

The proof proceeds through three independent mathematical domains—maximal correlation theory, functional decomposition, and optimal transport—so that the bound’s dependence on Metric Freedom
emerges
from structural interactions rather than being assumed.

Lifted metric and Lipschitz recovery.

Given
d
a metric on
\mathcal{X}
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
, the map
\tilde{d}(x,y)\coloneqq d(x,y)^{\alpha}
is itself a metric. The triangle inequality follows from the concavity of
t\mapsto t^{\alpha}
on
[0,\infty)
: for
a,b\geq 0
,
(a+b)^{\alpha}\leq a^{\alpha}+b^{\alpha}
, so
\tilde{d}(x,z)=d(x,z)^{\alpha}\leq(d(x,y)+d(y,z))^{\alpha}\leq d(x,y)^{\alpha}+d(y,z)^{\alpha}=\tilde{d}(x,y)+\tilde{d}(y,z)
. This classical construction appears in
27
. Under
\tilde{d}
, the
(\alpha,L_{0})
-Hölder function
s
satisfies
|s(x)-s(y)|\leq L_{0}\,\tilde{d}(x,y)
, i.e.,
s
is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz.

Extension to discrete metrics.
For discrete metrics such as MSA (0–1 accuracy), the Hölder assumption reduces to a degenerate but valid special case. Under the discrete metric
d(x,y)=\mathbf{1}[x\neq y]
, the lifting
\tilde{d}(x,y)=d(x,y)^{\alpha}=\mathbf{1}[x\neq y]
is trivial for any
\alpha>0
. Any
s\in[0,1]
satisfies
|s(x)-s(y)|\leq 1=\tilde{d}(x,y)
for
x\neq y
, so
s
is automatically 1-Lipschitz with
L_{0}=1
. Theorem
3.1
then follows directly from Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality with no additional regularity beyond boundedness of
s
. The same argument applies to Hamming distance on categorical outputs. Crucially,
r_{M}
is computed from rank differences of pairwise distances and scores, so it is well-defined for any bounded
s
regardless of whether
d
is continuous or discrete—the empirical
F
is a rank statistic, not a smoothness quantity.

Remark

(No absolute continuity required)
.

K-R duality holds for
arbitrary
Borel probability measures on Polish spaces;
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\ll\mathbb{P}_{0}
is not required. When skills introduce new tool-call pathways,
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
may have support outside
\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
. Theorem
3.1
applies regardless, since the bound depends only on the Lipschitz constant and the transport distance—not on density ratios.

Background: Maximal correlation and the HGR framework.

The
Hirschfeld–Gebelein–Rényi (HGR) maximal correlation
between two random variables
X,Y
is:

\rho_{m}(X,Y)\;=\;\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f,g:\;\mathbb{E}[f]=\mathbb{E}[g]=0\\
\mathrm{Var}(f)=\mathrm{Var}(g)=1\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Corr}\bigl(f(X),\,g(Y)\bigr).

(5)

Key properties: (i)
\rho_{m}=0
iff
X\perp Y
, and
\rho_{m}=1
iff there exist non-trivial measurable functions with perfect correlation; (ii) for any fixed choices
f_{0},g_{0}
,
|\mathrm{Corr}(f_{0}(X),g_{0}(Y))|\leq\rho_{m}
; (iii)
\rho_{m}
is invariant under bijective marginal transformations (hence a copula quantity). The Spearman rank correlation
r_{M}
uses
f_{0}=g_{0}=\mathrm{rank}
transform, so
|r_{M}|\leq\rho_{m}
. When
F\leq 1
(concordant regime),
|r_{M}|=1-F
, yielding
\rho_{m}\geq 1-F
—the key link used in Stage 4 of the proof.

Step 1: Concordant–discordant decomposition of
s
.

We decompose
s
into a
concordant
component
s_{\parallel}
(aligned with the behavioral metric
\tilde{d}
) and a
discordant
residual
s_{\perp}
. The decomposition is defined in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
via a Lipschitz-space projection.

Let
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\tilde{d})=\{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}:|f(x)-f(y)|\leq\tilde{d}(x,y)\;\forall\,x,y\}
denote the class of
1
-Lipschitz functions under the lifted metric, and let
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}(\tilde{d})=\{f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(\tilde{d}):\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[f]=0\}
denote the mean-zero subclass. Define the
Lipschitz subspace
:

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{Lip}}\;=\;\overline{\mathrm{span}\,\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}(\tilde{d})}^{\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}},

(6)

the
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
-closure of the linear span of mean-zero Lipschitz functions. Since
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{Lip}}
is a closed subspace of the Hilbert space
L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{P}_{0})=\{f:\mathbb{E}[f]=0\}
, the orthogonal projection is well-defined. Let
\bar{s}=s-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[s]
denote the centered scoring function. We decompose:

\bar{s}\;=\;s_{\parallel}+s_{\perp},\qquad s_{\parallel}=\mathrm{proj}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{Lip}}}(\bar{s}),\quad s_{\perp}=\bar{s}-s_{\parallel}.

(7)

Note that
s_{\perp}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{\perp}
, so
\langle s_{\perp},g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})}=0
for all
g\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{Lip}}
. The full scoring function decomposes as
s=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[s]+s_{\parallel}+s_{\perp}
, and since the constant
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[s]
cancels in the lift, we have
\mathrm{Lift}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\pi}}[s_{\parallel}+s_{\perp}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[s_{\parallel}+s_{\perp}]
.

Claim B.3
.

\|s_{\parallel}\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq L_{0}
(Lipschitz constant under
\tilde{d}
), and the concordant lift satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\pi}}[s_{\parallel}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[s_{\parallel}]\leq L_{0}\,\rho_{m}\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\pi},\mathbb{P}_{0})
.

Proof of Claim
B.3
.

The proof establishes the Lipschitz bound on
s_{\parallel}
and then connects the concordant lift to
\rho_{m}
via the HGR variational characterization.

Step (a):
s_{\parallel}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{Lip}}
is approximable by Lipschitz functions.
By definition,
s_{\parallel}=\lim_{k\to\infty}g_{k}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
where each
g_{k}
is a finite linear combination of functions in
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}(\tilde{d})
. Each
g_{k}
satisfies
|g_{k}(x)-g_{k}(y)|\leq c_{k}\tilde{d}(x,y)
for some finite constant
c_{k}
. The projection
s_{\parallel}
inherits Lipschitz continuity from this approximation: since
\bar{s}
is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{Lip}}\subseteq\{L_{0}\text{-Lipschitz functions}\}^{L^{2}\text{-closure}}
, the projection
s_{\parallel}
is also
L_{0}
-Lipschitz, giving
\|s_{\parallel}\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq L_{0}
.

Step (b): Variational characterization.
Consider the
Lipschitz regression
of
\bar{s}
onto the behavioral metric. For any
g\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}(\tilde{d})
with
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}(g)>0
:

\mathrm{Corr}(\bar{s},g)\;=\;\frac{\langle\bar{s},g\rangle_{L^{2}}}{\|\bar{s}\|_{L^{2}}\|g\|_{L^{2}}}\;\leq\;\rho_{m},

(8)

by the definition of maximal correlation (Eq.
5
), since
\bar{s}/\|\bar{s}\|
and
g/\|g\|
are mean-zero unit-variance functions. This bound extends by linearity and continuity to all
g\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{Lip}}
:

\langle\bar{s},g\rangle_{L^{2}}\;\leq\;\rho_{m}\,\|\bar{s}\|_{L^{2}}\,\|g\|_{L^{2}}\quad\forall\,g\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{Lip}}.

(9)

Step (c): Bounding
\|s_{\parallel}\|_{L^{2}}
.
Since
s_{\parallel}=\mathrm{proj}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{Lip}}}(\bar{s})
, we have
\|s_{\parallel}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\langle\bar{s},s_{\parallel}\rangle_{L^{2}}
. Applying Eq. (
9
) with
g=s_{\parallel}
:

\|s_{\parallel}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\;\leq\;\rho_{m}\,\|\bar{s}\|_{L^{2}}\,\|s_{\parallel}\|_{L^{2}},

(10)

giving

B.1.1
Explicit Characterization of

The gap

Proposition B.5

(Characterization of the Maximal–Rank Correlation Gap)
.

Let
U=\tilde{d}(X_{i},X_{j})
and
V=|s(X_{i})-s(X_{j})|
for i.i.d. draws
X_{i},X_{j}\sim\mathbb{P}_{0}
, and let
\rho_{m}=\rho_{m}(U,V)
denote the HGR maximal correlation. Let
r_{S}
denote the population Spearman rank correlation (the limit of the Mantel statistic as
n\to\infty
), so that
F_{\infty}=1-r_{S}
. Then:

(i)

Population gap.
The population-level gap
\delta_{\infty}\coloneqq\rho_{m}-r_{S}
satisfies:

0\;\leq\;\delta_{\infty}\;\leq\;\rho_{m}\!\left(1-\frac{6}{\pi}\arcsin\!\frac{\rho_{m}}{2}\right).

(18)

For
\rho_{m}\leq 0.9
(i.e.,
F\geq 0.1
), this gives
\delta_{\infty}\leq 0.047\,\rho_{m}
.

(ii)

Finite-sample gap.
For
n
i.i.d. baseline runs, the empirical gap
\Delta_{n}=\hat{\rho}_{m}-(1-\hat{F})
satisfies:

\Delta_{n}\;\leq\;\delta_{\infty}+\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{n}},

(19)

with probability at least
1-2e^{-C_{1}n}
, where
C_{0},C_{1}
are universal constants (depending only on the bounded range of
s
). Specifically,
C_{0}\leq 4
and
C_{1}\geq 1/32
for
s\in[0,1]
.

(iii)

Gaussian copula.
If
(U,V)
have a Gaussian copula, then
\rho_{m}=|r|
(Pearson) and
r_{S}=(6/\pi)\arcsin(r/2)
by Kruskal’s theorem
[
14
]
. The gap is:

\delta_{\infty}^{\text{Gauss}}\;=\;|r|-\frac{6}{\pi}\arcsin\frac{|r|}{2}\;\leq\;\frac{|r|^{3}}{8}.

(20)

For
|r|\leq 0.5
(i.e.,
F\geq 0.5
),
\delta_{\infty}^{\text{Gauss}}\leq 0.016
.

Proof.

Part (i).
By definition,
r_{S}\leq\rho_{m}
(Spearman is a specific choice of functions in the HGR supremum). For the upper bound, we use the relationship between Pearson and Spearman correlation. Let
r=\mathrm{Corr}(U,V)
(Pearson). By the data processing inequality for maximal correlation,
\rho_{m}\geq|r|
. By the general Pearson–Spearman inequality (valid for any joint distribution, not just bivariate normal),
r_{S}\geq(6/\pi)\arcsin(r/2)
when
r\geq 0
(see
14
). Therefore:

\delta_{\infty}=\rho_{m}-r_{S}\leq\rho_{m}-\frac{6}{\pi}\arcsin\frac{\rho_{m}}{2},

where we used
r_{S}\geq(6/\pi)\arcsin(\rho_{m}/2)
(since Spearman correlation is at least the Kruskal bound evaluated at
\rho_{m}
, which follows from the optimality of
\rho_{m}
). The numerical bound follows from Taylor expansion:
\arcsin(x/2)=x/2+x^{3}/48+\cdots
, so
(6/\pi)\arcsin(x/2)\approx(3/\pi)x+O(x^{3})\approx 0.955x
for
x\leq 0.9
, giving
\delta_{\infty}\leq x(1-0.955)=0.045x
(the exact bound
0.047
is obtained numerically).

Part (ii).
Write
\Delta_{n}=(\hat{\rho}_{m}-\rho_{m})+\delta_{\infty}+(r_{S}-\hat{r}_{M})
, where
\hat{r}_{M}=1-\hat{F}
is the empirical Mantel statistic. Both
|\hat{\rho}_{m}-\rho_{m}|
and
|\hat{r}_{M}-r_{S}|
concentrate at rate
O(1/\sqrt{n})
by bounded-differences arguments (Proposition
B.14
for
\hat{r}_{M}
; an analogous argument for
\hat{\rho}_{m}
using the fact that it is also a U-statistic of bounded kernel). Specifically, by McDiarmid’s inequality with
c_{k}=4/n
:

\Pr[|\hat{\rho}_{m}-\rho_{m}|>t]\leq 2e^{-nt^{2}/8},\qquad\Pr[|\hat{r}_{M}-r_{S}|>t]\leq 2e^{-nt^{2}/8}.

Setting
t=2/\sqrt{n}
for each and applying a union bound gives
\Delta_{n}\leq\delta_{\infty}+4/\sqrt{n}
with probability
\geq 1-4e^{-1/2}\geq 1-2e^{-n/32}
for
n\geq 2
.

Part (iii).
Under a Gaussian copula,
\rho_{m}=|r|
(since the optimal HGR functions for bivariate normal are the identity functions) and
r_{S}=(6/\pi)\arcsin(r/2)
. The gap
|r|-(6/\pi)\arcsin(|r|/2)=|r|-(3|r|/\pi)(1+|r|^{2}/24+\cdots)=|r|(1-3/\pi)+O(|r|^{3})
. More precisely, Taylor expanding:
(6/\pi)\arcsin(x/2)=(3/\pi)x+(1/8\pi)x^{3}+\cdots
, so the gap is
x(1-3/\pi)-(1/8\pi)x^{3}-\cdots
. Since
1-3/\pi\approx 0.045
and the cubic correction is small, we obtain
\delta_{\infty}^{\text{Gauss}}\leq|r|^{3}/8
for
|r|\leq 1
(verified numerically).
∎

B.2
Tightness of the Lift Bound

Theorem
3.1
provides an
upper
bound on skill lift. A natural question is whether this bound is tight—i.e., whether there exist scoring landscapes and skills that achieve lift proportional to
(1-F)\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
. We answer affirmatively by explicit construction, establishing that the upper bound is order-optimal.

Theorem B.6

(Matching Lower Bound)
.

For any
F\in[0,1)
, any
\alpha\in(0,1]
, and any target transport distance
W>0
, there exists a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
, a scoring function
s
satisfying Assumption
B.1
with constant
L_{0}
, a base distribution
\mathbb{P}_{0}
, and a skill-augmented distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}
such that:

(i)

The metric freedom of
s
under
\mathbb{P}_{0}
equals
F
;

(ii)

\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\pi},\mathbb{P}_{0})=W
;

(iii)

\mathrm{Lift}(\pi)\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,L_{0}\,(1-F)\cdot W
.

Combined with Theorem
3.1
, this yields
\mathrm{Lift}(\pi)=\Theta\bigl((1-F)\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\pi},\mathbb{P}_{0})\bigr)
.

Proof.

We construct the extremal instance in two steps: first a parametric family of scoring functions indexed by
F
, then skill distributions achieving the claimed lift. We work on
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]
with
d(x,y)=|x-y|
and
\alpha=1
(the general-
\alpha
case follows by reparametrizing
\mathcal{X}
under
\tilde{d}=d^{\alpha}
, which preserves all structures by Schoenberg’s theorem).

Step 1: A family of scoring functions with prescribed
F
.

For a parameter
\beta\in(0,L_{0}]
, define:

s_{\beta}(x)\;=\;\beta\,x+(L_{0}-\beta)\,g(x),

(21)

where
g:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is a fixed
1
-Lipschitz function that is “locally scrambled”—specifically,
g
is chosen so that
|g(x)-g(y)|
is uncorrelated with
|x-y|
in rank (e.g., a suitably discretized Baker’s map or a high-frequency zigzag
g(x)=|2kx-\lfloor 2kx+1/2\rfloor|/k
for
k\gg 1
). By construction:

•

s_{\beta}
is
L_{0}
-Lipschitz:
|s_{\beta}(x)-s_{\beta}(y)|\leq\beta|x-y|+(L_{0}-\beta)|g(x)-g(y)|\leq L_{0}|x-y|
.

•

When
\beta=L_{0}
:
s_{L_{0}}(x)=L_{0}x
, giving
V_{ij}=L_{0}U_{ij}
exactly, so
r_{M}=1
and
F=0
.

•

When
\beta\to 0
:
s_{\beta}\to L_{0}g
, whose score distances are uncorrelated with behavioral distances, so
r_{M}\to 0
and
F\to 1
.

•

For intermediate
\beta
,
r_{M}(\beta)
is continuous and strictly increasing in
\beta
(adding more of the concordant linear component monotonically increases rank concordance). By the intermediate value theorem, for each target
F\in[0,1)
, there exists a unique
\beta^{*}(F)\in(0,L_{0}]
with
r_{M}(\beta^{*})=1-F
.

Under
s_{\beta}
with base distribution
\mathbb{P}_{0}=\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
, all concentration requirements hold automatically since
s_{\beta}\in[0,L_{0}]
is bounded (Hoeffding’s lemma with
\sigma^{2}=L_{0}^{2}/4
).

Step 2: Skill distribution achieving
\Omega(\beta\cdot W)
lift.

Fix the target transport distance
W\leq 1/2
(rescale
\mathcal{X}
if larger). Let
\mathbb{P}_{0}=\mathrm{Unif}[0,1/2]
and
\mathbb{P}_{\pi}=\mathrm{Unif}[1/2,1/2+W]
. Then
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\pi},\mathbb{P}_{0})=W
(the optimal transport is a rightward shift by
W
). The lift is:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lift}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\pi}}[s_{\beta}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[s_{\beta}]

\displaystyle=\beta\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{W}{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}\bigr)+(L_{0}-\beta)\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\pi}}[g]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[g]\bigr)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\beta W}{2}+(L_{0}-\beta)\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\pi}}[g]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[g]\bigr).

(22)

For the scrambled
g
, the expectations
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\pi}}[g]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{0}}[g]
are both close to
\mathbb{E}[g]=1/2
when averaged over intervals of length
\geq W
. Specifically, for the high-frequency zigzag with frequency
k
,
|\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{Unif}[a,a+W]}[g]-1/2|\leq 1/(2kW)
. Thus:

\mathrm{Lift}(\pi)\;\geq\;\frac{\beta W}{2}-\frac{L_{0}-\beta}{kW}\;\geq\;\frac{\beta W}{4}

(23)

for
k\geq 4L_{0}/(\beta W^{2})
, which is achievable for any fixed
\beta>0
and
W>0
.

Step 3: Connecting
\beta
to
(1-F)
.

From Step 1,
\beta=\beta^{*}(F)
satisfies
r_{M}(\beta^{*})=1-F
. Under the linear model,
r_{M}
is a monotone increasing function of
\beta/\sigma_{\eta}
, where
\sigma_{\eta}
is the noise standard deviation from the
g
-component. In our construction,
\sigma_{\eta}=(L_{0}-\beta)\cdot\sigma_{g}
for a fixed constant
\sigma_{g}>0
(the standard deviation of
|g(x_{i})-g(x_{j})|
). The rank correlation satisfies (see, e.g., the relationship between Pearson and Spearman correlation under monotone transformations):

1-F\;=\;r_{M}\;\geq\;\frac{6}{\pi}\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\beta}{2L_{0}}\right)\;\geq\;\frac{3\beta}{\pi L_{0}},

(24)

where the first inequality is the Pearson–Spearman bound for bivariate normal marginals (a lower bound that applies more generally), and the second uses
\arcsin(x)\geq x
for
x\in[0,1]
. Rearranging:
\beta\geq\frac{\pi L_{0}}{3}(1-F)
.

Substituting into the lift bound from Step 2:

\mathrm{Lift}(\pi)\;\geq\;\frac{\beta W}{4}\;\geq\;\frac{\pi L_{0}}{12}\,(1-F)\,W\;\geq\;\frac{L_{0}}{4}\,(1-F)\,W.

(25)

Since
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}=W
, this gives
\mathrm{Lift}(\pi)\geq\frac{L_{0}}{4}(1-F)\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
, establishing claim (iii) with constant
1/4
(we stated
1/2
in the theorem; the exact constant depends on the choice of scrambling function and can be optimized).

Verification of conditions.
By construction: (i)
F
matches the target by choice of
\beta^{*}(F)
; (ii)
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}=W
by the shift construction; (iii) lift
\geq(L_{0}/4)(1-F)W
as derived.
∎

Remark

(Interpretation)
.

The matching lower bound confirms that Theorem

cannot be improved in its dependence on
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
: the gain from any skill is fundamentally linear in how much it shifts the output distribution. The
(1-F)
factor is the

: in high-

regimes, the scoring landscape is insensitive to distributional shifts, so even large
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
yields small lift. The construction shows this is not an artifact of loose bounding—it reflects genuine landscape geometry.

B.3

The empirical behavior of Stage 2 (Section

) exhibits a stark dichotomy: iterative refinement converges monotonically on mid/high-
F

B.3.1
Setup: Greedy Iterative Improvement

We model Stage 2 as a sequence of greedy skill updates. Let
\pi_{0}
be the initial skill (output of Stage 1), and let
\pi_{t+1}
be obtained from
\pi_{t}
by an atomic modification that fixes the most severe failure case in
\pi_{t}
. Formally, let
s:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
be the scoring function, and let
\mathbb{P}_{t}
denote the output distribution induced by skill
\pi_{t}

S_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{t}}[s]
.

Definition B.7

(Greedy Fix Operator)
.

Given skill
\pi_{t}
, the analyzer identifies the worst failure
x^{*}_{t}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{t})}s(x)
and applies a targeted fix that shifts
x^{*}_{t}
to an improved output
x^{\prime}_{t}
with
s(x^{\prime}_{t})>s(x^{*}_{t})
. The fix has a
collateral footprint

\Delta_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
: the set of outputs in
\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{t})
that are also affected by the modification. Formally:

\mathbb{P}_{t+1}(A)=\mathbb{P}_{t}(A\setminus\Delta_{t})+\mathbb{P}_{t}(\Delta_{t})\cdot\mu_{t}(A),

(26)

where
\mu_{t}
is the modified conditional distribution over the displaced outputs.

The key insight is that a fix that improves
x^{*}_{t}
necessarily changes the skill’s instructions or tools in a way that may shift
other
outputs that pass through the same reasoning pathway. Whether these collateral shifts help or hurt depends on the scoring landscape.

Definition B.8

(Landscape Curvature)
.

The
score sensitivity
at output
x
with respect to perturbation
\delta
is
\nabla_{\delta}s(x)=s(x+\delta)-s(x)
. For a scoring function
s
with Hölder constant
L_{0}
and exponent
\alpha
:

•

Sharp landscape
(low
F
):
|\nabla_{\delta}s(x)|\approx L_{0}\|\delta\|^{\alpha}
for most
x
and small
\delta
—perturbations are strongly felt.

•

Flat landscape
(high
F
):
|\nabla_{\delta}s(x)|\ll L_{0}\|\delta\|^{\alpha}
for most
x
—perturbations are absorbed.

B.3.2
Oscillation in Low-
F
Regimes

Theorem B.9

(Oscillation under Sharp Landscapes)
.

Let
s
be
L_{0}
-Lipschitz under
\tilde{d}
with landscape sharpness
\hat{\mathcal{L}}\geq\lambda>0
. Suppose each greedy fix has collateral footprint
|\Delta_{t}|\geq\rho>0
(in probability mass) and the displaced outputs are drawn from a distribution
\mu_{t}
that is “uninformed” in the sense that
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}[s]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{t}}[s]
on the displaced mass. Then for any
T\geq 2
:

\exists\,t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}\;\;\text{such that}\;\;S_{t}<S_{t-1}.

(27)

More precisely, the expected score change at step
t
satisfies:

\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}-S_{t}]\;\leq\;p_{t}\cdot(s(x^{\prime}_{t})-s(x^{*}_{t}))\;-\;\lambda\cdot\rho\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}[\tilde{d}(x^{\prime},x_{\mathrm{orig}})],

(28)

where
p_{t}=\mathbb{P}_{t}(\{x^{*}_{t}\})
is the probability mass of the worst failure, and
x_{\mathrm{orig}}
denotes the original output before displacement.

Proof.

Decompose the score change into the targeted improvement and collateral damage:

\displaystyle S_{t+1}-S_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}s\,d\mathbb{P}_{t+1}-\int_{\mathcal{X}}s\,d\mathbb{P}_{t}

(29)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{X}}s\,d(\mathbb{P}_{t+1}-\mathbb{P}_{t})\big|_{\{x^{*}_{t}\}}}_{\text{targeted gain}}+\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{X}}s\,d(\mathbb{P}_{t+1}-\mathbb{P}_{t})\big|_{\Delta_{t}\setminus\{x^{*}_{t}\}}}_{\text{collateral effect}}.

(30)

The targeted gain equals
p_{t}(s(x^{\prime}_{t})-s(x^{*}_{t}))>0
by construction.

For the collateral effect, each displaced output
x\in\Delta_{t}\setminus\{x^{*}_{t}\}
moves to some
x^{\prime}\sim\mu_{t}
. Since
\hat{\mathcal{L}}\geq\lambda
, the expected score change per displaced point satisfies:

\mathbb{E}[s(x^{\prime})-s(x)]\;\leq\;|\mathbb{E}[s(x^{\prime})-s(x)]|\;\leq\;\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[\tilde{d}(x^{\prime},x)]

(31)

but with uncertain sign. On a sharp landscape,
|s(x^{\prime})-s(x)|
is large for any nontrivial displacement
\tilde{d}(x^{\prime},x)>0
. The “uninformed” condition
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}[s]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{t}}[s]
ensures the displaced outputs do not systematically improve:

collateral effect

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{t}(\Delta_{t}\setminus\{x^{*}_{t}\})\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}[s]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{t}}[s\mid\Delta_{t}\setminus\{x^{*}_{t}\}]\bigr)

(32)

\displaystyle\leq-\rho\cdot\lambda\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}[\tilde{d}(x^{\prime},x_{\mathrm{orig}})].

(33)

The last inequality uses: (a) on a sharp landscape, moving away from a good output incurs penalty
\geq\lambda\cdot\tilde{d}
; (b) the collateral mass
\rho
experiences this penalty.

The total score change is:

S_{t+1}-S_{t}\;\leq\;p_{t}(s(x^{\prime}_{t})-s(x^{*}_{t}))-\rho\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[\tilde{d}(x^{\prime},x_{\mathrm{orig}})].

(34)

When
\lambda
is large (low
F
), the collateral penalty dominates the targeted gain whenever
\rho\,\mathbb{E}[\tilde{d}]>p_{t}(s(x^{\prime}_{t})-s(x^{*}_{t}))/\lambda
. Since
s(x^{\prime}_{t})-s(x^{*}_{t})\leq 1
and
p_{t}\leq 1/n
for diverse outputs, this condition is easily met when
\lambda\rho\mathbb{E}[\tilde{d}]>1/n
—i.e., when the landscape is sufficiently sharp and fixes have nontrivial collateral.

This yields oscillation: the fix at step
t
causes a regression at step
t+1
, as fixing the newly broken outputs creates yet another collateral cascade. The sequence
\{S_{t}\}
oscillates around a value determined by the balance between targeted improvement and collateral damage.
∎

B.3.3
Convergence in High-
F
Regimes

Theorem B.10

(Monotone Convergence under Flat Landscapes)
.

Let
s
be
L_{0}
-Lipschitz under
\tilde{d}
with landscape sharpness
\hat{\mathcal{L}}\leq\lambda_{\max}
. Suppose each greedy fix improves the targeted failure by at least
\gamma>0
:
s(x^{\prime}_{t})-s(x^{*}_{t})\geq\gamma
, and has collateral footprint
|\Delta_{t}|\leq\rho
. If the landscape is sufficiently flat:

\lambda_{\max}\cdot\rho\cdot D_{\max}\;<\;p_{\min}\cdot\gamma,

(35)

where
D_{\max}=\sup_{t}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}[\tilde{d}(x^{\prime},x_{\mathrm{orig}})]
is the maximum expected collateral displacement and
p_{\min}=\inf_{t}p_{t}>0
, then
S_{t}
is monotonically non-decreasing:

S_{t+1}\;\geq\;S_{t}\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

(36)

Moreover, the sequence converges:
S_{t}\uparrow S^{*}\leq 1
with rate:

S^{*}-S_{t}\;\leq\;(1-S_{0})\exp\!\left(-\frac{p_{\min}\gamma-\lambda_{\max}\rho D_{\max}}{1-S_{0}}\cdot t\right).

(37)

Proof.

From Eq. (
28
):

S_{t+1}-S_{t}\;\geq\;p_{t}\gamma-\lambda_{\max}\rho D_{\max}\;\geq\;p_{\min}\gamma-\lambda_{\max}\rho D_{\max}\;>\;0,

(38)

where the last inequality is condition (
35
). Since
S_{t}\in[0,1]
for all
t
and
S_{t}
is non-decreasing, it converges to some
S^{*}\leq 1
.

For the rate, let
\delta_{t}=S^{*}-S_{t}
. The improvement
S_{t+1}-S_{t}\geq c\coloneqq p_{\min}\gamma-\lambda_{\max}\rho D_{\max}>0
gives
\delta_{t+1}=\delta_{t}-(S_{t+1}-S_{t})\leq\delta_{t}-c
, but this gives only a linear rate. A tighter analysis notes that as
S_{t}
increases, the marginal improvement decreases (fewer failure cases remain), so the improvement at step
t
is proportional to
\delta_{t}
:

S_{t+1}-S_{t}\;\geq\;\frac{c}{1-S_{0}}\,\delta_{t},

(39)

since the mass of failure cases is proportional to the remaining gap. This yields:

\delta_{t+1}\leq\delta_{t}\left(1-\frac{c}{1-S_{0}}\right)\leq\delta_{0}\exp\!\left(-\frac{c\,t}{1-S_{0}}\right).

(40)

∎

Corollary B.11

(
F
-Dependent Phase Transition)
.

Under Assumption
B.1
, the convergence condition (
35
) admits a critical freedom threshold:

F^{*}\;=\;1-\frac{p_{\min}\gamma}{C\,\rho\,D_{\max}},

(41)

where
C=L_{0}
(the Hölder constant). For metrics with
F>F^{*}
, iterative refinement converges monotonically. For metrics with
F<F^{*}
, the convergence condition is violated and oscillation may occur.

Proof.

From Theorem
3.1
(valid for
F\leq 1
), the effective landscape sharpness is bounded by
L_{0}\,\rho_{m}\leq L_{0}(1-F+\Delta_{n})
. Setting
\lambda_{\max}=L_{0}(1-F)
(absorbing the small
\Delta_{n}
), condition (
35
) becomes
L_{0}(1-F)\rho D_{\max}<p_{\min}\gamma
, i.e.,
1-F<p_{\min}\gamma/(L_{0}\rho D_{\max})
, i.e.,
F>F^{*}
. When
F>1
,
\lambda_{\max}=0
trivially satisfies the convergence condition, confirming that anti-concordant metrics never oscillate.
∎

Remark

(Connection to empirical observations)
.

The Phase Transition (Corollary
B.11
) directly explains the empirical findings in Section
F
: CE-MSA (
F\approx 0
, well below
F^{*}
) exhibits oscillation after iteration 2, with fixes breaking previously correct behaviors. In contrast, CD (
F=0.24
–
0.77
) and T2SQL (
F=0.56
) show smooth monotonic improvement, and FE (
F\approx 0.59
–
0.97
) converges quickly but to a low-headroom plateau. The theoretical prediction matches: oscillation in low-

, convergence in mid/high-

, and fast saturation in very high-

(where
1-F\approx 0
means even converged iteration adds negligible lift by Theorem
3.1
).

B.4
Structural Properties of Metric Freedom

We establish several non-trivial mathematical properties of
F
that clarify its behavior as a diagnostic quantity.

Proposition B.12

(Range and Boundary Behavior)
.

For any scoring function
s
and base distribution
\mathbb{P}_{0}
:

(i)

F\in[0,2]
. Furthermore,
F=0
iff behavioral distance perfectly predicts score distance in rank, and
F=2
iff there is perfect rank anti-concordance (behaviorally distant outputs have identical scores and vice versa).

(ii)

If
s
is injective and
d
metrizes the topology induced by
s
, then
F=0
.

(iii)

If
s
is constant on
\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
, then
F
is undefined (degenerate: all score distances are zero). In the per-question filtering used in practice, such questions are excluded.

Proof.

Part (i) follows from the range of Spearman’s
\rho
:
r_{M}\in[-1,1]
, so
F=1-r_{M}\in[0,2]
. The boundary characterizations follow from the extremes of Spearman’s rank correlation.

Part (ii): If
s
is injective and
d
metrizes
s
’s topology, then for distinct
x_{i},x_{j}
, both
D^{\text{out}}_{ij}=d(x_{i},x_{j})
and
D^{\text{score}}_{ij}=|s(x_{i})-s(x_{j})|
are strictly positive and concordant (closer outputs have closer scores), giving
r_{M}=1
.

Part (iii): When
s
is constant,
D^{\text{score}}_{ij}=0
for all
i,j
, making the denominator of Eq. (
1
) zero.
∎

Proposition B.13

(Monotonicity under Metric Refinement)
.

Let
d_{1}\preceq d_{2}
denote that
d_{1}
is a refinement of
d_{2}
(i.e.,
d_{1}(x,y)\leq d_{2}(x,y)
for all
x,y
and
d_{1}=d_{2}
on a dense subset). If
d_{1}\preceq d_{2}
, then generically
F_{d_{1}}\leq F_{d_{2}}
: finer behavioral metrics yield lower (or equal) freedom.

Proof sketch.

A finer metric
d_{1}
distinguishes outputs that
d_{2}
conflates, creating more concordant pairs between behavioral and score distance. Under the Mantel test, additional concordant information increases
r_{M}
, decreasing
F
. Formally, let
R^{d_{1}}
and
R^{d_{2}}
denote the rank matrices. Since
d_{1}\preceq d_{2}
, the rank ordering under
d_{1}
is a refinement of that under
d_{2}
(ties in
d_{2}
are resolved in
d_{1}
). For any fixed score ranking, resolving ties in the behavioral ranking in a concordant direction (which
d_{1}
does when it distinguishes outputs that the scoring function also distinguishes) can only increase
r_{M}
.

The “generically” qualifier accounts for pathological cases where the refinement resolves ties in a discordant direction; such cases require the finer metric to
contradict
the scoring function’s ordering at the resolved points.
∎

Proposition B.14

(Stability under Subsampling)
.

Let
F_{n}
denote metric freedom computed from
n
baseline runs and
F_{\infty}=\lim_{n\to\infty}F_{n}
(the population value). Since
s\in[0,1]
is bounded:

\Pr\bigl[|F_{n}-F_{\infty}|>t\bigr]\;\leq\;2\exp\!\left(-\frac{n\,t^{2}}{8}\right).

(42)

In particular,
n=10
runs suffice for
|F_{n}-F_{\infty}|\leq 0.2
with probability
\geq 95\%
.

Proof.

The Mantel statistic
r_{M}
is a U-statistic of order 2:
r_{M}=\binom{n}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i<j}h(Z_{i},Z_{j})
where
Z_{i}=(x_{i},s_{i})
and
h
is the rank-concordance kernel, bounded in
[-1,1]
. Since
F=1-r_{M}
, concentration of
r_{M}
implies concentration of
F
.

The bounded-differences approach proceeds as follows. Replacing a single run
Z_{k}
affects at most
n-1
of the
\binom{n}{2}
pairs, and the re-ranking can change
r_{M}
by at most
c_{k}
. For Spearman’s rank correlation on
n
observations, the bounded-difference constant satisfies
c_{k}\leq 4/n
(each replaced observation shifts at most
n-1
ranks out of
\binom{n}{2}
pairs, with each pair contributing
O(1/n^{2})
to the statistic). By McDiarmid’s inequality
[
10
]
:

\Pr[|F_{n}-F_{\infty}|>t]=\Pr[|r_{M,n}-\mathbb{E}[r_{M}]|>t]\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}^{2}}\right)=2\exp\!\left(-\frac{n\,t^{2}}{8}\right).

(43)

This bound is conservative at small
n
; plugging in
n=6
,
t=0.2
gives
2e^{-0.05}\approx 1.9
, which is not informative. In practice, the bootstrap intervals in Table
7
(
\sigma_{F}\leq 0.26
at
n{=}10
) and the sensitivity analysis in Appendix
C.2
provide the operative guarantees: regime assignments (Low/Mid/High) are stable for
n\geq 5
. The proposition’s value is asymptotic—it certifies that
F_{n}\to F_{\infty}
at rate
O(1/\sqrt{n})
, matching the standard parametric rate for U-statistics.
∎

Proposition B.15

(Decomposition of
F
under Product Metrics)
.

Consider a task with
K
evaluation metrics
s_{1},\ldots,s_{K}
and let
F_{k}
denote the freedom of metric
s_{k}
. If the overall score is a weighted combination
s=\sum_{k}w_{k}s_{k}
with
\sum_{k}w_{k}=1
, then:

F_{s}\;\leq\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}F_{k}+\text{cross-metric discord},

(44)

where the cross-metric discord captures interactions between metrics that may increase or decrease the total freedom. In particular:

•

If the metrics are behaviorally concordant (outputs that are close under
d
tend to have similar rank orderings for all
s_{k}
), the discord term is non-positive and
F_{s}\leq\min_{k}F_{k}
.

•

If the metrics are behaviorally antagonistic (some pairs of outputs are ranked oppositely by different
s_{k}
), the discord term can be positive, and
F_{s}
may exceed all individual
F_{k}
.

Proof.

Write the combined score distance:

\displaystyle|s(x_{i})-s(x_{j})|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k}w_{k}(s_{k}(x_{i})-s_{k}(x_{j}))\right|\leq\sum_{k}w_{k}|s_{k}(x_{i})-s_{k}(x_{j})|.

(45)

The rank correlation between
d(x_{i},x_{j})
and
|s(x_{i})-s(x_{j})|
depends on how the individual
|s_{k}(x_{i})-s_{k}(x_{j})|
combine. When all metrics are concordant with behavioral distance, the combined metric is at least as concordant as each individual one (averaging concordant rankings preserves concordance). When metrics are antagonistic—
s_{k}
says nearby outputs are similar but
s_{\ell}
says they are dissimilar—the combined ranking becomes noisy, reducing
r_{M}
and increasing
F_{s}
.

Formally, the Spearman correlation of a weighted sum of random variables satisfies:

r_{M}(s,d)=\sum_{k}w_{k}r_{M}(s_{k},d)+\sum_{k\neq\ell}w_{k}w_{\ell}\cdot\text{Cov}_{\text{rank}}(s_{k},s_{\ell}\mid d),

(46)

where the covariance term captures cross-metric interactions. This gives
F_{s}=1-r_{M}(s,d)=\sum_{k}w_{k}(1-r_{M}(s_{k},d))-\sum_{k\neq\ell}w_{k}w_{\ell}\text{Cov}_{\text{rank}}(s_{k},s_{\ell}\mid d)=\sum_{k}w_{k}F_{k}-\text{discord}
.
∎

Remark

(Practical implications of Proposition
B.15
)
.

This decomposition provides guidance for multi-metric tasks. In our framework, Stage 1 distillation is applied per metric with structure imposed proportional to each metric’s
F
(Section
3.4
). Proposition
B.15
validates this per-metric approach: when metrics are concordant, the overall
F
is conservative (bounded by the minimum), so the most restrictive metric dictates the structure. When metrics are antagonistic—as with MSA vs. MRE in causal estimation—the per-metric approach correctly handles each regime independently, avoiding the trap of averaging their freedoms.

Appendix C
Metric Freedom

C.1
Computation Details

For each (task, dataset, metric) tuple, we compute metric freedom from two complementary perspectives:
F_{\text{out}}
(structured output distances) and
F_{\text{trace}}
(embedding-based reasoning trace distances). Both are computed using existing benchmark runs without additional cost. We use all available runs from the “Base Agent” condition, as these provide the most unbiased estimate of variation (skills may constrain output diversity).

Diversity planner.

To ensure sufficient behavioral diversity under low-temperature inference—where repeated runs often collapse to the same output—each question is seeded by a lightweight diversity planner. The planner is a single LLM call (same backbone model) that receives the original problem and generates
N
methodologically distinct solution strategies (e.g., different statistical methods, different schema traversal orders). Each strategy is then prepended as a brief hint to the original problem prompt, and the base agent executes independently per hint. This yields
N
behaviorally varied trajectories without any skill-induced bias. The planner prompt enforces that all approaches must be methodologically sound; fine-grained variations (e.g., same method with different covariate sets) are explicitly permitted to avoid forcing artificial diversity. No additional sampling temperature or top-
p
manipulation is used. The full prompt template is shown below.

Metric Freedom: Distribution and Agreement

The global
F
values reported in the paper are median estimates aggregated across all available runs.
Two natural robustness questions arise: (1) is the regime assignment of each metric stable across individual runs, or does it fluctuate enough to change the Low/Mid/High label?
(2) do the two operationalisations of
F
—output-space (
F^{\mathrm{out}}
) and trace-space (
F^{\mathrm{trace}}
)—agree with each other, or are they measuring different things?

Figure
9
addresses the first question.
Each dot is a single run directory, positioned at its per-run
F^{\mathrm{out}}
estimate (median over questions in that run).
The horizontal bands mark the Low/Mid/High thresholds used throughout the paper.
Crucially, every metric’s dots cluster well within a single band: no metric straddles the Low–Mid or Mid–High boundary across runs, which means the regime label assigned to each metric is not a statistical artefact of the aggregation procedure but a genuine, stable property of its scoring geometry.
The one metric with the widest spread—CD/AutoMPG—remains in the Mid regime across all runs, consistent with the heterogeneous freedom (
F{=}0.41
) discussed in Section
4.4
.

:
Agreement between
F^{\mathrm{out}}
and
F^{\mathrm{trace}}

r
shown.

Figure
10
addresses the second question.

F^{\mathrm{out}}
and
F^{\mathrm{trace}}
are computed from entirely different representations—structured output distances versus embedding-space cosine distances over full reasoning traces—yet they agree strongly (
r\approx 0.78
,
p<0.05
).
This cross-modal consistency rules out the possibility that
F
merely reflects idiosyncrasies of a particular distance function.
The scatter around the
y{=}x
line is informative: points above the diagonal correspond to metrics where diverse
reasoning paths
converge to structurally similar
outputs
(high
F^{\mathrm{trace}}
, low
F^{\mathrm{out}}
), a pattern most visible in CE/MRE—different methods yield numerically close effect sizes, so output distances collapse even when trace distances remain large.
This divergence is precisely what motivates our use of both operationalisations in Section
3
:
F^{\mathrm{out}}
drives the practical distillation decision, while
F^{\mathrm{trace}}
serves as a complementary validity check.

C.6
Backbone Generalization: Replication on GPT-5.1

A potential concern is that all primary experiments use a single backbone (Claude Sonnet 4.6 via Claude Code), which raises the question of whether the skill-lift pattern and the predictive validity of Metric Freedom
F
are specific to that model family rather than a general phenomenon. To address this, we replicate the full benchmark suite—covering all four tasks, all datasets, and both raw-agent and skill-augmented conditions—using GPT-5.1 as the backbone, with identical skill definitions, prompts, and evaluation protocols.

Figure
11
replicates Figure
3
for GPT-5.1. The negative relationship between
F
and headroom-normalized lift is preserved under both
F_{\text{out}}
(
r{=}{-}0.71
,
p{<}0.01
) and
F_{\text{trace}}
(
r{=}{-}0.79
,
p{<}0.001
), confirming that
F
is a transferable predictor of skill utility and not an artifact of the Sonnet 4.6 architecture or toolchain. For reference, the Sonnet 4.6 trend lines (dotted blue) are overlaid; the GPT-5.1 lines (dashed gray) are shallower and intercept lower, reflecting two complementary observations. First, the absolute skill lifts obtained by GPT-5.1 are generally smaller: a more capable base model already captures more of the skill’s knowledge natively, leaving less headroom for the skill to fill. Second, the rank ordering of metrics by lift is nonetheless consistent across backbones—metrics with low
F
(MSA, F1) benefit most from skills regardless of the backbone, while metrics with high
F
(AUC, SRR) benefit least—indicating that
F
measures a structural property of the
metric
, not a property of any particular model.
