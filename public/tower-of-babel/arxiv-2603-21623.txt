Neyman-Pearson multiclass classification under label noise via empirical likelihood Thanks: Correspondence to: Pengfei Li pengfei.li@uwaterloo.ca .
Qiong Zhang

Abstract
In many classification problems, the costs of misclassifying observations from different classes can be highly unequal. The Neyman–Pearson multiclass classification (NPMC) framework addresses this issue by minimizing a weighted misclassification risk while imposing upper bounds on class-specific error probabilities. Existing NPMC methods typically assume that training labels are correctly observed. In practice, however, labels are often corrupted due to measurement error or annotation, and the effect of such label noise on NPMC procedures remains largely unexplored. We study the NPMC problem when only noisy labels are available in the training data. We propose an empirical likelihood (EL)–based method that relates the distributions of noisy and true labels through an exponential tilting density ratio model. The resulting maximum EL estimators recover the class proportions and posterior probabilities of the clean labels required for error control. We establish consistency, asymptotic normality, and optimal convergence rates for these estimators. Under mild conditions, the resulting classifier satisfies NP oracle inequalities with respect to the true labels asymptotically. An expectation–maximization algorithm computes the maximum EL estimators. Simulations show that the proposed method performs comparably to the oracle classifier under clean labels and substantially improves over procedures that ignore label noise.



Keywords:
Corrupted labels, Data contamination, Density ratio model, Empirical likelihood, Supervised learning

Introduction

In many real-world machine learning applications, especially in high-stakes domains such as medical diagnosis and financial fraud detection, the consequences of different classification errors are highly asymmetric.
For instance, misclassifying a malignant tumor as benign can be far more consequential than the opposite error
(
Bokhari et al. 2021
)
, and approving a fraudulent loan carries greater risk than rejecting a legitimate one.
Traditional classifiers, which typically minimize the overall misclassification rate and treat all errors equally, do not account for such asymmetries.

The Neyman–Pearson multiclass classification (NPMC) framework addresses this limitation by explicitly constraining error rates for selected classes, thereby prioritizing safety and reliability over simple accuracy.
Specifically, let
(X,Y)
be a pair of random variables where
X\in{\mathcal{X}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
denotes the feature vector and
Y\in[K]=\{0,1,\dots,K-1\}
is the class label.
For each
k
, let
P_{k}^{*}(A):={\mathbb{P}}(X\in A|Y=k)
for any Borel set
A\subset{\mathcal{X}}
, and denote
X^{k}\triangleq X|(Y=k)\sim P_{k}^{*}
.
The NPMC problem
1
1

1

The NPMC problem reduces to classical overall misclassification error minimization when
\rho_{k}=w_{k}^{*}={\mathbb{P}}(Y=k)
for all
k
and
{\mathcal{S}}=\varnothing
.
seeks a classifier
\phi:{\mathcal{X}}\to[K]
that solves:

\begin{split}\min_{\phi}&\quad{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\rho_{k}P_{k}^{*}(\{\phi(X)\neq k\})\\
\text{subject to}&\quad P_{k}^{*}(\{\phi(X)\neq k\})\leq\alpha_{k},~\forall~k\in{\mathcal{S}}\subset[K],\end{split}

(1)

where
\rho_{k}
,
\alpha_{k}
, and
{\mathcal{S}}
are pre-specified weights, target error levels, and class indices with constrained errors, respectively.

When
K=2
, problem (
1
) reduces to the classical Neyman–Pearson binary classification task: minimizing the type II error while controlling the type I error.
This binary setting has been extensively studied; see, for example,
Scott and Nowak 2005
;
Scott 2007
;
Rigollet and Tong 2011
;
Tong 2013
;
Zhao et al. 2016
;
Tong et al. 2018
;
Tong et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Xia et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2024
.
In contrast, the general multiclass case (
K\geq 3
) remains relatively underexplored. Extending binary NP classification to the multiclass setting is non-trivial, in part because problem (
1
) may be infeasible for certain specifications of
\rho_{k}
and
\alpha_{k}
.
Recently,
Tian and Feng 2025
proposed a dual-based algorithm that checks asymptotic feasibility and provides an efficient solver when strong duality holds.
Their estimator further satisfies an NP oracle inequality, which compares the learned classifier
\widehat{\phi}
to the oracle classifier
\phi^{*}
that satisfies the error constraints:

P_{k}^{*}(\{\widehat{\phi}(X)\neq k\})\leq P_{k}^{*}(\{\phi^{*}(X)\neq k\})+O_{p}(\varepsilon(n)),~\forall~k\in{\mathcal{S}}

(2)

where
\varepsilon(n)
vanishes as the sample size
n
increases.

Despite its appeal, the NPMC framework fundamentally relies on clean, correctly labeled training data.
This assumption is often violated in practice due to measurement error, annotator inconsistency, or adversarial noise.
Empirical studies suggest that roughly 5% of labels in real-world datasets are incorrect
(
Yao et al. 2023
)
.
Label noise is present even in standard benchmarks: for instance,
Northcutt et al. 2021
find mislabeling in datasets ranging from MNIST to ImageNet.
In domain-specific datasets such as CheXpert
(
Irvin et al. 2019
)
, ambiguities in radiology reports can also result in erroneous labels.
More recently, in applications involving large language models, stochastic variability in model outputs has been shown to induce label noise relative to an underlying latent ground truth, further illustrating the pervasiveness of noisy labels in modern machine learning systems
(
Zhang and Martinez 2025
)
.

It is therefore natural to ask: What are the consequences of label noise for the NPMC problem?
In the binary case,
Yao et al. 2023
showed that
ignoring label noise can lead to overly conservative type I error and thus an inflated type II error
.
Their approach, however, requires prior knowledge of the noise transition (confusion) matrix—the conditional probability of a noisy label given the true label—which is rarely available in practice.
Moreover, extending such results to the multiclass setting is also non-trivial.
More broadly,
it remains unclear how label noise impacts the general NPMC framework and how it can be properly accounted for
, a gap that this paper aims to fill.

Concretely, suppose we observe
n
independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples
\{(X_{i},Y_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim P^{*}_{X,Y,\widetilde{Y}}
, where
Y_{i}
denotes the unknown true label and
\widetilde{Y}_{i}
is a noisy version of
Y_{i}
.
In practice, we only observe the
noisy training set

{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{train}}=\{(X_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, along with
m
additional i.i.d. feature-only samples
{\mathcal{D}}^{\mathrm{test}}={X_{i}}{i=n+1}^{n+m}
from the same distribution.

Our goal is to use only the noisy
{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{train}}
to learn a classifier
\widehat{\phi}
such that its predictions on
{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{test}}
satisfy the constraints in (
1
) with respect to the (unobserved) true labels
\{Y_{i}\}_{i=n+1}^{n+m}
.

We propose a novel empirical likelihood (EL) framework for solving the NPMC problem under label noise, without requiring prior knowledge of the noise transition matrix.
Our method captures the relationship between the true and noisy label distributions through an exponential tilting density ratio model (DRM).
By estimating the DRM parameters, we recover the key quantities–true class proportions and posterior probabilities of the clean labels–needed for error control under clean labels, which allows us to directly apply the clean-label error control procedure of
Tian and Feng 2025
to the noisy-label setting.
Computationally, we treat the true labels as latent variables and develop an efficient expectation–maximization algorithm to maximize the profile empirical likelihood and consistently estimate these quantities.

The proposed method has several key advantages.
First, it eliminates the need for any prior knowledge or bounds on the noise parameters, enhancing practical feasibility.
Second, the resulting maximum empirical likelihood estimators are statistically consistent and asymptotically normal with optimal convergence rates.
Third, under mild assumptions, we provide rigorous theoretical guarantees showing that the final classifier satisfies NP oracle inequalities.
Fourth, simulation studies demonstrate that our method achieves near-oracle performance—closely matching the error control of classifiers trained on clean labels—while significantly outperforming naive approaches that ignore label noise.
Finally, our framework naturally accommodates both binary and multiclass classification, thereby filling a notable gap in the existing literature.

2
Problem setting

To enable valid statistical inference, we must formalize the relationship between latent
Y
and observed
\widetilde{Y}
.
Following established work
(
Angluin and Laird 1988
;
Yao et al. 2023
;
Sesia et al. 2024
;
Bortolotti et al. 2025
)
, we adopt the standard instance independence assumption:

Assumption 2.1

(Instance-independent noise)
.

\widetilde{Y}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp X\mid Y
(i.e., the noisy label
\widetilde{Y}
is conditionally independent of features
X
given the true label
Y
).

This assumption captures the common scenario as shown in
Sesia et al. 2024
where label noise depends primarily on class membership rather than specific feature values, as frequently observed in real-world settings like crowdsourcing
(
Snow et al. 2008
)
.
Beyond its practical relevance, this assumption provides crucial tractability by establishing a clear relationship between the contaminated distribution
\widetilde{P}_{k}^{*}
of
\widetilde{X}^{k}
and the true conditional distribution
P_{k}^{*}
of
X^{k}
, as formalized in the proposition below.

Proposition 2.1

(Distributional relationships under label noise)
.

Let
\widetilde{w}_{l}^{*}={\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}=l)
and
w_{k}^{*}={\mathbb{P}}(Y=k)
denote the noisy and true class proportions, respectively.
Let
\pi_{k}^{*}(x)={\mathbb{P}}(Y=k|X=x)
and
\widetilde{\pi}_{l}^{*}(x)={\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}=l|X=x)
.
Define:

•

{\bm{M}}^{*}
: The confusion matrix with entries
M_{lk}^{*}={\mathbb{P}}(Y=k|\widetilde{Y}=l)
,

•

{\bm{T}}^{*}
: The noise transition matrix with entries
T_{lk}^{*}={\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}=l|Y=k)=\widetilde{w}_{l}^{*}M_{lk}^{*}/w_{k}^{*}
.

Then, under Assumption
2.1
, the following relationships hold:

\text{(Marginal)}\quad w_{k}^{*}=\sum_{l\in[K]}M_{lk}^{*}\widetilde{w}_{l}^{*},\quad\widetilde{w}_{l}^{*}=\sum_{k\in[K]}T_{lk}^{*}w_{k}^{*},

(3)

\text{(Conditional)}\quad\widetilde{P}_{l}^{*}=\sum_{k\in[K]}M_{lk}^{*}P_{k}^{*},\quad P_{k}^{*}=\sum_{l\in[K]}T_{lk}^{*}\widetilde{P}_{l}^{*},

(4)

\text{(Posterior)}\quad\widetilde{\pi}_{l}^{*}(x)=\sum_{k\in[K]}T_{lk}^{*}\pi_{k}^{*}(x).\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad

(5)

The proof is deferred to Appendix
\thechapter.C.1
.

Remark 2.1
.

The proposition demonstrates a fundamental duality: the pair
({\bm{M}}^{*},\widetilde{{\bm{w}}}^{*})
contains equivalent information to
({\bm{T}}^{*},{\bm{w}}^{*})
. This means we can focus our estimation efforts on either parameter group, as the other can be derived accordingly.
We consider
({\bm{T}}^{*},{\bm{w}}^{*})
since
{\bm{w}}^{*}
is used directly in the NPMC numerical solver as we show in Section
5.1
.

Based on these relationships, a naive two-step approach to estimating
\pi_{k}^{*}(x)
via (
5
) would require prior knowledge of the transition matrix
{\bm{T}}^{*}
, which is typically unavailable in practice.
Instead, we propose a joint estimation procedure that simultaneously learns
{\bm{T}}^{*}
and
\pi_{k}^{*}(x)
by directly leveraging these distributional relationships, which we detail in the next section.

3
Empirical likelihood based estimation under label noise

Since we observe contaminated labels
\widetilde{Y}\in[K]
rather than the true labels
Y
in the noisy label setting.
Conventional approaches that ignore this label noise yield estimators for
\{\widetilde{w}_{k}^{*},\widetilde{\pi}_{k}^{*}(x)\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
, which differ from the target quantities
\{w_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{*}(x)\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
needed for proper NP type error control.
To address this, we propose a flexible approach that directly estimates
\{w_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{*}(x)\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
from noisy labeled data.
Our approach uses density ratio modeling and empirical likelihood,
without requiring any prior knowledge
of the label corruption probabilities.

This section presents our estimator along with its numerical computation and statistical properties for general classification problems with label noise.

3.1
Exponential tilting and density ratio model

Consider
(X,Y,\widetilde{Y})
where
(X,Y)\sim P^{*}_{X,Y}
and
\widetilde{Y}
satisfies Assumption
2.1
.
Let
g(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be some representation of feature
x
such that:

\pi_{k}^{*}(x):={\mathbb{P}}(Y=k|X=x)=\frac{\exp((\gamma_{k}^{*})^{\dagger}+\langle\beta_{k}^{*},g(x)\rangle)}{\sum_{k^{\prime}\in[K]}\exp((\gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{*})^{\dagger}+\langle\beta_{k^{\prime}}^{*},g(x)\rangle)}.

(6)

with
(\gamma_{0}^{*})^{\dagger}=0
and
\beta_{0}^{*}=0
for identifiability.
This leads to the exponential tilting relationship between the class-conditional distributions:

\frac{dP_{k}^{*}}{dP_{0}^{*}}(x)=\exp(\gamma_{k}^{*}+\langle\beta_{k}^{*},g(x)\rangle),

(7)

where
dP_{k}^{*}/dP_{0}^{*}
denotes the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
P_{k}^{*}
with respect to
P_{0}^{*}
,
\gamma_{k}^{*}=(\gamma_{k}^{*})^{\dagger}+\log(w_{0}^{*}/w_{k}^{*})
.
With this reparameterization, we also have
\gamma_{0}^{*}=0
.

To ensure
P_{k}^{*}
s are valid probability measures, the parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{*}=\{\gamma_{k}^{*}\}_{k=1}^{K-1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{*}=\{\beta_{k}^{*}\}_{k=1}^{K-1}
must satisfy the constraints:

\int\exp(\gamma_{k}^{*}+\langle\beta_{k}^{*},g(x)\rangle)P_{0}^{*}(dx)=1,\quad\forall~k\in 1,\ldots,K-1.

(8)

Under the instance-independent noise assumption (Assumption
2.1
), the contaminated distributions of
\widetilde{X}^{l}\triangleq X|(\widetilde{Y}=l)\sim\widetilde{P}_{l}^{*}
relate to the true distributions as follows:

\frac{d\widetilde{P}_{l}^{*}}{dP_{0}^{*}}(x)=\sum_{k\in[K]}M_{lk}^{*}\frac{dP_{k}^{*}}{dP_{0}^{*}}(x)=\frac{1}{\widetilde{w}_{l}^{*}}\sum_{k\in[K]}\left\{w_{k}^{*}T_{lk}^{*}\exp(\gamma_{k}^{*}+\langle\beta_{k}^{*},g(x)\rangle)\right\}.

(9)

Both (
7
) and (
9
) are instances of the density ratio model (DRM)
(
Anderson 1979
;
Qin 2017
)
, where all measures (
\{\widetilde{P}_{l}^{*}\}_{l=0}^{K-1}
,
\{P_{k}^{*}\}_{k=1}^{K-1}
) are connected to the base measure
P_{0}^{*}
via a parametric form.
The DRM is highly flexible and encompasses many commonly used distribution families, including all members of the exponential family with appropriate specifications for
P_{0}^{*}
and
g(x)
.
For example, if the base distribution is normal and
g(x)=(x,x^{2})^{\top}
, the DRM reduces to the normal distribution family.

Remark 3.1

(Basis function selection)
.

The function
g
is commonly referred to as the basis function.
In our simulations and experiments, the simple choice
g(x)=x
performs well across a range of scenarios.
For more complex data, such as images, performance may be improved by using features extracted from pretrained deep neural networks or by adopting data-adaptive approaches, such as that of
Zhang and Chen 2022
.

3.2
Identifiability

In this section, we study the identifiability of the model in (
9
).
We emphasize that, in the absence of additional structural assumptions, the model is fundamentally unidentifiable—beyond the usual label permutation ambiguity encountered in mixture models.
In particular, multiple distinct parameterizations can induce exactly the same observed distributions.
We illustrate this issue through two examples below.

Example 3.1

(Algebraic unidentifiability of true distributions)
.

Consider a binary classification problem (
K=2
) where we observe noisy distributions
\widetilde{P}_{0}(x)
and
\widetilde{P}_{1}(x)
. Let the true underlying distributions be
P_{0}^{*}(x)
and
P_{1}^{*}(x)
, and let the true noise transition matrix be
{\bm{M}}^{*}
, such that:

\widetilde{P}_{0}(x)=(1-\rho_{0}^{*})P_{0}^{*}(x)+\rho_{1}^{*}P_{1}^{*}(x),~\widetilde{P}_{1}(x)=\rho_{0}^{*}P_{0}^{*}(x)+(1-\rho_{1}^{*})P_{1}^{*}(x),

where
\rho_{0}^{*}={\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}=1|Y=0)
and
\rho_{1}^{*}={\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}=0|Y=1)
.

If
P_{0}^{*}(x)
and
P_{1}^{*}(x)
are unconstrained, the model is unidentifiable. For any small constant
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, we can define a new, "contaminated" candidate distribution for class 1:

P_{1}^{**}(x)\triangleq(1-\epsilon)P_{1}^{*}(x)+\epsilon P_{0}^{*}(x).

Because
P_{1}^{**}(x)
is a convex combination of two valid probability density functions, it is also a valid density function. We can rewrite the true
P_{1}^{*}(x)
in terms of this new distribution:

P_{1}^{*}(x)=\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}P_{1}^{**}(x)-\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}P_{0}^{*}(x).

Substituting this back into the equation for the observed
\widetilde{P}_{0}(x)
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=(1-\rho_{0}^{*})P_{0}^{*}(x)+\rho_{1}^{*}\left[\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}P_{1}^{**}(x)-\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}P_{0}^{*}(x)\right],

\displaystyle=\left(1-\rho_{0}^{*}-\frac{\epsilon\rho_{1}^{*}}{1-\epsilon}\right)P_{0}^{*}(x)+\left(\frac{\rho_{1}^{*}}{1-\epsilon}\right)P_{1}^{**}(x).

Let
\rho_{1}^{**}=\frac{\rho_{1}^{*}}{1-\epsilon}
and
(1-\rho_{0}^{**})=1-\rho_{0}^{*}-\frac{\epsilon\rho_{1}^{*}}{1-\epsilon}
. As long as
\epsilon
is chosen to be sufficiently small such that
\rho_{1}^{**}<1
and
(1-\rho_{0}^{**})>0
, the new parameters
\{\rho_{0}^{**},\rho_{1}^{**},P_{0}^{*}(x),P_{1}^{**}(x)\}
are perfectly valid and satisfy the mixture equations to produce the exact same observed distributions
\widetilde{P}_{0}(x)
and
\widetilde{P}_{1}(x)
. Therefore, the true noise rates and class-conditional distributions are non-identifiable without further structural assumptions.

Example 3.2

(Unidentifiability due to linearly dependent features)
.

Recall our density ratio model formulation from (
7
):

\frac{dP_{1}^{*}}{dP_{0}^{*}}(x)=\exp(\gamma_{1}^{*}+\langle\beta_{1}^{*},g(x)\rangle).

Suppose the chosen feature representation
g(x)=(g_{1}(x),g_{2}(x))^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is not linearly independent across the support of
P_{0}^{*}
. Specifically, assume the data strictly lies on a manifold where
g_{1}(x)+g_{2}(x)=c
for some constant
c
.

Let
(\gamma_{1}^{*},\beta_{11}^{*},\beta_{12}^{*})
be the true parameters generating the data. The exponential tilt is:

\gamma_{1}^{*}+\beta_{11}^{*}g_{1}(x)+\beta_{12}^{*}g_{2}(x).

Now, consider a different set of parameters defined by shifting the feature coefficients by an arbitrary constant
\delta\neq 0
:

\beta_{11}^{**}=\beta_{11}^{*}+\delta,\quad\beta_{12}^{**}=\beta_{12}^{*}+\delta.

Evaluating the tilt with these new parameters yields:

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}^{*}+\beta_{11}^{**}g_{1}(x)+\beta_{12}^{**}g_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}^{*}+(\beta_{11}^{*}+\delta)g_{1}(x)+(\beta_{12}^{*}+\delta)g_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}^{*}+\beta_{11}^{*}g_{1}(x)+\beta_{12}^{*}g_{2}(x)+\delta(g_{1}(x)+g_{2}(x))

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{1}^{*}+\delta c)+\beta_{11}^{*}g_{1}(x)+\beta_{12}^{*}g_{2}(x).

By defining a new baseline parameter
\gamma_{1}^{**}=\gamma_{1}^{*}+\delta c
, we perfectly absorb the shift. The parameter sets
(\gamma_{1}^{*},\beta_{11}^{*},\beta_{12}^{*})
and
(\gamma_{1}^{**},\beta_{11}^{**},\beta_{12}^{**})
produce the exact same density ratio
\exp(\gamma_{1}^{**}+\langle\beta_{1}^{**},g(x)\rangle)\equiv\exp(\gamma_{1}^{*}+\langle\beta_{1}^{*},g(x)\rangle)
for all observable
x
. Thus, without assumptions guaranteeing the richness of the feature subspace (i.e., ruling out collinearity), the exponential tilt parameters are non-identifiable.

These examples highlight that the model is intrinsically unidentifiable without further structural constraints.
To resolve this issue, we impose a set of mild but essential assumptions that ensure identifiability, as stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1

(Identifiability)
.

Under mild assumptions (Assumptions
\thechapter.C.1
–
\thechapter.C.3
), the parameters
({\bm{M}}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{*})
and the reference distribution
P_{0}^{*}
in (
9
) are strictly identifiable.

Strict identifiability implies a true one-to-one mapping between the parameter space and the probability distributions of the observed data.
For our model, this means there is exactly one unique set of parameters
({\bm{M}}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{*})
and one unique reference distribution
P_{0}^{*}
that can generate the observed data, completely eliminating the label-switching (permutation) ambiguity that typically plagues mixture models.
To establish this strict identifiability, we require three standard regularity conditions.
Broadly, these conditions ensure that the latent mixture components are physically distinct and that the observed data provides sufficient variation to decouple them.
Specifically, we require the feature coefficients of the exponential tilts to be strictly distinct (Assumption
\thechapter.C.1
) and the feature mapping to explore a sufficiently rich subspace (Assumption
\thechapter.C.2
).
These are common requirements in the exponential family and finite mixture model literature to prevent component collapse and guarantee that the underlying densities are linearly independent.
Furthermore, we require the confusion matrix
{\bm{M}}^{*}
to be full rank, with specific structural constraints on its inverse (Assumption
\thechapter.C.3
).
Crucially, this final condition on the inverse matrix not only ensures the signal from the true latent components is preserved, but it effectively breaks the permutation symmetry inherent in standard mixture models, allowing for the strict, unique identification of all parameters.
The formal mathematical statements of these assumptions, along with the complete proof, are deferred to Appendix
\thechapter.C.4
.

3.3
Empirical likelihood based inference

To avoid restrictive parametric assumptions on the base distribution, we adopt a non-parametric approach on the estimation of
\pi_{k}^{*}(x)
and the rest of the parameters
{\bm{T}}^{*}
and
{\bm{w}}^{*}
, given a set of i.i.d. samples
\{(X_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
from
P^{*}_{X,\widetilde{Y}}
.

The log-likelihood function based on the contaminated set
{\mathcal{D}}=\{(X_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P^{*}_{X,\widetilde{Y}}
is

\ell=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l=0}^{K-1}\left[\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\log\left\{\widetilde{w}_{l}^{*}\widetilde{P}_{l}^{*}(\{X_{i}\})\right\}\right].

Using the exponential tilting relationship in (
9
), we obtain

\ell=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\log\left[\sum_{k\in[K]}\left\{w_{k}^{*}T_{lk}^{*}\exp(\gamma_{k}^{*}+\langle\beta_{k}^{*},g(X_{i})\rangle)\right\}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log P_{0}^{*}(\{X_{i}\}).

The empirical likelihood (EL)
(
Owen 2001
;
Qin and Lawless 1994
)
allows us to estimate parameters without assumptions on the form of
P_{0}^{*}
.
Specifically, let
p_{i}=P_{0}^{*}(\{X_{i}\})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and view them as parameters.
Let
{\bm{w}}=\{w_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}=\{{\bm{w}},\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},{\bm{T}}\}
.
The log-EL function becomes:

\ell({\bm{p}},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\log\left[\sum_{k\in[K]}\left\{w_{k}T_{lk}\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))\right\}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{i}.

(10)

The feasible values of
{\bm{p}}
must satisfy the following constraints:

p_{i}\geq 0,~\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=1,~\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))=1,~\forall~k=1,\ldots,K-1.

(11)

The first two conditions in (
11
) ensure that
P_{0}^{*}
is a valid probability measure, while the last follows from (
8
).

The inference for
\theta
is usually made by first profiling the log-EL with respect to
{\bm{p}}
.
That is, we define
p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\sup_{{\bm{p}}}\ell_{n}({\bm{p}},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
subject to the constraints in (
11
).
By the Lagrange multiplier method (see Appendix
\thechapter.C.2
for details), the maximizer is attained when

p_{i}=p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=n^{-1}\left[1+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\nu_{k}\left\{\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))-1\right\}\right]^{-1}

(12)

where the Lagrange multipliers
\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{K-1}
are the solution to

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))-1}{1+\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K-1}\nu_{k^{\prime}}\left\{\exp(\gamma_{k^{\prime}}+\beta_{k^{\prime}}^{\top}g(X_{i}))-1\right\}}=0,\quad k=1,\ldots,K-1.

(13)

The profile log-EL of
\theta
(after maximizing out
p_{i}
) is given by

p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\log\left[\sum_{k\in[K]}\left\{w_{k}T_{lk}\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))\right\}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}),

and the Maximum Empirical Likelihood Estimator (MELE) of
\theta
is defined as

\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}=\argmax p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}).

(14)

The conditional distribution estimators are then

\widehat{P}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})\exp(\widehat{\gamma}_{k}+\widehat{\beta}_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))\delta_{X_{i}},\quad\widehat{\pi}_{k}(x)=\frac{\exp(\widehat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}+\widehat{\beta}_{l}^{\top}g(x))}{\sum_{k^{\prime}}\exp(\widehat{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}+\widehat{\beta}_{k^{\prime}}^{\top}g(x))},

(15)

where
\widehat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}=\widehat{\gamma}_{k}+\log(\widehat{w}_{0}/\widehat{w}_{k})
.

3.4
Statistical guarantees

Theorem 3.2

(Estimator rate of convergence)
.

Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*}=({\bm{w}}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{*},{\bm{T}}^{*})
be the true values of
\theta
and
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}
be its MELE estimator in (
14
).
We have

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{*})\to N(0,\Sigma)

as
n\to\infty
where
\Sigma>0
is some non-degenerated covariance matrix.

The proof of the theorem is deferred to Appendix
\thechapter.C.3
.
The result establishes that the MELE estimator is consistent and asymptotically normal, achieving the optimal convergence rate.
This asymptotic normality plays a key role in deriving the NP oracle inequalities for the final classifier.

3.5
EM algorithm for profile EL maximization

Since the true labels
(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
are unobserved,
\widetilde{P}_{l}^{*}
become mixtures of
P_{0}^{*},\ldots,P_{K-1}^{*}
.
This complicates the maximization of (
14
) due to non-convexity.
To address the challenge, we treat the true labels as missing values and employ an Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm for its numerical computation.
We summarize the algorithm in Algorithm
1
and explain it below.

Algorithm 1

EM algorithm for classification with noisy labels via empirical likelihood

1:

Noisy labeled data
\{(X_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, feature mapping
g(x)
, number of classes
K
, convergence threshold
\epsilon

2:

Estimates
\widehat{w}_{k}
,
\widehat{\pi}_{k}(x)
for all
k\in[K]

3:

Initialize:
Choose initial parameters
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(0)}=({\bm{w}}^{(0)},\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{(0)},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(0)},{\bm{T}}^{(0)})

4:

Set initial profile log-EL
p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(0)})\leftarrow-\infty

5:

repeat

6:

E-step:
Compute posterior probabilities
\omega_{ik}^{(t)}
for each observation according to (
16
)

7:

M-step:
Update parameters:

8:

Update class proportions
w_{k}^{(t+1)}
via (
17
)

9:

Update transition matrix
{\bm{T}}^{(t+1)}
via (
18
)

10:

Update
(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}^{(t+1)},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(t+1)})
by solving the weighted multinomial logistic regression in (
19
)

11:

Adjust and update to obtain
\gamma_{k}^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\bar{\gamma}_{k}^{(t+1)}-\log(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i0}^{(t)})

12:

Update weights
p_{i}^{(t+1)}
via (
21
) to compute
p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)})

13:

until

p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)})-p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})<\epsilon

14:

Return:

15:

\widehat{w}_{k}\leftarrow w_{k}^{(t+1)}
,
\widehat{\pi}_{k}(x)\leftarrow\frac{\exp(\widehat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}+\widehat{\beta}_{k}^{\top}g(x))}{\sum_{k^{\prime}}\exp(\widehat{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}+\widehat{\beta}_{k^{\prime}}^{\top}g(x))}
where
\widehat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}=\gamma_{k}^{(t+1)}+\log\frac{w_{0}^{(t+1)}}{w_{k}^{(t+1)}}

The EM framework is particularly well-suited for problems with latent variables, as it iteratively handles the missing information via: an
expectation
step that computes conditional probabilities of the missing true labels; a
maximization
step that updates parameter estimates.

If the complete data
\{(X_{i},Y_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
were available, the complete data log-EL would be

\ell_{n}^{c}({\bm{p}},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\sum_{k\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l,Y_{i}=k)\log{\mathbb{P}}(X=X_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l,Y_{i}=k).

Using the chain rule and the instance-independent Assumption
2.1
, we decompose the joint probability into:

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(X=X_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l,Y_{i}=k)&={\mathbb{P}}(X=X_{i}|\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l,Y_{i}=k){\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l|Y_{i}=k){\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i}=k)\\
&={\mathbb{P}}(X=X_{i}|Y_{i}=k)T_{lk}^{*}w_{k}^{*}\\
&=P_{0}^{*}(\{X_{i}\})\exp\{\gamma_{k}^{*}+\langle\beta_{k}^{*},g(X_{i})\rangle\}T_{lk}^{*}w_{k}^{*},\end{split}

where the last equality is from the DRM in (
7
).
This yields the complete data profile log-EL:

\ell_{n}^{c}({\bm{p}},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\sum_{k\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}=k)\left[\log p_{i}+\{\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i})\}+\log T_{lk}+\log w_{k}\right].

We define the profile complete data log-EL as

p\ell_{n}^{c}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\sup_{{\bm{p}}}\ell_{n}^{c}({\bm{p}},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})

where the maximization is subject to the constraints in (
11
).
Using the same derivation for (
12
), the profile complete data log-EL becomes

p\ell_{n}^{c}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\sum_{k\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}=k)\left(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i})+\log T_{lk}+\log w_{k}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})

where
p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
is given in (
12
), and the
\nu_{k}
’s are the solutions to (
13
).

The EM algorithm is an iterative procedure that starts with an initial guess
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(0)}
.
At each iteration
t
, given the current parameter estimate
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}
, the algorithm proceeds in two steps:

•

E-step (Expectation step)
: The missing data is “filled in” by computing an educated guess based on the current parameter estimate
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}
.
This involves calculating the posterior distribution of the missing data given the observed data and the current parameters:

\begin{split}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}=&~{\mathbb{E}}(\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}=k)|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)};\widetilde{Y}_{i},X_{i})={\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i}=k|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)};\widetilde{Y}_{i},X_{i})\\
=&~\frac{\exp(\gamma_{k}^{(t)}+\langle\beta_{k}^{(t)},g(X_{i})\rangle)T_{\widetilde{Y}_{i},k}^{(t)}w_{k}^{(t)}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\exp(\gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{(t)}+\langle\beta_{k^{\prime}}^{(t)},g(X_{i})\rangle)T_{\widetilde{Y}_{i},k^{\prime}}^{(t)}w_{k^{\prime}}^{(t)}}.\end{split}

(16)

Then the expected complete data profile empirical log-EL at iteration
t
is

\begin{split}Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})=&~{\mathbb{E}}\{p\ell_{n}^{(c)}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)};{\mathcal{D}}\}\\
=&~\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}(\log w_{k}+\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i})+\log T_{lk})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\end{split}

•

M-step (Maximization step)
: Instead of maximizing log-EL, the M-step maximizes
Q
with respect to
\theta
instead,
i.e.,

\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)}=\argmax Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}).

Since
Q
is separable in
{\bm{w}}
,
{\bm{T}}
, and
(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})
, we can optimize each set of parameters separately.
Below are the updated parameters; the detailed derivations are provided in Appendix
\thechapter.A.1
.

\displaystyle w_{k}^{(t+1)}=

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}

(17)

\displaystyle T_{lk}^{(t+1)}=

\displaystyle~\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}.

(18)

The update of
(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{(t+1)},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(t+1)})
is slightly more complicated.
Note that
(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{(t+1)},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(t+1)})
maximizes the function

Q_{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{1+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\nu_{k}\left(\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))-1\right)\right\}.

At the optimal point, we show in Appendix
\thechapter.A.1
that the Lagrange multipliers

\nu_{k}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}:=\omega_{\cdot k}^{(t)}.

Thus, the stationary point of
Q_{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})
coincides with the stationary point of

\widetilde{Q}(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{\sum_{k\in[K]}w_{\cdot k}\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))\right\}.

Let
\bar{\gamma}_{k}=\gamma_{k}+\log(\omega_{\cdot k}^{(t)}/\omega_{\cdot 0}^{(t)})
, then the function
\widetilde{Q}
becomes

\widetilde{Q}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}(\bar{\gamma}_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{\sum_{k\in[K]}\exp(\bar{\gamma}_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))\right\},

(19)

which is the weighted log-likelihood of multinomial logistic regression model based on the dataset in Table
4
in Appendix
\thechapter.A.1
.
Thus,
(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}^{(t+1)},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(t+1)})
maximizes the weighted log-likelihood.
We can use any existing packages in
R
or
Python
to find the numerical value.
Finally, we compute

\gamma_{k}^{(t+1)}=\bar{\gamma}_{k}^{(t+1)}-\log(\omega_{\cdot k}^{(t)}/\omega_{\cdot 0}^{(t)}),~k\in[K].

(20)

The weights are

p_{i}^{(t+1)}=p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)})=n^{-1}\left\{\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{\cdot k}^{(t)}\exp(\gamma_{k}^{(t+1)}+\langle\beta_{k}^{(t+1)},g(X_{i})\rangle)\right\}^{-1}

(21)

since
\nu_{k}=\omega_{\cdot k}^{(t)}
at the optimal point.

The E-step and M-step are repeated iteratively until the change in the profile log-EL falls below a predefined threshold.
This stopping criterion is guaranteed to be met because the EM algorithm produces a sequence of estimates that monotonically increases the true objective—the profile log-EL, which we detail below.

Proposition 3.1

(Convergence of EM algorithm)
.

With the EM algorithm described above, we have for
t\geq 1
,
p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)})\geq p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})
.

The proof of Proposition
3.1
is given in Appendix
\thechapter.A.2
.
First note that
p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)})
is a sum of the logarithm of probabilities, hence this implies that
p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\leq 0
for any
\theta
that is feasible.
With this result, Proposition
3.1
guarantees that the EM algorithm eventually converges to at least a local maximum for the given initial value
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(0)}
.
We recommend using multiple initial values to explore the likelihood
function to ensure that the algorithm reaches the global maximum.
Second, in practice, we may stop the
algorithm when the increment in the log-EL after an iteration is no greater than, say,
10^{-6}
.

Remark 3.2

(Incorporating prior knowledge)
.

When prior knowledge about the transition matrix is available (e.g.,
T_{kk}^{*}\geq\xi_{k}>0
), the EM algorithm’s M-step can be modified to incorporate such prior information.
The analytical solution for this constrained update is provided in
\thechapter.A.3
.
To further improve numerical stability and convergence speed, penalties can be imposed on the diagonal elements
\{T_{kk}\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
.
The corresponding analytical solution for this penalized update is detailed in Appendix
\thechapter.A.4
.

4
Neyman-Pearson binary classification

This section focuses on binary classification, a fundamental setting widely used in practice.
We begin by reviewing the NP framework for clean-label binary classification, where the optimal classifier is derived from a likelihood ratio test.
We then incorporate the EL-based estimator from the previous section into this framework to achieve type I error control under noisy labels.
The resulting classifier provably controls the empirical type I error asymptotically.

4.1
Revisiting NP binary classification with clean labels

We begin by reviewing the NP binary classification framework without label noise.
Consider random variables
(X,Y)\sim P^{*}_{X,Y}
, where
w^{*}=\mathbb{P}(Y=1)
,
\pi^{*}(x)=\mathbb{P}(Y=1|X=x)
, and
X^{y}\triangleq X|(Y=y)\sim P_{y}^{*}
.
For a classifier
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
, the NP paradigm solves:

\begin{split}\min_{\phi}&~P_{1}^{*}(\{\phi(X)\neq 1\})\\
\text{s.t.}&~P_{0}^{*}(\{\phi(X)\neq 0\})\leq\alpha,\end{split}

(22)

where
\alpha\in(0,1)
is the desired type I error level, and the objective represents the type II error.
This formulation is a special case of the general NPMC problem in (
1
) with
\rho_{0}=0
,
\rho_{1}=1
,
\mathcal{S}=\{0\}
, and
\alpha_{0}=\alpha
.

When the joint distribution
P_{X,Y}^{*}
is known, the NP lemma
(
Sadinle et al. 2019
, Lemma 1)
establishes that the optimal classifier takes the form:

\phi_{\lambda}^{*}(x)=\mathbbm{1}\left(\frac{dP_{1}^{*}}{dP_{0}^{*}}(x)\geq\lambda\right)=\mathbbm{1}\left(\lambda\leq r(x,\pi^{*},w^{*})\right)

(23)

where
r(x,\pi,w)=\{(1-w)\pi(x)\}/\{w(1-\pi(x))\}
.
The value of
\lambda
controls the trade-off between type I and type II errors: a smaller
\lambda
increases the type I error while reducing the type II error.
Using Bayes’ theorem and the law of total expectation, it can be shown that the optimal
\lambda^{*}
in
\phi^{*}_{\lambda}
is determined by solving:

\alpha=P_{0}^{*}(\{\phi_{\lambda}^{*}(X)\neq 0\})=(1-w^{*})^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\{1-\pi^{*}(X)\}\mathbbm{1}\left(\lambda\leq r(X,\pi^{*},w^{*})\right)\right].

(24)

At the sample level, let
{\mathcal{D}}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P_{X,Y}
.
Given estimators
\widehat{w}
and
\widehat{\pi}(x)
for
w
and
\pi(x)
based on
{\mathcal{D}}
, respectively, we estimate
\lambda^{*}
by solving the empirical estimate of (
24
):

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{1-\widehat{\pi}(X_{i})\}\mathbbm{1}\left(\lambda\leq r(X_{i},\widehat{\pi},\widehat{w})\right)=\alpha(1-\widehat{w}).

(25)

The left-hand side of (
25
) is piecewise constant in
\lambda
, with jumps at
\{r(X_{i},\widehat{\pi},\widehat{w})\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
The estimator
\widehat{\lambda}
corresponds to the smallest jump point where the constraint is satisfied.

Once
\widehat{\lambda}
is obtained, we substitute it, along with the estimators for
\pi(x)
and
w
, into (
23
) to obtain the error-controlled classifier, denoted as
\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}
.

4.2
NP binary classification with noisy labels with statistical guarantees

Since our EL-based estimation allows us to directly estimate
\widehat{w}
and
\widehat{\pi}
from noisy labeled observations, we can simply plug in these estimators, as discussed in the clean label case, to obtain the final classifier with controlled type I error.

Specifically, let
\widehat{w},\widehat{\gamma},\widehat{\beta}
be the MELE estimators defined in (
14
) with
\widehat{\gamma}^{\dagger}=\widehat{\gamma}+\log\{(1-\widehat{w})/\widehat{w}\}
.
We define

\widehat{\pi}(x)=\left[1+\exp(-\{\widehat{\gamma}^{\dagger}+\widehat{\beta}^{\top}g(x)\})\right]^{-1}.

(26)

Let
\widehat{\lambda}
be the solution to (
25
) with the estimators
\widehat{\pi}
and
\widehat{w}
as defined above.
Our final binary classifier with type I error control is given by

\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}(x)=\mathbbm{1}(\widehat{\lambda}<r(x,\widehat{\pi},\widehat{w}))=\mathbbm{1}\left(\widehat{\pi}(x)\geq\frac{\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{w}}{1-\widehat{w}+\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{w}}\right),

(27)

where the complete expression for the threshold is implied by the context.

We now show that the type I error of this estimator is asymptotically controlled at the target level.
The following assumptions are required for our theoretical results:

Assumption 4.1

(Compactness)
.

The parameter space
\Theta=\{(\gamma,\beta):\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}},\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\}
is compact.

Assumption 4.2

(Bounded second moment)
.

There exists a constant
R>0
s.t.
{\mathbb{E}}\|g(X)\|_{2}^{2}\leq R^{2}
.

Theorem 4.1

(Error control guarantee)
.

Let
\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}(x)
be the binary classifier defined in (
27
).
Under Assumptions
4.1
and
4.2
, the type I error is controlled as follows:

P_{0}^{*}\left(\left\{\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}(X)\neq 0\right\}\right)\leq\alpha+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

The proof is deferred to Appendix
\thechapter.C.6
.
This theorem establishes that our classifier asymptotically controls the type I error at the target level
\alpha
under Assumptions
4.1
and
4.2
, which are standard in the literature (e.g.,
Tian and Feng 2025
).
These conditions are realistic in practice: Assumption
4.1
requires only that the parameter space be closed and bounded, which can be enforced by imposing
|\gamma|+\|\beta\|_{2}\leq B
for a sufficiently large
B
, while Assumption
4.2
demands only finite second moments of the feature map
g(X)
.
Under these mild conditions, the Rademacher complexity of the class
\{\pi(x):(\gamma,\beta)\in\Theta\}
is of order
n^{-1/2}
.
Combined with the fact that our EL-based estimators
(\widehat{\gamma},\widehat{\beta},\widehat{w})
converge at the usual
n^{-1/2}
rate, this ensures that the final classifier
\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}
preserves the Neyman–Pearson guarantee up to a vanishing
O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
term.

Remark 4.1

(NP umbrella algorithm type control)
.

The NP umbrella algorithm was developed by
Tong et al. 2018
to control errors in binary classification with uncontaminated data, and later extended to the noisy-label setting by
Yao et al. 2023
.
When domain knowledge of the noise transition matrix
{\bm{M}}^{*}
is available, they estimate the discrepancy between true and corrupted type I errors and propose a label-noise-adjusted NP umbrella algorithm with theoretical guarantees.
Specifically, for any
\delta>0
, they show that their classifier
\phi_{\delta}
satisfies

{\mathbb{P}}(P_{0}^{*}(\{\phi_{\delta}(X)\neq 0\})\leq\alpha)>1-\delta+O(\varepsilon(n)),

(28)

where
\varepsilon(n)
vanishes as
n
increases.
To our knowledge, this is the only work addressing error control under noisy labels.
It is therefore natural to compare our error control guarantee (Theorem
4.1
) with their result.
At first glance, our guarantee appears weaker than that of
Yao et al. 2023
.
In particular, we show that
\forall\epsilon>0
, there exist constants
C
and
N
such that for all
n\geq N
,

{\mathbb{P}}(P_{0}^{*}(\{\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}(X)\neq 0\})\leq\alpha+Cn^{-1/2})>1-\epsilon.

Comparing this with (
28
), the distinction lies in the quantities being controlled.
Our analysis bounds the deviation of the empirical type I error from the target level, which introduces a stochastic fluctuation of order
n^{-1/2}
.
In contrast, the NP umbrella framework provides a stricter guarantee, ensuring with high probability that the population type I error never exceeds the target level.
Importantly, our EL-based estimator is flexible and can be paired with different error control methods: when combined with the NP umbrella algorithm, it yields the same guarantee as in
Yao et al. 2023
.
Moreover, our method does not require prior knowledge of
{\bm{M}}^{*}
.
Further discussion and empirical results are provided in Appendix
\thechapter.B.2.3
.

5
Neyman-Pearson multiclass classification

In this section, we study the more general NPMC problem.
Extending NP binary classification to the multiclass setting is non-trivial for two main reasons as given in
Tian and Feng 2025
: (i) the NPMC problem may not always be feasible, and (ii) solving it requires a more involved dual-form numerical procedure.
To address this, we adopt their NPMC solver and combine it with our EL-based consistent estimators of
\{w_{k}^{*},\pi_{k}^{*}(x)\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
to handle NPMC under noisy labels.
When feasibility holds, we establish that the resulting classifiers control class-specific type I errors up to a term that vanishes as the sample size increases.
Empirically, we show that our method performs on par with the clean-label benchmark when the sample size is sufficiently large.

In this section, we first review their NPMC solver under clean data, then introduce our estimator along with its theoretical guarantees, and finally present the empirical results.

5.1
Revisiting NPMC with clean labels

Tian and Feng 2025
solves (
1
) via its dual formulation.
The Lagrangian associated with (
1
) is

\displaystyle L(\phi,\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})=

\displaystyle~\sum_{k\in[K]}\rho_{k}P_{k}^{*}(\{\phi(X)\neq k\})-\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{S}}}\lambda_{k}\{\alpha_{k}-P_{k}^{*}(\{\phi(X)\neq k\})\}

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{k\in[K]}\rho_{k}+\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{S}}}\lambda_{k}(1-\alpha_{k})-\sum_{k\not\in{\mathcal{S}}}\rho_{k}P_{k}^{*}(\{\phi(X)=k\})-\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{S}}}(\rho_{k}+\lambda_{k})P_{k}^{*}(\{\phi(X)=k\}),

where
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}=\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathcal{S}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{|{\mathcal{S}}|}
are the Lagrange multipliers.
For any fixed
\lambda
, the optimal classifier that minimizes the Lagrangian has the closed form
(
Tian and Feng 2025
, Lemma 2)
:

\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}(x)=\argmax_{k}\{c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},{\bm{w}}^{*})\pi^{*}_{k}(x)\},

(30)

with
c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},{\bm{w}})=\{\rho_{k}+\lambda_{k}\mathbbm{1}(k\in{\mathcal{S}})\}/w_{k}
.
If strong duality holds, the optimal
\lambda
can be obtained by solving the dual problem:

\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}=\argmax_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{\text{Card}({\mathcal{S}})}}G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})

(31)

where the Lagrange dual function admits equivalent expressions:

\displaystyle G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})=

\displaystyle\min_{\phi}L(\phi,\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})=L(\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}},\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left\{c_{\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}(X)}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},{\bm{w}}^{*}){\mathbb{P}}(Y=\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}(X)|X)\right\}+\sum_{k\in[K]}\rho_{k}+\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{S}}}\lambda_{k}(1-\alpha_{k}).

(32)

The key advantage of the dual formulation is that the dual objective
G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})
is concave, regardless of whether the primal problem is convex.
This concavity allows us to first solve for
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}
using a standard convex optimization method, and then substitute
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}
into (
30
) to obtain the optimal classifier
\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}}
.

Given a finite training set
{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{train}}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
Tian and Feng 2025
proposes the
NPMC-CX
(ConveX) estimator for the dual objective
G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})
:

\widehat{G}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{\widehat{\phi}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}(x_{i})}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},\widehat{{\bm{w}}})\widehat{{\mathbb{P}}}(Y=\widehat{\phi}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}(X_{i})|X=X_{i})+\sum_{k\in[K]}\rho_{k}+\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{S}}}\lambda_{k}(1-\alpha_{k}),

(33)

where

\widehat{\phi}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}(x)=\argmax_{k}\{c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},\widehat{\bm{w}})\widehat{\pi}_{k}(x)\},

(34)

\widehat{{\bm{w}}}
and
\{\widehat{\pi}_{k}(x)\}
are estimated using any suitable classifier trained on
{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{train}}
.
Let
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}=\argmax_{\lambda}\widehat{G}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})
; then, plugging
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}
into (
34
) yields the final classifier
\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}}
.

Under assumptions such as the Rademacher complexity of the function class
{\pi_{k}(x):\pi_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}}
vanishing as
n\to\infty
, they show that
\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}}
satisfies the NP oracle inequalities when the primal problem is feasible.
When the primal problem is infeasible, they further show that, with high probability, the dual optimal value is smaller than
1
.

Remark 5.1

(Dual problem, weak and strong duality)
.

For readers less familiar with optimization, we briefly explain the motivation for solving the dual problem and its connection to the primal problem.
The primal problem (
1
) involves constraints on class-specific error for certain classes, which can make direct optimization challenging.
By introducing Lagrange multipliers
\lambda
, we form the
Lagrangian
(
29
), and define the
dual function

G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})
as the minimum of the Lagrangian over all classifiers.
These two optimization problems are connected via the
weak duality
property, which states that for any feasible
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\geq 0
, the dual objective
G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})
provides a lower bound on the primal objective.
This ensures that maximizing the dual gives a value that never exceeds the optimal value of the primal.
If
strong duality
holds, meaning that the maximum of the dual objective equals the minimum of the primal, solving the dual problem yields the optimal multipliers
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}
, which can be substituted into (
30
) to recover the primal optimal classifier.
The key advantage of working with the dual is that
G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})
is concave regardless of the convexity of the primal, making the problem easier to solve with standard convex optimization techniques.
We refer interested readers to
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
for more details on duality.

5.2
NPMC with noisy labels with statistical guarantees

Since our EL-based procedure allows us to directly estimate
\widehat{{\bm{w}}}
and
\widehat{\pi}_{k}
from noisy labeled observations, we can plug in these estimators—just as in the clean-label setting—to construct a classifier with statistical guarantees.

Specifically, let
\widehat{{\bm{w}}}
,
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}
, and
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}
denote the MELE estimators in (
14
) for
{\bm{w}}^{*}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{*}
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{*}
, respectively.
Define

\widehat{\pi}_{k}(x)=\frac{\exp(\widehat{\gamma}_{k}^{\dagger}+\widehat{\beta}_{k}^{\top}g(x))}{\sum_{k^{\prime}}\exp(\widehat{\gamma}_{k^{\prime}}^{\dagger}+\widehat{\beta}_{k^{\prime}}^{\top}g(x))}.

Let
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}
be the minimizer of (
33
) with
\widehat{\pi}_{k}
and
\widehat{{\bm{w}}}
plugged in as above.
Our final
EL-CX
classifier is then defined as

\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}}(x)=\argmax_{k}\{c_{k}(\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}},\widehat{\bm{w}})\widehat{\pi}_{k}(x)\}.

(35)

Remark 5.2

(Numerical maximization of the dual problem)
.

To construct the final EL-CX classifier, in addition to computing
\widehat{\bm{w}}
and
\widehat{\pi}_{k}(x)
via the EM algorithm, one also needs to maximize
\widehat{G}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})
to obtain
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}
.
We perform this maximization using the Hooke–Jeeves algorithm proposed in
Tian and Feng 2025
(implemented in
pymoo

Blank and Deb 2020
).
We adopt the default initialization strategy in
pymoo
to generate the initial search points.
The optimization is constrained to the hypercube
[0,200]^{|{\mathcal{S}}|}
.

We now formally establish the theoretical properties of our EL-CX classifier below.
The following assumptions are required for our theoretical results.

Assumption 5.1

(Compactness)
.

The parameter space
\Theta=\{(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}):\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K-1},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d(K-1)}\}
is compact, and
\min_{k}w_{k}>0
.

Assumption 5.2

(Local strong concavity)
.

Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}
and
G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})
as defined in (
31
) and (
32
) respectively.
We assume that
G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})
is twice continuously differentiable and
\nabla^{2}G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*})\prec 0
.

Theorem 5.1
.

For any classifier
\phi
, denote the
k
th class-specific misclassification probability as
R_{k}(\phi)={\mathbb{P}}(\phi(X)\neq k|Y=k)
.
Let
\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}}
be our EL-CX based classifier in (
35
). When the NPMC problem (
1
) is feasible, under Assumptions
4.2
,
5.1
, and
5.2
, we have that for any
k\in{\mathcal{S}}
:

R_{k}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}})\leq\alpha_{k}+O_{p}(\varepsilon(n)),

where
\varepsilon(n)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

The proof is in Appendix
\thechapter.C.7
.
This theorem establishes that when the primal problem is feasible, our classifier asymptotically controls the class-specific errors of the target classes at the target level asymptotically.
Both Assumptions
5.1
and
5.2
are the same as that in
Tian and Feng 2025
.

Remark 5.3

(NPMC-ER approach)
.

Tian and Feng 2025
also proposes an alternative estimator for
G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})
based on a sample-splitting strategy.
While this estimator relaxes the assumption on the Rademacher complexity of the function class
\{\pi_{k}(x)\}
, it has lower efficiency due to sample splitting.
As shown in the proof of Theorem
5.1
, our model satisfies the Rademacher complexity condition, so we do not adopt this alternative estimator in our work.

5.3
Simulation study

In this section, we conduct empirical experiments to compare our proposed method with other approaches.
Following
Tian and Feng 2025
, we consider three typical settings to generate data:

(a)

Independent covariates
.

Discussion and concluding remarks

This paper addresses a critical challenge in safety-critical machine learning applications: maintaining reliable error control guarantees when the training data contain label noise. In domains like medical diagnosis or autonomous systems, label noise can systematically distort NPMC, leading to violated safety constraints (e.g., elevated false negatives in disease screening) with potentially severe consequences.

By extending the Neyman–Pearson classification framework to accommodate noisy labels, we have developed an empirical likelihood–based approach that preserves class-specific error guarantees without requiring prior knowledge of the noise transition matrix.
Theoretically, our method satisfies NP oracle inequalities under mild conditions. Empirically, it achieves near-optimal performance on both synthetic and real-world datasets, closely approximating the behavior of classifiers trained on clean labels.

Incorporating data-adaptive selection of the basis function
g
as in
Zhang and Chen 2022
, or even integrating it with deep neural networks, remains an important direction for future work.
In addition, extending our framework to handle more complex instance-dependent label noise
(
Xia et al. 2020
)
is another promising yet challenging avenue.

We present the complete derivation of the EM algorithm for the EL based inference under the general multiclass case with noisy labels.

\thechapter
.A.1
EM algorithm implementation

Consider the complete data
\{(X_{i},Y_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
with the same notation as Section
3.5
.
The complete data log empirical likelihood (EL) is:

\ell_{n}^{c}({\bm{p}},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\sum_{k\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l,Y_{i}=k)\log{\mathbb{P}}(X=X_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l,Y_{i}=k).

Using the chain rule and the instance-independent assumption in (
2.1
), we decompose the joint probability into:

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}(X=X_{i},\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l,Y_{i}=k)&={\mathbb{P}}(X=X_{i}|\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l,Y_{i}=k){\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l|Y_{i}=k){\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i}=k)\\
&={\mathbb{P}}(X=X_{i}|Y_{i}=k)T_{lk}^{*}w_{k}^{*}\\
&=P_{0}^{*}(\{X_{i}\})\exp\{\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i})\}T_{lk}^{*}w_{k}^{*},\end{split}

where the last equality follows from the DRM in (
7
).
This yields the complete data profile log-EL:

\ell_{n}^{c}({\bm{p}},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\sum_{k\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}=k)\left[\log p_{i}+\{\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i})\}+\log T_{lk}+\log w_{k}\right].

We define the profile complete data log-EL as

p\ell_{n}^{c}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\sup_{{\bm{p}}}\ell_{n}^{c}({\bm{p}},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})

where the maximization is subject to the constraints in (
11
).
Using the same derivation from Appendix
\thechapter.C.2
, the profile complete data log-EL becomes

p\ell_{n}^{c}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\sum_{k\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}=k)\left(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i})+\log T_{lk}+\log w_{k}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})

where
p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
is given in (
12
), and the
\nu_{k}
’s are the solutions to (
13
).

The EM algorithm is an iterative procedure that starts with an initial guess
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(0)}
.
At each iteration
t
, given the current parameter estimate
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}
, the algorithm proceeds in two steps:

•

E-step (Expectation step)
: The missing data is “filled in” by computing an educated guess based on the current parameter estimate
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}
.
This involves calculating the posterior distribution of the missing data given the observed data and the current parameters.

\begin{split}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}=&~{\mathbb{E}}(\mathbbm{1}(Y_{i}=k)|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)};\widetilde{Y}_{i},X_{i})={\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i}=k|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)};\widetilde{Y}_{i},X_{i})\\
=&~\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i}=k,\widetilde{Y}=\widetilde{Y}_{i},X=X_{i};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})}{\sum_{k^{\prime}}{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i}=k^{\prime},\widetilde{Y}=\widetilde{Y}_{i},X=X_{i};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})}\\
=&~\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(X=X_{i}|Y_{i}=k,\widetilde{Y}=\widetilde{Y}_{i};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}){\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}=\widetilde{Y}_{i}|Y_{i}=k;\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}){\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i}=k;\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})}{\sum_{k^{\prime}}{\mathbb{P}}(X=X_{i}|Y_{i}=k^{\prime},\widetilde{Y}=\widetilde{Y}_{i};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}){\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{Y}=\widetilde{Y}_{i}|Y_{i}=k^{\prime};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}){\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i}=k^{\prime};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})}\\
=&~\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(X=X_{i}|Y_{i}=k;\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})T^{(t)}_{\widetilde{Y}_{i},k}w_{k}^{(t)}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}}{\mathbb{P}}(X=X_{i}|Y_{i}=k^{\prime};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})T^{(t)}_{\widetilde{Y}_{i},k^{\prime}}w_{k^{\prime}}^{(t)}}=\frac{\exp(\gamma_{k}^{(t)}+\langle\beta_{k}^{(t)},g(X_{i})\rangle)T_{\widetilde{Y}_{i},k}^{(t)}w_{k}^{(t)}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\exp(\gamma_{k^{\prime}}^{(t)}+\langle\beta_{k^{\prime}}^{(t)},g(X_{i})\rangle)T_{\widetilde{Y}_{i},k^{\prime}}^{(t)}w_{k^{\prime}}^{(t)}}.\end{split}

(36)

Then the expected complete data profile empirical log-EL at iteration
t
is

\begin{split}Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})=&~{\mathbb{E}}\{p\ell_{n}^{(c)}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})|\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)};{\mathcal{D}}\}\\
=&~\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}(\log w_{k}+\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i})+\log T_{lk})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})\end{split}

•

M-step (Maximization step)
: Instead of maximizing log-EL, the M-step maximizes
Q
with respect to
\theta
instead,
i.e.,

\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)}=\argmax Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$},\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}).

Note that
Q
is separable in
{\bm{w}}
,
{\bm{T}}
, and
(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})
.
Thus, we can optimize each set of parameters separately, as described below.
We now present the details of the M-step with respect to the three sets of variables.

Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})=Q_{1}({\bm{w}};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})+Q_{2}({\bm{T}};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})+Q_{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})

where

\begin{split}Q_{1}({\bm{w}};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})=&~\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}\log w_{k}\\
Q_{2}({\bm{T}};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})=&~\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}\log T_{lk}\\
Q_{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})=&~\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))\\
&-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{1+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\nu_{k}\left(\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))-1\right)\right\}\end{split}

We can therefore optimize each set of parameters separately as follows:

–

Maximize over
{\bm{w}}
.

From the decomposition of
Q
, we have

{\bm{w}}^{(t+1)}=\argmax_{{\bm{w}}}Q_{1}({\bm{w}};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})

subject to the constraint that
w_{k}\in[0,1]
and
\sum_{k\in[K]}w_{k}=1
.
Using the method of Lagrange multipliers, the Lagrangian is

{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{w}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}\log w_{k}-\zeta\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}w_{k}-1\right).

At the optimum, we require

\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{w}}}}{\partial\zeta}=0,\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{w}}}}{\partial w_{k}}=0,~k\in[K].

Solving these
K+1
equations gives

w_{k}^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}.

–

Maximize over
{\bm{T}}
.

From the decomposition of
Q
, we have

{\bm{T}}^{(t+1)}=\argmax_{{\bm{T}}}Q_{2}({\bm{T}};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})

subject to the constraint that
\sum_{l\in[K]}T_{lk}=1
for all
k\in[K]
.
Using the method of Lagrange multipliers, the Lagrangian is

{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{T}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}\log T_{lk}-\sum_{j\in[K]}\zeta_{j}\left(\sum_{l\in[K]}T_{lk}-1\right).

At the optimum, we require

\begin{split}0=&~\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{T}}}}{\partial\zeta_{k}}=\sum_{l\in[K]}T_{lk}-1,~k\in[K]\\
0=&~\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{T}}}}{\partial T_{lk}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}{T_{lk}}-\zeta_{k},~l\in[K],~k\in[K].\end{split}

Solving these
K^{2}+K
equations yields

T_{lk}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}.

–

Maximize over
(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})
.

Note that
(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{(t+1)},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(t+1)})
maximizes the function

Q_{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{1+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\nu_{k}\left(\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))-1\right)\right\}

up to a constant that does not depend on
(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})
.
The maximizers
\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{(t+1)}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(t+1)}
are the solutions to the following system:

\frac{\partial Q_{3}}{\partial\gamma_{k}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\nu_{k}\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))+(\partial\nu_{k}/\partial\gamma_{k})\left\{\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))-1\right\}}{1+\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K-1}\nu_{k^{\prime}}\left\{\exp(\gamma_{k^{\prime}}+\beta_{k^{\prime}}^{\top}g(X_{i}))-1\right\}}=0.

Since the Lagrange multipliers
\nu_{k}
satisfy the equation in (
13
), this simplifies to

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}=\nu_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))}{\sum_{k\in[K]}\nu_{k}\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))}=n\nu_{k}

and at the optimum, we have
\nu_{k}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}:=\omega_{\cdot k}^{(t)}
.
Thus, the stationary point of
Q_{3}(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})
coincides with the stationary point of

\widetilde{Q}(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{\sum_{k\in[K]}w_{\cdot k}\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))\right\}.

Let
\bar{\gamma}_{k}=\gamma_{k}+\log(\omega_{\cdot k}^{(t)}/\omega_{\cdot 0}^{(t)})
, then the function
\widetilde{Q}
becomes

\widetilde{Q}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}(\bar{\gamma}_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{\sum_{k\in[K]}\exp(\bar{\gamma}_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(X_{i}))\right\}.

Thus, we have

\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}^{(t+1)},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(t+1)}=\argmax_{\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}\widetilde{Q}(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}).

Now, we show that
(\bar{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}^{(t+1)},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{(t+1)})
is the maximum weighted log-likelihood estimator under the multinomial logistic regression model based on the dataset in Table

The E-step and M-step are repeated iteratively until the change in the profile log-EL falls below a predefined threshold.
This stopping criterion is guaranteed to be met because the EM algorithm produces a sequence of estimates that monotonically increase the true objective–the profile log-EL.
We provide a proof of this convergence property in the next subsection.

\thechapter
.A.2
Proof of the convergence of EM algorithm

We establish the monotonic increase of the profile log-EL at each EM iteration.

Proof.

Consider the difference in profile log-EL between consecutive iterations:

\begin{split}&p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)})-p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})\\
=&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(\frac{p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)})}{p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left[\frac{\sum_{k\in[K]}\left\{w_{k}^{(t+1)}T^{(t+1)}_{\widetilde{Y}_{i},k}\exp(\gamma^{(t+1)}_{k}+\langle\beta^{(t+1)}_{k},g(X_{i})\rangle)\right\}}{\sum_{k\in[K]}\left\{w_{l}^{(t)}T^{(t)}_{\widetilde{Y}_{i},k}\exp(\gamma^{(t)}_{k}+\langle\beta^{(t)}_{k},g(X_{i})\rangle)\right\}}\right].\end{split}

Using the weights
\omega_{ik}^{(t)}
defined in (
36
), we can rewrite this as:

\begin{split}&p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)})-p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})\\
=&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(\frac{p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)})}{p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left\{\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}\frac{w_{k}^{(t+1)}T^{(t+1)}_{\widetilde{Y}_{i},k}\exp(\gamma^{(t+1)}_{k}+\langle\beta^{(t+1)}_{k},g(X_{i})\rangle)}{w_{k}^{(t)}T^{(t)}_{\widetilde{Y}_{i},k}\exp(\gamma^{(t)}_{k}+\langle\beta^{(t)}_{k},g(X_{i})\rangle)}\right\}.\end{split}

By Jensen’s inequality and the concavity of
\log(\cdot)
, we obtain:

\begin{split}&p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)})-p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})\\
\geq&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(\frac{p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)})}{p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}\log\left(\frac{w_{k}^{(t+1)}T^{(t+1)}_{\widetilde{Y}_{i},k}\exp(\gamma^{(t+1)}_{k}+\langle\beta^{(t+1)}_{k},g(X_{i})\rangle)}{w_{k}^{(t)}T^{(t)}_{\widetilde{Y}_{i},k}\exp(\gamma^{(t)}_{k}+\langle\beta^{(t)}_{k},g(X_{i})\rangle)}\right)\\
=&Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})-Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)}).\end{split}

Since
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)}
maximizes
Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})
by construction, we have:

Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})-Q(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)};\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})\geq 0.

Consequently:

p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t+1)})-p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}^{(t)})\geq 0,

which completes the proof of monotonic convergence.
∎

\thechapter
.A.3
Incorporating prior knowledge on transition matrix

We present modifications to the M-step when prior knowledge about the transition matrix
{\bm{T}}^{*}
is available.
Specifically, consider known lower bounds for the diagonal elements:

T_{kk}^{*}\geq\xi_{k},\quad\xi_{k}\in[0,1],\quad k=1,\ldots,K

where typical values might be
\xi_{k}=0.8
.
The updates for
{\bm{w}}
and
(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})
remain unchanged, while the update for
{\bm{T}}
becomes a constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{T}},{\bm{s}}}

\displaystyle\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}\log T_{lk}\right\}

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in[K]}T_{lk}=1,~k\in[K]

\displaystyle T_{kk}-\xi_{k}-s_{k}^{2}=0,~k\in[K]

where
{\bm{s}}=\{s_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are slack variables.
The Lagrangian is:

{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{T}},{\bm{s}}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}\log T_{lk}-\sum_{k\in[K]}\zeta_{k}\left(\sum_{l\in[K]}T_{lk}-1\right)-\sum_{k\in[K]}\kappa_{k}\left(T_{kk}-\xi_{k}-s_{k}^{2}\right).

The KKT conditions yield:

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle~\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{T}},{\bm{s}}}}{\partial\zeta_{k}}=\sum_{l\in[K]}T_{lk}-1,~k\in[K]

(39)

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle~\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{T}},{\bm{s}}}}{\partial\kappa_{k}}=T_{kk}-\xi_{k}-s_{k}^{2},~k\in[K]

(40)

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle~\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{T}},{\bm{s}}}}{\partial s_{k}}=2\kappa_{k}s_{k},~k\in[K]

(41)

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle~\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{T}},{\bm{s}}}}{\partial T_{lk}}=-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}{T_{lk}}-\zeta_{k}-\kappa_{k}\mathbbm{1}(k=l),~l\in[K],~k\in[K].

(42)

We analyze two cases based on condition (
41
):

•

Case I: Unconstrained solution (
\kappa_{k}=0
).
In this case, (
42
) implies that

T_{lk}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}/\zeta_{k}
.
Summing over
k
and using (
39
), we obtain

T_{lk}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}.

From (
40
), we require that

T_{kk}=-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=k)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}\geq\xi_{k}.

If this condition is not met, this case is infeasible.

•

Case II: Active constraint (
s_{k}=0
).
When
s_{k}=0
, (
40
) implies that
T_{kk}=\xi_{k}
and (
42
) implies that

-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}=\zeta_{k}T_{lk}+\kappa_{k}\mathbbm{1}(k=l)T_{lk}

Summing over
l
on both sides of this equation, we get

-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}=\zeta_{k}+\kappa_{k}T_{kk}=\zeta_{k}+\kappa_{k}\xi_{k}

Plugging this into (
42
), we obtain

T_{lk}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}+\kappa_{k}\xi_{k}-\kappa_{k}\mathbbm{1}(k=l)}.

(43)

For the case when
l=k
, we have

\xi_{k}=T_{kk}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=k)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}+\kappa_{k}(\xi_{k}-1)}

which implies

\kappa_{k}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=k)-\xi_{k}\}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}{\xi_{k}(\xi_{k}-1)}.

Substituting this expression for
\kappa_{k}
into the previous equation gives the closed-form update for
{\bm{T}}
.

To summarize, the complete update combines both cases:

T_{lk}^{(t+1)}=\begin{cases}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}&\text{if }\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=k)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}\geq\xi_{k}\\
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}+\kappa_{k}^{(t+1)}\{\xi_{k}-\mathbbm{1}(k=l)\}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where

\kappa_{k}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=k)-\xi_{k}\}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}{\xi_{k}(\xi_{k}-1)}.

\thechapter
.A.4
Penalized EM algorithm

To enhance numerical stability and accelerate convergence, we introduce penalties on the diagonal elements
\{T_{kk}\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
of the transition matrix.
Let
\eta_{k}\geq 0
denote prespecified penalty parameters.
The penalized profile log empirical likelihood becomes:

p\ell_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\log\sum_{k\in[K]}\left\{w_{k}T_{lk}\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(x))\right\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{i}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})+\sum_{k\in[K]}\eta_{k}\log T_{kk}.

This penalty structure prevents the diagonal elements from approaching zero while maintaining the validity of the EM algorithm framework.
Notably, the updates for
{\bm{w}}
and
(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})
remain unchanged from the unpenalized version.

For the transition matrix
{\bm{T}}
, we solve the constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{{\bm{T}}}

\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}\log T_{lk}+\sum_{k\in[K]}\gamma_{k}\log T_{kk}\right\}

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in[K]}T_{lk}=1,~k\in[K].

The corresponding Lagrangian function is:

{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{T}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{l\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\sum_{k\in[K]}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}\log T_{lk}-\sum_{k\in[K]}\zeta_{k}\left(\sum_{l\in[K]}T_{lk}-1\right)+\sum_{k\in[K]}\eta_{k}\log T_{kk}.

The optimality conditions yield the following system of equations:

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle~\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{T}}}}{\partial\zeta_{k}}=\sum_{l\in[K]}T_{lk}-1,~k\in[K]

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle~\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}_{{\bm{T}}}}{\partial T_{lk}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}}{T_{lk}}-\zeta_{k}+\frac{\eta_{k}\mathbbm{1}(k=l)}{T_{kk}},~k\in[K],~l\in[K].

Solving for the equalities above, we then have

T_{lk}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}(\widetilde{Y}_{i}=l)\omega_{ik}^{(t)}+\eta_{k}\mathbbm{1}(k=l)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{ik}^{(t)}+\eta_{k}}.

Density ratio model under normal model

In two of our simulation settings, we generate the training set from the following model:

and

Therefore, we have
g(x)=(1,x)^{\top}
with

\gamma_{k}^{*}=-\frac{1}{2}\left\{(\mu_{k}^{*})^{\top}(\Sigma^{*})^{-1}\mu_{k}^{*}-(\mu_{0}^{*})^{\top}(\Sigma^{*})^{-1}\mu_{0}^{*}\right\},~\beta_{k}^{*}=(\mu_{k}^{*}-\mu_{0}^{*})^{\top}(\Sigma^{*})^{-1}

and
(\gamma_{k}^{*})^{\dagger}=\gamma_{k}^{*}+\log\left(\pi_{k}^{*}/\pi_{0}^{*}\right)
.
In the special case where

We then have the density ratios of
X|Y=k
and
X|Y=1

=&~\exp\left(\frac{x^{\top}\{(\Sigma_{0}^{*})^{-1}-(\Sigma_{k}^{*})^{-1}\}x}{2}+\{(\Sigma_{k}^{*})^{-1}\mu_{k}^{*}-(\Sigma_{0}^{*})^{-1}\mu_{0}^{*}\}^{\top}x\right)\\
&\times\exp\left(\frac{(\mu_{0}^{*})^{\top}(\Sigma_{0}^{*})^{-1}\mu_{0}^{*}-(\mu_{k}^{*})^{\top}(\Sigma_{k}^{*})^{-1}\mu_{k}^{*}}{2}+d\log\frac{\sigma_{0}^{*}}{\sigma_{k}^{*}}\right)\end{split}

Under the special case where
\Sigma_{k}^{*}=(\sigma_{k}^{*})^{2}I_{d}
, we have
g(x)=(x^{\top},x^{\top}x)^{\top}
with
\gamma_{k}^{*}=\|\mu_{0}^{*}\|^{2}/\{2(\sigma_{0}^{*})^{2}\}-\|\mu_{k}^{*}\|^{2}/\{2(\sigma_{k}^{*})^{2}\}+d\log(\sigma_{0}^{*}/\sigma_{k}^{*})
and
\beta_{k}^{*}=((\mu_{k}^{*}/(\sigma_{k}^{*})^{2}-\mu_{0}^{*}/(\sigma_{0}^{*})^{2})^{\top},((\sigma_{0}^{*})^{-2}-(\sigma_{k}^{*})^{-2})/2)^{\top}

\thechapter
.B.2.3
NP umbrella type control

In this section, we discuss how to integrate our EL-based estimator under noisy labels with the framework of
Tong et al. 2018
to achieve error control in binary classification.

The NP umbrella algorithm, proposed by
Tong et al. 2018
, provides a flexible procedure that accommodates various score functions from binary classifiers.
It produces a classifier that controls the type I error at the target level with high probability while minimizing the type II error.
The procedure is as follows:

1.

Split the class-
0
data into two disjoint subsets:
S_{0}^{\text{train}}
and
S_{0}^{\text{cal}}
.

2.

Train a base scoring function
f:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
using
S_{0}^{\text{train}}
together with all class-
1
samples, where larger values of
f(x)
indicate a higher likelihood that
x
belongs to class
1
.

3.

Evaluate
f
on
S_{0}^{\text{cal}}
, obtaining scores
\{T_{j}=f(X_{j}):X_{j}\in S_{0}^{\text{cal}}\}
.

4.

Sort these scores in increasing order:
T_{(1)}\leq T_{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq T_{(m)}
, where
m=|S_{0}^{\text{cal}}|
.

5.

Define the NP umbrella classifier by choosing an index
k^{*}
and setting

\phi(x)=\mathbb{I}\{f(x)>T_{(k^{*})}\}.

Since
S_{0}^{\text{cal}}
contains only class-
0
samples, any misclassification corresponds to a type I error.
Thus, the number of misclassified calibration samples follows a
\text{Binomial}(m,\alpha)
distribution.
The index
k^{*}
is chosen as the smallest integer satisfying

{\mathbb{P}}\big(\text{Binomial}(m,\alpha)\geq k^{*}\big)=\delta,

where
\delta
is a user-specified tolerance level (e.g.,
\delta=0.05
).
By construction, with probability at least
1-\delta
over the calibration set, the classifier
\phi
satisfies
P_{0}^{*}(\phi\neq 0)\leq\alpha
.

Although effective for controlling type I error,
Yao et al. 2023
showed that the NP umbrella algorithm becomes too conservative when the training data are contaminated, i.e., when labels are corrupted as in Assumption
2.1
.
This is because ignoring label noise in the NP umbrella algorithm only guarantees
\widetilde{P}_{0}^{*}(\phi\neq 0)\leq\alpha
instead of
P_{0}^{*}(\phi\neq 0)\leq\alpha
.
To address this issue, they extended the NP umbrella algorithm to handle the noisy-label setting as follows:

1.

Let
\widetilde{S}_{0}
and
\widetilde{S}_{1}
respectively be the corrupted class
0
and
1
training set.

2.

Partition
\widetilde{S}_{0}
into three random, disjoint, nonempty subsets:
\widetilde{S}_{0}^{\text{train}},\widetilde{S}_{0}^{\text{est}},
and
\widetilde{S}_{0}^{\text{cal}}
.
Similarly, partition
\widetilde{S}_{1}
into two random, disjoint, nonempty subsets:
\widetilde{S}_{1}^{\text{train}}
and
\widetilde{S}_{1}^{\text{est}}
.

3.

Train a base scoring function
f:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
using
\widetilde{S}_{0}^{\text{train}}\cap\widetilde{S}_{1}^{\text{train}}
, where larger values of
f(x)
indicate a higher likelihood that
x
belongs to corrupted class
1
.

4.

Evaluate
f
on
\widetilde{S}_{0}^{\text{cal}}
, obtaining scores
\{T_{j}=f(X_{j}):X_{j}\in\widetilde{S}_{0}^{\text{cal}}\}
.

5.

Sort these scores in increasing order:
T_{(1)}\leq T_{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq T_{(m)}
, where
m=|\widetilde{S}_{0}^{\text{cal}}|
.
These serve as the candidate thresholds, just as in the original NP umbrella algorithm.

6.

Define the NP umbrella classifier by choosing an index
k^{*}
and setting

\phi(x)=\mathbb{I}\{f(x)>T_{(k^{*})}\}.

To account for label noise, the noise-adjusted NP umbrella algorithm (with known corruption levels) selects
k^{*}
as

k^{*}=\min\{k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:\alpha_{k,\delta}-\widehat{D}^{+}(T_{(k)})\leq\alpha\}\,

where
\alpha_{k,\delta}
satisfies
{\mathbb{P}}\big(\text{Binomial}(m,\alpha_{k,\delta})\geq k^{*}\big)=\delta
,

\widehat{D}^{+}(\cdot)=\widehat{D}(\cdot)\vee 0:=\max(\widehat{D}(\cdot),0)
, and

\widehat{D}(\cdot)=\frac{1-m_{0}^{*}}{m_{0}^{*}-m_{1}^{*}}\left(\widetilde{P}_{0}^{f}(\cdot)-\widetilde{P}_{1}^{f}(\cdot)\right).

(44)

Here,
\widehat{D}(t)
estimates the gap between type I errors computed on clean versus corrupted data, with
\widetilde{P}_{0}^{f}(t)
and
\widetilde{P}_{1}^{f}(t)
denoting empirical estimates of
{\mathbb{P}}(f(X)\leq t\mid\widetilde{Y}=0)
and
{\mathbb{P}}(f(X)\leq t\mid\widetilde{Y}=1)
, based on
\widetilde{S}_{0}^{\text{est}}
and
\widetilde{S}_{1}^{\text{est}}
, respectively.

K

Specifically, the density (or Radon-Nikodym derivative) of
\widetilde{P}_{l}^{*}
with respect to
P_{0}^{*}
for an observation

is given by:

\frac{d\widetilde{P}_{l}^{*}}{dP_{0}^{*}}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}M_{lk}^{*}\exp\big(\gamma_{k}^{*}+\langle\beta_{k}^{*},g(x)\rangle\big),\quad\forall l\in[K],

(48)

where
{\bm{M}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
is the mixing matrix with entries
M_{lk}^{*}
,
\gamma_{k}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
are the component-specific intercepts,
\beta_{k}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the feature coefficients, and

To ensure that the parameters
({\bm{M}}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{*})
and

are uniquely identifiable from the distributions

Assumption
\thechapter
.C.1

(Distinct features)
.

The coefficients

.

Assumption
\thechapter
.C.2

(Richness of feature space)
.

The image of the feature mapping,

Assumption
\thechapter
.C.3

The

\thechapter
.C.4.2
Supporting Lemma

Assumption
\thechapter.C.2
is crucial to ensure that the exponential functions in our mixture do not collapse into linear dependence over the data support.

Lemma
\thechapter
.C.3
.

Under Assumption
\thechapter.C.2
, for any finite set of pairwise distinct vectors
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the functions
f_{k}(x)=\exp(\langle\beta_{k},g(x)\rangle)
are linearly independent over
{\mathcal{X}}
. That is, if

\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\exp(\langle\beta_{k},g(x)\rangle)=0\text{ for all }x\in{\mathcal{X}},

then
c_{1}=\dots=c_{K}=0
.

Proof.

Let
z=g(x)
. By hypothesis,
F(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\exp(\langle\beta_{k},z\rangle)=0
holds for all
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, where
{\mathcal{Z}}=\{g(x):x\in{\mathcal{X}}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the image of
{\mathcal{X}}
. Because
{\mathcal{Z}}
contains a non-empty open set (Assumption
\thechapter.C.2
) and
F(z)
is real-analytic on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the identity theorem for real-analytic functions dictates that
F(z)=0
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Since the vectors
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are pairwise distinct, the finite set of difference vectors
\{\beta_{i}-\beta_{j}:i\neq j\}
does not contain the zero vector. Consequently, the union of the orthogonal hyperplanes
\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\langle\beta_{i}-\beta_{j},v\rangle=0\}
has Lebesgue measure zero in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We can therefore choose a direction vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that avoids all such hyperplanes. For this
v
, the inner products
\lambda_{k}=\langle\beta_{k},v\rangle
are strictly distinct scalars.

Evaluating
F(z)
along the line
z=tv
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
yields:

F(tv)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\exp(t\langle\beta_{k},v\rangle)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\exp(\lambda_{k}t)=0,\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

Without loss of generality, assume
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\dots>\lambda_{K}
. Dividing the equation by
\exp(\lambda_{1}t)
gives:

c_{1}+\sum_{k=2}^{K}c_{k}\exp\big((\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{1})t\big)=0.

Taking the limit as
t\to\infty
, the terms
\exp((\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{1})t)
vanish because
\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{1}<0
for
k\geq 2
. This implies
c_{1}=0
. Applying this argument recursively to the remaining terms yields
c_{k}=0
for all
k\in[K]
, concluding the proof.
∎

\widetilde{f}_{j}
yields:

\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{1j}\exp\big(\gamma_{k}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}+\langle\beta_{k}+\widetilde{\beta}_{j},g(x)\rangle\big)=\sum_{m=1}^{K}A_{km}\exp\big(\widetilde{\gamma}_{m}+\langle\widetilde{\beta}_{m},g(x)\rangle\big).

(50)

Step 2: Uniqueness of the Anchor Mapping.

Note that (
50
) must hold for every fixed
k\in[K]
. To see how linear independence applies, we bring all terms to one side:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\Big[A_{1j}\exp(\gamma_{k}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{j})\Big]\exp\big(\langle\beta_{k}+\widetilde{\beta}_{j},g(x)\rangle\big)

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{m=1}^{K}\Big[A_{km}\exp(\widetilde{\gamma}_{m})\Big]\exp\big(\langle\widetilde{\beta}_{m},g(x)\rangle\big)=0.

Before invoking linear independence, we must group terms with identical exponent vectors. Let
V
be the set of all strictly unique exponent vectors present across both sums. We can rewrite the equation as:

\sum_{v\in V}W_{v}\exp\big(\langle v,g(x)\rangle\big)=0,

where
W_{v}
is the net scalar coefficient for the unique vector
v
. Because the elements of
V
are now pairwise distinct by definition, Lemma
\thechapter.C.3
directly applies, dictating that
W_{v}=0
for all
v\in V
.

Suppose there is an “active” index
j
on the left side where
A_{1j}\neq 0
. The exponent vector for this term is
v^{*}=\beta_{k}+\widetilde{\beta}_{j}
. Because the original vectors
\{\widetilde{\beta}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
are pairwise distinct (Assumption
\thechapter.C.1
), the shifted vectors
\{\beta_{k}+\widetilde{\beta}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
are also strictly pairwise distinct. This means no other term in the first sum shares the exponent vector
v^{*}
.

Therefore, the net coefficient for
v^{*}
takes the form:

W_{v^{*}}=A_{1j}\exp(\gamma_{k}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{j})-D_{v^{*}},

where
D_{v^{*}}=A_{km}\exp(\widetilde{\gamma}_{m})
if
v^{*}
matches some
\widetilde{\beta}_{m}
in the second sum, and
D_{v^{*}}=0
if it does not.

Since
A_{1j}\neq 0
and the exponential function is strictly positive, the first term
A_{1j}\exp(\gamma_{k}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{j})
is non-zero. However, we established that
W_{v^{*}}=0
. The only way this is mathematically possible is if
D_{v^{*}}\neq 0
. This forces the conclusion that
v^{*}
must identically match one of the available vectors in the second sum.

Thus, for any active index
j
, the vector
\beta_{k}+\widetilde{\beta}_{j}
must perfectly equal
\widetilde{\beta}_{m}
for some
m\in[K]
.

Let
S=\{\widetilde{\beta}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{\beta}_{K}\}
be the set of available exponent vectors on the right-hand side. For a fixed active index
j
(where
A_{1j}\neq 0
), as we vary
k
from
1
to
K
, the left-hand side generates a set of
K
exponent vectors:
B_{j}=\{\beta_{1}+\widetilde{\beta}_{j},\dots,\beta_{K}+\widetilde{\beta}_{j}\}
. Because every vector generated on the left must be cancelled by a vector on the right, every vector in
B_{j}
must belong to
S
. Since
B_{j}\subseteq S
and both sets have exactly
K
elements, they must be identical:
B_{j}=S
.

Now, suppose there are two distinct active indices,
i
and
j
, where
A_{1i}\neq 0
and
A_{1j}\neq 0
. By the exact same cancellation logic, we must have
B_{i}=S
and
B_{j}=S
, which implies
B_{i}=B_{j}
. This set equivalence means that
\{\beta_{k}+\widetilde{\beta}_{i}\}_{k=1}^{K}=\{\beta_{k}+\widetilde{\beta}_{j}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. Letting
\mathcal{B}=\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{K}\}
, we can write this as:

\mathcal{B}+\widetilde{\beta}_{i}=\mathcal{B}+\widetilde{\beta}_{j}\implies\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}+(\widetilde{\beta}_{i}-\widetilde{\beta}_{j}).

This states that the finite set of vectors
\mathcal{B}
is invariant under translation by the vector
\Delta=\widetilde{\beta}_{i}-\widetilde{\beta}_{j}
. However, a finite set of real vectors cannot be invariant under translation by a non-zero vector.

Therefore, the translation vector must be zero, meaning
\widetilde{\beta}_{i}=\widetilde{\beta}_{j}
. Since the coefficient vectors are pairwise distinct, we must have
i=j
. Conclusively, there is exactly one index
j^{*}
such that
A_{1j^{*}}\neq 0
, and
A_{1j}=0
for all
j\neq j^{*}
.

Step 3: Establishing the Permutation.

Since only
A_{1j^{*}}\neq 0
, Equation (
50
) collapses to a single term on the left-hand side:

A_{1j^{*}}\exp\big(\gamma_{k}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{j^{*}}+\langle\beta_{k}+\widetilde{\beta}_{j^{*}},g(x)\rangle\big)=\sum_{m=1}^{K}A_{km}\exp\big(\widetilde{\gamma}_{m}+\langle\widetilde{\beta}_{m},g(x)\rangle\big).

By linear independence, this single term must match exactly one term on the right-hand side. For each
k
, there is exactly one index
m=\sigma(k)
where
A_{k,\sigma(k)}\neq 0
. This immediately gives
\beta_{k}+\widetilde{\beta}_{j^{*}}=\widetilde{\beta}_{\sigma(k)}
.

Evaluate this for the anchor
k=1
. Since
\beta_{1}=\mathbf{0}
, we have
\widetilde{\beta}_{j^{*}}=\widetilde{\beta}_{\sigma(1)}
. Because the vectors are distinct, it follows that
\sigma(1)=j^{*}
.

Step 4: Fixing the Permutation via Recoverability.

We have established that
A
contains exactly one non-zero entry per row at
(k,\sigma(k))
. Thus,
A
is a permutation matrix scaled by row-specific factors. From
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}={\bm{M}}A
, we take the inverse:
(\widetilde{{\bm{M}}})^{-1}=A^{-1}{\bm{M}}^{-1}
.

The inverse matrix
A^{-1}
has its non-zero entries at
(\sigma(k),k)
. Because
R(x)=1/(A_{1j^{*}}\widetilde{f}_{j^{*}}(x))
must be strictly positive (as a ratio of two densities), we must have
A_{1j^{*}}>0
. The equality of coefficients from Step 3 ensures
A_{k,\sigma(k)}=A_{1j^{*}}\exp(\gamma_{k}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{j^{*}}-\widetilde{\gamma}_{\sigma(k)})>0
. Since all non-zero entries of
A
are positive, all non-zero entries of
A^{-1}
are also strictly positive.

The diagonal entries of the new inverse matrix are given by:

[(\widetilde{{\bm{M}}})^{-1}]_{k,k}=[A^{-1}]_{k,\sigma^{-1}(k)}[{\bm{M}}^{-1}]_{\sigma^{-1}(k),k}.

Suppose the permutation is not the identity, meaning there is some
k
where
\sigma^{-1}(k)\neq k
. Then
[{\bm{M}}^{-1}]_{\sigma^{-1}(k),k}
is an off-diagonal entry. By Assumption
\thechapter.C.3
, all off-diagonal entries of
{\bm{M}}^{-1}
are nonpositive (
\leq 0
). This implies that
[(\widetilde{{\bm{M}}})^{-1}]_{k,k}\leq 0
, which explicitly contradicts the requirement in Assumption
\thechapter.C.3
that
(\widetilde{{\bm{M}}})^{-1}
must have strictly positive diagonal entries.

Therefore, we must have
\sigma(k)=k
for all
k\in[K]
. This forces
j^{*}=\sigma(1)=1
.

Step 5: Global Identifiability of
P_{0}
and Parameters.

Because
j^{*}=1
and
\sigma(k)=k
, we have
A_{11}\neq 0
and
A_{1j}=0
for all
j\neq 1
. Applying the anchor assumption
\widetilde{\beta}_{1}=\mathbf{0}
and
\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}=0
, we substitute this back into our ratio expression from Step 1:

R(x)=\frac{1}{A_{11}\exp(0)}=\frac{1}{A_{11}}.

The density ratio
R(x)
is therefore a constant. Because
p_{0}(x)
and
\widetilde{p}_{0}(x)
are both probability densities (due to the normalization of the rows of
{\bm{M}}
), they must integrate to 1 over
{\mathcal{X}}
. Thus:

\int\widetilde{p}_{0}(x)d\mu(x)=\int\frac{1}{A_{11}}p_{0}(x)d\mu(x)\implies 1=\frac{1}{A_{11}}\cdot 1\implies A_{11}=1.

Therefore,
R(x)=1
, which proves that
\widetilde{p}_{0}(x)=p_{0}(x)
for all
x
, uniquely identifying the reference distribution
P_{0}
.

With
A_{11}=1
and
\sigma(k)=k
, the relation
\beta_{k}+\widetilde{\beta}_{1}=\widetilde{\beta}_{k}
becomes
\beta_{k}=\widetilde{\beta}_{k}
. Equating the coefficients
A_{k,k}=A_{11}\exp(\gamma_{k}+\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}-\widetilde{\gamma}_{k})
yields
1=\exp(\gamma_{k}-\widetilde{\gamma}_{k})
, meaning
\gamma_{k}=\widetilde{\gamma}_{k}
.

Finally, since
A
is the identity matrix
I
, the relation
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}={\bm{M}}A
reduces to
\widetilde{{\bm{M}}}={\bm{M}}
. We conclude that the parameters
({\bm{M}},\gamma,\beta)
and the reference distribution
P_{0}
are strictly identifiable.
∎

\thechapter
.C.5
Preliminaries on Rademacher complexity

In this section, we introduce key definitions on Rademacher complexity and essential lemmas for our class-specific error control analysis.

\thechapter
.C.5.1
Definitions

Definition
\thechapter
.C.1

(Rademacher complexity)
.

Let
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
be independent Rademacher random variables (i.e.,
{\mathbb{P}}(Z_{i}=-1)={\mathbb{P}}(Z_{i}=1)=0.5
).
Given a function class
{\mathcal{F}}
and IID samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, the empirical Rademacher complexity of
{\mathcal{F}}
is defined to be:

\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})={\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}}\left(\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}f(X_{i})\right|\right).

The Rademacher complexity of
{\mathcal{F}}
is defined as:

\mathfrak{R}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})={\mathbb{E}}_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}}\{\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})\}
.

Definition
\thechapter
.C.2

(
\epsilon
-cover and covering number)
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a class of functions and
D
a metric on
{\mathcal{F}}
.

•

An
\epsilon
-cover
of
{\mathcal{F}}
with respect to
D
is a finite set
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{l}\}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
such that for every
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
, there exists
f_{i}
satisfying
D(f,f_{i})\leq\epsilon
.

•

The
\epsilon
-covering number
, denoted
{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon,{\mathcal{F}},D)
, is the smallest cardinality of any
\epsilon
-cover of
{\mathcal{F}}
with respect to
D
.

\thechapter
.C.5.2
Lemmas

Lemma
\thechapter
.C.4

(Bounds for Vapnik–Chervonenkis classes)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be fixed sample points with the empirical
L_{2}
norm defined as

\|f\|_{n}^{2}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{2}(X_{i}).

(51)

Then we have

\mathfrak{R}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})\leq\frac{24}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{B}\sqrt{\log{\mathcal{N}}(t,{\mathcal{F}},\|\|_{n})}\,dt

where
\sup_{f,g\in{\mathcal{F}}}\|f-g\|_{n}\leq B
.

Proof of Lemma
\thechapter.C.4
.

See
Wainwright 2019
.

Lemma
\thechapter
.C.5

(Uniform law via Rademacher complexity)
.

For a function class
{\mathcal{F}}
that is uniformly bounded by
M
(i.e.,
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq M
for all
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
), the following inequality holds with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})-{\mathbb{E}}[f(X)]\right|\leq 2\mathfrak{R}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})+M\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{n}}

where
\mathfrak{R}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})
is the Rademacher complexity of
{\mathcal{F}}
defined in Definition
\thechapter.C.1
.

Proof of Lemma
\thechapter.C.5
.

See
Wainwright 2019
.
∎

Lemma
\thechapter
.C.6

(Covering number of product of uniformly bounded functions)
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
and
{\mathcal{K}}
be two classes of functions that are uniformly bounded by
M_{H}
and
M_{K}
.
Define the product class

{\mathcal{F}}=\{f(x)=h(x)k(x):h\in{\mathcal{H}},\,k\in{\mathcal{K}}\}.

Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, the covering number of
{\mathcal{F}}
w.r.t. the empirical
L_{2}
norm in (
51
) satisfies

{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon,{\mathcal{F}},\|\cdot\|_{n})\leq{\mathcal{N}}\Big(\frac{\epsilon}{M_{H}+M_{K}},{\mathcal{H}},\|\cdot\|_{n}\Big)\cdot{\mathcal{N}}\Big(\frac{\epsilon}{M_{H}+M_{K}},{\mathcal{K}},\|\cdot\|_{n}\Big).

Proof of Lemma
\thechapter.C.6
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, let
\delta=\frac{\epsilon}{M_{H}+M_{K}}
.
Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{\delta}
be a
\delta
-cover of
{\mathcal{H}}
under
\|\cdot\|_{n}
, and let
{\mathcal{K}}_{\delta}
be a
\delta
-cover of
{\mathcal{K}}
.

For any
f=hk\in{\mathcal{F}}
, select approximations
\tilde{h}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\delta}
and
\tilde{k}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{\delta}
such that

\|h-\tilde{h}\|_{n}\leq\delta,\quad\|k-\tilde{k}\|_{n}\leq\delta.

Define
\tilde{f}:=\tilde{h}\tilde{k}
.
Then,
f-\tilde{f}=hk-\tilde{h}\tilde{k}=h(k-\tilde{k})+(h-\tilde{h})\tilde{k}
.
We have

\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{n}\leq\|h(k-\tilde{k})\|_{n}+\|(h-\tilde{h})\tilde{k}\|_{n}.

Next,

\|h(k-\tilde{k})\|_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h^{2}(X_{i})\,(k(X_{i})-\tilde{k}(X_{i}))^{2}}\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|h(X_{i})|\cdot\|k-\tilde{k}\|_{n}\leq M_{H}\delta.

Similarly,

\|(h-\tilde{h})\tilde{k}\|_{n}\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\tilde{k}(X_{i})|\cdot\|h-\tilde{h}\|_{n}\leq M_{K}\delta.

Combining, we get

\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{n}\leq(M_{H}+M_{K})\delta=\epsilon.

In summary, the collection
\{\tilde{h}\tilde{k}\colon\tilde{h}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\delta},\tilde{k}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{\delta}\}
forms an
\epsilon
-cover for
{\mathcal{F}}
.
This gives the bound

{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon,{\mathcal{F}},\|\cdot\|_{n})\leq{\mathcal{N}}\Big(\frac{\epsilon}{M_{H}+M_{K}},{\mathcal{H}},\|\cdot\|_{n}\Big)\cdot{\mathcal{N}}\Big(\frac{\epsilon}{M_{H}+M_{K}},{\mathcal{K}},\|\cdot\|_{n}\Big).

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma
\thechapter
.C.7

(Covering number of pointwise maximum functions)
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{F}}_{K}
be
K
classes of functions.
Define the maximum class

{\mathcal{F}}_{\max}=\left\{f(x)=\max_{k\in[K]}f_{k}(x):f_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\right\}.

Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, the covering number of
{\mathcal{F}}_{\max}
w.r.t. the empirical
L_{2}
norm in (
51
) satisfies

{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon,{\mathcal{F}}_{\max},\|\cdot\|_{n})\leq\prod_{k\in[K]}{\mathcal{N}}\Big(\frac{\epsilon}{K},{\mathcal{F}}_{k},\|\cdot\|_{n}\Big).

Proof.

For any
\epsilon>0
, let
\delta=\frac{\epsilon}{K}
.
Let
{\mathcal{F}}_{k,\delta}
be a
\delta
-cover of
{\mathcal{F}}_{k}
under
\|\cdot\|_{n}
.
For any
f=\max_{k}f_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\max}
, select approximations
\tilde{f}_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{k,\delta}
such that
\|f_{k}-\tilde{f}_{k}\|_{n}\leq\delta
.
Define
\tilde{f}:=\max_{k}\tilde{f}_{k}
.
Then for any
x
, we have

|f(x)-\tilde{f}(x)|\leq\max_{k\in[K]}|f_{k}(x)-\tilde{f}_{k}(x)|\leq\sum_{k\in[K]}|f_{k}(x)-\tilde{f}_{k}(x)|.

Combine this with the triangular inequality gives

\begin{split}\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{n}\leq&~\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}|f_{k}(X_{i})-\tilde{f}_{k}(X_{i})|\right)^{2}}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f_{k}(X_{i})-\tilde{f}_{k}(X_{i})\right)^{2}}\\
=&~\sum_{k\in[K]}\|f_{k}-\tilde{f}_{k}\|_{n}\leq K\delta=\epsilon.\end{split}

In summary, the collection
\{\tilde{f}_{k}\colon\tilde{f}_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{k,\delta}\}
forms an
\epsilon
-cover for
{\mathcal{F}}_{\max}
.
This gives the bound

{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon,{\mathcal{F}}_{\max},\|\cdot\|_{n})\leq\prod_{k\in[K]}{\mathcal{N}}\Big(\frac{\epsilon}{K},{\mathcal{F}}_{k},\|\cdot\|_{n}\Big).

This completes the proof.
∎

\thechapter
.C.6
Guaranteed type I error control under binary classification

This section is organized as follows: we first prove Theorem
4.1
, then present the supporting lemmas at the end.

\thechapter
.C.6.1
Proof of type I error under binary classification

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

To make the notation clearer, let
\pi^{*}(x)
and
w^{*}
denote the true values of
\pi(x)
and
w
in the data generating process.
Let
\widehat{\pi}(x)
and
\widehat{w}
be the corresponding estimators for
\pi^{*}(x)
and
w^{*}
in (
26
) and (
14
), respectively.
The final classifier is

\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}(x)=\mathbbm{1}\left(\widehat{\lambda}\leq r(x,\widehat{\pi},\widehat{w})\right),

where
\widehat{\lambda}
is the solution to

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{1-\widehat{\pi}(X_{i})\}\mathbbm{1}(\widehat{\phi}_{\lambda}(X_{i})=1)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{1-\widehat{\pi}(X_{i})\}\mathbbm{1}\left(\lambda\leq r(X_{i},\widehat{\pi},\widehat{w})\right)=\alpha(1-\widehat{w}).

Our goal is to show that the type I error
P_{0}(\{\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}(X)\neq 0\})
is controlled at level
\alpha
in probability.
Note that for any classifier
\phi
, the type I error can be rewritten as

P_{0}(\phi(X)\neq 0)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{X}[\{1-\pi^{*}(X)\}\mathbbm{1}(\phi(X)=1)]}{{\mathbb{P}}(Y=0)}.

We can therefore equivalently show that

{\mathbb{E}}_{X}[\{1-\pi^{*}(X)\}\mathbbm{1}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}(X)=1)]\leq\alpha(1-w^{*})+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

The difference of interest can be decomposed into

\begin{split}&{\mathbb{E}}_{X}[\{1-\pi^{*}(X)\}\mathbbm{1}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}(X)=1)]-\alpha(1-w^{*})\\
=&\underbrace{{\mathbb{E}}_{X}[\{\widehat{\pi}(X)-\pi^{*}(X)\}\mathbbm{1}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}(X)=1)]}_{T_{1}}+\underbrace{\alpha(\widehat{w}-w^{*})}_{T_{2}}\\
&+\underbrace{\left\{{\mathbb{E}}_{X}[\{1-\widehat{\pi}(X)\}\mathbbm{1}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}(X)=1)]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{1-\widehat{\pi}(X_{i})\}\mathbbm{1}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}(X_{i})=1)\right\}}_{T_{3}}.\end{split}

We now bound each term separately.

Bound on
T_{1}
:

By the Lipschitz property of
\pi(\cdot)
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

|T_{1}|\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{X}[|\widehat{\pi}(X)-\pi^{*}(X)|]\leq\frac{1}{4}\bigl(|\widehat{\gamma}-\gamma^{*}|+\|\widehat{\beta}-\beta^{*}\|_{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\|g(X)\|_{2}\bigr).

Since
{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\|g(X)\|_{2}<\infty
, this along with Theorem
3.2
implies that
|T_{1}|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.

Bound on
T_{2}
:

This term is straightforward.
Note that

|T_{2}|=\alpha|\widehat{w}-w^{*}|.

By the consistency of
\widehat{w}
, we have
|T_{2}|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.

Bound on
T_{3}
:

The classifier can be expressed as a thresholding rule:

\mathbbm{1}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}(x)=1)=\mathbbm{1}\left(\widehat{\pi}(x)\geq\frac{\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{w}}{(1-\widehat{w})+\widehat{\lambda}\widehat{w}}\right).

Consider the class of functions:

{\mathcal{F}}=\left\{f_{\gamma,\beta,t}(x)=(1-\pi_{\gamma,\beta}(x))\mathbbm{1}(\pi_{\gamma,\beta}(x)\geq t):\gamma\in\mathbb{R},\|\beta\|_{2}\leq B,t\in[0,1]\right\}

where
\pi_{\gamma,\beta}(x)=\{1+\exp(-\gamma-\beta^{\top}g(x))\}^{-1}
.
We then have

T_{3}\leq\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})-{\mathbb{E}}f(X)\right|.

Since
{\mathcal{F}}
is uniformly bounded by 1, by Lemma
\thechapter.C.5
, we have with probability at least
1-\delta
:

T_{3}\leq 2\mathfrak{R}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})+\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{n}},

where
\mathfrak{R}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})
is the Rademacher complexity of
{\mathcal{F}}
.
We have shown in Lemma
\thechapter.C.9
that

\mathfrak{R}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})=C^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{d+1}{n}}

where
C^{\prime}
is the universal constant specified in Lemma
\thechapter.C.9
.
Combining all bounds, we conclude that:

{\mathbb{E}}_{X}[\{1-\pi^{*}(X)\}\mathbbm{1}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\lambda}}(X)=1)]\leq\alpha(1-w^{*})+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

which completes the proof.
∎

\thechapter
.C.6.2
Rademacher complexity of weighted logistic classifiers

Lemma
\thechapter
.C.8

(Covering number of weighted logistic threshold functions)
.

Let
\|\cdot\|_{n}
denote the empirical
L_{2}
-norm defined in Lemma
\thechapter.C.6
. Consider the function class

{\mathcal{F}}=\Bigl\{f_{\gamma,\beta,t}(x)=\bigl(1-\pi_{\gamma,\beta}(x)\bigr)\,\mathbbm{1}\bigl(\pi_{\gamma,\beta}(x)\geq t\bigr):|\gamma|\leq B,\|\beta\|_{2}\leq B,t\in[0,1]\Bigr\},

where
B>0
is a constant, and
\pi_{\gamma,\beta}(x)=\bigl\{1+\exp\bigl(-\gamma-\beta^{\top}g(x)\bigr)\bigr\}^{-1}
is the logistic function with
g(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Then, for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the
\epsilon
-covering number of
{\mathcal{F}}
w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{n}
satisfies

\log{\mathcal{N}}\bigl(\epsilon,{\mathcal{F}},\|\cdot\|_{n}\bigr)\leq 2C(d+1)\log\left(1+\frac{4B\max\{G_{n},1\}}{\epsilon}\right).

where
C>0
is a universal constant independent of
d
,
B
,
\epsilon
, and
G_{n}^{2}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|g(X_{i})\|_{2}^{2}
.

Proof of Lemma
\thechapter.C.8
.

Let
\sigma(z)=\{1+\exp(-z)\}^{-1}
denote the logistic sigmoid function.
Define the auxiliary function classes:

\begin{split}{\mathcal{H}}=&~\Bigl\{x\mapsto 1-\sigma(\gamma+\beta^{\top}g(x)):|\gamma|\leq B,\|\beta\|_{2}\leq B\Bigr\},\\
{\mathcal{K}}=&~\Bigl\{x\mapsto\mathbbm{1}\bigl(\sigma(\gamma+\beta^{\top}g(x))\geq t\bigr):|\gamma|\leq B,\|\beta\|_{2}\leq B,t\in[0,1]\Bigr\}.\end{split}

Since both
{\mathcal{H}}
and
{\mathcal{K}}
are uniformly bounded by
1
, Lemma
\thechapter.C.6
implies

{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon,{\mathcal{F}},\|\cdot\|_{n})\leq{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon/2,{\mathcal{H}},\|\cdot\|_{n})\cdot{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon/2,{\mathcal{K}},\|\cdot\|_{n}).

We now bound each term separately.

Covering number of
{\mathcal{H}}
.

Let
h_{\gamma,\beta}\in{\mathcal{H}}
denote the function
x\mapsto 1-\sigma(\gamma+\beta^{\top}g(x))
.
Note that
\|\sigma^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 1/4
.
Thus, for any two parameters
(\gamma,\beta)
and
(\gamma^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
, we have

\begin{split}|\sigma(\gamma+\beta^{\top}g(x))-\sigma(\gamma^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime\top}g(x))|\leq\frac{1}{4}\bigl(|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}|+|\beta-\beta^{\prime}|\|g(x)\|_{2}\bigr).\end{split}

This along with
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
imply that the empirical
L_{2}
norm satisfies

\begin{split}\|h_{\gamma,\beta}-h_{\gamma^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}\|_{n}^{2}&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sigma(\gamma+\beta^{\top}g(X_{i}))-\sigma(\gamma^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime\top}g(X_{i}))\right)^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|g(X_{i})\|_{2}^{2}\right\}}_{G_{n}^{2}}\|\beta-\beta^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

Therefore, if
|\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}|\leq\epsilon
and
\|\beta-\beta^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon/G_{n}
, then
\|h_{\gamma,\beta}-h_{\gamma^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}\|_{n}\leq\epsilon
.
This implies that the
\epsilon
-covering number of
{\mathcal{H}}
is bounded by the product of:

•

The
\epsilon
-covering number of
[-B,B]\subset\mathbb{R}

•

The
\epsilon/G_{n}
-covering number of the radius
B
ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

Using standard bounded ball covering number results (see
Wainwright 2019
for example), the
\epsilon
-covering of a radius
B
ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is at most

\left(1+\frac{2B}{\epsilon}\right)^{d}.

Therefore, we have

{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon,{\mathcal{H}},\|\cdot\|_{n})\leq\left(1+\frac{2B}{\epsilon}\right)\left(1+\frac{2BG_{n}}{\epsilon}\right)^{d}.

Covering number of
{\mathcal{K}}
.

The class
{\mathcal{K}}
consists of indicator functions of half-spaces in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, as it can be rewritten as:

{\mathcal{K}}=\Bigl\{x\mapsto\mathbbm{1}(\gamma+\beta^{\top}g(x)\geq\log(t/(1-t))):|\gamma|\leq B,\|\beta\|_{2}\leq B,t\in[0,1]\Bigr\}.

Hence, its VC dimension is
d+1
.

By
Vershynin 2018
, there exists some universal constant
C>0
such that

{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon,{\mathcal{K}},\|\cdot\|_{n})\leq(2/\epsilon)^{C\cdot\text{VC}({\mathcal{K}})},

where
\text{VC}({\mathcal{K}})
is the VC dimension of
{\mathcal{K}}
.
Therefore, we have

{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon,{\mathcal{K}},\|\cdot\|_{n})\leq\left(\frac{2}{\epsilon}\right)^{C(d+1)}.

Covering number of
{\mathcal{F}}
.

Putting everything together yields:

\begin{split}\log{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon,{\mathcal{F}},\|\cdot\|_{n})\leq&~(d+1)\log\left(1+\frac{4B\max\{G_{n},1\}}{\epsilon}\right)+C(d+1)\log\left(\frac{4}{\epsilon}\right)\\
\leq&~2C(d+1)\log\left(1+\frac{4B\max\{G_{n},1\}}{\epsilon}\right).\end{split}

This completes the proof.
∎

\thechapter
.C.7
Guaranteed class specific error control under multiclass classification

\thechapter
.C.7.1
Proof of class-specific error control

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Recall that

R_{k}(\phi)={\mathbb{P}}(\phi(X)\neq k|Y=k)

Then by the strong duality, the optimal classifier is a feasible solution that satisfies
R_{k}(\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}})\leq\alpha_{k}
.
Therefore, we have

R_{k}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}})-\alpha_{k}=R_{k}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}})-R_{k}(\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}})+R_{k}(\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}})-\alpha_{k}\leq R_{k}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}})-R_{k}(\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}}).

Hence, we only need to bound

R_{k}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}})-R_{k}(\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}})\leq{\mathbb{P}}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}}(X)\neq\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}}(X)|Y=k).

Note that the RHS is a random variable that depends on the training data
{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{train}}
.
We wish to show that it converges to
0
in probability as
n\to\infty
.
Since the random variable is bounded uniformly by 1, by Markov’s inequality it suffices to show that

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{train}}}\left\{{\mathbb{P}}_{X}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}}(X)\neq\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}}(X)|Y=k)\right\}=0.

Using the dominant convergence theorem, we can exchange limits and expectations to see that the LHS is equal to

{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left\{\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{train}}}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}}(X)\neq\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}}(X)\big|Y=k)\right\}

if the inner limit exists pointwise.
It thus suffices to show that

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{train}}}(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}}(X)\neq\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}}(X))=0

holds almost surely in
X
.
For fixed
X
, we may write

\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}}(X)=\arg\max_{k}Z_{k},\text{ with }Z_{k}=\frac{\rho_{k}+\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}_{k}^{*}\mathbbm{1}(k\in{\mathcal{S}})}{w_{k}^{*}}\pi_{k}^{*}(X)

\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}}(X)=\arg\max_{k}\widehat{Z}_{k},\text{ with }\widehat{Z}_{k}=\frac{\rho_{k}+\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}_{k}\mathbbm{1}(k\in{\mathcal{S}})}{\widehat{w}_{k}}\widehat{\pi}_{k}(X).

Almost surely, there are no ties among
\{Z_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
, that is,
\eta>0
where
\eta
is the gap between the largest and second largest entries in this set.
By Lemma
\thechapter.C.11
and Theorem
3.2
, we have

\widehat{Z}_{k}\overset{p}{\to}Z_{k}

as
n\to\infty
.
As such, for any
\epsilon>0
, for
n
large enough, we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\max_{k\in[K]}|\widehat{Z}_{k}-Z_{k}|<\eta/2\right)\geq 1-\epsilon.

On this high probability set, we have that
\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}}(X)=\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}}(X)
, which implies that

{\mathbb{P}}_{{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{train}}}\left(\widehat{\phi}_{\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}}(x)=\phi^{*}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}}(X)\right)\geq 1-\epsilon.

Since this holds for all
\epsilon>0
, the conclusion follows.
∎

\thechapter
.C.7.2
Asymptotic convergence of dual problem

Lemma
\thechapter
.C.10

(Uniform convergence of dual objective)
.

Under Assumptions
4.2
and
5.1
, for any bounded set
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{|{\mathbb{S}}|}
, the empirical dual objective
\widehat{G}(\lambda)
converges uniformly to
G(\lambda)
almost surely:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|\widehat{G}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})-G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})|=0\right)=1.

Proof.

Let
\pi_{k}^{*}(x)=\mathbb{P}(Y=k|X=x)
denote the true conditional probability and
\widehat{\pi}_{k}(x)=\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(Y=k|X=x)
its empirical estimate.
We decompose the difference as follows:

\begin{split}|\widehat{G}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})-G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})|=&~\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{k}\left\{c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},\widehat{{\bm{w}}})\widehat{\pi}_{k}(X_{i})\right\}-{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\max_{k}\left\{c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},{\bm{w}}^{*})\pi_{k}^{*}(X)\right\}\right]\right|\\
\leq&~\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{k}\left\{c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},\widehat{{\bm{w}}})\widehat{\pi}_{k}(X_{i})\right\}-{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\max_{k}\left\{c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},\widehat{\bm{w}})\widehat{\pi}_{k}(X)\right\}\right]\right|\\
&+\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\max_{k}\left\{c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},\widehat{{\bm{w}}})\widehat{\pi}_{k}(X)\right\}\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\max_{k}\left\{c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},{\bm{w}}^{*})\pi_{k}^{*}(X)\right\}\right]\right|\end{split}

where
c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},{\bm{w}})=\{\rho_{k}+\lambda_{k}\mathbbm{1}(k\in{\mathcal{S}})\}/w_{k}.

Taking supremum over
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\Lambda
yields:

\begin{split}\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\Lambda}|\widehat{G}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})-G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})|\leq&~\underbrace{\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})-{\mathbb{E}}f(X)\right|}_{T_{1}}\\
&+\underbrace{\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\Lambda}\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\max_{k}\left\{c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},\widehat{{\bm{w}}})\widehat{\pi}_{k}(X)\right\}\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\max_{k}\left\{c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},{\bm{w}}^{*})\pi_{k}^{*}(X)\right\}\right]\right|}_{T_{2}},\end{split}

where

{\mathcal{F}}=\left\{f_{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},{\bm{w}},\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}(x)=\max_{k}\{c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},{\bm{w}})\pi_{k}(x)\}:\|\gamma\|_{2}\leq B,\|\beta\|_{2}\leq B,w_{k}\geq c_{k}>0,\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\Lambda\right\}

and
\pi_{k}(x)=\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(x))/\sum_{k^{\prime}}\exp(\gamma_{k^{\prime}}+\beta_{k^{\prime}}^{\top}g(x))
.
We now bound each of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
respectively.

Bound
T_{1}
:

Since
{\mathcal{F}}
is uniformly bounded and satisfies the conditions of Lemma
\thechapter.C.5
, we have with probability at least
1-\delta
:

T_{1}\leq 2\mathfrak{R}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})+\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{n}},

where
\mathfrak{R}_{n}({\mathcal{F}})
is the Rademacher complexity of
{\mathcal{F}}
.
By Lemma
\thechapter.C.13
, there exists a constant
C^{\prime}>0
such that:

\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F})\leq C^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{d+3}{n}}.

Thus,
T_{1}\to 0
almost surely as
n\to\infty
.

Bound
T_{2}
:

Using the inequality
|\max_{k}f_{k}-\max_{k}g_{k}|\leq\max_{k}|f_{k}-g_{k}|\leq\sum_{k}|f_{k}-g_{k}|
, we obtain:

\begin{split}T_{2}\leq&\sum_{k}\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\Lambda}{\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left|c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},\widehat{{\bm{w}}})\widehat{\pi}_{k}(X)-c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},{\bm{w}}^{*})\pi_{k}^{*}(X)\right|\\
\leq&\sum_{k}\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\Lambda}c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},\widehat{{\bm{w}}}){\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left|\widehat{\pi}_{k}(X)-\pi_{k}^{*}(X)\right|+\sum_{k}{\mathbb{E}}_{X}(\pi_{k}^{*}(X))\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\Lambda}|c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},\widehat{\bm{w}})-c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},{\bm{w}}^{*})|.\end{split}

Since
w_{k}^{*}
is bounded away from zero and
\pi_{k}(x)
is Lipschitz in its parameters, and
\widehat{{\bm{w}}}\to{\bm{w}}^{*}
by asymptotic normality, we have:

T_{2}\leq C\left[\|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{*}\|_{2}+\|g(X)\|_{2}\|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{*}\|_{2}+\sum_{k}\left\{\frac{1}{\widehat{w}_{k}}-\frac{1}{w_{k}^{*}}\right\}\right]\to 0

almost surely as
n\to\infty
.

Combining these results yields the desired uniform convergence:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|\widehat{G}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})-G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})|=0\right)=1.

∎

Lemma
\thechapter
.C.11

(Convergence of Lagrange multiplier)
.

Under Assumptions
4.2
,
5.1
, and
5.2
, we have

\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

Proof of Lemma
\thechapter.C.11
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, to show that
\|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
, it suffices to show that

\widehat{G}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*})>\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\partial B_{\epsilon}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*})}\widehat{G}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}).

(52)

Then by the concavity of
\widehat{G}
, we must have
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}\in B_{\epsilon}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*})
.
As such, if we can show that (
52

n\to\infty

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}\|_{2}>\epsilon\right)=0.

If this holds for all
\epsilon>0
, we get
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}
as
n\to\infty
.

To verify (
52
), observe that

\begin{split}&~\widehat{G}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*})-\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\partial B_{\tau}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*})}\widehat{G}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})\\
\geq&~\left\{G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*})-\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\partial B_{\tau}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*})}G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})\right\}-2\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\bar{B}_{\epsilon}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*})}|\widehat{G}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})-G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})|\\
>&~-2\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\bar{B}_{\epsilon}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*})}|\widehat{G}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})-G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})|,\end{split}

where the strict inequality follows from
\nabla^{2}G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*})\prec 0
, which guarantees
\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*}
is the unique maximizer of
G
in
\bar{B}_{\epsilon}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*})
.
By Lemma
\thechapter.C.10
,
\sup_{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\overline{B}_{\epsilon}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{*})}|\widehat{G}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})-G(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$})|\to 0
almost surely as
n\to\infty
. Thus, (
52
) holds almost surely, completing the proof.
∎

\thechapter
.C.7.3
Rademacher complexity of weighted multinomial logistic classifiers

Lemma
\thechapter
.C.12

(Covering number of weighted multinomial logistic classifier)
.

Let
\|\cdot\|_{n}
denote the empirical
L_{2}
-norm defined in Lemma
\thechapter.C.6
.
Let
B>0
be some fixed constant,
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{|{\mathbb{S}}|}
be any bounded set, and

{\mathcal{F}}=\left\{f_{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},{\bm{w}},\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}(x)=\max_{k}\{c_{k}(\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},{\bm{w}})\pi_{k}(x)\}:\|\gamma\|_{2}\leq B,\|\beta\|_{2}\leq B,w_{k}\geq 1/B,\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\Lambda\right\}

and
\pi_{k}(x)=\exp(\gamma_{k}+\beta_{k}^{\top}g(x))/\sum_{k^{\prime}}\exp(\gamma_{k^{\prime}}+\beta_{k^{\prime}}^{\top}g(x))
.

Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, the
\epsilon
-covering number of
{\mathcal{F}}
w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{n}
satisfies

\log{\mathcal{N}}\bigl(\epsilon,{\mathcal{F}},\|\cdot\|_{n}\bigr)\leq K^{2}(d+3)\log\left(1+\frac{2BG_{n}}{\epsilon}\right)

where
G_{n}^{2}=K(A/4)^{2}\left\{1+n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|g(X_{i})\|_{2}^{2}\right\}+A^{2}B^{2}+B^{2}
and
A=\max_{k}(\rho_{k}+B)B
.

Proof of Lemma
\thechapter.C.12
.

Since
\sup_{f,g\in{\mathcal{F}}}\|f-g\|_{n}
is bounded by a finite constant, we show the upper bound for the Rademacher complexity of
{\mathcal{F}}
via Lemma
\thechapter.C.4
.
As such, we consider the covering number of
{\mathcal{F}}
.
Denote

{\mathcal{G}}_{k}=\left\{g^{(k)}_{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},w,\lambda}(x)=C_{k}(\lambda,w)\pi_{k}(x):\|\gamma\|_{2}\leq B,\|\beta\|_{2}\leq B,w\geq 1/B,0\leq\lambda\leq B\right\},

where
C_{k}(\lambda,w)=\frac{\rho_{k}+\lambda\mathbbm{1}(k\in{\mathcal{S}})}{w}
.
By Lemma
\thechapter.C.6
, for any
t>0
, we have

\log{\mathcal{N}}(t;{\mathcal{F}},\|\cdot\|_{n})=\sum_{k}\log{\mathcal{N}}(t;{\mathcal{G}}_{k},\|\cdot\|_{n}).

(53)

Therefore, we only need to consider the covering number of
{\mathcal{G}}_{k}
.

We first find the Lipschitz-constant of
g^{(k)}
for any fixed
x
.
Note that the
C_{k}(\lambda,w)\leq(\rho_{k}+B)B
.
For any
x
, the softmax derivative satisfies
\partial\pi_{k}/\partial\eta_{j}=\pi_{k}(\delta_{kj}-\pi_{j})
with
\left|\partial\pi_{k}/\partial\eta_{j}\right|\leq 1/4
.
Therefore,

\left|\frac{\partial g^{(k)}}{\partial\gamma_{j}}\right|=C_{k}(\lambda,w)\left|\frac{\partial\pi_{k}}{\partial\gamma_{j}}\right|\leq\frac{C_{k}(\lambda,w)}{4}\leq(\rho_{k}+B)B/4,

and

\left\|\frac{\partial g^{(k)}}{\partial\beta_{j}}\right\|=C_{k}(\lambda,w)\left\|\frac{\partial\pi_{k}}{\partial\beta_{j}}\right\|\leq\frac{C_{k}(\lambda,w)}{4}\,\|g(x)\|_{2}\leq(\rho_{k}+B)B\|g(x)\|_{2}/4.

Moreover,

\left|\frac{\partial g^{(k)}}{\partial w}\right|=\frac{|\rho_{k}|+\lambda\,\mathbbm{1}(k\in\mathcal{S})}{w^{2}}\pi_{k}(x)\leq(\rho_{k}+B)B^{2},

and

\left|\frac{\partial g^{(k)}}{\partial\lambda}\right|=\frac{\mathbbm{1}(k\in\mathcal{S})}{w}\pi_{k}(x)\leq B.

For any
\theta=(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},w,\lambda),\theta^{\prime}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}^{\prime},\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\prime},w^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})
in the parameter space, we have that

\begin{split}\left|g^{(k)}_{\theta}(x)-g^{(k)}_{\theta^{\prime}}(x)\right|\leq&~\sup_{\tilde{\theta}}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}g^{(k)}_{\tilde{\theta}}(x)\right\|_{2}\cdot\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}\\
\leq&~\left(K\left(\frac{A}{4}\right)^{2}+K\left(\frac{A}{4}\right)^{2}\|g(x)\|_{2}^{2}+A^{2}B^{2}+B^{2}\right)^{1/2}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}\end{split}

where
A:=\sup_{\lambda,w}C_{k}(\lambda,w)\leq(\rho_{k}+B)B
.

This implies that the empirical
L_{2}
norm satisfies

\begin{split}\|g^{(k)}_{\theta}-g^{(k)}_{\theta^{\prime}}\|_{n}^{2}&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sigma(\gamma+\beta^{\top}g(X_{i}))-\sigma(\gamma^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime\top}g(X_{i}))\right)^{2}\\
&\leq\underbrace{\left\{K\left(\frac{A}{4}\right)^{2}+K\left(\frac{A}{4}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|g(X_{i})\|_{2}^{2}+A^{2}B^{2}+B^{2}\right\}}_{G_{n}^{2}}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

Therefore, if
\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon/G_{n}
, then
\|g^{(k)}_{\theta}-g^{(k)}_{\theta^{\prime}}\|_{n}\leq\epsilon
.
This implies that the
\epsilon
-covering number of
{\mathcal{G}}_{k}
is bounded by the
\epsilon/G_{n}
-covering number of the radius
B
ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Using standard bounded ball covering number results (see
Wainwright 2019
for example), the
\epsilon
-covering of a radius
B
ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{(K-1)(d+3)}
is at most

\left(1+\frac{2B}{\epsilon}\right)^{(K-1)(d+3)}.

Therefore, we have

{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon,{\mathcal{G}}_{k},\|\cdot\|_{n})\leq\left(1+\frac{2BG_{n}}{\epsilon}\right)^{(K-1)(d+3)}.

This combines with (
53
) gives

\log{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon;{\mathcal{F}},\|\cdot\|_{n})\leq K(K-1)(d+3)\log\left(1+\frac{2BG_{n}}{\epsilon}\right),

which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

.

.

, we have

.

\sup_{f,g\in{\mathcal{F}}}\|f-g\|_{n}\leq M

.

and
