On a PDE model for Learning in Stochastic Market Entry Games
Esther Bou Dagher, Misha Perepelitsa, Ewelina Zatorska

Abstract
We study a continuum model for stochastic reinforcement learning in repeated market entry games. Starting from a discrete-time microscopic learning rule, we derive a Fokker–Planck-type equation for the distribution of agents’ propensities and, using a kinetic closure, obtain a nonlinear one-particle equation of a mean-field type. For the resulting Cauchy problem, we prove existence and uniqueness of solutions and analyze their long-time behavior. The PDE captures two key phenomena observed in market entry dynamics: aggregate learning (the average number of entrants approaches market capacity) and sorting (propensities concentrate near extreme behaviors). The model also yields explicit characteristic time scales, showing that aggregate learning occurs faster than sorting, in agreement with experimental and computational evidence.

Introduction

A class of games in the study of social and economic behavior, called market entry games, describes a conflict situation in which players (agents) choose between two strategies: enter the market or stay out. The agent’s payoff is determined solely by the number of agents who decide to enter. A well-known example of this class of games is the El Farol Bar game, introduced by Arthur
[
1
]
.

A typical game of this type has a single symmetric mixed equilibrium, as well as pure-strategy and mixed asymmetric equilibria.

Naturally, one is interested in whether any of the equilibrium strategies emerge when the game is played repeatedly by agents who, independently of each other, attempt to adapt to changing “market conditions”.

Reinforcement learning is a fundamental modeling approach for market adaptation, and more generally serves as a fundamental paradigm for animal and human behavior, see for example
[
15
]
and references therein. To describe reinforcement learning processes, one introduces individual propensities of agents to play particular strategies. These propensities are updated after each round of the game and are determined by the agent’s payoffs, as in the basic reinforcement learning model introduced by Roth and Erev
[
23
]
, or in the related variant, fictitious stochastic play, introduced by Fudenberg and Levine
[
14
]
. Experimental studies of such models are reported in
[
23
,
11
,
12
]
.
The following patterns of behavior are typically observed in experimental studies of learning models in the long run
[
10
]
:
a) the average number of entries per round quickly approaches the market capacity; this pattern is referred to as “aggregate learning”;
b) over a long run of repeated plays, players’ strategies converge to a pure-strategy equilibrium compatible with the market capacity; this pattern is called “sorting”.

Both phenomena are ubiquitous in market entry games in which agents use either basic reinforcement learning or fictitious stochastic play. Experimental studies show that aggregate learning emerges relatively quickly, whereas sorting takes a much longer time to occur; see Duffy and Hopkins
[
10
]
.

The analytic treatment of learning models was carried out within the framework of stochastic approximation developed by Benaïm
[
3
]
, and further applied by Duffy and Hopkins
[
10
]
and Whitehead
[
25
]
. The authors prove that, in repeated market entry games with either basic reinforcement learning or fictitious stochastic play, and under fairly general conditions, agents’ strategies converge with probability one to a pure-strategy equilibrium. In that approach, learning models are viewed as stochastic dynamical systems of size
M
(the number of agents), describing the individual propensities of all agents under a stochastic updating rule. The stochastic dynamical system is approximated by a deterministic system of ODEs whose steady states correspond to Nash equilibria, with pure-strategy equilibria being stable and mixed equilibria unstable.

Another type of learning in games and the corresponding representation of such processes through kinetic PDEs were considered in
[
20
,
21
]
, where players are assumed to interact by playing a symmetric
2\times 2
game in randomly matched pairs. The learning is implemented either by reinforcing the strategy of a player according to the accumulated payoff or by increasing/decreasing the number of players using the strategy.

The purpose of this paper is twofold. First, we consider a Fokker–Planck equation for the function
W
, which describes the distribution of the propensities of all agents. This equation is derived as an approximation of the distribution generated by the discrete-time stochastic learning process. Then, following a kinetic closure approach based on the independence hypothesis, we reduce the dimensionality of the model and obtain a kinetic equation for the distribution of the propensity of a randomly selected agent (the one-particle distribution function).
The resulting equation, (
12
), is a non-linear transport-diffusion equation in which both the transport and diffusion coefficients are given by certain moments of the kinetic function.
This equation is a mean-field type equation where the transport velocity is defined by an expected (average) payoff.

We prove an existence and uniqueness theorem for the Cauchy problem for equation (
12
) and show that the asymptotic behavior of solutions as
t\to+\infty
corresponds to a state in which the average number of entrants is close to the market capacity, see the next section for details. That is, it corresponds to aggregate learning. Moreover, agents’ propensities become concentrated near the extreme values, corresponding to sorting. Thus, the one-particle, kinetic PDE effectively captures the asymptotic behavior of the stochastic learning process.

Second, the kinetic PDE approach provides formulas for the characteristic time scales of aggregate learning and sorting in terms of the microscopic parameters of the model. Using explicit expressions for the drift and diffusion coefficients in the partial differential equation, we find that the time scale of aggregate learning is shorter than that of sorting, in agreement with the experimental studies mentioned above.

Kinetic equation (
12
) is similar to the kinetic equation (3.1) obtained in
[
8
]
which was derived as a mean-field model for the behavior of rational agents interacting in a game-theoretic framework. In their model, the players change strategies in the direction of the gradient of the expected payoff with an exogenously added noise term. Conceptually, this is close to the reinforcement learning model considered in this paper. The difference is that in our model the players base their behavior on instantaneous payoffs rather than expected payoffs. Thus, the diffusion in our equation reflects the randomness of the actual actions taken by the players and depends functionally on the strategies currently selected by the players, rather than being due to a constant-variance white noise added to the mean field, as in
[
8
]
.

In many cases of interacting particle systems, the long-time behavior of solutions of kinetic equations can be characterized through a variational structure, from which convergence to equilibrium can be derived rather directly; see, for example
[
7
,
5
,
13
,
8
,
4
,
2
]
and
[
19
]
for a general variational structure/gradient flow viewpoint behind long-time asymptotics.

The equation we study, however, does not possess a natural “free energy” or Lyapunov functional that is minimized along trajectories and whose minimizers correspond to equilibrium states of learning and sorting. More precisely, there exists a non-decreasing “energy” functional, but its minima do not necessarily coincide with equilibrium states.

Moreover, the moments of the kinetic distribution that describe learning and sorting do not satisfy a closed system of ODEs. As a result, the dynamics cannot be reduced to a finite-dimensional system governing these aggregate quantities.

A natural starting point for analyzing the asymptotic behavior is the “energy” inequality, (
22
), which holds for generic solutions of the kinetic equation. It states that a weighted
L^{2}
norm of the solution decreases in time, with the rate given by the
L^{2}
norm of the its gradient.
The classical approach would be to estimate the rate using the Nash inequality and evaluate the rate of decay as a function of time and conclude that the
L^{2}
converges to zero. In our model, however, the diffusion coefficient is a certain moment in
x
–variable of the solution itself. Its time dependence is not known known a-priori, and this direct approach does not produce a conclusive result.
What we found is that instead of the
L^{2}
norm, we can consider the product of the weighted
L^{2}
norm and a moment of the solution:

\phi(t){}={}\beta(t)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}p^{2}(x)f^{2}(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}\right)^{3},\quad\beta(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)(1-p(x))f(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x},

where function
\beta(t)
appears as a lower bound for the diffusion coefficient, see Section
5
for details. Using the information contained in the energy inequality, one can prove that the function
\phi(t)

converges to zero as
t\to+\infty
, which is sufficient to show that the mass of
f(\cdot,t)
moves to the extreme values, that is, that the sorting phenomenon takes place.

To show that the aggregate learning takes place we need to show that the average proportion of agents entering the market, expressed by the moment
\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)f(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}
lies in the “optimal” interval
((M_{c}-1)/M,M_{c}/M),
where
M_{c}
is the critical market capacity and
M
is the total number of agents. We prove that, for large time, this moment remains close, within the bounds controlled by the model parameters, to a certain value
\kappa
in the optimal interval. Hence, the proportion of agents entering the market approaches this value.

The proof is indirect and is based on the fact that the transport coefficient in the equation is proportional to the quantity

a(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)f(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}-\kappa

(1)

measuring the deviation of the proportion of agents entering the market from
\kappa.
We show first, that either
a(t)
is sufficiently small or it has a limit at
t=+\infty.
Then, we use a contradiction-type argument to prove that if this limit is positive (negative), then the mass of
f(\cdot,t)
moves to the right (left) extreme value of
x,
in which case
a(t)
must be negative (positive).

Proving this fact, requires a careful study of the balance between the transport and the diffusion parts of the equation. For this reason, we restrict the model parameters, see Theorem
5.1
, to a regime in which transport “dominates” diffusion. At the same time we need to restrict the probability function
p(x),

appearing in (
1
) to have a positive, but arbitrarily small, lower bound. With these assumptions, we use the kinetic equation to evaluate the moment
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x,t)\psi(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}
where
\psi(x,t)
is a suitable “test” function solving a transport PDE, with the transport part as in the kinetic equation, and with the initial data growing exponentially at
x=\pm\infty.
Using the pointwise estimates on function
\psi
and its derivatives, we estimate the effect
of diffusion on the moment
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x,t)\psi(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}
as being at most exponential in time. Comparing this to the values of function
\psi(x,t)
which also grows exponentially but at a strictly faster rate,
we conclude that the mass of
f(\cdot,t)
to the left (right) of an arbitrary point
x_{0}
decreases to zero as time increases. Thus, this yields the desired conclusion.

The paper is organized as follows.

In Section
2
, we introduce the formal definition of the market entry game. In Section
3
, we derive the corresponding kinetic equation. In Section
4
, we establish the existence and uniqueness of strong solutions to the associated Cauchy problem.
We start the section by deriving several a priori estimates. These estimates are also used to prove uniqueness of strong solutions and, in Section
5
, to analyze the long-time behavior of solutions.
The strategy follows a classical approach: we begin by regularizing the potentially degenerate diffusion coefficient by a strictly positive one and then linearize the equation and apply standard existence results for linear parabolic equations.
Next, using a fixed-point argument, we construct solutions to the nonlinear problem with regularized diffusion. Finally, we pass to the limit in the regularization parameter and recover a solution to the original problem.

Section
5
is devoted to the study of long-time behaviour of our solution. We establish that, asymptotically, the solution of the kinetic PDE captures the state of aggregate learning and sorting, the latter being considerably more delicate.

Reinforcement learning process

We follow Erev and Rapoport
[
11
]
for the exposition of the Market Entry Game. There are
M
agents participating in the game. Let
\delta^{i}
denote the indicator function for agent
i
to enter the game:
\delta^{i}=1
if the agent enters, and
\delta^{i}=0,
otherwise. Let
M_{c}\geq 1
be the critical capacity of the market, which separates
the under-populated market (positive effect) from the over-populated market
(negative effect). Let
m
be the number of agents who enter the market,
h
be the payoff per unit of difference of
m
from the critical capacity
M_{c},
and
v>0
be the compensation for participating in the game. The payoff to agent
i
to defined to be equal to

\pi^{i}{}={}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v&\mbox{if }\delta^{i}=0,\\
v+h(M_{c}-m)&\mbox{if }\delta^{i}=1.\end{array}\right.

(2)

This game has a number of pure and mixed strategy Nash equilibria. The following description is taken from
[
10
]
. If
M_{c},
which we shall assume in this paper, is an integer, any profile of pure strategies which is consistent with either
M_{c}
or
M_{c}-1
entrants is a Nash equilibrium.

Moreover, there is a continuum of equilibria in which
M_{c}-1
players enter,
M-M_{c}
stay out, and one player enters with any probability.

Additionally, for
M_{c}>1,
there is a symmetric Nash equilibrium. This has the form

\bar{p}^{i}=\frac{M_{c}-1}{M-1},\quad i=1,\ldots,M,

where
\bar{p}^{i}
is the probability to enter for player
i
. Note that the expected number of entrants in the symmetric mixed equilibrium is

M(M_{c}-1)/(M-1)\in(M_{c}-1,M_{c}).
There are additional mixed asymmetric mixed equlibria of the form
j<M_{c}-1
players enter with probability one,
k<M-M_{c}
players stay out and the remaining
M-j-k
players enter with probability
(M_{c}-1-j)/(M-j-k-1).
In this case, the expected number of entrants is in the range
(M_{c}-1,M_{c}).

In all the equilibria mentioned above the expected number of entrants is between
M_{c}-1

and
M_{c}.

In the reinforcement learning process, the game is played repeatedly, and the state of the agent
i,
is defined by the propensities to enter and stay out after
n^{th}
round of the game:

(X^{i}_{1,n},X^{i}_{2,n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

The propensities change over one round of the game by the amount of payoff:

X^{i}_{1,n+1}=X^{i}_{1,n}+h(v+M_{c}-m_{n}),\quad X^{i}_{2,n+1}=X^{i}_{2,n},

if agent
i
enters the market, and

X^{i}_{1,n+1}=X^{i}_{1,n},\quad X^{i}_{2,n+1}=X^{i}_{2,n}+v,

if agent
i
doesn’t enter. Here,
m_{n}
is the number of agents who entered the market at round
n+1.

Furthermore, the model specifies the probability that agent
i
enters the market at each round as a function of propensity. For example, the probability used in
[
10
]
,

\frac{X^{i}_{1,n}}{X^{i}_{1,n}+X^{i}_{2,n}}.

To simplify the presentation, we assume that in (
2
) and other formulas,
v=0.
In this case, the propensity to stay out does not change in time:
X^{i}_{2,n}{}={}X^{i}_{2,0}.
Furthermore, we assume that initially,
X^{i}_{2,0}
are all equal to some
x_{0}.
Consequently, we only need to consider one propensity, the propensity to enter the market,
X^{i}_{1,n}
which we denote by

X^{i}_{n}.

Under such assumptions, all agents use the same function to map propensities to probabilities. Given propensity
X^{i}_{n}=x
of agent
i,
the probability to enter for this player is

p(x)=\frac{x}{x+x_{0}},

where it is assumed that propensities cannot take negative values.
In fact, the explicit formula for the probability function will not be needed in our analysis, and we opt to use a generic monotonely increasing function
p=p(x)
with values in the interval
(0,1]
defined on all of
x\in\mathbb{R},
eliminating by this the non-negativity restriction on propensities.

In this notation, the probability to enter for agent
i
equals
p(X^{i}_{n}).

Summarizing the model, the state of agent
i
is described by propensity
X^{i}_{n},
and the rule for its update is given as

X^{i}_{n+1}{}={}X^{i}_{n}{}+{}h(M_{c}-m_{n})\delta^{i}_{n},

(M1)

where
\delta^{i}_{n}
is the indicator function of the action of player
i
in
n^{th}
game,
and
m_{n}
is the number of agent who enter the game:
m_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\delta^{i}_{n}
where
\delta^{i}_{n}
are independent random variables such that
\mbox{\rm Prob}(\delta^{i}_{n}=1){}={}1-\mbox{\rm Prob}(\delta^{i}_{n}=0)=p(X^{i}_{n}).

It will be convenient for analysis to use a time variable
t
with arbitrary units and time step
\tau
and assume that rounds of the game occur at intervals
t_{n}=\tau n,\,n\in\mathbb{N}.
This will provide us with the a control parameter
\frac{1}{\tau},
the number of rounds per unit of time.

We say that the system moves, on average, to a state of aggregate learning if the expected number of agents entering the market approaches the interval of values in the Nash equilibria,
[M_{c}-1,M_{c}]
:

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[m_{n}\right]\geq M_{c}-1,\quad\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[m_{n}\right]\leq M_{c}.

(3)

The sorting of agents in the systems is described by the property that asymptotically agents are separated into groups with propensities equal to either
+\infty,
or
-\infty:

\forall R>0,\,i=1..M,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\{X^{i}_{n}\in(-R,R)\}\right]{}={}0.

(4)

Kinetic PDEs for the reinforcement learning

Denote by

W(\bar{x},t_{n}),\quad\bar{x}{}={}(x^{1},...,x^{M}),

the probability density function of the random vector
X_{n}.

The change of
W
from time
t_{n}
to

is described by

W
over a time step
\tau
can be used to obtain an asymptotic expansion of

W(\bar{x},t_{n+1})-W(\bar{x},t_{n}){}={}-h\sum_{i}\partial_{x^{i}}(A_{i}(\bar{x})W)+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\sum_{i,k}\partial^{2}_{x^{i}x^{k}}(D_{ik}(\bar{x})W){}+{}O(h^{3}),

where

\displaystyle A_{i}(\bar{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(M_{c}-m)\delta^{i}\,|\,X=\bar{x}\right],

\displaystyle D_{ik}(\bar{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(M_{c}-m)^{2}\delta^{i}\delta^{k}\,|\,X=\bar{x}\right],\quad i,k=1..M.

Here,
\{\delta^{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}

is a mutually independent set of random variables with values in
\{0,1\},
with
P(\delta^{i}=1)=p(x^{i}),

i=1..M,

m=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\delta^{i},

The first order approximation coefficients

are precisely the expected payoffs to players over one round of the play. Thus, at the first order of approximation the system is driven by the mean field dynamics.

To obtain a time-continuous equation for

we
assume that
W(\bar{x},t_{n})
is the restriction at

of a smooth density function
W(\bar{x},t)
defined for all times.
Under this assumption we derive the following kinetic equation for

\partial_{t}W+\frac{h}{\tau}\sum_{i}\partial_{x^{i}}(A_{i}(\bar{x})W)-\frac{h^{2}}{2\tau}\sum_{i,k}\partial^{2}_{x^{i}x^{k}}(D_{ik}(\bar{x})W){}={}\frac{O(h^{3})}{\tau}.

(5)

It can be verified that the matrix
D,
with entries
\{D_{ik}\},
is a symmetric and positive semi-definite matrix:

z^{T}Dz{}={}\mathbb{E}\left[(M_{c}-m)^{2}(z\cdot\delta)^{2}\,|\,X=\bar{x}\right]{}\geq{}0,\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{R}^{M}.

Moreover,
D
is positive definite unless the system is in the state where the number of market entrants

equals the critical capacity
M_{c}.

Thus, the third term in (
5

We will approximate (
5
) by keeping terms of order

h/\tau
and

h^{2}/\tau,
as these govern the leading-order dynamics of the underlying time-discrete stochastic process, and truncate higher-order terms. That is, we assume that

h,\tau\ll 1,\quad\frac{h^{2}}{\tau}\sim 1.

(6)

Without loss of generality, we will also set

\frac{h^{2}}{\tau}=1.

Thus, we arrive at equation

\partial_{t}W+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau}}\sum_{i}\partial_{x^{i}}(A_{i}(\bar{x})W)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,k}\partial^{2}_{x^{i}x^{k}}(D_{ik}(\bar{x})W){}={}0.

(7)

The results of this paper indicate that this truncation provides a good approximation: the asymptotic behavior of solutions to the truncated PDE agrees with that of the original stochastic process.

In the next step, we reduce the dimension of the problem by deriving a kinetic equation for the probability density of the propensity of a randomly selected agent (the one-particle distribution function), using an independence hypothesis.

f
be the one-particle distribution function

where

g(x,y,t)=\frac{1}{M(M-1)}\sum_{j,k:j\not=k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M-2}}W(\bar{x})\big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{j}=x\\

where
\bar{x}^{jk}

and

and
\bar{x}^{ijk}

and

In fact, only the second condition suffices, because the first follows from that. The independence condition is a plausible assumption to make, when the number of players is large, since the players interact with each other only through the mean attendance

Using this hypothesis in (

\kappa{}={}\frac{M_{c}-1}{M-1}\in[0,1],

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M-1}}\sum_{i}\partial_{x^{i}}(A_{i}(\bar{x})W)\big|_{x^{j}=x}\,{\rm d}\bar{x}^{j}{}={}(M-1)a(t)\partial_{x}(p(x)f(x,t)).

The computation for the diffusion term is similar.

\begin{split}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M-1}}\sum_{i,k}\partial^{2}_{x^{i}x^{k}}(D_{ik}(\bar{x})W)\big|_{x^{j}=x}\,{\rm d}\bar{x}^{j}{}&={}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M-1}}\partial^{2}_{x^{j}x^{j}}(D_{jj}(\bar{x})W)\big|_{x^{j}=x}\,{\rm d}\bar{x}^{j}\\
{}&={}\partial^{2}_{xx}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M-1}}D_{jj}(\bar{x})W\big|_{x^{j}=x}\,{\rm d}\bar{x}^{j}.\end{split}

We can write

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M-1}}D_{jj}(\bar{x})W\big|_{x^{j}=x}\,{\rm d}\bar{x}^{j}{}\\
&={}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[(M_{c}-m)^{2}(\delta^{j})^{2}\,|\,X=\bar{x}\right]W\big|_{x^{j}=x}\,{\rm d}\bar{x}^{j}\\
{}&={}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(M_{c}^{2}-2M_{c}\sum_{i}\delta^{i}+\sum_{i,k}\delta^{i}\delta^{k}\right)\delta^{j}\,|\,X=\bar{x}\right]W\big|_{x^{j}=x}\,{\rm d}\bar{x}^{j}\\
{}&={}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M-1}}\left(M_{c}^{2}p(x^{j})-2M_{c}p(x^{j})-2M_{c}\sum_{i:i\not=j}p(x^{i})p(x^{j})+p(x^{j})\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+{3}p(x^{j})\sum_{i:i\not=j}p(x^{i})+\sum_{i,k:i\not=k\not=j}p(x^{i})p(x^{k})p(x^{j})\right)W\big|_{x^{j}=x}\,{\rm d}\bar{x}^{j}\\
{}&={}(M_{c}^{2}-2M_{c}+1)p(x)f(x,t){}+{}({3}-2M_{c})p(x)(M-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(y)g(x,y,t)\,{\rm d}{y}\\
{}&\qquad\qquad\qquad+{}p(x)(M-1)(M-2)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}p(y)p(z){l}(x,y,z,t)\,{\rm d}{y}\,{\rm d}z.\end{split}

Note that, the factor
3
comes from the three ways of matching indices in

\sum_{i,k}\delta^{i}\delta^{k}\delta^{j}
: namely
i=j
,
k=j
, or
i=k
(with the remaining index different from
j
).

Using the closure relations (
11
), we obtain from the last equality:

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M-1}}D_{jj}(\bar{x})W\big|_{x^{j}=x}\,{\rm d}\bar{x}^{j}{}\\
&\quad={}\left((M_{c}-1)^{2}+(3-2M_{c})(M-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)f(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}\right.\\
&{}\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+{}(M-1)(M-2)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)f(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}\right)^{2}\right)p(x)f(x,t)\\
{}&\quad={}\left[(M-1)^{2}(\kappa-\int_{\mathbb{R}}pf\ {\rm d}{x})^{2}+(M-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}}pf\ {\rm d}{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-p)f\ {\rm d}{x}\right]p(x)f(x,t)\\
{}&\quad={}\left[(M-1)^{2}a(t)^{2}+(M-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}}pf\ {\rm d}{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-p)f\ {\rm d}{x}\right]p(x)f(x,t).\end{split}

With that, the diffusion term becomes

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M-1}}\sum_{i,k}\partial^{2}_{x^{i}x^{k}}(D_{ik}(\bar{x})W)\big|_{x^{j}=x}\,{\rm d}\bar{x}^{j}{}={}\left((M-1)^{2}a^{2}(t)+(M-1)b(t)\right)\partial^{2}_{xx}(p(x)f(x,t)),

where

b(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)f(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-p(x))f(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}.

Then, the equation for the kinetic density
f
is

\partial_{t}f+\frac{(M-1)}{\sqrt{\tau}}a(t)\partial_{x}(pf)-\frac{(M-1)^{2}}{2}\left(a^{2}(t)+\frac{1}{M-1}b(t)\right)\partial^{2}_{xx}(pf){}={}0,

(12)

for
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

The aggregate learning and the sorting conditions (
3
), (
4
)
are expressed in terms of function
f(x,t)
as

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)f(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}\geq\frac{M_{c}-1}{M},\quad\limsup_{t\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)f(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}\leq\frac{M_{c}}{M},

(13)

and

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{-R}^{R}f(x,t)\,{\rm d}{x}=0,\quad\forall R>0,

(14)

respectively.

4
Existence results

In this section, we show the existence and uniqueness of the strong solutions to the Cauchy problem for

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}f+\frac{(M-1)}{\sqrt{\tau}}a(t)\partial_{x}(pf)-\frac{(M-1)^{2}}{2}\left(a^{2}(t)+\frac{1}{M-1}b(t)\right)\partial^{2}_{xx}(pf){}={}0,\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+},\\
f(x,0)=f_{0}(x),\end{array}\right.

(15)

where

a(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\kappa-p(x))f(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x},

(16)

and

b(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)f(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-p(x))f(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}.

(17)

We start with some a-priori estimates verified by a generic sufficiently smooth solution to motivate our approach and further regularizations of the system. Existence of solution satisfying these a-priori estimates is proven in Theorem
4.4
.

4.1
A-priori estimates

We assume

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

,

Then, every sufficiently regular

\begin{split}|a(t)|<1,\quad 0\leq b(t)\leq 1,\quad\forall t\in[0,T].\end{split}

(21)

Moreover,

\begin{split}&\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)f^{2}(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{(M-1)^{2}}{2}\left(a^{2}(t)+\frac{1}{M-1}b(t)\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{x}(p(x)f(x,t))|^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t\\
&\hskip 199.16928pt\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)f_{0}^{2}(x)\ {\rm d}{x}.\end{split}

Proof.

The first equality follows from integration of equation (

The inequality (

∎

The next set of a-priori estimates, concerns quantities

and

\begin{split}\alpha(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)f(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x},\quad\beta(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-p(x))p(x)f(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}.\end{split}

We have the following result.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let assumptions of Lemma

There is

that depends only on

|\alpha^{\prime}(t)|\leq c_{0}\beta(t),\quad|\beta^{\prime}(t)|{}\leq{}c_{0}\beta(t),\quad t>0.

(25)

Proof.

From (

\begin{split}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\alpha(t)\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\beta(t)\leq 1.\end{split}

Because for all

∎

In particular, it follows from the above lemma that

From the previous section, it follows that (

The assumption for the exponential decay of initial data at infinity is quite restrictive, and in fact could be replaced by polynomial decay. The exponential decay plays a role only in the further parts of our manuscript devoted to the long-time behavior.

Remark 4.6

then asymptotically,

verifies the aggregate learning condition (

Our first long-time asymptotic result demonstrates that diffusion drives all mass towards extreme values

This is established in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let assumptions of Theorem
5.1
part (i) be satisfied.
Then, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)^{\frac{7}{4}}(1-p(x))^{\frac{1}{4}}f^{\frac{7}{4}}(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{as}\quad t\to+\infty.

(88)

Proof.

Let
g
be a solution of (
33
) given in Theorem (
4.4
). Given the regularity of the solution
g
, the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{2}(x,t)\ {\rm d}{x}
is continuous and weakly differentiable function, with

\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}{}={}2\int_{\mathbb{R}}g\partial_{t}g\ {\rm d}{x}\in L^{2}((0,T)),\quad\forall T>0.

Multiplying equation (
33
) by

and integrating by parts, we get

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}+\frac{c(t)}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)\partial_{x}g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}+\frac{d(t)}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p^{\prime}(x)\partial_{x}g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}+d(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)(\partial_{x}g)^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}=0.

Integrating by parts again, we get

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}-\frac{c(t)}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p^{\prime}(x)g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}-\frac{d(t)}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p^{\prime\prime}(x)g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}+d(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)(\partial_{x}g)^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}=0.

(89)

Hence,

\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}\leq c(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}}p^{\prime}(x)g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}+d(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}}p^{\prime\prime}(x)g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}.

(90)

From this, we obtain inequality

\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}\right)_{+}\leq C\left(|c(t)|+d(t)\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x},

(91)

where

is the maximum of
p^{\prime}
and
p^{\prime\prime}.

Multiplying equation (
33
) by
g/p
and integrating by parts we obtain estimate

{\rm ess}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{g^{2}}{p(x)}\ {\rm d}{x}{}+{}2\int_{0}^{T}d(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\partial_{x}g)^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t{}\leq{}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{g_{0}^{2}}{p}\ {\rm d}{x}.

Using the Nash inequality

\|g(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{6}{}\leq C\|g(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{4}\|\partial_{x}g(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2},\quad\forall t>0

we get

\int_{0}^{T}d(t)\|g(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{6}{}\,{\rm d}t\leq{}C\int_{0}^{T}d(t)\|g(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{4}\|\partial_{x}g(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\ {\rm d}t\\
\leq C{\rm ess}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|g(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{4}\int_{0}^{T}d(t)\|\partial_{x}g(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\ {\rm d}t.

Since
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g|\ {\rm d}{x}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f|\ {\rm d}{x}\leq 1,
and using estimate (
92
), we can find

independent of

such that

\int_{0}^{T}d(t)\|g(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{6}\ {\rm d}t\leq C,

meaning that

Since
\beta(t)
is uniformly bounded and
\beta(t)\leq b(t)\leq Cd(t),

\phi(t):=\beta(t)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}\right)^{3}\in L^{1}(0,\infty).

Moreover, we can compute

(94)

Recall that

Using this and (

\begin{split}\left(\phi^{\prime}(t)\right)_{+}\leq C\beta(t)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}\right)^{3}+3C\beta(t)\left(|c(t)|+d(t)\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}\right)^{3}\\
{}\leq{}C\beta(t)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}\right)^{3}{}\leq{}C\phi(t)\in L^{1}(0,\infty).\end{split}

Let
t_{1}<t_{2}
and
\phi(t_{2})\geq\phi(t_{1}).
By the fundamental theorem of calculus

\phi(t_{2})-\phi(t_{1})=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\phi^{\prime}(t)\,{\rm d}t\leq\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}(\phi^{\prime}(t))_{+}\,{\rm d}t.

(95)

Because

there is a sequence of points

such that

does not converge to

From these two sequences we can select monotone subsequences

By Hölder’s inequality, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)^{\frac{7}{4}}(1-p(x))^{\frac{1}{4}}f^{\frac{7}{4}}\ {\rm d}{x}\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)(1-p(x))f\ {\rm d}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}p^{2}(x)f^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}\right)^{\frac{3}{4}}\\

\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)^{\frac{7}{4}}(1-p(x))^{\frac{1}{4}}f^{\frac{7}{4}}\ {\rm d}{x}\rightarrow 0

Proof.

The proof uses a contradiction argument and is divided into several steps.

Step 1.
Setup of the contradiction argument.

After fixing
C_{0}
,
\hat{c_{p}}
and
\tau
so that condition (
86
) is satisfied, we will show that assumption

\lim_{t\to\infty}a(t)>\frac{\hat{c}_{p}}{p_{min}^{4}}\sqrt{\tau},\quad\left(\mbox{\rm or}\,\lim_{t\to\infty}a(t)<-\frac{\hat{c}_{p}}{p_{min}^{4}}\sqrt{\tau}\right)

(102)

necessarily implies that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{x<x_{0}}f(x,t)\,{\rm d}{x}{}={}0,\quad\left(\mbox{\rm or}\,\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{x>x_{0}}f(x,t)\,{\rm d}{x}{}={}0\right),

(103)

for any
x_{0}.
As we shall see below, the first inequality in (
102
) and the first limit in (
103
) then imply
\lim_{t\to\infty}a(t)<0,
which is a contradiction.

Indeed, it is enough to take
x_{0}
in (
103
) large enough, so that
p(x_{0})>\kappa

and write

a(t)=\kappa-\int_{\mathbb{R}}pf\ {\rm d}{x}{}={}\kappa-\int_{x<x_{0}}pf\,{\rm d}{x}-\int_{x\geq x_{0}}pf\,{\rm d}{x}{}\leq{}\kappa-\int_{x<x_{0}}pf\,{\rm d}{x}-p(x_{0})\int_{x\geq x_{0}}f\,{\rm d}{x}\\
{}={}\kappa-\int_{x<x_{0}}pf\,{\rm d}{x}-p(x_{0})\left(1-\int_{x<x_{0}}f\,{\rm d}{x}\right)\\
{}={}\kappa-p(x_{0})+p(x_{0})\int_{x<x_{0}}f\,{\rm d}{x}-\int_{x<x_{0}}pf{\rm d}{x}{}\to{}\kappa-p(x_{0})<0,

where we used

\int_{x<x_{0}}f\,{\rm d}{x}{}+{}\int_{x>x_{0}}f\,{\rm d}{x}=1.

This implies
\lim_{t\to\infty}a(t)<0,
contradicting the assumptions.
Similarly, one gets a contradiction starting with the second inequality in (
102
) and (
103
). For if
p_{min}<\kappa,
there is
x_{0}
such that
p(x_{0})<\kappa,
and we have

a(t)=\kappa-\int_{\mathbb{R}}pf\ {\rm d}{x}{}={}\kappa-\int_{x<x_{0}}pf\,{\rm d}{x}-\int_{x\geq x_{0}}pf\,{\rm d}{x}{}\geq{}\kappa-p(x_{0})\int_{x<x_{0}}f\,{\rm d}{x}-\int_{x\geq x_{0}}pf\,{\rm d}{x}\\
{}={}\kappa-p(x_{0})\left(1-\int_{x\geq x_{0}}f\,{\rm d}{x}\right)-\int_{x\geq x_{0}}pf\,{\rm d}{x}{}\to{}\kappa-p(x_{0})>0,

contradicting the fact that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}a(t)<0.

Because, as we have already shown,
\int_{[-R,R]}f(x,t)\,{\rm d}{x}\to 0,
for any
R>0,
it suffices to consider only one value of
x_{0}.
It will be chosen later.

Step 2.
Rescaling of the equation and of the test function.

We rescale the time
t
and change it to a new variable
s
defined by
t=\frac{\sqrt{\tau}}{M-1}s.
Let
h(x,s){}={}f(x,t)
, then
h
solves equation

\partial_{s}h{}+{}\bar{a}(s)\partial_{x}(ph){}-{}\frac{\sqrt{\tau}(M-1)}{2}\left(\bar{a}^{2}(s){}+{}\frac{\bar{b}(s)}{M-1}\right)\partial_{xx}^{2}(ph){}={}0,

(104)

where we set
\bar{a}(s){}={}a\left(\frac{\sqrt{\tau}}{M-1}s\right)
and
\bar{b}(s){}={}b\left(\frac{\sqrt{\tau}}{M-1}s\right).
We will also denote

\bar{d}(s){}={}\frac{\sqrt{\tau}(M-1)}{2}\left(\bar{a}^{2}(s){}+{}\frac{\bar{b}(s)}{M-1}\right).

(105)

First, observe that because
\bar{d}(s)>0
for
s\in[0,S]
, the function
h(x,s)
verifies an inequality similar to one in Lemma
5.7
on every interval
s\in[0,S]:

{\rm ess}\sup_{s\in[0,S]}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{4|x|}h^{2}(x,s)\ {\rm d}{x}{}+{}\int_{0}^{S}\!\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{4|x|}|\partial_{x}(p(x)h(x,s))|^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}s<C_{S},

(106)

for some
C_{S}
that depends on
(S,M,\tau,p,f_{0}).

In particular, the same way we deduced (
100
), we show that
h(x,s)
is integrable in
x
with weight
e^{|x|},

for every
s\geq 0
and there is a bound

\sup_{s\in[0,S]}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{|x|}h(x,s)\ {\rm d}{x}{}\leq{}C_{S},

(107)

for some
C_{S}.

We now look for a test function
\phi
that will allow us to get an analogue of estimate (
107
) in the reference system moving with the transport velocity
\bar{a}(s)p(x)
. To this purpose consider an even,
C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
function
\psi
with values

\psi(x){}={}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}e^{x}&x\geq 2\\
1&0\leq x\leq 1\end{array}\right.

and smoothly and monotonically interpolated on the intervals
[-2,-1]
and
[1,2].
Let

\begin{split}C_{0}=\max\left\{\sup_{x}|\partial_{x}\log\psi(x)|,\sup_{x}|\partial_{x}^{2}\log\psi(x)|\right\}<+\infty.\end{split}

(108)

The constant
C_{0}
together with
c_{p}
given in (
18
) and (
84
) can be now used to determine
\hat{c}_{p}
.
Let
\phi
be the solution of the transport equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{s}\phi(x,s){}+{}\bar{a}(s)p(x)\partial_{x}\phi(x,s){}={}0,\\
\phi(x,s_{0})=\psi(x).\end{array}\right.

(109)

We can show that that for any
s>s_{0},

\phi(x,s)
is dominated by exponential function
e^{|x|}.

Indeed, let
X(x_{0},s)
be a trajectory determined by the ODE:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dfrac{dX}{ds}=\bar{a}(s)p(X),\\
X(x_{0},s_{0}){}={}x_{0},\end{array}\right.

(110)

for some
s_{0}
large enough to be determined later on.
Thus it holds that:

|X(x_{0},s)-x_{0}|\leq\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\sup_{t\in(s_{0},s)}(|\bar{a}(t)p(X(x_{0},t))|\,{\rm d}t\leq s-s_{0},\quad\forall x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

Because
X(\cdot,s)
is a diffeomorphism
\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},
denoting by
Y(\cdot,s)
its inverse, we also have

|x-Y(x,s)|\leq s-s_{0},\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
\phi(x,s)=\psi(Y(x,s))
and
|\psi(x)|\leq{}2e^{|x|},
then

|\phi(x,s)|\leq 2e^{s-s_{0}}e^{|x|},

(111)

for all
s>s_{0}.
This means, due to (
107
), that
\phi
has a suitable growth to be a test function in the equation (
104
).

Step 3.
Derivation of the equation for
\int_{\mathbb{R}}h\phi\ {\rm d}{x}.

Let
\omega_{L}(x)
be a smooth cutoff function for interval
[-L,L],

equal
1,
for
x\in[-L,L]
and
0,
for
|x|>2L.

Multiplying equation (
104
) by
\phi\omega_{L}

we get

\partial_{s}(h\phi\omega_{L})+\bar{a}\partial_{x}(ph\phi\omega_{L}){}-{}\bar{d}\partial_{x}(\phi\omega_{L}\partial_{x}(ph)){}+{}\bar{d}\partial_{x}(ph\omega_{L}\partial_{x}\phi)\\
{}={}\bar{a}ph\phi\partial_{x}\omega_{L}{}+{}\bar{d}ph\omega_{L}\partial_{xx}^{2}\phi{}-{}\bar{d}\partial_{x}(ph)\phi\partial_{x}\omega_{L}+\bar{d}ph\partial_{x}\phi\partial_{x}\omega_{L}.

Integrating this equation over
\mathbb{R}\times[s_{0},s]
we get

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h\phi\omega_{L}\big|_{s_{0}}^{s}\ {\rm d}{x}{}=&{}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\!\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{a}(t)ph\phi\partial_{x}\omega_{L}\ {\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t{}+{}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\!\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{d}(t)ph\omega_{L}\partial_{xx}^{2}\phi\ {\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t\\
&\quad{}-{}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\!\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{d}(t)\phi\partial_{x}(ph)\partial_{x}\omega_{L}-\bar{d}(t)ph\partial_{x}\phi\partial_{x}\omega_{L}\ {\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t\\
&=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}+I_{4}.\end{split}

(112)

Using the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we can easily pass to the limit
L\to+\infty
in the left-hand side of (
112
). Indeed, since
h
is integrable with the exponential weight, see (
107
), and
\phi(x,s)
is bounded by the exponential function, see (
111
), the limit of this term equals to
\int_{\mathbb{R}}h\phi\big|_{s_{0}}^{s}\ {\rm d}{x}{}
.

We now pass to the limit
L\to+\infty
in
I_{1}
.
Using (
111
), (
106
) and the definition of function
\omega_{L},
we can estimate it as

|I_{1}|=\left|\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\!\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{a}ph\phi\partial_{x}\omega_{L}\ {\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t\right|{}\leq{}C_{s}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{4|x|}h^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2|x|}|\partial_{x}\omega_{L}|^{2}\ {\rm d}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,{\rm d}t\\
{}\leq{}C_{s}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\left(\int_{|x|>L}e^{-2|x|}\,{\rm d}{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}{\rm d}t\to 0,

(113)

as
L\to+\infty
. By the similar arguments, we can also show that
I_{3}\to 0
.

The remaining integrals,
I_{2}
and
I_{4}
, contain derivatives of
\phi,
which need to be estimated first.
For convenience we write

\partial_{x}\phi=\phi\partial_{x}\log\phi\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{xx}^{2}\phi{}={}\phi(\partial_{xx}^{2}\log\phi{}+{}(\partial_{x}\log\phi)^{2}).

Assuming now that
\partial_{x}\log\phi
and
\partial^{2}_{xx}\log\phi
are bounded, we can pass to the limit
L\to+\infty
in
I_{2}
and
I_{4}
to conclude that

\begin{split}\lim_{L\to+\infty}I_{2}=&\lim_{L\to+\infty}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{d}(t)ph\phi\omega_{L}(\partial_{xx}^{2}\log\phi{}+{}(\partial_{x}\log\phi)^{2})\ {\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t\\
=&\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{d}(t)ph\phi(\partial_{xx}^{2}\log\phi{}+{}(\partial_{x}\log\phi)^{2})\ {\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t.\end{split}

Thus, taking
L\to+\infty
in (
112
) implies that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}h\phi\big|_{s_{0}}^{s}\ {\rm d}{x}{}={}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{d}(t)ph\phi(\partial_{xx}^{2}\log\phi{}+{}(\partial_{x}\log\phi)^{2})\ {\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t,

(114)

provided that
\partial_{x}\log\phi
and
\partial^{2}_{xx}\log\phi
are bounded.

Step 4.
Estimate of
\partial_{x}\log\phi
and
\partial^{2}_{xx}\log\phi
.

Due to (
109
), the functions
u=\partial_{x}\log\phi
and
v=\partial^{2}_{xx}\log\phi,
satisfy:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{s}u+\bar{a}p\partial_{x}u{}={}-\bar{a}p^{\prime}u,\\
u(x,s_{0})=u_{0}(x)=\partial_{x}\log\psi(x),\end{array}\right.

(115)

and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{s}v{}+{}\bar{a}p\partial_{x}v{}={}-2\bar{a}p^{\prime}v-\bar{a}p^{\prime\prime}u,\\
v(x,s_{0})=v_{0}(x)=\partial^{2}_{xx}\log\psi.\end{array}\right.

(116)

Solving the equation for
u,
we get

|u(X(x_{0},s),s)|\leq|u_{0}(X(x_{0},s_{0}))|e^{\int_{s_{0}}^{s}|\bar{a}(\zeta)|p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta))\,{\rm d}\zeta}{}\leq{}C_{0}e^{\int_{s_{0}}^{s}|\bar{a}(\zeta)|p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta))\,{\rm d}\zeta}.

(117)

Similarly, we solve for
v:

\begin{split}&v(X(x_{0},s),s)\\
&\quad=e^{-2\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{a}(\zeta)p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta)){\rm d}\zeta}v_{0}(X(x_{0},s_{0}))\\
&\hskip 133.72786pt-\int_{s_{0}}^{s}e^{-2\int_{s_{0}}^{r}\bar{a}(\zeta)p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta)){\rm d}\zeta}\bar{a}(r)p^{\prime\prime}(X(x_{0},r))u(X(x_{0},r),r){\rm d}r.\end{split}

Since function
a(t)
has a limit and it is non-zero, the same applies to function
\bar{a}(s)
when
s\to+\infty.
Thus if
s_{0}
is large enough,
\bar{a}(s)
has a definite sign. We will only consider the case when
\lim_{s\to+\infty}\bar{a}(s)
is positive. The other case is considered similarly and will be omitted.
We can estimate:

\begin{split}&\left|\int_{s_{0}}^{s}e^{{-}2\int_{s_{0}}^{r}\bar{a}(\zeta)p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta)){\rm d}\zeta}\bar{a}(r)p^{\prime\prime}(X(x_{0},r))u(X(x_{0},r),r){\rm d}r\right|\\
&\leq\max_{r\in[s,s_{0}]}|u(X(x_{0},r),r)|\max_{x}\frac{|p^{\prime\prime}(x)|}{p^{\prime}(x)}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}e^{2\int_{s_{0}}^{r}{|\bar{a}(\zeta)|}p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta)){\rm d}\zeta}|\bar{a}(r)|p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},r))\,{\rm d}r\\
&=\max_{r\in[s,s_{0}]}|u(X(x_{0},r),r)|\max_{x}\frac{|p^{\prime\prime}(x)|}{p^{\prime}(x)}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}e^{2\int_{s_{0}}^{r}{|\bar{a}(\zeta)|}p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta)){\rm d}\zeta}\bar{a}(r)p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},r))\,dr\\
&={}\frac{1}{2}\max_{r\in[s,s_{0}]}|u(X(x_{0},r),r)|\max_{x}\frac{|p^{\prime\prime}(x)|}{p^{\prime}(x)}(e^{2\int_{s_{0}}^{{s}}{|\bar{a}(\zeta)|}p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta)){\rm d}\zeta}-1)\\
&\leq\max_{r\in[s,s_{0}]}|u(X(x_{0},r),r)|\max_{x}\frac{|p^{\prime\prime}(x)|}{p^{\prime}(x)}e^{2\int_{s_{0}}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}s}}{|\bar{a}(\zeta)|}p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta)){\rm d}\zeta},\end{split}

(118)

where we used that
p^{\prime}(x),p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta))\geq 0
for
\zeta\in[s_{0},+\infty)
.

From this and (
117
), we get

\begin{split}|v(X(x_{0},s),s)|{}&\leq{}\left(|{v_{0}}(X(x_{0},s_{0}))|{}+{}\max_{r\in[s,s_{0}]}|u(X(x_{0},r),r)|\max_{x}\frac{|p^{\prime\prime}(x)|}{|p^{\prime}(x)|}\right)e^{2\int_{s_{0}}^{s}{|\bar{a}(\zeta)|}p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta)){\rm d}\zeta}\\
&\leq C_{0}\left(1+\max_{x}\frac{|p^{\prime\prime}(x)|}{|p^{\prime}(x)|}\right)e^{3\int_{s_{0}}^{s}{|\bar{a}(\zeta)|}p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta))\,{\rm d}\zeta}\\
&\leq C_{0}(1+c_{p})e^{3\int_{s_{0}}^{s}{|\bar{a}(\zeta)|}p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta))\,{\rm d}\zeta}.\end{split}

(119)

We now claim for all
s,s_{0}
(
s\geq s_{0}
) and
x_{0},

\int_{s_{0}}^{s}{|\bar{a}(\zeta)|}p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta))\,{\rm d}\zeta=\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{a}(\zeta)p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta))\,{\rm d}\zeta\leq-\log p_{min}.

(120)

To see this, let us change the variable in the last integral
z=X(x_{0},\zeta).

Since
\bar{a}(\zeta)\geq 0,
on
\zeta\in[s_{0},+\infty),

we have
X(x_{0},s)\geq X(x_{0},s_{0}),
when
s>s_{0},
and so we get

\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{a}(\zeta)p^{\prime}(X(x_{0},\zeta))\,{\rm d}\zeta\\
{}={}\int_{X(x_{0},s_{0})}^{X(x_{0},s)}\frac{p^{\prime}(z)}{p(z)}\,{\rm d}z{}={}\log p(X(x_{0},s))-\log p(X(x_{0},s_{0}))\leq-\log p(X(x_{0},s_{0})){}\leq{}-\log p_{min}.

Using (
120
) in (
117
) and (
119
),
we conclude that

\sup_{s\geq s_{0},\,x\in\mathbb{R}}|u(x,s)|,\,\sup_{s\geq s_{0},\,x\in\mathbb{R}}|v(x,s)|{}\leq{}\frac{\hat{c}_{p}}{2p_{min}^{3}},\quad\hat{c}_{p}:=2C_{0}(1+c_{p}).

Thus, equation (
114
) is now established.

Step 5.
Derivation of (
103
) for the rescaled solution
h
.

From (
114
),
we conclude that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x,s)\phi(x,s)\ {\rm d}{x}
is differentiable in
s
and its derivative can be estimated as

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x,s)\phi(x,s)\ {\rm d}{x}{}\leq{}\frac{\hat{c}_{p}}{p_{min}^{3}}\bar{d}(s)\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x,s)\phi(x,s)\ {\rm d}{x}.

This implies that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x,s)\phi(x,s)\ {\rm d}{x}{}\leq{}e^{\frac{\hat{c}_{p}}{p_{min}^{3}}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{d}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(x,s_{0})\phi(x,s_{0})\ {\rm d}{x}{}={}C_{s_{0}}e^{\frac{\hat{c}_{p}}{p_{min}^{3}}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{d}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta}.

(121)

From (
110
) we obtain

X(x_{0},s)-x_{0}\geq p_{min}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{a}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta,

from which we conclude that

x-Y(x,s)\geq p_{min}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{a}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta
for any
x.

Then, for
x<-2,
using the definition of
\psi
and the fact that
Y(x,s)<x,
we get

\phi(x,s){}={}\psi(Y(x,s)){}={}e^{-Y(x,s)}{}={}e^{-Y(x,s)+x-x}{}\geq{}e^{-Y(x,s)+x}{}\geq{}e^{p_{min}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{a}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta}.

Using this in (
121
), we get

\int_{x<-2}h(x,s)\,{\rm d}{x}{}\leq{}C_{s_{0}}e^{-p_{min}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{a}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta+\frac{\hat{c}_{p}}{p_{min}^{3}}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{d}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta}.

(122)

Using the definition of
\bar{d},
(
105
), the bounds
\bar{a}^{2}(\zeta)\leq\bar{a}(\zeta),

|\bar{b}(\zeta)|\leq 1,
and
selecting
\tau
small enough so that

\sqrt{\tau}(M-1)<\frac{p_{min}^{4}}{\hat{c}_{p}},

we get

-p_{min}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{a}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta+\frac{\hat{c}_{p}}{p_{min}^{3}}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{d}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta{}\leq{}-\frac{p_{min}}{2}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{a}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta+\frac{\hat{c}_{p}}{2p_{min}^{3}}\sqrt{\tau}(s-s_{0}).

Finally, we choose
s_{0}
sufficiently large so that

\inf_{[s_{0},+\infty]}\bar{a}(s)>\frac{\hat{c}_{p}}{p_{min}^{4}}\sqrt{\tau},

then, as
s\to+\infty
, we have

-\frac{p_{min}}{2}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{a}(\zeta)\,{\rm d}\zeta+\frac{\hat{c}_{p}}{2p_{min}^{3}}\sqrt{\tau}(s-s_{0})\leq\left(-\frac{p_{min}}{2}\inf_{[s_{0},+\infty]}\bar{a}(s)+\frac{\hat{c}_{p}}{2p_{min}^{3}}\sqrt{\tau}\right)(s-s_{0})\to-\infty,

and the integral on the left-hand side of (
122
) converges to zero.
Thus, we obtain (
103
) with
x_{0}=-2.
This leads to a contradiction, as was noted above.

Step 6.
Derivation of the second inequality in (
103
).

Let be
s_{0}
is chosen sufficiently large so that

\sup_{[s_{0},+\infty]}\bar{a}(s)<-\frac{\hat{c}_{p}}{p_{min}^{4}}\sqrt{\tau}.

From the ODE for
X(x_{0},s)
we obtain

X(x_{0},s)-x_{0}\leq p_{min}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{a}(s)\,{\rm d}s,

from which we conclude that

x-Y(x,s)\leq p_{min}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{a}(s)\,{\rm d}s
for any
x.

Then, for
x>2,
using the definition of
\psi
and the fact that
Y(x,s)>x,
we get

\phi(x,s){}={}\psi(Y(x,s)){}={}e^{Y(x,s)}{}={}e^{Y(x,s)-x+x}{}\geq{}e^{Y(x,s)-x}{}\geq{}e^{-p_{min}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{a}(s)\,{\rm d}s}.

(123)

Using this in (
121
) we get

\int_{x>2}h(x,s)\,{\rm d}{x}{}\leq{}C_{s_{0}}e^{p_{min}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{a}(s)\,{\rm d}s+\frac{\hat{c}_{p}}{p_{min}^{3}}\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\bar{d}(s)\,{\rm d}s}\to 0,\quad s\to+\infty.

(124)

This implies the second inequality in (
103
) with
x_{0}=2,
and we arrive at a contradiction, as was noted above.

From (
101
) and the fact that
\kappa{}={}\frac{M_{c}-1}{M-1}\in(\frac{M_{c}-1}{M},\frac{M_{c}}{M}),

it follows that, if
\tau
is sufficiently small, then the condition for the aggregate learning, (
13
), is satisfied.

Remark 5.9
.

We can re-write equation (
12
), using (
6
), in the parameters
(h,\tau,M)
characterizing the original learning model as:

\partial_{t}f+\frac{h(M-1)}{\tau}a(t)\partial_{x}(pf)-\frac{h^{2}(M-1)^{2}}{2\tau}\left(a^{2}(t)+\frac{1}{M-1}b(t)\right)\partial^{2}_{xx}(pf){}={}0,\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}.

The coefficient
h(M-1)/\tau
in the transport term can be identified as a time scale for aggregate learning, i.e. the time scale for
a(t)
to diminish. Then, assuming that aggregate learning has already happened, i.e.
a(t)=0,
we informally assign the time of scale of sorting as the diffusion coefficient
h^{2}(M-1)/(2\tau).
