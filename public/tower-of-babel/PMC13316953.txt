Semen Quality and DNA Fragmentation After Limited Testicular Sperm Extraction in Non‐Azoospermic Men With ART Failure: A Retrospective Matched Cohort Study
Authors: Naser Amirjannati, Hamed Akhavizadegan, Arash Mohazzab, Negin Amirjannati, Manizheh Fathalian, Ghazaleh Eslamian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsTesticular sperm extraction (TESE) is sometimes used in non‐azoospermic men with persistent infertility, particularly in cases of high sperm DNA fragmentation index (DFI) or repeated intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) failure. However, the impact of TESE on subsequent semen quality and sperm DNA integrity remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of limited TESE on semen parameters and DFI in non‐azoospermic men.MethodsIn this retrospective matched cohort study, 160 non‐azoospermic men with at least three failed ICSI cycles were included between 2021 and 2024. Eighty patients who underwent limited TESE were compared with 80 age‐ and BMI‐matched controls. Semen parameters and DFI were assessed at baseline and after 3–6 months. Data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Between‐group comparisons were performed using independent‐samples t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. Changes (Δ) were compared using ANCOVA adjusted for baseline values. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsNo significant between‐group differences were observed in changes in sperm count (TESE: +8.6 ± 67.3 × 106 vs. control: +1.1 ± 53.3 × 106, p = 0.45), morphology (TESE: +0.01% ± 0.61% vs. control: +0.01% ± 0.46%, p = 0.78), progressive motility (TESE: +0.86% ± 8.18% vs. control: +1.36% ± 7.02%, p = 0.92), or DFI (TESE: −1.55% ± 10.60% vs. control: −0.08% ± 8.92%, p = 0.29). Effect sizes were small and not clinically meaningful.ConclusionLimited TESE does not appear to adversely affect semen parameters or sperm DNA integrity over a short‐term follow‐up in non‐azoospermic men. These findings should be interpreted with caution, and further well‐designed prospective studies are required to confirm long‐term safety and reproductive outcomes.

Introduction

Infertility is defined as the inability to conceive after 12 consecutive months of unprotected intercourse. According to a recent World Health Organization report, the global infertility rate among couples is approximately 17.5%, with male factors contributing to nearly 50% of cases [1]. Advances in assisted reproductive technologies (ART), especially intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), have significantly improved outcomes for male infertility [2].

Testicular sperm extraction (TESE) is a surgical technique used to retrieve sperm directly from testicular tissue, initially developed for men with azoospermia [3]. Since its first reported use in 1993 [4, 5], various modifications of the technique have been developed, including conventional TESE, microdissection TESE, testicular sperm aspiration (TESA), and more recently, open testicular mapping (OTEM) [6].

In selected non‐azoospermic patients—such as those with high DNA fragmentation index (DFI), repeated ICSI failures, or inability to provide a sample on the day of oocyte retrieval—TESE has also been employed [7, 8]. This is based on reports suggesting that testicular sperm may have lower DNA damage compared to ejaculated sperm [9, 10, 11]. However, the use of TESE in non‐azoospermic men raises important ethical and clinical concerns, as these patients are potentially capable of natural conception and may be exposed to unnecessary surgical risks [12].

While previous studies have compared the DFI of ejaculated versus testicular sperm, very few have investigated whether undergoing TESE itself alters subsequent semen quality or DNA integrity. Furthermore, the concept of “limited TESE”—referring to a minimally invasive single‐site testicular biopsy—remains poorly characterized in the literature. Thus, the current study aimed to evaluate whether limited TESE affects semen parameters and DFI over a short‐term follow‐up in non‐azoospermic men with a history of ART failure. This study also seeks to clarify the safety profile of limited TESE, an area with limited data despite its growing clinical application.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Avicenna Infertility and Recurrent Abortion Treatment Center in Tehran, Iran, between January 2021 and January 2024. A total of 160 non‐azoospermic infertile men were included. All participants had experienced at least three unsuccessful cycles of ICSI. The study group comprised 80 patients who had undergone limited TESE, while the control group consisted of 80 matched patients who had not undergone TESE. Patients were identified from clinical records. For each consecutive eligible TESE case, one non‐operated control was selected using frequency matching based on age (± 2 years) and body mass index (BMI; ±2 kg/m2) to minimize potential confounding. Given the ethical limitations of performing TESE in a randomized fashion on non‐azoospermic men, a retrospective cohort was the most feasible design.

Eligibility criteria included men aged 20–45 years with normal testicular size (greater than 2 × 4 cm), normal levels of reproductive hormones (luteinizing hormone [LH], follicle‑stimulating hormone [FSH], testosterone, and prolactin), and no history of smoking, alcohol use, or systemic diseases. TESE was performed only in patients with high DFI, repeated ICSI failure, or inability to produce a semen sample on the day of oocyte retrieval. Patients were excluded if they had a history of testicular trauma, biopsy, torsion, autoimmune disease, chronic illnesses such as diabetes or hypertension, or were using immunosuppressive drugs.

In this study, “limited TESE” refers to a minimally invasive, single‐site testicular biopsy performed through a small scrotal incision without exteriorization of the testis. A small tissue sample was obtained using a standardized “window” technique. This approach differs from conventional TESE, which may involve multiple or larger biopsies, and from microdissection TESE (micro‐TESE), which utilizes an operating microscope to identify seminiferous tubules. The limited TESE technique was designed to minimize tissue trauma and preserve testicular function. All TESE procedures were performed under local anesthesia using “window” technique. A 3‐mm horizontal incision was made on the scrotal skin, and the tunica albuginea was exposed without exteriorizing the testis. A small tissue sample (approximately 2 × 3 mm) was obtained with gentle pressure and transferred to the embryology laboratory in sperm culture medium. A single experienced surgeon performed all surgeries using a standardized approach to minimize variability. This method was chosen to minimize tissue trauma and preserve testicular function. In all 80 patients, limited TESE was performed unilaterally. The right testis was preferentially selected for consistency, unless a clinical indication (e.g., palpable abnormality or prior surgery) favored the left testis; no bilateral procedures were performed.

In our center, TESA is routinely reserved for azoospermic men or for same‐day sperm retrieval on the day of oocyte pickup. For non‐azoospermic patients with repeated ART failure and high DNA fragmentation index, the established clinical protocol is limited TESE. This approach provides a small tissue sample (2 × 3 mm) while preserving the option of histological evaluation if needed. Furthermore, some evidence suggests that testicular sperm obtained by TESE may have lower DNA fragmentation compared to TESA‐derived sperm, although this remains debated. Therefore, all eligible patients in this retrospective cohort underwent limited TESE, and TESA was not performed in this population.

Semen analysis was carried out according to WHO 6th edition guidelines [13]. After liquefaction at 37°C for 20 min, semen volume, sperm count, concentration, morphology, motility, progressive motility, total motility, and vitality were evaluated. Morphology was assessed using Diff‐Quik staining under oil immersion at 1000× magnification, and reported as normal forms according to WHO 6th (strict/Kruger) criteria [14].

DFI was evaluated using the Halosperm kit (Idea Verzan Farda, Cat. No. 062301 SD), based on the sperm chromatin dispersion method. Semen was diluted to a concentration of 5–10 million/mL, mixed with melted agarose microgel, and processed with denaturing and lysing solutions [15]. The samples were then stained and examined under a light microscope. Spermatozoa with small or no halos were classified as having DNA fragmentation, while those with large halos were considered intact [16].

The control group underwent semen analysis and DFI testing at the same intervals as the TESE group—specifically, at baseline and 3–6 months later—to ensure consistency in temporal comparison. Hormone testing, physical examination, and clinical records were reviewed to confirm eligibility prior to inclusion.

Although this was a retrospective study with a fixed sample size, a sample size estimation was performed to assess whether the study had sufficient power to detect clinically meaningful differences, particularly in DFI.

Based on previous literature, assuming a baseline DFI of 35% (standard deviation 11%) and an expected 15% change, a sample size of 80 participants per group would provide approximately 80% power at a two‐sided α level of 0.05. Therefore, the available sample size was considered adequate for the primary outcome.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 24.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables that were not normally distributed were log‐transformed to achieve approximate normality. Following transformation, normality assumptions were reassessed and confirmed. Statistical analyses were conducted using the transformed data; however, results are presented on the original (untransformed) scale as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for clinical interpretability.

Participants in the control group were frequency‐matched to the TESE group based on age (± 2 years) and body mass index (BMI; ±2 kg/m2) at the design stage to reduce potential confounding.

Baseline comparisons between groups were performed using independent‐samples t‐tests. Within‐group changes from baseline to follow‐up were assessed using paired t‐tests. To evaluate the effect of TESE, change (Δ) values (follow‐up minus baseline) were calculated for each parameter and compared between groups using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), adjusted for baseline values as well as matching variables (age and BMI) to control for residual confounding. All tests were two‐sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

At baseline, there were no statistically significant differences between the TESE and control groups in terms of age (TESE: 38.7 ± 4.0 years vs. control: 39.0 ± 3.5 years, p = 0.763), BMI (TESE: 27.9 ± 3.9 kg/m2 vs. control: 28.2 ± 3.6 kg/m2, p= 0.711), semen volume, sperm concentration, motility, morphology, vitality, or DFI (p > 0.05 for all). Discussion

This study evaluated the impact of limited TESE on semen parameters and DFI in non‑azoospermic infertile men. Our findings suggest that TESE, when performed selectively and using a minimally invasive technique, does not appear to lead to significant deterioration in sperm concentration, motility, morphology, vitality, or DFI over the 3–6 month follow‑up period. These results are of clinical relevance, suggesting that TESE might be cautiously considered in certain non‑azoospermic patients, particularly those with persistently high DFI or repeated ICSI failures [7, 8].

The rationale for using testicular sperm in this population stems from evidence that it may exhibit lower levels of DNA fragmentation compared to ejaculated sperm [17, 18]. Previous studies have reported improved reproductive outcomes, including higher pregnancy and live birth rates, when testicular sperm were used in men with high DFI [8]. However, the potential iatrogenic harm associated with surgical sperm retrieval—especially in men who are not azoospermic—has raised ongoing concerns [19, 20].

Our study is among the first to longitudinally assess whether TESE itself alters subsequent semen quality in men with ongoing spermatogenesis, and specifically to evaluate a limited, minimally invasive TESE technique. Most previous studies on testicular sperm retrieval have focused either on conventional TESE (involving larger or multiple biopsies) or on microdissection TESE (micro‑TESE) performed under an operating microscope— primarily in men with azoospermia or severe oligozoospermia [21, 22]. In conventional TESE, the extent of tissue extraction and testicular manipulation is substantially greater than in our limited approach, which has been associated with higher rates of postoperative pain, hematoma, fibrosis, and even temporary declines in testosterone [22, 23, 24]. Micro‑TESE, while more precise, still requires testicular exteriorization and can involve extensive dissection, particularly in men with non‑obstructive azoospermia. By contrast, our limited TESE used a single 3‑mm incision, a 2 × 3 mm tissue sample, and no exteriorization of the testis. To our knowledge, very few studies have reported on similarly minimally invasive techniques, and no prior study has used a matched control group to evaluate changes in sperm parameters or DFI following TESE in non‑azoospermic men. One small series described TESA outcomes in 24 men, but lacked a control group [25]. Thus, our work fills an important gap by providing controlled, short‑term safety data for a truly minimal TESE approach.

Several biological mechanisms explain why testicular biopsy could theoretically harm spermatogenesis and Leydig cell function. First, surgical disruption of the testicular microvasculature may lead to focal ischemia, reducing oxygen and nutrient supply to seminiferous tubules [24]. Second, the release of pro‑inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species following tissue injury can trigger an immunologic response, with recruitment of macrophages and lymphocytes that may damage adjacent germ cells [24, 26]. Third, fibrosis and scar formation in the tunica albuginea or interstitium can mechanically compress seminiferous tubules and impair sperm transport, as observed after repeated or large biopsies [23, 24]. Fourth, induction of anti‑sperm antibodies against exposed testicular antigens has been reported after TESE, potentially affecting sperm motility and function [27].

Our limited TESE technique was specifically designed to minimize these theoretical risks. The 3‑mm incision without testicular exteriorization reduces vascular disruption; the minimal tissue sample (2 × 3 mm) limits the area of injury and subsequent fibrosis; and the single, standardized biopsy avoids the cumulative trauma of multiple passes. Moreover, the lack of exteriorization may reduce exposure of testicular antigens to the systemic immune system, potentially lowering the risk of anti‑sperm antibody formation. Although direct evidence in non‑azoospermic men is limited, a prospective study by Ozturk et al. found no significant increase in anti‑sperm antibody levels after microdissection TESE in men with nonobstructive azoospermia, suggesting that testicular sperm retrieval does not uniformly trigger autoimmunity [28]. Importantly, we did not systematically measure postoperative hormone levels or testicular volumes; therefore, we cannot make claims about endocrine preservation. However, none of the participants reported clinical symptoms suggestive of hypogonadism (e.g., decreased libido, erectile dysfunction, or persistent fatigue) during the short‑term follow‑up. Future prospective studies with standardized endocrine and sonographic assessments are needed to fully evaluate the safety of limited TESE.

In recent years, non‑surgical laboratory techniques have emerged as alternatives to testicular sperm retrieval for men with high DFI. Among these, microfluidic sperm selection (MFSS) has gained attention because it mimics the natural sperm selection process in the female reproductive tract. MFSS uses microchannels to sort sperm based on motility and rheotaxis, without the centrifugal forces or oxidative stress associated with density gradient centrifugation [29]. Several studies have reported that MFSS significantly reduces DFI in the selected sperm population and improves clinical outcomes. For example, Kocur et al. showed that MFSS yielded sperm with markedly lower DFI and resulted in higher blastocyst euploidy rates compared to conventional gradients [29]. Likewise, a blinded controlled laboratory study by Quinn et al. found that microfluidic sorting reduced DNA damage while preserving sperm motility and morphology [30].

However, the evidence regarding the clinical efficacy of MFSS is not uniform. A recent meta‑analysis concluded that MFSS shows only marginal positive outcomes without statistical significance for clinical pregnancy or miscarriage rates when compared to standard sperm selection techniques [31]. Furthermore, MFSS does not address intrinsic DNA damage originating in the testis; it primarily removes fragmented sperm from the ejaculate [31], whereas testicular sperm may have inherently lower DNA damage because they bypass post‑testicular oxidative stress [10]. Direct comparisons between testicular sperm retrieval (e.g., TESE/TESA) and MFSS are scarce. One retrospective study compared TESA and MFSS in couples with high sperm DNA fragmentation and reported a trend toward higher live birth rates with TESA in the subgroup with DFI ≥ 40%, although no significant differences in pregnancy rates were observed overall [32]. Another study found that in men with DFI > 50%, MFSS achieved live birth rates comparable to TESA [33]. Thus, while MFSS offers a non‑invasive alternative, its role relative to limited TESE remains undefined. Well‑designed prospective studies—ideally with matched patient populations (e.g., men with DFI > 30% and at least one previous failed ICSI cycle)—are needed to clarify the comparative effectiveness of these two approaches.

Our findings have practical implications for clinicians counseling men with repeated ART failure and high DFI who are considering TESE. First, the absence of significant deterioration in semen parameters and DFI after limited TESE suggests that the procedure, when performed by an experienced surgeon using a minimally invasive technique, does not compromise future attempts at conception—either through subsequent ART cycles or, in principle, through natural conception. This can alleviate patient fears regarding iatrogenic testicular damage. Second, the stability of DFI after TESE implies that if testicular sperm are used for ICSI, the patient's ejaculated sperm quality remains available as a backup or for future cycles without unexpected decline. Third, we must counsel patients that our data are limited to a short follow‑up (3–6 months); longer‑term effects on fertility or testosterone cannot be excluded. Fourth, the decision to proceed with TESE should balance the potential benefits—namely, the use of lower‑DFI testicular sperm, which meta‑analyses have associated with improved live birth rates [9, 10, 11]—against the surgical risks, costs, and the current lack of high‑level evidence from randomized controlled trials. A shared decision‑making approach that incorporates the couple's reproductive history, female partner's age and ovarian reserve, and the patient's preferences is essential.

One methodological strength of this study is the use of a standardized, unilateral surgical approach performed by a single experienced surgeon. A minimally invasive “window” technique was employed in all limited TESE procedures, which likely reduced testicular trauma and limited inter‑operator variability. This consistency is clinically relevant, as earlier studies have indicated that biopsy size, surgical manipulation, and testicular handling can influence postoperative outcomes [34, 35]. By minimizing invasiveness, we may have lowered the risk of fibrosis, vascular compromise, or secondary immune responses—factors that have been associated with hypogonadism or impaired testicular function after more extensive procedures [36, 37]. Anti‑sperm antibodies were not directly measured in our cohort; nevertheless, previous prospective work has reported no significant increase in anti‑sperm antibody levels after testicular sperm extraction [28]. Another strength is the use of a well‑matched control group, with semen analyses and DFI testing repeated after a similar interval (3–6 months). Adjusting for baseline differences using ANCOVA strengthened the internal validity of our comparisons. Finally, the sample size was adequate to detect clinically meaningful changes in DFI and other semen parameters, as confirmed by our post‑hoc power calculation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the follow‑up period (3–6 months) is relatively short. Given that the human spermatogenic cycle lasts approximately 74 days, this timeframe may not be sufficient to capture delayed or subtle changes in semen quality. Longer‑term studies are needed. Second, the retrospective design carries an inherent risk of selection bias, despite our efforts to apply strict inclusion and matching criteria. Third, we did not systematically record reproductive outcomes of ART cycles that used testicular sperm (e.g., fertilization rates, embryo development, implantation, or live birth rates). Consequently, our conclusions are limited to laboratory semen parameters and DFI and cannot be directly generalized to clinical pregnancy or live birth. Fourth, and most importantly, endocrine outcomes were not systematically assessed. Testicular biopsy—even when limited—may theoretically affect Leydig cell function through vascular disruption, local inflammation, or subsequent interstitial fibrosis [38]. In this retrospective cohort, postoperative serum testosterone, FSH, LH, and testicular volume measurements were not routinely collected. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility of subclinical hypogonadism or subtle steroidogenic impairment after limited TESE. Although none of the participants reported clinical symptoms suggestive of hypogonadism (e.g., decreased libido, erectile dysfunction, persistent fatigue), the absence of biochemical confirmation remains a major gap. Until prospective studies include standardized hormonal panels (total testosterone, FSH, LH, and optionally SHBG) and scrotal ultrasound for testicular volume assessment before and after TESE, claims about the complete endocrine safety of limited TESE must remain provisional. Our findings, therefore, should be interpreted as limited to short‑term semen parameters and DFI, with no inference regarding long‑term testicular endocrine health. Fifth, we did not quantitatively record the sperm yield from the surgical specimens, which is an additional limitation. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts, extended follow‑up, and comprehensive evaluations of both reproductive and endocrine outcomes are necessary to validate and extend our observations.

Conclusion

In this retrospective matched cohort study, limited TESE did not appear to adversely affect semen parameters or sperm DNA fragmentation over a short‐term follow‐up in non‐azoospermic men. However, given the observational design and absence of statistically significant differences, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Larger, well‐designed prospective studies, ideally randomized controlled trials, are needed to confirm these results and to evaluate long‐term reproductive and endocrine outcomes.
