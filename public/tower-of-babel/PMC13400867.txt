Identification of long noncoding RNA LOC416755 and its polymorphisms associated with carcass traits in chicken
Authors: Yaohua Li, Jiahao Li, Siyu Guo, Xinyi Zheng, Wanxi He, Zihan Sun, Bolin Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are known to regulate skeletal muscle development in mammals, but their roles and potential as genetic markers in chickens remain largely unknown. Carcass traits such as breast muscle weight and body weight are economically important in poultry production. Based on our previous RNA-seq data (accession number PRJNA751251), LOC416755 is differentially expressed between the Soleus (SOL) and Pectoralis major (PEM) in Xinghua (XH) chickens. In this study, we further revealed that LOC416755 was highly expressed in the PEM, localized in the nucleus of chicken primary myoblasts, and progressively upregulated during myoblast differentiation. Nine SNPs were identified within LOC416755. Association analysis revealed that SNP1, SNP2, SNP4, SNP5, and SNP7 were significantly associated with shank length, full-bore weight, breast muscle weight, body weight, and abdominal fat weight (P < 0.05). Linkage disequilibrium analysis defined two haplotype blocks, and haplotype variation within block 2 showed a significant association with shank length. The heterozygous genotype consistently exhibited superior phenotypic values for most associated traits. Collectively, these results suggest a potential role for LOC416755 in skeletal muscle development and highlight its potential as a molecular marker for marker-assisted selection to accelerate breed improvement in chickens.

Introduction

Given the growing market for high-quality chilled chicken, carcass traits such as dressed weight and muscle yield have become critical to commercial value and consumer preference (Maharjan et al., 2021; Martinez et al., 2022). However, because carcass traits can only be measured postmortem, their direct use in conventional breeding programs is limited (Zhou et al., 2023).

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a class of RNA molecules with transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides in length that lack protein-coding capacity (Mercer et al., 2009). In recent years, emerging evidence has demonstrated that lncRNAs play important roles in gene regulatory networks through their interactions with DNA, RNA, or proteins, and are extensively involved in various biological processes, including transcriptional regulation, chromatin remodeling, epigenetic modification, and cell signaling (Mattick et al., 2023). In poultry genetics and breeding research, lncRNAs have been confirmed to be closely associated with economic traits such as fat deposition and skeletal muscle development (Guo et al., 2023; Niu et al., 2024). One such lncRNA, lncMGR, was recently reported to promote myoblast differentiation, inhibit myoblast proliferation in vitro, and induce myofiber hypertrophy and injury repair in vivo, thereby influencing muscle development (Guo et al., 2024).

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are DNA sequence polymorphisms arising from variations at single nucleotide positions. They are widely distributed throughout the genome and are characterized by high density, stability, and ease of genotyping (Brookes, 1999). In poultry breeding, SNPs are widely used as molecular markers for genetic map construction, trait association analysis, and marker-assisted selection (MAS) (Vignal et al., 2002). The application of high-density SNP arrays has been particularly effective in identifying genetic variants associated with growth and meat production traits, thereby enabling genomic selection strategies in commercial chicken populations (Volkova et al., 2024). In poultry breeding practices, MAS has been widely applied for the genetic improvement of economic traits, significantly enhancing selection efficiency and breeding progress (Dekkers, 2004; Cui et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2023).

Our previous RNA‑seq analysis (accession number PRJNA751251) initially identified a novel lncRNA, named LOC416755, which was found to be differentially expressed between the Soleus (SOL) and Pectoralis major (PEM) in Xinghua (XH) chickens (Cai et al., 2024), suggesting a potential link to muscle development. To further explore its function, in the present study, we characterized its molecular features and expression pattern, identified SNPs within LOC416755, and performed association, linkage disequilibrium (LD), and haplotype analyses with carcass traits. Our results showed that LOC416755 was highly expressed in the PEM compared with other tissues, localized in the nucleus of chicken primary myoblasts (CPMs), and exhibited progressively increasing expression during CPMs differentiation. Moreover, five SNPs and a haplotype block (block 2) within LOC416755 were significantly associated with carcass traits. These results suggest that LOC416755 expression and genetic variation correlate with skeletal muscle development.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The animal experiment in this study strictly followed the guidelines and approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the South China Agricultural University (approval ID: 2021-f074). Every effort was made to minimize animal suffering.

Experimental animals and carcass traits

A total of 397 chickens used in this study were provided by KwangFeng Industrial Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). These chickens were derived from a cross between a Chinese indigenous spotted chicken line and a Chinese indigenous yellow chicken line to produce F1 individuals. Fifteen pairs of F1 individuals were used to construct full-sib families to produce the F2 individuals. All F2 individuals were raised until 80 days of age. All tissue samples and carcass trait data used in this study were obtained as previously described (Zhou et al., 2023). A comprehensive set of carcass traits was evaluated (Supplementary Table 1), including: subcutaneous fat thickness (SFT), intermuscular fat width (IFW), full-bore weight (FBW; eviscerated weight including giblets), half-bore weight (HBW; eviscerated weight excluding giblets), abdominal fat weight (AFW), leg weight (LW), breast muscle weight (BMW), body weight (BW), shank length (SL), shank girth (SG), and carcass weight (CW). Additionally, the corresponding percentages of breast muscle weight (BMWP), leg weight (LWP), abdominal fat weight (AFWP), half-bore weight (HBWP), full-bore weight (FBWP), and carcass weight (CWP).

Cell culture

CPMs were isolated from the leg muscle tissues of 11-day-old chicken embryos and cultured as previously described (Cai et al., 2022a). Briefly, leg muscles were dissected away from the skin and bone, and then finely minced into pieces using sterile scissors in a petri dish. To release single cells, the tissue fragments were digested with 0.25% pancreatin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) for 20 min at 37 °C with gentle agitation. The digestion was neutralized by adding an equal volume of complete RPMI‑1640 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) containing 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA). The cell suspension was then centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min at room temperature, and the supernatant was discarded. To enrich CPMs and eliminate fibroblasts, the cell pellet was resuspended in culture medium and subjected to serial plating (differential adhesion) for 2 cycles, each lasting 40 min. Purified CPMs were subsequently cultured in RPMI‑1640 medium supplemented with 15% (vol/vol) FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ (Chen et al., 2020; Verma et al., 2020).

Genomic DNA Extraction and PCR Amplification

Genomic DNA was isolated from blood samples using the E.Z.N.A. SQ Blood DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. DNA concentration and purity (assessed by the 260/280 nm optical density ratio) were measured using a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Primer pairs were designed using Primer Primer 5 (PREMIER Biosoft, San Francisco, CA, USA) and synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Detailed primer information is provided in Supplementary Table 2. PCR amplification was carried out in a 30 μL reaction mixture containing 2 μL of template DNA, 15 μL of 2 × Rapid Taq Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), 1.2 μL each of forward and reverse primers, and 10.6 μL of ddH2O. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 34 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 58 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 15 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. To minimize PCR amplification artifacts and ensure accurate variant calling, the amplified products were Sanger sequenced in both forward and reverse directions by Tianyi Huiyuan Gene Technology Co., Ltd. (Tianyi Huiyuan Gene Technology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China).

RNA Extraction, Complementary DNA Synthesis, and Quantitative Real-Time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cultured cells using Trizol reagent (Takara, Shiga, Japan) as previously described (Chomczynski and Sacchi, 1987). Briefly, cells were homogenized in TRIzol reagent, followed by phase separation with chloroform, precipitation with isopropanol, washing with 75% ethanol, and resuspension in RNase-free water. The concentration of RNA samples is calculated by the NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer by measuring the absorbance. The purity of RNA samples is measured at 260/280 nm absorbance ratio (1.8-2.2) with a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer. And the integrity of all obtained RNA samples was assayed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed using the HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), following the recommended protocol. For Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR) analysis, amplification was carried out with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The chicken β-actin gene was employed as an endogenous reference for normalizing LOC416755 expression. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative 2−∆∆CT method. All primers used for qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was conducted using the RiboTM lncRNA FISH Probe Mix in conjunction with the RiboTM Fluorescent In Situ Hybridization Kit (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Specific FISH probes targeting LOC416755 were designed and synthesized by RiboBio (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) and labeled with Cy3 for fluorescence detection. Briefly, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. After PBS washing, pre-hybridization was carried out in hybridization buffer at 42°C for 1 h. Hybridization was then performed overnight at 42°C in a humidified chamber using the RiboTM lncRNA FISH Probe Mix, which consists of approximately 20 Cy3-labeled oligonucleotide probes specifically targeting LOC416755. Following hybridization, coverslips were washed sequentially with 2×SSC, 1×SSC, and 0.5×SSC at 42°C to remove unbound and weakly bound probes. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI before visualization under a confocal microscope.

Statistical analysis

Sanger sequencing results were imported into DNASTAR (DNASTAR, Inc., Madison, WI, USA) software, and sequence assembly was performed using the SeqMan module to obtain visualized sequence alignments. SNP genotyping and counting were conducted based on the alignment views. Genotypes were called by visual inspection of the sequencing peak profiles: a single, clean peak was classified as homozygous, while overlapping dual peaks with a secondary were classified as heterozygous (Al-Shuhaib and Hashim, 2023). The SNP data with a minor allele frequency (MAF) greater than 5% were recorded in an Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) (International HapMap Consortium, 2003). Basic genetic parameters, including SNP counts, allele frequencies, genotype frequency differences (P-values), Hardy Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) P-values (Pw), observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), and polymorphism information content (PIC), were calculated using standard Microsoft Excel functions. Specifically, the Pw was obtained using the “CHIDIST” function based on a chi-square distribution with one degree of freedom; Ho was calculated as the number of observed heterozygotes divided by the total number of individuals; He was computed as 1 - p2 - q2 (p and q being the allele frequencies of the two alleles at a given biallelic SNP locus); PIC was derived as 1 - (p2 + q2) - 2p2q2 (p and q being the allele frequencies; Botstein et al., 1980). LD analysis among SNPs was performed with Haploview software (Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA, USA; Barrett et al., 2005). All quality control filtering and haplotype block definition were carried out with the software's default thresholds. Genotype data were filtered using a HWE exact test P-value cutoff of 0.001, a minimum genotype call rate of 75%, a maximum of one Mendelian error and a MAF cutoff of 0.001. Haplotype blocks were defined by 95% confidence interval bounds for strong LD set to 0.70 and 0.98, a required fraction of strong LD in informative comparisons ≥ 0.95, and exclusion of markers with MAF < 0.05. Haplotype reconstruction and frequency estimation were conducted using PHASE 2.1 software (Stephens and Scheet, 2005). Due to missing phenotypic records for some individuals, these individuals were excluded only from analyses of the specific phenotypes for which data were unavailable resulting in varying sample sizes across individual SNP analyses. Association analyses between SNPs and phenotypic traits were conducted using a mixed linear model implemented in SPSS 26 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The statistical model was specified as follows:Y=μ+G+e

Y represents the phenotypic value of the carcass trait, μ is the overall population mean, G is the fixed effect of SNP genotype or haplotype, and e is the random residual error. All experimental chickens were female and were hatched in a single batch. Therefore, sex and batch effects were not included in the model. Multiple testing corrections were applied using the Bonferroni method. Association results that did not survive Bonferroni correction were evaluated at the significance threshold of P < 0.05 and are considered nominally significant, pending validation in an independent population. Significance between SNP sites is represented by letters where different letters indicate a significant difference (P < 0.05).

Results

Characterization of LncRNA LOC416755

Based on our previous RNA‑seq data (accession number PRJNA751251), LOC416755 was differentially expressed between the PEM and SOL (Fig. 1A) (Cai et al., 2024). To further characterize this lncRNA, we determined its genomic location, sequence conservation, and expression pattern. BLAST analysis against the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI; GRCg6a reference standard database) revealed that LOC416755 is located on chromosome 14, spanning positions 14,209,232 to 14,210,233, with a full-length transcript of 1,002 nucleotides (Supplementary Table 3). Sequence conservation analysis demonstrated that LOC416755 is predominantly conserved among avian species (Table 1). Coding potential assessment using the coding potential calculator 2 (CPC2) online tool confirmed that LOC416755 is a non-coding RNA (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Fig. 1). Expression profiling revealed that LOC416755 was upregulated during differentiation phases of CPM, with notably high expression levels detected in breast and leg muscle tissues (Fig. 1C-E). Furthermore, RNA FISH analysis demonstrated that LOC416755 is predominantly localized within the nucleus of CPM (Fig. 1F). Single nucleotide polymorphisms and genotyping of LOC416755

SNPs are widely used as genetic markers for association studies. To characterize genetic variants within the LOC416755 gene, Sanger sequencing was performed following PCR amplification. Based on the sequencing chromatograms, genotype classification and subsequent statistical analyses were performed for all individuals (Fig. 2). Through careful examination of the sequencing peak profiles, we identified nine SNPs within LOC416755, each presenting three distinct genotypes. The identified SNPs were as follows: NC_006101.5: g.14209456 C>A (SNP1), NC_006101.5: g.14209561 G>A (SNP2), NC_006101.5: g.14209736 G>A (SNP3), NC_006101.5: g.14209740 T>C (SNP4), NC_006101.5: g.14209760 C>T (SNP5), NC_006101.5: g.14209808 A>G (SNP6), NC_006101.5: g.14209983 T>G (SNP7), NC_006101.5: g.14210043 T>C (SNP8), and NC_006101.5: g.14210051 A>C (SNP9). Genotype and allele frequencies for these nine SNPs were calculated, with results summarized in Table 2. Alleles matching the reference database sequence were designated as wild-type, while variants identified through sequencing were classified as mutant alleles. Among the nine SNPs, only SNP2, SNP8, SNP9 exhibited a lower frequency of the wild-type allele compared to its mutant counterpart; for the remaining four SNPs, wild-type alleles were more prevalent. To further elucidate the population genetic characteristics of these variants, several additional parameters were evaluated. HWE was assessed for each SNP using chi-square tests. The results indicated that only SNP3, SNP6, and SNP8 deviated significantly from HWE (Pw < 0.05), whereas the remaining SNPs conformed to HWE expectations. Comparison of Ho and He revealed that SNP2, SNP6, SNP7, SNP8, and SNP9 displayed lower Ho than He, indicating relatively reduced genetic diversity. In contrast, the remaining SNPs exhibited Ho values exceeding He, with SNP5 showing nearly equivalent Ho and He, reflecting comparatively higher polymorphism. Association analysis of SNPs in LOC416755 with chicken traits

To determine whether the identified SNPs within LOC416755 are associated with economically important traits, we performed an association analysis by integrating genotype data for the eight SNPs that passed quality control (SNP6 was excluded due to severe HWE deviation; Pw < 0.001) with measurements for 17 carcass and growth traits (Table 3). The results revealed significant correlations between specific SNPs and muscle development related phenotypes. Specifically, SNP1 was significantly associated with SL, with the heterozygous genotype (CA) exhibiting the highest value among the three genotypes (P < 0.05). SNP2 showed significant associations with both FBW and BMW, where the heterozygous genotype (GA) had the highest mean values, and the mutant type (AA) displayed the lowest (P < 0.05). SNP4 was significantly associated with BW, with heterozygous (TC) and mutant (CC) individuals showing higher body weight than wild-type (TT) individuals (P < 0.05). SNP5 was significantly associated with AFW, with the heterozygous genotype (CT) showing the highest AFW (P < 0.05). Additionally, SNP7 exhibited significant associations with both FBW and BMW, following a similar pattern to SNP2, where heterozygous individuals had the highest values and mutant individuals the lowest (P <0.05). Linkage disequilibrium and haplotype analysis of SNPs in LOC416755

Haploview is a widely used software package that facilitates the visualization of LD patterns and the inference of population-level haplotype structure from genotype data. Accordingly, LD among the nine identified SNPs within LOC416755 was evaluated using Haploview software. After quality control, SNP6 was removed due to significant deviation from HWE. The LD structure, visualized based on both D' and r2 metrics, is presented in Fig. 3A-B. Applying the default threshold in Haploview for block definition, two haplotype blocks were delineated: block 1 comprised four SNPs (SNP1 through SNP4), and block 2 comprised three SNPs (SNP5, SNP7, and SNP8). Subsequent haplotype reconstruction and frequency estimation within this block were performed using PHASE 2.1 software. As summarized in Table 4, a total of 17 distinct haplotypes (designated H1 to H17) were resolved. To assess the potential impact of haplotype combinations on phenotypic traits, haplotypes with frequencies below 5% were excluded from further association analyses. A comprehensive joint analysis of the remaining haplotypes of block 2 revealed a statistically significant association with SG (P < 0.05; Supplementary Table 4). Discussion

LncRNAs have emerged as critical regulators of skeletal muscle development, yet their functional roles in chickens remain largely unexplored. In this study, we characterized the lncRNA LOC416755, a candidate identified from our previous RNA-seq analysis (accession number PRJNA751251; Cai et al., 2024), which exhibited a conserved avian sequence and a nuclear localization pattern in CPMs. The finding that LOC416755 is predominantly conserved among avian species suggests that it may exert evolutionarily conserved functions specific to birds, distinguishing it from many lncRNAs that show low sequence conservation (Rinn and Chang, 2012). The nuclear enrichment of LOC416755 implies a potential role in transcriptional or epigenetic regulation, as nuclear-localized lncRNAs often modulate gene expression by recruiting chromatin modifiers or forming RNA–protein complexes (Mattick et al., 2023). Expression profiling revealed that LOC416755 was upregulated during myoblast differentiation and highly expressed in breast and leg muscles, resembling the expression patterns of known myogenic lncRNAs such as lncRNA-Six1 (Cai et al., 2017; Cai et al., 2022b; Chen et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2025). Given that several chicken lncRNAs are upregulated during myoblast differentiation and regulate myotube formation and myofiber maturation (Cai et al., 2022b; Chen et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2024), LOC416755 may function similarly. Moreover, the higher expression in the PEM muscle, a fast-growing muscle in broilers, further suggests a correlation between LOC416755 and muscle growth performance, similar to myogenic factors differentially expressed in broiler breast muscle (Cai et al., 2017; Cai et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022).

Chicken meat represents one of the most widely consumed protein sources globally (Connolly and Campbell, 2023). Carcass traits have long served as critical breeding criteria in poultry production, influencing not only the visual appeal and consumer preference for poultry products but also their market valuation and overall economic returns (Maharjan et al., 2021; Martinez et al., 2022). Enhancing muscle development and reducing fat accumulation remain primary objectives in chicken breeding programs (Moreira et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2024). While traditional breeding approaches have relied heavily on phenotypic selection to achieve improvements in these traits, recent advances in genetic research have underscored the critical role of genetic polymorphisms in driving desirable phenotypic variation (Sun et al., 2020). In the present study, nine SNPs were identified within LOC416755, and their population genetic characteristics were evaluated. Under conditions lacking migration, mutation, natural selection, and assortative mating, a population attains HWE (Wigginton et al., 2005). HWE analysis revealed that SNP3, SNP6, and SNP8 deviated significantly from HWE (Pw < 0.05), which primarily attributable to the effects of artificial selection. In contrast, the remaining SNPs were found to conform to HWE, suggesting an equilibrium state in which various evolutionary forces may be counteracting one another. Notably, SNP2, SNP6, SNP7, SNP8, and SNP9 exhibited lower Ho than He, indicating reduced genetic diversity, which is consistent with the effects of artificial selection (Fenster et al., 2018). Breeders tend to fix favorable alleles, leading to a gradual decrease in heterozygosity over generations (Lozada-Soto et al., 2024). PIC values for all SNPs except SNP6 ranged from 0.25 to 0.5, suggesting intermediate genetic diversity, whereas SNP6 showed relatively low polymorphism (PIC < 0.25). These findings indicate that most SNPs within LOC416755 are sufficiently polymorphic to serve as effective molecular markers. Association analysis demonstrated that multiple SNPs within LOC416755 were significantly correlated with carcass traits related to muscle development and fat deposition. Specifically, SNP1 was associated with shank length, SNP2 and SNP7 with full-bore weight and breast muscle weight, SNP4 with body weight, and SNP5 with abdominal fat weight. The heterozygous genotype consistently exhibited superior phenotypic values for most associated traits, a pattern commonly observed in livestock breeding, where heterozygote advantage may reflect overdominance or linkage to functional variants. These results are similar to those reported in previous studies on lncRNA-associated SNPs in chickens (Li et al., 2020; Mei et al., 2016; Ren et al., 2017; Yuan et al., 2024). Integrating the PIC analysis with the observed associations between SNPs and carcass traits, it can be inferred that SNP1, SNP1, SNP5 and SNP7 exhibit substantial genetic variability, underscoring their considerable potential for application in selective breeding programs (Rocha et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024).

Characterization of LD and haplotype block structure is essential for reducing the complexity of genetic variation into interpretable units (Barrett et al., 2005; Gabriel et al., 2002). LD and haplotype analysis further refined the genetic architecture of LOC416755. After removing SNP6 due to HWE deviation, Haploview analysis using the default threshold defined two haplotype blocks: block 1 (SNP1-SNP4) and block 2 (SNP5, SNP7, SNP8). The presence of two distinct blocks suggests a recombination hotspot between these regions, which is common in genes under selection (Gabriel et al., 2002). Haplotype reconstruction identified 17 haplotypes, with block 2 haplotypes showing a significant association with SG. The predominance of specific haplotypes, such as H12 in block 2, indicates that certain allelic combinations are favored by selection. The fact that only block 2, not block 1, was associated with SG suggests that functional variants affecting this trait may reside within block 2 or in tight linkage with it. This finding underscores the importance of haplotype-based analyses, which can capture combinatorial effects that individual SNP analyses may miss (Clark, 2004).

Conclusions

In conclusion, our results demonstrated that LOC416755 is a nucleus-localized, avian-conserved lncRNA whose expression patterns suggest a potential role in skeletal muscle development. The identified SNPs and haplotypes within this gene show significant associations with key carcass traits, positioning LOC416755 as a promising candidate for MAS in chicken breeding programs.
