Pure isolation, culture, and postinjury lineage tracing of mouse visceral pleural mesothelial cells
Authors: Akikazu Endo, Yasunori Enomoto, Ryo Horiguchi, Shiori Meguro, Hideya Kawasaki, Isao Kosugi, Kenta Iijima, Shuji Takabayashi, Satoshi Baba, Toshihide Iwashita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPleural mesothelial cells (PMCs) are a major component of the pleura. The involvement of visceral PMCs has been suggested in several pleural or subpleural lung diseases, thus highlighting the importance of animal experiments using these cells. However, in wild-type rodent lungs, no direct and pure isolation method for visceral PMCs has been established so far. Using reanalyzed single-cell RNA sequencing data, we identified that mesothelin (Msln), a specifically expressed gene in visceral PMCs, can be useful for live cell sorting. After collecting cells by scraping the visceral pleura, MSLN+EpCAM−CD45−CD31−PDGFRα−CD146− cells with large size (≥1.4 times the median value of forward scatter in total cells) mostly exhibited WT1 protein (WT1 positivity in immunocytochemistry: 93.3% ± 0.8%). The sorted PMCs were culturable, and they responded generally predictably to several growth factors and cytokines, including genetic changes suggestive of a TGF-β–induced mesothelial-to-mesenchymal transition. In vivo experiments performed using PMC-specific reporter mice (Wt1-creERT2; tdTomato) demonstrated that intrapleural administration of bleomycin with carbon induced proliferation of PMCs in the visceral pleura. Importantly, some PMC-derived cells differentiated into α-smooth muscle actin-positive myofibroblasts with decreased MSLN expression, indicating that our method using MSLN is suitable only for uninjured lungs. In summary, we propose a novel and qualified method for visceral PMC isolation, which will aid in the elucidation of the mechanisms underlying pleura-related human lung diseases.Keywords: Mesothelial cell, Mesothelin, Single-cell RNA sequence, Isolation

Based on single-cell RNA sequencing data in mouse lungs, we identified mesothelin (MSLN), a protein specifically expressed on the cell membrane of pleural mesothelial cells (PMCs), and developed a direct isolation method using flow cytometry. PMCs isolated using this method readily proliferated in vitro and responded as expected to various stimuli, confirming that they could be properly cultured. Our novel isolation method is expected to improve the quality of future research using pleural mesothelial cells and ultimately contribute to elucidating the pathogenesis of human lung diseases affecting the pleural or subpleural regions.

Introduction

The pleura is a thin membrane that lines the inside of the thoracic cavity (parietal pleura) and covers the surface of the lungs (visceral pleura). In between the 2 pleurae is a small space called the pleural cavity, which normally contains a small amount of fluid. The pleura and the fluid help reduce friction between the lung lobes and chest wall during breathing, allowing the lungs to expand and contract smoothly.

Pleural mesothelial cells (PMCs) are a major component of both the parietal and visceral pleurae. Recent studies have focused the involvement of PMCs, particularly visceral PMCs, in several pleural or subpleural lung diseases, such as mesothelioma, pleurisy, pleural fibrosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and pleuroparenchymal fibroelastosis.1–6 This highlights the importance of evaluating the properties of these cells, which generally involves pure isolation of the primary cells. A common method to analyze the PMCs of rodents involves the enzymatic digestion of pleural tissue via the pleural administration of trypsin or pronase E.4,7,8 However, this conventional method struggles with contamination from other cell types, including immune cells and other mesenchymal cells, and it is also unable to distinguish visceral PMCs from parietal ones and mesothelial cells at the epicardium and diaphragm.

Therefore, this study aimed to identify a PMC-specific cell surface marker that can be useful for fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) and is applicable to wild-type mice. To achieve this, we utilized our previous dataset of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) from wild-type mouse lungs9 and a PMC-specific reporter mouse line (Wt1-creERT2; tdTomato).10,11 Furthermore, to determine whether PMCs isolated by our method are culturable and exhibit the similar response as previously reported, we validated this in in vitro primary culture. Last, using 2 in vivo lung and/or pleural fibrosis models, we observed postinjury lineage tracing of visceral PMCs and assessed changes in PMC marker expression.

Materials and methods

Preparation of the mouse primary cells and FACS

After the mice were euthanized, 5 mL of Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) was perfused thorough the right ventricle. Subsequently, 1 mL of DPBS was intratracheally injected, then the trachea was tied off. The lung was extracted from the thoracic cavity and washed with DPBS twice. Then, the lung was soaked in a 50-mL tube with a 3-mL protease solution cocktail containing Collagenase type I (final concentration [f.c.]: 900 U/mL, Worthington, #CLS1), Elastase (f.c. 1 U/mL, Worthington, #LS002292), and DNase I (f.c. 0.1 mg/mL, Roche, #11284932001) in Advanced DMEM/F-12 (Thermo Fisher, #12634010). After incubation for 30 minutes at 37 °C, the surface of all lung lobes, namely the visceral pleura, was scraped repeatedly with a soft rubber brush (Kobayashi Pharmaceutical, “Shikan-burashi”), then the primary cells were collected in a dish with the protease solution cocktail. Afterward, 10 mL of DPBS with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) was added, then the cells were centrifuged. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through 70-μm and 40-μm strainers. After centrifugation (400 g, 3 minutes), the cells were incubated with RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend, #420301) at room temperature for 3 minutes. Afterward, the cells were resuspended in DPBS with 3% FBS and centrifuged again (400 g, 5 minutes). Then, the cells were stained with rat anti-mesothelin (MSLN) antibody (dilution 1:50, LSBio, #LS-C179484) for 20 minutes on ice. After washing by centrifugation with 3% FBS twice (400 g, 3 minutes), the cells were stained with the anti-rat IgG secondary antibody of Alexa Fluor 647 (dilution 1:500, Invitrogen, #A-21247) for 20 minutes on ice. Additionally, after washing by centrifugation with 3% FBS twice (400 g, 3 minutes), the cells were stained with DAPI (f.c. 2 μg/mL, Nakalai, #19178-91) and the following antibodies for 20 minutes on ice: rat anti-CD45-PE/Cy7 (dilution 1:100, BioLegend, #103113); rat anti-CD31-PE/Cy7 (dilution 1:100, BioLegend, #102417); rat anti-CD326-PE/Cy7 (EpCAM) (dilution 1:100, Invitrogen, #25-5791-80); rat anti-CD140a-PE/Cy7 (PDGFRα) (dilution 1:50, BioLegend, #135911); and rat anti-CD146-PE/Cy7 (dilution 1:100, BioLegend, #134713). Afterward, the cells were washed by centrifugation with 3% FBS twice (400 g, 3 minutes), then sorted using Moflo Astrios EQ (Beckman Coulter). Data collection was performed using Summit Software v6.3.1 (Beckman Coulter). FlowJo v10.7.2 was employed for the analysis of all FACS data. A conventional isolation method using an intrapleural injection of trypsin was performed as described in a past paper.8

Mouse lines and the management, primary culture of PMCs, immunocytochemistry, immunohistochemistry, total RNA isolation, cDNA preparation, quantitative RT-PCR, establishment of the lung or pleural fibrosis mouse model, reanalysis of a dataset of scRNA-seq, and study approval are described in the Supplementary Material.

Statistical analysis

All results are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed via unpaired Student t-test (2-tailed) for comparisons between 2 groups or using a one-way analysis of variance with Bonferroni correction for comparisons between >2 groups. P <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of PMC-specific marker using scRNA-seq

To identify PMC-specific markers, we utilized our recent dataset of scRNA-seq from wild-type mouse lungs (GSE244237),9 in which the lungs were removed from the body, washed twice with DPBS, and then disaggregated into single cells; therefore, “parietal” PMCs were basically excluded. We found a visceral PMC cluster with high expressions of Wt1 and Pdpn (Figure 1A-D), which are canonical markers of PMC. Among the genes that were expressed specifically in this cluster, we focused on the Msln gene (Figure 1C and D), because the coding protein is known to be located at the cell membrane.

In immunostaining, as expected, the MSLN protein is specifically expressed in the visceral pleura (Figure 1E). In addition, upon observation with high magnification, MSLN-positive cells co-expressed WT1 in the nuclei, and the MSLN positivity among WT1-positive cells was 100% in all samples tested (n = 10) (Figure 1F).

MSLN is a specific cell surface marker for PMC and applicable to FACS

Using wild-type mice, we evaluated the utility of an anti-MSLN antibody in the isolation of visceral PMCs. To minimize contamination from other cell types (eg, immune cells, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and fibroblasts), the outer layer of the extracted mouse lungs was repeatedly scratched with a soft brush after 30 minutes of incubation with a proteinase cocktail solution (Figure 2A; see Materials and methods). Since PMCs are generally large cells, the collected cells were gated on a FACS panel, wherein only cells with forward scatter values (FSC) that were ≥1.4 times the median value were included. Then, the cells that were positive for MSLN, negative for lineage markers (EpCAM/CD45/CD31/PDGFRα/CD146), and negative for DAPI (ie, alive) were sorted (Figure 2B).

In immunocytochemistry using Cytospin, most sorted cells expressed the WT1 protein in the nuclei, in addition to the MSLN protein on the cell membrane (Figure 2C). The mean rate of WT1 positivity using this gating strategy was 93.3% ± 0.8%, which was significantly higher than that obtained by the conventional method using intrapleural trypsin8 and the methods without negative selection of lineage markers or those without restriction on FSC (Figure 2D and Supplementary Table 1). This high-purity method yielded 8000-12 000 PMCs per mouse.

Culture of sorted PMCs and response to growth factors/cytokines in vitro

To evaluate whether the sorted PMCs are culturable, first, the primary PMCs were seeded on collagen-coated plates and cultured for 3 days (Supplementary Figure 1A). Then we found PMC proliferation with ­retained positivity of nuclear WT1 and cytoplasmic podoplanin and some with cobblestone-like morphology (Supplementary Figure 1B), suggesting the cultivability of the sorted cells as PMCs.

Next, to assess whether the sorted PMCs can respond to external stimuli, as expected from the findings of previous studies, several growth factors/cytokines, including TGF-β1, TNF-α, EGF, BMP-4, PDGF-BB, or FGF-2, were administered to the PMCs. At least within our protocol, FGF-2 remarkably and EGF relatively induced PMC proliferation. In contrast, TGF-β1 had a tendency to slightly inhibit the proliferation in vitro (Supplementary Figure 1C). In qRT-PCR for the cultured PMCs (Supplementary Figure 1D), TGF-β1 administration upregulated the gene expression of Acta2, Col1a1, and Pdpn, and downregulated that of Wt1. TNF-α also upregulated the expression of Acta2 but downregulated those of Msln and Pdpn. TGF-β1 and TNF-α potentially affect the cell identity of PMCs, also termed as mesothelial-to-mesenchymal transition. Interestingly, the expression of Mcp1, a gene coding CCL2, was sensitive to various stimuli, increasing in the administration of TNF-α, BMP-4, and FGF-2 and decreasing in that of EGF and FGF-2. These results were generally, although not entirely, consistent with findings of previous studies on mesothelial cells,5,12–15 implying that our sorting method could adequately collect fresh primary PMCs.

PMC reporter mouse line and application to FACS

A tamoxifen-dependent PMC reporter line (Wt1-creERT2; tdTomato) was prepared to genetically label WT1-positive PMCs (Supplementary Figure 2A). Within the lungs of this mouse line, tdTomato fluorescence was confirmed only at the visceral pleura (Supplementary Figure 2B). However, upon observation with high magnification, we found that tdTomato could not mark all of the WT1-positive PMCs (induction efficiency: 78.7% ± 1.8%, n = 6), although all of the tdTomato-negative WT1-positive PMCs expressed MSLN (Supplementary Figure 2C). In flow cytometry, using both negative selection of lineage markers and FSC restriction at least 1.4 times the median value, MSLN-positive cells mostly showed positivity for tdTomato (88.3% ± 1.7%, n = 6) (Supplementary Figure 2D). The positivity gap between wild-type (the WT1 protein+/MSLN+ ratio on immunocytochemistry) and transgenic (Wt1-tdTomato+/MSLN+ ratio on flowcytometry) mice seemed to be primarily explained by the incomplete tamoxifen-induced recombination in this mouse line. On the other hand, consistent with the findings in wild-type mice, the tdTomato positivity was remarkably higher in our gating strategy than in the methods without negative selection or FSC restriction with a cutoff of <1.4. In contrast, more rigid FSC restriction with ≥1.8 times median value failed to increase the positivity rate and decreased the cell number for isolation; thus, 1.4 was a reasonable cutoff value (Supplementary Figure 2E).

Postinjury lineage tracing of visceral PMCs and the change in MSLN expression

Using an intratracheal bleomycin-induced lung parenchymal ­fibrosis model16,17 and an intrapleural bleomycin with carbon-­induced pleural fibrosis model,3 we evaluated postinjury lineage tracing of visceral PMCs in the area of pleural or subpleural lung fibrosis (Figure 3A and B). Immunohistochemistry identified that not intratracheal but intrapleural stimulation induced proliferation and α-smooth muscle actin (SMA) expression in tdTomato+ cells, hinting at the differentiation of PMCs into myofibroblasts (Figure 3C). Correspondingly, the expressions of WT1 and MSLN decreased in the area of pleural fibrosis induced by intrapleural stimulation. In contrast, the expressions were mostly, not completely, retained in the area of subpleural fibrosis induced by intratracheal stimulation (Figure 3D). Consistent with these observations, qRT-PCR showed that the gene expressions of Acta2, a gene coding αSMA, and Col1a1 were increased, and those of Wt1 and Msln were decreased in tdTomato+ PMC-derived cells from the pleural fibrosis model. In contrast, the expressions were not markedly unchanged in the PMC-derived cells from the lung parenchymal fibrosis model. Intriguingly, the expression of Pdpn was commonly increased in both fibrosis models (Figure 3E).

Discussion

This study established a novel and reproducible protocol to purely isolate visceral PMCs in wild-type mice based on our scRNA-seq data and immunostaining results. On FACS, visceral PMCs were successfully and freshly isolated using an expression pattern of MSLN+EpCAM−CD45−CD31−PDGFRα−CD146−. Importantly, the ­sorted cells were confirmed to be easily culturable and responded to external stimuli mostly as expected.

Previous studies have reported several methods for isolating PMCs in both humans and rodents. Several attempts have been made to isolate PMCs in humans, involving pleural fluid collection, enzymatic digestion, and additional FACS of the biopsied pleural tissues.18–20 In contrast, in rodents, intrapleural digestion methods using trypsin or pronase E without FACS has been widely used.4,7,8 However, these methods are prone to contamination with cells other than visceral PMCs, such as immune cells, mesenchymal cells, and other mesothelial cells within the thoracic cavity, including parietal PMCs. Our novel method could overcome this problem and realize high purity (>90%). In contrast, the primary culture experiments and in vivo experiments using PMC reporter mice in the present study revealed that MSLN expression can be downregulated by profibrotic or proinflammatory stimulation. In this context, our method would be suitable for PMC isolation from uninjured lungs.

MSLN is a cell surface glycoprotein with a molecular weight of 69 kDa.21 In humans, its protein expression has been reported in normal tissues, including the pleura, pericardium, and peritoneum, as well as in several malignant tumors, such as mesothelioma, ovarian cancer, and triple-negative breast cancer.22 However, the function of this glycoprotein has not been clearly identified. Although the present study demonstrates an experimental application of MSLN, future studies using conditional knockout and/or overexpression mouse lines with a Wt1-cre driver may be able to fully elucidate the function in mesothelial cells.

In summary, this study presents a novel technique that streamlines the isolation of visceral PMCs from wild-type mouse lungs. This protocol can improve the quality of experiments performed using PMCs and would be helpful for basic studies focusing on human pleural or subpleural lung diseases.
