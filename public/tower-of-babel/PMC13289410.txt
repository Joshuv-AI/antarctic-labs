Long-term survival in patients with premature ventricular complexes and no structural heart disease
Authors: Robin Bouleau, Michael Dismorr, Natalie Glaser, Martin Jonsson, Raffaele Scorza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPremature ventricular complexes (PVCs) are common and can cause concerns in both patients and their caregivers. In patients with structural heart disease, PVCs are associated with worse clinical outcomes. However, in patients without such disease, the prognostic implications of PVCs are more uncertain.MethodsIn this observational cohort study, we included patients who were diagnosed with PVCs at three major hospitals in Stockholm between 2010 and 2016. All included patients had normal results on echocardiography and stress test, and no history of structural heart disease. The expected survival from the age-matched, sex-matched and calendar year-matched Swedish population was obtained from the Human Mortality Database. Relative survival was estimated using the Ederer II method.ResultsA total of 803 PVC patients were included. Median age was 59 years, and 57% were women. In all, 185 (23%) patients had more than 10 000 PVCs per day and 86 (11%) had more than 20 000 PVCs per day. The observed survival was greater than the expected survival at 1, 5, 10 and 12 years (100% vs 99%, 97% vs 94%, 93% vs 86% and 90% vs 82%, respectively). The relative survival at 1, 5, 10 and 12 years was 100%, 104%, 109% and 111%, respectively. The median follow-up time was 10 years (maximum 14 years).ConclusionsPVC patients without structural heart disease had a favourable long-term survival, exceeding the expected survival of the general population. These results suggest that most PVC patients without structural heart disease may be given a reassuring message.

Introduction

Premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) are a common finding in clinical practice, and in the presence of structural heart disease, they are known to predict unfavourable clinical outcomes.1 2 However, the prognostic significance of PVCs in individuals without structural heart disease remains debated.

In 2012, Lee et al presented the first meta-analysis on the subject that showed an association between PVCs and worse cardiovascular outcomes in this patient group.3 However, the authors stated that several of the included studies did not use adequate diagnostic methods to rule out previously unknown heart disease. A year later, Ataklte et al4 published a second meta-analysis in which the authors found an increased risk of both sudden cardiac death and cardiovascular death in patients with frequent PVCs but no apparent structural heart disease.4 Nonetheless, the authors pointed out the risk of reversed causation, that PVC might just be a marker of previously unknown structural heart disease. More recently, Kiliç et al5 presented a third meta-analysis, again showing an association between PVCs and an elevated cardiovascular risk.5 However, like the earlier studies by Lee et al and Ataklte et al, only a minority of the included studies in Kiliç et al’s analysis employed diagnostic methods beyond patient history to exclude structural heart disease.

In contrast, some studies have instead shown a favourable short to mid-term prognosis for PVC patients where structural heart disease has been excluded with at least echocardiography.6 7 Accordingly, the impact of PVCs on the long-term prognosis of patients in whom structural heart disease has been ruled out remains unclear.

To evaluate the long-term relative survival of patients with PVCs without evidence of structural heart disease, we conducted an observational cohort study including only patients who had undergone a thorough cardiac evaluation showing no signs of structural abnormalities.

Methods

Study design

This observational cohort study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (STROBE)8 and the Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely-collected Health Data (RECORD) statement.9

Setting

We consecutively included patients who were diagnosed with PVCs at three hospital-based cardiology centres in Stockholm, Sweden, between 2010 and 2016. All patients who met the inclusion criteria and none of the exclusion criteria were included. The included hospitals are secondary centres with an approximate catchment area of about 15% of the Swedish population. The patients were referred to the cardiology centres by primary care physicians, emergency care providers or other cardiologists.

Study population

The International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) code to define PVCs was I49.3. Using the hospital databases, we identified all patients who were diagnosed with PVCs between 2010 and 2016. The PVC diagnosis was then confirmed by an experienced cardiologist (RS) through a review of the patient’s medical records. Only patients who had undergone long-term ECG recording (at least 24 hours) and had normal results on stress testing and echocardiography were included. An experienced clinical physiologist assessed all stress tests and echocardiographs. A normal stress test result was defined as the absence of electrocardiographic findings suggesting coronary artery disease or stress-induced ventricular arrhythmia. A normal echocardiography result was defined as a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of at least 55%, absence of more than mild valvular disease, normal ventricular dimensions and normal left ventricular wall thickness. To exclude structural heart disease, some patients underwent more advanced examinations such as MRI, cardiac scintigraphy or percutaneous coronary angiography at the request of the treating cardiologist. In such cases, we required that the supplementary examination had yielded normal results. All patients were linked to the National Patient Register, which provides access to all inpatient diagnoses nationwide since 1987 and diagnoses from specialised outpatient care nationwide since 2001.10 All patients with a history of heart disease (heart failure, myocardial infarction, cardiomyopathy, sustained ventricular tachycardia, survived cardiac arrest, moderate or severe valvular heart disease, myocarditis, previous cardiac surgery or percutaneous coronary revascularisation) were excluded. A flowchart of the inclusion process is shown in online supplemental figure 1. ICD-10 codes used to define baseline characteristics are listed in online supplemental table 1.

Exposure and outcomes

Exposure was defined as having received a PVC diagnosis (ICD-10 code I49.3) between 2010 and 2016, and that the PVC diagnosis was confirmed after review of the medical records. Outcome measures were all-cause mortality, observed versus expected survival and relative survival.

Data sources

The unique Swedish identity number11 was used to link patients to the National Patient Register,10 run by the National Board of Health and Welfare. The register contains data on all inpatient care since 1987 and hospital-linked outpatient care since 2001. The register was used to obtain data on baseline characteristics. The patients were also linked to the Swedish Cause of Death Register, also run by the National Board of Health and Welfare.12 The register contains all deaths in Sweden from 1952. The register was used to obtain vital status and date of death. Expected survival for the age-matched, sex-matched and calendar year-matched general population was obtained from the Human Mortality Database (http://www.mortality.org).13 The Human Mortality Database is an open-source database containing detailed and high-quality data on mortality and population estimates from numerous countries, including Sweden.

Statistical methods

Baseline characteristics are presented as medians and IQRs for continuous variables and as counts and percentages for categorical variables. All-cause mortality was assessed using the Kaplan-Meier survival function. Expected survival was assessed using data from the age-matched, sex-matched and calendar year-matched Swedish population obtained from the Human Mortality Database.

In survival analysis, overall survival is often used. A disadvantage of overall survival is that it includes all causes of death and not just death caused by the diagnosis of interest. However, using specific survival for a diagnosis of interest may lead to an underestimation of mortality from the disease, especially for diagnoses where it is not the disease itself that causes death but rather complications of the condition. Relative survival is a method for estimating cause-specific survival without information on the cause of death.14 15 This is achieved by dividing the overall survival in the study population by the expected survival of the general population. Relative survival was calculated using the Ederer II method.16 The Ederer II method adjusts the expected survival to account only for those patients who are alive at each time point. Subgroup analysis was performed based on age group, sex, PVC burden and symptoms at the time of diagnosis. Differences in relative survival between subgroups were analysed using a log-rank type test.

All patients were followed until the date of death or the end of follow-up (31 December 2023). Data management and statistical analyses were performed in R V.4.5.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Missing data

Data on PVC burden were missing for 91 (12%) patients. Saved 12-lead ECGs for determining PVC morphology were missing for 271 (34%) patients.

Results

Study population

A total of 803 PVC patients were included. Baseline characteristics are presented in table 1. The median age was 59 years (45–69), and 460 (57%) were women. Of the included patients, 185 (23%) had more than 10 000 PVCs per day and 86 (11%) had more than 20 000 PVCs per day. Among these patients, the most common morphology was that corresponding to a PVC origin in the right ventricular outflow tract (30%), followed by non-outflow tract left ventricular morphology (25%). Most patients (71%) reported symptoms related to PVCs at the time of diagnosis.

Survival

The mean follow-up time was 10 years (maximum 14 years), with a total follow-up time of 7906 patient-years. During follow-up, 64 patients died, corresponding to 9% of the PVC population. The survival at 1, 5, 10 and 12 years for the PVC patients was 100% (95% CI 99% to 100%), 97% (95% CI 96% to 98%), 93% (95% CI 91% to 94%) and 90% (95% CI 88% to 93%), respectively. The expected survival at 1, 5, 10 and 12 years from the age-matched, sex-matched and calendar year-matched Swedish population was 99%, 94%, 86% and 82%, respectively. Relative survival

Relative survival attempts to separate the mortality of the disease of interest from mortality due to all other causes. This is done by calculating the ratio between the observed survival (all-cause) of the study population and the expected survival of a similar group from the general population. One assumption using this method is that deaths related to PVCs are an insignificant part of all deaths in the general population. A relative survival rate less than 100% indicates that, during the specified time interval, the mortality in the study group exceeded that of the general population. A relative survival rate of 100% indicates that there is no excess mortality due to the disease of interest. Thus, the relative survival rate describes whether the disease of interest affects life expectancy.

The relative survival compared with the age-matched, sex-matched and calendar year-matched general Swedish population at 1, 5, 10 and 12 years was 100% (95% CI 100% to 101%), 104% (95% CI 102% to 105%), 109% (95% CI 107% to 111%) and 111% (95% CI 108% to 114%), respectively. Relative survival varied by age category, with higher relative survival at older ages. In the <60 years age category, the relative survival at 12 years was 102% (95% CI 102% to 103%), in the 60–70 years age category it was 113% (95% CI 109% to 118%) and in the >70 years age category it was 137% (95% CI 114% to 164%). There was a trend towards higher relative survival for men compared with women (at 12 years, 116% vs 108%), as well as for patients without symptoms compared with those with symptoms (at 12 years, 113% vs 111%), but the differences did not reach statistical significance. The relative survival did not significantly differ in relation to increased PVC burden. Discussion

In this observational cohort study of 803 patients with PVCs and no structural heart disease, the observed long-term survival of the PVC patients was higher than the expected survival from the age-matched, sex-matched and calendar year-matched Swedish population. At 12 years, the PVC cohort was 11% more likely to be alive, compared with the expected survival from the general population. With up to 14 years of follow-up, this is, to our knowledge, the most extensive study to examine the prognosis of a PVC cohort in which all participants have undergone at least echocardiography and stress test to rule out structural heart disease.

PVCs are known to be associated with adverse outcomes in patients with structural heart disease, but their prognostic significance in patients without such disease is still unclear. In 1985, Kennedy et al presented a landmark study investigating the long-term survival in 73 healthy participants with frequent and complex PVCs.17 The study had a median follow-up of 6.5 years and used a relative survival analysis. After excluding those with signs of cardiac disease through extensive testing, they found a favourable prognosis without an increased risk of death compared with the healthy US population, introducing the concept of the ‘benign PVC’.

Numerous studies have explored this topic using varying methods and with diverse results.1823 Many of them were part of the first meta-analysis on the subject, published by Lee et al.3 The analysis included 37 387 patients from eight studies published between 1971 and 2011 and showed an association between PVCs and adverse outcomes, including death, cardiovascular death, sudden cardiac death and ischaemic heart disease. The authors stated that many of the included studies did not use adequate diagnostic methods to rule out structural heart disease. They concluded that the risk of adverse outcomes was associated with the patient’s age and cardiovascular risk, suggesting that many of the studies may have included patients with occult structural heart disease. In 2013, Ataklte et al published a second meta-analysis, including 106 195 patients from 11 studies published between 1969 and 2011, showing that PVCs were associated with a substantially increased risk of sudden cardiac death and total cardiac death. Like the previous analysis by Lee et al, they noted that most studies did not use advanced testing, such as echocardiography, to exclude occult structural heart disease. It is worth noting that many studies included in these two meta-analyses are older, from a time when the possibilities for ruling out underlying structural heart disease were more limited than they are today. In 2025, Kiliç et al published a third meta-analysis, including 26 783 590 participants from 17 studies.5 They found that frequent PVCs (defined as ≥500 PVCs/day) were associated with an increased risk of atrial fibrillation (HR 1.69, 95% CI 1.39 to 2.05), heart failure (HR 1.73, 95% CI 1.50 to 2.00), stroke (HR 1.28, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.50) and all-cause mortality (HR 1.31, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.56). While this meta-analysis included more modern studies (published between 2009 and 2025), only 7 out of the 17 studies examined mortality and of these only 2 studies used echocardiography to rule out structural heart disease.7 24

A few studies have shown a more favourable prognosis for PVC patients when underlying structural heart disease has been excluded by at least echocardiography.6 7 17 18 In 2019, Lee et al studied 100 untreated PVC patients (mean age 52 years and median PVC burden 18.4%) who had a preserved LVEF.18 Over 5 years, only four patients developed left ventricular dysfunction (LVEF <50%), and many showed spontaneous PVC reduction. They concluded that in most patients with a preserved LVEF, active surveillance is a reasonable strategy due to the low risk of developing PVC-induced cardiomyopathy and the high degree of spontaneous improvement. These findings are somewhat in contrast with those of Dukes et al25 who presented a study showing an association between a higher frequency of PVCs and a decrease in LVEF, as well as increased mortality.25 However, the participants were all over the age of 65, and the study included participants with a history of coronary artery disease. In 2022, we presented results from the current cohort, showing a favourable mid-term prognosis compared with a sex-matched and age-matched population sample, after a median follow-up of 5.2 years.6 Unlike the current study, the follow-up period was significantly shorter and we did not present relative survival rates. In 2025, Ogiso et al studied 6332 patients without structural heart disease who underwent 24-hour ECG monitoring.7 They found no association between PVC frequency and heart failure or mortality, though the median follow-up was limited to 3 years.

In this current study, we aimed to exclude patients with underlying structural heart disease by only including those with normal results on at least echocardiography and a stress test, in accordance with the European Society of Cardiology guidelines at the time of inclusion.26 We found that the long-term survival in the PVC cohort exceeded the expected survival from the general population across all subgroups. The relative survival was highest in patients over 70 years (at 12 years 137%, 95% CI 114% to 164%). This is likely influenced by the exclusion of patients with structural heart disease, which is more prevalent in the general population at older ages, thereby shifting the results in favour of the PVC group. In the youngest age group (<60 years), where the prevalence of structural heart disease in the general population would be more uncommon, the relative survival at 12 years was 102% (95% CI 102% to 103%).

One somewhat surprising finding was that there was no clear association between PVC burden and survival. First, the observed survival in the PVC cohort was higher than the expected survival, regardless of the PVC burden. Second, while the group with the highest PVC burden (≥20 000 PVCs/day) had the lowest relative survival, no linear relationship was observed between PVC burden and relative survival, as the highest relative survival was found in the group with 10 000–19 999 PVCs per day, and not in the group with the lowest PVC burden. If the PVCs per se were the driver of complications and adverse outcomes, it would be reasonable to believe that the higher the PVC burden, the worse the prognosis. This highlights the importance of reliable methods of risk stratification, beyond the number of PVCs per day, to identify patients at risk of adverse events.

Another variable that has been linked to the prognosis of PVC patients is PVC morphology, with multifocal PVCs or PVCs originating in the left ventricle being associated with a worse outcome.27 28 In 2023, our research group presented a study investigating the effects of morphology on a composite endpoint of total mortality and cardiovascular morbidity, using a subset of patients from the current cohort.29 In that study, with a median follow-up of 5.3 years, outflow tract PVCs were associated with a more favourable prognosis than non-outflow tract PVCs and right ventricular PVCs were associated with a more favourable prognosis than left ventricular PVCs. In the current study, we did not investigate the role of PVC morphology on long-term survival, as we only had access to archived ECGs in a subset of patients. However, in this subgroup, the most common morphology was that of right ventricular outflow tract PVCs, followed by non-outflow-tract left ventricular PVCs. This is interesting, since right ventricular outflow tract PVCs are generally seen as the most common form of idiopathic PVC, and also the most benign, whereas non-outflow tract left ventricular PVCs are the form of PVC with the strongest association to underlying structural heart disease.30

Our study suggests a generally favourable prognosis for PVC patients without structural heart disease. However, PVC-induced cardiomyopathy is a known risk. While PVC burden is an important predictor of this condition, the picture is not entirely clear, as reduced cardiac function has been observed at PVC burdens as low as 6%, while others do not develop PVC-induced cardiomyopathy despite very frequent PVCs.31 Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that a subset of patients will experience this condition, which can be effectively treated with catheter ablation.32 Therefore, while most patients can be reassured after normal echocardiography and stress testing, regular follow-up in accordance with current guidelines is still essential.

Strengths and limitations

While providing a robust estimate of background mortality, the method of relative survival also has some drawbacks. First, the PVC patients in our study have all been seen by a cardiologist and undergone clinical investigation to exclude structural heart disease. In the general Swedish population, there will be an unknown number of matched controls with structural heart disease, which is likely to shift the study results in favour of the PVC group. Second, the PVC group was included at three hospitals in the Stockholm area, whereas the comparison cohort is from the whole country of Sweden. Regional differences regarding access to healthcare and patient demographics may have affected the study results. Between 2010 and 2023, residents of Stockholm County had a slightly higher average life expectancy than the population of the entire country. Life expectancy in Stockholm County ranged from 80.4 to 81.7 years for men and 84.2 to 85.4 years for women,33 compared with 79.9 to 81.2 years for men and 83.7 to 84.7 years for women across the country.33

Many patients experience a spontaneous decrease in PVC burden, which is likely to have a positive impact on their prognosis.18 In this study, we lack information on the development of PVC burden over time. Additionally, although we have information on medical treatment at inclusion, we lack information on subsequent treatment strategies, such as medical therapy or PVC ablation.

Only a subset of patients underwent cardiac CT angiography or cardiac MRI. We may have included patients with subtle myocardial dysfunction that was not identified by echocardiography.

Conclusion

In this observational cohort study, PVC patients without evidence of structural heart disease demonstrated favourable long-term survival, with no excess mortality compared with the age-matched, sex-matched and calendar year-matched general Swedish population. These findings support that many patients with PVCs can be given a reassuring message about their prognosis. Although comforting, a subset of patients will still experience adverse events, and future studies should focus on how to find patients at risk who may need closer follow-up and potential treatment.
