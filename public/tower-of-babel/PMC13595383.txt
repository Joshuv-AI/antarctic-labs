Comparing In Vivo Methylmercury Detoxification in Hunted Duck: Implications for Wildlife and Human Health
Authors: Samuel F. Lopez, Sarah E. Janssen, Brett A. Poulin, William P. Johnson, Michael T. Tate, Pieter Glatzel, Tylor J. Rosera, Grace J. Armstrong, Alain Manceau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Great Salt Lake (GSL) waterfowl have elevated mercury (Hg) concentrations, and in vivo detoxification of neurotoxic and bioaccumulative methylmercury (MeHg) can occur through Hg-selenium (Se) complexation, influencing the suitability of Hg consumption advisories. Here, we present Hg chemical speciation and Hg stable isotope measurements of brain, breast muscle, liver, and kidney samples from two GSL duck speciesnorthern shoveler (Spatula clypeata) and cinnamon teal (Spatula cyanoptera). Chemical and stable isotope measurements, along with high energy resolution fluorescence detected X-ray absorption near-edge structure (HERFD-XANES) spectroscopy, indicate the occurrence of in vivo MeHg demethylation in both bird species. The percentage of total Hg (THg) as MeHg (% MeHg) varied with tissue type (brain > breast muscle > kidney > liver), and differences between δ202 THg and δ202 MeHg were linearly correlated with % MeHg. Demethylated inorganic Hg was a mixture of Hg-dithiolate (Hg­(SR)2) and Hg-tetraselenolate (Hg­(Sec)4) in both bird species. Notably, liver THg concentrations were elevated in northern shoveler liver tissues relative to those of cinnamon teal (7.54 ± 4.69 mg/kg versus 2.19 ± 1.27 mg/kg, dry weight, respectively) and % MeHg liver values were significantly lower (40% ± 19% versus 68% ± 14%), indicating taxonomic differences in Hg detoxification and depuration. THg concentrations in waterfowl species from this study were comparable to previously reported levels, indicating that elevated Hg concentrations in northern shoveler and cinnamon teal at GSL have persisted over the past two decades. Due to in vivo demethylation, we show that THg is not an effective proxy for MeHg within duck species, carrying implications for current GSL waterfowl consumption advisories and assessments exposure risk. This study highlights important differences in MeHg detoxification between waterbird taxa and provides further insights into Hg toxicity risk to GSL waterbirds.

Introduction

Great Salt Lake (GSL) is a large terminal lake adjacent to Salt Lake City, Utah, USA, that is designated as a “Site of Hemispheric Importance” by the Western Shorebird Reserve Network. Over 10 million birds utilize GSL wetland and open water habitats during portions of their biannual migrations and/or for breeding and staging activities. , However, in 2005–2006, unprecedented mercury (Hg) consumption advisories were issued for three species of duck, cinnamon teal (Spatula cyanoptera), common goldeneye (Bucephala clangula), and northern shoveler (Spatula clypeata), following the discovery of total Hg (THg) concentrations exceeding the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) screening value of 0.3 mg/kg wet weight (w.w.) for methylmercury (MeHg) in the breast muscle tissue. , These advisories were implemented to protect human health, specifically waterfowl hunting communities that utilize GSL. The discovery of elevated Hg burdens in waterfowl coupled with observations of elevated water column MeHg (exceeding 30 ng/L in GSL bottom waters − and 10 ng/L in adjacent wetland habitat) raised additional concern about toxicological impacts of Hg bioaccumulation and biomagnification to GSL waterbirds.

Waterfowl consumption advisories for the GSL system have been updated recently on the basis of results from monitoring efforts led by the Utah Department of Environmental Quality (UTDEQ) and Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (UTDWR). Consumption advisories for northern shoveler and cinnamon teal were removed in 2022 following a Fall 2021 sampling campaign where lower Hg concentrations in breast muscle tissue samples were observed for both species (northern shoveler: 0.185 mg/kg, n = 25; cinnamon teal: 0.220 mg/kg, n = 21). , In September of 2025, based on results from an additional 2024/2025 sampling campaign, Hg consumption advisories for common goldeneye were also removed (0.229 mg/kg THg, n = 8). , However, consumption advisories for northern shoveler were reinstated following discovery of elevated Hg burdens (0.665 mg/kg THg, n = 30) compared to 2021/2022 levels. , Cinnamon teal were not monitored in 2024/2025, so it is not known whether Hg burdens in cinnamon teal are higher relative to 2021/2022 conditions.

Although MeHg poses the greatest risk to wildlife and human health, THg concentrations (a summation of both MeHg and inorganic Hg) in the breast muscle tissue have been utilized as a proxy for MeHg in previous GSL waterfowl monitoring efforts informing consumption advisory implementation. , The assumptions guiding this approach are as follows (1) MeHg is the primary form of Hg that bioaccumulates, (2) Hg in muscle tissues is primarily MeHg, and (3) breast muscle tissue concentrations are likely most representative of consumer exposure. However, this approach does not consider processes that may alter Hg speciation and distribution after the dietary uptake. Toxicological risk of MeHg, which accumulates in higher trophic level organisms through dietary sources, is in part governed by internal processes after dietary uptake, including depuration to external tissues (e.g., feathers), maternal transfer to offspring (e.g., eggs), and, most relevant to this study, in vivo degradation to inorganic Hg and potential excretion from the body. , Following assimilation into the bloodstream via uptake in the gastrointestinal tract, MeHg is retained in the proteins of tissues as a MeHg-cysteine (MeHg–Cys) complex. MeHg demethylation has been documented to occur primarily in the liver and kidney tissues in vertebrates, including giant petrel (Macronectes spp.), emperor penguins (Aptenodytes forsteri), long-finned pilot whales (Globicephala melas), − and multiple waterbird species, , and demethylation has also been documented in the spleen of Atlantic bluefin tuna (Thunnus thynnus). Analysis of vertebrate species has revealed that a common MeHg detoxification pathway is the demethylation of MeHg–Cys to primarily Hg-tetraselenolate (Hg­(Sec)4) and perceived minor Hg-dithiolate (Hg­(SR)2) − ,− , and Hg-diselenolate (Hg­(Sec)2) complexes, which can be further transformed into nanoparticulate Hg-selenide (HgSe(s)) via biomineralization. ,,− In birds, biomineralization of Hg as nanoparticulate HgSe(s) has only been observed in a higher trophic level, pelagic seabirds (giant petrel), ,, while detoxification of MeHg to Hg­(Sec)4 and Hg­(SR)2 (interpreted to be intermediates to HgSe(s)) has been documented in tissues of waterbirds and penguins with incomplete demethylation (e.g., % MeHg values ≥14%). ,

The application of high energy resolution fluorescence detected X-ray absorption near-edge structure (HERFD-XANES) spectroscopy has allowed for identification and quantification of biologically relevant Hg compounds (e.g., MeHg–Cys, Hg­(Sec)4, Hg­(SR)2, Hg­(Sec)2, and nanoparticulate HgSe(s)) in biological tissues; however, these measurements are difficult to perform due to the limited number of facilities with HERFD-XANES capabilities. − Recently, Hg stable isotope measurements were used to examine mass-dependent fractionation (MDFdenoted by δ202 THg) arising from in vivo demethylation of MeHg in waterbirds, fish, and marine mammals. − ,,, A product-reactant isotopic enrichment factor (εp/r) of −2.2 ± 0.1‰ associated with the demethylation of MeHg–Cys to inorganic Hg was calculated for waterbirds in which MeHg–Cys and Hg­(Sec)4, were the primary Hg species observed, along with perceived minor Hg­(SR)2 and Hg­(Sec)2 complexes. A later study of mercury detoxification in emperor penguins yielded distinct but similar Hg species-specific values for εHg(SR)2/MeHg (−1.9 ± 0.2‰) and εHg(Sec)4/MeHg (−2.3 ± 0.2‰). Further biomineralization of Hg­(Sec)4 to nanoparticulate HgSe(s) induced positive MDF, with an εHgSe/Hg(Sec)4 value of +1.6‰ documented in giant petrel tissues. Anomalies in odd isotope mass independent fractionation (MIF) of Hg isotopes (denoted by Δ199THg) have also been attributed to a nuclear volume effect (NVE) during the biomineralization of Hg­(Sec)4 to HgSe(s). Coupled chemical speciation and isotope analyses of Hg on sets of tissue samples can therefore provide insights into how wildlife internally process MeHg, which carries implications for toxicological risk and use of stable Hg isotope measurements for source apportionment of Hg.

Here, liver, kidney, brain, and breast muscle tissue samples from northern shoveler and cinnamon teal specimens collected at GSL were measured for Hg concentrations (THg and MeHg) and Hg stable isotope ratios, with a subset of samples measured for Hg chemical speciation via HERFD-XANES. The study goals were to determine whether contrasts in Hg burdens and detoxification efficiencies exist between these two duck species and others reported in previous studies. We posited that these two duck species would exhibit the MeHg to Hg­(Sec)4 demethylation reaction and associated εHg(Sec)4/MeHg observed in other waterbirds. Other primary goals were to characterize variability of Hg concentrations within different tissues (brain, liver kidney, and breast muscle) and to compare tissue Hg concentrations to toxicity reference values and EPA criterion to further assess toxicological risks of Hg to GSL waterfowl and to human consumers. This study provides insights into MeHg detoxification in waterfowl, including differences in in vivo detoxification between different bird species/taxa, and provides a critical examination of the role of in vivo MeHg demethylation in influencing estimated risk to wildlife and the suitability of consumption advisories.

Materials and Methods

Biological Tissues

Northern shoveler and cinnamon teal were collected between November 2021 and May 2022 by UTDWR from three wetland locations along the eastern shore of GSL: Farmington Bay, Ogden Bay, and Harold Crane (adjacent to Bear River Bay) Waterfowl Management Areas (Figure S1). These collections were conducted to characterize Hg concentrations in waterfowl muscle tissues to inform UTDEQ updates to GSL Hg consumption advisories. UTDWR provided a subset of specimens from the larger collection effort for this study (northern shoveler: n = 8, adult; cinnamon teal: n = 9, adult). Each individual specimen was necropsied to obtain brain, liver, kidney, and breast muscle tissues. The brain tissue had been compromised in one northern shoveler during collection efforts, and brain tissue data were therefore not available for this individual. Tissues were frozen at −20 °C until lyophilization and homogenization via a ball mill and were subsequently stored in a cool dark environment until analysis. Weights were obtained pre- and post-lyophilization, which were used to gravimetrically calculate water content of individual tissue samples. Additional lyophilized and homogenized tissue samples from individual adult northern shoveler (liver, n = 48) and cinnamon teal (liver, n = 29; breast muscle, n = 27) harvested in 2008 by UTDWR were also utilized in this study for comparison of past and present conditions.

THg and MeHg Concentration Analyses

Concentration analyses for THg and MeHg were performed by either the University of Utah (UU, Salt Lake City, Utah) or the U.S. Geological Survey M3 Research Laboratory (USGS M3RL, Madison, Wisconsin). A brief overview of analytical procedures is provided, though complete details of materials and methods can be found at https://www.usgs.gov/labs/m3-research-laboratory.

For MeHg analysis, approximately 10 mg of sample was digested in 4.5 mol/L OmniTrace (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) nitric acid (HNO3) for 8 h to break down organic matter in tissues, and the resulting extract was analyzed for MeHg via ethylation coupled to gas chromatography and cold vapor atomic fluorescence spectrometry. Recoveries from certified reference materials (CRMs), International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) 407 (95% ± 7%, n = 12; fish tissue) and IAEA 436 (97% ± 8%, n = 12; tuna fish flesh homogenate), were acceptable, and the relative standard deviation (RSD) of all triplicate samples (n = 8) was <5%. For THg analysis, approximately 10 to 50 mg of lyophilized tissue sample was analyzed via thermal decomposition using a modified version of EPA Method 7473. Recoveries from CRMs were acceptable (IAEA 407 = 104% ± 3%, n = 29; IAEA 436 = 105% ± 6%, n = 29), and the RSD of all triplicate samples was <5%.

Both THg and MeHg concentrations in tissue samples are reported on a dry-weight basis. Dry-weight concentrations were converted to wet weight (w.w.) using the measured moisture content for comparison to the EPA MeHg criterion of 0.3 mg/kg (w.w.) for the protection of human health and for comparison to other GSL monitoring efforts where tissue concentrations were reported on a wet weight basis.

HERFD-XANES Measurements

Hg L3-edge XANES spectra were measured at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility Extremely Brilliant Source (ESRF-EBS) on the ID26 beamline in the high energy resolution fluorescence detection mode (HERFD). ,,− The intensity of the Hg Lα1 line was measured by means of the Si(555) reflection of five crystals of 10 cm diameter spherically bent to a radius of 0.5 m. Energy was calibrated by assigning the maximum of the near-edge structure of the MeHg–Cys complex to 12279.8 eV, consistent with previous studies. ,, Spectra (12260 to 12360 eV in 0.2 eV steps) were collected on pressed pellets (5 mm diameter, 3 mm thick) at a temperature of 10–15 K and a scan time of 5 s to reduce exposure and repeated at pristine positions on the sample to increase the signal-to-noise ratio. Individual X-ray absorption scans were averaged, normalized to unity at E = 12360 eV, and data reduction and analysis were carried out as previously described. , Reference spectra of relevant chemical species included MeHg–Cys, Hg­(SR)2 (pH 7.4), MeHg-selenocysteine (MeHg-Sec), Hg­(Sec)4, Hg­(Sec)2, and nanoparticulate HgSe(s). The MeHg–Cys, Hg­(Sec)4, and Hg­(SR)2 spectra used for fitting were determined by iterative transformation factor analysis (ITFA) of spectra of biological samples collected over the beam session. HERFD-XANES spectra of the references and bird tissues (n = 9) are provided in the associated USGS ScienceBase data release. The proportions of the Hg species in tissues were evaluated using least-squares fitting (LSF) of the data with linear combinations of reference spectra, and the goodness of fit was assessed by the normalized sum-squared (NSS) difference between two spectra expressed as 1 NSS=∑(yexp−yfit)2∑yexp2 where y exp and y fit are experimental and fit values, respectively. The precision of a fit component was estimated to be equal to the variation of its value when the fit residual (NSS) was increased by 20%.

Hg Stable Isotope Analyses

Hg stable isotope measurements of bird tissues were made on both THg (n = 51) and MeHg (n = 56) at the USGS M3RL. Tissue samples for THg stable isotopes were prepared via digestion at 90 °C for 8–10 h in concentrated OmniTrace HNO3 followed by addition of 10% volume per volume (v/v) bromine monochloride (BrCl). Recoveries from CRMs from acid digests were acceptable (IAEA 407 = 110% ± 6%, n = 13).

Tissue samples were prepared for MeHg stable isotope analysis via distillation according to a modified version of EPA Method 1630. , Following distillation, distillates were acidified with OmniTrace HCl (1% v/v) and assessed for MeHg recovery, while an additional aliquot was oxidized with BrCl and analyzed for THg to assess for inorganic Hg (iHg) carryover. All sample distillates (% MeHg: 97% ± 7%, n = 69) and CRM distillates (% MeHg: 97% ± 8%, n = 16) contained little iHg impurity, so further separation via anion exchange chromatography was not necessary. Distillation recoveries greater than 80% were required for MeHg stable isotope analysis, a lower criterion was set for environmental samples because of variability (±10%) in original MeHg analyses as well as post-distillation recoveries (e.g., comparing two experimental values each with potential error). MeHg recoveries from distillation of samples (87% ± 6%, n = 69) and CRMs (DOLT-3: 90% ± 6%, n = 16) all exceeded 80% and were acceptable. MeHg in distillates was oxidized to Hg­(II) via addition of BrCl (1% v/v) at least 1 week prior to stable isotope analysis.

Hg stable isotopes were measured on a Neptune Plus multicollector inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer equipped with a custom gas liquid separator and using thallium (40 ng/mL) as a mass bias correction. The instrument was tuned to provide maximum signal strength and intensity (approximately 1 V per 1 ng/mL Hg solution) using previously outlined methods. Samples were analyzed using standard-sample bracketing with the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) 3133 and reported in delta notation. Sample digests of THg and distillates of MeHg were diluted prior to isotope analysis to <10% acid content and a Hg concentration between 0.5 and 1 ng/mL. A secondary isotope standard (NIST RM 8610) was monitored during sample analysis to ensure measurement precision and accuracy (δ202 THg = −0.55 ± 0.04‰, Δ199THg = −0.03 ± 0.03‰, Δ200THg = 0.00 ± 0.02‰, 2SD, n = 44). Isotopic data on all certified reference materials and standards, along with all sample results, are provided in the USGS data release associated with this publication (10.5066/P13XSQWN).

Data Analysis

All data visualization and statistical analyses were performed in OriginPro 2023. Differences in tissue Hg concentrations and % MeHg values between different tissues of each bird species were assessed via one-way ANOVA, and Tukey’s Honest Significant Different Test was used for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Differences in Hg concentrations and % MeHg values in the same tissue type between bird species were assessed via a two-sample t-test. Statistical significance for pairwise comparisons was assessed at the p < 0.05 level. The relationship between Δ199THg versus Δ201THg was evaluated with linear regressions weighted for both X and Y error of individual measurements.

Risk to waterfowl was assessed using the Bird Mercury Tool , which was developed to aid interpretation of injury to birds caused by tissue Hg concentrations. The tool was used to convert THg tissue concentrations to adult blood-equivalent THg concentrations for comparison to Toxicity Reference Values (TRVs) and effects concentrations (EC) to generate an estimate of percent injury to birds. A level of risk was assigned according to the following classification: none: < EC1%; low: ≥ EC1% and < EC5%; moderate: ≥ EC5% and < EC10%; high: ≥ EC10% and < EC20%; severe: ≥ EC20%. When applying the Bird Mercury Tool to assess toxicity risk to GSL waterfowl, we utilized the tool’s default suggested TRVs. These TRVs are provided in Table 9 of Ackerman et al. (2024).

Results and Discussion

Mercury Concentrations and Speciation in Tissues

In both northern shoveler (n = 8) and cinnamon teal (n = 9) collected in 2021–2022, mean THg concentrations exhibited a hierarchy by the tissue type, with liver and kidney tissues having higher THg concentrations than breast muscle and brain tissues (Figure a). Liver THg concentrations in northern shoveler (7.54 ± 4.69 mg/kg, dry weight [d.w.]) were significantly higher (p < 0.05) than brain (1.11 ± 0.88 mg/kg), kidney (2.76 ± 2.05 mg/kg), and breast muscle tissues (1.15 ± 0.67 mg/kg), while liver THg concentrations in cinnamon teal (2.19 ± 1.27 mg/kg) were significantly higher than brain (0.67 ± 0.43 mg/kg) and breast muscle tissues (0.79 ± 0.44 mg/kg), but nearly equivalent to that of kidneys (1.96 ± 1.15 mg/kg) (Table S1). While mean brain, kidney, and breast muscle tissue THg concentrations were not statistically different across species, mean liver THg concentrations in northern shoveler (7.54 ± 4.69 mg/kg) were approximately 4× higher than those in cinnamon teal (2.19 ± 1.27 mg/kg; t-test, p < 0.01). The ratio of THg concentration in the liver compared to the brain were on average 7 in northern shoveler and 3 in cinnamon teal (Figure a).

Box plots of (a) THg concentrations, (b) MeHg concentrations, and (c) percent MeHg values in brain, liver, kidney, and breast muscle tissues from northern shoveler (gray, n = 8) and cinnamon teal (orange, n = 9). Black diamonds represent measurements on tissue samples, open squares represent mean values, middle lines represent median values, and stems represent data within a factor of 1.5 of the interquartile range.

MeHg concentrations were similar across the two duck species and showed similar trends with tissue type (Figure b). The trend in MeHg concentration with tissue type were similar to trends observed for THg, but with a smaller range and with greater similarity among livers between bird species (northern shoveler: 2.81 ± 2.43 mg/kg, d.w.; cinnamon teal: 1.46 ± 0.88 mg/kg) and kidneys (northern shoveler: 1.69 ± 1.24 mg/kg; cinnamon teal: 1.30 ± 0.73 mg/kg). In both bird species, livers and kidneys showed elevated MeHg concentration relative to brains (northern shoveler: 0.95 ± 0.78 mg/kg; cinnamon teal: 0.57 ± 0.37 mg/kg) and breast muscles (northern shoveler: 0.83 ± 0.52 mg/kg; cinnamon teal: 0.63 ± 0.34 mg/kg) (Figure b and Table S2).

The percentage of THg as MeHg (% MeHg), as quantified by chemical measurements, differed with tissue type and followed the following hierarchy for both species: brain > breast muscle > kidney > liver (Figure c), consistent with previous studies on waterbirds documenting a greater degree of internal demethylation in kidney and liver tissues. ,,,, Mean % MeHg were similar between bird species in brain tissues (northern shoveler: 85% ± 4%; cinnamon teal: 85% ± 5%). However, mean % MeHg values were lower in northern shoveler relative to cinnamon teal for breast muscle (72% versus 78%), kidney (63% versus 68%), and liver tissues (40% versus 68%) (Table S3 and Figure c), with this difference being statistically significant in liver tissues (t-test, p < 0.01).

The HERFD-XANES spectra on a subset of tissues from the two bird species showed consistent shifts among tissues that are diagnostic of differences in Hg speciation. All brain and muscle tissues exhibited a sharp near-edge peak at 12,279.8 eV that is unique to MeHg–Cys, indicating a greater percentage of MeHg in these tissues (Figure S2). With the exception of one liver sample from cinnamon teal no. 4, liver and kidney spectra exhibited decreased amplitude of the near-edge MeHg–Cys peak, which is due to a greater proportion of demethylated Hg species in these tissues (Figure S2). Structural assignment from LSF analysis indicates that the demethylated Hg pool in the livers and kidneys of both bird species is a mixture of Hg­(Sec)4 and Hg­(SR)2, whereas demethylated Hg exists solely as Hg­(Sec)4 in the brain and breast muscle tissues (Table ). HgSe(s) was not identified in any of the tissue samples. The quantified % MeHg between chemical measurements and HERFD-XANES spectra was in excellent agreement and agreed within precision of the two independent methods across the samples in this study (Table ). Structural assignments from LSF analysis are based on the current best available library of reference spectra and the known biochemical transformation pathways of Hg.

Mercury Stable Isotope Values in Tissues

Mercury stable isotope measurements were evaluated from all the northern shoveler (n = 8) and cinnamon teal (n = 9) tissues for both the THg and MeHg isotope pools. Δ199THg was largely conserved among tissue sets (brain, liver, kidney, and breast muscles) within individual birds of both species (Figure ) despite variability in % MeHg (Figure c) and δ202 THg among tissue types (Figure ). Invariance of Δ199THg indicates an absence of MIF associated with internal Hg transformations, which is consistent with a previous study of waterbirds exhibiting internal Hg demethylation to inorganic species of Hg­(Sec)4 and Hg­(SR)2). One liver tissue sample from northern shoveler (northern shoveler no. 1) had high Δ199THg (2.99‰) compared with Δ199THg in the brain, kidney, and breast muscle tissues (2.08 ± 0.12‰, n = 3); however, we treat this sample as anomalous given the consistency of Δ199THg across all other bird tissue sets (Figures a,b). This anomalously high Δ199THg may indicate recent foraging in a different GSL habitat (e.g., open water versus wetland, shallow versus deep water column, etc. − ) or recent arrival at GSL from another location prior to collection from wetland habitats (Figure S1). The slope of Δ199THg/Δ201THg (northern shoveler: Δ199THg/Δ201THg = 1.12; cinnamon teal: Δ199THg/Δ201THg = 1.13) (Figure S3) is characteristic of MIF induced by the magnetic isotope effect during photochemical degradation of MeHg in the water column prior to bioaccumulation and is similar to that in invertebrate samples collected from GSL (Δ199THg/Δ201THg = 1.13) and in waterfowl collected from other locations. Based on these data, the observed odd-MIF preserved in northern shoveler and cinnamon teal tissues likely occurs prior to uptake and is a representative of MeHg in dietary items within GSL and surrounding wetlands.

In both northern shoveler and cinnamon teal, δ202THg differed systematically within tissues of individual birds based on the chemical speciation. Northern shoveler had largest differences between δ202THg within tissues of individual birds (up to 2‰ in northern shoveler no. 3), with liver samples having depleted δ202THg values and lower % MeHg values relative to other tissues (Figure a). In cinnamon teal, smaller differences in δ202THg were observed within tissues of individual birds (up to 1.1‰ in cinnamon teal no. 6 but negligible in cinnamon teal no. 2), with liver and kidney samples generally having depleted δ202THg values and lower % MeHg values relative to other tissues (Figure b). Previous studies of waterbirds and pelagic seabirds show that δ202MeHg exhibits little variation across different tissues of individual birds, , which is hypothesized to result from rapid replenishment relative to demethylation from a continuous supply of dietary MeHg with a relatively unvarying isotopic composition. Both δ202MeHg and Δ199MeHg showed little variability between tissues within individual birds (Figure c,d), regardless of differences in Hg speciation. This suggests that isotopic fractionation in both northern shoveler and cinnamon teal is best conceptualized as an open system with a continuous supply of reactant (MeHg) that is constantly replenished by dietary uptake, consistent with observations of isotopic fractionation in previous studies of waterbirds. ,

Differences between δ202THg and δ202MeHg (δ202THg–δ202MeHg) show strong linear correlation with % MeHg values in both northern shoveler (Figure a) and cinnamon teal (Figure b), suggesting that variation in δ202THg in tissues can be explained by the mixing of isotopically distinct iHg and MeHg endmembers. The linear correlation between δ202THg–δ202MeHg and % MeHg is interpreted to represent the isotopic fractionation of Hg during internal demethylation. Structural assignments indicate that iHg in northern shoveler and cinnamon teal tissues is a mix of Hg­(Sec)4 and Hg­(SR)2 (Table ), which suggests that these two chemical species are isotopically similar and are both products of in vivo demethylation, consistent with observation in other water birds. ,

We calculated the product-reactant isotope enrichment factor using the same approach outlined for other waterfowl which assumed an open system model with an continuous supply of reactant (i.e., dietary sources of MeHg) and a unidirectional reaction and instantaneous product. , A product-reactant isotope enrichment factor (εp/r) for the demethylation reaction of MeHg to iHg was determined from the linear regression between δ202THgδ202MeHg and % MeHg (Figure ). At 100% MeHg, the MeHg and THg pools are equivalent, yielding a δ202THg–δ202MeHg difference of near 0‰ in both duck species. The iHg end-member is represented by the theoretical y intercept at 0% MeHg, yielding εp/r for the demethylation reaction of MeHg to iHg that is −2.6 ± 0.4‰ in northern shovelers (Figure a) and −2.5 ± 0.2‰ in cinnamon teal (Figure b). The enrichment factors derived for both species are similar to εp/r = −2.2‰ for the demethylation of MeHg to iHg observed in other waterfowl with similar ranges of tissue % MeHg values, and to εp/r = −2.3‰ observed in emperor penguins.

Contrasts between Duck Species

The two bird species exhibited distinct differences in % MeHg values in liver tissues, with northern shoveler exhibiting lower % MeHg than cinnamon teal (p < 0.01) (Figure and Table S3). While similarity in εp/r for the two bird species and to literature values indicates demethylation via the same mechanism (MeHg → Hg­(Sec)4 or Hg­(SR)2), contrasts in % MeHg values may indicate differences in demethylation efficiency. The demethylation of MeHg to iHg has previously been noted to correlate with increased concentrations of THg in specific tissues, as noted in fish and birds. ,,, In northern shoveler tissues, there is a strong positive correlation (p < 0.01) between brain tissue-normalized THg concentrations and % iHg (Figure a), but this significant relationship does not exist in cinnamon teal tissues (p = 0.16) (Figure b). In northern shoveler, the presence of this relationship may indicate relatively slow depuration of demethylated Hg species (e.g., Hg­(Sec)4), as has been observed in other organisms. ,, The absence of a relationship between brain-normalized THg and % iHg in cinnamon teal tissues may be explained by several factors, including lower rates of demethylation and accumulation of demethylated products in liver tissues, or greater depuration of demethylated species relative to northern shoveler. The possibility of differences in diet and greater accumulation of exogenous iHg in northern shoveler was also considered. However, iHg within either species does not seem to be exogenous because we did not observe the expected differences in odd-MIF between tissues with varying %iHg due to an isotopic offset between iHg and MeHg pools induced by photochemical fractionation prior to uptake. In addition to calculated εp/r for both species being similar to one another and literature values, , this suggests that the presence of iHg in the tissues of both species is the result of in vivo demethylation. Therefore, we propose that contrasting %iHg values are a result of physiological differences in in vivo demethylation between these similar species; however, additional studies would be needed to test this hypothesis.

Hg demethylation in adult waterbird livers has been proposed to be a concentration-sensitive process, with demethylation occurring after THg has reached a threshold concentration. The observed threshold value was approximately 9.90 mg/kg (d.w.) in American avocet (Recurvirostra americana) and black-necked stilt (Himantopus mexicanus) (family: Recurvirostridae), and 7.50 mg/kg in Caspian tern (Hydroprogne caspia) and Forster’s tern (Sterna forsteri) (family: Laridae), with the difference in the threshold value attributed to taxonomic differences in Hg demethylation. In contrast, there is no strong evidence for a demethylation threshold in both northern shoveler and cinnamon teal (family: Anatidae), with liver % MeHg values of 58% ± 18% (n = 56) and 78% ± 15% (n = 38), respectively, in samples collected from 2008 and 2021/2022 (Figure S4). No discernible trends were observed between the THg concentration and % MeHg in both species (Figure S4), and chemical (Figure ), stable isotope (Figure ), and XANES (Table ) measurements provide strong evidence for in vivo demethylation of MeHg in liver tissues.

The lack of observed demethylation thresholds in northern shoveler and cinnamon teal further supports the possibility that the observed contrast in liver % MeHg (Figure ) is driven by a physiological difference in MeHg detoxification rather than accumulation of greater THg concentrations in northern shoveler. Additionally, these findings indicate that demethylation in livers of ducks (family: Anatidae) may generally occur at lower THg concentrations than in livers of terns (family: Laridae) and avocets/stilts (family: Recurvirostridae), which indicates important taxonomic differences in MeHg detoxification for waterfowl arising from biochemical (e.g., expression of selenoproteins or enzyme activity) or metabolic differences. Structural assignments suggest that demethylated Hg species in northern shoveler and cinnamon teal primarily consist of Hg­(Sec)4 and Hg­(SR)2 complexes, as previously shown for Clark’s grebe (Aechmophorus clarkii), Forster’s tern (S. forsteri), south polar skua (Stercorarius maccormicki), and emperor penguins. The formation of Hg­(Sec)4 was measured in protein extracts that contained Hg bound to selenoprotein P (SelP), proposed to facilitate the demethylation reaction. The exact mechanism resulting in the formation of Hg­(SR)2 via MeHg demethylation in multicellular organisms remains unknown and requires further evaluation. HgSe(s) granules, a product associated with further biomineralization of Hg­(Sec)4, were observed in liver tissues of larger higher trophic level birds such as giant petrels, ,, golden eagles (Aquila chrysaetos), black-footed albatross (Phoebastria nigripes), and cormorants. Whether this represents a greater ability of higher trophic level organisms to convert Hg­(Sec)4 to HgSe(s) or is a response driven by accumulation of extremely high (>100 mg/kg) tissue Hg concentrations ,, is not known.

Implications of In Vivo Methylmercury Detoxification for Consumption Advisories

Dry weight THg and MeHg tissue concentrations were converted to wet weight (w.w.) for comparison to the EPA MeHg criterion of 0.3 mg/kg (w.w.). In waterfowl analyzed in this study, THg in muscle tissues collected during the 2021–2022 sampling season exceeded the EPA MeHg criterion in 4 out of 8 and 2 out of 9 northern shoveler and cinnamon teal, respectively (Figure ). The mean THg concentration in the cinnamon teal muscle tissue (0.220 ± 0.124 mg/kg w.w.) was below the EPA MeHg criterion, and the mean THg concentration in the northern shoveler muscle exceeded the 0.30 mg/kg (w.w.) EPA criterion. However, using THg concentrations as a proxy for MeHg in waterfowl assessments may overestimate human health risks through dietary exposure pathways. For example, though it is commonly assumed that nearly 100% of THg exists as MeHg in muscle tissues, more than 20% of THg occurred as iHg species in the eight northern shoveler specimens analyzed in this study due to internal demethylation (Figure ). Although the mean muscle tissue THg concentration for these eight individuals exceeded the US EPA MeHg criterion value of 0.3 mg/kg, the mean MeHg concentration (0.244 ± 0.157 mg/kg, w.w; n = 8) was lower.

Utilizing THg rather than MeHg values to assess risk to human health via dietary exposure is even more consequential for other tissue types with lower % MeHg values. For instance, livers may be consumed by those harvesting GSL waterfowl, and THg concentrations in both 2021/2022 northern shoveler liver tissue (2.350 ± 1.470 mg/kg w.w., n = 8) and cinnamon teal liver tissue (0.648 ± 0.371 mg/kg w.w., n = 9) collected in 2021/2022 both greatly exceeded the 0.3 mg/kg EPA MeHg criterion. However, MeHg concentrations were far lower; northern shoveler livers had mean MeHg concentrations of 0.880 ± 0.801 mg/kg w.w. (n = 8), and mean MeHg concentrations in cinnamon teal livers were 0.435 ± 0.265 mg/kg w.w. (n = 9). Though both mean MeHg liver values still exceed the US EPA MeHg criterion, suggesting human consumption of liver tissue poses a greater risk than consumption of breast muscle tissue for these GSL waterfowl species, mean MeHg liver values are far closer to the 0.3 mg/kg w.w. US EPA criterion than estimates that use THg as a proxy for MeHg.

These concepts extend beyond the GSL system to other taxonomic groups. For instance, variation in % MeHg values has also been observed in muscle tissues of sport fish, which are often assumed to contain 100% of THg as MeHg. In certain fish species, using THg as a proxy for MeHg may overestimate the exposure risk. For example, muscle tissue samples from blue marlin (Makaira nigricans) collected in open-ocean environments contained mostly (>80%) detoxified Hg species (Hg­(Sec)4 and HgSe(s)), and a recent analysis of bluefin tuna demonstrated that up to 25% of THg in skeletal muscles is present as Hg­(Sec)4. These demethylated iHg species are understood to be far less hazardous than MeHg–Cys with regard to human consumption. , In such cases, using THg as a proxy for MeHg to compare to the EPA MeHg criterion is not accurate. We propose that chemical speciation (e.g., % MeHg) be considered in assessments related to consumption advisories, and we argue that use of THg as a proxy for MeHg, especially in comparison to MeHg dietary criteria for human health, is not appropriate without definitive knowledge of Hg speciation.

Implications of In Vivo Methylmercury Detoxification for Wildlife Risk Assessment

In addition to influencing human exposure risk and the suitability of consumption advisories, in vivo MeHg detoxification also governs the toxicological risk to wildlife. Here, we demonstrate that due to in vivo demethylation, using THg concentrations as a proxy for MeHg is not always appropriate when assessing the risk to wildlife. For this assessment, we utilized the Bird Mercury Tool, , which assumes that provided Hg concentrations are either a direct measurement of MeHg or associated with a sample with high % MeHg (e.g., use of THg as a proxy for MeHg is valid).

As a case study, we used the tool to estimate injury risk based on THg and MeHg concentrations in the liver and muscle tissues of northern shoveler no. 3. The liver THg concentration in northern shoveler no. 3 is 3.36 mg/kg w.w., which when compared to the suggested Hg TRVs utilized by the Bird Mercury Tool (refer to Table 9 in Ackerman et al. [2024]), , corresponds to a high risk of injury to birds. However, nearly 75% of Hg in the liver of northern shoveler no. 3 was demethylated, and the liver MeHg concentration of 0.846 mg/kg w.w. corresponds to a low risk of injury to birds. Thus, we demonstrate the potential for large discrepancies in the estimated toxicological risk when utilizing MeHg concentrations versus using THg as a proxy for MeHg. The muscle tissue THg concentration in northern shoveler no. 3 is 0.426 mg/kg w.w., while the muscle MeHg concentration is 0.324 mg/kg w.w. Both the muscle THg and MeHg concentrations correspond to a low risk of injury to birds, likely due to the higher % MeHg value in the muscle of northern shoveler no. 3 (76%). The low estimated risk of injury is consistent with the estimate based on liver MeHg (but is not consistent with the high estimated risk of injury based on liver THg), highlighting increased accuracy in assessing toxicological risks when Hg speciation is considered.

Measurements of THg are typically used as a cost-effective proxy for MeHg in studies and risk assessments of birds, since the majority of THg often exists as MeHg in bird tissues. However, this overlooks variation in the amount of MeHg demethylation among bird species, among individual birds of the same species, and among tissues within individual birds. In both duck species analyzed in this study, THg concentrations were higher but corresponding % MeHg values were lower in liver and kidney tissues relative to brain and muscle tissues (Figure ). This is particularly notable in northern shoveler livers, where significantly higher THg concentrations appear to be driven by demethylation and subsequent accumulation of demethylated iHg species (Hg­(Sec)4 and Hg­(SR)2) (Table ), leading to the discrepancies in risk estimates calculated for northern shoveler no. 3 based on liver THg and MeHg concentrations. For this reason, it has been recommended that THg measurements on tissues with typically high % MeHg values (e.g., breast muscle and brain tissues) should be used in toxicological risk assessments, where possible. However, high proportions of iHg can still exist in the brain (e.g., >50% iHg) and muscle (e.g., >90% iHg) of certain species such as sea birds (e.g., giant petrels), complicating this approach. Even more modest levels of demethylation (e.g., 20%) in tissues commonly assumed to have high % MeHg values can sometimes lead to discrepancies in risk estimates involving comparison to baseline TRVs if Hg speciation is not considered. For example, the THg concentration in the muscle tissue of northern shoveler no. 7 is 0.486 mg/kg w.w., exceeding the suggested Hg TRV for moderate risks. Approximately 23% of THg is demethylated, and the MeHg value of 0.354 mg/kg w.w. in the same sample is below the suggested Hg TRV for moderate risk. Even relatively modest amounts of demethylation can influence results of risk assessments, where THg concentrations are used as a proxy for MeHg and are compared to a baseline TRV.

Overall, for all 2021/2022 specimens analyzed in this study, the range of MeHg concentrations measured in northern shoveler harvested in 2021/2022 translates to low to moderate injury risk based on muscle tissues (0.099 to 0.549 mg/kg w.w.) and a low to moderate risk based on liver tissues (0.206 to 2.74 mg/kg w.w.) (Table S5). MeHg concentrations in cinnamon teal harvested at this time translate to a no to low estimated injury risk based on muscle tissues (0.011 to 0.334 mg/kg w.w.) and no to low risk based on liver tissues (0.036 to 0.924 mg/kg w.w.) (Table S6). This suggests a minimal to low risk of injury to cinnamon teal and a low to moderate risk of injury to northern shoveler utilizing eastern wetland habitats of GSLs.

Interannual and Spatial Trends: Implications for Waterbird and Human Health

Mean THg values in muscle tissues of northern shoveler and cinnamon teal collected during various monitoring efforts between 2004 and 2025 exhibit interannual variability with no clear unidirectional trend (Figure ; Tables S7 and S8). Northern shoveler collected during earlier monitoring efforts in 2004, and especially in 2005, had significantly higher muscle THg concentrations (2004:0.759 ± 0.313 mg/kg, w.w., n = 10; 2005:3.22 ± 3.62 mg/kg, w.w., n = 10; t tests, p < 0.01) than specimens analyzed in this study (0.339 ± 0.203 mg/kg, w.w., n = 8), resulting in exceedances of the EPA MeHg criterion and the initial implementation of waterfowl consumption advisories (Table S7). Furthermore, although only THg concentrations were reported, if a similar degree of demethylation to that in muscle tissues from 2021/2022 northern shoveler specimens analyzed in this study is assumed (% MeHg = 72%), the mean muscle concentrations from 2004 and 2005 collections correspond to moderate and severe risk of injury to birds, respectively. These northern shoveler specimens from 2004 and 2005 were collected from the Lee Creek and Goggin Drain area at the southern end of GSL, sites that were not included in subsequent sampling efforts. Most recently, UTDEQ and UTDWR collections from winter 2024–2025 also yielded a mean muscle tissue concentration of 0.665 mg/kg (w.w., n = 30), corresponding to a moderate risk of injury to birds and exceeding the EPA MeHg criterion by more than a factor of 2.

The greater mean THg concentrations associated with the 2004 and 2005 collection efforts may suggest that Hg bioaccumulation is elevated in waterfowl utilizing the southern end of GSL. This area is proximal to potential Hg sources such as mining and smelting operations and associated tailings piles, along with the Northwest Oil Drain, an input to Farmington Bay adjacent to the Crystal Unit of Farmington Bay Waterfowl Management Area. Past studies of Northwest Oil Drain have revealed that sediment THg concentrations exceed 6 mg/kg (d.w.) (in contrast to ≤0.53 mg/kg in sediment cores from open water GSL environments), and limited THg analysis of surficial sediments suggests that concentrations may be elevated at the southern end of GSL relative to northern locations. Although regional and global atmospheric deposition is the dominant input to open water environments at GSL − , it is likely that local industrial, urban, and shoreline proximal sources may have a greater relative role in waterfowl Hg exposure in southern areas.

Additionally, the southern end of GSL is near the boundary of the southward moving deep brine layer (DBL), which is known for elevated MeHg concentrations (>30 ng/L), and which presumably mixes into the overlying water column at the lake’s southern terminus. , North-to-south DBL sampling in 2018–2019 showed increasing THg and MeHg despite a coinciding decrease in density indicating mixing with the shallow brine during north-to-south transport (Figure S5). However, limited sampling showed no significant change in Hg burdens in waterfowl, including northern shoveler and cinnamon teal, during DBL and MeHg disappearance from GSL bottom waters across the period 2013–2016, suggesting that DBL dynamics have minimal influence on Hg bioaccumulation at the system scale. Furthermore, DBL absence during this period did not correspond with lower Hg concentrations in brine shrimp (Artemia franciscana) and brine flies (Ephydra hians and Ephydra gracilis), which are important food sources for northern shoveler, common goldeneye, and other waterfowl, gull, and shorebird species. ,− While it is possible that mixing of the DBL into the overlying water column could occur over a relatively small area and create a localized hotspot for MeHg bioaccumulation, the presence or absence of the DBL has minimal impact on Hg bioaccumulation throughout the larger GSL system.

Considering the minimal influence of the DBL on Hg bioaccumulation at GSL, future investigations of biogeochemical drivers of MeHg production, shoreline-proximal Hg sources, and diet (e.g., greater foraging on brine shrimp when utilizing open water habitats) could help to explain spatial and interannual variability in observed waterfowl Hg burdens. Notably, the mean muscle tissue concentration measured in northern shoveler collected from the Lee Creek and Goggin Drain area in 2005 was far higher than the EPA MeHg criterion and corresponded to severe risk of injury to birds. This highlights the need for additional characterization near the southern shore and more broadly across all GSL open-water and wetland habitats. Moreover, the fact that mean northern shoveler muscle tissue concentrations were below the EPA MeHg criterion during the 2021/2022 monitoring period but rose to more than twice that criterion in 2024/2025 underscores the need to understand drivers of interannual variability in Hg bioaccumulation in GSL waterfowl to inform management.

MercurySelenium Interactions in Great Salt Lake Waterfowl: Future Directions

It has been reported that Se can reduce MeHg toxicity via in-situ demethylation, and GSL provides a unique setting to examine MeHg detoxification in waterfowl given the presence of a waterbody-specific Se water quality standard and elevated waterfowl Hg concentrations. ,, While it has been assumed that co-occurring elevated Hg and Se levels in GSL waterfowl protect them from MeHg toxicity, this conclusion requires further consideration of Hg and Se chemical speciation within tissues. Tissue Hg/Se molar ratios less than 1:1 were previously assumed to be protective of MeHg toxicity, but this assumption is valid only if MeHg is primarily demethylated to Hg selenide (HgSe(s)). The Hg/Se molar ratio of 1:4 in one of the demethylated Hg species (Hg­(Sec)4) that is observed in northern shoveler and cinnamon teal demonstrates that the assumed 1:1 molar ratio corresponding to the HgSe(s) pathway may overestimate Se available for Hg detoxification. Additionally, because Hg­(Sec)4 has a greater than 1:1 Hg/Se molar ratio, the presence of this species may deplete the stock of bioavailable Se for selenoprotein synthesis and raises the risk of Se deficiency, which is toxic at elevated concentrations but is an essential micronutrient. However, given the well-documented elevated levels of Se at GSL, Se availability is likely not limited.

It was previously suggested that MeHg detoxification utilizing the perceived Hg­(SR)2 pathway could enable predators to demethylate MeHg even when the Se supply is low. However, although total Se measurements were not made on samples in this study, Se deficiency likely does not explain the structural assignment of Hg­(SR)2 in GSL waterfowl tissues. Selenium concentrations in GSL waters, sediments, invertebrates, and other waterbird species are generally elevated, ,,, and groundwater-fed wetlands along the south shore of GSL are affected by Se contamination from nonferrous metal production, with remediation in progress. Therefore, a Se deficiency in tissues of these two GSL waterfowl species is unlikely, and low dietary Se is unlikely to be a driver of Hg­(SR)2 formation in GSL waterfowl. This is supported by results of other studies, as structural assignments for Hg species in the blood and tissues of giant petrels and emperor penguins from non-Se-limited, marine systems suggest the presence of Hg­(SR)2 , and Hg­(SR)2 has been identified as an in vivo demethylation product rather than a Hg species from dietary sources. Because waterfowl analyzed in this study seem to exhibit the MeHg–Cys to Hg­(SR)2 detoxification pathway, this could suggest Hg­(SR)2 as an intermediate species in the conversion of MeHg–Cys to Hg­(Sec)4 or sterics preventing exchange of reduced S to reduced Se ligands. However, determining the exact mechanisms and drivers of the proposed Hg­(SR)2 detoxification pathway would require additional investigation, and, more generally, mechanistic studies using advanced analytical approaches may improve our understanding of in vivo MeHg detoxification pathways.

Conclusions

While in vivo demethylation of MeHg is understood to mitigate Hg toxicity in birds, waterfowl Hg consumption advisories at GSL ,, provide a unique context to examine this pathway and its implications for wildlife and human health. Our study documents in vivo MeHg detoxification in two GSL duck species known to have tissue Hg concentrations relevant to human health exposure risks. HERFD-XANES results suggest that Hg­(Sec)4 and Hg­(SR)2 are the two demethylated Hg species in both northern shoveler and cinnamon teal tissues, notably in the absence of HgSe(s). Our findings suggest that there are important differences in MeHg detoxification between waterbird taxa and that using THg measurements as a proxy for MeHg in risk assessments related to wildlife and human health (particularly, comparison to threshold-based criteria and toxicity reference values) can be misleading for the taxa capable of demethylation. Furthermore, while the migratory nature of both species leaves the exact location of Hg accumulation uncertain, our work highlights the complexities of Hg speciation in GSL waterfowl that influence toxicity risks to waterbirds and humans. Ultimately, more site-specific investigation into drivers of Hg bioaccumulation would be needed to understand the causes of elevated Hg concentrations and interannual variability in certain GSL waterbird species.
