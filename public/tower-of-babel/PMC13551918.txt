Alzheimer’s disease core biomarkers are associated with worse baseline and postoperative cognitive profiles in idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus: findings from the Bologna PRO-Hydro study
Authors: Chiara Riccietti, Luisa Sambati, Corrado Zenesini, Simone Baiardi, Maria Sasca Criante, Antonella D’Apolito, David Milletti, Luca Albini-Riccioli, Barbara Polischi, Giovanna Calandra-Buonaura, Elena Magelli, Davide Mascarella, Liliana Mazza, Luca Baldelli, Michelangelo Stanzani-Maserati, Pietro Cortelli, Sabina Cevoli, Giorgio Palandri, Piero Parchi, Giulia Giannini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus (iNPH) is a clinico-radiological syndrome affecting the elderly. Recent studies have reported a high prevalence of neurodegenerative copathologies in iNPH, particularly Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Neuropsychological studies in iNPH sample revealed variable and heterogeneous cognitive profile. Few studies have examined cognitive differences according to neurodegenerative copathology, and none has systematically applied a comprehensive neuropsychological battery while stratifying patients by AD biomarkers both before and after surgery. The main objective of this study was to assess baseline differences in the neuropsychological profile of iNPH patients with and without AD core biomarkers, using a comprehensive neuropsychological battery. The secondary aim was to investigate changes in neuropsychological functions from baseline to short-term postoperative outcomes in the overall sample and in subgroups with and without AD biomarkers.MethodsPatients underwent a standardized inpatient work-up including clinical evaluation, comprehensive neuropsychological assessment, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers analysis. A multidisciplinary team selected candidates for surgery. An extensive clinical and neuropsychological reassessment was performed six months after surgery.ResultsFrom 2015 to 2025, 226 (97 females) consecutive iNPH patients completed the baseline evaluation. Among these, 102 shunted iNPH patients (46 females) underwent six-month postoperative neuropsychological assessment. The cognitive profile in all individuals revealed deficits in attentive-executive functions with a significant improvement after surgery. Overall, 24.3% of iNPH participants showed a pathological phosphorylated tau/beta-amyloid 1–42 ratio (pTau/Aβ42+). At baseline, pTau/Aβ42+ (n = 53) patients revealed worse neuropsychological performances than those (n = 165) with normal pTau/Aβ ratio (pTau/Aβ42-), with more pronounced memory impairment. At six-month follow-up, the pTau/Aβ42 + group exhibited poorer cognitive outcomes compared to pTau/Aβ42- one, particularly in general cognition, short- and long-term verbal and visuo-spatial memory, and executive functions.ConclusionsNearly one-quarter of patients exhibited AD core biomarkers positivity. iNPH patients with AD core biomarkers copathology showed an earlier cognitive onset and worse neuropsychological performances at baseline. At follow-up, the pTau/Aβ42 + group demonstrated poorer cognitive outcomes in specific cognitive domains compared to the pTau/Aβ42 − group. These differences in cognitive profiles at baseline and six months after surgery in iNPH patients with AD core biomarker positivity may have clinical relevance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12987-026-00836-z.

Background

Idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus (iNPH) is a syndrome consisting of chronic ventricular dilatation, normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pressure and a symptomatic triad of dementia, gait dysfunction and urinary incontinence [1]. There is substantial heterogeneity in the clinical presentation, severity, and progression of iNPH symptoms, and diagnosis can be considered even in the absence of the full triad. Clinical benefit after shunt surgery ranges from 31% to 96% in the short-term follow-up [2–4], whereas studies investigating long-term outcomes have reported a gradual decline in treatment effect over time [3, 5–7].

iNPH prevalence ranges from 0.51 to 5.9% in the elderly population, increasing with age, but it is likely underestimated [8, 9]. As iNPH typically affects elderly patients, multiple medical comorbidities, including cerebrovascular/degenerative neurological diseases, can occur in this disorder.

Previous studies revealed a high rate of neurodegenerative disorders in iNPH patients [6].

Although iNPH and neurodegenerative disorders may have incidentally “co-occurred”, their high association is beyond what would be expected by chance alone, suggesting that patients with evidence of copathology (e.g., Alzheimer or Lewy body disease) should not be considered as having truly “idiopathic” NPH [6]. The association with neurodegenerative pathologies could explain the heterogeneity in presenting symptoms, clinical phenotype, and outcome after shunt surgery. The use of CSF biomarkers could provide information on the most frequent copathologies and on the severity of neurodegenerative processes in iNPH patients [10–13].

Recent studies focusing on neuropsychological profile of iNPH showed a phenotype that manifests as a pattern of prominent frontal subcortical dysfunction [14], in particular characterized by deficits in psychomotor processing speed, complex attention, and executive dysfunction [15]. Recent reviews specified that, although iNPH typically involves executive dysfunction, it may also be accompanied by more widespread deficits; therefore, a detailed cognitive analysis is a crucial part of a broader multidisciplinary consensus diagnosis [16, 17]. However, longitudinal evolution of cognitive impairment in iNPH requires further researches [16] since few studies have focused on the cognitive trajectory of iNPH [18].

Similarly, few studies analysed the association between neuropsychological profiles and CSF biomarkers. In one study on our iNPH cohort [19] including 71 patients, the subgroup with cognitive impairment at baseline (Mini-Mental State Examination - MMSE < 24) showed significantly lower levels of beta-amyloid 1–42 (Aβ42) compared to the subgroup with a MMSE ≥ 24 [20]. A subsequent study of 127 iNPH patients from our cohort [11] showed that, at baseline, iNPH patients with brain beta-amyloid copathology, defined with a pathological beta-amyloid 1–42/1–40 ratio (Aβ42/Aβ40+), had lower scores on the MMSE than those without copathology (Aβ42/Aβ40-) [11]. Additionally, higher levels of neurofilament light chain protein (NfL) were also associated with lower MMSE scores [11]. However, in this latter study, which involved a smaller sample, only the MMSE and the Brief Mental Deterioration Battery (BMDB) were considered, without evaluating the full neuropsychological profile using a comprehensive test battery.

Three studies by other research groups examining the cognitive impairment at baseline in relation to Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) core biomarkers in large iNPH samples (n = 455, n = 119 and n = 147) used only MMSE scores or the cognitive domain of the iNPH scale [21–23]. These studies showed that higher Aβ42 levels were associated with better baseline cognitive function [21], that baseline MMSE scores were lower in patients with a pathological Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio compared to those with a normal ratio [22] and that preoperative MMSE scores correlated negatively with total Tau (tTau), phosphorylated tau at threonine 181 (pTau), and NfL [23]. Other two studies, including additional neuropsychological tests, on 53 and 81 patients showed that patients with pTau levels above the pathological threshold performed worse on delayed recall [24], and that higher tTau and pTau levels correlated with poorer performance on the cognitive domain and on individual cognitive tests [25].

Although some studies have investigated CSF biomarkers in relation to overall outcomes (including gait impairment and urinary dysfunction) using the iNPH or Ranking scales [11, 21, 22, 25], only a few have specifically focused on cognitive outcomes after shunt surgery in association with these biomarkers [23, 26–30].

Three studies investigated short-term cognitive outcome (≤ 12 months). In one study on 140 iNPH patients undergoing shunt surgery, with a mean follow-up of 11.2 months, postoperative MMSE scores correlated negatively with tTau and NfL and positively with Aβ42 [23]. In another one study of 31 iNPH patients (20 iNPH and 11 iNPH + AD), a significant main effect of AD comorbidity was observed in MMSE scores at all postoperative time points (baseline, 3 months, 12 months, and 24–60 months). This study further found that iNPH patients exhibited stable improvements in working memory and response inhibition, whereas iNPH + AD patients failed to maintain initial gains [26]. A study on 49 iNPH patients showed that those with AD comorbidity performed significantly poorer on verbal memory tests compared with patients without neurodegenerative comorbidities [27].

Three studies from the same research group, focusing on long-term cognitive outcomes, reported that AD core biomarkers, such as pTau, Aβ38/Aβ42 ratio, Aβ42/pTau ratio, and postoperative changes in the Aβ42 toxic conformer ratio, were associated with poorer cognitive outcomes, as measured by MMSE and Frontal Assessment Battery (FAB) scores [28–30].

Taken together, studies focusing on neuropsychological evaluations in iNPH sample revealed variable and heterogeneous cognitive profiles. Studies investigating CSF biomarkers in iNPH suggested different profiles in patients with and without copathologies. However, no studies have extensively analysed patients with a comprehensive neuropsychological battery and stratified patients according to neurodegenerative copathologies, including AD biomarkers, both before and after surgery. This approach could clarify the clinical characteristics and distinct phenotypes of iNPH, shedding light on baseline differences that may influence outcomes.

Cognitive dysfunction is a key feature of iNPH, significantly affecting quality of life, functional capacity, independence in activities of daily living, and the degree of caregiver involvement.

The main objective of our study was to assess baseline differences in the neuropsychological profile of iNPH patients with and without AD core biomarkers, using a comprehensive neuropsychological battery. The secondary aim was to assess changes in neuropsychological functions from baseline to short-term outcomes after surgery in the entire sample and in subgroups with and without AD biomarkers.

Methods

Population, patient selection and clinical protocols

This is an observational prospective cohort study of patients with NPH belonging to the Bologna PRO-Hydro cohort [19]. The Bologna PRO-Hydro study prospectively recruited patients with suspected iNPH and undergoing MRI protocol and multidisciplinary evaluation, as previously described [19]. Patients with suggestive clinical symptoms and neuroradiological signs of iNPH were enrolled in the inpatient program, including thorough clinical evaluation and standardized CSF tapping test (TT). The diagnosis was established during a consensus meeting involving the multidisciplinary team after review of the clinical history, neurological examination, all pre-TT clinical and neuropsychological data, blood/CSF tests and comparisons between pre- and post-TT findings [19]. Based on the diagnosis, taking into consideration comorbidities and vascular risk factors, the multidisciplinary team established eligible patients for shunt surgery with programmable valves.

Six months after surgery, an extensive evaluation, including clinical evaluation, neurological examination, gait assessment and neuropsychological evaluation, was performed.

The clinical evaluation included the following assessments [19]: systematic assessment of symptoms and signs, 18-meters walking test and Timed up and go test (TUGT) [31], postural stability and tandem walking, iNPH Grading Scale [32] and Japanese Scale for iNPH–JSINPH [33], Gait Status Scale [32], Tinetti Assessment Tool [34], the Functional Independence Measure-FIM [35] and Modified Rankin Scale (mRankin) [36]. The following neuroradiological features were analysed: Evans’ Index, the callosal angle, the iNPH Radscale score, and the disproportionately enlarged subarachnoid-space hydrocephalus (DESH) score [37–40]. Vascular burden on magnetic resonance imaging was assessed using the Fazekas scale [41] and the Age-Related White Matter Changes (ARWMC) scale [42].

For the purpose of this study, we included patients with iNPH from the Bologna PRO-Hydro cohort [19], diagnosed as probable iNPH according to consensus guideline criteria [1], and undergoing shunt surgery. Patients with partial clinical assessments or lacking appropriate neuroimaging studies or neuropsychological evaluation at baseline were excluded. For the 6-month follow-up analysis, only iNPH patients who underwent surgery and completed the neuropsychological evaluation were included.

Neuropsychological assessment

A comprehensive battery of neuropsychological tests was administered to assess all major cognitive domains. Specifically, global cognition was evaluated with the MMSE [43], the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [44] and the BMDB [45]; verbal memory with the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT): immediate (RAVLT-IR) and delayed recall (RAVLT-DR) [46]; short visual memory with Immediate Visual Memory test (IVM) [46]; visuo-constructive function through Copy Design Simple (CDS) [46]; short term verbal memory and verbal working memory with Digit Span Forward (DSF) and Backward (DSB) and short-term visuo-spatial memory and visuo-spatial working memory with Corsi Span Forward (Corsi-F) and Backward (Corsi-B) [47]; selective attention with the Barrage test [45]; visuo-constructional praxis with Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure Test (ROCF) direct copy (ROCF-DC) and visuo-spatial long term memory with its delayed recall (ROCF-DR) [48]; executive functions with the Simple Verbal Analogies Test (SVAT) [49], Stroop Color and Word test (SCWT) [50] and with the FAB [51]; language with phonemic verbal fluency (PVF) and semantic verbal fluency (SVF) [52]. All patients were evaluated in the seated position. Test results were corrected for age, sex and education according to Italian standardizations. Normative data were used to define normality and non-normality on each cognitive test.

Depression and anxiety were evaluated with the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) [53] and the State Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) [54]. Apathy was evaluated with the Apathy evaluation scale [55]. Basic and instrumental activities of daily living were evaluated using the ADL Index [56] and the IADL Index [57], respectively.

The assessment was carried out by trained neuropsychologists at baseline and at 6-month follow-up.

At the 6-month follow-up, the neuropsychological evaluation was conducted using parallel forms.

CSF biochemical analysis

CSF samples were obtained by a lumbar puncture during the TT, centrifuged routinely or in case of blood contamination (even mild), divided into aliquots, and stored in polypropylene tubes at − 80 °C until analysis. All analyses were carried out by personnel blinded to patients’ diagnoses. CSF for biomarker analysis was collected immediately after the first drained aliquot of 2 mL, which was used for standard cytochemical examination. The TT and neuropsychological evaluations were performed during the inpatient program, with the neuropsychological assessment conducted 1–2 days before the TT.

In CSF samples, we measured tTau, pTau, Aβ42, and Aβ40 by automated chemiluminescent enzyme-immunoassay (CLEIA) on the Lumipulse G600II platform (Fujirebio Europe NV, Gent, Belgium). The mean interassay coefficients of variation were ˂8% for all biomarkers. We calculated the Aβ42/40 ratio according to a previously published formula [58]. The cut-off value for Aβ42/ Aβ40 + was ≤ 0.67 while the cut-off value for pathological pTau181/Aβ 42 ratio (pTau181/Aβ 42+) was ≥ 0.08 [59]. AD copathology was defined according to the 2024 NIA–AA criteria [60].

In the same CSF samples, we measured NfL concentration by a validated commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (NfL ELISA kit, IBL, Hamburg, Germany) [61]. The mean intra- and interassay coefficients of variation for NfL analyses were 2 and 10%, respectively.

CSF AD biomarkers (tTau, pTau, Aβ42, and Aβ40) were analyzed using the same analytical platform Fujirebio LUMIPULSE G. Since June 2019 (i.e., after the release of new calibrators by Fujirebio), all samples have been prospectively measured as part of routine clinical laboratory practice. To ensure analytical reproducibility, samples collected before June 2019 (n = 87) were re-analyzed between 2020 and 2021. NfL was prospectively measured in all CSF samples as part of routine clinical practice.

Statistical analysis

The patients’ characteristics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables with normal distribution, or median and interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed variables, and with absolute (n) and relative (%) frequencies for categorical variables.

Given the complex, multidimensional nature of the clinical data, all statistical analyses in this study were strictly considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

The univariable associations of the demographic and clinical characteristics and the neuropsychological profiles with the presence of a positive CSF AD profile (pTau/Aβ42+, Aβ42/Aβ40+) at baseline were evaluated using the Student t-test for continuous variables with normal distribution, the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test for continuous variables with non-normal distribution, and the Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables.

The neuropsychological functions were compared before and after surgery in the whole group and in the subgroups (pTau/Aβ42+, pTau/Aβ42-, Aβ42/Aβ40+, Aβ42/Aβ40-) using the paired Student t-test or the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

The changes in neuropsychological functions from baseline to the follow-up visit were compared between the pTau/Aβ42 + and pTau/Aβ42- groups using multivariable linear regression models. The dependent variable was the change in neuropsychological score from baseline to the follow-up. The models included group status (pathological vs. not) and the respective baseline neuropsychological score as covariates. Additionally, the models were adjusted for baseline NfL levels to account for observed baseline differences between pTau/Aβ42 + and pTau/Aβ42- groups, and the potential correlation between NfL and cognitive performance [11, 23, 25]. Parallel analyses were conducted to define groups based on Aβ42/Aβ40. Since neuropsychological test scores were already corrected for age, sex, and education according to Italian normative data, these variables were not included as additional covariates in the regression models. Results are presented as Beta coefficients (β), with 95% Confidence Intervals (95% CI), representing the adjusted mean difference between groups. The β coefficients are graphically presented with forest plots. Effect sizes for all pre- to post-surgery changes were calculated using Cohen’s d and interpreted as mild (> 0.2–0.5), moderate (> 0.5–0.8), and large (> 0.8). To balance the risk of Type I and Type II errors, we prioritized the reporting of uncorrected p-values alongside standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d), which better capture the magnitude and clinical relevance of effects without relying solely on dichotomous significance thresholds. Following the methodological perspective outlined by Rothman [62], formal adjustments for multiple comparisons were not strictly applied to the main narrative interpretation. In highly intercorrelated and multifactorial clinical phenotypes, overly conservative corrections can substantially increase the risk of false negatives (Type II errors), potentially obscuring clinically meaningful patterns.

A p-value lower than 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analysis was performed using Stata SE 14.2 and R version 4.5.1.

Of the 226 patients, CSF analysis was performed in 218. Fifty-three of the 218 iNPH patients (24.3%) showed a pathological level of the pTau/Aβ42 ratio (pTau/Aβ42+), while 61 of 218 showed a pathological level of the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio (Aβ42/ Aβ40+).

Comparisons between pTau/Aβ42 + and pTau/Aβ42- are shown in Table 1.

The pTau/Aβ42 + group was older than the pTau/Aβ42- (78.72 ± 4.55 vs. 75.72 ± 5.71, p = 0.0006) and showed a higher age at disease onset (74.74 ± 4.82 vs. 71.59 ± 6.48, p = 0.0024) without difference in disease duration. Compared to pTau/Aβ42- patients, pTau/Aβ42 + patients more frequently presented with cognitive impairment and less frequently with urinary symptoms at disease onset (20.75% vs. 9.70% and 1.89% vs. 14.55%, respectively, p = 0.021). At baseline, the pTau/Aβ42 + group showed a lower frequency of urinary dysfunction (67.9% vs. 82.4%, p = 0.034) and a lower level of independence in daily activities, as measured by the FIM scale [105 (96–119) vs. 111 (101–120), p = 0.0217]. No differences in motor performance, clinical scale scores, or vascular burden on MRI were observed between the two groups. In contrast, significant differences in Evan’s Index, callosal angle, and DESH score were found. Higher CSF NfL levels were observed in the pTau/Aβ42 + group compared with the pTau/Aβ42- one [1252 (920–1688) vs. 899.5 (680–1252), p = 0.0013].

Demographic and clinical characteristics comparisons between Aβ42 + and Aβ42- are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Similar to the comparison based on pTau/Aβ42 status, when considering Aβ status, the Aβ42/ Aβ40 + group was older, had a lower level of education and a higher age at disease onset without a difference in disease duration. This group more frequently presented with cognitive impairment at disease onset and showed a lower level of independence in daily activities, as measured by the FIM scale. No statistical differences in CSF NfL levels were observed.

Follow-up and cognitive outcome at the six-month follow-up visit

The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. Of the 226 iNPH patients (129 males) who completed the inpatient program, 160 (92 males) underwent surgery, while 60 (33 males) did not. Among the 160 shunted patients, 102 (56 males) completed both clinical and neuropsychological evaluations 6 months after surgery (Fig. 1), whereas 46 (32 males) did not undergo the 6-month neuropsychological assessment (Fig. 1).

Study flow-chart

Comparing the 102 patients who completed the follow-up evaluation with the 106 patients who either did not undergo surgery, did not complete the neuropsychological evaluation, or completed it outside the designated timeframe after surgery, no clinically relevant differences were observed in the neuropsychological profiles (Supplementary Table 3). Isolated differences were found in MOCA [21.21 (19.43; 23.34) vs. 26.64 (26.11; 30.01), p = 0.0339] and in the time execution of the Stroop test [28.38 (13.07; 46.25) vs.16.27 (7.91; 26.95), p = 0.0027].

Neuropsychological performance assessed at baseline (during the inpatient program) and six months after surgery demonstrated a significant improvement (Table 3). In particular, significant improvements were observed in short- and long-term visuo-spatial memory (Corsi-F: p = 0.0382, IVM: p = 0.0237, ROCF-DR: p = 0.0008), short-term verbal memory (DSF: p = 0.0225), verbal working memory (DSB: p = 0.0132), visuo-constructional praxis (CDS: p = 0.0237, ROCF-DC: p = 0.0099), attention and executive functions (Barrage score: p = 0.0044, Stroop test: p = 0.0229, FAB: p = 0.0023) and both phonemic and semantic verbal access to language (Phonemic Fluency: p = 0.0196, Semantic Fluency: p = 0.0355).

The analysis of effect sizes for each neuropsychological test revealed mild effects for short-term verbal memory (RAVLT-IR: Cohen’s d = 0.32), long-term visuo-spatial memory (ROCF-DR: Cohen’s d = 0.49), phonemic language access (Phonemic Fluency: Cohen’s d = 0.23), executive functions (FAB: Cohen’s d = 0.27), visuo-constructional praxis (ROCF-DC: Cohen’s d = 0.31), and state and trait anxiety (STAI-S: Cohen’s d = 0.22; STAI-T: Cohen’s d = 0.26).

Among 102 patients who completed the follow-up evaluation, 30 (29.4%) patients were Aβ42/Aβ40+, and 24 (23.5%) pTau/Aβ42+.

Considering the differences between baseline and six months post-surgery in the pTau/Aβ42 + and pTau/Aβ42 − groups, the pTau/Aβ42 + group (n = 24/102) showed less cognitive improvement compared with the pTau/Aβ42 − group. In particular, the pTau/Aβ42 + group did not exhibit the same cognitive improvement as the pTau/Aβ42 − group in the short-term verbal memory (DSF) and working verbal memory (DSB), the accuracy of the selective attention (Barrage score), the visual-constructional praxis (ROCF-DC) and the visuo-spatial long term memory (ROCF-DR), the phonemic and semantic access to language (Phonemic and Semantic Fluency), the short-term visuo-spatial memory (Corsi-F, IVM) and in the screening for the executive functions (FAB).

Detailed data on neuropsychological tests battery at baseline and at the six-month follow-up visit according pTau/Aβ42 status are presented in Supplementary Table 4. It is noteworthy that both groups (pTau/Aβ42 + and pTau/Aβ42−) showed improvement in some tests; however, statistical significance was more frequently observed in the group without copathology.

Multivariable linear models assessing the development of neuropsychological differences between the pTau/Aβ42 + and pTau/Aβ42 − groups, adjusted for baseline neuropsychological test scores, revealed poorer cognitive performances in the pTau/Aβ42 + group. Specifically, worse changes from baseline to follow-up were observed in general cognition [BMDB: β= -0.484 (95% CI= -0.966; -0.001), p = 0.049], short-term verbal memory [RAVLT-IR: β= -3.285 (-6.563; -0.006), p = 0.050], long-term verbal memory [RAVLT-DR: β= -1.347 (-2.396; -0.297), p = 0.012], verbal working memory [DSB: β= -0.543 (-1.041; -0.046), p = 0.032], long-term visuo-spatial memory [ROCF-DR: β= -4.076 (-7.403; -0.749), p = 0.017] and short-term visuo-spatial memory [Corsi-F: β= -0.487 (-0.943; -0.031), p = 0.037; IVM: β= -2.259 (-3.783; -0.736), p = 0.004] (Table 4).

Discussion

This is one of the few studies that investigated the cognitive profile of a large iNPH cohort through a comprehensive neuropsychological battery and in relation to a complete assessment of AD core biomarkers.

The neuropsychological profile of our iNPH cohort at baseline reveals typical cognitive deficits in attention-executive functions and visuo-constructional praxis. In our cohort, nearly one-quarter of patients exhibited positivity for AD core biomarkers. Specifically, 61 of 218 (28.0%) patients showed a pathological Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio (Aβ42/ Aβ40+), and 53 of 218 patients (24.3%) showed a pathological pTau/Aβ42 ratio (pTau/Aβ42+).

iNPH patients with AD core biomarkers copathology exhibited clinical and neuropsychological differences at baseline. Considering the more restrictive ratio, patients with a pathological pTau/Aβ42 (pTau/Aβ42+) were older, had a higher age at disease onset, more frequently presented with a cognitive onset, and showed reduced functional independence at baseline compared to those with normal pTau/Aβ42 (pTau/Aβ42–). The first group demonstrated poorer performance across most cognitive domains evaluated, including general cognition, short- and long-term verbal and visuo-spatial memory, visuo-constructional praxis, language access, and attentional-executive functions.

At the 6-month follow-up, the overall sample showed an improvement in short-term verbal and visuo-spatial memory, long-term visuo-spatial memory, access to language and attentional-executive functions. The majority of these changes showed mild effect sizes, as calculated using Cohen’s d.

Considering AD biomarker subgroups, the pTau/Aβ42 + group exhibited poorer cognitive outcomes at follow-up compared to the pTau/Aβ42 − group, particularly in general cognition, short- and long-term verbal and visuo-spatial memory, and executive function. Similar results in clinical features and neuropsychological profiles at baseline, as well as at 6 months after surgery, were observed when using the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio instead of the pTau/Aβ42 ratio. The effect sizes of these changes across subgroups were generally mild, with moderate effects mainly observed in memory-related domains.

Baseline neuropsychological results in our iNPH cohort were consistent with previous reports, showing prominent frontal-subcortical dysfunction [14], even if it may be accompanied by more widespread deficits [16]. Indeed, in the early stages of iNPH, cognitive deficits occur primarily in the executive functions and working memory, which are supported by frontostriatal circuits; as the disease progresses, cognition declines globally [63]. In particular a recent study on 53 iNPH patients found that verbal memory and executive functions are the most affected cognitive domains [64] and another study on 64 patients showed more pronounced impairment in executive functions [65]. However, a recent review showed that cognitive testing varied significantly across articles, and ultimately a specific cognitive profile was not identified [17].

Considering follow-up in our entire sample, patients showed short-term postoperative improvements in cognitive functions, with the greatest gains in short-term verbal and visuo-spatial memory, long-term visuo-spatial memory, access to language, and attentional-executive functions. Comparisons with previous studies are limited by the wide variety of cognitive assessments used, ranging from general screening tests (MMSE, Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination Revised, MoCA), to cognitive domain within NPH-specific scales (iNPH grading scale, JSNPH scale, iNPH-scale) [66], and to domain-specific neuropsychological tests focusing on memory, attention, and executive functions (RAVLT, FAB, Trail Making Test-A, Stroop) [17, 27]. This variability in methods limits the reliability of the data, the true prevalence of cognitive impairment before and after surgery, and the extent of postoperative cognitive improvement.

A systematic review and meta-analysis including 33 studies (2461 patients) evaluating outcomes and adverse events across different CSF diversion procedures (ventriculoperitoneal, ventriculoatrial, and lumboperitoneal shunting), showed that cognitive deficits responds less favourably to treatment, whereas gait disturbances often improve progressively over the first 3–6 months [3]. More recently, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 54 studies (4811 patients) reported gait improvement in 72% of cases, while urinary and cognitive dysfunctions improved in approximately 50% of patients following surgery [4]. A Cochrane review of four randomized controlled trials (140 participants: 73 who underwent immediate CSF shunting and 67 controls with delayed shunting) found very uncertain evidence regarding the effect of CSF shunting on cognitive function within six months after surgery (SMD = 0.35; 95% CI − 0.04 to 0.74; 2 studies, 104 participants; very low-certainty evidence) [67].

Concerning the baseline neuropsychological profile in relation to AD core biomarkers, previous studies focused on associations with different biomarkers, showing that iNPH patients with beta-amyloid copathology exhibited poorer cognitive performance, particularly in general cognition (MMSE), executive functions (FAB) [11, 20, 22, 28, 29] and memory [24]. These results are consistent with our findings, despite different biomarkers and neuropsychological assessments being used.

In particular, a large study of 455 iNPH patients found that higher Aβ42 levels were associated with better baseline cognitive function, whether assessed using the MMSE or the cognitive domain of the iNPH scale [21]. Another study including 119 iNPH patients reported lower baseline MMSE scores in those with a pathological Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio compared to those with a normal ratio [22]. Similarly, a study of 147 iNPH patients revealed that preoperative MMSE scores were negatively correlated with tTau, pTau, and NfL [23]. A regression model using dichotomized biomarker concentrations showed that patients with low pTau (cut-off 27.5 ng/l, OR 3.4) and low tTau (cut-off 211 ng/l, OR 3.7) were more likely to have a preoperative MMSE score above 26 [23]. Another study of 53 iNPH patients investigated CSF biomarkers and neuropsychological profiles using the MMSE and domain-specific tests assessing memory, visuo-spatial and executive functions [24], showing that pTau-positive patients performed worse on delayed recall than pTau-negative patients. Moreover, amyloid-positive patients (defined with either decreased Aβ42 and/or a decreased Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio) demonstrated lower delayed recall scores, although this association disappeared after Bonferroni correction [24]. Recently, a study of 81 patients evaluated correlations between cognitive functions, assessed across attention, executive and visual and verbal memory domains, and a broad panel of biomarkers reflecting amyloid and tau pathology, neuronal degeneration, and astrogliosis. A higher tTau and pTau correlated with poorer performance on the cognitive domain and on individual cognitive tests [25].

Regarding surgical outcomes in relation to CSF biomarkers, most studies focused on global outcome measures, including gait and urinary domains, assessed through iNPH-specific scales or the Rankin score. These studies, even those conducted on large samples, have not demonstrated a robust impact of core AD biomarkers on postoperative outcomes [11, 21, 22, 25].

In our previous study of 89 iNPH patients, a favourable outcome was defined as an improvement of at least 1 point on the mRankin scale 6 months after shunt surgery, identifying 42 responders and 47 non-responders. In the univariate analysis, neither the continuous CSF biomarker levels (tTau, pTau, Aβ42, Aβ40 and Aβ42) nor positivity for α-synuclein real-time quaking-induced conversion (a seed amplification assay detecting misfolded α-synuclein aggregates), were associated with postoperative outcome [11]. One study on 376 patients defined improvement by means of difference on iNPH scale between 12 months follow-up post-surgery and the preoperative visit, classifying patients who improved (delta iNPH scale > 0) and who deteriorated (delta iNPH scale < 0). Higher preoperative levels of NfL and tTau were associated with less improvement one year after shunt surgery, and patients who deteriorated had higher preoperative levels of NfL and tTau [21]. One study involving 119 iNPH patients [22], which defined clinical improvement after surgery as a postoperative increase of ≥ 5 points on the iNPH scale, analysed biomarkers related to astrogliosis, axonal, amyloid and tau pathology, and synaptic dysfunction (glial fibrillary acidic protein, chitinase-3-like protein 1, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, NfL, Aβ38, Aβ40, Aβ42, Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, soluble amyloid precursor protein alfa and beta, tTau, pTau, growth-associated protein 43 and neurogranin). A relative increase in the synaptic protein neurogranin was associated with postoperative improvement, whereas the tTau×Aβ42/Aβ40 and pTau×Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios did not predict shunt responsiveness [22]. These findings were recently confirmed by a study on 81 iNPH patients investigating an extended battery of biomarkers reflecting amyloid- and tau-related pathology, neuronal degeneration, and astrocytic activation (Aβ38, Aβ40, Aβ42, Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, soluble amyloid precursor protein alfa and beta, tTau, pTau, Monocyte chemoattractant protein 1, and NfL), and outcome measured using the iNPH scale. No significant differences in biomarker concentrations were found between improved and non-improved groups after a median follow-up of 8 months, with “improved” defined as a postoperative increase of at least 5 points [25]. The low Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio did not show predictive value [25].

However, these studies primarily focused on general outcomes, such as levels of dependence or improvement in the classic symptom triad, whereas only a few have analysed cognitive domains in detail, examining the relationship between the neuropsychological profile and CSF biomarkers, as well as their association with postoperative cognitive outcomes [23, 26–30].

Studies on cognitive outcomes in relation to AD core biomarkers differed in their assessment of beta-amyloid copathology (e.g., pTau, tTau, Aβ38/Aβ42 ratio, Aβ42/pTau ratio, postoperative changes in the Aβ42 toxic conformer ratio), timing of follow-up (short- vs. long-term), and cognitive assessments, with most relying solely on the MMSE without examining detailed cognitive functions. Despite these methodological limitations, previous studies have suggested differential postoperative responses in patients with and without beta-amyloid copathology, particularly in working and verbal memory.

In line with these previous studies, we found that both pTau/Aβ42 + and Aβ42/Aβ40 + ratios were associated with worse cognitive outcomes at 6 months after shunt surgery compared with negative ratios. Beyond general cognition, the pTau/Aβ42 + group performed worst in short- and long-term verbal and visuo-spatial memory, verbal working memory and visuo-constructional praxis compared to the pTau/Aβ42- group. As previous studies reported worse cognitive performances in patients with high NFL levels [11, 23, 25], and baseline NfL differences were observed between subgroups, a multivariable linear regression model, adjusted for both baseline neuropsychological scores and NfL levels, was performed to accurately identify cognitive tests influenced by AD core biomarkers, while minimizing the effect of potential confounding factors.

Three previous studies focused on cognitive outcomes at short-term follow-up. In one study on 140 iNPH patients undergoing shunt with a mean follow-up after surgery of 11.2 months, postoperative MMSE outcome correlated negatively with tTau and NfL, and positively with Aβ42. In regression models using dichotomized biomarker concentrations, patients in the low pTau (cut-off 27.5 ng/l, OR 4.5), tTau (213.5 ng/L, OR 8.1), NfL (1045 ng/L, OR 3.7), Aβ38 (1710 ng/L, OR 2.5), and Aβ40 (3714 ng/L, OR 2.2) groups were more likely to have MMSE scores above 26 (p < 0.05) compared to patients in the high-concentration groups [23]. One study on 31 iNPH patients used a mixed effect model to examine main and interaction effects of time since surgery (baseline, 3 months, 12 months, and 24–60 months) and AD comorbidity (20 iNPH and 11 iNPH + AD) on activities of daily living and iNPH symptoms. A significant main effect of AD comorbidity on MMSE scores was found, as the iNPH + AD scored lower than the iNPH group at all-time points [26]. iNPH patients showed stable improvements in working memory and response inhibition, while iNPH + AD patients failed to maintain initial gains. A study on 49 iNPH patients divided participants into three groups (iNPH with AD comorbidity, iNPH with Parkinson’s spectrum disorder and iNPH without AD or Parkinson’s spectrum disorder comorbidity), according to CSF biomarkers such as pTau and dopamine transporter imaging. Neuropsychological assessments were conducted at baseline and 12 months after surgery using the MMSE for general cognition, the FAB for executive functions, and the EU-iNPH scale (which includes four tests evaluating verbal learning and memory, manual dexterity, and executive functions). Patients without comorbidities showed a remarkable increase in memory and learning ability after surgery, iNPH patients with AD comorbidity performed significantly poorer than those without comorbidities on verbal memory testing [27].

Regarding studies focusing exclusively on long-term follow-up (> 12 months), one study found that pTau, the Aβ38/Aβ42 ratio, and the Aβ42/pTau ratio predicted cognitive prognosis. In this study of 36 iNPH patients undergoing a lumboperitoneal shunt, CSF was collected via lumbar puncture before surgery and through a reservoir puncture 2 years after surgery. The “improved cognitive” group (n = 25) included patients who either maintained an MMSE score ≥ 25 or improved by ≥ 3 points within 2 years after surgery, whereas the “poor cognitive” group (n = 11) comprised those with MMSE scores < 24 points or without an improvement of at least 3 points after surgery. Analysis of the Aβ38/Aβ42 ratio revealed that, while the “improved cognitive” group showed a tendency to shift from Aβ42 to Aβ38 over the 2 years following surgery, the “poor cognitive” group exhibited almost no change [28]. Moreover, pTau levels (sensitivity of 71.4%, specificity of 77.8%, cut-off value of 22.0 pg/mL), Aβ38/Aβ42 ratio (77.8%, 81%, 3.58), and Aβ42/pTau ratio (76%, 72.7%, 14.6) in preoperative CSF have the potential to determine postoperative prognosis. However, this study, includes in the “improved group” all the patients that improved by 3 points in MMSE, even if they remained under the normality cut-off with this change [28]. Subsequent study on 40 iNPH patients found that those with high pTau levels (≥ 30 pg/mL; n = 12) gradually declined toward baseline in MMSE and FAB scores by the third postoperative year, whereas patients with low pTau (< 30 pg/mL; n = 28) maintained an improvement in cognitive functions thereafter [29]. Another study on 51 patients from the same group confirmed these results and, after further subdividing the low-pTau group according to the postoperative change in the Aβ42 toxic conformer ratio, found that the increased-conformer subgroup (whose postoperative Aβ42 toxic conformer ratio rose to levels close to those seen in the Alzheimer’s disease group) showed a decline in MMSE scores between 1 and 2 years after surgery [30].

However, all these studies relied on neuropsychological assessments using only the MMSE or a few additional tests (FAB, RAVLT, Stroop exploring executive functions and verbal memory), without employing a comprehensive battery that could more accurately characterize the various cognitive functions.

These data suggest that iNPH is a complex and often misdiagnosed disease, characterized by heterogeneous clinical presentations with variable domain involvement. As a disorder primarily affecting the elderly, concomitant neurodegenerative and cerebrovascular conditions may influence both the phenotypic presentation and postoperative outcomes. A multidisciplinary team dedicated to iNPH, employing a standardized protocol that includes comprehensive clinical and instrumental evaluations, is crucial for achieving an accurate diagnosis and optimizing patient management.

Our findings of differential cognitive outcomes at baseline and six months post-surgery in iNPH patients with AD core biomarker positivity may have important clinical implications.

From a clinical perspective, our results suggest that the presence of AD core biomarker at baseline should be carefully considered by the multidisciplinary team in the decision-making process for shunt surgery. Furthermore, these findings should be thoroughly discussed with patients and, in particular, with caregivers and family members, addressing the risks and benefits of the procedure and setting realistic expectations. This is especially important regarding cognitive outcomes, which may not improve to the same extent as motor and urinary domains of the classic iNPH triad in patients with AD biomarker positivity.

From a physiopathological point of view, a significant proportion of iNPH patients present CSF AD biomarker positivity. Causes of dementia in iNPH have been poorly understood and are attributed to several factors, including ventriculomegaly-associated stretching and compression of neuronal pathways involved in cognition, interstitial edema, impaired cerebral blood flow, changes in intracranial pressure, and accumulation of waste products such as beta-amyloid and hyperphosphorylated tau [68]. In particular, impaired clearance of waste solutes, such as soluble beta-amyloid leading to beta-amyloid plaques, has emerged as an important mechanism behind dementia [69]. One recent study involving 30 iNPH patients found delayed clearance of the CSF tracer gadobutrol from the entorhinal cortex and the adjacent subcortical white matter, suggesting impaired glymphatic circulation [68]. The glymphatic system refers to a paravascular pathway within the brain that mediates the transport of fluids and solutes and facilitates the removal of metabolic waste products from neural tissue [68].

The presence of a neurodegenerative component in some iNPH patients is suggested by the observation that the beneficial effect of shunt surgery on cognitive function may decline over time, a feature that could distinguish these cases from truly “idiopathic” NPH. Characterizing the neuropsychological profiles and gait patterns across different iNPH phenotypes, and investigating their associations with neuroradiologic features, neurodegenerative CSF biomarkers, and vascular burden, may help elucidate the underlying mechanisms of iNPH and identify potential predictors of favourable outcomes. Therefore, AD core biomarkers should be considered, as they are associated with an earlier cognitive onset and a worse cognitive profile in iNPH, as well as with limited cognitive benefits following surgery, which in turn may affect functional independence and quality of life.

In the present study, AD copathology was defined according to the 2024 NIA–AA criteria for AD diagnosis and staging, which propose a biology-based framework to bridge research and clinical practice [60]. Accordingly, CSF hybrid ratios pTau/Aβ42 and Aβ42/Aβ40 were considered for diagnostic and staging purposes. Increasing evidence indicates that using biomarker ratios minimize the effect of the interindividual variability in Aβ42 levels, which is particularly relevant in conditions with altered CSF dynamics [70], including iNPH, in which reduced CSF Aβ42 levels may occur independently of underlying AD pathology, potentially leading to misclassification when Aβ42 is considered in isolation [20, 71]. Therefore, the adoption of ratio-based biomarkers in the present study represents the most reliable approach for the assessment of AD copathology using CSF, minimizing and compensating for inter-individual variations in biomarker concentrations. Current criteria did not distinguish between pTau/Aβ42 or Aβ42/Aβ40 in either diagnostic or staging contexts. However, the former combines distinct pathophysiological processes: cerebral Aβ deposition and the secondary release of soluble hyperphosphorylated tau in response to Aβ accumulation. Accordingly, some authors have proposed a CSF biomarker-based staging of AD in which a reduction in Aβ42/40 is considered an earlier marker than pTau/Aβ42 [72].

The strengths of this study include that all patients were evaluated and followed up at a single centre using a systematic approach, which ensured uniformity in data collection. Additionally, the study included a large sample and employed a detailed and comprehensive neuropsychological battery at both baseline and follow-up assessments.

Limitations of the study include the smaller sample evaluated at six months after surgery (n = 102 out of 160 shunted patients). The reasons for missing postoperative neuropsychological assessments, detailed in Fig. 1, were primarily related to comorbidities that prevented evaluation, inability to assess patients within the established timeframe, or pandemic-related restrictions that limited access to our centre. Importantly, comparisons between patients who completed follow-up and those who did not undergo postoperative neuropsychological assessment did not reveal substantial differences, indicating comparable baseline characteristics between the two groups. Nevertheless, attrition bias cannot be entirely excluded. Another limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size of the subgroup with AD copathology, which nonetheless reflects the expected proportion in this population and is in line with rates reported in the literature [11, 22, 26–27, 29–30]. Moreover, these CSF biomarkers have not been confirmed in vivo using amyloid PET imaging or cortical biopsy, and APOE genotyping was not systematically performed in our sample. As discussed above, the potential influence of altered CSF dynamics on biomarker measurements cannot be entirely excluded, despite the use of pTau/Aβ42 and Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios. In addition, certain neuroradiological features, such as cerebral and hippocampal atrophy, medial temporal atrophy score, and cortical thickness, were not assessed. Postmortem neuropathological studies are needed to confirm the presence and quantify the burden of Alzheimer’s pathology in iNPH patients. Although in our study parallel forms were used at follow-up assessments to minimize potential practice effects [73, 74], parallel versions were not available for all administered tests, which may represent a further limitation. However, a study specifically investigating practice effects in iNPH patients [75] reported no significant learning effects across neuropsychological measures, supporting the interpretation that the observed changes are unlikely to be driven by practice effects.

Conclusions

Nearly one-quarter of patients exhibited AD core biomarker positivity. These patients differed from those without AD biomarkers, showing greater involvement of general cognition, short- and long-term verbal and visuo-spatial memory, visuo-constructional praxis, language, and attentional-executive functions at baseline. Moreover, patients with AD copathology showed more severe cognitive impairment after surgery compared to patients without AD biomarkers. This neurodegenerative copathology could play a role in determining a specific iNPH phenotype, but further studies are needed to investigate this hypothesis. However, CSF biomarkers used to identify AD copathology in iNPH patients should ideally be confirmed through amyloid PET imaging or cortical biopsy. Further analyses are needed to correlate the neuropsychological profile with neuroradiological characteristics, examine cognitive outcomes in relation to improvements in other clinical domains, and assess levels of dependence and quality of life. In addition, future studies should investigate the predictive role of neuropsychological tests and biomarkers in identifying patients at risk of reduced long-term benefit.
