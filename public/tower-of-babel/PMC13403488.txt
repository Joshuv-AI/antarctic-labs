Immunogenomic landscape of the T cell repertoire after human lung transplantation and its clinical relevance
Authors: Wenyu Jiao, Yufeng Shen, Katherine D Long, Tyla Young, Constanza Bay Muntnich, Adriana Prada Rey, Morcel Khwajazadah, Joshua H Wang, Vineetha Mohan, Kortney Rogers, Arnold Valena, Joseph Costa, Luke Benvenuto, Joshua Sonett, Philippe Lemaitre, Frank D’Ovidio, Selim Arcasoy, Jianing Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-term lung transplant survival is limited by rejection and infection, processes governed by T cells, the posttransplant dynamics of which are poorly defined. Using flow cytometry and T cell receptor (TCR) sequencing of serial bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and peripheral blood samples, we monitored T cell chimerism, phenotypes, and clonotypic dynamics in a small cohort of lung transplant recipients. We identified alloreactive clones via mixed lymphocyte reaction/TCR sequence pairing and microbial-reactive clones via database matching. We found a recipient-derived tissue-resident memory T cell repertoire progressively established in BAL, stabilized by preexisting, multitissue-shared clones. Clonal graft–blood overlap was significantly mediated by CD8+ nonalloreactive recipient T cells of multitissue origin. During infection, pathogen-specific clones expanded in BAL, enriched within the nonalloreactive repertoire. A higher host-vs-graft to graft-vs-host T cell ratio was associated with accelerated recipient repopulation, and enrichment of host-vs-graft clones in BAL was associated with early rejection and pulmonary dysfunction. Our exploratory findings provide novel insight into tissue-resident memory T cell establishment and specialization after lung transplantation and highlight TCR features that warrant further validation as potential correlates of graft outcomes.Keywords: lung transplantation, tissue-resident memory T cells, T cell receptor repertoire, alloreactive T cells, rejection, infection, allograft dysfunction

Introduction

Lung transplantation (LuTx) is the final therapeutic option for patients with end-stage respiratory diseases. Although short-term outcomes have improved, long-term survival remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate of 60%.1 Rejection remains the primary complication limiting transplant success, even with lifelong immunosuppression.2,3 Although most early rejection episodes can be managed by intensifying immunosuppression, these treatments increase infection risk and predispose patients to high-grade late rejections,4 both of which are key risk factors for chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD), the leading cause of mortality post-LuTx.5

T cells play a central role in mediating both graft rejection and host immune defense against pathogens. Human lungs are not only enriched with microbiomes but also carry large numbers of T cells and antigen-presenting cells. This environment may lead to bidirectional alloresponses after transplant: graft-vs-host (GvH) and host-vs-graft (HvG). To dissect the complex immunobiology in clinical LuTx settings, it is critical to distinguish detrimental donor-reactive recipient T cells (HvG-reactive) from protective T cell subsets, such as microbial-reactive recipient T cells or recipient-reactive donor T cells (GvH-reactive) that combat infection or rejection, respectively. Therefore, distinguishing donor vs recipient T cells and integrating their functional phenotypes (alloreactive vs microbial-reactive) with clonotype tracking via T cell receptor β (TCRβ) sequencing offers a promising approach. This strategy can map T cell origin, distribution, and participation in alloresponses and mucosal homeostasis, enabling mechanistic insights into posttransplant (post-Tx) complications with implications for immunosuppression optimization and new therapies.

Both donor- and recipient-derived tissue-resident memory T cells (TRMs) are present in human solid organ grafts.6 Unlike circulating T cells, TRMs in allografts may be partially shielded from the effects of immunosuppression,7 potentially protecting the organ against infection or promoting tissue homeostasis. In organs highly enriched with TRMs (eg, intestines and lungs), slower replacement of donor T cells by recipient T cells in the graft mucosa is associated with reduced rejection and improved graft survival.8–10 To determine the dynamics of TRM repertoire establishment in lung allografts and how functional TCR clones correlate with LuTx outcomes, we conducted longitudinal profiling of donor- and recipient-derived T cells in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), transbronchial biopsies (TBBx), and peripheral blood. This approach comprehensively characterized the immunogenomic landscape of post-LuTx T cell repertoires, including chimerism, phenotypic evolution, pre-Tx tissue origin, and alloreactive and microbial-reactive clonal dynamics. BAL-to-blood comparisons revealed how tissue-resident clones emerge temporally and are associated with rejection, infection, and pulmonary dysfunction. Our study also provides a unique window into human TRM repertoire establishment, demonstrating preferential persistence and functional compartmentalization, revealing principles of tissue-adapted immunity beyond transplantation.

Materials and methods

Human subject recruitment

The study was approved by the Columbia University Institutional Review Board (approval numbers AAAS6206 and AAAR2681). All subjects provided their written, informed consent. Our cohort enrolled 13 adult patients (Supplementary Table S1): 6 single-lung transplant recipients (patients [Pts] 1, 2, 3, 7, 11, 12), 6 double-lung transplant recipients (Pts 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 13), and 1 double-lung-and-liver transplant recipient (Pt 9). Pt 2 and Pt 5 were excluded from cohort analyses due to early death and limited specimen availability. All lung allografts were procured from donors after brain death without normothermic regional perfusion. Additional details on the clinical protocol are available in the Supplementary Material.

Cell isolation, cellular staining by flow cytometry, and carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE)-mixed lymphocyte reactions (MLRs)

Lymphocyte isolation protocols are provided in the Supplementary Material. Donor and recipient cells were distinguished using human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I allotype-specific antibodies selected according to donor–recipient HLA typing (Supplementary Table S2), as previously described.11 Flow cytometry antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table S3. Flow cytometry sampling is summarized in Supplementary Fig. S1A. The CFSE-MLR assays were performed as described previously9 and are briefly introduced in the Supplementary Material.

TCRβ complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) sequencing (TCRβ-seq)

Genomic DNA isolation and TCRβ-seq sample collection (Supplementary Fig. S1B) are detailed in the Supplementary Material. TCR sequencing data were retrieved from Adaptive Biotechnologies's ImmunoSEQ Analyzer. Data analysis followed our previously published method.12–14 Mappable clones refer to clones that were detectable in sequenced pre-Tx lung, lymph node, and/or MLR CFSElow populations from the donors or recipients. Unique sequence counts and total template counts of TCRs identified in different pre-Tx tissues are summarized in Supplementary Table S4. Cumulative frequency was calculated as a percentage of all sequences weighted by copy numbers in designated populations. The unique clone numbers of mappable clones, mappable alloreactive clones, and the cumulative frequency of alloreactive clones of post-Tx samples are summarized in Supplementary Table S5. The numbers of pathogen-reactive TCRs collected from publicly available databases are listed in Supplementary Table S6.15–20

Flow diagram of experimental design and data analysis

We generated a flow diagram (Supplementary Fig. S2) to illustrate the rationale behind the experimental design within each main figure, as well as the relationships among the various analyses presented across the main figures and their associated Supplementary Figures and Tables.

Results

Multilineage chimerism in blood and BAL post-Tx

A total of 13 adult LuTx recipients were enrolled and transplanted in our cohort since November 2020 (Supplementary Table S1). We performed flow cytometry to distinguish donor- and recipient-derived cells from T/B cell/monocyte lineages (Supplementary Fig. S3A) in post-Tx peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) and BAL samples using HLA allotype-specific antibodies (Supplementary Table S2), as described previously.11 BAL is commonly used as a surrogate to monitor immune changes in the lung allograft.10,21 Recipient CD14+ monocytes exhibited rapid infiltration into BAL compared with recipient CD3+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. S3B), reminiscent of our data in human intestinal transplantation.8 Low levels of donor T cell chimerism (<3%) were detected in blood (Supplementary Fig. S3C). Recipient T cell reconstitution kinetics in BAL showed interpatient heterogeneity (Supplementary Fig. S3C).

Graft-infiltrating recipient T cells gradually establish a stable repertoire with TRM phenotypes in the lung allograft, a stability primarily driven by preexisting recipient TCRs

In line with a previous report,10 we evaluated the TRM features of CD8 T cells in post-Tx BAL by flow cytometry and found that overall, donor cells were maintained as TRMs (Fig. 1A, B), while graft-infiltrating recipient cells (Supplementary Fig. S3C) gradually obtained TRM features by upregulating expression of CD69, CD103, and CD49a (Fig. 1A, B; Supplementary Fig. S3D, E). To investigate the relationship between tissue residency and T cell clonal stability, we calculated the Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) values (Fig. 1C) between TCR repertoires from BAL and PBMC samples collected at sequential post-Tx time points in each patient with TCRβ-seq data available (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Notably, JSD values in BAL significantly declined over time, whereas those in PBMCs remained stable. This suggests the progressive establishment of a recipient-derived TRM pool with enhanced repertoire similarity and stability in the lung allograft but not in the periphery. We further assessed the proportions of naïve T cells, central memory T cells, effector memory T cells, and terminally differentiated effector memory T cells reexpressing CD45RA among recipient CD4 and CD8 T cells in post-Tx BAL and PBMCs (Supplementary Fig. S4). The relative proportions of these subsets remained stable over time in BAL (Supplementary Fig. S4A), whereas in PBMCs, the proportion of naïve T cells progressively decreased, and the proportion of effector memory T cells increased over time (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Therefore, the relative composition of naïve and memory T cell subsets did not significantly impact the progressive stabilization of the TCR repertoire in BAL. Even though PBMC samples showed a progressively decreasing proportion of naïve T cells, their repertoires remained largely divergent over time (Fig. 1C). To evaluate the impact of sampling intervals on repertoire stability, we analyzed JSD values against difference in postoperative day (ΔPOD; time between adjacent samples) and found no association with longer intervals, indicating that timing variability did not bias our interpretation (Supplementary Fig. S5).

To delineate mechanisms underlying post-Tx BAL repertoire stabilization, we partitioned TCRs detected in post-Tx samples into preexisting (pre-Tx detected) donor and recipient TCRs, and “de novo” (post-Tx emergent) clonotypes. These were subclassified by initial detection time point and assessed for clonal persistence via recurrence rates of unique CDR3 sequences (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Fig. S6A, B). Preexisting donor TCRs were not discussed further in this analysis given their overall less dominant presence in post-Tx samples, although they demonstrated considerable levels of clonal recurrence in post-Tx BAL, in line with their TRM phenotypes. We observed that pre-Tx recipient TCRs detected across multiple PODs progressively accumulated in both BAL and PBMC repertoires, with many TCR sequences identified at early point(s) recurring at later stages (mean ± standard deviation: 30.32% ± 18.90% for PBMCs, 17.55% ± 10.04% for BAL). In contrast, while newly detected post-Tx TCRs constituted a substantial proportion of both the BAL and PBMC repertoires, only a minor fraction demonstrated recurrent detection (mean ± standard deviation: 1.27% ± 1.38% for PBMCs, 1.77% ± 2.30% for BAL) (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Fig. S6C). Quantitative analysis using normalized area under the curve (AUC) metrics showed pre-Tx recipient TCRs (first detected at the earliest POD) had significantly higher recurrence rates than post-Tx newly detected TCRs in both BAL and PBMCs (Fig. 1F). This demonstrates that repertoire stability post-Tx was primarily maintained by the preexisting recipient TCRs rather than newly detected clones.

Cross-tissue preexisting recipient TCR sequences exhibited enhanced clonal recurrence in both BAL and blood compartments post-Tx

To delineate how pre-Tx recipient tissue origins (Supplementary Table S4) shape post-Tx repertoire establishment, we systematically categorized recipient TCRs detected in post-Tx BAL and PBMC samples into 7 groups based on their pre-Tx detection patterns: (1) lymphoid-PBMC-lung shared, (2) PBMC-lung shared, (3) lymphoid-lung shared, (4) lymphoid-PBMC shared, (5) lung only, (6) PBMC only, and (7) lymphoid only. Notably, although single-tissue-derived TCRs (groups 5–7) constituted substantial fractions of pre-Tx repertoires, their representation was markedly reduced in both post-Tx BAL and PBMCs (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. S7A). Conversely, multitissue shared TCRs (groups 1–4), particularly triple-shared clones (group 1), dominated the post-Tx repertoires (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. S7A).

We evaluated the potential impact of naïve and memory phenotypes on TCR compartmental overlap among pre-Tx recipient samples (Supplementary Fig. S7B), as repertoires enriched for naïve T cells (with high diversity and low clonality) tend to exhibit a lower degree of clonal overlap compared with those enriched for memory T cells.22 As expected, pre-Tx recipient PBMCs with a higher median proportion of naïve T cells (Supplementary Fig. S7B) contained a substantial fraction of “PBMC only” TCRs (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. S7A), whereas pre-Tx lung samples with a lower median proportion of naïve T cells (Supplementary Fig. S7B) harbored a much smaller proportion of “lung only” TCRs (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. S7A). The distinct repertoire sizes of the triple-shared, double-shared, and nonshared compartments are detailed in Supplementary Table S4. We then quantified the relative detection rates of multitissue-shared TCRs vs nonshared TCRs in post-Tx BAL and PBMC samples, normalized relative to their pre-Tx repertoire sizes (Supplementary Table S4). In representative Pt 8, shared TCR subsets, particularly lymphoid-PBMC-lung triple-shared TCRs, demonstrated significantly higher relative detection rates (all >1) compared with nonshared TCRs (Fig. 2B). This pattern was consistent across all patients in normalized AUC analyses of detection kinetics for both BAL and PBMC samples, with triple-shared TCRs exhibiting higher relative detection rates than double-shared TCRs (Fig. 2C). To quantify the temporal similarity of TCR repertoires across tissue origins, we assessed sequence overlap (template-weighted) among nonshared, double-shared, and triple-shared TCR subsets of post-Tx BAL and PBMC samples (representative patient: Fig. 2D). Pooled analysis across all patients revealed significantly higher median overlap proportions in triple-shared vs double-shared or nonshared subsets (Fig. 2E). Although PBMC repertoires exhibited a strict hierarchical overlap pattern (triple- > double- > nonshared), BAL samples showed a similar but less pronounced trend.

Our observation is not solely attributable to higher clonal abundance of broadly shared vs nonshared clones. Even when restricting the analysis to clones that follow the power-law distribution by excluding the outlier top dominant clones in abundance plots23 (Supplementary Fig. S8A), relative detection rate (Supplementary Fig. S8B) and temporal overlap (Supplementary Fig. S8C) analyses recapitulate the results observed with all clones (Fig. 2). Collectively, our data demonstrate that TCRs detected in multiple pre-Tx recipient tissues—including peripheral blood, lung-associated lymph nodes (LLNs), and lung parenchyma—exhibit superior engraftment potential in both BAL and blood compartments. These multitissue-derived TCRs showed significantly higher recurrence rates and were the primary drivers of post-Tx TCR repertoire stability.

T cell clonal overlap between blood and BAL remains substantial for ≥1 year post-Tx, primarily mediated by both multitissue-derived recipient TCRs present prior to transplantation and recipient nonalloreactive CD8 TCRs

To elucidate the spatial dynamics of TCR repertoire distribution post-LuTx, we systematically analyzed T cell clonal overlap between PBMC and BAL compartments (Fig. 3A) across 3 postoperative periods: early (POD <90), mid (POD 90-365), and late (POD >365). We quantified the proportion of BAL TCR sequences detectable in corresponding PBMC samples. No significant temporal pattern emerged in overlapping fractions across periods (Fig. 3B), suggesting persistent T cell crosstalk between peripheral blood and lung allografts throughout the first year post-Tx, with occasionally lower early-phase overlap (eg, in Pt 9) potentially attributable to residual donor T cells in grafts. Aggregate analysis revealed that BAL–PBMC clonal overlap was significantly enriched for recipient-derived pre-Tx TCRs (Fig. 3C), particularly triple-shared (lymphoid-PBMC-lung) TCRs (Fig. 3D) and CD8 nonalloreactive subsets (CD8 non-HvG) (Fig. 3E). Thus, tissue-experienced, nonalloreactive recipient CD8 T cells appear to primarily contribute to the BAL–blood crosstalk after LuTx.

Microbial-reactive TCRs were enriched in lung allografts during infection and predominantly composed of nonalloreactive T cell populations

All patients experienced ≥1 post-Tx infection, many of which were recurrent and involved diverse pathogens (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S1). We hypothesized that nonalloreactive T cells (defined by nonreactivity to donor or recipient antigens in pre-Tx allogeneic MLRs) may persist and expand pathogen-specific clones during infection. Using a strategy we previously validated in human intestinal transplantation,14 we systematically identified microbial-reactive TCRs by cross-referencing our dataset with published TCR-seq databases (including COVID-19-specific clones),15–20 focusing on CDR3β (V-J-AA) signatures. In patients with post-Tx Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) or cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection (Pts 8, 9, 11, 13), the most clinically significant viral pathogens, we observed significant enrichment of EBV- or CMV-reactive TCRs in BAL and lung allografts during active infection compared with that of peripheral blood (Fig. 4A, B). These findings suggest that our TCR mapping approach tracks biologically meaningful pathogen-specific responses. In representative Pt 13, microbial-reactive TCRs were annotated by alloreactive status across lung, BAL, and PBMC compartments (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Fig. S9A). Pooled analysis of all patients across our cohort (Fig. 4D; Supplementary Fig. S9B) revealed a consistent pattern: both donor- and recipient-derived nonalloreactive T cell clones demonstrated significantly greater enrichment for microbe-reactive TCRs than their alloreactive counterparts. This microbial-focused specialization was observed universally across all tissue compartments (lung allograft, BAL, and peripheral blood), suggesting nonalloreactive clones primarily mediate pathogen surveillance.

HvG–GvH imbalance drives recipient T cell repopulation dynamics in BAL and HvG-reactive TCR enrichment in BAL correlates with early rejection and impaired pulmonary function

To quantify the bidirectional alloresponses and their association with graft outcomes after LuTx, we monitored GvH- and HvG-reactive TCRs in post-Tx BAL samples from 8 patients with available pre-Tx specimens (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. S10A–C). An illustrative clonal distribution pie matrix for Pt 8 (representative patient) shows each alloreactive TCR category proportion within the donor- or recipient-mappable CD4 and CD8 TCR repertoires in pre-Tx LLN and post-Tx BAL samples. Total post-Tx BAL template counts indirectly reflect the contribution of pre-Tx unmappable TCRs to these repertoires when total TCR templates are used as denominators (Supplementary Fig. S10A). Five patients (Pts 4, 7, 8, 12, 13) with faster recipient T cell repopulation in the BAL (Supplementary Fig. S3C) showed dominant HvG- over GvH-reactive clones, which differed from that of the remaining 3 patients (Pts 1, 9, 11), who had slower recipient T cell repopulation in BAL (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. S10B). A statistically significant correlation was identified between rapid recipient T cell repopulation and a dominant ratio of HvG- to GvH-reactive TCRs in BAL (Fig. 5B), which was quantified by AUC normalized by days of measurement for both the replacement rate and HvG vs GvH alloreactivities in the BAL. To directly assess preexisting recipient HvG clones, we focused on recipient-mappable TCRs, defined as TCRs detectable in pre-Tx unstimulated or MLR CFSElow T cell populations from recipient LLNs, and used these as the denominator for HvG/non-HvG quantification (Fig. 5C, D; Supplementary Fig. S10C, D; Supplementary Table S5). This approach allowed for a precise quantification of HvG and non-HvG TCR composition using recipient-mappable clones as the denominator. During early acute cellular rejection (ACR) in lung allografts (Pt 7, POD 22; Pt 9, POD 68), we observed high levels of HvG TCRs among recipient-mappable repertoires in BAL and, to a lesser extent, TBBx, but not in PBMCs (Fig. 5C), indicating local HvG clonal expansion correlates with early rejection. In liver allografts of Pt 9, higher HvG TCR levels were observed during mild/moderate ACR (POD5) compared with minimal ACR (POD81) (Fig. 5C), reinforcing the association between HvG-reactive TCRs and rejection. In some patients, early post-Tx (within POD90) HvG peaks were identified in BAL, but not PBMCs, despite negative histopathology for ACR, but preceded late complications (eg, late ACR [POD >200 in Pts 8, 13], de novo donor-specific antibody [Pts 4, 11, 13], CLAD [Pt 4], or death [Pt 13, POD303; Pt 4, POD1556]), suggesting possible undiagnosed subclinical rejection and the need for immunosuppression refinement (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Fig. S10C).

To investigate the relationship between HvG responses and pulmonary function despite temporal mismatches in measurement timing, we stratified the post-Tx course into 3 distinct phases based on BAL HvG TCR dynamics: peak, decline, and baseline (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Fig. S10C, D). We then analyzed the corresponding forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) measurements (normalized to baseline FEV1 values) within each phase (Supplementary Fig. S10D). We observed a significant inverse correlation between HvG TCR levels and pulmonary function across phases (Fig. 5D). In contrast, there was no association between microbial-reactive TCR levels and pulmonary function (Fig. 5D). These findings suggest graft-infiltrating recipient HvG-reactive T cells potentially mediate rejection and may contribute to allograft dysfunction.

To rule out outlier effects in our small cohort, we performed a leave-one-out cross validation analysis (Supplementary Fig. S11A, B). The association between BAL HvG-reactive TCR enrichment and decreased pulmonary function remained consistent upon excluding any individual patient. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic analysis was used to evaluate the discriminative value of the BAL HvG clone percentage for clinically significant FEV1 decline (Supplementary Fig. S11C). A 15% HvG clone cutoff yielded a sensitivity of 0.8 and specificity of 0.67, with an AUC of 0.83, indicating strong discriminative performance.24

Discussion

Our study provides unprecedented insight into the establishment, maintenance, and functional specialization of TRM repertoires in humans. By overcoming the critical limitation of longitudinal access to nonlymphoid tissues—a major barrier in human immunology—we reveal conserved principles of tissue-adapted immunity with broad implications beyond transplant science. We demonstrated the progressive establishment of a recipient-derived TRM repertoire in post-LuTx BAL, with repertoire stability predominantly driven by preexisting, multitissue-shared recipient TCRs. Extending our intestinal transplant findings14—where single-cell RNA sequencing revealed distinct transcriptional programs in cross-tissue vs tissue-restricted T cell clones—we demonstrate in human LuTx that mucosal T cells have circulating and lymphoid counterparts that preferentially repopulate allografts. Beyond tissue adaptability, a subset of these multitissue-primed T cells likely retains recirculating capacity. This aligns with emerging evidence of recirculating TRMs supported by both murine and human studies.6,25–29 Although recirculating TRMs exist in both CD4+ and CD8+ lineages, their dynamics are context-dependent—varying by tissue site and host environment.29–34 Notably, we found that nonalloreactive CD8+ recipient T cells, rather than CD4+ T cells, played a more active role in BAL–blood crosstalk and were more enriched for microbial-reactive TCRs that contribute to host defense against pathogens.

Our study provides complementary insights into intragraft T cell biology compared to a recent study that analyzed explanted CLAD lungs and identified clonally expanded NKG2D+ cytotoxic CD8+ TRMs as pathogenic intragraft T cells.35 Although the CLAD lungs in that cohort were enriched for CMV- and EBV-reactive TCRs, their alloreactivity could not be assessed without pre-Tx donor cells. Our longitudinal data address this gap by linking T cell residency, allospecificity, and microbial reactivity. Critically, we show that microbial-reactive TCRs are preferentially enriched among nonalloreactive rather than HvG-reactive TCRs, whereas graft-infiltrating HvG-reactive T cells are associated with rejection and pulmonary dysfunction.

It is important to note that the nonalloreactive TCRs defined in our study are specific to a single donor–recipient pair within our cohort. Indeed, prior work by our colleagues has demonstrated that a very high proportion of human T cells—if not all—possesses alloreactive potential against a broad array of allogeneic stimulators, as measured by MLRs.23 Consequently, it is reasonable to infer that pathogen-reactive T cells may frequently exhibit cross-reactivity to alloantigens presented by diverse major histocompatibility complex combinations, consistent with previous human studies.36 However, in the context of LuTx, where recipients are typically exposed to a single allogeneic donor (excluding re-Tx scenarios), we observed that HvG-reactive TCRs—unlike pathogen-reactive TCRs, which were more often classified as non-HvG—were disproportionately enriched in post-Tx BAL and TBBx samples compared with peripheral blood samples during early rejection. However, it is worth noting that HvG and non-HvG TCRs in each LuTx patient in our cohort exhibited comparable overall sequence similarities to microbial-reactive TCRs in post-Tx BAL samples, as quantified by minimal Levenshtein distance distributions (Supplementary Fig. S12).

Overimmunosuppression to control rejection increases susceptibility to infection, but the relationship is interrelated and bidirectional. The concept of “heterologous immunity”—in which virus-specific T cells can cross-react with alloantigens to enhance alloimmunity—has been documented.36,37 Barrier organ transplants, particularly the lungs and intestines, are especially vulnerable due to their extensive and often hidden surface areas, making them prone to infection-related rejection.1,38 Beyond barrier organs, studies in rodent skin transplantation39,40 and human kidney transplantation41 have shown that immune responses to commensal organisms and CMV, respectively, can promote graft rejection. These findings underscore the need for effective and balanced control of both infection and rejection to improve clinical outcomes across a wide range of solid organ transplants. To this end, we established innovative multidisciplinary platforms that elucidate rejection and infection mechanisms. The resulting mechanism-informed datasets pave the way for future refinement of immunosuppression to simultaneously overcome rejection and infectious complications.

Our study has several limitations. First, the small LuTx cohort precluded meaningful multivariate analyses, and COVID-19–related disruptions prevented early post-Tx blood sampling from Pts 1 and 4. In addition, microbial-reactive TCR mapping was constrained by incomplete reference databases, and functional validation of pathogen reactivity was beyond our study scope. The observed association between HvG TCRs and rejection was identified in only a subset of patients, which may be partially explained by the presence of subclinical or histologically undetectable rejection.42 Although our data suggest that lung-infiltrating HvG T cells may be linked to pulmonary dysfunction—a signal supported by the robustness of our leave-one-out cross validation/receiver operating characteristic analyses despite the small sample size—larger cohorts are required to confirm the generalizability and clinical utility of these features.

By dissecting bidirectional alloresponses and tissue-residency dynamics, this study charts new pathways for developing precision therapies that selectively modulate alloimmunity, such as targeting rejection-associated effector genes in allograft mucosa13 while preserving antimicrobial immunity. This addresses a critical unmet need in transforming LuTx outcomes.
