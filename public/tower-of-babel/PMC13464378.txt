Early amyloid pathology in mice synergistically worsens post-sepsis behavioral impairment and microglial synaptic phagocytosis
Authors: Kate A. Giffin, Niyati Puranik, Angela C. Bustamante, Marc Padilla, Zhuoer Zeng, Grace Randolph, Leah Wheaton, Shannon Moore, Michael Newstead, Scott J. Denstaedt, Joanna Spencer-Segal, Benjamin H. Singer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis survivors often experience long-term cognitive and mental health impairment. We lack a clear understanding of the biological mechanisms driving impairment after sepsis and the factors that make a brain vulnerable to dysfunction after sepsis. We hypothesize that amyloid pathology, which is neuroinflammatory and accumulates with age even in cognitively normal people, is a latent risk factor for post-sepsis decline driven in part by neuroinflammation and microglial dysfunction. We induced sepsis via polymicrobial abdominal infection (cecal ligation and puncture; CLP) in young adult 5xFAD mice to model early amyloid pathology. 5xFAD CLP mice demonstrated generalized conditioned fear response and impaired cognition in the puzzle box task. Brain transcriptomics revealed persistent upregulation of neuroinflammatory pathways after resolution of the infection, with a distinct increase in 5xFAD CLP survivor mice. Complement gene expression and C3aR protein were heightened in 5xFAD mice, while C3 increased after infection. Microglial synaptic phagocytosis, a potential mechanism driving brain dysfunction after sepsis, was increased in 5xFAD CLP survivor mice 3 weeks after infection. While there was a small C3aRhigh microglial population in 5xFAD brains, the change in synaptic phagocytosis occurred in microglia with basal levels of C3aR expression. Overall, our findings suggest that early amyloid pathology synergistically heightens the behavioral change, neuroinflammation, and microglial synaptic phagocytosis caused by sepsis.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition caused by dysregulated response to infection (M. Singer et al., 2016) that can significantly reshape daily life for years after the initial illness. Patients who survive sepsis often experience cognitive impairment, new or worsening psychiatric disorders, and clinically significant symptoms of mood disorders (Bienvenu et al., 2015; Iwashyna et al., 2010; Rabiee et al., 2016). Without additional understanding of the biological mechanisms and the factors that make a patient vulnerable to post-sepsis impairment, we lack critical knowledge to address this increasing clinical problem.

Neuroinflammation has been consistently associated with impairments after sepsis. Rodents who have survived sepsis often have long-term cognitive and memory impairments that are associated with inflammation (Barichello et al., 2005; Giridharan et al., 2023; Moraes et al., 2015; Tuon et al., 2008). Previous work from our lab has demonstrated that sepsis survivors have impaired extinction learning accompanied by hallmarks of neuroinflammation, including myeloid cell infiltration, pro-inflammatory transcriptional phenotype in microglia, and elevated chemokine and cytokine levels (B. H. Singer et al., 2016). Sepsis-induced neuroinflammation may persist weeks to months after infection (Moraes et al., 2015; B. H. Singer et al., 2016; Valdés-Ferrer et al., 2013). While this phenomenon has been well described, the proximal mechanisms linking inflammation to brain dysfunction are unclear.

Furthermore, outcomes from sepsis are heterogeneous across patient populations. Because neuroinflammation has a prominent role, factors that heighten neuroinflammation are promising aspects that could make a brain vulnerable to dysfunction after sepsis. Amyloid beta (Aβ) is best known for its role in neurodegenerative Alzheimer's disease, but it also accumulates during normal aging alongside neuroimmune changes. Aβ has been found in the brains of healthy individuals over the age of 60 without cognitive decline (Crystal et al., 1988; Jansen et al., 2015; Parnetti et al., 2019; Rodrigue et al., 2012). Yet Aβ can result in chronic neuroinflammation (Ahmad et al., 2019; Hampel et al., 2021; Heneka et al., 2025; Lian et al., 2016; Morales et al., 2014) and may set the stage for a heightened neuroinflammatory response to challenge (LaRocca et al., 2021; Neher and Cunningham, 2019). Some literature suggests that systemic infection promotes neuroinflammation and accelerates Alzheimer's disease neuropathology (Gasparotto et al., 2018; Heneka et al., 2025; Itzhaki et al., 2020; Wendeln et al., 2018). Alzheimer's disease patients who died with infection have altered neuroinflammatory processes including microglia number or morphology, increased brain cytokine levels, and blood-brain barrier breakdown, (Asby et al., 2021; Granholm et al., 2024; Lopez-Rodriguez et al., 2021; Poloni et al., 2021). The reverse may also be true: asymptomatic Aβ pathology, common in cognitively normal elderly people, may also prime the brain for enhanced neuroinflammatory response to sepsis. This is an increasingly important avenue for research as sepsis occurs more commonly in older people, who are already at higher risk for cognitive dysfunction, and improved medical care means more of these patients are surviving their critical illness (Singer et al., 2019). We thus sought to further understand the role of Aβ as a risk factor for brain dysfunction in the setting of sepsis, independent of its roles in aging or neurodegenerative disease.

Aβ can act like a cytokine, interacting with immune signals and cells including microglia (Gitter et al., 1995; Hemonnot et al., 2019; Weaver, 2020). Extensive alterations to microglial transcriptomics, phenotype, and function have been described both clinically and preclinically in the setting of amyloid pathology (Ahmad et al., 2019; Fujikawa and Tsuda, 2023). Aβ can induce microglial proliferation, phagocytosis, morphological change, and cytokine release. Furthermore, microglial activation is positively correlated with amyloid load in patients (Fan et al., 2017).

Microglia are also activated in sepsis and involved in the functional consequences, though the exact microglial changes that alter brain function are unclear (Denver and Cunningham, 2025). Synaptic dysfunction mediated by microglia, including phagocytosis of synapses, has been proposed as a convergent mechanism of many neurological conditions (Scott-Hewitt et al., 2023). A decline in synapse numbers has been widely observed in Alzheimer's disease, with this decline in synapses correlated to cognitive decline and presumably linked to disrupted brain circuits and function (DeKosky and Scheff, 1990; Terry et al., 1991; Wishart et al., 2006). Erroneous or excessive removal of synapses by microglia is a mechanism that deserves close scrutiny for potential involvement in sepsis, in part because microglial synaptic phagocytosis is mediated by immune pathways that are activated in sepsis such as complement cascade proteins. The complement system is an important regulator of innate immunity and microglial synaptic elimination in both development and neurologic disease (Gomez-Arboledas et al., 2021). Complement activation is a hallmark of sepsis (Daskoulidou et al., 2025; Fattahi et al., 2020). Aβ activates complement pathways, and brain complement proteins have been found even in early stages of neurodegeneration (Morgan, 2018; Weaver, 2020). Complement 3a receptor (C3aR) is a mediator of late stage Aβ pathology, suggesting it may have a role in pre-clinical disease progression (Gedam et al., 2023; Lian et al., 2016; Litvinchuk et al., 2018). Thus, both illness and Aβ pathology set the stage for complement-mediated microglial synaptic phagocytosis. Indeed, microglial synaptic pruning has been observed after polymicrobial abdominal infection in mice (Chung et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2023). However, to our knowledge the impact of sepsis on microglial synaptic phagocytosis in the setting of pre-existing Aβ pathology has not been previously evaluated.

We hypothesize that early amyloid pathology results in a neuroinflammatory environment, and when sepsis occurs, the double hit of Aβ accumulation and sepsis could cause an acceleration of brain dysfunction in part through complement-mediated microglial synaptic phagocytosis. We investigated the link between sepsis and early Aβ accumulation by inducing sepsis via abdominal infection (cecal ligation and puncture; CLP) in the 5xFAD mouse model of Aβ pathology at 4-6 months of age. Sepsis survival and Aβ synergistically heightened behavioral impairment and neuroinflammation. Immunohistochemistry and flow cytometry demonstrated increased C3aR expression in a subpopulation of microglia in 5xFAD mice. The combination of sepsis and Aβ increased microglial synaptic phagocytosis after recovery from infection. However, this change occurred in microglia with basal, rather than high, levels of C3aR expression. We found evidence that the neurological consequences of sepsis are worse in the setting of early Aβ pathology, suggesting that amyloid may make the brain vulnerable and microglial synaptic phagocytosis may be a mechanism of post-sepsis decline.

Methods

Additional details are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Mice

Animal experiments and procedures were previously approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Animal Care & Use Committee (PRO00008999). All procedures were performed in accordance with recommendations from the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Animals were cared for by the University of Michigan Unit for Laboratory Animal Medicine (ULAM) facility and staff.

Preclinical amyloid accumulation was modeled using adult (4-6 months old) male and female 5xFAD mice on the C57BL/6 background (JAX-MMRC 034848). Age-matched littermate male and female C57BL/6 mice were used as wildtype controls. Mice were grouphoused in a temperature and light/dark cycle-controlled environment with ad libitum access to food and water.

Abdominal sepsis

Abdominal sepsis was modeled in mice using cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) as previously described (B. H. Singer et al., 2016; Walley et al., 1996). A 1-2 cm laparotomy was performed under anesthesia with ketamine and xylazine. The cecum was ligated with a silk suture and punctured through and through with a 19-gauge needle. A single dose of imipenem/cilastatin (0.5 mg/mouse) and saline (1 mL) was administered at the time of surgery. Anesthesia controls were anesthetized in the same manner as the CLP mice, however, no surgery was performed. To reduce neuroinflammatory effects of anesthesia, unoperated controls that did not experience anesthesia or surgery were also used. Infrequent deaths which occurred within 5 days post CLP surgery were considered expected and attributed to surgery/infection.

Experimental design

Experimental time points for all groups are in reference to the surgery date of mice undergoing CLP in a given cohort, even when not all mice underwent surgery. All the mice used in this study were age matched. The timepoint of 5 days after CLP was used to examine changes during subacute infection. Resolution timepoints of 14, 21, and 25 days after CLP were used to determine changes after resolution of the infection. Behavioral experiments were performed 14 days after CLP, at which point locomotion returned to baseline levels so behavioral change could not be attributed to sickness behavior (Fig. 1G). RNAseq was performed on hemibrain tissue harvested 25 days post-CLP. Behavioral tests

Contextual fear conditioning

To assess hippocampal-dependent learning and memory, mice underwent contextual fear conditioning 14 days after sepsis as previously described (B. H. Singer et al., 2016).

Puzzle box

To assess multimodal cognitive functioning, mice were tested using the puzzle box 14 days after sepsis. In the puzzle box task, mice must learn to escape from an open, bright averse environment to the closed, dark environment by way of a passage connecting both zones (Ben Abdallah et al., 2011; O'Connor et al., 2014). With each successive trial, the passage is progressively obstructed, resulting in increased difficulty in task completion.

Elevated zero maze

To assess approach-avoidance behavior 14 days after sepsis, a standard murine elevated zero maze (San Diego Instruments, Inc., San Diego, CA) was used. Mice completed a single video recorded trial lasting 300 s, and during this time the mice were free to explore the testing track.

Brain transcriptomics

RNA isolation, sample preparation, and RNA sequencing

RNA was isolated from brain hemispheres 25 days after CLP infection for transcriptomics using Trizol (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) followed by removal of contaminating DNA using DNAse I. Between 3 and 6 μg of isolated RNA was sent to the Advanced Genomics Core at the University of Michigan for quality control checks, library preparations, sequencing, and initial data quality control. All samples in this study had an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) greater than 8 (Pulido-Salgado et al., 2018). Sample cDNA libraries were prepped using the Quantseq 3′ mRNA-seq Library Prep Kit FWD (Lexogen). Libraries were subsequently sequenced on Illumina's NOVASeq platform using the 100 cycle flowcell. Raw data is available for download at the National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus (series accession number GSE176506) (Barrett et al., 2013; Edgar et al., 2002).

Differential expression and gene enrichment analysis

We performed differential expression analysis on the bulk RNAseq data to identify genes that were significantly changed by either genotype or CLP using DESeq2 (v1.48.2) (Love et al., 2014). Multigroup analyses were performed using the R package baySeq (v2.42.0) (Hardcastle and Kelly, 2010) to identify genes in which expression in 5xFAD mice undergoing CLP differed from all other combinations of groups. Gene enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was conducted using ShinyGo (v0.85) (Ge et al., 2020).

Flow cytometry assay for microglial synaptic phagocytosis

To approximate levels of microglial synaptic phagocytosis, we used flow cytometry to identify the percentage of microglia that contained the synaptic protein SNAP25 (Aw et al., 2020; Dissing-Olesen et al., 2023). Mice were euthanized with CO2 and perfused. Whole brains were immediately mechanically minced and incubated in a phagocytosis inhibitor cocktail, followed by enzymatic digestion, myelin clearing, and surface antigen staining (Table S1). Samples were washed, fixed, held overnight at 4°C, and permeabilized, followed by intracellular antigen staining. Samples were analyzed on the Attune NxT flow cytometer (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA).

Gating was performed by first selecting singlets cells based on scatter and DAPI (Supp. Fig. 1). Myeloid cells were identified as the CD11b+/CD45+ cell population. Microglia were distinguished from border-associated macrophages as Ly6G−/Ly6C−/CD206-. SNAP25+ and C3aR+ cells were identified by setting the minimum threshold at which there were <1% positive events in the contemporaneous full-minus-one (FMO) control sample of mixed cells from all brains included in each individual experiment.

Immunohistochemistry

Brain tissue for immunohistochemistry was prepared by perfusion with 1x PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were post-fixed, cryoprotected, frozen, and sectioned in 40 μm sections. Immunostaining was performed with anti-C3aR (clone 14D4, 1:100, Hycult, catalogue number HM1143) as previously described (B. H. Singer et al., 2016). Sections were visualized and photographed using an Olympus BX-70 microscope.

ELISA

ELISA was used to measure brain C3 and Aβ1-42 protein. Brain tissue lysis buffer was supplemented with EDTA (2 mM), cOmplete protease inhibitor (Sigma-Aldrich), Halt phosphatase inhibitor (Thermo-Fisher), and FUT-175 (50 μg/mL, BD Biosciences). Total protein content was assayed by BCA assay (Pierce). Total C3 was measured by anti-mouse C3 ELISA (Abcam, Boston, MA). Soluble amyloid beta was isolated by homogenization in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Aβ1-42 was assayed by (anti-human amyloid beta, DAB142, R&D Systems).

Statistics

Transcriptomic data were analyzed and graphed in R. All other data were analyzed and graphed in GraphPad Prism 10. Ordinary 2-way ANOVA was used to compare data from experiments that included two factors and four groups. Unpaired t-tests were used in experiments with two groups. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with logrank (Mantel-Cox) test was used to analyze mortality and puzzle box data. Threshold of significance for all tests was p-value ≤ 0.05. Graphs created in Prism are shown as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

Results

To model the interaction of sepsis survival and amyloid pathology, we studied adult (4-6 months) male and female 5xFAD C57BL/6 J and wildtype control mice after sepsis. This age was selected to minimize potential floor and ceiling effects in behavioral outcomes. 5xFAD C57BL/6 mice demonstrate few behavioral deficits at 6 months of age and earlier (Forner et al., 2021; Neuner et al., 2019) and we found no behavioral differences between wildtype and 5xFAD mice that did not experience CLP (Fig. 1). 5xFADs with the C57BL/6 J genetic background have a delayed onset of amyloid plaque burden, but neurodegenerative pathology begins early. These mice have been shown to have significant brain transcriptomic changes by 4 months of age (Bundy et al., 2019; “Neuropathology of 034848 and 034840,” n.d.).

Amyloid plaque deposition due to sepsis or peripheral inflammation alone has been reported months after inflammatory insults (Basak et al., 2021; Gasparotto et al., 2018; Wendeln et al., 2018), but we did not observe increases in soluble amyloid after infection. We found that two weeks into sepsis survival, soluble Aβ1-42 was significantly elevated in whole brain homogenates of 5xFAD mice compared to wildtypes (2-way ANOVA, p < 0.001) but was not increased by CLP in either genotype when measured by ELISA (Supp. Fig. 2A). 5xFAD mice did not have more severe sepsis. Both genotypes experienced similar mortality and weight loss in the 2 weeks following sepsis across multiple cohorts (Supp. Fig. 2, logrank test for survival p = 0.35; repeated measure ANOVA for weight change with significant effect of time and animal, but no effect of genotype, p = 0.21).

Amyloid pathology and sepsis survival result in distinct behavioral deficits

We used several behavioral tests to determine the combined effect of amyloid pathology and sepsis survival on cognition and affect 14 days after CLP. At this timepoint, mice are no longer at elevated risk of death, have regained weight, and do not display obvious illness behaviors that would confound interpretation of behavioral tests (B. H. Singer et al., 2016). We confirmed that there was no sickness-induced lethargy at this timepoint as all groups had the same total distance traveled in the elevated zero maze (Fig. 1G).

First, we evaluated sepsis survivors' response to contextual fear conditioning 14 days after CLP. Our prior work did not find impaired acquisition of contextual fear memory after sepsis in wildtype mice, though sepsis survivor mice did demonstrate increases in freezing response (B. H. Singer et al., 2016). 5xFAD and age-matched wildtype littermates after CLP were compared to anesthesia controls (n = 42). Percent freezing during the pre-shock period increased during each day of conditioning, as expected (Fig. 1A). On the third day of training, there was a significant interaction effect between genotype and sepsis on percent freezing (2-way ANOVA, p = 0.002 for genotype/infection interaction), where 5xFAD CLP mice froze at a significantly higher rate than wildtype CLP (Šídák's multiple comparisons test, p = 0.0039) or 5xFAD anesthesia (Šídák's multiple comparisons test, p = 0.0012) mice (Fig. 1A). There was no significant (p > 0.05) difference observed between groups in freezing in the trained context during the probe trial, in the absence of foot shock (Fig. 1B). However, in the novel context there was a significant interaction between genotype and infection (2-way ANOVA, p = 0.002). Freezing was significantly increased in the 5xFAD CLP mice when compared to the 5xFAD anesthesia control group (Šídák's multiple comparisons test, p < 0.0001) and wildtype CLP mice (Šídák's multiple comparisons test, p < 0.0001), and wildtype CLP mice had higher freezing than wildtype anesthesia mice (Šídák's multiple comparisons test, p = 0.0001) (Fig. 1C).

To determine whether anxiety-like behavior was increased by amyloid pathology and/or sepsis, mice underwent the elevated zero maze test 14 days after CLP (n = 43). Sepsis was associated with a significant (2-way ANOVA, p = 0.002) decrease in distance traveled in the open corridor. There was no difference by 5xFAD genotype or an interaction between infection and genotype (Fig. 1F).

These results suggest that while 5xFAD sepsis survivors have increased generalization of fear conditioning, they do not have a specific anxiety-like phenotype or a deficit in forming hippocampal dependent memory. Given that patients often experience changes in overall cognition following sepsis (Calsavara et al., 2018), we then performed a test of multimodal brain function. Puzzle box is a problem-solving behavioral paradigm aimed at examining general cognition and executive function in mice (Ben Abdallah et al., 2011). Mice (n = 90 in 5 cohorts) were tested 14 days post-surgery or treatment conditions. A mixture of anesthesia and unoperated controls were used. In the initial open door trial, mice had to pass from a brightly lit aversive compartment to a dark compartment via an open underpass. There were small but statistically significant differences in the rate of completion of the open door task (Fig. 1D, p < 0.01) with 5xFAD CLP mice completing the task slower than wildtype control (pairwise longrank test p = 0.017) but not 5xFAD control or wildtype CLP (p = 0.37 and p = 0.48, respectively). Although 5xFAD CLP mice were slightly slower than the wildtype controls, they were still able to complete the task at a comparable rate to the other groups. These results suggest that there are not specific motor or sensory deficits unique to 5xFAD sepsis survivors that impair performance in escape from the open arena. In the closed door trial, the door was blocked, and mice needed to use the underpass to get to the dark compartment. 5xFAD CLP mice did significantly worse than other groups on this trial, with fewer 5xFAD CLP mice escaping than mice in the other groups (Fig. 1E, p = 0.0013 vs wildtype control, p = 0.026 vs wildtype CLP, p = 0.04 vs 5xFAD control). This further suggests that 5xFAD sepsis survivor mice experience high-level cognitive deficits distinct from those induced by sepsis survival or amyloid deposition alone.

Overall, behavioral tasks revealed that CLP induces greater anxiety-like behavior in both wildtype and 5xFAD mice. 5xFAD CLP mice had generalization of fear conditioning to a novel context and deficits in global cognition. The interaction between amyloid pathology and sepsis survival worsens the negative outcomes of infection both on affect and cognition.

Differential expression analysis reveals persistent neuroinflammation after CLP and increase in neuroinflammation in 5xFAD compared to wildtype survivors

To better understand the impact of sepsis and amyloid pathology on brain gene expression after recovery from infection, we examined QuantSeq data from brain hemispheres at 25 days post-sepsis (n = 65). Using the generalized linear model (GLM) framework of DESeq2, we examined several contrasts to understand the effect of sepsis and genotype on gene expression (design: ∼Treatment + Genotype + Treatment:Genotype).

To understand the main effect of sepsis, we compared wildtype unoperated to wildtype CLP. 98 genes were identified as differentially expressed with an adjusted p-value or Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) of < 0.05 and a |log2FC > 0.6| (Supp. Table 2, Fig. 2A). The genes that were upregulated by CLP were in immune pathways, including IL-17 signaling, neutrophil and leukocyte aggregation, and response to bacterium (Table 1). The effect of sepsis in 5xFADs was more muted. When comparing 5xFAD unoperated to 5xFAD CLP, there were 10 differentially expressed genes (Supp. Table 3, Fig. 2B). Two genes overlapped between the comparisons, S100a8 and S100a9, resulting in the IL-17 signaling and neutrophil and leukocyte aggregation pathways also being labeled as enriched (Table 1). To understand the main effect of genotype, we compared the wildtype unoperated and the 5xFAD unoperated groups. There were 6 genes that were differentially expressed (Thy1, Psen1, Ccl6, Ccl3, Cst7, Fcer1g; Supp. Table 4, Fig. 2C–Table 1). The gene with the highest fold change was Thy1, and the promoter for this gene is used to drive the 5xFAD genotype.

To understand whether each genotype responds to sepsis differently, we compared the wildtype CLP and 5xFAD CLP groups. 83 genes were differentially expressed (Supp. Table 5, Fig. 2D). These genes largely fell into the category of amyloid pathology related genes or immune system genes, including complement, chemokine, and interferon genes. Select significant ShinyGO pathways included phagosome, lysosome, immune response, and complement and coagulation cascades (Table 1). Finally, we examined the interaction term to understand whether the effect of treatment on gene expression was different across genotypes. There were no differentially expressed genes in the interaction (Supp. Table 6).

The DESeq2 results highlighted the persistence of inflammation after CLP but may have been underpowered to detect interactions among genotype and sepsis survival when using a pairwise comparison approach to assess each comparison individually. Furthermore, the imbalance in group size and expression variability across groups could lead to spurious conclusions when using pairwise comparisions. We thus employed a Bayesian framework for further analysis.

baySeq differs from frequentist gene expression approaches as it not only allows for the simultaneous comparisons of multiple groups, but it also uses a Bayesian approach to examine data for patterns of differential expression. In this framework, we are able to ask how gene expression in 5xFAD CLP survivor mice differs from all other groups across all combinations of groups without performing multiple pairwise comparisons.

This analysis revealed 82 transcripts that passed the FDR threshold of 0.05 (Supp. Table 7). These genes were largely upregulated in the 5xFAD CLP group compared to all other groups (Fig. 3). Complement pathways: C3 ligand is increased by sepsis while C3aR receptor is increased by 5xFAD genotype

Complement cascade genes were differentially expressed in the 5xFAD CLP group compared to the other groups in both the DESeq2 and baySeq analyses, including the gene C3ar1 (Supp. Tables 5 and 7). Given these results and the significance of complement signaling in neuroinflammation, amyloid pathology, and synaptic phagocytosis, we investigated whether complement component C3 ligand and C3aR receptor protein levels were increased along with the heightened gene expression. We found that 5 days after CLP, C3 ligand in perfused brain tissue was increased by sepsis in both wildtype and 5xFAD mice (Figs. 4A and 2-way ANOVA, p < 0.001). Although microglia constitutively express C3ar1 (Quell et al., 2017), C3aR expression was below the level of detection by immunohistochemistry in most cells in the cortex and hippocampus of wildtype mice (Fig. 4B–D). C3aR was noticeably present in the 5xFAD control and 5xFAD CLP mice 14 days post-CLP with dense staining in the hippocampus and subiculum (Fig. 4B). Similarly, in the cortex there was little C3aR expression in the wildtype control and CLP mice compared to 5xFAD control and 5xFAD CLP mice 14 days after CLP (Fig. 4C). Quantification of the C3aR + cortical cells revealed a significant interaction between genotype and infection (2-way ANOVA, p = 0.0396), where there was no effect of CLP in wildtypes (Šídák's multiple comparisons test, p = 0.9) but a significant effect of CLP in 5xFADs (Šídák's multiple comparisons test, p = 0.0203). There was significant increase in C3aR + cells in 5xFAD CLP mice compared to wildtype CLP (Šídák's multiple comparisons test, p < 0.0001) and in 5xFAD control mice compared to wildtype control mice (Šídák's multiple comparisons test, p = 0.002). During subacute sepsis (5 d post-CLP), there also was no difference by genotype in SNAP25 positivity (Fig. 5B). Discussion

As more patients survive critical illness, it is imperative that the mechanisms driving brain dysfunction after sepsis are well understood. These consequences are heterogeneous among sepsis survivors (Bienvenu et al., 2015; Dijkstra-Kersten et al., 2020; Jackson et al., 2003; Rabiee et al., 2016). To begin addressing the factors that underlie the heterogeneity of brain dysfunction after sepsis, we have focused on one potential neuroinflammatory latent risk factor: asymptomatic amyloid pathology.

Many cognitively normal elderly people have significant amyloid deposition (Aizenstein et al., 2008; Crystal et al., 1988; Nelson et al., 2012; Parnetti et al., 2019; Walker, 2020). Absence of symptoms does not mean that Aβ is inert. Aβ plaques induce inflammatory responses and immune training in glia, leading microglia and astrocytes to have an exaggerated acute neuroinflammatory responses to subsequent inflammation (Ahmad et al., 2019; Holtman et al., 2015; Lopez-Rodriguez et al., 2021; Walker, 2020). Alzheimer's disease neuropathology may worsen brain dysfunction during acute infection. Prior cognitive impairment is a risk factor for delirium (Davis et al., 2015; Umoh et al., 2024), and diagnosis of a neurodegenerative disorder is associated with greater infection risk (Hu et al., 2023). Furthermore, Alzheimer's disease patients who died with infection have increased neuroinflammatory changes (Asby et al., 2021; Granholm et al., 2024; Lopez-Rodriguez et al., 2021; Poloni et al., 2021; Rakic et al., 2018).

Conversely, infections or systemic inflammation may cause or worsen amyloid pathology (Itzhaki et al., 2020). Many microorganisms have been linked to Alzheimer's disease (Bu et al., 2015), but a causative role has not been firmly established. Systemic inflammation and delirium can accelerate cognitive decline in patients with Alzheimer's disease (Fong et al., 2009; Holmes et al., 2009). Many studies have found increased amyloid pathology, including plaque deposition, after systemic inflammation (Manabe and Heneka, 2022; Neher and Cunningham, 2019). However, this evidence is mixed, as others have shown a reduction in Aβ after LPS (Heneka et al., 2025).

Informed by these two lines of research and the prevalence of brain dysfunction in sepsis survivors, here we ask a related but distinct question: could amyloid pathology be a latent risk factor for brain dysfunction after sepsis? We modeled early amyloid pathology with 4-6 month old 5xFAD mice. There were no behavioral differences between wildtype and 5xFADs that did not experience CLP, as generally at this age amyloid neuropathology has begun but behavior change is limited. Sepsis severity was comparable between the genotypes. We did not see a change in Aβ1-42 at 14 days post-CLP. This aligns with previous work examining how mice with early amyloidosis respond to CLP showing that Aβ staining, insoluble Aβ1-40 and soluble Aβ1-42 was increased only after at least three months (Basak et al., 2021; Giridharan et al., 2023; Rotaru-Zavaleanu et al., 2022; Vo et al., 2025). CLP likely has subtle and regionally different effects on amyloid in mice. In the future, it would be important to examine amyloid plaques/fibrillar amyloid, spatial distribution of amyloid change, and longer time periods after sepsis.

Our results suggest a synergistic worsening of behavioral impairments in mice that both survive sepsis and express Aβ. In the fear conditioning task, all groups acquired fear memory and retrieved the memory in the trained context. Previously, CLP-surviving male APP/PS1 mice were shown to have impaired memory in the novel object recognition compared to APP/PS1 mice that received sham surgery, but there was no comparison to wildtype mice (Giridharan et al., 2023). However, other groups reported that neither genotype nor sepsis changed the performance on novel object recognition task or Y-maze test (Rotaru-Zavaleanu et al., 2022; Vo et al., 2025). Although we did not observe a memory deficit in fear conditioning, the CLP groups had higher rates of freezing in the novel context, with 5xFAD CLP mice freezing the most. Fear generalization has been observed in wildtype CLP survivors before (Bourhy et al., 2022). Here, we show that amyloid pathology worsens the generalization process while preserving fear memory acquisition and retrieval in the trained context. This could be due to a PTSD-like or anxiety-like phenotype. One group reported an increase in anxiety in TgCRND8 mice with amyloid that received sepsis compared to those who did not, but did not compare this finding to wildtype sepsis mice (Vo et al., 2025). We found that sepsis increased anxiety-like behavior in both wildtype and 5xFAD mice. Further work is required to determine the exact deficit driving fear memory generalization in 5xFAD sepsis survivors, with different implications for underlying circuits. In addition to heightened anxiety, these mice could have perceptual deficits (Dunsmoor and Paz, 2015), greater forgetting of stimulus attributes, or errors in consolidation or retrieval of context-specific memories (Jasnow et al., 2017).

We then evaluated multimodal general cognition with the puzzle box task (Ben Abdallah et al., 2011). While there was a statistically significant difference in how quickly the 5xFAD CLP mice escaped through the open door compared to wildtype controls, 5xFAD CLP survivor mice were able to complete the task at similar rates as the other groups, indicating no specific motor or sensory deficits unique to 5xFAD sepsis survivors that impair escape. In the more challenging closed door trial, the 5xFAD sepsis survivors completed the task at significantly worse rates than the other groups. This suggests that for tasks requiring problem solving, amyloid worsens the effects of sepsis on cognition. Similar to the fear generalization finding, this may be rooted in inadequate perceptual processing that caused difficulty discovering the underpass.

We found that early Aβ neuropathology enhanced the neuroinflammatory response to CLP. In pairwise comparisons of bulk RNA-seq of brain hemispheres 25 days after CLP, IL-17 pathway genes, specifically S100a8 and S100a9, were upregulated in both wildtype and 5xFAD sepsis survivors. Similar findings have been reported between 12 h and 7 days after CLP in hippocampal tissue in wildtype mice (Barter et al., 2019; Gao et al., 2024; Hamasaki et al., 2019; Liao et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025). Here, we found that neuroinflammation persists 25 days after the surgery, when the illness should be resolved, in brain tissue beyond the hippocampus. The time course of neuroinflammation and how it impacts behavior, glial physiology, and neuronal function is an important one to understand since the transition between acute sepsis and long-term brain dysfunction is a long-standing theme in the field (Giffin and Singer, 2026). While we observed an increase in inflammatory transcripts and microglial synaptic phagocytosis after 3 weeks, there are certainly changes occurring during acute and subacute infection that influence disease course that should be targeted by future research. Furthermore, Aβ neuropathology heightened persistent neuroinflammation. In the direct comparison of wildtype CLP and 5xFAD CLP transcripts, complement, chemokine, and interferon genes were upregulated. To overcome the limitations of multiple pairwise comparisons, we used a Bayesian approach to determine how the gene expression of 5xFAD CLP mice differed from all other groups across all combinations of groups (Hardcastle and Kelly, 2010). Most of the differentially expressed genes were neuroinflammatory, including many complement genes.

Upregulation of transcripts in phagosome, lysosome, and especially complement pathways pointed towards microglial synaptic phagocytosis, which is regulated by the complement system (Gomez-Arboledas et al., 2021; Hammond et al., 2019; Lian et al., 2015, 2016; Litvinchuk et al., 2018). Microglial complement expression drives synapse dysfunction in sepsis (Chung et al., 2023) and advanced amyloid pathology and inflammation induced by LPS can synergistically drive microglial neuroinflammation and neurophysiologic dysfunction (Lopez-Rodriguez et al., 2021). Additionally, microglia and innate immunity induced by sepsis can mediate cognitive decline, as microglial depletion can significantly improve memory after CLP (Gao et al., 2024) and cognitive dysfunction induced by neurotoxic Aβ oligomers administered after recovery of CLP (De Sousa et al., 2021). Mixed results have been reported about markers of microglial activation in sepsis survivors with pre-existing amyloid, with some reporting increased staining for Iba1 depending on region and timepoint examined (Giridharan et al., 2023; Rotaru-Zavaleanu et al., 2022; Vo et al., 2025) while others reported no difference (Basak et al., 2021).

We assessed a microglial functional output: synaptic phagocytosis. Microglia-mediated synaptic dysfunction has been proposed as a convergent mechanism of neurological disorders (Henstridge et al., 2019; Miyanishi et al., 2021; Scott-Hewitt et al., 2023; Vilalta and Brown, 2018). The 5xFAD CLP mice had increased microglial synaptic phagocytosis 3 weeks after sepsis compared to wildtype CLP mice. We hypothesized that the increased C3aR expression observed in 5xFADs may drive a state of heightened sensitivity to C3 signaling during sepsis. We observed a population of C3aRhigh microglia only in 5xFAD mice that also exhibited high proportions of SNAP25 positivity. Unexpectedly, however, the change in SNAP25+ proportion with CLP in 5xFAD mice occurred in C3aRlow microglia. Since work with reporter mice indicates that C3ar1 is expressed in the vast majority of microglia (Quell et al., 2017), even if protein cannot be detected in all cells through flow cytometry or immunostaining, we attempted to modulate rates of synaptic phagocytosis even within this C3aRlow population. We avoided constitutive knockout of C3 or C3ar1 to study this signaling pathway more definitively, as that would have altered synaptic pruning during development and the independent effect of amyloid pathology. Instead, we took a pharmacological approach with the small molecule C3aR inhibitor SB290157 (Quell et al., 2017), which has been reported to normalize behavior change and reduce gliosis, inflammatory cytokines, and in vitro microglia phagocytosis in a variety of neuroinflammatory models (Lian et al., 2016; Wadhwa et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). However, several pharmacologic studies have cast doubt on its specificity to C3aR and whether it acts as an antagonist under all conditions (Li et al., 2020; Mathieu et al., 2005; Rodriguez et al., 2024). We did not find any difference in microglial synaptic phagocytosis with C3aR antagonist treatment, and the most likely explanation for this result is impaired efficacy of the drug, which we were unable to verify. Future experiments effectively targeting C3aR signaling or specific populations of C3aR-expressing microglia will be important to fully understand this pathway in the intersection of sepsis and amyloid. Additionally, we studied 5xFAD mice relatively early in Aβ deposition to model the impact of sepsis in patients with asymptomatic amyloid pathology and to avoid masking possible interactions with strong main effects of 5xFAD genotype. The impact of complement signaling on microglial synaptic phagocytosis may be more subtle in this experimental paradigm than in advanced neuropathology when neuroimmune signaling intensifies (Gomez-Arboledas et al., 2024).

Our work complements previous studies of microglial synaptic pruning after CLP in rodents without amyloid. In one study, CLP induced microglial neuronal contact, with morphology indicative of a phagocytic phenotype (Shulyatnikova et al., 2022). Microglial engulfment of synaptic protein was increased after CLP (Jiang et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2023) and associated with loss of dendritic spines, electrophysiological changes, and behavioral change, strengthening the intuitive link between microglial synaptic pruning and impaired function. Finally, memory deficits and synaptic loss in sepsis survivors were attenuated by depleting microglia or injecting anti-C1q antibody (Chung et al., 2023). These manipulations provide further evidence that microglial- and complement-mediated synaptic elimination plays an important role in brain dysfunction after sepsis. We additionally showed microglial synaptic pruning in many thousands of microglia in hemibrains, including regions beyond the hippocampus, with a high-throughput flow cytometry assay, determining that C3aR is likely not a marker of pro-phagocytic microglia, and investigating amyloid pathology as an intensifier.

Our use of a flow cytometry assay for microglial synaptic pruning is a strength and a limitation. Traditionally, microglial synaptic phagocytosis has been examined via microscopy, which is time-intensive, regionally restricted, and the number of cells analyzed is limited (Dissing-Olesen et al., 2023; Schafer et al., 2014; Tremblay et al., 2010). The FEAST methodology adapted here (Dissing-Olesen et al., 2023) allowed us to examine thousands of microglia simultaneously from hemibrains, greatly increasing our power to detect small subpopulations engulfing synapses. However, there is day-to-day variation in total number of microglia isolated and in SNAP25 positivity. We did not directly visualize microglial synaptic phagocytosis, and future experiments could focus on developing hypotheses about regions most affected by sepsis where microscopy experiments would be most effective. A dearth of feasible flow cytometry antibodies for specific synapses meant that we were unable to examine whether excitatory, inhibitory, or other kinds of synapses were primarily engulfed. The method is sufficiently labor intensive that the number of animals that can be studied simultaneously is limited. To preserve feasibility and rigor, we conducted experiments in a pairwise manner, limiting our ability to statistically assess the effects of 5xFAD genotype and sepsis survival in the usual 2 or 3 factor design. This also prevented us from rigorously assessing sex differences, although we included male and female mice in all experiments. Considering the known sex differences in Alzheimer's disease pathology, neuroinflammation, behavior, microglial biology, and response to infection, this is an important target for follow-up study (Barter et al., 2019; Bundy et al., 2019; Han et al., 2021; Klein and Flanagan, 2016; Lopez-Lee et al., 2024).

Due to the complexity and variability of both the behavioral and physiologic measures, we prioritized performing experiments contemporaneously with relatively large littermate cohorts, rather than exactly matching age and sex across groups. We modeled early amyloid pathology with 4-6-month-old male and female 5xFAD C57BL/6 J mice. This two-month span was important for experimental logistics and feasibility, and we took care to ensure that ages were balanced within individual experiments, but it is possible that age variability affected outcomes. While we included both sexes in all experiments, we were generally underpowered to rigorously assess whether the results differed by sex, and this should be considered in future studies.

A major future direction is whether this change in microglial synaptic phagocytosis changes synapse numbers. One group found that female TgCRND8 and wildtype mice 3 months post CLP had lower synaptophysin protein levels (Vo et al., 2025), indicating a potential sex-dependent effect of CLP on synapses. Furthermore, the mechanism of increased microglial pruning after sepsis needs to be identified. There could be a pro-phagocytic post-sepsis microglial phenotype, more synapses could be tagged for removal, and neuron/synapse intrinsic factors could increase rates of synaptic phagocytosis. Neuronal apoptosis has been observed both clinically and preclinically in sepsis (Giffin and Singer, 2026), so microglia might be engulfing dead, dying, or damaged neuronal material. In this case, microglial synaptic phagocytosis would be a sign uncovering excessive neuronal damage (scavenging) rather than evidence of a cell gone rogue that is actively removing synapses or causing damage to neurons (culling) (Pereira-Iglesias et al., 2025). If there is a pro-phagocytic microglial phenotype that develops after sepsis, it will be important to determine markers of this phenotype and target these cells to fully understand their role. It is crucial that future work directly examines the number and quality of synapses and/or dendritic spines, the function of neurons that have been contacted by microglia, and that live-imaging will improve such that we can observe microglia engulfing synaptic material in real-time (Eyo and Molofsky, 2023). Finally, behavior change will need to be linked to these or additional mechanisms to fully understand the biological basis of brain dysfunction after sepsis.

There are several other neuroimmune modulators of microglial synaptic pruning that are promising targets for future research. Several modulators of microglial synaptic phagocytosis had increased gene expression in the 5xFAD CLP mice compared to other groups, including C1qa, C1qc, Trem2, Abca1, and Psen1. Additional receptors for C3 and its breakdown products, including C3R/CD11b, P2Y6, TREM2, and TAM receptors, deserve scrutiny considering the increase in brain C3 we observed. C1q and C5aR are other complement proteins that may be involved as they have separately been shown to be involved in microglial synaptic pruning after sepsis and in Alzheimer's disease (Chung et al., 2023; Schartz et al., 2024; Scott-Hewitt et al., 2023). Many other neuroinflammatory pathways have been shown to mediate synaptic pruning and may play a role including interferons, MAPK and OXTR/ERK/STAT3, and HMGB1 (Jiang et al., 2023; Scott-Hewitt et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2023).

Finally, regionality should be considered for future research. Our studies of transcriptional response and microglial synaptic phagocytosis were carried out in hemibrains. This was an intentional choice to understand global changes after sepsis and maximize the number of cells captured via flow cytometry. Additionally, our behavioral findings indicated subtle global changes rather than a localizable deficit associated with a specific brain region or circuit. While most similar research has focused the hippocampus and cortex, it will be important to examine other regions in connection with specific behavioral, histological, and functional changes.

Our findings suggest that early amyloid pathology enhances behavioral change and neuroinflammation in sepsis survivor mice. We presented both circumstantial transcriptomic and direct flow cytometry evidence to support an increase in synaptic phagocytosis after sepsis in brains made vulnerable to neuroinflammatory challenge by amyloid pathology. While we hypothesized that signaling via C3aR was involved, our findings did not support that C3aRhigh microglia were drivers of synaptic phagocytosis after sepsis. This work may encourage future research on the potential role of other complement components, as well as other mechanisms of microglial activation and synaptic phagocytosis, as potential causes of sustained brain dysfunction after sepsis. Other important outstanding questions include how microglial synaptic phagocytosis disrupts brain functioning, which synapses/circuits/regions are most affected, the role of sex differences, and other neuroimmune mechanisms. As we deepen our understanding of the mechanisms of brain dysfunction after sepsis, it is imperative to continue to study latent risk factors that may modulate those mechanisms.
