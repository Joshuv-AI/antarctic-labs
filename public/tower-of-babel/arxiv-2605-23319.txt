Tractable Maximization of Budgeted Phylogenetic Diversity on Networks Utilizing Node Scanwidth
Niels Holtgrefe, Jannik Schestag

Abstract
Identifying a subset of taxa that maximizes Phylogenetic Diversity (PD) is a cornerstone of quantitative conservation planning. Traditionally, PD is defined over a phylogenetic tree in which leaves resemble present-day taxa and the branch lengths capture the estimated evolutionary distinctiveness. While PD maximization is computationally tractable on trees with unit costs, the problem becomes NP-hard when transitioning to phylogenetic networks or to budgeted versions in which protecting taxa incurs non-homogeneous costs. In this paper, we address these two challenges by providing definitions and a comprehensive analysis of three distinct variants of budgeted PD on networks. We conduct our study through the lens of a small structural parameter, node scanwidth (nsw), which measures the “tree-likeness” of a phylogenetic network. We show that two of the considered variants can be optimized in 𝒪 ∗ ​ ( 2 {nsw} ⋅ B 2 ) \mathcal{O}^{*}(2^{\nswwithoutN}\cdot B^{2}) time, where B B is the budget. For the computationally harder, third variant, we provide an algorithm to compute PD scores in 𝒪 ∗ ​ ( 3 {nsw} ) \mathcal{O}^{*}(3^{\nswwithoutN}) time. We further contribute the first exact algorithms to compute node scanwidth, recognizing that the utility of algorithms based on nsw depends on the ability to compute nsw and its corresponding decomposition. Our approaches integrate data reduction rules, dynamic programming, and an Integer Linear Programming formulation. We validate our theoretical results through extensive experiments on highly reticulated, simulated networks containing several hundred taxa, using heterogeneous costs. Our implementation computes PD scores and optimal nsw in fractions of a second, even on the most challenging instances. Furthermore, our budgeted optimization algorithms significantly outperform existing benchmarks for computing PD on networks, which were previously limited to unit-cost scenarios. The software makes analyses even on networks with a thousand taxa tractable in practice.

Introduction

The biosphere is currently undergoing what scientists describe as the Sixth Mass Extinction
[
1
,
6
]
.
Unlike previous events driven by natural phenomena, the current decline in biodiversity is largely anthropogenic, resulting from habitat destruction, climate change, and over-exploitation.
In this urgent context, conservation biologists face the daunting “agony of choice”: Given limited resources, which species should be prioritized for protection?
[
34
]

Functional diversity—the range of biological traits and roles within an ecosystem—is often considered the gold standard for prioritization
[
24
,
28
]
.
However, collecting exhaustive trait data for hundreds of thousands of species remains a major bottleneck
[
10
]
.
Phylogenetic diversity (PD) has thus emerged as a desired proxy, operating on the principle that the evolutionary history captured in a phylogeny reflects the underlying feature diversity of a clade.
Classically, the basis of PD is a phylogenetic tree in which the leaves represent present-day species and edge weights can model phylogenetic differences between species.
The PD score of a set of species
A
then is the total weight of the edges between the root of the tree and any species in
A

[
10
]
.

Fortunately, PD can be computed with a greedy algorithm on trees
[
10
,
26
,
30
]
, such that even very large instances can be solved optimally within seconds
[
25
]
.
Several extensions that make the problem more realistic were introduced, but always at the cost of computational complexity.
One of the first generalizations introduced was adding integer costs to species
[
27
]
, which however also generalizes the weakly NP-hard
Knapsack
problem.

While PD on trees is well-understood, biological reality is rarely strictly bifurcating
[
16
]
. Evolutionary processes such as hybridization, horizontal gene transfer, and incomplete lineage sorting necessitate the use of phylogenetic networks
[
16
,
23
]
; see
Figure

1
. By moving beyond trees, PD measures can account for the reticulate nature of evolution, capturing the unique contributions of taxa with multiple ancestries. This ensures that conservation priorities reflect the true genetic heritage and evolutionary potential stored within a complex ecosystem, rather than an oversimplified approximation of a tree.
On networks, however, even simple variants of PD maximization become NP-hard
[
3
,
31
,
35
]
.
While budgetary constraints (in its most general form the Generalized Noah’s Ark Problem) have been studied on trees
[
21
,
27
]
, their integration with network models remains unexplored.

Our Contribution

In this paper, we address this gap by considering budgeted PD on networks, where taxa are assigned integer costs.
As optimization of PD on trees with a budget is already NP-hard
[
27
]
and many variants of PD on networks are W[2]-hard with respect to the solution size
[
17
,
32
]
, we can not hope for tractable algorithm with respect to tree-likeness or the budget alone.
Instead, we propose a combined parameterization with respect to the budget plus the tree-likeness of the network, captured by its
node scanwidth

[
4
]
(formal definition in the next section).
Specifically, our research provides the following contributions to the study of computational phylogenetic diversity.

New Budgeted PD Variants:
We introduce budgeted variants of phylogenetic diversity maximization on networks, in which species may have varying costs of protection.

Optimizing Budgeted PD:
We show, in
Section

4
, how to solve three budgeted variants of PD on networks efficiently with respect to the combined parameter of budget (
B
) and node scanwidth (
{\nswwithoutN_{{{N}}}}
). We provide two
O^{*}(2^{{\nswwithoutN_{{{N}}}}}\cdot\min^{2}(B,\overline{B}))
time algorithms, where
\overline{B}
is the total cost of all species minus the budget.
We further show that values for the third variant can be computed in
\mathcal{O}^{*}(3^{\nswwithoutN_{{{N}}}})
time.

Computing Node Scanwidth:

We consider, in
Section

3
, with
node scanwidth
a small measure of the tree-likeness of a network, which has so far hardly been considered in literature
[
4
,
29
]
.
We present reduction rules and an exact algorithm that finds the optimal node scanwidth, and we further provide an
Integer Linear Program
formulation.

Experimental Validation:
We provide a full implementation and validation of our four algorithms, in
Section

5
. Empirical testing confirms that node scanwidth can be computed efficiently on large and realistic networks. Our new implementation also improves upon the existing phylogenetic diversity algorithm in
[
13
]
, even on non-binary networks.

Related work

In recent years, phylogenetic diversity on networks has been studied extensively, with various measures and complexity results established for rooted and semi-directed models
[
3
,
17
,
18
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
35
]
.
While theoretical progress is significant, practical implementations remain scarce.
To our knowledge, the only scalable software packages are
PaNDA

[
13
]
and an implementation for a PD variant in
[
31
]
for which we consider lower and upper bounds.
Leveraging bounded scanwidth,
PaNDA
can optimize unit-cost instances with level-15-networks with hundreds of taxa in a matter of minutes
[
13
]
, while in
[
31
]
a bigger parameter is considered.

Lemma 1

(
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bigstar}
)
.

Let

Let

All values of

(1)

Computing Node Scanwidth

In this section, we show how to solve
Node Scanwidth tree-extension
, that is, we find an optimal tree-extension together with the node scanwidth for a given DAG
G
.
We do this in two steps; first we apply reduction rules to identify and remove easy structures in
{N}
, and then we show how to solve
Node Scanwidth tree-extension
on the remaining small subgraphs with an algorithm based on
[
12
]
, or with an
Integer Linear Program
.

Throughout this section, let
G=(V,E)
be a DAG—not necessarily a phylogenetic network.
As we operate with general graphs, reticulations in this section are defined to have in-degree at least
2
and can have out-degree
\geq 2
.
This ensures that every non-leaf vertex is either a reticulation, a tree-vertex, or a chain-vertex.
We note that there might be several roots, and leaves may be reticulations.

3.1
Preliminary Observations

The bag of any vertex contains all its parents.
We conclude the following.

Observation 2
.

The node scanwidth of any graph is at least the maximum in-degree of its vertices.

For a given graph
G
with tree-extension
\Gamma
, we observe that

is also a tree-extension when removing edges from

For each vertex

that does not have larger node scanwidth.

Observation 3
.

For any graph

We continue with an observation that will be particularly useful when computing

Observation 4
.

Let

be a chain vertex with exactly one

There is an an optimal tree-extension

of

in which

is the only child of

in

and

Proof of

Assume first that in a tree-extension

is directly above

The claims about the bags of

and

Let

be an optimal tree-extension of

If

has more than one child, then attach all children that do not have a path to

to another child of

.
If

is directly above

in
\Gamma
, then we are done.
Assume that this is not the case.
Since there is an arc

in

from

to

in
\Gamma
.
Define
\Gamma^{*}
to be the tree that results from

by removing

(and attaching all
\Gamma
-children of

to the
\Gamma
-parent of

) and inserting

directly above

We observe that
\Gamma^{*}
is a tree-extension since each directed path that exists in
\Gamma
also exists in
\Gamma^{*}
except for the ones from

to a vertex of

, which do not correspond to edges in

It remains to show that the node scanwidth does not increase with the operation.
Since

only has one child

and

are affected, that is, the vertices of
P
.
The bags of vertices in

now contain

Because

∎

Lastly, we consider the following easy cases.

Observation 5
.

If

If

To see that

Observation 6
.

Let

be a bi-connected graph with exactly one reticulation

Then, the node scanwidth of

The optimal tree-extension is a path of

Since

Since

3.2
Data Reduction Rules

We continue with rules that reduce the complexity of a graph

6
Discussion

In this work, we found a good trade-off between realistic models of phylogenetic diversity and computational efficiency.
By moving beyond tree models to explicit phylogenetic networks that are not even necessarily binary, combined with non-uniform costs per taxa, we provide a framework that better represents the reticulate nature of life on Earth.

Our
\mathcal{O}^{*}(2^{{\nswwithoutN_{{{N}}}}}\cdot\min^{2}(B,\overline{B}))
solvers have proven efficient when
{\nswwithoutN_{{{N}}}}
is small—as is common in many empirical biological datasets
[
12
]
—such that the addition of budgetary constraints does not necessarily lead to an explosion in the algorithm’s running time.

Our practical implementations validate the theoretical results and establish a new baseline for efficiency, also.
By beating state-of-the-art algorithms
[
13
]
on unitary instances while simultaneously handling arbitrary (non-binary) network topologies and non-uniform conservation cost, we provide a tool that is ready for deployment in conservation planning.

From a theoretical perspective, we wonder whether
b-Max-Tree-PD
can be solved in
\mathcal{O}^{*}(2^{B})
time.
Relatedly, we suspect that
Min-Tree-PD
can not be solved in
\mathcal{O}^{*}(c^{{\nswwithoutN_{{{N}}}}}\cdot B^{d})
time for constants
c
and
d
.
If true, it could be interesting to investigate whether
Min-Tree-PD
can at least be solved in
\mathcal{O}^{*}(c^{\ell}\cdot B^{d})
time for constants
c
and
d
, where
\ell
is the vertex-level.
This is immediate for binary networks but appears significantly more difficult for arbitrary networks.
Addressing these questions will be useful for further refining the algorithmic tools available to protect our planet’s vanishing biodiversity.

A.5
\sum_{A\subseteq B}2^{|A|}=3^{|B|}
for any set
B

We briefly show that
\sum_{A\subseteq B}2^{|A|}=3^{|B|}
for any set
B
, as it can be found in standard monographic literature such as
[
11
]
.
We actually show this for a more general case with
a\in\mathbb{N}
, but
a=2
yields our result.

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subseteq B}a^{|A|}

We consider the different sizes of
A
independently.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{|B|}\sum_{A\subseteq\binom{B}{k}}a^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{|B|}\binom{|B|}{k}\cdot a^{k}

We multiply by 1 to see that this is now the formula of the binomial coefficient.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{|B|}\binom{|B|}{k}\cdot a^{k}\cdot 1^{|B|-k}=(a+1)^{|B|}
