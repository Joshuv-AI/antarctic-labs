1.0 INTRODUCTION
1.1 Purpose of the Project
The overarching objective of the proposed project was to demonstrate a process-intensified
approach to economically viable, modular H2 production from waste biomass using catalytic
membrane reactors (CMRs) based on carbon molecular sieve (CMS) hollow-fiber membranes.
Specifically, the CMRs were developed to selectively remove H2 during the high-temperature
water-gas shift (WGS) reaction to circumvent thermodynamic limitations on converting CO to
CO2 and H2. To this end, our core technical activities were to (1) develop high-performance CMRs
based on thermally stable, processible polymer-derived CMS membranes and (2) design and
prepare new multicomponent CO2-tolerant nano-catalysts using a unique flame-based aerosol
process for WGS reaction.

1.2 TRA System

The Technology Readiness Assessment (TRA) System for the proposed membrane reactor
technology includes two key components, membranes and catalysts. The success of the technology
lies in its individual performance and synergistic integration with comparable kinetics.

1.3 Commercial Application
The targeted commercial application is small-scale hydrogen production from biomass. Notably,
the technology will enable H2 production from waste carbonaceous feedstocks, such as municipal
solid waste (MSW), legacy coal waste, and waste plastics, which are otherwise incinerated or
disposed of in landfills. H2 production provides carbon-neutral energy while alleviating a burden
on local communities. Depending on the need, the CO2 can be further purified for food
applications, enhanced oil recovery, or chemical conversion. CO2 can also be used for storage. The
processes can also be assisted by the 45Q tax credit for negative carbon emissions.

The membranes also have potential for various industrial gas separations, including H2/CO2,
H2/CO, H2/CH4, He/N2, He/CH4, and CO2/CH4. The flame-aerosol high-entropy metal oxide
catalysts may have potential for industrial applications.

2.0 MATURATION OF THE TRA SYSTEM
2.1 Beginning TRL of the TRA System
As shown in the Project Description, the proposed technology was based on comprehensive
preliminary data, including the two key components (membranes and catalysts), which met TRL
3 (Experimental proof of concept). Specifically, polymer-derived carbon molecular sieve (CMS)
materials exhibited very high H2 permeability (> 200 Barrer) and H2/CO2 selectivity (>100) at 150
– 350 ℃, and they were fabricated into thin-film composite membranes; metal oxide-based
catalysts were routinely produced using flame aerosol systems.

2.2 Proposed Research to Mature the TRA System
Our proposed research work aimed to bring the technology to TRL 4 (Laboratory scale validation
in a relevant environment) through the following activities in this project:
 • For membranes, we proposed to prepare and optimize CMS hollow fiber membrane
 modules to achieve H2 permeance of 1,000 GPU and H2/CO2 selectivity of 40.

 • For catalysts, we proposed to design and prepare nano-catalysts with high WGS activity
 and stability under CMR conditions.
 • More importantly, we proposed to design, prepare, and characterize CMRs for high-
 temperature WGS reaction by integrating H2-selective membranes, catalysts, and

 on the newly developed membranes for H2/CO2 separations.

Performance Attributes: We have made progress in membrane and catalyst development, and in
integrating both components into membrane reactors for the water-gas shift reaction. The
technology reached TRL 4 by the end of the project. The details are summarized below.

We fabricated CMS HFMs that maintain stable mixed-gas H₂/CO₂ separation (H2/CO2
selectivity=103 and H2 permeance=1020 GPU at 375 oC) under simulated WGS reaction
conditions. We established repeatable spinning/carbonization and acid-doping protocols, then
fabricated multi-fiber bundles (≥32 fibers) and demonstrated stable mixed-gas H₂/CO₂ separation
at 200–300 °C with CO and steam. High H₂ permeance was maintained while H₂/CO₂ selectivity
remained in the targeted range, with multi-hundred-hour durability.

We synthesized flame-aerosol high-entropy metal-oxide (HEMO) WGS catalysts. A
(FeCrNiCoMn)Ox spinel outperformed a conventional Fe–Cr catalyst at high temperature (e.g.,
~90% CO conversion at 450 °C), and a (FeCrNiCoMn)₃O₄ variant met our kinetic target with a
CO₂ reaction order of −0.42, mitigating product inhibition. We demonstrated CMRs with a
synthesized HEMO catalyst at ~350 °C, 30 psig, and DGHSV≈12,500 h⁻¹, and we increased CO
conversion by ~20% over a packed bed and achieved up to ~60% H₂ recovery. The CMRs also
exhibited strong H2 purification (H2/(CO2+CO+H2) > 98%) and CO2 capture performance
(CO2/(CO2+CO+H2) > 95%) at various conditions. We completed 400-hour and multi-cycle
stability tests without any performance loss and >100-h continuous operation of the CMRs for
WGS reaction.

We performed a model-based sensitivity analysis using our validated MATLAB membrane-
reactor framework (1-D, isothermal, plug-flow; counter-current permeation with WGS kinetics)
to rank the technical levers that control performance. H₂/CO₂ selectivity governed dry H₂ purity
and CO₂ slip; and maintaining selectivity in the 40–60 range achieved > 90% dry H₂ without further
downstream purification. These results defined clear design targets for CMR, including
maximizing effective membrane area and permeance per footprint, minimizing leakage and
permeate pressure, and operating in the temperature window that balances kinetics and selectivity.

We conducted techno-economic analysis (TEA) by comparing our CMR flow sheet with the NREL
solvent-capture framework. Specifically, the assessment in which we substitute the conventional
high-temperature and low-temperature reactors with a single high-temperature catalytic membrane
reactor, uses a tube-side steam sweep to in situ remove H2, and sends the permeate to a PSA does
achieve the COE reduction target. The reason is steam intensity, where the required steam-to-dry-
gas ratio (SDGR) to maintain high H2 capture and conversion increases natural-gas OPEX from
$1.4 to $47.4 MM/yr, thereby offsetting the savings from smaller reactors and lower catalytic
volumes. These designs result from compromised steam duty objectives, combined with
evaporative plus SDGR integration, dried-gas permeate-side pressurization, active membrane

2.3 Potential Post-Project Maturation and Commercialization of the TRA System
With the successful laboratory testing of the membranes, we are ready to scale up membrane
production and conduct a field test of the membrane process with 50–500 lb/hr of actual syngas,
which will bring the technology to TRL 6 (Engineering/pilot-scale, similar (prototypical) system
validation in a relevant environment). We are discussing with industrial partners and seeking future
funding opportunities to achieve these goals. We will also provide the critical membrane reactor
technology and work with industrial partners to design, engineer, and operate the demonstration
system.

3.0. REFERENCE MATERIAL
3.1. Definition of TRA System
NETL’s interpretation (Section 3.2) of the DOE TRL definitions (Appendix Y) is based on a view
of technology maturation in which “components” are integrated into a “system” that is being
assessed for its technology readiness. To clearly and consistently apply the DOE TRL definitions,
one must first precisely identify what “system” is being assessed, defined herein as the
“Technology Readiness Assessment (TRA) System.” Since most technologies can be viewed as
subsystems within larger systems, multiple choices are available for defining the TRA System.

However, note that the choice of the “level” of the TRA System affects how TRLs are assessed:
  • A TRL 3 is achieved for the specified TRA System when analytical performance
 predictions for each of the TRA System’s critical components have been validated in
 separate experiments (i.e., without integration across components). Accordingly, the table
 in Section 3.2 shows the required scope of TRL 3 as “single component” and the required
 integration of TRL 3 as “none.”
  • A TRL 4 or 5 is achieved for a given TRA System when the targeted performance of each
 of its critical, multi-component subsystems (or the entire TRA system) have been
  • validated in a laboratory environment (TRL 4) or relevant environment (TRL 5).
  • Achieving TRLs 6 to 9 requires testing of the entire, fully integrated, TRL system.

Consider, for example, a fuel cell stack. Its critical components are multiple, identical fuel cells. In
turn, the critical components of each fuel cell are an anode, cathode, and electrolyte. If one wished
to assess the technology readiness of the fuel cell stack, the TRA System would be defined as an
integrated system of multiple fuel cell subsystems, and a TRL 6 could only be achieved by
successfully testing an entire stack of integrated fuel cells. However, if one instead wished to assess
the technology readiness of only the fuel cell, the TRA System would be defined as an integrated
system of cathode, anode, and electrolyte components, and a TRL 6 could be achieved by
successfully testing just a single, integrated fuel cell. In both cases, achievement of TRL 6 could
be claimed, but only in the context of the properly specified TRA System.
