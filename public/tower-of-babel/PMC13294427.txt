Biomass derived carbon dots from green synthesis to multimodal bioimaging applications
Authors: Keqi Zhao, Zhenghao Huang, Yasin Abdi Saed, Xiang Sun, Ziliang Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biomass-derived carbon quantum dots (BCDs) have attracted considerable research attention as a novel category of sustainable fluorescent nanomaterials, attributed to their adjustable photoluminescence, superior biocompatibility, and eco-friendly synthesis methods. BCDs are made from renewable biomass sources like plants, algae, animal byproducts, and microorganisms, following the principles of green chemistry. This makes them much better for the environment and cheaper to make than carbon dots made in the traditional way. The field still has three big problems, though: the luminescence mechanism is still not well understood, with different pathways like carbon-core and surface-state emissions not having a single theoretical framework; optical modulation strategies are still not well developed, with quantum yields often falling below 30% and poor batch-to-batch consistency making it hard to standardize applications; and not enough is known about in vivo metabolic pathways and long-term toxicity to allow for systematic toxicological evaluation and clinical translation. The complex luminescence mechanisms, including carbon-core-state, surface-state, molecular-state, and cross-linked enhanced emission (CEE), are thoroughly examined to clarify the structure–property relationships that dictate their optical behavior. By using controlled synthesis and surface modification techniques, BCDs can be made to emit light in the visible to near-infrared (NIR) range. This makes them perfect for use in different types of multimodal imaging, such as single-photon, multi-photon, and photoacoustic bioimaging. Their natural ability to emit light, along with their low toxicity to cells and high stability in light, makes it possible to see cells and tissues in high detail. We talk more about the problems we are having right now with standardizing synthesis and controlling optical properties with precision. Future research should concentrate on refining reaction conditions and clarifying luminescence mechanisms to promote the clinical application of BCDs as next-generation, sustainable imaging agents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04725-0.

Introduction

Biomass-derived carbon dots (BCDs) are a new type of carbon-based nanomaterial that could be very useful in medicine. Their unique biological origin comes from biomass, like herbal medicines and plant extracts, which gives them natural healing properties. The creation of bioactive carbon dots (BP-CDs) from biomass is influenced by traditional Chinese medicine [1]. Recent studies have uncovered significant parallels between the carbonization methods (such as charring techniques) and surface passivation strategies employed in traditional Chinese medicine processing and the synthesis approaches of contemporary biomass-derived carbon dots (BCDs) [2]. For instance, Zhang et al. [3] used a “materials-herbology” approach. Using a hydrothermal method (180 °C, 6 h), they turned leaves from Scutellaria baicalensis into carbon dots (CDs) that were all the same size (1–5 nm). These CDs, which had a lot of carboxyl and hydroxyl groups on their surfaces, were mixed with baicalein (BAI) in a sodium alginate hydrogel (BCG) to make a composite system. This system is very important for controlled drug release, antioxidant and antibacterial activities, and helping cells heal. In the same way, Liu’s team [4] was able to make fluorescent carbon dots (CDs) using the traditional Chinese medicine Alisma orientale as a carbon source through a green pyrolysis method (200 °C, 18 h). The average particle size of these CDs is 10.14 ± 0.30 nm, and their structure is amorphous. Their surfaces are rich in hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, which makes it possible for them to catalytically generate·OH radicals. When they are excited at 320 nm, they give off blue fluorescence at 405 nm with a quantum yield of 15.8%. In biological contexts, the CDs demonstrate minimal cytotoxicity to HeLa cells (cell viability exceeding 95%) and facilitate multicolor cellular imaging under multi-wavelength excitation.

Bio-based carbon nanodots (BP-CDs/BCDs) have clear advantages over traditionally made carbon nanodots when it comes to both sustainability and performance in applications. These materials are usually made through green synthesis routes that use renewable biomass precursors, like plants, animals, algae, organic molecules, or polymers [5]. These precursors are not only cheap and easy to find, but they also give the carbon dots great biocompatibility and environmental friendliness. BCDs can be made in large quantities using green methods like hydrothermal and microwave-assisted synthesis, which have a much smaller effect on the environment.

The core–shell structure of BCDs has a sp2/sp3-hybridized carbon core and a surface rich in functional groups that contain oxygen and nitrogen. This makes them highly bioactive, low in toxicity, and easy to disperse in water [6]. As a result, they can emit light over a wide range of wavelengths, from the visible to the near-infrared (NIR) range. This makes them useful for deep tissue penetration and minimal biological interference, which opens up new possibilities for accurate diagnosis and treatment in biomedicine [5]. Additionally, heteroatoms (like nitrogen and sulfur) and long-chain compounds found in biomass act as self-passivating agents, allowing for simultaneous carbonization and surface functionalization. This not only keeps the biomass source’s pharmacological activity, but it also adds fluorescence properties, which makes the treatment more effective. To keep bioactivity, a “active structure preservation strategy” is used, which involves polymerization at low temperatures before carbonization. This keeps important functional groups in biomolecules like polysaccharides and proteins from breaking down when they get too hot. New functional groups that are made during synthesis may also work together with the original active components to improve performance even more.

Biomass-derived carbon quantum dots (BCDs) have a lot of potential for use in biomedical imaging because they are easy to make in a green way and their fluorescence properties can be changed. In multimodal imaging, BCDs have successfully facilitated fluorescence-photoacoustic dual-modal imaging, enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio by approximately 40% in in vivo tumor models and significantly improving resolution. In particular, single-photon and multi-photon fluorescence imaging methods that use BCDs have been able to label HeLa cells with high contrast and see the edges of brain tumors very clearly [7]. The long-lasting phosphorescence imaging mode raises the signal-to-noise ratio for tumor imaging to 3.5 times that of regular dyes. Photoacoustic imaging, which uses both light and sound to create images, gets around the optical diffusion limit in biological tissues, allowing for high-resolution imaging of deep-seated lesions [8]. The intrinsic biocompatibility of BCDs markedly diminishes immune rejection in vivo, offering solutions for disease diagnosis characterized by substantial penetration depth and signal specificity [9]. Additionally, BCDs demonstrate potential for theranostic applications. For example, fluorescence signals make it possible to monitor the treatment process in real time during imaging-guided photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT). In biosensing, they make it possible to find disease biomarkers with great sensitivity [10]. These features make BCDs the perfect nanoplatform for combining effective imaging and therapeutic functions, opening up a new technological path for precision medicine. As we learn more about how BCDs are made and how they affect living things, these materials are expected to connect old medical knowledge with new nanotechnology, leading to the creation of cheap diagnostic and therapeutic tools [11].

The innovation of this review lies in systematically integrating the diversity of biomass sources, the complex and often co-existing luminescence mechanisms, and the specific performance requirements for multimodal bioimaging applications—including one-photon, two-photon, and photoacoustic imaging—within a unified “precursor structure–performance tuning–application-driven” framework. In contrast to existing reviews primarily focused on synthesis methods, single applications, or specific precursor categories, this work critically analyzes how to exploit the intrinsic properties of biomass for the rational design of carbon dots, thereby addressing the research gap between understanding fundamental mechanisms and developing practical imaging probes.

The sources, structures and properties of BCDs

Biomass-derived carbon quantum dots (BCDs) are nanomaterials that have no dimensions and a core–shell structure made up of graphitic sp2/sp3 hybrid carbon cores and shells rich in functional groups (–COOH, –OH, –NH2) [12, 13]. They are divided into Graphene Quantum Dots(GQDs), Carbon Quantum Dots(CQDs), Carbon Nanodots(CNDs), and Carbonized Polymer Dots(CPDs). They have tunable photoluminescence (300–600 nm), quantum yields of more than 80%, and upconversion luminescence because of quantum confinement and surface state effects. Structural characterization reveals a lattice spacing of 0.21–0.34 nm (HRTEM), distinctive XPS peaks (C–C/C=C, C–O, C=O), and D/G bands in Raman spectra, thereby affirming their graphitic/amorphous hybrid characteristics. Among them, multifunctional bio-carbon dots (BCDs) show great promise for bioimaging because they scatter very little light, use very little energy, and have a high metabolic rate [14].

After nearly two decades of development, the synthesis of carbon quantum dots (CQDs) has matured into a well-established system, primarily comprising two routes: “top-down” and “bottom-up” approaches (Fig. 1) [15–18]. Top-down methods (arc discharge, electrochemical cutting, laser ablation, chemical oxidation [19]) decompose graphitic carbon sources to produce uniform nanocrystals, though they are energy-intensive and require advanced equipment, as demonstrated by Pittman’s team achieving an amine-to-acidic-group ratio of ~ 1.35 through surface modification [20]. On the other hand, bottom-up strategies use small molecules or biomass in hydrothermal or microwave conditions. These conditions keep functional groups intact while allowing for precise functionalization. The best reaction temperatures are between 100 and 200 °C [21], and the duration must be carefully controlled to avoid lowering the quantum yield or increasing the particle size [22]. BCDs have amazing biomedical potential because they can image cells (SNR > 20:1), find tumor biomarkers at femtomolar levels, and deliver drugs (loading efficiency > 15%). These materials come from a variety of biological sources (plants, microorganisms, proteins, sugars, etc.) [23] that are part of natural carbon cycles. They often have heteroatoms (N, P, S) that allow for self-doping during synthesis without any extra changes [19], which makes the surfaces more biocompatible and bioactive. There are still problems with controlling size on a large scale and standardizing characterization, but future work will focus on advanced doping methods and hybrid designs for healthcare, energy, and environmental technologies that can do more than one thing.

Plant-derived biomass carbon dots

Eukaryotic plants

Plant-derived biological carbon quantum dots (Plant-Derived Biological Carbon Dots, BCDs) leverage a sustainable eukaryotic raw material system, classified by taxonomic hierarchy and functional properties into the following categories: Rosales [e.g., orange trees [32] (Rutaceae, Citrus genus, the source of orange juice), roses [33], chrysanthemums[34]]; Asterales [e.g., sunflowers [35], dandelions [36]]; Lamiales [e.g., mint genus [37]]; Poales [e.g., lemongrass[38]]; Ericales [e.g., blue honeysuckle [39]]; Solanales [e.g., black nightshade [40] (Solanaceae, Solanum genus)]; Fagales [e.g., ginkgo nuts[41] (Ginkgoaceae, Ginkgo genus)]; Poales [e.g., bamboo leaves [42], soybean meal, rice husks, and corn cobs]; Rubiales [e.g., goji berries[46] (Rubiaceae, Lycium genus)]; Cucurbitales [e.g., winter melon fruit [47], honeysuckle [39], morning glory petals [48]]; Zingiberales [e.g., turmeric [49] (Zingiberaceae, Curcuma genus)]. Functional small-molecule precursors primarily refer to medicinal plants and fruits/vegetables rich in active components such as polyphenols, flavonoids, and alkaloids (e.g., ginkgo leaves, goji berries, gardenia). Under mild hydrothermal or microwave conditions, these aromatic and heteroatom-rich molecules undergo condensation, polymerization, and partial carbonization, forming carbon dots with a carbon core and a surface enriched with oxygen/nitrogen functional groups. Such carbon dots exhibit both tunable photoluminescent properties and retained (or even enhanced) bioactivities like antioxidant and antimicrobial effects, demonstrating unique advantages in the biomedical field.

Plant-based biological carbon quantum dots (BCDs) are made from materials that naturally take part in the carbon cycle (like photosynthesis). They show great biosafety and photoluminescence properties after being changed by plant components like hydroxyl, polyphenol, and flavonoid groups. Alimin’s team [41] made nitrogen-doped carbon dots from ginkgo leaves. These dots had absorption peaks at 220 nm (π–π transition) and 290 nm (n–π transition), and when they were excited at 350 nm, they gave off blue fluorescence at 435 nm. Fei’s team [42] made carbon dots from bamboo leaves, which had a n–π* transition absorption peak at 370 nm and an optical bandgap of 3.2 eV, proving that they were semiconductors. Xie et al. [46] made self-doped nitrogen carbon quantum dots from Ningxia goji berries (Lycium barbarum) using a one-pot hydrothermal method at 180 °C for 8 h. When excited at 415 nm, the dots gave off fluorescence at 500 nm and showed a big change in behavior depending on the excitation (redshift from 365 to 465 nm). Surface analysis showed that there were a lot of C–OH and C=O functional groups. This made the fluorescence stable in the pH range of 7–13 and made it possible to detect Cr(VI) at very low levels (0.16 μmol/L). Tsai’s team [47] used microwave-assisted extraction (70 W, 5 min) and found that carbonizing gardenia at 160–200 °C greatly improved the properties of the resulting carbon dots (CDs-2). These CDs-2 had a quantum yield of 1.81%, which is 90% higher than the 0.95% for CDs-1 from untreated fruit. They also had a fluorescence lifetime of 3.98 ns and kept 92% of their intensity after 120 min under UV light. The improvements are due to oxygen-rich functional groups (like O=C–OH) that formed during carbonization and made it easier for electrons to move. Their research shows that native plant compounds, like flavonoids and polysaccharides, directly affect the surface of quantum dots, making it possible to change the light they emit and providing a solid base for biomedical uses.

Carbon quantum dots (CQDs) made from agricultural waste like coffee husks, corn straw, and soybean, walnut, and laver show that it is possible to make materials in a way that is better for the environment. Liu’s team [39] made biological carbon quantum dots (BCDs) from Lonicera caerulea L. leaves using a hydrothermal method that only took one step (200 °C, 10 h). The BCDs had an average size of 2.6 nm, a graphitic structure (XRD peak at 28.79°), and hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amino groups on the surface (C/O/N content: 63.23%/33.43%/3.34%). The optical properties showed a 283 nm absorption peak (π–π* transition) and a 450 nm blue emission when excited at 365 nm. This showed that the properties depended on the excitation wavelength and were stable across pH 3–12. Mu et al. [43] synthesized CQDs from corn straw through hydrothermal treatment (220 °C, 5 h), resulting in an average size of 2.28 nm and a graphitic structure (XRD: 22.8°; Raman ID/IG: 0.93). The CQDs showed blue fluorescence (440 nm emission at 360 nm excitation), a quantum yield of 3.8%, and the ability to selectively detect Fe3⁺ (LOD: 5.32 μM). They were also stable when the pH and UV levels changed. Cheng’s team [44] used a hydrothermal method (200–220 °C, 6 h) to make CQDs from soybean, walnut, and laver. The CQDs were about 10 nm in size. Elemental analysis verified the natural doping of magnesium, copper, and iron. The CQDs had blue fluorescence (430 nm emission), changed color depending on the light that hit them (380–500 nm redshift), and stayed stable when stored for a long time (60 days) in high ionic strength (1 M NaCl). Suraj et al. [45] employed slow pyrolysis (500 °C, N₂ atmosphere) to synthesize CQDs from coffee husks, resulting in particles measuring 5.38 nm, characterized by a significant presence of hydroxyl, carbonyl, and amino groups (FTIR/XPS). These CQDs showed green fluorescence (522 nm emission at 450 nm excitation), emission that depended on excitation (370–500 nm redshift), and stability across pH 3–12, as shown by a zeta potential of − 61.4 mV. Together, plant components that are already in the plant (like cellulose and lignin) help with surface functionalization, which allows for tunable luminescence (blue/green emission, excitation-dependent shifts) and strong stability. Their natural origin and non-toxic makeup show that they are biocompatible, which could make them useful in medicine.

BCDs derived from traditional Chinese medicine, such as zingiberales turmeric-based E-BCDs [49], are high in flavonoids and alkaloids and have antibacterial and antiviral effects. Rai’s team [90] showed that they could be useful for bioimaging and delivering drugs. Improving synthesis (for example, fagales ginkgo-based N-CQDs achieving optimal photoluminescence at 200 °C [50]) makes it easier for cells to take up the material, and defect-state structures make it possible to change the luminescence. For example, LGH-BCDs [38] made from lemongrass in the lamiaceae family show upconversion luminescence with low toxicity, which means they can penetrate deeply and cause little damage to tissue. The near-infrared afterglow carbon dots (CNDs) derived from rosales black nightshade [40] have a lifetime of 5.9 h (tissue penetration depth of 20 mm) through photo-oxidation. They combine a high signal-to-noise ratio with cancer targeting to improve tumor resection surgery in mice.

Eukaryotic algae

Eukaryotic algae, such as Euglenophyta, Chlorophyta, Rhodophyta, Phaeophyta [51], and Charophyta [52], are significant sources for biomass-derived carbon quantum dots (BCDs). First, as part of the carbon cycle, they provide unique metabolic pathways (synthesizing polysaccharides, pigments, and polyphenols) that make natural precursors for BCDs. Second, their metabolic products (carbohydrates, pigments, polyphenols, vitamins) [53] can be turned into stable, low-toxicity quantum dots through green synthesis, which opens up a lot of possible uses.

Carbon quantum dots (CDs) are a new type of nanomaterial that have a lot of potential uses in biomedicine and environmental monitoring because they are very fluorescent, not very toxic, and compatible with living things. Li’s team [54] used microalgae powder as the starting material and carbonized it with formaldehyde under hydrothermal conditions (180 °C for 10 h) to speed up the process. Following centrifugation, carbon quantum dots (MCQDs) were acquired. The MCQDs are 1–8 nm in size and have a quantum yield of 4.3%. When excited at 360 nm, they emit blue light at 435 nm and change color from red to blue when the excitation wavelength changes from 350 to 625 nm. MCQDs were utilized for two-photon imaging of MCF-7 cells, achieving a cell survival rate of 86% at a concentration of 800 μg/mL, underscoring their significance in bioimaging. Ankit Kumar Singh’s team [55] synthesized nitrogen-phosphorus-doped carbon dots (A-NPCDs) from Dunaliella salina as raw material in a single step using a hydrothermal method at 200 °C for 3 h, without any chemical additives. The A-NPCDs are 2–8 nm in size and are made up of C (52.2%), O (42.9%), N (2.6%), and P doping. They have a quantum yield of 8% and emit blue light at 415 nm when excited at 340 nm. They also show excitation wavelength dependence (redshift from 350 to 400 nm) and good pH stability (3–11). They can detect Hg(II) and Cr(VI) at levels as low as 0.018 μM. They were used to take fluorescence images of HEK-293 cells, which were still alive at a concentration of 75 μg/mL. The sensing mechanism works by using inner filter effects and electron transfer. This shows the benefits of using plant-based carbon dots in biomedicine and environmental monitoring. Shao’s team [56] made carbon dots using a microwave-assisted method and made ratiometric fluorescent probes (PC-CDs) by linking them to phycocyanin (PC) through EDC/NHS coupling agents. The probes have two emission peaks (450 nm and 645 nm) and are very selective for ONOO⁻ when excited at 365 nm. They can detect concentrations as low as 0.5 μM and as high as 100 μM, and they stay stable in pH 7.4 buffer solution. They have low cytotoxicity and were successfully used for fluorescence imaging of HEK-293 cells, achieving intracellular ONOO⁻ ratio detection; the mechanism involves both dynamic and static quenching effects, providing a high-precision tool for biosensing of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in pathological environments such as cancer and neurodegenerative diseases. Deming Dong’s team [91] developed a green hydrothermal method using Chlorella biomass as a precursor to synthesize carbonized polymer dots (c-CPDs) with excitation wavelength-dependent luminescence properties. They saw a possible fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) effect between c-CPDs and chlorophyll in living algal cells. This caused the living algae to glow red, while the dead algae glowed in the colors of c-CPDs themselves because chlorophyll was no longer active. This made it easy to tell the difference between living and dead microalgae. Xiangyan Chen’s team [92] used carrageenan oligosaccharides (CAO) from marine red algae as both reducing and stabilizing agents to make CAO-capped gold nanoparticles (CAO-AuNPs). These nanoparticles were filled with the chemotherapy drug epirubicin (EPI) to make a smart drug delivery system that responds to changes in pH. Shuyu Liu’s team [93] came up with a new way to use carbon quantum dots (CQDs) as fluorescent probes, using an EDC/NHS-mediated covalent coupling strategy to keep an eye on how the metabolic changes of reduced glutathione (GSH), phycocyanin, and superoxide dismutase (SOD) in Microcystis aeruginosa change as it grows (7–95 days). Their research demonstrated the pivotal role of GSH metabolism in microalgal cells: it not only displayed robust synergistic correlations with phycocyanin (R2 = 0.9895) and SOD (R2 = 0.9669), but also preserved the cellular antioxidant defense system through interconnected physiological processes, including GSH/GSSG interconversion, phytochelatin (PC) synthesis, and the ASA-GSH cycle.

Eukaryotic algae have advantages for monitoring the environment, producing energy, farming, and medicine. Subsequent investigations ought to concentrate on enhancing near-infrared carbon dots, AI-facilitated synthesis, and biocompatibility assessments to propel clinical applications.

Based on the content of Sect. 1.1, “Plant-Derived Biomass Carbon Dots,” it is evident that the properties of plant-derived carbon dots are closely related to the chemical composition of their precursors. Medicinal plants rich in polyphenols and flavonoids tend to form carbon dots with oxygen-rich surfaces that retain their pharmacological activity, achieving quantum yields of 5–25%. In contrast, agricultural wastes, primarily composed of cellulose and lignin, generally exhibit lower quantum yields but offer advantages in cost-effectiveness and scalability, making them more suitable for environmental remediation. A key insight is that moderate carbonization temperatures help balance the crystallinity of the carbon core with the retention of surface functional groups, thereby optimizing luminescence performance. Current challenges primarily focus on achieving efficient and tunable emission from the visible to the near-infrared region, as well as overcoming the issue of batch-to-batch inconsistency arising from the inherent complexity of biomass precursors.

Animal-derived biomass carbon dots

In recent years, carbon dots made from biomass from animals have gotten a lot of attention because they come from a lot of different sources, are easy to make, and have better properties. Studies have looked at a wide range of animal precursors, such as animal tissues, secretions, and waste products.

Using insect and marine biological waste like silkworm cocoons, shrimp shells, crab shells, and fish scales, several research teams have made high-performance carbon dots that can be used in medicine and as catalysts. Marzieh Tahmasbi’s team [57] used a multi-step green synthesis method with natural silkworm cocoons. This involved boiling the cocoons, carbonizing them at 500 °C and activating them with KOH at 800 °C to make a porous structure, purifying them with HCl, loading them with Fe₃O₄ magnetic material, and adsorbing copper with ultrasonic waves to make a magnetic nitrogen-doped carbon-supported copper catalyst (MNC-Cu). FT-IR, XRD, and SEM tests showed that it had a uniform structure and magnetic properties. This is very important for drug development, like for antibiotics and antihypertensive drugs. Tian’s team [72] made carbon quantum dots from silkworm cocoons by pyrolysis, which made hydroxyl/carboxyl groups on the surface of the carbonized silk proteins. The luminescence mechanism uses quantum confinement in sp2-carbon cores and surface-state transitions (n–π/π–π) to work. You can change the luminescence by changing the composition of the precursors and the temperature at which they are pyrolyzed. Higher temperatures make the crystals more crystalline, which changes the emission wavelengths, and functional groups control the quantum yield and pH response. These dots have anti-inflammatory properties and may be useful for fluorescence imaging. P.E. Chen’s group [61] made N-doped carbon dots from shrimp shell waste using the hydrothermal method (190 °C, 16 h, 6.5% yield). The N-CDs had an absorption/emission range of 530/555 nm and a quantum yield of 33.2%. Their fluorescence changed with pH, getting stronger at pH 2 and weaker at pH 13. Through a combination of internal filtration and photoinduced electron transfer, Fe3⁺ detection reached a sensitivity of 0.66 μM and was very selective against 16 cations and 13 anions. The material showed great photostability (60 min of UV), thermal stability (20–90 °C), and antioxidant properties, making it perfect for multi-mode sensing. Yao’s team [62] made magneto-fluorescent N-doped carbon quantum dots from crab shell waste using a microwave-assisted hydrothermal method. The CQDs made from chitin as a carbon source and chelator had a uniform size (4.0–4.5 nm), a quantum yield of 33.2%, and magnetic properties that could be changed for T₁/T₂-weighted MRI. They also showed pH-responsive luminescence, the ability to sense Fe3⁺, and excellent photostability and biocompatibility, making them a flexible platform for multimodal biomedical applications. Jacopo Segato’s team [94] made carbon dots (CDs) from sea bass scales using hydrothermal synthesis (200 °C, 24 h, 32% yield) and added them to gelatin films to make them better at blocking UV light. Adding 5% Polysorbate-20 (PS) improved the dispersion of CDs, keeping the particle size between 5 and 10 nm and lowering the transmittance to 3% at 275 nm. This synergy, which was caused by PS hydroxyl interactions with CDs and gelatin, made it possible to change the level of UV protection while keeping transparency. The study presents an innovative method for repurposing fishery waste into wearable protective materials. Wu’s team [95] made blue fluorescent carbon dots (SS-CDs) from shrimp shell waste using the hydrothermal method. These CDs have emission that can be changed by changing the excitation wavelength from π–π (235 nm) and n–π (335 nm) transitions. They allow for two types of detection: at pH 9.0, Co2⁺ binding stops fluorescence (range 10–600 μM, limit 1.68 μM); at pH 3.8, Fe3⁺ doping gives TMB chromogenesis peroxidase-like activity, which lets glucose be detected (limit 6.02 μM). Tuning hydrothermal parameters makes it possible to customize and expand analytical uses by using a dual-mode mechanism.

Zhao’s team [58] created a green way to make carbon quantum dots (EYO CDs) from egg yolk oil. They used chicken feathers, bones, and egg yolk and white as starting materials. The focus was on environmental monitoring and sustainable synthesis. By heating yolk powder to 260 °C and then extracting and purifying it with water, spherical CDs with C/O/Fe were made. The CDs were less than 10 nm in size. These dots absorbed light at 270 nm (π–π* transition) and emitted light at 420 nm when excited at 365 nm, with a quantum yield of 5.01%. FTIR verified the presence of O–H (3364 cm−1) and COO− (1635/1400 cm−1) groups. In mice, a dosage of 223 mg/kg decreased bleeding time from 8.38 to 2.07 min (P < 0.01) through the activation of the intrinsic coagulation pathway (APTT↓, FIB/PLT↑, P < 0.05). Sohel Das’s team [66] made 3.4–4.09 nm metal-doped carbon quantum dots (MgCQD/ZnCQD) from waste chicken feathers using a microwave-assisted hydrothermal method (350 °C, pH 11, 900W/6 min). The best performance was seen with ZnCQD, which had a bandgap of 2.71 eV and a quantum yield of 10.34%. The pH-dependent dual-mode sensing mechanism allows for the detection of 4-NP at pH 2 through internal filtering (over 90% quenching) and Hg2⁺ at pH 7 through dynamic quenching (52–70% efficiency). This has been confirmed by fluorescence lifetime and FTIR, making it an environmentally friendly way to monitor. Ye’s team [59] made 3.2 nm carbon quantum dots from old chicken bones using hydrothermal methods (180 °C, 4 h). The CQDs with –OH/C=O groups emitted light at 465 nm when they were excited at 380 nm. They stayed stable at pH 2–6 and ionic strength 0.25–2 mol/L, with the highest intensity at pH 7. A method for finding detergents was made using SDBS-enhanced fluorescence (LOD: 0.17 μg/mL, recovery: 85.2–104.2%). The material is an eco-friendly fluorescent probe for environmental monitoring because it has a cell viability of more than 95% at 20 μg/mL. Cardoso-Avila’s team [60] made carbon quantum dots (CDs) from chicken egg white powder using an alkali-catalyzed method. This involved mixing the powder with hydroxides (KOH/LiOH/NaOH), ethanol, and glutaraldehyde, then shaking it for six hours at room temperature without cleaning it. There is very little use of solvents and no energy input in the process. CDs absorb light at 530 nm and give off light at 555 nm. At higher concentrations, the quantum yield (QY) drops below 10%. It is highest at 25.6% (KOH, 0.3 M), 23.3% (LiOH, 0.2 M), and 24.2% (NaOH, 0.2 M). FTIR shows that there are –OH/–COOH groups on the surface, and the Zeta potential is less than − 30 mV, which means the surface is stable. This study shows that food-grade precursors can be used to make high-performance CDs using green synthesis that lasts.

Biomass from mammals (like hair, skin, and milk) can be turned into carbon quantum dots with adjustable optical properties and great biocompatibility through green synthesis. This shows that they have a lot of potential for use in bioimaging, sensing, and medicine. Sun’s team [65] made luminescent-tunable sulfur-nitrogen co-doped carbon quantum dots from hair fibers using one-step sulfuric acid carbonization (40–140 °C, 24 h). The best results were at 140 °C, which gave the smallest size (2–5 nm) and the highest quantum yield (29%). Increasing the temperature from 40 to 140 °C made the particles smaller, from 7.5 to 3.1 nm, and shifted the emission peaks from 383 to 470 nm. XPS/FTIR analyses pinpointed significant dopants (thiophene-S, pyridinic nitrogen) that influenced luminescence. The material showed that it could emit light depending on how excited it was, upconvert light, and stay stable in a pH range of 1–13. This shows that it could be useful for bioimaging and sensing. Zhang’s team [71] made carbon quantum dots (HCDs/SCDs) from hair and skin precursors by heating them to 300 °C and passing microwaves through them. The result was 2–6 nm particles with a lattice spacing of 0.267 nm that contained C/O/Fe elements. Optical characterization showed that HCDs absorbed light at 270 nm and emitted light at 420 nm, with quantum yields of 86.06% and 51.35%, respectively. In zebrafish models, the CDs exhibited favorable biocompatibility at concentrations ranging from 0.2 to 1.6 mg/mL. Furthermore, a dosage of 223 mg/kg significantly decreased bleeding time from 8.38 to 2.07 min (P < 0.01) by modulating APTT, FIB, and PLT levels to activate coagulation pathways. The CDs also made it possible to detect ATP/NADH/Fe3⁺ in a linear range of 0–100 μM, which could be useful for bioimaging and sensing. Shabbir’s team [73] made carbon quantum dots using skim milk as a precursor through a microwave-hydrothermal method (190–260 °C, 1–8 h), which produced uniform 2–3 nm particles. The material had absorption and emission peaks at 280 and 420 nm, and the best quantum yield was found in samples that had been made for 3 h. The CDs showed that they could change their behavior based on pH (they got stronger at pH 2 and weaker at pH 13) and that they could detect Fe3⁺ (with a detection limit of 0.66 μM). Toxicity tests showed that longer synthesis times made the material much more biocompatible (cell viability went from less than 20% at 4 mg/mL to more than 20%). This study found a way to balance optical performance and biosafety by changing the synthesis parameters. This is a new way to use biosensing.

Biochar carbon dots derived from amino acids, peptides and proteins

Amino acids, peptides, and proteins are great building blocks for making biomass carbon dots (BCDs) that are very biocompatible and have great optical properties. Vijay Bhooshan Kumar’s team [64] made N-doped carbon dots from 20 amino acids using a one-step hydrothermal method at 200 °C for 6 to 10 h. The CDs had a size range of 3 to 9 nm, a lattice spacing of 0.21 nm, and a yield of 29%. XPS verified pyridinic/pyrrolic N-doping, lowering the Arg-CQDs bandgap to 2.1 eV and producing light in the 440–740 nm range. The quantum yield was 86% (Arg-CQDs), and the luminescence depended on the excitation wavelength (e.g., His-CQDs: 450 → 600 nm). Arg-CQDs achieved a record refractive index of 2.15 and a fluorescence lifetime of 7.99 ns, showing that they are great for bioimaging. Song’s research [80] made chiral BCDs from D-/L-cysteine, showing that D-BCDs have better antibacterial activity against Gram-positive bacteria and fungi through a triple mechanism, which greatly speeds up wound healing in animal models. Andrés Ferrer-Ruiz’s research [67] demonstrated that microwave pyrolysis of L-glutamic acid yields carbon dots with adjustable graphitization (enhanced above 160 °C, peaking > 200 °C), where annealing causes emission red-shift while decreasing quantum yield. Liu et al. [96] created S/N-co-doped orange-red CDs (SNBCDs) with a 147 nm Stokes shift to find GSH in the Golgi apparatus (linear range: 10–460 μM, LOD: 0.25 μM). Tao et al. [97] produced hair-derived blue CDs (B-BCDs, QY: 38%) for CLZ detection (LOD: 67 ng/mL), demonstrating low cytotoxicity and superior accuracy compared to HPLC. Zhang’s study [68] showed that BCDs with multi-cyclic dipeptide structures work better as catalysts than natural hydrolases, allowing membrane catalysts to instantly hydrolyze esters. Even though BCDs made from amino acids are more expensive to make, they work better against bacteria because they can interact with them. Shao Huang’s group [85] made dextran-guanidinylated CDs (DG-CD) that had a strong positive charge and a MIC of 5 μg/mL against MRSA. These CDs stopped biofilm formation and encouraged macrophage M2 polarization to speed up keratitis healing in mice.

Biological carbon dots from other sources

Biomass carbon dots (BCDs) can be made from more than just plants, animals, and protein sources. They can also be made from fungi, bacteria, carbohydrates, ionic liquids, and nucleic acids [86]. Phototrophic bacterial carbon dot [83] imaging technology combines phototrophic bacteria with CQDs to make it possible to take high-resolution, real-time pictures of living things. This method improves photoluminescence by taking advantage of the unique photosystems of phototrophic bacteria and carbon dots working together. Puja Kumari [82] devised a hydrothermal technique utilizing fungal biomass to synthesize carbon nanodots (CNDs) for the sensitive detection of food adulterants, exhibiting favorable biocompatibility and imaging proficiency in HEK 293 cells. Buddhadev Mukherjee [76] made BCDs from sugar molecules, which had a precision of 12–28 nm and a resolution of 54–62 nm in SMLM imaging. BCDs made from glucose reached a resolution of 61 nm for super-resolution applications. Wang’s team [83] made red-emitting carbon quantum dots (BCDs) from phototrophic bacteria using a microwave-assisted hydrothermal method. The BCDs we got are very soluble in water (quantum yield: 15.17%) and very biocompatible, which lets us take stable cellular images for more than an hour and label specific lysosomes. This study presents an innovative approach for the development of long-wavelength fluorescent probes. Zhang’s team [84] used a hydrothermal method to make very small spirulina-derived carbon dots (SpiCDs). The dots were quickly excreted by the kidneys, as shown by red fluorescence in urine. These SpiCDs offer a visualization-based approach for the early diagnosis of acute kidney injury (MCKI). Wu’s study [78] found that CDs made from chitosan and NaOH give off blue-green light when they are hit by light. Chitosan gives the CDs functional groups, and NaOH makes the light brighter and less likely to be quenched. These CDs also allow for 623-ms RTP in PVA/boric acid systems and increase graphene capacitance by 184%, making them useful for security, sensing, and energy storage. Gao’s team [87] made VOIMBr-BCDs that keep the structures of ionic liquids. These compounds are better at fighting MRSA than vancomycin and help wounds heal in mice by making ROS and using electrostatic interactions. Liu’s study [88] created “polarity adaptive carbon dots” (pad-CDs) using phenylenediamine and imidazole ionic liquids. These dots change their surface dipole to match the polarity of the solvent, which helps them spread evenly in different media and creates intramolecular charge transfer states. Sara Zarrabi [89] showed that dsRNA nanocomposites with CDs or mesoporous silica nanoparticles can lower the viral titers of TuMV and BCTV in tobacco plants. This makes dsRNA delivery more effective for better viral control. These BCDs become more biocompatible and effective for imaging and detecting bacteria when they use bacterial or metabolically relevant biomass as their carbon source. One-step microwave-assisted carbonization can make near-infrared carbon quantum dots (NIR-BCDs), which can speed up electron transfer and turn on important genes [98]. These dots can be used to deliver drugs and treat cancer.

The synthesis method of biomass carbon dots

After almost twenty years of work, the synthesis of carbon quantum dots (CQDs) has become a well-established system with two main methods: “top-down” and “bottom-up.” Top-down methods (arc discharge, electrochemical cutting, laser ablation, chemical oxidation [18]) break down graphitic carbon sources to make uniform nanocrystals, but they are energy-intensive and need advanced equipment. For example, Pittman’s team [20] used surface modification to get an amine-to-acidic-group ratio of about 1.35. Bottom-up strategies, on the other hand, use small molecules or biomass in hydrothermal or microwave conditions. This keeps functional groups intact while allowing for precise functionalization. The best reaction temperatures are between 100 and 200 °C, and the duration must be carefully controlled to avoid a lower quantum yield or larger particle size [50].

Different carbon dots made from biomass have different functions because they were made using different synthesis methods. Carbon dots derived from Atractylodes macrocephala [99] were synthesized using a hydrothermal method and demonstrate a triple synergistic therapeutic mechanism: efficient scavenging of reactive oxygen species (removal rate > 95%), significant inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α/IL-6/IL-1β), and restoration of the intestinal barrier by enhancing the expression of mucins and tight junction proteins. In a colitis model, this material exhibited enhanced therapeutic efficacy relative to 5‑ASA by modulating the PI3K‑Akt/Jak‑STAT/TGF‑β/MAPK signaling pathways, while also demonstrating colon-targeting specificity and favorable biosafety (Fig. 3A). The flowers of the selfheal and pagoda trees [99] produce selenium/metal co-doped carbon dots using a one-step hydrothermal method. These dots have antioxidant properties and can be used for cellular imaging (Fig. 3B). Orange peel waste [100] is turned into carboxyl-rich carbon dots through electrochemical processing. These dots can be used as metal-free recyclable catalysts (Fig. 3C). The solvothermal method makes boron-doped carbon dots from herbal waste [101]. These dots can only detect Fe3⁺. The microwave method makes ginger carbon dots that help wounds heal by moving fibroblasts (Fig. 3D). Using a microwave-assisted hydrothermal method, carbon dots made from ginger were quickly made. These carbon dots enhance fibroblast migration and angiogenesis, indicating beneficial therapeutic effects in wound healing applications. This method gives us new ideas for tissue engineering and regenerative medicine (Fig. 3E). Papaya seeds [102] make ultrasensitive Fe3⁺ probes (detection limit: 2.35 μM) in just 90 s (Fig. 3F). Thermal degradation of apple pomace, rapeseed residue, and potato peel [103] produces carbon dots with superior sensing properties(Fig. 3G). Pyrolytic conversion of Platycodon residues [104] produces N,S-co-doped porous carbons suitable for drug delivery and tissue engineering scaffolds (Fig. 3H). Luminescence mechanisms and structural design of BCDs

Biomass-derived carbon quantum dots (BCDs) exhibit exceptional optical properties and biocompatibility, enabling broad applications in biomedicine, environmental monitoring, and photocatalysis [107]. The luminescence mechanisms of biomass-derived carbon quantum dots (BCDs) involve complex and synergistic contributions from carbon-core states, surface states, molecular states, and crosslinking-enhanced emission (CEE).Carbon-core state luminescence originates from electron transitions from the ground state (S₀) to excited singlet states (S1…Sₙ), followed by vibrational relaxation to S₁ and radiative decay back to S₀ (S₀ → S₁ → S₀), resulting in photon emission [108]. The emission is classified as single-photon or multi-photon photoluminescence based on the quantity of absorbed photons. When the singlet–triplet energy gap (ΔEST) is small (< 0.3 eV), delayed photoluminescence or phosphorescence can happen through intersystem crossing (ISC) and reverse ISC (RISC) processes [109–111]. The emission intensity is closely related to the amount of sp2-conjugated domains in the precursor. Biomass with complex carbon frameworks (like polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon segments) makes it easier for large sp2-conjugated systems to form, which in turn increases near-infrared (NIR) emission. Surface-state luminescence is linked to surface functional groups (like –OH and –COOH) and defect states [112]. For instance, adding oxygen-rich passivating agents (like PEG or FA) to BCQD surfaces causes energy levels to split, which makes Stokes shifts big and emission move into the NIR range. Molecular-state luminescence comes from fluorescent molecules that are made during precursor carbonization by crosslinking reactions (like stable luminescent intermediates that are made during biomass pyrolysis). These emitting units, which are either embedded in or attached to the carbon core, directly affect the NIR luminescence properties [113]. Crosslinking-enhanced emission (CEE) improves radiative decay efficiency by limiting the vibrational and rotational motions of fluorophores, especially in BCDs that don’t have long conjugated structures. These mechanisms don’t work on their own; they are controlled by the biomass precursor’s structural features (like the complexity of the carbon skeleton and the amount of heteroatoms), which lets us fine-tune the optical properties of BCDs and gives us a theoretical basis for their targeted biomedical uses [114]. Core-state luminescence

Core-state luminescence comes from the unique sp2-hybridized carbon core structure of biomass-derived carbon quantum dots (BCDs). It creates photoluminescence through π → π* electronic transitions (with a characteristic signal at 285.3 eV), and its luminescence performance is mostly determined by the size and stability of the carbon core’s conjugated domains. Gao’s team [120] found that softwood biomass (like Pinus sylvestris), which has a lot of G-type lignin, helps make a longer conjugated aromatic structure. This greatly increases the fluorescence quantum yield (FQY) of carbon quantum dots (CQDs). The conjugated domains get bigger, which makes π-electron delocalization stronger, lowers the bandgap, and makes the luminescence redder. XPS analysis of the C1s spectrum of the carbon core showed a characteristic peak of sp2-hybridized carbon (C=C bonds) at 284.8 eV. The relative amount of sp2 carbon went up as the hydrothermal reaction time went from 2 to 8 h. At the same time, the fluorescence intensity went up, reaching a maximum of 3.4%. This confirms that the sp2-hybridized carbon core is what makes things glow. The combination of FTIR and XPS analysis showed that longer processing times make the C=C bond vibration signals at 1594 cm⁻1 and 1515 cm−1 stronger. This means that the conjugated domains of the carbon core are still expanding and getting better organized. Li’s group [121] made FeN/GQDs using a solvothermal method (180 °C, 9 h) and pineapple leaf extract as the carbon source. XPS showed that the carbon core was sp2-hybridized (C1s peak at 284.8 eV), which means that π-electrons were spread out. Iron incorporation via Fe–N coordination and nitrogen doping (pyridinic/graphitic N) altered electron density, increased conjugated domains, and decreased the bandgap. This caused near-infrared fluorescence (emission at 750 nm) with a 150 nm Stokes shift, which proved that carbon-core transitions were happening. The material’s fluorescence stayed stable for 30 days because functional groups (C=O at 1640 cm⁻1 and others at 1380 cm⁻1) stabilized the structure through hydrogen bonding and coordination, which stopped quenching. When used to find glucose, it had a detection limit of 0.36 μM, which could help manage diabetes.

These results not only help us understand how luminescence works better, but they also give us effective and long-lasting material solutions for biomedical diagnostics, environmental monitoring, and energy conversion. Future endeavors must concentrate on refining synthesis methodologies and clarifying fundamental mechanisms to enhance clinical and industrial applications.

Surface-state luminescence

The molecular structure, functional groups, and defect states on the surface of BCDs cause surface-state luminescence. It has a big effect on their optical properties through surface modification, doping, and defect engineering, and it is a key part of near-infrared emission. This mechanism is directly related to the functional groups and surface defects that are attached to the carbon skeleton. The level of surface passivation is very important for luminescence performance. Increasing surface passivation lowers the number of non-radiative recombination centers, which greatly improves the efficiency of photoluminescence. Adding new functional groups makes more energy gaps, which changes how electrons are distributed and encourages photons to be emitted. These energy gaps not only make the Stokes shifts bigger (moving the emission spectrum to longer wavelengths), but they also let you control the emission wavelength redshift even more with oxygen-rich passivators like PEG, FA, and PEI. For example, carbon dots made from sucrose and passivated with PEG, FA, or PEI show emission peaks that are redshifted from 450 nm to 504, 520, and 534 nm, respectively. The redshift magnitude follows the order PEG-SCDs > FA-SCDs > PEI-SCDs [122].

These near-infrared emission properties—low photon loss and high biocompatibility—make BCDs very promising for biosensing and bioimaging uses. Researchers can improve the luminescent properties of BCDs by carefully designing and controlling the functional groups on the surface. This gives them very flexible optical performance [123–126]. Core-state luminescence comes from the quantum confinement effect in sp2 carbon domains, which shows up as π → π* transitions and short-wavelength blue/green emission. These transitions can be seen in XPS spectra at about 285.3 eV (C=C/C–C) and a distinct G-band in Raman spectra. Surface-state luminescence, on the other hand, is mostly caused by defect-induced trap states, which are energy levels that functional groups like C=O, –COOH, and –NH2 introduce into the bandgap. When electrons and holes recombine at these sites, they emit light that is redshifted and very sensitive to the local environment, such as pH and ionic species. Recent progress in single-particle spectroscopy has been crucial in disentangling these contributions, uncovering heterogeneous emission profiles within individual samples that are typically obscured in ensemble measurements. For instance, Gao Wenhua’s team showed that L-CQDs made from different lignin sources have very different optical properties. L-CQDs made from C-lignin (which has a unique linear structure and many catechol functional groups) tend to have more surface state defects, which makes the red-shifted emission more noticeable and the material more sensitive to changes in the environment. Conversely, L-CQDs sourced from G/S-type lignin, characterized by a more cross-linked network structure, exhibit more advanced sp2 carbon cores, demonstrating enhanced core-state luminescence properties [127].

In summary, surface-state luminescence presents a complex mechanism of “defect modulation–energy gap optimization–environmental response” that not only facilitates precise methods for adjusting the emission wavelength and efficiency of BCDs but also creates new opportunities for applications necessitating highly specific optical signals, including biomedical precision detection and environmentally responsive sensing. It also provides theoretical support for differentiating between core-state and surface-state luminescence contributions and comprehending the heterogeneity of carbon dots [127].

Molecular-state luminescence

Molecular-state luminescence is the light that fluorescent molecules or structures emit when they are embedded in or attached to the surface of BCDs. Their unique electron transition mechanisms interacting with the band structure of BCDs make them an important way to improve the optical properties and increase the number of ways BCDs can be used. These molecules, which are usually intermediates from precursor crosslinking reactions, not only change the way photons are emitted and absorbed when they bind to the surface of a carbon quantum dot, but they also change the band structure of the carbon dots to make new emission energy levels. This increases the frequency of electron transitions, which greatly increases the intensity and efficiency of photoluminescence [128, 129].

This fluorescence mechanism based on structure lets you change the emission wavelength of carbon dots made from soybeans to make them better for biomedical imaging, especially in the near-infrared range where they can get through tissue best and autofluorescence is lowest. Li’s team [130] discovered that carbon dots produced hydrothermally at 150–220 °C from soybean protein isolate, soybean milk powder, or whole soybeans as carbon sources exhibit a regular lattice structure (fringe spacing ~ 0.21 nm) and elevated nitrogen content. These carbon dots have a fluorescence emission peak around 550 nm, behave differently when excited between 340 and 540 nm, and have a maximum quantum yield of 8.7%, which is much higher than the 2.2% yield of cellulose-derived carbon dots. This superior performance is due to the fact that the amino acid backbone stays intact during the carbonization of soybean protein. This allows nitrogen to self-dop and creates a luminescent center with a large sp2 domain and a well-ordered carbon core, which is an example of the “molecular state fluorescence” mechanism.

Molecular-state luminescence is a new way to improve the photoluminescence performance of BCDs by using “band structure regulation–near-infrared enhancement–functional synergy.” This shows that it has a lot of potential uses in the fields of nanomaterial design and biosensing [131].

Cross-linking enhanced emission

Cross-Linking Enhanced Emission (CEE) is the main way to fine-tune the photophysical properties of Biomass-derived Carbon Quantum Dots (BCDs) using chemical cross-linking methods. By using the molecular-scale confinement effect of a three-dimensional network structure, it greatly improves photoluminescence performance and broadens functional applications. The main idea is that the three-dimensional network made by cross-linking agents not only keeps the material structure stable, but it also limits the movement of fluorophores (which makes it harder for molecules to vibrate and rotate), which keeps the excited state stable and makes it last longer. At the same time, it stops energy loss that doesn’t involve radiation (like energy loss through molecular vibration and rotation), improves electron transition pathways, and thus boosts the efficiency of radiative recombination and the brightness of photoluminescence [132].

In systems derived from biomass, the hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amine groups prevalent in precursors readily establish intra- and intermolecular cross-linked networks, primarily governed by esterification and amidation under hydrothermal conditions. These networks have physical cross-linking structures, covalent cross-linking frameworks, coupling of luminescent centers, and intraparticle hydrogen bonds. The rigidification of the molecular structure further constrains the dynamic behavior of surface fluorophores, minimizes non-radiative decay pathways, and significantly enhances radiative recombination efficiency. For instance, the team of Tao Suyong and Yang Bai [118] has shown that the “Crosslink-Enhanced Emission” mechanism in carbonized polymer dots works because crosslinking creates a spatial confinement effect that affects the luminescence performance. The sample with the most crosslinking density, CPDsCH3-1, has the most ordered carbon core, the longest room-temperature phosphorescence lifetime (902.99 ms), and the best luminescence properties. On the other hand, the sample with a lot of methyl (CPDsCH3-3) has almost no phosphorescence because of more steric hindrance and less crosslinking density. The team systematically controlled the internal crosslinking density and steric hindrance in the CPDs by carefully controlling the methyl ratio in the precursors, which were copolymers of acrylic acid and methacrylic acid. Theoretical calculations and spectroscopy show that the confinement environment is what really affects the luminescence properties by changing the overlap of electron clouds, the splitting of energy levels, and the chance of intersystem crossing. This finding goes beyond what was previously known about “fixing fluorophores” and gives us a new way to change how carbon dots work.

The CEE effect works even better when it works with the molecular-state luminescence mechanism. When polycyclic aromatic compounds or heterocyclic molecules (like flavonoid or alkaloid derivatives that are rich in medicinal herbs) are kept in biomass precursors and covalently attached to the carbon core during carbonization, the optical properties of these molecular fluorophores (like large Stokes shifts and excitation-dependent emission) make the cross-linking-enhanced luminescence effect even stronger [133].

Researchers can precisely adjust the emission wavelength, quantum yield, and stability of BCDs by designing the cross-linking network. This includes controlling the ratio of covalent and non-covalent cross-linking and the placement of functional groups. This means that they improve the clarity and sensitivity of bioimaging and build highly selective ratiometric detection platforms in the sensing field [134]. Ji Yibing’s team [135] made blue/red dual-emissive carbonized polymer dots (CPDs) for biosensing applications. They used the cross-linking enhanced emission (CEE) mechanism. These CPDs have the best of both worlds: they have the benefits of regular carbon dots and a new polymer-derived luminescence mechanism. In this mechanism, sub-fluorophores (like C=O and C = N) show more fluorescence when they cross-link or aggregate. Using sodium alginate and TCPP precursors, hydrothermal synthesis produced CPDs that emitted blue light (around 450 nm) from cross-linked polymer structures and red light (around 650 nm) from TCPP’s macrocyclic conjugation. The research illustrates CEE-directed material design via a ratiometric sensing platform that integrates ALP/BChE enzymatic reactions with Cu2⁺-mediated fluorescence response, facilitating the sensitive identification of liver disease biomarkers and promoting the advancement of functional carbon dot development for biomedical applications.

In short, CEE has become an important way to improve the optical properties of BCDs because it works in many different ways, such as “structural confinement, radiative enhancement, molecular synergy, and functional tuning.” It has a lot of uses in biomedical detection and nanomaterial design, like sensing disease biomarkers and imaging with high clarity [136].

Although existing luminescence mechanism theories provide a foundation for understanding BCDs, these mechanisms often coexist and couple in practical systems, and a unified theoretical model for accurately predicting their luminescent behavior is still lacking. The absence of such a theoretical framework hinders the transition from empirical exploration to rational design. The key to future breakthroughs lies in integrating first-principles calculations with advanced in situ spectroscopic techniques (such as time-resolved spectroscopy and single-particle spectroscopy) to elucidate the evolution of luminescence centers at the atomic/molecular level, thereby constructing predictive models capable of quantitatively linking “precursor structure–synthesis conditions–luminescent properties.

Optical tuning

BCDs have very adjustable optical and structural properties [137]. They have strong visible photoluminescence that is good for bioimaging and sensing, and their emission can be changed by changing the synthesis parameters (temperature, time, and precursor) [138]. They are almost spherical (2–10 nm) in shape, have a uniform size, and are very stable. They have an amorphous carbon core with sp2 domains (for optoelectronic performance) and sp3 carbons (for structural stability) [86]. Surface groups like –OH, –COOH, and –NH₂ make things more hydrophilic, easier to spread out, and allow for specific biomolecular interactions that help with sensing, targeted delivery, and lower immunogenicity [139]. Efficient synthesis methods (like microwave and hydrothermal) make sure that production can be scaled up and performance stays the same, which makes them even more useful.

Because carbon dots (BCDs) come in so many different shapes and sizes, scientists still don’t fully understand how they work or how they glow when light hits them [140]. Different theories have been put forward to explain the photoluminescence phenomenon. These include electron bandgap transitions in conjugated π regions, size-dependent emission (quantum confinement effect), surface state-derived emission (surface defects, functional groups), and molecular emitters [140–142]. Surface states are regarded as the primary factor among these [143].

There are many ways to change the photoluminescence properties of BCDs. You can change the emission wavelength (400–650 nm) and increase the quantum yield (5–25%) of biomass carbon quantum dots (BCDs) by adding functional groups to their surfaces, doping them with heteroatoms, or making nanocomposites. Advanced purification methods like HPLC and Gel Permeation Chromatography (GPC) can make monodispersity better and lower cytotoxicity. Heteroatom doping (N, S, B) changes the electronic structure, and co-doping (N/S, N/P) increases quantum yields by 30–50%. Doping with heteroatoms, such as nitrogen, sulfur, boron [144], and metals [145], has become a useful way to change the fluorescent properties of BCDs [143]. When different heteroatoms work together, they can cause more surface defects, lower non-radiative recombination, and thus increase photoluminescence intensity (B, S, and N-doped BCDs [146], N and P-doped BCDs [147], and N and S-doped BCDs [148]). In conclusion, mounting evidence indicates that precursors abundant in heteroatoms may eliminate the necessity for supplementary doping. Metal doping (Mn, Zn, Fe) results in red-shifted emission (600–700 nm). Nanocomposites make stability and functionality even better. These strategies let you control the emission wavelength from blue to near-infrared, raise the quantum yield from less than 5% to more than 30%, and make BCDs useful for biosensing and bioimaging. Recent progress uses the targeting accuracy of CRISPR-Cas systems to direct BCDs for both bioimaging and therapy. This shows a promising path in nanotechnology for theranostics.

Doping engineering: heteroatom, metal, and self-doping strategies

Heteroatom doping, metal doping, and self-doped functional molecules are three main ways to change the properties of biomass-derived carbon quantum dots (BCDs). Heteroatom doping (e.g., N, B, P, S, Se), implemented during precursor selection or post-synthesis, concurrently influences band structure, surface chemistry, and bioactivity [149]. For example, nitrogen doping makes graphitic or pyridinic nitrogen, which makes separate energy “stepping stones” that improve quantum yield and hydrophilicity; boron doping makes the sp2 network bigger to improve electron mobility; and selenium doping uses Se–radical reversibility to give antioxidant properties (for example, by scavenging reactive oxygen species) [150]. Through π-conjugation extension (e.g., cyclocondensation with o-diaminoarenes red-shifts emission by 50–150 nm) and surface engineering (e.g., dehydrating carboxyl groups to form donor–acceptor pairs via amidation), it is possible to fine-tune optical properties. Post-synthetic treatments, such as HNO₃ reflux for red-shifting, NH3·H2O/N2H4 reduction for blue-shifting, and a 20–40% increase in quantum yield, allow for full-spectrum emission coverage [151].

Metal doping (such as Mn, Zn, or Fe) uses d–d transitions to move photoluminescence into the near-infrared window (> 15% quantum yield). It also creates metal–organic coordination structures (like Zn–N4) that increase catalytic activity by 3–5 orders of magnitude and allows drugs to be released in response to the tumor microenvironment (12.6 wt.% loading capacity) while also allowing for multimodal imaging (T1 relaxivity of 8.7 mM−1 s−1)[152]. Self-doping strategies use natural biomass elements, like sulfur and nitrogen in human hair, to make intrinsic doped structures without any outside help. For example, sulfur–nitrogen co-doped CQDs made from hair contain 8.5 at.% nitrogen (50–60% pyrrolic N) and 3.2 at.% sulfur. They have a 45% quantum yield and can detect Hg2⁺ at levels as low as 0.1 nM, which is 10 times more sensitive than regular CQDs [153, 154]. CQDs made from Ganoderma lucidum polysaccharides keep their β-1,3/1,6-glucan structures, have a TLR2 affinity of 12 nM (three times higher than natural polysaccharides), and raise the percentage of CD86 + cells from 15 to 60% in immune activation tests [155]. Controlling the carbonization conditions (180–260 °C, 2–12 h) systematically improves self-doped CQDs: the emission redshifts from 450 to 620 nm, the quantum yield goes up from 5 to 45%, the ID/IG ratio goes down from 1.2 to 0.7, and the C=C content goes up from 35 to 60% [156]. Cheng’s team [157] research shows that a hybrid system of carbon dots (CDs) and cyanobacteria improves photosynthetic efficiency by 2.4 times by converting red light (620 nm) and moving electrons. XPS analysis shows that the CDs have a characteristic C1s peak at 284.8 eV, which corresponds to sp2-hybridized C=C bonds. This makes a π-electron delocalized structure that is the basis for core-state luminescence. The CDs absorb light across a wide range of wavelengths (350–690 nm) and emit light in the red region (676 nm). The large Stokes shift shows that the light comes from intrinsic π → π transitions. Transient absorption spectroscopy shows that charge injection into the photosynthetic electron transport chain happens in about 22 ps. This greatly speeds up linear electron transfer (by 51.5%) and NADPH generation (by 30%). The CDs work in two ways: they change ultraviolet-green light into red–orange light (640–710 nm) and inject electrons directly into the photosynthetic system. This makes light-energy use more efficient. This research presents an innovative material approach for augmenting photosynthesis (Fig. 5A–C). Hybridization and compositing of biomass-derived carbon quantum dots

The functionality and application range of biomass-derived carbon quantum dots (BCDs) are greatly improved by materials hybridization and composite strategies. BCDs can be combined with polymers, inorganic matrices, or single-atom metals to make composite materials that work better together. This can be done through covalent bonds or physical interactions like electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding, and π–π stacking [159, 160]. These composites keep the intrinsic luminescence and catalytic activity of BCDs while adding the mechanical strength and new functions of the matrix. This makes them useful in energy, catalysis, and biomedicine [161]. When combined with inorganic nanoparticles like metal oxides or metals, they work together to improve optoelectronic performance by acting as optical amplifiers that increase photoluminescence intensity and quantum yield. They also have a lot of potential in catalysis and sensing [11, 108]. Heteroatom doping (e.g., N, S, F) introduces semiconductor-like quantum properties, leading to multi-level photoluminescence flickering and enhanced photothermal conversion efficiency for cancer therapy [162] (Fig. 6A). For instance, graphitic dots copolymerized with methacrylate yield self-healing anticorrosive films, while hydrothermal co-processing with metal precursors forms heterojunctions (e.g., Ru-NP/g-CD, NiFe-LDH/g-CD, TiO₂-g-CD) with enhanced photocatalytic and electrocatalytic performance via covalent bonding. A significant application is Wang et al.’s composite system [163], which combines self-targeted carbon dots with thermosensitive hydrogels for delivering diclofenac sodium to the eyes. The optimized hydrogel matrix (Poloxamer 407:188:HPMC E50 = 21:1:1) demonstrates biphasic drug release, enhanced corneal permeability, prolonged retention, and favorable cytocompatibility with CD44 targeting, highlighting the potential of carbon dot–hydrogel composites in biomedical applications. Lou Ying’s team [164] concentrated on carbon dots, specifically carbon quantum dots (CQDs) and graphene quantum dots (GQDs) (Fig. 6B), which utilize biomass as a carbon source from plants and their derivatives, animals and their derivatives, and urban waste. They gave a thorough introduction to how to characterize the structure and composition of carbon dots made from biomass, how to control the color of fluorescence, and how to improve quantum yield (QY) through heteroatom doping and surface modification. Yan’s team [165] says that the color of the light that carbon dots give off is mostly changed by conjugation expansion and defect engineering. For example, bigger conjugated π-domains make the HOMO–LUMO gap smaller, which makes the light shift to the red end of the spectrum. Electronic transitions in conjugated structures (like intrinsic carbon to graphitic nitrogen) and surface-state emissions from functional groups (like pyrrolic N, pyridinic N, and –NH2) also affect photoluminescence (Fig. 6C). The luminescent properties of BCDs can be effectively tuned through heteroatom (N, S, B) doping, metal ion (Mn, Zn) coordination, or inorganic/polymer compositing. Nitrogen doping commonly enhances quantum yield, while sulfur/selenium doping facilitates near-infrared emission and antioxidant activity. Metal doping can induce red/near-infrared emission and catalytic performance, though its biosafety requires careful evaluation. Achieving precise and tunable emission from the visible to near-infrared II region remains a research challenge. Future work should integrate first-principles calculations with spectroscopic characterization to elucidate electronic structure evolution and employ machine learning to optimize synthesis parameters for rational design.

Although strategies such as doping and surface modification can tune the optical properties of BCDs, achieving efficient, predictable, and repeatable luminescence tuning from the visible to the near-infrared II region remains a core challenge. This is primarily limited by two factors: the unclear structure–property relationships between dopants/functional groups and luminescence centers, and the difficulty in predicting the “structure–property” correlations due to the inherent complexity of biomass precursors. Future efforts should focus on developing precise “directional modification” chemistry and integrating high-throughput computing with machine learning to identify key synthesis parameters that most influence target optical properties, such as specific emission wavelengths and high quantum yields, thereby enabling on-demand customization of optical performance.

Applications in bioimaging

Bioimaging powered by carbon dots now includes three different types of imaging [166]: one-photon (sub-200 µm resolution for real-time tracking of live cells), two-photon (> 1 mm penetration, < 500 nm resolution for deep-brain and tumor mapping), and photoacoustic (centimeter-scale depth, ~ 100 µm resolution for vascular or inflammatory imaging). Multimodal fusion platforms combine MRI/CT contrast with ratiometric colorimetric/fluorescent logic to make it possible to see organelles, tumors, neurodegeneration, and drug delivery in real time and with high fidelity. By manipulating the surface state (for example, with dynamic ROS nano-regulators [168]), their functions can be fine-tuned. These BCDs are used in many areas, including bioimaging (like HeLa cells [167] and E. coli), biomedicine (like stopping thyroid cancer [168], neuroblastoma [169], and delivering anti-inflammatory drugs [170]). All platforms have low phototoxicity, surface chemistry that is easy to target, and quick clearance from the kidneys. This makes them useful for both imaging and therapeutic purposes in the clinic.

One-photon photoluminescence bioimaging

One-photon photoluminescence bioimaging leverages the tunable photophysical properties of biomass-derived carbon dots (BCDs), such as quantum yields exceeding 80% and excellent photobleaching resistance, to enable dynamic tracking of subcellular structures (e.g., mitochondria) and high-contrast delineation of tumor boundaries. This method uses the tunable photoluminescence properties of BCDs, such as a quantum yield of over 80%, an emission wavelength range of 450–650 nm, and photostability of over 90%, to enable dynamic organelle tracking (monitoring mitochondrial membrane potential), tumor margin delineation (with a signal-to-noise ratio of at least 8:1), and drug delivery visualization. Surface functionalization (with TPP targeting groups and folate receptor ligands) greatly improves biological specificity. The development of near-infrared carbon dots (NIR-BCDs) is gradually overcoming the limitation of tissue penetration depth (< 200 μm). This technology has become an important tool for live-cell imaging, cancer diagnostics, and nanodrug research because it has low phototoxicity, can label cells in multiple colors, and is very biocompatible. Future integration with two-photon excitation or multimodal imaging is anticipated to enhance its applications in deep-tissue imaging. Chen’s team [171] used clover leaves, which are easy to find, as the carbon source to make deep-red, narrow-band carbon dots (DR-NBE-CDs) in one step using a solvothermal method. To show that this method works for other greens, they also made similar dots from ten other common greens, such as mint, spinach, and pothos. The DR-NBE-CDs that came out of this process have a record FWHM of 21 nm at 673 nm, a photoluminescence quantum yield of 52.35%, and excellent photostability. The researchers made the first high-color-purity deep-red CD-LEDs (CIE: 0.692, 0.307) by using a PTAA/PO-T2T exciplex co-host system that allows for efficient Förster energy transfer. These LEDs have a maximum luminance of 579 cd m⁻2 and an external quantum efficiency of 0.57%. Research on single-photon imaging with BCDs has reached a relatively mature stage, demonstrating excellent performance in subcellular organelle labeling and high-contrast in vitro imaging. However, their limited tissue penetration depth (typically < 200 μm) remains a major bottleneck for in vivo applications. Future developments should focus on designing BCDs with higher near-infrared quantum yields, superior photostability, and active targeting capabilities. Two-photon photoluminescence bioimaging

Two-photon photoluminescence bioimaging uses near-infrared femtosecond lasers (800 nm) to get sub-micrometer resolution (< 500 nm laterally, < 1.5 μm axially) with > 1 mm tissue penetration. It does this by using fluorophores with high two-photon absorption cross-sections (δ > 100 GM), like quantum dots, to reduce scattering interference. This method allows for 3D mapping of neurons, analysis of the tumor microenvironment, and tracking of embryonic development. Ongoing improvements in adaptive optics and AI-based reconstruction are helping to fix problems like high system costs and thermal effects. Xu Shuping’s team [172] created a new fluorescent nanoprobe using carbonized polymer dots (CPDs) in a one-step solvothermal process at 200 °C with N,N-diethyl-p-phenylenediamine as the starting material. These CPDs have a special D-π-A structure, and their main advantage is that they can transfer charge between molecules, which makes their fluorescence intensity very sensitive to the polarity of the environment. This allows for strong fluorescence emission in lipid droplets with low polarity and very little emission in high-polarity cytoplasm, which gives excellent imaging contrast. (Fig. 7B). Sai Praneeth Thota’s team [173] made fluorescent carbon dots (CDs) from peanut shells using a one-step hydrothermal method at 220 °C. These CDs show upconversion luminescence, which means they give off light between 380 and 580 nm when excited by near-infrared light between 700 and 800 nm. This makes it possible to take pictures of deep tissue. They have a quantum yield of 17.1%, which is much higher than most CDs made from biomass. This shows that they have great potential for bioimaging. Yeast cell imaging, flow cytometry, and MTT assays showed that the cells were biocompatible and functional, which showed that they were not very toxic and could be used in biological applications.

Photoacoustic bioimaging

Photoacoustic bioimaging (PAB) uses near-infrared BCDs (700–1100 nm absorption) to get around the limits of optical diffusion. It can penetrate tissue on the centimeter scale with a resolution of about 100 μm through laser-induced thermoelastic ultrasound emission. BCDs are great contrast agents because they can be adjusted to absorb light, have a high photothermal conversion efficiency (> 40%), and are very biocompatible. This makes them useful for deep-tissue applications like imaging cerebral blood vessels, checking for new blood vessels in tumors, and monitoring inflammation. A research group led by Ozioma Udochukwu Akakuru [81] made a very hydrophilic iron-doped asphaltene carbon dot composite (ACD-Fe) using a low-temperature (80 °C) oxidation process. The material was made by combining oxygen and nitrogen functional groups that are abundant on the surface with FeCl₃ at room temperature. It has strong absorption in the ultraviolet–visible range and tunable near-infrared absorption that goes all the way to the NIR-I/II window. This makes it good for deep-tissue photoacoustic imaging. The research confirmed its biomedical potential via electron microscopy characterization, cellular assays, and murine MRI investigations. Wang’s work [174] added biomass CQDs to alginate/cellulose hydrogels, which improved their photoluminescence and mechanical properties (the elastic modulus went up by 4.7 × and 1.5 × , respectively). It also showed high sensitivity for detecting Fe3⁺ and AuNP in biosensing (Fig. 7C). Jia’s team [81] created Hypocrella bambusae-derived CDs (HBCDs) that emit red light (610 nm), absorb light over a wide range of wavelengths (350–800 nm), and can be used for PDT/PTT guided by both photoacoustic and fluorescence imaging. Their effective production of 1O₂ (QY 0.38) and heat (27.6%) under a 635 nm laser demonstrates HBCDs’ promise for photoacoustic imaging-enhanced theranostics.

These advances show that carbon dots could be used to combine deep-tissue imaging with therapeutic functions. However, more work is needed to make multimodal probes and AI-enhanced 3D reconstruction work for precision medicine.

Multi-modal imaging

Recent developments in carbon nanodot-based probes exhibit exceptional multifunctionality for dual-modal imaging, attaining enhanced performance via customized structural design. Zheng’s team [40] made carbon nanodots (1.9–5.8 nm) that can be activated by photooxidation for dual-mode imaging. These CDs show near-infrared fluorescence (670/720 nm) and an ultralong afterglow (5.9 h) because energy is released when they are photooxidized. With a tissue penetration of 20 mm, they effectively target tumors (SNR = 8.3) and assist in surgical resection in mice. Du’s team [175] created a folic acid-targeted gadolinium-doped carbon dot (Gd-CDs-FA) material using a one-pot hydrothermal method. The substance has a quantum yield of 48.2% and a longitudinal relaxation rate that is three times higher than that of clinical gadolinium-based contrast agents. It allows for targeted imaging of hepatocellular carcinoma cells (HepG2) and facilitates synergistic fluorescence-MRI dual-modal imaging. Ozioma Udochukwu Akakuru’s group [176] made an iron-doped asphaltene carbon dot composite (ACD-Fe) that lets you do both MRI and fluorescence imaging at the same time. The material shows a longitudinal relaxivity of 1.33 mM−1 s−1, which is four times higher than free Fe3⁺. This improves T₁-weighted tumor contrast. It also fluoresces evenly when excited at 405 nm. Li’s study [177] successfully created iodine-doped carbon dot-ferroferric oxide hybrid nanoparticles (I@CNDs-Fe₃O₄) by using a one-pot hydrothermal method with kelp as the starting material. The reaction took place at 150–200 °C for 24 h. The resulting substance had a consistent particle size, with an average of 7.21 nm, and a surface full of hydrophilic groups. It showed tunable emission properties in fluorescence imaging, with a peak emission at 440 nm and a quantum yield of 22.3%. The material had an X-ray attenuation slope of 180 HU·L/g for CT imaging, which was better than the clinical contrast agent iohexol. It had a high transverse relaxation rate of 177.4 mM−1·s−1 in MRI imaging. These properties all point to the fact that the material works very well for multi-modal imaging (Fig. 7D).

These engineered carbon nanodot probes use folic acid targeting, asphaltene complexation, and photooxidation activation to get targeted recognition, better signaling, and long-lasting luminescence. This new platform shows a lot of promise for accurate tumor diagnosis, guiding surgery, and advanced multimodal biomedical imaging.

Prospects for the future development and challenges of BCDs

Biomass-derived carbon quantum dots (BCDs) have emerged as promising nanomaterials in biomedical imaging, sensing, drug delivery, and environmental monitoring, owing to their green and controllable synthesis, excellent optical properties, and favorable biocompatibility. This review systematically summarizes the key advancements in the sustainable synthesis, luminescence mechanism elucidation, and multimodal bioimaging applications of BCDs, revealing the intrinsic relationships between precursor chemical structures and final imaging performance. Despite their considerable potential, the translation of BCDs from the laboratory to clinical and industrial-scale applications faces three fundamental challenges: the lack of a unified theoretical framework to accurately predict complex luminescent behaviors; insufficient precision in optical modulation, particularly the controllable synthesis in the near-infrared region; and poor batch-to-batch consistency, constrained by the complexity of biomass precursors and the unpredictability of synthesis processes.To address these challenges, future research should focus on breakthroughs in the following directions:

Mechanistic Understanding and Rational Design: Integrating in situ/operando spectroscopic techniques with first-principles calculations to decouple the interplay among core-state, surface-state, and molecular-state luminescence at the atomic/molecular level, thereby establishing predictive “structure–property” theoretical models and advancing the transition from empirical exploration to a rational design paradigm.

Process Innovation and Quality Control: Establishing a comprehensive quality control system spanning “raw material standardization—process precision—intelligent optimization.” This includes developing standardized pre-treatment protocols for biomass precursors, employing advanced synthesis platforms such as microfluidic reactors to achieve precise control over reaction parameters, and incorporating machine learning and artificial intelligence to analyze high-throughput synthesis and performance data. Such an approach enables the reverse optimization of processing windows and real-time prediction and control of product quality, fundamentally addressing batch-to-batch consistency issues.

Functional Integration and Clinical Translation: Developing multimodal theranostic probes that combine efficient near-infrared emission, targeting capability, and biodegradability. Concurrently, it is essential to systematically conduct internationally compliant evaluations of long-term in vivo biosafety, pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity, and organ accumulation to provide comprehensive safety and efficacy data supporting their eventual clinical translation.

Through interdisciplinary collaboration and systematic technological innovation, overcoming the core bottlenecks in mechanistic understanding, controllable synthesis, and safety assessment will enable BCDs to transition from fundamental research to industrial applications. Ultimately, they are poised to become safe, efficient, and sustainable nanotechnological solutions that advance precision medicine, environmental health, and agricultural sustainability.
