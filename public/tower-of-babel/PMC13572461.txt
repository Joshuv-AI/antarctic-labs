Multivariate analysis of the pomological traits, biochemical composition, antioxidant capacity, and mineral content of strawberry tree (Arbutus andrachne L.) fruits
Authors: Canan Aydınlıoğlu, Yazgan Tunç, Mehmet Yaman, Muhammed Ali Köse, Metin Gönültaş, Ercan Yıldız, Adem Güneş, Ahmet Sümbül, Kadir Uğurtan Yılmaz, Ali Khadivi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe strawberry tree (Arbutus andrachne L.) is an underutilized Mediterranean fruit species with considerable potential as a source of nutritionally valuable and functionally important bioactive compounds. In the present study, 30 naturally growing genotypes collected from Defne district of Hatay Province, Türkiye, were comprehensively evaluated over two consecutive growing seasons (2024 and 2025) to determine the extent of variation in fruit pomological traits, color parameters, biochemical composition, antioxidant capacity, and mineral content. Fruit width, fruit length, fruit weight, CIELAB color coordinates (L*, a*, and b*), total phenolic content, total flavonoid content, total anthocyanin content, total antioxidant capacity, and 16 mineral elements were assessed. In addition to univariate comparisons, multivariate statistical approaches, including correlation analysis, principal component analysis, heat map analysis, and multiple regression analysis, were applied to reveal trait interrelationships and genotype differentiation. The results demonstrated significant genotype-dependent variation for all evaluated traits, indicating broad phenotypic diversity within the studied germplasm. Fruit weight ranged from 0.29 to 1.81 g, total phenolic content from 321.61 to 507.01 mg GAE 100 g⁻1 FW, total flavonoid content from 125.54 to 288.87 mg CAE 100 g⁻1 FW, total anthocyanin content from 7.22 to 20.07 mg C3G 100 g⁻1 FW, and antioxidant capacity from 31.54% to 64.09% inhibition. Potassium was the predominant mineral, varying between 2867.29 and 8134.66 mg kg⁻1 DW. Genotype ‘AA-7’ was distinguished by superior fruit size and weight, ‘AA-19’ by outstanding macro-mineral accumulation, ‘AA-14’ by the highest antioxidant capacity, ‘AA-3’ by elevated phenolic content, and ‘AA-23’ by the highest anthocyanin concentration. Multivariate analyses revealed strong coordination among fruit size traits, structured associations among color parameters, partial coupling among biochemical variables, and an integrated pattern of mineral accumulation. Multivariate analyses further revealed that several genotypes, particularly ‘AA-7’, ‘AA-14’, ‘AA-19’, and ‘AA-23’, occupied distinct positions in the multivariate space, reflecting unique combinations of pomological, biochemical, and mineral attributes. Overall, the findings highlight the substantial breeding, conservation, and functional food potential of naturally growing strawberry tree germplasm.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58706-5.Keywords: Arbutus andrachne, Pomological traits, Antioxidant capacity, Phenolic compounds, Genetic resourcesSubject terms: Biochemistry, Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

The strawberry tree (Arbutus andrachne L.), a member of the Ericaceae family, is an evergreen species widely distributed throughout the Mediterranean basin and parts of the Middle East. The species is well adapted to Mediterranean climatic conditions characterized by hot and dry summers and mild winters, and it commonly occurs in natural maquis vegetation and rocky slopes. In addition to its ecological importance in Mediterranean forest ecosystems, Arbutus species have recently attracted considerable attention due to their edible fruits and their potential as a source of bioactive compounds with nutritional and functional value1. Strawberry tree fruits are traditionally consumed fresh or processed into various products such as jams, beverages, and fermented foods, and have been increasingly recognized as promising functional foods rich in phenolic compounds and antioxidants.

Recent research has highlighted that strawberry tree fruits contain substantial amounts of phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and anthocyanins, which contribute significantly to their antioxidant capacity and health-promoting properties. These compounds are associated with various biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and cardioprotective effects2,3. In addition, strawberry tree fruits possess notable levels of essential macro- and microelements, making them an important potential source of dietary minerals4. Consequently, there has been growing scientific interest in evaluating the nutritional composition and phytochemical profile of strawberry tree fruits, particularly within the context of promoting underutilized Mediterranean fruit species as alternative sources of functional food ingredients.

In addition to their nutritional attributes, strawberry tree populations exhibit substantial genetic and phenotypic diversity. Natural populations often display significant variation in fruit morphology, color characteristics, and biochemical composition, largely driven by genotype and local environmental conditions. Such variability represents important opportunities for identifying superior genotypes with desirable traits, including larger fruit size, enhanced antioxidant capacity, or improved mineral composition5. The characterization of this diversity is particularly valuable for germplasm conservation and breeding programs aimed at improving the quality and commercial potential of underutilized fruit species.

Fruit quality in strawberry tree is determined by the interaction of multiple attributes, including pomological traits, biochemical composition, pigment accumulation, and mineral content. Pomological parameters such as fruit weight and size are important indicators of yield potential and consumer preference. Likewise, color characteristics, particularly those related to anthocyanin accumulation, influence visual appeal and serve as indicators of fruit maturity and quality6. At the biochemical level, phenolic and flavonoid compounds represent major contributors to antioxidant activity and play key roles in plant defense and fruit nutritional value7. Meanwhile, mineral composition reflects both the nutritional quality of the fruit and the physiological processes governing nutrient uptake and accumulation within plant tissues8.

Although several studies have investigated the nutritional or biochemical characteristics of strawberry tree fruits, integrative research simultaneously examining pomological traits, biochemical properties, antioxidant capacity, and mineral composition remains relatively limited. Moreover, advanced multivariate statistical approaches are increasingly used in horticultural research to explore complex relationships among fruit traits and to identify key variables contributing to genotype differentiation9,10. Such approaches allow a more comprehensive understanding of phenotypic diversity and facilitate the identification of promising genetic resources.

Therefore, the present study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the pomological traits, biochemical composition, antioxidant capacity, and mineral content of naturally growing strawberry tree genotypes collected from Hatay Province, Türkiye. In addition, multivariate statistical analyses were employed to reveal trait relationships and identify patterns of variability among genotypes. The findings of this study are expected to contribute to a better understanding of the phenotypic diversity present in strawberry tree populations and to support future breeding, conservation, and functional food development efforts involving this valuable Mediterranean fruit species. The novelty of this study lies in the combined application of correlation analysis, principal component analysis, heat map analysis, and multiple regression modeling to simultaneously assess fruit quality attributes and identify key factors contributing to genotype differentiation. The findings provide a comprehensive framework for the selection, conservation, and potential utilization of superior strawberry tree germplasm.

Material and methods

Plant material and sampling

Thitry naturally growing genotypes of strawberry tree were selected from the Defne district of Hatay Province, Türkiye. The sampling area ranged between 36°04′–36°14′ N latitude and 36°03′–36°07′ E longitude, at altitudes between 59 and 455 m above sea level. Detailed geographic information for each genotype is presented in Fig. 1 and Table 1. The study area is characterized by a typical Mediterranean climate, with hot and dry summers and mild, rainy winters. According to data from the Turkish State Meteorological Service11, the total annual precipitation in Defne district was 1257.1 mm in 2024 and 535.0 mm in 2025. The mean annual temperatures were 20.0 °C and 20.6 °C, respectively, while the average relative humidity values were recorded as 71.8% and 71.1%. The two-year averages were 896.1 mm for precipitation, 20.3 °C for temperature, and 71.5% for relative humidity (Supplementary Table S1).

Genotypes were identified in situ based on morphological distinctiveness, vigor, fruit characteristics, and spatial distribution. To minimize the probability of sampling closely related or clonal individuals, a minimum distance of 200 m was maintained between selected genotypes. Only healthy, mature, and fully productive trees were included in the study. All selected genotypes were naturally growing under non-cultivated conditions without irrigation, fertilization, or pruning practices.

Fruit sampling was conducted during two consecutive growing seasons (2024 and 2025) from the same individual trees to ensure temporal consistency. Fruits were harvested at the full commercial ripening stage, determined by uniform external coloration and physiological maturity.

Fruits were sampled from different canopy orientations and heights to ensure representative sampling. Mechanically damaged, diseased, or physiologically abnormal fruits were excluded.

Immediately after harvest, samples were transported to the laboratory under cooled conditions and processed on the same day. Pomological and color measurements were performed on fresh fruits, while the remaining samples were homogenized and prepared for biochemical and mineral analyses as described in subsequent sections.

Fruit pomology

Fruit pomological analyses were performed using a total of 150 fruits per genotype (50 fruits per replication, three replications). Fruit length (mm) and fruit width (mm) were measured using a digital caliper (Insize, Model No. 1108–150, China) with a precision of ± 0.01 mm. Fruit weight (g) was determined using a precision digital balance (Radwag AS 220.R2, Radom, Poland) with a sensitivity of 0.01 g.

Fruit color parameters were measured at three different points on the equatorial region of each fruit using a portable colorimeter (WR10 Fruit Colorimeter, Shenzhen Wave Optoelectronics Technology Co., Ltd., China). Color values were recorded according to the CIELAB color space as L* (lightness), a* (red–green coordinate), and b* (yellow–blue coordinate). The device was calibrated before measurements using the manufacturer’s standard white calibration plate. The reported L, a, and b* values represent the mean of three measurements taken from different points on the equatorial region of each fruit.

All measurements were conducted under laboratory conditions at 22 ± 2 °C and relative humidity of 50–60%. Mean values were calculated from triplicate measurements for each fruit.

Mineral composition

The mineral composition of strawberry tree fruits, including aluminum (Al), boron (B), calcium (Ca), cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), copper (Cu), iron (Fe), potassium (K), magnesium (Mg), manganese (Mn), sodium (Na), nickel (Ni), phosphorus (P), lead (Pb), sulfur (S), and zinc (Zn), was determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES).

Mineral analyses were conducted in three biological replicates per genotype, with each replicate consisting of 50 fruits (total n = 150 fruits per genotype). Fruits were washed with deionized water, air-dried, and oven-dried at 68 °C for 48 h in a forced-air drying oven (Nükleon NKD1000, Ankara, Türkiye) until constant weight was achieved. The dried samples were ground into a fine homogeneous powder using a laboratory mill (Retsch ZM 200, Haan, Germany) equipped with a stainless-steel rotor and 0.5 mm sieve.

For acid digestion, 0.25 g of the powdered sample was accurately weighed using an analytical balance and transferred into polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) digestion vessels. Samples were treated with 5 mL concentrated nitric acid (HNO₃, 65%, suprapure grade, Merck, Germany) and 2 mL hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂, 30%, Merck, Germany). Digestion was performed using a microwave digestion system (Milestone ETHOS UP, Sorisole, Italy) according to the protocol described by Miller13. After complete digestion, the solutions were cooled to room temperature, quantitatively transferred, and diluted to a final volume of 25 mL with ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ·cm, Human Power I + , Human Corporation, Germany,Türkiye distributor). The digestates were filtered through 0.45 µm membrane filters prior to instrumental analysis.

Elemental concentrations were quantified using an ICP-OES system (Perkin-Elmer Optima 2100 DV, Shelton, CT, USA). Instrumental operating conditions were set according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Calibration curves were prepared using multi-element standard solutions (Merck, Germany) at five concentration levels, and linearity coefficients (R2) exceeded 0.999 for all analytes.

Analytical quality control was ensured through the inclusion of reagent blanks, duplicate samples, and certified reference material (CRM, NIST 1573a – Tomato Leaves, National Institute of Standards and Technology, USA). Recovery values ranged between 93–105%. Limits of detection (LOD) and limits of quantification (LOQ) were calculated as three and ten times the standard deviation of blank measurements, respectively.

All mineral concentrations were expressed as mg kg⁻1 on a dry weight (DW) basis.

Chemical characteristics

Sample preparation

Biochemical analyses were performed in three biological replicates per genotype, with each replicate consisting of 50 fruits (total n = 150 fruits per genotype). Fresh fruits were homogenized immediately after harvest using a laboratory blender (Waring Commercial 8010ES, USA) under controlled conditions to minimize enzymatic oxidation. Extraction was carried out following Hannachi et al.14 with standardized modifications suitable for high-sugar fruit matrices. Briefly, 1 g of fresh fruit homogenate was extracted with 10 mL of 80% (v/v) methanol (HPLC grade, Merck, Germany). Samples were subjected to maceration under constant agitation (150 rpm) for 24 h at 25 °C using an orbital shaker (IKA KS 130 basic, Staufen, Germany). Extracts were filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper and centrifuged (Hettich H-1650R, Tuttlingen, Germany) at 11,200 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected, transferred into amber glass vials, flushed with nitrogen to minimize oxidative degradation, and stored at 4 °C. All extracts were analyzed within 24 h. Independent extractions were performed for each replicate to ensure analytical reproducibility. Extraction repeatability was assessed, and relative standard deviation (RSD) values were below 5% for all biochemical parameters.

Total antioxidant capacity (TAC, DPPH radical scavenging assay)

Antioxidant capacity was determined according to Sharma and Bhat15 using the DPPH radical scavenging assay. A freshly prepared 0.26 mM DPPH (1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl) solution in methanol was used as the reactive agent. For each assay, 100 µL of fruit extract was mixed with 2.9 mL ethanol and 1 mL DPPH solution. The mixture was vortexed (DLAB MX-S, 2500 rpm, 15 s) and incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 30 min in a temperature-controlled incubator (Memmert IN55, Schwabach, Germany). Absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1900, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with 1 cm quartz cuvettes. Methanol served as the blank. Percentage inhibition of DPPH radicals was calculated according to Eq. (1). All measurements were conducted in triplicate, and intra-assay RSD values were below 4%.

Total phenolic content (TPC)

Total phenolic content (TPC) was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method as described by Lamuela-Raventós16. Briefly, 0.5 mL of fruit extract was mixed with 4.1 mL distilled water, 100 µL Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (Merck, Germany), and 1 mL of 2% sodium carbonate solution. The reaction mixture was incubated at 25 ± 1 °C for 2 h in the dark (Memmert IN55 incubator). Absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a Shimadzu UV-1900 spectrophotometer. Quantification was performed using a gallic acid calibration curve (10–100 mg L⁻1), with correlation coefficients (R2) ≥ 0.999. LOD and LOQ were calculated as three and ten times the standard deviation of blank measurements, respectively. Results were expressed as mg GAE 100 g⁻1 FW. Inter-assay RSD values were below 5%.

Total flavonoid content (TFC)

Total flavonoid content (TFC) was determined using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method17. One milliliter of fruit extract was mixed with 3.3 mL methanol, 0.1 mL of 10% aluminum chloride hexahydrate (AlCl₃·6H₂O), and 0.1 mL of 1 M potassium acetate. The final volume was adjusted to 5 mL using a calibrated micropipette (Eppendorf Research plus, Hamburg, Germany). After incubation for 30 min at 25 °C in the dark, absorbance was recorded at 415 nm using a Shimadzu UV-1900 spectrophotometer. Quantification was based on a catechin calibration curve (10–100 mg L⁻1; R2 ≥ 0.998), and results were expressed as mg CAE 100 g⁻1 FW. Repeatability tests yielded RSD values below 5%.

Statistical analysis

Fruit samples were collected from the same trees during two consecutive growing seasons (2024 and 2025). To enhance the reliability of the results and minimize the influence of seasonal variability, the mean values obtained from both years were calculated and used in all statistical analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP® Pro 17 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA)19. Prior to parametric analyses, data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. When necessary, appropriate data transformations were applied to satisfy parametric assumptions. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to evaluate differences among genotypes for pomological traits, biochemical composition, antioxidant capacity, and mineral composition. When significant differences were detected, mean comparisons were performed using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test at a 5% significance level (p < 0.05). Mean values were grouped and denoted by different letters according to their statistical significance. Multivariate analyses were conducted using OriginPro® 202520. Correlation matrix analysis was conducted using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) to evaluate the strength and direction of linear relationships among the studied variables. Statistical significance was assessed at p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001. The correlation matrix was visualized as a heatmap in which both color gradient and circle size were proportional to the magnitude and direction of the correlation coefficients. Positive correlations were represented by red tones and negative correlations by blue tones, while statistically significant correlations were indicated by asterisks. Principal component analysis was applied to reduce data dimensionality and identify the main sources of variation among genotypes. Sampling adequacy was verified using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was used to confirm suitability for factor analysis. Principal component analysis was conducted using Varimax rotation with Kaiser normalization. Components with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 were retained according to Kaiser’s criterion. Factor loadings with |loading|≥ 0.26 were considered significant contributors. This threshold was selected based on recommendations for the interpretation of PCA loadings in datasets with comparable sample sizes, where loading values around 0.25–0.30 are considered meaningful indicators of variable contribution21. A two-dimensional biplot was generated to visualize genotype distribution along the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) and to illustrate variable associations. Heat map analysis was performed using z-score standardized data to visualize variation and similarity patterns among genotypes and evaluated traits. Hierarchical clustering based on Euclidean distance and Ward’s linkage method was applied to both rows (genotypes) and columns (variables) to identify grouping tendencies and relationships. Multiple regression analysis was carried out using the stepwise method within the linear regression module of SPSS® software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA)22, following the procedures described by Norusis23 and Efe et al.24. Separate regression models were constructed using fruit weight, L* (lightness), a* (redness), total antioxidant capacity, and total anthocyanin content as dependent variables. Model adequacy was evaluated using the multiple correlation coefficient (r), coefficient of determination (r2), adjusted r2, regression coefficients (β), and t- and p-values. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) values, and VIF values greater than 10 were considered indicative of multicollinearity. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Descriptive statistics among genotypes

The pomological, mineral, and biochemical characteristics of the strawberry tree genotypes were comprehensively evaluated, and the descriptive statistical parameters for each trait are presented in Table 2. The results of the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed statistically significant differences among the evaluated genotypes (p < 0.05), confirming the presence of considerable phenotypic diversity within the studied population. The highest coefficients of variation (CV) were observed for chromium (71.43%), lead (59.26%), b* color coordinate (56.04%), cadmium (53.03%), and manganese (42.45%), indicating substantial heterogeneity for these traits. In contrast, boron (20.11%), a* color coordinate (16.79%), total phenolic content (15.05%), fruit width (12.64%), and fruit length (10.96%) exhibited comparatively lower variability, reflecting greater uniformity among genotypes.

Overall, 22 out of the 26 evaluated traits (84.62%) showed CV values exceeding 20.00%, demonstrating a high level of genetic and phenotypic variability among the examined genotypes, in agreement with previous findings25. Traits characterized by CV values above this threshold are considered particularly informative for discriminating among genetic materials and are therefore valuable descriptors in germplasm characterization studies26. Moreover, traits displaying a broader quantitative range generally correspond to higher CV values, which enhances their usefulness in selection and breeding programs 27. Conversely, traits with lower CV values tend to exhibit greater stability and consistency across genotypes, making them reliable indicators of uniform phenotypic expression26.

Fruit size attributes exhibited clear genotype-dependent divergence. Fruit width ranged from 7.30 mm (‘AA-12’) to 14.92 mm (‘AA-7’), while fruit length varied between 7.80 mm (‘AA-23’) and 12.74 mm (‘AA-14’). Fruit weight showed an even broader amplitude, spanning from 0.29 g (‘AA-12’) to 1.81 g (‘AA-7’). The distinct separation into Tukey groups reinforces the conclusion that these differences are statistically robust and biologically meaningful. Such magnitude of variation suggests the presence of exploitable genetic variability within the evaluated germplasm. Comparable inter-genotypic differences in fruit dimensions and weight have been documented in strawberry tree populations by Çelikel et al.28 and Sağbaş et al.5, emphasizing the predominant role of genotype in shaping pomological performance. Although the fruit weights recorded in the present study (0.29–1.81 g) were lower than those reported by Çolak29 (4.67–7.88 g), both studies revealed substantial genotype-dependent variation, highlighting the existence of considerable phenotypic diversity within natural populations. Likewise, Beyhan et al.30 reported considerable variation among selected genotypes in Bolu province, further corroborating the consistency of size-related heterogeneity across different ecological contexts. Moreover, the broader morphological and genetic differentiation identified in naturally growing populations by Ersoy et al.31 supports the interpretation that such phenotypic divergence is rooted in underlying genetic diversity. From a breeding perspective, genotypes producing larger and heavier fruits—such as ‘AA-7’—represent strategically valuable material for yield-oriented improvement and cultivar development.

Color parameters displayed a similarly structured pattern of differentiation. The L* value ranged from 14.36 (‘AA-18’) to 33.50 (‘AA-20’), indicating substantial divergence in fruit lightness. The a* parameter, reflecting red pigmentation intensity, reached its highest levels in ‘AA-27’ (118.58), ‘AA-8’ (114.92), ‘AA-15’ (114.63), ‘AA-28’ (112.82), ‘AA-30’ (108.57), and ‘AA-26’ (107.83). These elevated a* values denote intensified red coloration, a trait commonly associated with advanced ripening and enhanced market appeal. The increase in a* values and intensified red coloration observed in certain genotypes aligns with previous findings showing that strawberry tree fruit chemical composition and antioxidant properties change significantly with ripening stage, reflecting pigment accumulation processes during maturation32. The b* value ranged from 2.72 (‘AA-18’) to 31.08 (‘AA-20’), revealing marked differences in yellow–orange tonal components among genotypes. Similar genotype-based color discrimination using CIELAB metrics was reported by Markovski33, confirming the robustness of this analytical framework in detecting phenotypic divergence. Compared with the L, a, and b* ranges reported by Çolak29, the present genotypes exhibited lower lightness and markedly higher red color intensity, indicating substantial differences in fruit pigmentation. Despite these quantitative differences, both studies revealed considerable variation in CIELAB coordinates, confirming fruit color as a highly variable genotype-dependent trait in strawberry tree germplasm. Furthermore, the variation observed in L*, a*, and b* coordinates may reflect underlying biochemical transitions during fruit maturation, including shifts in carotenoid and phenolic profiles, as suggested by Scarano et al.6.

Taken together, the consistent and statistically supported differentiation across pomological and color parameters demonstrates that the evaluated population harbors substantial morphological diversity. The convergence of quantitative variation and literature-supported genetic differentiation underscores the strategic value of this germplasm. Such diversity provides a reliable basis for selecting superior genotypes that integrate favorable fruit size and visually attractive coloration, thereby supporting targeted breeding initiatives and long-term genetic resource management.

Recent metabolomic studies on strawberry tree fruits have similarly highlighted strong variability in flavonoid profiles among genotypes and ripening stages, demonstrating that flavonoid accumulation is highly genotype-dependent and closely associated with fruit biochemical maturation processes39.

Previous research has similarly reported considerable genotype-dependent variation in anthocyanin concentration among strawberry tree populations, emphasizing the strong influence of genetic factors on pigment biosynthesis and fruit quality attributes37.

When the biochemical parameters are evaluated collectively, several genotype-specific biochemical profiles become evident. For instance, ‘AA-3’ was characterized by the highest total phenolic concentration and very high flavonoid levels, ‘AA-9’ exhibited the highest flavonoid content, ‘AA-14’ showed the strongest antioxidant capacity, and ‘AA-23’ presented the highest anthocyanin accumulation. Beyond compositional diversity, these findings underline the significant nutraceutical potential of strawberry tree fruits. Recent studies have demonstrated that strawberry tree fruits contain diverse phenolic compounds with recognized biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and health-promoting effects, suggesting their potential use as functional food ingredients35,36. In particular, the high concentrations of phenolics, flavonoids, and anthocyanins reported for certain genotypes indicate that strawberry tree fruits may represent valuable natural sources of bioactive compounds for nutraceutical and functional food applications. Moreover, the pronounced biochemical variability detected among genotypes provides an important opportunity for selecting elite genotypes with enhanced bioactive composition for breeding programs and value-added product development.

Correlation matrix analysis (CMA)

The heatmap-based Pearson correlation matrix (Fig. 3) revealed structured and biologically meaningful relationships among pomological, color, biochemical, and mineral traits in the studied strawberry tree genotypes. As expected, fruit size attributes formed a coherent cluster: fruit width, fruit length, and fruit weight were strongly and positively associated with each other, indicating that genotypes producing larger fruits also tended to accumulate greater fresh mass. This pattern suggests that overall fruit growth is coordinated through common developmental and/or source–sink mechanisms rather than being driven by independent trait-specific pathways40. Heatmap-based Pearson correlation matrix showing the relationships among the examined traits in the studied strawberry tree genotypes. For an explanation of the abbreviations, see Table 1.

Color coordinates also showed a clear internal structure. Lightness (L*) exhibited a very strong positive association with the yellow–blue coordinate (b*), implying that lighter fruits tend to display higher b* values (more pronounced yellow/orange tonal components). In contrast, the redness coordinate (a*) displayed an opposite tendency relative to fruit lightness, indicating that darker fruits typically exhibit stronger red pigmentation. Together, these relationships are consistent with maturation-linked color transitions in which pigment accumulation and surface optical properties change in a coordinated manner across genotypes6,41.

Biochemical traits displayed selective, rather than uniform, coupling. Total phenolic content and total flavonoid content tended to covary in the same direction, supporting the interpretation that flavonoids constitute a major fraction of the phenolic pool and that these traits share upstream regulation within the phenylpropanoid pathway42. Notably, antioxidant capacity did not mirror total phenolics perfectly across all genotypes, suggesting that overall antioxidant performance is influenced not only by the “amount” of phenolics but also by their qualitative composition (e.g., relative contribution of different phenolic subclasses) and potential synergistic interactions with other antioxidants43. This decoupling is typical for fruit systems where compositional shifts can alter radical scavenging efficiency without proportional changes in bulk phenolic indices7.

Mineral traits exhibited a pronounced “block” structure, with many macro- and microelements showing positive intercorrelations, indicating that mineral accumulation is not random but coordinated, likely reflecting shared uptake, transport, and storage processes8,44. Such clustering also suggests that genotypes with strong accumulation capacity for one mineral often tend to accumulate others, supporting the feasibility of multi-mineral enhancement through genotype-based selection44. In contrast, elements with weaker or inconsistent associations may be more sensitive to genotype-specific physiology or micro-environmental heterogeneity, which warrants targeted interpretation rather than broad generalization8.

Overall, the correlation matrix highlights that the evaluated germplasm expresses (i) tightly coupled fruit-size architecture, (ii) a strongly coordinated color system (particularly L*–b* co-variation and the inverse tendency between L* and a*), (iii) partially shared regulation among phenolic-related traits, and (iv) a coordinated mineral accumulation network. These structured relationships provide an integrative framework for understanding trait co-expression and offer practical guidance for selecting genotypes depending on the intended target (pomological performance, color/anthocyanin enrichment, antioxidant-oriented quality, or mineral density)8.

Multiple regression analysis (MRA)

Multiple regression analysis was performed to determine the most influential predictors associated with key pomological, colorimetric, biochemical, and mineral traits in strawberry tree genotypes (Table 6). Fruit weight was primarily explained by fruit size attributes. Fruit width and fruit length entered the regression model sequentially and together accounted for a substantial proportion of the variation in fruit weight (r2 = 0.76; adjusted r2 = 0.74). Both variables showed positive standardized beta coefficients (β = 0.55 and β = 0.40, respectively), indicating that increases in fruit dimensions directly contributed to higher fruit mass. This strong relationship suggests that fruit mass accumulation in the studied genotypes is largely governed by coordinated fruit expansion processes rather than independent developmental pathways. The simultaneous contribution of both dimensional parameters indicates that overall fruit size architecture plays a central role in determining fruit weight variation among genotypes. For the redness parameter (a*), both L* (lightness) and total phenolic content were identified as significant predictors. The negative standardized beta coefficients (β = − 0.46 and β = − 0.33, respectively) indicate that increases in fruit lightness and phenolic concentration were associated with a reduction in redness intensity. This pattern suggests that fruit color expression is influenced by both optical properties of the fruit surface and biochemical composition. In particular, the negative association between phenolic concentration and redness indicates that the total phenolic pool may include compounds that are not directly related to red pigment formation. Therefore, variations in phenolic accumulation among genotypes may alter fruit coloration indirectly rather than intensifying red pigmentation. Lightness (L*) itself was strongly predicted by the b* color coordinate. The regression model showed extremely high explanatory power (r2 = 0.96), accompanied by a very high standardized beta coefficient (β = 0.98). This result indicates a very strong coupling between fruit lightness and the yellow–blue tonal component. The magnitude of this relationship suggests that variation in fruit brightness among genotypes is largely driven by changes in the b* coordinate, indicating that both color parameters vary simultaneously during fruit coloration processes. Total antioxidant capacity exhibited a significant negative relationship with sodium concentration (β = − 0.38). Although the explanatory power of the model was moderate (r2 = 0.14), the result indicates that mineral composition may partially influence antioxidant performance in strawberry tree fruits. The negative regression coefficient suggests that higher sodium accumulation may be associated with reduced antioxidant activity in certain genotypes. This relationship may reflect physiological interactions between mineral balance and oxidative metabolism within fruit tissues. Total anthocyanin content showed a significant negative association with fruit width (β = − 0.53), indicating that genotypes producing larger fruits tended to contain lower anthocyanin concentrations. This pattern may reflect a dilution effect, where increasing fruit size leads to a reduction in concentration-based secondary metabolites. Alternatively, the relationship may indicate a physiological trade-off between fruit growth and pigment biosynthesis, where carbon resources are preferentially allocated either to structural growth or to secondary metabolite production.

Based on the available literature, the simultaneous integration of pomological, biochemical, colorimetric, and mineral variables into multiple regression models has not previously been reported for strawberry tree germplasm. Consequently, the present study offers a novel perspective on the determinants of fruit quality by quantifying the interrelationships among these traits within a unified statistical framework. The resulting models enabled the identification of key variables associated with genotype differentiation and fruit quality variation, thereby providing new insights into the biological relationships governing trait expression in strawberry tree fruits.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal component analysis (PCA) provided an integrative overview of how pomological, colorimetric, biochemical, and mineral traits jointly structure phenotypic differentiation among the studied strawberry tree genotypes (Table 7). The dominance of PC1 (35.99%) by a coherent set of mineral loadings—headed by zinc (0.31), potassium (0.30), magnesium (0.30), phosphorus (0.29), copper (0.28), sulfur (0.28), manganese (0.27), and calcium (0.26)—indicates that mineral composition represents the primary multivariate axis of variability. Such co-loading of macro- and microelements is consistent with the concept that fruit mineral profiles often behave as a coordinated “multi-element signature,” shaped by shared uptake, transport, and partitioning processes and expressed as a composite nutritional gradient among genotypes. Comparable multi-element clustering has been frequently observed in fruit quality studies employing chemometrics, where PCA effectively separates genotypes or samples based on integrated mineral patterns rather than single-element effects9,10.

In contrast, PC2 (15.84%) was primarily driven by fruit size attributes (fruit width, 0.43; fruit weight, 0.39; fruit length, 0.33) together with surface optical properties (L*, 0.36 and b*, 0.35), while total anthocyanin content (− 0.26) loaded negatively. This configuration suggests that PC2 captures a morpho-chromatic axis, where larger fruits tend to be associated with higher lightness and yellowish tonal components, whereas anthocyanin concentration shifts in the opposite direction. Importantly, the negative loading of total anthocyanin content relative to size-related traits is compatible with a concentration-based “dilution” pattern, in which increases in fruit mass or volume may reduce concentration indices of pigments even when the total pigment pool does not scale proportionally. This interpretation aligns with broader evidence that color development and phenolic or pigment dynamics during fruit maturation are strongly stage-dependent and frequently decouple from purely morphological expansion, particularly in underutilized Mediterranean fruits such as strawberry tree, where ripening entails substantial remodeling of compositional traits that can be resolved through multivariate ordination39,45.

Finally, PC3 (9.23%) highlighted a distinct pigmentation-biochemical contrast, being strongly associated with a* (0.53) and accompanied by sodium (0.39) and total flavonoid content (0.27), while fruit weight (0.26) also contributed positively, whereas b* (− 0.35) and L* (− 0.36) loaded negatively. This pattern indicates that the third axis reflects redness-centered color differentiation coupled with specific compositional features, reinforcing that color metrics in strawberry tree fruits are not merely visual descriptors but proxies for underlying biochemical transitions. Indeed, recent studies on strawberry tree have emphasized that shifts in phenolic subclasses and associated quality traits across developmental stages can be effectively discriminated using multivariate approaches, supporting the interpretation that PC3 likely captures genotype-dependent differences in pigment expression and related phenolic balance rather than simple lightness variation35,46.

Together, the first three principal components accounted for 61.06% of the total variance, demonstrating that a substantial proportion of the phenotypic diversity present in the studied germplasm can be effectively described within a relatively limited multivariate framework. Furthermore, the retention of six components with eigenvalues exceeding 1.0, explaining 78.32% of the cumulative variance, highlights the complex and multidimensional nature of fruit quality variation in strawberry tree. The results indicate that mineral accumulation patterns (PC1), the combined influence of fruit size and color attributes together with pigment-related trade-offs (PC2), and biochemical variation associated with fruit redness (PC3) constitute the major sources of genotype differentiation. The trait structure revealed by PCA provides valuable insight for germplasm evaluation and selection, enabling the identification of superior genotypes according to specific breeding and utilization objectives, including enhanced mineral composition, improved pomological performance, or superior pigment- and antioxidant-related fruit quality.

Beyond the general clustering patterns observed in the PCA biplot, several genotypes—specifically ‘AA-7’, ‘AA-12’, ‘AA-15’, ‘AA-18’, ‘AA-19’, ‘AA-20’, ‘AA-23’, ‘AA-28’, and ‘AA-30’—were positioned outside the 95% confidence ellipse (Fig. 4). This ellipse delineates the multivariate region in which approximately 95% of the genotype scores are expected to occur under the assumption of multivariate normality. Consequently, genotypes located beyond this boundary can be interpreted as statistically divergent from the central distribution of the population, indicating that their overall trait profiles differ markedly from the majority of the evaluated genotypes.

The displacement of these genotypes outside the ellipse suggests the presence of distinctive combinations of morphological, biochemical, or mineral traits, or alternatively the occurrence of extreme values in one or more variables that separate them from the main genotype cloud. From a methodological perspective, the use of confidence ellipses in PCA provides an effective graphical approach for detecting such multivariate outliers and for visualizing the extent of phenotypic variability within the dataset. Identifying these divergent genotypes is particularly valuable in germplasm evaluation studies, as they may represent genetically unique or agronomically promising materials with potential relevance for breeding and selection programs47,48.

Heat map analysis (HMA)

Heat map analysis revealed clear clustering patterns among the studied genotypes (Fig. 5). The hierarchical clustering procedure applied to the variables revealed distinct grouping patterns among the evaluated traits. The detailed cluster formation stages of the variables are presented in Supplementary Table S2. The similarity distance among variables ranged from 0.74 to 285.55, indicating a wide range of relationships among pomological, colorimetric, biochemical, and mineral attributes. The closest association was observed between fruit width and fruit length, which clustered together with the lowest similarity distance (0.74), confirming that fruit dimensional traits are strongly interconnected and jointly contribute to overall fruit morphology. As the clustering stages progressed, the similarity among variables gradually decreased while the level of dissimilarity increased, reflecting the multidimensional structure of the evaluated traits. The most distant relationship was detected between fruit width and the color parameter a*, with a similarity distance of 285.35, indicating that fruit size traits and redness-related color attributes are largely independent variables in the studied genotypes. Overall, the clustering pattern suggests that morphological traits tend to group separately from colorimetric and biochemical variables, highlighting the complex and multivariate nature of fruit quality characteristics in strawberry tree genotypes.

Hierarchical cluster analysis demonstrated substantial variation in similarity among the evaluated strawberry tree genotypes, with similarity coefficients ranging from 1.43 to 13.99 (Supplementary Table S3). The highest degree of resemblance was observed between ‘AA-2’ and ‘AA-30’, which were grouped together at a similarity coefficient of 1.43, reflecting a closely related phenotypic profile. Conversely, the largest dissimilarity occurred between ‘AA-1’ and ‘AA-4’, with a similarity coefficient of 13.99, indicating pronounced divergence across the assessed pomological, biochemical, and mineral characteristics. The broad range of similarity values confirms the existence of considerable phenotypic diversity within the investigated germplasm. The dendrogram further provided a clear representation of the relationships among genotypes and facilitated their classification into several distinct clusters. Genotypes sharing comparable trait combinations were associated at lower distance levels, whereas more divergent genotypes were incorporated into clusters only at higher similarity thresholds. This clustering pattern emphasizes the heterogeneous structure of the population and suggests the occurrence of unique phenotypic configurations in certain genotypes. Such diversity constitutes a valuable genetic resource for conservation efforts and offers important opportunities for the identification and selection of superior genotypes in breeding programs targeting improved fruit quality, nutritional value, and functional characteristics.

The heterogeneous clustering pattern observed in the multivariate analyses suggests that the studied population is composed of distinct phenotypic groups rather than a uniform assemblage of individuals. Considering that the sampled genotypes originated from different geographic locations and altitudinal gradients (59–455 m) within the Defne district, the observed differentiation likely reflects the broad phenotypic diversity present in the natural population.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated that naturally growing strawberry tree genotypes from Hatay, Türkiye, possess substantial phenotypic, biochemical, and mineral diversity, confirming the species as a valuable genetic resource for future horticultural utilization. Significant genotype-dependent variation was recorded across pomological traits, fruit color parameters, antioxidant capacity, phenolic and flavonoid contents, anthocyanin accumulation, and mineral composition, indicating broad opportunities for targeted selection. In particular, ‘AA-7’ was distinguished by superior fruit size and weight, ‘AA-19’ by its outstanding macro-mineral accumulation, ‘AA-14’ by the highest antioxidant capacity, ‘AA-3’ by elevated phenolic content, and ‘AA-23’ by superior anthocyanin concentration. Multivariate analyses further revealed that fruit size traits were strongly coordinated, color parameters followed structured maturity-related patterns, and mineral accumulation exhibited an integrated network behavior. The identification of several statistically divergent genotypes also emphasized the presence of highly distinctive materials within the studied population. From a breeding perspective, genotypes ‘AA-7’, ‘AA-14’, ‘AA-19’, ‘AA-3’, and ‘AA-23’ should be considered priority candidates for future selection programs because of their superior fruit size, antioxidant capacity, mineral composition, phenolic content, and anthocyanin concentration, respectively. In contrast, the genetically and phenotypically distinct genotypes identified through multivariate analyses should be prioritized for germplasm conservation to ensure the preservation of the broad diversity present within natural populations. The combined conservation and utilization of these elite and divergent genotypes will provide valuable genetic resources for developing improved strawberry tree cultivars with enhanced fruit quality, nutritional value, and functional food potential.
