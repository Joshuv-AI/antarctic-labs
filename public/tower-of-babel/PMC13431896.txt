Case report: fulminant myocarditis with biopsy-proven eosinophilic infiltration and burned-out myocardial pathology at autopsy
Authors: Rui Katano, Atsushi Tada, Hirokazu Sugino, Toshiyuki Nagai, Toshihisa Anzai, Doralisa Morrone, Jabir Abdullakutty, Deepti Ranganathan

Introduction

Fulminant myocarditis is an acute myocarditis characterized by severe haemodynamic compromise requiring inotropes and/or mechanical circulatory support (MCS).1 Among the spectrum of myocarditis, establishing a histological diagnosis of eosinophilic or giant cell myocarditis by endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) is particularly important, as these subtypes have critical implications for the timely initiation of immunosuppressive therapy.2 We report a fatal case of fulminant myocarditis progressing to burned-out myocardial pathology despite intensive supportive care.

Case presentation

The case was a 54-year-old woman with a history of hypertension and dyslipidemia who presented to a local clinic with several days of fever and dyspnoea. She was hypotensive, with markedly elevated cardiac enzymes. Electrocardiography (ECG) showed ST-segment elevation in leads I, aVL, and V4–6. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed regional left ventricular (LV) systolic dysfunction involving the lateral and posterior wall with pericardial effusion. She subsequently experienced cardiac arrest and was transferred to another hospital on mechanical ventilation and venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO). Although acute myocardial infarction was initially suspected, the absence of mechanical complications and significant coronary stenosis on angiography made this diagnosis unlikely, raising suspicion for fulminant myocarditis. After Impella CP insertion, she was transferred to our hospital.

Upon arrival, her blood pressure was 77/74 mmHg, heart rate 77 beats/min, and body temperature 36.8°C. She was supported with VA-ECMO at 2.9 L/min and an Impella CP providing 1.7 L/min at performance level P3, without catecholamine. Laboratory testing demonstrated markedly elevated cardiac biomarkers, including creatine kinase of 1518 U/L, troponin T of 33 790 ng/L, and N-terminal prohormone of brain natriuretic peptide of 24 624 pg/mL. The peripheral eosinophil count was <500 cells/mm3, and there was no history of recent drug use or vaccination. Additionally, tests for antinuclear and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies were negative. Computed tomography and chest radiography revealed bilateral pulmonary infiltration (Figure 1A and B), and ECG showed sinus rhythm, low voltage, and a wide QRS complex (Figure 2A). TTE showed global LV akinesis, severe right ventricular (RV) hypokinesis, circumferential LV wall thickening with marked cavity reduction, and minimal pericardial effusion (see Supplementary material online, Video S1). EMB obtained from the RV revealed myocardial necrosis with inflammatory cell infiltration, including prominent degranulating eosinophils (Figure 3A–C), consistent with eosinophilic myocarditis (EM).

In accordance with the guidelines,3 high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg/day for 3 days) was initiated, resulting in an initial reduction in cardiac enzyme levels (Figure 4). Because the LV cavity was nearly obliterated, leading to suction events, the Impella performance level was reduced to P1. On Day 4, ECG showed a slight increase in R-wave amplitude (Figure 2B), and TTE demonstrated modest improvement in LV wall motion and wall thickness (see Supplementary material online, Video S2), prompting transition to maintenance prednisolone (1.0 mg/kg/day).

From Day 5 onward, cardiac enzymes rose again with worsening pulmonary congestion (Figure 1C and 4), and continuous haemodiafiltration was initiated for acute kidney injury. The Impella CP was upgraded to an Impella 5.5 due to frequent suction events caused by septal interference and the device-related moderate aortic regurgitation, which limited effective LV unloading. Despite adjunctive therapy with dobutamine (3.0 μg/kg/min) and inhaled nitric oxide to facilitate left ventricular unloading, the haemodynamic response was inadequate, and the Impella 5.5 achieved <1.0 L/min of flow at performance level P3.

Given the rapidly progressive course and lack of recovery despite intensive support for EM, a more aggressive immunosuppressive strategy was pursued for suspected severe immune-mediated myocarditis. Accordingly, tacrolimus was initiated on Day 6 with a target trough concentration of 10–15 ng/mL, and mycophenolate mofetil was added on Day 7. Cardiac enzyme levels began to decline from Day 7, and the Impella 5.5 flow increased to ∼1.5 L/min at performance level P4. However, despite achieving the target tacrolimus trough concentration by Day 10, there was no recovery of electrical or mechanical cardiac activity, and ECG and TTE continued to demonstrate an asystolic state (Figure 2C; see Supplementary material online, Video S3). Advanced therapies, including durable mechanical support as a bridge to transplantation, were also considered. However, profound biventricular failure and progressive multiorgan dysfunction made meaningful recovery unlikely even with further escalation of support. Given the absence of myocardial recovery, the limited potential for cardiac recovery was explained to the patient’s family. Following careful discussion, a decision was made to discontinue MCS, and the patient died on Day 27.

Autopsy revealed a heart weight of 250 g, without cardiomegaly. Macroscopically, the heart showed biventricular dilatation with marked myocardial thinning (LV wall 10 mm, RV wall 1 mm) and a softened myocardial texture, without mural thrombi (Figure 5A and B). Microscopically, there was near-complete loss of viable cardiomyocytes, with interstitial fibrosis and inflammation composed of lymphocytes and histiocytes (Figure 5C–F). No eosinophils or multinucleated giant cells were identified. Owing to the advanced degree of myocardial degeneration, the sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodal cells were indistinct.

Discussion

We report a fatal case of fulminant myocarditis managed with MCS and immunosuppressive therapy. There is a case report of a patient with fulminant myocarditis who survived after 56 h of cardiac arrest supported by VA-ECMO.4 In the current case, despite intensive supportive and disease-directed treatment, the patient developed persistent electrical silence and ultimately died. Autopsy revealed extensive loss of cardiomyocytes and widespread replacement fibrosis, indicating that irreversible myocardial destruction had already occurred and corresponded closely with the clinical course. The need for MCS other than Impella, QRS duration >120 ms on initial ECG, and biventricular dysfunction are considered predictors of poor prognosis in myocarditis,5 all of which were present in the current case.

Mechanical unloading with Impella reduces LV wall stress and may attenuate inflammatory responses and myofibroblast activation.6 The PROPELLA concept has therefore been proposed to promote myocardial recovery through prolonged unloading in fulminant myocarditis. However, in the present case, frequent suction events, septal interference, and device-related aortic regurgitation limited effective unloading despite escalation to Impella 5.5. Although a transient decline in cardiac biomarkers was observed, this did not translate into electrical or mechanical recovery, suggesting that extensive myocardial loss had already progressed beyond a reversible stage.

Although eosinophilic infiltration was observed in myocardial tissue, the clinical course was unusually severe and atypical for EM, with rapidly progressive biventricular failure, refractory haemodynamics, and persistent asystole, and giant cell myocarditis was considered in the differential diagnosis. Importantly, giant cells were absent in the biopsy specimen; however, sampling error remains a significant limitation, with a reported sensitivity of 80%–93% within 2–4 weeks of disease onset.7 A significant diagnostic challenge lies in the fact that 24.1% of histologically confirmed cases do not present with peripheral eosinophilia, potentially leading to the underdiagnosis of this condition without an endomyocardial biopsy. Moreover, while EM is generally regarded as more responsive to corticosteroid therapy than giant cell myocarditis, several reports describe that EM is diagnosed only at autopsy, implying that the treatment responsiveness may be heterogeneous.8

This case highlights the lethal pathophysiology of fulminant myocarditis, characterized by massive myocardial loss, for which MCS and immunosuppressive therapy may be insufficient once irreversible damage has occurred. Earlier identification of patients at risk for extensive myocardial destruction and refinement of strategies for timely therapeutic escalation and goal setting remain important challenges for future investigation.
