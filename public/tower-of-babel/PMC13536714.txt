Preferences for postoperative exercise in patients after total hip or knee arthroplasty: a discrete choice experiment
Authors: Yue Wang, Mengnv Zhou, Jie Wu, Qiongling Zhan, Caixia Liu, Zhen Wang, Yifei Cheng, Qiangqiang Han, Hui Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative exercise is associated with important benefits for patients undergoing total hip or knee arthroplasty, yet adherence to prescribed exercise programs remains suboptimal. Understanding patient preferences is essential for designing patient-centered interventions. This study aimed to assess patient preferences for postoperative rehabilitation exercise programs and determine the relative importance of specific program attributes.MethodsFrom March to July 2025, we conducted a cross-sectional survey using a discrete choice experiment (DCE) design at the Department of Joint Surgery, First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China. A total of 295 patients who had undergone primary elective THA or TKA were included. The survey was administered from postoperative day 2 onwards, at which point participants had already commenced rehabilitation. Participants were asked to choose between hypothetical rehabilitation scenarios defined by five attributes: pain severity experienced during rehabilitation exercises, presence of a healthcare provider, exercise intensity, frequency, and duration. Conditional logit and mixed logit models were employed to estimate preference weights and investigate preference heterogeneity across subgroups.ResultsPain severity during rehabilitation exercises was identified as the most critical determinant of choice, with patients strongly preferring mild or moderate pain over severe pain (OR: 2.41, 95% CI: 1.76–3.07 and OR: 3.84, 95% CI: 2.88–4.79, respectively). This was followed by the presence of a healthcare provider (OR: 1.44, 95% CI: 1.24–1.63) and exercise intensity. Patients expressed a strong preference for rehabilitation programs characterized by mild-to-moderate pain, professional guidance, and moderate exercise intensity. Conversely, exercise frequency and duration had a relatively limited impact on decision-making. Subgroup analyses stratified by gender and age showed largely consistent preference patterns across populations, with variations in effect magnitude.ConclusionsPatients prioritize safety-driven rehabilitation protocols that balance functional recovery with physical comfort, specifically favoring effective pain management and professional support. These findings provide empirical evidence to guide the development of patient-centered, precision-oriented rehabilitation pathways that may enhance long-term adherence and clinical outcomes.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15211-9.

Introduction

With the global aging population continuing to expand, the prevalence of osteoarthritis (OA) has increased substantially, positioning it as a predominant chronic illness and a critical burden on healthcare systems worldwide [1]. Research indicates that over 500 million individuals worldwide are currently affected by OA, with the prevalence reaching as high as 50% among those aged 60 and older [2]. Predominantly manifesting as knee and hip osteoarthritis, the condition is characterized by joint stiffness and pain that typically intensifies during physical exertion [3]. As the disease progresses, restricted range of motion profoundly impairs patients’ quality of life (QoL), functional independence, and psychological well-being, thereby imposing a substantial socioeconomic burden on patients, their families, and society at large [4]. For individuals with end-stage OA, joint replacement surgery remains the definitive and primary therapeutic intervention. Global population aging and increasing rates of obesity have contributed to a rising demand for joint replacement surgery among older adults and individuals with obesity [5, 6]. Moreover, longer life expectancy accompanied by sustained health and functional activity has increased expectations of productivity in later life, thereby further amplifying demand for joint replacement procedures [7]. In Germany, the incidence of total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is projected to increase steadily from 2016 to 2040, with the greatest rise in TKA observed among patients aged 40–69 years, while the largest increase in THA is expected among older adults [8]. These trends indicate that the aging population represents a major driver of demand for joint replacement surgery. In addition, findings from Iqbal et al. highlight the high prevalence of obesity among patients undergoing joint replacement, underscoring obesity as a key factor influencing joint health and postoperative outcomes [9]. Based on data from the National Inpatient Sample database (1990–2003) and projections from the United States Census Bureau, demand for primary THA and primary TKA is expected to increase by 174% and 673%, respectively, by 2030 [10].

Postoperative exercise is associated with important benefits for patients undergoing total joint arthroplasty (TJA), including improvements in joint function, pain relief, quality of life, and reduction in complications [11, 12]. However, no single postoperative exercise regime has been shown to be clearly superior to another [11], highlighting that how programes are designed and delivered, particularly in ways that align with patients’ preferences, may be as important as the exercise itself. In practice, this design gap has real consequences: adherence to functional exercise programes among TJA recipients remains suboptimal, with rates at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively reported to be (62.60 ± 11.87)%, (57.47 ± 11.16)%, and (52.67 ± 10.33)%, respectively [13]. Another study reported that only 25% of patients undergoing TJA were fully adherent to their prescribed functional exercises [14]. Importantly, responsibility for this adherence gap lies not solely with patients but is shared with the rehabilitation team, whose role is to design programs that patients can feasibly and willingly follow [11]. This poor adherence is influenced by a multitude of factors, including insufficient knowledge about exercise techniques, low recognition of the importance of rehabilitation, fear of pain, anxiety regarding joint displacement (or dislocation), lack of physical stamina, and insufficient support from healthcare professionals. This points to a fundamental need to reorient programe design around patients’ actual preferences and lived priorities, a person-centred approach that places the patient’s perspective at the heart of rehabilitation planning [15, 16]. Geidl et al. [17] demonstrated that exercise programs tailored to individuals’ physical activity preferences may represent a promising, patient-centered strategy for supporting participation in physical activity.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to quantify the preferences of patients following hip or knee arthroplasty for postoperative exercise programs and to determine the relative importance of key attributes, such as pain severity experienced during rehabilitation exercises, presence of a healthcare provider, exercise intensity, frequency, and duration. This study was conducted from the patient’s perspective to assist clinicians and healthcare policymakers in designing patient-centered rehabilitation services that support adherence to postoperative exercise programs. To achieve this, we employed a discrete choice experiment (DCE). Unlike traditional satisfaction surveys that evaluate attributes in isolation, DCE is a stated-preference method grounded in Lancaster’s characteristics theory of demand and random utility theory [18]. It mimics real-world decision-making by requiring respondents to make trade-offs between competing service attributes, thereby providing a more rigorous estimation of utility and willingness to participate [19].

Methods

The design, conduct, and reporting of this DCE adhered to the checklist of Good Research Practices for Conjoint Analysis Task Force published by the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) [18, 20] and detailed adherence to the ISPOR Conjoint Analysis Checklist is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Identifying attributes and levels

Establishing attributes and their associated levels is a critical step in the design of a DCE. Attributes and levels are typically identified through several recognized methods, including literature reviews, in-depth interviews, priority setting, focus group discussions, and expert consultations [21]. Initially, we conducted a literature review to identify potential factors influencing patient adherence to functional exercise. The search identified the following candidate factors: postoperative pain, guidance from healthcare professionals, exercise intensity, frequency, duration, rehabilitation exercise instructional materials, social and family support, psychological factors, and QoL [22–25]. Subsequently, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 10 patients who were asked to list and rank the factors most relevant to their exercise participation. The six highest-ranked factors were pain severity experienced during rehabilitation exercises, presence of a healthcare provider, exercise intensity, frequency, duration, and rehabilitation exercise instructional materials; social and family support, psychological factors, and QoL were ranked lower and were therefore not carried forward. Finally, we consulted an expert panel comprising one orthopedic surgeon, two rehabilitation specialists, and one nurse to review the six candidate attributes. The panel noted that rehabilitation exercise instructional materials substantially overlapped in clinical function with healthcare provider guidance, and recommended excluding the former to avoid attribute redundancy in the DCE design. By synthesizing the findings from these three stages, the five final attributes were determined: pain severity experienced during rehabilitation exercises, presence of a healthcare provider, exercise intensity, frequency, and duration, as shown in Table 1.

Experimental design

The construction of choice sets is a critical step in DCE design. Following the determination of attributes and levels, hypothetical choice sets comprising various combinations of these attributes were generated. In this process, respondents were required to trade off the benefits and downsides of two or more alternatives within a set to select their preferred option.

With five attributes included in this study, a full factorial design would yield 162 distinct profiles (3 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 3), resulting in a theoretical total of 13,041 choice sets. Given the potential cognitive burden on patients, a D-optimal efficient design was employed using the Custom Design module in JMP Pro software (Version 13.0; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) to select a statistically efficient subset of profiles. The D-optimal criterion maximises the determinant of the information matrix [det(X′X)], thereby minimising the generalised variance of the parameter estimates for a given number of runs [26]. A main-effects-only model was specified, as the primary objective was to estimate the utility weight for each attribute level rather than interaction effects. The coordinate-exchange algorithm was applied with multiple random starts to reduce the risk of converging on a local optimum. The resulting design comprised 16 choice sets, each presenting two hypothetical rehabilitation profiles (Option A and Option B) defined simultaneously by all five attributes. The D-efficiency of the final design was 63.5%, indicating adequate precision for main-effects estimation given the run-size constraint [27]. All 162 possible profiles were considered clinically plausible, as no attribute combination was logically contradictory or physiologically impossible in the context of postoperative rehabilitation; therefore, no profiles were excluded prior to design generation. The complete design matrix is provided in Supplementary Table S2. To further alleviate the cognitive and response burden, particularly for elderly participants, the 16 choice sets were divided into two blocks of 8 tasks each, with participants randomly assigned to one block, as shown in Fig. 1. In addition to the standard choice tasks, a dominance test was included to assess response validity and ensure participants’ comprehension.

Population

Patients were recruited between March and July 2025 from the Department of Joint Surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 18 years or older; (2) underwent primary elective THA or TKA; and (3) provided informed consent to participate in the study.

Patients were excluded if they met the following criteria: (1) diagnosed with deep vein thrombosis (DVT) either preoperatively or postoperatively; or (2) had comorbid severe cardiac, cerebral, hepatic, or renal dysfunction, or other significant organic diseases.

The survey was administered from postoperative day 2 onwards, at which point participants had already commenced postoperative rehabilitation exercises.

Sample size and data collection

In healthcare research, the rule of thumb is widely applied to determine sample size [28]. According to this rule, the minimum sample size (N) depends on the number of choice tasks (t), the number of alternatives (a), and the number of analysis units (c), expressed by the equation N≥500c/(t×a).

In this study, the sample size was calculated using this rule of thumb based on a main-effects model. The DCE questionnaire comprised two blocks. For each block, the number of choice tasks was t = 8, the number of alternatives was a = 2, and the largest number of levels for any attribute (analysis units) was c = 3. Therefore, the minimum acceptable sample size per block was calculated as n≥(500 × 3)/(8 × 2) = 93.75. To account for a potential 20% rate of invalid questionnaires, the required sample size was adjusted to 117.2 participants per block. Consequently, the total target sample size was determined to be N ≥ 2 × 117.2 ≈ 235 participants. The survey was administered face-to-face by two trained research assistants. Prior to data collection, a single structured training session was conducted, covering an introduction to the questionnaire, an explanation of the discrete choice experiment methodology, and standardised instructions on how to guide participants through the choice tasks. This training was designed to ensure consistency in questionnaire administration and to minimise interviewer-induced response bias.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using StataMP 17 software. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while those with a non-normal distribution were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Choice data were analyzed using a conditional logit model as a baseline, followed by a mixed logit model to account for preference heterogeneity. These models were used to estimate the impact of attributes and levels on patients’ choices.

Results

A total of 304 questionnaires were initially collected. Of these, 9 were excluded due to failure to pass the dominance task, incomplete data, or withdrawal from the survey. Consequently, 295 patients were included in the final analysis. These patients were evenly distributed across the two survey blocks, yielding a total of 4,720 observations derived from the choice sets.

Characteristics of respondents

An overview of the participant characteristics is provided in Table 2. The mean age of the participants was 64.12 years (SD 9.81), and the majority were female (194/295, 65.76%). The majority of patients reported a low income (66.44% with an average monthly income of ≤ 2,000 RMB), a characteristic likely attributable to the high proportion of farmers (53.9%) in the study population. The population was predominantly married (91.86%), with a mean number of children of 3.21 ± 0.65. The mean number of comorbidities was 2.32 ± 1.00. The average duration of joint pain prior to surgery was 51.40 ± 61.82 months.

Preferences for postoperative rehabilitation attributes

The mixed logit model results, as shown in Table 3, indicated clear patient preferences regarding the attributes of rehabilitation exercise following joint replacement. Specifically, pain severity emerged as the strongest predictor of option utility, with participants favoring scenarios associated with mild (Coefficient = 0.88, SE = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.61–1.15; OR: 2.41, 95% CI: 1.76–3.07) and moderate pain (Coefficient = 1.34, SE = 0.13, 95% CI: 1.10–1.59; OR: 3.84, 95% CI: 2.88–4.79) over severe pain (p < 0.001 for both). The involvement of a healthcare provider was also viewed favorably (Coefficient = 0.36, SE = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.23–0.50; OR: 1.44, 95% CI: 1.24–1.63; p < 0.001). For exercise intensity, moderate (Coefficient = 0.26, SE = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.09–0.43; OR: 1.29, 95% CI: 1.08–1.51; p < 0.01) but not light intensity was significantly preferred over vigorous activity. Patients showed a slight aversion to increasing exercise frequency from once to twice weekly (Coefficient = − 0.24, SE = 0.11, 95% CI: − 0.46 to − 0.02; OR: 0.79, 95% CI: 0.61–0.96; p < 0.05), while exercising three times per week did not result in a significant change in utility. The duration of each session (whether 0–20 min or 20–40 min) did not significantly influence patient choices compared to the reference range of 40–60 min.

Assessment of model fit

Based on the model fit statistics presented in Table 4, the mixed logit model demonstrated superior performance over the conditional logit model, as indicated by a higher log-likelihood value (-1415.35 vs. -1493.97) and lower AIC (2868.70) and BIC (2991.43) values. These results suggest that the mixed logit model better accounts for preference heterogeneity among patients undergoing joint arthroplasty in the context of rehabilitation exercise programs. Consequently, the mixed logit model was selected for further subgroup analyses.

Heterogeneity in preferences

Subgroup analyses stratified by gender and age showed largely consistent preference patterns across populations, with variations in effect magnitude, as shown in Table 5.

Among males, both mild and moderate pain severity were strongly preferred (Coefficient = 0.89, SE = 0.25; Coefficient = 1.50, SE = 0.24; both p < 0.001). Females exhibited similar preference directions, with slightly smaller coefficients for moderate pain (Coefficient = 1.28, SE = 0.15; p < 0.001). In both genders, involvement of a healthcare provider significantly increased choice probability (male: Coefficient = 0.41, SE = 0.13, p < 0.01; female: Coefficient = 0.34, SE = 0.08, p < 0.001).

Preferences by age group followed broadly similar directions. Participants aged < 60 years preferred mild and moderate pain levels (Coefficient = 0.89 and 0.95, respectively; both p < 0.001), whereas those aged ≥ 60 years showed a similar coefficient for mild pain (Coefficient = 0.87, p < 0.001) and a larger coefficient for moderate pain (Coefficient = 1.52, p < 0.001). The presence of a healthcare provider was positively valued in both age groups (< 60 years: Coefficient = 0.24, SE = 0.12, p < 0.05; ≥60 years: Coefficient = 0.41, SE = 0.09, p < 0.001).

Intensity preferences were modest across subgroups. Moderate intensity had significant positive coefficients among males (Coefficient = 0.29, SE = 0.15, p < 0.05), females (Coefficient = 0.25, SE = 0.11, p < 0.05), and participants aged ≥ 60 years (Coefficient = 0.28, SE = 0.10, p < 0.01). Light intensity was significant only among participants aged ≥ 60 years (Coefficient = 0.23, SE = 0.12, p < 0.05). Frequency and duration did not show consistent effects across subgroup strata.

Marginal effects analysis

Figure 2 illustrates the marginal effects of key variables on the outcome of interest. The results demonstrate that moderate and mild pain levels are associated with the largest positive marginal effects. The presence of a healthcare provider also yielded a notable positive marginal effect. By contrast, increases in frequency and duration of exercise, particularly frequencies of twice weekly, show a reduced or even negative marginal effect. Differences by exercise intensity reveal modest positive effects for both light and moderate levels. These findings suggest that tailoring interventions to pain severity and incorporating professional guidance yield the greatest benefits, whereas further increasing exercise frequency or duration does not confer additional gains.

Discussion

In this study, the preferences of arthroplasty patients for postoperative rehabilitation exercises were investigated using a DCE. Our findings showed that pain was the most important attribute, followed by guidance from a healthcare provider and exercise intensity. Patients expressed a strong preference for rehabilitation programs involving mild or moderate pain. This highlights that pain management strategies must be considered a core element when designing rehabilitation protocols for the postoperative recovery period. The results also indicated that exercise programs guided by professionals were significantly more attractive to patients than unguided alternatives, reflecting a strong demand for safety and efficacy during the recovery process. Regarding exercise intensity, participants exhibited a clear preference for moderate intensity over high intensity. This aligns with the demographic profile of the sample, which was characterized by an older population (mean age 64.12 years) and a high proportion of farmers (53.9%); these groups tend to prioritize rehabilitation modalities with lower physical risk and discomfort. Subgroup analyses suggested variations in coefficient magnitude. Within the female subgroup, twice-weekly exercise was associated with lower utility than once-weekly exercise. Among patients aged ≥ 60 years, both light- and moderate-intensity exercise had significant positive coefficients relative to vigorous intensity.

The findings of this study exhibit both similarities to and differences from previous DCEs regarding rehabilitation preferences. McKibben et al. [29] conducted a DCE among patients receiving physical therapy after lower-extremity fractures and examined preferences for type of therapy, total cost, therapy duration, residual pain, and post-therapy mobility. In contrast, the present study focuses specifically on postoperative exercise following primary elective THA or TKA and evaluates modifiable exercise-delivery attributes, including pain experienced during exercise, the presence of a healthcare provider, exercise intensity, weekly exercise frequency, and session duration. By extending DCE evidence to a Chinese arthroplasty population, this study provides arthroplasty-specific evidence for designing patient-centered postoperative exercise programs after joint replacement. Existing literature has indicated that pain is a critical determinant in treatment decision-making [30]. The avoidance of severe pain during rehabilitation reflects not only a pursuit of comfort but, more profoundly, a demand for safety. This observation is consistent with the results of Li et al. [16], who similarly found that patients prioritized ‘high safety’ as the most important attribute when investigating exercise preferences among individuals with depression. The significant preference for the involvement of a healthcare provider observed in this study is in line with findings from Paul et al. [31] in patients with Parkinson’s disease and Shields et al. [32] in the context of cardiac rehabilitation, consistently highlighting the strong patient demand for professional guidance. Regarding exercise intensity, patients in this study favored moderate intensity while avoiding vigorous intensity. This finding aligns with the conclusion of Geidl et al. [17], who reported a preference for light-to-moderate training among stroke survivors. However, it stands in contrast to the results of Aboagye et al. [33], which indicated a preference for vigorous aerobic training among a working population with non-specific low back pain. Additionally, a systematic review regarding inpatient physical rehabilitation experiences noted that stroke patients expressed interest in high-intensity and high-volume exercise regimens [34]. This discrepancy in preferences may be attributed to the heterogeneity of study populations. Our participants were predominantly elderly (mean age: 64.12 years); compared to working-age populations, this demographic is more inclined to pursue moderate rehabilitation within the boundaries of ensuring safety and avoiding pain exacerbation. The coefficient for twice-weekly exercise was negative relative to once-weekly exercise, consistent with the direction of findings reported by Lokkerbol et al. [15] and Aboagye et al. [33]. However, the coefficient for three times weekly was not statistically significant. Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as indicating a general aversion to higher exercise frequency. Regarding session duration, no significant influence on preferences was observed in the current study. In contrast, Geidl et al. [17] found that stroke survivors significantly favored shorter exercise sessions (β = 0.284, P < 0.001). This suggests that rehabilitation preferences are context-dependent and may vary across different disease and cultural settings.

The findings of this study have clear implications for the design of postoperative rehabilitation programs and clinical practice following arthroplasty. Pain severity emerged as the primary driver of patients’ choices regarding rehabilitation programs. This suggests that postoperative care should prioritize pain management and patient comfort, incorporating strategies such as optimized early analgesia, progressive training intensities, and contextualized pain education. These measures are essential to improve treatment adherence and minimize the risk of program discontinuation. The strong preference for professional guidance suggests that close guidance by medical staff is a key attribute of the treatment plan. By establishing standardized programs and ensuring continuity of care from hospital to home, healthcare providers can facilitate adherence and mitigate patient anxiety regarding exercise. The preference for moderate-intensity training and the aversion to excessive exercise frequency suggest that high-burden regimens may act as barriers to participation. Future rehabilitation programs should prioritize a moderate load principle with gradual progression to make the treatment attractive to patients. Subgroup analyses revealed variations in preferences across different patient demographics, which suggests that future intervention strategies should adopt a more granular, precision-rehabilitation approach by integrating factors such as age, gender, and et al. Collectively, our findings highlight the necessity of developing exercise programs that are both patient-centered and safety-driven. In terms of resource allocation and policy formulation, priority should be given to investing in pain management systems and expanding the coverage of professional guidance. Such measures are vital for effectively enhancing the acceptance of and long-term adherence to rehabilitation following arthroplasty.

Limitations and future perspectives

Although the sample size of this study was adequate, the participants were predominantly elderly (mean age: 64.12 years) and rural residents (with farmers accounting for 53.9%), most of whom had a low income. Consequently, the findings regarding rehabilitation preferences following arthroplasty may not be generalizable to broader populations. Future research should include patient groups with more diverse geographical and socioeconomic backgrounds to verify the generalizability of these preferences.

As the DCE is an experimental design grounded in hypothetical scenarios, the elicited preferences may diverge from the actual decisions patients make in real-world clinical settings. This potential discrepancy is a recognized limitation in most rehabilitation preference studies [29, 33, 35]. Although we attempted to minimize this bias through detailed attribute descriptions, actual patient behavior in real-life rehabilitation contexts may still differ. Furthermore, to maximize information recovery, an ‘opt-out’ option was not included in this study; this forced-choice design may have introduced a degree of selection bias. Future research could consider combining revealed preference data or employing mixed-methods approaches to validate the accuracy of these hypothetical choices. Finally, the final DCE questionnaire was not independently pilot-tested before formal administration. Although the attributes and levels were developed through a literature review, patient interviews, and expert consultation, this may have limited our ability to identify potential difficulties in interpreting the final choice tasks. Future studies should incorporate cognitive interviews or pilot testing to assess the comprehensibility and face validity of the final questionnaire before full administration.

Several additional factors related to the study population warrant acknowledgement. The survey was administered from postoperative day 2 onwards, and all participants had already commenced rehabilitation at the time of data collection. Preferences may differ across the postoperative trajectory: patients in the early recovery phase typically experience higher pain levels and greater functional dependence, which may heighten their sensitivity to pain-related attributes and the presence of healthcare providers compared with patients at later stages of recovery. As data were not stratified by postoperative timepoint, the extent to which preferences may shift over the course of rehabilitation remains unknown and represents an important direction for future research. Additionally, patients with a history of contralateral joint replacement were not excluded. Prior surgical experience may confer greater familiarity with rehabilitation demands and potentially influence attribute preferences. However, contralateral replacement history was not recorded as a covariate, precluding formal adjustment for this factor. Future studies should consider collecting and controlling for this information to better isolate the preferences of first-time versus experienced arthroplasty patients.

Future longitudinal studies should investigate the association between preferences elicited via DCE and actual long-term adherence to rehabilitation programs, as well as clinical functional outcomes such as pain and range of motion. Such research is essential to verify whether the critical attributes identified in this study, specifically pain control and professional guidance, truly translate into improved patient outcomes in real-world practice. Furthermore, given the increasing prevalence of telemedicine, it is necessary for future DCE research to specifically incorporate and evaluate the utility of various telerehabilitation technologies, such as guidance via mobile applications and wearable devices. Assessing the trade-offs between these digital interventions and traditional face-to-face therapy will provide a vital evidence base for developing efficient and acceptable hybrid rehabilitation models.

Conclusions

Based on a DCE, this study systematically quantified the preferences of arthroplasty patients regarding key attributes of postoperative rehabilitation. The findings reveal a distinct inclination towards rehabilitation protocols that offer clear, perceptible benefits alongside a moderate physical burden. This suggests that postoperative clinical pathways must strike a balance between effective pain control, professional support, and moderate exercise load. Overall, this study provides empirical evidence from the patient’s perspective to inform the optimization of post-arthroplasty interventions, facilitating a shift toward more patient-centered, precision-oriented, and highly acceptable care models. Future validation among broader populations, coupled with the integration of preference data into clinical decision-making processes, will be instrumental in further enhancing the effectiveness of rehabilitation services.
