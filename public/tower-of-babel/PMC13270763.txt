Prevalence and clinical determinants of diabetic gastroparesis in type 2 diabetes: a symptom- and scintigraphy-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Marwa M Abokresha, Mena Adley Wahba Tousb, Manal Esayed Ez Elden, Waleed Ahmed Diab, Bahaa O Taha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic gastroparesis (DG) is a frequently underdiagnosed complication of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D), characterized by delayed gastric emptying without mechanical obstruction. Its nonspecific symptoms often lead to delayed diagnosis and suboptimal management.ObjectiveTo assess the prevalence and clinical predictors of DG in patients with T2D, and to investigate the relationship between gastrointestinal symptoms and objectively measured delayed gastric emptying.MethodsA cross-sectional study included 130 randomly selected patients with T2D attending a tertiary care outpatient clinic. Comprehensive clinical assessment, metabolic profiling, and screening for diabetes-related complications were performed. Symptoms suggestive of DG were assessed using the Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index (GCSI), while delayed gastric emptying was objectively confirmed using gastric emptying scintigraphy (GES) with a standardized low-fat radiolabeled meal.ResultsDelayed gastric emptying was observed in 13.1% of participants. Patients with delayed gastric emptying had significantly higher body mass index (BMI), longer duration of diabetes, and poorer glycemic control compared with those without delayed emptying (all p < 0.05). Multivariate analysis identified HbA1c (OR 6.99), BMI (OR 3.89), and duration of diabetes (OR 2.75) as independent predictors of delayed gastric emptying. Symptom-based gastroparesis according to GCSI showed limited agreement with scintigraphic delayed gastric emptying.ConclusionDG appears to be a clinically relevant but potentially underrecognized complication of T2D. Poor glycemic control, obesity, and longer duration of diabetes are associated with delayed gastric emptying. Limited agreement between symptom severity and objectively measured gastric emptying supports the continued role of gastric emptying scintigraphy as the diagnostic reference standard.Clinical trial registryClinicalTrials.gov (U.S. National Library of Medicine registry) Clinical trial numberNCT05584462 Keywords: Diabetic gastroparesis, T2D, Gastric scintigraphy

Introduction

Gastroparesis is a persistent gastrointestinal motility condition characterized by delayed gastric emptying without any mechanical obstruction. It is characterized by a lack of proper coordination between stomach motility and emptying, resulting in significant clinical morbidity [1]. Although idiopathic gastroparesis represents the most common category, diabetes mellitus remains one of the most important identifiable causes; postoperative cases are also recognized [2].

Diabetic gastroparesis (DG) represents a component of a broader pathological and clinical spectrum affecting the entire gastrointestinal tract [3].

The reported prevalence of DG varies across studies, partly reflecting differences in diagnostic modalities. It is more commonly observed in type 1 diabetes(T1D) than in type 2 diabetes (T2D). Delayed gastric emptying has been reported in approximately 5–12% of individuals with T1D, particularly in those with longer disease duration and autonomic neuropathy [4]. In contrast, T2D has an estimated symptomatic case prevalence of 1% to 4%, while asymptomatic delayed gastric emptying may occur more frequently [5].

Numerous risk factors may contribute to the pathophysiology of DG; however, uncontrolled blood glucose is the primary factor that can lead to autonomic neuropathy, including involvement of the vagus nerve in the stomach, and it affects the interstitial cells of Cajal, which are responsible for the electrical stimulation of the gastric wall. Another cause includes abrupt fluctuations in blood glucose levels from hyperglycemia to hypoglycemia, which disrupt stomach motility [6].

The clinical presentation of gastroparesis symptoms is typically nonspecific, encompassing early satiety, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal fullness. These symptoms also vary in intensity, ranging from mild to severe, potentially impacting the patient’s nutritional status and leading to various comorbidities that affect patient quality of life [7].

Establishing a definitive diagnosis of gastroparesis is complex, as it involves not only the nonspecific clinical symptoms but also the confirmation of gastric delay through gastric emptying scintigraphy (GES), which measures the rate of stomach emptying after the intake of a solid radiolabeled meal [8].

The Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index (GCSI) is a validated score that plays a key role in assessing symptom severity; nevertheless, its diagnostic capability has not been fully studied [9].

Numerous factors can predispose individuals to the development of diabetic gastroparesis. These characteristics include female gender, longer diabetes duration, poor glycemic control, obesity, and diabetic microvascular complications such as neuropathy and retinopathy [10].

Diabetic gastroparesis, despite its clinical importance, is often underdiagnosed, particularly in individuals with T2D, as its symptoms may be misattributed to other gastrointestinal conditions. With the global rise in the prevalence of T2D, improved recognition and understanding of diabetic gastroparesis have become increasingly important [11].

Although diabetic gastroparesis has been investigated previously, important gaps remain in the integrated evaluation of symptom-based screening tools against objective gastric emptying measurements in patients with T2D. In particular, real-world outpatient data that simultaneously assess symptom burden, metabolic risk factors, microvascular complications, and gastric emptying scintigraphy within the same cohort are limited. So, the main aim of this study is to identify the clinical manifestations and risk variables associated with diabetic gastroparesis in individuals with T2D. The second aim is to assess the diagnostic accuracy of the Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index (GCSI) in comparison to gastric scintigraphy in identifying gastroparesis in patients living with diabetes.

Methods

A cross-sectional observational study was performed at an outpatient diabetic clinic in a tertiary hospital, encompassing one hundred thirty patients with T2D. It was approved by the Institutional Review Board Medical Ethical Research Committee of faculty of medicine, Assiut university on December 27th, 2022, with IRB local approval number 17,101,982,it was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment in the study.

The study was prospectively registered in the ClinicalTrials.gov database (Identifier: NCT05584462; first registration on 18 October 2022.

Sampling

A simple random sampling technique was employed to select participants who met the following criteria: individuals over 18 years of age with a confirmed diagnosis of T2D, registered within the health system at outpatient clinics. Based on the collected data, a comprehensive sampling frame included approximately 600 patients who met the inclusion criteria.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they had T1D, disease duration less than 5 years, incomplete records of previous gastrointestinal surgery, were pregnant females, had known medical conditions affecting gastric motility (e.g., hypothyroidism, neurological disorders), or were currently using prokinetic agents and antidiabetic medications that influence gastric motility and GLP-1 receptor agonists.

Sample size

The sample size was determined using EPI-Info (version 7.2). We used a 29% prevalence of gastroparesis in T2D [12]., a 7% margin of error, an 90% confidence level, and a design effect of 1; as a result, the minimum sample size calculated was 114 cases, which we expanded to 130 instances to accommodate potential loss.

Randomization

Each patient in the sampling frame was assigned a unique identifier, and a computer-generated random number sequence was used to select 130 patients from an initial pool of approximately 600 eligible individuals. Selected patients were contacted and assessed for eligibility; however, not all selected individuals were included due to refusal to participate or failure to complete the required investigations. The final cohort consisted of patients who fulfilled all inclusion criteria and completed all study procedures

Included participants

Clinical evaluations and physical examinations were conducted on randomly selected participants. The evaluations encompassed variables including age, sex, body mass index, duration of diabetes, HbA1c, and treatment strategy (oral medicine versus insulin). Participants additionally underwent evaluation for diabetes-related problems. The method included a fundus retinal examination to evaluate retinopathy, nephropathy assessment by urine albumin and serum creatinine levels, cardiac assessment with ECG and echocardiography, and peripheral neuropathy evaluation through monofilament testing.

A history of hypoglycemia was recorded as a binary variable (yes/no) based on patient-reported events during the preceding 3 months, without stratification by severity or frequency.

Patients over 60 or exhibiting any alarming gastrointestinal symptoms had a diagnostic upper endoscopy to rule out any organic disease.

Gastroparesis cardinal symptom index score (GCSI)[13]

Assessment of the symptoms was conducted with the Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index (GCSI), which is a validated score adapted from the Patient Evaluation of Upper Gastrointestinal Symptom Severity Index (PAGI-SYM). It comprises nine items divided into three subscales: nausea/vomiting (three items), postprandial fullness/early satiety (four items), and bloating (two items).The total score is calculated as the average of all item scores, providing a final score between 0 and 5, where higher scores indicate greater symptom intensity.

Diagnosis of gastroparesis

a-Symptoms based diagnosis according to GCSI score

Gastroparesis was defined in this study by the presence of characteristic symptoms with a Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index (GCSI) mean score > 2, which has been suggested in previous studies as an indicator of clinically significant gastroparesis symptoms [14].

b-Definite diagnosis by gastric emptying scintigraphy (GES)

Each subject consumed a standardized low-fat, egg-white meal labelled with technetium-99 m sulphur colloid, in accordance with the consensus protocol set forth by the Society of Nuclear Medicine and Molecular Imaging (SNMMI) and the American Neurogastroenterology and Motility Society (ANMS). Sequential gamma camera images were captured from both the anterior and posterior views at baseline, as well as at 1, 2, and 4 h following the meal. Gastric retention was calculated as the percentage of radioactivity remaining in the stomach after correcting for decay. Delayed gastric emptying (Fig. 1) is indicated by the retention of over 10% of the meal at the fourth hour [15].

Statistics

Data management and statistical analysis included data entry, cleaning, revision, variable documentation, and statistical analysis conducted using IBM SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) software, version 26. Qualitative data were presented as frequencies and percentages. Numerical data were assessed for normality using Shapiro-Wilk tests and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and range, depending on their distribution. The chi-square (χ²) and Fisher’s exact tests were employed to compare categorical variables. Independent t-tests and Mann-Whitney U tests were utilized to evaluate mean differences between two groups. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of delayed gastric emptying. Candidate variables were selected a priori based on clinical relevance. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values < 5 considered acceptable, and all variables were retained in the final model. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was utilized to assess the diagnostic efficacy of the cutoff point for the Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index (GCSI). The significance level was set at a p-value below 0.05.

Agreement between symptom-based classification and scintigraphic delayed gastric emptying was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic. The symptom-based classification was defined using the conventional threshold of GCSI > 2, while delayed gastric emptying was defined as > 10% gastric retention at 4 h on scintigraphy. Kappa was calculated using the full study cohort (n = 130) (Table 1).

Results

The study included 130 individuals, with females accounting for 73.1% and males for 26.9%. The average age of the participants was 56.1 ± 6.1 years. The average body mass index (BMI) was 25.05 ± 2.9 kg/m² (range: 20–33 kg/m²). The average glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level among the participants was 8.15 ± 1.45%, indicating inadequate glycemic control. The average duration of diabetes was 7.48 ± 1.96 years (Table 2).

ROC curve analysis demonstrated high discriminative ability of GCSI for delayed gastric emptying (AUC = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.88–1.00, p < 0.001). The cutoff identified within this cohort was 1.5, yielding a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 91.2%. Internal validation using bootstrap resampling (1000 samples) demonstrated stability of the association between GCSI and delayed gastric emptying, with minimal bias in the regression coefficient (− 0.057) and a statistically significant effect (p = 0.001; 95% CI: −1.918 to − 0.912). These findings reflect performance within the current study cohort (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The current study emphasizes DG as a significant health burden in T2D. DG is likely underestimated, particularly among patients with poorly controlled, long-standing T2D, as these individuals frequently report nonspecific upper gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, fullness, and bloating.

This study assessed the prevalence of DG and found rates of 13.1% based on delayed gastric emptying and 20.8% based on symptoms, which are higher than the 2–10% reported by Asad D et al. [16] The heightened prevalence may be ascribed to the hospital-based design of the study conducted in a tertiary care institution, where patients often demonstrate a prolonged duration of diabetes and a higher incidence of microvascular and macrovascular problems. This finding indicates that the condition is frequently underdiagnosed in people with T2D, mainly because of its vague symptoms and widespread underdiagnosis [17]. Another potential explanation for the increased prevalence of diabetic gastroparesis in the current study is that most participants were female, as diabetic gastroparesis is more prevalent among females [18]. The results of this study demonstrate that diabetes-related sequelae, including retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, and peripheral or coronary artery disease, were more prevalent in patients with DG compared to those without. The pathophysiology of gastroparesis closely aligns with the consequences of chronic and poorly controlled diabetes mellitus.

Patients with DG experienced hypoglycemia more frequently. However, the direction of this relationship is likely complex and potentially bidirectional. Delayed gastric emptying, early satiety, and reduced oral intake may contribute to glycemic variability and increase the risk of hypoglycemia, rather than hypoglycemia directly explaining these symptoms [19]. In addition, autonomic neuropathy, a recognized complication of diabetes, may contribute to both hypoglycemia and gastroparesis. Furthermore, delayed and irregular gastric emptying can disrupt nutrient absorption, leading to fluctuations in blood glucose levels, including postprandial hyperglycemia followed by subsequent declines [20].

However, in this study, hypoglycemia was assessed as a binary variable without detailed characterization of frequency or severity; further studies with more detailed assessment may help to better clarify its relationship with gastroparesis-related symptoms.

The current study identifies uncontrolled diabetes mellitus as the primary risk factor for diabetic gastroparesis, along with Almogbel et al.‘s results that poor glycemic control is a significant driver of delayed gastric emptying [21]. Longer duration of diabetes and increased body mass index were also significant predictors in our study. Similar observations were reported by Jain S et al., who found that gastroparesis symptoms were frequent in patients with poor glycemic control, longer disease duration, and higher BMI [22]. Furthermore, a study from Pakistan demonstrated that prolonged diabetes duration (> 10 years), elevated HbA1c, and obesity significantly increased the likelihood of gastroparesis-related symptoms, further supporting our findings [23].

These findings further emphasise the crucial role of glycemic control and blood glucose regulation in the pathophysiology of diabetic gastroparesis. Previous studies have reported delayed gastric emptying in approximately 30–50% of patients with longstanding poorly controlled T2D [24]. In contrast, gastric emptying in well-controlled T2D patients has been reported to be normal or even slightly accelerated [25].

In the present study, symptom-based classification using the GCSI showed poor agreement with scintigraphic delayed gastric emptying (κ = 0.107), aligning with previous reports that indicate limited correspondence between gastrointestinal symptoms and objective gastric emptying measurements. Additional analysis using continuous variables revealed a statistically significant moderate positive correlation between GCSI score and percentage gastric retention at 4 h, suggesting that symptom severity generally rises with delayed gastric emptying at the population level, although symptoms alone do not reliably identify delayed gastric emptying in individual patients.

The poor agreement between symptom-based assessment and scintigraphic delayed gastric emptying observed in this study highlights that delayed gastric emptying may occur despite minimal symptoms and still contribute to glycemic variability in insulin-treated patients because of mismatch between nutrient absorption and exogenous insulin action [26].

Revicki et al. emphasised that the GCSI was originally developed as a symptom severity assessment tool rather than a definitive diagnostic test [27]., and Parkman et al. similarly observed that symptom severity does not reliably predict scintigraphic abnormalities [28].

While most studies have applied thresholds GCSI (> 2–2.3) to define clinically significant symptom burden, our results indicate that a lower cutoff (1.5) may better discriminate patients with scintigraphic delay, However, as this represents a derivation finding from a single-center cohort without external validation, its use should be considered exploratory. This lower cutoff point possibly reflecting differences in population characteristics or disease severity. Collectively, these findings reinforce that the GCSI is a useful screening tool but should not replace gastric scintigraphy, which remains the diagnostic gold standard.

This study has several strengths. The concurrent use of the GCSI and GES enabled direct comparison between symptom-based assessment and the diagnostic gold standard, allowing a more accurate evaluation of the relationship between symptoms and gastric emptying. Consistent with prior literature demonstrating modest and variable correlations, GCSI showed limited but potentially useful discriminatory ability at the group level within this hospital-based, higher-risk population. In addition, the use of a predefined sample size and a random sampling strategy supports a more reliable estimation of gastroparesis prevalence within this cohort.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The single-centre, hospital-based, cross-sectional design may limit generalizability and precludes causal inference. Selection bias is possible, as patients with more severe disease are more likely to be referred to tertiary care settings. Furthermore, the relatively low number of outcome events (n = 17), partly due to the use of scintigraphy-based diagnosis, resulted in a suboptimal events-per-variable ratio, which may increase the risk of overfitting and limit the stability and generalizability of the regression and ROC-derived estimates. Finally, cardiac autonomic neuropathy was not assessed using standardized ECG-based indices or formal autonomic function testing. We need future multicenter prospective studies with a larger cohort of diabetic gastroparesis patients to validate and enhance their clinical characterization.

Conclusion

DG may be an overlooked complication of T2D, particularly in patients with poor glycaemic control, elevated BMI, longer disease duration, and recurrent hypoglycaemic episodes. Symptom-based assessment showed limited agreement with scintigraphic findings, while GES remains the diagnostic gold standard. Future multicentre studies with external validation are needed to further evaluate the optimal GCSI cutoff for detecting delayed gastric emptying in patients with T2D.
