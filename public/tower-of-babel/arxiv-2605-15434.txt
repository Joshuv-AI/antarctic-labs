Counting solutions to the quadratic determinant equation
Jonathan Chapman and Akshat Mudgal

Abstract
. Given h , N ∈ ℕ h,N\in\mathbb{N} satisfying 1 ⩽ h ⩽ N 2 1\leqslant h\leqslant N^{2} , we prove an asymptotic formula for the number of solutions to the equation x 1 ​ x 2 − x 3 ​ x 4 = h x_{1}x_{2}-x_{3}x_{4}=h with x 1 , … , x 4 ∈ [ − N , N ] ∩ ℤ x_{1},\ldots,x_{4}\in[-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z} . We use a combination of combinatorial and analytic arguments in physical space along with bounds for Kloosterman sums. Our main result concerns the case when h = N 2 + O ⁡ ( N ) h=N^{2}+O(N) , wherein we obtain square-root cancellation error terms by bypassing Kloosterman sum bounds and exploiting an additional symmetry available in this setting via Ramanujan sums. This confirms a speculation of Dhanda–Haynes–Prasala in a very general form.

1.
Introduction

Many classical topics in analytic number theory concern counting solutions to
Q(\bm{x})=h
, where
h
is some positive integer,
Q\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}]
is some quadratic form and
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{4})
varies over lattice points in some expanding region. This is especially true when
Q
satisfies
Q(\bm{x})=x_{1}x_{4}-x_{2}x_{3}
. For instance, a well known result of Selberg implies that for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
, one has

|\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}:Q(\bm{x})=1\ \text{and}\ x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{4}^{2}\leqslant N^{2}\}|=6N^{2}+O(N^{4/3}).

(1.1)

The error term here hasn’t been improved since Selberg’s result and it is conjectured the error term should of the order
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon})
, see
[
12
]
. Similarly, the binary additive divisor problem concerns estimates for the quantity

|\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}:Q(\bm{x})=h\ \text{and}\ x_{3}x_{4}\leqslant N^{2}\}|=\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant N^{2}}d(n)d(n+h)

(1.2)

for positive integers
h,N
, where
d(n)=\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{N}}\mathds{1}_{n=xy}
is the divisor function. This is a very well-studied problem, see
[
5
,
15
,
16
]
and the references therein, in part due to its close connections to the fourth moment of the Riemann zeta function on the half line
[
8
]
.

In this paper, we are interested in analysing the quantity

T(h,N)=|\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}:Q(\bm{x})=h\ \text{and}\ \bm{x}\in[-N,N]^{4}\}|=\sum_{|n|\leqslant N^{2}}d^{\prime}(n)d^{\prime}(n+h),

where
d^{\prime}(n)=\sum_{x,y\in[-N,N]}\mathds{1}_{n=xy}
can be interpreted as a restricted version of the divisor function. This specific setting has seen significant recent activity, see
[
1
,
3
,
4
,
6
,
7
,
14
]
. Since this is a problem concerning a quadratic form in four variables with sharp cut-offs instead of smooth weights and with
h\in[1,2N^{2}]
being quite arbitrary, the circle method does not seem to give an asymptotic formula for
T(h,N)
with strong error terms and uniformity in
h
, see
[
17
]
. Nevertheless, the circle method heuristic would predict that the main term contribution should be of the order
N^{2}
with the leading coefficient being a product of local densities and the real density. Thus, for every prime
p
, we define the
p
-adic density

\sigma_{p}(h)=\lim_{n\to\infty}p^{-3n}\{\bm{x}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\mathbb{Z})^{4}:Q(\bm{x})\equiv h\;(\Mod{p^{n}})\}.

Similarly, we also define the real density
\sigma_{\infty}(h/N^{2})
and the singular series
\mathfrak{S}_{h}
to be

\sigma_{\infty}(h/N^{2})=\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}(2\eta)^{-1}\int_{[-1,1]^{4}}\mathds{1}_{|Q(\bm{x})-h/N^{2}|<\eta}{\,{\rm d}}\bm{x}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mathfrak{S}_{h}=\prod_{p\ \text{prime}}\sigma_{p}(h).

With this in hand, the circle method heuristic would predict that
T(h,N)\sim\sigma_{\infty}(h/N^{2})\mathfrak{S}_{h}N^{2}
.

Despite the aforementioned issues with sharp cut-offs and
h\in[1,2N^{2}]
being arbitrary, we are able to prove the above heuristic quantitatively by using a combination of combinatorial and analytic arguments in physical space along with bounds for Kloosterman sums.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
h,N\in\mathbb{N}
, the quantities
\sigma_{\infty}(h/N^{2})
and
\mathfrak{S}_{h}
exist, and

T(h,N)=\sigma_{\infty}(h/N^{2})\mathfrak{S}_{h}N^{2}+O_{\varepsilon}(N^{3/2+\varepsilon}).

A natural point of comparison for this is its smoothened analogue, and so, let
w:\mathbb{R}^{4}\to[0,1]
be some smooth, compactly-supported function satisfying some further nice properties such as
w(\bm{0})=0
, see
[
9
]
. Here, a nice result of Heath-Brown
[
9
, Theorem 4]
gives us

T_{w}(N^{2},N):=\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}}w(\bm{x}/N)\mathds{1}_{Q(\bm{x})=N^{2}}=\sigma_{\infty,w}\mathfrak{S}_{h}N^{2}+O_{\varepsilon,w}(N^{3/2+\varepsilon}),

(1.3)

where
\sigma_{\infty,w}
is a smoothened version of
\sigma_{\infty}(1)
, see
[
9
, Theorem 3]
.
One can approximate the sharp cut-off with smooth weights and then use (
1.3
), but this makes the error terms extremely weak, see
[
17
, Theorem 3.6]
. Nevertheless, despite dealing with sharp cut-offs, our error term in Theorem
1.1
matches in strength its analogue in (
1.3
). Combining Theorem
1.1
with various properties of
\sigma_{\infty}(h/N^{2})
and
\mathfrak{G}_{h}
gives the following corollary.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
\lambda\in[0,2)
. Then
0<\sigma_{\infty}(\lambda)\ll 1
. Moreover, there exists
C_{\lambda}>0
such that for any
N\geqslant C_{\lambda}
and
1\leqslant h\leqslant 2N^{2}
, writing
\Delta=h-\lambda N^{2}
, we have that

T(h,N)=\sigma_{\infty}(\lambda)\frac{1}{\zeta(2)}\bigg(\sum_{d\mid h}\frac{1}{d}\bigg)N^{2}+O_{\varepsilon}(N^{3/2+\varepsilon}+N^{\varepsilon}|\Delta|).

This was previously known when
\lambda=0
with error terms
\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}(N+|h|)
, see
[
4
,
6
]
. These works employ very different types of techniques which can not analyse the case when
\lambda>0
. In the special case when
h
is a prime
p
satisfying
1<p/N^{2}<2
, Martin–White–Yip
[
14
]
proved an asymptotic formula for
T(p,N)
; see also work of Dhanda–Haynes–Prasala
[
6
, Theorem 2]
for the case when
\{h,N\}=\{p,\lfloor p^{1/2}\rfloor\}
or
\{h,N\}=\{p^{2},p\}
for some prime
p
.

Noting the analogous conjectured bounds in the setting of (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), see
[
4
, §1]
, as well as the results of
[
4
,
6
]
, one might conjecture that the first error term in Corollary
1.2
should be
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon})
, thus exhibiting square-root cancellation. When
\lambda=1
, we can exploit an additional available symmetry via Ramanujan sums to bypass the application of Kloosterman sum bounds and confirm the above speculation. This is our main result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
h,N\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
1\leqslant h\leqslant 2N^{2}
. Writing
\Delta=h-N^{2}
, we have that

T(h,N)=\left(\frac{8}{\zeta(2)}-4\right)\bigg(\sum_{d\mid h}\frac{1}{d}\bigg)N^{2}+O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon}+N^{\varepsilon}|\Delta|).

In order to highlight the novelty of the asymptotic formula given by Theorem
1.3
, we will compare this to other known asymptotic results in related settings. Thus, for any
h,N\in\mathbb{N}
, define

E(h,N)=T(h,N)-(8\zeta(2)^{-1}-4)N^{2}(\sum_{d\mid h}1/d).

Then Theorem
1.3
delivers the following corollary.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
h,N\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
h=N^{2}+O(N)
. Then
E(h,N)\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{1+\varepsilon}.

When
h
is taken to be some arbitrary power of some prime, this also confirms a speculation of Dhanda–Haynes–Prasala
[
6
]
. The latter authors employed very different type of arguments, which in particular relied very crucially on divisibility properties of primes, to prove Corollary
1.4
in the special case when
\{h,N\}=\{p,\lfloor p^{1/2}\rfloor\}
or
\{h,N\}=\{p^{2},p\}
for some prime
p
. They further speculated that this should hold when
h
is a higher power of some prime, but that this does not seem to follow from the types of arguments given in their proof. Thus Corollary
1.4
confirms their speculation in a very general form.

We can compare Corollary
1.4
to its smoothened analogue in (
1.3
) which satisfies the much weaker upper bound

E_{w}(h,N)=T_{w}(h,N)-\sigma_{\infty,w}\mathfrak{S}_{h}N^{2}\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{3/2+\varepsilon}.

We now consider the
\ell^{2}
analogue of this as described in (
1.1
), that is, we define

E_{\ell^{2}}(h^{\prime},N)=|\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}:Q(\bm{x})=h^{\prime}\ \text{and}\ x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{4}^{2}\leqslant N^{2}\}|-6N^{2}\sum_{d|h^{\prime}}d^{-1}

for all
1\leqslant h^{\prime}\leqslant N^{2}
. In this case, an application of the deep spectral theory of automorphic forms gives us, for any
1\leqslant h^{\prime}\leqslant N^{2}
, the bound

E_{\ell^{2}}(h^{\prime},N)\ll h^{\prime 1/3}N^{4/3}\sum_{d|h^{\prime}}\frac{1}{d};

see
[
12
, Theorem 12.4]
. On the other hand, when
h=N^{2}+O(N)
, this upper bound matches the size of the main term, and thus, is significantly larger than our upper bound in Corollary
1.4
. For
h=N^{2}+O(N)
, the only known upper bound of the form
E_{\ell^{2}}(h,N)=o(N^{2}\sum_{d|h}1/d)
follows from a general class of results proven by Oh
[
18
]
using techniques from dynamics, although the latter do not seem to given any quantitative power saving upper bound. Next, we consider the binary additive divisor problem described in (
1.2
), and so, we define

E_{\rm div}(h^{\prime},N)=|\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{N}^{4}:Q(\bm{x})=h^{\prime}\ \text{and}\ x_{3}x_{4}\leqslant N^{2}\}|-M_{\rm div}(h^{\prime},N)

for all
h^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
, where
M_{\rm div}(h^{\prime},N)
is the main term described by Motohashi in
[
16
, Theorem 1]
. When
h=N^{2}+O(N)
, we know that
N^{2}\ll M_{\rm div}(h,N)\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{2+\varepsilon}
. Using spectral theory of automorphic forms, Motohashi
[
16
]
proved that

E_{\rm div}(h,N)\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{4/3+\varepsilon},

see also work of Meurman
[
15
]
on this topic. While this compares favourably to our error term in Theorem
1.1
, this is still significantly larger than our upper bound in Corollary
1.4
.

Returning to our asymptotic formula described in Theorem
1.1
, we note that we can explicitly calculate
\sigma_{\infty}(\lambda)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{h}
. In particular, letting
{\rm Li}_{2}
denote the
dilogarithm function
(see (
5.4
)), we obtain the following estimates.

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
h\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\lambda>0
. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{h}=\zeta(2)^{-1}\sum_{d\mid h}\frac{1}{d}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \sigma_{\infty}(\lambda)=4J(\lambda)+8K(\lambda),

(1.4)

where
J(\lambda)
and
K(\lambda)
are defined by (
3.7
) and (
4.4
) respectively. Moreover, if
\lambda\in(0,1)
, then

\sigma_{\infty}(\lambda)=16-8(1-\lambda)\log(1-\lambda)+4\lambda(\mathrm{Li}_{2}(\lambda)-2(1+\zeta(2))-2\lambda\log\lambda+2(1-\lambda)\log^{2}\lambda),

whilst if
\lambda\in(1,2)
, then

\displaystyle\sigma_{\infty}(\lambda)=8+8(\lambda-1)(\log(\lambda-1)-1)+4\lambda(\zeta(2)-\log^{2}\lambda-2\mathrm{Li}_{2}(1/\lambda)).

Finally, we have
\sigma_{\infty}(1)=8-4\zeta(2)
, and
0<\sigma_{\infty}(\lambda)\leqslant 48
for all
\lambda\in[0,2)
.

Proof ideas

We will now give an outline of our proof ideas.
Suppose
ax-by=h
for some
a,x,b,y\in[-N,N]\cap\mathbb{Z}
. After removing the contribution of the solutions satisfying
axby=0
, we divide our proof into analysing two terms. The first of these counts, up to some symmetry factor, the
additive-type solutions
, that is, solutions to the equation
ax+by=h
, with
a,x,b,y\in[N]
, where
[N]:=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
. Similarly, the second term counts the
difference-type solutions
which count
a,x,b,y\in[N]
such that
ax-by=h
.

Let us focus on the additive-type solutions, and so, after dividing out by
d:=(x,y)
, which also must divide
h
, we get that these equal

\sum_{d|h,d\leqslant N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{a,b\in[N]}\mathds{1}_{au+bv=h/d}.

Estimating the innermost sum is equivalent to counting points in an arithmetic progression with common difference
u
. Indeed,
b\equiv(h/d)\overline{v}\;(\Mod{u})
, where
\overline{v}
is the inverse of
v
in
(\mathbb{Z}/u\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. Moreover, the conditions
a,b\in[N]
translate to constraints on the starting and end points
S_{u,v,h/d}
and
T_{u,v,h/d}
of the arithmetic progression which contains
b
. We write the number of admissible choices of
b
as

u^{-1}(T_{u,v,h/d}-S_{u,v,h/d})\ \mathds{1}_{T_{u,v,h/d}\geqslant S_{u,v,h/d}}+E_{u,v,h/d}\ \mathds{1}_{T_{u,v,h/d}\geqslant S_{u,v,h/d}},

where the first term can be interpreted as a main term contribution and
E_{u,v,h/d}
is the sum of three error terms. The first of these error terms is simply a characteristic function which enforces a congruence condition on
v
modulo
u
, and the other two are of the form
\psi((r_{1}+r_{2}\overline{x})/y)
, where
r_{1},r_{2}
are non-zero integers depending on
h/d
and
N
, and
\{x,y\}=\{u,v\}
, and
\psi
is the
sawtooth function
defined in (
2.1
).

We first analyse the contribution of the main term
u^{-1}(T_{u,v,h/d}-S_{u,v,h/d})
. We begin by applying Möbius inversion to rewrite the factor
\mathds{1}_{(u,v)=1}
as
\sum_{k|(u,v)}\mu(k)
, thus introducing a further auxiliary sum. Our next idea here is to turn the discrete sum over
u,v
into it’s continuous version. In this endeavour, we note that the terms
S_{u,v,h/d}
and
T_{u,v,h/d}
can take two possible values each, and thus, we have to do a case-by-case analysis and prove that the discrete-to-continuous approximation in each case costs at most
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon})
. After applying a further change of variables along with various further analytic manoeuvres, we able to show that the additive main term is approximately

N^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k\leqslant N/d}\frac{\mu(k)}{k^{2}}\int_{(0,1)^{2}}(\alpha\beta)^{-1}\mu((0,\alpha)\cap(\lambda-(0,\beta))){\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta,

where
\mu
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
, see Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
. We now show that the above double integral is precisely the real density corresponding to the additive-type solutions. Moreover, one can massage the double arithmetic sums to obtain the singular series. One can proceed similarly in the case of difference-type solutions. Thus, we obtain the main terms for Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
.

What remains to estimate is the contribution of the error terms
E_{u,v,h/d}
. The congruence error terms can be bounded by
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon})
via standard elementary number theoretic lemmas. The analysis of sawtooth error terms requires a lot more work and is precisely the reason why we get the
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{3/2+\varepsilon})
error term in the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
. We begin our analysis by proving that for any choice of
T_{u,v,h/d}
and
S_{u,v,h/d}
, the condition that
T_{u,v,h/d}\geqslant S_{u,v,h/d}
is equivalent to the vector
(u,v)
lying inside a convex subset of
[1,N/d]\times[1,N/d]
. In particular, for every fixed
u\in[1,N/d]
, the variable
v
varies in some interval
I_{u}
of integers such that
|I_{u}|\leqslant N/d
. We can now use the truncated Fourier expansion of the sawtooth function along with some analytic and elementary number theoretic arguments to reduce the problem to estimating sums of the form

\sum_{d|h,d\leqslant N}\sum_{0<|s|\leqslant N^{100}}\frac{1}{s}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in I_{u},\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}e(sr\overline{v}/u)|

(1.5)

where
r
is some fixed, non-zero integer. Using the upper bound
|I_{u}|\leqslant N/d
along with upper bounds on incomplete Kloosterman sums, we can prove that this is bounded above by

\sum_{d|h,d\leqslant N}\frac{N}{d}\sum_{0<|s|\leqslant N^{100}}\frac{1}{s}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\frac{(sr,u)^{1/2}}{u^{1/2}}.

Cauchy’s inequality and standard elementary number theory lemmas allow us to bound the above by
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{3/2+\varepsilon})
. This finishes our proof outline of Theorem
1.1
.

For the purposes of Theorem
1.3
, we have to be more careful in our analysis. We first note that since
h=N^{2}+\Delta
, the number of difference-type solutions is at most

\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}(1+\Delta/N)^{2}\ll N^{\varepsilon}(1+\Delta).

Thus, it suffices to estimate the additive-type solutions. We proceed exactly as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, until we reach (
1.5
). Here, upon utilising the fact that
h=N^{2}+\Delta
, we are able to show that for all
1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d
, the interval
I_{u}
satisfies
|I_{u}|=u+O(|\Delta|/dN)
. Thus, upon incurring a further cost of the order
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{\varepsilon}|\Delta|)
, we can ensure that each
I_{u}
is a complete interval modulo
u
. We can now use properties of Ramanjuan sums along with the triangle inequality, see (
2.2
), to bound (
1.5
) by

N^{\varepsilon}\sum_{0<|s|\leqslant Q}\frac{1}{s}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}(sr,u).

As before, we can estimate these via elementary methods, this time obtaining the
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon})
error term.

2.
Preliminaries

We begin by recording the following elementary lemma, see
[
4
, Lemma 2.3]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
m
be a positive integer and let
M\geqslant 1
be a real number. Then

\sum_{1\leqslant y\leqslant M}(y,m)\ll_{\varepsilon}Mm^{\varepsilon}.

Given
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote
\lVert x\rVert=\min_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|x-n|
and
e(x)=e^{2\pi ix}
. We also define

\psi(x)=x-\lfloor x\rfloor-1/2,

(2.1)

where
\lfloor x\rfloor
is the greatest integer less than or equal to
x
. The function
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to[-1/2,1/2)
is referred to as the
saw-tooth function
. We will require the following truncated Fourier expansion for
\psi(x)
, see for example,
[
11
, §5]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
Q\geqslant 1
be a real number. For any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\psi(\theta)=\sum_{0<|h|\leqslant Q}\frac{e(\theta h)}{2\pi ih}+O\left(\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{Q\lVert\theta\rVert}\right\}\right).

Given positive integers
y,n
, we will need the following bounds for Ramanujan sums

|\sum_{t\in(y/\mathbb{Z}y)^{\times}}e(nt/y)|=|\sum_{d|(n,y)}d\mu(y/n)|\leqslant\sum_{d|(n,y)}d,

(2.2)

see
[
2
, Theorem 8.6]
. We will also need the following consequence of the Weil bounds on Kloosterman sums recorded by Hooley
[
10
, Lemma 2]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
a,b,u
be integers with
u>0
. Then

\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant x<u,\\
(x,u)=1\end{subarray}}e((ax+b\overline{x})/u)\bigg|\leqslant d(u)(b,u)^{1/2}u^{1/2},

where
d(u)
denotes the number of divisors of
u
, and
\overline{x}
denotes the inverse of
x
in
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.

Applying the divisor bound, we see that the right-hand side is bounded by
O_{\varepsilon}((b,c)^{1/2}c^{1/2+\varepsilon})
. This estimate can be used to derive the following classical bound on incomplete Kloosterman sums due to Hooley
[
11
, Lemma 3]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
I
be an interval, let
u,r
be non-zero integers such that
u,v\leqslant N^{10}
. Then

\sum_{v\in I,(v,u)=1}e(r\overline{v}/u)\ll N^{\varepsilon}\frac{(r,u)^{1/2}}{u^{1/2}}(|I|+u\log u).

For the sake of completeness, we record its proof below.

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

For every
z\in\mathbb{Z}/u\mathbb{Z}
, define
f_{I}(z)=|I\cap(z+u\cdot\mathbb{Z})|
. Moreover, for any
f:\mathbb{Z}/u\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
, define its Fourier transform
\hat{f}:\mathbb{Z}/u\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
as

\hat{f}(\xi)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}/u\mathbb{Z}}f(x)e(x\xi/u).

Applying orthogonality gives

f_{I}(x)=u^{-1}\sum_{\xi=0}^{u-1}\hat{f_{I}}(\xi)e(-\xi x/u).

(2.3)

Moreover, for any
0\leqslant\xi\leqslant u-1
, we see that

\hat{f_{I}}(\xi)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}/u\mathbb{Z}}f_{I}(x)e(x\xi/u)=\sum_{y\in I}e(y\xi/u).

Since
I
is an interval, we deduce that

|\hat{f_{I}}(\xi)|\ll\min\left\{|I|,\|{\xi/u}\|^{-1}\right\}.

(2.4)

We apply (
2.3
) to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in I,(v,u)=1}e(r\overline{v}/u)=\sum_{x\in(\mathbb{Z}/u\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}f_{I}(\xi)e(r\overline{x}/u)=u^{-1}\sum_{x\in(\mathbb{Z}/u\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}e(r\overline{x}/u)\sum_{\xi=0}^{u-1}\hat{f_{I}}(x)e(-\xi x/u).

Interchanging the order of sums, we find that the right-hand side is

u^{-1}\sum_{\xi=0}^{u-1}\hat{f_{I}}(\xi)\sum_{x\in(\mathbb{Z}/u\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}e((r\overline{x}-\xi x)/u)\ll u^{-1}\sum_{\xi=0}^{u-1}|\hat{f_{I}}(\xi)|(r,u)^{1/2}u^{1/2+\varepsilon},

where this inequality follows from applying the triangle inequality and Lemma
2.3
. Thus, we conclude from (
2.4
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in I,(v,u)=1}e(r\overline{v}/u)

\displaystyle\ll N^{\varepsilon}\frac{(r,u)^{1/2}}{u^{1/2}}\sum_{\xi=0}^{u-1}|\hat{f_{I}}(\xi)|\ll N^{\varepsilon}\frac{(r,u)^{1/2}}{u^{1/2}}(|I|+\sum_{\xi=1}^{u-1}\|{\xi/u}\|^{-1})

\displaystyle\ll N^{\varepsilon}\frac{(r,u)^{1/2}}{u^{1/2}}(|I|+u\log u).\qed

3.
Additive-type solutions

We commence our investigation into solutions of the quadratic determinant equation by studying additive-type solutions. Suppose then that
a,b,x,y\in[N]
satisfy

ax+by=h.

(3.1)

Observe that the greatest common divisor
d\in[N]
of
x
and
y
must divide
h
for this equation to be valid. Dividing both sides by
d
leads to the equation

au+bv=h/d\qquad(a,b\in[N],\;u,v\in[N/d],\;\gcd(u,v)=1).

Our goal therefore is to compute the following sum:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{1\leqslant a,b\leqslant N}\mathds{1}_{au+bv=h/d}.

Notice that (
3.1
) implies
bv\equiv(h/d)\;(\Mod{u})
. Given coprime
u
and
v
, let
b_{0}\in[u]
be the unique integer satisfying
b_{0}v\equiv(h/d)\;(\Mod{u})
and let
a_{0}=(h/d-b_{0}v)/u
. We then see that any
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
au+bv=h/d
must satisfy
b=b_{0}+su
and
a=a_{0}-sv
for some
s\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus, the number of such
a,b\in[N]
is equal to the total number of integers
s
in the range

U:=\max\left\{\frac{1-b_{0}}{u},\frac{a_{0}-N}{v}\right\}\leqslant s\leqslant\min\left\{\frac{N-b_{0}}{u},\frac{a_{0}-1}{v}\right\}=:V.

(3.2)

The number of such
s
is exactly

(\lfloor V\rfloor-\lfloor U\rfloor+\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}(U))\mathds{1}_{V\geqslant U}=(V-U)\mathds{1}_{V\geqslant U}+\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}(U)\mathds{1}_{V\geqslant U}-(\psi(V)-\psi(U))\mathds{1}_{V\geqslant U},

where
\psi(x):=x-\lfloor x\rfloor-1/2
is the sawtooth function.

We have therefore reduced our problem to studying
\mathcal{M}(h,N)+\mathcal{E}(h,N)
, where

\mathcal{M}(h,N):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}(V-U)\mathds{1}_{V\geqslant U}

(3.3)

is the
main term
and

\mathcal{E}(h,N):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}(\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}(U)\mathds{1}_{V\geqslant U}-(\psi(V)-\psi(U))\mathds{1}_{V\geqslant U}),

(3.4)

is the
error term
, with
U
and
V
being as defined in (
3.2
). We postpone the analysis of
\mathcal{E}(h,N)
until §
6
. The remainder of this section is devoted to examining the main term
\mathcal{M}(h,N)
. We proceed to show that this discrete sum can be approximated by an integral.

Observe that

V-U=\min\left\{\frac{N}{u},\frac{h/d}{uv}-\frac{1}{v}\right\}-\max\left\{\frac{1}{u},\frac{h/d}{uv}-\frac{N}{v}\right\}=V^{\prime}-U^{\prime},

where
V^{\prime}=V+b_{0}/u
and
U^{\prime}=U+b_{0}/u
. Since our expressions for
V^{\prime}
and
U^{\prime}
do not involve
a_{0}
or
b_{0}
, we can extend the definitions of
V^{\prime}
and
U^{\prime}
to all positive real numbers. Explicitly, for all
\alpha,\beta\in(0,\infty)
, we define

V^{\prime}=V^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta,h/d,N)=\min\left\{\frac{N}{\alpha},\frac{h/d}{\alpha\beta}-\frac{1}{\beta}\right\},\quad U^{\prime}=U^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta,h/d,N):=\max\left\{\frac{1}{\alpha},\frac{h/d}{\alpha\beta}-\frac{N}{\beta}\right\},

and

F_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/d)=\max\{0,V^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta,h/d,N)-U^{\prime}(\alpha,\beta,h/d,N)\}.

Before moving on, we record the trivial inequalities

0\leqslant F_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/d)\leqslant\frac{N}{\max\{\alpha,\beta\}}.

(3.5)

Our expression for the main term has therefore become

\mathcal{M}(h,N)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}F_{N}(u,v,h/d).

As we would like to replace the discrete sum of
F_{N}
by an integral over real numbers, we need to remove the coprimality condition. This is achieved via Möbius inversion. Starting from the classical identity

\sum_{k\mid n}\mu(k)=\mathds{1}_{n=1}\quad(n\in\mathbb{N}),

we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(h,N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d}F_{N}(u,v,h/d)\sum_{k\mid(u,v)}\mu(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{k\leqslant N/d}\mu(k)\sum_{1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/dk}F_{N}(ku,kv,h/d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{k\leqslant N/d}\frac{\mu(k)}{k}\sum_{1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/dk}F_{N}(u,v,h/dk).

For fixed
d\mid h
and
k\leqslant N/d
with
d\leqslant N
, we now investigate the sum

\sum_{1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/dk}F_{N}(u,v,h/dk).

As indicated previously, by extending the definition of
F_{N}
to all real numbers, we can replace this discrete sum with an integral

S_{N}(h/dk)=\int_{1}^{N/dk}\int_{1}^{N/dk}F_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/dk){\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha.

Recalling the trivial bound (
3.5
) for
F_{N}
, we see that

S_{N}(h/dk)=\int_{1}^{\lfloor N/dk\rfloor+1}\int_{1}^{\lfloor N/dk\rfloor+1}F_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/dk){\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha+O(N\log N),

and so

\displaystyle\bigg\lvert S_{N}(h/dk)-\sum_{1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/dk}F_{N}(u,v,h/dk)\bigg\rvert

\displaystyle\ll N\log N+\sum_{1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/dk}

\displaystyle\int_{u}^{u+1}\int_{v}^{v+1}|F_{N}(u,v,h/dk)-F_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/dk)|{\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta.

We now show that this sum of integrals is much smaller than the expected size of the main term.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
and
h\in\mathbb{Z}
with
0\leqslant h\leqslant 2N^{2}
. Let
d,k\in[N]
with
d\mid h
and
k\leqslant N/d
. We have

\sum_{1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/dk}\int_{u}^{u+1}\int_{v}^{v+1}|F_{N}(u,v,h/dk)-F_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/dk)|{\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta\ll N(\log N)^{2}.

Proof.

Let
I=[h/dk-2,h/dk+2]
. Using (
3.5
), the contribution of the cases when either
u
or
v
lies in
I
is

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{u\in I}\sum_{1\leqslant v\leqslant N/dk}\int_{u}^{u+1}\int_{v}^{v+1}|F_{N}(u,v,h/dk)-F_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/dk)|{\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta

\displaystyle\ll N\sum_{u\in I}\sum_{1\leqslant v\leqslant N/dk}\int_{u}^{u+1}\int_{v}^{v+1}\left(\frac{1}{v}+\frac{1}{\beta}\right){\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha\ll N\sum_{u\in I}\sum_{1\leqslant v\leqslant N/dk}\frac{1}{v}\ll N\log N.

If neither
u
nor
v
lie in
I
, then we note that

\frac{N}{u}-\frac{h/dk}{uv}\leqslant\frac{-1}{v}\Leftrightarrow Nv-h/dk\leqslant-u\Leftrightarrow v\leqslant h/(dkN)

and

\frac{1}{u}-\frac{h/dk}{uv}\geqslant\frac{-N}{v}\Leftrightarrow Nu+v\geqslant h/(dk)\Leftrightarrow u\geqslant h/(dkN).

We now perform a case by case analysis. Our first case is when
u,v\notin I
satisfy
u,v\leqslant h/(dkN)
. Then

F_{N}(u,v,h/dk)=\max\left\{0,\frac{N}{u}+\frac{N}{v}-\frac{h/dk}{uv}\right\}=\max\left\{0,\frac{N(u+v-h/(dkN))}{uv}\right\}.

For the maximum to be positive on the right-hand side, we can assume that
u+v\geqslant h/(dkN)
. Now, let
\alpha,\in[u,u+1)
and
\beta\in[v,v+1)
be real numbers. Then a standard Taylor expansion can be used to give the upper bound

\displaystyle|F_{N}(u,v,h/dk)-F_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/dk)|

\displaystyle\ll N\left(\frac{h/(dkN)-v}{u^{2}v}+\frac{h/(dkN)-u}{v^{2}u}\right).

Using the fact that
u+v\geqslant h/(dkN)
, we see that the right-hand side is bounded above by
O(N/(uv))
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant h/(dkN),\\
u,v\notin I\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\int_{u}^{u+1}\int_{v}^{v+1}|F_{N}(u,v,h/dk)-F_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/dk)|\ {\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant h/(dkN),\\
u,v\notin I\end{subarray}}\frac{N}{uv}\ll N(\log N)^{2}.

We now proceed to our second case where
u,v\notin I
are such that
u\leqslant h/(dkN)
and
v>h/(dkN)
. In this case, we have

F_{N}(u,v,h/dk)=\frac{N-1}{v}

and so, given
\alpha,\in[u,u+1)
and
\beta\in[v,v+1)
, we see that

F_{N}(u,v,h/dk)-F_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/dk)=(N-1)(v^{-1}-\beta^{-1})\ll\frac{N}{v^{2}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u\leqslant h/(dkN)<v\leqslant N/dk,\\
u,v\notin I\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\int_{u}^{u+1}\int_{v}^{v+1}|F_{N}(u,v,h/dk)-F_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/dk)|{\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta

\displaystyle\ll N\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u\leqslant h/(dkN)<v\leqslant N/dk,\\
u,v\notin I\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{v^{2}}\ll N\sum_{h/(dkN)<v\leqslant N/dk}\frac{1}{v}\ll N\log N.

A similar analysis yields the same bound for the third case where
u,v\notin I
are such that
u>h/(dkN)
and
v\leqslant h/(dkN)
.

The final case to consider is when
u,v\notin I
are such that
u,v>h/(dkN)
. Here, we find that

F_{N}(u,v,h/dk)=\max\left\{0,\frac{h/(dk)-u-v}{uv}\right\}.

This quantity is positive only when
u+v<h/(dk)
. Thus, letting
u,v\notin I
be integers satisfying
u,v>h/(dkN)
and
u+v<h/(dk)
, and letting
\alpha\in[u,u+1)
and
\beta\in[v,v+1)
, we may apply Taylor expansion to deduce that

|F_{N}(u,v,h/dk)-F_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/dk)|\ll\frac{h/dk-v}{u^{2}v}+\frac{h/dk-u}{uv^{2}}\ll\frac{h/dk}{uv}\left(\frac{1}{u}+\frac{1}{v}\right)\ll\frac{N}{uv},

As in the first case, we may now deduce that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h/(dkN)\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/dk,\\
u+v<h/(dk),\\
u,v\notin I\end{subarray}}\int_{u}^{u+1}\int_{v}^{v+1}|F_{N}(u,v,h/dk)-F_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/dk)|{\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta\ll N(\log N)^{2},

and the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma
3.1
therefore delivers the following expression for the main term:

\mathcal{M}(h,N)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{k\leqslant N/d}\frac{\mu(k)}{k}S_{N}(h/dk)+O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon}).

(3.6)

Our next task is to modify the integral
S_{N}(h/dk)
to more closely align with the main term appearing in Theorem
1.1
. Given
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we define

J(\lambda):=\int_{(0,1)^{2}}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\ \mu((0,\beta]\cap(\lambda-(0,\alpha])){\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta,

(3.7)

where
\mu
denotes the Lebesgue measure on the real line. It is immediate from the definition that
J(\lambda)=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus(0,2)
. Moreover, in view of the crude bound

\mu((0,\beta]\cap(\lambda-(0,\alpha]))\leqslant\min\{\alpha,\beta,\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\}\qquad(0\leqslant\alpha,\beta\leqslant 1),

(3.8)

it is easy to verify that
J(\lambda)
is well-defined and satisfies
0<J(\lambda)\leqslant 4
for all
0<\lambda<2
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
N,h,d,k
be as in Lemma
3.1
. Writing
h=\lambda N^{2}
, we have

S_{N}(h/dk)=\frac{N^{2}}{dk}J(\lambda)+O(N(\log N)^{2}).

Proof.

Recalling (
3.5
) and applying a change of variables, we see that

\displaystyle S_{N}(h/dk)

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{dk}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\int_{dk/N}^{1}F_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/N){\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{dk}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\max\left\{0,\min\left\{\frac{N}{\alpha},\frac{h}{N\alpha\beta}-\frac{1}{\beta}\right\}-\max\left\{\frac{1}{\alpha},\frac{h}{N\alpha\beta}-\frac{N}{\beta}\right\}\right\}{\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{dk}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\max\left\{0,\min\left\{\beta,\frac{h}{N^{2}}-\frac{\alpha}{N}\right\}-\max\left\{\frac{\beta}{N},\frac{h}{N^{2}}-\alpha\right\}\right\}{\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha.

Now observe that for any
t,x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
with
x_{1}\leqslant y_{1}
and
x_{2}\leqslant y_{2}
we have

\mu([x_{1},y_{1}]\cap(t-[x_{2},y_{2}]))=\max\{0,\min\{y_{1},t-x_{2}\}-\max\{x_{1},t-y_{2}\}\}.

Recalling that
h=\lambda N^{2}
, we therefore infer

S_{N}(h/dk)=\frac{N^{2}}{dk}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\ \mu\left(\left[\frac{\beta}{N},\beta\right]\cap\left(\lambda-\left[\frac{\alpha}{N},\alpha\right]\right)\right){\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha.

Furthermore, in view of the bound

\frac{N^{2}}{dk}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\frac{1}{\alpha N}\ d\beta d\alpha\ll\frac{N}{dk}(\log N+\log(dk))\ll N\log N,

we may write

S_{N}(h/dk)=\frac{N^{2}}{dk}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\ \mu\left(\left[0,\beta\right]\cap\left(\lambda-\left[0,\alpha\right]\right)\right){\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha+O(N\log N).

If we extend the range of both integrals from
[dk/N,1]
to
(0,1)
, then

S_{N}(h/dk)=\frac{N^{2}}{dk}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\ \mu\left(\left[0,\beta\right]\cap\left(\lambda-\left[0,\alpha\right]\right)\right){\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha+O(I_{1}+I_{2}+N\log N),

where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{dk}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\int_{0}^{dk/N}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\ \mu([0,\beta]\cap(\lambda-[0,\alpha])){\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{dk}\int_{0}^{dk/N}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\ \mu([0,\beta]\cap(\lambda-[0,\alpha])){\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha.

Hence, to complete the proof, it only remains to show that
I_{1},I_{2}=O(N(\log N)^{2})
.

The crude bound (
3.8
) is enough to establish

I_{1}\leqslant\frac{N^{2}}{dk}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{dk/N}1{\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha=N\log(N/dk)\leqslant N\log N.

We now consider
I_{2}
. Recall that

\mu([0,\beta]\cap(\lambda-[0,\alpha]))=\mu([\max\{0,\lambda-\alpha\},\min\{\beta,\lambda\}]).

Let
\ell=\min\{\lambda,dk/N\}
. We may assume
\ell>0
, as otherwise
I_{2}=0
and we are done. Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\ell}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\ \mu([0,\beta]\cap(\lambda-[0,\alpha])){\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\int_{\lambda-\alpha}^{\lambda}\frac{\alpha+\beta-\lambda}{\beta}{\,{\rm d}}\beta+\int_{\lambda}^{1}\frac{\alpha}{\beta}{\,{\rm d}}\beta\right){\,{\rm d}}\alpha

\displaystyle=\ell(1-\log(\lambda))+\lambda\int_{0}^{\ell/\lambda}\left(\frac{1}{t}-1\right)\log(1-t){\,{\rm d}}t.

Observe that the function
t\mapsto(t^{-1}-1)\log(1-t)
is continuous on
(0,1)
and has finite limits as
t\to 0^{+}
or
t\to 1^{-}
. Consequently, the integrand appearing in the final integral above is uniformly bounded by some absolute constant. Since we are in the case when
\lambda\neq 0
, we know that
N^{-2}\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 2
, and so

\int_{0}^{\ell}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\ \mu([0,\beta]\cap(\lambda-[0,\alpha])){\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha\ll\ell(1+|\log(\lambda)|)\ll\frac{dk}{N}\log(N).

If
\lambda\geqslant dk/N
, then
\ell=dk/N
and this bound leads to the desired estimate
I_{2}=O(N\log N)
. If instead
0<\lambda<dk/N\leqslant 1
, then
\ell=\lambda\geqslant N^{-2}
and the previous bound implies that

\displaystyle I_{2}+O(N\log N)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{dk}\int_{\lambda}^{dk/N}\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\int_{0}^{\lambda}\frac{\beta}{\beta}{\,{\rm d}}\beta+\int_{\lambda}^{1}\frac{\lambda}{\beta}{\,{\rm d}}\beta\right){\,{\rm d}}\alpha

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\lambda N^{2}}{dk}(1+|\log(\lambda)|^{2})\ll N(\log N)^{2},

as required.
∎

By combining all of these observations, we have therefore established the following.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
N,h\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leqslant h\leqslant 2N^{2}
, and write
h=\lambda N^{2}
. Then the number of solutions
(a,b,x,y)\in[N]^{4}
to (
3.1
) is equal to

\frac{J(\lambda)}{\zeta(2)}N^{2}\sum_{d|h}\frac{1}{d}+\mathcal{E}(h,N)+O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon}),

where
J(\lambda)
is given by (
3.7
) and
\mathcal{E}(h,N)
is defined in (
3.4
) with
U
and
V
as in (
3.2
).

Proof.

In view of Lemma
3.2
, the expression (
3.6
) becomes

\mathcal{M}(h,N)=J(\lambda)N^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k\leqslant N/d}\frac{\mu(k)}{k^{2}}+O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon}).

Note that

0\leqslant\sum_{d|h}\frac{1}{d}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d}\leqslant\frac{1}{N}\sum_{d\mid h}1.

The result now follows from the divisor bound and the classical approximation

\sum_{k\leqslant X}\frac{\mu(k)}{k^{2}}=\frac{1}{\zeta(2)}+O\left(\frac{1}{X}\right),

see for example
[
2
, §3.7]
.
∎

4.
Difference-type solutions

We now turn our attention to solutions of the difference equation

ax-by=h\qquad(a,b,x,y\in[N]).

(4.1)

Most of the steps of our analysis of this equation are the same as in our investigation of the additive equation (
3.1
) in the previous section. For this reason, we omit many of the details and focus on the aspects that differ between the two arguments.

Suppose
d\in[N]
is such that
d\mid h
. Given coprime
u,v\in[N/d]
, let
b_{0}\in[u]
be the unique integer such that
b_{0}v\equiv-h/d\;(\Mod{u})
, and let
a_{0}=(h/d+b_{0}v)/u
. We then set

U:=\max\left\{\frac{1-a_{0}}{v},\frac{1-b_{0}}{u}\right\};\quad V:=\min\left\{\frac{N-a_{0}}{v},\frac{N-b_{0}}{u}\right\}.

(4.2)

As with the additive equation, we can express the number of solutions to (
4.1
) in the form
\mathcal{M}(h,N)+\mathcal{E}(h,N)
, where these terms are as given in (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) but with
U
and
V
now defined by (
4.2
). For the rest of this section, we focus on establishing an asymptotic formula for the main term
\mathcal{M}(h,N)
. The error term will be analysed in §
6
.

To remove the explicit dependence of the main term on
a_{0}
and
b_{0}
, we set

V^{\prime}=V+a_{0}/v=\min\left\{\frac{N}{v},\frac{N}{u}-\frac{h/d}{uv}\right\}\ \ \text{and}\ \ U^{\prime}=U+a_{0}/v=\max\left\{\frac{1}{v},\frac{1}{u}-\frac{h/d}{uv}\right\}

In contrast with the case of additive-type solutions, it is significantly easier to describe the value of
(V^{\prime}-U^{\prime})\mathds{1}_{V^{\prime}\geqslant U^{\prime}}
. Indeed, we observe that

V^{\prime}=\frac{N}{v}\Leftrightarrow(u-v)\leqslant\frac{h}{dN};\qquad U^{\prime}=\frac{1}{v}\Leftrightarrow(u-v)\geqslant\frac{h}{d},

and so

uv(V^{\prime}-U^{\prime})\mathds{1}_{V^{\prime}\geqslant U^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}Nv+h/d-u,\quad&\text{if }u-v\geqslant h/d;\\
(N-1)v,&\text{if }h/dN\leqslant u-v<h/d;\\
Nu-h/d-v,&\text{if }v/N\leqslant u-h/dN<v;\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(4.3)

In view of the bounds

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{h}{duv}\mathds{1}_{u-v\geqslant h/d}\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{u}\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{1+\varepsilon},

we can remove the “
h/d-u
” and “

” terms from the first and third cases respectively by incurring an additional

error term in our final count. We can similarly replace “

” with

in the second case. Since for any fixed choice of

Combining all of the observations of the previous paragraph with Möbius inversion allows us to rewrite our main term as

\mathcal{M}(h,N)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{k\leqslant N/d}\frac{\mu(k)}{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}G_{N}(u,v,h/dk)+O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon}),

where, for all

Using a very similar proof strategy, we can prove the following analogue of Lemma

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/dk}\int_{u}^{u+1}\int_{v}^{v+1}|G_{N}(u,v,h/dk)\ -G_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/dk)|{\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha\ll N(\log N)^{2}.

This shows that our main term is

\mathcal{M}(h,N)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{k\leqslant N/d}\frac{\mu(k)}{k}T_{N}(h/dk)+O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon}),

where

\displaystyle T_{N}(h/dk)

\displaystyle:=\int_{1}^{N/dk}\int_{1}^{N/dk}G_{N}(\alpha,\beta,h/dk){\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{dk}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\int_{dk/N}^{1}\left(\mathds{1}_{\alpha-\beta\geqslant\frac{h}{N^{2}}}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)+\mathds{1}_{\frac{h}{N^{2}}\leqslant\alpha<\frac{h}{N^{2}}+\beta}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{h/N^{2}}{\alpha\beta}\right)\right){\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{dk}\int_{(dk/N,1)^{2}}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\mu([h/N^{2},\beta+h/N^{2}]\cap[0,\alpha]){\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta.

Using analogous calculations as in the proof of Lemma

where, for all

(4.4)

Together these observations deliver the following analogue of Proposition

5.
The singular series and real density

In this section, we examine the integrals (
3.7
) and (
4.4
) appearing in our expressions for the number of additive-type and difference-type solutions respectively and compute their exact values. We also discuss how these integrals and the term
\zeta(2)^{-1}\sum_{d\mid h}(1/d)
can be interpreted as the real density and the singular series respectively for their associated counting problems.

5.1.
The singular series

We begin with the singular series. For each prime
p
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
N_{p,k}(h)
denote the number of solutions to
x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}x_{4}\equiv h\;(\Mod{p^{k}})
, where
x_{1},\ldots,x_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we also let
\nu_{p}(n)
denote the largest non-negative integer
r
such that
p^{r}
divides
n
. With this in hand, we prove the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
p
be prime. For any
m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and any integer
k>10\nu_{p}(m)
, one has

N_{p,k}(m)=p^{3k}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{p}+\cdots+\frac{1}{p^{\nu_{p}(m)}}\right).

(5.1)

In particular,

\sigma_{p}(m):=\lim_{k\to\infty}p^{-3k}N_{p,k}(m)=\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{p}+\cdots+\frac{1}{p^{\nu_{p}(m)}}\right).

Proof.

Given
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}x_{4}\equiv m\;(\Mod{p^{k}})
, we define
\eta=\min\{\nu_{p}(x_{1}),\nu_{p}(x_{3})\}
. Note that
0\leqslant\eta\leqslant\nu_{p}(m)
. For any
0\leqslant i\leqslant\nu_{p}(m)
, the number of pairs
(x_{1},x_{3})
satisfying
\eta=i
is precisely

p^{2k-2i}(2(1-1/p)-(1-1/p)^{2})=p^{2k-2i}(1-1/p^{2}).

Given any such pair
(x_{1},x_{3})
, say, with
\nu_{p}(x_{1})=i
, we see that fixing
x_{4}
then fixes
x_{2}\;(\Mod{p^{k-i}})
. Moreover each such fixed value of
x_{2}\;(\Mod{p^{k-i}})
lifts to precisely
p^{i}
distinct
x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}x_{4}\equiv m\;(\Mod{p^{k}})
. Thus, the number of solutions
x_{1},\dots,x_{4}
with
\eta=i
is
p^{3k-i}(1-1/p^{2})
. Summing this over all
0\leqslant i\leqslant\nu_{p}(m)
delivers the expression in (
5.1
).
∎

Combining Lemma
5.1
with the facts that

\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{\zeta(2)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\prod_{p}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\nu_{p}(h)}\frac{1}{p^{j}}\right)=\sum_{d\mid h}\frac{1}{d}

(5.2)

immediately delivers the formula for the singular series in Proposition
1.5
.

5.2.
Real density of additive-type solutions

We now turn to the real density for (
3.1
). For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
0<\eta\leqslant 1
, let

J_{\eta}(\lambda):=\frac{1}{2\eta}\int_{(0,1)^{4}}\mathds{1}_{|x_{1}x_{4}+x_{2}x_{3}-\lambda|<\eta}{\,{\rm d}}\mathbf{x};\quad\widetilde{J}_{\eta}(\lambda):=\int_{(\eta,1)^{2}}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\ \mu((0,\beta]\cap(\lambda-(0,\alpha])){\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta.

Our goal is to show that
J_{\eta}(\lambda)\to J(\lambda)
as
\eta\to 0^{+}
. We accomplish this by first approximating
J_{\eta}(\lambda)
by
\widetilde{J}_{\eta}(\lambda)
, and then approximating
J(\lambda)
by
\widetilde{J}_{\eta}(\lambda)
. This latter approximation will also subsequently aid in our proof of Corollary
1.2
. We first require the following lemma to handle error terms which arise in these calculations.

Lemma 5.2
.

For all
0\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 2
and
0<\eta\leqslant 1
, we have

I(\lambda;\eta):=\int_{(0,1]^{2}}\mathds{1}_{\lambda-2\eta<xy<\lambda+2\eta}{\,{\rm d}}x{\,{\rm d}}y=O(\eta\log(2/\eta)).

Proof.

We may assume throughout that
\lambda-2\eta<1
, as otherwise
I(\lambda;\eta)=0
and we are done.
If we additionally have
\lambda+2\eta\geqslant 1
, then
|1-(\lambda+2\eta)|\leqslant 4\eta
and so

I(\lambda;\eta)=\int_{(0,1]^{2}}\mathds{1}_{xy>\lambda-2\eta}{\,{\rm d}}x{\,{\rm d}}y\leqslant\int_{(\lambda-2\eta,1]^{2}}{\,{\rm d}}x{\,{\rm d}}y\ll\eta^{2}.

Henceforth, we assume
\lambda+2\eta<1
. First consider the case where
\lambda\leqslant 6\eta
. The desired estimate for
I(\lambda;\eta)
now follows from the crude bound

\displaystyle I(\lambda;\eta)\leqslant\int_{(0,1]^{2}}\mathds{1}_{xy<\lambda+2\eta}{\,{\rm d}}x{\,{\rm d}}y=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\min\{1,(\lambda+2\eta)/y\}}{\,{\rm d}}x{\,{\rm d}}y

\displaystyle=(\lambda+2\eta)(1-\log(\lambda+2\eta))

\displaystyle\leqslant 8\eta(1+\log(2/\eta)).

Now suppose instead that
\lambda>6\eta
. By considering when the integrand of
I(\lambda;\eta)
is non-zero, we find that

\displaystyle I(\lambda;\eta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\lambda-2\eta}^{1}\int_{(\lambda-2\eta)/y}^{\min\{1,(\lambda+2\eta)/y\}}{\,{\rm d}}x{\,{\rm d}}y=\int_{\lambda+2\eta}^{1}\frac{4\eta}{y}{\,{\rm d}}y+\int_{\lambda-2\eta}^{\lambda+2\eta}\left(1-\frac{\lambda-2\eta}{y}\right){\,{\rm d}}y

\displaystyle=4\eta\log\left(\frac{1}{\lambda+2\eta}\right)+4\eta-(\lambda-2\eta)\log\left(1+\frac{4\eta}{\lambda-2\eta}\right)

\displaystyle=O(\eta\log(2/\eta))-(\lambda-2\eta)\log\left(1+\frac{4\eta}{\lambda-2\eta}\right).

Invoking the Taylor expansion
\log(1+x)=x-O(x^{2})
for
0<x<1
now delivers the final required bound

(\lambda-2\eta)\log\left(1+\frac{4\eta}{\lambda-2\eta}\right)=4\eta-O\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda-2\eta}\right)=O(\eta).\qed

Lemma 5.3
.

For all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
0<\eta\leqslant 1
, we have

J_{\eta}(\lambda)=\widetilde{J}_{\eta}(\lambda)+O(\eta(\log(2/\eta))^{2})=J(\lambda)+O(\eta(\log(2/\eta))^{2}),

where
J(\lambda)
is defined by (
3.7
).
In particular, for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
J_{\eta}(\lambda)\to J(\lambda)
as
\eta\to 0^{+}
.

Proof.

We may assume that
0\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 2
, as otherwise
J_{\eta}(\lambda)=\widetilde{J}_{\eta}(\lambda)=J(\lambda)=0
and we are done.
We begin by observing that

\displaystyle J_{\eta}(\lambda)=

\displaystyle\;(2\eta)^{-1}\int_{(\eta,1)^{2}}\frac{1}{ab}\int_{0}^{a}\int_{0}^{b}\mathds{1}_{\lambda-\eta<u+v<\lambda+\eta}{\,{\rm d}}u{\,{\rm d}}v{\,{\rm d}}a{\,{\rm d}}b

\displaystyle+O\left(\eta^{-1}\int_{0}^{\eta}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\mathds{1}_{\lambda-2\eta<by<\lambda+2\eta}{\,{\rm d}}b{\,{\rm d}}y{\,{\rm d}}a{\,{\rm d}}x\right).

Performing a change of variables and invoking Lemma
5.2
to bound the error term delivers

J_{\eta}(\lambda)=(2\eta)^{-1}\int_{(\eta,1)^{2}}\frac{1}{ab}\int_{\lambda-\eta}^{\lambda+\eta}\mu([0,b]\cap(z-[0,a])){\,{\rm d}}z{\,{\rm d}}a{\,{\rm d}}b+O(\eta\log(2/\eta)).

Using the crude estimate

|\mu([0,b]\cap(z-[0,a]))-\mu([0,b]\cap(z^{\prime}-[0,a]))|\leqslant|z-z^{\prime}|,

(5.3)

we deduce

\displaystyle J_{\eta}(\lambda)+O(\eta\log(2/\eta))

\displaystyle=\int_{(\eta,1)^{2}}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\mu([0,\beta]\cap(\lambda-[0,\alpha])){\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta+O\left(\int_{(\eta,1)^{2}}\frac{\eta}{\alpha\beta}{\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{J}_{\eta}(\lambda)+O(\eta(\log(2/\eta))^{2}).

It only remains to extend the range of integration from
(\eta,1]^{2}
to
(0,1]^{2}
. Using (
3.8
), we obtain the following bounds:

\displaystyle\int_{(0,\eta]^{2}}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\mu([0,\beta]\cap(\lambda-[0,\alpha])){\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta\leqslant\int_{(0,\eta]^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha\beta}}{\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta\ll\eta,

\displaystyle\int_{(0,\eta]}\int_{(\eta,1]}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\mu([0,\beta]\cap(\lambda-[0,\alpha])){\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta\leqslant\eta\int_{(\eta,1]}\frac{1}{\alpha}{\,{\rm d}}\alpha\ll\eta\log(2/\eta),

\displaystyle\int_{(\eta,1]}\int_{(0,\eta]}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\mu([0,\beta]\cap(\lambda-[0,\alpha])){\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta\leqslant\eta\int_{(\eta,1]}\frac{1}{\beta}{\,{\rm d}}\beta\ll\eta\log(2/\eta).

Combining these with the definition of
\widetilde{J}_{\eta}(\lambda)
establishes the desired estimate.
∎

Our final task is to provide an explicit formula for the real density
J(\lambda)
.
The most technical aspect of this calculation is the appearance of the dilogarithm function

\mathrm{Li}_{2}(x):=-\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\log(1-t)}{t}{\,{\rm d}}t=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{n^{2}}\qquad(x\in[-1,1]).

(5.4)

For our purposes, we only consider the dilogarithm restricted to
[0,1]
and we record all the properties we need in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

The dilogarithm function defined above is continuous on
[0,1]
and has the special values

\mathrm{Li}_{2}(0)=0;\quad\mathrm{Li}_{2}(1/2)=\frac{1}{2}(\zeta(2)-(\log(2))^{2});\quad\mathrm{Li}_{2}(1)=\zeta(2).

Moreover, we have the
reflection formula
1
1

1

Here we interpret
\log(0)\log(1)=0
.

\mathrm{Li}_{2}(x)+\mathrm{Li}_{2}(1-x)=\zeta(2)-\log(x)\log(1-x).

Proof.

All of these properties follow readily from the definition of the dilogarithm and the Taylor expansion of the logarithm; see
[
13
]
for further details.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, let
J(\lambda)
be defined as in (
3.7
). Then we have

J(\lambda)=\begin{cases}\lambda(\log^{2}(\lambda)-2\log(\lambda)+2-\mathrm{Li}_{2}(\lambda)),\quad&\text{if }0<\lambda<1;\\
2-\zeta(2),&\text{if }\lambda=1;\\
2+2(\lambda-1)(\log(\lambda-1)-1)+\lambda(\zeta(2)-\log^{2}(\lambda)-2\mathrm{Li}_{2}(1/\lambda)),&\text{if }1<\lambda<2;\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

As noted in the remarks following (
3.7
), it is immediate from the definition that
J(\lambda)=0
whenever
\lambda\leqslant 0
or
\lambda\geqslant 2
. We therefore only consider
0<\lambda<2
.

First, consider the case where
0<\lambda<1
. We have

J(\lambda)=\int_{(0,1)^{2}}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\mu((\max\{0,\lambda-\alpha\},\min\{\beta,\lambda\})){\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3},

where

\displaystyle I_{1}=\int_{\lambda}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\min\{\beta,\lambda\}}{\alpha\beta}{\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha=\int_{\lambda}^{1}\frac{\lambda}{\alpha}{\,{\rm d}}\alpha+\lambda\left(\int_{\lambda}^{1}\frac{1}{\alpha}{\,{\rm d}}\alpha\right)^{2}=\lambda(\log(\lambda)-1)\log(\lambda),

\displaystyle I_{2}=\int_{0}^{\lambda}\int_{\lambda}^{1}\frac{\lambda-(\lambda-\alpha)}{\alpha\beta}{\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha=-\lambda\log(\lambda),

and

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\lambda}\int_{\lambda-\alpha}^{\lambda}\frac{\alpha+\beta-\lambda}{\alpha\beta}{\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha=\lambda-\int_{0}^{\lambda}\frac{(\lambda-\alpha)(\log(\lambda)-\log(\lambda-\alpha))}{\alpha}{\,{\rm d}}\alpha

\displaystyle=\lambda+\lambda\int_{0}^{\lambda}\frac{(1-t)\log(1-t)}{t}{\,{\rm d}}t=\lambda(2-\mathrm{Li}_{2}(\lambda)),

Adding all of these integrals delivers the required formula for
0<\lambda<1
. In view of the fact that
t\log(t)\to 0
as
t\to 0^{+}
, performing the same calculations with
\lambda=1
and invoking Lemma
5.4
gives

J(1)=2-\mathrm{Li}_{2}(1)=2-\zeta(2).

Now consider the case where
1<\lambda<2
. We have

\displaystyle J(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{\lambda-1}^{1}\int_{\lambda-\alpha}^{1}\frac{\beta-(\lambda-\alpha)}{\alpha\beta}{\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha=\int_{\lambda-1}^{1}\frac{(\lambda-\alpha)\log(\lambda-\alpha)-(\lambda-\alpha-1)}{\alpha}{\,{\rm d}}\alpha

\displaystyle=2+2(\lambda-1)(\log(\lambda-1)-1)+\lambda\int_{1-\frac{1}{\lambda}}^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\frac{\log(\lambda)+\log(1-t)}{t}{\,{\rm d}}t

\displaystyle=2+2(\lambda-1)(\log(\lambda-1)-1)-\lambda\log(\lambda)\log(\lambda-1)+\lambda(\mathrm{Li}_{2}(1-\lambda^{-1})-\mathrm{Li}_{2}(\lambda^{-1})).

Applying the reflection formula from Lemma
5.4
allows us to write

\mathrm{Li}_{2}(1-\lambda^{-1})=\zeta(2)+\log(\lambda)\log(1-\lambda^{-1})-\mathrm{Li}_{2}(\lambda^{-1}).

Inserting this into the above expression for
J(\lambda)
finishes the proof.
∎

5.3.
Real density of difference-type solutions

For all
\eta>0
, let

K_{\eta}(\lambda):=(2\eta)^{-1}\int_{(0,1)^{4}}\mathds{1}_{|ax-by-\lambda|<\eta}{\,{\rm d}}a{\,{\rm d}}b{\,{\rm d}}x{\,{\rm d}}y.

Following similar calculations as in the proof of Lemma

K_{\eta}(\lambda)=\widetilde{K}_{\eta}(\lambda)+O(\eta(\log(2/\eta)^{2})=K(\lambda)+O(\eta(\log(2/\eta)^{2})\qquad(\lambda\in\mathbb{R}),

where

.

Lemma 5.6
.

For each

, let

be defined as in (

\displaystyle K(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,1)^{2}}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\mu([\lambda,\min\{\alpha,\beta+\lambda\}]{\,{\rm d}}\beta{\,{\rm d}}\alpha=\int_{\lambda}^{1}\frac{\alpha-\lambda}{\alpha}{\,{\rm d}}\alpha-\int_{\lambda}^{1}\frac{(\alpha-\lambda)\log(\alpha-\lambda)}{\alpha}{\,{\rm d}}\alpha

\displaystyle\int_{\lambda}^{1}\frac{\log(\alpha-\lambda)}{\alpha}{\,{\rm d}}\alpha

Inserting this into our previous expression completes the proof.
∎

. Thus, without loss of generality, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}(U)\leqslant\sum_{d|h}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant v\leqslant N/d,\\
v\equiv r_{1}\;(\Mod{u})\end{subarray}}1,

for some fixed
r_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
. The innermost sum on the right hand side admits a trivial upper bound of the shape
O(N/du)
. Thus,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}(U)\ll\sum_{d|h,d\leqslant N}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\frac{N}{du}\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{1+\varepsilon},

which concludes our proof of Lemma
6.1
.
∎

It remains to bound the contribution of the second and third terms in (
6.1
). Our goal is to prove the following.

Lemma 6.2
.

We have

\sum_{d|h,d\leqslant N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\psi(V)\mathds{1}_{V\geqslant U}\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{3/2+\varepsilon}.

The case when
\psi(V)
is replaced by
\psi(U)
can be analysed similarly. In order to prove Lemma
6.2
, we require the following preliminary estimate.

Lemma 6.3
.

For every
1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d
, let
I_{u}
be some interval such that
|I_{u}|\leqslant N/d
. Let
r_{1},r_{2}\leqslant N^{10}
be some non-zero integers. Then

\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in I_{u}\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\psi((r_{1}+\overline{v}r_{2})/u)\ll N^{\varepsilon}(N/d)^{3/2}.

Proof.

We start by employing the truncated Fourier expansion of
\psi
. Thus, applying Lemma
2.2
with
Q=N^{100}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in I_{u},\\
(v,u)=1\end{subarray}}\psi((r_{1}+\overline{v}r_{2})/u)=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in I_{u},\\
(v,u)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{0<|s|\leqslant Q}\frac{e(s(r_{1}+\overline{v}r_{2})/u)}{2\pi is}

\displaystyle+\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in I_{u},\\
(v,u)=1\end{subarray}}O\left(\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{Q\|{(r_{1}+r_{2}\overline{v})/u}\|}\right\}\right).

(6.2)

The absolute value of the first term on the right-hand side is bounded above by

\sum_{0<|s|\leqslant Q}\frac{1}{2\pi s}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in I_{u},\\
(v,u)=1\end{subarray}}e(sr_{2}\overline{v}/u)\bigg|\ll N^{\varepsilon}\sum_{0<|s|\leqslant Q}\frac{1}{s}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\frac{(sr_{2},u)^{1/2}}{u^{1/2}}(|I_{u}|+u\log u).

Here, the latter inequality follows from Lemma
2.4
. Note that
u,|I_{u}|\leqslant N/d
, and so, the above is

\ll N^{\varepsilon}(N/d)\sum_{0<|s|\leqslant Q}\frac{1}{s}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\frac{(sr_{2},u)^{1/2}}{u^{1/2}}.

Now, applying Cauchy’s inequality to the inner sum and using Lemma
2.1
, we find that

\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\frac{(sr_{2},u)^{1/2}}{u^{1/2}}\leqslant\bigg(\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}(sr_{2},u)\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg(\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\frac{1}{u}\bigg)^{1/2}\ll N^{\varepsilon}(N/d)^{1/2}.

Putting everything together, we see that the first term on the right-hand side in (
6
) is

\ll N^{\varepsilon}(N/d)^{3/2}\sum_{0<|s|\leqslant Q}\frac{1}{s}\ll N^{\varepsilon}(N/d)^{3/2}.

We now analyse the second term on the right-hand side in (
6
). We begin by noting that for any
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
\|{t/u}\|\geqslant 1/u
unless
u\mid t
. Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in I_{u},\\
(v,u)=1\end{subarray}}\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{Q\|{(r_{1}+r_{2}\overline{v})/u}\|}\right\}\ll\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in I_{u},\\
(u,v)=1,\\
r_{1}+r_{2}\overline{v}\equiv 0\;(\Mod{u})\end{subarray}}1+\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in I_{u},\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{u}{Q}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d,\\
u\nmid r_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{N/d}{u}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d,\\
u\mid r_{2}\end{subarray}}N/d+\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\frac{N^{2}}{Q}\ll N^{1+\varepsilon}/d.

(6.3)

This completes the proof of Lemma
6.3
.
∎

We now return to the proof of Lemma
6.2
.

Proof of Lemma
6.2
.

Notice that both
u,v
always lie in the set
[1,N/d]\times[1,N/d]
. Let us consider the case of additive-type solutions first. The assumption that
U=(1-b_{0})/u
holds is equivalent to the condition that
\alpha_{1}u+\beta_{1}v\leqslant\gamma_{1}
for some choice of
\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\gamma_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
. A similar comment holds for fixing
V
to equal either
(N-b_{0})/u
or
(a_{0}-1)/v
. Thus, fixing
U
and
V
to take specific values as recorded in (
3.2
) and then subsequently fixing the condition that
V\geqslant U
is equivalent to ensuring that the vector
(u,v)
lies in a specific convex region
\mathcal{C}
inside
[1,N/d]\times[1,N/d]
. This would mean that for any fixed
u\in[1,N/d]\cap\mathbb{Z}
, the set
\mathcal{C}\cap(\{u\}\times\mathbb{Z})
is precisely of the form
\{u\}\times I_{u}
where
I_{u}
is some interval of integers. Moreover, since this is a subset of
[1,N/d]\times[1,N/d]
, we see that
|I_{u}|\leqslant N/d
. A similar comment holds for the case when we fix some
v\in[1,N/d]\cap\mathbb{Z}
and consider all
u\in[1,N/d]\cap\mathbb{Z}
which satisfy the above conditions. Now since there are two possibilities each for
U
and
V
, all of which look like
(r+q\overline{x})/y
where
\{x,y\}=\{u,v\}
and
r,q
are non-zero integers lying in the interval
[-N^{10},N^{10}]
such that
y\nmid q
, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{d|h,d\leqslant N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\psi(V)\mathds{1}_{V\geqslant U}\ll\sum_{d|h,d\leqslant N}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\sum_{1\leqslant x_{i}\leqslant N/d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{i}\in I_{x_{i}},\\
(x_{i},y_{i})=1\end{subarray}}\psi((r_{i}+q_{i}y_{i})/x_{i}).

We may now apply Lemma
6.3
to deduce that

\sum_{d|h,d\leqslant N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1\end{subarray}}\psi(V)\mathds{1}_{V\geqslant U}\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{3/2+\varepsilon}\sum_{d|h,d\leqslant N}1/d^{3/2}\ll N^{3/2+\varepsilon}.

This is precisely the desired bound.
∎

One can proceed mutatis mutandis when we study
\psi(V)
with
\psi(U)
and when we consider difference-type solutions.

7.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2

We now bring together all of the observations and results developed over the course of the paper to establish Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2
. We begin with the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Our goal is to obtain an asymptotic formula for the number
T(h,N)
of integer solutions
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in[-N,N]^{4}
to the equation

x_{1}x_{4}-x_{2}x_{3}=h.

By the divisor bound, there are at most
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon})
solutions where at least one of the
x_{i}
is equal to
0
. Consider then solutions where the
x_{i}
are all non-zero. There are
16
choices for the signs of the
x_{i}
. Since
h\geqslant 1
, the four choices which cause
x_{1}x_{4}<0<x_{2}x_{3}
result in no solutions. The four choices of signs for which
x_{2}x_{3}<0<x_{1}x_{4}
lead to counting solutions over
[N]^{4}
to the additive equation (
3.1
). Similarly, the remaining eight sign choices reduce our problem to counting solutions over
[N]^{4}
to the difference equation (
4.1
). Putting this all together, we have

T(h,N)=4T_{+}(h,N)+8T_{-}(h,N)+O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon}),

(7.1)

where

T_{\pm}(h,N):=|\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in\{1,\ldots,N\}^{4}:x_{1}x_{4}\pm x_{2}x_{3}=h\}|.

We evaluated the leading order of the quantities
T_{\pm}(h,N)
in Propositions
3.3
and
4.1
, and so

T(h,N)=\frac{4J(h/N^{2})+8K(h/N^{2})}{\zeta(2)}N^{2}\sum_{d\mid h}\frac{1}{d}+4\mathcal{E}_{+}(h,N)+8\mathcal{E}_{-}(h,N)+O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon}),

(7.2)

where the error terms
\mathcal{E}_{+}(h,N)
and
\mathcal{E}_{-}(h,N)
are as defined in Propositions
3.3
and
4.1
respectively. Both of these error terms take the form (
6.1
). Invoking Lemmas
6.1
and
6.2
, we have

4\mathcal{E}_{+}(h,N)+8\mathcal{E}_{-}(h,N)\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon}.

Finally, Proposition
1.5
allows us to rewrite the main term in (
7.2
) into the form

T(h,N)=\sigma_{\infty}(h/N^{2})\mathfrak{S}_{h}N^{2}+O_{\varepsilon}(N^{3/2+\varepsilon}),

which finishes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
∎

We now turn our attention to Corollary
1.2
. The key feature of this result is that the leading constant is bounded away from zero in terms of a given fixed
\lambda\in[0,2)
. To deduce this from Theorem
1.1
, we need to understand how the real density
\sigma_{\infty}(\lambda)
varies with
\lambda
. The following lemma is suitable for this purpose.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda\in[0,2)
. Let
\sigma_{\infty}(\lambda)
be as defined in Theorem
1.1
. For all
\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[-N^{2},N^{2}]
, we have

\sigma_{\infty}\left(\lambda+\frac{\Delta}{N^{2}}\right)=\sigma_{\infty}(\lambda)+O\left(\frac{|\Delta|\log^{2}(N)}{N^{2}}\right).

Proof.

The result is immediate if
\Delta=0
, so assume
\Delta\neq 0
and set
\eta=|\Delta|/N^{2}
. Lemma
5.3
shows that

J(\lambda+\eta)+O(\eta\log^{2}(2/\eta))=\widetilde{J}_{\eta}(\lambda+\eta)=\int_{(\eta,1)^{2}}\frac{1}{\alpha\beta}\ \mu((0,\beta]\cap(\lambda-(0,\alpha])){\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta.

In view of the crude bound (
5.3
) and the estimate

\int_{(\eta,1)^{2}}\frac{\eta}{\alpha\beta}{\,{\rm d}}\alpha{\,{\rm d}}\beta\ll\eta\log^{2}(2/\eta),

the triangle inequality reveals that

|J(\lambda+\eta)-J(\lambda)|\leqslant|J(\lambda+\eta)-\widetilde{J}_{\eta}(\lambda+\eta)|+|J(\lambda)-\widetilde{J}_{\eta}(\lambda)|+O(\eta\log^{2}(2/\eta))\ll\eta\log^{2}(2/\eta).

A similar reasoning, combined with (
5.5
), leads to the bounds

|J(\lambda-\eta)-J(\lambda)|,|K(\lambda+\eta)-K(\lambda)|,|K(\lambda-\eta)-K(\lambda)|\ll\eta\log^{2}(2/\eta).

Recalling that
\eta\log^{2}(2/\eta)\ll\Delta N^{-2}\log^{2}(N)
and
\sigma_{\infty}(\lambda)=4J(\lambda)+8K(\lambda)
ends the proof.
∎

We now present our proof of Corollary
1.2
.

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

Fix some
\lambda\in[0,2)
. Proposition
1.5
informs us that
0<\sigma_{\infty}(\lambda)\leqslant 48
. Writing
\Delta=h-\lambda N^{2}
, Theorem
1.1
and (
1.4
) show that

T(h,N)=\sigma_{\infty}\left(\lambda+\frac{\Delta}{N^{2}}\right)\frac{N^{2}}{\zeta(2)}\sum_{d\mid h}\frac{1}{d}+O_{\varepsilon}(N^{3/2+\varepsilon}).

The result now follows by applying Lemma
7.1
to the above and invoking the divisor bound

1\leqslant\sum_{d\mid h}\frac{1}{d}\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}.\qed

8.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section, we will present our proof of Theorem
1.3
. Thus, let
h,N
be positive integers and let
h=N^{2}+\Delta
. We first consider the number of solutions to
ad-bc=h
with
abcd=0
and
-N\leqslant a,b,c,d\leqslant N
. As in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we see that the number of such solutions is at most
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon})
. We will now show that the number of difference-type solutions, that is, the number of solutions satisfying
ad-bc=h
with
1\leqslant a,b,c,d\leqslant N
, is at most
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{\varepsilon}(1+|\Delta|)).
Indeed, suppose
ad-bc=h=N^{2}+\Delta
for some
a,b,c,d\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. This implies that

a\geqslant ad/N\geqslant(N^{2}+\Delta+bc)/N\geqslant N+\Delta/N.

Since
a\in[N]
, we see that there are at most
O(1+|\Delta|/N)
many choices for
a
. Similarly, we have at most
O(1+|\Delta|/N)
many choices for
d
. Fixing such
a,d
, we see that
bc
is fixed. Finally since we know that
bc\neq 0
, we can use the divisor estimate to fix
b,c
in
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{\varepsilon})
many ways. Thus, the number of difference solutions is at most

\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}(1+|\Delta|/N)^{2}\ll N^{\varepsilon}(1+\Delta^{2}/N^{2})\ll N^{\varepsilon}(1+|\Delta|),

where the final step uses the fact that
|\Delta|=|h-N^{2}|\leqslant N^{2}
.

Hence, it suffices to estimate the number of additive-type solutions. We now follow our strategy described in §
3
. In particular, the main term here is

J(h/N^{2})\zeta(2)^{-1}N^{2}\sum_{d|h}1/d.

Applying Lemma
7.1
, we see that upon incurring a further cost of order
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{\varepsilon}|\Delta|)
, we may replace
J(h/N^{2})=J(1+\Delta/N^{2})
by
J(1)
. Substituting the value of
J(1)
from Lemma
5.5
into the above expression and noting (
7.1
), we find that our main term contribution to
T(h,N)
is precisely

(8\zeta(2)^{-1}-4)N^{2}\sum_{d|h}1/d.

Thus, we now focus on the additive-type error terms. Recalling the definition of
U
and
V
in (
3.4
), we see that

U=\frac{a_{0}-N}{v}\ \Leftrightarrow\ u\leqslant\frac{h}{dN}-\frac{v}{N}=\frac{N}{d}-\frac{v}{N}+\frac{\Delta}{dN}

and

V=\frac{N-b_{0}}{u}\ \Leftrightarrow\ v\leqslant\frac{h}{dN}-\frac{u}{N}=\frac{N}{d}-\frac{u}{N}+\frac{\Delta}{dN}.

Now note that
u,v\leqslant N/d
, and so, if
U\neq(a_{0}-N)/v
, then we must have

N/d-v/N+\Delta/(dN)<u\leqslant N/d.

The contribution of these cases to the error term
\mathcal{E}(h,N)
in (
3.4
) is at most

\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{1\leqslant v\leqslant N/d}\ \sum_{N/d-v/N+\Delta/(dN)<u\leqslant N/d}\ 3\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{1\leqslant v\leqslant N/d}\left(\frac{\Delta}{dN}+\frac{v}{N}\right)\ll\Delta+N.

(8.1)

We can similarly estimate the contribution of the terms when
V\neq(N-b_{0})/u
. Thus, we may assume that
U=(a_{0}-N)/v
and
V=(N-b_{0})/u
, in which case,
V\geqslant U
is equivalent to the condition that
N(u+v)\geqslant h/d=N^{2}/d+\Delta/d
. Thus the contribution of these terms to
\mathcal{E}(h,N)
is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1,\\
u+v\geqslant\frac{N}{d}+\frac{\Delta}{dN}\end{subarray}}(\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}(U)-(\psi(V)-\psi(U))\mathds{1}_{u\leqslant\frac{N}{d}-\frac{v}{N}+\frac{\Delta}{dN}}\mathds{1}_{v\leqslant\frac{N}{d}-\frac{u}{N}+\frac{\Delta}{dN}}.

As in the estimation of (
8.1
), we can remove the factors
\mathds{1}_{u\leqslant\frac{N}{d}-\frac{v}{N}+\frac{\Delta}{dN}}
and
\mathds{1}_{v\leqslant\frac{N}{d}-\frac{u}{N}+\frac{\Delta}{dN}}
from the above expression by incurring an error term of size
O(\Delta+N)
. Moreover, the contribution of the term
\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}(U)
to the above sum can be shown to be
O_{\varepsilon}(N^{1+\varepsilon})
exactly as in the proof of (
8.1
). Hence, it remains to show that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1,\\
u+v\geqslant\frac{N}{d}+\frac{\Delta}{dN}\end{subarray}}\psi(V)\ll N^{1+\varepsilon}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u,v\leqslant N/d,\\
(u,v)=1,\\
u+v\geqslant\frac{N}{d}+\frac{\Delta}{dN}\end{subarray}}\psi(U)\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{1+\varepsilon}.

(8.2)

We will only record the proof of the first inequality, with the proof of the second bound following in the same vein with suitable modifications.

For our proof of the first inequality in (
8.2
), we will require the following lemma.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
\sigma>0
be a parameter. For every
1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d
, let

be some interval such that
|I_{u}|=u+O(\sigma)
. Let
r_{1},r_{2}\leqslant N^{10}
be some non-zero integers. Then

\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\sum_{v\in I_{u},(u,v)=1}\psi((r_{1}+\overline{v}r_{2})/u)\ll N^{1+\varepsilon}(1+\sigma)/d.

Proof.

We proceed as in Lemma
6.3
and consider the truncated Fourier decomposition as in (

6

, we see that upon adding or removing at most
\sigma

a complete interval modulo

. This means that the first term in the right hand side of (

\bigg|\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in I_{u},\\

\sigma

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\sum_{0<|s|\leqslant Q}\frac{1}{s}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant v^{\prime}\leqslant u,\\
(v^{\prime},u)=1\end{subarray}}e(sv^{\prime}r_{2}/u)\Big|

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\sum_{0<|s|\leqslant Q}\frac{1}{s}\sum_{r|(u,sr_{2})}r\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{\varepsilon}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\sum_{0<|s|\leqslant Q}\frac{(u,sr_{2})}{s}

\displaystyle=N^{\varepsilon}\sum_{0<|s|\leqslant Q}\frac{1}{s}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}(u,sr_{2})\ll_{\varepsilon}N^{1+\varepsilon}/d.

Thus, we are finished with the proof of Lemma
8.1
.
∎

We now return to the proof of the first inequality in (

). The left hand side of this inequality can be written as

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\
d\leqslant N\end{subarray}}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant N/d}\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{N}{d}+\frac{\Delta}{dN}-u\leqslant v\leqslant\frac{N}{d},\\

Note that for each fixed

lying in an interval of length

. Applying Lemma

, we see that the above sum is at most

\ll_{\varepsilon}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|h\\

which is precisely the desired bound.
