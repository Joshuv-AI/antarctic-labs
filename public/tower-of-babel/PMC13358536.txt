Prognostic value of pretreatment systemic immune-inflammation index for overall survival in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma in China: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Chenghai He, Kexin Chen, Yu Zhao, Liwei Ni, Ruonan He, Yaping Zhang, Nanting Sun, Guodong Li, Zhichao Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) has been identified as an independent risk factor for poor prognosis in patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC). However, findings reported in different studies are contradictory. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to clarify the relationship between pre-treatment SII and long-term survival outcomes in ESCC.MethodsSystematic searches were carried out in PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, CNKI, Wanfang, and SinoMed from their respective inceptions up to April 13, 2026. Eligible studies enrolled patients with histologically confirmed ESCC and a clearly defined pretreatment SII cut-off, only hazard ratios (HRs) from multivariate Cox regression were extracted. Non-original articles, low-quality studies, and those lacking key data were excluded. Two reviewers independently extracted data and assessed study quality using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Publication bias was evaluated with funnel plots and Egger’s test. Outcomes were overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), and progression-free survival (PFS).ResultsTen retrospective cohort studies (3,660 patients) were included, all conducted in China with NOS scores of 6–8. Elevated pretreatment SII was correlated with poorer OS (pooled HR =1.52, 95% CI: 1.36–1.69, P<0.001; I2=28%) and DFS/PFS [pooled HR =1.77, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.29–2.43, P<0.001; I2=67%]. The subgroup analysis indicated that the sample size might be the source of potential heterogeneity (P=0.02). Elevated SII correlated with male gender [odds ratio (OR) =1.44, 95% CI: 1.21–1.71], advanced T stage (OR =1.44, 95% CI: 1.09–1.91), advanced N stage (OR =1.31, 95% CI: 1.02–1.68), and advanced tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage (OR =1.52, 95% CI: 1.05–2.19). No evidence of publication bias was observed in DFS/PFS (Egger’s P=0.40), potential bias was observed for OS (Egger’s P=0.005), but the results remained robust after trim-and-fill correction (HR =1.42, 95% CI: 1.22–1.66).ConclusionsIn Chinese patients with ESCC, pretreatment SII appears to be a promising prognostic biomarker in ESCC, elevated levels predict poorer OS and DFS/PFS, and are associated with more advanced clinicopathological stage. Large scale prospective studies, especially in other ethnic populations, are needed to confirm the clinical applicability of this indicator.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) ranks eleventh in global cancer incidence and seventh in cancer-related mortality, with 510,716 new cases and 445,129 deaths reported worldwide in 2022 (1). Characterized by high malignancy and dismal prognosis with limited long-term survival, it remains a critical clinical health issue to be addressed. Two predominant histological subtypes are recognized, consisting of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC), with distinct geographical distribution. ESCC is predominant in East Asia, southern Central Asia and South Africa, whereas EAC mainly occurs in Northern Europe and North America (2).

Currently, the main therapeutic approaches for EC patients consist of surgical treatment, chemotherapy (CT), radiotherapy (RT) and chemoradiotherapy (CRT). Esophagectomy combined with lymphadenectomy is regarded as the most effective treatment option for EC patients. Nevertheless, this approach only confers a marginal survival advantage. A substantial number of esophageal carcinoma patients continue to face risks of both recurrence and systemic dissemination of malignant cells, even after radical surgical procedures. Moreover, many advanced treatment approaches, including targeted therapy and immunotherapy, have not been able to significantly improve the prognosis of EC patients (3). Therefore, the identification of non-invasive and readily available prognostic biomarkers will enable more accurate outcome prediction and personalized treatment strategies for patients with EC.

During the past several years, numerous factors about the prognosis of ESCC have been widely explored, including tumor histopathological type, tumor biological activity, tumor location, tumor differentiation degree, and tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage (4). The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), calculated as platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count, has emerged as a significant prognostic biomarker in various malignancies. Extensive research has demonstrated significant associations between increased SII levels and unfavorable clinical outcomes across diverse malignancies. This correlation has been well-documented in multiple cancer types, including but not limited to oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma (5), primary hepatic malignancies (6), pulmonary neoplasms (7,8), renal cell carcinoma (9), colorectal adenocarcinoma (10), uterine cervical cancer (11), and adenocarcinomas of the gastroesophageal junction (12). However, different studies have reached different conclusions regarding the prognostic value of SII in ESCC. Some studies suggest that SII is significantly associated with overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS)/progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with ESCC (13,14), whereas others found no significant association. For instance, Gao found that although elevated SII prior to treatment was an independent predictor of OS and DFS in patients with stage I–II ESCC, SII did not retain independent prognostic significance for OS in stage III patients (15). In a study involving 370 patients with ESCC, Xu found that SII was not significantly associated with OS in either univariate or multivariate Cox regression analyses (16). Similarly, in a study of elderly ESCC patients (aged ≥70 years) receiving RT or CRT, one previous study by Zhang revealed that SII correlated with OS in univariate analysis, it failed to retain independent predictive value in multivariate models, implying that its predictive performance might be affected by age disparities and treatment confounding factors (17). This controversy could be attributed to disparities in research design, sample scale, enrolled population characteristics and statistical approaches. To address these controversies, we used meta-analyses to clarify the relevance between SII in Chinese patients with ESCC and to explore its correlation with clinicopathological features. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (18) (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0305/rc).

Methods

Search strategies

We conducted a comprehensive exploration of the published works in the Web of Science, Embase, PubMed, CNKI, Wanfang and SinoMed with a time frame of the build-up to April 13, 2026, using the search terms: (esophageal OR esophagus) AND (neoplasm OR carcinoma OR cancer OR tumor) AND (systemic immune-inflammation index OR SII) AND (survival OR prognosis OR prognostic). To ensure the comprehensiveness of the search, we also repeatedly screened the references that were included in the research for any eligible articles that might have been missed during the database searches. Given that the current meta-analytic investigation was conducted through existing articles, no ethical approval was needed.

Study selection

Eligible investigations were selected in accordance with the PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study design) framework and the following inclusion criteria: (I) population: patients with histologically confirmed ESCC; (II) intervention/exposure: pre-treatment SII index measured from peripheral blood before any anti-cancer treatment is initiated, with a clearly defined cut-off value; (III) comparison: patients stratified into high and low SII groups according to the predefined cut-off value; (IV) outcomes: hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence interval (CI) for OS, DFS or PFS derived from multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses; (V) study design: retrospective or prospective cohort studies. Exclusion criteria encompassed: (I) duplicate publications; (II) non-original research articles including reviews, letters, case reports, conference proceedings, meta-analysis and editorials; (III) studies lacking essential data for survival analysis; (IV) preclinical animal studies; (V) articles with a Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) score of less than 6 points that are of low quality.

Data processing

Two independent evaluators screened all retrieved records, extracted data, with the methodological quality of included studies being evaluated. All discrepancies were settled either through joint discussion or by seeking advice from a third researcher. The following information was extracted from each eligible study using a data extraction form: first author’s name, publication year, country, sample size, treatment modality, median age, TNM stage, SII cut-off value, method for determining the cut-off value, survival outcomes reported (OS, DFS/PFS), HRs with 95% CIs from multivariate analysis, and relevant clinicopathological characteristics. When a study reported both unmatched and propensity score-matched (PSM) results, data from the PSM cohort were preferentially extracted to minimize potential confounding bias. We assessed the quality of the included studies using the NOS, which is a 9-point scale (19). Articles achieving an NOS score of six and above were considered to have acceptable methodological quality and were included in the final analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were all conducted using RevMan 5.3 and Stata 18.0 software. OS was defined as the primary outcome, whereas DFS and PFS were set as secondary outcomes. To evaluate the prognostic effect of pretreatment SII on survival outcomes, pooled HRs and corresponding 95% CIs were computed. For the analysis of clinical and pathological characteristics, odds ratios (OR) with 95% CIs were applied to evaluate the association between SII and multiple categorical variables (gender, T stage, N stage, TNM stage and histological differentiation). A P value less than 0.10 in the Q-test or an I2 value exceeding 50% was deemed to indicate significant heterogeneity, if this was the case, a random-effects model was utilized, if not, a fixed-effects model was applied. To explore possible causes of heterogeneity, we performed a priori subgroup analyses based on the following variables: (I) sample size (>292 vs. ≤292); (II) SII cut-off value (>519.86 vs. ≤519.86); (III) treatment modality (surgery vs. CRT or RT). The cut-off thresholds for sample size and SII values were determined based on the median values from the 10 included studies. Sensitivity assessments were conducted via a stepwise exclusion approach to evaluate the robustness of the pooled effect sizes. To assess publication bias, funnel plots and Egger’s linear regression test were utilized, a P value below 0.05 was considered to indicate the presence of publication bias. A trim-and-fill analysis was conducted to estimate the adjusted pooled effect size after accounting for theoretically missing studies. All statistical assessments were two-tailed, with a P value below 0.05 being considered statistically significant. To ensure consistency in effect sizes, the HR was uniformly expressed as the risk associated with high SII relative to low SII.

Results

Study information

The processes of literature search and study selection are illustrated in Figure 1. A database search initially yielded 237 records, including 60 from PubMed, Web of Science [63], EMBASE [73], CNKI [25], Wanfang [11] and SinoMed [5]. Following comprehensive manual screening and filtering, we ultimately incorporated 10 studies involving 3,660 patients into the present meta-analysis (14,15,20-27). The baseline features of the 10 included studies are shown in Table 1. All studies were conducted in China, with publication years ranging from 2016 to 2024. Sample sizes were 82 to 916 cases, and the median was 292 cases. Eight studies included patients for whom surgery was the primary treatment modality, whilst two studies included patients receiving CRT or RT. The SII cut-off values across the studies ranged from 307.00 to 1,196.60, with a median of 519.86. All 10 studies employed multivariate analysis and reported OS as an outcome measure, three studies also reported DFS or PFS. Based on the NOS assessment, the methodological quality of the included studies was overall high, with scores ranging from 6 to 8, detailed scores are presented in Table S1.

Pooled analysis

Association of SII with OS

Ten studies involving 3,660 patients reported HR for OS and the relevant data are presented in Table 2. The synthesized analysis found that individuals with a raised SII experienced markedly reduced OS relative to those with lower SII (HR =1.52, 95% CI: 1.36–1.69, P<0.001). Because the potential heterogeneity among these studies is relatively small (P=0.18, I2=28%, Figure 2), we used a fixed effects model. In addition, we conducted subgroup analyses on the sample size, treatment methods, and SII cut-off values to explore the correlation between SII and OS. The results are as follows: when stratified by sample size (sample size >292: HR =1.42, 95% CI: 1.25–1.60, P<0.001, I2=0% vs. sample size ≤292: HR =1.97, 95% CI: 1.55–2.52, P<0.001, I2=4%), stratified by the cut-off value for SII (cut-off value >519.86: HR =1.55, 95% CI: 1.25–1.93, P<0.001, I2=0% vs. cut-off value ≤519.86: HR =1.58, 95% CI: 1.30–1.92, P<0.001, I2=49%) and stratified by the treatment modality (surgery: HR =1.65, 95% CI: 1.37–1.98, P<0.001, I2=41% vs. CRT/RT: HR =1.44, 95% CI: 1.15–1.80, P=0.001, I2=0%). The subgroup analysis results indicated that the sample size might be the source of heterogeneity (P=0.02). In contrast, the SII cutoff value (P=0.91) and the treatment method (P=0.37) were unable to explain the observed heterogeneity (Figure 3).

Relationship between SII and DFS/PFS

Three studies involving 1,051 patients reported the HR for DFS/PFS. As there was potential heterogeneity in these studies (P=0.05, I2=67%), we used a random-effects model to synthesize HRs (Figure 4). The findings indicate that an elevated SII demonstrates a clear connection to reduced DFS/PFS (pooled HR =1.77, 95% CI: 1.29–2.43, P<0.001).

Forest plots of the SII-DFS/PFS association. CI, confidence interval; DFS, disease-free survival; df, degrees of freedom; IV, inverse variance; PFS, progression-free survival; SE, standard error; SII, systemic immune-inflammation index.

Relationship between SII and clinicopathologic characteristics

We collected five clinicopathologic characteristics, including gender, histological grade, TNM stage, T stage and N stage and used ORs with 95% CI as parameters to determine their relationship with SII in ESCC. High SII was linked to male gender (pooled OR =1.44, 95% CI: 1.21–1.71, P<0.001; I2=17%, 9 studies), advanced T stage (T3–T4 vs. T1–T2: pooled OR =1.44, 95% CI: 1.09–1.91, P=0.01; I2=62%, 6 studies), advanced N stage (N1–N3 vs. N0: pooled OR =1.31, 95% CI: 1.02–1.68, P=0.03; I2=58%, 6 studies), and advanced TNM stage (III–IV vs. I–II: pooled OR =1.52, 95% CI: 1.05–2.19, P=0.03; I2=72%, 5 studies). The combined data also showed that SII in ESCC patients was not significantly connected with histological grade (poor vs. well/moderate: pooled OR =0.86, 95% CI: 0.71–1.04, P=0.13; I2=0%, 5 studies) (Table 3 and Figure 5).

Sensitivity analysis

In order to maintain the stability of the study, we conducted a sensitivity analysis of OS using a literature-by-literature culling approach. We removed one study at a time and combined the remaining studies to assess whether each study affected the results and ultimately we observed that the effect sizes were all kept within a certain range (1.32–1.82) (Figure 6), which suggests that our study’s analysis is reliable.

Publication bias

Publication bias was examined by means of funnel plots and Egger’s test. The funnel plot for OS appeared slightly asymmetric, and Egger’s test indicated potential publication bias (P=0.005) (Figure 7A). For DFS/PFS, the funnel plot was visually symmetric, with Egger’s test demonstrating no significant evidence of publication bias (P=0.401) (Figure 7B). To evaluate the impact of potential bias for OS, a trim-and-fill analysis was performed. The method imputed six hypothetically missing studies, and the adjusted pooled HR remained statistically significant and consistent in direction with the original estimate (adjusted HR=1.42, 95% CI: 1.22–1.66, P<0.001 (Figure 8).

Discussion

Accurately identifying prognostic indicators to predict adverse outcomes and guide treatment strategies is of utmost importance for ESCC patients. Hematological biomarkers are regarded as an ideal option for prognostic assessment due to their accessibility. The SII, a novel systemic marker of inflammation, has distinct advantages such as simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and wide clinical applicability, making it superior to some other prognostic indicators. Numerous clinical investigations have confirmed that SII serves as a valuable prognostic indicator in various tumor diseases (28). In this meta-analysis of ten Chinese studies including 3,660 patients with ESCC, elevated pretreatment SII was markedly linked to poorer OS and DFS/PFS. Importantly, we identified sample size as a contributor to heterogeneity in OS (P=0.02). After stratifying by sample size, the heterogeneity within the larger-study subgroup (>292 patients) was completely eliminated (I2=0%), and the within-subgroup heterogeneity for smaller studies was also trivial (I2=4%). The pooled HRs remained statistically significant in both strata. In contrast, SII cut-off value and treatment modality did not significantly modify the prognostic effect of SII (P=0.91 and P=0.37). These findings support the robustness of SII as a prognostic marker across diverse clinical scenarios, while emphasizing the importance of interpreting pooled estimates from small studies with caution. In addition, SII levels showed a notable correlation with clinicopathologic features such as Male, TNM stage, T stage and N stage. These findings suggest that SII is not only an independent predictor of prognosis in ESCC patients, but its close association with disease progression also makes it promising as an important biomarker for assessing tumor progression.

Regarding the secondary outcomes, the pooled analysis of DFS/PFS indicated that increased pretreatment SII level was markedly linked to a poorer prognosis. However, due to the limited number of included studies (a total of 3) and moderate to high heterogeneity (P=0.05, I2=67%), this result should be interpreted with caution. Due to the small number of studies, meaningful subgroup analyses could not be conducted to explore the potential causes of this heterogeneity. Contributing factors may include differences in the definition and assessment of DFS/PFS, differences in follow-up plans, and differences in treatment methods among different studies. Larger-scale prospective studies are necessary to validate this conclusion.

Inflammation has been increasingly recognized as a pivotal factor influencing aspects of tumourigenesis, progression, metastasis and dissemination, as supported by accumulating evidence (29). The SII equals platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count. Among its components, neutrophils have garnered substantial attention for their tumor-promoting properties, while platelets contribute to cancer progression by shielding malignant cells from natural killer (NK) cell-mediated cytotoxicity and mechanical stress, conversely, lymphocytes demonstrate critical anti-tumor capabilities through their cancer cell elimination functions (30-33). Numerous meta-analytic studies have investigated SII’s prognostic relevance across different malignancies (28,34,35). Notably, a large-scale meta-analysis encompassing 15 independent studies demonstrated that increased SII was consistently connected with unfavorable OS outcomes in diverse cancer types (34). These findings have been further substantiated by subsequent meta-analyses, which have validated SII’s prognostic utility in specific cancers like gastric malignancies, non-small cell lung carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma (35-37).

SII is vital for the diagnosis and treatment of ESCC patients. SII can effectively reflect the tumor burden and inflammatory status of ESCC patients. Tumor cells can release a variety of cytokines, stimulate bone marrow hematopoiesis, increase platelets and at the same time trigger an inflammatory response, ultimately manifesting as lymphocyte depletion and concomitant elevation of the SII. Elevated SII values serve as a robust indicator of a more severe inflammatory response, weaker anti-tumor immune function and an increased likelihood of recurrence and metastasis, indicating a more critical condition. In terms of treatment, SII can influence clinical decision-making. For patients with elevated SII, more aggressive comprehensive treatment strategies, such as preoperative neoadjuvant RT or postoperative adjuvant therapy, are recommended. During the course of ESCC treatment, SII can serve as a dynamic monitoring index to evaluate treatment efficacy. A gradual decrease in SII towards the normal range indicates that the treatment is effective and the patient’s condition is under control. Conversely, a persistent rise or lack of significant change in SII may suggest that the treatment is insufficient, necessitating adjustments to the treatment program. We hope that we can enhance prognostic outcomes for the patients by monitoring the changes in SII to adjust the patient’s treatment regimen.

Although this meta-analysis provides insights, several insufficiencies need to be considered. Firstly, as the locations of all studies were in China, the applicability of SII’s prognostic value across diverse ethnic populations is unclear. Second, the studies we included were retrospective. Furthermore, the SII cut-off value varied greatly among different studies, ranging from 307 to 1,196.6, which may be affected by the heterogeneity of the population in each study, differences in statistical methods, differences in treatment methods, differences in laboratory data testing, and differences in study design. Finally, only three studies reported the data on DFS/PFS, and the heterogeneity of this indicator was quite high (I2=67%), which may affect the clinical practical application of SII as a prognostic biomarker.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis demonstrates that increased pre-treatment SII is closely linked to unfavorable clinical outcomes in Chinese patients with ESCC. Furthermore, strong relationships were observed between pre-treatment SII elevation and advanced tumor characteristics, including higher TNM stage, T stage, and N stage. These collective findings of SII may serve as a promising and readily accessible prognostic biomarker in ESCC management. However, given the limitations of the study, further large-scale prospective studies are needed to standardise the optimal cut-off values and validate their clinical utility.
