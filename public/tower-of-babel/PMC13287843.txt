Characteristics of Adolescents and Young Adults Visiting an Emergency Department Due to Self-Harm or Suicide Attempts: Risk Factors for Severe Medical Outcome and Short-Term Revisits
Authors: Kyungseok Park, Tae Han Kim, Kyoung Jun Song, Jonghwan Shin, Jin Hee Jung, Hui Jai Lee, Yeong Hyeok Kim, Stephen Gyung Won Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicide and self-harm among adolescents or young adults represent major public health issues, characterized by high recurrence rates and significant social burdens. This study aimed to identify demographic and clinical characteristics, as well as risk factors associated with severe medical outcomes and short-term revisits among this population.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 1,445 adolescents or young adults (≤ 24 years old) who visited an emergency department (ED) designated as a regional psychiatric emergency center in Seoul, Korea, for suicide attempts or self-harm from January 2015 to December 2022. Data collected included demographics, medical and psychiatric histories, details of the incidents, and clinical outcomes. Severe medical outcomes were defined as admissions to non-psychiatric wards, intensive care units, surgical hospitalization, or transfers. Short-term ED revisits within 60 days were analyzed. Multivariable logistic regression was employed to identify independent factors associated with severe medical outcomes and ED revisits.ResultsAmong the study participants, 1,090 (75.4%) were female, and most incidents were impulsive and non-lethal. Severe medical outcomes occurred in 8.7% of visits and were significantly associated with planned suicide attempts (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.92; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.02–3.62) and the use of high-lethality methods such as pesticides, chemicals, inhalation, hanging, and falls. Short-term revisits within 60 days occurred in 7.1% of patients, with significant factors associated with revisits including female gender (aOR, 1.93; 95% CI, 1.04–3.55), living alone (aOR, 1.57; 95% CI, 1.00–2.44), expressed requests for help (aOR, 1.68; 95% CI, 1.04–2.74), and history of psychiatric treatment (aOR, 2.41; 95% CI, 1.15–5.04).ConclusionTargeted ED-based and community interventions, along with timely psychiatric care, tailored to the specific characteristics and risk profiles of adolescents or young adults may help reduce severe outcomes and prevent repeat self-harm and suicide attempts.

INTRODUCTION

Suicide and self-harm are significant public health concerns both in Korea and internationally.12 Globally, suicide accounts for over 700,000 deaths annually.3 Since 2018, Korea has reported the highest suicide rate among Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries, with notable increases among adolescents or young adults.4 Individuals who attempt suicide or engage in self-harm often require immediate medical attention due to unpredictable and serious incidents, commonly resulting in emergency department (ED) visits.2456

Adolescents or young adults are known to especially be prone to repeated suicide attempts and self-harm.7 Korean nationwide data indicates a rising trend in ED visits due to suicide attempts among this demographic, with marked increases in individuals under 10 years old and those in their twenties.8 Although lethality is generally lower in this group, repeated attempts substantially increase the risk of eventual suicide and long-term disability, thereby imposing significant social and healthcare burdens.9 Several studies have described the demographic and clinical characteristics of adolescents presenting to the ED with self-harm or suicide attempts. For example, Tomaszek et al.10 reported on the demographic and clinical profiles of adolescent suicide attempters admitted to an ED during the COVID-19 pandemic, underscoring the role of psychosocial stressors. In addition, Lee et al.11 demonstrated that media-based exposure to nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI) was associated with increased ED visits for self-harm among Korean youths, highlighting the growing influence of sociocultural factors on suicidal behaviors. While these studies provide important epidemiologic insights, few have specifically examined risk factors for severe medical outcomes and short-term revisits among adolescents or young adults in Korea. Identifying such risk factors is therefore crucial for targeted prevention, yet data stratified by demographic characteristics remain limited.

In this study, we planned to evaluate the characteristics of suicide attempts or self-harm injuries among adolescents or young adults visiting an ED designated as a regional psychiatric emergency center, analyzing risk factors associated with severe injuries and short-term revisits.

METHODS

Study design and setting

A retrospective study was conducted at an urban academic hospital ED in Seoul, Korea. The study ED annually receives approximately 50,000 emergency patients. It serves as a specialized psychiatric emergency center and participates in the nationwide Psychiatric Crisis Response Centers (PCRC) program, aimed at providing post-discharge interventions and community connections for suicide-related visits.12 A dedicated psychiatric emergency social worker coordinates patient care and data collection for patients visiting with self-injury and suicide attempts.

Study population

All patients who visited the study ED due to suicide attempts or self-harm between January 2015 and December 2022 were included in the initial analysis. Patients who visited ED with only suicidal ideation were excluded. Patients were divided into four age groups (≤ 24, 25–40, 41–60, ≥ 61) to compare their characteristics. The cutoff of ≤ 24 years was chosen to represent adolescents or young adults, a group with particularly high risk of suicidal behaviors.7 The 25–40, 41–60, and ≥ 61 years categories were defined according to developmental life stages, reflecting early adulthood, midlife, and older adulthood, respectively, each associated with distinct psychosocial and medical risk profiles.

To identify repeated ED visits by the same patient, ED visit records spanning one year before and after the study period were reviewed.

Data collection and variables

Demographic information of all patients enrolled in the PCRC program was collected through face-to-face or telephone interviews by PCRC social workers. The collected information was recorded in electronic medical records and subsequently transferred to the national PCRC database, named the Suicide Prevention Emergency Department Information System. Variables included demographics, incident details, medical and psychiatric history, suicidal ideation, and ED clinical outcomes.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was severe medical outcome at ED discharge, defined as admissions to non-psychiatric wards, intensive care units (ICUs), surgical hospitalization, or transfers due to medical priorities. The secondary outcome was short-term ED revisits within 60 days for separate suicide attempts or self-harm incidents, requiring re-enrollment in the PCRC database.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests, while continuous variables were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum or Kruskal-Wallis tests. Patients aged 24 or below were selected for detailed analysis. The variables were compared according to the outcome variables. Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified independent factors associated with severe medical outcomes and short-term revisits. Data analysis was conducted using STATA/SE version 18, with significance defined as a two-tailed P value < 0.05.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul Metropolitan Government- Seoul National University (SMG-SNU) Boramae Medical Center and the requirement for informed consent was waived (IRB No. 30-2023-108).

A total of 1,445 adolescents or young adults were enrolled in final analysis. The distribution of age in the group was plotted in Fig. 3. Comparative analyses of patient characteristics were performed according to severe medical outcomes (Table 2). The proportion of patients with prior suicide attempts was lower among patients with severe medical outcomes compared to those without severe medical outcomes (46.8% vs. 64.4%, P < 0.001). Planned suicide attempts or self-injury were more frequent in the severe medical outcome group (14.3% vs. 7.8%, P = 0.012). A higher proportion of patients in the severe medical outcome group had no history of past or current psychiatric treatment (46.0% vs. 24.5%, P < 0.001). In multivariable logistic regression identifying factors associated with severe medical outcomes, planned attempts were significantly associated with severe outcomes (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.92; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.02–3.62). Methods involving pesticides, chemicals, inhalation, hanging, and falls were also significantly associated with severe outcomes (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

This study identified key characteristics and risk factors among adolescents or young adults visiting EDs after suicide attempts or self-harm. Female predominance, frequent impulsivity, and lower-lethality methods were observed, consistent with prior literature.13 Severe medical outcomes were more frequently observed among individuals with fewer prior attempts, emphasizing the critical importance of primary prevention strategies. Frequent revisits, particularly among females and socially isolated individuals, highlight the need for continued psychiatric care and interventions targeting social support.14

The findings in this study were consistent with previous reported evidence on youth suicidal behaviors.10 We also observed a female predominance among adolescents or young adults presenting to the ED after suicide attempts. A Swedish multicenter study found nearly half of youth attempts were impulsive and that these were just as likely to result in serious medical injury as planned attempts.15 The level of planning preceding a suicide attempt is another key predictor of medical outcome severity. Research indicates that well-planned attempts tend to be more lethal and cause more serious injuries. For instance, a study of depressed individuals who attempted suicide compared those who planned their attempt (≥ 3 hours of planning) versus those who acted impulsively. The planned-attempt group had significantly more severe medical consequences from their attempts, meaning they were more likely to sustain life-threatening injuries requiring hospital/ICU care.16

In our study, the subset of patients who carried out well-planned attempts or chose highly lethal methods (e.g. hanging, jumping from heights, pesticide poisoning) had significantly higher rates of severe medical outcomes, in some cases requiring ICU admission or surgical management. In other studies, the lethality of the method is a pivotal determinant of outcomes: for example, case fatality rates range from over 75% for firearms to only 1–4% for cutting or overdose.17 One study of medically serious attempts reported that individuals requiring ICU admission were significantly less likely to have used self-poisoning or cutting (lower-lethality methods) compared to those treated on regular ward.18 Nonetheless, the strong association we observed between planned attempts and severe injuries highlights that clinicians must swiftly identify high-intent cases for aggressive medical and psychiatric intervention.

Repeated suicide attempts and ED revisits emerged as another key concern. Many individuals in our cohort had a history of prior attempts, and females and socially isolated youths were especially prone to short-term revisits after an index attempt. Recent prospective data underscore the high risk of recurrence in youth: one French cohort (SURAYA) reported a 17.8% re-attempt rate within just 3 months of a first hospital-treated suicide attempt in adolescents. The study also reported prior suicidal behavior is the strongest predictor of future attempts.19 Similarly, a large California study found that adolescents with any self-harm history were far more likely to cycle back to EDs for subsequent self-harm, with a five-year repeat rate around 19%.20

Meanwhile, the frequent repeat attempters in our sample (often young women or those lacking social supports) emphasize the need for sustained psychiatric care and follow-up. Each ED presentation for self-harm should be seen as a critical opportunity to interrupt an escalating trajectory; without effective intervention, repeated non-fatal attempts greatly increase the cumulative risk of eventual suicide or long-term disability.12

Our study has several limitations. This retrospective, single-center design and reliance on self-reported data present several limitations. Although suicide-related deaths (death on arrival or during ED care) were descriptively presented in Table 1, they were excluded from subsequent analyses because patient or guardian interviews could not be performed, resulting in substantial missing data for key variables. In addition, our estimation of revisit rates may have been underestimated, as we were unable to capture ED revisits to other hospitals outside of our institution. Our findings may have been influenced by survivor bias, as patients with prior attempts or without psychiatric treatment who died from fatal outcomes were not included in the analyses. However, the number of such fatal cases in the ≤ 24 years group was small, so this bias is unlikely to have substantially altered the overall results. Moreover, our study did not fully distinguish between NSSI and true suicidal attempts, as the assessment of intent was often subjective and difficult to ascertain, which may have influenced the observed associations. Future multicenter, prospective studies could address these limitations.

The number of adolescents or young adults visiting an ED due to self-harm and suicide attempts is rapidly increasing, and their characteristics differ from those of other age groups. An appropriate ED-based and community intervention tailored to the characteristics of the target age group is necessary to reduce the suicide rate.
