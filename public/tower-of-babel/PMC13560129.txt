Risk factors and a prediction model of poor prognosis in patients with invasive aspergillosis in a general hospital
Authors: Xin Li, Mingyue Niu, Minghuai Zhang, Qianqian Zheng, Xing Ge, Wenhui Zhang, Tingting Yu, Hong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study retrospectively analyzed the epidemiological trends of invasive aspergillosis (IA) at the Anhui Public Health Clinical Center. By evaluating the clinical risk factors associated with IA, an individualized nomogram prediction model was constructed. This study aims to improve early diagnosis and clinical management of severe invasive aspergillosis.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of 307 patients diagnosed with IA between January 2019 and March 2024. Patients were categorized into two groups based on clinical outcomes: the favorable outcome group (n = 268) and the unfavorable outcome group (n = 39). Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and multivariable logistic regression analyses were employed to identify independent risk factors associated with poor prognosis. Subsequently, a nomogram was constructed to develop a risk prediction model for IA.ResultsFour variables associated with poor prognosis were identified via LASSO regression: ICU length of stay, elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), elevated blood glucose, and renal dysfunction (all P < 0.05). Multivariable logistic regression revealed that ICU length of stay (OR = 1.049, 95%CI: 1.003–1.096, P = 0.028), C-reactive protein (OR = 1.007, 95%CI: 1.003–1.012, P = 0.001), blood glucose (OR = 1.119, 95%CI: 1.041–1.206, P = 0.003), and renal failure (yes vs. no, OR = 3.187, 95%CI: 1.412–7.048, P = 0.004) were independent risk factors for poor prognosis. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was 0.810, with a bootstrap-validated AUC of 0.7962, demonstrating favourable discrimination and stability. Calibration curves indicated favourable agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test showed no significant deviation (P = 0.615), confirming favorable model fit.ConclusionsIA detection increased significantly over the past two years. We developed and validated a risk-factor-based nomogram that demonstrates good predictive accuracy and robustness in identifying poor prognosis among IA patients. This individualized tool, accessible via an interactive web-based application, provides clinicians with a good basis for early risk stratification and timely therapeutic intervention, ultimately aiming to improve clinical outcomes in high-risk populations.

Introduction

Invasive Aspergillus species are among the most prevalent causes of human fungal infections, manifesting in a broad spectrum of diseases, particularly in immunocompromised individuals and patients with chronic pulmonary conditions [1]. Notably, invasive aspergillosis (IA) frequently co-occurs with bacterial infections; their synergistic interaction can exacerbate disease severity and contribute to unfavorable clinical outcomes [2]. In recent years, surveillance data from our institution indicate a significant and sustained increase in the incidence of invasive aspergillosis, which is associated with a higher rate of adverse clinical outcomes.

However, the limited sensitivity of current mycological diagnostic tests poses a significant challenge for timely clinical diagnosis and intervention. Consequently, identifying specific risk factors and early recognition of high-risk patients are paramount for disease prevention, early therapeutic initiation, and the ultimate improvement of survival rates [3]. Despite this need, favourable scientific techniques or good methods based on single parameters to accurately predict patient outcomes remain scarce. Nomograms, as comprehensive risk assessment tools, can provide individualized predictions of clinical events by integrating multiple variables from logistic regression models, and have gained widespread application in clinical risk evaluation [4–6].

This study aims to investigate the evolving epidemiological trends of IA in recent years and analyze the risk factors associated with infection and poor prognosis. Furthermore, we sought to develop and validate an individualized nomogram to predict the risk of unfavorable outcomes in patients with IA, providing a scientific basis for early accurate diagnosis and the management of severe infections.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Anhui Public Health Clinical Center. We screened all inpatients (n = 432) whose sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) cultures tested positive for Aspergillus species in the Microbiology Laboratory between January 2019 and March 2024. Medical records for each case were independently reviewed, and the clinical diagnosis of IA was adjudicated by experienced clinicians based on established diagnostic criteria. Consequently, a total of 307 patients meeting the inclusion criteria for IA were enrolled in the study. Based on their clinical outcomes, these patients were categorized into two groups: the favorable outcome group (n = 268) and the unfavorable outcome group (n = 39).

Diagnostic and exclusion criteria

The clinical diagnosis of invasive aspergillosis was established in accordance with the revised and updated consensus definitions from the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer and the Mycoses Study Group Education and Research Consortium (EORTC/MSGERC). Patients were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria: (1) incomplete or missing essential clinical information in medical records; (2) repeated hospitalizations with an interval of less than one month between admissions; (3) pregnancy.

Methods

Data collection

Clinical data of patients with IA admitted to the Anhui Public Health Clinical Center between January 2019 and March 2024 were retrospectively collected. Data were retrieved from the Hospital Infection Management System, Laboratory Information System, and Electronic Medical Record system. All data were verified against original medical records to ensure accuracy. The diagnosis of IA in this study was strictly based on the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer/Mycoses Study Group (EORTC/MSG) consensus criteria revised in 2008 (De Pauw B, et al., Clin Infect Dis, 2008). To rule out respiratory colonization or specimen contamination, all patients enrolled in this study met the criteria of “Proven” or “Probable” IA as determined retrospectively and independently by two experienced clinicians combined with radiologic and clinical data. Patients with only fungal colonization or “Possible” diagnosis were excluded. Among the 432 patients with positive fungal culture in the laboratory, 307 patients with clinical diagnosis of invasive aspergillosis finally entered the next clinical study. For laboratory parameters, the first test results obtained upon admission were used for statistical analysis. In cases of recurrent IA infections where the interval between hospitalizations exceeded one month, the initial clinical data from each admission were recorded separately. The collected variables included: (1) General information: department, specimen type, time of submission, and primary diagnosis; (2) Clinical characteristics: comorbidities, age, sex, smoking history (including intensity), and history of chronic or immune-related diseases; (3) Clinical symptoms; and (4) Laboratory findings: C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), white blood cell (WBC) count, absolute neutrophil count (ANC), absolute eosinophil count (AEC), hemoglobin, albumin, and globulin concentrations, as well as absolute counts of CD4 + and CD8 + T-lymphocyte subsets. Patients were categorized into two groups based on their clinical outcomes at discharge: the favorable outcome group and the unfavorable outcome group. The data were extracted from the front page of medical records. The patients whose condition was improved and laboratory indicators were significantly improved at discharge were included in the favorable outcome group, and the rest of the patients were re-reviewed by two doctors with clinical experience. The patients who were discharged automatically due to economic or family reasons, died, gave up treatment due to deterioration, and were in the unfavorable outcome group.

Variable selection and data management

Potential risk factors associated with IA were identified through an extensive literature review and expert consultations. To ensure data integrity and accuracy, clinical information for each patient was collected by a dedicated researcher using a standardized collection form. Subsequently, all data underwent independent double-entry and cross-verification by two investigators.

The collected variables included: (1) Demographics: age, sex, and body weight; (2) Comorbidities; (3) Pre-infection medication history: including glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, and various antibiotic classes (e.g., penicillins, cephalosporins, macrolides, carbapenems, and glycopeptides); (4) Clinical interventions: Intensive Care Unit (ICU) admission, mechanical ventilation, and invasive procedures; and (5) Laboratory parameters: infection-related hematological and biochemical indicators. The compiled dataset was then subjected to systematic investigation, comparative analysis, and statistical evaluation.

Fungal culture and identification

Respiratory specimens, including qualified sputum and BALF, were collected from all participants for fungal culture. The specimens were inoculated onto blood agar and Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) and incubated at 28 °C for a duration of 48 to 168 h. Following the observation of fungal growth, colonies were identified through morphological examination using lactophenol cotton blue (LPCB) staining and proteomic analysis via mass spectrometry. Species-level identification was performed using the MALDI Biotyper system (Bruker Daltonics; distributed/serviced by Zhengzhou Autobio Diagnostics Co., Ltd.).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.3.0 (http://www.r-project.org/). Variables with substantial missing data, such as absolute CD4 + and CD8 + T-lymphocyte counts, were excluded from the analysis. A total of 307 participants were assessed for missing data. The missing rates for individual variables ranged from 0% to 16.3%. Variables that included missing values included BMI (n = 27,8.8%), LDL (n = 50,16.3%), CRP (n = 10,3.3%), TG (n = 10, 16.3%), 3.3%), Glu (n = 4,1.3%), COPD_year (n = 1,0.3%), Cough (n = 1,0.3%), WBC (n = 1,0.3%), NEU (n = 1,0.3%), Lym (n = 1,0.3%) and ALT (n = 1,0.3%). Multiple imputation with chained equations (MICE) was used to handle missing data. For continuous variables, predictive mean matching (PMM) method was used. For binary categorical variables, logistic regression (logreg) was used. The imputation model included all variables with missing values and all clinically relevant covariates to improve imputation accuracy.

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s) and compared using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data are expressed as median and interquartile range [M (Q1, Q3)] and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages (%), with inter-group comparisons performed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

To identify predictors of poor prognosis in patients with invasive aspergillosis, potential covariates identified in univariate analysis were subjected to LASSO regression. Four key features were selected to construct the predictive model. Independent risk factors were identified and incorporated into a nomogram.

The model’s performance was rigorously validated across three dimensions: (1) Discriminatory power, evaluated via the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve AUC; (2) Calibration, assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and GiViTI calibration belts, with internal validation performed using the Bootstrap method (1,000 resamples); and (3) Clinical utility, evaluated through Decision Curve Analysis (DCA). To enhance clinical accessibility, a dynamic web-based nomogram application was developed. The overall workflow of the predictive modeling is summarized in Fig. 1, following the TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis) statement. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 307 patients were included in this retrospective analysis. The cohort was predominantly male (211/307, 68.73%), while females accounted for 31.27% (96/307). The median age of the overall cohort was 69.00 (58.00, 77.00) years old, and the median BMI was 21.56 (18.78, 24.22) kg/m². In terms of clinical characteristics, the median length of hospital stay was 11.00 (7.00, 18.00) days, and the median duration of antibiotic use was 10.00 (7.00, 16.00) days. Notably, over 70% of the patients received corticosteroid treatment during their hospitalization. Detailed baseline clinical characteristics and laboratory results are summarized in Table 1.

The most prevalent comorbidities among the hospitalized patients were chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD; 29.97%), diabetes mellitus (23.45%), and malignancy (17.26%), while hypertension and renal failure both accounted for 18.24%, highlighting the substantial burden of chronic diseases in this population. Hematologic disorders were also noted, including neutropenia (14.66%), hematologic malignancies (11.40%), and thrombocytopenia (4.89%). In contrast, bronchial asthma, atrial fibrillation, coronary heart disease, pancytopenia, and liver cirrhosis were relatively infrequent, each with a prevalence of less than 3%. Additionally, autoimmune diseases (12.38%) and cerebrovascular diseases (10.75%) represented significant proportions of the cohort. The study period recorded the data after the peak of SARS-CoV-2 infection after December 22, and SARS-CoV-2 accounted for 83.39%, indicating that the vast majority of samples were infected with SARS-CoV-2 after December 2022. Distribution of aspergillus species and departmental origin

Regarding the etiology of infection, Aspergillus fumigatus was the most prevalent species, identified in 184 cases (59.93%), significantly outnumbering other species as the predominant pathogen. This was followed by A. flavus (n = 46, 14.98%) and A. niger (n = 43, 14.01%), which exhibited comparable proportions. A. terreus ranked fourth, accounting for 8.14% (n = 25) of the isolates, while all other species combined represented only 3.28% of the total. Although Aspergillus infections were distributed across various clinical settings, the Department of Respiratory Medicine recorded the highest burden with 148 cases (48.21%). The ICU (n = 37, 12.05%) and the Department of Hematology (n = 32, 10.42%) followed, collectively accounting for 22.47% of the cohort. This distribution suggests that critically ill patients and those with hematological malignancies constitute the primary populations undergoing screening and diagnostic testing for invasive aspergillosis.

Regarding the temporal distribution, the detection of IA remained low prior to December 2022, with monthly cases consistently staying below 10. However, from the beginning of 2023, the monthly detection rate increased significantly, consistently exceeding 10 cases per month. Two prominent peaks were observed in March 2023 and January 2024. Notable seasonal variations were evident throughout the year, with higher incidence rates concentrated during the periods of from April to May and from October to December (Fig. 2).

Predictors selection and model building

Predictors selection

In the univariate analysis, several clinical parameters showed significant differences between the favorable and unfavorable outcome groups (P < 0.05), including ventilator hours, length of ICU stay, WBC count, neutrophil (NEU) count, lymphocyte (Lym) count, CRP, glucose (Glu), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglycerides (TG), diabetes mellitus (DM), cardiac failure, renal failure, hypoalbuminemia and fever. Given the possible confounding effect of duration of ventilator use, an attempt was made to remove this indicator in further analysis, and the model remained stable and this indicator was removed before final screening. Lasso regression was further used to screen the core predictors of poor prognosis. In this study, the optimal λ value was determined by 10-fold cross validation and the 1 se criterion was selected to simplify the variables and effectively avoid the multicollinearity problem between variables while taking into account the prediction efficiency of the model. The final four core predictors of ICU length of stay, C-reactive protein, blood glucose and renal failure were obtained, which can provide good variable basis for the subsequent construction of prognostic risk prediction model, and further ensure the stability and clinical application value of the model. (Fig. 3).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of prognostic factors

Taking the occurrence of poor prognosis as the dependent variable (occurrence = 1, no occurrence = 0), the four core variables selected by Lasso regression were included in the analysis. Multivariate Logistic regression analysis was used to construct the prediction model, and stepwise regression analysis was used to verify the independence of variables.

The results showed that ICU length of stay (OR = 1.049, 95%CI: 1.003–1.096, P = 0.028), C-reactive protein (OR = 1.007, 95%CI: 1.003–1.012, P = 0.001), blood glucose (OR = 1.119, 95%CI: 1.041–1.206, P = 0.003) and renal failure (yes vs. no, OR = 3.187, 95%CI: 1.412–7.048, P = 0.004) were independent risk factors for poor prognosis. (Table 2).

The results of collinearity diagnosis showed that the variance inflation factor (VIF) of all included variables was less than 1.1, and the tolerance was greater than 0.9, indicating that there was no obvious multicollinearity between variables, and the model was reasonable and reliable.(Table 3).

Model formula: Logit (P) = -3.956 + 0.048× length of ICU stay + 0.007× C-reactive protein + 0.112× blood glucose + 1.159× renal failure (yes = 1, no = 0).

Performance evaluation of the nomogram

This study systematically evaluated the prediction performance of the poor prognosis risk nomogram model from the four dimensions of discrimination, calibration, clinical utility and clinical intervention, and carried out Bootstrap internal validation (Fig. 5). The specific results were as follows:

ROC curve analysis showed that the area under the curve (AUC) of the model was 0.810 (95%CI: 0.737–0.883), and the best Cutoff value was 0.153. The sensitivity of the model was 0.641 (95%CI: 0.490–0.792), the specificity was 0.843 (95%CI: 0.800-0.887), the positive predictive value was 0.373 (95%CI: 0.257–0.489), and the negative predictive value was 0.942 (95%CI: 0.257–0.489). 0.912–0.971), the overall accuracy was 0.818 (95%CI: 0.817–0.819), and the Kappa consistency coefficient was 0.371 (95%CI: 0.242-0.500). The above indicators showed that the model had good discrimination ability, and could effectively distinguish the high-risk group and low-risk group of poor prognosis.

The results of calibration analysis showed that the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness of fit test (χ²=6.287, P = 0.615) showed that there was no statistically significant difference in fitting. The Brier score of the model was 0.0902, the calibration slope was 1, and the occurrence probability was consistent with the actual occurrence of the event. The overall prediction bias was small, and the model calibration performance was good.

The results of clinical decision curve analysis (DCA) showed that within the risk threshold range of 8%-90%, the net clinical benefit of the model was significantly higher than that of the full intervention strategy and no intervention strategy, which confirmed that the model had ideal clinical practical value, and could effectively screen the high-risk population with poor prognosis, and provide a good reference for early clinical risk stratification and individualized intervention.

In order to clarify the screening efficiency and intervention cost-effectiveness of the model population under different risk thresholds, this study draws a clinical intervention curve, which can directly reflect the proportion of the total number of high-risk groups and the true proportion of poor prognosis. Combined with the cost-effectiveness ratio corresponding to each risk threshold, it can be seen that a risk threshold greater than 0.2 can effectively screen out high-risk groups in clinical application. It can also ensure a better intervention cost performance, which can provide reference for the accurate allocation of clinical resources.

After internal validation by Bootstrap repeated sampling, the mean AUC value of the model was 0.7962, and the 95% confidence interval was 0.6853–0.901, which was close to the AUC value of the original model (0.810) and the confidence interval was moderate, which confirmed that the discrimination performance of the model was stable and the internal validation effect was ideal. The overall generalization prediction efficiency is reliable.

Discussion

The escalating use of corticosteroids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and immunosuppressive agents, coupled with the growing population of patients with malignancies or immunodeficiencies, has rendered invasive fungal infections a formidable threat to global public health. Among these, IA is associated with disproportionately high mortality rates [7]. Recent epidemiological estimates indicate that over 2 million individuals annually develop IA secondary to COPD, intensive care admission, lung cancer, or hematological malignancies. Furthermore, life-threatening mycoses affect more than 6.55 million people each year, resulting in over 3.75 million deaths [8]. Compared with the statistical data from ten years ago, this statistical result has undergone significant changes [9]. Therefore, it is very important to re-evaluate the situation and epidemiological trends of IA infections. Given that fungal infections frequently lead to clinical deterioration and dismal outcomes, identifying independent risk factors for poor prognosis is paramount for the early recognition and optimized management of severe fungal diseases.

Previous research on IA has predominantly focused on specific high-risk subpopulations, such as patients with COPD, those admitted to the ICU, or those with pulmonary tuberculosis. Furthermore, these studies often evaluated Aspergillus as a single entity, frequently overlooking species-specific differences and their clinical implications. In contrast, the present study adopted a more inclusive approach by enrolling all patients diagnosed with IA within a comprehensive general hospital, providing a favourable retrospective analysis across a broad spectrum of underlying conditions. While established literature has identified a rising prevalence of fungal infections driven by increasing cohorts of patients with COPD, AIDS, malignancies, asthma, autoimmune diseases, and transplants [10], our findings offer a more nuanced perspective. By combing the baseline data of 307 patients, it was found that COPD (n = 92, 29.97%) and malignant tumors (n = 53, 17.26%) were still the most common underlying diseases complicated with fungal infection. However, it is worth noting that patients with diabetes mellitus (n = 72, 23.45%), hypertension (n = 56, 18.24%) and renal failure (n = 56, 18.24%) also occupied a high proportion. This suggests that the high risk of secondary invasive aspergillosis should not be ignored in patients with cardiovascular, cerebrovascular and metabolic diseases in addition to patients with traditional respiratory and hematological diseases.

In October 2022, the World Health Organization (WHO) released its inaugural Fungal Priority Pathogens List (FPPL), categorizing 19 fungal pathogens into three priority tiers-critical, high, and medium-based on their public health impact. The critical priority group comprises Cryptococcus neoformans, Candida auris, Aspergillus fumigatus, and Candida albicans. Among these, Aspergillus species represent the most ubiquitous filamentous fungi isolated from humans and animals and are the primary source of IA worldwide [11]. While IA is predominantly caused by A. fumigatus, other species such as A. flavus, A. niger, A. nidulans, and A. terreus are also significant pathogens [12]. Consistent with global epidemiological trends [13], our findings identified A. fumigatus as the predominant species, accounting for nearly 60% of cases, followed by A. flavus, A. niger, and A. terreus. Regarding departmental distribution, the Department of Respiratory Medicine accounted for the largest proportion of cases (n = 148, 48.21%), followed by the Intensive Care Unit (n = 37, 12.05%) and the Department of Hematology (n = 32, 10.42%). The substantial combined prevalence in the ICU and Hematology (22.47%) reinforces the understanding that critically ill patients and those with hematological malignancies remain the primary populations requiring heightened surveillance for IA.

In terms of temporal distribution, with the total number of patients in the hospital basically flat, a distinct shift in the incidence of IA was observed during the study period. Prior to December 2022, the detection rate remained relatively low, typically fewer than 10 cases per month. However, a notable upward trend emerged in 2023, with monthly cases consistently exceeding 10 and reaching pronounced peaks in March 2023 and January 2024. It was found that the incidence reached its nadir in late spring and summer, while autumn and winter, especially October to December, were the peak periods of IA infection, showing time-dependent fluctuation characteristics. These findings are consistent with previous studies in China. Similar to this result, atmospheric fungal colony concentrations were significantly higher in autumn and winter than in other seasons in Japanese statistics [14], suggesting that the environmental fungal burden may directly affect the clinical prevalence of IA.

Furthermore, from the perspective of time distribution, after 2019 and the end of 2022, the detection rate of molds showed an obvious upward trend, which was highly consistent with the time node of COVID-19 epidemic. It has also been widely reported in previous literature that patients infected with novel coronavirus have severe immune dysfunction and are more likely to be complicated with Aspergillus infection [15]. Moreover, the data of this study suggest that after the peak of COVID-19 epidemic, the high incidence trend and seasonal characteristics of fungal infection (high incidence in autumn and winter) still exist [14].

To further assess the risk factors for adverse consequences caused by invasive Aspergillus infection, after reviewing literature and conducting expert interviews, and excluding indicators with excessive data loss and lagging clinical intervention indicators (such as the duration of mechanical ventilation, etc.) to avoid overfitting, We used rigorous LASSO regression (10-fold cross-validation) and multivariate Logistic stepwise regression to select four core predictors: length of ICU stay, elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), elevated blood glucose (Glu), and renal failure.

Firstly, the length of ICU stay independently reflects the severity of the patient’s condition and the involvement of multiple organ functions. The longer the ICU stay, the higher the risk of microecological imbalance and secondary multi-drug resistant organism (MDRO) infection, which greatly aggravation the incidence of poor prognosis.

Secondly, renal failure was proved to be one of the strongest independent risk factors for poor prognosis (OR = 3.187). Patients with renal failure are often accompanied by severe immune system abnormalities and excessive inflammatory responses, which easily lead to systemic inflammatory cascade that is difficult to control due to the reduced clearance rate of proinflammatory cytokines [16–18]. This finding further emphasizes the importance of protecting renal function and monitoring drug nephrotoxicity during antifungal therapy.

In addition, as a classic acute inflammation marker, the significant increase of CRP level often indicates that the fungal load is heavy or has caused severe systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS).

Finally, elevated blood glucose (Glu) significantly increases the risk of infection and worsens prognosis. Hyperglycemia not only provides favorable conditions for fungal proliferation, but also seriously weakens the chemotactic and phagocytic functions of neutrophils [18, 19].It is worth noting that the results of this study suggest that a simple “past history of diabetes” was not included as an independent risk factor in the final model. Acute poor blood glucose control during the course of the disease (with an increase in the absolute value of Glu levels) is the core link leading to the deterioration of prognosis. This suggests that it is important to implement strict blood glucose management while anti-infective treatment in clinical practice.

Additionally, while previous literature suggests that certain clinical parameters may predispose patients to fungal infections or adversely affect their prognosis, these associations were not statistically significant in our cohort. For instance, hypoalbuminemia, which is prevalent in patients with COPD, has been reported to compromise the synthesis of secretory IgA in mucosal surfaces, thereby increasing susceptibility to fungal colonization [20]. Furthermore, thrombocytopenia may precipitate alveolar capillary hemorrhage, resulting in intrapulmonary bloody exudates that provide an iron-rich milieu, a critical growth factor for fungi such as Aspergillus and Mucorales, which can exacerbate the severity of invasive pulmonary fungal infections [21]. The absence of such correlations in our study may be attributable to the relatively limited sample size. We anticipate that expanding the cohort in future multi-center studies might help elucidate these as potential predictive factors.

In conclusion, once invasive aspergillosis is diagnosed, the disease progresses rapidly and the prognosis is poor. Based on the original clinical intention of early prediction, this study successfully constructed a nomogram risk prediction model based on the four conventional indicators of ICU length of stay, CRP, blood glucose and renal insufficiency. The model not only had good prediction discrimination (AUC = 0.810) and calibration, but also clinical decision curve (DCA) and clinical intervention curve (CIC) also confirmed its high early clinical application value and cost-effective intervention. The Dynamic Nomogram can be used by clinicians to visually assess individualized risk, which can provide a good basis for rational allocation of medical resources and early optimization of antifungal treatment.

Deficiencies and prospects

Although good specific fungal diagnostic markers such as 1, 3-β-D-glucan (G test) and galactomannan (GM test) were included in the early data collection of this study, these markers were not included in the final model due to a high proportion of missing data in previous clinical practice in the real world. This reflects the lack of awareness of routine submission of fungal specific serological indicators in early clinical practice. Second, although the model was rigorously validated by internal Bootstrap and showed good stability, this study is still a single-center retrospective cohort analysis. In the future, multi-center prospective cohort studies with large sample size are needed for external validation to further confirm its applicability to a wider population.
