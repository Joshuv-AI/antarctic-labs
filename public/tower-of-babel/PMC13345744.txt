Geographic and Socioeconomic Variation in Aesthetic Plastic Surgery Pricing Across the United States: A State-Level Analysis of Income, Gross Domestic Product, and Real Economic Burden
Authors: Ibrahim Güler, Gerrit Grieb, Armin Kraus, Henrik Stelling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAesthetic surgery represents a major sector of direct patient payments in the United States. Despite the economic significance of this patient-funded market, systematic evidence on geographic pricing patterns and their socioeconomic determinants remains limited.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to characterize geographic variation in advertised prices for common aesthetic surgery procedures across US metropolitan areas and evaluate their relationship with regional economic indicators and purchasing power.MethodsAdvertised prices for 6 common aesthetic procedures were analyzed across 100 US metropolitan areas, yielding 600 city–procedure observations. Price dispersion was quantified using descriptive statistics and coefficient of variation (CV). State-level mean prices were correlated with per capita personal income and gross domestic product. Nominal prices were further adjusted for regional cost of living using Regional Price Parities (RPP) to estimate real economic costs.ResultsMean advertised prices ranged from USD 6234 for liposuction to USD 14,639 for facelift procedures, with facelifts demonstrating the greatest dispersion (CV 38.8%). State-level analysis (n = 33) revealed a significant association between per capita personal income and procedure prices (r = 0.529, P = .0016). Adjustment for RPP altered price rankings: New York remained the most expensive state, whereas Michigan and Kentucky ranked second and third in real cost, surpassing California. The affordability gap was substantial, with a single average procedure consuming nearly 20% of annual per capita income in Kentucky compared with ∼10% to 11% in higher-income states.ConclusionsPricing for aesthetic procedures in the United States demonstrates marked geographic stratification. After accounting for regional purchasing power, the real economic burden of aesthetic procedures is often greater in lower-income states, indicating that regional wealth and purchasing power play a central role in shaping access within the patient-funded aesthetic surgery market.Level of Evidence: 5 (Therapeutic) For image description, please refer to the figure legend and surrounding text.

Aesthetic plastic surgery has evolved into a resilient and rapidly expanding pillar of the US healthcare economy. According to The Aesthetic Society, over 1.67 million surgical procedures were performed in 2023, a 2.9% increase from 2022 and a robust 10.2% rise since 2019.1 Yet, this sector operates within a unique economic paradigm: unlike the insurance-subsidized broader healthcare system, aesthetic surgery is overwhelmingly elective and consumer driven, placing the full financial responsibility directly on the patient.

Despite the substantial financial commitment required, with procedure costs often ranging from several thousand to tens of thousands of dollars, price transparency in aesthetic surgery remains limited. Patients frequently encounter difficulty obtaining consistent pricing information, because advertised prices can vary substantially between providers, geographic regions, and market conditions. This opacity creates challenges for patients attempting to make informed financial decisions and may contribute to healthcare inequalities.

Geographic variation in healthcare pricing has been well documented for insurance-covered procedures, where regional cost of living, provider concentration, and market dynamics explain substantial price variation.2,3 However, the cash-pay aesthetic surgery market, where patients bear full financial responsibility, remains understudied. This gap is particularly problematic given the substantial financial burden and limited price transparency that characterize this market. Understanding these patterns is crucial for multiple stakeholders: patients seeking affordable care, surgeons making practice location decisions, and policymakers concerned with healthcare accessibility and market efficiency.

This study provides a systematic analysis of geographic price variation across 6 common aesthetic surgical procedures across 100 US metropolitan areas, aggregated to the state level. By integrating pricing data with state-level economic indicators, we examine the relationship between regional economic conditions and surgical prices, providing insights into the accessibility and relative economic burden of aesthetic surgery in the United States, beyond mere nominal price differences. Importantly, this study focuses on consumer-facing advertised prices as the primary decision-relevant signal for patients in a cash-pay market.

METHODS

Data Sources

Aesthetic Surgery Pricing Data

The dataset employed in this study is the “US Surgery Dataset” hosted on Kaggle (accessed November 2025) and licensed under the Community Data License Agreement—Permissive 1.0. According to the documentation, the underlying data were originally sourced from AestheticMatch, Inc. (www.aestheticmatch.com) and are utilized here in compliance with the attribution requirements of the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license. The dataset comprises 600 unique observations (100 cities × 6 procedures), representing advertised consumer-facing market rates across diverse US metropolitan areas. For each city–procedure combination, the dataset provided the average advertised price, price range, and number of surgeons performing the procedure. Although the specific primary data collection methods are not publicly documented, this study treats the data as secondary-analysis material.4 Importantly, the prices captured in this dataset represent bundled, consumer-facing advertised procedure prices rather than itemized cost breakdowns. As such, individual cost components, including surgeon fees, anesthesia charges, operating room or facility fees, implant costs, and ancillary expenses such as compression garments or postoperative care, cannot be isolated or analyzed separately. These advertised prices reflect the aggregated price signal most commonly encountered by patients during initial market exploration and should be interpreted accordingly. These values should not be interpreted as standardized or audited cost structures, but rather as market-facing price estimates.

Economic Indicators

State-level socioeconomic data were obtained from the US Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA). We utilized 2024 estimates for nominal gross domestic product (GDP) per capita and 2023 data for per capita personal income. To account for geographic variations in purchasing power, 2022 Regional Price Parities (RPP) were used to calculate cost-of-living–adjusted (“real”) prices. The RPP index (Base US = 100) normalizes prices across states, enabling comparisons of real economic burden independent of nominal price differences.5-8

Data Validation and Provider Qualification

Given the dataset's origin from a consumer-facing platform, manual validation was performed on a random sample of 60 city–procedure observations (10% of the dataset) by cross-referencing with the American Board of Medical Specialties and state medical boards. The validation identified 3 provider categories9,10:

Board-certified plastic surgeons, certified by the American Board of Plastic Surgery (ABPS): ∼75% of validated providers.

Specialized aesthetic surgeons, often certified by the American Board of Facial Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery (ABFPRS): ∼20% (predominantly for facial procedures).

Unverified/Mixed Entries: <5%. This heterogeneity is a defining characteristic of the US aesthetic surgery market. Consequently, the data represent the complete “consumer-facing” market rather than an exclusively ABPS-certified subset, ensuring findings reflect actual price signals influencing patient decisions.

Procedures Analyzed

Six procedures were selected based on market prevalence and data availability: breast augmentation, breast reduction, facelift, liposuction, rhinoplasty, and abdominoplasty.1

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.12 with standard statistical and data visualization libraries. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

Descriptive Statistics and Aggregation

Cities were mapped to their corresponding states to enable state-level aggregation. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation [SD], median, and range) were calculated for each procedure. The coefficient of variation (CV) was computed to quantify relative price variability. Affordability metrics: Price-to-income ratios and price-to-GDP ratios were computed to assess relative affordability across states. Price-to-income ratios represent the mean surgery cost as a percentage of per capita personal income, whereas price-to-GDP ratios illustrate economic burden relative to state economic output. Real prices were derived by adjusting nominal means using the state-specific RPP index.

Correlation Analysis

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess linear relationships between procedure prices and socioeconomic indicators.

Outlier Analysis and Robustness Check

The District of Columbia (DC) was identified as an extreme outlier (GDP per capita z-score = 5.20, >5 SD). Because of its unique federal economic structure, DC was excluded from correlation analyses to prevent distortion. However, it was retained in descriptive tables and maps for completeness. Robustness check: Sensitivity analyses confirmed that excluding DC did not artificially generate significance but rather clarified the underlying trend. With n = 33 states, both correlations remained highly significant (Income: r = 0.529, P = .0016; GDP: r = 0.482, P = .0045), confirming the robustness of the socioeconomic–pricing relationship across standard state jurisdictions.

Ethical Considerations and Reporting

This study utilized only publicly available, de-identified aggregate pricing data and did not involve human participants or patient-level information. Therefore, institutional review board approval was not required. All data sources are properly attributed in accordance with their respective licenses.

Economic Determinants and Affordability Metrics

Analysis of state-level economic indicators revealed significant positive correlations between aesthetic surgery pricing and both per capita personal income and GDP per capita. Following exclusion of DC as a prespecified outlier (see Methods), correlation analyses were performed on n = 33 states.

Per capita personal income demonstrated a moderate positive correlation with mean procedure costs (Pearson r = 0.529, P = .0016, R2 = 0.280), indicating that states with higher per capita personal incomes showed significantly higher average surgery prices. This relationship explained 28.0% of the variance in procedure pricing across states. GDP per capita similarly exhibited a significant positive correlation (Pearson r = 0.482, P = .0045, R2 = 0.232), explaining 23.2% of price variance. Removing the statistical distortion introduced by the DC (GDP z-score = 5.20) clarified the strong positive correlations for both income and GDP, confirming that the observed positive correlations were not dependent on inclusion of DC.

Affordability rankings and correlation plots for both income and GDP are presented in Figure 9. Price-to-income ratios ranged from 9.08% (DC) to 19.40% (Kentucky). Among the 33 states included in correlation analyses, the range was 10.77% (Illinois) to 19.40% (Kentucky). DISCUSSION

National Benchmarks and Market Validity

Our analysis provides a comprehensive snapshot of consumer-facing pricing in the US aesthetic surgery market. To validate the reliability of our dataset, we compared our mean advertised prices with the 2024 physician fee ranges reported by the American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS). Our findings demonstrate strong concordance with national benchmarks. For instance, our calculated mean for breast augmentation ($7480) falls within the upper range of the ASPS reported physician fee range ($4575-$8000), and our mean abdominoplasty price ($11,032) aligns closely with the ASPS average range ($8000-$13,500).4,11

This positioning reflects the scope difference between datasets: ASPS reports surgeon fees exclusively, whereas our consumer-facing advertised prices represent bundled costs that typically encompass surgeon fees, anesthesia, facility or operating room charges, and postoperative care. The relative contribution of each component to geographic price variation cannot be determined from the available data; however, differences in facility type (ambulatory surgery center vs hospital-based setting), local anesthesia fee structures, and ancillary service inclusion likely contribute to the observed dispersion alongside surgeon-specific pricing strategies. This direct correlation supports the validity of using advertised pricing as a proxy for the total market rates encountered by patients during their initial decision making. To contextualize these findings within national volume trends, The Aesthetic Society reports that liposuction, breast augmentation, and abdominoplasty remained among the top 3 surgical procedures in 2023.1,4,11

Our analysis demonstrates that although these procedures are widely accessible geographically, their relative financial burden varies substantially based on state-level income (r = 0.529, P = .0016) and regional purchasing power (r = 0.482, P = .0045). These robust associations explain 28% and 23% of price variance, respectively, suggesting that geographic location is a primary determinant of surgical affordability, independent of procedural type.

The “Midwest Premium”: Real Economic Burden Beyond Nominal Pricing

A central finding of this study is the identification of a phenomenon we term the “Midwest Premium”. Initially, our nominal data confirm the expected “Coastal Premium”, where metropolitan hubs like New York ($13,182) and California ($10,686) command the highest advertised prices. However, our RPP adjustment reveals a counterintuitive divergence between nominal prices and real economic burden.

After accounting for regional purchasing power, the real cost of surgery is frequently higher in inland states. Michigan ($11,855 real price) and Kentucky ($11,843 real price) emerge as the second- and third-most expensive markets nationally in real terms, surpassing California ($9541 real price). Although California demonstrates high nominal prices, residents benefit from higher purchasing power (RPP = 112). Conversely, in lower-income inland states, aesthetic surgery consumes a disproportionately large share of local wages.

Critically, in Kentucky, a single average procedure consumes 19.4% of annual per capita income, nearly double the burden observed in New Jersey (11.7%) or Illinois (10.77%). This 2-fold difference in relative affordability demonstrates that patients in lower-income states face a substantially greater financial barrier to aesthetic procedures, despite somewhat lower advertised prices. This pattern likely reflects lower provider density and reduced competition in inland markets compared with saturated coastal regions. Where coastal markets feature competitive pressure that compresses real prices, inland markets with fewer competing surgeons lack comparable constraints on pricing power, allowing providers to maintain higher margins. The emergence of Kentucky and Michigan as the second- and third-most expensive markets in real terms, despite their lower nominal price profiles, warrants specific consideration. In both states, the combination of below average per capita income, below-average RPP, and moderate nominal procedure costs produces disproportionately high real economic burden. Additionally, the geographic distribution of plastic surgeons across the United States is markedly asymmetric, with nearly half of all health service areas lacking any plastic surgeon, and local economic conditions rather than provider supply have been identified as the dominant determinant of procedure pricing.12,13 Similarly, Colorado's position as the most expensive state for abdominoplasty and Massachusetts’ leading position for breast augmentation illustrate that the coastal-inland pricing dichotomy does not uniformly apply at the procedure level. Colorado's elevated abdominoplasty pricing may reflect a selective, premium-positioned provider market in a high-demand metropolitan corridor (Denver–Boulder), where affluent demographics and potentially limited provider competition for body contouring procedures may contribute to higher price points. In Massachusetts, the concentration of academic medical centers and specialty-focused practices in the greater Boston area may contribute to a high-price environment for breast augmentation, driven by provider reputation, institutional overhead, and a regional patient population with above-average willingness to pay. These procedure-specific deviations from the general geographic pattern underscore that local market structure and demand characteristics can override broader regional pricing trends.

Drivers of Procedural Variance

Our study reveals distinct differences in price variability between procedures, reflecting differences in cost structure and market customization. The remarkably low coefficient of variation for breast augmentation (CV = 18.6%) likely reflects the stabilizing effect of fixed material costs. Because breast implants represent a commoditized, fixed-cost component of surgery, they function as a price anchor that constrains the range of total price elasticity.

In contrast, facelifts (CV = 38.8%) and liposuction (CV = 37.9%) displayed the highest relative variability. For facelifts, pricing is driven predominantly by variable factors, including operative time, surgical technique (eg, mini-lift vs deep-plane), and surgeon expertise. For liposuction, variability may additionally reflect differences in the use of energy-based technologies, ultrasound-assisted liposuction, or radiofrequency skin-tightening devices, which carry substantial capital and per-case disposable costs that can exceed the cost of breast implants. The wide range of available approaches, from traditional tumescent liposuction to technology-intensive body contouring, likely contributes to the broad price dispersion observed. For both procedure types, the absence of a dominant standardized material cost allows for wider price stratification based on technique, technology, and provider reputation. Intermediate procedures demonstrate variability consistent with their mixed cost structures. Breast reduction (CV = 27.1%), abdominoplasty (CV = 23.2%), and rhinoplasty (CV = 22.7%) reflect combinations of standardized materials and procedure-specific customization. These patterns suggest that procedural complexity and customization potential directly correlate with price heterogeneity, standardized services exhibit tight price clustering, whereas individualized services permit broader stratification based on provider reputation and skill.14

Provider Heterogeneity and Market Transparency

Our validation process revealed a heterogeneous provider landscape: ∼75% of validated providers were ABPS-certified plastic surgeons, whereas ∼20% were facial plastic surgeons (ABFPRS/Otolaryngology) focusing on head and neck procedures.9 This provider mix reflects the open, cash-pay aesthetic market where patients encounter multiple specialties without credentialing-based market segmentation.

Critically, robust socioeconomic correlations (r ≈ 0.50 for both income and GDP) were observed in the aggregating pricing data. This finding suggests that regional economic forces, specifically local wealth and cost of living, act as the primary unifying determinant of pricing across all major aesthetic specialties (eg, ABPS and ABFPRS) that participate in the cash-pay market. From a transparency perspective, however, this heterogeneity underscores a challenge for consumers, as patients typically cannot reliably distinguish provider qualifications from advertised prices alone. Wide price variability (CV ranging 19%-39% across procedures) may reflect not only geographic and economic factors but also differences in training, experience, facility standards, and ancillary service quality. Consequently, determining whether board certification type correlates with price stratification, complication rates, or patient satisfaction remains critical information not uniformly available to price-shopping consumers.15-18

Practical Implications for Patients and Surgeons

These findings carry direct implications for multiple stakeholders in the aesthetic surgery market. For patients, the data provide an empirical framework for setting realistic price expectations based on geographic location and for understanding that the true financial burden of a procedure depends not only on the advertised price but also on local income levels and purchasing power. A patient in Kentucky considering an average-priced procedure faces nearly twice the relative financial commitment of a patient in New Jersey, a disparity that is invisible in nominal price comparisons alone. This information may support more informed financial planning, including realistic assessment of financing needs and geographic comparison of options.

For aesthetic surgeons, these data provide a practical benchmarking framework for regional price positioning and competitive strategy. Practitioners in high-burden, low-competition markets may use this information to contextualize their pricing relative to both local affordability and national benchmarks, whereas surgeons in saturated coastal markets gain insight into competitive pricing pressures. Furthermore, professional societies and accrediting organizations may leverage such data to advocate for greater price transparency standards across the cash-pay aesthetic surgery sector.

Methodological Considerations: DC Exclusion and Robustness

The decision to exclude the DC from correlation analyses warrants specific mention. DC demonstrated extreme outlier status with a GDP per capita ($263,220) >5 SDs above the mean, yet its surgery prices ranked only 21st nationally. This discordance reflects DC's unique federal economic structure, which inflates GDP without a corresponding rise in consumer service pricing.

Excluding DC was a prespecified method to prevent statistical distortion based on theoretical considerations regarding jurisdictional comparability. Importantly, all key correlations remained highly significant in the robustness check (income: r = 0.529, P = .0016; GDP: r = 0.482, P = .0045, n = 33 states), confirming that identified trends are valid across standard state economies and were not dependent on DC's statistical outlier status.19,20

Limitations

This study is subject to several limitations. Data are derived from a single aggregation platform, which does not comprehensively represent all practicing surgeons or all advertised prices in each market.4 Regional variation in provider participation in aggregation platforms could introduce sampling bias. Although the source platform aggregates consumer-facing pricing data, the precise methodology underlying its data collection, including the sampling frame, update frequency, and verification procedures, is not publicly documented. Findings should therefore be interpreted with this uncertainty in mind.

The number of cities contributing to each state-level estimate ranged from 1 to 15 (Table 2). States represented by a single city may be disproportionately influenced by individual provider pricing, whereas states with more contributing cities benefit from greater averaging of outliers.

Advertised rates serve as initial price signals but may differ from final transaction costs because of individualized surgical plans, financing, or bundled anesthesia fees. However, assuming systematic discounting is not geographically biased, the relative rank-order of states remains valid. Advertised prices remain the critical initial price signal in the consumer decision journey and are worthy of policy attention regardless of postconsultation adjustments. Additionally, some advertised prices may represent introductory or “starting at” quotes rather than final procedure costs, potentially underestimating actual out-of-pocket expenditure. However, this effect would be expected to operate across all geographic markets and is unlikely to systematically alter the relative rank-order of states.

The advertised prices in our dataset represent aggregated procedure costs and do not permit disaggregation into individual components such as surgeon fees, anesthesia, facility charges, or implant costs. Consequently, the relative contribution of each component to observed geographic variation cannot be quantified. Regional differences in facility type (eg, ambulatory surgery centers vs hospital-based settings), anesthesia pricing models, and ancillary service inclusion may contribute to the observed price heterogeneity independently of surgeon-related factors. This limitation restricts causal interpretation of individual price drivers and requires that findings be interpreted at the level of aggregate market behavior rather than component-specific cost attribution.

The analysis does not account for critical differences in provider credentials and surgical scope, factors that directly influence price and quality. Specifically, our aggregated pricing data cannot differentiate price stratification based on the provider's certification tier: (1) full board certification (ABPS), (2) subspecialty certification (eg, ABFPRS/otolaryngology), or (3) scope of practice (eg, Aesthetic Physicians/Cosmetic Surgeons with nonsurgical primary board certifications). These variations in qualifications, coupled with differences in facility accreditation and surgeon experience, are legitimate drivers of price variation. Higher-priced markets may thus reflect superior qualifications or quality assurance standards, which our data do not capture.

The data provide a snapshot in time, precluding analysis of temporal pricing trends or inflation effects. Longitudinal data would be necessary to examine whether geographic price disparities are widening or narrowing over time.

Aggregating city data to the state level may obscure within-state variation, particularly in large, economically diverse states like California or Texas. The cities captured in the dataset are predominantly larger metropolitan areas, which tend to have higher local incomes than their respective state averages. Pairing city-level pricing with whole-state economic indicators may therefore overstate the real economic burden in states with substantial intrastate income disparities, because patients accessing aesthetic surgery are more likely to reside in the wealthier urban portions of each state. Furthermore, 17 US states were not represented in the dataset, and findings cannot be generalized to those markets.

The dataset includes the number of surgeons listed per city procedure on the source platform but does not capture the total number of practicing surgeons per market or state. Consequently, the relationship between provider density, population size, and pricing could not be formally assessed and warrants investigation in future studies linking pricing data to verified workforce registries.

CONCLUSIONS

This analysis demonstrates that consumer-facing aesthetic surgery prices closely track established physician fee benchmarks while capturing the bundled nature of real-world transaction costs encountered by patients. When adjusted for state-level income and regional purchasing power, substantial affordability gaps emerge, with inland states such as Kentucky facing nearly double the relative economic burden of coastal markets despite comparable or lower nominal prices. These findings underscore that geographic location and local socioeconomic conditions are primary determinants of access to elective aesthetic surgery.

Marked heterogeneity in pricing across procedures and provider types, combined with wide regional variation in relative affordability, highlights the need for greater price transparency and systematic monitoring of out-of-pocket cost burdens in the cash-pay sector. Future work should link price data to quality metrics and provider density data to determine whether price disparities reflect superior value, market competition effects, or amplify socioeconomic inequities.
