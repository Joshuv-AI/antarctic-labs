Inflammation and carcinogenesis: molecular targets and small-molecule intervention strategies
Authors: Wan-Jung Lu, Ritu Ojha, Mandeep Rana, Vinod Gautam, Jiun-Yi Li, Shao-An Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammation is a finely tuned host defense mechanism whose perpetual activation is a driver, promoter, and supporter of carcinogenesis. Key mediators of chronic inflammatory processes, viz. NF-kB, JAK-STAT, inflammasomes, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and cytokine network, if left unanswered, foster the tumour-supportive environment. Within the tumour microenvironment (TME), inflammatory cells, in combination with stromal and cancerous cells, modulate these pathways and regulate critical tumour hallmarks. Therefore, efforts have been made to understand and tackle the interface between the inflammation-cancer axis, but therapeutic outcomes remain limited. In this context, integrating systems-level biological insights with precision-driven medicinal chemistry may pave the way towards next-generation anti-inflammatory chemotherapeutics. The current review underlines the critical involvement of inflammation in cancer development by providing a comprehensive overview of key molecular pathways. A special emphasis was placed on understanding the medicinal chemistry campaign over the last 5 years for the development of inflammation-targeting small-molecule therapeutics in cancer.Keywords: Inflammation, small-molecule therapeutics, cancer, NSAIDs, COX

Introduction

Cancer, the most formidable transnational health concern, is not only driven by genetic instability but also by the complex biosphere in which oncogenic cells arise and evolve. In the last few decades, it has become increasingly evident that oncologic diseases cannot be fully understood through a cancer-cell-centric lens. Instead, cancer progression is now recognised as the result of a dynamic interplay between neoplastic cells and a heterogeneous microenvironment shaped by immune, stromal, vascular, and metabolic signals and markers1,2. Among these, inflammation has emerged as a central orchestrator that influences almost every stage of tumour biology. It plays an evident role in the earliest phases of cellular transformation to metastatic dissemination and therapeutic response, which influences inflammation as one of the hallmarks of cancer3. Normally, inflammation is a fundamental host defense mechanism that is essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis and protecting against infection and injury, but when left untackled, it becomes a powerful driver of many diseases, like diabetes, cardiovascular disorders, Alzheimer’s, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and cancer4.

Normally, inflammation can broadly be categorised as acute or chronic based on its duration and underlying mechanisms. Under physiological conditions, acute inflammation is the primary response to any invasion, and it is tightly controlled and self-limiting. Generally, it is characterised by the rapid recruitment of immune cells that help to efficiently resolve the invasion or injury in order to restore tissue homeostasis and architecture. However, when this balance is disrupted, acute inflammation becomes chronic, and it leads to sustained immune activation and continuous tissue damage, which ultimately causes pathological remodelling5–8. Chronic inflammatory states may arise from persistent infections, environmental exposures, autoimmune disorders, or metabolic dysregulation, which creates a permissive environment for disease progression9. A growing body of epidemiological and experimental precedents has established chronic inflammation as a major contributor to cancer, with approximately 20% of malignancies linked to inflammatory conditions or persistent infections, for instance, Helicobacter pylori-induced gastric cancer, hepatitis B or C-related hepatocellular carcinoma, and Human papillomavirus (HPV)-driven cervical cancer are among the leading causes of inflammation-induced cancer10–12. Unlike transitory physiological inflammation, tumour-associated inflammation is often persistent and establishes well within the TME, owing to which it promotes genomic instability, alters cellular metabolism, and facilitates other key hallmarks of cancer, including immune evasion, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remodelling13–16. Interestingly, the connection between inflammation and cancer has long been recognised, and scientific interest in the relationship between chronic inflammation and malignancy dates back centuries. In 1863, Virchow’s seminal observations stated that tumours emerge from sites of repeated inflammatory injury17. Building on this foundation, in 1915, Yamagiwa demonstrated that experimentally induced chronic inflammation could drive oncogenesis in animal models18. Further research through the 20th century continued to substantiate these ideas. With time, advances in molecular biology have since revealed the intricate signalling networks underlying this relationship, including the involvement of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors, as well as key pathways such as nuclear factor (NF-κB), signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), Janus kinase (JAK), and inflammasomes, which collectively regulate cell proliferation, survival, and immune responses19,20. As mentioned earlier, inflammation exerts a dual role in cancer, where acute responses support immune surveillance and tumour elimination; on the contrary, chronic inflammation drives tumour initiation, progression, and metastasis21,22. Any strategy aimed at targeting inflammation in cancer must therefore navigate this distinction carefully, i.e. suppressing tumour-promoting inflammatory signalling without dismantling the immune activity that restrains tumour growth also23–27. But despite these significant advances in understanding the inflammation-cancer axis, translating mechanistic understanding into effective clinical interventions remains a genuine challenge, and small-molecule agents capable of modulating multiple interconnected inflammatory and oncogenic pathways simultaneously represent a promising but underexplored direction. In this context, multiple reviews have been published in the previous few years, highlighting the importance of understanding the cancer-inflammation axis28–30. Particularly, this review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the relationship between inflammation and carcinogenesis, with a particular emphasis on emerging medicinal chemistry strategies targeting key inflammatory mediators and signalling networks, from the last 5 years of literature. This review discusses the drug discovery endeavours carried out by scientists globally to lay down the compendium of single-targeting, dual-targeting, multitargeting molecules, and prodrugs, targeting various aspects of inflammation in ­cancer by reporting their in vitro, in vivo, and PK studies. Moreover, we paid emphasis on understanding the key inflammatory pathways and mediators in the cancer setup. Along with that, a compilation on the repurposing of various inflammatory targeting drugs in cancer has been provided, and the future of the various advances in this field has been laid out.

Molecular basis of tumour and inflammation: hand in glove relationship

Literature precedents have reported infection as a major driver of inflammation-associated carcinogenesis. In this context, inflammation represents a fundamental innate immune response to disrupted tissue homeostasis, which is mainly coordinated through the interplay of myeloid and lymphoid cell populations, which tries to suppress the oncogenic activity31. But the ultimate outcome of this response, viz., tumour suppression or promotion, depends on the balance between immune activation and regulatory control of the body32. Comprehensive transcriptomic and single-cell analyses consistently demonstrate that both primary and metastatic tumours exhibit elevated expression of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and immune cell infiltrates33,34. These inflammatory features actively shape the TME and are frequently associated with poor clinical outcomes35. Notably, even tumours traditionally categorised as “non-inflammatory” can generate localised inflammatory responses, a phenomenon generally referred to as tumour-elicited inflammation. This reflects the intrinsic ability of mutagenic cells to recruit immune populations and establish cytokine-driven networks that remodel surrounding tissues. Interestingly, the origin of tumour-associated inflammation varies depending on tissue context also36. For instance, in barrier tissues such as skin and mucosal surfaces, early oncogenic events disrupt epithelial integrity, enabling microbial components to activate myeloid cells and initiate cytokine-mediated inflammatory circuits. In contrast, in sterile tissues (protected by barriers), inflammation arises primarily from intrinsic stress signals associated with oncogenic transformation, which consists of metabolic imbalance, hypoxia, and cell death37. Beyond shaping the TME, inflammation also directly contributes to tumour initiation. For instance, reactive oxygen and nitrogen species generated by activated immune cells induce DNA damage and mutagenesis in neighbouring epithelial cells37,38. Concurrently, inflammatory cytokines alter epigenetic programs that regulate oncogenes and tumour suppressors. As a consequence, key signalling pathways, particularly NF-κB and STAT3, support the survival and expansion of transformed cells (mutagenic cells), especially in tissues that are undergoing regenerative proliferation. Taken together, these mechanisms establish a very permissive environment that is characterised by genetic instability and clonal expansion, thereby facilitating the early tumour development39,40.

While inflammation is critical during tumour initiation, its role becomes even more pronounced during metastatic progression, which accounts for the majority of cancer-related deaths. Multiple epidemiological studies have reported reduced cancer incidence and metastasis following long-term aspirin (anti-inflammatory drug) use which underscores the importance of inflammatory signalling in metastatic success. Various experimental preclinical studies have also reported that suppression of inflammatory pathways similarly reduces metastatic colonisation and enhances the clearance of micro metastatic lesions41,42. Noteworthy to mention that metastasis begins with local invasion that is often accompanied by partial epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), which enhances cellular motility and invasiveness. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumour necrosis factor (TNF) and interleukin (IL-1β) regulate transcriptional programs driving EMT, while stromal interactions are mediated by factors such as IL-11 and transforming growth factor (TGF-β), which further support invasion and immune evasion43,44. Additionally, recruitment of myeloid cells to the invasive front promotes extracellular matrix remodelling through the secretion of proteolytic enzymes and contributes to localised immunosuppression. In contrast, infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer cells tries to exert anti-tumour effects, which are associated with improved clinical outcomes. Inflammatory signalling also enhances metastatic competence by promoting stem-like properties through NF-κB- and STAT3-dependent mechanisms, thereby expanding the pool of tumour-initiating cells capable of dissemination45. Metastatic tumour cells during circulation interact with neutrophils and monocytes to form heterotypic clusters that facilitate vascular adhesion, extravasation, and protection from immune surveillance46. In addition to it, systemic inflammatory conditions, such as obesity and chronic infections, further amplify these processes and accelerate metastatic seeding47. Following dissemination, inflammatory mediators within distant tissues can reactivate dormant tumour cells and support metastatic outgrowth48. At the same time, inflammation also drives extensive cellular plasticity within the TME because cytokine- and growth factor-mediated signalling dynamically reshapes the phenotypes of cancer cells, fibroblasts, and immune populations49. As a consequence, cancer-associated fibroblasts and myeloid cells adopt diverse functional states that regulate tumour growth, immune suppression, metabolic adaptation, and maintenance of cancer stem cell niches. Moreover, adaptive immune cells also undergo context-dependent reprogramming, including T cell exhaustion and regulatory T cell plasticity50–52. Apart from tumour and TME, anti-cancer therapies further contribute to this inflammatory landscape because chemotherapeutics and immunotherapies induce the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) from dying tumour cells, which can enhance immune activation53. However, this response may also resemble wound-healing processes characterised by stromal and myeloid cell-derived cytokines and growth factors, but mediators such as IL-6 family cytokines and epidermal growth factor ligands activate survival signalling pathways, which promote cancer stem-like phenotypes and reduce therapeutic sensitivity. In addition, therapy-induced tissue damage can also facilitate microbial translocation and systemic inflammation, thereby accelerating tumour progression or metastasis (Figures 1 and 3)32,54,55.

A growing body of evidence indicates that inflammatory signalling is closely linked to metabolic reprogramming and cellular stress responses in cancer. This can be explained as activation of immune cells during inflammation is accompanied by shifts in energy metabolism and biosynthetic pathways, which influence macrophage and T cell polarisation and ultimately shape tumour-promoting or suppressive functions, which depend upon the balance56,57. Transcription factors such as NF-κB act as key integrators of inflammatory and metabolic cues, reinforcing these adaptive responses58. In parallel, autophagy serves as an important regulator of inflammatory homeostasis by removing damaged organelles and limiting excessive cytokine production. However, impaired autophagy or mitophagy can lead to the accumulation of ROS and activation of inflammasomes, thereby sustaining chronic inflammation and promoting tumour development59. These interconnected processes further highlight the complexity of inflammation-driven cancer progression and its dependence on coordinated metabolic and stress-response pathways30. These mechanisms converge on key signalling pathways such as NF-κB, JAK-STAT3, inflammasomes, and oxidative stress, which are discussed in detail in the following section.

Key pro-tumour inflammatory pathways

Nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB)

NF-κB is a family of structurally related transcription factors that play a central role in regulating inflammation, immune responses, cell survival, and proliferation. This family mainly consists of five members: RelA (p65), RelB, c-Rel, NF-κB1 (p50, derived from p105), and NF-κB2 (p52, derived from p100)60,61. These proteins form homo- or heterodimers that share a conserved Rel homology domain, which is responsible for dimerisation, nuclear localisation, DNA binding, and interaction with inhibitory IκB proteins. Under basal conditions, NF-κB dimers remain sequestered in the cytoplasm through association with IκB proteins, which mask nuclear localisation signals and prevent transcriptional activity62. Activation of NF-κB proceeds through two mechanistically distinct routes viz., canonical and non-canonical pathways. The canonical pathway responds rapidly to pro-inflammatory stimuli, viz., TNF-α and IL-1β, microbial components, and antigen receptor signalling63. As a consequence, the IKK complex (IKKα, IKKβ, and the regulatory subunit NEMO) gets activated which phosphorylates IκBα and targets it for ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. The liberated p50/RelA dimers translocate to the nucleus and initiate the transcription of genes governing inflammation, survival, and immune activation64,65. The noncanonical pathway is more selective in its upstream triggers, responding primarily to TNF superfamily receptors that include CD40, BAFF-R, LTβR, and RANK. Therefore, activation of these receptors stabilises NF-κB-inducing kinase, which in turn activates IKKα homodimers and drives processing of p100 into p52, generating p52/RelB complexes that regulate lymphoid development and immune differentiation66,67. Together, these two pathways give NF-κB the flexibility to respond to a wide range of physiological and pathological inputs while producing distinct transcriptional outputs. Under normal conditions, tight regulatory control of NF-κB activity is what keeps inflammation productive rather than pathological. In cancer, that control breaks down. Constitutive NF-κB activation is documented across a wide spectrum of solid tumours and haematologic malignancies, where it sustains survival signalling, suppresses apoptosis, and drives the cytokine and chemokine secretion (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6) that establishes and maintains a tumour-promoting microenvironment68. Importantly, NF-κB does not act in isolation but interacts extensively with other signalling pathways, including ROS-mediated signalling, STAT3, and JAK cascades, forming interconnected networks that amplify tumour-promoting responses (Figure 2)69,70. The transcriptional targets of NF-κB in cancer extend further: ICAM-1, CXCR4, and CXCL8 that support invasion, metastasis and tumour vascularisation, while NF-κB activation in tumour-associated macrophages impairs dendritic cell maturation and blunts effective antitumour immunity70,71. Within the TME, NF-κB further contributes to immune evasion58. Activation of NF-κB signalling has been well documented in cancer stem cell populations across multiple malignancies, including glioblastoma, leukaemia, breast, prostate, pancreatic, ovarian, and colorectal cancers72,73. In cancer stem cell populations, it interacts with transcriptional regulators such as Nanog, Sox2, CD44, and KLF4 to promote self-renewal and tumour heterogeneity, which thereby leads to therapeutic resistance74. Despite its well-established tumour-promoting functions, NF-κB can also exert context-dependent tumour-suppressive effects75. In certain settings, NF-κB activation induces expression of pro-apoptotic ligands or antioxidant responses that limit excessive oxidative stress. It can also suppress inflammasome activation and modulate cytokine maturation76,77. Therefore, given its central role, NF-κB has been extensively explored as a therapeutic target. However, direct inhibition remains challenging due to its essential physiological functions and potential toxicity. Current therapeutic strategies primarily target upstream regulators or downstream effectors78. Clinically approved agents such as proteasome inhibitors (bortezomib and ixazomib), Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitors (ibrutinib), and biologics targeting receptors such as RANKL and IL-1 indirectly modulate NF-κB signalling. This indirect targeting strategy reflects the need for selective and context-dependent modulation of NF-κB activity73,79–83. Advancing beyond these agents will require a more granular understanding of how NF-κB activity is differentially regulated across tumour types and microenvironmental states.

JAK-STAT pathway

The JAK/STAT pathway is an evolutionarily conserved signalling system that enables cells to translate extracellular cues into coordinated transcriptional responses. Initially, it was characterised in haematopoietic and immune cells as a mediator of cytokine signalling, but now this pathway is widely recognised as a central regulator of inflammation, host defense, and tumour biology84. Functionally, JAK/STAT signalling influences diverse cellular processes, including proliferation, survival, differentiation, apoptosis, metabolic adaptation, and immune regulation. Therefore, its ability to integrate inflammatory and growth-related signals places it at the intersection of chronic inflammatory diseases and cancer progression85. Notably, cytokines are the primary activators of JAK/STAT signalling. Specifically, members of the IL-6 cytokine family, including IL-6, IL-11, IL-31, and oncostatin M, are prominent inducers, alongside other cytokines such as IL-10, IL-21, and type I and II interferons86. Additional stimuli like growth factors, chemokines, and pathogen-sensing pathways such as toll-like receptors can also activate JAK/STAT signalling either directly or through secondary cytokine release. However, in the context of cancer, pathway activation often arises not only from inflammatory cytokines produced by immune and stromal components of the TME, but also from autocrine signalling loops within tumour cells themselves87. Therefore, engagement of ligand with cytokine receptors induces conformational changes that facilitate recruitment of Janus kinases (JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2) to receptor-associated cytoplasmic domains88. Activated JAKs phosphorylate specific tyrosine residues on receptor tails, which therefore creates a docking sites for STAT transcription factors. Subsequent phosphorylation of STAT proteins promotes their dimerisation and nuclear translocation, where they regulate transcription of genes governing survival, proliferation, and immune modulation89.

Among the seven STAT family members, STAT3 and STAT5 have attracted the most oncological attention. Similar to the JAK pathway, STAT3 drives activation of genes supporting cell survival, proliferation, angiogenesis, and metabolic reprogramming while simultaneously suppressing antitumour immune responses90. Persistent STAT3 signalling has been linked to enhanced tumour growth, acquisition of stem-like characteristics, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and metastatic dissemination across multiple tumour types. STAT5 plays a prominent role in haematological malignancies by contributing to leukemogenesis and immune evasion91. Beyond their canonical transcriptional roles, STATs participate in various non-transcriptional activities that further support tumour biology. For instance, mitochondrial STAT3 has been implicated in the regulation of oxidative phosphorylation and metabolic flexibility which enables the oncogenic cells to adapt to fluctuating energy demands92. In parallel, JAK mediates histone modifications and interacts with chromatin remodelling complexes which help to link inflammatory inputs to durable changes in gene expression programs93.

Under physiological conditions, the pathway is kept under surveillance by several layers of negative regulators, viz., protein tyrosine phosphatases that include SHP2 and PTPN6, suppressor of cytokine signalling proteins, protein inhibitors of activated STAT, and lastly soluble cytokine receptors that act as decoys94. Any hindrance or loss in these feedback mechanisms plays a favourable role in promoting inflammation-associated cancers, and its consequences extend beyond the tumour cell. Chronic JAK/STAT activation reshapes the TME by expanding immunosuppressive populations such as regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and alternatively activating macrophages that collectively impair antitumour immunity and facilitate its progression. Notably, STAT3, in particular, plays a central role in promoting immune evasion by upregulating immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1 and inhibiting the dendritic cell maturation95,96. These immune alterations bypass the antitumour surveillance and therefore facilitate malignant progression. Consequently, aberrant JAK/STAT signalling serves as a molecular bridge that links chronic inflammation to oncogenesis and has become a compelling therapeutic target97. This pathway does not operate in isolation as NF-κB-driven IL-6 secretion feeds directly into STAT3 activation, while oxidative stress amplifies signalling through both pathways. Therefore, it establishes a self-reinforcing inflammatory loop that is difficult to interrupt at any single node (Figure 2)98,99. Given its central role in both tumour-intrinsic and immune-mediated processes, the JAK/STAT pathway represents a compelling therapeutic target100. However, extensive crosstalk with pathways such as NF-κB and redox signalling necessitates utilising precision-based approaches to tackle the resistance or underperformance101. Future therapeutic strategies will likely rely on combination therapies and biomarker-guided interventions to effectively exploit JAK/STAT signalling in cancer and inflammatory diseases102.

Arachidonic acid pathway (eicosanoid biology)

The arachidonic acid pathway is a metabolic process of converting phospholipids into bioactive lipid mediators, which control fever, pain, and inflammation. It mainly comprises of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), prostaglandins, and their downstream metabolite prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and lipoxygenases (LOX). Cyclooxygenases are the rate-limiting enzymes in the arachidonic acid cascade, which catalyse the biosynthesis of prostanoids, among which PGE2 is the most abundant and biologically active103. Beyond classical prostaglandin synthesis, COX enzymes can also metabolise endocannabinoids to generate prostaglandin glycerol esters and prostaglandin ethanolamides, which are collectively termed as prostamides104,105. Under physiological conditions, COX-2 expression is momentarily high during inflammatory responses, where it tries to provide defense and repair of injured tissue. contributes to host defense and tissue repair. However, sustained COX-2 overexpression and excessive PGE2 production are frequently observed across a wide spectrum of human malignancies and are closely associated with the establishment of a tumour-promoting inflammatory microenvironment. Therefore, the arachidonic acid pathway constitutes a central axis linking inflammation to tumorigenesis. Persistent activation of this pathway in response to any stimuli supports a malignant transformation of cells by promoting cell survival, proliferation, angiogenesis, and immune evasion in oncogenic cells106,107. Importantly, COX-2 expression is tightly regulated by upstream inflammatory pathways108. For instance, transcription factors such as NF-κB and AP-1 directly induce COX-2 gene expression, while oxidative stress further amplifies its activity, establishing a feed-forward loop between inflammation and redox signalling. In parallel, cytokine-driven pathways, including JAK/STAT signalling, also enhance COX-2 expression, which signifies that, its integration within the broader inflammatory network that drives tumour progression109,110. Additionally, elevated PGE2 levels are also known to exert pleiotropic effects on tumour biology. PGE2 promotes angiogenesis through upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and enhances invasion and metastasis by stimulating matrix metalloproteinase activity111,112. It also modulates intracellular signalling pathways such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and MAPK, which further support proliferation, survival, and metabolic adaptation. Moreover, current evidence suggests that prostamides may influence cytokine production, cell proliferation, and apoptosis, thereby contributing to the complex interplay between inflammation and cancer progression113–115. In addition, COX-2/PGE2 signalling contributes to immune evasion by suppressing the cytotoxic activity of T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells, while promoting immunosuppressive cell populations within the TME. Consistent with these oncogenic roles, aberrant COX-2 expression has been documented in numerous cancers, including those of the breast, colon, prostate, pancreas, lung, skin, and head and neck116,117. Pharmacological inhibition of COX enzymes by agents such as aspirin and other non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) reduces PGE2 production and has been associated with decreased cancer incidence and mortality, supporting a protective role against solid tumour formation. However, the occurrence of more side effects due to a lack of selectivity remains the biggest hurdle to the success of COX inhibitors118.

Further, LOXs catalyse the conversion of arachidonic acid into hydroperoxyeicosatetraenoic acids (HPETEs), which are subsequently reduced to the corresponding hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acids (HETEs). These enzymes are classified based on the positional specificity with which they oxygenate arachidonic acid, giving rise to 5-, 8-, 12-, and 15-LOX isoforms. Accordingly, 5-, 8-, 12-, and 15-HETE represent the principal metabolites generated by mammalian LOXs119. In addition to arachidonic acid metabolism, 15-LOX-1 can efficiently oxidise linoleic acid to produce 13-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid120. Within the 5-LOX pathway, 5-HPETE is further converted into leukotriene A4, a key intermediate that gives rise to biologically active leukotrienes such as LTB4 and the cysteinyl leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, and LTE4), which act as mediators of inflammation121. Similarly, lipoxins are another class of bioactive lipids that are formed through coordinated activity of multiple LOX enzymes, typically involving 15-LOX- or 12-LOX-derived intermediates that are subsequently processed by 5-LOX. In coordination with cyclooxygenases and other lipoxygenases, including 12- and 15-LO, 5-LO participates in the broader lipid signalling network that regulates both the initiation and resolution of inflammation120. Dysregulated 5-LO expression has been reported in a variety of solid and haematological malignancies122. Functionally, 12-LOX has been linked to promotion of angiogenesis and tumour growth and reports displaying that when it is inhibited, it reduces cellular proliferation and promotes apoptosis through modulation of signalling pathways such as ERK, PI3K, and caspase activation123. Importantly, the arachidonic acid cascade is highly interconnected, and inhibition of COX-2 can redirect substrate flux towards the 5-LO pathway, which highlights the need to consider pathway compensation in therapeutic design124. Targeting 5-LOX has also shown therapeutic promise, with several inhibitors demonstrating anti-inflammatory and anticancer effects in preclinical models. However, some of these agents exert off-target cytotoxic effects independent of 5-LOX inhibition, highlighting the need for improved selectivity125. In contrast, the role of 15-LOX isoforms remains complex, as they exhibit both pro- and anti-tumourigenic activities depending on cellular context. A major challenge in this field is the limited availability of isoform-specific inhibitors which complicates the precise delineation of LOX functions and their therapeutic exploitation126. These lipid mediators have emerging roles in modulating immune responses and tumour cell behaviour.

ROS/RNS-mediated oxidative stress

ROS were historically viewed as harmful byproducts of cellular metabolism because of their capacity to damage nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids. However, literature precedents have reported that ROS, along with RNS (reactive nitrogen species), are bona fide signalling mediators that participate in the redox-sensitive pathways. These pathways govern proliferation, differentiation, immune activation, and metabolic flexibility127. Then the question arises is that What keeps this signalling functional rather than destructive? The simple answer is the presence of sophisticated antioxidant and redox-buffering infrastructure that maintains a dynamic equilibrium between ROS generation and detoxification. However, when that equilibrium is lost due to any reason, the resulting oxidative stress becomes a contributor to cancer pathogenesis and a range of other chronic disorders by causing genetic imbalance and activation of other pathways128,129.

To our interest, the role of ROS in cancer is not simply harmful; instead, it is contextual. At moderate levels, they function as signalling intermediates that support proliferation, stress adaptation, survival, and also influence autophagy130. This is a biologically meaningful ambiguity as the same signalling molecule that helps a stressed cell adapt, but at higher concentrations or over longer time courses, becomes a driver of mutagenesis and oncogenic pathway activation. This can be explained as sustained accumulation of ROS increases mutational burden, which engages with MAPK and NF-κB cascades to support the proliferative, migratory, and angiogenic behaviours that characterise tumour expansion131–133. Within the TME also redox dysregulation shapes the inflammatory landscape in multiple ways involving tumour and immune cells. Hydrogen peroxide-mediated signalling promotes the secretion of chemotactic factors that recruit macrophages and cancer-associated fibroblasts, while oncogene-driven mitochondrial ROS production can activate inflammasome complexes and stimulate pro-inflammatory cytokine release134. RNS introduces an additional layer of complexity as the nitric oxide species reacts with superoxide to generate peroxynitrite, which is a highly reactive species that amplifies both oxidative damage and inflammatory signalling. These processes converge on transcription factors NF-κB and AP-1 and later establish a self-sustaining feedback loops that entrench chronic inflammation and maintain tumour-supportive conditions135–137. At the immune level, oxidative stress impairs effector cell function and tilts the TME towards immunosuppression, further facilitating disease progression and therapeutic resistance138. Therefore, ROS serve as critical regulators of cancer biology, functioning as indispensable signalling molecules under physiological conditions but acting as potent drivers of genomic damage, inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and immune evasion when present in excess. Understanding the context-dependent nature of ROS signalling is therefore essential for the development of therapeutic strategies that effectively target redox imbalances in cancer139–141.

Cytokines and chemokines

Cytokines are low-molecular-weight signalling proteins that regulate cellular proliferation, differentiation, survival, and apoptosis through receptor-mediated activation of intracellular transcriptional programs142. Major cytokine families, including interferons, interleukins, TNF superfamily members, chemokines, and growth factors, play essential roles in physiological homeostasis and disease pathogenesis143. Dysregulated cytokine networks contribute to cancer initiation and progression by sustaining chronic inflammation and facilitating immune evasion within the TME. Several cytokines, such as IFN-α, IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12, IL-15, and GM-CSF, demonstrate antitumour activity by directly suppressing tumour growth or enhancing immune-mediated clearance (Figure 3), and their mechanism of action are discussed in the following sections briefly144.

Interleukins (ILs)

ILs represent a diverse class of cytokines that are known to critically regulate immune homeostasis, inflammation, and antitumour immunity145. IL-2, originally identified as a T-cell growth factor, plays a central role in adaptive immune responses by promoting the proliferation, differentiation, and cytotoxic activity of T lymphocytes and natural killer cells146. Generally, it comprises three receptor subunits, viz. IL-2Rα, IL-2Rβ, and IL-2Rγ-whose assembly determines binding affinity and downstream signalling through JAK-STAT, MAPK, and PI3K pathways. Noteworthy to mention that, IL-2 maintains the immune balance by enhancing the effector T-cell responses and immune surveillance, and it also supports regulatory T-cell expansion147. However, impaired IL-2 signalling is associated with poor outcomes in several malignancies, whereas therapeutic administration can stimulate antitumour immunity and improve immunotherapeutic responses148. Similarly, IL-10 exerts complex and context-dependent functions in cancer. Generally, it is produced by multiple immune cell types and certain tumour cells, and it signals through IL-10Rα and IL-10Rβ to activate JAK1-TYK2-STAT3 pathways. As a consequence, induction of anti-inflammatory mediators helps to suppress excessive immune activation149. This immunosuppressive capacity can facilitate tumour progression by dampening antigen presentation and effector T-cell responses150. Paradoxically, IL-10 can also enhance antitumour activity by stimulating tumour-resident CD8+ T cells by increasing interferon-γ production and cytotoxic potential, and thereby restraining tumour growth in specific settings151. Additionally, IL-12 and IL-15 serve as key immunostimulatory cytokines that reinforce antitumour immunity. IL-12, primarily produced by antigen-presenting cells, and signals through IL-12Rβ1/β2 to activate JAK2-TYK2-STAT4 pathways, which promote T helper 1 differentiation and interferon-γ production while suppressing Th2 polarisation152. However, IL-15 is mainly characterised by its trans-presentation mechanism via IL-15Rα, which supports the proliferation, maintenance, and survival of natural killer cells and memory CD8+ T cells, distinguishing its functions from IL-2 and enhancing long-term immune responses153.

In contrast, IL-1 and IL-6 predominantly drive tumour-promoting inflammation. IL-1 signalling through NF-κB and MAPK pathways amplifies pro-inflammatory cytokine production, angiogenesis, and metastatic potential, while also contributing to therapy resistance153,154. IL-6, acting through classical and trans-signalling mechanisms, activates the JAK-STAT3 axis to promote tumour cell survival, angiogenesis, and immune evasion. Moreover, elevated IL-6 levels generally correlate with poor prognosis and reduced responses to immune checkpoint blockade. Taken together, these cytokines illustrate the dual and context-dependent roles of interleukin signalling in cancer, highlighting their potential as both therapeutic targets and modulators of antitumour immunity155–157.

Tumour necrosis factor (TNF)

TNF was originally identified as a mediator of endotoxin-induced haemorrhagic tumour necrosis, leading to early interest in its potential as an anticancer agent158. Subsequent molecular characterisation revealed that TNF, also known as cachectin, plays a central role in host responses to infection, including acute inflammatory shock and cancer-associated cachexia158. Although it was initially considered therapeutically promising due to its tumouricidal properties under certain conditions, but clinical application of TNF has been constrained by a narrow therapeutic window and limited cytotoxicity at physiologically tolerable doses. However, current understanding recognises TNF as a context-dependent mediator that can promote tumour progression by shaping a tumour-supportive microenvironment159. The mechanisms through which TNF supports tumour progression are multiple and interconnected. It stimulates angiogenesis, enhancing vascularisation and nutrient supply to support tumour growth and dissemination160. In addition, TNF influences immune regulation within the TME by impairing antitumour immune responses. Within the TME, its effects on immune regulation are particularly consequential. TNF limits cytotoxic CD8+ T cell accumulation and function through activation-induced cell death, reduces NK cell activity by modulating inhibitory and activating receptor expression, and promotes both the expansion of regulatory T cells and the survival of myeloid-derived suppressor cells160–162. Collectively, it reinforces the immunosuppressive conditions by enhancing the expansion and suppressive activity of regulatory T cells and supports the survival of myeloid-derived suppressor cells, which allow tumours to evade immune clearance163. TNF further compounds this, which collectively reinforces immune evasion. It enhances recruitment of tumour-associated macrophages and stabilises PD-L1 expression on tumour cells, and directly dampening the immune surveillance163. These multifaceted effects highlight TNF as a critical regulator of tumour-immune interactions and underscore the therapeutic potential of TNF blockade to enhance antitumour immunity and improve responses to immune checkpoint inhibition164.

Interferons

Interferons constitute a critical family of cytokines that bridge innate and adaptive immunity and play a complex, context-dependent role in cancer biology. Based on receptor usage and structural features, interferons are classified into three major types: type I interferons (IFN-α, IFN-β, IFN-ε, IFN-κ, and IFN-ω), type II interferon (IFN-γ), and type III interferons (IFN-λ1–λ4)165. IFN-Is, including IFN-α and IFN-β, constitute a critical arm of the innate immune response to diverse cellular stresses and pathogenic challenges166. These cytokines signal through a heterodimeric receptor complex composed of IFNAR1 and IFNAR2, which initiates downstream activation of JAK1 and TYK2. Subsequent phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT2 promotes formation of transcriptional complexes that induce interferon-stimulated genes, while parallel activation of MAPK and PI3K pathways amplifies immune and inflammatory signalling167,168. In cancer, IFN-I signalling has long been associated with tumour-suppressive effects, including induction of senescence and apoptosis, as well as enhancement of cytotoxic T-cell responses through improved dendritic cell maturation, antigen presentation, and immune memory formation169. Emerging evidence, however, highlights the context-dependent nature of IFN-I activity. Acute and robust IFN-I responses typically reinforce antitumour immunity and tumour immunosurveillance, whereas chronic or low-level signalling can promote immune suppression, therapy resistance, and tumour progression through upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules, by recruiting of suppressive immune populations, and induction of stem-like and mesenchymal phenotypes170,171. Furthermore, IFN-γ, the sole member of the type II interferon family, similarly exerts multifaceted roles in tumour biology172. It enhances antigen presentation, promotes cytotoxic immune activity, and suppresses angiogenesis and regulatory T-cell survival, which collectively support antitumour responses173. Nonetheless, sustained IFN-γ exposure can also drive immune evasion by inducing expression of inhibitory molecules such as PD-L1 and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase, while alterations in receptor signalling or downstream JAK/STAT pathways enable tumour cells to escape its cytotoxic effects174,175. These findings underscore the necessity for precise temporal and contextual modulation of interferon signalling to maximise therapeutic benefit while minimising protumour consequences172.

Chemokines

Chemokines are chemotactic cytokines that guide the immune cell during inflammation, and they are subcategorised into four types, viz. CXC, CC, CX3C, XX. They are a central regulatory network that helps in linking chronic inflammation to tumour initiation and progression. Within inflammation-driven cancers, these mediators coordinate dynamic interactions among malignant cells, stromal elements, and infiltrating immune populations, as a consequence re-shaping the TME176. By regulating immune cell recruitment, spatial organisation, and functional polarisation, chemokines influence tumour cell proliferation, invasiveness, stemness, and survival while also modulating angiogenesis and fibrotic remodelling177. Although certain chemokine signals facilitate antitumour immune responses through the recruitment of effector lymphocytes, many chemokine-driven pathways reinforce immunosuppressive and tumour-promoting conditions. The net biological outcome depends on disease stage, receptor expression patterns, and the activation status of immune cells143,178. For instance, the CCL2-CCR2 pathway supports tumour growth and metastatic dissemination by recruiting immunosuppressive populations such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells, regulatory T cells, and tumour-associated macrophages, while also promoting angiogenesis179. Similarly, the CCR4 signalling axis, activated by ligands CCL17 and CCL22 produced within tumours, drives accumulation of regulatory T cells and contributes to resistance to immune checkpoint blockade180. The CCL5-CCR5 pathway further amplifies inflammatory tumour progression by activating signalling cascades, such as PI3K-AKT and NF-κB which thereby enhances proliferation, survival, and immune evasion181. Additional chemokine networks such as CXCL8-CXCR1/2 and CXCL12-CXCR4, promote angiogenesis, metastasis, and recruitment of suppressive immune cells that reinforce chronic inflammatory conditions that sustain tumour growth182,183. These pathways collectively establish a protumourigenic inflammatory milieu. Targeted modulation of chemokine signalling, therefore, represents a promising strategy to disrupt inflammation-driven cancer progression and enhance the effectiveness of immunotherapeutic interventions184.

Inflammasomes (NLRP3)

Inflammasomes are multiprotein signalling platforms that function as central regulators of innate immune surveillance by sensing pathogen-derived and endogenous stress signals. Among these, members of the nucleotide-binding oligomerisation domain-like receptor (NLR) family act as cytosolic pattern-recognition receptors that coordinate inflammatory signalling and host defense185. At the core of this system are NLR family proteins, 14 of which have been identified in humans and they function as pattern-recognition receptors that sense cellular stresses; as a consequence, they initiate the downstream signalling186. Depending on the stimulus and cellular context, activated NLRs can engage NF-κB and MAPK cascades directly, or assemble inflammasome complexes that activate caspase-1 and drive maturation of IL-1β and IL-18187,188. It is worth noting that dysregulation of these pathways has been implicated in metabolic, autoimmune, and chronic inflammatory disorders, and increasing evidence indicates that these pathways also influence tumour biology by modulating cellular proliferation, tissue remodelling, and immune responses189. Among the NLR family members, the NLRP3 inflammasome is the most extensively studied in the context of oncology. In response to pathogen-associated or damage-associated molecular patterns, NLRP3 interacts with the adaptor protein ASC and procaspase-1 to form an active inflammasome complex190. This assembly triggers caspase-1 activation, resulting in cytokine maturation and induction of pyroptosis. While pyroptosis serves as a protective mechanism to eliminate damaged or infected cells but continuous activation of NLRP3 promotes chronic inflammation and reinforces tumour-supportive signalling networks. In particular, IL-1β and IL-18 amplify NF-κB-dependent inflammatory loops which in return enhance angiogenesis, and reshape the TME towards an immunosuppressive state191,192. Yet NLRP3 is not uniformly tumour-promoting. For example, in colitis-associated colorectal cancer, it was observed that reduced inflammasome activity correlates with increased tumour burden and IL-18 that contributes to epithelial barrier integrity and antitumour immune function. This finding illustrates how the same signalling platform can have opposing effects depending on tumour type and inflammatory context190. Considerably, this context-dependence signalling activation complicates therapeutic targeting. For instance, in breast cancer models, inhibiting NLRP3 or caspase-1 has reduced mitogenic signalling, followed by limited cell cycle progression and enhanced apoptosis, which positions the inflammasome as a metabolically responsive node whose suppression can have genuine antitumour effects193. In colorectal cancer, NLRP3 has been implicated in connecting chronic intestinal inflammation to tumour initiation via MAPK-mTOR-S6K1 signalling and genetic or pharmacological pathway suppression attenuates tumorigenesis in preclinical models194. Furthermore, regulation of NLRP3 expression by microRNAs has also been shown to influence invasive behaviour and metastatic potential195. Beyond epithelial malignancies, aberrant inflammasome signalling contributes to lung cancer development and may influence therapeutic resistance by reshaping cytokine networks within the TME196. Notably, NLRP3 activation has been reported to suppress antitumour T-cell responses following dendritic cell-based immunotherapy, which suggests that selective modulation of inflammasome activity could enhance immunotherapeutic efficacy197. Furthermore, Pharmacological inhibitors, including MCC950, CY-09, Ac-YVAD-cmk, and VX-765 have demonstrated antiproliferative and anti-inflammatory activity in preclinical settings, providing a starting point for translational development. Hence, its bidirectional influence on tumour progression and immune surveillance highlights both the challenges and targeting inflammasome signalling effectively will require tumour-type-specific strategies informed by a clear understanding of the inflammatory state being modulated198,199.

TME and immune cells

TME is not a passive backdrop to tumour biology, but it is an active and continuously evolving ecosystem whose composition and signalling state are as consequential as the genetic alterations within the tumour cells themselves. It is shaped by the coexistence of malignant cells alongside macrophages, neutrophils, mast cells, dendritic cells, NK cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and lymphoid populations, including T and B cells. All of these cells interact with fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and mesenchymal stromal cells through receptor-ligand contacts and cytokine-chemokine mediated signalling networks (Figure 3)200,201. Importantly, these interactions are not isolated events but are tightly regulated by upstream inflammatory pathways that includes ROS-driven oxidative stress, NF-κB activation, STAT3 signalling, and COX-2/PGE2-mediated lipid signalling, which collectively shape immune cell recruitment, activation, and function. The balance between antitumour immunity and tumour-promoting inflammation is therefore dictated not only by immune cell composition but also by the signalling networks that define their functional states. In advanced malignancies, this balance is typically skewed towards chronic inflammation and immune evasion, promoting tumour progression15,202,203. Therefore, various immune cells and their phenotypes play a vital role in oncogenic setup, and their role is briefly discussed in the following paragraphs.

Firstly, tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) illustrate this dynamic with particular clarity. As one of the most abundant immune populations in solid tumours, TAMs exert broad influence over angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodelling, invasion, and metastasis. Generally, clinical reports demonstrated that high TAM infiltration is consistently correlated with poor clinical outcomes across cancer types204. Moreover, macrophage polarisation plays a decisive role in determining tumour behaviour. For instance, classically activated M1-like macrophages support inflammatory and cytotoxic responses, but on contrary, M2-like TAMs dominate in established tumours and promote immunosuppression, angiogenesis, and tissue remodelling through the secretion of IL-10, TGF-β, and VEGFR205. Importantly, macrophage phenotypes are not fixed as they remain highly plastic and are continually reshaped by local cytokine gradients and metabolic conditions within the TME. This plasticity is both a biological vulnerability of tumours and a therapeutic opportunity206. Moving further, T lymphocytes also display an analogous functional duality. For instance, cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and Th1-polarized CD4+ T cells are associated with favourable outcomes through their capacity to kill tumour cells and sustain antitumour immunity. But Th2 and Th17 subsets on contrary can promote tumour progression by recruiting immunosuppressive myeloid populations and sustaining chronic inflammation207. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) further reinforce immune tolerance by suppressing effector T-cell activity through the involvement of CTLA-4, IL-10, and TGF-β mediated pathways which thereby facilitates tumour immune escape. In certain inflammatory contexts, however, Tregs may also restrain excessive carcinogenic inflammation, underscoring the context-dependent nature of immune regulation208. Moreover, beyond cellular composition of TME, the mood of cytokine and chemokine environment within tumours often proves to be a stronger determinant of disease behaviour than cell counts alone. For instance, presence of IL-6, IL-17, and IL-23 sustain the chronic activation of NF-κB, STAT3, AP-1, and SMAD pathways, that drives proliferation, survival, angiogenesis, and immune suppression in a self-reinforcing manner98,209. Concurrently, chemokine-driven recruitment of MDSCs and other suppressive populations amplifies local immunosuppression through nutrient depletion, ROS generation, and direct inhibition of T-cell activation210,211. Persistent inflammatory signalling further induces expression of immune checkpoint molecules like PD-L1, CTLA-4, and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase that collectively attenuate cytotoxic immune responses and contribute to therapeutic resistance212,213.

Therefore, it can be stated that Inflammation and carcinogenesis is most accurately understood not as a collection of parallel pathways but as an integrated signalling network in which redox imbalance, inflammatory transcriptional programs, lipid mediators, and immune cell dynamics converge and mutually reinforce one another to shape tumour evolution214. These transcriptional programs sustain cytokine and chemokine production, which in return recruit and repolarize immune cells within the TME and develops a feed-forward loop that which once gets established then it is difficult to interrupt at any single node215. Further lipid mediators generated through COX-2 activity, particularly PGE2, further modulate this network by suppressing antitumour immunity and enhancing tumour cell survival. NLRP3 inflammasome activation integrates stress and danger signals into this architecture by regulating cytokine maturation and immune activation in ways that are highly context-dependent216. Thus the cumulative effect of these interconnected processes is a transition from acute, protective inflammation to a chronic, tumour-promoting state characterised by immune evasion, sustained proliferative signalling, and resistance to therapy217. Thus, this system’s level view of inflammation in cancer does more than clarifying the mechanism, but it reveals therapeutic logic. Rational design of next-generation therapies will depend on this kind of integrated understanding of the self-sustaining inflammatory circuits that underlie tumour progression218.

Anti-inflammatory therapeutics for cancer

Targeting inflammation has emerged as a rational strategy in cancer therapy, given its central role in tumour initiation, progression, and resistance. From a medicinal chemistry perspective, diverse classes of anti-inflammatory therapeutics have been explored to modulate key signalling pathways and the tumour microenvironment. These include anti-infective agents that indirectly suppress inflammation-driven carcinogenesis, NSAIDs, and small molecules or biologics that modulate inflammatory cytokines and chemokine networks. In addition, natural anti-inflammatory compounds and their derivatives have gained attention as structurally diverse scaffolds with multitarget potential. Collectively, these approaches highlight the chemical and pharmacological diversity available for intervention along the inflammation-cancer axis. In the following subsections, each of these therapeutic classes will be discussed in detail with emphasis on their molecular targets and translational relevance.

Anti-infective therapeutics

As mentioned above, various infectious diseases are the primary cause of inflammation-induced cancers. This is because of the persistent presence of chronic inflammation during the tenure. Therefore, anti-infective therapeutics, viz. antiviral, antibacterial, and antifungal therapies, represent a genuinely preventive tier of cancer medicine, not merely an adjunct to it (Table 1). Viral infections such as the hepatitis B and C are leading causes of hepatocellular carcinoma, where continuous immune activation and inflammatory signalling contribute to liver damage and tumorigenesis219. Notably, preventive strategies, particularly vaccination, have significantly reduced cancer incidence, which is very well documented in HBV immunisation program reports. The use of interferon-based regimens, nucleotide analogues, and direct-acting antivirals has suppressed the viral replication and supported immune clearance which reduces the cancer risk and thereby improves the survival of infected individuals. However, they don’t eliminate it entirely which is a reminder that host and environmental factors remain independently operative220–222. Similarly, persistent infection with high-risk HPV is responsible for the majority of cervical cancers. Prophylactic HPV vaccination has demonstrated remarkable success in reducing infection rates and precancerous lesions, and it underlines that the infection can be intervened at the early stage before inflammatory damage accumulates223. Some oncogenic viruses, such as Epstein-Barr virus, by contrast, remain therapeutically unaddressed despite their association with multiple malignancies and they display a conspicuous gap in the field224. Similarly, among bacterial pathogens, H. pylori carries the strongest evidence base for the inflammation-induced gastric cancer. Its eradication with antibiotic regimens reduces both the incidence and recurrence of gastric cancer, and this relationship is very well-established to inform clinical guidelines225. Fusobacterium nucleatum has more recently been implicated in colorectal cancer progression through inflammatory pathway modulation. However, due to a lack of specific antibiotics, broad-spectrum antibiotic use remains limited due to potential disruption of beneficial microbiota and lack of target specificity225,226. Moreover, fungal infection contributions to cancer-associated inflammation are less characterised but not negligible. For instance, associations of Candida species with oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma suggest antifungal strategies may carry preventive value in specific contexts, though clinical translation remains at an early stage227. Therefore, these findings position anti-infective therapy as a legitimate and underutilised instrument in cancer prevention, particularly when deployed with appropriate specificity.

Various anti-inflammatory therapeutics reported for cancer.

NSAIDs

NSAIDs occupy a pragmatic middle ground in cancer prevention and are commonly used for managing pain, fever, and cardiovascular risk through inhibition of COX enzymes. Among them, aspirin has attracted the most sustained attention. Its regular use is associated with a reduced risk across several inflammation-related cancers, with the evidence strongest for colorectal cancer and it is highly recommended as a preventive approach in high-risk populations228. In patients with chronic hepatitis, long-term low-dose aspirin has been associated with lower rates of hepatocellular carcinoma which suggests that the anti-inflammatory benefit extends across different aetiologies of inflammation-driven cancer229. In Lynch syndrome, one of the hereditary cancer syndromes, aspirin has demonstrated a measurable reduction in cancer incidence. These reports support its role as a chemopreventive agent, especially in cancers driven by chronic inflammation230,231. Similarly, other NSAIDs like celecoxib, ibuprofen, ketoprofen, oxicam, and many more have been evaluated across a range of tumour types with varying outcomes, detailed in Table 1. The pattern across this literature is consistent and displays that anti-inflammatory activity in the right context can translate to meaningful chemopreventive effect, but the degree of benefit is tumour-type dependent and influenced by dose duration and patient selection.

Lipid-lowering drugs

Literature precedents have reported that the connection between dyslipidemia and cancer is mediated, at least in part, through inflammation. Elevated levels of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol sustains a low-grade inflammatory state that over time may contribute to tumour-permissive tissue conditions. Owing to this, statins, widely used HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors developed for cardiovascular risk reduction which suppress cholesterol synthesis and carry secondary anti-inflammatory effects that have prompted serious inquiry into their oncological potential232. Therefore, early clinical observations suggested that long-term statin use, particularly beyond 5 years, was associated with reduced CRC risk, and some studies noted lower incidence among statin users undergoing colonic screening233,234. When large-scale clinical trials were conducted, the reports displayed modest or less consistent results among the different population profiles and disease states235,236. Interestingly, stronger protective effects of statins have been reported in high-risk groups, such as patients with viral hepatitis, diabetes, or liver cirrhosis, particularly in reducing HCC risk. Moreover, there are some literature evidences of protection against H. pylori-associated gastric cancer also with the use of statins237,238. These studies exhibit the promising potency of statins for different subgroups of cancer, but still, the clinical validation needs more well-designed studies.

Similarly, Metformin, a first-line treatment for type 2 diabetes and it has also attracted analogous interest. Epidemiological data suggest that metformin use is associated with a lower rate of several cancers, including liver, colorectal, pancreatic, and breast cancers239. Notably, multiple studies have reported a significant reduction in HCC risk among diabetic patients taking metformin240. Clinical trials further support its preventive role, as they depicted that metformin intake is linked with reduced recurrence of colorectal polyps and adenomas241. And metformin is now being explored in combination regimens, including with aspirin in the ASAMET trial, to determine whether additive anti-inflammatory effects translate to enhanced cancer prevention242. Its low cost and established safety profile make it a practical candidate for repurposing, though the quality of evidence supporting definitive recommendations requires further strengthening through well-designed prospective trials, and Table 1 provides information regarding the usage of various statins and metformin in multiple cancer types.

Targeting anti-inflammatory agents

Various inflammatory mediators, like cytokines and chemokines, exhibit a crucial role in inflammation. Therefore, scientists tried to explore this domain in treating cancer. It started with cytokine-directed therapy which has moved from a conceptually attractive idea to a clinically tested strategy, with results that are instructive both for what they demonstrate and for the complications they reveal (Table 1). Among these, inhibition of IL-1 signalling has shown notable clinical relevance. For instance, in a large clinical trial originally designed for cardiovascular disease, the anti-IL-1β antibody canakinumab (CANTOS trial) unexpectedly demonstrated a significant reduction in lung cancer incidence and mortality that signifies the strong link between systemic inflammation and tumour development. However, the accompanying increase in serious infection risk due to immune suppression underscored a challenge that runs through this entire therapeutic category, i.e. anti-inflammatory potency and immunosuppressive risk are difficult to dissociate. Therefore, patient selection is not a minor operational consideration but a central determinant of benefit-to-risk balance243. Similarly, blockade of IL-1α by using monoclonal antibodies like MABp1 has shown benefits in advanced cancers by improving symptoms such as fatigue and weight loss, with acceptable safety profiles. Therefore, it can be positioned as a useful therapeutic in palliative-adjacent care244. Furthermore, TNF inhibitors like monoclonal antibodies such as infliximab and receptor decoys like etanercept, have been evaluated in multiple cancer settings globally. However, the results didn’t seem to be promising in terms of anticancer activity but they may contribute to disease stabilisation and are particularly useful in managing immune-related toxicities associated with immunotherapy. Therefore, TNF inhibitors are under exploration in combination therapies both clinically and at preclinical levels also245,246. IL-6 is another key mediator linking inflammation and cancer progression. While IL-6-targeted therapies such as tocilizumab, clazakizumab, and siltuximab are effective in treating inflammatory disorders and immune-related complications like cytokine release syndrome, but their direct anticancer efficacy as monotherapy remains modest that is why they are also being used in combination strategies to provide holistic inhibition of key pathways in the cancer environment237,247. TGF-β inhibition represents a mechanistically distinct approach. Small-molecule inhibitors such as galunisertib, and vactosertib have exhibited acceptable safety and modest activity in pancreatic and liver cancer, particularly when used in combination with standard-of-care agents. Moreover, ongoing research is exploring more potent inhibitors and antibody-based approaches targeting this pathway248,249. Additionally, strategies targeting immune-suppressive myeloid populations within TME have also gained traction in the past few years. Therapeutics targeting receptors such as CSF1R, CCR2, and CCR5 aim to modulate tumour-associated macrophages, while CXCR2 and CXCR4 antagonists (SX-682 and BL-8040, respectively) interfere with myeloid-derived suppressor cell recruitment. Therefore, when they are combined with checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab, these approaches have produced encouraging results in metastatic melanoma and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma250,251. Further, multiple therapeutics targeting other aspects of cytokines and chemokines are mentioned in Table 1.

Natural anti-inflammatory supplements in cancer prevention

In addition to conventional drugs, several natural compounds occupy a complicated position in cancer prevention research as they are mechanistically plausible, often practically active, but frequently inconsistent in clinical translation. Globally, several natural compounds have been explored for their anti-inflammatory and potential anticancer effects after the successful identification and translation of vincristine and vinblastine (Table 1). For instance, vitamin C, widely known for its antioxidant properties, has shown anticancer activity mainly when administered at high intravenous doses. Under these conditions, it acts as a pro-oxidant rather than functioning through its traditional antioxidant role252. Similarly, vitamin D plays a key role in immune regulation by balancing pro- and anti-inflammatory responses253. Clinical observational studies have reported that higher circulating levels of vitamin D are associated with a lower risk of certain cancers, such as colorectal and prostate cancer, and it may correlate with improved outcomes in some cancer patients254. However, large clinical trials (AMATERASU) have produced inconsistent results regarding its role in cancer prevention, which suggests that routine supplementation may not provide a universal benefit255.

Moreover, curcumin has also been investigated in several clinical trials for its potential anticancer and anti-inflammatory effects, although clinical evidence remains limited and less consistent compared to preclinical findings. Early-phase studies have shown that curcumin is safe and well-tolerated, even at relatively high oral doses. Reportedly, in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer, curcumin has been evaluated in combination with standard chemotherapy, which has been reported to have potential therapeutic benefit. It has also been studied as a chemopreventive agent in colorectal neoplasia and as a dietary supplement in head and neck cancer, with some evidence indicating modulation of cancer-related biomarkers. However, not all trials have demonstrated clear benefits, highlighting the need for further well-designed studies. Additionally, omega-3 fatty acids are also known for their anti-inflammatory properties and have also been investigated in cancer prevention. While overall evidence remains limited but some studies suggest that they may reduce the risk of colorectal adenomas, particularly in individuals with low baseline levels256. In contrast, other dietary supplements such as β-carotene, vitamin E, selenium, and folic acid have shown inconsistent results or even adverse effects, which signifies the need for caution257. Interestingly, garlic-derived supplements, due to their antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties, have demonstrated some benefit in reducing cancer-related mortality, particularly in gastric cancer, although their effect on incidence is less clear and underexplored258. Similarly, berberine (plant-derived alkaloid) has also shown promise in reducing the recurrence of colorectal adenomas during preclinical studies259. Hence, while natural supplements offer potential as supportive roles but their clinical efficacy varies across individuals. Therefore, further well-designed studies are required to establish their role in cancer prevention and therapy.

Despite considerable progress in the development of inflammation-targeting small molecules, their successful translation into clinical oncology remains challenging. One major limitation is the extensive redundancy and crosstalk among inflammatory signalling pathways, whereby inhibition of a single target can be compensated by alternative mechanisms, which ultimately reduces the therapeutic efficacy. In addition, many inflammatory mediators are involved in normal physiological processes; therefore, the risk of off-target effects and systemic toxicities is associated with long-term treatment with anti-inflammatory agents. Another significant challenge is the marked heterogeneity of tumours, both between patients and within individual tumours, which influences the inflammatory landscape and therapeutic responsiveness. Variations in genetic alterations, immune-cell composition, and tumour microenvironmental factors can also result in differential treatment outcomes which underscores the need for biomarker-guided patient stratification and personalised therapeutic approaches. Addressing these challenges will be essential for maximising the clinical potential of inflammation-targeted small-molecule interventions in cancer therapy.237,260

Small-molecule therapeutic intervention

Considering the critical involvement of key inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB, ROS-mediated signalling, JAK/STAT, inflammasomes, and cytokine networks, extensive efforts have been directed towards the development of small-molecule therapeutics targeting these axes. Over the past 5 years, a diverse range of compounds has been reported, encompassing single-target inhibitors, dual inhibitors, multitarget-directed ligands, and prodrug strategies designed to modulate these interconnected pathways. These approaches reflect a shift towards rational design aimed at overcoming pathway redundancy and therapeutic resistance. In the following section, recent advances in small-molecule development are summarised, with particular emphasis on design strategies, structure-activity relationships (SAR), and their in vitro and in vivo pharmacological evaluations.

Future perspective

The central challenge in targeting the inflammation-carcinogenesis axis is no longer limited to identifying inflammatory mediators, but rather to accurately interpreting the inflammatory state of a tumour and intervening in a context-dependent manner. A critical conceptual advancement in this field is the recognition that inflammation exerts dual and temporally distinct roles in cancer. Therefore, the central therapeutic challenge is not merely the suppression of inflammation, but the ability to differentiate between tumour-promoting and tumour-inhibiting inflammatory states and modulate them accordingly. Merging these two states into a single therapeutic target has historically been the field’s most consequential oversimplification. Furthermore, understanding this duality at the molecular level reveals why generally blunt suppression fails. Common inflammatory signals like TNF-α, interleukins, and interferons are not simply pro- or anti-tumourigenic, as their effects are a function of duration and context. The same logic applies to immune cell composition within the TME. Tumour-associated macrophages and regulatory T cells do not simply coexist with tumour cells but they are actively shaped by the TME into immunosuppressive phenotypes. Therefore, reversing that polarisation instead of depleting these populations outright is emerging as the more tractable and durable therapeutic objective. Advances in gene editing and synthetic biology are also expanding what is therapeutically conceivable and allow modifications in order to re-educate the cells. For instance, CRISPR-based modifications of immune checkpoints and inflammatory pathways allow engineers to redesign immune cells rather than simply stimulate or suppress them. One of the more clinically meaningful applications has been in CAR-T cell development, where editing exhaustion-related genes has shown a genuine potential to sustain anticancer activity within the immunosuppressive state of TME, where earlier adoptive cell therapies got neutralised before they could produce an effect415. Synthetic biological circuits capable of dynamically regulating cytokine production further offer opportunities to fine-tune immune responses in real time. In parallel, emerging RNA-editing technologies provide reversible modulation of inflammatory signalling and offer an additional layer of safety compared to permanent genomic alterations416.

Furthermore, the integration of microbiome research into cancer biology is gaining momentum, and it represents a transformative frontier. The rationale behind this momentum is the central role of gut microbiota in the regulation of systemic immunity and active involvement in the modulation of inflammatory responses in a way that is now understood to affect tumour progression and therapeutic outcomes. Reports have shown that specific microbial taxa, including Bifidobacterium and Akkermansia, have been associated with improved responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors. On the other hand, continuous sustainment of a dysbiosis state leads to low-grade chronic inflammation that accelerates tumour progression417. Importantly, the microbiome also influences the metabolism, efficacy, and toxicity profile of chemotherapeutic agents, meaning that two patients on identical regimens may experience meaningfully different outcomes based on gut ecology alone. Emerging strategies to exploit this include microbiome engineering, probiotic interventions, and faecal microbiota transplantation holds a promise for enhancing therapeutic efficacy and may enable precise manipulation of host-microbe interactions418. Moving further, technological advances, viz., artificial intelligence and machine learning, are simultaneously transforming how we study inflammation within tumours. Single-cell and spatial omics platforms have made it feasible to map immune and inflammatory cell populations at a resolution that bulk tissue analysis cannot achieve, and this will help to understand and identify unrecognised cell states and inflammatory niches that correlate with therapeutic resistance419. In the past, analysing the omics datasets was a major hurdle, which is where machine learning becomes genuinely useful, not as a buzzword, but as a practical tool for integrating multi-omics data, modelling how inflammatory networks evolve, and identifying predictive biomarkers. For instance, AI-powered immune cell knowledge graphs, C2S-scale, and many others are emerging AI-powered tools that are shaping the traditional way of understanding inflammation and immune responses in tumours. In the context of drug discovery, AI-driven drug pipelines are also identifying novel targets, for instance, TLR4 modulators, and enabling the rational design of small molecules that would be difficult to arrive at through conventional screening alone420. From a medicinal chemistry standpoint, the architecture of inflammatory signalling networks poses a fundamental design problem. Traditional single-target inhibitors consistently underperform because of compensatory pathway activation, which restores the signalling output that was being suppressed. The field has responded by moving towards multitarget-directed ligands, hybrid molecules, and prodrug strategies which are capable of simultaneously engaging inflammatory interconnected nodes. Moreover, drug repurposing offers a pragmatic strategy for accelerating clinical translation compared to de novo compounds though initial challenges related to dose optimisation, long-term toxicity, and context-dependent effects need to be carefully addressed421. Similarly, drug delivery is equally consequential. A molecule that modulates the TME and its pathways effectively during in vitro studies but distributes poorly in vivo, which might be due to degradation before reaching the tumour, off-target effects, low permeability, and low solubility. This is where nanotechnology delivery systems come into play. Engineered nanoparticles, antibody drug conjugates, and stimuli-responsive nanoparticles address this by enabling spatially targeted delivery of therapeutics, improving local drug concentrations while reducing systemic exposure. That said, the gap between preclinical promise and clinical validation in anti-inflammatory nanomedicine remains wide and closing it will require more rigorous translational studies than the field has produced to date422. Immunotherapeutic strategies remain central to this landscape. PD-1 and CTLA-4 checkpoint inhibitors have genuinely reshaped oncology, yet response rates across tumour types remain modest, and the immunosuppressive character of many TMEs continues to limit their reach. Combining checkpoint inhibitors with anti-inflammatory agents, microbiome modulation, or radiotherapy is a logical response to this limitation as each modality is addressing a different dimension of immune dysfunction423.

Ultimately, the field’s most important conceptual task is reframing inflammation- from a target to be suppressed towards a dynamic system to be reprogrammed. Precision medicine frameworks should integrate genomic, immunological, and inflammatory biomarker data for patient stratification before treatment failure, rather than after because this can prevent the failure of clinical outcomes. Further, sex hormone status, metabolic phenotype, and environmental exposures are meaningful modulators of inflammatory biology that trial design can no longer treat as confounders424,425. If these variables are incorporated systematically, and if multidisciplinary integration of medicinal chemistry, immunology, biology, and computational science continues to deepen, then inflammation may ultimately prove to be not merely a driver of tumour progression but also one of its most therapeutically actionable vulnerabilities.

Conclusion

The dynamic interplay between inflammation and carcinogenesis has emerged as a central paradigm in modern cancer biology. Accumulating evidence indicates that chronic inflammation is not merely the byproduct of tumour development, but it is significantly contributing to tumour initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Persistent activation of inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB, JAK/STAT, ROS-mediated signalling, inflammasomes, and cytokine-chemokine networks, establishes a self-sustaining loop that fosters a tumour-promoting microenvironment. The aforementioned activation of the signalling provides a safe haven for the tumour progression events and establishes a tumour-promoting microenvironment by regulating the multiple hallmarks of cancer, including proliferation, survival, angiogenesis, immune evasion, and metastatic dissemination. Despite the significant advances in terms of the understanding of mechanisms in hands, therapeutic strategies targeting individual inflammatory mediators have shown limited clinical efficacy. This limitation is largely supported by the pathway redundancy, extensive crosstalk, and compensatory signalling within the tumour microenvironment. Thus, a clear shift in the development of more integrated therapeutic strategies capable of modulating multiple signalling nodes simultaneously has been exploited to extract the beneficial outcome in the suppression of tumour progression and supporting environment. In this direction, small-molecule agents, including dual inhibitors, multitarget-directed ligands, and prodrug systems, are being largely fabricated with improved efficacy, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic performance. The aim of such therapeutics is not only to provide the onset of therapeutic effects but also to prolong effects with a multidirectional approach to encounter inflammation-related issues. It is also well established that the convergence of inflammation biology and medicinal chemistry could provide a robust framework for anticancer drug development. Specifically, a shift in the development of therapeutic modalities from single-target inhibition towards network-level modulation of inflammatory signalling could be explored to achieve more effective and durable therapeutic outcomes. In this context, the recent findings on employing the structure-based approaches, hybrid scaffold design, and systematic structure-activity relationship studies have enabled the identification of compounds with enhanced potency and multitarget capabilities. However, several challenges still in this direction need an exhaustive mechanistic and contextual exploration. For instance, the context-dependent and dual roles of inflammatory mediators complicate therapeutic targeting, as excessive inhibition may disrupt physiological immune functions. Furthermore, translating promising preclinical findings into clinical success remains difficult due to limitations in predictive models and variability in patient responses. Thus, targeting the dynamic interplay between inflammatory networks and tumour biology may ultimately shift current treatment paradigms from pathway inhibition to system-level reprogramming, which will ultimately transform the current understanding of inflammation from a supportive process to a therapeutically actionable vulnerability.
