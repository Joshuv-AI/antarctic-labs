High-Load Amino Acid Nanoemulsion: Physicochemical Optimization and Dermal Bioactivity
Authors: Pornnapa Thanomrak, Kittipong Sanookpan, Sirikool Thamnium, Jittima A. Luckanagul, Vipaporn Panapisal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amino acids such as GABA, glycine, and proline play critical roles in extracellular matrix renewal, but their high hydrophilicity limits dermal delivery. This study aimed to develop a stable oil-in-water nanoemulsion capable of incorporating a high amino acid load (20% w/w) and to evaluate its biological activity. Nanoemulsions were prepared using high-pressure homogenization at varying surfactant concentrations (5–7% w/w) and pressures (80–120 MPa). Physicochemical properties, stability, and amino acid content were evaluated, and biological activity was assessed in fibroblasts. The selected formulation (5% surfactant, 120 MPa), identified based on a balance of droplet size, stability, and biological response, showed improved stability and retained >95% of actives over 12 weeks. It significantly enhanced collagen and elastin gene expression. These findings demonstrate that process optimization enables stable high-load amino acid nanoemulsions with potential applications in dermal delivery.

Introduction

Amino acids serve as the fundamental building blocks of proteins, being relatively small biomolecules. Predominantly consisting of carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, and oxygen, these molecules exhibit a molecular architecture characterized by an amino group (−NH2), a carboxyl group (−COOH), a hydrogen atom and an R group (side chain). They exist in both anionic and cationic forms, a state referred to as “zwitterion” or “dipolar” ions. Amino acids are synthesized by processes within the human body and acquired from dietary sources. They play a crucial role in controlling body processes and are a major component of skin structure. Studies have demonstrated that amino acids can improve skin structure through various pathways, with specific effects varying depending on the types of amino acids employed. They have been found to possess skin-rejuvenating properties, particularly in addressing skin aging. Amino acids have the potential to increase collagen synthesis by serving as major components of collagen, such as glycine and proline. Jemec and Moe investigated the effect of 5% l-proline and 5% glycine in topical treatment on skin ulcers in prolidase deficiency. The use of 5% l-proline and 5% glycine in an ointment demonstrated a significant reduction of the ulcers. Furthermore, in the case report of a patient with prolidase deficiency showing skin ulceration, treatment with topical 5% proline in white soft paraffin ointment showed that the ulcers improved, decreasing in size and undergoing re-epithelization. The ulcers demonstrated almost complete healing. Additionally, gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) has been reported to promote elastin fiber formation by increasing the expression of tropoelastin, fibrillin, and fibulin-5, and to reduce collagen degradation through downregulation of matrix metalloproteinase-1 (MMP-1). In addition to demonstrating positive outcomes in vitro studies, GABA has also proven effective in enhancing skin hydration and elasticity in in vivo studies. Recently, combinations of amino acids have demonstrated multiple beneficial effects on skin aging, with complementary roles in improving skin structure. The use of an amino acid combination for improving skin irritation and skin damage was investigated. The components of the Amino Acid Complex (AAComplex) were glycine, arginine, and taurine. AAComplex enhanced cell regeneration in 47 h. Moreover, products containing AAComplex reduced skin irritation through a decrease in heat shock protein 27 (HSP-27) and interleukin-1 alpha (IL-1α) levels, which are biomarkers referring to skin irritation. In addition, the product also significantly increased skin hydration and reduced skin redness in vivo studies. Based on these considerations, glycine and proline were selected as primary structural components of collagen, with concentrations (5% w/w each) aligned with previously reported topical applications. Glycine was included due to its well-documented biological role and favorable solubility, whereas proline, a cyclic amino acid, presents relatively lower solubility, limiting its incorporation at higher concentrations. GABA was incorporated at a higher proportion (10% w/w) due to its reported role in modulating extracellular matrix remodeling, including the regulation of elastin-related proteins and reduction of collagen degradation. This concentration was selected within a practically acceptable range based on formulation feasibility and tolerability considerations. The selected ratio (5% glycine, 5% proline, and 10% GABA) was therefore designed to balance structural contribution, biological activity, and physicochemical constraints of the system.

This study focuses on the combination of glycine, proline, and GABA to address a critical aspect of skin aging: the degradation of key structural components, such as collagen and elastin fibers. Glycine and proline play crucial roles in collagen fiber composition, , while GABA helps reduce MMP-1 levels, thereby decreasing collagen degradation. Additionally, GABA promotes elastin fiber synthesis and improves skin hydration. , However, amino acids face a significant challenge in skin permeation due to their physicochemical properties. The permeability coefficients of amino acids typically range from 1 to 50 × 10–5 cm/h, indicating a very limited ability to penetrate the skin. This limited permeability is primarily attributed to the hydrophilic nature of amino acids and their low partitioning into the lipophilic stratum corneum, which serves as the main barrier to dermal absorption. Consequently, conventional topical formulations, such as aqueous solutions or simple emulsions, often fail to effectively deliver these hydrophilic molecules into deeper skin layers.

Nanoemulsions are thermodynamically unstable but kinetically stable colloidal systems composed of oil, water, and surfactants, with droplet sizes typically ranging from 20 to 200 nm. Their small droplet size and narrow distribution confer enhanced optical clarity, reduced gravitational separation, and increased interfacial surface area, which can improve the solubilization and delivery of active compounds. Consequently, nanoemulsions are widely used as delivery systems in cosmetic, pharmaceutical, and biomedical applications. Although nanoemulsions have been extensively investigated for dermal delivery, most studies have focused on low concentrations of active compounds or single-component systems. The formulation of nanoemulsions containing high concentrations of multiple hydrophilic actives remains challenging due to interfacial instability, osmotic effects, and potential interactions between solutes and surfactants. Therefore, systematic investigation of formulation and processing parameters is required to enable stable incorporation of high-load amino acid systems. Given the hydrophilic nature of amino acids, these compounds are primarily distributed within the aqueous phase and interfacial region rather than being encapsulated within the oil droplets.

The physicochemical properties of nanoemulsions, including droplet size, polydispersity index, and zeta potential, are strongly influenced by formulation and processing parameters such as surfactant type and concentration, oil phase composition, and emulsification method. In high-pressure homogenization (microfluidization), increasing pressure and the number of passes generally reduce droplet size; however, beyond approximately four cycles, further size reduction may be negligible. Higher surfactant concentrations enhance interfacial coverage, thereby reducing droplet aggregation and promoting smaller droplet sizes. , The incorporation of hydrophilic or ionic compounds, such as amino acids, can further influence interfacial behavior, osmotic balance, and electrostatic interactions, affecting droplet size distribution, zeta potential, and overall stability. Previous studies have demonstrated that nanoemulsion systems can enhance the dermal delivery of hydrophilic and bioactive compounds through improved interfacial interaction and increased effective surface area. − Supporting this, our previous work showed that a 10% w/w GABA-loaded nanoemulsion with similar composition enhanced skin permeation by approximately 2.9-fold compared to an aqueous solution in a Franz diffusion model. In addition, soybean oil, rich in linoleic and oleic acids, may further contribute to enhanced skin penetration. ,

In this study, the challenge of nanoemulsion systems lies in delivering a high concentration of an amino acid cocktail consisting of 5% w/w glycine, 5% w/w proline, and 10% w/w GABA into the skin, due to their differing molecular structures and physicochemical properties. Additionally, amino acids interact with the oxyethylene chain in the interfacial layer, resulting in changes in physical and chemical properties of nanoemulsions, such as larger droplet sizes. We hypothesize that optimizing surfactant concentration and homogenization pressure can facilitate stable incorporation of high-load amino acids while enhancing dermal bioactivity. Therefore, this study aims to systematically evaluate the effects of microfluidization process parameters and surfactant concentration on the physicochemical properties and stability of nanoemulsions containing a highly loaded amino acid cocktail of glycine, l-proline, and GABA.

Preparation of Amino Acid Cocktail Nanoemulsions, Modified from Reference

Amino acid cocktail nanoemulsions (AAC-NE) were formulated using soybean oil (10% w/w) as the oil phase and a combination of nonionic surfactants, polysorbate 80 (Tween 80) and sorbitan oleate (Span 80), at total surfactant concentrations of 5% or 7% w/w (Table ), corresponding to a hydrophilic–lipophilic balance (HLB) value of 8 based on preliminary formulation considerations. The oil phase also contained 0.02% w/w butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) as an antioxidant. The aqueous phase consisted of glycine (5% w/w), l-proline (5% w/w), and GABA (10% w/w) dissolved in ultrapure water, with phenoxyethanol (1% w/w) added as a preservative.

Characterization of Amino Acid Cocktail Nanoemulsions

After a 24 h equilibration period, all amino acid cocktail nanoemulsion formulations were evaluated for physical properties, including appearance, pH, droplet size, size distribution, and zeta potential, while chemical properties were analyzed by HPLC. Physical stability was subsequently assessed by monitoring changes in appearance, droplet size, size distribution, and zeta potential. During accelerated stability testing, physical parameters (appearance, pH, size distribution, and zeta potential) were monitored, and chemical stability was evaluated using HPLC.

Physical Characterization

The physical characteristics of the AAC-NE were evaluated in terms of appearance, droplet size, size distribution, zeta potential, and pH. All formulations were visually inspected for homogeneity and phase separation. Mean droplet size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were determined by dynamic light scattering (Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument, Malvern Instruments, Malvern WR14 1XZ, UK) following 100-fold dilution with ultrapure water to minimize multiple scattering effects. The pH of the nanoemulsions (approximately 10 mL) was measured at 25 ± 2 °C using a calibrated pH meter (SevenCompact pH/ion meter S220, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Chemical Characterization

To improve analytical reliability, amino acids were derivatized using two separate methods. Glycine and GABA were quantified using one derivatization approach, while a combined derivatization method was used for glycine and proline. After validating the glycine content, proline was estimated indirectly based on the combined signal.

GABA-HN and Glycine-HN (Gly-HN) Derivatives

Derivatization of total amino acids with 2-hydroxynaphthaldehyde (HN) was performed based on protocol modified from ref . The nanoemulsions were mixed with 0.6 mL of borate buffer (pH 8) and then 1 mL of 2-hydroxynaphthaldehyde solution (0.3% w/v in methanol). The mixture was heated in a water bath at 80 °C for 10 min. After that, the mixture was cooled and protected from light for 10 min and then adjusted to 5 mL with methanol before HPLC analysis.

Proline + Glycine-HN (Pro + Gly-HN) Derivatives

All formulations of AAC-NE were mixed with 0.6 mL of borate buffer pH 8 and 3 mL of HN solution (0.3% in methanol). The mixture was heated in a water bath at 50 °C for 40 min. After that, the mixture was cooled and protected from light for 10 min and then adjusted to 5 mL with methanol before HPLC analysis.

HPLC Determination of Amino Acid Content Modified from Reference

The HPLC equipped with a UV detector (Model LC-20AD, Shimadzu, Japan) was used for analysis. Separation was performed on ACE Excel 5 C18-AR (4.6 mm × 250 mm) (Advance Chromatography Technologies Ltd., UK) at 25 °C. The mobile phase consists of methanol and water (62:38, v/v). The mobile phase was filtered through 0.45 μm membrane filters and vacuum degassed before use. The injection volume was 5 μL and isocratically eluted at a flow rate of 0.95 mL/min. The UV detector was set at 330 nm for GABA-HN and Gly-HN derivatives and 280 nm for Gly-Pro-HN derivative. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. The HPLC method was validated for specificity, linearity, accuracy, precision, detection limit, and quantitation limit.

Determination of the Percentage of Active Content and Labeled Amount

All formulations of AAC-NE were subjected to a 2500-fold dilution for the analysis of GABA-HN and Gly-HN derivatives, and a 50-fold dilution for the analysis of Pro + Gly-HN derivatives, using purified water as the solvent. Subsequently, the total amino acids content was assessed through high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Stability Test of Amino Acid Cocktail Nanoemulsions

Stability studies of the AAC-NE were conducted with minor modifications from the method reported by ref .

Screening Physical Stability

Centrifugation

All nanoemulsion formulations were subjected to centrifugation at 3,500 rpm for 30 min using a microcentrifuge (Model MX-305, Tokyo, Japan). Samples were subsequently examined for evidence of phase separation.

Ostwald Ripening Rate

Nanoemulsion formulations were stored in sealed glass vials, protected from light, and maintained at ambient temperature. Physical appearance, droplet size, size distribution, and zeta potential were monitored weekly for 2 months. The Ostwald ripening rate (ω) was calculated from the slope of the linear relationship between the cube of the mean droplet radius (R3) and time, according to the following equation: ω=drN3dt 3 where r represents the droplet radius and t denotes time (hours).

Heating–Cooling Cycles (6 Cycles)

Nanoemulsion formulations were subjected to heating–cooling stress by alternating storage at 45 °C for 48 h and 4 °C for 48 h. This cycle was repeated six times. Physical appearance, droplet size, size distribution, and zeta potential were evaluated at the end of each cycle.

Accelerated Stability

Based on droplet size (130–200 nm), active content, labeled total amino acid content (Section ), and physical stability (Section ), selected nanoemulsion formulations were evaluated under both ambient and accelerated conditions.

Physical Stability

Selected formulations were stored in sealed glass vials, protected from light, and maintained at 25 ± 2 °C (ambient condition) and 40 °C (accelerated condition) for 12 weeks. Physical stability was evaluated at 0, 4, 8, and 12 weeks by monitoring appearance, pH, droplet size, size distribution, and zeta potential.

Chemical Stability

Chemical stability of the selected formulations stored under the same conditions was assessed over 12 weeks. The percentage of total amino acids remaining was quantified by HPLC at 0, 4, 8, and 12 weeks.

Cell-Based Studies

Cell-based experiments were conducted using samples prepared from a single batch. Each experiment was repeated in at least three independent runs under identical conditions, and measurements were performed in triplicate. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Cell Viability Assay

The selected nanoemulsion formulation was diluted with ultrapure water prior to bioactivity evaluation in NIH-3T3 fibroblast cells. Cell viability was assessed using an MTT assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 6.0 × 104 cells per well and treated with either the amino acid cocktail solution or the selected nanoemulsion at concentrations of 0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05, and 0.1% w/v. After 24 h of incubation, absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (SPECTROstar Nano, BMG LABTECH, Germany). Untreated cells served as the control. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR)

NIH-3T3 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 6.0 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h with the amino acid cocktail solution, the selected nanoemulsion formulation, or a blank nanoemulsion (vehicle control) at a final concentration of 0.01% w/v. The concentration of 0.01% w/v was selected as the highest concentration that maintained acceptable cell viability while enabling detection of biological responses. At higher concentrations, increased cytotoxicity was observed, which could confound the interpretation of gene expression results. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent following the manufacturer’s protocol, and RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, DE, USA). Subsequently, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase kit. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using a SYBR Green detection system (SensiFAST SYBR) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the reference gene, and relative gene expression levels were normalized accordingly.

Statistical Analysis

Unless otherwise stated, physicochemical characterization and stability studies were conducted using three independently prepared batches, with measurements performed in triplicate for each batch, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Cell-based experiments were conducted using samples prepared from a single batch and repeated in at least three independent runs, with measurements performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. A two-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to evaluate the effects of homogenization pressure and surfactant concentration on nanoemulsion properties. Repeated measures ANOVA was used to compare physical and chemical stability parameters over time, with sphericity assessed using Mauchly’s test. One-way ANOVA was used to evaluate gene expression differences among treatment groups. Post hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s test.

Results and Discussion

Preparation of Amino Acid Cocktail Nanoemulsions

The nanoemulsions were composed of soybean oil, polysorbate 80, and sorbitan monooleate at different concentrations, along with relatively high concentrations of amino acids, including gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA, 10% w/w), glycine (5% w/w), and l-proline (5% w/w). Coarse emulsions were produced using a high-speed homogenizer, followed by droplet size reduction through high-pressure homogenization at different pressures. After 24 h equilibration, all formulations (Figure ) exhibited an opaque white appearance. The nanoemulsions exhibited low viscosity and Newtonian flow behavior, consistent with their relatively low oil phase content. Therefore, additional rheological analysis was not expected to provide substantial differentiation among formulations.

At a surfactant concentration of 5% w/w, the formulations processed at pressures of 80 and 100 MPa produced droplets with sizes below 200 nm (157.0 ± 0.9 to 194.2 ± 2.0 nm), whereas increasing the pressure to 120 MPa resulted in larger droplets exceeding 200 nm (213.6 ± 2.2 nm). Droplet size uniformity and narrow size distribution are critical indicators of nanoemulsion quality and stability. In this study, both surfactant concentration and homogenization pressure significantly influenced droplet size. Increasing surfactant concentration from 5% to 7% w/w generally reduced droplet size, likely due to improved interfacial coverage and stabilization during droplet disruption. Adequate surfactant availability enables rapid adsorption at newly formed interfaces, thereby minimizing recoalescence during high-pressure homogenization. However, increasing homogenization pressure did not consistently lead to further size reduction. For formulations containing 5% w/w surfactant, higher pressure resulted in larger droplets, suggesting insufficient interfacial stabilization and increased recoalescence during processing. This behavior is consistent with the “over-processing” phenomenon, where excessive turbulent forces increase droplet collision frequency and promote coalescence when surfactant coverage is insufficient. , Importantly, these findings indicate that droplet size reduction during processing does not necessarily translate into improved long-term stability, which is also governed by interfacial stabilization and storage conditions.

The interaction between amino acids and nonionic surfactants also plays a significant role in interfacial behavior. Amino acids exist predominantly as zwitterions near their isoelectric points (GABA ≈ 7.3, glycine ≈ 5.96, and proline ≈ 6.30). − Nonionic surfactants such as polysorbate 80 may acquire a slight negative charge through hydrogen bonding with hydroxyl ions and adsorption of ionic species. ,, Electrostatic interactions between amino acids and the oxyethylene chains of the surfactant likely contributed to the observed negative zeta potential values and overall interfacial stabilization. Although increasing surfactant concentration slightly reduced the magnitude of the zeta potential, possibly due to compression of the electrical double layer at higher ionic strength, , all formulations maintained zeta potentials below – 30 mV, indicating adequate colloidal stability.

Although transmission electron microscopy (TEM) can provide visual confirmation of droplet morphology, dynamic light scattering (DLS) was considered sufficient for characterization of droplet size and distribution in this system. In addition, sample preparation for TEM may introduce structural artifacts in soft colloidal systems such as nanoemulsions.

All formulations exhibited comparable GABA and proline contents (Table ), with no significant influence of surfactant concentration or homogenization pressure. The corresponding labeled amounts remained within an acceptable range (Table ), indicating minimal loss during processing. In contrast, glycine content was significantly influenced by surfactant concentration. Formulations containing 7% w/w surfactant consistently showed higher glycine content and labeled amounts than those containing 5% w/w surfactant, suggesting improved retention at higher surfactant levels, possibly due to enhanced interfacial stabilization.

HPLC analysis indicated that surfactant concentration and homogenization pressure did not significantly affect GABA and proline contents, whereas glycine content was higher in formulations containing 7% w/w surfactant. This trend suggests that glycine retention may be more sensitive to interfacial stabilization than GABA and proline. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that increased surfactant concentration enhances interfacial area, thereby improving the retention of hydrophilic actives at the oil–water interface. The observed loss of amino acids during processing is also consistent with earlier studies attributing such effects to heat generated during high-pressure homogenization.

All AAC-NE remained physically stable after centrifugation at 3,500 rpm for 30 min, with no evidence of phase separation. Ostwald ripening behavior during 2-month storage at ambient temperature was evaluated by monitoring changes in the cube of the mean droplet radius (R3) as a function of time (Figure ), and the corresponding Ostwald ripening rates are summarized in Table . Nanoemulsions prepared with 7% w/w surfactant exhibited higher Ostwald ripening rates than those containing 5% w/w surfactant, particularly at lower homogenization pressure. Nevertheless, the absolute ripening rates for all formulations remained low, indicating good resistance to Ostwald ripening and consistent physical stability throughout the 2-month storage period.

Thermal stability of nanoemulsions was evaluated using heating–cooling cycles, and changes in droplet size were monitored as the primary indicator of physical stability (Figure ). All formulations containing 7% w/w surfactant exhibited a marked increase in droplet size after the first cycle, with progressive growth observed in subsequent cycles. In contrast, nanoemulsions containing 5% w/w surfactant demonstrated improved thermal stability. The formulation processed at 80 MPa showed an initial increase in droplet size after cycle 1, followed by stabilization, while the formulations processed at 100 and 120 MPa exhibited delayed or minimal changes throughout the cycles. Variations in size distribution and zeta potential followed similar trends (data not shown). Formulations were selected based on droplet size, resistance to Ostwald ripening, thermal stability, and amino acid retention, rather than a single performance parameter.

Z-average droplet size of AAC-NE during heating–cooling cycle stability testing.

Ostwald ripening analysis demonstrated that nanoemulsions containing 7% w/w surfactant exhibited higher ripening rates than those containing 5% w/w surfactant, particularly at lower homogenization pressures. This behavior may be explained by the irregular molecular packing of polysorbate 80 and sorbitan monooleate at the oil–water interface. Previous studies have shown that specific polysorbate 80-sorbitan monooleate ratios result in loosely packed interfacial films, increasing susceptibility to molecular diffusion and droplet growth. , The presence of amino acids at the interface may further amplify steric repulsion, leading to increased ripening rates. Heating–cooling cycle testing confirmed that formulations with lower Ostwald ripening rates exhibited superior thermal stability. Nanoemulsions containing 5% w/w surfactant showed greater resistance to droplet growth under thermal stress, supporting their selection for accelerated stability testing.

Accelerated Stability Study

Based on the physical stability screening results, all nanoemulsion formulations containing 5% w/w surfactant (NE5) were selected for accelerated stability evaluation. The formulations were stored at ambient temperature and 40 °C for 12 weeks, and their physical and chemical stability was monitored at 0, 4, 8, and 12 weeks.

Physical Stability

Physical stability was primarily assessed by monitoring changes in Z-average droplet size during storage, as droplet size is a sensitive indicator of nanoemulsion instability. As shown in Figure , storage duration significantly affected droplet size, with the extent of change depending on both homogenization pressure and storage temperature. At ambient temperature, nanoemulsions prepared at 80 MPa exhibited a significant increase in droplet size after 8 weeks, whereas formulations prepared at 100 and 120 MPa remained relatively stable throughout the 12-week storage period. At 40 °C, nanoemulsions prepared at 80 MPa showed an earlier and more pronounced increase in droplet size, while formulations prepared at 100 and 120 MPa exhibited delayed droplet growth, with significant changes observed after 8 and 12 weeks, respectively. These results indicate that homogenization pressure plays an important role in initial droplet size reduction and short-term stability. However, long-term stability cannot be attributed to pressure alone. The effectiveness of droplet stabilization is also governed by interfacial coverage, which is influenced by surfactant concentration and adsorption kinetics during processing. In this context, formulations with insufficient interfacial stabilization may undergo recoalescence despite high homogenization pressure, particularly under elevated temperature conditions. Therefore, the observed stability behavior reflects a balance between droplet disruption during processing and subsequent interfacial stabilization, rather than a monotonic improvement with increasing pressure. Changes in polydispersity index and zeta potential followed trends consistent with droplet size evolution (data not shown). The pH of all formulations showed a slight decrease after 4 weeks of storage but remained within a narrow range throughout the study period.

Chemical stability was evaluated by monitoring the percentage of remaining amino acids during storage. The percentage of GABA remaining, presented in Figure , showed that nanoemulsions prepared at 80 and 100 MPa exhibited a reduction in GABA content after 12 weeks of storage at ambient temperature, whereas the formulation prepared at 120 MPa remained stable. At 40 °C, all formulations demonstrated a significant decrease in GABA content after 12 weeks, with no significant differences observed between homogenization pressures. This temperature-dependent degradation behavior is consistent with previous findings reported for GABA-loaded nanoemulsions stored under accelerated conditions.

Overall, the accelerated stability results demonstrated that nanoemulsions prepared at higher homogenization pressures (100–120 MPa) exhibited superior physical and chemical stability during long-term storage, particularly under elevated temperature conditions.

Accelerated stability studies revealed that homogenization pressure played a critical role in long-term physical and chemical stability. Nanoemulsions processed at higher pressures (100–120 MPa) demonstrated improved resistance to droplet growth and amino acid degradation during storage, particularly under elevated temperature conditions. At 40 °C, formulations prepared at 80 MPa exhibited earlier and more pronounced instability, which may be attributed to their larger interfacial area facilitating heat transfer and accelerating degradation reactions. These findings indicate that while smaller droplets are often desirable for delivery purposes, excessive interfacial areas may compromise chemical stability, particularly for thermally sensitive hydrophilic actives such as amino acids. It should be noted that proline quantification was performed indirectly due to coelution with glycine, which may introduce some uncertainty in absolute values. Therefore, results should be interpreted primarily in terms of relative trends across formulations.

Cell-Based Analysis

Cell Viability

The cytocompatibility of the amino acid cocktail solution and the selected nanoemulsion formulation was evaluated in NIH-3T3 fibroblast cells using an MTT assay. Cells were treated with increasing concentrations (0.001–0.1% w/v) of either the amino acid cocktail solution or the nanoemulsion formulation. As shown in Figure , the amino acid cocktail solution maintained high cell viability across all tested concentrations. In contrast, the nanoemulsion formulation exhibited a concentration-dependent reduction in cell viability at concentrations ≥ 0.01% w/v, while cell viability remained above acceptable levels at lower concentrations. These results indicate that the selected nanoemulsion formulation is cytocompatible at concentrations suitable for subsequent bioactivity evaluation.

Gene Expression Analysis

The effects of the amino acid cocktail solution and the selected nanoemulsion formulation on extracellular matrix-related gene expression in NIH-3T3 fibroblast cells were assessed by quantitative real-time PCR. As presented in Figure , treatment with the amino acid cocktail nanoemulsion significantly upregulated the expression of collagen-related genes (Col1a2 and Col3a1) and elastin compared with both the untreated control and the amino acid cocktail solution. In addition, the nanoemulsion formulation moderately increased the expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-1a, MMP-3, and MMP-9) relative to the control and cocktail solution groups. The amino acid solution was used as a control under identical conditions, and the nanoemulsion formulation showed enhanced gene expression compared to the solution, indicating a potential contribution of the delivery system. In addition, the blank nanoemulsion was evaluated to assess the effect of the formulation components, suggesting that the observed biological response was not solely attributed to the vehicle. Although a reduction in cell viability was observed at this concentration (0.01% w/v), the level of viability remained sufficient to support gene expression analysis. Therefore, the observed biological responses are interpreted with consideration of potential cytotoxic effects.

Cell-based studies demonstrated that the amino acid cocktail nanoemulsion significantly enhanced the expression of extracellular matrix–related genes, including Col1a2, Col3a1, and elastin, compared with both the amino acid cocktail solution and untreated controls under identical experimental conditions. These findings suggest improved cellular availability of the active components. The observed effect may be associated with enhanced interaction of the nanoemulsion system with cellular membranes, as surfactants are known to influence membrane permeability. Glycine and proline are major structural components of collagen fibers, and their role in promoting collagen synthesis has been well documented. − , In addition, GABA has been reported to stimulate elastin fiber formation by upregulating tropoelastin and related elastic fiber components, which is consistent with the increased elastin expression observed in this study. The modest upregulation of MMP-1a, MMP-3, and MMP-9 may indicate involvement in extracellular matrix remodeling processes; however, increased MMP expression can also be associated with matrix degradation. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Overall, the results suggest an enhanced biological response at the gene expression level; however, such changes do not necessarily translate into corresponding protein expression or functional outcomes. Further studies involving protein-level analysis (e.g., ELISA or Western blot) are required to confirm the functional relevance of these observations.

The enhanced biological response observed for the nanoemulsion system may be explained by several potential mechanisms. The nanoscale droplet size provides a high interfacial surface area, which can facilitate closer interaction between the formulation and the cell membrane. In addition, nonionic surfactants such as polysorbate 80 are known to interact with lipid bilayers and may transiently increase membrane fluidity, thereby promoting the transport of hydrophilic molecules into cells. Furthermore, the nanoemulsion system may help maintain amino acids in a readily available form at the cellular interface, enhancing local concentration gradients and facilitating uptake [38]. These mechanisms are proposed based on established principles of nanoemulsion systems and membrane interaction and were not directly investigated in the present study. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports describing enhanced cellular uptake of hydrophilic compounds in nanoemulsion-based systems. , However, direct extrapolation to the present formulations should be made with caution. Importantly, the present study does not include direct evaluation of skin permeation or deposition. Therefore, the observed biological effects should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of enhanced cellular availability rather than confirmation of dermal delivery performance. Moreover, given the inherent variability of cell-based assays, repeated independent runs were conducted to improve the robustness of the observed trends.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that a high-load amino acid cocktail comprising GABA, glycine, and proline can be incorporated into an oil-in-water nanoemulsion system with acceptable physicochemical stability and preliminary biological activity. Surfactant concentration and homogenization pressure were identified as key parameters influencing droplet size, interfacial behavior, and stability. The selected formulation exhibited favorable stability and enhanced gene expression responses compared to the amino acid solution. However, these findings are based on in vitro evaluation and should be interpreted as preliminary. Further studies, including protein-level analysis and skin permeation assessments, are required to confirm functional efficacy and dermal delivery performance. Overall, the results highlight the importance of balancing processing conditions and interfacial stabilization in the design of nanoemulsion-based delivery systems for hydrophilic bioactive compounds.
