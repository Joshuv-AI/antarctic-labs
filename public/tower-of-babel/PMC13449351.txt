Hardware in context

The use of temperature control devices finds many applications in physical, analytical and research laboratories. In this paper, we will focus on their application in the field of electroanalytical and spectroscopic measurements of liquid samples. These measurements can be carried out in macroscopic or microfluidic systems, where temperature control is essential for ensuring measurement quality. This regulation can be achieved by maintaining a stable temperature during direct measurements, or by applying well-controlled temperature gradients to the sample.

Liquid samples, either aqueous or organic, typically require temperature control in a range of −10° to 80°C. Thus, the most common temperature control devices for this purpose are circulation thermostats. In these systems, the liquid sample is placed in a jacketed glass or plastic cell, in which the thermostat pump circulates a heat transfer liquid; usually water or a cryogenic fluid.

These devices are usually bulky due to the large excess of heat transfer liquid required over the sample volume, so it could take a significant amount of time to reach the desired temperature. A large variety of models are commercially available, ranging from a few liters to industrial-scale machines.

For very small sample volumes or systems where a faster response is needed, thermoelectric or Peltier devices are commonly used instead of circulation thermostats. These devices are based on the generation or absorption of heat when an electrical current is passed through a junction of dissimilar conductors or semiconductors (the Peltier effect) [1].

Commercially, there are several small-volume circulation thermostats that use Peltier devices to control the temperature of the heat transfer liquid, such as the Lauda LOOP series [2], Kruss PT31 [3], Huber Cold Heat Circulator Piccolo 280 [4], and in microfluidic applications, macroscopic Peltier devices are commonly used to control the overall temperature of the system [5], like the commercially available Fluigent microfluidic temperature regulation system [6].

Micro-Peltier devices have recently been proposed for local temperature control within microfluidic systems [7].

For spectroscopy analyses, thermoelectric devices are used to control the temperature of the spectroscopic cell and to carry out studies of temperature gradients. Typically, the device encapsulates the cell, leaving two openings for the entry and exit of light or laser beams [8]. Several companies like Shimadzu [9] or the specialist Quantum Northwest [10] offer several options for single or multiple thermoelectric cell holders.

For electroanalytical measurements, there are no commercially available thermoelectric devices for electrochemical cells, very few examples of their use can be found in the literature [11]. One possible explanation for this lack of adapted thermal control system is that bulk electrochemical cells require stirring to homogenize the sample after the addition of reagents. This difficulty also applies to titration and quantification protocols. (e.g. calibration or standard addition methods).

Coupling a thermoelectric device with agitation is difficult because commercial thermoelectric devices are square or tubular and therefore not suitable for all cell shapes. Therefore, we propose an instrument comprising a thermoelectric device coupled with a 3D-printed copper-doped PLA thermal guide shaped to fit our custom sized cell (e.g. electrochemical or titration cells), coupled with a magnetic stirring device in the bottom of the cell, providing both agitation and temperature control.

Hardware description

The thermoelectric temperature control and stirring system is designed for experiments that require a compact system for the simultaneous agitation and thermoregulation of a liquid sample. Typical examples include pH monitoring or titration, and other electroanalytical measurements carried out in an electrochemical cell. This device has two main original features: first, the combination of thermoregulation and agitation in the same instrument; and second, the ability to manufacture the thermal guide that interfaces the solution and the thermoelectric module in any required shape by 3D printing it with copper-doped PLA filament (Table 1).

A simple search for commercially available thermoelectric components shows two types of thermal interface: The first involves liquid circulation (Fig. 1), which we want to avoid as it makes our instrument bulkier and requires a jacketed cell, significantly decreasing the efficacy of the magnetic stirring. Furthermore, such devices are already commercially available. The second type is simply a ceramic square flat surface element that interfaces directly with the bottom of our cell (Fig. 2).

The latter is designed for use under a flat-bottomed container, but this is not possible in our case because the magnetic stirrer is positioned there. However, the flat, square device is not compatible with lateral cell positioning since the cell shapes are either cylindrical or conical. To solve this problem, we designed a 3D-printed component to facilitate thermal exchange between the thermoelectric module and the cell. This component features a flat interface for the thermoelectric module and a conical interface to accommodate the cell (Fig. 3) (Table 3).

The material used is an important factor for this function, as a high heat transfer capacity is required, which is uncommon in plastic materials used in 3D printing, such as PLA, PETG or ABS. The variety of materials available on the market continues to grow, and we have identified filaments containing metal powders that can be used on consumer 3D printers. We have therefore selected a copper-doped filament that offers sufficient thermal conductivity for the interface piece (Fig. 4).

Distribution of functions of the agitation and temperature control system.

Our integrated instrument has a three-level design, with magnetic stirring in the lower section, a cell holder and a thermoelectric device in the middle section, and a cell cover with an opening to allow different covers, probes, and liquid dispensers to be adapted. The small size of this system makes it easy to replicate for laboratory use and for conducting parallel experiments.

The system, which will be described in detail in the following section, is a combination of two devices: a thermostatic module and an agitator module. These are managed by a central control unit.

Thermostat description

An important feature of this device is that there should be no empty space between the cell and the thermal guide of the thermoelectric module. The cell support comprises two halves (Fig. 5a), one of which is our 3D-printed thermal guide that interfaces the thermoelectric module with the cell. The other half is non-heat conducting and serves to securely hold the cell in place. This casing isolates the cell from the external environment. However, to improve heat insulation, we have added a space inside the casing that is filled with insulating foam sticks. The current design can hold a cell with a volume of around 100 ml, which is suitable for potentiometric titrations (Fig. 5b).

Thermostat module, a) CAD assembly of the two printed parts., b) Complete assembly.

Stirrer description

The stirrer module is simple in design and operates by rotating a small magnetic bar within the cell. To achieve this, a fan with two magnets is positioned on top. This set forms the basis of our system, but it can also be used as a standalone stirrer with the provided cover. Our conceptual approach is to use each function separately (Fig. 6).

Complete CAD model of the stirrer.

Control box description

The Arduino Uno board is connected to a terminal application that manages both the stirrer module and the thermostat (Fig. 7). The electronic control system generates pulse width modulation (PWM) signals via a MOSFET transistor to drive the stirrer and via an H-bridge to drive the Peltier element. Thermal regulation is ensured by a proportional–integral–derivative (PID) algorithm, with the setpoint temperature specified by the user. The system uses two PT1000 probes to measure the sample and room temperatures. These probes provide the necessary information for the algorithm to reach the setpoint temperature, either by heating or cooling, depending on whether the room temperature is higher or lower than the setpoint temperature. The two PT1000 probes are connected to the Arduino board via MAX31865 chips that communicate using a Serial Peripheral Interface (SPI) protocol.

3D printed parts

The 3D-printed components are designed using Autodesk Fusion 360. These files are available in both CAD and STL formats. All 3D parts can be created using consumer printers. We printed our parts using a Creality K1 Max with a 0.4 mm brass nozzle and tough polylactic acid (PLA) filament. The thermal guide was an exception, as we used copper-doped filament on an Artillery X1 3D printer with a 0.4 mm steel nozzle to print it. As this print requires a solid part, the infill was set to 100%. The rest of the elements can be printed with the standard settings (three wall lines and an infill set to 20%). We recommend using printing supports for overhanging parts.

The stirrer module necessitates two main parts (Fig. 8a and 8b) and four spacers (Fig. 8d). The lid (Fig. 8c) is only necessary if you wish to use the stirrer alone or perform the procedure described in Section 5.1 (Fig. 19).

Software

The Arduino code drives the entire device, including the stirrer and the thermoregulator part. Control is performed using commands which are described in the following section. At this stage, the user can start the regulation process by entering the command “REGUL [space] [Value] [CR] [LF]”. The software displays the log with the following fields: <Time>, <PID Temperature>, <Room Temperature>, <PWM>, <+/- Sign (Heating/Cooling) > . The “REGUL [space] [0] [CR] [LF]” command stops the regulation (Fig. 53).

Example of terminal control.

The algorithm uses two PID controllers: one for cooling the sample with the Peltier element and a second for heating it. The available functions allow you to set all PID parameters. An initial setting is saved, enabling you to adjust the controller parameters to better match the inertia of your system. The 'SETPOINT' command is the main function of the code. This is based on a state machine that manages the transition between the two control modes of the Peltier device. (Supplementary file: StateChart.ppt).

The software includes a parsing function that enables basic functions to be interpreted for our system. The accepted format is standard: Function [space] Argument 1 [space] Argument 2 [LF]. Arguments can be integers or floating-point numbers. The available functions are described in the following table. (Section 6.2 software instructions).

Validation and characterization

Characterization is based on typical use of the device with two standard operational tests, which are designed to determine the time required to reach the setpoint. A third test demonstrates the stability of the thermostatic stirrer over an extended operating period of around 16 h. The last test establishes the maximum achievable temperature difference in this configuration.

The tests below illustrate two scenarios in which the sample must either be heated (Fig. 54,a) to reach the setpoint temperature or cooled (Fig. 54,b). The box around the setpoint ‘±0.2°C’ shows the time taken for the system to settle at the setpoint value.

Test conducted at a control temperature with 70 ml water sample, a) 21°C, b) 16°C.

The operation of the algorithm is illustrated by the PWM waveform (Supplementary File: Data.xls/ Graph_1) during the Warm-up phase for the test at 21°C. The temperature rise phase, during which the PWM is at its maximum, is followed by a damping phase consisting of intermittent cooling with a pulse width modulation (PWM) value of 50. Regulation begins when the sample temperature returns to a level close to the setpoint temperature. The second diagram (Supplementary file: Data.xls/ Graph_1), conducted at 16°C, demonstrates a scenario in which the damping phase is unnecessary. In this case, the system switched directly from ‘cooling’ mode to ‘temperature control’ mode.

A long-term test conducted overnight at a set temperature of 20°C demonstrates the system's stability (Fig. 55). While the room temperature decreases during the night, the temperature of our sample remains stable.

Test conducted overnight at a control temperature of 20°C using an 80 ml water sample.

The temperature varies from 19.95°C to 20.06°C. This illustrates the accuracy limit of the MAX31865 digital temperature converter. Its 15-bit resolution provides an accuracy of around 0.03°C [12]; so, the temperature values span five converter values. The temperature is after all regulated to ± 0.06°C, with a standard deviation of 0.017 and an average of 19.99°C.

The maximum cooling capacity was also tested by setting an unachievable set point of 1°C. We recorded a maximum temperature difference of 8°C between the room and the sample (Fig. 56). We therefore recommend to not use this device when the desired temperature difference exceeds +/- 7°C as compared to the ambient temperature.

Maximum Cooling test, using a 80 ml water sample.

Conclusion

The proposed 3D-printed thermoregulator and magnetic stirrer device for analytical or electrochemical measurements in 10 to 100 ml electrochemical cells is an innovative alternative to the actual experimental set-up using thermostats/cryostats in jacketed cells and independent magnetic stirrers. As cell shapes are increasingly custom-designed, manufacturing a 3D printed thermal guide permits the device adaptation to different geometries. Thanks to the versatility of the 3D-printed thermal guide, this instrument can be adapted for other uses, such as spectroscopy or controlling chemical reactors. Additionally, the instrument's small size will also allow it to be used in field experiments as soon as it is fitted with an autonomous power source.

The test results show that, for a sample volume of 80 ml, our instrument can reach the setpoint temperature within 60 min, depending on the difference between the sample and setpoint temperatures. It can then keep the sample temperature stable within an interval of ± 0.03°C for several hours (overnight), while simultaneously stirring the sample solution at the desired speed.

The efficiency of our system depends on the heat transfer capacity of the guide. We use a standard copper-doped PLA filament here, which is easy to produce and widely available thanks to the popularity of consumer 3D printers. This design method eliminates the need for jacketed cells to circulate fluid and machined metal thermal guides. The emergence of new polymeric/metallic filaments will likely improve heat transfer capacity in the near future, thus reducing the time taken to reach the setpoint temperature.

An alternative would be to increase the power of our Peltier device from 1 A to 2 A; however, we prefer to avoid this due to cost issues and the significant increase in power consumption, as this would make it difficult to find a suitable autonomous power source.

The software can also be improved by using a PID algorithm capable of handling current reversal in the H-bridge. This will then improve tracking of a setpoint close to ambient temperature.

Our longer-term objective is to add autonomous data logging capabilities into this instrument and to integrate it into an electrochemical measuring or titration station. This station will comprise potentiostatic, pH and/or ion-selective electrodes (ISE) and voltammetric measurements (trace metals), as well as a liquid handling system with pumps to add and clean the sample from the cell, and burettes to carry out titration experiments. The goal is to offer a complete, automated system for electroanalytical measurements for environmental research applications.
