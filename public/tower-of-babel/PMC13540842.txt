Influence of a simple increased adductor angle on gross motor development in infants
Authors: Yu-Qi He, Xiao-Mei Liu, Yi-Mei Fan, Meng-Yan Tang, Pian-Pian Fan, Wen-Tao Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the influence of a simple increased adductor angle on gross motor development in infants, providing a theoretical basis for early identification and intervention.MethodsA total of 1,132 infants aged 18 months or younger, born between July 1, 2020, and July 31, 2023, were enrolled. Participants were registered and prospectively followed up at the child healthcare department of a tertiary hospital. Basic demographic data and developmental milestone ages were collected from the hospital’s child healthcare information system. The adductor angle was assessed using the Infant Neurological Motor Assessment 20-Item Scale (INMA20) at corrected ages ranging from 28 days to 2 months for preterm infants and chronological ages within the same range for full-term infants. The cut-off ages for gross motor developmental milestones were established based on the Alberta Infant Motor Scale (AIMS) criteria.ResultsChi-square analysis revealed a statistically significant difference in the incidence of a simple increased adductor angle between preterm and full-term infants (P < 0.05). A logistic regression model with Firth correction indicated that a simple increased adductor angle had no significant effect on gross motor development outcomes in infants. However, the wide confidence intervals suggest that small effects cannot be completely excluded.ConclusionPreterm infants demonstrate a higher incidence of a simple increased adductor angle compared to full-term infants. However, this condition appears to have minimal impact on gross motor development in both preterm and full-term infants. Further research is warranted to establish a more accurate and developmentally appropriate normal range for the adductor angle during infancy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07388-z.

Introduction

INMA20 was developed by Professor Bao Xiulan of Peking Union Medical College Hospital, based on the simplified version of the French Amiel-Tison 0–1 Year Motor 52 Test [1]. This assessment systematically evaluates neuromotor development in infants and can detect mild brain dysfunction associated with abnormal motor development [2]. Amiel-Tison introduced the Amiel-Tison Angle (ATA) to assess the adductor muscle group of the legs, and this method has been demonstrated to possess high reliability [3, 4]. In the simplified INMA20 for infants aged 0–1 year, the adductor angle test is performed in the supine position. The examiner gently abducts the infant’s legs to their maximum extent, and the angle formed between the thighs is measured. Abnormal adductor angle manifests as a reduced angle (indicating increased muscle tone) or an enlarged angle (indicating decreased muscle tone).However, Preterm infants exhibit physiological hypotonia due to immature central nervous system development. Da Silva ES et al. [5, 6] reported that even among healthy preterm infants, all muscle tone scores were lower than in term infants at the initial assessment. Such abnormal angles can directly interfere with the developmental process of lower limb gross motor skills in infants and affect the final developmental outcome by altering hip joint range of motion, lower limb muscle strength balance, and limb control ability.

A simple increased adductor angle refers to an isolated increased adductor angle with no other abnormal neuromotor findings on the INMA20 examination. Clinically, when assessing infants aged 1 month using the INMA20 and the current adductor angle criteria (normal range: 40°-80° at 1 month) [1], a noticeable number of cases present with a simple increased adductor angle, often causing concern among clinicians and parents. However, no existing literature clarifies whether such findings require special intervention or whether they influence future motor development. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the impact of a simple increased adductor angle on gross motor development in infants, providing a theoretical foundation for early detection and intervention.

Materials and methods

Materials

Study Population A convenience sampling method was used to enroll eligible children in the study. Infants born between July 1, 2020, and July 31, 2023, who were registered and continuously followed in the child healthcare department of a tertiary hospital, were included. Participants were ≤ 18 months old at the time of data collection. Those with gestational age < 37 weeks were assigned to the preterm group, and those ≥ 37 weeks were assigned to the full-term group. The inclusion criteria included the following: Corrected age of 28 days for preterm infants; Chronological age of 28 days for full-term infants; All infants had complete data on gross motor developmental milestones; Parents who voluntarily participated in this study and signed the informed consent form. The exclusion criteria included the following: NICU admission; Infants with other combined abnormalities; Moderate to severe asphyxia; Severe hyperbilirubinemia; Ischemic-hypoxic encephalopathy or other brain injuries; Congenital endocrine or inherited metabolic disorders; Congenital heart disease; Infants with definite spasticity, cerebral palsy, or other neurological disorders. The criteria for elimination from the study included participant withdrawal from the study, which accounted for up to 20% of the missing data values.

Data collection

Evaluation of the adductor angle

Infants underwent the INMA20 evaluation between the corrected ages of 28 days and 2 months for preterm infants and between 28 days and 2 months for full-term infants. Evaluations were conducted by professionals with at least 3 years of pediatric healthcare experience and evaluation qualifications. Abnormal findings were verified by another evaluator with similar qualifications. Given that adductor angle measurement was examiner-dependent, standardized training and unified operating procedures were conducted for all examiners prior to data collection to ensure consistency. Infants were placed in the supine position for adductor angle assessment. During quiet alertness and passive cooperation, the angle was measured using a standard goniometer. The INMA20 comprises five dimensions: ①Audiovisual responses: red ball tracking, facial tracking, auditory response; ②Muscle tone: continuous fist clenching, sitting posture and head control, prone position head lift and hand support, scarf sign, adductor angle, popliteal angle, foot dorsiflexion angle; ③Sitting and motor skills: independent sitting, voluntary hand grasping, rolling over, active crawling; ④Reflexes: asymmetric tonic neck reflex, knee reflex, lateral support response, parachute response;⑤Posture: traction response, prone traction response [7]. All results were recorded in tabular form as “normal” or “abnormal”. According to INMA20 standards for infants aged 1 month, the normal adductor angle range was 40°-80°.

Gross motor development evaluation

Basic demographic information and milestone developmental ages were collected through the hospital’s child healthcare information system. Gross motor development (rolling over, sitting, crawling, and walking) was evaluated using the AIMS, a highly valid assessment tool introduced to China in 2009 [8–10]. Previous studies indicated that the AIMS demonstrates high consistency with the Gesell Developmental Scale and the Peabody Gross Motor Development Scale, showing sensitivity in identifying motor abnormalities in preterm infants compared to normal infants [11–13].

The cut-off ages for gross motor developmental milestones were established based on the AIMS criteria:①Rolling over: Transitioning from the prone position to the supine position without trunk rotation (90% achieved by 8 months);②Sitting independently: Ability to sit without support (90% achieved by 8 months); ③Crawling: Alternating abdominal crawling in prone position (90% achieved by 9 months);④Independent walking: Ability to walk without assistance (90% achieved by 14 months).In this study, the AIMS was not administered to the infants, only the cut-off ages for gross motor developmental milestones were established based on the AIMS criteria.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.11. Count data were presented as frequency (percentage), and measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). Differences in INMA20 results between groups were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, with a two-sided significance level set at α = 0.05. A logistic regression model with Firth correction was constructed to assess the association between the adductor angle and gross motor development outcomes. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

General characteristics

A total of 1,132 infants were included, comprising 793 (70.1%) full-term infants and 339 (29.9%) preterm infants. Among the full-term infants, 699 (88.1%) had a normal result of the INMA20, and 94 (11.9%) had a simple increased adductor angle. Among preterm infants, 267 (78.8%) had a normal result of the INMA20, and 72 (21.2%) had a simple increased adductor angle (Figure 1). The difference in the incidence of a simple increased adductor angle between full-term and preterm infants was statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Table 1).

However, the wide confidence intervals suggest that small effects cannot be completely excluded (Table 3).

Discussion

Higher incidence of a simple increased adductor angle in preterm infants

This study demonstrated that the incidence of a simple increased adductor angle was higher in preterm infants compared to full-term infants, indicating relatively reduced lower limb muscle tone in preterm infants during the early postnatal period. The altered regulation of muscle tone in preterm infants may result from premature separation from the maternal environment, which could interfere with the normal development and maturation of the neuromuscular system. Previous studies have reported an increased Amiel-Tison adductor angle in preterm infants due to hypotonia associated with immaturity, a finding consistent with the elevated incidence of a simple increased adductor angle observed in this study. Furthermore, this study revealed a statistically significant association between birth weight and gross motor development outcomes, corroborating the findings of Arockia Pramila C et al. [14]. Their research indicated a significant correlation between infant muscle tone and birth weight, with lower birth weight among preterm infants being negatively associated with muscle tone. Notably, although preterm infants exhibited a higher prevalence of an isolated increased adductor angle, this condition did not significantly impair subsequent gross motor development. This observation was supported by Pedersen SJ et al., whose research on early motor development in preterm infants suggested that assessments of muscle tone and movement at 7 months of age lack sufficient specificity for diagnosing cerebral palsy, as such tone abnormalities were typically transient [6]. Additionally, this study found that the proportion of abnormal crawling and walking patterns remained relatively high in full-term infants at later stages, which may be attributable to the use of corrected age for preterm infants when defining motor milestones and the preterm infants included in this study were free of neurological injury and related diseases, representing relatively healthy preterm infants. Although an association was observed in our study, the direction and magnitude of the effect should be interpreted with caution.

For preterm infants with multiple high-risk factors, numerous studies have demonstrated that hypotonia could adversely affect long-term motor outcomes. Vungarala P et al. reported that at a corrected age of 8 months, reduced muscle tone in preterm infants was associated with impaired gross motor development [15]. Furthermore, Forslund M et al. observed, through a four-year longitudinal follow-up, that at 4 years of age, the degree of hip abduction in preterm infants remained significantly greater than that in full-term infants [16]. Additionally, several studies have shown that even preterm infants who appear neurodevelopmentally normal during preschool years may exhibit poorer performance in gross motor function and other functional domains relative to their full-term peers [17]. Research by Majewska J et al. further revealed that preterm children without diagnosed disabilities still demonstrated inferior gait quality, muscle tone, coordination, and rapid alternating movement abilities at ages six to seven [18]. Therefore, it is recommended that preterm infants undergo early assessment of lower limb muscle tone to facilitate timely identification of impairments and enable prompt intervention, thereby minimizing potential long-term impacts on gross motor development.

Effects of a simple increased adductor angle on gross motor development in infants

Gross motor development serves as a key indicator of infants’ neurological development, encompassing the acquisition of motor milestones such as head control, rolling over, sitting, crawling, standing, and walking. This study found that a simple increased adductor angle in the early postnatal period was not associated with a statistically significant difference in gross motor outcomes during infancy. These findings suggest that healthcare professionals should provide clear explanations to parents during clinical consultations to help alleviate unnecessary concerns. However, the wide confidence intervals suggest that small effects cannot be completely excluded. Furthermore, the currently recommended normal range for the adductor angle in 1-month-old infants according to INMA20 (40°–80°) may be overly narrow and warrants further investigation. Chaudhari S et al. previously examined changes in passive muscle tone during the first year of life and reported adductor angles among full-term infants as follows: 70°–94° at 3 months, 90°–112° at 6 months, 101°–125° at 9 months, and 118°–142° at 12 months [19]. Notably, the adductor angle observed at 3 months exceeded the INMA20 reference range for infants aged 1–3 months (40°–80°) by 14°–30°. In contrast, the Hammersmith Infant Neurological Examination (HINE) scale assigns the highest score of 3 for an adductor angle between 80° and 150°, regardless of age, while a score of 0 is given for angles below 80° [20], which differs substantially from the INMA20 criteria. Therefore, further research is needed to establish a more accurate and developmentally appropriate reference range for the adductor angle in full-term infants aged 1–3 months.

In conclusion, preterm infants indicate a higher incidence of a simple increased adductor angle compared to full-term infants. However, this condition appears to have minimal impact on gross motor development in both preterm and full-term infants. However, the wide confidence intervals suggest that small effects cannot be completely excluded. Further research is warranted to establish a more accurate and developmentally appropriate normal range for the adductor angle during infancy. The present study has several limitations. First, no formal sample size calculation was performed prior to study initiation, and the sample size of infants with increased adductor angle was relatively small. Second, We failed to systematically collect detailed data on perinatal complications and family socioeconomic factors during enrollment, which could introduce unmeasured residual confounding.Furthermore, the assessment window was narrow, limited to 1 month of age for full-term infants and corrected 1 month of age for preterm infants. In addition, no intra/inter-rater reliability analysis for adductor angle measurement may introduce measurement bias, sparse data with wide confidence intervals, residual confounding, limited intervention information, and potential model instability also represent limitations of this study.Future research should enroll larger sample sizes, prolong follow-up durations, and further refine the aforementioned influencing factors to more accurately leftacterize developmental trajectories and enhance the generalizability of study findings.
