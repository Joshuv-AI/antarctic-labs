Pan-cancer and single-cell analysis identifies MAGI2-AS3 as an immune regulator and prognostic biomarker with a focus on colorectal cancer
Authors: Fatemeh Maghool, Maedeh Mirzamohammadi, Seyed Ali Taheri Hatkehlouei, Samane Mohammadzadeh, Aida Heidari, Mohammad Hassan Emami, Pouria Samadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are critical regulators of cancer progression, immune dynamics, and therapeutic response. MAGI2-AS3 has been implicated in multiple malignancies; however, its pan-cancer relevance and specific role in colorectal cancer (CRC) remain incompletely understood.MethodsMAGI2-AS3 expression was analyzed across cancers using bulk (TCGA, GTEx) and single-cell RNA-seq data. Functional enrichment, survival (Kaplan–Meier, Cox regression), diagnostic (ROC), immune infiltration (8 algorithms), immunotherapy response, drug sensitivity, and genomic/epigenetic analyses were performed. Expression was validated by RT-qPCR in 10 paired CRC tissues.ResultsMAGI2-AS3 expression exhibited marked heterogeneity across cancer types, with consistent downregulation in CRC and preferential enrichment in stromal and myeloid cell populations. Functional analyses linked MAGI2-AS3 to epithelial–mesenchymal transition, immune regulation, and cytokine signaling. While prognostic associations were context dependent across cancers, CRC demonstrated a distinct pattern: MAGI2-AS3 was consistently downregulated and showed robust diagnostic performance. Paradoxically, higher expression within tumors was associated with poorer survival outcomes and an inflamed yet immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment characterized by reduced response to immune checkpoint blockade, indicating a complex, compartment-specific role. Drug sensitivity and epigenetic analyses further underscored cancer-type–specific regulatory patterns, which were experimentally validated by RT-qPCR in CRC tissues.ConclusionMAGI2-AS3 functions as a context-dependent regulator with strong diagnostic relevance and complex prognostic implications in colorectal cancer. Its involvement in immune modulation, therapeutic response, and tumor progression highlights its potential as a biomarker for precision oncology, warranting further mechanistic and clinical investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12672-026-05447-6.Keywords: MAGI2-AS3, Immunotherapy, Immune infiltration, Prognosis, Pan-cancer

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of global mortality, with over 20 million new cases annually, posing profound challenges to public health and quality of life. Despite advances in therapy, a definitive cure remains elusive, underscoring the need to deepen our understanding of oncogenic mechanisms and identify novel therapeutic targets [1]. The advent of large-scale multi-omics resources, such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [2], the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project [3], and the single-cell Tabula Sapiens atlas [4], has enabled systematic pan-cancer analyses that uncover both shared and tumor-specific molecular features linked to clinical outcomes.

Among the non-coding genome, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides with limited protein-coding potential, have emerged as critical regulators of tumorigenesis [5–7]. While microRNAs have been extensively studied [8–10], lncRNAs remain relatively underexplored despite their ability to modulate gene expression through interactions with DNA, RNA, and proteins in both nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments [6, 11, 12].

MAGI2 Antisense RNA 3 (MAGI2-AS3), located at chromosome 7q21.11, is one such lncRNA increasingly implicated in cancer biology [13]. It is dysregulated across multiple solid tumors, including bladder [14], esophageal [15], prostate [16], and gastric cancers [17], and its expression correlates with key clinical features such as stage, metastasis, and survival. Functionally, MAGI2-AS3 influences diverse pathways, from epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) to immune modulation, positioning it as a promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarker [18–21].

Colorectal cancer (CRC; colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) and rectum adenocarcinoma (READ)) serves as an ideal model to investigate MAGI2-AS3, as it consistently exhibits downregulation of this lncRNA, aligning with its putative tumor-suppressive role, and demonstrates strong associations with immune infiltration and checkpoint regulation in pan-cancer analyses. Coupled with access to patient-derived CRC tissues, this context allows us to integrate bioinformatic discovery with experimental validation, bridging molecular insight and clinical relevance.

In this study, we comprehensively characterized the expression patterns, molecular associations, and clinical relevance of MAGI2-AS3 across multiple malignancies, with a specific focus on colorectal cancer (CRC). Guided by this objective, we formulated a two-part hypothesis. First, MAGI2-AS3 exhibits cancer-type–specific expression profiles and distinct prognostic associations across different tumor types. Second, in CRC, its dysregulation specifically modulates tumor–immune interactions and influences therapeutic response. To test this framework, we integrated bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing with prognostic, diagnostic, and immune infiltration analyses, alongside genomic, epigenetic, and drug sensitivity profiling. These computational findings were validated experimentally using RT-qPCR in 10 paired CRC tissues. Together, this multi-modal approach defines the context-dependent role of MAGI2-AS3 as a candidate biomarker in cancer. An overview of the study design is presented in Fig. 1.

Materials and methods

Study design and data acquisition

To mitigate platform- and cohort-specific technical variation between TCGA and GTEx, batch effects were adjusted using the ComBat algorithm (empirical Bayes framework) from the sva R package, with cancer type and sample type (tumor/normal) preserved as biological covariates during adjustment (Supplementary Table S2). Differentially expressed genes and lncRNAs (DEGs/DE-lncRNAs) were identified through RNA-sequencing data analysis using the R package edgeR, applying the thresholds of |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1, FDR < 0.05, and P < 0.05. FDR control was managed using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. For correlation analyses with MAGI2-AS3, genes were considered significantly correlated if they exhibited an absolute Pearson correlation coefficient (|R|) ≥ 0.3 with FDR < 0.05, and the top correlated genes were selected based on descending |R| values. Genetic mutation data were obtained from the cBioPortal database (http://www.cbioportal.org/). Additionally, we reviewed current literature on the role of MAGI2-AS3 in carcinogenesis, incorporating findings from in vitro studies, animal model investigations, and assessments of MAGI2-AS3 expression in clinical samples. A summary of all datasets and analytical workflow is presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Expression level analysis

We used RNA-seq data from the TCGA database and the Expression Analysis-Box Plots module of GEPIA2 to generate box plots comparing MAGI2-AS3 expression between tumor and normal tissues from the GTEx database. The analysis parameters were set with a P-value cutoff of 0.01 and a log2FC (fold change) cutoff of 1, matching TCGA normal and GTEx data. Additionally, we explored the expression of MAGI2-AS3 in different cell lines from various tissue sources using the GSE36133 dataset. For single-cell analysis, raw data from the Tabula Sapiens project (CZ CELLxGENE webtool) were processed using the Seurat R package (v4.3.0) [22]. Cells with fewer than 200 detected genes, more than 5,000 detected genes, or > 10% mitochondrial gene content were excluded to remove potential low-quality or stressed cells. Gene expression counts were normalized using Seurat’s LogNormalize method, with expression values scaled by a factor of 10,000 and log-transformed. Highly variable genes were identified using the ‘vst’ method (n = 2,000). Data were then scaled, and dimensionality reduction was performed using PCA followed by UMAP and t-SNE (dims = 1–20). We utilized the pre-computed cell type annotations and clustering structure provided directly by the original Tabula Sapiens consortium, without performing independent re-clustering or resolution optimization. Visualization was performed using t-SNE plots to depict cellular landscapes and violin plots to display MAGI2-AS3 expression across cell populations.

Hierarchical cluster analysis

We analyzed the top 10 genes correlated with MAGI2-AS3 in each cancer type using circular dendrogram visualizations. Utilizing the ggraph package in R, we created circular dendrograms to represent the hierarchical clustering of these genes based on their correlation with MAGI2-AS3. This method enabled us to pinpoint the genes that have the strongest associations with MAGI2-AS3 across different cancer types.

Functional enrichment analysis

To elucidate the biological significance of MAGI2-AS3 and its top correlated genes, we conducted functional enrichment analysis. Using the R package ClusterProfiler, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and hallmark pathway analyses. The results were visualized with bar plots generated by ClusterProfiler, highlighting the enriched pathways. Specifically, for CRC, we utilized the BEST web tool data [23] to conduct GO and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). This comprehensive approach allowed us to identify and visualize the biological processes, pathways, and gene sets significantly associated with MAGI2-AS3 in CRC.

Prognostic significance MAGI2-AS3 across pan-cancer

The study also examined how the expression of the MAGI2-AS3 lncRNA correlates with key clinical outcomes including overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), progression-free survival (PFS), and recurrence-free survival (RFS). To explore these associations, we utilized Cox proportional hazard regression models and Kaplan–Meier survival analyses. These analyses were conducted using the R programming environment, incorporating the “survival,” “survminer,” and “ggplot2” packages. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value and FDR of less than 0.05.

Diagnostic importance MAGI2-AS3 in pan-cancer

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of MAGI2-AS3, TCGA tumor samples and GTEx normal tissues were integrated into a unified dataset containing expression values, cancer type, sample label (tumor = 1, normal = 0), and data source. Only cancer types with at least 10 tumor and 10 normal samples were included. For each eligible cancer type, a univariate logistic regression model using MAGI2-AS3 expression as the sole predictive feature (LogisticRegression, max_iter = 1000, random_state = 42) was trained using 10-fold stratified cross-validation. MAGI2-AS3 expression was standardized within each training fold to prevent data leakage. Diagnostic performance was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and results were reported as mean area under the curve (AUC) ± standard deviation across folds.

Cancer-associated immune infiltration analysis and response to immunotherapy

In this study, we assessed the correlation between MAGI2-AS3 expression and immune-related parameters in the tumor microenvironment (TME), focusing on immune cell infiltration and response to checkpoint inhibitors. We employed eight widely used algorithms to estimate immune cell infiltration, including CIBERSORT [24], CIBERSORT ABS [24], EPIC [25], ESTIMATE [26], MCP-counter [27], Quantiseq [28], TIMER [29], and xCell [30]. Spearman’s rank correlation test was utilized to calculate partial correlation coefficients (cor) and associated P values. The results were visualized using heatmap and scatter plot representations. Additionally, we explored the association of MAGI2-AS3 with immunomodulators and predicted response to checkpoint inhibitors using data from the BEST web tool [23].

To explore the clinical significance of MAGI2-AS3 in immunotherapies, we retrieved immunotherapy cohorts containing expression data for and treatment information (e.g., CAR-T, anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA4) CRC from the BEST web tool [23]. Differential expression analysis (DEA) was performed between response and non-response groups based on MAGI2-AS3 gene expression in these datasets were shown by boxplots.

Assessing pan-cancer drug sensitivity of MAGI2-AS3

To investigate the correlation between MAGI2-AS3 expression and drug sensitivity across cancer types, we utilized publicly accessible bioinformatics tools, CPADS (Cancer Pan-drug sensitivity analysis, https://smuonco.shinyapps.io/CPADS/) and BEST web tool for cancer-specific drug response data. CPADS was employed to perform a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis, examining the association between MAGI2-AS3 expression levels and sensitivity to a wide array of chemotherapeutic agents. For CRC, we leveraged the BEST tool to focus on MAGI2-AS3 expressions in CRC cell lines and patient-derived samples, assessing its impact on resistance to targeted therapies. Finally, data were analyzed and visualized using R software for correlation analysis and heatmap generation.

Analysis of genomic and methylation alterations

Mutation and copy number variation analysis of MAGI2-AS3 were conducted using data retrieved from the TCGA database and analyzed with cbioportal, maftools [31] and GISTIC2.0 [32] tools. The findings were visualized using heatmaps generated with the ggplot2 package in R. Additionally, changes in the methylation status of MAGI2-AS3 in normal and tumor tissue samples were explored using methylation data from the SMARTapp database. The SMART (Shiny Methylation Analysis Resource Tool) App is a web application designed for comprehensive analysis of DNA methylation data from TCGA project [35]. Aggregation was performed using the mean beta-value across selected probes, as supported by the SMART App’s built-in functionality for multi-CpG summarization. Results

MAGI2-AS3 is dysregulated in pan-cancer

The functional roles of MAGI2-AS3 have been investigated across various human cancers (Supplementary Table S4). Numerous studies have consistently shown dysregulation of this lncRNA in cancer cells, highlighting its involvement in malignancy. Moreover, these studies have reported that silencing MAGI2-AS3 exerts suppressive effects on the malignant behavior of these cells.

MAGI2-AS3 expression was analyzed in pan-cancer using RNA-seq data from TCGA and GTEx. Significant differences were found in 23 cancer types. MAGI2-AS3 was notably overexpressed in pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), and thymoma (THYM), but downregulated in multiple cancers including Adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), Bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), Breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), Cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CESC), COAD, Kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), Ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV), Prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), READ, Testicular germ cell tumors (TGCT), Thyroid carcinoma (THCA), Uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), and Uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS) (Fig. 2A). These results suggest a role for MAGI2-AS3 in carcinogenesis. In cell lines, high expression was observed in 1321N1 (astrocytoma), 143B (osteosarcoma), 22Rv1 (prostate cancer), 23,132/87 (gastric cancer), and 42-MG-BA (glioma), while YH-13 and YKG1 (glioblastoma), YMB-1 (breast cancer), and ZR-75-1/30 (ductal carcinoma) showed marked downregulation (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table S5). Single-cell analysis revealed MAGI2-AS3 expression in monocytes and fibroblasts of the large intestine (Fig. 2C, D), as well as in epithelial subtypes such as keratocytes, surface ectodermal cells, mesothelial cells, and pancreatic A/D cells (Fig. 2E).

MAGI2-AS3 correlates with key genes across pan-cancer

In this study, we analyzed TCGA pan-cancer data to identify genes correlated with MAGI2-AS3. We found a consistent set of highly correlated genes across multiple cancer types, highlighting the potential role of MAGI2-AS3 in tumorigenesis and cancer progression (Supplementary Table S6; Fig. 3). We examined the genes most correlated with MAGI2-AS3 expression across TCGA pan-cancer datasets. The dendrogram revealed consistent clusters of genes co-expressed with MAGI2-AS3 across multiple tumor types, including bladder (BLCA), breast (BRCA), colon (COAD/READ), lung (LUAD/LUSC), kidney (KIRC/KIRP), pancreas (PAAD), prostate (PRAD), and uterine cancers (UCEC). Several transcription factors (e.g., RUNX1T1, ZEB1, ZEB2), adhesion molecules (CNRIP1, DCN, COLMP, LRCH2), and signaling regulators (DDR2, CRISPLD2, AKT3) showed strong positive correlation. Genes related to extracellular matrix organization (FBLN5, FBN1, SNED1, TIMP2), cytoskeletal remodeling (CFL2, SHROOM4, MYL3), and developmental regulation (WT1, HAND2, FOXF1) were also enriched among the top correlates. These associations highlight that MAGI2-AS3 expression tracks with key regulators of cell adhesion, extracellular matrix remodeling, developmental signaling, and EMT-related transcription factors. Importantly, these correlated gene networks were observed consistently across diverse cancer types, suggesting that MAGI2-AS3 may function as part of a broader regulatory program influencing tumor progression, invasion, and microenvironmental interactions (Fig. 3).

Pan-cancer prognostic significance for MAGI2-AS3

We conducted a comprehensive pan-cancer survival analysis to assess the prognostic potential of MAGI2-AS3 across 33 TCGA tumor types, evaluating its association with four clinical endpoints: OS, DSS, PFI, and DFI. This resulted in 125 distinct statistical tests (33 tumor types × 4 endpoints, excluding cancers lacking specific endpoints). To control for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Although 24 associations reached standard log-rank test significance (p < 0.05), none remained significant after FDR adjustment, underscoring the exploratory nature of these findings.

Notably, the strongest nominal signals were observed in BLCA and KIRC, where MAGI2-AS3 overexpression was consistently associated with poorer outcomes across three of the four endpoints (e.g., BLCA OS: p = 0.0025; KIRC PFI: p = 0.0025). Similar multi-endpoint trends, though less pronounced, were also evident in low-grade glioma (LGG) and uveal melanoma (UVM). While these patterns warrant attention, they must be interpreted with caution: in the absence of FDR-corrected significance, they represent hypothesis-generating signals rather than validated prognostic relationships (Fig. 5).

Consistent with this context-dependent behavior, Cox regression analyses revealed heterogeneous hazard ratios across cancer types, some indicating increased risk (HR > 1) and others suggesting potential protective effects (HR < 1) (Fig. 6A–D). Taken together, these results do not support a universal prognostic role for MAGI2-AS3. Instead, they highlight select tumor types, particularly BLCA, KIRC, LGG, and UVM, as priority candidates for future, adequately powered validation studies. This cautious interpretation aligns with best practices for high-dimensional survival screening and strengthens the scientific integrity of our pan-cancer assessment. Against this backdrop, CRC shows a consistent and robust pattern: high MAGI2-AS3 expression in TCGA COAD is associated with poorer DSS and PFS, with hazard ratios indicating increased mortality and progression risk, a trend further supported in independent GEO cohorts. These CRC-specific findings provide strong evidence for the adverse prognostic impact of MAGI2-AS3.

MAGI2-AS3 exhibits distinct diagnostic potential across multiple cancer types

A total of 28 cancer types were analyzed using a logistic regression classifier with standardized features and 10-fold stratified cross-validation. Model performance, measured by the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), showed excellent results (AUC ≥ 0.9) for 16 cancer types, good (0.8 ≤ AUC < 0.9) for 5, fair (0.7 ≤ AUC < 0.8) for 2, and poor (AUC < 0.7) for 5. The top-performing cancer types included CESC (1.000 ± 0.000), UCS (0.998 ± 0.006), OV (0.995 ± 0.010), UCEC (0.989 ± 0.013), and BRCA (0.987 ± 0.009). Notably, COAD (0.923 ± 0.015) and READ (0.942 ± 0.012) also demonstrated strong predictive accuracy. Overall, the model exhibited robust and consistent performance across most cancer types, underscoring its effectiveness for cancer classification (Fig. 7).

MAGI2-AS3 correlated with immune infiltration, tumor immunogenicity and response to immunotherapy

We systematically evaluated the relationship between MAGI2-AS3 expression and immune features across 24 cancer types (pan-cancer) and conducted a focused analysis in CRC. At the pan-cancer level, MAGI2-AS3 showed highly heterogeneous associations with immune cell infiltration and immune-related pathway activity. Correlations with immune cell abundance varied markedly by cancer type, with positive associations observed in cancers such as COAD, THCA, and UCEC, but weak or absent correlations in others, including KIRC and Acute myeloid leukemia LAML. Generally, our analysis revealed consistent positive correlations between MAGI2-AS3 expression and several anti-tumor immune populations, including CD8 + cytotoxic T cells, effector memory T cells, activated NK cells, and M1 macrophage (Fig. 8A; Supplementary Table S13). Similarly, enrichment of immune pathways related to cytokines, chemokines, and their receptors was inconsistent across cancers (Fig. 8B; Supplementary Table S14). These findings indicate that MAGI2-AS3 does not act as a universal immune regulator, and its immune-related effects are strongly context dependent.

In CRC, high MAGI2-AS3 expression is associated with a paradoxical immune signature. It positively correlates with pro-inflammatory chemokines and their receptors (e.g., CCL2/5, CXCL9/10/13; CCR2, CCR5, CXCR3/6), consistent with increased immune cell infiltration (Fig. 8C). Notably, the CRC immunotherapy cohorts analyzed comprised patients treated with anti–PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy as well as combined anti–PD-1/PD-L1 and anti–CTLA-4 regimens. Across these independent datasets, responders consistently exhibited lower MAGI2-AS3 expression, indicating that elevated MAGI2-AS3 is associated with resistance to immune checkpoint blockade (Fig. 8D–E).

In CRC-specific analysis (COAD + READ), MAGI2-AS3 expression showed strong positive correlations with stromal and myeloid populations—most notably cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs; r = 0.76–0.79, p < 0.001) and M2 macrophages/monocytes (r = 0.57–0.69)—while associations with T cell subsets were modest (r = 0.36–0.51). No major immune populations exhibited significant negative correlations. This stromal-enriched, myeloid-predominant signature coincided with reduced sensitivity to standard chemotherapy agents (5-fluorouracil, oxaliplatin, irinotecan) in pharmacogenomic datasets. Correlation between MAGI2-AS3 expression and sensitivity to various anti-cancer drugs

We systematically evaluated the association between MAGI2-AS3 expression and sensitivity to a comprehensive panel of anticancer drugs using publicly available pharmacogenomic datasets (Fig. 9B; Supplementary Table S15). The analysis included conventional chemotherapeutics, targeted kinase inhibitors, epigenetic modulators, DNA damage response agents, and emerging therapeutics.

Across multiple cancer types, MAGI2-AS3 expression exhibited heterogeneous but statistically significant correlations with drug responses. Notably EGFR and SRC family kinase inhibitors: High MAGI2-AS3 expression positively correlated with sensitivity to Erlotinib, Gefitinib, Lapatinib, and Dasatinib (p < 0.001), suggesting involvement in oncogenic kinase signaling. mTOR pathway inhibitors: Rapamycin and Temsirolimus showed significant associations (p < 0.001), indicating a role for MAGI2-AS3 in regulating growth and survival pathways.Multi-kinase inhibitors: Sorafenib, Sunitinib, and Pazopanib were negatively correlated with MAGI2-AS3 expression (p < 0.01), consistent with context-dependent drug responses. Cytotoxic agents: DNA-damaging drugs (Cisplatin, Gemcitabine, Camptothecin, Doxorubicin) and microtubule inhibitors (Paclitaxel, Docetaxel, Vinblastine) also showed significant correlations (p < 0.05–0.01). Emerging therapeutics: Bortezomib (proteasome inhibitor), Vorinostat (HDAC inhibitor), and PARP inhibitors (ABT-888) were highly correlated with MAGI2-AS3 expression (p < 0.001), linking it to epigenetic regulation and DNA damage response pathways.

Among these, afatinib demonstrated the strongest positive correlation with MAGI2-AS3 expression, indicating potential increased sensitivity, whereas motesanib displayed a negative correlation, suggesting resistance in high-expression contexts. These two drugs were selected as representative examples of clinically relevant correlations.

Focusing on CRC, MAGI2-AS3 exhibited a coherent drug-response profile (Fig. 9C). Sensitivity: Selected chemotherapeutics (e.g., Cisplatin, Gemcitabine) and PARP inhibitors, highlighting potential therapeutic avenues for CRC patients with elevated MAGI2-AS3 levels.

Experimental validation of MAGI2-AS3 showed significant down-regulation

MAGI2-AS3 expression was measured in CRC and adjacent normal tissues using RT-qPCR. Results showed a significant reduction in tumor samples (p < 0.001), normalized to β-actin and analyzed by the 2 − ΔΔCt method (Fig. 10E). This consistent downregulation suggests MAGI2-AS3 may contribute to CRC pathogenesis and could serve as a biomarker for distinguishing malignant from normal tissues. The paired analysis showed a highly consistent and significant difference between groups, with a mean paired difference of 4.07 (95% CI: 3.99–4.16; paired t-test, p < 0.001), supporting the robustness of the observed expression pattern. MAGI2-AS3 tumor expression showed a possible association with disease stage and varied across tumor locations, but no clear location-specific pattern was observed. Spearman correlation analysis indicated a very weak positive correlation (rho = 0.045, p = 0.902), suggesting no significant relationship between TNM stage and MAGI2-AS3 expression in this small cohort (Fig. 10F, G and H).

MAGI2-AS3 orchestrates an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in colorectal cancer

High expression of the lncRNA MAGI2-AS3 in CRC tumors promotes the recruitment and accumulation of diverse immune cell populations, including myeloid cells (macrophages, dendritic cells, monocytes) and lymphoid cells (T and B cells). It concurrently activates key immune signaling pathways, notably chemokine/cytokine networks and antigen presentation machinery, as evidenced by positive correlations with corresponding gene signatures across multiple CRC cohorts. Despite fostering an inflamed microenvironment, MAGI2-AS3 expression is associated with the co-upregulation of critical immunoinhibitory checkpoints, including PD-L2 (PDCD1LG2), CTLA4, LAG3, and IDO1. This creates a paradoxical state where recruited immune cells become functionally exhausted or suppressed, failing to mount effective anti-tumor immunity, a hallmark of “immune-excluded” or “dysfunctional hot” tumors. Clinically, high MAGI2-AS3 expression serves as a robust biomarker of poor response to anti–PD-1/PD-L1 therapy. Analysis of independent patient cohorts demonstrates significantly lower MAGI2-AS3 levels in responders (R) compared to non-responders (NR) (Wilcoxon p = 0.0026 and p = 0.011, respectively) [33, 34]. This positions MAGI2-AS3 as a potential therapeutic target to reverse immunotherapy resistance in CRC (Fig. 11).

Discussion

LncRNAs have emerged as pivotal regulators of cancer biology, often exhibiting functional duality that defies simple classification as oncogenes or tumor suppressors. Our comprehensive analysis of MAGI2-AS3 across 23 cancer types reveals striking expression heterogeneity. The lncRNA is significantly overexpressed in cancers such as PAAD, SKCM, and THYM. In contrast, it is markedly underexpressed in adrenocortical carcinoma ACC, and BRCA. This heterogeneity is also evident in cancer cell lines. MAGI2-AS3 is elevated in prostate (22Rv1) and gastric (23132/87) lines but suppressed in glioblastoma (YH-13) and breast (YMB-1) lines. These patterns suggest that MAGI2-AS3 does not follow a single, universal functional role across cancers. Instead, its role is dynamically shaped by the molecular and cellular context of each tumor. Rather than a weakness, this context dependency reflects a fundamental principle of lncRNA biology: their functions are contingent on epigenetic states, tissue-specific interactomes, and microenvironmental cues [19, 35]. Focusing on CRC a malignancy in which MAGI2-AS3 consistently demonstrates tumor-suppressive behavior, provides a coherent anchor through which to interpret its broader pan-cancer roles and reconcile apparent contradictions.

Compared with other well-characterized immune-related lncRNAs, MAGI2-AS3 exhibits a distinct functional profile. For instance, NEAT1 primarily promotes immune evasion by impairing NK cell function and enhancing tumor immunosuppression, while MALAT1 drives chemoresistance and suppressive signaling within the tumor microenvironment [36]. In contrast, MAGI2-AS3 exhibits context-dependent duality. It correlates with pro-inflammatory chemokines, promoting immune cell infiltration. At the same time, it associates with immunosuppressive checkpoint pathways. Together, these features create an immune-rich but functionally suppressed tumor microenvironment, especially in CRC. This dual role highlights MAGI2-AS3 as a nuanced immune rheostat rather than a unidirectional regulator, underscoring its novelty as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.

We propose that MAGI2-AS3 functions as a molecular rheostat, integrating inputs from three interrelated regulatory layers: epigenetic control, pathway-specific interactions, and TME dynamics. This integrative framework explains how the same lncRNA can suppress tumor growth in one context while promoting progression in another, and it offers a roadmap for its clinical translation as both a biomarker and therapeutic target.

Epigenetic regulation as the primary switch

DNA methylation serves as the foundational layer governing MAGI2-AS3 expression. In CRC, BLCA, and BRCA, promoter hypermethylation correlates strongly with transcriptional silencing, aligning with poorer clinical outcomes and more aggressive tumor phenotypes [19, 35, 37]. For instance, in bladder cancer, reduced MAGI2-AS3 expression, driven by hypermethylation, inhibits the miR-15b-5p/CCDC19 axis, thereby suppressing proliferation and invasion [37]. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), promoter hypermethylation silences MAGI2-AS3, which increases tumor invasiveness. Restoring its expression counteracts this effect by acting through the miR-31-5p/androgen receptor (AR) axis, thereby inhibiting tumor progression [19]. These findings underscore a tumor-suppressive role when MAGI2-AS3 is epigenetically intact.

Conversely, in gastric and pancreatic adenocarcinomas, hypomethylation or active transcriptional programs permit MAGI2-AS3 overexpression, enabling oncogenic functions. In gastric cancer, this leads to sustained ZEB1 expression through sponging of miR-141/200a, thereby driving EMT and metastasis [17]. This epigenetic plasticity, where methylation status dictates functional output, positions MAGI2-AS3 as a responsive element in the cancer epigenome. Critically, this mechanism is therapeutically tractable. DNA methyltransferase inhibitors, such as azacitidine, which is already in clinical trials for ovarian cancer [38], could reactivate MAGI2-AS3 in tumors where it is epigenetically silenced. This reactivation might restore the tumor-suppressive effects seen in CRC and renal cell carcinoma [39, 40].

Recent work by Lukina et al. (2025) provides independent validation of MAGI2-AS3 epigenetic silencing in ovarian cancer, demonstrating a significant inverse correlation between promoter hypermethylation and gene expression (r < − 0.5, p < 0.01). When combined with two other methylated lncRNAs, MAGI2-AS3 contributed to a diagnostic panel with high accuracy (AUC = 0.989), reinforcing its potential as a pan-cancer epigenetic biomarker [41]. These findings support the conserved role of methylation in regulating MAGI2-AS3 across tumor types and underscore the value of multi-marker panels for early detection, while highlighting the need for larger prospective studies to confirm clinical utility.

Tissue-specific molecular interactions define functional output

Beyond epigenetic control, the functional consequences of MAGI2-AS3 expression depend on its molecular partners, which vary by tissue type. In CRC, MAGI2-AS3 stabilizes the MAGI2 protein—a known scaffold that enhances PTEN activity and suppresses AKT signaling [42]. It also promotes apoptosis via Fas/FasL upregulation [43] and inhibits Wnt/β-catenin signaling by interacting with β-catenin and axin [17]. These actions converge to suppress tumor growth, migration, and invasion. Single-cell RNA sequencing further localizes MAGI2-AS3 expression predominantly to monocytes and fibroblasts within the CRC TME, suggesting its tumor-suppressive effects may be partially mediated through stromal and immune compartments.

In stark contrast, gastric cancer leverages MAGI2-AS3 for oncogenic gain. Here, it acts as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA), sequestering miR-141/200a and thereby derepressing ZEB1, a master regulator of EMT [17]. This not only enhances metastatic potential but also aligns with our enrichment analyses linking MAGI2-AS3 to cytoskeletal organization, actin binding, and anchoring junctions, processes central to cell motility and invasion. Similarly, in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), MAGI2-AS3 exerts tumor-suppressive effects by sponging miR-23a-3p to upregulate PTEN and by enhancing cisplatin sensitivity through the miR-1269a/PTEN/AKT axis [44, 45]. These divergent roles highlight that MAGI2-AS3 does not possess intrinsic oncogenic or suppressive activity; rather, its impact is determined by the signaling landscape into which it is embedded.

Further supporting this notion, MAGI2-AS3 interacts with the RNA-binding protein RBMS3 in prostate cancer, forming a complex that induces exon skipping in multiple genes to facilitate invasion [46]. Meanwhile, in breast cancer, it functions as a sponge for miR-374a, upregulating PTEN and inhibiting migration [47], and independently enhances Fas/FasL-mediated apoptosis [43]. Zhou et al. further demonstrated that MAGI2-AS3 suppresses MCF-7 proliferation by upregulating MAGI2 and inhibiting Wnt/β-catenin signaling [48]. Collectively, these studies illustrate that MAGI2-AS3’s functional repertoire is defined by its RNA and protein interactors, which are themselves shaped by tissue-specific gene expression programs.

Integration with the tumor microenvironment and immune modulation

The third regulatory layer highlighted by our study involves the TME, in which MAGI2-AS3 both shapes and reflects immune–stromal interactions. Single-cell analyses indicate that in CRC, MAGI2-AS3 is predominantly expressed in monocytes and fibroblasts, positioning it at the interface of stromal remodeling and immune regulation. Consistent with this localization, MAGI2-AS3 expression correlates positively with antigen presentation machinery (e.g., HLA class II genes) and chemokine signaling pathways, supporting a role in immune cell recruitment and immune activation within the CRC TME [13].

Importantly, this immune-inflamed state is accompanied by features of immune dysfunction. In CRC, elevated MAGI2-AS3 is associated with altered expression of immune checkpoint pathways and reduced clinical benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors, including anti–PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy and combined anti–PD-1/PD-L1 plus anti–CTLA-4 regimens [49]. Together, these findings support a model in which high MAGI2-AS3 marks an inflamed but functionally compromised immune microenvironment, rather than an effectively anti-tumor immune response. One plausible explanation is that sustained chemokine-driven immune recruitment leads to chronic immune stimulation, ultimately promoting T-cell exhaustion or ineffective immune surveillance. Alternatively, MAGI2-AS3–associated stromal activation may contribute to a spatially restricted or myeloid-dominated TME that limits productive T-cell reinvigoration following checkpoint blockade. While these mechanisms remain speculative, they align with emerging paradigms of “immune-hot yet ICI-resistant” tumors in CRC [50].

This dual immune phenotype has direct therapeutic implications. Tumors with low MAGI2-AS3 expression may represent relatively immune-quiescent or “immune-cold” states that retain the capacity for effective immune priming upon checkpoint inhibition. Conversely, high MAGI2-AS3 expression may define a hyper-inflamed but T-cell–exhausted tumor microenvironment. Patients in this subgroup might benefit from combination therapies. These could include immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) paired with epigenetic modulators or agents that target myeloid or stromal components to reverse immune dysfunction. Although prospective clinical validation in CRC immunotherapy trials is required, our findings position MAGI2-AS3 as a candidate predictive biomarker of immunotherapy resistance rather than simple immune activation.

Notably, the immune-modulatory behavior of MAGI2-AS3 distinguishes it from other well-characterized immune-related lncRNAs. NEAT1 and MALAT1 are largely associated with unidirectional immunosuppressive effects, including impaired NK cell activity, enhanced regulatory signaling, and resistance to therapy [36, 51, 52]. In contrast, MAGI2-AS3 appears to function as a context-dependent immune rheostat, simultaneously promoting immune infiltration while constraining effective antitumor immunity. This nuanced role may explain its divergent prognostic and therapeutic associations across cancer types and underscores the importance of tumor-specific interpretation when considering MAGI2-AS3 as a biomarker or therapeutic target.

Prognostic and therapeutic implications

The context-dependent nature of MAGI2-AS3 is starkly reflected in survival analyses. Elevated expression correlates with longer OS in CRC, LUAD, PAAD, and KIRC, cancers where it acts as a tumor suppressor. Conversely, high MAGI2-AS3 predicts shorter OS in LUSC and BLCA, consistent with its oncogenic roles in specific subsets or stages of these diseases [37]. This dichotomy reinforces the necessity of cancer-type-specific interpretation and cautions against blanket assumptions about lncRNA function.

From a therapeutic standpoint, MAGI2-AS3 is highly tractable. In tumors where it is silenced (e.g., CRC, BLCA, HNSCC), strategies to restore expression, such as DNA demethylating agents [38], CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) [53], or lncRNA-targeted gene therapy, could reactivate tumor-suppressive networks. Preclinical evidence supports this: MAGI2-AS3 overexpression sensitizes esophageal cancer cells to radiotherapy [15], and its restoration in nasopharyngeal carcinoma reduces cisplatin resistance [54]. In hepatocellular carcinoma and clear cell renal carcinoma, MAGI2-AS3 inhibits proliferation via miR-374b-5p/SMG1 and miR-629-5p/PRDM16 axes, respectively [39, 40], offering additional mechanistic templates for therapeutic exploitation.

Conversely, in cancers where MAGI2-AS3 drives progression (e.g., gastric cancer via EMT), targeted knockdown using antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) or CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) could abrogate its oncogenic effects [53]. The feasibility of such approaches is supported by successes with other lncRNAs, such as MALAT1 and HOTAIR, in preclinical models.

While numerous molecular biomarkers show promise for prognostic stratification CRC, their clinical adoption is often limited by heterogeneous validation and modest predictive power when used alone. Our findings on MAGI2-AS3 support the emerging view that individual markers are best positioned as complementary tools rather than standalone replacements for established staging systems. Consistent with Duan et al.‘s recommendation, the translational potential of MAGI2-AS3 will likely be realized through its integration into multi-modal biomarker panels, pending prospective validation in large, diverse clinical cohorts to confirm its incremental prognostic value and practical utility [55].

However, Major hurdles include efficient and tumor-specific delivery, limited stability of RNA-based therapeutics in vivo, and potential off-target or immune-stimulatory effects. Current approaches, including antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), siRNAs, and CRISPR-based transcriptional repression, show therapeutic promise. However, they require improved delivery systems to achieve effective intratumoral concentrations. Lipid nanoparticles or ligand-directed carriers are potential solutions [53]. Despite these challenges, recent clinical successes of RNA-based therapies suggest that targeting oncogenic lncRNAs like MAGI2-AS3, particularly in biomarker-selected patient subsets, may become feasible with continued advances in RNA delivery technologies and precision oncology frameworks.

Network-level insights and biomarker potential

Beyond individual pathways, MAGI2-AS3 occupies a central node in lncRNA–mRNA interaction networks. In CRC, Samadi et al. reported that MAGI2-AS3 exhibits 220 node interactions within a co-expression network involving HAND2-AS1, MIR100HG, and LINC00578, strongly correlating with TNM staging genes [56]. This high connectivity suggests MAGI2-AS3 acts as a regulatory hub in CRC carcinogenesis. Similarly, its consistent co-expression with ZFPM2, RBMS3, and MAGI2 across cancers highlights its integration into core tumor-suppressive or oncogenic modules.

Notably, ZFPM2-AS1, an oncogenic lncRNA, promotes gastric cancer by inactivating p53 via MIF interaction [57]. While distinct from MAGI2-AS3, this illustrates how lncRNA networks can converge on master regulators like p53 or PTEN. MAGI2-AS3’s ability to modulate both PTEN and p53-associated pathways (indirectly via MAGI2 stabilization) positions it as a high-leverage node for therapeutic intervention.

Moreover, MAGI2-AS3 shows promise as a minimally invasive biomarker. In breast cancer, its reduced expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) correlates with advanced stage, metastasis, and poor survival, with high diagnostic accuracy [33]. A similar liquid biopsy approach could be developed for CRC, especially given the strong association between MAGI2-AS3 levels and tumor stage.

CRC-Specific interpretation of the paradoxical role of MAGI2-AS3

A central and intriguing finding of this study is the seemingly paradoxical role of MAGI2-AS3 in CRC. While bulk transcriptomic analyses show that MAGI2-AS3 is significantly downregulated in CRC tumors compared to adjacent normal tissues, suggesting a tumor-suppressive role, higher expression within tumors is associated with poorer prognosis and reduced response to immune checkpoint blockade. This apparent contradiction can be reconciled by considering the cellular context of MAGI2-AS3 expression [58].

A recent study by Shanmei Du et al. (2025) investigated MAGI2-AS3 expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from breast cancer patients and found that its levels were significantly downregulated compared to healthy controls. Lower MAGI2-AS3 expression correlated with advanced TNM stage, higher pathological grade, distant metastasis, and poorer overall survival, highlighting its tumor-suppressive role and potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker [33]. These findings parallel the bulk downregulation of MAGI2-AS3 observed in CRC tumors in our study, supporting its general tumor-suppressive function.

Importantly, our single-cell RNA-seq analyses indicate that MAGI2-AS3 is preferentially enriched in stromal and immune compartments, particularly monocytes and fibroblasts, rather than being uniformly expressed in malignant epithelial cells. Therefore, the “high expression” observed in bulk tumor samples likely reflects increased representation or activation of specific stromal/immune populations within the TME, rather than elevated expression within cancer cells themselves. This distinction is critical when interpreting survival associations.

The CRC survival analyses were conducted using bulk tumor RNA-seq data; thus, higher MAGI2-AS3 expression likely represents a composite signal driven by the tumor microenvironment. We propose that tumors with relatively higher MAGI2-AS3 levels correspond to a distinct TME subtype characterized by stromal activation and chronic immune infiltration. Although this environment appears immune-inflamed, it may be functionally dysregulated, consistent with our findings of altered chemokine signaling and poor immunotherapy response. In this context, MAGI2-AS3 may not function purely as a tumor suppressor but rather as a marker or mediator of a specific, immune-modified TME state associated with adaptive resistance.

This compartment-specific model provides a biologically coherent explanation for the dual observations of overall tumor downregulation and adverse prognostic associations in CRC. It also underscores the importance of integrating single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data when interpreting lncRNA function in heterogeneous tumor ecosystems.

Limitations, challenges and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the experimental validation cohort was relatively small (n = 10 paired CRC and adjacent normal tissues) and was designed primarily to confirm differential expression rather than to evaluate prognostic, stage-specific, or immune-related associations. Detailed longitudinal clinical outcomes, MSI status, tumor stage stratification, and matched immune marker data (e.g., CD8 or PD-L1 immunohistochemistry) were not available for this cohort, precluding direct clinicopathological and immune validation at the tissue level. Larger, clinically annotated CRC cohorts with integrated transcriptomic, spatial, and immune profiling will be required to substantiate the prognostic and immunological associations suggested by our bioinformatic analyses.

Second, all pan-cancer associations reported here are observational in nature. Although we identified correlations between MAGI2-AS3 expression and survival, immune checkpoints, pathway signatures, and drug sensitivity, these relationships do not establish causality. The proposed mechanisms, such as miRNA sponging, pathway modulation, or immune regulation, are inferred from co-expression patterns and prior literature. Definitive mechanistic validation will require functional experiments, including CRISPRi/CRISPRa-mediated perturbation of MAGI2-AS3 in CRC cell lines, RNA pull-down or CLIP-seq to identify direct molecular interactors, and co-culture systems with immune cells to clarify its role in tumor–immune crosstalk. Such studies are essential to determine whether MAGI2-AS3 actively regulates immune responses or primarily reflects specific tumor microenvironment states.

Third, while we discuss potential therapeutic strategies (e.g., antisense oligonucleotides, CRISPR-based modulation, or epigenetic reactivation), these approaches remain speculative. Targeting lncRNAs poses substantial challenges, including tissue-specific delivery, off-target effects, RNA stability, and intracellular localization constraints. Moreover, the context-dependent behavior of MAGI2-AS3 across tumor types, tumor-suppressive in some cancers yet potentially oncogenic in others, necessitates cancer-specific functional validation before considering translational applications.

Finally, although TCGA tumor and GTEx normal datasets were carefully normalized and batch-corrected prior to analysis, residual batch effects between independently generated cohorts cannot be completely excluded. Future studies using prospectively collected, uniformly processed tumor–normal pairs and multi-center validation datasets will be important to confirm the robustness of these findings.

The observed correlations between MAGI2-AS3 expression and drug sensitivity highlight its potential as a predictive biomarker, though the underlying molecular mechanisms require experimental validation. MAGI2-AS3 may modulate therapeutic response by regulating ABC efflux transporters, apoptosis and DNA damage response pathways, or PI3K/AKT and Wnt/β-catenin signaling networks [59, 60]. Future functional studies employing CRISPR-based modulation and pharmacological assays will be essential to establish causal links and determine whether MAGI2-AS3 can ultimately guide precision therapy or rational drug combinations [61].

Conclusion

In summary, MAGI2-AS3 is not a static oncogene or tumor suppressor but a dynamic molecular integrator whose function is calibrated by epigenetic regulation, tissue-specific interactomes, and immune microenvironmental cues. In colorectal cancer, it acts as a tumor suppressor by stabilizing MAGI2, enhancing PTEN signaling, promoting apoptosis, and modulating immune activity—making it a compelling biomarker for prognosis and immunotherapy response. Across other cancers, its role shifts in accordance with local regulatory landscapes, underscoring the necessity of precision oncology approaches that account for contextual biology. Future work should prioritize functional validation in CRC models, clinical correlation with immunotherapy outcomes, and development of MAGI2-AS3-targeted therapeutics—steps that will translate this nuanced biology into tangible clinical benefit.
