Acute Stanford Type B Aortic Dissection Rapidly Progressed to Type A During a Single Computed Tomography Angiography Examination Despite Stable Hemodynamics

Serial imaging obtained over several minutes demonstrated proximal extension into the ascending aorta, highlighting the importance of vigilant imaging assessment and the potential limitation of single-phase computed tomography angiography (CTA) when conservative management is initially considered.

Acute Stanford type B aortic dissection is generally managed conservatively when patients are hemodynamically stable and do not exhibit complications such as malperfusion, rupture, or refractory pain, in accordance with current guidelines.1 However, proximal extension of type B dissection into the ascending aorta represents a catastrophic event that mandates urgent surgical intervention. Although such progression has been reported, it is usually accompanied by clinical deterioration or hemodynamic instability. We report a rare case in which acute Stanford type B aortic dissection progressed to type A within minutes during a single computed tomography angiography (CTA) examination, despite stable hemodynamics and no dramatic change in symptoms. This case highlights an important limitation of single-phase imaging and provides a cautionary message when conservative management of acute type B aortic dissection is considered.Take-Home Messages•Acute Stanford type B aortic dissection may progress to type A within minutes, even in hemodynamically stable patients.•Real-time imaging during CTA can reveal rapid proximal extension and serves as an important caution when conservative management is considered.

Case Presentation

An 84-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with persistent chest pain. She denied back pain, syncope, or neurological symptoms. Her medical history was unremarkable for connective tissue disease. On arrival, she was hemodynamically stable, with no signs of shock or end-organ malperfusion on physical examination.

Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm without ischemic changes. Laboratory findings were unremarkable. Given the clinical suspicion of acute aortic pathology, contrast-enhanced CTA was performed.

Investigations

Initial arterial-phase CTA demonstrated acute Stanford type B aortic dissection. The dissection flap extended distally throughout the thoracoabdominal aorta to the aortic bifurcation. At this time point, no definite involvement of the ascending aorta was identified (Figure 1A). Delayed-phase images acquired approximately 30 seconds later demonstrated new extension of the dissection flap into the ascending aorta, suggesting proximal propagation (Figure 1B). Because this unexpected finding was discordant with the initial diagnosis and could critically alter management, repeat CTA was performed 3 minutes after the initial scan to confirm the extent of proximal involvement. The repeat images showed more conspicuous dissection of the ascending aorta, confirming acute Stanford type A aortic dissection (Figure 1C). Sagittal CTA reconstructions further illustrated the longitudinal progression of the dissection from the descending thoracic aorta toward the ascending aorta over this short time interval (Figures 2A to 2C). Notably, throughout the entire imaging sequence, the patient remained hemodynamically stable and reported no new symptoms.

Management

Given the confirmed proximal extension of the dissection into the ascending aorta, emergent surgical intervention was warranted. The patient underwent total arch replacement with open stent grafting on the same day. The operative strategy followed standard institutional practice and was not intended to represent a novel surgical technique.

Discussion

Acute Stanford type B aortic dissection is commonly treated with optimal medical therapy when uncomplicated, and conservative management is associated with favorable early outcomes in most patients.1 Current European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery/Society of Thoracic Surgeons guidelines emphasize careful imaging surveillance in patients with medically managed type B aortic dissection because disease progression may occur unpredictably.2 The present case uniquely demonstrates real-time proximal extension during CTA despite stable hemodynamics. This observation highlights a potential limitation of single-phase imaging and underscores the importance of vigilant imaging assessment even in apparently stable patients. Nevertheless, proximal extension into the ascending aorta remains a feared complication because of its association with high mortality and the need for urgent surgery. A previous report has demonstrated that acute aortic dissection can progress rapidly on short-term serial imaging.3 However, such progression was associated with hemodynamic instability, worsening pain, or other clinical deterioration that prompted repeat imaging and escalation of care. In contrast, the present case is notable for the absence of such warning signs, with proximal extension occurring despite stable hemodynamics and unchanged symptoms.

The principal value of this case lies in the imaging findings rather than the surgical technique. Capturing the progression of dissection during a single CTA examination provides rare insight into the dynamic nature of acute aortic dissection and underscores an important limitation of single-phase CTA. Reliance on a single arterial-phase acquisition may underestimate proximal involvement in the hyperacute setting.

From a clinical standpoint, this observation has important implications for the conservative management of acute Stanford type B aortic dissection. Hemodynamic stability alone does not exclude rapid anatomical progression. When subtle interval changes are suspected during imaging, prompt reassessment should be considered, as delayed recognition of proximal extension may have catastrophic consequences.

Conclusions

Acute Stanford type B aortic dissection may progress to type A within minutes, even in hemodynamically stable patients without overt clinical deterioration. This case underscores the importance of vigilance and careful imaging assessment when conservative management is considered.
