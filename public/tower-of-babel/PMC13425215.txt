From ‘Me too’ to ‘Me better’ and ‘Me cooler’: the recent rheumatology advances in China
Authors: Wenwen Xu, Shuang Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The past 2 years (post-COVID) have witnessed a speeding progression of rheumatology in China. This viewpoint focused on high-impact clinical investigations conducted by researchers from mainland China and highlighted China’s contributions that have changed or probably will change clinical practice. It is noteworthy that China is in a process of transitioning from validation studies (‘Me too’ type) dominance to the enhanced therapeutic strategies and repurposing innovations (‘Me better’ and ‘Me cooler’ type), positioning itself as a key player in shaping the future of rheumatology.

INTRODUCTION

Chinese rheumatologists have accelerated research efforts to address unmet needs in systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases over the past 2 years. Leveraging randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and high-quality multicentre real-world studies, China has generated evidence that not only complemented international guidelines but also introduced paradigm-shifting approaches. The studies are operationally divided into 3 categories: a validation study (‘Me too’) validates existing therapeutic strategies or explores new drugs in the same class with noninferiority over current standards; an enhanced therapeutic strategy (‘Me better’), on the other hand, is literally an upgrade of the existing treatment through superior efficacy, safety, or accessibility; intriguingly, a repurposing study (‘Me cooler’) is designated to those pioneering novel clinical applications of existing tools to address unmet needs, with transformative potential. This framework adapts established drug innovation taxonomies from translational medicine to the context of clinical research. RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS

Two phase III RCTs on new Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi) in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) have been completed, which were led by Peking Union Hospital and Peking University People’s Hospital, respectively. Ivarmacitinib—a JAK1-selective inhibitor developed by Hengrui Pharmaceutical Company—achieved 75.1% ACR20 response in methotrexate (MTX)-refractory RA (vs 40.4% in placebo), with a safety profile comparable to other JAKi [1]. Similarly, peficitinib, a pan-JAKi, demonstrated its efficacy (ACR20 response: 56.6% vs 24.2% placebo) and tolerability in Asian populations, supporting its role in MTX-inadequate or intolerant patient with RA [2]. Of note, the global phase III trial of ivarmacitinib for atopic dermatitis (QUARTZ3, NCT04875169)—spanning 51 research centres across Canada and China—successfully met all primary and key secondary endpoints, demonstrating consistent efficacy and safety in both Asian and non-Asian populations. This milestone not only paves the way for global regulatory submissions of ivarmacitinib in AD but also exemplifies China’s capacity to lead multinational trials; though AD falls outside rheumatology, it is critical to emphasise that ivarmacitinib’s primary rheumatological indication remains RA. In addition, an IL-6 receptor antagonist biosimilar BAT1806/BIIB800 was confirmed in a phase III trial to be equivalent to the originator tocilizumab in MTX-refractory patient with RA [3], which renders its simultaneous approval by the European Medicines Agency (EMA), the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and Sino-FDA in 2024—a first for any Chinese-developed biosimilar. These ‘Me Too’ products are apparently broadening accessibility and providing cost-effective alternatives as targeted synthetic or biological disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (ts/bDMARDs) options in the management of RA.

In light of the concept of ‘geo-medicine’, iguratimod (a China-/Japan-approved conventional synthetic DMARD) has proven its superiority to MTX-monotherapy in early RA, with higher ACR20 response rates (77.44% vs 65.87%) and improved minimal radiographic progression (57.4% vs 47.8%) in the SMILE study (n = 895), led by Shanghai Renji Hospital [4]. Concurrently, through 12 years of rigorous trials (TRIFRA/YISTAR studies), Tripterygium wilfordii—an herb-derived medicine—was validated as noninferior to MTX. This fostered a 2023 International Consensus which was steered by Dr Xuan Zhang from Beijing Hospital, with an attempt to integrate traditional Chinese medicine into global practice [5]. However, given the well-established array of treatments, it will be a tremendously difficult task to carve a niche in RA management globally.

Likewise, to identify novel mode-of-action treatment is getting extremely tougher, yet Chinese researchers have not given up trying. An example is a S1PR1 agonist proximod, which showed dose-dependent lymphocyte suppression and favourable safety profile in an early-phase trial [6]. Although it still holds its promise, this ‘Me too’ product might end up with ‘Me neither’—because previously, multiple S1PR1 modulators had failed to survive and thrive beyond phase II trials for RA therapy. In another groundbreaking pilot study conducted by Anhui Provincial Hospital, the investigators tested fourth-generation autologous chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy (CAR-T; CD19-targeted, plus secreting anti-IL-6/TNFα antibodies) in 3 refractory patient with RA [7] (also see ‘Cell therapy’ section below).

Nevertheless, digital health innovations are reshaping the care delivery of RA in China. A pragmatic RCT led by Dr Zhanguo Li demonstrated AI-driven mobile apps significantly improve disease activity and patient-reported outcomes, setting an implementation model for digital therapeutics in chronic disease management [8].

SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS

The KDIGO 2024 guidelines for lupus nephritis (LN) [9] had adopted a landmark trial led by Shanghai Renji Hospital, which provided evidence that leflunomide is noninferior to azathioprine as a maintenance regimen [10]. In parallel, 2023 EULAR guidelines [11] cited a phase I/II single-armed trial also conducted by Renji team, which evaluated SGLT-2 inhibitor dapagliflozin in adult patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) (18 patients with LN), confirming its safety profile despite no significant reduction in proteinuria; yet a signal of renal protection was captured among those with eGFR lower than 90 mL/min/1.73 m² at baseline [12]. These ‘Me cooler’ studies highlight repurposing potentials for certain drugs in LN management.

A multicentre open-labelled RCT led by Shanghai Ruijin Hospital demonstrated that upfront low-dose mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) combined with glucocorticoids (GC) and hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) significantly reduces subsequent severe relapse (10.8% vs 27.7%, RR = 0.39) and LN incidence (1.5% vs 13.8%, RR = 0.11) in new-onset patient with SLE with high anti-dsDNA titres but no major organ involvement [13]. Complementing this, PRESS trial (n = 333) led by Peking Union Hospital provided high-level evidence for GC withdrawal in stable SLE. HCQ maintenance achieved noninferior relapse rates compared to low-dose GCs (11.2% vs 4.7%, Pnon-inferiority = 0.034), whereas complete withdrawal led to higher risk of relapse (26.1%), underscoring HCQ’s critical role in sustaining remission and aligning with the current good practice protocol [14].

China’s biologics pipeline further expanded with telitacicept, a dual BAFF/APRIL inhibitor. A phase IIb trial demonstrated sustained efficacy in active SLE, securing early domestic approval and prompting global phase III studies [15]. The global phase III trials (REMESLE-1/2, NCT06456567 and NCT05306574) across North America, Europe, and Asia-Pacific regions will enrol >500 patients with active SLE to evaluate long-term efficacy and cross-ethnic applicability. Furthermore, a stunning phase I trial of BCMA/CD19 dual-target autologous CAR-T for severe SLE/LN demonstrated clinical response in all 12 patients, with 11 patient with LN eliminating autoantibodies within 1 month. Immune reconstitution allowed sustained drug-free remission, highlighting its robust efficacy and acceptable safety profile [16].

Collectively, these advancements—spanning full-scale SLE management—illustrate China’s ambition to refresh SLE care.

Dermatomyositis and systemic sclerosis: with a focus on interstitial lung disease

Recent advances in dermatomyositis (DM) research highlight China’s role in addressing high-risk, east Asian-dominant phenotypes through innovative therapies. A multicentre real-world study led by Shanghai Renji Hospital demonstrated that tofacitinib significantly improved outcomes in anti-MDA5+DM-ILD, achieving 1-year transplant-free survival rates of 67.6% compared to 53.5% with calcineurin inhibitors; hence, a lower mortality risk (HR: 0.72) was obtained [17]. This milestone study was built on a previous single-centre pilot study suggesting tofacitinib’s efficacy in early-stage nonrapid progressive MDA5+DM-ILD [18]. Also, single-cell sequencing of MDA5+DM-ILD patients (both peripehral blood mononuclear cell and lung tissue) revealed dominant type I interferon hyperactivity, rationalising the repurposing of JAKi and echoing the synergy of bench-to-bed translational research [[17], [18], [19]]. In fact, the 2023 ACR/CHEST guidelines have already adopted relevant evidence and conditionally recommend JAKi as first-line or second-line therapy for myositis-associated ILD [20]. The latest real-world evidence probably will have further impact on the amendments of international guidelines.

A post hoc analysis of the EUSTAR database led by investigators from Shanghai Renji Hospital and European collaborators compared 4 legitimate immunosuppressants—tocilizumab, rituximab, mycophenolate mofetil, and cyclophosphamide—in systemic sclerosis-interstitial lung disease (SSc-ILD). Over a median follow-up of 11 months, none demonstrated superiority over another in preserving forced vital capacity (FVC), underscoring the urgent need for personalised management in this heterogeneous disease [21]. This large-scale real-world study, albeit yielding a ‘Me too’ result, is helpful in terms of filling the knowledge gap owing to the absence of head-to-head comparison trials. In addition, it also reflects Chinese rheumatologists’ proactive attitude in international collaborations.

Other rheumatic diseases: IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD) and polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR)

A multicentre RCT (WInS IgG4-related disease [IgG4-RD] trial) led by Peking Union Hospital proved that maintaining low-dose immunosuppressants ± GC reduces relapse rates (12.2% or 14.2% vs 52.1% in withdrawal group), establishing a benchmark for remission maintenance [22]. Building on this foundation, a multicentre RCT by Dr Lingli Dong’s team introduced thalidomide as a ‘zero GC’ maintenance for relapse prevention. In this trial, 57 patients received thalidomide or placebo alongside a 3-month GC tapering. At 12 months, thalidomide reduced relapse rates to 13.8% vs 67.8% with placebo, achieving a 75.8% remission rate (vs 32.1% placebo) and demonstrating a favourable risk-benefit profile [23]. This positions thalidomide as an effective GC-sparing option for relapse-prone IgG4-RD. In another pilot trial led by the team, telitacicept achieved 60% partial remission in refractory IgG4-RD [24], offering a possible B-cell targeted option for this disease, although dwarfed by the late phase III inebilizumab MITIGATE Trial (anti-CD19 B-cell depletion) [25]. Meanwhile, in a single-arm trial for retroperitoneal fibrosis (n = 8), a novel regimen based on in silico analyses repurposing sirolimus (an mTOR inhibitor) combined with prednisone showed preliminary efficacy: reducing fibrotic tissue volume by 50%, decreasing acute inflammatory markers by 70%, and normalising kidney function in most patients [26].

Two trials validated tofacitinib as a GC-sparing agent in PMR. The first phase II single-arm clinical trial with Simon’s two-step design (n = 14) investigating the use of JAKi in patients with active PMR, which has demonstrated rapid symptom relief and sustained low disease activity [27]. The EAST-PMR trial (n = 67), on the other hand, suggested noninferiority of JAKi to prednisone in relapse prevention [28]. As comparison, a newly published baricitinib phase II BACHELOR trial in early PMR was conducted by French colleagues, which was more stringently deployed (randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled) and smartly designed (with a technically GC-free baricitinib-alone arm) [29]. The take-home message is that our Chinese investigators are still in the middle of the learning curve before we can present more ‘better’ and ‘cooler’ studies.

Cell therapy and future directions

Inspired by Dr Georg Schett’s group pioneering advancements in CAR-T therapy for autoimmune diseases [30], China has rapidly emerged as a key contributor to this field, demonstrating innovative ‘Me better’ strategies.

Government-driven initiatives underpin this progress: substantial state investment complements proactive policies including fast-track drug approvals, financial incentives, and infrastructure development (eg, biopharmaceutical industrial parks and cell therapy research centres) [31]. This ecosystem fosters clinical leadership, evidenced by over 1000 CAR-T trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (as of May 2025)—including 106 targeting rheumatic autoimmune diseases (eg, RA, SLE)—solidifying China’s prominent role in CAR-T research and development.

Against this backdrop, TyU19, a universal CD19-targeted CAR-T product derived from healthy donors, was developed and implemented by Dr Huji Xu’s team from Shanghai Changzheng Navy Hospital. TyU19 achieved deep B-cell depletion and sustained remission in a pilot study that enrolled 3 patients with refractory immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (anti-SRP) and diffuse cutaneous systemic sclerosis (dcSSc), all of whom had failed conventional therapies [32]. Remarkably, all patients achieved and maintained drug-free remission by 6 months. This ‘off-the-shelf’ CAR-T technology is offering a more cost-effective with better feasibility approach for difficult-to-treat patients. Along with aforementioned other advances, such as BCMA/CD19 dual-target autologous CAR-T trial in SLE/LN, CD19-targeted plus anti-IL-6/TNFα antibodies-secreting autologous CAR-T in RA, massive endeavours have been made by researchers from China to explore the avenue of cell therapy. Critically, China’s policy-industry-clinical integration—exemplified by tariff exemptions reducing viral vector costs—fuels scalable innovation. However, future success still hinges on conditioning refinement, durability of efficacy, long-term safety, and cost reduction. Furthermore, in light of the dynamic changes in this field, T-cell engager is a rising star which deserves attention from all stakeholders.

An intriguing tight bound between cutting-edge biotech startups and visionary clinicians has been observed in the development of cell therapy, which might set up a model accelerating translational research.

Conclusion

China’s rheumatology landscape has evolved from validation studies to enhanced therapeutic strategies and repurposing applications, which leads to a more appealing skyline, such as innovation in targeted treatments, digital health, and cell therapies. Unfortunately, hardcore de novo innovation is still short in supply. It is noteworthy that regional disparities do exist, as evidenced by the aforementioned contributions, which largely came from a few major cities’ key institutions. Therefore, cultivating the environment for innovation, sustaining investment in translational infrastructure, fostering international partnerships, and embracing AI-driven precision medicine in a sense of equality will be the roadmap to future success—we are on the right track, and the only thing that needs to be done is to keep the momentum going.
