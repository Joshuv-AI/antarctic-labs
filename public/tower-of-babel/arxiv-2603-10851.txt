New Upper Bounds for the Classical Ramsey Numbers R ⁡ ( 4 , 4 , 4 ) R(4,4,4) , R ⁡ ( 3 , 4 , 5 ) R(3,4,5) and R ⁡ ( 3 , 3 , 6 ) R(3,3,6)
Luis Boza

Abstract
The inequality R ⁡ ( k 1 , … , k r ) ≤ 2 − r + ∑ i = 1 r R ⁡ ( k 1 , … , k i − 1 , k i − 1 , k i + 1 , … , k r ) R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})\leq 2-r+\sum_{i=1}^{r}R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i-1},k_{i}-1,k_{i+1},\ldots,k_{r}) is well known, and it is strict whenever the right-hand side and at least one of the terms in the sum are even. Except for two known cases, the best upper bounds for classical Ramsey numbers with at least three colors have so far been obtained from this inequality. In this paper we present new bounds such as R ⁡ ( 4 , 4 , 4 ) ≤ 229 R(4,4,4)\leq 229 , R ⁡ ( 3 , 4 , 5 ) ≤ 157 R(3,4,5)\leq 157 and R ⁡ ( 3 , 3 , 6 ) ≤ 91 R(3,3,6)\leq 91 .

1
Introduction

Let
k_{1},\ldots,k_{r}\geq 2
be integers. The classical multicolor Ramsey number
R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})
is defined as the smallest integer
N
such that for every
r
-coloring
G
of the edges of
K_{N}
there exists
i
such that
G
contains a subgraph isomorphic to
K_{k_{i}}
whose edges all have color
i
.
If
k_{1}=\ldots=k_{r}=k
, it is also denoted by
R_{r}(k):=R(\underbrace{k,\ldots,k}_{r})
.
Clearly, permuting the
k_{i}
does not change the value of the Ramsey number and
R(2,k_{2},\ldots,k_{r})=R(k_{2},\ldots,k_{r})
.

Few exact values are known, among them
R_{2}(4)=18

[
3
]
,
R(3,5)=14

[
3
]
,
R(3,6)=18

[
4
]
,
R(4,5)=25

[
5
]
and
R(3,3,4)=30

[
1
]
.

Let

P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})=2-r+\sum_{i=1}^{r}R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i-1},k_{i}-1,k_{i+1},\ldots,k_{r}).

If
k_{1}=\ldots=k_{r}
, we also write
P_{r}(k_{1})
.
The following general upper bound, implicit in
[
3
]
, is well known:

Theorem 1.1
.

R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})\leq P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})
.
Moreover, the inequality is strict if the right-hand side is even and at least one of the terms in the sum is even.

By repeated application of Theorem
1.1
one obtains the following upper bounds:

R(3,4,4)\leq 77
,
R(3,3,5)\leq 57
,
R(3,4,5)\leq 157
,
R_{3}(4)\leq 230
,
R(3,3,3,4)\leq 149
and
R_{7}(3)\leq 12861
(see
[
6
]
for further details).

For
r\geq 3
, there are only two known upper bounds for
R(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})
that improve those obtained from Theorem
1.1
, namely
R_{4}(3)\leq 62

[
2
]
and
R(3,3,4)=30
.

If
G
is an
r
-coloring and
v\in V(G)
, we denote by
d_{i}(v)
the number of edges incident with
v
that have color
i
. If
G
has
n
vertices, then clearly
\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}(v)=n-1
.

We denote by
N_{i}(v)
the set of the
d_{i}(v)
vertices that are adjacent to
v
by an edge of color
i
, and if
A\subseteq V(G)
we write
G[A]
for the
r
-coloring of the edges of the complete graph on vertex set
A
induced by
G
.

We denote by
{\mathcal{R}}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};n)
the set of
r
-colorings of the edges of
K_{n}
such that for each
i
there is no subgraph of
G
isomorphic to
K_{k_{i}}
whose edges are all of color
i
. Thus
R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})
is the smallest
n
such that
{\mathcal{R}}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};n)=\emptyset
.

If
G\in{\mathcal{R}}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};n)
,
v\in V(G)
and
1\leq i\leq r
, then it is well known that

G[N_{i}(v)]\in{\mathcal{R}}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i-1},k_{i}-1,k_{i+1},\ldots,k_{r};d_{i}(v))
,
since if
G[N_{i}(v)]
contained a subgraph
H
isomorphic to
K_{k_{i}-1}
with all edges of color
i
, then the vertices of
H
together with
v
would induce in
G
a copy of
K_{k_{i}}
whose edges all have color
i
.

As an easy consequence we have:

Lemma 1.2
.

Let
G\in{\mathcal{R}}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};n)
, let
v\in V(G)
, and let
1\leq i_{0}\leq r
. Then:

•

d_{i_{0}}(v)\leq R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-1,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r})-1
.

•

If
n=P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})-1
, then

d_{i_{0}}(v)=R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-1,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r})-1
.
Moreover, the number of edges of
G
with color
i_{0}
is

\frac{(P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})-1)\bigl(R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-1,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r})-1\bigr)}{2}.

Proof.

For every
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
we have

G[N_{i}(v)]\in{\mathcal{R}}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i-1},k_{i}-1,k_{i+1},\ldots,k_{r};d_{i}(v)).

Hence
d_{i}(v)\leq R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i-1},k_{i}-1,k_{i+1},\ldots,k_{r})-1
, and in particular

d_{i_{0}}(v)\leq R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-1,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r})-1.

If
n=P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})-1
, then

\displaystyle P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})-2=\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{i}(v)\leq d_{i_{0}}(v)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq i_{0}\end{subarray}}^{r}(R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i-1},k_{i}-1,k_{i+1},\ldots,k_{r})-1)

\displaystyle=d_{i_{0}}(v)+1-r+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq i_{0}\end{subarray}}^{r}R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i-1},k_{i}-1,k_{i+1},\ldots,k_{r}),\text{ so }

\displaystyle d_{i_{0}}(v)\geq(P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})-2)-\left(1-r+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq i_{0}\end{subarray}}^{r}R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i-1},k_{i}-1,k_{i+1},\ldots,k_{r})\right)

\displaystyle=R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-1,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r})-1.

Since
G
is regular of degree
R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-1,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r})-1
in color
i_{0}
and has
P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})-1
vertices, the number of edges of color
i_{0}
is

\frac{(P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})-1)\bigl(R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-1,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r})-1\bigr)}{2}.

∎

Since

(P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})-1)\bigl(R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-1,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r})-1\bigr)/2

must be an integer,
P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})
and
R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-1,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r})
cannot be simultaneously even, which yields Theorem
1.1
.

In this paper we obtain new upper bounds for classical Ramsey numbers with
r\geq 3
that are not derived from Theorem
1.1
. Among them are
R_{3}(4)\leq 229
,
R(3,4,5)\leq 157
and
R(3,3,6)\leq 91
.

2
Main results

The next result provides a criterion that allows one to improve the general bound of Theorem
1.1
in certain modular cases.

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume that

1.

P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
;

2.

there exists
i_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
with
k_{i_{0}}\geq 4
such that

R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-1,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r})=P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-1,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r})\not\equiv 1\!\!\!\!\pmod{3};

3.

and

R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-2,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r})\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.

Then

R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})\leq P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})-1.

Proof.

Assume that
R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})=P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})
. Let

G\in{\mathcal{R}}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r};P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})-1)
and let
v\in V(G)
. Since

G[N_{i_{0}}(v)]\in{\mathcal{R}}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-1,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r};d_{i_{0}}(v))

and, by Lemma
1.2
,
G[N_{i_{0}}(v)]
has

M:=\frac{\bigl(P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-1,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r})-1\bigr)\bigl(R(k_{1},\ldots,k_{i_{0}-1},k_{i_{0}}-2,k_{i_{0}+1},\ldots,k_{r})-1\bigr)}{2}

edges of color
i_{0}
, it follows that
v
lies in
M
triangles of color
i_{0}
.
Therefore, summing over all vertices yields
(P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})-1)M
.
Each triangle is counted three times, so this sum must be a multiple of
3
.

By the hypotheses, neither
P(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r})-1
nor any of the two factors defining
M

is a multiple of
3
, which is a contradiction. Hence the result follows.
∎

As applications of Theorem
2.1
we obtain the following improvements:

Theorem 2.2
.

R_{3}(4)\leq 229.

Proof.

Let
r=3
,
k_{1}=4
,
k_{2}=4
,
k_{3}=4
and
i_{0}=1
. If
P_{3}(4)\leq 229
, then the result follows from Theorem
1.1
. We may assume that
P_{3}(4)\geq 230
. Since
R_{2}(4)=18\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
,
P(3,4,4)=2-r+R(4,4)+2R(3,3,4)=2-3+18+2\cdot 30=77
,
while
230\leq P_{3}(4)=2-3+3R(3,4,4)
, it follows that
R(3,4,4)\geq 77
, hence

R(3,4,4)=P(3,4,4)=77\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.

Therefore
P_{3}(4)=2-3+3\cdot 77=230\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
, and Theorem
2.1
yields the claim.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

R(3,4,5)\leq 157
.

Proof.

Let
r=3
,
k_{1}=3
,
k_{2}=4
,
k_{3}=5
and
i_{0}=2
. If
P(3,4,5)\leq 157
, then the result follows from Theorem
1.1
. We may assume that
P(3,4,5)\geq 158
. Since
R(3,5)=14\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
,

P(3,3,5)=2-r+2R(3,5)+R(3,3,4)=2-3+2\cdot 14+30=57
, and

158\leq P(3,4,5)=2-3+R(4,5)+R(3,3,5)+R(3,4,4)\leq 2-3+25+R(3,3,5)+77,

we have
R(3,3,5)\geq 57
, hence
R(3,3,5)=P(3,3,5)=57\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.

Moreover,
R(3,4,4)\geq 158-(2-3+25+57)=77
, so
R(3,4,4)=77
. Thus
P(3,4,5)=2-3+25+57+77=158\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
, and Theorem
2.1
gives the result.
∎

Theorem 2.4
.

R(3,3,6)\leq 91
.

Proof.

Let
r=3
,
k_{1}=3
,
k_{2}=3
,
k_{3}=6
and
i_{0}=3
. If
P(3,3,6)\leq 91
, then the result follows from Theorem
1.1
. We may assume that
P(3,3,6)\geq 92
. Since
R(3,3,4)=30\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
,
P(3,3,5)=2-r+2R(3,5)+R(3,3,4)=2-3+2\cdot 14+30=57
,
and

92\leq P(3,3,6)=2-3+2R(3,6)+R(3,3,5)=2-3+2\cdot 18+R(3,3,5),

we obtain
R(3,3,5)\geq 57
, hence
R(3,3,5)=P(3,3,5)=57\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.

Therefore
P(3,3,6)=2-3+2\cdot 18+57=92\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
, and Theorem
2.1
yields the claim.
∎

Theorem 2.5
.

R(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{10},4)\leq 608152553
.

Proof.

Let
r=11
,
k_{1}=\ldots=k_{10}=3
,
k_{11}=4
and
i_{0}=11
. If
P(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{10},4)\leq 608152553
, then the result follows from Theorem
1.1
. We may assume that
P(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{10},4)\geq 608152554
.

From
R_{7}(3)\leq 12861
, by repeated application of Theorem
1.1
we obtain

R_{8}(3)\leq 102882
,
R_{9}(3)\leq 925931
,
R_{10}(3)\leq P_{10}(3)\leq 9259302
and
R_{11}(3)\leq P_{11}(3)\leq 101852313
.

From
R(3,3,3,4)\leq 149
, by repeated application of Theorem
1.1
we obtain

\displaystyle R(3,3,3,3,4)\leq 900,\;R(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{5},4)\leq 6333,\;R(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{6},4)\leq 50854,

\displaystyle R(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{7},4)\leq 458853,\,R(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{8},4)\leq 4596748,\,R(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{9},4)\leq 50630025,

\displaystyle\text{and }R(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{10},4)\leq P(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{10},4)\leq 608152554.

Since we are assuming
P(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{10},4)\geq 608152554
, we deduce

P(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{10},4)=608152554\not\equiv 1\!\!\!\!\pmod{3}.

If
R_{11}(3)\leq 101852312
, then
P(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{10},4)=2-11+10R(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{9},4)+R_{11}(3)\leq 2-11+10\cdot 50630025+101852312=608152553
, which is a contradiction. Hence we may assume that
R_{11}(3)=P_{11}(3)=101852313\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.

If
R_{10}(3)\leq 9259301
, then
P_{11}(3)=2-11+11R_{10}(3)\leq 2-11+11\cdot 9259301=101852302
,
a contradiction. Therefore we may assume that
R_{10}(3)=9259302\not\equiv 1\pmod{3}
, and Theorem
2.1
yields the result.
∎

Finally, we note that any further improvement obtained from Theorem
2.1
is a consequence of Theorems
2.2
–
2.5
. In particular, for
r\leq 10
no improved upper bounds are obtained for
R(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{r-1},5)
and
R(\underbrace{3,\ldots,3}_{r-2},4,4)
beyond those derived from Theorem
1.1
.
