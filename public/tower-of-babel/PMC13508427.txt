Molecular characteristics and clinicopathology of CTNNB1-mutated endometrial cancer: Implications for fertility-sparing management in young women
Authors: Wen-Qi Li, Hai-Xia Wu, Han-Bo Li, Guang-Nan Shuai, Qing Zhang, Yan Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe incidence rate of endometrial cancer (EC) is increasing among young women who require fertility preservation. The identification of molecular biomarkers can improve clinical outcomes in these patients. This study evaluated the role of CTNNB1 mutations in EC and whether the CTNNB1 gene can serve as a molecular marker in fertility-sparing management.MethodsRetrospective cohorts of patients with pathologically confirmed EC were included in the study. The molecular landscape and TCGA-based molecular classification were analyzed through targeted next-generation sequencing and immunohistochemistry. The clinicopathological and molecular characteristics of CTNNB1-mutated and CTNNB1-wild-type patients were assessed. For young patient who received fertility-sparing treatment (FST), the responses to progestin treatment were evaluated.Results CTNNB1 mutations were significantly more common in the age group ≤ 40 years than in the age group > 40 years, indicating that CTNNB1 had a significant role in young patients with EC seeking fertility preservation. Most cases carrying CTNNB1 mutations were in the no specific molecular profile group. The most frequent CTNNB1 hotspot mutations were Ser37Phe on exon3 (16.13%). Moreover, CTNNB1 mutations were significantly associated with higher rates of endometrioid EC (p = 0.0001) and earlier stages (FIGO I or II) than the absence of mutations (p = 0.0457). Nonetheless, there were no significant differences in other pathological risk factors between the mutant and wild-type groups. The immunohistochemical expression of β-catenin can be used as a surrogate for CTNNB1 mutation status; nonetheless, specificity was associated with the frequency of CTNNB1 mutations. Patients with CTNNB1 mutations showed poor response to progestin treatment, possibly because of the reduced expression of progesterone receptors since the nuclear expression of β-catenin in the endometrium was negatively correlated with progesterone receptor expression.ConclusionsCTNNB1 status in EC can help predict the response to fertility-sparing treatment, allowing early stratification and risk assignment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16370-y.

Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) is a common gynecologic cancer. The incidence of EC in women of reproductive age is increasing, and 4% of cases occur in women younger than 40 years [1]. Although the primary treatment of EC is hysterectomy, continuous progestin therapy may be considered for patients who wish to preserve fertility [2, 3]. Nonetheless, the complete remission rate of progestin treatment in patients with atypical endometrial hyperplasia and early-stage EC is 66–74% and 49–72%, respectively [4]. However, approximately 30% of individuals are insensitive to progestin therapy, which is also known as progestin resistance [5].

Historically, the criteria for fertility-sparing treatment (FST) has been based on histological and imaging assessments in patients with early-stage of endometrioid endometrial carcinoma (EEC) [6, 7]. Advances in molecular characterization based on data from 2013 The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) improved the clinical and prognostic classification of EC [8]. TCGA identified four molecular subgroups of EC: POLEultramutated, microsatellite instability hypermutated, low-copy-number, and high-copy-number. Although molecular classification can improve prognosis and select patients for FST [9], little is known about the molecular characteristics of EC in FST.

CTNNB1is a commonly mutated gene in EC, with its mutations occurring in approximately 20–25% of tumors [10]. The CTNNB1gene encodes β-catenin, crucial for Wnt/β-catenin signaling and gene transcription regulation [11]. The clinicopathological characteristics of patients with CTNNB1mutations and the effect of these mutations on prognosis have gained attention in recent years because of the widespread use of molecular classifications in EC [12, 13]. CTNNB1 mutations are associated with younger age and a lower risk of recurrence of EC. However, compared with other low-grade tumors, CTNNB1mutations are associated with worse outcomes, including higher recurrence and lower overall survival, particularly in patients with exon 3 mutations [12]. These data indicate that CTNNB1 is a potential therapeutic target and a molecular marker for cancer prognosis. However, the effect of CTNNB1 mutations on the response to hormone therapy in young patients with early-stage EC who require FST is unclear.

This study assessed the role of CTNNB1 in young patients with EC and the ability of CTNNB1 to predict the response to progestin therapy.

Materials and methods

Cohort selection

Cohorts of patients with EC was recruited from the Tianjin Central Hospital of Obstetrics and Gynecology (Tianjin, China) from 2018 to 2023. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tianjin Central Hospital of Obstetrics and Gynecology. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. In Cohort 1, patients were histologically diagnosed with EC through hysteroscopic biopsy or hysterectomy, and they underwent molecular characterization using a targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) panel. Patients who presented to our institution at the time of recurrence or with a concurrent advanced malignancy were excluded. In Cohort 2, we included patients under 40 years of age who were histologically diagnosed with EC through hysteroscopic biopsy. These patients simultaneously met the eligibility criteria for conservative treatment, which included an endometrioid histotype, tumor grades of 1 or 2, and the absence of extrauterine metastases, lymphovascular space invasion, myometrial invasion, or cervical invasion. Patients who did not have treatment planning at our institution or who lacked sufficient follow-up were excluded from the study. The CONSORT diagram summarizing case selection is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. All cases included were reviewed by at least two gynecologic pathologists. Clinicopathological information was obtained by reviewing electronic medical records. Tumors were classified according to standard histopathological criteria following the 2020 World Health Organization classification of tumors [14]. Data on tumor histological type, myometrial invasion depth, lymphatic/vascular space invasion, and postoperative adjuvant therapies were collected and analyzed. The tumor stage was determined using the 2023 International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) Staging System [15].

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue sections were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and serially sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. The immunohistochemical expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PgR), p53, MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, MSH6, and β-catenin was evaluated in all tumor samples. All antibodies were purchased from Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Tissues with nuclear staining for ER, PgR, MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6 were scored as positive. The cases in which MLH1-PMS2 or MSH2-MSH6 was negative were considered to have mismatch repair deficiency (MMRd). Abnormal p53 expression (p53abn) was defined as p53 nuclear overexpression, the complete absence of nuclear expression, and cytoplasmic overexpression. Nuclear staining for β-catenin was considered positive, and membrane/cytoplasmic staining was considered “normal.”

The immunostaining of ER and PgR was graded semiquantitatively by measuring the percentage and intensity of staining of tumor cells. H-scores were determined by multiplying the percentage of positively stained cells by staining intensity. Staining intensity was assessed using a 4-grade scale (0, none; 1, weak; 2, moderate; and 3, strong). Immunohistochemistry assessment was performed by two trained gynecologic pathologists.

tNGS panel

Firstly, wax blocks containing tumor cells exceeding 20% as determined by hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining were selected. The areas of interest were marked on control HE slides and manually dissected under a microscope using a sterile blade by a pathologist. DNA was extracted from two to four formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded 10-µm-thick sections using the Quick Extraction Kit (Tiangen, China). Samples then were analyzed using a customized tNGS panel of 26 genes. Only nucleotide variations present in more than 1% of the reads were considered for mutational calls. Variant allele frequency is also known as mutation frequency. The obtained sequences were analyzed using VarDict (implemented in Perl) and DeteX version 3.0. All gene mutations were confirmed using IGV software version 2.11.4.

Molecular classification

We used a TCGA-based molecular classification. This method uses IHC parameters (MMR and p53) and NGS to identify pathogenic POLE mutations. Tumors were classified into four molecular subtypes: (1) POLE ultramutated (POLEmut), characterized by the presence of a mutation in the POLE hotspot exonuclease domain; (2) Mismatch repair deficiency (MMRd), defined as microsatellite instability hypermutated (MSI-H) based on NGS or MMR deficiency indicated by IHC; (3) p53abn, identified by the presence of a pathogenic TP53 genetic alteration or abnormal p53 expression indicated by IHC; (4) no specific molecular profile (NSMP), if any of the defining features of the other subtypes were lacking.

Evaluation of responses to progestin treatment and follow-up

The responses to progestin treatment were defined as follows: complete response (CR), absence of hyperplastic or cancerous lesions; partial response (PR), presence of residual lesions with the degeneration and atrophy of endometrial glands; stable disease (SD), presence of residual lesion without degeneration or atrophy of endometrial glands; progressive disease (PD), progression to a lesion of higher grade or clinically progressive disease, including myometrial invasion, extrauterine disease, or lymph node metastasis. Recurrence was defined as atypical hyperplasia or carcinoma developed after achieving CR. Treatment responses before CR were evaluated using dilation and curettage or hysteroscopic biopsy every 6 months during the initial treatment. All cases were followed until December 2024.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 6 (San Diego, CA). Molecular and clinicopathologic features were compared using χ2 statistics or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. H-scores were compared using the t-test for continuous variables. Correlation analysis was performed using Spearman correlation analysis. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of less than 0.05.

Results

Molecular landscape of ECs

We performed tNGS on tumor tissues and peripheral blood samples from 130 patients with EC. The tNGS panel contained 26 genes closely related to EC diagnosis and treatment. Gene mutation sites, frequency of mutations, patient age, FIGO stage, histological types, and molecular classifiers are shown in Fig. 1A. The five most common mutated genes were PTEN (75.38%), PIK3CA (70.77%), KRAS (38.46%), CTNNB1 (36.92%), and BRCA2 (36.92%).

Clinicopathological characteristics of CTNNB1-mutated EC

The clinical and pathological characteristics of CTNNB1-mutated EC are summarized in Table 1. The patients in the CTNNB1-mutant group (n = 48, 36.92%) were younger than the WT group (40.5 vs. 55.5 years; p < 0.0001). The histological types and molecular classifiers differed significantly between the two groups. Most cases (97.92%) in the mutated group were EECs, of which 42/47 (89.36%) were grades 1–2. In addition, 12 patients (12/44, 27.27%) met the criteria for FST and received progestin treatment. While in the WT group, 70.73% were EECs, and the other were non-EECs (24/82, 29.27%), including clear cell carcinoma and mixed cell carcinoma, etc.

In patients who received surgery, there were no significant between-group differences in pathological features, including myometrial invasion depth, lymphatic/vascular space invasion, and lymph node metastasis (Table 2). CTNNB1 mutations were associated with earlier stages (FIGO I or II) than the absence of mutations (p = 0.0457). Nonetheless, there were no significant differences in the number of patients who required adjuvant therapy.

Correlation between β-catenin IHC and CTNNB1 mutations

The CTNNB1 gene encodes the protein β-catenin, which, in the context of mutations in exon 3 of CTNNB1or activation of the canonical Wnt pathway, is translocated from the cell membrane to the cytoplasm and nucleus, where it subsequently activates downstream transcriptional activity [16]. β-catenin protein expression was evaluated by immunohistochemistry in all tumor samples, with two IHC expression patterns, membrane positive or nucleus positive, are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2A. Then the consistency of nuclear expression of β-catenin with CTNNB1 mutations was evaluated. The nuclear expression of β-catenin was positive in 77.08% (37/48) of tumors carrying CTNNB1 mutations (sensitivity of 77.08%). The specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were 90.24%, 82.22%, and 87.06%, respectively (Table 3, left panel).

β-catenin nuclear expression was negative in 11 samples with CTNNB1 mutations. Of these, five samples with low mutation frequency of CTNNB1 (less than 5%) were identified. The low frequency of mutations may lead to negative immunohistochemical staining. Therefore, we examined β-catenin staining patterns in 38 samples whose frequency of CTNNB1 mutations was higher than 5%. Nuclear and membrane staining was positive in 32 and 6 cases, respectively, corresponding to a sensitivity of 84.21% and a specificity of 85.87% (Table 3, right panel).

Correlation between β-catenin and hormone receptor expression

Based on above findings, a hypothesis was proposed that CTNNB1 mutations may be involved in the regulation of female hormones in young patients, thereby could be contributing to carcinogenesis. To investigate whether CTNNB1 mutations are linked to hormone receptor expression, ER and PgR expression was measured in the endometrium of patients younger than 40 years. Among the 35 patients in this age group, 24 carried CTNNB1 mutations. There were no significant differences in H-scores of ER and PgR staining between the mutated and WT groups (Fig. 3A). While in the patients received FST, ER and PgR expression were also not significantly correlated with CTNNB1status (Supplementary Fig. 2B). However, in daily practice, pathologic diagnosis of EC presents challenges due to the complex morphological changes observed in endometrial glands. This variability suggests that the sensitivity to progestin treatment may differ spatially throughout the endometrium [17]. Considering the spatial heterogeneity in IHC staining, we observed a decrease in epithelial PgR expression decreased in β-catenin nuclear positive regions (with CTNNB1 mutations) but remained unchanged in adjacent normal regions (Fig. 3B). While the epithelial ER expression was retained both in mutated and normal regions.

To assess the correlation between β-catenin and PgR nuclear expression, we randomly selected 10 high-power fields (400×) on the same slide. The number of cells with nuclear or membrane expression of β-catenin and the number of PgR-positive or PgR-negative cells in each field were counted. Then, correlation analysis was performed. There was a significant positive correlation between the percentage of cells with positive nuclear expression of β-catenin and the percentage of PgR-negative cells (R = 0.709, p = 0.028) (Fig. 3C), suggesting that CTNNB1 mutations correlate with progestin resistance.

Hormonal therapy and clinical outcomes

To elucidate the relationship of CTNNB1 mutations with progestin resistance, we collected 18 EEC patients who received progestin treatment and had complete clinical data and follow-up records. The median age in this cohort was 32 years (range, 22–38 years), and the median follow-up time was 27.5 months (range, 13–75 months). The characteristics and oncologic outcomes of patients are presented in Table 4. Two patients in each group were under treatment after the follow-up period and did not reach the endpoint. In the CTNNB1-mutated group (n = 11), total follow-up time ranged from 13 to 75 months. At 6 months from the start of treatment, three cases showed treatment failure in the initial response (SD) and eight cases of PR. Nine cases achieved CR, and the median time to CR was 16 months (range, 8–46 months). Two cases (22.2%) recurred after CR (at 9 and 18 months). In the CTNNB1 WT group (n = 7), total follow-up time ranged from 18 to 51 months. Five cases achieved CR, and the median time to CR was 7 months (range, 4–22 months). Patients with CTNNB1 mutations showed poor response to progestin treatment, and the rate of CR 6 months after treatment was lower in CTNNB1-mutated patients than in WT patients (0 vs. 28.6%). Moreover, the median time to CR was significantly longer in the mutated group than in the WT group.

Discussion

This study evaluated the clinical relevance of genetic mutations in EC, especially in patients younger than 40 years. The frequency of CTNNB1 mutations was significantly higher in the age group ≤ 40 years, indicating that CTNNB1 has clinical value in young patients with EC undergoing FST. CTNNB1 status was associated with poor response to progestin therapy. Moreover, β-catenin could be used as a predictive biomarker of response to progestin treatment.

CTNNB1 mutations are common in EC. TCGA analysis revealed that tumors with CTNNB1mutations were more common in the microsatellite-stable and low-copy-number groups [8]. Similarly, in our cohort, CTNNB1 mutations occurred in 70.83% of patients with NSMP, especially those younger than 40 years. Furthermore, the incidence of KRAS mutations was lower in patients with CTNNB1 mutations.

CTNNB1 mutations were present in approximately 37% of EC cases and 45% of EEC cases. The most common mutations in EC occurred in CTNNB1exon 3, which encodes the N terminal (ubiquitin-binding) region of β-catenin. Exon 3 mutations inhibit the ubiquitination of β-catenin and its degradation by the proteasome, causing β-catenin accumulation in the cytoplasm and activation of the Wnt signaling pathway [16, 18]. These mutations are more common in three phosphorylation sites in GSK-3β (S33, S37, and T41) and casein kinase 1α (S45) and in the sites of interaction with F-box/WD repeat-containing protein 1 A (D32 and G34) [10]. In our study, 90.32% of mutations were located on exon 3, and the amino acid most commonly mutated was Ser37 (32.14%).

ECs with CTNNB1mutations are more common in younger patients and are more likely to exhibit favorable clinicopathologic features, such as low-grade endometrioid histology, myometrial invasion of < 50%, and absence of lymphatic/vascular space invasion [12, 19]. However, compared with other low-grade low-stage tumors, CTNNB1mutations, especially those in exon 3, are associated with higher recurrence and lower overall survival [12, 20]. CTNNB1exon 3 mutations had an independent prognostic value in the NSMP group [21]. Thus, cases with CTNNB1mutations may constitute the fifth molecular group of EC [22]. We did not determine recurrence and overall survival rates because of the short follow-up time and the small number of cases. Notwithstanding, there was no significant increase in pathological risk factors in the mutant group, such as deep myometrial invasion and lymphatic/vascular space invasion.

β-catenin IHC is a surrogate for CTNNB1 mutation status. In a cohort with low-grade, early-stage EEC, the sensitivity and specificity of β-catenin IHC for CTNNB1exon 3 mutations were 91% and 89% [20]. Another study found that the sensitivity and specificity of β-catenin IHC for predicting CTNNB1mutations in EC were 100% and 85% [11]. Consistent with these studies, our findings demonstrated that β-catenin IHC had a high specificity and PPV for CTNNB1 mutations. In addition, most cases with CTNNB1 mutations and positive nuclear expression of β-catenin had positive staining in ≤ 10% of tumor cell nuclei. The focal nature of nuclear staining and the low mutation rates can potentially increase the false-negative rate in small biopsy specimens. Therefore, the nuclear expression β-catenin should be analyzed with caution.

Conservative management is based on treatment with progestins, including medroxyprogesterone acetate or megestrol acetate, for at least 6 months [1]. The selection of patients eligible for FST is based on imaging (ultrasound or MRI) and endometrial biopsy according to current guidelines. Molecular classification can identify patients who may benefit from hormone therapy [23]. The Proactive Molecular Risk Classifier for Endometrial Cancer (ProMisE) reliably evaluates the response to FST in young patients [9, 24]. Chung et al. [25] used the ProMisE in early-stage EC patients receiving FST and found that the response rate was 44.4% and 82.2% in cancers with MMRd and NSMP, respectively. Consistent with this finding, Raffone et al. [26] reported that the recurrence rate was significantly higher in patients with MMRd tumors than in those with mismatch repair proficient tumors. Puechl et al. [27] showed that the prognosis of patients with p53 mutations was the worst, with 50% of patients requiring radical surgery after progesterone treatment. These results suggest that MMRd and p53-mutant tumors are associated with worse outcomes of FST. Nonetheless, it is difficult to assess the prognostic value of POLE mutations in patients undergoing FST because of the small number of cases.

CTNNB1mutations serve as molecular classifiers of EC, especially in young patients. Studies showed that the increased nuclear expression of β-catenin after progestin therapy was significantly associated with disease progression [28]. However, in our study, the nuclear expression of β-catenin varied throughout the endometrium, and the complex and diverse pathological changes in the endometrium during therapy limit comparing the percentage of positively stained cells. Therefore, it is unknown whether the percentage of cells positively stained for β-catenin can predict the response to FST.

Treatment failure in some patients is due to progestin resistance. Nonetheless, underlying mechanisms are unclear [5]. Progestin suppresses cancer growth by binding to the PgR and activating PgR signaling pathways. The downregulation of PgR during continuous progestin therapy can lead to progesterone unresponsiveness [29]. Our study demonstrated that the positive nuclear expression of β-catenin was negatively correlated with the positive nuclear expression of PgR, suggesting that CTNNB1 mutations were associated with progestin resistance. Patients with CTNNB1 mutations showed poor response to progestin treatment and longer time were needed to achieve CR.

This study has limitations. First, the median follow-up of patients who received progestin therapy was short. Second, referral and clinical biases in patients who underwent sequencing may exist. Third, the study design was retrospective, and the sample size was small. Therefore, larger studies are needed to assess the ability of CTNNB1 status to predict hormone therapy response and the outcomes of FST.

Molecular classification is changing the risk profile assessment for patients with EC. Nonetheless, further studies are needed to identify molecular markers for EC in young patients who undergo conservative treatment.
