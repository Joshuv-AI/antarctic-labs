From Surgical Innovation to Population Impact: Robot‐Assisted Radical Prostatectomy and the Evolution of Surgical Public Health in Brazil's Unified Health System
Authors: Leonardo Oliveira Reis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBrazil's Unified Health System (Sistema Único de Saúde, SUS) represents one of the world's largest universal health systems and provides a unique real‐world platform for integrating prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and survivorship across the cancer continuum. While One Health frameworks have traditionally focused on infectious diseases, environmental exposures, and planetary health, their application to surgical oncology and health‐system innovation remains insufficiently explored. This article proposes that the SUS offers a valuable model for operationalizing One Health and population health principles in prostate cancer care by connecting environmental, social, biological, and healthcare system determinants across the patient journey. The recent incorporation of robot‐assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) into the SUS represents a critical milestone in this evolution. Beyond the adoption of an advanced surgical technology, this initiative illustrates the capacity of a universal health system to evaluate, implement, and monitor complex innovations while balancing effectiveness, equity, sustainability, and population‐level impact. The Brazilian experience highlights the emergence of a learning health system in which real‐world evidence, implementation science, surgical quality assessment, and health policy interact to guide innovation at the national scale. In this context, RARP serves as a case study of how technological advances can be integrated into publicly funded healthcare while generating evidence relevant to diverse populations and healthcare settings. By bridging concepts from Surgical Public Health, population health, health‐systems science, implementation science, and One Health, the SUS provides a distinctive framework for understanding how surgical innovation can contribute to resilient and equitable cancer care. As translational oncology increasingly incorporates environmental, societal, and biological determinants of disease, Brazil offers important lessons on the governance, evaluation, and dissemination of high‐complexity surgical technologies within universal health systems. The insights emerging from this experience may help inform future strategies to optimize prostate cancer care and strengthen health‐system resilience globally.Keywords: Brazil's Unified Health System, One Health Model, radical prostatectomy, real‐world, Surgical Public Health

Surgical Public Health is the field dedicated to optimizing the population‐level delivery, accessibility, quality, equity, safety, effectiveness, and sustainability of surgical care through evidence‐based policy, health‐system design, and continuous learning. Historically, public health focused on vaccination, sanitation, infectious disease, and prevention. Modern public health increasingly recognizes surgery as an essential health service and a determinant of population health.

The Brazilian experience is explored as a resilient and maturing model of “Surgical Public Health,” where innovation, equity, implementation science, health technology assessment, and outcomes research converge within a universal health system. Public health, population health, and health‐systems science focus primarily on people and healthcare delivery, whereas One Health explicitly recognizes that human health is interconnected with animal health and environmental systems.

Prostate cancer outcomes are shaped not only by genes, physicians, hospitals, and policies but also by environmental exposures, ecosystem conditions, occupational risks, companion animal health, and broader human–environment interactions. One Health offers a broader framework for understanding the upstream determinants of prostate cancer risk, outcomes, and disparities.

From Epidemiological Transition to Surgical Demand

The global shift toward noncommunicable diseases has redefined priorities in urologic oncology. Prostate cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies in men and a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide, with persistent disparities across regions and populations [1]. Although prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) testing has contributed to earlier detection of prostate cancer, its role in population‐based screening remains controversial because of concerns regarding overdiagnosis and overtreatment. Current recommendations emphasize shared decision‐making and individualized risk assessment rather than universal screening approaches. Therefore, efforts to improve prostate cancer outcomes should be understood within a broader framework that includes timely diagnosis, risk stratification, equitable access to treatment, and long‐term survivorship care [2].

Brazil faces a substantial and growing prostate cancer burden, reflecting demographic aging, regional disparities in healthcare access, and variations in diagnostic capacity. Prostate cancer is the most commonly diagnosed non‐cutaneous malignancy among Brazilian men and remains a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality. Despite improvements in diagnosis and treatment, significant geographic and socioeconomic inequalities persist, with disparities observed in access to specialized care, advanced diagnostics, and definitive treatment [3]. These challenges underscore the importance of evaluating strategies that can improve both the quality and equity of prostate cancer care within a universal health system.

Within this context, health‐system design becomes a determinant of oncological outcomes. Unlike fragmented systems, Brazil's Unified Health System (SUS) provides universal access across the full continuum of care, from primary prevention to tertiary surgical intervention, creating a unique platform to address inequities in prostate cancer management at scale.

SUS as an Integrated Platform for Prostate Cancer Care

The SUS integrates community‐based primary care, diagnostic pathways, and specialized oncological services within a coordinated national framework. The Family Health Strategy functions as the entry point, facilitating risk identification, PSA‐based screening pathways, and referral to secondary and tertiary care. This structure enables earlier detection and more consistent linkage to treatment compared with opportunistic models. Importantly, the SUS incorporates digital health infrastructure (e‐SUS) and population‐level surveillance systems that allow longitudinal tracking of patients across the care continuum. For prostate cancer, this creates opportunities to evaluate real‐world patterns of diagnosis, staging, treatment selection, and outcomes in a manner not feasible in fragmented healthcare systems [4].

From a One Health perspective, this integration extends beyond clinical care, incorporating social determinants, environmental exposures, and population‐level risk factors into a unified framework that shapes cancer outcomes [5]. Reframing One Health in Cancer

One Health has traditionally been applied to infectious diseases, zoonoses, and environmental health. However, its underlying principle, interconnected determinants of health, has direct relevance to surgical oncology. Outcomes in prostate cancer are influenced not only by tumor biology and surgical technique but also by access to care, socioeconomic status, environmental exposures, and health‐system organization [6, 7].

One Health aligns with efforts to reduce disparities in cancer care by recognizing that health outcomes are shaped not only by biological factors and healthcare access but also by broader environmental and societal conditions. In prostate cancer, disparities in screening, diagnosis, treatment access, and survivorship may reflect complex interactions among social determinants, occupational exposures, environmental risks, and health‐system performance. Viewed through this lens, One Health complements public health, population health, and health‐systems science by expanding the focus beyond healthcare delivery to encompass the environmental, occupational, ecological, and comparative determinants of cancer across the continuum from prevention to survivorship.

In this context, surgical care becomes a population health intervention. Access to high‐quality radical prostatectomy, appropriate patient selection, and perioperative optimization are integral to reducing mortality and morbidity at scale. The SUS provides a framework for systematically aligning these elements.

Surgical Public Health and the Adoption of Robot‐Assisted Radical Prostatectomy

Robot‐assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) has become the dominant surgical approach for localized prostate cancer in high‐income settings, associated with reduced blood loss, shorter hospital stay, and improved short‐term functional recovery. However, the extent to which these benefits translate into measurable improvements in population‐level outcomes, access to care, and health equity remains to be determined, and access to RARP remains uneven globally, often restricted by cost, infrastructure, and workforce limitations [8].

The formal incorporation of RARP into the SUS (Portaria SECTICS/MS 72, 2025), following national health technology assessment, represents a landmark in Surgical Public Health. This policy decision reflects a shift from selective availability toward structured, equitable integration of advanced surgical technology within a universal system [9], which poses significant implementation challenges that warrant careful consideration. Successful deployment requires substantial investments in infrastructure, equipment acquisition and maintenance, workforce training, credentialing, and long‐term quality monitoring. Regional disparities in healthcare resources may further influence access to robotic platforms, raising concerns about the equitable distribution of benefits across geographically diverse populations. In addition, the allocation of resources to high‐cost technologies must be evaluated within the broader context of competing healthcare priorities and potential opportunity costs in a publicly funded system.

From a surgical perspective, this development raises key questions regarding patient selection, volume–outcome relationships, and system‐level implementation. Centralization of care, surgeon experience, and institutional volume remain critical determinants of oncological and functional outcomes. The SUS, with its tiered network of referral centers, offers a potential model for balancing centralization with geographic accessibility [10].

Therefore, claims of enhanced equity and accessibility should be interpreted cautiously and supported by ongoing real‐world evidence. In this regard, the incorporation of RARP into the SUS should be viewed not as the conclusion of a policy process but as the beginning of a continuous learning cycle involving implementation, evaluation, quality improvement, and reassessment of value within the health system.

Real‐World Evidence and Surgical Quality at Scale

One of the most significant implications of integrating RARP into the SUS is the capacity for large‐scale real‐world evidence generation. With millions of patients managed within a unified system, Brazil can develop comprehensive datasets capturing perioperative outcomes, oncological control, functional recovery, and long‐term survival. Such data are essential for addressing persistent uncertainties in prostate cancer surgery, including: comparative effectiveness of robotic versus open and laparoscopic approaches; impact of surgical volume and learning curves on outcomes; functional outcomes (continence, erectile function) in diverse populations; and cost‐effectiveness in middle‐income settings [11].

This positions the SUS as a potential global reference for pragmatic, population‐based evaluation of surgical technologies, complementing evidence derived from high‐income, highly selected cohorts.

Equity, Access, and Oncological Outcomes

Disparities in prostate cancer outcomes are closely linked to differences in access to early diagnosis and definitive treatment. Evidence from the United States demonstrates persistent racial and socioeconomic gaps in both treatment utilization and survival [12]. Similar patterns are observed in middle‐income countries, often driven by geographic and structural barriers.

The integration of RARP into a universal system could mitigate these disparities by standardizing access to high‐quality surgical care. However, this potential will depend on effective implementation strategies, including: structured training programs to expand the robotic surgery workforce; regional distribution of high‐volume centers; integration with early detection pathways to avoid stage migration bias; and continuous monitoring of equity indicators [13]. Without such measures, technological adoption risks reinforcing existing inequities rather than reducing them [10].

Balancing Innovation With Sustainability

The incorporation of high‐cost technologies into publicly funded systems necessitates careful evaluation of cost‐effectiveness and opportunity cost. While RARP offers clinical advantages, its long‐term value depends on appropriate patient selection, efficient utilization, and integration within broader care pathways. Health technology assessment frameworks, such as those employed by CONITEC, are critical in this process [9]. They ensure that decisions are informed by clinical evidence, economic evaluation, and societal values. For urologists, this underscores the importance of engaging with policy processes and contributing high‐quality data to inform decision‐making.

While RARP may offer clinical and operational advantages, robotic platforms are associated with substantial resource consumption, including energy use, disposable instruments, complex supply chains, and increased healthcare waste. As healthcare systems increasingly pursue sustainability goals, evaluating the environmental footprint of advanced surgical technologies becomes an important component of responsible innovation. Incorporating environmental metrics alongside clinical, economic, and equity outcomes would strengthen future assessments of RARP and align surgical decision‐making with the broader One Health objective of promoting human health while preserving environmental sustainability [10].

Implications for Global Practice

The Brazilian experience provides several insights: (1) Integration over fragmentation: Coordinated care pathways may enhance consistency in prostate cancer management. (2) Real‐world data as a complement to trials: Large‐scale observational data can inform practice in diverse populations. (3) Equity as a quality metric: Access to advanced surgical care should be considered a core outcome. (4) System‐level thinking: Surgical innovation must be evaluated within the broader health‐system context.

These considerations are increasingly relevant as global systems also face pressures related to aging populations, rising costs, and the need for sustainable innovation.

Conclusion

Brazil's Unified Health System (SUS) illustrates how a universal, integrated health system can extend beyond prevention and primary care to encompass high‐complexity surgical oncology while addressing broader societal, environmental, and healthcare system determinants of health. The incorporation of RARP represents a pivotal step in aligning surgical innovation with equity, evidence generation, and population‐level impact within a publicly funded healthcare system.

For the urological community, the challenge is not only to refine surgical techniques but also to engage in system‐level design, implementation, evaluation, and continuous learning to ensure that technological advances translate into meaningful improvements in health outcomes. The Brazilian experience demonstrates that the value of surgical innovation lies not solely in the technology itself, but in its capacity to strengthen health systems, reduce disparities, and improve access to high‐quality care.

In this context, Surgical Public Health emerges as a conceptual framework that connects public health, population health, health‐systems science, and One Health perspectives in prostate cancer care. By integrating prevention, equity, environmental and societal determinants, healthcare delivery, and surgical innovation, this framework provides a comprehensive approach to optimizing cancer outcomes at the population level. As a large‐scale real‐world laboratory, the SUS offers important lessons for the future of prostate cancer care in both middle‐ and high‐income settings.
