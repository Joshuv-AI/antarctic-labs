Abstract
. This paper develops a policy gradient method for entropy-regularized mean-field control in the discounted infinite-horizon setting. We consider randomized feedback policies and a coupled representative-particle/population system, in which the representative state evolves jointly with a population law governed by a McKean–Vlasov equation. The resulting value function is therefore defined on the product space ℝ d × 𝒫 2 ​ ( ℝ d ) \mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) . A key distinction from existing policy gradient methods for mean-field control is that, after computing the value function under a fixed policy, our approach does not require solving an additional equation to obtain the policy gradient. Instead, we derive an explicit policy gradient formula directly in terms of the value function. The formulation is based on an instantaneous advantage function, which quantifies the gain of taking a given action relative to the current randomized policy. We establish a Gâteaux policy-gradient formula, which gives the first-order variation of the objective along arbitrary policy perturbations, and then derive the corresponding ascent direction under finite-dimensional policy parametrization. The resulting formula leads to a model-based actor–critic scheme. The critic is obtained by solving the associated linear stationary Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation for the value function, using cylindrical functions to represent dependence on the population law. The actor is then updated according to the derived policy-gradient formula. We further analyze the well-posedness of the PDE in a polynomial-growth function class. Finally, we illustrate the proposed method through numerical experiments on an LQR model and a crowd-motion problem.



1.
Introduction

Many control problems involve a large population of identical agents whose dynamics interact weakly, and only through the empirical distribution of the population. Such models arise naturally in applications including opinion formation
[
75
,
40
]
, biological swarming and flocking
[
34
,
31
]
, crowd dynamics
[
30
]
, and financial systemic risk
[
26
]
. See also
[
24
,
25
,
17
]
for general background. When the number of agents
N
is large, directly optimizing the full interacting
N
-particle system becomes prohibitively difficult, both computationally and analytically. The mean-field viewpoint then replaces the full microscopic system by a limiting problem in which a representative agent interacts with the distribution of the population. This limit is closely related to the theory of interacting particle systems, propagation of chaos, and McKean–Vlasov (MKV) stochastic differential equations, where under suitable assumptions, the empirical measure of the
N
-particle system converges to a measure-valued limit, and the state of a representative particle is then described by a stochastic differential equation whose coefficients depend on its own law; see, for instance,
[
24
,
49
]
.

From this large-population perspective, two different limiting formulations emerge. If one starts from a
central
control problem for the full population, or equivalently takes the mean-field limit of a socially optimal large-agent control problem, one is led to
mean-field control
(MFC), which is also known as McKean–Vlasov optimal control. If instead each agent optimizes an individual objective against the population and one
then passes to the limit, one obtains a
mean-field game
(MFG), whose solution concept is Nash equilibrium. In this sense, MFC and MFG should be viewed as two distinct asymptotic regimes of large-population systems of exchangeable interacting agents. The key difference lies in whether the
optimization is collective or strategic, and in the order in which optimization and passage to the limit are performed; see
[
22
,
49
,
50
,
29
,
17
]
. The present paper is concerned exclusively with the control formulation, namely MFC.

A distinctive feature of the present framework is the formulation of
the controlled dynamics through a
decoupled system
: a
MKV equation governing the evolution of the population law,
coupled with an independent stochastic differential equation for a
representative agent
[
20
,
8
]
. In this formulation, the population flow

(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is generated by the MKV equation and is
deterministic, while the representative agent evolves according to its
own SDE driven by an independent Brownian motion. A key advantage of
this decoupled structure is that it allows the value function to be
defined not only at the level of the population distribution, as is
standard in the Wasserstein-space formulation
[
57
]
, but also
for any given initial position of the representative agent. The value
function therefore takes as arguments both an initial state

s\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the representative agent and an initial
population law
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, a joint
dependence that plays a central role in the derivation of the policy
gradient and in the design of the actor-critic algorithm.

We work in the infinite-horizon discounted setting with entropy regularization and randomized feedback policies. This choice is natural for several reasons. First, entropy regularization promotes exploration and smooths the optimization over policies, a feature that is central in continuous-time reinforcement learning and remains analytically useful even
when the controlled dynamics are known
[
76
,
45
,
46
]
.
Second, the temperature parameter
\lambda>0
connects the regularized problem to the classical deterministic formulation and yields a family of problems with better structural properties. Third, in the linear-quadratic setting, entropy regularization leads to explicit Gaussian optimal policies, which provide a tractable benchmark for the general theory.

The goal of this paper is to develop a model-based actor–critic method for mean-field control problems. Actor–critic methods are a standard class of algorithms in reinforcement learning, combining a critic, which evaluates the current policy, with an actor, which updates the policy
[
70
, Chapter 13]
. In our setting, for a fixed randomized feedback policy, the critic evaluates its performance through a HJB equation. The actor then improves the policy within a parametrized class, using a policy gradient formula for the performance functional, which is derived from a policy gradient theorem. We work in the model-based setting, that is, the dynamics are known. This allows us to focus
on the analytic structure of the problem and provides a foundation for
future data-driven extensions in
[
12
]
.

A key difficulty is the structure of the policy gradient. In classical single-agent control, the gradient of the performance functional with respect to the policy parameters depends only on the state process and the corresponding advantage function. In the mean-field setting, by contrast, the performance functional depends on the policy through both the representative dynamics and the population flow induced by the same policy. As a result, the policy gradient theorem provided in this work decomposes into two structurally distinct contributions: a
representative term
, capturing the direct effect of the policy through the state dynamics, and a
population term
, capturing the indirect effect through the dependence of the value function on the population law. This decomposition has no counterpart in the standard single-agent theory and is one of the main contributions of the present paper.

Contributions.

(1)

Policy gradient theorem.

We derive a rigorous policy gradient theorem for the coupled
representative agent-population system in the infinite-horizon setting. The
resulting formula separates the representative and population
contributions to the gradient of the performance functional,
expressed in terms of two advantage functions: the representative
advantage function, which extends the classical single-agent
structure, and the population advantage function, which is specific
to the mean-field framework and involves the derivative of the
value function on probability measure space. We also establish a parametric version of this
formula for families of randomized policies, providing a rigorous
basis for gradient-based policy improvement.

(2)

Policy evaluation and infinite-horizon mean-field LQR.

We analyze the linear stationary Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation on

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
arising in the policy
evaluation step and establish classical well-posedness results. We also study
the infinite-horizon entropy-regularized mean-field LQR problem,
deriving an explicit characterization of the optimal value function
and the associated optimal Gaussian policy. This LQR analysis provides
both an explicit benchmark for the general theory and a natural
reference setting for the numerical experiments.

(3)

Actor-critic algorithm and numerical experiments.

Based on the theoretical analysis, we introduce a finite-dimensional
actor-critic algorithm combining a Galerkin approximation of the
stationary HJB equation for policy evaluation with the policy
gradient formula for policy improvement. Basis functions are
constructed using cylindrical functions of the population law, for
which the Lions derivatives entering the HJB operator can be
evaluated in closed form. Numerical experiments on the mean-field
systemic risk model and on a nonlinear two-dimensional crowd-aversion
problem corroborate the theoretical results and demonstrate the
practical effectiveness of the proposed method.

[

,

,

]
for dynamics depending on

[

,

. Feynman–Kac representations for HJB

[

,

]
. Viscosity-solution

[

[

. Related viscosity theories for equations on

[

[

]
, although most of the above theory is formulated in

Our analysis is complementary to this literature. Motivated by policy iteration (see

Numerical methods for mean-field control and policy gradient.
A broad range of numerical methods has been developed for mean-field
control problems with known dynamics. PDE-based and augmented-Lagrangian
methods have been proposed for mean-field type control problems with
congestion effects
[
2
,
1
]
,
while MKV control problems have also been approached through
numerical schemes for the associated forward-backward stochastic
differential equations
[
33
]
. We refer
to
[
52
]
for a comprehensive survey on FBSDE for MKV, and dynamic programming based methods, including particle
methods, and PDE-based discretizations. Related particle
approximations for second-order PDEs on the Wasserstein space have been
studied in
[
11
]
. In high-dimensional
regimes, these methods are complemented by a growing literature based on
deep learning and neural-network parametrizations; see, for instance,

[
62
,
28
,
63
]
.

A related approximation paradigm, in the context of actor-critic, is linear value-function approximation,
which is classical in reinforcement learning and is widely used to make
policy evaluation and policy iteration computationally feasible in large
or continuous state spaces; see, for instance,

[
71
,
48
,
4
,
80
]
.
In the mean-field-control setting,
[
15
]
develops
learning algorithms with linear function approximation for finite-state,
finite-action MFC problems and proves quantitative performance bounds. In the linear-quadratic
setting, convergence and error analyses of policy-gradient methods have
been studied in both discrete and continuous time

[
27
,
77
,
38
]
.
The present paper is complementary to these works. Methods developed for
discrete-time MDPs or mean-field MDPs do not transfer directly to the
continuous-time diffusion setting. Indeed, the conventional
state-action value function collapses to the value function as the
sampling interval tends to zero, so that its action dependence survives
only at the infinitesimal level through an instantaneous advantage
rate; see
[
74
,
47
]
. This motivates formulations of actor-critic methods that are
intrinsic to the continuous-time dynamics.

We would like to mention two lines of work that is most related to this paper (also see Remarks
4.3
,
4.7
and
4.10
for more detailed discussion).
[
37
]
derived a policy gradient formula for MFC in
finite horizon by differentiating the HJB characterization of the
value function with respect to the policy parameter, and
more recently, they generalized this algorithm to moment neural network approximations
[
56
]
.
Our work is closest in spirit to the above two works. Differentiating the HJB provides a
natural route for parametric actor–critic methods, but it also requires
solving an additional sensitivity equation, or equivalently an additional
critic-type problem, to compute the ascent direction.
In contrast, our approach derives an explicit policy-gradient formula
directly in terms of the value function. This avoids the need for a separate
sensitivity equation and leads to a simpler actor update. In addition, our
formulation is first developed at the level of randomized feedback policies
themselves. We characterize the Gâteaux policy gradient, namely the
first-order variation of the objective along arbitrary admissible policy
perturbations, before specializing it to finite-dimensional policy
parametrizations. This gives a parameterization-free description of policy
improvement and then yields an explicit ascent direction for parametrized
policies. Finally, the policy gradient formulation is expressed through two instantaneous advantage functions, one for the representative-agent dynamics and one for the population-law dynamics. These advantages are defined from the value function and the infinitesimal generators, and do not require derivatives of the drift or diffusion with respect to the policy parameters. This form is closer to the classical policy-gradient structure in reinforcement learning, where the policy gradient is expressed in terms of an advantage function.

On the other hand,
[
79
]
also derives a related instantaneous advantage q-function for mean-field control. Their formulation is population-based,
whereas ours is built on the coupled representative-agent/population system.
More importantly, the advantage function plays a different role.
[
79
]
uses the instantaneous
advantage to construct the integrated
q
-functions for all test policies in a neighbourhood of the target policy. Then the martingale property of the integrated q-functions is used to solve the MFC. This integrated
q
-function approach has been further developed in
[
78
]
for MFG and MFC problems, including jump-diffusion dynamics. More recent works extend this continuous-time
q
-learning framework to MFC with controlled common noise
[
60
,
61
]
. In that setting, the conditional population law is stochastic and the policy-improvement step becomes more implicit, leading to fixed-point characterizations of the optimal policy. In contrast with these papers, we use the pointwise-in-action
q
-function directly
to derive the policy-gradient formula, yielding an explicit actor update once

V^{\pi}
and its derivatives are available. Thus, our actor–critic pipeline
does not require an auxiliary integrated
q
-function or testing over a
neighborhood of policies.

2.
Problem Formulation

Let
d,n,m\in\mathbb{N}
. The state space is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the action
space is a Borel set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and the
population state is described by a measure in

\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the set of Borel probability measures on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite second moment. We equip

\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with the
2
-Wasserstein distance

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu,\varrho):=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\varrho)}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-y|^{2}\,\gamma(dx,dy)\right)^{1/2}.

We consider an infinite-horizon discounted mean-field optimal control
problem. Starting from an initial condition

(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
s

denotes the state of a representative agent and
\mu
the distribution
of the population, a social planner seeks to optimize the collective
performance of the system over time. The objective is to maximize the
expected discounted reward generated by the controlled mean-field
dynamics. This leads to the optimal value function over an admissible set
\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}

(1)

\displaystyle V^{*}(s,\mu)\,:=\,\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}}V^{\pi}(s,\mu),\qquad(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Here
V^{\pi}(s,\mu)
denotes the value associated with a policy

\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}
when the system starts from
(s,\mu)
.
Whenever there exists a policy
\pi^{*}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}
such that

V^{*}(s,\mu)=V^{\pi^{*}}(s,\mu)
for all

(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we call

\pi^{*}
an optimal policy.

More precisely, for a given policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}
, the
corresponding value is defined by

(2)

\displaystyle V^{\pi}(s,\mu)\,:=\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta t}\,r_{\lambda}^{\pi}(s_{t},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\;\bigg|\;s_{0}=s,\ \mu_{0}=\mu\right],

where
\beta>0
is the discount factor,
r_{\lambda}^{\pi}
is the running
reward, and
(s_{t},\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denotes the controlled
representative-population state process. In classical mean-field
control, one often works with deterministic feedback controls. In the
present work, by contrast, we allow for randomized feedback policies.
This choice is natural in reinforcement learning, where exploration
plays a central role and is naturally encoded through Markov kernels.
Accordingly, we take
\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}
to be the class of Markov
kernels

\pi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}),\qquad(x,\mu)\longmapsto\pi(\cdot|x,\mu),

such that
\pi(\cdot|x,\mu)\ll\nu
for every
(x,\mu)
, with jointly
measurable density
p_{\pi}(\cdot|x,\mu)
, where
\nu
denotes a

\sigma
-finite reference measure on

(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{A}))
. The running reward in
(
2
) is the entropy-regularized averaged reward

(3)

\displaystyle r_{\lambda}^{\pi}(x,\mu)

\displaystyle:=r^{\pi}(x,\mu)+\lambda\,\mathcal{H}\bigl(\pi(\cdot|x,\mu)\bigr)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{A}}r(x,\mu,a)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}a|x,\mu)-\lambda\int_{\mathcal{A}}\log p_{\pi}(a|x,\mu)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}a|x,\mu),

where
\lambda>0
is the temperature parameter. Entropy regularization
is standard in continuous-time reinforcement learning and promotes
exploration; see, for instance,

[
76
,
45
,
46
,
47
,
79
,
37
]
.

We now introduce the controlled dynamics associated with a policy

\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}
. Let

b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

and

\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}

be measurable coefficients. The averaged drift and diffusion matrix
induced by
\pi
are defined by

\displaystyle b^{\pi}(x,\mu):=\int_{\mathcal{A}}b(x,\mu,a)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}a|x,\mu),\qquad\Sigma^{\pi}(x,\mu):=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\sigma(x,\mu,a)\sigma(x,\mu,a)^{\top}\,\pi(\mathrm{d}a|x,\mu),

and we write

\sigma^{\pi}(x,\mu):=\bigl(\Sigma^{\pi}(x,\mu)\bigr)^{1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
for the symmetric positive semidefinite
square root of
\Sigma^{\pi}
. The representative state process then
solves

(4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s_{t}=b^{\pi}(s_{t},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma^{\pi}(s_{t},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},\qquad s_{0}=s,

while the population distribution
\mu_{t}
is generated by the McKean-Vlasov
equation

(5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\tilde{s}_{t}=b^{\pi}(\tilde{s}_{t},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma^{\pi}(\tilde{s}_{t},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{B}_{t},\qquad\tilde{s}_{0}\sim\mu,\qquad\mu_{t}=\Law(\tilde{s}_{t}),

where
B
and
\tilde{B}
are independent
d
-dimensional Brownian
motions. The pair
(s_{t},\mu_{t})
appearing in (
2
) is
therefore obtained by coupling a representative agent with the
population law generated by (
5
).

Formally, for each initial condition
(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and each horizon
T>0
, we work on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
endowed with a filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
such that
\mathcal{F}_{0}
contains all
\mathbb{P}
-null sets of
\mathcal{F}
, the filtration is right-continuous, and
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
supports two independent
d
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}
-Brownian motions
B
and
\tilde{B}
, together with an
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable
\tilde{s}_{0}
satisfying
\Law(\tilde{s}_{0})=\mu
and independent of
(B,\tilde{B})
. The representative component starts from the deterministic value
s
. Under Assumption
3.1
(below), the population equation admits a unique strong
\mathbb{F}
-adapted solution
\tilde{s}
, and we then define
\mu_{t}:=\Law(\tilde{s}_{t})
; in particular,
(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
is a deterministic flow in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
; see

[
29
, Chapter 4]
. Once this flow is fixed, due to Assumption
3.1
, the representative dynamics is an
\mathbb{F}
-adapted stochastic differential equation, and therefore it also admits a unique strong solution.

Remark 2.1

(On randomized and aggregated formulations)
.

The state–law formulation adopted here, namely a representative state
coupled with a population law flow on

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, is consistent with the
general McKean–Vlasov framework of
[
20
]
in
the uncontrolled case and
[
8
]
in the control
setting.

Given a stochastic policy
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}),
the most direct non-averaged formulation is to sample actions continuously in time according to the current state and population law, namely

(6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s_{t}=b(s_{t},\mu_{t},a_{t}^{s})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(s_{t},\mu_{t},a_{t}^{s})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},\quad a_{t}^{s}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t},\mu_{t}),

(7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\tilde{s}_{t}=b(\tilde{s}_{t},\mu_{t},a_{t}^{\mu})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(\tilde{s}_{t},\mu_{t},a_{t}^{\mu})\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{B}_{t},\quad a_{t}^{\mu}\sim\pi(\cdot|\tilde{s}_{t},\mu_{t}),\quad\mu_{t}=\Law(\tilde{s}_{t})

Formally, one may realize the sampling through a measurable map

\Phi_{\pi}(x,\mu,u)
such that
\Phi_{\pi}(x,\mu,U)\sim\pi(\cdot|x,\mu)

for
U\sim{\rm Unif}[0,1]
, and then set

a_{t}^{s}=\Phi_{\pi}(s_{t},\mu_{t},U_{t}^{s})
,

a_{t}^{\mu}=\Phi_{\pi}(\tilde{s}_{t},\mu_{t},U_{t}^{\mu})
. The difficulty is that, on
the usual product space, a continuum of independent samples in time need
not be jointly measurable in
(t,\omega)
, so the coefficients may fail
to be progressively measurable; see
[
73
, Remark 2.1]
. A rigorous pathwise construction
is nevertheless possible on a suitable enriched product space, for
instance a rich Fubini extension in the sense of
[
69
]
; this is
precisely the route taken in
[
37
]
for (
6
)–(
7
).

A different non-averaged interpretation is to sample the action only on a
time grid and then keep it constant between intervention times. This
produces an SDE with piecewise constant random controls on the usual
probability space. Related piecewise constant randomizations also appear
in
[
8
]
within the randomized McKean–Vlasov
control problem, while in the continuous-time reinforcement learning
literature such sampled dynamics are studied in

[
73
,
44
]
. In general, discretely sampled dynamics do not coincide with the
aggregated dynamics; rather, they converge to them as the mesh size
tends to zero; see
[
44
, Theorems 4.1–4.2]
.

By contrast, the aggregated formulation
(
4
)–(
5
) is the appropriate law-level
representation of the continuously randomized model
(
6
)-(
7
). More precisely, whenever
(
6
)-(
7
) is constructed on an enriched space and is
well posed, the law of its state process solves the same
McKean–Vlasov martingale problem as the aggregated system (
4
)–(
5
). Hence,
under weak uniqueness, both formulations induce the same law and
therefore the same value functional; see

[
79
, Lemma A.1]
,

[
36
,
49
]
and
[
37
, Proof of Prop. 2.1]
.

Organization of the paper.

The rest of the paper is devoted to approximating the optimal policy of
(
1
) through an actor–critic iteration. Given a
parametrized randomized policy
\pi_{\omega}
, each iteration consists of
two steps:

(1)

Policy evaluation.
For the current policy

\pi_{\omega}
, the critic computes or approximates the value function

V^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu)
by solving the associated linear stationary
HJB equation.

(2)

Policy improvement.
The actor updates the policy parameters
using a policy gradient formula for the performance functional

J(\omega)=\mathbb{E}[V^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu)]
, for instance through the
gradient ascent step

\omega\leftarrow\omega+\alpha\nabla_{\omega}J(\omega)
.

In Section
3
, we study the policy-evaluation step and show
that, for a fixed admissible policy, the value function is the unique
classical solution of the associated stationary Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman
equation. In Section
4
, we derive the policy
gradient theorem and its parametric version for the coupled
representative agent–population system. In Section
5
, we describe how the
actor–critic method can be implemented in practice in the model-based
setting. Section
6
analyzes the entropy-regularized
infinite-horizon mean-field linear-quadratic regulator, which provides
an explicit benchmark for the general theory. Finally, Section
7
presents
numerical examples illustrating our method.

3.
The Stationary HJB Equation

The central object of study in this section is the value function

V^{\pi}
defined in (
2
). Our goal is to characterize
it as the unique classical solution of a stationary Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman
(HJB) equation.

3.1.
The infinitesimal generator and stationary HJB equation

The dynamics of the pair
(s_{t},\mu_{t})
under
policy
\pi
are governed by (
4
)-(
5
). To differentiate a function
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to the
measure argument
\mu
, we rely on the notion of
Lions
derivative
, introduced by Lions in his lectures at
the Collège de France
[
54
]
and further developed
in
[
24
,
29
]
.
Applying the Itô-Lions formula to a smooth function
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
and
taking expectations, one identifies the infinitesimal generator of

(s_{t},\mu_{t})
as the operator

(8)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi}F)(s,\mu)

\displaystyle:=b^{\pi}(s,\mu)\cdot\nabla_{s}F(s,\mu)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu):\nabla^{2}_{ss}F(s,\mu)

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Bigl[b^{\pi}(\xi,\mu)\cdot\partial_{\mu}F(s,\mu)(\xi)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Sigma^{\pi}(\xi,\mu):D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}F(s,\mu)(\xi)\Bigr]\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi),

where
\xi\mapsto\partial_{\mu}F(s,\mu)(\xi)
denotes the Lions
derivative of
F
with respect to
\mu
, evaluated at

(s,\mu,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We also denote by
D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}F(s,\mu)(\xi)
the Jacobian
matrix of
\xi\mapsto\partial_{\mu}F(s,\mu)(\xi)
with respect to

\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For a
comprehensive treatment we refer to
[
24
, Chapter 5]

and
[
29
, Chapter 1]
. Also
A:B:=\mathrm{Tr}(A^{\top}B)
is the Frobenius inner product,

\nabla_{s}F
and
\nabla^{2}_{ss}F
are the gradient and Hessian of
F

with respect to
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The generator (
8
) has two parts: the first line is the
classical Itô generator for
s_{t}
driven by
b^{\pi}
and
\Sigma^{\pi}
, that is (
4
),
while the integral term captures the variation of
F
as the measure

\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{s}_{t})
evolves under the McKean-Vlasov
equation (
5
).

In order to simplify the exposition we introduce the following class of functions.

Definition 3.1

(
\mathcal{C}^{2,2}
functions with polynomial growth)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. A map

F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{k}

belongs to

\mathcal{C}^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

if, for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the map

s\mapsto F(s,\mu)
is twice continuously differentiable on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and, for every
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the map

\mu\mapsto F(s,\mu)
is Lions differentiable on

\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Moreover, the maps

(s,\mu,\xi)\mapsto\partial_{\mu}F(s,\mu)(\xi)
and

(s,\mu,\xi)\mapsto D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}F(s,\mu)(\xi)
are jointly
continuous.

We say that
F\in\mathcal{C}^{2,2}_{\mathrm{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

if

F\in\mathcal{C}^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

and there exists a constant
C_{F}>0
such that, for all
(s,\mu,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle|F(s,\mu)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{F}\bigl(1+|s|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr),

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{s}F(s,\mu)\|+|\partial_{\mu}F(s,\mu)(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{F}\bigl(1+|s|+|\xi|+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr),

\displaystyle\|D^{2}_{ss}F(s,\mu)\|+\|D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}F(s,\mu)(\xi)\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{F}\bigl(1+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr),

where
m_{p}(\mu)
denotes the
p-
th moment of
\mu
.

To establish
\mathcal{C}^{2,2}_{\mathrm{poly}}

regularity of
V^{\pi}
and the existence of a solution of the HJB equation in the
classical sense, we additionally require the following smoothness on
the policy-aggregated coefficients.

(A1)
\mathcal{C}^{2,2}
-regularity of averaged coefficients.

Let
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}
be fixed. We assume that, for every component

h\in\{b_{i}^{\pi},\sigma_{i,j}^{\pi}:i,j=1,\dots,d\}
, the derivatives

\nabla_{s}h(s,\mu)
,
D^{2}_{ss}h(s,\mu)
,
\partial_{\mu}h(s,\mu)(\xi)
,
and
D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}h(s,\mu)(\xi)
exist for all

(s,\mu,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
are bounded by a constant
K_{\pi}
, and are locally Lipschitz continuous in
(s,\mu,\xi)
.
Also assume that
r_{\lambda}^{\pi}\in\mathcal{C}^{2,2}_{\mathrm{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

Remark 3.2
.

Using
Section

3.1
we can assume for simplicity that
K_{\pi}>0
is big enough such that for all

s\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(9)

\displaystyle|b^{\pi}(s,\mu)|+\|\sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu)\|\leq K_{\pi}\bigl(1+|s|+\sqrt{m_{2}(\mu)}\bigr).

Also, since the bounded derivatives in
Section

3.1
ensure the existence of
L_{\pi}>0
such that for all
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\displaystyle|b^{\pi}(s,\mu)-b^{\pi}(s^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})|+\|\sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu)-\sigma^{\pi}(s^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})\|\leq L_{\pi}\bigl(|s-s^{\prime}|+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\bigr).

Moreover, since
\Sigma^{\pi}=\sigma^{\pi}(\sigma^{\pi})^{\top}
, it follows from

Section

3.1
and the product rule that

\Sigma^{\pi}\in\mathcal{C}^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
,
and there exists
C_{\pi}>0
such that, for all
(s,\mu,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(10)

\displaystyle\|\Sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu)\|\leq C_{\pi}\bigl(1+|s|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr),

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{s}\Sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu)\|+|\partial_{\mu}\Sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu)(\xi)|+\|D^{2}_{ss}\Sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu)\|+\|D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}\Sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu)(\xi)\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\pi}\bigl(1+|s|+\sqrt{m_{2}(\mu)}\bigr).

We can now state the main result of this section, which characterizes the infinite-horizon value function as the unique classical solution of the stationary Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation.

Theorem 3.3

(Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation)
.

Let
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}
be a fixed admissible policy and suppose
Assumption
3.1
holds. Then there exists a constant

\bar{\beta}_{\pi}>0
, depending only on the structural constants in
Assumption
3.1
, such that the following holds. If
\beta>\bar{\beta}_{\pi}
, then the value function
V^{\pi}
defined in (
2
) is well defined and belongs to

\mathcal{C}^{2,2}_{\mathrm{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.
Moreover,
V^{\pi}
is a classical solution of

(11)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi}-\beta)V^{\pi}(s,\mu)+r_{\lambda}^{\pi}(s,\mu)=0,\qquad(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where
\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi}
is given by (
8
).
Finally,
V^{\pi}
is the unique classical solution of
(
11
) in the class

\mathcal{C}^{2,2}_{\mathrm{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Moreover, assume in addition for all
(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(12)

\displaystyle 2\,s\cdot b^{\pi}(s,\mu)+\|\sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu)\|_{F}^{2}\leq C_{0}-\kappa|s|^{2}+C_{\mathrm{diss}}\,m_{2}(\mu)

holds. Then the value function

V^{\pi}
is well defined for every
\beta>0
and there exists
C_{r}>0
such that
|r_{\lambda}^{\pi}(s,\mu)|\leq C_{r}(1+|s|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu))
. Furthermore, if
C_{0}=0

then
V^{\pi}
is also well defined for
\beta=0
.

The proof of
Theorem

3.3
is provided in
Section

A.1
. Several remarks on the above theorem follows.

First, the constant
\bar{\beta}_{\pi}
in
Theorem

3.3
is defined by
\bar{\beta}_{\pi}=\max\{\beta_{0}(2),\beta_{\mathrm{var}}(2)\}
, where
\beta_{0}(2)
and
\beta_{\mathrm{var}}(2)
are given explicitly in
Remarks

A.2
and
A.5
, and depend only on
d
and
K_{\pi}
.

Second, once the policy
\pi
is fixed, the stationary HJB equation becomes linear. The proof of
Proposition

6.4
then relies on two preliminary lemmas. The first one provides explicit exponential-in-time
L^{p}
bounds for the state processes, while the second one gives exponential-in-time bounds for the derivatives with respect to the state and measure variables. These estimates are the key input in the infinite-horizon argument, since they quantify precisely how the growth in time interacts with the discount factor. Starting from these identities, the proof combines the finite-horizon result of
[
38
]
with a passage to the limit as
T\to\infty
. The previous lemmas ensure that this limit can be performed both at the level of the value function and in the equation itself, which yields the classical solution of the stationary HJB equation together with uniqueness in the polynomial-growth class. The argument is also closely related in spirit to the McKean-Vlasov PDE framework of
[
20
]
, although the latter deals with finite-horizon problems, whereas here we consider an infinite-horizon discounted setting.

4.
Policy Gradient

This section develops the policy improvement step for the mean-field
control problem introduced in Section
2
. The
policy evaluation step has already been addressed in
Section
3
: given a feedback randomized policy
\pi
, the
value function
V^{\pi}
is well defined and can be characterized as the
unique classical solution of a stationary HJB equation. The goal of
the present section is to derive an explicit formula for the gradient
of the performance functional with respect to a parametric family of
policies.

A distinctive feature of the mean-field setting is that the
performance functional depends on two initial data: the initial law

\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
generating the population flow,
and the initial distribution
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

of the representative agent. Throughout this section,
\mu
is fixed
and the performance of a policy
\pi
is measured by

J_{\mu,\mu_{0}}(\pi)\;:=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V^{\pi}(s,\mu)\,\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}s).

For a parametric family
\{\pi_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}
we write

(13)

J(\omega)\;:=\;J_{\mu,\mu_{0}}(\pi_{\omega})\;=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu)\,\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}s).

The main difficulty is that
J(\omega)
depends on
\omega
both
through the representative dynamics and through the induced mean-field
flow. In what follows we will provide a policy gradient theorem and a result for the parametrized case.

Remark 4.1
.

For any
s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Dirac measure
\delta_{s_{0}}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Hence, by choosing
\mu_{0}=\delta_{s_{0}}
, the results of this section apply to the pointwise performance functional
J_{\mu,s_{0}}(\pi):=V^{\pi}(s_{0},\mu)
as well.

4.1.
Policy gradient theorem

Let
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}
and let
\varphi
be a measurable
signed kernel
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{A})
satisfying

(14)

\varphi(\cdot\mid s,\mu)\ll\pi(\cdot\mid s,\mu),\qquad\int_{\mathcal{A}}\varphi(\mathrm{d}a\mid s,\mu)=0,\qquad(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

with bounded Radon-Nikodym derivative
\psi:=\mathrm{d}\varphi/\mathrm{d}\pi\in L^{\infty}
. The zero-mass condition in (
14
)
ensures that
\pi^{\varepsilon}
remains a probability kernel for small

\varepsilon
. For
|\varepsilon|
small, define the perturbed kernel

(15)

\pi^{\varepsilon}(\mathrm{d}a\mid s,\mu):=\pi(\mathrm{d}a\mid s,\mu)+\varepsilon\,\varphi(\mathrm{d}a\mid s,\mu)=\bigl(1+\varepsilon\psi(s,\mu,a)\bigr)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}a\mid s,\mu),

and assume
\pi^{\varepsilon}
is an admissible feedback randomized
policy for all sufficiently small
|\varepsilon|
.

Before introducing the policy gradient theorem, we introduce the following advantage functions.

Definition 4.2

(Advantage functions)
.

Let
\pi
be a feedback randomized policy with value function

V^{\pi}
. For
(s,\mu,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathcal{A}
, define the

representative advantage function

q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{rep}}(s,\mu,a)\;:=\;r(s,\mu,a)-\lambda\log p_{\pi}(a\mid s,\mu)+b(s,\mu,a)\cdot\nabla_{s}V^{\pi}(s,\mu)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Sigma(s,\mu,a):D^{2}_{ss}V^{\pi}(s,\mu),

and for
(\xi,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{A}
, define the

population advantage function

q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{pop}}(s,\mu,\xi,a)\;:=\;b(\xi,\mu,a)\cdot\partial_{\mu}V^{\pi}(s,\mu)(\xi)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Sigma(\xi,\mu,a):D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}V^{\pi}(s,\mu)(\xi).

Remark 4.3

(Interpretation of the advantage functions)
.

In standard reinforcement learning, the advantage function can be
written through the Bellman evaluation identity as

A^{\pi}(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[V^{\pi}(S^{\prime})\mid S=s,\mathfrak{a}=a\right]-V^{\pi}(s).

Thus, it compares the effect of taking the action
a
with the value
generated by the policy. In the single-agent continuous-time setting,
the same structure is expressed through the infinitesimal generator, so
that the corresponding action-dependent term is given by the running
reward plus the generator applied to the value function, minus the
discount term.

The present mean-field setting preserves this structure. The
representative advantage function

q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{rep}}
is the natural extension of the
single-agent continuous-time term: it contains the entropy-regularized
instantaneous reward together with the contribution of the generator
acting on the state variable
[
47
,
5
,
74
,
82
]
. The population advantage function

q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{pop}}
is specific to the mean-field
framework and describes the additional contribution coming from the
dependence of
V^{\pi}
on the population law through its Lions
derivatives. In this way, the classical advantage structure is
decomposed into a representative part and a population part.

Finally, let us note that
[
79
]
introduces two
q-functions in the McKean–Vlasov control setting, an integrated
q-function and an essential q-function. The integrated q-function is a
central object in the proposed learning procedure. Since its weak
martingale characterization is formulated with respect to all test
policies in a neighbourhood of the target policy, its practical
implementation relies on averaging the corresponding loss over nearby
test policies. The essential q-function plays the role of a
pointwise-in-action quantity for policy improvement, and in this
respect our kernels
q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{rep}}
and

q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{pop}}
are closer in spirit to that object, although
our formulation is for the coupled representative-agent / population
system rather than for the population-only formulation. The main
difference is that, in our actor–critic pipeline, the policy gradient
theorem requires only the pointwise-in-action kernels

q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{rep}}
and
q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{pop}}
, which are obtained
directly from
V^{\pi}
and its derivatives. Therefore, no auxiliary
integrated q-function is needed, nor does our approach require testing
over a neighbourhood of the target policy.

Definition 4.4

(Discounted occupancy measure)
.

Let
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}
be a fixed policy, and let
(s_{t}^{\pi},\mu_{t}^{\pi})_{t\geq 0}
be the state-population process associated with (
4
)-(
5
) starting from
s_{0}^{\pi}=s\sim\mu_{0},
and
\mu_{0}^{\pi}=\mu
. The discounted occupancy measure
\rho_{\mu,\mu_{0}}^{\pi}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined by

\displaystyle\rho_{\mu,\mu_{0}}^{\pi}(B):=\beta\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta t}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{(s_{t}^{\pi},\mu_{t}^{\pi})\in B\}}\,\mathrm{d}t\right],\qquad B\in\mathcal{B}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\bigr).

Remark 4.5

(Equivalent representations of
\rho_{\mu,\mu_{0}}^{\pi}
)
.

For
t\geq 0
, let
P_{t}^{\pi}(s_{0},\mu;\cdot):=\Law(s_{t}^{\pi}\mid s_{0},\mu)
.
Then, by Tonelli’s theorem,
\rho_{\mu,\mu_{0}}^{\pi}
admits the representation

\rho_{\mu,\mu_{0}}^{\pi}(B)=\beta\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{1}_{B}(x,\mu_{t}^{\pi})\,P_{t}^{\pi}(s_{0},\mu;\mathrm{d}x)\,\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}s_{0})\right)\mathrm{d}t,\qquad B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

Moreover, denoting by
\delta_{\mu_{t}^{\pi}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

the Dirac measure at
\mu_{t}^{\pi}
, the previous identity can be written
equivalently, in measure form, as

\rho_{\mu,\mu_{0}}^{\pi}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}\nu)=\beta\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}P_{t}^{\pi}(s_{0},\mu;\mathrm{d}x)\,\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}s_{0})\right)\delta_{\mu_{t}^{\pi}}(\mathrm{d}\nu)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Theorem 4.6

(Gâteaux policy gradient)
.

Fix a baseline policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}
. Let
\varphi
be a signed kernel
satisfying (
14
), and let
\pi^{\varepsilon}
be as
in (
15
), admissible for all

|\varepsilon|\leq\varepsilon_{0}
. Suppose Assumption
3.1

holds for both
\pi
and
\pi^{\varepsilon}
, with
|\varepsilon|\leq\varepsilon_{0}
. Assume
\mu,\;\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\beta>2\bar{\beta}_{\pi}

\bar{\beta}_{\pi}
from
Theorem

. Then
the map

\varepsilon\mapsto J(\varepsilon):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(s,\mu)\,\mu_{0}(\mathrm{d}s)

is differentiable at

, with

(16)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}J(\varepsilon)\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}=\frac{1}{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{(s,\mu)\sim\rho_{\mu,\mu_{0}}^{\pi}}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{A}}q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{rep}}(s,\mu,a)\,\varphi(\mathrm{d}a\mid s,\mu)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathcal{A}}q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{pop}}(s,\mu,\xi,a)\,\varphi(\mathrm{d}a\mid\xi,\mu)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi)\right].

The proof of
Theorem

4.6
is provided in
Section

A.2
.

Remark 4.7
.

The preceding theorem, together with Theorem
4.9
,
is a key contribution of this paper and distinguishes our approach from existing
policy-gradient methods and related
q
-function-based algorithms. Compared with
[
37
]
, we provide a parameterization-free formulation of the policy gradient, which describes the first-order variation of the objective under arbitrary admissible perturbations of the policy. Compared with

[

-functions to derive the
policy-gradient formula directly, while their method requires computing the integrated q-functions for all test policies in a neighbourhood of the target policy.

4.2.
Parametric policy gradient

The Gâteaux gradient formula (
16
) expresses the
directional derivative of
J
in terms of an arbitrary signed kernel

\varphi
. In practice, policies are often parametrized by a finite
dimensional vector
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and one wishes to compute

\nabla_{\omega}J(\omega)
directly. The parametric policy gradient
theorem below reduces this computation to a weighted expectation of
the advantage functions against the score function. To state the result precisely, we require the
following two assumptions on the parametric family.

(A2) Parametric differentiability of averaged
coefficients.

For
\nu
-almost every
a\in\mathcal{A}
and every

(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the map

\omega\mapsto\log p_{\pi_{\omega}}(a\mid s,\mu)
is continuously
differentiable. Moreover, for every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
there exists
C_{K}>0
and a function
g_{K}(\cdot,s,\mu)\in L^{1}(\mathcal{A},\nu)
such that for all
\omega\in K
and all

(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the maps
\omega\mapsto p_{\pi_{\omega}}(\cdot\mid s,\mu)
,

\omega\mapsto b^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu)
,
\omega\mapsto\Sigma^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu)
,
and
\omega\mapsto r_{\lambda}^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu)
are continuously
differentiable, satisfying

(17)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\bigl\|\nabla_{\omega}p_{\pi_{\omega}}(a\mid s,\mu)\bigr\|\leq g_{K}(a,s,\mu)\qquad\text{for $\nu$-a.e. }a\in\mathcal{A}.\\
&\|\nabla_{\omega}b^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu)\|\leq C_{K}\bigl(1+|s|+\sqrt{m_{2}(\mu)}\bigr),\\
\|\nabla_{\omega}r_{\lambda}^{\pi_{\omega}}&(s,\mu)\|+\|\nabla_{\omega}\Sigma^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu)\|\leq C_{K}\bigl(1+|s|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr).\end{aligned}

(A3) Uniform regularity of the parametric family.

Assumption
3.1
holds for every
\pi_{\omega}
, with
Lipschitz constant
L_{\pi}
and regularity constant
K_{\pi}
independent
of
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Remark 4.8

(On the Assumptions
3.1
-
4.2
)
.

The Gaussian policy
\pi_{\omega}(\cdot\mid s,\mu):=\mathcal{N}(f_{\omega}(s,\mu),\Sigma_{0})
, with
\Sigma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}

positive definite, satisfies Assumptions
3.1
-
4.2
whenever

f_{\omega}
is Lipschitz with linear growth in
(s,\mu)
, belongs to

\mathcal{C}^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

with all spatial and Lions derivatives of at most polynomial growth
in
(s,\mu,\xi)
, and
\omega\mapsto f_{\omega}(s,\mu)
is continuously
differentiable with at most polynomial growth in
(s,\mu)
; all
uniformly in
\omega
on compact sets. In particular, this holds when

f_{\omega}
is linear in
s
and
m_{1}(\mu)
, or when
f_{\omega}
is a
cylindrical neural network
[
55
, Section 2.2]
with
\mathcal{C}^{2}
activation
function of at most linear growth, such as
\tanh
or
\mathrm{GELU}
.

Theorem 4.9

(Parametric policy gradient)
.

Let
\{\pi_{\omega}\}_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}
be a family of feedback
randomized policies satisfying Assumptions
3.1
-
4.2
. Fix

\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and set
\pi:=\pi_{\omega}
. Assume

\mu,\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\beta>\widetilde{\beta}_{\pi}:=\max(\bar{\beta}_{\pi},\,2\beta_{0}(2))
. Then the map
\omega\mapsto J(\omega)
, defined in (
13
), is differentiable at
\omega
, and

(18)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\omega}J(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,\mu)\sim\rho_{\mu,\mu_{0}}^{\pi}\\
a\sim\pi(\cdot|s,\mu)\end{subarray}}\!\Bigl[q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{rep}}(s,\mu,a)\,\nabla_{\omega}\log p_{\pi}(a|s,\mu)\Bigr]\!+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,\mu)\sim\rho_{\mu,\mu_{0}}^{\pi}\\
\xi\sim\mu\\
a\sim\pi(\cdot|\xi,\mu)\end{subarray}}\!\Bigl[q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{pop}}(s,\mu,\xi,a)\,\nabla_{\omega}\log p_{\pi}(a|\xi,\mu)\Bigr].

Equivalently, this can be written as

(19)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\omega}J(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{(s,\mu)\sim\rho_{\mu,\mu_{0}}^{\pi}}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{A}}q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{rep}}(s,\mu,a)\,\nabla_{\omega}\log p_{\pi}(a\mid s,\mu)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}a\mid s,\mu)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{(s,\mu)\sim\rho_{\mu,\mu_{0}}^{\pi}}\!\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\int_{\mathcal{A}}q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{pop}}(s,\mu,\xi,a)\,\nabla_{\omega}\log p_{\pi}(a\mid\xi,\mu)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}a\mid\xi,\mu)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi)\right].

The proof of
Theorem

4.9
is given in
Section

A.2
.

Remark 4.10
.

The identity (
18
) keeps the classical
policy-gradient form of
advantage times score
, but now with two
terms. The first expectation involves

q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{rep}}
and corresponds to the representative-agent
contribution, whereas the second involves

q^{\pi}_{\mathrm{pop}}
and captures the population contribution.

From a computational viewpoint, once
V^{\pi}
has been evaluated or
approximated, the quantities entering
(
18
) are obtained directly from

V^{\pi}
,
b^{\pi}
,
\Sigma^{\pi}
, and the score function

\nabla_{\omega}\log p_{\pi}(a\mid s,\mu)
. The expectations with respect
to
\rho_{\mu,\mu_{0}}^{\pi}
can then be approximated from trajectories of
the controlled dynamics under the current policy
\pi_{\omega}
, together
with samples of actions drawn from
\pi_{\omega}(\cdot\mid s,\mu)
.
This is precisely the mechanism underlying the actor–critic algorithm
described in Section
5
.

The formula (
18
) should be contrasted
with the policy-gradient representation in
[
37
]
. In

[
37
]
, the gradient is obtained by differentiating the HJB
characterization of the value function with respect to the policy parameter.
As a result, the gradient itself is characterized as the solution of another
linear HJB equation, whose source term is constructed from the value function
under the current policy. Thus, computing the ascent direction requires solving
an additional policy-evaluation-type problem. By contrast, (
18
) gives an explicit gradient
formula once
V^{\pi}
and its derivatives are available. This makes the actor
step more direct.

Moreover, the differentiated HJB equation in

[
37
]
contains an additional term
H_{\omega}[J]
, which
involves the derivatives
\nabla_{\omega}b^{\pi_{\omega}}
and

\nabla_{\omega}\Sigma^{\pi_{\omega}}
. As emphasized in

[
37
, Remark 3.3]
, when the underlying dynamics are unknown, this term is explicitly computable only in special settings, such as separable models. In
contrast, our gradient formula is expressed directly through the
pointwise-in-action kernels derived from
V^{\pi}
and its
derivatives. This form avoids differentiating the aggregated drift and
diffusion with respect to the policy parameters, and is therefore better suited
for the model-free extensions developed in the follow-up paper
[
12
]
.

Finally, we note that Theorem 3.1 of
[
37
]
is equivalent to our parametric policy-gradient formula (
18
).
When our initial distribution
\mu_{0}
of the representative state is taken to be a
Dirac measure
\mu_{0}=\delta_{s}
, our pointwise form of (
18
) agrees with their gradient
G(s,\mu)
. For a general initial
distribution
\mu_{0}
, averaging their pointwise gradient
\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu_{0}}[G(s,\mu)]
gives exactly our gradient
\nabla_{\omega}J
defined in (
18
).

5.
Actor-Critic Algorithm

We now use the previous results to formulate an actor–critic algorithm. For a fixed policy
\pi_{\omega}
, the critic
provides a finite-dimensional approximation of the value function

V^{\pi_{\omega}}
solving (
11
), while the actor updates

\omega
through the policy gradient formula derived in
Section
4
.

For the critic, we use a linear finite-dimensional ansatz on

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with cylindrical basis functions to
encode the dependence on the population law. This choice is consistent
with classical linear value-function approximation in reinforcement
learning for large state spaces

[
71
,
48
,
4
,
80
]
,
as well as with related approximation methods in mean-field control

[
15
]
.

Once such a finite-dimensional critic has been obtained, either by the
Galerkin procedure below or by any other method, the policy gradient
theorem can be applied directly. This yields an actor update that retains both the
representative-agent and the population-law contributions of the
coupled mean-field problem.

5.1.
Policy evaluation via Galerkin approximation

Before introducing the Galerkin approximation, we emphasize that our actor-critic algorithm is not tied to a particular numerical method for solving the linear HJB equation. In principle, the critic can be computed using other approximation schemes, such as nonlinear function approximation with deep neural networks
[
56
]
, or estimated through martingale-based characterizations of the value function
[
79
]
. The Galerkin method presented here should therefore be viewed as one possible critic approximation scheme, corresponding to linear function approximation. We focus on the Galerkin approach for two reasons. First, it gives a transparent and stable way to approximate the value function through projection onto a finite-dimensional function space. Second, this linear-projection structure is well suited for our subsequent extension to the model-free reinforcement-learning setting
[
12
]
, where the dynamics are unknown and the critic must be estimated from data. In that setting, nonlinear function approximation for Bellman-type equations can suffer from the double-sampling issue, which may introduce instability in policy evaluation. By contrast, the Galerkin formulation leads to a more tractable projected critic equation and provides a useful starting point for developing stable model-free algorithms.

Let
\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\}
be smooth functions on

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and define the trial space
\mathbb{V}_{n}:=\mathrm{span}\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\}.

We approximate the value function by

(20)

\displaystyle V_{\theta}(s,\mu):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}\,\phi_{i}(s,\mu)=\theta^{\top}\Phi(s,\mu),\qquad\Phi:=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n})^{\top},

where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector of critic coefficients. Although
the basis functions are defined on

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the approximation is finite-dimensional
because only finitely many of them are used. In practice, the
dependence on
\mu
will be chosen through cylindrical functions, as
explained in Subsection
5.2
.

For a fixed policy
\pi_{\omega}
, we impose the HJB residual associated
with
V_{\theta}
to be orthogonal to the trial space with respect to the
reference measure
\mathfrak{m}
. Thus, we seek

\theta^{*}(\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that for every
j=1,\dots,n
,

(21)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Bigl((\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi_{\omega}}V_{\theta^{*}(\omega)})(s,\mu)-\beta V_{\theta^{*}(\omega)}(s,\mu)+r_{\lambda}^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu)\Bigr)\phi_{j}(s,\mu)\,\mathfrak{m}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}\mu)=0,

where
\mathfrak{m}
is a fixed Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Since the equation is linear in
V
, (
21
)
is equivalent to the linear system

(22)

A(\omega)\,\theta^{*}(\omega)=b(\omega),

where

(23)

\displaystyle A_{ji}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\phi_{j}(s,\mu)\,\Bigl(\beta\,\phi_{i}(s,\mu)-(\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi_{\omega}}\phi_{i})(s,\mu)\Bigr)\,\mathfrak{m}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}\mu),

\displaystyle b_{j}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}r_{\lambda}^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu)\,\phi_{j}(s,\mu)\,\mathfrak{m}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}\mu).

Consequently, whenever
A
is invertible, the solution of (
22
) is given by
\theta^{*}(\omega)=(A(\omega))^{-1}b(\omega)
. In any case, independently of the particular approximation used for

A(\omega)
and
b(\omega)
, the corresponding critic is obtained as the
Galerkin approximation

(24)

\displaystyle V_{n}^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu):=V_{\theta^{*}(\omega)}(s,\mu).

5.2.
Cylindrical basis functions and moment features

A central issue in the mean-field setting is the representation of the
measure variable. A convenient class of functions is given by
cylindrical functions, for which the Lions derivatives can be computed
explicitly. This makes them particularly attractive for Galerkin
approximations of the HJB equation.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and fix feature functions

\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k}\in C_{b}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We define the associated
moment map

m(\mu):=\bigl(m_{1}(\mu),\dots,m_{k}(\mu)\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\qquad m_{i}(\mu):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi_{i}(\xi)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi),\quad i=1,\dots,k.

A function
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
is called cylindrical
with respect to the feature family
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
if there
exists a smooth map
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

F(s,\mu)=\Psi\bigl(s,m(\mu)\bigr).

Thus, the dependence on
\mu
is reduced to finitely many moments or,
more generally, finitely many linear observables of
\mu
.

This construction is useful for the policy-evaluation step for two
reasons. First, it leads to a finite-dimensional parametrization of the
measure argument. Second, the derivatives entering the HJB operator
become explicit. Indeed, if
F(s,\mu)=\Psi(s,m(\mu))
, then

(25)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \nabla_{s}F(s,\mu)=\nabla_{s}\Psi\bigl(s,m(\mu)\bigr),&\qquad D^{2}_{ss}F(s,\mu)=D^{2}_{ss}\Psi\bigl(s,m(\mu)\bigr),\\
\partial_{\mu}F(s,\mu)(\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\partial_{m_{i}}\Psi\bigl(s,m(\mu)\bigr)\,\nabla\varphi_{i}(\xi),&\qquad D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}F(s,\mu)(\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\partial_{m_{i}}\Psi\bigl(s,m(\mu)\bigr)\,D^{2}_{\xi\xi}\varphi_{i}(\xi).\end{aligned}

Therefore, once the feature functions
\varphi_{i}
are fixed, the
operator
\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi_{\omega}}F
can be evaluated
explicitly for cylindrical functions. This motivates choosing, in the
Galerkin approximation (
21
), basis
functions of the form

\phi_{j}(s,\mu)=\psi_{j}\bigl(s,m(\mu)\bigr)
,
j=1,\dots,n
, with

\psi_{j}
smooth on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
. In this setting, the problem is
naturally expressed in terms of the reduced variables

(s,m(\mu))\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and the reference measure may therefore
be chosen as a Borel probability measure
\overline{\mathfrak{m}}
on

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, built from
N>0
observations

\{(s_{\tau},m_{\tau})\}_{\tau=1}^{N}
, with
m_{\tau}=m(\mu_{\tau})
, sampled on the time grid

t_{\tau}=(\tau-1)\Delta t
along the trajectories of
(
4
)–(
5
). A natural choice is to take
\overline{\mathfrak{m}}
as the empirical
discounted occupancy measure on the reduced space, namely

(26)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathfrak{m}}=\beta\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}e^{-\beta t_{\tau}}\Delta t\,\delta_{(s_{\tau},m(\mu_{\tau}))},\qquad\text{or}\qquad\overline{\mathfrak{m}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\delta_{(s_{\tau},m(\mu_{\tau}))},

the latter corresponding to the empirical undiscounted measure on the
finite observation window. In Section
7.1
, we also implemented the undiscounted measure. Our numerical results indicate that the algorithm is robust with respect to the choice of measure
\overline{\mathfrak{m}}
.

In practice, one may average over
L>0
independent trajectories in order
to reduce the variance of the empirical estimator. This is naturally
reflected in the definition of
\overline{\mathfrak{m}}
by averaging the
empirical measure across trajectories. Consequently, if

\{(s_{\eta},m_{\eta})\}_{\eta=1}^{N_{D}}
, with
N_{D}=N\cdot L
, denotes the
collection of
N
observations obtained from these
L
trajectories,
then one empirical approximation of (
23
),
based on the undiscounted measure, is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{ji}(\omega)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N_{D}}\sum_{\eta=1}^{N_{D}}\psi_{j}(s_{\eta},m_{\eta})\,\Bigl(\beta\,\psi_{i}(s_{\eta},m_{\eta})-(\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi_{\omega}}\phi_{i})(s_{\eta},m_{\eta})\Bigr),

\displaystyle\widehat{b}_{j}(\omega)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N_{D}}\sum_{\eta=1}^{N_{D}}r_{\lambda}^{\pi_{\omega}}(s_{\eta},m_{\eta})\,\psi_{j}(s_{\eta},m_{\eta}),

for
i,j=1,\dots,n
. From a modeling viewpoint, the choice of the features
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k}
should reflect the expected structure of the value function. In linear-quadratic problems, moment-based features are especially natural, because the value function depends on
\mu
through a small number of low-order moments; see
Proposition

6.4
. For more general problems, one may enrich the family of features by adding nonlinear observables of the population distribution.

5.3.
Policy gradient

Let
\pi_{\omega}
be a parametrized policy, with parameter

\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and let
V_{n}^{\pi_{\omega}}
be either the critic obtained
from (
24
), or, more generally, any
finite-dimensional approximation of
V^{\pi_{\omega}}
for which the
derivatives entering the policy gradient formula are well defined and
can be evaluated. The policy gradient theorem from
Section
4
provides an exact gradient formula
in terms of a representative contribution and a population
contribution. For implementation, we keep exactly the same structure,
but replace the exact value function
V^{\pi_{\omega}}
by its
approximation
V_{n}^{\pi_{\omega}}
.

Accordingly, we define

q_{\mathrm{rep},n}^{\pi_{\omega}}
and

q_{\mathrm{pop},n}^{\pi_{\omega}}
as in

Definition

4.2
, with
V^{\pi}
replaced by

V_{n}^{\pi_{\omega}}
. The resulting approximate policy-gradient
direction is

(27)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\omega}J_{n}^{\pi_{\omega}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\Biggl(\int_{\mathcal{A}}q_{\mathrm{rep},n}^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu,a)\,\nabla_{\omega}\log p_{\pi_{\omega}}(a\mid s,\mu)\,\pi_{\omega}(\mathrm{d}a\mid s,\mu)

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\int_{\mathcal{A}}q_{\mathrm{pop},n}^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu,\xi,a)\,\nabla_{\omega}\log p_{\pi_{\omega}}(a\mid\xi,\mu)\,\pi_{\omega}(\mathrm{d}a\mid\xi,\mu)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi)\Biggr)\rho^{\pi_{\omega}}(\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}\mu).

When the critic is expanded in cylindrical basis functions,

q_{\mathrm{rep},n}^{\pi_{\omega}}
and

q_{\mathrm{pop},n}^{\pi_{\omega}}
can be evaluated explicitly. Indeed,
in that case the derivatives of
V_{n}^{\pi_{\omega}}
are given directly by
the formulas in (
25
). For numerical purposes, (
27
) may be
approximated from a finite collection of observations

\{(s_{\eta},\mu_{\eta})\}_{\eta=1}^{N_{D}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
together with nonnegative weights

\{w_{\eta}\}_{\eta=1}^{N_{D}}
. This yields the empirical approximation

(28)

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla_{\omega}J_{n}^{\pi_{\omega}}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{\eta=1}^{N_{D}}w_{\eta}\Biggl(\int_{\mathcal{A}}q_{\mathrm{rep},n}^{\pi_{\omega}}(s_{\eta},\mu_{\eta},a)\,\nabla_{\omega}\log p_{\pi_{\omega}}(a\mid s_{\eta},\mu_{\eta})\,\pi_{\omega}(\mathrm{d}a\mid s_{\eta},\mu_{\eta})

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\int_{\mathcal{A}}q_{\mathrm{pop},n}^{\pi_{\omega}}(s_{\eta},\mu_{\eta},\xi,a)\,\nabla_{\omega}\log p_{\pi_{\omega}}(a\mid\xi,\mu_{\eta})\,\pi_{\omega}(\mathrm{d}a\mid\xi,\mu_{\eta})\,\mu_{\eta}(\mathrm{d}\xi)\Biggr).

Several remarks on numerically computing the above gradient are in order.
First, the integral can be approximated by empirical mean through sampling actions
a_{j}
from the current policy and sampling states
s_{i}
from the current
\mu_{\eta}(\xi)
. Second, regarding the weight
w_{\eta}
,
when the measure in
(
27
) is chosen as the discounted
occupancy measure
\rho^{\pi_{\omega}}
, we consider

L
independent trajectories

\{(s_{\tau}^{\ell},\mu_{\tau}^{\ell})\}_{\tau=0}^{N-1}
,

\ell=1,\dots,L
, of the controlled process

(s_{t}^{\pi_{\omega}},\mu_{t}^{\pi_{\omega}})
sampled on the time grid

t_{\tau}=\tau\Delta t
, and identify the observations in
(
28
) with these data, so that

N_{D}=LN
and

w_{\tau}^{\ell}=\frac{1}{L}e^{-\beta t_{\tau}}\Delta t
. However, numerically we found that simply setting
w_{\eta}=\frac{1}{N_{D}}
also provides a good approximation of the ascent direction.
Finally, regardless of the specific approximation used for

\nabla_{\omega}J_{n}^{\pi_{\omega}}
, the parameter update is of the form

\omega^{(k+1)}=\omega^{(k)}+\alpha_{k}\,\widehat{\nabla_{\omega}J_{n}^{\pi_{\omega}}},

where
\alpha_{k}>0
is the stepsize.

The discussion above leads to
Algorithm

1
.

.

.

for

6.
Infinite-Time Mean-Field Linear Quadratic Regulator

In this section we analyze the infinite-horizon mean-field linear-quadratic regulator with stationary randomized feedback policies and entropy regularization. Our aim is to derive an explicit characterization of the optimal policy and of the associated value function in terms of a stationary algebraic Riccati system. Besides its intrinsic interest as a solvable class of mean-field control problems, this model is also relevant as a benchmark for entropy-regularized reinforcement learning, where randomized policies and Shannon regularization naturally arise in connection with exploration.

6.1.
Problem setting

Let
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
and set
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
. For notational simplicity we take a one-dimensional Brownian motion; the extension to a multi-dimensional Brownian motion is immediate by interpreting
\|\cdot\|_{F}
and
\sigma\sigma^{\top}
accordingly. Fix
(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and consider the representative and population dynamics introduced in (
4
)-(
5
), where
\tilde{s}_{0}\sim\mu
and
s_{0}=s
, with coefficients

(29)

b(x,\mu,a)=Ax+\bar{A}\,\bar{\mu}+Ba,\qquad\sigma(x,\mu,a)=\gamma+Dx+\bar{D}\,\bar{\mu}+Fa,\qquad\bar{\mu}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x),

for fixed matrices
A,\bar{A},D,\bar{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
B,F\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
, and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
Q,\bar{Q}\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}
,
N\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{m}
,
I\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
, and
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
H\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We consider the running reward

(30)

r(x,\mu,a)=-\Bigl(x^{\top}Qx+\bar{\mu}^{\top}\bar{Q}\,\bar{\mu}+a^{\top}Na+2a^{\top}Ix+2M^{\top}x+2H^{\top}a\Bigr),

and, for
\lambda>0
, the entropy-regularized reward
r_{\lambda}
introduced in (
3
). For
\beta>0
we define

V^{\pi}(s,\mu):=\mathbb{E}^{s,\mu,\pi}\!\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta t}\,r_{\lambda}(s_{t},\mu_{t},a_{t}^{s})\,\mathrm{d}t\right],\qquad V^{*}(s,\mu):=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}}V^{\pi}(s,\mu),

where
\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}
denotes the class of all stationary randomized feedback policies
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
such that
\pi(\cdot\mid x,\mu)
admits a density
p_{\pi}(x,\mu,\cdot)
with respect to Lebesgue measure,
V^{\pi}(s,\mu)<\infty
for every
(s,\mu)
, and, for every initial condition
(s,\mu)
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-\beta t}\bigl(\mathbb{E}|s_{t}|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu_{t})\bigr)=0.

Remark 6.1
.

A sufficient, but not necessary, condition to ensure that
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}
is that
b^{\pi}
,
\sigma^{\pi}
, and
r_{\lambda}^{\pi}
satisfy Assumption
3.1
, and that the dissipativity estimate (
12
) holds. Thus,
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}
by
Theorem

3.3
. In the LQ setting, Assumption
3.1
is ensured by assuming that
m_{\pi}\in\mathcal{C}^{2,2}
with bounded derivatives, that
q_{\pi}
and
\mathcal{H}\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x,\mu)\bigr)
belong to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{poly}}^{2,2}
, and that
m_{\pi}
has at most linear growth in
(x,\sqrt{m_{2}(\mu)})
, where

m_{\pi}(x,\mu):=\int_{\mathcal{A}}a\,\pi(\mathrm{d}a\mid x,\mu),\qquad q_{\pi}(x,\mu):=\int_{\mathcal{A}}a^{\top}Na\,\pi(\mathrm{d}a\mid x,\mu),

and
\mathcal{H}\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x,\mu)\bigr)
is defined in (
3
). A sufficient condition for (
12
) is that
2\,x^{\top}Ax+\|Dx\|_{F}^{2}\leq c_{0}-\alpha|x|^{2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
for some
c_{0}\geq 0
and some
\alpha>0
such that the remaining terms involving
\bar{A},\bar{D},B,F
and
m_{\pi}
can be absorbed by Young’s inequality.

Definition 6.2

(Stabilizability, detectability, and mean-square stabilizability)
.

The pair
(A,B)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
is

stabilizable
if there exists
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
such that

A+B\Theta
is Hurwitz. The pair

(A,H)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{q\times d}
is

detectable
if there exists
L\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times q}
such that

A-LH
is Hurwitz.

(A4) Discounted LQ conditions.

Let
A_{\beta}:=A-\frac{\beta}{2}I_{d},
and
\tilde{A}_{\beta}:=A+\bar{A}-\frac{\beta}{2}I_{d}.
We assume that the following conditions hold:

(H1)

N\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{m}
and

Q-I^{\top}N^{-1}I\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d},\qquad Q+\bar{Q}-I^{\top}N^{-1}I\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d}.

(H2)

The pair
(\tilde{A}_{\beta},B)
is stabilizable, and there exist

\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
and
P_{0}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d}
such that

(A_{\beta}+B\Theta)^{\top}P_{0}+P_{0}(A_{\beta}+B\Theta)+(D+F\Theta)^{\top}P_{0}(D+F\Theta)\preceq-I_{d}.

Remark 6.3
.

Assumption
(H1)
implies in particular that the pairs
(A_{\beta},(Q-I^{\top}N^{-1}I)^{1/2})
and
(\tilde{A}_{\beta},(Q+\bar{Q}-I^{\top}N^{-1}I)^{1/2})
are detectable. On the other hand, when
D=F=0
, condition
(H2)
is equivalent to assuming that both pairs
(A_{\beta},B)
and
(\tilde{A}_{\beta},B)
are stabilizable.

Let us consider the quadratic function

(31)

V(s,\mu)=-\Bigl((s-\bar{\mu})^{\top}K(s-\bar{\mu})+\bar{\mu}^{\top}\Lambda\,\bar{\mu}+2Y^{\top}s+R\Bigr),

where
K,\Lambda\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}
,
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
R\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy the algebraic system

(32)

\displaystyle\beta K

\displaystyle=Q+KA+A^{\top}K+D^{\top}KD-U^{\top}S^{-1}U,

(33)

\displaystyle\beta\Lambda

\displaystyle=Q+\bar{Q}+\Lambda(A+\bar{A})+(A+\bar{A})^{\top}\Lambda+(D+\bar{D})^{\top}K(D+\bar{D})-W^{\top}S^{-1}W,

(34)

\displaystyle\beta Y

\displaystyle=M+A^{\top}Y+D^{\top}K\gamma-U^{\top}S^{-1}O,

(35)

\displaystyle\beta R

\displaystyle=\gamma^{\top}K\gamma-O^{\top}S^{-1}O-\frac{\lambda}{2}\Bigl(m\log(\pi_{0}\lambda)-\log\det S\Bigr),

where

(36)

S:=N+F^{\top}KF,\qquad O:=H+B^{\top}Y+F^{\top}K\gamma,

and

(37)

U:=I+B^{\top}K+F^{\top}KD,\qquad W:=I+B^{\top}\Lambda+F^{\top}K(D+\bar{D}).

Here
\pi_{0}
denotes the mathematical constant.

Proposition 6.4
.

Assume
Section

6.1
and let
(K,\Lambda,Y,R)
be a solution of
(
32
)-(
35
). Let
V
be given by
(
31
), and let
\pi^{*}
be defined as

(38)

\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid s,\mu)=\mathcal{N}\!\left(-S^{-1}\bigl(Us+(W-U)\bar{\mu}+O\bigr),\;\frac{\lambda}{2}S^{-1}\right).

Then

V^{*}(s,\mu)=V^{\pi^{*}}(s,\mu)=V(s,\mu)\qquad\text{for all }(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

and
\pi^{*}
is the unique optimal stationary feedback policy in
\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}
.

The proof of
Proposition

6.4
is provided in
Section

A.3
.

7.
Numerical Examples

7.1.
Example 1: Mean-field systemic risk model

Mean-field models of systemic risk describe a large population of interacting agents whose individual states are attracted to the current population average, while each agent can act through a control representing, for instance, borrowing or lending to the system; See
[
26
,
24
]
. In our stationary discounted setting introduced in
Section

6
, this leads to a one-dimensional linear-quadratic specification in which the state is penalized through its deviation from the population mean and the control is penalized quadratically.

7.1.1.
Problem setup

We consider the one-dimensional mean-field LQR dynamics in the particular systemic risk configuration, namely

\mathrm{d}s_{t}=\bigl(A(s_{t}-\bar{\mu}_{t})+Ba_{t}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\gamma\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},\qquad a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot\,|\,s_{t},\mu_{t}),\qquad\bar{\mu}_{t}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\,\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}x),

where
\mu_{t}=\Law(\tilde{s}_{t})
is the population law generated by the mean-field environment (
5
). This corresponds to the general framework of
Section

6
with
d=m=1,

\bar{A}=-A
,
D=\bar{D}=F=0,
and
I=M=H=0.
We take the running reward

(39)

r(x,\mu,a)=-\Bigl(Q(x-\bar{\mu})^{2}+\bar{Q}\,\bar{\mu}^{2}+Na^{2}\Bigr),

with
Q,\bar{Q}\geq 0
and
N>0
. Our goal is to maximize the expected long-run regularized reward

(40)

\displaystyle V^{*}(s,\mu)\,:=\,\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{add}}}\left\{V^{\pi}(s,\mu):=\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta t}\,r_{\lambda}^{\pi}(s_{t},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\;\bigg|\;s_{0}=s,\ \mu_{0}=\mu\right]\right\},

for every
(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
where the entropy-regularized
r_{\lambda}^{\pi}(s,\mu)
is as in (
3
) with
r(s,\mu,a)
defined in (
39
). Following
Proposition

6.4
, the optimal policy is given by

(41)

\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid s,\mu)=\mathcal{N}\!\left(-N^{-1}B^{\top}\bigl(K(s-\bar{\mu})+\Lambda\,\bar{\mu}\bigr),\frac{\lambda}{2}N^{-1}\right).

The corresponding optimal value function is

(42)

V^{*}(s,\mu)=V^{\pi^{*}}(s,\mu)=-\Bigl((s-\bar{\mu})^{\top}K(s-\bar{\mu})+\bar{\mu}^{\top}\Lambda\,\bar{\mu}+R\Bigr),

where
K,\Lambda\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{d}
and
R\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy the reduced algebraic system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\beta K&=Q+KA+A^{\top}K-KBN^{-1}B^{\top}K,\\
\beta\Lambda&=Q+\bar{Q}-\Lambda BN^{-1}B^{\top}\Lambda,\\
\beta R&=\gamma^{\top}K\gamma-\frac{\lambda}{2}\Bigl(m\log(\pi_{0}\lambda)-\log\det N\Bigr).\end{cases}

7.1.2.
Policy parametrization and value approximation

Following the theoretical optimal policy (
41
), we consider the family of Gaussian policies parametrized as

(43)

\pi_{\omega}(\cdot\mid s,\mu)=\mathcal{N}\bigl(\omega_{1}(s-\bar{\mu})+\omega_{2}\bar{\mu},\,\lambda\bigr),

where
\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. On the other hand, following (
42
), the value function can be approximated by the quadratic cylindrical ansatz

(44)

V_{\theta}(s,\mu)=\theta_{3}\,(s-\bar{\mu})^{2}+\theta_{2}\,\bar{\mu}^{2}+\theta_{1},\qquad\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

that is, following the notation of (
20
), we are taking
\phi_{1}(s,\mu)=1
,
\phi_{2}(s,\mu)=\bar{\mu}^{2}
, and
\phi_{3}(s,\mu)=(s-\bar{\mu})^{2}
.

7.1.3.
Implementation and numerical results

We implement the model-based actor–critic procedure described in
Section

5
for the scalar mean-field LQR example. The actor is parametrized by the Gaussian policy (
43
), and the critic is sought in the quadratic space generated by (
44
). Throughout the experiment we use

A=-1,\qquad B=1,\qquad Q=1,\qquad\bar{Q}=1,\qquad N=\tfrac{1}{2},\qquad\beta=1,\qquad\gamma=0.5,\qquad\lambda=0.2.

The population is initialized with mean
\bar{\mu}_{0}=1
, and the initial representative state is sampled from
\mu_{0}=\mathcal{N}(1,1)
. Since the population dynamics are closed at the level of the mean
\bar{\mu}_{t}
, no auxiliary particle approximation of the population law is needed in this example. The representative state is sampled from the exact closed-loop Gaussian transition, while
\bar{\mu}_{t}
is propagated through the corresponding deterministic mean equation. The discounted integrals are approximated on
[0,T]
, with
T=8
and
\Delta t=0.05
. We use
100
Monte Carlo trajectories per actor step, a constant learning rate
\alpha=5\times 10^{-2}
, and run
1000
policy-improvement iterations.

For a fixed actor parameter
\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
, the aggregated coefficients are

b^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu)=A(s-\bar{\mu})+B\bigl(\omega_{1}(s-\bar{\mu})+\omega_{2}\bar{\mu}\bigr),\qquad\Sigma^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu)=\gamma^{2}.

Thus, the Galerkin critic is computed by solving the linear system (
22
), with
A(\omega)
and
b(\omega)
defined in (
23
), after replacing the general generator
\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi_{\omega}}
by the present LQR generator. For the basis

\phi_{1}(s,\mu)=1,\qquad\phi_{2}(s,\mu)=\bar{\mu}^{2},\qquad\phi_{3}(s,\mu)=(s-\bar{\mu})^{2},

one obtains

\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi_{\omega}}\phi_{1}=0,\qquad\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi_{\omega}}\phi_{2}=2B\omega_{2}\,\bar{\mu}^{2},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi_{\omega}}\phi_{3}=2(A+B\omega_{1})(s-\bar{\mu})^{2}+\gamma^{2}.

At each actor step, the identities above are inserted into the Galerkin system (
22
), with
A(\omega)
and
b(\omega)
defined in (
23
), to compute the critic coefficients
\theta^{(k)}
and then update the actor through the policy-gradient direction in (
28
). From a computational viewpoint, in this benchmark the critic space has dimension three, so policy evaluation reduces to solving a
3\times 3
linear system. Moreover, the Gaussian structure of the policy allows the conditional expectation over the action variable in the actor update to be evaluated analytically. The remaining numerical cost comes from the empirical approximation of the discounted occupancy measure along simulated trajectories. Since the closed-loop dynamics are linear and Gaussian for each fixed actor parameter, these trajectories are sampled from the exact transition law, while the population mean is propagated through its deterministic closed equation.

The closed-form Riccati solution provides a benchmark for the optimal feedback and the optimal value function.

\bar{\mu}

Figure

We also implement the undiscounted empirical measure
\overline{\mathfrak{m}}
in (
26
), which can be viewed as a biased empirical approximation of the discounted occupancy measure in (
27
), since it assigns uniform rather than discounted weights along the finite time window. The corresponding results, shown in
Figure

with stepsize
\alpha=1\times 10^{-1}
, illustrate the same qualitative behavior as in the discounted case.

7.2.
Example 2: Crowd-aversion transport

The second example illustrates the actor–critic method in a nonlinear mean-field setting for which no closed-form Riccati benchmark is available. The objective is to transport a representative agent toward a prescribed target while penalizing trajectories that pass through regions of high crowd density. The crowd is described by a population law
\mu_{t}
, which is approximated numerically by an empirical measure generated by a finite particle system.

7.2.1.
Problem setup

We consider the controlled diffusion on
\mathbb{R}^{2}

\mathrm{d}s_{t}=a_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},\qquad a_{t}\sim\pi_{\omega}(\cdot\mid s_{t},\mu_{t}),

where
a_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the control,
\sigma>0
is a constant volatility, and
\mu_{t}
denotes the population distribution observed by the representative agent. The reward is defined by

(45)

r(s,\mu,a)=-\left(\frac{1}{2}\|a\|^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|s-s_{\mathrm{tar}}\|^{2}+\gamma\,(K_{\eta}*\mu)(s)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|s\|^{2}\right),

where
s_{\mathrm{tar}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the target location,
\kappa>0
controls the strength of the attraction to the target,
\gamma>0
controls the crowd-aversion penalty, and
\rho\geq 0
is a confinement parameter. The crowd-density term is computed through the Gaussian kernel convolution

(K_{\eta}*\mu)(s):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|s-s^{\prime}\|^{2}}{2\eta^{2}}\right)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}),

with bandwidth
\eta>0
. The goal is to maximize the corresponding infinite-horizon entropy-regularized objective (
2
) with reward given by (
45
).

Remark 7.1
.

The four terms in (
45
) have distinct roles. The first one penalizes large controls, the second one attracts the representative agent toward
s_{\mathrm{tar}}
, the third one penalizes positions located in high-density regions of the crowd, and the last one adds a mild confinement on the unbounded domain.

7.2.2.
Policy parametrization and value approximation

We use a Gaussian policy on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with fixed exploration variance,

\pi_{\omega}(\cdot\mid s,\mu)=\mathcal{N}\bigl(m_{\omega}(s,\mu),\sigma_{a}^{2}I_{2}\bigr),

where the mean is parametrized as
m_{\omega}(s,\mu)=Wf(s,\mu)
, with
\omega\equiv W\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times d_{f}}
. In order to generate trajectories without an Euler–Maruyama approximation, we use an affine feature map depending on the state and on the empirical population mean,

f(s,\mu)=\bigl(1,\,s_{1},\,s_{2},\,s_{1}-s_{\mathrm{tar},1},\,s_{2}-s_{\mathrm{tar},2},\,\bar{\mu}_{1},\,\bar{\mu}_{2}\bigr),\qquad\bar{\mu}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}x\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x).

With this choice, the finite-particle approximation of the aggregated representative–population system is an affine linear diffusion. Hence, at each actor–critic iteration, the training trajectories are sampled from the exact Gaussian transition of this affine system, computed through the corresponding matrix exponential.

The critic is constructed as in the Galerkin policy-evaluation procedure of
Section

5.1
. To represent the dependence on the population law, we use kernel-embedding features

u_{j}(\mu):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-c_{j}\|^{2}}{2h^{2}}\right)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x),\qquad j=1,\dots,M_{\mu},

where
\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M_{\mu}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
are fixed centers and
h>0
is the bandwidth. For the state variable we use Gaussian radial basis functions
\psi_{m}(s)=\exp(-\|s-z_{m}\|^{2}/(2s_{x}^{2}))
,
m=1,\dots,M_{s}
. The critic basis is then given by

\phi_{m,0}(s,\mu)=\psi_{m}(s),\qquad\phi_{m,j}(s,\mu)=\psi_{m}(s)\,u_{j}(\mu),\qquad m=1,\dots,M_{s},\quad j=1,\dots,M_{\mu}.

Thus, with
n=M_{s}(M_{\mu}+1)
, the value function is approximated in
\mathbb{V}_{n}=\mathrm{span}\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\}
by
V_{n}^{\pi_{\omega}}(s,\mu)=\theta^{\top}\Phi(s,\mu)
.

7.2.3.
Implementation and numerical results

The implementation follows the actor–critic algorithm of
Section

5
. For each actor parameter
W
, the critic is obtained by solving the Galerkin system (
22
), with
A(W)
and
b(W)
defined as in (
23
), after replacing the abstract generator
\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi_{\omega}}
by the known generator of the crowd-aversion dynamics. The resulting critic
V_{n}^{\pi_{\omega}}
is then inserted into the empirical policy-gradient formula (
28
) to update the actor.

The numerical parameters are

\sigma=0.2,\qquad\beta=0.1,\qquad\kappa=1000,\qquad\gamma=10,\qquad\eta=0.8,\qquad\rho=0.1.

The entropy parameter is
\lambda=2
, the exploration standard deviation is
\sigma_{a}=0.25
, and the target is
s_{\mathrm{tar}}=(2,0)
. In the reported experiment, both the initial population law and the initial representative law are centered near
(-2,0)
, with Gaussian perturbations of standard deviation
0.02
. The implementation also allows these two initial laws to be chosen independently.

The population law is approximated by an empirical measure

(46)

\widehat{\mu}_{t}^{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}}.

Accordingly, the crowd-density term is evaluated as

(K_{\eta}*\widehat{\mu}_{t}^{N})(s_{t})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\exp\left(-\frac{\|s_{t}-X_{t}^{i}\|^{2}}{2\eta^{2}}\right).

From an implementation viewpoint, the main additional cost in this crowd-aversion example comes from the particle approximation of the population law. Since
\widehat{\mu}_{t}^{N}
is represented by the empirical measure in (
46
), several realizations of the coupled representative–population dynamics must be generated to assemble the Galerkin system and evaluate the policy-gradient direction. Nevertheless, the implementation exploits the affine structure of the actor with respect to the state and the empirical population mean, which allows us to sample the coupled system from its exact Gaussian transition.

At each actor–critic iteration we generate
L=24
independent simulations, each with
N=64
particles representing the crowd. The discounted objective is truncated at
T=5
and evaluated on the time grid
\Delta t=0.05
. We run
500
actor updates with constant stepsize
\alpha=5\times 10^{-4}
. The actor matrix is initialized at zero, its entries are clipped for stability, and the Galerkin system is solved with ridge parameter
10^{-2}
. The RBF centers for both the state and measure features are placed on a
5\times 5
Cartesian grid on
[-8,4]^{2}
. State and particle locations are clipped only when evaluating the RBF features; the simulated dynamics themselves are not clipped.

Unlike the LQR example, this nonlinear crowd-aversion problem does not admit a closed-form optimal policy or value function. We therefore assess the method through diagnostics that measure stability and qualitative performance.

The trajectory plot illustrates whether the learned policy transports the representative agent from the initial region near
(-2,0)
toward the target while accounting for the crowd-aversion term. Finally,

\bar{\mathrm{m}}
, defined in (

), is considered. Note that its qualitative behavior is similar to the discounted case shown in

Appendix A
Proofs of the Main Results

A.1.
Proofs for
Section

3

We first establish well-posedness of the system together with moment
estimates that are explicit in time. Estimates of this type are
standard in the literature; see, for instance,
[
8
,
20
]
. However, for the infinite-horizon
argument we need bounds with an explicit exponential dependence on
t
, so that they can be combined with the discount factor
e^{-\beta t}
when passing to the limit as
T\to\infty
. A generic finite-horizon
bound of the form
C_{T}
, with no explicit control of its dependence on

T
, would not be sufficient for this purpose. We therefore state and
prove the corresponding estimates for general
p
-moments.

Lemma A.1

(Well-posedness and exponential moment growth)
.

Suppose Assumption
3.1
holds.
Fix an even integer
p\geq 2
.
Then, for every
(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the system
(
4
)-(
5
) admits a unique strong
solution on
[0,\infty)
. Moreover, there exist constants

C_{p}\geq 1
and
\beta_{0}(p)>0
, depending only on
p
and the
structural constants in Assumption
3.1
, such that for all

t\geq 0
,

(47)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[|s_{t}|^{p}\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[|\tilde{s}_{t}|^{p}\right]\leq C_{p}\,e^{\beta_{0}(p)\,t}\bigl(1+|s|^{p}+m_{p}(\mu)\bigr).

In particular, for
p=4
, for each
T>0
there exists
C_{T}>0

such that

(48)

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}^{s,\mu,\pi}\!\left[|s_{t}|^{4}\right]+\mathbb{E}^{s,\mu,\pi}\!\left[|\tilde{s}_{t}|^{4}\right]\Bigr)\leq C_{p}e^{\beta_{0}(p)T}\bigl(1+|s|^{4}+m_{4}(\mu)\bigr),

and
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}m_{2}(\mu_{t})\leq C_{T}(1+m_{4}(\mu)^{1/2})
.

Proof.

Step 1 (Existence and uniqueness).

Under Assumption
3.1
, the McKean-Vlasov SDE (
5
)
has globally Lipschitz and linearly growing coefficients

b^{\pi}
and
\sigma^{\pi}
; see Remark
3.2
.
Hence, by the standard well-posedness theory for McKean-Vlasov SDEs,
(
5
) admits a unique strong solution
\tilde{s}
for
every initial law
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
The population flow
\mu_{t}:=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{s}_{t})
is therefore
well defined.
Conditioning on the deterministic flow
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, the
representative equation (
4
) is an SDE with
progressively measurable coefficients, globally Lipschitz in
s
and
with linear growth, so it also admits a unique strong solution.

Step 2 (
p
-moment bound for
\tilde{s}_{t}
).

Let
f(x)=|x|^{p}
. Since
p\geq 2
is even,
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

\nabla f(x)=p|x|^{p-2}x,\qquad D^{2}f(x)=p(p-2)|x|^{p-4}xx^{\top}+p|x|^{p-2}I_{d}.

For
n\geq 1
, define the stopping time
\tau_{n}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\,|\tilde{s}_{t}|>n\}.

Applying Itô’s formula to
f(\tilde{s}_{t})
on
[0,t\wedge\tau_{n}]

gives

(49)

\displaystyle|\tilde{s}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}|^{p}=|\tilde{s}_{0}|^{p}+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{n}}\Bigl[\nabla f(\tilde{s}_{u})\cdot b^{\pi}(\tilde{s}_{u},\mu_{u})+\tfrac{1}{2}D^{2}f(\tilde{s}_{u}):\Sigma^{\pi}(\tilde{s}_{u},\mu_{u})\Bigr]\mathrm{d}u+M_{t\wedge\tau_{n}},

where
M_{t}
is a local martingale,
hence
\mathbb{E}[M_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}]=0
. Using (
9
), Cauchy-Schwarz, and Young’s
inequality, there exists a constant
C_{p}>0
, depending only on

p
and
K_{\pi}
, such that for all
(x,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(50)

\displaystyle|\nabla f(x)\cdot b^{\pi}(x,\mu)|\leq C_{p}\bigl(1+|x|^{p}+m_{2}(\mu)^{p/2}\bigr),\quad D^{2}f(x):\Sigma^{\pi}(x,\mu)\leq C_{p}\bigl(1+|x|^{p}+m_{2}(\mu)^{p/2}\bigr).

Taking expectations in (
49
) yields

\mathbb{E}\!\left[|\tilde{s}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}|^{p}\right]\leq m_{p}(\mu)+C_{p}\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl(1+\mathbb{E}\!\left[|\tilde{s}_{u\wedge\tau_{n}}|^{p}\right]+m_{2}(\mu_{u})^{p/2}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}u.

Set
y_{n}(t):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[|\tilde{s}_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}|^{p}\right]
,
y(t):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[|\tilde{s}_{t}|^{p}\right],
and
z(t):=\sup_{n\geq 1}y_{n}(t).
Since
\mu_{u}=\mathcal{L}(\tilde{s}_{u})
, Hölder’s inequality gives

m_{2}(\mu_{u})^{p/2}=\Bigl(\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{s}_{u}|^{2}]\Bigr)^{p/2}\leq\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{s}_{u}|^{p}]=y(u).

Therefore, for every
n\geq 1
,

(51)

\displaystyle y_{n}(t)\leq m_{p}(\mu)+C_{p}\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(1+y_{n}(u)+y(u)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}u.

Now, for each fixed
u\geq 0
, Fatou’s lemma implies
y(u)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}|\tilde{s}_{u\wedge\tau_{n}}|^{p}\right]\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}y_{n}(u)\leq z(u).
Hence (
51
) implies

y_{n}(t)\leq m_{p}(\mu)+C_{p}\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(1+2z(u)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}u,\qquad n\geq 1.

Taking the supremum over
n
gives

z(t)\leq m_{p}(\mu)+C_{p}\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(1+2z(u)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}u.

By Grönwall’s inequality,
z(t)\leq\bigl(m_{p}(\mu)+1\bigr)e^{2C_{p}t}
for
t\geq 0
. Since
y(t)\leq z(t)
, we conclude that

(52)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[|\tilde{s}_{t}|^{p}\right]\leq\bigl(m_{p}(\mu)+1\bigr)e^{\beta_{0}t},\qquad\beta_{0}:=2C_{p}.

Step 3 (
p
-moment bound for
s_{t}
).

Define
\sigma_{n}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\,|s_{t}|>n\},
and
v_{n}(t):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[|s_{t\wedge\sigma_{n}}|^{p}\right].
Repeating the same localized Itô argument for (
4
),
and using (
52
) to control

m_{2}(\mu_{t})^{p/2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle v_{n}(t)\leq|s|^{p}+C_{p}\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(1+v_{n}(u)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}u+C_{p}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\!\left[|\tilde{s}_{u}|^{p}\right]\,\mathrm{d}u.

Using (
52
), it follows that

v_{n}(t)\leq|s|^{p}+C_{p}\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(1+v_{n}(u)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}u+C_{p}\bigl(m_{p}(\mu)+1\bigr)\int_{0}^{t}e^{\beta_{0}u}\,\mathrm{d}u.

Another application of Grönwall’s inequality yields

v_{n}(t)\leq C_{p}e^{\beta_{0}t}\bigl(1+|s|^{p}+m_{p}(\mu)\bigr),\qquad t\geq 0,

after enlarging
C_{p}
if necessary. Letting

and using
Fatou’s lemma gives

(53)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[|s_{t}|^{p}\right]\leq C_{p}e^{\beta_{0}t}\bigl(1+|s|^{p}+m_{p}(\mu)\bigr).

Step 4 (Conclusion).

Combining (
52
) and
(
53
), and enlarging the constant if necessary,
we obtain (
47
).
For
p=4
, the same argument gives the finite-horizon estimate
(
48
). Finally, for
t\in[0,T]
,

m_{2}(\mu_{t})=\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{s}_{t}|^{2}]\leq\Bigl(\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{s}_{t}|^{4}]\Bigr)^{1/2}\leq C_{T}\bigl(1+m_{4}(\mu)^{1/2}\bigr),

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark A.3

(A dissipative case)
.

Assume that there exist constants
\kappa>C_{\mathrm{diss}}>0
and

C_{0}\geq 0
such that, for all

Applying Itô’s formula to

, one obtains

Applying Itô’s formula to

Lemma A.4

(Exponential bounds for the derivatives of the decoupled flow)

Suppose Assumption
3.1
holds.
Fix an even integer

For

be the

5
). For each

denote the unique strong
solution of

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{x,\mu}=b^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},\qquad X_{0}^{x,\mu}=x.

Then, for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,\mu}
,
D_{xx}^{2}X_{t}^{x,\mu}
,

\partial_{\mu}X_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi)
, and

D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}X_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi)

p
and

one has, for all

,

,

, and

(54)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,\mu}\|^{p}+\|D^{2}_{xx}X_{t}^{x,\mu}\|^{p}+\|\partial_{\mu}X_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi)\|^{p}+\|D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}X_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi)\|^{p}\right]\leq C_{p,d}(1+K_{\pi}^{p})e^{\beta_{\mathrm{var}}(p)t}.

Proof.

Under Assumption
3.1
, the coefficients
b^{\pi}
and
\sigma^{\pi}
are twice differentiable with respect to the state variable and Lions differentiable with respect to the measure argument, with bounded derivatives up to second order. Therefore, the differentiability results of
[
20
, Theorem 3.1 and Proposition 4.1]
apply, and the derivatives
\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,\mu}
,
D^{2}_{xx}X_{t}^{x,\mu}
,
\partial_{\mu}X_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi)
, and
D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}X_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi)
are well defined. It remains to prove the estimate (
54
). For simplicity, write

J_{t}^{x,\mu}:=\nabla_{x}X_{t}^{x,\mu},\qquad H_{t}^{x,\mu}:=D^{2}_{xx}X_{t}^{x,\mu},\qquad U_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi):=\partial_{\mu}X_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi),\qquad V_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi):=D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}X_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi).

We denote by
\sigma^{\pi}_{\ell}
the
\ell
-th column of
\sigma^{\pi}
,

\ell=1,\dots,d
.
Since only upper bounds are relevant, we may enlarge
K_{\pi}
once and
for all and assume
K_{\pi}\geq 1
.

Step 1 (Bounds for the first spatial derivative).

Differentiating the decoupled SDE with respect to
x
gives

(55)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}J_{t}^{x,\mu}=\nabla_{s}b^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})J_{t}^{x,\mu}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\nabla_{s}\sigma_{\ell}^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})J_{t}^{x,\mu}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{\ell},\qquad J_{0}^{x,\mu}=I_{d}.

Since each entry of
\nabla_{s}b^{\pi}
and
\nabla_{s}\sigma^{\pi}_{\ell}

is bounded by
K_{\pi}
, we have

(56)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{s}b^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})\|\leq\sqrt{d}\,K_{\pi},\qquad\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\|\nabla_{s}\sigma_{\ell}^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})\|^{2}\leq d^{2}K_{\pi}^{2}.

Applying Itô’s formula to
\|J_{t}^{x,\mu}\|^{p}
, integrating over

[0,t]
, and taking expectations, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|J_{t}^{x,\mu}\|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\|I_{d}\|^{p}+p\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|J_{r}^{x,\mu}\|^{p-2}\left\langle J_{r}^{x,\mu},\nabla_{s}b^{\pi}(X_{r}^{x,\mu},\mu_{r})J_{r}^{x,\mu}\right\rangle\right]\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{p}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|J_{r}^{x,\mu}\|^{p-2}\|\nabla_{s}\sigma_{\ell}^{\pi}(X_{r}^{x,\mu},\mu_{r})J_{r}^{x,\mu}\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{p(p-2)}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|J_{r}^{x,\mu}\|^{p-4}\left\langle J_{r}^{x,\mu},\nabla_{s}\sigma_{\ell}^{\pi}(X_{r}^{x,\mu},\mu_{r})J_{r}^{x,\mu}\right\rangle^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}r.

Using (
56
), and

\langle z,Az\rangle\leq\|A\|\,\|z\|^{2}
, and recalling

,
we infer that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|J_{t}^{x,\mu}\|^{p}\right]\leq d^{p/2}+a_{p,d}K_{\pi}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|J_{r}^{x,\mu}\|^{p}\right]\mathrm{d}r.

Grönwall’s inequality gives

(57)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|J_{t}^{x,\mu}\|^{p}\right]\leq d^{p/2}e^{a_{p,d}K_{\pi}^{2}t}.

Step 2 (Bounds for the second spatial derivative).

Differentiating (
55
) once more with respect to
x

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}H_{t}^{x,\mu}

\displaystyle=\Bigl[\nabla_{s}b^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})H_{t}^{x,\mu}+D^{2}_{ss}b^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})[J_{t}^{x,\mu},J_{t}^{x,\mu}]\Bigr]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\Bigl[\nabla_{s}\sigma_{\ell}^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})H_{t}^{x,\mu}+D^{2}_{ss}\sigma_{\ell}^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})[J_{t}^{x,\mu},J_{t}^{x,\mu}]\Bigr]\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{\ell},\qquad H_{0}^{x,\mu}=0.

By Assumption
3.1

(59)

\displaystyle\|D^{2}_{ss}b^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})[J_{t}^{x,\mu},J_{t}^{x,\mu}]\|+\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\|D^{2}_{ss}\sigma_{\ell}^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})[J_{t}^{x,\mu},J_{t}^{x,\mu}]\|\leq(d+1)K_{\pi}\|J_{t}^{x,\mu}\|^{2}.

Applying Itô’s formula to
\|H_{t}^{x,\mu}\|^{p}
in
(

), integrating over
[0,t]
, taking
expectations, and using (

),
(

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|H_{t}^{x,\mu}\|^{p}\right]\leq a_{p,d}K_{\pi}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|H_{r}^{x,\mu}\|^{p}\right]\mathrm{d}r+C_{p,d}K_{\pi}^{p}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|J_{r}^{x,\mu}\|^{2p}\right]\mathrm{d}r.

Using (

) with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|H_{t}^{x,\mu}\|^{p}\right]\leq C_{p,d}(1+K_{\pi}^{p})e^{C_{p,d}K_{\pi}^{2}t}.

Step 3 (Bounds for the Lions derivative).

Let
(\widetilde{\Omega},\widetilde{\mathcal{F}},\widetilde{\mathbb{P}})

be an independent copy of the original probability space, and denote
by
\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\xi,\mu}
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}U_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\Bigl[\nabla_{s}b^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})U_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi)+F_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi)\Bigr]\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\Bigl[\nabla_{s}\sigma_{\ell}^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})U_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi)+G_{t,\ell}^{x,\mu}(\xi)\Bigr]\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{\ell}

and

Taking expectations and using (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|F_{t}^{x,\mu}(\xi)\|^{p}\right]+\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|G_{t,\ell}^{x,\mu}(\xi)\|^{p}\right]\leq C_{p,d}K_{\pi}^{p}e^{C_{p,d}K_{\pi}^{2}t}.

Step 4 (Bounds for

Let

\displaystyle:=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}b^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\xi,\mu})[\widetilde{J}_{t}^{\xi,\mu},\widetilde{J}_{t}^{\xi,\mu}]+\partial_{\mu}b^{\pi}(X_{t}^{x,\mu},\mu_{t})(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{\xi,\mu})\widetilde{H}_{t}^{\xi,\mu}\right],

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{t,\ell}^{x,\mu}(\xi)

Combining (

and

(74)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\partial_{\mu}r_{\lambda}^{\pi}(X_{t}^{s,\mu},\mu_{t})(X_{t}^{\xi,\mu})\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\partial_{\mu}r_{\lambda}^{\pi}(X_{t}^{s,\mu},\mu_{t})(X_{t}^{\xi,\mu})\,D^{2}_{xx}X_{t}^{\xi,\mu}\right|\right]\leq Ce^{\beta_{0}(2)t}\bigl(1+|s|+|\xi|+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr).

Integrating (

)-(
74
)
over
[0,T]
, multiplying by
e^{-\beta t}
, and using

\beta>\bar{\beta}_{\pi}
, we conclude that there exists a constant

C>0
, independent of

, such that for all

(s,\mu,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

(75)

\displaystyle|U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu)|

\displaystyle\leq C\bigl(1+|s|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr),

(76)

\displaystyle|\nabla_{s}U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu)|+|\partial_{\mu}U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu)(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C\bigl(1+|s|+|\xi|+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr),

(77)

\displaystyle\|D^{2}_{ss}U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu)\|+\|D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu)(\xi)\|

\displaystyle\leq C\bigl(1+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr).

Step 4 (Passage to the limit in the derivatives).

Let

K_{R}:=\bigl\{(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):|s|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu)\leq R\bigr\},\qquad B_{R}:=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|\xi|\leq R\}.

By (
72
), the family
(U_{T}^{\pi})_{T>0}
is
uniformly Cauchy on
K_{R}
. Using the representation formulas from
[
37
, Appendix A.1, Step 2]
together with the estimations
(
73
)-(
74
), we
obtain, for every
D_{\alpha}\in\{1,\nabla_{s},\partial_{\mu},D_{ss}^{2},D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}\}
, for all
T^{\prime}>T\geq 0
, all
(s,\mu)\in K_{R}
, and all
\xi\in B_{R}
,

\displaystyle|D_{\alpha}U_{T^{\prime}}^{\pi}(s,\mu)-D_{\alpha}U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{R}\int_{T}^{T^{\prime}}e^{-(\beta-\bar{\beta}_{\pi})t}\,\mathrm{d}t,

Since
\beta>\bar{\beta}_{\pi}
, the right-hand sides tend to zero as

T\to\infty
. Hence, for each
D_{\alpha}
, the families
\{D_{\alpha}U_{T}^{\pi}\}_{T\geq 0}
are locally uniformly Cauchy and thus converge locally uniformly to
continuous limits.

By (
71
), the limit of
U_{T}^{\pi}
is precisely

V^{\pi}
. The local uniform convergence of
U_{T}^{\pi}
,

\nabla_{s}U_{T}^{\pi}
, and
D^{2}_{ss}U_{T}^{\pi}
implies that
V^{\pi}
is twice
continuously differentiable in

, with

\nabla_{s}V^{\pi}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\nabla_{s}U_{T}^{\pi},\qquad D^{2}_{ss}V^{\pi}=\lim_{T\to\infty}D^{2}_{ss}U_{T}^{\pi}.

For the measure derivative, let
\vartheta,\vartheta^{\prime}
be square-integrable
random variables with laws
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
, and set

\vartheta_{\lambda}:=\vartheta+\lambda(\vartheta^{\prime}-\vartheta)
,

\mu_{\lambda}:=\Law(\vartheta_{\lambda})

is Lions
differentiable, the mean-value formula on

\displaystyle U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu^{\prime})-U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu)=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\partial_{\mu}U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu_{\lambda})(\vartheta_{\lambda})\cdot(\vartheta^{\prime}-\vartheta)\right]\mathrm{d}\lambda.

Passing to the limit

in (

using the local uniform convergence of

and

\partial_{\mu}U_{T}^{\pi}
together with dominated convergence, we obtain
that

is Lions differentiable and

\partial_{\mu}V^{\pi}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\partial_{\mu}U_{T}^{\pi}.

Applying the same argument to

\xi

D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu)(\xi)

D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}V^{\pi}
exists and is jointly continuous. Therefore,
V^{\pi}\in\mathcal{C}^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

Letting
T\to\infty
in (
75
),
(
76
), and (
77

V^{\pi}\in\mathcal{C}^{2,2}_{\mathrm{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

Step 5 (The stationary HJB equation).

Evaluating (

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi}-\beta\bigr)U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu)+r_{\lambda}^{\pi}(s,\mu)=-\partial_{t}V_{T}^{\pi}(0,s,\mu).

Since the problem is time-homogeneous,
V_{T}^{\pi}(t,s,\mu)=U_{T-t}^{\pi}(s,\mu),

and therefore

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}V_{T}^{\pi}(0,s,\mu)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}T}U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-\beta T}r_{\lambda}^{\pi}(X_{T}^{s,\mu},\mu_{T})\right].

Thus, by (
70

(80)

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{t}V_{T}^{\pi}(0,s,\mu)\right|\leq\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-\beta T}r_{\lambda}^{\pi}(X_{T}^{s,\mu},\mu_{T})\right]\right|\leq Ce^{-(\beta-\beta_{0}(2))T}\bigl(1+|s|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr)\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}0.

It remains to pass to the limit in

\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi}-\beta\bigr)U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu)
.
The terms involving
\nabla_{s}U_{T}^{\pi}
and
D^{2}_{ss}U_{T}^{\pi}
converge
pointwise by Step 4. For the Lions terms, using
(
9
), (
10
),
(
76
), and (
77
), we obtain

\displaystyle|b^{\pi}(\xi,\mu)\cdot\partial_{\mu}U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu)(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{s,\mu}(1+|\xi|^{2}),

\displaystyle|\Sigma^{\pi}(\xi,\mu):D_{\xi}\partial_{\mu}U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu)(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{s,\mu}(1+|\xi|^{2}),

with constants independent of
T
. Since

\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the dominating function is

\mu
-integrable, and dominated convergence yields

(81)

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi}-\beta\bigr)U_{T}^{\pi}(s,\mu)\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi}-\beta\bigr)V^{\pi}(s,\mu).

Combining (
79

80
),
and (
81

obtain

(\mathcal{L}_{b,\Sigma}^{\pi}-\beta)V^{\pi}(s,\mu)+r_{\lambda}^{\pi}(s,\mu)=0.

Thus
V^{\pi}
is a classical solution of (
11

Step 6 (Uniqueness).

Let
U\in\mathcal{C}^{2,2}_{\mathrm{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
be another classical solution of (

(s,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and define
the stopping times
\tau_{n}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:|s_{t}|+|\tilde{s}_{t}|\geq n\},
with

Applying the Itô-Lions formula to

e^{-\beta(t\wedge\tau_{n})}U(s_{t\wedge\tau_{n}},\mu_{t\wedge\tau_{n}})
on

[0,T]
, taking expectations, and using (
11
), we obtain

(82)

\displaystyle U(s,\mu)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{s,\mu,\pi}\!\left[e^{-\beta(T\wedge\tau_{n})}U(s_{T\wedge\tau_{n}},\mu_{T\wedge\tau_{n}})\right]+\mathbb{E}^{s,\mu,\pi}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T\wedge\tau_{n}}e^{-\beta t}r_{\lambda}^{\pi}(s_{t},\mu_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\right].

Since

which tends to zero as

in (
82

then

Hence

unique in the class

\mathcal{C}^{2,2}_{\mathrm{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

Lemma A.6

(Stability with respect to policy perturbations)
.

Suppose
Section

3.1
holds. Let
\pi
be a fixed feedback policy and

\varphi
a signed kernel satisfying (
14
)
with
M_{\psi}:=\|\psi\|_{\infty}<\infty
. For
|\varepsilon|

small enough that
\pi^{\varepsilon}
is admissible, let

(s_{t}^{\varepsilon},\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon})
and
(s_{t}^{\pi},\mu_{t}^{\pi})
be the
solutions of (
4
)-(
5
) under
\pi^{\varepsilon}

and
\pi
respectively, with the same initial condition
s_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
,

\mu_{0}^{\varepsilon}=\mu_{0}^{\pi}=\mu
, and the same driving Brownian motions.
Then there exist constants
C>0
and
\beta_{\mathrm{stab}}>0
,
depending only on
L_{\pi}
,
M_{\psi}
, and
\beta_{0}(2)
, such that
for every
t\geq 0
,

(83)

\mathbb{E}\bigl[|s_{t}^{\varepsilon}-s_{t}^{\pi}|^{2}\bigr]+\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon},\mu_{t}^{\pi})\leq C\,\varepsilon^{2}\,e^{\beta_{\mathrm{stab}}\,t}\bigl(1+|s|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr).

In particular,
\mathbb{E}[|s_{t}^{\varepsilon}-s_{t}^{\pi}|^{2}]+\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon},\mu_{t}^{\pi})\to 0

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, for each fixed
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Set
\delta s_{t}:=s_{t}^{\varepsilon}-s_{t}^{\pi}
and

\delta\tilde{s}_{t}:=\tilde{s}_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\tilde{s}_{t}^{\pi}
.
Since both pairs of processes are driven by the same Brownian motions

B
and
\tilde{B}
, the differences satisfy

\mathrm{d}(\delta s_{t})=\bigl(b^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(s_{t}^{\varepsilon},\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon})-b^{\pi}(s_{t}^{\pi},\mu_{t}^{\pi})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\bigl(\sigma^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(s_{t}^{\varepsilon},\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon})-\sigma^{\pi}(s_{t}^{\pi},\mu_{t}^{\pi})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},

and analogously for
\delta\tilde{s}_{t}
.

Step 1: Decomposition of the coefficient differences.
By linearity of the averaged coefficients in the control measure:

(84)

b^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(s^{\varepsilon},\mu^{\varepsilon})-b^{\pi}(s^{\pi},\mu^{\pi})=\varepsilon\,b^{\varphi}(s^{\varepsilon},\mu^{\varepsilon})+\bigl[b^{\pi}(s^{\varepsilon},\mu^{\varepsilon})-b^{\pi}(s^{\pi},\mu^{\pi})\bigr],

where
b^{\varphi}(s,\mu):=\int_{\mathcal{A}}b(s,\mu,a)\,\varphi(\mathrm{d}a|s,\mu)
.
By the linear growth of
b
in
Section

3.1
and the zero-mass
condition (
14
),

|b^{\varphi}(s,\mu)|\leq C_{b}\bigl(1+|s|+\sqrt{m_{2}(\mu)}\bigr),

and by the Lipschitz condition of
Section

3.1
, the second term
of (
84
) is bounded by

L_{\pi}(|\delta s|+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu^{\varepsilon},\mu^{\pi}))
.

For the diffusion coefficient, write

(85)

\sigma^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(s^{\varepsilon},\mu^{\varepsilon})-\sigma^{\pi}(s^{\pi},\mu^{\pi})=\bigl[\sigma^{\pi}(s^{\varepsilon},\mu^{\varepsilon})-\sigma^{\pi}(s^{\pi},\mu^{\pi})\bigr]+\bigl[\sigma^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(s^{\varepsilon},\mu^{\varepsilon})-\sigma^{\pi}(s^{\varepsilon},\mu^{\varepsilon})\bigr].

The first bracket is bounded by

L_{\pi}(|\delta s|+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu^{\varepsilon},\mu^{\pi}))

by
Section

3.1
. For the second bracket, since

\pi^{\varepsilon}=(1+\varepsilon\psi)\pi
with
|\varepsilon|M_{\psi}<1
,
we have for every
(s,\mu)
and every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

z^{\top}\Sigma^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(s,\mu)z=\int_{\mathcal{A}}z^{\top}\Sigma(s,\mu,a)z\,\bigl(1+\varepsilon\psi(s,\mu,a)\bigr)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}a\mid s,\mu),

from which

(86)

(1-M_{\psi}|\varepsilon|)\,\Sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu)\leq\Sigma^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(s,\mu)\leq(1+M_{\psi}|\varepsilon|)\,\Sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu).

Since the map
A\mapsto A^{1/2}
is operator-monotone on the cone of
symmetric positive semidefinite matrices (a consequence of the
Löwner-Heinz inequality, which holds for any semidefinite matrix), taking square roots
in (
86
) gives

\sqrt{1-M_{\psi}|\varepsilon|}\,\sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu)\leq\sigma^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(s,\mu)\leq\sqrt{1+M_{\psi}|\varepsilon|}\,\sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu).

Using the elementary inequalities
1-\sqrt{1-x}\leq x
and

\sqrt{1+x}-1\leq x
for
x\in[0,1)
, we conclude that

\|\sigma^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(s,\mu)-\sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu)\|\leq M_{\psi}|\varepsilon|\,\|\sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu)\|.

By the linear growth of
\sigma^{\pi}
from
Section

3.1
:

(87)

\|\sigma^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(s,\mu)-\sigma^{\pi}(s,\mu)\|^{2}\leq C\,\varepsilon^{2}\bigl(1+|s|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr).

Note that (
87
) holds without any ellipticity
assumption on
\Sigma^{\pi}
; the sandwich argument requires only that

\Sigma^{\pi}
be positive semidefinite, which holds automatically.
Combining both brackets in (
85
), we have

(88)

\bigl\|\sigma^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(s^{\varepsilon},\mu^{\varepsilon})-\sigma^{\pi}(s^{\pi},\mu^{\pi})\bigr\|^{2}\leq C\Bigl(|\delta s|^{2}+\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu^{\varepsilon},\mu^{\pi})+\varepsilon^{2}\bigl(1+|s^{\varepsilon}|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu^{\varepsilon})\bigr)\Bigr).

Step 2: Itô formula, Grönwall, and exponential bound.
Applying Itô’s formula to
|\delta s_{t}|^{2}
, taking expectations
(the stochastic integral is a true martingale by the moment estimates
of Lemma
A.1
), and using Young’s inequality
together with (
84
)-(

), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\delta s_{t}|^{2}\bigr]\leq C_{1}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\delta s_{t}|^{2}\bigr]+C_{1}\,\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon},\mu_{t}^{\pi})+C_{1}\,\varepsilon^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[1+|s_{t}^{\pi}|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu_{t}^{\pi})\bigr],

where
C_{1}>0
depends only on
L_{\pi}
and
M_{\psi}
. An identical estimate
holds for
\delta\tilde{s}_{t}
. Since

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon},\mu_{t}^{\pi})\leq\mathbb{E}[|\delta\tilde{s}_{t}|^{2}]
,
setting
\Phi_{t}:=\mathbb{E}[|\delta s_{t}|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[|\delta\tilde{s}_{t}|^{2}]

and summing the two estimates gives

(89)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Phi_{t}\leq C_{2}\,\Phi_{t}+C_{2}\,\varepsilon^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[1+|s_{t}^{\pi}|^{2}+|\tilde{s}_{t}^{\pi}|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu_{t}^{\pi})\bigr],

where
C_{2}
depends only on
C_{1}
. By Lemma
A.1

with
p=2
, the factor on the right-hand side satisfies

\mathbb{E}\bigl[1+|s_{t}^{\pi}|^{2}+|\tilde{s}_{t}^{\pi}|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu_{t}^{\pi})\bigr]\leq Ce^{\beta_{0}(2)t}\bigl(1+|s|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr).

Since
\Phi_{0}=0
, Grönwall’s inequality applied
to (
89
) gives

\Phi_{t}\leq C_{2}\varepsilon^{2}(1+|s|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu))\int_{0}^{t}e^{C_{2}(t-r)}\,C\,e^{\beta_{0}(2)r}\,\mathrm{d}r\leq C^{\prime}\varepsilon^{2}e^{\max(C_{2},\,\beta_{0}(2))\,t}\bigl(1+|s|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr),

where the last step uses
e^{C_{2}(t-r)}e^{\beta_{0}(2)r}\leq e^{\max(C_{2},\beta_{0}(2))t}
for

.
Setting
\beta_{\mathrm{stab}}:=\max(C_{2},\,\beta_{0}(2))
and recalling

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon},\mu_{t}^{\pi})\leq\mathbb{E}[|\delta\tilde{s}_{t}|^{2}]\leq\Phi_{t}
gives (
83
).
∎

.

at

For the entropy part, the identity

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}\left[-\lambda\int_{\mathcal{A}}p_{\pi^{\varepsilon}}\log p_{\pi^{\varepsilon}}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}a)\right]=-\lambda\int_{\mathcal{A}}\bigl(\log p_{\pi}(a|x,\nu)+1\bigr)\,\varphi(\mathrm{d}a|x,\nu).

Since

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}r_{\lambda}^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(x,\nu)=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\bigl(r(x,\nu,a)-\lambda\log p_{\pi}(a|x,\nu)\bigr)\,\varphi(\mathrm{d}a|x,\nu)=:r_{\lambda}^{\varphi}(x,\nu).

3.1

with
C_{r}>0
independent of
\varepsilon
. Taking expectations against the
\pi^{\varepsilon}

with

(93)

e^{-\beta t}\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}^{s,\mu,\pi^{\varepsilon}}[r_{\lambda}^{\pi^{\varepsilon}}(s_{t}^{\varepsilon},\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon})]-\mathbb{E}^{s,\mu,\pi^{\varepsilon}}[r_{\lambda}^{\pi}(s_{t}^{\varepsilon},\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon})]}{\varepsilon}\right|\leq M_{\psi}C_{r}\,e^{-(\beta-\beta_{0}(2))t}\bigl(1+|s|^{2}+m_{2}(\mu)\bigr),

which is integrable since

\displaystyle A_{2}(\varepsilon;s)

For

, fix

Since
