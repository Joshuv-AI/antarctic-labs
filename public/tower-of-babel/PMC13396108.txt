Screening on the Paretic Side is Superior for Detecting Possible Sarcopenia in Individuals After Chronic Stroke: Preliminary Findings
Authors: Ana Luiza M de Oliveira, Ana Carolina J Galvão, Maria Clara F de Oliveira, Lavínia P dos Santos, Amanda A O Leopoldino, Janaine C Polese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe onset of stroke can lead to structural muscle changes, such as stroke-related sarcopenia. The screening of sarcopenia can be accomplished by the SARC-CalF questionnaire, which includes the measurement of calf circumference. However, there is a lack of evidence on whether calf circumference should be assessed in the paretic or non-paretic limb. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the difference between sarcopenia screening on the paretic and non-paretic sides in individuals after chronic stroke.MethodsCross-sectional study that included individuals after chronic stroke. Sarcopenia was assessed by the SARC-CalF instrument. The calf circumference measurement was performed in both limbs. Introduction

The onset of stroke can cause structural changes in the muscle phenotype, as evidenced by the replacement of slow-contracting, fatigue-resistant fibers (type I) with predominantly fast-contracting, more fatigable fibers (type II) [1]. This change is particularly evident in the hemiparetic limb and, when combined with metabolic changes and disuse after stroke, may be associated with significant functional consequences in this population, such as walking deficits [1]. Hemiparesis refers to partial weakness affecting one side of the body, and is a consequence of brain damage, where specific areas of the brain associated with movement and coordination are affected due to insufficient oxygen supply [2]. Another commonly observed disorder in this population is stroke-related sarcopenia, a muscle syndrome that affects the individual in a global and progressive way and is characterized by a reduction in muscle strength and quantity, as well as a reduction in physical performance, which is the indicator of the severity of the condition [3]. Sarcopenia is highly relevant and well-documented in recent literature, showing changes such as rapid muscle mass decline, structural alterations in muscle fibers, and a pronounced mechanism of muscle catabolism [4]. This alteration increases the risk of falls and fractures, impairs the ability to perform activities of daily living, and contributes to a reduction in quality of life [5–9].

According to the revised European consensus on definition and diagnosis developed by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP2), sarcopenia is called "primary" (or age-related) when there is no cause other than natural aging [3]. The stroke-related sarcopenia is characterized as secondary, once its origins relate to a primary disease, and different stages of this condition can also be defined as pre-sarcopenia, sarcopenia, and severe sarcopenia [3]. This consensus defines some criteria for sarcopenia screening and diagnosis. Screening is fundamental so that patients can be properly assessed and receive early treatment, aiming for the best probable results [10]. Thus, screening for sarcopenia is largely carried out using the SARC-F questionnaire, which includes a symptom score based on five self-reported questions about strength, walking, getting up from a chair, climbing stairs, and falls [7]. However, the SARC-F questions only concern muscle function (strength and performance), without assessing issues such as decreased skeletal muscle volume and muscle weakness, which may eventually alter the patient's functional outcome [11, 12]. For this reason, new questionnaires have been developed, such as the SARC-CalF, which includes the measurement of calf circumference to the SARC-F questionnaire [13].

Therefore, according to the latest consensus, sarcopenia screening is relevant to detecting probable occurrence of sarcopenia and thus allows the early diagnosis and treatment to avoid these consequences [3]. Given that recent literature presents different measurement criteria to make a more accurate assessment, there is a lack of agreement on whether the screening should be assessed in the paretic or non-paretic limb in individuals after stroke [13, 14]. Thus, considering the relevance of sarcopenia screening, this study aims to fill this gap by investigating the difference between sarcopenia screening on the paretic and non-paretic sides in individuals after chronic stroke. We hypothesized that screening on the paretic side will be superior for detecting possible sarcopenia in this population.

Methods

Study Design

This is a cross-sectional study approved by the Ethics and Research Committee of the instution Faculdade Ciências Médicas de Minas Gerais (FCMMG) (CAAE:65413922.5.0000.5134) in December 15, 2022. The study was conducted at FCMMG between December 2022 and December 2023. The study was performed according to Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments. All study participants provided written consent prior to data collection for participation and publication. This study was elaborated following the STROBE statement, and the completed checklist is provided as Supplementary Material (S1 Appendix) [15]. Researchers were trained prior to the start of data collection to reduce assessment bias.

Sample

The sample was selected by convenience and individuals were recruited from the general community in Belo Horizonte, Brazil. The study included individuals over 6 months after the stroke, aged 20 years or older, with unilateral involvement, who were able to walk independently and who agreed to participate in the study after signing the Informed Consent Form. Individuals with incomprehensible aphasia and who scored less than the following scores on the Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE), being 13 points the cutoff point for illiterates, 18 points for low and medium education, and 26 points for high education [16], were excluded.

Instruments

Demographic and clinical data were assessed by an evaluation form developed by the researchers, and the cognitive function was assessed using the MMSE. After the initial evaluation, individuals were assessed regarding some functional outcomes, such as their level of physical activity, lower limb muscle strength, and walking speed, for characterization purposes. The Maximum Activity Score (MAS) and the Adjusted Activity Score (AAS) of the Human Activity Profile questionnaire [17] were used to assess the individual’s level of physical activity. Lower limb muscle strength (knee extensors and plantar flexors) was assessed by the Modified Sphygmomanometer Test [18]. Finally, walking speed was evaluated using the 10-meter walking test with habitual speed [19]. Afterwards, sarcopenia was screened in the studied population.

Sarcopenia Screening

Participants were screened for sarcopenia through the SARC-CalF questionnaire, which includes questions covering five domains of strength, assistance during walking, getting up from a chair, climbing stairs, and history of falls, associated with an additional item that measures the calf circumference. Each item is scored from 0 to 2, except for the calf circumference item, which is scored 0 if the result is greater than 34 cm for men and 33 cm for women, and 10 points if the measure is equal to or less than these values. The calf circumference measurement was made using a tape measure around the widest part of the individuals’ calves, bilaterally, while they were still in a standing position. The initially assessed side was randomly selected. During measurement, the patient was asked to transfer the body weight onto the contralateral side to the one being measured. A total SARC-CalF score of ≥ 11 points was indicative of a positive screening for sarcopenia, and the score was given for each hemibody separately, considering the calf circumference measure. This tool presents high sensitivity and specificity for screening sarcopenia in community-dwelling elderly individuals and patients with stroke [7, 13, 20, 21].

Sample Calculation

A priori sample size calculation was conducted for the Pearson chi-square goodness-of-fit test. Based on a two-sided significance level of 5% (α = 0.05), a statistical power of 80% (1−β = 0.80), and a medium effect size according to Cohen's criteria (w = 0.30), a minimum sample of 62 participants was required. This sample size provides adequate power to detect meaningful differences between observed and expected frequency distributions [22].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics and tests for normality were performed for all investigated variables. The Pearson chi-square analysis was used to determine whether the observed frequencies differed from the expected frequencies. All the analyses were carried out using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 15.0, with a significance level set at < 0.05.

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that sarcopenia screening on the paretic side provided a greater number of individuals with possible sarcopenia compared to screening on the non-paretic side when applying the SARC-Cal-F questionnaire to individuals after chronic stroke. The variable that distinguishes sarcopenia screening on the paretic and non-paretic side is the calf circumference measurement, a simple, quick, and low-cost assessment. Although there is a clear understanding of how to correctly measure calf circumference, the available literature provides less guidance on which hemibody is ideal for taking this measurement [23]. Thus, several studies do not agree on which side to assess, demonstrating a lack of standardization that can affect the screening results. Considering this background, a few studies specifically measured the calf circumference in the non-dominant calf in community elderly individuals [24]. Another study assessed the calf circumference only on the right side, overlooking the presence of hemiparesis that developed in individuals after a stroke [13].

It is important to consider the specificities found in stroke-related sarcopenia that differ from primary sarcopenia, such as a rapid decline in muscle mass, structural changes in muscle fibers, and bilateral differences in physical and functional performance generated by the brain injury, which can also trigger a mechanism of significant muscle catabolism [4]. In addition, individuals after stroke are likely to present loss of the motoneurons, muscle fiber shift, degeneration of neuromuscular junctions, adjacent reinnervation, and atrophy, which reflects in muscle wasting and loss of physical performance, indicating the onset of stroke-related sarcopenia [4]. Such alterations are largely observed on the paretic side of the individual but are reflected in the subject's overall function, resulting in balance impairments, decreased ability to walk or other gait alterations, motor control deficits, and psychological consequences such as fear of falling [2]. Thus, considering that the paretic side already has greater structural and biochemical changes resulting from brain injury, measuring calf circumference on this side may overestimate the presence of sarcopenia.

Screening the paretic side identified eight additional individuals as probable cases of sarcopenia. Thus, the screening on the non-paretic side may underestimate the presence of sarcopenia, since it could exclude individuals with probable sarcopenia. Screening tools should accurately identify individuals at probable risk of sarcopenia, so that the diagnosis could be subsequently confirmed and its severity assessed [3]. Thus, overestimating the presence of sarcopenia is a better way of achieving the purpose of the screening tool compared to underestimation. This approach can be more beneficial to individuals after stroke, since early treatment is associated with better post-stroke outcomes, such as fitness and gait improvement, as well as strength in the paretic leg hamstrings and quadriceps in chronic hemiparetic stroke patients [25]. Sarcopenia has been associated with a higher risk of many adverse health outcomes, such as cognitive impairment, falls, functional decline, hospitalization, depression, and even an increased risk of mortality. Therefore, screening individuals after stroke for sarcopenia, mainly when considering the hemiparetic side, can cover a greater number of probable sarcopenic individuals and could be a tool to minimize or prevent these negative outcomes [26]. This study also demonstrated that the average calf circumference difference between the paretic and non-paretic sides was 1 cm, considered statistically significant. The results showed that the mean calf circumference for the paretic limb was 34.5 ± 5.9 cm, while the non-paretic limb had a mean calf circumference of 35.5 ± 5.9 cm. In the SARC-Cal-F assessment, a cutoff point of 34 cm or less for men and 33 cm or less for women was used for sarcopenia screening [13]. A study showed that these cut-off values were effective in detecting sarcopenia in individuals after stroke in both sexes [27]. In addition, another study proposed a cut-off point of 30.5 cm for calf circumference measurement, showing an area under the curve of 0.85, with a sensitivity of 81.8%, specificity of 90.1%, Kappa value of 0.72, and Youden index of 0.72, demonstrating high accuracy in the diagnosis of sarcopenia. However, this prior study only included individuals in the acute or subacute stage after stroke [28]. The findings indicate that the mean calf circumference of the paretic limb is closer to the sarcopenia screening threshold, which corroborates a more efficient screening. This suggests that a relatively minor difference in calf circumference measurements between the non-paretic and paretic sides can have significant implications for clinical practice.

This study has important limitations. First, it should be noted that the sample was selected by convenience, which was limited to a specific population and may not allow the generalization of the results to other settings and populations. The single-center design is also a limitation, and multicenter studies with larger samples are needed to expand these findings. In addition, only individuals after chronic stroke were included in the study, excluding individuals in the earlier stages of the disease, such as individuals after acute and subacute stroke, which limits the scope of the results. Individuals who were unable to walk and those with cognitive deficits were also excluded, which may affect the generalizability to more severely affected stroke survivors. Furthermore, sarcopenia screening may differ across stroke subtypes (e.g., ischemic, hemorrhagic) and in more vulnerable subgroups, such as the very elderly (aged 85 or older) and future investigations should address this matter. Finally, this study assessed screening only and did not include diagnostic confirmation using gold-standard criteria (e.g., muscle mass assessments by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry or bioimpedance, and handgrip strength evaluation), which is an important direction for future research. These limitations require caution on the interpretation of the results and highlight the need for future studies to address these gaps.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that sarcopenia screening using the SARC-CalF on the paretic side in individuals after stroke identified more individuals with probable stroke-related sarcopenia than by assessing the non-paretic side. The screening of the non-paretic side may underestimate the condition and lead to exclusion of potential cases in the studied population. Further research should be conducted to expand this knowledge in larger and more diverse samples and to investigate their implications for the sarcopenia diagnosis process.
