Quotient-Categorical Representations for Bellman-Compatible Average-Reward Distributional Reinforcement Learning
Ege C. Kaya, Aliasghar Pourghani, Vijay Gupta, Abolfazl Hashemi

Abstract
Average-reward reinforcement learning requires estimating the gain and the bias, which is defined only up to an additive constant. This makes direct distributional analogues ill-posed on the real line. We introduce a quotient-space formulation in which state-indexed bias laws are identified up to a common translation, together with a categorical parameterization that respects this symmetry. On this quotient-categorical space, we define a projected average-reward distributional operator and show that it is well-defined, non-expansive in a coordinate Cramér metric, and admits fixed points. We then study sampled recursions whose mean-field maps are asynchronous relaxations of this operator. In an idealized centered-reward setting, a one-state temporal-difference update enjoys almost sure convergence together with finite-iteration residual bounds under both i.i.d. and Markovian sampling. When the gain is unknown, we augment the recursion with an online gain estimator, and prove non-expansiveness and Markovian convergence of the resulting coupled scheme. Finally, we show that synchronous exact updates are gain-independent at the quotient-law level, isolating a structural contrast between ideal quotient distributions and practical fixed-grid categorical representations.

Introduction

Distributional reinforcement learning (DRL) is well developed in the discounted setting, where the standard contraction arguments and Bellman-compatible return distributions support both analysis and algorithms
[
3
,

gain
, which is the long-run average reward, and the
bias
, which solves the average-reward analogue of the Bellman equation, namely the Poisson equation. The bias is the object that distinguishes states and policies with the same gain, captures transient differential performance pre-stationarity, and underlies average-reward evaluation and policy improvement
[
32
,
41
,
26
]
. However, it is defined only up to an additive constant. This creates an obstruction for distributional modeling: a distributional bias cannot be represented as an ordinary state-indexed family of laws on
\mathbb{R}
with a fixed origin, because translating every state’s bias by the same constant should not change the object being learned.

We therefore model state-indexed bias laws modulo common translations. The resulting quotient viewpoint preserves the additive-constant symmetry of the Poisson equation, and our categorical construction turns this quotient object into a finite-dimensional representation. This makes it possible to define projected Bellman operators for the distributional bias, compare categorical iterates in a coordinate Cramér metric, and write temporal-difference (TD) algorithms whose fixed points are Bellman-compatible projected quotient-categorical bias laws. Our objective is complementary to the average-reward distributional criterion studied, e.g., by
35
. That line of work refines the average-reward criterion itself through limiting per-step reward distributions. We instead refine the bias: the Bellman-compatible object that records state-dependent differential performance. Thus the goal is not merely to describe long-run reward statistics, but to construct a distributional analogue of the scalar bias that can be used in average-reward dynamic-programming and TD-learning arguments.

The remaining challenge is algorithmic. The projected maps we obtain are non-expansive rather than contractive, so discounted DRL contraction arguments do not apply. We show that the sampled categorical updates can be written as TD / stochastic approximation (SA) recursions in the classical sense
[

,

,

,

,

]

[
11
,
7
]
.

Contributions.
We put average-reward distributional bias estimation on firm foundations by defining the correct quotient-categorical target and proving convergence guarantees for TD-style categorical recursions. Our main contributions are as follows.

1.

We establish that the quotient space of state-indexed law families by common translation is the natural space for a distributional analogue of the scalar bias.

2.

We define an exact quotient-space operator and a quotient-categorical projection algorithm, proving in particular that the projected operator is well-defined, non-expansive, and admits a fixed point.

3.

We analyze a centered-reward TD recursion for the quotient-categorical bias distribution, whose mean-field maps are non-expansive asynchronous relaxations of the projected operator, with a.s. convergence and finite-iteration residual bounds.

4.

We introduce a practical online gain-estimated one-state TD recursion, which is non-expansive and admits a mean-field map with correct fixed points.

Preliminaries

We work with a finite average-reward MDP
[
32
]

(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},R,P)
, where
\mathcal{S}=\{1,\dots,m\}
is the finite set of states and
\mathcal{A}
is the finite set of actions,
R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
is the deterministic reward function and
P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the transition kernel. A policy
\pi
is fixed throughout, and we focus on its evaluation. With
\pi
fixed, the induced process is a Markov reward process with transition matrix
P^{\pi}
, where
P^{\pi}_{ij}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}P(j\mid i,a)\,\pi(a\mid i)

[
41
]
. Throughout the paper, we assume that the induced Markov chain
\{S_{t}\}
is irreducible and aperiodic, and write
\mu
for its unique stationary distribution. We denote by
R_{ij}
the finite-valued random one-step reward conditioned on
(S_{t}=i,S_{t+1}=j)
. We define the expected one-step reward vector as

r^{\pi}_{i}:=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}P^{\pi}_{ij}\mathbb{E}[R_{ij}],\quad\text{for all }i\in\mathcal{S}.

(1)

The (scalar)
gain
or
average reward
is then

\bar{r}^{\pi}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\mu_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}P^{\pi}_{ij}\mathbb{E}[R_{ij}]=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\mu_{i}r^{\pi}_{i}.

(2)

The
bias
or
relative value function

[
32
]

v^{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is any solution of the Poisson equation
[
28
]

v^{\pi}=r^{\pi}-\bar{r}^{\pi}\mathbf{1}+P^{\pi}v^{\pi}.

(3)

The Poisson equation is, in some sense, the analogue of the Bellman equation
[
5
]
in the average-reward setting, where the discount factor has been set to
1
and the rewards have been centered.

The Poisson equation can equivalently be written as
(I-P^{\pi})v^{\pi}=r^{\pi}-\bar{r}^{\pi}\mathbf{1}
. Since the null space of
(I-P^{\pi})
contains any constant vector
c\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the solutions of (
3
) are unique up to addition of a common constant. This scalar translation-invariance property of solutions of (
3
) provides the intuition for the quotient-categorical formulation constructed in the rest of the work.

4
Quotient-categorical bias estimation

In this work, we seek to estimate the distribution of the bias.
As motivated by the discussion in Section
3
, our first objective is to recover an analogue of the translation-invariance property in the distributional setting. Let
\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}:=\{\eta=(\eta_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{S}}:\eta_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\}
denote the set of state-indexed collections of probability laws on
\mathbb{R}
with finite second moment. For
c\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\tau_{c}(x):=x+c
, and define
common-translation
on
\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}
by
((\tau_{c})_{\#}\eta)_{i}:=(\tau_{c})_{\#}\eta_{i}
, where
\#
denotes pushforward. We also define the equivalence relation
\approx
on any pair
\eta,\zeta\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}
by

\eta\approx\zeta\iff\exists c\in\mathbb{R}\text{ such that }\zeta=(\tau_{c})_{\#}\eta,

(4)

and denote the quotient space by
\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}/\approx
. Let us write
B_{ij}:=R_{ij}-\bar{r}^{\pi}
for the centered one-step reward, and
\nu_{ij}:=\mathrm{Law}(B_{ij})
. We now define the average-reward distributional operator.

Definition 1
.

The
average-reward distributional operator

\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}\to\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}
is defined componentwise:

(\mathcal{T}\eta)_{i}:=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}P_{ij}\,(\nu_{ij}\,\ast\,\eta_{j}),\quad\text{for all }i\in\mathcal{S}.

(5)

Proposition 2
.

For every
\eta\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathcal{T}\bigl((\tau_{c})_{\#}\eta\bigr)=(\tau_{c})_{\#}\mathcal{T}\eta.

(6)

Consequently,
\mathcal{T}
induces a well-defined operator on the quotient space
\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}/\approx
:

\overline{\mathcal{T}}:\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}/\approx\;\to\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}/\approx,\quad\overline{\mathcal{T}}\bigl([\eta]\bigr):=[\mathcal{T}\eta].

(7)

The quotient formulation is instructive in seeing how the gain is not part of the quotient target itself. If we center rewards by an arbitrary constant
g\in\mathbb{R}
instead of
\bar{r}^{\pi}
, the resulting state-indexed collection of laws differs only by a common translation. The following proposition formalizes this.

Proposition 3
.

Let
g\in\mathbb{R}
. Define the
g
-centered average-reward distributional operator

(\mathcal{T}_{g}\eta)_{i}:=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}P_{ij}(\nu_{ij}^{(g)}\,\ast\,\eta_{j}),\quad\text{where }\nu_{ij}^{(g)}:=\mathrm{Law}(R_{ij}-g).

(8)

Then, for any
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\eta\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}
,
[\mathcal{T}_{g}\eta]=[\mathcal{T}_{g^{\prime}}\eta]
in
\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}/\approx
.

Guided by these observations, we introduce a categorical representation class which respects the translation-invariance property of the bias law. Let
\Theta=\{\theta_{1}<\cdots<\theta_{d}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
be an ordered support with constant stride
\Delta:=\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}>0
for
k=1,\dots,d-1
.
1
1

1

It is straightforward to use state-dependent supports and non-uniform stride, we forgo these for simplicity of exposition.
Let
\Delta_{d}
denote the
d
-dimensional simplex. For
p\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
, define the representative family of laws

\eta_{i}^{p,c}:=\sum_{k=1}^{d}p_{i,k}\,\delta_{\theta_{k}+c},\quad\text{for all }i\in\mathcal{S}.

(9)

The state-indexed collection of coefficients
p
therefore uniquely characterizes a quotient class of categorical distributions
[\eta^{p}]:=[\eta^{p,c}]\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}/\approx
, independently of shift
c
. We call this a
quotient-categorical
representation: categorical because
each state law is represented on the finite grid
\Theta
, and quotient because
the common shift
c
is not part of the state, but only a choice of representative.

For each
c\in\mathbb{R}
, define the
c
-shifted support
\Theta_{c}:=\{\theta+c:\theta\in\Theta\}
, and let
\Pi^{\Theta_{c}}_{\mathrm{C}}
denote the standard linear-interpolation categorical projection
[
3
]
onto
\Theta_{c}
, applied over all states. We will show that this projection is shift-equivariant: first shifting a distribution by
c
and then projecting onto the
c
-shifted categorical support is equivalent to projecting it onto the unshifted support and then shifting by
c
.

Lemma 4

(Projection shift-equivariance)
.

For every
\eta\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,

\Pi^{\Theta_{c}}_{\mathrm{C}}\bigl((\tau_{c})_{\#}\eta\bigr)=(\tau_{c})_{\#}(\Pi^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C}}\eta).

(10)

This same fact is illustrated in Figure
1
. Since every representative
\eta^{p,c}
is supported on a
c
-shifted copy
\Theta_{c}
of the same support
\Theta
, its Cramér geometry is determined entirely by the cumulative masses over the support. For
p\in\Delta_{d}
, let
F_{p}(\theta_{k}):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}
for
k=1,\dots,d-1
. We define, for
p,q\in\Delta_{d}
, the coordinate Cramér metric by

\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C}}(p,q)^{2}:=\Delta\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\bigl(F_{p}(\theta_{k})-F_{q}(\theta_{k})\bigr)^{2}.

(11)

The statewise extension of this is the so-called supremum-Cramér metric, where for
p,q\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
,

\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}(p,q):=\max_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C}}(p_{i},q_{i}).

(12)

The next proposition shows that these coordinate metrics coincide exactly with the Cramér metrics on the corresponding representative families.

Proposition 5
.

For every
p,q\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,

\ell_{\mathrm{C}}(\eta^{p,c}_{i},\eta^{q,c}_{i})=\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C}}(p_{i},q_{i}),\quad\text{for all }i\in\mathcal{S}.

(13)

Consequently,

\ell_{\mathrm{C},\infty}(\eta^{p,c},\eta^{q,c})=\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}(p,q),\quad\text{for all }c\in\mathbb{R}.

(14)

Proposition
5
shows that the representative families
\eta^{p,c}
faithfully encode the relevant Cramér geometry in the coefficient vectors
p\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
, regardless of the shift
c
, as long as the same
c
is chosen for both representatives. We now use this representation to define the projected analogue of the
g
-centered operator
\mathcal{T}_{g}
on categorical quotient coordinates. Proposition
3
previously showed that, at the quotient level,
\mathcal{T}_{g}
does not depend on the choice of
g
: changing
g
only changes the representative of the output, but lands in the same equivalence class. After categorical projection, however, the representative matters, since the projection is performed relative to a fixed shifted support
\Theta_{c}
.

Definition 6
.

Let
g\in\mathbb{R}
and
p\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
and choose
c\in\mathbb{R}
arbitrarily. Applying
\mathcal{T}_{g}
to the representative family
\eta^{p,c}
and projecting each component back onto
\Theta_{c}
yields another categorical family on
\Theta_{c}
. We define
\mathcal{G}_{g}(p)=q\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
to be the unique element that satisfies

\Pi^{\Theta_{c}}_{\mathrm{C}}(\mathcal{T}_{g}\eta^{p,c})_{i}=\eta^{q,c}_{i},\quad\text{for all }i\in\mathcal{S}.

(15)

We note that this definition is independent of the choice of the representative shift
c
. Indeed, since by definition
\eta^{p,c^{\prime}}=(\tau_{c^{\prime}-c})_{\#}\eta^{p,c}
, the same argument as in Proposition
2
gives

\mathcal{T}_{g}\eta^{p,c^{\prime}}=(\tau_{c^{\prime}-c})_{\#}\mathcal{T}_{g}\eta^{p,c}.

(16)

Figure 1
:

The left panel illustrates Lemma
4
: translating both the law and support preserves the coefficient vector
p
.
The right panel illustrates that projecting a translated law back onto the same support generally gives different coefficients
q\neq p
.

The application of Lemma
4
shows that projection onto
\Theta_{c^{\prime}}
or onto
\Theta_{c}
produces categorical families with the same coefficient vector
q
. Therefore,
\mathcal{G}_{g}
is a well-defined operator on
\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
for any
g\in\mathbb{R}
. Lastly, we define
\mathcal{G}:=\mathcal{G}_{\bar{r}^{\pi}}
as the “correctly-centered” projected operator.

We now state the main theorem of this section, establishing the non-expansiveness of
\mathcal{G}_{g}
in the coordinate Cramér metric.

Theorem 7

(Non-expansiveness of
\mathcal{G}_{g}
)
.

For every
g\in\mathbb{R}
, and every
p,q\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
,

\ell_{\mathrm{C},\infty}^{\Theta}\bigl(\mathcal{G}_{g}(p),\mathcal{G}_{g}(q)\bigr)\leq\ell_{\mathrm{C},\infty}^{\Theta}(p,q).

(17)

In particular, the correctly-centered operator
\mathcal{G}
is non-expansive.

Proof sketch.

Fix a common representative shift
c
and compare the representative families
\eta^{p,c}
and
\eta^{q,c}
. For each state
i
, the
i
th component of
\mathcal{T}_{g}
is a
P_{ij}
-weighted average of convolved laws
\nu_{ij}^{(g)}\,\ast\,\eta_{j}
. Convolution with a fixed law is non-expansive in the Cramér metric, and the subsequent
P_{ij}
-averaging is also non-expansive. Thus
\mathcal{T}_{g}
is non-expansive in
\ell_{\mathrm{C},\infty}
. Composing with the standard non-expansive categorical projection and then using Proposition
5
yields the claim.
∎

Corollary 8

(Existence of fixed points)
.

Let
g\in\mathbb{R}
.
\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{G}_{g})
is nonempty.

5
A centered stochastic approximation recursion

In the previous section, we defined the
g
-centered projected operator
\mathcal{G}_{g}
and established its non-expansiveness in the coordinate Cramér metric. In particular,
\mathcal{G}
is a non-expansive operator with at least one fixed point. We now turn from this exact projected backup to an SA thereof, based on samples from interaction with the MDP. Customarily, SA in RL comes in the form of a TD update, where we observe only one transition at a time and thus asynchronously modify only one state block.

A one-state sample is defined to be a tuple
y=(s,b,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Y}:=\mathcal{S}\times[-1,1]\times\mathcal{S}
.
2
2

2

Note that rewards lying in
[0,1]
implies that centered rewards lie in
[-1,1]
.
For
p\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
, we define
H(p,y)=q\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
, the one-sample backup map, by

q_{u}=\begin{cases}p_{u},&u\neq s,\\
\tilde{q},&u=s,\end{cases}

(18)

where
\tilde{q}\in\Delta_{d}
is the unique coefficient vector satisfying
\Pi^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C}}\bigl((\tau_{b})_{\#}\eta^{p_{s^{\prime}},0}\bigr)=\eta^{\tilde{q},0}
. The asynchronous recursion is thus the well-known stochastic Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann (SKM) iteration
[

,
27

p_{k+1}=p_{k}+\alpha_{k}\bigl(H(p_{k},Y_{k})-p_{k}\bigr),\quad k\geq 0.

(19)

The next proposition rewrites the

-centered projected operator
\mathcal{G}_{g}
blockwise in categorical coefficients, which is the form needed to derive asynchronous SA updates. Before the statement, we introduce a slight abuse of notation. For
u\in\Delta_{d}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
, we write
\eta^{u,c}:=\sum_{k=1}^{d}u_{k}\delta_{\theta_{k}+c}
.
In particular,
(\eta^{p,c})_{i}=\eta^{p_{i},c}
for
p\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
.

Proposition 9
.

Let
g\in\mathbb{R}
,
p\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
, and
i\in\mathcal{S}
. The
i
th block of
\mathcal{G}_{g}(p)
is the unique vector
q_{i}\in\Delta_{d}
such that

\eta^{q_{i},0}=\Pi^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C}}\Bigl(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}P_{ij}(\nu_{ij}^{(g)}\,\ast\,\eta^{p_{j},0})\Bigr).

(20)

The next lemma simply states that the one-sample backup map is also a non-expansion.

Lemma 10
.

For every
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
p,q\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
,

\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(H(p,y),H(q,y)\bigr)\leq\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}(p,q).

(21)

We will now work on creating an asynchronous relaxation of
\mathcal{G}
through the use of the one-sample backup map
H
. Let
\rho\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
such that
\rho_{\min}:=\min_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\rho_{i}>0
. We define

D_{\rho}:=\mathrm{diag}(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{m}),\qquad h_{\rho}(p):=p+D_{\rho}\bigl(\mathcal{G}(p)-p\bigr).

(22)

h_{\rho}
is the
mean-field map
of the one-sample stochastic update under state-sampling law
\rho
, in the standard SA sense. Of particular interest will be when
\rho=\mu
, the stationary distribution of the Markov chain under
\pi
. We have the following favorable results on this averaged map.

Proposition 11
.

Suppose
Y=(S,B,S^{\prime})
is sampled such that
S\sim\rho
and

\mathbb{P}(S^{\prime}=j\mid S=i)=P_{ij},\qquad B\mid(S=i,S^{\prime}=j)\sim\nu_{ij}\quad\text{for all }i,j\in\mathcal{S}.

(23)

Then,
\mathbb{E}\bigl[H(p,Y)\bigr]=h_{\rho}(p)
for all
p\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
,

is non-expansive in
\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}
, and
\mathrm{fix}(h_{\rho})=\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{G})
. In particular, the same conclusions hold with

The next corollary will be useful in translating residual bounds for

into residual bounds for

Corollary 12
.

For every
p\in\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
,

\rho_{\min}\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(p,\mathcal{G}(p)\bigr)\leq\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(p,h_{\rho}(p)\bigr)\leq\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(p,\mathcal{G}(p)\bigr).

(24)

We state the two main SA results for the centered-reward recursion approximating

The Markovian regime captures online asynchronous TD-learning along a single trajectory of the policy-induced chain. In both settings, the quantity we control is the mean-field residual
\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(p_{k},h_{\rho}(p_{k})\bigr)

Theorem 13

(Centered recursion i.i.d. convergence)

Suppose the samples

\mathbb{P}(S^{\prime}_{k}=j\mid S_{k}=i)=P_{ij},\qquad B_{k}\mid(S_{k}=i,S^{\prime}_{k}=j)\sim\nu_{ij}\quad\text{for all }i,j\in\mathcal{S}.

The finite-iteration bounds above use a two-phase step size refinement. A coupled recursion for when the gain is unknown

The centered recursion isolates the non-expansive structure, but it is not directly implementable unless the gain
\bar{r}^{\pi}
is known. In practice, we observe uncentered rewards
R
, not
B:=R-\bar{r}^{\pi}
.

Proposition 15
.

In general, replacing the centered reward
B=R-\bar{r}^{\pi}
by the raw reward
R
in the one-sample backup (
18
) does not yield an SA of
\mathcal{G}
.

We now replace centered rewards by uncentered ones, and augment the quotient-categorical update with an online estimator
g
of the gain
\bar{r}^{\pi}
, on the product space
\mathcal{Z}:=\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}\times[0,1]
. The goals of this section are threefold: first, to show that the one-sample augmented update is non-expansive. Second, to identify its mean-field map and its fixed points. Third, to relate the resulting augmented residual to the two quantities of actual interest, namely, the projected-operator residual
\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(p,\mathcal{G}(p)\bigr)
, and the gain error
\lvert g-\bar{r}^{\pi}\rvert
. For
g\in\mathbb{R}
, define the gain-parameterized one-sample backup map
H_{g}:\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\Delta^{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
as follows: For an uncentered sample
y=(s,r,s^{\prime})
, let
H_{g}(p,y)=q
, where

q_{u}=\begin{cases}p_{u},&u\neq s,\\
\tilde{q},&u=s,\end{cases}

(28)

where
\tilde{q}\in\Delta_{d}
is the unique coefficient vector satisfying
\Pi^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C}}\bigl((\tau_{r-g})_{\#}\eta^{p_{s^{\prime}},0}\bigr)=\eta^{\tilde{q},0}
. The corresponding mean-field map is defined in terms of the stationary state distribution
\mu
as
h^{(g)}_{\mu}(p):=p+D_{\mu}(\mathcal{G}_{g}\bigl(p)-p\bigr)

d_{\lambda}\bigl((p,g),(q,g^{\prime})\bigr):=\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}(p,q)+\lambda\lvert g-g^{\prime}\rvert.

We will now be simultaneously using a coupled SKM recursions with the same step size, one for the categorical coefficients, and the other for the gain:

\begin{gathered}p_{k+1}=p_{k}+\alpha_{k}\bigl(H_{g_{k}}(p_{k},Y_{k})-p_{k}\bigr),\\
g_{k+1}=g_{k}+\alpha_{k}(R_{k}-g_{k}),\end{gathered}

(30)

where
Y_{k}=(S_{k},R_{k},S_{k+1})

Proposition 16
.

For every uncentered sample
y=(s,r,s^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Y}
, and all
(p,g),(q,g^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Z}

(31)

The next proposition identifies the mean-field map associated with the stationary distribution
\mu

Proposition 17
.

Let
Y=(S,R,S^{\prime})
be a one-step sample from the stationary distribution of the policy-induced chain, i.e.,

S\sim\mu,\qquad\mathbb{P}(S^{\prime}=j\mid S=i)=P_{ij},\qquad R\mid(S=i,S^{\prime}=j)\sim\mathrm{Law}(R_{ij})

for all

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl(H_{g}(p,Y),R\bigr)\bigr]=\bigl(h^{(g)}_{\mu}(p),\bar{r}^{\pi}\bigr)\quad\text{for all }(p,g)\in\mathcal{Z}.

(33)

Moreover, the map

\mathrm{fix}\bigl((p,g)\mapsto\bigl(h^{(g)}_{\mu}(p),\bar{r}^{\pi}\bigr)\bigr)=\{(p,\bar{r}^{\pi}):p\in\mathrm{fix}(\mathcal{G})\}

Corollary 18

For every

\mu_{\min}\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(p,\mathcal{G}(p)\bigr)\leq d_{\lambda}\bigl(\bigl(p,g\bigr),\bigl(h^{(g)}_{\mu}(p),\bar{r}^{\pi}\bigr)\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\lvert g-\bar{r}^{\pi}\rvert\leq\lambda^{-1}d_{\lambda}\bigl(\bigl(p,g\bigr),\bigl(h^{(g)}_{\mu}(p),\bar{r}^{\pi}\bigr)\bigr).

(34)

8
Conclusion

We introduced a quotient-categorical formulation for average-reward distributional bias estimation, resolving the additive-constant ambiguity of the scalar bias at the level of state-indexed laws. This viewpoint leads to a projected operator that is well-defined and non-expansive in a coordinate Cramér metric, and to TD-style SA schemes with a.s. convergence and finite-iteration residual guarantees. When the gain is unknown, augmenting the categorical recursion with an online gain estimate preserves the same non-expansive structure and yields joint control of the projected residual and gain error. We empirically corroborated these predictions on a five-state MDP, and included a continuous-state neural function approximation experiment in Appendix
E
. The same machinery should also extend to multivariate categorical representations by replacing the one-dimensional cumulative embedding and projection with their multivariate analogues, following the construction of
22
. Our theory focuses on finite-state fixed-policy evaluation with fixed categorical supports. Theoretical analysis of richer function approximation and control settings is a natural next step.

\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C}}\bigl(H(p,y)_{s},H(q,y)_{s}\bigr)=\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C}}(L_{b}p_{s^{\prime}},L_{b}q_{s^{\prime}}).

(56)

Using Proposition

\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C}}(L_{b}p_{s^{\prime}},L_{b}q_{s^{\prime}})\leq\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C}}(p_{s^{\prime}},q_{s^{\prime}}).

(57)

Taking the maximum over all state blocks proves the result.
∎

B.5
Proof of Theorem

We first state the following proposition.

Proposition 23

(Two-phase i.i.d. residual refinement)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
13
, fix
a_{1}\in\bigl(\frac{2}{3},1\bigr)
and set

\varepsilon_{a_{1}}:=\frac{3a_{1}-2}{6},\qquad\kappa_{a_{1}}:=\frac{2^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}}{\log 2}.

(65)

Let
T
be the largest integer such that

(k+1)^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}\leq\kappa_{a_{1}}\log(k+1),\qquad 1\leq k\leq T.

(66)

If the centered SKM recursion uses the two-phase step size

\alpha_{k}=\begin{cases}(k+1)^{-a_{1}},&1\leq k\leq T,\\
(k+1)^{-2/3},&k>T,\end{cases}

(67)

then there exists
C_{\mathrm{iid}}>0
such that, for all
k\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(p_{k},h_{\rho}(p_{k})\bigr)\Bigr]\leq C_{\mathrm{iid}}(k+1)^{-1/6}\min\Bigl\{\kappa_{a_{1}}\log(k+1),(k+1)^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}\Bigr\}.

(68)

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(p_{k},\mathcal{G}(p_{k})\bigr)\Bigr]\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{iid}}}{\rho_{\min}}(k+1)^{-1/6}\min\Bigl\{\kappa_{a_{1}}\log(k+1),(k+1)^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}\Bigr\}.

(69)

Proof.

Since the tail exponent in (
67
) is the critical value
2/3
, the a.s. convergence assertion remains the separate
a>\frac{2}{3}
assertion in Theorem
13
. The role of the two-phase schedule is to interpolate between the near-critical transient behavior of an exponent
a_{1}>\frac{2}{3}
and the critical last-iterate residual behavior.

We first record the constants used below. Let

D:=\sqrt{\theta_{d}-\theta_{1}},\qquad M_{2}:=\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|}D,

(70)

and define

S_{3a_{1}/2}:=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}(t+1)^{-3a_{1}/2},\quad\omega_{a_{1}}:=\frac{1-2^{-a_{1}}}{2},\quad\nu_{a_{1}}:=\frac{1-2^{-a_{1}}}{1-a_{1}},\quad\eta_{a_{1}}:=1-(3/4)^{1-a_{1}}.

(71)

Also let

R_{a_{1}}:=\frac{(4/3)^{a_{1}}}{1-4^{-a_{1}}},

(72)

K_{a_{1}}:=\frac{D}{\sqrt{\pi\omega_{a_{1}}}}+\frac{2M_{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{S_{3a_{1}/2}}{\sqrt{\nu_{a_{1}}\eta_{a_{1}}}}+\frac{2^{1+a_{1}/2}R_{a_{1}}}{\sqrt{1-a_{1}}}\right),

(73)

and

B_{T}:=\max\left\{\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}},\,8^{1/6}(T+2)^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}\right\}.

(74)

The final constant can be chosen as

C_{\mathrm{iid}}:=\max\left\{K_{a_{1}}+6M_{2},\;K_{a_{1}}B_{T}+\frac{2M_{2}\sqrt{6}}{\sqrt{\pi}}+6M_{2}\right\}.

(75)

The threshold in (
66
) is well defined. Indeed, for
x\geq 2
, the function
x^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}/\log x
decreases until
x=\exp(1/\varepsilon_{a_{1}})
and then increases to infinity. Its value at
x=2
is
\kappa_{a_{1}}
. Hence the set of integers satisfying (
66
) is a nonempty initial interval, and after its last element
T
the reverse inequality holds.

We now derive the residual estimate. Work in the cumulative-coordinate embedding associated with
\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}
. The block-sup Cramér metric is the block-sup norm of Euclidean cumulative-coordinate vectors, and this block-sup norm is bounded by the ambient Euclidean norm. Under the i.i.d. assumptions, the noise

U_{k}:=H(p_{k-1},Y_{k})-h_{\rho}(p_{k-1})

(76)

is a martingale difference and satisfies
\|U_{k}\|_{\mathrm{C},2}\leq M_{2}
. Define the averaged noise process

\overline{U}_{k}=(1-\alpha_{k})\overline{U}_{k-1}+\alpha_{k}U_{k},\qquad\overline{U}_{0}=0.

(77)

By orthogonality of martingale differences in the Euclidean cumulative-coordinate space and Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\overline{U}_{k}\|_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\overline{U}_{k}\|_{\mathrm{C},2}\bigr]\leq M_{2}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{k}\alpha_{t}^{2}\prod_{r=t+1}^{k}(1-\alpha_{r})^{2}}.

(78)

Let

v_{k}:=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\alpha_{t}^{2}\prod_{r=t+1}^{k}(1-\alpha_{r})^{2}.

(79)

We claim that
v_{k}\leq\alpha_{k}
for all
k\geq 1
. Since

v_{k}=(1-\alpha_{k})^{2}v_{k-1}+\alpha_{k}^{2},

(80)

it is enough by induction to verify

\alpha_{k}^{-1}-\alpha_{k-1}^{-1}\leq 1.

(81)

In the first phase this follows from the mean-value theorem:

(k+1)^{a_{1}}-k^{a_{1}}\leq a_{1}k^{a_{1}-1}<1.

(82)

In the second phase,

(k+1)^{2/3}-k^{2/3}\leq\frac{2}{3}k^{-1/3}<1.

(83)

At the transition point,

\alpha_{T+1}^{-1}-\alpha_{T}^{-1}=(T+2)^{2/3}-(T+1)^{a_{1}}\leq(T+1)^{2/3}-(T+1)^{a_{1}}+\frac{2}{3}(T+1)^{-1/3}<1,

(84)

because
a_{1}>2/3
. Therefore (
78
) gives

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\overline{U}_{k}\|_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigr]\leq M_{2}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}.

(85)

Following the inexact Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann estimate of
11
, with (
85
), yields

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(p_{k},h_{\rho}(p_{k})\bigr)\Bigr]\leq\frac{D}{\sqrt{\pi\tau_{k}}}+\frac{2M_{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\tau_{k}-\tau_{t}}}+6M_{2}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}},

(86)

where
\tau_{k}:=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\alpha_{t}(1-\alpha_{t})
. It remains to bound the middle sum in the two phases.

Primary phase.

Assume
1\leq k\leq T
, so
\alpha_{t}=(t+1)^{-a_{1}}
. We use

\omega_{a_{1}}(k+1)^{1-a_{1}}\leq\tau_{k}\leq\frac{1}{1-a_{1}}(k+1)^{1-a_{1}}.

(87)

The upper bound is immediate by integral comparison. For the lower bound,
1-\alpha_{t}\geq 1-2^{-a_{1}}
and the standard estimate
\tau_{k}\geq\tau_{1}k^{1-a_{1}}
imply the displayed inequality.

Split the middle sum at
\lfloor k/2\rfloor
. For
t\leq\lfloor k/2\rfloor
,

\tau_{k}-\tau_{t}\geq\nu_{a_{1}}\eta_{a_{1}}(k+1)^{1-a_{1}},

(88)

and therefore

\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\tau_{k}-\tau_{t}}}\leq\frac{S_{3a_{1}/2}}{\sqrt{\nu_{a_{1}}\eta_{a_{1}}}}(k+1)^{-(1-a_{1})/2}.

(89)

For
t>\lfloor k/2\rfloor
,

\alpha_{t}^{1/2}\leq 2^{a_{1}/2}(k+1)^{-a_{1}/2}.

(90)

Using the left-shifted integral comparison

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau_{k}-\tau_{t}}}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{t+1}(1-\alpha_{t+1})}\int_{\tau_{t}}^{\tau_{t+1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\sqrt{\tau_{k}-\tau}},

(91)

and the ratio bound

\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\alpha_{t+1}(1-\alpha_{t+1})}\leq R_{a_{1}},

(92)

we get

\sum_{t=\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}^{k-1}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\tau_{k}-\tau_{t}}}\leq\frac{2^{1+a_{1}/2}R_{a_{1}}}{\sqrt{1-a_{1}}}(k+1)^{-(1-a_{1})/2}.

(93)

Combining these estimates with (
86
) and (
87
) gives

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(p_{k},h_{\rho}(p_{k})\bigr)\Bigr]\leq(K_{a_{1}}+6M_{2})(k+1)^{-(1-a_{1})/2}.

(94)

Since

(k+1)^{-(1-a_{1})/2}=(k+1)^{-1/6}(k+1)^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}},

(95)

and (
66
) makes the minimum equal to
(k+1)^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}
in this phase, (
94
) proves the claimed bound for
k\leq T
.

Secondary phase.

Now let
k>T
, so the tail stepsizes satisfy
\alpha_{t}=(t+1)^{-2/3}
. For any
t^{\prime}\geq T
,

\tau_{k}-\tau_{t^{\prime}}=\sum_{t=t^{\prime}+1}^{k}\alpha_{t}(1-\alpha_{t})\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=t^{\prime}+1}^{k}(t+1)^{-2/3}\geq\frac{3}{2}\bigl((k+1)^{1/3}-(t^{\prime}+1)^{1/3}\bigr).

(96)

Split the middle sum in (
86
) at
T
.

For the tail part
t>T
, we have
\alpha_{t}^{3/2}=(t+1)^{-1}
. By (
96
),

\sum_{t=T+1}^{k-1}\frac{(t+1)^{-1}}{\sqrt{\tau_{k}-\tau_{t}}}\leq\int_{T}^{k}\frac{(x+1)^{-1}}{\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}((k+1)^{1/3}-(x+1)^{1/3})}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(97)

With
u=(x+1)^{1/3}
and
c=(k+1)^{1/3}
, this integral equals

\sqrt{6}\int_{(T+1)^{1/3}}^{(k+1)^{1/3}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u\sqrt{(k+1)^{1/3}-u}}

(98)

and is bounded by

\sqrt{6}\,(k+1)^{-1/6}\log(k+1).

(99)

To see the last step, write
x=((k+1)/(T+1))^{1/3}>1
in the logarithmic expression obtained after integration:

\frac{\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{x-1}}{\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{x-1}}=2x-1+2\sqrt{x^{2}-x}\leq 4x-1\leq 3x^{3}\leq(T+1)x^{3}=k+1.

(100)

It remains to control the finitely many primary-phase terms
t\leq T
. Evaluating the first two terms in (
86
) at
T+1
and using the primary-phase estimate gives

\frac{D}{\sqrt{\pi\tau_{T+1}}}+\frac{2M_{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\tau_{T+1}-\tau_{t}}}\leq K_{a_{1}}(T+2)^{-(1-a_{1})/2}.

(101)

If
k+1\geq 8(T+2)
, then (
96
) with
t^{\prime}=T+1
gives

\tau_{k}-\tau_{T+1}\geq\frac{3}{4}(k+1)^{1/3},

(102)

and hence these early terms are bounded by

K_{a_{1}}\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}(k+1)^{-1/6}.

(103)

If instead
k+1<8(T+2)
, then

K_{a_{1}}(T+2)^{-(1-a_{1})/2}\leq K_{a_{1}}8^{1/6}(T+2)^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}(k+1)^{-1/6}.

(104)

Thus, in both cases, the early terms are bounded by

K_{a_{1}}B_{T}(k+1)^{-1/6}.

(105)

Combining (
99
), (
105
), and the terminal term
6M_{2}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}=6M_{2}(k+1)^{-1/3}\leq 6M_{2}(k+1)^{-1/6}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(p_{k},h_{\rho}(p_{k})\bigr)\Bigr]\leq\left(K_{a_{1}}B_{T}+\frac{2M_{2}\sqrt{6}}{\sqrt{\pi}}+6M_{2}\right)(k+1)^{-1/6}\log(k+1).

(106)

For
k>T
, the definition of the last crossing
T
gives

(k+1)^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}>\kappa_{a_{1}}\log(k+1),

(107)

so the minimum in the proposition is
\kappa_{a_{1}}\log(k+1)
. Since
\kappa_{a_{1}}\geq 1
, the choice (

12
.

B.6
Proof of Theorem
14

We first state a lemma and then a proposition.

Lemma 25

(Markovian inexact-KM reduction)
.

Consider an SKM recursion on a compact subset of a finite-dimensional normed space, driven by a finite-state irreducible and aperiodic Markov chain, with samplewise non-expansive update map
H
and non-expansive stationary mean-field map
h
. Let the step sizes be either
\alpha_{k}=(k+1)^{-a}
with
a>\frac{4}{5}
, or the matched two-phase schedule in Proposition
26
. Let

\tau_{k}:=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\alpha_{t}(1-\alpha_{t}),\qquad\sigma(0):=1,\qquad\sigma(u):=\min\{1,(\pi u)^{-1/2}\}\quad(u>0).

(110)

There are constants
A_{0},A_{1},A_{2}>0
such that the iterates satisfy

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|x_{k}-h(x_{k})\|\bigr]\leq A_{0}\sigma(\tau_{k})\left(1+\sum_{t=2}^{k}\alpha_{t}\omega_{t-1}\right)+A_{1}\sum_{t=2}^{k}\alpha_{t}\sigma(\tau_{k}-\tau_{t})\omega_{t-1}+A_{1}\omega_{k},

(111)

where the deterministic envelope
\omega_{k}
obeys

\omega_{k}\leq A_{2}\tau_{k}\sqrt{\alpha_{k+1}}.

(112)

Proof.

This is the estimate obtained in the proof of
7
before specializing the last step to a single polynomial step size. The only Markovian ingredient is the Poisson-equation decomposition of

H(x_{k},Y_{k+1})-h(x_{k}),

(113)

which writes the Markovian noise as a martingale difference plus three telescoping error terms. Since the chain is finite and the update map is samplewise non-expansive on a compact set, the Poisson solution is uniformly bounded and Lipschitz on the compact state space. The auxiliary correction process
U_{k}
in that proof therefore satisfies the deterministic envelope (
112
). Applying the inexact Krasnosel’skiĭ–Mann residual estimate to
z_{k}=x_{k}-U_{k}
gives (
111
); the three displayed terms correspond respectively to the initial-distance/inexactness constant, the convolution with the correction process, and the final correction
U_{k}
.

The polynomial form is used in
7
only when converting (
111
) into an explicit rate. The preceding Poisson-equation reduction uses the displayed regularity properties of the step sizes, namely monotonicity and bounded adjacent ratios, to control the telescoping error terms. These properties hold for the two-phase schedule because the transition between the two phases is continuous and each phase is polynomial. The critical-tail summations needed to turn (
111
) into a rate are verified explicitly in Proposition
26
below.
∎

Proposition 26

(Two-phase Markovian residual refinement)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
14
, fix
a_{1}\in\bigl(\frac{4}{5},1\bigr)
and set

\varepsilon_{a_{1}}:=\frac{5a_{1}-4}{10},\qquad\kappa_{a_{1}}:=\frac{2^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}}{\log 2}.

(114)

Let
T
be the largest integer such that

(k+1)^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}\leq\kappa_{a_{1}}\log(k+1),\qquad 1\leq k\leq T.

(115)

Set
\gamma_{T}:=(T+1)^{-a_{1}}(T+2)^{4/5}
, and use the two-phase step size

\alpha_{k}=\begin{cases}(k+1)^{-a_{1}},&1\leq k\leq T,\\
\gamma_{T}(k+1)^{-4/5},&k>T.\end{cases}

(116)

Then there exists
C_{\mathrm{mk}}>0
such that, for all
k\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(p_{k},h_{\mu}(p_{k})\bigr)\Bigr]\leq C_{\mathrm{mk}}(k+1)^{-1/10}\min\Bigl\{\kappa_{a_{1}}\log(k+1),(k+1)^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}\Bigr\}.

(117)

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(p_{k},\mathcal{G}(p_{k})\bigr)\Bigr]\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{mk}}}{\mu_{\min}}(k+1)^{-1/10}\min\Bigl\{\kappa_{a_{1}}\log(k+1),(k+1)^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}\Bigr\}.

(118)

Proof.

The threshold is well defined by the same argument as in Proposition
23
: the function
x^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}/\log x
decreases from its value at
x=2
until
x=\exp(1/\varepsilon_{a_{1}})
and then increases to infinity. Thus the set of integers satisfying (
115
) is a nonempty initial interval. The factor
\gamma_{T}
makes the schedule continuous at the transition, since
\alpha_{T+1}=\alpha_{T}
, and hence non-increasing.

For
k\leq T
, the recursion has only used the polynomial schedule
\alpha_{t}=(t+1)^{-a_{1}}
. The polynomial Markovian residual bound of
7
therefore gives

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\ell^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{C},\infty}\bigl(p_{k},h_{\mu}(p_{k})\bigr)\Bigr]\leq C(k+1)^{-(1-a_{1})/2}.

(119)

Since

(k+1)^{-(1-a_{1})/2}=(k+1)^{-1/10}(k+1)^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}},

(120)

and the minimum in the proposition is
(k+1)^{\varepsilon_{a_{1}}}
in this phase, the desired bound follows for
k\leq T
.

It remains to consider
k>T
. We apply Lemma
25
in the cumulative-coordinate norm that represents the block-sup Cramér metric. Since
T
is fixed once
a_{1}
is fixed, the two-phase schedule gives constants
0<c_{\tau}<C_{\tau}<\infty
such that, for all
k>T
,

c_{\tau}(k+1)^{1/5}\leq\tau_{k}\leq C_{\tau}(k+1)^{1/5}.

(121)

Indeed, the tail contribution is a constant multiple of
\sum_{t>T}(t+1)^{-4/5}
, and the finitely many transition values are absorbed into
c_{\tau}
and
C_{\tau}
. Combining (
121
) with (
112
) yields

\omega_{k}\leq C(k+1)^{-1/5}.

(122)

Consequently,

\sum_{t=2}^{k}\alpha_{t}\omega_{t-1}\leq C\left(1+\sum_{t=T+1}^{k}(t+1)^{-1}\right)\leq C\log(k+1).

(123)

The first term in (
111
) is therefore bounded by
C(k+1)^{-1/10}\log(k+1)
, and the final correction term (
122
) is smaller than this bound.

It remains to bound the convolution term in (
111
). The finitely many terms with
t\leq T
contribute at most
C(k+1)^{-1/10}
after increasing
C
, again using (
121
); the finitely many near-transition cases are absorbed into the same constant. For the tail terms
T<t<k
, (
122
) gives
\alpha_{t}\omega_{t-1}\leq C(t+1)^{-1}
, and the tail lower bound gives

\tau_{k}-\tau_{t}\geq c\bigl((k+1)^{1/5}-(t+1)^{1/5}\bigr).

(124)

Thus

\sum_{t=T+1}^{k-1}\alpha_{t}\sigma(\tau_{k}-\tau_{t})\omega_{t-1}\leq C\sum_{t=T+1}^{k-1}\frac{(t+1)^{-1}}{\sqrt{(k+1)^{1/5}-(t+1)^{1/5}}}.

(125)

By the same integral comparison used in the i.i.d. proof, with the change of variables
u=(x+1)^{1/5}
, the last display is at most

C\int_{(T+1)^{1/5}}^{(k+1)^{1/5}}\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{u\sqrt{(k+1)^{1/5}-u}}\leq C(k+1)^{-1/10}\log(k+1).

(126)

The omitted
t=k
endpoint is bounded by
C(k+1)^{-1}
and is therefore harmless. Combining these bounds in (

Appendix D
Synchronous gain-free structure

The previous section handles raw rewards by estimating the gain online. We close with a complementary exact-law observation: if one forms a full synchronous sampled backup before choosing categorical representatives, then the centering constant disappears already at the quotient level. This is a stronger, samplewise invariance statement than the fixed-point invariance of the exact quotient operator, but it lives in the idealized space of quotient distributions rather than in the practical fixed-grid categorical representation. To make the statement precise, let

\mathbf{Y}=\bigl((S^{\prime}_{i},R_{i})\bigr)_{i\in\mathcal{S}}

(160)

be a synchronous one-step sample such that, for each
i\in\mathcal{S}
,

\mathbb{P}(S^{\prime}_{i}=j)=P_{ij},\qquad R_{i}\mid(S^{\prime}_{i}=j)\sim\mathrm{Law}(R_{ij}),

(161)

with the coordinates sampled independently across
i
. Thus
\mathbf{Y}
contains one sampled successor and reward for every state. For
\eta\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}
and
g\in\mathbb{R}
, define the exact synchronous sample map by

\bigl(\mathcal{H}_{g}(\eta,\mathbf{Y})\bigr)_{i}:=(\tau_{R_{i}-g})_{\#}\eta_{S^{\prime}_{i}},\qquad i\in\mathcal{S}.

(162)

Changing
g
changes each centered reward
R_{i}-g
by the same additive amount. Since the update is synchronous, that same shift appears in every output block.

Proposition 29
.

For every synchronous sample
\mathbf{Y}
, every
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, and every
\eta\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}

\mathcal{H}_{g^{\prime}}(\eta,\mathbf{Y})=(\tau_{g-g^{\prime}})_{\#}\mathcal{H}_{g}(\eta,\mathbf{Y}).

(163)

[\mathcal{H}_{g}(\eta,\mathbf{Y})]=[\mathcal{H}_{g^{\prime}}(\eta,\mathbf{Y})]\quad\text{in }\mathcal{F}^{\mathcal{S}}/\approx.

(164)

Thus the exact synchronous quotient sample is independent of the centering constant.

Proof.

For every state
i
,
R_{i}-g^{\prime}=(R_{i}-g)+(g-g^{\prime})
, so
(\tau_{R_{i}-g^{\prime}})_{\#}\eta_{S^{\prime}_{i}}=(\tau_{g-g^{\prime}})_{\#}\bigl((\tau_{R_{i}-g})_{\#}\eta_{S^{\prime}_{i}}\bigr)

is common to every state block, so the two output families represent the same element in the equivalence class.
∎
