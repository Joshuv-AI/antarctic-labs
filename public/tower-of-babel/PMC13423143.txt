Extending the age limit of paediatric care for young people from refugee backgrounds
Authors: Sarah Cherian, Claire Breidahl, Avisha Hamilton, Kajal Hirani
Published: 2026

Adolescence is a time of transition across physical, psychological, developmental, cognitive, education and social domains, spanning 10–19 years of age. It is a concurrent period of change in health access, with paediatric services initiating transition to adult and/or primary care necessitating increased independence and self-advocacy for the adolescent and family. Transition risks vary across adolescence, with those impacted by long-term conditions, complex disability, oncological conditions and/or palliative needs commonly requiring formal pathways to prevent disruption to care when entering adult services.

Adolescents from refugee-like backgrounds (including humanitarian entrants, asylum-seekers, unaccompanied minors, internally displaced and family reunification) experience additional barriers to health access overseas during flight, in transit and resettlement.1 There is a complex interplay with physical health, psychological well-being, social engagement, education and developmental maturity which can be disrupted, delayed and/or impacted at all stages of refugee transit and resettlement. Given that prefrontal cortex maturation, governing executive functioning and higher-order cognition, continues into the mid-20s and is influenced by trauma, environmental disruptions and cultural transitions, the implications for refugee-background adolescents taking on autonomy are profound.2 Specific refugee health needs, cross-cultural aspects of care as well as developmentally appropriate and trauma-informed principles of management of adolescent refugees require a holistic framework which embeds multidisciplinary care across health, development and education.1 3

Adolescence is a high-risk period for diagnosis and management of health conditions, with emerging physical and mental health needs coinciding with transition to adult hospitals and/or primary care and requiring coordination across jurisdictions. For older refugee-like adolescents, these risks are compounded by paediatric health service intake age restrictions (commonly <16 years) limiting access to timely investigations and diagnosis compared with locally born peers. Undertaking investigations, diagnosis and management of conditions may be prevented due to limited overseas health access secondary to conflict, refugee status, protracted transit with intergenerational impacts of war, interrupted education (young person and/or parents), disrupted family units, stigma, financial barriers and reduced health literacy.

With escalating numbers of globally displaced adolescents, there is a pressing need to address their evolving comprehensive healthcare needs across all domains at the critical juncture of transition to adulthood.4 This viewpoint examines the rationale for extending the paediatric access age-limit for adolescents from refugee-like backgrounds to ensure culturally nuanced, developmentally appropriate and trauma-informed care.

Complex health profiles and delayed diagnoses

Adolescents from refugee-like backgrounds often present with a substantial burden of health issues, including infections, oral health concerns, immunisation gaps, long-term conditions of childhood, nutritional deficiencies, sexual and reproductive health conditions that have been previously undiagnosed or untreated1 5 beyond trauma experiences.6 These issues may be further compounded by health risk behaviours such as substance use and mental health problems reinforcing the need for multidisciplinary age-appropriate psychosocial assessments routinely conducted in paediatric services.6 7 Poor sexual and reproductive health knowledge in a population with a high risk of sexual violence, at an age associated with emergence of risky sexual behaviours, warrants targeted culturally appropriate sexual health assessment and counselling during consultations. It is important that both basic refugee issues (eg, catch-up immunisations, identifying nutritional deficiencies or infections) and complex health and adolescent-specific concerns are undertaken, which commonly requires timely dual accessibility to community healthcare and specialist multidisciplinary paediatric refugee services.

Complications related to general and specific learning difficulties and/or disability are typically minimised for locally born children with access to adequate healthcare and early intervention including allied health and specialist paediatrician input. Conversely, adolescents from refugee-like backgrounds often have no prior developmental assessment, limited access to early intervention and/or prolonged periods without formal education or health surveillance. Paediatric refugee services function as a bridge to ensure comprehensive resettlement review enabling appropriate investigations, linkages to allied health and referral for subspecialist assessment across referring paediatric services, receiving adult hospitals and/or primary care. However, when paediatric intake is restricted to under 16 years, older adolescents may miss this opportunity entirely. Adult services are not routinely equipped to undertake foundational developmental assessments, with potential substantial downstream impacts on future socioeconomic, educational and vocational trajectories.

Social determinants of health impacting transition into adulthood

Adolescents from refugee-like backgrounds commonly experience multiple adversities including loss of home, death or separation of family members, violence, malnutrition, prolonged transit through refugee camps and closed immigration detention.1 3 8 9 Housing pressures, food and financial insecurities may persist, impacting on ability to physically mature, feel safe and/or require the adolescent to seek employment at the cost of educational attendance. Psychosocial impacts are commonly exacerbated by acculturation stressors associated with resettlement in a new country including experiencing new social norms for relationships, navigating a new culture, financial hardship, integration into unfamiliar education systems and forming new peer relationships, while facing potential racism and discrimination. As a result of these social determinants, chronological age alone poorly reflects developmental readiness or healthcare needs. Promotion and protection of the rights of refugee adolescents to healthcare, education, social services and participation in decision making is essential as outlined by the United Nations Conventions of the Rights of Children.10 Healthcare models and service provision need to be aligned with the level of maturity and ensuring personal autonomy, rather than determined solely by an arbitrary age of transition, but also universally accessible regardless of visa status and/or country of origin.

Adolescents from refugee-like backgrounds may have missed several years of formal education and are required to integrate into high school or may have aged-out of schooling without any formal qualifications, significantly impacting future tertiary education and vocation opportunities.7 9 11 While many paediatric services have integrated pathways for educational and vocational support, most adult healthcare systems are not fully equipped to provide necessary supports. Exclusion from paediatric care based solely on chronological age risks exacerbating inequity at a critical juncture of social transitions.

Language barriers pose additional difficulties in education and vocations, as well as health settings for adolescents from refugee-like backgrounds. Communication post-resettlement can further be impacted by rarity of primary dialect interpretation and reduced health literacy, leading to delays in symptom recognition or reporting (box 1). Language and/or literacy barriers augment challenges of communicating complex diagnoses and management options, with subsequent risk of misdiagnoses, incomplete screening and/or inadequate informed consent where surgical intervention is indicated and saliently may prevent adolescents and families from asking questions or providing feedback. Appropriate use of interpreter services is therefore critical, with longer and repeat consultations often required. Sourcing external interpreters for rare dialects to facilitate true engagement and bidirectional communication may be necessary.12 Proper utilisation of professional interpreters will empower adolescents and families in consultations, enable staff to ascertain health knowledge and literacy, and identify gaps in understanding (or health pathways) that can be addressed prior to transition to adult services.

Case vignette

A 17-year-old adolescent female of Burmese refugee-background was referred to the tertiary paediatric refugee health service regarding congenital right torticollis and right ear abnormalities identified on post-arrival screening. Preliminary screening assessments were undertaken with a Burmese in-person interpreter (second language). The family spoke a rarer dialect (primary language), accessible intermittently by telephone interpreter.

The patient was born overseas via uncomplicated term vaginal delivery within a refugee camp. Her parents themselves were refugees as youth, with no formal education and experienced protracted intergenerational transit, chronic food insecurity and health access barriers due to ongoing conflicts and human rights violations. Right torticollis was noted at 2 months of age, with no formal investigation or treatment. The patient reported initially to be asymptomatic despite clinical findings. However, when subsequently assessed in her primary dialect she articulated right-sided neck pain, intermittent paraesthesia (right arm and leg) and reduced self-esteem. No other neurological, cognitive or developmental concerns were raised, nor relevant family history.

Following comprehensive paediatric tertiary review identifying other dysmorphisms and neurological changes, urgent investigations were organised. Cervical spine radiographs demonstrated reduced disc space and partial fusion of the C5/C6 vertebral bodies, superior and inferior endplate wedging of C4, and dysplastic lateral atlantoaxial and right C2–3 facet joints. The atlantodental interval was widened (5 mm). MRI and CT further characterised a complex craniocervical junction abnormality with a dysplastic dens, dysplastic atlanto-axial and atlanto-occipital articulation on the right side with a pars defect of the right C2 pedicle. Dynamic views demonstrated evidence of atlantoaxial instability, with cervical cord impingement on the cervical medullary junction. With the presence of multiple congenital anatomical abnormalities, an underlying genetic cause was suspected. The patient had a normal chromosomal microarray and was referred to the state Genetic Services.

Due to age, the patient required referral to state adult tertiary Spinal and Neurosurgery services. Strong advocacy from the paediatric refugee health team was required to ensure urgent triage, raise awareness of refugee status and wider social determinants of health, enable appropriate communication with the family and facilitate attendance at imaging and specialist appointments. Management options were discussed initially in Burmese, with the patient waitlisted for urgent spinal fusion surgery within 6 weeks. The family felt inadequately consented, with concerns around use of Burmese in the context of limited health literacy and parental education. Surgery was delayed providing time for further discussions in the family’s primary dialect with both neurosurgeons and paediatricians. This enabled discussion to address the patient’s concerns, advocacy around transition to adult services, provide appropriate safety-netting and obtain iterative informed consent to proceed with surgical intervention.

As part of standardised adolescent care, a culturally sensitive psychosocial assessment identified concerns across social, emotional, educational and sleep domains, as well as adolescent developmental needs beyond the surgical concerns. This prompted further exploration of coping strategies and protective factors, and implementation of measures to support her developing autonomy while transitioning to adult care.

Safeguarding, ethical and legal considerations

Healthcare provision to adolescents from refugee-like backgrounds presents distinct ethical, medicolegal, trauma and research challenges, requiring careful balancing of informed consent and confidentiality, and the evolving decision-making capacity of adolescents alongside family involvement and cultural expectations.1 7 12 While many adolescents take on adult roles, including caregiving responsibilities for younger siblings in the context of parental death or separation, they may lack the cognitive capacity for autonomous complex healthcare decision-making often assumed in adult healthcare services, necessitating carer or guardian support. Decision-making capacity is further shaped by linguistic diversity, trauma, intellectual disability, cultural stigma and cultural gender norms, warranting multifaceted assessment of capacity and shared decision-making.

Adolescents from refugee-like backgrounds may have experienced or have ongoing risks associated with cultural practices such as female genital mutilation/cutting (FGM/C) or early/forced child marriage,9 which are illegal in many high-income countries. Family-centred care allows access to broader family and transit histories. Adolescents may be unaware of or unable to recall experiences of FGM/C, which warrants further gynaecological referral due to potential significant sexual and reproductive health sequelae. Maternal/sibling histories may also provide important insight into potential risks of FGM/C or early/forced child marriage, providing opportunities for counselling to mitigate ongoing risks for adolescent girls, highlighting the value of family-centred care within paediatric services. Concurrently adolescent-focused care, with independent consultations are crucial to support sensitive disclosures such as those of sexual violence and promote health independence in consultations. These dual requirements are strengths of paediatric services, supporting increase in the age of transition to 18 years.

Adolescents from refugee-like backgrounds may have unknown or incorrectly assigned dates of birth and may be older or younger than assigned on legal documents.1 5 Being misclassified as an adult carries significant legal, developmental and educational consequences. This may lead to premature transition out of paediatric care, inappropriate placement in adult services, and misalignment with school enrolment, or missing high school entirely. Paediatric services are uniquely positioned to conduct developmental and educational assessments, gather collateral information and support age determination and legal amendment where possible. Access to paediatric services until 18 years of age therefore enables appropriate developmental, educational and legal assessments prior to transition to adulthood.

Unaccompanied minors (aged <18 years without a parent or legal guardian) from refugee-like backgrounds have markedly increased physical and mental health issues and safeguarding risks and as such, care may require expediting through healthcare service models.1 They have often experienced significant additional adversities and continue to remain vulnerable, including from abuse, predation and neglect. Given these complexities, comprehensive assessments, which can guide developmentally and trauma-informed care pathways, support guardianship arrangements and age-appropriate educational integration are best provided within paediatric services.

The Western Australian service model

The Western Australian tertiary multidisciplinary paediatric refugee health service at Perth Children’s Hospital has been permitted to assess outpatient adolescents between 16 and 18 years of age. Services are provided through the public health system with access to both humanitarian entrants (including unaccompanied minors) and asylum-seekers. The Refugee Health clinicians undertake advocacy to ensure timely and equitable access beyond refugee-specific paediatric services as well as access to appropriate education and mental health support, which is particularly important for asylum-seekers where they are without Medicare access limiting services, intervention and therapy. The paediatric hospital refugee health staff work closely with the community refugee health team, broader government health providers (Humanitarian Entrant Health Service, WA Tuberculosis Control Program), primary care physicians and non-government organisations to promote health access regardless of age and aligning to universal health access principles.

Clinical research and service evaluation identified key healthcare gaps for adolescents, including the need for incorporating routine physical, psychosocial, oral health, immunisation catch-up, educational and health risk behaviour screening across multiple domains.5 7 9 Screening has now been culturally modified and translated into an electronic version, increasing accessibility for ethnolinguistically diverse youth. Broader adolescent health issues (including cultural child protection concerns) and neurodevelopmental needs are routinely assessed, with expedited pathways to allied health and disability funding, with embedded hospital school services support for appropriate educational/vocational planning. The paediatric refugee team facilitates comprehensive resettlement health assessments, mitigates risks related to missed health diagnoses and ensures safer transition to adult health services, building health literacy and empowering adolescents.

Conclusions

Equipping paediatric refugee services to accept adolescents up to 18 years of age would support the medical, psychosocial, developmental transition of adolescents into adulthood, improve adult service engagement, fundamentally improving longitudinal health outcomes. Adult health service models also need to accommodate the developmental needs of adolescents from refugee-like backgrounds, particularly those who have experienced conflict and may present with complex or undiagnosed health concerns requiring coordinated care across services and/or jurisdictions. Future models of care should consider maturity rather than chronological age for the transition to adult services. Central to this process is a comprehensive assessment of needs (ability to benefit from services), true participation in decision-making by the young person and flexibility of provision up to the age of 25 from services currently engaged with refugee background young people, often limited by a maximum age restriction of 16 years. This may include the incorporation of Adolescent and Young Adult specialty care for refugee-background young people to safely bridge the developmental continuum, aligned with recent evidence that impactful refugee health models are holistic, culturally responsive, community-linked, sustainably resourced and creatively delivered by a competent workforce.13
