Fall-related injuries in older adult cancer survivors: a decade of racial disparities
Authors: Asmaa Namoos, Nicholas Thomson, Vanessa Sheppard, Michel Aboutanos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Older adults with cancer often experience treatment related and disease related complications that directly affect mobility, balance, strength, and physical reserve [1]. Chemotherapy induced peripheral neuropathy can impair sensation, proprioception, and gait stability [2, 3]. Sarcopenia and cancer related muscle loss can reduce strength and increase frailty [4]. Endocrine therapy, chemotherapy, radiation, surgery, prolonged inactivity, pain, fatigue, and multimorbidity may also contribute to bone loss, weakness, impaired balance, and functional decline [5]. These complications provide a clinically plausible pathway linking cancer treatment to increased vulnerability to fall related injuries.

Among older adults with cancer, fall related injuries are therefore not simply geriatric events; they are survivorship complications that can accelerate functional decline, increase healthcare use, disrupt cancer care, and threaten independence [6]. As cancer survival improves, more adults are living longer after diagnosis while managing the combined burden of aging, cancer treatment, comorbid disease, and persistent functional limitations [7]. This makes fall related injuries an increasingly important outcome in cancer survivorship research, particularly among adults aged 65 years and older [8].

Although falls among older adults have been widely studied, fall related injuries among cancer survivors remain less clearly characterized. Reported estimates of falls in cancer populations vary widely across studies because of differences in cancer type, treatment exposure, age, care setting, follow up period, and outcome definition [9]. This distinction is important because a fall and a fall related injury are not the same outcome [10]. A fall may be self-reported, captured through a survey, or mentioned during routine clinical care [11]. In contrast, an electronic health record documented fall related injury represents a narrower but clinically meaningful event, often severe enough to generate medical evaluation, diagnostic coding, imaging, treatment, emergency care, or follow up [12]. Therefore, EHR documented fall related injuries may capture a different survivorship burden than self-reported falls alone. Because fall related injuries are more likely to require medical attention than non-injurious falls, they represent a clinically important subset of fall events with direct implications for survivorship care, emergency care use, functional decline, and healthcare burden.

Racial differences in falls and fall related injuries are also complex. In general, older adult populations, several studies have reported higher fall rates among White adults compared with Black adults, particularly when falls are measured using self-report or community-based survey data [12, 13]. However, these findings may not directly apply to older adult cancer survivors or to EHR documented fall related injury outcomes. Cancer survivorship introduces additional pathways to injury, including treatment related neuropathy, bone fragility, frailty, mobility limitation, rehabilitation needs, symptom burden, and frequent interaction with the healthcare system. Because of these cancer specific factors, patterns observed in the general older adult population may not be sufficient to explain injury risk among survivors [14, 15].

Despite growing evidence on falls among older adults and fall risk among cancer survivors, limited evidence has examined racial differences in documented fall related injuries among older adult cancer survivors. This gap is important because EHR documented fall related injuries may capture clinically visible events that differ from self-reported falls in severity, healthcare contact, and documentation. Examining fall related injury as a distinct survivorship outcome may therefore provide insight into injury burden and potential disparities in this vulnerable population.

The present study examined the incidence and prevalence of documented fall related injuries among older adult cancer survivors over a 10-year period. We further evaluated whether documented fall related injury risk differed between Black and White cancer survivor cohorts. By focusing on EHR documented fall related injuries, this study aims to inform survivorship care, fall prevention, and equity focused screening strategies for older adults living after cancer.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study utilizes a dataset of 19,370 older adults aged 65 years and above, specifically focusing on Black and White cancer survivors who have experienced falls, as identified through their medical records. The study period spans from January 1, 2013, to December 31, 2023.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the electronic health records (EHRs) available through the TriNetX network at Virginia Commonwealth University Health System (VCUHS). The dataset includes detailed demographic information (age, sex, race, and ethnicity), diagnosis codes (ICD-10) related to survivors who had malignant cancers, and records of fall-related injuries, specifically identifying cases where patients arrived at the emergency room (ER) with injuries classified as severe or related to older adult falls, see the study flowchart in the supplement file.

The EHR data encompasses comprehensive patient records, capturing a wide range of clinical variables essential for robust epidemiological analysis. Diagnosis codes were based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), ensuring standardized and accurate identification of medical conditions. Data extraction was facilitated by the informatics team at Virginia Commonwealth University’s (VCU). Kenneth and Dianne Wright Center for Clinical and Translational Research. Their support ensured the accuracy and completeness of the dataset, enabling detailed analysis of patient outcomes.

The observational informatics program at VCUHS provided secure access to the TriNetX database, adhering to stringent data governance protocols designed to protect patient privacy and comply with regulatory standards. This program ensured that all data handling processes met ethical and legal requirements, guaranteeing the integrity and confidentiality of patient information.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using the analytic tools available within the TriNetX platform. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables, including age, were reported as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables, including sex, race, and ethnicity, were reported as frequencies and percentages. Incidence proportion was calculated as the number of patients with a new documented fall related injury divided by the number of eligible patients at risk during the study period. Prevalence was calculated as the proportion of patients with any documented fall related injury during the study period. Incidence rates were calculated as the number of new documented fall related injury cases per person day.

For the comparison between Black and White cancer survivor cohorts, TriNetX generated measures of association, including risk difference, risk ratio, odds ratio, 95% confidence intervals, Z values, and p values. Statistical significance was assessed using 2-sided p values, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Because the analysis was conducted within the TriNetX platform, the study was limited to the statistical functions available through the platform.

Software and tools

Data analysis was conducted using the analytical tools available within the TriNetX network. These tools facilitated the processing and analysis of large datasets, enabling comprehensive statistical evaluations and visualizations.

The mean age of participants was 79 years, with a standard deviation of 8 years, highlighting the predominantly older adult cohort at heightened risk of fall-related injuries. The study delineates clear trends in the incidence and prevalence of fall-related injuries among cancer survivors, with noticeable differences across age groups. For those aged 65–69 years, the incidence proportion stood at 0.0627, with a prevalence of 0.0712 and an incidence rate of approximately 4.80 cases per person-day. As age increased, so did the incidence and prevalence rates; the group aged 80–84 exhibited the highest figures, with an incidence proportion of 0.1102, prevalence of 0.1285, and an incidence rate of 9.32 cases per person-day, indicating a heightened vulnerability among the oldest subsets of the population.

Transitioning from age to sex-related differences, the data show that males had a slightly higher incidence rate at 5.67 cases per person-day compared to females, who had a rate of 5.19 cases per person-day. Despite this, females exhibited a higher prevalence rate of 0.0797, suggesting that while fewer females may experience falls, those who do fall tend to do so repeatedly. This contrast highlights the nuanced differences in fall dynamics between genders.

In terms of race, Black or African American survivors exhibited both higher incidence proportions and prevalence rates (0.0759 and 0.0896, respectively) compared to their White counterparts, who showed incidence proportions and prevalence rates of 0.0687 and 0.0753, respectively. This disparity may reflect underlying socio-economic and healthcare access differences that affect recovery and preventive care availability.

Ethnic differences further accentuate disparities, with Hispanic or Latino survivors experiencing the highest incidence rate at 0.156 cases per person-day, significantly outpacing their Non-Hispanic or Latino peers who recorded an incidence rate of 5.45 cases per person-day. This stark contrast may be influenced by cultural, linguistic, and systemic barriers that affect health outcomes and access to preventive measures. Conversely, individuals of unknown ethnicity had the lowest figures in all categories, indicating potential issues in data capture or healthcare engagement.

Comparative analysis of fall incidence between black and white cancer survivor cohorts

Table 3 provided statistical data compares the incidence of falls between Black and White cancer survivor cohorts. Within the Black Cancer Survivor cohort, which includes 5,290 patients, 260 patients experienced falls, resulting in a fall risk of 4.915%. Conversely, the White Cancer Survivor cohort, encompassing 14,080 patients, documented 570 falls, corresponding to a risk of 4.048%. The risk difference between the cohorts is 0.867% (95% CI: 0.199% to 1.534%), indicating a statistically significant higher risk of falls among Black cancer survivors compared to their White counterparts. The risk ratio and odds ratio are 1.214 (95% CI: 1.052 to 1.401) and 1.225 (95% CI: 1.054 to 1.424), respectively, suggesting the likelihood of falls is about 21–22% higher in the Black cohort. The statistical significance of these findings is confirmed with a p-value of 0.0080, highlighting a notable disparity in fall incidence between the two groups.

Discussion

Our study examined documented fall related injuries among older adult cancer survivors over a 10-year period and identified a meaningful burden of injury in this population. Approximately 7% of survivors experienced a new documented fall related injury, and nearly 8% had any documented fall related injury during the study period. Fall related injury burden increased with age, with the highest incidence and prevalence observed among survivors aged 80 to 84 years. These findings are consistent with prior work showing that older adults with cancer are vulnerable to falls and fall related injuries because of treatment related neuropathy, impaired balance, frailty, muscle weakness, bone loss, comorbidity burden, and functional decline.

The age-related pattern observed in this study is clinically important. Older cancer survivors may have limited physiologic reserve, greater multimorbidity, higher medication burden, and more persistent treatment related symptoms than younger survivors. As a result, fall related injuries in this population may represent more than isolated injury events. They may signal worsening functional vulnerability, reduced independence, increased healthcare utilization, and greater need for survivorship care planning. These findings support the need to integrate fall risk screening into routine oncology and survivorship care, particularly for adults aged 75 years and older [16].

Our finding of higher documented fall related injury risk among Black or African American cancer survivors should be interpreted cautiously and in relation to prior literature. Several studies in general older adult populations have reported lower fall rates among Black or African American adults compared with White adults, particularly when falls are measured through self-report or community based survey data [17, 18]. The present study differs from that literature because it focuses on older adult cancer survivors and captures EHR documented fall related injuries rather than all falls or self-reported falls. Therefore, the observed higher documented injury risk among Black cancer survivors should not be interpreted as evidence that race itself increases fall risk. Instead, this finding should be understood as an EHR documented fall related injury disparity within an older adult cancer survivor cohort.

Several explanations may account for this observed difference. EHR documented fall related injuries may reflect not only fall occurrence, but also injury severity, healthcare seeking, emergency care use, access to rehabilitation, survivorship follow up, coding practices, and documentation patterns [19]. Cancer related factors may also contribute, including differences in treatment exposures, neuropathy burden, frailty, bone health, comorbidity profiles, pain, and functional decline [20–22]. Broader cancer disparities literature supports the role of unequal access to cancer care, communication, symptom management, supportive services, and survivorship resources in shaping cancer outcomes [23]. However, direct evidence comparing documented fall related injury outcomes by race among older adult cancer survivors remains limited. For this reason, our findings should be considered hypothesis generating rather than causal.

The finding that Black cancer survivors had a higher documented fall related injury risk than White survivors highlight the need for more detailed survivorship research. Future studies should determine whether this disparity persists after accounting for cancer type, stage, treatment exposure, comorbidity burden, neuropathy, frailty, functional status, medication use, prior falls, rehabilitation referral, insurance status, and social determinants of health. Such work is needed to clarify whether the observed difference reflects clinical risk, structural barriers, documentation patterns, healthcare utilization, or a combination of these factors.

The Hispanic or Latino subgroup had the highest incidence rate in the stratified analysis, but this finding should be interpreted with caution because the subgroup was small. Rather than drawing strong conclusions, this result suggests the need for future studies with larger and more diverse Hispanic or Latino cancer survivor samples. More complete ethnicity data will be important for understanding whether language access, survivorship resources, care coordination, and structural barriers contribute to fall related injury risk in this population.

Strengths

This study has several strengths. It used a large EHR based cohort of 19,370 older adult cancer survivors across a 10-year period, allowing estimation of documented fall related injury burden by age, sex, race, and ethnicity. The use of TriNetX data from Virginia Commonwealth University Health System enabled analysis of routinely documented clinical outcomes in a real-world cancer survivor population. The focus on EHR documented fall related injuries is also clinically meaningful because these events are more likely to involve healthcare contact, diagnostic coding, treatment, emergency care, or follow up. This approach captures a subset of fall events with direct relevance to survivorship care, functional decline, and healthcare utilization.

Limitations

The study also has limitations. The analysis relied on structured EHR data and ICD 10 diagnosis codes, which may under capture fall related injuries that were not medically evaluated, not coded, or documented only in clinical notes. Absence of a diagnosis code should not be interpreted as true absence of a fall related injury. Because TriNetX uses code-based indicators, missingness may reflect under documentation, coding variation, care received outside the health system, or incomplete structured capture. Coding practices may also have changed during the 10-year study period, which could influence observed patterns.

The analysis was limited by the statistical tools available within TriNetX and did not adjust for cancer type, stage, treatment exposure, comorbidity burden, frailty, neuropathy, functional status, medication use, socioeconomic status, or healthcare access. Therefore, the racial comparison should be interpreted as a descriptive EHR documented disparity, not as a causal estimate.

Conclusion

This study shows that documented fall related injuries are common among older adult cancer survivors and increase with age. Black cancer survivors had a higher EHR documented fall related injury risk than White survivors; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and not as evidence that race itself causes falls. Rather, the observed difference may reflect clinical risk, injury severity, healthcare utilization, survivorship care, structural factors, or EHR documentation patterns. Future research should incorporate detailed clinical, functional, treatment related, and social risk data to clarify the mechanisms underlying this disparity and support equitable fall prevention strategies in oncology survivorship care.
