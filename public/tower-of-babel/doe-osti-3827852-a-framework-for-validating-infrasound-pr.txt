ABSTRACT
Infrasound propagation predictions support event association, arrival-window estimation, phase
interpretation, detectability assessment, and source characterization across regional to global distances.
However, prediction differences are often difficult to interpret because source representation,
atmospheric specification, model configuration, observational processing, and uncertainty are not
reported consistently. This report proposes a staged validation framework for infrasound propagation
prediction in atmospheric monitoring. The framework distinguishes numerical verification, controlled
intercomparison, retrospective validation, and semi-blind prediction-skill assessment. It treats
atmospheric inputs as versioned benchmark objects, matches model-output classes to compatible
validation targets, separates observations from interpretations, and defines source metadata, station-
outcome categories, failure-mode labels, uncertainty reporting, and tiered conformance. The
framework supports reusable and diagnostic comparisons while identifying the conditions under
which specific propagation predictions are scientifically interpretable.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Infrasound signals are low-frequency sound waves that can travel long distances through the
atmosphere. They are used to detect and interpret explosions, volcanic eruptions, meteoroid entries,
rocket launches, severe storms, ocean-wave activity, and other energetic events. They also support
international nuclear-test monitoring through the global infrasound network associated with the
Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty.
The central problem is that infrasound predictions are not always reported in a consistent way. A
difference between a predicted signal and an observed signal may be caused by the model, but it may
also be caused by uncertainty in the source, incomplete knowledge of the atmosphere, station noise,
data-processing choices, or uncertainty in the observed arrival time. Without a common reporting
structure, it can be difficult to determine why two predictions differ or why a prediction does or does
not match an observation.
This report proposes a practical validation framework for infrasound propagation predictions. The
framework is designed to help researchers and monitoring organizations compare predictions in a
transparent and reusable way. It identifies what should be defined before a prediction is made,
including the source description, atmospheric information, station information, expected observation,
and comparison method. It also identifies what should be reported afterward, including uncertainties,
station outcomes, and the likely reason for any mismatch between predictions and observations.
The report emphasizes that different prediction tools answer different questions. Some tools provide
broad arrival-time windows. Others estimate possible paths through the atmosphere, signal strength,
or the probability that a station should detect a signal. These outputs should not be judged by one
universal standard. Each prediction should be compared only with observations that are appropriate
for that type of output.
A key recommendation is to begin with a Minimum Viable Benchmark: one well-documented event,
one fixed atmospheric description, one defined set of stations, and one primary question to test. This
approach allows a single group to apply the framework without waiting for a large, coordinated
campaign. The same structure can later support broader comparisons involving more events, more
stations, and multiple prediction tools.
The main conclusion is that infrasound model validation should be diagnostic rather than competitive.
The goal is not to rank models, but to determine when a prediction is reliable, what assumptions it
depends on, and what information is missing when it fails. By making the comparison process more
transparent, this framework can improve confidence in infrasound-based monitoring and support
more consistent interpretation of detections and non-detections.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Infrasound and Atmospheric Monitoring
Infrasound refers to acoustic waves below the lower limit of typical human hearing, conventionally
below 20 Hz [1, 2]. Although generally inaudible, it is of substantial geophysical and monitoring value
because low-frequency energy can propagate over regional to global distances under favorable
atmospheric conditions [3, 4]. In the atmosphere, infrasonic waves are refracted, guided, attenuated,
scattered, and redirected by temperature, winds, density stratification, viscosity, molecular relaxation,
turbulence, topography, and the vertical structure of the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, and
thermosphere [e.g, 5, 6-9]. The recorded signal is therefore not simply a source signature but a coupled
source–atmosphere–receiver observable carrying information about both the generating process and
the propagation environment [4, 10, 11].
Long-range propagation is governed primarily by effective sound speed along the source–receiver
path, combining thermodynamic sound speed and the wind component projected along propagation
[5, 12]. Favorable winds and vertical gradients can form waveguides that return energy to the ground
far from the source, while other states suppress propagation toward the same station [13, 14]. This
directional and seasonal dependence enables remote detection but makes interpretation sensitive to
the atmospheric state at the time of the event [15-18].
Infrasound is generated by diverse natural and anthropogenic sources (Figure 1). Natural sources
include volcanic explosions and degassing, earthquakes, landslides, ocean-generated microbaroms,
severe storms, lightning, auroral processes, meteoroids, and atmospheric entries [19-26];
anthropogenic sources include chemical and mining explosions, industrial accidents, nuclear
explosions, aircraft, rocket launches and anomalies, and re-entry vehicles [27-33]. This diversity gives
broad monitoring utility but creates a demanding interpretation problem, since signals from different
sources can overlap in frequency, duration, apparent velocity, and amplitude while a single source can
produce multiple arrivals through different paths.
Source class should therefore be explicit in benchmark design. Volcanic, ocean-generated,
atmospheric-entry, rocket, weather, and explosion-related examples differ in altitude, duration,
geometry, repeatability, radiation, and station-outcome patterns [e.g., 25, 26, 27, 34-41]; a useful
benchmark should state which class is being tested rather than pooling all residuals into one
population.

1.2. Infrasound in Support of the CTBT Verification Regime
The most prominent global application of infrasound is support for the verification regime of the
Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT) [20, 43]. The Preparatory Commission for the
Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty Organization (CTBTO PrepCom) develops and maintains
the International Monitoring System (IMS), which combines seismic, hydroacoustic, infrasound, and
radionuclide technologies to provide technical capability for detecting signals associated with nuclear
explosive tests in different environments [44-46]. Within this system, infrasound provides sensitivity
to atmospheric and near-surface acoustic sources and complements the other technologies by
sampling the atmosphere as a propagation medium. Globally distributed IMS infrasound stations
(Figure 2) equipped with microbarometers support estimates of arrival time, apparent velocity, back
azimuth, signal duration, and frequency content [46, 47].
The IMS network and related national, regional, and temporary deployments also form a unique
observing system for atmospheric science and hazard monitoring. Its global geometry and long
operating history make it a practical testbed for validation practice, but only if propagation
assumptions, station detectability, noise, and atmospheric variability are reported in a way that can be
compared across events and monitoring uses.
IMS-oriented studies already treat detections, data products, and detection capability as network-scale
quantities; the remaining need is a reusable event-based structure that carries source, atmospheric, and
processing provenance with each monitoring product [e.g., 44, 48, 49-51].

1.3. From Detection to Interpretation
Detection alone rarely completes the monitoring interpretation. A station record should be associated
with a source, separated from noise and unrelated signals, characterized by arrival products, and
interpreted through the source-receiver geometry and atmospheric state. Array processing can provide
back azimuth, apparent velocity, and coherence; single-sensor records can still provide timing,
waveform character, period, and amplitude[e.g., 52, 53, 54]. Both cases require documented processing
choices because observed products are estimates, not immutable ground truth.
Observational and processing studies show that reported arrivals depend on array processing, station
geometry, coherent noise, and detection algorithms; arrival products should therefore be treated as
part of the measurement chain rather than neutral bookkeeping [e.g., 55, 56-60].
Propagation modeling provides the quantitative bridge across this coupled system [e.g., 61, 62, 63].
Model predictions estimate arrival windows, plausible paths, detectability, back-azimuth consistency,
celerity, transmission loss, and source-region or source-altitude constraints, but their meaning depends
on the source, atmosphere, station metadata, and output-selection rules used to produce them.
The interpretive value of propagation modeling depends on how predictions are generated and
reported. A predicted arrival time has limited meaning without source metadata, atmospheric
specification, propagation assumptions, frequency band, station coordinates, output-selection criteria,
and uncertainty estimates, and an apparent mismatch may reflect the propagation calculation or any
of these inputs. This makes validation a matter of scientific practice, not only software evaluation.

1.4. Aim and Contribution of this Report
This report proposes the validation architecture that infrasound propagation prediction currently
lacks: a shared specification for what should be documented, how outputs and observables may
legitimately be compared, and how uncertainty and failure should be reported, so that propagation

 11

predictions become reusable across studies, source classes, and monitoring applications. The
framework is the deliverable. It is designed to be adopted, tested, and refined at whatever level of
completeness a given study can support, while remaining stable enough for individual benchmark
cases to accumulate around it.
This framework contributes six linked elements: target-specific validation, model-output
compatibility, atmospheric-input levels, event-class separation, qualified detection and non-detection
categories, and tiered conformance from a Minimum Viable Benchmark to expanded coordinated
exercises. It also fixes shared conventions, including geodesic celerity and separation of observations
from interpretations. The framework does not rank tools; it asks whether a comparison is interpretable
for a stated monitoring use.
This focus is deliberately different from a propagation-code review or a method-by-method software
comparison. Earlier work, including Waxler and Assink [61], included benchmark-style examples that
illustrate how propagation methods can be contrasted under specified atmospheric assumptions and
why common inputs are useful. Those examples are valuable, and they demonstrate the need for a
broader community specification that links source representation, atmospheric specification, station
metadata, observational products, validation targets, uncertainty, and monitoring interpretation. The
object here is different: a method comparison asks which tool is more accurate, whereas this
framework asks what information should be preserved before any such comparison can be interpreted.
When a prediction is used to support event association, detectability assessment, phase interpretation,
or source characterization, the comparison should also control source representation, observational
product, station status, detection threshold, uncertainty, and decision context.

2. THE PRACTICE PROBLEM: WHY A SHARED VALIDATION
 STANDARD IS NEEDED
Propagation modeling is now integral to atmospheric monitoring, event association, and source
interpretation, with direct relevance for IMS-based support of the CTBT verification regime [e.g., 62,
64, 65-75]. Calculations are used to test source consistency, arrival windows, back azimuths,
meaningful non-detections, and celerity regimes; undocumented assumptions can therefore affect the
evidentiary chain connecting a pressure perturbation to a monitoring interpretation.
The need follows from the coupled nature of the problem. A recorded signal is shaped by the source,
atmospheric propagation, instrument and array response, local noise, and analysis procedure, so
observables such as arrival time, back azimuth, apparent velocity, period, and amplitude are system-
level quantities rather than direct source properties. A validation standard is needed to determine
which parts of this chain are robust, which are conditional, and which are dominated by uncertainty.
An uneven validation culture lets similar studies reach different levels of confidence while using
language that appears comparable. One study may use modeling only to define a broad association
window, another to infer a preferred phase family, another to interpret a non-detection or support
source-energy inference. These uses require different evidentiary standards, and a shared standard
makes the level of inference explicit.
A central difficulty is the attribution problem. When two predictions differ, a discrepancy in arrival
time, back azimuth, turning height, or transmission loss may reflect the propagation formulation, but
it may also reflect different atmospheric specifications, interpolation, source-altitude assumptions,
frequency choices, attenuation treatments, or the criteria used to select arrivals from a family of paths.
The same ambiguity arises against observations: a residual may reflect an inaccurate prediction or an
uncertain pick, a broad emergent onset, incorrect phase association, station noise, local scattering, or
a source-time error.
Validation should therefore distinguish four activities of different evidentiary value: (i) verification
asks whether a calculation behaves consistently under controlled conditions; (ii) controlled
intercomparison asks whether different tools agree when given the same source, receiver, atmosphere,
frequency, and output specifications; (iii) retrospective validation asks whether predictions reproduce
observations for real events once source, station, and atmospheric information are assembled; and (iv)
prediction-skill assessment asks whether outputs remain useful prospectively or under semi-blind
conditions. These should not be conflated.
Atmospheric uncertainty is often the dominant limit on prediction. Temperature, wind, density,
attenuation, and unresolved variability control refraction, ducting, turning height, transmission loss,
shadow zones, and multipathing; when these inputs are poorly constrained, a mismatch may reflect
the atmosphere more than the propagation method [18, 60, 76-79].
Event class should also be part of the design. Source classes differ enough in source altitude, geometry,
duration, radiation pattern, and source-time behavior that pooling them into one residual distribution
risks producing a result that is statistically tidy but physically ambiguous [e.g., 21, 80, 81]. Near-surface
impulsive, elevated impulsive, atmospheric-entry and re-entry, volcanic, and long-range network cases
each impose different constraints, and Section 4 develops these classes in full.
Non-detections are part of the same practice problem. A station-level non-detection can be
meaningful only when propagation, source strength, station noise, and data availability are considered
together. If the atmosphere strongly favors propagation toward a quiet, operational station and no
signal is observed, the non-detection may constrain source strength or association; if the atmosphere

 15

is uncertain or the station noise is high, the same non-detection may have limited diagnostic value.
Validation should therefore distinguish qualified non-detections from missing or unusable data, using
the station-outcome categories defined in Section 6.
The strongest validation outcome is diagnostic: it identifies robust observables, dominant uncertainty
sources, and recurring failure modes for a stated event class. Practice should proceed in stages, from
idealized checks to frozen-input real-event comparisons, sensitivity studies, and prospective or semi-
blind applications.
Event reanalyses, empirical detectability studies, and propagation assessments illustrate the same risk:
source information, station selection, atmospheric specification, and model formulation can each
change the interpretation of a signal, so residuals should not be attributed to model behavior unless
the relevant inputs are fixed and documented [e.g., 32, 50, 59, 82-88].

3. SCOPE, TERMINOLOGY, AND GOVERNING PRINCIPLES
This report addresses the validation architecture for infrasound propagation predictions used in
atmospheric monitoring. The emphasis is on validation design, traceability, uncertainty
characterization, and reporting practice [e.g., 89]. Propagation models are treated as components of a
larger source–atmosphere–propagation–observation chain. The objective is not to review propagation
theory in detail or to prescribe a single computational method. The objective is to propose the
conditions under which predictions can be compared meaningfully across source classes, atmospheric
specifications, station geometries, observational products, and monitoring applications.
The term framework, or protocol, is used in a practical rather than regulatory sense: a repeatable
structure for defining inputs, outputs, comparisons, and uncertainty, not a mandate that every study
implement every element. Its purpose is to provide a defensible structure for reasoning and reporting
whose implementation scales with source class, data quality, atmospheric information, and monitoring
use. The framework is modular: source metadata, atmospheric levels, observational grading, and
metric definitions can be adopted incrementally, and groups with different resources, data access, or
mandates can contribute at different levels of completeness within a common structure.
The framework uses the terms summarized in Table 2. A benchmark case is a curated source-
atmosphere-station-observation package for one or more prediction targets; a benchmark set samples
multiple monitoring problems; a validation target is the observable being evaluated; a prediction
product is the standardized model output; and a validation metric compares them.
Seven governing principles structure the framework.
Principle 1: Validation should be target-specific. Arrival-time prediction is primarily sensitive to
source time, source location, source altitude, effective sound speed, and path geometry. Back-azimuth
prediction is sensitive to source–receiver geometry, crosswind structure, array processing, and
multipathing. Detection prediction is sensitive to transmission loss, source strength, signal period,
station noise, and detection thresholds. Amplitude prediction is sensitive to source radiation,
atmospheric absorption, scattering, turbulence, topography, instrument response, and site conditions.
A benchmark designed for one target should not be interpreted as validation of all targets.
Principle 2: Event-class separation is physically necessary. Near-surface explosions, elevated sources,
moving atmospheric entries, volcanic sources, repeated sources, and network-level long-range cases
impose different source and propagation constraints. Event-class separation allows results to identify
the monitoring contexts in which predictions are robust and the contexts in which source
characterization or atmospheric sampling dominates uncertainty.
Principle 3: Atmospheric inputs are benchmark objects. Atmospheric products differ in temporal
sampling, horizontal resolution, vertical resolution, upper-altitude extent, data assimilation, and
representation of the middle and upper atmosphere. Even when two studies use the same named
product, differences in extraction time, interpolation, smoothing, merging, upper-atmospheric
extension, and range-dependence treatment can alter the resulting prediction. Atmospheric metadata
should therefore be sufficient to reconstruct the propagation environment.
Principle 4: Observations and interpretations should remain separate. An arrival-time pick is an
observation or measurement product. A back-azimuth estimate is a derived array product. A phase-
family assignment and source association are interpretations. Maintaining this separation prevents
circular validation, such as labeling an observed arrival as stratospheric solely because one model
predicts a stratospheric path.

 17

Principle 5: Uncertainty is part of the prediction product. A deterministic arrival time, back azimuth,
or transmission-loss estimate is incomplete without information about sensitivity to source,
atmosphere, frequency, and configuration.
Principle 6: Reproducibility requires common input and output schemas. A benchmark package
should include the source, atmospheric, station and observational, model-output, and interpretation
metadata specified in Table 7, together with validation metrics and version information, recorded so
that another investigator can reconstruct and rerun the comparison.
Principle 7: Conformance is tiered. A study can report minimum conformance through the Minimum
Viable Benchmark, while standard and extended conformance add range-dependent atmospheres,
qualified non-detections, sensitivity tests, perturbation ensembles, amplitude products, spatial
weighting, or semi-blind exercises. Each tier is defensible when the tier and omitted elements are
stated explicitly.
The scope is intentionally bounded. The framework does not attempt to declare one propagation
method generally superior, nor does it require every benchmark case to support every observable. It
defines the information needed to decide what a particular comparison can and cannot show. A timing
benchmark for a well-located impulsive source, a detectability benchmark for a long-range network
case, and an amplitude benchmark for a calibrated source are all legitimate, but they answer different
monitoring questions.

4. BENCHMARK-CASE ARCHITECTURE: EVENT CLASSES, SOURCE
 REPRESENTATION, AND PROPAGATION REGIMES
A useful benchmark case is more than an event for which infrasound was recorded. It is a structured
source–atmosphere–station–observation package designed to evaluate a defined validation target
under documented assumptions, and its value depends on the rigor with which the source,
atmosphere, observational products, and metrics are defined. Without event-class organization and
source-representation discipline, residuals are exposed to the attribution problem of Section 2, arising
from source ambiguity, atmospheric uncertainty, or observational processing rather than the
propagation calculation itself.
Benchmark cases should be selected to serve explicit validation purposes. A case intended to evaluate
arrival-time prediction requires reliable source time, source location, station geometry, and arrival
picks. A case intended to evaluate back-azimuth consistency requires array observations with
documented processing uncertainty. A case intended to evaluate detectability requires station noise,
detection thresholds, source-strength assumptions, and qualified non-detections. A case intended to
evaluate amplitude or transmission loss requires substantially stronger source and instrument
constraints than a timing-only case. The validation target determines the necessary metadata.
Independent source constraints are especially important, because a propagation benchmark should
avoid circular dependence between the model and the source definition. If source altitude is chosen
only because it gives the best travel-time agreement at one station, the case becomes source fitting
rather than an independent benchmark. Complex cases remain valuable, but their uncertainty should
be explicit and explored through source-sensitivity tests.
Near-surface impulsive sources provide a foundational class: controlled chemical explosions, mining
blasts, and accidental industrial explosions with relatively well-constrained origin time and location
[e.g., 29, 33, 79, 85, 90-92]. Such events have tested propagation over hundreds to thousands of
kilometers, but they should still report uncertainty in yield, source-time function, and near-source
coupling.
Elevated impulsive sources form a distinct class because source altitude directly influences acoustic
path geometry, travel time, launch conditions, transmission loss, and phase-family assignment [31]. A
benchmark should provide best-estimate altitude, uncertainty range, and independent evidence used
to constrain the source height. Predictions should be reported for the nominal altitude and, where
appropriate, as a sensitivity envelope across plausible altitudes.
Atmospheric-entry and re-entry sources are important but challenging because radiation may be
distributed along a trajectory rather than concentrated at a point [27, 93-99]. Benchmarks should state
whether the event is natural or artificial, controlled or uncontrolled, and whether the source is
represented as a fixed point, trajectory nodes, an altitude interval, or a station-dependent segment
(Figure 3). Rocket launches and launch anomalies are related but distinct moving or elevated sources
unless the target is a re-entry or descent segment [e.g., 30, 31, 36, 100, 101, 102].

Volcanic and geophysical sources form a different class because the process may be impulsive,
sustained, repetitive, emergent, or distributed: explosions, jetting, degassing, pyroclastic density
currents, lahars, and dome collapse [21, 103-105]. Source-time behavior and spectral content may
matter as much as location, and repeated or quasi-repeatable sources are especially valuable because
source geometry stays approximately fixed while the atmosphere varies.
Long-range network-level cases are essential for monitoring relevance, since most operational
monitoring relies on detections at hundreds to thousands of kilometers, where atmospheric effects
dominate. Their value lies in the network pattern: which stations detected, which did not, which
arrivals were marginal, and how these relate to source azimuth, range, winds, and station noise. Such
cases should include detections and qualified non-detections whenever possible.
Source representation should be treated as a benchmark variable. A point source suits localized
emission; a finite-altitude source suits cases where source height matters but horizontal extent is small;
a trajectory source suits atmospheric-entry and re-entry events; and a distributed source may be needed
for volcanic flows, sustained eruptions, or extended phenomena (Figure 3). The controlled
comparison should fix the source representation, and later sensitivity tests can evaluate plausible
alternatives.
Propagation-regime and phase-family labels should be applied conservatively. Celerity is a useful
diagnostic but cannot uniquely identify a physical path, because different regimes produce overlapping
celerity ranges and range-dependent atmospheres produce mixed or ambiguous arrivals [13, 14, 106-
108]; Section 7 specifies how celerity is reported and scored. Observed phase labels should remain
provisional unless supported by multiple observables.
Future event-based applications should be balanced rather than convenient (Table 4): baseline cases
establish reproducibility under favorable conditions, atmosphere-sensitive cases test robustness under
realistic uncertainty, source-sensitive cases evaluate altitude, trajectory, and source-time effects, and
network-level cases evaluate monitoring relevance. Difficult cases should be included deliberately,

because they reveal where atmospheric uncertainty, source ambiguity, station noise, or model
assumptions become limiting; their purpose is diagnostic rather than punitive.

5. ATMOSPHERIC SPECIFICATION AND UNCERTAINTY HIERARCHY
Atmospheric specification is among the most consequential elements of infrasound propagation
validation [77, 109]. Temperature, wind, density, attenuation, and unresolved variability determine
effective sound-speed structure, refraction, ducting, shadow zones, transmission loss, scattering, and
directional asymmetry [5, 7, 86, 87, 102, 110]. The atmospheric input should therefore be documented
well enough to reconstruct, compare, and perturb the propagation environment.
Standard atmospheric specifications used for infrasound propagation include empirical climatologies
such as the Horizontal Wind Model (HWM) [111, 112] and the Naval Research Laboratory Mass
Spectrometer and Incoherent Scatter Radar empirical atmosphere model (NRLMSIS) family [113];
ground-to-space (G2S) composites [18]; and operational reanalyses such as the fifth-generation
European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts atmospheric reanalysis (ERA5) [114].
Product choice, merging, and upper-atmospheric extension are benchmark variables.
Infrasound propagation depends on temperature, winds, density, pressure, attenuation, and turbulence
[e.g., 115, 116-120]. The effective sound-speed profile combines thermodynamic sound speed with
along-path wind and controls whether energy is refracted upward, guided, returned to the ground, or
directed away from a station.
A benchmark should report atmospheric diagnostics in the relevant propagation azimuth, not only
raw temperature and wind profiles. For a single path, the along-path effective sound-speed profile
provides a physically interpretable summary of ducting conditions. For a network-level event, effective
sound speed should be evaluated separately for each station azimuth because the same atmospheric
state can favor propagation toward one sector while suppressing it toward another. Wind conventions
and source-to-station azimuth conventions should be stated explicitly to avoid ambiguity in along-
path and cross-path projections.
Validation requires a common atmosphere, but its form depends on the target and atmospheric-input
level. A controlled intercomparison may use one frozen profile; a long-range or network benchmark
should preserve path-dependent structure through a versioned field, source-receiver extraction, or
predefined set of path-specific profiles with fixed extraction rules.
Figure 4 shows path-specific diagnostics for the 8 October 2009 Indonesian atmospheric-entry event
and IMS station I05AU [121]. The example illustrates why atmospheric provenance should include
path sampling, wind projection, vertical structure, range dependence, and the extraction convention
defining the frozen atmosphere.
Atmospheric-specification, microbarom, gravity-wave, turbulence, nonlinear-propagation, and
remote-sensing studies further support treating the atmosphere as a documented, versioned input and
evaluating uncertainty with more than a single deterministic profile [e.g., 82, 83, 118, 122, 123-125].
A single atmospheric input cannot serve all validation purposes, so the framework defines six
atmospheric-input levels from idealized profiles (Level A) through standardized one-dimensional
atmospheres (Level B), event-specific range-independent (Level C) and range-dependent (Level D)
atmospheres, perturbed or ensemble atmospheres (Level E), and retrospective best-estimate
reconstructions (Level F). These levels are not a quality ranking; they answer different questions.

Atmospheric provenance does not require a single preferred product. It requires that participants use

alternatives so disagreements can be tested against atmosphere, model, or source assumptions.
Range dependence is a major atmospheric issue for regional and long-range monitoring, where energy
may traverse changing winds, topography, synoptic systems, stratospheric jets, gravity waves, and
latitude-dependent middle-atmosphere structure. Benchmarks should state whether the atmosphere is
one-dimensional, two-dimensional along path, or three-dimensional.

Vertical extent and upper-atmospheric uncertainty require explicit attention. Infrasound can sample
the stratosphere, mesosphere, and thermosphere depending on frequency, range, and atmospheric
state, and where predicted paths reach weakly constrained altitudes this limitation should be identified.
A benchmark should report maximum altitude, vertical grid spacing, upper-boundary treatment, and
the altitude range relevant to the predicted paths.
Unresolved atmospheric variability is a major source of prediction uncertainty. Gravity waves, tides,
turbulence, small-scale wind shear, and fine vertical structure can produce multipathing, travel-time
shifts, shadow-zone arrivals, and transmission-loss fluctuations. Sensitivity to such variability should
be reported as diagnostic information, including perturbation strategy, ensemble members where
applicable, and interpretation rule.
Attenuation and frequency dependence matter especially for amplitude and detectability. Atmospheric
attenuation depends on classical absorption, molecular relaxation, viscosity, thermal conduction,
composition, frequency, and altitude [7]. It may be secondary for timing-only benchmarks but is

 26

central for detection and amplitude benchmarks, because a returned path is not a predicted detection
(Section 7).
The method classes in this framework reflect a larger modeling literature in which ray, modal,
parabolic-equation, finite-difference, full-wave, and data-driven approaches support different
products. Ray-based and empirical studies often suit screening or detectability; finite-frequency, full-
wave, high-top-atmosphere, and related numerical approaches support different validation
observables and should be matched to compatible targets [e.g., 36, 51, 83, 93, 126, 127-129].

6. OBSERVATIONAL DATA, ARRAY PRODUCTS, AND METADATA
 REQUIREMENTS
A propagation benchmark is only as interpretable as the observational products used for comparison.
Arrival times, back azimuths, apparent velocities, periods, amplitudes, and detection-status
classifications are processed measurement products, not unqualified ground truth, and should carry
provenance and uncertainty. This strengthens their use: a documented arrival or qualified non-
detection is more informative than an undocumented pick or missing waveform.
Station metadata provide the geometric and instrumental basis for comparison. Each case should
report station code, coordinates, elevation, station and sensor type, sampling rate, operational status
during the event window, and waveform availability; for arrays, also aperture, number and coordinates
of elements, geometry, wind-noise-reduction system, and known outages or degraded channels.
Waveform provenance should be documented for every validation product: data source, time window,
sampling rate, gaps, instrument correction, filtering, preprocessing, timing standard, clock correction,
and uncertainty. Filtering choices should specify type, passband, order, phase response, and causal or
acausal implementation.
Detection and arrival grouping should be transparent. If a propagation model is used to define the
search window, that dependency should be documented to avoid circular validation.
Arrival time is common but not uniquely defined. It may refer to first visible onset, first coherent array
detection, envelope rise, maximum amplitude, beam maximum, spectral maximum, or analyst-selected
time. The definition, processing band, and uncertainty should be stated for each arrival.
Back azimuth and apparent velocity are essential array-derived observables. Observed back azimuth
should be reported with uncertainty, processing band, window length, apparent velocity, coherence
or quality measure, and processing method.
Signal period and frequency content should be reported whenever possible because propagation,
attenuation, and array coherence are frequency dependent. The frequency band used for detection
should be distinguished from the frequency used in the model.
Amplitude is valuable but demanding. When amplitude is used, the benchmark should define whether
it is peak overpressure, peak-to-peak pressure, root-mean-square pressure, beam amplitude, spectral
amplitude, envelope maximum, or band-limited amplitude. If calibration is uncertain, relative
amplitude patterns may be more defensible than absolute amplitude residuals.
Station noise is central to detection and non-detection interpretation. Detection thresholds may use
signal-to-noise, array coherence, false-alarm rate, or analyst review, but the criterion, processing band,
search window, noise estimate, and array parameters should be stated.
Station outcomes should remain granular: observed detection, marginal detection, ambiguous signal,
confirmed non-detection, probable non-detection, unavailable data, unusable data, or unexamined
data. Missing data and noisy intervals should not be treated as non-detections.

7. VALIDATION OBSERVABLES, DIAGNOSTIC METRICS, AND
 FAILURE-MODE TAXONOMY
Infrasound propagation validation should not reduce to a single measure of agreement, because
predictions support several distinct monitoring tasks; the metric should match the intended use of the
prediction.
Validation observables can be organized by evidentiary requirement. First-order observables are arrival
time, back azimuth, celerity, and station-level detection status. Second-order observables include
apparent velocity, phase-family consistency, path-altitude and turning-height diagnostics, ground-
return distance, arrival inclination where available, and arrival-window width. Higher-tier observables,
including transmission loss, amplitude, waveform character, duration, spectral evolution, and
detection probability, are valuable but require stronger source, atmospheric, station, and processing
constraints.
For validation, internal model outputs should be translated into a common prediction product
appropriate to the validation target, identifying whether the result is deterministic, sensitivity-based,
or ensemble-based, since these are interpreted differently. The product should also carry the
distinction between Type A uncertainty, from repeatable or statistically estimated quantities such as
pick variability, array-processing scatter, noise, and ensemble spread, and Type B uncertainty, from
source-time constraints, atmospheric-product choice, wind and temperature biases, attenuation
assumptions, station metadata, and processing choices, so that residuals are interpreted against the
correct uncertainty budget.
Arrival time is the most widely used first-order observable. The timing residual is predicted minus
observed arrival time; signed residuals reveal systematic early or late prediction, and absolute residuals
summarize overall agreement. For moving or distributed sources, residuals depend on source node,
trajectory segment, and emission-time convention.
Back azimuth provides directional information independent of travel time. The angular residual should
be calculated using circular statistics because azimuth wraps at 360 degrees. The geodesic direction is
not always the same as the modeled arrival direction, especially when lateral refraction, crosswinds, or
three-dimensional atmospheric structure are represented.
Celerity is useful but should not be overinterpreted. Because different paths produce overlapping
celerities and moving sources produce station-dependent source relationships, celerity should be
reported as a diagnostic proxy that preserves travel time, range, back azimuth, source-time convention,
phase-family interpretation, and uncertainty, rather than used on its own to assign a path.
For benchmark purposes, celerity should use the World Geodetic System 1984 (WGS84) ellipsoid
geodesic distance [130] between the frozen source reference and station, or the assigned trajectory
node or segment for moving sources. The same convention applies to observed and predicted celerity;
other distance measures may be auxiliary sensitivity diagnostics.
Detection-status validation should compare predicted and observed station outcomes using qualified
categories rather than binary labels alone. A path is not a predicted detection unless source strength,
transmission loss, signal period, station noise, and detection thresholds support that category.
Amplitude and transmission loss are high-value but high-difficulty metrics. Amplitude residuals should
generally be expressed in logarithmic form and should be used only when amplitude definition,
measurement window, filter band, instrument correction, and source-amplitude assumption are
documented.

 29

Model–observation matching should be defined before residuals are calculated, because matching on
closest time alone can mislead when another predicted branch is more consistent in back azimuth,
celerity, phase family, or signal character; multiple plausible matches can be retained when justified.
Unmatched observations and unmatched predictions should also be retained, since these outcomes
are diagnostic and should not be discarded merely because they complicate residual statistics.
Failure modes should be classified systematically. Useful categories include missed observed arrival,
false predicted detection, timing mismatch, back-azimuth mismatch, phase-family mismatch,
detectability mismatch, amplitude or transmission-loss mismatch, unstable prediction, observational
insufficiency, and source-definition insufficiency. The purpose is diagnostic rather than punitive.
Metric summaries should be stratified by event class, atmospheric-input level, validation target, station
type, range, azimuth sector, and observation-quality grade, since pooled residual distributions can hide
the most important information.
For network-level summaries, station outcomes should be reported both unweighted and with
predefined spatial weights, so dense clusters or overrepresented azimuth sectors do not mask failures
elsewhere. The weighting rule should be fixed before scoring and reported with the package.
Where uncertainty estimates are available, residuals should be interpreted relative to the uncertainty
budget, not only as absolute differences. A timing residual that is large relative to pick uncertainty but
small relative to plausible atmospheric variability means something different from one that exceeds
both.

8. STEPWISE VALIDATION PROCEDURE FOR ATMOSPHERIC
 MONITORING APPLICATIONS
The procedure integrates event-class design, atmospheric specification, observational metadata,
metrics, and failure-mode classification. Targets, source and station metadata, atmospheric inputs,
matching rules, and scoring rules should be fixed before predictions are interpreted.
Step 1 is to define the validation objective. The benchmark should state whether it evaluates numerical
behavior, controlled intercomparison, retrospective model–data agreement, prediction skill, detection
capability, amplitude behavior, or monitoring-interpretation support.
Step 2 is to define the benchmark case and source representation. Controlled cases should use a fixed
source representation and, for entry or re-entry cases, the geometric discretization defined in Section
4 and Figure 3. Alternative geometries should be treated as sensitivity tests.
Step 3 is to define the station set and observational products. Array stations should include processing
products such as back azimuth, apparent velocity, coherence, processing band, and uncertainty. When
multiple arrivals are observed at one station, for example a stratospheric return followed by a
thermospheric return, each should be a separate entry with its own timing, back azimuth, and quality
grade, not reduced to a single detection flag.
Step 4 is to specify and freeze the baseline atmospheric input. The baseline package should define
units, coordinate systems, time standard, altitude convention, wind convention, frequency convention,
station-coordinate convention, and required output schema.
Step 5 is to conduct the controlled intercomparison and model–observation comparison. Each
calculation should use the frozen baseline package, produce the required prediction product, and
document model configuration sufficiently for reproducibility.
Step 6 is to conduct sensitivity analysis and diagnostic synthesis. Results should be stratified by event
class, atmospheric-input level, observable, station type, range, azimuth, and observation grade. Failure
modes and dominant-limitation categories should be assigned conservatively.
The procedure concludes with archiving and monitoring guidance. A reusable package should preserve
Table 7 reporting fields, scoring rules, sensitivity-test definitions, failure-mode assignments, and the
interpretation of robust, conditional, or unsupported outcomes.
The procedure is intentionally scalable. Beyond the Minimum Viable Benchmark, expanded
benchmarks add range dependence, source-altitude sensitivity, qualified non-detections, perturbation
ensembles, transmission-loss fields, and repeated or semi-blind cases.
Benchmark cases should be allowed to evolve: source information may be revised, atmospheric
products may improve, station metadata may be corrected, and arrival picks refined. Versioned
packages allow these improvements without erasing earlier results.

8.1. The Minimum Viable Benchmark: A Single-Study Entry Point
A Minimum Viable Benchmark is the smallest scientifically useful entry point: one well-documented
event, one frozen atmospheric input, one station or small station set, one primary validation target,
and a defined output schema evaluated against first-order metrics. It can be completed by a single
group using the propagation tool it already runs, and it defines the minimum conformance tier
corresponding to Steps 1-4. The same structure can be used for near-surface, atmospheric-entry, re-
entry, or other event classes when the source representation and uncertainties are stated.

9. DISCUSSION

9.1. Validation Practice, not Model Ranking
The framework shifts emphasis from model comparison to validation practice. Its purpose is not to
rank tools in general but to define what is predicted, what is observed, what assumptions connect
them, and which uncertainty components limit the comparison. The measurand is not simply an
infrasound arrival but a defined validation observable, such as arrival time, back azimuth, detection
status, transmission loss, or an arrival-window product, for a specified source–atmosphere–station
configuration.
Earlier benchmark-style comparisons, including those discussed by Waxler and Assink [61], show how
propagation methods can be contrasted under specified atmospheric assumptions and why common
inputs are useful. The framework here addresses a broader validation question: what information
should be preserved before a model-output difference can be read as a monitoring-relevant success,
limitation, or failure. The answer requires traceable observations, compatible output classes,
predefined scoring rules, and explicit station-level outcomes in addition to physically credible
calculations.

9.2. Limitations
Several limitations cannot be solved by framework design alone. Some sources remain poorly
constrained in origin time, altitude, trajectory, radiation pattern, or strength; some atmospheric states
are under-observed, especially in the middle and upper atmosphere, where unresolved gravity waves,
tides, turbulence, and fine-scale shear can scatter energy into nominal shadow zones or alter
transmission loss in ways deterministic products miss; and some station records carry high noise,
incomplete calibration, missing provenance, or ambiguous association. The framework can expose
these limitations and prevent them from being misclassified as model failure, but it cannot remove the
underlying information deficit.
Balloon-borne sensors can sample signals within the propagation channel rather than only at the
ground [e.g., 30, 131]. They are not treated here as a benchmark class because platform motion, altitude
history, wind advection, sensor response, sparse geometry, and platform-specific processing require
additional conventions.

9.3. Detection-Status Validation
Detection-status validation requires particular care because a predicted path is not a predicted
detection. A benchmark should define the detection criterion for each station class, including the
analysis band, search window, station-noise estimate, signal-to-noise or coherence threshold, false-
alarm tolerance, and quality control. For arrays, thresholds may be expressed through coherent-
processing products such as Progressive Multi-Channel Correlation (PMCC) family duration,
correlation or consistency measures, slowness-grid constraints, back-azimuth stability, and apparent-
velocity bounds [e.g., 52, 132, 133, 134]; for single sensors, through band-limited amplitude, envelope
duration, spectral content, and local noise.

9.4. Implementation and Infrastructure
Computational feasibility constrains implementation. Ray methods suit screening, source-altitude
sensitivity, and large ensembles; normal-mode and parabolic-equation methods provide frequency-
domain and finite-frequency information for suitable paths; and full-wave methods are best reserved

 33

for selected reference cases. A mature program therefore needs tiered participation rather than
requiring every participant to run every method.
Some propagation tools are open source, while others are restricted or embedded in operational
workflows. Reproducible validation therefore should specify benchmark inputs, output schemas,
model versions, configuration files, and post-processing rules rather than mandating one software
stack.
Implementation experience from numerical propagation, detection-capability estimation, and open
modeling tools supports this staged input-output approach, because different solvers and source
representations target different observables while still benefiting from shared inputs and reporting
conventions.

9.5. Relationship to Adjacent Validation Cultures
Implementation will depend on community incentives and data access. Datasets carry different access
conditions, from openly available to institutionally, operationally, or treaty-constrained, and the
framework should accommodate a range of arrangements without prescribing one distribution model.
What matters is that the metadata, input specifications, and output products of each case are described
in enough detail that results can be interpreted, compared, and, where access permits, independently
reproduced.
Community practice should apply Section 8 to well-documented events, freezing the benchmark-
purpose statement and baseline package before outputs are interpreted. Benchmark libraries should
be organized by event class and validation target, preserve the full station-level outcome pattern, and
report results by observable rather than as universal model scores.
The framework accommodates any receiver platform if station metadata, detection criteria, and
uncertainty estimates are documented; balloon-specific conventions for receiver altitude, platform
motion, and altitude-dependent noise remain future work.
The framework is offered as a starting point for collective practice, not as a finished benchmark or a
claim of community consensus. Its immediate value is to give prospective collaborators a common
language for selecting events, freezing inputs, documenting station outcomes, and comparing results
before larger exercises are attempted. A practical first step is small: assemble one well-documented
event as a Minimum Viable Benchmark and report it using the conventions defined here. As cases
accumulate, informal propagation comparison can evolve into reproducible, community-informed
validation for CTBT-supporting IMS applications and broader atmospheric monitoring.

10. ACTIONABLE RECCOMENDATIONS
The benchmarking framework is intended to be adopted progressively. The most useful near-term
step is the Minimum Viable Benchmark in Section 8: one group, one well-documented event, and the
propagation tool already in use. This entry point is especially relevant to CTBT-supporting IMS
applications, where the value of a prediction depends on traceable source, station, atmospheric,
processing, and uncertainty assumptions.
First, each study should state the validation target explicitly and limit claims to that target. Agreement
in arrival time should not be presented as validation of transmission loss, amplitude, detectability, or
phase-family interpretation unless those quantities were defined and evaluated.
Second, controlled cases should freeze the source representation, station set, atmospheric input,
coordinate conventions, output schema, and matching rules before model results are interpreted. For
atmospheric-entry and re-entry events this includes the geometric discretization of the source defined
in Section 4 and illustrated in Figure 3. Alternative source geometries are scientifically valuable but
should be treated as sensitivity tests and reported separately from the controlled baseline.
Third, reports should preserve the full station-level outcome pattern, keeping the eight categories
defined in Section 6 separate rather than collapsing them to a single detection flag. This distinction is
essential for network-level interpretation because a missing waveform, a noisy record, and a physically
meaningful non-detection do not carry the same evidentiary value.
Fourth, network summaries should guard against spatial sampling bias by reporting both unweighted
station-level results and the spatially normalized summary defined in Section 7, so that an
overrepresented station cluster cannot mask failures in sparsely sampled sectors.
Fifth, celerity calculations should use the standardized WGS84 ellipsoid geodesic distance convention
defined in Section 7, applied identically to observed and predicted celerity, with any alternative
distance measure reported only as an auxiliary diagnostic.
Finally, benchmark packages should be versioned and reusable. At minimum, the archive should
include Table 7 reporting fields, scoring rules, and version information; expanded packages can add
range-dependent atmospheres, perturbation ensembles, source-sensitivity tests, transmission-loss
products, and semi-blind exercises.
By treating the source, atmosphere, observational products, and model output as documented and
separable elements rather than as a single undifferentiated comparison, the framework allows a
disagreement between prediction and observation to be diagnosed rather than merely scored. It can
be satisfied at the scale of a single well-documented event and extended toward coordinated
intercomparison without altering its conventions, so adoption can proceed incrementally as resources
allow. Establishing these shared conventions is the precondition for moving infrasound propagation
validation from study-specific practice toward assessment that is reproducible and comparable across
the field.

11. CONCLUSIONS
Infrasound propagation predictions are most useful when their assumptions, inputs, outputs, and
uncertainties are reported in a way that allows others to interpret and reuse them. This report presents
a validation framework for traceable, event-class-aware, uncertainty-aware benchmarking of
infrasound propagation predictions used in atmospheric monitoring, with immediate relevance to
International Monitoring System infrasound interpretation in support of the Comprehensive Nuclear-
Test-Ban Treaty verification regime.
Three takeaways follow. First, a benchmark must define its validation observable and monitoring use
before model outputs are interpreted. Second, source representation, atmospheric specification,
observational products, and comparison metrics must remain separable so that residuals can be
diagnosed rather than merely scored. Third, conclusions should be expressed as conditional
monitoring guidance: robust for a stated event class and atmospheric-input level, conditional on
specified source or atmospheric assumptions, or unsupported by the available observations.
By fixing what must be specified before a model is run, separating observation from interpretation,
and reporting results by observable and atmospheric-input level rather than as a single score, the
framework gives the community a shared basis for reproducible validation. Its adoption is incremental:
a single well-documented event can satisfy the minimum standard, and larger coordinated exercises
can extend the same conventions without redefining them. The framework is therefore usable at
whatever scale a study can support and citable on its own as a reference structure for making future
benchmarks interpretable.
The framework is offered as a starting point for collective work, not as a finished benchmark or a
claim of community consensus. It gives prospective collaborators a common language for selecting
events, sharing inputs, documenting station outcomes, and comparing results before committing to a
coordinated exercise. A practical first step is small and concrete: assemble a single well-documented
event as a Minimum Viable Benchmark and report it using the conventions defined here. As
documented events accumulate around common conventions, informal propagation comparison can
give way to reproducible, community-informed validation.
