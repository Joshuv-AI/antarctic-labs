A quadratic Grassmann manifold optimization problem arising from quantum embedding methods
Thomas Ayral, Eric Cancès, Fabian M. Faulstich, Lin Lin, Alicia Negre

Abstract
This article presents a mathematical analysis and numerical strategies for solving the optimization problem of minimizing the quadratic function J ⁡ ( P ) = Tr ​ ( B ​ P ) − 1 2 ​ Tr ​ ( A ​ P ​ A ​ P ) J(P)=\text{Tr}(BP)-\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}(APAP) , where A , B ∈ ℝ sym M × M A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym} , with A ⪰ 0 A\succeq 0 , over the Grassmann manifold Gr ⁡ ( m , ℝ M ) {\rm Gr}(m,\mathbb{R}^{M}) . While this problem is non-convex and typically admits non-global local minima - posing challenges for Riemannian optimization and self-consistent field (SCF) algorithms - we identify cases where the global minimizer can be obtained by solving an auxiliary convex problem. When this approach is not directly applicable, the solution to the auxiliary problem still serves as an effective initialization for Riemannian optimization methods and SCF algorithms, significantly improving their performance. This work is motivated by applications in quantum embedding methods, particularly in the construction of bath orbitals, where such optimization problems naturally arise.

Introduction

This article is concerned with the mathematical analysis and numerical treatment of the quadratic Grassmann optimization problem

\min_{P\in\mathcal{M}}J(P),

(1)

where
\mathcal{M}
is the real Grassman manifold

\mathcal{M}:={\rm Gr}(m,\mathbb{R}^{M}):=\left\{P\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}\;|\;P^{2}=P,\;{\rm Tr}(P)=m\right\},

(2)

(the set of rank-

(3)

where

This quadratic Grassmann optimization problem arises naturally in embedding methods used in quantum chemistry and physics. In
[

7

2
Mathematical analysis

In this analysis, the Grassmann manifold
\mathcal{M}
is viewed as a Riemannian submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}
endowed with the metric induced by the Frobenius inner product
\langle Y,X\rangle:={\rm Tr}(X^{T}Y)
.

2.1
First and second order optimization conditions

For completeness and the reader’s convenience, this section collects several known results.
The tangent space to
\mathcal{M}
at
P\in\mathcal{M}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\mathrm{sym}}\,:\,XP+PX=X\}

\displaystyle=\{X=[\Omega,P]:\Omega^{T}=-\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm antisym}\}

\displaystyle=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\mathrm{sym}}\,:\,PXP=(1-P)X(1-P)=0\}.

(7)

The orthohonal projection of a matrix
X
in
\mathbb{R}_{\rm sym}^{M\times M}
onto
\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathcal{M}
has an explicit expression:

\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathcal{M}}(X)=[[X,P],P],\qquad\mbox{ for all }P\in\mathcal{M},\;X\in\mathbb{R}_{\rm sym}^{M\times M}.

(8)

Let
J:\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}\to\mathbb{R}
be the cost function introduced in (
3
) and

G(P):=\nabla J(P)=B-APA

(9)

the gradient of
J
at point
P\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}
. The Riemannian gradient
{\rm grad}_{\mathcal{M}}J(P)
of the restriction of
J
to
\mathcal{M}
at some point
P\in\mathcal{M}
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm grad}_{\mathcal{M}}J(P)=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathcal{M}}(\nabla J(P))=[[G(P),P],P].

(10)

Equation (
10
) provides a natural extension

\mathcal{G}:\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}\ni P\mapsto\mathcal{G}(P):=[[G(P),P],P]\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}

of the vector field
{\rm grad}_{\mathcal{M}}J(P)
, originally defined on
\mathcal{M}
, to the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}
.
This allows us to easily compute the Riemannian Hessian of
J
at
P
as

\displaystyle\forall X\in\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathcal{M},\quad\mbox{Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}J(P)[X]=\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathcal{M}}\big(D\mathcal{G}(P)[X]\big),

(11)

where
D\mathcal{G}(P)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym})
is the derivative of
\mathcal{G}
at
P\in\mathcal{M}
.
Since
J
is quadratic, its Hessian is independent of
P
, and explicitly given by

\forall P\in\mathcal{M},\quad\forall X\in T_{P}\mathcal{M},\quad D^{2}J(P)[X]=-AXA.

Combining this with (
8
) this yields

\displaystyle D\mathcal{G}(P)

\displaystyle=[D^{2}J(P)[X],P],P]+[[G(P),X],P]+[[G(P),P],X]].

(12)

Note that if

P=U\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{m}&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)U^{T}\in\mathcal{M}\quad\mbox{and}\quad X=U\begin{pmatrix}X_{\rm oo}&X_{\rm ov}\\
X_{\rm vo}&X_{\rm vv}\end{pmatrix}U^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M},

then

\displaystyle[X,P]=U\begin{pmatrix}0&-X_{\rm ov}\\
X_{\rm vo}&0\end{pmatrix}U^{T}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\Pi_{T_{P}\mathcal{M}}X=[[X,P],P]=U\begin{pmatrix}0&X_{\rm ov}\\
X_{\rm vo}&0\end{pmatrix}U^{T}.

(13)

Moreover,
X\in T_{P}\mathcal{M}
iff
X_{\rm oo}=0
and
X_{\rm vv}=0
.
The subscripts
\rm o
and
\rm v
stand for “virtual” and “occupied”. In mean-field quantum chemistry models such as Hartree–Fock and Kohn–Sham, the matrix
P
represents the one-body density matrix of the quantum state, and decomposing
\mathbb{R}^{M}
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{M}=\mathrm{Ran}(P)\oplus\ker(P)

the occupied orbitals are the elements in
\mathrm{Ran}(P)
and the virtual orbitals the elements in
{\rm Ker}(P)
.

We deduce from (
11
)–(
13
) that

\displaystyle\forall X\in\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathcal{M},\quad\mbox{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}J(P)[X]

\displaystyle=[[D^{2}J(P)[X],P],P]+[[G(P),X],P]+[[[[G(P),P],X]],P],P]

\displaystyle=-[[AXA,P],P]+[[G(P),X],P].

Following the notation from
[
10
]
, we finally obtain

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}J(P)

\displaystyle=\Omega(P)+K(P),

(14)

with

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Omega(P):\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathcal{M}&\to&\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathcal{M}\\
X&\mapsto&[[G(P),X],P]\end{array}\qquad\begin{array}[]{rcl}K(P):\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathcal{M}&\to&\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathcal{M}\\
X&\mapsto&-[[AXA,P],P]\end{array}.

(15)

The term
K(P)\equiv\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathcal{M}}(D^{2}J(P))\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathcal{M}}
is simply the projection onto the tangent space of the Hessian of the function
J
, while the term
\Omega(P)
originates from the curvature of the Grassmann manifold
\mathcal{M}
.

Proposition 1

(Optimality conditions)
.

Let

P_{\star}\in\mathcal{M}
and
U\in O(M)
such that

\displaystyle P_{\star}=U\begin{pmatrix}I_{m}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}U^{T},\qquad A=U\begin{pmatrix}A_{\rm oo}&A_{\rm ov}\\
A_{\rm vo}&A_{\rm vv}\end{pmatrix}U^{T},\qquad B=U\begin{pmatrix}B_{\rm oo}&B_{\rm ov}\\
B_{\rm vo}&B_{\rm vv}\end{pmatrix}U^{T}.

(16)

•

First-order condition:
P_{\star}
is a critical point of
J
on
\mathcal{M}
if and only if

\displaystyle[G(P_{\star}),P_{\star}]=[B-AP_{\star}A,P_{\star}]=0\quad\mbox{or equivalently}\quad B_{\rm ov}=A_{\rm oo}A_{\rm ov}.

(17)

•

Second-order condition: if
P_{\star}
satisfies (
17
), a necessary condition for
P_{\star}
being a local minimizer of
J
on
\mathcal{M}
is

\displaystyle\forall X=U\begin{pmatrix}0&X_{\rm ov}\\
X_{\rm vo}&0\end{pmatrix}U^{T}\in\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathcal{M},\quad\langle X,\mbox{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}J(P_{*})[X]\rangle\geq 0,

(18)

or equivalently

{\rm Tr}\left(G_{\rm vv}(X_{\rm ov}^{T}X_{\rm ov})\right)-{\rm Tr}\left(G_{\rm oo}(X_{\rm ov}X_{\rm ov}^{T})\right)-{\rm Tr}\left(X_{\rm ov}A_{\rm vo}X_{\rm ov}A_{\rm vo}+A_{\rm oo}X_{\rm ov}A_{\rm vv}X_{\rm ov}^{T}\right)\geq 0,

with
G_{\rm oo}=B_{\rm oo}-A_{\rm oo}^{2}
and
G_{\rm vv}=B_{\rm vv}-A_{\rm vo}A_{\rm ov}
.

Proof.

The first assertion is a direct consequence of (
9
), (
10
) and (
13
), and of the fact that

\displaystyle G(P_{\star})=\begin{pmatrix}G_{\rm oo}&G_{\rm ov}\\
G_{\rm vo}&G_{\rm vv}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}B_{\rm oo}-A_{\rm oo}^{2}&B_{\rm ov}-A_{\rm oo}A_{\rm ov}\\
B_{\rm vo}-A_{\rm vo}A_{\rm oo}&B_{\rm vv}-A_{\rm vo}A_{\rm ov}\end{pmatrix}.

From (
16
), we obtain

AXA=U\begin{pmatrix}*&A_{\rm ov}X_{\rm vo}A_{\rm ov}+A_{\rm oo}X_{\rm ov}A_{\rm vv}\\
A_{\rm vo}X_{\rm ov}A_{\rm vo}+A_{\rm vv}X_{\rm vo}A_{\rm oo}&*\end{pmatrix}U^{T}.

and thus

\displaystyle K(P_{\star})[X]=U\begin{pmatrix}0&-(A_{\rm ov}X_{\rm vo}A_{\rm ov}+A_{\rm oo}X_{\rm ov}A_{\rm vv})\\
-(A_{\rm vo}X_{\rm ov}A_{\rm vo}+A_{\rm vv}X_{\rm vo}A_{\rm oo})&0\end{pmatrix}U^{T}.

On the other hand, using the fact that
[G(P_{*}),P_{*}]=0
is equivalent to
G_{\rm ov}=G_{\rm vo}^{T}=0
, we get

\displaystyle\Omega(P_{\star})[X]=U\begin{pmatrix}0&X_{\rm ov}G_{\rm vv}-G_{\rm oo}X_{\rm ov}\\
G_{\rm vv}X_{\rm vo}-X_{\rm vo}G_{\rm oo}&0\end{pmatrix}U^{T}.

This yields

\displaystyle\langle X,\mbox{\rm Hess}_{\mathcal{M}}J(P_{\star})[X]\rangle

\displaystyle=2{\rm Tr}\left(X_{\rm ov}^{T}(X_{\rm ov}G_{\rm vv}-G_{\rm oo}X_{\rm ov})\right)-2{\rm Tr}\left(X_{\rm ov}^{T}(A_{\rm ov}X_{\rm vo}A_{\rm ov}+A_{\rm oo}X_{\rm ov}A_{\rm vv})\right).

Rearranging the terms in the traces, we obtain the expected result.
∎

2.2
Aufbau principle

The following property turns out to be very useful to design fixed-point iterative algorithms to solve the Euler-Lagrange equations associated to the constrained optimization problem (
1
)–(
3
) .

Theorem 2
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}
be a positive semidefinite matrix,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}
, and
P_{\star}
a global minimizer of (
1
) with
J
given by (
3
). Then, there exists an orthogonal basis
(x_{k})_{1\leq k\leq M}
of
\mathbb{R}^{M}
diagonalizing both
P_{\star}
and
G(P_{\star})
such that

\displaystyle P_{\star}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}x_{k}^{T},\quad G(P_{\star})=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\lambda_{k}x_{k}x_{k}^{T},\quad x_{k}^{T}x_{l}=\delta_{kl},\quad\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{M}.

(19)

In addition, if
A
is invertible, then

\lambda_{m}<\lambda_{m+1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{\star}=\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,\mu]}(G(P_{\star}))\quad\mbox{for any }\mu\in[\lambda_{m},\lambda_{m+1}).

(20)

In words, the above results mean that
P_{\star}
is the orthogonal projector on an
m
-dimensional vector subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{M}
spanned by eigenvectors of
G(P_{\star})
associated with the lowest
m
eigenvalues. A similar property is satisfied by the (unrestricted) Hartree–Fock problem in quantum chemistry (see e.g.
[
38
]
, or
[
3
]
for a mathematical presentation). In that case, the Grassmann manifold is the set of admissible Hartree–Fock density matrices and the cost function is the Hartree–Fock energy functional. That function is also the sum of a linear term and a quadratic term, the latter being itself the sum of two terms: the Hartree term and the exchange term. The analogue of (
19
) is referred to as the
Aufbau
principle in the quantum chemistry literature, while the analogue of (
20
) is called the
no-unfilled shell property

[
18
]
. In Hartree–Fock theory, these two properties originate from the fact that the exchange term perfectly compensates the excess of Hartree energy due to self-interaction
[
38
]
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We infer from (
1
) that
G(P_{\star})
and
P_{\star}
commute. They are therefore simultaneously diagonalizable, i.e.,

P_{\star}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}x_{k}^{T},\quad G(P_{\star})=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mu_{k}x_{k}x_{k}^{T},\quad x_{k}^{T}x_{l}=\delta_{k,l},~~~1\leq k,l\leq M.

(21)

Let
m<k\leq M
and

P:=\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}x_{i}x_{i}^{T}+x_{k}x_{k}^{T}=P_{\star}+x_{k}x_{k}^{T}-x_{m}x_{m}^{T}.

(22)

Clearly,
P\in\mathcal{M}
and

\begin{split}J(P)-J(P_{\star})&={\rm Tr}\left(G(P_{\star})(x_{k}x_{k}^{T}-x_{m}x_{m}^{T})\right)-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\left(A(x_{k}x_{k}^{T}-x_{m}x_{m}^{T})A(x_{k}x_{k}^{T}-x_{m}x_{m}^{T})\right)\\
&=(\mu_{k}-\mu_{m})-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\left(A^{\frac{1}{2}}(x_{k}x_{k}^{T}-x_{m}x_{m}^{T})A^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{\frac{1}{2}}(x_{k}x_{k}^{T}-x_{m}x_{m}^{T})A^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\\
&=(\mu_{k}-\mu_{m})-\frac{1}{2}\lVert S_{km}\rVert^{2},\end{split}

with
S_{km}:=A^{\frac{1}{2}}(x_{k}x_{k}^{T}-x_{m}x_{m}^{T})A^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}
. As
P_{\star}
is a minimizer of
J
on
\mathcal{M}
, the right-hand side is nonnegative and therefore

\mu_{k}-\mu_{m}\geq\frac{1}{2}\lVert S_{km}\rVert^{2}\geq 0.

Reordering the eigenvalues
(\mu_{j})_{1\leq j\leq M}
in non-decreasing order, we obtain (
19
). In particular,

\lambda_{m+1}-\lambda_{m}\geq\frac{1}{2}\lVert A^{\frac{1}{2}}(x_{k}x_{k}^{T}-x_{m}x_{m}^{T})A^{\frac{1}{2}}\rVert^{2}.

If
A
is invertible, then
A^{\frac{1}{2}}(x_{k}x_{k}^{T}-x_{m}x_{m}^{T})A^{\frac{1}{2}}\neq 0
and therefore
\lambda_{m+1}>\lambda_{m}
. The formula
{P_{\star}=\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,\mu]}(G(P_{\star}))}
then follows from functional calculus for Hermitian matrices.
∎

2.3
Convexification

We begin this section with the following key observation.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\widetilde{J}:\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}\to\mathbb{R}
be the quadratic function defined by

\widetilde{J}(D):={\rm Tr}(CD)+\frac{1}{4}\|[A,D]\|^{2}\quad\mbox{with}\quad C:=B-\frac{1}{2}A^{2}.

(23)

Then, for all
P\in\mathcal{M}
,
\widetilde{J}(P)=J(P)
. In particular,
J
and
\widetilde{J}
have the same critical points on
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

Let
P\in\mathcal{M}
. We note that

\displaystyle\|[A,P]\|^{2}

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}([A,P]^{T}[A,P])=-{\rm Tr}([A,P]^{2})=-2{\rm Tr}(APAP)+2{\rm Tr}(A^{2}P^{2}),

and since
P^{2}=P
, we have

{J(P)={\rm Tr}(BP)-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(APAP)={\rm Tr}(CP)+\frac{1}{4}\|[A,P]\|^{2}=\widetilde{J}(P)}
.
∎

We note that the function
\widetilde{J}
is convex, which motivates considering the relaxed problem of minimizing
\widetilde{J}
over the convex hull of
\mathcal{M}

\mathcal{K}:={\rm CH}(\mathcal{M})=\{D\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}\;|\;0\preceq D\preceq I_{M},\,{\rm Tr}(D)=m\}.

Theorem 4
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}
be a positive semidefinite matrix, and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}
.
The set
\mathcal{D}_{\star}
of the (global) minimizers of the convex optimization problem

\widetilde{I}=\min_{D\in\mathcal{K}}\widetilde{J}(D)

(24)

is a non-empty, compact, convex subset of
\mathcal{K}
, and we have the following properties:

1.

The function
\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}\ni D\mapsto\nabla\widetilde{J}(D)=C-\frac{1}{2}[[A,D],A]\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}
takes a constant value
H_{\star}
on
\mathcal{D}_{\star}
.

2.

Let
\mu_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{M}
be the eigenvalues of
H_{\star}
, counted with their multiplicities and ranked in non-decreasing order. Moreover, define

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\star}:=\left\{D\in\mathcal{K}\;|\;\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,\mu_{m})}(H_{\star})\preceq D\preceq 1_{(-\infty,\mu_{m}]}(H_{\star})\right\}.

Then

{\mathcal{D}}_{\star}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\star},

(25)

and

\displaystyle\forall D_{\star}\in\mathcal{D}_{\star},\quad\forall\widetilde{D}_{\star}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\star},\quad\widetilde{D}_{\star}\in\mathcal{D}_{\star}\Leftrightarrow\;[A,D_{\star}-\widetilde{D}_{\star}]=0\;\Rightarrow\;{\rm Ran}(D_{*}-\widetilde{D}_{*})\subset\mathcal{V},

(26)

where
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the largest
A
-invariant vector subspace of
{\rm Ker}(H_{\star}-\mu_{m})
.

3.

If
\mu_{m}<\mu_{m+1}
, then

P_{\star}:=\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,\mu_{m}]}(H_{\star})

(27)

is the unique minimizer of (
24
), i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{\star}=\{P_{\star}\}
, and
P_{\star}
is also the unique global minimizer of the original problem (
1
)–(
3
).

Proof.

The function
\widetilde{J}
is convex on
\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}
and
\mathcal{K}
is a compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}
. The set
\mathcal{D}_{\star}
of minimizers of problem (
24
) therefore is a non-empty compact convex subset of
\mathcal{K}
. Let
D_{\star}\in\mathcal{D}_{\star}
be a (global) minimizer of (
24
). Using convexity, one has, for any
D\in\mathcal{K}
,

\forall\theta\in[0,1],\quad\widetilde{J}((1-\theta)D_{\star}+\theta D)\geq\widetilde{J}(D_{\star}).

(28)

For small
\theta
,
\theta\geq 0
, one can expand (
28
) to get

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}((1-\theta)D_{\star}+\theta D)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{J}(D_{\star}+\theta(D-D_{\star}))=\widetilde{J}(D_{\star})+\theta\langle\nabla\widetilde{J}(D_{\star}),D-D_{\star}\rangle+o(\theta),

yielding the Euler inequality

\forall D\in\mathcal{K},\quad{\rm Tr}(H_{\star}D)\geq{\rm Tr}(H_{\star}D_{\star}),\quad\mbox{with}\quad H_{\star}:=\nabla\widetilde{J}(D_{\star})=C-\frac{1}{2}[[A,D_{\star}],A].

(29)

Denoting by
\mu_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{M}
the eigenvalues of
H_{\star}
, we have

\min_{D\in\mathcal{K}}{\rm Tr}(H_{\star}D)=\min_{D\in\mathcal{M}}{\rm Tr}(H_{\star}D)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mu_{k},

and

D_{\star}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\star}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{D\in\mathcal{K}}{\rm Tr}(H_{\star}D)=\left\{D\in\mathcal{K}\;|\;\mathds{1}_{(-\infty,\mu_{m})}(H_{\star})\preceq D\preceq 1_{(-\infty,\mu_{m}]}(H_{\star})\right\}.

Let
\widetilde{D}_{\star}
be another minimizer of (
24

\displaystyle\forall\theta\in[0,1],\quad\widetilde{I}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{J}((1-\theta)D_{\star}+\theta\widetilde{D}_{\star})=\widetilde{J}(D_{\star}+\theta(\widetilde{D}_{\star}-D_{\star}))

\displaystyle=\widetilde{I}+\theta{\rm Tr}(H_{\star}(\widetilde{D}_{\star}-D_{\star}))+\frac{\theta^{2}}{4}\lVert[A,\widetilde{D}_{\star}-D_{\star}]\rVert^{2}.

(30)

This implies
{\rm Tr}(H_{\star}(\widetilde{D}_{\star}-D_{\star}))=0
which yields
\widetilde{D}_{*}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\star}
and therewith (
25
). Moreover, (
30
) implies
[A,\widetilde{D}_{\star}-D_{\star}]=0
, which yields

\nabla\widetilde{J}(\widetilde{D}_{*})=C-\frac{1}{2}[[A,\widetilde{D}_{\star}],A]=C-\frac{1}{2}[[A,D_{\star}],A]=H_{\star},

proving the first assertion of the theorem.

Let
D_{\star}\in\mathcal{D}_{\star}
and
\widetilde{D}_{\star}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\star}
. As
{\rm Tr}(H_{\star}D_{\star})={\rm Tr}(H_{\star}\widetilde{D}_{\star})
, we deduce from (
30
) that
\widetilde{D}_{\star}\in\mathcal{D}_{\star}
if and only if
[A,D_{\star}-\widetilde{D}_{\star}]=0
. This proves the equivalence in (
26
). To prove the implication, we observe that
\widetilde{D}_{\star}
is in
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\star}
if and only if
{\rm Ran}(\widetilde{D}_{\star}-D_{\star})\subset{\rm Ker}(H_{\star}-\mu_{m})
and
[A,\widetilde{D}_{\star}-D_{\star}]=0
, that is if and only if there exists a family
(u_{1},\cdots,u_{d})
of vectors in
{\rm Ker}(H_{\star}-\mu_{m})
such that

\widetilde{D}_{\star}-D_{\star}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}n_{j}u_{j}u_{j}^{T}

with

d\leq{\rm dim}({\rm Ker}(H_{\star}-\mu_{m})),\quad n_{j}\in[-1,1]\setminus\{0\},\quad\sum_{j=1}^{d}n_{j}=0,\quad Au_{j}=\alpha_{j}u_{j},\quad u_{j}^{T}u_{k}=\delta_{jk}.

This condition implies that
{\rm Ran}(D_{*}-\widetilde{D}_{*})=\mbox{Span}(u_{1},\cdots,u_{d})\subset\mathcal{V}
, where
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the largest
A

{\rm Ker}(H_{\star}-\mu_{m})
. This completes the proof of the second assertion.

The third assertion readily follows from (
25

\mu_{m}<\mu_{m+1}

is the singleton
\{1_{(-\infty,\mu_{m}](H_{\star})}\}

3
.

Proposition 6

Let

(31)

In particular, if

)–(

Proof.

Problem (
1
)–(
3

O(M)

P_{*}
is a minimizer of
J
on
\mathcal{M}
and

Q_{*}:=UP_{*}U^{T}
and is a minimizer of
J_{U}
on
\mathcal{M}

J_{U}(Q):=\text{Tr}((UBU^{T})Q)-\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}((UAU^{T})Q(UAU^{T})Q).

Since
A
and
B
commute,
there exists
U\in O(M)
such that

UAU^{T}=\text{diag}(a_{1},\dots,a_{M}),\quad UBU^{T}=\text{diag}(b_{1},\dots,b_{M}),

and thus

UCU^{T}=\text{diag}(c_{1},\dots,c_{M})\quad\mbox{with}\quad c_{j}=b_{j}-\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{2}.

After a suitable reordering of the columns of

\displaystyle J_{U}(Q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}b_{i}Q_{ii}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}a_{i}a_{j}Q_{ij}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}b_{i}Q_{ii}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}((a_{i}-a_{j})^{2}-a_{i}^{2}-a_{j}^{2})Q_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}b_{i}Q_{ii}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{M}Q_{ij}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}(a_{i}-a_{j})^{2}Q_{ij}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}Q_{ii}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}(a_{i}-a_{j})^{2}Q_{ij}^{2}.

Consequently, the cost function

reaches a minimum if

, and

Since

, the minimizers
Q_{*}
of

Q_{\star}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}I_{k}&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)+\Delta_{\star}\quad\mbox{with}\quad\Delta_{\star}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&0\\
0&\delta&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right)

(32)

where

\quad k:=\max\{j\;:\;c_{j}<c_{m}\},\quad\delta^{2}=\delta=\delta^{*},\quad{\rm Tr}(\delta)=m-k,\quad[\delta,\text{diag}(a_{k+1},\dots,a_{k+p})]=0,

Multiplying

by

U
on the right, we obtain (
31

Finally, if

and therefore

. The matrix

[1]
. After multiplying

by

We now investigate the case when

\epsilon=0

\displaystyle A_{0}=U_{0}\text{diag}(a_{1}^{0},\dots,a_{M}^{0})U_{0}^{T},\quad B_{0}=U_{0}\text{diag}(b_{1}^{0},\dots,b_{M}^{0})U_{0}^{T},

Expanding

Proposition 7

(34)

with

Proof.

3.3
DMET bath-construction problem

Density Matrix Embedding Theory (DMET), introduced by Knizia and Chan, is a static quantum embedding method based on the idea that the entanglement between a local fragment and its environment is effectively low rank. This observation leads to a singular value decomposition of a reference state, producing a minimal bath that effectively captures the fragment-environment interaction and reduces the full many-body problem to a self-consistent finite-dimensional embedding
[
18
,
19
,
8
,
20
,
40
]
.
Several works have modified the original DMET fixed-point formulation, reformulating or replacing the correlation-potential optimization to improve numerical robustness and expose the structure of the embedding map
[
41
,
42
,
23
,
15
]
. These variants define alternative nonlinear projection maps on reduced density matrices whose stability, convexity properties, and convergence behavior differ in essential ways from the original scheme.
The DMET bath construction can be viewed not merely as an SVD-based compression, but as a structured unitary transformation acting on the one-particle Hilbert space
[
25
]
. In particular, Householder and Block-Householder transformations recast bath generation as a parametrized unitary rotation, enabling ensemble or multi-state embeddings and allowing additional optimality or physically motivated constraints to be imposed on the bath orbitals
[
33
,
43
,
32
,
24
,
14
]
.
Beyond the standard one-body DMET matching principle, several extensions enrich the information content of the embedding to better capture fragment-environment correlations. Representative directions include incorporating higher-order fluctuations and screening physics
[
5
,
16
,
17
,
34
,
31
,
26
]
and introducing correlated bath wavefunctions
[
39
]
.The purpose of this section is to explain how the bath-construction problem encountered in the extension of DMET introduced in
[
25
]
reduces to problem (
1
)–(
3
).

Consider an
N
-body fermionic quantum system with associated one-particle state space
\mathcal{H}
. For simplicity, we assume that
\mathcal{H}
is of finite-dimension
L
. This is the case in practice in quantum chemistry since the electronic Hamiltonian is discretized in a finite basis of atomic orbitals (or other kinds of basis functions), as well as for lattice models with finite numbers of sites. We also assume that the system is non-magnetic and non-relativistic, which allows us to work with a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. The most common problem in quantum chemistry is to compute the properties of the ground state of the electronic Hamiltonian
H_{N}
of the system under study. In mathematical terms, this amounts to computing quantities of interest (e.g., electronic density, interatomic forces, etc.) derived from the normalized eigenfunction
\Psi_{N}^{0}
of
H_{N}
associated with its lowest eigenvalue
E_{N}^{0}
, referred to as the ground-state wave function and ground-state energy, respectively.
The major difficulty is that
H_{N}
is a self-adjoint operator acting on the fermionic
N
-particle state space
\mathcal{H}_{N}:=\bigwedge^{N}\mathcal{H}
, the antisymmetrized tensor product of
N
copies of
\mathcal{H}
. Since
{\rm dim}(\mathcal{H}_{N})={L\choose N}
, computing the
N
-electron ground-state energy and wave function amounts to finding the lowest eigenpair of an
{L\choose N}\times{L\choose N}
real symmetric matrix. The electronic problem therefore suffers from the curse of dimensionality, making it numerically intractable for large values of
L
and
N
.

DMET is an iterative method for computing approximations of some entries of the ground-state one-particle reduced density matrix (1-RDM), a key quantity in quantum physics. DMET can be interpreted as a domain decomposition method for the quantum many-body problem (see
[
9
,
25
]
for more details). Its works as follows. First, the system is partitioned into
N_{\rm f}
non-overlapping fragments on the basis of physical arguments, leading to an orthogonal decomposition of
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{{\rm frag},1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{H}_{{\rm frag},N_{\rm f}},\quad{\rm dim}(\mathcal{H}_{{\rm frag},x})=\ell_{x},\quad\sum_{x=1}^{N_{\rm f}}\ell_{x}=L.

(40)

At each DMET iteration, the state of the whole system is represented by a coarse descriptor. In conventional DMET, this coarse descriptor is a Slater-type 1-RDM, i.e. a rank-
N
orthogonal projector corresponding to a unique
N
-body state represented in
\mathcal{H}_{N}
by a Slater determinant. The set of such 1-RDMs is thus

\displaystyle{\Gamma}_{\rm Slater}:

\displaystyle=\{\gamma\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{H})\;|\;\gamma^{2}=\gamma,\;{\rm Tr}(\gamma)=N\},

where
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{H})
is the space of symmetric operators on
\mathcal{H}
. In the extensions of DMET introduced in
[
25
]
, the set of coarse descriptors is the larger set

{\Gamma}_{\rm MS}:=\{\gamma\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathcal{H})\;|\;0\preceq\gamma\preceq 1,\;{\rm Tr}(\gamma)=N\}

of all
N
-representable mixed-state 1-RDMs. The condition
0\preceq\gamma\preceq 1
corresponds to the Pauli principle
[
27
]
. From a geometrical point of view,
{\Gamma}_{\rm Slater}={\rm Gr}(N,\mathcal{H})
, where, for any real vector space
\mathcal{X}
and integer
1\leq n\leq{\rm dim}(\mathcal{X})-1
,

{\rm Gr}(n,\mathcal{X}):=\left\{M\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{X})\;|\;M^{2}=M,\;{\rm Tr}(M)=n\right\}

is the real Grassmann manifold of the rank-
n
orthogonal projectors in
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{X})
, and
{\Gamma}_{\rm MS}
is the convex hull of
{\Gamma}_{\rm Slater}
.

At each DMET iteration, we have at hand a trial global descriptor
\gamma
. From
\gamma
, we construct for each fragment
x
, a cluster subspace
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus},x}^{\gamma}
satisfying
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm frag},x}\subset\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus},x}^{\gamma}\subset\mathcal{H}
of dimension
(\ell_{x}+m_{x})
with
1<m_{x}\ll L
, which we decompose as the orthogonal sum of the fragment subspace
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm frag},x}
and the so-called bath subspace
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm bath},x}^{\gamma}
:

\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus},x}^{\gamma}=\mathcal{H}_{{\rm frag},x}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{{\rm bath},x}^{\gamma},\quad\mbox{with}\quad\dim(\mathcal{H}_{{\rm bath},x}^{\gamma})=m_{x}.

As detailed below, our method for constructing
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus},x}^{\gamma}
is to minimize in some sense (vide infra) the entanglement between
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus},x}^{\gamma}
and its orthogonal complement
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm env},x}^{\gamma}:=(\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clust},x}^{\gamma})^{\perp}
.
The many-body Hamiltonian
H_{N}
of the whole system is then written in second quantization and projected on the Fock space generated by
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus},x}^{\gamma}
. This leads to a quantum many-body problem of dimension
2^{\ell_{x}+m_{x}}\ll{L\choose N}
, which can be solved either by exact diagonalization if
2^{\ell_{x}+m_{x}}
is not too large, or by advanced approximation methods. The solutions of the
N_{\rm f}
cluster subproblems are then recombined to form the trial global descriptor for the next iteration, and the process is iterated until convergence. This elementary DMET procedure is commonly combined with relaxation and extrapolation methods to achieve or accelerate convergence.

We now focus on the bath-construction subproblem: Given

(i)

a fragment subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\rm f}
of dimension
\ell
,

(ii)

a bath dimension
m
,

(iii)

a trial 1-RDM
\gamma
in
{\Gamma}_{\rm Slater}
or in
{\Gamma}_{\rm MS}
,

construct a bath subspace
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm bath}}^{\gamma}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
of dimension
m
that is orthogonal to
\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}
. Equivalently, construct a cluster subspace
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus}}^{\gamma}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
of dimension
(\ell+m)
containing
\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}
.

Without loss of generality, we can identify
{\mathcal{H}}
with
\mathbb{R}^{L}
and assume that
\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}=\text{span}(e_{1},\dots,e_{\ell})
, where
(e_{1},\dots e_{L})
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{L}
. Then, the orthogonal projectors
\Pi_{\rm frag}
and
\Pi_{\rm clus}^{\gamma}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}
and
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus}}^{\gamma}
, respectively, are represented by block matrices of the form

\Pi_{\rm frag}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{\ell}&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right),\quad\Pi_{\rm clus}^{\gamma}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{\ell}&0\\
0&P_{\rm clust}^{\gamma}\end{array}\right),\quad\mbox{with}\quad P_{\rm clust}^{\gamma}\in{\rm Gr}(m,\mathbb{R}^{M}),\quad M=L-\ell.

(41)

The idea is to choose
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus}}^{\gamma}
in such a way that the 1-RDM
\gamma
is as disentangled as possible with respect to the cluster-environment partition of the system. Note that if
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\rm S_{1}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\rm S_{2}}
defines a partition of the system into two subsystems
\rm S_{1}
and
\rm S_{2}
, it is not possible to determine whether a quantum state is disentangled with respect to the
\rm S_{1}
-
\rm S_{2}
partition from its 1-RDM alone. On the other hand, if a quantum state with 1-RDM
\gamma
is disentangled w.r.t. the
\rm S_{1}
-
\rm S_{2}
partition, then
\Pi_{{\rm S_{1}}}\gamma\Pi_{{\rm S_{2}}}=0
, where
\Pi_{\rm S_{1}}
and
\Pi_{\rm S_{2}}
are the orthogonal projectors onto
\mathcal{H}_{\rm S_{1}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\rm S_{2}}
, respectively. We will therefore use the quantity
\Pi_{{\rm S_{1}}}\gamma\Pi_{{\rm S_{2}}}
as a proxy of entanglement, and define
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus}}^{\gamma}
as the range of an orthogonal projector solving

\displaystyle\Pi_{\rm clus}^{\gamma}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Pi\in{\rm Gr}(\ell+m,\mathbb{R}^{L})\\
\Pi\,\Pi_{\rm frag}=\Pi_{\rm frag}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{J}^{\gamma}(\Pi),\quad\mbox{with}\quad\mathcal{J}^{\gamma}(\Pi):=\|\Pi\gamma\Pi^{\perp}\|^{2},\quad\Pi^{\perp}=I_{L}-\Pi.

(42)

Note that the condition
\Pi\,\Pi_{\rm frag}=\Pi_{\rm frag}
is equivalent to
\Pi
being of the form

\Pi=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{\ell}&0\\
0&P\end{array}\right)\quad\mbox{with}\quad P\in{\rm Gr}(m,\mathbb{R}^{M}).

(43)

Decomposing
\gamma
as

\displaystyle\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{\rm frag}&\gamma_{{\rm frag},\rm ext}\\
\gamma_{{\rm ext},\rm frag}&\gamma_{\rm ext}\end{pmatrix},\quad\gamma_{\rm frag}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times\ell}_{\rm sym},\quad\gamma_{\rm ext}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym},\quad\gamma_{{\rm frag},\rm ext}=\gamma_{{\rm ext},\rm frag}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{(L-\ell)\times\ell},

(44)

an elementary calculation shows that for any
\Pi
of the form (
43
), it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\gamma}(\Pi)

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{\ell}&0\\
0&P\end{array}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{\rm frag}&\gamma_{{\rm frag},\rm ext}\\
\gamma_{{\rm ext},\rm frag}&\gamma_{\rm ext}\end{pmatrix}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&(I_{M}-P)\end{array}\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}(\gamma_{{\rm frag},\rm ext}(1-P)\gamma_{{\rm ext},{\rm frag}})+{\rm Tr}\left(P\gamma_{\rm ext}(1-P)\gamma_{\rm ext}\right)

\displaystyle=2J(P)+\|\gamma_{{\rm ext},\rm frag}\|^{2},

with

J(P)={\rm Tr}(BP)-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(APAP),\quad A:=\gamma_{\rm ext},\quad\mbox{and}\quad B:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\gamma_{\rm ext}^{2}-\gamma_{{\rm ext},\rm frag}\gamma_{{\rm frag},\rm ext}\right).

(45)

The matrix
C
in (
23
) is then given by

C:=B-\frac{1}{2}A^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{{\rm ext},\rm frag}\gamma_{{\rm frag},\rm ext}.

(46)

Since
\gamma\succeq 0
, we have
A\succeq 0
.
Problem (
42
) is therefore equivalent to (
1
)–(
3
) with the above choices of matrices
A
and
B
.
Note that we chose the Frobenius norm to measure the magnitude of
\Pi\gamma\Pi^{\perp}
. Other choices, for instance involving the kinetic energy of the 1-RDM
\gamma
, could also be considered, leading to different Grassmann optimization problems.

In this work, full disentanglement is said to be achieved if

\mathop{\rm min}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Pi\in{\rm Gr}(\ell+m,\mathbb{R}^{L})\\
\Pi\,\Pi_{\rm frag}=\Pi_{\rm frag}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{J}^{\gamma}(\Pi)=0\quad\mbox{(full disentanglement criteria)}.

(47)

Equivalently, full disentanglement is achieved if there exists a cluster subspace
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus}}^{\gamma}
for which
\gamma
is block-diagonal in the decomposition

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus}}^{\gamma}\oplus{\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus}}^{\gamma}}^{\perp}.

In other words, full disentanglement can be achieved if we can find an
(\ell+m)
-dimensional
\gamma
-invariant subspace of
\mathcal{H}
containing
\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}
.

In conventional DMET,
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{Slater}}
. In this case, the following result holds.

Proposition 8
.

If
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{Slater}}
and
m\geq\ell
, then full disentanglement can be achieved and constructing an admissible
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus}}^{\gamma}
reduces to performing a QR decomposition of the matrix
\gamma_{\rm frag,ext}
.

Proof.

Since
\gamma^{2}=\gamma
,
\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}+\gamma\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}
is a
\gamma
-invariant subspace of
\mathcal{H}
containing
\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}
of dimension lower or equal to
2\ell
. An orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}+\gamma\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}
can be obtained by enriching the basis
(e_{1},\cdots,e_{\ell})
by Gramm–Schmidt orthonormalization of
(\gamma e_{1},\cdots,\gamma e_{\ell})
, which amounts to performing a QR decomposition of
\gamma_{\rm frag,ext}
. Any
(\ell+m)
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathcal{H}
containing
\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}+\gamma\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}
is an admissible
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus}}^{\gamma}
.
∎

Note that
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm clus}}^{\gamma}
is unique if
m=\ell
and
{\rm dim}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}+\gamma\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag})=2\ell
, a non-degeneracy condition invoked in
[
9
]
, and usually satisfied in practice in conventional DMET.

In the proposed extensions of DMET, we only assume
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{MS}}
. The following result provides a necessary and sufficient condition for full disentanglement to be achievable.

Proposition 9
.

Any
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\rm MS}
can be uniquely decomposed as

\begin{split}&\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_{i}P_{i},\quad P_{i}\in{\rm Gr}(m_{i},\mathbb{R}^{L}),\quad m_{i}\geq 1,\quad P_{i}P_{j}=\delta_{ij}P_{i},\quad 1\leq i\leq s\leq L,\\
&\quad 0<n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots<n_{s}\leq 1,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_{i}m_{i}=N.\end{split}

(48)

The linear space

\mathcal{X}:=\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}+\left(P_{1}\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}\oplus\cdots\oplus P_{s}\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}\right)

is the smallest
\gamma
-invariant subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{L}
containing
\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}
.
Full disentanglement can therefore be achieved if and only if
m\geq{\rm dim}(\mathcal{X})-\ell
.

Proof.

The decomposition (
48
) is the usual spectral decomposition of
\gamma
. Indeed, since
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\rm MS}
, its spectrum
\sigma(\gamma)
lies in the range
[0,1]
. Defining
s:=|\sigma(\gamma)\cap(0,1]|
, we have

\sigma(\gamma)\cap(0,1]=\{n_{1},\cdots,n_{s}\}\quad\mbox{with}\quad 0<n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots<n_{s}\leq 1,

and

\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_{i}P_{i}\quad\mbox{with}\quad P_{i}:={\mathds{1}}_{\{n_{i}\}}(\gamma).

Denoting by
m_{i}
the rank of
P_{i}
(i.e., the multiplicity of the eigenvalue
n_{i}
), we have

P_{i}P_{j}=\delta_{ij}P_{i},\quad 0\leq i\leq s,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_{i}m_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_{i}{\rm Tr}(P_{i})={\rm Tr}(\gamma)=N.

Since
\gamma
is real symmetric, the above decomposition is unique.

Since
\gamma P_{i}=n_{i}P_{i}
, we have that

\displaystyle\gamma\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}+\gamma P_{1}\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}+\cdots+\gamma P_{s}\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_{i}P_{i}\right)\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}+P_{1}\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}+\cdots+P_{s}\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}\subset P_{1}\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}\oplus\cdots\oplus P_{s}\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}\subset\mathcal{X},

which proves that
\mathcal{X}
is a
\gamma
-invariant subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{L}
containing
\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}
.

Finally, let
\mathcal{Y}
be a
\gamma
-invariant subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{L}
containing
\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}
. As
\gamma\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}\subset\mathcal{Y}
, we have that
f(\gamma)\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}\subset\mathcal{Y}
for any Borel function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. In particular, we have for all
1\leq i\leq s
,
P_{i}\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}={\mathds{1}}_{\{n_{i}\}}(\gamma)\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}\subset\mathcal{Y}
. As we also have
\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}\subset\mathcal{Y}
by hypothesis, we obtain that
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathcal{Y}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Note that if
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\rm Slater}
, then

s=1
,
n_{1}=1
, and
P_{1}=\gamma
.
In this case,

\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag}+\gamma\mathcal{H}_{\rm frag},

and
\dim(\mathcal{X})\leq 2\ell
. In the general case
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\rm MS}
, we have the bound

\dim(\mathcal{X})\leq\ell+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\min(m_{i},\ell).

Consequently, full disentanglement can be achieved whenever

m\geq\sum_{i=1}^{s}\min(m_{i},\ell).

This bound improves upon the previously reported condition

m\geq s\ell
in
[
24
,
13
]
,
which was derived using arguments based on minimal polynomials and
Householder rotations.
An optimal bound can be obtained by computing

\dim(\mathcal{X})
via a singular value decomposition.

4
Numerical methods

We consider three classes of numerical methods for solving (
1
)–(
3
):

1.

Riemannian optimization methods (Section
4.1
);

2.

self-consistent Field (SCF) algorithms exploiting the Aufbau principle proved in Theorem
2
(Section
4.2
);

3.

methods consisting in solving the convexified problem (
24
), which, according to the results established in Theorem
4
, provides the global minimum of (
1
)–(
3
) if the matrix
H_{\star}
is gapped, and an hopefully good initial guess if
H_{\star}
is not gapped (Section
4.3
).

4.1
Riemannian optimization methods

Although it is possible to use Riemannian optimization methods directly on Grassmann manifolds, it is sometimes more efficient from a numerical viewpoint to reformulate an optimization problem on a Grassmann manifold
\mathcal{M}:={\rm Gr}(m,\mathbb{R}^{M})
into an optimization problem on the Stiefel manifold

\mathcal{N}:={\rm St}(m,\mathbb{R}^{M}):=\{V\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times m}\;:\;V^{T}V=I_{m}\}.

Denoting by
J_{\rm St}:\mathbb{R}^{M\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
the function defined by

\forall V\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times m},\quad J_{\rm St}(V):=J(VV^{T}),

we have indeed

\min_{P\in\mathcal{M}}J(P)=\min_{V\in\mathcal{N}}J_{\rm St}(V),

and
P_{\star}
is a minimizer of the LHS if and only if
P_{\star}=V_{\star}V_{\star}^{T}
, with
V_{\star}
a minimizer of the RHS. Note however that if
V_{\star}
is a minimizer of
J_{\rm St}
on
\mathcal{N}
, then so is
V_{\star}U
for all
U\in O(m)
. To perform the simulations presented in Section
5
, we used the Riemannian trust-region algorithm with default parameters and convergence criterion
\|\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}J(P_{k})\|\leq 10^{-12}
or
\|\nabla_{\mathcal{N}}J_{\rm St}(V_{k})\|\leq 10^{-12}
implemented in manopt.jl, a Riemannian optimization library in Julia
[
4
]
, to seek minimizers either of
J
on
\mathcal{M}
, or of
J_{\rm st}
on
\mathcal{N}
.

4.2
SCF algorithms

In view of the Aufbau principle established in Theorem
2
, the Roothaan algorithm
[
30
]
, originally introduced to solve the Hartree–Fock equations, provides a natural SCF algorithm to solve (
1
)–(
3
). It reads

P_{k+1}^{\rm Rth}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{P\in\mathcal{M}}{\rm Tr}(G(P_{k}^{\rm Rth})P)\quad(\mbox{recall that }G(P)=B-APA).

(49)

Each iteration is easy to perform if
L
is not too large: we just have to compute
G(P_{k}^{\rm Rth})
, diagonalize this matrix in an orthonormal basis, and construct the orthogonal projector
P_{k+1}^{\rm Rth}
on the vector space spanned by the lowest
m
eigenvalues. The output of iteration
k
is unique if and only if there is a gap between the
m^{\rm th}
and
(m+1)^{\rm st}
eigenvalue of
G(P_{k}^{\rm Rth})
. The Roothaan algorithm was investigated in detail for the (unrestricted) Hartree–Fock setting, which is also a quadratic Grassmann optimization problem for which the Aufbau principle is valid. It was shown
[
12
,
22
]
that either the iterates converge, or they asymptotically oscillate between two states, none of them being a critical point of the Hartree–Fock problem. It was also shown numerically in
[
10
]
that for non-quadratic cost functions, the Roothaan algorithm may either converge, or oscillate between two or more states, or have a chaotic behavior, depending on the cost function and the initial guess. In Hartree–Fock or Kohn–Sham Density Functional Theory (DFT) computations, the Roothaan algorithm is stabilized and accelerated using extrapolation (e.g. DIIS
[
28
,
29
]
), relaxation (e.g. ODA
[
11
]
), or a combination of both (e.g. EDIIS/DIIS scheme
[
21
]
). Recall that the ODA is initialized by choosing
D_{0}\in\mathcal{K}
and reads

P_{k+1}^{\rm ODA}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{P\in\mathcal{M}}{\rm Tr}(G(D_{k})P),\qquad D_{k+1}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{D\in[D_{k},P_{k+1}^{\rm ODA}]}J(D),

(50)

where
[D_{k},P_{k+1}^{\rm ODA}]
is the segment line in
\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}_{\rm sym}
joining
D_{k}
and
P_{k+1}^{\rm ODA}
. Parameterizing this set as

[D_{k},P_{k+1}^{\rm ODA}]=\{(1-\alpha)D_{k}+\alpha P_{k+1}^{\rm ODA},\;\alpha\in[0,1]\},

and introducing the function
f_{k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f_{k}(\alpha)=J((1-\alpha)D_{k}+\alpha P_{k+1}^{\rm ODA}),

we see that, since
J
is quadratic,
f_{k}
is a quadratic polynomial. Its minimizer
\alpha_{k}
on
[0,1]
can therefore be computed analytically. Unless ODA has converged,
f^{\prime}_{k}(0)\neq 0
,
\alpha_{k}
is unique, and we have

D_{k+1}^{\rm ODA}=(1-\alpha_{k})D_{k}+\alpha_{k}P_{k+1}^{\rm ODA}.

Remarkably, for the special case of the quadratic function
J
defined in (
3
), we have the following result.

Proposition 10
.

Consider a quadratic function
J
of the form (
3
). Then,

1.

the sequence
(P_{k}^{\rm ODA})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated of the ODA (
50
) initialized with
D_{0}=P_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
coincides with the sequence
(P_{k}^{\rm Rth})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by the Roothaan algorithm (
49
) initialized with the same
P_{0}
;

2.

denoting by
P_{k}:=P_{k}^{\rm Rth}=P_{k}^{\rm ODA}
, the sequence
(J(P_{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-increasing and converges to a critical value of
J
on
\mathcal{M}
, and any accumulation point
P_{\star}
of the sequence
(P_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a critical point of
J
on
\mathcal{M}
satisfying the Aufbau principle (
19
);

3.

under the additional assumption that the matrix
A
is invertible, the whole sequence
(P_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a a critical point of
J
on
\mathcal{M}
satisfying the Aufbau principle (
19
).

Proof.

We adapt arguments from
[
11
,
12
,
22
,
22
]
, where the Roothaan algorithm and the ODA were studied in the Hartree–Fock setting. First, we have

f_{k}(\alpha)=a_{k}\alpha^{2}+b_{k}\alpha+c_{k},

with

\displaystyle c_{k}=f_{k}(0)=J(D_{k}),

\displaystyle b_{k}=f_{k}^{\prime}(0)=\langle\nabla J(D_{k}),P_{k+1}^{\rm ODA}-D_{k}\rangle={\rm Tr}(G(D_{k})(P_{k+1}^{\rm ODA}-D_{k}))\leq 0,

\displaystyle a_{k}=-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}(A(P_{k+1}^{\rm ODA}-D_{k})A(P_{k+1}^{\rm ODA}-D_{k}))=-\frac{1}{2}\left\|A^{1/2}(P_{k+1}^{\rm ODA}-D_{k})A^{1/2}\right\|^{2}\leq 0.

which leads to the fact that the minimum of
f_{k}
on the interval
[0,1]
is reached at
\alpha_{k}=1
. Hence, in this special case, ODA boils down to the Roothaan algorithm, and we thus have
D_{k}=P_{k}^{\rm ODA}=P_{k}^{\rm Rth}=:P_{k}
for all
k\geq 1
. This proves the first assertion.

To prove the second one, we use the fact that as
\alpha_{k}=1
for all
k
, we have that

\displaystyle J(P_{k+1})

\displaystyle=a_{k}+b_{k}+c_{k}=J(P_{k})+{\rm Tr}(G(P_{k})(P_{k+1}-P_{k}))-\frac{1}{2}\left\|A^{1/2}(P_{k+1}-P_{k})A^{1/2}\right\|^{2}

so that

\displaystyle 0\leq\underbrace{-{\rm Tr}(G(P_{k})(P_{k+1}-P_{k}))}_{\geq 0}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|A^{1/2}(P_{k+1}-P_{k})A^{1/2}\right\|^{2}\leq J(P_{k})-J(P_{k+1}).

(51)

This shows that the sequence
(J(P_{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-increasing, thus converges to some
J_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}
, and that

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\underbrace{(-{\rm Tr}(G(P_{k})(P_{k+1}-P_{k})))}_{\geq 0}<+\infty\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\left\|A^{1/2}(P_{k+1}-P_{k})A^{1/2}\right\|^{2}<\infty.

(52)

Let
P_{\star}\in\mathcal{M}
be an accumulation point of the sequence
(P_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Since

P_{k+1}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{P\in\mathcal{M}}{\rm Tr}(G(P_{k})(P-P_{k})),

we must have

\min_{P\in\mathcal{M}}{\rm Tr}(G(P_{\star})(P-P_{\star}))=0;

otherwise the sequence non-negative numbers
(-{\rm Tr}(G(P_{k})(P_{k+1}-P_{k})))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
would not go to zero. This implies that
P_{\star}
is a critical point of
J
on
\mathcal{M}
satisfying the Aufbau principle (
19
) and such that
J(P_{\star})=J_{\star}
. The second assertion is proved.

Finally, if the matrix
A
is invertible, we deduce from the second bound in (
52
) and the equivalence of the norms in finite dimension that

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\left\|P_{k+1}-P_{k}\right\|^{2}<\infty.

In particular,
\|P_{k+1}-P_{k}\|\to 0
. To prove that
(P_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges, we use the same arguments as in
[
22
]
. Let
\Gamma:=\{P\in\mathcal{M}\;:\;J(P)=J_{\star}\}
and
P\in\Gamma
. As
J
is real-analytic, there exist, for each point
P\in\Gamma
,
\delta_{P}>0
,
\kappa_{P}>0
and
\theta_{P}\in(0,1/2]
such that the Łojasiewicz’s inequality
[
44
]
holds:

\forall P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}\mbox{ s.t. }\|P^{\prime}-P\|\leq\delta_{P},\quad(J(P^{\prime})-J_{\star})^{1-\theta_{P}}\leq\kappa_{P}\|\nabla_{\mathcal{M}}J(P^{\prime})\|=\kappa_{P}\|[G(P^{\prime}),P^{\prime}]\|.

(53)

Since
\Gamma
is non-empty (it contains
P_{\star}
) and compact, we obtain that there exist
\delta>0
,
\kappa>0
and
\theta\in(0,1/2]
such that

\forall P\in\mathcal{M}\mbox{ s.t. }{\rm d}(P,\Gamma)\leq\delta,\quad(J(P)-J_{\star})^{1-\theta}\leq\kappa\|[G(P),P]\|.

By compactness
J(P_{k})\to J_{\star}
implies
d(P_{k},\Gamma)\to 0
and we thus have, using the fact that
[G(P_{k}),P_{k+1}]=0
, that there exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\forall k\geq k_{0},\quad(J(P_{k})-J_{\star})^{1-\theta}\leq\kappa\|[G(P_{k}),P_{k}]\|=\kappa\|[G(P_{k}),P_{k}-P_{k+1}]\|\leq M\|P_{k}-P_{k+1}\|,

with
M=2\kappa\max_{P\in\mathcal{M}}\|G(P)\|_{\rm op}
.
We infer from the above inequality and the concavity of the function
\mathbb{R}_{+}\ni x\mapsto x^{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}
that for all
k\geq k_{0}
,

\displaystyle(J(P_{k})-J_{\star})^{\theta}-(J(P_{k+1})-J_{\star})^{\theta}

\displaystyle\geq\theta(J(P_{k})-J_{\star})^{\theta-1}\left(J(P_{k})-J(P_{k+1})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\theta}{M}\frac{\left(J(P_{k})-J(P_{k+1})\right)}{\|P_{k}-P_{k+1}\|}.

Using (
51
) and the invertibility of
A
, we obtain that there exists
\alpha>0
such that

J(P_{k})-J(P_{k+1})\geq\alpha\|P_{k}-P_{k+1}\|^{2}.

We thus get

\forall k\geq k_{0},\quad(J(P_{k})-J_{\star})^{\theta}-(J(P_{k+1})-J_{\star})^{\theta}\geq\frac{\alpha\theta}{M}\|P_{k}-P_{k+1}\|.

As the left-hand side is a summable series, so is the right-hand side. This implies that the sequence
(P_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges.
∎

4.3
Optimal damping algorithm for the convexified problem

To solve the convexified problem (
24
), we use the ODA, which reads in this specific setting

P_{k+1}^{\rm conv}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{P\in\mathcal{M}}{\rm Tr}(\widetilde{G}(D_{k}^{\rm conv})P),\qquad D_{k+1}^{\rm conv}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{D\in[D_{k}^{\rm conv},P_{k+1}^{\rm conv}]}\widetilde{J}(D),

(54)

with

\widetilde{G}(D)=\nabla\widetilde{J}(D)=C-\frac{1}{2}[[A,D],A].

We have that

D_{k+1}^{\rm conv}=(1-\beta_{k})D_{k}^{\rm conv}+\beta_{k}P_{k+1}^{\rm conv},

where the optimal damping parameter
\beta_{k}
is obtained by solving

\beta_{k}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\widetilde{J}((1-\beta)D_{k}^{\rm conv}+\beta P_{k+1}^{\rm conv})=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\widetilde{f}_{k}(\beta),

with

\widetilde{f}_{k}(\beta)=\widetilde{a}_{k}\beta^{2}+\widetilde{b}_{k}\beta+\widetilde{c}_{k},

and

\widetilde{a}_{k}=\frac{1}{4}\|[A,P_{k+1}^{\rm conv}-D_{k}^{\rm conv}]\|^{2},\quad\widetilde{b}_{k}={\rm Tr}(\widetilde{G}(D_{k}^{\rm conv})(P_{k+1}^{\rm conv}-D_{k}^{\rm conv})),\quad\widetilde{c}_{k}=J(D_{k}^{\rm conv}).

Some applicative examples

We first analyze low-dimensional illustrative examples (
M=2
or
3
,
m=1
) allowing us to show the possible behaviors of the various numerical methods introduced in the previous sections. We then present an example originating from DMET bath construction problems, in which the matrices
A
and
B
are obtained using formulae (

)–(
46
), where
\gamma

5.1
Analysis of low-dimensional illustrative examples

The purpose of this section is to show that

•

the functional
J
may have local, non-global minima on
\mathcal{M}
;

•

these local minima have attraction basins with positive measure not only for Riemannian optimization methods, but also for the SCF algorithm (
49
);

•

while the Roothaan algorithm applied to the minimization of

on
\mathcal{M}
always converges (in a sense specified in Proposition (
10
)), this is not the case when this algorithm is applied to the minimization of
\widetilde{J}
on
\mathcal{M}
, although the functions
J
and
\widetilde{J}
coincide on
\mathcal{M}
;

•

the set of minimizers of
\widetilde{J}
on
\mathcal{K}
may contain no point belonging to
\mathcal{M}
, in which case minimizing
\widetilde{J}
on
\mathcal{M}
using e.g. the ODA (

), does not provide the global minimizer on
J
on
\mathcal{M}
.

Consider first the case when
M=2
and
m=1
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
A
is diagonal and
C
traceless and consider the matrices

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&a+\sqrt{\alpha}\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}c+\frac{a^{2}}{2}&\beta\\
\beta&c+\frac{(a+\sqrt{\alpha})^{2}}{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad C=\begin{pmatrix}c&\beta\\
\beta&-c\end{pmatrix},\quad a,\alpha\geq 0,\quad\beta,c\in\mathbb{R}.

The set
\mathcal{M}
is diffeomorphic to the unit circle (the set of rank-
1
orthogonal projectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can be represented as a great circle of the Bloch sphere): any
P\in\mathcal{M}
can be written in a unique way as

P_{x,z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{2}+x\sigma_{x}+z\sigma_{z}\right)\quad\mbox{with}\qquad x,z\in\mathbb{R},\;x^{2}+z^{2}=1,\quad\sigma_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

When the variable
(x,z)
span
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the matrix
P
defined by the above formula spans the affine space of trace-
1

2\times 2
real-symmetric matrices. The restriction of
J
and
\widetilde{J}
to this affine space can be written in terms of the
(x,z)
variable. We have for all
(x,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
J(P_{x,z})=j(x,z)
,
\widetilde{J}(P_{x,z})=\widetilde{j}(x,z)
with

given by

\displaystyle j(x,z)=\beta x+cz-\frac{1}{8}(2a^{2}+2a\sqrt{\alpha})x^{2}-\frac{1}{8}(2a^{2}+2a\sqrt{\alpha}+\alpha)z^{2}+\frac{1}{8}(2a^{2}+2a\sqrt{\alpha}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{j}(x,z)=\beta x+cz+\frac{1}{8}\alpha x^{2}.

It is clear that on
\mathbb{S}^{1}:=\{(x,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,x^{2}+z^{2}=1\}
, we have
\widetilde{j}(x,z)=j(x,z)
as expected.

Existence of local, non-global minima of
J
on
\mathcal{M}

For
\beta=c=0
,
j
has two non-degenerate global minimizers
X_{0,1}
and
X_{0,-1}
on the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
and two non-degenerate global maximizers
X_{1,0}
and
X_{-1,0}
given by

X_{0,1}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
1\end{array}\right),\quad X_{0,-1}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
-1\end{array}\right),\quad X_{1,0}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
0\end{array}\right),\quad X_{-1,0}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-1\\
0\end{array}\right).

corresponding respectively to the following two global minimizers and two global minimizers of
J
on
\mathcal{M}
:

P_{0,1}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right),\quad P_{0,-1}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right),\quad P_{1,0}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1\\
1&1\end{array}\right),\quad P_{-1,0}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{array}\right).

For
\beta=0
and
0<c<\alpha/4
,
P_{0,1}
and
P_{0,-1}
still are non-degenerate local minima of

on
\mathcal{M}
, but

is a non-global minima (
J(P_{0,1})=c
), while

is a global minima (

).

The Roothaan algorithm (
49
) may converge to local non-global minima

Proposition
10
shows that the Roothaan algorithm always converge. However, it does not necessarily converge to a global minimum. To prove that, let us observe that, in the simple setting considered here, the Roothaan algorithm can be reformulated as a dynamical system on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Introducing the function
F:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
with components

\displaystyle F_{1}(x,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{8}(2a^{2}+2a\sqrt{\alpha})x-\frac{\beta}{2}}{\left(\left(\frac{1}{8}(2a^{2}+2a\sqrt{\alpha})x-\frac{\beta}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{8}(2a^{2}+2a\sqrt{\alpha}+\alpha)z-\frac{c}{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}},

\displaystyle F_{2}(x,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{8}(2a^{2}+2a\sqrt{\alpha}+\alpha)z-\frac{c}{2}}{\left(\left(\frac{1}{8}(2a^{2}+2a\sqrt{\alpha})x-\frac{\beta}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{8}(2a^{2}+2a\sqrt{\alpha}+\alpha)z-\frac{c}{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}},

which maps
\mathbb{S}^{1}
into itself, the iterates of the Roothaan algorithm are given by
(x_{k+1}^{\rm Rth},z_{k+1}^{\rm Rth})=F(x_{k}^{\rm Rth},z_{k}^{\rm Rth})
. Getting back to the case when
\beta=0
and
0<c<\alpha/4
, we see that
X_{0,1}
is a fixed point of
F
and that the Jacobian matrix of
F
at
X_{0,1}
is

F^{\prime}(X_{0,1})=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{2a^{2}+2a\sqrt{\alpha}}{2a^{2}+2a\sqrt{\alpha}+\alpha-4c}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Under the condition
0<c<\alpha/4
,
F^{\prime}(X_{0,1})
is a contraction, which shows that the attraction basin of
P_{0,1}
for the Roothaan algorithm (
49
) contains an open neighborhood of
P_{0,1}
in
\mathcal{M}
.

The Roothaan algorithm applied to
\widetilde{J}
does not always converge

For
\alpha=1
,
c=0
and
|4\beta|<1
, we have

j(x,z)=\beta x+\frac{1}{8}x^{2}=\frac{1}{8}(x+4\beta)^{2}-2\beta^{2}.

This function therefore has two global minimizers on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, given by

X_{+}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-4\beta\\
\sqrt{1-16\beta^{2}}\end{array}\right),\quad X_{-}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-4\beta\\
-\sqrt{1-16\beta^{2}}\end{array}\right)

corresponding respectively to the following two global minimizers of
J
on
\mathcal{M}
:

P_{+}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1+\sqrt{1-16\beta^{2}}}{2}&-2\beta\\
-2\beta&\frac{1-\sqrt{1-16\beta^{2}}}{2}\end{array}\right),\quad P_{-}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1-\sqrt{1-16\beta^{2}}}{2}&-2\beta\\
-2\beta&\frac{1+\sqrt{1-16\beta^{2}}}{2}\end{array}\right).

The minimum of
J
on
\mathcal{M}
is given by
J(P_{+})=J(P_{-})=-2\beta^{2}
. The Roothaan algorithm can be formulated as an alternate direction method
[
12
]
. In terms of the function
\widetilde{j}
, it reads

x_{k+1}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{x\in[-1,1]}\left(\frac{\beta x}{2}+\frac{1}{8}x_{k}x\right)=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{x\in[-1,1]}\left((4\beta+x_{k})x\right)=\left|\begin{array}[]{ll}\{-1\}&\mbox{ if }4\beta+x_{k}>0,\\
\ \{1\}&\mbox{ if }4\beta+x_{k}<0,\\
{[}-1,1{]}&\mbox{ if }4\beta+x_{k}=0.\end{array}\right.

It follows that almost surely, the Roothaan algorithm applied
\widetilde{J}
will oscillate between the states
P_{1,0}
and
P_{0,1}
, which are not critical points of
\widetilde{J}
(or, equivalently,
J
) on
\mathcal{M}
.

Minimizing
\widetilde{J}
on
\mathcal{K}
does not always provide the global minimizers of
J
on
\mathcal{M}

We now consider the case when
M=3
and
m=1
and introduce the matrices

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&0&0\\
0&a_{2}&0\\
0&0&a_{3}\end{pmatrix},\quad C=\begin{pmatrix}\beta&-\frac{\alpha}{2}(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}&0\\
-\frac{\alpha}{2}(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}&\beta&-\frac{\alpha}{2}(a_{3}-a_{2})^{2}\\
0&-\frac{\alpha}{2}(a_{3}-a_{2})^{2}&\beta\end{pmatrix},

with
0\leq a_{1}<a_{2}<a_{3}
,
\alpha>0
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. We have for all
D\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\rm sym}
such that
{\rm Tr}(D)=1
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}(D)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}(CD)+\frac{1}{4}\lVert[A,D]\rVert^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}C_{ij}D_{ij}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}(a_{i}-a_{j})^{2}D_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{M}C_{ii}D_{ii}+\sum_{i\neq j}(a_{i}-a_{j})^{2}\left(D_{ij}+2\frac{C_{ij}}{(a_{i}-a_{j})^{2}}\right)^{2}-\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{C_{ij}}{(a_{i}-a_{j})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\beta+2(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}(D_{12}-\alpha)^{2}+2(a_{3}-a_{2})^{2}(D_{23}-\alpha)^{2}+2(a_{3}-a_{1})^{2}D_{13}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-2\alpha^{2}((a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}+(a_{3}-a_{2})^{2}).

We thus have

\mathcal{D}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\begin{subarray}{c}D\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\rm sym}\;\\
{\rm Tr}(D)=1\end{subarray}}\widetilde{J}(D)=\left\{D=\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}&\alpha&0\\
\alpha&n_{2}&\alpha\\
0&\alpha&n_{3}\end{pmatrix},\;n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\in\mathbb{R},\;n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}=1\right\}.

Since for any

, while for

small enough, we have

It follows that for

on

. The global minimizer of

Taking now

5.2
DMET bath construction for the benzene molecule

Numerical tests on molecular systems can be done using the following methodology:

Step 1:

The ground-state spin-unpolarized 1-RDM of the molecular system under consideration is computed at the chosen level of theory (e.g. Hartree–Fock, configuration interaction single-double - CISD -, coupled-cluster single-double - CCSD -, complete active space self-consistent field - CASSCF -) in the chosen gaussian atomic basis set, using the PySCF Python library

35

37

Using Löwdin orthonormalization, the non-orthogonal, atom-centered, gaussian-orbital basis is transformed into an orthonormal, and still reasonably localized, basis, which we call the Löwdin basis for convenience. In particular, each basis function of the Löwdin basis is essentially localized around a specific atom, which allows us to obtain a decomposition (
40

\gamma_{\rm GS}
the spin-unpolarized ground-state 1-RDMs of the molecule in the Löwdin basis for the chosen level of theory. This matrix, together with the list of atoms each Löwdin orbital is attached to, is exported in a file.

Step 2:

The rest of the calculation:

•

\gamma_{\rm GS}
of the

•

is performed by a homemade code.

For the sake of brevity, we only report here calculations on the benzene molecule (C

6
Conclusion

The quadratic Grassmann optimization problem (
1
)–(
3
) arising from the DMET bath-construction problem (
42
) has interesting mathematical and numerical properties. As the Hartree–Fock problem - a famous quadratic Grassmann optimization problem -, it satisfies an Aufbau principle (Theorem
2
). A major qualitative difference between these two problems is that the basic Roothaan self-consistent field (SCF) algorithm always converges to a local minimizer of (
1
)–(
3
) (Proposition
10
), whereas for Hartree–Fock, it often oscillates between two points which are not critical points of the Hartree–Fock functional
[
11
]
. Another interesting specificity of the cost function
J
defined in (
3
) is that the
convex
quadratic function
\widetilde{J}
defined in (
23
) takes the same values as
J
on the Grassmann manifold
\mathcal{M}
. Minimizing
\widetilde{J}
on the convex hull
{\rm CH}(\mathcal{M})
, using e.g. the optimal damping algorithm (ODA), yields a global minimizer
D_{\star}\in{\rm CH}(\mathcal{M})
, and the matrix
H_{\star}:=\nabla\widetilde{J}(D_{\star})
is independent of the found minimizer. The results in Theorem
4
show that if
H_{\star}
satisfies the gap condition
\mu_{m}<\mu_{m+1}
, then
D_{\star}
is a
global
minimizer of the non-convex problem (
1
)–(
3
). Otherwise, the numerical tests we have run so far seem to indicate that
D_{\star}
provides a good starting point for Riemannian optimization methods to reach the assumed global minimum of
J
on
\mathcal{M}
.
