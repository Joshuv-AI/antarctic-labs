 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 PV panels are subjected to wind loads during normal outdoor operation, where th wind speed,
 wind direction, panel angle, and array layout play a large role in the overall loading magnitude.
 For floating PV systems, where panels are installed on floating rafts, these forces can lead to a
 dynamic displacement of the raft and mounted PV hardware. The Contractor and Participant will
 perform fluid-structure interaction simulations of this phenomenon for a variety of wind speeds,
 directions, and panel angles to characterize these forces and help design mooring/tethering lines
 to resist and anchor raft movement.

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: Participant will provide geometric definitions of the floating platform, mounting
 hardware, and PV panels. Participant will identify a range of tilt angles to test and Contractor
 will divide the total number of simulations to explore this space with existing computational
 resources.

 Task 1 Results: AccuSolar provided detailed design drawings of the floating solar array with
 dimensions of the floating platform and PV panels. An isometric view, plan view and profile
 view were provided, and dimensions not readily available from the dimensioned drawings (e.g.,
 the spacing between elements at different panel tilt angles) were provided via email
 correspondence with AccuSolar engineers. The drawings included dimensions of panel sizes,
 base frame sections and connections, row spacing, separation distances from the platform and
 elevations and offsets from mounting hardware. An example of such a technical drawing is
 shown in Figure 1. A range of operational tilt angles between 6° and 18° and a safe stow angle of
 0° were specified by AccuSolar for testing. This was sufficient to set up and run the PVade
 simulations [1]. The constraints of the high-performance computing (HPC) were considered by
 NLR when running simulations to sweep the parameter space at sufficient resolution.

 Task 2: Contractor will add functionality to PVade software to enable arrays of panels to
 undergo wind-driven, rigid-body displacement consistent with being mounted to a monolithic
 floating structure.

 Task 2 Results: To extend PVade to the problem of floating installations, we introduced new
 modeled elements to represent the forces from mooring lines, hydrodynamic drag, buoyancy, and
 gravity that are unique to floating platforms vs land-based PV systems. The combined simulation
 and model algorithm uses mooring lines represented by spring-dashpot elements, optional
 hydrodynamic drag forces calculated from simplified form factors and assumes a linear response
 of buoyancy forces based on the submerged volume of the float. Additional PVade software
 development to support this task included an updated mesh refinement algorithm to account for
 the much smaller distance separating panels from the water’s surface, modified inflow boundary
 conditions more consistent with water surface roughness vs land-based systems, and the addition
 of “slip” mesh adaptation in the direction parallel to walls to avoid decreasing the quality of cells
 between panels and water during mesh motion. The updated PVade model-simulation force
 coupling used an extension of the previous predictor-corrector approach to solve for the updated
 position of the combined mass of modules and float.

 A forced oscillation case study was conducted to test and validate the PVade simulation against
 benchmark results by moving the cylinder to approximate the oscillatory motion and dynamic
 behavior of a floating platform. Across a range of different driving frequencies, good agreement
 between the PVade simulation [1] and benchmark results [2] were shown for both the mean drag
 force coefficient and maximum lift force coefficient. This provided confidence that even when
 undergoing the sort of rigid body motion expected in our floating platform case, the integrated
 force values were accurate. Furthermore, we identified the “lock-in” region, where the vortex
 shedding from the cylinder matches the prescribed oscillation frequency, with a high degree of
 accuracy over a range of different driving frequencies.

 Task 3: Contractor will perform simulations on these floating PV systems at wind speeds, wind
 directions, and tilt angles of interest specified by Participant.

 Task 3 Results: NLR discussed a range of wind speeds, wind directions, and tilt angles with
 AccuSolar to define the parameter space for design load considerations in operational and stow
 positions. A solar array containing 7 rows with spacing and geometry provided by AccuSolar
 were defined in the PVade software. NLR performed simulations within the constraints of the
 HPC allocations for a parameter sweep spanning four tilt angles (0°, 6°, 12°, and 18°) four wind
   speeds (20 mph, 40 mph, 90 mph, and 140 mph) and seven wind directions (90°, 120°, 150°,
 180°, 210°, 240°, and 270°). In the annotated schematic shown in Figure 3, our direction
 convention dictates that a 270° wind impacts row 1 first, while a 90° wind would impact row 3
 first.

 4

 Fig. 3. The direction conventions used to understand the limits and test conditions of our
 parameter sweep in a profile view (left) and top-down view (right); note that for a positive tilt angle
 orientation (shown above left) a 270° wind means impacting the bottom of the panel, while a 90°
 wind means hitting the top or sun-facing side of the panel. Furthermore, while this figure shows
 only 3 rows to be consistent with the schematic provided by the Participant, our final simulations
 use 7 rows to better capture the behavior of panels in larger arrays.

 Task 4: Contractor will develop a Final CRADA report to be reviewed by Participant including
 time-varying loads on mooring cables and integrated panel areas. Preparation and submission in
 accordance with the terms of this agreement.

 NLR carried out a series of PVade simulations on the HPC for flow input conditions specified by
 the parameter sweep outlined in Task 3. Simulation outputs provided time series of accumulated
 forces directly applied over the integrated panel areas on the 7 rows in the solar array. The loads
 on the mooring lines depend heavily on the number, arrangement, and overall system design.
 Thus, in this report, we present the results in terms of the integrated panel loads which can be
 distributed onto separate mooring line connections per panel section or row. Mean and standard
 deviation values of the three-dimensional forces in the X-direction (drag force), Y-direction (side
 force) and Z-direction (lift force) on each of the 7 panel rows were calculated and line polar plots
 of the forces are shown in Figure 4 as a function of wind direction at operational tilt angles of 6°,
 12° and 18° and a stow tilt angle of 0° at a wind speed of 140 mph, chosen for this brief
 summary since it reflects a “worst case scenario”.

 Time-varying loads on the integrated panel areas of the 7 rows in Figure 4 for all tilt angles show
 that mean values (lines in the polar plots) of lift forces in the Z-direction are more than two times
 larger in magnitude than drag forces in the X-direction and an order of magnitude larger than
 side forces in the Y-direction. The largest mean and standard deviation (shaded regions in the
 polar plots) values of uplift (positive force in Z-direction) occur on row 1 for wind approaching
 the array at a wind direction of 270°. There is only a small reduction in forces from the first row
 (row 1 for 270° wind direction and row 7 for 90° wind direction) to downstream rows for the
 stow case at 0° tilt angle. Shielding of downwind panel rows 2-4 at this wind direction is
 observed with increasing lift force reduction compared to row 1 with increasing tilt angle from
 6° to 18°. Further into the array from rows 5-7, lift forces increase above the shielded rows 2-4
 and are accompanied by an increased unsteady component or standard deviation of forces
 compared to row 1. The mean and standard deviation values in rows 5, 6 and 7 increase in

 5

 magnitude with increasing tilt angle from 6° to 18° but remain below the maximum forces on
 row 1. In contrast, for wind approaching the array at 90° wind direction, significant downward
 forces are exerted on row 7 with shielding observed on rows 2-6. Notably, the unsteady
 component of forces on downstream rows decreases for this nose-down configuration as flow is
 deflected upward and the effects of vortex shedding from the first row are reduced. Although the
 mean values of the Y-direction forces are small, the standard deviations of the Y-forces increase
 considerably for oblique winds at 240° and with increasing tilt angle. Such unsteady components
 of lift forces on downstream rows in the interior of the array for wind approaching the front or
 back of the array and side forces for oblique winds should be considered in the design of the
 floating components that support the panels within acceptable limits of motion of the mooring
 system.
