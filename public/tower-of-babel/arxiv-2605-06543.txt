A Rayleigh criterion for mechanical instability: inducing activity by chemo–mechanical coupling
Aaron Beyen, Francesco Casini, Christian Maes

Abstract
Instabilities in thermodynamic systems are often undesirable, as they can lead to loss of control or even catastrophic behavior. Yet, the same mechanisms can also generate rich nonequilibrium behavior and may play a constructive role in living systems. We introduce a theoretical framework, inspired by Rayleigh’s analysis of thermoacoustic instabilities, to study the emergence of mechanical activity. In particular, we derive Rayleigh-like criteria governing the onset of activity and the generation of rotational motion in a slow Newtonian probe coupled to driven chemical processes, described by Markov jump processes. These criteria are expressed in terms of the phase relation between entropic and frenetic contributions, providing a transparent condition for when chemical driving results in sustained rotational or active mechanical motion.



I
Introduction

In classical physics, instabilities often mark the onset of motion. A paradigmatic example is the thermoacoustic instability analyzed by Rayleigh, where heat input, when appropriately phased with pressure oscillations, drives self-sustained sound,
Rayleigh1878Explanation
;
Sujith2021Thermoacoustic

Rayleigh01041883

A closely related question arises in biophysical systems. There, mechanical activity is not imposed externally but emerges from underlying chemical processes that continuously dissipate energy. Molecules consume fuel, reactions proceed out of equilibrium, and yet the conditions under which this chemical driving produces persistent motion remain only partially understood. What plays the role of Rayleigh’s phase criterion in such chemo-mechanical systems?

In this work, we address that question at a general level. Rather than modeling specific biological details, we develop a thermodynamically consistent framework that captures how nonequilibrium chemical driving couples to mechanical degrees of freedom. By combining local detailed balance with semi-reciprocity, we derive Rayleigh-like criteria for the emergence of activity, expressed in terms of the phase relation between entropic and frenetic contributions. This provides a physically transparent condition for when driven chemistry generates sustained mechanical motion, the basic example of bio-chemo-mechanics,
Sun2022BiochemoMech
;
Feng2025MechanoChemoBio
.

To keep the discussion sharp and simple, we work in the deliberately restricted setup of a probe (point-particle) moving on a circle, coupled to a collection of fast, independent but driven jump processes, abbreviated as “jumpers”. This minimal setup allows us to focus entirely on the transfer of nonequilibrium features and on the mechanism of activity generation in particular. Upon elimination of the fast internal degrees of freedom, the probe may acquire rotational motion and/or

VI
Conclusion

The transfer of a low-entropy condition and associated nonequilibrium features from one set of degrees of freedom to another is a central question of nonequilibrium statistical mechanics with important relevance to biology. In the present paper, we have focused on theoretical modeling of chemo-mechanical transfer in a hybrid dynamical setup, and we found the emergence of activity and rotational motion. More specifically, we have shown that sustained mechanical activity can be generated
for an underdamped probe by coupling it to driven Markov jump processes,
while maintaining semi-reciprocity and local detailed balance with a single thermal bath. By activity for the probe on the ring, we mean here the occurrence of two sharp peaks in the otherwise symmetric velocity distribution.
The construction makes explicit that activity does not require

ad hoc
nonconservative forces at the mechanical level.
Instead, it emerges from a thermodynamically consistent
chemo–mechanical coupling in which the internal jump dynamics
is driven by a nonzero chemical affinity.

By keeping the setup simple yet structurally faithful to local detailed balance, we sharpen the conceptual understanding of how nonequilibrium chemistry can generate mechanical motion, and under what precise conditions it does so. More generally, that is an instance of the importance of the time-symmetric fluctuation sector, as summarized by the frenesy
fren1
;
frenesy
, only possible thanks to the presence of net dissipation.
One finds that the friction has an entropic and frenetic component, with the latter enabling negative friction; see also
PeiMaes2025
;
beyen2025couplingelasticstringactive
. The origin of activity is thus traced to the interplay between entropy production and dynamical activity (frenesy), rather than to explicit violation of mechanical laws. Interestingly, the criteria for the occurrence of rotational forces and for the emergence of negative linear friction are very similar to those derived by Rayleigh for the combustion instability. Also, the velocity and phase space distribution show the characteristic of a Rayleigh oscillator.

Appendix A
Coupling diffusion to reaction

There exists a multitude of ways by which coupling to jumpers produces motion,
[
67
]
. A recurrent mechanism in small-scale systems is to couple a diffusive degree of freedom (
e.g.
representing a mechanical position
x(t)
) to an internal jump process, describing chemical transitions, conformational changes, or binding–unbinding events. In this section, we review representative constructions of
models in which the mechanical motion is overdamped diffusion
x(t)
, as is typical for biological motion,
and the chemical dynamics is described by a finite-state Markov process
\sigma(t)
. Naturally, time-scale separation between mechanics and chemistry, and weak
vs
strong coupling influence the possible outcomes for the reduced probe dynamics as well.

A.1
Semi-reciprocity and local detailed balance

The naive statement of Newton’s third law (“equal and opposite forces”) is not generally invariant under arbitrary coordinate transformations. More to the point is that interactions should not create or destroy total momentum,
i.e.
the total stress-energy tensor should be divergence-free. Yet, in the overdamped limit, you partially lose the clean variational (Hamiltonian) structure, and reciprocity has to be reformulated again. For a particle coupled to a chemical process, we are satisfied with the presence of a joint (free) energy
U(x,\sigma)
where
x
is the mechanical and
\sigma
is the chemical coordinate. If the reversible part of the joint dynamics is generated by that energy, we say that the setup is satisfying semi-reciprocity. If there is no extra driving, we would then have detailed balance and equilibrium distributions with respect to that energy. We also exclude here the case where the ‘mechanical’ and ‘chemical’ temperatures are different. The only driving we allow is via chemical affinities.

That brings us to the second pillar of our modelling and thermodynamic formulation, known as local detailed balance,
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
A priori
, the mechanics is slow and undriven, but is open to receive energy from the reaction part. The latter is about chemistry, which is fast and driven, with dissipation in a thermal environment at fixed temperature
T
. For that purpose, we model it as a collection of Markov jump processes with transition rates
k_{x}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
for the jump
\sigma\to\sigma^{\prime}
which may depend on the position
x
of the probe. This is the basic structure underlying flashing ratchets, molecular motors, and chemically driven active particles. Then, under local detailed balance, the antisymmetric part of
k_{x}
, denoted as
s_{x}
in (
2
), is interpreted as the entropy change (per
k_{B}
) in the thermal bath to which the jumper is connected, from the transition
\sigma\to\sigma^{\prime}
at fixed probe position
x
. In other words, under local detailed balance, the heat
q_{x}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=-q_{x}(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma)
released to the bath during
\sigma\to\sigma^{\prime}
is found from taking the log-ratio between forward and backward transitions

\displaystyle q_{x}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{k_{x}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})}{k_{x}(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma)}\right)=k_{B}T\,s_{x}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})

(28)

which, following the First Law of thermodynamics, involves energy changes and work. Equation (
3
) is such an example.

To emphasize the above points, we briefly discuss a couple of well-known examples.

A.2
No feedback as thermal disequilibrium

When the probe dynamics for
x(t)
is externally driven by switching potentials
U(x,\sigma)
, as specified by the state
\sigma(t)
of the jumper, and
the jump rates
k(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
for
\sigma(t)
are
x
-independent, then there
is no feedback from mechanics to chemistry. That implies there is no reciprocity, except when interpreting the chemistry to take place at infinite temperature.
The nonequilibrium condition (violation of detailed balance) can then be ascribed to the presence of different thermal baths at very high (in fact, infinite) temperatures. That is the scenario of the classical flashing ratchet model

[
68
,
69
]
, where the particle alternates between two periodic potentials,
one asymmetric and one flat. The switching is externally imposed,
often modeled as a two-state Markov chain with fixed rates.
Each potential separately satisfies detailed balance for the diffusion, but global detailed balance is violated because the time-dependent switching is at a different (often infinite) temperature. Transport results from the interplay of spatial asymmetry and temporal
modulation. The mechanism can be interpreted as rectification of diffusion. There is no emerging activity here.

In rocking ratchets

[
70
]
,
a time-dependent force replaces switching potentials. The chemical or driving sector acts as a prescribed
protocol. Again, these models are conceptually simple but thermodynamically incomplete,
as the energy cost of switching is not explicitly modeled, invisible behind infinite temperature.

Moreover, the linear instability discussed in the main text is only present if the bath feels a backreaction to the oscillator chain. That follows from the formula (
11
) since without backreaction the Born-Oppenheimer distribution
\rho_{x}(\sigma)
would not depend on
x
such that
\nu(x)
vanishes.

A.3
Feedback without semi-reciprocity

Consider yet another model for the diffusion process
x(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\Gamma\dot{x}(t)=\sigma(t)f(x(t))+\sqrt{2\Gamma k_{B}T}\,\xi(t)

where the rates (or the chemistry) for the process
\sigma(t)=\pm 1
now depend on the mechanical state

\displaystyle k_{x}(1,-1)=\alpha e^{-\Upsilon x},\qquad k_{x}(-1,1)=\alpha e^{\Upsilon x}+\delta

Hence, there is feedback, but quite generally, for a given
f(x)
, there is no joint (dimensionless) energy function
\beta U(x,\sigma)
as in (
29
), (
3
) for which

\partial_{x}U(x,\sigma)=-\sigma f(x),\quad\text{ and }\quad\beta(U(x,1)-U(x,-1))=\log\frac{k_{x}(1,-1)}{k_{x}(-1,1)}=\log\frac{\alpha e^{-\Upsilon x}}{\alpha e^{\Upsilon x}+\delta}

The example is
ad hoc
, yet it stands for a class of bio-physical models where there is feedback without semi-reciprocity.

As a more realistic illustration, we briefly discuss the case of (early) models of molecular motors (flashing ratchets with binding),

[
71
,
13
]
. Consider, as an example of (
29
), the diffusion of a loaded motor protein in two periodic potentials
U(x,\pm 1)
,

\Gamma\dot{x}(t)=-\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}(x(t),\sigma(t))+F_{\text{load}}+\sqrt{2\Gamma k_{B}T}\,\xi(t)

(30)

depending on the value
\sigma(t)=\pm 1
: they are the two conformational states, each with a distinct interaction with the filament. In the bounded state (
\sigma=-1
), the motor interacts with a periodic asymmetric potential
U(x,-1)
along
x
that reflects ATP-driven energy input. In the unbounded state (
\sigma=1
), the motor diffuses freely on a flat potential
U(x,1)=
constant. The motor’s position along the filament,
x(t)
, is periodic and influenced by thermal noise at physical temperature
T
. The transition rates between the bounded and unbounded conformation are

\displaystyle k_{x}(-1,1)=(a(x)e^{{\beta}\Delta G}+c(x))\,e^{\beta U(x,-1)},\qquad k_{x}(1,-1)=(a(x)+c(x))\,e^{\beta U(x,1)}

(31)

We have chosen to take the same temperature
k_{B}T=\beta^{-1}
(same thermal bath) for the jumping. The
a
-term corresponds to the transitions induced by the chemical reaction, and the
c
-term represents the thermally excited ones, both spatially periodic.
\Delta G
is the chemical driving force that fuels the motor’s conformational changes and enables directed motion against external loads.

For the two-state jumper
\sigma(t)
in (
31
) we can find the energy gap

e(x)=k_{B}T\,\log\frac{k_{x}(1,-1)}{k_{x}(-1,1)}=U(x,1)-U(x,-1)+k_{B}T\,\log\frac{a(x)+c(x)}{a(x)e^{{\beta}\Delta G}+c(x)}

for each
x
. For having semi-reciprocity, looking at (
30
), we must have

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\log\frac{a(x)+c(x)}{a(x)e^{{\beta}\Delta G}+c(x)}=0

which fails unless
c(x)=0
, or
a(x)=a,c(x)=c
do not depend on
x
, or
\Delta G=0
. In these cases, since we took the same thermal bath for the diffusion and for the jumping, there is detailed balance when
F_{\text{load}}=0
. The case
\Delta G=0
means that the system is considered without any ATP- and ADP-fueling.

Appendix C
Numerical scheme

We integrate the equations of motion for the case (
14

17
) using a stochastic algorithm that combines a deterministic mechanical update for the probe with a continuous-time Markov chain jump process for the bath variables.

The mechanical degrees of freedom, particle position
x
and velocity

\Delta t

(x_{i},\vec{\sigma}_{i})

(39)

where
N_{0}^{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{\sigma_{j,i},0}
is the number of
internal units in state
0
at timestep
i

\displaystyle v_{i+1}

Next, after the mechanical update, the internal state vector
\vec{\sigma}_{i}=(\sigma_{1,i},\ldots,\sigma_{N,i})\in\{0,1,2\}^{N}
evolves as
N
independent continuous-time Markov chains. Defining the escape rate

\Lambda_{x}(\sigma)=\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\neq\sigma}k_{x}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})

we have that over a time interval
\Delta t
, the probability that jumper
\sigma_{j}
(currently in state
\sigma_{j,i}
) undergoes at least one transition, is approximated by

p_{j,i}=1-e^{-\Lambda_{x}(\sigma_{j,i})\,\Delta t}

This is exact for a two-state system and is a standard approximation for multi-state systems under the assumption that
\Delta t
is small enough that at most one jump occurs per unit per step. Then, every jumper
\sigma_{j}
is assigned a uniform random variate
u_{j}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
such that
\sigma_{j}
jumps if
u_{j}<p_{j,i}
and remains the same otherwise. Whenever a jump occurs, the destination state is selected by drawing
r_{j}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,\,\Lambda_{x}(\sigma_{j,i}))
and
comparing against the rate
k_{x}(0,1)
. For example, for a jumper
\sigma_{j}
in state
0
, it transitions to
1
if
r_{j}<k_{x}(0,1)
, and to
2
otherwise.

In conclusion, the full update at each step
i\to i+1
proceeds as follows:

1.

Compute the acceleration
a_{i}
in (
39
) from the current state
(x_{i},\vec{\sigma}_{i})
.

2.

Update the velocity:
v_{i+1}=v_{i}+a_{i}\,\Delta t
.

3.

Update the position:
x_{i+1}=(x_{i}+v_{i+1}\,\Delta t)\bmod L
.

4.

Evaluate the transition rates
k_{x_{i+1}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
at the new position.

5.

For each jumper
\sigma_{j}
, independently sample whether a jump occurs and, if so, the destination state.

6.

Store
(x_{i+1},\,v_{i+1},\,\vec{\sigma}_{i+1})
