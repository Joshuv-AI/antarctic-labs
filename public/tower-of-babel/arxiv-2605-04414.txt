Uniform Mixing in Chiral Quantum Walks
Luke Levine, Jessy Jacob Mesapam, Benjamin Mustico, Christino Tamon, Gabriel Tucker, Hanmeng Zhan

Abstract
This paper studies uniform mixing in continuous-time quantum walks. We show that for some unitary signing σ \sigma , the complete graph K n σ K^{\sigma}_{n} has probabilistic uniform mixing. In contrast, Ahmadi et al. (2003) proved that no complete graph has uniform mixing except for K 2 K_{2} , K 3 K_{3} , and K 4 K_{4} . Our technique is based on a stopping rule for quantum walks which reduces global to local uniform mixing. As a corollary, we found an orientation of H ⁡ ( n , 4 ) H(n,4) that mixes to uniform faster than any other Hamming graphs, which improves a result of Godsil and Zhan (2019). We also show that there are infinite families of oriented circulants with average uniform mixing. This is a chiral violation of a No-Go theorem due to Godsil (2013) which states that no graph has average uniform mixing except for K 2 K_{2} . Keywords : quantum walk, uniform mixing, oriented graph, unitary signing.

Introduction

Quantum walk on graphs is a natural quantum analogue of random walk on graphs.
It has proved useful in developing quantum communication protocols (for example, entanglement generation)
and quantum algorithms for important computational problems (for example, Grover search).

Given a graph
X
with adjacency matrix
A(X)
, the continuous-time quantum walk on
X
is given by
the time-varying unitary matrix
U_{X}(t)=\exp(-iA(X)t)
. The mixing matrix
M_{X}(t)
is obtained
from
U_{X}(t)
by taking entry-wise squared norm (which is the quantum mechanical Born rule for
the quantum to classical transition). In uniform mixing at time
t
, we require all entries of
M_{X}(t)

to be the constant
1/n
, where
n
is the number of vertices of
X
.
This implies that if we measure the quantum walk at time
t
, then the corresponding probability
distribution is uniform over the vertices of
X
.

In their seminal result, Moore and Russell
[
20
]
initiated the study of uniform mixing in
continuous-time quantum walks. They observed that a quantum walk on the
n
-cube exactly mixes to
uniform at time
\frac{\pi}{4}n
. In contrast, a classical random walk on the
n
-cube requires
\Omega(n\log n)

to converge to the uniform distribution in the limit. This shows a quantum advantage for uniform mixing
in both the quantitative (faster time) and qualitative (exact mixing versus mixing in the limit) aspects.

Another motivation for exploring uniform mixing is provided by the
W state
(see
[
10
,
5
]
).
For three qubits, the W state is given by

e_{100}+e_{010}+e_{001}
.
Here,
e_{100}
denote the tensor product
e_{1}\otimes e_{0}\otimes e_{0}
where
e_{0}
and
e_{1}

are the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
Note that this can be generalized to
n
qubits and is one of the target states for uniform mixing.
This useful entangled quantum state has interesting applications for secure communication in quantum networks
(such as anonymous transmission
[
17
]
and leader election
[
9
]
).
In certain applications, the W state is known to be more robust compared to the well-known GHZ state

e_{000}+e_{111}
.

It is known that classical random walks rapidly mix on the complete graphs. On the other hand,
Ahmadi
et al.

[
2
]
observed curiously that no complete graph
K_{n}
has uniform mixing except
for
K_{2},K_{3}
, and
K_{4}
. In this work, we show that for any
K_{n}
there is a unitary signing
\sigma

so that
K_{n}^{\sigma}
has uniform mixing in expected
n^{3/2}
time. Our result is based on a simple
stopping rule which reduces uniform mixing to
local
uniform mixing. This is reminiscent of
stopping rules for exact mixing in Markov chains (see
[
18
]
) and is inspired by a similar
technique used in Xie
et al.

[
24
]
(on anti-Zeno effects in perfect state transfer).

As an implicit corollary, we show there is an oriented Hamming graph
H(n,4)
which has uniform mixing
at time
\pi/3\sqrt{3}
. This is
faster
than the mixing times of any unoriented Hamming graphs,
which include
H(n,2)
,
H(n,3)
, and
H(n,4)
, and the Cartesian product of
K_{1,3}
with itself
(which has uniform mixing at time
2\pi/3\sqrt{3}
).

For the second part of this work, we consider

mixing in quantum walks.
The average mixing matrix
\widehat{M}_{X}
is the Cesaro limit of its mixing matrix,

These results suggest an interesting interplay between chirality and group-theoretic properties of

2.1
Quantum walks

Given a graph
X
, let
A(X)
denote its adjacency matrix whose spectral decomposition is

A(X)=\sum_{r}\lambda_{r}E_{r}

where
E_{r}
are the orthogonal projectors onto the
\lambda_{r}
-eigenspace.
The continuous-time quantum walk on
X
is defined as the unitary matrix

U_{X}(t)=\exp(-itA(X))=\sum_{r}e^{-i\lambda_{r}t}E_{r}

where the second equality followed from the spectral theorem.

We observe that quantum walks on switching equivalent graphs are similar.
This holds as

\exp(-itA(X^{\sigma_{2}}))=D^{\dagger}\exp(-itA(X^{\sigma_{1}}))D

(2)

by
Eq.

1
and
D^{\dagger}D=I
.

The
mixing
matrix of a quantum walk is given by

M_{X}(t)=U_{X}(t)\circ\overline{U_{X}(t)}

where
A\circ B
denotes the Schur product of matrices defined by
(A\circ B)_{jk}=A_{jk}B_{jk}
.
Note that the
(a,b)
-entry of
M_{X}(t)
encodes the probability of measuring

b
at time
t
when starting at
a
(according to the Born rule of quantum mechanics).

Definition 1
.

A graph
X
of order
n
has uniform mixing at time
t
if
M_{X}(t)=\frac{1}{n}J_{n}
.
On the other hand, we say
X
has local uniform mixing from vertex
a
at time
t
if

M_{X}(t)e_{a}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbbm{1}_{n}
.

We state a useful property of uniform mixing under Cartesian graph products.

Lemma 1
.

(Moore and Russell
[
20
]
)

If
X
has local uniform mixing from vertex
a
at time
t
and

Y
has local uniform mixing from vertex
b
at time
t
, then

X\Box Y
has local uniform mixing from vertex
(a,b)
at time
t
.

Measured walks

In our quantum walks, we allow partial measurements at a vertex.
Formally, if
a\in V(X)
, the partial measurement relative to vertex
a
is given by
the set
\mathfrak{M}=\{P_{0},P_{1}\}
of projection operators, where

P_{1}=e_{a}e_{a}^{T}\ \ \ P_{0}=I-e_{a}e_{a}^{T}.

If we apply the measurement operator
\mathfrak{M}
to a state

{\psi}=c_{a}e_{a}+\sum_{b\neq a}c_{b}e_{b}
,
where
|c_{a}|^{2}+\sum_{b\neq a}|c_{b}|^{2}=1
,
then the outcome is

\mathfrak{M}{\psi}\mapsto\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{ with probability $|c_{a}|^{2}$ }\\
0&\mbox{ with probability $1-|c_{a}|^{2}$}\end{array}\right.

The corresponding
post-measurement
states for each outcome are given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e_{a}&\mbox{ if outcome is $1$,}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|c_{a}|^{2}}}\sum_{b\neq a}c_{b}e_{b}&\mbox{ if outcome is $0$.}\end{array}\right.

For more information on quantum measurements, see Nielsen and Chuang
[
22
]
(page 87).

We summarize useful facts about equitable partitions for
Hermitian
matrices (see Haemers
[
15
]
).
Let
A
be a
n\times n
Hermitian matrix and let
\pi=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{m}V_{j}
be a partition of
[n]
.
We call each
V_{j}
a cell of
\pi
.
Let
A[V_{j},V_{k}]
be the submatrix of
A
whose rows are indexed by elements in
V_{j}
and whose
columns are indexed by elements in
V_{k}
. Let
n_{j}=|V_{j}|
denote the size of cell
V_{j}
.

Definition 2
.

(Complex equitable partition)
The partition
\pi
is called equitable
if each submatrix
A[V_{j},V_{k}]
has a constant row sum and a constant column sum.
That is, for each
j,k
, there are complex constants
r_{jk},c_{jk}\in\mathbb{C}
so that

\displaystyle A[V_{j},V_{k}]\mathbbm{1}_{n_{k}}

\displaystyle=r_{jk}\mathbbm{1}_{n_{j}}\ \ \ \mbox{ (constant row sum)}

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{n_{j}}^{T}A[V_{j},V_{k}]

\displaystyle=c_{jk}\mathbbm{1}_{n_{k}}^{T}\ \ \ \mbox{ (constant column sum).}

(4)

As
A
is Hermitian,
r_{jk}=\overline{c_{kj}}
. In particular, this implies that
r_{jj}
and
c_{jj}
are both real.
Let
S
be the normalized characteristic
n\times m
matrix of the partition
\pi
where

S_{jk}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{k}}}[\![j\in V_{k}]\!].

(5)

As
S
is the matrix representation of
\pi
, we often refer to
S
as
the
equitable partition.

Lemma 2
.

The characteristic matrix
S
satisfies the following:

(i)

S^{T}S=I_{m}
.

(ii)

SS^{T}
is a block diagonal
m\times m
matrix
\diag(D_{1},\ldots,D_{m})
, where
D_{j}=\frac{1}{n_{j}}J_{n_{j}}
.

(iii)

[SS^{T},A]=0
.

Proof.

(i)
This follows since the
j
th column of
S
is the normalized characteristic vector of
V_{j}

and the subsets
V_{j}
are disjoint.

(ii)
This holds since the outer product of the normalized characteristic vector of
V_{j}

is the normalized all-one matrix
B_{j}=\frac{1}{n_{j}}J_{n_{j}}
.

(iii)
It suffices to show
B_{j}
commutes with
A[V_{j},V_{j}]
for each
j
.
Note
B_{j}A[V_{j},V_{j}]=c_{jj}J_{n_{j}}
and
A[V_{j},V_{j}]B_{j}=r_{jj}J_{n_{j}}
.
As
r_{jj}=c_{jj}
, we are done.
∎

The
quotient
matrix of
A
relative to
\pi
is the
m\times m
matrix defined by

B:=S^{T}AS.

Claim 1
.

The quotient matrix
B
is a Hermitian
m\times m
matrix whose
jk
-entry is given by

B_{jk}=\sqrt{|r_{jk}||c_{jk}|}\ e^{i\Theta}

where
\Theta=\Arg(r_{jk})=\Arg(c_{jk})
.

Proof.

The
jk
-entry of
B
is given by

B_{jk}=e_{j}^{T}(S^{T}AS)e_{k}=(Se_{j})^{T}A(Se_{k}).

Therefore, as
Se_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{j}}}\mathbbm{1}_{n_{j}}
and similarly for
Se_{k}
, we have

B_{jk}=c_{jk}\sqrt{\frac{n_{k}}{n_{j}}}=r_{jk}\sqrt{\frac{n_{j}}{n_{k}}}.

This shows that
r_{jk}
and
c_{jk}
have the same principal argument, that is,
\Arg(r_{jk})=\Arg(c_{jk})
,
since
n_{j}
and
n_{k}
are both real.
Also, since
n_{j}r_{jk}=n_{k}c_{jk}
, we have

B_{jk}=\sqrt{|r_{jk}||c_{jk}|}\ e^{i\Arg(r_{jk})}.

To show
B
is Hermitian, note that

B_{kj}=\sqrt{|r_{kj}||c_{kj}|}\ e^{i\Arg(r_{kj})}=\sqrt{|c_{jk}||r_{jk}|}\ e^{-i\Arg(r_{jk})}=\overline{B_{jk}}

as
r_{kj}=\overline{r_{jk}}
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

The quantum walk on
B
and the quantum walk on

are related as follows:

e^{-iBt}=S^{T}e^{-iAt}S.

Moreover, if
V_{i}=\{u\}
and

The first claim holds since

.
The second claim follows from

Theorem 2
.

Let
X
be a graph of order
n
whose Hermitian adjacency matrix
A
has zero as a simple eigenvalue
with the corresponding eigenvector
\mathbbm{1}_{n}
.
Then,
\widehat{X}
has uniform mixing in expected
n^{3/2}
time.

Proof.

The adjacency matrix of
\widehat{X}
is given by

A(\widehat{X})=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbbm{1}_{n}^{T}\\
\mathbbm{1}_{n}&A(X)\end{pmatrix}

whose eigenvalues are
\pm\sqrt{n}
and
\theta\in\Sp(A(X))
with corresponding eigenvectors

\psi_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{n}\end{pmatrix},\ \ \ \psi_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
z_{\theta}\end{pmatrix}

where
z_{\theta}
is a normalized eigenvector of
A(X)
corresponding to a nonzero eigenvalue
\theta
.

In the quantum walk, the start vertex is either conical or not.
If the start vertex is the conical vertex
0
, then

e^{-iA(\widehat{X})t}e_{0}=\sum_{\pm}\frac{e^{\mp i\sqrt{n}t}}{\sqrt{2}}\psi_{\pm}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\sqrt{n}t)\\
-\frac{i}{\sqrt{n}}\sin(\sqrt{n}t)\mathbbm{1}_{n}\end{pmatrix}

which shows uniform mixing at
t_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\arccos(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}})\approx\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{n}}
.

On the other hand, if the start vertex is not conical, say
e_{u}
, then:

e^{-iA(\widehat{X})t}e_{u}=\sum_{\pm}\frac{\pm e^{\mp i\sqrt{n}t}}{\sqrt{2n}}\psi_{\pm}+\sum_{\theta\neq 0}\langle e_{u},\psi_{\theta}\rangle e^{-i\theta t}\psi_{\theta}.

This implies that the amplitude at the conical vertex is given by

\langle e_{0},e^{-iA(\widehat{X})t}e_{u}\rangle=\sum_{\pm}\frac{\pm e^{\mp i\sqrt{n}t}}{2\sqrt{n}}=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{n}}\sin(\sqrt{n}t).

By linearity, this extends to any normalized vector that is orthogonal to
e_{0}
.
At time
t_{1}=\pi/2\sqrt{n}
, the quantum walk is at the conical vertex with probability
1/n
.
Thus, if we perform a partial measurement
\{P_{0}=e_{0}e_{0}^{T},P_{1}=I-e_{0}e_{0}^{T}\}
, the expected waiting time
for the measurement to return
P_{0}
is
n
. Once the quantum walk arrives at the conical vertex, we appeal to the first case
and will achieve uniform mixing after time
t_{0}
.
Let

be the random variable for the total time until uniform mixing. Then,

\mathbb{E}[T]=n\times\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{n}}+t_{0}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n}).

But, after scaling the adjacency matrix by
n
(so that it has bounded norm), we obtain a uniform mixing time of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3/2})
.
∎

Given an oriented graph

Claim 2

There is a signing
\sigma
of
K_{4}
so that
K_{4}^{\sigma}
has uniform mixing at
\pi/3\sqrt{3}
.

Proof.

We sign
K_{4}
using
\sigma
as follows:

A(K_{4}^{\sigma})=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i&-i&-i\\
i&0&-i&i\\
i&i&0&-i\\
i&-i&i&0\end{pmatrix}.

Observe that
K_{4}^{\sigma}
is switching equivalent to
K_{1}+\vec{K}_{3}
(by a
-i

But,
K_{1}+\vec{K}_{3}
and

have the same quotient under an equitable partition which places the conical

3

Finally, observe that

has a switching automorphism that sends the conical vertex to any other vertex.

This is a speedup of Zhan’s observation that

5
Average mixing

The
average mixing
matrix
\widehat{M}_{X}
of a graph
X
is the Cesaro limit of its mixing matrix
M_{X}(t)
.
That is,
\widehat{M}_{X}
is given by

\widehat{M}_{X}=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}M_{X}(t)dt

Using the spectral theorem, we have that (see
[
11
,
7
]
)

\widehat{M}_{X}=\sum_{r}E_{r}\circ\overline{E}_{r}.

(6)

As
\widehat{M}_{X}
is a doubly stochastic matrix, it is a Markov chain constructed from taking
the ergodic limit of a continuous-time
quantum
process
U_{X}(t)
.

Definition 3
.

A graph
X
of order
n
has average uniform mixing if
\widehat{M}_{X}=\frac{1}{n}J_{n}
.

Godsil proved the following strong
No-Go
theorem for average uniform mixing on graphs.

Theorem 4
.

(Godsil
[
11
]
)
A graph
X
has average uniform mixing if and only if
X=K_{2}
.

In stark contrast to
Theorem

4
, we show there are infinite families of oriented graphs with average
uniform mixing.

Theorem 5
.

Any
(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
-circulant with distinct eigenvalues has average uniform mixing.

Proof.

Let
X
be a circulant of order
n
with adjacency matrix
A(X)=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\lambda_{r}E_{r}
, where
the eigenvalues
\lambda_{r}
are all distinct, and

E_{r}=|\zeta_{r}\rangle\langle\zeta_{r}|
where
|\zeta_{r}\rangle
is the
r
-th column of the Fourier matrix
whose
j
-th entry is
\langle j|\zeta_{r}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\zeta_{n}^{jr}
, for
j,r=0,1,\ldots,n-1
.
Then,

\widehat{M}_{X}=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}E_{r}\circ\overline{E}_{r}=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{n^{2}}J_{n}=\frac{1}{n}J_{n}

as
\overline{E}_{r}=E_{-r}
.
∎

Recall we call a graph
oriented
if its edges have
\{\pm i\}
weights.

Corollary 5
.

The oriented odd-cycle has average uniform mixing.

Proof.

The eigenvalues of an oriented cycle

of order

are
\lambda_{k}=2\sin(2\pi k/n)
, for

,
which are all distinct when
n
is odd. As
\vec{C}_{n}
is a
(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})

5.1
Non-abelian No-Go theorem

Next, we consider Cayley graphs of non-abelian groups.
We will appeal to the continuous-time version of a result of Godsil and Zhan (see
[
13
]
, Theorem 10.4).

Lemma 4
.

Given any graph
X
of order
n
with Hermitian adjacency matrix
A=\sum_{r}\lambda_{r}E_{r}
, we have

\Tr\widehat{M}_{X}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{d}m_{k}^{2}\ \geq\ 1

where
m_{r}
is the multiplicity of
\lambda_{r}
. Moreover, equality is achieved if each
E_{r}
has constant diagonal
and each eigenvalue is simple.

Proof.

By
Eq.

6
, we have

\Tr\widehat{M}_{X}\ =\ \sum_{r}\Tr(E_{r}\circ\overline{E_{r}})\ =\ \sum_{r}\sum_{a}|(E_{r})_{a,a}|^{2}.

Apply Cauchy-Schwarz on the inner sum to get

\sum_{a}|(E_{r})_{a,a}|^{2}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{a}(E_{r})_{a,a}\right)^{2}.

But,
\Tr(E_{r})=\sum_{a}(E_{r})_{a,a}=m_{r}
, the multiplicity of eigenvalue
\lambda_{r}
. Therefore,

\Tr\widehat{M}_{X}\ \geq\ \frac{1}{n}m_{r}^{2}\ \geq\ 1,

where the last inequality follows by a standard optimization argument.
∎

If
\widehat{M}_{X}=\frac{1}{n}J_{n}
, then its trace achieves the lower bound of
1
.

For an irreducible representation
\rho
of a finite group

be
the degree (or dimension) of
\rho

Theorem 6
.

(Babai
[
4
]
)

Let
X=\Gamma(G,S)
be a Cayley graph over a finite group

with a connection set

where

S=S^{-1}
(

is closed under taking inverses) and

(

has no self-loops).
Then,

1.

A(X)=\sum_{g\in S}R(g)
where

is the left-regular representation of

.

2.

There is a unitary matrix

so that

is a diagonal matrix with entries

(repeated

3.

The eigenvalues of

are given by the eigenvalues of
\sum_{g\in S}R_{\rho}(g)
(each repeated

Let

be the Cayley graph of a nonabelian finite group

Any nonabelian finite group has an irreducible representation of degree larger than one.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced a reduction from global to
local
uniform mixing using
weak measurements. This is similar to the reduction from perfect state transfer to
fractional revival (see
[
24
]
). It can also be viewed as a
stopping rule
for uniform mixing
in quantum walks. We then combined this technique with
unitary signing
of graphs (see
[
3
]
).
Using this sign-and-reduce technique, we show that there are oriented complete graphs
with probabilistic (Las Vegas) uniform mixing. This complements the fact that no complete graph
has uniform mixing (except
K_{2},K_{3},K_{4}
).

On the other hand, by unitary signing alone, we found families of oriented circulants with
average uniform mixing which is a No-Go violation of Godsil’s theorem
[
11
]
.
Here, chirality helps construct oriented graphs with
distinct
eigenvalues
in
(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})
-circulants. But, we identified a barrier towards extending this
to nonabelian groups (due to a basic fact from group representation).

We conclude with a few questions for future explorations:

(a)

Are there high-diameter trees with probabilistic uniform mixing?

(b)

Is there a speed limit for uniform mixing? In particular, can the methods developed by
Luby
et al.

[
19
]
be used to optimized our Las Vegas algorithm?

(c)

Are there graphs with fast Monte Carlo uniform mixing?
Here, we require algorithms with bounded running time but which achieves
\epsilon
-uniform mixing
for some negligible
\epsilon>0
.
