TimeX: Phenotype Onset Extraction from Clinical Narratives
Authors: Fangyi Chen, Shiyi Jiang, Quan M. Nguyen, Casey N. Ta, Kai Wang, Wendy K. Chung, Chunhua Weng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Disease phenotype onset is critical for timely and accurate diagnosis and clinical decision-making, yet it remains poorly characterized in the literature. Estimating phenotype onset using electronic health record (EHR) data holds promise but remains challenging. Researchers often resort to EHR documentation timestamps as proxies for phenotype onset, which can be inaccurate. Conventional natural language processing (NLP) approaches suffer from limited scalability and generalizability, and struggle to interpret implicit or vague temporal expressions. To address these gaps, we introduce TimeX, a novel open-source pipeline that leverages Llama-3.1, using instruction-based prompting for extracting phenotype onset from clinical narratives. TimeX employs a modular workflow comprising family history filtering, phenotype extraction, negation handling, and temporal information extraction to estimate phenotype onset. It yielded an average accuracy of 81.24% in timestamp extraction using 102 manually annotated clinical notes from the Columbia University Irving Medical Center, substantially outperforming all five baselines by at least 14.86%. A case study of four rare disease cohorts revealed that narrative-derived phenotype onset is more precise than that based on documentation timestamps. TimeX supports accurate and scalable phenotype onset extraction, with the potential to enable more precise disease trajectory characterization and timely disease diagnosis.

Introduction

Electronic health record (EHR) data are a rich resource for analyzing phenotypes, identifying early risks, and supporting timely diagnosis of diseases. Traditional phenotyping efforts primarily focus on what symptoms or conditions are present, while largely overlooking when they emerge, evolve, or resolve1–4. Understanding when a phenotype first appears provides key diagnostic and prognostic insights, aiding differential diagnosis, severity assessment, and treatment planning5–7. However, extracting phenotype onset from EHRs remains a significant hurdle, as temporal information in EHRs is often implicit, inconsistently documented, and embedded within unstructured narratives8. Documentation timestamps are often used as surrogates for disease onset; however, they reflect when events are recorded rather than when they occur, introducing systematic errors that can distort event sequencing, bias time-to-event analyses, and undermine model validity9–12. Instead of relying solely on recorded timestamps, we hypothesize that temporal information extracted from narrative notes may provide a more accurate representation of the true onset and progression of patient symptoms and clinical events.

Temporal information extraction (TIE) has been a longstanding challenge in natural language processing, especially for the tasks of automatic identification and normalization of temporal expressions and their association with events in text13. Such pipelines usually perform three sequential subtasks: detecting medical events, extracting and normalizing temporal expressions, and establishing temporal relations to support chronological reasoning. Early foundational work by Zhou et al. proposed one of the first comprehensive architectures for TIE and reasoning in EHRs14. The pipeline integrated temporal tagging, event encoding, and constraint-based reasoning to model clinical narratives and continues to influence modern clinical TIE systems.

Early TIE approaches15–19 combined rule-based heuristics (e.g., regular expressions, pattern matching) with machine-learning models (e.g., conditional random fields, support vector machines). These hybrid systems achieved moderate success but required extensive feature engineering and domain-specific annotation, limiting their scalability and generalizability among different diseases and institutions. Subsequently, the emergence of deep learning and transformer-based architectures (e.g., BioBERT20, ClinicalBERT21) substantially improved performance in entity recognition and temporal relation extraction by learning contextualized representations that capture event semantics and temporal dependencies22–28. Nevertheless, these models still face persistent challenges, including the reliance on large amounts of annotated datasets, difficulty in reasoning over implicit temporal cues, and limitations in modeling long-range document-level dependencies.

Recently, large language models (LLMs) have become the most powerful tools capable of performing diverse tasks, including text summarization, text generation, and question answering29–31. There have been some studies that leverage LLMs to extract phenotypes or medical concepts from clinical narratives32–34. However, using LLMs for TIE has not received much attention. Despite their success across general-domain applications, it remains unclear how effectively LLMs can identify, interpret, and reason over the ambiguous and context-dependent temporal expressions in clinical text.

In this paper, we present TimeX, a lightweight, LLM-based pipeline for extracting phenotype onset from clinical narratives. Although current knowledge bases such as Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO)35 incorporate information on phenotype onset from existing literature, they remain scarce (only 1.2% of the phenotypic abnormality annotations had onset information36) and lack the fine-grained temporal granularity necessary for comprehensive clinical interpretation or modeling. To the best of our knowledge, TimeX is the first open-source, portable, end-to-end system specifically designed for temporal phenotype onset extraction. Our proposed system, TimeX, as shown in Fig. 1, consists of the following key modules: (1) data preprocessing, (2) phenotype extraction, (3) temporal expression extraction and normalization, and (4) phenotype onset estimation. In a case study, we applied TimeX to four rare disease cohorts to characterize systematic discrepancies between phenotype onset times estimated from narrative text and corresponding documentation timestamps. These findings show that using clinical narratives to extract onset information can more accurately approximate true clinical timelines. Case study on rare disease cohorts

Hundreds of millions of people worldwide suffer from rare diseases that often cause chronic morbidity, significantly reducing the quality of life37,38. Individuals with rare diseases often experience prolonged diagnostic delays, typically with an average of 5 years between symptom onset and confirmed diagnosis, and a substantial proportion of patients remain undiagnosed. Early identification of phenotypic manifestations is thus crucial in streamlining rare disease diagnosis and optimizing subsequent clinical management. In this study, we applied TimeX to four rare disease cohorts as a case study to demonstrate its robustness in identifying phenotype onset timestamps and its potential to improve rare disease diagnosis and management.

We focused on rare diseases that emerge or are typically diagnosed only after adulthood. Four rare diseases were selected for investigation of their phenotype onsets due to their urgent need for early diagnosis and reduction in misdiagnosis and delayed diagnosis, including Behcet syndrome, Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension (PAH), Fabry disease, and Whipple’s disease39–42. Detailed definitions of the four rare disease cohorts are provided in Supplementary Data 1–4.

For the cohort of Behcet syndrome, we selected all clinical notes that fall into the top 15 note types (e.g., Surgical Path Event, Follow-up Visit, and Letter) that contain the most frequent occurrence of phenotypes based on prior literature43. A full list of selected note types is detailed in Supplementary Table 4. For the PAH cohort, we limited the selection to a maximum of five notes per patient, drawn from the top five note-type categories to reduce computational burden. All available notes were included for Fabry and Whipple’s disease cohorts due to the small sample size.

Among the four cohorts, the PAH cohort included the largest number of patients and clinical notes, comprising 9363 patients with a total of 24,700 notes. To ensure that TimeX performs at the same accuracy level on rare disease cohorts, we randomly sampled 30 narrative notes from each cohort, resulting in a total of 120 notes and 113 distinct phenotypes. TimeX achieved an accuracy of 85.87% (precision: 0.81, recall: 0.62, and F1 score: 0.7) in temporal expression extraction and an accuracy of 83.39% (95% CI: 79.15–87.29%) in temporal expression normalization. Based on the results, TimeX showed consistent performance of TIE and normalization in rare disease cohorts.

Across the four cohorts shown in Fig. 3, we observed that approximately 75-85% phenotype terms extracted from clinical narratives lacked temporal information. Among terms with temporal attributes, times were mainly expressed implicitly, such as “2 weeks ago” and “several months ago.” A limited number of timestamps were expressed explicitly but may also lack the granularity needed for disease progression modeling, such as “in 2015” and “in Sep 2010.” The significant amount of relative time expressions within the notes suggested that it is imperative to extract and reason for phenotype onset information instead of relying on the documentation timestamps. Discussion

In this study, we developed and evaluated an open-source, LLM-based temporal phenotype extraction pipeline that accurately identifies and normalizes temporal information of phenotypes from clinical notes. TimeX significantly outperformed the baseline. It effectively retained only the patient’s medical conditions by removing family history, achieving precise phenotype extraction. In contrast, the baseline frequently mistaken family history as the patient’s own conditions, leading to false positives. It also showed limited ability to infer implicit temporal expressions, often defaulting to the documentation datetime. We evaluated the performance of TimeX on extracting explicit timestamps such as “in 2005” and “on 02/08/15,” and implicit timestamps such as “2 days ago” and “over a week.” TimeX achieved an accuracy of 78.38% (95% CI: 64.8–91.89%) in extracting explicit temporal expressions and 70.31% (95% CI: 64.19–75.98%) accuracy for implicit timestamp extraction. Moreover, the baseline model constantly fabricated timestamps even when no associated temporal information was present in the text.

Additionally, we observed that the lightweight LLMs have difficulty in interpreting lengthy, complex clinical narratives when identifying the temporal expressions associated with a specific phenotype. This echoes a well-known issue: LLMs struggle with long contexts. To systematically evaluate this effect, we performed additional experiments to assess the impact of note length on the performance of TimeX. Clinical notes were stratified into short- and long-text groups using multiple character-length thresholds, and performance in TIE and normalization was evaluated across these strata (Supplementary Fig. 1). TimeX demonstrated improved performance on notes shorter than 3000 characters compared to longer notes, suggesting that the segmentation-based approach effectively reduces irrelevant information and enhances model focus. However, this strategy did not yield additional performance gain for notes shorter than 2500 characters, indicating a limitation in capturing cross-sentence event–time relationships when relevant temporal cues extend beyond the selected local context or require additional contextual information for accurate inference. Future work will focus on more advanced methods for identifying temporally relevant spans across entire documents, including intra-document retrieval strategies44 that prioritize semantically relevant sentences while preserving temporally informative evidence distributed across distant text segments. In parallel, we will investigate task-aware summarization approaches, such as phenotype- and temporally conditioned summarization to generate compact representations of clinically salient information while maintaining critical event–time relationships. These representations may improve both the accuracy and efficiency of TIE and normalization. While further refinement is needed, this study establishes an important foundation for automated temporal phenotype extraction and demonstrates the feasibility of leveraging LLMs to capture complex temporal patterns in clinical narratives.

Besides its demonstrated performance and portability, TimeX offers a robust foundation for temporal phenotyping for diverse diseases, enabling more accurate disease characterization, better understanding of disease trajectories, earlier diagnosis, and improved patient care. Our analysis revealed notable discrepancies between documentation time in both structured data and narratives and phenotype onset estimated from narratives, indicating that documentation timestamps alone may underestimate phenotype onset. Incorporating temporal information from unstructured clinical narratives can thus improve onset estimation and potentially facilitate accurate modeling of disease progression. In rare diseases, where diagnostic delays are common due to heterogeneous symptoms and limited awareness, TimeX identified several phenotypes appearing prior to formal diagnosis, suggesting their potential as early indicators. For instance, oral ulcer and skin rash typically emerge over 5 years before the average diagnosis age of Behcet syndrome45. Recognizing such early features could meaningfully accelerate diagnosis and mitigate disease progression risk.

Despite the promising performance of LLMs in temporal phenotyping, several challenges remain. Much of the phenotype onset information is implicitly stated or missing in clinical notes, leading to uncertainty in estimating onset times. Although we integrated complementary data sources such as structured EHR fields and note timestamps to address this issue, future work could incorporate additional modalities, such as laboratory results, encounter details, and medication records, to further improve temporal estimation. This finding should be interpreted with caution, as the observation that narrative-derived onset times may be more precise than documentation timestamps was supported by validation on a limited subset of cases. Larger-scale evaluation with rigorously annotated ground truth is needed to confirm its generalizability and robustness. Lastly, further evaluation on downstream tasks such as disease prediction is needed to assess the clinical utility of TimeX in the real-world setting.

We present TimeX, a generalizable, LLM-based pipeline for identifying temporal phenotype onset information from clinical narratives. Using four rare disease cohorts, we consistently observed that the onset information extracted from clinical notes is more accurate than documentation timestamps. TimeX demonstrates strong accuracy in phenotype onset extraction. Further fine-tuning of LLMs and investigation of phenotype-specific temporal patterns are needed to advance insights into disease progression over time.

Methods

The study was approved by Columbia University Irving Medical Center (CUIMC) Institutional Review Board under protocol number AAAR3954. Data used in this study were obtained from the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership database at CUIMC, which contains structured and unstructured EHRs of all patients from 1985 to 2025.

Data preprocessing

To avoid confounding phenotype extraction with hereditary context, sentences describing family history were removed from the raw clinical notes (e.g., “father with asthma and myocardial infarction”) using the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct model46. The model was guided through a step-by-step instruction process with an explicit definition of family history in the clinical context and detailed guidance for locating relevant content, as provided in Supplementary Table 1. After filtering family history, the remaining content of each note was then passed to the following phenotype extraction module for further processing.

Phenotype extraction

After family history removal, we extracted phenotypes from processed clinical narratives. For this task, we adopted PhenoBERT23, a state-of-the-art transformer-based model designed for phenotype extraction and normalization to HPO35. PhenoBERT demonstrated strong performance in identifying phenotype mentions across diverse clinical narratives while maintaining computational efficiency23. Phenotype negation detection was performed using the trained BERT-based model from Aken et al.47, which effectively distinguishes affirmed from negated clinical concepts. Additionally, an optional post-process step can be implemented to filter non-targeted phenotypes by leveraging external knowledge bases (e.g., HPO or curated disease-phenotype resources43) to select desired phenotypes for downstream analyses. For instance, scleroderma extracted by PhenoBERT from notes will be excluded if the desired cohort is patients with Behcet syndrome, as scleroderma is not associated with Behcet syndrome according to HPO.

Temporal expression extraction and normalization

This module is intended to identify and normalize temporal expressions that are explicitly or implicitly associated with phenotypes mentioned in clinical narratives. For this task, we employed the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct foundation model46. Two implementation strategies were developed. In the first approach, the full-length clinical text was used as the input alongside the corresponding HPO terms. Temporal extraction was guided through an instruction-based prompting pipeline to detect and normalize time expressions associated with phenotypes.

In the second approach, we extracted a specific segment from the clinical text for each HPO term. Each segment was then processed using an instruction-based prompt combined with a hidden CoT strategy, in which the model was guided through sequential reasoning steps to locate relevant temporal expressions, estimate timestamps from the expressions, and normalize timestamps into a standardized format. To generate these segments, we extracted text spans corresponding to individual HPO phenotype terms. Specifically, for each clinical note, we first located all target sentences that contain the target HPO term. We then extracted each of the target sentences along with its preceding three sentences and its following two sentences as the desired segment of the note. The extracted timestamps were subsequently normalized to the ISO 8601 format (YYYY-MM-DD) to ensure consistency in phenotype onset estimation across downstream analyses. Temporal expressions in clinical narratives often vary in both format and granularity; thus, we established a set of normalization rules to standardize them. Specifically, explicit temporal expressions referring to a particular month or year were normalized to the first day of the month or year. For instance, the expression “in March 2010” was normalized to 2010-03-01, and the expression “in 2015” was converted to 2015-01-01. Workflow evaluation

To ensure the accuracy of the proposed workflow, we conducted a manual evaluation for two key components: (1) the accuracy, precision, recall (sensitivity), F1 score, and specificity of identifying and removing sentences describing family history, (2) the accuracy, precision, recall (sensitivity), F1 score, and specificity of temporal expression extraction at the span-level, and (3) the accuracy with 95% confidence interval (CI) of the extracted timestamps in the specified normalized format associated with phenotype mentions. Phenotype onset estimation

Phenotype onset was estimated by integrating information from multiple sources, including condition start times recorded from structured EHR data, note documentation datetimes, and phenotype timestamps extracted from clinical narratives. Structured conditions were mapped from Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine - Clinical Terms (SNOMED-CT)52 to HPO terms using PhenoBERT. Normalized timestamps were converted to age since the date of birth. For each phenotype per patient, the earliest onset identified across the three sources was used as the final onset estimate.

Documented note datetimes were retrieved for comparison with extracted phenotype timestamps. We applied quality control measures that excluded timestamps occurring before a patient’s birthdate or after the corresponding note documentation time, as well as outliers with values in age greater than three times the standard deviation. To assess the temporal discrepancies and identify significant differences among the onset estimates from documentation-based, narrative-based, and aggregated sources, we conducted the Friedman test followed by Dunn’s post hoc pairwise test with multiple-comparison correction53,54.

Baseline model

We compared TimeX against a CoT step-by-step baseline, adapted from recent temporal question-answering work that evaluates temporal reasoning using the “when”-type template55. In this setting, a general-purpose LLM receives a list of timestamped events and a temporal query (e.g., “When was the last time [phenotype] was documented?”). Instead of relying on a series of structured events, this baseline directly uses clinical notes as input, reflecting a more realistic EHR context where temporal information is often embedded within the free-text narratives. In our study, the model received each clinical note as input along with the target phenotype and was prompted with a “when”-type temporal question (e.g., “When did the patient initially experience chest pain?”). The prompt incorporated the instruction “Let’s think step by step” to elicit sequential reasoning, guiding the model to identify relevant temporal expressions within the note, interpret their contextual meaning (e.g., symptom onset, duration, or recurrence), and normalize the inferred date to the ISO 8601 format (YYYY-MM-DD). Additionally, we compared TimeX with a standard benchmark for TIE and normalization (SUTime)56. Besides, approaches using state-of-the-art open-source LLMs (Gemma2-27B57, Mistral-7B58, and GPT-OSS-20B59) were used as baselines as well.
