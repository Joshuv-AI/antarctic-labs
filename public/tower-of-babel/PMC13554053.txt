A novel asymmetric thirteen level inverter for medium voltage applications
Authors: D Anil Kumar, Lakshmi Prasanna, Ch Nayak Bhukya, Pappala Vamsi Krishna, P M Venkatesh, K Arunyuvaraj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultilevel inverters (MLIs) are widely employed in medium-voltage and high-power applications because of their ability to enhance output waveform quality and reduce the voltage stress imposed on power semiconductor devices. Nevertheless, many existing MLI configurations require a large number of switches and auxiliary components, which increases system complexity, implementation cost, and overall device stress. This study presents a compact and efficient multilevel inverter topology that addresses these limitations while ensuring reliable operation and improved power quality. The proposed inverter is a novel asymmetric triple-source thirteen-level topology that utilizes only ten unidirectional switches and three isolated DC sources. A level-shifted pulse width modulation (LS-PWM) scheme is adopted to generate the required switching signals. The performance of the proposed configuration is investigated through MATLAB/Simulink simulations, thermal assessment using the PLECS platform, and experimental verification using a DSP-based prototype. The developed inverter produces thirteen distinct voltage levels while maintaining a low total standing voltage (TSV) of 4 p.u., thereby reducing switch voltage stress and enhancing device utilization. Thermal analysis demonstrates a peak efficiency of 96.8%, whereas the multilevel output structure contributes to improved harmonic performance and superior output voltage quality. The close agreement between simulation and experimental results validates the effectiveness, reliability, and practical applicability of the proposed topology for renewable energy systems and medium-voltage power conversion applications.Keywords: Multi-level inverter, Level shifted pulse width modulation, Total standing voltage, Total harmonic distortion, Reduced switch count, Triple source thirteen levelSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

The demand for reliable, efficient, and uninterrupted power supply has increased significantly across diverse sectors, including industrial systems, public transportation, communication networks, aviation, traction, battery storage, and residential applications. This growth is primarily driven by rising global energy consumption and rapid technological advancements. To address these requirements, efficient and controllable power conversion systems are essential. Early power control devices, such as thyratrons, mercury-arc rectifiers, magnetic amplifiers, and rheostat controllers, were limited by large size, high cost, poor efficiency, and reduced reliability. The introduction of self-commutating semiconductor devices marked a major transition, enabling the development of modern power electronic converters with improved performance and flexibility.

In many applications, conversion between direct current and alternating current is a fundamental requirement, particularly in systems involving inverters and rectifiers. The rapid integration of renewable energy sources, along with the expansion of smart grids and electrified transportation, has accelerated the adoption of advanced power electronic converters in applications such as HVDC transmission, microgrids, FACTS devices, electrical drives, electric vehicles, and smart energy systems1–3. Among various converter technologies, MLI have gained significant attention due to their ability to generate high-quality output voltage with reduced harmonic distortion and lower switching losses1,2. Classical MLI topologies, including flying capacitor (FC), neutral point clamped (NPC), and cascaded H-bridge (CHB) inverters, have been widely studied and implemented in medium- and high-power applications4,5.

Despite their advantages, conventional MLI topologies exhibit several limitations, including a high number of semiconductor devices, complex control strategies, capacitor voltage balancing challenges, and increased system cost and size. To overcome these issues, extensive research has focused on reduced device count multilevel inverters (RDC-MLIs), hybrid structures, and switched-capacitor-based configurations6–11. These approaches aim to improve efficiency and compactness while maintaining high-quality output performance. However, conventional single- and multi-source MLIs still require a significant number of components, such as diodes, capacitors, and switches, which increases system complexity. In cascaded configurations, the symmetric or asymmetric behaviour depends on the ratio of isolated DC sources, while switched-capacitor-based designs often require multiple switches for voltage level generation. Additionally, many topologies require two switches to withstand peak output voltage, leading to increased device stress.

Several recent studies have addressed these challenges by proposing innovative MLI configurations. The topologies reported in12–15 reduce the standing voltage across switches; however, they require bidirectional switches and additional H-bridge circuits, increasing the overall component rating and complexity. Advanced designs in16,17 demonstrate higher voltage level generation with fewer components. A cascaded switched-capacitor-based MLI is introduced in18, where front-end and back-end stages are combined using different sources. Similarly, the work in19 demonstrates a 13-level output using cascaded H-bridge units, although polarity reversal is achieved through additional circuitry. The packed E-cell (PEC) topology proposed in20 provides self-voltage balancing and boosting capability but requires continuous DC-link voltage monitoring. An improved asymmetric 13-level topology is presented in21, while cascaded sub-module configurations in22–24 reduce switch count and DC source requirements. However, most of these designs rely on back-end H-bridge circuits for polarity generation, resulting in increased voltage stress on switches25.

Recent advances in multilevel inverter technology have focused on improving output voltage quality while reducing component count, voltage stress, and implementation cost. Several innovative configurations, including pencil-shaped MLIs26, diamond-shaped MLIs27, cross-switched neutral-point-clamped (CSNPC) and neutral-point-clamped-based MLIs28, asymmetric T-type MLIs29, and switched-capacitor-based MLIs30,31, have been reported in the recent literature. These topologies offer advantages such as enhanced voltage boosting capability, improved harmonic performance, and increased power density. However, many of these structures require additional passive components, voltage-balancing circuits, clamping devices, or a larger number of semiconductor switches, which increase design complexity and implementation cost. Switched-capacitor-based topologies can achieve voltage gain enhancement without auxiliary converters; nevertheless, capacitor charging, voltage balancing, and long-term reliability remain important challenges30,31. Similarly, CSNPC and T-type configurations can reduce conduction losses and improve voltage utilization, but they generally involve more complex control strategies and higher component requirements28,29. For medium-voltage applications, several recently reported topologies rely on auxiliary balancing networks or additional polarity-generation stages, leading to increased TSV and overall converter complexity29–31. Consequently, the development of compact multilevel inverter topologies capable of generating a greater number of voltage levels with fewer components, reduced TSV, and simplified control continues to be an important research direction.

Further developments include asymmetric topologies that eliminate additional DC sources and capacitors while improving performance32. Diode-based and packed H-bridge configurations are explored in33–36, whereas cross-connected and switched-diode structures are proposed to enhance voltage level generation with reduced components37,38. Despite these advancements, many MLI designs still suffer from increased complexity, higher cost, and the requirement of multiple passive and active components39,40. The use of geometric progression in DC source magnitudes is a common approach to increase voltage levels in cascaded structures41–46. In addition, advanced applications such as power electronic transformers47, harmonic suppression techniques48, and intelligent forecasting methods using machine learning49 further highlight the growing importance of efficient power conversion systems. Thermal management considerations, as discussed in50, also play a crucial role in ensuring system reliability and performance.

Despite significant progress, existing MLI topologies continue to face key challenges, including high switch count, increased TSV, dependence on additional polarity generation circuits, and complex control requirements. These limitations restrict their practical implementation in compact, cost-effective, and high-efficiency systems. Therefore, there is a clear need for a simplified MLI topology capable of generating higher voltage levels with fewer components, reduced TSV, and improved operational efficiency.

Asymmetric multilevel inverters have emerged as a promising solution for medium-voltage applications because they can generate a larger number of output voltage levels using a reduced number of semiconductor devices and isolated DC sources, resulting in improved voltage resolution and a more compact converter structure51,52. Nevertheless, the availability and effective management of unequal DC-link voltages remain important considerations for practical deployment53. In renewable-energy-based systems, these voltage levels can be naturally obtained from photovoltaic arrays, battery storage units, fuel-cell stacks, and hybrid energy-storage systems operating at different voltage ratings53,54. As a result, asymmetric configurations offer an efficient approach to increasing voltage levels while reducing the overall component count. In addition, lowering the TSV across the power switches helps reduce device voltage stress and enhances the suitability of the inverter for medium-voltage and high-power applications51,54. These advantages have motivated continued research into asymmetric MLI topologies with simplified architectures, improved reliability, and enhanced cost-effectiveness.

To address these challenges, this work proposes a novel single-phase asymmetrical multilevel inverter topology designed to minimize the number of power devices involved in the conduction path. The proposed inverter is capable of generating thirteen voltage levels (13L) using a reduced number of switches and DC sources, making it suitable for low-voltage applications such as photovoltaic systems, electric vehicles, and other high power quality applications. The developed thirteen-level inverter topology does not utilize switched capacitors, flying capacitors, or DC-link balancing capacitors for voltage-level generation. Consequently, capacitor sizing, energy-storage design, and voltage-balancing calculations are not applicable to the proposed converter. By eliminating capacitor-based voltage boosting and balancing networks, the proposed topology achieves a simpler structure with reduced component count, lower implementation cost, and enhanced operational reliability.

Modulation scheme

Figure 4 illustrates the implementation of the level-shifted pulse width modulation (LSPWM) strategy in the proposed inverter topology. In this scheme, the switching pulses are generated by comparing a single sinusoidal reference signal with multiple level-shifted carrier signals. Specifically, twelve carrier waveforms are arranged in a vertically shifted manner to span the required voltage levels, enabling multilevel operation. The intersection of the reference signal with each carrier waveform determines the switching instants, thereby producing the gating signals for the power semiconductor devices.

where and denote the amplitudes of the reference and carrier signals, respectively. This formulation ensures proper scaling of the reference signal relative to the total carrier band, thereby maintaining linear modulation and minimizing harmonic distortion.

Through the comparison process, twelve distinct pulse signals are generated. During the positive half-cycle of the reference waveform, pulse generation is achieved by comparing the reference signal with the upper set of carriers . Conversely, during the negative half-cycle, the lower set of carriers is utilized to produce the corresponding pulses. This structured separation ensures symmetrical operation and balanced switching across all voltage levels.

Figure 5 presents the logical implementation used to translate these pulses into switching commands for the inverter switches . The generated pulses are processed through combinational logic circuits, where specific pulse combinations are assigned to each switch based on the switching states defined in Table 1. Logic gates are employed to group the pulses corresponding to required voltage levels, while complementary signals are produced using NOT gates to ensure proper operation of paired switches. This approach guarantees correct switching sequences, avoids short-circuit conditions, and enables the generation of the desired multilevel output voltage waveform with reduced harmonic distortion and improved efficiency.

Thermal modelling

To evaluate the thermal performance and efficiency of the proposed thirteen-level inverter, a detailed thermal analysis was carried out using the PLECS simulation environment. The thermal model incorporates both conduction and switching losses of the semiconductor devices employed in the inverter. The power switches were modelled using their practical electrical characteristics, including on-state resistance, switching energy, and thermal parameters obtained from the device datasheet. The conduction losses are produced when the switches conduct current during their ON state and are mainly dependent on the current flowing through the device and its on-state resistance. The switching losses arise during turn-on and turn-off transitions due to the simultaneous presence of voltage and current across the switch. These losses were evaluated using the thermal module available in PLECS, which calculates the instantaneous power dissipation of each switch throughout the switching cycle.

Figure 7 illustrates the distribution of switching losses among the ten switches under resistive and resistive–inductive load conditions. It can be observed that the losses are reasonably distributed across the semiconductor devices, indicating balanced utilization of the switches. Minor variations in the loss distribution are attributed to differences in the conduction intervals and voltage stress experienced by individual switches during different operating modes. The overall efficiency of the proposed inverter was determined by comparing the output power delivered to the load with the total power dissipated in the semiconductor devices.

Experimental results

Figure 19 presents the laboratory prototype developed to validate the practical feasibility of the proposed inverter configuration. The experimental implementation was carried out using the TMS320F28379D digital signal processor (DSP) for real-time control and pulse generation. The DSP generates switching commands based on the implemented modulation algorithm and transfers gating signals to the inverter switches through an isolated driver circuit, ensuring reliable and safe operation. The hardware setup employs three isolated DC sources rated at 10 V, 20 V, and 30 V, which are combined to realize the required voltage levels of the thirteen-level inverter. The power stage is constructed using IRFP460 MOSFET switches and MUR860 ultrafast recovery diodes to achieve efficient switching performance with reduced reverse recovery losses. For performance evaluation under different operating conditions, both a 30 Ω resistive (R) load and a 30 mH resistive–inductive (RL) load are considered. The experimentally obtained output voltage, current waveforms, and switching pulses are illustrated in Figs. 20, 21, 22 and 23.

Overall, the experimental results verify that the proposed inverter topology achieves stable multilevel voltage generation, effective harmonic mitigation, and reliable performance under both resistive and inductive loading conditions, thereby demonstrating its suitability for practical renewable energy conversion systems.

The present work is primarily devoted to the development, performance evaluation, thermal assessment, and experimental verification of the proposed thirteen-level inverter operating under open-loop conditions. To ensure a thorough analysis, the effects of load variations, modulation index changes, reference frequency adjustments, and carrier frequency variations have been examined through both simulation and hardware implementation. The results demonstrate satisfactory performance and stable operation over a broad range of operating conditions. However, the investigation of advanced closed-loop control strategies and grid-connected operation is beyond the scope of the current study. Future work will explore the integration of the proposed inverter with renewable energy systems and utility-grid applications using suitable closed-loop voltage and current control techniques to enhance dynamic performance, system stability, and power management.

In contrast, the proposed configuration achieves thirteen-level operation using only ten switches, three DC sources, and no additional diodes or capacitors, leading to reduced component count, lower total standing voltage, and improved switch utilization. The obtained cost factor of 27 is the lowest among the compared topologies, demonstrating superior hardware efficiency and reduced implementation cost. Overall, the reduced device count, minimized voltage stress, and improved utilization factors highlight the effectiveness of the proposed architecture. These advantages make the inverter highly suitable for medium-voltage renewable energy applications, particularly photovoltaic energy conversion systems where compactness, efficiency, and reliability are essential.

Advanced inverter configurations such as pencil-shaped MLIs58 and diamond-shaped MLIs59 provide enhanced voltage gain and improved harmonic performance; nevertheless, they generally require a larger number of semiconductor devices and more complex converter structures. Similarly, CSNPC60 and asymmetric T-type61 topologies improve voltage utilization and reduce conduction losses but rely on sophisticated control algorithms, clamping structures, and additional gate-driver circuits. Hybrid, switched-diode, and packed E-cell topologies62–64 improve voltage boosting capability and waveform quality; however, they increase converter complexity through the use of additional passive components, voltage-balancing mechanisms, or continuous monitoring requirements.

In contrast, the proposed topology generates thirteen output voltage levels using only ten unidirectional switches and three isolated DC sources without employing switched capacitors, clamping circuits, or auxiliary voltage-balancing networks. The topology achieves a low total standing voltage of 4 p.u., a reduced cost factor of 27, and a maximum efficiency of 96.8%. Furthermore, the elimination of capacitor-balancing requirements simplifies implementation and enhances reliability. These characteristics demonstrate that the proposed inverter provides an effective balance between component count, voltage stress, efficiency, and implementation simplicity, making it a promising candidate for renewable-energy integration and medium-voltage power-conversion applications.

Conclusion

This study introduced a novel asymmetric triple-source thirteen-level multilevel inverter topology intended for medium-voltage and high-power applications. The proposed configuration produces thirteen output voltage levels using only ten unidirectional switches and three isolated DC sources, thereby reducing the number of power devices, simplifying the circuit structure, and lowering implementation costs compared with existing thirteen-level inverter topologies. Furthermore, the topology achieves a TSV of 4 p.u., which reduces the voltage stress on semiconductor devices and enhances switch utilization. The performance of the proposed inverter was evaluated through MATLAB/Simulink simulations under different operating conditions, including load variation, modulation index adjustment, and frequency control. The obtained results verified stable multilevel voltage generation, effective output-voltage regulation, and improved harmonic performance using the level-shifted pulse-width modulation strategy. A comparative assessment with recently reported topologies demonstrated that the proposed inverter requires fewer switches and exhibits a lower cost factor while maintaining satisfactory output quality and reduced voltage stress. Thermal analysis performed in the PLECS environment was used to quantify conduction losses, switching losses, and overall efficiency. The results indicated a maximum efficiency of approximately 96.8%, confirming the low-loss and high-efficiency characteristics of the proposed configuration. In addition, the thermal analysis revealed a balanced distribution of power losses among the switching devices, supporting reliable operation under practical loading conditions. The feasibility of the proposed inverter was further verified using a TMS320F28379D DSP-based experimental prototype. The experimental results showed close agreement with the simulation outcomes, validating the switching strategy, the generation of thirteen voltage levels, and stable operation under both resistive and resistive–inductive loads. Overall, the analytical, simulation, thermal, and experimental investigations confirm that the proposed topology is a compact, efficient, and reliable solution for renewable-energy integration and medium-voltage power-conversion systems.
