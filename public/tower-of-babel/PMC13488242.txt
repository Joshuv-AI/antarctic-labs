Protein adsorption at material interface: mechanistic design framework for engineering ceramic scaffolds for bone repair applications
Authors: Gayathri S S, Sunita Nayak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCeramic materials are widely used in bone tissue engineering applications due to their chemical similarity to human bone, along with their inherent biocompatibility. Bone scaffolds fabricated with ceramics thus serve as a framework for supporting new bone formation through cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation, and subsequently integrate with the host tissue. However, the cells are unable to attach directly to the implanted ceramic surfaces. Instead, they interact with the dynamic layer of proteins adsorbed on the material surface, which mediates cell adhesion and proliferation via specific integrin-ligand signalling. Therefore, rather than a passive process, protein adsorption can be utilized as a design criterion to develop advanced ceramic scaffolds. Despite extensive efforts in surface modification, the mechanistic relationship between ceramic surface properties, protein adsorption behaviour, and subsequent osteogenic signalling remains fragmented across the literature. This review emphasizes the central and often underexplored role of protein adsorption in mediating the initial cell–ceramic interactions critical for bone tissue regeneration. In contrast to previous pieces of literature, this paper critically examines the studies in the past decade, with special focus on the last five years, on how the protein–ceramic interaction can be manipulated to improve its biocompatibility, which adds to the novelty of this review. By critically evaluating the in vitro and in vivo studies, we propose that the protein adsorption on ceramic scaffolds can be treated as a controllable bio-instructive design parameter for next-generation osteoinductive ceramics. Introduction

The intrinsic regeneration potential of human bone can be challenged under various conditions, such as tumour resection, trauma, infection, and surgery, necessitating medical interventions [1–3]. The common traditional therapeutic strategies for defect repair include bone grafting. Depending on the source, bone grafts can be autografts (obtained from the same individual), allografts (harvested from a donor of the same species), or xenografts (obtained from a donor of a different species, like bovine or porcine). Among these, autografts are considered the gold standard due to their inherent osteogenic properties. The bone grafts provide a three-dimensional framework for supporting and regenerating the damaged bone. However, their clinical application is limited by various factors such as donor site morbidity, limited availability, the possibility of immune rejection, and additional surgical burden. These limitations have driven the development of synthetic bone scaffolds, which can provide a three-dimensional support for bone regeneration. This accelerated the search for alternative approaches that can overcome the issues related to traditional grafting strategies regarding availability, biological performance and safety. Bone tissue engineering emerged as an interdisciplinary research area that combines materials science, molecular biology, and cell biology, aiming to regenerate bone defects. Bone tissue engineering largely relies on biomaterials and focuses on developing alternatives to conventional bone grafts [4]. The fabricated porous bone scaffolds support cell attachment and tissue regeneration [5]. The materials widely used to manufacture bone scaffolds include ceramics, metals, and polymers [6]. However, ceramics are particularly noteworthy due to their resemblance in mineral composition to human bone. Ceramics composed of calcium phosphate can improve bone formation by supporting bone mineralization. Apart from chemistry, the surface topography, surface charge, and architecture of these ceramic scaffolds are also comparable to bone tissue. Along with their ability to support physically, the possibility to tune the porosity, composition, and degradation of the ceramic scaffolds makes them versatile [7, 8]. When implanted on damaged sites, these scaffolds promote tissue regeneration of bone via adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation of cells [9]. The bioinert ceramic materials, such as alumina, cannot inherently promote osseointegration. In addition, ceramic materials may often suffer from limited bioactivity and poor mechanical properties [10]. These limitations underscore the need for focusing on how the cells interact with the ceramic materials. The biological interface of the implanted ceramic materials remains less explored. The cross-talk between cells and materials at the interface influences cell behaviour and the regeneration processes [11]. The cells are attached to the materials using contact sites, followed by cell signalling [12]. Protein adsorption can occur within seconds when a material contacts body fluid, serving as a fundamental biological process that triggers a complex cascade of cellular responses [13]. These initial processes are crucial in determining the material’s biocompatibility and its integration with host tissue. The potential of using protein adsorption as a tunable design parameter for controlling cellular responses remains underexplored. The cellular signalling and downstream osteogenic pathways can be strongly influenced by modulating the protein adsorption behaviour on the ceramic scaffold surfaces. Therefore, the protein adsorption can be repositioned from a passive event to an active design strategy for developing more effective and biologically instructive ceramic scaffolds.

Literature search methodology

Electronic databases, such as Google Scholar, PubMed, and Scopus, were used to conduct a literature search for identifying relevant studies related to protein adsorption on ceramic scaffolds. The articles published from 2014 to 2026 were focused primarily on highlighting recent advances in protein adsorption on ceramic scaffolds. In addition, landmark studies published before this period were included to provide the necessary fundamental background information.

Combinations of keywords such as “protein adsorption”, “ceramic bone scaffolds”, “bone tissue engineering”, “bone repair”, “bioceramics”, “hydroxyapatite”, “β-tricalcium phosphate”, “calcium phosphate biomaterials”, “surface properties”, “cell attachment”, “integrin signalling”, “osteogenesis”, “osteoimmunolgy”, “surface modification”, “characterization techniques”, and “macrophage polarization” were used as the search strategy. Only peer-reviewed research and review articles published in English were included in this review. Particular emphasis was given to studies involving modifications of surface properties, the influence of protein adsorption, and subsequent biological responses, such as cell attachment, osteogenic differentiation, integrin signalling, and immune modulation. Studies with experimental, analytical, proteomic, or computational approaches to evaluate protein adsorption on material surfaces were also included for the review. However, studies with limited relevance to material–protein interactions, those without sufficient experimental validations, and non-English papers were considered less relevant and excluded.

Ceramic bone scaffolds

Ceramic materials are well known for their biocompatibility and osteoconductivity, which help them to heal bone fractures. There are different types of ‘biocompatible ceramics’, ranging from bioinert substances like zirconia and alumina to bioactive materials like hydroxyapatite (HA), bioactive glasses (BG), and glass ceramics, as well as resorbable calcium phosphates [14]. Accordingly, there are three families of ceramic materials: bioinert, bioactive, and biodegradable ceramics, as summarized in Table 1 [15, 16]. The bioinert ceramics, comprising alumina and zirconia, are stable and non-reactive in biological environments. However, they are not able to form bonds with the bone. They often exhibit micromovements at the bone-to-implant interface, which can lead to prosthesis failure. On the other hand, bioactive ceramics are designed to interact with the biological environment, thereby enhancing bonding with bone tissue. This category includes materials like hydroxyapatite and bioactive glasses. Biodegradable (or bioresorbable) bioceramics, such as calcium phosphates, are gradually absorbed and replaced by the body’s tissues over time. They include β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP), calcium phosphate, dicalcium phosphate dihydrate (DCPD) [16–19]. Along with similarity in bone composition, ceramics draw significant attraction due to their excellent physicochemical properties, which make them well-suited for bone repair applications [20–22]. The calcium to phosphate (Ca2+/PO43−) ratio of HA is 1.67, whereas β-TCP has a ratio of 1.50. In addition, HA has been shown to have good stability at body temperature compared to β-TCP [23, 24]. However, unlike the synthetic stoichiometric calcium phosphate materials, the bone mineral in humans is non-stoichiometric due to ionic impurities and structural defects. Consequently, the Ca2+/PO43− ratio of human bone mineral is variable depending on factors like age, physiological conditions, and anatomical site, with reported values ranging from 0.58 to 2.34 [25, 26]. Certain other qualities, such as resistance to oxidation, wear resistance, low coefficient of friction, and rigidity, are also attributed to the ceramic materials [27]. The favourable mechanical properties further enhance their bone repair potential. But their inherent brittleness makes them prone to fractures. Owing to their irreplaceable qualities, ceramic materials can be modified to overcome their limitations [28]. For this, researchers have adopted various strategies, including second-phase strengthening, fine-grain strengthening, and sintering process optimization, to improve the mechanical properties of calcium phosphate ceramics [29]. The bioactivity of these materials is remarkable. Bioactivity refers to the ability of the material to form bonds with the bone. Ceramic materials, especially bioglasses, form a layer of hydroxycarbonate apatite on their surface, which in turn helps bone formation [30]. The surface microstructure and porosity of these materials aid in proper cell attachment and bone repair [20]. The surface properties can be further tailored/modified to augment their biological and mechanical properties. Several techniques, like biofunctionalization, polishing, sand blasting, acid etching, coating, ultraviolet light treatment, and laser treatment are shown to enhance the surface properties of inert ceramics like zirconia [31]. Ceramic materials became an exceptionally effective material for bone repair due to their specific characteristics, including osteoconductivity, bioactivity, biocompatibility, mechanical strength, and the ability to be functionalized with therapeutic agents.

However, the biological and mechanical performance of the ceramic materials does not depend completely on their intrinsic properties, rather it also relies on the architectural and functional attributes of the fabricated scaffolds. This includes the surface morphology, pore architecture, mechanical strength, and degradation kinetics [32, 33]. The ceramic scaffold fabrication techniques can be classified as conventional and non-conventional techniques. The fabrication techniques range from conventional methods, such as freeze-drying, solvent casting and particulate leaching, gas foaming, sol-gel method, slip casting, thermal-induced phase separation, and electrospinning, to advanced techniques like 3D printing. The conventional methods often help in the fabrication of ceramic scaffolds with good porosity. For instance, solvent casting is commonly used to fabricate highly porous ceramic-polymer composite scaffolds. Scaffolds have been developed by combining different ceramic materials with various polymers like poly-ε-caprolactone (PCL), poly(lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA), poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), polyurethane (PU), and poly(lactic acid) (PLA) via solvent casting and particulate leaching methods [34–38]. Highly porous three-dimensional ceramic scaffolds can also be fabricated through freeze-drying [39]. But these conventional methods often fail at patient specificity. Recent technologies, such as additive manufacturing (3D printing), aid in developing patient-specific geometries with precise control over their architecture [40].

Protein adsorption on ceramic materials

When a biomaterial encounters the body fluids, the very first step is its interaction with proteins. This is crucial for the initiation of a downstream biological cascade [86]. Consequently, the cells cannot interact directly with the bare material surface, but rather with the adsorbed layer of proteins. Such protein-mediated modifications influence the integration of material with the bone, as they promote the recruitment and attachment of osteogenic cells [87]. Properties of ceramics

Several features of the implanted ceramic scaffolds influence the protein adsorption to a greater extent.

Porosity and pore size

The proportion of void structures on a material surface can be represented by porosity [88]. The microporous surfaces of the ceramic materials provide sites for the proteins. The number of micropore structures in the scaffold can affect serum protein adsorption, as these proteins can be retained in the micropores [89]. Consequently, an increase in porosity enhances the surface area, allowing more proteins to adsorb [90]. A functional bone scaffold has an ideal porosity range of 50–90% [91]. According to Wang et al., the in vivo study done in mouse models suggested that the porous biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP) scaffolds enhanced osteoinduction via Bone Morphogenetic Protein-2 (BMP-2) adsorption [92]. Studies found that proteins like albumin and fibronectin are adsorbed more in microporous HA. Compared to the non-microporous HA, there was a tenfold increase in the adsorption of these proteins on the microporous ones. The increase in microporosity provides more adsorption area to interact with the proteins. Likewise, recombinant human bone morphogenetic protein-2 (rhBMP-2) was adsorbed more on BCP scaffolds with a higher microporosity of 48.4% than those with 24.3% of microporosity [90]. Proteins like fibrinogen and insulin are shown to adsorb more on microporous ceramic particles like HA and BCP and improve cell adhesion further [93]. In addition to the number of micropores, the size of the micropores also influences protein adsorption. Although the size of osteoblasts ranges from 10–50 μm, they usually prefer larger pore sizes to proliferate [94]. A 50–500 μm pore size range is preferred for load-bearing applications [95]. Ren et al. proved that microgrooves on hydroxyapatite scaffolds induced osteogenic gene expression through increased protein adsorption, as shown in Fig. 2. Microgrooves of size ranging 25–30 µm were formed on the surface of porous hydroxyapatite scaffolds with 80% porosity and a pore size of 750–900 µm. The material–protein interaction was studied by incubating the scaffolds in bovine serum albumin (BSA), followed by desorbing the adsorbed proteins using sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), which were then further quantified using Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. The release behaviour of previously adsorbed BSA from both scaffolds was evaluated at different time points. Both the HA and HAG (HA scaffolds with microgrooves) exhibited rapid release at the initial 4 h. The HAG showed slightly higher BSA release, suggesting increased BSA adsorption. The authors were able to correlate the higher BSA release with the enhanced cell proliferation and improved osteogenesis of cells on these scaffolds. The microgroove structures allowed the distinct linear spread and directed growth of human placenta-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hPMSCs) [96].

Surface roughness

Protein adsorption on a scaffold is also governed by its surface roughness [97]. Biomaterials with surface irregularities promote cell adhesion and growth by providing more surface area. The nanometer-scale surface topography in scaffolds significantly influences cellular behaviour by modulating adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation [98, 99]. This effect is primarily due to the changes in the spatial arrangement of proteins on the material surface. Experimental evidence supports the significant role of surface roughness of ceramic scaffolds in protein adsorption. In 2021, when Raymond et al. used hydrothermal treatment to harden 3D-printed α-TCP scaffolds, an interlocked acicular needle-shaped nanocrystalline microstructure was formed on their surface, as depicted in Fig. 3. Surprisingly, the protein adsorption study revealed a significant increase in the protein penetration in these hydrothermal-treated scaffolds compared to the biomimetic ones (hardened via immersion in water, 37 °C for 7 days). Although the hydrothermal ones showed increased protein adsorption, higher initial cell numbers were observed on the biomimetic ones. This indicates that the enhanced protein adsorption alone may not necessarily be sufficient for the enhanced cell attachment. In this case, the cells preferred plate-like surface morphology over the needle-like structures. Twice the number of cells were observed initially on the biomimetic scaffolds than on the hydrothermal ones when the cell viability quantification was performed [100]. In contrast, surface roughness in the micrometre range minimally affects protein adsorption as the material surface appears relatively smooth [101]. The incorporation of nano-hydroxyapatite (nano-HAp) into poly (lactic acid) (PLA) microspheres has been reported to increase surface roughness and hydrophilicity. Protein adsorption of the samples was carried out using BSA as a model protein. There was a significant increase in the surface roughness and protein adsorption with increasing nano-HAP concentration. All the scaffolds showed a rapid increase in protein adsorption in the first 2–3 h, followed by a plateau phase. The PLA microspheres with 30% nano-HAP exhibited the highest adsorption of nearly ~16–17 mg/g, whereas only ~11 mg/g of the proteins were adsorbed on the PLA alone at the 7th hour of incubation. This increased protein adsorption was further correlated with improved adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells, highlighting the role of both hydroxyapatite and surface-induced protein interactions in regulating cellular responses. Such findings demonstrate that modulation of surface roughness can indirectly influence cell behaviour through its effect on protein adsorption at the biomaterial interface [102]. Researchers have also used post-fabrication treatments that effectively tuned the surface roughness. For instance, alkaline surface treatment of poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL)-based composite scaffolds incorporating hydroxyapatite and β-tricalcium phosphate with 1 M NaOH has been shown to increase surface roughness, as confirmed by atomic force microscopy. In addition, the chemical etching also promoted enhanced BSA adsorption. In the BCA assay, the surface-treated scaffolds showed BSA adsorption of around ~33–39 µg/ml, while nearly ~22–24 µg/ml BSA was adsorbed on the untreated ones. The increased adsorption was further associated with enhanced preosteoblastic cell adhesion and proliferation [103]. Hydroxyapatite surfaces with nanoscale topography have been shown to exhibit enhanced roughness and wettability compared to microscale topography. Notably, nanoscale surfaces adsorbed more proteins like vitronectin and were also found to maintain the native conformation of the adsorbed proteins [104]. Overall, the scaffolds can better resemble the natural environment of the bone by enhancing the porosity and surface roughness, enabling faster tissue growth.

Particle size

Particle size is a key parameter that influences protein adsorption. In the context of hydrogels, the increased particle size of the microgels has been shown to enhance cell attachment, likely due to the formation of a more favourable microenvironment for protein adsorption [105]. However, minimal literature is available on ceramic scaffolds that addresses the direct relationship between particle size and protein adsorption. Hydroxyapatite shows a greater affinity towards BSA. Dry ball milling was employed for various time durations (1, 15, 30, and 45 min) to reduce the particle size of HA. A significant increase in BSA adsorption was observed with a decrease in particle size, confirmed via the BCA protein assay. Specifically, the hydroxyapatite particles that were ball-milled for 45 min showed the highest BSA adsorption of 10.18 mg g−1. Furthermore, the adsorbed BSA also showed a very low cumulative release of 13.14 ± 0.36%, indicating the stronger interaction of BSA with hydroxyapatite [106]. He et al. found a significant change in protein adsorption due to changes in the surface morphology caused by the particle size of hydroxyapatite coatings. When the smaller deposited particles favoured the adsorption of negatively charged bovine albumin, the larger particles enhanced the adsorption of positively charged lysozyme. This can be attributed to the increased electrostatic interaction of negatively charged hydroxyapatite coating with larger particle sizes, which enhances the adsorption of positively charged proteins while hindering the negatively charged ones [107]. The particle size is highly related to the scaffolds’ mechanical strength and pore size, which in turn have an active role in protein adsorption. It is possible to fabricate ceramic scaffolds of the desired pore size by adjusting the particle size of the powders used [108]. The porosity tends to increase as the average particle size increases. For instance, an increase in the average particle size from 6 to 123 µm can raise the porosity from 15.68% to 30.88% [109].

Surface charge

The charge on the ceramic scaffolds also governs the protein adsorption behaviour and subsequent cell attachment. Most proteins contain charged amino acids [110], which help them interact with the scaffold surfaces. It has been found that scaffold surfaces with positive charges adsorb more proteins [111]. The charge of the scaffold surface determines whether to promote or reduce the adsorption of the proteins. For instance, negatively charged surfaces like silica attract positively charged proteins like lysozyme, but repel negatively charged proteins such as BSA [112]. Positively and negatively polarized surfaces were generated on hydroxyapatite discs via high temperature polarization technique. Enhanced cell attachment was observed on both the negative and positive surfaces. This can be attributed to surface charge-mediated ionic interactions, where negatively charged surfaces attract Ca2⁺ ions that influence the adsorption and organization of cell adhesion proteins, while positively charged surfaces interact with negatively charged ions such as Cl⁻, thereby modulating protein-mediated cell attachment at the biomaterial interface [113]. Mao et al. fabricated piezoelectric BaTiO3/β-TCP (BTCP) ceramics by polarizing them under 3.2 kV·mm−1 for 10 min at ambient temperature to form positive and negative surface charges. The electrically polarized ceramic surfaces significantly altered the protein adsorption behaviour. For instance, polarized ceramics showed faster fibronectin adsorption compared to the non-polarized controls. The negatively charged surfaces showed the highest total protein adsorption (~0.45 mg/g) after immersing in cell culture medium. These surfaces attract positively charged ions like Ca2⁺, Na⁺, and K⁺, which subsequently promote protein accumulation and cell attachment. Finally, polarized surface charges were found to influence intracellular Ca2⁺ signaling in BMSCs. Cells cultured on negatively charged BTCP⁻ surfaces exhibited significantly elevated intracellular Ca2⁺ levels, showing approximately a 2.5-fold increase compared to non-polarized controls. This enhancement was attributed to the activation of voltage-gated ion channels by the negatively charged surface, promoting extracellular Ca2⁺ influx. Since intracellular Ca2⁺ acts as a critical secondary messenger in osteogenic differentiation, the increased calcium signalling suggests that negatively polarized surfaces may enhance osteogenesis through electrically mediated cellular signalling pathways. The increased fibronectin adsorption and enhanced intracellular calcium concentration significantly improved the expression of integrin α5β1, phospho-focal adhesion kinase (P-FAK), and phospho-extracellular signal-regulated kinase (P-ERK) proteins. This promoted osteogenic differentiation, ultimately by FAK/ERK and BMP/Smad signalling pathways [114]. Ceramic materials, like alumina and zirconia, possess an electronegative surface charge at physiological pH. This leads to increased protein adsorption compared to metal surfaces [115]. When alkaline phosphatase (ALP), a bone-mineralizing enzyme, was immobilized on alpha-alumina (Al2O3) and yttria-stabilized zirconia (Y-TZP). This imparted a negative charge to these ceramic materials, −30.7 ± 4.6 mV and −25.7 ± 5.2 mV for ALP immobilized alumina and Y-TZP surface, respectively. Subsequently, their surface hydrophilicity was also enhanced (water contact angles 43.7 ± 1.5⁰ and 40.9 ± 2.3⁰, respectively), promoting subsequent cell adhesion [116]. Additionally, the surface potential can be modulated by creating electrically modified patterns on hydroxyapatite scaffolds using a scanning electron beam, and it was shown to influence the protein adsorption behaviour [117].

Functional groups

The surface functional groups in the ceramic scaffolds can determine the nature and extent of protein adsorption. For instance, the alumina surface was functionalized with various groups, including –CH2, -OH, -COOH, and -NH₂, using the self-assembled monolayer technique. Then, BSA was immobilized on these surfaces. The -NH2 and -COOH groups demonstrated the highest BSA immobilization (26.1 μg/cm2) and further cell proliferation, and -CH2 showed the least protein immobilization [118]. This was supported by another study, when glass–ceramic scaffolds were functionalized using amino silane (APTES), which has free amine functional groups, promoting further cell attachment and proliferation [119]. Compared to -OH and -COOH groups, proteins like fibronectin are shown to have increased adsorption on -NH2 containing surfaces, which promotes enhanced cell attachment [120]. Thiol (-SH) and amino (-NH2) functional groups were successfully introduced on the surface of mesoporous bioactive glass scaffolds. The subsequent studies using human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs) showed significant cell adhesion, along with proliferation. The enhanced mineralization and osteogenesis of the thiol and amino group functionalized scaffolds compared to the control scaffolds can be attributed to the improved protein adsorption facilitated by the functionalized scaffolds [121].

Wettability

Wettability can influence the adsorption behaviour of proteins, as suggested by several studies. The wettability is based on the angle at which a water droplet forms on the solid surface [122]. The ability of ceramics to enhance wettability was evident when calcium phosphate (CaP) coatings were deposited on biodegradable polymeric scaffolds. The CaP coatings made the hydrophobic surfaces hydrophilic by improving their wettability [123]. In a study conducted in 2017 by Sorrentino et al., ceramic materials, alumina and zirconia-platelet toughened alumina composites showed the lowest contact angles, indicating enhanced wettability compared to polymer (highly cross-linked polyethylene) and metal (cobalt–chromium–molybdenum alloy). Consequently, both the ceramic materials showed selective protein adsorption compared to the other materials. Out of all four materials, only zirconia-platelet toughened alumina was able to retain the protein collagen, a protein relevant for cell adhesion and spreading. In addition, both ceramic materials adsorbed fibronectin, whereas the other materials could not [124]. Later in 2019, Kazimierczak et al. incorporated nanohydroxyapatite (nanoHA) in various contents along with chitosan and agarose to form chitosan/agarose/nanoHA_H (high content) and chitosan/agarose/nanoHA_L (low content) scaffolds. Interestingly, both the scaffolds exhibited high water absorbance while testing the wettability. Further protein adsorption study revealed that the scaffold with higher nanoHA content adsorbed more proteins. That is, 763.20 ± 86.00 µg/cm2 of proteins was adsorbed from the human blood plasma on chitosan/agarose/nanoHA_H, which was significantly higher than that on chitosan/agarose/nanoHA_L (647.80 ± 64.54 µg/cm2) [125]. The wettability of the ceramic scaffolds can be adjusted by varying the ceramic composition. For instance, alumina–zirconia–titania composites demonstrated high hydrophilicity, especially with high titania content, and expressed increased cell viability with increased wettability [126]. In contrast, Chao Liu et al. used a silicone oil-assisted laser-heat process to transform the hydrophilic surface of zirconia ceramics into superhydrophobic, which significantly reduced bacterial adhesion and improved corrosion resistance. However, the superhydrophobic surface might impede cell attachment due to reduced solid–liquid contact area [127]. Moreover, the contact angle is strongly correlated with water absorption and capillary rise. A study using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) suggested that surfaces with lower contact angles (more hydrophilic) facilitate better fluid absorption and potentially better nutrient and oxygen flow [128]. Studies show that contact angles between 60 and 80 degrees promoted a maximum adhesion of fibroblast cells. Whereas when the surface contact angle increased from 0 to 106 degrees, it decreased the adhesion of osteoblast cells. This can be due to changes in protein adsorption [98, 129].

Crystal plane

Apart from all other factors, a recent study suggests that even the crystal plane of hydroxyapatite influences protein adsorption behaviour. For hydroxyapatite, there are two types of crystal planes: ‘a-plane’ with a positive charge due to the abundance of calcium ions, and a ‘c-plane’ with a negative charge due to the abundance of phosphate ions [130]. The crystal plane in the human bone apatite is more similar to the a-plane structure. When studied, the hydroxyapatite with a-plane orientation showed better protein adsorption, with a higher affinity towards the model protein BSA. When the a-plane HA was evaluated for the serum protein adsorption behaviour using fetal bovine serum (FBS), it showed significantly higher protein adsorption. The mass spectrometry analysis revealed the adsorbed proteins: vitamin K epoxide reductase complex 1 and secreted frizzled-related protein 3. Surprisingly, these proteins are important for bone cell differentiation. On further study using the a-plane hydroxyapatite, it showed a higher zeta potential of −7.95 mV (−16.98 mV for hydroxyapatite). The a-plane hydroxyapatite also demonstrated an enhanced wettability (73.4°) when compared to hydroxyapatite (53.9°). The protein adsorption on the a-plane orientation was also in agreement with these parameters. The a-plane hydroxyapatite was able to adsorb significantly higher BSA protein of 212 ng·mm−2, whereas the commercially available hydroxyapatite was able to adsorb only 28.4 ng mm − 2. The serum protein adsorption experiment also showed that the a-plane hydroxyapatite adsorbed a significantly higher amount of protein (2.07 µg mm−2) compared to that of commercially available hydroxyapatite (1.28 µg mm−2). Further, MALDI-TOF/MS analysis followed by two-dimensional electrophoresis confirmed that the a-plane adsorbed 37 proteins, which was significantly higher than the commercially available hydroxyapatite (12 proteins) [131].

Properties of protein

Apart from the scaffold characteristics, the properties of proteins themselves affect how they adsorb onto the implant surfaces.

Solubility

The solubility of proteins governs protein adsorption at ceramic surfaces. The solubility of proteins depends on their compatibility with their surrounding aqueous environment. The exposure of polar and charged amino acid residues on the protein surface enhances solubility through favourable interactions with water molecules, and the hydrophobic residues are typically buried within the protein core. Protein solubility influences adsorption onto ceramic scaffold surfaces, since highly soluble proteins remain strongly hydrated in solution, whereas the proteins with lower solubility exhibit a greater tendency to adsorb onto material surfaces. Recent data-driven analyses also confirmed that proteins exhibiting hydrophobic surfaces favour higher deposition on material surfaces. Whereas highly soluble proteins tend to remain in solution, forming weaker and reversible protein layers [132, 133]. In contrast, insoluble proteins like collagen cannot diffuse to the implant site. Instead, the cells typically deposit them on the implants [134]. Even though the solubility restricts the free diffusion of the collagen, they are of great importance as they are the predominant structural protein present in the extracellular matrix, which offers structural stability and encourages cell proliferation [135]. In a study, the robocasted β-TCP scaffolds were coated with type I collagen gel and were shown to improve their tissue repair and regeneration capabilities [136].

Size

The size of the proteins can significantly influence their adsorption on scaffold surfaces. Larger proteins have more binding sites than smaller ones, making them easier to adsorb [137]. For instance, fibrinogen, a glycoprotein of 340 kDa present in human blood plasma, possesses adhesion epitopes that can be identified by eukaryotic cells [138, 139]. On the other hand, a higher diffusion coefficient enables the faster diffusion of small proteins than larger ones to reach the scaffold structures [140]. Hydroxyapatite was shown to selectively adsorb proteins depending on size. For instance, proteins with a size ranging from 22 to 32 kDa were preferably adsorbed on hydroxyapatite surfaces when studied using rat serum proteins [141].

Charge

The charged proteins can adsorb more quickly to the oppositely charged surfaces. On the other hand, like charges may repel, lowering the binding affinity. Besides, the protein’s total charge can influence its structure by altering its hydrophobic and hydrophilic areas, affecting adsorption behaviour. Environmental factors like ionic strength and pH can alter the protein’s charge, influencing the strength and nature of their interplay with the scaffold surface [142]. The overall net charge of proteins will be zero at their isoelectric point (IEP). The proteins show a positive charge when the IEP goes beyond the solution pH. Similarly, the net charge will be negative when the IEP is lower than the pH. However, proteins are not restricted to adsorbing on oppositely charged surfaces. Due to non-homogeneous charge distribution, they can also get adsorbed on likely charged surfaces. For example, BSA, having a net negative charge at neutral pH, can selectively expose its positive residues. Furthermore, there will be a minimum electrostatic repulsion between proteins at the IEP, which helps in more protein adsorption [140]. BSA and lysozyme showed a maximum adsorption at pH 4.0 on hydroxyapatite [143].

Stability

Proteins can be categorized into soft proteins and hard proteins in the context of their stability. Takaaki and Norde initially proposed this concept in the 1990s itself [144]. According to this concept, soft proteins have lower structural stability than hard proteins, which are highly stabilized due to their large Gibbs energy. However, the hard proteins adsorb on hydrophilic surfaces only when the electrostatic interactions are favourable. In contrast, they can adsorb on hydrophobic interfaces regardless of charge interactions. But irrespective of the electrostatic interaction, the soft proteins can adhere to both hydrophobic and hydrophilic surfaces [145]. The less stable proteins tend to unfold rapidly as they have less intramolecular crosslinking. Rapid unfolding helps these proteins make more contact points with the scaffold surfaces, which helps in easy and fast adsorption [146]. The unfolding might expose specific amino acids or hydrophobic surfaces within the protein structure, favouring adsorption [147]. For example, BSA undergoes conformational changes upon adsorption onto HA surfaces, forming stacked β-sheet structures, as evidenced by thioflavin T staining and circular dichroism spectroscopy [148].

Theoretical models explaining protein adsorption on scaffold surface

The adsorption behaviour of proteins on the material surfaces can be explained using various theories. Langmuir adsorption isotherm, initially used for gas–solid interaction, is a fundamental model to explain protein adsorption on material surfaces. This model assumes that the proteins (adsorbate) are adsorbed on the scaffold surfaces (adsorbent) in a monolayer fashion. According to this model, protein adsorption occurs on a homogeneous surface having discrete active sites. In addition, only one molecule is adsorbed to each of the active sites, and these molecules cannot interact with each other [149–151]. The Langmuir model explains the key physics of molecule interactions at surfaces, forming the groundwork for future advances in understanding interfacial phenomena, creating novel adsorbent materials, and designing engineering processes [152]. Freundlich adsorption isotherm is another model describing the adsorption at the liquid–solid interface. This model applies to adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces. Accordingly, different sites on the surface show different affinity towards a protein. In contrast to the Langmuir model, this model advocates multilayer adsorption [153–155].

The DLVO (Derjaguin–Landau–Verwey–Overbeek) theory explains the protein–surface interaction based on two interactions—van der Waals and electrostatic forces [156]. The theory was modified (XDLVO) by incorporating the Lewis acid–base (AB) interactions, as studies pointed out that the AB interactions dominate the other two interactions. The XDLVO theory has been applied to examine the adsorption of BSA on various surfaces, revealing that acid–base interactions significantly influence adsorption outcomes [157]. This theory explains protein adsorption on material surfaces by assessing the balance between attractive and repulsive forces at the interface. In the context of blood-contacting surfaces, the hydrophobic and hydrophilic properties of materials influence these interactions, affecting protein behaviour. Hydrophobic surfaces may enhance van der Waals attraction, while hydrophilic surfaces rely on hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions [158]. Another model called the spreading particle model explains the conformational change of the proteins once adsorbed on material surfaces and was prevalent from the 1990s. According to the spreading particle model, proteins are assumed to be particles, and once adsorbed, they spread discretely and symmetrically without any overlap. The proteins get unfolded on adsorption and spread over the free unoccupied spaces, forming a monolayer of protein [159, 160].

Vroman effect: competitive adsorption

The multi-protein adsorption shown by serum proteins in the blood on a surface can be better described by the Vroman effect, named after Le Vroman [161]. This effect explains how proteins competitively adsorb on the material surfaces. According to this, the material surface is initially adsorbed with proteins of lower binding affinity, which are then replaced by proteins of higher binding affinity [162]. The proteins that are adsorbed initially are the abundant ones. But later on, the high-affinity proteins replace them, irrespective of their lower concentration. Therefore, this process occurs in a time-dependent manner [163, 164]. The sequence of proteins that gets adsorbed influences the development of the protein layer on ceramic scaffolds. In scaffolds made of ceramics, the competitive adsorption of various plasma proteins, such as albumin, fibrinogen, and vitronectin, can be elucidated using the Vroman effect. These proteins play distinct roles in cell adhesion and proliferation, which are critical for effective tissue regeneration [165]. Owing to its low molecular weight and abundance in human blood, albumin shows the initial adsorption on material surfaces [166, 167]. Over time, fibrinogen—larger and with a higher affinity for many surfaces, particularly hydrophobic ones—displaces albumin through a competitive adsorption process, forming a more stable protein layer [168]. The fibrinogen layer formation on the ceramic scaffold surface according to the Vroman effect is diagrammatically depicted in Fig. 4. Ma et al. in 2019 analysed the competitive adsorption of BSA and lysozyme on β-TCP nanocoatings. The obtained results were consistent with the Vroman effect. BSA, a larger protein with a higher molecular weight, completely replaced the smaller lysozymes at the adsorption equilibrium. However, the lysozymes could not replace BSA; instead, they adsorbed on the BSA surface [169].

From protein adsorption to cellular attachment on the ceramics: the complete story

Types of proteins involved

Proteins in the extracellular matrix, serum, and immune system are involved in the ceramic surface interaction post-implantation. This interaction acts as a functional interface, promoting cell adhesion and tissue integration.

Certain adhesive proteins in the extracellular matrix, such as fibronectin and vitronectin, have a prominent role in adsorption onto ceramic surfaces and mediating cell adhesion [170]. Likewise, the adsorption of another adhesive protein, laminin, is also shown to induce osteoconductivity in hydroxyapatite material [171]. Usually, it is seen that fibroblast accumulation deposits type III collagen (extracellular matrix protein) at the implant–host interface [172].

Serum proteins like albumin, globulin, and fibrinogen tend to get adsorbed on the surface of ceramic implants. Of these, albumin is the most prevalent one in the blood. A study by Boix et al. demonstrated preferential albumin adsorption on the HA surface. Whereas enhanced fibrinogen adsorption was shown by β-TCP. At the same time, both proteins showed lower adsorption on BCP. Also, albumin was not at all adsorbed on the zirconia. These differences can be due to the changes in the surface heterogeneities of various ceramic materials [173]. Dental scaffolds, once implanted, exhibited adsorption of salivary proteins along with serum proteins, including enzyme modulator proteins and nucleic acid-binding proteins [174].

Bone morphogenic proteins, like BMP-2, also get deposited on the ceramic implants, promoting bone regeneration [175]. The complement system involves certain proteins, a crucial part of the body’s innate immune system. Complement proteins like C3 and C5 are adsorbed to the material surfaces and trigger certain immune pathways that elicit inflammatory responses. Ceramic materials can activate the complement proteins, as evidenced by the induction of C3 cleavage by TCP material. Moreover, C3 deposition was found on other ceramic materials, including hydroxyapatite, alumina, and zirconia [176].

Role of water molecules: water shell formation

Water has a vital role in mediating protein adsorption. Before protein adsorption, water molecules cover the scaffold surface once implanted to form a layer [140]. The ceramic scaffolds, which have inherent hydrophilic properties, can naturally attract water molecules [177]. This water layer acts as an intermediate, further facilitating the interaction of the scaffold surface with the proteins.

The water molecules found in the bio-interface can be classified into tightly bound water (non-freezing water) and scarcely bound water (freezing water or free water). The tightly bound water interacts strongly with the surface, whereas the latter interacts scarcely. Intermediate water (loosely bound water or freezing-bound water) is found between the other two states, showing a weak interaction with the material surface. This intermediate water exhibits crystallization temperatures below 0 °C. The intermediate water has an important biological role as it can be used as an indicator of bioinertness and biocompatibility of biomaterials. Techniques like differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), time-resolved in situ attenuated total reflection infrared (ATR-IR) spectroscopy, and solid-state NMR can be utilized to detect intermediate water [178, 179]. Zhu et al. demonstrated that the hydration layer significantly influences the promotion of organic–inorganic interactions at calcite surfaces. The adsorbed water molecules near the calcite crystal create a hydrated microenvironment, which in turn influences the protein adsorption. The study demonstrated acrylic acid (AA) dimer adsorption. The well-structured water layer on the calcite (104) plane creates competition between the AA dimer and water molecules, affecting the interaction strength [180]. The hydration of inorganic biomaterials such as calcium phosphates significantly influences their electronic properties, ionic conductivity, and biological functionality. Studies revealed that mesoporous BG interacts with water to form distinct hydration layers and bulk-type water within their pores, affecting water and fluid diffusion [181].

Okada et al. investigated the water structure on hydroxyapatite and showed the occurrence of all three water forms on the apatite for the first time. DSC was done to estimate the water structure at different water contents. In addition, based on the protein adsorption studies, they concluded that the quantity of proteins adsorbed on the surface of inorganic material is inversely proportional to the amount of intermediate water present [182].

Protein–water shell interaction

All the biomolecules, including proteins, possess a layer of water molecules over them, forming a hydration shell. The proteins from the bulk start diffusing to the material surface once the materials are implanted. The next step is the interaction of diffused proteins with the intermediate water layer present on the material surface. This enables the anchorage of the proteins to the water molecules via their hydrophilic groups [183–186]. The initial interaction happens between the water shell on ceramic biomaterial surfaces and the hydration shell of the proteins, subsequently forming protein adlayers [187].

The adsorption of proteins on ceramic scaffolds involves the combination of non-covalent interactions, comprising hydrogen bonding, electrostatic forces, and hydrophobic interactions, which collectively provide stability while allowing dynamic binding. Functional groups in proteins, such as amides, carboxyl, and hydroxyl, can establish hydrogen bonds either with the hydration layer or directly with the ceramic surface, stabilizing the initial attachment of proteins [188]. Proteins exhibiting non-covalent interactions displace the water molecules from their adsorption sites. Consequently, the hydration layer undergoes reorganization, with the remaining water molecules forming a network that interacts with both the adsorbed protein and the scaffold surface. Simultaneously, proteins get trapped by the water molecule network and are concentrated in closely packed arrangements [189]. Hydrophobic interaction comes into play if the material surface is non-polar, where hydrogen bond formation is impossible, as it lacks any specific functional groups. During protein adsorption on these surfaces, water displacement occurs from both the surface and the protein’s hydration shell, decreasing free energy. This thermodynamic driving force facilitates protein adsorption on non-polar surfaces. In contrast, hydrogen bonds are broken and replaced to displace the hydrogen-bonded water layer from the polar surfaces [160]. Conformational adjustments of adsorbed proteins

Upon adsorption, proteins undergo partial unfolding or conformational changes, which enhance their interaction with material surfaces, such as ceramic scaffolds [137]. Protein unfolding is crucial, as it exposes otherwise hidden amino acid sites within proteins [190]. Webster et al. demonstrated an increased adsorption of vitronectin on nanophase alumina, followed by an enhanced unfolding. This enabled the exposure of specific cell-adhesive epitopes in vitronectin that are essential for further cell interaction, evidenced by the increased osteoblast adhesion. The authors claimed that the increased adsorption and unfolding of the protein to nanophase alumina might be due to the enhanced adsorption of divalent calcium ions [191]. Still, the protein interaction and conformational changes on ceramic material surfaces remained unclear. In 2017, Gruian et al. noticed a change in the tertiary structure of haemoglobin when adsorbed on a silica–calcium–phosphate ceramic substrate. Here, the tetrameric haemoglobin was dissociated into either monomers or dimers [192]. Later on, in 2024, Gobbo et al. noticed that when fibronectin was adsorbed on the bioglass S53P4, they underwent a conformational change with a significant increase in β-sheet content. In addition, the random coil numbers were reduced following the adsorption [193]. Albumin exhibited a major loss in its α-helix when adsorbed on the HA surface. But in contrast, a reduction of β-sheet structures was observed in fibrinogen once adsorbed on BCP [173]. Techniques such as atomic force microscopy (AFM), cryoelectron microscopy, and fluorescence resonant energy transfer (FRET) can be used to analyse the conformational changes of proteins on the material surfaces [147]. The unfolding of the proteins after adsorption maximizes their contact area and the exposure of bioactive domains. For instance, the unfolding of fibronectin exposes the arginine–glycine–aspartic acid (RGD) motif. This RGD can activate αvβ5 integrins, the transmembrane receptors found in the cells, promoting cell attachment [140, 194]. The RGD tripeptide is also found in other proteins such as fibrinogen and vitronectin. The primary function of these RGD motifs is to act as a recognition site for integrin receptors in cells, promoting cell adhesion [195].

Cell attachment and cell-surface interaction

The subsequent step following the protein adsorption is cell adhesion. The interaction between cells and the adsorbed proteins is crucial, as it governs further events, including cell adhesion and proliferation. The transmembrane heterodimeric receptors called integrins act as a molecular bridge between cells and proteins. Integrins are composed of two subunits—α and β. These receptors are crucial for osteogenic differentiation, bone formation, and tissue maintenance. The cells can specifically adhere to the arginine–glycine–aspartic acid (RGD) motifs present in the adsorbed protein layer via the integrin receptors and initiate a cascade of intracellular signalling events. Eight different integrins are involved in the overall bone formation, namely—α1β1, α2β1, α3β1, α4β1, α5β1, α9β1, α11β1, and αvβ3. Different integrin types are involved in distinct functions during osteogenesis, as summarized in Table 3. For example, the bone-resorbing osteoclast cells express high levels of αvβ3 vitronectin receptors, which can bind to the RGD peptides. Wang et al. demonstrated preferential adsorption of vitronectin and laminin on BCP, which promoted the attachment and proliferation of mesenchymal stem cells, evidenced by their increased expression of α2β3 and αvβ5 integrins [160]. The integrin-mediated cell signalling is necessary for cell spreading, proliferation, and differentiation [196–198].

Different types of integrins and their properties involved in osteogenesis

Upon ligand binding, the integrins undergo a conformational change from bent to an extended active conformation [203]. Cell adhesion to the protein layer initiates cellular signalling events, resulting in the formation of focal adhesions. This involves a series of molecular interactions. Initially, both the integrin subunit binds to the specific ligands, and tend to form a cluster on the cell membrane after binding to the RGD sequence. For instance, α5β1 and αvβ3 bind to the RGD motifs of fibronectin and vitronectin. But in some integrins, the ligand binds through a single domain, as in α1β1 and α2β1, in which it binds via the α-subunit. The ligand interaction with the integrin subunit triggers the downstream signaling including the recruitment of talin, a protein found in the cytoplasm. Talin then binds to the cytoplasmic domains of integrins, which helps stabilize their conformation [204–206].

Talin bears binding sites for the cytoskeletal protein actin and other proteins like vinculin. Talin thereby acts as a mechanosensor linking the extracellular matrix to actin, which is necessary for cell spreading. Vinculin strengthens its interaction with the actin cytoskeleton [207, 208]. Focal adhesion kinase (FAK) binds to the activated integrins, auto-phosphorylates, and thus gets activated. The phosphorylation promotes the binding of Src family kinases. This recruits other proteins like paxillin to the focal adhesion site, forming the signalling hub. These events culminate in cell spreading, but can also destabilize the focal adhesion to initiate cell migration [209]. Furthermore, focal adhesions can activate signalling events like mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) pathways, responsible for the survival and proliferation of attached cells [210]. For example, both Wnt/β-catenin and FAK/ERK pathways are activated in osteoblasts. All these signalling cascades promote cell differentiation, enhance extracellular matrix production, stimulate mineralization, and regulate bone remodelling. Apart from the biochemical signals, integrins can also mediate mechanotransduction. That is, they can respond to mechanical stimuli like stiffness [211, 212]. Although protein adsorption is crucial for mediating cell–material interactions, excessive adsorption can have a negative impact on cellular adhesion. For instance, the adsorption of high concentrations of proteins prevents conformational changes upon binding, thereby masking binding sites from the cells. This can limit the integrin binding and reduce cell attachment [213].

Protein adsorption, osteoimmunology, and macrophage response

Besides the osteogenic activity promoted by the adsorbed layer of proteins, they are also involved in influencing the host immune response as depicted in Fig. 6. The adsorption of proteins such as albumin, fibronectin, fibrinogen, complement proteins, and immunoglobulins can elicit the host immune response. Since macrophages are among the first cells recruited to the site of the material, their interaction with the protein layer determines the inflammatory response and the subsequent outcomes. For instance, as a key effector of foreign body reaction (FBR), the direct contact of the macrophages with the material surface leads to the secretion of various chemokines and pro-inflammatory cytokines (interleukin-1β (IL-1β), Tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)). Prolonged inflammation results in the formation of foreign body giant cells (FBGCs) through the fusion of macrophages. This can lead to phagocytosis, fibrous encapsulation, and subsequently premature failure of the implanted scaffolds [214]. The function of macrophages typically depends on their phenotype, specifically M1 and M2. The classically activated M1 phenotype is responsible for the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (like TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), which in turn causes tissue damage if sustained for a long time. However, the activated M2 phenotype produces TGF-β1 and IL-10, which promote tissue repair. Therefore, studies found that switching the phenotype from M1 to M2 can enhance the tissue repair process. In addition, M2 macrophages are shown to inhibit osteoclastogenesis while promoting osteogenesis [215]. The adsorbed proteins can modulate the polarization of macrophages either towards M1 or towards M2. Hence, manipulating the protein adsorption on ceramic scaffolds can modulate the immune response and subsequent bone regeneration [216, 217]. Recent studies have demonstrated that the surface properties of ceramic materials, like surface charge, wettability, roughness, and chemical composition, can indirectly regulate macrophage response through the selective modulation of protein adsorption [217, 218]. Consequently, protein adsorption can also be considered as a key immunoregulator as well as a determinant of cell adhesion.

Experimental methods to measure the protein adsorption on ceramic scaffolds

Characterization of protein adsorption is essential to understand the material–protein interaction and to predict subsequent cellular responses. Various analytical methods have been employed to evaluate the protein adsorption on ceramic surfaces, as summarized in Figure 7.

Schematic representation of various experimental methods used to measure protein adsorption on ceramic scaffolds

Colorimetric assays

Colorimetric assays such as the Bradford assay and bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay are commonly used for the quantification of total protein adsorbed on ceramic surfaces. Bradford assays are widely employed due to their simplicity and cost-effectiveness. This assay is based on the binding of the dye Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 to proteins, which leads to a shift in the absorbance maximum of the dye that can be quantified spectrophotometrically [47]. The scaffold samples can be pre-wetted with PBS and then immersed in a solution of known BSA concentration. The supernatant is collected at various intervals, such as 2, 4, 8, and 24 h, and mixed with Bradford reagent. The BSA concentration was determined using UV–visible spectroscopy (595 nm) using a standard curve [219, 220]. In the BCA assay, the peptide bond of proteins reduces cupric ions (Cu+2) to cuprous ions (Cu+) under an alkaline environment and a purple coloured complex is formed when Cu+ complexes with bicinchoninic acid, which can be measured spectrophotometrically (at 562 nm) [221]. Measuring the residual protein concentration of the supernatant indirectly enables the quantification of the adsorbed proteins; however, the adsorbed proteins can be eluted using SDS and measured colorimetrically to get the direct information [222]. Colorimetric assays only provide information regarding the total quantity of adsorbed proteins and do not distinguish between different protein species or any conformational changes occurring after adsorption, making them a valuable preliminary quantitative evaluation method.

Fluorescence-based assays

Fluorescence-based methods enable the visualization, as well as the quantification, of the adsorbed proteins on ceramic surfaces. In this strategy, fluorescent labels can be conjugated to proteins to monitor their adsorption on ceramic surfaces. For instance, a fluorescent probe, fluorescein thioureidoaminocaproic acid (FTCA), was covalently linked to rhBMP-2 for evaluating its adsorption and desorption on hydroxyapatite scaffolds. This enabled the authors to evaluate the adsorption kinetics of the protein on the material surface by measuring the fluorescence intensity using a fluorescence spectrometer [223]. Fluorescent labelling is also employed to understand how the proteins are adsorbed on the surface of a material. Albumin was labelled with a fluorescein isothiocyanate conjugate (FITC) and incubated with glass–ceramic porous rods to understand how proteins are adsorbed on their surface and how they infiltrate into the inner parts. A yellowish-green colour on the surface was visualized using fluorescent microscopy, revealing a higher adsorption of the albumin on the glass–ceramic surface [224]. Therefore, fluorescence-based approaches provide valuable insights into the localization and distribution of proteins on ceramic surfaces. However, the fluorescent labelling can sometimes interfere with the protein conformation and also can perturb the interactions between the protein and the material [225].

Quartz crystal microbalance (QCM)

Quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) is a label-free and extremely sensitive method that is used to investigate protein adsorption on material surfaces in real time. QCM works based on the piezoelectric properties of a quartz crystal sensor. The change in the resonance frequency of the crystal induced by the protein adsorption can be used for quantitative analysis of protein adsorption behaviour. The decrease in frequency is proportional to the mass of adsorbed proteins [226]. Studies have been done to investigate the adsorption of salivary proteins, such as peroxidase, lactoferrin, and lysozyme, on zirconia material using QCM, which showed a significantly higher adsorption for lactoferrin [227]. In addition, QCM has also been employed to investigate the adsorption of serum proteins like fibrinogen and fibronectin on hydroxyapatite powders [228]. QCM analysis was done to study the binding stability and adsorption kinetics of bioactive peptides. For instance, QCM analysis was done to compare the binding of modified and unmodified BMP-2 mimetic peptides on the quartz crystal coated with hydroxyapatite [229]. Advanced strategies, such as quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation monitoring (QCM-D), are being employed to understand the conformational changes of proteins after adsorption [230].

Proteomic and mass spectrometry-based approaches

Strategies based on proteomics and mass spectrometry have emerged as powerful tools for characterizing proteins adsorbed on ceramic material surfaces. The proteomic studies enable the identification of all of the individual proteins present in the adsorbed layer, whereas the conventional methods quantify only the total proteins that are adsorbed on the surface. This is particularly important as cellular responses depend on the specific protein composition, along with the quantity of adsorbed proteins.

Recent studies have used liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) to study the protein adsorption behaviour on various ceramic materials. In one study, proteomic analysis was performed to identify proteins adsorbed on calcium phosphate silicate ceramic discs after soaking the samples in 10% fetal bovine serum, followed by LC–MS/MS. This approach enabled the authors to identify and compare the number of proteins adsorbed on their developed ceramic samples with the hydroxyapatite samples, and also enabled them to understand the number of unique proteins adsorbed on each scaffold, along with the number of common proteins adsorbed on both, and the number of significantly higher proteins [231]. Comparative proteomic studies have also been done on β-TCP and HA. In this study, proteomics was used to evaluate the serum protein adsorption on both materials. In addition, hMSCs were cultured on these materials, and their protein profiling was also done. LC–MS/MS analysis was performed for the protein analysis of both. The proteins identified from the LC–MS/MS were further validated using the software Scaffold, along with the X! Tandem database search algorithm [232]. Studies have further expanded the proteomic applications to evaluate the biological activity of proteins adsorbed on ceramic materials. For instance, a label-free liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) proteomics approach was used to characterize the complex protein composition adsorbed on calcium phosphate surfaces after incubating them with different hemoderivatives such as citrate plasma, platelet lysate with plasma, and washed platelet lysate. In addition, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to quantify the selected growth factors adsorbed on the ceramic surfaces, and tube-formation assays with human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) was used to evaluate the angiogenic potential. The proteomic analysis revealed the adsorption of non-regenerative and anti-angiogenic proteins on the calcium phosphate surfaces [233]. Proteomic investigations have recently extended to investigate the complexity of protein adsorption on materials under clinical environments. In a study involving patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty, β-TCP and PCL-TCP scaffolds were incubated in autologous clinical samples obtained from the femoral medullary cavity and surgical tissue collectors containing blood, bone fragments, muscle, and adipose tissue. LC–MS/MS combined with bioinformatic analysis revealed the adsorption of more than 2000 autologous proteins on both biomaterials. β-TCP exhibited higher total protein adsorption, whereas PCL-TCP demonstrated greater proteomic diversity and environment-dependent variations in protein adsorption profiles, which were strongly influenced by scaffold surface roughness and wettability [234]. Such proteomic-based approaches, therefore, facilitate the rational design of ceramic scaffolds by tailoring surface properties to selectively modulate protein adsorption.

Bioinformatic approaches

Advanced strategies like bioinformatics, machine learning (ML), and computational biology have emerged to enhance the understanding of protein adsorption on material surfaces. Researchers have started exploring ML and quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR) models to predict protein adsorption behaviours [235]. In addition, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have provided insights into protein adsorption on ceramic surfaces. In a study, an MD simulation was done to investigate the adsorption behaviour of various proteins on different crystallographic surfaces of hydroxyapatite. The study revealed that basic proteins exhibited stronger adsorption on hydroxyapatite compared to acidic ones. The study further demonstrated that ionic interactions, water-mediated interactions, and the surrounding ionic environment significantly influenced the protein adsorption [236].

Protein adsorption as a tunable parameter

Given its role in cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation, protein adsorption on ceramic scaffolds has been a pivotal area of research in bone tissue engineering. Prioritizing the modulation of the initial protein adsorption layer to promote optimal integrin engagement and focal adhesion formation offers a more direct route that can be controlled to enhance cellular attachment than strategies that target downstream, post-adsorptive cell behaviour. Thus, protein adsorption can be considered as a tunable parameter to develop efficient ceramic scaffolds for bone repair applications. Several reasons underscore the need for focusing on protein adsorption. Majorly, the adsorbed proteins guide cell attachment and osteogenesis. By evaluating the studies from the past decade, it is possible to categorize the strategies to tune protein adsorption behaviour on ceramic scaffolds into five.

Key studies from the past 10 years involving ceramic scaffolds and protein adsorption

Phase and composition tuning

The phase composition and chemical makeup of the ceramics affect the protein adsorption behaviour. In 2014, Wang et al. highlighted that adjusting the phase composition of calcium phosphate can significantly impact its bioactivity and osteogenic potential [92]. Different types of calcium phosphate ceramics were fabricated with similar porous structures and different phase compositions. All of them exhibited similar adsorption behaviour of serum proteins. Further, the highest adsorption was of two adhesive proteins—fibronectin and vitronectin, and BMP-2. Among the others, biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP), with a composition of 30% hydroxyapatite and 70% tricalcium phosphate, showed superior osteoinduction, characterized by increased BMP-2 and OCN gene expression. Trace ions are commonly used to dope calcium phosphate ceramics, altering their chemical composition, which can also alter the protein adsorption. These strategies were widely employed to mimic the natural composition of human bone, altering the biological and mechanical properties of the ceramic scaffolds. Ions such as magnesium (Mg), strontium (Sr), fluoride (F), manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), silicon (Si), and silver (Ag) are widely used to dope the ceramics [237, 238]. For instance, incorporating magnesium with hydroxyapatite was shown to enhance the protein adsorption as well as the proliferation of cells [239]. When strontium was doped with the calcium-deficient hydroxyapatite in BCP, it significantly enhanced its osteoinductivity. Particularly, the protein adsorption capability of these materials was evaluated using fetal calf serum. The study proved that the incorporation of strontium significantly enhanced their protein adsorption ability, irrespective of the concentration of proteins. In addition, the doped BCP enhanced the ALP and OCN gene expression in cells. Also, the strontium-doped BCP showed an enhanced apatite formation following the immersion in SBF. The apatite formation, protein adsorption, and gene expression of the BCP samples are shown in Fig. 8 (a),(b),(c). Aligning with these results, when the scaffolds were implanted in the dorsal muscle of dogs, the strontium-doped BCP promoted more new bone formation compared to the normal ones. The authors concluded that the strontium incorporation makes them similar to natural bone, resulting in better osteogenesis and related gene expression. Further, strontium was shown to stimulate osteogenesis via the Ras/MAPK pathway. The incorporation of strontium imparted more micropores in the BCP scaffolds, which enhanced the protein adsorption as well as osteoinductivity [240].

Architectural control

The architecture of the fabricated scaffolds, such as surface topography and porosity, can be tuned to modify protein adsorption behaviour. Zhang et al. suggested that the size of the microstructure of ceramics critically affects protein adsorption and osteogenesis. Two TCP ceramics were prepared with varying micropore sizes: TCP-S (0.65 ± 0.25 μm) and TCP-B (1.58 ± 0.65 μm). The XRD pattern, SEM images, and the micropore distribution of both ceramics are shown in Fig. 8 (d). Due to the large surface area, TCP-S adsorbed more proteins than TCP-B (Fig. 8 (e)), and also enhanced cell proliferation and differentiation of hBMSCs. TCP-S enhanced alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity (Fig. 8 (f)), a cell surface glycoprotein which is a good osteoblast differentiation marker, and OCN, OPN gene expression. The canine model confirmed the abundant ectopic bone formation by TCP-S. When TCP-S showed cell-mediated resorption, but no resorption was found in TCP-B in vivo [241]. Additionally, highly porous glass–ceramic scaffolds with 31% porosity exhibited a significant increase in protein adsorption compared to BCP scaffolds. Additive manufacturing (3D printing) helps to gain control over scaffold architecture. For instance, digital light projection (DLP) printing was employed to develop hydroxyapatite scaffolds with micronano surface porosity. The scaffolds with a high quantity of micropores with a surface roughness of 138.796 nm showed a superior protein adsorption (100 μg of proteins) compared to those with medium micropores (80 μg). This was because secondary micropores provided more sites for the protein to get adsorbed. Accordingly, the cells were able to spread better on these scaffolds, exhibiting more pseudopodia. The in vivo evaluation of these scaffolds with micronanopores demonstrated enhanced osteoinduction and tissue integration [242]. Dai et al. in 2025 combined the particulate leaching method with indirect 3D printing to develop calcium-deficient hydroxyapatite (CDHA) scaffolds with hierarchical pores. The scaffolds were composed of micropores (< 10 μm), minor pores (∼10–100 μm), and macro pores (300–400 μm). When incubated with the model protein BSA, these scaffolds showed significantly higher protein adsorption of around 140 μg/g, whereas the BCP scaffolds were able to adsorb only 40 μg/g. This can be attributed to the enhanced specific surface area of the fabricated scaffolds due to the presence of more hierarchical pores. Their superior osteoinductivity was demonstrated using a murine intramuscular implantation model. Further, the rabbit calvarial defect model confirmed their in situ bone regeneration potential [243].

Surface chemistry and functionalization

The surface chemistry of the implanted ceramic scaffolds can be considered as yet another decisive factor to govern protein adsorption. In 2023, Gobbo et al. adopted four different methods for the surface modification of bioactive glasses (BGs)–soaking in simulated body fluid (SBF) for 72 h, soaking in TRIS for 72 h, grafting APTES (3-aminopropyltrietoxysilane), and grafting quaternized APTES. Further, they assessed how these modifications affected the physicochemical properties and protein interaction. The soaking method enhanced the wettability and apatite layer formation. However, soaking in SBF promoted thicker hydroxyapatite layer formation than TRIS. Grafting introduced amine groups to the BG surface, reducing its wettability but potentially improving specific protein interactions due to the presence of these functional groups. APTES grafting resulted in a higher affinity for fibronectin, leading to stronger and more uniform protein adsorption on the bioactive glasses, confirmed using confocal microscopy [244, 245]. As fibronectin plays a crucial role in mediating integrin-mediated cell signalling, Aung et al. grafted it on zirconia disks. The protein grafting imparted enhanced wettability and surface roughness to the zirconia disks. When the untreated zirconia disks showed a contact angle of 71.7 (± 1.65°), the fibronectin-treated disks reduced the contact angle to 21.7° ± 2.92°when treated with 10 μg/ml of fibronectin. MG 63 cells were able to attach and proliferate well on these fibronectin-functionalized disks, and also showed an enhanced ALP activity and mineralization [246]. Owing to the role of RGD motifs of proteins in mediating cellular attachment, efforts have been made to coat proteins and peptides on ceramic bone scaffolds to promote faster osseointegration. For instance, in 2013, HAP fibres were fabricated via electrospinning, and RGD peptide was incorporated to form the nanocomposite bone scaffold. Surprisingly, MC3T3-E1 cells showed higher expression (tenfold higher than that of normal HA scaffold) of bone sialoprotein (BSP), which promotes the attachment and differentiation of osteogenic cells [181]. Later in 2022, Migliorini et al. proved that ceramic scaffolds functionalized with RGD peptide promoted in vivo ossification using a rabbit model. Alumina cylinders were coated with silica coating and further coated with RGD derived from fibronectin. In vivo, RGD-functionalized ceramics facilitated faster osteoid integration with the implant than titanium implants, as depicted in Fig. 9. The osteoid formation and bone-to-implant contact showed notable improvement between 6 and 12 weeks [247]. Recently, in 2024, Garcia-de-Albeniz et al. functionalized polymer-infiltrated zirconia scaffolds with RGD peptide along with the antibacterial peptide LF1-11. These functionalized ceramics enhanced the adhesion and spreading of human mesenchymal stem cells compared to the non-functionalized ones. The average cell area in the functionalized scaffolds was fourfold that of the latter. The presence of antibacterial peptides significantly reduced the adhesion of S. aureus [183]. Additionally, nanoscale coatings can also regulate protein adsorption. For instance, when BCP scaffolds were dip-coated with nano-hydroxyapatite, protein adsorption, apatite formation, and osteoblast formation were substantially increased compared to uncoated BCP scaffolds. The findings can be attributed to the increased nanoscale topography and surface energy of the coated scaffolds [248].

Composite and hybrid system

Incorporating ceramic particles into polymeric matrices boosted protein adsorption and osteoinductivity significantly, reinforcing their role as an effective modulator of protein adsorption. When nanohydroxyapatite was used to reinforce chitosan composite hydrogel, it promoted better differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells than the hydrogels without the reinforcement. The hydrogel with nanohydroxyapatite showed a significant increase in protein adsorption. The presence of nanohydroxyapatite enhanced the hydrophilicity and surface area of the hydrogel, resulting in increased protein adsorption [249]. Many such studies have been done later to incorporate hydroxyapatite in various composite hydrogels, resulting in good protein adsorption and stem cell differentiation [125, 250]. In 2021, magnesium-doped hydroxyapatite was mixed with polylactic acid (PLA) to develop composite scaffolds via 3D printing, and showed good protein adsorption [251]. Recently, in 2024, Ansari et al. developed a 3D-printed triphasic composite scaffold by incorporating wollastonite into a matrix composed of poly (lactic acid) and polycaprolactone. Surprisingly, the incorporation of wollastonite reduced the protein adsorption. The polylactic acid alone exhibited 104.1 μg/ml of proteins adsorbed on it, whereas the increasing concentration of wollastonite reduced the amount of proteins adsorbed on it. That is, 56.53 μg/ml of proteins were adsorbed on the scaffolds with 5% wollastonite, 45 μg/ml for 10% wollastonite, 43.07 μg/ml for 20% wollastonite, and 20.76 μg/ml for 40% of wollastonite. This was consistent with the cell proliferation study, where the wollastonite incorporation decreased cell proliferation from 24 to 48 h. But the reinforcement supported mineralization and differentiation in cells [252].

Electrical modulation

Electrical cues can modulate cell functionality. Ceramic composites can be made electrically active, thereby altering the protein adsorption behaviour. In one such approach, Ba0.5Sr0.5TiO3 (BST) was incorporated with hydroxyapatite, and it showed a significant adsorption of proteins when protein adsorption studies were carried out using BSA and FBS [52]. The BCP scaffolds were electrically polarized to study the adsorption behaviour of BSA. The polarization enhanced the wettability and protein adsorption of the BCP. The authors found that the surface charge formation via electric polarization can be altered by varying the percentage of hydroxyapatite [253]. In another notable example, the hydroxyapatite surface was electrically stimulated, which in turn enhanced the adsorption of fibronectin. Notably, the RGD sequence of fibronectin, which is crucial for integrin-mediated cell signalling, was exposed to an electric field application. This study reveals that external electrical stimuli can dynamically tune the accessibility of bioactive sites [254].

Recent advances and emerging trends in modulating ceramic–protein interface

Beyond the strategies mentioned above, research has been directed to more novel ways to develop bioactive ceramic scaffolds for bone repair applications. In the last few years, novel approaches have been adopted to transform ceramic scaffolds from passive protein-adsorbing substrates into dynamic biointerfaces capable of adaptive and selective biological communication.

Artificial intelligence and predictive modelling

The interest has been growing in recent years in computational modelling and artificial intelligence. Artificial intelligence and its associated fields, such as deep learning and machine learning, are increasingly being applied in the field of biomaterials. Machine learning helps to study the relation between protein adsorption and surface properties of the material, and to even quantitatively predict the percentage of proteins adsorbed on the material surfaces [255]. Barreiro et al. used molecular dynamics (MD) simulation to understand how the protein silk fibroin interacts with the surface of hydroxyapatite. The MD simulation even helped to understand the conformational changes in the proteins when adsorbed onto the surface [256]. This study provides insights into the potential of MD simulation to predict protein–ceramic interaction at the implantation site. In 2023, Jiang et al. used various computational simulation approaches along with infrared spectroscopy (IR) and ATR-FTIR to study the interactions between two proteins—bovine serum albumin and lysozyme—with silica material [257].

Metal organic framework (MOF) functionalization

Metal–organic frameworks gained attention in recent years. MOFs are highly porous materials formed through organic linking of metal ions or clusters. MOFs are shown to adsorb various proteins [258, 259]. In a study, β-TCP scaffolds were 3D printed and functionalized with zinc–cobalt bimetallic organic framework (Zn/Co-MOF). This imparted ROS scavenging ability to the ceramic scaffolds. Additionally, they enhanced osteogenic differentiation of rBMSCs and the maturation of chondrocytes [260]. Despite many MOF–ceramic hybrids showing promising biological outcomes, there is still no published study (to our knowledge) in the past 5 years that presents a fully quantified protein adsorption assay (e.g. model protein or serum proteins) specifically on MOF–ceramic scaffold surfaces. This represents an opportunity for future work.

Bioinspired approaches

The field of biomaterials has increasingly been attracted to bioinspired strategies, as they emulate the features found in nature. For example, Wu et al. fabricated micro- and nano-structured hierarchical porous calcium phosphate scaffolds, inspired by natural wood architecture. This was achieved via the combined approaches of 3D printing and hydrothermal treatment. The presence of pores in the micro- and nano-levels enhanced the hydrophilicity and protein adsorption of the scaffolds and ultimately promoted the osteoinductivity [261]. Beyond the architectural mimicry, the natural extracellular matrix interactions also inspired the researchers. For instance, the BCP disks were functionalized with a recombinant fusion protein that combines fragments from both fibronectin and cadherin 11. This approach actually mimicked two fundamental events in cell–material interaction—ECM anchoring via fibronectin, and cell–cell communication via cadherin. The functionalization of BCP with the recombinant fusion protein significantly enhanced protein adsorption, cell adhesion, and differentiation. The synergistic presentation of cell-binding domains has contributed to these results. The authors demonstrated that these scaffolds enhanced the phosphorylation of FAK, leading to increased cell adhesion [262]. Similarly, researchers have drawn inspiration from natural adhesive proteins, such as polydopamine secreted by sea mussels. Owing to their inherent adhesive properties, they are well-suited to attach to any material surfaces and also promote protein adsorption. Recently, β-TCP scaffolds were coated with polydopamine, and they were able to significantly enhance cell attachment and in vivo bone regeneration [263]. The various biomimetic approaches can modulate the protein adsorption on ceramic bone scaffolds and are able to bridge the gap between material design and bioactivity.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Protein adsorption can be considered as a biological preface that determines how the implanted biomaterial works. Understanding protein adsorption on ceramic scaffolds is fundamental to advancing bone tissue engineering. Owing to the tunable physicochemical properties of ceramic materials, they can act as good substrates for protein adsorption, which in turn aids in cellular attachment and proliferation. The adsorption of proteins depends on various surface properties of the ceramic scaffolds as well as notable properties of the proteins themselves. The Vroman effect underscores the competitive and dynamic nature of protein adsorption, critical for understanding multi-protein environments. Along with the amount of protein adsorbed on the ceramic material surfaces, the type of proteins, their conformation and orientation, packing density, and exchange dynamics can influence the further biological outcomes.

As protein adsorption is a key factor in governing cellular attachment, differentiation, and proliferation, it can be used as a strategic tool to modulate cell–material interactions. Studies demonstrate that higher protein adsorption correlates with better osteoinduction and bone regeneration in vivo. Focusing on the protein adsorption aspect can result in utilizing ceramic materials for faster bone repair applications. Even though peptide-coated ceramic scaffolds demonstrated promising in vitro and in vivo results, their clinical translation remains limited.

Commercially available ceramic materials also reflect certain interfacial design parameters discussed in this review. For instance, MasterGraft™ (Medtronic, USA) Resorbable Ceramic Granules are medical-grade, highly porous bone void fillers made of HA and β-TCP combination that promotes bone growth at the defect site. The granules exhibit 80% interconnected porosity, which supports osteoinduction. The HA/β-TCP composition, along with the controlled pore architecture, may influence the protein adsorption behaviour, thereby resulting in stem cell attachment, colonization and bone formation. Chronos® (DePuy Synthes, USA), with 33–37% porosity composed of β-TCP, is used as a bone gap filler. In addition, MyBone Custom (Cerhum, Belgium), which contains more than 95% hydroxyapatite, is reported as the first commercially available customized 3D-printed bone grafts certified to ISO 13485 [273], intended for maxillofacial and orthopaedic applications. These products can be considered as the safer and more effective option for bone defect repair. However, mechanical properties, incomplete osseous ingrowth, and bioactivity of the materials remain challenging. Adopting techniques to enhance protein adsorption on implants can help address these limitations by promoting faster osseointegration. With the rise of personalized medicine, 3D printing (additive manufacturing) helps to create complex patient-specific scaffolds. This enables the fabrication of scaffolds with precisely controlled micro- and nano-architectures. These structures will enable fine-tuning of protein adsorption and release, optimizing cellular attachment and subsequent tissue formation. In the case of peptide or protein-incorporated ceramic scaffolds, concerns such as long-term biocompatibility and protein stability must be addressed. Computational models and bioinformatics approaches can be employed to predict protein adsorption behaviour and identify optimal scaffold designs, significantly reducing experimental trial-and-error and accelerating material development. Looking forward, the next generation of ceramic bone scaffolds will likely combine omics technologies and AI-based approaches to develop scaffolds that promote faster bone repair. Furthermore, there is a pressing need for extensive long-term in vivo evaluations to better understand the interactions between ceramic scaffolds, adsorbed proteins, and surrounding biological environments. This would bridge the gap between lab-scale research and clinical applications.
