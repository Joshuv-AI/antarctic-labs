Unveiling disparities: exploring the gender gap in digital literacy among faculty in a higher educational medical institute (DIGI-GAP), a mixed-methods study
Authors: Akanksha Pal Singh, Ravneet Kaur, Rashmi Ramachandran, Margaret Bearman, Abhishek Nagarajappa, Simran Kaur, Ambuj Roy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital literacy is increasingly integral to medical education, yet gender disparities may affect faculty engagement. This exploratory study examined gender-based differences in digital literacy among non-clinical faculty at a premier Indian medical institution, aiming to identify disparities and inform targeted interventions.MethodsA mixed-methods exploratory design was employed, integrating a structured online survey and in-depth semi-structured interviews. The questionnaire, developed specifically for this study after reviewing existing instruments, measured self-reported digital skills, usage frequency, and confidence levels. The survey was distributed to 64 purposively sampled non-clinical faculty. The interviews (conducted among eight faculty members) explored contextual barriers and enablers. Data were collected between March 2024 and January 2025. Chi-square (or Fisher’s exact test where applicable) tests assessed gender-based differences (significance: p < 0.05).ResultsOf twenty-six faculty who responded to the survey (response rate 40.6%), 53.8% were women and 46.2% men. Over 85% reported more than 11 years of experience with computers, smartphones, and the internet; men reported longer cumulative usage. Most described moderate overall digital literacy. All participants (100%) were confident in basic digital skills; however, women reported significantly lower confidence in advanced tasks, and 31% felt behind peers in digital competencies (p = 0.02). Most faculty wished to acquire further digital skills. Qualitative findings revealed that societal expectations, time constraints, and gendered task-delegation patterns may disproportionately limit women’s engagement in our setting. Men were more frequently assigned digital responsibilities. Younger faculty showed higher engagement irrespective of gender. Despite equitable institutional access to tools and training, disparities in participation and confidence persisted.ConclusionsThe gender gap in digital literacy appears to be driven primarily by differences in confidence, perception, and socio-cultural expectations rather than access inequalities in our context. The preliminary findings suggest that gender-responsive training, mentorship, revised task-delegation practices, and institutional policy reforms may help promote equitable faculty participation in the digital academic environment. Since this is a single-site exploratory study, larger studies with more sample size and multiple contexts are needed to confirm and generalize these findings.Trial registrationRegistered with the Clinical Trials Registry – India. Background

Digital literacy encompasses the skills to use digital technologies safely and securely. It includes basic computer and smartphone operations, retrieval and evaluation of online information digital content creation, and online collaboration [1, 2]. It is a foundational competency for university teachers, shaping how they deliver education and prepare students for a technologically advanced world [3].

In medical education specifically, digital literacy is crucial because students interact with complex digital systems such as electronic health records (EHRs), telemedicine platforms, simulation tools, and large biomedical databases. With evolving healthcare ecosystems, clinicians increasingly need to evaluate online medical research, participate in digital collaboration networks, and utilize decision-support tools [4]. Therefore, digital literacy empowers medical educators to support clinical reasoning, ensure safe patient care, and promote effective learning [5]. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the integration of information and communication technology (ICT) into teaching strategies, which highlighted varying levels of proficiency among medical teachers [6, 7].

Gender has been recognized as a significant factor influencing digital literacy across multiple settings [8].

Globally, the digital divide, in terms of both access and skills, has been documented to disproportionately affect women, shaped by cultural norms, economic constraints and stereotypes that associate technology and digital skills with men [8–12].

According to the Global System for Mobile Communications Association’s Mobile Gender Gap Report 2025, India had the second highest (33%) gender gap in internet use and smartphone ownership (39%)in the Asia-Pacific region [13].

Faculty in higher education institutions (HEI) are purportedly better equipped to overcome these barriers, given equitable access to resources, training and the need to use technology in everyday tasks [14]. HEIs have specific technology requirements for faculty, including the use of electronic health records, learning and students’ management systems, and simulation technologies, and thus, gender disparities may influence technology-related decision-making [15, 16]. However, whether women in HEIs are impacted by gender-related constraints, norms, and biases remains unexplored.

While a growing body of literature addresses gender disparities in digital literacy across various educational settings, the specific context of teaching faculty in Medical Institutes remains largely unstudied. Given the potentially amplified influence of medical faculty on a large number of students, who in turn interact with millions of patients, understanding this gap is important. This exploratory study, therefore, examined gender-based differences in digital literacy among pre and para-clinical faculty at a premier Indian medical institute, with an aim of generating preliminary insights to inform future research and interventions.

Methodology

Aims and objectives of the study

This study aimed to understand the use of digital techniques and the perceptions of gender inequality in digital literacy amongst faculty members in pre and para-clinical departments of a premier medical educational institute in India. The primary objective was to explore whether women faculty members perceived fewer opportunities to use digital tools for teaching and research, as compared to men. The study also intended to examine the actual difference in digital literacy levels between genders, to understand men’s perspective of gender disparities, and to provide preliminary recommendations to address the identified gaps.

Study design

An exploratory mixed methods design was used, sequentially integrating both quantitative and qualitative components. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institute Ethics Committee of the All-India Institute of Medical Sciences, New Delhi (Ref. No. AIIMSA00755/02.02.2024, RP-52/2024), and registered with CTRI (Reg. No. CTRI/2024/11/076904, Ref No. REF/2024/11/094394) [Registered on: 18/11/2024].

Setting and participants

The study was conducted at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), New Delhi, a nationally ranked premier medical institution of India [17], from March 2024 to January 2025.

The participants were non-clinical faculty members of both genders (men and women) from pre- and para-clinical departments. (Anatomy, Physiology, Biochemistry, Pharmacology, Pathology, Microbiology) Pre- and para-clinical faculty were selected because they conduct greater proportions of large and small-group undergraduate teaching, and were therefore expected to have greater engagement with digital teaching resources. While pre and para-clinical faculty also contribute to post-graduation teaching and PhD supervision, the undergraduate focus permitted exploration of usage of digital tools in foundational medical education.

Faculty members not engaged in teaching, training, or research with undergraduate medical students were excluded from the study, and the survey was not sent to them. The questionnaire included gender as a mandatory variable with binary response options (male/female). Individuals who preferred not to disclose gender or chose not to participate are reflected within the overall non-response and were not captured separately.

Sampling and recruitment

A four-level hierarchy system exists in faculty positions (Assistant Professor, Associate Professor, Additional Professor and Professor) within the organization. To ensure representation across all seniority levels and both genders, a purposive sampling strategy was used. Participation was voluntary, and all participants gave informed consent before taking part. The study was conducted in two phases.

The first phase consisted of a quantitative component in which an online non-anonymous structured survey with a brief participant information sheet was distributed via email to 64 faculty members across four hierarchial levels (four male and four female faculty members at each hierarchical level). Initially, 4 male and 4 female faculty members were invited at each level (n = 32), followed by an additional round of invitations to faculty members per level in cases of non-response, resulting in a total of 64 invitations. In the second phase, a purposive sub-sample was selected from survey respondents, based on gender, seniority and survey response patterns, to ensure diversity of perspectives.

Development of questionnaire and interview guide

The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study, as no existing validated instrument fully addressed the institutional context and the study objectives. The development process involved a thorough review of instruments used in prior studies of digital literacy in educational and health care settings (Lucas et al(2021), Digal et al. (2024), Habiba and Ahmed, (2020); retention of relevant concepts; and rephrasing or redesigning of all items to fit the institutional and cultural context. The constructs were aligned with the TPACK framework.

The survey was divided four sections: (1) demographic details; (2) self-rated digital literacy and usage, assessed using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from very low to very high; (3) frequency of usage, assessed using a Likert scale from very frequently to never; and (4) attitudes and perceptions, assessed using a 5-point Likert scale from strongly agree to strongly disagree, along with yes/no/maybe responses. The instrument was not validated for psychometric properties, but was subjected to internal review by the research team prior to administration. The questionnaire is provided as supplementary material 2.

An interview guide based on literature review and findings of phase I was prepared. The interview explored participants’ backgrounds, their own engagement with the digital and what they thought about gender differences, including the relationship between societal expectations and their own experiences, including challenges and barriers. The interview guide is provided as supplementary material 1.

Data collection

Phase 1 (quantitative)

Faculty members who agreed to participate in the study signed a consent form electronically, and completed the online survey. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout.

Phase 2 (qualitative)

A written informed consent was obtained for the conduct and audio-recording of the interviews. Both men and women faculty members were invited for the interviews. Semi-structured interviews were conducted in a private location within the institute by a trained qualitative researcher. These interviews were designed to gain an understanding of the participants’ experiences, practices, opportunities, and challenges related to digital literacy. Each interview lasted approximately 30–45 min. Participants had the option to choose English or Hindi, and could pause or terminate the interview at any point.

Data were analysed after each interview to look for new information. Subsequent interviewees were invited if data saturation was not achieved. A total of eight interviews were conducted (four among men and four among women), after which data saturation was deemed to be achieved. The interviewees included four women and four men.

Audio recordings were carefully transcribed verbatim using Turboscribe (an AI-assisted transcription platform). The transcripts were reviewed and de-identified to protect the privacy of participants during the data analysis.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were entered in Microsoft Excel and analyzed using statistical software SPSS version 29. Demographic characteristics, usage of digital devices, purpose of using digital devices and self-rated digital literacy levels, were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Each Likert item was assigned a numerical value (e.g., 1 = very low/strongly disagree, 5 = very high/strongly agree). Given the small sample size and the ordinal nature of the Likert-scale responses, we used non-parametric statistical tests and reported medians with interquartile ranges. Chi-square test (or Fisher’s Exact test when expected cell counts were < 5) was used to assess associations between gender and other categorical variables such as self-reported digital literacy level, frequency of digital tool usage, attitudes and perceptions toward digital technologies, and confidence in specific digital skills. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Qualitative data were analyzed using NVivo version 15. The transcripts were examined to find emerging themes and patterns. A thematic analysis was conducted iteratively, and coding was done to identify key experiences, practices, and challenges related to digital literacy. The codes were organized into broader themes, such as perceived digital literacy levels, attitudes towards digital technology, usage of digital technology, and opportunities to learn digital skills. To strengthen the validity and credibility of the qualitative data, verification was conducted by member-checking (sharing findings with the interviewed faculty to validate the researchers’ interpretations) and triangulation of qualitative findings with quantitative results.

Conceptual framework

We adopted the TPACK (Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge) framework [18] to explore the digital gender gap by identifying how gender influences teachers’ knowledge and integration of technology, pedagogy, and content. (Fig. 1).

Conceptual framework (TPACK model)

This framework helped in structured analysis of gender differences in technological self-efficacy, attitudes, and socio-cultural factors that influence digital literacy in educational settings.

Characteristics of the study participants

Digital technology usage by participants

The majority of participants reported 11–25 years of usage across all device categories, with men consistently showing longer usage durations across all three domains than women. The maximum number of participants had used a computer and the internet for 21–25 years, with the number of men exceeding that of women in both parameters (M: W, 6:4 and 7:5, respectively). Smartphone usage was most commonly reported as 11–15 years, again with more men than women.

Self-rated digital literacy levels amongst the participants

Most participants self-rated their digital literacy skills as ‘Moderate’. One woman rated her literacy as ‘low’ and one man as ‘very high’. Self-reported confidence in basic digital skills was high among the participants (Table 2). All 26 respondents (100%) reported confidence in computer use, internet browsing, and email communication. Confidence was lower for data analysis (77%) and lowest for cybersecurity awareness (35%).

Utilization patterns of digital tools and technologies

Most participants used digital technology for their core tasks, such as creating documents, sending emails, creating PowerPoint presentations, etc. Men used cloud storage and file-sharing sites most frequently. Younger age groups tended to use search engines more frequently.

Most digital activities, showed no significant gender differences (p > 0.05), A statistically significant gender difference was observed in social media usage (p = 0.028) (Fig. 2).

Attitudes and perceptions of participants

The majority (88%) of participants reportedly enjoyed using digital devices. All participants expressed willingness to learn more about digital technologies; 96.2% believed that incorporating digital tools and resources could improve their teaching.

A significantly higher proportion of women (31%) felt that they lagged behind their colleagues in digital skills (p = 0.02) (Fig. 3),although both groups agreed on the existence of gender gap in digital literacy at the societal level (Fig. 3).

Gender differences in digital literacy perceptions and experiences

Phase 2-Findings from the in-depth interviews

A total of eight interviews were conducted, including four men and four women. There are three analytical areas in this context. The first describes differences between genders; the second outlines barriers and facilitators to the uptake of digital technologies; and the third suggests strategies to address the gender imbalance.

Participants’ accounts of gender differences

Perceived digital literacy levels

Most participants, regardless of gender, rated their digital literacy as “moderate,” indicating awareness of proficiency gaps and recognition of the need for further learning.

“I would consider myself moderate…. because there are standards… with respect to other faculty.” (A senior-level faculty member- man),

“Moderate, not very high but moderate”(A junior-level faculty member- woman).

Generational Differences

Both groups perceived younger and male colleagues as more conversant with new digital tools.

“The young generation of facultys are more knowledgeable technologically compared to the older generation” (A senior-level faculty member- man),

“Male colleagues probably know about new technology.” (A senior-level faculty member- woman).

Attitudes toward digital technology

Both men and women faculty members demonstrated strong interest in improving their digital skills, particularly in Artificial Intelligence (AI) and data management.

“I would like to learn to make my PPT more interesting by using AI.” (A senior-level faculty member- man),

“I want to learn how AI can be used in our core responsibilities…” (A mid-level faculty member- woman).

Help-seeking behaviour

A nuanced gender difference emerged in help-seeking. While women reported greater openness to seeking assistance, men appeared more reluctant due to concerns about self-image or cultural expectations.

“Male faculty might not be comfortable accepting that they don’t know something” (A senior-level faculty member (man),

“A male might also not know how to do a particular thing, but he cannot give it to a female, saying I can’t do it, he will try to figure out how to do it” (A mid-level faculty member, woman).

Gendered task assignment

Digital and technical responsibilities were more frequently assigned to male faculty, often based on assumed technical competence.

“I remember when new LCD TVs were being installed in the department. The head gave the responsibility to two male faculty… thinking that men might do it better.” (A senior-level faculty member- man),

“Sometimes the projector goes off or something, the male will be called upon” (A mid-level faculty member- woman).

“work where digital technology is used, or any purchasing or related work generally goes to male” (A mid-level faculty member- man).

Women faculty members demonstrated hesitation to learn digital techniques and a preference to frequently delegating digital tasks to their male colleagues when appropriate expertise was available.

“A female will just ask a male… ‘please, you know it, do it for me.” (A mid-level faculty member- woman).

Technology usage patterns

Both men and women used digital tools for personal tasks and basic academic functions (e.g., Microsoft Office, UPI payments) with similar frequency.

“I make online payments… I am very comfortable doing this.” (A senior-level faculty member- man).

“I create PowerPoint videos for my classes, participate in online meetings via Zoom and Teams, and perform data entry and analysis in Excel, including basic statistics.” (A senior-level faculty member- woman).

Though there was equity in using basic applications and basic digital tasks, advanced applications like coding, data analysis software, and machine learning tools were more commonly used by men.

“For my data analysis… I do Python coding, MATLAB…” (A Junior level faculty member, man.

Clear generational differences were also evident, in digital habits and tools used with younger faculty showing higher engagement with advanced digital devices and applications, while older faculty often favoured traditional methods such as transparencies.

“Some senior faculty still use transparencies… while younger faculty use stylus-enabled devices.” (A senior-level faculty member (man).

“Youngsters… they are definitely more savvy than others irrespective of gender.” (A mid-level faculty member (woman).

Discussion

This exploratory, single-site study offers insights into gender gaps in digital literacy among pre-clinical faculty at a medical institute in India. Within the context of a resource-rich, equity-oriented setting, findings indicate that gender disparities are not primarily due to differential access to tools or training, but rather by confidence gaps, societal expectations, and gendered patterns of task delegation. Given the small, purposive sample, these findings are contextual to this single-site study.

Consistent with previous research, both men and women in this study reported comparable access to basic digital technologies; however, differences emerged in usage patterns, confidence levels, and perceptions. The majority of participants rated their digital literacy as ‘moderate’; however, a higher proportion of women (31%) reported feeling behind peers in digital skills (p = 0.02. These findings align with Antonio and Tuffley [19], who reported that women in academics tend to underrate their own digital competence relative to actual performance, a phenomenon described as ‘confidence gap’ rather than an actual ‘competence gap ‘Similarly, Lucas et al. [20], in their study among in-service teachers in Portugal, reported that gender affected digital competence ratings, even when overall proficiency levels were comparable, with higher levels favouring the men. Studies from Karnataka, India [21] and Bangladesh [22] further support the observation that women faculty perceive themselves as less digitally proficient than their male counterparts, particularly in formal academic settings.

The perception of digital literacy is an important determinant of use of digital resources and technology. To help with this, institutions may offer confidence-building workshops tailored for women faculty that focus on emphasizing hands-on experiences with advanced tools.

The finding that cybersecurity awareness was the domain with greatest gender disparity reflects patterns documented in previous studies.

The finding that men were more likely to be assigned technology-related tasks in this setting highlights persistent gender stereotypes. Other workplace studies also show that men tend to dominate ICT-related roles, resulting in increased exposure and skill development opportunities. [11, 19] Task delegation practices to ensure equitable distribution of digital responsibilities across gender and seniority levels may be a practical intervention at institutional level.

Time constraints, particularly disproportionate burden of domestic and caregiving responsibilities on women, emerged as a prominent barrier. This finding is consistent with Warschauer [9] who emphasized social inclusion in digital age is shaped not only by access, but by socio-environmental and cultural factors.

A consistent generational pattern emerged across both quantitative and qualitative data, with younger faculty, regardless of gender, were perceived more digitally proficient. This is similar to the findings of Digal et al. [23] and Portillo et al. [24] and Lucas et al. [20] e. With younger generations having better digital literacy, gender disparities may gradually reduce as newer generations assume roles as medical faculty. This underscores the need for mentoring networks that connect older faculty with younger colleagues for mutual knowledge exchange.

Interestingly, a gendered divergence was noted in help-seeking behaviour. Women were more open to seeking help, whereas men were reluctant to do so due to concerns about self-image. [8, 25] As Cheryan et al. [11]observed, masculine norms of self-reliance may impede learning in technical domains. Women faculty’s openness to seeking guidance could be harnessed through mentorship programs and peer-learning initiatives that normalize learning gaps and promote shared digital growth.

The findings that gender disparities persisted, despite equitable access, in a resource-rich institution are noteworthy. It suggests that, within this context, socio-cultural and psychological factors, may be more potent factors leading to gender gap than access. However, these findings are exploratory and contextual. Small sample, self-selection bias and single-site, non-representative sampling frame limit the generalizability of these results. Larger, multi-site studies, with higher response rates, are needed to confirm these patterns across different medical contexts in India and beyond.

Strengths and limitations

Use of exploratory mixed-methods design is a key strength of the study, enabling triangulation of quantitative and qualitative findings, and providing contextual depth. Purposive sampling ensured representation of both genders across all four hierarchical levels. The TPACK framework provided a theoretically coherent lens for interpreting the findings. Validity was achieved by member-checking with a subset of participants.

However, limitations must be acknowledged. The study was conducted at a single premier medical institute, and findings cannot be generalized to less-resourced or regional medical colleges, where digital access and gender norms may differ substantially.

Small sample size for the quantitative component (only 26 participants) limits the statistical power and generalizability of the results. The survey response rate of 40.6% may have introduced non-response bias, as those who did not respond may differ systematically from the respondents. The use of binary gender options (male/female) and making gender a mandatory variable may have contributed to non-response among individuals who preferred not to disclose or did not identify within these categories. Additionally, reasons for non-participation were not captured. Self-reported digital literacy may be affected by social desirability and gender-related confidence gaps. The exclusion of clinical faculty narrowed the scope, limiting the understanding of gender disparities among all medical faculty. These limitations highlight the need for larger, multi-site studies to confirm these preliminary findings.

Conclusion

The DIGI-GAP study provides contextual evidence that the digital gender gap among medical teaching faculty at a premier institute may be less apparent in terms of access, but persists in terms of self-perceptions, gendered patterns of task distribution, and confidence levels. Within this exploratory sample, digital literacy experiences of faculty are shaped more by gendered social norms and responsibilities than by institutional access. The findings point towards the potential value of gender-responsive training, mentorship, and equitable digital participation. Larger, multi-site and longitudinal research is needed to validate these insights.
