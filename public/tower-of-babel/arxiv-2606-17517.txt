Killed resolvents and measure-valued stopping gains for reflected optimal stopping with max-type rewards
Louis Shuo Wang, Jiguang Yu, Ye Liang

Abstract
We study an infinite-horizon optimal stopping problem for a normally reflected two-dimensional diffusion in the positive quadrant with nonsmooth max-type reward G ⁡ ( x 1 , x 2 ) = x 1 ∨ α ​ x 2 G(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}\vee\alpha x_{2} . The paper develops a conditional measure-theoretic framework for the associated reflected obstacle problem. The main innovation is to show that the stopping gain Γ = c + r ​ G − ℒ ​ G \Gamma=c+rG-\mathcal{L}G is a signed measure, not a function: the kink of G G generates an explicit negative surface measure on Δ = { x 1 = α x 2 } \Delta=\{x_{1}=\alpha x_{2}\} . We then prove that the correct potential representation uses the resolvent of the reflected diffusion killed on first entry into the stopping set, rather than the unrestricted reflected resolvent. Under explicit monotonicity, regularity, and measure-superharmonicity assumptions, we derive an epigraph representation, a continuation-side boundary-trace condition, and a candidate verification theorem. The framework clarifies hidden regularity and uniqueness assumptions in multidimensional nonsmooth optimal stopping.

Introduction

1.1
The problem

We consider an infinite-horizon optimal stopping problem for a normally
reflected diffusion
X=(X^{1},X^{2})
living in the closed quadrant

\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}=[0,\infty)^{2}
. The decision maker chooses a stopping time
\tau
to
maximize the discounted expected reward net of a running cost,

V(x)=\sup_{\tau}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[e^{-r\tau}G(X_{\tau})-\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds\right],\qquad G(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}\vee\alpha x_{2}.

(1)

Two structural features distinguish (
1
) from the classical
one-dimensional theory and make it a genuinely two-dimensional, nonsmooth
problem. First, the state process is constrained to the quadrant by normal
reflection at the two coordinate axes, so the generator is supplemented by
Neumann-type boundary behaviour and the corner of the quadrant requires
separate attention. Second, the max-type reward is only piecewise affine: it
has a Lipschitz kink along the diagonal
\Delta=\{x_{1}=\alpha x_{2}\}
, across
which the second derivatives of
G
concentrate as a surface measure. Both
features destroy the smoothness that the textbook free-boundary calculus
silently assumes, and both are routinely glossed over in heuristic
treatments.

Max-type rewards of the form
x_{1}\vee\alpha x_{2}
are the canonical payoff in
the valuation of American options on the maximum of several assets and in
related exchange- and dual-strike contracts; the qualitative structure of the
associated exercise regions was analysed in
Broadie and Detemple 1997
;
Villeneuve 1999
;
Bayer et al. 2020
;
Battauz et al. 2015
;
Dai and Kwok 2005
;
Detemple 2014
;
Soner and Tissot-Daguette 2025
;
Wang and Yu 2025
;
Whitehead et al. 2012
. Reflected
dynamics in the orthant arise when the underlying state is a regulated or
constrained process, as in queueing networks, inventory and production models,
and economic models with reflecting barriers; the probabilistic foundations
go back to the Skorokhod-problem analyses of Tanaka
Tanaka 1979
, Lions
and Sznitman
Lions and Sznitman 1984
, Saisho
Saisho 1987
, and Harrison
and Reiman
Harrison and Reiman 1981
, with general nonsmooth domains and
oblique reflection treated in
Dupuis and Ishii 1993
;
Hino et al. 2021
;
Lundström and Önskog 2019
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2026
;
Lipshutz and Ramanan 2018
;
DeBlassie 1996
;
Duan and Peng 2019
;
Leimkuhler et al. 2023
;

1.3
Three recurring gaps, and what this paper does about them

Treatments of problems of the type (
1
) frequently rely,
explicitly or implicitly, on three steps that are not in fact justified at the
stated level of generality. The purpose of this paper is to isolate these
steps, to correct them, and to assemble what remains into a transparent
conditional framework in which every nonelementary input is named.

(i)

Regularity is not a corollary of being a viscosity supersolution.

A continuous viscosity supersolution of a linear elliptic inequality does not
by itself carry the Sobolev or measure structure needed to run an
Itô–Krylov–Tanaka argument. We therefore separate the verification
principle (Theorem
2.11
) from any regularity claim,
and we state the hypotheses—
W^{2,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}
regularity in the
continuation set, a signed-measure extension of
(\mathcal{L}-r)u-c
, and
membership in a generalized Itô class—as explicit assumptions rather than
as consequences.

(ii)

The stopping-gain object is a signed measure, not a function.

Because
G
has a kink on
\Delta
, the natural object

\Gamma:=c+rG-\mathcal{L}G
is not an ordinary function: its singular part is a
surface measure on
\Delta
produced by the local time of the projected
process
Y=X^{1}-\alpha X^{2}
at zero. We compute this diagonal measure
explicitly via Tanaka’s formula and the occupation-density/co-area identity
(Section
4
), obtaining

\Gamma^{\Delta}(dx)=-\frac{q(x)}{2\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}}\,\sigma_{\Delta}(dx),\qquad q=n^{\top}a\,n\geq 0,

which is nonpositive as a measure. A consequence, easy to overlook, is that a
literal pointwise sign condition such as “
\Gamma\geq 0
on the stopping set”
is meaningless on the part of the stopping set that meets
\Delta
in positive
length unless the diagonal component is separated out.

(iii)

The potential must be killed at the stopping time.

This is the central correction. After applying the Itô–Tanaka identity up
to the optimal stopping time
\tau_{\mathcal{D}}
, the value admits the
potential representation

V(x)=G(x)-\mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathcal{D}}}e^{-rs}\,dA_{s}^{\Gamma}\right]=G(x)-R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}\Gamma(x),

where
R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}
is the resolvent of the reflected diffusion
killed upon entry into
\mathcal{D}
. It is generally not equal
to the unrestricted reflected resolvent
R_{r}^{\mathrm{R}}(\Gamma\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}})
,
because the reflected process, if continued past
\tau_{\mathcal{D}}
, may
re-enter the continuation region
\mathcal{C}
and thereby accumulate spurious
post-stopping occupation. The optimal stopping representation only “sees” the
path up to
\tau_{\mathcal{D}}
, and the potential must be killed accordingly.

1.4
Main contributions

Within the conditional framework, the paper makes the following contributions.
A verification theorem (Theorem
2.11
) is proved under
explicit measure-superharmonicity hypotheses, with no hidden regularity
theorem. A conditional epigraph theorem
(Theorem
3.6
) derives the graph structure of the
stopping set from monotonicity of the stopping advantage
H=V-G
alone, and
yields monotonicity of the boundary under a horizontal monotonicity
hypothesis; we explain (Remark
3.3
) why this
cannot be obtained from order preservation of the diffusion, the relevant
coupling having the wrong sign under our cost convention. The singular
stopping-gain measure is computed in closed form
(Section
4
), including the reflection corner
analysis (Lemma
4.3
). The corrected
killed-resolvent representation is established
(Theorem
5.3
) and recast as a
continuation-side boundary-trace condition
(Proposition
5.5
). Finally, candidate boundaries are
validated by a verification argument
(Theorem
5.7
) rather than by appeal to a
Fredholm-type uniqueness statement that the trace condition alone does not
support.

The novelty is not a single hard theorem but the discipline of the framework:
each step that the heuristic literature performs silently is here either
proved or flagged as a model-specific hypothesis, and the two genuinely
substantive corrections—the measure-valued treatment of the diagonal kink
and the killed (rather than unrestricted) resolvent—are made precise and
quantitative. This is what makes the framework safe to instantiate in concrete
models, where the named assumptions can be checked one at a time.

2
Model, obstacle problem, and verification

This section fixes the probabilistic framework, the sign convention for the
obstacle problem, and the verification principle used throughout the paper.
The formulation is deliberately conditional: the reflected diffusion is
assumed to have the required Markov and continuity properties, and the
verification theorem is stated under explicit measure-superharmonicity
hypotheses. We do not infer Sobolev or measure regularity from viscosity
supersolution status alone.

2.1
Reflected diffusion and standing assumptions

Let

\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:=[0,\infty)^{2},\qquad\mathring{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}:=(0,\infty)^{2}.

Let
W=(W^{1},W^{2})
be a two-dimensional Brownian motion on a filtered
probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})

satisfying the usual conditions. For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, we consider the
normally reflected diffusion

X_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}\mu(X_{s})\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X_{s})\,dW_{s}+L_{t},\qquad t\geq 0,

(2)

where
L=(L^{1},L^{2})
is the reflection process. The reflection is normal on the
coordinate axes:

L^{i}\text{ is continuous and nondecreasing},\qquad L^{i}_{0}=0,\qquad\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{t}^{i}>0\}}\,dL_{t}^{i}=0,\quad i=1,2.

(3)

Let
a(x):=\sigma(x)\sigma(x)^{\top}
. The interior generator is

\mathcal{L}f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mu_{i}(x)\partial_{i}f(x)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}(x)\partial_{ij}f(x),\qquad x\in\mathring{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}.

Assumption 2.1

(Standing assumptions on the reflected diffusion)
.

The following hypotheses hold.

(A1)

The coefficients
\mu:\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
are
locally Lipschitz and have at most linear growth.

(A2)

The diffusion matrix
a=\sigma\sigma^{\top}
is locally uniformly elliptic in

\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
: for every compact
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
there exists
\lambda_{K}>0
such
that
\xi^{\top}a(x)\xi\geq\lambda_{K}|\xi|^{2}
for all
x\in K
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

(A3)

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, the reflected SDE
(
2
)–(
3
) has a unique strong
solution. The family
(X^{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}
is strong Markov.

(A4)

The solution depends continuously on the initial condition in the following
pathwise sense: for every
T>0
, every compact
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, and some

q\geq 1
,

\lim_{y\to x}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|X_{t}^{y}-X_{t}^{x}|^{q}\right]=0,\qquad x\in K.

(A5)

The reward and running cost satisfy
G(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}\vee\alpha x_{2}
with

\alpha>0
, and
c:\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\to[0,\infty)
is continuous. Additional local
regularity of
c
is imposed only when needed.

(A6)

The value function introduced in (
4
) below is finite and
has polynomial growth: there exist
C_{V}>0
and
p\geq 1
such that

0\leq V(x)\leq C_{V}(1+|x|^{p}),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}.

Moreover, the payoff terms appearing below are integrable for all stopping
times under consideration.

Remark 2.2

(On reflected SDE assumptions)
.

Assumptions (A1)–(A4) are stated explicitly. They should
not be read as automatic consequences of the displayed SDE alone. Existence,
uniqueness, and stability for reflected SDEs are delicate properties of the
Skorokhod problem, the domain, the reflection field, and the coefficients

Tanaka 1979
;
Lions and Sznitman 1984
;
Saisho 1987
;
Harrison and Reiman 1981
;
Dupuis and Ishii 1993
.
For normal reflection in sufficiently regular domains, the classical framework
is provided by Lions and Sznitman
Lions and Sznitman 1984
. In the present
paper these properties are part of the standing hypotheses.

Lemma 2.3

(A sufficient Lyapunov condition for discounted integrability)
.

Suppose there exists a function
\Psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
,
\Psi\geq 1
, satisfying
the reflected Neumann condition

\partial_{i}\Psi=0\quad\text{on }\{x_{i}=0\},\qquad i=1,2,

and constants
K\geq 0
,
\lambda<r
, and
C_{\Psi}>0
such that

\mathcal{L}\Psi(x)\leq\lambda\Psi(x)+K,\qquad x\in\mathring{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+},

and

G(x)^{+}+c(x)\leq C_{\Psi}\Psi(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}.

Then

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds\right]<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[e^{-rt}G(X_{t})^{+}\big]<\infty.

Proof.

Apply Itô’s formula with reflection to
e^{-rt}\Psi(X_{t})
and localize. The
Neumann condition
\partial_{i}\Psi=0
on
\{x_{i}=0\}
removes the reflection
term, since
dL^{i}
is carried by
\{X^{i}=0\}
. Writing

\phi(t):=\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{-rt}\Psi(X_{t})]
, the drift inequality

\mathcal{L}\Psi-r\Psi\leq(\lambda-r)\Psi+K
gives, after taking expectations
and using the martingale part has zero mean,

\phi^{\prime}(t)\leq-(r-\lambda)\phi(t)+Ke^{-rt}.

Multiplying by the integrating factor
e^{(r-\lambda)t}
and integrating from

0
to
t
yields

\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{-rt}\Psi(X_{t})]\leq e^{-(r-\lambda)t}\Psi(x)+K\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(r-\lambda)(t-s)}\,e^{-rs}\,ds.

Since
r-\lambda>0
and
r>0
, the right-hand side is bounded uniformly in

t\geq 0
, so
\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{x}[e^{-rt}\Psi(X_{t})]<\infty
; in particular
the discounted moments of
\Psi
are finite. Integrating the differential
inequality against
\mathcal{L}\Psi-r\Psi\leq-(r-\lambda)\Psi+K
and using
Tonelli’s theorem shows that

\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rs}\Psi(X_{s})\,ds\big]<\infty
. The estimates
for
G^{+}
and
c
follow from the pointwise bound
G^{+}+c\leq C_{\Psi}\Psi
.
∎

2.2
Value function and dynamic programming

Let
\mathcal{T}
denote the set of
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-stopping times with values
in
[0,\infty]
. We use the convention

e^{-r\tau}G(X_{\tau}):=0
on
\{\tau=\infty\}
. The value function is

V(x)=\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[e^{-r\tau}G(X_{\tau})-\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds\right],\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}.

(4)

The stopping and continuation sets are

\mathcal{D}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:V(x)=G(x)\},\qquad\mathcal{C}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:V(x)>G(x)\}.

Lemma 2.4

(Immediate stopping lower bound)
.

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,
V(x)\geq G(x)
.

Proof.

Choose the stopping time
\tau=0
in (
4
).
∎

Proposition 2.5

(Dynamic programming principle)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, for every bounded stopping
time
\theta
and every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,

\displaystyle V(x)=\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\Bigg[

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\theta}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds+e^{-r\tau}G(X_{\tau})\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau\leq\theta\}}

\displaystyle+e^{-r\theta}V(X_{\theta})\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau>\theta\}}\Bigg].

Proof.

This is the standard dynamic programming principle for an optimal stopping
problem driven by a strong Markov process
El Karoui 1981
;
Peskir and Shiryaev 2006
;
Wang et al. 2025
.
The proof uses the strong Markov property at the bounded stopping time

\theta
, concatenation of stopping rules after
\theta
, and the
integrability hypotheses in Assumption
2.1
. No
continuity of bounded stopping-time payoffs is inferred from the ordinary
Feller property.
∎

Lemma 2.6

(Lower semicontinuity of the value)
.

Assume, in addition to Assumption
2.1
, that the
uniform integrability required to pass to the limit below holds locally in the
initial state. Then
V
is lower semicontinuous on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. If
V
is
continuous, then
\mathcal{D}
is closed and
\mathcal{C}
is open.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
and
\varepsilon>0
. Choose a bounded stopping time

\tau\leq T
such that

V(x)\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[e^{-r\tau}G(X_{\tau})-\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds\right]+\varepsilon.

For
y
close to
x
, realize
X^{x}
and
X^{y}
on the same probability space
and use the same stopping rule
\tau
. By the pathwise continuity in
Assumption
2.1
(A4), continuity of
G
and

c
, and the stated uniform integrability,

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-r\tau}G(X_{\tau}^{y})-\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-rs}c(X_{s}^{y})\,ds\right]\longrightarrow\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-r\tau}G(X_{\tau}^{x})-\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-rs}c(X_{s}^{x})\,ds\right].

Since the same
\tau
is admissible for the process started from
y
,

V(y)\geq\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-r\tau}G(X_{\tau}^{y})-\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-rs}c(X_{s}^{y})\,ds\right].

Therefore
\liminf_{y\to x}V(y)\geq V(x)-\varepsilon
. Letting

\varepsilon\downarrow 0
proves lower semicontinuity. If
V
is continuous,
then
H:=V-G
is continuous and nonnegative, so
\mathcal{D}=\{H=0\}
is closed
and
\mathcal{C}=\{H>0\}
is open.
∎

2.3
Obstacle problem and viscosity convention

The obstacle problem is written in max form:

\max\{(\mathcal{L}-r)V-c,\;G-V\}=0\quad\text{in }\mathring{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}.

(5)

The normal reflection condition is

\partial_{i}V=0\quad\text{on }\{x_{i}=0\},\qquad i=1,2,

(6)

understood in the relaxed reflected-viscosity sense below. For a smooth test
function
\varphi
, define

F[\varphi](x):=\max\{(\mathcal{L}-r)\varphi(x)-c(x),\;G(x)-\varphi(x)\}.

(7)

At a contact point,
u(x)=\varphi(x)
, so the second term in
(
7
) equals
G(x)-u(x)
.

Definition 2.7

(Reflected viscosity solution)
.

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous and of polynomial growth.

(i)

u
is a
reflected viscosity subsolution
of
(
5
)–(
6
) if, whenever

\varphi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
and
u-\varphi
has a local maximum at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,
with
u(x)=\varphi(x)
, the following hold. If
x\in\mathring{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}
, then

\max\{(\mathcal{L}-r)\varphi(x)-c(x),\;G(x)-u(x)\}\leq 0.

If
x_{i}=0
for some
i\in\{1,2\}
, then

\min\left\{\max\{(\mathcal{L}-r)\varphi(x)-c(x),\;G(x)-u(x)\},\partial_{i}\varphi(x)\right\}\leq 0.

(ii)

u
is a
reflected viscosity supersolution
if, whenever

\varphi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
and
u-\varphi
has a local minimum at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,
with
u(x)=\varphi(x)
, the following hold. If
x\in\mathring{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}
, then

\max\{(\mathcal{L}-r)\varphi(x)-c(x),\;G(x)-u(x)\}\geq 0.

If
x_{i}=0
for some
i\in\{1,2\}
, then

\max\left\{\max\{(\mathcal{L}-r)\varphi(x)-c(x),\;G(x)-u(x)\},\partial_{i}\varphi(x)\right\}\geq 0.

(iii)

u
is a
reflected viscosity solution
if it is both a reflected

Remark 2.8

(Sign convention)
.

The convention in Definition
2.7
is the standard one
for an equation
F=0

Crandall et al. 1992
: subsolutions are tested
from above and satisfy

, while supersolutions are tested from below and

B_{i}\varphi=\partial_{i}\varphi

Proposition 2.9

(Viscosity characterization of the value)
.

Under the dynamic programming principle and the standing integrability
hypotheses,
V
is a reflected viscosity solution of
(
5
)–(
6
).

Proof.

The proof is the standard viscosity argument from the dynamic programming

;

;

. At an interior

gives

2.4
Verification under measure superharmonicity

We now state the verification principle in the form used later. The theorem is
intentionally formulated with explicit regularity and measure assumptions. A
continuous viscosity supersolution of a linear elliptic inequality does not by
itself provide the Sobolev or measure structure needed for an
Itô–Krylov–Tanaka formula
Krylov 1987
.

Let
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathring{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}
be open and define

\tau_{\mathcal{O}^{c}}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}\notin\mathcal{O}\}
.

Definition 2.10

(Generalized Itô class)
.

A continuous function
u:\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to belong to the generalized
Itô class for
X
if the following holds. Whenever the distribution

(\mathcal{L}-r)u-c
is represented in the interior by a signed Radon measure

\mu_{u}
, there is a corresponding signed continuous additive functional

A^{\mu_{u}}
such that, after localization,

e^{-rt}u(X_{t})=u(x)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-rs}\,dA_{s}^{\mu_{u}}+M_{t}+\text{reflection boundary terms},

where
M
is a local martingale. If
u
satisfies the reflected Neumann
condition in the trace sense, the reflection boundary terms vanish.

Theorem 2.11

(Verification under measure superharmonicity)
.

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous and of polynomial growth. Assume:

(V1)

u\geq G
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
.

(V2)

u=G
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\setminus\mathcal{O}
.

(V3)

For some
p>2
,
u\in W^{2,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{O})
,
and
(\mathcal{L}-r)u-c=0
a.e. in
\mathcal{O}
.

(V4)

On
\mathring{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}
, the distribution

(\mathcal{L}-r)u-c
extends to a signed Radon measure
\mu_{u}
satisfying

\mu_{u}\leq 0
in the sense of measures; equivalently, for every nonnegative

\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathring{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+})
,

\langle(\mathcal{L}-r)u-c,\phi\rangle\leq 0
.

(V5)

u
belongs to the generalized Itô class of
Definition
2.10
, and the reflected boundary
contribution is nonpositive in the supermartingale calculation. In particular,
this holds if
u
satisfies the normal-reflection condition
\partial_{i}u=0

on
\{x_{i}=0\}
,
i=1,2
, in the relevant trace sense.

(V6)

The localization and uniform-integrability conditions
needed to take expectations and pass to the limit hold for
u(X)
,
G(X)
,

c(X)
, and the additive functional
A^{\mu_{u}}
.

Then
u\geq V
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. If, in addition,
\tau_{\mathcal{O}^{c}}<\infty

\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. for every
x
, and the stopped Itô formula is exact on

[0,\tau_{\mathcal{O}^{c}}]
, then
u=V
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
and
\tau_{\mathcal{O}^{c}}
is
optimal.

Proof.

Let
\tau
be a stopping time and let
(\tau_{n})
be a localizing sequence such
that all terms below are integrable. Applying the generalized
Itô–Krylov–Tanaka formula
Krylov 1987
;
Revuz and Yor 2013
to

e^{-rt}u(X_{t})
on
[0,\tau_{n}]
gives

e^{-r\tau_{n}}u(X_{\tau_{n}})=u(x)+\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds+\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}e^{-rs}\,dA_{s}^{\mu_{u}}+M_{\tau_{n}},

after the reflection contribution has been controlled by (V5). Since

\mu_{u}\leq 0
, the finite-variation term associated with
A^{\mu_{u}}
is
nonpositive. Taking expectations gives

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[e^{-r\tau_{n}}u(X_{\tau_{n}})-\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds\right]\leq u(x).

Using
u\geq G
,

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[e^{-r\tau_{n}}G(X_{\tau_{n}})-\int_{0}^{\tau_{n}}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds\right]\leq u(x).

Letting
n\to\infty
by (V6) and taking the supremum over
\tau

yields
V(x)\leq u(x)
.

Now apply the same formula on
[0,t\wedge\tau_{\mathcal{O}^{c}}]
. On

\mathcal{O}
, assumption (V3) gives
(\mathcal{L}-r)u-c=0
, so no
measure-superharmonic defect is accumulated before
\tau_{\mathcal{O}^{c}}
. Hence

u(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[e^{-r(t\wedge\tau_{\mathcal{O}^{c}})}u(X_{t\wedge\tau_{\mathcal{O}^{c}}})-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{\mathcal{O}^{c}}}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds\right].

Letting
t\to\infty
and using (V2) and (V6) gives

u(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[e^{-r\tau_{\mathcal{O}^{c}}}G(X_{\tau_{\mathcal{O}^{c}}})-\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathcal{O}^{c}}}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds\right]\leq V(x).

Together with
V\leq u
, this proves
u=V
, and
\tau_{\mathcal{O}^{c}}
is
optimal.
∎

Remark 2.12

(No hidden regularity theorem)
.

Theorem
2.11
is a verification theorem, not a
regularity theorem. The assumptions that
u\in W^{2,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}
, that

(\mathcal{L}-r)u-c
extends to a nonpositive signed measure, and that the
generalized Itô formula applies are substantive hypotheses

Krylov 1987
;
Bensoussan and Lions 1982
;
Yu et al. 2026
;
Friedman 1982
. They are not deduced here
from viscosity supersolution status.

3
Conditional geometry of the stopping set

This section isolates the geometric assumptions needed to represent the
stopping set as an epigraph. The main point is that the graph structure is not
derived here from order preservation of the reflected diffusion, nor from
monotonicity of the infinitesimal stopping gain. It is derived from a direct
monotonicity assumption on the stopping advantage
H:=V-G
.

3.1
Structural monotonicity assumptions

Throughout this section define

H(x):=V(x)-G(x)
,

\mathcal{D}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:H(x)=0\}
,
and

\mathcal{C}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:H(x)>0\}
.
By Lemma
2.4
,
H\geq 0
. The following assumption is the
structural input for the graph theorem.

Assumption 3.1

(Vertical monotonicity of the stopping advantage)
.

For every
x_{1}\geq 0
, the map
x_{2}\longmapsto H(x_{1},x_{2})
is nonincreasing on

[0,\infty)
.

When a monotonicity statement for the boundary itself is required, we impose
the following additional condition.

Assumption 3.2

(Horizontal monotonicity of the stopping advantage)
.

For every
x_{2}\geq 0
, the map
x_{1}\longmapsto H(x_{1},x_{2})
is monotone on

[0,\infty)
, with a direction independent of
x_{2}
. That is, either all
horizontal sections are nonincreasing, or all horizontal sections are
nondecreasing.

Remark 3.3

(Logical status of the graph assumption)
.

The epigraph theorem below assumes monotonicity of
H=V-G
. We do not claim
that Assumption
3.1
follows from order preservation of the
reflected SDE or from monotonicity of
\Gamma:=c+rG-\mathcal{L}G
. Order
preservation may be useful for proving monotonicity of
V
itself under
compatible monotonicity of the reward and cost, but monotonicity of
V
does
not imply monotonicity of
H=V-G
. In particular, a common attempted coupling
proof of vertical monotonicity has the wrong sign under the cost convention
used here. If
z\leq y
coordinatewise and
c
is coordinatewise nonincreasing,
then along an order-preserving coupling
c(X_{s}^{z})-c(X_{s}^{y})\geq 0
. Thus the cost
difference contributes with the opposite sign from the one needed to prove
that
H
is vertically nonincreasing. Any verification of
Assumption
3.1
is therefore a model-specific task.

Remark 3.4

(Optional order-preservation assumption)
.

If the reflected diffusion is order preserving and
c
is coordinatewise
nonincreasing, then one may obtain monotonicity of
V
by the usual coupling
argument. This observation is separate from Assumptions
3.1

and
3.2
, and it is not used to prove the epigraph structure
below.

3.2
Conditional epigraph theorem

For
x_{1}\geq 0
, write the vertical section of the stopping set as

\mathcal{D}(x_{1}):=\{x_{2}\geq 0:(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathcal{D}\}
.

Assumption 3.5

(Nonempty vertical stopping sections)
.

For every
x_{1}\geq 0
,
\mathcal{D}(x_{1})\neq\varnothing
.

Theorem 3.6

(Conditional epigraph structure)
.

Assume that
V
is continuous. Suppose that Assumptions
3.1

and
3.5
hold. Define

b(x_{1}):=\inf\mathcal{D}(x_{1})
for
x_{1}\geq 0
.
Then
b
is well defined with values in
[0,\infty)
, and

\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:x_{2}\geq b(x_{1})\}
.

If, in addition, Assumption
3.2
holds, then
b
is
monotone. More precisely,
H
nonincreasing in
x_{1}
implies
b
nonincreasing,
whereas
H
nondecreasing in
x_{1}
implies
b
nondecreasing.

Proof.

Fix
x_{1}\geq 0
. By Assumption
3.5
,
\mathcal{D}(x_{1})
is
nonempty, so
b(x_{1})<\infty
. Let
z_{2}\in\mathcal{D}(x_{1})
and let
y_{2}\geq z_{2}
.
Since
H\geq 0
and
H(x_{1},z_{2})=0
, Assumption
3.1
gives

0\leq H(x_{1},y_{2})\leq H(x_{1},z_{2})=0
. Hence
H(x_{1},y_{2})=0
, so

y_{2}\in\mathcal{D}(x_{1})
. Therefore every vertical stopping section is an upper
interval.

Because
V
and
G
are continuous,
H
is continuous, so
\mathcal{D}=\{H=0\}

is closed. It follows that
\mathcal{D}(x_{1})=[b(x_{1}),\infty)
, and hence

\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:x_{2}\geq b(x_{1})\}
.

Now assume horizontal monotonicity. If
H
is nonincreasing in
x_{1}
, let

x_{1}\leq y_{1}
and take
x_{2}>b(x_{1})
, so that
H(x_{1},x_{2})=0
. By horizontal
monotonicity,
0\leq H(y_{1},x_{2})\leq H(x_{1},x_{2})=0
, so
x_{2}\in\mathcal{D}(y_{1})
and

b(y_{1})\leq x_{2}
. Letting
x_{2}\downarrow b(x_{1})
gives
b(y_{1})\leq b(x_{1})
, so

b
is nonincreasing. If
H
is nondecreasing in
x_{1}
, the same argument with
the roles of
x_{1}
and
y_{1}
reversed gives
b(x_{1})\leq b(y_{1})
, so
b
is
nondecreasing.
∎

3.3
Nonemptiness as an assumption or barrier condition

Assumption
3.5
is a boundary-at-infinity condition: for
every fixed
x_{1}
, immediate stopping eventually becomes optimal as
x_{2}

increases. This property is not a local consequence of an inequality such as

(\mathcal{L}-r)G-c\leq-\eta
for large
x_{2}
. Such an inequality may be useful
in concrete examples, but by itself it does not rule out stopping strategies
that wait for favourable excursions or exploit movement in the other
coordinate. A rigorous proof of nonemptiness requires a global argument. The
following barrier condition is one possible sufficient hypothesis.

Assumption 3.7

(Optional superharmonic barrier at infinity)
.

There exist
R>0
, a continuous function
W:\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
, and an open set

\mathcal{U}_{R}\supseteq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:x_{2}>R\}
such that:

(B1)

W\geq G
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
.

(B2)

W=G
on
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:x_{2}\geq R\}
.

(B3)

W
satisfies the reflected Neumann condition on the
coordinate axes in the trace or reflected-viscosity sense.

(B4)

W
is globally
r
-superharmonic relative to the running
cost:
(\mathcal{L}-r)W-c\leq 0
in the measure-superharmonic sense required by
Theorem
2.11
.

(B5)

The verification hypotheses of
Theorem
2.11
apply to
W
with continuation region
contained in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\setminus\{x_{2}\geq R\}
.

Proposition 3.8

(Barrier implies nonempty sections)
.

If Assumption
3.7
holds, then
V(x)=G(x)
for all

x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
with
x_{2}\geq R
. Consequently,
Assumption
3.5
holds.

Proof.

By Assumption
3.7
and the verification theorem,

W\geq V
. Since
W=G
on
\{x_{2}\geq R\}
and
V\geq G
, we obtain

G(x)\leq V(x)\leq W(x)=G(x)
for
x_{2}\geq R
. Hence
V=G
on
\{x_{2}\geq R\}
.
Therefore, for every
x_{1}\geq 0
,
[R,\infty)\subseteq\mathcal{D}(x_{1})
, so

\mathcal{D}(x_{1})\neq\varnothing
.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

Proposition
3.8
is only a sufficient condition. In
applications one may verify Assumption
3.5
by other
global arguments. What is not used in this paper is an unsupported inference
from the local far-field inequality
(\mathcal{L}-r)G-c\leq-\eta
alone.

3.4
Regularity status

The preceding theorem gives a graph representation under monotonicity of the
stopping advantage. It does not prove continuity, local Lipschitz regularity,
or smooth fit of the free boundary. Those properties are delicate and depend
on additional hypotheses; they are therefore imposed explicitly when needed.
Let

\Delta:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:x_{1}=\alpha x_{2}\}

denote the diagonal kink of the reward.

Assumption 3.10

(Local free-boundary regularity)
.

Whenever a killed-resolvent boundary characterization or a boundary trace
statement is used, we assume that the boundary
b
obtained in
Theorem
3.6
satisfies

b\in C((0,\infty))\cap W^{1,\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\infty))
.
Moreover, for every compact interval
I\Subset(0,\infty)
,

\inf_{x_{1}\in I}b(x_{1})>0
.

Remark 3.11

(Regularity is not automatic)
.

Assumption
3.10
is not a consequence of
Theorem
3.6
. It is a separate free-boundary
regularity hypothesis. In the optimal stopping literature, continuity,
Lipschitz regularity, and smooth fit are usually proved under additional
problem-specific assumptions. Continuity results for two-dimensional optimal
stopping boundaries and probabilistic proofs of local Lipschitz regularity
require hypotheses beyond the mere existence of an epigraph representation; see
Peskir
Peskir 2019
, De Angelis and Stabile
De Angelis and Stabile 2019
,
De Angelis
De Angelis 2015
, and De Angelis and Peskir

De Angelis and Peskir 2020
.

The following deterministic observation may be useful for excluding jumps when
a strict sign separation of the stopping gain is known. It is not a
probabilistic regularity theorem.

Lemma 3.12

(Strict sign separation excludes jumps away from the diagonal)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{1},x_{2}):x_{2}\geq b(x_{1})\}
, where
b
is monotone.
Let
U\Subset\mathring{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}\setminus\Delta
be open, and suppose that

\Gamma
is continuous on
U
. Assume further that there exists
\eta>0
such
that
\Gamma(x)\geq\eta
for
x\in U\cap\mathcal{D}
and
\Gamma(x)\leq-\eta
for

x\in U\cap\mathcal{C}
. Then
b
has no jump discontinuity whose vertical jump
segment is contained in
U
.

Proof.

Assume, for definiteness, that
b
is nondecreasing and has a right jump at

x_{0}
:
b(x_{0})<b(x_{0}+):=\lim_{x\downarrow x_{0}}b(x)
. Choose

y_{0}\in(b(x_{0}),b(x_{0}+))
such that
z:=(x_{0},y_{0})\in U
. Since
y_{0}>b(x_{0})
, the
epigraph representation gives
z\in\mathcal{D}
, so
\Gamma(z)\geq\eta
. On the
other hand, for
x>x_{0}
sufficiently close to
x_{0}
one has
y_{0}<b(x)
, hence

(x,y_{0})\in\mathcal{C}
. By continuity of
\Gamma
on
U
, letting

x\downarrow x_{0}
gives
\Gamma(z)\leq-\eta
. This contradiction excludes such a
jump. The cases of left jumps and nonincreasing
b
are identical after
reversing the relevant inequalities.
∎

Assumption 3.13

(Weak smooth fit away from the diagonal)
.

At
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-almost every differentiability point

z=(x_{1},b(x_{1}))\in\partial\mathcal{C}\cap\mathring{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}\setminus\Delta
of
the free boundary, the continuation-side non-tangential gradient trace of
V

exists and satisfies

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\to z\\
x\in\mathcal{C}\ \mathrm{n.t.}\end{subarray}}\nabla V(x)=\nabla G(z),\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\to z\\
x\in\mathcal{C}\ \mathrm{n.t.}\end{subarray}}\nabla(V-G)(x)=0.

Remark 3.14

(Use of smooth fit)
.

Assumption
3.13
is imposed when needed; it is not proved
from the standing assumptions. Even for multidimensional diffusions without
reflection, smooth fit depends on regularity of the boundary point and on
additional analytic or probabilistic hypotheses

Peskir 2019
;
De Angelis and Peskir 2020
;
Lamberton and Zervos 2013
. In the present
reflected setting, those hypotheses must be verified separately in any concrete
model.

4
The singular stopping-gain measure

The max-type reward
G(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}\vee\alpha x_{2}
is not
C^{2}
across the
diagonal
\Delta:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:x_{1}=\alpha x_{2}\}
. Consequently, the
object
\Gamma:=c+rG-\mathcal{L}G
must be interpreted as a signed measure
rather than as an ordinary function. This section gives the precise
decomposition of
\Gamma
into its absolutely continuous part away from

\Delta
and its singular diagonal part. The sign convention is fixed
throughout:

\Gamma=c+rG-\mathcal{L}G
.

4.1
The absolutely continuous part

On the two open regions

\mathcal{R}_{1}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:x_{1}>\alpha x_{2}\}

and

\mathcal{R}_{2}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:x_{1}<\alpha x_{2}\}
,
the reward is smooth and equals
G(x)=x_{1}
on
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and

G(x)=\alpha x_{2}
on
\mathcal{R}_{2}
. We write
G_{\mathrm{sm}}
for this
piecewise smooth representative away from
\Delta
. The absolutely continuous
part of the stopping-gain measure is defined by

\Gamma^{\mathrm{ac}}(x):=c(x)+rG(x)-\mathcal{L}G_{\mathrm{sm}}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\setminus\Delta.

(8)

Equivalently,

\Gamma^{\mathrm{ac}}(x)=c(x)+rx_{1}-\mu_{1}(x),\quad x\in\mathcal{R}_{1},\qquad\Gamma^{\mathrm{ac}}(x)=c(x)+r\alpha x_{2}-\alpha\mu_{2}(x),\quad x\in\mathcal{R}_{2}.

There is no second-derivative contribution in either smooth region because

G_{\mathrm{sm}}
is affine there.

4.2
The explicit diagonal measure

Set

\displaystyle Y(x):=x_{1}-\alpha x_{2}
,

\displaystyle n:=\nabla Y=(1,-\alpha)

and

\displaystyle|n|=\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}
.
Then

G(x)=\frac{x_{1}+\alpha x_{2}+|Y(x)|}{2}.

Let

\displaystyle q(x):=n^{\top}a(x)n=a_{11}(x)-2\alpha a_{12}(x)+\alpha^{2}a_{22}(x)
.
For the one-dimensional continuous semimartingale

Y_{t}:=Y(X_{t})=X_{t}^{1}-\alpha X_{t}^{2}
, the continuous martingale quadratic variation
satisfies

d\langle Y\rangle_{t}=q(X_{t})\,dt
.
Tanaka’s formula
Revuz and Yor 2013
;
Karatzas and Shreve 2014
applied to
|Y_{t}|

gives

\displaystyle d|Y_{t}|=\sgn(Y_{t})\,dY_{t}+dL_{t}^{0}(Y)
,
where
L^{0}(Y)
denotes the symmetric local time of
Y
at zero. Hence the
singular finite-variation term in
dG(X_{t})
is

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\,dL_{t}^{0}(Y)
.
The occupation-density formula gives

L_{t}^{0}(Y)=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{|Y(X_{s})|<\varepsilon\}}q(X_{s})\,ds.

In distributional notation this may be written as

\displaystyle dL_{t}^{0}(Y)=q(X_{t})\,\delta_{0}(Y(X_{t}))\,dt
.
Since

\displaystyle\delta_{0}(Y(x))\,dx=\frac{1}{|\nabla Y|}\,\sigma_{\Delta}(dx)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}}\,\sigma_{\Delta}(dx)
,
where
\sigma_{\Delta}
is one-dimensional surface measure on
\Delta
, the
singular part of
\mathcal{L}G
is

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}G)^{\Delta}(dx)=\frac{q(x)}{2\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}}\,\sigma_{\Delta}(dx)
.
Therefore, with the convention
\Gamma=c+rG-\mathcal{L}G
, the diagonal part of

\Gamma
is the signed measure

\Gamma^{\Delta}(dx)=-\frac{q(x)}{2\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}}\,\sigma_{\Delta}(dx).

(9)

Equivalently, for every bounded Borel function
F
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}F(x)\,\Gamma^{\Delta}(dx)=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}}\int_{\Delta}F(x)q(x)\,\sigma_{\Delta}(dx).

Combining (
8
) and (
9
), we obtain the
measure decomposition

\Gamma=\Gamma^{\mathrm{ac}}\,dx+\Gamma^{\Delta}.

(10)

Remark 4.1

(Sign of the diagonal contribution)
.

Since
a(x)
is nonnegative definite,
q(x)=n^{\top}a(x)n\geq 0
. Hence

\Gamma^{\Delta}\leq 0
as a signed measure. Therefore a condition such as
“
\Gamma\geq 0
on a stopping set” cannot be imposed literally as a measure if
the stopping set contains a positive-length portion of
\Delta
, unless the
diagonal component is separated from the absolutely continuous part. In later
arguments, any positivity condition on
\Gamma
must specify whether it
concerns
\Gamma^{\mathrm{ac}}
away from
\Delta
, the full signed measure, or
a decomposition in which the diagonal part is treated separately.

4.3
Reflection and the corner term

The generalized Itô–Tanaka formula for
G(X)
also contains possible
reflection terms. Away from the origin these terms vanish formally because

\partial_{1}G=0
on
\{x_{1}=0,\ x_{2}>0\}
and
\partial_{2}G=0
on

\{x_{2}=0,\ x_{1}>0\}
. Indeed, on
\{x_{1}=0,\ x_{2}>0\}
one has
G=\alpha x_{2}
,
while on
\{x_{2}=0,\ x_{1}>0\}
one has
G=x_{1}
. However,
G
is nonsmooth at the
corner
(0,0)
. Thus the disappearance of the reflection contribution is not
automatic unless the possible corner term is controlled. We impose the
following approximation condition.

Assumption 4.2

(No corner reflection contribution)
.

There exists a family
G_{\varepsilon}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
,
\varepsilon>0
, such
that
G_{\varepsilon}\to G
locally uniformly on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, and, for every
compact
K\subset\{x_{1}=0,\ x_{2}>0\}
,
\partial_{1}G_{\varepsilon}\to 0
uniformly on

K
, while for every compact
K\subset\{x_{2}=0,\ x_{1}>0\}
,

\partial_{2}G_{\varepsilon}\to 0
uniformly on
K
. Moreover, for every
t>0
and
every starting point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}_{x}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{t}|\partial_{1}G_{\varepsilon}(X_{s})|\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{s}=(0,0)\}}\,dL_{s}^{1}+\int_{0}^{t}|\partial_{2}G_{\varepsilon}(X_{s})|\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{s}=(0,0)\}}\,dL_{s}^{2}\Bigg]=0.

(11)

Lemma 4.3

(Vanishing reflection contribution for
G
)
.

Under Assumption
4.2
, the reflection contribution in
the generalized Itô–Tanaka formula for
G(X)
vanishes. More precisely,

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{i}G_{\varepsilon}(X_{s})\,dL_{s}^{i}\right]=0,\qquad t>0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}.

Consequently, no additional corner additive functional appears in the
Itô–Tanaka formula for
G(X)
.

Proof.

By the normal-reflection condition,
dL_{s}^{i}
is carried by
\{X_{s}^{i}=0\}
,

i=1,2
. For
i=1
, the part of
dL_{s}^{1}
carried by
\{X_{s}^{1}=0,\ X_{s}^{2}>0\}

gives no contribution in the limit, because
\partial_{1}G_{\varepsilon}\to 0

locally uniformly on compact subsets of that face. For
i=2
, the same
argument applies on
\{X_{s}^{2}=0,\ X_{s}^{1}>0\}
, using

\partial_{2}G_{\varepsilon}\to 0
. The only remaining possible contribution comes
from times at which
X_{s}=(0,0)
. This contribution is precisely controlled by
(
11
). Hence the expected reflection term tends to
zero as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
∎

Remark 4.4

(If the corner condition fails)
.

If Assumption
4.2
is not verified, then the
Itô–Tanaka formula for
G(X)
may contain an additional corner additive
functional supported at
(0,0)
. In that case the stopping-gain measure must be
enlarged to

\Gamma=\Gamma^{\mathrm{ac}}\,dx+\Gamma^{\Delta}+\Gamma^{\mathrm{corner}}
,
where
\Gamma^{\mathrm{corner}}
is the signed measure associated with the
corner reflection contribution. The present paper works under
Assumption
4.2
and therefore takes

\Gamma^{\mathrm{corner}}=0
.

4.4
Itô–Tanaka identity for the reward

Under Assumption
4.2
, the generalized Itô–Tanaka
formula for
G(X)
may be written, after localization, as

\displaystyle e^{-rt}G(X_{t})

\displaystyle=G(x)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-rs}\big(\mathcal{L}G_{\mathrm{sm}}(X_{s})-rG(X_{s})\big)\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{s}\notin\Delta\}}\,ds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-rs}\,dL_{s}^{0}(Y)+M_{t},

where
M
is a local martingale and the reflection contribution has vanished.
Equivalently, using the signed measure
\Gamma
in
(
10
),

e^{-rt}G(X_{t})=G(x)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds-\int_{0}^{t}e^{-rs}\,dA_{s}^{\Gamma}+M_{t},

(12)

where
A^{\Gamma}
is the signed continuous additive functional associated with
the signed measure
\Gamma

Revuz and Yor 2013
;
Fukushima et al. 2011
.
Identity (
12
) is the convention used in the
killed-resolvent representation of the value function.

5
Killed-resolvent representation and conditional boundary
characterization

This section gives the potential-theoretic representation of the
value function. The relevant resolvent is the resolvent of the reflected
diffusion killed upon entry into the stopping set, not the unrestricted
reflected resolvent of the process continued after stopping. Throughout this
section we work under the assumptions of
Sections
2
–
4
. In
particular,
\Gamma=\Gamma^{\mathrm{ac}}\,dx+\Gamma^{\Delta}
denotes the signed
stopping-gain measure defined in (
10
), and

A^{\Gamma}
denotes the signed continuous additive functional associated with

\Gamma
.

5.1
The killed resolvent

Let
\tau_{\mathcal{D}}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}\in\mathcal{D}\}
be the first entry time
into the stopping set. For a signed smooth measure
\nu
for which the
following expression is well defined, define the resolvent killed upon entry
into
\mathcal{D}
by

\displaystyle R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}\nu(x):=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathcal{D}}}e^{-rs}\,dA_{s}^{\nu}\right]
,

x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
.
If
\nu=f\,dx
, this becomes

R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathcal{D}}}e^{-rs}f(X_{s})\,ds\right].

(13)

By contrast, the unrestricted reflected resolvent is

R_{r}^{\mathrm{R}}f(x):=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-rs}f(X_{s})\,ds\right].

(14)

In general,

R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}f(x)\neq R_{r}^{\mathrm{R}}(f\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}})(x).

(15)

Indeed, the unrestricted process in (
14
) continues
to evolve after
\tau_{\mathcal{D}}
and may later re-enter
\mathcal{C}
. The
killed resolvent (
13
) counts only the
occupation accumulated before the first entry into
\mathcal{D}
.

Assumption 5.1

(Admissibility of killed potentials)
.

For every signed measure
\nu
used below, the positive and negative parts

\nu^{+}
and
\nu^{-}
are smooth measures for the reflected diffusion and satisfy

R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}\nu^{+}(x)+R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}\nu^{-}(x)<\infty
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. The
killed potential
R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}\nu
is assumed to admit the
continuation-side traces used below.

Remark 5.2

(Killing is essential)
.

Formula (
15
) is the central object in this
section. The optimal stopping representation obtained from Itô–Tanaka
applies only up to the optimal stopping time. Therefore the potential term is a
killed potential over the pre-stopping path. Replacing it by the full reflected
resolvent of
f\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}}
would incorrectly include post-stopping
occupation of
\mathcal{C}
.

5.2
Value representation

The following result is the resolvent representation of the value
function.

Theorem 5.3

(Killed-resolvent representation)
.

Assume that
\tau_{\mathcal{D}}
is optimal and that the generalized
Itô–Tanaka identity (
12
) may be applied up to

\tau_{\mathcal{D}}
, with the required localization and uniform integrability.
Then, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,

V(x)=G(x)-R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}\Gamma(x).

(16)

Equivalently,

V(x)=G(x)-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathcal{D}}}e^{-rs}\,dA_{s}^{\Gamma}\right].

Proof.

Since
\tau_{\mathcal{D}}
is optimal,

\displaystyle V(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[e^{-r\tau_{\mathcal{D}}}G(X_{\tau_{\mathcal{D}}})-\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathcal{D}}}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds\right]
.
Apply the generalized Itô–Tanaka identity (
12
) to

G(X)
on
[0,t\wedge\tau_{\mathcal{D}}]
:

e^{-r(t\wedge\tau_{\mathcal{D}})}G(X_{t\wedge\tau_{\mathcal{D}}})=G(x)+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{\mathcal{D}}}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{\mathcal{D}}}e^{-rs}\,dA_{s}^{\Gamma}+M_{t\wedge\tau_{\mathcal{D}}},

where
M
is a local martingale. After localization, expectation removes the
martingale term. Passing to the limit
t\to\infty
using the assumed uniform
integrability gives

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[e^{-r\tau_{\mathcal{D}}}G(X_{\tau_{\mathcal{D}}})-\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathcal{D}}}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds\right]=G(x)-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathcal{D}}}e^{-rs}\,dA_{s}^{\Gamma}\right].

The right-hand side is precisely
G(x)-R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}\Gamma(x)
. This proves
(
16
).
∎

Remark 5.4

(Incorrect unrestricted formula)
.

The representation (
16
) replaces the generally
incorrect identity

V(x)=G(x)-R_{r}^{\mathrm{R}}(\Gamma\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{C}})(x)
. The latter would
be valid only under additional special circumstances preventing post-stopping
occupation of
\mathcal{C}
by the unrestricted reflected process, or under a
convention in which the process is actually killed at
\tau_{\mathcal{D}}
.

5.3
Boundary trace condition

Assume now that the stopping set has the epigraph representation from
Theorem
3.6
:

\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:x_{2}\geq b(x_{1})\}
. Write

\displaystyle z_{b}(x_{1}):=(x_{1},b(x_{1}))
.
Since
V=G
on
\partial\mathcal{C}
, the killed potential in
(
16
) has zero continuation-side trace on the free
boundary.

Proposition 5.5

(Continuation-side trace condition)
.

Assume the hypotheses of Theorem
5.3
and
the free-boundary regularity needed for the following trace to exist. Then, for
every boundary point at which the trace is well-defined,

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\to z_{b}(x_{1})\\
x\in\mathcal{C}\end{subarray}}R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}\Gamma(x)=0.

(17)

If non-tangential traces are used, (
17
) is interpreted as

\lim_{x\to z_{b}(x_{1}),\,x\in\mathcal{C}\ \mathrm{n.t.}}R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}\Gamma(x)=0
.

Proof.

For
x\in\mathcal{C}
, Theorem
5.3
gives

R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}\Gamma(x)=G(x)-V(x)
. Let
x\to z_{b}(x_{1})
from within

\mathcal{C}
. Since
V
and
G
are continuous and
V=G
on the boundary, the
right-hand side tends to zero.
∎

Remark 5.6

(Why the boundary value itself is not the equation)
.

One should not write
R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}\Gamma(z_{b}(x_{1}))=0
as a substantive
boundary equation unless a nontrivial trace convention has been specified. If
the killed process is started at a point of
\mathcal{D}
, then

\tau_{\mathcal{D}}=0
, and the killed potential vanishes trivially. The
meaningful condition is the continuation-side trace (
17
).

When a killed Green kernel exists, the killed potential may be written
schematically as

R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}\Gamma(x)=\int_{\mathcal{C}}G_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}(x,y)\Gamma^{\mathrm{ac}}(y)\,dy+R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}\Gamma^{\Delta}(x),\qquad x\in\mathcal{C}.

(18)

Here
G_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}
is the Green kernel of the reflected diffusion killed
upon entry into
\mathcal{D}
, and
R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}}\Gamma^{\Delta}
is the killed
potential of the diagonal surface measure

\Gamma^{\Delta}(dx)=-\frac{q(x)}{2\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}}\,\sigma_{\Delta}(dx)
. The
boundary condition is the continuation-side trace of
(
18
).

5.4
Candidate verification rather than Fredholm uniqueness

Let
h:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a candidate boundary. Define

\mathcal{D}_{h}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:x_{2}\geq h(x_{1})\}
,

\mathcal{C}_{h}:=\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{h}
,
and

\tau_{h}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}\in\mathcal{D}_{h}\}
.
For an admissible signed measure
\nu
, define the killed resolvent relative to

\mathcal{C}_{h}
by

\displaystyle R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}_{h}}\nu(x):=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}e^{-rs}\,dA_{s}^{\nu}\right]
.
The candidate value associated with
h
is

U_{h}(x):=G(x)-R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}_{h}}\Gamma(x).

(19)

The next theorem is a conditional verification result. It is not a uniqueness
theorem for a Fredholm equation.

Theorem 5.7

(Candidate verification)
.

Let
h
be a candidate boundary and define
U_{h}
by (
19
). Assume:

(C1)

U_{h}
is continuous and of polynomial growth.

(C2)

U_{h}\geq G
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
.

(C3)

U_{h}=G
on
\mathcal{D}_{h}
.

(C4)

In
\mathcal{C}_{h}
,
(\mathcal{L}-r)U_{h}=c
in the weak,
viscosity, or generalized Itô sense required by
Theorem
2.11
.

(C5)

U_{h}
satisfies the reflected Neumann condition on the
coordinate axes in the trace or reflected-viscosity sense.

(C6)

U_{h}
is globally measure-superharmonic relative to the
running cost:
(\mathcal{L}-r)U_{h}-c\leq 0
as a signed measure in the interior, in
the sense required by Theorem
2.11
.

(C7)

The localization, additive-functional, and
uniform-integrability hypotheses of Theorem
2.11
hold
for
U_{h}
and
\tau_{h}
.

Then
U_{h}=V
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, and
\tau_{h}
is optimal. If, in addition, the true
stopping set is known to be an epigraph

\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{1},x_{2}):x_{2}\geq b(x_{1})\}
, then
h=b
at every point where both
graph representatives are defined with the same convention.

Proof.

By (C1), (C2), (C4), (C5), (C6),
and (C7), the verification theorem
(Theorem
2.11
) applies to
U_{h}
with

\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{C}_{h}
. Therefore
U_{h}\geq V
. Applying the stopped
generalized Itô formula to
U_{h}(X)
up to
\tau_{h}
, using

(\mathcal{L}-r)U_{h}=c
in
\mathcal{C}_{h}
, the reflected boundary condition, and
the integrability hypotheses, gives

U_{h}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[e^{-r\tau_{h}}U_{h}(X_{\tau_{h}})-\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds\right].

By the contact condition (C3),
U_{h}(X_{\tau_{h}})=G(X_{\tau_{h}})
on

\{\tau_{h}<\infty\}
. Hence

U_{h}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[e^{-r\tau_{h}}G(X_{\tau_{h}})-\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}e^{-rs}c(X_{s})\,ds\right]\leq V(x).

Thus
U_{h}=V
, and
\tau_{h}
is optimal. If the true stopping set is known to be
an epigraph with boundary
b
, then
\{U_{h}=G\}=\{V=G\}=\mathcal{D}
. Since

\mathcal{D}_{h}
is also an epigraph and
U_{h}=G
on
\mathcal{D}_{h}
by
(C3), the two epigraphs coincide under the stated convention, and
therefore
h=b
.
∎

Remark 5.8

(No uniqueness from the trace condition alone)
.

The boundary trace condition

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\to z_{h}(x_{1})\\
x\in\mathcal{C}_{h}\end{subarray}}R_{r}^{\mathcal{C}_{h}}\Gamma(x)=0

does not
by itself imply uniqueness of
h
. It also does not imply
U_{h}=G
throughout

\mathcal{D}_{h}
. Uniqueness of a candidate boundary follows only after a
verification argument: one must prove majorization
U_{h}\geq G
, contact on the
whole candidate stopping set, global measure-superharmonicity, the reflected
boundary condition, and the required integrability properties.

Remark 5.9

(Main conclusion)
.

The defensible structural conclusion is therefore:

Given structural monotonicity and regularity assumptions, the reflected
optimal stopping problem admits a killed-resolvent boundary-trace
representation.

The theory does not assert unsupported general free-boundary regularity, an
unrestricted-resolvent Fredholm equation, or an epigraph theorem derived from
order preservation alone.
