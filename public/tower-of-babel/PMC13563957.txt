Crimean-congo haemorrhagic fever virus in ticks and small ruminants in western Iran: epidemiological insights and genotyping
Authors: Abdoreza Amrollahi, Meysam Moravedji, Saber Esmaeili, Nikwan Khademi Baneh, Tahmine Jalali, Maryam Rahravani, Mahsa Tavakoli, Zahra Hosseini, Mostafa Salehi-Vaziri, Mohammad Hassan Pouriayevali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCrimean–Congo haemorrhagic fever (CCHF) is an emerging zoonotic disease of significant public health concern caused by Crimean–Congo haemorrhagic fever virus (CCHFV), primarily transmitted by tick vectors. Its spread is further compounded by global warming, tick range expansion, and traditional farming practices. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of CCHFV in ticks parasitizing livestock in Kermanshah Province, Iran, which borders Iraq, a country that has experienced major CCHF outbreaks in recent years.MethodsSerum and tick samples were collected from 302 small ruminants (SRs) in six districts, considering previous CCHF cases and regional differences. Following RNA extraction from both ticks and sera, a homemade real-time RT-PCR was used to detect CCHFV infection.ResultsAll sera tested negative, while 0.67% (4/604) of ticks were infected with CCHFV (Hyalomma asiaticum and Rhipicephalus sanguineus were positive). This study confirms the circulation of CCHFV in the western region of Iran and, more importantly, reports the detection of an Aigai virus (formerly CCHFV genotype VI or AP-92-like strain) in tick populations for the first time in Iran.ConclusionsThese findings emphasize the need for continued molecular surveillance, vector control, and cross-border cooperation to mitigate the risk of CCHF transmission in humans.Keywords: Crimean-Congo Haemorrhagic Fever, Arbovirus, Viral Haemorrhagic Fevers, Emerging Infectious Diseases, Hyalomma ticks, Rhipicephalus sanguineus, Zoonotic diseases, Tick-borne diseases, Molecular epidemiology, Iran

Background

Emerging tick-borne viral diseases pose a growing threat to public health worldwide, with increasing prevalence and expanding geographic distributions driven by globalization, urbanization, climate change, and ecological disruption [1]. Among these pathogens, Crimean–Congo haemorrhagic fever virus (CCHFV) is one of the most significant tick-borne viruses and has been identified by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a high-priority emerging pathogen requiring intensified research and development efforts due to its high potential to cause future epidemics [2].

CCHFV, also known as Orthonairovirus haemorrhagiae, belongs to the genus Orthonairovirus within the family Nairoviridae and order Bunyavirales. CCHFV is an enveloped, negative-sense, single-stranded RNA virus with a tripartite genome of approximately 19 kb, comprising small (S, ~1.7 kb), medium (M, ~5.4 kb), and large (L, ~12 kb) segments. These segments encode nucleoprotein (NP), envelope glycoproteins (Gn and Gc) and the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), respectively. CCHFV exhibits remarkable genetic diversity, with strains traditionally grouped into several major clades (Africa 1–3, Asia 1–2, Europe 1). Note that viruses formerly classified as CCHFV genotype VI (Europe-2 or AP-92-like) have been reclassified by the ICTV as a distinct species, Aigai virus (AIGV), due to sufficient phylogenetic divergence [3, 4].

Ticks act as both reservoirs and vectors of CCHFV, playing a pivotal role in the natural maintenance and transmission of this virus among wildlife, livestock, and humans [5]. Although several tick species are involved in viral circulation, hard ticks of the genus Hyalomma are considered the primary vectors. These ticks sustain CCHFV transmission by passing the virus through transovarial and transstadial transmission mechanisms, allowing CCHFV to persist across generations and spread across diverse geographic regions [6]. Consequently, monitoring viral infection in the tick population represents a critical component of an integrated One Health surveillance system for CCHFV, as it facilitates the tracking of natural viral transmission cycles and the development of strategic intervention plans [7].

In addition to the natural cycle, human infections occur through the bite of infected ticks, direct exposure to the blood, tissues, or bodily secretions of infected animals, and person-to-person transmission through contact with infected blood or body fluids, especially within healthcare environments (nosocomial transmission) [8, 9]. CCHFV maintains dormant circulation in livestock, small mammals, and ground-feeding birds, and due to limited viremia, a history of exposure to the virus is usually detected only by serological testing [10]. On the other hand, active and ongoing virus transmission can be detected by examining human cases, livestock and tick populations. Therefore, one way to monitor the disease is to establish active tick surveillance systems to identify areas with the highest risk of infection and to prevent transmission before widespread dissemination [11].

Another aspect of CCHF control is the role of animal carriers and ecological considerations in disease surveillance. In non-human vertebrates, clinical signs are generally absent; several species have been reported to experience viremia for 7–15 days without apparent symptoms [12]. This undetected infection is a key epidemiological feature that enables sustained circulation in endemic areas and creates conditions conducive to future outbreaks [5, 13]. Close monitoring and surveillance of domestic small ruminants (SRs) can minimize this risk.

The Middle East remains a significant hotspot for CCHFV, as ecological and human activities create an ideal environment for its transmission. Iran is considered endemic for CCHF because of the consistently high number of confirmed human cases reported annually (up to 1200 cases and 200 deaths annually between 2000 and 2012) [14]. The history of CCHF in Iran dates back to 1970, with a reported seropositivity of 45% in sheep [15]. Subsequently, numerous sero-epidemiological studies showed the circulation of CCHF in humans and livestock in this region [16, 17]. The first human fatality attributed to this disease was identified in 1999 in Chaharmahal and Bakhtiari Province, Iran. Since the establishment of the Arboviral and Viral Haemorrhagic Fever Reference Laboratory at the Pasteur Institute of Iran, at least 50 human CCHF cases have been reported annually [18–20]. According to the latest data from this National Reference Laboratory, a total of 1872 positive cases of the disease (CCHF) have been reported between 2000 and 2025 (unpublished data). The disease has been reported in most provinces, with the highest incidence observed in the southeastern (Sistan and Baluchestan), northeastern (Razavi Khorsan), central (Isfahan), and southwestern (Fars) regions, indicating the impact of climatic conditions, livestock trading corridors from hyper-endemic neighbouring countries [21].

Molecular sequencing studies have identified multiple CCHFV strains circulating in different regions, indicating the diversity of at least four genotypes: Asia 1 and Asia 2, Europe 1 and Europe 2 (AP92-like) [22]. However, according to the most recent classification by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV), the Europe 2 genotype (AP92-like) is classified as a separate virus species named Aigai virus (AIGV) belonging to the genus Orthonairovirus (family Nairoviridae) [23]. This reclassification is based on significant phylogenetic divergence from all CCHFV genotypes [23]. The AP92-like strain (now AIGV), initially identified in Greece, although it has generally been associated with mild symptoms or asymptomatic infections in humans [24], has been associated with human death in Iran, highlighting its public health importance [25]. Recently, Tavassoli et al. (2024) reported a high prevalence of CCHFV infection in ticks collected from livestock in western Iran, highlighting the importance of tick surveillance in endemic areas [26].

The transmission dynamics of CCHFV in Iran are closely tied to those of its neighbouring countries, forming a regional epidemiological continuum. To the west, Iraq has experienced major outbreaks in recent years; in 2022 alone, over 212 confirmed cases were reported, linked to uncontrolled livestock trade, traditional slaughter practices, and abundant tick populations [27]. To the northwest, Turkey remains one of the most highly endemic countries globally, reporting thousands of cases over the past two decades, with transmission patterns closely linked with agricultural and animal husbandry activities [28]. To the east, both Pakistan and Afghanistan face a substantial disease burden, characterised by periodic outbreaks and serological evidence of widespread livestock infection, which is further complicated by population movement and limited surveillance infrastructure [29, 30]. This regional interconnectedness is sustained by the cross-border movement of nomadic herds, legal and illegal livestock trade, and the migration of birds carrying infected Hyalomma ticks, all of which facilitate the continuous circulation and genetic exchange of the virus across the region.

The spread of tick-borne diseases is closely linked to vector distribution, which can be influenced by climate change, environmental factors, and the introduction of additional pathogens into new regions [31–34]. The movement of migratory birds facilitates Hyalomma tick dispersal across borders, and global warming creates new suitable tick-associated habitats beyond established regions [35].

The Kermanshah Province of Iran has substantial gaps in tick surveillance due to its large nomadic community and a livestock population exceeding 140,000 animals, which create ideal conditions for zoonotic tick transmission. Despite its proximity to Iraq and its role in livestock trade, comprehensive data on CCHFV circulation in ticks and livestock from this region remain limited.

This study aimed to assess the prevalence of CCHFV infection in ticks and sera collected within the Kermanshah Province to elucidate transmission dynamics and provide critical insights to enhance preventive measures and control strategies for both livestock and human populations.

Methods

Study area

Kermanshah Province features diverse landscapes (mountains, plains, semi-arid zones), a Mediterranean to semi-arid climate (hot summers, cold winters), and predominantly traditional and small-scale livestock husbandry with nomadic or pastoral systems favouring tick-host contact. This province, situated in western Iran, shares a 371 km border with Iraq, a region that has experienced a significant number of CCHF cases in recent years [36]. Moreover, Kermanshah Province has witnessed an increasing number of CCHF cases, with a mortality rate of approximately 21% [37]. The current study included six districts characterized by varying CCHF incidence and different geographical conditions (Sarpolzahab, Salasbabajani, Kangavar, Javanrod, Sonqor, Kermanshah). These districts were chosen based on the location of the most recent positive cases detected by the reference lab, livestock density, and the province’s geographical distribution to encompass the entire spectrum of risk.

Sample collection and preparation

Sampling was carried out during the warmer months (May–July 2024), when tick activity peaked. Six districts were selected for our study (Fig. 1), with five sampling locations in each district chosen based on geographic direction (north, south, east, west, and centre). All animals included in the study were privately owned. Informed consent was obtained from the owners before sampling, and sampling was performed with official authorization from the Kermanshah Provincial Veterinary Organization. A total of 302 small ruminants (SRs) were examined for ticks, and blood samples were collected from each. The entire body of each sheep and goat was thoroughly examined, with particular attention given to the ears, neck nape, perineum, scrotum, and base of the tail for the presence of ticks. Ticks were individually collected from each small ruminant and immediately pooled according to host animal (pooling criterion: per host animal) in a sterile 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. Each animal, along with its corresponding tick pool and blood sample, was assigned a unique identification code at the time of collection. Samples were labelled using waterproof markers and recorded in standardized data collection forms, including information on animal ID, location, date of sampling, and specimen type. A chain-of-custody form accompanied all samples during transport to ensure traceability and documentation of sample transfer between laboratories. All sampling procedures were performed in accordance with national biosafety regulations. Field personnel used appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE), including disposable gloves, protective clothing, and masks, to minimize the risk of pathogen exposure.

The study focuses on six sampling locations in Kermanshah province, Iran: Sarpolezahab (A), Salasbabajani (B), Javanrod (C), Kermanshah (D), Sonqor (E), and Kangavar (F). These sites were chosen to reflect the province’s diverse urban and rural areas

Collected tick specimens were divided into two groups: one group was preserved in 70% ethanol for morphological identification, while the second group was designated for molecular detection of Crimean–Congo haemorrhagic fever virus (CCHFV) and transported to the Department of Arboviruses and Viral Haemorrhagic Fevers at the Pasteur Institute of Iran (National Reference Laboratory). Ticks intended for molecular analysis were placed in sterile, labelled dry tubes and then preserved in RNAlater to maintain nucleic acid integrity. During field collection, tick samples were kept at ambient temperature and transferred to 4 °C within 6–12 hours and subsequently stored at − 20 °C.

Blood samples were centrifuged within 4–6 hours after collection to separate serum, which was then aliquoted into sterile cryovials. Serum samples were temporarily stored at 4 °C for up to 24 hours before being frozen at − 20 °C or at − 70 °C for longer-term storage prior to RNA extraction.

For transportation to the Pasteur Institute of Iran, both tick and serum samples were maintained under appropriate cold-chain conditions using insulated containers with ice packs (2–8 °C) or dry ice for samples requiring temperatures of − 20 °C or lower. Transport duration ranged from 12 to 48 hours depending on the distance from the sampling districts. Temperature inside the transport containers was monitored using calibrated digital thermometers, with readings recorded at the time of departure and upon arrival; where available, portable temperature data loggers were also used to verify continuous cold-chain maintenance. Upon arrival at the laboratory, all samples were immediately stored at − 20 °C or − 70 °C until RNA extraction. All procedures followed standard biosafety and cold-chain guidelines for arboviral molecular diagnostics to ensure preservation of viral RNA integrity (Fig. 1).

Tick identification

Tick specimens were identified morphologically to the genus and species level using the comprehensive illustrated identification guide by Hosseini-Chegeni [38]. To ensure accuracy and minimize misidentification risks, particularly for morphologically similar taxa such as those within the Rhipicephalus spp. group or certain Hyalomma spp., identifications were cross-checked against additional standard references, including Telmadarraiy et al. [39] and other keys cited in the literature [40]. Ambiguous or borderline specimens were reviewed in consultation with an external expert at the Pasteur Institute of Iran. After the ticks were fixed using forceps and microscope holders, morphological identification was performed using a binocular stereomicroscope (Nikon SMZ-1, Japan, 10×/22 mm eyepieces with external illumination). Standard features were examined to differentiate the ticks. These features include the base of the capitulum and its shape, hypostome and palp length and shape, festoons, scutum, genital and respiratory pore, and different body parts [38]. Several species were deemed unidentifiable because of damage to their morphological structures sustained during collection.

RNA extraction and RT-PCR

The samples were transported to the reference laboratory under controlled temperature conditions. The ticks were defrosted, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and transferred to microtubes containing 200 µL of PBS and stainless-steel beads. These tubes underwent tissue lyser steps to homogenize tick tissue and parts (Qiagen Tissuelyser II). Homogenized tick solution and serum samples underwent RNA extraction using the Zybio Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit (magnetic bead method).

After extraction, homemade real-time RT-PCR was performed using specific primers/probes (Table 1). This homemade assay was previously validated in our laboratory using the approved IVD detection kit and the field-collected samples (unpublished data). The real-time PCR program consisted of an initial reverse transcription step at 50 °C for 20 minutes, followed by an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 minutes, and 40 cycles of amplification with denaturation at 95 °C for 10 seconds, annealing at 50 °C for 10 seconds, and extension at 72 °C for 1 minute. During real-time analysis, amplification and dissociation curves were examined in relation to the negative and positive controls to confirm the presence of CCHFV RNA.

PCR amplification of positive cases for CCHF

The conventional PCR was performed specifically on the RNA extracts that tested positive in the real-time RT-PCR assay. The S gene segment was amplified using a one-step RT-PCR protocol with specific primers targeting a conserved region of the S segment. Primers IranF2 (5′-TGGACACCTTCACAAACTC-3′) and IranR3 (5′-GACAAATTCCCTACACCA-3′) were designed to generate a 536-bp amplicon [41]. The reaction mixture with a total volume of 25 µL included 12.5 µL of 2 × 1-Step Master Mix from Biotechrabbit, Germany, containing reverse transcriptase and DNA polymerase, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers IranF2 and IranR3 at a concentration of 2.5 µM, 1.25 µL of 20X Enzyme RT-RI Blend, 7.25 µL of RNase-free deionized water, and 2 µL of extracted RNA as the template [42].

The thermal cycling protocol began with reverse transcription at 50 °C for 20 minutes, followed by an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 2 minutes. The amplification phase consisted of 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 seconds, primer annealing at 50 °C for 10 seconds, and extension at 72 °C for 1 minute. The final extension step was performed at 72 °C for 5 minutes, and the reaction was maintained at 4 °C until further processing. Amplified PCR products were purified using the Qiagen PCR Product Cleanup Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) to remove residual primers and nucleotides before sequencing.

Phylogenetic and sequencing analysis

Sanger sequencing was performed on the amplified products using an ABI Genetic Analyzer and a BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit. Nucleotide sequence homology searches were performed at the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), and initial BLAST analysis was used to compare the sequences against the GenBank database.

To ensure robust phylogenetic accuracy and accurate genotypic clustering, a comprehensive dataset of 47 representative CCHFV S-segment sequences was retrieved from GenBank. These reference sequences were carefully selected to represent all major established global lineages, diverse geographical regions, and various isolation years. The newly obtained sequence and the reference dataset were aligned using the ClustalW algorithm implemented in MEGA 11 software.

Phylogenetic analysis and sequence divergence were calculated utilizing the Kimura 2-parameter (K2P) substitution model. This model was selected as it adequately accounts for transition and transversion biases and represents a well-established standard in CCHFV molecular epidemiology, facilitating direct and standardized comparisons with existing literature. The phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method in MEGA 11. To evaluate the topological robustness and node support of the tree, 10,000 bootstrap replicates were performed.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS software (version 15.0). Fisher’s exact test was used to assess associations between categorical variables, including tick species and CCHFV infection status. The Chi-square test was employed to compare CCHFV seroprevalence between host species (goats vs. sheep). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Results

A total of 604 hard ticks (Ixodidae), comprising 229 males and 375 females, were collected from 302 livestock (209 sheep and 93 goats) across six districts of Kermanshah Province in Iran. The geographic distribution is listed below, with sampling efforts yielding relatively balanced proportions across regions: Sarpolezahab (18.2%, n = 55), Kermanshah (16.6%, n = 50), Javanrod (16.6%, n = 50), Kangavar (16.6%, n = 50), Sonqor (16.6%, n = 50), and Salasbabajani (15.6%, n = 47).

The ticks were identified and classified into 8 taxonomic groups based on their morphological characteristics (Table 2). The most abundant species was Rhipicephalus bursa (33.44%, n = 202), followed by Rhipicephalus sanguineus (27.98%, n = 169), Hyalomma anatolicum (10.76%, n = 65), Hyalomma asiaticum (8.28%, n = 50), Haemaphysalis inermis (5.46%, n = 33), Hyalomma dromedarii (4.64%, n = 28), Dermacentor marginatus (2.5%, n = 15), and Rhipicephalus annulatus (0.67%, n = 4). A total of 6.62% (n = 40) of ticks remained undifferentiated because of ambiguous morphological features.

Of the 604 ticks analysed, 418 (69.2%) were collected from sheep and 186 (30.79%) from goats, with some animals hosting multiple tick species. Among sheep, R. bursa (n = 79) and R. sanguineus (n = 62) were predominant, whereas among goats, R. sanguineus (n = 33) and H. anatolicum (n = 9) were the most common species (Table 2).

The investigation of 604 ticks using real-time RT-PCR targeting a conserved 189-bp fragment of the S segment detected CCHFV in 4 samples (0.67%). Two H. asiaticum ticks and two R. sanguineus specimens were virus-positive. No statistically significant associations were observed between CCHFV positivity and host species (sheep vs. goats: χ2 test, p = 0.27), tick sex (male vs. female: Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.35), or geographic distribution (comparison across all districts: χ2 test, p = 0.43). However, a marginal increase in prevalence was noted in low-altitude areas (e.g., Kangavar).

Serum molecular evaluation

None of the collected serum samples tested positive for CCHFV following molecular assessment.

Phylogenetic results

The S-segment sequence of the new CCHFV isolate obtained in this study was deposited in the GenBank database under the accession number (PV030925.1). Phylogenetic analysis of the S-segment was conducted using a comprehensive dataset of 47 CCHFV sequences, representing established global lineages and diverse geographical regions (Fig. 2). The resulting phylogenetic tree demonstrated that the newly sequenced isolate, 9 H, clusters within the AIGAI virus clade, formerly Europe 2, AP92-like genotype. Specifically, the isolate formed a well-supported clade alongside another Iranian strain (Iran-4675) and grouped closely with established sequences from Greece, Turkey, and Bulgaria, supported by a high bootstrap value. This topological placement indicates that the 9 H isolate shares high genetic homology and a close evolutionary relationship with the Europe 2 lineage strains circulating in the Mediterranean and Middle Eastern regions, thereby confirming its phylogenetic classification. Discussion

This study confirmed the circulation of CCHFV in tick populations of Kermanshah Province, western Iran, a region with documented human CCHF cases [14, 37, 43, 44]. Molecular screening detected CCHFV RNA in 0.67% of ticks, including H. asiaticum and R. sanguineus, confirming the ongoing low-level circulation of the virus in the region. Phylogenetic analysis revealed the presence of the AP92 strain (Europe-2 lineage), which, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported in ticks from this region.

Ticks from various species, including Hyalomma, Rhipicephalus, Haemaphysalis, and Dermacentor, were detected in the present study, consistent with previous research [39, 40, 45]. Several studies have reported the presence of ticks from diverse species that typically parasitize domestic animals, with notable genera including Hyalomma, Rhipicephalus, Dermacentor, and Haemaphysalis [39, 40, 45]. Variations in species distribution may reflect ecological and seasonal differences [46].

In the present study, both H. asiaticum and R. sanguineus tested positive for CCHFV. The predominance of H. asiaticum as an infected species aligns with previous reports from western Iran [47]. H. asiaticum is known to thrive in arid environments, as demonstrated in studies from China, which also reported H. asiaticum as the dominant CCHFV-carrying species [48]. CCHFV has been detected in various tick species across different regions. For instance, R. sanguineus has been found to carry the virus in countries such as Bulgaria, Turkey, Côte d’Ivoire (Ivory Coast), and northern Iran [49–52]. Rhipicephalus spp. have been documented as vectors of AP92-like viruses across various regions, consistent with our findings [23]. CCHFV-positive ticks were collected from two different hosts. All ticks recovered from each host belonged to a single species, and no co-infestation with other tick species was observed. The detection of CCHFV in R. sanguineus, a tick species not traditionally regarded as a primary vector, raises concerns about the viral maintenance cycle in this uncommon vector, given the changes in ecological conditions. Therefore, the widespread distribution of CCHFV across diverse tick species underscores the importance of ongoing surveillance and targeted vector control strategies to mitigate the risk of CCHF transmission to humans.

Notably, we collected serum samples from all sheep, but none tested positive for the CCHF virus. This finding aligns with studies reporting challenges in virus isolation due to a narrow window of viremia in livestock, which is often short-lived and frequently undetectable even during active circulation, and clinical manifestations are minimal in various livestock species [53].

The results of this study contribute to the epidemiological and molecular analyses of CCHF in Kermanshah Province, Iran. Although the AIGV strain has been previously documented in Iran, including reports of fatal human cases [25], its detection in the tick vector population within the country has not been elucidated. Notably, the present study documents, for the first time, the presence of AP92-like strain in ticks in Iran.

The present findings provide important evidence for the enzootic maintenance of AP92-like strain in local vector populations despite sporadic detection and highlight the role of tick surveillance in predicting human exposure risk. Given the recent reclassification of the AP92-like strain as a distinct Orthonairovirus species by ICTV [23]. The detection of this virus in ticks collected in Iran expands its known geographic distribution and underscores the need for further ecological investigations. Although human cases associated with AIGV have been previously reported [23, 25], human infection appears to be relatively rare, which may be attributed to lower pathogenicity or vector-host preferences. Current findings expand the known distribution of AP92-like strain in Iran. With the co-circulation of several phylogenetic variants, Iran remains a hotspot for zoonotic orthonairovirus surveillance.

There were no significant associations between CCHFV positivity and host species (sheep vs. goats; p = 0.27), tick sex (male vs. female; p = 0.35), or geography (p = 0.43), except for a marginal increase in prevalence among low-altitude areas. This lack of significant associations may be due to several reasons, including the relatively low overall prevalence (0.67%), likely reducing the statistical power to detect differences; CCHFV transmission is influenced by complex ecological and biological factors that are not fully captured by these variables; for example, transient viremia in livestock may obscure host-specific patterns if animals clear the virus before sampling [12]; and tick species are distributed relatively uniformly across districts with similar ecological conditions within Kermanshah, minimizing geographic variation. The slightly higher prevalence observed in low-altitude regions is likely attributed to favourable climatic conditions for the tick [54]; however, a larger sample size is needed to confirm this trend.

Further studies should aim to better understand the interactions among transmission vectors, animal hosts, viruses, and socioeconomic factors that affect disease transmission. A One Health model of intervention, in which human, animal, and environmental health are evaluated concurrently, would be ideal. This health model could include evaluating farmers, patients, livestock, and tick studies to obtain an overall picture of CCHF infection. The involvement of migratory birds in the distribution of ticks and pathogens is noteworthy, as the latter can introduce new genera of tick species and viral agents to different geographical regions. [14, 55]. Other tick species that transmit this disease include Hyalomma, Ixodes, and Haemaphysalis, which are known to depend on migratory birds in Europe and Asia [56, 57]. Consequently, the distribution of ticks on birds is influenced by factors such as species, season, and region, with ground-feeding birds being particularly affected [14, 58]. In the present research, positive CCHFV cases have been found in H. asiaticum and R. sanguineus. Although bird-mediated dispersal of these tick species remains poorly documented, some studies provide evidence supporting their potential dissemination by migratory birds (as potential true carriers or even mechanical vectors) [59, 60].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted over a limited time frame, potentially missing seasonal variations in tick activity and viral prevalence in the collected samples. Although the sample size was substantial, it may not fully represent the diversity of tick populations across all Iranian provinces. Additionally, the absence of detectable virus in sheep sera could reflect transient viremia, which complicates detection in livestock and potentially requires more frequent sampling to capture peak viremia periods. Lastly, we used morphological identification methods instead of molecular identification.

As there is still no effective vaccine for CCHF prevention, emphasis should be placed on developing preventive measures. Other worthwhile health promotion activities are crucial for promoting early identification of disease signals and for seeking early medical attention once farmers and livestock workers notice features of flu-like illness.

Conclusions

Therefore, understanding the presence of CCHFV in tick populations in Kermanshah is pivotal; however, this study underscores the need for future cooperation among scientists, healthcare workers, and the public to protect populations against this infectious disease.
