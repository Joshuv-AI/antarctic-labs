Impact of IVUS Guidance on Clinical and Procedural Outcomes in Intracoronary Brachytherapy
Authors: Chloe Kharsa, Gal Sella, Devin Olek, Mangesh Kritya, Joseph Elias, Muhammad Faraz Anwaar, Elia El Hajj, Albert E. Raizner, Bin Teh, Andrew Farach, Alpesh Shah, Neal S. Kleiman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntravascular brachytherapy (ICBT) has re-emerged as an effective treatment for in-stent restenosis (ISR). Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) enables detailed assessment of stent expansion, neointimal proliferation, and other mechanical causes of ISR, potentially allowing more tailored procedural strategies and improved lesion preparation before radiation therapy.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to evaluate the clinical utility of IVUS guidance during ICBT for ISR, with emphasis on long-term clinical outcomes.MethodsWe performed a retrospective analysis of patients undergoing ICBT for ISR between 2016 and 2024. Patients were stratified according IVUS use during the procedure. The primary endpoint was target lesion revascularization at follow-up. Secondary endpoints included all-cause mortality and postprocedural myocardial infarction.ResultsA total of 221 patients were included (132/221 [59.7%] IVUS-guided). IVUS use was associated with larger vessel diameter (3.6 ± 0.04 mm vs 3.3 ± 0.05 mm; P < 0.001), advanced lesion preparation (65/132 [49.2%] vs 17/89 [19.1%]; P < 0.001), and longer brachytherapy dwell time (332.2 ± 2.6 s vs 313.4 ± 4.2 s; P < 0.001). At a median follow-up of 717.9 ± 46.1 days, all-cause mortality (11/132 [8.3%] vs 21/89 [23.6%]; P = 0.03) and postprocedural myocardial infarction (15/132 [11.4%] vs 23/89 [25.8%]; P = 0.005) were more frequent in the non-IVUS group. Cox regression analysis showed higher target lesion revascularization in the IVUS group (HR: 1.68; 95% CI: 1.05-2.68; P = 0.03).ConclusionsIVUS-guided ICBT for ISR was safe but was not independently associated with improved long-term outcomes.

In-stent restenosis (ISR) continues to pose a therapeutic challenge in contemporary interventional cardiology, particularly in patients with diffuse, recurrent, or multilayered restenotic lesions.1 Although drug-eluting stents and drug-coated balloons have significantly reduced ISR rates, a subset of patients remains refractory to these modalities.2 In such cases, intracoronary brachytherapy (ICBT) has re-emerged as a valuable therapeutic option, offering targeted antiproliferative radiation to inhibit neointimal hyperplasia and reduce restenosis.3, 4, 5

ICBT, however, is a technically demanding procedure that requires precise lesion preparation and accurate radiation delivery to optimize outcomes and minimize geographic miss.6 Proper guidance during the procedure is critical for evaluating stent expansion, identifying mechanical factors contributing to restenosis, and ensuring complete lesion coverage. In this context, intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) has emerged as a valuable adjunctive imaging modality.7 IVUS provides detailed cross-sectional visualization of the coronary vessel,8 allowing operators to assess stent underexpansion, malapposition, or neoatherosclerosis—factors that are often underestimated on angiography. Moreover, IVUS can assist in tailoring the selection of balloon size and radiation source train length, thereby enhancing procedural precision.9,10

No randomized trials currently support intravascular imaging–guided, ISR-specific strategies, and data on long-term outcomes remain limited.11, 12, 13 Despite its theoretical advantages, the use of IVUS during ICBT is not standardized and is often left to operator discretion. As a result, there is no clear consensus regarding its routine use, and limited data are available on whether IVUS guidance translates into improved clinical outcomes following ICBT.

In this study, we sought to assess the impact of IVUS guidance during ICBT for ISR by comparing procedural characteristics and long-term outcomes in patients treated with vs without IVUS guidance. Our aim was to determine whether IVUS use improves postprocedural clinical endpoints.

Methods

The data sets generated and/or analyzed during the current study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Study design and patient selection

This is a retrospective study conducted at a single tertiary care center, encompassing all patients who underwent ICBT for ISR between June 2016 and December 2024. Patients aged 18 years or older were included if they had a single ISR lesion in either a native coronary artery or a bypass graft, with a vessel between 2.5 and 4.0 mm. Only patients with at least 1 year of follow-up were considered eligible.

Exclusion criteria included multivessel coronary artery disease requiring percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in more than 1 vessel, residual stenosis >30% after repeat angioplasty, and a history of therapeutic chest radiation.

Definitions

ISR was defined as a ≥50% luminal narrowing (visually assessed) within a previously stented segment, accompanied by objective signs of myocardial ischemia,1 defined as the presence of ischemic symptoms, evidence of inducible ischemia on noninvasive stress testing, ischemic electrocardiographic changes, or hemodynamically significant stenosis on invasive physiological assessment (fractional flow reserve ≤0.80 or instantaneous wave-free ratio ≤0.89), when available.14

Follow-up angiography was reserved for patients with recurrent chest pain or other clinical indications.

Target lesion revascularization (TLR) was defined as any repeat PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting targeting the original lesion site, including the stented segment and extending 5 mm proximally and distally from the stent margins. Only clinically driven TLR events, confirmed by angiography and requiring revascularization, were included.

Target vessel revascularization or vessel restenosis was defined as any repeat PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting targeting the treated vessel, including the stented segment and extending 5 mm proximally and distally from the stent margins, irrespective of whether the original lesion itself was treated. This is distinct from TLR, which is restricted to repeat interventions at the specific index lesion.

Vessel diameter was measured using IVUS when imaging was performed; in cases without IVUS, vessel diameter was determined by visual estimation by experienced interventional cardiologists at the time of the procedure, based on angiography.

Cardiac hospitalizations included admissions for heart failure or chest pain within 1 year of the index procedure.

Data collection

Patient demographics, procedural characteristics, and clinical outcomes were extracted from electronic health records. Brachytherapy-specific technical data were independently reviewed by a designated physicist (D.O.) through analysis of procedural videos and operative documentation.

Study endpoints

All endpoints were defined according to the Academic Research Consortium criteria to standardize outcome assessment.15

The primary endpoint was the rate of TLR at 2 years. Secondary endpoints included 2-years all-cause mortality, as well as postprocedural myocardial infarction (MI). Bleeding events were defined by the Bleeding Academic Research Consortium criteria.16 Each outcome was assessed over the follow-up period, and the timing of events was recorded to allow for time-to-event analyses using Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves and Cox proportional hazards modeling. Long-term outcomes were assessed through follow-up chart reviews documenting outpatient and inpatient encounters.

This study was approved by the Houston Methodist Institutional Review Board and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the institutional review board waived the requirement for informed consent.

Brachytherapy technique

All procedures were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. After successful balloon angioplasty of the restenotic lesion, beta radiation was delivered using a 6F guide catheter and the Beta-Cath 3.5 F System (Novoste Corporation), which uses a strontium-90/yttrium-90 source to emit beta radiation. Over the years, our institution has had access to all 3 commercially available source train lengths. At times, only a single size was available; more recently, 40 mm and 60 mm trains have been routinely in use.

IVUS (Philips, Boston Scientific Corp.) was selectively used at the operator’s discretion to guide lesion assessment, optimize lesion preparation, and confirm adequate stent expansion before brachytherapy. IVUS was primarily employed in cases with ambiguous angiographic findings, diffuse ISR, or when mechanical factors such as stent under expansion or malapposition were suspected. It was also used to assist in accurate measurement of vessel diameter and lesion length for optimal source train selection and margin coverage. In cases of focal ISR with clear angiographic characteristics and satisfactory lesion preparation, IVUS was not routinely utilized.

To ensure complete lesion coverage and reduce the risk of edge restenosis, the selected source train was chosen to extend approximately 10 mm beyond both the proximal and distal margins of the angioplasty-treated segment. In cases where anatomical constraints or technical limitations prevented full margin coverage, shorter safety margins were employed.

Radiation dosing was determined collaboratively by the interventional cardiologist in collaboration with the physicist. The prescribed dose was standardized to a depth of 2 mm from the center of the radioactive source. Vessels with diameters of 2.7 to 3.5 mm received 18.4 Gy, whereas vessels measuring 3.35 to 4.0 mm received 23 Gy.

Following radiation delivery, the catheter was withdrawn, and final angiographic was performed to confirm adequate lesion coverage and vessel patency. All patients were prescribed dual antiplatelet therapy for a minimum of 6 months, in accordance with prevailing guidelines, to mitigate thrombotic risk.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD and compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square or Fisher exact test.

Time-to-event analyses were performed using KM survival curves for the outcomes of TLR, all-cause mortality, and major adverse cardiovascular events, comparing IVUS-guided vs non-IVUS interventions. Differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test.

To evaluate the association of IVUS and other clinical and procedural variables with outcomes, Cox proportional hazards models were constructed. Covariates were selected a priori based on established clinical relevance. The multivariable models included age, sex, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, smoking status, vessel diameter, lesion length, and lesion preparation.

HRs with 95% CIs were reported. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and graphical inspection of survival curves. A 2-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Stata (version 17.0; StataCorp).

Results

Baseline and procedural characteristics

A total of 221 patients underwent ICBT for ISR, of whom 132 (59.7%) were treated with IVUS-guided intervention and 89 (40.3%) without IVUS guidance. The mean age of the cohort was 65.2 ± 0.7 years, with no significant difference between the IVUS and non-IVUS groups (65.3 ± 1.0 vs 64.9 ± 1.1 years; P = 0.75). Female representation was comparable (26.5% vs 31.5%; P = 0.42), and body surface area was similar (2.0 ± 0.0 m2 in both groups; P = 0.96). The prevalence of common cardiovascular risk factors—including hypertension (96.4%), diabetes mellitus (65.2%), and hyperlipidemia (91.9%)—was high across the cohort, with no statistically significant differences between groups (all P > 0.05). Active smoking was more frequent in the IVUS group (50/132 [37.9%] vs 21/89 [23.6%]; P = 0.03). Unstable angina was the most frequent clinical presentation, observed in 48.0% of patients, with higher rates in the IVUS group (71/132 [53.8%] vs 35/89 [39.3%]; P = 0.04).

The distribution of target vessels—including the left anterior descending artery, left circumflex artery, right coronary artery, and others—was balanced (P = 0.76). Vessel diameter was larger in the IVUS group (3.6 ± 0.04 mm vs 3.3 ± 0.05 mm; P < 0.001), whereas lesion length and calcification rates were comparable. The use of advanced lesion preparation techniques was more frequent in the IVUS group. Laser or cutting/scoring balloons were used in 49.2% (66/132) of IVUS cases vs 19.1% (17/89) in the non-IVUS group (P < 0.001). Noncompliant balloons were more commonly used in the non-IVUS group (68.5% vs 43.9%). The balloon diameter used for lesion preparation was larger in the IVUS group (3.6 ± 0.05 mm vs 3.4 ± 0.06 mm; P = 0.004), although balloon length did not differ (P = 0.80). Radiation dose from procedural imaging (dose area product), contrast volume were similar between groups (21,632 ± 1,582 vs 20,364 ± 3,185 mGy cm2; P = 0.71). Therapeutic brachytherapy dose (Gy), determined by dwell time and vessel size, was slightly higher in the IVUS group, reflecting the longer dwell times required for larger vessels (332.2 ± 2.6 s vs 313.4 ± 4.2 s; P < 0.001), whereas the number of dwells was comparable. Postprocedural outcomes

At a mean follow-up of approximately 2 years (717.9 ± 46.1 days), the IVUS group had a shorter follow-up duration was shorter in the IVUS group compared with the non-IVUS group (607.6 ± 48.2 vs 881.4 ± 87.0 days; P = 0.003).

Acute procedural complications were rare, with flow-limiting dissection occurring in 1/221 (0.5%) of patients, perforation in 4/221 (1.8%), and acute vessel thrombosis in 1/221 (0.5%), with no differences between both groups (all P > 0.05).

During follow-up, all-cause mortality was higher in the non-IVUS group (8.3%, (11/132) vs 23.6% (21/89), P = 0.03), while cardiac death rates were similar (5/132 [3.8%] vs 5/89 [5.6%]; P = 0.86). Postprocedural MI occurred more frequently in the non-IVUS group (15/132 [11.4%] vs 23/89 [25.8%]; P = 0.005). Other clinical outcomes—including cardiac hospitalizations, new-onset arrhythmia, bleeding events, postprocedural chest pain, repeat angiography, and stenosed vessel occurrence—did not differ significantly between groups (all P > 0.05).

Vessel restenosis rates were also comparable (P = 0.30), with 114/221 (51.6%) of patients experiencing <70% stenosis and 77/221 (34.8%) experiencing >70% stenosis. Time-to-restenosis was shorter in the IVUS group (445.8 ± 44.3 days vs 651.7 ± 78.1 days; P = 0.01). Survival analysis

KM survival analysis was performed to assess the association between IVUS use and our endpoints over a 2-year period (730 days). The KM curves demonstrated a higher incidence of TLR in the IVUS-guided group. Unadjusted Cox proportional hazards modeling indicated that IVUS use was associated with an increased risk of TLR over 2 years in unadjusted Cox analysis (HR: 1.68; 95% CI: 1.05-2.68; P = 0.03) (Figure 2). In contrast, although all-cause mortality showed a trend toward lower rates in the IVUS group on KM curves, IVUS use was not associated with a reduction in mortality over 2 years in the unadjusted Cox model (HR: 0.55; 95% CI: 0.26-1.17; P = 0.12) (Figure 3).Figure 2Kaplan-Meier Curve for Freedom from TLR Stratified by IVUS Guidance at 2-YearsTLR = target lesion revascularization; other abbreviation as in Figure 1.Figure 3Kaplan-Meier Curve of 2-Year Overall Survival Stratified by IVUS GuidanceAbbreviations as in Figure 1.

Discussion

The aim of this study is to evaluate the association between IVUS guidance and procedural and clinical outcomes during ICBT for ISR in a real-world cohort, with reported complication rates. Our findings indicate that IVUS-guided ICBT for ISR influenced procedural decision-making but was not independently associated with improved long-term clinical outcomes. In our cohort, IVUS was preferentially applied in patients with larger vessels, as reflected by longer dwell times, and in lesions requiring more advanced lesion preparation techniques, such as laser or cutting/scoring balloons, which may suggest greater procedural complexity. This pattern suggests that IVUS serves primarily as a tool for lesion assessment in anatomically and mechanically challenging ISR cases rather than as a universal procedural adjunct.17

Interestingly, univariate analysis showed a higher incidence of TLR in the IVUS-guided group, but IVUS was not an independent predictor of TLR in multivariate analysis. Instead, female sex emerged as the sole independent predictor of TLR at 2 years after adjustment. These findings suggest that the observed increase in TLR in the IVUS group likely reflects confounding by indication: IVUS was preferentially used in lesions considered more complex or higher-risk, including diffuse restenosis or suspected mechanical failure. Despite more aggressive lesion preparation and longer dwell times, these inherently challenging lesions may be predisposed to recurrence, highlighting the difficulty in isolating the causal effect of IVUS in observational analyses.

It is also important to recognize that contemporary ISR represents a heterogeneous entity. Although classical ISR is typically defined as restenosis occurring within 9 to 12 months following the index intervention, later presentations may reflect neoatherosclerosis within previously implanted stents. Neoatherosclerosis, including the development of lipid-rich plaques or calcified nodules within the stented segment, has been increasingly recognized as an important mechanism of late stent failure and may influence the response to therapies such as ICBT. These lesion types are often more complex and may be associated with higher recurrence rates despite optimal procedural technique.18

All-cause mortality was numerically lower in the IVUS group but did not reach statistical significance, and multivariate modeling confirmed that IVUS use was not independently associated with survival benefit. Postprocedural MI was more frequent in the non-IVUS group, which may reflect differences in lesion characteristics or procedural approach, although this association was not maintained in adjusted analyses. Other secondary endpoints—including cardiac hospitalizations, new-onset arrhythmia, bleeding events, repeat angiography, and stenosed vessel occurrence—were comparable between groups. Collectively, these results suggest that IVUS guidance does not confer a measurable reduction in long-term adverse events in this setting, despite influencing procedural strategy.

Comparison with prior literature underscores the nuances of IVUS application. Megaly et al reported lower TLR and numerically reduced target lesion failure among IVUS-guided ICBT procedures, suggesting potential benefit. The discrepancy with our study likely reflects differences in patient selection, lesion complexity, and operator practice patterns. Our findings are consistent with prior data and guideline recommendations indicating that, although intracoronary imaging is broadly supported during coronary stent procedures, its real-world use remains selective and is often applied in complex or higher-risk lesions, such as left main disease, calcified lesions, or stent failure.19 This selective use may limit the ability to observe direct clinical benefits in observational analyses. Importantly, prior studies have confirmed the safety of IVUS guidance, with no increase in procedural complications, flow-limiting dissections, perforations, or acute thrombosis.20

From a procedural standpoint, IVUS remains valuable for assessing vessel diameter, stent expansion, and lesion morphology, potentially guiding the selection of lesion modification techniques. However, our study does not demonstrate that this procedural information translates into improved TLR or mortality outcomes. This suggests that IVUS may be most beneficial in complex or ambiguous cases, rather than as a routine adjunct for all ICBT procedures.

However, it is important to acknowledge the intrinsic limitations of the phased-array IVUS platform used in this study. Although IVUS is useful for evaluating lumen dimensions and overall stent expansion, it has limited capability for detailed tissue characterization within restenotic segments. Specifically, phased-array IVUS cannot reliably distinguish between neoatherosclerosis, dense fibrotic tissue, and calcific components within the stented segment. Similarly, precise identification of multiple stent layers, assessment of calcium arc and thickness, or differentiation between eruptive and noneruptive calcified nodules may be challenging with this imaging modality.21

Higher-resolution intracoronary imaging techniques, such as optical coherence tomography or high-definition IVUS, may offer superior spatial resolution and improved visualization of intrastent pathology. These modalities allow more accurate assessment of stent layering, calcium distribution, and plaque characteristics, which may be relevant for procedural planning, lesion preparation strategies, and optimization of brachytherapy dosimetry.

In addition, optical coherence tomography provides superior longitudinal resolution compared with conventional phased-array IVUS, potentially allowing more precise assessment of lesion length and facilitating better selection of brachytherapy source train length. These imaging capabilities may be particularly relevant in complex ISR cases where accurate lesion characterization and treatment planning could influence the effectiveness of ICBT.22,23

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, nonrandomized design introduces the potential for selection bias, as IVUS use was determined by operator discretion rather than standardized criteria. Second, the sample size is relatively small, which may limit the statistical power to detect subtle differences in clinical outcomes such as TLR or mortality. Third, we could not systematically account for temporal trends in procedural techniques and device use (eg, laser or Shockwave therapy), which were more frequently adopted in later years; this, combined with shorter follow-up in IVUS cases, may have influenced lesion preparation strategies and observed TLR rates. Fourth, several procedural and lesion-specific variables were not captured in detail, including the pattern of ISR (e.g., Mehran classification), geographic miss, precise distal and proximal reference vessel diameters, and detailed lesion composition, which may affect brachytherapy outcomes. Fifth, the absence of routine angiographic follow-up limits definitive assessment of restenosis rates and target lesion failure, as repeat angiography was overall infrequent in our cohort, although similarly distributed between groups, potentially leading to underestimation of these outcomes. Finally, as a single-center study, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other patient populations or practice settings. Future prospective, randomized studies with standardized protocols and longer follow-up are needed to better define the role of IVUS in ICBT for ISR.

Conclusions

IVUS-guided ICBT for ISR was not independently associated with reduced TLR or all-cause mortality at 2-years of follow-up. It was rather employed in larger vessel size and was frequently used with advanced lesion modification techniques, and a lower incidence of postprocedural MI at midterm follow-up. These findings suggest that IVUS is a valuable tool for procedural planning in complex ISR, but its clinical benefit in routine practice remains uncertain. Prospective, randomized studies are warranted to confirm these results and to identify patient and lesion subsets that may derive the greatest benefit from IVUS guidance.PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: In patients with complex or recurrent ISR treated with ICBT, IVUS provides detailed assessment of vessel and stent-related characteristics that are not always visible on angiography alone.
