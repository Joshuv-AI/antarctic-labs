Swinging Waves in the Ablowitz-Ladik Equation Journal: Physica D
I. V. Barashenkov, Frank S. Smuts

Abstract
We construct a novel family of exact cnoidal wave and soliton solutions of the focusing and defocusing Ablowitz-Ladik equations. Unlike cnoidal waves that were obtained by earlier authors, the phase variable of the new solutions exhibits a nonlinear dependence on time and site number; the wave “swings". Our approach hinges on the existence of a two-point map governing the absolute value of the complex field; this map gives rise to standing waves centred arbitrarily relative to the lattice sites. Having derived stationary solutions, we use these as a basis for constructing waves with nonzero velocity. The localised members of the new family comprise dark solitons with the nontrivial asymptotic behaviour. We identify periodic and quasiperiodic patterns and establish an explicit quantisation rule for the velocity of the wave circulating in a closed loop of N N sites.

2
Standing waves

2.1
Amplitude map

Letting
U_{n}(t)=u_{n}e^{2i\omega t}
with time-independent
u_{n}
gives an equation for standing waves, that is,
stationary, nonpropagating, patterns:

(u_{n-1}+u_{n+1})(1+\sigma|u_{n}|^{2})=2\omega u_{n}.

(2.1)

Equation (
2.1
) has two invariants
[
29
]
:

I_{1}=\frac{i}{2}(u_{n}u_{n+1}^{*}-u_{n}^{*}u_{n+1})

(2.2)

and

I_{2}=|u_{n+1}|^{2}+|u_{n}|^{2}+\sigma\,|u_{n}u_{n+1}|^{2}-\omega\,(u_{n}u_{n+1}^{*}+u_{n+1}u_{n}^{*}).

(2.3)

Writing
u_{n}=\phi_{n}e^{i\theta_{n}}
, with
\phi_{n}=|u_{n}|
,
the invariants acquire the form

I_{1}=\phi_{n}\phi_{n+1}\sin(\theta_{n+1}-\theta_{n})

(2.4)

and

\displaystyle I_{2}=\rho_{n}+\rho_{n+1}+\sigma\rho_{n}\rho_{n+1}-2\omega\phi_{n}\phi_{n+1}\cos(\theta_{n+1}-\theta_{n}),

(2.5)

where
\rho_{n}=|u_{n}|^{2}
.
Eliminating the phase variable between (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) gives

(\rho_{n}+\rho_{n+1}+\sigma\,\rho_{n}\rho_{n+1}-I_{2})^{2}=4\omega^{2}(\rho_{n}\rho_{n+1}-I_{1}^{2}).

(2.6)

Subtracting the left- and right-hand sides of (
2.6
) from their expressions with
n\to n-1
,
we obtain a symmetric second-difference equation
for the amplitude
\rho_{n}
:

\rho_{n+1}-2\rho_{n}+\rho_{n-1}=-\frac{4\sigma\omega^{2}}{(\rho_{n}+\sigma)^{2}}+2\frac{1+\sigma I_{2}+2\omega^{2}}{\rho_{n}+\sigma}-2(\rho_{n}+\sigma).

(2.7)

The two-point map (
2.6
) serves as a conservation law for the difference equation (
2.7
).

The existence of a two-point map indicates an effective “translation invariance" of equation (
2.7
), meaning
that there is a continuous function
\rho(x)
such that the sequence
\rho_{n}=\rho(x_{n})

with
x_{n}=\mu n+x^{(0)}
solves (
2.7
) for any value of the translation parameter
x^{(0)}
.
(See
[
38
,
39
]
.) The function
\rho(x)
should satisfy the advance-delay equation

\rho_{+}-2\rho+\rho_{-}=-\frac{4\sigma\omega^{2}}{(\rho+\sigma)^{2}}+2\frac{1+\sigma I_{2}+2\omega^{2}}{\rho+\sigma}-2(\rho+\sigma),

(2.8)

where
\rho_{\pm}
is our short-hand notation for
\rho(x\pm\mu)
.

Equation (
2.8
) can be solved explicitly.
As a candidate solution
we try a class of periodic wavetrains

\rho(x)=A-B\thinspace\text{cn}^{2}(x,m),\quad A\geq 0.

(2.9)

Here, the exact definition of the elliptic modulus
m
(
0\leq m\leq 1
)
is specified by the Jacobi delta identity:

\text{dn}^{2}(x,m)+m\text{sn}^{2}(x,m)=1.

(In what follows, we routinely omit the dependence of the Jacobi functions on the modulus; that is, we write
\text{sn}y
for
\text{sn}(y,m)
,

\text{cn}y
for
\text{cn}(y,m)
, and so on.)
The Ansatz (
2.9
) is proposed based on the analogy with the cnoidal-wave solution of the differential nonlinear Schrödinger equation.
(See
D
).

With the choice of (
2.9
), the left-hand side of equation (
2.7
) can be represented as a rational function of
\rho
:

\rho_{+}+\rho_{-}=2A+\frac{2B}{m^{2}\text{sn}^{4}\mu}\frac{P_{2}(\rho)}{(\rho-\rho_{*})^{2}},

(2.10)

where

P_{2}(\rho)=B\thinspace\text{cn}^{2}\mu\thinspace(\rho-A)+m\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}\mu\thinspace\text{dn}^{2}\mu\thinspace(\rho-A+B)\left(\rho-A+\frac{m^{\prime}}{m}B\right),

with
m^{\prime}=1-m
,
and

\rho_{*}=A+B\frac{\text{dn}^{2}\mu}{m\text{sn}^{2}\mu}.

(2.11)

Comparing the positions of the pole in the left- and right-hand sides of (
2.8
) we observe that
\rho_{\star}=-\sigma
. Equating the corresponding coefficients of the Laurent expansions then gives

\displaystyle B=-m\frac{\text{sn}^{2}\mu}{\text{dn}^{2}\mu}(A+\sigma),

(2.12)

\displaystyle\omega^{2}=\frac{\text{cn}^{2}\,\mu}{\text{dn}^{4}\mu}(1+\sigma A)^{3}

(2.13)

and

I_{2}=\frac{\sigma\text{dn}^{2}\mu-(2A+\sigma m\text{sn}^{2}\mu)\text{cn}^{2}\mu}{\text{dn}^{4}\mu}(1+\sigma A)^{2}-\sigma.

(2.14)

The value of the invariant
I_{1}^{2}
can be determined from equation (
2.6
):

I_{1}^{2}=\frac{A(A-B)}{\text{dn}^{2}\mu}\Big(m^{\prime}\text{sn}^{2}\mu-\sigma A\text{cn}^{2}\mu\Big).

(2.15)

In (
2.12
)-(
2.15
), the parameter
A
is constrained by

\displaystyle 0\leq A\leq m^{\prime}\frac{\text{sn}^{2}\mu}{\text{cn}^{2}\mu},

\displaystyle\quad\sigma=

\displaystyle 1;

(2.16)

\displaystyle m\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}\mu\leq A<1,

\displaystyle\quad\sigma=-

\displaystyle 1.

(2.17)

It is worth noting that
B
is negative for
\sigma=1
but positive for
\sigma=-1
.

Note that equation (
2.13
) determines only the absolute value of the frequency
\omega
while its sign can be chosen arbitrarily.
This is due to the maps (
2.6
) and (
2.7
)
being invariant under the change
\omega\to-\omega
. The stationary solutions corresponding to frequencies

\omega
and
\tilde{\omega}=-\omega
are related by the
staggering transformation

u_{n}\to\tilde{u}_{n}=(-1)^{n}u_{n}.

(2.18)

The sign of
I_{1}
can also be chosen arbitrarily. Note that there is no correlation between the signs of
I_{1}
and
\omega
.
If
u_{n}
is a solution of the stationary equation (
2.1
) with a given
\omega
, then
the sequence
u_{n}^{*}
is another solution of that equation, with the same

but opposite value of

.

Finally, writing
\rho+\rho_{+}
and
\rho\rho_{+}
as quadratic polynomials in

, where

\zeta=\zeta(x)=1-m\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}\lambda\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}(x+\lambda)

(2.19)

and

(2.20)

we obtain

\rho+\rho_{+}+\sigma\rho\rho_{+}-I_{2}=-4\sigma(1+\sigma A)^{2}\frac{\text{cn}\mu}{\text{dn}^{3}\mu}\times(\text{cn}\mu+\text{dn}\mu)\thinspace\mathcal{R}(x),

(2.21a)

where

\mathcal{R}(x)=\frac{1}{\zeta(x)}-\frac{\sigma A\thinspace\text{cn}\mu\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}\lambda+\text{sn}^{2}\mu\text{dn}^{2}\lambda}{\text{sn}^{2}\mu\thinspace\text{dn}\mu\thinspace(1-m\thinspace\text{sn}^{4}\lambda)}.

(2.21b)

We will use the representation (
2.21
) in sections

and
3.2
below.

2.2
Standing waves with constant phase
(

)

Before constructing waveforms with variable phase,

Using (

and taken into account that

We have also introduced a parameter

To fix

Using the addition theorems for the elliptic integrals (see

Of special interest is the limit of infinite period:

In the focusing case (
\sigma=1

sending

(4.2)

Solutions of the defocusing (

(4.9)

Here, the carrier’s wavenumber is given by

\xi_{n}

The dark soliton represents a region of low intensity

propagating

In the continuum nonlinear Schrödinger equation, the

can be obtained just by the Galilean transformation. By contrast, in
a discrete system, localised structures propagating over the moving wave constitute an independent family with no obvious relation to dark solitons
travelling over a stationary background.

Periodic and Quasiperiodic Solutions

5.1
Travelling periodic waves

A physically meaningful subclass of solutions comprises waves on a ring: a finite chain with joined ends.
These have to satisfy the periodicity conditions

U_{n+N}=U_{n}
, where
N
is the number of sites in the chain.
The periodicity of the complex variable
U_{n}=\phi_{n}e^{i\Theta_{n}}
requires
\phi_{n+N}=\phi_{n}

and constrains its phase.

The amplitude
\phi_{n}
is periodic provided a natural number
\ell
of periods of the cnoidal wave matches the length of the chain:

2K(m)\times\ell=\mu N
.
This equation defines a set of admissible values of the chain spacing in the interval

0<\mu<2K(m)
:

\mu=\mu_{\ell}:=\frac{2\ell}{N}K(m),\quad\ell=1,2,...,N.

(5.1)

(The index
\ell
can be visualised as the number of wave crests on the ring.)

The constraint the phase should satisfy is

\Theta_{n+N}=\Theta_{n}+2\pi w,

(5.2)

where
w
(“winding number") is an integer.
Substituting
\Theta_{n}
from (
3.24b
) gives

\beta\left[\Pi(\xi_{n+N})-\Pi(\xi_{n})\right]=2\pi w-(\kappa\mu+k)N.

(5.3)

Both terms in the left-hand side of (
5.3
) are time-dependent; however the time derivative of the bracketed expression vanishes for
\mu=\mu_{\ell}
.
Therefore the left-hand side maintains its value for all
t
— in particular, for
t
rendering
\xi_{n}=0
:

\Pi(\xi_{n+N})-\Pi(\xi_{n})=\Pi(\mu_{\ell}N).

(5.4)

Since
\mu_{\ell}N=2\ell K(m)
, the right-hand side in (
5.4
) equals
\Pi(2\ell K(m))=2\ell\thinspace\Pi(\alpha^{2},m)
, where
\Pi(\alpha^{2},m)
is the complete
elliptic integral of the third kind:

\Pi(\alpha^{2},m)=\int_{0}^{K(m)}\frac{d\eta}{1-\alpha^{2}\text{sn}^{2}(\eta,m)}.

Solving (
5.3
) for
k
gives then

k=\beta\left[\Pi(\mu_{\ell})-\frac{2\ell}{N}\Pi(\alpha^{2},m)\right]-\arctan\left(\frac{I_{1}}{\omega}\frac{1+\sigma A}{A+\sigma m\text{sn}^{2}\mu_{\ell}}\right)+2\pi\frac{w}{N},

(5.5)

w\in\mathbb{Z}
.
To avoid any notational misinterpretation, we remind that
\Pi(\mu_{\ell})
in the line above stands for the
incomplete
elliptic integral:

\Pi(\mu_{\ell})=\Pi\left(\alpha^{2};\mathrm{am}(\mu_{\ell},m);m\right)=\int_{0}^{\mu_{\ell}}\frac{d\eta}{1-\alpha^{2}\text{sn}^{2}(\eta,m)}.

For each
A
,
m
,
N
and
\ell
, equation (
5.5
) defines a set of wavenumbers
k=k_{w}(A,m,N,\ell)
— and, by virtue of equation (
3.12
), — a set of
velocities
V_{w}(A,m,N,\ell)
, where
w=1,2,...,N
.
This means that in the Ablowitz-Ladik necklace of
N
elements,
the wave pattern with an amplitude
A
,
\ell
crests and the spacing between the crests given by
2K(m)

can travel at any of
N
nonequivalent velocities.

5.2
Standing wave quantisation

Of particular interest are stationary (
V=0
) periodic patterns.

Waves travelling with zero velocity are selected by choosing
k=0
in equation (
3.12
). On the other hand,
letting
k=0
in
equation (
5.5
) allows one to express
w
as a function of
A
and
m
:

w=\frac{\beta N}{2\pi}\left[\frac{2\ell}{N}\Pi(\alpha^{2},m)-\Pi(\mu_{\ell})\right]+\frac{N}{2\pi}\arctan\left(\frac{I_{1}}{\omega}\frac{1+\sigma A}{A+\sigma m\text{sn}^{2}\mu_{\ell}}\right).

(5.6)

For a generic pair of
m
and
A
the expression (
5.6
) does not assume an integer value — and so there are no stationary periodic solutions with this pair of parameters.
However for a given elliptic modulus
m
there is a particular set of integers
w
and
amplitudes

A_{w}(m)

such that the right-hand side of (
5.6
) with
A=A_{w}

equals
w
. The quantised amplitudes
A_{w}
characterise a set of standing waves on an
N
-site ring of the length
\mu N
, with
\ell
crests.
Figure
4
illustrates the graphical determination of
A_{w}
.

Figure 4
:
The right-hand side of equation (
5.6
) for a fixed
m
and varied
A
.
The left panel pertains to
\sigma=1
and the right one to
\sigma=-1
; in either case
N=60
,
m=0.9
and
\ell=3
.
Intersections of the
w(A)
curve with the horizontal gridlines (corresponding to the integer values of
w
)
give
the quantised standing wave amplitudes,
A_{w}
.
Thus, the horizontal coordinate of the blue blob in (a) is
A_{1}
and the coordinate of a similar blob in (b) is
A_{3}
.
The notation
m^{\prime}\mathrm{tn}^{2}\mu
in the left panel stands for
(1-m)\text{sn}^{2}(\mu_{\ell},m)/\text{cn}^{2}(\mu_{\ell},m)
. In the right panel,

m\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}\mu=m\text{sn}^{2}(\mu_{\ell},m)
.

5.3
Quasiperiodic solutions

The approach of section
5.1
can be trivially extended to the quasiperiodic boundary condition

U_{n+N}=U_{n}e^{i\gamma},

(5.7)

with
0<\gamma<2\pi
.

Indeed, choosing
\mu
to be given by
\mu_{\ell}
, one of the values in (
5.1
),
we make sure that the absolute value of
U_{n}
is
N
-periodic.
The boundary condition
(
5.7
) translates then into the following constraint on
the phase of the solution (
3.24
):

\Theta_{n+N}=\Theta_{n}+\gamma+2\pi\tilde{w},

(5.8)

where
\tilde{w}
is an integer. This is equivalent to (
5.2
) with
w=\gamma/(2\pi)+\tilde{w}
.
All we need to do to satisfy (
5.8
) is
let
k
be defined by
equation (
5.5
) with
w=\gamma/(2\pi)+\tilde{w}
.

6
Concluding Remarks

We have constructed novel classes of travelling-wave solutions of the focusing and defocusing Ablowitz-Ladik equation.
The modulus of each complex solution is expressible in terms of the Jacobi
cn
function while its phase (complex angle) involves the Legendre’s elliptic integral of the third kind.
Unlike cnoidal waves obtained by earlier authors, the phase variable has a nonlinear dependence on time.
The wave “swings"; see Fig
3
(b,d).

As its wavelength (the period of the Jacobi function) tends to infinity, the travelling wave transforms to a soliton, bright or dark.
The emerging bright soliton of the focusing equation is extensively documented
in the literature while the dark soliton solution of its defocusing counterpart is new. The latter object represents a localised depression
travelling over a non-stationary exponential wave background.

Lattice solitons in generic models may propagate without exciting radiation and thus defying the Peierls-Nabarro barrier
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
.
The corresponding special, isolated, propagation speeds depend on the discretisation parameter and
are referred to as the sliding velocities
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
.
The Ablowitz-Ladik solitons determined in
section
4
can propagate with an arbitrary speed; thus any
V
is a sliding velocity here.
This property is not necessarily related with the integrability of the Ablowitz-Ladik equation;
there are nonintegrable lattice models whose solitons can slide at an arbitrary speed
[
46
]
.

Despite its modulus being expressible in terms of a periodic function, the wave travelling at a generic speed is neither periodic nor quasiperiodic.
The wave can be seen as circulating around a ring of
N
sites only if it moves with one of special velocities.
These velocities form a set of
N
values that is determined by the amplitude of the wave, its length and the inter-site spacing.

We conclude with three remarks on the possible relevance of our new Ablowiz-Ladik solutions to other lattice systems.

First, the transformation
q_{n}(t)=e^{-in\varphi}U_{n}(t)
, where
\varphi
is a constant angle,
takes solutions of the Ablowitz-Ladik equation (
1.3
)
to those of the discrete Hirota equation
[
52
]

i\frac{\partial q_{n}}{\partial t}+\left(e^{i\varphi}q_{n+1}+q_{n-1}e^{-i\varphi}\right)(1+\sigma|q_{n}|^{2})=0.

(6.1)

In particular, the quasiperiodic Ablowitz-Ladik
travelling waves constructed in section
5.3
and
satisfying the boundary condition (
5.7
) with
\gamma=N\varphi

are mapped to periodic solutions of (
6.1
) with
q_{n+N}=q_{n}
.

Choosing
\varphi=\pi/2
, we obtain periodic waves of
the complex discrete modified KdV equation
[
33
]

\frac{\partial q_{n}}{\partial t}+\left(q_{n+1}-q_{n-1})\right(1+\sigma|q_{n}|^{2})=0.

(6.2)

Second,
the swinging-wave solutions may be deformed by homotopy continuation from the Ablowitz-Ladik lattice to its nonintegrable counterpart,
the discrete nonlinear Schrödinger equation:

i\frac{\partial U_{n}}{\partial t}+U_{n-1}+U_{n+1}+2\sigma|U_{n}|^{2}U_{n}=0.

(6.3)

The latter system plays an immense role in physical applications, most notably in optical and matter-wave settings
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.
Previous studies analysed its solutions
as perturbations of the Ablowitz-Ladik
linear-phase soliton and breather

[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
.
The homotopy continuation of the new cnoidal-wave and soliton solutions
of the Ablowitz-Ladik equation should yield nontrivial-phase
solutions of equation (
6.3
).

Finally, it is worth noting that the Ablowitz-Ladik equations describe the integrable motions of a discrete curve
on the sphere
[
53
]
and the asymptotic behavior of the temperature correlation function of the quantum XY spin chain
[
54
]
.
New classes of solutions may admit interpretation in these
and similar contexts.

Appendix A
Addition theorem for elliptic integral

Cayley
[
48
]
casts the addition theorem into the following simple form:

\Pi(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})-\Pi(\xi_{1})-\Pi(\xi_{2})=\int_{0}^{R}\frac{dr}{1+\mathcal{A}r^{2}}.

(A.1)

Here
\Pi(\xi)
is our short-hand notation for Legendre’s elliptic integral of the third kind, equation (
3.17
). The upper limit of integration in (
A.1
)
is given by

R=\frac{\alpha^{2}\thinspace\text{sn}\thinspace\xi_{1}\thinspace\text{sn}\thinspace\xi_{2}\thinspace\text{sn}(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})}{1-\alpha^{2}+\alpha^{2}\thinspace\text{cn}\thinspace\xi_{1}\thinspace\text{cn}\thinspace\xi_{2}\thinspace\text{cn}(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})},

and the parameter
\mathcal{A}
is defined by

\mathcal{A}:=(1-\alpha^{2})\left(1-\frac{m}{\alpha^{2}}\right).

Note that
\mathcal{A}>0
regardless of whether
\alpha^{2}<0
or

m<\alpha^{2}<1
.

Doing the integral in (
A.1
) we arrive at two cases of the addition theorem, different in the sign in front of the right-hand side:

\displaystyle\Pi(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})-\Pi(\xi_{1})-\Pi(\xi_{2})=\pm\sqrt{\frac{\alpha^{2}}{(1-\alpha^{2})(\alpha^{2}-m)}}

\displaystyle\times\arctan\left[\frac{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}(\alpha^{2}-m)(1-\alpha^{2})}\thinspace\text{sn}\thinspace\xi_{1}\thinspace\text{sn}\thinspace\xi_{2}\thinspace\text{sn}(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})}{1-\alpha^{2}+\alpha^{2}\thinspace\text{cn}\thinspace\xi_{1}\thinspace\text{cn}\thinspace\xi_{2}\thinspace\text{cn}(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})}\right].

(A.2)

The top (positive) sign in (
A.2
) corresponds to
\alpha^{2}>0
and the bottom (negative) one to
\alpha^{2}<0
.

The occurrence of two signs has a simple explanation. When
\alpha^{2}>0
, the integral
\Pi(\xi)
grows faster than
\xi
,
whereas when
\alpha^{2}<0
, it grows slower than linearly. Therefore,
\Pi(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2})
is greater than
\Pi(\xi_{1})+\Pi(\xi_{2})
in
the former case and less in the latter.

There is some confusion in the reference literature about the correct form of the addition theorem.
Zhuravskii
[
49
]
writes the rule (
A.2
) with only a negative sign on the right-hand side, but does not make clear whether he considers the case
\alpha^{2}<0
exclusively.
Byrd and Friedman
[
50
]
also give (
A.2
) with only a negative sign, while erroneously claiming that their addition result is valid for either sign of
\alpha^{2}
.
Finally, the most recent resource
[
51
]
presents the addition theorem in a form that is exactly reducible to equation (
A.2
).

Appendix B
Travelling increment, regular and singular

As the coordinate
\xi
is varied, the
argument of the arctangent in (
3.20b
) may develop singularities.
In such a case, the
equation (
3.20b
) will not
define a continuous function of
\xi
.
In this Appendix we characterise the parameters
\sigma
and
\alpha^{2}
that give rise to the singular behaviour.

Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the denominator of the fraction
q(\xi)
in (
3.20b
):

\mathcal{D}(y)=1-\alpha^{2}\left[\text{sn}^{2}\mu+y\thinspace\text{cn}\mu\thinspace\text{dn}\mu\right],

(B.1)

where

y=y(\xi)=\text{sn}\thinspace\xi\thinspace\text{sn}(\xi+\mu).

As
\xi
varies from
-\infty
to
\infty
,
the
2K(m)
-periodic function
y(\xi)
oscillates between
y_{\mathrm{min}}=-\text{sn}^{2}\lambda

and
y_{\mathrm{max}}=\text{cn}^{2}\lambda/\text{dn}^{2}\lambda
, and
the linear function
\mathcal{D}(y)

maps the interval
(y_{\mathrm{min}},y_{\mathrm{max}})
onto
(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{min}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{max}})
.
To determine whether the argument of the arctangent in (
3.20b
) is singular or not, we need to determine whether the
range
(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{min}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{max}})
contains the origin.

In the case of the defocusing Ablowitz-Ladik equation (
\sigma=-1
) the parameter
\alpha^{2}
is negative.
When
\text{cn}\mu>0
, the lowest value of
\mathcal{D}(y)
is attained at
y=y_{\mathrm{min}}
:

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{min}}=1-\alpha^{2}\left(\text{sn}^{2}\mu-\text{cn}\mu\thinspace\text{dn}\mu\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}\lambda\right).

(B.2)

Using identities satisfied by the Jacobi functions, this expression is cast in the form with a positive lower bound:

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{min}}=1-\alpha^{2}\frac{(1-\text{cn}\mu)(1+\text{cn}\mu+\text{dn}\mu)}{1+\text{dn}\mu}\geq 1.

On the other hand, when
\text{cn}\mu<0
, the function
\mathcal{D}(y)
reaches its minimum at
y=y_{\mathrm{max}}
:

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{min}}=1-\alpha^{2}\left(\text{sn}^{2}\mu+\text{cn}\mu\thinspace\text{dn}\mu\frac{\text{cn}^{2}\lambda}{\text{dn}^{2}\lambda}\right).

(B.3)

Transforming to functions of double argument, this becomes

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{min}}=1-\alpha^{2}\left[(1-m)\text{sn}^{2}\mu+\text{cn}\mu+\text{dn}\mu\right].

(B.4)

The bracketed expression in (
B.4
) is positive; hence
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{min}}\geq 1
.

Turning to the focusing case (
\sigma=1
), the parameter
\alpha^{2}
becomes positive (
m\leq\alpha^{2}\leq 1
).
When
\text{cn}\mu>0
, the maximum of
\mathcal{D}(y)
for the fixed
\alpha^{2}
is given by

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{max}}=1-\alpha^{2}\left(\text{sn}^{2}\mu-\text{cn}\mu\thinspace\text{dn}\mu\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}\lambda\right).

Transforming to the double argument, this becomes

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{max}}=1-\alpha^{2}\frac{1-\text{cn}\mu}{1+\text{dn}\mu}(1+\text{dn}\mu+\text{cn}\mu).

The lowest value of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{max}}
over all admissible
\alpha^{2}
corresponds to
\alpha^{2}=1
.
One readily checks that this value is positive:

\left.\phantom{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{max}}\right|_{\alpha^{2}=1}=\frac{\text{cn}\mu(\text{dn}\mu+\text{cn}\mu)}{1+\text{dn}\mu}>0.

Therefore,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{max}}
is positive regardless of the choice of
\alpha^{2}
.
On the other hand, the minimum of
\mathcal{D}(y)
for the fixed
\alpha^{2}
is given by (
B.3
)
which we write as

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{min}}=1-\alpha^{2}\left[1+\frac{\text{cn}\mu(\text{dn}\mu-\text{cn}\mu)}{1+\text{dn}\mu}\right].

(B.5)

The minimum value (
B.5
) is positive respectively negative if
\alpha^{2}<\alpha_{c}^{2}
respectively
\alpha^{2}>\alpha_{c}^{2}
, where

\alpha_{c}^{-2}:=\frac{1+\text{cn}\mu}{1+\text{dn}\mu}(1+\text{dn}\mu-\text{cn}\mu).

(B.6)

(Note that
m\leq\alpha_{c}^{2}\leq 1
.)
Thus the function
\mathcal{D}(y)
crosses through zero if the characteristic
\alpha^{2}
lies in the interval
(\alpha_{c}^{2},1)
— and remains positive for all
y
if
\alpha^{2}
is in
the complementary interval
(m,\alpha_{c}^{2})
.

Finally, it remains to consider the situation where
\sigma=1
and
\text{cn}\mu<0
. In this case the expression in the square brackets in (
B.1
) oscillates between two positive values:

S_{-}\leq\text{sn}^{2}\mu+\text{cn}\mu\thinspace\text{dn}\mu\thinspace y(\xi)\leq S_{+},

where

S_{\pm}=\frac{1\mp\text{cn}\mu}{1+\text{dn}\mu}(1+\text{dn}\mu\pm\text{cn}\mu)\geq 0.

As a result, the denominator
\mathcal{D}
fills the range
(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{min}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{max}})
, where

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{max}}\geq 0
for all
\alpha^{2}\in(m,1)
while
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{min}}
is positive respectively negative
for
\alpha^{2}<\alpha_{c}^{2}
respectively
\alpha^{2}>\alpha_{c}^{2}
. This time, the threshold value of
\alpha^{2}
is given by

\alpha_{c}^{-2}:=\frac{1-\text{cn}\mu}{1+\text{dn}\mu}(1+\text{cn}\mu+\text{dn}\mu).

(B.7)

(Like the transition point defined by (
B.6
), this critical value satisfies
m\leq\alpha_{c}^{2}\leq 1
.)

To summarise, in the defocusing situation (
\sigma=-1
)
equation (
3.20b
) defines a continuous function of
\xi\in(-\infty,\infty)
regardless of the value of
\alpha^{2}
and sign of
\text{cn}\mu
.
In the focusing case (
\sigma=1
)
the arctangent remains a continuous function of
\xi
for
\alpha^{2}<\alpha_{c}^{2}
but has jump discontinuities when
\alpha^{2}>\alpha_{c}^{2}
,
where
\alpha_{c}^{2}
is as in (
B.6
) and (
B.7
) for
\text{cn}\mu>0
and
\text{cn}\mu<0
, respectively.
To obtain a continuous function
\chi(\xi)
for
\alpha^{2}>\alpha_{c}^{2}
,
\sigma=1
(and so to retain the relation (
3.20
)),
we need to
consider the analytic continuation of the function (
3.20b
)
to a Riemann surface.

Appendix C
Equivalence of travelling and stationary phase

The construction of the travelling wave solution in sections
3.1
and
3.2

assumes the agreement of the “travelling" phase increment and its stationary counterpart,
\Delta(\xi)
.
Once the phase component of the solution has been determined,
the procedure needs to be validated by verifying this agreement.
In this Appendix, we demonstrate that the difference between
\Delta(\xi)
and
\chi(\xi)
(the variable part of the “travelling" increment)
is
indeed a constant.
Therefore, the travelling and stationary increments can be made equal just by a proper choice of
\kappa
.

Equations (
3.7
), (
3.8
) and (
2.21
) give

\cot\Delta(\xi)=\left[\frac{2(-\sigma)(1+\sigma A)^{2}}{\omega I_{1}}\times\frac{\text{cn}\mu(\text{cn}\mu+\text{dn}\mu)}{\text{dn}^{3}\mu}\mathcal{R}(\xi)\right],

(C.1)

with
\mathcal{R}
as in (
2.21b
).
The quantity
\cot\Delta
defines a meromorphic function of
\zeta
,
with a pole at the origin:

\cot\Delta=-s_{\omega}\sigma\frac{\sqrt{1+\sigma A}}{I_{1}}\mathcal{G}\times\left(\mathcal{R}_{0}+\frac{1}{\zeta}\right).

(C.2)

Here
\zeta
is as in (
2.19

\zeta=1-m\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}\lambda\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}(\xi+\lambda),

while
the residue and analytic part of (

) are straightforward from (

) and (
2.21b
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=2\frac{\text{cn}\mu+\text{dn}\mu}{\text{dn}\mu},\quad\mathcal{R}_{0}=-\frac{\sigma A\thinspace\text{cn}\mu\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}\lambda+\text{sn}^{2}\mu\text{dn}^{2}\lambda}{\text{sn}^{2}\mu\thinspace\text{dn}\mu\thinspace(1-m\thinspace\text{sn}^{4}\lambda)}.

In the expression for
the “travelling" increment, equation (
3.20
),
the cotangent of
\chi
can also be treated as a meromorphic function of
\zeta
.
To determine poles and residues of that function, we note that
\text{sn}(\xi+\mu)\thinspace\text{sn}\thinspace\xi
can be written as

\text{sn}(\xi+\mu)\thinspace\text{sn}\thinspace\xi=\frac{1-m\thinspace\text{sn}^{4}\lambda-\zeta}{m\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}(\lambda)\thinspace\zeta}.

Equation (
3.20b
) gives then

\cot\chi=s_{\omega}\thinspace\sigma\frac{\mathcal{F}}{I_{1}\sqrt{1+\sigma A}}\left(X_{0}+\frac{X_{1}\zeta_{1}}{\zeta-\zeta_{1}}\right),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\frac{\text{dn}\mu}{m\text{sn}^{2}\mu}(A-B)^{2},

\displaystyle\quad\zeta_{1}=1-m\text{sn}^{4}\lambda,

\displaystyle X_{1}=-m\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}\lambda(1-\alpha^{2}\text{sn}^{2}\mu),

Making use of the identity

\cot(\Delta-\chi)=\frac{1+\cot\chi\cot\Delta}{\cot\chi-\cot\Delta},

the trigonometric function of the difference of the increments can be expressed as a ratio

\cot(\Delta-\chi)=\frac{Y_{0}+Y_{1}(\zeta-\zeta_{1})^{-1}+Y_{2}{\zeta}^{-1}}{Z_{0}+Z_{1}(\zeta-\zeta_{1})^{-1}+Z_{2}\zeta^{-1}},

(C.3)

with the residues

independent of
\xi
.
Requiring the ratio (

) to be a constant as a function of
\xi
amounts to demanding it to be a constant as a function of
\zeta
.
The latter requires the residues of the numerator and denominator — at
\zeta_{0}

\frac{Y_{0}}{Z_{0}}=\frac{Y_{1}}{Z_{1}}=\frac{Y_{2}}{Z_{2}}.

Substituting for
Y_{n}
and
Z_{n}
, the above relations reduce to

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\,\frac{X_{1}-X_{0}}{1+\sigma A}+\mathcal{G}\left(\mathcal{R}_{0}+\frac{1}{\zeta_{1}}\right)=

\displaystyle 0,

(C.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{2}X_{0}\frac{X_{1}-X_{0}}{1+\sigma A}+\mathcal{F}\mathcal{G}\thinspace\mathcal{R}_{0}X_{1}-I_{1}^{2}=

\displaystyle 0.

(C.5)

Note that every term in (
C.4
) and (
C.5
) is expressible as a rational function of
z=\sigma(A-B)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{0}=-\frac{\thinspace\text{cn}\mu\thinspace\text{dn}\mu\thinspace z+(\text{cn}\mu+1)(\text{dn}\mu+1)}{2(\text{cn}\mu+\text{dn}\mu)},

\displaystyle X_{0}=\frac{m}{z}\left(m\text{sn}^{4}\mu\frac{1-\text{cn}\mu}{1+\text{dn}\mu}-\text{cn}\mu\thinspace\text{dn}\mu\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}\mu\right)-m\left[\text{cn}\mu\thinspace\text{dn}\mu\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}\mu+\frac{1-\text{cn}\mu}{1+\text{dn}\mu}(1-m\text{sn}^{4}\mu)\right],

\displaystyle X_{1}=\frac{m(1-\text{cn}\mu)}{1+\text{dn}\mu}\left(\frac{m\text{sn}^{4}\mu}{z}+m\text{sn}^{4}\mu-1\right),\quad\frac{X_{1}-X_{0}}{1+\sigma A}=\frac{m\thinspace\text{cn}\mu\thinspace\text{sn}^{2}\mu}{\text{dn}\mu}\frac{1}{z},

\displaystyle I_{1}^{2}=z\left(z\text{dn}^{2}\mu-m\text{sn}^{2}\mu\right)\times\left(\text{sn}^{2}\mu-z\text{cn}^{2}\mu\right).

Consequently, the left-hand side of (
C.4
) is a polynomial of the first order and that of (
C.5
) second order in
z
.

Verifying that the coefficient of each power of
z
in (
C.4
) and (
C.5
) is zero, we conclude that
\cot(\Delta-\chi)
is indeed
\xi
-independent.
Specifically,

\cot(\Delta-\chi)=\frac{Y_{2}}{Z_{2}}=s_{\omega}\sigma\frac{\mathcal{F}}{I_{1}}\frac{X_{1}-X_{0}}{\sqrt{1+\sigma A}}.

(C.6)

Appendix D
Cnoidal wave of the nonlinear Schrödinger equations

Cnoidal wave solutions of the focusing and defocusing nonlinear Schrödinger equations
are well known in the literature.
To highlight the similarities with our solution of the Ablowitz–Ladik equation
and to facilitate the transition to its continuum limit, we briefly reproduce their derivation here.

Substituting an Ansatz
\psi(x,\tau)=p(\eta)e^{i\varphi(\eta)+i\tilde{\omega}\tau}
with
\eta=x-\varv\tau
in equation (
3.28
),
we decompose it into a system of ordinary differential equations for the real-valued functions
p
and
\varphi
:

\displaystyle\varphi_{\eta\eta}p+2\varphi_{\eta}p_{\eta}-\varv p_{\eta}=0,

(D.1)

\displaystyle p_{\eta\eta}+\varv\varphi_{\eta}p-\tilde{\omega}p-\varphi_{\eta}^{2}p+2\sigma p^{3}=0.

(D.2)

The order of equation (
D.1
) is reduced by introducing an integrating factor. We obtain

\varphi_{\eta}=\frac{\varv}{2}+\frac{J_{1}}{r},

(D.3)

where
r=p^{2}
and
J_{1}
is a constant of integration. Feeding (
D.3
) into (
D.2
) yields

p_{\eta\eta}+\left(\frac{\varv^{2}}{4}-\tilde{\omega}\right)p+2\sigma p^{3}-\frac{J_{1}^{2}}{p^{3}}=0.

(D.4)

An implicit solution of (
D.4
) is given by the quadrature:

\displaystyle\pm 2(\eta-\eta_{0})=\int\frac{dr}{\sqrt{-U(r)}},

(D.5)

\displaystyle U(r)=\sigma r^{3}+\left(\frac{\varv^{2}}{4}-\tilde{\omega}\right)r^{2}-J_{2}r+J_{1}^{2},

where
J_{2}
and
\eta_{0}
are further constants of integration.

The solution
r(\eta)
is periodic if the cubic polynomial
U(r)
has three real roots:

U=\sigma(r-a)(r-b)(r-c).

(D.6)

We order the roots as

\displaystyle c\leq 0\leq a\leq b

\displaystyle\quad(\sigma=1);

(D.7)

\displaystyle 0\leq b\leq a\leq c

\displaystyle\quad(\sigma=-1).

Doing the elliptic integral in (
D.5
) and inverting the function
\eta(r)
gives an explicit expression for
r(\eta)
:

r=a+(b-a)\text{cn}^{2}\left[\sqrt{\sigma(b-c)}(\eta-\eta_{0}),m\right],\quad m=\frac{b-a}{b-c}.

(D.8)

Equations (
D.3
), (
D.5
) and (
D.6
) possess a scaling invariance:

\displaystyle\eta\to\lambda\eta,\quad r\to\lambda^{-2}r,\quad\varphi\to\varphi;

\displaystyle\varv\to\lambda^{-1}\varv,\quad(a,b,c,\tilde{\omega})\to\lambda^{-2}(a,b,c,\tilde{\omega}),\quad J_{1}\to\lambda^{-3}J_{1},\quad J_{2}\to\lambda^{-4}J_{2}.

(D.9)

In what follows, we fix the value of
b-c
— specifically,
we
set
b-c=\sigma
— while keeping in mind that the family of solutions may be extended at any stage by applying the scaling (
D.9
).
With this choice, equation (
D.8
) simplifies to

r=a+\sigma m\text{cn}^{2}(\eta-\eta_{0},m).

(D.10)

Here
m
(
0\leq m\leq 1
) and
a
(
a\geq 0
)
can be considered independent parameters.
In the focusing situation (
\sigma=1
) the inequalities (
D.7
) imply
0\leq a\leq m^{\prime}
, where
m^{\prime}=1-m
;
in the defocusing case (
\sigma=-1
) we have
a\geq m
.
The original parameters
\tilde{\omega}
,
J_{1}
, and
J_{2}

are expressible via this new set:

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}=\frac{\varv^{2}}{4}-m^{\prime}+m+3\sigma a,

(D.11)

\displaystyle J_{1}=\tilde{s}_{1}\sqrt{a(a+\sigma m)(m^{\prime}-\sigma a)},

(D.12)

\displaystyle J_{2}=\sigma(mm^{\prime}-3a^{2})+2a(m^{\prime}-m).

In (
D.12
),
\tilde{s}_{1}=\pm 1
is a sign factor that may be chosen arbitrarily.

Substituting (
D.10
) in (
D.3
) and performing the integration, we recover the phase variable:

\varphi=\frac{\varv}{2}\eta+{\tilde{s}_{1}}\sqrt{\frac{a(m^{\prime}-\sigma a)}{a+\sigma m}}\Pi({\tilde{\alpha}}^{2},\mathrm{am}\thinspace\eta,m),

(D.13)

where

{\tilde{\alpha}}^{2}=\frac{m}{m+\sigma a}

(D.14)

is positive for
\sigma=1
and negative for
\sigma=-1
.

The amplitude (
D.10
) and phase (
D.13
) constitute the cnoidal wave solution of
the focusing and defocusing nonlinear Schrödinger equations (
3.28
).
