 ABSTRACT

This project, a Cooperative Research and Development Agreement (CRADA) partnership between A. O.
Smith and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), was initiated to develop a heat pump water heater
(HPWH) based on thermoelectric (TE) heat pump technology capable of seeing widespread market
adoption by having a one-year payback versus electric resistance options.

To realize a one-year payback relative to a $400 electric resistance water heater (ERWH), one pathway
would be to achieve a product retail price below $525 (within $125 of the ERWH retail price), if Uniform
Energy Factor (UEF) were also boosted from 0.95 up to 1.25. Through experimental prototype fabrication
and evaluation, this project aimed to evaluate the feasibility of this pathway by utilizing commercially
available bismuth telluride (BiTe) TE technology, along with proposed designs for heat transfer and system
packaging.

Based on experimental results in the project, the cost target was met, but the experimentally measured
efficiency results fell short of the 30% improvement target. The shortcomings were traced to insufficient
heat transfer between the ambient air and the TE devices, which is shown in a parametric study that
illustrates the importance of reducing the thermal resistances in the TE heat exchangers to meet the desired
COP and UEF metrics. A measured UEF of 1.00 was achieved in experimental prototype evaluation, which
is still substantially better than the typical 0.95 of electric resistance models. The original UEF goal is
predicted to be achievable with sufficiently favorable heat transfer.

1. PROJECT OVERVIEW

1.1 STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

The project objective was to develop a heat pump water heater (HPWH) based on thermoelectric (TE) heat
pump technology that delivers a Uniform Energy Factor (UEF) of 1.25—an improvement of over 30%
versus 0.95, the best available electric resistance water heater (ERWH)—at a retail price at scale of $525.
This would result in annual utility energy savings of $125, a payback period of one year, and a product that
offers consumers a cost-effective upgrade without the “sticker shock” currently preventing widespread
adoption of vapor compression HPWHs.

To realize a one-year payback relative to a $400 ERWH, the product retail price must be below $525 (within
$125 of the ERWH retail price). The project pursued prototyping a system to evaluate this possibility using
mass market bismuth telluride (BiTe) TE technology, along with the team’s proposed designs for heat
transfer and system packaging.

1.2 PROJECT DESCRIPTION

Two main types of electric residential storage water heaters are available today: ERWHs, with UEFs
ranging from 0.91 to 0.95, and vapor compression heat pump water heaters (HPWHs) with UEFs ranging
from 2.0 to 4.0. The retail price of HPWHs is about 3.5 times that of ERWHs. Thus, there is a wide
efficiency gap and a wide price gap in the market, requiring consumers to make large first cost investments
to improve their water heating efficiency. This project explored developing a TE-driven HPWH with
modest incremental cost over ERWHs to help fill this gap and provide consumers with additional options
for electric water heaters.

1.3 SYSTEM OVERVIEW

The system concept, as originally conceived in the proposal, is described in this section. It ended up having
only minor differences with the fabricated prototype.

The system concept is to use a tank and jacket (the sheet metal surrounding the insulation around the tank)
identical to conventional ERWHs to ensure easy replacement of existing systems, as well as compatibility
with existing manufacturing lines. Heating was provided by a series of eight TE modules (50 W nominal
heating power each and maximum of 75 W at 0°F temperature lift) that heat water pumped from the bottom
of the tank to the final desired temperature before delivering it to the top of the tank. A single 4.5 kW
resistance element in the upper portion of the tank was retained for backup heating during high hot water
demand situations. The TE modules were placed in series, and each TE module incrementally increases the
water temperature until the desired temperature is reached, as shown in Error! Reference source not
found.. This means that each TE module sees a different lift. This is a strategy to increase system efficiency
that is not generally available for small vapor compression systems, since implementing the strategy with
vapor compression technology would require multiple compressors, multiple expansion devices, multiple
evaporators, and multiple condensers. The TE modules extract heat from the ambient air with the aid of
extruded aluminum heat exchangers and a small fan. The only two moving parts in the system are the pump
and the fan, and all the components can be housed between the tank and jacket used in ERWHs. The pump
and power supply for the TE modules can be placed in the access panel where the lower element electronics
would traditionally be located, minimizing design changes relative to ERWHs. A 3D model of the proposed
design is shown in Figure 2.

Figure 1: With the TE modules placed in series, most TE modules (especially D) see a reduced
temperature lift for higher COP. Only the final TE (position A) sees the full temperature lift.

The input power to the TE modules and pump speed can be varied to provide water maintained at the set
point temperature (e.g. 130°F) to the tank top and allow the system to continuously operate at the best
balance of efficiency and capacity. Two thermistors will be used to measure the temperature of the water
entering and leaving the TE modules. These provide inputs for controlling the TE module capacity and the
pump flow rate. An example set of possible operating modes are as follows:

 - During standby, the TE modules will provide ~ 65 W of heat to offset tank losses, and the pump
 will operate at a very low flow rate of ~5 gallons per hour (gph).
 - During small draws (approximately <15 gph), the TE modules will operate at or below their
 nominal capacity (200 W) to ensure high-efficiency operation.

 2

 - During medium draws (approximately >15 and <30 gph), the water will be heated using the TE
 modules operating in “boost” mode, ~750 W capacity (assuming 10 TE’s at 0°F temperature lift),
 to decrease recovery time while maintaining efficiency higher than electric resistance.
 - During very large draws (approximately >30 gph), the upper element will be used in addition to
 TE modules to achieve the fastest recovery.

Medium draws will be identified using a thermostat (like the one typically used to control the lower heating
element) that will close to indicate a medium or larger hot water demand and initiate “boost” mode for the
TE modules. The location of this thermostat will need to be optimized to maximize the system efficiency
and recovery rate. During large draws, the upper element will be operated by a thermostat, as is typically
done on an ERWH.

The variable heat pump capacity is expected to deliver a medium draw UEF profile without relying on the
4.5 kW backup resistance heat. The team’s confidence in this strategy is based on experience with a ~800
W prototype in an external pumped loop, evaluated at ORNL in 2019 (Patel 2018, Patel 2020), which was
able to deliver a UEF medium draw with two run periods totaling about 7 h, without reliance on backup
heat, and with some capacity to spare.

 2. BENEFITS TO THE FUNDING DOE OFFICE'S MISSION

2.1 POTENTIAL TECHNOLOGY ADVANCES

More widespread adoption of HPWH technologies would improve energy efficiency of water heating and
lead to substantial national primary energy savings as well as utility cost savings for consumers.

The primary challenges that will be addressed by the R&D of this project are related to integrated system
design and controls. Key challenges include the following:

 - Identifying the required TE capacity that provides sufficient heating capacity to avoid reliance on
 the backup resistance element during the UEF draw profile

 3

 - Identifying the quantity of TE modules that minimizes the payback period and delivers the most
 efficiency improvement

 - Designing TE controls that optimize the performance of the system

 - Designing the heat transfer system for these particular operating conditions and cost requirements

When this concept is successful, this method of TE boosting could potentially be added to other product
categories, such as electric TE heat pump–boosted gas water heaters, TE heat pumps for solar backup,
temperature boosting in hydronic distribution systems, high-temperature commercial disinfection water
heating, among others.

 3. TECHNICAL DISCUSSION OF WORK PERFORMED BY ALL PARTIES

The following outcomes were achieved in this project:

 • Fabricated prototype thermoelectric heat pump (TEHP) unit

 • Integrated TEHP with AO Smith electric resistance 40-gal tank

 • Implemented controls for two heating stages

 • Experimentally reached First Hour Rating (FHR) goal of >45 gal.

 • Experimentally achieved 13% higher efficiency over electric resistance under UEF draw profile

 • Developed models for tank temperature stratification and thermoelectric module performance

3.1 TECHNICAL SCOPE SUMMARY

The overall technical approach involved experimental prototype fabrication and modeling activities
proceeding in parallel. A breadboard prototype was fabricated and tested based on a design that was
described in the original proposal for the work. In parallel, a design model was developed and used to
inform design decisions and explore controls approaches. A second protype (packaged) was planned for
the final stage of the project, but was not pursued due to the change in priorities.

The proposed design primarily consisted of four 40×40 mm TE modules in series, sandwiched between a
water-carrying minichannel heat exchanger on the hot side of the TEs, and a set of aluminum plain-finned
air heat exchangers on the cold side. Consistent with a “breadboard” prototype design, the components were
placed outside the standard water heater envelope for ease of access and rapidity of fabrication.

Additionally, a benchtop-scale TE experiment was conducted to provide component validation data (Figure
3). Data was collected from this setup for conditions typical for a UEF (water entering at 58°F, Figure 4 –
right) and during recovery of the water heater (water entering at 75°F, Figure 4 – left) The COPs for the
75°F condition (Figure 4 – left) dropped with increasing power, as expected. On the other hand, the 75°F
entering water scenario (in which the ambient is warmer than the entering water) showed a counterintuitive
trend with COP increasing with increasing power (Figure 4 – right). In parallel to that experimental effort,
a model of a water-to-air TE HP heat exchanger previously developed at ORNL was adapted to
accommodate the air-to-water configuration for this TE HPWH concept.

 4. EXPERIMENTAL PROTOTYPE

4.1 INTRODUCTION

Residential water heating in the US consumes 3.0 EJ of energy annually (Mills, 2022), or about 3% of all
energy used nationally each year. Energy for water heating is mainly consumed in the form of natural gas
and electricity, and the efficiency of water heaters, represented by the energy factor (EF), determines how
much energy (in the form of heated water) can be delivered by the energy consumed. Improving the EF of

 5

residential water heaters can have a large impact on national energy consumption, but only if the technology
is adopted by US consumers in an already well-established market. The current residential water heater
market is dominated by two designs; natural gas fired (NG) and electric resistance (ER). NG units make up
about 48% of all installed residential water heaters and 47% of residential water heater shipments (EIA
2020, AHRI 2023). Electric water heaters take up most of the remaining 52% & 53%, respectively, with
other liquid propane and fuel oil units contributing <5%. Of the electric water heaters, almost all units are
ER, and only an estimated 1% (Mills, 2022) are high-efficiency heat pump water heaters (HPWH). The
largest factor in slow adoption of HPWHs is the high purchase price compared to the low purchase prices
of traditional NG and ER units. HPWHs utilize less operating energy and therefore can have a lower lifetime
cost to consumers, but a larger initial investment is required. To make progress in reducing energy used by
the residential water heating sector, technology solutions must both raise the UEF above existing NG and
ER technologies and lower retail prices to a level that consumers will adopt.

Improving appliance energy performance at a marginal cost increase using TE technology has been
investigated by Patel et al. (2018) for application in electric clothes dryers and by Kumar et al. (2021) in
dishwashers; both resulted in lower energy consumption of an experimental prototype compared to the
baseline electric resistance heating method. This study’s goal is to similarly reduce the energy consumption
of water heaters by improving the COP of the heating process through TE technology. A TE heating module
using ambient air as the heat source and raising the temperature of domestic water as the heat sink was
added to a commercially available ER water heater tank. Water was pumped from the bottom of the tank to
an external loop that fed into the TE heating module and was either sent as part of a hot water draw or
recirculated back into the top of the tank for temperature recovery.

4.2 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND CONTROLS

A commercially available two-element ER water heater (ERWH) unit was modified to add a TE heating
module as the primary form of water heating. The bottom element of the ERWH was removed and replaced
with an indicator light, and a combination pump/flow meter was piped into the bottom drain of the tank.
From there, the pump was connected to a set of TE heating modules piped in series that sat above the tank.
After exiting the TE heating modules, the water was piped to the hot connection port on top of the tank,
where it could then be sent into the demand draw line or allowed to re-enter the top of the tank to promote
tank stratification during tank heating. Figure 3 shows the experimental tank setup in the environmental
chamber and a CAD rendering. The photo in Figure 3 shows that the TE assembly was fabricated outside
the water heater, and the CAD concept rendering shows how the TE assembly would be capable of fitting
inside the water heater product envelope. The TE assembly would fit in the space between the tank and the
product sheet metal housing. That gap is normally filled with foam insulation.

The experimental prototype had two thermoelectric heat pump assemblies plumbed in series, and each
assembly was constructed by sandwiching four thermoelectric modules between an aluminum fin heat sink
and a mini channel heat exchanger, as illustrated in Figure 4. Ambient air was pushed across the aluminum
fin heat sinks by two 4 ¾” fans, and water was pumped up through the mini channel heat exchangers by a
pump at a flow rate of 4 GPH (“high flow mode”) or 2 GPH (“low flow mode”) according to the control
scheme. The water flow rate was chosen to be two discrete values, and the power sent to the TE modules
to heat the water in the HP circuit was variable through a PID-controlled power source.

A control scheme was developed to operate the heating modules according to the demand needs of the UEF
test. Programmed in LabVIEW, the code followed the steps described in Figure 6 and was controlled using
two temperature measurements in the tank: Tlow (TC3 on the tank tree from Figure 5) and THigh (TC12 on
the tank tree from Figure 5). The controls essentially consist of three thermostatic controllers, each with its
own setpoint and deadband, two based on Thigh and one based on Tlow. Collectively, these three thermostatic
controllers decide which of four modes to be in at each moment of control execution (state 1 off, state 2

 8

low TE heat, state 3 high TE heat, or state 4 aux heat).

Beginning from “Start”, the control scheme first checks if THigh is in danger of failing to be able to deliver
usably hot water. If so, the highest-power mode (auxiliary electric resistance heat) is used until Thigh reaches
its setpoint. When THigh is within the auxiliary mode’s deadband and auxiliary mode is not active, high heat
TE mode is active until THigh reaches the setpoint. The low-power TE heating mode is then used until the
bottom of the tank has been raised above a lower setpoint. The lower the state number, the higher the
efficiency and the lower the heating power. If all temperature checks are satisfied, the unit turns off all
heating elements and water pumping.

The tank temperature profile during the UEF performance test is summarized in Figure 7. The
thermoelectric heating modules were powered by a PID-controlled power source to heat the water to a

setpoint temperature; this test heated the incoming tap water from 58°F to a setpoint of 128°F. The
thermocouples used for state control temperatures (THigh and TLow) are bolded in black, and the TE control
state is shown on the bottom of the graph changing between states 0 through 4. In this experiment, the high

Figure 7. The TEHPWH water temperature levels under the 24-hour UEF test cycle. The test consists of
multiple draws of hot water (125° F) during a 24-hour period to simulate hot water use in residential
applications according to the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) test procedure.

The heat pump energy performance of the TEHPWH is summarized in Figure 8. The power level and COP
of the unit changed with the different heating states. The COP during low heating State 2 was slightly above
1.10, while the COP during high heating State 3 was slightly below 1.10 and occurred for a longer duration
leading to an average HP COP = 1.09. The UEF of a water heater is commonly between 5-10% lower than
the COP due to energy and heat loss in the system, and the resulting UEF of 1.00 follows this trend.

4.4 SUMMARY

In this work, the energy consumption of a conventional electric water heater was reduced by modifying it
to use a thermoelectric heat pump (TEHP) as the primary method of water heating. The results demonstrated
an increase in UEF while performing under conditions similar to the CFR test procedure for UEF ratings.

During a 24-hour UEF test, the TEs were able to provide all the tank heating by operating about ¾ of the
time. The TEs ran slightly less than half of their operating time in low-power mode (consuming a combined
180-230 W of electrical power), and the rest in high-power mode (consuming a combined 420 W of power).
In the low-power mode, the two TE banks (with 4 TEs each) had COPs of about 1.15 for one bank, and
1.08 for the other. In high-power mode, their COPs were about 1.10 and 1.07.

The ability of the TEHP to operate above a heating COP of 1.00 aided in the reduction in energy consumed
by the unit over the course of the UEF test. The TEHP system’s energy efficiency can be further improved
by increasing the number of TE modules used, optimizing the temperature setpoints for system state control,
and optimizing the water flowrate of the heat pump circuit.

and it was found the computation time was 2000 times less for similar accuracy. Furthermore, at increasing
flowrate the publication (Cruz-Larado et. al., 2023) had increasing errors such that the lower 2/3rds of the
tank were not described within 10°C. The model in (Rendall, et. al., 2021) has shown similar accuracy at
many flowrates and is significantly faster.

6. COMMERCIALIZATION AND MARKET ADOPTION

This project is designed to develop a new more affordable electric heat pump water heating technology. A
system based on TE heat pump has a number of advantages over vapor compression HPWHs: its small size
relative to air source HPWHs of the same tank volume enables use in smaller spaces, avoiding issues with
expensive installation costs; it also offers a straightforward installation process for plumbers, and is highly
suitable for the distributor wholesale channel (plumbers) and the retail channel (big box stores), maximizing
the reach to retrofit markets.

The path to commercial success is to first demonstrate the performance at a cost that delivers a payback
rapid enough to drive consumer adoption of the TE HPWH such that it becomes the new normal standard
replacement system. As shown in Figure 11, a TE HPWH product could offer consumers and manufacturers
a “good-better-best” set of options. Currently, the electric water heater market is a bifurcated “good/best”
one.

6.1 KNOWN BARRIERS AND KEY PATHWAYS TO MARKET ADOPTION

Although traditional vapor compression HPWHs remain at low market penetration (~3%) (Namara, 2024)
after many years in the market, the proposed concept addresses many of their shortcomings.

The most important barrier to driving HPWH market adoption is first cost. The proposed system has a
projected incremental retail price of $147 greater than ERWH, as shown in Table 5. This is made possible
by utilizing low heating capacity and by the straightforward layout, which adds only two moving parts (a
pump and a fan) to the ERWH. As shown in Figure 11, TE water heaters can fit between two water heating
technologies that already exist on the market, both in cost and in efficiency: ERWHs and HPWHs. If the
cost is at or approaching that of HPWHs, it will be difficult to justify using this technology. Therefore, it is
essential that the costs associated with the BiTe be minimized through working with sources, effective
supplier vetting, and supply chain management.

The system is robust because it has only two moving parts (a fan and a pump), both of which are commodity
items with high reliability. In case of a heat pump failure, the water heater can continue to provide hot water
by relying on the 4.5 kW resistance elements like an ordinary ERWH does.

Many barriers that apply to traditional vapor compression HPWHs are addressed. For example, because the
TE capacity is so small, the air circulation required to operate the system, and the cooling imposed on its
surroundings, will be much smaller (and quieter), making it more suitable for confined space installations
(like closets).

One potential barrier is the need for occasional condensate management. Under some operating conditions
(such as when the surrounding air has high relative humidity), condensate may form on the cold heat
exchanger (HX) fins. Although many codes require the temperature and pressure (T-P) valve to be plumbed
to a drain, in practice in the field, most ERWH tanks are not plumbed to a drain. However, the condensate
issue for the TE HPWH is less pronounced than for vapor compression HPWHs: because the TE capacity
is small and the air flow rate is small, much less condensate is expected. In a dry climate, condensation may
not be an issue at all. In many climates, a simple evaporation pan may suffice. In humid locations,
condensate could be mitigated or prevented by using a high airflow mode, or it may be necessary to plumb
the condensate to a drain during some humid location installations.

An additional barrier to TE HPWH adoption is cleaning and replacement of air filters. Although the air
around the top of the water heaters will be fairly clean and the air flow across the TEs will be small, it
remains to be seen whether air filter replacement is needed. If it is needed, it would be a new requirement
for this drop-in replacement for electric resistance heaters. Possible difficulties obtaining replacement filters
are mitigated by A. O. Smith’s robust distributor and retail channels.

Before conducting the work in this project, a potential barrier was whether a low-capacity TE heat pump
would be able to provide heating for a full UEF draw profile without relying on backup resistance heat.
Conventional vapor compression HPWHs, with a heating capacity of approximately 2 kW, are able to do
this, but before this project, it was an open research question how low of a capacity will still be capable of
meeting the load. The average load of the medium draw profile is 212 W (not counting tank losses).
However, this load is not distributed evenly throughout the 24-h period. In experimental testing in this
project, it was shown that the TE heat pump with approximately 200-500 W of capacity (depending on low-
power or high-power operating mode) was able to provide enough heat to deliver a medium-draw UEF load
profile without relying on electric resistance elements. One advantage of the proposed concept that enabled
this is the single-pass heating: water is heated to 130°F and delivered to the tank top in one pass. This
maintains stratification, which maximizes actual water delivery capacity for a given heating capacity. A
second advantage of the proposed concept is that TEs can ramp up their power well beyond their nominal
rated capacity.

6.3 TARGET MARKET AND IMPACT

The originally proposed target market for the project was the ~5 M unit/year residential storage electric
resistance replacement market. With such a small incremental cost, and if able to provide a simple payback
within 1 year, the TEWH product could have the potential to become the new standard replacement electric
water heater, with massive impact on primary energy consumption and water heating utility costs for
consumers.

This technology is relevant for new and retrofit applications. The potential for a market niche for low-cost
TE HPWHs exists because manufacturers cannot easily lower the cost of a vapor compression HPWH, even
when reducing efficiency by using smaller compressors, condensers, and evaporators. These components
have relatively fixed base costs regardless of their size, so the relationship between price and COP/UEF is
not linear, and vapor compression-based HPWHs will tend toward a larger, more expensive size.

During the course of the project, in July 2023, DOE published a notice of proposed rule (NOPR) regarding
consumer water heaters, followed by a Final Rule (FR) in July 2024, requiring that all residential water
heaters manufactured on or after May 6, 2029 with effective volume above 35 gallons have a uniform

 18

energy factor (UEF) of at least 2.3. Since thermoelectric heat pumps in the water heating application
generally have COP much lower than 2.3, the use of thermoelectric (TE) heat pump water heater (HPWH)
technology will be limited to water heaters with effective volume less than 35 gallons. Relevant product
options could include a smaller tank size for undersink applications, and/or a low-cost "booster" product
option that could be added to an existing water heater.

A note on the new definition of “effective volume:” this applies to systems equipped with a mixing valve,
allowing the temperature of water stored in the tank to be higher than the temperature delivered by the
system. The effective volume is the actual volume multiplied by the ratio of (tank storage temperature
minus 58°F) to (delivery temperature minus 58°F).

Water heating accounts for 3.5 quads of primary energy use annually (BTO Scout). The electric water
heating market is dominated by electric-resistance storage water heaters with UEF = 0.91 to 0.95. Full
adoption of the TE HPWH with UEF of 1.00 in today’s electric water heating sector would result in 140
TBtu/yr of primary energy savings (460 TBtu/yr with a UEF of 1.25). However, starting in 2029 a large
fraction of electric water heaters will be required to have a UEF of 2.3. Thus, in the post-2029 market, the
primary energy potential will be limited to savings that can be achieved in water heaters with less than 35
gallon effective volume. At this time, numbers are not readily available for the quantity of under 35 gallon
water heaters.

7. PLANS FOR FUTURE COLLABORATION

A few ideas of alternative TE HPWH configurations were discussed but ultimately AO Smith did not judge
a market viable path for them at this time. AO Smith has shown interest in further development of the hot
water tank stratification model and potentially revisiting TE concepts for other or emerging product
categories.

Accurate and computationally-efficient tank stratification models are important for optimizing integrated
heat pump water heater design. HPWHs are more efficient and effective when the tank is well stratified,
with the water at the bottom as cold as possible. Two ways to control this is adjusting wrap-around
condenser placement and understanding the impact of dip tubes on tank mixing.

Since experimentally constructing and evaluating tanks with alternative aspect ratios and dip tubes is
expensive, improvements to this tank stratification model will shorten development time by enabling rapid
dip tube selection and reduce condenser cost (and thus product cost) through optimization while still
meeting efficiency standards and meeting customer’s needs. Continuing to develop the hot water tank
stratification model aligns with one of the ultimate goals of the TEHPWH project, which is allowing mass
market adoption of energy-efficient products that improve energy affordability for consumers by allowing
products with UEFs greater than that of electric resistance heaters to be more affordable with shorter
payback periods.

8. CONCLUSIONS

The energy consumption of a conventional electric water heater was experimentally improved by modifying
an electric resistance water heater to use a thermoelectric heat pump (TEHP) as the primary method of
water heating. The results demonstrated an increase in UEF while performing under conditions similar to
the CFR test procedure for UEF ratings. The cost targets were largely shown to be feasible, however the
experimentally measured efficiency feel short of project targets, largely due to excessive resistance to heat
transfer between thermoelectric modules and air.

 19

Improved efficiency can be achieved by a variety of measures that could be explored in future work,
including:

 1. Improved air-to-TE heat exchanger design with reduced approach temperature between air and
 thermoelectric substrate

 2. Advanced TE modules with higher efficiency through some combination of stronger Seebeck
 effect, lower self-conduction, and lower electrical resistance

 3. More efficient fan and pump

 4. Improved control strategy that results in more duration at lower power (higher efficiency) mode

 5. Reduced heat losses from water lines, by routing the lines through the insulated area between the
 tank and sheet metal wall of the water heater.

 6. Improved TE-to-water heat exchanger design with reduced approach temperature between water
 and thermoelectric substrate

 7. Optimization of water flowrates to minimize pump power consumption while delivering water to
 the tank at the desired temperature

 8. Optimization of air flowrates to balance fan power consumption with airside heat transfer, to
 optimize overall system efficiency

The ability of the TEHP to operate above a heating COP of 1.00 led to a reduction in energy consumed by
the unit over the course of the UEF test. The TEHP system’s energy consumption can be further reduced
by optimizing the number of TE modules used, the temperature setpoints for state control, and the water
flowrate of the heat pump circuit.

Also in this work, improvements to the state-of-the-art 1D hot water tank stratification model were made
to reduce the errors associated with time steps not equal to the Courant number. The model computes 2,000
times faster than the most recently published model at similar accuracy for one flowrate. The model has
also proven to be accurate at multiple flowrates (Rendall, et. al., 2021) whereas the most recently published
model (Cruz-Larado et. al., 2023) has significant errors as the flowrate increases. The improvement to the
model will allow for the tank model to be implemented in system level models.

The project was carried out with great success in the first year, but the technology become obsolete due to
the proposed rulemaking by DOE on HPWHs. Thus, the project tasks for the second year were not
completed besides for this CRADA final report.
