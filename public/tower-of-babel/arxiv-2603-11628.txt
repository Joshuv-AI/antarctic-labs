T-systems: a theory of orthonormal functions with a tridiagonal differentiation matrix
Arieh Iserles, Marcus Webb

Abstract
The starting point of this paper is that a spectral method is essentially a combination of an orthonormal basis of the underlying Hilbert space with Galerkin conditions. The choice of an orthonormal basis depends on a number of desirable features which we explore in the context of spectral methods for time-dependent partial differential equations in a single space dimension. A central role in ensuring many of the above features is played by the differentiation matrix of the underlying orthonormal system. In particular, it is beneficial if this matrix is skew-symmetric and tridiagonal. While orthonormal systems with this feature have been characterised in A. Iserles & M. Webb, “Orthogonal systems with a skew-symmetric differentiation matrix”, Found. Comput. Maths 19 (2019), 1191–1221, employing Fourier transforms, in this paper we provide an alternative characterisation using the differential Lanczos algorithm, which can be implemented constructively. It is valid for inner products that obey an ‘integration-by-parts condition’, inclusive of L 2 \mathrm{L}_{2} and Sobolev norms on the real line. Motivated by quest for integration methods that conserve Hamiltonian energy, we conclude the paper replacing inner products by more general sesquilinear forms and presenting preliminary results. Here the Fourier transform characterisation generalises to spectral theory of Schrödinger operators and the differential Lanczos algorithm generalises to the differential Arnoldi algorithm.

Introduction

1.1
Spectral methods for time-dependent PDEs

The original motivation for the present work is the design of numerical methods for time-dependent dispersive equations of the form

{\mathrm{i}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=-\Delta u+G(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},t,u)u,\qquad t\geq 0,\quad\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}\in\mbox{\Bbb R}^{d},

(1.1)

where
u:\mbox{\Bbb R}^{d}\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mbox{\Bbb C}
and we are given a sufficiently regular initial value
u_{0}
at
t=0
. The given function
G:\mbox{\Bbb R}^{d}\times[0,\infty)\times\mbox{\Bbb C}\to\mbox{\Bbb R}
is also assumed to be sufficiently regular. Important examples, all with significant applications in quantum mechanics, include

•

G(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},t,u)=V(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath})
: the linear Schrödinger equation,

•

G(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},t,u)=\lambda|u|^{2}
: the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, and

•

G(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},t,u)=V(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},t)+\lambda|u|^{2}
: the Gross–Pitaevskii equation.

It is well known that the evolution of (
1.1
) is unitary, i.e.
\|u(\,\cdot\,,t)\|_{\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\sBbb R})}\equiv\|u_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\sBbb R})}
, a property of central importance in physical applications, because it corresponds to the fact that
|u(\,\cdot\,,t)|^{2}
is a probability distribution. It is thus, in the spirit of geometric numerical integration
[
3
,
8
]
, natural to respect it under discretisation. An added bonus is that a unitary discretisation is automatically stable in the
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
norm and hence by the Lax Equivalence Theorem
[
18
, p. 360]
, if the numerical method is also consistent, then it is convergent.

The equation (
1.1
), defined for
\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}\in\mbox{\Bbb R}^{d}
, represents a natural setting for both analysis and computation of dispersive equations of quantum mechanics
[
23
]
. It is often presented in different spatial domains, in particular on the torus
\mbox{\Bbb T}^{d}
, but this is often motivated by the quest for simpler analysis and faster numerical solution, both through the agency of Fourier series and the Fast Fourier Transform, rather than by genuine physical considerations.

The ‘free space’ setting,
\mbox{\Bbb R}^{d}
, presents considerable challenges for numerical discretisations. Naive use of finite differences or finite elements leads to a countable number of degrees of freedom. Restricting the domain to a sufficiently large box (with either zero Dirichlet or periodic boundary conditions), but how large is ‘large enough’?

.
This, in particular, is highly relevant to dispersive equations: in general we can say very little about the support of the solution as time evolves.

An important exception to the principle of “free space setting results in an infinite number of degrees of freedom” is presented by
spectral methods.
While more familiar for pure boundary-value problems, spectral methods can be used also for time-dependent PDEs of the form

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}[u]+f(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},t,u),\qquad(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},t)\in\Omega\times\mbox{\Bbb R}_{+},

(1.2)

where
\Omega\subseteq\mbox{\Bbb R}^{d}
,
f
is a sufficiently regular function and
\mathcal{L}
is a possibly nonlinear differential operator defined on a function space with appropriate boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega

[
9
]
. Let
\Phi=\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mbox{\sBbb Z}_{+}}
be a sufficiently smooth orthonormal basis of
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\Omega)
,

\langle\varphi_{m},\varphi_{n}\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{m}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath})\varphi_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath})\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}=\delta_{m,n},\qquad m,n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+},

and suppose that each
\varphi_{n}
shares the same boundary conditions as (
1.2
). A spectral method approximates the solution as a truncated expansion in the basis
\Phi
,

u(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},t)\approx u_{N}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\hat{u}_{n}(t)\varphi_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}).

(1.3)

To determine the coefficients
\hat{u}_{n}
we may use Galerkin conditions which constrain the residual of the numerical solution to be orthogonal to the approximation space spanned by
\varphi_{0},\ldots,\varphi_{N}
. By orthonormality of
\Phi
, this is equivalent to

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\hat{u}_{m}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}=\left\langle\mathcal{L}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\hat{u}_{n}\varphi_{n}+f,\varphi_{m}\right\rangle\!,\qquad m=0,\ldots,N.

(1.4)

Note that, if
\mathcal{L}
is linear, (
1.4
) simplifies to

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\hat{u}_{m}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\hat{u}_{n}\langle\mathcal{L}\varphi_{n},\varphi_{m}\rangle+\langle f,\varphi_{m}\rangle=0,\qquad m=0,\ldots,N,

which, for
f=f(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},t)
, is a linear ODE system. The initial conditions for (
1.4
) can be obtained by expanding the original initial condition in the basis
\Phi
,

\hat{u}_{n}=\int_{\Omega}u(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},0)\overline{\varphi_{n}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath})}\,\mathrm{d}\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath},\qquad n=0,\ldots,N.

An important insight is that the boundedness (or otherwise) of
\Omega
plays no role whatsoever in the definition of a spectral method. This makes spectral methods particularly suitable for problems of the type (
1.1
), in particular once the initial condition
u_{0}
is very smooth – ideally, analytic. It does not eliminate the difficulties entirely — it merely delegates them to the computation of the associated integrals.

There are five properties on which a time-stepping method for PDEs is typically judged:

Stability:

The numerical evolution operator must be well posed. This is not an optional extra because, unless a method is stable, its convergence is not assured.

Unitarity:

if the exact solution is unitary, i.e. conserves the
\mathrm{L}_{2}
norm, ideally we want our method to inherit this feature. Note that unitarity implies stability.

Conserved quantities:

Many time-dependent PDEs conserve quantities such as a Hamiltonian energy or momentum as
t
evolves. Ideally, a numerical solution should either respect this feature or at least do so in some approximate sense in long time intervals
[
8
]
. Unitarity is a special case of this property.

Fast convergence:

The cost of time-stepping Galerkin equations (
1.4
) typically scales as a power of
N
. Therefore, a critical advantage rests with orthogonal systems yielding approximations that converge rapidly, hence require small
N
. This is the well known trade-off of computational PDE theory: finite differences and finite elements result in very large algebraic (or ODE) systems which are sparse, while spectral methods often yield small, yet dense, systems. There can be exceptions to this (see
[
29
]
and
[
28
]
).

Rapid time stepping:

Equations (
1.4
) need be solved by a time-stepping ODE solver, perhaps using a splitting method
[
26
]
. While much depends on the nature of the original PDE and on the ODE solver, a critical ingredient is fast computation of an expansion in the basis
\Phi
(and the inverse operation of evaluating an expansion on a grid of points). This is the secret for the attraction of periodic boundary conditions, that allow for the use of a fast Fourier transform, to determine the first
N
coefficients in
{\cal O}\!\left(N\log_{2}N\right)
operations.

Unless we are fortunate enough to have periodic boundary conditions, stability of a time-stepping spectral method is the first stumbling block. Once applied to boundary-value problems in domains with, say, Dirichlet boundary conditions, a common choice of a basis is orthogonal polynomials: spectral methods using orthogonal polynomials are most familiar on intervals and their tensor products, and recently there have been efforts to extend this to simplexes
[
6
,
28
]
. However, at least in a naïve implementation, orthogonal polynomials do not generally yield stable and well-conditioned spectral methods, hence fail already at the first (and most important) hurdle.

From now on we assume
d=1
, in other words that there is just one space variable. It is straightforward to generalise the material of this paper by tensorisation to
\mbox{\Bbb R}^{d}
,
\mbox{\Bbb T}^{d}
and parallelepipeds for all
d\in\mbox{\Bbb N}
. It may also be possible to generalise to
d\geq 2
using sparse grids, but this is more technically involved and deferred to future work.

1.2
Differentiation matrices

Understanding why orthogonal polynomials can lead to unstable spectral methods for time-dependent PDEs will help us to develop and describe an alternative.

Let
\Phi=\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mbox{\sBbb Z}_{+}}\subset\mathrm{C}^{1}(a,b)
be an orthonormal and complete basis of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, rich enough so that
\mathrm{L}_{2}(a,b)\subseteq\mathcal{H}
. Since
\varphi_{m}^{\prime}\in C(a,b)\subset\mathcal{H}
, it follows that

\varphi_{m}^{\prime}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mbox{\curly D}_{n,m}\varphi_{n},\qquad m\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+},

for some coefficients
\mbox{\curly D}_{n,m}\in\mbox{\Bbb C}
. The infinite-dimensional
differentiation matrix

D
is a fundamental concept in the application of orthonormal bases to spectral methods
[
9
,
33
]
and, roughly speaking, it plays the role of the derivative operator in a setting whereby a function
f:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mbox{\Bbb C}
is represented by its expansion coefficients in the basis
\Phi
: if
f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{f}_{n}\varphi_{n}
then
f^{\prime}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{f}_{n}\varphi_{n}^{\prime}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\mbox{\curly D}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$f$\unboldmath}})_{n}\varphi_{n}
.

Subject to the right boundary conditions (see Subsection 2.1), the differential operator is skew-self-adjoint. Given that the differentiation matrix replaces the differential operator in the discretised world, we might also wish it to be skew-self-adjoint, i.e.
\mbox{\curly D}^{*}=-\mbox{\curly D}
. This is an immensely fruitful assumption, which we illustrate by a number of examples.

The simplest possible example is the
advection equation

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x},\qquad t\geq 0,\quad x\in\mbox{\Bbb R},

whose solution is a unilateral shift,
u(x,t)=u(x+t,0)
. Its spectral approximation is
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}=\mbox{\curly D}\,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}
(we might truncate the equations but this makes no difference to our argument), whose solution is
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{t\mbox{\smallcurly D}}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}(0)
. Since
D
is skew-self-adjoint,
\mathrm{e}^{t\mbox{\smallcurly D}}
is unitary and (in the
\ell_{2}
norm)
\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}(t)\|=\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}_{0}\|
. Hence we have stability and, even better, unitarity.

Our next example is the diffusion equation

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left[a(x)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right]\!,\qquad t\geq 0,\quad x\in\mbox{\Bbb R},

where
a(x)>0
for all
x
. Our spectral method is
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}=\mbox{\curly D}\mathcal{A}\mbox{\curly D}\,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix (since
\Phi
is an orthonormal basis) that depends on
a(x)
and the choice of
\Phi
.
D
being skew-self-adjoint implies that
\mbox{\curly D}\mathcal{A}\mbox{\curly D}
is symmetric and negative semi-definite, so that
\,\mathrm{d}\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}(t)\|/\,\mathrm{d}t\leq 0
and the
\ell_{2}
norm of the solution is a decreasing function, just as the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
norm of the exact solution.

Our final example is the dispersive equation (
1.1
). After discretisation,

{\mathrm{i}}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}=-\mbox{\curly D}^{2}\,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}+\mathcal{G}(t,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}})\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}},

where
\mathcal{G}
is a self-adjoint matrix whose exact form depends on
G
and
\Phi
. Therefore

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}\|^{2}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}={\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}}^{*}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}+{({\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}^{\prime})}^{*}}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}={\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}}^{*}({\mathrm{i}}\mbox{\curly D}^{2}-{\mathrm{i}}{\mbox{\curly D}^{*}}^{2})\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}-{\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}}^{*}[{\mathrm{i}}\mathcal{G}(t,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}})-{\mathrm{i}}\mathcal{G}(t,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}})^{*}]\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}=0.

(1.5)

We deduce that, like (
1.1
), the solution of (
1.5
) is unitary. What is perhaps even more remarkable is that the proof of unitarity (and stability) is
exactly
the same as the proof of unitarity (and well posedness) of the original PDE, just replacing the differential operator by a differentiation matrix and
\mathrm{L}_{2}
by
\ell_{2}
norm.

Once our differentiation matrix is skew-symmetric, we automatically gain both stability and unitarity for a wide range of initial-value problems. Of course, the other three desiderata need not be automatically satisfied. We address Hamiltonian invariants in Section 4. Insofar as the speed of convergence is concerned, relatively little is known: in general, the speed of convergence in
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
by orthogonal sequences (as opposed to a compact interval) is a largely open issue. Partial results, of relevance to dispersive equations, are reported in
[
12
]
but in general there is no comprehensive theory. Rapid time stepping has a number of aspects. Firstly, it is convenient to work with tridiagonal differentiation matrices: in Sections 2–3 we show that orthogonal bases with such matrices can be characterised completely in three situations: Cauchy, periodic and zero Dirichlet boundary conditions, and this material forms the bulk of this paper. Secondly, it is possible to characterise
all
orthogonal bases so that
D
is skew symmetric, tridiagonal and the expansion can be computed by either fast Fourier or fast cosine or fast sine transform. There are exactly five such bases, which have been described in
[
15
]
. Other strategies for fast time stepping include splitting methods
[
1
,
26
,
2
]
and special methods tailored for high-dimensional Schrödinger-type equations like Hagedorn functions, Gaussian beams and the Gaussian wave-packet transform
[
23
]
.

In this paper we describe a toolbox for stable spectral methods in a number of settings. Perhaps the most important is the real line, but our theory has been extended also to bounded intervals with periodic boundary conditions. Although in principle we can also use it in a compact interval or in
[a,\infty)
for some
a\in\mbox{\Bbb R}
, our construction appears to deliver orthonormal bases with poor approximation properties. In that case one may consider an alternative approach, described in
[
19
]
, whereby the differentiation matrix
D
is no longer tridiagonal (or banded) but it has a different helpful structure, allowing to form the first
N
terms of
\mbox{\curly D}\,\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}
in
{\cal O}\!\left(N\right)
operations.

In Section 2 we sketch the theory of orthonormal systems with tridiagonal, skew-Hermitian differentiation matrices using the Fourier transform. This theory has been already described in
[
13
]
for
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
and in
[
17
]
for Sobolev spaces on the real line with Cauchy boundary conditions, here we extend the setting to other types of boundary conditions. In Section 3 we introduce a new, alternative approach towards the construction of such systems, the
differential Lanczos algorithm.
We accompany our construction with a number of detailed examples. Finally, in Section 4 we replace inner products by sesquilinear forms. This is motivated by our quest to explore preservation of Hamiltonian energy by spectral methods for the linear Schrödinger equation. We prove that, in general, it is impossible to conserve both unitarity and Hamiltonian energy in this setting. Moreover, preservation of Hamiltonian energy leads to upper-Hessenberg differentiation matrices which are neither tridiagonal nor skew symmetric. However, at least in the examples we have explored, such a differentiation matrix is
almost
tridiagonal and skew symmetric, a phenomenon whose importance and detailed analysis are matter for future research.

2
On differentiation matrices

2.1
Skew-Hermitian differentiation matrices

The connection between skew-self-adjointness of an operator, and skew-Hermitian-ness of its matrix with respect to a basis for the underlying space depends intimately on the inner product (more generally a duality pairing, but we will not discuss such further abstractions). The setting for our theory is therefore chosen to be a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, upon which the differential operator can be defined at least densely on
D(\mbox{\curly D})\subset\mathcal{H}
. The following property is crucial.

Definition 1

We say that the Hilbert space
\cal{H}
satisfies the Integration-by-Parts (IbP) property if

\mathrm{Re}\,(\langle f^{\prime},g\rangle+\langle f,g^{\prime}\rangle)=0,\qquad f,g\in\mathcal{H}.

(2.1)

In the special case when
\cal H
is a subspace of
\mathrm{L}_{2}(a,b)
with the standard inner product (where
a=-\infty
or
b=\infty
are possible), integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\langle f^{\prime},g\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f^{\prime}(x)\overline{g(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[f\,\overline{g}\right]_{a}^{b}-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\overline{g^{\prime}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(b)\overline{g(b)}-f(a)\overline{g(a)}-\langle f,g^{\prime}\rangle.

Therefore, with this inner product, the IbP property is equivalent to

\mathrm{Re}\,[f(b)\overline{g(b)}-f(a)\overline{g(a)}]=0,

(2.2)

for all
f,g\in\cal H
.

The condition (
2.2
) and its generalisation to a Sobolev setting are consistent with three scenarios:

1.

a=-\infty
,
b=\infty
and
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
implies Cauchy boundary conditions. This is the setting of
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
17
]
which we will revisit briefly in Subsection 2.2;

2.

(a,b)\subset\mbox{\Bbb R}
with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions;

3.

Periodic boundary conditions in a bounded interval
(a,b)
. As a matter of fact, we may allow a mildly more general setting of
twisted periodicity,
whereby there exists
\kappa\in(-\pi,\pi]
such that
f(a)=\mathrm{e}^{{\mathrm{i}}\kappa}f(b)
for every
f\in{\cal H}
. This has applications to Bloch waves in solid state physics
[
22
]
and in the theory of sound waves
[
24
,
31
]
.

Yet, IbP is true in a considerably more general Hilbert spaces. Thus, let
v_{k}\geq 0
,
k\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}
, not all zero. Following
[
17
]
, we let

\langle f,g\rangle_{v}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}v_{k}\int_{a}^{b}f^{(k)}(x)\overline{g^{(k)}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(2.3)

whereby the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{v}
comprises all functions
f
such that
\langle f,f\rangle_{v}<\infty
.

It is helpful to define
v(\xi)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}v_{k}\xi^{2k}
. The proof of the IbP property is a straightforward generalisation of the special case
v(\xi)\equiv 1
, which corresponds to the
\mathrm{L}_{2}(a,b)
inner product. In particular,
v(\xi)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}\xi^{2k}
results in orthogonality in the
Sobolev space

\mathrm{H}^{p}(a,b)
.

2.2
T-systems

Let
\tilde{\Phi}=\{\tilde{\varphi}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathrm{L}_{2}(a,b)
(where
a=-\infty
or
b=\infty
are possible) and assume that
\tilde{\varphi}_{n}\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}(a,b)
. We say that it is a
T-system
if the differentiation matrix
\tilde{\mbox{\curly D}}
is both skew-Hermitian and tridiagonal
2
2

2

T stands for tridiagonal.
. In other words,

\tilde{\varphi}_{n}^{\prime}=-\overline{b}_{n-1}\tilde{\varphi}_{n-1}+{\mathrm{i}}c_{n}\tilde{\varphi}_{n}+b_{n}\tilde{\varphi}_{n+1},\qquad n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+},

(2.4)

where
c_{n}\in\mbox{\Bbb R}
and
b_{-1}=0
. We assume that
b_{n}\neq 0
,
n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}
, so that
\widetilde{\mbox{\curly D}}
is irreducible.

The main reason why tridiagonality is important is that it leads to affordable linear algebra. Note that the matrix
\widetilde{\mbox{\curly D}}
, acting on expansion coefficients of
f\in\mathcal{H}
, plays the same role as the differential operator applied to
f
, hence we ‘replace’ differentiation by a multiplication with (in practice, truncated) tridiagonal matrix.

Suppose that
b_{n}=|b_{n}|\mathrm{e}^{{\mathrm{i}}\theta_{n}}
,
n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}
, and set

\varphi_{n}(x)=\exp\!\left({\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\theta_{l}\right)\!\tilde{\varphi}_{n}(x),\qquad n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}.

Substituting into (
2.4

\varphi_{n}^{\prime}=-|b_{n-1}|\varphi_{n-1}+{\mathrm{i}}c_{n}\varphi_{n}+|b_{n}|\varphi_{n+1},\qquad n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}.

(2.5)

Therefore, we can assume without loss of generality that
b_{n}>0
,
n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}
(hence replacing
|b_{m}|
with
b_{m}
in the above expression, as we do in the sequel). In other words, the main diagonal of
D
is pure imaginary, while its principal sub- and super-diagonals are strictly negative and strictly positive, respectively.

We completely characterise in the sequel all the T-systems corresponding to Cauchy and periodic boundary conditions, while arguing that in the presence of zero Dirichlet boundary conditions (whether in a finite or semi-infinite interval) the approach of T-systems is unlikely to work. In the latter case we may adopt an alternative approach, introduced in

Commencing from (

\displaystyle\alpha_{n+1,\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{n-1}^{2}\alpha_{n-1,0}-{\mathrm{i}}c_{n}\alpha_{n,0},

\displaystyle\alpha_{n+1,\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,\ell-1}+b_{n-1}^{2}\alpha_{n-1,\ell}-{\mathrm{i}}c_{n}\alpha_{n,\ell},\qquad\ell=1,\ldots,n-1,

\displaystyle\alpha_{n+1,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,n-1}-{\mathrm{i}}c_{n}\alpha_{n,n},

\displaystyle\alpha_{n+1,n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,n}=1.

This approach has not been pursued further in

Definition 2

Let
g\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}(a,b)
. The linear space

{\mbox{\curly K}}_{n}(g)=\mathrm{Span}\,\{g,g^{\prime},\ldots,g^{(n-1)}\}

The name is modelled upon the familiar Krylov subspaces
\mathrm{Span}\,\{\mbox{\boldmath$v$\unboldmath},A\mbox{\boldmath$v$\unboldmath},\ldots,A^{n-1}\mbox{\boldmath$v$\unboldmath}\}

[

\alpha\in\mbox{\Bbb C}

\dim\mbox{\curly K}_{n}(g)=n

[

\displaystyle b_{0}^{-1}\langle\varphi_{0}^{\prime}-{\mathrm{i}}c_{0}\varphi_{0},\varphi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle b_{0}^{-1}\left(\langle\varphi_{0}^{\prime},\varphi_{0}\rangle-{\mathrm{i}}c_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

by the definition of
c_{0}
.

Now we assume that properties
(i)
and
(ii)
hold for a given
n
, and wish to show it holds when
\varphi_{n+1}
is generated by the Algorithm
1
. Just as in the base case,
\psi_{n+1}
is not identically zero, because
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}_{n+1}(\varphi_{0})\setminus\mathcal{K}_{n}(\varphi_{0})
by Definition
3
, and
b_{n-1}\varphi_{n-1}-{\mathrm{i}}c_{n}\varphi_{n}\in\mathcal{K}_{n}(\varphi_{0})
by the inductive hypothesis. Therefore,
b_{n}
is non-zero and
\varphi_{n+1}
is well-defined and normalized. As in the base case, property
(ii)
is satisfied by combining lines 4, 5 and 6 of the algorithm.

To complete the proof, we need to show that
\psi_{n+1}
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{K}_{n}(\varphi_{0})
, for then
\{\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n+1}\}
forms an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{K}_{n+1}(\varphi_{0})
by combining this fact with the inductive hypothesis. Now, note that both parts of the inductive hypothesis imply
\langle\varphi_{j}^{\prime},\varphi_{n}\rangle=0
for
j=0,1,\ldots,n-2
and
\langle\varphi_{n-1}^{\prime},\varphi_{n}\rangle=b_{n-1}
. Also, the IbP property implies that
\langle f^{\prime},g\rangle=-\langle f,g^{\prime}\rangle
for all
f,g\in\mathcal{H}(\varphi_{0})
. Using these facts, we show the following.

\langle\psi_{n+1},\varphi_{n}\rangle=\langle\varphi_{n}^{\prime}+b_{n-1}\varphi_{n-1}-{\mathrm{i}}c_{n}\varphi_{n},\varphi_{n}\rangle={\mathrm{i}}c_{n}-0-{\mathrm{i}}c_{n}=0.

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{n+1},\varphi_{n-1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{n}^{\prime},\varphi_{n-1}\rangle+b_{n-1}\langle\varphi_{n-1},\varphi_{n-1}\rangle-{\mathrm{i}}c_{n}\langle\varphi_{n},\varphi_{n-1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\langle\varphi_{n},\varphi_{n-1}^{\prime}\rangle+b_{n-1}-0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-b_{n-1}+b_{n-1}=0.

For all
j=0,1,\ldots,n-2
,

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{n+1},\varphi_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{n}^{\prime},\varphi_{j}\rangle+b_{n-1}\langle\varphi_{n-1},\varphi_{j}\rangle-{\mathrm{i}}c_{n}\langle\varphi_{n},\varphi_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\langle\varphi_{n},\varphi_{j}^{\prime}\rangle+0-0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

This completes the proof.

\Box

Note that this whole procedure was perfectly abstract. At no point did we require that the Hilbert space is a subspace of
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
, and indeed we will show some examples where this setting is not used. Furthermore, it appears possible to use a degenerate, symmetric bilinear (or Hermitian sesquilinear) product instead of an inner product, as long as
b_{n}\neq 0
for all
n
. Indeed, non-degeneracy was only used to prove that
b_{n}\neq 0
. However, the IbP property was certainly necessary, which is a limitation. We show in Section 4 that the approach can be generalised for degenerate, Hermitian sesquilinear forms that do not satisfy the IbP property.

3.2
Examples

We present in this section six different examples in which T-functions are derived using the differential Lanczos algorithm. In five of these examples the triangle in Figure

3.2.2
Example 2: Malmquist–Takenaka functions

We set

\varphi_{0}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{1-2{\mathrm{i}}x},\qquad x\in\mbox{\Bbb R}.

Our contention is that underlying T-system is

Assuming that

Now let us evaluate

3.2.6
Example 6: Schrödinger evolutions

The solution of the
linear Schrödinger equation in semiclassical coordinates,

{\mathrm{i}}\varepsilon\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=-\varepsilon^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}+V(x)u,\qquad x\in\mbox{\Bbb R},\quad t\geq 0,

(3.7)

where
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
is a small parameter (the square root of the ratio between the mass of an electron and that of a nucleus or several nuclei) and

the interaction potential, can be composed, using splitting methods, from the solutions of two ‘split’ equations,

{\mathrm{i}}\varepsilon\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=-\varepsilon^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}},\qquad x\in\mbox{\Bbb R},\quad t\geq 0,

(3.8)

the

, and the linear ODE

{\mathrm{i}}\varepsilon\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=-V(x)u,\qquad x\in\mbox{\Bbb R},\quad t\geq 0,

whose solution is
u(x,t)=\exp\left({\mathrm{i}}t\varepsilon^{-1}V(x)\right)u(x,0)
. The solution of the equation (
3.8
) represents more of a challenge, because naive numerical methods on the real line (e.g. finite differences or finite elements) require either an infinite number of degrees of freedom or an arbitrary truncation of the infinite space interval. However, as it is easy to verify directly,
u
is an inverse Fourier transform of ‘twisted’ Fourier transform of the initial value,

u(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{u}(\xi,0)\mathrm{e}^{{\mathrm{i}}\xi^{2}\varepsilon t+{\mathrm{i}}x\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi,\qquad x\in\mbox{\Bbb R},\quad t\geq 0.

(3.9)

Spectral methods have a critical advantage once, as in our case, the spatial domain is infinite: in principle, all we need is to expand

u(x,0)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\varphi_{n}(x)

(3.10)

in an orthonormal system
\Phi
(in practice, of course, we truncate the expansion) and use one of many spectral methods
[
9
]
to evolve the
a_{n}
s so that
u(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tilde{a}_{n}(t)\varphi_{n}(x)
(of course,
\tilde{a}_{n}(0)=a_{n}(0)
). An alternative approach is described in
[
10
]
: we expand the initial condition like in (
3.9
) but
instead of evolving the
a_{n}
s we evolve the
\varphi_{n}
s!
In other words,

u(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\chi_{n}(x,t)
, where, using (
3.9

\chi_{n}(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\varphi}_{n}(\xi)\mathrm{e}^{{\mathrm{i}}\xi^{2}\varepsilon t+{\mathrm{i}}x\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi,\qquad x\in\mbox{\Bbb R},\quad t\geq 0.

The underlying inner product being
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
, we are within the setting of Subsection 2.3. Thus, assuming that
\Phi
is a T-function system,

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{i}}^{n}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{n}(\xi)\sqrt{w(\xi)}\mathrm{e}^{{\mathrm{i}}x\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi,\qquad n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+},

\displaystyle\chi_{n}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{i}}^{n}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{n}(\xi)\sqrt{\mathrm{e}^{{\mathrm{i}}\xi^{2}\varepsilon t}w(\xi)}\mathrm{e}^{{\mathrm{i}}x\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

where

is the

that its differentiation matrix is exactly the same as that of

. Moreover, the explicit form of the

\displaystyle\chi_{n}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{i}}^{n}}{\sqrt{2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi}(1-2{\mathrm{i}}\varepsilon t}}\left(\frac{2{\mathrm{i}}\varepsilon t+1}{2{\mathrm{i}}\varepsilon t-1}\right)^{\!n/2}\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{x^{2}}{2{\mathrm{i}}\varepsilon t-1}\right)\!\mathrm{H}_{n}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+4\varepsilon t^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{(1+2{\mathrm{i}}\varepsilon t)^{n}}{(1-2{\mathrm{i}}\varepsilon t)^{n+1}}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{{\mathrm{i}}t\varepsilon x^{2}}{1+4\varepsilon^{2}t^{2}}\right)\!\varphi_{n}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+4\varepsilon^{2}t^{2}}}\right)\!,\qquad n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}.\hskip 30.0pt

The triangle in Figure

applies in the present case. While (

The differential Lanczos algorithm is identical to Example 1 (and yields the same sequence

4
H-systems: systems with Hessenberg differentiation matrices

4.1
Sesquilinear forms

Let
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
be a symmetric bilinear (or Hermitian sesquilinear) form over some real (or complex) Banach space. To be clear, our convention will be linearity in the first argument and conjugate linearity in the second argument. As we have already commented towards the end of Subsection 3.1, if the form satisfies the IbP property then the differential Lanczos algorithm remains valid, although unless the sesquilinear form is positive definite there is no guarantee that the
b_{n}
s are always positive. It is possible to obtain
b_{n}=0
, whereby the process terminates. Let us examine some examples that do not sastisfy the IbP property.

As an example, let us consider again the linear Schrödinger equation (
3.7
) given on the real line, except that for simplicity we set
\varepsilon=1
(the so-called “atomistic coordinates”). The equation has the Hamiltonian

H[u](x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\left|\frac{\partial u(x,t)}{\partial x}\right|^{2}+V(x)|u(x,t)|^{2}\right]\!\,\mathrm{d}x,

which stays constant for
t\geq 0
, i.e. is determined solely by the initial condition. In other words,
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}u(\,\cdot\,,t),u(\,\cdot\,,t)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}=\mbox{const}
, where

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}v,w\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[V(x)v(x)\overline{w(x)}+v^{\prime}(x)\overline{w^{\prime}(x)}]\,\mathrm{d}x.

(4.1)

Note that (
4.1
) is positive definite if
V(x)\geq 0
,
x\in\mbox{\Bbb R}
, but this is not always true: an important example is the Lennard-Jones potential
V(x)=(\sigma/x)^{12}-(\sigma/x)^{6}
for some
\sigma>0
, modelling the interaction of electronically neutral atoms or molecules, where
\sigma
is the distance of the interacting particles.

The solution of (
3.7
) conserves both the energy of the system (i.e. the
\mathrm{L}_{2}
norm of the solution) and Hamiltonian energy. In an ideal situation, we would wish to design a system
\Phi
which is orthonormal with respect to both sesquilinear forms, so that both energies are preserved. As we have already shown, if
\Phi
is orthonormal with respect to the
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
inner product, then the
\mathrm{L}_{2}
norm is conserved by a Galerkin scheme, but what if it is orthonormal with respect to the
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\cdot,\cdot\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}
sesquilinear form?

We write the solution as

u_{N}(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_{n}(t)\varphi_{n}(x).

For a Galerkin scheme the right-hand-side is discretised by the matrix

\displaystyle L_{j,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}-\varphi_{j}^{\prime\prime}+V\cdot\varphi_{j},\varphi_{k},\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(-\varphi_{j}^{\prime\prime\prime}+V^{\prime}\varphi_{j}+V\varphi_{j}^{\prime}\right)\overline{\varphi_{k}^{\prime}}+V\left(-\varphi_{j}^{\prime\prime}+V\varphi_{j}\right)\overline{\varphi_{k}}\,\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{j}^{\prime\prime}\overline{\varphi_{k}^{\prime\prime}}+2V\varphi_{j}^{\prime}\overline{\varphi_{k}^{\prime}}+V^{\prime}\left(\varphi_{j}\overline{\varphi_{k}^{\prime}}+\varphi_{j}^{\prime}\overline{\varphi_{k}}\right)+V^{2}\varphi_{j}\overline{\varphi_{k}}\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

Evidently, this matrix is Hermitian, so the solution to the semidiscretisation, namely

a_{n}(t)=\exp({\mathrm{i}}tL)a_{n}(0),

evolves unitarily i.e. its vector 2-norm is preserved. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}u_{N}(\cdot,t),u_{N}(\cdot,t)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}|a_{n}(t)|^{2}\qquad\text{(since $\Phi$ is orthonormal w.r.t. $\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\cdot,\cdot\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}$)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}|a_{n}(0)|^{2}\qquad\text{ since the evolution is unitary on the coefficients}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}u_{N}(\cdot,0),u_{N}(\cdot,0)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}.

Hence, the Hamiltonian of the solution to the semi-discretised equation is preserved.

4.2
Hamiltonian sesquilinear forms

4.2.1
Satisfaction of simultaneous invariants

Recall that the linear Schrödinger equation, given as a Cauchy problem, has two important invariants, the
\mathrm{L}_{2}
energy and the Hamiltonian energy (
4.1
). The following demonstates a fundamental limitation on the associated spectral bases.

Theorem 8

Let
\Phi
be a complete orthonormal basis for
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
. Then
\Phi
cannot be orthonormal with respect to
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\cdot,\cdot\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}
for any function
V:\mbox{\Bbb R}\to\mbox{\Bbb R}
that is continuous on some interval
[a,b]
.

Proof
Let us assume that
\Phi
is orthonormal with respect to
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\cdot,\cdot\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}
. Then
\langle\varphi_{n},\varphi_{m}\rangle=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{n},\varphi_{m}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}
for all
m,n
, so
\langle f,g\rangle=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}f,g\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}
for all
f,g\in\mathrm{span}(\Phi)
. This implies that
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\cdot,\cdot\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}
is an inner product on
\mathrm{span}(\Phi)
(because so is
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
) and
\Phi
is complete in
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
in both inner products. This implies that
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\cdot,\cdot\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}
extends continuously to all of
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
and
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}f,g\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}=\langle f,g\rangle
for all
f,g\in\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
. Let
f
be a smooth function supported in a closed subinterval of
(a,b)
. There are infinitely many such functions
f
. Then for any compactly supported smooth function
g
,

-\langle f^{\prime\prime},g\rangle=\langle f^{\prime},g^{\prime}\rangle=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}f,g\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}-\langle Vf,g\rangle=\langle(1-V)f,g\rangle.

(4.2)

Therefore,
f^{\prime\prime}(x)=(V(x)-1)f(x)
for all
x\in[a,b]
and
f(a)=f^{\prime}(a)=0
. Since
V
is continuous in
[a,b]
, there is only one such
f
by Picard–Lindelöf theory, which is a contradiction.

\Box

It is well known in
geometric numerical integration
that simultaneous conservation of distinct invariants is often impossible except by the exact solution
[
34
,
8
]
. Theorem
8
fits into this pattern, as it implies that it is impossible for a Galerkin spectral method to unconditionally preserve both the
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
norm and the Hamiltonian.

For the sake of greater generality now consider the simultaneous satisfaction of both
H(u)=(u,u)_{V}
and of the Sobolev-like norm
\|u\|_{v}=\sqrt{\langle u,u\rangle_{v}}
, where

\langle f,g\rangle_{v}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}v_{k}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f^{(k)}(x)\overline{g^{(k)}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x,

with
v_{k}\geq 0
,
k\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}
, and
v(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}v_{k}x^{2k}>0
,
x\in\mbox{\Bbb R}
. (Of course, we assume that
v\not\equiv 1
.) Note that
v(x)\equiv 1
corresponds to the
\mathrm{L}_{2}
inner product,
v(x)=1+x^{2}
to
\mathrm{H}^{1}
etc. and recall the material of Subsection 2.3 on T-systems orthogonal with respect to
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{v}
.

Theorem 9

Suppose that
\Phi
is a complete orthonormal basis for
\mathrm{H}_{v}^{1}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
with respect to the inner product
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{v}
and orthogonal with respect to the inner product
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\cdot\,,\,\cdot\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}
,

\langle\varphi_{m},\varphi_{n}\rangle_{v}=\delta_{m,n},\quad\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{m},\varphi_{n}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}=\lambda_{m}\delta_{m,n},\qquad m,n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}.

(4.3)

Then each
\lambda_{m}
is an eigenvalue and
\varphi_{m}
a corresponding eigenfunction of

-y^{\prime\prime}+Vy=\lambda\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}v_{k}y^{(2k)},\qquad x\in\mbox{\Bbb R},

(4.4)

with Cauchy boundary conditions.

Proof
Let
m,n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}
. Repeatedly integrating by parts,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{m},\varphi_{n}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[V(x)\varphi_{m}(x)-\varphi_{m}^{\prime\prime}(x)]\overline{\varphi_{n}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\langle\varphi_{m},\varphi_{n}\rangle_{v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}v_{k}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{m}^{(2k)}(x)\overline{\varphi_{n}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x.

It now follows from (
4.3
) that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[-\varphi_{m}^{\prime\prime}(x)+V(x)\varphi_{m}(x)-\lambda_{m}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}v_{k}\varphi_{m}^{(2k)}(x)\right]\overline{\varphi_{n}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

The theorem follows because
\Phi
is dense in
\mathrm{H}_{v}^{1}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
.

\Box

Note that, if the Sobolev-like inner product is just the familiar
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
inner product, (
4.4
) reduces to the Sturm–Liouville eigenvalue problem

-y^{\prime\prime}+Vy=\lambda y,\qquad x\in\mbox{\Bbb R}.

(4.5)

Being defined on the entire real line, the Sturm–Liouville problem (
4.5
) has a point spectrum if
V
is real summable in every compact subinterval of
ℝ

[
25
]
. However, this point spectrum is not necessarily complete in
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
. A sufficient condition for completeness is that
V
be locally integrable and
V(x)\to+\infty
as
|x|\to\infty

[
32
, Thm XI11.67]
.

4.2.2
Example: Harmonic potential

The example of the
harmonic potential

V(x)=x^{2}
is indicative of a more general state of affairs which has been the subject of Theorem
9
. Our point of departure are Hermite functions (
3.3
) and we wish to compute
\rho_{m,n}=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{m},\varphi_{n}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{x^{2}}
. Since Hermite functions obey (
2.5
) with
b_{n}=\sqrt{(n+1)/2}
and
c_{n}\equiv 0
, we have, by virtue of orthonormality,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{m}^{\prime}(x)\varphi_{n}^{\prime}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{mn}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{m-1}(x)\varphi_{n-1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\frac{\sqrt{m(n+1)}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{m-1}(x)\varphi_{n+1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt\mbox{}-\frac{\sqrt{(m+1)n}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{m+1}(x)\varphi_{n-1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt\mbox{}+\frac{\sqrt{(m+1)(n+1)}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{m+1}(x)\varphi_{n+1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\sqrt{mn}}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{(m+1)(n+1)}}{2}\right]\!\delta_{m,n}-\frac{\sqrt{m(n+1)}}{2}\delta_{m-1,n+1}-\frac{\sqrt{(m+1)n}}{2}\delta_{m+1,n-1}.

The standard three-term recurrence relation for orthonormal Hermite polynomials implies that

x\varphi_{n}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\varphi_{n-1}(x)+\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{2}}\varphi_{n+1}(x),

therefore

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{2}\varphi_{m}(x)\varphi_{n}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{mn}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{m-1}(x)\varphi_{n-1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\sqrt{m(n+1)}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{m-1}(x)\varphi_{n+1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\mbox{}+\frac{\sqrt{(m+1)n}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{m+1}(x)\varphi_{n-1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt\mbox{}+\frac{\sqrt{(m+1)(n+1)}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{m+1}(x)\varphi_{n+1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\sqrt{mn}}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{(m+1)(n+1)}}{2}\right]\!\delta_{m,n}+\frac{\sqrt{m(n+1)}}{2}\delta_{m-1,n+1}+\frac{\sqrt{(m+1)n}}{2}\delta_{m+1,n-1}.

Consequently,

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{m},\varphi_{n}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{x^{2}}=[\sqrt{mn}+\sqrt{(m+1)(n+1)}]\delta_{m,n}=(2n+1)\delta_{m,n},\qquad m,n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}.

(4.6)

Thus,
\Phi
is orthogonal with respect to the Hamiltonian sesquilinear form but it is not normalised. This is consistent with Theorem
8
. Note that (
4.6
) is not just a confirmation of (
4.5
) in this setting but also a reflection of the well known fact that the
\varphi_{n}
s are eigenfunctions of the linear Schrödinger equation with the harmonic potential
V(x)=x^{2}
.

Of course, it is easy to orthonormalise
\Phi
with respect to the Hamiltonian sesquilinear form, setting
\psi_{n}(x)=\varphi_{n}(x)/\sqrt{2n+1}
. Alas,
\{\psi_{n}\}_{n\in\mbox{\sBbb Z}_{+}}
is no longer orthonormal with respect to the
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
norm and, while its differentiation matrix is tridiagonal,

\psi_{n}^{\prime}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{n(2n-1)}{2(2n+1)}}\psi_{n-1}(x)-\sqrt{\frac{(n+1)(2n+3)}{2(2n+1)}}\psi_{n+1}(x),\qquad n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+},

it is no longer skew symmetric.

Spectra of Schrödinger operators are known explicitly in few settings, cf. for example
[
20
]
. For example, the eigenfunctions corresponding to
V(x)=x^{4}
can be expressed in terms of
Heun functions.
In our case, however, the knowledge of the spectrum is just one requirement, the other is that the eigenfunctions form (subject to proper renormalisation) a T-system. Even if, like in the case of the harmonic potential, both conditions are satisfied, Theorem
8
demonstrates that
\Phi
cannot be simultaneously orthonormal with respect to the two sesquilinear forms. Moreover, there is no reason why the differentiation matrix with respect to eigenfunctions of a differential operator should be tridiagonal, hence T-system conditions in general are not satisfied.

4.3
Differential Krylov subspaces revisited

Let
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\cdot,\cdot\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
be a sesquilinear form. We consider its differentiation matrix
\mbox{\curly D}=(d_{m,n})_{m,n\in\mbox{\sBbb Z}_{+}}
. In general we cannot expect
D
to be a tridiagonal matrix and
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\cdot,\cdot\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
does not have the IbP property unless
V
is a constant function. Hence the assumptions of the differential Lanczos algorithm do not necesssarily hold. Instead, we should consider an analogue of Arnoldi’s algorithm, in which an orthonormal basis of the space is generated so that the differential operator (in place of a square matrix in the classical case) is represented by an upper Hessenberg matrix.

1:

A seed function
\varphi_{0}
and a Hermitian sequilinear form
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\cdot,\cdot\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
on
\mathrm{span}(\{\varphi_{0}^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mbox{\sBbb Z}_{+}})
such that
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{0},\varphi_{0}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=1
.

2:

\Phi=\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, differentiation matrix
\mbox{\curly D}=(D_{m,n})_{m,n\in\mbox{\sBbb Z}_{+}}
.

3:

for

n=0,1,2,\ldots

do

4:

\psi_{n+1}=\varphi_{n}^{\prime}

5:

for

k=0,\ldots,n

do

6:

D_{k,n}=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\psi_{n+1},\varphi_{k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

7:

\psi_{n+1}=\psi_{n+1}-D_{k,n}\varphi_{k}

8:

end

for

9:

D_{n+1,n}=\left|\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\psi_{n+1},\psi_{n+1}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}\right|^{1/2}

10:

\varphi_{n+1}=D_{n+1,n}^{-1}\psi_{n+1}

11:

end

for

Algorithm 2

The Differential Arnoldi Algorithm

Theorem 10

If
D_{n+1,n}>0
for all
n
, then Algorithm
2
generates
\Phi=\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, and upper Hessenberg matrix
D
(with elements
D_{k,j}
) such that for all
n=1,2,\ldots
,

(i)

\{\varphi_{0},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{K}_{n}(\varphi_{0})
with respect to
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\cdot,\cdot\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

(ii)

\varphi_{n-1}^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}D_{k,n-1}\varphi_{k}(x)
.

Proof
Let us prove the result by induction. We start with the base case
n=1
. We have by lines 2 to 6,

\psi_{1}=\varphi_{0}^{\prime}-D_{0,0}\varphi_{0}.

(4.7)

This function is not identically zero because
\varphi_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}_{1}(\varphi_{0})\setminus\mathcal{K}_{0}(\varphi_{0})
by Definition
3
, and
\varphi_{0}\in\mathcal{K}_{0}(\varphi_{0})
. We have assumed that
D_{1,0}
is non-zero, so
\varphi_{1}
is well-defined and normalized by line 8 of the algorithm. Furthermore, property
(ii)
is satisfied by combining lines 2 to 8 of the algorithm. Finally,
\varphi_{1}
is orthogonal to
\varphi_{0}
because:

\displaystyle\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{1},\varphi_{0}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{1,0}^{-1}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{0}^{\prime}-D_{0,0}\varphi_{0},\varphi_{0}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{0,0}^{-1}\left(\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{0}^{\prime},\varphi_{0}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}-D_{0,0}\right),

which is zero by line 4 of the algorithm.

Now we assume that properties
(i)
and
(ii)
hold for a given
n
, and we wish to show it holds when
\varphi_{n+1}
is generated by Algorithm
2
. Just as in the base case, we have that
\psi_{n+1}
is not identically zero, because
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}_{n+1}(\varphi_{0})\setminus\mathcal{K}_{n}(\varphi_{0})
by Definition
3
, and lines 4 and 5 only subtract off elements of
\mathcal{K}_{n}(\varphi_{0})
, by the inductive hypothesis and line 8 of the algorithm. We have assumed that
D_{n+1,n}
is non-zero, so
\varphi_{n+1}
is well-defined and normalized. Lines 2 to 8 imply that
D_{n,n-1}\varphi_{n}=\varphi_{n-1}^{\prime}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}D_{k,n-1}\varphi_{k}
, so property
(ii)
is satisfied.

To complete the proof, we need to show that
\psi_{n+1}
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{K}_{n}(\varphi_{0})
, for then
\{\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n+1}\}
forms an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{K}_{n+1}(\varphi_{0})
by combining this fact with the inductive hypothesis. We first need to show that
D_{k,n}=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{n}^{\prime},\varphi_{k}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
for any
0\leq k\leq n
. This follows from line 4 of the algorithm combined with the orthonormality of
\{\varphi_{0},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\}
. Now, for
j=0,1,\ldots,n
,

\displaystyle\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\psi_{n+1},\varphi_{j}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{n}^{\prime}-\sum_{k=0}^{n}D_{k,n}\varphi_{k},\varphi_{j}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{n}^{\prime},\varphi_{j}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}-\sum_{k=0}^{n}D_{k,n}\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{k},\varphi_{j}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{n}^{\prime},\varphi_{j}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}-D_{j,n},

by the orthonormality of
\{\varphi_{0},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\}
. The fact that
D_{j,n}=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\varphi_{n}^{\prime},\varphi_{j}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
completes the proof.

\Box

4.4
Example: an H-system for a quartic potential

Let us consider the case
V(x)=x^{4}
and seed function proportional to the Gaussian
\phi(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}
. We start by normalising
\phi
with respect to the inner product
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\cdot,\cdot\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}
:

\|\phi\|_{V}^{2}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[x^{4}\phi(x)^{2}+\phi^{\prime}(x)^{2}]\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(x^{4}+x^{2})\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{5}{4}\pi^{1/2},

(4.8)

so

\varphi_{0}(x)=\alpha_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}=\frac{2}{5^{1/2}\pi^{1/4}}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}.

(4.9)

Then,
\psi_{1}(x)=\varphi_{0}^{\prime}(x)=-\alpha_{0}x\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}
. Since
\phi_{0}
is even and
\psi_{1}
is odd, we have
D_{0,0}=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\psi_{1},\varphi_{0}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}=0
. Now we normalise
\psi_{1}
to obtain
\varphi_{1}
(we spare you the details):

\varphi_{1}(x)=\alpha_{1}x\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}=-2\frac{2^{1/2}}{21^{1/2}\pi^{1/4}}x\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2},

(4.10)

which came from
D_{1,0}=\|\psi_{1}\|_{V}=21^{1/2}/10^{1/2}
. The next basis function is where we see the non-skew-Hermitian nature of the differentation matrix. We have
\psi_{2}(x)=\varphi_{1}^{\prime}(x)=\alpha_{1}(1-x^{2})\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}
. We can compute
D_{1,1}=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\psi_{2},\varphi_{1}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}=0
by parity, and

\displaystyle D_{0,1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\psi_{2},\varphi_{0}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{0}x^{4}(1-x^{2})\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}+\left(\alpha_{1}(x^{3}-3x)\right)\left(-\alpha_{0}x\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{210^{1/2}}.

Indeed, we see that
D_{0,1}\neq D_{1,0}
. The subsequent basis functions are odd and even polynomials multiplied by
\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}
. We plot the first six basis functions (generated using a symbolic implementation of Algorithm
2
in Julia) in Figure

.

4.5
When are H-systems almost-T-systems?

Of course,
D
is in general neither skew-symmetric nor tridiagonal. However, an intriguing computational observation is that it misses on both properties by a very small margin. Fig.
4.2
displays a bar chart of size of the elements of the matrix
|\mbox{\curly D}|=(|D_{m,n}|)_{m,n=0,\ldots,16}
for four different functions
V
(that define a sesquilinear form
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\cdot,\cdot\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{V}
), the seed function a multiple of
\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}
. The most prominent feature are the two leading off-diagonals. (The diagonal is of course zero.) While behind the super-diagonal all elements are zero (the matrix is lower Hessenberg), the terms under the sub-diagonal are very small. In other words, while
|D_{n,n+1}|
and
|D_{n+1,n}|
are relatively large (and, although this is not apparent from Fig.
4.2
,
|D_{n,n+1}+D_{n+1,n}|
is small), the terms
|D_{m,n}|
are small for
n\leq m-2
, hardly noticeable to a naked eye. The practical implication of this observation (which, as things stand, is just an observation, with neither proof or even a decent hand-waiving explanation) is that, although the system
\Phi
, orthogonal with respect to the bilinear product (
4.1
), is neither orthogonal in the
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
inner product nor a T-system, it fails on both counts only slightly.

Figure 4.2:
A two dimensional bar chart of
|\mbox{\curly D}|
for four different potentials
V
, starting from a Gaussian seed function.

A theoretical conformation of the observation following from Fig.
4.2
is unavailable, neither is an understanding of its practical implications. Both are a matter for future research.

First, T-systems can be generated using a version of the Lanzcos algorithm, which finds an orthogonal basis tridiagonalising a symmetric matrix, to the differential operator. There are two consequences to this: new T-systems can be generated algorithmically from a smooth seed function without the need for an appropriate differentiation matrixt that would make the system orthogonal and without the Fourier transform; also, the analysis of the approximation properties T-systems may now be tackled using the theory of Krylov subspace approximation!

Second, we began to develop a methodology around spectral methods that preserve a Hamiltonian form. We showed in section 4.1 that a sufficient condition for this property is to ensure that the basis is orthonormal with respect to the associated sesquilinear form. It turns out that we can indeed generate bases with this kind of orthonormality, but we must use a differential analogue of the Arnoldi Algorithm instead of the Lanczos algorithm (because the differential operator is not necessarily skew-selfadjoint with respect to the Hamiltonian form), and the resulting differentiation matrix is upper-Hessenberg instead of skew-symmetric tridiagonal. We will call these systems H-systems.

We did not investigate H-systems in detail, but there appear to be some interesting properties worthy of investigation. In particular, we found that the differentiation matrix is very close to skew-symmetric tridiagonal, the reasons for which we leave as an open problem.
