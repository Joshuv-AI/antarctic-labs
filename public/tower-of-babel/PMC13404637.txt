Randomized phase II trial of nivolumab and ipilimumab with or without stereotactic body radiation therapy in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer: the CheckPRO, CA209-8TY trial
Authors: Rikke Løvendahl Eefsen, Nicklas Juel Spindler, Susann Theile, Gina Al-Farra, Helle W Hendel, Torben Lorentzen, Gitte Fredberg Persson, Claus P Behrens, Inna Markova Chen, Kasper Madsen, Lisa Sengeløv, Inge Marie Svane, Henriette Lindberg, Per Kongsted, Dorte Lisbeth Nielsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) is among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality in men worldwide. Treatment options for mCRPC typically include chemotherapy and androgen receptor pathway inhibitors. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have demonstrated a limited effect in mCRPC. We hypothesized that the addition of stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) could enhance immune responses and improve treatment outcomes.MethodsPatients with mCRPC who had received at least two prior lines of therapy were randomized 1:1 to receive SBRT with nivolumab and ipilimumab (arm A) or nivolumab and ipilimumab (arm B). The dual primary endpoints in the study were prostate-specific antigen (PSA) response rate and objective response rate (ORR). Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), PSA, radiologic progression-free survival, and toxicity.ResultsWe enrolled 91 patients, and 81 patients received at least one treatment with ICIs and were eligible for evaluation of the efficacy and safety endpoints. Of the evaluable patient population, the confirmed PSA response rate was 21.6% (95% CI 9.8% to 38.2%) in arm A, and 20.5% (95% CI 9.8% to 35.3%) in arm B. ORR was 16.7% (95% CI 4.7% to 37.4%) and 22.2% (95% CI 10.1% to 39.2%) in arms A and B, respectively. Median OS was 10.2 months (95% CI 7.1 to 15.2) in arm A and 9.2 months (95% CI 7.1 to 14.4) in arm B. Treatment-related adverse events grade 3–4 were observed in 27 patients (33.3%).ConclusionsThe addition of SBRT to nivolumab and ipilimumab did not improve outcomes; however, a fraction of the patients had a response to the treatment combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab. Explorative translational research is needed to identify possible biomarkers of response to immunotherapy with ICIs in mCRPC.Trial registration numbersEuropean Union Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu) EudraCT number: 2018-003461-34 and on https://clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05655715).Keywords: Prostate Cancer, Immunotherapy, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Abscopal, Radiotherapy/radioimmunotherapy

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Introduction

In the USA, prostate cancer is the most frequent cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths among men.1 Androgen deprivation therapy is initiated in case of metastatic disease. On progression to metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), additional treatment options may include taxane-based chemotherapy, androgen receptor pathway inhibitors, radionucleotide therapy, and for patients with mutations in the homologous recombination repair pathway, poly-ADP ribose polymerase inhibitors, all of which can contribute to improved survival outcomes.2–9

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have changed the treatment landscape in multiple tumor types. However, their effectiveness in prostate cancer remains limited, which is largely attributed to the low immunogenicity of prostate cancer, characterized by a ‘cold’ tumor microenvironment.10–14 As such, the tumor microenvironment hosts a high number of immunosuppressive cells and a low number of CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, both of which pose major barriers to the effectiveness of single-agent ICI.15–17

Immunotherapy in cancer is used to induce a robust antitumor immune response.18 19 Research has shown that combining radiation therapy and immunotherapy can produce a synergistic antineoplastic effect via the abscopal effect.19–27 Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) has the potential to enhance immune response and activate the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway, leading to increased production of interferon, which modulates the innate and adaptive immune responses against tumor cells.28–31 We hypothesized that patients with mCRPC would be more likely to respond to dual ICI when SBRT was added to the treatment regimen. SBRT was given to a metastatic lesion to enhance the efficacy of dual ICI through a systemic antitumor immune response via the abscopal effect.20 22 23 To test this hypothesis, we designed a randomized, phase II study to evaluate the response to nivolumab and ipilimumab, administered with or without SBRT. The secondary objectives were safety, quality of life, and efficacy, defined as clinical benefit rate (CBR), radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS), and overall survival (OS). The exploratory endpoints were to identify possible immunological and genetic predictive and prognostic biomarkers.

Patients and methods

Trial design and patients

The CheckPRO trial (CA209-8TY) was a single-center, investigator-initiated, randomized trial conducted at Herlev and Gentofte Hospital, Copenhagen, Denmark (online supplemental figure 1). The trial was registered in the European Union Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu) EudraCT number: 2018-003461-34 and on https://clinicaltrials.gov (NCT 05655715), see online supplemental materials. All participants provided written and oral informed consent to the trial.

Key eligibility criteria included patients≥18 years of age with mCRPC characterized by histological confirmation of metastatic adenocarcinoma. Participants were required to have previously received at least two prior lines of therapy in the metastatic setting: at least one ARPI and one taxane-based chemotherapy. Additionally, eligible patients had a performance status of 0 or 1 and could not have received prior immunotherapy. A metastatic lesion eligible for SBRT was mandatory, and biopsies and blood samples were collected, as previously described.32

Treatment

Participants were stratified by and randomized in a 1:1 ratio. Those in arm A received SBRT to a metastatic lesion found eligible for SBRT at the multidisciplinary SBRT team conference. The SBRT regimen consisted of 24 Gy in three fractions in 1 week, the first fraction administered on day 1 of cycle 1. Systemic therapy was with the ICIs, nivolumab (3 mg/kg) intravenously and ipilimumab (1 mg/kg intravenously), administered every third week for four cycles. Participants in arm B received the same systemic regimen of ICIs without SBRT. After the initial four ICI cycles, the systemic therapy continued with nivolumab monotherapy, 480 mg intravenously once per month for up to 52 weeks. Patients who completed 1 year of therapy entered a follow-up period. If disease progression occurred within 52 weeks, participants could be offered reinduction therapy with ICIs without SBRT.

Endpoints and assessments

The co-endpoints were the prostate-specific antigen (PSA) response rate and the objective response rate (ORR). Secondary endpoints were PSA PFS, radiographic PFS, clinical benefit rate, OS, and safety. The endpoints were evaluated in patients who were randomized and who received at least one cycle of ICI. The results of the intended-to-treat (ITT) patient population are also presented.

A PSA response was defined as a≥50% decrease in PSA (PSA50), confirmed by a follow-up PSA test≥3 weeks later. The endpoint PSA50 is in line with endpoints in comparable studies.10 For patients with measurable disease according to RECIST V.1.1, an objective response (OR) was defined as a partial response (PR) or a complete response (CR) in accordance with Prostate Cancer Working Group 3 RECIST V.1.1 (PCWG3-RECIST 1.1) criteria.33 Patient evaluations occurred every 8 weeks, using an 18F-fluoride-positron emission tomography/CT, and were continued until disease progression or end of study. Duration of response (DOR) was assessed from response according to RECIST V.1.1 to progressive disease (PD). The imaging was assessed by experienced specialists in radiology (GA-F) and in nuclear medicine (HWH).

Clinical beneficial rate included patients with a CR, PR, stable disease for more than 6 months, including patients with measurable disease, or non-PD documented in three or more assessments in patients with evaluable disease based on bone lesions. Survival analyses included median rPFS, median PSA PFS, and median OS.

For initial biomarkers analyses, OS analyses were stratified by baseline lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), neutrophil–lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and C-reactive protein (CRP). For these analyses, patients were dichotomized as low versus high using cutoffs for LDH>upper limit of normal (ULN), NLR≥5 and CRP of >10 mg/L. For patients below 70 years, the LDH ULN was 205, while the LDH ULN for patients above 70 years was 255.

Statistical analyses

The sample size was calculated using a two-stage design with two experimental arms. The study had the co-primary endpoints, PSA response rate and ORR. For the sample size calculation, we used the confirmed PSA-response. We anticipated that both arms could have a clinically meaningful PSA response rate of 25% vs a 10% null rate. We selected a predefined threshold to select the superior treatment arm with a minimum of 20% difference between the two arms if the superior arm had a ≥25% PSA response rate. Alpha was 5% and with 80% power in both stages, which yielded a sample size of 80 patients. A total of 90 patients would be included, with a goal of 40 patients in each arm and to account for non-evaluable patients. The design included a preplanned interim analysis after the first stage of 20 patients had been treated in each arm. To open the second stage, a minimum of two patients had to have a confirmed PSA-response in the given treatment arm to continue. Given the study design with two experimental arms, the study was not designed to compare the treatment arms directly. However, the sample size method used calculates a predefined testing procedure with the following features: A minimum of eight patients had to have confirmed PSA-response to conclude that one treatment arm is promising.34 If treatment in arm A and/or B reached ≥8 responders, then the treatment passed the testing procedure and would be considered promising. If a treatment arm did not reach ≥8 responders, then it would not be regarded as promising. If the number of responders was ≥8 in both treatment arms, then the treatment in both arms A and B would be considered promising. One arm was superior to the other if the difference between responders in arm A or in arm B was ≥5. The sample size and the above-mentioned testing procedure were calculated by applying the published SAS code (V.9.4).

PFS and OS were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, including the two-sided 95% CI. No p values were used to estimate differences or similarities, as the groups were two independent experimental arms that needed to pass the interim analysis to continue. Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were evaluated with descriptive statistics. Statistical analyses were performed in R, V.4.3.0.

Results

Patients and treatment

Between November 2019 and January 2024, a total of 130 patients were screened and 91 were randomized to either arm A (45 patients) or arm B (46 patients). 8 patients in arm A and 2 patients in arm B did not receive the intended therapy due to clinical deterioration shortly after randomization, resulting in 37 patients remaining in arm A and 44 patients in arm B, eligible for response, survival, and safety analysis. Seven patients completed 1 year of therapy. Three of these received ICIs reinduction within the 52-week follow-up period (figure 1).

Consort flow diagram of patients enrolled in the CA209-8TY, CheckPRO trial. TRAE, treatment-related adverse event.

Baseline characteristics of the patients were numerically similar between the two groups (table 1). Notably, 64.9% had measurable disease at baseline in arm A and 81.8% in arm B according to RECIST V.1.1. The median number of treatments with nivolumab administered in the first 52 weeks was 3 (range 1–14) in both arms A and B. Ipilimumab was given for a median of three cycles (range 1–4) in both arms. 11 (29.7%) and 19 patients (43.2%) received the intended 4 cycles of nivolumab and ipilimumab in arms A and B, respectively. Out of the 30 patients that completed 4 cycles of ipilimumab and nivolumab, 23 patients (28%) continued nivolumab monotherapy. The remaining seven patients stopped therapy after four cycles of ipilimumab and nivolumab, six due to PD and one due to TRAE. 8 patients (9.9%) received less than the 4 intended doses of ipilimumab and nivolumab due to TRAE, but continued nivolumab monotherapy, and 43 patients (53%) discontinued therapy within 3 months due to PD (n=35) or TRAE (n=8), (online supplemental table 1). For all patients, a metastatic lesion was chosen for SBRT, but the SBRT planning in detail was only performed for patients randomized to arm A (table 1 and online supplemental table 2). The logistics differed between patients in arms A and B, as patients in arm A had to wait to start therapy cycle 1 day 1 (C1D1) until the SBRT plan was ready, in comparison to patients in arm B, who could be planned for C1D1 of dual ICI right after randomization. The logistics in timing of SBRT was the main reason for dropouts in arm A (n=8), as these patients had rapid clinical deterioration in the period from randomization to C1D1. The reasons for dropouts in arm A were medullary compression (n=3), clinical deterioration (n=2), rapid liver transaminase increase (n=1), clinically significant CRP increase (n=1), and SBRT not possible (n=1). Dropouts in arm B (n=2) had medullary compression (n=1) and clinically significant CRP increase (n=1).

Efficacy

The confirmed PSA response rate was 21.6% (95% CI 9.8% to 38.2%, n=8/37) in arm A and 20.5% (95% CI 9.8% to 35.3%, n=9/44) in arm B. Among the 60 patients with measurable disease, the OR according to PCWG3-RECIST 1.1 was 16.7% (95% CI 4.7% to 37.4%, n=4/24) and 22.2% (95% CI 10.1% to 39.2%, n=8/36) in arms A and B, respectively. The ORR for the entire cohort was 20% (see figure 2 and online supplemental figure 2). PSA change over time is illustrated for arms A and B (see online supplemental figure 3). The CBR was 21.6% (95% CI 9.8% to 38.2%, n=8/37) in arm A and 22.7% (95%CI 11.5% to 37.8%, n=10/44) in arm B.

At the time of data cut-off on 1 July 2025, the median follow-up time was 57.2 months (25.3 months lower quartile and 66.5 months, upper quartile). Time on treatment is illustrated in the swimmer plot for patients in arms A and B (see online supplemental figure 4). The median PSA PFS for arm A was 2.6 months (95% CI 1.9 to 3.8), in arm B 2.5 months (95% CI 1.9 to 3.0) and in the total cohort 2.5 months (95% CI 2.1 to 3.7). Likewise, median rPFS was 2.1 months (95% CI 1.9 to 3.8) in arm A and 1.9 months (95% CI 1.8 to 2.5) in arm B (please see figure 3 and online supplemental figure 5). The 6 months rPFS rate was 22.6% (95% CI 10.7% to 37.2%) in arm A and 14.8% (95% CI 6.1% to 27.0%) in arm B. Median OS was 10.2 months (95% CI 7.1 to 15.2) in arm A, 9.2 months (95% CI 7.1 to 14.4) in arm B (see figure 4), and 10.1 months (95% CI 7.3 to 13.0) in the total population (see online supplemental figure 6). The 6-month OS rate was 70.3% (95% CI 52.8% to 82.3%) in arm A and 77.3% (95% CI 61.9% to 87.1%) in arm B, while the 1-year OS rate was 45.4% (95% CI 28.9% to 60.5%) in arm A and 40.5% (95% CI 26.0% to 54.5%) in arm B. Median DOR was overall 5.5 months (95% CI 1.5 to 18.9) (see online supplemental table 3) and online supplemental figure 7). The median OS for the ITT patient population was very similar to the median OS of the evaluable patients in the trial. The mOS ITT was from the date of randomization to death or censored for patients in arm A (n=45) was 10.5 months (95% CI 6.1 to 13.7) and for patients in arm B (n=46) mOS ITT was 9.3 months (95% CI 6.9 to 14.3). The mOS of the total ITT patient population was 9.4 months (95% CI 7.2 to 11.7), please see online supplemental figure 8.

Safety

All patients in the trial experienced at least one adverse event, with 70.4% experiencing grade (G) 3–4 AE. G3–4 TRAEs were observed in 32.4% of arm A and 34.1% of arm B, in total observed in 27 patients (33.3%). All TRAEs occurring at any grade in at least 5% of the patients are listed (table 2). 10 patients discontinued therapy due to TRAEs, 8 of these occurred within the first 3 months of treatment, and 2 at a later timepoint (see online supplemental table 1). All discontinuations occurred prior to documented disease progression. The TRAEs leading to treatment termination in arm A included colitis (three patients), colitis combined with hypophysitis, myositis, troponin elevation, and hepatitis. In arm B, termination was due to colitis, colitis combined with thyroiditis, and erythema multiforme. No substantial differences in TRAEs were observed between arms A and B. No treatment-related deaths were observed.

Treatment-related adverse events (TRAE)

Discussion

This single-center, investigator-initiated, randomized phase II trial successfully met the primary endpoint of PSA response for ≥8 patients in arms A and B, suggesting that the combination treatment with nivolumab and ipilimumab is beneficial for a subgroup of patients with mCRPC. However, the addition of SBRT did not increase the response rates or survival outcomes in the evaluable nor the ITT patient population, indicating that our primary hypothesis was not supported.

How to achieve an optimal antitumor immune response in prostate cancer is unknown; however, theoretically, the recognition of tumor antigens such as PSA and prostate-specific membrane antigen may lead to an immune activation of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells.35 36 We hypothesized that the addition of SBRT to ICI could enhance the antitumor antigen presentation, leading to immune activation and improved outcomes. However, the presence of regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the tumor microenvironment may inhibit effector T-cell function, posing a challenge to achieving an effective antitumor immune response.37 38 Because Tregs infiltrate the tumor microenvironment and often express CTLA-4, which is associated with immune evasion and a poor prognosis in various cancers, including prostate cancer, it was considered relevant to target CTLA-4 for an optimal antitumor immune response in this trial.37–41 The combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab has been validated in several trials and is approved for the treatment of malignant melanoma and renal carcinoma.42–45 Our trial used the approved dosing regimen for patients with renal cell carcinoma.45

Prior studies have reported that a subgroup of patients with mCRPC respond to ICI with or without SBRT.10–14 In the Checkmate 650 (CM650) trial, patients were enrolled into two cohorts after failing one line of therapy (cohort 1) or at least two lines of therapy (cohort 2).10 Our study population resembles cohort 2 of the CM650 trial; however, notable differences exist. We enrolled a more mixed patient population with and without visceral metastases, including those with liver metastases. In the CM650 trial, patients were eligible after one line of cytotoxic chemotherapy, with 64.4% having received three or more lines of therapy in the metastatic setting. In contrast, nearly all patients in our trial had received at least three prior lines of therapy, including a minimum of two lines of cytotoxic chemotherapy. We found a relatively short DOR, which might reflect the heavily treatment-refractory patient population. In line, the longer rPFS and OS observed in the CM650 trial may be attributed to the earlier treatment line compared with our study cohort. In the NEPTUNES (Nivolumab and Ipilimumab Treatment in Prostate Cancer with Embedded/Unique Novel Enriched Signature) trial, patients with mCRPC and either a high inflammatory signature, a mismatch repair deficiency, or a mutation in one of the DNA damage repair pathway genes, were enrolled.46 In the trial, a composite response rate of 32% was observed. The patient population was quite like the patients in this trial and reflects that if patients are selected based on a genomic or inflammatory signature, it is more likely that they respond to immunotherapy with ICI. Notably, about 50% of patients had measurable disease in the NEPTUNES trial, compared with approximately 80% in the current study. This difference should be considered when interpreting the response results, which are solely on patients with measurable disease.

The Keynote-199 study evaluated monotherapy with pembrolizumab in three distinct cohorts: cohort 1 (PD-L1 positive), cohort 2 (PD-L1 negative), and cohort 3 (predominant bone disease), none of which directly align with our trial population.11 The cohort most comparable to ours was cohort 3, which reported an OS of 14.1 months; however, only 10% of patients in this cohort had visceral metastases at baseline, further limiting comparability.

Kwon et al explored the combination of radiation therapy and ICI in patients with mCRPC and bone metastasis. Patients received 8 Gy single fraction to a bone metastasis in combination with ipilimumab versus RT and placebo. They found no difference in response rates or survival between the two cohorts.12 However, a long-term follow-up analysis revealed a significantly longer OS for those patients receiving both radiation therapy and ipilimumab.13

Conversely, in another phase II trial including 31 patients with mCRPC where patients received ICI with the PD-1 inhibitor avelumab and SBRT, 20 Gy in a single fraction to one or two disease sites, an ORR of 31% was reported. The median rPFS and OS were 8.4 and 14.1 months, respectively.14 It is notable that although 51% of patients had visceral metastases, only 29% had received ≥3 prior treatment lines. In our study, about 80% of the patients had measurable disease and nearly all had ≥3 prior lines of therapy prior to enrollment. Accordingly, the patient populations differ, with our cohort being more heavily pretreated than the phase II ICE-PAC cohort. This discrepancy in treatment history makes direct comparison more difficult, yet the ORR of 31% suggests a potential efficacy of combining ICIs with SBRT in selected patients. In the NEPTUNES trial, patients with mCRPC whose tumor had an immunogenic signature, such as deficient mismatch repair/microsatellite instability-high (dMMR/MSI-high) tumor, DNA damage repair gene loss, or with a high inflammatory infiltrate, achieved an overall composite response rate of 32% with ipilimumab plus nivolumab.46 This finding supports that identification of a subgroup of patients with mCRPC may be beneficial for treatment with ICI.

The TRAEs observed in this study align with previously reported TRAEs associated with dual ICI.46–48 For some patients the TRAEs were substantial, underscoring the need for patient selection, as most patients with mCRPC are older men who have comorbidities and are therefore more fragile.

A major limitation of this study was the number of patients who dropped out after randomization and before treatment in C1D1. The main reason for this was the waiting time for the SBRT plan to be ready for C1D1. In this SBRT planning period, some of the randomized patients had major clinical deterioration and were no longer fit for therapy when reaching C1D1. This issue was pronounced in arm A, resulting in a higher dropout rate than in arm B. The ITT patient population was therefore not achieved, as the goal of the trial was to have 40 patients in each treatment arm who received the planned treatment to ensure that the statistical plan was complete. The results should therefore be interpreted with caution. We present the data from the evaluable patient population, which affects the real randomization design and entails a risk of introducing bias favoring one group over the other due to dropouts in arms A and B. Therefore, the data should be interpreted with caution and considered more observational. Notably, the mOS of the ITT population in arm A and B was very similar to the mOS of the evaluable patient population. Therefore, these findings are considered relevant to report. Another limitation is the lack of translational data at this point, as research on biomarkers is ongoing. The lack of genomic data of the patients limits the interpretation of the data, as we already know that patients with MSI-high tumor or DNA damage repair mutations can respond to immunotherapy with ICIs.10 Additionally, the proportion of patients with measurable disease was high; however, the effective evaluable population for ORR was smaller than the total number of patients enrolled in the trial, which may reduce the statistical power for this endpoint. Additionally, the long accrual period and limited sample size had an impact on the novelty of the findings.

The SBRT approach used in arm A did not improve outcomes; however, delivery was heterogeneous, and correlative immune data are not yet available. Further studies are therefore needed to determine whether SBRT can produce an abscopal effect. Notably, a subset of patients with mCRPC derived clinical benefit from nivolumab and ipilimumab, and exploratory translational research, including genomic data, will be needed to understand the antitumor immune response in prostate cancer.
