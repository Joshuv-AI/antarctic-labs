A Simulation-Free Topological Basis for Building Compact
Koopman Models of Protein Folding

Ziad Fakhoury, Gabriele C Sosso, Scott Habershon

Abstract

Unravelling protein-folding
mechanisms and kinetics is a key challenge
to biochemical science. The variational approach for Markov processes
(VAMP) is a powerful tool to build Markov Models that capture key
kinetic and structural information despite the conformational complexity
and long time scales associated with protein folding. However, VAMP-based
Markov models use data from exhaustive molecular dynamics (MD) simulations
to construct an underlying basis set describing the “coarse-grained”
kinetics; the same MD data can be used to predict transition probabilities
between partitioned configuration space, enabling the extraction of
folding time scales and mechanism. Here, we propose an alternative
strategy for Markov model construction that does not rely on extensive,
computationally demanding MD data for configuration space partitioning.
Specifically, we show that graph-driven sampling (GDS) can generate
a complete “landscape” of intermediate contact-maps
linking unfolded and folded protein conformations; importantly, extensive
MD simulations are not required in GDS. When combined with a physically
intuitive shortest-contact-hop metric to discriminate different intermediate
states, GDS mapping of protein-folding configuration space generates
a reliable “structurally aware” partitioning for VAMP
model construction. To demonstrate this strategy, we show that a GDS-constructed
Markov model variationally improves folding time scale estimates for
all-atom models of the WW domain proteinand has the additional
advantage of easily resolving kinetic traps in the folding landscape
that have proven challenging to confirm otherwise. Together, the combination
of GDS and VAMP opens a new route toward rapid characterization of
protein-folding intermediates and kinetics traps to help address frontier
challenges such as protein misfolding, aggregation, and protein design.

Introduction

Protein structure prediction
has recently been successfully addressed
with the development of deep learning techniques using mined protein-sequence
alignment data.
,
 On the other hand, understanding
the dynamic pathways by which proteins obtain their folded native
state remains an important open problem that is critical for better
understanding of important biophysical phenomena such as ligand-binding
interactions, protein–protein interfaces, protein misfolding,
and allosteric regulation. “Foldability”
is also an often-neglected feature in contemporary computational protein
design workflowsa drawback that could be addressed with better
access to computational insights into folding mechanism and kinetics.
,

In general, the time scale separation between fast local fluctuations
encoded in atomistic models of proteins and the slow relevant conformational
transitions gives rise to a rare event problem that hinders the computational
study of protein-folding. Advances in both algorithms and hardware
have provided some limited access to extremely long molecular dynamics
(MD) trajectories of folding events, with a particularly prominent
example being the microsecond-long MD simulations generated for several
fast folding proteins using the custom-made ANTON computing architecture. However, extracting kinetic and mechanistic properties
from this collection of dataspanning over 1 ms with MD snapshots
taken every 2.5 ps for systems containing up to 561 protein atoms
(alongside ≈ 4300 water molecules)is a daunting task,
albeit an important one for the study of protein dynamics.

Markov
State Models (MSMs) have emerged as a powerful frameworkk
for modeling protein folding dynamics by discretizing conformational
space into metastable states, and subsequently estimating the transition
probabilities between them.
−

 By leveraging existing collections of MD simulations,
MSMs are linear models of protein dynamics which enable an efficient
and straightforward reconstruction of folding pathways and the computation
of long-time scale kinetics. Constructing accurate MSMs requires careful
state discretization and sufficient a priori MD sampling,
making this approach sensitive to a number of user-specified parameters
as well as the nature of the available MD data.
,

To address the choice of state discretization, the variational
approach to conformational dynamics (VAC) and the more general variational
approach for Markov processes (VAMP) were introduced as frameworks
to optimize MSMs by maximizing a variational score, offering a principled
way to compare different discretizations.
,
 Furthermore, these variational strategies allow for the generalization
of MSMs to Markov models defined with “soft states”functions
that act as a basis to express conformational probability densities
and dynamical models. This paved the way for neural networksVAMPNetsto
replace traditional state selection methods with neural-network-parametrized
“soft states” that maximize the variational score, improving
state-space resolution and kinetic accuracy. However, these variational approaches still have a high dependency
on MD data generationa severe computational bottleneck that
hinders their broader applicability. MD simulations for fitting both
discrete-state MSMs and “soft state” Markov models is
an active area of research, with current approaches making use of
active, adaptive and enhanced sampling techniques.
−

Recently, we have proposed a simulation strategy to generate
protein
folding pathways using a contact-map-based representation, termed
graph-driven sampling (GDS).
,
 Crucially, we are able
to generate a folding pathway ensemble without performing extensive
MD simulations or using predefined order parameters. The GDS approach
directly identifies and characterizes those regions of the protein
structural landscape that are explored in the dynamic folding processinformation
that could provide an alternative route to constructing a suitable
state definition for a Markov model.

In this work, we hypothesize
that the intermediate states defined
by the contact-map–based folding trajectories available from
GDS can inform the construction of an ideal basis set for building
a Markov modelwithout resorting to any a priori MD simulations. Specifically, each contact map in our GDS trajectories
represents a distinct “state” of the protein that reflects
significant conformational changes in the protein structure. As the
protein folds, the sequential transformation captured in its contact
map traces a pathway through a reduced conformational space that is
both physically interpretable and computationally tractable. By using
this set of states as a basis, a Markov model can be constructed to
(i) propagate probability densities defined on contact map states;
(ii) identify slow transition modes in the contact map representation,
and; (iii) quantify kinetic parameters such as relaxation time scales
and metastable state residence times.

The key advantage with
this approach is that, unlike the popular
VAMPNet and the earlier MSM approach, a basis for a Markov Model can be built without
any MD data a priori. Generating such data usually
incurs a considerable cost; in fact, VAMPNets are currently trained
on an initial smaller MD data set that are then used to seed further
MD simulations in an iterative fashion.
,−

 However, given that our approach identifies a suitable basis without
the need of any MD data to begin with, it can enable either strategic
use of enhanced MD simulations or rate
estimates from path sampling and optimization techniques.
,
 This would represent a paradigm shift in how future Markov models
for protein folding are constructed.

The proposed approach therefore
bridges the gap between discrete
structural protein conformational transitions and continuous kinetic
modeling of protein dynamics. The discrete contact-map representation
used in GDS circumvents many challenges encountered in direct MD simulations.
For example, the demand for long simulation times to sample rare transitions
and the difficulty in selecting an appropriate order parameter are
circumvented in GDS by providing an alternative “coordinate
system” in which the most significant folding events are emphasized.
In turn, as shown below, the Markov model built upon this basis offers
a systematic and quantitative description of the folding kinetics
that is not only predictive but also interpretable in terms of underlying
molecular events.

Furthermore, the residue–residue graph
representation of
a protein conformation, known as a contact map, is thought to encode
the structurally relevant information defining the protein’s
topology. They have historically been a key representation used in
seminal works in protein folding.
−

 Therefore, contact maps,
as employed in GDS, provide an efficient reduced representation that
captures large-scale structural rearrangements. Despite not being
suited to the study of localized conformational changes, such as side-chain
rotations, GDS can reveal details of the folding mechanism and associated
time scales.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows.
First, we
apply the GDS workflow outlined above to a toy 2D protein folding
landscape modeled as a rugged funnel. We confirm that one can define
a suitable basis set for Markov model construction, without resorting
to long MD trajectories beforehand; this basis is generated using
intermediate configurations from a path-sampling algorithm defined
on a discrete grid, in a similar fashion to our (more general) GDS
algorithm. Second, we use our GDS/VAMP methodology to investigate
folding of the designed GTT variant of the FIP35 WW domain, a single chain protein with 35 residues with
a folding topology defined by two β hairpins. We find that using
GDSin conjunction with a physically motivated structure similarity
metric defined in protein contact-map spaceenables construction
of a suitable basis set for a Markov model; we subsequently demonstrate
the accuracy of this model by parametrization and kinetic evaluation
using existing ANTON MD trajectory data. Importantly, this process definitively identifies a ‘kinetic
trap’ within the folding pathway(s) of the FIP35 WW domain
which previous conventional VAMP-based approaches struggled to resolve.
As such, our results clearly highlight the potential of this combined
GDS/VAMP approach to redefine exploration of protein folding dynamics,
simultaneously offering a route to rapidly identifying elusive folding
intermediates and kinetic bottlenecks that escape detection by more
traditional partitioning methods.

Graph Driven Sampling

In this article, we propose to
replace MD simulations for configuration space partitioning with an
alternative, order-parameter-free scheme that enables fast, accurate
and physically interpretative sampling of relevant protein-folding
configurations. We have recently shown how the ensemble of physically
realizable protein-folding pathways can be generated using directed
walks in the space of inter-residue contact-maps. Combined with a
back-transformation scheme to move from protein contact-maps to Cartesian
coordinates, we have demonstrated how this approach can generate protein-folding
ensembles without resorting to extensive MD simulations.
,
 Here, we propose that this GDS scheme can be used to create a compact
and accurate basis for Koopman model construction, circumventing the
need for MD simulations or order-parameter-based partitioning, and
therefore providing a significant advantage for targeted simulation
strategies.

time scales governing the kinetics. Therefore, we expect a basis informed
with intermediates extracted from GDS simulations to serve well in
decomposing our dynamical system under the VAMP framework; this is
the key idea of this article.

Markov Models in Contact-Map
Space

Many computational
studies of protein folding have made use of the assumption that the
formation of native contacts categorizes the folding mechanism.
,
 However, simply using the fraction of native contacts, Q, as a reaction coordinate for folding has been shown to have severe
non-Markovian artifacts.
−

 It also lacks structure specificity;
a particular Q can be obtained from several topologically
distinct conformations, such that this approach may struggle to resolve
multiple pathways or trapped intermediates. It has been shown thatby
finding optimal weightings for different contacts in a similar reaction
coordinate to Qone can better interpret the
resulting modified reaction coordinate and gain insight into critical
events within the folding path. However,
the same work also found that the highly cooperative nature of contacts
lead to a degeneracy over the optimal reaction coordinates. This finding
is not surprising in the context of our discussion on the choice of
metrics to measure structural similarity of contact maps, where the influence of a specific contact on
the structural similarity of protein structure can vary, and depends
on the environment described by the rest of the topology. Instead, a Markov model constructed with state
definitions that can both minimally cover and successfully
distinguish the relevant parts of state space would be anticipated
to be expressive enough to identify different pathways and intermediates.

Given that our previously reported SCH metric () is able to effectively capture
structurally similarity, we argue that using SCH-based radial Gaussian
basis functions centered around significant intermediates (i.e., contact-maps)
should serve as a “good” set of basis functions for
construction of a Markov model describing protein folding. Indeed,
contact-maps serve as a compact descriptor with great specificity
for protein-folding topologies, and we have shown already that the
SCH metric is effective in identifying collections of correlated structures
during folding. In turn, this approach
offer a good balance between dealing with the high-dimensional fully
atomistic description of a protein and the often-too-simplified native
contacts coordinate Q that can fail to capture important
complexities of the folding process.

Results and Discussion

To demonstrate how GDS can be used to construct an accurate MSM
for complex dynamic processes, we begin here by presenting a illustrative
example for a model two-dimensional (2D) “folding funnel”
representative of the rugged landscapes traversed by more realistic
protein-folding paths. Following this proof-of-concept, we turn to
construction of a Markov model for a much more realistic protein-folding
process, namely folding of the 35-residue WW domain protein (described
by an atomistic force-field with explicit water). We demonstrate that
GDS constructs a basis set for Markov modeling that is accurate, interpretable,
and capable of resolving featuressuch as important kinetic
trapsthat have proven difficult to sufficiently resolve using
previous partitioning approaches. Furthermore, we subsequently demonstrate
that the time scales and mechanism of folding extracted from our GDS-based
MSM agree with (and, indeed, improve upon) previous estimates.

Typically, τ is chosen to capture slow processes and equilibrate
fast processes; in other words, the lagtime τ should be selected
so that the system behaves in a Markovian fashion. However, no reliable
method currently exists to achieve this a priori.
We use a simple heuristic discussed in the Supporting Information, from which we find that 1.5 time-units was a suitable
decision.

Following selection of lagtime τ, we fit a reversible
Koopman
model using the unbiased Koopman reweighting algorithm. We then compared the two modelsspecifically
the model generated using GDS basis selection and the model generated
using a grid basisby evaluating the eigenvalues of the three
slowest modes (which, according to the variational principle, should
be maximized). We find that the model built with the GDS basis yields
eigenvalues of (0.748, 0.670, 0.590), in excellent agreement with
those found with using the complete space-covering basis (0.750, 0.672,
0.594). For a more detailed validation, we compared the three corresponding
eigenfunctions of both models (). Again, these are in excellent agreement with each
other; most importantly, the characteristic dynamics associated with
each reaction-coordinate (RC) are clearly aligned. For example, for
the slowest RC (A1 and B1), there is a clear “diagonal bias” in both
eigenmodes, indicating that the slowest RC reflects the presence of
the inner PES barrier to the folded-state () and the lowered energetic barriers in the
lower left-hand corner of the PES ().

To summarize, the results presented here for a rugged 2D model
of a protein-folding landscape demonstrate that the GDS framework
can be used to generate a compact and accurate basis for Markov model
construction. In other words, we have shown that efficient GDS simulations
canin principlereplace extensive MD trajectory sampling
in the search for a Markov basis. However, the broader utility of
GDS in this context must clearly be demonstrated for a more realistic,
higher-dimensional problem. In the following section, we demonstrate
that the success of GDS for model construction readily carries over
to constructing accurate Markov models for a fully atomistic model
of a folding protein in a solvent environment.

GDS-Constructed Markov
Model for WW Domain Folding

To demonstrate the wider applicability
of our GDS methodology in
the context of an actual protein-folding problem, we investigated
the folding of the GTT variant of the FIP35 WW Domain (a). This 35-residue protein
represents a prototypical folding example, being one of 12 “fast
folders” that have been studied by long-time scale MD simulations
using the dedicated ANTON computer. The
available MD data for this protein comprises two MD trajectories totalling
a simulation time of more than 1 ms. The folded state was chosen as
a configuration with a high value of the Q
DESRES parameter constructed in the initial ANTON study; this order parameter
measures structural similarity to the known folded state.

To proceed, we initiated 100 GDS
runs starting from a “null”
contact-map representing an entirely unfolded configuration of the
protein sequence; the contact-map of the folded protein was used as
the target end-point for all GDS trajectories. The GDS graph sequences
were chosen to be of length N = 10, inclusive of
unfolded and folded end points, and the target objective function
of used the parameter k
cont = 0.05. This choice was made based on Figure S2 in the Supporting Information (SI),
where it is clear that structures within this distance are kinetically
accessible. Our SA procedure employed 104 iterations of
single-contact perturbation proposal moves, with a Metropolis-Hasting
acceptance-rejection scheme starting with a temperature of 10–3 decreasing linearly (we note that the temperature
scale does not map on to typical physical temperatures because the
units and scale of the objective function in are somewhat arbitrary; instead, the initial
temperature used here was chosen using initial test simulations).
Each single-contact perturbation proposal involves randomly selecting
a graph from the current sequence, selecting a random contact for
“bit-flipping” (breaking or forming a contact), then
proceeding to attempt to reconstruct the proposed graph.

The resulting
contact-maps obtained from the GDS procedure sample
possible physical pathways of intermediate structures connecting the
unfolded and folded states. However, given the simple nature of , the sampled contact-maps
might correspond to structures that are not stable on the relevant
atomistic PES. To address this, we back-transformed from contact maps
to fully solvated protein structures in Cartesian space, and subsequently
allowed these structures to relax on the PES using short MD runs.
This process biases the generated path-ensemble toward sampling more
stable intermediates on the folding landscape. Here, intermediate
contact maps were ”back-mapped” into an all-atom, explicit
solvent system modeled via the GROMACS MD package using the CHARMM36m-TIP3P force-field.
,
 In this “back-mapping” procedure, we first obtain
a biased structures in the fully atomistic protein–water system
that is consistent with the current target contact map. This is achieved
by running a high temperature MD simulation with a strong V
GRP bias applied, resulting in quickly decorrelated
configurations that respect the GRP restraints. We subsequently cool
this configuration (again, still applying V
GRP bias) to obtain a more thermodynamically stable representative structure.
Finally, we remove V
GRP restraints and
perform a short MD simulation in which the representative solvated
protein structure relaxes further (noting that the contact-map is
now free to “drift” from the original target). Notably,
this simple procedure only requires around 1 ns of MD simulation time,
which itself only requires a few minutes of ‘real-world’
time. Further details of this procedure are given in the SI. In total, relaxing the 800 GDS intermediates
requires approximately 800 ns of aggregate MD. Importantly, these
simulations are not used to construct the basis itself, which is already
generated by GDS, but only to obtain locally relaxed atomistic representations.
In comparison, Kleiman and Shukla used approximately 1, μs of
aggregate MD in an iterative VAMPbased adaptive sampling scheme for
a similarly sized fast-folding protein. Despite showing significant improvements over previous schemes,
Kleiman and Shukla’s scheme was not able to explore more than
small conformational changes from the folded structure. The goal in
these schemes is similar to our goal here: constructing a basis such
that MD can be sampled efficiently. However, Kleiman and Shukla’s
results highlights both the key distinction and advantage of our GDS
scheme has over the iterative approaches; iterative approaches still
require non-negligible MD simulations to progressively learn the relevant
configurational representation, whereas GDS provides a suitable basis a priori with negligible MD costs.

In total, GDS sampling
yielded 800 physically relevant intermediate
contact-maps; this number was further pruned to 608 by removing closely
similar structures (specifically, a structure was pruned if it had
a SCH distance metric value of less than 0.1 to any other existing
structure). Finally, the overall Koopman model basis set was doubled
in size to 1216 basis functions by placing two basis functions () at each retained contact-map;
these basis functions used different γ-values of 0.1 and 0.2,
respectively. While not essential, we find that using this approach
ensures better coverage of configuration space and a more accurate
Koopman model.

We note that GDS does not rely on time scale
separation; instead,
GDS generates nonbiased folding pathways thatby constructionconnect
unfolded and target folded structures through pathways in “contact-map
space”. The concepts of rates and time-scales emerge after
GDS construction of a MSM basis; here, we obtain this kinetic data
using available ANTON MD trajectories as a proxy, enabling model comparison
against previous work. GDS does not guarantee sampling over slow dynamic
modes, but does (by construction) generate paths that definitively
fold from an unfolded structure to the final target structure. We
expect that such paths would a priori contain intermediate
structures associated with the slowest dynamic modes.

To understand this discrepancy, and provide a more complete comparison

by discretizing the space spanned by our GDS-informed Koopman modes.
Here, we partitioned our Koopman model data using regular space clustering, a Voronoi partitioning scheme that ensures that
no two Voronoi centers lie within a defined threshold. We chose the
kinetic threshold to be 40 ns as measured by the commute map computed using the five slowest modes found
from our Koopman model. This threshold was chosen to balance accuracy
with number of discrete states, whichin this caseamounted
to 232. An implied time scale (ITS) convergence plot is presented
in the Supporting Information, showing
that 100 ns is a suitable time scales.

From analysis of our

 even though our discretized MSM only uses 232 states rather than
the 1000-state model employed previously. This observation represents
a very strongly supportive result for our GDS-based strategy; the
variational principle for MSM construction suggests that an optimal
approximation of the transfer operator maximizes the time scales (subject
to use of a consistent lagtime), so the observation that our MSM exhibits
longer time scales than previous studies supports the GDS approach
for MSM construction proposed here.

The eigenvectors obtained
from our MSM may not necessarily correspond
to the Koopman modes identified earlier. To investigate whether the
two “slow” processes obtained in our Koopman model correspond
to folding-unfolding and trapping, respectively, we visualized the
corresponding MSM right-eigenvectors; these act as natural corresponding
reaction-coordinates in MSMs. As shown in , our interpretation of the slowest modes
from our Koopman models aligns well with our MSM. The eigenvector
associated with the slowest time scale (A) corresponds to the transition between
unfolded and folded protein states, whereas the eigenvector associated
with the second slowest time scale corresponds to transitions between
the trapped and nontrapped states (B). Furthermore, we see that our MSM identifies
the trapped state as the “kinetically furthest” unfolded
state away from the folded state; in other words, our MSM reflects
the observation that the trapped state is required to transition back
to an unfolded state before proceeding to the folded state, as one
would naturally expect.

Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced a new strategy for constructing
effective basis sets for Markov models by sampling discrete protein-folding
pathways, rather than relying on extensive MD simulations. For a fully
atomistic (and solvated) protein-folding problem, we have demonstrated
how a graph-based representation of the folding landscapeas
provided by the simple and efficient GDS algorithmcan be used
to identify key intermediate structures in contact-map space. This
approach bypasses the need for extensive, pre-existing MD data in
basis construction for Markov models, opening a route toward targeted
simulations or rate approximations to be performed more efficiently
once an informative basis has been established by GDS. We strongly

We first illustrated this strategy using a 2D model that is reminiscent
of a rugged protein-folding funnel. We then demonstrated the approach
for the folding the GTT variant of the FIP35 WW domain protein in
explicit water. The basis sets constructed using GDS were found to
provide an accurate and efficient representation of folding kinetics,
boosted by the use of the topologically informed SCH distance metric.
The resulting Koopman models and MSMs readily identify the important
slow dynamical processes, identify key folding intermediates, and
even reveal kinetic
traps that were not clearly resolved through ‘standard’
order parameters such as the fraction of native contacts. These findings
validate the effectiveness of GDS as a complex-landscape exploration
strategy, and provide strong evidence for the use of GDS in defining
a structurally meaningful basis for Markov models of protein folding.
We emphasize that the approach taken here is quite general, and could
equally be applied to study protein–protein interaction networks,
drug–protein binding, protein misfolding, and other complex
transient biophysical processes.

We emphasize again that our
focus here is explicitly on addressing
step (A)basis generation (or model construction)in
deploying MSMs, rather than kinetic parametrization (B). As such,
kinetic information for GDS-constructed few-state models is obtained
using the available ANTON trajectory data, enabling a direct comparison
to previous few-state models on a consistent kinetic footing. We restate
that, in future studies where a direct comparison is no longer necessary,
targetted enhanced sampling methods can utilized effectively with
a preconstructed basis.

By using the variational principle for
Markov processes, we have
shown that our model captures folding dynamics more accurately than
previous comparable studies of the GTT variant. In high-dimensional
systems, where MD sampling of the entire conformational space is typically
not feasible, leveraging our GDS-generated basis becomes advantageous
over iterative active sampling methods. Simply put, we can achieve
accurate coverage and kinetic connectivity from cheap GDS simulations,
rather than extensive MD simulations. A further interesting direction
for future research is to consider whether unbiased GDS simulations
could be used to automatically generate a set of reaction-coordinates
for further biased simulations based on metadynamics, for example,
potentially offering a further analysis route for complex free-energy
landscapes associated with protein folding and similar processes.
The contact map representation is well suited to enhanced sampling
for large conformational changes such as exploring intermediates for
drug targetting.

The path forward
from this work is clear; we now need to develop
efficient estimates of free energies and conformational transition
rates in the space of contact-maps to effectively extract mechanisms
and kinetics for the whole folding process, without relying on exhaustive
MD sampling. This is not an intractable problem; free energies of
contact maps have been studied previously.
,
 Emerging methods for computing committor functions can make calculation
of neighboring-contact-map transition rates fast and accessible.
,
 Koopman reweighting is now being applied to enhanced sampling trajectories, which can be utilized effectively with a preconstructed
basis. Combining such approaches with GDS is an exciting avenue for
future work.
