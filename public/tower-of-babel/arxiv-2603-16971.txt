Median-Extremes Alternation Permutations: Initial Structural Results
David Carr

Abstract
We define a deterministic family of permutations generated by an alternating center-edge extraction process on the ordered set [ n ] = { 1 , 2 , … , n } [n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\} . Starting from the ordered list ( 1 , 2 , … , n ) (1,2,\dots,n) , one repeatedly removes the median element or elements of the current list, then removes its extreme elements, alternating these two operations until the list is exhausted. The resulting output is a permutation π n ∈ S n \pi_{n}\in S_{n} , which we call the Median-Extremes Alternation (MEA) permutation. Although the construction is elementary, the resulting permutations exhibit unexpectedly rigid combinatorial structure. We prove that π n \pi_{n} is always an alternating permutation, with parity-dependent alternating type. As a consequence, its descent set is completely determined by the parity of n n . We also prove an exact formula for the inversion number, inv ⁡ ( π n ) = ⌊ ( n − 1 ) 2 4 ⌋ , \operatorname{inv}(\pi_{n})=\left\lfloor\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor, which immediately yields a characterization of the sign of π n \pi_{n} . In addition, we give an exact recursive description of the family and a recursive formula for the inverse permutation.



1.  Introduction

This note studies a deterministic family of permutations generated by a simple alternating extraction rule. Begin with the ordered list
(1,2,\dots,n)
. First remove the middle element or elements of the current list; then remove the smallest and largest remaining elements; then repeat these two operations in alternation until no values remain. Reading the removed values in order produces a permutation of
[n]
.

We call this family the Median-Extremes Alternation (MEA) permutations. The rule is computationally trivial, but the resulting objects are far more structured than one might initially expect. In particular, the MEA permutations fall into an alternating pattern determined entirely by the parity of
n
, and several standard permutation statistics admit clean exact formulas.

The purpose of this note is to formalize the MEA construction and record its first structural properties. The main results are:

1.

an exact recursive description of
\pi_{n}
, split by parity;

2.

a proof that
\pi_{n}
is alternating;

3.

an explicit description of the descent set of
\pi_{n}
;

4.

the inversion formula

\operatorname{inv}(\pi_{n})=\left\lfloor\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor;

5.

the induced characterization of the sign of
\pi_{n}
;

6.

an exact recursive description of
\pi_{n}^{-1}
.

The recursive description is the central proof engine throughout: each MEA permutation consists of a short parity-dependent prefix followed by a rank-shifted copy of a smaller MEA permutation.

The family belongs naturally to the landscape of alternating permutations. Its inversion numbers coincide with the quarter-squares sequence

0,0,1,2,4,6,9,12,16,20,25,30,\dots,

recorded in OEIS as sequence A002620. We make no claim to an exhaustive survey of related constructions; rather, the point of this note is to isolate a simple deterministic rule that produces a rigid alternating family with exact recursive structure and an explicit inversion formula.

2.  Definition of the MEA permutation

For each integer
n\geq 1
, let
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, and let

R_{0}=(1,2,\dots,n)

denote the ordered list of remaining values.

We define the Median-Extremes Alternation permutation
\pi_{n}\in S_{n}
by the following procedure. At every stage, the remaining values are kept in increasing order.

On a median step:

•

if the current list has odd length
2k+1
, append its unique middle element;

•

if the current list has even length
2k
, append its two middle elements in left-to-right order.

Then remove the appended element or elements from the list.

On an extreme step:

•

append first the minimum remaining value and then the maximum remaining value;

•

remove both values from the list.

The process begins with a median step and then alternates median and extreme steps until no values remain. The resulting output sequence is the permutation
\pi_{n}
, written in one-line notation.

Remark
.

Because the process begins with a median step, every extreme step is applied to a list of even length. Hence the extreme step always removes exactly two values.

Examples.
The first few MEA permutations are

\pi_{1}=[1],\quad\pi_{2}=[1,2],\quad\pi_{3}=[2,1,3],\quad\pi_{4}=[2,3,1,4],\quad\pi_{5}=[3,1,5,2,4],

and continuing,

\pi_{6}=[3,4,1,6,2,5],\quad\pi_{7}=[4,1,7,3,5,2,6],\quad\pi_{8}=[4,5,1,8,3,6,2,7].

3.  Exact recursive structure

The family
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
admits an exact recursive decomposition by parity.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\pi_{n}
denote the MEA permutation of
[n]
.

If
n=2m+1
is odd with
m\geq 1
, then

\pi_{2m+1}=[m+1,1,2m+1]\,\|\,T_{2m+1},

where
T_{2m+1}
is obtained from
\pi_{2m-2}
by replacing each entry
r
with

\begin{cases}r+1&\text{if }r\leq m-1,\\
r+2&\text{if }r\geq m.\end{cases}

If
n=2m
is even with
m\geq 2
, then

\pi_{2m}=[m,m+1,1,2m]\,\|\,T_{2m},

where
T_{2m}
is obtained from
\pi_{2m-4}
by replacing each entry
r
with

\begin{cases}r+1&\text{if }r\leq m-2,\\
r+3&\text{if }r\geq m-1.\end{cases}

Here
\|
denotes concatenation.

Proof.

We begin with the odd case. Let
n=2m+1
. The initial list is

(1,2,\dots,m,m+1,m+2,\dots,2m,2m+1).

The first step is a median step, so the unique middle element
m+1
is appended. Removing it leaves

(1,2,\dots,m,m+2,\dots,2m,2m+1).

The next step is an extreme step, so the minimum and maximum remaining elements, namely
1
and
2m+1
, are appended and removed. Thus the permutation begins with

[m+1,1,2m+1].

The remaining values are

(2,3,\dots,m,m+2,m+3,\dots,2m),

which form an ordered list of length
2m-2
. Relabel these remaining values in increasing order by

2\mapsto 1,\ 3\mapsto 2,\ \dots,\ m\mapsto m-1,

and

m+2\mapsto m,\ m+3\mapsto m+1,\ \dots,\ 2m\mapsto 2m-2.

Under this relabeling, the induced process on the remaining list is exactly the MEA process defining
\pi_{2m-2}
. Undoing the relabeling sends an entry
r
of
\pi_{2m-2}
to

\begin{cases}r+1&\text{if }r\leq m-1,\\
r+2&\text{if }r\geq m.\end{cases}

This proves the odd recursion.

Now let
n=2m
. The initial list is

(1,2,\dots,m,m+1,\dots,2m).

The first step is a median step on an even-length list, so the two middle elements
m
and
m+1
are appended in left-to-right order. Removing them leaves

(1,2,\dots,m-1,m+2,\dots,2m).

The next step is an extreme step, so the minimum and maximum remaining elements, namely
1
and
2m
, are appended and removed. Thus the permutation begins with

[m,m+1,1,2m].

The remaining values are

(2,3,\dots,m-1,m+2,m+3,\dots,2m-1),

which form an ordered list of length
2m-4
. Relabel these remaining values in increasing order by

2\mapsto 1,\ 3\mapsto 2,\ \dots,\ m-1\mapsto m-2,

and

m+2\mapsto m-1,\ m+3\mapsto m,\ \dots,\ 2m-1\mapsto 2m-4.

Again the induced process is exactly the MEA process defining
\pi_{2m-4}
. Undoing the relabeling sends an entry
r
of
\pi_{2m-4}
to

\begin{cases}r+1&\text{if }r\leq m-2,\\
r+3&\text{if }r\geq m-1.\end{cases}

This proves the even recursion.
∎

Remark
.

Proposition 3.1 is the main structural mechanism behind the subsequent results. Each MEA permutation is built from a short parity-dependent prefix followed by a rank-shifted copy of a smaller MEA permutation.

4.  Alternating structure and descent set

We now show that every MEA permutation is alternating, with alternating type determined solely by the parity of
n
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For odd
n
, the permutation
\pi_{n}
is alternating of down-up type:

\pi_{n}(1)>\pi_{n}(2)<\pi_{n}(3)>\pi_{n}(4)<\pi_{n}(5)>\cdots.

For even
n
, the permutation
\pi_{n}
is alternating of up-down type:

\pi_{n}(1)<\pi_{n}(2)>\pi_{n}(3)<\pi_{n}(4)>\pi_{n}(5)<\cdots.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
. The claims are immediate for
n=1,2
, and direct inspection gives

\pi_{3}=[2,1,3],\qquad\pi_{4}=[2,3,1,4],

which satisfy the stated alternating patterns. Since the odd recursion reduces
\pi_{2m+1}
to
\pi_{2m-2}
and the even recursion reduces
\pi_{2m}
to
\pi_{2m-4}
, the cases
n=1,2,3,4
serve as base cases for both parity chains.

Assume the theorem holds for all smaller values arising in Proposition 3.1.
First let
n=2m+1
be odd. By Proposition 3.1,

\pi_{2m+1}=[m+1,1,2m+1]\,\|\,T_{2m+1},

where
T_{2m+1}
is a rank-shifted copy of
\pi_{2m-2}
. Since
m+1>1
and
1<2m+1
, the first two inequalities are correct. The permutation
\pi_{2m-2}
is even, so by the inductive hypothesis it is alternating of up-down type. Rank-shifting preserves all order relations, so the tail
T_{2m+1}
is also alternating of up-down type. Its first two entries are the images of the first two entries of
\pi_{2m-2}
, namely
m
and
m+2
, so

2m+1>m<m+2.

Thus the alternating pattern continues correctly across the join between the prefix and tail, and thereafter throughout the tail. Hence
\pi_{2m+1}
is alternating of down-up type.

Now let
n=2m
be even. By Proposition 3.1,

\pi_{2m}=[m,m+1,1,2m]\,\|\,T_{2m},

where
T_{2m}
is a rank-shifted copy of
\pi_{2m-4}
. The prefix satisfies

m<m+1>1<2m.

Again
\pi_{2m-4}
is even, so by the inductive hypothesis it is alternating of up-down type, and rank-shifting preserves this. The first two entries of the tail are the images of the first two entries of
\pi_{2m-4}
, namely
m-1
and
m+2
, so

2m>m-1<m+2.

Therefore the alternating pattern continues across the join and throughout the tail. Hence
\pi_{2m}
is alternating of up-down type.
This completes the induction.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

The descent set of
\pi_{n}
is given by

\operatorname{Des}(\pi_{n})=\{1,3,5,\dots,n-2\}\quad\text{if $n$ is odd,}

and

\operatorname{Des}(\pi_{n})=\{2,4,6,\dots,n-2\}\quad\text{if $n$ is even.}

In particular,

|\operatorname{Des}(\pi_{n})|=\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor.

Proof.

This is immediate from Theorem 4.1: descents occur exactly at the positions where the alternating inequalities point downward.
∎

5.  Inversion number and sign

We now compute the inversion number of
\pi_{n}
exactly.

Theorem 5.1
.

For every
n\geq 1
,

\operatorname{inv}(\pi_{n})=\left\lfloor\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor.

Equivalently,

\operatorname{inv}(\pi_{2m+1})=m^{2},\qquad\operatorname{inv}(\pi_{2m})=m(m-1).

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
. Direct computation gives

I(1)=0,\qquad I(2)=0,\qquad I(3)=1,\qquad I(4)=2.

Let
I(n)=\operatorname{inv}(\pi_{n})
.

First let
n=2m+1
be odd. By Proposition 3.1,

\pi_{2m+1}=[m+1,1,2m+1]\,\|\,T_{2m+1},

where
T_{2m+1}
is a rank-shifted copy of
\pi_{2m-2}
. Since rank-shifting preserves inversion number,

\operatorname{inv}(T_{2m+1})=I(2m-2).

We count inversions in three pieces: inversions inside the prefix, cross-inversions between the prefix and tail, and inversions inside the tail.

Within the prefix
[m+1,1,2m+1]
, there is exactly one inversion, namely
(m+1,1)
.
For cross-inversions (the tail contains the values
\{2,3,\dots,m,m+2,\dots,2m\}
):

•

the entry
m+1
exceeds exactly the
m-1
lower values in the tail;

•

the entry
1
exceeds no tail entry;

•

the entry
2m+1
exceeds every tail entry, of which there are
2m-2
.

Thus the total number of cross-inversions is

(m-1)+0+(2m-2)=3m-3.

Therefore

I(2m+1)=1+(3m-3)+I(2m-2)=3m-2+I(2m-2).

By the inductive hypothesis,

I(2m-2)=(m-1)(m-2)=m^{2}-3m+2.

Hence

I(2m+1)=3m-2+(m^{2}-3m+2)=m^{2}.

Now let
n=2m
be even. By Proposition 3.1,

\pi_{2m}=[m,m+1,1,2m]\,\|\,T_{2m},

where
T_{2m}
is a rank-shifted copy of
\pi_{2m-4}
. Again,

\operatorname{inv}(T_{2m})=I(2m-4).

Within the prefix
[m,m+1,1,2m]
, there are exactly two inversions, namely
(m,1)
and
(m+1,1)
.
For cross-inversions (the tail contains the values
\{2,3,\dots,m-1,m+2,\dots,2m-1\}
):

•

the entry
m
exceeds exactly the
m-2
lower values in the tail;

•

the entry
m+1
also exceeds exactly the
m-2
lower values in the tail;

•

the entry
1
exceeds no tail entry;

•

the entry
2m
exceeds every tail entry, of which there are
2m-4
.

Thus the total number of cross-inversions is

(m-2)+(m-2)+0+(2m-4)=4m-8.

Therefore

I(2m)=2+(4m-8)+I(2m-4)=4m-6+I(2m-4).

By the inductive hypothesis,

I(2m-4)=(m-2)(m-3)=m^{2}-5m+6.

Hence

I(2m)=4m-6+(m^{2}-5m+6)=m^{2}-m=m(m-1).

This completes the induction.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

The sign of
\pi_{n}
is

\operatorname{sgn}(\pi_{n})=(-1)^{\left\lfloor\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor}.

In particular,
\pi_{n}
is an odd permutation if and only if

n\equiv 3\pmod{4}.

Proof.

The sign of a permutation is
(-1)^{\operatorname{inv}(\pi_{n})}
, so the first statement follows immediately from Theorem 5.1. For the congruence characterization, write
n
modulo
4
:

•

if
n=4k
, then
\operatorname{inv}(\pi_{n})=2k(2k-1)
, which is even;

•

if
n=4k+1
, then
\operatorname{inv}(\pi_{n})=(2k)^{2}
, which is even;

•

if
n=4k+2
, then
\operatorname{inv}(\pi_{n})=(2k+1)(2k)
, which is even;

•

if
n=4k+3
, then
\operatorname{inv}(\pi_{n})=(2k+1)^{2}
, which is odd.

Hence
\pi_{n}
is odd if and only if
n\equiv 3\pmod{4}
.
∎

6.  Recursive description of the inverse permutation

The same recursive decomposition yields an exact description of the inverse permutation.

Proposition 6.1
.

The initial inverse permutations are

\pi_{1}^{-1}=[1],\qquad\pi_{2}^{-1}=[1,2],\qquad\pi_{3}^{-1}=[2,1,3],\qquad\pi_{4}^{-1}=[3,1,2,4].

For
n\geq 5
, let
n=2m+1
be odd. Then
\pi_{n}^{-1}
is determined by

\pi_{n}^{-1}(m+1)=1,\qquad\pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=2,\qquad\pi_{n}^{-1}(n)=3,

and for all other
j\in[n]
,

\pi_{n}^{-1}(j)=3+\pi_{2m-2}^{-1}(r),

where

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=j-1

\displaystyle\text{if }2\leq j\leq m,

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=j-2

\displaystyle\text{if }m+2\leq j\leq n-1.

Let
n=2m
be even. Then
\pi_{n}^{-1}
is determined by

\pi_{n}^{-1}(m)=1,\qquad\pi_{n}^{-1}(m+1)=2,\qquad\pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=3,\qquad\pi_{n}^{-1}(n)=4,

and for all other
j\in[n]
,

\pi_{n}^{-1}(j)=4+\pi_{2m-4}^{-1}(r),

where

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=j-1

\displaystyle\text{if }2\leq j\leq m-1,

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=j-3

\displaystyle\text{if }m+2\leq j\leq n-1.

Proof.

This follows directly by inverting the relabeling maps in Proposition 3.1. In the odd case, the prefix entries
m+1
,
1
, and
n
occupy positions
1
,
2
, and
3
, respectively, and every other value belongs to the tail obtained from
\pi_{2m-2}
by the relabeling

r\mapsto r+1\quad(r\leq m-1),\qquad r\mapsto r+2\quad(r\geq m).

Inverting this relabeling gives the stated formula, with all tail positions shifted by three.

In the even case, the prefix entries
m
,
m+1
,
1
, and
n
occupy positions
1
,
2
,
3
, and
4
, respectively, and every other value belongs to the tail obtained from
\pi_{2m-4}
by the relabeling

r\mapsto r+1\quad(r\leq m-2),\qquad r\mapsto r+3\quad(r\geq m-1).

Inverting this relabeling gives the stated formula, with all tail positions shifted by four.
∎

7.  Conclusion

The Median-Extremes Alternation construction produces a deterministic family of permutations with unexpectedly rigid structure. The family admits an exact recursive description, belongs to the class of alternating permutations, has a parity-determined descent set, and satisfies the simple inversion formula

\operatorname{inv}(\pi_{n})=\left\lfloor\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{4}\right\rfloor.

These properties together make MEA a natural object for further combinatorial study.

Several directions remain open. One may seek a direct nonrecursive formula for
\pi_{n}
and
\pi_{n}^{-1}
, a characterization of the cycle structure of
\pi_{n}
, the order of
\pi_{n}
under iteration, and a more detailed comparison with other distinguished subclasses or constructions of alternating permutations. Another question is whether the inversion graphs associated with the MEA family admit a useful extremal interpretation beyond the quarter-squares count itself.

We emphasize that the present note is deliberately focused in scope. Its goal is to isolate the MEA construction and establish its first exact properties, rather than to claim an exhaustive classification of all related permutation families.
