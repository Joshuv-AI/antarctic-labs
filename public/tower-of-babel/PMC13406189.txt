Characterizing cutaneous manifestations of systemic autoimmune rheumatologic diseases in Filipino skin
Authors: Geraldine T. Zamora, Josef Symon S. Concha, Juan Raphael M. Gonzales, Arunee H. Siripunvarapon, Amanda Christine F. Esquivel, Marian Rosel D. Villaverde, Anna Kristina Gutierrez-Rubio, Mae Ramirez-Quizon, Jemima Albayda, Rochelle L. Castillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Cutaneous findings provide important clues to systemic autoimmune rheumatologic diseases (SARDs), but presentation varies across skin types and may be underrecognized in patients of color. In the Philippines, diagnostic challenges are amplified by limited specialists and a lack of pigmented-skin learning materials for general practitioners.Objective:To develop online educational resources on cutaneous manifestations of SARDs in Filipino skin (phases 1 and 2a), and characterize the demographic and clinical profile of these patients (phase 2b).Methods:Phase 1 videos were created and posted online for sharing. Phase 2 was a single-center cross-sectional descriptive study that included consenting adult Filipinos at least 18 years old who presented with the above-mentioned SARDs and skin lesions in the Philippine General Hospital’s Dermatology and Rheumatology outpatient clinics from August 2021 to August 2022. Demographic, medical data, and deidentified photographic images of characteristic lesions were captured. Investigators described the observed differences in the skin disease presentation of Filipino skin. Descriptive statistics were applied.Results:Forty-six Filipinos, 85% being female, with a mean age of 39.1 years, participated in our study. The majority (48%) had a primary diagnosis of systemic lupus erythematosus, followed by psoriasis (26%). Most (96.6%) were on immunomodulators and 57% on glucocorticoids. Three (7%) were on biologic medications. Notable differences of lesions between Filipino and Caucasian skin include the former being: (a) dark red, purple or dark brown instead of pink and bright erythema; (b) masked by pigmentation or the “blending” phenomenon; (c) more marked in contrast between hyper- and hypopigmentation; (d) more gray and black psoriatic plaques; resembling purpuric dermatoses; and (e) with blurring of skin tautness and edema in those with systemic sclerosis. There are no significant differences observed in the areas of affectation.Limitations:Phase 2 was limited by the pandemic and methodological constraints, highlighting the need for further research on cutaneous manifestations of rheumatologic diseases in Filipinos.Conclusion:There are differences in lesion morphology, pigmentation, and scarring, but not in areas of affectation, in the darker skin of Filipinos with SARD. Collaboration between dermatologists and rheumatologists is essential for the effective management of these patients.

Introduction and objectives

Cutaneous manifestations frequently signal the presence of a systemic autoimmune rheumatologic disease (SARD). While some skin signs, such as the malar rash of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), are considered easily recognizable, the spectrum of possible cutaneous findings remains vast, requiring a trained eye and years of experience. The appearance of skin lesions may vary depending on the skin type, as well as differences in culture and habit.1 For example, patients of color with psoriasis (PsO) are more likely to remain undiagnosed,2 partly due to discrepancies between textbook descriptions and actual presentation on Asian skin. Thus, there is a need to highlight the differences in the skin manifestations of SARDs across the spectrum of skin types and to educate physicians on these differences.3

In the Philippines, a geographically-dispersed country with a population close to 110 million, this challenge is further compounded by a shortage of physicians, with roughly 1 rheumatologist serving half a million Filipinos and 1 dermatologist per 100,000 of the population; the absence of a rheumatology-dermatology subspecialty group; Filipinos’ heterogeneous racial background consisting of varying4 degrees of Austronesian, Malay, Chinese, and Spanish ancestry, leading to a wide range of cutaneous phenotypes and skin tones with distinctively-appearing lesions; and finally, the lack of a formal registry to record and analyze these unique skin findings. We therefore aimed to characterize the cutaneous manifestations of SARDs in adult Filipinos for instructional purposes. Phase 1 aimed to produce 5 online shareable educational videos about skin manifestations among Filipinos with SLE, psoriatic arthritis (PsA), systemic sclerosis (SS), primary vasculitis, and dermatomyositis (DM). Phase 2a aimed to establish a photo database, while Phase 2b aimed to describe the demographic and clinical profiles of these patients.

Methods

Phase 1 involved making a script and creating animation and voice recordings for each video, followed by posting in official social media accounts of the Department of Dermatology and the Division of Rheumatology of the University of the Philippines – Philippine General Hospital (UP-PGH).

Phase 2 involved a single-center cross-sectional descriptive study that characterized skin lesions in adult Filipino patients with systemic inflammatory rheumatologic conditions in the UP-PGH Dermatology and Rheumatology outpatient clinics from August 2021 to August 2022. Patients at least 18 years old with skin lesions ascertained by the clinician-investigator using clinical judgment and a diagnosis of SLE, SS, DM, PsA, and primary vasculitides were recruited. The first 3 were through the 2019, 2013, and 2017 American College of Rheumatology - European League Against Rheumatism criteria, respectively.5–7 PsO was diagnosed clinically or through biopsy the 2006 Classification Criteria for Psoriatic Arthritis8 was used to diagnose PsA. Medical records were used to identify clinical and laboratory data, including race; age; sex at birth; presence or absence of predetermined morphologic/clinical features; the inflammatory rheumatologic diagnosis of the patient; and current treatment. Images of characteristic lesions were compiled in a secure and encrypted photo database. A free-text field for investigator descriptive assessments was included in the case report forms to allow the investigators to describe the observed differences in skin disease presentation of Filipino skin. The presence of predetermined morphologic features in established cutaneous criteria was compared with the written cutaneous exam findings of the investigators that freely described the lesions seen in the Filipino cohort. Descriptive statistics were applied.

Results

Phase 1 videos are posted online and may be viewed and shared through links (Supplementary Appendix S1, https://links.lww.com/IJWD/A87).

For Phase 2, 46 patients consented to have their data gathered and photographs taken. The mean age was 39.1 years with 85% being female. The majority (48%) had a primary diagnosis of SLE, followed by PsA (26%), DM (9%), and diffuse SS (4%). Eleven (24%) consented to a skin biopsy. Only 2 patients who were newly diagnosed with psoriatic disease were not on immunomodulators. More than half (57%) of the patients were on systemic glucocorticoids, while 1 (with localized morphea) was on topical glucocorticoids. Three (7%) were on biologic medications. Observations in Filipino skin disease presentations are compiled in Table 2. Key differences were described in disease activity (erythema, scale) presentation, pigmentation changes, and scarring or fibrosis markers. Pink and bright-red erythema, common in Caucasian skin disease, presents as dark-red erythema, violaceous, or dark brown in Filipino skin. There is also masking of erythema by pigmentation, which could be seen as a “blending” phenomenon. The contrast between hyperpigmentation and hypopigmentation in poikilodermatous lesions is more marked in Filipino skin. There are gray and black plaques in Filipinos with PsO compared to the bright-red silvery scales in Caucasian skin. In vasculitis, the prominence of pigmentation resembles more pigmented forms of purpuric dermatosis, which is a close differential. In scleroderma, the intensity of pigmentation in Filipino skin blurs the tautness of the skin and edema. The distribution and predilection sites of lesions remained consistent across both dark and light skin types, that is, there are no significant differences observed in the affected areas of the body. Discussion

Skin manifestations are often the first and most prominent signs of an underlying inflammatory rheumatologic condition and thus serve invaluable clues to facilitating timely diagnosis and management.9 However, what trainees learn from textbooks is usually based on Caucasian skin. There is also the challenge of interrater reliability, which tends to be high (W = 0.835) when grading skin manifestations of DM among different skin tones. Patients with lighter skin tones were represented in a higher number of DM-related educational materials compared with patients with darker skin tones.10 There is no available data describing the epidemiology and the variation in the clinical presentation of skin disease in lupus and DM in the Southeast Asian region or patients with pigmented skin.

We found differences in lesion morphology, pigmentation, and scarring in darker Filipino skin versus lighter skin types, but distribution remains similar and can be a key clue in the diagnosis. Cutaneous manifestations of rheumatologic diseases present differently in Filipino skin compared to lighter skin tones, primarily due to variations in skin pigmentation. In Filipino skin, classic erythema tends to appear more subtle and may present as violaceous or hyperpigmented lesions rather than the typical erythematous appearance seen in lighter skin types. Additionally, Filipino skin is prone to marked hyperpigmentation or postinflammatory hyperpigmentation following the resolution of active lesions, such as the malar rash of acute cutaneous lupus erythematosus or discoid lupus. This hyperpigmentation can obscure underlying erythema, complicating diagnosis. Secondary features like scaling remain prominent but may contrast less distinctly against darker skin, further contributing to diagnostic challenges. A high index of suspicion is needed for early diagnosis, and dermoscopy or biopsy findings may be more frequently required to confirm the diagnosis.

There are significant differences in the presentation and severity of PsO, as well as the impact on quality of life among racial/ethnic groups. As in our patients, PsO in dark-skinned individuals presents with less distinguishable erythema and increased risk of hyperpigmentation compared to light-skinned individuals. There are also thicker plaques, more scaling, and greater body surface involvement in the latter. These differences may be attributed to genetics and socio-economic factors.

Limitation

The data collection for Phase 2, albeit with a duration of 1 year, was hindered by the pandemic and the limitation of face-to-face outpatient consultations due to the UP-PGH being converted into the country’s national COVID referral center. Standardized cutaneous disease severity measures were not performed as the majority of data collectors have not yet received formal training in the application of these tools. A future study that would validate the interrater reliability and clinical usefulness and applicability of these cutaneous disease severity measures, particularly in skin of color, could be very valuable to the field. Most of the patients were already receiving treatment, which could affect lesion morphology. Disease duration was not taken into account as early disease could present differently than late or refractory disease. Additionally, we were not able to account for antibody stratification in patients with myositis since a comprehensive myositis antibody panel still was not available in the country during data collection time, as this could also influence different presentations. It is recommended to continue gathering and documenting cutaneous manifestations among Filipinos with rheumatologic conditions to improve knowledge and awareness on the differences of these from Caucasian skin available in textbooks and most literature of study.

Conclusions

Forty-six Filipinos, 85% being female, with a mean age of 39.1 years, participated in our study. The majority (48%) had a primary diagnosis of SLE, followed by PsA (26%). Compared with Caucasian or lighter skin, there are differences in lesion morphology, pigmentation, and scarring in darker, Filipino skin; however area of distribution remains similar. Pigmentation may coexist with active dermatological conditions, such as erythema, potentially complicating the diagnosis and assessment of skin disease activity, leading to inadequate treatment. It remains uncertain whether pigmentation serves solely as a marker of previous disease activity or if it reflects ongoing skin pathology in individuals with darker skin tones. A skilled clinician’s evaluation is crucial; collaboration between dermatologists and rheumatologists is essential for the timely and effective management of these patients. There is a need to conduct larger studies with broader geographic and racial diversity in patients with pigmented skin.
