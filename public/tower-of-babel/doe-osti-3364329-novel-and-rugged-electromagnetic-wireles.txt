 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Novel and rugged electromagnetic wireless system for long-term subsurface CO2 plume monitoring and
saturation mapping is a Phase I SBIR project led by Cyentech Consulting LLC to develop a permanently
installed, outside-casing electromagnetic monitoring system for long-term carbon storage monitoring.
The project addresses a major challenge in carbon capture and storage: the need for reliable, cable-free
downhole monitoring that can detect CO2 plume movement and saturation changes without
compromising well integrity through casing perforations or external umbilical cables.

The proposed system uses compact EM modules mounted externally on steel casing in injection and
monitoring wells. Each module integrates a multi-turn loop antenna for formation sensing, a toroidal
antenna for casing-coupled wireless communication and power transfer, supercapacitors for energy
storage, microcontrollers, and rugged packaging for harsh downhole environments. The system is
designed to operate at depths of approximately 2,600 to 12,000 ft with a target lifetime exceeding 10
years. In Phase I, the project achieved the following:

 • Loop-Antenna Sensing: Developed COMSOL-based electromagnetic models and an efficient
 simulation framework to evaluate outside-casing loop antennas for CO₂ plume and saturation
 monitoring.
 • Wireless Communication: Designed and tested toroidal-antenna-based casing-coupled telemetry,
 including laboratory demonstrations using Universal Software Radio Peripheral (USRP) hardware
 and Quadrature Phase-Shift Keying (QPSK) modulation.
 • Wireless Power Transfer: Evaluated energized-casing power delivery and demonstrated that
 cement isolation can significantly improve received signal strength and power-transfer efficiency.
 • System Integration Path: Defined the architecture for rugged control electronics, power
 management, and compact downhole modules suitable for future integrated prototype
 development.

This Phase I effort demonstrated the technical feasibility of a cable-free EM sensing, communication, and
charging platform for long-term CO₂ storage monitoring. The developed technology can reduce
monitoring costs, preserve well integrity, improve confidence in carbon storage safety, and support future
applications in geothermal energy, oil and gas, nuclear waste disposal, and subsurface regulatory
monitoring.

1. OVERVIEW
1.1 Background
Carbon capture and storage (CCS) is a critical technology to mitigate the effects of CO2 emissions and slow
the progression of global warming. Sequestration in sedimentary formations is the most mature
technology for the long-term storage of CO2 in subsurface pore space. In the International Energy Agency
Clean Technology Scenario (IEA, 2020), cumulatively more than 100 GtCO2 should be permanently stored
by 2060, requiring a significant scale-up of CO2 storage from today’s level at 42-49 MtCO2/year (Global
CCS Institute, 2024) and creating a huge market opportunity (see Figure 1).

  Figure 1: The global carbon capture and storage market was valued at USD 2.72 billion in 2023. It is predicted to
 reach around USD 15.88 billion by 2033, growing at a solid compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 19.30% from
 2024 to 2033 (Precedence Research Reports, 2024).

CCS faces significant challenges, particularly around the safe, long-term storage of CO2. The high costs of
capturing, transporting, and storing CO2 make widespread adoption difficult, and the risk of leakage,
though small, must be carefully managed. Over the years, great efforts have been made to various develop
geophysical methods for subsurface CO2 monitoring, for example, seismic (repeated 3D surface seismic,
surface-to- borehole vertical seismic, borehole-to-borehole cross-well seismic profiling, etc.), distributed
acoustic sensing (DAS), electromagnetic (controlled-source electromagnetic, magnetotellurics, borehole
resistivity logging, cross well electrical resistivity tomography, etc.), and gravity methods (Yang, 2019;
Gasperikova, 2022). Among those geophysical methods, electromagnetic (EM) techniques are of
particular interest because they have the potential to provide a more precise measure of CO2 saturation.
However, the spatial resolution of subsurface EM measurements is in general low due to the diffusive
properties of EM waves in lossy underground formations. Besides, the highly conductive metallic casings
oftentimes become a major obstacle to achieving high resolution and high precision subsurface sensing if
EM measurements are taken inside casings.
Embedding electrical or electromagnetic (EM) sensors in the cement outside the well casing can help
reduce interference from the metallic casing and enhance EM signal detection (Zeng, 2017; Bergmann,
2012). However, transmitting monitoring data from deep subsurface environments to the surface poses
significant challenges. Traditionally, this data is sent via an umbilical cable, either inside or outside the
well casing. The internal option requires perforating the casing, which compromises well integrity.

                                                                                                       5|Page

Alternatively, placing the cable outside the casing within the cement annulus risks creating a potential
pathway for CO2 leakage (Freifeld, 2009).
To overcome these challenges using EM methods, during Phase I, the development of a novel and rugged
EM wireless sensing, communication, and charging system designed for long-term monitoring of carbon
storage sites is initiated and studied. This system combines deep subsurface high-resolution EM sensing
with robust wireless data transmission and charging. As depicted in Figure 2, a series of EM modules are
permanently mounted externally on the metallic casing of both injection and monitoring wells. Each EM
module consists of a multi-turn loop coil antenna, toroidal coil antenna, supercapacitors, microcontrollers,
and a protective case engineered to endure the harsh downhole environment. The multi-turn loop coil
antenna will generate electromagnetic waves that propagate through the surrounding geological
formations. These signals will be received by the loop coil antenna in other EM modules on the same well
or adjacent monitoring wells. The toroidal coil antenna serves dual purposes—receiving and transmitting
data along the conductive casing, enabling long-distance bidirectional communication between the
surface and deep subsurface. Data gathered from the subsurface can be transmitted wirelessly to the
surface, where it can be processed locally or uploaded to the cloud for remote access and analysis.
Meanwhile, control commands of the EM modules can be sent from the surface to the downhole. To
maintain long-term system functionality, an energized-casing-based wireless power transfer technology
will be employed, regularly charging the downhole sensors, antennas, and control electronics in the deep
subsurface, ensuring reliable and uninterrupted operation. Electrical power is transmitted from the
wellhead through the conductive casing, with a return electrode positioned at a distance to complete the
circuit. A small portion of this transmitted power is harvested by each EM module to charge its
supercapacitors, which then power the antennas and microcontrollers within the EM module. The EM
module is designed to ensure that all components can withstand the high-temperature and high-pressure
conditions present at depths of 2,600 to 12,000 feet, with a lifespan exceeding 10 years. It can be installed
during the construction of production wells or in dedicated monitoring wells, providing a flexible and
reliable solution for long-term carbon storage site monitoring.
During Phase I, we demonstrated the feasibility of the proposed system and 1) studied and optimized the
multi-turn loop antenna for electromagnetic sensing in underground environments; 2) studied and
optimized the toroidal antennas for the wireless communication system; 3) studied the energized-casing
system for the wireless charging system, and 4) initiated the design of compact control electronics for the
entire wireless system.
1.2 Loop-Antenna Electromagnetic Sensing for Long-Term CO₂ Plume Monitoring
As shown in Figure 2, the proposed monitoring system uses a series of rugged electromagnetic modules
permanently mounted outside the metallic casing of injection and monitoring wells. The primary sensing
component of each module is a multi-turn loop antenna wound around the casing and embedded within
the cement annulus. Unlike conventional downhole monitoring systems that require electrical cables,
casing perforations, or sensors placed inside the production tubing, the outside-casing loop-antenna
configuration is designed to preserve well integrity while enabling long-term electromagnetic monitoring
of the surrounding storage formation.
The loop antenna operates as an inductive transmitter and receiver. When energized by an alternating
current, the transmitting loop generates low-frequency electromagnetic fields that diffuse through the
casing, cement, and surrounding geological formations. Receiver loops installed at other depths or in
adjacent wells measure the resulting magnetic-field response. Because the electrical resistivity of the
reservoir changes as saline brine is displaced by CO₂, these loop-to-loop measurements provide a basis
for detecting CO₂ plume migration and estimating saturation changes through time-lapse electromagnetic

A major Phase I focus was to evaluate whether loop antennas mounted outside steel casing can generate

detectable magnetic-field responses outside casing and that the received signal follows physically

These results support the feasibility of the loop-antenna sensing concept and define the path for the next

1.3 Toroidal-Antenna Wireless Communication and Energized-Casing Power Transfer
In addition to loop-antenna sensing, the proposed outside-casing electromagnetic monitoring system
requires a reliable method to transmit data and deliver power between the surface and permanently
installed downhole modules. Conventional downhole monitoring systems typically rely on umbilical cables
placed either inside the casing or in the cement annulus outside the casing. These approaches can increase
installation complexity and may compromise well integrity by requiring casing penetrations or by creating
potential leakage pathways along the cemented annulus. To avoid these limitations, this project uses
toroidal antennas wound around the metallic casing to enable casing-coupled wireless communication
and energized-casing wireless power transfer.
The toroidal antenna serves as the primary interface between the downhole module and the steel casing.
For communication, an alternating signal applied to the toroidal coil induces current along the casing,
allowing information to be transmitted between downhole modules and the surface without a direct
electrical cable. This configuration supports bidirectional communication: monitoring data can be
transmitted upward to the surface, while control commands and operating instructions can be sent
downward to the installed modules. Because signal attenuation increases with depth and formation
conductivity, the system can also support multi-hop communication, in which intermediate toroidal
modules receive, amplify, and retransmit signals along the casing to extend telemetry range and improve
link reliability.
A major Phase I activity was to evaluate the feasibility of this casing-coupled wireless communication
concept through both simulation and laboratory testing. Numerical models were developed to study
received voltage, channel capacity, bandwidth, and the influence of key antenna and formation
parameters, including ferrite-core size, number of coil turns, transmitter position, wire radius, cement
properties, source current, and noise level. The results showed that communication performance can be
significantly improved by increasing ferrite-core dimensions, optimizing transmitter placement relative to
casing ends, and increasing the number of coil turns within practical design limits. Laboratory testing was
also performed using toroidal antennas coupled to steel pipe, USRP hardware, and QPSK modulation. The
experimental results demonstrated successful short-range wireless communication and showed that
symbol rates up to approximately 10 ksps could be achieved under laboratory conditions.
The same toroidal-antenna and casing-coupled architecture is also used for wireless power transfer. In
this mode, the casing is energized from the surface using an alternating current source, and a portion of
the transmitted electromagnetic energy is harvested by downhole toroidal antennas. The harvested
energy is stored in high-reliability supercapacitors and used to power loop-antenna sensing,
communication electronics, microcontrollers, and onboard data acquisition. This approach is essential for
long-term autonomous operation because the EM modules are intended to remain permanently installed
outside the casing for many years without a wired power supply.

                                                                                               8|Page

Phase I simulations modeled the wireless power transfer system as a casing-coupled two-port network
and evaluated received voltage and power-transfer efficiency under different completion conditions. The
results indicated that the cement layer can improve power-transfer performance by reducing leakage
current from the casing into the surrounding formation. In representative simulations, the peak power-
transfer efficiency increased from approximately 1.4% without cement to approximately 12.5% with
cement, demonstrating that energized-casing wireless charging is a feasible pathway for periodically
recharging downhole modules.
Together, the toroidal-antenna communication and power-transfer subsystems provide the support
infrastructure needed for the loop-antenna sensing network. They allow the monitoring modules to
operate without downhole umbilical cables, preserve casing integrity, and support long-term deployment
in harsh subsurface environments. In the next development stage, these subsystems will be further
optimized and integrated with the loop-antenna sensors, supercapacitors, firmware, and compact control
electronics to form a complete outside-casing electromagnetic monitoring module for CO₂ storage
applications.

2. PHASE I ACCOMPLISHMENT
2.1 Major Activities and Technical Progress
During Phase I, Cyentech Consulting LLC advanced the core technical elements of the proposed outside-
casing electromagnetic monitoring system for long-term CO₂ plume monitoring and saturation mapping.
The work followed the project’s original technical objectives: developing multi-turn loop antennas for
electromagnetic sensing, evaluating toroidal antennas for casing-coupled wireless communication,
analyzing energized-casing wireless power transfer, and defining compact control electronics for a rugged
downhole EM module. These activities directly support the project goal of creating a permanently
installed, cable-free monitoring system that can operate outside metallic casing while preserving well
integrity.
The first major accomplishment was the development of physics-based electromagnetic models for loop-
antenna sensing in cased-well environments. The project team built and exercised COMSOL models,
including 2D axisymmetric and 3D configurations, to evaluate multi-turn loop antennas wound around
metallic casing and embedded in cement. These models included representative casing, cement, and
formation properties and were used to study antenna geometry, operating frequency, transmitter–
receiver spacing, depth of investigation, and signal coupling. The simulations confirmed that outside-
casing loop antennas can generate measurable magnetic-field responses and that the received fields show
physically consistent attenuation trends governed by formation conductivity, casing effects, and skin
depth.
In parallel, Cyentech initiated the development of an efficient electromagnetic simulation package for
loop-antenna modeling in large and heterogeneous subsurface formations. A lightweight 2.5D solver was
implemented to reduce computational cost while preserving key out-of-plane electromagnetic physics.
The solver was verified against analytical solutions in homogeneous media and produced magnetic-field
responses in good agreement with reference calculations. This capability provides a foundation for future
antenna optimization, sensitivity analysis, and inversion-oriented modeling of CO₂ storage reservoirs
where the formation can be approximated as invariant along one direction.
The project also made significant progress on the casing-coupled wireless communication subsystem. The
team developed numerical models for toroidal antennas wound around steel casing and evaluated the
effects of ferrite-core size, transmitter position, coil turns, wire radius, noise level, cement properties, and

                                                                                                    9|Page

source current on received signal strength and channel capacity. The modeling results showed that
communication performance can be improved substantially through antenna design optimization,
particularly by increasing ferrite-core dimensions, increasing coil turns within practical limits, and placing
the transmitter away from casing-end effects.
To validate the communication concept experimentally, a laboratory-scale testbed was constructed using
toroidal antennas coupled to steel pipe, USRP hardware, and QPSK modulation. The test demonstrated
successful short-range wireless communication through the toroidal-antenna/casing configuration. The
demodulation results showed clear QPSK constellation clustering at lower symbol rates, with achievable
symbol rates up to approximately 10 ksps under laboratory conditions, although the constellation became
more dispersed as the symbol rate increased. These results support the feasibility of using toroidal
antennas for wireless data transfer between downhole modules and surface equipment.
The third major technical area was wireless power transfer. The project team analyzed an energized-
casing power-delivery concept in which current injected at the surface propagates along the casing and is
harvested by downhole toroidal antennas. A two-port equivalent-circuit model was used to quantify
received voltage and power-transfer efficiency. The simulations showed that cement isolation can
improve power-transfer performance by reducing leakage current into the surrounding formation. In
representative cases, the peak power-transfer efficiency increased from approximately 1.4% without
cement to approximately 12.5% with cement, indicating that energized-casing wireless charging is a
feasible approach for periodically recharging supercapacitors in downhole modules.
Finally, Phase I established the system-level architecture for the integrated EM module. The proposed
module combines a multi-turn loop antenna for formation sensing, a toroidal antenna for communication
and power transfer, supercapacitor-based energy storage, microcontroller-based control electronics, and
protective packaging suitable for high-temperature, high-pressure, and corrosive downhole environments.
Initial control-electronics planning was completed, including discussions on compact PCB design, power
management, data acquisition, synchronization, and long-term reliability. This work defines the
integration path for Phase II, where the sensing, communication, charging, and control subsystems will
be combined into a rugged prototype for more complete validation.
Overall, Phase I demonstrated the technical feasibility of the proposed outside-casing electromagnetic
monitoring concept. The project produced validated modeling workflows, initial high-performance
simulation capability, toroidal-antenna communication experiments, wireless power-transfer estimates,
and a clear architecture for the integrated downhole module. These accomplishments reduce the
technical risk of the proposed system and provide a strong basis for Phase II development toward a
deployable, long-life CO₂ storage monitoring technology.

Task 1: Design and Test Loop Antenna System for Subsurface CO2 Plume Monitoring

2.1.1 Develop and optimize loop antenna design in the COMSOL simulations
Under Task 1, we developed and exercised a COMSOL workflow to design and optimize loop antenna for
subsurface sensing in cased vertical wells. Leveraging the cylindrical symmetry of a concentric casing–
cement stack over a conductive half-space, we reduce the 3D problem to a 2D axisymmetric model in r–
z, representing the transmitter as a circumferential coil (multi-turn thin-wire) driven in the frequency
domain. The model explicitly includes multiple metallic casings and cement annuli with their measured
conductivities and magnetic properties; electrically thin metallic coatings are treated either as explicit
solids or via an impedance boundary condition. The formation is modeled as a conductive half-space, with
optional anisotropy. To minimize truncation artifacts, the outer boundary is placed several skin depths
from the source and terminated with an absorbing condition such as cylindrical PML, and the mesh is
refined near thin layers and material transitions with element sizes tied to local skin depth.
Using this setup, we conduct parametric sweeps over loop radius, number of turns, inter-turn spacing,
axial placement relative to collars, and operating frequency, while tracking transmit efficiency and
coupling (e.g., received magnetic field and mutual impedance), and the radial decay that determines
depth of investigation. The 2D axisymmetric reduction reproduces the expected circumferential field
behavior and reduces runtime sufficiently to enable broad sweeps. Preliminary results show that receiver
responses along the borehole exhibit decay consistent with skin-depth predictions, which we are using to
bracket the operating band and select coil geometry and material for the required depth of investigation.

2.1.2 Development of efficient simulation package for loop antenna modeling.
To capture the heterogeneity expected around CO₂ storage sites, we are developing a high-performance
C++ simulation package that supports both 2.5D and full 3D electromagnetic modeling of loop antennas
in cased wells and layered, laterally variable formations. The 2.5D workflow uses a Fourier (kᵧ)
decomposition to reduce runtime while preserving out-of-plane physics; the 3D workflow targets complex
geologies and hardware layouts that don’t preserve cylindrical symmetry. The code implements
frequency-domain Maxwell solves with robust treatment of high-contrast media (steel casings, cement,
conductive formations), including optional anisotropy and thin-layer impedance models. Numerical
efficiency is achieved through curl-conforming finite elements with parallel computing, block
preconditioners tailored to the H(curl) operator, algebraic multigrid for vector fields, and optional GPU
acceleration. Source and receiver models include multi-turn loops and dipole abstractions, with mutual
impedance and field components reported directly for sensitivity analyses.
Current progress includes a lightweight 2.5D finite-element solver that reproduces the analytical response
of a loop antenna in a homogeneous formation. As illustrated in Figure 5, the solver’s magnetic-field
predictions for a dipole source at 2 kHz closely match the analytical solution along a straight observation
line, confirming correctness of the formulation. Typical runtime is about 30 s per source—substantially
faster than a full 3D simulation—while retaining the relevant out-of-plane physics. The same 2.5D
framework applies to CO₂ sequestration settings that are laterally invariant along one direction, enabling
efficient studies of monitoring sensitivity without resorting to full 3D models.

2.2.1 Study of wireless communication system using toroidal antennas outside a steel casing
This wireless system is a long-distance and real-time communication system, it’s important to know if the
channel capacity is high enough to send the data between transceivers. The capacity of a communication
system provides a theoretical upper bound on an achievable data rate and decides how many sensors
could be placed at the bottom of the well indirectly. Even with a measurable receiving voltage signal, the
capacity might not meet the need for real-time communication. In this section, we will analyze several
approaches to improve the measured signal, and more importantly, the communication performance to

see if this system meets our need to send subsurface condition data measured by sensors in real-time.
The schematic diagram of proposed wireless communication system is shown in Figure 6. Signal
transmission and power transfer could be transmitted from top to bottom and vice versa. There are only
two units in the figure and any of them could be used as transmitter and receiver. The transceiver unit
includes battery set, toroidal antenna (in Figure 6 (right)), and shield or cover which could hold controlling
circuit. Note that the toroidal antenna consists of coils that circulate around a ferrite core with a high
relative magnetic permeability.

Underground signal transmission over long distance often faces limited data transfer speeds. In addition,
energy supplies are very limited deep below the surface, making the energy requirement to transfer a
certain amount of data under critical consideration. Therefore, to characterize and evaluate the
performance of underground signal transmission, channel capacity and energy efficiency (the energy
needed to transfer certain amount of data) are the two important characteristics to evaluate the system
performance. To determine the channel capacity, we utilize a COMSOL Multi-physics model to first
compute the amplitude of the received voltage signal at certain distance and frequency. Subsequently,
the bandwidth can be assessed from the voltage spectrum. After collecting these two parameters, the

channel capacity can be computed by the equation 𝐶 = 𝐵𝑙𝑜𝑔2 (1 + 𝑁), where C is the ideal channel

Different effects on communication performance
In wireless communication, the channel capacity is determined by the channel’s characteristics,
modulation scheme, and error correction coding. In our wireless system, the generalized Shannon channel
capacity equation is used, and the channel capacity can be improved by increasing the Signal-to-Noise
ratio and achieving better bandwidth allocation. However, in our single-access channel system, the signal
has only one peak, so we cannot adjust the bandwidth too much to improve the channel capacity.
Therefore, channel capacity improvement can be achieved by amplifying the power of the received signal
and reducing the noise power. To achieve these goals, we have optimized the source and receiver’s

positions, ferrite core sectional area, coil turns, wire radius, noise levels, cement effects, and source
current intensity, and obtained much better channel capacity.
 a. Ferrite Core Size Effect
The increase in the size of the transceiver can amplify the received signal by increasing the rate of change
in magnetic flux. We can expand the height of the ferrite core to increase the size. Additionally, while
maintaining the same current intensity for the transmitter, increasing the cross-sectional area of the
transceiver can also amplify the excitation magnetic source. Therefore, by combining the last two factors,
the receiving voltage signal can be enhanced as well. Figure 7 (left) shows the receiving voltage signal
distribution with different heights of the transmitter and receiver. The intensity of the voltage signal
increases by approximately two orders of magnitude when the height is raised from 4 cm to 40 cm. Once
we have the voltage signal from the receiver, the channel capacity can be calculated. Figure 7 (right) shows
the channel capacity spectrum with different heights of transceivers, and the differences are huge with
different heights. The channel capacity at a height of 4 cm is quite limited, approximately 400 bps.
However, as the height increases, there is a corresponding increase in channel capacity, reaching 1.25
kbps for a height of 20 cm and further rising to 2 kbps for the 40 cm height scenario.

 c. Turns of Coils Effect
Regardless of whether it's a loop coil or a toroid coil antenna, the number of turns consistently remains a
critical factor to consider. This is extremely important when the distance spans thousands of meters,
where physical constraints limit the number of achievable turns. Generally, increasing the number of turns
contributes to improving signal reception. In our simulation, we list several cases, including the number
of turns from 50 to 1000.
The toroid's shape is adaptable and can be configured into various forms, including circular, elliptical, or
other geometric shapes. However, due to the lossy medium around the pipe, the magnetic field undergoes
rapid decay. To mitigate this, the rectangular-shaped ferrite core ring is a better choice to avoid less
surrounding medium because the internal surface of the ferrite core is parallel to the pipe surface.

Task 4: Develop Robust Control Electronics to Manage Components in the EM Module

Task 4 focused on the development path for rugged, compact, and power-efficient control electronics that
can coordinate all functional components of the outside-casing EM module. The proposed EM module
integrates multiple subsystems: a multi-turn loop antenna for CO₂ plume sensing, a toroidal antenna for
wireless communication and power transfer, supercapacitors for local energy storage, microcontrollers
for system control, and protective packaging for long-term operation in the cement annulus outside the
well casing. Because the module is intended to remain permanently installed in a harsh downhole
environment, the electronics must be designed for high reliability, low power consumption, compact
geometry, and long service life. The proposal identified this electronics package as a critical part of the
overall system because it enables the sensing, telemetry, charging, synchronization, and data-
management functions to operate as a single autonomous downhole unit.
The control electronics are expected to perform several essential functions. First, they must control the
loop-antenna transmitter and receiver operations, including excitation timing, frequency selection, signal
acquisition, amplification, filtering, and digitization. The EM signals received are expected to be weak
because they travel through conductive casing, cement, and lossy subsurface formations; therefore, low-
noise signal conditioning and stable timing control are important for repeatable time-lapse measurements.
Second, the electronics must manage toroidal-antenna communication, including command reception
from the surface, data packet generation, communication scheduling, and coordination with possible
relay or multi-hop telemetry modules. Third, the control system must regulate the energy harvested
through the energized-casing power-transfer subsystem and safely charge the onboard supercapacitors.
This requires power-management circuits capable of controlling charge/discharge cycles, monitoring
stored energy, preventing over-voltage or deep discharge, and allocating available power among sensing,
communication, and standby modes.

4. CONCLUSION
This Phase I project demonstrated the technical feasibility of a novel outside-casing electromagnetic
wireless system for long-term CO₂ plume monitoring and saturation mapping. The proposed technology
addresses a critical limitation of conventional downhole monitoring approaches: the need to obtain
reliable subsurface measurements over long periods without compromising well integrity through casing
perforations, downhole umbilical cables, or cable paths in the cement annulus. By integrating
electromagnetic sensing, casing-coupled wireless communication, energized-casing wireless power
transfer, and rugged control electronics into compact modules mounted externally on steel casing, the
project establishes a promising pathway toward cable-free, long-life monitoring of geological CO₂ storage
sites.

The Phase I effort successfully advanced the core components of the system. For the sensing function,
Cyentech developed physics-based COMSOL models and an initial efficient 2.5D simulation capability to
evaluate multi-turn loop antennas installed outside metallic casing. These tools were used to study
antenna geometry, operating frequency, casing effects, cement properties, formation resistivity, and
depth of investigation. The results confirmed that loop antennas can generate measurable
electromagnetic responses in cased-well environments and can provide sensitivity to formation resistivity
changes associated with CO₂ plume movement and saturation variation. The project also made substantial
progress on supporting wireless infrastructure required for autonomous downhole operation. Toroidal
antennas were investigated for casing-coupled data transmission, and both simulation and laboratory
testing were performed to evaluate communication performance. The laboratory test using toroidal

                                                                                            23 | P a g e

antennas, steel pipe, USRP hardware, and QPSK modulation demonstrated the feasibility of short-scale
wireless data transmission. In parallel, energized-casing wireless power transfer was analyzed using
numerical models and equivalent-circuit concepts. The results showed that cement isolation can
significantly improve power-transfer efficiency by reducing leakage current, supporting the feasibility of
periodically recharging downhole supercapacitors.

Together, these accomplishments validate the central concept of the project: a permanently installed
outside-casing EM module can sense formation changes, communicate data, and harvest power without
direct electrical cables from the surface. The work reduced major technical risks associated with loop-
antenna sensing, toroidal-antenna telemetry, and energized-casing power delivery. It also defined the
system architecture for future integration of antennas, supercapacitors, microcontrollers, power-
management electronics, and protective packaging suitable for high-temperature, high-pressure, and
corrosive downhole environments.

The outcomes of Phase I provide a strong foundation for Phase II development. The next stage should
focus on integrated prototype fabrication and testing, refinement of the loop-antenna design for realistic
multilayered and anisotropic CO₂ storage formations, extension of simulation tools for sensitivity and
inversion studies, improvement of communication range and bit-error-rate performance, optimization of
wireless charging efficiency, and validation under representative laboratory and field-like conditions.
Successful completion of these steps will move the technology toward a deployable monitoring platform
for carbon storage operators.
