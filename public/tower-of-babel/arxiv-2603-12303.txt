Quantum Reservoir Autoencoder for Blind Decryption: Two-Phase Protocol and Noise Resilience
Hikaru Wakaura, Taiki Tanimae

Abstract
We instantiate the quantum reservoir autoencoder (QRA) with a noise-induced reservoir employing reset noise channels and address two open problems: noise-resilient reversibility and blind decryption. For a single-ciphertext protocol with 10 data qubits and random (non-optimized) reset probabilities, the open-system reservoir suppresses shot-noise sensitivity by ten orders of magnitude, yielding mean-squared error (MSE) ∼ 10 − 14 \sim 10^{-14} compared with ∼ 10 − 3 \sim 10^{-3} without reset channels ( N shots = 1000 N_{\mathrm{shots}}=1000 ). A two-phase protocol trains per-position decoding weights from M M shared training plaintexts and decrypts previously unseen messages at MSE ∼ 10 − 4 \sim 10^{-4} , with no statistically significant performance difference among ideal, shot-noise, and reset-plus-shot-noise conditions ( p > 0.05 p>0.05 , 16 seeds). Experiments at N q = 5 N_{q}=5 , 7, and 10 reveal a sharp phase transition at plaintext length N c ≈ N q ​ ( N q + 1 ) / 2 + 8 N_{c}\approx N_{q}(N_{q}{+}1)/2+8 , providing a design rule for the minimum qubit count. Two blind decoder variants that lack ground-truth targets—a single-ciphertext cross-path iteration (MSE ≈ 0.3 \approx 0.3 ) and a multi-sample regression variant (MSE ≈ 0.53 \approx 0.53 , worse than random)—establish that shared training data is the irreducible requirement for blind decryption. A comparison with variational quantum circuit baselines shows that the fixed-reservoir analytic-readout architecture is dramatically more noise-robust: a quantum recurrent neural network protocol is completely destroyed under depolarizing noise, whereas the QRA remains invariant.



I
Introduction

Quantum reservoir computing exploits fixed quantum dynamics coupled with a
trainable linear readout layer to process sequential
data
4
;
15
;
12
.
Unlike variational quantum algorithms that require costly iterative
optimization of parameterized circuits, QRC employs a quantum dynamical
system whose intrinsic nonlinear evolution generates expressive
high-dimensional feature vectors from a small number of physical
qubits
9
;
11
;
8
.
This architecture has been successfully applied to time-series
prediction
8
;
3
;
17
and waveform
generation, demonstrating the viability of quantum systems as
computational reservoirs.

A companion paper
20
recently established that
QRC can support
reversible
information transformation: given
an input sequence encoded through a quantum reservoir, the original
input can be reconstructed from the reservoir output via a
four-equation encode–decode protocol.
Using a Hamiltonian-driven reservoir with 76-dimensional feature
vectors, machine-precision reconstruction
(MSE
\sim 10^{-17}
) was achieved under ideal conditions.
However, shot noise degraded the MSE to
\sim 10^{-1}
, and blind
decryption—where the decoder reconstructs plaintext without any
prior knowledge of it—was identified as an open
challenge
20
.

In this work we address both open problems within the QRA
framework established in Ref.
20
,
instantiating the reservoir with the noise-induced architecture
introduced by Dudaš et al.
3
.
Unlike Hamiltonian-driven QRC, this architecture incorporates
reset noise channels with tunable parameters, enabling the
reservoir dynamics to be shaped by the noise itself.
We use random (non-optimized) noise parameters and
find that the resulting open-system dynamics inherently suppress
shot-noise sensitivity.
Our contributions are sixfold:

1.

We demonstrate that the QRA supports reversible information
transformation with 56-dimensional feature vectors (
\langle Z_{i}\rangle
,

\langle Z_{i}Z_{j}\rangle
, and bias), achieving ideal-condition MSE

\sim 10^{-17}
for plaintext lengths up to
N_{c}=35
.

2.

We find that the open-system QRA regime with random
(non-optimized) reset noise probabilities achieves an MSE of

\sim 10^{-14}
under combined reset-plus-shot noise, representing
a ten-order-of-magnitude improvement over the closed-system
reservoir under shot noise alone (
\sim 10^{-3}
), demonstrating
that the noise-induced reservoir architecture inherently suppresses
measurement-noise sensitivity.

3.

We introduce a two-phase blind decryption protocol that resolves
the open challenge posed in Ref.
20
. The
protocol trains per-position decoding weights from
M
shared
training plaintexts (Phase 1) and applies them to decrypt previously
unseen messages without knowledge of the plaintext (Phase 2).

4.

We provide statistical validation through paired
t
-tests and
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across 16 random seeds, establishing
that the two-phase protocol shows no significant performance
difference among three noise conditions (
p>0.05
).

5.

We evaluate a Single-C blind decoder variant in which the
decoder has
zero
knowledge of the plaintext throughout
the entire ALS iteration.
The self-consistent cross-path estimation saturates at
MSE
\approx 0.3
—comparable to the uninformative

essential ingredient that enables the two-phase protocol’s success.

6.

We benchmark against a quantum variational autoencoder
(
\zeta
-QVAE

) baseline using the same protocol
and feature dimension, showing that the QRA’s fixed

II
Background

II.1
Reversible QRC protocol

We present the four-equation reversible QRC protocol in sufficient
detail for this paper to be self-contained; see also
Ref.
20
for additional context and the
original derivation.
Consider two parties who share quantum reservoirs
R_{a}
and
R_{b}
, and
wish to encrypt a plaintext vector
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}}
.
Each party holds a pair of keys: distributed (public) keys
A
and
B
,
and secret keys
\alpha
and
\beta
, each of length
N_{c}+N_{q}+1
,
where
N_{q}
is the number of qubits.
The protocol is defined by four simultaneous equations:

\displaystyle R_{a}\bigl(F(A,\mathbf{C})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bm{\gamma},

(1)

\displaystyle R_{a}\bigl(G(\alpha,\bm{\gamma}^{\prime})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C},

(2)

\displaystyle R_{b}\bigl(F(B,\mathbf{C})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bm{\gamma}^{\prime},

(3)

\displaystyle R_{b}\bigl(G(\beta,\bm{\gamma})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C},

(4)

where
\bm{\gamma}
and
\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}
are ciphertext vectors, and

R(\mathbf{x})
denotes the reservoir projection

R(\mathbf{x})=V(\mathbf{x})\,W
with feature matrix
V
and
readout weight matrix
W
.
Equations (
1
)–(
4
) define a
reservoir
autoencoder
: Eqs. (
1
) and (
3
) constitute the
encoder (
\mathbf{C}\to\bm{\gamma}
via the quantum reservoir), while
Eqs. (
2
) and (
4
) constitute the decoder
(
\bm{\gamma}\to\mathbf{C}
).
The reservoir feature vectors serve as the nonlinear embedding,
analogous to the hidden representation in a neural
autoencoder
7
;
6
.

The encoding function
F
and decoding function
G
are defined
element-wise as

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{C})_{i}

\displaystyle=\tanh\!\bigl(k_{i}\,C_{i}+k_{N_{c}+(i\bmod n_{e})}\bigr),

(5)

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{e})_{i}

\displaystyle=\tanh\!\bigl(s_{i}\,e_{i}+s_{N_{c}+(i\bmod n_{e})}\bigr),

(6)

where
n_{e}=N_{q}+1
is the offset key length.
The
\tanh
nonlinearity ensures bounded outputs while preserving the
element-wise invertible structure.

The readout weight
W
satisfies

W=(V^{\top}V+\lambda I)^{-1}V^{\top}\mathbf{y},

(7)

where
\lambda>0
is the Tikhonov regularization
parameter
19
.
For the reservoir projection to be exact, the feature dimension

D=\mathrm{dim}(V)
must satisfy
D\geq N_{c}
.

The four equations are solved simultaneously via alternating least
squares (ALS)
5
: at each iteration, one of
the four unknowns (
W_{\mathrm{enc},a}
,
W_{\mathrm{dec},a}
,

W_{\mathrm{enc},b}
,
W_{\mathrm{dec},b}
) is updated while the
other three are held fixed.
The loss function is

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{Path\,1}}+\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{Path\,2}}\bigr),

(8)

where Path 1 denotes the route

\mathbf{C}\xrightarrow{F(A,\cdot)}R_{a}\xrightarrow{\bm{\gamma}}G(\beta,\cdot)\xrightarrow{}R_{b}\to\hat{\mathbf{C}}
,
and Path 2 follows the symmetric route through
R_{b}
then
R_{a}
.
A key insight from Ref.
20
is that the
encoding-side feature matrix
V_{\mathrm{enc}}
equals the
decryption-side feature matrix
V_{\mathrm{dec}}
for each reservoir
(since both process the same encoded input), yielding a factor-of-two
computational speedup.

II.2
Noise-induced reservoir architecture

The noise-induced reservoir architecture employed by the QRA
was introduced by
Dudaš et al.
3
as a framework where noise channel
parameters serve as optimizable degrees of freedom rather than
undesirable error sources.
The central element is the
probabilistic reset channel

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{PR}}
, which acts on single-qubit density
matrices as

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{PR}}(\rho)=(1-p)\,\rho+p\,|0\rangle\!\langle 0|,

(9)

where
p\in[0,1]
is the reset probability.
This is a completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) map with three
Kraus operators
14
:

\displaystyle K_{0}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-p}\;I,

(10)

\displaystyle K_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p}\;|0\rangle\!\langle 0|,

(11)

\displaystyle K_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p}\;|0\rangle\!\langle 1|,

(12)

satisfying
\sum_{k}K_{k}^{\dagger}K_{k}=I
.
One can verify Eq. (
9
) by direct computation:

\sum_{k}K_{k}\rho K_{k}^{\dagger}=(1-p)\rho+p\,|0\rangle\!\langle 0|(\langle 0|\rho|0\rangle+\langle 1|\rho|1\rangle)=(1-p)\rho+p\,|0\rangle\!\langle 0|
,
where the last equality uses
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)=1
.

The QRA reservoir circuit at each time step
t
with scalar input

u_{t}=\phi(\mathbf{x}_{t})
consists of four layers applied to

N_{q}
qubits:

1.

Encoding layer:

\mathrm{RX}_{i}\bigl(\theta(1+p_{i})\bigr)
on each qubit
i
,
where
\theta=s\cdot u_{t}
and
s
is a scaling factor, followed
by the reset noise channel
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{PR}}^{(i)}
with
probability
p_{i}
.

2.

Entanglement layer:

\mathrm{RZZ}_{ij}\bigl(\theta(1+p_{ij})\bigr)=\mathrm{CNOT}_{ij}\,\mathrm{RZ}_{j}\bigl(\theta(1+p_{ij})\bigr)\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{ij}
on each entangling pair
(i,j)
in a
pair-separable topology
\{(0,1),(2,3),\ldots\}
, followed by

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{PR}}^{(j)}
with probability
p_{ij}
.

3.

Rotation layer:

\mathrm{RY}_{i}(p_{i}^{\prime}\pi)
—an input-independent rotation
controlled solely by the noise parameter, followed by

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{PR}}^{(i)}
with probability
p_{i}^{\prime}
.

4.

Output layer:

\mathrm{RZ}_{i}\bigl(\theta(1+p_{i}^{\prime\prime})\bigr)
on each qubit,
followed by
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{PR}}^{(i)}
with probability

p_{i}^{\prime\prime}
.

The total number of noise parameters is

n_{\mathrm{noise}}=N_{q}+n_{\mathrm{pairs}}+N_{q}+N_{q}
,
where
n_{\mathrm{pairs}}=\lfloor N_{q}/2\rfloor
for the
pair-separable scheme and the four terms correspond to the encoding,
entanglement, rotation, and output layers.
For
N_{q}=10
, this gives
n_{\mathrm{noise}}=10+5+10+10=35

independent noise probabilities.

In the original noise-induced reservoir framework of Ref.
3
, the noise
probabilities
\{p_{i}\}
are optimized via dual annealing to minimize
prediction MSE.
In this work, however, the
\{p_{i}\}
are drawn from

\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
and held fixed; the ALS readout-weight
solver adapts the linear readout to the given noise profile
(see Section
IV.3
).

The observable feature vector at time step
t
consists of
single-qubit and two-qubit Pauli-
Z
expectation values augmented
with a bias term:

\mathbf{v}_{t}=\Bigl(\langle Z_{1}\rangle_{t},\ldots,\langle Z_{N_{q}}\rangle_{t},\;\langle Z_{1}Z_{2}\rangle_{t},\ldots,\langle Z_{N_{q}-1}Z_{N_{q}}\rangle_{t},\;1\Bigr).

(13)

For
N_{q}=10
, the feature dimension is

D=10+\binom{10}{2}+1=56
.

The state is initialized to
|+\rangle^{\otimes N_{q}}
and evolves
recurrently across the input sequence without reset between time
steps.
In the pure-state simulation mode (Exps 1–4), the same gate
angles are applied but the CPTP channels are omitted; the noise
parameters affect only the gate rotations.

Relationship between the two simulation modes.

The pure-state and density-matrix simulations share the same
four-layer circuit architecture with
n_{\mathrm{noise}}=35

parameters.
In the pure-state mode (Exps 1–4), the noise parameters modulate
the gate angles as described above, but no CPTP channel is applied;
the state evolves unitarily.
In the density-matrix mode (Exps 5–6), each gate is followed by
a genuine CPTP reset channel
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{PR}}
, modeling
the open quantum system faithfully.
In both cases, the noise parameters
\{p_{i}\}
serve a dual role:
gate-angle modulation and (in the density-matrix mode) reset
channel probability.
The key physical difference is that density-matrix mode produces
mixed states, which contract the Bloch sphere and suppress
shot-noise variance.
The shot-noise experiment (Exp 3, pure-state with binomial sampling)
serves as an intermediate reference that isolates the effect of
measurement noise from the open-system dynamics.

II.3
The blind decryption problem

In the single-ciphertext (Single-C) protocol described in
Section
II.1
, the ALS solver requires knowledge of the
plaintext
\mathbf{C}
at
both
the encoding and decoding
sides to construct the feature matrices and solve for the readout
weights.
This is adequate for verifying reversibility but fundamentally
incompatible with practical encrypted communication, where the
decoding party must recover
\mathbf{C}
without prior access to it.

This
blind decryption
problem was explicitly identified as an
open challenge in Ref.
20
(Section 6.4):
the decoder needs the weight matrix
W_{\mathrm{dec}}
to reconstruct

\mathbf{C}
, but computing
W_{\mathrm{dec}}
via Eq. (
7
)
requires the feature matrix
V_{\mathrm{dec}}
, which itself depends on

G(\mathbf{s},\bm{\gamma})
—and thus on
\bm{\gamma}
, which
encodes
\mathbf{C}
.
Breaking this circular dependency is the key technical challenge
addressed by our two-phase protocol.
We note that all results in this paper are derived from independent
numerical experiments using the QRA reservoir; the protocol
equations [Eqs. (
1
)–(
4
)] and their ALS
solution method are fully specified above and can be verified
without reference to Ref.
20
.

III
Two-phase blind decryption protocol

III.1
Protocol overview

We resolve the blind decryption problem by separating the protocol
into two phases:

•

Phase 1 (key establishment):
Both parties share
M

training plaintexts
\{\mathbf{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{C}_{M}\}
.
The sender encrypts each plaintext and transmits the ciphertext;
the receiver uses the known plaintext–ciphertext pairs to train
a position-wise decoding model.

•

Phase 2 (blind communication):
The sender encrypts
a new plaintext
\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{test}}
that the receiver
has never seen.
The receiver applies the frozen decoding model from Phase 1 to
recover
\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{test}}
.

III.2
Phase 1: shared training

Encryption.

For each training plaintext
\mathbf{C}_{j}
(
j=1,\ldots,M
),
the sender computes

\bm{\gamma}_{j}=V_{\mathrm{enc}}\bigl(F(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{C}_{j})\bigr)\;W_{\mathrm{enc},j},

(14)

where
W_{\mathrm{enc},j}
is obtained from Eq. (
7
).
Since
D\geq N_{c}
, the projection satisfies

\bm{\gamma}_{j}=F(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{C}_{j})
to machine precision.

Augmented feature construction.

The receiver computes the decoded sequence

\mathbf{d}_{j}=G(\mathbf{s},\bm{\gamma}_{j})
and constructs an
augmented feature matrix

\Phi_{j}=\bigl[\,V_{\mathrm{dec}}(\mathbf{d}_{j})\;\big|\;\mathbf{d}_{j}^{\circ 1}\;\big|\;\cdots\;\big|\;\mathbf{d}_{j}^{\circ K}\,\bigr]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}\times D_{\mathrm{aug}}},

(15)

where
\mathbf{d}_{j}^{\circ k}
denotes the element-wise
k
-th power
of
\mathbf{d}_{j}
,
K
is the polynomial degree, and

D_{\mathrm{aug}}=D+K
.
For
N_{q}=10
and
K=7
, we have
D_{\mathrm{aug}}=56+7=63
.
The polynomial basis serves as a universal approximator for the
element-wise inverse mapping

d_{j,i}=G(\mathbf{s},F(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{C}_{j}))_{i}\mapsto C_{j,i}
,
which is a smooth, bounded function of a single variable for each
position
i
.

Per-position ridge regression.

For each position
i=0,\ldots,N_{c}-1
, the receiver collects
M

training pairs
\{(\Phi_{j}[i,:],\;C_{j,i})\}_{j=1}^{M}
and solves

\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{dec},i}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{w}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\bigl(\Phi_{j}[i,:]\,\mathbf{w}-C_{j,i}\bigr)^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2},

(16)

yielding
N_{c}
independent weight vectors of dimension
D_{\mathrm{aug}}
.

III.3
Phase 2: blind decryption

Given a new ciphertext
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{test}}
(encrypted from
an unknown
\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{test}}
), the receiver computes the
decoded sequence
\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{test}}=G(\mathbf{s},\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{test}})
, constructs the augmented feature
matrix
\Phi_{\mathrm{test}}
via Eq. (
15
), and
recovers each position as

\hat{C}_{\mathrm{test},i}=\Phi_{\mathrm{test}}[i,:]\;\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{dec},i}.

(17)

The weight vectors
\{\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{dec},i}\}
are frozen from
Phase 1; the receiver has
never
seen

\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{test}}
.

III.4
Relationship to single-ciphertext protocol

The Single-C protocol (Section
II.1
) and the two-phase
protocol represent complementary approaches.
Single-C jointly optimizes all readout weights for a
specific

plaintext via ALS and achieves in-sample precision at the machine
limit.
The two-phase protocol sacrifices in-sample precision
(MSE
\sim 10^{-4}
versus
\sim 10^{-17}
) in exchange for

generalization
: the ability to decrypt arbitrary unseen
messages.
In a practical communication scenario, Phase 1 establishes a shared
decryption capability, after which Phase 2 enables continuous blind
message exchange.

III.5
Single-C blind decoder variant

A natural question is whether the blind decryption problem can be
solved
within
the Single-C framework by replacing the
plaintext
\mathbf{C}
in the decoder’s training target with a
cross-path estimate, thereby eliminating the need for shared
training data entirely.
We formalize this as the
Single-C blind decoder
variant.

In the standard Single-C ALS (Section
II.1
), the decoder
weights are computed with the
true
plaintext as the regression
target:

W_{\mathrm{dec},b}=\mathrm{solve\_W}(V_{\mathrm{dec},b},\;\mathbf{C}).

(18)

In the blind decoder variant, the decoder has
zero knowledge

of
\mathbf{C}
.
Instead, it uses the current reconstruction estimate

\hat{\mathbf{C}}
obtained from the complementary path:

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{dec},b}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{solve\_W}(V_{\mathrm{dec},b},\;\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{est}}),

(19)

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{dec},a}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{solve\_W}(V_{\mathrm{dec},a},\;\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{rec},1}),

(20)

where
\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{est}}
is initialized to the
ciphertext
\bm{\gamma}
(a random-like proxy carrying partial
information about
\mathbf{C}
through the encoding path) and

\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{rec},1}=V_{\mathrm{dec},b}\,W_{\mathrm{dec},b}
is the Path 1 reconstruction.
At each ALS iteration, the estimate is updated via

\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{est}}\leftarrow\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathrm{rec},2}
(the Path 2 reconstruction),
creating a self-consistent feedback loop between the two paths.
Crucially, the encoder side still uses
\mathbf{C}
implicitly
through the encoding functions
F(A,\mathbf{C})
and

F(B,\mathbf{C})
, preserving the cryptographic structure of the
protocol.

The blind decoder variant represents the most restrictive
decryption scenario: the decoder receives only the ciphertext

\bm{\gamma}
and must bootstrap the plaintext recovery
from self-consistent cross-path iteration alone, without any
shared training data (unlike the two-phase protocol) or
direct access to
\mathbf{C}
(unlike the standard Single-C
protocol).

III.6
Two-phase blind decoder variant

A natural extension of the Single-C blind decoder is to ask whether
the self-consistent cross-path iteration can be improved by

statistical averaging
over
M
training samples, as in the
standard two-phase protocol.
We formalize this as the
two-phase blind decoder
, which
combines the multi-sample ridge regression of the two-phase protocol
(Section
III.2
) with the cross-path estimation strategy
of the blind decoder (Section
III.5
).

The algorithm proceeds as follows.
Given
M
training plaintexts
\{\mathbf{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{C}_{M}\}
,
the sender encrypts each one (Step 1) and the receiver computes
augmented feature matrices
\{\Phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
(Step 2), exactly
as in the standard two-phase protocol.
However, the receiver does
not
have access to

\{\mathbf{C}_{j}\}
for the per-position regression
[Eq. (
16
)].
Instead, the regression target is replaced by a cross-path estimate

\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{j}
, initialized to the raw decoded values

\mathbf{d}_{j}=G(\mathbf{s},\bm{\gamma}_{j})
and iteratively
refined:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{dec},i}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbf{w}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\!\bigl(\Phi_{j}^{(1)}[i,:]\,\mathbf{w}-\hat{C}_{j,i}^{\mathrm{est}}\bigr)^{\!2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2},

(21)

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{j,i}^{\mathrm{rec,1}}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{j}^{(1)}[i,:]\;\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{dec},i}^{(1)},

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{dec},i}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbf{w}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\!\bigl(\Phi_{j}^{(2)}[i,:]\,\mathbf{w}-\hat{C}_{j,i}^{\mathrm{rec,1}}\bigr)^{\!2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{w}\|^{2},

(23)

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{j,i}^{\mathrm{est}}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\Phi_{j}^{(2)}[i,:]\;\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{dec},i}^{(2)},

(24)

where superscripts
(1)
and
(2)
denote Path 1 and Path 2,
respectively.
The iteration is repeated for
N_{\mathrm{iter}}
cycles.
Note that the encoder side retains access to
\mathbf{C}
through
the encryption functions
F(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{C})
, as in the
Single-C blind decoder.

The two-phase blind decoder tests whether the statistical averaging
inherent in the
M
-sample regression—which is the key advantage
of the standard two-phase protocol—can compensate for the absence
of ground-truth targets.
If the
M
-sample averaging suffices, the MSE should approach the
standard two-phase level (
\sim 10^{-4}
); if not, the results
will clarify whether shared training data or statistical averaging
is the more fundamental ingredient for blind decryption.

IV
Experimental setup

IV.1
QRA reservoir parameters

All experiments use
N_{q}=10
qubits in a pair-separable entanglement
topology.
The feature dimension is
D=56
for the Single-C protocol and

D_{\mathrm{aug}}=63
for the two-phase protocol (polynomial degree

K=7
).
Keys are drawn from
\mathrm{Uniform}(-1,1)
with length

N_{c}+N_{q}+1
.
Plaintext elements are drawn independently from

\mathrm{Uniform}(-1,1)
.
All simulations use the Qulacs quantum circuit
simulator
18
.

IV.2
Noise conditions

We evaluate three noise conditions:

1.

Ideal:
Pure-state (statevector) simulation with no
measurement noise. Noise parameters modulate gate angles as
described in Section
II.2
.

2.

Shot noise:
Pure-state simulation with
N_{\mathrm{shots}}=1000

measurement shots.
Each expectation value
\langle O\rangle
is sampled via a binomial model:

p_{\mathrm{meas}}=(1+\langle O\rangle)/2
,

n_{\mathrm{counts}}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(N_{\mathrm{shots}},p_{\mathrm{meas}})
,

\hat{O}=2n_{\mathrm{counts}}/N_{\mathrm{shots}}-1
.

3.

Reset + shot noise:
Density matrix simulation with
per-gate CPTP reset noise channels [Eqs. (
9
)–(
12
)]
and
N_{\mathrm{shots}}=1000
shot noise.
This represents the full open-quantum-system model.

IV.4
Statistical testing

To assess the significance of performance differences among noise
conditions, we aggregate final MSE values across seeds as follows.
For each seed, we average the MSE over the three trials to obtain one
representative value per seed.
We then apply two paired statistical tests to the
\log_{10}(\mathrm{MSE})

values:

•

Paired
t
-test:
parametric, assuming normally
distributed differences.

•

Wilcoxon signed-rank test:
nonparametric, with no
distributional assumptions.

For the Single-C comparisons (Exps 1, 3, 5), we have 16 paired
samples per
N_{c}
value.
For the two-phase comparisons (Exps 2, 4, 6), we have 4 paired
samples per
N_{c}
value, reflecting the reduced seed count in
Exp 6.

Single-C blind decoder

We now present the results of the Single-C blind decoder variant
(Section

), in which the decoder has
zero

\mathbf{C}
throughout all ALS iterations.
Unlike the two-phase protocol, which requires
M

plaintexts, the blind decoder attempts to recover
\mathbf{C}

from self-consistent cross-path iteration alone.
Experiments were conducted at
N_{q}=5
(
D=16
) and
N_{q}=7

(
D=29
) to evaluate the role of feature dimensionality.

V.9.1
Convergence behavior at
N_{q}=5
(
D=16
)

Figures
10
–
12
show
the convergence of the blind decoder loss under all three noise
conditions for
N_{q}=5
.
In stark contrast to the standard Single-C protocol
(Fig.
1
, MSE
\sim 10^{-17}
), the blind decoder
converges rapidly to a plateau at MSE
\approx 0.28
–
0.42

within approximately 5 iterations and shows no further improvement.
The three noise conditions—ideal, shot (
N_{\mathrm{shots}}=1000
), and
reset+shot—produce indistinguishable convergence curves
(Figs.

–
12
),
indicating that the bottleneck is entirely algorithmic.
Note that
D=16<N_{c}
for
N_{c}\geq 18
, so the rank condition

D\geq N_{c}
is violated at larger plaintext lengths; yet the MSE
plateau is equally flat for
N_{c}=5
(
D>N_{c}
) and
N_{c}=35

(
D\ll N_{c}
), suggesting that the rank condition is not the

VI
Discussion

VI.1
Reset noise channels as shot-noise suppressors

The most striking finding of this work is the ten-order-of-magnitude
improvement in Single-C MSE when reset noise channels are
introduced alongside shot noise (Exp 5 versus Exp 3).
Critically, this improvement arises
without
explicit
optimization of the noise probabilities
\{p_{i}\}
: the reset
probabilities are drawn uniformly at random from
[0,1]
and remain
fixed.
The ALS readout-weight solver adapts
W_{\mathrm{enc}}
and

W_{\mathrm{dec}}
to the given noise profile, effectively learning
to exploit the reset channel dynamics.
The result is noteworthy: an open-system reservoir
with 35 random noise parameters achieves MSE
\sim 10^{-14}

under shot noise, whereas a closed-system (pure-state) reservoir
with the same 35 noise-modulated parameters saturates at

\sim 10^{-3}
.
The noise configuration determines a density matrix evolution where
the resulting expectation values
\langle Z_{i}\rangle
become insensitive to
shot-noise fluctuations, and the linear readout layer adapts to this
specific noise fingerprint.

This mechanism is distinct from quantum error
correction
14
, which requires encoding logical
qubits in larger Hilbert spaces.
Rather, it operates at the reservoir level: the noise parameters
shape the
dynamics
of the open quantum system to produce
a feature space with favorable noise properties.
Concretely, the reset channel drives the density matrix toward

|0\rangle\!\langle 0|
, which concentrates the expectation values of

Z
-basis observables near the eigenvalue
+1
.
Since the variance of the binomial shot-noise estimator scales as

p_{\mathrm{meas}}(1-p_{\mathrm{meas}})/N_{\mathrm{shots}}
—which is
minimized when
p_{\mathrm{meas}}\to 0
or
1
—the
reset channels naturally suppress the shot-noise variance of
feature components whose expectation values are pushed toward

\pm 1
.
The linear readout layer then learns to weight these
low-variance components appropriately, compensating for any
information loss.
We verified that the noise probabilities across all seeds and
trials are uniformly distributed (mean
0.48\pm 0.28
;
Fig.
19
), with no
systematic concentration near 0 or 1, confirming that the
suppression effect is not an artifact of extreme noise configurations
but rather a generic property of the QRA architecture.

Figure 19:

\{p_{i}\}
used

(a) Overall histogram across 210 noise parameters (3 trials

This is conceptually related to classical noise-induced phenomena
such as stochastic resonance
2
, where an optimal
noise amplitude enhances signal detection.
It also shares the spirit of noise tailoring in randomized
compiling
21
, although here the mechanism is
structural rather than optimization-based: the CPTP reset channels
generically contract the Bloch sphere, and the linear readout
adapts to exploit this contraction.
We provide a semi-quantitative argument for the suppression
magnitude.
For a single observable
\langle Z\rangle
, the shot-noise variance is

\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{shot}}=(1-\langle Z\rangle^{2})/N_{\mathrm{shots}}
.
In the pure-state case, typical expectation values yield

|\langle Z\rangle|\sim 0.5
, giving
\sigma^{2}\sim 7.5\times 10^{-4}
for

N_{\mathrm{shots}}=1000
.
Under a reset channel with probability
p
, the post-channel
expectation value becomes

\langle Z\rangle_{\mathrm{after}}=(1-p)\langle Z\rangle_{\mathrm{before}}+p
,
which monotonically approaches
+1
as
p\to 1
.
For the typical random
p_{i}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
, many
features will have
\langle Z\rangle_{\mathrm{after}}
pushed well toward

+1
, reducing
1-\langle Z\rangle^{2}\to 0
and hence

\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{shot}}\to 0
.
With
D=56
features and
N_{c}\leq 35
, the reconstruction MSE
inherits the suppressed per-feature variance via the linear readout,
which qualitatively accounts for the transition from

\sim 10^{-3}
(pure-state) to
\sim 10^{-14}
(reset+shot).
We emphasize that a rigorous analytical theory—deriving tight
bounds on the MSE as a function of the noise profile—remains an
open problem; the present evidence is empirical, based on consistent
observations across 16 independent random seeds (each with a
distinct random noise profile) and 10 plaintext lengths.

VI.2
Resolution of the blind decryption challenge

The two-phase protocol resolves the open challenge of blind
decryption identified in Ref.
20
.
The key insight is the
element-wise
structure of the
encoding and decoding functions [Eqs. (
5
)–(
6
)]:
for each position
i
, the decoded value
d_{i}=G(\mathbf{s},F(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{C}))_{i}
is a smooth, bounded function of

C_{i}
alone.
The polynomial basis augmentation of Eq. (
15
)
provides a universal approximator for the inverse of this
single-variable function, enabling per-position regression to learn
the mapping
d_{i}\mapsto C_{i}
from training data.

The statistical equivalence of the three noise conditions for the
two-phase protocol (Table
5
) is a particularly
important result for practical deployment.
It indicates that the two-phase protocol can be directly implemented
on near-term quantum hardware without significant performance
degradation from measurement noise or open-system dynamics.

VI.3
Autoencoder interpretation

The four-equation protocol
[Eqs. (
1
)–(
4
)] is naturally interpreted as a

reservoir autoencoder
: the encoder maps plaintext
\mathbf{C}

through the quantum reservoir to a latent representation

\bm{\gamma}
(ciphertext), and the decoder maps
\bm{\gamma}
back
to
\mathbf{C}
.
We adopt the name
quantum reservoir autoencoder
(QRA) to
reflect this encode–decode structure, following the companion
paper
20
.
We note that the QRA differs from a classical
autoencoder
7
;
6
in an important
respect: the ciphertext
\bm{\gamma}
has the same dimension as
the plaintext
\mathbf{C}
, so there is no information bottleneck
or dimensionality reduction.
The “autoencoder” terminology refers to the
encoder–latent–decoder
architecture
, not to the
data-compression function.
Furthermore, the QRA requires no gradient-based training of the
nonlinear mapping: the quantum reservoir provides a fixed nonlinear
feature embedding, and only the linear readout weights are optimized.
The QRA also differs from quantum autoencoders in the sense of
Romero et al.
16
, which compress quantum states into
fewer qubits via parameterized circuits; the QRA instead
processes classical data through a quantum reservoir and returns
classical output.
The noise-induced reservoir of Dudaš
et al.
3
serves as one concrete realization of the
nonlinear feature map within the QRA framework; other reservoir
architectures (e.g., Hamiltonian-driven
20
)
are equally valid instantiations.

VI.4
Comparison with quantum variational autoencoder

Table
6
reveals a substantial performance gap between
the QRA and the
\zeta
-QVAE baseline
10
.
Both methods employ the same four-equation protocol and feature
dimension (
D=56
), but differ in two key respects:
(i) the QRA uses a fixed reservoir with a linear readout
(ridge regression), whereas the
\zeta
-QVAE variationally optimizes
147 circuit parameters; and
(ii) the two noise models differ (reset channels versus depolarizing
noise), though the ideal and shot-noise conditions are directly
comparable.

Under ideal conditions, the QRA achieves machine-precision MSE
(
\sim 10^{-17}
) while the
\zeta
-QVAE yields MSE
\sim 0.2
.
This gap originates primarily in the
solver architecture

rather than the quantum circuit itself: the QRA’s ridge regression
yields an exact closed-form solution whenever the rank condition

D\geq N_{c}
is satisfied, whereas the
\zeta
-QVAE’s alternating
optimization over variational circuit parameters must navigate a
high-dimensional nonconvex landscape where convergence to a global
optimum is not guaranteed.

For blind decryption, the QRA two-phase protocol achieves
MSE
\sim 10^{-4}
versus
\sim 0.3
–
0.4
for the
\zeta
-QVAE,
again reflecting the advantage of the analytic readout.
We note that this comparison does not imply that variational quantum
circuits are inherently inferior; rather, it demonstrates that for
the specific task of reversible information transformation, the
reservoir computing paradigm—a fixed, sufficiently expressive
nonlinear embedding combined with analytic linear readout—is
better suited than end-to-end variational optimization.
A fairer comparison would require optimizing the
\zeta
-QVAE solver
or employing gradient-based methods, which we leave for future work.

VI.5
Complementary roles of Single-C and two-phase protocols

The two protocols serve complementary purposes:

•

Single-C
achieves the highest possible precision for
a
known
plaintext, making it suitable for one-time
verification of reservoir reversibility or secure in-sample
processing.

•

Two-phase
sacrifices in-sample precision in exchange
for the ability to decrypt
arbitrary unseen
messages,
establishing the feasibility of blind decryption as a proof of
concept.

In a practical deployment, Phase 1 would serve as a key
establishment procedure, after which Phase 2 enables ongoing blind
message exchange.

VI.6
The blind decoder barrier: necessity of shared training data

The Single-C blind decoder experiments (Exps 7–12) provide a
controlled test of whether self-consistent cross-path iteration
alone can break the circular dependency between
W_{\mathrm{dec}}

and
\mathbf{C}
.
The answer is decisively negative: across 6 experimental conditions
(3 noise levels
\times
2 qubit counts), 10 plaintext lengths, and
48 independent samples per configuration, the MSE consistently
saturates at
\approx 0.3
—approximately the level expected from
predicting a
\mathrm{Uniform}(-1,1)
random variable by its mean
(MSE
=1/3
).

This result has three important implications.
First, it establishes that the blind decryption problem
(Section
II.3
) cannot be solved by replacing the
ground-truth target with a bootstrap estimate in the ALS framework:
the self-consistent iteration converges to a fixed point that does
not correspond to the true plaintext.
Second, the noise-independence of the blind decoder MSE
(Table
7
) confirms that the bottleneck is

algorithmic
—the absence of an informative training
signal—rather than physical (noise, feature dimension, or qubit
count).
Third, and most importantly, the comparison with the two-phase
protocol (MSE
\sim 10^{-4}
) quantifies the value of shared
training data: the
M
training plaintexts in Phase 1 provide
exactly the external information needed to break the

W_{\mathrm{dec}}
–
\mathbf{C}
circular dependency, yielding a
three-order-of-magnitude improvement over the blind decoder.
The performance of the blind decoder (
\sim 0.3
) is notably
comparable to that of the
\zeta
-QVAE baseline
(
\sim 0.3
–
0.4
; Table
6
),
suggesting that variational circuit optimization in the

\zeta
-QVAE suffers from a similar inability to escape the
bootstrap fixed point without explicit plaintext information.

Precision and application scope.

We note that the two-phase MSE of
\sim 10^{-4}
corresponds to
a root-mean-squared error of
\sim 0.01
on
[-1,1]
-valued
plaintext elements.
This precision is insufficient for exact bitwise decryption as
required in conventional cryptography, but is relevant to
emerging applications involving continuous-valued quantum data
(e.g., quantum sensor outputs, analog signal encryption) where
approximate reconstruction within a known error tolerance is
acceptable.
The Single-C protocol under reset+shot noise achieves far higher
precision (MSE
\sim 10^{-14}
), approaching exact reconstruction,
but requires knowledge of the plaintext.
Improving the two-phase precision—for instance, through
error-correcting post-processing or adaptive training—remains
an important direction for practical deployment.

VI.7
Phase transition in the two-phase protocol

The
N_{q}
-dependence experiments (Exps 13–18;
Section
V.10
) reveal a previously uncharacterized
phase transition in the two-phase protocol.
Below the critical plaintext length
N_{c}^{*}\approx D_{\mathrm{aug}}
,
the per-position ridge regression is overdetermined and achieves
MSE
\sim 10^{-4}
independent of
N_{q}
.
Above
N_{c}^{*}
, the regression becomes underdetermined and the MSE
jumps to
0.1
–
0.25
, comparable to a random predictor.
The transition is sharp (occurring within a factor of
\sim 1.2
in

N_{c}
) and is preserved across all three noise conditions.

The quadratic scaling
D_{\mathrm{aug}}=N_{q}(N_{q}+1)/2+1+K

[Eq. (
25
)] provides a practical design rule:
for a target plaintext length
N_{c}
, the minimum qubit count
satisfying
D_{\mathrm{aug}}>N_{c}
is

N_{q}\gtrsim\lceil\sqrt{2N_{c}}\;\rceil
.
At
N_{q}=10
(
D_{\mathrm{aug}}=63
), all tested
N_{c}
values
(
\leq 35
) lie well below the transition, explaining why the
original experiments (Exps 2, 4, 6) showed no
N_{c}
-dependent
degradation.
Extending the protocol to longer plaintext lengths (e.g.,

N_{c}=100
) would require
N_{q}\gtrsim 14

(
D_{\mathrm{aug}}\approx 113
), which remains within the reach
of near-term density matrix simulation.

VI.8
Two-phase blind decoder: failure of statistical averaging

The two-phase blind decoder experiments (Exps 19–24;
Section
V.11
) provide a controlled test of whether
the
M
-sample statistical averaging of the two-phase protocol can
compensate for the absence of ground-truth regression targets.
The answer is decisively negative: the MSE (
\approx 0.53
) is not
only far above the standard two-phase level (
\sim 10^{-4}
) but
is also
worse
than the Single-C blind decoder (
\approx 0.3
)
and the random baseline (
1/3
).

This apparently paradoxical degradation—adding more data makes
the result worse—can be understood as follows.
In the Single-C blind decoder, the ALS iteration operates on a

single
plaintext–ciphertext pair, and the cross-path
feedback can partially align the reconstruction with the encoding
structure.
In the two-phase blind decoder, the per-position regression fits

M
training pairs whose targets are all incorrect (drawn from
cross-path estimates); the
M
-sample averaging reduces the
variance of these incorrect targets but does not reduce their

bias
.
The regression therefore converges to a low-variance but
high-bias estimator, which is worse than the noisier but less
biased Single-C cross-path iteration.

Together with the Single-C blind decoder results
(Section
V.9
), the two-phase blind decoder
establishes a hierarchy of blind decryption approaches:

1.

Standard two-phase
(ground-truth targets):
MSE
\sim 10^{-4}
(below phase transition).

2.

Single-C blind decoder
(self-consistent ALS on
one sample): MSE
\approx 1/3
.

3.

Two-phase blind decoder
(
M
-sample regression
with cross-path targets): MSE
\approx 0.53
.

This ordering demonstrates that the ground-truth training labels
are the single most important factor for blind decryption: neither
self-consistent iteration nor multi-sample statistical averaging
can substitute for explicit plaintext information.

VI.9
Comparison with variational quantum circuit baseline

As a further baseline, we compare the QRA with a quantum recurrent
neural network (QRNN) approach
1
, which implements
a similar encrypt–decrypt protocol using a parameterized quantum
circuit optimized with the parameter-shift rule.
The QRNN baseline employs 10 hidden qubits, 2 circuit layers, and
30 epochs of end-to-end (E2E) training with the Adam optimizer
(learning rate 0.02), evaluated under three noise conditions:
ideal (statevector), shot noise (
N_{\mathrm{shots}}=1000
), and depolarizing
noise (
p_{1q}=0.001
,
p_{2q}=0.01
).
A prediction-only (Pred-only) variant, analogous to the blind decoder
concept, freezes the decoder at the identity-function pretrained
weights and trains only the encoder.

The comparison reveals several noteworthy differences.
First, the QRA achieves dramatically higher precision under
ideal conditions: MSE
\sim 10^{-17}
(Single-C) and

\sim 10^{-4}
(two-phase) versus cross-entropy loss
\sim 0.26

for the QRNN E2E at
N_{c}=10
.
While the metrics differ (MSE versus cross-entropy), the QRA’s
near-zero reconstruction error is qualitatively superior to the
QRNN’s substantial residual loss.

Second, and more importantly, the two approaches exhibit a
striking difference in
noise robustness
.
Under depolarizing noise, the QRNN’s cross-entropy loss exceeds

\ln 2\approx 0.693
for
all
tested
N_{c}
values,
indicating complete protocol failure (performance worse than
random guessing).
In contrast, the QRA under reset+shot noise shows no
statistically significant performance degradation for the
two-phase protocol (Table
5
), and the
Single-C protocol degrades by only three orders of magnitude
(from
\sim 10^{-17}
to
\sim 10^{-14}
).
This disparity may reflect the fundamental difference between
the reservoir computing paradigm (fixed dynamics, analytic
readout) and the variational paradigm (gradient-based optimization
through noisy circuits), where noise corrupts both the forward
pass and the gradient estimation.

Third, the QRNN’s Pred-only variant achieves good performance for
short sequences (
N_{c}\leq 8
, cross-entropy loss
<0.01
) but
degrades rapidly for
N_{c}\geq 12
.
This partial success contrasts with the QRA’s Single-C blind
decoder, which fails at MSE
\approx 0.3
for all
N_{c}
.
The difference arises because the QRNN’s identity-function
pretraining provides a better initial approximation of the decoder
for short sequences, where the quantum state transformation is
nearly invertible.
The QRA’s reservoir transformation, being a fixed nonlinear
mapping, does not share this near-invertibility property, and
the blind decoder cannot recover from the poor initial estimate.

VI.10
Limitations

Several limitations should be noted.

First, while the pure-state and density-matrix simulation modes
share the same four-layer circuit architecture with 35 noise
parameters (Section
II.2
), they differ in whether the
CPTP reset channels are applied.
The density-matrix mode introduces genuine open-system dynamics
(mixed states, Bloch sphere contraction), which is the physical
mechanism underlying the shot-noise suppression effect.
The shot-noise-only experiment (Exp 3, pure-state with binomial
sampling) serves as a controlled comparison that isolates the
contribution of measurement noise from the open-system dynamics.

Second, the density matrix simulation required for the full
reset-noise model (Exps 5, 6) scales as
O(4^{N_{q}})
, which
restricted the number of random seeds in Exp 6 to 4 and the
maximum
M
to 100.
The statistical power of the two-phase significance tests
(Table
5
) is consequently limited, as
discussed in Section
V.7
.

Third, the
N_{q}
-dependence study (Exps 13–18;
Section
V.10
) confirms the
O(N_{q}^{2})

feature-dimension scaling and identifies a sharp phase transition
at
N_{c}\approx D_{\mathrm{aug}}
[Eq. (
25
)].
While this provides a clear design rule, the exponential
computational cost of density matrix simulation
[
O(4^{N_{q}})
] limits exploration to
N_{q}\leq 10
for the
full noise model; extending to
N_{q}\geq 15
would require
approximate simulation methods.
The computational cost of density matrix simulation renders

N_{q}>15
infeasible without approximations for the open-system
model.

Fourth, and critically, the current analysis does not address the
cryptographic security of the protocol in a formal sense, and the
results should be interpreted as a proof of concept for
reservoir-based information transformation rather than a
deployment-ready cryptographic scheme.
The encoding and decoding functions
F
and
G

[Eqs. (
5
)–(
6
)] are deterministic,
smooth, and element-wise, which may be exploitable by an adversary
with access to the reservoir structure.
The security of the scheme rests on the assumption that the
quantum reservoir dynamics are sufficiently complex to prevent
inversion without knowledge of the shared keys and noise
configuration; this assumption has not been formally verified.
In particular, Phase 1 of the two-phase protocol exposes
M

plaintext–ciphertext pairs
\{(\mathbf{C}_{j},\bm{\gamma}_{j})\}
,
which could enable a chosen-plaintext attack if the adversary can
observe or influence the training set.
A rigorous security analysis—including threat models, assumptions
about adversarial access, computational complexity bounds, and
comparison with established post-quantum
schemes
13
—is required before any practical
deployment and is left for future work.

VII
Conclusion

We have demonstrated that the quantum reservoir autoencoder (QRA)
supports reversible information transformation and enables blind
decryption through a two-phase protocol.
Our results establish nine main findings:
(i) the QRA achieves machine-precision reconstruction under ideal
conditions with 56-dimensional feature vectors;
(ii) the open-system QRA regime with random (non-optimized) reset
noise probabilities suppresses shot-noise sensitivity by ten orders
of magnitude, yielding MSE
\sim 10^{-14}
compared to
\sim 10^{-3}

in the closed-system regime under shot noise;
(iii) the two-phase protocol resolves the open blind decryption
challenge by learning per-position decoding weights from shared
training data;
(iv) the two-phase protocol exhibits noise-independent performance,
with no significant MSE differences among ideal, shot-noise, and
reset-plus-shot-noise conditions;
(v) a Single-C blind decoder variant in which the decoder has zero
knowledge of the plaintext saturates at MSE
\approx 0.3
,
establishing that shared training data is the essential ingredient
for blind decryption and that self-consistent iteration alone
cannot break the
W_{\mathrm{dec}}
–
\mathbf{C}
circular
dependency;
(vi) a comparison with a quantum variational autoencoder
(
\zeta
-QVAE) baseline reveals a substantial performance advantage
for the QRA, attributable primarily to the analytic
readout solver;
(vii) the two-phase protocol exhibits a sharp phase transition at

N_{c}\approx D_{\mathrm{aug}}=N_{q}(N_{q}+1)/2+1+K
, below which
MSE
\sim 10^{-4}
and above which the reconstruction fails—the

O(N_{q}^{2})
scaling of
D_{\mathrm{aug}}
provides a practical design
rule for selecting the minimum qubit count for a target plaintext
length;
(viii) a two-phase blind decoder variant, combining multi-sample
regression with cross-path estimation, yields MSE
\approx 0.53

(worse than the random baseline of
1/3
), demonstrating that
statistical averaging cannot substitute for ground-truth training
labels and that the failure of blind decryption is fundamental
rather than statistical; and
(ix) a comparison with a quantum recurrent neural network
(QRNN)
1
baseline reveals that the QRA’s
fixed-reservoir architecture provides dramatically superior noise
robustness: the QRNN protocol is completely destroyed under
depolarizing noise (loss
>\ln 2
for all
N_{c}
), while the QRA
maintains noise-invariant performance.

These results establish the feasibility of noise-resilient reversible
information transformation and blind decryption using quantum
reservoirs.
The two-phase protocol’s noise invariance and the identification of
the
D_{\mathrm{aug}}
-governed phase transition are particularly
relevant for near-term quantum devices
15
, as they
suggest both that error mitigation may not be required for this class
of reservoir-based protocols and that the required qubit count can be
precisely determined from the target plaintext length.
The systematic failure of all blind decryption attempts—across
Single-C, two-phase, and multi-sample variants—establishes that
shared training data is the irreducible requirement for blind
decryption within the reservoir autoencoder framework.
We emphasize that the current work is a proof of concept;
practical deployment requires addressing several open questions:
scalability to larger qubit counts, formal cryptographic security
analysis under realistic threat models, theoretical bounds on the
minimum training set size
M
, reduction of the two-phase
reconstruction error to levels suitable for discrete-valued
encryption, and experimental validation on quantum hardware.
