MAVEN-T: Multi-Agent enVironment-aware Enhanced Neural Trajectory predictor with Reinforcement Learning
Wenchang Duan, Zhenguo Gao, Yaoliang Yu, Yi Shi

Abstract
Trajectory prediction remains a critical yet challenging component in autonomous driving systems, requiring sophisticated reasoning capabilities while meeting stringent real-time deployment constraints. While knowledge distillation has demonstrated effectiveness in model compression, existing approaches fail to preserve complex decision-making capabilities during distillation, particularly in dynamic multi-agent scenarios. This paper introduces MAVEN-T, a teacher-student framework achieving state-of-the-art trajectory prediction through complementary architectural co-design and progressive distillation. The teacher employs hybrid attention mechanisms for maximum representational capacity, while the student uses efficient architectures optimized for deployment. Knowledge transfer occurs via multi-granular distillation with adaptive curriculum learning that dynamically adjusts complexity based on performance. Critically, the framework incorporates reinforcement learning to break the ”imitation ceiling” of traditional distillation—enabling the student to verify, refine, and optimize teacher knowledge through dynamic environmental interaction, potentially achieving more robust decision-making than the teacher itself. Extensive experiments on NGSIM and highD datasets demonstrate 6.2× parameter compression and 3.7× inference speedup while maintaining state-of-the-art accuracy, establishing a new paradigm for deploying sophisticated reasoning models under resource constraints.

Introduction

Autonomous driving has emerged as a representative application of intelligent perception and decision-making systems, with broad potential for improving transportation safety, efficiency, and mobility. However, Autonomous driving systems require sophisticated perception and decision-making capabilities while operating under stringent real-time constraints. Current trajectory prediction models face a fundamental trade-off between computational efficiency and reasoning sophistication, creating barriers to practical deployment in safety-critical applications.

Recent advances have demonstrated impressive performance through sophisticated architectures. Tamba introduced selective state-space models achieving linear computational complexity while maintaining prediction accuracy. However, current approaches still require substantial computational resources exceeding edge deployment constraints, as they assume a single model architecture must simultaneously optimize for both representational capacity and computational efficiency.

Current approaches suffer from three fundamental gaps:
(1)
Existing frameworks assume architectural similarity between teacher and student models, preventing exploitation of complementary design principles.
(2)
Fixed distillation objectives fail to adapt to dynamic driving scenario complexity.
(3)
Approaches neglect the hierarchical nature of driving decisions, from perceptual features to semantic reasoning.

This work proposes Multi-Agent enVironment-aware Enhanced Neural Trajectory predictor
MAVEN-T
, addressing these limitations through three innovations:
Complementary Architectural Design
employing different principles for teacher (hybrid attention with Mamba blocks and shift-window attention) and student (GRU-based modeling with squeeze-and-excitation mechanisms).
Progressive Adaptive Curriculum
dynamically adjusting distillation complexity based on student performance and scenario characteristics.
Multi-Granular Knowledge Distillation
capturing knowledge transfer across perceptual, contextual, and semantic levels.

The main contributions include:
(1)
A teacher-student framework with complementary architectural designs.
(2)
Progressive adaptive curriculum learning adjusting distillation complexity dynamically.
(3)
Multi-granular distillation objectives preserving complete decision-making capabilities.
(4)
Demonstration of significant computational reductions while maintaining performance on autonomous driving benchmarks.

Extensive experiments demonstrate that MAVEN-T achieves substantial computational efficiency gains while preserving sophisticated reasoning capabilities, enabling practical deployment of advanced autonomous driving models in resource-constrained environments.

III
Methodology

III-A

Overall Architecture Design

This work presents a novel teacher–student knowledge–distillation framework that addresses the computational constraints of real-time autonomous driving while preserving sophisticated reasoning capabilities. The approach leverages complementary architectural designs wherein the teacher model maximises representational capacity through hybrid attention mechanisms, whereas the student model achieves deployment efficiency via parameter-efficient adaptations.

The student network (lower) employs lightweight GRU–SE encoding and LoRA-parameterised policy heads for efficient knowledge transfer. Multi-level distillation enables feature alignment across intermediate representations while maintaining policy optimisation through environmental feedback.

III-B

Surround-aware Graph Neural Encoder

The surround-aware encoder addresses the fundamental challenge of modeling complex inter-vehicle relationships in dynamic driving environments. This component processes multi-vehicle trajectory data through a graph neural network architecture that captures both spatial proximity and temporal dependencies between vehicles.

The encoder operates on context matrices derived from multi-vehicle trajectory sequences, where each vehicle
i
at timestep
t
is represented by state vector
s_{t}^{i}=[x_{t}^{i},y_{t}^{i},v_{t}^{i},\theta_{t}^{i},a_{t}^{i}]
encompassing position, velocity, orientation, and acceleration. These states are aggregated into context matrices
\mathbf{C}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_{context}}
that encode spatiotemporal relationships across the vehicle ensemble.

The graph construction process establishes dynamic adjacency matrices
\mathbf{A}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
based on spatial proximity and interaction relevance:

\mathbf{A}_{t,ij}=\begin{cases}\exp\left(-\frac{d_{ij}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)&\text{if }d_{ij}<r_{thresh}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(3)

where
d_{ij}
represents the Euclidean distance between vehicles
i
and
j
,
\sigma
controls the decay rate, and
r_{thresh}
defines the interaction radius threshold.

The core processing pipeline applies causal convolutional layers followed by Graph Attention Network v2 (GATv2) operations. The causal CNN preserves temporal causality while extracting local temporal patterns:

\mathbf{H}^{(0)}=\text{CausalCNN}(\mathbf{C}_{1:t})

(4)

Subsequently, GATv2 layers model inter-vehicle attention mechanisms through improved attention coefficient computation:

\alpha_{ij}^{(l)}=\frac{\exp(\text{LeakyReLU}(\mathbf{a}^{T}[\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(l)}\oplus\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}_{j}^{(l)}]))}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}(i)}\exp(\text{LeakyReLU}(\mathbf{a}^{T}[\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(l)}\oplus\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}_{k}^{(l)}]))}

(5)

where
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(l)}
denotes the hidden representation of vehicle
i
at layer
l
,
\mathbf{W}^{(l)}
represents learnable transformation matrices,
\mathbf{a}
is the attention parameter vector, and
\oplus
indicates concatenation.

The node update mechanism aggregates neighborhood information through weighted summation:

\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(l+1)}=\sigma\left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}\alpha_{ij}^{(l)}\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}_{j}^{(l)}\right)

(6)

To enhance training stability and convergence, RMSNorm normalization replaces traditional batch normalization:

\text{RMSNorm}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathbf{x}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{2}+\epsilon}}\odot\mathbf{g}

(7)

where
\mathbf{g}
represents learnable scale parameters and
\epsilon
provides numerical stability.

The dual-layer GATv2 architecture with interleaved RMSNorm operations produces rich vehicle interaction representations that capture both local neighborhood effects and global traffic flow patterns. This encoding forms the foundation for subsequent attention mechanisms in both teacher and student architectures, providing contextually-aware vehicle representations essential for sophisticated driving policy learning.

III-C

Hybrid Attention Mechanism in Teacher Model

The teacher model employs a sophisticated hybrid attention architecture that combines complementary temporal and spatial modeling capabilities to achieve comprehensive scene understanding. This design addresses the dual challenges of capturing long-range temporal dependencies and modeling complex spatial interactions in autonomous driving scenarios.

Fig. 2:
Hybrid attention mechanism in the teacher model. The architecture processes temporal sequences through Mamba blocks with state space modeling (top), applies shift-window attention for spatial reasoning (middle), and employs mixture-of-experts routing for computational efficiency (bottom). Mathematical formulations show the core operations at each stage.

The hybrid attention architecture, illustrated in Figure
2
, demonstrates the sequential processing flow from temporal Mamba blocks through spatial shift-window attention to expert routing mechanisms.

The teacher encoder incorporates Mamba blocks for efficient temporal sequence modeling prior to attention computation. Given the output from adaptive deformable speed-aware pooling
\mathbf{F}_{pool}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d_{model}}
, the Mamba block processes temporal dependencies through state space models:

\mathbf{h}_{t}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{t}

(8)

\mathbf{y}_{t}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{h}_{t}+\mathbf{D}\mathbf{x}_{t}

(9)

where
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D}
represent learnable state space parameters,
\mathbf{h}_{t}
denotes the hidden state, and
\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{y}_{t}
correspond to input and output at timestep
t
.

The Mamba block enables linear computational complexity
O(T)
for sequence length
T
, contrasting favorably with quadratic complexity of standard attention mechanisms. This efficiency gain proves crucial for processing extended temporal horizons in driving scenarios.

Following temporal processing, the architecture generates query, key, and value representations through learned projections:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=\text{Mamba}(\mathbf{F}_{pool})\mathbf{W}_{Q}

(10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}

\displaystyle=\text{Mamba}(\mathbf{F}_{pool})\mathbf{W}_{K}

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\text{Mamba}(\mathbf{F}_{pool})\mathbf{W}_{V}

(12)

The shift-window attention mechanism partitions the spatial feature map into non-overlapping windows of size
M\times M
, computing self-attention within each window to reduce computational complexity while maintaining modeling capacity:

\text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V})=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{T}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}+\mathbf{B}\right)\mathbf{V}

(13)

where
\mathbf{B}
represents the relative position bias matrix that encodes spatial relationships between windows.

The decoder architecture integrates mixture-of-experts (MoE) transformers to achieve computational efficiency while maintaining representational capacity. The MoE mechanism routes inputs to a subset of expert networks:

\text{MoE}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}G(\mathbf{x})_{i}\cdot E_{i}(\mathbf{x})

(14)

where
G(\mathbf{x})
represents the gating function that produces expert selection probabilities,
E_{i}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the
i
-th expert network, and
N
signifies the total number of experts.

The gating mechanism employs a learned router network with top-
k
selection strategy:

G(\mathbf{x})=\text{TopK}(\text{softmax}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{W}_{g}),k)

(15)

where
\mathbf{W}_{g}
represents the gating network parameters and
k=2
experts are selected per input to balance computational efficiency and model capacity.

The decoder concludes with Mamba blocks that provide additional temporal refinement of the multimodal representations before final policy generation. This hybrid architecture effectively combines the linear scalability of state space models with the spatial modeling capabilities of window-based attention, enabling the teacher model to process complex driving scenarios with both computational efficiency and representational sophistication. The integration of MoE further optimizes computational allocation by activating only relevant expert networks for each input, resulting in significant efficiency gains without compromising performance.

Multi-granular feature alignment.

Low-, mid-, and high-level objectives
transfer perception, attention, and semantics:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{low}}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbf{F}^{T}_{\text{low}}-\text{Adapter}(\mathbf{F}^{S}_{\text{low}})\right\rVert^{2},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{att}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{THW}\!\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{w=1}^{W}\bigl|\mathbf{A}^{T}_{t,h,w}-\mathbf{A}^{S}_{t,h,w}\bigr|^{2},

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{semantic}}

\displaystyle=-\log\frac{\exp\!\bigl(\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{z}^{T},\mathbf{z}^{S})/\tau\bigr)}{\sum_{i}\exp\!\bigl(\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf{z}^{T},\mathbf{z}^{S}_{i})/\tau\bigr)}.

(18)

Reinforcement-augmented distillation.

The student network employs Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to optimize driving policy through environmental feedback. The reward function
\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t})
combines multiple safety and efficiency objectives:

\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t})=w_{1}R_{\text{safety}}(s_{t},a_{t})+w_{2}R_{\text{comfort}}(s_{t},a_{t})+w_{3}R_{\text{efficiency}}(s_{t},a_{t})

(19)

where safety reward penalizes collision risk and traffic violations:

R_{\text{safety}}(s_{t},a_{t})=-\exp\left(-\frac{d_{\min}(s_{t},a_{t})}{d_{\text{safe}}}\right)-\lambda_{\text{viol}}\mathbf{1}_{\text{violation}}(s_{t},a_{t})

(20)

comfort reward encourages smooth acceleration profiles:

R_{\text{comfort}}(s_{t},a_{t})=-\|a_{t}-a_{t-1}\|^{2}-\mu\max(0,|a_{t}|-a_{\max})

(21)

and efficiency reward promotes progress toward destination:

R_{\text{efficiency}}(s_{t},a_{t})=v_{t}\cos(\theta_{\text{heading}}-\theta_{\text{target}})-\nu\Delta t

(22)

The PPO objective maximizes expected cumulative reward with clipped policy updates:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}}=\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\min\left(r_{t}(\theta)\hat{A}_{t},\text{clip}(r_{t}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_{t}\right)\right]

(23)

where
r_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}|s_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_{t}|s_{t})}
and
\hat{A}_{t}
represents the advantage estimate.

The training alternates between distillation and RL phases with annealing weights:

\alpha_{t}=\alpha_{0}\exp(-\lambda_{\alpha}t),\quad\beta_{t}=\beta_{0}(1-\exp(-\lambda_{\beta}t))

(24)

Adaptive curriculum with hyperparameter specification.

Scenario complexity quantification employs empirically-tuned weights based on cross-validation:

\mathcal{C}(s)=0.3N_{\text{objects}}+0.4V_{\text{relative}}+0.3\mathcal{H}_{\text{trajectory}}

(25)

where
N_{\text{objects}}
denotes the number of surrounding vehicles,
V_{\text{relative}}
represents relative velocity magnitude, and
\mathcal{H}_{\text{trajectory}}
measures trajectory entropy. The curriculum advances according to:

\mathcal{C}_{k+1}=\mathcal{C}_{k}+\Delta\mathcal{C}\cdot\min\left(1,\frac{\text{Acc}_{\text{stu}}-\text{Acc}_{\text{th}}}{\text{Acc}_{\text{margin}}}\right)

(26)

with
\Delta\mathcal{C}=0.1
,
\text{Acc}_{\text{th}}=0.85
, and
\text{Acc}_{\text{margin}}=0.05
determined through grid search validation.

Regularisation and stability.

To avoid catastrophic forgetting when the curriculum changes,
Elastic-Weight Consolidation (EWC) is applied:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{EWC}}=\sum_{i}\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\mathbf{F}_{i}\,\bigl(\theta_{i}-\theta_{i}^{*}\bigr)^{2},

(27)

where
\mathbf{F}_{i}
is the Fisher information and
\theta_{i}^{*}
the
parameter snapshot at the previous curriculum stage.

Unified training objective.

The student training combines knowledge distillation with reinforcement learning through a unified loss function. The task-specific loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}
corresponds to the student loss defined in Eq.
28

\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{student}}=\alpha_{t}\|\text{Output}_{\text{stu}}-\text{Output}_{\text{tea}}\|^{2}-\beta_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}}

(28)

The complete training objective integrates all distillation components:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}+\xi_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\text{low}}+\zeta_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\text{att}}+\eta_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\text{semantic}}+\psi_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\text{EWC}}

(29)

where stage-dependent weights follow exponential scheduling:

\xi_{t}=\xi_{0}e^{-\lambda_{\xi}t},\quad\zeta_{t}=\zeta_{0}e^{-\lambda_{\zeta}t},\quad\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}(1-e^{-\lambda_{\eta}t}),\quad\psi_{t}=\psi_{0}

(30)

IV
Experiments

We conduct comprehensive experiments to evaluate MAVEN-T against state-of-the-art trajectory prediction methods on real-world datasets. Our evaluation demonstrates significant improvements in prediction accuracy while achieving substantial computational efficiency gains through the proposed knowledge distillation framework.

IV-A

Experimental Setup

Datasets.

We evaluate on two widely-used real-world trajectory prediction datasets: NGSIM, containing detailed vehicle trajectories collected on I-80 eastbound in San Francisco Bay Area and US 101 southbound in Los Angeles at 10Hz sampling frequency; and highD, comprising 110,500 vehicle trajectories collected on a 420-meter bidirectional highway segment near Cologne, Germany at 25Hz sampling frequency.

Evaluation Metrics.

We employ standard metrics: Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for trajectory distribution quality assessment, Average Displacement Error (ADE) measuring average error across the prediction horizon, and Final Displacement Error (FDE) evaluating endpoint prediction accuracy.

Baseline Methods.

We compare against recent approaches including V-LSTM, S-LSTM, CS-LSTM for sequential modeling; STDAN for hierarchical motion modeling; WSiP for wave pooling interactions; and C2F-TP representing current state-of-the-art coarse-to-fine frameworks. For student networks, we compare with MobileNet-Traj, DistilBERT-Traj, and Lightweight-LSTM architectures.

Theoretical Analysis and Mathematical Foundations

V-A

Convergence Analysis of Progressive Distillation

Theorem 1

(Convergence of MAVEN-T Training)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}^{(k)}
denote the total loss at curriculum stage
k
. Under the following conditions:

1.

The teacher network
f_{\theta_{T}}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L_{T}

2.

The student network
f_{\theta_{S}}
satisfies the universal approximation property

3.

The curriculum complexity function
\mathcal{C}(s)
is monotonically increasing

4.

Learning rates satisfy
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}=\infty
and
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}^{2}<\infty

Then the student network converges to a local minimum of the combined objective:

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_{\theta_{S}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}^{(k)}\|^{2}]=0

(37)

Proof.

The proof follows from the convergence properties of stochastic gradient descent under the given Lipschitz and smoothness conditions. The progressive curriculum ensures that the loss landscape becomes increasingly well-conditioned as training progresses.

Define the Lyapunov function
V^{(k)}=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}^{(k)}]
. The curriculum progression guarantees:

V^{(k+1)}-V^{(k)}\leq-\mu\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla_{\theta_{S}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}^{(k)}\|^{2}]+\frac{L^{2}\eta_{k}^{2}}{2}

(38)

where
\mu>0
is the strong convexity parameter in the neighborhood of the optimum.

Summing over all stages and applying the learning rate conditions yields the desired convergence result.
∎

V-B

Generalization Bound Analysis

Theorem 2

(PAC-Bayes Generalization Bound)
.

With probability at least
1-\delta
, the true risk of the distilled student network satisfies:

\mathcal{R}(\hat{\theta}_{S})\leq\hat{\mathcal{R}}(\hat{\theta}_{S})+\sqrt{\frac{\text{KL}(Q\|P)+\ln(2\sqrt{n}/\delta)}{2(n-1)}}

(39)

where
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
denotes empirical risk,
Q
is the posterior over student parameters,
P
is the prior, and
n
is the sample size.

Approximation Error Analysis

The distillation error can be decomposed as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{total}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{\text{approx}}+\mathcal{E}_{\text{estimation}}+\mathcal{E}_{\text{optimization}}

(40)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\theta_{S}}\|\pi_{\theta_{T}}-\pi_{\theta_{S}}\|_{\mathcal{H}}+\|\pi_{\hat{\theta}_{S}}-\pi_{\theta_{S}^{*}}\|_{\mathcal{H}}+\|\pi_{\tilde{\theta}_{S}}-\pi_{\hat{\theta}_{S}}\|_{\mathcal{H}}

(41)

where
\mathcal{H}
denotes the reproducing kernel Hilbert space of policies,
\theta_{S}^{*}
is the optimal student parameter, and
\tilde{\theta}_{S}
is the computed parameter.

VII
Conclusion

This paper presents MAVEN-T, a knowledge distillation framework resolving the conflict between reasoning complexity and deployment efficiency in autonomous driving. The core principle employs complementary co-design: a powerful teacher with hybrid attention mechanisms paired with a lightweight GRU-based student. Multi-granular adaptive curriculum distillation, enhanced by reinforcement learning, transfers nuanced decision-making capabilities while breaking the imitation ceiling. MAVEN-T achieves state-of-the-art prediction accuracy with 6.2× parameter compression and 3.7× inference speedup. The findings validate that distinct, synergistic teacher-student architectures provide an effective pathway for deploying advanced reasoning in resource-constrained, safety-critical systems. In the future, the proposed framework can be further extended toward more adaptive and parameter-efficient learning paradigms
[
21
,
20
]
. These directions may further improve the flexibility, robustness, and scalability of trajectory prediction systems in complex real-world environments.
