Comparative analysis of laboratory-based and portable qPCR platforms: CFX, MIC, and Biomeme Franklin for African swine fever and avian influenza detection
Authors: Agathe Auer, Tirumala B. K. Settypalli, Andriy Rozstalnyy, Artem Metlin, Adi Steinrigl, Pawin Padungtod, William G. Dundon, Charles E. Lamien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid diagnosis is critical for the early detection and control of transboundary animal diseases such as African Swine Fever (ASF) and Avian Influenza (AI), particularly because it enables timely containment measures at the farm level. In low-resource settings where sample transport, cold chain maintenance, and access to centralized laboratories are challenging, portable qPCR systems offer a clear operational advantage by enabling on-site or near-site testing thus reducing diagnostic turnaround and response time. This study evaluated three qPCR platforms for detecting African Swine Fever Virus (ASFV) and Avian Influenza Virus (AIV): two laboratory-based systems, CFX96 Touch (Bio-Rad) and MIC (BioMolecular Systems), and the portable qPCR platform Franklin by Biomemex. Seventy-two ASF virus samples representing genotypes I, II, IX, XIV, XXIII, and a genotype I/II recombinant from eleven countries were tested on two laboratory platforms (CFX96, MIC) and one rapid platform (Franklin) alongside 50 AI virus samples (H5N1, H9N2) from seven countries.ResultsBoth laboratory platforms achieved consistent results. Franklin required protocol optimization when the WOAH-recommended protocol produced predominantly false-negative results, but adapted amplification conditions restored performance. Limits of detection per reaction calculated by probit analysis were: CFX96 21.28 (14.85–68.67), MIC 32.76 (21.12–96.11), Franklin with lyophilized reagents 4.12 (2.71–14.97), and Franklin with adapted protocol 6.85 (4.33–20.11). After adaptation, all platforms exhibited high diagnostic sensitivity and specificity for ASFV detection. For the AIV detection protocol, false negative results were observed on MIC and Franklin platforms compared to the CFX96.ConclusionsThe laboratory platforms tested in this study (CFX96 and MIC) provided robust high-throughput capabilities, while the portable system (Franklin) offered superior sensitivity but required protocol adaptation of established WOAH-recommended procedures.

Background

African Swine Fever Virus (ASFV) and Avian influenza Virus (AIV) are two of the most economically and epidemiologically significant transboundary animal diseases, with severe consequences for food security, livelihoods, and international trade [10, 23, 25, 45]. ASF continues to spread across multiple regions, while highly pathogenic AI viruses pose persistent threats to poultry production and carry zoonotic potential [4–7, 19]. For both diseases accurate and rapid detection is critical for surveillance and outbreak containment. Effective surveillance and rapid laboratory confirmation are therefore central to national and international disease control, forming the basis for early detection, and implementation of control measures. Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) is the gold standard for both ASFV and AIV detection due to its high sensitivity and specificity [46–48]. Early detection of both viruses is essential for implementing timely containment measures and preventing disease spread [13]. POC qPCR systems enable decentralized molecular testing through compact, battery-powered instrumentation and accelerated thermal cycling. These platforms perform real-time fluorescence-based detection of DNA and RNA targets, typically supporting multiplex assays with total run times of 30–60 min [24]. For example, the Franklin system employs smartphone-based assay programming and data analysis, while the Biomeme platform integrates simplified nucleic acid extraction workflows and lyophilized reagents to facilitate field deployment [8]. Similarly, the PicoGene mobile real-time PCR system combines rapid amplification with sensitive optical detection in a lightweight format, supporting on-site molecular analyses for pathogen detection [30]. However, portable qPCR platforms have inherent limitations. Mendoza-Gallegos et al. [31] reported that the predecessor of the current Franklin system, while effective, exhibited reduced reaction precision, lower thermal cycling efficiency, and sensitivity limitations for low-copy-number targets. These observations emphasize the need to assess not only the sensitivity and specificity of portable qPCR systems but also their reproducibility. Recent field evaluations have demonstrated the potential of portable qPCR systems for ASFV detection, with studies in Vietnam using the T-COR8 (Tetracore Inc., Rockville, MD) and the Franklin system showing comparable sensitivity and specificity to the World Organization of Animal Health (WOAH)-listed reference assays ([26, 36, 42] Daigle et al., 2021). Similarly, a portable system (RAPID®, Idaho Technology, Salt Lake city, UT) was applied to AIV surveillance in wildlife and poultry operations [40], highlighting their importance for controlling highly pathogenic strains that pose threats to both animal and human health.

Despite these promising developments, a systematic comparison of portable and laboratory-based qPCR platforms for both ASFV and AIV detection remains limited, particularly regarding protocol adaptation requirements and performance across diverse genotypes and geographical samples. This study compared two laboratory-based qPCR platforms (Bio-Rad CFX96 and MIC) with one portable system (Franklin) for detecting ASFV across multiple genotypes from eleven countries and AIV subtypes H5N1 and H9N2.The CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA) is a benchtop qPCR instrument with 96 wells and multiplex fluorescence detection and served as the reference platform. The Magnetic Induction Cycler (MIC) (MIC; Biomolecular Systems, Australia) is a compact benchtop system using magnetic induction heating, providing real-time detection in 48 wells, multiplex capability, and compatibility with standard chemistries (MIC; Biomolecular Systems, Australia). The Biomeme Franklin™ (Biomeme Inc., USA) is a battery-powered field device with 9 wells, up to three channels, and smartphone control [8]. It succeeds the three9™, improving thermal uniformity, ramp rates, fluorescence detection, and hardware robustness while remaining compact and low power. In this study we determined detection limits of all three systems and assessed protocol adaptation requirements, and evaluated whether WOAH-recommended protocols require optimization for portable platforms.

Material and Methods

Sample description

ASFV DNA samples used were archived at − 20 °C at the Animal Production Health Laboratory, Seibersdorf, Austria, and confirmed positive by PCR in previous studies. The samples originated from Senegal (n = 1), Mali (n = 1), Burkina Faso (n = 10) [32], Tanzania (n = 4) [12], Mongolia (n = 3) [2], Namibia (n = 1) [33], Ivory Coast (n = 4) [14], Democratic Republic of Congo (n = 18) [34], Indonesia (n = 1) [17], Nigeria (n = 2) [29], Ethiopia (n = 5) [1], Vietnam (n = 8), Laos (n = 4), Zambia (n = 3), Mozambique (n = 5), and Kenya (n = 2) (unpublished). The collection represented ASFV genotypes I, II, IX, XVI, XXIII, and recombinant viruses. ASF was confirmed positive by each National Reference Laboratories from Mali, Burkina Faso, Tanzania, Mongolia, Namibia, Ivory Coast, Democratic Republic of Congo, Indonesia, Nigeria, Ethiopia, Vietnam, Laos, Zambia, Mozambique and Kenya where samples originated. Extraction methods are described in the published manuscript cited after the samples country of origin.

ASFV-negative control samples included Salmonella spp. (n = 44) (stored samples from the repository of the Animal Production and Health Laboratory, Joint FAO/IAEA centre) and other pathogens: Porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus 1 and 2 (n = 2), Swine vesicular disease virus (n = 1), Influenza A virus (n = 1), Campylobacter jejuni (n = 1), Campylobacter coli (n = 1), Listeria monocytogenes (n = 1), Shigella flexneri (n = 1), Escherichia coli (n = 1) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa provided by the Department for Molecular Biology of the Austrian Institute for Veterinary Disease Control (AGES) Mödling, Austria. All samples were ASFV-negative by qPCR following King et al. [27].

Preparation of the standard sample for quantification

Briefly, the targeted fragment of the p72 gene of ASFV was amplified by PCR using the primers indicated in Bastos et al., 2003. The positive PCR products were purified and cloned into the pGEM®-T Vector system (Promega). The plasmids were sequenced by LGC genomics to confirm the presence of the correct target. The plasmid concentration was determined fluorometrically using Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen) and a NanoDrop 3300 Fluorospectrometer (Thermo Scientific) and converted into copy numbers as previously described by [3].

The DNA from an ASFV positive clinical sample from Burkina Faso was quantified based on a standard curve constructed from the serially diluted plasmid.

Serial dilutions were prepared from this sample and used to determine each method's lower limits of detection.

AIV samples included 50 positive and 28 negative samples. Positive samples were archived RNA stored at − 80 °C, confirmed by RT-PCR [20, 38] and retested using Nagy et al. (2020) protocol. Negative samples derived from poultry positive for Newcastle Disease Virus (n = 10) and Infectious Bronchitis Virus (n = 18) (unpublished).

Platform specifications and protocols

The platforms were assessed for sensitivity and specificity, while the WOAH-recommended real-time PCR protocol described by King et al. [27], performed on a standard laboratory real-time PCR cycler (CFX platform), was used as the reference method for comparison [49] (Table 1).

Assays 1, 2 and 4: Primers targeting the p72 gene, and a FAM-labelled probe published by King et al., 2003, with TAMRA quencher replaced by BHQ1, were amplified using the five qPCR master mixes. The running times on the CFX96 Touch Deep Well Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) were 40 min for iQ Supermix (Assay no. 1 and 2). Briefly, reactions were prepared using iQ™ Supermix in a final volume of 20 µl, containing 2 µl of template DNA, 10 µl of master mix, 0.4 µl of each primer (4 pmol/µl), and 0.5 µl of probe (2.5 pmol/µl). Thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 58 °C for 30 s. When applying the original amplification conditions described by King et al. [27], false-negative results were observed for assay 4. Sensitivity improved after adapting the amplification protocol to the standard DNA cycling conditions used on the Franklin™ platform, consisting of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 1 s and 58 °C for 20 s.

For assay 3, a final volume of 20ul was reached by adding 18 ul of water and 2 ul of DNA. the amplification protocol to the standard DNA cycling conditions used on the Franklin™ platform, consisting of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 1 s and 60 °C for 20 s.

To determine whether the false-negative results observed with the Franklin device were specific to the King et al. [27] protocol, we tested an alternative WOAH-recommended assay across all platforms. Accordingly, in assays 5–7 we applied the WOAH-recommended protocol described by Nagy et al. [35] without any modification and a final primer concentrations of 1.4 µM each and a probe concentration of 0.4 µM. The reaction mixture, amplification conditions, and result interpretation strictly followed the manufacturer’s instructions, using 2 µl of RNA per reaction in a total reaction volume of 20 µl [47]. Amplification was performed using the following thermoprofile: 45 °C for 10 min, 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 64 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 10 s. Fluorescence signal acquisition was performed in the FAM channel at the end of the 64 °C annealing step.

Analytical sensitivity and limit of detection

We assessed analytical sensitivity for assays 1 to 4 (Table 1) using serial dilutions of ASFV DNA quantified by spectrophotometry against known plasmid standards [32]. Franklin platforms were tested at 30, 20, 10, and 5 gene copies/reaction,laboratory platforms at 70, 60, 50, 40, 30, 20, and 10 gene copies/reaction. Each dilution was tested in duplicate across four runs (n = 8 replicates total). The LOD of assays no 1–4 was determined by using the "ecotox" package in R version 4.0.5 (2021–03–31) via RStudio Version 1.4.1106. The package’ecotox’ calculates the predicted detection limits and their confidence limits (CL) using probit analysis according to [22, 39, 44].

Statistical Methods

Inter-assay agreement was assessed using Fleiss' kappa (categorical data) and Spearman correlation (Ct values, non-normal distribution by Shapiro–Wilk test). Bland–Altman plots assessed platform agreement. Analysis used R (v4.4.2) with significance at p < 0.05. ROC analysis was performed using the pROC package in R with CFX96 as reference standard. AUC values with 95% confidence intervals were calculated, and optimal classification thresholds were determined using maximum Youden index (sensitivity + specificity—1). Visualization used ggplot2.

Results and discussion

Adjustment of amplification protocol for Franklin qPCR platform

On the Franklin platform, two reagent systems were tested: the lyophilized GoStrips from Biomeme, which include primers and probes targeting the ASFV p72 gene, and the King et al. [27] protocol using iQ Supermix qPCR reagent. When using the King et al. [27] protocol with the iQ qPCR master mix, only five samples out of 72 from the positive panel were detected as positive. Detection improved only after modifying the amplification protocol (Table 2). False negative results are highlighted in red (Fig. 1A). Therefore, for all subsequent comparison studies, we used the adapted amplification protocol with the iQ master mix on the Franklin platform. After adjusting the run time only, the portable qPCR platform operated reliably with the iQ master mix. Assay sensitivity

This comparison was only made for ASFV. The Franklin platform using lyophilized reagents demonstrated the lowest LOD per reaction (4.12, 95% CI: 2.71–14.97), indicating highest sensitivity among tested platforms. CFX96 and MIC showed LODs of 21.28 (14.85–68.67) and 32.76 (21.12–96.11) respectively, while Franklin with adapted amplification protocol achieved 6.85 (4.33–20.11). The LODs were not significantly different. These findings align with Elnagar et al. [18], demonstrating that portable platforms paired with simplified extraction techniques can achieve sensitivity levels comparable to conventional platforms like Bio-Rad CFX96 (Table 3). Comparative performance analysis

ROC analysis confirmed high performance across platforms: MIC (AUC = 0.985), and Franklin (AUC = 1 for lyophilized reagents and adapted amplification protocol). Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated minimal bias (−0.12) between CFX and Franklin platforms [9],Fig. 2). Inter-assay agreement using CFX as comparator revealed variable correlations. Franklin with adapted amplification protocol showed strong correlation (Spearman's ρ = 0.763, bias = −0.037), while Franklin with lyophilized reagents showed lower correlation (ρ = 0.054, bias = −1.594), and MIC showed weak correlation (ρ = 0.205, bias = 2.56). Despite correlation variability, all platforms achieved perfect agreement (Fleiss' Kappa = 1) (Table 4). Zowawi et al. [50] evaluated a Biomeme-based portable RT-PCR platform for COVID-19 diagnostics and reported 98.4% agreement with four RT-PCR platforms: Abbott m2000, Applied Biosystems 7500, Abbott RealTime SARS-CoV-2 assay, and GeneXpert each using a different SARS-CoV kit. Similarly, a study evaluating the PicoGene PCR1100 for rabies detection [16] found that the portable RT-PCR device provided comparable sensitivity and specificity to conventional laboratory-based qPCR assays, with a detection limit of 10 copies/µL. A study on EBOV detection [21] highlighted the feasibility of a rapid qPCR system using the Biomeme Franklin Three9 RT-qPCR platform. The study demonstrated that the system could perform rapid wildlife carcass surveillance with lyophilized reagents, eliminating the need for cold-chain logistics and specialized laboratory infrastructure. A recent study suggested that both Biomeme FranklinTM and Truenat, another chip-based real-time RT-CPR, can detect SARS-CoV-2 and influenza in NP swabs although Truelab® is a more promising and potentially deployable diagnostic platform for SARS-CoV-2 and influenza viruses’ detection in an austere environment [11].

LOD differences reflect variations in reagent composition, thermocycling efficiency, and fluorescence detection sensitivity [18, 31]. Franklin's nine-sample capacity limits large-scale testing, typical of the throughput-portability trade-off in portable thermocyclers [31, 50]. However, this limitation is offset by simplified sample processing since conventional workflows require sample shipment to laboratories for DNA extraction before amplification [18]. In addition, there are several in-the-field extraction methods available [15, 28, 41]. Protocol standardization and real-world performance validation will determine portable qPCR integration feasibility in ASFV surveillance programs. It should be noted, however, that a key limitation of the present study is that all comparisons were performed using nucleic acids previously extracted under optimal laboratory conditions. A true assessment of Franklin’s performance under field conditions would require testing workflows in which either whole blood or tissue samples are directly processed in the field followed by on-site amplification. This approach would better reflect the real-world scenarios envisioned for portable qPCR deployment. Protocol standardization and further validation under field conditions will ultimately determine the feasibility of integrating portable qPCR into ASFV surveillance programs.

Conclusion

CFX96 demonstrated robust performance with standardized protocols, suitable for high-throughput laboratory testing. In contrast, the MIC is best considered a medium-throughput device, due to the need for using relatively tedious specialized plasticware, while still providing reliable performance with standardized protocols. Franklin provides similar performance as the lab-based machines, which is an important step towards the use of qPCR platforms directly in the field, but require adaptation of published protocols.’
