Intraoperative opioid-sparing effect of a quadratus lumborum block using low-concentration bupivacaine and dexmedetomidine in cats undergoing ovariohysterectomy
Authors: Fumihiko Takusagawa, Yoshimi Takusagawa, Taro Kimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the intraoperative opioid-sparing effect of quadratus lumborum block (QLB) using low-concentration bupivacaine combined with dexmedetomidine in cats undergoing ovariohysterectomy.MethodsA total of 28 cats scheduled for ovariohysterectomy and classified as American Society of Anaesthesiologists status I were enrolled and randomly allocated to either a bupivacaine (0.125%) and saline (BS) or bupivacaine (0.125%) and dexmedetomidine (1 µg/kg) (BD) group. The cats underwent ultrasound-guided QLB in sternal recumbency using the allocated solution. If heart rate or mean arterial pressure increased by 20% or more from baseline during surgery, fentanyl (2 µg/kg IV) was administered as rescue analgesia. Postoperative pain was assessed 1, 2, 3, 6 and 12 h after extubation using the Short-Form UNESP-Botucatu Multidimensional Composite Pain Scale. Buprenorphine (0.02 mg/kg IM) was administered if the pain score was 4/12 or higher.ResultsThe cumulative intraoperative dose of fentanyl was significantly lower in the BD group (1 µg/kg [range 0–6]) than in the BS group (2 µg/kg [range 0–8]; P = 0.030). No significant difference was observed between the groups in terms of the proportion of cats requiring intraoperative or postoperative rescue analgesia, postoperative pain scores at any time point, intraoperative physiological parameters or time-related variables.Conclusions and relevanceQLB performed in sternal recumbency using low-concentration bupivacaine with dexmedetomidine was associated with a modest reduction in intraoperative opioid requirements during ovariohysterectomy in cats. However, as this study design could not differentiate between the local and systemic effects of dexmedetomidine, further research is needed to clarify their relative contributions and determine the optimal adjunctive dose.

Introduction

Regional anaesthesia is a growing component of multimodal analgesia in human and animal anaesthesiology.1,2 Interfascial plane blocks (IFPBs) are a regional technique that have been actively developed and refined. 3 IFPBs offer wider sensory coverage across multiple nerve segments and may reduce the risk of direct nerve injury and systemic side effects.4 –7 However, because local anaesthetic agents are injected between fascial layers in IFPBs, rather than around the target nerves, a larger volume of anaesthetic solution is generally required to achieve effective analgesia. 4

Cats are more sensitive to these drugs than dogs, 2 necessitating lower maximum doses when performing regional anaesthesia, including IFPBs.

The quadratus lumborum block (QLB) is a regional technique that anaesthetises both the lumbar spinal nerves and the sympathetic trunk. This provides both somatic and visceral analgesia for procedures involving the middle to lower abdomen.8,9 The intraoperative and postoperative analgesic efficacy of the QLB has been demonstrated in cats and dogs undergoing celiotomy.10 –14 As the QLB is a type of IFPB, relatively large volumes of local anaesthetic are typically required.4,8,9 Therefore, approaches that allow for reducing the total dose or concentration of local anaesthetic may decrease the risk of systemic toxicity in cats.

Lower concentrations of local anaesthetics can shorten and weaken nerve blockade.15 –17 However, several adjuvants have been found to effectively increase the effects of local anaesthetics in both humans and animals. 1 Among these, dexmedetomidine enhances and prolongs the effects of local anaesthetics, potentially by blocking hyperpolarisation-activated cation currents in C fibres.18,19

Our primary aim was to evaluate the effect of adding dexmedetomidine to low-concentration bupivacaine for QLB on intraoperative antinociception in cats undergoing ovariohysterectomy, assessed as an opioid-sparing effect. Postoperative analgesic efficacy was also evaluated using pain scores and rescue analgesic requirements. We hypothesised that adding dexmedetomidine would reduce intraoperative fentanyl requirements compared with bupivacaine alone.

Materials and methods

Study design and animals

This was a prospective, randomised, controlled and blinded clinical trial. The study protocol was approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kimura Animal Hospital (approval number KAH2025-02). Informed consent was obtained from the owners or legal custodians of all enrolled cats. Based on pilot data, 13 cats per group were required to detect a significant difference in the intraoperative fentanyl dose (alpha = 0.05 and 80% power). A total of 14 cats per group was recruited to allow for an approximately 10% dropout rate.

All enrolled cats were classified as American Society of Anaesthesiologists physical status I (ASA I) based on owner-provided medical history, physical examination, haematology, serum biochemistry and thoracic radiography before enrolment. All cats underwent ovariohysterectomy at Seibozaka Animal Hospital between August 2025 and March 2026. Exclusion criteria included age under 3 months or over 5 years, body condition score (BCS) below 3/9 or above 7/9, 20 known spinal anomalies, infection at the injection site, analgesic use (including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and gabapentin) within 7 days preoperatively or a Short-Form UNESP-Botucatu Multidimensional Composite Pain Scale (MCPS-SF) score of 4/12 or higher before premedication. 21

The cats were divided into either the bupivacaine and saline (BS) group, in which the cats received 0.125% bupivacaine combined with normal saline for QLB, or the bupivacaine and dexmedetomidine (BD) group, in which the cats received 0.125% bupivacaine combined with dexmedetomidine for QLB. Cats were sequentially enrolled and randomly assigned to treatment groups via a predefined allocation sequence generated using Random.org (Randomness and Integrity Services) by a hospital staff member not involved in perioperative management or outcome assessment. Treatment solutions were prepared and labelled according to group allocation. The staff member selected the appropriate solution, removed its label and provided it to the anaesthetist, while the unused solution was discarded.

Anaesthesia and monitoring

On the day of surgery, the animals were given water, but food was withheld for at least 8 h before anaesthesia. All cats received 1.0 mg/kg subcutaneous (SC) maropitant (Cerenia; Zoetis) upon admission. Premedication with 1.0 mg/kg intramuscular (IM) alfaxalone (Alfaxan Multidose; Jurox) and 4 µg/kg dexmedetomidine (Dexdomitor 0.1; Orion Pharma) was administered. An intravenous (IV) 24 G catheter (Surflo Flash IV, Terumo) was placed in the cephalic vein 10 mins after premedication. Additional IV alfaxalone was administered to effect, and tracheal intubation was performed using an appropriately sized cuffed endotracheal tube (Fujisystems) once loss of the swallowing reflex and jaw tone were confirmed. The endotracheal tube was then connected to a rebreathing circle system via an anaesthetic machine (Acoma Medical Industry), and anaesthesia was maintained with isoflurane (Riken Vets Pharma, distributed by Nippon Zenyaku Kogyo) in 100% oxygen. Vaporiser settings were adjusted to maintain end-tidal isoflurane concentrations (ETIso) at 1.3–1.5%. After intubation, 25 mg/kg IV cefazolin (Cefazolin for Animal Use; Fujii Pharmaceutical) and 0.1 mg/kg SC meloxicam (Inflacam Injection; Kyoritsu Seiyaku) were administered, and lactated Ringer’s solution (Lactec; Otsuka Pharmaceutical Factory) was infused at 5 ml/kg/h. Physiological monitoring included continuous measurement of heart rate (HR), pulse oximetric oxygen saturation (SpO2), respiratory rate (RR), end-tidal carbon dioxide concentration (PETCO2), ETIso and oesophageal temperature (ET). Systolic (SAP), diastolic (DAP) and mean arterial pressure (MAP) were measured using an oscillometric device and recorded every 2 mins. The cuff was placed on a hindlimb, and its width was selected to approximately 40% of the limb circumference. All parameters were measured using a multiparameter monitor (ePM 12M Vet; Shenzhen Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics).

QLB procedure

All QLB procedures were performed by the same veterinary anaesthetist (FT), who was experienced in ultrasound-guided regional anaesthesia, utilising an echogenic nerve block needle (Sonolect needle USG type CCR; Hakko) and a TOP syringe (TOP, Japan). The anaesthetist was blinded to group allocation. The final injectate volume was 0.8 ml/kg (plus 0.4 ml for priming of the extension tube), delivering a dose of 1 mg/kg of 0.125% bupivacaine combined with either dexmedetomidine at 1 μg/kg (BD group) or an equivalent volume of saline at 0.01 ml/kg (BS group). Bupivacaine and the allocated adjuvant were mixed in a single syringe immediately before use.

The previously described sternal QLB technique was used in all cases. 22 The cat was positioned in sternal recumbency, and the lumbar area was clipped and aseptically prepared bilaterally. We placed the ultrasound probe between the second and third lumbar transverse processes aligned with the orientation of the transverse processes, with the reference mark directed craniolaterally. We then identified the lumbar vertebra, quadratus lumborum and psoas minor muscles on the ultrasound image, with the reference mark positioned on the right side of the screen. We inserted the needle in-plane craniolaterally to caudomedially from the edge of the probe under ultrasound guidance. We then positioned the needle tip in the fascial plane between the quadratus lumborum and psoas minor muscles. After confirming negative aspiration of blood, we injected 0.4 ml/kg of the prepared solution into the fascial plane. We confirmed the correct deposition of the solution by ultrasound visualisation of hydrodissection of the target fascial plane. We performed all procedures sequentially, starting on the right side, followed by the left. For each animal, we recorded the total duration of the QLB procedure.

Ovariohysterectomy and anaesthetic management

After completion of the QLB, the cats were positioned in dorsal recumbency, and surgical preparation was performed. During surgical preparation, ETIso was adjusted as needed to maintain an appropriate anaesthetic depth, as indicated by the absence of body movement, palpebral reflex and strong jaw tone, as well as ventromedial rotation of the globe. Based on a previous clinical study that included QLB in cats, 11 ovariohysterectomy was initiated 10 mins after completion of the block. Baseline HR and MAP values were recorded immediately before the start of ovariohysterectomy, after ETIso adjustment. Thereafter, HR, SpO2, RR, PETCO2, ETIso, ET, SAP, DAP and MAP values were recorded every 2 mins. If HR or MAP did not return to within 20% of baseline after the initial fentanyl administration, additional doses of fentanyl (2.0 µg/kg IV) were administered until both parameters returned to within 20% of baseline values. The timing of each rescue administration was recorded according to surgical stages: incision to celiotomy (INC), first ovariectomy (OV1), second ovariectomy (OV2), hysterectomy (HYS) and closure (CLO) (Table 1). The cumulative intraoperative fentanyl dose was calculated for each cat.

If MAP dropped below 70 mmHg, we reduced ETIso by 0.1–0.2%. If MAP remained below 70 mmHg despite ETIso reduction to 0.6%, we administered an IV bolus of lactated Ringer’s solution (3 ml/kg) over 5 mins. All anaesthetic management, physiological recordings and QLB procedures were performed by the same veterinary anaesthetist (FT) who was blinded to group allocation.

All surgeries were performed using a standard ovariohysterectomy approach by the same veterinary surgeon (YT) who was blinded to group allocation. 23 After completion of surgery, isoflurane was discontinued, and atipamezole (10 µg/kg IM, Antisedan; Orion) was administered. Extubation was performed once the swallowing reflex had returned. Once SpO2 remained above 97% for at least 2 mins, the cat was transferred to a kennel in the cat ward. Surgical duration, defined as the interval from the first skin incision to completion of skin closure, and anaesthetic duration, defined as the interval from endotracheal intubation to extubation, were recorded.

Postoperative pain management

Pain assessment was performed using the MCPS-SF immediately before premedication and then 1, 2, 3, 6 and 12 h after extubation. If the score was 4 or higher, we administered buprenorphine (0.02 mg/kg IM) as postoperative rescue analgesia. The cats who received rescue analgesia were reassessed for pain 30 and 60 mins after administration to ensure that the score had decreased to below 4. If rescue analgesia was required, the scores for all subsequent time points were imputed using the last observation carried forward method. All MCPS-SF assessments were performed by the same veterinary anaesthetist (FT) who remained blinded to group allocation throughout the study.

Outcomes and statistical analysis

We compared age (months), body weight (kg), BCS, the alfaxalone dose required for anaesthesia induction, mean intraoperative physiological parameters (HR, SpO2, RR, PETCO2, ETIso, ET and MAP) during anaesthetic maintenance, QLB procedure time, surgical duration and total anaesthetic duration between the two groups.

The primary outcome was the between-group difference in cumulative intraoperative fentanyl dose. We also compared the proportion of cats who required intraoperative rescue analgesia, both throughout surgery and during each surgical stage, as well as postoperative MCPS-SF scores between groups. We recorded any intraoperative haemodynamic or respiratory complications and any complications related to the use of QLBs.

We analysed categorical variables using Fisher’s exact test. We assessed the normality of continuous variables with the Shapiro–Wilk test. We analysed normally distributed continuous data using the Student’s t-test and non-normally distributed data using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Postoperative MCPS-SF scores were analysed using a generalised estimating equation (GEE) model with a Gaussian distribution and an exchangeable correlation structure to account for repeated measurements within cats. The model included treatment group, time point and their interaction as explanatory variables, with cats specified as the clustering variable. Post-hoc between-group comparisons at each time point were performed with the Holm correction for multiple comparisons. For cumulative intraoperative fentanyl dose, the between-group difference was estimated as the median difference with a 95% confidence interval (CI). For categorical outcomes, effect sizes were expressed as risk differences with 95% CIs. Categorical data are presented as counts (%), normally distributed data as mean ± SD and non-normally distributed data as median (range). All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University). Statistical significance was set at P <0.05.

One cat in the BD group was discharged at the owner’s request after the 3-h postoperative pain assessment and received buprenorphine (0.02 mg/kg IM). This cat was not considered to have required postoperative rescue analgesia because buprenorphine administration was not triggered by an MCPS-SF score of 4/12 or higher. Consequently, MCPS-SF scores at 6 and 12 h after extubation were unavailable for this cat. Postoperative MCPS-SF scores were analysed using available observations. Analyses were performed according to the allocated treatment group. Discussion

Our results suggest that the addition of dexmedetomidine to low-concentration bupivacaine for QLB was associated with an intraoperative opioid-sparing effect in cats undergoing ovariohysterectomy. Lower concentrations of local anaesthetics are associated with several disadvantages, including shorter duration of action and lower nerve-blocking intensity.15 –17 Previous studies have shown that dexmedetomidine can potentiate the effects of local anaesthetics in regional anesthesia,19,23 –26 and a similar effect may have contributed to the reduced intraoperative opioid requirement observed in the BD group. This augmentation of nerve-blocking intensity may partly compensate for the disadvantages of low-concentration bupivacaine. Our findings also support the use of relatively high volumes of dilute bupivacaine during QLB in cats to minimise the risk of local anaesthetic systemic toxicity.

Although adding dexmedetomidine to bupivacaine for QLB significantly reduced cumulative fentanyl requirements, the absolute reduction was modest. This may partly explain the absence of a significant between-group difference in the proportion of cats requiring intraoperative rescue fentanyl. In addition, the proportion of cats requiring intraoperative rescue analgesia in both groups was higher than previously reported in studies of QLBs in cats.11 –13 Several methodological differences may explain this discrepancy. Previous studies demonstrating the opioid-sparing effect of QLB have included both methadone and dexmedetomidine in the premedication protocol.11 –13 Methadone provides potent perioperative analgesia,27,28 likely reducing the need for rescue opioids in those studies. In contrast, we used alfaxalone and dexmedetomidine for premedication. Injectable methadone is not available in Japan, and we also intentionally avoided opioid premedication to allow evaluation of the opioid-sparing effect of QLB, necessitating an opioid-free anaesthetic protocol. The absence of baseline opioid administration likely contributed to the higher intraoperative fentanyl requirements in the present study. Furthermore, we used 0.125% bupivacaine, which is lower than standard concentrations in veterinary regional anaesthesia. This concentration was selected to maintain the recommended total dose of 1 mg/kg in cats, 29 while allowing an injectate volume of 0.4 ml/kg/side for QLB. This low bupivacaine concentration may have resulted in weaker nerve blockade in some cats, partly explaining the higher proportion requiring intraoperative rescue analgesia compared with previous studies using similar injectate volumes.11 –13 The relatively small sample size may also have limited the precision of the estimated effect size and reduced the ability to detect a difference in the proportion of cats requiring rescue fentanyl.

The proportion of cats requiring rescue fentanyl was significantly lower in the BD group during the second ovariectomy, but not during the first ovariectomy. This may relate to the progressive onset of the interfascial plane block and differences in anatomy, physiology or surgical manipulation between the two ovaries. The small sample size may also have contributed. In addition, several cats in our cohort required rescue fentanyl during uterine manipulation. The uterus is partly innervated by visceral afferent fibres travelling with the pelvic nerve, which merge with the sacral spinal nerves. 30 A previous cadaveric study of QLB targeting L2 in cats showed that injectate spread typically extended from caudal thoracic segments to, at most, L3. 9 Because QLB in the present study also targeted L2, sensory input from the uterus travelling via the pelvic nerve was unlikely to be affected. This may explain why analgesia was insufficient during uterine traction in some of the cats. Most previous studies demonstrating strong opioid-sparing effects of QLB in cats have evaluated its use in ovariectomy rather than ovariohysterectomy.11 –14 This difference in surgical procedure may partly explain the differences in intraoperative opioid requirements between the present study and previous reports.

Our findings suggest a degree of interindividual variability in the opioid-sparing effect of QLB, particularly in the BS group. Although the BD group showed lower cumulative fentanyl requirements, this study cannot determine whether dexmedetomidine reduced interindividual variability in response to QLB, as the sample size was not designed to evaluate differences in variability. Similar variability has been reported with other interfascial plane blocks, with anatomical, demographic and technical factors appearing to influence the distribution of injectate and the effectiveness of the block. 4 Our use of a relatively low concentration of bupivacaine may have further contributed to this interindividual variation in block intensity. Differences in individual sensitivity to local anaesthetics may affect the degree of nerve blockade achieved with dilute local anaesthetic solutions. 31 Although the target fascial plane and hydrodissection were confirmed via ultrasound in all cats, visualisation of injectate spread does not ensure successful sensory blockade in fascial plane blocks. As there is no consensus on the definition or assessment of a successful fascial plane block, 32 incomplete sensory blockade in some cats cannot be excluded, representing a study limitation. Despite this variability, our overall results suggest that QLBs with low-concentration bupivacaine may contribute to reducing intraoperative opioid requirements in cats undergoing ovariohysterectomies.

No significant differences in postoperative MCPS-SF scores or postoperative rescue analgesic requirements were observed between groups, which may be explained by the modest absolute difference in intraoperative fentanyl requirements between groups. In addition, meloxicam was administered to all cats as part of the standard protocol for postoperative pain and inflammatory control, which may have reduced postoperative pain scores in both groups and limited the ability to detect between-group differences in postoperative pain scores or rescue analgesic requirements. However, postoperative MCPS-SF scores remained low in both groups, and rescue analgesia was rarely required. This suggests that QLBs, as part of multimodal analgesia with meloxicam, contribute to clinically relevant postoperative analgesia despite using a low concentration of bupivacaine. Exposure of nerve fibres to low concentrations of local anaesthetics produces decremental nerve conduction even without complete nerve blockade.33,34 This phenomenon may attenuate nociceptive signal transmission and could explain the antinociceptive effects of QLB and dilute bupivacaine observed in the present study. In addition, postoperative MCPS-SF pain scores remained low beyond the expected duration of action of the bupivacaine, which would have been further shortened by the low concentrations used. 12 Regional anaesthesia has been reported to reduce peripheral and central sensation, thereby attenuating subsequent hyperalgesia. 35 This mechanism may have contributed to the sustained postoperative analgesic profile observed here.

This study had several limitations. First, QLB was performed in sternal recumbency, an approach previously developed and reported by the present authors. 22 However, the technique has not yet been evaluated in injectate spread studies, such as cadaveric investigations. Nevertheless, its anaesthetic-sparing effects and effective avoidance of possible inadvertent organ injury along the needle trajectory have been reported.22,36 Therefore, we considered the use of this technique reasonable. Further studies are warranted to compare different QLB techniques using the same anaesthetic protocol. Second, intraoperative rescue fentanyl administration was based on increases in HR or MAP, which are indirect and non-specific indicators of intraoperative nociception. These variables may be influenced by multiple factors during anaesthesia and surgery, including anaesthetic depth, cardiovascular status and ventilation. In addition, systemic absorption of dexmedetomidine injected into the interfascial plane could not be excluded and may have attenuated HR responses, thereby influencing the decision to administer fentanyl. Because all cats also received IM dexmedetomidine as premedication, the additional contribution of interfascially administered dexmedetomidine could not be clearly distinguished from systemic effects. Its dose was selected empirically, as, to our knowledge, no previous studies have evaluated dexmedetomidine as an adjuvant to local anaesthetics for IFPBs in cats. Further studies are warranted to determine the optimal dose and evaluate the extent of systemic absorption following QLB. Third, a control group receiving saline instead of bupivacaine or no QLB was not included in this study. Therefore, the efficacy of QLB with bupivacaine, with or without dexmedetomidine, could not be evaluated against placebo or no-block conditions. Such a control group was not included because administering a placebo solution instead of local anaesthetic in clinical patients raised ethical concerns, and a no-block control group would have compromised blinding. Fourth, outcomes other than the primary outcome – namely, the difference in cumulative intraoperative fentanyl dose between groups – may have been subject to a type II error. This is because the sample size calculation was based solely on the expected difference in the primary outcome. Further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to investigate the overall proportion of cats requiring intraoperative rescue fentanyl, rescue analgesic requirements at each surgical stage and the postoperative analgesic efficacy of QLB with low-concentration bupivacaine and dexmedetomidine.

Conclusions

QLB performed in sternal recumbency using low-concentration bupivacaine with dexmedetomidine modestly reduced intraoperative opioid requirements compared with bupivacaine alone in cats undergoing ovariohysterectomy. In addition, QLB using low-concentration bupivacaine, with or without dexmedetomidine, provided low postoperative pain scores and minimal rescue analgesia when combined with meloxicam. Further studies are warranted to investigate the optimal dose of adjunctive dexmedetomidine for QLB in cats and to clarify the contribution of systemic absorption to the observed effects.
