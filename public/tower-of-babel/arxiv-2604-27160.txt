Embeddings of Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces with General Weights
Michael Gnewuch, Peter Kritzer, Klaus Ritter

Abstract
We study embeddings between reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces H ⁡ ( K ) H(K) of functions of d ∈ ℕ ∪ { ∞ } d\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\} variables. The kernels K K are superpositions of weighted finite tensor products of a fixed univariate kernel. The basic idea for the embeddings is to compensate a change of the univariate kernel by a suitable transformation of the weights. For the proofs we employ ( d ∈ ℕ d\in{\mathbb{N}} ) and develop ( d = ∞ d=\infty ) a discrete calculus on the cone of all weights, where completely monotone weights play a particular role. We sketch how to apply the embedding results to computational problems, as, e.g., numerical integration or function recovery.



1
Introduction

Our goal is to study computational problems on Hilbert
spaces
H
of functions that depend on
d
variables;
the parameter
d
is also referred to as the
dimension
of the
problem. A typical situation is the approximation of a continuous
linear
solution operator
defined on
H
and mapping to
a Banach space
G
,

S\colon H\to G,

by suitable algorithms.
Here we interpret
S
as an operator that, in a given computational
problem, maps an input function
f\in H
to the corresponding
(exact)
solution
g\in G
. The goal is to approximate this solution, and the
approximation error is commonly measured in the norm of
G
.
Typical examples of such a setting are numerical integration,
function recovery, or well-posed linear operator equations.

This paper is concerned with the case where
d
is a
potentially huge positive integer,
or where
d
is infinite. In the latter case, the elements of the
space
H
depend on infinitely (but countably) many variables.
Such a situation naturally occurs
in models that are based on infinite sequences
of independent random variables, as for example in
the context of
PDEs with random coefficients, see, among many others,

Cohen and DeVore 2015
,

Kuo and Nuyens 2016
, and
Adcock et al. 2022
.

A standing assumption in our paper is that the function spaces
H

are
reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
, and that they are

weighted
in the sense of
Sloan and Woźniakowski 1998
.
Let us explain this in more detail. Let
\mathcal{U}_{d}
be the power set of

\{1,\ldots,d\}
for finite
d
, and the set of all finite subsets
of the positive integers if
d=\infty
. We assign non-negative real
numbers
\gamma_{u}
to each set
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}

of variable indices, indicating the
joint influence of the variables
x_{j}
with
j\in u

on the functions
f\in H
.
A weight with a larger value corresponds to
a stronger influence,
a weight with a smaller value corresponds to
a weaker influence.
This setting is also motivated by applications
beyond random PDEs, as for example in financial
engineering, where it may frequently be the case that the variables

x_{j}
have diminishing influence with growing
j
.

The major part of this paper is devoted to the case where
d=\infty
,
simply because this case is more elaborate and technically more
demanding than the case of finite
d
. We may view the case

d=\infty
as the limiting case of tractability analysis, which,
roughly speaking, deals with finite
d
tending to
\infty
.
For tractability analysis we refer to the trilogy

Novak and Woźniakowski 2008
;
Novak and Woźniakowski 2010
;
Novak and Woźniakowski 2012
.

We assume that the kernels
K
of the spaces

H=H(K)
considered here
are superpositions of weighted tensor product kernels.
More precisely,
K
takes the form

K({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}):=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\gamma_{u}k_{u}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})

(1)

with weights

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}:=(\gamma_{u})_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}

and finite tensor products

k_{u}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}):=\prod_{j\in u}k(x_{j},y_{j})

of a given kernel
k
over an arbitrary domain
D
.
A suitably chosen domain
{\mathfrak{Z}}\subseteq D^{\mathbb{N}}

and
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathfrak{Z}}

may be needed to ensure the convergence in
(
1
) in the case
d=\infty
.

Considering the elements of the Hilbert space
H(k)
with
reproducing kernel
k
as univariate functions,
the elements of the Hilbert spaces
H(k_{u})
are

|u|
-variate functions, since they only depend
on the variables with indices in
u
.
We add that finite tensor products are often employed
in the analysis of high-, yet finite-dimensional computational
problems.

In many cases in the literature, authors impose a certain
structure on the weights
\gamma_{u}
for
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
. The
most commonly considered case is that of product weights, which are
defined in terms of a sequence
(\gamma_{j})_{j\geq 1}
of non-negative
real numbers, and for which

\gamma_{u}:=\prod_{j\in u}\gamma_{j}

for every
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
(with the convention that

\gamma_{\emptyset}=1
).
However, in recent years it has turned out that for several
applications of interest, including the analysis of the
aforementioned PDEs with random coefficients, one needs to allow for
more general weights that lack the product structure, in particular
POD (product and order dependent), or the even more involved SPOD
(smoothness-driven, product and order dependent) weights,
as used, e.g., in
Hakula et al. 2024
; see
Section
3

for the definition of POD and finite-order weights.

It is known that structural
properties of the function space
H(K)
,
for finite or infinite
d
, may substantially facilitate the error
analysis for particular types of algorithms.
A key feature for the error analysis is the orthogonal
decomposition of
H(K)
that is induced by the representation
(
1
) of the kernel
K
, if

1\not\in H(k)
.
To give an example, in the analysis of
randomized algorithms, one frequently would like to know about the
variance of the algorithm, for which it is
most convenient if (
1
) induces
an ANOVA (an acronym for “Analysis of Variance”)
decomposition of the functions in
H(K)
.
To provide another example, for deterministic algorithms, often an
anchored decomposition is favorable. We refer to
Baldeaux and Gnewuch 2014
and

Dick and Gnewuch 2014
, as well as the references therein for
further details. Moreover, note that anchored decompositions
also play an important role in the construction of
multivariate decomposition methods (originally named changing
dimension algorithms) as introduced in
Kuo et al. 2010
, and studied
further in several follow-up papers.

Hence, we may be in a situation where we would like to analyze a
certain (type of) algorithm in a space
H(K)

that does
not
allow for an effective error analysis.
In such a case, we are interested in a way to transfer to a
different function space where we can derive a meaningful error
bound that also yields a useful
bound when going back to the original space. This
can be done by finding suitable embeddings between weighted
function spaces (in the sense of Sloan and
Woźniakowski).

This approach has been developed for the case of
tensor products of weighted Hilbert spaces
in several papers, see, e.g.,

Hefter and Ritter 2015
and
Gnewuch et al. 2017a
. It is known that, in terms of the
weights, the case of a tensor product space
H(K)

exactly corresponds to the
assumption of the weights being of product form,
where
K
given by (
1
) satisfies

K({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+\gamma_{j}k(x_{j},y_{j}))

(2)

in both cases
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
d=\infty
.

In the present paper,
motivated by applications where one
does not have product weights,
but, e.g., POD or other types of weights, we try to
establish embedding results that cover general weights with as
few additional assumptions
as possible, thereby moving away from the tensor product
assumption for the function spaces
H(K)
under consideration.

Embeddings of function spaces relying on more general classes
of weights, but with specific multivariate building blocks,
like Sobolev spaces of mixed smoothness,
have already been studied in the literature. See,
e.g.,

Hefter et al. 2016
,
Hinrichs and Schneider 2016
,
Gnewuch et al. 2017b
,

Gilbert et al. 2022
.
To our best knowledge, the present paper is the first to address
embeddings for
general weights and general tensor product kernels
k_{u}
.

Let us now give a short outlook on the paper.
In Section
2
we study
the set
\mathcal{M}_{d}
of completely monotone weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
,
where the latter are considered as set functions,
as well as the
sum operator

T_{d,C}^{\uparrow}
,
given by
T_{d,C}^{\uparrow}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}:={\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}
with

\gamma^{\uparrow}_{u}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\\
u\subseteq v\end{subarray}}C^{2\left|v\right|}\gamma_{v}

for every
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
. Here
C
denotes a fixed positive
constant, and

\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}C^{2\left|v\right|}\gamma_{v}<\infty

(3)

is required to hold in the case
d=\infty
. As the main result
for
d=\infty
, we determine the range and the inverse
T^{\downarrow}_{d,C}

of
T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}
, see Theorem
2
;
here the range is a proper subset of
\mathcal{M}_{d}
, defined by an
additional
\sigma
-continuity property.
For
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
we present a known result concerning the
range and the inverse in Theorem
1
, see

Aigner 1979
and
Matúš 1994
;
here the range is equal to
\mathcal{M}_{d}
.

In Section
3
we investigate
particular classes of weights, namely product, POD, and
finite-order weights. First of all, we study the complete
monotonicity and the summability (
3
) for these kinds of
weights. Furthermore, we study invariance properties of the
aforementioned classes of weights with respect to the mappings

T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}
and
T^{\downarrow}_{d,C}
. For the
applications we have in mind, it is crucial to find out whether
the decay of the weights is invariant under
T_{d,C}^{\uparrow}
and

T^{\downarrow}_{d,C}
, respectively.
This is because the decay of the weights is intimately related to
the decay of the minimal errors for the
computational problems we are aiming at,
and may,
at least implicitly, occur in the corresponding error bounds,
which can already be seen, e.g., in
Hickernell et al. 2010
,

Kuo et al. 2010
, and
Plaskota and Wasilkowski 2011
. Results regarding invariance
of the decay are given in
Proposition
5
, Corollary
1
, and
Proposition
6
for POD, product, and finite-order
weights, respectively.

After having studied the fundamental properties of weights and their
transformations via
T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}
and
T^{\downarrow}_{d,C}

in Sections
2
and
3
,
we formally introduce the function spaces under consideration in
this paper and the basic embedding results
in Section
4
.

In addition to
k
and
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, which define
K
via
(
1
),
we first of all consider another reproducing kernel

k^{\uparrow}
such that
k
and
k^{\uparrow}
have a common
domain and

H(k)\subseteq H(1+k^{\uparrow}).

We add that the stronger assumption

H(k)\subseteq H(k^{\uparrow})
is typically not satisfied in the
applications to the computational problems we have
in mind.

Secondly, we consider the weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}:=T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, where
C
may be
chosen as an arbitrary upper bound on the norm of the embedding of

H(k)
into
H(1+k^{\uparrow})
.
In this way we obtain a pair of reproducing kernels of the
same form, namely
K
over the domain
{\mathfrak{Z}}
and
analogously
K^{\uparrow}
with
k

and
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
replaced by
k^{\uparrow}
and
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}
, respectively, over the
domain
{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}
. We show that the domains
satisfy
{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}\subseteq{\mathfrak{Z}}
and that
the restriction

f\mapsto f|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}}

defines a bounded linear operator from

H(K)
to
H(K^{\uparrow})
of
norm at most one.
See Theorem
4
for the case
d=\infty
and
Theorem
3
for the case
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
;
in the latter case we have
{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}={\mathfrak{Z}}
, implying that
the restriction is actually the identical embedding.

Section
5
shows how the findings on the
embeddings of function spaces with general
weights can be used when one
would like to derive error bounds for a given computational problem
on a Hilbert space
H(K)
with reproducing kernel
K
of the
form (
1
) by switching back and forth to an
RKHS with a similar, but more favorable structure.
This discussion is held in a setting as general as possible, i.e.,
without specifying the computational problem;
the essential assumption is that the solution operator
S
and
all algorithms under consideration are bounded linear operators.
The sum operator
T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}
and a straightforward
application of the results from Section
4
may be
used to obtain upper error bounds. Likewise, the inverse

T^{\downarrow}_{d,C}
of the sum operator may be used to obtain
lower error bounds. More precisely, if
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
then

T^{\downarrow}_{d,C}
for finite
d
or the
canonical extension of
T^{\downarrow}_{d,C}
to
\mathcal{M}_{d}
for
d=\infty

may be used directly.
Otherwise, the weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
are substituted by completely monotone
weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\ast}\leq{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
in the first place.
See Corollary
2
for the upper bounds and
Corollary
3
for the lower bounds.

An application to concrete computational problems of the theory
developed, such as, e.g.,
L^{p}
-approximation in the case

d=\infty
, is deferred to a follow-up paper.

The paper is rounded off by an appendix containing
background on completely monotone weights and related concepts.

2
General Weights

2.1
Notation

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
.
For
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
we denote the set
\{1,\ldots,d\}
by
[d]
, and for

d=\infty
we denote by
[d]
the whole set
{\mathbb{N}}
. We put

\mathcal{U}_{\infty}:=\{u\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\text{$u$ finite}\}

and

\mathcal{U}_{d}:=\{u\subset{\mathbb{N}}:u\subseteq[d]\}

for
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The size of a finite set
u
is denoted by
|u|
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(1)},\ldots\colon\mathcal{U}_{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}

denote families of real numbers, indexed by
\mathcal{U}_{d}
. We write
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\geq{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\prime}
if
\gamma_{u}\geq\gamma^{\prime}_{u}
for every
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
, and
\lim_{n\to\infty}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(n)}={\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\left({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(n)}\right)_{u}=\gamma_{u}
for
every
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
. Moreover, the family
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
that is constant
and equal to zero is denoted by
{\boldsymbol{0}}
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a countably infinite index set. For a
family
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
of non-negative real
numbers the decay is defined by

\decay\left(\boldsymbol{a}\right):=\sup\left\{\tau>0:\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}a_{v}^{1/\tau}<\infty\right\},

with the convention
\sup\emptyset=0
. For instance,
we have

\decay\left(\boldsymbol{a}\right)=\lambda
for
\mathcal{V}:={\mathbb{N}}
and

a_{v}:=v^{-\lambda}
with
\lambda\geq 0
.

We use the term “weights” and the notation

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\left(\gamma_{u}\right)_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}

for any family of non-negative real numbers,
indexed by
\mathcal{U}_{d}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{W}_{d}
denotes
the set of all weights, i.e.,

\mathcal{W}_{d}:=\{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\colon\mathcal{U}_{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}:{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\geq{\boldsymbol{0}}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{U}_{d}}.

2.2
Complete Monotonicity

We define the difference operators
\Delta_{v}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{U}_{d}}
by

\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}:=\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|w|}\gamma_{u\cup w}

for
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{U}_{d}}

and
u,v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
.
Note that
\Delta_{\emptyset}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}={\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.

Lemma 1
.

For
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{U}_{d}}
and
u,v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
we have

u\cap v\neq\emptyset\hskip 10.00002pt\Rightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=0.

Proof.

In any case

\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=\sum_{w_{1}\subseteq v\cap u}\ \sum_{w_{2}\subseteq v\setminus u}(-1)^{|w_{1}|+|w_{2}|}\gamma_{u\cup w_{2}}.

Moreover,

\sum_{w_{1}\subseteq v\cap u}(-1)^{|w_{1}|}=0

if
v\cap u\neq\emptyset
.
∎

Let us consider differences
\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
of orders one and
two, i.e., with
|v|=1
and
|v|=2
.
If

v=\{s\}
for some
s\in[d]
, then

\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=\gamma_{u}-\gamma_{u\cup\{s\}}

for every
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
.
If
v=\{s_{1},s_{2}\}
for some distinct

s_{1},s_{2}\in[d]
, then

\displaystyle\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{u}-\gamma_{u\cup\{s_{1}\}}-\gamma_{u\cup\{s_{2}\}}+\gamma_{u\cup\{s_{1},s_{2}\}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{u}-\gamma_{u\cup\{s_{1}\}}-\left(\gamma_{u\cup\{s_{2}\}}-\gamma_{u\cup\{s_{1},s_{2}\}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\Delta_{\{s_{1}\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}-\left(\Delta_{\{s_{1}\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u\cup\{s_{2}\}}

for every
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
.
The following lemma, which
gives a recursion formula for the
operators
\Delta_{v}
,
illustrates that we can interpret all the expressions

\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}
with
v\neq\emptyset

as iterated differences.

Lemma 2
.

For
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{U}_{d}}
,
u,v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
, and
s\in[d]
we have

\left(\Delta_{v\cup\{s\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}-\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u\cup\{s\}}.

Proof.

If
s\in v
then

\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u\cup\{s\}}=0
by Lemma
1
,
which yields the claim. If
s\not\in v
then

\left(\Delta_{v\cup\{s\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|w|}\left(\gamma_{u\cup w}-\gamma_{u\cup w\cup\{s\}}\right)=\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}-\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u\cup\{s\}}

as claimed.
∎

Next, we show an auxiliary result regarding the composition
of difference operators.

Lemma 3
.

We have
(\Delta_{v}\circ\Delta_{w}){\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\Delta_{v\cup w}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

for all
v,w\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
and all
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{U}_{d}}
.

Proof.

Lemma
2
yields

\Delta_{v\cup\{s\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\Delta_{\{s\}}(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})

for every
s\in[d]
and every
v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
.
The statement of the present lemma trivially
holds true for
v=\emptyset
, and inductively we obtain

\Delta_{v\cup\{s\}\cup w}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\Delta_{\{s\}}(\Delta_{v\cup w}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=\Delta_{\{s\}}(\Delta_{v}(\Delta_{w}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}))=\Delta_{v\cup\{s\}}(\Delta_{w}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}),

which completes the proof.
∎

Definition 1
.

The weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}
are called

completely monotone

if

\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}\geq 0

for all
u,v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
. We denote by

\mathcal{M}_{d}:=\{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}:\text{$\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\geq{\boldsymbol{0}}$ for every $v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}$}\}

the set of all completely monotone weights in
\mathcal{W}_{d}
.

The notion of complete monotonicity from
Definition
1
is a particular instance of a
general concept
for set functions, see
Kurtz 1974
.
Moreover, for
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
complete monotonicity of
functions on the set
\mathcal{U}_{d}
, or equivalently on the discrete unit
cube
\{0,1\}^{d}
, is analogous
to complete monotonicity of functions on the continuous
unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
. See Appendices
A.1
and
A.2

for details.
The study of complete monotonicity of weights in the context
of weighted function spaces (in the sense of Sloan and
Woźniakowski) seems to be new.

Completely monotone weights satisfy the following
monotonicity conditions,
the first of which has
already been employed
in the literature on high-dimensional integration,
see, e.g.,
Dick et al. 2013
.

Lemma 4
.

For
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
and
v,w\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
we have

v\subset w\hskip 10.00002pt\Rightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\left(\gamma_{v}\geq\gamma_{w}\ \wedge\ \Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\geq\Delta_{w}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right).

Proof.

Due to Lemma
3

it suffices to consider the case
w=v\cup\{s\}
with

s\not\in v
. Using Lemma
2

and
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
we obtain

\gamma_{v}=\left(\Delta_{\emptyset}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{v}=\left(\Delta_{\{s\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{v}+\left(\Delta_{\emptyset}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{w}\geq\left(\Delta_{\emptyset}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{w}=\gamma_{w}

as well as

\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=\left(\Delta_{w}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}+\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u\cup\{s\}}\geq\left(\Delta_{w}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}

for any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
.
∎

Let
\max\emptyset:=0
by convention.
For
d=\infty
and
u,v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
we define
u\leq v

if
\max u\leq\max v
to turn

\mathcal{U}_{d}
into a directed set, and we put

\mathcal{N}_{d}:=\{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}\colon\lim_{u}\gamma_{u}=0\}.

For
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
we put
\mathcal{N}_{d}:=\mathcal{M}_{d}
.

Lemma 5
.

For
v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
the difference operator
\Delta_{v}

satisfies
\Delta_{v}(\mathcal{M}_{d})\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{d}
as well as

\Delta_{v}(\mathcal{N}_{d})\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{d}
.

Proof.

Let
v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
. Due to Lemma
3
and the definition of

\mathcal{M}_{d}
, we obtain for
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
and each
w\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
that

\Delta_{w}(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=\Delta_{v\cup w}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\geq{\boldsymbol{0}}
, i.e.,

\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
.

Now consider
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{N}_{d}
in the case
d=\infty
.
Since
\mathcal{N}_{d}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{d}
, we know
that
\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
. Furthermore, Lemma
4
yields

\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\leq\Delta_{\emptyset}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}={\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
. Due to the definition of

\mathcal{N}_{d}
, these facts imply that
\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{N}_{d}
.
∎

2.3
The Sum Operator and Its Inverse

For any
C>0
we consider the set

\mathcal{S}_{d,C}:=\left\{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}:\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}C^{2|v|}\gamma_{v}<\infty\right\}

of weights and the sum operator

T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}\colon\mathcal{S}_{d,C}\to\mathcal{W}_{d}

given by

\left(T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\\
u\subseteq v\end{subarray}}C^{2\left|v\right|}\gamma_{v}.

Clearly
\mathcal{S}_{d,C}=\mathcal{W}_{d}
for
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
To simplify the notation in proofs of results,
we write
\mathcal{S}_{d}
and
T^{\uparrow}_{d}

instead of
\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
and
T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}
, respectively, as long as

C
is considered to be fixed.

In the sequel we show that the sum operator is injective, and we
determine its range and
its inverse.
First of all, we note that

T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}(\mathcal{S}_{d,C})=T^{\uparrow}_{d,1}(\mathcal{S}_{d,1})

(4)

for every
C>0
.

Lemma 6
.

For every
C>0
we have

Lemma 8
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}:=(\rho_{u})_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{U}_{d}}

with
q\leq p

and

u\subseteq v\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathcal{U}_{p}\\
v\subseteq w\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|v|}\rho_{w}=(-1)^{|u|}\sum_{v\subseteq[p]\setminus[q]}\rho_{u\cup v}.

Proof.

We have

Lemma 14
.

Assume that
d=\infty
. Let
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
with

C>0
. Put
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}:=T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
. We have

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\left(\gamma_{u}^{\uparrow}\right)^{1/\tau}<\infty\hskip 10.00002pt\Rightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/\tau}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C^{1/\tau}}

for every
\tau>0
and

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/\tau}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,\sqrt{2}C^{1/\tau}}\hskip 10.00002pt\Rightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\left(\gamma_{u}^{\uparrow}\right)^{1/\tau}<\infty

for every
\tau\geq 1
.

Proof.

Let
\tau>0
.
We use
\gamma_{u}^{\uparrow}\geq C^{2|u|}\,\gamma_{u}
to obtain

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\left(\gamma^{\uparrow}_{u}\right)^{1/\tau}\geq\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}C^{2|u|/\tau}\,\gamma_{u}^{1/\tau},

which yields the first implication.

For the proof of the second implication in the case
\tau\geq 1

we employ
an inequality sometimes referred to as Jensen’s inequality. This inequality
states that
(\sum y_{i})^{p}\leq\sum y_{i}^{p}
, if we consider non-negative

y_{i}
and
p\in{(0,1]}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\left(\gamma^{\uparrow}_{u}\right)^{1/\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\\
u\subseteq v\end{subarray}}C^{2|v|}\,\gamma_{v}\right)^{1/\tau}\leq\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\\
u\subseteq v\end{subarray}}C^{2|v|/\tau}\,\gamma_{v}^{1/\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}C^{2|v|/\tau}\,\gamma_{v}^{1/\tau}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\\
u\subseteq v\end{subarray}}1=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}2^{|v|}\,C^{2|v|/\tau}\,\gamma_{v}^{1/\tau}

for every
\tau\geq 1
, which yields the second implication.

3
Particular Classes of Weights

In the sequel, we study some important classes of weights of
a particular structure, namely:

(i)

Product weights
, which are of the form

\gamma_{u}=\prod_{j\in u}\gamma_{j}

for all
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
, where
(\gamma_{j})_{j\in[d]}

is any non-increasing sequence
of non-negative real numbers.
By convention the empty product is
equal to one, i.e.,
\gamma_{\emptyset}=1
.

(ii)

Product and order-dependent (POD) weights

of the form

\gamma_{u}=\Gamma_{|u|}\prod_{j\in u}\gamma_{j}

for all
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
with
(\gamma_{j})_{j\in[d]}
as in (i)
and with a sequence
(\Gamma_{k})_{k\in[d]\cup\{0\}}
of
non-negative real numbers that satisfies
for some constants
a,C_{a}>0
the estimate

\Gamma_{k}\leq C_{a}(k!)^{a}\hskip 12.91663pt\text{for all $k\in[d]\cup\{0\}$.}

Note that the latter property trivially holds true
for
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. For POD weights as before with
d=\infty
we put

p:=\decay((\gamma_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}).

(iii)

Finite-order weights of order
\omega\in{\mathbb{N}}
, which satisfy

\gamma_{u}=0\hskip 8.61108pt\text{for all $u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}$ with $|u|>\omega$.}

Weighted function spaces, and in particular, spaces
involving product weights have been introduced in
Sloan and Woźniakowski 1998
.
Product and order-dependent weights are introduced in
Kuo et al. 2012

as a generalization of product weights.
Finite-order weights are introduced for spaces of functions with a
finite number of variables in
Dick et al. 2006
. Subclasses include,
e.g., finite-diameter weights, see
Novak and Woźniakowski 2008
, finite-intersection
weights, see
Gnewuch 2012
, and finite-projective dimension (FPD)
weights, see
Dick and Gnewuch 2014
.

3.1
Complete Monotonicity

At first,
we discuss whether
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}

for any
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
of the form (i), (ii), or (iii).

Remark 2
.

Due to Lemma
4
,
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
implies, in particular,

\gamma_{v}\geq\gamma_{w}
for all
v,w\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
with
v\subset w
.
Obviously, this monotonicity property need not hold for
finite-order weights.

The same holds true for POD weights of the form (ii),
which has already been pointed out in
Dick et al. 2013
.
Indeed, assume that the POD weights are of the particular form

\gamma_{u}=\left((|u|+\ell)!\right)^{a}\prod_{j\in u}\gamma_{j}

(17)

for some
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
and for all
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
,
as considered in
Dick et al. 2013
.
Assume that
\gamma_{j}>0
for all
j\in[d]
. Then
for
v:=\{2,\dots,s\}
and
w:=\{1,\dots,s\}

with
s\in[d]
we have equivalence of

\gamma_{v}\geq\gamma_{w}
and

\gamma_{1}\leq\frac{1}{\left(s+\ell\right)^{a}}.

For
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
this implies

\gamma_{i}\leq\frac{1}{\left(d+\ell\right)^{a}}

for every
i\in[d]
, so that for POD weights of the
form (
17
) with
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}

and strictly positive
\gamma_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,[d]
, the weights

\gamma_{i}
have to be rather small. For
d=\infty

POD weights of the form (
17
) with all

\gamma_{j}>0
cannot be monotonically
decreasing and thus, in particular,
do not satisfy
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
.

For product weights the property
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
,
as well as
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{N}_{d}
,
\mathcal{A}_{d}
, or
\mathcal{P}_{d}
in the case

d=\infty
,
is easily characterized as follows.

Proposition 1
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
be product weights according to (i). We have

\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=\gamma_{u}\prod_{j\in v}(1-\gamma_{j})

(18)

for
u,v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
with
u\cap v=\emptyset
.
Moreover,

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}\hskip 10.00002pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,j\in[d]\colon 0\leq\gamma_{j}\leq 1.

(19)

If additionally
d=\infty
and
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
, then we have the
following equivalences:

(a)

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{N}_{d}\Leftrightarrow\lim_{j\to\infty}\gamma_{j}=0
,

(b)

T^{\downarrow}_{d}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=0\Leftrightarrow\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}=\infty
,

(c)

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}\Leftrightarrow\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}<\infty\Leftrightarrow{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}
.

Proof.

For product weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
according to (i) we have

\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|w|}\gamma_{u\cup w}=\gamma_{u}\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|w|}\prod_{j\in w}\gamma_{j}=\gamma_{u}\prod_{j\in v}(1-\gamma_{j})

for
u,v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
with
u\cap v=\emptyset
, which yields
(
18
) and, due to the definition of
\mathcal{M}_{d}
,
also (
19
).

Now let
d=\infty
and
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
. Concerning (a): Note that due
to (
19
) we have
\gamma_{u}\leq\gamma_{\max u}

for all
\emptyset\neq u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
. Hence

\lim_{j\to\infty}\gamma_{j}=0
implies

\lim_{u}\gamma_{u}=0
, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{N}_{d}
.
The reverse implication follows trivially from

\gamma_{j}=\gamma_{\{j\}}
for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Concerning (b): Due to
(
18
) we have

C^{2|u|}\left(T^{\downarrow}_{d}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=\lim_{s\to\infty}\left(\Delta_{[s]\setminus u}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=\gamma_{u}\lim_{s\to\infty}\prod_{j\in[s]\setminus u}(1-\gamma_{j}).

Let
u_{1}:=\{j\in{\mathbb{N}}:\gamma_{j}=1\}
.
If
u_{1}
is an infinite set (which is equivalent to
u_{1}={\mathbb{N}}
), then

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\gamma_{j}=\infty
and
T^{\downarrow}_{d}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=0
.

So let us assume that
u_{1}
is finite.
Suppose first that
T^{\downarrow}_{d}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=0
. Then we choose
u=u_{1}
and obtain

\lim_{s\to\infty}\prod_{j\in[s]\setminus u_{1}}(1-\gamma_{j})=0,

(20)

as
\gamma_{u_{1}}\neq 0
. Moreover,
(
20
) is equivalent to

\lim_{s\to\infty}\sum_{j\in[s]\setminus u_{1}}\gamma_{j}=\infty,

which, in turn, is equivalent to
\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\gamma_{j}=\infty
.

Suppose on the other hand that
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is such that

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\gamma_{j}=\infty
. Then we have, for all
v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
,

0\leq\prod_{j\in v}(1-\gamma_{j})\leq\exp\left(-\sum_{j\in v}\gamma_{j}\right),

and thus, for all
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
,

\lim_{s\to\infty}\prod_{j\in[s]\setminus u}(1-\gamma_{j})=0.

This completes the proof of (b).

Concerning (c): Recall
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\neq 0
. If

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}
, then Theorem
2

assures that
\left(T^{\uparrow}_{d}\circ T^{\downarrow}_{d}\right){\boldsymbol{\gamma}}={\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.
Hence
T^{\downarrow}_{d}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\neq 0
and (b) implies

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\gamma_{j}<\infty
. In case we have

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\gamma_{j}<\infty
, we obtain

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\gamma_{u}=\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}(1+\gamma_{j})<\infty
,
i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}
. Finally,
\mathcal{P}_{d}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{d}
, see Theorem
2
.
∎

3.2
Summability

Next, we discuss whether
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
for any

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
of the form (i), (ii), or (iii) in the non-trivial
case
d=\infty
.

To do so for POD weights, we need
the following result,
which is essentially a variation of the statement in

Dick and Gnewuch 2014
.

Lemma 15
.

Assume that
d=\infty
. Let
C>0
and let
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
be
POD weights with
a
and
p
according to (ii).
Then we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq[j]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}C^{2|u|}\gamma_{u}\geq C^{2}\Gamma_{1}\gamma_{j}

(21)

for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Furthermore,
if either

p>a\hskip 12.91663pt\text{and}\hskip 12.91663pt\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\gamma_{j}<\infty

(22)

or

p=a\geq 1\hskip 12.91663pt\text{and}\hskip 12.91663pt\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{j}\right)^{1/p}<1

(23)

holds, then there exists a constant
c>0

such that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq[j]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}C^{2|u|}\gamma_{u}\leq c\gamma_{j}

(24)

for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
c
may depend on
C
and
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.

Proof.

Since
\gamma_{\{j\}}=\Gamma_{1}\gamma_{j}
, estimate
(
21
) obviously holds true.

To prove the second statement of the lemma, we use arguments very similar
to the ones used in
Dick and Gnewuch 2014
in the proof of Theorem 5.
We first prove estimate (
24
)
under the assumption that

p\geq\max(1,a)\hskip 12.91663pt\text{and}\hskip 12.91663pt\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{j}\right)^{1/p}<1.

Since
p\geq\max(1,a)
, we may use
Jensen’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq[j]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}C^{2|u|}\gamma_{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq[j]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}\Gamma_{|u|}\prod_{i\in u}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq[j]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}C_{a}\left(|u|!\right)^{a}\prod_{i\in u}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{a}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq[j]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}|u|!\prod_{i\in u}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{1/p}\right)^{p}.

We now use for a multi-index

\nu=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{j})\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{j}
the notations

|\nu|:=\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{j}
and

\nu!:=\prod_{i=1}^{j}\nu_{i}!
.
Moreover, we use the shorthand

T_{p}(C):=\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{1/p}.

Then we obtain with the help of the
multinomial formula and the formula for the (convergent) geometric series,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq[j]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}|u|!\prod_{i\in u}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{1/p}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\{0,1\}^{j}\\
\nu_{j}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{|\nu|!}{\nu!}\prod_{i=1}^{j}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{\nu_{i}/p}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{j}\\
\nu_{j}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{|\nu|!}{\nu!}\prod_{i=1}^{j}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{\nu_{i}/p}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt=\sum_{\kappa\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{j}\\
|\nu|=\kappa\end{subarray}}\frac{\kappa!}{\nu!}\prod_{i=1}^{j}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{\nu_{i}/p}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{j-1}\\
|\nu|=\kappa\end{subarray}}\frac{\kappa!}{\nu!}\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{\nu_{i}/p}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt=\sum_{\kappa\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{1/p}\right)^{\kappa}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{1/p}\right)^{\kappa}\,\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt=\frac{1}{1-\sum_{i=1}^{j}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{1/p}}-\frac{1}{1-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{1/p}}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\leq\frac{\left(C^{2}\gamma_{j}\right)^{1/p}}{\left(1-T_{p}(C)\right)^{2}}.

(25)

Altogether, we get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq[j]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}C^{2|u|}\gamma_{u}\leq C_{a}\left(1-T_{p}(C)\right)^{-2p}C^{2}\gamma_{j}.

In particular, we have shown
that condition (
23
) implies inequality (
24
).

Now assume that condition (
22
) holds.
We consider the non-trivial case
\gamma_{1}>0
, and we put

\tau:=1
if
a<1
and choose
\tau\in(a,p)

if
a\geq 1
.

We put
R:=\left(2\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{1/\tau}\right)^{\!\tau}

and note that, by the definition of
p
in (ii),
we have
R\in(0,\infty)
. Moreover, we define

\widetilde{C}:=C/\sqrt{R}
, and we note that

(|u|!)^{\alpha}\cdot x^{|u|}\leq\exp\left(x^{1/(1-\alpha)}\right)\cdot|u|!

(26)

for every
x>0
and every
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Due to Jensen’s inequality we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq[j]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}C^{2|u|}\gamma_{u}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq[j]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}C_{a}\left(|u|!\right)^{a}\prod_{i\in u}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=C_{a}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq[j]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}\left(|u|!\right)^{a}R^{|u|}\prod_{i\in u}\left(\widetilde{C}^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{a}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq[j]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}\left(|u|!\right)^{a/\tau}R^{|u|/\tau}\prod_{i\in u}\left(\widetilde{C}^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{1/\tau}\right)^{\tau}

\displaystyle\leq C_{a}\exp(\tau R^{1/(\tau-a)})\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq[j]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}|u|!\prod_{i\in u}\left(\widetilde{C}^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{1/\tau}\right)^{\tau}

\displaystyle\leq C_{a}\exp(\tau R^{1/(\tau-a)})\left(1-T_{\tau}(\widetilde{C})\right)^{-2\tau}\widetilde{C}^{2}\gamma_{j},

where in the penultimate step we have used
(
26
) with
\alpha=a/\tau

and
x=R^{1/\tau}
, and in the last step we have used
\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(\widetilde{C}^{2}\gamma_{i}\right)^{1/\tau}=1/2<1
and
(
3.2
)
(with
p
replaced by
\tau
and
C
replaced by
\widetilde{C}
).
∎

The following result is well known for product weights,
and for variants in the case of POD weights we refer to

Kuo et al. 2012
and
Dick and Gnewuch 2014
.

Proposition 2
.

Assume that
d=\infty
.

For product weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
according to (i) and every
C>0

we have

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}\hskip 10.00002pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\gamma_{j}<\infty.

For POD weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
with
a
and
p
according to (ii)
and for every
C>0
the following statement holds:
If
p>a
and
\Gamma_{1}>0
, then

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}\hskip 10.00002pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\gamma_{j}<\infty.

Furthermore, if
p=a\geq 1
, then

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{j}\right)^{1/p}<1
implies
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
.

For finite-order weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

according to (iii) and every
C>0
we have

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}\hskip 10.00002pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\gamma_{u}<\infty.

Proof.

In the case (i) the statement follows from

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}C^{2|u|}\gamma_{u}=\prod_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}(1+C^{2}\gamma_{j}).

Consider the case (ii). Assume first that
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
.
If
\Gamma_{1}>0
, then estimate (
21
)
implies
\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\gamma_{j}<\infty
.
Assume now that either
p>a
and
\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\gamma_{j}<\infty

or that
p=a\geq 1
and
\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{j}\right)^{1/p}<1
.
Due to Lemma
15
we have
estimate (
24
), which yields
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
.

For finite-order weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
of order
\omega\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have

\min\{1,C^{2\omega}\}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\gamma_{u}\leq\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}C^{2|u|}\gamma_{u}\leq\max\{1,C^{2\omega}\}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\gamma_{u},

hence
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
is satisfied if and
only if the weights
(\gamma_{u})_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}
are summable.
∎

3.3
Invariance Properties

Now,
we consider the following question:
If
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
belongs to one of the classes specified by (i), (ii), or
(iii), does
T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
or
T^{\downarrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

belong to the same class?

Remark 3
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
be finite-order weights of order
\omega
. From the definition
of
T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}
and the representation of

T^{\downarrow}_{d,C}
according
to Lemma
7
we see immediately that the
weights
T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and
T^{\downarrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

are again finite-order
weights of the same order
\omega
(as long as
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
lies in

\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
or
\mathcal{M}_{d}
, respectively).

Remark 4
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
be product weights according to (i).
If
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
, then we have

\Gamma_{\emptyset}:=\prod_{j\in[d]}(1+C^{2}\gamma_{j})<\infty,

which follows in the case
d=\infty
from Proposition
2
.
Furthermore, for
u\in\mathcal{U}
,

\displaystyle\left(T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}

\displaystyle=C^{2|u|}\gamma_{u}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\\
v\cap u=\emptyset\end{subarray}}C^{2|v|}\gamma_{v}=C^{2|u|}\prod_{j\in u}\gamma_{j}\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus u}(1+C^{2}\gamma_{j})

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\emptyset}\prod_{j\in u}\frac{C^{2}\gamma_{j}}{1+C^{2}\gamma_{j}}.

We conclude that
T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
are product weights if
and only if
\Gamma_{\emptyset}=1
, which is equivalent to
the trivial case
\gamma_{j}=0
for all
j\in[d]
. In any case,

T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
are very particular POD weights, namely
with
\Gamma_{k}:=\Gamma_{\emptyset}
for
k\in[d]
.

The natural question to ask next is whether the image

T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
of POD weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
are again POD weights. Also
the answer to this question is in general negative, as the
following example illustrates.

Let
d:=3
and
C:=1
. Moreover, let
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
be POD weights
with
\gamma_{\{1,2,3\}}>0
. Suppose that the weights

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}:=T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
are again POD weights, which
means that they can be represented in the form

\gamma^{\uparrow}_{u}=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{|u|}\prod_{j\in u}\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}

for some

\widetilde{\gamma}_{1},\widetilde{\gamma}_{2},\widetilde{\gamma}_{3}>0

and

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{0},\dots,\widetilde{\Gamma}_{3}>0
. We obtain

\gamma_{\{1,2\}}^{\uparrow}=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{2}\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}\widetilde{\gamma}_{2}=\gamma_{\{1\}}^{\uparrow}\gamma_{\{2\}}^{\uparrow}\frac{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{2}}{(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1})^{2}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma_{\{1,3\}}^{\uparrow}=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{2}\widetilde{\gamma}_{1}\widetilde{\gamma}_{3}=\gamma_{\{1\}}^{\uparrow}\gamma_{\{3\}}^{\uparrow}\frac{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{2}}{(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1})^{2}},

which implies

\frac{\gamma_{\{1,2\}}^{\uparrow}}{\gamma_{\{2\}}^{\uparrow}}=\frac{\gamma_{\{1,3\}}^{\uparrow}}{\gamma_{\{3\}}^{\uparrow}}.

(27)

Specifically, for

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}:=3,\ \gamma_{2}:=2,\ \gamma_{3}:=1,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}:=3,\ \Gamma_{2}:=4,\ \Gamma_{3}:=5,

we obtain

\gamma_{\{2\}}=6,\ \gamma_{\{3\}}=3,\ \gamma_{\{1,2\}}=24,\ \gamma_{\{1,3\}}=12,\ \gamma_{\{2,3\}}=8,\ \gamma_{\{1,2,3\}}=30,\

and therefore

\gamma_{\{2\}}^{\uparrow}=68,\ \gamma_{\{3\}}^{\uparrow}=53,\ \gamma_{\{1,2\}}^{\uparrow}=54,\ \gamma_{\{1,3\}}^{\uparrow}=42,

so that (
27
) is not satisfied. We conclude that the weights

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}
are no longer POD weights.

Regarding
T^{\downarrow}_{d,C}
, it is also not hard to see that
for product weights according to (i) with

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
the transformed weights

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\downarrow}:=T^{\downarrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

are in general not even of POD form.
Indeed,
let
d\geq 3
and
\gamma_{1}:=1
,
\gamma_{2}:=1/2
, and

\gamma_{j}:=0
for
j\geq 3
.
Due to Proposition
1
, we have
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
and

\left(\Delta_{[s]\setminus u}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=\gamma_{u}\prod_{j\in[s]\setminus u}(1-\gamma_{j})

for
s\in[d]
and
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
. Therefore

\gamma_{u}^{\downarrow}=C^{-2|u|}\gamma_{u}\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus u}(1-\gamma_{j})

in both cases
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
d=\infty
.
Assume now that the weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\downarrow}
are of POD form, i.e.,

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\downarrow}_{u}=\Gamma^{\prime}_{|u|}\prod_{j\in u}\gamma^{\prime}_{j}

for every
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
with suitably chosen
\Gamma^{\prime}_{k}\geq 0
and
\gamma_{j}^{\prime}\geq 0
.
Since
\gamma^{\downarrow}_{\{1\}}=C^{-2}/2

and
\gamma^{\downarrow}_{\{2\}}=0
, we conclude that

\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}>0
and
\gamma^{\prime}_{2}=0
.
It follows that
\gamma^{\downarrow}_{u}=0
whenever
2\in u
.
However,
\gamma^{\downarrow}_{\{1,2\}}=C^{-4}/2
,
which is a contradiction.

Therefore we are aiming at weaker results for product and for POD
weights.

Proposition 3
.

Consider product weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
according to (i) and any
C>0
.
If
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
then

{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\leq T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\leq c\cdot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}

for the product weights
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
given by

\eta_{u}:=C^{2|u|}\gamma_{u}

for
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}

and

c:=\prod_{j\in[d]}(1+C^{2}\gamma_{j})\in{[1,\infty)}.

If
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
then

c^{\prime}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\leq T^{\downarrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\leq{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}

for the product weights
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}
given by

\zeta_{u}:=C^{-2|u|}\gamma_{u}

for
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
and

c^{\prime}:=\prod_{j\in[d]}(1-\gamma_{j})\in[0,1].

Furthermore,
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
if and only if
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
.

Proof.

For product weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
we put

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}:=T^{\uparrow}_{d}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.
For any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
,

\gamma_{u}^{\uparrow}=\eta_{u}\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus u}(1+C^{2}\gamma_{j}),

see Remark

Furthermore,

1\leq\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus u}(1+C^{2}\gamma_{j})\leq c,

which yields the bounds for
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}
as claimed.
Obviously, the weights
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
are of product form,
and Proposition
2
yields
c<\infty
as well as
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
.

For product weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
we put
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\downarrow}:=T^{\downarrow}_{d}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.
As shown in Remark

, we have

\gamma^{\downarrow}_{u}=C^{-2|u|}\gamma_{u}\,\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus u}(1-\gamma_{j}).

0\leq c^{\prime}\leq\prod_{j\in[d]\setminus u}(1-\gamma_{j})\leq 1.

Hereby we obtain the bounds for
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\downarrow}

{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}

Proposition

{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}

and
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}

∎

Remark 5

Proposition 4
.

Consider POD weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
with
a

according to (ii) and any
C>0
. We put

c:=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}(|w|!)^{a}\prod_{j\in w}(2^{a}C^{2}\gamma_{j})\in[1,\infty].

If
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
then

{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\leq T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\leq c\cdot{\boldsymbol{\xi}}

for the POD weights
{\boldsymbol{\eta}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}

given by

\xi_{u}:=C_{a}\left(|u|!\right)^{a}\prod_{j\in u}\left(2^{a}C^{2}\gamma_{j}\right)=C_{a}\left(2^{|u|}|u|!\right)^{a}\eta_{u}.

If

{\boldsymbol{0}}\leq T^{\downarrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\leq{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}

for the POD weights
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}

\zeta_{u}:=C^{-2|u|}\gamma_{u}.

Proof.

For POD weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}

the weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}:=T^{\uparrow}_{d}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
take the form

\displaystyle\gamma_{u}^{\uparrow}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\\
u\subseteq v\end{subarray}}C^{2|v|}\gamma_{v}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\\
u\subseteq v\end{subarray}}\Gamma_{|v|}\prod_{j\in v}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j\in u}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{j}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\\

which yields the lower bound
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}\geq{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
.
For an upper bound on the terms
\gamma_{u}^{\uparrow}

\gamma_{u}^{\uparrow}\leq C_{a}\prod_{j\in u}\left(C^{2}\gamma_{j}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\\

Now let us turn to weights

∎

3.4
The Decay of Transformed Weights

In the sequel, we consider the case
d=\infty
.
As before, we use the notation

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/\tau}=(\gamma_{u}^{1/\tau})_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}
for

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}
and
\tau>0
.

We relate the decay of the transformed weights

T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
to the decay of the original weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
.
The relevance of the results from the present section
will be indicated
in Remark
13
below.

At first we point to an extremal case.

Remark 8
.

For
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
we put
\gamma_{u}:=2^{-j}
if
u=\{1,\dots,2^{j}\}
for any
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\gamma_{u}:=0

otherwise. Obviously we have
\decay({\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=\infty
. Let
C:=1

as well as
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

\{1,\dots,2^{j}\}\subseteq v\subseteq\{1,\dots,2^{j+1}\}
,
so that
(T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})_{v}\geq 2^{-(j+1)}

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\left(T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)^{1/\tau}_{u}\geq\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}2^{2^{j}}\cdot 2^{-(j+1)/\tau}=\infty

for every
\tau>0
. Therefore we have
\decay(T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=0
.

Let us now check the decay of the transformed weights
T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

for the particular classes (i)–(iii) of weights studied before.
To this end, we note that if
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
belongs to one of these
classes, then
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/\tau}
belongs to the same class.

Proposition 5
.

Consider POD weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
according to (ii) with
\Gamma_{1}>0
,

, and
\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\gamma_{j}<\infty
, and any

.
Then we have

\decay\left(T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)=\decay({\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=p.

Proof.

At first, we note that

\decay((\gamma_{j}^{1/\tau})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}})=p/\tau
for
every
\tau>0
.
Let us consider the POD weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/\tau}
for any
\tau>0
.
We first show
\decay({\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=p
.

Due to
\gamma_{\{j\}}=\Gamma_{1}\gamma_{j}
, we obtain

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\gamma_{u}^{1/\tau}\geq\Gamma_{1}^{1/\tau}\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\gamma_{j}^{1/\tau}

for all

. Since
\Gamma_{1}>0
, this yields
\decay({\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\leq p
.

Let
\tau\in(0,p)

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\gamma_{j}^{1/\tau}<\infty
,
and our assumption

Lemma
15
, there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq[j]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}\gamma_{u}^{1/\tau}\leq c\cdot\gamma_{j}^{1/\tau}

for all

. This implies

.

Due to Proposition
2
we have

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
, hence
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}:=T^{\uparrow}_{d,C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is well-defined.
Next we show

Let

of Lemma

Now Proposition
2

yields

Now we use our assumption

which
implies, in particular,

. Let

and let

\tau=p=1

In both cases we have

2
,

Due to the second implication from

we obtain

∎

Since product weights

4
Function Space Embeddings

Now we study embeddings of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
(RKHSs); for elementary facts on RKHSs and
their kernels we refer to
Aronszajn 1950

and
Paulsen and Raghupathi 2016
. For a reproducing kernel
M\colon{\mathfrak{Z}}\times{\mathfrak{Z}}\to{\mathbb{K}}
we
denote its uniquely determined RKHS by

H(M)
, the corresponding norm by
\|\cdot\|_{M}
,
and we say that
M
is a reproducing kernel over the domain
{\mathfrak{Z}}
.

At first, we state two basic facts concerning embeddings of RKHSs.
For reproducing kernels
M
and
M^{\prime}
over a common domain
{\mathfrak{Z}}

we say that
H(M)
is contractively contained in
H(M^{\prime})
,
if
H(M)\subseteq H(M^{\prime})
with an embedding of norm at most
one.
Furthermore, we denote by
M\otimes M^{\prime}
the function
given by

M\otimes M^{\prime}\big((x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\big):=M(x_{1},y_{1})\cdot M^{\prime}(x_{2},y_{2})

for all
(x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\in{\mathfrak{Z}}\times{\mathfrak{Z}}
.
It follows immediately from Schur’s product theorem,
see, e.g.,
Paulsen and Raghupathi 2016
, that

M\otimes M^{\prime}
is again a reproducing kernel.

For the following result see, e.g.,
Paulsen and Raghupathi 2016
.

Lemma 16
.

The space
H(M)
is contractively contained in
H(M^{\prime})
if and only
if
M^{\prime}-M
is a reproducing kernel.

The following result, which deals with tensor products, is easily
derived from Lemma
16
.

Lemma 17
.

If
H(M_{i})
is contractively contained in
H(M^{\prime}_{i})

for
i=1,2
, then
H(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})
is contractively contained
in
H(M^{\prime}_{1}\otimes M^{\prime}_{2})
.

Next, we consider reproducing kernels
M
and
M^{\prime}
over
domains
{\mathfrak{Z}}
and
{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}
,
respectively, such that
{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathfrak{Z}}
.
We write

H(M)\sqsubseteq H(M^{\prime})

if
f|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}}\in H(M^{\prime})
for
every
f\in H(M)
. The restriction theorem
yields the following generalization of
Lemma
16
.

Lemma 18
.

We have
H(M)\sqsubseteq H(M^{\prime})
with a restriction mapping

f\mapsto f|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}}
of norm at most one, if and only if

M^{\prime}-M|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}\times{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}}
is a reproducing kernel.

Proof.

The restriction theorem says that
H(M|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}\times{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}})
consists of all functions
f|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}}
with

f\in H(M)
,
and

\|g\|_{M|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}\times{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}}}=\inf\{\|f\|_{M}:\text{$f\in H(M)$, $f|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}}=g$}\}

for every
g\in H(M|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}\times{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}})
, see, e.g.,

Paulsen and Raghupathi 2016
.

Suppose that
H(M)\sqsubseteq H(M^{\prime})
with a restriction mapping
of norm at most one. Let

g\in H(M|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}\times{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}})
. We obtain
g\in H(M^{\prime})
and
\|g\|_{M^{\prime}}\leq\|f\|_{M}

for every
f\in H(M)
with
f|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}}=g
. Therefore

\|g\|_{M^{\prime}}\leq\|g\|_{M|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}\times{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}}},

which shows that

H(M|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}\times{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}})
is
contractively contained in
H(M^{\prime})
.
Due to Lemma
16
we have that

M^{\prime}-M|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}\times{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}}
is a reproducing kernel.

Conversely, suppose that the latter holds true.
Due to Lemma
16
we obtain for

f\in H(M)
that
f|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}}\in H(M^{\prime})
and

\|f|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}}\|_{M^{\prime}}\leq\|f|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}}\|_{M|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}\times{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\prime}}}\leq\|f\|_{M}

holds, which shows that
H(M)\sqsubseteq H(M^{\prime})
with a
restriction mapping of norm at most one.
∎

4.1
Superpositions of Weighted Tensor Product Kernels

We study embeddings and restriction mappings
between RKHSs of functions of
d
variables,
where
d\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
. The corresponding reproducing
kernels are assumed to be superpositions of weighted tensor product
kernels.

The starting point for the construction of
this kind of spaces is given by a
reproducing kernel
m\neq 0
over an arbitrary domain

D\neq\emptyset
.
The elements of
H(1+m)

are considered to be functions of a single variable
x\in D
.
For
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
the tensor product kernel
m_{u}
is defined by

m_{u}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}):=\prod_{j\in u}m(x_{j},y_{j})

for all
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in D^{[d]}
,
where by convention
m_{\emptyset}=1
.
The functions
f\in H(m_{u})
, which are formally defined
on
D^{[d]}
, may be identified with functions of
|u|
variables,
since
f({\mathbf{x}})
depends only on the coordinates

x_{j}
with
j\in u
.

In addition to the reproducing kernel
m
, we consider weights

{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}
such that

{\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},m}:=\biggl\{{\mathbf{x}}\in D^{[d]}:\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\eta_{u}m_{u}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}})<\infty\biggr\}

is non-empty.
This allows us to define a reproducing kernel
M^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},m}
by

M^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},m}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}):=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\eta_{u}m_{u}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})

for all
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},m}
. The elements of
H(M^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},m})
are
functions of
d
variables.

Remark 9
.

In the case
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
we obviously have

{\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},m}=D^{[d]}=D^{d}\neq\emptyset
.

In the case
d=\infty
we have

{\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},m}\neq\emptyset\hskip 10.00002pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}q^{|u|}\eta_{u}<\infty

with
q:=\inf_{x\in D}m(x,x)
,
and
{\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},m}
is the maximal domain for the
reproducing kernel
M^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},m}
. See
Gnewuch et al. 2014
for
details.
For the discussion of the particular cases of product weights, POD
weights, and finite-order weights we refer to
Proposition
2
with
C:=q^{1/2}
.

Actually, we consider a pair of reproducing kernels

m,m^{\uparrow}
over the domain
D
,
a pair of weights
{\boldsymbol{\eta}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\uparrow}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}
,
and a constant
C>0

with the following properties:

(A1)

We have
m,m^{\uparrow}\neq 0
and

H(m)\subseteq H(1+m^{\uparrow}).

Moreover,

C\geq\sup\{\|f\|_{1+m^{\uparrow}}:f\in H(m),\ \|f\|_{m}\leq 1\}.

(A2)

We have
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
and

{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\uparrow}=T_{d,C}^{\uparrow}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}.

(A3)

We have

{\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\uparrow}\!,m^{\uparrow}}\neq\emptyset
.

Remark 10
.

Observe that
H(m)\neq\{0\}
is
continuously embedded into
H(1+m^{\uparrow})

due to the closed graph theorem.
Consequently, the constant
C
may be any upper bound on
the norm of this embedding. It follows that

H(m)
is contractively contained in
H(C^{2}(1+m^{\uparrow}))
.

Remark 11
.

In the case
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
the assumption (A3) is trivially satisfied,
since
{\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\uparrow},m^{\uparrow}}={\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},m}=D^{[d]}=D^{d}\neq\emptyset
, cf. Remark
9
.

Consider the case
d=\infty
. For every
q\geq 0
we have

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}q^{|u|}\eta_{u}^{\uparrow}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\\
u\subseteq v\end{subarray}}q^{|u|}C^{2|v|}\eta_{v}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}(1+q)^{|v|}C^{2|v|}\eta_{v}.

Using Remark
9
, we conclude that (A3) is equivalent to

{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,\widetilde{C}}
with

\widetilde{C}:=(1+\inf_{x\in D}m^{\uparrow}(x,x))^{1/2}\cdot C.

Since
\widetilde{C}^{2}\geq\inf_{x\in D}m(x,x)
, which
follows from
Remark
10
, we may use Remark
9
again to conclude that
(A3) implies
{\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},m}\neq\emptyset
.

Given (A1)–(A3), we study the reproducing kernels

M:=M^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},m}\hskip 12.91663pt\text{and}\hskip 12.91663ptM^{\uparrow}:=M^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\uparrow}\!,m^{\uparrow}}

over the corresponding maximal domains

{\mathfrak{Z}}:={\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},m}\hskip 12.91663pt\text{and}\hskip 12.91663pt{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}:={\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\uparrow}\!,m^{\uparrow}},

respectively.

4.2
Functions of Finitely Many Variables

We consider the setting described in Section
4.1

and confine ourselves in this subsection to
the case
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
H(M)
and

H(M^{\uparrow})
consist of functions over the common
domain
{\mathfrak{Z}}={\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}=D^{d}
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. If
(A1)
and
(A2)
are satisfied, then
the space
H(M)
is contractively contained in
H(M^{\uparrow})
.

Proof.

Put

\widehat{m}_{u}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}):=\prod_{j\in u}\left(C^{2}(1+m^{\uparrow}(x_{j},y_{j})\right)=C^{2|u|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\\
v\subseteq u\end{subarray}}m^{\uparrow}_{v}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})

for
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
and
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in D^{d}
.
First note that

\begin{split}M^{\uparrow}&=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\eta_{v}^{\uparrow}\,m^{\uparrow}_{v}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\\
v\subseteq u\end{subarray}}C^{2|u|}\eta_{u}\right)m^{\uparrow}_{v}\\
&=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}C^{2\left|u\right|}\eta_{u}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}\\
v\subseteq u\end{subarray}}m^{\uparrow}_{v}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\eta_{u}\widehat{m}_{u}.\end{split}

Therefore

M^{\uparrow}-M=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}\eta_{u}\left(\widehat{m}_{u}-m_{u}\right).

Since
H(m)
is contractively contained in
H(C^{2}(1+m^{\uparrow}))
,
see Remark
10
,
the same holds true for the spaces
H(m_{u})
and
H(\widehat{m}_{u})
,
see Lemma
17
. Hence Lemma
16
yields the claim.
∎

4.3
Functions
of Infinitely Many Variables

In the sequel, we consider again the setting described in
Section
4.1
, and focus in this subsection on the case

d=\infty
. We have the following kind of embedding
or, more precisely, restriction result.

Theorem 4
.

Let
d=\infty
. If
(A1)–(A3)
are satisfied, then we have

{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}\subseteq{\mathfrak{Z}}
and

H(M)\sqsubseteq H(M^{\uparrow}).

Moreover,
the norm of the corresponding restriction mapping

H(M)\to H(M^{\uparrow}),f\mapsto f|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}},

is at most one.

Proof.

We proceed as in the proof of Theorem
3
, and to do so
we only have to verify some summability conditions.
Again we consider

\widehat{m}_{u}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}):=\prod_{j\in u}\left(C^{2}(1+m^{\uparrow}(x_{j},y_{j})\right)=C^{2|u|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\\
v\subseteq u\end{subarray}}m^{\uparrow}_{v}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})

for
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}
and
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in D^{\mathbb{N}}
.
First of all, we note that

\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}\eta_{v}^{\uparrow}\,|m^{\uparrow}_{v}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})|=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\\
v\subseteq u\end{subarray}}C^{2|u|}\eta_{u}\,|m^{\uparrow}_{v}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})|<\infty

for
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}
. Indeed, for
{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{y}}
we employ the
definition of
{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}
, and in the general case we use
the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality together with

|m^{\uparrow}_{v}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})|^{2}\leq m^{\uparrow}_{v}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}})\cdot m^{\uparrow}_{v}({\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{y}}).

Thus we have

\displaystyle M^{\uparrow}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}\eta_{v}^{\uparrow}\,m^{\uparrow}_{v}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\\
v\subseteq u\end{subarray}}C^{2|u|}\eta_{u}\right)m^{\uparrow}_{v}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}C^{2\left|u\right|}\eta_{u}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}\\
v\subseteq u\end{subarray}}m^{\uparrow}_{v}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}\eta_{u}\widehat{m}_{u}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})

(28)

with absolutely convergent series.

Now, due Remark
10
,
we know that

m(x,x)\leq C^{2}(1+m^{\uparrow}(x,x))

for all
x\in D
. This implies for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}
that

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}\eta_{u}m_{u}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}})\leq\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}\eta_{u}\widehat{m}_{u}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}})\leq\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}\eta_{v}^{\uparrow}m_{v}^{\uparrow}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}})<\infty,

i.e., we have
{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}\subseteq{\mathfrak{Z}}
.
Moreover, we obtain for all
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}
that

\left(M^{\uparrow}-M\right)({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}\eta_{u}\left(\widehat{m}_{u}-m_{u}\right)({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})

and the sum converges absolutely.
As already stated in the proof of Theorem
3
,
the fact that
H(m)
is contractively contained in

H(C^{2}(1+m^{\uparrow}))
implies that
the same holds true for the spaces

H(m_{u})
and
H(\widehat{m}_{u})
.
This implies that
\widehat{m}_{u}-m_{u}
is a reproducing kernel,
cf. Lemma
16
, and this property is inherited by

\left(\widehat{m}_{u}-m_{u}\right)|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}\times{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}}
.
From that it is easily seen that

M^{\uparrow}-M|_{{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}\times{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}}
is a reproducing kernel.
Now Lemma
18
yields the statement of the theorem.
∎

In the final remark of this section we demonstrate that the
domains
{\mathfrak{Z}}
and
{\mathfrak{Z}}^{\uparrow}
can indeed be different.

Remark 12
.

We again consider the weights from
Remark
8
in the case

d=\infty
:
For
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
we put
\eta_{u}:=2^{-j}
if
u=\{1,\dots,2^{j}\}
for any
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\eta_{u}:=0
otherwise.
Furthermore, we consider the kernel
m
on the unit interval

D:=[0,1]
given by

m(x,y)=1
if
x=y\in(0,1]
and
m(x,y)=0

otherwise. We take
m^{\uparrow}:=m

and may therefore choose
C=1
.
Then, on the one hand, we have for all
{\mathbf{x}}\in[0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
that

M({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\infty}}\eta_{u}\,m_{u}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}2^{-j}\prod_{\nu=1}^{2^{j}}m(x_{\nu},x_{\nu})\leq\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}2^{-j}<\infty.

. On the other hand,

5
Application to Computational Problems

Now we describe how to apply our findings from the previous
sections to the study of the complexity of continuous computational
problems.

In tractability analysis we are interested in continuous
computational problems that are defined on a scale of function
spaces
H:=H_{d}
with
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where the parameter

d
typically denotes the number of variables the functions in

H_{d}
depend on. An important goal is to find algorithms that scale
well with respect to the
dimension parameter
d
and help us to identify whether
the curse of dimensionality can be broken in a given setting.

Tractability analysis still deals with a finite (although
arbitrarily large) number of variables. Now many applications
rely on models that depend on an infinite (but still countable)
number of variables,
which motivates the study of spaces
H:=H_{\infty}
of functions
that depend on infinitely-many variables.
Examples include stochastic models that are based
on infinite sequences of independent random variables.
The complexity analysis of the resulting continuous problems may
be viewed as the limit of tractability analysis for

d
-variate functions, where
d
tends to infinity.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Some specific features of the function spaces can be very helpful
for the analysis of certain types of algorithms. For instance, for
the analysis of unbiased randomized algorithms it may be extremely
helpful if the norm on
H_{d}
induces an ANOVA
decomposition on
H_{d}
, which can be used to analyze
the error (
=
variance) of the algorithms see, e.g.,
Baldeaux and Gnewuch 2014
,
in particular, Lemma 2.1. Contrarily, for the analysis of
deterministic algorithms it may be helpful to use the Taylor
expansion of input functions in certain points and therefore
it is beneficial to work with an underlying anchored function space
decomposition, see, e.g.,
Dick and Gnewuch 2014
.

In general, a suitable embedding or restriction mapping between
functions spaces may help to transfer results from
a function space with favorable features to another
function space.
There are several general embedding results known in the case where

H_{d}
is the
d
-fold tensor product of
reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces of a single variable;
examples include Hilbert spaces with reproducing
kernels of the form (
2
),
where the weights
\gamma_{u}
are product weights,
and tensor products of
spaces of increasing smoothness, see,
e.g.,
Hefter and Ritter 2015
,
Gnewuch et al. 2017a
, and
Gnewuch et al. 2019
.

Due to the machinery we have developed in the previous
sections, we now have a general embedding approach at hand that
still works in the case where
H_{d}
is not necessarily of tensor
product form. This is, e.g., the case if we consider weighted RKHSs,
where the weights are not of product form but are, e.g., product
and order-dependent or
of finite-order instead, see Section
3
.

Let us now consider for
d\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
the reproducing
kernel

K:=M^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},k}

as defined in Section
4.1
by means of a

(B1)

reproducing kernel

k\neq 0
over a domain
D

and weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}
such that

{\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},k}\neq\emptyset
.

Suppose that we are, e.g., interested in solving a computational problem
on the space
H:=H(K)
of functions of
d
variables,
further specified by a Banach space
G
and a continuous
linear solution operator

S\colon H(K)\to G.

Solution operators
of this type arise, e.g., for numerical integration,
function recovery, or well-posed linear operator equations.

The study of computational
problems on weighted RKHSs was initiated in
Sloan and Woźniakowski 1998
for

d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. For more results in the case of finitely
many variables we refer to the monographs
Novak and Woźniakowski 2008
;
Novak and Woźniakowski 2010
;
Novak and Woźniakowski 2012
.
The study of numerical problems on weighted RKHSs of functions with
an infinite number of variables was initiated in
Hickernell and Wang 2001
,

Hickernell et al. 2010
, and
Kuo et al. 2010
. More recent results can be
found in
Gnewuch et al. 2017a
, and, for closely related spaces, in

Gnewuch et al. 2019
;
Gnewuch et al. 2024a
;
Gnewuch et al. 2025
.

We are allowed to use (deterministic or randomized) linear
algorithms
A
, and the error criterion we consider is the
(deterministic or randomized) worst case error

\err\left(A,S,K\right):=\sup_{\|f\|_{K}\leq 1}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\|Sf-Af\|^{2}_{G}\right)\right)^{1/2},

where
\mathbb{E}
means
taking the expected value of the expression in parentheses.
In the case of deterministic algorithms this simplifies to

\err\left(A,S,K\right):=\sup_{\|f\|_{K}\leq 1}\|Sf-Af\|_{G}.

Assume that for some reason the norm and the underlying function
decomposition on
H(K)
induced by the given univariate
kernel
k
is not well-suited for the type of error
analysis one wants to perform, e.g., deriving upper error bounds for
specific algorithms or lower error bounds valid for whole classes
of algorithms. Therefore we choose a different

(B2)

reproducing kernel
l\neq 0
over the domain
D

that satisfies either

H(k)\subseteq H(1+l)

(29)

if we are interested in upper error bounds or

H(l)\subseteq H(1+k)

(30)

if we are interested in lower error bounds.

We may choose, e.g., an anchored or an ANOVA kernel
l
. For
certain weights
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}
we may obtain a RKHS

H(M^{{\boldsymbol{\rho}},l})
with a more favorable function space
decomposition, so that in this setting we may perform the type of
error analysis we are interested in.
We add that the stronger assumptions

H(k)\subseteq H(l)
or
H(l)\subseteq H(k)
are
typically not satisfied in the
applications to the computational problems we have
in mind.

The question is now:
How to choose the weights

{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
such that we can transfer our results on

H(M^{{\boldsymbol{\rho}},l})
back to
H(K)
?

This will be described in the next two subsections, where we
treat upper and lower error bounds separately.

5.1
Upper Error Bounds

Throughout the study of upper error bounds we assume
the following:
The assumptions (B1) and (B2) with inclusion
(
29
) are satisfied, and

C^{\uparrow}>0
is an upper bound on the
norm of the embedding of
H(k)
into
H(1+l)
.

Assuming that
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C^{\uparrow}}
,
we simply choose

{\boldsymbol{\rho}}:={\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}:=T^{\uparrow}_{d,C^{\uparrow}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},

and assuming that
{\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}\!,l}\neq\emptyset

L^{\uparrow}:=M^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow},l}.

For product, POD, and finite-order weights,
conditions for
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C}
to hold for any value of

are presented in Proposition
2
.

Accordingly, the assumptions (A1)–(A3) are satisfied with

m:=k,\ m^{\uparrow}:=l,\ C:=C^{\uparrow},\ {\boldsymbol{\eta}}:={\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\ {\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{\uparrow}:={\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}.

Due to Theorems

and

we have

{\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}\!,l}\subseteq{\mathfrak{Z}}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},k}
and

H(K)\sqsubseteq H(L^{\uparrow})
,
and the corresponding
(embedding or restriction) map

\mathfrak{i^{\uparrow}}\colon H(K)\to H(L^{\uparrow})

Assume that the continuous linear map

in the sense that it satisfies

For any linear algorithm
A^{\uparrow}
on
H(L^{\uparrow})
we get a
“restricted algorithm”

H(K)

.

Corollary 2
.

If
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,C^{\uparrow}}
and

, then we have

Consequently, any upper error bound for
S^{\uparrow}
and
A^{\uparrow}

on the unit ball in
H(L^{\uparrow})
also holds for
S
and
A
on
the unit ball in
H(K)
.

Remark 13
.

It is important to also observe
the decay of the transformed weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}
,
due to its relation to minimal errors for
computational problems and its appearance in error bounds.

For the case
d\in{\mathbb{N}}

prominent role in the

Novak and Woźniakowski 2008

Novak and Woźniakowski 2010
;

.
The inverse of the exponent of strong tractability is (essentially) the

Dick et al. 2013
,
and also to numerous theorems in
Dick and Pillichshammer 2010
,

Dick et al. 2022

;
Novak and Woźniakowski 2010

Novak and Woźniakowski 2012
.

In the case

best possible convergence rates of the minimal errors depend

For corresponding results we refer to, e.g.,

Hickernell et al. 2010
,
Kuo et al. 2010
,
Gnewuch 2012
,

Plaskota and Wasilkowski 2011
, and
Gnewuch et al. 2025
.

This explains the relevance of

, and Proposition

Appendix A
Complete Monotonicity

In Section
A.1
we show that complete monotonicity of
weights, as introduced in Definition
1
, is a
particular instance of a general notion that applies to set functions.
In Section
A.2
we study the case
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and relate complete
monotonicity of weights to the corresponding notion for functions
on the unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
.

A.1
Completely Monotone Set Functions

Complete monotonicity of non-negative functions is defined and
studied in
Kurtz 1974
in the following setting.
Let
{\mathfrak{B}}
denote the Borel
\sigma
-algebra in a Polish
space
X
that is locally compact and
\sigma
-compact. For

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\colon{\mathfrak{B}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
u,v\in{\mathfrak{B}}
let

\left(\Delta^{\prime}_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}:=\gamma_{v\cup u}-\gamma_{u}.

The function
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

is called completely monotone if
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\geq{\boldsymbol{0}}
and

(-1)^{n}\left(\Delta^{\prime}_{v_{n}}\dots\Delta^{\prime}_{v_{1}}\right){\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\geq{\boldsymbol{0}}

for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and all (non-empty)

v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in{\mathfrak{B}}
;
the set of all completely monotone functions
on
{\mathfrak{B}}
will be denoted by
\mathcal{M}({\mathfrak{B}})
.

In the present paper we consider
functions
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\colon\mathcal{U}_{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
Here the assumptions from
Kurtz 1974
are met
if
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, since
{\mathfrak{B}}=\mathcal{U}_{d}
if
X:=[d]
with
d\in{\mathbb{N}}

is equipped with the discrete metric, but not
for
d=\infty
. However, for merely the definition of complete
monotonicity from
Kurtz 1974
to make sense it suffices to consider
any set
X
and any collection
{\mathfrak{B}}
of subsets of
X
that is closed
with respect to finite unions. Obviously, the latter holds
true also for
\mathcal{U}_{d}
with
d=\infty
.
For functions
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\colon\mathcal{U}_{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with

d\in{\mathbb{N}}
complete monotonicity is also studied in

Matúš 1994
.

Lemma 21
.

For
d\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
let
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\colon\mathcal{U}_{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and

v:=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}
with
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and pairwise
different
s_{i}\in[d]
.
For every
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
we have

\left(\Delta^{\prime}_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\left(\Delta_{\{s_{\ell}\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u\cup\{s_{1},\dots,s_{\ell-1}\}}

(33)

and

\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=(-1)^{n}\left(\Delta^{\prime}_{\{s_{n}\}}\dots\Delta^{\prime}_{\{s_{1}\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}.

(34)

Proof.

For the proof of (
33
)
we note that

\left(\Delta^{\prime}_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\left(\gamma_{u\cup\{s_{1},\dots,s_{\ell}\}}-\gamma_{u\cup\{s_{1},\dots,s_{\ell-1}\}}\right)=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\left(\Delta_{\{s_{\ell}\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u\cup\{s_{1},\dots,s_{\ell-1}\}}.

For the proof of (
34
) we note that

\left(\Delta_{\{s_{1}\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=\gamma_{u}-\gamma_{u\cup\{s_{1}\}}=-\left(\Delta^{\prime}_{\{s_{1}\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}.

Inductively, using Lemma
2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\Delta_{\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n+1}\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\left(\Delta_{\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}-\left(\Delta_{\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u\cup\{s_{n+1}\}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=(-1)^{n}\cdot\left(\left(\Delta^{\prime}_{\{s_{n}\}}\dots\Delta^{\prime}_{\{s_{1}\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}-\left(\Delta^{\prime}_{\{s_{n}\}}\dots\Delta^{\prime}_{\{s_{1}\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u\cup\{s_{n+1}\}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=(-1)^{n+1}\left(\Delta^{\prime}_{\{s_{n+1}\}}\dots\Delta^{\prime}_{\{s_{1}\}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u},

which completes the proof of (
34
).
∎

The next lemma shows that
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{U}_{d})
and
\mathcal{M}_{d}
,
which has been introduced in Definition
1
,
coincide.

Lemma 22
.

For every
d\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
we have
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{U}_{d})=\mathcal{M}_{d}
.

Proof.

The inclusion
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{U}_{d})\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{d}
follows immediately from
(
34
).

To establish the reverse inclusion we note that

\left(\Delta_{w}\Delta^{\prime}_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}=\sum_{\widetilde{w}\subseteq w}(-1)^{|\widetilde{w}|}\left(\gamma_{v\cup u\cup\widetilde{w}}-\gamma_{u\cup\widetilde{w}}\right)=\left(\Delta_{w}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{v\cup u}-\left(\Delta_{w}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}

for every
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\colon\mathcal{U}_{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and all
u,v,w\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
. Lemma
5
yields

\Delta_{w}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
, and therefore

\Delta_{w}\Delta^{\prime}_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\leq{\boldsymbol{0}}
, see
Lemma
4
, which is equivalent to

-\Delta^{\prime}_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
. Inductively, we obtain

(-1)^{n}\left(\Delta^{\prime}_{v_{n}}\dots\Delta^{\prime}_{v_{1}}\right){\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}

for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and all
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
.
Since
\mathcal{M}_{d}\subseteq\mathcal{W}_{d}
, we conclude that

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{U}_{d})
.
∎

A.2
Completely Monotone Functions on
[0,1]^{d}

Throughout this section we have
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
At first, we consider real-valued
functions on the continuous unit cube

[0,1]^{d}
. In this context we will use the following
notation with
w\subseteq[d]
for the definition of complete
monotonicity.
For a non-empty set
w
and a point
z\in[0,1]^{d}

we write
z_{w}
to denote the projection
of
z
onto those components with indices in
w
;
we define
z_{w}
to be void if
w=\emptyset
.
Furthermore, we put
-w:=[d]\setminus w
.
For two points
z,t\in[0,1]^{d}
we write
z_{-w}\!:\!t_{w}

to denote the point where
those components of
z
with indices in
w
are replaced by the
corresponding components of
t
.
Finally,
\psi(z):=(1-z_{1},\dots,1-z_{d})
for
z\in[0,1]^{d}
.

A function
f\colon[0,1]^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}

is called completely monotonically increasing
if

\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|v|-|w|}f(x_{-w}\!:\!y_{w})\geq 0

holds for all non-empty
v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
and

x,y\in[0,1]^{d}
such that
x_{j}\leq y_{j}
for every
j\in v
.
See, e.g.,
Gnewuch et al. 2024b
. Moreover,

f
will be called completely monotonically decreasing if

f\circ\psi
is completely monotonically increasing.

Lemma 23
.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. A function
f\colon[0,1]^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
completely monotonically decreasing if and only if

\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|w|}f(x_{-w}\!:\!y_{w})\geq 0

for all non-empty
v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
and

x,y\in[0,1]^{d}
such that
x_{j}\leq y_{j}
for every
j\in v
.

Proof.

Let
v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
be non-empty and
x,y\in[0,1]^{d}
.
For
\widetilde{x}:=\psi(x)
,
\widetilde{y}:=\psi(y)
,
and
\widehat{y}:=(\widetilde{x}_{-v}\!:\!\widetilde{y}_{v})

we obtain

(\widetilde{x}_{-(v\setminus w)}\!:\!\widetilde{y}_{v\setminus w})=(\widehat{y}_{-w}\!:\!\widetilde{x}_{w})

for every
w\subseteq v
. Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|v|-|w|}f(\widetilde{x}_{-w}\!:\!\widetilde{y}_{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|w|}f(\widetilde{x}_{-(v\setminus w)}\!:\!\widetilde{y}_{v\setminus w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|w|}f(\widehat{y}_{-w}\!:\!\widetilde{x}_{w}).

For
j\in v
we have equivalence of

x_{j}\leq y_{j}
and
\widehat{y}_{j}\leq\widetilde{x}_{j}
.
∎

Note that the concept of complete monotonicity
is closely linked to the concept of
the variation of a function in the
sense of Vitali and in the sense of Hardy and Krause. Indeed,
these concepts involve sums of differences
of function values that can be shown to be all non-negative for
completely monotonically increasing functions, and all non-positive
for completely monotonically decreasing functions.
See
Aistleitner and Dick 2015
for details.

Of course, every
function
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\colon\mathcal{U}_{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}

may be identified in a canonical way with a
function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
More precisely, let
\varphi\colon\{0,1\}^{d}\to\mathcal{U}_{d}
be given by

\varphi(x):=\{j\in[d]:x_{j}=1\}.

We obtain a bijection
\Phi
between
the set of all real-valued functions on
\mathcal{U}_{d}

and the set of all real-valued functions on
\{0,1\}^{d}

by

(\Phi{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})(x):=\gamma_{\varphi(x)}

for
x\in\{0,1\}^{d}
and

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\colon\mathcal{U}_{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

The next lemma shows that

\mathcal{M}_{d}
coincides with the set of all restrictions of non-negative,
completely monotonically decreasing functions
to the Hamming cube
\{0,1\}^{d}
,
up to the identification via
\Phi
.

Lemma 24
.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
The mapping
\Phi
defines a bijection from
\mathcal{M}_{d}
onto

\mathcal{M}_{d}^{\prime}:=\{f|_{\{0,1\}^{d}}:\text{$f\colon[0,1]^{d}\to{[0,\infty)}$ completely monotonically
decreasing}\}.

Proof.

For
f\colon[0,1]^{d}\to{[0,\infty)}
let
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}
be
defined by

\Phi{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=f|_{\{0,1\}^{d}}.

(35)

Furthermore, let
u,v\subseteq[d]
with
u\cap v=\emptyset
and
let
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{d}
be defined by

\varphi(x)=u
and
\varphi(y)=v
.
Note that
u\cup w=\varphi(x_{-w}\!:\!y_{w})
for every
w\subseteq v
.
Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|w|}\gamma_{u\cup w}=\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|w|}\gamma_{\varphi(x_{-w}:y_{w})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|w|}f(x_{-w}\!:\!y_{w}).

(36)

Moreover,
x_{j}=0<1=y_{j}
for
j\in v
.
Due to Lemma
23
,
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}

if
f
is completely monotonically decreasing, i.e.,

\mathcal{M}_{d}^{\prime}\subseteq\Phi(\mathcal{M}_{d})
.

For the proof of the reverse inclusion we extend the mapping

\varphi
from
\{0,1\}^{d}
to
[0,1]^{d}
by

\varphi(x):=\{j\in[d]:x_{j}>0\}.

For
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
let
f\colon[0,1]^{d}\to{[0,\infty)}

be defined by

f(x):=\gamma_{\varphi(x)}

for every
x\in[0,1]^{d}
. Obviously, we have (
35
). Thus it
remains to show that
f
is completely monotonically decreasing.

For
x\in[0,1]^{d}
we put

\lceil x\rceil:=(\lceil x_{1}\rceil,\dots,\lceil x_{d}\rceil)\in\{0,1\}^{d}
. Clearly
\varphi(x)=\varphi(\lceil x\rceil)
,
and therefore
f(x)=f(\lceil x\rceil)
. It follows that
f
is
completely monotonically decreasing, if

\alpha:=\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|w|}f(x_{-w}\!:\!y_{w})\geq 0

for all non-empty
v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
and
x,y\in\{0,1\}^{d}

such that
x_{j}\leq y_{j}
for every
j\in v
, see Lemma
23
.
For
v,x,y
chosen in this way we put

v_{1}:=\{j\in v:x_{j}<y_{j}\}=\{j\in v:x_{j}=0,\ y_{j}=1\}

and
v_{2}:=v\setminus v_{1}
.
Let
w_{i}\subseteq v_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq 2
. We have

(x_{-(w_{1}\cup w_{2})}\!:\!y_{w_{1}\cup w_{2}})=(x_{-w_{1}}\!:\!y_{w_{1}}),

which implies

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{1}\subseteq v_{1}}\sum_{w_{2}\subseteq v_{2}}(-1)^{|w_{1}|+|w_{2}|}f(x_{-(w_{1}\cup w_{2})}\!:\!y_{w_{1}\cup w_{2}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{w_{1}\subseteq v_{1}}(-1)^{|w_{1}|}f(x_{-w_{1}}\!:\!y_{w_{1}})\sum_{w_{2}\subseteq v_{2}}(-1)^{|w_{2}|}.

Therefore
\alpha=0
if
v_{2}\neq\emptyset
, so that
it remains to consider the case
v_{2}=\emptyset
, i.e.,
v=v_{1}
.
Without loss of generality we may assume that
y_{j}=0
for

j\not\in v
, so that
v=\varphi(y)
. Let
u:=\varphi(x)
.
Since
u\cap v=\emptyset
, the first part of the proof is
applicable, and (
A.2
) together with
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
yields
\alpha\geq 0
. This concludes the proof of

\Phi(\mathcal{M}_{d})\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{d}^{\prime}
.
∎

Appendix B
Finite Measures, CDFs and Densities on
\mathcal{U}_{d}

The major findings from Theorems
1
and
2
may be
reformulated in terms of finite measures, densities (w.r.t. the
counting measure), and cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) as
follows.

Obviously, the finite measures

\mu
on the power set of
\mathcal{U}_{d}
may be identified with the
elements
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,1}
via

\gamma_{u}:=\mu(\{u\})
and
\mu(A):=\sum_{w\in A}\gamma_{w}

for
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
and
A\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{d}
.
That is,
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is the density of
\mu
(w.r.t. the counting measure).
For
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,1}

and
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}:=T^{\uparrow}_{d,1}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
we have

\gamma^{\uparrow}_{u}=\mu(\{w\in\mathcal{U}_{d}:u\subseteq w\})

by definition, so that
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}

may be considered as the CDF associated to
\mu
.
We have
T^{\downarrow}_{d,1}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}={\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.
If we interpret
T^{\downarrow}_{d,1}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}
as the derivative of
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}
,
then we may conclude that the
derivative of the CDF is the density of
\mu
,
a kind of result which is well-known in the continuous case.
Moreover,

\left(\Delta_{v}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}\right)_{u}=\mu(\{w\in\mathcal{U}_{d}:u\subseteq w\wedge v\cap w=\emptyset\}),

which follows by induction on
|v|
using Lemma
2
.
For
s,r\in{\mathbb{N}}

let

A(s,r):=\{w\in\mathcal{U}_{d}:[s]\setminus[r]\cap w=\emptyset\}.

Since

\bigcup_{r\in{\mathbb{N}}}\bigcap_{s\in{\mathbb{N}}}A(s,r)=\bigcup_{r\in{\mathbb{N}}}\{w\in\mathcal{U}_{d}:\max w\leq r\}=\mathcal{U}_{d},

the
\sigma
-continuity of
\mu
yields

\gamma^{\uparrow}_{u}=\mu\left(\bigcup_{r\in{\mathbb{N}}}\bigcap_{s\in{\mathbb{N}}}\{w\in A(s,r):u\subseteq w\}\right)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\lim_{s\to\infty}\left(\Delta_{[s]\setminus[r]}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\uparrow}\right)_{u}

for every
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
, which is the continuity property required
in the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{d}
,
cf. (
9
).

Conversely, let
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in T^{\uparrow}_{d,1}(\mathcal{S}_{d,1})
.
For
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\downarrow}:=T^{\downarrow}_{d,1}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d,1}
we have

T^{\uparrow}_{d,1}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\downarrow}={\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
,
so that
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is the CDF for the measure with density

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\downarrow}
.

Appendix C
Maximal Completely Monotone Weights

In this section we assume that

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{d}
. In this
case lower error bounds have been obtained in Section
5.2
by
means of weights of the form
T^{\downarrow}_{d,C^{\downarrow}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\ast}
with any choice of

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\ast}\in\Gamma=\{\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}:\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\leq{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\}
,
cf. Remark
14
.

Lemma 25
.

For every
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{d}
the set

\Gamma
contains a maximal element.

Proof.

Since the trivial weights
{\bf 0}
are
in
\Gamma
, the set is not empty.
Endowed with the relation
\leq
the set
\Gamma
is partially
ordered. Let
I
be some index set, and let

({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(i)})_{i\in I}
be a totally ordered family in
\Gamma
.
Then we may define
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\in\mathcal{W}_{d}
via

\widehat{\gamma}_{u}:=\sup\{\gamma^{(i)}_{u}:i\in I\}

for all
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
. Note that

0\leq\widehat{\gamma}_{u}\leq\gamma_{u}
for each
u\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
,
due to the definition of
\Gamma
. We now show that

\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
is completely monotone. Let
u,v\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
and

\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. Due to the definition of the supremum,
we find for each
w\subseteq v
some index
i_{w}\in I
such that

\widehat{\gamma}_{u\cup w}-\gamma^{(i_{w})}_{u\cup w}\leq\varepsilon/2^{|v|}.

Since
({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(i)})_{i\in I}
is totally ordered
and
\{i_{w}:w\subseteq v\}
is a finite subset of
I
, there
exists an index
j\in\{i_{w}:w\subseteq v\}

such that

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(i_{w})}\leq{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(j)}

for every
w\subseteq v
. In particular,

\gamma^{(i_{w})}_{u\cup w}\leq\gamma^{(j)}_{u\cup w}\leq\widehat{\gamma}_{u\cup w}

for all
w\subseteq v
. Therefore we have

\begin{split}\left(\Delta_{v}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\right)_{u}&=\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|w|}\widehat{\gamma}_{u\cup w}=\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|w|}\left(\gamma^{(j)}_{u\cup w}+\left(\widehat{\gamma}_{u\cup w}-\gamma^{(j)}_{u\cup w}\right)\right)\\
&\geq\sum_{w\subseteq v}(-1)^{|w|}\gamma^{(j)}_{u\cup w}-\sum_{w\subseteq v}\left(\widehat{\gamma}_{u\cup w}-\gamma^{(j)}_{u\cup w}\right)\geq\left(\Delta_{v}\gamma^{(j)}\right)_{u}-\varepsilon\\
&\geq-\varepsilon,\end{split}

where in the last inequality we used that
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(j)}
is completely
monotone.
Letting
\varepsilon
tend to zero, we see that

\left(\Delta_{v}\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\right)_{u}\geq 0
. Thus

\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
is completely monotone and therefore an
upper bound of

({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(i)})_{i\in I}
in
\Gamma
. Due to Zorn’s lemma the
set
\Gamma
has a maximal element
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\ast}
.
∎

Considering Lemma
25
, it is a natural question whether

\Gamma
contains a greatest element.
As we shall see, this is in general not the case.

Example 1
.

Let
d:=2
and
C:=1
. We consider the sum operator

T:=T_{2,1}^{\uparrow}
as a linear mapping on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{U}_{2}}
, i.e.,

\left(T{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right)_{u}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{U}_{2}\\
u\subseteq v\end{subarray}}\eta_{v}

for
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{U}_{2}}
.
Obviously
T
is a bijection, and due to Theorem
1
we
have
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
if and only if
T^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\geq 0
.

As a counter-example,
we consider the weights
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathcal{W}_{2}
with

\gamma_{\emptyset}=5,\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma_{\{1\}}=5,\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma_{\{2\}}=3,\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma_{\{1,2\}}=1.

It is easily checked that

\left(T^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{\emptyset}=-2,\hskip 10.00002pt\left(T^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{\{1\}}=4,\hskip 10.00002pt\left(T^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{\{2\}}=2,\hskip 10.00002pt\left(T^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)_{\{1,2\}}=1,

and therefore
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\not\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
.

Now consider
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(\varepsilon)}\in\mathcal{W}_{2}
given by

\eta_{\emptyset}^{(\varepsilon)}=5,\hskip 10.00002pt\eta_{\{1\}}^{(\varepsilon)}=5,\hskip 10.00002pt\eta_{\{2\}}^{(\varepsilon)}=1+\varepsilon,\hskip 10.00002pt\eta_{\{1,2\}}^{(\varepsilon)}=1,

for
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Observe that

{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(0)}\leq\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\leq{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is equivalent to

\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}={\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(\varepsilon)}
with
0\leq\varepsilon\leq 2
.
It is easily checked that

\displaystyle\left(T^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)_{\emptyset}=-\varepsilon,\hskip 10.00002pt\left(T^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)_{\{1\}}=4,

\displaystyle\left(T^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)_{\{2\}}=\varepsilon,\hskip 10.00002pt\left(T^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)_{\{1,2\}}=1,

and therefore
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(\varepsilon)}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
if and only if
\varepsilon=0
.
We conclude that
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(0)}
is a maximal element of
\Gamma
.

In the next step, consider

{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{(\varepsilon)}\in\mathcal{W}_{2}
given by

\zeta_{\emptyset}^{(\varepsilon)}=5,\hskip 10.00002pt\zeta_{\{1\}}^{(\varepsilon)}=3+\varepsilon,\hskip 10.00002pt\zeta_{\{2\}}^{(\varepsilon)}=3,\hskip 10.00002pt\zeta_{\{1,2\}}^{(\varepsilon)}=1,

for
\varepsilon\geq 0
.
It is easily checked that

\displaystyle\left(T^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)_{\emptyset}=-\varepsilon,\hskip 10.00002pt\left(T^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)_{\{1\}}=2+\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\left(T^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)_{\{2\}}=2,\hskip 10.00002pt\left(T^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{(\varepsilon)}\right)_{\{1,2\}}=1.

As before, we conclude that
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{(0)}
is a maximal element
of
\Gamma
. Since
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(0)}\neq{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{(0)}
, the set
\Gamma

does not contain a greatest element.
