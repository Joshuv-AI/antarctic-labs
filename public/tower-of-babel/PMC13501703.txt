A noncanonical neuroligin 3-centered complex promotes functional recovery of spinal cord injury
Authors: Weiwei Zou, Musheng Chen, Nan Chen, Huan Chen, Hanlin Liang, Xiaoyun Lu, Sisi Xu, Peilin Liu, Yuan Wang, Le Hu, Bin Liu, Limin Rong, Mangmang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hUC-MSCs) hold great potential for spinal cord injury (SCI) treatment, their intrinsic mechanisms are not fully understood. Given their multipotency, especially their neuronal transdifferentiation potential, we hypothesized that investigating the in situ transcriptional reprogramming of transplanted hUC-MSCs within the spinal cord microenvironment (SCE) could shed light on crucial genes for SCI repair.MethodsDiD-labeled hUC-MSCs were intrathecally transplanted in rats with or without sub-acute spinal cord bilateral hemisection injury and subsequently retrieved for RNA-seq. Comparative analysis of the transcriptomes of hUC-MSCs and functional screenings in vitro and in vivo, including heterologous synapse formation assay, transplantation of MSCs with gene overexpression or knockdown, AAV-mediated neuron-specific gene expression in SCI rats, behavioral tests, and motor evoked potentials (MEPs) were performed to identify the novel target gene Neuroligin 3 (Nlgn3). Immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry (IP-Mass spec), cell aggregation assay, and immuno-electron microscopy were used to reveal the functional interacting partners of Nlgn3. Moreover, RT-qPCR, western blotting, immunofluorescence staining, and co-IP were used to elucidate the underlying mechanism.ResultsUsing RNA-seq and functional screening, we identified NLGN3 as a neuronal cell adhesion molecule (CAM) activated by the SCE in transplanted hUC-MSCs to promote therapeutic efficacy. Critically, the neuron-specific restoration of Nlgn3 in the injured spinal cord alone was sufficient to achieve a comparable therapeutic effect. Mechanistically, Nlgn3 recruits the synaptic vesicle proteins Sar1a and Hspa8 to modulate synaptic strength. The combinatorial restoration of Nlgn3 with either Sar1a or Hspa8 synergistically enhanced SCI repair, highlighting the functional importance of this noncanonical Nlgn3-Sar1a-Hspa8 axis.ConclusionsThis work unveils a novel therapeutic role for Nlgn3 in SCI treatment, demonstrating its ability to both enhance MSC transplantation efficacy and directly promote neural circuit reconstruction. We also propose a combinatorial strategy of targeting the noncanonical Nlgn3-centered protein complex. Furthermore, our study provides a valuable framework for uncovering novel therapeutic targets by investigating the transcriptional reprogramming of transplanted MSCs within injury microenvironments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-05100-7.

Background

Spinal cord injury (SCI) typically results in permanent damage to the sensorimotor function due to the very limited regeneration potential of neuronal networks in adult mammals [1, 2]. The functional recovery of SCI primarily relies on the appropriate reconstruction of synaptic communications between cortical projection and propriospinal neurons. A synapse is an intercellular complex consisting of apposed pre- and postsynaptic terminals, with a synaptic cleft between them, mediating the transmission of information between two neurons or between a neuron and its target cell [3, 4]. Synaptic cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) play pivotal roles in neuronal communication. They not only bridge adjacent pre- and postsynaptic specializations through homophilic or heterophilic interactions but also mediate trans-synaptic signaling across the synaptic cleft [5–7]. Despite the extensive description of various trans-synaptic CAMs during development, the key players that facilitate the reestablishment of neuronal circuits after injury are not comprehensively understood.

Over the last few decades, a substantial body of evidence has highlighted the considerable potential of stem cell-based regenerative medicine in treating injuries and disorders of the central nervous system (CNS) [8, 9]. Human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hUC-MSCs) are particularly promising for SCI treatment due to their easy accessibility, rapid self-renewal, low immunogenicity, and reduced tumorigenesis risk [10, 11]. The current understanding of MSCs' therapeutic effects underscores their immunomodulatory and paracrine activities, which contribute to neuroprotection, neurogenesis, and angiogenesis. While in vitro studies confirm MSCs' potential for neuronal transdifferentiation under specific conditions, its occurrence within the in vivo spinal cord microenvironment (SCE) and its contribution to functional recovery remain debated [12–15]. This leads us to critical questions: Can the SCE induce neuronal reprogramming of transplanted MSCs? If so, do these activated neuron-associated genes possess neurorestorative functions? And can even expressing these key genes in injured neurons also promote neural circuit reconstruction? To answer these questions, we performed RNA-seq on hUC-MSCs pre- and post-transplantation in a rat model with or without sub-acute SCI (Fig. 1A). Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments in this study were conducted in accordance with the experimental plan approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Southern Medical University (SMUL2018065). The use of hUC-MSCs was approved by the Stem Cell Clinical Trials Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (No. 201908). All procedures using primary human cells were under the supervision of Cell-gene Therapy Translational Medicine Research Center in the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University.

Animals

Adult Sprague–Dawley rats weighing 220–250 g were purchased from the Lab Animal Center of Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China). All rats were maintained in the Lab Animal Center of Southern Medicine University under specific pathogen free conditions. Rats were randomly allocated to their respective experimental groups in a completely randomized block design. Random numbers were generated using the standard = RAND() function in Microsoft Excel. Each rat was considered to be an experimental unit. Animals handlers were blinded to group allocation. Euthanasia was achieved by an intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital at a dose of 150 mg/kg.

Rat models of spinal cord bilateral hemisection injury

Adult rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital (30 mg/kg). The T9 and T11 bilateral hemisection was performed as previously described [15]. In brief, following a laminectomy at the T9-11 level, we performed a left-side over-hemisection at T9, followed by a right hemisection at T11 using a scalpel. After suturing the muscle and skin, the rats were kept on a heated blanket until they regained consciousness. Bladder emptying was conducted twice daily, and urinary infections, if present, were treated with a 7-day course of antibiotics (cefazolin sodium, 10 mg/kg). Rats were included in the statistical analysis only if they had undergone successful modeling and had survived the entire experimental period. For hUC‑MSC transplantation, cyclosporin A (10 mg/kg; Aladdin, Shanghai, China) was administered subcutaneously once per day until the end of the experiment to minimize immune responses.

Basso, beattie, and bresnahan (BBB) scoring

Hindlimb locomotor function was assessed weekly based on the BBB open-field rating scale [16]. All BBB behavior tests were conducted by investigators who were blinded to the treatment groups. In this study, the final BBB score for each treatment group was calculated as the mean value of twelve total scores, derived from three biological replicates with four animals each.

Electrophysiological measurements

Motor evoked potential (MEP) recording was performed at the end of each experiment as previously described. In brief, the motor cortex was exposed in anesthetized rats immobilized on a stereotactic device. Stimulation electrodes were precisely positioned in the representative posterior limb area of the right motor cortex, while recording electrodes were inserted into the gastrocnemius muscle of the contralateral posterior limb. Both amplitude (mV) and latency (ms) were obtained using the BL-420 A/F data acquisition and analysis system (TAIMENG SOFTWARE, Chengdu, China). The parameters were configured for a square wave stimulation waveform, fine voltage mode, 1 ms wave width, 50 ms delay, 10 Hz frequency, and 10 V intensity. In this study, the mean value for each treatment group was calculated from six rats (n = 2/group/replicate).

hUC-MSC DiD labeling, transplantation, and retrieval

hUC-MSCs were labeled with 1,1-Dioctadecyl-3,3,3,3-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine (DiD) (AAT Bioquest, #22033) as previously described [17] Briefly, 1 × 106 cells/ml were incubated with 5 μM of DiD in HBSS for 20 min at 37 °C, washed twice with growth medium, and resuspended in 40ul of PBS for intrathecal injection using a micro-injection pump (R404, RWD, PRC). Rats with (SCI) or without (sham) spinal cord bilateral hemisection injury were intrathecally implanted with DiD-labeled hUC-MSCs (DiD-hUC-MSCs) at 2 weeks post injury (wpi). At day one and day three post-transplantation (dpt), rats were sacrificed, and their spinal cords were dissected to isolate the DiD-labeled cells. In parallel, DiD-hUC-MSCs were passaged 8 times in vitro, and their self-renewal capacity and persistence of DiD labeling were evaluated using growth curves and flow cytometry.

RNA extraction, RNA-Seq and data analysis

Total RNAs were extracted from DiD-hUC-MSCs retrieved from rat spinal cords with (SCI-1d and SCI-3d) or without SCI (sham-1d and sham-3d) at two different time points. RNA samples were sent to Novogene for library construction and high-throughput sequencing. We sequenced a total of 20 DiD-hUC-MSC libraries from five different groups, including three biological replicates for DiD-hUC-MSCs before implantation (Ctr), four replicates each for the sham-1d, sham-3d and SCI-1d groups, and five replicates for the SCI-3d group. Trimmomatic (v0.39) [18] was used to eliminate adapter sequences, poly-N sequences, and low-quality reads (quality scores below 30). The clean reads were initially mapped to the rat reference genome (Rnor_6.0) to remove potential contamination of reads from rat spinal cord. The filtered high-quality reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using the STAR aligner (v2.7.10) [19]with default parameters. Samtools (v1.16.1) [20] was used to retain properly paired reads using the parameter “-f 4”. Subsequently, gene expression levels were quantified via featureCounts (v2.0.3) [21] using annotated gene models to generate raw read count data for downstream differential expression analysis. All the RNA-Seq data was deposited into the GEO database with a super-series number GSE252197.

Reverse transcription (RT) and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells or spinal tissue using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo fisher, 15,596,018). 1 μg of total RNA was used for RT using the reverse transcription system (Thermo Fisher, 4,311,235). RT reactions were diluted 10 times and 2.5 ul was used as a template for each Real-time PCR reaction. PCR reactions were performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Yeasen, 11203ES08) and analyzed on the StepOnePlus™ system (Applied Biosystems). PCR primers were listed in Table S1.

cDNA cloning and plasmid construction

Total RNA from rat brains was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Scientific, 15,596,018). RT was carried out using the HiScript® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, R312-02). Rat cDNAs for Slitrk3, Crispld2, Nlgn3, Lrfn5, Lrrtm1, Lrrtm3, Lingo2, Epha7, and Sema4a were PCR amplified using gene-specific primers and gel purified by the GeneJET PCR Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, K0702). cDNAs were then cloned into either the lentiviral expression vector pLVX-IRES-puro (632,183, Clontech) or the AAV vector pAAV-hsynI by homologous recombination using the ClonExpress® Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, C115-02).

To construct lentiviral shRNA vectors targeting Nlgn3, oligonucleotides specifically targeting rat Nlgn3 mRNA at two different sites were annealed, and subcloned into the AgeI and EcoRI sites of pLKO.1 vector (Addgene, #8453), respectively. shRNAs targeting Luciferase served as the negative control. Primer sequences and plasmids were listed in Supplementary Table S1 and S2, respectively.

Virus generation

For lentivirus production, the lentiviral expression vector was co-transfected with the packaging vectors, pCMV-deltaR8 (Addgene, #12263) and pCMV-VsVg (Addgene, #8454) into LentiX-293 T cells using PEI (Biohub, 78BioPEI25000-100 mg). The culture medium containing the viruses was collected twice, at 48 h and 72 h post-transfection, and then subjected to ultracentrifugation for virus concentration. Cells transduced with a specific amount of lentivirus were selected using 2 μg/ml puromycin (Sigma) for 2 days. The efficiency of knockdown and overexpression was evaluated using real-time PCR and western blotting.

The adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) were generated according to previously described method. Briefly, pAAV, pUCmini-iCAP-PHP.eB (Addgene, #103005), and pHelper (ZoomaBio, ZK736) were co-transfected into Lenti-X 293 T cells at a ratio of 1:4:2. The medium containing AAV was collected at 72 h and 120 h post-transfection. AAV particles were released from 293 T cells by digestion with Salt-active nuclease (SAN; ArcticZymes, 70910-202) and precipitated, along with particles in the medium, using PEG8000 (Sigma, 89510-1 KG-F). AAV particles were further purified by ultracentrifugation in the iodixanol density gradient at 350,000 g for 2 h at 18 °C, and concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter devices (Millipore, UFC910024). Viral titer was quantified using real-time PCR. The purified AAVs were adjusted to a concentration of 1.25 × 1014 vg/mL, and 5 × 1012 vg of AAV particles in 40-ul DPBS were intrathecally administered to rats one week before SCI. IF staining and W.B. of spinal sections were used to evaluate AAV-mediated gene expression at least two weeks after injection.

Heterologous synapse formation assay

Heterologous synapse formation assay was performed as described by Biederer et al. [22]. Briefly, 1 × 104 hUC-MSCs overexpressing Flag-tagged candidate genes were co-cultured with 1.6 × 105 cortical neurons at 6 DIV in 24-well plates. After 24 h, the cultures were fixed and immunostained with mouse anti-Flag antibody alongside rabbit antibodies against various synaptic markers, followed by fluorescent secondary antibodies. Images were acquired using an Olympus Fluoview FV1000 inverted fluorescence microscope. The percentage of MSCs overexpressing candidate genes overlapping with SynI signals was quantified using Fiji imaging analysis software (http://fiji.sc/Fiji).

Western blotting (W.B.)

Briefly, cells or spinal tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 5 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, 1% sodium deoxycholate), freshly supplemented with 1 mM PMSF, 5 mg/mL leupeptin (Roche, 11,034,626,001), 5 mg/mL pepstatin (Roche, 11,524,488,001), and 10 mg/mL aprotinin (Roche, 11,583,794,001). Solubilized proteins were obtained by centrifugation at 13,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, 23,227). Equal amounts of total proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE and electrically transferred onto nitrocellulose (NC) blotting membranes (GE, 1,060,001). The membrane was then blocked in PBST (1 × PBS with 0.05% Tween 20) containing 5% milk, followed by incubation with primary antibodies, and subsequently with the horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-coupled secondary antibody. Signals were developed using Western Lightning Plus ECL (Perkinelmer, NEL105001EA). The relative expression levels of proteins were quantified using ImageJ (Version 1.8.0). Antibodies used in this study were listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining and confocal microscopy

Cells fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were permeabilized in PBST (PBS with 3% BSA and 0.2% Triton X-100) for 15 min and blocked in PBST with 10% BSA for 2 h. Subsequently, the cells were stained with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 2 h in the dark at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min at room temperature. Microscopic images were acquired using the Olympus FV1000 confocal microscope. Antibodies used in this study were listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Protein immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry (mass-spec)

After co-culturing MSCs with cortical neurons at either 6 DIV or 28 DIV, the mixed culture was lysed using RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. 1 mg of soluble proteins were incubated either with 2 μg of rabbit anti-Nlgn3 antibody (Abcam, ab259348) or with normal rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, 2729S) for 16 h with rotation at 4 °C. The following day, 20 μl of Protein G Sepharose (GE, 17–0618-01) was added to the protein-antibody mixture and incubated for 2 h. The immunoprecipitated proteins conjugated to Sepharose were washed five times with RIPA buffer and eluted by boiling in SDS sample buffer containing 5% β-mercaptoethanol for 10 min. Proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE and silver-stained using Pierce™ Silver Stain for Mass Spectrometry kit (Thermo, 24,600). The entire lane of proteins on the gel was sent to Applied Protein Technology for LC–MS/MS analysis. The raw mass spectrometry data of sequenced peptides were mapped to the corresponding database using the Proteome Discoverer 2.5 to identify proteins. Proteins pulled down by IgG were regarded as background and filtered out from those captured by the Nlgn3 antibody.

Immunogold-electron microscopy (immunogold-EM)

In brief, the rat spinal cord sections were first fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and then with 0.5% OsO4. After fixation, sections were embedded in epoxy resin and cut into slices with a thickness of 60 nm. The slices were incubated with monoclonal first antibodies, followed by nanogold-conjugated secondary antibodies. Images of synapses were captured using a transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL 1400plus).

Cortical axon regeneration assay (scratch assay)

Primary cultures of rat cortical neurons and scratch assay were performed as previously described [17]. Cerebral cortices were dissected from brains of rat fetuses at E18 and minced into small pieces (1–2 mm) using sterile scissors and forceps. The chunks of cortex were enzymatically dissociated in dissection medium (DMEM supplemented with 4.2 mg/mL papain (KEH, 9001-73-4) and 20 μg/mL DNase I (Roche, 4,716,728,001) for 1 h and then gently triturated into a single-cell suspension. 4.8 × 105 neurons were plated onto each well of a 24-well culture dish pre-coated with 10 μg/mL poly-D-lysine (Sigma, P6407-5MG) in culture medium (neurobasal medium (Gibco, 21103-049) supplemented with 1 × B27 (Thermo, 17,504,044), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 11,360,070), 0.5 mM glutamine (Gibco, 35,050,061), and 10 μg/mL gentamicin (Sigma, G1264)). At 7 DIV, neurons were transduced with lentivirus either overexpressing EGFP or Nlgn3, or carrying shRNAs targeting the mRNA of Luciferase or Nlgn3. At 10 DIV, a scratch injury was created using the tip of a 10-μl pipette tip. Axon regeneration induced by 100 ng/ml of recombinant human BDNF (rhBDNF; Genscript, Z03208) was used as the positive control. At 17 DIV, cells were fixed and stained with anti-βIII-tubulin or anti-MAP2 antibodies, followed by fluorescent secondary antibodies. Images of stained neurites were acquired in the center of each scratch on an Olympus Fluoview FV1000 inverted fluorescence microscope and subsequently thresholded in ImageJ.

Cell aggregation assay

Lenti-X 293 T cells, cultured in 35 mm dishes, were individually transfected with the expression plasmids pLVX-IRES-Flag-Nlgn3-puro, pLVX-IRES-HA-Hspa8-puro, and pLVX-IRES-Myc-Sar1a-puro. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were split and resuspended at a final density of 5 × 106 cells/ml. Subsequently, cells overexpressing each of the three proteins were mixed in pairwise combinations in a total 0.3 ml of HBSS and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min with rotation. Otsu’s method was applied to threshold the images, and the Pearson’s coefficient was subsequently calculated. Antibodies are detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Sample sizes for in vivo experiments (e.g., n = 12 rats/group/time point for BBB scoring and n = 6 rats/group for MEP recordings and major histological quantifications) were based on our previous work. In vitro sample sizes were set to include at least three independent biological replicates per condition, each with two technical replicates. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Pairwise comparisons between two groups were analyzed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. One-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test for selected pairs of columns that correspond to our specific biological hypotheses was performed to determine statistical significance among multiple groups. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001; n.s., not significant. All data were analyzed using SPSS (version 22.0, IBM Corp.) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.3.0, GraphPad Software).

The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Results

Synaptic CAMs are highly induced in SCE-reprogrammed hUC-MSCs

In this study, we used a rat model with bilateral hemisections at T9 and T11 [15] to facilitate unambiguous assessment of neuronal circuit reconstruction at lesion sites (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A). To retrieve hUC-MSCs after intrathecal transplantation, we labeled hUC-MSCs in vitro with the lipophilic fluorescent dye 1,1-Dioctadecyl-3,3,3,3-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine (DiD). The self-renewal capacity and persistence of DiD labeling in DiD-labeled hUC-MSCs (DiD-hUC-MSCs) were confirmed by growth curves (Additional file 1: Fig. S1B) and flow cytometry (Additional file 1: Fig. S1C), respectively. The successful generation of the rat model of SCI and the therapeutic effects of DiD-hUC-MSC transplantation at the subacute phase of SCI (sub-SCI) were validated by western blotting (W.B.) (Additional file 1: Fig. S1D), immunofluorescence (IF) staining (Additional file 1: Fig. S1E), Basso, Beattie, and Bresnahan (BBB) scoring (Additional file 1: Fig. S1F), and motor evoked potentials (MEPs) recording of hindlimbs (Additional file 1: Figs. S1G and S1H).

To identify candidate genes in transplanted hUC-MSCs that promote functional recovery of SCI, we performed RNA-seq on DiD-hUC-MSCs retrieved from rats with or without SCI at one and three days post-transplantation (dpt) (Fig. 1A and Additional file 1: Fig. S2A). The purity of retrieved DiD-labeled human cells was verified before RNA-seq by assessing mRNA levels of the human marker Xrcc5 and the stem cell marker CD44 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2B). Additionally, to exclude potential sequence contamination from the rat spinal cord, the clean reads were initially mapped to the rat reference genome to remove non-human reads before alignment to the human genome. Comparative transcriptome analysis of DiD-hUC-MSCs before (Ctr) and after transplantation into the rat spinal cord without SCI (Sham-1d and Sham-3d) or with SCI (SCI-1d and SCI-3d) was conducted to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (|log2FC|> 1, adjusted p < 0.05) using pairwise comparisons: Sham vs. Ctr, SCI vs. Ctr, and SCI vs. Sham at two different time points (Fig. 1A). Surprisingly, the difference between Sham and SCI at the same time point was less remarkable than that between different time points, as revealed by the unbiased hierarchical clustering of DEGs (Fig. 1B) and principal component analysis (PCA) (Additional file 1: Fig. S2C). This indicates that general factors in the spinal cord at two weeks post-injury (wpi) primarily influence the transcriptional program of implanted hUC-MSCs, whereas the specific contribution of SCI‑induced inflammation appears comparatively modest. As a result, 7034 genes in hUC-MSCs were regulated by the SCE, with 3459 (49.2%) being activated and 3575 (50.8%) repressed (Fig. 1B). Intriguingly, GO analysis revealed that SCE-activated genes were highly enriched in cell–cell adhesion via plasma-membrane adhesion molecules, synapse assembly, regulation of neuron projection development, and neuron maturation (Fig. 1C), suggesting neuronal differentiation of hUC-MSCs within the SCE. In contrast, SCE-repressed genes were associated with cell proliferation, consistent with the diminished self-renewal capacity of differentiated MSCs. Given the importance of synaptic CAMs and axon guidance cues in neural circuit generation [6, 7, 23, 24], we selected nine SCE-activated genes, namely Slitrk3, Crispld2, Nlgn3, Lrfn5, Lrrtm1, Lrrtm3, Lingo2, Epha7, and Sema4a for further functional investigation. As shown in Fig. 1D, these nine CAMs exhibited minimal expression in pre-transplanted hUC-MSCs, whereas SCE, particularly following SCI, significantly upregulated their expression in post-transplanted hUC-MSCs. To further analyze the putative synapse-inducing activity of these genes, we performed mixed-culture assays with primary rat cortical neurons and hUC-MSCs overexpressing Flag-tagged candidate genes [22] (Additional file 1: Figs. S2D and S2E). Double IF staining with antibodies against the Flag-tag and the presynaptic vesicle marker synapsin I (Syn1) showed that Nlgn3 overexpression (OE) in MSCs significantly enhanced presynaptic differentiation (Fig. 1E and F). Furthermore, Nlgn3 was found to induce the development of both glutamatergic and GABAergic presynaptic terminals, as evidenced by the increased expression of specific markers for glutamatergic presynaptic terminals (vesicular glutamate transporter 1 (VGlut1) and 2 (VGlut2)), and GABAergic presynaptic specializations (glutamic acid decarboxylase 67 kDa (GAD67) and the vesicular GABA transporter (VGAT)) [25] (Additional file 1: Fig. S2F). Taken together, combining RNA-seq and the mixed culture assay, we screened for Nlgn3 as a SCE-activated synaptic CAM in transplanted hUC-MSCs which can promote synapse formation.

Genetically engineered MSCs with elevated Nlgn3 expression augment the therapeutic efficacy for SCI repair

Despite Nlgn3's role in synaptic signaling and non-syndromic autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [26, 27], its impact on SCI recovery remains unexplored. We first examined how SCI and hUC-MSC transplantation affected Nlgn3 expression in the rat spinal cord. The results showed that SCI significantly decreased the expression of Nlgn3 in spinal neurons, whereas hUC-MSC transplantation restored it (Additional file 1: Figs. S1D and S3). To further investigate Nlgn3's role in MSC-mediated SCI recovery, we utilized genetically modified rat bone marrow MSCs (rMSCs) that either overexpressed Flag-tagged Nlgn3 (rMSC-Nlgn3) or carried mixed short hairpin RNAs (shNlgn3#1 and #2) targeting Nlgn3 (rMSC-shNlgn3) (Fig. 2A and B). At 4 weeks post-transplantation (wpt), we first compared the protein levels of GFAP, MAP2 and MBP, widely used markers of astrocytic scarring, neuronal growth and remyelination, respectively. The SCI‑induced increase in GFAP and decreases in MAP2 and MBP were significantly reversed by rMSC transplantation, and this effect was modestly, but not significantly, enhanced by Nlgn3 (Fig. 2C). We further examined the differences in neurite regrowth among different treatment groups. In the PBS group, clear gaps between the two stumps of each cut remained, and minimal regrowth of nerve fibers was observed within the lesions, as evidenced by the very low intensity of βIII-tubulin-, serotonin (5-HT)-, and MAP2-positive signals filling in the gaps (Fig. 2D and E). In contrast, while rMSC-EGFP transplantation significantly increased neurite regrowth, this effect was further augmented by Nlgn3 OE but attenuated by Nlgn3 KD (Fig. 2E and F). Consistently, weekly behavioral tests revealed that Nlgn3 expression levels in rMSCs positively correlated with the extend of locomotor function restoration (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, MEP recordings demonstrated that Nlgn3 activation significantly increased the amplitude without affecting the latency, suggesting that Nlgn3 enhances motor recovery after SCI through strengthening synaptic transmission (Fig. 2H and I). Taken together, these results demonstrate that the SCE-induced Nlgn3 expression in transplanted MSCs contributes to MSC-mediated functional recovery following SCI. Neuron-specific Nlgn3 reactivation promotes functional recovery after SCI

Because SCI significantly reduced Nlgn3 expression in spinal neurons (Additional file 1: Fig. S3), we hypothesized that directly enhancing Nlgn3 expression in spared propriospinal neurons in vivo could promote SCI repair. To test this, we utilized AAV-mediated gene delivery to selectively express Flag-tagged Nlgn3 (AAV-Nlgn3-Flag) in rat spinal cord neurons, driven by the human synapsin 1 (Hsyn1) promoter (Fig. 3A) [17]. AAV-mRuby2 and MSC-Nlgn3 served as the negative and positive control, respectively. Both IF staining and W.B. showed that AAV-mediated OE effectively rescued the reduction of Nlgn3 in the injured spinal cord tissues, surpassing the levels of Nlgn3 observed in both intact tissue and with MSC transplantation (Fig. 3B and C). Moreover, neuron-specific OE of Nlgn3 significantly increased the refill of neurites into lesion sites at T9 and T11, including numerous serotonergic axons (Fig. 3C–E). Additionally, BBB scoring revealed a significant functional improvement in AAV-Nlgn3-Flag group as early as 1 wpi, even earlier than that of MSC-Nlgn3 transplantation (Fig. 3F). By the end of the experiment, AAV-Nlgn3-Flag and MSC-Nlgn3 treatments achieved a similar extent of locomotor recovery (Fig. 3F–H). Nlgn3 interacts with vesicle trafficking proteins Sar1a and Hspa8 at synapses

We next aimed to explore the detailed molecular mechanisms through which Nlgn3 facilitates the reconstruction of spinal circuits following SCI. To capture the putative trans-synaptic interactions during the establishment, differentiation or specification of synaptic contacts, we conducted Nlgn3 pulldown followed by mass spectrometry (MS) using detergent-solubilized lysates from cocultures of MSC-Nlgn3-Flag with rat cortical neurons at 10 DIV and 28 DIV, respectively. The Nlgn3 interactome was acquired by filtering out non-specific bindings co-immunoprecipitated with the negative IgG control (Fig. 5A). Unexpectedly, the Nlgn3 pull-down did not enrich any members of its canonical ligands, the Nrxns. Instead, we identified proteins involved in vesicle-mediated transport, synaptic transmission, and axonogenesis [29, 30] (Fig. 5B–D). These included synaptotagmin 1 (Syt1) [31, 32]; small guanosine triphosphatases (GTPases) of the Rab and ADP-ribosylation factor (Arf) family proteins involved in synaptic vesicle (SV) priming and recycling (Rab3a, Rab5c, Rab14, Arf1, Arf3, Arf4, and Arf5) [33]; the AP2 adaptor complex proteins involved in clathrin-mediated endocytosis (Ap2b1, Ap2m1) [34]; secretion associated Ras related GTPase 1A (Sar1a) [35]; and heat shock protein family A member 8 (Hspa8, also known as Hsc70) [36, 37] (Fig. 5D). This profile suggests the functional involvement of Nlgn3 in regulating synaptic transmission. From these hits, we focused on genes encoding proteins involved in vesicle-mediated transport, such as Sar1a, Hspa8, Hspa1b (Additional file 1: Fig. S4A), and ADP-ribosylation factor GTPase-activating protein 3 (Arfgap3) (Additional file 1: Fig. S4B). Notably, when overexpressed in HEK293T cells, both Myc-tagged Sar1a and HA-tagged Hspa8 consistently complexed with Flag-Nlgn3 or Syt1, as revealed by reciprocal immunoprecipitation followed by Western blotting (IP-WB) (Fig. 5E). To investigate whether Nlgn3 colocalizes with Sar1a and Hspa8 in neurons, we double immunostained primary rat cortical neurons at 28 DIV. We observed a significant degree of co-localization among endogenous Nlgn3, Sar1a, Hspa8, and Syt1 in soma, dendrites, and axons, presenting as overlapping punctate structures (Fig. 5F). Remarkably, Nlgn3 exhibited a higher extent of colocalization with Sar1a than with Hspa8. Additionally, we assessed the distribution of Sar1a and Hspa8 in SCI rats without and with MSC-Nlgn3 transplantation. The results showed that Sar1a and Hspa8, which partially colocalized with spinal neurons, were reduced by SCI but rescued by MSC-Nlgn3 transplantation (Additional file 1: Figs. S4C and S4D).

Because both Sar1a and Hspa8 are functionally involved in SV trafficking, we hypothesized that postsynaptic Nlgn3 may form trans-synaptic complexes with presynaptic Sar1a and Hspa8 to facilitate the alignment of pre- and postsynaptic elements, promoting synapse formation. To directly assess whether Nlgn3 interacts with Sar1a and Hspa8 in trans on cellular surfaces, we performed cell aggregation assays. As shown in Fig. 5G, HEK293T cells expressing Nlgn3 significantly aggregated with those expressing Sar1a or Hspa8. These results indicate that Nlgn3 can interact with Sar1a or Hspa8 on the cell surface of opposing cells.

Due to the dynamic and activity‑dependent nature of large trans‑synaptic protein complexes, it is technically difficult to reliably capture Nlgn3‑mediated synapse regeneration together with its putative presynaptic partners Sar1a and Hspa8 at defined stages after SCI by ultrastructural EM. Therefore, in the present study we focused on determining their endogenous subcellular distribution under physiological conditions by performing immunogold-EM on spinal cord sections from intact rats. Nlgn3 localized predominantly to the postsynaptic density (PSD), as previously reported (Fig. 5H), whereas Sar1a (Fig. 5I) and Hspa8 (Fig. 5J) were detected at both pre‑ and postsynaptic sides, particularly within synaptic vesicle clusters and lateral to the active zone, indicating their potential involvement in organized trans‑synaptic units. Taken together, these data establish the baseline synaptic localization and potential interaction sites of Nlgn3, Sar1a and Hspa8 in intact spinal cord, but do not directly visualize newly formed synapses at the ultrastructural level after SCI.

Nlgn3 modulates the clustering of Sar1a and Hspa8 at synapses in mature neurons

Given the prominence of Nlgn3 in synaptic organization, we next sought to determine its impact on the recruitment of Sar1a and Hspa8 at synapses. Mature cortical neurons at 28 DIV were transduced with various lentiviruses to achieve Nlgn3 OE or KD (Additional file 1: Figs. S5A and S5B). Using IF, we quantified how changes in Nlgn3 expression affected the number, average size and average fluorescence intensity of Nlgn3‑, Syn1‑, Sar1a‑ and Hspa8‑positive puncta (Fig. 6F–I). Co‑immunostaining for Nlgn3 and the presynaptic vesicle marker Syn1 demonstrated that Nlgn3 OE and KD had little effect on the number of Syn1‑positive puncta, but markedly increased and decreased their average size, respectively; consequently, the average fluorescence intensity of Syn1 puncta was higher with Nlgn3 OE and lower with Nlgn3 KD, suggesting that Nlgn3 plays a pivotal role in regulating synaptic strength in mature neurons (Fig. 6A, F and G). Similar relationships were observed for Sar1a puncta, with Nlgn3 OE and KD mainly affecting their average size and resulting intensity, but not their number (Fig. 6B and H). In contrast, Nlgn3 expression levels significantly affected both the number and the size of Hspa8‑positive puncta in opposite directions—higher Nlgn3 was associated with fewer but larger Hspa8 puncta—so that the average fluorescence intensity of Hspa8 puncta remained essentially unchanged (Fig. 6C and I). Additionally, Nlgn3 enhanced both excitatory and inhibitory synapses, specifically marked by VGLUT1 and GAD67, respectively, without intensifying those marked by VGLUT2 and VGAT (Additional file 1: Figs. S5C and S5D). The interaction of Nlgn3 with Sar1a and Hspa8 prompted us to investigate their potential contribution to SCI repair. We then investigated whether the combinatorial induction of Nlgn3 with either Sar1a or Hspa8 in neurons could synergistically contribute to SCI repair. For this purpose, we conducted intrathecal injections of AAV-Nlgn3-Flag alone or in combination with either AAV-Sar1a-Myc (AAV-Nlgn3/Sar1a) or AAV-Hspa8-HA (AAV-Nlgn3/Hspa8) in rats with sub-SCI and compared their therapeutic effects. Electrophysiologically, both combinations significantly increased MEP amplitude, indicating enhanced relay efficiency of spinal circuitry (Fig. 7G and H). Thus, Nlgn3, with Sar1a or Hspa8, synergistically enhances SCI recovery.

Discussion

Although numerous synaptic CAMs are known to regulate synaptic signaling, key components for post-injury synaptic reconstruction remain poorly defined. Using implanted hUC-MSCs as a screening platform, we discovered a novel role for Nlgn3 in promoting locomotor recovery after SCI. Mechanistically, Nlgn3 interacts with synaptic vesicle proteins Sar1a and Hspa8 in neurons, synergistically enhancing functional recovery of SCI. Our findings not only reveal a mode of action of Nlgn3 in SCI repair but also suggest a combinatorial approach targeting the Nlgn3 interactome for SCI treatment.

MSCs and SCI

Extensive research has detailed MSC gene expression changes in response to in vitro neuronal differentiation or inflammation induction. However, the complexity of the SCE prevents a complete understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing the role of MSCs in injured spinal cords using cellular models alone. We previously demonstrated that hUC-MSCs remain in the injured rat spinal cord for weeks post-transplantation, indicating that SCE-reprogrammed MSCs may integrate into the damaged tissue to facilitate functional recovery [17, 38]. This led us to hypothesize that transcriptionally reprogrammed MSCs could serve as a valuable model for identifying genes that promote structural plasticity and regeneration after SCI, rather than to conclude that MSCs necessarily convert into neurons in vivo. To capture the early changes in gene expression at the transcriptional level in vivo, we retrieved DiD-hUC-MSCs after intrathecal administration from both intact and SCI rats at 1 and 3 dpt. Consistent with our speculation, the SCE-activated genes were mainly enriched in neuronal differentiation, especially associated with the regulation of neuron projection development, synapse assembly, presynapse organization, and postsynaptic specialization (Fig. 1C). Previous studies, including our own, have revealed the sustained transcriptional repression of synaptic transmission-related genes in the injured spinal cord, such as ion channels, neurotransmitter receptors, and axon/dendrite-related genes, indicating a permanent impairment of neurotransmission. Therefore, we were particularly interested in genes encoding proteins potentially involved in axon guidance and synapse formation. For instance, postsynaptic Lrfn5, known to promote synapse development and regulate excitatory synaptic strength through trans-synaptic interaction with presynaptic LAR-RPTPs, was of interest [39]. Additionally, Lrrtm1 and Lrrtm3, members of the synaptogenic LRRTM proteins, are another class of Nrxn ligands that interact with PSD-95 in postsynaptic terminals [40]. Furthermore, the postsynaptic Slit- and Trk-like (Slitrk) family members were considered due to their roles in coordinating with presynaptic protein tyrosine phosphatase σ (PTPσ) and PTPδ to trigger excitatory and inhibitory synapse formation, respectively [41, 42]. We hypothesized that the activation of these molecules could facilitate functional recovery after SCI. However, only Nlgn3 successfully passed the in vitro functional validation, likely due to the artificial culture's inability to fully mimic the intricate SCE. To circumvent this issue, our recent efforts have shifted to in vivo screening using a CRISPR-mediated gene activation system delivered within an all-in-one AAV to activate endogenous target genes in the spinal neurons. In conclusion, our data suggest that SCE-reprogrammed MSCs, expressing neuronal wiring ligands or receptors, may act as mediators bridging the CST and spared propriospinal neurons, thereby facilitating the reestablishment of spinal circuitry. Our study, therefore, highlights that engineered hUC-MSCs overexpressing neural circuit reconstruction genes represent a promising therapeutic strategy to enhance the clinical efficacy of stem cell therapy for SCI. Importantly, even though MSCs overexpressing Nlgn3 enhance neuronal marker expression and functional recovery, our present results do not demonstrate that transplanted MSCs are converted into induced neurons or neuron‑like cells in vivo, and they do not reveal the exact mechanisms leading to higher pan‑neuronal marker levels. This represents an important limitation of the current study and will be an important focus for future investigations.

The Nlgn3 interactome and SCI

Numerous CAMs are functionally involved in the connection between pre- and postsynaptic neurons during development, while only a limited number have been identified as true contributors to synaptic remodeling after SCI in adults [43]. Among them, Nlgns are the most extensively studied postsynaptic CAMs, encoded by Nlgn1-4 in rodents and an additional gene, NLGN5, in humans. Nlgn1 was the first neuroligin to be cloned and is predominantly localized at excitatory synapses, where it regulates synaptic transmission and plasticity [44, 45]. Recent work has further shown that overexpression of the Nlgn1–Nrxn1 pair in neural stem cells can promote functional recovery after SCI [46]. Nlgn2 is selectively targeted to inhibitory synapses and controls inhibitory synaptic transmission, whereas Nlgn4 in mice and humans localizes to glycinergic/inhibitory and excitatory synapses, respectively [47, 48]. In contrast, Nlgn3 is the only neuroligin isoform that is consistently found at subsets of both excitatory and inhibitory synapses [25]. Moreover, Nlgn3 has attracted particular interest because its various mutations are strongly associated with ASDs [49, 50].

The exploration of direct interactors of Nlgn3 at synapses has revealed several identified molecules, including its well-known canonical axonal receptor βNrxn [51, 52], PSD-95 [53, 54], MAM domain-containing glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchor proteins (MDGAs) [55, 56], and secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine-like 1 (Sparcl1) [57]. In a more recent study, Yoshida et al. discovered presynaptic PTPδ as a noncanonical Nlgn3 interactor, competing with Nrxns to regulate the development of sociality [58]. Despite these findings, comprehensive proteomic studies on the Nlgn3-interacting networks are lacking.

We hypothesized that Nlgn3 may achieve its therapeutic function in treating SCI through protein–protein interactions. Using IP-MS, we identified proteins involved in the constitution of SVs as well as those participating in exocytosis and recycling of SVs that may interact with Nlgn3. Both Sar1a and Hspa8, participating in SV trafficking, have been shown to be present in the proteomics of the synaptic boutons and play roles in axonal elongation and neurotransmitter release, respectively [29]. In addition to proteins exclusively associated with SV trafficking, our Nlgn3 immunoprecipitated proteins encompass those linked to synaptic connections, localized at either presynaptic or postsynaptic sites (Fig. 5B and C). These proteins may collaborate with Nlgn3 to reconnect the post- and presynaptic compartments after SCI. For example, the neural cell adhesion molecule 1 (Ncam1), as a member of the immunoglobulin (Ig) superfamily of CAMs, was present in our Nlgn3 interactome, promoting stabilization of synaptic contacts through the homophilic interaction [59–61]. Moreover, Nlgn3 may also interact with the large-conductance Ca2 + -activated K channels (Kcnma1) which localizes at presynaptic terminals, regulating transmitter release [62]. Furthermore, Nlgn3 may interact with postsynaptic Robo2 which can complex with its extracellular ligand Slit and presynaptic Nrxns to establish synaptic connections [63]. Additionally, we detected tyrosine 3-monooxygenase/tryptophan 5-monooxygenase activation protein beta (Ywhab) and DLG associated protein 2 (Dlgap2), which may interact with Nlgn3 at postsynaptic density as PSD-95-associated proteins [54, 64, 65]. Intriguingly, the X-linked autism-related genes, Dlgap2 [66] and methyl-CpG binding protein 2 (Mecp2), both emerged in the Nlgn3 protein interactome, implying that these genes may collaboratively function within a complex [67]. Of note, the absence of Nrxns from our Nlgn3 interactome does not rule out the possibility that Nrxns may be mediators of the effect of Nlgn3. A plausible explanation could be the use of three detergents, 0.5% Deoxycholate, 1% NP-40 and 0.1% SDS, in RIPA buffer to solubilize and release membrane-associated proteins for IP experiments, which may disrupt the Nlgn3-containing macromolecular complex. Collectively, elucidating the protein interaction network of Nlgn3 is needed for a better understanding of their respective synaptic functions and how these interacting partners coordinate with each other under physiological and pathological conditions.

Based on our data, we propose a model centered around Nlgn3 (Fig. 8): Nlgn3 promotes functional recovery of SCI by orchestrating transsynaptic interactions with synaptic vesicle proteins on spared axons. Conclusions

This study reveals novel therapeutic effects and molecular mechanisms of Nlgn3 in SCI repair. First, transplantation of MSCs overexpressing Nlgn3 significantly enhances MSC-mediated SCI recovery. Second, the therapeutic effect of Nlgn3 extends to spinal neurons, as neuron-specific Nlgn3 restoration promotes functional recovery after SCI. Finally, by elucidating the underlying mechanisms, we propose a combinatorial strategy targeting the noncanonical Nlgn3-centered protein network for SCI treatment. Importantly, this work also emphasizes the value of investigating the transcriptional reprogramming of transplanted MSCs within the injury microenvironment as a means to discover more effective therapeutic targets.
