Global trends, research hotspots, and clinical evidence mapping of music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment: a bibliometric and visualization analysis from 2006 to 2026

Qiangli Lei, Qing Liu

Abstract

BackgroundCognitive impairment is an important public health issue in ageing societies. Music therapy, as a safe and accessible non-pharmacological intervention, has been increasingly used in the management of cognitive impairment. However, the overall research pattern, evolution of research hotspots, and features of clinical evidence in this field have not been systematically reviewed. This study aimed to analyze the current status, major topics, and development trends of research on music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment from 2006 to 2026.MethodsA stratified multi-database search strategy was used. The Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) served as the main data source. Bibliometric and visualization analyses were performed using Bibliometrix, CiteSpace, and VOSviewer. The Scopus database was also used to cross-validate key findings. In addition, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) retrieved from PubMed were analyzed to further assess the clinical evidence base.ResultsA total of 835 publications were included from WoSCC. From 2006 to 2025, the annual number of publications increased steadily, with an average annual growth rate of 13.35%. The publication output reached its peak in 2025, and the exponential model showed a good fit to the growth trend (R2 = 0.913). The United States, China, the United Kingdom, and Australia were the leading contributing countries, while institutions such as the University of Melbourne made notable contributions. The main research topics focused on dementia, music therapy, Alzheimer’s disease, quality of life, and cognitive dysfunction. Over time, research hotspots have extended toward cognitive improvement, long-term care, and neuroplasticity-related mechanisms. A total of 250 RCTs were identified from PubMed, indicating a growing body of clinical evidence.ConclusionResearch on music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment has shown continuous growth. Core topics mainly involve cognitive function, behavioral symptoms, quality of life, and care support. Mechanistic studies, especially those related to neuroplasticity, have become an important research frontier. Future studies should place greater emphasis on high-quality clinical research, clarify the mechanisms of music therapy, and promote its precise application and clinical translation.

Introduction

Cognitive impairment is an important public health challenge in the context of population ageing. It covers different clinical stages and conditions, including mild cognitive impairment, dementia, and Alzheimer’s disease (). Patients often show impairments in memory, attention, executive function, and language. These changes are often accompanied by behavioral and psychological symptoms. As a result, cognitive impairment can affect daily living ability, social function, and quality of life (). With the rapid ageing of the global population, the number of people living with cognitive impairment continues to increase. This condition has become one of the major causes of disability, long-term care dependence, and death (). By 2050, the number of people with dementia worldwide is expected to exceed 150 million, which will further increase medical, social, and economic burdens (). Therefore, exploring safe, effective, and sustainable intervention strategies has become an important direction in the prevention and management of cognitive impairment.

Current treatment for cognitive impairment is still mainly based on pharmacological interventions. However, existing treatments have certain limitations in slowing disease progression and improving long-term outcomes. Non-pharmacological interventions have therefore become an important part of comprehensive management for cognitive impairment. Among these approaches, music therapy is an intervention centered on musical experience and therapeutic interaction. It has been widely used in clinical care and rehabilitation practice for patients with cognitive impairment (). Through music listening, singing, rhythmic activities, and structured music training, music therapy can evoke emotional responses, promote social interaction, and increase patient engagement (; ). Previous studies have shown that music interventions may help relieve behavioral and psychological symptoms, such as anxiety, depression, and agitation. They may also have positive effects on maintaining cognitive function and improving quality of life (; ). In patients with mild cognitive impairment and dementia, music-based interventions have shown potential value in improving several cognitive domains, including memory, attention, and executive function (). These findings provide important support for the application of music therapy in people with cognitive impairment.

As the clinical value of music therapy has received increasing attention, its potential mechanisms have also gained support from neuroscience research. Studies have shown that musical stimulation can activate broad brain networks related to memory, attention, and emotional processing. This provides a neurobiological basis for the effects of music therapy on cognitive and neuropsychiatric symptoms (; ). In patients with Alzheimer’s disease and mild cognitive impairment, relatively preserved musical memory is considered one of the important foundations for the therapeutic effects of music interventions (). In addition, music activities that combine auditory, motor, and social elements may enhance cognitive engagement and social interaction (). These findings offer theoretical support for the use of music therapy in populations with cognitive impairment. With the deepening of related research, the application settings of music therapy have gradually expanded from institutional care to community and home-based environments. Its intervention goals have also extended from symptom relief to cognitive health promotion, functional maintenance, and long-term care support ().

The continued expansion of research scope and application settings has led to a rapid increase in related studies. Music therapy for cognitive impairment has gradually developed into an interdisciplinary research field involving neuroscience, geriatrics, rehabilitation medicine, nursing, psychology, and music therapy (; ). The publication of many randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses has provided a relatively rich evidence base for the clinical use of music therapy (). However, most existing studies have focused on intervention effects. The knowledge structure, research hotspots, and development trends of this field have not been systematically examined. Bibliometrics, through quantitative analysis and knowledge map visualization, can reveal patterns of disciplinary development, research hotspots, and academic collaboration. It therefore provides an important perspective for understanding the evolution of a research field (). For this reason, a systematic analysis from a macro-level perspective is needed to clarify the knowledge structure, research hotspots, and future directions of music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment.

Based on this background, the present study analyzed publications on music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment from 2006 to 2026. It aimed to systematically examine the global research landscape, knowledge base, hotspot evolution, and development trends in this field. In addition, randomized controlled trials were analyzed to further evaluate the characteristics of clinical evidence. This study may provide useful references for future research design, clinical practice, and translational application.

Materials and methods

Data sources and search strategy

Given the differences among databases in literature coverage, indexing systems, and subject scope, this study used a stratified multi-database search strategy. Records from different databases were not directly merged for analysis. The Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) was selected as the main data source for the bibliometric analysis. As one of the most widely used citation databases, WoSCC provides a well-established citation indexing system. It is also highly compatible with mainstream bibliometric tools, such as Bibliometrix, CiteSpace, and VOSviewer. Therefore, it is suitable for visual analyses of knowledge structure, research hotspots, and development trends. To reduce the potential bias caused by using a single database and to improve the robustness of the findings, Scopus was also included. Based on the WoSCC analysis, Scopus was used to cross-validate key indicators, including publication growth trends, country or regional distribution, and major contributing institutions.

Research on music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment involves both the evolution of academic knowledge and the accumulation of clinical intervention evidence. For this reason, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in PubMed were also searched and analyzed. PubMed has a standardized Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) indexing system and strict literature inclusion standards. It is therefore highly useful for identifying clinical evidence (). By combining subject-related search terms with publication type restrictions, representative RCTs in this field can be identified with relatively high accuracy. This provides additional evidence for understanding the clinical application and translational value of major research topics. By integrating results from WoSCC, Scopus, and PubMed, this study assessed the development of the field from two perspectives: knowledge evolution and clinical application.

The WoSCC search was completed on May 31, 2026. The time span was set from January 1, 2006 to May 31, 2026. The search strategy consisted of music therapy-related terms (TS1) and cognitive impairment-related terms (TS2): TS1 = (“music therapy” OR “music intervention*” OR “music-based intervention*” OR “therapeutic music” OR “music listening” OR “music medicine” OR “musical intervention*” OR “singing intervention*” OR “singing therap*”); TS2 = (“cognitive impairment” OR “mild cognitive impairment” OR “cognitive decline” OR “cognitive dysfunction” OR “cognitive disorder*” OR dementia OR “Alzheimer disease” OR “Alzheimer’s disease”). The final search formula was TS = TS1 AND TS2. During literature screening, only English-language Articles and Reviews were included. Conference abstracts, editorial materials, book chapters, news items, corrections, and other non-research publications were excluded.

To examine the consistency of results across databases, Scopus was searched at the same time. A search strategy broadly consistent with that used in WoSCC was applied in the TITLE-ABS-KEY field to ensure comparability ().

PubMed was used to retrieve RCTs and to evaluate the development of clinical research on music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment. The search was conducted up to May 31, 2026. The search formula was as follows: (“music therapy” OR “music intervention” OR “music-based intervention” OR “therapeutic music” OR “music listening” OR “singing intervention”) AND (“cognitive impairment” OR “mild cognitive impairment” OR “cognitive decline” OR “cognitive dysfunction” OR “cognitive disorder” OR dementia OR “Alzheimer disease” OR “Alzheimer’s disease”) AND (“randomized controlled trial”[Publication Type] OR randomized[Title/Abstract]) AND (“2006/01/01”[Date - Publication]: “2026/05/31”[Date - Publication]).

Data analysis

The publications retrieved from WoSCC were used as the main dataset. Bibliometric analysis and visualization were conducted using Bibliometrix (v5.0.1), CiteSpace (v6.4. R1), and VOSviewer (v1.6.20) (; ). Bibliometrix was used for basic statistical analysis, collaboration network construction, and thematic structure analysis. CiteSpace was used for citation burst detection, temporal evolution analysis, and identification of the knowledge base. VOSviewer was used to construct and visualize co-occurrence, co-citation, and collaboration networks. In this study, multiple-country publications (MCPs) were defined as publications in which the authors’ affiliations came from two or more countries or regions.

In the Bibliometrix analysis, automatic layout optimization was used to improve the network structure, and the Walktrap algorithm was applied to identify thematic clusters. Keyword co-occurrence analysis was normalized using the Association Strength method. Isolated nodes and weakly related terms were removed, and only high-frequency keywords were retained for analysis. In CiteSpace, the time slice was set to 1 year. The Pathfinder algorithm was used for network pruning, and nodes were selected based on the g-index with k = 25. These settings were used to identify research frontiers and patterns of knowledge evolution. In VOSviewer, the Fractional Counting method was used to construct networks. The minimum occurrence threshold for keywords was set at five.

Data from Scopus were mainly used for cross-validation of key results. RStudio was used to analyze publication trends, country or regional distribution, and institutional contributions. These analyses were conducted to assess the consistency of results across databases. RCTs retrieved from PubMed were analyzed using Bibliometrix for descriptive statistics and thematic analysis. This analysis was used to supplement the understanding of development trends and research topics in clinical intervention studies.

In addition, Microsoft Excel 2024 was used for data organization and statistical processing. Adobe Illustrator 2026 was used for figure refinement and final editing.

Results

General characteristics and publication trends

A total of 835 publications were finally included in the analysis. These publications were published in 268 academic journals and involved 3,627 authors. Internationally co-authored publications accounted for 24.43% of all records. Each publication had an average of 5.69 authors. The average annual growth rate during the study period was 13.35% ().

The annual number of publications increased from 4 in 2006 to 102 in 2025, showing a clear upward trend. From 2006 to 2016, research output grew slowly. After 2017, the annual number of publications increased markedly and remained at a relatively high level after 2020. The cumulative number of publications also continued to rise. The fitted curve was generally consistent with the observed trend, reflecting the long-term growth pattern of publications in this field (, ). The prediction interval shown in gradually widened over time. This indicates greater uncertainty in the model estimates for later publication trends. This interval was used to describe the uncertainty range of model prediction, rather than the statistical confidence interval of the actual annual publication counts.

The comparison of different functional models showed that both the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and the Akaike information criterion (AIC) supported the exponential model as the best-fitting model (,). The exponential model was highly consistent with the observed annual publication trend (R2 = 0.913). This suggests a clear pattern of cumulative growth in research output in this field. Cross-validation based on the Scopus database further showed that the annual publication trend was generally consistent with the WoSCC results (). Both databases showed a sustained increase in publication output.

Contributions of countries or regions and institutions

The distribution of publications by country or region showed that research on music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment has developed into a broad international field (, ). The United States ranked first with 142 publications, followed closely by China with 141 publications. Together, these two countries contributed nearly one-third of all publications. The United Kingdom ranked third with 96 publications, followed by Australia with 75 publications. Overall, research activity was mainly concentrated in North America, Europe, and the Asia-Pacific region.

In terms of multiple-country publications, China and Australia each published 24 internationally collaborative papers. The United Kingdom and the United States published 20 and 17, respectively. When calculated as the proportion of multiple-country publications among total national output, the Netherlands ranked among the highest at 44.0%, followed by Australia at 32.0%. The corresponding proportion for the United States was 11.9%.

The country-level research pattern was also reflected in the distribution of institutions (, ). The University of Melbourne ranked first with 77 publications. Taipei Medical University and the University of London each published 48 papers, followed by the University of Toronto with 44 publications. High-output institutions were mainly located in Australia, the United Kingdom, Canada, and China. The Scopus database showed a similar distribution pattern (). The University of Melbourne remained the most productive institution. The University of Toronto, the University of London, the University of Nottingham, and Anglia Ruskin University also ranked among the leading institutions.

Thematic structure and development trends

Thematic evolution analysis showed clear thematic continuity in research on music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment (). From 2006 to 2021, “dementia” and “Alzheimer’s disease” formed the main thematic paths. These themes continued into the period from 2022 to 2026. During the same period, early themes related to “depression” and “therapy” gradually became linked with “older adults” and “cognitive impairment.” This suggests that the research focus expanded from dementia and Alzheimer’s disease to older adults and cognitive outcome assessment.

The thematic map showed the distribution of different themes within the research structure (). “Dementia,” “music therapy,” and “therapy” were located in the basic-theme area and showed high relevance. Trend topic analysis showed changes in research themes over time (). Early topics included “agitated behavior,” “nursing,” “management,” and “preferred music.” These studies mainly focused on behavioral symptom management, nursing practice, and individualized music use. In the middle stage, topics such as “anxiety,” “depression,” “neuropsychiatric symptoms,” and “quality of life” became more active. Research outcomes expanded from symptom-related indicators to psychological status and quality of life. In recent years, “cognitive impairment,” “family caregivers,” “engagement,” “music-based interventions,” and “plasticity” have continued to appear. These topics indicate growing attention to cognitive function, caregiver involvement, music intervention models, and potential mechanisms.

Discussion

Overall research landscape

Using a stratified multi-database strategy based on WoSCC, Scopus, and PubMed, this study systematically examined the development of music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment from two perspectives: knowledge structure and clinical evidence. The results showed that research in this field has continued to grow over the past two decades and entered a faster stage of development after 2017. The exponential model also suggested a clear pattern of knowledge accumulation. Publication trends, collaboration networks, and the development of RCTs showed good consistency. These findings suggest that music therapy for cognitive impairment has gradually moved beyond early exploration and has formed a relatively stable research base and academic community.

The country and institution analyses showed that the United States, China, the United Kingdom, and Australia are the main contributors to this field. China and the United States made important contributions to both total publication output and RCT production, suggesting that this topic has received broad attention across different regions. Australia showed strong performance in institutional influence, author contribution, and international collaboration. The University of Melbourne has long occupied a central position in the institutional collaboration network. High-impact scholars such as Baker and Tamplin are also mainly from Australian research teams. This reflects a relatively stable research system in Australia at the intersection of music therapy and cognitive impairment. The United States played an important bridging role in the international collaboration network. Its broad international links may help promote knowledge exchange and resource integration among different research teams.

The author co-citation network, journal co-citation network, and highly cited publication analysis together revealed a relatively stable knowledge base in this field. Scholars such as Raglio, Baker, and Särkämö were important knowledge sources. Journals such as International Psychogeriatrics, International Journal of Geriatric Psychiatry, and Journal of Alzheimer’s Disease provided major academic platforms for the dissemination of research findings. Highly cited publications were mainly randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses. This suggests that the evidence base in this field has gradually developed from single clinical observations to evidence-based synthesis. It also provides an important reference for future study design and intervention evaluation.

The 250 randomized controlled trials included from PubMed showed that music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment already have a certain clinical evidence base. RCTs in this field have continued to increase. Their focus has gradually expanded from symptom assessment and treatment effect observation to cognitive function, quality of life, mental health, and care-related outcomes. This change suggests that the clinical value of music therapy is no longer limited to symptom relief. It is increasingly linked to patients’ overall functional status and long-term health benefits. This also provides support for future evidence assessment, intervention optimization, and clinical translation.

Research hotspots and frontier directions

Cognitive improvement and rehabilitation value

This study found that cognitive improvement has remained a core topic in research on music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment. It held an important position in the keyword co-occurrence network, thematic evolution analysis, and RCT evidence map. Dementia and Alzheimer’s disease have long been central themes in this field. This reflects a sustained focus on the key clinical need to slow cognitive decline and maintain functional independence. Cognitive decline is not only a major feature of disease progression. It is also an important cause of functional impairment, increased care dependence, and reduced quality of life (). Given the limited effects of disease-modifying treatments, delaying cognitive decline and preserving existing abilities remain important goals in the management of cognitive impairment ().

Music activities involve several cognitive processes, including attention, memory, language, executive function, and emotional processing. They can also engage broad brain networks at the same time (). Meta-analyses have shown that music therapy can improve overall cognitive function in patients with dementia (). Randomized controlled studies have also found that regular singing and music listening may help maintain memory and orientation, and may slow the decline of global cognitive function (). In addition, the ability of familiar music to evoke autobiographical memory is relatively preserved in patients with Alzheimer’s disease. This may provide a potential neurocognitive basis for the effects of music interventions ().

This trend was also reflected in the thematic evolution analysis of the present study. The continued activity of “rehabilitation” suggests that research attention has extended from cognitive improvement to cognitive rehabilitation. Unlike studies that focus only on changes in cognitive scores, cognitive rehabilitation emphasizes the use of preserved abilities. It also focuses on maintaining daily function and social participation. In recent years, several studies have shown that singing training, active music participation, and individualized music listening may improve cognitive performance. They may also support functional maintenance and social engagement (; ; ). Therefore, the value of music therapy in cognitive impairment is expanding from symptom management to rehabilitation support.

Quality of life improvement and care support

Beyond cognitive function, quality of life has gradually become an important outcome for evaluating music therapy interventions. In this study, “quality of life” was closely linked with core keywords such as “dementia,” “Alzheimer’s disease,” and “music therapy.” In recent years, themes such as “caregiver,” “family caregivers,” “engagement,” and “mental health” have remained active. This suggests that research attention has extended from individual patients to long-term care and social support. This change is closely related to the clinical features of cognitive impairment. Patients with cognitive impairment often experience behavioral and psychological symptoms, such as anxiety, depression, agitation, and social withdrawal. These symptoms can weaken social participation and subjective well-being, and they are important causes of reduced quality of life (; ). As the disease progresses, care needs continue to increase. Patient quality of life and caregiver burden also become closely linked over time ().

Clinical studies have shown that music interventions can improve quality of life in patients with dementia and may have positive effects on emotional problems such as anxiety and depression (; ; ). Community singing and group music activities can promote social participation and emotional communication, thereby improving patients’ lived experience (). At the same time, the continued activity of themes such as “family caregivers” and “engagement” suggests that music therapy research is no longer limited to patients alone. It is beginning to consider the possible shared benefits for both patients and caregivers. Related studies have shown that music interventions may strengthen emotional connection and interaction quality between patients and caregivers, and may also reduce care-related stress to some extent (). These findings indicate that the value of music therapy is extending from individual symptom improvement to long-term care support. Its combined effects on cognition, emotion, and social function also provide an important entry point for future mechanistic research.

From efficacy evaluation to neuroplasticity mechanisms

Clinical studies have suggested that music therapy may have positive effects on cognitive function, emotional status, and quality of life. However, its underlying mechanisms remain to be clarified. In the present study, “plasticity” appeared as an emerging theme, and “magnetic resonance imaging” was also observed in the RCT trend topics. These findings suggest that research in this field is extending from clinical effect evaluation to the exploration of neuroplasticity and imaging-based mechanisms.

The introduction of neuroimaging methods provides new tools for observing changes in brain function after music therapy and for exploring possible pathways of action. In recent years, studies using functional magnetic resonance imaging, electroencephalography, and functional near-infrared spectroscopy have shown that music interventions may affect brain network connectivity related to cognitive and emotional processing. They may also promote functional reorganization of the brain (; ; ). These findings provide a neurobiological basis for understanding the clinical effects of music therapy. They also move the field from efficacy evaluation toward mechanism explanation.

For patients with Alzheimer’s disease, some neural networks related to music processing and musical memory remain relatively preserved during disease progression (). Even when memory function is clearly impaired, patients may still recognize familiar music and show related emotional responses (). Based on this feature, music therapy may work by activating preserved neural networks, promoting functional compensation, and enhancing the adaptive capacity of the nervous system (). Increasing research on preserved musical memory, changes in brain functional connectivity, and neuroplasticity regulation has provided new evidence for explaining the possible mechanisms of music therapy (; ; ).

Nevertheless, current evidence on the mechanisms of music therapy still comes mainly from small-sample neuroimaging studies and exploratory research. Studies differ in intervention forms, observation indicators, and assessment methods. These differences limit the comparison and integration of existing evidence. Future studies may combine functional magnetic resonance imaging, electroencephalography, functional near-infrared spectroscopy, neurotrophic factors, inflammatory markers, and other objective indicators. Such work could help build an evidence chain from brain network changes and biological effects to clinical benefits. It may also provide a theoretical basis for the more precise application of music therapy.

Clinical evidence and translational challenges

This study found that cognitive improvement, quality of life enhancement, and care support have become important research themes in music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment. These themes have also gradually entered the core outcome system of clinical research. The RCT evidence map showed that early studies mainly focused on behavioral and psychological symptoms such as agitation, anxiety, and depression. Later studies gradually expanded to cognitive function, social participation, and overall health outcomes (; ; ). The development of research hotspots was generally consistent with the direction of clinical evidence. This suggests that music therapy research is forming a continuous pathway from the identification of clinical needs to intervention evaluation and application expansion.

Even so, the growth rate of RCTs remains lower than that of the overall literature output. Compared with pharmacological interventions, music therapy is more individualized and context-dependent. Intervention content often needs to be adjusted according to patients’ cognitive status, music preferences, participation ability, and care environment. This increases the difficulty of study design and standardized implementation (). In addition, studies differ greatly in intervention form, frequency, duration, and outcome measures. Some studies also have small sample sizes and limited long-term follow-up. These factors reduce the comparability of findings and limit evidence synthesis.

With the accumulation of clinical studies, the field is no longer focused only on whether music therapy is effective overall. More attention is now being given to optimizing intervention forms, identifying suitable populations, and improving implementation pathways (). This change suggests that research on music therapy for cognitive impairment is moving from efficacy verification toward intervention optimization and precise application. Future studies need clearer consensus on intervention standardization, core outcome measures, and long-term effect evaluation. More multicenter randomized controlled trials and real-world studies are also needed to improve the quality of evidence and promote translation into clinical practice.

Strengths and limitations

Compared with previous studies that mainly relied on a single database or bibliometric analysis alone, this study combined bibliometric analysis with evidence from randomized controlled trials. It systematically evaluated music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment from both disciplinary development and clinical application perspectives. The findings describe the evolution of the knowledge structure, research hotspots, and development trends in this field. They also provide a reference for understanding the clinical evidence base and translational pathway of related research.

This study also has several limitations. First, databases differ in literature coverage, citation indexing systems, and metadata standards. Although this study used a unified search strategy and standardized analysis process, differences in database coverage may still have affected the statistics for some countries, institutions, and authors. Second, only English-language publications were included. This may have led to underrepresentation of studies from some non-English-speaking countries and regions. Because the implementation of music therapy is closely related to cultural background, care models, and healthcare systems, language restriction may have affected the complete presentation of the global research landscape to some extent.

In addition, bibliometric analysis mainly uses publication and citation data to construct knowledge maps. It can reflect the general trends and knowledge structure of a field, but it cannot directly assess study design quality, risk of bias, or strength of evidence. Although the RCT analysis added a clinical evidence perspective, several issues still require further systematic evaluation. These include differences in efficacy among music intervention models, suitable populations, core outcomes, and long-term effects.

Conclusion

Based on the WoSCC, Scopus, and PubMed databases, this study conducted a systematic bibliometric analysis and clinical evidence assessment of music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment from 2006 to 2026. The results showed that publication output in this field has continued to increase, with a faster stage of development emerging in the past decade. The United States, China, the United Kingdom, and Australia were the main contributing countries. Institutions such as the University of Melbourne played an important role in international collaboration and knowledge production. Author and journal co-citation analyses showed that this field has formed a relatively stable knowledge base. The findings also reflected clear interdisciplinary links among neuroscience, geriatric medicine, psychology, and rehabilitation medicine.

The evolution of research hotspots showed that music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment have gradually expanded from early management of behavioral and psychological symptoms to cognitive rehabilitation, mental health promotion, and care support. In recent years, topics such as cognitive function, quality of life, family caregivers, and optimization of music interventions have remained active. This suggests that research goals are moving from symptom relief toward the improvement of overall health outcomes. At the same time, the emergence of new topics such as neuroplasticity indicates a growing interest in explaining the potential mechanisms of music therapy.

The analysis of randomized controlled trials showed that music therapy interventions for cognitive impairment have developed a certain clinical evidence base. Quality of life, cognitive function, and dementia-related psychological problems are important outcomes in current RCT research. However, cognitive outcome assessment, intervention optimization, and feasibility studies still need further attention in future clinical research. More multicenter studies with large samples and long-term follow-up are needed. Future research should also promote the standardization of intervention protocols and core outcome measures. In addition, neuroimaging and biomarker studies should be integrated to clarify underlying mechanisms, thereby supporting the clinical translation and application of music therapy in the management of cognitive impairment.
