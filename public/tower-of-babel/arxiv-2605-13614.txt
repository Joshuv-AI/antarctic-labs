Search for pair production of additional neutral scalars within the Inert Doublet Model in a final state with two electrons or two muons in proton-proton collisions at s = 13 ​ \TeV \sqrt{s}=13\TeV and 13.6 \TeV


Abstract
A first dedicated search for pair production of new scalars predicted by the Inert Doublet Model is performed using proton-proton collisions. Data were collected with the CMS detector at the CERN LHC at s = 13 ​ \TeV \sqrt{s}=13\TeV and 13.6 \TeV , corresponding to integrated luminosities of 138 \fbinv and 35 \fbinv , respectively. Within this model, four additional scalar bosons ( \PH , \HepParticle A, \PSHp , and \PSHm ) are predicted. Through an additional discrete symmetry, the lightest new scalar, \PH , is stable, rendering it a viable dark matter candidate. These candidates can originate from quark-antiquark annihilation producing an offshell \PZ boson that decays to a pair of the new scalars. The target final state consists of exactly two opposite-charge same-flavour leptons (electrons or muons), with missing transverse momentum due to the stable neutral scalars, and very little hadronic activity. A parameterised neural network is used to separate the signal from the standard model background. No significant excess of events is observed. Exclusion limits at 95% confidence level are set on the production cross section of the two new neutral scalars, \PH and \HepParticle A, expressed in terms of their masses, m \PH m_{\PH} and m \HepParticle ​ A m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}} , in the m \PH m_{\PH} \vs m \HepParticle ​ A − m \PH m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}}-m_{\PH} plane. The observed (expected) exclusion region reaches m \PH = 108 ​ ( 106 ) ​ \GeV m_{\PH}=108\,(106)\GeV for m \HepParticle ​ A − m \PH = 78 ​ ( 76 ) ​ \GeV m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}}-m_{\PH}=78\,(76)\GeV and at m \PH = 70 ​ \GeV m_{\PH}=70\GeV , covers the range of m \HepParticle ​ A − m \PH = 40 m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}}-m_{\PH}=40 –90 (35–90) \GeV .

Introduction

The standard model (SM) of particle physics is the most successful
framework for describing the behaviour of the known
particles. Nevertheless, the SM is unable to account for numerous
observations. In particular, multiple cosmological measurements point
towards the existence of dark matter (DM)

[
95
]
, the abundance of which is predicted
to significantly outweigh that of normal matter. Although the specific
nature of DM remains unknown, one of the most widely supported
scenarios is that DM takes the form of a weakly interacting,
long-lived massive
particle
[
95
,
103
]
. The SM,
however, contains no such particle. Assuming that DM is a particle
that can interact with normal matter, it might be possible to detect
it experimentally. This can happen in a number of
ways
[
95
]
. One possibility is that stable
DM particles passing through our solar system could directly interact
with normal matter, imparting energy that can be detected in
direct-detection experiments. Another possibility is through the
detection of SM particles produced through annihilation of DM
particles. Alternatively, DM could be produced at a collider, leading,

\eg
to a missing energy
signature
[
13
,
62
]
. This paper focuses on the
latter signature, searching for DM production at the CERN LHC.

The search for a massive DM candidate has naturally led to the development of
several beyond-the-SM theories. One particular model is the Inert Doublet
Model (IDM), a two-Higgs-doublet model with an additional, unbroken

\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry

[
67
,
89
,
18
,
36
,
76
,
81
]
.
As a result of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry, additional particles from the
second Higgs doublet do not couple directly to the fermionic fields, whilst
also rendering the lightest of these new particles stable. Ultimately, this
introduces a weakly interacting, massive, stable particle, which is a viable
DM candidate. Although many phenomenological studies exist that
explore the sensitivity at future colliders

[
89
,
80
,
107
,
29
,
84
,
25
,
81
,
74
,
79
,
65
,
76
,
75
,
3
,
4
,
14
,
71
]
,
no dedicated search for IDM scalars has yet been performed with experimental
data. Existing experimental analyses with collider data are generally not
sensitive to a large part of the parameter space, for example because of high threshold
requirements on the missing transverse momentum (
\ptmiss
)
[
104
]
, and
their reinterpretation under the IDM hypothesis leads to very loose constraints

[
66
,
23
,
21
,
85
]
.

In this paper, we present a search for IDM signatures in proton-proton
(
\Pp\Pp
) collisions at centre-of-mass energies of 13
\TeV
and 13.6
\TeV
delivered by the LHC between 2016 and 2022, and recorded by the CMS

138
\fbinv
and 35
\fbinv
at
\sqrt{s}=13\TeV
and 13.6
\TeV
,
respectively. The main target process is the production
\Pp\Pp\to{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}\PH

{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}
and
\PH
are the additional neutral
IDM scalars of which
\PH
is the dark matter candidate. The scalar

{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}
subsequently decays into
\PZ\PH

(electrons or muons) accompanied by
\ptmiss

the two escaping

For this reason, this analysis focuses on

0.2
The Inert Doublet Model

The Inert Doublet Model is a two-Higgs-doublet model with an additional,
unbroken
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

symmetry
[
67
,
89
,
18
,
36
,
76
,
81
]
.
Following the convention detailed in Ref.
[
76
]
, the
doublets, denoted
\phi_{\mathrm{D}}
and
\phi_{\mathrm{S}}
, have the following transformation
properties under the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry:

\mathbb{Z}_{2}:\,\phi_{\mathrm{D}}\longrightarrow-\phi_{\mathrm{D}},\;\phi_{\mathrm{S}}\longrightarrow\phi_{\mathrm{S}},\;SM\longrightarrow SM,

(1)

where
SM
stands for all other SM fields.

Because of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry remaining unbroken during the
spontaneous symmetry breaking mechanism,
\phi_{\mathrm{D}}
is forced
to have zero vacuum expectation value, whereas
\phi_{\mathrm{S}}

undergoes the same mechanism as in the SM, leading to the usual
125
\GeV
SM Higgs boson,
\Ph
. This configuration defines the inert
vacuum
[
72
]
. The
\phi_{\mathrm{D}}
doublet leads
to four new scalars
\PH,{\HepParticle{A}{}{}},\PSHpm
, with masses
m_{\PH}
,
m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}}
, and
m_{\PSHpm}
,
respectively. In addition to the masses of the IDM particles, two
coupling constants are chosen, leading to the following five free
parameters in the IDM:
m_{\PH}
,
m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}}
,
m_{\PSHpm}
,
\lambda_{2}
, and

\lambda_{345}
. Here,
\lambda_{2}
is the coupling constant for the
quartic vertex between four IDM particles, and
\lambda_{345}
is the
coupling constant for the
\Ph\PH\PH
vertex. The couplings between
the new scalars and the SM gauge bosons are determined solely by
quantities from the SM electroweak (EW) sector. The exact IDM vertex
factors are presented in
\eg
Ref.
[
66
]
. The scalars from
the two doublets do not mix because of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry.

The
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry forbids any direct coupling between the SM fermion
fields and the IDM particles, and as such the IDM particles only couple
directly to bosons. Furthermore, as the symmetry is exact, remaining unbroken
after the symmetry breaking mechanism, IDM particles are always produced in
pairs. Consequently, the lightest of the IDM particles is stable, thereby
providing a viable DM candidate. Following the conventions in
Refs.
[
81
,
76
]
,
\PH
is chosen to be the
lightest among
\PH
,
{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}
, and
\PSHpm
.
Unlike other two-Higgs-doublet models,
\PH/{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}
do not correspond to
scalar/pseudoscalar particles as
\phi_{\mathrm{D}}
has no direct coupling to the
fermions. As a result, the exact roles of
\PH
and
{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}
can be exchanged
under a suitable redefinition of the coupling constants, and, therefore, the
choice of which neutral particle is lightest is arbitrary.

At a collider, a typical IDM signature is that of SM EW gauge bosons
with
\ptmiss
from the DM particles. Possible final
states include monojet, monophoton, invisible Higgs boson decays, or
multilepton channels (see
\eg
Refs
[
22
,
24
]
). This analysis focuses on
the OCSF dilepton (electrons or muons) plus
\ptmiss
final state, with example production mechanisms shown in
Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
Leading-order Feynman diagrams
of: (left)
\PH\PH\Pellp\Pellm
production through
{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}\PH
production,
(middle)
\PH\PH\Pellp\Pellm\PGn\PAGn
through
\PSHpm{\HepParticle{\PSH}{}{\mp}}
production, and (right)

\PH\PH\Pellp\Pellm
through

The IDM free parameters are subject to numerous theoretical and
experimental constraints. In this work, the constraints that are
applied follow Ref.
[
76
]
, with more recent updates
described in
Refs.
[
77
,
79
,
66
,
81
,
29
,
102
]
.
The two-loop constraints on the scalar couplings presented in
Ref.
[
29
]
are not applied, as the code is not
publicly available, instead the leading-order (LO) values for constraints
are used. Constraints on the potential stemming from vacuum
stability, perturbativity, and perturbative unitarity are included,
together with EW precision observables evaluated using

2HDMC

[
68
]
. Results for the EW precision
constraints are tested using the oblique parameters

S,\,T,\,U

[
9
,
97
,
98
,
92
]
and compared to the
latest PDG values
[
95
]
. All points in the
parameter space are required to reside in the inert
vacuum. Cases with a possible on-shell decay of
the Higgs boson to IDM particles must satisfy the most recent bound
on SM Higgs boson decays to invisible particles, an observed
upper limit at 95% confidence level on the branching ratio

\mathcal{B}(\Ph\to\text{inv})<0.11

[
12
]
. Using
MicrOMEGAs

[
20
]
, agreement with the upper bound for the relic density as measured by
the Planck experiment is imposed
[
5
]
. A detailed
discussion of regions where the IDM can match the observed relic
density can be found in Ref.
[
81
]
. Direct-detection
constraints are taken from the LZ
Collaboration
[
2
]
. Finally, the mass hierarchy is
required to obey the constraints from searches for supersymmetric
neutralinos at the CERN LEP reinterpreted within the
IDM
[
90
]
(referred to as LEP reinterpretation in
the following), and that the new particles would not contribute to the
decay widths of the EW gauge bosons at LO.

For the OCSF dilepton final state, the signal cross section is usually
dominated by the
{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}\PH
production shown in
Fig.
1
, left. As this process has only SM
couplings, the sensitivity predominantly depends on
m_{\PH}
and
m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}}-m_{\PH}
. At
tree level, the quartic coupling between IDM bosons,
\lambda_{2}
, does
not contribute, and as such the choice of
\lambda_{2}
is irrelevant
for signal production. Diagrams involving
\lambda_{345}
, which
couples the IDM particles to the SM Higgs boson as shown in
Fig.
1
, right, are strongly suppressed for

m_{\PH}>m_{\Ph}/2
, the target region for this analysis. In addition,
strong upper constraints on the value of
\lambda_{345}
stem from
direct-detection experiments
[
76
,
102
]
,
meaning that this parameter cannot be arbitrarily large. Even
considering the largest value allowed today, the sensitivity of this
analysis is not affected, and
\lambda_{345}
is set to 10
-6
. The
IDM mass
m_{\PSHpm}
has a lower limit of
m_{\PSHpm}=m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}}
and an approximate
upper limit of
m_{\PSHpm}=m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}}+50\GeV
within the range of
m_{\PH}
and
m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}}
values considered in this analysis. This parameter contributes to the
dilepton signal primarily through the diagram in
Fig.
1
, middle. However, this process generally
has a cross section over an order of magnitude smaller than the

{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}\PH
production, as well as poorer discrimination against the SM
background. By varying
m_{\PSHpm}
within the allowed range, no effect is
found on the sensitivity. For the signal production, the choice is
made to fix
m_{\PSHpm}
to
m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}}+50\GeV
. With these assumptions, the number
of free parameters is brought down to two and a scan is performed over

m_{\PH}
and
m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}}-m_{\PH}
.

0.3
The CMS detector

The central feature of the CMS apparatus is a superconducting solenoid of
6
\unit
m internal diameter, providing a magnetic field of 3.8
\unit
T. Within
the solenoid volume are a silicon pixel and strip tracker, a lead tungstate
crystal electromagnetic calorimeter (ECAL), and a brass and scintillator hadron
calorimeter, each composed of a barrel and two endcap sections. Forward
calorimeters extend the pseudorapidity
\eta
coverage provided by the barrel
and endcap detectors. Muons are measured in gas-ionisation detectors embedded
in the steel flux-return yoke outside the solenoid.

Events of interest are selected using a two-tiered trigger
system
[
45
,
60
]
. The first level (L1) is
composed of custom hardware processors, which use information from the
calorimeters and muon detectors to select events at a rate of about
100
\unit
kHz
[
53
]
. The second level, known as the
high-level trigger (HLT), is a software-based system that runs a version of the full
event reconstruction optimised for fast processing, reducing the event rate to
about 1 to 5
\unit
kHz.

A detailed description of the CMS detector, together with a definition
of the coordinate system used and the relevant kinematic variables, is
given in Ref.
[
39
]
. The upgraded configuration of
the detector for the LHC Run 3 is given in Ref.
[
58
]
.

0.5
Event reconstruction

A global event description is available using the particle-flow (PF)
algorithm
[
44
]
. Using a combination of the information
provided by the tracker, calorimeters, and muon systems, the PF algorithm aims
to reconstruct individual particles (PF candidates), classifying them as
electrons, photons, muons, charged hadrons, or neutral hadrons.

The primary vertex is taken to be the vertex corresponding to the hardest
scattering in the event, evaluated using tracking information alone, as
described in Section 9.4.1 of Ref.
[
42
]
.

Electron candidates
[
55
]
are selected in the range

\abs{\eta}<2.5
, ignoring those that fall within the transition
region between the ECAL barrel and endcap components,
1.444<\abs{\eta}<1.566
. Muon candidates
[
49
]
are selected
with
\abs{\eta}<2.4
. For electrons (muons), loose and tight working
points are defined in Ref.
[
55
]

(Ref.
[
49
]
) using selection criteria on discriminating
variables, as well as the relative isolation of the lepton. Here, the
relative isolation is defined as the scalar
\pt
sum of all photons,
neutral and charged hadrons within an angular distance of
\Delta R\equiv\sqrt{\smash[b]{(\Delta\phi)^{2}+(\Delta\eta)^{2}}}<0.3\,(0.4)
for
electrons (muons), and divided by the
\pt
of the lepton candidate,
where
\phi
is the azimuthal angle in radians. Loose (tight)
leptons are required to have
\pt>10\,(20)\GeV
. For genuine electrons
(muons) originating from the primary vertex, the loose and tight
working points have efficiencies of approximately 90% (98%) and 70%
(92%), respectively. Corrections are applied to account for
contributions from charged and neutral pileup particles. Finally, events
containing tau leptons can contribute indirectly from leptonic decays
involving electrons or muons, which account for less than 3% of the total
signal cross section.

Jets are clustered from PF candidates using the anti-
\kt
algorithm
[
31
,
32
]
with a distance
parameter of 0.4. Two techniques are used, depending on the
data-taking period, to mitigate contributions from pileup. For Run 2
data, charged particles identified as having originated from pileup
vertices are discarded, and an offset correction is applied to correct
for remaining neutral
contributions
[
44
]
. Additionally, for jets with

\pt<50\GeV
, an multivariate pileup discriminant is applied,
using a tight working point
[
54
]
. For 2022 data,
the pileup-per-particle identification algorithm
(PUPPI)
[
54
,
26
]
is used to mitigate
the effect of pileup at the reconstructed-particle level, making use
of local shape information, event pileup properties, and
tracking information. Charged particles identified as having
originated from pileup vertices are discarded. The local information
is used to derive a weight encoding the probability that a neutral
candidate comes from a pileup vertex. Each candidate’s four-momentum
is then scaled by this weight, creating PUPPI-weighted PF
candidates, before being clustered into jets, labelled as PUPPI
jets. For both methods, to adjust the average measured energy scale
of the jets to that of particle-level jets, corrections are derived
using simulated events, as a function of the reconstructed jet
\pt
and

\eta
. In situ measurements of the momentum balance in dijet,

\gamma\text{+jets}
,
\PZ\text{+jets}
, and multijet events are used to determine any
residual differences between the jet energy scale in data and in
simulation, and appropriate corrections are
made
[
43
]
. The energy resolution is also
corrected in simulation to improve the agreement with data. Jets are
selected in the range
\abs{\eta}<2.4
and with
\pt>20\GeV
. Jets
with an identified electron or muon within
\Delta R<0.4
are
rejected. Events with jets consistent with reconstruction
failures are vetoed.

The vector
\ptvecmiss
is computed as the negative
vector
\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}
sum of all PF candidates in an event, and its
magnitude and azimuthal angle are denoted as
\ptmiss
and
\phi^{\text{miss}}
, respectively. For Run 2,
\ptmiss
is defined using PF candidates,
whereas, for Run 3, PUPPI-weighted PF candidates are utilised. Any correction
applied to individual objects is propagated correspondingly to the

\ptmiss

[
51
]
. Specific event filters have been designed to reduce
events with spurious
\ptmiss
arising from reconstruction failures or from genuine
showers not originating from the
\Pp\Pp
collisions
[
51
]
.

The specific features of heavy-flavoured jets, in particular the
presence of displaced vertices, are used in a multivariate jet tagging
method. A working point of the
DeepJet

algorithm
[
28
]
is used to tag
\PQb
quark jets with

{\pt>20\GeV}
and
{\abs{\eta}<2.4}
, corresponding to a 90%
efficiency for correctly identifying
\PQb
jets and a 40 (4)%
probability of misidentifying a charm jet (light-flavour or gluon jet)
as a bottom jet.

0.6
Event selection

Events are selected in three steps. First, events are required to pass
criteria based on HLT algorithms that require the presence of at least
one electron (muon), satisfying isolation and identification
requirements, and
\pt
thresholds of 27–32 (24–27)
\GeV
. Exact threshold
values depend on the data-taking period. Second, a basic set of
preselection criteria is applied that remove reducible SM backgrounds,
while retaining a high efficiency for signal events. Third, a
parameterised neural network
[
15
]
is utilised to
further separate signal from backgrounds, and the output of the
network is used to define the signal region (SR).

0.6.1
Preselection

The final state contains a dilepton pair and two DM particles.
Based on this, the preselection requires all events to
contain at least two tight leptons, with the leading lepton passing
year-specific
\pt
requirements, and
a subleading lepton with
\pt>20\GeV
. The leading lepton
\pt

thresholds are chosen to be
2\GeV
above the thresholds implemented
in the lepton triggers, with values outlined in
Table
0.6.1
.

Each event is required to contain a dilepton pair—with no
constraints yet on the charges or flavours of the pair—that pass the
following requirements. As the signal leptons recoil against the DM
particles, the dilepton
\pt
is expected to be antiparallel with

\ptvecmiss
, leading to the requirement
\abs{\Delta\phi(\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\Pell\Pell},\ptvecmiss)}>1\unit{rad}
. The recoil also
boosts the dilepton pair, motivating the requirement

p_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\Pell\Pell}>15\GeV
, and the dilepton pair is more
collimated, hence the requirement
\cos(\Delta\phi_{\Pell\Pell})>-0.75
. The dilepton pair is required to have an invariant
mass of
12<m_{\Pell\Pell}<80\GeV
. Finally, with little hadronic activity
expected in the signal, any event with two or more jets with

\pt>30\GeV
and
\abs{\eta}<2.4
is vetoed.

0.6.2
Variable definitions

The following kinematic variables are particularly powerful for separating the signal from SM background processes with two leptons and
\ptmiss
in the final state. The transverse mass is defined as follows:

\mT(\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{a}},\ptvecmiss)=\sqrt{2\pt^{\,\mathrm{a}}\ptmiss(1-\cos[\Delta\phi(\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{a}},\ptvecmiss)])},

(2)

where
\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{a}}
refers to the
\pt
of
the individual leptons. In this paper, we also consider a different
definition where
\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{a}}
refers to the
\pt
of the dilepton system. In this case, the invariant mass of the
dilepton system can be included to obtain the transverse mass with
energy,
\mT^{E}
, defined as:

\mT^{E}(\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\Pell\Pell},m_{\Pell\Pell},\ptvecmiss)=\sqrt{2\sqrt{(\pt^{\,\Pell\Pell})^{2}+m_{\Pell\Pell}^{2}}\ptmiss(1-\cos[\Delta\phi(\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\Pell\Pell},\ptvecmiss)])}.

(3)

The stransverse mass (
\mTii
)
[
86
,
87
,
91
]
is the generalisation of the
\mT
for the case where two visible and two non-interacting
particles are produced. The
\pt
of the two visible particles is given by

\pt^{\,\mathrm{a}}
and
\pt^{\,\mathrm{b}}
. In this case, the
\pt
of the two
invisible particles,
\vec{q}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{1}}
and
\vec{q}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{2}}
, are
unknown, but their sum is constrained by
\vec{q}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{1}}+\vec{q}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{2}}=\ptvecmiss
. Furthermore, to include the case where the
non-interacting particles have mass
\chi
,
\mTii
uses a
generalised version of the transverse mass,
\mT^{\prime}
:

\mT^{\prime}(\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{a}},\ptvecmiss,\chi)=\sqrt{2\pt^{\,\mathrm{a}}\sqrt{(\ptmiss)^{2}+\chi^{2}}\left(1-\cos[\Delta\phi(\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{a}},\ptvecmiss)]\right)},

(4)

The definition of
\mTii
is then:

\mTii(\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{a}},\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{b}},\ptvecmiss,\chi)=\min_{\vec{q}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{1}}+\vec{q}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{2}}=\ptvecmiss}\left[\max\left\{\mT^{\prime}(\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{a}},\vec{q}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{1}},\chi),\mT^{\prime}(\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{b}},\vec{q}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{2}},\chi)\right\}\right],

(5)

where ”
\min
” is a minimisation with the constraint

\vec{q}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{1}}+\vec{q}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\mathrm{2}}=\ptvecmiss
, and
\max\{\mT^{\prime}(\dots),\mT^{\prime}(\dots)\}
returns
the maximum of the two
\mT^{\prime}
calculations. To use
\mTii
for its
discriminatory power, the variable is calculated under two mass
hypotheses. The first is with
\chi=0\GeV
, which corresponds to the
SM-like case where the invisible particles are massless neutrinos. The
second is
\chi=80\GeV
, which is the IDM-like case where the
invisible particles,
\PH
, are massive. Different choices of values
for
\chi
,
\eg
matching the generated signal
m_{\PH}
, do not impact the
signal vs. background discrimination power.

Finally, the jet-Z balance (JZB)
[
40
]
is defined as:

\mathrm{JZB}=\abs{\ptvecmiss+\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\Pell\Pell}}-\abs{\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\Pell\Pell}}.

(6)

For SM
\PZ/\HepParticle{\PGg}{}{\ast}\text{+jets}
events, where the
\ptmiss
stems
from detector effects rather than the underlying physical process,
this quantity is expected to be symmetric around 0. Whereas, for SM
events with neutrinos, or signal events, the dilepton
\pt
and
\ptmiss
are correlated, leading to a
JZB distribution skewed towards more negative values, allowing
discrimination against
\PZ/\HepParticle{\PGg}{}{\ast}\text{+jets}
events.

0.6.3
Signal region selection using a parameterised neural network

With the preselection defined in Table
0.6.1
, an
additional selection is applied specifically targeting the signal
topology. Every event is required to have an OCSF dilepton pair, with
both leptons passing the tight working point (
N_{\Pell,{\text{tight}}}=
2). A veto is applied on any event with an
additional loose lepton. Finally, to reduce the background from

\ttbar
and
\PQt\PW
events, a veto on any event containing a

\PQb
-tagged jet is applied.

Signal events have a broad range of possible topologies depending on the masses

(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})
. To ensure a good signal-background separation across all points,
a parameterised neural network (pNN) is used
[
15
]
. These extend
normal neural networks by also including the signal parameters
(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})
as
input, resulting in the network learning how to discriminate signal versus
background depending on the values of
(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})
. As a consequence, only a
single network, which can achieve optimal performance for each point, is
required for the entire parameter space. Furthermore, given that pNN
outputs vary smoothly as functions of
(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})
, it is possible to
interpolate between the simulated signal points, thus ensuring strong
sensitivity over the entire parameter space.

To reduce the complexity of the pNN model, as well as reduce training
times, the choice of input features is optimised using boosted
decision trees (BDT) in an iterative process. The process starts with
a large set of inputs involving both basic kinematic variables and
derived variables. Subsequently, for each individual signal point, an

XGBoost
BDT is trained using all inputs
[
37
]
. To quantify
the importance of each feature, the
XGBoost
”Gain” measure is
utilised, which corresponds to the average improvement to the loss
score each feature provides over the whole tree. The least important
feature is removed, the BDT is trained again, and the process is
repeated until the signal efficiency, at a background efficiency of
0.1% found by cutting on the BDT output score, reduces by more than
1%. This background efficiency is chosen as it approximately
corresponds to the background efficiency in the most sensitive regions
of the analysis. As the kinematic distributions vary with the signal
points, leading to different variables being selected, the
optimisation procedure is performed for all points, and the final set
of features is the union of the features selected for each
point. Furthermore, to take into account the fact that a NN can
”derive” variables, all the basic kinematic variables are kept
independently of their impact. After the procedure, a total of 26
features, 14 basic variables and 12 derived variables, are selected:

•

leading/subleading lepton
\pt
,
\eta
,
\phi
;

•

leading/subleading jet
\pt
,
\eta
,
\phi
;

•

\ptmiss
,
\phi^{\text{miss}}
;

•

dilepton
\pt^{\,\Pell\Pell}
,
\eta^{\,\Pell\Pell}
,

\phi^{\,\Pell\Pell}
,
m_{\Pell\Pell},\Delta R_{\Pell\Pell}
;

•

JZB (Eq. (
6
));

•

transverse mass,
\mT(\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\Pell\Pell},\ptvecmiss)
(Eq. (
2
));

•

transverse mass with energy,
\mT^{E}(\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\Pell\Pell},\ptvecmiss)
(Eq. (
3
));

•

stransverse masses,
\mTii(\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\Pell_{1}},\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\Pell_{2}},\ptvecmiss,\chi=0,80\GeV)
(Eq. (
5
));

•

\Delta\phi(j_{1},\ptvecmiss)
;

•

\Delta\phi(j_{1},\vec{p}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\,\Pell\Pell})
.

Each of the simulated samples are split into three sets: training, validation, and
test with fractions of the total being 50%, 20%, and 30%, respectively.
The training data set is used to optimise the pNN parameters, and the validation
set is used to measure the performance of the model during training. Finally,
the test set is used to evaluate the performance of the model, to
ensure that there is no overtraining, and to obtain the results of the analysis.
Events are initially weighted according
to their relative expected yields. Before training, the background samples are
reweighted such that the total sum of background weights equals the total sum
of all signal weights. The signal samples are also reweighted such that the sum
of weights for each sample is equal. During training, the signal samples are
passed through the pNN with the IDM parameters
(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})
at which they were
simulated. The background samples are randomly assigned
(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})
values
from the set of signal
(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})
values used in the training.

The pNN is implemented using
PyTorch

[
96
]
, and consists of a
feedforward network with six layers, each layer containing 60 hidden units and
an ELU activation function
[
38
]
, where the architecture was
determined over a grid search of different hyperparameters. The model is
trained using the Adam optimiser
[
83
]
with a learning rate of

10^{-4}
to minimise the weighted binary cross-entropy loss
function
[
106
]
. The pNN performance is observed to be
consistent with individual
XGBoost
BDTs trained separately on each signal
point.

The SR is defined using the pNN output, leveraging the fact that the
signal peaks towards an output of 1. Ideally, separate binning could be used
for each signal, optimising the sensitivity for every point, however this leads
to ambiguity when interpolating between points. Instead, a single set of bins
is used for the entire parameter space, but with separate binning for each
channel,
\Pep\Pem
and
\PGmp\PGmm
. Despite using the same binning, the loss in sensitivity
was observed to be less than 1%. To ensure that the distributions remain
smooth, each bin is required to have a maximum of 10% statistical uncertainty
in the MC predictions. As the signal distributions peak towards a pNN value of
1, the optimisation procedure starts at 1 and then uses a sliding window towards
lower pNN values. If a bin has a background statistical uncertainty of less
than 10% for all signal hypotheses then it is accepted, and the sliding window
starts again from the previous bin. This is repeated down to a pNN value of
0.9, which approximately corresponds to a minimum signal efficiency of 50%.
The SR selection is summarised in Table
0.6.3
.
For all signal mass points, the dominant signal contribution (
>
99%) in the SR is from the

\PH\PH\Pell\Pell
final state, where
\Pell=\Pe,\PGm,
or
\PGt
.

Backgrounds are estimated using a combination of simulated samples and
techniques based on control samples in data. Utilising the latter
leads to improved sensitivity compared to a simulation-only approach
by providing tighter constraints on the dominant background
normalisations and reducing the associated systematic
uncertainties. After the preselection and signal selection outlined in
Section
0.6
, the largest background is
\PZ/\HepParticle{\PGg}{}{\ast}\text{+jets}

requirement of
\mathrm{pNN}(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})>0.9

significantly reduced, with the largest remaining background sources
in the most significant bin (close to 1) being
\PW\PW
,
\ttbar
,

\PZ\PZ
, and
\PW\PZ
. For the estimation of the backgrounds from
data, two variables are used, namely the pNN output for the
discrimination of the signal against each background source, and the
dilepton
\pt
, which allows to better discriminate between the

\ttbar
and
\PW\PW
components (see Section
0.7.2
). For
each of the main background processes, their shape is determined with
simulation, while their normalisation is constrained with data using
CRs enriched in each process. An additional source of the background
in the SR and CRs is
\PW
+jets and QCD multijet events where one or
more jets are misidentified as leptons. These are estimated using a
misidentification background determination method based on control
samples in data. An overview of the CR selections is shown in
Table
0.7
, and a description of each CR is given
in this section. Other minor backgrounds (from
\PZ/\HepParticle{\PGg}{}{\ast}\text{+jets}
, single top quark
and

simulations.

The
\PZ\PZ
control region

The
\PZ\PZ
process constitutes the largest background, stemming from
one of the Z bosons decaying to an OCSF lepton pair, and the other Z
boson decaying to neutrinos. This can be emulated with a four-lepton
selection containing two OCSF dilepton pairs
[
57
]
. One
of the OCSF dilepton pairs is required to pass the preselection shown
in Table
0.6.1
. The other dilepton pair is removed
from the event and its
\pt
is added vectorially to the
\ptmiss
,
emulating the neutrino contribution. Furthermore, the dilepton pair
that acts as the neutrino pair is required to only pass the loose
lepton selection, and have
\abs{m_{\Pell\Pell}-m_{\PZ}}<10\GeV
to remove

\gamma^{*}\to\Pell\Pell
contributions. For events with four leptons
of the same flavour (
\Pep\Pem\Pep\Pem/\PGmp\PGmm\PGmp\PGmm
) there is an ambiguity in
pairing the leptons. In this case, the pair with an invariant mass
closest to the
\PZ
boson is chosen as the candidate neutrino
pair. Each event requires four loose leptons, at least two of which
are tight. Events with more than four loose leptons are vetoed.

After four-lepton events are selected, the
\PZ\PZ
CR
requires a looser selection on the pNN output than in the SR,

\mathrm{pNN}(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})>0.8
, to increase the number of events. The
\PZ\PZ

normalisation was found to not depend strongly on the pNN threshold,
given the large statistical uncertainty of the data sample. Finally,
the pNN output score is used as the observable in the fit when
extracting a potential signal.

0.7.2
The
\PW\PW
/
\ttbar
control region

For
\PW\PW
and
\ttbar
production, events with different-flavour leptons are
produced in equal amount to events with same-flavour leptons, excluding
reconstruction-specific effects. This symmetry is utilised to define two CRs to
constrain the
\PW\PW
and
\ttbar
background normalisations.

Each event is required to have exactly two tight opposite-charge
different-flavour leptons,
\Pepm\PGmmp
, and must pass the
preselection shown in Table
0.6.1
. Following this,
each event is required to have
\mathrm{pNN}(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})>0.9
to match the SR phase
space. The events are further split into those with no
\PQb
jets,

N_{\text{\PQb jets}}=0
, resulting in a
\PW\PW
/
\ttbar
CR, and
those with
N_{\text{\PQb jets}}\geq 1
, giving a
\ttbar
-dominated
CR. For both CRs, when extracting a potential signal, instead of
fitting to the pNN output like in the
\PZ\PZ
CR, the dilepton
\pt

distribution is used as this provides better discrimination between
the
\PWm\PWp
and
\ttbar
processes.
The selected
\ttbar
events typically have a greater dilepton
\pt
than those from the
\PWm\PWp
process.
To calculate the optimal
binning, a sliding window method is applied—starting from low
\pt

and scanning towards higher
\pt
—which accepts a bin if the
statistical uncertainties of the
\PWm\PWp
and
\ttbar
processes are
both below 10%. Signal events can enter the CR through the

\PH\PH\PGt\PGt
or
\PH\PH\Pell\Pell\PGn\PAGn
final states, however, after
the requirement
\mathrm{pNN}(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})>0.9
this contribution is negligible.

0.7.3
The
\PW\PZ
control region

The
\PW\PZ
contribution entering the SR comes primarily from the
fully leptonic final state, with negligible contributions from lepton+jets
and fully-hadronic decays. These can contribute in two ways. First, when the
leptons from the decay of the
\PZ
boson form the signal dilepton pair, and the
lepton from the
\PW
boson decay falls out of acceptance. Second, when the
dilepton pair is formed from one lepton from the
\PZ
boson decay and with the
lepton from the
\PW
boson decay, and the remaining lepton from the
\PZ
boson
falls out of acceptance. These are both estimated using a three-lepton
selection.

The first
\PW\PZ
CR requires exactly three tight leptons, containing an OCSF
dilepton pair, which passes the preselection in Table
0.6.1
.
Events with an additional loose lepton are vetoed. For events with three
leptons of the same flavour (
\Pe\Pe\Pe,\PGm\PGm\PGm
) the ambiguity in
identifying the pair coming from the
\PZ
boson is resolved by choosing the pair
that produces an invariant mass closest to the
\PZ
boson mass. Subsequently,
events are required to pass
\mathrm{pNN}(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})>0.9
to match the SR phase
space. The
\Pep\Pem
and
\PGmp\PGmm
channels are combined into one CR, keeping the
data-taking years separated, and the pNN output is used as the observable in
the fit when extracting a potential signal. The second
\PW\PZ
CR, targeting
the case where one lepton is from the
\PZ
boson decay and the other from the

\PW
boson decay, has identical requirements to the first, but requires a same-charge
same-flavour dilepton pair.

0.7.4
Estimates of backgrounds with misidentified leptons

Background processes that contain jets can enter the different selections when
one or more of the jets are incorrectly reconstructed as a lepton. This
contribution is referred to as MisID events, and is estimated using the
”misidentification method” from Ref.
[
41
]
.

This method uses the misidentification rate, which is defined as the
probability that a jet, that has already been reconstructed as a loose lepton,
also passes the tight lepton requirements. The misidentification rate is
measured using a sample enriched in
\PZ
+jets events. Each event
requires two tight leptons with
\pt>20\GeV
that form an OCSF pair which
satisfy
\abs{m_{\Pell\Pell}-m_{Z}}<15\GeV
. A third loose lepton (electron or muon)
is also required, with
\pt>20\GeV
, which acts as the probe lepton for
measuring the misidentification rates. To suppress the contributions from
diboson events with neutrinos in the final state, all events must pass

\ptmiss<25\GeV
. Events with a fourth loose lepton are vetoed to reduce the
contribution from the
\PZ\PZ\to 4\Pell
process. Contributions from
events with prompt third leptons are subtracted using estimates from
simulation. The misidentification rate is estimated in bins of the loose lepton

\pt
and
\eta
, separately for electrons and muons.

The MisID estimate is then calculated using data events with the same selection
as the SR or CRs, but where one or more lepton fails
the tight lepton requirements. In the case of the SR and
the
\PW\PW/\ttbar
CR, events where one lepton fails are predominantly

\PW
+jets events, and those where both fail are primarily QCD multijet
events. Using the misidentification rates, the data events are weighted to
calculate the expected number of MisID events with all leptons passing the
tight selection. Contamination from prompt events is subtracted using
simulation.

To constrain the normalisations of the MisID estimates, dedicated MisID CRs
are defined, exploiting the fact that jets can be misidentified as
leptons of either charge. The first MisID CR requires events with exactly two
tight leptons that form a same-charge same-flavour dilepton pair,

\Pepm\Pepm/\PGmpm\PGmpm
, which passes the preselection in
Table
0.6.1
. Events with an additional loose lepton are vetoed, as
are those with a
\PQb
-tagged jet. Each event must then pass
\mathrm{pNN}(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})>0.9
to match the
SR phase space, and then the pNN score is used as the variable in
the fit when extracting the signal. In addition, another MisID CR is defined,
which has the same requirements as the first, but instead demands the dilepton
pair to be same-charge and different-flavour,
\Pepm\PGmpm
. In this case,
the variable used in the fit is the dilepton
\pt
.

0.8
Systematic uncertainties

Several sources of experimental and theoretical uncertainties are considered in
this analysis, affecting either the total yield of a process, or the yield and
shape of the distributions. An overview of the systematic uncertainties and
their correlations is given in the following.

Lepton efficiencies

The trigger, reconstruction, and identification
efficiencies of electrons and muons have been measured in simulation, and
corrected to match the observed efficiencies in data from
\PZ/\HepParticle{\PGg}{}{\ast}\text{+jets}
events,
parameterised as a function of lepton
\pt
and

\eta

[
55
,
49
,
47
]
. The corrections are
derived independently for the selection criteria of interest. The uncertainties
associated with the corrections are propagated to the likelihood model. Except
for the lepton trigger efficiencies, the lepton uncertainties are assumed
correlated across years. However, electron and muon corrections are
uncorrelated with each other, and when different working points are chosen, the
corresponding uncertainties are kept uncorrelated.

Jet calibration

The effects of the jet energy scale (JES) and jet energy
resolution (JER) uncertainties are studied explicitly by varying them by one
standard deviation according to the source of uncertainty, and propagating the
changes to
\ptmiss

[
43
]
. For the signal and the minor
backgrounds, the impact on the simulated yields is studied in the SR as
functions of the pNN output, with a fit procedure to remove the statistical
contributions in the less-populated bins. In total, 27 JES uncertainty sources
are considered for each data-taking year, with partial correlations between the
different data sets for Run 2. The JER uncertainties are uncorrelated between
all data-taking years. For Run 2, there is an additional uncertainty in the jet
pileup identification efficiency.

Jet flavour tagging

The efficiency of the
\PQb
-tagging
algorithm is measured in simulation, and corrected to match the
efficiencies observed in data. These are parameterised as
functions of the jet
\pt
,
\eta
and generator-level
flavour
[
28
]
. This uncertainty is partially
correlated across data-taking years.

Pileup reweighting

Simulated events are weighted to match the distribution of
the number of interactions to the distribution measured in the data. To model the
uncertainty associated with this procedure, the total inelastic
\Pp\Pp
cross
section is varied by
\pm
5%
[
48
]
. This uncertainty is
correlated across data sets.

Level-1 trigger inefficiency

During the 2016 and 2017 data-taking
periods, partial mistiming of signals in the forward region of
the ECAL endcaps (
{2.5<\abs{\eta}<3.0}
) led to a
reduction in the L1 trigger
efficiency
[
53
]
. A separate correction was
determined using an unbiased data sample and applied to
simulated events to reproduce the loss of efficiency. The
uncertainty in the correction is propagated through the
analysis, leading to a maximum 2% effect on the pNN output
shape. This problem was resolved before the 2018 data-taking
period.

Integrated luminosity

The uncertainty in the integrated luminosity is
separated into correlated and uncorrelated contributions across data-taking
years
[
56
,
46
,
50
,
59
]
. Eight
independent sources are identified to take into account the correlations across
Run 2 data sets: an uncorrelated uncertainty of 1.0%, 2.0% and 1.5% for 2016, 2017,
and 2018, respectively; an uncertainty correlated across all years of 0.6%, 0.9%
and 2.0% for 2016, 2017 and 2018, respectively; additional correlated
uncertainties of 0.6% and 0.2% for 2017 and 2018, respectively. For Run 3, the
total uncertainty is 1.4% in 2022. These uncertainties do not affect the

\ttbar
,
\PWm\PWp
,
\PW\PZ
, and
\PZ\PZ
backgrounds which are estimated from
data.

Drell–Yan modelling

No dedicated CR is defined for
\PZ/\HepParticle{\PGg}{}{\ast}\text{+jets}
, which
provides a subdominant contribution in the most sensitive bins of the signal
region. At lower values of pNN output, however, the
\PZ/\HepParticle{\PGg}{}{\ast}\text{+jets}
process becomes
more significant, particularly for lower mass splitting signal samples. The
modelling of this background is checked using a sideband region, which follows
identical selections to the SR, but instead requires
0.8<\mathrm{pNN}(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})<0.9
.
A simultaneous fit to this region and to all other CRs is performed
to extract a normalisation factor for
\PZ/\HepParticle{\PGg}{}{\ast}\text{+jets}
events. This
normalisation factor is found to be generally around 1.25, with an absolute
uncertainty of approximately 0.25. Instead of applying this factor directly to
the SR contribution from the
\PZ/\HepParticle{\PGg}{}{\ast}\text{+jets}
process, a conservative
uncertainty of 75% is assigned to the
\PZ/\HepParticle{\PGg}{}{\ast}\text{+jets}
yields predicted from
simulation, and included as a nuisance parameter in the statistical model. In
addition, to cover the uncertainty in the shape of the
\PZ/\HepParticle{\PGg}{}{\ast}\text{+jets}
background, a
theory systematic uncertainty is defined by using no
corrections, or twice the NLO/LO factor corrections. All Drell–Yan uncertainties are
uncorrelated across the data-taking years.

Parton distribution functions

The uncertainty from the
choice of the PDFs is estimated following the PDF4LHC
recommendations for Run 2
[
30
]
and Run
3
[
16
]
, and embedded into the produced
samples. These uncertainties are propagated using either the
Hessian method or the MC method, depending on the choice of generator.
This uncertainty is correlated across data sets.

Renormalisation and factorisation scales

Theoretical
uncertainties are included to account for the truncation of the
perturbative ME calculations, with higher-order terms not
included. Each scale is varied independently by factors of 2 and
0.5, and the resulting uncertainties are split into uncorrelated
systematic uncertainties for
\PV,\PV\PV,\PV\PV\PV
, and
top-quark processes. This uncertainty is correlated across data sets.

MC statistical uncertainties

The limited size of the simulated samples results
in statistical fluctuations around the nominal prediction. The bin-by-bin
uncertainty is accounted for using the Barlow–Beeston method

[
19
]
. As described in Section
0.6.3
, the uncertainty in
the signal interpolation is covered by the statistical uncertainty of the
samples. For any interpolated signal distribution, the bin-by-bin statistical
uncertainty is given by the average uncertainty of the neighbouring simulated
mass points.

0.9
Results

A binned maximum likelihood fit is performed simultaneously to all SRs
and CRs using the CMS
Combine
package
[
61
]
. The
fit uses inputs from the pNN output distributions in all regions
except the different-flavour ones, for which the dilepton
\pt

distributions provide better discrimination between the
\PW\PW
and

\ttbar
processes. The normalisations of the dominant backgrounds, including MisID events, are
constrained through the simultaneous fit to the CRs. Systematic
uncertainties are incorporated into the likelihood as nuisance
parameters. Uncertainties that only affect the normalisation of a
process are treated as log-normal nuisance parameters. Uncertainties
that affect the normalisation and shape of the distributions are
included through vertical morphing from different shape templates that
represent the
\pm
1 standard deviation variations, and are
incorporated into the likelihood with a Gaussian constraint term. The
full likelihood function is formed by taking the product of the
individual bin likelihoods and the probability density functions of
the nuisance parameters. The likelihood function,
\lumi
, that is
maximised is hence given by:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\mu,\boldsymbol{\theta})=&\prod_{i}\mathrm{P}(d_{i}^{\mathrm{SR}}|B_{i}^{\mathrm{SR}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu S_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\\
&\times\prod_{\mathrm{CR}}\Bigl(\prod_{i}\mathrm{P}(d_{i}^{\mathrm{CR}}|B_{i}^{\mathrm{CR}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\Bigr)\\
&\times\prod_{j}\mathrm{P}(\theta_{j}),\end{split}

(7)

where
{\mathrm{P}(x|y)=y^{x}\re^{-y}/x!}
is the Poisson
distribution, and
d_{i}^{X}
and
B_{i}^{\mathrm{X}}
are the observed number of
events and the expected number of SM events, respectively, in bin
i

of channel
X
. The index
i
runs over all of the bins across each of two signal regions, and eight control regions,
separately for the four data-taking years.
The signal term
S_{i}
represents the expected number of
signal events in bin
i
, and
\mu
denotes the signal strength
parameter. The symbol
\boldsymbol{\theta}
represents the
nuisance parameters that model systematic uncertainties that are
constrained by the Gaussian distributions denoted
\mathrm{P}(\theta_{j})
. The expected
number of SM events,
B_{i}^{\mathrm{X}}
, can be decomposed into the separate
processes:

\begin{split}B_{i}^{\mathrm{X}}=&\mu_{\PW\PW}N_{\PW\PW,\>i}^{\mathrm{X}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu_{\ttbar}N_{\ttbar,\>i}^{\mathrm{X}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu_{\PZ\PZ}N_{\PZ\PZ,\>i}^{\mathrm{X}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\mu_{\PW\PZ}N_{\PW\PZ,\>i}^{\mathrm{X}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\\
&+\mu_{\mathrm{MisID}}N_{\mathrm{MisID},\>i}^{\mathrm{X}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+N_{\text{Other},\>i}^{\mathrm{X}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\end{split}

(8)

where
N_{p,\>i}^{\mathrm{X}}
is the expected number of events for process
p
,

\mu_{\mathrm{p}}
are the parameters that modify the total rate of each process, and
N_{\text{Other},\>i}^{\mathrm{X}}
is the sum of the subdominant processes yields from MC, namely
\PZ/\HepParticle{\PGg}{}{\ast}\text{+jets}
, single top and triboson production.

A maximum likelihood fit to data is performed assuming a signal with
\mu=0
and
allowing all other parameters to float.
The distributions after this fit for mass points

(m_{\PH},m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}})=(70,120)\GeV
and
(70,160)\GeV
are shown in
Figs.

.

0.10
Summary

The Inert Doublet Model predicts additional scalars, including two
neutral scalars
\PH
and
{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}
, which couple only to bosons. The
lightest neutral scalar,
\PH
, is stable and provides a viable dark
matter candidate. The pair production of such new scalars is
investigated in a final state containing two electrons or two
muons. The search is performed using proton-proton collisions at

\sqrt{s}=13\TeV
and
13.6\TeV
, corresponding to integrated luminosities of
138
\fbinv
and 35
\fbinv
, delivered by the LHC and
recorded by the CMS experiment between 2016 and 2018, and in 2022,
respectively. After a preselection to remove the largest standard
model backgrounds, a parameterised neural network is trained to
discriminate the different signal mass points from the remaining
backgrounds. Dedicated control regions for each of the dominant
backgrounds are constructed. A simultaneous fit of the signal region
together with the control regions is used to set 95% confidence level
exclusion limits on the signal production cross section in the
m_{\PH}
vs.

m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}}-m_{\PH}
plane. The observed (expected) exclusion region reaches

m_{\PH}=108\,(106)\GeV
for
m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}}-m_{\PH}=78\,(76)\GeV
and, at
m_{\PH}=70\GeV
,
covers the range of
m_{{\HepParticle{A}{}{}}}-m_{\PH}=40
–90 (35–90)
\GeV
. These results
represent the first limits on the masses of the neutral scalars in the
Inert Doublet Model obtained by a dedicated search using collision
data. These exclusion limits significantly extend the constraints from
previous direct and indirect measurements and dark-matter searches.
