Executive Summary

 In this program, development of a Transient-capable Hydrogen-hybrid for Off-Road (THOR), consisting
of a direct-injection 13L concept engine, a high-capability air system with an electric compressor, and a
series electric drive with battery energy storage was initiated. The program completed planned work up
to the first Go/No-Go milestone prior to termination by the DOE due to policy direction changes. The
completed work (analysis, simulation and single cylinder or small-scale testing) indicated that the goals of
greater than 9% fuel efficiency improvement, equivalent transient load response to the baseline diesel
engine, and U.S. Tier 5 off-road emissions were achievable. Specifically, it was found via simulation that
the system could achieve 10-19% fuel efficiency improvements varying by machine application, and that
diesel-equivalent load response (primarily evaluated by machine cycle productivity) was best achieved
utilizing high-power battery hybridization – this was due to the eBooster requiring substantial energy
which must be generated by the engine even if there is battery energy storage. Tier 5 emissions
compliance was preliminarily analyzed through small scale catalyst H2-specific testing, and subsequent
modeling, with a direction for modular aftertreatment approaches. A range of engine-out emissions were
analyzed with a high confidence plan to produce full-scale demonstration aftertreatment systems. The
program ended with a mature system concept ready to move into the procurement and build phases, but
none of the prototype power system was validated in test and no technoeconomic analysis was
performed.

 Inspecting the tabulated results, it can be seen how the baseline configuration still struggles to meet
the energy/productivity of the reference diesel engine power system with 3.5% and 5.5% deficits. The
H2-ICE only without eBooster or battery hybrid does worse on productivity with 4% and 8% deficits but
has 3% improved efficiency for the excavator. Some insight into these differences is that the eBooster is
using substantial energy which must be generated by the engine if there is no battery storage, and that
the D8 is less transient and steadier of operation than the excavator. Hence, there is more a difference in
productivity and efficiency for the excavator with and without the eBooster.

 The addition of a high-power battery into the system greatly improves the productivity (blue rows in
Table 1) and can effectively perfectly meet the application power demand. The battery system power
required for each application is significant, and is surprisingly proportionally consistent, and more power
is required for the higher power excavator rating. The D8 efficiency was only able to be improved by an
average of 3.9% due to the more steady, already well-aligned operation in high-efficiency engine areas,
and lack of energy braking/recovery. Conversely, the excavator improved greatly to 12.8% on average
exceeding our target of 9% due to the opposite characteristics from the D8. The excavator was able to
take much more advantage of running the engine steady and at lower engine speeds in higher efficiency
zones once sufficient battery power could be quickly dispatched for transient loads. To push both
applications over the 9% goal additional efficiency was required from the core engine. The application of
an over-expanded Miller cycle (green rows in Table 1) was enough to obtain 10.2% and 19.1% efficiency
improvements for the two applications with an average of 14.7%. The Miller cycle strategy generally
lowers airflow, reduces peak power capability, slows transient load response, and increases efficiency.
Despite the inherent Miller cycle productivity detriments, the same power level of battery system was
able to compensate and maintain productivity at excellent levels of <0.4% deficit. Overall, Caterpillar
believes this analysis and concept definition meets or exceeds the 9% efficiency improvement of and H2-
ICE through hybridization.

Tier 5 Emissions Compliance
 The major criteria emissions for U.S. Tier 5 Off-road emissions compliance is NOx and the THOR project
has completed significant catalyst aftertreatment analysis and bench testing to define four emissions
module options, Task 5.6. Updated (for H2 combustion products) catalysts models were exercised over a
range of engine-out NOx conditions to understand possible NOx and N2O tailpipe emissions from the
THOR engine system. The range of NOx was large due to the uncertainty of the THOR H2 combustion rich
limit, choice of transient lambda limit, and air system capability – all of which were planned to be refined
during subsequent single cylinder and multicylinder testing. Knowing this, Caterpillar has ensured a
successful path to Tier 5 emissions though a modular design aligned to a higher than likely NOx range from
1 to 5 g/kWh cycle engine-out emissions (literature, other internal programs, & CFD suggest < 1 g/kWh is
obtainable). The key conclusions to support Tier 5 emissions compliance were as follows:

 • NOx: Only the 3 and 5 g/kWh engine-out NOx conditions exceeded the Tier 5 tailpipe emissions
 limit of 0.04 g/kWh and only for the two module options with the smallest SCR catalysts.
 • N2O: Only engine-out NOx levels greater than 3 g/kWh had problematic N2O make, but this can
 be mitigated with configurations without an oxidation catalyst and low N2O SCR formulations can
 also be explored.

Power System Packaging Feasibility
 Although not officially required for Go/No-Go #1, a packaging feasibility study was completed and
fully documented in Task 2.3 for the D8 dozer and 374 hydraulic excavator applications. The study was
restricted to the engine bay area to focus on the H2-ICE, air and aftertreatment aspects of the hybrid. The
high-level conclusion was that the two-stage Layout 2 air system had significant conflicts fitting into the
machines with the single-stage Layout 1 and eBooster Layout 6 fitting without major issues. The L2 low-
pressure turbo and intercooler were significant conflicts in both machines, but more so in the D8.
Significant machine frame and structure redesigns would likely be required for the L2 air system, which
made the L1 a preferred solution. The upstream eBooster of Layout 6 (L1 + eBooster) was found to work
in multiple locations for each application, but the intercooler with an eBooster would need further
exploration, indicating that only using the eBooster for transient assist would package best without the
need for an intercooler.

configuration. The idea was to optimize the core engine air, fuel, and combustion system to be more

Hybrid System Simulations
 The configurable engine plant model in Dynasty developed was useful in air system selection
simulations. Higher fidelity engine models were in the process of being developed with different air
system configurations. The configurability of the simulation allowed for easy switching between engine
models to compare their performance and assess their hybrid component sizing requirements. The engine
model with an eBooster can have the eBooster power automatically routed to the high voltage DC bus,
making the L5 controller aware of the extra power it needs to supply. The powertrain can be set up as a
battery hybrid or as a series electric without energy storage, and these configurations can then be
compared to determine the benefits of hybridization. The hybrid simulations use advanced optimization
routines residing in the L5 controller (Task 3.1 and 3.2) to determine where to run the engine, and how to
use the battery energy, with the goal of seeking optimal performance based on sets of constraints – this
allowed the series electric hybrid architecture to dynamically respond as boundary conditions and power
sources/sinks changed.
Hybrid System Setup and Creating baseline
 Three air systems have been identified for both the baseline engine and hybrid power system through
1D steady-state and constant-speed Load Acceptance (CSLA) simulations. The priority has been to define
the baseline engine and simulate its fuel consumption using a Simulink hybrid model without a high-
voltage battery. Previously, efforts focused on setting up this model in MATLAB Simulink, enabling
simulations with either non-electric or electric air systems. Two non-electric options feature a two-stage
system with one variable geometry turbo stage (L2) and a single-stage variable geometry turbo (L1). The
electrified air system is the eBooster in upstream configuration (L6). Improvements have streamlined the
model, making it possible to run larger batches for tasks like battery sizing, evaluating air system
performance, and analyzing eBooster power consumption.

 The present engine models were created in Simulink with performance maps generated through the
1D Dynasty steady state simulation, as this was initially a fast way to analyze the hybrid concept
performance tradeoffs. Transient performance dynamic characteristics were captured through CSLA
simulations in Dynasty and numerically added to the Simulink engine model. The engine model was also
able to capture power consumption by eBooster transiently. The eBooster was activated when the base
engine was unable to meet the transient power needs by itself. First, the baseline engine case was
simulated with the three selected air systems.

Catalyst Development for H2 with High Water Concentration
 A test plan was developed to address one of the primary concerns associated with applying
conventional diesel aftertreatment strategy to a lean-burn H2-ICE application, which is the known
inhibition effect that high water concentrations can have on the NOX conversion efficiency of SCR
catalysts. Diesel engines typically produce water concentrations ranging from 3-10% whereas water
concentration in lean-burn H2-ICE applications can vary from 12-25% depending on engine operating
conditions. To address these concerns, kinetic testing of a Cu-SCR catalyst samples was conducted on a
Core Bench Reactor bench with the goal of calibrating a 1D catalyst model that accounts for the impact of
water inhibition of the SCR reaction. The results of the kinetic testing were then utilized to fit a model to
predict NOx conversion at the 15% water concentration, approximately the water concentration
anticipated to be present in the engine exhaust. The accuracy of the model predictions at steady state
conditions at an NO2/NOX ratio of 0 can be found in Figure 27.

 Figure 27: NOX conversion is plotted against the y-axis on the left while the error between the
 experimental and simulation results can be found on the right y-axis.

 Utilizing the SCR model calibration, four different aftertreatment configurations were screened for
suitability by executing a simulation-based screening. Configuration 1 will be referred to as the baseline
aftertreatment configuration that was tested. The main difference between Configuration 1 and 2 is the
size of the SCR brick, which is larger in Configuration 2. Configuration 3 and 4 have varying sizes of SCR

components, and no DOC or DPF components, which may not be required to meet the Tier 5 emission
standards.

 Due to the uncertainty of the NOX levels emitted by the engine, the aftertreatment screening was
designed to be a sensitivity analysis to determine which of the four aftertreatment systems had the best
overall performance at the beginning of the catalyst life. The steady state conditions that were simulated
are based on the output of engine performance simulations. NOX emissions at engine out were varied
between 1-5 g NOX/kW-hr at specific engine conditions to quantify the impact of engine-out NOX
emissions on tailpipe NOX and N2O emissions. The amount of ammonia dosed during these steady state
points was optimized for maximum NOX conversion and minimal N2O emissions.

 The outcome of the aftertreatment simulations is shown in Figure 28, which depicts the tailpipe (TP)
NOX and N2O emissions at the optimized ANR (Ammonia to NOX ratio) and operating temperature for each
steady state condition. Configurations 1 and 4, which both had the smallest SCR components, had steady
state points that exceeded the NOX emission limits when the engine out emissions were 3 g NOX/kWh or
greater at peak torque. However, these same aftertreatment systems were able to meet the proposed
NOX emission requirement of 0.04 g NOX/kWh at all other engine operating conditions that were
simulated. Configurations 3 and 4, which both had the largest SCR catalysts, met the tailpipe NOX emission
requirement under all simulated conditions regardless of the engine out NOX level. Interestingly, it was
found that Configuration 1 and 2, which both included a DOC and DPF, met or exceeded the proposed
Tier 5 N2O emission standards when the engine out emissions were 3 g NOX/kWh or greater at most
operating conditions while Configurations 3 and 4, which did not include a DOC, met the N2O requirement
under all simulated conditions. The DOC in Configuration 1 and 2 increased the ratio of NO2/NOX entering
the SCR component, increasing the Fast SCR reaction and driving up the production of N2O. If a
configuration with a DOC were chosen and the engine emissions were 3 g NOX/kWh or higher, it is possible
that a low-N2O SCR technology would need to be incorporated into the aftertreatment system to stay
below the Tier 5 final regulatory limit. These simulations demonstrate that all four aftertreatment
configurations could provide a solution that meets the Tier 5 emission regulation standards depending on
the emission profile of the engine. The final decision of which aftertreatment configuration is best suited
for this application will ultimately be determined by the NOX emissions generated by the engine.
