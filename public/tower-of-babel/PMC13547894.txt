Hypophosphataemia following iron infusion: a narrative review of an increasingly recognised complication
Authors: Brenda Ta, Stephanie Baddock, Jane Ludington, Angela Sheu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParenteral iron is widely prescribed by medical practitioners, with more than 600 000 outpatient prescriptions dispensed in 2025 through the Australian Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme. While intravenous iron has transformed the management of iron deficiency, hypophosphataemia is now recognised as a common complication, with incidences reported as high as 92% depending on formulation. Although most cases are mild and transient, vulnerable patient groups may develop profound and symptomatic hypophosphataemia, sometimes requiring hospitalisation and intravenous phosphate replacement. The clinical spectrum ranges from subtle biochemical abnormalities to lethargy, weakness and myalgias and, rarely, severe manifestations including muscle weakness, osteomalacia, myopathy, arrhythmias and cardiomyopathy. This narrative review presents five illustrative cases, highlighting the varied presentations of iron‐induced hypophosphataemia and potential patient groups at high risk. To address this growing challenge, we present an institutionally endorsed management pathway based on our review of the literature and current practice after multidisciplinary consultation. The algorithm emphasises early recognition of high‐risk patients, primary prevention by using formulations of iron associated with the lowest risk of hypophosphataemia, timely identification of symptomatic hypophosphataemia and coordinated correction of concurrent electrolyte disturbances, with the aim of improving patient safety and outcomes.Keywords: ferric carboxymaltose, FGF23, fibroblast growth factor 23, hypophosphataemia, hypophosphatemia, intravenous iron, iron infusion, iron therapy, osteomalacia, phosphate homeostasis, urinary phosphate wasting

Introduction

Iron deficiency is common worldwide, and although oral formulations are recommended first line, poor absorption and tolerability limit their use. 1 , 2 Intravenous infusions, which exist in multiple formulations, negate this problem. 1 , 2 Available formulations in Australia (percentage of prescriptions as recorded through the pharmaceutical benefits scheme in 2024) include ferric carboxymaltose (FCM; Ferinject®, 91%), ferric derisomaltose (FDI; Monofer®, 6.28%), iron polymaltose (IPM; Ferrosig®, 2.10%) and iron sucrose (IS; Venofer®, 0.6%). 3 , 4 Newer formulations of intravenous iron such as FCM and FDI are increasingly preferred in the outpatient setting due to greater stability and ability to infuse higher doses with lower rates of hypersensitivity reactions. 5 , 6

Hypophosphataemia following intravenous iron infusion has a reported incidence of 43.5% to 92%, with a nadir at 2 weeks and persistence to 90 days following the infusion. 7 , 8 , 9 Hypophosphataemia is often transient, self‐limiting and clinically insignificant. However, some cases may be severe and present with significant symptoms requiring hospitalisation for intravenous treatment and monitoring. The severity of hypophosphataemia can be categorised as mild (0.5–0.8 mmol/L; 1.8–2.5 mg/dL), moderate (0.3–0.5 mmol/L; 1.0–1.7 mg/dL) and severe (<0.3 mmol/L; 0.9 mg/dL). 10 Clinical presentations vary from asymptomatic to fatigue, bone pain, generalised weakness and, in severe cases, myopathy, cardiac arrhythmias and respiratory failure. 11 , 12 This narrative review aims to illustrate the varied clinical presentations of hypophosphataemia following intravenous iron infusions through five representative cases and to synthesise the current literature to define the scope, risk factors and underlying pathophysiology of this condition. We also present an institutionally endorsed management guideline, developed from this review and current clinical practice, to support prevention and management of clinically significant hypophosphataemia.

Methods

A literature search was initially performed using the PubMed database to identify original research articles (including clinical trials, observational studies and case reports) and review articles relevant to hypophosphataemia following intravenous iron therapy, restricted to the English language. The search aimed to capture evidence on pathophysiology, incidence, formulation‐specific risk, clinical consequences and management strategies. Key search terms included ‘intravenous iron infusion’, ‘hypophosphataemia’, ‘phosphate’, ‘ferric carboxymaltose’, ‘ferric derisomaltose’, ‘iron polymaltose’, ‘iron sucrose’ and ‘osteomalacia’. Reference lists of included articles were also screened to identify additional relevant publications. No formal restrictions on publication date were applied. The initial search was conducted between December 2024 and February 2025, with an updated search performed in April 2026 to capture recent studies. Case material was obtained through review of hospital medical records.

Epidemiology

Reported incidences of hypophosphataemia vary according to the specific iron preparation used. Rates are highest with FCM (38.7%–92.1%) and significantly lower (<10%) in other preparations. 8 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 The highest reported rate of hypophosphataemia following FCM (92.1%) was in a Japanese cohort of 39 participants, all with malabsorptive diseases. 7 A recently published retrospective Australian study examined the prevalence and natural history of hypophosphataemia following all iron infusions in one tertiary centre. 9 Following 7063 infusions (including 4990 IPM and 1320 FCM) the risk of hypophosphataemia was highest following IPM (OR 2.33), followed by FCM (OR 1.79) compared to FDI. In two randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comparing FCM and FDI in 245 individuals, the incidence of hypophosphataemia was 74% and 8% respectively, and severe hypophosphataemia (serum phosphate <0.32 mmol/L) only occurred with FCM (11%). 8 Specifically in a RCT comparing FCM and FDI in 97 individuals with inflammatory bowel disease, the hypophosphataemia incidence following FCM was higher (51% vs 8.3%). 18

Risk factors for iron infusion‐induced hypophosphataemia include vitamin D deficiency, recurrent blood loss, severe iron deficiency, malabsorptive disorders (including inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease and post‐bariatric surgery), normal renal function, low body weight, higher serum parathyroid hormone and low baseline phosphate levels. 2 , 14 Patients with these characteristics are at particular risk of developing severe and refractory hypophosphataemia. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 While impaired renal function and consequent phosphate retention are generally protective, hypophosphataemia may still occur in patients with kidney disease. 24 Repeated infusions further increase risk and are a well‐recognised contributor with a 48% higher likelihood of hypophosphataemia (<0.6 mmol/L). 9

In addition to these established risk factors, the impact of concurrent antiresorptive therapy is increasingly recognised. Multiple case reports describe a heightened risk of hypophosphataemia and hypocalcaemia with combined use of antiresorptive agents and iron infusion due to decreased mobilisation of calcium and phosphate from bone. 12 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 Given widespread prescription of potent antiresorptives such as denosumab in the community, greater awareness of this interaction is crucial.

Pathophysiology

Serum phosphate is largely maintained by balancing intestinal uptake with renal phosphate excretion. Regulatory hormones include fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23), 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D and parathyroid hormone (PTH). 29 FGF23 reduces renal phosphate reabsorption by downregulating sodium‐dependent phosphate transporters (NPT2a and NPT2c). 29 , 30

In iron deficiency, FGF23 transcription is stimulated, but levels of intact FGF23 (iFGF23) and serum phosphate are steadily maintained by enhanced cleavage of FGF23 to inactive fragments. 2 , 31 , 32 Intravenous iron has been proposed to inhibit this cleavage process, leading to increased iFGF23 and therefore renal phosphate wasting and hypophosphataemia (Fig. 1.). 2 , 6 , 24 , 32 As baseline FGF23 is already elevated in iron deficiency, hyperphosphaturia can be profound. In a study of FCM in 25 subjects, iFGF23 levels increased significantly within 1 day and the degree of elevation correlated with the magnitude of hypophosphataemia on day 7. 32 Similarly, in eight subjects receiving IPM, FGF23 levels negatively correlated with serum phosphate and 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D. 33

Increased iFGF23 also reduces 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D resulting in reduced intestinal phosphate and calcium absorption. The resulting mild hypocalcaemia stimulates parathyroid hormone driving secondary hyperparathyroidism and further phosphaturia, 29 which can lead to persistent hypophosphataemia even after normalisation of FGF23 levels. 2 , 8 Together this cascade results in six abnormalities, referred to as the 6H syndrome: hyperphosphaturic hypophosphataemia, high FGF23, hypovitaminosis D, hypocalcaemia and secondary hyperparathyroidism. 34 Management of hypophosphataemia therefore requires simultaneous correction of all concurrent abnormalities.

The variable presentation of hypophosphataemia between iron formulations is likely due to differences in the carbohydrate moieties surrounding the iron core. 8 Various carbohydrate moieties have been developed to facilitate safer administration of iron and reduce the risk of toxicities and hypersensitivity reactions. 6 FCM and IPM share similar moieties with 1,4‐O‐glycosidic bonds, and both cause high rates of hypophosphataemia, whereas FDI differs with a 1,6‐O‐glycosidic bond. 9 , 35 This difference could potentially explain the lower rates of hypophosphataemia with FDI.

Clinical manifestations and diagnosis

Phosphate is critical for intracellular signalling, energy metabolism, bone mineralisation and enzymatic function. 16 Common symptoms include generalised weakness, fatigue, bone pain and myalgias occurring across the spectrum of hypophosphataemia. 11 Some of these symptoms overlap with iron deficiency anaemia and may not be identified as a complication of treatment. Severe hypophosphataemia can cause cardiac arrhythmias, metabolic encephalopathy, rhabdomyolysis, impaired cardiac contractility and respiratory failure, although these manifestations are rare with iron infusion‐induced hypophosphataemia and primarily described in case reports. 11 , 12 Fracture risk appears elevated by two‐fold after one dose of FCM compared to FDI, and osteomalacia has been reported with chronic hypophosphataemia persisting for more than 12 months in patients receiving repeated doses of parenteral iron. 36 , 37 , 38

Despite the high incidence of hypophosphataemia now reported in the literature, particularly following FCM, symptoms attributed to hypophosphataemia were rarely reported in initial drug trials. 2 This suggests that for many, hypophosphataemia is asymptomatic and self‐limiting and may be an incidental finding on pathology testing. The severity of biochemical abnormalities does not always correlate with symptom severity. The clinical significance of moderate to severe hypophosphataemia in otherwise asymptomatic healthy adults (such as Patient 5) is uncertain due to a paucity of published experience in this population. A small number of studies identified correlations between hypophosphataemia and increased risk of falls 39 and respiratory infections, 40 likely through muscular weakness.

Once hypophosphataemia is identified, further evaluation may be undertaken to confirm the diagnosis of infusion‐induced hypophosphataemia. This includes assessment for renal phosphate wasting and concurrent electrolyte abnormalities by measuring serum ionised calcium, parathyroid hormone, 25‐hydroxyvitamin D and alkaline phosphatase and calculating the tubular phosphate reabsorption (TmP/GFR) from a spot urinary collection. Absolute urinary phosphate levels may be low, hence TmP/GFR is preferred, which takes into account urinary and plasma phosphate and creatinine levels collected at the same time. The urinary sample should be collected from the second void of the day to avoid spuriously elevated urinary phosphate levels associated with overnight collections. Low TmP/GFR in the context of hypophosphataemia is consistent with inappropriate renal phosphate wasting. This evaluation is particularly relevant in patients with moderate to severe or persistent hypophosphataemia. Both 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D and FGF23 can be measured, though these are limited in commercial laboratories, and results can be delayed. Chronic or unexplained cases of hypophosphataemia warrant exclusion of other causes (e.g. Fanconi's syndrome, tumour‐induced osteomalacia). In patients suspected to have osteomalacia (e.g. persistent bone pain or fractures after recurrent iron infusions or hypophosphataemia >12 months), x‐ray, bone scintigraphy and bone mineral density should be considered. 34

Natural history/prognosis

The natural history of hypophosphataemia following iron infusion is poorly defined. Longitudinal studies suggest a nadir at 1 to 2 weeks after infusion, observed across different iron formulations and consistent with the pattern seen in our small series of hospitalised patients receiving IPM. 8 , 9 , 41 , 42 However, the severity and duration of hypophosphataemia vary widely according to retrospective studies and case reports, with most data derived from FCM.

Although often self‐limiting, hypophosphataemia may persist for weeks, particularly following FCM. The PHOSPHARE trials demonstrated not only a higher incidence of hypophosphataemia with FCM than FDI but also more prolonged hypophosphataemia at day 35 (43% vs 0.9%). 8 Similarly, among patients with inflammatory bowel disease, 13.7% of patients who received FCM had sustained hypophosphataemia (<0.64 mmol/L) at 6 weeks compared to 1.9% in the FDI group. 41 Prolonged hypophosphataemia exceeding 2 months has also been reported, with some patients requiring oral phosphate supplementation for up to 6 months. 43 , 44

Although less well studied, IPM appears equally relevant. A single‐centre review found that over 10% of patients who received either IPM or FCM had persistent hypophosphataemia (<0.64 mmol/L) at 90 days. 9 The clinical impact of IPM‐related hypophosphataemia may be under‐recognised as IPM is often used in hospital settings due to lower costs, and electrolyte abnormalities may be attributed to comorbid illness or malnutrition. As demonstrated in our series, IPM resulted in significant hypophosphataemia across a heterogeneous population, including patients without identifiable risk factors, highlighting the importance of considering recent iron exposure in the medical history.

Treatment

Management of iron infusion‐induced hypophosphataemia is largely extrapolated from case reports and treatment of other renal phosphate wasting disorders such as X‐linked hypophosphataemia (XLH).

Oral phosphate replacement, typically as sodium dihydrogen phosphate, is suitable for symptomatic patients with mild to moderate hypophosphataemia. Gastrointestinal intolerance with abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea is common and may limit adherence. Intestinal phosphate absorption may be further limited by decreased levels of 1,25 dihydroxy vitamin D secondary to high iFGF‐23 levels. To minimise precipitation of salts and impaired absorption, oral phosphate should be administered separately from elemental calcium and magnesium. We recommend initiating oral elemental phosphate 500 mg (mixed with juice) twice a day, titrated every 3–4 days according to serum phosphate levels and tolerability. Concurrent hypocalcaemia should be treated with oral elemental calcium (600 mg as calcium carbonate or 333 mg as calcium citrate twice daily), administered at least 2 h after oral phosphate. Hypomagnesemia should also be corrected, as it may contribute to PTH resistance.

Intravenous phosphate replacement is reserved for patients with severe or symptomatic hypophosphataemia, critically low serum phosphate levels or intolerance of oral therapy. Standard infusion protocols generally recommend a maximum replacement rate of 10 mmol/h due to the risk of hypocalcaemia. However, higher rates up to 15–20 mmol/h have been shown to be safe in intensive care settings. 45 Estimation of the total body phosphate deficit is challenging, and recurrence is common due to ongoing renal phosphate wasting. Thus, combined intravenous and oral replacement of phosphate and associated electrolytes is often required.

Monotherapy with phosphate replacement is not recommended for moderate to severe cases as it can worsen hypocalcaemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism and phosphaturia. 30 In these cases, the addition of oral calcitriol (1,25‐hydroxyvitamin D analogue) inhibits parathyroid hormone and enhances gastrointestinal absorption and renal reabsorption of phosphate and calcium. Calcitriol should be commenced at 0.25 mcg once or twice daily and titrated every 2–3 days to achieve normal phosphate levels. In our case series, doses up to 0.75 mcg twice daily were used without inducing hypercalcaemia or hypercalciuria. If hypercalcaemia emerges, elemental calcium should be reduced first, followed by calcitriol if required.

Burosumab is a recombinant human monoclonal antibody targeting FGF23, thereby directly inhibiting FGF‐23‐mediated phosphaturia. It was approved in Australia in 2021 for the treatment of XLH in which a loss of function mutation in the PHEX gene causes excessive levels of FGF23. In XLH, burosumab has been shown to effectively increase serum phosphate, reduce pain, improve function and promote healing of pseudofractures secondary to osteomalacia. 46 Burosumab can be associated with arthralgias, gastrointestinal intolerance and injection site reactions. Due to its potent and immediate effect, phosphate replacement and calcitriol should be withheld for 1 week prior to burosumab administration to minimise the risk of hyperphosphataemia.

Given its inhibitory effect on FGF23, a single dose of burosumab was used successfully in Patient 1, who developed persistent, refractory and severe hypophosphataemia following IPM. This occurred in the context of prior exposure to FCM 6 months earlier, which had also resulted in hypophosphataemia requiring intravenous phosphate replacement and 3 months of oral supplementation. Following IPM, the patient required two hospital admissions for intravenous phosphate replacement (up to 80 mmol every 24 h). Despite initial stabilisation, ongoing second daily outpatient intravenous phosphate in addition to high‐dose calcitriol and phosphate was necessary to maintain serum phosphate above 0.6 mmol/L. Moderate hypophosphataemia persisted for 5 weeks until a single 20‐mg (0.3 mg/kg) dose of burosumab was administered with informed consent (approved on an individual patient basis by the Drug and Therapeutics Committee). The pre‐treatment phosphate level of 0.47 mmol/L normalised within 5 days with sustained improvement following this single dose, without the need for further calcitriol or phosphate supplementation. The patient subsequently received FDI 13 months later without recurrence of hypophosphataemia.

There are two previously published reports of burosumab treatment for persistent hypophosphataemia and pseudofractures following FCM resulting in improvement in symptoms, BMD and HRpQCT. 37 , 47 While burosumab effectively targets the underlying mechanism of iron infusion‐induced hypophosphataemia, the high cost precludes routine use and there is no approved indication at present. Heightened recognition of the risk of hypophosphataemia with iron infusions and avoidance of certain formulations in high‐risk populations would be more cost‐effective and sustainable as a means of primary prevention.

Recommendations

A consensus statement published in 2025 focused on risk assessment following FCM and formal guidelines for the management of hypophosphataemia following any iron infusion are currently lacking. 48 In high‐risk patients (e.g. history of malabsorption, malnutrition, concurrent antiresorptive therapy, repeated doses of iron infusion over 6 months, low body weight, vitamin D deficiency, high PTH and/or low baseline serum phosphate), we strongly recommend primary prevention by choosing iron formulations with lower incidence of hypophosphataemia.

Consistent evidence supports lower incidence, severity and duration of hypophosphataemia with FDI compared to IPM and FCM. 8 , 9 Despite its availability since 2017 and similar PBS criteria and costs ($285.59, as of April 2025), FDI accounted for just 6.3% of prescriptions in 2024, compared to 91% for FCM. 4 Importantly, there are no known moderate or severe adverse effects specifically attributed to FDI. 8 , 49 Both FCM and FDI are associated with low rates of severe hypersensitivity reactions; however, retrospective data suggest FDI may have higher rates of mild to moderate reactions, including Fishbane‐type reactions. 50 The difference in uptake between FCM and FDI may reflect earlier approval and greater clinician familiarity with FCM, as well as variability in institutional formulary availability. Increasing awareness of formulation‐specific risk profiles may support more tailored prescribing, particularly in patients at higher risk of hypophosphataemia.

The following protocol for the identification and management of hypophosphataemia following intravenous iron has been developed and approved by our institution following our review of the literature and multidisciplinary consultation (Fig. 2). Although hypophosphataemia is common, most cases are mild and self‐limiting and do not require treatment. We recommend a risk‐stratified approach to monitoring serum phosphate in patients receiving intravenous iron, with targeted screening in those at high risk of clinically significant or protracted hypophosphataemia (e.g. history of malabsorption, malnutrition, concurrent antiresorptive therapy, repeated doses of iron infusion over 6 months, low body weight, vitamin D deficiency, high PTH and/or low baseline serum phosphate). Routine screening of all patients may not be feasible. Oral treatment with phosphate and calcitriol is usually sufficient; however, patients with potentially life‐threatening hypophosphataemia (<0.3 mmol/L) or significant symptoms should be referred to the emergency department for assessment and consideration of intravenous phosphate replacement. Cardiac monitoring should be considered as per local hospital protocol.

Most cases of hypophosphataemia will resolve over 2–4 weeks, and oral phosphate and calcitriol should be weaned according to serum phosphate levels. Patients with concurrent antiresorptive therapy, multiple doses of parenteral iron or significant malabsorptive issues may require extended monitoring (e.g. 6–8 weeks as seen in our case series). In severe cases, phosphate supplementation and calcitriol may be required for prolonged periods, including months to years, as illustrated in case reports. 51 , 52 Notably, the presence of antiresorptive therapy or repeated parenteral iron dosing has been associated with an increased risk and severity of hypocalcaemia. 27

In the longer term, addressing the underlying cause of iron deficiency anaemia is paramount as hypophosphataemia may recur with subsequent parenteral iron treatments. If oral iron replacement is not tolerated, preference should be given to iron infusions with the lowest risk of hypophosphataemia.

Patients who are due to receive denosumab within 4 weeks of receiving parenteral iron require close monitoring of serum electrolytes. It has been suggested to monitor serum calcium and phosphate at 7, 14 and 28 days following co‐administration. 27 We strongly advise against delay or abrupt cessation of denosumab due to well documented concerns of rebound osteoclastic activity, bone density loss and multiple vertebral fractures.

Conclusion

Intravenous iron‐induced hypophosphataemia is common and, although often mild and asymptomatic, can have a significant impact, resulting in severe symptoms and hospitalisation. This has been shown to prolong hospital stays, which contributes to patient morbidity and adds to the burden of an already stretched healthcare system. 6 The clinical presentation varies widely according to the iron preparation, with more severe and persistent hypophosphataemia occurring following ferric carboxymaltose (Ferinject®) and iron polymaltose (Ferrosig®). Prevention of clinically relevant hypophosphataemia may be possible with the selected use of alternative formulations such as ferric derisomaltose (Monofer®).

We have presented five illustrative cases of hypophosphataemia following intravenous iron, highlighting potential risk factors. Increased vigilance of this complication is required, particularly in patients receiving frequent parenteral iron, or with malabsorption, malnutrition or concurrent antiresorptive therapy.

Given the limited availability of formal guidelines for the identification and management of hypophosphataemia following an iron infusion, we have synthesised current evidence from the literature alongside these clinical cases to present a practical, institutionally endorsed management algorithm. This approach emphasises early recognition of high‐risk patients, identification of symptomatic hypophosphataemia and coordinated management of concurrent electrolyte abnormalities.

As hypophosphataemia may be identified and managed by different clinicians than the prescriber of iron therapy, careful medication history is required to identify the iron as the causative agent. With parenteral iron increasingly used across inpatient and outpatient settings, all physicians should be alert to this common yet often overlooked complication.
