Lattice boltzmann method for investigation of flow through square in-line cylinders with transverse oscillation

ABSTRACT
A numerical research work is carried out to examine fluid–structure interaction in flow over six inline square cylinders undergoing transverse oscillations via the lattice Boltzmann method. Simulations are performed at Reynolds number of Re = 80, with a non-dimensional gap spacing (s/d = 1.0) and an oscillation amplitude ratio of A/d = 0.2. Introduction

Flow over multiple bluff bodies organized in proximity is one of the utmost substantial problems in structural and thermal engineering, with direct importance to systems such as heat exchanger tube banks, bridge piers, offshore risers, overhead transmission cables, chimney stacks, and wind-screen filters1,2. When numerous cylinders are positioned in an inline (tandem) configuration, the wake shed by each upstream body alters the approach flow experienced by all downstream bodies, which generates complex time-varying hydrodynamic forces via wake interference1,3. This complexity increases noticeably when the cylinders are not stationary but perform prescribed oscillations, as the coupling between vortex shedding, cylinder motion, and inter-cylinder wake interaction leads to a rich ladder of flow phenomena. This includes lock-on, quasi-periodic behavior, and chaotic dynamics, that cannot be characterized by single-body or stationary-cylinder analyses alone4,5. The main parameters for an inline oscillating cylinder configuration comprise the Reynolds number (Re), spacing ratio (s/d, where s is the face-to-face distance and d the cylinder dimension), oscillation amplitude ratio (A/d), normalized oscillation frequency (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{e}/{f}_{o}$$\end{document}), and the phase of oscillation1,6. The current research work continues the stationary inline-cylinder regime map of Sewatkar et al.1 by introducing transverse cylinder oscillations, with the goal of investigating whether the four flow patterns recognized for stationary cylinders persist, alter, or give way to new regimes under oscillatory forcing, and of quantifying the impact of oscillation frequency on lift coefficient (CL), drag coefficient (Cd), and Strouhal number (St) for every cylinder in the configuration.

The transversely oscillating single cylinder has been explored broadly4–6 and delivers the fundamental reference for recognizing more complex multi-cylinder arrangements. Bishop and Hassan4 were among the first to demonstrate that when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{e}$$\end{document} tends to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{o}$$\end{document}, a synchronization (commonly termed lock-on) happens over a finite range of excitation frequencies, and that within this range the lift force and cylinder displacement undergo a near 180° phase shift accompanied by hysteresis. Tanida et al.5 established the presence of a vital synchronisation range over which force coefficients achieve their maximum values, and furthermore described that lock-on for in-line oscillations arises at excitation frequencies about double the natural shedding frequency. Bearman and Obasaju6 established that the width of the synchronisation range improves monotonically with oscillation amplitude. The vortex-wake topology in the lock-on regime was methodically categorized by Williamson and Roshko7 for A/d = 0.2–5 and Re up to several thousand, which leads three canonical shedding modes namely, the symmetric Kármán mode (2 S), the symmetric paired mode (2P), and the asymmetric mixed mode (P + S). In mathematical research works, Singh et al.8 found that the synchronisation range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{e}/{f}_{o}$$\end{document} = 0.8 to 1.2 for Re = 100 is reliable with the investigational outcomes of Tanida et al.5 at Re = 80. Placzek18 developed a prominent agreement with previous investigational outcomes in a numerical simulation of transverse oscillation at Re = 100, which further link examined frequency responses to detailed vortex shedding modes. Most currently, Zhang et al.23 utilized Floquet stability analysis to validate that as oscillation amplitude upsurges, the mean recirculation region shrinks and force coefficients increase, with 3D instability thresholds separating into distinct amplitude stages (0 ≤ A/D< 0.3, 0.3–0.4, and 0.4–0.7). Chauhan et al.24 investigated through an experiment that improving forcing frequency lowers the recirculation length and Cd, while also bringing vortices nearer to the cylinder surface.

For two cylinders in tandem, both stationary and oscillating arrangements have been examined. In case of stationary configurations, Tanida et al.5 observed that at small spacings (s/d < 5) the changing lift on the upstream cylinder is closely suppressed because of the downstream cylinder prevents upstream vortex shedding, while the downstream cylinder experiences fluctuating lift amplitudes eight to ten times more than the upstream body. Haniu et al.10 found two separate flow regimes above and below an optimum spacing of s/d = 3 for square cylinders at Re = 26,700 and 55,200. In this each regime generating various Strouhal numbers and mean drag coefficient (Cd). Liu and Chen11 reported spacing-needy hysteresis between two flow modes for Re = 2,000–16,000. At a small spacing’s (Mode 1) the upstream shear layer roll-up is suppressed and no coherent vortex forms in the gap, where at larger spacing’s (Mode 2) the shear layer undergoes intermittent roll-up and the subsequent vortices interact with the downstream cylinder to change its wake dynamics considerably. The sudden transition between these modes gives rise to the observed discontinuous jumps in force coefficients. When oscillation is created, the interaction becomes stronger. Mahir and Rockwell12 showed through an experiment that at phase angles of 0° and 180°, two tandem oscillating circular cylinders can generate locked-on patterned vortices across a range of excitation frequencies. Jester and Kallinderis13 provided numerically that the phase angle of the two cylinders directs wake formation and that in-phase oscillation drives the wake into an anti-symmetric shedding mode. Zhang25 determined for Re = 100 and gap ratios L/D = 2 to 7 that oscillation amplitude and gap interact to govern fluid forces. At small gaps the upstream cylinder’s lift leads, while at large gaps the relationship reverse. Mithun26 further confirmed that the time-based wake appearances of two inline square cylinders executing transverse oscillations with a phase difference depend sensitively on the inter-cylinder spacing and the ratio \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{e}/{f}_{o}$$\end{document}, with calculations accomplished at Re = 100 and A/d = 0.4D. Lee et al.27 proven for Re = 185 and A/d = 0.2 that force coefficient signals demonstrate modulation that alters with both oscillation frequency and spacing, while Xu et al.31 described that streamwise cylinder vibration can regularize vortex shedding into lock-on patterns at specific spacing ratios.

Studies involving more than two inline cylinders in oscillatory flow are comparatively scarce, yet they reveal phenomena qualitatively distinct from those observed in one- and two-cylinder systems. Harichandan and Roy19 studied three inline circular cylinders at low Re (100 and 200) for s/d = 2 and 5, finding sparse von Kármán vortex streets at Re = 200 and continuous wake patterns at Re = 100 for the tandem arrangement at s/d = 2; at s/d = 5, impingement of vortex streets shed from upstream and intermediate cylinders significantly disrupted shedding from the downstream body, and FFT analysis confirmed alike shedding frequencies for all three cylinders. Soufi and Sabeur17 examined numerically the two-dimensional flow past a circular cylinder under combined in-line oscillation and unidirectional current, demonstrating that lock-on can be achieved at modest current-to-oscillation velocity ratios of 0 to 0.4. Nguyen et al.21 reviewed confined-flow phenomena for a circular cylinder between parallel plates, highlighting the role of blockage ratio in triggering two-to-three-dimensional flow transitions, the analogy of separated shear layers to mixing layers, and the emergence of multiple vortex shedding modes. Jian Wu et al.22 investigated oscillation-amplitude effects on a square cylinder at Re = 2.2 × 104 using the k–ω SST turbulence model with an ALE dynamic mesh, finding that higher amplitudes transfer more energy from cylinder to fluid and produce pronounced non-Gaussian distributions of fluctuating surface pressure, particularly at the trailing-edge corners. The most directly relevant multi-cylinder oscillating study is that of Sewatkar et al.20, who quantitatively examined flow past a row of transversely oscillating inline cylinders across a systematic range of s/d, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{e}/{f}_{o}$$\end{document}, Re, and A/d. Six distinct flow patterns were identified — synchronous lock-on, synchronous non-lock-on, quasi-periodic lock-on, quasi-periodic non-lock-on-I, quasi-periodic non-lock-on-II, and chaotic non-lock-on — and the study found that increasing A/d, Re, s/d, or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{e}/{f}_{o}$$\end{document} progressively diminishes wake interaction behind the cylinders, with higher oscillation frequencies introducing additional spectral peaks into the CL signal.

The most directly relevant antecedent to the present investigation is the comprehensive stationary-cylinder study of Sewatkar et al.1, who examined flow across six inline fixed square cylinders for Re = 80–320 and 0.5 ≤ s/d ≤ 10. For Re = 100, four fundamental flow regimes were identified as a function of spacing: synchronous (s/d ≤ 1.1), quasi-periodic-I (1.2 ≤ s/d ≤ 1.3), quasi-periodic-II (1.4 ≤ s/d ≤ 5.0), and chaotic (s/d ≥ 6.0). The synchronous pattern, characterized by a single dominant frequency in the CL spectrum, was attributed to a single coherent wake behind each cylinder, whereas the quasi-periodic and chaotic patterns arose from the growth and shedding of vortices in the inter-cylinder spaces. Notably, negative drag was observed on the second and third cylinders for s/d < 2.0, and the RMS lift coefficient (CL, rms) of the third and second cylinders approached unity for s/d = 3.5, while the fourth cylinder’s CL, rms nearly vanished for s/d = 0.5. For spacing ratios s/d = 2.5–6.0, abrupt changes in CL, rms of the 4th, 5th, and 6th cylinders were found to be consistent with corresponding changes in Cd. These stationary results provide both the benchmark and the direct motivation for the present work: it remains unknown whether these four regimes persist when the cylinders are set into transverse oscillation, and how the introduction of oscillation frequency as an additional parameter modifies the CL, Cd, and St characteristics of each cylinder in the array. Related additional contributions on oscillating cylinder dynamics from Yang et al.28, Krishna29, Jester and Kallinderis30, and Munir et al.14 and Taheri et al.15 have further established the sensitivity of wake formation to oscillation phase and amplitude, reinforcing that the stationary-cylinder flow map cannot be simply extended to oscillating configurations without direct numerical investigation. Griffin and Ramberg9 additionally demonstrated that increasing oscillation amplitude reduces the lateral spacing of vortices, a suppression mechanism with direct implications for inter-cylinder interference in a multi-body arrangement.

The above-mentioned review identifies a clear gap in the literature: while Sewatkar et al.1 confirmed a comprehensive flow-regime map for six inline stationary square cylinders, and Sewatkar et al.20 examined oscillating cylinder rows, the specific question of whether and how the four stationary flow regimes (synchronous, quasi-periodic-I, quasi-periodic-II, and chaotic) are preserved, modified, or replaced when the same six-cylinder inline arrangement is subjected to transverse oscillations of varying frequency has not been addressed. This is a consequential gap: the oscillation frequency governs the lock-on state, wake interference topology, and spectral content of fluid forces in ways that cannot be inferred from either the stationary multi-cylinder map1 or the oscillating two-cylinder studies12,13,25, as nonlinear wake interference in a six-body arrangement introduces interactions that are qualitatively different from those in a two-cylinder system. The present study directly addresses this gap with a systematic two-dimensional numerical investigation of flow past six inline transversely oscillating square cylinders, examining the effects of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{e}/{f}_{o}$$\end{document} on flow patterns, CL, Cd, and St across a range of spacing ratios and at Re = 80.

Novelty of the research Work

The original contribution of the current study is the systematic numerical analysis of flow around a range of transversely oscillating square multi-cylinders, a subject that has gained relatively little attention in the current literature (most of the research works are limited to single- and two-cylinder arrangements). Here, the lattice Boltzmann method is employed to study the flow past an array of six in-line square cylinders with varying oscillation frequency ratios (0.1 < \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{e}/{f}_{o}$$\end{document} ≤ 2.0), and the characteristics of wake flow, vortex shedding and aerodynamic forces are examined in detail. This study identifies and categorizes different flow patterns, including quasi-periodic non-lock-on, lock-on and quasi-periodic lock-on, and shows that the lock-on range is broader than that of single cylinders due to greater wake interaction. It also shows the presence of a connected wake structure around the entire array of cylinders and multipolar vortex formation at higher oscillation frequencies. The distributions of Cd and CL along the array are also discussed and show different force distributions between upstream and downstream cylinders. In summary, this comprehensive study employing the lattice Boltzmann framework sheds new light on fluid-structure interactions in complex multi-body oscillating systems and fills a gap in the literature.

Numerical and physical problem descriptions

The physical arrangement under examination comprises of six identical square cylinders organized in an inline (tandem) arrangement and exposed to transverse oscillations in a 2D uniform cross-flow, as shown in Fig. 1. The streamwise and transverse coordinates are denoted by x and y, respectively. Each cylinder has the same side length d, which serves as the characteristic length scale of the problem, and the face-to-face spacing between any two successive cylinders is uniform and equal to s. The flow approaches the leading cylinder with a constant and uniform free-stream velocity U0.

The selection of six cylinders as the representative array size for the current research work is based on the systematic domain study documented by Sewatkar et al.1, who examined the influence of the number of cylinders on the Cd for inline stationary square cylinders at s/d = 4.0 and Re = 80. In that study, computations were performed for arrays of 4, 5, 6 7, 8, 9, and 10 cylinders, and it was demonstrated that the Cd of the individual cylinders remains essentially unchanged when six or more cylinders are existing in the field. Specifically, consistent results were obtained for arrays of four to six cylinders, confirming that a six-cylinder configuration is the minimum arrangement that adequately represents the fully developed inline cylinder interaction. On the basis of this finding, the number of cylinders is set to six in the current research work, which is reliable with the approach adopted by Sewatkar et al.1 and Sewatkar et al.20.

The computational domain is discretized using a uniform Cartesian lattice, as per the standard lattice Boltzmann framework. The lattice spacing (Δx) and time step (Δt) are constant throughout the domain, which make sure uniform grid resolution. The grid density is controlled by providing the number of lattice nodes along each side of the square cylinder. No grid stretching or non-uniform meshing is used in the current work.

The local mass density \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\rho\:$$\end{document} and local momentum density \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\rho\:u$$\end{document} at the node are calculated from the following equations:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\rho\:=\sum\:{f}_{i}$$\end{document}8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\rho\:u=\sum\:{f}_{i}{e}_{i}$$\end{document}

The solution of Eq. (1) is accomplished by means of collision (BGK collision operator) and propagation (Eqs. 9 and 10). Mathematically, the solution of Eq. (1), obtained by the above method, has been shown to recover the Navier-Stokes equations when the lattice structure has the required symmetry. The lattice Boltzmann algorithm used in this work has a second-order accuracy. Equation (5) is the governing equation in an inertial frame of reference. The term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{i}\frac{\delta\:V}{\delta\:t}$$\end{document} in Eq. Data Reduction Techniques

Results for velocity and pressure fields over the computational domain are obtained using LBM based code. Additional parameters such as coefficient of drag and lift, its mean and rms values, St, and vorticity etc. are calculated from velocity and pressure fields. This section describes the definition of these parameters and the method used for computation of these parameters.

Coefficient of Drag (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{d}}$$\end{document})

The coefficient of drag (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{d}$$\end{document}) describes the characteristics amount of aerodynamic drag caused by fluid flow. Mathematically, it is defined as:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{d}}=\frac{{F}_{d}}{0.5\times\:{\uprho\:}\times\:\mathrm{A}\times\:{{\mathrm{U}}_{0}}^{2}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{F}}_{\mathrm{d}}\:$$\end{document}is the drag force, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\uprho\:}$$\end{document} is the density, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{U}}_{0}$$\end{document}is the uniform inlet velocity and A isthe frontal area of the body. Drag force comprises of two components: pressure drag andviscous drag. The pressure drag is obtained by adding pressure forces at all the grid points at upstream and downstream faces of the cylinder, force in the direction of flow being positive. The pressure force at one grid point on the surface is computed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{A}}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}({\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}-{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{i}-1,\mathrm{j}})$$\end{document} at the upstream face and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{A}}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}({\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{i}+1,\mathrm{j}}-{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}})$$\end{document} at the downstream face, where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{A}}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}$$\end{document}=∆y * 1, 1 being length in third dimension, i, j corresponds to indices of grid points position in streamwise and transverse direction respectively. The viscous drag is obtained by adding the viscous forces at all grid points on all the upper and lower lateral faces of the cylinder. Viscous force at one grid on the upper face is calculated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upmu\:}{\mathrm{A}}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}({\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}-{\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{i}-1,\mathrm{j}})/\varDelta\:\mathrm{y}$$\end{document}and at one grid point on lower surface is calculated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upmu\:}{\mathrm{A}}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}({\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}-{\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}-1})/\varDelta\:\mathrm{y}$$\end{document}, where µ is the viscosity of fluid and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{A}}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}}$$\end{document}=∆x * 1.

Coefficient of Lift (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathbf{C}}_{\boldsymbol{L}}$$\end{document})

The coefficient of lift (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{L}$$\end{document}) describes the characteristics amount of aerodynamic lift caused by fluid flow. Mathematically, it is defined as:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{L}}=\frac{{\mathrm{F}}_{\mathrm{L}}}{0.5\mathrm{*}{\uprho\:}\mathrm{*}\mathrm{A}\mathrm{*}{{\mathrm{U}}_{0}}^{2}}\:\:$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{F}}_{\boldsymbol{L}}$$\end{document}is the lift force, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\rho\:$$\end{document} is the density, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{U}_{0}$$\end{document}is the uniform inlet velocity and A is the frontal area of the body. Similar to the drag force, the lift force also consists of pressure force and viscous force. Computations for coefficient of lift are done in the similar manner as that for the coefficient of drag.

Strouhal Number (St)

The Strouhal number is the non-dimensional parameter which describes the frequency of vortex shedding. It is mathematically given as:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{S}\mathrm{t}=\frac{f\mathrm{*}\mathrm{d}}{{\mathrm{U}}_{0}}$$\end{document}

where f is the frequency of vortex shedding. The St is a function of Re and d. If the time series of lift coefficient is sinusoidal, the St is computed by obtaining the time period between two consecutive maxima observed in the time series of lift coefficient. In the flow regime where time series of lift coefficient does not show any patterns, most dominant frequency is obtained by power spectrum analysis of lift signals without initial transitions. The power spectrum is obtained as the magnitude of square of Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) of time signals.

Vorticity

Vorticity offers a basic structure of complex fluid motion. In the current study, vorticity contours are realized using the computation of the vorticity on each grid point within the computational domain. The resulting vorticity field is then employed to locate vortex centers and measure their strength as outlined in the next methodology. Vorticity is the curl of the velocity field and is the local rotation or circulation divided by unit area in the flow. In the case of two dimensional flows, the vorticity is a vector that is perpendicular to the motion plane.16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{w}}_{0}=\frac{\partial\:\mathrm{u}}{\partial\:\mathrm{y}}-\frac{\partial\:\mathrm{v}}{\partial\:\mathrm{x}}$$\end{document}

Power spectra analysis

Spectral analysis yields the power spectral density, a strictly positive real-valued frequency dependent function of a time-dependent signal. This method shows the frequency content of the signal and helps to identify the dominant periodic elements. The current research uses power spectral analysis of CL and Cd signals to identify the main frequencies that control the flow behavior. This provides the deeper understanding of the data and of the system which produces this data. Thus, the power spectra analysis of the present flow systems has been helpful to understand the flow behavior. TECPLOT plotting software has an in-built library which directly gives the power spectra of the given data. Boundary circumstances

At inlet, uniform velocity U = Uo and sinusoidal time-varying velocity V=-v are used. The convective boundary condition, which allows vortices to pass through the exit plane undisturbed at the outlet, is provided by following equation:

17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{\partial\:fi}{\partial\:\tau\:}+{\rm Uc}\:\frac{\partial\:fi}{\partial\:x}=0$$\end{document}

Here, i is in the range of 1 to 8 and Uc denotes stream wise velocity at computational domain’s exit that is spatially averaged. The convective boundary condition may have a negative impact on a tiny flow pattern close to outflow.

The bounce-back condition is used to apply the no-slip circumstances on the cylindrical surface, with u = v = 0. The unstable simulation begins for flow at rest and continues until full flow develops. When analyzing the data, the first transient portion is not taken into account. In the quasi-periodic lock-on-I regime, however, the lift and drag signals exhibit modulation due to the presence of multiple frequency components. The corresponding power spectra show the coexistence of the imposed oscillation frequency and an additional peak associated with the natural vortex shedding frequency, consistent with observations reported by Singh et al.8 for a single oscillating cylinder.

In the present multi-cylinder configuration, the persistence of this secondary frequency component over a wider range of oscillation frequencies suggests stronger wake interaction effects. This behavior is attributed to extended shear-layer interaction and wake coupling across the cylinder array, which can delay complete synchronization and lead to a broader lock-on regime compared to a single cylinder case.

Conclusion

This research work provides a widespread numerical investigation of flow past six inline transversely oscillating square cylinders using the lattice Boltzmann method at Re = 80, s/d = 1.0, and A/d = 0.2. The influence of oscillation frequency ratio \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{e}/{f}_{o}$$\end{document} on wake dynamics, vortex shedding behavior, and aerodynamic force characteristics has been scientifically examined. The outcomes of this study reveal that oscillation frequency plays a vital role in governing flow regimes and fluid–structure interaction. Three unique flow regimes are identified, namely quasi-periodic non-lock-on-I (0.1≤\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{f}_{e}/{f}_{o}\:$$\end{document}≤0.6), synchronous lock-on (0.8≤\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{f}_{e}/{f}_{o}\:$$\end{document}≤1.4), and quasi-periodic lock-on-I (1.6≤\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{f}_{e}/{f}_{o}\:$$\end{document}≤2.0). Within the lock-on regime, vortex shedding synchronizes with cylinder oscillation, which leads in highly coherent wake structures and single-frequency dominated force signals. Outside this regime, the coexistence of natural shedding and forcing frequencies results in to modulated lift and drag responses.

A crucial finding is that the synchronization range for the six-cylinder arrangement is considerably wider than that of a single oscillating cylinder, which indicates improved wake interaction and collective dynamics within the configuration. The wake structure grows into a single coherent pattern inclosing all cylinders, whereas higher oscillation frequencies promote vortex merging and formation of multipolar structures downstream. In terms of force characteristics, the first cylinder consistently experiences the highest mean drag, whereas downstream cylinders exhibit complex variations due to wake interference. The mean drag and CL, rms increase with oscillation frequency and attain peak values near the lock-on condition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{e}/{f}_{o}\text{}\:=1.0$$\end{document}, followed by a drop at higher frequencies. Overall, this work covers the understanding of multi-body oscillating bluff-body flows by signifying that oscillatory forcing considerably alters classical stationary flow regimes. The findings afford new insights into wake synchronization, force distribution, and flow control mechanisms in multi-cylinder systems, with possible applications in heat exchangers, offshore structures, and flow-induced vibration mitigation.
