 Probabilistic Learning, Dimension Reduction, and Spectral
 Representations for High-Dimensional Uncertainty
 Quantification
 Roger Ghanem
 University of Southern California

Abstract
This report presents a unified framework for uncertainty quantification and probabilistic model-
ing in high-dimensional systems characterized by limited data, complex dependencies, and model
error. Central to the approach is the identification and exploitation of low-dimensional manifold
structure underlying quantities of interest. By combining polynomial chaos expansions, projection-
based dimension reduction, and probabilistic learning on manifolds, the framework enables efficient
representation, inference, and sampling of stochastic systems. Extensions addressing epistemic
uncertainty, multi-fidelity modeling, stochastic PDE constraints, and generative sampling are in-
corporated into a coherent operator-theoretic perspective. Applications across fluid mechanics,
structural dynamics, multiscale materials, and plasma physics illustrate the generality and compu-
tational advantages of the approach.

1 Introduction
The increasing reliance on high-fidelity computational models in science and engineering has brought
to the forefront a fundamental challenge: how to quantify and propagate uncertainty in systems
governed by a large number of parameters, often under severe data limitations. These systems are
frequently characterized by heterogeneous and non-stationary behavior, strong statistical depen-
dence among parameters, and the presence of model error. Traditional uncertainty quantification
(UQ) approaches, while mathematically rigorous, encounter severe limitations as dimensionality
increases, leading to exponential growth in computational cost and loss of accuracy.
 A key observation emerging from this body of work is that, although the parameter space may
be high-dimensional, the quantities of interest (QoIs) often exhibit intrinsic low-dimensional struc-

ture. In particular, QoIs can frequently be represented as functions supported on low-dimensional
manifolds embedded within the ambient stochastic space. This observation shifts the focus from
global parameterizations to adaptive representations that align with the geometry induced by the
QoI itself.
 The framework developed herein is built upon this principle, combining spectral representations,
dimension reduction, and data-driven probabilistic learning into a unified methodology. At its core
lies the idea that uncertainty propagation and inference can be recast as problems of learning and
manipulating probability measures supported on low-dimensional structures.

2 Dimension Reduction via Adaptive Spectral Representa-
 tions
Polynomial Chaos Expansions (PCE) have long served as a cornerstone for representing stochastic
systems, providing spectral decompositions of QoIs in terms of orthogonal polynomial bases. While
highly effective in low-dimensional settings, their direct application to high-dimensional problems is
hindered by the rapid growth in basis size. This limitation motivates the development of adaptive
representations that concentrate variability into a reduced set of effective coordinates.
 The basis adaptation framework introduced earlier addresses this challenge by constructing
isometric transformations of the input random variables such that the QoI depends primarily on a
small number of transformed directions. In this setting, the stochastic representation is no longer
tied to the original coordinates but instead aligned with directions that are most informative for
the QoI. This induces a reduced representation in which the complexity of the approximation is
governed by the intrinsic dimension of the QoI rather than that of the parameter space.
 Building on this idea, Projection Pursuit Adaptation (PPA) introduces a data-driven mechanism
for identifying these directions. By combining concepts from projection pursuit regression with
polynomial chaos expansions, PPA simultaneously learns the optimal low-dimensional subspace and
constructs an associated spectral approximation. This formulation can be interpreted as a projection
of the QoI onto a sequence of optimal directions in an underlying Gaussian Hilbert space, resulting
in a representation that converges in the mean-square sense to the governing physical solution.
Importantly, the learned representation retains consistency with the probabilistic structure of the
problem while enabling efficient reuse of data across multiple QoIs.
 From a functional-analytic perspective, these methods can be viewed as constructing reduced
subspaces of the underlying Hilbert space of random variables, with the adaptation process identi-
fying basis elements that maximize variance or information content with respect to the QoI.

3 Probabilistic Learning on Manifolds
While adaptive spectral methods address the representation of QoIs, they do not fully resolve the
challenge posed by limited data. Probabilistic Learning on Manifolds (PLoM) provides a com-
plementary framework for learning probability measures directly from data, particularly in the
small-sample regime.
 The core idea of PLoM is to approximate the unknown probability distribution of a high-
dimensional random vector by leveraging its intrinsic geometric structure. This is achieved by first
estimating the probability density using kernel methods, then identifying a low-dimensional mani-
fold through diffusion maps, and finally constructing a stochastic dynamical system—formulated as

 2

an Itô stochastic differential equation—whose invariant measure matches the learned distribution.
The resulting model acts as a generative mechanism capable of producing additional realizations
consistent with the observed data.
 A distinguishing feature of PLoM is its ability to operate effectively when the available dataset is
small, a regime in which traditional statistical learning methods often fail. By embedding the data
within a manifold-informed stochastic process, PLoM effectively regularizes the learning problem,
constraining the probability measure to remain consistent with the observed geometric structure.
 Several extensions enhance the capability of this framework. Incorporating constraints derived
from governing equations allows the learned distribution to satisfy physical laws, enabling the
solution of stochastic boundary value problems in a data-driven manner. Improvements in the
construction of the manifold basis, such as the introduction of transient anisotropic kernels, provide
enhanced representation of heterogeneous data and better capture statistical dependencies. More
recent developments decouple geometry learning from sampling by formulating stochastic dynamics
directly in latent space and employing smooth lifting operators to reconstruct ambient variables,
thereby improving both stability and interpretability.
 These developments position PLoM as a bridge between data-driven learning and physics-based
modeling, enabling probabilistic inference in settings characterized by high dimensionality and
limited data.

4 Unified Treatment of Uncertainty
A central objective of this work is the unified treatment of different sources of uncertainty, including
aleatory variability, epistemic uncertainty, and model error. Traditional approaches often treat these
sources separately, leading to fragmented analyses and inconsistent representations.
 Extended Polynomial Chaos frameworks address this limitation by promoting parameters of
probabilistic models to random variables themselves. In this formulation, epistemic uncertainty—arising
from lack of knowledge about model parameters or distributions—is embedded directly into the
stochastic representation. As a result, the output of the model is no longer a single probability
distribution but a family of distributions parameterized by epistemic variables.
 This construction enables the propagation of both aleatory and epistemic uncertainty within
a single computational framework. By coupling PCE with kernel density estimation, one obtains
explicit mappings from input uncertainties to output probability density functions. This, in turn,
allows for the evaluation of sensitivities not merely of scalar quantities but of entire distributions.
Functional sensitivity indices can thus be defined, providing a detailed characterization of how input
uncertainties influence different regions of the output space, including failure domains.
 Model error is incorporated by treating the coefficients of the spectral representation as ran-
dom variables, reflecting uncertainty due to limited data or imperfect modeling assumptions. A
Bayesian updating procedure can then be employed to refine these coefficients in light of observed
data, resulting in hierarchical probabilistic models that capture both parametric uncertainty and
structural discrepancies.
 From an operator perspective, these constructions can be interpreted as mappings between
spaces of probability measures, with spectral expansions providing explicit representations of these
operators and enabling their efficient evaluation.

5 Multi-Fidelity Modeling and High-Dimensional Learning
In many practical applications, multiple models of varying fidelity are available, each offering differ-
ent trade-offs between accuracy and computational cost. However, leveraging such models effectively
is challenging, particularly when their parameterizations differ or when correlations between them
are weak.
 The introduction of shared low-dimensional manifolds provides a mechanism for aligning these
models within a common representation space. By applying basis adaptation techniques to each
model, one can identify latent coordinates in which the models exhibit stronger correlation. This
alignment enables the construction of multi-fidelity estimators that combine information from dif-
ferent models while preserving the accuracy of high-fidelity simulations.
 Furthermore, the integration of dimension reduction with machine learning architectures, such
as multi-fidelity networks, allows for the encoding of complex relationships among models without
requiring explicit hierarchical structure. This results in surrogate models that achieve improved
accuracy, reduced variance, and enhanced robustness to model discrepancies.

6 Applications
The methodologies described above have been applied to a wide range of problems in computa-
tional science and engineering. In fluid mechanics, they enable the probabilistic characterization
of solutions to nonlinear partial differential equations, including Navier–Stokes flows with uncer-
tain parameters and hypersonic combustion systems with complex chemical kinetics. In structural
mechanics, they support the analysis of high-dimensional dynamical systems, including vibration
analysis and damage detection in complex assemblies.
 In the context of multiscale materials, the framework facilitates the propagation of uncertainty
across scales, capturing dependencies between microstructural features and macroscopic properties.
Applications to plasma physics demonstrate the ability to extract meaningful statistical information
from limited simulation data, providing insights into transport phenomena in tokamak boundary
regions.
 In optimal control and inverse problems, the integration of probabilistic learning with surrogate
modeling enables efficient exploration of parameter spaces and robust decision-making under uncer-
tainty. Across these domains, the unifying feature is the ability to construct accurate probabilistic
representations with significantly reduced computational cost.

7 Synthesis
The developments presented in this report converge toward a common conceptual framework in
which uncertainty quantification is viewed as the problem of learning and manipulating probability
measures on low-dimensional manifolds embedded in high-dimensional spaces. Spectral represen-
tations, such as polynomial chaos expansions, provide a means of parameterizing these measures,
while manifold learning techniques identify the intrinsic structure that governs their behavior.
 This perspective suggests a deeper connection between probabilistic modeling, functional anal-
ysis, and geometry. In particular, the combination of Hilbert space representations with manifold-
based learning can be interpreted as constructing reduced-order representations of operators acting

on spaces of random variables. This viewpoint opens the door to systematic integration of data-
driven and physics-based methods.
