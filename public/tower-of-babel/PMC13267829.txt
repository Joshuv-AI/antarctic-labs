Use of “R&R” for Medical Students and Physicians with Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder: A Conceptual Framework
Authors: David S Im, Carrie M Tamarelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAttention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is an increasingly recognized yet under-addressed challenge within the medical profession, affecting medical students and physicians (including residents and faculty). While current literature emphasizes high rates of depression, anxiety, and burnout in these groups, ADHD – despite its persistence into adulthood and its impact on academic, clinical, and interpersonal functioning – receives comparatively less attention. Reported ADHD prevalence among medical students, residents, and practicing physicians varies widely and may be underestimated due to stigma, underreporting, and diagnostic bias. Untreated ADHD can hinder academic performance, impede residency matching, threaten patient care, and worsen mental health. In prior work, we introduced the 5-Minute Video Summary (5MVS), a multisensory, five-minute physician-narrated video that distills a scientific article’s key findings while also commenting on strengths and limitations using varied visuals and vocal delivery to support attention and retention. The present manuscript conceptually extends this ADHD-supportive, technology-enabled approach “upstream” to the foundational task the 5MVS depends on: completing the initial reading of scientific literature. We introduce “Record and Read” (R&R), a self-implemented strategy that leverages auditory reinforcement and the production effect. Learners record themselves reading an article aloud, then listen to the recording while following along and highlighting salient points. R&R is intended to reduce procrastination, increase sustained engagement, and improve comprehension for medical students and physicians with ADHD, potentially offering flexibility and independence, though technological access and stamina demands may pose barriers. While we propose R&R as a complementary tool for medical students and physicians with ADHD to promote both engagement with full-text articles and efficient consolidation, this approach has yet to be systematically studied via pilot, acceptability, or feasibility testing – underscoring its need for empirical evaluation.Keywords: attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, medical education, scientific article reading, innovative educational tool, medical learner, physician

Introduction

An increasing body of literature describes the many challenges associated with pursuing a career in the medical profession, including significant rates of depression, anxiety, and burnout among medical students, residents, and practicing physicians compared to the general population.1–3 Relatively less attention has been devoted to the occurrence of attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) in medical learners and physicians.

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interferes with functioning or development.4 Symptoms of ADHD can include not seeming to listen when spoken to, easy distractibility, forgetfulness, making careless mistakes in work or school, procrastination, difficulty sustaining attention, difficulty completing tasks, excessive fidgetiness or restlessness, impulsivity, becoming easily bored or impatient, and blurting out answers or interrupting others.4 While originally conceptualized as a childhood disorder, research has shown that ADHD persists in up to 60% of adults who experienced symptoms as children.5

In addition to the core symptoms noted above, individuals with ADHD often struggle with executive function, manifested by difficulty starting tasks promptly, organizing and planning steps, pausing before reacting, and shifting flexibly when priorities change; these have manifested as slower, less efficient planning on problem-solving tasks and greater difficulty filtering out distracting information and responding consistently.6 For medical students and physicians with ADHD, this can make reading scientific articles – with their long length, dense text, use of jargon, and minimal use of visual aids – particularly challenging.

In this perspective, we present a conceptual proposal to assist medical students and physicians with ADHD with scientific article reading. Of note, this proposal does not represent empirical research and has not been subject to feasibility, acceptability, or validation studies; as such, its purely conceptual nature is emphasized.

ADHD in Medical Learners and Physicians: Prevalence, Impact, and Accommodations

Existing research shows variable ADHD prevalence among medical students, from 0.9–1.4% (AAMC registry data)7,8 to 24.4% (self-report studies).9 Data in residents and physicians are scarcer, with extremely low rates reported in residents (0.06% in emergency medicine residents as reported by program directors)10 and in practicing physicians (0.32% per AAMC survey).11 This sharp drop relative to both medical student data and general adult population estimates (3–5%) may reflect selection bias (ie, because ADHD may hinder successful completion of focused study, clinical rotations, scholarly activities, and other tasks necessary to complete medical training, medical residents and practicing physicians may represent a sample pre-selected not to have ADHD); alternatively, it may reflect underreporting due to perceived stigma, fear of professional repercussions, and lack of recognition by these groups and their loved ones.12–14 Other contributing factors include potential diagnostic bias among clinicians (who may be reluctant to prescribe stimulants)15 and compensatory mechanisms masking symptoms until demands intensify.13,16

Untreated ADHD has the potential to adversely impact academic performance,17 clinical reliability,18 and interpersonal relationships,5 and may increase risk for depression, anxiety, and substance use.5 Medical students with ADHD are less likely to graduate on time or match into residency,8 and untreated symptoms may adversely affect patient care. Notably, even when diagnosed and treated, many adults with ADHD discontinue medication due to perceived inefficacy, underscoring the need for better interventions and sustained support.19,20

Accommodation strategies – ranging from task management supports and environmental modifications to workflow pacing and explicit feedback – are available under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) of 1990 and the Americans with Disabilities Amendments Act (ADAAA) of 2008 and have been shown to aid those with ADHD in medical training.5,21 These can be tailored to individual needs, including structured reading plans and regular coordination among care providers, faculty, and supervisors.22 However, the published literature seldom addresses a crucial, potentially challenging area for medical students and physicians with ADHD: the reading of scientific articles.

Scientific Articles: ADHD-Related Barriers and Educational Tools

Scientific article reading is foundational in medical education and practice. While medical students may use articles less than textbooks, their importance grows during clinical rotations, when students are expected to reference current literature. For residents, reading scientific articles is crucial for meeting Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) competencies in medical knowledge and practice-based learning.23 Practicing physicians rely on scientific articles for continuing medical education, board certification, and research, especially within academic settings where scholarly work is often required for career advancement.24

Scientific articles commonly present challenges for medical students and physicians with ADHD. They are often lengthy, use third-person language, and contain complex jargon, all of which can be difficult for individuals with ADHD who struggle with sustained attention, engagement, and reading comprehension – especially when the material is outside their area of interest.4,25–27 Additionally, scientific articles typically feature dense text and few visual aids, despite evidence that people with ADHD benefit from multimodal, stimulating presentations of information.28 Consequently, traditional article formats may hinder understanding and retention for readers with ADHD.

In our previous work,29 we described the concept of the 5-Minute Video Summary (5MVS), an innovative educational tool designed to help medical students and physicians with ADHD efficiently absorb key information from scientific articles through engaging five-minute video presentations. Unlike traditional video abstracts, the 5MVS provides not only an overview of the article’s content but also commentaries on its strengths and limitations, delivered by an objective physician narrator rather than the article’s author and enhanced by varied visuals and vocal delivery to capture attention. The accessible recordings are intended for flexible, multisensory learning and aim to improve information retention and engagement. The effectiveness of the 5MVS can be evaluated using real-time comprehension questions and comparative studies tracking knowledge retention among users with and without access to these summaries.

Another Innovative Tool: The Record and Read (R&R) Approach

One limitation of the 5MVS is that it requires investment of significant time by the individual creating it to make it available, and the number of scientific articles needing review by medical students and physicians likely far exceeds the number of faculty or other dedicated educators available to create such summaries. While this limitation may be partially mitigated by the increasing availability of artificial intelligence (AI) tools capable of generating summaries, audio narration, or even video content, there are risks associated with use of these tools (discussed later).30 Therefore, while the 5MVS still has potential utility from the standpoint of being created by medical educators trying to make specific foundational articles accessible and digestible for medical students and physicians with ADHD, it is of less practical value when, for example, medical students and residents are attempting to read scientific articles as part of a self-chosen or assigned scholarly project, or when residents and faculty physicians are attempting to complete literature reviews to inform clinical decision-making, educational presentations, or research projects. In these latter contexts, an educational tool enhancing the ability of medical students and physicians with ADHD to more efficiently read self-sequestered scientific articles would be of broader and more practical benefit.

The Record and Read (R&R) approach, in theory, might address some of these challenges. This approach makes use of the premise that much of what an individual reads is processed linguistically,31 such that content is often “spoken” in one’s mind (subvocal pronunciation), articulated quietly, or verbalized out loud while read.32 It also harnesses the production effect whereby recall improves for material that is read aloud compared to material read silently.33 We developed R&R through a combination of lived faculty experience and subsequent grounding in learning theory. R&R began somewhat serendipitously when the first author (DI) experimented with recording himself reading full-text articles for maintenance of board certification in anticipation of long flights, given his difficulty finding uninterrupted time and sustaining efficient engagement with dense scientific reading. He found that listening to the recordings during travel helped him “get through” the article and grasp key points, and that the benefit increased further when he read along during playback while highlighting salient information. Based on this experience, we formalized the method as a simple, self-implemented routine and conceptualized it using educational and cognitive frameworks (described below).

With the R&R approach, an individual first records himself reading the content of the scientific article out loud, focusing mainly on the text of the article (including the abstract), making an effort to enunciate clearly and at a pace that supports this goal while facilitating progress through the article content. This can be done using a voice memo application included in many cellular phones, allowing for easy execution in most settings, as with this approach the reading does not need to be performed loudly to be effectively captured/recorded. Next, the individual silently/visually reads the article while simultaneously playing the recording of herself reading the article aloud through earbuds or headphones connected to a cellular phone or other device playing the recording. It is advised that the individual use a pen or highlighter to note particularly salient portions of the article while listening to its reading (if working with a printed copy of the article) or employ the highlighting function on a computer’s document displaying software (eg, Adobe Acrobat Reader) to highlight salient information (if working with an electronic version of the article on the computer) while listening to the verbal presentation.

The individual has the option of pausing the playback of the recording at any time to briefly process what appear to be important points and can rewind and replay any portions of the recording to facilitate clearer grasp of information that may be unclear on first “pass” of read-listening. Once finished with the read-listening of the article, the individual can test understanding and retention of the material by utilizing an artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted program such as their medical school/university-provided Chat GPT program to generate 5 to 10 multiple-choice questions covering the most important points of the article, with answers provided after each question-response set is presented. The following sample prompt could be entered into such a program for this purpose:

Can you create 5 multiple choice questions to test retention based on the important information in the following article, anchoring every question and rationale to specific passages.

The individual can then copy and paste the article content into the GPT prompt box (which provides for the most detailed, relevant questions, as opposed to simply entering the article’s citation/reference into the GPT prompt box, which results in more general questions not derived from the specific article being reviewed).

Because the initial step of the R&R approach simply involves a mechanical, vocal reading of the article content (regardless of whether the reader actually understands or remembers every nuance of this content), this approach has an advantage of minimizing pressure on the medical student or physician with ADHD to immediately grasp and retain what is being read on first pass through the article (thus lending itself to use with articles of varying levels of content complexity) – making it less likely for the reader to quit the task early in the reading of the article (as may commonly occur when a medical student or physician with ADHD normally tries to read a scientific article). Assuming the individual has no primary reading impairment (such as dyslexia), this first R&R step should be relatively easy to complete, and based on our anecdotal observations should take about 20 minutes.

The second R&R step – read-listening through the article as described above – may require more effort to process, understand, and retain material than the first step, but these facets of reading are facilitated by the fact that (1) the reader is hearing their own voice confidently and steadfastly reading the article, as if content has already been mastered (thus potentially conveying more confidence to the reader with ADHD that the article-reading task is surmountable), (2) the reader’s own recorded voice will continue to speak until the article has been completely read (thus providing an expectation of task completion), and (3) as noted above, the recording can be paused, rewound, and replayed at any points to facilitate adequate understanding and retention of material, coupled if necessary with use of manual or electronic highlighting or underlining of the article text to capture key concepts. Based on our anecdotal observations, this step may take as long or slightly longer than the first R&R step, depending on how often the reader chooses to pause, rewind, and/or replay portions of the article recording (30 to 40 minutes).

Additional potential advantages of the R&R approach to reading scientific articles are that (1) it can be utilized by medical students/physicians themselves and applied to any specific articles they wish/need to read, without depending on other individuals to implement; (2) for medical students and physicians with ADHD not accustomed to or confident giving oral presentations, this approach provides regular opportunities to practice reading scientific content out loud (first R&R step) while immediately observing one’s performance afterward during the read-listening (second R&R) step, providing a useful springboard for identifying ways to improve one’s oral presentation skills; (3) the execution of this approach can occur in many different settings, provided an individual has access to technology that supports it (eg, a cellular phone with a voice memo function, headphones or earbuds to enable private and unobstructed read-listening); and (4) while the overall time required to read a scientific article using the R&R approach may be on average about one hour, this, based on our anecdotal observations, is significantly shorter/more efficient of a timeline to complete scientific article reading for medical students and physicians with ADHD than the time it would take to complete this task without such an approach (for many individuals with ADHD, several hours to days).

As noted earlier, individuals with ADHD often struggle with executive function – starting tasks, planning and organizing steps, inhibiting quick reactions, shifting priorities, and showing efficient visuolingual processing (ie, coordinating what they see with language, such as quickly reading dense text or tables and extracting meaning without losing the thread). These difficulties can appear as slower, less efficient planning and trouble filtering distractions and responding consistently.6 R&R could uniquely help medical students and physicians with ADHD by making reading easier to start (a brief, active recording task), adding structure, and pairing audio with text to keep attention anchored and support comprehension/retention – especially when visuolingual processing is taxed by complex articles. For individuals without ADHD, R&R may still help in these respects, but would be less uniquely necessary because persistent, functionally impairing problems with initiation, interference control, and sustained engagement are less common.6

Conceptually, the potential utility of the R&R method is supported by various cognitive and educational frameworks, including cognitive load theory,34 dual coding theory,35 self-regulated learning,36 and active learning.37 Cognitive load theory (CLT) explains learning in terms of working-memory limits: when a task demands more mental “bandwidth” than a learner has available, comprehension and performance suffer. CLT distinguishes intrinsic load (the unavoidable complexity of the material), extraneous load (unnecessary effort caused by how the task is structured or presented) and germane load (productive effort spent organizing and understanding information).34 This framework supports the R&R method because full-text scientific articles carry high intrinsic load for many learners, as they require integrating dense methods, results, and interpretation while maintaining attention across long, structured texts. R&R could help manage that load by adding a clear structure – recording, then replaying while tracking the text – which can reduce extraneous demands like losing one’s place or repeatedly restarting, while also spreading processing across listening and reading. With fewer resources wasted on task management, learners can devote more germane effort to meaning-making tasks such as highlighting key points, linking findings to prior knowledge, and consolidating what they have read.

Dual coding theory proposes that memory is supported by two linked systems – one for verbal information (words/sounds) and one for non-verbal information (mental images/visual cues) – and that learning improves when instruction builds connections within and between these systems, making later retrieval easier.35 The R&R method fits this framework by presenting the same text through coordinated channels: the learner produces the verbal stream by reading aloud (strengthening the verbal code and leveraging the production effect), then re-encounters it through listening while simultaneously tracking the written text and adding highlights (creating a visual/perceptual trace tied to the same ideas). In dual-coding terms, R&R repeatedly couples auditory-verbal input with concurrent visual information (the page and the learner’s markings), encouraging tighter cross-links between what the words sound like and what the key points look like on the page – an accessible, self-implemented structure that could support attention, engagement, and recall for medical students and physicians with ADHD.

Self-regulated learning (SRL) describes how learners take active control of their learning through a repeating cycle of planning, monitoring performance, and reflecting on results.36 In this framework, the R&R method could serve as a practical SRL support for medical students and physicians with ADHD by turning “read the paper” into a structured, self-directed routine: in the forethought phase, learners set a concrete goal (eg, complete an initial full-text pass) and commit to recording; during performance, they use strategies that support monitoring and control - reading aloud, then listening while following along and highlighting - to sustain attention and track understanding; and in self-reflection, their highlights and notes provide evidence to evaluate what they grasped, identify gaps, and plan next steps (eg, revisit a section).

Active learning (AL) refers to approaches that move learners beyond passively taking in information and instead require them to do something with the material – constructing understanding through activities that improve retention, recall, and application.37 The R&R method aligns with AL by turning the initial pass through a scientific article into an active task: learners generate the content by reading it aloud (rather than silently skimming), then re-engage with it by listening while tracking the text and highlighting key points, which prompts selection, organization, and meaning-making. For medical students and physicians with ADHD, this structured “produce-and-annotate” workflow could support sustained engagement and reduce procrastination while creating a more efficient, hands-on way to build comprehension than passive reading alone.

Relative to other learning strategies like metacognitive training38 and attentional control,39 R&R is a concrete, self-implemented reading approach that could support task initiation and sustained attention for learning, particularly for learners with ADHD, thus complementing these other strategies. Whereas metacognitive training strengthens planning, monitoring, and evaluation of learning,38 R&R adds multisensory structure (reading aloud, then listening while tracking/highlighting) that may reduce procrastination and increase engagement with full-text articles, potentially making metacognitive regulation easier to apply. Similarly, attentional control targets top-down shifting of attention away from intrusive emotional thoughts (eg, worry, self-criticism) and back to task goals,39 while R&R structures the reading process itself; together, these strategies might lower the effort needed to begin and sustain engagement when negative thoughts arise.

R&R may introduce limitations and unintended consequences despite its theoretical appeal. Adding a recording step plus synchronized listening, reading, and highlighting can increase coordination demands, which may feel burdensome when articles already have high intrinsic complexity. It can also be time- and energy-intensive; reading aloud and then re-reading may contribute to fatigue/voice strain and be hard to sustain amid busy clinical schedules and sleep debt, potentially increasing avoidance. Feasibility may be limited by reading disorders, technology/setting requirements, and discomfort with recording. Finally, it must be emphasized that at this stage, R&R has not been systematically evaluated using feasibility, acceptability, or pilot testing and thus is a purely conceptual or theoretical innovation in need of empirical validation; its potential benefits are thus hypothetical and not yet rooted in evidence.

Risks and limitations should also be considered when pairing R&R with AI-generated practice questions.30 Generative AI tools can produce plausible-sounding but incorrect content (hallucinations), omit key nuances, or generate misleading answer rationales, so outputted questions should be treated as draft study aids that require verification against the article. Bias is also possible: question framing or what is considered “important” may reflect model or training-data biases (ie, the AI model can import typical patterns from its training and from common medical discourse that are not explicitly in an article, potentially skewing emphasis or interpretation); this can be practically tempered by asking the AI tool to anchor every question and answer rationale to specific passages. Copyright and licensing constraints are also important to be aware of – copying and pasting full-text articles into an AI system may violate publisher terms or exceed permitted educational use, depending on the source and access agreement. Finally, learners should follow institutional and health system policies on approved AI platforms, data handling, and acceptable use (eg, using university-provided tools when required, not uploading restricted content, and complying with privacy/security rules).30

Despite the above-noted limitations, R&R represents a novel conceptual approach that has theoretical potential to help medical students and physicians with ADHD read scientific articles in a timely, effective manner. In formal educational settings, R&R could be offered as an optional, ADA/ADAAA-aligned learning strategy within study-skills training, workshops for neurodiverse learners, learning specialist services, and residency academic support – framed explicitly as a conceptual, unvalidated strategy. Medical schools and residency programs could integrate it into journal clubs, evidence-based medicine (EBM) courses, and scholarly projects by (1) providing brief training on the workflow (record → listen-read with highlighting → optional self-testing), (2) supplying access to needed tools (headphones, voice-recording apps, PDF annotation software, and institution-approved GPT/AI for practice questions), and (3) building “protected time” blocks for article processing to reduce initiation barriers. Implementation could also include privacy-conscious guidance (eg, record only permissible content, use secure devices). Finally, programs could pair rollout with feasibility evaluation – uptake, perceived burden/fatigue, time-on-task, and comprehension/persistence metrics – to determine where R&R fits best (eg, long dense articles versus shorter readings) and for whom.

Conclusion

ADHD in medical students and physicians is understudied, has potentially daunting consequences professionally and personally, and warrants practical, effective interventions to help manage its symptoms within the context of the rigors of medical training and practice, given the limitations of pharmacotherapy. In this perspective piece, we propose another innovative tool to assist medical students and physicians with the important task of reading scientific articles. While grounded in educational and cognitive frameworks and in theory being of potential help to medical students and physicians with reading of scientific literature, at present the R&R approach is purely conceptual in nature and lacking systematic development, pilot/feasibility/acceptability testing, or empirical validation and thus any data to support its use. Moreover, ethical issues involving the use of AI tools to test retention and understanding need to be considered. Future research should start with feasibility, acceptability, and pilot testing of R&R and then move to adequately powered comparative trials. Studies could randomize medical students, residents, and physicians – with and without ADHD – to (a) traditional silent reading, (b) R&R, (c) 5MVS-supported reading, and (d) AI-assisted summarization, using standardized articles and controlling for article difficulty and prior familiarity. Quantitative outcomes should include comprehension (immediate quiz/multiple-choice questions and short-answer application), delayed retention (eg, 1–2 weeks), efficiency (time-on-task, completion, procrastination latency), and downstream performance (eg, journal club contributions, evidence synthesis accuracy, clinical-reasoning vignettes). Qualitative measures should capture user experience, perceived cognitive load/fatigue, and practical barriers (eg, technology, time). Analyses should stratify participants with ADHD (including medication/accommodations status) while enrolling comparison groups without ADHD to test differential benefit, identify moderators (eg, baseline reading speed, working memory, anxiety/burnout), and determine for whom and under what conditions R&R is most usable and effective.
