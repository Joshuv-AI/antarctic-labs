Integrative care for severe challenging behaviors and psychotic-like symptoms in an adolescent girl with Xia–Gibbs syndrome: a case report
Authors: Ilaria Noschese, Cora Cravero, David Cohen, Boris Chaumette, Camille Verebi, Claudine Laurent-Levinson, Xavier Benarous, Cyril Hanin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis case report addresses a clinical gap in the management of severe challenging behaviors (CBs) in Xia–Gibbs Syndrome (XGS), a rare genetic disorder caused by variants in the AHDC1 gene, highlighting the importance of integrative, multidisciplinary assessment to disentangle overlapping medical, developmental, and psychiatric contributors.Case presentationA 17-year-old French adolescent girl of Chinese descent with XGS and intellectual development disorder was referred to inpatient care for severe CBs and suspected schizophrenia. A comprehensive assessment of pain-related factors led to the identification and treatment of several co-occurring medical conditions (gastritis, local care for atopic dermatitis, progestin for dysmenorrhea). Prolonged clinical observations showed that most psychotic-like symptoms resulted instead from developmentally appropriate stress-coping strategies and depressive symptoms. Other identified risk factors for CBs were medication side effects, unmet sensory integration needs, and environmental stressors. Providing interventions on all these factors associated with environmental adaptations and optimized developmental care led to marked clinical improvement.ConclusionsThis case illustrates that in patients with complex neurodevelopmental disorders, comprehensive medical assessment should be prioritized over psychiatric assessment when possible, considering symptoms overlap and communication difficulties. As most risk factors for CBs can mutually reinforce and self-sustain, integrative care for these patients should consider concomitant interventions targeting both risk factors and CBs themselves.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13256-026-06304-4.

Background

Xia–Gibbs syndrome (XGS) is a rare genetic disorder caused by de novo heterozygous variants in AHDC1, a gene encoding a transcription factor involved in DNA binding, chromatin architecture, and gene regulation [1]. The XGS, first described by Xia et coll. in 2014 [2], encompasses a wide range of developmental disabilities, in particular intellectual developmental disorder (IDD), hypotonia, and epilepsy, associated with other clinical features such as craniofacial dysmorphisms [3].

We present the case of a 17-year-old female diagnosed with XGS associated with severe challenging behaviors (CBs), IDD, and psychotic-like symptoms, following the CARE guideline (for CAse REports). CBs are maladaptive, disruptive behaviors exhibited by individuals with developmental disabilities, such as self-injury or hetero-aggression [4]. Risk factors of CBs are varied and include all sources of stress. Youths with complex neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) due to rare or genetic conditions are at very high risk of CBs due to the co-occurrences of multiple somatic conditions and a high rate of life stressors [5].

Our objectives are: (1) to contribute to the growing body of literature on XGS and (2) to illustrate how a multidisciplinary comprehensive assessment of all risk factors for CBs helps optimize outcomes of patients with behavioral and NDDs.

Case presentation

The patient was a 17-year-old French-born female whose parents were both of Chinese origin. She was referred to an inpatient care unit for severe CBs, including repetitive head-banging and hitting her right ear. She exhibited unexplained episodes of crying, screaming, and collapsing. Psychotic features were suspected, considering her bizarre behaviors (that is, prolonged staring without apparent reason, self-directed speech without clear communicative intent, repetitive vocalization). We also noted reduced appetite associated with impulsive eating behaviors and an inability to remain seated during meals. She had sleep difficulties with delayed sleep onset and reduced sleep duration. Social interactions were limited, while she maintained substantial autonomy in personal care, such as toileting and dressing.

Patient information

The parents (father, 46, mother, 45) and the siblings (two sisters, 3 and 18) were in good health with no reported medical conditions or genetic disorders. Both parents were from Asian ethnic background. No complications were observed during pregnancy, but hypotonia and feeding difficulties were noted in the neonate. Delays in developmental acquisitions were observed with autonomous walking only at 2.5 years and first words at 3 years. She presented atypical eating behaviors, sleeping difficulties, and motor stereotypes. By age 2, she was diagnosed with strabismus and left renal agenesis. Cerebral MRI found a retrocerebellar arachnoid cyst. The patient received speech, motor, and occupational therapies between ages 3 and 6, then attended a special educational facility for youth with severe learning difficulties (IME, Institut Médico-Éducatif). The diagnosis of moderate IDD was supported by developmental history and standardized assessments. She demonstrates partial autonomy in sphincter control and requires assistance with toileting. Feeding skills are relatively preserved, with a degree of independence during meals. Academic skills were limited to a preschool level, including early graphomotor abilities and pre-academic learning. At 10 years of age, the patient underwent a Vineland assessment, which indicated developmental ages ranging from 1 year (socialization) to 3 years (communication, daily living skills, motor skills).

A genetic evaluation initiated at age 3, involving two CGH arrays (including one high-resolution), showed no structural abnormalities. Subsequent candidate gene studies (ETHM1, FMR1) were negative, and the IDefix Panel (version 1, 253 genes) for IDD was normal. Whole-genome sequencing performed during adolescence identified a de novo nonsense variant in the AHDC1 gene (NM_001029882.3): c.2773C > T (p.Arg925*), leading to a premature stop codon. This pathogenic variant was classified as ACMG category 5, that is, with a high imputability level for her clinical presentation, diagnosing her with XGS.

Timeline

By age 12, she started presenting anxiety symptoms, with finger-biting and inappropriate laughter in potentially stressful situations. By age 14, clinicians suspected auditory hallucinations. These symptoms worsened during the COVID-19 lockdowns with the sudden closure of her educational facility. The patient spent much time at her father's workplace with almost no developmentally appropriate activities. Increased agitation, self-directed speech, and social withdrawal led first-line clinicians to suggest early-onset schizophrenia. Several lines of antipsychotics were prescribed sequentially over several months in response to worsening symptoms, with partial or no clinical improvement (Table 1). Therapeutic intervention

We first treated all causes of pain and discomfort, that is, providing antibiotics and Proton Pump Inhibitor for Helicobacter pylori gastritis, local care for atopic dermatitis, and progestin for dysmenorrhea (Table 1).

Antipsychotic drugs were discontinued, and loxapine 25 mg was only prescribed in case of severe agitation. A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) was prescribed, sertraline 25 mg/day, to treat anxiety and depressive symptoms. Sertraline was selected due to its favorable safety profile in adolescents with neurodevelopmental disorders and its efficacy in treating anxiety-related symptoms [6]. Sertraline has been approved by the French regulatory agency for the treatment of anxiety disorders since the age of 6 years old. The treatment was initiated at a low dose and closely monitored for side effects, including behavioral activation or gastrointestinal symptoms.

Adaptations to her environment were provided. The patient wore special equipment to enhance her safety, prevent physical injuries, and address her sensory integration needs: a protective helmet for head-banging and a specialized chair to minimize harm during rocking. The specialized chair was designed to provide postural support and predictable vestibular and proprioceptive sensations, thereby reducing the risk of injury induced by the patient herself due to ill-adapted sensation-seeking behaviors.

Weekly sessions of sensorimotor intervention therapy were provided in a Snoezelen to provide relaxing times. Functional analysis was conducted by a behavioral psychologist to identify reinforcers of CBs and to promote more adaptive alternative behaviors. A speech therapist helped the paramedical team by including enriched communication tools into their routines with the patient and providing adequate support for communication about pain, stressors, anxiety, or mood. A time timer and a visual calendar to help her manage time, establish predictable routines, and reduce repetitive demands via CBs.

Finally, weekly family-focused interventions, including information about medical hypotheses, psychoeducation, tracking clinical changes, and addressing the parents’ worries, were provided. Follow-up and outcomes

Significant improvement in her psychotic-like symptoms was observed in the weeks following the integrative treatment. Agitation and self-injurious behaviors were significantly reduced, accompanied by improved social responsiveness and participation in social activities with peers and adults. At discharge, the family reported a considerable improvement in functioning and a notable reduction in care burden. Standardized measures such as the Aberrant Behavior Checklist were not used systematically, which is a significant limitation. However, clinical improvement was documented through repeated multidisciplinary observations across settings. The Clinical Global Impression-Improvement subscale, retrospectively rated by the caring clinician, yields a score of one (very high improvement).

Discussion and conclusions

Patient X's clinical presentation and psychiatric manifestations were evaluated within the context of XGS, a rare genetic disorder known to cause multiple NDDs.

A comprehensive assessment and treatment of all the patient’s medical comorbidities, that is, gastritis, dysmenorrhea, cerumen impaction, and atopic dermatitis, significantly reduced pain and discomfort, which could have contributed to maintaining CBs over the last months. According to our experiences and in line with [4, 5], clinicians caring for youths with NDDs and severe CBs should first provide an exhaustive assessment of medical comorbidities before completing a psychiatric evaluation, considering the overlap in the expressions of physical and psychological distress in youths with poor communication skills. In addition, sensory integration issues and medication-induced side effects are also common contributors to CBs [7].

Prolonged clinical observations of the patient after the treatment of co-occurring medical conditions showed that some of these behaviors were more in line with internalizing symptoms (anxiety and depressive symptoms) with atypical features frequently reported in individuals with NDDs [5, 8]. Our vignette also illustrates some of the main barriers to appropriate treatment for anxiety and depression in youths with IDD (for example, lack of training of professionals or difficulties documenting premorbid functioning), despite a 4–6 times higher risk compared to the general population [9]. Here, we adopted the probabilistic approach for the diagnosis of depressive disorder recommended in European Guidelines for the care of psychiatric disorders in individuals with IDD [10], that is, putting emphasis on the assessment of the change in current functioning and on “biological” symptoms of depression (that is, sleep, appetite).

Several epidemiological studies also showed that exposure to stressful life events and treatment discontinuation during the COVID-19 pandemic have contributed to the surge in the rate of emotional symptoms observed in youths with NDDs [11, 12]. In addition, the irritable mood frequently associated with emotional symptoms in children and adolescents can contribute to the higher risk of CBs (for example, impulsive, aggressive behaviors). In turn, ill-adapted environmental reactions to these CBs can reinforce the youth’s rejection and isolation and perpetuate a negative mood [13]. This cycle underscores the crucial need for an integrated approach that combines an adequate response to CBs (for example, behavioral interventions and adapted security equipment) with addressing all associated medical/ psychiatric/ developmental risk factors for CBs [14]. Considering the inherent limitations of case reports, further studies providing stronger evidence should be conducted to determine how CB-focused interventions can improve patients’ clinical outcomes (for example, reduced length of stay, improved overall functioning).

While no specific treatment exists for XGS, confirming the AHDC1 mutation helps one to be particularly attentive to known XGS-related medical conditions. A better knowledge of XGS’s clinical phenotype makes us more concerned about the patient’s difficulties in communicating her emotional distress and better addressing her needs. It led us to overturn the diagnosis of schizophrenia and inadequate pharmacological treatment.

This case highlights the importance of an integrative care strategy for youths with complex NDDs due to rare or genetic syndromes. Addressing somatic and psychiatric comorbidities, CBs, and environmental factors through an integrated management approach, in addition to optimized care for IDD, has improved our patient's condition. Future research should include prospective cohort studies and interventional designs, for example, by comparing outcomes using standardized measures (such as the Aberrant Behavior Checklist) between patients with complex neurodevelopmental disorders and severe CBs receiving usual care or an integrative care model, including a comprehensive assessment of all medical, developmental, and psychiatric contributors to CBs.
