Improving Critical Appraisal of Cancer‐Related Health Information: A Pilot Randomised Trial of the Informed Health Choices‐Cancer Programme
Authors: Mengqi Li, Marie Tierney, Claire Beecher, Maura Dowling, Austin G Duffy, Caitriona Duggan, David Robert Grimes, Avril Kennan, Claire Kilty, Allen Nsangi, Andrew D Oxman, Derek C Stewart, Elaine Toomey, Declan Devane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPeople impacted by cancer may be confronted with conflicting and misleading cancer‐related information that is everywhere in daily life. The Informed Health Choices‐Cancer (IHC‐C) programme was developed to equip participants with the skills and knowledge necessary to think critically and make well‐informed health choices.AimsTo assess the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary outcomes of the IHC‐C programme.MethodsWe conducted an online pilot randomised trial to compare the IHC‐C programme with a waitlist control within a prespecified recruitment window. Adults impacted by cancer (current patients, survivors, informal caregivers and loved ones) were recruited and randomised 1:1. The intervention was a 4‐week, nine‐unit online learning programme. Primary outcomes assessed feasibility (recruitment, retention, adherence, technical feasibility, and data‐collection processes) and acceptability (participation rates, participation burden, and overall acceptability). Secondary outcomes included eHealth literacy, critical thinking and decision‐making skills, which were assessed post‐intervention.ResultsForty‐three participants were randomised (22 intervention; 21 waitlist control), achieving 72% of the recruitment target. In the intervention group, 91% accessed the platform and 64% completed all nine units. Outcome assessment completion was 64% (14/22) in the intervention group and 81% (17/21) in the control group. Acceptability was high among intervention completers, as indicated by both quantitative and qualitative measures. eHealth literacy scores were high in both groups, with ceiling effects and no between‐group difference (Hodges‐Lehmann median difference −1.0, 95% CI –5.0 to 1.0). Critical thinking and decision‐making quiz scores were higher in the intervention group in exploratory analyses (median difference: 1.0, 95% CI: 1.0–2.0), although the distributions clustered near the upper range.ConclusionsThe IHC‐C programme was feasible to deliver and acceptable to participants within a defined recruitment period. These findings support progression to a fully powered trial, with refinements to engagement strategies and outcome measurement.Trial registrationThis trial was registered on ISRCTN registry with ID ISRCTN17391470Keywords: misinformation, online learning resource, people impacted by cancer, pilot randomised trial

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of death worldwide and contributes substantially to the global disease burden [1]. Beyond mortality, cancer entails enduring physical, emotional, and social implications that extend across the disease trajectory. These demands generate sustained needs for timely, accurate, and understandable health information. People impacted by cancer, including the patients, survivors, caregivers, and loved ones, always rely on such information to manage care and make informed health choices [2, 3, 4]. Cancer care is therefore not only clinically complex but also inherently information‐intensive [5, 6].

Although healthcare professionals remain the most trusted and preferred sources of information [7, 8], advances in communication technologies have reshaped the dynamics of information seeking. Many individuals impacted by cancer increasingly obtain information outside clinical encounters, potentially reducing direct interaction with professionals [9, 10]. Notably, one in three patients reports actively searching for information independently [11]. Among people impacted by breast cancer, more than 90% report passive exposure to information through media or interpersonal networks, and more than 60% actively seek information online [12].

Expanded digital access, however, has intensified concerns regarding information quality, accuracy, commercial bias, and consistency in cancer care [13]. More than half (52%) of people impacted by cancer report encountering conflicting information during their search for guidance [14]. Research of social media platforms further illustrates the scale of the problem: 41% of the most‐viewed TikTok posts on prostate cancer and 40% of comparable Instagram posts have been classified as misleading [15, 16]; on Pinterest, misinformation rates ranged from 4% to 26% across cancer types [17]; and 44% of cancer‐related posts on X (formerly Twitter) contain misinformation, with nearly one‐third including harmful or inaccurate content [18].

The World Health Organization (WHO) has labelled this environment as an ‘infodemic’, an overabundance of information, including misinformation [19, 20]. Misinformation can be inaccurate, outdated, incomplete, or misleading, and is disseminated irrespective of intent. Misinformation often achieves greater visibility and engagement than factual content and may be up to 28 times more likely to be shared [18, 21]. Consequently, more than 70% of individuals report occasionally accepting misinformation as true [22]. In cancer care, exposure to misinformation may delay clinical treatment, increase psychological distress, distort health behaviours and attitudes, and contribute to preventable harm, including mortality.

Responses to cancer‐related misinformation generally fall into two categories: external control strategies and internal capability‐building strategies [23]. External control strategies target factors outside the individual, including regulatory policies, platform governance, content moderation, and technological safeguards that shape the information environment or restrict exposure to misleading content [24, 25, 26]. Internal capability‐building strategies focus on strengthening individuals' capacity to understand, interpret, and evaluate health information [27, 28, 29]. Although environmental controls are essential, systemic regulation alone cannot eliminate exposure [23]. In complex and emotionally charged contexts such as cancer care, strengthening individual capability represents a sustainable and ethically grounded approach. Rather than attempting to counter each false claim, equipping individuals with transferable appraisal skills offers a scalable response.

Two competencies are central to this approach [30]: health literacy and critical thinking. Health literacy broadly refers to the ability to obtain, understand, and apply health information to support health‐related decisions [31, 32]. Higher‐level competencies within health literacy frameworks, often termed critical health literacy, encompass the critical appraisal of health information and claims. While related to critical health literacy, we conceptualise critical thinking in this study as a distinct competency focused specifically on analysing and appraising the reliability and relevance of health claims. Health literacy provides the broader capacity to access and use health information; critical thinking provides the evaluative skills to judge its trustworthiness. Together, these competencies support informed decision‐making and may protect against the adverse effects of misinformation. Existing interventions provide important components of this agenda. Decision aids present treatment options alongside benefits and harms [33]; deliberative methods expose participants to structured expert input [34]; and participatory approaches encourage active engagement in care [35]. However, these efforts typically address specific decisions or conditions and rarely focus on teaching transferable skills for critically appraising health claims across diverse cancer‐care contexts. Few evaluations of interventions assess critical appraisal competencies [36].

The Informed Health Choices‐Cancer (IHC‐C) programme was developed to address this gap. Building upon the Informed Health Choices (IHC) 49 Key Concepts (KCs) framework, which has been successfully adapted across diverse populations and educational levels [37], the IHC‐C programme prioritised and tailored these concepts to a cancer‐related context [38]. The programme comprises a nine‐unit learning resource developed through stakeholder engagement, iterative refinement, and structured testing [39]. It aims to equip people impacted by cancer with the skills and knowledge to critically evaluate the reliability of health claims and make well‐informed choices. This pilot study tests the feasibility and acceptability of the IHC‐C programme and examines its preliminary effects on eHealth literacy and critical thinking and decision‐making skills among people impacted by cancer. Rather than targeting a single treatment decision, the programme aims to develop transferable appraisal skills applicable across evolving cancer information environments.

Methods

Portions of this section are reproduced from the published protocol for this pilot trial to ensure methodological transparency and to enable comparison between the preregistered protocol on ISRCTN (ISRCTN17391470) and the final report [40, 41], consistent with text recycling best practices (https://textrecycling.org/resources/best‐practices‐for‐researchers/).

Design

We conducted an online, randomised, parallel‐group pilot trial comparing the IHC‐C programme with a waitlist control. This design enabled a controlled assessment of feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary outcomes during the intervention phase, while ensuring that all participants subsequently had access to the programme; therefore, the findings are intended to inform the design of a future definitive trial. Participants were randomised in a 1:1 ratio to receive either the IHC‐C programme immediately or delayed access after the 4‐week intervention period. The trial was unblinded; participants were aware of group allocation and understood that the intervention materials were provided either immediately or after a delay. The trial is reported in accordance with the CONSORT extension to pilot trials [42].

Participants

Adults impacted by cancer were eligible for participation. Four participant groups were included: (1) current patients (diagnosed with any cancer and currently undergoing treatment), (2) cancer survivors (diagnosed with any cancer and completed treatment, with or without ongoing follow‐up care), (3) informal caregivers (provide most unpaid informal care for a person diagnosed with cancer and self‐identify as an informal caregiver), and (4) loved ones (family members or friends who support a person diagnosed with cancer and self‐identify as a loved one). Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, ability to commit to the study for at least 4 weeks, and internet access. Exclusion criteria were current participation in a similar intervention study and, for informal caregivers, caregiving situations that were irregular or frequently changing.

Recruitment

Participants were recruited using convenience sampling through both online and offline channels. Online recruitment included targeted social media campaigns and email outreach to relevant charities, community groups, and cancer‐related organisations. Offline recruitment included printed posters distributed in cancer support centres and affiliated organisations. Interested individuals were directed to an online expression‐of‐interest (EOI) survey, in which they indicated their interests and provided their email addresses. Individuals who expressed interest received detailed study information and an online consent form via email. Confidentiality and secure data‐handling procedures were communicated at this stage. Recruitment was prespecified to occur within a fixed time window (12 May 2025 to 31 October 2025) to assess feasibility of meeting the target sample size within defined operational constraints. Recruitment was not extended beyond this period, and the trial proceeded with the number of participants enroled within the allocated timeframe. This approach allowed evaluation of recruitment capacity under conditions relevant to planning a future definitive trial. Individuals who completed the EOI survey underwent eligibility screening. Eligible participants who provided informed consent and completed baseline assessments were randomised.

Study Procedures

The trial was conducted entirely online (on Moodle). Interested individuals first completed an online EOI form. Eligible individuals received detailed study information and then, within 48 hours, provided electronic informed consent. Following baseline assessments, participants were automatically randomised to either the IHC‐C programme group or a waitlist control group. Participants allocated to the intervention group received immediate access to the IHC‐C online learning platform for 4 weeks. Participants allocated to the waitlist control group received no intervention during this initial period.

During the intervention phase, participants were instructed to complete the programme at their own pace within 4 weeks. Engagement with the learning materials was recorded through system‐generated logs. Participants who had not initiated or completed the programme received a standardised reminder email in week three. If no login activity or minimal engagement was recorded by mid‐week four, a personalised follow‐up email was sent to encourage participation. Participants were provided with a study‐specific email address for technical and procedural support, and queries were addressed within 48 hours.

Participants in the waitlist control group completed baseline assessments without receiving access to the programme during the initial 4‐week study period. At the end of this period, participants in the intervention group completed feasibility and acceptability assessments, and both groups completed secondary outcome measures. Following completion of outcome assessments, participants in the waitlist control group were granted delayed access to the learning platform in accordance with the study design. During this phase, they received the same materials, support, and reminder procedures as the intervention group.

The Informed Health Choices‐Cancer Programme

The IHC‐C programme was developed from the 49 Informed Health Choices Key Concepts, which were prioritised in the initial stage of the IHC‐C project for relevance to people impacted by cancer [38]. The programme consists of a nine‐unit online learning resource designed using evidence‐based pedagogical principles (please see published protocol for more details [41]). Content incorporates multiple educational formats, including narrated texts, videos, online articles, social media posts, and interactive activities. Development followed a human‐centred design approach and involved collaboration with public and patient involvement (PPI) participants, oncologists, cancer nurses, and cancer researchers [39]. Each unit addresses a specific concept and follows a consistent instructional structure, requiring approximately 30 minutes to complete, and underwent iterative drafting, review, and refinement to ensure clarity and alignment with defined learning objectives. Two rounds of pilot testing with key interest holder(s) were conducted prior to implementation in this trial.

The programme is hosted on the Moodle learning management system (LMS) and is accessible via internet‐enabled devices. Participants may revisit units, and progress and responses are saved automatically.

Randomisation and Allocation

After completion of informed consent and baseline assessments, participants were randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either the IHC‐C programme group or the waitlist control group. Randomisation was implemented automatically using the QuestionPro built‐in randomisation function [43], ensuring balanced allocation throughout the recruitment period. Allocation occurred at the individual level and was concealed from the research team during enrolment and assignment.

Due to the nature of the intervention, participants and intervention administrators were not blinded to group allocation. However, outcome data were collected using standardised online surveys; one member of the data analysis team remained blinded to group assignment during analysis.

Sample Size

Consistent with guidance for pilot trials, a formal power calculation was not undertaken. Instead, a target sample size of 30 participants per group (total n = 60) was set to allow for an anticipated 15%–25% attrition rate, with the aim of retaining approximately 20–25 participants per group for the assessment of feasibility outcomes, including recruitment, retention, and adherence (please see published protocol for more details [41]).

Outcomes

Participant Characteristics

Baseline characteristics included age group, gender, educational background, ethnicity, employment status, internet access, participant role (as defined in the eligibility criteria), cancer diagnosis, time since diagnosis (for patients and survivors), and the relationship to the individual with cancer was also recorded for the informal carers/loved ones.

Primary Outcomes

The primary outcomes assessed the feasibility and acceptability.

Feasibility measures included: (1) Recruitment efficiency: the number and proportion of participants who expressed interest, consented, deemed eligible, and randomised within the recruitment period. (2) Retention: the number and proportion of participants who remain in the programme from baseline to the final outcome assessment. (3) Adherence: the extent to which participants follow the intervention protocol and engage with the programme, which includes login frequency, time spent on the platform, and interaction with programme materials. (4) Technical feasibility: the capability of the online platform to deliver the intervention and support user access. (5) Data collection efficacy: the number and proportion of completed outcome assessments and identification of any issues with measurement tools. Feasibility data were collected from system‐generated platform logs and the end‐of‐intervention surveys.

Acceptability measures included: (1) Participation rates: the number and proportion of participants who complete individual units and assigned activities. (2) Participation burden: assessment of the effort required by participants to engage with the programme and complete tasks. (3) Participants' perspectives: based on participants' responses to open‐ended questions about their experience with each unit and the overall programme. (4) General acceptability: participants' perceptions of the programme, including their views on its content, format, duration, overall acceptability, and willingness to recommend. Acceptability data were collected through the online platform analytics and end‐of‐intervention surveys.

Secondary Outcomes

The preliminary effectiveness outcomes collected to inform a future definitive trial were: (1) eHealth literacy, measured with the 8‐item eHealth Literacy Scale (eHEALS) post‐intervention [44], the eHEALS assesses perceived skills in finding, evaluating, and applying electronic health information. Each item is rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), yielding a total score ranging from 8 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater perceived eHealth literacy; (2) critical thinking and decision‐making skills, assessed post‐intervention using the structured evaluation tool Critical Thinking in Cancer and Health Information Evaluation: Scenario‐Based Assessment developed for the IHC‐C programme. The assessment consists of four scenarios related to cancer and health information, each followed by 4‐5 multiple‐choice questions evaluating the ability to identify unreliable claims, judge evidence, and make informed decisions. Responses are scored as correct (1) or incorrect (0), producing a summed score across all items (17 questions total), with higher scores indicating stronger critical appraisal and decision‐making skills; and (3) perceived cognition and behaviour change, captured through two study‐specific questions. Please see published protocol for more details [41].

Statistical Analysis

Analyses of recruitment, allocation, retention, and intervention access followed the intention‐to‐treat principle, using the full randomised sample according to original group assignment.

Baseline characteristics were summarised using counts and percentages for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables.

Feasibility and acceptability outcomes were analysed descriptively. Recruitment efficiency, allocation, retention, and intervention access were summarised using the full randomised sample. Acceptability survey responses were summarised among participants who accessed the intervention and completed post‐intervention assessments. Engagement metrics derived from system‐generated logs, including the number of active days, the number of logins, total time spent on the platform, and recorded activity events, were aggregated at the individual level over the intervention period and summarised using medians and IQRs. Progression through the intervention was summarised using platform‐derived unit completion timestamps aggregated across prespecified study‐week intervals corresponding to the planned intervention schedule. All feasibility figures present directly observed platform‐derived data without modelling or transformation. Free‐text responses to open‐ended acceptability questions were analysed using a descriptive qualitative content analysis, following the approach described by Elo and Kyngäs [45]. This approach is commonly used to summarise brief textual responses in survey data. Responses were reviewed and summarised for content and illustrative examples; this analysis was exploratory and not intended to achieve thematic saturation or generate a formal thematic framework.

Secondary outcomes were analysed using complete cases with available post‐intervention data; missing data were not imputed. Between‐group comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Hodges‐Lehmann estimators with 95% confidence intervals were reported to describe median differences between groups. Distributions of continuous secondary outcomes were visualised using box‐and‐whisker plots with individual participant values overlaid; boxes represented medians and IQRs, and whiskers extended to 1.5 times the IQRs. Exploratory subgroup analyses by participant cancer experience were descriptive and hypothesis‐generating using medians and IQRs. Cancer experience categories were self‐defined at enrolment. In line with the non‐role‐specific aims of the intervention and the conceptual overlap between informal caregivers and loved ones, these categories were combined for analysis due to limited analytic utility when examined separately in this heterogeneous pilot sample.

All statistical tests were two‐sided with a nominal significance threshold of p < 0.05. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons, as secondary and subgroup analyses were exploratory and intended to inform the design of a future definitive trial. Data management and descriptive summaries were performed using Microsoft Excel (version 16.89.1), and statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.2).

Results

Participants' Baseline Characteristics

Baseline demographic and diagnostic characteristics were comparable between the intervention and waitlist control groups (Table 1). A total of 43 participants were randomised. Most participants were female (35, 81%) and aged 25–64 years, with the largest proportions in the 45–54 (15, 35%) and 35–44 (10, 23%) age groups. The majority identified as White (40, 93%), had a university degree (29, 67%), and were employed (28, 65%). Participants represented diverse cancer experiences, including 12 (28%) current patients, 16 (37%) survivors, and 15 (35%) caregivers or loved ones, with breast cancer being the most frequently reported diagnosis (17, 40%). Among caregivers or loved ones, most were first‐degree relatives (9, 21%). The median time since cancer diagnosis among patients and survivors was 2.6 years (IQR 1.0–5.1). Distribution patterns were similar across the intervention and waitlist control groups.

Primary Outcomes

Feasibility

Recruitment Efficiency and Data Collection Efficacy

The EOI survey was viewed 950 times across recruitment channels. Of these, 135 individuals (14%) responded. Among respondents, 47 (35%) started but did not complete the survey and 12 (8.9%) indicated that they were not interested in participating. A total of 76 individuals (56% of respondents) expressed interest in learning more about the trial. Of those expressing interest, 49 (64%) accessed the consent form. One individual (2.0%) did not complete consent and 48 (98%) provided informed consent; three (6.1%) withdrew after consent and 45 (92%) provided baseline data. Two individuals could not be contacted because of invalid email addresses. Forty‐three participants met the inclusion criteria and were randomised, representing 72% of the target recruitment of 60 participants within the prespecified recruitment period. Participants were randomised in a 1:1 ratio, with 22 (51%) allocated to the intervention group and 21 (49%) to the waitlist control group (Figure 1). All randomised participants were included in the intention‐to‐treat feasibility analyses. In the intervention group, 20 participants (91%) accessed the platform. Fourteen (64%) completed all units and the outcome assessment, while six (27%) completed part of the programme but did not complete the outcome assessment. In the waitlist control group, 17 participants (81%) completed the outcome assessment. Feasibility metrics derived from platform logs were available for 20 intervention participants; post‐intervention outcome data were available for 14 intervention participants and 17 control participants.

CONSORT flow diagram.

Retention, Participation, and Completion Rates

Of the 22 participants randomised to the intervention group, 20 (91%) accessed the learning platform and two (9.1%) had no recorded login activity. Fourteen participants (64%) completed all nine units, one (4.5%) completed unit 1‐3, three (14%) completed only unit 1, and two (9.1%) accessed the platform without completing any units.

Progression through the intervention varied but generally advanced over the four study week intervals (Figure 2). During Weeks 1‐2, most participants (15 of 20) remained within units 0–2. Between Weeks 2‐3, progression trajectories diverged: nine participants advanced to unit 6 or higher, whereas 11 demonstrated limited change. Following the reminder issued in Week 3, additional advancement was observed in Weeks 3‐4, with eight participants reaching the final units. By Weeks 4‐5, progression had largely plateaued, reflecting either completion or disengagement. Among participants who completed the intervention (n = 14), progression was sustained across intervals, with 12 reaching the highest units by Weeks 4–5. In contrast, among non‐completers (n = 6), five demonstrated early stagnation within units 0–2, with minimal progression after Weeks 2‐3.

Progression through intervention units across study week intervals.

In the waitlist control group, 17 (81%) of 21 participants completed the follow‐up outcome assessment prior to receiving delayed access to intervention materials.

Adherence and Engagement

Among participants who completed the intervention (n = 14), engagement was sustained across multiple days, with a median of 4.5 active days (IQR 4.0–5.75), 5.5 logins (IQR 4.25–7.0), and 2.0 units per login session (IQR 1.1–2.0). Median total time spent on the platform was 3.53 hours (IQR 2.05–4.04). Recorded interaction with programme materials was high, with a median of 341 system‐generated activity logs (IQR 321.75–369.25).

In contrast, participants who did not complete the intervention (n = 6) demonstrated minimal engagement, with a median of 1.0 active day (IQR 1.0–1.75), 1.5 logins (IQR 1.0–2.0), and 0.1 hours of total use (IQR 0.01–0.27), alongside low recorded interaction of 35 activity logs (IQR 33.25–54.5).

Technical Feasibility

Of the 22 participants allocated to the intervention group, 20 (91%) accessed the platform. All participants who completed the intervention (14/14) reported that the platform was easy to access. Eleven participants (79%) reported no technical issues, while three (21%) reported minor issues that did not affect participation (e.g., difficulty accessing the mobile application) (Table 2). The ‘mark as done’ function on the learning platform enabled monitoring of unit completion and user activity; however, two participants (14%) reported that the number of required clicks created a minor burden during use. Overall, no major technical barriers to intervention delivery were identified.

Acceptability

Quantitative Acceptability

Among participants who completed the intervention (n = 14), acceptability was high across domains. All participants (100%) rated the intervention as acceptable or very acceptable. Thirteen (93%) reported that the programme met their expectations, and 13 (93%) indicated willingness to recommend it to others impacted by cancer (Table 2).

All participants (100%) rated the overall workload as acceptable. Most reported the effort required to complete the intervention as low to moderate (n = 10, 71%), while three (21%) rated the effort as high and one (7%) as very high.

Content quality and clarity were rated as acceptable or very acceptable by all participants (100%), and all considered the content relevant or very relevant to cancer‐related information needs. Thirteen participants (93%) rated the content as useful or very useful.

The delivery format, including videos, images, and written materials, was rated as acceptable or very acceptable by all participants (100%), and participants found it supported learning and was easy to follow. Regarding duration, twelve participants (86%) rated the overall programme length as acceptable or very acceptable, and 13 (93%) rated the length of individual units as acceptable or very acceptable.

Qualitative Acceptability

Nine participants (64%) provided free‐text responses regarding their experience with the intervention. Overall, responses reflected high acceptability. Participants commonly highlighted ease of access, clear, and lay language, and engaging multimodal content with real‐world examples. Several participants valued the programme's emphasis on informed decision‐making and patient autonomy.

Programme is easy to access and easy to understand.

Secondary Outcomes—Preliminary Effects of the Intervention

eHealth Literacy

Total eHEALS scores were high in both groups and clustered toward the upper end of the scale (Figure 4A). The median score was 32.0 (IQR 29.0–35.0) in the control group and 32.5 (IQR 32.0–35.0) in the intervention group. No statistically significant between‐group difference was observed (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, W = 95, p = 0.34; Hodges‐Lehmann estimate of the median difference [control minus intervention] −1.0, 95% CI −5.0 to 1.0).

Secondary outcomes: preliminary effects of the intervention. (A) Distribution of eHEALS total scores by group. (B) Distribution of quiz scores assessing critical thinking and decision‐making skills by group. Boxes represent medians and interquartile ranges; points indicate individual participants.

Critical Thinking and Decision‐Making Skills

Quiz scores assessing critical thinking and decision‐making skills were also high in both groups, with limited variability and clustering near the upper bound of the scale (Figure 4B). The median score was 16.0 (IQR 15.0–16.0) in the control group and 17.0 (IQR 17.0–17.0) in the intervention group. A between‐group difference was observed in this exploratory analysis (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, W = 195, p = 0.0013; Hodges‐Lehmann estimate of the median difference [intervention minus control] 1.0, 95% CI 1.0 to 2.0).

Perceived Cognition and Behaviour Change

Among participants who completed the intervention (n = 14), most reported perceived improvements in their understanding and evaluation of cancer‐related information. Twelve participants (86%) agreed or strongly agreed that their understanding of cancer‐related information and misinformation had improved. Ten participants (71%) agreed or strongly agreed that they had changed or intended to change how they engage with cancer information, for example, by checking sources before liking, sharing, or commenting.

Exploratory Subgroup Analyses by Cancer Experience

Exploratory analyses examined critical thinking and decision‐making skills quiz scores and eHEALS scores by participant cancer experience category (current patient, cancer survivor, or loved one/caregiver).

In the control group, quiz scores showed modest variation across participant types. Current patients and loved ones/caregivers had similar median scores (both median 16, IQR 16–16 and 15–16, respectively), whereas survivors showed slightly lower and more variable scores (median 15, IQR 14.5–16). In the intervention group, quiz scores were uniformly high across participant types, with median scores at or near the upper limit of the scale (median 17, IQR 16–17 for current patients; median 17, IQR 17–17 for survivors; median 17, IQR 17–17 for loved ones/caregivers).

For eHEALS scores, greater between‐participant‐type differences were observed in the control group. Current patients had the highest scores (median 36, IQR 32–40), followed by cancer survivors (median 32, IQR 29–33.5), whereas loved ones/caregivers had lower and more variable scores (median 29, IQR 22–32). In contrast, eHEALS scores in the intervention group were more similar across participant types, with overlapping distributions (median 32, IQR 32–33 for current patients; median 34.5, IQR 33.5–36.25 for survivors; median 32, IQR 32–35 for loved ones/caregivers).

Discussion

This pilot randomised trial demonstrates that delivering the IHC‐C learning resource is feasible within a defined recruitment period and acceptable to individuals affected by cancer. As intended for a pilot trial, the findings are interpreted in relation to feasibility indicators and optimisation of intervention delivery. Recruitment and randomisation were largely completed within the prespecified window; data collection procedures were implemented as planned; and most participants allocated to the intervention accessed and engaged with the programme. Acceptability was consistently high among completers across usability, workload, and perceived relevance. Preliminary signals were observed for critical thinking and decision‐making skills, whereas eHealth literacy scores demonstrated ceiling effects and no detectable between‐group difference. Collectively, these findings support progression to a definitive trial while identifying areas requiring optimisation.

Recruitment processes were operationally successful but revealed important considerations for scale‐up. The expression‐of‐interest survey generated 950 views; however, only 76 individuals expressed interest, and 43 were randomised. Although this fell short of the target of 60 participants, it exceeded the prespecified progression criterion (≥ 70%) and achieved the minimum sample required to assess feasibility parameters. The discrepancy between initial exposure and enrolment underscores a common challenge in fully remote trials: online visibility does not reliably translate into participation [46, 47]. Recruitment was restricted to a fixed time window and relied primarily on social media; modest extension and multi‐channel strategies may improve yield in a definitive trial. Early‐stage engagement metrics should therefore not be assumed to predict enrolment.

Intervention completion was lower in the intervention arm than in the waitlist control arm. Voluntary participation without financial compensation may have contributed to this pattern, as earlier developmental phases included reimbursement. The higher completion observed in the waitlist arm may reflect motivational dynamics inherent to delayed‐access designs, whereby participants remain engaged in anticipation of receiving the intervention [48]. Variable adherence is common in digital survivorship interventions, particularly when intervention burden is self‐managed [49]. Variable completion is consistent with patterns observed in asynchronous digital survivorship interventions, where flexible pacing and self‐directed engagement are integral features rather than indicators of intervention failure. In contrast, retention to outcome assessment among enroled participants was higher in the waitlist control group than in the intervention group (81% vs. 64%). Once baseline procedures were completed, many participants remained engaged through study completion. The fully online format likely reduced logistical barriers. However, participants who completed baseline assessments may represent a motivated subgroup with sustained interest, and the streamlined study flow, with minimal delay between enrolment and intervention access, may have further supported retention.

Engagement patterns provide direction for refinement. Non‐completers disengaged early, with limited progression beyond the first two units, indicating a critical early engagement window. In this pilot, all nine units were released simultaneously, and only one reminder email was sent in week three. Among completers, median logins were 5.5 (IQR 4.25–7.0), with approximately two units completed per session, and several participants completed the programme shortly after the reminder. Taken together with the participant‐reported effort required (21% high effort; 7% very high effort), these findings suggest that reliance on self‐directed progression may be insufficient in a fully voluntary context. A staggered‐release model, combined with more structured reminder prompts, may strengthen sustained engagement. System‐generated logs further showed that completers accessed the programme across multiple days and accumulated greater total usage time, whereas non‐completers demonstrated early stagnation. Login activity clustered toward the end of the week, particularly on Fridays and weekends, suggesting alignment with discretionary time. Scheduling content release or prompts around these periods may improve completion rates.

As a pilot study, this trial was not powered to detect definitive intervention effects; secondary outcomes were therefore interpreted cautiously. Both the knowledge‐based quiz and the eHEALS scores clustered near the upper end of the scales, indicating pronounced ceiling effects. The educational profile of participants, combined with voluntary enrolment into a learning‐focused programme, suggests a self‐selected sample with relatively high baseline information capability, which likely contributed to observed ceiling effects. Restricted variability likely limited sensitivity to detect between‐group differences, even if a true effect were present. Although these measures provided useful descriptive information regarding participant capability, their distributional constraints limit their suitability as primary endpoints in a definitive trial. Future trials may benefit from incorporating performance‐based or scenario adaptive outcome measures capable of detecting incremental gains among participants with higher baseline literacy. The absence of clear between‐group differences should therefore be interpreted as a measurement limitation rather than evidence of ineffectiveness.

No statistically significant between‐group difference was observed in eHealth literacy. Although scores trended higher in the intervention group, statistical power was limited. Time since diagnosis differed modestly between arms at baseline (intervention: 2.0 [0.9–4.3] years; control: 3.9 [1.1–5.4] years), with a longer median duration in the control group, which may be associated with greater cumulative exposure to health information and more developed information‐seeking skills, which may partially explain high control‐group scores. Stratification or adjustment for time since diagnosis should be considered in a definitive trial. Several intervention studies have reported sustained improvements in eHealth literacy at one‐ or 2‐month follow‐up [50]. The absence of post‐intervention follow‐up in the present trial limits assessment of durability. A future trial should incorporate follow‐up assessments aligned with the intervention period (e.g., 4 weeks post‐intervention) and at longer intervals. In contrast, a statistically significant between‐group difference was observed in quiz scores for critical thinking and decision‐making. However, scores in both groups clustered near the upper end of the distribution, and substantial overlap remained. The observed difference should therefore be interpreted cautiously and may reflect distributional characteristics rather than a clear separation in central tendency. Nonetheless, combining objective quiz performance with self‐reported literacy provides a broader assessment of perceived and demonstrated capability. Further psychometric evaluation of the critical thinking measure will be necessary to ensure adequate sensitivity in a fully powered trial.

Subgroup analyses were exploratory and limited by small participant sizes. In the control group, baseline eHEALS scores varied by participant type, with current patients reporting higher scores than survivors and caregivers or loved ones. This pattern may reflect greater exposure to health information during active treatment. Caregivers and loved ones demonstrated lower and more variable scores, consistent with heterogeneous informational roles. In the intervention group, eHEALS scores appeared to converge across participant types, suggesting a potential reduction in disparities relative to the control group. A similar pattern was observed in performance on the critical thinking and decision‐making quizzes. Although preliminary, these findings support formal evaluation of participant type as a potential moderator in a definitive trial. Notably, caregivers and loved ones were combined due to limited numbers for this analysis; therefore, clear operational definitions and prespecified stratification will be required, particularly given the central role of caregivers in information navigation and decision‐making.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. It employed a randomised design with prespecified feasibility objectives and transparent reporting. Detailed system‐generated engagement metrics provided objective insights into usage patterns. Recruitment occurred within a predefined window, and feasibility processes were analysed according to intention‐to‐treat principles. Among completers, acceptability was consistently high across usability, workload, and perceived relevance.

However, limitations are inherent to pilot design. The sample size was small and not powered for definitive effect estimation. The trial was unblinded, and outcome assessment could not be fully blinded. Engagement metrics reflected interaction frequency rather than attention, comprehension, or behavioural change. Finally, the absence of longer‐term follow‐up precludes conclusions regarding sustained impact.

Implications for Future Research

The findings support progression to a fully powered trial while identifying areas for optimisation. Recruitment patterns in this pilot suggest that future studies should further examine strategies to optimise both participation rates and sample representativeness. Two‐thirds of participants held a university degree, and the voluntary, online format likely favoured individuals with higher educational attainment and digital familiarity, which may limit the transferability of these findings. A fully powered trial may benefit from testing additional recruitment pathways, including partnerships with oncology services, survivorship clinics, and cancer support networks, to reach a broader population. Recruitment through clinical settings may require attention to timing and context, as individuals attending active treatment appointments may be less receptive to research invitations. Future studies should also consider collecting brief information from individuals who decline participation or discontinue the programme to better understand barriers to enrolment and engagement. In this pilot, non‐completers typically disengaged within the first two units, suggesting that early engagement is a critical window; understanding the reasons for early discontinuation, whether related to burden, relevance, competing demands, or other factors, would inform retention strategies for a definitive trial.

Future research should also: (1) strengthen early engagement strategies through structured unit release and scheduled reminder prompts; (2) formally evaluate participant type as a potential moderator of intervention effects; (3) predefine analytic approaches that account for varying levels of intervention exposure; (4) refine outcome measures to reduce ceiling effects and improve sensitivity; (5) incorporate post‐intervention and longer‐term follow‐up assessments and (6) broaden recruitment strategies to improve sample representativeness across educational and socioeconomic backgrounds. A definitive randomised controlled trial with adequate statistical power will be required to evaluate effectiveness and to examine moderators of engagement and outcomes.

Conclusion

This pilot randomised trial demonstrates that the IHC‐C programme is feasible to deliver and acceptable within a defined recruitment period. The study provides insight into recruitment dynamics, engagement patterns, subgroup variability, and outcome measure performance. With targeted refinements in programme structuring, reminder integration, and outcome selection, the programme is positioned for rigorous evaluation in a future definitive trial.
