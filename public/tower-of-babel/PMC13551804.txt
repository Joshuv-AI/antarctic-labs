Gnao1 acts as a gatekeeper to alleviate neuropathic pain by silencing pro-nociceptive signaling cascades
Authors: Jieyi Cai, Hui Li, Lü Chen, Ling Luo, Xin Wu, Min Yuan, Maolin Han, Shirong Wen, Changlai Zhu, Yun Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuropathic pain is a debilitating condition with limited effective treatments, highlighting an urgent need for non-opioid analgesic targets. Gαo, encoded by the Gnao1 gene, is an abundant G protein in the nervous system and couples to multiple inhibitory GPCRs, but its role in chronic pain remains poorly understood.MethodsSpatial transcriptomics and bulk RNA-sequencing were performed on the spinal cord and dorsal root ganglia (DRG) of mice following spared nerve injury (SNI). Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated Gnao1 overexpression or knockdown was performed in the spinal cord or DRG, followed by pain behavioral testing using von Frey filaments, acetone evaporation test, and mechanical conflict avoidance (MCA) assay. Microglial activation, neuronal injury, and signaling pathways were examined by immunohistochemistry (IHC), Western blotting (WB), and calcium imaging. In vitro studies used BV2 microglia and primary DRG neurons with lentiviral Gnao1 overexpression and LPS or db-cAMP stimulation.ResultsSpatial transcriptomics revealed that Gnao1 is enriched in the spinal dorsal horn (SDH) and significantly downregulated after SNI. Bulk RNA-seq confirmed Gnao1 downregulation in pain-processing regions (DRG and SDH). IHC showed that its cellular localization shifted from neurons to glial cells. Overexpression of Gnao1 in the spinal cord or DRG markedly alleviated SNI-induced mechanical allodynia, cold hypersensitivity, and pain-related avoidance behaviors. Mechanistically, transcriptomic profiling revealed that Gnao1 overexpression broadly suppressed pro-inflammatory and pro-nociceptive pathways. Experimental validation confirmed that Gnao1 overexpression reduced p38, ERK, and NF-κB phosphorylation, and decreased microglial activation. In primary DRG neurons, Gnao1 overexpression also lowered cAMP levels and suppressed db-cAMP-evoked Ca2+ influx.ConclusionGnao1 acts as a gatekeeper negative regulator of neuropathic pain by silencing distinct pro-nociceptive signaling cascades, and highlights its potential as a non-opioid analgesic target.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-026-02436-6.

Introduction

Chronic neuropathic pain affects 11–40% of the global population and is characterized by persistent hyperalgesia and allodynia, severely impairing quality of life [1, 2]. Current treatments, including pharmacotherapy [3], neuromodulation such as transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation [4], psychological therapy [5], and personalized treatment strategies [6], are limited by insufficient efficacy, drug tolerance, and risks of dependence. The abuse and addiction potential of opioids, in particular, represents a prominent public health crisis [7]. Consequently, there remains a critical shortage of safe and effective analgesics for this highly prevalent condition, making the development of novel therapeutic strategies an urgent medical need.

The pathogenesis of chronic pain involves peripheral and central sensitization processes [8]. Following tissue injury, locally released inflammatory mediators sensitize peripheral nociceptors, which is typically associated with acute pain. If inflammation persists, increased excitability of primary sensory neurons and diminished inhibitory control lead to enhanced neurotransmitter release, glial activation, and central sensitization. Chronic pain states are often linked to long-lasting or even irreversible central sensitization. Various cell surface receptors and ion channels play key roles in this process, including transient receptor potential channels [9], Toll-like receptors [10], G protein-coupled receptors [11], P2X receptors [12], and various sodium, potassium, and calcium channels [13–15]. In recent decades, the deepening understanding of chronic pain mechanisms has provided significant opportunities for identifying new drug targets [16–18].

G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) represent a major class of drug targets, largely due to their involvement in regulating a wide array of physiological and pathophysiological processes [19]. Canonical GPCR signaling is transduced via heterotrimeric G proteins (Gα, Gβ, Gγ subunits). Upon activation, Gα exchanges GDP for GTP and dissociates from Gβγ to regulate downstream effectors to mediate cellular responses [20]. Many GPCRs have been linked to pain modulation [21, 22]. For example, GPR151 is upregulated in the spinal cord after nerve injury and promotes chronic pain via MAPK signaling, and its inhibition alleviates pain [23]. Mechanistic investigations reveal that GPR151 activates ERK-dependent neuroinflammation [24] and couples with P2X3 to modulate calcium signaling and microglial activation [25]. Other GPCRs, including GPR160, GPR55, GPR83, GPR34, GPR40, GPR177, and GPR39, have also been implicated in pain regulation [26–33]. Clinically used analgesics such as cannabinoids and opioids target GPCRs [34, 35].

Chronic pain often involve dysregulation of multiple GPCRs, making it difficult to achieve ideal therapeutic outcomes by targeting a single receptor [36]. Attention has shifted to downstream G proteins, which are considered to offer broader regulatory potential and advantages as drug targets [37]. G proteins consist of various subunits, with the type of Gα subunit largely determining receptor coupling specificity and downstream signaling direction. The Gαo protein (encoded by Gnao1), a member of the Gαi family, is one of the most abundant membrane proteins in the mammalian nervous system. It couples with various GPCRs, such as GABAB receptors, α2-adrenergic receptors, adenosine A1 receptors (A1R), and dopamine D2 receptors (D2R), playing crucial roles in regulating neurotransmitter release, neuronal excitability, and neuronal development [38–40]. Studies show that Gαo-null mice exhibit various neurobehavioral abnormalities, including widespread tremor, epileptic seizures, motor control deficits, and hyperalgesia [41, 42]. Our previous work showed that Gnao1 knockdown in spinal neurons induces “self-mutilation”-like behavior in mice [43], suggesting a potential role for Gαo in pain pathway regulation.

Here we identify Gnao1 as a critical negative regulator of neuropathic pain. Using spatial transcriptomics, bulk RNA-seq, behavioral assays, and in vitro models, we found that Gnao1 is downregulated in the spinal dorsal horn (SDH) and dorsal root ganglia (DRG) after nerve injury. Restoring its expression alleviates pain hypersensitivity and neuroinflammation, while knockdown does not worsen pain. Mechanistically, Gnao1 overexpression suppressed multiple pro-nociceptive and pro-neuroinflammatory signaling cascades, including p38, ERK, NF-κB, and the cAMP/Ca2+ axis, in microglia and DRG neurons. These findings position Gnao1 as a gatekeeper against neuropathic pain and suggest that enhancing Gnao1 function could represent a novel therapeutic strategy.

Materials and methods

Animals

All experimental animals were procured from the Experimental Animal Center of Nantong University. All surgical and experimental procedures were scrutinized and sanctioned by the Nantong University Animal Ethics Management Committee. The animal treatments were carried out in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. C57BL/6J mice were accommodated in pathogen-free breeding compartments and exposed to a 12-hour light-dark cycle, having free access to water and food. The mice utilized in this study were all male and were adapted to the environment for at least 1 week before the experiment. It was guaranteed that all mice were 8–10 weeks old and weighed 21–25 g at the onset of the experiment. The welfare of laboratory mice is scrupulously ensured and their usage is minimized to the greatest extent possible.

Spared nerve injury model

The SNI model, an established rodent model of neuropathic pain, was utilized according to a standard protocol [44]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (induction at 3%, maintenance at 1.5%). A lateral incision was made on the left thigh, and the underlying muscle was gently retracted to visualize the sciatic nerve and its three terminal branches. The tibial nerve and common peroneal nerve were tied with 6–0 silk sutures and transected distally, with a 2‑mm segment removed, while the sural nerve was carefully preserved. The muscle and skin incisions were closed separately using 6‑0 silk sutures. For sham‑operated controls, the sciatic nerve was exposed but left intact. After recovery from anesthesia, the animals were used for subsequent experiments, including behavioral testing.

Behavioral assessments

All behavioral testing and quantitative analyses were performed by experimenters who were blinded to the genotype and treatment conditions of the mice. Behavioral assessments were conducted between 9:00 and 14:00 daily.

von Frey filament assay

Mice were housed in a plexiglass enclosure measuring 8.8 cm × 8.8 cm × 4.4 cm, positioned on a metal mesh with a spacing of 2 mm. The ambient temperature was maintained at (23 ± 2) °C, with humidity levels ranging from 40% to 70%, ensuring a quiet environment throughout the experiment. Prior to the formal testing phase, laboratory animals underwent acclimatization to the experimental conditions for two days, during which they were placed in the test box for durations of 1 to 2 h daily. For formal assessments, mice were allowed an adaptation period of no less than 30 min upon entering the test box before being subjected to stimuli administered in ascending order based on weight, using von Frey filaments that exerted varying pressure strengths from 0.16 g to 4 g. The filaments stimulated the center of each animal’s hind paw until hair fibers bent as an indicator of full force application. Each stimulation lasted for three seconds and was separated by intervals exceeding thirty seconds. Each intensity level was tested five times, and rapid reflexive leg withdrawal elicited by stimulation was recorded as an effective response if it occurred at least three out of five trials.

Mechanical Conflict Avoidance (MCA) test

The MCA test was performed using a commercial three-chamber MCA system to assess voluntary avoidance of noxious mechanical stimulation [45]. The apparatus consists of an aversive brightly lit chamber, a corridor embedded with retractable mechanical probes, and a preferred dark chamber. During testing, mice are placed in the lit chamber and allowed to escape to the dark chamber via the corridor. The mechanical probes within the corridor produce a painful stimulus upon foot contact without causing tissue damage. Prolonged escape latency (the time taken to escape from the lit chamber) reflects enhanced avoidance motivation toward the noxious probes and serves as a key measure of pain‑related behavior in this assay. We conducted a two-day operant MCA test following an adapted protocol recently described in the literature. One day before testing, mice were habituated to the MCA apparatus for 5 min under conditions with the LED light turned off, the partition door open, and the mechanical probes fully retracted (0 mm height). On the test day, each mouse was placed in the lit chamber with the lid closed. After 10 s, the LED light was turned on, followed by removal of the partition door at 20 s. The escape latency was defined as the time from door removal until the mouse crossed the midpoint of the probe-embedded corridor, with a cutoff time of 120 s. After all mice in each given group completed testing at 0 mm probe height, the probe was raised to 5 mm and the procedure was repeated.

Acetone Evaporation Test

To assess cold-evoked nocifensive behavior, a drop (50 µL) of acetone was unilaterally applied to the left hind paw to produce evaporative cooling. The total duration of reflexive withdrawal or attention-directed behaviors (lifting, shaking, or licking of the paw) was recorded for 60 s following application. To avoid behavioral sensitization due to repeated noxious stimulation, acetone was applied only once per test session, and the recorded behavioral durations were subsequently averaged for analysis.

Virus injection

To generate the adeno-associated virus (AAV) for Gnao1 overexpression, the mouse Gnao1 coding sequence (1065 bp, NM_010308.3) was synthesized and cloned into the pAAV-CMV-EGFP-P2A-3xFLAG-WPRE proviral plasmid. The construct was subsequently packaged into the AAV2/8 serotype, designated as AAV-Gnao1. For Gnao1 knockdown, a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting Gnao1 (sequence: 5′-GCGTGGAGTATGGTGACAA-3′) was inserted into the pAAV-U6-shRNA/spgRNA v2.0-CMV-EGFP-WPRE vector and packaged into the AAV2/9 serotype, designated as AAV-Gnao1-shRNA. All viruses were constructed and packaged by OBiO Biology (Shanghai, China).

Virus delivery was performed via two distinct routes: intrathecal injection and DRG injection. For intrathecal injection, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane. The hair over the lumbar region was shaved and the skin disinfected with povidone-iodine solution. Animals were positioned on a plexiglass tube to arch the spine and widen the intervertebral space. Using a Hamilton microsyringe fitted with a 30-gauge needle, 10 µL of virus (titer: 1 × 10¹² TU/mL) was injected into the intrathecal space between the L5 and L6 vertebrae.

DRG injection was carried out according to a previously established method [46]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, and core body temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a heating pad. The skin and muscles on the left side of the lumbar spinal column were incised and retracted layer by layer to expose the intervertebral foramen, allowing direct access to the L4, L5, and L6 spinal nerves and their associated DRGs. A glass micropipette was used to inject 2 µL of virus (titer: 1 × 10¹² TU/mL) directly into the DRG at a rate of 3 µL/min. Following the injection, the micropipette was left in place for 5 min before being slowly withdrawn. The muscle and skin layers were then sutured sequentially. Subsequent experiments were conducted 21 days after virus injection to allow for sufficient transgene expression or knockdown.

Western blotting (WB)

Tissue samples were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). The homogenates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully collected, and protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Solarbio). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per sample) were separated by 10–15% SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST for 1 h at 25 °C and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in TBST containing 0.1% Triton X-100 and 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Primary antibodies used were against: Gnao1, p-ERK, ERK, p-p38, p38, p-NF-κB, and GAPDH (for details, see Table S7). Following three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at 25 °C. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL Plus detection kit (Thermo Fisher) and quantified using an Odyssey Fc imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences). Band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) and normalized to the corresponding loading control on the same blot.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

RNA from tissue or cell samples was isolated using the TRIzol™ reagent (15596018CN, Invitrogen). Template cDNA was prepared by reverse transcription using the SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis SuperMix (11752250; Thermo Fisher Scientific). qRT-PCR was performed using the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (4312704; Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a A StepOne™ Real-Time PCR System. Gene expression levels were quantified using the 2−∆∆Ct method with GAPDH as the internal control. The primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 6.

Immunohistochemistry and immunocytochemistry

L4-L6 spinal cord segments and DRGs were collected from mice transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and post-fixed in the same fixative overnight. Tissue sections were prepared at a thickness of 30 μm (free-floating) for spinal cord or 12 μm for DRGs using a cryostat. For immunocytochemistry, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min at room temperature (RT). Immunofluorescence staining was performed as previously described [47]. Briefly, sections or cells were blocked with 5% donkey serum for 2 h at RT, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies against: Gnao1, CGRP, ATF3, GFAP, NeuN, IBA-1, GS, p-p38, TuJ1, or for cAMP staining (for details, see Table S7). After three washes, sections were incubated with corresponding fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000; Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA) for 1.5 h at RT, washed thoroughly, and mounted with coverslips. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Leica) and analyzed with ImageJ software.

For quantitative colocalization analysis, 5–6 sections per mouse from 3 mice per group were analyzed using ImageJ/Fiji software (National Institutes of Health, USA) with the JACoP plugin. Manders’ colocalization coefficients were calculated after Costes automatic thresholding (Costes P < 0.05) to minimize user bias. M1 represents the fraction of Gnao1 signal overlapping with the marker signal, and M2 represents the fraction of the marker signal overlapping with Gnao1. All colocalization analyses were performed on raw, unprocessed images following background subtraction, as described previously [48].

RNA-sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from the SDH and DRG of mice, including wild-type controls and those with either Gnao1 knockdown or overexpression. Tissues were collected from both the ipsilateral (injured) and contralateral (control) sides 7 days after SNI or CCI. Following extraction, RNA quality was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, Nanodrop, and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads and fragmented. cDNA libraries were constructed through reverse transcription, end-repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform. Raw reads were processed with “fastp” to remove adapters and low-quality sequences, yielding clean reads. These reads were aligned to the reference genome using “HISAT2”, and ribosomal RNA were filtered out using “Bowtie2”. Transcript assembly and expression quantification (FPKM/TPM) were performed using “StringTie” and “RSEM”. Differential gene expression analysis between defined comparison groups was conducted using “DESeq2”, with significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as those with |Fold change| > 1.5 and false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p value < 0.05. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied for multiple testing correction. Functional enrichment analysis of these genes for Gene Ontology (GO) terms and KEGG pathways was performed using a hypergeometric test, with FDR ≤ 0.05 indicating significant enrichment.

Spatial transcriptomics

Tissue samples of spinal cord were collected from naïve mice and from mice at 7 and 14 days after SNI. Samples were flash-frozen in isopentane, embedded in OCT, and cryosectioned onto Visium Spatial slides (10x Genomics). Following H&E staining and brightfield imaging, the optimal tissue permeabilization time was determined using a Visium Tissue Optimization slide. Spatially barcoded cDNA was then synthesized in situ, amplified, and used for library construction according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform. Raw sequencing data were processed with Space Ranger (10x Genomics) for slide image alignment, tissue spot detection, and fiducial alignment. Reads were aligned to the reference genome using STAR, and gene expression matrices were generated with UMI counting and correction. Downstream analysis was performed with Seurat. Gene expression data were normalized with SCTransform. Dimensionality reduction via principal component analysis (PCA) and graph-based clustering were applied to identify distinct spatial domains. Differential gene expression analysis for cluster markers was conducted using MAST, and spatially variable genes were identified with the markvariogram method. Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes for GO terms and KEGG pathways was performed using a hypergeometric test (FDR ≤ 0.05).

Cell culture and transfection

Primary DRG neuron culture

DRG were isolated from postnatal day 1–3 (P1–P3) mice under sterile conditions. The DRGs were digested in DMEM containing 1 mg/mL collagenase IV and 0.25% trypsin at 37 °C for 30 min. Digestion was terminated by adding DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). After centrifugation, cells were resuspended and seeded onto culture dishes pre-coated with poly-L-lysine (0.1 mg/mL) at a density of approximately 5 × 10⁴ cells per dish. The culture medium consisted of Neurobasal-A supplemented with B27 (1×), GlutaMAX (1×), penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL), and 50 ng/mL nerve growth factor (NGF). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 5–7 days, with half-medium changes every 2 days.

BV2 cell culture

BV2 cells (immortalized mouse microglial cell line) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL).

Cell transfection

Recombinant lentiviruses encoding mouse Gnao1 (LV-Gnao1) or empty vector (LV-Con) were constructed using the pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro vector (System Biosciences). For DRG neuron transduction, neurons were cultured for 3–4 days after plating to allow process outgrowth. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh Neurobasal-A/B27 medium containing lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10–20, supplemented with polybrene (8 µg/mL). After 6 h of incubation at 37 °C, the virus-containing medium was replaced with fresh culture medium. Transduced neurons were cultured for an additional 3–5 days to allow Gnao1 overexpression before subsequent experiments. For BV2 cell transduction, the culture medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing lentivirus at an MOI of 10–30, supplemented with polybrene (8 µg/mL). After 18–20 h of incubation at 37 °C, the virus-containing medium was removed and replaced with fresh DMEM. For stable cell line selection, puromycin (2 µg/mL) was added 48 h post-transduction, and resistant cells were maintained in puromycin-containing medium for at least 7 days. Overexpression of Gnao1 was confirmed by Western blotting in both cell types.

Calcium imaging

DRG neurons were cultured in 96‑well black-wall clear-bottom plates for 3–5 days prior to calcium imaging. The Fluo-4 Calcium Assay Kit (Beyotime, S1061) was used for detection of intracellular Ca2+ concentration according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, culture medium was removed and neurons were gently washed once with pre‑warmed Assay Buffer provided in the kit. The neurons were then loaded with Fluo-4 staining solution containing 2 µM Fluo-4 AM, 1× solubility enhancer, and assay buffer. Loading was performed at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After three washes, dye-loaded cells were placed on a temperature-controlled stage (37 °C) of a fluorescence microscope. Images were acquired every 2.36 s. Baseline fluorescence (Ex/Em = 490/525 nm) was recorded for 30–60 s, followed by addition of db-cAMP to a final concentration of 500 µM. Recording continued for 5 min. Changes in intracellular Ca2+ were expressed as ΔF/F = (F – F0)/F0, where F0 is the average baseline fluorescence before stimulation. Five independent experiments were performed. For each experimental condition, at least 30–50 individual neurons were analyzed.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using Prism 9 (GraphPad Software). For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Dunnett’s or Tukey’s post hoc test as appropriate. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). All behavioral, biochemical, and morphological experiments were performed under balanced conditions with appropriate controls and were analyzed in a blinded manner. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Spatial transcriptomic identifies a SDH cluster enriched for Gnao1 with reduced expression after SNI

We performed 10× Genomics spatial transcriptomic sequencing on lumbar (L4-L6) spinal cord sections at 7 and 14 days after unilateral SNI (Fig. 1A). Unsupervised clustering identified 8 clusters (Fig. 1B), which corresponded to neurons (MAP2), astrocytes (GFAP), microglia (Aif1/IBA1, TMEM119), oligodendrocytes (MAG), and oligodendrocyte precursor cells (PDGFRα) (Fig. 1C). Mapping these clusters back onto the H&E-stained tissue sections revealed their anatomy-specific distribution patterns (Fig. 1D). Cluster C5 was enriched in laminae I-III of the SDH, a key region for pain processing, and was therefore selected for further analysis.

Within C5, DEG analysis between the ipsilateral (SNI) and contralateral (control) sides was performed (screening criteria: |Fold change| > 1.5, p-value < 0.05; Table S1). At 7 days post-SNI (dpi), upregulated DEGs on the ipsilateral side included the complement C1q family (C1qa, C1qb, C1qc) and cathepsin S (Ctss), genes previously implicated in inflammatory pain [49–51]. Markers of the astrocytic (GFAP) and microglial (Aif1, TMEM119, Csf1r) activation were also significantly upregulated. At 14 dpi, additional neuropathic pain-associated genes were upregulated ipsilaterally, including the purinergic receptor P2rx4, the potassium channel Kcna4, and the sodium channel Scn3a. Notably, Gnao1 was significantly downregulated in the ipsilateral C5 cluster at both time points (Fig. 1E). Spatial transcriptomic data suggested Gnao1 expression in neurons and microglia (Fig. 1F). Immunohistochemistry in naïve and post-SNI spinal cord confirmed that Gnao1 is predominantly expressed in the SDH (Fig. 1G), co-localizing with NeuN and IBA1 but not GFAP (Fig. 1H; Fig. S1).

Together, these results indicate that Gnao1 is expressed in SDH neurons and microglia and is downregulated after nerve injury, suggesting a potential negative regulatory role in pain progression.

RNA-seq confirms Gnao1 downregulation in ipsilateral DRG and SDH

Whole-transcriptome sequencing was performed on two key pain-related tissues, the DRG and SDH, at 7 days post-SNI (Fig. 2; Table S2, S3). In SDH, besides the marked upregulation of classical central sensitization markers such as GFAP, Aif1, and Csf1r on the ipsilateral side, several other potential pain-related genes were significantly elevated, including Nms and Sprr1a. Nms is involved in circadian rhythm and feeding [52] and may indirectly influence nociception via opioid-related neuroplasticity [53]. Sprr1a, an axon regeneration-related gene [54], shows sustained upregulation in chronic pain and contributes to its maintenance [55]. Conversely, myelin-related genes (Pmp2, Pmp22) and the extracellular matrix glycoprotein gene Mfap5 were downregulated ipsilaterally (Fig. 2A, a2; Table S2). In DRG, the pain-related gene ATF3, Gpr151, Nts and Npy showed pronounced upregulation (Fig. 2B, b2; Table S3). GPR151 promotes neuroinflammation and neuronal hyperexcitability via MAPK/ERK signaling pathways and P2X3 coupling, thereby sustaining chronic pain. Nts and Npy are core component of the endogenous pain-modulating system and act widely across pain pathways through their specific receptors [56–58].

Critically, Gnao1 was significantly downregulated on the ipsilateral side in both DRG and SDH (Fig. 2A, a2; Fig. 2B, b2), which was validated by qPCR and WB (Fig. 2A, a3-a4; Fig. 2B, b3-b4). Immunofluorescence double-labeling in DRG revealed that Gnao1, expressed in both neurons and satellite glial cells contralaterally, shifted predominantly to satellite glial cells on the ipsilateral side after SNI (Fig. 2B, b5). We also examined Gnao1 colocalization with various neuronal markers (NF200, TH, IB4, and CGRP) in naïve DRG, and found that it was broadly expressed across these neuronal subpopulations (Fig. S2).

In summary, transcriptome sequencing demonstrates extensive transcriptomic remodeling in the DRG and SDH following SNI-induced neuropathic pain. Gnao1 is consistently downregulated in critical peripheral (DRG) and central (SDH) pain-processing regions, and its cellular localization within the DRG shifts from neurons to satellite glial cells. This spatiotemporal change in Gnao1 expression, together with the dysregulation of other pain-associated genes (e.g., Sprr1a, Gpr151), collectively suggests that Gnao1 may play an important negative regulatory role in both peripheral and central sensitization during neuropathic pain. Its decreased expression likely contributes to the mechanisms underlying pain chronicity.

Rescue of Gnao1 expression in spinal cord or DRG alleviates neuropathic pain

To functionally investigate the role of Gnao1 in neuropathic pain, we employed viral-mediated strategies to manipulate its expression in the spinal cord and DRG, and systematically evaluated the impact on SNI-induced pain behaviors.

We first examined the effect of overexpressing Gnao1 in the spinal cord. Three weeks prior to SNI or sham surgery, AAV-Gnao1 (an AAV expressing full-length Gnao1) or an empty control vector (AAV-Con) was intrathecally administered. Robust GFP expression confirmed successful viral delivery (Fig. 3A, a1). This intervention successfully increased Gnao1 protein levels in the SDH (Fig. 3A, a2-a3). Without affecting basal nociception, AAV-Gnao1 treatment significantly attenuated SNI-induced pain hypersensitivity, as evidenced by increased mechanical withdrawal threshold in the von Frey test, reduced duration of lifting/licking behaviors in the acetone evaporation test, and shortened escape latency in the MCA test (Fig. 3A, a4–a6). It also suppressed SNI-induced upregulation of IBA1, GFAP, and CGRP in the ipsilateral SDH (Fig. 3A, a7-a9). DRG-specific AAV-Gnao1 similarly elevated Gnao1 levels (Fig. 3B, b1-b2) and reversed pain behaviors (Fig. 3B, b3–b5). Moreover, Gnao1 overexpression in the DRG reduced the number of for ATF3-positive neurons, a marker of neuronal injury (Fig. 3B, b6).

We next asked whether knocking down Gnao1 would exacerbate injury-induced pain hypersensitivity. AAV2/9 expressing Gnao1-targeting shRNA (AAV-Gnao1-shRNA) or AAV-Con was delivered intrathecally into the spinal cord or via microinjection to the L4–L6 DRG three weeks before SNI (Fig. 4A, a1; Fig. 4B, b1). AAV-Gnao1-shRNA effectively reduced Gnao1 protein levels in both tissues (Fig. 4A, a2-a3; Fig. 4B, b2-b3). Gnao1 knockdown did not affect basal nociception nor exacerbate pain (Fig. 4A, a4-a6; Fig. 4B, b4-b6). A plausible explanation is that SNI itself substantially decreases Gnao1 expression, potentially driving it to a functional minimum, thereby masking any additive effect of the experimental knockdown.

In summary, restoring Gnao1 expression in either the spinal cord or the DRG produced analgesic effects, whereas its knockdown did not aggravate the pain phenotype.

Transcriptomic reveals broad suppression of pro-nociceptive pathways by Gnao1 overexpression

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the analgesic effect of Gnao1 overexpression and the lack thereof following its knockdown, we performed RNA sequencing on SDH and DRG tissues collected 7 days post-SNI from mice with spinal or DRG-specific Gnao1 overexpression, knockdown, and their respective controls.

In SDH, Gnao1 overexpression upregulated 75 DEGs, primarily involved in histamine secretion and metabolism, and downregulated 929 DEGs. The downregulated genes were significantly enriched in immune system processes and multiple pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, including the MAPK, TNF, Toll-like receptor, and NF-κB pathways (Fig. 5A, a2 − a3; Table S4). This downregulated gene set contained numerous well-established pro-nociceptive and pro-neuroinflammatory molecules (Fig. 5A, a4), including specific GPCRs (e.g., Gpr84, Gpr132), Toll-like receptors (e.g., Tlr2, Tlr9), the purinergic receptor P2rx7, the ion channel Trpv1, STING-interferon signaling axis-related molecules, and various pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1α, IL-1β, and Ctss). In contrast, Gnao1 knockdown induced only 95 upregulated genes, primarily enriched in general inflammatory responses (Fig. 5B, b1 − b3). Notably, the potent pro-nociceptive genes significantly downregulated by overexpression were not upregulated in the knockdown group, indicating that knockdown failed to elicit transcriptional effects opposite to those of overexpression. Twenty overlapping genes between overexpression-downregulated and knockdown-upregulated sets were predominantly immunoglobulin subtypes (Fig. 5C), suggesting that although Gnao1 overexpression and knockdown exert opposing regulatory effects on a subset of immune-related genes, knockdown failed to broadly upregulate the key pro-nociceptive pathway molecules suppressed by overexpression. This may represent the molecular basis for knockdown inability to produce functional alterations in pain phenotypes. Collectively, the spinal transcriptomic data indicate that the analgesic effect of Gnao1 overexpression stems from its coordinated suppression of multiple pro-inflammatory and pro-nociceptive signaling modules (GPCRs, TLRs, P2X7, TRPV1, and the STING axis). The failure of knockdown to worsen pain may stem from its modest and counteracting effects (e.g., upregulation of some inflammatory pathways but downregulation of AGE-RAGE) (Fig. 5D).

In the DRG, transcriptomic changes exhibited similar bidirectional characteristics. Gnao1 overexpression significantly reshaped the transcriptional landscape, with DEGs strongly enriched in immune response pathways (Fig. 6A; Table S5). A key finding was the specific downregulation of genes encoding various immunoglobulin subtypes, as well as other pain- and inflammation-related molecules, such as the sensory neuron-specific GPCR Mrgprb5 and the anti-inflammatory protease inhibitor Slpi (Fig. 6A and C). Conversely, Gnao1 knockdown led to the upregulation of genes with clear pro-nociceptive potential, including heat shock proteins Hspa1a/Hspa1b, the pro-neuroinflammatory lipid chaperone Fabp7 [59], and the inflammatory cytokine IL-1β (Fig. 6B and C). Although knockdown also affected pathways related to circadian rhythm and stress response, its net effect remained an upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators (Fig. 6B).

In conclusion, the analgesic effect of Gnao1 overexpression in both the spinal cord and DRG is primarily achieved through its coordinated suppression of multiple convergent pro-inflammatory and pro-nociceptive signaling pathways, positioning Gnao1 as a core negative regulator of neuroinflammation.

Gnao1 overexpression suppresses p38/ERK/NF-κB and cAMP signaling to relieve neuropathic pain

It is well known that p38, ERK, and NF-κB are classic pro-nociceptive pathways. After nerve injury, p38 activation in SDH microglia promotes IL-1β, TNF-α, and BDNF release, enhancing neuronal excitability and central sensitization [60]. Meanwhile, ERK activation in SDH neurons phosphorylates NMDA/AMPA receptors, increasing glutamatergic transmission and pain hypersensitivity [1]. NF-κB, as a downstream transcriptional integrator of p38 and ERK, further upregulates inflammatory cytokines and amplifies persistent neuropathic pain [61]. Therefore, we validated this. WB and immunohistochemistry results showed that, compared with the control group, overexpression of Gnao1 in the spinal cord significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of p38, ERK, and NF-κB signaling (Fig. 7A), suggesting that Gnao1 blocks the development of central sensitization at the spinal level by inhibiting the p38/ERK/NF-κB cascade. Similarly, activation of p38 and ERK in the DRG was markedly suppressed in the Gnao1 overexpression group (Fig. 7B, b1-b4), indicating that Gnao1 also inhibits pro-nociceptive signaling at the level of peripheral sensory neurons, thereby reducing peripheral sensitization.

The cAMP signaling pathway plays a central role in promoting hyperalgesia and neuronal excitability in the context of sensory neurons and pain [62]. Upon tissue inflammation or injury, numerous inflammatory mediators are released locally, which activate GPCRs on sensory neuron terminals and thereby initiating the cAMP signaling. Immunostaining for cAMP in DRG sections revealed that cAMP signals were significantly decreased in the Gnao1 overexpression group (Fig. 7B, b5), indicating that Gnao1 overexpression effectively inhibits the activity of the cAMP signaling pathway and consequently blocks GPCR-cAMP axis-mediated hyperalgesia.

Taken together, overexpression of Gnao1 alleviates neuropathic pain by simultaneously inhibiting multiple pro-nociceptive signaling pathways, including p38, ERK, NF-κB, and cAMP, thereby reducing both central sensitization and peripheral sensitization.

Gnao1 overexpression suppresses LPS-induced inflammation and inhibits p38/NF-κB signaling in BV2 microglia, as well as p38/ERK and cAMP/Ca2+ signaling in DRG neurons

Inflammatory responses and microglial activation are key drivers of chronic pain. We therefore established an in vitro inflammatory model using LPS-stimulated BV2 microglia. LPS treatment significantly increased the mRNA levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 (Fig. 8A). To investigate the role of Gnao1, BV2 cells were transduced with a lentivirus carrying the Gnao1 sequence (LV-Gnao1) or empty vector (LV-Con). WB confirmed elevated Gnao1 protein levels in the LV-Gnao1 group (Fig. 8B). After 24 h of LPS stimulation, qPCR revealed that, qPCR revealed that LV-Gnao1-transduced cells exhibited significantly lower IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α expression compared to LV-Con controls (Fig. 8C), suggesting that Gnao1 overexpression suppresses LPS-induced microglial inflammation. WB further showed that Gnao1 overexpression inhibited LPS-induced activation of the p38 and NF-κB signaling pathways (Fig. 8D). Thus, in BV2 microglia, Gnao1 alleviates LPS-induced inflammation by suppressing the p38/NF-κB axis.

Similarly, in cultured DRG neurons, LPS stimulation upregulated cytokine (Fig. 9A). LV-Gnao1 transduction increased Gnao1 protein levels (Fig. 9B), attenuated LPS-induced cytokine expression (Fig. 9C), and suppressed activation of p38 and ERK1/2 signaling (Fig. 9D). Gnao1 overexpression also decreased the proportion of cAMP-positive neurons (Fig. 9E). Because elevated cAMP in sensory neurons leads to increaseD intracellular Ca2+, we stimulated control and Gnao1-overexpressing DRG neurons with the cAMP analog db-cAMP and measured Ca2+ responses using the Fluo-4 calcium imaging assay. In control neurons, db-cAMP elicited a robust Ca2+ response, whereas Gnao1-overexpressing showed a significantly reduced ΔF/F0 and a lower percentage of responding cells (Fig. 9F). These results indicate that in DRG neurons, Gnao1 overexpression not only inhibits LPS-induced inflammation via the p38/ERK pathway but also reduces db-cAMP-evoked Ca2+ influx by lowering cAMP levels, thereby decreasing neuronal excitability and nociceptive sensitivity.

In summary, Gnao1 overexpression simultaneously inhibits p38/NF-κB-driven inflammation in microglia and p38/ERK/cAMP-Ca2+ excitability axis in DRG neurons, contributing to pain relief.

Discussion

Neuropathic pain remains a major clinical challenge due to its complex pathophysiology and the limited efficacy of current treatments. In this study, we employed the SNI model to investigate the role of Gnao1 in neuropathic pain. Spatial transcriptomics of the spinal cord after SNI identified a dorsal horn cluster enriched for Gnao1 that was significantly downregulated post-injury. Bulk RNA-seq confirmed Gnao1 downregulation in both SDH and DRG following SNI. Immunohistochemistry showed that under normal conditions, Gnao1 is primarily neuronal, after nerve injury, its expression decreases and its localization shifts to satellite glial cells in the DRG and to microglia in the spinal cord. Overexpression of Gnao1 in the spinal cord or DRG alleviated SNI-induced pain behaviors. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that Gnao1 overexpression broadly suppresses pro-inflammatory and pro-nociceptive pathways. We validated that Gnao1 inhibits p38/ERK/NF-κB signaling, reduces neuroinflammation and glial activation, and lowers cAMP-dependent Ca2+ responses in DRG neurons. These findings establish Gnao1 as a negative regulator of neuropathic pain and offer a potential target for non-opioid analgesic development.

Gnao1 as a convergent brake on pain-associated transcriptional reprogramming

Our spatial transcriptomic analysis of the spinal cord after SNI revealed that Gnao1 is enriched in a dorsal horn cluster (C5) corresponding to laminae I-III, a key region for pain processing. Within this cluster, Gnao1 was significantly downregulated at both 7 and 14 days post-injury, a finding confirmed by bulk RNA‑seq and qPCR in both SDH and DRG. Importantly, the genes downregulated by Gnao1 overexpression in the SDH were strongly enriched for immune system processes and the NF-κB pathway, including multiple GPCRs (Gpr84, Gpr132), Toll-like receptors (Tlr2, Tlr9), P2rx7, Trpv1, and proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1α, IL-1β, Ctss). This transcriptional signature indicates that Gnao1 acts as a broad-spectrum suppressor of neuroinflammation. For instance, GPR84 is a pro-inflammatory GPCR that amplifies microglial activation and has been implicated in neuropathic pain [63]. Similarly, P2X7 receptors are well-known triggers of NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL-1β release in chronic pain [64, 65]. By simultaneously suppressing these multiple pro-nociceptive modules, Gnao1 prevents the feed-forward inflammatory loops that drive pain chronification.

In contrast, Gnao1 knockdown produced only modest and heterogeneous transcriptional changes, failing to upregulate the same set of pro-inflammatory genes that were suppressed by overexpression. This may explain why knockdown did not aggravate pain behavior, that is, endogenous Gnao1 is already profoundly reduced after nerve injury, and further knockdown cannot push it below a functional floor. The partial overlap between genes downregulated by overexpression and those upregulated by knockdown (mostly immunoglobulins) suggests that Gnao1’s regulatory scope extends beyond simple opposition, it may also engage context-dependent mechanisms that are not fully captured by loss-of-function approaches.

Gnao1 inhibits the p38/ERK/NF-κB cascade in both central and peripheral compartments

We validated that Gnao1 overexpression significantly reduces the phosphorylation of p38, ERK, and NF-κB in the SDH and DRG after SNI or LPS stimulation in vitro. These three pathways are intimately linked to pain hypersensitivity. The p38 activation in spinal microglia is an early event after nerve injury, leading to the production of IL-1β, TNF-α, and BDNF, which subsequently enhances neuronal excitability [60]. ERK activation in spinal neurons phosphorylates NMDA and AMPA receptors, promoting central sensitization [1]. NF-κB serves as a downstream transcriptional integrator of both p38 and ERK, driving the expression of multiple inflammatory genes [66]. Our finding that Gnao1 simultaneously dampens all three nodes indicates that it acts upstream of these cascades. Given that Gαo is coupled to inhibitory GPCRs (e.g., GABAB, adenosine A1 receptors), its activation is known to decrease adenylyl cyclase activity and reduce cAMP levels [67], which in turn limits PKA-mediated activation of p38 and ERK. This aligns with our observation that Gnao1 overexpression reduces cAMP levels in DRG neurons. Thus, Gnao1 may tonically restrain the p38/ERK/NF-κB axis through a Gαi-cAMP-PKA signaling relay.

The cAMP-Ca2+ axis: a novel downstream effector of Gnao1 in sensory neurons

An unexpected but important finding was that Gnao1 overexpression not only suppressed LPS-induced inflammation but also reduced basal cAMP levels and blunted db-cAMP-evoked Ca2+ influx in DRG neurons. The cAMP-Ca2+ crosstalk is well established in nociceptors, elevated cAMP activates PKA, which then phosphorylates and sensitizes TRPV1 and HCN2 channels, leading to Ca2+ entry and enhanced neuronal firing [62]. Additionally, cAMP can activate Epac, which triggers Ca2+ release from intracellular stores. By lowering cAMP, Gnao1 disrupts this positive feedback loop. Our calcium imaging data directly demonstrated that Gnao1 overexpression significantly reduced both the peak amplitude and the percentage of responding neurons to db-cAMP. These results suggest that Gnao1 exerts a dual brake, it limits both inflammatory signaling (via p38/ERK/NF-κB) and direct excitatory signaling (via cAMP-Ca2+) in DRG neurons. This dual action is particularly relevant for the treatment of chronic pain, where peripheral sensitization and central sensitization co-exist.

Why did Gnao1 knockdown fail to worsen pain?

One might expect that reducing Gnao1 would remove a negative regulator and thus exacerbate pain. However, we found no significant difference between the AAV-Gnao1-shRNA group and the control group in pain behaviors. This can be explained by three non-exclusive possibilities. First, nerve injury itself already decreases Gnao1 expression to a very low level, such that additional knockdown cannot produce further change. The “floor effect” is strongly supported by our quantitative expression data. RNA-seq and qPCR revealed that after SNI, endogenous Gnao1 mRNA and protein levels in the SDH and DRG are reduced by approximately 70‑80% compared to sham controls. This substantial downregulation suggests that residual Gnao1 expression is already near a functional minimum, therefore, further knockdown by AAV-shRNA may not reduce Gnao1 below a functionally relevant threshold, masking any additional behavioral effect. Second, compensatory mechanisms may exist, such as partial substitution of Gαo function by other Gi/o family members (e.g., Gαi1, Gαi2, Gαi3). Third, our RNA-seq data showed that knockdown induced a mixed transcriptional response, in which some inflammatory genes were upregulated whereas others (e.g., AGE-RAGE pathway) were downregulated, potentially neutralizing each other. This contrasts with the strong, unidirectional suppressive effect observed with Gnao1 overexpression. Collectively, although the absence of a loss-of-function phenotype does not rule out an endogenous role for Gnao1, the robust gain-of-function effects together with the injury-induced downregulation provide strong circumstantial evidence that Gnao1 acts as a negative regulator. Thus, gain-of-function strategies may be more effective than loss-of-function approaches for therapeutic intervention targeting Gnao1.

Cell-type specificity: microglia versus neurons

Our immunostaining revealed that under naïve conditions, Gnao1 is predominantly expressed in neurons. However, after nerve injury, its expression becomes detectable in microglia of the SDH as well, suggesting that Gnao1 may acquire additional functions in glial cells under pathological conditions. To directly test this possibility, we employed BV2 microglial cells and found that Gnao1 overexpression suppressed LPS-induced p38/NF-κB activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production, indicating a direct anti-inflammatory effect in microglia. In DRG neurons, Gnao1 overexpression also inhibited p38/ERK signaling and reduced cAMP-dependent Ca2+ responses. Thus, Gnao1 acts in both cell types to limit pain. The dual cellular targeting is advantageous because neuropathic pain involves both neuronal hyperexcitability and glial-driven neuroinflammation. Therapeutic strategies that enhance Gnao1 function (e.g., using positive allosteric modulators of Gαo-coupled receptors or direct Gαo activators) could simultaneously dampen both compartments.

Comparison with other GPCR- and G protein-based pain targets

Several GPCRs have been explored as analgesic targets, but most clinical trials have yielded mixed results. For example, CB2 receptor agonists showed promise in preclinical models but failed to demonstrate robust efficacy in human neuropathic pain [68]. Similarly, targeting individual GPCRs such as GPR151 or GPR160 has been effective in rodent models but may not address the redundancy of inflammatory mediators [69]. In contrast, targeting the common downstream G protein Gαo offers the advantage of integrating signals from multiple inhibitory GPCRs that converge on Gαi/o. Indeed, Gαo is the most abundant G protein in the nervous system and is activated by diverse antinociceptive receptors (e.g., GABAB, adenosine A1, α2-adrenergic, µ-opioid). Enhancing Gαo function could therefore amplify the natural inhibitory tone without the side effects associated with direct receptor agonists (e.g., opioid-induced respiratory depression). Our study provides proof-of-concept that increasing Gnao1 expression is sufficient to relieve neuropathic pain, and the effect size (e.g., ~ 70% increase in Gnao1 protein) was well tolerated without overt behavioral abnormalities. Nevertheless, these findings are based on rodent models, and translation to human therapy will require further validation.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the viral overexpression approach is not cell‑type specific and therefore does not discriminate between the contribution of neuronal versus microglial Gnao1 to the analgesic effect. Future studies using conditional knockout or knockin mice in specific cell populations will be necessary to dissect cell-type-specific contributions. Second, we focused on SNI models, whether Gnao1 is similarly involved in other pain etiologies (chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy, diabetic neuropathy, or bone cancer pain) remains to be tested. Third, although we demonstrated that Gnao1 overexpression inhibits p38/ERK/NF-κB and cAMP/Ca2+ signaling, the direct biochemical link between Gαo and these pathways requires further investigation. Future studies employing co-immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry could help identify Gαo-interacting proteins under both basal and inflammatory conditions. Additionally, proximity-labeling techniques such as BioID or APEX2, combined with proteomic analysis, may provide a comprehensive map of the Gαo signaling complex in sensory neurons and microglia, thereby revealing previously unrecognized binding partners that mediate its anti-nociceptive effects. Fourth, growing evidence indicates that neuropathic pain involves sex-dependent mechanisms, with microglial responses predominating in males and T cell mediated pathways in females [70]. Our main experiments were performed exclusively in male mice, which is a clear limitation. To address this, we overexpressed Gnao1 in female mice and conducted behavioral tests after SNI. The results showed that Gnao1 overexpression also alleviated pain behaviors in females (Fig. S3), suggesting no obvious sex difference in its analgesic effect. Future studies encompassing both sexes and mechanistic analyses are needed to fully evaluate the translational potential of Gnao1-targeted therapies. Finally, translational hurdles include the need for a safe and efficient method to upregulate Gnao1 in humans, which might be achieved by intrathecal delivery of AAV-Gnao1, as we did in mice, or by small-molecule modulators that enhance Gαo activity.

Conclusions

In summary, this study establishes Gnao1 as a previously underappreciated negative regulator of neuropathic pain. Gnao1 expression is consistently downregulated in the DRG and SDH after nerve injury. Restoring Gnao1 expression alleviates pain hypersensitivity by concurrently suppressing pro-nociceptive and pro-neuroinflammatory pathway in both microglia and sensory neurons (Fig. 10). Although the absence of a pain-exacerbating phenotype after Gnao1 knockdown does not conform to a classical loss-of-function expectation, the robust gain-of-function effects, together with the injury-induced downregulation and the reversal of multiple pain-related pathological changes, strongly suggest that gain-of-function strategies may be more promising than loss-of-function approaches for therapeutic development targeting Gnao1. Therefore, targeting Gnao1 might offer a new avenue for non-opioid analgesics that address both the inflammatory and hyperexcitability components of chronic pain, but this possibility requires further investigation in preclinical and clinical settings.

Fig. 10Schematic diagram of Gnao1 regulation in chronic pain: downregulation promotes neuroinflammation and neuronal hyperexcitability, while overexpression exerts analgesic effects. Under physiological conditions, Gnao1 is expressed in neurons and glial cells (microglia in the spinal dorsal horn and satellite glial cells in the DRG). After SNI, Gnao1 expression is downregulated (top panel), leading to reduced synthesis of the Gαo protein, decreased levels of free GTP-Gαo, and increased dissociated Gβγ dimers. Both changes result in elevated cAMP levels, activation of downstream MAPK pathways and ion channels, enhanced NF-κB phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, and subsequent production of various proinflammatory cytokines, thereby triggering neuroinflammation (upper middle panel). In the DRG (upper left), Gnao1 downregulation increases cAMP, activates p38/ERK signaling, and promotes Ca2+ influx, which enhances neuronal excitability and facilitates pain signal transmission. In the spinal dorsal horn (upper right), Gnao1 downregulation activates p38/ERK and NF-κB signaling in microglia, promoting microglial activation and the release of abundant inflammatory mediators, which further contribute to the initiation and maintenance of chronic pain. By contrast, Gnao1 overexpression in the spinal cord and DRG (lower panel) inhibits cAMP production and Ca2+ influx in DRG neurons, thereby suppressing neuronal hyperexcitability. In the spinal dorsal horn, Gnao1 overexpression inhibits microglial activation and the inflammatory response, collectively alleviating chronic pain. Black arrows indicate causal relationships; red upward arrows denote promotion or increase; green downward arrows denote inhibition or decrease
