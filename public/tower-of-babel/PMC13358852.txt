Critical appraisal of open lung ventilation strategies in thoracic surgery: the role of PEEP and recruitment maneuvers
Authors: Andres Zorrilla-Vaca, Michael C Grant, Matthew B Allen
Published: 2026

Approximately one third of patients who undergo lung resection develop postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) (1), which remain the leading cause of morbidity and mortality after thoracic surgery (1). These patients are often exposed to multiple contributors to acute lung injury (ALI), including pre-existing lung disease, surgical trauma, and other mechanisms intrinsic to one-lung ventilation (OLV), such as atelectrauma and ischemia-reperfusion injury. Optimizing intraoperative ventilatory management is therefore a central component of lung-protective care in thoracic surgery. Research has increasingly focused on “open-lung” ventilation strategies, which typically involve interventions to maximize alveolar compliance by utilizing positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and/or alveolar recruitment maneuvers to prevent and reverse atelectasis.

Whether these improvements in lung mechanics translate into improved postoperative outcomes has remained uncertain. The PROTHOR trial addressed this question in a large, multicenter, randomized comparison of higher fixed PEEP (10 cmH2O) with recruitment maneuvers against a conventional lower-PEEP approach (5 cmH2O) during OLV and found no reduction in PPCs despite improved intraoperative oxygenation (2). Moreover, the trial observed more hemodynamic instability in the intervention group (37% vs. 14% hypotensive events). The trial was stopped early for futility and potential harm. These results are an important contribution to the literature not because they suggest that PEEP is unimportant, but because they expose the limits of applying a fixed intraoperative open-lung strategy across a heterogeneous thoracic surgical population. PROTHOR therefore shifts the question from whether higher PEEP with recruitment is beneficial in general to considerations of which patients may benefit, under what conditions, and as part of what broader perioperative lung-protective strategy.

A higher-PEEP open-lung strategy has the potential to counteract several mechanisms involved in ALI, particularly in the setting of OLV, where alveolar collapse, oxidative stress and a systemic inflammatory response predispose to lung injury. Physiologic studies have demonstrated that increasing the level of PEEP from 5 to 10 cmH2O can promote more homogeneous ventilation and reduce ventilation-perfusion mismatch, particularly when combined with recruitment maneuvers (3). However, the relationship between PEEP and lung protection is thought to be U-shaped. Excessive PEEP with recruitment maneuvers may provoke a cascade of harmful effects increasing the risk of barotrauma and volutrauma with diffuse epithelial disruption and surfactant dysfunction accompanied by increased driving pressure (DP) and mechanical power.

In PROTHOR, investigators compared two fixed ventilatory approaches during OLV: higher PEEP (10 cmH2O) with recruitment maneuvers versus lower PEEP (5 cmH2O) without routine recruitment. These settings were informed by physiologic data suggesting favorable effects on compliance and oxygenation (4), but they were not individualized to patient-specific mechanics or recruitability. While a PEEP of 10 cmH2O may be a reasonable practice for many patients, the generalizability of this approach may be limited by heterogeneity in body habitus, respiratory mechanics, and expiratory flow limitation (5).

The higher-PEEP recruitment strategy appeared to improve respiratory mechanics and alveolar gas exchange, but did not reduce the composite PPC endpoint within 5 postoperative days (53.6% vs. 56.4%). Similarly, a recent post-hoc analysis found that an individualized open-lung ventilation approach did not reduce mechanical power—a metric associated with ALI (6). Other isolated intraoperative ventilatory strategies, including lower tidal volume (4 vs. 6 mL/kg) or recruitment maneuvers in the absence of PEEP titration have not consistently improved PPCs (7).

PROTHOR should be interpreted alongside other trials of perioperative lung-protective strategies (4,8,9). In contrast to PROTHOR’s pragmatic fixed intraoperative approach, the iPROVE-OLV trial evaluated a broader strategy that combined individualized intraoperative PEEP titration with selective postoperative high-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) and reported significantly fewer severe PPCs (6% vs. 15%) (8). Taken together, these studies raise the possibility that isolated intraoperative ventilatory optimization may be insufficient to improve postoperative outcomes, and supplementation with targeted perioperative respiratory support may be more effective.

Prespecified subgroup analyses suggested possible heterogeneity in treatment effect, with signals of benefit in patients who underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and among those with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). These findings should be interpreted cautiously, because subgroup analyses in an otherwise negative trial are best viewed as hypothesis-generating, particularly in the absence of adjustment for multiple comparisons. Nevertheless, the subgroup findings are physiologically intriguing. The apparent signal in thoracoscopic cases may reflect differences in transpulmonary pressures and chest wall mechanics associated with thoracic insufflation but could also relate to broader differences in extent of surgical injury, postoperative pain, or baseline patient characteristics compared with open procedures. The use of high PEEP and recruitment maneuvers in patients with COPD requires careful consideration due to the risk of overdistention and heterogeneous peripheral airway obstruction (10). Among patients with preexisting pulmonary hyperinflation, the magnitude of intrinsic PEEP increases during OLV (11), raising the possibility that patients with expiratory flow limitation and baseline hyperinflation may be especially vulnerable to overdistention and exacerbated pulmonary shunt with external PEEP.

Rather than identifying clinical subgroups for whom routine use of higher PEEP with recruitment is advisable, PROTHOR underlines the likelihood that response to such interventions is heterogeneous. Identifying patient phenotypes defined by recruitability and ventilation heterogeneity during OLV has been successfully demonstrated (12), and could potentially inform use of open-lung strategies in high-risk thoracic surgical populations (13).

The cardiovascular consequences of high PEEP and recruitment maneuvers reflect increased intrathoracic pressure and right ventricular afterload, with effects depending on baseline cardiac function, volume status, chest wall elastance, and pulmonary vascular resistance (14,15). In PROTHOR, intraoperative complications were more frequent with higher PEEP and recruitment maneuvers (49.8% vs. 31.3%), including higher rates of hypotension (37.3% vs. 14.3%) and new arrhythmias (9.9% vs. 3.9%). Although these events did not result in measurable organ dysfunction (i.e., acute myocardial infarction, acute renal failure, stroke), their higher frequency in the intervention arm further weakens the case for routine use of a fixed higher-PEEP recruitment strategy, particularly in patients with limited cardiovascular reserve. Other ventilatory strategies based on electrical impedance tomography (EIT) have emerged to incorporate hemodynamic features such as cardiac output and ventilation/perfusion matching (16). In the setting of OLV, combining these measurements could guide PEEP titration while minimizing hemodynamic impairment.

In summary, PROTHOR argues against the routine use of a fixed higher-PEEP recruitment strategy for all patients undergoing OLV during thoracic surgery. At the same time, it does not rule out a role for open-lung approaches applied more selectively, individualized to patient-specific respiratory mechanics, or integrated into perioperative respiratory support pathways. Future studies should prioritize phenotype-guided patient selection, individualized ventilatory management, and selective integration of respiratory support in the postoperative period to optimize recovery from thoracic surgery.
