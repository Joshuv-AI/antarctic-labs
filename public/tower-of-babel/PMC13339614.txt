Preserved Spermatogenesis and Hormonal Function in Men Receiving a Pretomanid Regimen (BPaMZ) for Pulmonary Drug-Resistant Tuberculosis
Authors: Pauline Howell, Antonio Lombardi, William Brumskine, Lali Mikiashvili, Kim Boekelheide, Joanna Moreira, Alda Holsta, Matthew Betteridge, Lindsay Thompson, Angela Crook, Paul Bruinenberg, Maria Beumont, Eugene Sun, Francesca Conradie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pretomanid is a component of the World Health Organization-recommended treatment regimen for drug-resistant tuberculosis in patients aged ≥ 14 years. While preclinical rodent studies identified testicular toxicity as a potential clinical liability at elevated doses, no corresponding effects were observed in nonhuman primates nor clinical hormone assessments. This phase 2 clinical trial assessed testicular safety of therapy with bedaquiline, pretomanid, moxifloxacin, and pyrazinamide in 26 adult males with drug-resistant tuberculosis recruited from South Africa and Georgia. The study included 26 weeks of treatment followed by 52 weeks of safety monitoring until study week 78. The primary endpoint measured change in total sperm count from baseline at week 26, with secondary endpoints evaluating sperm concentration, semen volume, and reproductive hormone concentrations. Results demonstrated favorable testicular safety outcomes. Mean total sperm count increased from 48.8 to 68.8 × 106 per ejaculate by week 26. Fifteen participants (68%) showed increased sperm counts, while 17 (77%) exhibited higher sperm concentrations from baseline to week-26 Thirteen participants (59%) experienced ≥ 50% improvement in both parameters. Two participants demonstrated > 50% decreases in total sperm count; however, sperm concentration reductions were < 50%, suggesting low semen volume as a factor. Testosterone levels increased from baseline, peaking at week 44, while inhibin B demonstrated steady increases, peaking at week 78. Follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinizing hormone remained stable throughout the study period. All assessable participants achieved sputum culture conversion by week 12. Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed no correlation between pretomanid plasma concentrations and sperm count changes. These findings provide reassurance that pretomanid does not impair human spermatogenesis nor disrupt reproductive hormone homeostasis.

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global health challenge, with an estimated 10.7 million people developing the disease in 2024.1 Prior TB treatment regimens required lengthy multidrug therapy with significant tolerability issues and high rates of nonadherence, often resulting in increased mortality and drug resistance.2 The estimated number of people affected by rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB) in 2023 was 390,000, of whom only ~164,500 (42%) received treatment.1

The development of pretomanid represents a significant advancement in TB treatment. In August 2019, pretomanid received US FDA approval as part of the BPaL regimen (bedaquiline, pretomanid, and linezolid) for treating adults with highly drug-resistant pulmonary TB.3–5 Currently, the World Health Organization recommends BPaLM (BPaL plus moxifloxacin) regimens as standard of care for treating patients with RR-TB, with BPaL serving as the preferred first-line regimen for pre-extensively drug-resistant (preXDR) TB.2 Importantly, these pretomanid-containing regimens are approved for routine use in patients aged 14 years and older, establishing their role across adolescent and adult populations globally. This WHO endorsement represents a paradigm shift toward shorter, more effective treatment options for drug-resistant TB. Pretomanid-containing regimens enable 6-month, all-oral treatment for eligible patients with RR-TB and are recommended by WHO in preference to longer regimens. When pretomanid cannot be used, patients often revert to substantially longer treatment (e.g., 9 months or ≥ 18 months) with greater burden and potential toxicity. Therefore, targeted clinical data addressing potential male reproductive toxicity are needed to support safe, equitable access to these shorter, effective regimens.

Preclinical studies have suggested a potential class effect of nitroimidazole antibiotics, with pretomanid and metronidazole showing similar testicular toxicity, particularly at the seminiferous tubules, occurring in rodent models.6,7 Metronidazole, a long-established nitroimidazole used widely in adults and children, also produced dose-and duration-dependent degeneration of the seminiferous epithelium and infertility in rats at high exposures, with fertility restored in most animals after a drug-free recovery period; in contrast, clinical guidance notes no evidence that metronidazole affects fertility in men. Delamanid, another nitro-heterocycle used in RR-TB in adults and children, had no effect on male or female fertility in animals.

In nonclinical toxicology studies of pretomanid, testicular degeneration and atrophy occurred in rats with repeated doses of pretomanid at ≥ 30 mg/kg/day (which results in exposure ~ 1.4-fold that of the indicated clinical dose). Testicular effects showed evidence of being partially reversible, albeit very slowly, in rats dosed for 7 days at 100 mg/kg/day; however, testicular effects were not reversible in rats dosed for 14 days. As expected, there was a dose-related decrease in fertility in male rats who received pretomanid ≥ 30 mg/kg/day that was associated with decreased sperm count and motility. This effect on fertility in male rats was partially reversible at 30 mg/kg/day but irreversible at 100 mg/kg/day (Ambroso et al, unpublished data, submitted manuscript, 2026). Juvenile toxicology studies were conducted in rats from postnatal day 7 to 63 at 0, 3, 10, 30, or 100 mg/kg/day, and included a 4-or 16-week recovery period in one study and reproductive function in another. In these studies, testicular findings were limited to the 100 mg/kg/day group compared to 30 mg/kg/day in adult rats demonstrating that juvenile animals are possibly less sensitive, but certainly no more sensitive to the testicular toxicity compared to adult rats.

Repeat-dose toxicology studies in sexually mature monkeys (0, 50, 150, or 300 mg/kg/day for up to 3 months, with a 20-week recovery period) found no direct testicular effects of pretomanid and no effects on testosterone, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), or inhibin B at any dose tested (up to 4.5 times the human equivalent dose).8 At 150 and 300 mg/kg/day, sperm motility and total sperm count decreased, and abnormal sperm increased, but these changes were considered secondary to reduced food intake and weight loss rather than a direct testicular effect. All sperm parameters returned to control levels during recovery as food intake and body weight improved.

Reproductive hormones provide a mechanistic readout of testicular function. Luteinizing hormone (LH) and FSH are pituitary gonadotrophins; LH stimulates testosterone synthesis by Leydig cells, whereas inhibin B is produced by Sertoli cells within the seminiferous tubules. In seminiferous tubule degeneration or testicular atrophy, inhibin B would be expected to fall with a compensatory rise in FSH; testosterone may also fall if Leydig cell function is impaired.9,10

Male reproductive hormones were measured across four clinical trials of pretomanid in more than 800 male participants (NCT01498419, NCT 02193776, NCT 02342886 and NCT03338621). An analysis and summary of these data has been published.11 The hormone data demonstrated either no change with pretomanid treatment or an improvement in male reproductive hormone parameters, correcting a borderline hypogonadal state that existed before treatment due to TB disease and the associated systemic illness. In study NC-002 (NCT01498419), pretomanid was evaluated at the doses of 100 and 200 mg daily for 8 weeks and there was no difference between doses in hormone parameters comparing values at baseline and end of treatment or during posttreatment follow-up; pretomanid is known to be dose-proportional at steady state (AUC(0–24 h,ss)) in this dose range.11

The primary purpose of the BPaMZ/SEM study was to evaluate the potential effect of once daily pretomanid (200 mg) on human sperm parameters and reproductive hormones while being dosed as part of the antimicrobial regimen BPaMZ (bedaquiline, pretomanid, moxifloxacin, and pyrazinamide) for 26 weeks in adult male participants with drug-resistant TB.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This was an open-label phase 2 study that aimed to rule out clinically severe adverse effects on testicular function. The study was designed to enroll participants with drug-resistant TB, defined per protocol as resistance to either rifampicin or isoniazid or both. BPaMZ regimen was chosen over the BPaL (bedaquiline, pretomanid, and linezolid) regimen to avoid a confounding effect since linezolid-related testicular atrophy and seminiferous tubule degeneration were seen in rats and dogs.12 The study was designed before the SimpliciTB results13 were available, which subsequently showed not only rapid culture conversion/strong bactericidal activity but also increased hepatotoxicity with the BPaMZ regimen compared with the control regimen.

Participants were treated for 26 weeks with the following daily regimen: pretomanid 200 mg, bedaquiline 200 mg during the first 8 weeks and 100 mg during the following 18 weeks, moxifloxacin 400 mg, and pyrazinamide 1,500 mg. After completion of treatment, participants entered a 52-week follow-up period. An external committee continuously monitored semen and safety parameters throughout dosing and did not find any signal of reproductive toxicity that required a change in study conduct or early termination.

The study protocol and all associated documents were reviewed and approved by the independent ethics committees and/or institutional review boards at each participating site. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the initiation of any study procedures, in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was sponsored by the Global Alliance for TB Drug Development (TB Alliance). This study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04179500) on November 25, 2019 prior to enrollment of any participants.

Participants and procedures

Study participants were enrolled at three sites in South Africa (N = 18) and one site in Georgia (N = 8) between September 2021 and December 2022. Sixty-five participants were screened and 26 enrolled. Key inclusion criteria included male sex; age 18 years and older; positive sputum molecular test for TB; diagnosis of TB resistant to rifampicin and/or isoniazid but susceptible to fluoroquinolones. The key reasons for exclusion included: unable to confirm drug susceptibility at screening; resistance to fluoroquinolones; inability to produce a semen sample; azoospermia; HIV infection with low CD4+ count (< 100 cells/μL); and chronic hepatitis B or C. Per protocol, participants were required to use a double method of contraception throughout treatment and for 18 weeks after the last dose.

Semen samples were obtained at baseline, week 12, week 26, and week 44. The week 44 samples (18 weeks after completion of treatment) were collected to ensure coverage of potential delayed toxicity or eventual recovery of semen parameters encompassing 1.5 cycles of seminiferous epithelium (cycle length is ~75 days) including epididymal transit time (~10 days). At each time point, the semen parameter value was the average of values from two semen samples collected at least 7 days apart using standardized collection protocols.

Participants were required to abstain from sexual activity for at least 48 h prior to sample collection, and treatment adherence was monitored through participant diaries.

Blood samples for hormone measurements were obtained at baseline, week 2, 4, 8, 12, 26, 44, and all follow-up visits. Sputum cultures evaluating sputum culture conversion were obtained at baseline, monthly on treatment, and at every follow-up visit. Sparse plasma sampling for pretomanid pharmacokinetic analysis was performed to assess any exposure–response relationships.

Rigorous safety monitoring was performed, with safety bloods (including full liver function tests) done weekly for the first 8 weeks, monthly thereafter until end of treatment and in follow-up. At baseline, potential participants with elevated liver enzyme results below three times the upper limit of normal could be discussed with the trial medical monitor for inclusion, but higher liver enzyme levels were exclusionary. Hepatitis B and C testing was part of the required screening procedures and a confirmatory test excluded the participant from the trial. Detailed hepatotoxicity guidance was available for the management of participants with elevated liver enzymes.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was the change in total sperm count (sperm/ejaculate) from baseline at end of treatment (week 26). Secondary endpoints included change in total sperm count at week 12 and 44; changes over time in sperm concentration (sperm/mL), semen volume and male reproductive hormones (testosterone, inhibin B, FSH, and LH); proportion of participants with sputum culture conversion to negative status in liquid culture (MGIT) at week 4, monthly on treatment and every subsequent visit through week 78; change in weight and BMI from baseline; incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), defined as adverse events that started or worsened on or after the first administration of BPaMZ up to and including 14 days after the last study drug was administered; safety laboratory measurements, including observed and change from baseline; vital signs; quantitative and qualitative electrocardiogram (ECG) results. Plasma concentrations of pretomanid were summarized using descriptive statistics.

Statistical analysis

The primary analysis was carried out on the intention-to-treat (ITT) population (defined as all participants who took at least one dose of study medication) and the per-protocol (PP) population (defined as the ITT population excluding participants who discontinued study treatment before week 26 (primary endpoint assessment) and/or had major protocol deviations for analysis). TB efficacy analysis was carried out on the ITT, TB-specific modified ITT (TB-MITT), and TB-specific PP (TB-PP) populations. Safety analyses were undertaken on all enrolled participants who received at least one dose of treatment.

Twenty evaluable male participants who completed the trial were considered adequate to rule out substantial effect of the regimen on sperm parameters as agreed to with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA). This target was pragmatic rather than formally powered. It was intended to exclude clinically severe effects on testicular function rather than detect small changes. Analyses were descriptive and focused on participant-level change from baseline; a ≥ 50% decline in total sperm count and/or sperm concentration was considered potentially clinically meaningful when interpreted alongside absolute values and WHO lower reference limits (39 × 106 per ejaculate and 16 × 106/mL, respectively). Twenty-six participants were enrolled to account for any participants withdrawing consent, discontinuing treatment or being lost to follow-up.

All statistical analyses were based on descriptive statistics for the whole population and by key groups. All analyses were carried out using STATA Version 18.0. There were three planned analyses: one after all participants had completed 26 weeks of treatment, another at week 44, and a final analysis at week 78.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics and disposition

Of 26 participants enrolled, 22 (85%) completed treatment with BPaMZ for 26 weeks and contributed data toward the primary endpoint (Figure 1). Eighteen (69%) participants completed the additional 52-week safety monitoring follow-up. Four participants discontinued therapy early due to elevated liver enzymes,2 resistance to pyrazinamide identified after enrollment1 and withdrawal of consent.1 Eighteen participants completed follow-up through 78 weeks.

The mean age of the 22 participants who completed treatment (Table 1) was 35 years (range 18 to 58 years), 68% were Black, and 36% were living with HIV (all with CD4+ counts > 100 cells/μL and receiving antiretroviral therapy). Their mean BMI was 20 kg/m2 (range 15.3 to 25.6 kg/m2). Drug resistance patterns included rifampicin resistance only (42%) and rifampicin and isoniazid resistance (58%), with all participants confirmed susceptible to fluoroquinolones and second-line injectables.

Sperm parameter outcomes

The primary objective of this study was to assess the change from baseline in total sperm count at 26 weeks (Figure 2, Table 2). Ancillary analyses of sperm concentration were also carried out as no single semen parameter is expected to predict fertility potential. Spermatogenesis was improved at week 26, as shown by an increase in mean total sperm count from baseline of 20.0 × 106 sperm/ejaculate (48.8 to 68.8 × 106 sperm/ejaculate), and by a shift to the right in sperm count distribution. There was large variability in sperm parameters among the participants, but mean sperm count increased at week 26 regardless of baseline count, which ranged between 0.4 and 337.0 × 106 sperm/ejaculate, including in participants with a low sperm count and concentration at baseline.

At week 26, 15/22 participants (68.2%) experienced an increase in sperm count and 17/22 (77.3%) showed increases in sperm concentration, with 13/22 (59.1%) showing a 50% or greater improvement in both. 7/22 participants (31.8%) exhibited a decrease in total sperm count but only 2/22 had a 50% or greater decline in sperm count during dosing, with week 26 absolute values of 36 and 106 million/ejaculate (typical reference range in healthy fertile men is ≥ 39 million/ejaculate).14 These two participants’ sperm concentrations had a less than 50% decline, suggesting that lower ejaculate volume at week 26 (rather than impaired spermatogenesis) may have contributed in these individuals. A detailed case narrative review of the two participants with > 50% declines in total sperm count identified plausible alternative explanations, including reduced semen volume, intercurrent illness, and expected intraindividual variability, with no supportive hormonal pattern for testicular toxicity. Five out of 22 participants (22.7%) had a decrease in sperm concentration.

No meaningful changes in sperm and semen endpoints occurred between weeks 26 and 44, with no increases that would suggest benefit from discontinuing pretomanid or TB treatment. Mean semen volume was unchanged throughout follow-up. Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed no correlation between plasma concentrations of pretomanid and the change from baseline in sperm count (Figure S1), as higher pretomanid trough concentrations were not associated with smaller increases in sperm count, suggesting the absence of a dose–response relationship for reproductive toxicity.

Reproductive hormones

Reproductive hormone changes showed an improvement in gonadal status, consistent with the improvement in spermatogenesis (Figure 3). Median testosterone rose from 547 ng/dL at baseline to 600 ng/dL at week 26, peaking at 646 ng/dL at week 44. Median inhibin B increased from 122 pg/mL at baseline to 137 pg/mL at week 26 and reached 172 pg/mL at week 78. FSH and LH remained stable throughout follow-up, consistent with an absence of treatment-associated testicular toxicity.

Tuberculosis treatment efficacy

Sputum cultures converted to negative by week 12 in all microbiologically assessable participants (13 participants with positive cultures at baseline) and remained negative for all assessable participants until the completion of follow-up at week 78. At the week 78 follow-up, favorable outcomes were observed in 11/14 participants (78.6%) in the TB-MITT population and 12/12 (100%) in the TB-PP population. No microbiologically confirmed failures or relapses were observed among assessable participants; non-favorable status reflected participants who were un-assessable due to early discontinuation and/or missing endpoint cultures.

Safety and tolerability

The adverse event profile was as expected for this regimen. Treatment-emergent adverse events (Table 3) were reported in ~ 81% of participants, with the most common being pleuritic pain (19.2%), upper respiratory tract infections (15.4%), and hyperuricemia (15.4%). Two participants discontinued study treatment due to hepatic enzyme elevations (Table S1). Both developed marked transaminase elevations during treatment (including grade 4 ALT), but did not qualify as Hy’s law. Both events resolved after treatment withdrawal.

There were two serious adverse events during the treatment phase: infective exacerbation of bronchiectasis (considered unrelated to treatment, the participant completed treatment but subsequently died from meningitis during the follow-up phase, also considered unrelated to study treatment) and increased liver enzymes (considered possibly related to treatment, treatment was discontinued). Two additional serious adverse events occurred during follow-up: lung abscess and cystic lung disease, both deemed unrelated to treatment.

A total of 14 hepatic TEAEs were reported in 8/26 (30.8%) participants, and 5/26 (19.2%) participants had at least one grade ≥ 3 hepatic laboratory event (worst grade 4 in one participant). Because 14 hepatic TEAEs occurred in eight participants, some participants experienced more than one hepatic event. The liver enzyme elevations were generally reversible and consistent with known hepatotoxicity profiles of the component drugs. Increased uric acid was observed in six participants, likely due to the use of pyrazinamide, a well-known effect of this drug. No clinically meaningful findings were observed in vital signs or ECG results.

The safety and tolerability profile of the BPaMZ regimen was as expected for the four drugs included. No new safety concerns overall or specifically related to testicular function were identified.

DISCUSSION

Study implementation and challenges

To our knowledge, this is the first study to prospectively investigate testicular safety in men with drug-resistant TB. The study was challenging to implement due to several factors, including lack of experience at participating sites in investigating reproductive endpoints, coordination complexities across multiple countries with different regulatory environments and the need for standardized laboratory protocols across different countries with varying laboratory capabilities. Furthermore, cultural stigma surrounding masturbation and semen sample collection in certain populations, length of required abstinence and difficulty ensuring adherence to abstinence requirements required a multifaceted approach involving education and community leaders. In addition, there is a large and expected variability in sperm parameters which are further compounded by the age, underlying comorbidities and TB disease of the participants.

Primary findings

Spermatogenesis generally improved during treatment with BPaMZ for 26 weeks, as shown by increases in total mean sperm count and sperm concentration, including in those participants with a low sperm count and concentration at baseline. Although direct longitudinal semen data during first-line treatment of drug-susceptible TB are limited, gonadal endocrine status appears to improve with clinical recovery during effective TB therapy as evidenced by testosterone increases after treatment completion in pulmonary TB cohorts.15 We would expect a similar direction of change in drug-resistant TB with efficacious therapy, and our findings suggest pretomanid does not attenuate this recovery-associated improvement in spermatogenesis. Only sporadic decreases in sperm count exceeding 50% were seen in two participants, and this decrease was not seen in sperm concentration, suggesting a reduction in semen volume may have been a factor, as well as ongoing comorbidities. Secondary analyses of sperm parameters at 12 and 44 weeks aligned well with the primary results. Mean semen volume remained stable throughout the 44-week follow-up.

Great variability was observed within and between participants in sperm count, sperm concentration, and semen volume. No evident correlation was detected between the mean pretomanid trough concentration and the primary outcome, which is also supportive of a lack of effect of pretomanid on spermatogenesis. The data suggest that pretomanid does not negatively impact reproductive safety in adult males with DR-TB at exposures associated with the clinically recommended dose.

Male reproductive toxicity observed in a rat fertility study has informed precautionary restrictions for males of reproductive potential and has, in turn, complicated enrolment of boys aged < 14 years in pretomanid studies. Interim findings from an ongoing single-dose pediatric pharmacokinetic study (NCT05586230) have characterized pediatric pretomanid pharmacokinetics without a safety signal to date. Together with the current BPaMZ-SEM semen and hormone data, this provides reassurance to support planned multiple-dose pediatric studies enrolling both boys and girls, with appropriate monitoring and in accordance with local regulatory requirements.

Hormonal assessment

Male reproductive serum hormones (testosterone, inhibin B, FSH, and LH) were evaluated at baseline and over time. If pretomanid treatment were associated with testicular toxicity, one would expect to see the following: no change or a minimal decrease in testosterone, an increase in FSH, no change or minimal increase in LH, and a decrease in inhibin B. Hormone profiles were not indicative of testicular toxicity, as in fact there was an improvement over time in the gonadal status of the study participants, consistent with an overall improvement in their health status, as shown by a progressive increase in weight and BMI.

The hormonal findings in the present study are also consistent with those from four prior trials of pretomanid in men with drug-susceptible TB.11 The mean sperm count between week 26 and week 44, which represents dechallenge of pretomanid, did not increase, further supportive of a lack of effect of pretomanid on spermatogenesis.

Supportive evidence

Results from a paternity survey conducted in four completed studies of pretomanid or pretomanid-containing regimen (of 4–6-month duration) provide additional supportive information associated with pretomanid administration. Males who had participated in these studies answered a limited questionnaire about partner births that occurred while they were receiving or after they had completed treatment with pretomanid. The number of children fathered among pretomanid-exposed participants (46 children fathered by 40/322 men completing the survey) was comparable to the HRZE comparator group (26 children fathered by 21/82 men).

Study limitations

This study enrolled adult men only and therefore cannot directly address potential effects in peripubertal boys or young adolescents. However, animal data are reassuring: juvenile rats were no more sensitive, and possibly less sensitive, than adults to pretomanid-related testicular toxicity, suggesting that adolescents are unlikely to be at greater risk than adults.

Further limitations of the study include the small sample size in this difficult-to-accrue study, the substantial expected variability in sperm count both between and within participants, and the largely unknown normal sperm counts in men with protracted illness and drug-resistant tuberculosis disease. The study was conducted across multiple sites in different countries, which presented logistical challenges for standardized laboratory analysis and cultural considerations regarding semen collection.

Despite these limitations, the consistent semen parameter findings across multiple timepoints and the supportive hormonal data provide evidence for the reproductive safety of pretomanid in this population. The external monitoring committee provided continuous oversight and there were no safety signals requiring protocol modification.

The improvement in reproductive parameters observed during treatment likely reflects the overall health improvement associated with effective TB treatment.

CONCLUSIONS

This study provides the first targeted evaluation of male reproductive safety in patients with drug-resistant TB receiving the BPaMZ regimen. Improvement in sperm count and reproductive hormones suggests that pretomanid does not impair spermatogenesis or reproductive function in humans at clinical exposures, and that observations in preclinical rodent studies are likely to be species specific, similar to metronidazole. BMI (and body weight) increased over time, indicating overall improvement in the health status of these participants, which may have contributed to improved spermatogenesis.

The study adds to the body of evidence that pretomanid-containing regimens can be used safely in male patients of reproductive age without concerns about adverse effects on fertility. These findings support the continued use and expansion of pretomanid-containing regimens in the treatment of drug-resistant tuberculosis.
