 Executive Summary
Stainless-steel clad Pu from Japan’s Fast Critical Assembly (FCA) reactor is currently being
dispositioned at the Savannah River Site. The electrolytic dissolver, operated by Savannah River
Nuclear Solutions in H-Canyon, is being utilized to dissolve the material. The resulting solutions
are transferred to the Concentration, Storage, and Transfer Facilities, operated by Savannah
River Mission Completion (SRMC), for subsequent vitrification at the Defense Waste Processing
Facility (DWPF). In support of the FCA mission startup, a preliminary evaluation was conducted
by the Savannah River National Laboratory to assess the impact of the FCA discards on the liquid
waste system.

Since the FCA preliminary evaluation was issued, a revision to the fissile mass loading was
approved by Department of Energy – Environmental Management with a U fissile mass loading
of 7,144 g/m3 in addition to the existing International Atomic Energy Agency safeguards and
security limit of 2,500 g/m3 total Pu. Discussions between H-Canyon and SRMC led to several
evaluations of FCA discards to Sludge Batch 11 (SB11), rather than waiting until SB12. The
projected mass of Pu from FCA discards (up to 344 kg) is a significant fraction of the total Pu in
the Tank Farm (~40%). This quantity of Pu has never been transferred to one sludge batch in the
history of DWPF processing. Previous glass testing demonstrated that 1 wt.% Pu could be
incorporated in glass under well mixed conditions; however, undissolved PuO 2 was present in the
glass after less ideal mixing of the batch materials. While FCA discards will not reach 1 wt% Pu
in glass; historical DWPF pour stream data only contains up to 0.01 wt.%. To address the data
gap, the objective of this study was to fabricate a glass containing ~0.2 wt% Pu and confirm that
there are no undissolved Pu phases present if all FCA discards are transferred to SB11. Rather
than adding PuO2 to the melter feed as was done previously, an FCA simulant was selected that
would go through a neutralization step to mimic H-Canyon processing followed by chemical
processing of SB11 sludge to represent DWPF operations.

For the sludge-only and coupled operations glasses produced in this study, the measured U and
Th concentrations were lower than targeted. All other analytes were consistent with target
concentrations. A second coupled operations glass was fabricated with additional U 3O8 to correct
for the low U concentration in the first glass. Several sludge components were lower than the
targeted concentrations due to the loss of material during the experimental melter feed fabrication
(Fe, Mn, Ni, Ti), which reduced the waste loading to 38% from the target of 40%. Pu solubility is
independent of these compositional changes in the glass matrix, and the low U and Th
concentrations and reduced waste loading do not impact the overall conclusions of this work. At
a measured Pu concentration in glass of ~0.2 wt%, undissolved Pu phases were not identified in
any of the glasses evaluated in this study. The Pu concentrations in each of the fabricated glasses
is more than double the amount that is projected from FCA discards (~0.07 wt% Pu). From a Pu
perspective in glass, there are no issues with adding all FCA metal and oxide batches to SB11.

1.0 Introduction

Stainless-steel clad Pu from Japan’s Fast Critical Assembly (FCA) reactor is currently being
dispositioned at the Savannah River Site. The electrolytic dissolver, operated by Savannah River
Nuclear Solutions in H-Canyon, is being utilized to dissolve the material. The resulting solutions
are transferred to the Concentration, Storage, and Transfer Facilities, operated by Savannah
River Mission Completion (SRMC), for subsequent vitrification at the Defense Waste Processing
Facility (DWPF). In support of the FCA mission startup, an evaluation was conducted by the
Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL) to assess the impact of the FCA discards on the
liquid waste system. At that time, the 2,500 g/m3 fissile mass loading limit in glassa,1 had been
approved by the Department of Energy-Environmental Management (DOE-EM), starting at
Sludge Batch 11 (SB11). To generate sludge batch projections for the FCA evaluation, the SRMC
System Planning group assumed that a further increase to 3,500 g/m 3 would be approved by the
start of FCA discards into SB12.2 To meet the Fe:Pu-239eq ratio of 160:1 for DWPF processing
and the 3,500 g/m3 limit, it was necessary to split the FCA discards among three sludge batches
(SB12 through SB14).3

Since the FCA preliminary evaluation was issued, another revision to the fissile mass loading was
approved by DOE-EM; a U fissile mass loading of 7,144 g/m3 in addition to the existing
International Atomic Energy Agency safeguards and security limit of 2,500 g/m 3 total Pu.4
Discussions between H-Canyon and SRMC led to several evaluations of FCA discards to SB11,
rather than waiting until SB12. The projected mass of Pu from FCA discards (up to 344 kg) is a
significant fraction of the total Pu in the Tank Farm (~40%). 5 This quantity of Pu has never been
transferred to one sludge batch in the history of DWPF processing. Previous glass testing using
a PuO2 source demonstrated that 1 wt.% Pu could be incorporated in glass under well mixed
conditions; however, undissolved PuO2 was present in the glass after less ideal mixing of the
batch materials.6 While FCA discards will not reach 1 wt% Pu in glass; historical DWPF pour
stream data only contains up to 0.01 wt.%. To address the data gap, the objective of this study
was to fabricate a glass containing ~0.2 wt% Pu and confirm that there are no undissolved Pu
phases present if all FCA discards are transferred to SB11. Rather than adding PuO 2 to the melter
feed as was done previously, an FCA simulant was selected that would go through a neutralization
step to mimic H-Canyon processing followed by chemical processing of the SB11 sludge to
represent DWPF operations.

3.0 Glass Composition Development

The elemental concentrations of the Tank 40H blend projection provided by SRMC System
Planning (on a calcine basis) were converted to oxides as shown in Appendix Table A-1. 13 These
projections represent a heel of 40 inches and 11 FCA metal batches (~226 kg Pu, uncertainty
included). The resulting concentration of PuO2 in glass at 40% waste loading (WL) is 0.03 wt%.
Typical processing at DWPF is at ~36% WL, so 40% WL was used for this evaluation to cover
compositional uncertainty in achieving the target WL. Since the intent was to evaluate the transfer
of all FCA metal and oxide batches to SB11 (~344 kg Pu, uncertainty included), 5 the target Pu
concentration in glass at 40% WL was increased to 0.2 wt% to bound the composition. It was later
projected in an SRMC system impact evaluation that the Pu concentration in glass would be ~0.07
wt%, so the selection of 0.2 wt% Pu for this testing allowed for a sufficient margin for compositional
uncertainty.14

An existing non-radioactive Tank 40H SB11 sludge simulant was identified for this testing, which
was based on a heel of 90 inches.15,16 Note that a higher heel was assumed for the simulant than
the Tank 40H projections (40 inches) shown in Appendix Table A-1. The original measured
composition of the simulant (Appendix Table A-2) was modified for the sludge-only composition
to include the following:
  A simulated FCA dissolver solution neutralized to pH 11 with NaOH (discussed in Section
 4.1)
  Target concentrations in glass at 40% WL for ThO2 (0.55 wt%) and U3O8 (3.21 wt%)

The target Tank 40H sludge simulant composition representing sludge-only processing at DWPF
is shown in Appendix Table A-3.

To evaluate the impact of compositional variation, a sludge simulant representing coupled
operations with the Salt Waste Processing Facility (SWPF) was also developed. The original Tank
40H sludge simulant (Appendix Table A-2) was modified for the coupled operations composition
to include the following:
  Neutralization of a simulated FCA dissolver solution to pH 11 with NaOH (discussed in
 Section 4.1)
  Monosodium titanate (MST) simulated slurry representing 2,800 gallons of Precipitate
 Reactor Feed Tank slurry (assuming a 0.1 g/L MST strike and 28 strikes per cycle within
 the SWPF Alpha Strike Process followed by washing to a sodium concentration of < 0.7
 M)

The target coupled operations sludge simulant composition is shown in Appendix Table A-3. Note
that the ThO2 and U3O8 concentrations are lower than the sludge-only composition to reflect
dilution from coupled operations.

Both the sludge-only and coupled operations sludge simulant compositions were combined with
compositions of available vendor fritsb,17 over the interval of 28-44% WL. Each of the resulting
glass compositions was evaluated against the Product Composition Control System (PCCS)
Measurement Acceptance Region (MAR) criteria to determine whether the composition would
pass the Slurry Mix Evaporator (SME) acceptability process. 18 Frit 473 (8B2O3-8Li2O-5Na2O-
79SiO2, wt%) was selected for this testing as it had large operating windows, while also being

4.0 Experimental Procedures

4.1 Preparation of Sludges for Testing
Sludge samples used in testing were generated by combining non-radioactive SB11 sludge
simulant with U3O8, ThO2, and a Pu-bearing simulant of FCA dissolution material that was
developed for a separate task.15,19 Additional salt species and MST were added as needed to
simulate the chemical compositions encountered during coupled operation with the SWPF. The
composition of this sludge simulant is given in Table 4-1. Trim chemicals generally employed in
sludge simulant testing were omitted from the sludge simulant used to perform this testing (Ag,
Rh, Ru, Pd and Hg). The omission of these elements is not expected to impact the solubility of
Pu in glass while the addition of these masses at a small scale would be challenging and result
in the generation of toxic waste and flammable gas. Elimination of noble metals and mercury
would likely lead to higher gas generation and final formate concentrations resulting from glycolate
destruction,20 but these differences in gas production and formate generation were not expected
to impact the results of this testing.

The masses of the components used in Sludge Receipt and Adjustment Tank (SRAT) receipt
preparation are given in Table 4-2. Additional salt components used for coupled operations
simulation included sodium sulfate, sodium aluminate, sodium hydroxide, sodium carbonate,
sodium nitrite, and sodium nitrate. After the observation of solids settling and flocculation in stock
mixtures of 15 wt% MST, a subsample of MST slurry was pulled for confirmation of solids content
prior to use in experiments. Solids measurement yielded a result of 13.13 wt% MST, resulting in
a need to add ~14% more MST slurry than previously anticipated. Otherwise, the MST slurry was
used as received. Th and U were added as oxides (specifically, ThO 2 and U3O8). Neutralized Pu-
containing slurry was generated by combining Pu-bearing FCA simulant with 50% NaOH solution
prior to blending into the SRAT receipt.

The Pu-containing slurry was generated using a separate Pu-bearing simulant solution designed
to mimic the conditions of the FCA flowsheet dissolution material at a mass intended to increase
the amount of Pu to ~0.2 wt% in the final glass product.19,21 In an effort to simulate the chemical
conditions of Pu expected in tank waste, the simulated FCA dissolver solution was neutralized
with 50% NaOH solution to approximately 0.3 M. A final OH concentration of 0.3 M was chosen
over the traditional concentration of 1.2 M OH to avoid two potentially problematic scenarios: 1.)
unrealistically high sodium concentrations in the final glass product, and 2.) the uncertainty in final
Pu concentration caused by washing the high sodium from the neutralized slurry. The choice of
a 0.3 M caustic adjustment endpoint was not expected to impact the solubility of Pu in the final
glass product. The measured composition of the FCA simulant and calculated compositions of
the 50% NaOH solution and resulting neutralized Pu-containing slurry are given in Table 4-3.

4.2 Chemical Pre-Treatment of Sludge Materials
Chemical pre-treatment of melter feed in the DWPF consists of a SRAT cycle followed by a SME
cycle. In the SRAT cycle, nitric and glycolic acids are added to chemically adjust the sludge to
achieve a desired ratio of oxidizing and reducing components for favorable reactivity in the melter
as well as chemically reduce mercury species such that they may be removed via boiling.
Following the execution of the SRAT cycle, the sludge is then transferred to the SME where glass
frit is added and excess water is removed to reduce the heat load required by the melter.

A simplified SRAT cycle was performed using a glass beaker, Teflon ®-coated stirring rod, and
heating plate within the SRNL Shielded Cells Facility (SCF). An image of the apparatus employed
in melter feed pre-treatment is given in Figure 4-1. A K-type thermocouple was used to monitor
temperature during chemical pre-treatment. The thermocouple was suspended over the slurry
blend using a frame constructed of stainless-steel tubing. A 400-mL beaker was employed to
ensure that sufficient headspace was present to contain sludge that may be released via foaming
or bubbling caused by gas release. Sludge components were added to a glass beaker and placed
on top of the stir plate. A negligible amount of Antifoam Y-17112 was added to the experiment
prior to initiation to ensure that the sludge did not boil over the edge of the glass beaker. Stirring
was then started and maintained such that an obvious vortex was maintained in the beaker. The
beaker was then heated to a temperature of 93 °C ± 2 °C. Nitric acid was added first to the sludge
in small aliquots. The addition of these aliquots caused small decreases in temperature due to
the addition of room-temperature liquids. The slurry temperature was allowed to recover prior to
making further acid additions. Once nitric acid additions were complete, glycolic acid was added
in a similar method (using small aliquots with temperature recovery time of approximately 5-10
minutes between each liquid addition). Following the addition of glycolic acid, heating and mixing

 6

was allowed to continue until obvious gas generation had ceased (approximately 1-2 hours). Once
no visible gas was being actively released, heating was stopped and the beaker was allowed to
cool to ≤ 80 °C. Once cooled, the sludge (now referred to as SRAT product) was transferred to a
high-density polyethylene bottle for storage. De-ionized water was used as necessary to rinse
used components (beaker, stir bar, thermocouple) into the polyethylene bottle to ensure minimal
loss of material. In order to prepare a viable SME product (melter feed material), glass frit was
added to the bottled SRAT product and mixed. The amounts of Frit 473 added to each SRAT

4.3 Glass Fabrication and Analysis
Due to the high level of radioactivity, most of this work was performed remotely in the SRNL SCF
except when sub-samples were removed for further analysis.
4.3.1 Fabrication
Approximately 150 g of each of the sludge-only and coupled operations SME product were divided
into four 100 mL quartz or alumina crucibles. Deionized water was used to rinse the SME product
bottles. Each set of drying crucibles was placed into an oven and dried overnight at ~110 °C. The
crucibles were allowed to cool for approximately 10 minutes before being handled by the
manipulators. Dried SME product was scraped directly into a 100 mL platinum/gold alloy crucible.
The crucible was placed in the furnace at room temperature and heated to ~1150 °C. Once the
furnace reached 1150 °C, the sample remained in the furnace at the melting temperature for 3
hours. After 3 hours, the crucible was removed from the furnace, and the bottom of the crucible
was quenched in a shallow pool of water in a stainless-steel pan. The surfaces of the glasses
were then visually examined to confirm that they were homogeneous (dark and shiny
appearance). Each SME product sample produced approximately 32.5 g of glass.
4.3.2 Chemical Composition Analysis
Several grams of each glass were ground in an agate mechanical pulverizing mixer mill. The
glass was sieved and the portion that passed through a 200-mesh (<75 μm) brass sieve was used
for the dissolutions. Four replicate samples (nominally 0.25 g each) of the sieved glass were
dissolved for analysis by AF and AR dissolutions.24,25 Concurrent with each set of dissolutions,
two samples of the Analytical Reference Glass-1 (ARG-1) were also dissolved to confirm the
performance of the dissolution methods.26 Aliquots from both dissolution methods were analyzed
by ICP-ES.27 A multi-element standard solution containing known concentrations of Al, B, Fe, K,
Li, Na, and Si was also analyzed with each set of samples to confirm the accuracy of the ICP-ES
measurements.28 Blanks processed through the dissolutions for ICP-ES were also submitted for
analysis to check for any gross contamination.

Pu concentrations in AF dissolutions were determined by isotope dilution mass spectrometry
(IDMS) utilizing the inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS). The IDMS
methodology utilizes a 244Pu spike, using NBL Program Office Certified Reference Material 126A
for quality control. Samples were prepared using column extraction (TEVA®) and then prepared
for analysis on the ICP-MS.29,30

To confirm the U concentrations in the first set of samples, the AF dissolutions were submitted for
secondary analysis. The Pu matrix was removed utilizing column extraction (UTEVA ®).31 Column
prepared samples were diluted in 2% HNO3 and analyzed ICP-ES.27
4.3.3 Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)
Approximately 4-5 small glass shards were selected from each glass sample to assess
homogeneity and elemental species with an SEM and electron dispersive X-ray spectrometer
(EDS).32 Each set of glass shards were adhered to an aluminum stub using double-sided carbon
tape and were subsequently carbon coated to provide the conductivity required for SEM imaging.
Samples were imaged using the backscattered electron detector (BSD) to identify any
inhomogeneities across the visible portions of the sample. Elemental differences are readily
identifiable using the BSD as differences in image contrast correspond to relative elemental
composition/mass across a sample. The EDS detector was utilized to collect incidental x-rays
generated from the electron beam interacting with the sample to identify elements within areas of
interest.

 8

4.3.4 X-ray Diffraction (XRD)
Approximately 0.1 g of the minus 200-mesh powder from each glass was submitted for XRD
analysis. The samples were further ground in an agate mortar and pestle to reduce the particle
size and to homogenize the samples. The fine powder was then placed on a low background slide
and a few drops of ethanol used to disperse the sample. 33 Data were collected by step scanning
over the 2θ range of 5-70° with a step size of 0.02° and a dwell time of 12 s per step. Search-
match identification was performed with Jade software (Version 8) from Materials Data Inc using
PDF5+ 2024 from the International Centre for Diffraction Data.

5.0 Results and Discussion – Chemical Pre-Treatment

The pre-treatment chemical adjustments for sludge-only and coupled operations were performed
without issue. Measured elemental concentrations and the percent relative standard deviations
(%RSD) are given in Table 5-1. During a review of these results, it was apparent that the
concentrations of U and Th were low compared to the expected concentrations derived from
calculation. The measured mass ratios of critical elements are given in Table 5-2. Values
expected based on calculation are also given in parentheses. The Fe:U and Fe:Th ratios are lower
than expected while Fe:Al and Fe:Ti ratios remain close to expected values, which suggests that
subsampling bias may have had a significant impact. U3O8 and ThO2 powders are much denser
than the slurry, which was already thinner than typical sludge due to water additions, and it was
hypothesized that these particles could have settled while subsamples of slurry were being
collected for the AR dissolutions.

Based on the low U and Th concentrations, a decision was made to omit the preparation of the
AF dissolutions and subsequent ICP-ES and ICP-MS analysis. A second decision was made to
repeat these sludge-only and coupled operations pre-treatment experiments while proceeding in
parallel with the melting of the original SME products. Methods were discussed to reduce losses
of U and Th in the second set of experiments to better represent the target concentrations. Due
to timing of experiments and analytical work, glass composition data was available prior to the
start of the repeated pre-treatment experiments. Based on the low measured U concentrations in
both glasses (to be discussed in Section 6.2.3), a third decision was made to add additional U 3O8
to the SME product during a second set of experiments to account for any U that could be lost
throughout the process prior to melting.

6.5 XRD
XRD spectra for the sludge-only and coupled operations glasses are shown in Figure 6-8 and
Figure 6-9. The characteristic amorphous hump is observed for both samples. The peaks in the
sludge-only glass represent crystals having a spinel structure. A chromium iron oxide phase
(Cr2FeO4) was chosen as representative for this spinel structure based on the SEM-EDS results.
This result is not surprising based on the PCCS results for this glass, which failed the liquidus
temperature and Cr2O3 constraints as shown in Table 6-7. No crystalline phases were detected
in the coupled operations glass.

Small crystalline peaks were observed in the second coupled operations glass (Figure 6-10),
which correspond to the spinel structure. Based on the EDS results, a manganese iron nickel

 24

oxide phase (Mn0.15Fe2Ni0.85O4) was selected, but is not an exact match since there are likely
structural substitutions with Cr and Ti that were identified by EDS. These XRD results are only
qualitative, but peak intensities are much lower than Figure 6-8, which indicates the overall wt%
of spinel is less than the sludge-only glass. The presence of spinel crystals was not expected
based on the PCCS results. Transition metals commonly found in spinel (Fe, Mn and Ni) were all
lower than the target concentrations, which would reduce the liquidus temperature. It was
speculated that the spinel crystals formed due to melting conditions. There were several instances
of the furnace malfunctioning prior to the final glass melting. It is possible that the temperature in
the furnace drifted below the liquidus temperature causing spinel crystals to form. The furnace
could not be powered on after this testing completed, so temperature verifications could not be
completed.

7.0 Conclusions

For the sludge-only and coupled operations glasses produced in this study, the measured U and
Th concentrations were lower than targeted. All other analytes were consistent with target
concentrations. A second coupled operations glass was fabricated with additional U 3O8 to correct
for the low U concentration in the first glass. Several sludge components were lower than the
targeted concentrations due to the loss of material during the experimental melter feed fabrication
(Fe, Mn, Ni, Ti), which reduced the WL to 38% from the target of 40%. Pu solubility is independent
of these compositional changes in the glass matrix, and the low U and Th concentrations and
reduced WL do not impact the overall conclusions of this work. At a measured Pu concentration
in glass of ~0.2 wt%, undissolved Pu phases were not identified in any of the glasses evaluated
in this study. The Pu concentrations in each of the fabricated glasses is more than double the
amount that is projected from FCA discards (~0.07 wt% Pu). From a Pu perspective in glass,
there are no issues with adding all FCA metal and oxide batches to SB11.
