Global Convergence and Error Propagation in Neural Gradient Flows: A Riemannian Optimization Framework.
Shixin Zheng siuzheng@umd.edu, Yiwei Wang yiweiw@ucr.edu, Haizhao Yang hzyang@umd.edu

Abstract
We develop a geometric convergence theory for neural-network optimization within the minimizing movement scheme (MMS) framework. Reformulating each neural MMS step as a minimization over the set of increments in a Hilbert space, we show that under a C 2 C^{2} network with locally non-degenerate Jacobian this increment set is a boundaryless smooth embedded submanifold, on which a natural preconditioned (Gauss–Newton-type) gradient flow in parameter space induces exactly the Riemannian gradient flow. Under a strict interior-localization condition and an explicit data condition, the reached sublevel set is geodesically convex and the subproblem objective is geodesically strongly convex on it; both the continuous Riemannian gradient flow and its discrete companion via the exponential map converge linearly to the unique subproblem minimizer. Propagating finite-time inner-solver inexactness and neural-approximation error through the MMS iterations yields a uniform function-space tracking bound and an explicit trajectory budget, so the inexact neural iterates converge to an O ⁡ ( δ ) O(\delta) -neighborhood of the global minimum. Numerical experiments on nonlinear regression and a small-scale latent-diffusion testbed indicate that the Gauss–Newton-type inner solver achieves smaller trajectory errors with substantially fewer inner iterations than first-order baselines.

Introduction

Many scientific computing and machine-learning problems can be formulated as minimizing an energy functional

\min_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{F}[u],

(1)

where
\mathcal{H}
is a real Hilbert space (e.g.,
L^{2}(\Omega)
) and
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously Fréchet differentiable, coercive, and nonnegative (the last condition is without loss of generality, since adding a constant does not change the minimizer or the gradient flow). When
\mathcal{F}
is convex, the associated continuous-time gradient flow

\partial_{t}u_{t}=-\nabla\mathcal{F}[u_{t}]

(2)

provides a canonical dynamics that converges to a global minimizer under certain conditions.
A classical time discretization of the gradient flow (
2
) is the implicit Euler method,
which admits the variational characterization known as the
minimizing movement scheme
(MMS)
(
5
)
.
This paper studies a neural-network based spatial discretization of MMS, which is numerically investigated in
22
, and provides a geometric convergence theory by viewing each neural MMS step as a Riemannian optimization problem on a Hilbert-embedded manifold of
increments
.

1.1
Minimizing Movement Scheme (MMS)

Given stepsize
\tau>0
and an iterate
u^{n}\in\mathcal{H}
, the MMS solves the optimization problem

u^{n+1}=J_{\tau}(u^{n}),\qquad J_{\tau}(x):=\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{F}}(x):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\frac{1}{2\tau}\|u-x\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\mathcal{F}[u]\right\}.

(3)

at each time step. Here,
u^{n}
is an approximation to the gradient flow solution
u
of (
2
) at
t=n\tau
.
Throughout, we assume
\mathcal{F}
is
m_{\mathcal{F}}
-strongly convex with
m_{\mathcal{F}}>0
, so the objective in (
3
) is strongly convex and the resolvent operator
J_{\tau}
is well-defined and single-valued for any
\tau>0
. As the time step
\tau\downarrow 0
, the continuous interpolants of the discrete MMS sequence converge uniformly to the unique continuous trajectory of the associated gradient flow, which itself converges exponentially to the unique global minimizer
u^{*}
of (
1
) as time tends to infinity.
Therefore, since the exact discrete iterate
u^{N}
approximates the gradient flow state at time
T=N\tau
, it can be regarded as an approximation of
u^{*}
for sufficiently small
\tau
and sufficiently large
N
.
For completeness, we detail the rigorous convergence of this scheme in Appendix
B
.

1.2
Neural-network Parametrization of MMS (Neural MMS)

To solve the MMS subproblem (
3
) in practice,
one must restrict from the infinite-dimensional space
\mathcal{H}
to a finite-dimensional model class.
Classical spatial discretization, such as finite elements, spectral methods, or finite differences, introduce
a mesh that scales exponentially with the spatial dimension
d
, making them impractical for
high-dimensional problems such as those arising in molecular dynamics, optimal control, or
mean-field games. A natural alternative is to use neural networks as a mesh-free parametrization for the spatial variable
(
41
;
22
)
.
Neural networks offer a compelling alternative for several reasons.
First, they are
mesh-free
: classical discretizations such as finite elements tie the
function representation to a fixed set of grid points or quadrature nodes, so the two cannot
be varied independently. By contrast, the parametrization
w\mapsto u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\cdot\,;w)

defines a function over the entire domain that can be evaluated at
any
point,
decoupling the function representation from the choice of quadrature or collocation points. This flexibility is
particularly attractive in high dimensions, where tensor-product grid-based
discretizations suffer from an exponential growth in degrees of freedom.
Second, deep networks possess a powerful
compositional approximation
structure:
they can represent functions with hierarchical features using a number of parameters
that scales polynomially (rather than exponentially) with dimension for suitable function
classes
(
47
;
33
)
.
Third, the parametrization is
adaptive
: unlike fixed basis functions, the network
automatically adjusts its internal representation to the solution structure during training.
Finally, and crucially for this paper, the MMS proximal structure provides a natural

regularization
of the nonconvex neural optimization landscape, as the quadratic penalty

\|u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)-u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}/(2\tau)
forces the next iterate to stay close to the
previous one, which is precisely the locality that enables our Riemannian convergence theory.

Specifically, we restrict the optimization domain from
\mathcal{H}
to the model class
\mathrm{NN}\subset\mathcal{H}
, the image of a smooth parametrization
u_{\mathrm{NN}}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathcal{H}
. Unlike Galerkin methods, the class
\mathrm{NN}
is not a vector subspace of
\mathcal{H}
(the sum of two same-architecture networks need not be representable in that architecture), but it is the image of a finite-dimensional parameter space under the parametrization map.
This distinction is important for the analysis. In a classical Galerkin
discretization, the trial space is a finite-dimensional linear subspace of

\mathcal{H}
and therefore inherits the Hilbert-space geometry. Hence, if
\mathcal{F}
is
strongly convex on
\mathcal{H}
, its restriction to the Galerkin space remains
strongly convex, and the discrete MMS subproblem is still a convex optimization
problem. Moreover, the trial-space and quadrature errors can be analyzed by
standard approximation and perturbation arguments. By contrast, the neural model
class is a nonlinear parametrized set rather than a vector space, so the
Hilbert-space convexity of
\mathcal{F}
does not directly translate into convexity
with respect to the parameters. This loss of linear structure is the main reason
we reformulate the neural MMS step geometrically in function space.

In the theoretical part of this work, the neural network is used purely as a
parametrization
of functions in a Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}
(e.g.
L^{2}(\Omega)
or
H^{1}(\Omega)
). The spatial variable
x\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a

continuous
argument, and the analysis is carried out at the function-space level.
Concretely, for each parameter
w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the network defines a function

x\mapsto u_{\mathrm{NN}}(x;w)
and we identify
u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)
with an element of
\mathcal{H}
.
When sampling or quadrature is used in computation, it serves only to approximate inner products and integrals in
\mathcal{H}
and does not change the analytical (infinite-dimensional) formulation.

Let
u_{\mathrm{NN}}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathrm{NN}\subset\mathcal{H}
denote the network realization from parameter space where
p
is the number of parameters, and write
u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}:=u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n})
. The neural MMS step solves

w^{n+1}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{\frac{1}{2\tau}\|u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)-u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n})\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\mathcal{F}[u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)]\right\},

(4)

While MMS enjoys a clean convex-analytic theory in
\mathcal{H}
, the neural parameterization typically makes (
4
) nonconvex in
w
, thus the global convergence of gradient-based optimization methods for (
4
) is difficult to achieve.

It is natural to reformulate (
4
) in function space by defining the fixed-architecture neural model class
\mathcal{N}:=\{u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w):w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\}
(note that
\mathcal{N}
is not a subspace of
\mathcal{H}
, since the sum of two neural networks with the same architecture need not be representable in that architecture). In the function space, we consider

u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN}}=J_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}),\qquad J_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}(x):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{u_{\mathrm{NN}}\in\mathcal{N}}\left\{\frac{1}{2\tau}\|u_{\mathrm{NN}}-x\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\mathcal{F}[u_{\mathrm{NN}}]\right\}.

(5)

Well-posedness of
J_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}
– existence and uniqueness of the minimizer over
\mathcal{N}
– follows from Assumption
1
together with Theorem
15
via the identification (
34
). Our goal is to recover a global convergence theory by changing viewpoints: we do not optimize over the entire parameter space at each step; instead, we optimize over a local set of
increments
in
\mathcal{H}
that forms a Riemannian submanifold.

1.3
Equivalent Formulation via Increment Optimization

For
v\in\mathcal{H}
, define the auxiliary increment functional
g(\cdot;v):\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
by

g(h;v):=\frac{\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}{2\tau}+\mathcal{F}\bigl[\,h+v\,\bigr].

(6)

Then the MMS update (
3
) is equivalent to

h^{n}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}g(h;u^{n}),\qquad u^{n+1}=u^{n}+h^{n},

so that
h^{n}=u^{n+1}-u^{n}
is the optimal increment. Likewise, the neural MMS update (
5
) can be (formally) written as an increment minimization:

h^{n}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{h\in\{u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)-u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}:\,w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\}}g(h;u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}),\qquad u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN}}=u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}+h^{n}.

(7)

This reformulation separates the
functional
geometry in
\mathcal{H}
from the
parameter
nonconvexity, and it is the starting point of our geometric analysis.

1.4
Increment Manifold and Riemannian-Optimization Viewpoint

Because the proximal term in (
6
) penalizes large increments (when
\tau
is small), the optimizer of (
7
) is expected to be “local” around the previous parameters
w^{n}
under certain locality assumption. We formalize this through the following assumption.

Assumption 1

(Locality of the neural increment)
.

At each step
n\geq 0
, there exists a radius
r_{w^{n}}>0
(possibly depending on
w^{n}
) such that the increment problem (
7
) admits at least one global minimizer that lies in the open neighborhood of
w^{n}
:

\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{h\in\{u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)-u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}:\,w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\}}g(h;u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}})\ \cap\ \Big\{u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)-u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}:\,\|w-w^{n}\|_{2}<r_{w^{n}}\Big\}\ \neq\ \emptyset.

Assumption
1
formalizes a
locality of global minimizers
:
although the increment problem is posed over the full parameter space,
at least one global minimizer is required to lie near
u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n})
.
This is enough to ensure that the local Riemannian gradient flow on
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n}))

attains the same global minimum value as the unconstrained problem (see Section
3
).

Although Assumption
1
has the appearance of a circular hypothesis (“the global minimum is local”), it is a verifiable consequence of two standard ingredients: an output-isolation margin
\rho^{\rm in}_{w^{n}}>0
around
u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n})
outside the inner ball
\{w:\|w-w^{n}\|_{2}\leq r_{w^{n}}/2\}
, and a sufficiently small step
\tau
satisfying
2\tau(\mathcal{F}[u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n})]-\mathcal{F}_{\inf})<(\rho^{\rm in}_{w^{n}})^{2}
. Under this condition, the strict interior-localization estimate (Lemma
10
) places the entire decreasing sublevel set of the increment objective inside the inner ball, which implies Assumption
1
and ensures the Riemannian gradient flow analyzed in Section
3
never approaches
\partial\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n}))
. Output isolation
\rho^{\rm in}_{w^{n}}>0
is a local form of injectivity of
u_{\mathrm{NN}}
, plausible away from the symmetry orbits of the network architecture; we elaborate in Appendix
C.1
, where Theorem
41
establishes a less conservative variant via the original outer-ball output-isolation radius.

Intuitively, the proximal parameter
\tau
enforces locality
in function space. Indeed, by the property of MMS, we have

\|u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN}}-u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq 2\tau(\mathcal{F}[u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}]-\mathcal{F}[u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN}}])\leq 2\tau(\mathcal{F}[u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}]-\mathcal{F}_{\inf}).

Thus the neural increment is small in the
\mathcal{H}
-norm when
\tau
is small;
moreover, this localization becomes stronger in the late-stage regime where
the energy gap
\mathcal{F}[u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}]-\mathcal{F}_{\inf}
is small.
The additional non-degeneracy and output-isolation conditions serve precisely
to convert this function-space localization into a parameter-space localization
near
w^{n}
.

Under Assumption
1
, define the
increment manifold

\mathcal{M}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}):=\Big\{u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)-u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}:\,\|w-w^{n}\|_{2}<r_{w^{n}}\Big\}\subset\mathcal{H},

(8)

together with its compact
localization image

\mathcal{K}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}):=\Big\{u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)-u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}:\,\|w-w^{n}\|_{2}\leq r_{w^{n}}/2\Big\}\subset\mathcal{M}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}).

(9)

Then, the neural MMS step becomes

h^{n}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{h\in\mathcal{M}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}})}g(h;u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}),\qquad u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN}}=u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}+h^{n}.

(10)

In Section
2.1
, under a local non-degeneracy condition on the Jacobian of
u_{\mathrm{NN}}
, we show that
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
is a smooth embedded Riemannian submanifold of
\mathcal{H}
and identify its tangent spaces and orthogonal projectors; this turns (
10
) into a Riemannian optimization problem in function space. The image
\mathcal{K}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}})
is used in §
3
only as a compact localization device for the Riemannian gradient flow trajectory.

1.5
Main Results

Our main results are as follows.

•

Geometry of the increment set (Proposition
5
).

Under a
C^{2}
network with
L
-Lipschitz Jacobian and a locally non-degenerate Jacobian, the increment manifold
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
— the image of an
open
parameter ball
B(\tilde{w},r_{\tilde{w}})
under the parametrization — is a
p
-dimensional boundaryless smooth embedded submanifold of
\mathcal{H}
. The image
\mathcal{K}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
of the corresponding
closed
inner ball
\{\|w-\tilde{w}\|_{2}\leq r_{\tilde{w}}/2\}
is a compact subset of
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
and serves as the working set of the analysis.

•

Riemannian-gradient-flow interpretation (Section
2
).

A natural preconditioned (Gauss–Newton-type) gradient flow in parameter space induces exactly the Riemannian gradient flow of
g(\cdot;v)
on
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
in
\mathcal{H}
, connecting Gauss–Newton preconditioning to intrinsic function-space dynamics.

•

Continuous and discrete subproblem convergence (Theorems
15
,
16
).

Under a strict interior-localization condition (Lemma
10
: small step
\tau
relative to a local output-isolation margin), the decreasing-energy sublevel set
S
reached by the Riemannian gradient flow is contained in
\mathcal{K}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
and lies inside a strongly geodesically convex normal neighborhood
\mathcal{G}
of the origin (Lemma
11
). On this invariant sublevel set we establish geodesic strong convexity and smoothness of
g(\cdot;v)
under an explicit
data condition
on
(v,\tau,\mathcal{F},L,\lambda_{\tilde{w}})
(Theorem
14
), giving exponential convergence of the Riemannian gradient flow and a matching linear-rate guarantee for the discrete Riemannian gradient descent that takes its steps along the geodesics (i.e., via the exponential map).

•

Global error propagation for inexact neural MMS (Theorems
27
,
28
).

We quantify how (i) finite-time inexactness of the inner solver — either the continuous Riemannian gradient flow run for time
T
or the discrete Riemannian gradient descent run for
K
steps — and (ii) neural approximation error
\varepsilon
in
J_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}
propagate through MMS iterations. Under a step-size condition, the inexact iterates track the exact MMS iterates uniformly and converge to an
O(\delta)
-neighborhood of the true minimizer when
m_{\mathcal{F}}>0
.

•

Parameter-space convergence (Theorem
33
).

The function-space tracking bound transfers to a uniform tracking bound on the parameter iterates themselves, together with an explicit trajectory-length budget that confines the entire sequence
\{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}\}
to a bounded region of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

\mathcal{H}
down to the equivalent algorithmic views (Riemannian gradient flow on
\mathcal{M}
and preconditioned gradient descent in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
), highlighting where each layer of discretization enters.
Figure

\{u^{n}\}
in
\mathcal{H}
converge to the minimizer
u^{*}
, while the neural MMS iterates
\{u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}}\}
, constrained to the model class
\mathcal{N}
, track the exact iterates within a uniform distance
\delta
at every step.

Scope of this paper.

We distinguish four sources of error in a practical neural MMS computation.
Let
u(t_{n})
denote the exact gradient-flow solution at time
t_{n}=n\tau
,

u^{n}:=J_{\tau}^{n}(u^{0})
the exact MMS iterate in
\mathcal{H}
,
and
u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}\in\mathcal{N}
the neural MMS iterate produced by running the
inner Riemannian solver for time
T
at each step.
Formally,

u(t_{n})-u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}=\underbrace{\bigl[u(t_{n})-u^{n}\bigr]}_{\text{(I) time discr.}}+\underbrace{\bigl[u^{n}-\tilde{u}^{n}\bigr]}_{\text{(II) trial space}}+\underbrace{\bigl[\tilde{u}^{n}-\hat{u}^{n}\bigr]}_{\text{(III) sampling}}+\underbrace{\bigl[\hat{u}^{n}-u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}\bigr]}_{\text{(IV) inner opt.}}.

(11)

Here,
\tilde{u}^{n}
and
\hat{u}^{n}
are conceptual intermediate iterates
corresponding respectively to the population and empirical neural MMS problems, (I) is the classical time-discretization error of MMS;
(II) arises from restricting to the neural model class
\mathcal{N}
;
(III) is the quadrature or sampling error from replacing population quantities
by
N
-sample empirical approximations;
and (IV) is the inner-optimization residual from stopping the Riemannian
solver at finite time
T
.
This paper focuses on term
(IV)
.
Terms (II) and (III) are not analyzed separately; motivated by universal
approximation theory and Monte Carlo estimates, their combined effect is
absorbed into the one-step precision
\varepsilon
of
Assumption
21
, leaving a detailed
architecture- and sample-size-dependent analysis for future work.
Under Assumption
21
, Theorem
27

shows that the inner-optimization error (IV) decays as
O(e^{-\nu T})
and
propagates stably through the MMS iterations.

1.6
Related Work

A central question in neural network optimization is whether gradient-based methods converge to global minimizers, and if so, at what rate and under what assumptions on the architecture.
We organize the related literature along three axes that clarify how our result fits into the broader landscape: (i) NTK and over-parameterized convergence, (ii) mean-field and feature-learning approaches, and (iii) second-order and Riemannian geometric methods.
We then discuss related variational and gradient-flow frameworks.
Table
1
provides a concise comparison.

(i) NTK regime and over-parameterization.

The neural tangent kernel (NTK) framework of
23
shows that in the infinite-width limit the kernel stays constant during gradient descent, reducing training to a linear ODE and yielding global convergence at a linear rate governed by
\lambda_{\min}(\text{NTK})
.
Subsequent works extended this to finite but large width:

16
proved global convergence for deep over-parameterized ResNets with width
\mathrm{poly}(n)
;

3
established linear convergence of SGD for FC, CNN, and ResNet architectures with width
\mathrm{poly}(n,L)
;

48
obtained linear-rate convergence for deep ReLU networks;
and
13
sharpened the width requirement to
\mathrm{polylog}(n,1/\varepsilon)
under a data-margin assumption.
For shallow networks,
39
achieved moderate over-parameterization (
\sqrt{p}>n
).
A key limitation was identified by
18
: the NTK variance scales as
\exp(d/n_{\text{width}})
, so the frozen-kernel approximation breaks down when depth is non-negligible relative to width.
All of these results require width at least polynomial in
n
, and their convergence rates depend on
\lambda_{\min}(\text{NTK})
, which degrades with depth.
Our framework is complementary: it applies to
fixed, finite-width
architectures and obtains convergence through the Riemannian geometry of the increment manifold, with a rate independent of NTK conditioning.

(ii) Mean-field and feature learning.

The mean-field perspective
(
35
;
34
)
treats neurons as particles whose distribution evolves under a Wasserstein gradient flow, yielding global convergence for two-layer networks that—unlike the NTK regime—permits feature learning.
Extensions to three-layer
(
42
;
37
)
and deep ResNet
(
32
)
architectures exist, and

12
obtained linear-rate convergence with feature-learning guarantees.
However, all these results require width
\to\infty
(mean-field limit) and typically characterize limiting dynamics rather than providing non-asymptotic finite-width rates.
Our result operates at fixed finite width with explicit exponential convergence guarantees.

(iii) Second-order and Riemannian geometric methods.

The closest predecessor to our work is
10
, who showed that the Gauss–Newton method induces a Riemannian gradient flow on a low-dimensional submanifold of the function space, achieving geometric convergence at a rate
independent of NTK conditioning
in both underparameterized and overparameterized regimes.
Our work extends this perspective from finite-dimensional Euclidean settings to a continuous Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and crucially operates on a
boundaryless increment manifold
(the image of an open parameter ball) within the MMS framework, with a strict interior-localization device keeping the analysis confined to a compact subset of the open chart.
In addition, we provide a
global multi-step error propagation
analysis (Theorem
27
), showing that the inexact neural MMS iterates converge to an
O(\delta)
-neighborhood of the true minimizer—a feature absent from single-subproblem analyses.

9
achieved quadratic convergence for a Gram–Gauss–Newton method but requires sufficient over-parameterization and square loss.
The natural gradient method
(
4
;
46
)
preconditions using the Fisher information metric on the
parameter space
;
in contrast, our Gauss–Newton preconditioner arises from the
function-space geometry
of the increment manifold embedded in
\mathcal{H}
.

Variational and gradient-flow frameworks.

Neural discretizations of gradient flows via energetic variational principles were introduced by
22
.
The MMS has deep connections to the JKO scheme
(
25
)
in optimal transport.
The broader Riemannian optimization literature
(
1
;
7
)
provides algorithms and convergence theory on classical matrix manifolds (Stiefel, Grassmann); our increment manifold
\mathcal{M}
differs in that it is defined implicitly through a neural parameterization as the image of an open parameter ball, so it is a
boundaryless
embedded submanifold of
\mathcal{H}
whose geometry depends on the architecture and the current parameters.
The theory of gradient flows in metric spaces
(
5
)
and the KLS framework
(
19
;
6
)
give convergence guarantees under tame geometry.
Our analysis uses a different mechanism: we establish geodesic strong convexity on the increment manifold, providing stronger (exponential) convergence rates.

2
From Preconditioned Gradient Flow to Riemannian Gradient Flow

The goal of this section is twofold: (i) to show that the increment set
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))

carries the structure of an embedded Riemannian submanifold of
\mathcal{H}
, and (ii) to demonstrate that
a natural Gauss–Newton-type preconditioned gradient flow in parameter space induces
exactly the Riemannian gradient flow on this manifold.
The key insight is that the Gauss–Newton preconditioner
(J^{*}J)^{-1}J^{*}
in parameter space
is precisely the orthogonal projector onto the tangent space of
\mathcal{M}
in
\mathcal{H}
; thus,
the preconditioned flow is geometrically natural—it performs steepest descent on the manifold
with respect to the ambient Hilbert metric.

u_{\mathrm{NN}}
sends the ball

in parameter space to the increment manifold
\mathcal{M}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN}})\subset\mathcal{H}
, and the preconditioned gradient descent (PGD) in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
induces exactly the Riemannian gradient flow (RGF) on
\mathcal{M}
.

2.1
The Increment Set as an Embedded Riemannian Submanifold

Definition 2

(
C^{2}
with
L
-Lipschitz Jacobian)
.

We say the neural network
u_{\mathrm{NN}}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathcal{H}
has an
L
-Lipschitz Jacobian
if
u_{\mathrm{NN}}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{p};\mathcal{H})
and its Fréchet derivative
\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{p},\mathcal{H})
satisfies

\left\lVert\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{1})-\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{2})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq L\left\lVert w_{1}-w_{2}\right\rVert_{2}\qquad\text{for all }w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},

(12)

with constant
L>0
depending on the activation and architecture.

Definition 3

(Non-degeneracy Jacobian)
.

We call the neural network
u_{\mathrm{NN}}
to have a
\lambda
-non-degenerate Jacobian at
w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
if
there exists
\lambda>0
such that

\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)^{*}\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)\succeq 4\lambda^{2}{I}_{p}

where
\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)
denotes the Fréchet derivative

(
)

and
\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)^{*}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes the adjoint,
i.e.,

\langle\mathrm{D}{u}_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)z,\mathrm{D}{u}_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)z\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq 4\lambda^{2}\|z\|_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}^{2},\qquad\forall z\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

Here
\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)^{*}\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is the Gram matrix of the partial derivatives
\partial_{w_{j}}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\cdot;w)
with respect to the
\mathcal{H}
-inner product, so the non-degeneracy condition amounts to uniform positive definiteness of this Gram matrix.

Lemma 4

(Local preservation of positive definiteness)
.

Assume
u_{\mathrm{NN}}
is a neural network that has an
L
-Lipschitz Jacobian and a
\lambda_{\tilde{w}}
-non-degenerate Jacobian at
\tilde{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

Define

B(\tilde{w},r_{\tilde{w}}):=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\left\lVert w-\tilde{w}\right\rVert_{2}<r_{\tilde{w}}\}

(13)

where

r_{\tilde{w}}:=\min\left\{\frac{\lambda_{\tilde{w}}}{L},\ \frac{\lambda_{\tilde{w}}^{2}}{2L\bigl(\sqrt{\left\lVert\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}+\lambda_{\tilde{w}}^{2}}+\left\lVert\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\bigr)},\ \frac{\lambda_{\tilde{w}}^{2}(1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}})}{4L\,\mathrm{Lip}_{u_{\mathrm{NN}}}(1+\tau L_{\mathcal{F}})}\right\}.

(14)

All three entries depend only on the local Jacobian data
(\lambda_{\tilde{w}},\,\left\lVert\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}},\,L,\,\mathrm{Lip}_{u_{\mathrm{NN}}})
and the
\mathcal{F}
-condition ratio
\kappa:=(1+\tau L_{\mathcal{F}})/(1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}})

\nu=(1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}})/\tau

Then for all

\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)^{*}\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)\succeq\lambda_{\tilde{w}}^{2}I_{p}.

(15)

Therefore,

is an immersion on

Proof

For any unit vector
z\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}

\|\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)[z]\|_{\mathcal{H}}\geq\|\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w})[z]\|_{\mathcal{H}}-\|(\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)-\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))[z]\|_{\mathcal{H}}\geq\|\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w})[z]\|_{\mathcal{H}}-L\|w-\tilde{w}\|_{2}.

Taking the minimum over

gives

\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}\!\big(\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)^{*}\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)\big)}\geq\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}\!\big(\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w})^{*}\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w})\big)}-L\|w-\tilde{w}\|_{2}.

Since

(17)

2.2
Preconditioned Gradient Flow as Riemannian Gradient Flow.

Recall (
10
):

\begin{cases}h^{n}=\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{h\in\{u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)-u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n}):w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\}}g(h;u_{\mathrm{NN}}^{n})\\[10.00002pt]
u_{\mathrm{NN}}^{n+1}=h^{n}+u_{\mathrm{NN}}^{n}\end{cases},

In parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, this corresponds

\begin{cases}w^{n+1}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}g(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)-u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n});u_{\mathrm{NN}}^{n}),\\[10.00002pt]
u_{\mathrm{NN}}^{n+1}=u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n+1}).\end{cases}

(19)

Now consider a preconditioned gradient flow for solving (
19
):

\frac{\mathrm{d}w_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\left(\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{t})^{*}\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{t})\right)^{-1}\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{t})^{*}\nabla g(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{t})-u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n});u_{\mathrm{NN}}^{n}),\qquad w_{0}=w^{n},

(20)

where
\left(\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{t})^{*}\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{t})\right)^{-1}
is the preconditioner, and the gradient term
\nabla g(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{t})-u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n});u_{\mathrm{NN}}^{n})
is the gradient
\nabla g(h;u_{\mathrm{NN}}^{n})
evaluated at
h=u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{t})-u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n})
.

Define the induced trajectory in
\mathcal{H}
as

h_{t}:=u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{t})-u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n})\in\mathcal{H}.

By the chain rule and (
20
),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}h_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{t})\frac{\mathrm{d}w_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{t})\left(\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{t})^{*}\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{t})\right)^{-1}\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{t})^{*}\nabla g(h_{t};u_{\mathrm{NN}}^{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbf{P}_{h_{t}}\left(\nabla g(h_{t};u_{\mathrm{NN}}^{n})\right),

which is a Riemannian gradient flow on
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n}))
.

Remark 8
.

The theoretical framework assumes the objective functional
g
depends primarily on the function values
h
, rather than their spatial derivatives
\nabla h
. Consequently, variational problems governed by high-order differential operators, such as solving the Poisson equation via minimization of the Dirichlet energy (
H^{1}
-norm), do not naturally fit within the proposed analysis. If one takes
\mathcal{H}=H^{1}
and uses the Sobolev norm, the assumptions on the neural network (e.g., Jacobian Lipschitz continuity) may no longer hold, as the network is much more sensitive in the
H^{1}
norm. In a discrete setting, the Lipschitz constant of the gradient would scale with the inverse square of the grid spacing (i.e.,
L\propto\mathcal{O}(dx^{-2})
).

3
Convergence of the Increment Subproblem

Having established that the increment set
\mathcal{M}
is a Riemannian submanifold and the
preconditioned gradient flow is the Riemannian gradient flow, we now turn to the convergence
of this flow. The main challenge is that
\mathcal{M}
is curved (it is not a linear subspace of
\mathcal{H}
),
so standard Euclidean convexity arguments do not directly apply.
This section develops three key properties: (i) strict interior localization of the reached sublevel set inside the compact image
\mathcal{K}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
under a small-step output-isolation condition (Lemma
10
); (ii) existence of a strongly geodesically convex normal neighborhood
\mathcal{G}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
of
0
containing
\mathcal{K}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
(Lemma
11
); and (iii) geodesic strong convexity and smoothness of the subproblem
objective
g(\cdot;v)
on the reached sublevel set inside
\mathcal{G}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
(Theorem
14
).
Together, these yield exponential convergence of the Riemannian gradient flow (Theorem
15
).

Lemma 10

3.2
Geodesic Strong Convexity of the Subproblem Objective

Lemma 12

(Strong convexity and Smoothness of
g(\cdot;v)
on
\mathcal{H}
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})
be a real Hilbert space with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
.
Assume
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is Fréchet differentiable with
L_{\mathcal{F}}
-Lipschitz gradient, i.e.

\|\nabla\mathcal{F}[x]-\nabla\mathcal{F}[y]\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq L_{\mathcal{F}}\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{H},

and
m_{\mathcal{F}}
-strongly convex, i.e.

\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}[x]-\nabla\mathcal{F}[y],x-y\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq m_{\mathcal{F}}\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{H}.

Fix
\tau>0
and
v\in\mathcal{H}
. Recall the definition of
g
(
6
), we have

g(h;v)=\frac{\|h\|^{2}}{2\tau}+\mathcal{F}\!\bigl[h+v\bigr],\qquad h\in\mathcal{H}.

Then
g(\cdot;v)
has
\mu
-Lipschitz gradient and is
\nu
-strongly convex with

\mu=\frac{1}{\tau}+L_{\mathcal{F}},\qquad\nu=\frac{1}{\tau}+m_{\mathcal{F}}.

Proof

See Appendix
A.6
.

Lemma 13

(Properties of
g(\cdot;v)
)
.

Let
g
be as in (
6
) and write
h^{*}(v):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}g(h;v)
. Then:

(a)

h^{*}(v)
is the unique solution of
h/\tau+\nabla\mathcal{F}[v+h]=0
. Equivalently,
h^{*}(v)=J_{\tau}(v)-v
is the exact-MMS increment at
v
;

(b)

g(0;v)=\mathcal{F}[v]
;

(c)

\sqrt{2g(0;v)/\nu}=K(v)
, where

K(v):=\sqrt{2\tau\mathcal{F}[v]/(1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}})};

(d)

\mu/\nu=(1+\tau L_{\mathcal{F}})/(1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}})=:\kappa
;

(e)

the prefactor
4L\mu/(\nu\lambda_{\tilde{w}}^{2})=4L\kappa/\lambda_{\tilde{w}}^{2}
.

Proof

Item (a) follows because
g(\cdot;v)
is
\nu
-strongly convex with
\nu>0
(Lemma
12
), hence has a unique minimizer characterized by the first-order condition
\nabla g(h;v)=h/\tau+\nabla\mathcal{F}[v+h]=0
.
Item (b) is immediate from
g(0;v)=\|0\|^{2}/(2\tau)+\mathcal{F}[v]
.
Item (c) follows from
\nu=(1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}})/\tau
together with
g(0;v)=\mathcal{F}[v]
:

\sqrt{2g(0;v)/\nu}=\sqrt{2\mathcal{F}[v]\cdot\tau/(1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}})}=K(v).

Items (d)–(e) are direct algebra.

The next theorem is the main technical result of this section. Its informal message: under (i) the strict interior-localization condition (Lemma
10
), which forces the reached sublevel set
S
into the compact image
\mathcal{K}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))\subset\mathcal{G}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
, and (ii) the data condition
C(v)\leq 1
, the curvature correction
\langle\mathrm{D}\mathbf{P}[\xi]\nabla g,\xi\rangle
from ambient to manifold Hessian is controlled by
\|\nabla g\|
on the relevant set. The bound is delicate: on the normal neighborhood
\mathcal{G}
the correction reaches its full size
\nu
(yielding only geodesic convexity,
\operatorname{Hess}g\geq 0
), whereas on the smaller sublevel set
S
the correction halves to
\nu/2
(yielding
\nu/2
-strong convexity — the strong-convexity constant degrades by a factor of two). No claim of strong convexity is made on the full open chart
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
.

Theorem 14

(Geodesic strong convexity and smoothness of

Fix

\tilde{w}

from (

holds, so the parameter-space sublevel set

from (

(H3)

the data condition

C(v)\;:=\;\frac{4L\kappa}{\lambda_{\tilde{w}}^{2}}\,\max\!\bigl\{\,\|h^{*}(v)\|_{\mathcal{H}},\ K(v)\,\bigr\}\;\leq\;1

Then

on

(b).

The reached sublevel set

Satisfiability of the data condition (

Proof
[Proof sketch (full proof in Appendix
A.8
)]
The Riemannian Hessian of
g
at
h\in\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
decomposes as

\langle\operatorname{Hess}g[\xi],\xi\rangle=\langle\nabla^{2}g[\xi],\xi\rangle+\langle\mathrm{D}\mathbf{P}_{(h)}[\xi]\,\nabla g,\xi\rangle
.
The first term inherits the ambient bounds
\nu\|\xi\|^{2}\leq\langle\nabla^{2}g[\xi],\xi\rangle\leq\mu\|\xi\|^{2}
.
The second (curvature correction) term is bounded in absolute value by
\frac{2L}{\lambda_{\tilde{w}}^{2}}\|\nabla g\|\,\|\xi\|^{2}
via Lemma
9
, so the analysis reduces to controlling
\|\nabla g\|
on the relevant domain.

Part (a) — on
\mathcal{G}
.

Using
\nabla g(h^{*})=0
and
\mu
-Lipschitz
\nabla g
,
\|\nabla g(h)\|\leq\mu\|h-h^{*}\|\leq\mu(\|h\|+\|h^{*}\|)
. For
h=\Phi(w)
with
w\in B(\tilde{w},r_{\tilde{w}})
the Lipschitz continuity of
u_{\mathrm{NN}}
gives
\|h\|\leq\mathrm{Lip}_{u_{\mathrm{NN}}}r_{\tilde{w}}
, so
\|\nabla g\|\leq\mu(\mathrm{Lip}_{u_{\mathrm{NN}}}r_{\tilde{w}}+\|h^{*}\|)
uniformly on
\mathcal{M}\supset\mathcal{G}
. The curvature correction therefore splits into two pieces, each
\leq\nu/2
:

\frac{2L\mu}{\lambda_{\tilde{w}}^{2}}\mathrm{Lip}_{u_{\mathrm{NN}}}r_{\tilde{w}}\leq\frac{\nu}{2}\quad\text{by the third entry of~\eqref{eq:rwdef}},\qquad\frac{2L\mu}{\lambda_{\tilde{w}}^{2}}\|h^{*}\|=\frac{\nu}{2}\cdot\frac{4L\kappa}{\lambda_{\tilde{w}}^{2}}\|h^{*}\|\leq\frac{\nu}{2}\quad\text{by~\eqref{eq:data-condition}}.

Summing, the correction is at most
\nu
, giving
\operatorname{Hess}g\geq\nu-\nu=0
on
\mathcal{G}
. This non-negativity together with the unique-geodesic property of Lemma
11
(a) yields geodesic convexity of
g(\cdot;v)
on
\mathcal{G}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
— but not strong convexity at this stage; the strong-convexity bound is recovered in part (c) on the smaller set
S
.

Part (b).

S\subset\mathcal{K}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))\subset\mathcal{G}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
by hypothesis (H2) and Lemma
11
(b); sublevel sets of geodesically convex functions on a geodesically convex set are themselves geodesically convex.

Part (c) — on
S
.

On
S
the
\|h\|+\|h^{*}\|
split is replaced by the tighter sublevel-set bound
\|h-h^{*}\|\leq\sqrt{2g(0;v)/\nu}=K(v)
(Lemma
13
(c)), giving
\|\nabla g\|\leq\mu K(v)
— a
single
term, independent of
r_{\tilde{w}}
. The correction is then

\frac{2L\mu}{\lambda_{\tilde{w}}^{2}}K(v)=\frac{\nu}{2}\cdot\frac{4L\kappa}{\lambda_{\tilde{w}}^{2}}K(v)\leq\frac{\nu}{2}
by (
27
), yielding
\operatorname{Hess}g\geq\nu-\tfrac{\nu}{2}=\tfrac{\nu}{2}
on
S
. Uniqueness of the minimizer follows from geodesic strong convexity on
S
together with
0\in S
.

Part (d).
The upper bound
\operatorname{Hess}g\leq\tfrac{3\mu}{2}
on
S
follows symmetrically from the same
S
-bound on
\|\nabla g\|
.

3.3
Convergence of Riemannian Gradient Flow

The convergence proof combines three standard ingredients from Riemannian optimization:

1.

Energy dissipation
: Along the gradient flow
\dot{h}_{t}=-\operatorname{grad}g(h_{t};v)
,
the energy
g(h_{t};v)
is nonincreasing, so the trajectory remains in the sublevel set
S
.

2.

Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality
: Geodesic
\nu/2
-strong convexity on
S
implies

\|\operatorname{grad}g(h;v)\|^{2}\geq\nu(g(h;v)-g(h_{\infty};v))
for all
h\in S
.

3.

Grönwall’s inequality
: Combining (1) and (2) yields the differential inequality

\frac{d}{dt}R_{t}\leq-\nu R_{t}
, which gives
R_{t}\leq R_{0}e^{-\nu t}
.

The distance bound (part (c)) follows from the first variation formula and a similar Grönwall argument.

Theorem 15

(Convergence of the Riemannian gradient flow)
.

Fix
\tilde{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
v=u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w})
, and assume hypotheses (H1)–(H3) of Theorem
14
hold (in particular, the strict interior-localization condition of Lemma
10
and the data condition (
27
)). Let
\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
be the open embedded chart,
\mathcal{K}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
its compact localization image,
\mathcal{G}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
the strongly geodesically convex normal neighborhood of Lemma
11
, and define the reached sublevel set

S:=\{h\in\mathcal{M}:g(h;v)\leq g(0;v)\}.

By Theorem
14
(b),
S\subset\mathcal{K}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))\subset\mathcal{G}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
, and
S
is geodesically convex and compact. When this theorem is invoked at
\tilde{w}=w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}
along the neural MMS trajectory, the geometric data
(\mathcal{M}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}),\mathcal{K}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}),\mathcal{G}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}),S_{n},h_{\infty}^{(n)})
are iterate-dependent; the constants
\mu,\nu,\kappa
depend only on the data (Lemma
13
), and Assumption
23
provides the uniform bounds
\inf_{n}\lambda_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}\geq\lambda_{\infty}>0
,
\sup_{n}C(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})\leq 1
, and the uniform interior-localization condition needed for the downstream chaining (Lemma
24
, Theorem
27
).
Let
(h_{t})_{t\geq 0}\subset\mathcal{M}
be a solution of the Riemannian gradient flow

\frac{\mathrm{d}h_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\operatorname{grad}g(h_{t};v),\qquad t\geq 0,

with initial condition
h_{0}=0\in S\subset\mathcal{M}
. Then the following hold:

(a)

The trajectory remains in the compact sublevel set
S
, and consequently stays inside
\mathcal{K}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))\subset\mathcal{G}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
at positive distance from
\partial\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
(in particular, no boundary or projected-flow construction is needed). Moreover,

h_{t}\to h_{\infty}=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{M}}g(h;v)\qquad\text{as }t\to\infty,

and the minimizer
h_{\infty}
lies in
S
.

(b)

Defining the energy gap

R_{t}:=g(h_{t};v)-g(h_{\infty};v)\geq 0,

we have for all
t\geq 0
,

R_{t}\leq R_{0}e^{-\nu t},

where
R_{0}=g(0;v)-g(h_{\infty};v)
depends on
v
(and hence on the iterate
w^{n}
when applied at MMS step
n
).

(c)

Defining the flow gap

D_{t}:=d_{\mathcal{M}}(h_{t},h_{\infty}),

we have for all
t\geq 0
,

\|h_{t}-h_{\infty}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq D_{t}\leq D_{0}e^{-\nu t/2},

where
D_{0}=d_{\mathcal{M}}(0,h_{\infty})
depends on
v
.

Proof
[Proof sketch (full proof in Appendix
A.9
)]
The proof combines three standard ingredients from Riemannian optimization, all on the geodesically convex sublevel set
S\subset\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{M}
:

1.

Energy dissipation & interior invariance.
Along the gradient flow
\dot{h}_{t}=-\operatorname{grad}g(h_{t};v)
, the energy
g(h_{t};v)
is nonincreasing, so the trajectory remains in
S
. By Theorem
14
(b),
S\subset\mathcal{K}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
, which is a compact subset of the open chart
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
at positive distance from
\partial\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
. Consequently the trajectory never approaches the chart boundary, the flow is well-defined for all
t\geq 0
on
\mathcal{M}
without any boundary or projected-flow construction, and standard Picard–Lindelöf arguments apply.

2.

Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality.
Geodesic
\nu/2
-strong convexity on
S
(Theorem
14
(c)) implies

\|\operatorname{grad}g(h;v)\|^{2}\geq\nu\,(g(h;v)-g(h_{\infty};v))
for all

(
7
, Lemma 11.28)
.
Combining with (1) gives
\frac{d}{dt}R_{t}\leq-\nu R_{t}
, hence
R_{t}\leq R_{0}e^{-\nu t}
by Grönwall.

3.

First variation formula.
Setting
\phi(t):=\frac{1}{2}d_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}(h_{t},h_{\infty})
and
using the geodesic strong convexity of
g(\cdot;v)|_{S}
at both
h_{t}
and

(both in
\mathcal{G}
, where the connecting geodesic is unique by Lemma

),
one obtains
\phi^{\prime}(t)\leq-\nu\phi(t)
, yielding
D_{t}\leq D_{0}e^{-\nu t/2}
. The ambient

\|h_{t}-h_{\infty}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq D_{t}
follows since
\mathcal{M}
is embedded.

The continuous-time exponential bound of Theorem
15
has a natural discrete-time counterpart: on the same sublevel set
S
, discrete Riemannian gradient descent (RGD) on
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
that takes its steps along the geodesics — i.e., via the exponential map — inherits a matching linear contraction rate. We formulate the discrete iteration with the exponential map rather than a generic retraction because it makes the geodesic-smoothness step in the proof immediate and matches the canonical Riemannian descent picture; the practical algorithm we actually implement is parameter-space preconditioned gradient descent (PGD), the trivial-to-implement Euler discretization of the parameter-space flow, and by the parameter–manifold equivalence of Section
2
PGD performs the same descent dynamics on
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
. Theorem
16
is therefore the discrete-time companion of the practical iteration; we do not formalize a per-step coordinate identification because no implementation actually computes the exponential map.

Theorem 16

(Discrete Riemannian gradient descent on the sublevel set)
.

Assume the hypotheses of Theorem
15

. By Theorem

is geodesically
\alpha

\beta
-smooth on the sublevel set
S

\alpha:=\tfrac{\nu}{2},\qquad\beta:=\tfrac{3\mu}{2}.

Consider the discrete Riemannian gradient descent iteration on
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
that takes its steps along the geodesics, i.e., via the exponential map:

h_{k+1}\;=\;\mathrm{Exp}_{h_{k}}\bigl(-\eta\,\operatorname{grad}f(h_{k})\bigr),\qquad k\geq 0,\quad h_{0}=0.

(29)

By Lemma
11
,
S\subset\mathcal{G}
lies inside a strongly geodesically convex normal neighborhood of
0
, on which
\mathrm{Exp}_{h_{k}}
is well defined for all tangent vectors of magnitude
\leq\pi/(2\Lambda)
(in particular for
\eta\,\|\operatorname{grad}f(h_{k})\|

For any constant step size

0<\eta\leq 1/\beta=2/(3\mu)

R_{k}:=f(h_{k})-f(h_{\infty})
and the flow gap

\displaystyle R_{k}\;

\displaystyle\leq\;(1-\alpha\eta)^{k}R_{0}\;=\;\Bigl(1-\tfrac{\nu\eta}{2}\Bigr)^{\!k}\bigl(g(0;v)-g(h_{\infty};v)\bigr),

(30)

\displaystyle\|h_{k}-h_{\infty}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\;\leq\;D_{k}\;

\displaystyle\leq\;\sqrt{\tfrac{4R_{0}}{\nu}}\,\Bigl(1-\tfrac{\nu\eta}{2}\Bigr)^{\!k/2},

(31)

where

as in Theorem

In particular, with the maximal step

, the energy contraction factor is

\kappa=\mu/\nu=(1+\tau L_{\mathcal{F}})/(1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}})
is the data-side condition number.

Proof
[Proof sketch]
Since
h_{k+1}=\mathrm{Exp}_{h_{k}}(-\eta\,\operatorname{grad}f(h_{k}))
lies on the geodesic emanating from
h_{k}
in direction
\xi_{k}:=-\eta\,\operatorname{grad}f(h_{k})
, geodesic
\beta
-smoothness of
f
on
S

(

gives the descent inequality

f(h_{k+1})\leq f(h_{k})+\langle\operatorname{grad}f(h_{k}),\xi_{k}\rangle+\tfrac{\beta}{2}\|\xi_{k}\|^{2}.

Plugging in
\xi_{k}=-\eta\,\operatorname{grad}f(h_{k})
,

f(h_{k+1})\;\leq\;f(h_{k})-\eta\bigl(1-\tfrac{\beta\eta}{2}\bigr)\|\operatorname{grad}f(h_{k})\|^{2}\;\leq\;f(h_{k})-\tfrac{\eta}{2}\|\operatorname{grad}f(h_{k})\|^{2}

for
\eta\leq 1/\beta
. In particular
f(h_{k+1})\leq f(h_{k})
, so
h_{k+1}\in S
and the iteration is well-defined.
Geodesic
\alpha
-strong convexity on
S
implies the Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality
\|\operatorname{grad}f(h)\|^{2}\geq 2\alpha\,(f(h)-f(h_{\infty}))
on
S

(
7
, Lemma 11.28)
. Combining,

f(h_{k+1})-f(h_{\infty})\;\leq\;(1-\alpha\eta)\bigl(f(h_{k})-f(h_{\infty})\bigr),

which iterates to (
30
).
For the flow gap (
31
), geodesic
\alpha
-strong convexity on
S
yields
f(h_{k})-f(h_{\infty})\geq\tfrac{\alpha}{2}d_{\mathcal{M}}(h_{k},h_{\infty})^{2}

(
7
, Theorem 11.21)
, so

D_{k}^{2}\leq(2/\alpha)R_{k}\leq(2/\alpha)(1-\alpha\eta)^{k}R_{0}=(4/\nu)(1-\nu\eta/2)^{k}R_{0}
,
and
\|h_{k}-h_{\infty}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq D_{k}
since
\mathcal{M}
is embedded in
\mathcal{H}
.

Remark 17

(Inexact linear-solve and Levenberg–Marquardt damping)
.

In practice the exact Gauss–Newton system
(J^{*}J)^{-1}J^{*}\nabla g
is replaced by an inexact one, e.g., a conjugate-gradient solve truncated to tolerance
\varepsilon_{\rm CG}
and/or Levenberg–Marquardt damping
(J^{*}J+\rho I)^{-1}
. The resulting RGD step can be analyzed as a perturbed-gradient iteration
h_{k+1}=\mathrm{Exp}_{h_{k}}(-\eta\,\operatorname{grad}f(h_{k})+e_{k})
with
\|e_{k}\|\leq\gamma\,\eta\,\|\operatorname{grad}f(h_{k})\|+\eta\,\delta
for
\gamma\in[0,1)
and
\delta\geq 0
controlled by
\varepsilon_{\rm CG}
and
\rho
. A standard perturbed-PL argument
(
7
, Theorem 11.31)
gives linear convergence up to an error floor proportional to
\delta^{2}
:

f(h_{k})-f(h_{\infty})\leq(1-(1-\gamma)\alpha\eta)^{k}(f(h_{0})-f(h_{\infty}))+O(\delta^{2}).

A precise statement is left to future work; Theorem
16
is the exact-step counterpart.

4
Global Error Propagation for Neural MMS with Inexact Increment Solutions

Having established exponential convergence of the Riemannian gradient flow for a single
increment subproblem (Theorem
15
), we now address the global behavior
of the neural MMS across multiple iterations.
The key question is: how do the errors from (i) finite-time truncation of the inner flow
and (ii) neural approximation of the proximal map accumulate over iterations?
The answer relies on the contraction property of the proximal map
J_{\tau}
:
at each MMS step, the contraction factor
1/(1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}})
absorbs the per-step errors,
yielding a uniform
\delta
-tracking bound (Theorem
27
).
Figure
4
illustrates this one-step error decomposition.

Recall that
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously Fréchet differentiable, coercive, and
m_{\mathcal{F}}
-strongly convex. For
\tau>0
, define the proximal map

J_{\tau}(x):=\mbox{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{F}}(x):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\Big(\frac{1}{2\tau}\|u-x\|^{2}+\mathcal{F}[u]\Big).

(32)

Since
m_{\mathcal{F}}>0
and
\tau>0
, the objective in (
32
) is
(\frac{1}{\tau}+m_{\mathcal{F}})
–strongly convex and hence
J_{\tau}
is single-valued and well-defined.

Let
\{u^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
denote the exact MMS sequence defined by

u^{n+1}=J_{\tau}(u^{n}),\qquad n\geq 0.

Lemma 18

(Lipschitz continuity of
J_{\tau}
)
.

Since
\mathcal{F}
is
m_{\mathcal{F}}
–strongly convex with
m_{\mathcal{F}}>0
, the proximal map satisfies

\|J_{\tau}(x)-J_{\tau}(y)\|\leq\frac{1}{1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}}\|x-y\|,\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{H}.

Proof

Write
\hat{x}=J_{\tau}(x)
and
\hat{y}=J_{\tau}(y)
. By the optimality conditions,
x-\hat{x}=\tau\nabla\mathcal{F}(\hat{x})
and
y-\hat{y}=\tau\nabla\mathcal{F}(\hat{y})
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is
m_{\mathcal{F}}
–strongly convex,
\nabla\mathcal{F}
is
m_{\mathcal{F}}
–monotone, so

\langle(x-\hat{x})-(y-\hat{y}),\hat{x}-\hat{y}\rangle\geq\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}\|\hat{x}-\hat{y}\|^{2}
.
Rearranging and applying Cauchy–Schwarz gives
(1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}})\|\hat{x}-\hat{y}\|\leq\|x-y\|
.

Also recall

J_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}(x):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{u_{\mathrm{NN}}\in\mathrm{NN}}\left\{\frac{1}{2\tau}\left\lVert u_{\mathrm{NN}}-x\right\rVert^{2}+\mathcal{F}[u_{\mathrm{NN}}]\right\}.

(33)

With Assumption
1
, Lemma
10
(which contains the minimizer strictly inside the inner ball), and Theorem
15
(which gives existence and uniqueness of the minimizer on
\mathcal{M}(x)
), we have

J_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}(x)=h_{\infty}+x,

(34)

where

h_{\infty}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{h\in\mathcal{M}(x)}g(h;x)

is uniquely determined by
x
. Thus,
J_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}
is well-defined.

Let
\{u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence of Riemannian gradient flow (RGF) running until
T
starting from the previous
u_{\mathrm{NN},T}^{n}
. That is,

u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}:=u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}+h_{T},

(35)

where
\{h_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\subset\mathcal{M}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})
solves

\frac{\mathrm{d}h_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\operatorname{grad}g(h_{t};u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})

with
h_{0}=0
.

In particular,

u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},\infty}=u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}+h_{\infty}=J_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}\big(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}\big).

(36)

Corollary 19

(Finite-time error of RGF)
.

There exists a sequence
\{\bar{C}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
with
\bar{C}_{n}>0
such that for all
n\geq 1
,

\|u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},\infty}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\;\leq\;\bar{C}_{n}\,e^{-\nu T/2},\qquad\nu=\frac{1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}}{\tau}\ \text{(Lemma~\ref{lem:g-properties})}.

(37)

Explicitly,
\bar{C}_{n}:=D_{0}^{(n)}=d_{\mathcal{M}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})}(0,h_{\infty}^{(n)})
is the geodesic distance on the increment manifold from the origin to the inner-flow minimizer at step
n
.

Proof

Step 1: identify
\bar{C}_{n}
.

By (
35
) and (
36
),
u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},\infty}=h_{T}-h_{\infty}^{(n)}
. Theorem
15
(c) gives
\|h_{T}-h_{\infty}^{(n)}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq D_{T}\leq D_{0}^{(n)}e^{-\nu T/2}
. Setting
\bar{C}_{n}:=D_{0}^{(n)}
yields (
37
).

Step 2: explicit bound on
D_{0}^{(n)}
.

Under the data condition (
27
) at iterate
u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}
, we bound
D_{0}^{(n)}
via a CAT chord-to-geodesic comparison. Since
h_{\infty}^{(n)}\in S_{n}
and
0\in S_{n}
, the sublevel-set bound (
102
) together with Lemma
13
(c) gives

\|h_{\infty}^{(n)}-h^{*}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\sqrt{2g(0;u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})/\nu}=K(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}).

Combined with Lemma
13
(a) and the triangle inequality,

\|h_{\infty}^{(n)}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\;\leq\;2\,\max\bigl\{K(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}),\,\|h^{*}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})\|_{\mathcal{H}}\bigr\}\;\leq\;\frac{\lambda_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}^{2}}{2L\kappa},

(38)

where the second inequality uses
C(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})\leq 1
. Since
\mathcal{M}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})
is locally
\mathrm{CAT}(\Lambda_{n}^{2})
with
\Lambda_{n}:=2L/\lambda_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}^{2}
(Lemma
9
), and
\Lambda_{n}\,\|h_{\infty}^{(n)}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1/\kappa\leq 1
by (
38
), the chord-to-geodesic comparison
(
8
, Part II, Prop. 1.4 and Thm. 4.1)
yields

D_{0}^{(n)}=d_{\mathcal{M}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})}(0,h_{\infty}^{(n)})\leq c_{\Lambda}^{(n)}\,\|h_{\infty}^{(n)}\|_{\mathcal{H}},

where the chord/geodesic constant
c_{\Lambda}^{(n)}\geq 1
depends only on the dimensionless product
\Lambda_{n}\,\|h_{\infty}^{(n)}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
, which is bounded by
1/\kappa
by (
38
). Combining:

\bar{C}_{n}\;=\;D_{0}^{(n)}\;\leq\;c_{\Lambda}^{(n)}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}^{2}}{2L\kappa}.

(39)

Remark 20

(Uniform boundedness of
\bar{C}_{n}
along the trajectory)
.

The explicit bound (
39
) from the proof of Corollary
19
is pointwise in
n
. Uniform boundedness along the trajectory follows under Assumption
23
: by Remark
47
, the derived uniform operator-norm bound
\sup_{n}\|\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})\|_{\rm op}<\infty
implies
\sup_{n}\lambda_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}<\infty
(singular values are dominated by the operator norm), and
\Lambda_{n}\,\|h_{\infty}^{(n)}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1/\kappa
remains uniformly
\leq 1
for all
n
. Setting
c_{\Lambda}:=\sup_{n}c_{\Lambda}^{(n)}<\infty
, we obtain

\sup_{n\geq 0}\bar{C}_{n}\;\leq\;c_{\Lambda}\cdot\frac{\bigl(\sup_{n}\lambda_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}\bigr)^{2}}{2L\kappa}\;<\;\infty,

so the bound (
37
) chains uniformly across the trajectory:
\sup_{n}\bar{C}_{n}\,e^{-\nu T/2}=\Theta(e^{-\nu T/2})
.

4.1
One-Step Error Decomposition

Assumption 21

(Neural proximal approximation precision)
.

The computed neural proximal map
J_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}
approximates the exact proximal
map
J_{\tau}
at the one-step level. More precisely, there exists a nonnegative
function
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{NN}}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that, for all relevant
inputs
x\in\mathcal{H}
arising in the error-propagation analysis,

\|J_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}(x)-J_{\tau}(x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{NN}}(x).

We write

\varepsilon:=\sup_{x}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{NN}}(x),

where the supremum is taken over the relevant set of inputs along the exact and
inexact MMS trajectories. The target accuracy
\delta>0
is chosen so that

\varepsilon<\frac{\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}}{1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}}\,\delta.

(40)

Interpretation of the one-step precision
\varepsilon
and its decomposition into trial-space and sampling/quadrature errors is discussed in Appendix
C.7
(Remark
46
).

Assumption 22

(Non-degeneracy on all increment manifolds)
.

Let
\{w^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the parameters generated by the neural MMS (or the inexact increment solver). Assume
u_{\mathrm{NN}}
has
\lambda_{w^{n}}
-non-degenerate Jacobian at all
w^{n}
for all
n
. That is, for all

,

\lambda_{\min}\!\big(\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n})^{*}\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n})\big)\geq\lambda_{w^{n}}^{2},

and the radii
\{r_{w^{n}}\}
are chosen so that Lemma

applies on each ball

(cf. (

Assumption 23

(Trajectory uniformity)
.

Along the inexact trajectory

generated by the neural MMS, the following uniform bounds hold:

(U1)

Uniform non-degeneracy.
There exists

such that

This strengthens Assumption
22
from per-iterate positivity to a uniform positive lower bound.

(U2)

Uniform data condition.
The hypothesis of Theorem

holds at every iterate:

\sup_{n\geq 0}\,C(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})\;\leq\;1,

where

is the data condition of Theorem

(see (
27
); the

(U3)

Uniform output isolation around the inner ball.
There exists

(U4)

Lemma 24

(Induction Step)
.

Suppose
\left\lVert u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\delta
, and assume Theorem
14
applies at the iterate
\tilde{w}=w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}
(i.e., the data condition (
27
) holds at
u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}
). If one runs the Riemannian gradient flow starting from
u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}
on the manifold
\mathcal{M}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})
for time
T=T_{n}
satisfying

T_{n}\;\geq\;\frac{2}{\nu}\log\!\left(\bar{C}_{n}\cdot\frac{1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}}{\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}(\delta-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon}\right),

(41)

where
\varepsilon
is in Assumption
21
and
\bar{C}_{n}
is the step-
n
constant from Corollary
19
, then the error at the next step is bounded:

\left\lVert u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n+1}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\;\leq\;\delta.

By Lemma
13
and Lemma
4
, the constants
\nu,\mu,\kappa
in (
41
) depend only on the data. The genuinely iterate-dependent quantities are
\bar{C}_{n}
and the sublevel set
S_{n}
; under Assumption
23
together with Remark
20
,
\sup_{n}\bar{C}_{n}<\infty
, so
\sup_{n}T_{n}<\infty
and the inductive bound chains uniformly.

Proof

By Lipschitz continuity of
J_{\tau}
(Lemma
18
), if
\|u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}<\delta
then

\|J_{\tau}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})-J_{\tau}(u^{n})\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\frac{1}{1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}}\|u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}<\frac{\delta}{1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}}.

(42)

By the triangle inequality and (
36
), (
37
), (
40
), (
42
),

\displaystyle\|u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n+1}\|_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\leq\|u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},\infty}\|_{\mathcal{H}}+\|u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},\infty}-u^{n+1}\|_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\|u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},\infty}\|_{\mathcal{H}}+\|J_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})-J_{\tau}(u^{n})\|_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\leq\|u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},\infty}\|_{\mathcal{H}}+\|J_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})-J_{\tau}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})\|_{\mathcal{H}}+\|J_{\tau}(u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})-J_{\tau}(u^{n})\|_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\leq\bar{C}_{n}\,e^{-\nu T/2}+\varepsilon+\frac{1}{1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}}\|u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

(43)

In particular, if
\|u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}<\delta
then

\|u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n+1}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\;\leq\;\bar{C}_{n}\,e^{-\nu T/2}+\frac{\delta}{1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}}+\varepsilon.

Choosing
T
so that

\bar{C}_{n}\,e^{-\nu T/2}+\delta/(1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}})+\varepsilon\leq\delta

is equivalent to

e^{-\nu T/2}\leq\bar{C}_{n}^{-1}\bigl(\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}\delta/(1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}})-\varepsilon\bigr),

i.e., to the threshold (

). This ensures
\|u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n+1}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\delta
, closing the induction.

Remark 25
.

We emphasize that the error decomposition in (
4.1
) is fundamentally universal and independent of the specific optimization algorithm used for the inner MMS problem. Structurally, any numerical inner solver yields a three-part accumulation:
(i) the contraction from the proximal operator,
(ii) the spatial approximation error
\varepsilon
of the neural network, and
(iii) an inexact optimization error.
The primary theoretical contribution of our preconditioned gradient flow analysis is showing that, under the standing assumptions and Assumption
22
(and uniformly along the trajectory under Assumption
23
), the Gauss–Newton-type flow specifically guarantees a rigorously bounded, exponentially decaying estimate
\bar{C}_{n}\,e^{-\nu T/2}
at each step
n
for this inexact optimization step.

Lemma
24
’s threshold (
41
) chooses a uniform
T_{n}
sufficient for the per-step bound
e_{n}\leq\delta
, but only yields a uniform-in-
n
bound on
e_{n}=\|u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
– not a summable one. The downstream parameter-space convergence theorem (Theorem
33
) requires a finite trajectory length
\sum_{n}\Delta_{n}<\infty
, which in turn requires
\sum_{n}e_{n}<\infty
(entering via Corollary
30
and (
57
)). We therefore record below a refinement of Lemma
24
obtained by strengthening the choice of
T_{n}
to grow linearly in
n
; this is exactly the geometric-tracking bound used in the proof of Theorem
33
(b) (
59
). The bounds (
45
), (
46
), (
47
) are stated as a corollary (rather than as a remark or an inline note) so that they are citable as formal equations in that proof.

Corollary 26

(Geometric tracking under growing
T_{n}
)
.

Fix
\eta_{0}\in\bigl(0,\,(1-\rho)\delta-\varepsilon\bigr)
(a positive interval by Assumption
21
) and strengthen Lemma
24
’s threshold to require
T_{n}
to grow linearly in
n
:

T_{n}\;\geq\;\frac{2}{\nu}\Bigl(\log\!\frac{\bar{C}_{n}}{\eta_{0}}+n\log\!\frac{1}{\rho}\Bigr),

(44)

so that
\bar{C}_{n}\,e^{-\nu T_{n}/2}\leq\eta_{0}\rho^{n}
. Then Lemma
24
’s recurrence (
4.1
) yields the geometric-tracking bound

e_{n}\;:=\;\|u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\;\leq\;\rho^{n}\,e_{0}\,+\,n\,\eta_{0}\,\rho^{n-1}\,+\,\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\rho}\,(1-\rho^{n});

(45)

i.e.
e_{n}
decays geometrically toward an asymptotic floor of
\varepsilon/(1-\rho)
. Two regimes follow:

•

(I) Perfect-approximation regime (
\varepsilon=0
):
the trajectory error sum is finite,

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e_{n}\;\leq\;\frac{e_{0}}{1-\rho}+\frac{\eta_{0}}{(1-\rho)^{2}}.

(46)

•

(II) Uniform-approximation regime (
\varepsilon>0
):
the geometric component decays as in (I), but
\liminf_{n}e_{n}=\varepsilon/(1-\rho)>0
. For any finite horizon
N\geq 0
,

\sum_{n=0}^{N}e_{n}\;\leq\;\frac{e_{0}}{1-\rho}+\frac{\eta_{0}}{(1-\rho)^{2}}+\frac{(N+1)\,\varepsilon}{1-\rho}.

(47)

Proof

Under (
44
),
\bar{C}_{n}\,e^{-\nu T_{n}/2}\leq\eta_{0}\rho^{n}
, so the per-step accumulator in (
4.1
) is
\eta_{n}:=\bar{C}_{n}e^{-\nu T_{n}/2}+\varepsilon\leq\eta_{0}\rho^{n}+\varepsilon
. Iterating the recurrence
e_{n+1}\leq\rho\,e_{n}+\eta_{n}
with
e_{0}
as base case,

e_{n}\leq\rho^{n}e_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\rho^{n-1-k}\,\eta_{k}\leq\rho^{n}e_{0}+\eta_{0}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\rho^{n-1}+\varepsilon\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\rho^{n-1-k}=\rho^{n}e_{0}+n\eta_{0}\rho^{n-1}+\frac{\varepsilon(1-\rho^{n})}{1-\rho},

which is (
45
).
Summing in
n\geq 0
:
\sum_{n}\rho^{n}=1/(1-\rho)
,
\sum_{n}n\rho^{n-1}=1/(1-\rho)^{2}
, so the geometric components contribute
e_{0}/(1-\rho)+\eta_{0}/(1-\rho)^{2}
. The
\varepsilon
-floor contributes
0
in regime (I) and
\varepsilon\sum_{n=0}^{N}(1-\rho^{n})/(1-\rho)\leq(N+1)\varepsilon/(1-\rho)
for finite
N
in regime (II).

4.2
Accumulated Global Error Bounds

The following theorem is the culminating result of the paper.
Informally, it states that if each inner solve is accurate enough
(controlled by the flow time
T
and the approximation precision
\varepsilon
),
then the inexact neural iterates stay within distance
\delta
of the exact MMS iterates
for all time, and converge to an
O(\delta)
-neighborhood of the global minimizer.
The proof is by induction: at each step, the one-step error decomposes into
(i) a
contraction term

\frac{\delta}{1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}}
from the Lipschitz constant of
J_{\tau}
,
(ii) a
finite-time truncation error

\bar{C}_{n}\,e^{-\nu T/2}
(with the
n
-indexed constant
\bar{C}_{n}
of Corollary
19
; bounded uniformly in
n
under Assumption
23
by Remark
20
) from stopping the inner flow early, and
(iii) a
neural approximation error

\varepsilon
.
Choosing
T
large enough makes (ii) negligible, and the condition on
\varepsilon

Remark 29

(Continuous vs. discrete inner solver)
.

Comparing (

5
Parameter-Space Convergence

Sections
2
–
4
establish a function-space convergence theory by interpreting the parameter-space preconditioned gradient flow as the Riemannian gradient flow on the increment manifold
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n}))\subset\mathcal{H}
and propagating the inexactness through the neural MMS iterations. We now translate this function-space guarantee back to parameter space. Since neural networks may admit symmetries (e.g., permutations of hidden units), convergence is most naturally stated as convergence of

to an

-neighborhood of

\{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}\}
to a single point.

Figure

illustrates the picture: along the inexact trajectory

(Lemma
4
); under Assumption
23
these radii are uniformly bounded below by
r_{\infty}:=\inf_{n}r_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}>0
, and the entire trajectory remains confined to a single bounded set in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
(Theorem
33
(b)). Each parameter step is controlled by the corresponding function-space step via the inverse-stability bound
\|w^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}\|_{2}\leq\lambda_{\infty}^{-1}\|u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
(Corollary
30
below).

Let
u^{*}\in\arg\min_{\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{F}
, which is unique by

-strong convexity. We translate the function-space neighborhood convergence into per-step parameter displacement using the local non-degeneracy of the Jacobian.

Corollary 30

(Per-step parameter displacement bound)
.

Adopt the standing assumptions in Sections
2
–
4
. Let
\{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the inexact parameter iterates and

. Suppose

has a
\lambda_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}
-non-degenerate Jacobian at

and the next iterate satisfies

\|w^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\lambda_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}}\,\|u^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}\|_{\mathcal{H}},\qquad n\geq 0.

(51)

Under Assumption
23
(U1) and the derived uniform local-radius bound (Remark

),
\inf_{n}r_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}\geq r_{\infty}>0
, so the bound chains uniformly along the trajectory.

Proof

The bi-Lipschitz bound established in the proof of Proposition
5
gives

\|u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)-u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{H}}\geq(2\lambda_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}-Lr_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}})\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2}\geq\lambda_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2}

Corollary
30
bypasses the nonconvexity of the parameter-space objective by leveraging the function-space Riemannian gradient flow on the geodesically strongly convex increment manifold (Sections
3
–
4
) and translating its convergence into a per-step parameter displacement via the local non-degeneracy of
\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}
alone—no global bi-Lipschitz constant is required.

We next combine this per-step bound with the function-space convergence (Theorem

) to obtain a global convergence theorem in parameter space. The key idea: exponential decay of
\|u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{*}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
yields a summable total parameter trajectory length, which (i) ensures the iterates form a Cauchy sequence in

, and (ii) bounds the depletion of the non-degeneracy budget along the trajectory.

Lemma 31

(Non-degeneracy decay along the trajectory)
.

Suppose
u_{\mathrm{NN}}
has
L
-Lipschitz Jacobian and a
\lambda_{w^{0}}
-non-degenerate Jacobian
at

. Let
\{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the inexact parameter iterates and write

\Delta_{n}:=\|w^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}\|_{2}

L\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Delta_{k}<2\lambda_{w^{0}},

(52)

the Jacobian at

remains non-degenerate, with

\sigma_{\min}\!\big(\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})\big)\ \geq\ 2\lambda_{w^{0}}-L\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Delta_{k}\ =:\ 2\lambda_{n},

so

is
\lambda_{n}
-non-degenerate at
w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}
(in the sense of Definition

Remark 32

(

as Weyl proxy vs. actual non-degeneracy)
.

The quantity

above is a

on the actual non-degeneracy constant

of Assumption

\lambda_{n}\leq\lambda_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}
. The lemma certifies

step-wise as long as (

by itself guarantee

for the entire infinite trajectory. The uniform positivity
\inf_{n}\lambda_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}\geq\lambda_{\infty}>0
is the content of Assumption
23
(U1); under the initial-gap condition of Theorem
33
(iii), the proof of Theorem

(b) establishes both that (

and that
\inf_{n}\lambda_{n}\geq\lambda_{\infty}
.

Proof

By induction on
n
. At
n=0
,
\sigma_{\min}(\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{0}))\geq 2\lambda_{w^{0}}
by hypothesis.
Suppose the bound holds at step

. By the

-Lipschitz continuity of
\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}
and the Weyl-type inequality

|\sigma_{\min}(A)-\sigma_{\min}(B)|\leq\|A-B\|_{\rm op}
,

\sigma_{\min}\!\big(\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T})\big)\ \geq\ \sigma_{\min}\!\big(\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T})\big)-L\Delta_{n}\ \geq\ 2\lambda_{w^{0}}-L\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Delta_{k}\ =\ 2\lambda_{n+1},

which is positive by (
52
).

We are now ready to state the main convergence theorem in parameter space.

k=0,\ldots,n
and using
\|u^{k+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{k}_{\mathrm{NN},T}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq e_{k+1}+\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|_{\mathcal{H}}+e_{k}
together with the a-priori telescope (

,

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Delta_{k}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\lambda_{\infty}}\Bigl(\sum_{k=0}^{n}(e_{k}+e_{k+1})+\sum_{k=0}^{n}\|u^{k+1}-u^{k}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\Bigr)\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\lambda_{\infty}}\Bigl[2E_{\infty}+\frac{2+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}}{\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}}\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\Bigr]\;\leq\;\frac{S}{\lambda_{\infty}},

(59)

where

is the trajectory budget from (

\Delta_{n}\leq S/\lambda_{\infty}<r_{\infty}
by the second bound in hypothesis (iii), so
\Delta_{n}<r_{\infty}\leq r_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}
, validating Corollary
30
.

Non-degeneracy at step
n+1
.

By Lemma
31
,
\lambda_{n+1}=\lambda_{w^{0}}-(L/2)\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Delta_{k}\geq\lambda_{w^{0}}-(L/2)\cdot S/\lambda_{\infty}>\lambda_{\infty}
, by the first bound in hypothesis (iii). Since

(Weyl proxy is a lower bound; Remark

), also
\lambda_{w^{n+1}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}\geq\lambda_{n+1}>\lambda_{\infty}
.

This proves
(\mathrm{IH}_{n+1})
, closing the induction. Taking

in (

55
).

(a) Function-space convergence.

Part (b) establishes Assumption
23
(U1) along the trajectory; Assumption

(U2) is verified along the way under the initial-gap condition (Remark

Remark 34

(Practical relevance and numerical evidence)
.

Theorem
33
provides a
worst-case
guarantee in which the
non-degeneracy budget shrinks monotonically as iterations proceed.
However, this pessimistic scenario need not arise in practice:
the actual non-degeneracy constant may stay roughly constant (or even improve)
along the optimization trajectory, well above the worst-case lower bound.

The numerical experiments (see Figure
7
) monitor the minimum singular value
s_{\min}(J(w))

throughout the MMS iterations and provide supporting evidence:

•

For the Gauss–Newton (GN) inner solver,
s_{\min}
remains bounded away from zero
at an
O(10^{-2})
level throughout the iterations, indicating that the Jacobian
non-degeneracy is
maintained
along the GN trajectory—far better than the
worst-case decay predicted by Lemma
31
.

•

In contrast, Adam and L-BFGS drive
s_{\min}
to near-machine-precision levels
(
\sim 10^{-14}
), indicating rapid loss of Jacobian non-degeneracy.

These observations suggest that the preconditioned (Gauss–Newton) gradient flow
naturally preserves the manifold structure that underlies the convergence theory,
making the effective convergence horizon much larger than the worst-case bound.
Understanding why the GN flow preserves Jacobian non-degeneracy—and whether this
can be proved rigorously—is an interesting direction for future work.

6
Numerical Experiments

The theory developed above establishes a convergence analysis of the
Gauss–Newton-type preconditioned flow as the inner solver for each neural MMS proximal
subproblem: under local non-degeneracy and curvature-control assumptions, the induced
parameter-space dynamics coincides with the Riemannian gradient flow of the increment
objective on the neural increment manifold and can converge to a global minimum of the subproblem. In realistic neural-network training,
however, several of these technical assumptions, such as uniform Jacobian non-degeneracy
and geodesic convexity of the increment manifold, are difficult to verify directly. Therefore,
the goal of this section is not to provide a numerical verification of all assumptions
in the theory. Instead, the experiments are designed to address two complementary goals.
First, we measure how closely the neural MMS iterates track the exact Hilbert-space
trajectory (supporting the global error-propagation result of Theorem
27
).
Second, we examine whether the Gauss–Newton-type inner solver is an effective method
for solving the neural MMS subproblems across a range of benchmarks, both in the
underparameterized regime where the theoretical assumptions are expected to hold,
and in the overparameterized regime where Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) regularization is often needed.

We mainly focus on a general nonlinear regression problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in numerical experiments. At the population level, we may view nonlinear regressions as an optimization problem in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mu)
with energy

\mathcal{F}[u]=\frac{1}{2}\|u-f^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}(u({\bm{x}})-f^{*}({\bm{x}}))^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu\ ,

where
f^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is the target function,
\mu({\bm{x}})
is the distribution of data. Given training data
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
with
y_{i}=f^{\ast}({\bm{x}}_{i})
,
\mu
is replaced by the empirical measure
\mu_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}
. Let
u_{w}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
denote a neural network parameterized by
w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the goal is to determine parameters
w
that minimize the empirical risk

\mathcal{F}[u_{w}]=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(u_{w}({\bm{x}}_{i})-y_{i}\bigr)^{2}.

(60)

Let
u^{n}(\cdot)=u_{w^{n}}(\cdot)
denote the neural network function at time step
n
. The minimizing movement scheme updates the parameters by solving the optimization problem

w^{n+1}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathcal{J}^{n}(w),

(61)

at each step, where

\mathcal{J}^{n}(w)=\frac{1}{2\tau N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(u_{w}({\bm{x}}_{i})-u^{n}({\bm{x}}_{i})\bigr)^{2}+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(u_{w}({\bm{x}}_{i})-y_{i}\bigr)^{2}.

(62)

Here,
\tau>0
is the temporal stepsize.

Gauss–Newton flow and its discretization

We first define the Gauss-Newton flow in this specific setting. Let
\mathbf{u}(w)=(u_{w}({\bm{x}}_{1}),\dots,u_{w}({\bm{x}}_{N}))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and the target vector
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})^{\top}
.
We introduce the auxiliary mapping
\Phi(w):\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
defined as

\Phi(w)=\mathbf{u}(w)-\mathbf{u}^{n}.

(63)

The MMS objective (
62
) can be rewritten as a composite function
g^{n}(\Phi(w))
, where
g^{n}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is the strictly convex quadratic function:

g^{n}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{1}{2\tau N}\|\mathbf{z}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2N}\left\|\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{u}^{n}-\mathbf{y}\right\|^{2}.

(64)

The continuous Gauss–Newton flow for minimizing this composite objective is defined by

\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\left(\mathrm{D}\Phi(w)^{\!\top}\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{z}}g^{n}\mathrm{D}\Phi(w)\right)^{-1}\nabla_{w}\mathcal{J}(w),

(65)

where
\mathrm{D}\Phi(w)=J(w)
is the standard Jacobian matrix
J(w)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
with entries
J_{ij}=\partial u_{w}(x_{i})/\partial w_{j}
. The Hessian of the convex function
g^{n}
with respect to
\mathbf{z}
is

\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{z}}g^{n}=\left(\frac{1}{\tau N}+\frac{1}{N}\right)I_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau}\right)I_{N}.

(66)

Substituting this into (
65
), the approximate Hessian matrix becomes:

H_{\text{GN}}=J^{\top}\left[\frac{1}{N}\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau}\right)I\right]J=\frac{1}{N}\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau}\right)J^{\top}J.

(67)

The gradient of the objective
\mathcal{J}(w)
is given by:

\nabla_{w}\mathcal{J}(w)=J^{\top}\left[\frac{1}{\tau N}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{n})+\frac{1}{N}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{y})\right].

(68)

Combining these, the Gauss–Newton flow simplifies to:

\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\left(\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau}\right)J^{\top}J\right)^{-1}J^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{n})+(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{y})\right).

(69)

This flow is used in all numerical experiments below.

Remark 35
.

The theoretical analysis in Sections 2–4 is based on the
preconditioned flow

\frac{dw}{dt}=-\Bigl(\mathrm{D}\Phi^{n}(w)^{\!\top}\mathrm{D}\Phi^{n}(w)\Bigr)^{-1}\nabla_{w}g^{n}\bigl(\Phi^{n}(w)\bigr),\qquad w(0)=w^{n},

which uses
(D\Phi^{\top}D\Phi)^{-1}
as preconditioner rather than
incorporating
\nabla^{2}_{z}g^{n}
explicitly.
Since
\mathrm{D}\Phi^{n}(w)=J(w)
and

\nabla_{w}g^{n}(\Phi(w))=(D\Phi^{n})^{\!\top}\nabla g^{n}
,
the flow can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-(J^{\!\top}J)^{-1}J^{\!\top}\left(\frac{1}{\tau N}\Phi(w)+\frac{1}{N}\left(\Phi(w)+{\bm{u}}^{n}-{\bm{y}}\right)\right)

(70)

\displaystyle=-(J^{\!\top}J)^{-1}J^{\!\top}\left(\frac{1}{\tau N}({\bm{u}}(w)-{\bm{u}}^{n})+\frac{1}{N}\left({\bm{u}}(w)-{\bm{y}}\right)\right)\ .

Comparing (
70
) with (
69
), the two
flows differ only by the scalar factor

\frac{1}{1+1/\tau}
in the preconditioner, which amounts to a reparametrization of time and leaves the parameter-space trajectory unchanged.

Although the theoretical framework is based on the continuous Riemannian gradient flow, obtaining the accurate solution for Eq. (
69
) is computationally prohibitive for deep neural networks due to the high dimensionality of the Jacobian. To balance computational efficiency with the benefits of curvature information, we discretize the flow using an explicit Euler method. Rather than fixing
\delta t
, we adaptively select the step size via a monotone backtracking line search combined with a trust-region-style norm clip on the search direction (contraction factor
0.5
, at most
8
backtracking steps, and maximum step norm
5.0
unless stated otherwise). The underlying linear system is solved using the conjugate gradient (CG) method, limited to a maximum of 30 iterations. This combination balances the efficiency of an inexact linear solve with the robustness afforded by adaptive step sizes.

The theoretical framework relies on the non-degeneracy of the Jacobian at the initial condition along the MMS trajectory. This assumption guarantees that the pullback metric
G(w)=Du_{\rm NN}(w)^{\ast}Du_{\rm NN}(w)
remains strictly positive definite, allowing the Gauss–Newton dynamics to be interpreted as a Riemannian gradient flow. However, as noted by
10
, in the over-parameterized regime
(p>N)
. even if the non-degeneracy condition
\lambda_{w_{0}}>0
holds generically, it is often very small, leading to severe ill-conditioning of
J^{\rm T}J
. This practical reality necessitates the use of LM regularization to stabilize the GN updates. Because LM damping modifies the effective metric (replacing
J^{\top}J
with
J^{\top}J+\rho I
), the resulting regularized dynamics diverge from the exact Riemannian gradient flow of the original pullback metric. To systematically address this gap between our idealized theoretical analysis and practical over-parameterized landscapes, we consider both the underparameterized and overparameterized regimes. In the latter regime, LM regularization may be
applied to stabilize the GN updates and the goal is to demonstrate stability and performance in practice.

6.1
Error accumulation in MMS steps

In this subsection, we numerically track the error at each MMS step at the trajectory level, which corresponds to the error analysis in Theorem
27
. A solver that resolves the neural proximal subproblem more

quasi-Newton baselines on this

diagnostic, which is distinct

Exact MMS reference for nonlinear regression.

For the quadratic energy

(71)

Equivalently,

u^{n}_{\mathrm{exact}}=(1+\tau)^{-n}u^{0}_{\mathrm{prescribed}}+\Big(1-(1+\tau)^{-n}\Big)f^{\ast}.

(72)

6.3.3
Latent diffusion model on MNIST

We further test the MMS inner solvers in a simple latent diffusion
(
44
)
experiment
on MNIST
(
28
)
, since score matching is itself a nonlinear regression problem: the score network
s_{\theta}
is trained to regress the noise
\varepsilon
from noisy latent codes
(z_{t},t)
, so the score-matching energy
E(\theta)
has the same least-squares structure as the regression objectives considered earlier in this section. The Gauss–Newton framework therefore applies directly, with the Jacobian now taken with respect to
\theta
and the residual vector given by the stacked noise-prediction errors. The purpose of this experiment is not to obtain state-of-the-art
generative performance, but to examine whether the Gauss–Newton-type inner
solver remains competitive with AdamW in a stochastic, higher-dimensional
neural MMS subproblem.

We first train a convolutional variational autoencoder (VAE) to obtain a
low-dimensional latent representation of the MNIST images
(
27
)
. The encoder follows a standard convolutional autoencoder design
(
43
)
: two
stride-two convolutional layers are used to progressively downsample the

28\times 28
image to a
7\times 7
spatial feature map, while increasing
the number of channels from
1
to
w/2
and then to
w
. A third
convolutional layer with stride one further processes the
w
-channel feature
map without changing its spatial resolution. In our experiments
w=64
, so
the encoder has the channel progression

1\longrightarrow 32\longrightarrow 64\longrightarrow 64.

All convolutional layers use SiLU nonlinearities. The final feature map of size

w\times 7\times 7
is then flattened and mapped by two fully connected layers
to the latent mean and log-variance,

\mu_{\phi}(x),\ \log\sigma_{\phi}^{2}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}},\qquad d_{z}=16.

The decoder first maps
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}
back to a
w\times 7\times 7

feature map and then applies two transposed convolutional layers and a final
convolutional layer to produce the image logits. The VAE is trained with the
standard reconstruction–KL objective
(
27
)
:

\mathcal{L}_{\rm VAE}(\phi,\psi)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\Bigl[{\rm BCE}\bigl(D_{\psi}(z_{i}),x_{i}\bigr)+\beta_{\rm VAE}\,D_{\rm KL}\bigl(q_{\phi}(z_{i}|x_{i})\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(0,I)\bigr)\Bigr],

where
z_{i}=\mu_{\phi}(x_{i})+\sigma_{\phi}(x_{i})\xi_{i}
,
\xi_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
,
and we use
\beta_{\rm VAE}=0.1
. After training the VAE, we use the encoder
mean
\mu_{\phi}(x)
as the latent code and normalize the latent variables by
their empirical mean and standard deviation:

\tilde{z}_{0}=(\mu_{\phi}(x)-\bar{z})/s_{z}.

This produces a small-scale latent-diffusion testbed in the spirit of latent
diffusion models
(
44
)
, while keeping the experiment simple
enough for controlled optimizer comparisons.

On this normalized latent space, we use the standard forward diffusion process
of denoising diffusion probabilistic models
(
20
)

z_{t}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\tilde{z}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),

where
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{k=1}^{t}(1-\beta_{k})
, and
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{T}

is a linear noise schedule with
T=1000
. The score network is trained in the
usual noise-prediction form

s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)\approx\varepsilon.

In our implementation,
s_{\theta}
is a residual MLP with sinusoidal time
embedding. More precisely, the time index
t
is first mapped to a sinusoidal
embedding
e(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{t}}
, with
d_{t}=128
. The input

(z_{t},e(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}+d_{t}}
is projected to a hidden representation of
width
64
. We then apply two residual blocks of the form

h\mapsto h+W_{2}\,\sigma\!\left(W_{1}\,{\rm LN}(h)\right),

where
{\rm LN}
denotes layer normalization and
\sigma
is the SiLU
activation. A final layer normalization and linear output layer map the hidden
state back to
\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}}
. The last linear layer is initialized to zero,
so that the initial score prediction is close to zero. For a mini-batch
\{(z_{t}^{i},t_{i},\varepsilon_{i})\}_{i=1}^{B}
, the basic
score-matching energy is

E(\theta)=\frac{1}{2Bd_{z}}\sum_{i=1}^{B}w(t_{i})\left\|s_{\theta}(z_{t}^{i},t_{i})-\varepsilon_{i}\right\|^{2}.

where
w(t)
is a weighting function. The precise choice of
w(t)
is not essential for our optimizer-comparison
purpose. We use a mild decreasing weight
w(t)=3-2t/(T-1)
, which assigns
larger weights to lower-noise denoising tasks while keeping the overall loss
scale comparable across time levels.
The MMS version uses the previous score network
s_{\theta^{n}}
as the proximal
reference and defines the inner objective at outer step
n
by

\mathcal{L}_{\rm MMS}^{n}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\tau Bd_{z}}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\left\|s_{\theta}(z_{t}^{i},t_{i})-s_{\theta^{n}}(z_{t}^{i},t_{i})\right\|^{2}+E(\theta).

We compare AdamW and a damped Gauss–Newton-type method for solving this inner
problem. In the Gauss–Newton update, we use the Levenberg–Marquardt linear system (
79
) with damping
\rho=10^{-3}

shows that GN attains a substantially lower final score-matching energy than AdamW (approximately
2.4\times 10^{-1}
vs.
3.1\times 10^{-1}

In particular, GN reaches its plateau within roughly
20

25

100

5

In summary, across the numerical experiments considered here, solving the MMS subproblems
with the Gauss–Newton-type method generally yields results that are comparable to, and in
most cases better than, those obtained with Adam, AdamW, L-BFGS, Muon, Shampoo, and SOAP. From the viewpoint of computational

subproblem is solved only approximately, using

Thus, although each Gauss–Newton step is more expensive per iteration, the substantially smaller number of inner
iterations can lead to a lower overall CPU time.

7
Conclusions

We have developed a geometric convergence theory for neural network optimization
within the minimizing movement scheme framework.
Our contributions are threefold.

First, we showed that by reformulating each neural MMS step as an increment
optimization problem, the feasible set naturally carries the structure of a
boundaryless smooth embedded Riemannian submanifold of the ambient Hilbert space (using an open parameter ball as the chart domain), together with a compact inner localization image used to control the gradient-flow trajectory.
Under a locally non-degenerate Jacobian condition together with a strict interior-localization condition, we established that the reached sublevel set of the increment objective lies in a strongly geodesically convex normal neighborhood of the origin, and we identified the manifold’s tangent spaces and orthogonal projectors.

Second, we proved that a preconditioned (Gauss–Newton-type) gradient flow in
parameter space induces exactly the Riemannian gradient flow on the increment
manifold. By establishing geodesic strong convexity and smoothness of the
subproblem objective on an appropriate sublevel set, we showed that this
Riemannian gradient flow converges exponentially to the unique subproblem minimizer.

Third, we quantified how finite-time inexactness and neural approximation error
propagate through MMS iterations, proving that the inexact neural iterates
track the exact MMS iterates uniformly and converge to an
O(\delta)
-neighborhood
of the global minimizer when the energy functional is strongly convex.

Several limitations of the current framework suggest directions for future work.
The analysis assumes that the objective functional depends on the function values
in the
L^{2}
-sense; extending to functionals involving spatial derivatives (e.g.,

H^{1}
-norms arising in PDE problems) would require controlling the Jacobian
Lipschitz constant in stronger norms, where it may scale unfavorably with spatial
discretization.
The locality assumption (Assumption
1
), while shown to hold
for sufficiently small step sizes, may be restrictive for large-step or adaptive
methods in practice.
Finally, the convergence theory is formulated for the continuous-time Riemannian
gradient flow; a rigorous analysis of the discretized Gauss–Newton updates
(including Levenberg–Marquardt regularization and inexact linear solves) remains
an important open problem.

A.7
Riemannian connection and Hessian on Hilbert-embedded manifolds

The proofs of Theorems

and
15
use the following standard decomposition of the Riemannian Hessian for an embedded submanifold of a flat ambient Hilbert space. The result is classical (see, e.g.,
1
,
7
); we record it here for completeness.

Lemma 37

(Riemannian connection and Hessian on Hilbert-embedded manifolds)
.

Let
(\mathcal{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})
be a real Hilbert space endowed with its flat metric. Let
u_{\mathrm{NN}}
be a neural network with
L
-Lipschitz Jacobian and
\lambda_{\tilde{w}}
-nondegenerate Jacobian at
\tilde{w}
, so that
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
as defined in (
16
) is an embedded Riemannian submanifold of
\mathcal{H}
(Proposition
5
). Denote by
\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}
the flat (ambient) connection on
\mathcal{H}
and by
\nabla^{\mathcal{M}}
the induced Levi–Civita connection on
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
. Then:

1.

Flat connection on
\mathcal{H}
.

For any smooth vector field
Y:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and any
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
,

\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}_{\xi_{u}}Y=\mathrm{D}Y(u)[\xi],

that is, the Levi–Civita connection on
\mathcal{H}
is simply the Fréchet differential of
Y
.

2.

Induced connection on
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
.

For smooth vector fields
X,Y
tangent to
\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
, the induced connection is

\nabla^{\mathcal{M}}_{X_{h}}Y=\mathbf{P}_{h}(\mathrm{D}Y(h)[X]),

(88)

where
h\in\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
.

3.

Riemannian Hessian.

For the smooth function
g(\cdot;v):\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))\to\mathbb{R}
defined in (
6
), its Riemannian Hessian at
h\in\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
applied to
\xi_{h}\in T_{h}\mathcal{M}(u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\tilde{w}))
is

\operatorname{Hess}g(h;v)[\xi_{h}]=\mathbf{P}_{h}\left(\mathrm{D}\operatorname{grad}g(h;v)[\xi_{h}]\right)=\mathbf{P}_{h}\left(\nabla^{2}g(h;v)[\xi_{h}]+\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{t=0}\mathbf{P}_{h(t)}\left(\nabla g(h;v)\right)\right),

(89)

where
h(0)=h
and
h^{\prime}(0)=\xi_{h}
.
Consequently, the quadratic form of the Riemannian Hessian satisfies

\langle\operatorname{Hess}g(h;v)[\xi_{h}],\xi_{h}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle\nabla^{2}g(h;v)[\xi_{h}],\xi_{h}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\langle\mathrm{D}\mathbf{P}_{(h)}[\xi_{h}]\nabla g(h;v),\xi_{h}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(90)

Proof

These are standard results; see, e.g.,
1
. We outline the key steps. (i) Since the metric on
\mathcal{H}
is constant, the Christoffel symbols vanish, so
\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}
coincides with the Fréchet derivative. (ii) For an embedded Riemannian submanifold, projecting the ambient connection onto the tangent space yields (
88
). (iii) Differentiating
\operatorname{grad}g(h;v)=\mathbf{P}_{h}\nabla g(h;v)
and projecting again gives (
89
)–(
90
).

Appendix B
Convergence of the Minimizing Movement Scheme in Hilbert Space

For completeness, we include the standard result on the convergence of the minimizing movement scheme in Hilbert space
(
5
;
36
)
.

Theorem 38
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space, and let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuously Fréchet differentiable, coercive, and
m_{\mathcal{F}}
-strongly convex with
m_{\mathcal{F}}>0
. Assume moreover that, for every
E\in\mathbb{R}
, the sublevel set

S_{E}^{\mathcal{F}}:=\{u\in\mathcal{H}:\mathcal{F}[u]\leq E\}

is compact in
\mathcal{H}
. Given
u^{0}\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{F})
and
\tau>0
, define the minimizing movement scheme by

u^{n+1}=J_{\tau}(u^{n}),\qquad J_{\tau}(x):=\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{F}}(x):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\frac{1}{2\tau}\|u-x\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\mathcal{F}[u]\right\}.

(112)

If
1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}>0
, then the minimization problem in (
112
) admits a unique solution, making
J_{\tau}
well defined and single-valued.

Let
\hat{u}_{\tau}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{H}
denote the piecewise linear interpolation associated with the sequence
\{u^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
. As
\tau\downarrow 0
, the interpolants
\hat{u}_{\tau}
converge in
\mathcal{H}
, uniformly on
[0,T]
, to the unique solution

u\in W^{1,2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})

of the gradient flow equation
u_{t}=-\nabla\mathcal{F}[u]
associated with
\mathcal{F}
and the initial datum
u^{0}
. Moreover, for any two gradient flow solutions
u_{0},u_{1}
, one has the
m_{\mathcal{F}}
-contractivity estimate

\|u_{1}(t)-u_{0}(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq e^{-m_{\mathcal{F}}(t-s)}\|u_{1}(s)-u_{0}(s)\|_{\mathcal{H}},\qquad 0\leq s<t.

Proof

Step 1: Well-posedness and Energy Estimate.
The objective function in (
112
) is given by
\Phi(u)=\frac{1}{2\tau}\|u-u^{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\mathcal{F}[u]
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is continuously Fréchet differentiable, coercive, and has compact sublevel sets,
\Phi(u)
is coercive and bounded from below, guaranteeing the existence of a minimizer. Furthermore, because
\mathcal{F}
is
m_{\mathcal{F}}
-convex,
\Phi(u)
is strictly convex provided
1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}>0
. Thus,
u^{n+1}
exists and is unique. Moreover,
\Phi(u^{n+1})\leq\Phi(u^{n})
indicates

\frac{1}{2\tau}\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|^{2}\leq\mathcal{F}[u^{n}]-\mathcal{F}[u^{n+1}]

(113)

Summing this inequality over
n=0,\dots,N-1
, we have

\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|^{2}\leq 2\tau(\mathcal{F}(u^{0})-\mathcal{F}(u^{*}))\leq\tau C,

(114)

where
u^{*}
is the global minimizer of
\mathcal{F}
. Hence,

\|u^{n+1}-u^{n}\|^{2}\rightarrow 0,\quad n\rightarrow\infty

(115)

Step 2: Interpolations and Compactness.
Define the piecewise affine interpolation
\hat{u}_{\tau}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{H}
and the piecewise constant interpolation
\bar{u}_{\tau}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{H}
by

\hat{u}_{\tau}((n+\theta)\tau)=(1-\theta)u^{n}+\theta u^{n+1},\qquad\theta\in[0,1],\quad n=0,\dots,N-1,

and

\bar{u}_{\tau}(t)=u^{n+1},\qquad t\in(n\tau,(n+1)\tau],\quad n=0,\dots,N-1.

Then, (
114
) indicates that

\int_{0}^{T}\|\dot{\hat{u}}_{\tau}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tau\left\|\frac{u^{n+1}-u^{n}}{\tau}\right\|^{2}\leq 2(\mathcal{F}[u^{0}]-\mathcal{F}[u^{*}])

(116)

Hence,
\hat{u}_{\tau}
is bounded in
W^{1,2}([0,T],\mathcal{H})
and we can extract a subsequence (not relabeled) such that

\hat{u}_{\tau}\rightharpoonup u~\text{in}~L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H}),\quad\dot{\hat{u}}_{\tau}\rightharpoonup\dot{u}~\text{in}~L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})

(117)

for a limit
u\in W^{1,2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})
.

Moreover, for any
0\leq s<t\leq T
, since
\hat{u}_{\tau}
is absolutely continuous on
[0,T]
, we have

\hat{u}_{\tau}(t)-\hat{u}_{\tau}(s)=\int_{s}^{t}\dot{\hat{u}}_{\tau}(r)\,dr.

Therefore, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

\|\hat{u}_{\tau}(t)-\hat{u}_{\tau}(s)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\int_{s}^{t}\|\dot{\hat{u}}_{\tau}(r)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\,dr\leq|t-s|^{1/2}\|\dot{\hat{u}}_{\tau}\|_{L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})}\leq|t-s|^{1/2}\bigl(2(\mathcal{F}[u^{0}]-\mathcal{F}[u^{*}])\bigr)^{1/2}

Hence
\{\hat{u}_{\tau}\}_{\tau}
is uniformly Hölder continuous on every
[0,T]
. Since
\hat{u}_{\tau}
lie in the compact sublevel set
S_{\mathcal{F}(u^{0})}^{\mathcal{F}}
, by the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, by extracting a further subsequence (not relabeled), we have the uniform convergence

\hat{u}_{\tau}\rightarrow u~\text{in}~C^{0}([0,T];\mathcal{H})

(118)

Notice
\hat{u}(n\tau)=\bar{u}(n\tau)
, and
\bar{u}_{\tau}
is piecewise constant, we have

\|\hat{u}_{\tau}-\bar{u}_{\tau}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T];\mathcal{H})}\leq\sqrt{\tau}\bigl(2(\mathcal{F}[u^{0}]-\mathcal{F}[u^{*}])\bigr)^{1/2}\rightarrow 0,\quad\tau\rightarrow 0^{+}.

(119)

Hence, for the piecewise constant interpolation, we have

\bar{u}_{\tau}\rightarrow u~\text{in}~L^{\infty}([0,T];\mathcal{H})

(120)

Step 3: Convergence to the Gradient Flow.
Next, we show that the limit
u
satisfies the gradient flow equation

u_{t}=-\nabla\mathcal{F}(u)

(121)

Since
\mathcal{F}
is Fréchet continuously differentiable, the Euler–Lagrange equation for the minimizing movement scheme reads

\frac{u^{n+1}-u^{n}}{\tau}=-\nabla\mathcal{F}[u^{n+1}],\qquad n=0,1,\dots,N-1.

Recalling the piecewise affine interpolation
\hat{u}_{\tau}
and the piecewise constant interpolation
\bar{u}_{\tau}
, we have

\dot{\hat{u}}_{\tau}(t)=-\nabla\mathcal{F}[\bar{u}_{\tau}(t)]\qquad\text{for a.e. }t\in(0,T).

(122)

We have proved that up to a subsequence,

\hat{u}_{\tau}\to u\qquad\text{in }C^{0}([0,T];\mathcal{H}),\dot{\hat{u}}_{\tau}\rightharpoonup\dot{u}\qquad\text{weakly in }L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H}),\bar{u}_{\tau}\to u\qquad\text{in }L^{\infty}([0,T];\mathcal{H}).

Since the trajectories
\bar{u}_{\tau}(t)
remain in the compact sublevel set

S_{\mathcal{F}[u^{0}]}^{\mathcal{F}}

and since
\nabla\mathcal{F}
is continuous on bounded subsets of
\mathcal{H}
, it follows that

\nabla\mathcal{F}[\bar{u}_{\tau}]\to\nabla\mathcal{F}[u]\qquad\text{in }L^{\infty}([0,T];\mathcal{H}),

and therefore also in
L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})
. We now pass to the limit in (
122
). For any
\phi\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})
,

\int_{0}^{T}\langle\dot{\hat{u}}_{\tau}(t),\phi(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,dt=-\int_{0}^{T}\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}[\bar{u}_{\tau}(t)],\phi(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,dt.

Letting
\tau\to 0
, the weak convergence of
\dot{\hat{u}}_{\tau}
and the strong convergence of
\nabla\mathcal{F}[\bar{u}_{\tau}]
yield

\int_{0}^{T}\langle\dot{u}(t),\phi(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,dt=-\int_{0}^{T}\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}[u(t)],\phi(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,dt.

Since
\phi
is arbitrary, we conclude that

\dot{u}=-\nabla\mathcal{F}[u]\qquad\text{in }L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H}).

That is,
u
satisfies the gradient flow equation on
[0,T]
. Finally, since
\hat{u}_{\tau}(0)=u^{0}
for all
\tau
and
\hat{u}_{\tau}\to u
in
C([0,T];\mathcal{H})
, we also have

u(0)=u^{0}.

Therefore
u
is a solution of the gradient flow equation with initial datum
u^{0}
.

Step 4: Contractivity and Uniqueness.
Since
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is
m_{\mathcal{F}}
-convex, i.e.,

\mathcal{F}[v]\geq\mathcal{F}[u]+\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}[u],\,v-u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\frac{m_{\mathcal{F}}}{2}\|v-u\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathcal{H}.

Then the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{F}
is
m_{\mathcal{F}}
-monotone:

\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}[u]-\nabla\mathcal{F}[v],\,u-v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq m_{\mathcal{F}}\|u-v\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathcal{H}.

(123)

Let
u_{0},u_{1}\in W^{1,2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})
be two solutions of the gradient flow equation

\dot{u}_{j}(t)=-\nabla\mathcal{F}[u_{j}(t)],\qquad j=0,1.

Since
u_{0},u_{1}\in W^{1,2}([0,T];\mathcal{H})
, using the gradient flow equation and (
123
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|u_{1}(t)-u_{0}(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}[u_{1}(t)]-\nabla\mathcal{F}[u_{0}(t)],\,u_{1}(t)-u_{0}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\leq-\,m_{\mathcal{F}}\|u_{1}(t)-u_{0}(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

By Grönwall’s inequality, for all
0\leq s<t\leq T
,

\|u_{1}(t)-u_{0}(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq e^{-m_{\mathcal{F}}(t-s)}\|u_{1}(s)-u_{0}(s)\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

In particular, if
u_{1}(0)=u_{0}(0)=u^{0}
, then
u_{1}\equiv u_{0}
on
[0,T]
. Hence the gradient flow solution with initial datum
u^{0}
is unique.

The previous theorem establishes that the minimizing movement scheme converges, as
\tau\downarrow 0
, to the solution of the gradient flow of
\mathcal{F}
on every finite time interval. To relate this evolution to the minimization problem for
\mathcal{F}
, one further needs the long-time behavior of the gradient flow. Under the assumption
m_{\mathcal{F}}>0
, the latter converges exponentially fast to the unique global minimizer
u^{\ast}
of
\mathcal{F}
.

Remark 39
.

The contractivity estimate above is the continuous-time counterpart of the
contraction property of the proximal map associated with the minimizing movement
scheme. Indeed, let

J_{\tau}(x):=\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\frac{1}{2\tau}\|u-x\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\mathcal{F}[u]\right\}.

If
u=J_{\tau}(x)
and
v=J_{\tau}(y)
, then the optimality conditions give

\frac{u-x}{\tau}+\nabla\mathcal{F}[u]=0,\qquad\frac{v-y}{\tau}+\nabla\mathcal{F}[v]=0.

Subtracting the two identities and testing with
u-v
, we obtain

\frac{1}{\tau}\|u-v\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\left\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}[u]-\nabla\mathcal{F}[v],u-v\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\frac{1}{\tau}\langle x-y,u-v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

Using the
m_{\mathcal{F}}
-monotonicity of
\nabla\mathcal{F}
, it follows that

\left(\frac{1}{\tau}+m_{\mathcal{F}}\right)\|u-v\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\tau}\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|u-v\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Therefore,

\|J_{\tau}(x)-J_{\tau}(y)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\frac{1}{1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}}\|x-y\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Thus the continuous gradient flow is
e^{-m_{\mathcal{F}}t}
-contractive,
whereas one step of the minimizing movement scheme is

(1+\tau m_{\mathcal{F}})^{-1}
-contractive. This discrete contraction is the
stability mechanism used in the error-propagation analysis for the neural MMS
iterations.

Theorem 40

(Exponential convergence to the minimizer)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space, and let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
be Fréchet differentiable and
m_{\mathcal{F}}
-convex with
m_{\mathcal{F}}>0
, i.e.,

\mathcal{F}[v]\geq\mathcal{F}[u]+\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}[u],\,v-u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\frac{m_{\mathcal{F}}}{2}\|v-u\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathcal{H}.

Assume that
\mathcal{F}
admits a global minimizer
u^{\ast}\in\mathcal{H}
. Then
u^{\ast}
is unique. Moreover, for every initial datum
u^{0}\in\mathcal{H}
, the corresponding gradient flow solution

\dot{u}(t)=-\nabla\mathcal{F}[u(t)],\qquad u(0)=u^{0},

satisfies

\|u(t)-u^{\ast}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq e^{-m_{\mathcal{F}}t}\|u^{0}-u^{\ast}\|_{\mathcal{H}},\qquad t\geq 0.

In particular,

u(t)\to u^{\ast}\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{H}\quad\text{as }t\to\infty.

Proof

Since
u^{\ast}
is a global minimizer and
\mathcal{F}
is Fréchet differentiable, we have

\nabla\mathcal{F}[u^{\ast}]=0,

so
u^{\ast}
is a stationary solution of the gradient flow equation. By the
m_{\mathcal{F}}
-monotonicity of
\nabla\mathcal{F}
,

\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}[u]-\nabla\mathcal{F}[v],\,u-v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq m_{\mathcal{F}}\|u-v\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathcal{H}.

As proved in Theorem (
38
), we have

\|u(t)-u^{\ast}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq e^{-m_{\mathcal{F}}t}\|u^{0}-u^{\ast}\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

This proves the claim.

In conclusion, combining the previous two theorems, the discrete iterate
u^{N}
first approximates the gradient flow solution at time
T=N\tau
, and for large times this state in turn approaches
u^{\ast}
. In this sense,
u^{N}
provides an approximation of the global minimizer. Formally, the total approximation may be viewed as consisting of two parts: a discretization error between
u^{N}
and the gradient flow state
u(N\tau)
, and a long-time relaxation error between
u(N\tau)
and the minimizer
u^{\ast}
.

(II)

A step size

Theorem 41

Fix step

It suffices to show

C.2
Jacobian non-degeneracy (Definition
3
)

The non-degeneracy condition
\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)^{*}\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w)\succeq 4\lambda^{2}I_{p}
requires the
Gram matrix of the partial derivatives
\partial_{w_{j}}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\cdot;w)
(with respect to the

\mathcal{H}
-inner product) to be uniformly positive definite. We discuss when this holds and
when it can fail.

When it holds.

The non-degeneracy is essentially a generic property in the underparameterized regime
(
p\leq N
), provided the architecture is rich enough and the data
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are in
“general position.” Concretely:

•

For two-layer tanh or sigmoid networks with
p\leq N
and generic random initializations,
the empirical Gram matrix
J(w)^{\top}J(w)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is almost surely positive definite.

•

For deep fully-connected networks with smooth activations, results from

16
and subsequent works show that with high probability over random
initialization,
\sigma_{\min}(J(w^{0}))
is bounded away from zero by an architecture-dependent
constant when the width is sufficient relative to the input dimension and sample size.

•

Empirically, our experiments (Figure
7
) confirm that

s_{\min}(J(w))
stays at the
O(10^{-2})
level along the GN trajectory in
underparameterized regimes.

When it fails or weakens.

The non-degeneracy can fail or weaken in several practically important scenarios:

•

Overparameterization
(
p\gg N
): the Gram matrix

J(w)^{\top}J(w)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
has rank at most
N
, so it is rank-deficient and
cannot satisfy the strict bound
\succeq 4\lambda^{2}I_{p}
. In this regime, our theory
applies after a damping fix: replacing
J^{\top}J
with
J^{\top}J+\rho I
for some
\rho>0

(Levenberg–Marquardt-type regularization), or working with the dual Gram matrix

JJ^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
which is full-rank (cf.
9
).
The numerical experiments in Section
6
demonstrate that the damped
GN flow continues to perform well in this regime.

•

Symmetries and parameter redundancy
: networks with permutation symmetries of
hidden units have automatically a degenerate Hessian along symmetry directions. The
non-degeneracy condition is then satisfied not on the full
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, but on a quotient by
the symmetry group; our local analysis tolerates this since it only requires non-degeneracy
in a neighborhood of the current iterate (after fixing a representative).

•

Activation saturation
: at large weights, common activations such as
\tanh

and sigmoid saturate, causing
\partial_{w_{j}}u_{\mathrm{NN}}
to become small in magnitude.
The non-degeneracy constant
\lambda_{w}
is then small, making the manifold “thin”
and the convergence radius
r_{w}
shrink.

Localized version.

Theorem
33
only requires non-degeneracy at the initial point
w^{0}
,
together with a small-initial-gap condition that ensures the trajectory does not exhaust
the non-degeneracy budget. This is significantly weaker than requiring non-degeneracy along
the entire trajectory and aligns with the practical observation that “warm-started”
optimization succeeds while cold-started runs in pathological regions can fail.

C.3
Lipschitz Jacobian (Definition
2
)

Definition
2
packages two requirements:
C^{2}
regularity of
u_{\mathrm{NN}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and an
L
-Lipschitz bound on its first Fréchet derivative,

\|\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{1})-\mathrm{D}u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{2})\|_{\rm op}\leq L\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{2}
. The
C^{2}
assumption is needed for the Riemannian Hessian of
g(\cdot\,;v)
in Section
3
and for the exponential-map analysis underlying the discrete RGD theorem (Theorem
16
) to be pointwise well defined; the Lipschitz bound then quantifies the modulus of continuity of the Jacobian. Whether
L
is finite (and how large it is) depends critically on the choice of ambient norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
.

When
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\Omega)
.

For a fully-connected network
u_{\mathrm{NN}}(\cdot;w):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
with
C^{2}
activation

\sigma
and bounded weights, both
u_{\mathrm{NN}}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{p};L^{2}(\Omega))
and
L<\infty
hold; the
Lipschitz constant depends polynomially on the depth, width, and the second derivative bound

\sup|\sigma^{\prime\prime}|
. The typical smooth activations

\tanh
, sigmoid, softplus, GELU, and Swish all satisfy this. ReLU networks are excluded:
\sigma
is not
C^{2}
at the kink, so Definition
2
fails. They can be brought back into scope by smoothing the activation (e.g.,
\mathrm{ReLU}_{\varepsilon}
, GELU as a
C^{\infty}
approximant) or by working with the generalized Jacobian in a non-smooth variant of the theory, which we do not pursue here.

When
\mathcal{H}=H^{1}(\Omega)
.

The Lipschitz constant depends on the second derivatives of the network with respect to
the spatial variable
x\in\Omega
, in addition to the parameter
w
. For PDE-motivated
problems where the Sobolev
H^{1}
-norm is the natural ambient norm, the Lipschitz constant
can be very large. In a discrete setting,
L
can scale as
O(dx^{-2})
where
dx
is the
spatial mesh size, making the assumption effectively unusable for high-resolution PDEs.
This limitation is discussed in Remark in Section
2
, and motivates restricting
the present theory to
L^{2}
-type cost functionals.

C.4
Smoothness and strong convexity of
\mathcal{F}
(Lemma
12
)

The convergence theory requires
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
to be continuously Fréchet differentiable,

L_{\mathcal{F}}
-smooth, and
m_{\mathcal{F}}
-strongly convex.

When the assumptions hold.

Many natural cost functionals in scientific computing and machine learning satisfy these
conditions on suitable function spaces:

•

Linear regression / least squares.
If

\mathcal{F}[u]=\frac{1}{2}\|u-f^{*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}
for a target
f^{*}\in L^{2}(\mu)
, then
\mathcal{F}
is

1
-strongly convex and
1
-smooth on
L^{2}(\mu)
.

•

Quadratic energies of elliptic PDEs.

\mathcal{F}[u]=\frac{1}{2}a(u,u)-\langle f,u\rangle
where
a
is a coercive symmetric bilinear form
yields a strongly convex quadratic functional, with
m_{\mathcal{F}}
and
L_{\mathcal{F}}
given by the
coercivity and continuity constants of
a
.

•

Strongly convex perturbations.
Adding a Tikhonov regularizer
\frac{\lambda}{2}\|u\|^{2}

to a convex but non-strongly-convex functional makes it
\lambda
-strongly convex.

When they fail.

The strong convexity assumption fails for many problems of interest:

•

Cross-entropy losses (logistic / softmax classification) are convex but not strongly
convex on the entire space; strong convexity holds only on bounded sublevel sets.

•

Variational forms of nonlinear PDEs (e.g.,
p
-Laplacian, mean curvature flow) are
typically convex but not uniformly strongly convex.

Extending the present theory to the merely-convex setting (
m_{\mathcal{F}}=0
), where the proximal map is non-expansive but not contractive, is an interesting direction for future work.

(eq. (

Remark 44

(Satisfiability of the data condition)
.

The hypothesis (
27
) is a condition on the data
(v,\tau,\mathcal{F},L,\lambda_{\tilde{w}})
, equivalent to

\max\!\bigl\{\,\|h^{*}(v)\|_{\mathcal{H}},\ K(v)\,\bigr\}\;\leq\;\frac{\lambda_{\tilde{w}}^{2}}{4L\kappa},

which requires the Jacobian non-degeneracy
\lambda_{\tilde{w}}
to dominate both the proximal increment
h^{*}(v)=J_{\tau}(v)-v
and the sublevel-set radius
K(v)
. Three regimes in which the condition is automatic:

(i)

Near a minimizer.
As
v\to u^{*}
,
\nabla\mathcal{F}[v]\to 0
and the first-order condition
h^{*}/\tau+\nabla\mathcal{F}[v+h^{*}]=0
forces
h^{*}(v)\to 0
. If
\mathcal{F}\geq 0
is normalized so that
\mathcal{F}[u^{*}]=0
, then
K(v)\to 0
as well, and
C(v)\to 0
.

(ii)

Small-
\tau
regime.
For fixed
v
,
\|h^{*}(v)\|=O(\tau)
(from
h^{*}=-\tau\nabla\mathcal{F}[v+h^{*}]
) and
K(v)=O(\sqrt{\tau})
, so
C(v)\to 0
as
\tau\downarrow 0
. The data condition is therefore automatic for sufficiently small step size.

(iii)

Sufficient non-degeneracy.
For fixed
\tau
and
v
, the condition holds whenever
\lambda_{\tilde{w}}\geq 2\sqrt{L\kappa\,\max\{\|h^{*}(v)\|,K(v)\}}
, i.e., when the local Jacobian is well-conditioned relative to the proximal data.

C.7
Neural proximal approximation precision (Assumption
21
)

Remark 46

(Interpretation of the approximation precision)
.

The scalar
\varepsilon
represents the one-step accuracy of the computed neural proximal map relative to the exact proximal map. It packages the two non-optimization errors in the four-way decomposition (
11
): the trial-space error from restricting the proximal problem to the neural class
\mathcal{N}
, and the sampling or quadrature error from replacing population quantities by empirical approximations. Introducing the ideal population neural proximal map

\bar{J}_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}(x):=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{u\in\mathcal{N}}\bigl\{\tfrac{1}{2\tau}\|u-x\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\mathcal{F}[u]\bigr\},

which uses the exact functional
\mathcal{F}
but restricts the minimization to
\mathcal{N}
, the triangle inequality gives

\|J_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}(x)-J_{\tau}(x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}\;\leq\;\underbrace{\|\bar{J}_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}(x)-J_{\tau}(x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{trial-space error}}\;+\;\underbrace{\|J_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}(x)-\bar{J}_{\tau,\mathrm{NN}}(x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}}_{\text{sampling/quadrature error}}.

Assumption
21
packages these two contributions through the single scalar
\varepsilon
: universal approximation theory
(
21
)
bounds the trial-space error as the architecture is enriched, while Monte Carlo or quadrature estimates bound the sampling error as the number of samples increases. Condition (
40
) is equivalent to
\delta>(1+1/(\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}))\varepsilon
, so the global error bound obtained by the stability argument can only resolve the exact MMS trajectory up to a neighborhood whose size is proportional to
\varepsilon
.

,

Verifiability of (U1)–(U2).

Under the initial-gap condition of Theorem

Verifiability of (U3): global inverse-stability.

For (U3), the cleanest sufficient condition is
global inverse-stability
of the network map: if there exists

\|u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{1})-u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w_{2})\|_{\mathcal{H}}\;\geq\;\sigma\,\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{2},\qquad\forall\,w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},

(129)

then
\rho_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}\geq\sigma\,r_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}\geq\sigma\,r_{\infty}

on which

is injective. See Remark

C.9
Initial gap and warm start (Theorem
33
(iii))

The initial gap condition (
54
) of Theorem
33
,

\Big(1+\frac{1}{\tau m_{\mathcal{F}}}\Big)\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|_{\mathcal{H}}+2\delta<\frac{2\lambda_{w^{0}}\,\lambda_{\infty}}{L},

formalizes the requirement that the initial parameter
w^{0}
should produce a network output

u_{\mathrm{NN}}(w^{0})
already close enough to the target
u^{*}
. In practice this can be ensured by:

•

Pretraining / warm starting.
Initializing from a previously trained model on
a related task or from a coarser discretization.

•

Multi-grid / continuation.
Solving a sequence of problems at increasing
resolution, using the solution from the previous level as the warm start.

•

Sufficient over-parameterization.
For wide enough networks at standard
random initializations,
\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
is provably small with high probability under
mild assumptions on
u^{*}

(
23
;
16
)
.

The condition is also informative in the negative direction: if
\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
is too
large relative to
\lambda_{w^{0}}/L
, the parameter trajectory may exhaust the non-degeneracy
budget before convergence is achieved. This is consistent with the empirical wisdom that
“cold start” optimization on highly nonconvex landscapes can fail catastrophically,
while warm-started training succeeds reliably.

Remark 50

(Interpretation of the initial-gap condition)
.

The condition (
54
) requires the initial function-space gap
\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
to be sufficiently small relative to
\lambda_{w^{0}}/L
. Geometrically, it enforces two constraints: (i) the total parameter trajectory length must not exceed the non-degeneracy budget
2\lambda_{w^{0}}/L
, and (ii) each individual per-step displacement must fit within the uniform local non-degeneracy ball of radius
r_{\infty}=\inf_{n}r_{w^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}}
. The choice of
\lambda_{\infty}
is a free parameter: smaller
\lambda_{\infty}
relaxes (i) but tightens (ii) (since
r_{\infty}
depends on
\lambda_{\infty}
via (
14
)), and the optimal choice balances the two.

Remark 51

(Validity regimes for the parameter-space conclusions)
.

The proof of Theorem
33
(b)–(d) is stated in the
perfect-approximation regime
(
\varepsilon=0
) of Corollary
26
(I), in which the summable tracking error (
46
) yields a finite trajectory-length bound and hence Cauchy convergence in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. In the
uniform-approximation regime
(
\varepsilon>0
) of Corollary
26
(II):

•

Function-space convergence (a)
continues to hold under (
45
):
\|u^{n}_{\mathrm{NN},T}-u^{*}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\delta+\rho^{n}\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
, with
\delta
understood as the asymptotic-floor accuracy.

•

Trajectory length (b), Cauchy property (c), and parameter-space neighborhood convergence (d)
hold only on the finite horizon
N^{*}
on which the partial sum (
47
) is bounded by the right-hand side of (
54
); explicitly,
N^{*}
scales as
1/\varepsilon
. Beyond
N^{*}
, the iterates may drift through the parameter space at a rate
O(\varepsilon)
per step (a “noise floor” inherited from the NN approximation), and are only
approximately Cauchy
up to an
O(\varepsilon)
residual.

Closing this gap rigorously in the uniform-approximation regime requires either
\varepsilon_{n}
to decay or a separate argument showing that the parameter-space dynamics stay confined despite the persistent floor; both are beyond the scope of the present revision.

Remark 52

(Relation to “warm start” practice)
.

Hypothesis (iii) of Theorem
33
formalizes a familiar empirical observation: nonconvex training succeeds reliably when starting close to a good basin. Our theory makes this precise: as long as the initial function-space gap is within the non-degeneracy budget set by
\lambda_{w^{0}}
, the entire trajectory remains in the favorable region, and convergence to an
O(\delta)
-neighborhood of
u^{*}
is guaranteed.
