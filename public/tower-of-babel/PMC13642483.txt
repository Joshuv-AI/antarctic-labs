A Deterministic Spectral‐Design Method for Guided‐Mode Resonances
Authors: Joong Hyun Pyo, Yu Sung Choi, Robert Magnusson, Jae Woong Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTResonance frequency and linewidth are key metrics for guided‐mode resonance (GMR) devices and their independent control is important for practical applications. Trial–solution‐based approaches have been used to obtain appropriate element designs with desired key metrics. In this paper, we propose a dual‐ridge compound unit‐cell structure for fully independent key‐metric control in a deterministic manner. We derive a concise design‐parametric search space that analytically relates the key GMR metrics with the unit‐cell geometry without undesirable complications which are inherent in the previous approaches. On this basis, we demonstrate completely independent control on the multiple key GMR metrics. In addition, our proposed approach describes how non‐Hermitian and topological features such as Fermi arcs and exceptional points emerge in GMR spectra.

Introduction

Guided mode resonances (GMR) produce versatile spectral features for optical filters, sensors, lasers, and many others [1, 2, 3, 4]. In particular, GMRs around the second‐order band gap condition are of interest because they produce the strongest spectral expressions associated with total reflection or transmission peaks [5, 6, 7, 8, 9], symmetry‐protected bound states in the continuum (BIC) [10, 11], and topological edge states near normal‐incidence condition [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Key metrics for GMR properties in this spectral region are band gap center frequency ω c, band gap width ω g, and resonance linewidth Δω. Optimizing a GMR element design reduces to a multi‐dimensional parametric search problem finding unit‐cell designs for desired metrics {ω c, ω g, Δω}.

GMR elements based on a single‐ridge unit cell have been widely used for its structural simplicity and subsequent advantages in design and experiments. In this case, a major design parameter is relative ridge width to the grating period, which is often referred to as fill factor F. F simultaneously controls key metrics ω c, ω g, and Δω in general. Independent control of these metrics is important in many applications in practice and it demands additional tuning parameters such as ridge height h and layer thicknesses t n because the number of design‐parametric degrees of freedom should be fundamentally larger than the number of desired metrics. Nevertheless, such parametric tuning often fails to deliver an optimal design because respective impacts of F, h, and t n on ω g and Δω are nontrivial in general.

Toward this end, compound unit‐cell designs including multiple grating ridges may provide an efficient approach to the independent control as multiple fill factors and relative positions of ridges within the unit cell are available for additional design‐parametric degrees of freedom for directly controlling diffractive‐coupling amplitudes prescribed for the target metrics. They have been exploited as an efficient structure for spectral filters [17] and quasi‐bound states in the continuum (quasi‐BICs) [18, 19]. In those works, high‐Q resonances are obtained by breaking the mirror symmetry of a unit cell and subsequent modification of radiation properties of band‐edge states. Although delicate linewidth control methods were provided on such bases, a comprehensive systematic approach for independent resonance metric tuning has not been established, in spite of its immediate impact on GMR element development.

In this paper, we provide a comprehensive treatment of dual‐ridge compound unit‐cell structures and demonstrate how one can obtain completely independent control on the key GMR metrics. We utilize a coupled‐mode model for GMRs in our cases of interest. The model yields closed‐form expressions for ω g and Δω in terms of fill factors and relative positions of two grating ridges. These expressions reveal a deterministic method of independently controlling ω g and Δω. We perform rigorous numerical analysis and it quantitatively confirms the feasibility of our proposed approach.

Moreover, change in F alters average dielectric constant ε 0 = Fε high + (1 − F)ε low which determines guided‐mode wave function and pertaining quantities including its propagation constant, Bragg‐reflection frequency ω B, group speed v, and diffraction rate coefficients κ 1 and κ 2 as well as further simultaneous changes in m ± and δ. These concurrent changes give rise to unavoidable complications in the structure optimization for desired key metrics.

Such complications can be avoided in dual‐ridge compound unit–cell structures in Figure 1a. The two ridges are characterized by their center‐to‐center spacing factor S and individual fill factors F 1 and F 2. These parameters form three‐dimensional (3D) search space {F 1, F 2, S} and thereby provide the enough number of degrees of design freedom for potentially independent control of ω g and Δω ±. In this 3D search space, we can configure a plausible subspace that avoids the complication arising from strong fill‐factor dependence of ε 0 for the single‐ridge unit–cell structures. Such subspace is obtained by restricting net fill factor F = F 1 + F 2 to be constant. Any changes in F 1, F 2, and S within the constant‐F subspace do not alter average dielectric constant ε 0, minimizing undesirable modifications of interaction coefficients associated with it. In particular, the subspace keeps ω B, v, κ 1, and κ 2 being constant within the CMT model. Considering ω B >> |δ| for sufficiently strong GMRs in general, we can reasonably assume that the band‐gap‐center frequency ω c in Equation (4) is practically constant over this parametric subspace. Subsequently, we focus on the independent control of ω g and Δω ± hereafter.

Making convenient use of the constant‐F subspace, we define the fill‐factor difference χ = F 1 − F 2 that sets fill factors by F 1 = (F−χ)/2 and F 2 = (F+χ)/2. Then, parametric subspace {χ, S} within range {[−F, +F], [F/2, 1 − F/2]} is our geometrically accessible constant‐F search space which we will use hereafter. Note that this parametric search space is a plane with its center at (χ, S) = (0, 1/2) and spans of (Δχ, ΔS) = (2F, 1 − F). It is worth noting in this search space that a grating design reduces to a single‐ridge unit–cell structure at special locations. The grating design at the center (0, 1/2) is a half‐period (Λ → Λ/2) single‐ridge unit‐cell structure because of the mirror symmetry. On the outer boundaries at χ = ±F or S = [1 ± (1 − F)]/2, the design structure becomes a right‐period (Λ → Λ) single‐ridge unit–cell structure because the two ridges merge into a single ridge. In addition, the grating design is mirror‐symmetric with respect to y–z plane along two vertical lines at χ = 0 and S = 1/2.

This agreement of the analytic model for the practically important SiN‐SiO2 material platform is one of the key contributions of our approach because the required parametric scans can be obtained without any significant time and computational resource consumption, which are required for the rigorous numerical computation such as the FEM method. In addition, we note that the χ‐S plane as an efficient parametric space is derived from the closed‐form expressions in Section 2. This displacement is close to the ω G‐preserving displacement for Figure 3c. For this particular parametric displacement, the resonance position of the lower band‐edge GMR remains constant. The associated spectral profile change demonstrates remarkably large linewidth tuning from 22.5 to 0.345 nm while keeping the peak wavelength exactly fixed at 1.259 μm.

The opposite case of resonance location change with a fixed linewidth is also readily possible by taking an appropriate Δω‐preserving displacement. Figure 3e shows spectral profile change for such a parametric displacement indicated by the black arrow with “[Δp]L±” mark in Figure 3a. We confirm that the linewidth is fixed at 0.5 nm while the resonance location varies over 50 nm from 1.247 to 1.297 μm in this demonstration.

Importantly, all these demonstrations are feasible by systematically changing χ and S without altering other conditions including the net fill factor F, period, and ridge height. In other words, GMR devices for the independent metric controls in Figure 3c–3e can be fabricated on a single uniform thin‐film substrate by a single lithographic process. Therefore, our proposed dual‐ridge compound unit‐cell approach enables remarkably efficient parametric controls of GMR metrics in favor of applications in practice.

Fermi Arcs in Parameter Space

Resonance‐metric control in periodic lattice structures is immediately related to real and imaginary band structures of corresponding resonance states in general. Real and imaginary band structures respectively describe resonance position and linewidth in k space, as also explicitly revealed in Equations ((2), (3), (4), (5), (6)) in our approach here. In the non‐Hermitian physics perspective, particular parametric conditions for real or imaginary bandgap closure are of importance. In our approach, such conditions are expressed by Re[ω G] = 0 for the real bandgap closure and Im[ω G] = 0 for the imaginary bandgap closure, respectively, where ω G represents complex bandgap which is expressed by the following equation:

in our approach. For the real bandgap closure condition of Re(ω G) = 0, the upper and lower GMR bands touch each other and form a flat, dispersionless segment over a certain k range around Γ‐point. This flat segment of merged bands is referred to as real Fermi arc [22]. For the imaginary bandgap closure condition of Im(ω G) = 0 on the other hand, the bandgap remains opened while the linewidths of the two bands coincide over a certain k range around Γ‐point. This uniform linewidth segment is referred to as imaginary Fermi arc in contrast to the real Fermi arc [22]. A real or imaginary Fermi arc terminated by two non‐Hermitian degenerate points known as exceptional point (EP). At EPs, Re[ω G] = Im[ω G] = 0 and the eigenstates coalesce into a single state.

On these arcs, the eigenvalue splitting, ω + − ω − = 2(m + m − + vk)1/2 in Equation (3), becomes purely real for m + m − > −(vk)2 or otherwise purely imaginary. According to Equation (6) in the purely real splitting case, Δω + and Δω − coincide at Im(δ) and their explicit k dependence vanish. Consequently, we obtain almost uniform linewidth over the entire GMR bands, as shown in Figure 4b for the angle‐resolved transmittance spectra and Figure 4c for the spectral profiles in the reflection. Intriguingly, extremely narrow resonance persists over a wide range of incident angle and wavelength. This property is obtained at any parametric condition on the blue part of the ∞‐shape loop terminated by EPs in Figure 4a.

The remaining piece of the loop indicated by the red segments in Figure 4a connects the EPs on the other side. This segment is referred to as real Fermi arc. On this real Fermi arc, the eigenvalue splitting is purely imaginary because m + m − + (vk)2 < 0. Consequently, corresponding GMR dispersion band does not have a band gap, that is, the two band‐edge states appear at a single shared frequency. Instead of being separated in frequency, the two band‐edge states are distinguished in their linewidths. The narrower linewidth state among them dominates physical responses of the structure, potentially creating unique non‐Hermitian dynamics. The real Fermi arc is also known as a critical interface where any parametric displacement passing through it leads to band‐topological phase transition. Therefore, the emergence of the real and imaginary Fermi arcs here motivates further in‐depth study on non‐Hermitian and topological photonics taking advantages of efficient resonance‐metric‐control capability and convenient measurement schemes utilizing inherent leakage radiation as a natural probe that delivers the microscopic effects directly towards the optical far field.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have provided a comprehensive description of key GMR metrics in explicit terms of unit‐cell design parameters and proposed a deterministic design method for desired resonance properties. In particular, a dual‐ridge compound unit‐cell structure provides remarkably efficient parametric tuning of dispersion band and linewidth of a GMR in a fully independent manner. Therefore, our results can be immediately used for practical GMR devices such as filters, sensor templates, and surface‐emitting light sources. In addition, we have found out that the dual‐ridge unit‐cell design allows a direct access to emerging physics features associated with unique non‐Hermitian dynamics and topological wave effects such as EP singularities, Fermi arcs, and band‐topological phase transition.

All these benefits originate from the remarkably enhanced design‐parametric controllability on complex‐valued Fourier transform {ε n } which fundamentally determines coupling strengths and phases of elementary diffraction processes. For example, the dual‐ridge compound unit‐cell structures for high index‐contrast conditions demonstrated remarkably efficient spectral‐band engineering of multimode GMR devices including wideband reflectors, polarizers, and bandpass filters [8, 23, 24]. Therefore, it is of great interest to further develop our proposed approach for novel GMR elements involving bound‐states in the continuum, topological localization and beam generation, and many others.
