A case report of coexistence of acute appendicitis and Meckel’s diverticulitis causing generalized acute peritonitis
Authors: P’wèréw Rodolphe Plante, Bertrand Tamassi Essobiyou, Kokou Eyram Sorsy, Margueritte Messier, Goerges Dubreuil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:The coexistence of appendicitis and Meckel’s diverticulitis is a rare condition that can lead to acute peritonitis. It is difficult to diagnose preoperatively and requires emergency surgical intervention. We report a case presenting with both lesions.Case presentation:A 43-year-old female patient was admitted with generalized peritoneal irritation syndrome, developing in a febrile context and associated with a biological inflammatory syndrome. Computed tomography revealed signs of peritonitis and an inflamed appendix. Laparoscopy, converted to laparotomy, revealed coexisting appendicitis and Meckel’s diverticulitis, which were responsible for generalized peritonitis. An appendectomy and ileal resection to remove the Meckel’s diverticulum were performed, with end-to-end ileoileal anastomosis and cleaning of the peritoneal cavity. The postoperative course was uneventful. Pathological examination showed no malignant degeneration in the surgical specimens.Clinical discussion:The coexistence of appendicular inflammation and a Meckel’s diverticulum is a rare cause of acute peritonitis. We report a case in which the aetiology appears to be appendicular inflammation that spread to Meckel’s diverticulum, facilitated by an anatomical defect in the parietal attachment of the right colon. There is no consensus on the surgical approach to non-inflammatory Meckel’s diverticula in the context of appendicitis, but surgical intervention is indisputable in cases of dual inflammation. An appendectomy and diverticulectomy were performed, with a systematic pathological examination to look for signs of malignancy.Conclusion:The diagnostic and therapeutic urgency of this condition remains a challenge, particularly because the coexistence of these lesions should be considered when a typical picture of acute appendicitis is encountered.

Introduction

Meckel’s diverticulum is the remnant (or vestige) of the omphalomesenteric duct[1]. It is the most common congenital malformation of the digestive tract, affecting approximately 2% of the general population[1]. The lifetime risk of complications is estimated at 4%, including gastrointestinal bleeding, obstruction, or diverticulitis[1]. The diagnosis is rarely considered, and imaging tests, particularly in the absence of complications, are of limited help. An exploratory laparoscopy is often required to identify the diverticulum[1]. Acute appendicitis is one of the most common surgical emergencies worldwide, with a lifetime risk of 10% in France[2] and 7–8% in the United States[3]. The latter is approximately 100 times more prevalent than Meckel’s diverticulitis. Concomitant Meckel’s diverticulitis and acute appendicitis are rarely reported in the literature, and clinically, these two conditions can be difficult to differentiate based on assessment and imaging alone[4]. We report the concomitant presence of these two nosological entities in a single patient. Clinical observation

The patient was a 43-year-old woman admitted with abdominal pain and an infectious syndrome that had been developing over about 5 days in the context of constipation. Her medical history included chronic constipation and obesity, with a BMI greater than 45 kg/m². There was no evidence of inflammatory bowel disease. Clinical examination in the emergency department revealed diffuse abdominal pain with generalized peritoneal irritation. Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 9700 cells/mm³ and a C-reactive protein of 250 mg/L. Renal function tests and blood ionogram were normal. Lactate levels were negative. A CT scan revealed signs of peritonitis, with inflammation of the distal ileal loops, infiltration of abdominal fat, and an inflammatory appendix in contact with a dilated caecum, located medially in the umbilical region (Fig. 1). A laparoscopic exploration was therefore indicated, and she was taken to the operating room approximately 1 hour after admission. The patient underwent laparoscopic exploration (Fig. 2), which was converted to laparotomy due to difficulty with exposure in the context of significant loop dilatation (Fig. 3). This allowed approximately 300 mL of purulent fluid to be aspirated and revealed a gangrenous, perforated Meckel’s diverticulum, a phlegmonous appendix in contact, and the absence of parietal adhesions of the right colon. A laparotomy was therefore performed, involving a wedge resection of the ileum, removal of the Meckel’s diverticulum with end-to-end ileoileal anastomosis, and an antegrade appendectomy, with peritoneal cleaning and drainage using a Blake drain. Postoperative treatment consisted of triple antibiotic therapy, comprising injectable ceftriaxone at 2 g per day, intravenous metronidazole at 500 mg every 8 hours, and intramuscular gentamicin at 160 mg per day for 3 days, given the fecal nature of the peritonitis and in accordance with the department’s current protocol, along with analgesics/antipyretics, antispasmodics, enoxaparin at a prophylactic dose, and filling solutions.

The postoperative course was uneventful, with bowel function and feeding resuming on day 3, allowing for the removal of the drains. The surgical wounds were clean, and the abdomen was normal. The follow-up blood tests were satisfactory. She was discharged from the hospital on day 6 post-op. The patient attended a follow-up appointment 30 days after the operation and experienced no complications. Pathological examination of the surgical specimens revealed an ulcerated, perforated, non-degenerated Meckel’s diverticulum associated with peri-appendicitis.

Comments

The first description of Meckel’s diverticulum was made by Fabricius Hildanus in 1598. However, it is named after Johann Friedrich Meckel, who identified its embryonic origin in 1809 and defined it as a congenital anomaly with a prevalence of 2%[6]. It is located on the mesenteric edge of the ileum, usually between 30 and 90 cm from the ileocaecal valve, and contains all layers of the small intestinal wall, making it a true diverticulum[6]. It results from incomplete obliteration of the proximal part of the omphalomesenteric duct during the 7th week of gestation, making it the most common congenital malformation of the gastrointestinal tract[7]. Although it usually does not cause symptoms, approximately 4% of people with a Meckel’s diverticulum will experience complications, including obstruction, intussusception, bleeding, diverticulitis, perforation, or malignant degeneration[1,8]. However, establishing a precise diagnosis before surgery is difficult[9]. Furthermore, although acute appendicitis is a common condition, it is important to bear in mind that Meckel’s diverticulitis is a differential diagnosis[10] that can mimic the same clinical signs.

However, as shown in our patient’s case, these two conditions can occur simultaneously, as reported by certain authors[11]. Indeed, Senocak et al described a clinical case presenting with both acute appendicitis and perforated Meckel’s diverticulitis. Although the preoperative clinical examination and imaging tests suggested a classic diagnosis of appendicitis, the presence of a large volume of intra-abdominal pus raised suspicion of another condition[11]. The clinical picture may also vary with the patient’s age. Jumbi et al reported a case of concomitant acute appendicitis and perforated Meckel’s diverticulitis in an infant. The case initially presented with intestinal obstruction and later developed perforation[12]. Various approaches have been described in the literature for managing Meckel’s diverticulum depending on the patient’s age, clinical presentation, and intraoperative findings. Symptomatic or complicated Meckel’s diverticula are removed by diverticulectomy or segmental resection, whereas removal of incidentally discovered asymptomatic Meckel’s diverticula is controversial[13]. Ueberrueck et al recommended that removal of an asymptomatic Meckel’s diverticulum should depend primarily on the severity of appendicular inflammation [13,14]. According to their retrospective study, resecting a normal Meckel’s diverticulum in the presence of appendicular complications such as gangrene or perforation increases postoperative morbidity[14]. In addition, Groebli et al recommended resecting an incidental Meckel’s diverticulum, especially in cases of unconfirmed appendicitis. The diverticulum’s morphology and the patient’s hemodynamics should also be considered when making a decision[15]. In our case, there was no ambiguity about the therapeutic indication, given the damage to both organs, the appendix and Meckel’s diverticulum. We performed an appendectomy and segmental ileal resection, removing Meckel’s diverticulum. This aligns with the recommendations of certain authors [1,13]. Given their proximity, the pathophysiological hypothesis posits that inflammation spreads from the appendix to Meckel’s diverticulum, or vice versa. Meckel’s diverticulitis is more common in Crohn’s disease [1,16]. The surgical procedure in this case would have consisted of ileocolonic resection with a Kono-S anastomosis, as recommended in the literature[17].

Histologically, the pathological examination, as reported by most authors[4,6,11,12], showed signs of inflammation without evidence of malignancy.

Conclusion

Although there is no physiological link between the appendix and Meckel’s diverticulum, and each can present with distinct pathologies and evolve independently, they can still co-occur in the same patient, posing a significant challenge for diagnosis and management. The case presented was a surgical emergency; it was managed efficiently and appropriately, with no postoperative complications.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The strengths of this case report lie in the rarity of this pathological association and in the clarity of the illustrations. The main limitation remains the lack of a clear description of the aetiopathogenesis.
