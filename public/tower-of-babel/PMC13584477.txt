Early twice-daily toothbrushing and adolescent dental caries: a causal inference analysis using the Longitudinal Study of Australian Children
Authors: Hesham M. Al-Sharani, Nicole Stormon, Alice Richardson, Yuehan Zhang, Tehzeeb Zulfiqar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDental caries is a highly prevalent chronic childhood condition, yet longitudinal evidence linking early oral hygiene behaviours to long-term outcomes remains limited. This study estimated whether twice-daily toothbrushing at age 2–3 years was associated with lower incident dental caries through adolescence, under a target trial emulation framework.MethodsData were from the Longitudinal Study of Australian Children (LSAC) B-cohort (N = 5,107). Children with caries at or before Wave 2 (age 2–3) were excluded, yielding an incident sample of 2,970. Associations were estimated using multivariable logistic regression, propensity score matching, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE), and g-computation. The outcome was carer-reported dental caries. Confounders were identified via directed acyclic graph (DAG) analysis. Effect modification by area-level socioeconomic position (SEP) was assessed through a pre-specified equity analysis.ResultsAmong 2,941 children in the analytic sample, 1,559 (53.0%) developed carer-reported caries by age 14–15. Caries incidence was lower among children who brushed twice daily: 660 of 1,359 (48.6%) versus 899 of 1,582 (56.8%) in the less frequent group. The primary adjusted odds ratio (aOR) was 0.73 (95% CI: 0.63–0.85, p < 0.001). A dose-response gradient was observed (p-trend < 0.001). Effect estimates were similar across analytical approaches (OR range: 0.72–0.75). In non-disadvantaged areas, protective associations were observed (aOR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.54–0.79). In disadvantaged areas, the association was weaker and not statistically significant (aOR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.71–1.17; interaction p = 0.035). An exploratory falsification analysis using dental pain yielded a null result (aOR = 1.06, 95% CI: 0.91–1.23, p = 0.47), but this outcome was interpreted cautiously because dental pain may lie downstream of caries.ConclusionsTwice-daily toothbrushing by age 2–3 years was associated with a lower risk of incident adolescent caries. Estimates from multiple causal inference approaches were consistent with an absolute risk reduction of approximately 8% points (NNT ≈ 13), under assumptions of no unmeasured confounding, adequate positivity, and correct model specification. Protective associations appeared concentrated in non-disadvantaged areas, suggesting that oral hygiene promotion should be combined with broader preventive strategies addressing structural determinants of oral health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08999-9.

Background

Dental caries is one of the most prevalent chronic conditions globally, affecting an estimated 3.69 billion people; untreated caries of permanent teeth remains the most common oral condition [1]. Among children, approximately 514 million are affected in the primary dentition [1, 2]. In addition to pain and infection, early childhood caries can impair nutrition, disturb sleep, reduce school performance, and diminish oral health-related quality of life [3]. The condition disproportionately affects socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, contributing to persistent oral health inequities across the life course [4].

Paediatric dentistry guidelines recommend twice-daily toothbrushing with age-appropriate amounts of fluoride toothpaste from eruption of the first tooth [5]. United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) guidance separately supports fluoride varnish from primary-tooth eruption and fluoride supplementation where water fluoride is deficient [6]. Mechanical biofilm disruption combined with topical fluoride exposure inhibits demineralisation and promotes enamel remineralisation [7]. However, population-level evidence from rigorous longitudinal studies remains limited, as randomised trials of sustained behavioural interventions across childhood are difficult to conduct and sustain; the USPSTF evidence review highlights the broader preventive evidence base for fluoride interventions in young children [6].

Existing longitudinal evidence is limited by short follow-up periods, small samples, and incomplete confounder adjustment. Reviews identify poor oral hygiene and infrequent brushing as risk factors for early childhood caries, but many included studies were cross-sectional or at high risk of bias [8–10]. Cross-sectional studies demonstrate associations between brushing frequency and caries prevalence, but cannot establish temporal precedence or address confounding. Causal inference methods remain underused in oral health research [11, 12]. To our knowledge, no previous study has used target trial emulation with multiple causal estimators to examine early toothbrushing and adolescent caries. These methods address confounding under explicit assumptions, and convergence across complementary estimators can strengthen interpretation when results are consistent across approaches with different modelling assumptions [13]. Target trial emulation offers a framework for structuring observational studies to approximate the design of a randomised trial, explicitly defining time zero, eligibility, and the causal estimand [14, 15].

This study estimated the effect, under a target trial emulation framework and the assumptions required for causal identification, of twice-daily toothbrushing at age 2–3 years on carer-reported dental caries through adolescence, using data from the Longitudinal Study of Australian Children (LSAC). We used multiple complementary causal methods to assess whether estimates converged under stated assumptions. Additionally, we examined whether associations varied by area-level socioeconomic position, treating this as a pre-specified equity analysis requiring cautious interpretation.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies. Data were from the Longitudinal Study of Australian Children (LSAC), a nationally representative longitudinal study initiated by the Australian Government in 2004 to examine the development, health, and wellbeing of Australian children and their families. LSAC comprises two cohorts: the B-cohort (infants aged 0–1 year at recruitment) and the K-cohort (children aged 4–5 years). This study used the B-cohort exclusively, as it captures oral hygiene behaviours from infancy.

The B-cohort enrolled 5,107 children at Wave 1 (2004) using a two-stage cluster sampling design based on Medicare Australia enrolment records, stratified by state/territory and urban/rural classification, providing national coverage of the Australian population [16]. Data were collected biennially across eight waves (2004–2018) through a combination of face-to-face interviews, parent self-administered questionnaires, and direct child assessments in later waves. At Wave 8 (2018; age 14–15), 3,031 participants had complete exposure and outcome data, representing a retention rate of 59.4%.

The study was approved by the Australian Institute of Family Studies Ethics Committee (protocol 04–26).

Participants and target trial estimand

We framed this analysis as a target trial emulation [14, 15]. Time zero was Wave 2 (age 2–3 years). Wave 2 was selected as time zero because it represents the earliest wave at which tooth cleaning frequency was systematically assessed in the LSAC questionnaire, and by which most children would have erupted primary teeth. Children with caries reported at or before Wave 2 were excluded from the primary analysis, restricting to those caries-free at baseline.

The primary estimand was the average treatment effect (ATE; in causal inference, “treatment” denotes the exposure under study, not a clinical intervention) of twice-daily toothbrushing at age 2–3 years compared with less frequent brushing, on incident dental caries by age 14–15, among children caries-free at time zero. We note that propensity score matching targets the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT), and this distinction is acknowledged in the interpretation of results.

As a pre-specified sensitivity analysis, we repeated the primary analysis including children with prevalent caries at Wave 2 to assess the robustness of the exclusion criterion.

Variables

The primary exposure was carer-reported tooth cleaning frequency at Wave 2. The LSAC item asked how often a toothbrush was used to clean the study child’s teeth, with response options “more than twice a day”, “twice a day”, “once a day”, “less often than once a day”, and “don’t clean teeth” [17]. We created a binary variable: twice-daily brushing (twice daily or more) versus less frequent brushing (less than twice daily). Secondary analyses examined ordinal categories (“never/less than once daily”, “once daily”, “twice daily”, “more than twice daily”) to assess dose-response.

The primary outcome was carer-reported incident dental caries at Wave 8 (age 14–15). The LSAC oral health item asked whether the study child had problems with his/her teeth, specifically “cavities or dental decay”; response options were yes/no [17]. Among children caries-free at Wave 2, we defined incident caries as a yes response at Wave 8. Wave 8 was selected as the outcome assessment point because it represents an advanced follow-up wave, maximising the observation period (12 years) to capture cumulative caries experience through adolescence. Evidence on proxy- and self-reported caries suggests such measures can identify population patterns but may under- or overestimate clinical disease and vary by sociodemographic factors [18]. We therefore interpret self-reported caries as an important measurement limitation, especially for subgroup analyses.

Confounders were identified via directed acyclic graph (DAG) analysis (Additional file 1: Figure S4) and measured at or before Wave 2: area-level socioeconomic position (the Socio-Economic Indexes for Areas [SEIFA] Index of Relative Socioeconomic Advantage and Disadvantage, dichotomised as disadvantaged [quintiles 1–2] versus non-disadvantaged [quintiles 3–5]), parental education (university degree versus no degree), household structure (two-parent versus other), geographic remoteness (regional/remote versus major city), sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) consumption (daily versus less frequent), and maternal smoking during pregnancy (any versus none). Geographic remoteness was included to account for broad differences in service access and community context across major city and regional/remote areas. Dental service utilisation (dental visit by Wave 4) was positioned as a potential mediator on the causal pathway between toothbrushing and caries and was therefore excluded from the primary confounder adjustment set; its inclusion is examined in sensitivity analyses. Child sex was excluded from the confounder set because it showed negligible association with the outcome (standardised mean difference [SMD] = 0.006) and does not satisfy the backdoor criterion in our DAG. Breastfeeding history was similarly excluded as it showed minimal association with the outcome (SMD = 0.012) and its inclusion risked introducing bias through adjustment for a potential collider on the socioeconomic–health behaviour pathway. Maternal smoking during pregnancy was included as it is associated with both reduced health-promoting behaviours and increased caries risk through developmental and behavioural pathways. Primary analyses were unweighted, targeting sample-average causal effects. Robust standard errors accounted for clustering within primary sampling units. Positivity was assessed by inspecting propensity score overlap (Additional file 1: Figure S1), the distribution of IPTW weights, and covariate balance after matching and weighting (Additional file 1: Table S2). Very large or unstable weights were not observed; stabilised IPTW weights ranged from 0.87 to 1.17 after truncation. These diagnostics are standard for propensity-score analyses [19–21], while TMLE implementation followed targeted learning and tmle package guidance [22, 23]. Implications for population generalisability are addressed in the Limitations.

Sensitivity analyses included: E-value calculation using the odds ratio (OR) to risk ratio (RR) conversion appropriate for common outcomes [26]; dose-response analysis across ordinal cleaning categories; multiple imputation for missing confounder data (m = 20, predictive mean matching, with all confounders, exposure, and outcome as predictors); inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) to address socioeconomically patterned attrition; stratified analyses by area-level socioeconomic position with formal interaction testing; inclusion of the 61 prevalent cases; re-analysis with the original seven-confounder set (including child sex and breastfeeding) to assess specification sensitivity; an exploratory falsification analysis examining the association between toothbrushing and dental pain at age 14–15 [27]; a doubly robust estimator combining outcome regression and treatment weighting; and generalised linear mixed model (GLMM) and generalised estimating equation (GEE) models to assess geographic clustering and alternative correlation structures. Dental pain was selected because it is a nonspecific oral symptom sharing some confounding structure with caries, but it is not a perfect negative control because it can occur downstream of untreated caries.

Analyses were conducted on complete cases with non-missing confounder data. Missing confounder data were minimal: maternal smoking during pregnancy had 29 missing values (1.0%); all other confounders were complete. Multiple imputation was performed as a sensitivity analysis.

Attrition was socioeconomically patterned (Additional file 1: Table S1); inverse probability of censoring weighting was used to assess its impact on effect estimates. The IPCW model included baseline variables associated with attrition and caries risk, including brushing exposure, SEIFA, parental education, household structure, remoteness, maternal smoking during pregnancy, SSB consumption, child sex, and breastfeeding history. Unweighted and IPCW-weighted distributions of baseline characteristics for the retained subset with complete censoring-model covariates are presented in Additional file 1: Table S3. Effect modification by area-level socioeconomic position was specified a priori based on the equity hypothesis; no formal multiplicity adjustment was applied, so subgroup and interaction p-values were interpreted cautiously.

All analyses used R version 4.3.0 [28] with packages MatchIt [21], WeightIt [29], tmle [23], SuperLearner [24], cobalt [30], mice [31], and survey [32].

Results

Of 5,107 children enrolled at Wave 1, 4,565 had exposure data at Wave 2 (age 2–3), and 3,031 (59.4% of baseline) had complete exposure and outcome data. After excluding 61 children with prevalent caries at Wave 2 (2.0%), the primary incident sample comprised 2,970 children. The complete-case analytic sample with all six confounders non-missing comprised 2,941 (99.0%). Loss to follow-up affected 2,076 participants (40.7%); retained participants had higher parental education (SMD = 0.385), were more likely from two-parent households (SMD = 0.299), and had higher breastfeeding rates (SMD = 0.287) compared with those lost to follow-up (Additional file 1: Table S1).

Among the analytic sample, 1,359 (46.2%) met twice-daily brushing guidelines at age 2–3 and 1,559 (53.0%) developed caries by age 14–15. Caries incidence was 660 of 1,359 (48.6%) among children who brushed twice daily versus 899 of 1,582 (56.8%) among those who brushed less frequently; crude OR = 0.72 (95% CI: 0.62–0.83).

Table 1 presents baseline characteristics by exposure status. Exposure groups were similar on area-level disadvantage and geographic remoteness. The propensity score model achieved a C-statistic of 0.53, indicating that measured covariates weakly predicted brushing frequency. This weak discrimination is consistent with the small baseline differences between exposure groups and does not itself indicate poor overlap. Propensity score distributions overlapped substantially (Additional file 1: Figure S1). After 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching (1,343 matched pairs), all standardised mean differences were reduced below 0.05. Full matching retained all observations with comparable balance. Stabilised IPTW weights ranged from 0.87 to 1.17 (mean = 1.00). Propensity score diagnostics, including overlap plots and variance ratios for binary covariates, are presented in Additional file 1: Figures S1–S2 and Table S2.

Table 2 presents effect estimates across methods with 95% confidence intervals. All approaches yielded consistent protective effects. The primary regression estimate was aOR = 0.73 (95% CI: 0.63–0.85, p < 0.001). Across all methods, adjusted odds ratios ranged from 0.72 (full matching) to 0.75 (PS matching), with overlapping confidence intervals (Fig. 1).

Analysis of ordinal brushing categories, restricted to children with non-missing detailed frequency responses (N = 2,346), revealed a dose-response gradient (Table 3). Compared with children brushing less than once daily, those brushing once daily had aOR = 0.86 (95% CI: 0.64–1.16), twice daily aOR = 0.64 (0.47–0.86), and more than twice daily aOR = 0.78 (0.44–1.37; p-trend < 0.001). The dose-response gradient provides indirect support for a causal interpretation per Bradford Hill’s criteria.

Stratified analyses suggested possible effect modification by area-level disadvantage. Among children in non-disadvantaged areas (SEIFA quintiles 3–5; N = 1,915), consistent protective associations were observed across all methods (regression aOR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.54–0.79; IPTW OR = 0.66; PS matching OR = 0.67; all p < 0.001). Among children in disadvantaged areas (quintiles 1–2; N = 1,026), the association was weaker and not statistically significant (regression aOR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.71–1.17; IPTW OR = 0.91; PS matching OR = 0.93; all p > 0.262). The formal interaction test yielded p = 0.035 (likelihood ratio test). Table 4 presents the stratified results.

Although the interaction was pre-specified and statistically significant (p = 0.035), it should be interpreted cautiously given the borderline p-value, multiple sensitivity and subgroup analyses, and the possibility of differential outcome ascertainment by socioeconomic position.

As an exploratory falsification analysis, we examined the association between twice-daily toothbrushing and dental pain at age 14–15 [27]. The adjusted association was null: aOR = 1.06 (95% CI: 0.91–1.23, p = 0.47). The IPTW estimate was similarly null (OR = 1.06). This finding provides limited reassurance against a broad residual association with nonspecific oral symptoms, but dental pain is an imperfect negative control because it may occur downstream of dental caries; it should not be interpreted as definitive validation of the confounder adjustment strategy.

All sensitivity analyses yielded estimates consistent with the primary result. Including the 61 prevalent cases gave aOR = 0.75 (95% CI: 0.65–0.87). IPCW-weighted analysis yielded aOR = 0.73 (95% CI: 0.65–0.83), indicating that socioeconomically patterned attrition did not materially affect estimates. Survey-weighted analysis produced aOR = 0.76 (95% CI: 0.64–0.90). Multiple imputation (m = 20, N = 2,970) produced aOR = 0.73 (95% CI: 0.63–0.84), closely aligned with the complete-case estimate. Including dental service utilisation as a seventh confounder yielded aOR = 0.72 (95% CI: 0.62–0.83), confirming that its exclusion did not materially affect the primary result. Re-analysis with the original seven-confounder set (adding child sex and breastfeeding history, replacing maternal smoking) yielded aOR = 0.72 (95% CI: 0.62–0.83, N = 2,915), confirming robustness to confounder specification.

E-values indicated moderate robustness to unmeasured confounding: for the primary regression estimate, E-value = 1.62 (confidence interval bound = 1.39) [26]. An unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both toothbrushing and caries by a risk ratio of at least 1.62 to fully explain away the observed association.

A generalised linear mixed model with state-level random intercepts yielded aOR = 0.72 (95% CI: 0.62–0.84), with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.007, indicating minimal geographic clustering. GEE models with exchangeable and AR (1) correlation structures produced consistent estimates (OR range: 0.76–0.79).

Discussion

Using explicit causal inference methods [11, 12], this study found results consistent with a protective effect of twice-daily toothbrushing at age 2–3 years on adolescent caries risk, conditional on the assumptions required for causal identification. In this cohort, more than half of adolescents developed carer-reported caries by age 14–15. Using 12 years of follow-up data from a nationally representative Australian birth cohort and multiple complementary analytical methods, twice-daily toothbrushing at age 2–3 years was estimated to reduce adolescent caries risk under the stated identifying assumptions. Effect estimates were similar across analytical approaches, strengthening confidence that the finding is not an artefact of a single modelling strategy [13]. However, the observational design, self-reported measures, modest E-value, and potential for residual confounding mean that the findings should be interpreted as consistent with, rather than definitive proof of, a causal effect. Protective associations appeared weaker among children in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas, suggesting that structural barriers may attenuate the benefit of individual oral hygiene behaviour in these populations. These findings support current clinical guidelines recommending twice-daily brushing from early childhood, while highlighting that behavioural interventions alone are unlikely to address persistent oral health inequities in Australia.

Our findings are consistent with and extend prior evidence. Systematic reviews have identified poor oral hygiene, infrequent toothbrushing, and related behavioural factors as risk factors for early childhood caries [8–10]. The present study advances this evidence through three key contributions: a 12-year follow-up period extending from toddlerhood through adolescence, a target trial emulation framework ensuring temporal precedence between exposure and incident outcome, and convergence across multiple complementary causal estimators. These methods remain relatively uncommon in oral health research [12] and can strengthen the basis for causal interpretation when assumptions are plausible and transparently discussed.

Previous analyses of the LSAC cohort have examined dental caries but without the same target trial framing. Stormon et al. [33] described age, period, and cohort patterns of caries experience in LSAC, documenting a high burden of disease across childhood, while a subsequent analysis [34] examined oral health epidemiology and parent–child concordance within the same cohort. Another LSAC analysis examined family-level predictors of dental caries and injuries, highlighting the importance of parental education, parental smoking, family health, and parenting context [35]. Our analysis extends this work by applying DAG-informed confounder selection and multiple causal methods specifically designed to address the limitations of conventional multivariable adjustment.

The observed effect likely reflects multiple mechanisms. Toothbrushing can disrupt dental biofilm, while fluoride exposure promotes remineralisation and inhibits demineralisation; fluoride toothpaste has established caries-preventive efficacy in children and adolescents [7, 36]. Early establishment of brushing routines may reflect and reinforce broader parental health supervision that persists across childhood. We cannot separate the effect of fluoride toothpaste from the mechanical action of brushing itself; these components are typically co-delivered and may be synergistic. Notably, the dose-response gradient observed in this study adds indirect support for causal interpretation, although residual confounding remains possible; dose-response is one of Bradford Hill’s considerations for causal inference [37].

The attenuation of protective associations in disadvantaged areas aligns with fundamental cause theory [38]: populations with fewer resources may be less able to translate health behaviours into health benefits. The two area-level strata analysed here likely differ across several structural determinants, including access to fluoridated water and affordable fluoride toothpaste, availability and affordability of preventive dental services, transport barriers, local food environments, exposure to marketing and availability of cariogenic foods and drinks, and competing demands on parental time [4, 38]. Regional and remote residence may further influence access to dental care and preventive programmes, although exposure groups were similar on remoteness at baseline and state-level clustering was minimal. This finding is consistent with the broader literature on socioeconomic gradients in oral health [4] and highlights that behavioural interventions operate within structural constraints. However, the interaction p-value (0.035) should be interpreted with caution given multiple subgroup comparisons, and the possibility of differential outcome measurement by socioeconomic position warrants particular consideration. Replication in studies with clinically assessed outcomes would strengthen this finding.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include the nationally representative cohort with 12-year follow-up, target trial framing with explicit time-zero definition and exclusion of prevalent cases, application of multiple complementary causal methods with convergent risk differences, confounder adjustment informed by DAG analysis (Additional file 1: Figure S4), and an exploratory falsification analysis that provided limited additional reassurance while being interpreted cautiously.

This study has several limitations. First, both exposure and outcome were carer-reported. Over-reporting of toothbrushing due to social desirability could misclassify some less-frequent brushers into the exposed group, likely attenuating associations if non-differential. Outcome ascertainment depends on carer awareness of dental disease, dental service use, and recognition of caries, all of which may vary by socioeconomic position. Differential misclassification is therefore a substantial concern for the subgroup analysis: if carers in disadvantaged areas were less likely to be aware of untreated or asymptomatic caries, the apparent attenuation of brushing benefit in these areas could be biased. Conversely, higher symptom burden or problem-oriented dental attendance could increase detection in disadvantaged areas. Second, attrition was substantial and socioeconomically patterned (40.7% of baseline participants lost by Wave 8). IPCW-weighted and multiple-imputation analyses yielded estimates close to the primary result, but selection bias may persist if the censoring model was misspecified or if attrition depended on unmeasured determinants of both brushing and caries. Third, primary analyses were unweighted; while appropriate for sample-level causal inference, this limits direct population generalisability, although survey-weighted sensitivity analyses yielded consistent results. Fourth, we could not distinguish fluoride toothpaste use from brushing behaviour or assess technique, duration, or supervision. Fifth, the propensity score model had weak discrimination (C-statistic = 0.53). This indicates that measured covariates only weakly predicted toothbrushing and explains the small adjustment from crude to adjusted estimates; it does not remove the possibility of unmeasured confounding. Finally, the E-value was modest (1.62 for the point estimate), meaning that moderate unmeasured confounding from factors such as genetic susceptibility, water fluoride concentration, detailed dietary patterns, parental oral health literacy, or dental attendance patterns could potentially explain the observed association.

Implications

These findings provide empirical support for clinical guidelines recommending twice-daily toothbrushing from early childhood and align with the WHO Global Strategy and Action Plan on Oral Health 2023–2030, which includes a target of a 10% relative reduction in the combined global prevalence of the main oral diseases and conditions by 2030 [39]. Paediatric providers should counsel parents on establishing brushing routines by age 2–3. However, the weaker association in disadvantaged populations underscores that behavioural interventions alone are unlikely to eliminate oral health inequities. Effective strategies should extend to structural interventions, including community water fluoridation where appropriate and access to affordable fluoride toothpaste [40], alongside broader oral-health promotion, sugar-reduction, and preventive-care strategies consistent with the WHO Global Strategy and Action Plan on Oral Health 2023–2030 [39].

Internationally, structural and system-level programmes have improved child oral health and, in some settings, narrowed inequalities. In Scotland, the Childsmile programme of universal nursery-based supervised toothbrushing was associated with lower caries experience, with the greatest benefit among children in the most deprived areas [41]. Following the UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy, children’s hospital admissions for caries-related tooth extractions fell by an estimated 12% [42], and community water fluoridation remains an equity-positive measure where feasible [40]. Such measures act on the upstream determinants - product affordability, fluoride availability, and access to preventive care - that our findings suggest constrain the benefit of toothbrushing in disadvantaged populations, and are therefore more likely to deliver equitable reductions in caries when combined with individual oral-hygiene promotion than behavioural advice alone.

Conclusions

Twice-daily toothbrushing by age 2–3 years was associated with a lower risk of incident adolescent caries, with estimates from multiple causal inference approaches suggesting an absolute risk reduction of approximately 8% points (number needed to treat [NNT] ≈ 13). Protective associations appeared to be concentrated in non-disadvantaged areas, suggesting that individual behavioural interventions alone may be insufficient to reduce socioeconomic inequities in oral health. These findings support the importance of combining oral hygiene promotion with broader preventive strategies addressing structural determinants of health. However, interpretation should consider study limitations, including potential residual confounding, reliance on self-reported measures, differential outcome ascertainment, substantial attrition, and limited generalisability to disadvantaged populations. Further longitudinal studies using clinically assessed outcomes are needed to confirm and extend these findings.
