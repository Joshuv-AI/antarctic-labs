Targeting glucagon signaling in metabolic disorders, functional insights from zebrafish receptor knockouts
Authors: Ashraf Al Madhoun, Md Zubbair Malik, Shaima Al-Beloushi, Nermeen Abukhalaf, Lavina Miranda, Sindu Jacob, Rasheeba Nizam, Sumi John, Preethi George, Arshad Channanath, Fatemah Bahman, Meijiang Liao, Rasheed Ahmad, Wenbiao Chen, Abdelhamid Bekri, Pierre Drapeau, Thangavel Alphonse Thanaraj, Fahd Al-Mulla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glucagon receptor (GCGR) signaling is essential for glucose and lipid homeostasis, making it a potential therapeutic target for metabolic disorders. Zebrafish possess two GCGR co-orthologs, GCGRa and GCGRb, however, their distinct function remain unclear. In this study we employed CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing to generate GCGRa⁻/⁻, GCGRb⁻/⁻, and double-knockout (GCGR⁻/⁻) zebrafish to dissect isoform-specific functions. RNA-Seq analysis was performed to characterize transcriptomic alterations, while an overfeeding protocol was used to assess metabolic tolerance, and ligand-response assays in cell lines evaluated isoform activation dynamics. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that both isoforms regulate overlapping but distinct metabolic pathways. Functional enrichment analysis linked GCGRa to lipid and energy metabolism, cholesterol biosynthesis and glucose homeostasis, through key signaling cascades such as glucagon, PPARγ and PI3K-AKT. In contrast, GCGRb loss altered fatty acid β-oxidation, GPCR signaling, and oxidative phosphorylation networks, implicating roles in metabolism and cellular stress. The GCGR⁻/⁻ primarily impacted core metabolic networks including lipid, gluconeogenesis and energy metabolism, indicating complementary and overlapping functions of both receptors in maintaining hepatic metabolic homeostasis. Ligand-response assays revealed that GCGRb, but not GCGRa, is activated by both glucagon (GCGa) and glucagon like-peptide-1 (GLP1a), supporting the post-duplication receptor diversification theory. Notably, all knockouts exhibited impaired growth under high-nutrient conditions, confirming GCGR’s role in diet-responsive development. This study provides the first systematic functional comparison of zebrafish GCGR isoforms, establishing zebrafish as a valuable model for investigating glucagon-based metabolic regulation and therapeutic interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06275-1.

Introduction

Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes are metabolic disorders characterized by hyperglycemia resulting from insulin deficiency or resistance [1]. Traditional treatments focus on targeting insulin secretion or sensitivity, but often cause various side effects [2]. Given the role of glucagon in glucose homeostasis, targeting its signaling has emerged as a novel strategy. The glucagon receptor (GCGR) belongs to class B (Family B) of G protein- coupled receptors (GPCRs) family, regulates glucagon’s effects on hepatic-enriched glucose production and lipid metabolism [3, 4]. GCGR double knockout (GCGR−/−) models in mice and zebrafish offered significant insights into glucagon’s role in metabolism regulation as a potential therapeutic target.

Congenital GCGR deletion results in normoglycemia despite insulinopenic conditions, highlighting glucagon’s crucial role in diabetes pathogenesis [5]. Notably, GCGR−/− mice exhibit significant α-cell hyperplasia, hyperglucagonemia, and increased glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP1) secretion, contributing to enhanced insulin secretion and glucose tolerance [6–8]. Furthermore, these mice resist streptozotocin (STZ)-induced hyperglycemia [9, 10], supporting glucagon antagonism as a potential strategy for managing T1D [11].

Acute and chronic glucagon signaling disruptions in GCGR−/− mice have distinct metabolic consequences. Acute GCGR blockade lowers blood glucose [12], while chronic inhibition induces GLP1 as a compensatory mechanism to maintain normoglycemia [6]. However, prolonged glucagon antagonism has been associated with dyslipidemia and hepatic dysfunction, suggesting combination therapy to maximize efficacy [13]. Dual knockout (KO) models (GCGR−/−/GLP1R−/−) showed an interplay between glucagon and incretin signaling to achieve optimal glycemic control [6, 14].

Glucagon is implicated in broad metabolic regulatory mechanisms where GCGR−/− mice exhibit altered lipid, amino acid metabolism and energy homeostasis, suggesting a liver/pancreatic α-cell axis cross-talk [9, 15–18]. Pharmacological interventions targeting this axis may offer new therapeutic avenues for metabolic disorders beyond diabetes.

Zebrafish models have emerged as valuable tools for studying glucagon signaling due to their genetic tractability and suitability for high-throughput screening. The zebrafish genome contains two glucagon receptor genes, GCGRa and GCGRb, co-orthologs of the human GCGR which may have resulted from a whole-genome duplication event in teleost fish [19]. Phylogenetic and synteny analyses demonstrated that GCGRa and GCGRb share significant homology with the human GCGR. Notably, GCGRa and GCGRb exhibit distinct expression patterns in different tissues [19], implying a potential functional divergence between the two receptors. KO of these genes using transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALEN) results in phenotypes like GCGR−/− mice [19, 20]. Transcriptomic analysis of global GCGR-deficient zebrafish revealed widespread metabolic alterations [21], highlighting its broader metabolic role and underscoring the potential of zebrafish as a model for glucagon-related disorders.

Notably, the distinct functional contributions of GCGRa and GCGRb remain to be fully elucidated. To address this, we generated single and double KOs of both genes using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing. We analyzed the transcriptome of each clone and subjected them to an overfeeding protocol to assess metabolic tolerance and gene functional role in GCGR signaling. These approaches provide a comprehensive insight into the distinct functions of GCGRa and GCGRb within zebrafish physiology and prompt for a potential use in pharmaceutical applications.

Materials and methods

Fish husbandry

Tubingen long fin (TL) Wild-type zebrafish (Danio rerio) were housed at the Animal Core Facility, Dasman Diabetes Institute, Kuwait. The fish were reared in a temperature-controlled aquarium at 28.5 °C, with a 12 h light/dark cycle, and the developmental stages were tracked as described previously [22]. The fish were bred in accordance with the guidelines for the laboratory use of zebrafish described by Westerfield [23]. The study was approved by the Animal Care Committee of the Dasman Diabetes Institute, Kuwait. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of Animal Research: Reporting of in vivo Experiments (ARRIVE). The following protocol was adapted to overfeed the zebrafish: At 6 days post fertilization (dpf), larvae were divided into two groups. One group received a normal diet (GP 100–300 Micron Weaning Diet, Brine Shrimp Direct, Ogden, UT, USA), and the other group received peeled Artemia salina cysts (Grade A Brine Shrimp Eggs, BSEACASE, Brine Shrimp Direct) twice a day. The larvae were maintained on their respective diets for two weeks, and the body length and size were monitored on days 10 and 15 (i.e. 16- and 21-dpf, respectively).

CRISPR-cas9 gene editing, sgRNA and cas9 preparation and microinjection

Gene-specific guide RNAs (gRNA) were envisioned utilizing the online tool CRISPRscan (http://www.crisprscan.org/). The primers used for the gRNA preparation and DNA sequence are listed in Supplemental Table 1. Synthesis of gRNA sequences and of Cas9 mRNA were performed as described by [24]. Wild-type larvae were collected and processed for microinjection. About 1 nL drop of a mix of 30 ng/µL of gRNA and 100 ng/µL of Cas9 mRNA was manually injected into one-cell stage embryos using a Picospritzer III pressure ejector. 24 h post injections, the embryos were sorted, and some were used to test for the CRISPR-Cas9 efficiency. The larvae were left to grow. At adulthood, injected fish were crossed with the WT fish, to generate the heterogeneous founder. The GCGR⁻/⁻ut fish was generated using the GCGRb gRNA and CRISPR-Cas9 on GCGRa⁻/⁻ fish, as background. The progeny of the founder fish was sequenced for both GCGRa and GCGRb mutations, and the fish with a mutation that matches the GCGRb⁻/⁻ was chosen for further studies.

Fin clipping of adult zebrafish and genomic DNA extraction

Adult fish were anesthetized in 160 mg/L tricaine methane-sulfonate (MS222). From each fish, a piece of the caudal fin was sliced with a sharp blade, collected into a sterile 1.5 mL tube, and then immediately returned to water. Genomic DNA was extracted from the clipped caudal fin in a total volume of 20 µL of 50 mM NaOH. Then, samples were boiled for 10 min (min) and buffered with 2 µL of 100 mM Tris–HCl pH 8, as previously described in [25].

DNA sequencing and bioanalyzer fractionation

DNA sequencing was performed using primers listed in Supplemental Table 1. The PCR reactions contained 1 µL of genomic DNA, 2.5 µL of 10x PCR buffer, 0.5 µL of each primer (10 µM), 0.5 µL of dNTP (10 µM), 0.125 µL of Taq DNA polymerase (Invitrogen), and water up to 25 µL. The PCR reaction protocol was performed at 1 cycle of 94 °C for 5 min, then 40 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 45 s and finally a terminal cycle of 72 °C for 10 min. All samples were sequenced using Sanger method on the Applied Biosystems 3730xl DNA Analyzer. In another set of experiments, PCR products were fractionated on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system using a High Sensitivity DNA Electrophoresis Kit, as described by the manufacturers (Agilent Technologies) and then the samples were quantified using High Sensitivity DNA assay software [26].

High-resolution in situ hybridization

Larvae at 4-dpf were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 18 h, dehydrated in ascending methanol concentrations (25, 50, and 75%, in PBS), followed by 100% methanol, and stored at -20 °C. The whole mount in situ hybridization procedure was performed according to the protocol described [27, 28] with minor modifications. Briefly, Larvae were rehydrated in an inverted gradient with PBST (1x PBS, 0.1% Tween 20). Larvae were then digested with proteinase K (20 µg/ml in PBST, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) for 12 min at room temperature. After 15 min wash, Larvae were post-fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, then were re-washed again for 15 min. The Larvae were incubated in hybridization mix (50 µg/ml heparin, 500 µg/ml wheat germ tRNA, 50% formamide; 5x SSC; pH 4.5; 0.1% Tween 20) for 60 min at 70 °C. Then, the mRNA probe solution (100 µg/ml RNA in hybridization mix) was applied for 8 h at 70 °C. The Larvae were then neutralized at room temperature, and were treated as follows: 30 min hybridization mix; 60 min wash; 7 h closed-loop-recycling of alkaline phosphatase-conjugated anti-hapten antibody (at ratio 1 : 3000, Roche); 3 h wash; and finally, 20 min of Tris-HCl buffer pH 9.5 as described in [27]. Enzymatic reaction was detected using NBT/BCIP solution (Roche) under constant observation for color development. After staining, labelled Larvae were mounted in 90% glycerol, and images were acquired with a Zeiss Stereo microscope (Discovery V12, Jana, Germany). The sequences of primers used in the synthesis are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from Larvae (20–60), at different developmental stages, using the miRNeasy kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). Fish were homogenized with QIAzol lysis reagent, as described in the manufacturer’s protocol. The first cDNA strand was synthesized from 500 ng RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). Real-time quantitative Reverse transcriptase (RT-qPCR) was performed as described previously [29]. RT-qPCR was performed using a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Each cycle involved denaturation for 15 s at 95 °C; and an annealing/extension for 1 min at 60 °C. Gene expression relative to Larvae 2-dpf was calculated using the comparative cycles to the threshold (CT) method, as previously described [29]. Results were normalized to the zebrafish glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene expression, and data (mean ± SEM) were expressed as fold changes in expression relative to controls, as previously indicated [30].

RNA sequencing and data analysis

A total of 40ng of purified RNA was used for whole transcriptome sequencing. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, transcriptome libraries were prepared using TruSeq stranded mRNA kit (Illumina Inc. USA). Prepared libraries were validated and quantified using a bioanalyzer (Agilent, California, United States) and qubit fluorometer Thermofisher Scientific, Massachusetts, United States), respectively. Paired-end sequencing was carried out on the Novaseq 6000 system (Illumina Inc. USA). The resulting bcl files were converted to Fastq using bcl2fastq program version 2.20. Quality control of Fastq files was performed using FastQC (v0.11.9) [31]. We used Trimmomatic (v0.39) [32] for adaptor and quality trimming and for the removal of very short reads. HISAT2 (v2.1.0) [33] was used for data alignment. To enumerate the number of reads that are associated with the genes, we used htseq-count tool in HTSeq (v 0.9.1) [34]. Differential gene expression (DEGs) analysis was carried out using Bioconductor package edgeR [35], adopting the default setting.

Functional enrichment analysis

We used enrichment analysis to classify genes into hierarchical categories and to identify gene regulatory networks based on the associated biological processes and KEGG pathways [36, 37]. We used the DAVID bioinformatics tool (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) [38] to perform Gene ontology (GO) biological process-based analyses and functional enrichment analyses of DEGs. We considered a p-value of < 0.05 as the threshold value to recognize significantly enriched pathways. We then visualized the most enriched GO terms, with p-values indicative of statistical significance, in ggplot2 [39].

Total glucose measurement in larvae

Ten Larvae (3 different batches) were collected and homogenized for total glucose baseline measurement. The homogenate was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 2 min, and 1.5 µL of the supernatant was applied to an Accu-Chek Performa glucometer strip to quantify free glucose levels using the Accu-Chek Performa Nano device.

Cell culture, CRE-luciferase assay for receptor-ligand activity

HEK293T Cells were grown in DMEM/F12 media (Invitrogen Corporation, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Invitrogen Corporation) and 100 U/mL of penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cells were transfected with GCGRa or GCGRb constructs (cloned at Prof. Wenbiao Chen laboratory), CRE-luciferase reporter plasmid and Rinella-luciferase construct (transfection efficiency indicator) as previously described [40]. Briefly, 5 µg of plasmid DNA was mixed with lipofectamine 3000, as recommended by the manufacturers (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and added to the cells in antibiotics-free media. The media was changed to DMEM/F12 medium after 24 h of transfection, and the transfected cells were seeded into a 96-well plate. The cells were treated with increasing concentrations of zGCGa (HSEGTFSNDYSKYLETRRAQDFVQWLMNA) or zGLP1a (HAEGTYTSDVSSYLQDQAAQSFVAWLKSGQP) peptides (synthesized by ProteoGenix, Schiltigheim, France). After a 4 h incubation at 37 °C, the cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS, lysed, and assayed per the Dual-Luciferase Kit protocol (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Luciferase activity was detected using the GloMax Navigator Microplate Luminometer (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) assay. The value denoting the activity of the luciferase reporter vector in the presence of peptides was normalized by subtracting the value denoting the activity of the luciferase reporter alone, in the absence of peptides. The obtained data were then normalized to that from empty vector CMV and to the lowest concentration value. Relative luciferase units (RLUs) represent luciferase activity normalized to Renilla-luciferase activity [41].

Analysis of hepatic GCGR isoform expression using public RNA-seq data

To validate liver-specific expression and metabolic regulation of GCGR isoforms, we reanalyzed publicly available RNA-sequencing data from zebrafish liver tissue (GEO accession: GSE183023). This dataset includes liver samples collected from adult zebrafish under two experimental conditions: normal-fed (control) and overfed animals. Overfeeding was used as a metabolic challenge to model obesity-associated metabolic stress, allowing assessment of how GCGR isoforms respond to nutrient excess.

Normalized expression data were downloaded from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database and analyzed using the R statistical environment with the DESeq2 package. Expression values for GCGRa and GCGRb were extracted across all samples in both the control and overfed groups. Transcript abundance was quantified as counts per million (CPM) to account for sequencing depth and enable comparison across samples. Statistical differences in gene expression between groups were assessed using Student’s t-test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while values between 0.05 and 0.10 were reported as trends. The analysis included 6–8 zebrafish per group, providing sufficient statistical power to detect biologically relevant differences in hepatic gene expression under metabolic stress.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism (La Jolla, San Diego, CA, USA) and SPSS for Windows version 19.01 (IBM SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY, USA), following the previously established methods [42, 43]. Data is expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), Data from three independent experiments, each with n ≥ 30 embryos, unless otherwise specified. Comparisons between two independent groups were made using the unpaired Student’s T-test. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess associations between variables, as described previously [43, 44]. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Result

Generation of zebrafish GCGRa and GCGRb KO using CRISPR editing

Using CRISPR/Cas9 technology, we initially generated GCGRa−/− and GCGRb−/− KO zebrafish lines before producing a double GCGR−/−. Both GCGRa and GCGRb genes are composed of 13 exons, and we targeted exon 3, which corresponds to the N-terminal region of the proteins (Fig. 1A). The CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing efficiency was high, resulting in four distinct deletions for GCGRa and two deletions along with one deletion-insertion event for GCGRb (Supplemental Fig. 1A and B, respectively). These mutations introduced multiple premature stop codons downstream of the deleted nucleotides due to frameshifts in the open reading frame, except for the GCGRb mutant carrying a 12-nucleotide deletion, which generated a truncated protein missing three amino acids (Supplemental Fig. 1C and D). We selected the GCGRa (-8, designated GCGRa−/−) and GCGRb (-12, designated GCGRb−/−) mutant alleles, for further characterization (Fig. 1A).

To confirm the mutant generation, we performed quantitative PCR (qPCR) on genomic DNA, and fractionated products using a bioanalyzer. As illustrated in Fig. 1B, DNA from heterozygous fish exhibited both full-length and truncated alleles. In contrast, wild type and homozygous mutant fish displayed only a single band, corresponding to either the full-length (wild type) or truncated (mutant) form. This pattern was consistently observed for both GCGRa and GCGRb genes, confirming the expected genotypic differences among the fish groups (Fig. 1B). DNA sequencing further confirmed the GCGR−/− double KO fish.

The RNA expression of both GCGRa and GCGRb mutant fish was significantly reduced as demonstrated by whole-mount in situ hybridization (WM-ISH, Fig. 1C). Notably, the loss of GCGRa or GCGRb did not impair embryonic development, and mutant fish were viable and fertile; this phenotype was also observed in GCGR−/− fish. To assess gene expression dynamics, we performed RT-qPCR for GCGRa and GCGRb expression during early embryonic development. Both genes followed a similar pattern with a statistically significant increase at 4-dpf (Fig. 1D). Notably, in mutant fish, we observed a significant reduction in RNA expression at 4- and 6-dpf. Accordingly, we used the 4-dpf for further studies.

Dual KO GCGR−/−, GCGRa−/− and GCGRb−/− alters hepatic gene expression and reveals synergistic effects in metabolic regulation

To investigate the transcriptional changes associated with GCGR−/−, GCGRa−/− and GCGRb−/− KOs, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on whole larvae from wild type (WT) and KO fish models (Fig. 2A). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct clustering of GCGR−/−, GCGRa−/− and GCGRb−/− samples, compared to the WT group, indicating significant transcriptomic differences. The GCGR−/− exhibiting the most distinct separation, suggesting an additive or synergistic effect of dual receptor loss on gene expression (Fig. 2B). Similarly, hierarchical clustering of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) showed distinct expression patterns across the KO models. The heatmap revealed substantial alterations in gene expression, with the GCGRa−/−, GCGRb−/−, and GCGR−/−, groups displaying distinct and overlapping transcriptional changes compared to WT. Notably, the double KO showed the most widespread transcriptomic shift, reinforcing the idea that both receptors contribute to gene regulation (Fig. 2C). Further, comparative analysis using a Venn diagram identified 8,319, 7,318, and 6,307 DEGs in the GCGRa−/−, GCGRb−/− and GCGR−/−groups, respectively, relative to WT (Fig. 2D). Among these, 2,429 genes were commonly dysregulated across all three KO models, indicating a core set of genes regulated by GCGRa−/− and GCGRb−/−. Additionally, 2,096 genes were notably altered in GCGRa−/−, while 1,824 were specific to GCGRb−/−, highlighting receptor-specific effects. The double KO shared substantial overlap with both single KOs but also exhibited 1,805 distinctly dysregulated genes, suggesting additional regulatory effects when both receptors are absent. Volcano plots further illustrated the magnitude of gene expression changes, with significantly upregulated and downregulated genes highlighted for each KO condition. In GCGRa−/− vs. WT (Fig. 2E), key genes such as cyp26a1, igfbp, and carboxypeptidase A were among the most significantly altered. Similarly, in GCGRb−/− vs. WT (Fig. 2F), a significant dysregulation of genes such as cyp26b1, insig1, and hpx was observed. The GCGR−/− (Fig. 2G) exhibited a combination of transcriptional changes observed in both single KOs, along with additional exclusively dysregulated genes, further suggesting that GCGRa−/− and GCGRb−/− function in a cooperative or compensatory manner to regulate hepatic gene expression. Overall, these results reveal that the loss of GCGRa−/− and GCGRb−/− leads to widespread and distinct transcriptional alterations and provides crucial insights into the molecular mechanisms of GCGR signaling and its broader implications in metabolic regulation.

Differential hepatic expression of gcgra and gcgrb under metabolic stress

In adult fish, GCGR isoforms are predominantly expressed in the liver [45]. To further examine whether GCGR isoforms respond to metabolic stress in the liver, we analyzed publicly available RNA-sequencing data from adult zebrafish liver (GEO dataset GSE183023) comparing normally fed and overfed animals. Overfeeding in zebrafish is widely used as a model of nutrient excess and metabolic imbalance.

The analysis confirmed that both GCGRa and GCGRb are expressed in adult zebrafish liver [45]. However, their transcriptional responses differed under metabolic challenge. Hepatic GCGRa expression was significantly decreased in overfed fish compared with controls (Supplemental Fig. 2), indicating that this receptor isoform is responsive to nutrient excess. In contrast, GCGRb expression showed a slight increase in overfed fish, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest distinct regulatory patterns for the two GCGR paralogs in the zebrafish liver, where GCGRa appears to be more sensitive to metabolic stress while GCGRb maintains relatively stable expression.

GCGR isoforms regulate distinct and overlapping hepatic-enriched metabolic pathways

To gain insights into the biological pathways and functional processes affected by GCGRa and GCGRb loss, we performed pathway enrichment analysis on differentially expressed genes from RNA-seq data. Functional annotation of GCGRa−/− upregulated genes revealed significant enrichment of lipid metabolism, oxidoreductase activity, cholesterol biosynthesis, acylglycerol homeostasis, and phospholipid metabolism (Fig. 3A). Additionally, pathways related to glucagon signaling, PI3K-AKT signaling, PPAR signaling and gluconeogenesis were significantly altered, suggesting a compensatory metabolic response to GCGRa loss (Fig. 3A). In contrast, downregulated genes in GCGRa−/− were predominantly associated with visual perception, MAPK signaling, cAMP-mediated signaling, skeletal muscle function, and glutathione metabolism. Notably, processes related to cell cycle regulation, inflammatory response, and sensory perception were also significantly repressed, indicating broader systemic effects of GCGRa loss (Fig. 3B).

Similarly, pathway analysis of GCGRb−/− upregulated genes highlighted key metabolic processes, including lipid metabolism, lipoprotein metabolism, cholesterol biosynthesis, oxidative phosphorylation, and gluconeogenesis. Additional enrichment was observed in response to oxidative stress, hypoxia, extracellular matrix organization, and ECM-receptor interactions, suggesting that GCGRb may play a role in cellular stress response and tissue remodeling (Fig. 3C). Conversely, GCGRb−/− downregulated genes were significantly associated with visual perception, photoreceptor maintenance, steroid biosynthesis, MAPK signaling, GPCR signaling, and cAMP-mediated pathways. These findings suggest that GCGRb may regulate key developmental and signaling pathways beyond its metabolic role (Fig. 3D).

To further dissect the impact of GCGRa and GCGRb KOs on hepatic metabolism, we analyzed the expression patterns of genes involved in gluconeogenesis. The heatmap analysis revealed distinct expression changes across the WT and mutant clones. Several key gluconeogenic genes, including pck1, g6pc, fbp1, and pdk4, were significantly dysregulated in both single and double KOs. Notably, GCGR−/− exhibited the most pronounced alterations, indicating a more substantial disruption of gluconeogenic pathways when both receptors were absent (Fig. 3E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GCGRa and GCGRb regulate overlapping and distinct metabolic pathways. While the GCGR−/− exhibits the most substantial transcriptional shifts in gluconeogenesis, highlighting the cooperative role of both receptors in maintaining hepatic metabolic balance (Fig. 3).

GCGR−/− KO induces extensive metabolic and signaling dysregulation

To further understand the transcriptomic changes induced by the simultaneous loss of GCGR signaling, we analyzed the biological processes and pathways enriched among differentially expressed genes in the GCGR−/− compared to WT. The upregulated DEGs in GCGR−/− were predominantly associated with lipid metabolism, cholesterol biosynthesis, gluconeogenesis, response to hypoxia, and lipoprotein metabolism (Fig. 4A). Other significantly enriched pathways included fatty acid metabolism, phospholipid catabolic processes, steroid biosynthesis, and ECM-receptor interactions, highlighting extensive metabolic reprogramming in response to receptor loss. Additionally, pathways related to cell proliferation, oxidative stress response, and glucose homeostasis were significantly altered, indicating a compensatory mechanism to maintain metabolic balance (Fig. 4A). Conversely, the downregulated DEGs in GCGR−/− were primarily associated with visual perception, lens development, extracellular matrix organization, and response to stimulus, suggesting broader systemic effects beyond metabolic regulation. Other significantly downregulated pathways included serotonin biosynthesis, cAMP-mediated signaling, cell proliferation, apoptosis, and GPCR signaling. Notably, key metabolic pathways such as linoleic acid metabolism, MAPK signaling, PI3K pathways, and retinol metabolism were also significantly repressed, indicating a disruption in signaling networks that regulate energy metabolism and cellular function (Fig. 4B). In addition, all three KO models showed differential expressions in critical metabolic pathways such as PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling, gluconeogenesis, and lipid metabolism (Supplemental Fig. 3).

Disruption of GCGRa and GCGRb alters cholesterol biosynthesis and fatty acid metabolism

To investigate the effects of GCGRa and GCGRb KOs on lipid metabolism, we analyzed the expression of genes involved in cholesterol biosynthesis and fatty acid metabolism. Heatmap analysis revealed substantial transcriptional changes in key genes associated with cholesterol biosynthesis (Fig. 5A) and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 5B). Several cholesterol biosynthetic genes, including hmgcs1, srebf2, lss, insig2, and ceptp, exhibited differential expression across KO models, suggesting that GCGRa and GCGRb regulate cholesterol homeostasis through transcriptional control of biosynthetic enzymes. Notably, the GCGR−/− exhibited the most pronounced shifts in cholesterol biosynthesis-related gene expression, indicating a cumulative or synergistic regulatory effect when both receptors are absent (Fig. 5A). Similarly, genes involved in fatty acid metabolism displayed substantial dysregulation (Fig. 5B). Key genes such as acaca, fasn, acat1, and scd showed altered expression patterns across the KO conditions, suggesting a significant impact on lipid synthesis, oxidation, and transport. The differential regulation of cpt1ab, acadl, and hadhab, which are essential for fatty acid β-oxidation, highlights potential disruptions in lipid catabolism and energy homeostasis due to GCGRa and GCGRb loss.

GCGR isoforms differentially regulate glucose metabolism and peptide responsiveness

To investigate the physiological relevance of GCGRa and/or GCGRb absence in vivo, glucose content was measured at 4-dpf larvae. As shown in Fig. 6A, all mutant lines exhibited a significant reduction in glucose levels compared to WT fish. Specifically, the glucose level was markedly reduced in GCGRa−/− larvae, while GCGRb−/− and GCGR−/− mutants displayed intermediate levels, suggesting partially redundant or distinct roles in glucose regulation.

To assess the functional properties of these receptors, we performed dose–response luciferase assays in HEK293 cells co-transfected with either GCGRa or GCGRb, a CRE-luciferase (Firefly) reporter constructs and Rinella luciferase construct as an internal normalizing control. Cells were then stimulated with increasing concentrations of synthetic zebrafish glucagon (zGCGa) or glucagon like peptide-1 (zGLP1a). In contrast, GCGRb was activated by both zGCGa and zGLP1a (Fig. 6C). These findings indicate that GCGRa is glucagon-selective, while GCGRb has a dual substrate specificity responding to both glucagon and GLP1, suggesting distinct ligand specificity and functional divergence between the isoforms.

Discussion

The zebrafish has emerged as a potent and versatile model for studying GCGR and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP1R) signaling pathways [45–47], providing information on metabolic regulation that is directly relevant to human conditions like diabetes, obesity, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [48]. Our study exploited CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing to generate GCGRa and GCGRb single and double KO in zebrafish, allowing us to delineate their distinct and overlapping roles in metabolic regulation. These models exposed both conserved and species-specific mechanisms with implications for therapeutic development.

Due to teleost-specific genome duplication, zebrafish have two GCGR paralogs, GCGRa and GCGRb, which provide a unique opportunity to study sub-functionalization in glucagon signaling [49]. Our results demonstrate that GCGRb responds to both glucagon and GLP1, which mirrors the ligand multivalent character observed in other teleosts as compared to the strict mammalian GCGR specificity [49, 50]. This receptor-ligand flexibility in evolution may have significant implications for the design of dual-targeting therapies. Despite gene duplication, zebrafish GCGR KOs phenocopy key aspects of mammalian GCGR deficiency, including hypoglycemia, impaired gluconeogenesis, and altered lipid metabolism [8, 10, 18]. These similarities validate the use of zebrafish as a robust model system for studying human metabolic syndromes and underscore its utility in translational research.

The transcriptomic results from the present study revealed that GCGRa and GCGRb regulate discrete but synergistic pathways critical for maintaining metabolic homeostasis. GCGR−/− exhibited the most dramatic metabolic dysregulation, mirroring human GCGR loss-of-function phenotypes, such as improved glucose tolerance in association with dyslipidemia [51, 52]. Notably, GCGRb−/− models mitochondrial impairment by exhibiting disturbance of oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid β-oxidation, while GCGRa−/− models altered PPARγ-regulated adipogenesis, suggesting isoform-specific function in NAFLD pathogenesis [52–54]. Furthermore, the upregulation of PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling in all KO models mimics the adaptive responses observed in human diabetic liver [55, 56], indicative of possible resistance mechanisms to GCGR-based therapies. These data provide a mechanistic rationale to the metabolic consequences of GCGR modulation and highlight the need for pathway-specific therapeutic considerations.

Consistent with the findings from the GCGR KO transcriptomic data, results from our experiments on diet-responsive growth and development attribute a central role for GCGR signaling in diet responsive growth. Under standard feeding conditions, zebrafish lacking GCGR isoforms did not exhibit overt morphological defects, consistent with previous rodent studies involving single or double KOs of GCGR and GLP1R [14], However, when exposed to nutrient overload, GCGR-deficient zebrafish displayed significantly impaired growth. This suggests that GCGR acts as a key mediator in aligning dietary input with growth output, likely through systemic and tissue-level regulation of energy allocation.

In rodent models, the loss of GCGR leads to a metabolically favorable profile, including resistance to diet-induced obesity, improved glucose regulation, and increased energy expenditure. These effects are largely attributed to enhanced GLP1 secretion and reduced hepatic fat accumulation via sympathetic nervous system activation [9, 15]. In contrast, human studies depict a more complex story. While GCGR blockade improves glycemic control, it does not promote weight loss and may even produce adverse effects, such as dyslipidemia, elevated plasma amino acids, and α-cell proliferation [57]. Sipos et al. observed weight loss in only one of six patients with GCGR mutations, underscoring the variability in human responses and the multifaceted role of GCGR signaling [58]. In addition, clinical interventions using small molecule antagonists, antisense oligonucleotides, and monoclonal antibodies have yielded mixed results [52, 57], likely due to differences in hormonal feedback loops and a weaker GLP1 counter-response mechanism in humans.

In addition to their shared evolutionary roots, GLP1R and GCGR have diverged in humans to fulfil more specialized physiological roles. While GCGR primarily regulates metabolic functions, including hepatic glucose output, lipolysis, and ketogenesis, GLP1R plays a broader role by modulating satiety, insulin secretion, and contributing to tissue growth and neurogenesis [59]. The fact that GCGRb is responsive to both GLP1a and GCGa, highlights a retained ancestral functional plasticity that has been lost through receptor specialization in humans. Understanding the dual responsiveness in zebrafish may provide important insight into designing multifunctional drugs that can target simultaneously multiple metabolic pathways, such as energy balance, and promote tissue regeneration.

Analysis of an independent zebrafish liver transcriptomic dataset further supports differential regulation of the two GCGR paralogs. In this dataset, GCGRa liver-expression was reduced in overfed animals, whereas GCGRb showed only a modest, non-significant increase. Although these changes were limited at the transcript level, they suggest that the two receptors may respond differently to metabolic stress. This pattern is consistent with the broader concept that duplicated genes in teleosts often undergo regulatory divergence, potentially allowing distinct contributions to metabolic homeostasis.

Our ligand-response assays confirmed that GCGRb, but not GCGRa, is activated by both GCGa and GLP1a, supporting the post-duplication receptor diversification theory. This finding aligns with the observations of Oren et al. [47]. showing that zebrafish glucagon peptides activate multiple class B G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), indicative of functional plasticity following genome duplication. In addition, recent work by Liu et al. [60] reported differential ligand selectivity between zebrafish GCGR paralogs, with GCGRa exhibiting higher glucagon specificity and GCGRb displaying broader responsiveness to both glucagon and GLP-1, further supporting the model proposed here.

Li et al. initially characterized GCGRa and GCGRb functions utilizing TALEN technology, showing that loss of either gene leads to α-cell proliferation, mild hypoglycemia, and hyperglucagonemia [45]. Our results validate these findings, as GCGR KO zebrafish lines displayed significantly reduced glucose levels at 3-dpf. A notable key distinction lies in the timing when GCGR gene expression peaks: while Li et al. [48]. reported steady early embryonic expression, our RT-qPCR data revealed the expression peak at 4-dpf. This divergence can be attributed to the RT-qPCR method sensitivity used in our study, compared to the standard RT-PCR protocol used by Li et al. In addition, we cannot rule out the possibility that, at later developmental stages, the total pool of RNA become enriched with RNA from other pathways diluting the relative abundance of GCGR transcripts [61].

In conclusion, the reported zebrafish GCGR models offer an integrative system for elucidating the multifaceted roles of glucagon signaling in metabolic regulation. By delineating the distinct functions of GCGRa and GCGRb, we established a template for precision targeting of the receptors in metabolic disease, emphasizing the need for balancing efficacy against safety. These findings not only advance our fundamental knowledge of GCGR/GLP1R biology but also provide new opportunities for disease-altering therapies that exploit evolutionary insight to improve human health. The zebrafish model, with its unique genetic and physiological features, remains an indispensable tool for understanding the complexities of metabolic diseases and the development targeted interventions.

Study limitations

Several limitations exist in this study. First, rescue experiments are not performed; therefore, although the knockout phenotypes presented are robust, direct causal validation through receptor re-expression could not be achieved due to current technical constraints associated with Tol2-based transgenesis. Second, ligand–response analyses were limited to zGCG and zGLP1 and did not include additional synthetic or endogenous peptides of other species, nor were they complemented by detailed kinetic profiling, which may enhance the receptors specificity and signaling dynamics. Third, systemic metabolic measurements such as circulating lipids, glucose, amino acids, and hormone levels under different dietary or stress conditions were not included because these measurements are not technically feasible in the embryos of zebrafish due to the extremely low blood volume and the immature circulatory and endocrine systems. Fourth, functional studies were largely confined to the embryonic and larval stages, and potential adult-specific phenotypes remain to be identified. Additionally, our future studies using isolated liver tissue fish and/or single-cell approaches will be required to resolve the tissue-autonomous effects on the liver metabolism. Finally, energy-balance-related behavioral and physiological assays, including locomotor activity, feeding behavior, and stress responses, were not performed at embryonic stages due to limited available tools and the strong influence of ongoing neurodevelopmental maturation, which limits their interpretability in the context of systemic metabolic homeostasis, nevertheless, it worth studying the behavioral and stress on adult fish.

Despite these limitations, our work presents the first comprehensive framework for specifically addressing the zebrafish GCGRa and GCGRb in vivo function from both the genetic and transcription-level perspectives.
