 What is the dGen Tool?

• The Distributed Generation Market Demand (dGen)
  model simulates customer adoption of distributed
  energy resources (DERs) for residential, commercial,
  and industrial entities in the United States or other
  countries through 2050. (NREL 2025)

• Examples of DERs include:​
• Solar photovoltaic (PV)​
• Wind​
• Energy storage​
• Geothermal heap pumps
• The model supports many types of analysis and can
  help answer a range of DER-related questions.

 How dGen Works

1. dGen considers factors that affect a person’s choice to adopt distributed
 generation technologies:
 ◦ It saves money
 ◦ Whether their neighbors have solar panels
 ◦ If the person considers themselves to be innovative.
2. Using an agent-based approach—agents in this sense represent individual
 decision makers in a given location—it looks at real data about things like:
 ◦ Electricity use
 ◦ Demographics
 ◦ Property assessments
 ◦ Roof scans.
3. Then dGen models different combinations of future situations, such as
 lower distributed generation technology prices or different tax incentives,
 to simulate how the agent, and the rest of agent’s community, makes
 decisions—all the way out to the year 2050. (NREL 2021)

 Technology Adoption

• dGen projects the adoption of technologies based on the “diffusion of
 innovations” framework.
 o Novel technologies “diffuse” into populations following a
 logistic pattern:
 ▪ Early adopters
 ▪ Mass adoption
 ▪ Late adopters.
 o Diffusion is based on the financial attractiveness of the
 investment. (Bass, 1969)
 o The technology never diffuses completely through an entire
 population, as there are some consumers for whom the
 technology is not relevant or beneficial. (Rogers, 2003)
• dGen generates a model of potential adoption of a technology across
 the residential, commercial, and industrial sectors. (NREL 2020)

 Load Forecasting and Integrated Resource Planning

• Projecting how many DERs are adopted and where they
 are located is a critical input for utility peak-load
 forecasting and integrated resource planning.
• For example, solar adoption forecasts are used to
 estimated future load.
• These forecasted loads are used to identify an optimal set
 of resources to procure over the planning period.
• Erroneous assumptions about DER adoption have
 significant impacts on the resources that are procured, as
 well as the resulting operation of the utility system.

Example:
• A recent study demonstrated that the costs of incorrectly
  forecasting rooftop solar capacity are high, with an average
  net present savings (money saved over time) of $2.3
  million/terawatt hour of utility sales. (NREL 2025)

 Policy Analysis

 The dGen model can be used to explore the sensitivity of various DERs to
 policy and market factors.

Example:
• NV Energy’s changes to their net energy metering tariffs
  increase fixed charges and reduce credit for exported

• NV Energy’s new net energy metering tariff was

Locational Value of Distributed Energy Resources (DER)

 • All technologies modeled within the dGen framework leverage a
 database of highly resolved geospatial information and use an
 agent-based approach.
 • Because each agent is assigned an actual location, it can be
 associated with several attributes from data sets in the underlying
 geospatial database using a simple spatial overlay.
 • The spatial layers include, but are not limited to, energy
 consumption profiles, census demographics, zoning and building
 counts, and resources.
 • The combination of these spatial layers allows dGen to identify
 the most promising sites and regions for DER deployment.
 • The model can easily be trained on new sources of data and
 applied at multiple spatial resolutions—national, state, and even
 Using load, wind resource, and siting availability spatial layers,
 distribution-feeder level. (NREL 2025) dGen identifies distributed wind economic potential in Minnesota.

Case Studies

Distributed Solar Adoption in Orlando: A Household-Level
 Model for Distribution Resource Planning

Description: Orlando requested an analysis used
LiDAR roof scans, customer level electricity
consumption, property assessment, and other
data to provide a solar adoption forecast at the
household level.
Technology: Solar
Impact: In the scenario 248 MW of rooftop solar
PV adoption was projected by 2030, rising to 370
MW by 2050.
• Most of this adoption is concentrated in the
  single-family owner-occupied residential sector.
  (NREL, 2021)                                       Image from NREL, PR-6A20-77308, 2021

Assessment of the Economic Potential of Distributed Wind in
 Colorado, Minnesota, and New York

  distributions of economic potential for behind-the-meter
  distributed wind energy systems serving primarily rural or
