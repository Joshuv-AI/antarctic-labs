Steerable but Not Decodable: Function Vectors Operate Beyond the Logit Lens
Mohammed Suhail B Nadaf

Abstract
Function vectors (FVs)—mean-difference directions extracted from in-context learning demonstrations—can steer large language model behavior when added to the residual stream. We hypothesized that FV steering failures reflect an absence of task-relevant information: the logit lens (projecting intermediate activations through the model’s unembedding) would fail alongside steering. We were wrong. In the most comprehensive cross-template FV transfer study to date—4,032 pairs across 12 tasks, 6 models from 3 families (Llama-3.1-8B, Gemma-2-9B, Mistral-7B-v0.3; base and instruction-tuned), 8 templates per task—we find the opposite dissociation: FV steering succeeds even when the logit lens cannot decode the correct answer at any layer. This steerability-without-decodability pattern is universal: steering exceeds logit lens accuracy for every task on every model, with gaps as large as − 0.91 -0.91 . Only 3 of 72 task × \times model instances show the predicted “decodable without steerable” pattern (all Mistral). FV vocabulary projection reveals that FVs achieving > 0.90 >0.90 steering accuracy still project to incoherent token distributions, indicating FVs encode computational instructions rather than answer directions . FVs intervene optimally at early layers (L2–L8); the logit lens detects correct answers only at late layers (L28–L32). The previously reported negative cosine-transfer correlation ( r = − 0.572 r=-0.572 ) dissolves at scale: pooled r r ranges from − 0.199 -0.199 to + 0.126 +0.126 , and cosine adds Δ ​ R 2 ≤ 0.011 \Delta R^{2}\leq 0.011 beyond task identity. Post-steering logit lens analysis reveals a model-family divergence: Mistral FVs rewrite intermediate representations (delta up to + 0.91 +0.91 ); Llama/Gemma FVs produce near-zero changes despite successful steering—evidence for two distinct mechanisms. Activation patching confirms causal localization: easy tasks achieve perfect recovery ( 1.000 1.000 ) at targeted layers; hard tasks show zero recovery everywhere. These findings challenge the assumption that additive steering operates through linearly decodable subspaces.

Introduction

Activation steering—adding a learned direction to a model’s hidden states at inference time—is an emerging tool for controlling large language model (LLM) behavior without retraining.
Applications range from refusal induction
[
1
]
to truthfulness enhancement
[
13
]
and representation engineering
[
31
]
.
The approach rests on the
linear representation hypothesis

[
22
]
: that task-relevant behaviors correspond to linear directions in activation space, so that a “function vector” (FV) extracted from in-context learning demonstrations
[
9
,
26
]
can reliably steer the model when added to its hidden states.

For safety-critical deployments, the relevant test is not whether an FV works on the same prompt template from which it was extracted, but whether it transfers to
different
template formulations of the same task.
An adversary need only rephrase a prompt to evade a template-specific steering intervention
[
23
,
30
]
.
Yet prior work evaluates FVs almost exclusively in-distribution
[
9
,
26
,
14
]
.

A preliminary study
[
18
]
evaluated cross-template transfer across 3 tasks and 8 templates on Llama-3.1-8B, finding a Simpson’s paradox: the aggregate negative correlation between cosine similarity and transfer accuracy (
r=-0.572
) dissolved within individual tasks, driven by the fact that 2 of 3 tasks had near-zero in-distribution steering accuracy.
However, that study suffered from several limitations: only 3 tasks (1 working, 2 failing), a single model family, shared template surface forms across tasks, no diagnostic analysis to distinguish information absence from intervention failure, and hardcoded analysis thresholds.

We address all of these limitations.
Our study evaluates 12 tasks spanning 5 principled computational categories, 6 models from 3 families, with task-specific templates, logit lens analysis, FV vocabulary projection, independent baselines, and data-derived thresholds.
We frame four research questions:

1.

For which computational task types does mean-difference FV extraction succeed, and for which does it fail?

2.

Is cross-template transfer success predicted by geometric alignment (cosine similarity), or by in-distribution steering efficacy?

3.

When FV steering succeeds, can the model’s own logit lens decode the correct answer at intermediate layers—or does the FV operate through pathways invisible to the model’s output head?

4.

How do these patterns vary across model families and between base
vs.
instruction-tuned variants?

Question 3 is the most novel—and produced the most surprising answer.
By combining FV steering evaluation with the logit lens—the model’s own unembedding applied at each intermediate layer—we construct a
2\times 2
matrix: {logit lens decodes correctly, logit lens fails}
\times
{FV succeeds, FV fails}.
We expected the upper-right cell—
decodability without steerability
—to be populated.
Instead, it is empty on 4 of 6 models and contains only 3 of 72 task
\times
model instances (all Mistral).
The lower-left cell—
steerability without decodability
—is the actual finding (10/72 instances): FVs steer behavior through representational subspaces that the model’s own unembedding cannot decode at any intermediate layer.
Unlike linear probing, the logit lens uses no learned parameters, sidestepping the probe complexity critique
[
11
]
.

Contributions.

1.

The most comprehensive cross-template FV transfer study to date: 12 tasks, 6 models, 672 cross-template pairs per model (4,032 total), with pre-registered difficulty predictions and honest assessment of prediction failures.

2.

A
steerability-without-decodability dissociation
: additive FV steering succeeds for tasks where the logit lens (the model’s own unembedding)
cannot
decode the correct output at any intermediate layer. The predicted opposite cell (decodable without steerable) has only 3 instances across all models (all Mistral, for reverse_word and sentiment_flip). This demonstrates that FVs operate through non-unembedding-aligned subspaces invisible to standard interpretability tools.

3.

FV vocabulary projection
: projecting extracted function vectors through the model’s unembedding reveals universally incoherent token distributions—even for FVs achieving
>
0.90 steering accuracy—providing direct evidence that FVs encode
computational instructions
rather than answer directions.

4.

Demonstration that the previously reported negative cosine-transfer correlation (
r=-0.572
) was a small-sample artifact that dissolves at 4
\times
task scale: cosine adds
\Delta R^{2}\leq 0.011
beyond task identity.

5.

Cross-model validation across 3 families (Llama, Gemma, Mistral) showing the inverted dissociation, null geometry result, and template-style invariance are architecture-general findings, plus a novel model-family mechanistic divergence revealed by post-steering logit lens analysis.

6.

Activation patching
localizing where cross-template transfer is causally concentrated: tasks with high IID accuracy show perfect recovery at specific layers, while genuinely hard tasks show zero recovery everywhere—confirming that the difficulty hierarchy reflects causal localization, not just statistical correlation.

7.

A concrete evaluation protocol and open-source codebase for validating steering interventions.

Background and Related Work

Function vectors and in-context learning.

Hendel et al. 2023
demonstrated that in-context learning (ICL) creates task-specific directions in activation space.

Todd et al. 2024
extended this to function vectors: mean-difference vectors between ICL and non-ICL activations that, when added to the residual stream, induce task behavior without demonstrations.

Liu et al. 2023
proposed in-context vectors for more controllable steering.
All prior work evaluates FVs in-distribution; we extend to systematic cross-template evaluation across 12 tasks and 6 models.

Activation steering and the linear representation hypothesis.

Turner et al. 2023
introduced activation addition as an optimization-free steering method.

Panickssery et al. 2023
applied contrastive activation addition to Llama 2.

Zou et al. 2023
proposed representation engineering as a general control framework.

Arditi et al. 2024
showed refusal behavior is mediated by a single direction.

Park et al. 2023
articulated the linear representation hypothesis;
Marks & Tegmark 2023
found linear structure in truth-value representations;
Tigges et al. 2023
demonstrated linear representations of sentiment;
Hernandez et al. 2024
studied linearity of relation representations.
We decompose this hypothesis into two separable claims:
linear decodability
(information is decodable by the model’s own unembedding at intermediate layers) and
linear steerability
(behavior can be induced by an additive intervention), and show they come apart—but in the opposite direction from what naive intuition predicts.

Probing, the logit lens, and vocabulary projection.

Belinkov 2022
surveyed probing classifiers.

Hewitt & Liang 2019
raised concerns about probe complexity—a high-capacity probe may “find” information that is not linearly accessible to the model itself.
The
logit lens

[
17
]
avoids this by projecting intermediate activations through the model’s own final LayerNorm and unembedding matrix
W_{U}
, requiring no learned parameters.

Belrose et al. 2023
proposed the
tuned lens
with per-layer affine corrections; we use the parameter-free version.
Vocabulary-space analysis has been applied to MLP layers as key-value memories
[
7
]
and weight matrix analysis
[
5
]
.
We extend this to projecting
function vectors
through
W_{U}
, revealing what tokens each FV “votes for”—a diagnostic that, to our knowledge, has not been explored in prior work.

Mechanistic interpretability and prompt sensitivity.

Our patching methodology follows causal tracing
[
16
,
6
]
.

Wang et al. 2022
analyzed IOI circuits;
Olsson et al. 2022
identified induction heads;
Geva et al. 2023
dissected factual recall.

Sclar et al. 2023
quantified prompt formatting sensitivity; our work extends prompt sensitivity analysis to the internal geometry of steering vectors.
Simpson’s paradox
[
24
]
in ML evaluation was first identified in activation steering by our preliminary work
[
18
]
; we now test whether it persists at
4\times
scale.

3.4
Function Vector Extraction and Steering

We extract FVs using the mean-difference method
[
26
]
.
For task
t
, template
k
, and layer
\ell
:

\text{FV}_{t,k,\ell}\;=\;\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{h}_{\ell}^{(\text{pos})}\bigr]-\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{h}_{\ell}^{(\text{neg})}\bigr],

(1)

where
\mathbf{h}_{\ell}^{(\text{pos})}
and
\mathbf{h}_{\ell}^{(\text{neg})}
are residual stream activations at layer
\ell
and the final token position for positive (15 correct ICL demos) and negative (15 incorrect demos) prompts respectively.
At inference time, we add the FV with scalar multiplier
\alpha
:

\mathbf{h}_{\ell}^{\prime}\;=\;\mathbf{h}_{\ell}+\alpha\cdot\text{FV}_{t,k,\ell}.

(2)

We sweep
\alpha\in\{0.5,1.0,1.5,2.0,2.5,3.0,4.0,5.0\}
with refinement around the best
\alpha
and report best accuracy across all
\alpha
and layers.

IID
evaluation uses the same template for extraction and test;
OOD
(cross-template) uses different templates (
56
directed pairs per task).

Definition 1

(IID gating)
.

A task
t
on model
m

passes the IID gate
if its mean IID accuracy across templates exceeds
\tau=0.10
.
Tasks failing the gate are excluded from causal analysis.

3.5
Logit Lens and FV Vocabulary Projection

To distinguish
information absence
from
intervention failure
, we apply two parameter-free analyses to zero-shot activations.

Logit lens.
For each task, template, and layer
\ell
, we project the zero-shot residual stream through the model’s own final LayerNorm and unembedding:

\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\ell}=\text{LayerNorm}_{\text{final}}(\mathbf{h}_{\ell})\cdot W_{U}+\mathbf{b}_{U}.

(3)

We measure top-
k
accuracy (
k\in\{1,5,10\}
): the fraction of examples where the correct output token appears among the
k
highest-scored tokens.
No training data or hyperparameters are required.
For sentiment_flip, where first-token accuracy is uninformative, we use a contrast vector approach measuring sentiment polarity classification accuracy (details in Appendix
I
).

FV vocabulary projection.
We project each FV through the same pipeline:

\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{t,k,\ell}=\text{LayerNorm}_{\text{final}}(\text{FV}_{t,k,\ell})\cdot W_{U}+\mathbf{b}_{U}.

(4)

The top-50 tokens by score reveal what the FV “pushes toward.”
We compute the
correct output fraction
: the proportion matching any correct task output.

These analyses produce the
2\times 2
steerability-decodability matrix (Table

).

Universal template vector analysis.

The steerability-decodability gap direction (steering
>

But the
direction

Across all 6 models, the “decodable without steerable” cell is nearly empty (3/72), while “steerable without decodable” is substantially populated (10/72).

5.4
Post-Steering Logit Lens Reveals Model-Family Divergence

A natural follow-up question: when FV steering succeeds, does it
create
logit-lens-readable information at downstream layers?
We apply the logit lens to post-steering activations and compute the delta from zero-shot readings.
This reveals a striking model-family divergence.

Llama/Gemma: near-zero delta.

For “steerable-not-readable” tasks on Llama/Gemma, FV injection barely changes the logit lens reading.
On Llama Base first_letter, the maximum post-steering top-10 accuracy is
0.035
across all templates and layers—essentially zero.
On Llama Base country_capital, max post-steering readability is
0.078
—near floor.
The FV steers the final output without leaving a trace in the logit lens at intermediate layers, consistent with the FV operating through a non-representational channel (attention reweighting, MLP gating, or a subspace orthogonal to
W_{U}
).

Mistral: massive delta.

For the same category of tasks on Mistral, FVs massively rewrite internal representations.
On Mistral IT object_color, the FV causes logit lens readability to jump from
0.012
to
0.918
(T7), a delta of
+0.906
.
On Mistral IT antonym, delta reaches
+0.895
.
The FV doesn’t just steer output—it rewrites intermediate representations so thoroughly that the logit lens can now decode the correct answer at
>90
% accuracy.

Delta magnitude as a predictor.

Post-steering logit lens delta correlates strongly with IID steering accuracy (
r=0.61
–
0.88
across models,
p<0.05
on all), far exceeding the predictive power of cosine alignment (
r<0.20
within-task).
This suggests that what matters for FV steering is not how similar source and target FVs are, but how strongly the source FV perturbs the model’s internal representations.

These results are consistent with a
dual-mechanism hypothesis
: Mistral FVs write directly into the residual stream subspace spanned by
W_{U}
(representational), while Llama/Gemma FVs alter attention routing or MLP gating without changing logit-lens-visible representations (modulatory).

6
Discussion

6.1
Decomposing the Linear Representation Hypothesis

Our results show that the linear representation hypothesis
[
22
]
conflates two separable claims:
(1)
Linear decodability:
task-relevant information can be decoded from the residual stream by the model’s own unembedding.
(2)
Linear steerability:
task behavior can be induced by an additive intervention.
These come apart in the opposite direction from what we predicted:
steerability without decodability
, not the reverse.
The gap is universally negative, with extremes of
-0.91
(first_letter on Llama IT: FV accuracy
0.960
, logit lens
0.047
) and
-0.82
(country_capital on Llama Base: FV accuracy
0.880
, logit lens
0.056
).

Three lines of evidence converge on the interpretation that FVs encode
computational instructions
rather than
answer directions
:
(1) Incoherent vocabulary projections: FVs project to garbage tokens even at
>0.90
steering accuracy.
(2) Early-layer intervention, late-layer readability: FVs work best at L2–L8, logit lens peaks at L28–L32.
The 20+-layer temporal gap between optimal intervention and detectable output is consistent with the FV encoding a process that requires many layers to execute, not an answer that could be detected immediately.
(3) Template-style invariance (
p>0.30
on all models): the FV captures an abstract, style-invariant instruction.

6.2
The Simpson’s Paradox at Scale

The preliminary study
[
18
]
reported a pooled negative correlation of
r=-0.572
.
At
4\times
task scale, this dissolves.
The pooled
r
is now
+0.013
(Llama Base),
+0.058
(Gemma Base),
+0.126
(Gemma IT),
+0.070
(Llama IT),
-0.199
(Mistral Base), and
-0.187
(Mistral IT).
The original negative correlation was a small-sample artifact: with only 3 tasks (1 working, 2 failing), the between-task composition effect dominated.
At 12 tasks, hierarchical regression shows task identity explains
40
–
59
% of transfer variance, while cosine adds
\Delta R^{2}\leq 0.011
.

Mistral’s negative pooled
r
is genuine and structurally interesting.
It arises not because alignment predicts worse transfer, but from task-level composition: easy tasks cluster at high alignment and high transfer; hard tasks scatter at varied alignment and low transfer; the mix tilts negative.
The within-task mean
r
for Mistral IT is
positive
(
+0.139
), confirming a true Simpson’s paradox within that model family.

6.3
Cross-Model Comparison and the Dual-Mechanism Hypothesis

The broad patterns—template style invariance, cosine irrelevance, steering
>
readability gap direction—hold across all 6 models.
The most model-dependent task is hypernym (range:
0.085
on Llama Base to
0.902
on Gemma Base), demonstrating that task category predicts success only as a proxy for model competence.
Instruction tuning consistently improves FV steering (mean
\Delta
:
+0.21
Llama,
+0.09
Gemma,
+0.07
Mistral) without changing the fundamental dissociation pattern.

Three independent lines of evidence converge on a mechanistic divergence between model families:
(1)
Post-steering logit lens
: Mistral FVs massively rewrite the residual stream into
W_{U}
-readable form (delta up to
+0.91
); Llama/Gemma FVs produce near-zero changes (Section
5.4
).
(2)
Activation patching
: Llama models show higher patching recovery (
0.275
–
0.295
) than Gemma IT (
0.101
), suggesting Llama concentrates cross-template computation at discrete layers while Gemma distributes it (Section
5.5
).
(3)
FV norms
: Gemma FV L2 norms (
{\sim}280
) are
15
–
30\times
larger than Mistral’s (
{\sim}10
), yet Gemma’s norms correlate weakly negatively with transfer (
r=-0.03
to
-0.05
), while Mistral IT is the only model with a positive norm-transfer correlation (
r=+0.13
).

Three independent lines of evidence—post-steering logit lens deltas, patching recovery rates, and FV norm-transfer correlations—converge on a
dual-mechanism hypothesis
: Mistral FVs operate
representationally
, writing answer-related information directly into the residual stream subspace that
W_{U}
reads; Llama/Gemma FVs operate
modulatorily
, altering attention routing or MLP gating without leaving logit-lens-visible traces.
This distinction echoes findings from circuit-level mechanistic work:
Geva et al. 2022
and
Geva et al. 2023
show that MLP layers write to the residual stream in vocabulary space, while attention heads primarily route information—consistent with the possibility that Mistral FVs activate MLP-like pathways while Llama/Gemma FVs engage attention-routing pathways.
The Gemma norms may reflect an architectural scaling convention rather than a mechanistic difference—the critical variable is not magnitude but alignment with
W_{U}
.
Directly testing this via attention-head-level patching (which heads mediate the FV effect on each family?) is a concrete next step, but the convergent evidence already establishes the divergence as a systematic finding rather than noise.

6.4
Implications for AI Safety

•

Steering effects may be invisible to standard monitoring.
FVs steer behavior through pathways the logit lens cannot detect—especially on Llama/Gemma. Safety monitoring relying on vocabulary-projection tools may miss FV-style interventions entirely.

•

FV vocabulary projection is not a reliable quality check.
FVs achieving
>0.90
accuracy project to incoherent tokens. A practitioner inspecting the projection would incorrectly conclude the FV is broken.

•

Verify IID efficacy before deployment.
IID accuracy is the single best predictor of FV quality. Geometric similarity (cosine) and vocabulary projection tell you nothing. Only behavioral evaluation is reliable.

6.5
Limitations

All tasks require single-token or short outputs; open-ended generation may differ.
All models are 7–9B parameters; larger models may develop more unembedding-aligned representations, narrowing the gap.
We evaluate only the mean-difference extraction method; other methods (contrastive activation addition, learned steering vectors) may yield different profiles.
The logit lens at early layers may inherently fail because intermediate representations are not yet in a form the final unembedding can decode—the tuned lens
[
3
]
could partially address this by learning per-layer affine corrections, but doing so would introduce learned parameters and expose our analysis to the probe complexity critique
[
11
]
we specifically designed the logit lens approach to avoid; we therefore report logit lens results only and leave tuned lens comparison as future work.
Eight templates per task, while substantially more than prior work, do not cover adversarial phrasings.

6.6
Future Work

Key extensions include: (1) Tuned lens comparison—would learned per-layer affine corrections detect what the logit lens misses? (2) Attention-head-level patching to directly test the dual-mechanism hypothesis: if Llama/Gemma FVs operate modulatorily, specific attention heads should mediate the cross-template effect. (3) Scaling to 70B+ models to test whether the steerability-decodability gap narrows as models develop richer unembedding-aligned representations. (4) Connection to sparse autoencoders
[
4
]
: do SAE features detect what the logit lens misses? (5) Alternative extraction methods (contrastive activation addition, PCA) on the same task battery.

7
Conclusion

We conducted the most comprehensive study of cross-template function vector transfer to date, evaluating 12 tasks spanning 5 computational categories across 6 models from 3 families.
We designed the study to test whether the logit lens could explain FV steering failures.
The data answered with a surprise: the dissociation between decodability and steerability exists, but in the opposite direction.

Our central finding is a
steerability-without-decodability inversion
: FV steering succeeds even when the logit lens—the model’s own unembedding with no learned parameters—cannot decode the correct output at any layer.
This gap is universal: steering exceeds readability for every task on every model, with gaps as large as
-0.91
.
The predicted opposite pattern (decodable without steerable) has only 3 of 72 instances.
FV vocabulary projection reveals that even highly effective FVs project to incoherent token distributions, suggesting FVs encode computational instructions that the model’s nonlinear processing executes through pathways invisible to the unembedding.
Activation patching provides causal confirmation: tasks with strong steering concentrate cross-template transfer at specific residual stream layers (perfect recovery), while hard tasks show zero recovery everywhere.
Post-steering logit lens analysis reveals a model-family mechanistic divergence: Mistral FVs massively rewrite intermediate representations, while Llama/Gemma FVs operate through non-representational channels.
The previously reported negative cosine-transfer correlation dissolves at scale (pooled
r
:
-0.199
to
+0.126
;
\Delta R^{2}\leq 0.011
), and thousands of individual dissociation cases—template pairs with high cosine alignment (
>0.80
) but failed transfer—confirm that geometric similarity is not functionally predictive.

The practical implication: not only must practitioners verify IID efficacy before deploying steering vectors, but they should not rely on standard interpretability tools (logit lens, vocabulary projection) to validate or monitor steering effects.
FVs can steer model behavior through subspaces that these tools are blind to—a finding with direct relevance for both the capabilities and safety of activation steering.

Appendix I
Statistical Details

I.1
Logit Lens Computation

For each example
i
at layer
\ell
, we compute
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\ell}^{(i)}=\text{LayerNorm}_{\text{final}}(\mathbf{h}_{\ell}^{(i)})\cdot W_{U}+\mathbf{b}_{U}
and sort logits in descending order.
A prediction counts as top-
k
correct if the correct output token’s ID appears among the
k
highest-scoring token IDs.
For multi-label tasks (synonym, hypernym, object_color), we accept any valid alternative’s first token.

Sentiment polarity (sentiment_flip).

We construct a contrast vector
\mathbf{d}_{\text{sent}}=(\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{\text{neg}}-\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{\text{pos}})/\|\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{\text{neg}}-\bar{\mathbf{e}}_{\text{pos}}\|
from mean token embeddings.
Polarity classification accuracy is the fraction of examples where
\text{sign}(\cos(\text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{h}_{\ell}),\mathbf{d}_{\text{sent}}))
matches the expected sentiment direction.

I.2
Multiple Comparisons

We apply Bonferroni correction wherever multiple hypothesis tests are conducted:
within-task correlations (12 tests,
\alpha_{\text{corrected}}=0.0042
),
logit lens
vs.
FV paired tests (12 tests,
\alpha_{\text{corrected}}=0.0042
).
