BPA-induced differential methylation of the androgen receptor promoter CpG island in medaka germline and somatic cells
Authors: Anjana Giri, Jayasree Nath, Ramji K Bhandari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe androgen receptors (Ar) are fundamental mediators of androgen signaling, critical for male fertility. Environmental endocrine disruptors (EEDs) can impair male fertility; however, it remains unclear whether EED-induced fertility impairments are linked to DNA methylation alterations in the ar promoter within germ cells, or if a germline-to-soma transfer of these epigenetic profiles affects ar transcription in the testis leading to fertility impairment. To address this, we exposed medaka (Oryzias latipes) to bisphenol A (BPA, 100 µg/l, an EED) and determined fertility defects, testis histology, DNA methylation alterations in the CpG island of arα promoter, and mRNA levels of arα mRNA in the testicular germ cells and soma. Reduced fertility was observed in F0 and F2 generations, which was accompanied by alterations in the testicular tubular structure and germ cell landscape. In F0 males, the testicular germ cells maintained increased levels of DNA methylation on the CpG island of the arα promoter at the ground state of epigenetic reprogramming of germ cells (15 days post-fertilization, dpf) and as such in sperm. In F2 males, testis showed elevated expression of DNA methyltransferase enzyme genes. F2 germ cells maintained significantly higher levels of DNA methylation. The elevated DNA methylation profile was maintained in testicular somatic cells and correlated with significantly decreased arα mRNA levels. The present results demonstrate that ancestral BPA exposure induces transgenerational male subfertility by altering the methylation profile of arα promoter and gene expression, highlighting the profound risk EEDs pose to reproductive health in vertebrates and population stability in wildlife.

Introduction

Androgens are essential for male reproductive function. Hypothalamic gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) stimulates the release of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) from the anterior pituitary. In mammals, LH promotes androgen production, primarily testosterone, dihydrotestosterone (DHT), and androstenedione from the testes [1, 2]. In zebrafish (Danio rerio), 11-ketotestosterone (11-KT, a non-aromatizable androgen), 11β-hydroxyandrostenedione (OHA), and 11-ketoandrostenedione (OA), are key androgenic products [3]. Fathead minnows possess a specialized steroidogenic pathway that converts testosterone into potent 11-oxygenated androgens, particularly 11-KT, which exhibits stronger testicular activity than testosterone or DHT [4]. In medaka and many other teleosts, 11-KT is considered the dominant androgen, which is essential for spermatogenesis, secondary sexual characteristics, and male reproductive function [5–9]. Androgens bind to the intracellular androgen receptor in the Sertoli cells and germ cells, triggering transcriptional programs that initiate and maintain spermatogenesis and promote secondary sexual characteristics [10, 11]. A female-typical external appearance, undescended testes located in abdominal cavity, disrupted germ cell development, and reduced weights of the anterior and dorsal lateral lobes of the epididymis and vas deferens were found in the Ar knockout mice [12]. The ar knockout male zebrafish develop reduced testicular size, decreased sperm motility, delayed spermatogenesis characterized by an accumulation of spermatogonia, reduced numbers of spermatocytes and spermatozoa, smaller spermatogenetic cysts, and spermatogenic arrest at the second meiotic division [13, 14]. In the medaka, the loss of ar impacted male fertility, testicular morphology, and courtship behavior [7, 15]. Hence, accumulating evidence suggests that androgens play a crucial role in male fertility in both fish and mammals.

Epidemiological and laboratory animal studies have reported decreased sperm count, infertility, testicular dysgenesis syndrome, and other reproductive disorders due to exposure to environmental endocrine disruptors (EEDs) [16,17]. EEDs can alter the androgen signaling pathway. Bisphenol A (BPA), an EED, is widely used as a chemical additive in the manufacturing of plastic materials and possesses both estrogenic and anti-androgenic properties [18–21]. BPA has been detected in the majority of the urine and blood samples collected from all age groups, children and adults with 7.1 μg/l (95th percentile) [22–25], suggesting its ubiquitous presence and potential environmental contamination. Although the endocrine toxicity of BPA in humans has been debated for many decades, the presence of BPA in the blood and urine of humans with various reproductive and metabolic health effects [20,26], and the ability of BPA at varying concentrations to cause reproductive and metabolic impairments in laboratory animals clearly suggest the reproductive health risks posed by BPA [20, 27–29]. BPA-induced reproductive defects have been demonstrated to be heritable, affecting the reproduction in the unexposed offspring of the exposed parents and grandparents [30–34], further illustrating the far-reaching reproductive health effects of BPA. Mechanistically, BPA disrupts androgen receptor signaling by enhancing androgen receptor-silencing mediator for retinoid and thyroid receptors (AR-SMRT) and androgen receptor-nuclear receptor corepressor (AR-NCoR) interactions, which inhibits AR function and Sertoli cell proliferation—an essential process for germ cell maturation [16, 18]. Studies have reported a genomewide increase in DNA methylation in mouse spermatocyte in response to higher BPA doses [35, 36]. In addition to global hypomethylation of sperm DNA, aberrant DNA methylation of long QT intronic transcript 1 (Lit1), paternally expressed gene 3 (Peg3), zinc-finger protein that regulates apoptosis, and cell cycle arrest (Zac) have been associated with oligozoospermia and abnormal protamine ratios in men with male infertility [36], indicating widespread disruption of epigenetic landscape in affected individuals showing that epigenetic fidelity is required throughout germ cell development. Therefore, it is imperative to study the effects of EEDs, including BPA, on the establishment of differential DNA methylation and its maintenance throughout the lifespan of reproductive cells [37–39].

EEDs can induce intra-, inter-, and transgenerational reproductive effects in laboratory animals ranging from fish to mammals [40, 41]. Effects of environmental or dietary stressors can be passed on to unexposed offspring via germline transmission and transgenerational epigenetic inheritance (TEI). Most transgenerational epigenetic effects have been found to be mediated by male germline [30, 31, 42–44], suggesting that EEDs affect the epigenetic profile of developing male gametes undergoing sexual differentiation of germ cells, particularly at the time of genomewide epigenetic reprogramming [41]. In medaka, the methylome undergoes reprogramming in post-fertilization embryos and in germ cells in a manner comparable to that observed in mammals, where DNA demethylation begins immediately after fertilization, erasing sperm genome methylation marks in the first cell cycle [37, 45, 46]. The embryonic genome remains hypomethylated until 16-cell stage, then gradually regains methylation through gastrulation, signaling active genomic regulation [45, 47, 48]. Epigenetic reprogramming occurs in somatic cells and germplasm of embryos at post-fertilization cleavage stages and in developing germ cells during sex determination, respectively [45, 49, 50]. Epigenetic reprogramming of embryonic soma and germline cells is sensitive to extrinsic stressors, which can alter the defined epigenetic circuitry established for the specification of cell lineages and cellular differentiation. It has been found that exposures during these epigenetically sensitive periods can leave long-lasting epigenetic effects in the germline, leading to intergenerational phenotypes in offspring. Although it is yet to be fully explored how specific the EED-induced epigenetic effects are and what mechanisms are involved in maintaining mitotic and meiotic stability of EED-established epigenetic alterations during various phases of germ and somatic cell differentiation, understanding of their role in regulating reproductive function in humans and non-human organisms remains to be of paramount importance.

DNA methylation plays a crucial role in the precise regulation of gene expression [51]. Epigenetic writer enzymes, mainly DNA methyltransferases (Dnmt3a and Dnmt3b), play a key role in establishing and maintaining cell-specific DNA methylation levels crucial for development and disease [37, 52]. In fish and mammals, EEDs have been shown to induce genomewide DNA methylation in both germline and somatic cells [53, 54]. Estrogenic EEDs negatively impact male reproduction and associated gene expression, including those of androgen receptors [55]. Epigenetic studies suggest that germline DNA methylation levels show corresponding correlation with tissue transcriptional alterations. It is not clear whether somatic cells of the target tissue maintain the same or similar levels of DNA methylation and whether such alterations are associated with dysregulation of target genes and resulting phenotypes. In the context of male fertility, gene knockout studies demonstrate that the loss of function of androgen receptors leads to fertility impairment [56, 57], and environmental EEDs of estrogenic or anti-androgenic nature can alter androgen function [58, 59]. It is not clear whether EED-induced fertility defects involve altered epigenetic profiles of the androgen receptor in the germline, and whether such profiles are inherited by somatic cells during TEI. Genomewide screening studies show EEDs inducing massive DNA methylation alterations in single or multiple CpG sites of the gene promoter region [60–62]. Understanding the role of DNA methylation by which it maintains exposure memory on ar in male germ cells is critical for discovering the mechanisms underlying the paternal origin of TEI and understanding fertility defects across generations. This seems to be a critical knowledge gap. To address this, we induced transgenerational fertility impairments in medaka and determined DNA methylation and arα expression across both germ cells and testicular soma of exposed and unexposed generations to show how stably epigenetic marks are maintained across generations and potentially transferred to the soma of unexposed offspring. The results provide insights into how vulnerable future generations are to ancestral EED exposure-induced fertility impairments.

Results

Male fertility was impaired in F2 generation due to ancestral BPA exposure

To determine whether BPA induces differential testicular impairment due to direct or transgenerational exposure, histological and fertility examination was performed (Supplementary Information, Fig. S3). F0 generation males in the control group showed normal morphology of testis with progression of spermatogenesis (Fig. 1A), whereas BPA-exposed males had testis with asynchronous spermatogenic germ cell population (Fig. 1B, dotted line) and thickened interstitial tissue as well disintegrated seminiferous tubule structure. In the F2 generation testis, control lineage had normal testicular morphology (Fig. 1C), whereas transgenerational effects of BPA were pronounced particularly marked by the loss of germ cells (Fig. 1D, dotted line), precocious stage of spermiogenesis, and asynchronous stages of spermatogenic germ cells (black arrows). In the F0 generation, mating of males with females from control lineage showed a decreased rate of fertilization (P < .07, Fig. 1E), whereas in the F2 generation, fertility was significantly decreased as indicated by decreased rate of fertilization (P < .01, Fig. 1F).

Bar graphs show fertilization efficiency of males in percentage fertilization. Symbols on the line connecting two bars indicate statistical significance (∇, P < .07, **P < .01). For each generation, for control and BPA exposure three biological replicates were used. For each replicate three male and three female fish were used.

Expression of DNA methyltransferase genes was elevated in the testis along with global DNA methylation

To determine if the fertility defects were associated with altered epigenetic processes, the mRNA levels of DNA methyltransferases (dnmt1, dnmt3aa, and dnmt3bb) and global DNA methylation were determined in the whole testis (Fig. 2). The expression of dnmt1 and dnmt3aa was significantly elevated in the F2 generation testis (P < .001), whereas the expression of dnmt3bb was increased in both F0 and F2 testes (P < .05). The global DNA methylation was decreased in F0 testis (P < .01), whereas the levels were significantly increased in the F2 testis (P < .001).

Asterisks indicate statistical significance (*P < .05, ***P < .001, Student’s t-test, Graphpad Prism v10). Green bars indicate F0 generation and crimson bars indicate F2 generation. Data were derived from three biological replicates. Each biological replicate in each exposure group contained six male and six female fish. For qPCR analysis, three technical replicate wells were used per sample.

Transgenerational inheritance of ancestrally established differential DNA methylation profile from germ cells to soma was observed

To confirm the heritability of exposure-induced germline differential methylation and their passage to somatic cells in the unexposed subsequent generation, we examined DNA methylation levels in the same region of the arα promoter in the spermatogonia of F2 adult males and in their somatic cells by pyrosequencing of the target region (see Supplementary Information, Figs S4 and S5). The spermatogonia of the F0 males maintained significantly higher DNA methylation levels in the arα promoter, which was maintained by spermatogonia of the F2 males (Fig. 4A). A similar trend was seen in the methylation level of the somatic cells of the F2 generation males (Fig. 4B). Expression of arα gene in those somatic cells was significantly decreased (Fig. 4C). This suggested that the BPA-induced F0 DNA methylation signatures were heritable within germ cells across generations and that they were also heritable from germline to soma in the unexposed offspring, suggesting transgenerational germline-to-soma passage of altered DNA methylation signatures. Although it was not possible to show methylation pattern of arα promoter of F1 sperm in the present study, the genomewide DNA methylation alteration data presented elsewhere support that F0 generation sperm signatures survive F1 generation reprogramming and persist in F1 sperm and then are transferred to F2 somatic cells [63].

Discussion

Gene–environment interaction results in the development of phenotypic traits that are either adaptive or maladaptive to the changing environment. Although distinct epigenetic alterations are associated with the observed phenotypes in laboratory animal studies [20, 63–65] and wild populations inhabiting extreme environments [66, 67], mechanistically, it is not yet clearly understood whether epigenetic alterations resulting from gene–environment interactions are causative for the phenotype or consequences of the altered phenotype. Some environmentally induced epigenetic alterations established in developing germ cells have been found to be heritable across many generations [63, 65, 68, 69]. A little is known about how environmentally induced epigenetic information is transmitted to subsequent generations and maintained, despite the finding that promoter-associated CpG islands can be transmitted from parents to their offspring [70] and that H3K9me3 histone marks instruct epigenetic writers to maintain inherited DNA methylation in the imprinted locus of a mammalian gene H19 [71]. Using BPA as an EED and medaka fish as an experimental animal [34,72], the present study investigated the dynamics of heritability of DNA methylation profile in the arα promoter of developing germ cells at the ground state of epigenetic reprogramming (15-dpf) [45, 46], in spermatogonia and sperm of the directly exposed males in F0 adulthood, in spermatogonia and supporting testicular somatic cells of the unexposed F2 male offspring to understand whether paternal origin of fertility defects is associated with intergenerational inheritance of BPA-induced DNA methylation imprint in the arα promoter, and whether it is transferred from germline to soma affecting the transcriptional status of ar gene in gonadal somatic cells. Results suggest that DNA methylation established in developing germ cells (PGCs) is retained by spermatogonia and sperm in adulthood and subsequently transgenerationally transferred to offspring through male germline transmission, which can eventually be inherited by offspring testicular somatic cells. Our recent findings in the medaka further support this pattern of intergenerational passage of BPA-induced genomewide differential DNA methylation from grandparental and paternal germline to the offspring liver [63]. Previous studies in various animal models provide evidence for the association of differential sperm DNA methylation with offspring phenotypes, although mechanistically unclear [20, 44, 69, 73–76]. Hence, accumulating evidence suggests that some epigenetic alterations established by gene–environment interaction in the germline can stably pass through generations and are associated with altered phenotypic traits.

Fertilization efficiency of the F0 generation BPA-exposed group showed a slight decrease (P < .07), whereas F2 generation showed a significant reduction in fertility, which was accompanied by alterations in the testicular tubular structure and germ cell landscape. BPA-induced testicular toxicity and its effects in male fertility have been investigated in many vertebrate species [20, 77–79]. The observed loss of spermatogenic germ cells aligns with phenotypic alterations in medaka (Oryzias latipes) and zebrafish (Danio rerio) testes, indicating that the impact of BPA on male reproductive function and fertility is persistent in fish and across generations [31, 80]. Phenotypes induced by direct exposure of adults are different from those in developing organisms. In developing zebrafish, low-dose BPA exposure (0.001 μM) caused reduction in sperm volume, density, motility, and velocity [81]. High dose of BPA has been found to induce testicular damage (decrease in spermatocytes, increase in germ cell apoptosis, downregulation of meiotic genes mainly ccnb1 and sycp3, and meiotic arrest) in both adult and developing individuals [82], whereas low concentration effects have been found to be minimal or none in adults but significant in developing organisms [20, 77, 83–85]. These results suggest that developmental exposure to low concentration BPA can result in skewed adult phenotypes with possibility for their heritability to offspring.

A significant increase in the expression of DNA methyltransferase genes along with elevated global DNA methylation levels in the whole testis of F2 generation suggested global hypermethylation of testicular cell genome. Consistent with the altered expression profile of epigenetic writer genes, those F2 fish showed an increased incidence of BPA-induced fertility defects along with disrupted tubules and less defined testicular germ cell morphology. In the developing germ cells at 15-dpf stage, at which germ cells attain a ground state of epigenetic reprogramming, BPA established differential DNA methylation levels (hypermethylation) in the promoter of arα gene, which remained unaltered in the sperm of F0 individuals. The male germ cells (spermatogonia) isolated from F0 testis also maintained a significantly elevated percentage of DNA methylation on the promoter of arα gene, suggesting that the differential DNA methylation profile on arα promoter was mitotically and meiotically stable during spermatogenesis. Very few studies have demonstrated mitotic and meiotic stability of differential methylation in germ cells. In mice, methylated promoter CpG islands of embryonic stem cells were retained as such even after their differentiation to male germ cells and sperm, which were later found stable in the somatic cells driving offspring phenotype [70]. In the rat, increased numbers of differentially methylation regions (DMRs) caused by DDT exposure were found in mature PGCs, prospermatogonia, and spermatogonia compared to other spermatogenic stages of germ cells [61, 86]. Exposure to androgens and estrogens can induce transgenerational epigenetic reprogramming, altering ar expression and leading to phenotypic shifts [32, 87]. The EEDs, mainly BPA, exacerbate these effects by inducing epigenetic alterations in the androgen receptor signaling pathway. Accumulating evidence demonstrates BPA exposure causing DNA hypermethylation or demethylation in the ar promoter, reducing ar mRNA and protein levels in males, increasing ar expression in females, inducing intersex phenotypes, and leading to transgenerational reproductive impairments across generations in various fish species [64, 88–91]. In mice, BPA exposure can stimulateAr gene expression in prostate mesenchymal cells [92], decrease sperm count, motility, and fertility along with altered global DNA methylation pattern in the sperm [91]. The present results conform to previous findings that BPA-induced fertility defects are associated with altered arα promoter CGI methylation and the expression of arα gene in the testis.

In the present study, DNA methylation in the arα promoter was assessed in 15-dpf primordial germ cells (PGCs) when epigenetic reprogramming is at the ground state as well as in mature sperm of F0 males. The inclusion of these germ cell developmental stages was to determine if the observed methylation pattern is present within the germline across mitotic and meiotic germ cell development. The level of BPA-induced arα promoter DNA methylation was increased in F0 germ cells, which remained significantly higher than control in F2 offspring germ cells, which is consistent with a potential role in transgenerational inheritance as demonstrated in mice and rats [70, 93]. Artificially induced heritable DNA methylation levels in the parental germline are either resistant to epigenetic reprogramming of genome prior to blastula stage or erased and reestablished in all resulting cell types [93]. Depending on the chromatin status of the genomic locus that is inherited by offspring somatic cells, the differential DNA methylation will contribute to transcriptional regulation or genomic stability of somatic cells [70]. Considering these two reports, the present findings suggest for the possibility that the inheritance pattern of differential methylation observed on the CpG island of the arα promoter could either be resistant to epigenetic reprogramming of embryo and PGC genome at F1 and F2 generation or it was reprogrammed but established later in development [71]. This remains to be clarified in the future.

The increased level of DNA methylation on the arα promoter CpG island was accompanied by significant reduction in the levels of arα mRNA in the testicular somatic cells. To understand cell-specific DNA methylation, we isolated germ cells and soma from the testis at F0 and F2 generations. The DNA methylation levels and the expression pattern of genes in F2 generation germ cells and soma are derived through TEI as F0 parent fish were exposed to BPA during early embryogenesis. The intracellular carryover effects of BPA could be possibly seen in F1 generation, but in F2 generation, the effects were mediated by F1 sperm. F2 generation did not experience any carryover but via inheritance since F1 germ cells were not in direct contact with the stressor. The observed inheritance pattern seemed to be novel affecting the male germline and somatic cells in F2 generation. The male fish that showed this TEI pattern had developed reduced fertility. BPA-induced male subfertility has been demonstrated in many experimental animal model organisms [20, 39, 64, 82, 85, 91, 94], including medaka [31] and humans [78]. The evidence for inheritance of transgenerational epigenetic alterations from germline to soma has been barely demonstrated [61, 63, 95], except for the study that used embryonic stem cells with experimentally modified DNA methylation in metabolic imprinted gene promoter CpG islands to demonstrate the TEI of altered DNA methylation in germ cells and soma across three generations [70]. The potential impact of germline transmission of environmentally induced epigenetic alterations (epimutations) on lifestyle-related diseases is substantial, particularly given the complex and reciprocal interactions between human activity and environmental exposures. Globally, exposure to ubiquitous chemicals, mainly BPA and phthalates, is unavoidable [96], and transgenerational consequences in male reproductive cells cannot be overruled.

EEDs, particularly those with estrogenic properties, exacerbate fertility defects and potentially induce epigenetic alterations in genes in the AR pathway. In fish (zebrafish, medaka, and rare minnow), environmentally relevant BPA exposure causes DNA hypermethylation or demethylation in the ar promoter, reduced ar mRNA and protein levels in males, increased expression in females, intersex phenotypes, and transgenerational reproductive impairments across generations in zebrafish and other models like medaka and rare minnow. These changes involved altered histone marks (e.g. H3K4me3 and H3K27me3), miRNA profiles targeting ar, global DNA methylation disruptions (including reduced dnmt1 transcription), and interactions with estrogen/androgen signaling, linking BPA to sex ratio biases, diminished male fertility, and persistent reproductive toxicity even at low doses [64, 82, 88, 97]. Environmental stressors, such as temperature fluctuations, have also been shown to elicit stage-dependent epigenetic changes that modulate androgen receptor pathways, potentially exacerbating sex ratio biases under climate change scenarios [98,99].

Overall, the present study provides important mechanistic insights into how ancestral BPA exposure induces transgenerational male fertility impairments by identifying a stable epigenetic alteration at the arα promoter. The study demonstrates that BPA-induced hypermethylation occurs in primordial germ cells during the ground state of epigenetic reprogramming, is maintained through spermatogenesis, and is subsequently recapitulated in somatic testicular cells of unexposed F2 males, where it is associated with reduced arα expression and impaired fertility. The evidence for mitotic and meiotic stability of a locus-specific epigenetic mark, together with its germline-to-soma transmission across generations, represents a significant new observation, since previous studies primarily focused exclusively on germline inheritance. The findings further corroborate the mechanistic link between environmental endocrine disruption, epigenetic memory, and long-term reproductive outcomes. The persistence of epigenetic modifications into subsequent generations of the BPA exposure lineage highlights the potential for BPA to induce long-lasting reproductive consequences beyond the directly exposed individuals. The presence of such modifications in the somatic cells and germline may predispose the individuals with a history of ancestral BPA exposure to exacerbation of reproductive and metabolic health when they experience a second stressor. This has been briefly demonstrated in medaka [100, 101] and mice [102] and warrants further investigation. These results underscore that EEDs do not merely cause transient damage; they fundamentally reprogram the reproductive fitness of future generations. Such TEI represents a silent but profound threat to the long-term reproductive health and viability of wildlife populations and highlights an urgent need to re-evaluate the cumulative, multi-generational risk of environmental contaminants. To protect the health of current and future generations from endocrine disruption and epigenetic inheritance of EED-induced health effects, it is essential to develop strategies to intercept disease pathways induced by environmental and dietary EEDs.

The present study focused on correlative effects of an EED on key regulators of male reproduction. Limitations of the study include the demonstrated causal link between epigenetic regulation of androgen receptors and its role in fertility-associated outcomes. The study focused on the dynamics of arα methylation, intergenerational inheritance, and association with fertility outcomes in unexposed grand offspring, since literature suggests that the androgen receptor is critical for male fertility. It did not systematically evaluate other androgen-responsive or steroidogenic genes and pathways leaving some underlying mechanisms unclear as to whether arα represents a uniquely sensitive epigenetic target or whether the observed reproductive impairment reflects broader epigenomic disruption affecting multiple components of the androgen signaling network. Future studies incorporating functional rescue approaches, such as CRISPR-dCas9-mediated epigenome editing, and expanded analysis of steroidogenic and androgen-regulated genes will be necessary to distinguish gene-specific effects from global epigenetic dysregulation.

Materials and methods

Care and handling of fish

The Hd-rR strain of medaka was used in the study. All procedures of animal care, handling, and euthanasia were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of North Carolina Greensboro (#20-002 and #22-1169) and the University of Missouri (#43230). Approximately 4-month-old breeding pairs of adult medaka (eight males and eight females) were reared in 20 l glass aquaria in a recirculating water system under a 14:10 h of light:dark cycle and at a water temperature of 26 ± 1°C. Water exchange was maintained at 25% every 4 h daily. The broodstock fish were fed daily with Otohime granular food (Reed Mariculture, USA) and freshly hatched brine shrimp (Artemia sp) nauplii. Eggs were collected from the breeding pairs, pooled, detangled by rolling with fingers in a Petri dish containing embryo solution, and sorted under a brightfield stereoscope to select fertilized eggs.

Bisphenol A solution preparation and exposure

The BPA solution was prepared, the concentration in the dosing solution was determined, and uptake by embryos was quantified using the method previously described by Bhandari et al. [31]. Briefly, BPA was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (CAS no. 80-05-7, purity ≥ 99%, Cat. 239658) and dissolved in alkaline water at pH 9.8. From the 100 mg/l stock solution, the dosing solution of 100 μg/l BPA was prepared by serially diluting the dosing solution (×10) with medaka embryo solution. The final pH of the dosing solution was maintained at neutral after serial dilution. The concentration of BPA in the dosing solution varied by 10% from the nominal concentration [31]. For vehicle controls, the same volume alkaline water was serially diluted with medaka embryo solution and pH balanced to match that of exposure solution.

For exposure, 50 fertilized eggs (F0) were used per exposure group (control and BPA) and placed in glass Petri dishes until the first feeding stage. Three glass Petri dishes were used per treatment (50 ml) and each Petri dish served as a biological replicate. To collect primordial germ cells at 15-dpf stage, another set of 50 embryos were raised using the same strategy. The control group received no BPA, whereas the exposure group received 100 μg/l BPA. To determine how much BPA was taken by the embryos during the exposure period, uptake was measured in 24 h and 7 days. The uptake was 28.6 pictograms/mg egg in 24 h and 178 picograms/mg egg in 7 days [31]. This study is part of a larger research project [31–34, 37, 43, 45, 46, 63, 100, 101, 103–112]. The results of BPA uptake have been previously reported elsewhere [31, 103].

Embryos were exposed for 7 days, spanning the critical window of sex determination in medaka, which occurs between 5 and 7 days post-fertilization (dpf). The control water and test chemical solutions were replaced daily until hatch. There were no further chemical exposures for these fish (F0) after hatching or any subsequent generations (F1 and F2). Newly hatched juveniles were transferred to floating netted trays that were placed in 20 l aquaria with flowing water and aeration at 12 dpf, when they started their first feeding. Some of the juveniles (n = 15–20 per replicate) were sampled at 15-dpf stage to collect primordial germ cells (PGCs) at the ground state of epigenetic reprogramming of germ cells. At 30 dpf, all remaining juveniles were transferred to aquaria and maintained. Three biological replicate tanks were maintained per exposure group with 30 fish in each tank. Culture of the two treatment lineages (control and BPA) followed through production of F2 generation.

Phenotypic data collection

Phenotypic data were collected according to the method previously published [31]. Briefly, to collect data for fecundity and fertilization, six males from the control lineage were paired with six females from the treated lineage and six females from the control lineage were mated with six males from the treated lineage for each biological replicate. Additionally, six males and six females from each treatment group were mated to determine fecundity and fertilization regardless of sex of origin. Total egg production/per day was recorded, and fertilization rate was determined by taking into account the total eggs fertilized out of total eggs produced by the breeding pairs over the collection period. The data were collected continuously for 17 days. On the day of sample collection, morphometric measurements of body (weight and total length), gonads, and liver were taken. Fish were dried with a paper towel to remove water from the body before wet weight measurement. Total length was measured from the tip of the snout to the end of the tail. Gonadosomatic index (GSI) was calculated as [(gonad weight/body weight) × 100], and hepatosomatic index (HSI) was calculated as [(liver weight/body weight) × 100].

PGCs and sperm isolation

To collect PGCs, a previously established method was adopted [37]. Briefly, a total of 50 15-dpf larvae were euthanized, and gonadal ridge was dissected from each larva with a fine scalpel under the bright stereoscope (Nikon SMZ1200), and pooled. Germ cells were dispersed from the gonadal ridge tissue with collagenase and trypsin treatment in L-15 medium for 30 min at 28°C. A graded Percoll layer containing 12.5%, 25%, 50%, and 80% Percoll (diluted with L15 medium) was prepared in a 5 ml borosilicate tube. Dispersed cells were carefully loaded to the top of the Percoll and centrifuged at 1000× g for 20 min. The medaka were transgenic for olvas-EGFP transgene, so PGCs were marked with EGFP fluorescence. The germ cells retained between 25% and 50% Percoll layers were collected and examined under the fluorescence microscope to determine purity. The cells were treated with GFRα1 antibody-coated magnetic beads for 30 min and pulled down using a magnetic tube stand. After microscopically confirming that isolated cells were EGFP-positive, the germ cells were then used for DNA isolation. Approximately, 10 000 EGFP-positive germ cells were used per replicate to isolate DNA.

From the F0 and F2 generation adult males (120-dpf stage), testes were collected. For gene expression analysis, testes from six males/replicate were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C analysis. To collect germ cells and sperm, testes from three other males (those used for fertility analysis) were transferred to a parafilm containing one drop of L-15 medium and cut with fine scissors into four pieces. The tissues together with L-15 medium were dissociated as mentioned above for 15-dpf gonadal ridge isolation and loaded onto respective 5-ml borosilicate tubes containing Percoll gradient (12.5%, 25%, 50%, and 80% Percoll, each 500 μl). The 5 mL tube was then centrifuged at 1000× g for 20 min. Sperm remained at the bottom layer (80% Percoll). Somatic cells and other germ cell types remain in the layers less than 75% Percoll. The spermatogonia were separated from rest of the cells with GFRα1 antibody-coated magnetic beads, whereas rest of the cells were loaded onto the Percoll gradient. GFRα1 antibody is selective for type 1 spermatogonia. Isolated spermatogonia and somatic cells (from 12.5%–50% Percoll) were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until analysis. Sperm was collected from 80% Percoll layer. The purity of sperm was confirmed on a slide glass (2 μl) under the brightfield microscope. The collected sperm was diluted with PBS for five times (1:9 ratio), and DNA was isolated and used for Pyrosequencing. Approximately, 50 000 sperm cells were collected per testis.

Tissue collection, fixation, embedding, sectioning, and histological staining

Testes samples (n = 3) were dissected and fixed in 1 ml of Bouin’s solution for histopathological analysis. Samples were fixed for 48 h at room temperature with gentle agitation. After fixation, tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series consisting of 25% ethanol followed by 85% ethanol, 95% ethanol for 30 min. They were then cleared in xylene for 20 min and infiltrated with paraffin wax for 2 h. Paraffin blocks were prepared, and 7 μM sections were prepared from each testis with a microtome. Hematoxylin was used to stain the nuclei, and remaining cytoplasmic components were stained with eosin according to the method previously published [105].

RNA/DNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, DNA methylation, and real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA and genomic DNA were collected from 15-dpf PGCs, testis tissues from F0 and F2 males, isolated germ and somatic cells of F2 males using AllPrep DNA/RNA/miRNA Universal Kit (QIAGEN, Cat No: 80 224) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and the procedure previously described elsewhere. Genomic DNA from sperm was isolated by Qiagen DNA Micro Kit (#56304). The concentration of both RNA and genomic DNA was determined by Nanodrop 2000 and confirmed in Qubit. The cDNA was synthesized using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Thermofisher #4374966). The arα primers for qPCR were designed by using Primer3 web version (https://primer3.ut.ee/) [113] and the primers for dnmt expression were obtained from the stock in-house previously published [37]. All primer sequences are provided in the supplementary information page. The qPCR was performed using Powerup SYBR Green Mastermix (Applied Biosystems, A25742) on QuantStudio 3 real-time qPCR equipment (Applied Biosciences). All primers were validated for their efficiency before assaying the samples. Gene expression was analyzed by the standard 2−ΔΔCt method.

Global DNA methylation (5mC) ELISA and bisulfite pyrosequencing

5mC ELISA: Global DNA methylation was determined by 5mC DNA ELISA kit (Zymoresearch, D5326) according to the manufacturer’s instruction manual. Briefly, 100 ng gDNA was used per replicate and three replicates were used per sample. At least three samples were used per exposure group. The assay was performed according to the previously published method [37]. Briefly, the isolated DNA was denatured at 98°C for 5 min in a thermal cycler, cooled on ice for 10 min, and later transferred to respective wells in the buffer-coated plate, treated with antibody (anti-5mC antibody, 1:1000) and secondary antibody (1:1000), and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. After washing, HRP developer was applied to each well to develop color and examined in a spectrophotometer at 405 nm (BioTek Synergy 2 Plate Reader). A standard curve was generated and the 5mC percentage was determined by %5mC = e^ [(absorbance − y intercept)/slope] according to the manufacturer’s (Zymoresearch, CA, USA) instruction manual and the method we previously described [11, 103].

Bisulfite pyrosequencing: Methylation of specific cytosines within CpG dinucleotides was quantified by pyrosequencing using a PyroMark Gold Q96 Reagents (Qiagen, #972804). PCR and sequencing primers were designed by using Methprimer software as provided in the Supplementary Information (Figs S1 and S2) and according to Zeybel et al. [114]. A 10 μl of biotin-labeled PCR product was mixed with streptavidin-coated Sepharose beads, washed in 70% ethanol, denatured in 0.01% sodium azide, washed in a wash buffer (Qiagen, PyroMark Wash Buffer), and primers were annealed at 80°C. Samples were run in triplicate. Assay efficiency was validated by unmethylated and methylated DNA according to the manufacturer using Qiagen EpiTect PCR Control DNA Set (#59695). CpG methylation data were analyzed by Pyro Q-CpG software 1.0.6.

Statistical analysis

Fertility, gene expression, and DNA methylation between control and exposure groups were determined by Student’s t-test suing GraphPad Prism v9. Unless otherwise stated, data are presented as mean ± SEM.
