PIP4K attenuates PIP5K lipid kinase activity by disrupting membrane-mediated dimerization
Authors: Benjamin R. Duewell, Michael Worcester, Michael J. Chirumbolo, Alayna Beam, Samantha M. Fernandez-Ortiz, Gerald R. V. Hammond, Scott D. Hansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceCells must maintain a stable pool of phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate [PI(4,5)P2] lipids to properly regulate endocytosis, actin-based membrane protrusions, and ion channel flux at the plasma membrane. Misregulation of PI(4,5)P2 lipid synthesis can enhance PI3K-mediated production of PI(3,4,5)P3, which drives cell proliferation and metastasis. In this study, we determine how the primary enzyme responsible for generating PI(4,5)P2 lipids in cells, phosphatidylinositol-4-phosphate 5-kinase (PIP5K), is regulated by PIP4K. By disrupting membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K, PIP4K provides a direct mechanism for attenuating PIP5K lipid kinase activity. Overall, this work establishes a model for understanding how the interaction between PIP4K and PIP5K regulates PI(4,5)P2 lipid homeostasis in healthy and diseased states.

Despite being only a minor component of eukaryotic cell membranes, phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate [PI(4,5)P2] plays a major functional role in membrane signaling pathways (1, 2). PI(4,5)P2 lipids function as cofactors that recruit numerous peripheral membrane binding proteins to the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane and allosterically activate numerous integral membrane proteins. This role has been shown to be critical for ion channel gating, cell signaling, membrane-cytoskeletal anchoring, and endocytosis (3–9). PI(4,5)P2 lipids also function as a precursor for the production of PI(3,4,5)P3 lipids, which control cell fate decisions, directed cell migration, neurogenesis, and adhesion (10–12). These key roles make PI(4,5)P2 lipids fundamental to the functioning of eukaryotic membranes. Genetic perturbations that limit PI(4,5)P2 availability in early development are embryonic lethal, demonstrating the necessity of this lipid for living systems (13). Less severe mutations that modulate PI(4,5)P2 levels in cells underlie disease such as cardiomyopathies, cataract development, intellectual disability formation, and cancer (14, 15). Although the importance of PI(4,5)P2 lipids for cellular functions is self-evident, our understanding of how PI(4,5)P2 levels are stably maintained in cells is poorly understood.

Eukaryotes express two types of lipid kinases that regulate PI(4,5)P2 production: type I phosphatidylinositol-4-phosphate 5-kinase (PIP5K) enzymes and type II phosphatidylinositol-5-phosphate 4-kinase (PIP4K) enzymes. Type I PIP5K enzymes phosphorylate the 5-OH of a phosphatidylinositol-4-phosphate substrate (PI(4)P) (16, 17), while type II PIP4K enzymes utilize the less abundant phosphatidylinositol-5-phosphate (PI(5)P) lipid (18, 19). Due to the low abundance of PI(5)P in cells, however, this second pathway contributes only a minor fraction of the total plasma membrane pool of PI(4,5)P2 (20). Despite the existence of these two pathways, studies have shown that PIP5K enzymes are primarily responsible for generating PI(4,5)P2 in mammalian cells (1, 21). The PIP5K family consists of three paralogs (α, β, and γ), which display tissue-specific expression patterns and serve nonredundant roles in cell signaling (22, 23). Compared to constitutively dimeric PIP4K (24), PIP5K paralogs exist as monomers in solution but undergo membrane-mediated dimerization which potentiates their lipid kinase activity (25).

Several mechanisms have been proposed to control PI(4,5)P2 levels at the plasma membrane, including phospholipase-mediated cleavage of the PI(4,5)P2 lipid head group (26), lipid phosphatases (27, 28), and interorganelle lipid transport (29). Recently, cellular PI(4,5)P2 levels were also shown to depend on PIP4K-mediated inhibition PIP5K lipid kinase activity (30, 31). Cells lacking all three human PIP4K paralogs uniquely display a sustained increase in PI(4,5)P2 lipids that is twofold higher than wild type cells (31). In this context, elevated levels of PI(4,5)P2 alters PI(3,4,5)P3 synthesis and Akt phosphorylation (30, 31). Although this phenotype depends on the interaction between PIP4K and PIP5K, the mechanism of PIP4K-mediated inhibition of PIP5K remains unclear.

Based on the ability of PIP5K paralogs to undergo membrane-mediated dimerization, we hypothesized that interactions with PIP4K could modulate PI(4,5)P2 synthesis by regulating dimerization of PIP5K. In our previous study, membrane-mediated dimerization of purified and fluorescently labeled AF647-PIP5K was inferred mainly by quantifying changes in diffusion as a function of the total membrane-bound density of PIP5K (25). To more directly observe membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K in vitro, we established a single molecule Förster resonance energy transfer (smFRET) assay to visualize homo- and heterodimerization of PIP5K paralogs on supported lipid bilayers (SLBs) using Total Internal Reflection Fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy. Using this PIP5K FRET sensor, we found that purified PIP4K interacts with the dimer interface of PIP5K and blocks membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K. This direct interaction attenuates PIP5K lipid kinase activity in vitro and in vivo. Corroborating these results, live cell imaging of endogenously tagged PIP5K revealed changes in lateral membrane diffusion that depend on homodimerization of PIP5K and interactions with PIP4K. Guided by structural prediction, we generated PIP4K mutants that fail to block membrane-mediated dimerization or inhibit PIP5K lipid kinase activity in vitro and in vivo. Overall, this work reveals the molecular basis for PIP4K-mediated inhibition of PIP5K providing a mechanism for understanding cellular PI(4,5)P2 lipid homeostasis.

Results

Membrane-Mediated Dimerization of PIP5K Visualized by smFRET.

Membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K occurs in a protein density–dependent manner on PI(4,5)P2 containing membranes (25). Membrane surface densities that favor PIP5K dimerization, however, are not well suited for direct visualization by dimerization using single molecule TIRF microscopy. As a result, we previously inferred PIP5K dimerization by monitoring changes in the dwell time and diffusion coefficient of Alexa Fluor 647 (AF647) PIP5K in the presence of unlabeled PIP5K (25). A limitation of this approach is that interactions with other peripheral membrane binding proteins can potentially modulate the dwell time and diffusion coefficient of AF647-PIP5K in a manner that is indistinguishable from homodimerization of PIP5K. This presents a challenge interpreting whether a PIP4K regulates membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K or another biophysical property. Due to these limitations, we developed a single molecule FRET sensor to visualize membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K.

Homodimerization of PIP5KB is regulated by conserved residues (i.e., human Asp-51 and Arg-254) that form salt-bridges at the dimer interface (Fig. 1A). Using a combination of existing X-ray crystallography data (32, 33), AlphaFold structure prediction, and multiple sequence alignments of PIP5K family proteins, we identified candidate residues near the dimer interface that could potentially be labeled with fluorescent dyes to establish a FRET sensor for measuring dimerization (Fig. 1A). Positioned in a solvent exposed loop near the dimer interface, we identified the nonconserved Pro-163 residue with a predicted separation of 11.5 Å at the PIP5KB dimer interface (Fig. 1A). To engineer site-specific labeling, we identified all the solvent exposed cysteines through labeling of wildtype PIP5KB with Alexa Fluor 488 (AF488) maleimide. Electrospray ionization mass spectroscopy (ESI-MS) and accessible surface area calculations identified Cys-110 and Cys-411 as solvent exposed residues, while Cys-175 and Cys-256 were buried and protected from maleimide labeling. Based on these results, we mutated the solvent exposed residues (i.e., C110S, C411S) and introduced a P163C mutation for site-specific labeling near the dimer interface (Fig. 1A). This mutant is referred to as the PIP5KB FRET construct. To confirm the mutations did not perturb the catalytic efficiency of PIP5KB, we performed lipid kinase assays to measure PI(4,5)P2 lipid production on SLBs (Fig. 1B). No significant change in the catalytic efficiency was observed for the PIP5KB FRET construct compared to the wild type kinase (Fig. 1C).

To establish the smFRET assay to visualize membrane-mediated dimerization, we labeled purified PIP5KB FRET construct (referred to as PIP5KB) with either Alexa Fluor 555 (AF555) or Alexa Fluor 647 (AF647) (Fig. 1D). These fluorescent dyes have high quantum yields, robust photostability, and a Förster Radius (R0) of 51 Å, making them an ideal pair for smFRET (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Using Total Internal Reflection Fluorescence Microscopy (TIRF-M), we visualized the sensitized emission of AF647-PIP5KB (acceptor) with single molecule resolution in the absence and presence AF555-PIP5KB (donor) on SLBs containing PI(4,5)P2 lipids (Fig. 1E). Conditions were optimized to excite AF555-PIP5KB and visualize AF647-PIP5KB emission with minimal direct excitation of AF647 using either a 532 or 561 nm laser (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Under these conditions, a distinct population of bright AF647-PIP5KB molecules were observed only in the presence of both the donor and acceptor labeled PIP5KB (Fig. 1 F and G and Movie S1). The frequency of observing PIP5KB dimers by smFRET was calculated based on a threshold molecular brightness of visualized AF647-PIP5KB (acceptor) that exceeded the brightest particles in our control conditions (Fig. 1 H and I). Comparing the FRET lifetime to the total membrane dwell times of AF647-PIP5KB, revealed that the lifetime of PIP5K homodimers was relatively transient (τ1 = 0.14 ± 0.03 s) compared to the total membrane dwell time of a single membrane-bound kinase (τ1 = 0.89 ± 0.03 s, τ2 = 2.7 ± 0.3 s, α = 0.94) (Fig. 1J).

Mutational Analysis and Competition Validate Specificity of smFRET Sensor.

Membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K is regulated by salt bridges formed between aspartic acid and arginine residues (Fig. 1A). Previous studies showed that mutations in these residues disrupt homodimerization and attenuate PIP5K lipid kinase activity (25, 32). To validate the specificity of the smFRET PIP5KB dimerization sensor, we characterized a dimerization deficient PIP5KB mutant, D51R (Fig. 2A). The D51R mutation was introduced into the PIP5KB FRET construct and labeled with the acceptor fluorophore (AF647). We performed the smFRET assay with a wild type AF555-PIP5KB (donor) and mutant AF647-PIP5KB (D51R, acceptor). Under these conditions, we observed a significant decrease in the frequency of smFRET compared to the wild type PIP5KB FRET construct (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Consistent with PIP5K dimerization requiring electrostatic interactions between kinase domains, the frequency of observing membrane-bound PIP5KB dimers by smFRET was inversely proportional to the buffer ionic strength (Fig. 2C). PIP5KB dimers detected by smFRET also exhibited a characteristic decrease in membrane diffusivity compared to the monomeric species (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). This change in membrane binding behavior occurs in PIP5K density-dependent manner and requires an intact dimer interface (25). Overall, these results validate our smFRET approach enabling the quantification of PIP5K dimerization across experimental conditions to determine how various protein–protein interactions modulate membrane-mediated homodimerization of PIP5K.

In vivo, PIP5K associates with the plasma membrane where competition for protein–protein and protein–lipid interactions is predicted to modulate membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K. In our smFRET assay, competition with unlabeled PIP5KB is expected to reduce the probability of dimerization between donor and acceptor labeled PIP5KB if membrane-bound dimers are in dynamic equilibrium with monomeric species (Fig. 2D). Consistent with this prediction, the addition of unlabeled PIP5KB significantly reduced the frequency of particles observed by FRET, while the addition of the PIP5KB (D51R) dimer interface mutant was unable to interfere (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). In contrast, experiments performed in the presence of a peripheral membrane binding protein with broad specificity to anionic lipids in vitro (i.e., LactC2) was unable to disrupt membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5KB observed by smFRET (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Together, these results demonstrate that an intact dimer interface is required to observe PIP5KB smFRET in vitro. In addition, AF555-PIP5KB does not support FRET with membrane-bound AF647-LactC2.

Overexpression of catalytically dead PIP5K paradoxically increases the total plasma membrane density of PI(4,5)P2 lipids in cells (30, 34). One hypothesis is that catalytically dead PIP5K promotes membrane-mediated dimerization of endogenously expressed PIP5K. To test this hypothesis, we performed smFRET using donor labeled AF555-PIP5KB FRET (D266K) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). We previously showed that the D266K mutant binds to PI(4,5)P2 containing membranes similar to the wild type kinase in vitro (35). Using AF555-PIP5KB (D266K) as the FRET donor, we detected a similar frequency of smFRET events compared to the wild type PIP5KB donor and acceptor pair (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B and C). Consistent with catalytically dead PIP5KB (D266K) increasing membrane-mediated dimerization, we observed an increase in lipid kinase activity when wildtype PIP5KB was combined with PIP5KB (D266K) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). Together, these data support a model by which overexpression of catalytically dead PIP5KB promotes membrane-mediated dimerization of endogenous PIP5K, which increases cellular PI(4,5)P2 levels through enhanced lipid kinase activity.

Membrane-Mediated Heterodimerization of PIP5K Paralogs.

PIP5K paralogs share a conserved dimer interface that is expected to facilitate membrane-mediated heterodimerization (Fig. 3A). Since PIP5K paralogs have similar tissue expression profiles (36), heterodimerization of paralogs could provide a mechanism that modulates cellular PI(4,5)P2 levels. Previous efforts to detect heterodimerization of PIP5K paralogs have relied on coimmunoprecipitation (31, 37) and colocalization visualized at the plasma membrane (3, 30, 38). Here, we tested whether purified PIP5KA and PIP5KB can heterodimerize on SLBs using single molecule TIRF-M. By comparing the membrane dwell times and diffusion coefficient of AF647-PIP5KB in the absence and presence of dark unlabeled PIP5KB (Fig. 3A), we previously detected membrane-mediated homodimerization of PIP5KB (25). Repeating these experiments in the presence of PIP5KA, revealed a similar increase in membrane dwell time and reduction in membrane diffusion for AF647-PIP5KB (Fig. 3 B and C). We previously showed that these changes in membrane dwell time and lateral diffusion are not observed for fluorescently labeled PIP5K containing a dimer interface mutation (25). Together, these observations indicate that PIP5K paralogs can heterodimerize on membranes.

Having established that the smFRET assay detects homodimerization of PIP5KB, we next tested whether competition via heterodimerization with PIP5KA could reduce the frequency of PIP5KB homodimerization (Fig. 3D). Like the competition experiments that utilized dark unlabeled PIP5KB (Fig. 2D), the addition of unlabeled PIP5KA blocked homodimerization of the PIP5KB FRET construct based on mass action (Fig. 3E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). To determine whether we could directly visualize heterodimerization by smFRET, we combined AF555-PIP5KB (donor) and AF647-PIP5KA (acceptor) on PI(4,5)P2 containing membranes (Fig. 3F). Like the PIP5KB homodimerization FRET sensor, we visualized a high frequency of PIP5KA-PIP5KB heterodimers by smFRET compared to the acceptor alone control (Fig. 3G). To validate the specificity of the heterodimer FRET signaling, we repeated the experiments using the dimerization deficient mutant AF647-PIP5KA (D92R). Like experiments performed with AF647-PIP5KB (D51R), smFRET based on heterodimerization was rarely detected in the presence of the AF647-PIP5KA (D92R) acceptor with the dimer interface mutation (Fig. 3G).

PIP4K Blocks Membrane-Mediated Dimerization of PIP5K.

Due to differences in their specificity loop sequences, purified PIP4K and PIP5K require different densities of PI(4,5)P2 lipids for membrane localization in vitro (30, 35). Leveraging this difference in membrane avidity, we quantified the ability of PIP5KB to directly bind and recruit AF488-PIP4KA to membranes containing 2% PI(4,5)P2 (Fig. 4A). Under these conditions, AF488-PIP4KA alone remains primarily in solution with minimal membrane localization (Fig. 4B). Following the addition of PIP5KA, however, we observed robust membrane recruitment of AF488-PIP4KA (Fig. 4B). In contrast, experiments performed with the dimerization deficient PIP5KA (D92R) mutant failed to enhance AF488-PIP4KA membrane localization (Fig. 4C). We observed similar results following the addition of PIP5KB, wild type, or a dimerization deficient mutant (i.e., D51R) (Fig. 4C).

To test whether PIP4K blocks membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K, we performed smFRET competition experiments (Fig. 4D). Consistent with our previous results, homodimerization of PIP5KB measured by smFRET showed a significant decrease in the FRET frequency in the presence of PIP4KA (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). To test whether the decrease in PIP5K FRET was due to molecular crowding on our supported bilayer, rather than a direct PIP4K–PIP5K interaction, we performed the smFRET assay in the presence of the PI(4,5)P2 lipid binding domain derived from PLCδ. This PH domain bound to membranes but could not associate with either the acceptor or donor labeled PIP5KB. We found that PLCδ PH resulted in a small decrease in the smFRET efficiency (Fig. 4E). Together, this shows that the decrease we see from the addition of PIP4KA in our FRET assay results from direct blockage of the dimer interface and not solely based on competition for PI(4,5)P2 lipid binding sites. Taken together, these data support the hypothesis that PIP4K inhibits PIP5K activity by blocking PIP5K dimerization by competing for binding at the dimer interface.

Molecular Basis of PIP4K–PIP5K Interaction.

To map the interaction site between PIP4K and PIP5K, we modeled potential binding sites using AlphaFold multimer (39). Based on structure prediction, M60/L61/M62 residues in PIP4KA form a hydrophobic interaction at the dimer interface of PIP5K (Fig. 4F and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). To determine whether these residues were required for PIP4K–PIP5K complex formation, we quantified the ability of membrane-bound PIP5K to recruit AF488-PIP4K to membranes containing 1% PI(4,5)P2. Consistent with the mutant PIP4KA being unable to interact with PIP5KA, we did not observe PIP5K-mediated membrane recruitment of AF488-PIP4KA (M60R, L61E, M62R) (Fig. 4 G and H). This result mimics the inability of dimerization deficient PIP5K (D92R) to enhance membrane recruitment of wild type AF488-PIP4K (Fig. 4 G and H). Using the smFRET assay, we tested whether PIP4KA (M60R, L61E, M62R) could block membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5KB (Fig. 4I). Consistent with our previous results, the frequency of PIP5K homodimerization was significantly reduced in the presence of PIP4KA (Fig. 4J and SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). By contrast, the presence of PIP4KA (M60R, L61E, M62R) did not significantly change the frequency of PIP5KB smFRET (Fig. 4J and SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). To ensure that differences observed between wildtype and mutant PIP4KA were not due to protein misfolding, we measured the secondary structure content of PIP4K using circular dichroism (CD). Comparing the CD spectra of PIP4KA (M60R, L61E, M62R) and the wild type kinase yielded indistinguishable results (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B). In addition, the size exclusion chromatography elution profiles for wild type and mutant PIP4K were nearly identical (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C). Last, both PIP4K constructs displayed similar cooperative membrane binding in response to PI(4,5)P2 synthesis (SI Appendix, Fig. S8).

PIP4K Mutants Fail to Inhibit PIP5K Lipid Kinase Activity.

Since membrane-mediated dimerization enhances PIP5K catalytic activity (25), we hypothesized that PIP4K could regulate PIP5K lipid kinase activity by blocking homodimerization. To determine whether the M60R, L61E, M62R mutations could alter PIP4K-mediated inhibition of PIP5K, we measured lipid kinase activity on supported membranes. Consistent with our previous observations (30), the presence of wild type PIP4K attenuated PIP5K activity after a density of ~2% PI(4,5)P2 was generated (Fig. 5A). This is the density of PI(4,5)P2 lipids required to localize PIP4K to membranes in the absence of PIP5K (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). By contrast, the PIP4KA (M60R, L61E, M62R) mutant was unable to inhibit PIP5K lipid kinase activity (Fig. 5B). Performing these experiments in the presence of monomeric PIP5KA (D92R) revealed no PIP4K-dependent modulation of kinase activity (Fig. 5B). Together, these results suggest that PIP4K binds to the PIP5K dimer interface, thus creating a PIP5K-(PIP4K)2 ternary complex that is less catalytically active compared to homodimeric PIP5K.

When performing lipid kinase reactions in a manner that simply monitors the phosphorylation of PI(4)P to PI(4,5)P2, we are limited to using low concentrations of PIP5KA (i.e., 1 to 2 nM) to capture the reaction kinetics. Under these conditions only a fraction of the total membrane-bound PIP5K exist as dimers (25). To examine the effect of PIP4K-mediated inhibition in the presence of higher PIP5K membrane densities that favor dimerization, we reconstituted a bistable lipid kinase-phosphatase competitive reaction that generates PI(4)P and PI(4,5)P2 compositional patterns on SLBs (Fig. 5C) (40). We previously showed that membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K is critical for establishing and maintaining these PIP compositional patterns (25). To determine whether PIP4KA destabilizes PIP compositional patterns through the direct interaction with PIP5K, we spiked in PIP4K and monitored the change in surface area of the PI(4,5)P2 patterns. Adding the wild type PIP4KA caused an immediate dissolution of the PI(4,5)P2 patterns that converged to a new steady-state condition (Fig. 5 D and E and Movie S2). In contrast, the addition of PIP4KA (M60R, L61E, M62R) showed no appreciable change in PI(4,5)P2 patterns (Fig. 5 D and E).

PIP4K–PIP5K Binding Interface Is Required to Modulate PI(4,5)P2 in Cells.

Overexpression of PIP5K paralogs in cells increases the density of PI(4,5)P2 at the plasma membrane, while co-overexpression of PIP4K paralogs suppresses this effect (30). We therefore tested if the PIP4KA–PIP5KA interface that we identified here was necessary to suppress PIP5K-mediated production of PI(4,5)P2 at the plasma membrane (PM). As expected, overexpression of GFP-PIP5KA increased PI(4,5)P2 as measured with the low affinity biosensor, TubbycR332H (41). This effect was reversed by co-overexpression of BFP-PIP4KA (Fig. 5 F and G). By contrast, a mutated BFP-PIP4KA (M60R, L61E, M62R; denoted 4K*) barely attenuated the PIP5KA-mediated increase in PI(4,5)P2 levels (Fig. 5 F and G). We made an additional PIP4KA mutant denoted 4K∆, whereby the hydrophobic loop containing M61-M62 was truncated, replacing residues 54 to 62 with a short GGSGG linker. This construct also has a far weaker ability to reverse the PIP5KA-mediated increase in PM PI(4,5)P2 (Fig. 5 F and G). To study the effects of expressed PIP4Ks on endogenous PIP5Ks, we previously generated a myristoylated PIP4KA for constitutive plasma membrane targeting (30). This construct reduces steady-state PI(4,5)P2 in cells. We once again observed a reduction in PM PI(4,5)P2 levels in cells expressing myristoylated PIP4KA, but not when we introduced the 4K* mutation or 4K∆ truncation (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B).

A potential trivial explanation for the failure of mutant PIP4KA to suppress PIP5K in cells is that the proteins adopt a nonfunctional conformation. To rule this out, we capitalized on the selectivity loop mutant A371E in PIP4KA, which was shown to swap enzyme activity from a PI(5)P 4-kinase to become a PI(4)P 5-kinase (42). A fusion of this mutant PIP4KA to FKBP (FKBP-4K5K) could be recruited to the plasma membrane by rapamycin-induced dimerization with a coexpressed FRB domain, targeted by a palmitoylated and myristoylated N-terminal peptide derived from Lyn kinase (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). This recruitment led to a robust increase in plasma membrane PI(4,5)P2 detected with low affinity TubbycR332H biosensor (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 B and D). We next introduced the 4K* mutations or 4K∆ truncation into this A371E mutant. The mutant kinases recruited as efficiently as the unmutated enzyme (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C) and retained robust PI(4)P 5-kinase activity (SI Appendix, Fig. S10D). Consistent with our biophysical characterization of purified PIP4K (SI Appendix, Figs. S7 and S8), the PIP5K interaction-disrupting mutations still allowed PIP4K to adopt a functional kinase conformation in vivo.

Previously, we demonstrated that acute plasma membrane recruitment of PIP4K orthologues decreases steady state PI(4,5)P2 (30). Therefore, we tested whether introducing the 4K* and 4K∆ mutations into the wildtype PI(5)P 4-kinase background would attenuate this decrease. Whereas acute PM recruitment of FKBP-PIP4KA showed a potent reduction of PI(4,5)P2 in HEK293A cells (Fig. 5 I and J), neither PIP4KA mutant was able to reduce PI(4,5)P2 levels (Fig. 5 I and J) despite equally robust plasma membrane recruitment (SI Appendix, Fig. S10E).

PIP4K Modulates Membrane Diffusion of Endogenously Tagged PIP5KA in Live Cells.

To date, dimerization of PIP5K has not been visualized in living cells. Although the FRET biosensor described here can inspire future efforts to visualize dimerization in vivo, the site-specific labeling strategy employed for the in vitro reconstitution of membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K does not immediately translate to cellular experiments. To visualize dimerization and quantify the plasma membrane density of PIP5K in vivo, we genome edited HEK293A cells to endogenously tag PIP5KA. Using this approach, we tagged one PIP5KA locus with mNeonGreen (mNG) and the second with a Halo tag (Fig. 6A). We confirmed the specificity of endogenous labeling by demonstrating that pooled siRNAs targeting PIP5K ablate fluorescence (Fig. 6A). Similar to observations of overexpressed PIP5K (30), endogenous mNG- or HaloTag-PIP5KA exhibit plasma membrane localization (Fig. 6A). Using dual labeled cells, we quantified the fraction of colocalization events between mNG-PIP5KA and JF635-HaloTag-PIP5KA, which yielded a probability of ~1% compared to a control (Fig. 6B). Considering the total membrane-bound density of PIP5KA equals 1 to 2 particles/µm2 (Fig. 6C), the probability of membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5KA is relatively low in wild type HEK293A cells. Comparing these results to density-dependent measurements of PIP5K dimerization on supported membranes, our in vivo measurements are in good agreement with in vitro observations (25). At a density of ~1 particles/µm2, approximately 25% of membrane-bound PIP5K molecules exist as homodimers on reconstituted membranes containing 4% PI(4,5)P2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Competing factors such as PIP4K or PIP5K paralogs, as well as lower PI(4,5)P2 densities, are expected to decrease the probability of PIP5K dimerization at the cellular plasma membrane.

Next, we sought to modulate the probability of PIP5K membrane-mediated dimerization through ectopic expression of either PIP5K or PIP4K. For these experiments we quantified the change in lateral membrane diffusion of endogenously tagged JF635-labeled HaloTag-PIP5K1A based on the single molecule displacement or step size. Changes in single molecule displacement are proportional to the frictional drag coefficient or membrane contact surface area of peripheral membrane binding proteins (43). Consistent with our in vitro measurements of PIP5K membrane-mediated dimerization (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S11), ectopic expression of wild type mNG-PIP5KA, but not a dimerization interface mutant D107R, shifted the single molecule displacement distribution to increase the fraction of short distance displacements for JF635-labeled HaloTag-PIP5K1A (Fig. 6D). As described in our previous work reporting the discovery of PIP5K membrane-mediated dimerization (25), single molecule displacement distributions can be fit to a model describing a mixture of slow (D1) and fast (D2) diffusing species. Since our in vitro experiments on SLBs revealed that dimerization shifts PIP5K diffusion toward the slower population (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S11), we evaluated cellular changes in the fraction of slow diffusing PIP5K molecules (i.e., alpha, α) across conditions. This analysis revealed that PIP5K1A overexpression, but not a dimerization deficient D107R mutant, increased the fraction of slow diffusing molecules by 1.4-fold (i.e., 19.8 to 28.6%) (Fig. 6D). This trend was observed over a broad range of mNG-PIP5KA expression levels (Fig. 6E), consistent with in vitro measurements of PIP5K membrane-mediated dimerization (SI Appendix, Fig. S11) (25). By contrast, ectopic expression of myristoylated wild type PIP4K, but not mutants, decreased the fraction of endogenous JF635-labeled HaloTag-PIP5K1A molecules that exhibited slow diffusion by 0.74-fold change or to 14.6% of the total population (Fig. 6D). Overall, these experiments demonstrate that changes in membrane diffusion can serve as a proxy for detecting PIP5K–PIP5K and PIP4K–PIP5K interactions in vivo.

We hypothesized that the low frequency of PIP5K membrane dimers detected under native conditions, results from a combination of low endogenous expression of PIP5K paralogs and relatively high expression of PIP4K. To determine whether genetic silencing of PIP4K paralogs modulates endogenous JF635-labeled HaloTag-PIP5K1A membrane diffusion, a proxy for dimerization, we treated cells with pooled siRNAs targeting all three PIP4K paralogs. As previously characterized (30), we validated the knockdown efficiency by quantifying the decrease in fluorescence intensity of endogenously tagged PIP4KA, PIP4KB, and PIP4KC in HEK293A cells (Fig. 6F). Working under conditions that caused 80 to 90% knockdown efficiency of PIP4K paralogs, we observed a decrease in membrane diffusion of endogenously expressed and JF635-labeled HaloTag-PIP5K1A corresponding to a 1.3-fold increase in the fraction of slow diffusing molecules (i.e., 19.7 to 25.4%) (Fig. 6G). This change in membrane dynamics mirrored the decrease in membrane diffusion caused by ectopic expression of mNG-PIP5KA (Fig. 6D), consistent with membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5KA being regulated by competition with PIP4K.

Discussion

We developed a smFRET sensor to directly visualize membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K on SLBs. Compared to the total dwell time of a membrane-bound kinase, the lifetime of a PIP5K dimer is relatively transient. As a result, a single PIP5K molecule can exist in a dynamic equilibrium between monomers and dimers while membrane-bound. Using the smFRET assay, we determined that PIP4K blocks membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K. This interaction is capable of attenuating PIP5K lipid kinase activity in vitro and in vivo. Although we previously reported interactions between PIP4K and PIP5K on SLBs using smTIRF microscopy (30), the molecular basis of this interaction was unknown. Recent advances in the structural prediction of protein–protein interactions (39) allowed us to model different PIP4K–PIP5K binding interfaces. While some predicted mutations failed to disrupt the PIP4K–PIP5K binding interface (i.e., PIP4K R104D, SI Appendix, Fig. S12), our mutational analysis confirmed that PIP4K residues M60, L61, and M62 mediate interactions with the dimer interface of PIP5K. Importantly, these mutations did not alter the structural integrity, catalytic activity, or intrinsic membrane binding properties of PIP4K. Comparing these mutations to those previously reported to disrupt the interaction with PIP5KA (31), we have identified the same hydrophobic loop as driving the interaction. However, the reported mutations are expected to have a less potent effect, with mostly like-for-like hydrophobic substitutions in the core MLM motif and only a single hydrophobic residue, M60, mutated to a charged residue (M60E in that case). Importantly, this hydrophobic motif is conserved even in distantly related eukaryotic clades, such as recently identified choanoflagellate and sponge PIP4K orthologues (44).

Sequence conservation shared between the dimer interfaces of PIP5K paralogs facilitates heterodimerization, which we detected in vitro by quantifying changes in single molecule dwell times, membrane diffusion, and smFRET efficiency. Although our study was unable to establish whether PIP5K paralogs prefer to homodimerize vs. heterodimerize, our results highlight the importance of considering the total cellular concentration of PIP5K when trying to predict the extent of membrane-mediated dimerization in vivo. Interestingly, PIP5KA and PIP5KB exhibit different sensitivity to PI(4,5)P2 lipids, with PIP5KA generally exhibiting longer membrane dwell times compared to PIP5KB. As a result, PIP5KA has a higher apparent activity compared to PIP5KB. One prediction is that PIP5KA-PIP5KB heterodimers will simply exhibit an intermediate activity. However, it is also possible that heterodimers will exhibit weak activity compared to homodimers. Molecular dynamic simulations indicate that membrane-bound PIP5K dimers alternate between subunits during cycles of catalysis (45). Based on this model, we speculate that PIP5KA-PIP5KB heterodimers will not efficiently toggle between subunit membrane engagement and catalysis due to an imbalance in overall membrane affinity between the subunits. Further investigation is needed to understand the probability and functional significance of heterodimerization.

Considering our results, membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K could serve an important role in controlling transient and localized bursts of PI(4,5)P2 synthesis during cell signaling. Dimerization could also facilitate interactions with small GTPases like Rac1 or Arf6, or other proteins like Daam2 or Disheveled that have been shown to interact with PIP5K (32, 46–48). PIP5K activity is also reportedly enhanced during Wnt signaling pathway and phagocytosis (3, 49, 50). In these contexts, membrane proteins could cluster PIP5K into distinct signalosomes on the inner leaflet that stimulate density-dependent dimerization via increased local concentration. The role PIP4K serves in controlling these interactions remains to be investigated. Future studies investigating PIP5K dimerization in vivo would benefit from developing new probes to more easily visualize and quantify PIP5K dimerization in live cells. The PIP5K FRET sensor described here relies on site specific labeling with fluorescent dyes in vitro. Although this tool inspires future experiments, the current form cannot easily be utilized for cell-based assays. Because of the limitation of the FRET sensor, we assessed PIP5K dimerization in vivo based on a combination of plasma membrane surface density measurements of endogenously tagged PIP5KA and changes in single molecule diffusion correlated with PIP4K and PIP5K expression. Consistent with our in vitro measurements of PIP5K membrane-mediated dimerization on SLBs, we observed changes in endogenously tagged PIP5KA membrane diffusion indicative of enhanced dimerization in the absence of PIP4K. Although the increase in slow diffusing PIP5KA molecules was only 1.3-fold, this can still explain the resulting increase in PI(4,5)P2 levels when considering that dimeric PIP5K is 20-fold more active compared to monomeric PIP5K (25).

Recent studies have implicated PIP5K activity being necessary for tumor progression in both prostate and breast cancers (51, 52). PI(4,5)P2 generated by PIP5K is a precursor for second messengers, including inositol trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG) generated by phospholipase C (PLC) at the plasma membrane. In addition, PI(4,5)P2 lipids are the substrate for PI3K-mediated production of phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate (PI(3,4,5)P3). Elevated levels of PI(3,4,5)P3 drive Akt/mTOR pathway activation, promoting undesired cell proliferation and cancer cells metastasis (53–55). Similarly, misregulation of PIP4K increases the total plasma membrane density of PI(4,5)P2, which promotes elevated levels of PI(3,4,5)P3 following stimulation with insulin (31, 56). Environmental conditions that modulate gene expression of PIP4K and PIP5K paralogs could change the total cellular concentration of PI(4,5)P2 lipids by modulating the abundance of homo- and heterodimers. It remains unclear what homeostatic mechanisms cells have evolved to respond to tissue specific changes in PIP4K and PIP5K membrane density. Having established that membrane-mediated dimerization of PIP5K is a mechanism for tuning PI(4,5)P2 synthesis, deciphering how various cellular conditions modulate PIP5K dimerization and PIP4K–PIP5K complex formation will be critical for understanding PI(4,5)P2 lipid homeostasis in healthy and diseases states.

Materials and Methods

Rational Design of PIP5KB FRET Construct.

Accessible surface area calculations were performed on zebrafish PIP5KA (4TZU.pdb) and human PIP5KB AlphaFold structure using UCSF Chimera. Following labeling of native solvent exposed cysteines in wild type PIP5KB with AF488-maleimide, the kinase was trypsin digested and analyzed by 2D electrospray ionization mass spectroscopy (ESI-MS) (UC Berkeley MS Facility). Our MS analysis resulted in 67.1% peptide sequence coverage, resulting in two of four cysteines detected in various peptide fragments (i.e., C175 and C411) (SI Appendix, Table S1). Of the detected peptides, 1% of C175 and 82% of C411 containing peptides showed an increase in molecular weight due to AF488 maleimide conjugation. Since peptides containing C110 and C256 were not detected by ESI-MS, we relied on UCSF Chimera accessible surface area calculations to estimate solvent exposure. We concluded that C110 was solvent exposed and C256 was buried. Through trial and error, we determined that mutation C175S disrupts kinase activity of recombinantly purified PIP5KB. The ideal combination of the mutations for the PIP5KB FRET construct presented here is C110S, P163C, C411S. PIP5KB FRET constructs (C110S, P163C, C411S) were fluorescently labeled via their singular surface exposed cysteine residue with either Alexa647-C2-maleimide or Alexa555-C2-maleimide (ThermoScientific). After quenching the labeling reaction with 10 mM DTT, unreacted dye was buffer exchange using a Cytiva NAP-25 column (Fisher scientific, Cat# 10004064) and then loaded on a 24 mL Superdex 200 10/300 column GE Healthcare, Cat# 17-5174-01) equilibrated in 20 mM Tris [pH 8.0], 200 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM TCEP. Peak fractions were concentrated using a 30 kDa MWCO Vivaspin 6 centrifuge tube and snap frozen at a final concentration of 1 to 30 µM using liquid nitrogen.

Protein Purification.

PIP4K and PIP5K proteins (wildtype and mutants) were purified from bacteria and insect cells, respectively, as previously described (25, 30, 35, 40). PLCδ-PH and LactC2 were purified as previously described (40, 57). Refer SI Appendix for detailed protein purification protocols.

Assay for Measuring the Kinetics of PI(4,5)P2 Production.

The kinetics of PI(4)P phosphorylation were measured on SLBs formed in IBIDI chambers and visualized using TIRF microscopy as previously described. Imaging or reaction buffer contained 20 mM HEPES [pH 7.0], 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM ATP, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM EGTA, 20 mM glucose, 200 µg/mL beta casein (ThermoScientific, Cat# 37528), 20 mM BME, 2 mM Trolox, 320 µg/mL glucose oxidase (Serva, Cat# 22780.01 Aspergillus niger), 50 µg/mL catalase (Sigma, Cat# C40-100MG Bovine Liver). Trolox was prepared with UV treatment as previously described (40). Perishable reagents (i.e., glucose oxidase, catalase, and Trolox) were added 5 to 10 min before image acquisition. For all experiments, we monitored the change in PI(4,5)P2 membrane density using a solution concentration of 20 nM AF488-GGGGG-PLCδ, Cy3-GGGGG-PLCδ, or AF647-GGGGG-PLCδ. Density of PIP lipids (lipids/µm2) was calculated assuming a footprint of 0.72 nm2 for DOPC lipids (40).

Reconstitution of PIP Compositional Patterns.

Reactions were executed on SLBs having a composition of 96% DOPC, 2% PI(4)P, and 2% PI(4,5)P2 as previously described (40). The reaction buffer contained a final concentration 20 mM HEPES [pH 7.0], 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM ATP, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM EGTA, 20 mM glucose, 200 µg/mL beta casein, 20 mM BME, 320 µg/mL glucose oxidase, 50 µg/mL catalase, 2 mM Trolox, and the protein concentrations indicated in the figure legend.

Quantification of Lipid Kinase Activity.

When comparing PIP5K lipid kinase activity across conditions (+/–PIP4K), we report the time to 95% reaction completion. Since PIP4K-mediated inhibition of PIP5K does not occur until a threshold density of PI(4,5)P2 is generated to efficiently recruit PIP4K, there is a delay in the membrane recruitment of PIP4K. Therefore, comparing the reaction half-time would yield similar PIP5K reaction kinetics in absence or presence of PIP4K.

Cell Culture and Transfection.

HEK293A cells (ThermoFisher, cat# R70507) were maintained in low-glucose DMEM (ThermoFisher, Cat# 10567022) with 10% heat-inactivated FBS (ThermoFisher, cat# 10438-034), 100 U/mL Penicillin-Streptomycin (ThermoFisher, Cat# 15140122), and 0.1% chemically defined lipid supplement (ThermoFisher, cat# 11905031), at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were passaged 1:5 twice weekly using TrypLE (ThermoFisher, cat# 12604039). For imaging, cells were seeded on 35 mm #1.5 glass-bottom dishes (CellVis, cat# D35-20-1.5-N) precoated with 5 µg human fibronectin (ThermoFisher, cat# 33016-015). At 50 to 80% confluency, cells were transfected using 1 µg DNA with 3 µg Lipofectamine2000 (ThermoFisher, cat# 11668019) in 200 µL Opti-MEM (ThermoFisher, cat# 51985091), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Imaging was performed 18 to 24 h posttransfection. For siRNA, 200 pmol siRNA pools were combined with 6.6 µL DharmaFECT in 0.2 ml serum-free media before adding to cells. Cells were imaged after a further 48 h. We used siGENOME pools against PIP5KA, PIP4KA, B, or C, vs. an ON-TARGETplus Nontargeting Control Pool siRNA (all from Horizon Discovery).

Gene Editing.

As previously described (30), we combined CRISPR/Cas9-mediated targeting of an allele with homology-directed repair to tag PIP5KA and PIP4KA/B/C at the N terminus with either mNeonGreen (mNG) or HaloTag. For additional details, refer to SI Appendix.

Refer to SI Appendix for detailed information concerning molecular biology, plasmid inventory, protein purification protocols, SLBs, genome editing, live cell imaging, single molecule tracking, and data analysis.
