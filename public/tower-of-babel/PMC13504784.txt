Family planning service quality and contraceptive discontinuation among short-acting contraceptive users in Indonesia: a survival analysis of the 2017 Indonesia demographic health survey
Authors: Lasiah Susanti, Adi Utarini, Siswanto Agus Wilopo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContraceptive discontinuation rates in Indonesia are higher among short-acting contraceptive users. The quality of family planning services was suspected to be the cause. The Indonesian Demographic Health Survey (IDHS) assesses family planning, discontinuation rates, and factors related to family planning. This study aimed to analyze short-acting contraceptive discontinuation and the quality of family planning services using The IDHS 2017 data.MethodsThis research analyzes data from the 2017 IDHS of women aged 15–49 years who had been using contraception in the last five years preceding the survey. Data on contraceptive discontinuation rates, reasons for discontinuation, contraceptive methods, sources of family planning services, caregivers, contraceptive decision makers, and contraceptive payment methods were extracted from the survey. Family planning service quality was assessed through the method information index (MII) from the 2017 IDHS. Survival analysis and the Cox proportional hazard model were used to analyze family planning service quality and contraceptive discontinuation.ResultsThe overall contraceptive discontinuation rate for short-acting contraceptive method (pills, injectable) in Indonesia was 43.6%. The complete MII or informed choice rate was 30%. Episodes with complete MII discontinue earlier than those incomplete. Approximately 60% of the discontinuations involved the use of injectables, but pill users discontinued earlier. The main reason for discontinuation is side effects. The Cox proportional hazard model revealed several variables that were significantly associated: complete MII (Hazard Ratio or HR 1.1), the use of a pill (HR 2.9) or an injectable contraceptive (HR 2.8), switching to other methods (HR 7.5), switching to long-acting reversible contraception (LARC) (HR 6,9), the use of other service sources (HR 2.0), age ≤ 29 years (HR 1.4), and age 30–34 years (HR 1.2).ConclusionsThe number of contraceptive discontinuation episodes in Indonesia was quite high, with few informed choices, although those with informed choices discontinued earlier. It is possible that those with informed choice acknowledge the contraceptive side effects and then choose to discontinue. Considering those who discontinue and switch to other methods, it is crucial to improve the quality of counseling with the aim of making optimal informed choices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04587-9.Keywords: Contraceptive discontinuation, Family planning service quality, Short-method contraceptive

Introduction

Contraceptive use offers health and non-health benefits to women of reproductive age. The use of contraceptives prevents pregnancy-related health risks, reduces the newborn and infant death rates through spacing pregnancies, and ultimately contributes to controlling population size and improving wellbeing, sustainability, and economic development [1]. Contraceptive discontinuation could increase levels of maternal morbidity and mortality, as well as infant and newborn mortality [2]. The continuation of contraceptive use has been used to evaluate family planning programs. Studies have also revealed that the quality of family planning services has a positive effect on the prevalence and continuity of contraceptive use. A study revealed predicted probabilities of contraceptive use were 55% for low-quality care, 62% for medium-quality, and 67% for high-quality care [3]. Another study found high-quality care was significantly associated with modern contraceptive use, with aOR: 3.12 [4]. Furthermore, women dissatisfied with their family planning services had 4.67 times the odds to discontinuing using LARC [5]. Hence, the discontinuation rate represents the quality of family planning services and has been used as the primary indicator of the quality of family planning services [3, 6].

Existing studies in Indonesia have predominantly concentrated on the determinants of contraceptive use or discontinuation [7–11]. Some studies have reported high discontinuation rates of hormonal contraceptive methods in Indonesia, suggesting the need for strengthening the quality and effectiveness of contraceptive counseling services [10, 12]. A study linked the structural and process quality of family planning services to modern contraceptive use and reported significant roles of structural and process quality in modern contraceptive use [11]. However, there remains a limited understanding of the factors influencing discontinuation of short-acting contraceptive methods in Indonesia, particularly regarding socio-economic determinants and the quality of information provided to users, and their implications for informed choice.

Informed choice is beneficial for the quality of family planning services. Informed choice is the result of contraceptive counseling; thus, proper counseling contributes to informed choices [13, 14]. Information exchange during contraception counseling was mentioned in the family planning quality of care framework as one of the six elements of quality. Other elements include the choice of method, interpersonal relations, technical competence, follow-up and continuity mechanisms, and an appropriate constellation of services [15].

The Method Information Index (MII) is one way to measure information exchange between providers and clients during contraception counseling. It is also an instrument to ensure that the client is given complete information during counseling [13, 14, 16]. Based on the MII, counseling should provide sufficient information to the client, including the choice of method, possible side effects, and actions if they experience side effects. Although the MII is a subjective measurement from the client’s point of view, it is important for the provider to comprehend the client’s perspective on received service quality [15].

Informed choice is strongly associated with contraceptive continuation. Studies have demonstrated that as the MII score increases, discontinuation rates decrease [6]. A demographic (i.e., age) and socioeconomic factors (education, residence, and wealth index) were associated with contraceptive discontinuation rates. One study reported that the contraceptive discontinuation rate was higher among young women aged 20–24 years and 30–34 years [16]. A case‒control study revealed that most contraceptive discontinuations occur in women with no formal education [17]. More than half women (55%) with discontinued contraception had only a primary education [18]. Contraceptive users who discontinue, citing distance from their residence to healthcare facilities that provide contraceptive services are also one of the reasons for discontinuation [17, 19, 20]. Contraceptive discontinuation often occurs in low-income women [20]. This is associated with contraception costs and access to healthcare facilities that provide contraceptive services [17].

According to the Indonesian Demographic Health Survey (IDHS) 2017, the overall discontinuation rate of Indonesian contraceptives for 12 months is 34%. With respect to the contraceptive method, the highest discontinuation rates were observed among short-acting method users: pills users (46%), injectables (28%), and condoms (27%). The discontinuation rates among LARC users, such as intrauterine devices (IUDs) and implants, were only 9% and 6%, respectively [21]. From 2007 to 2017, the Indonesian contraceptive prevalence rate recorded a very slow increase, similar to stagnant growth, at 61% in 2007 and 64% in 2017 [2]. In addition, most contraceptive users in Indonesia use short-acting contraceptives such as injectables (72.9%) or pills (19.4%). However, short-acting contraceptives are widely known for their side effects, such as menorrhagia, metrorrhagia, nausea, headache, breast pain, weight gain, mood swings, skin problems, dyslipidemia, cholestasis, migraine, hypertension, stroke, deep vein thrombosis, venous thromboembolism, myocardial infarction, and arterial diseases [22–24]. A study in Tanzania involving 3,950 subjects revealed that experiencing side effects and expecting pregnancy were the main reasons for contraceptive discontinuation [25]. Another study in Bangkok on 1,880 subjects also revealed that experiencing side effects and expecting pregnancy were the main reasons for contraceptive discontinuation [20]. Contraceptive side effects are expected and known, although they are not the intended outcome. Therefore, it is very possible to educate contraceptive users about those side effects to prevent discontinuation because they experience side effects. To accomplish this, informed choice via adequate counseling on contraceptive methods is necessary [6, 13]. Moreover, IDHS data revealed that the prevalence of Indonesian contraceptive-informed choices (MII score = 3) was only 25.3%, which is much lower than the global average of 41.43% [21]. Thus, to provide a better explanation of short-acting contraceptive method discontinuation and the quality of family planning services, further explorative research aimed at analyzing contraceptive discontinuation rates, informed choices, and other associated factors is important. This study provides new insights into the factors influencing low Method Information Index (MII) scores in Indonesia, with potential implications for strengthening the quality of family planning services.

Materials and methods

This study used data on the history of women aged 15–49 years extracted from the 2017 Indonesia Demographic Health Survey (IDHS). The IDHS 2017 is a population-based survey with nationally representative data on women of reproductive age, covering 49,261 households and 49,627 women of reproductive age. This study employed two-stage stratified sampling to ensure the representativeness of the national, regional, and residence levels of data and to minimize sampling errors. To address this, in the first stage of sampling, primary sampling units are selected proportionally to size within each stratum, using data from the Indonesia Central Bureau of Statistics to form survey clusters (by province and rural‒urban area and implicitly stratified by welfare concentration). In the second stage, a fixed number of households were selected by equal probability systematic sampling in the selected cluster. The sample size was calculated by considering the adequacy of survey indicators such as household characteristics, fertility, fertility preferences, determinants of fertility, family planning, early childhood mortality, maternal and child health, maternal and child nutrition, malaria, and HIV/AIDS. For example, to calculate fertility with adequate precision for a subnational region, samplers must include 800 women for a country with a high total fertility rate to 1,000 women for a country with a low total fertility rate. Hence, a country with five subnational regions needs approximately 5,000 women to estimate fertility and child mortality indicators. These numbers then corrected via the compromise allocation method approach, adjusted for non-response, and then distributed proportionally across 34 provinces for the urban/rural domain [26].

The data analysis included episodes of short-acting contraceptive use (injectables, pill) and long-term contraceptive use (implant, IUD) in women aged 15–49 years in the data files of the IDHS 2017 study and not in individual women. Each episode was created from a contraceptive event from contraceptive calendar data; thus, one episode presented one observation of contraceptive use for only one method. Therefore, one woman may present with one or more episodes of contraceptive use with different methods. The variables from the IDHS 2017 used for this study are described in Additional file 1. The data extraction process is shown in the flowchart (Fig. 1).

Flowchart for the data extraction process from IDHS 2017

Extracted data included all women aged 15–49 years who used contraception and whose date of onset and date of contraceptive discontinuation were 12, 24, and 36 months. Contraceptive methods include short-acting methods (pill, injectables) and LARC (Implant, IUD).

Descriptive analysis was carried out to assess the distributions of episodes, methods, demographic factors, socioeconomic factors, family planning service characteristics, and reasons for discontinuation. The distributions and bivariate analyses of contraceptive discontinuation episodes, contraceptive methods, discontinuation reason, demographic factors, socioeconomic factors, and family planning service factors associated with the MII were assessed. Survival analysis was performed to analyze family planning service quality and contraceptive discontinuation. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were drawn for MII, switching, and contraceptive methods. To obtain contraceptive discontinuation rates and assess factors associated with contraceptive discontinuation, bivariate and multivariable analyses using Cox proportional hazard model were performed for each contraceptive discontinuation within 12, 24, and 36 months. The contraceptive discontinuation rate was calculated for every 100 episodes. Multivariable analysis included only factors that were significantly associated with each other in the bivariate analysis. For the final model, a relative goodness of fit test was conducted using Akaike Information Criterion.

Results

Table 1 describes the distributions of episodes, methods, various demographic and socioeconomic factors, and family planning service characteristics. Among the contraceptive episodes, 39.7% were discontinued. Among all the discontinuation episodes, 24.2% involved switching methods. The discontinuation and switching methods were measured at 12-month intervals from 12 to 36 months. Approximately 60% of contraceptive episodes involved the use of the injectable method. Moreover, the number of contraceptive episodes involving LARC was much lower, with IUDs at 6.5% and implants rate at 8.5%. More than 60% of the contraceptive episodes have used private service sources and most of the contraceptive episodes involved family planning services by nonphysicians (81.7%). More than half (53.1%) of the contraceptive episodes were decided together by decision makers. Most contraceptive episodes involved users without an insurance payment method (88.7%).

Discussion

Global data from the DHS Program reported an average contraceptive discontinuation rate of 36.14%, with most countries reporting a contraceptive discontinuation rate > 30% [26]. Similar to our findings, Indonesia’s overall percentage of contraceptive discontinuation episodes was quite high, at 39.7%. Moreover, the IDHS 2017 results revealed that the contraceptive discontinuation rate was 28.7%. This difference is likely because this research only included episodes of injectables, pills, IUDs, and implants that provide MII data and last ≤ 36 months. According to the IDHS survey from 2002 to 2017, the rate of discontinuation of contraceptive use in Indonesia increased gradually from 21 to 29% [21]. Another study of 3,950 respondents in Tanzania reported an overall discontinuation rate of 44.6%, which was higher than the rate reported in Indonesia [25].

Survival analysis revealed that episodes with informed choices (MII score = 3) discontinued earlier than those with uninformed choices did (MII score < 3). Those with informed choice had a 10% higher hazard for contraceptive discontinuation. Several studies reported similar results, where the MII score was significantly associated with contraceptive discontinuation, and better scores could contribute to contraceptive continuation [6, 13, 27]. Amidst contraceptive discontinuation episodes, only 30% of the data were informed, which is only slightly higher than the percentage of informed choice data from the IDHS 2017 report (25.3%) and lower than the global average informed choice [28]. Research found the lowest index is for information about what to do if experiencing side effects, followed by information about side effects, and the highest index is about the availability of other methods [29].

Our study found the highest rate of discontinuation among short-acting contraceptive users, with 60% using injectables, followed by pills at 25%. Similar to the findings of a study in Uganda and Pakistan, short-acting contraceptive method was associated with a greater risk of contraceptive discontinuation [6]. This phenomenon is consistent with other studies in Tanzania and Kenya, where the highest rates of discontinuation were recorded for pills and injectables [30, 31]. In addition, hormonal methods (injectables and pills), which are known for their side effects, are highly hazardous to contraceptive discontinuation.

Contraceptive use is associated with various side effects that vary according to the underlying mechanisms of action of each method, with the majority attributable to hormonal influences. Commonly reported side effects are amenorrhea, irregular period/bleeding, and weight gain, relevant to a study from Performance Monitoring Action (PMA) Ethiopia, where bleeding changes were cited as the most frequent side effects [32]. Side effect experience may further exacerbate the adherence burden of contraceptive methods, particularly short-acting methods that require consistent user compliance. These methods, such as pills, depend on sustained user behavior and are therefore more susceptible to discontinuation when side effects occur. Our study found episodes with pills contraceptive method had a higher risk of discontinuation than injectables. Same as a study in Kenya using KDHS data that stated pill users have the highest overall propensity to discontinue [30].

One plausible explanation is that informed users are more capable of recognizing side effects and therefore make timely decisions to discontinue or switch methods. Informed choice does not necessarily imply method continuation; rather, it reflects women’s autonomy in decision-making. Discontinuation following informed counseling may represent a rational and adaptive response to experienced side effects, rather than an indication of poor service quality. The interplay of side effects, high adherence burden, and insufficient informational support contributes to the higher discontinuation rates observed among short-acting hormonal contraceptive methods compared with LARC.

Among the family planning service factors, the highest discontinuation rate was observed among those who used other family planning service sources (pharmacists, drugstores), mostly with other caregivers (nonhealthcare workers). These family planning service sources, such as pharmacists and drugstores, had an HR 2.1, with government service sources as the baseline. Considering that pharmacists and drugstores providing pills had the highest discontinuation rates, thus could be explained by these findings. Moreover, family planning services at pharmacists and drugstores were provided by nonphysician healthcare workers who were not fully trained for family planning service counseling.

Almost 90% of the contraceptive discontinuation episodes have used the non-insurance payment method. Interestingly, the highest discontinuation rate was for JKN non-PBI. However, further multivariable analysis revealed that these factors were not significantly associated. Although Indonesia has JKN as social insurance, not all healthcare services affiliated to JKN provide family planning services. As we know, family planning decisions should include both husbands and wives. In our study, most contraceptive episodes involved the decision to provide contraceptive services together, but this decision was not significantly associated with contraceptive discontinuation.

In terms of sociodemographic factors, the highest discontinuation rates were observed among those ≤ 29 years of age, those with a secondary educational level, those with residency in rural areas, and those with a poor wealth index. The Cox proportional hazard model revealed that only age ≤ 29 years and 30–34 years was significantly associated with contraceptive discontinuation. This age tends to lead to the use of short-acting contraceptives for spacing births. This could also be related to knowledge and understanding of side effects as the main reason for contraceptive discontinuation. These findings are relevant to previous research, where the highest discontinuation rates were reported for individuals aged 25–34 years [30], and younger age was associated with an increased hazard of discontinuation [30, 31, 33].

A study of the MII and its determinants using the IDHS from 2007 to 2012 revealed that selected sociodemographic characteristics (age, education, occupation, urban–rural residence, living in the Java-Bali region or not, exposure to newspaper, television or radio, parity, sources to obtain contraceptives, current method used, or knowledge of five modern contraceptives) are not significantly associated with informed choice [29]. In contrast, another study in Ethiopia reported that age, education, occupation, place of residence, and visiting private clinics were significantly associated with informed choice [34].

In Indonesia, the complex socio-cultural context may mediate the interpretation of information and consequently influence decisions to discontinue contraceptive use. As the majority of the population is Muslim, religious beliefs and the views of religious leaders should be acknowledged as important contributing factors [35, 36]. From an Islamic perspective, contraceptive use is permissible for valid reasons, such as maternal health, childcare, family welfare, and well-being by supporting pregnancy planning and appropriate birth spacing. The provided contraceptive methods should not pose significant health concerns (such as side effects), nor should they result in permanent infertility (unless there are medical conditions in which pregnancy is hazardous)[35]. However, hormonal contraceptive side effects, such as amenorrhea or irregular menstruation, are not only medical issues but also have religious implications. Altered menstrual patterns make it difficult to distinguish between menstruation (haidh) and non-menstrual bleeding (istihadhah), thereby affecting the permissibility of religious practices[37, 38]. Additionally, beliefs about preserving the body’s “natural” state may lead to the perception that altered menstrual patterns are harmful despite medical safety, thereby contributing to discontinuation [39].

Information and counseling enable contraceptive users to understand method availability, advantages, and disadvantages, and the recognition and management of side effects. Nevertheless, during the counselling process, if users perceive providers as dismissive of their concerns or as promoting a specific method, even high-quality information may be met with mistrust[40]. This underscores the importance of shared decision-making in aligning contraceptive choices with user preferences, thereby enhancing satisfaction and supporting continued use [41]. In Indonesia, decision-making tools (DMT) and the Balanced Counseling Strategy (BCS) have been introduced to support client-centered approaches[42–45]. Yet, in light of our findings—where informed users who experienced side effects were more likely to discontinue use—this suggests that the implementation of these programs has not yet been fully effective in preparing users to anticipate and manage method-related side effects. As well, findings from family planning service factors (service sources, caregivers) and sociodemographic factors (age) emphasize the necessity of strengthening regulatory frameworks and improving counselling training for service providers in non-clinical contraceptive service settings, such as pharmacies and drugstores, particularly for younger users (≤ 34 years) of short-acting contraceptive methods.

This study has several limitations. First, the use of retrospective contraceptive calendar data from the 2017 IDHS introduces the possibility of recall bias, which may affect the accuracy of reported contraceptive histories, discontinuation timing, and reasons for discontinuation. Second, the Method Information Index (MII) is based on women’s self-reported recall of counseling content and may not accurately reflect the actual quality or depth of provider–client communication. This measure is also subject to recall and social desirability bias. Third, as an observational study using secondary cross-sectional data, causal relationships cannot be established, and residual confounding may remain due to unmeasured factors such as cultural influences, method satisfaction, and fertility intentions. Fourth, the analysis is conducted at the contraceptive episode level, meaning multiple episodes may come from the same individual, potentially violating independence assumptions and affecting standard error estimates. Fifth, the study is limited to selected modern contraceptive methods, which may limit generalizability to all contraceptive users.

This study has several limitations. The use of retrospective IDHS 2017 calendar data may introduce recall bias in reporting contraceptive use, discontinuation timing, and reasons for discontinuation. The Method Information Index (MII) is based on self-reported recall of counseling and may not accurately reflect actual counseling quality, and is subject to recall and social desirability bias. As an observational study using secondary cross-sectional data, causal relationships cannot be established, and residual confounding may remain due to unmeasured factors such as cultural influences, method satisfaction, and fertility intentions. the use of contraceptive episodes as the unit of analysis means that multiple observations may originate from the same individual. This may violate the assumption of independence between observations. The study is limited to selected modern contraceptive methods which may limit generalizability to all contraceptive users. Finally, self-reported reasons for discontinuation may be oversimplified and do not fully capture the complexity of decision-making processes. Despite these limitations, the study benefits from a large, nationally representative sample and robust statistical methods, providing valuable insights into contraceptive discontinuation patterns and associated factors in Indonesia.

Conclusions

Contraceptive discontinuation episodes in Indonesia were relatively high, with a low proportion of informed choices. Users with informed choices tended to discontinue earlier, likely due to greater awareness of hormonal side effects associated with short-acting methods, leading to timely discontinuation or switching methods. However, switching commonly remained within short-acting methods, resulting in repeated cycles of switching and discontinuation.

These findings underscore the need to strengthen contraceptive counselling through structured anticipatory guidance and shared decision-making, including the use of decision-making tools (DMTs) and the Balanced Counseling Strategy, to better prepare users for side effects, support appropriate method switching, and improve continuation. However, the existence of these tools alone is insufficient, as their effectiveness is contingent upon the fidelity and quality of implementation in routine practice. In many settings, particularly high-volume and non-clinical service points such as pharmacies and drugstores, counseling is likely to be brief, variable, and not fully aligned with the intended family planning counselling principles. Regulatory efforts are therefore needed not only to standardize brief, competency-based counseling protocols, but also to ensure monitoring of implementation quality and provider adherence. In addition, targeted counseling interventions should prioritize younger women (≤ 34 years) and users of short-acting methods such as pills and injectables, who may be more vulnerable to discontinuation due to side effects and insufficient counseling support.

Future research should explore the implementation fidelity of counseling interventions such as DMT and the BCS across various service delivery settings, particularly non-clinical providers such as pharmacies and drugstores. Further studies are also needed to examine user-provider interactions in real-world counseling contexts and to assess how counseling quality influences method switching patterns and long-term contraceptive continuation.
