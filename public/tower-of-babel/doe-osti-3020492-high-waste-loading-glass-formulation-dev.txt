 SECTION 1.0
 INTRODUCTION

 Projections of the number of high level waste (HLW) canisters to be produced in the
Hanford Tank Waste Treatment and Immobilization Plant (WTP) (e.g., [1]) are based upon the
inventory of the tank wastes, the anticipated performance of the sludge treatment processes, and
current understanding of the capability of the borosilicate glass waste form. The HLW
processing rate is dependent on both the achievable waste loadings in the glass and the
achievable melting rates. The WTP HLW melter design, unlike earlier Department of Energy
(DOE) melter designs, incorporates a glass bubbler system. The bubblers create active glass pool
mixing which improves heat and mass transfer and thereby glass melting rate. The WTP HLW
melters each have a glass surface area of 3.75 m2 and depth of ~1.1 m. The two melters in the
HLW facility together are designed to produce up to 7.5 MT of glass per day at 100%
availability. Further increases in HLW processing rates can potentially be achieved by
optimization of the feed and glass formulations to increase melt rates and waste loading in the
glass product. Increasing the waste loading also has the added benefit of decreasing the number
of canisters for storage.

 The baseline WTP glass formulation efforts have been conservative in terms of
achievable waste loadings. These baseline formulations have been specified to ensure that the
glasses are homogenous, contain essentially no crystalline phases, are processable in joule­
heated, ceramic­lined melters, and meet WTP contract requirements. The overall WTP mission
will require the immobilization of tank waste compositions that are dominated by mixtures that
include aluminum, chromium, bismuth, iron, phosphorous, zirconium, and sulfur compounds as
waste loading limiting components. In order to improve waste loadings, DOE’s Office of River
Protection (ORP) initiated a testing program at the Vitreous State Laboratory (VSL) of the
Catholic University of America (CUA) to develop and characterize HLW glasses for wastes that
are limited by Al, Al plus Na, Bi, and Cr [1, 2]. Results of that work have demonstrated the
feasibility of increases in waste loading from about 25 wt% to 33­50 wt% (based on oxide
loading) in the glass, depending on the waste stream. It is expected that these higher waste
loading glasses will reduce the HLW canister production requirement by about 25% or more [3].
Furthermore, it has been shown that a key technological risk area relates to the strong
dependence of glass production rate on waste composition [3]. The extent of this variation across
the full spectrum of HLW waste types needs to be quantified in order to accurately project waste
treatment rates.

 Under a separate contract with Bechtel National Incorporated (BNI) to support the WTP,
VSL has developed and tested glass formulations for WTP HLW waste compositions to provide
data to meet the WTP contract requirements and to support system design activities [4­9]. That
work was based upon small­scale batch melts (“crucible melts”) using waste simulants. Selected
formulations were also tested in continuously fed, joule­heated melters (DM100 and DM1200)
[10­15]. That testing was directed towards waste streams from the then­planned early feed tanks
for the WTP (i.e., AZ­101, AZ­102, C­106/AY­102, and C­104/AY­101). These wastes are high

 9

in iron (AZ­101, AZ­102 and C­106/AY­102) or thorium (C­104/AY­101) and are significantly
different from those used in subsequent enhancement tests performed for ORP (i.e., wastes
limited by Al, Al/Na, Bi, and Cr). Baseline glass formulations to treat these high­Fe wastes were
developed under the BNI contract. During that time, the throughput requirement for the HLW
melter was initially 400 kg/(m2·day), which was subsequently increased to 800 kg/(m2·day). As a
result, the baseline high­Fe HLW glass formulations for WTP perform only slightly better than
the 800 kg/(m2·day) processing rate requirement. Furthermore, the baseline waste loadings for
the Fe­limited HLW compositions are only slightly higher than the BNI contract minimum. In
subsequent work performed for ORP on other HLW compositions, VSL has developed small­
scale test methods to assess the processing rates of melter feeds and included them as an integral
part of glass formulation development. This methodology was used successfully to develop glass
formulations for high­Al Hanford HLW that showed processing rates in excess of
2000 kg/(m2·day) while achieving high waste loadings. The same methodology can be applied to
the development of improved glass formulations for other Hanford HLW streams in order to
provide ORP with a significantly more robust operating envelope with reduced risk of
throughput shortfalls. Consequently, a wide range of HLW Hanford waste streams rich in
constituents such as iron [16], bismuth [17], sulfur [18, 19], chromium [20], strontium [21], and
zirconium [22], which have the potential to limit waste loadings and production rates, are being
addressed in a series of glass formulation development and melter testing tasks.

 A number of HLW batches to be processed at the WTP have P2O5 concentrations that
extend up to about thirteen weight percent on a calcined solids basis. These high­phosphorus
HLW streams frequently contain other components such as bismuth, iron, calcium, sulfur, and
various rare earth elements, which when combined with phosphorus, can form secondary phases
that can limit waste loading or lead to poor cold cap behavior and slow processing rates.
Previous studies for ORP have explored some of the HLW streams that contain relatively high
concentrations of bismuth [17] or sulfur, both of which also contained significant amounts of
phosphorus [18]; however, these studies did not focus on HLW streams for which glass waste
loading is limited primarily by phosphorus. Consequently, the present work focused on
developing high waste loading glass formulation with good processing characteristics to treat
selected high phosphorus Hanford HLW stream. Future studies will address the effects of various
forms of phosphate (e.g., aluminum, bismuth, calcium, iron, sodium) on waste loading,
secondary phase formation, and processing characteristics of other high phosphorus HLW
streams [23]. A high­phosphorus HLW composition derived from the inventory data on insoluble
components from the Best Basis Inventory (BBI) Hanford Tank Waste Operations Simulator
(HTWOS) model run (April 17, 2011) [24] was used as the basis for the present glass
formulation development and melter test work. Testing was designed to identify glass and melter
feed formulations that optimize waste loading and waste processing rate while meeting all
processing and product quality requirements. The work included preparation and characterization
of crucible melts to evaluate the effects of waste loading on glass properties and the maximum
waste loadings that can be achieved. The testing included an assessment of melt rate using a
Vertical Gradient Furnace (VGF) system to develop new formulations with both high waste
loadings and high melt rates. The results from crucible­scale testing supported subsequent
DM100 melter tests designed to further assess the enhanced glass and feed formulations with
respect to processing rate and product quality. The DM100­BL, which is one of two DM100
melter systems installed at VSL, was selected as the platform for these tests due to its extensive

 10

previous use in processing rate determination for various HLW streams and glass compositions
[3, 11­17, 20­22, 25­35]. This work builds on previous work performed at VSL for ORP to
increase waste loadings in HLW glass formulations and increase processing rates [3, 16, 17, 20­
22. 26, 27, 32, 33]. The testing was performed according to the Test Plan that was developed for
this work [36] which is responsive to an ORP statement of work 9SOW) [37].

1.1 Test Objectives

 The primary objective of the work described herein was to develop and identify HLW
glass compositions and glass forming additive blends that achieve high waste loadings and
processing rates for high phosphorus HLW streams while maintaining acceptable glass
properties. This was accomplished through a combination of crucible­scale tests, vertical
gradient furnace tests, and confirmation tests on the DM100 melter system.

1.3 DM100 Melter System
        1.3.1 DM100 Feed System

 A schematic diagram of the DM100 vitrification system is shown in Figure 1.1. The
melter feed is introduced in batches into a feed container that is mounted on a load cell for
weight monitoring. The feed is stirred with a variable speed mixer and constantly recirculated
except for periodic, momentary interruptions during which the weight is recorded. A peristaltic
pump is used to provide a uniform delivery of feed to the melt surface. Feed is directed from the
recirculation loop that extends to the top of the melter and then diverted to the peristaltic pump,
which regulates the flow of feed through a Teflon­lined feed line and water­cooled feed tube into
the melter.

 11

 1.3.2 Melter System

 Cross­sectional diagrams of the DM100­BL melter are shown in Figures 1.2.a­c. The
DM100­BL unit is a ceramic refractory­lined melter fitted with five electrodes: two pairs of
opposing Inconel 690 plate electrodes and a bottom electrode. Power can be supplied in either
three­phase or single­phase configurations. All of the tests in the present work were performed
with the upper and lower electrodes on each side connected together and powered by a
single­phase supply; the bottom electrode was not powered. Melt pool agitation is achieved by
either a removable lance entering from the top of the melter or a permanent bubbler installed
through the bottom electrode. In these tests, the lance bubbler was used. The glass product is
removed from the melter by means of an airlift discharge system. The melter has a melt surface
area of 0.108 m 2 and a variable glass inventory of between 110 kg, when only the bottom pair of
 P P

electrodes is used, and about 170 kg when both pairs of electrodes are used, which was the case
in the present tests.

 1.3.3 Off-Gas System

 For operational simplicity, the DM100­BL is equipped with a dry off­gas treatment
system involving gas filtration operations only. Exhaust gases leave the melter plenum through a
film cooler device that minimizes the formation of solid deposits. The film­cooler air has
constant flow rate and its temperature is thermostatically controlled. Consequently, under
steady­state operating conditions, the exhaust gases passing through the transition line (between
the melter and the first filtration device) can be sampled at constant temperature and airflow rate.
The geometry of the transition line conforms to the requirements of the 40­CFR­60 air sampling
techniques. Immediately downstream of the transition line are cyclonic filters followed by
conventional pre­filters and high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters. The temperatures of
the cyclonic filters are maintained above 150°C while the temperatures in the HEPAs are kept
sufficiently high to prevent moisture condensation. The entire train of gas filtration operations is
duplicated and each train is used alternately. An induced draft fan completes the system.

1.4 Experimental Procedures and Methods
 1.4.1 Feed Conversion by Vertical Gradient Furnace (VGF) Testing

 Figure 1.3 shows a schematic diagram of the VGF setup. The temperature gradient inside
the VGF is maintained by two separate sets of heating elements, both of which are arranged in
cylindrical form and aligned along their axes. The inner heater is set at 1150oC, which is the
nominal temperature of the glass pool, and the ambient heater is set at 600oC, which is similar to
the melter plenum temperature. A ceramic crucible (4 inches tall) is used to contain the reacting
melter feed. The temperature gradient in the test crucible is shown in Figure 1.4. For a typical
feed conversion test, 10 grams of glass of identical chemical composition to the test feed
(expressed on an oxide basis) is preheated in the ceramic crucible positioned in the inner heater
before the dried melter feed (to yield 17 grams of glass) is introduced. Feed reactions under the
controlled temperature gradient are allowed to continue for the designated test duration

 12

(typically, from 30 to 90 minutes) and then stopped by rapid cooling in room temperature air.
The top surface of the reacted feed material is then inspected and photographed. The crucibles
with their feed contents are then cross­sectioned to reveal the conversion progress of feed blends.
The saw cuts of the crucibles are performed dry (without lubricant) to avoid loss of any soluble
material.

 To characterize the reacted feed material, visual inspection and digital imaging of the top
(by photography) and cross section (using an optical scanner) of the reacted sample are
performed. The VGF results are used to evaluate the melt rate on a relative scale using the degree
of melting that has occurred, the structure of the feed materials that are undergoing reaction and
transformation, and the feed conversion progression with time. A VGF sample showing
relatively more feed conversion and melting is classified as fast, whereas a sample showing
relatively less feed conversion and melting is classified as slow. In cases where multiple VGF
samples are tested, a numerical ranking of relative melt rate is assigned based on a correlation
using feeds that were tested on the VGF and whose melt rates had been determined previously in
DM100 melter tests [26]. The numerical rankings of feed samples based on the extent of feed
conversion from observation of the top and cross section of VGF samples is given in Table 1.1.
The rankings span the range of 1 to 6 with a feed sample ranked 1 expected to exhibit fast
processing rate and a feed sample ranked 6 expected to show slow processing rate.

 1.4.2 Feed Samples from Melter Tests

 Feed samples were taken directly from as­received drums and the melter feed
recirculation line during each test. Feed samples were poured into a platinum/gold crucible and
placed into a programmed furnace for drying and fusion to form a glass. The glass produced
from this fusion is ground to less than 200 mesh and sealed in 20­ml vials for subsequent
analysis by X­ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF), or by acid digestion followed by
direct­current plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (DCP­AES) on the resulting solution. The
feed samples are also characterized for their density, pH, water content, and glass yield.

 1.4.3 Glass Product

 The glass product is discharged from the melter into 5­gallon steel pails periodically
using an air­lift system. The discharged product glass is sampled at the end of each test by
removing sufficient glass from the top of the pails for compositional analysis and secondary
phase determination. In addition, the Product Consistency Test (PCT, 7 days at 90oC) and
Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP) were performed on samples of the glass
product from the DM100 melter tests. The PCT and TCLP were also performed on the crucible
melt compositions prior to their selection for the melter tests to ensure their compliance with the
present WTP contract requirements.

samples are then subjected to XRF analysis to determine the concentrations of all elements
except boron and lithium. A series of National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)

        1.4.3.1 Viscosity

 The melt viscosity, η, is measured using a Brookfield viscometer. Measurements are
performed in the temperature range of 950­1250ºC and the data are interpolated to standard
temperatures using the Vogel­Fulcher equation: ln η = [A/(T­To)]+B, where A, B, and To are
fitting parameters. The equipment is calibrated at room temperature using standard oils of known
viscosity and then checked at 950­1250ºC using a NIST standard reference glass (SRM 711).
Both precision and accuracy of the viscosity measurements are estimated to be within ±15
relative%.

 1.4.3.2 Electrical Conductivity

 The electrical conductivity, σ, of each glass is determined by measuring the resistance of
the glass melt as a function of frequency using a calibrated platinum/rhodium electrode probe
attached to a Hewlett­Packard model 4194A impedance analyzer. Measurements are performed
over similar temperature ranges to those employed for the melt viscosity measurements. The
results are analyzed to obtain the DC electrical conductivity. The electrical conductivity data are
then interpolated to standard temperatures using the Vogel­Fulcher equation: ln  = [A/(TTo)] +
B, where A, B and To are fitting parameters. Estimated uncertainties in the electrical conductivity
measurements are  20 relative%.

 1.4.3.3 Product Consistency Test (PCT)

 The product consistency test (PCT, ASTM C 1285) is used to evaluate the relative
chemical durability of glasses by measuring the concentrations of the chemical species released
from 100­200 mesh crushed glass (75­149 μm) to the test solution (de­ionized water in this case).
The tests are performed at 90ºC, in accordance with the current WTP contract requirement. The
ratio of the glass surface area to the solution volume for this test is about 2000 m­1 (typically, 4 g
of 100­200 mesh glass is immersed in 40 ml of deionized water). All tests are conducted in
triplicate, in 304L stainless steel vessels, and in parallel with a standard glass included in each
test set. The internal standard is the Argonne National Laboratory Low Activity Waste Reference
Material (ANL­LRM) glass [40] and/or the Defense Waste Processing Facility (DWPF) ­
Environmental Assessment (EA) glass [41], both of which have undergone round­robin testing.
The leachates are sampled at seven days. One milliliter of sampled leachate is mixed with 20 ml
of 1M HNO3 and the resulting solution is analyzed by DCP­AES; another 3 ml of sampled
leachate is used for pH measurement.

 1.4.3.4 Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP)

 The TCLP is performed at VSL using SW­846 Method 1311, which employs leaching of
crushed glass (< 3/8”) in a sodium acetate buffer solution for 18 hours at 22C with constant
end­over­end agitation. A mass of about 100 grams of glass is leached in 2 liters of TCLP
extract, according to the extraction method for non­volatiles. The surface area to volume ratio for
this test is about 20 m­1, which is about two orders of magnitude lower than that in the PCT. The
leachates are analyzed by DCP­AES according to VSL standard operating procedures.

 1.4.3.5 Secondary Phases by SEM

 Secondary phases in the glass samples are determined by optical microscopy and
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with energy dispersive X­ray spectroscopy (EDS).
Secondary phases due to crystallization and phase separation can be identified using these
methods. Quantitative determination of the amount of crystals in glass samples is performed by
SEM in conjunction with image analysis.

 1.4.3.6 Secondary Phases by XRD

 Powder X­ray diffraction spectroscopy (XRD) data are gathered using a Thermo­Fisher
X­tra theta­theta diffractometer fitted with a Cu X­ray tube. Each sample is ground and sieved to
325 mesh particles. Five weight percent of a corundum internal standard is mixed with the
powdered sample for quantitative analysis of the crystalline phases. Each powder pattern is
scanned from 5 to 80 degrees in 2 theta using a collection time of 2 seconds per data point.
JADE9 Software is used to perform the search­match identification of the crystalline phases as
well as the whole pattern fitting to determine the weight percent of each crystalline phase in the
sample.

 1.4.3.7 Heat Treatment

 Glass samples (about 5 grams each) are heat­treated in platinum, platinum­gold, or
platinum­rhodium crucibles (5 ml) at a pre­melt temperature of 1200°C for 1 hour, followed by
heat treatment for 70 hours at prescribed temperatures between 800°C and 950°C. At the end of
the heat­treatment period, the glass samples are quenched by contacting the crucible with cold
water. This quenching freezes in the phase assemblage in equilibrium with the melt at the
heat­treatment temperature. The sample is then prepared for SEM/EDS examination or XRD
analysis by grinding and sieving. Microscopic and spectroscopic examination or quantitative
XRD is used to determine the volume fraction of crystalline phases and the identity of the
dominant crystalline phases.

 SECTION 2.0
 WASTE SIMULANT AND GLASS FORMULATION DEVELOPMENT

 A high­phosphorus HLW stream was used for the present tests. This section describes the
selected waste composition and corresponding simulant, and the testing to develop new glass
formulations.

2.1 High Phosphorus Simulant Composition

 The HTWOS model calculated the compositions of 4106 HLW batches to be processed at
the WTP, which are given in the River Protection Project System Plan, Revision 6 [42]. A
review of the compositions of these HLW batches shows that the total amount of phosphorus to
be processed is 3.9E+5 kg (as P2O5). The P2O5 concentrations in the waste batches vary from
0.59 wt% to 13.00 wt%, with 95 batches containing at least 10 wt% of P2O5. If the minimum
component limit in HLW glass for P2O5 (3 wt%) is applied to these wastes, the waste loadings
are as low as 23.07 wt% for phosphorus limited wastes. Many of the high phosphorus wastes
also contain relatively high concentrations of bismuth and sulfur. In order to select a high
phosphorus waste for the present testing, it was important to avoid wastes for which glass
formulation development will be limited by sulfur or bismuth instead of phosphorus since such
wastes have been addressed in previous tests [3, 18, 43]. In addition, the acceptable P2O5
loadings in a typical HLW borosilicate glass tend to decrease with increasing concentrations of
calcium because of the formation of apatite and other Ca­ and P­bearing secondary phases. A
survey of phase diagrams of the P2O5­CaO­Na2O­Li2O­F system shows that, in addition to
apatite which melts at 1640°C, many other types of calcium phosphates can form depending on
the glass composition. These include, for example, calcium orthophosphate (Ca3P2O8, melting
point = 1583°C), NaCaPO4 (1830°C), and Na6CaP2O9 (1800°C). These types of phosphates could
crystallize from a Ca­ and P­rich HLW glass melt and limit the achievable waste loadings. The
waste compositions from HTWOS model calculations show CaO concentrations ranging from
0.36 wt% to 10.24 wt%, with 44 batches (out of 4106) exceeding CaO concentration of 7 wt%.
These 44 waste batches (Batch 1904 through 1947), however, contain relatively low
concentrations of P2O5 (< 2.73 wt%) and were not selected for the present testing. Conversely,
the high­P HLW wastes are found to be generally low in CaO. For example, among the 95 waste
batches that contain at least 10 wt% of P2O5, the maximum CaO concentration is only 2.60 wt%.

 The waste batch selected for testing is Number 4089 in the HTWOS run. The most
abundant components in HLW Batch 4089 are Al2O3 (27.50 wt%), Na2O (24.18 wt%), and P2O5
(12.80 wt%). The amounts of Bi2O3 and SO3 are 3.43 wt% and 1.67 wt%, respectively, and the
CaO concentration is 1.74 wt%. Table 2.1 lists the composition of HTWOS waste batch Number
4089 with the minor oxides (< 0.05 wt%) removed. Removal of these oxides is intended to
maintain a manageable number of oxides in testing. There are 21 oxide components in the
abbreviated HLW composition in Table 2.1. In addition, to avoid the use of radioactive materials

 The feed and glass were processed without significant difficulties throughout the majority

observed in the glass discharge.

4.1 Analysis of Feed Samples
 4.1.1 General Properties

 Feed samples from as­received drums were analyzed to confirm physical properties and
chemical composition. The measured solids content of the as­received feed samples was used to
calculate the amount of water to be added to achieve the target solids content of 500 g glass per
liter. Melter feed samples were also taken during each melter test from either an in­line sampling
port or directly from the feed tube at the end of a test. Sample names and measured properties are
given in Table 4.1. Density, pH, water content, glass conversion ratio, and oxide composition by
XRF and DCP­AES were measured on the samples. The measured glass conversion ratios for all
feed samples from the melter tests were within five percent of the target on a weight­per­weight
basis, validating the use of the target conversion ratio for calculating glass production rates. The
water content (55.6 – 56.8%), density (1.37 – 1.40 g/ml), glass yield (0.367 – 0.379 kg/kg), and
pH (9.2 – 9.4) varied within narrow ranges for the melter feed samples. The expected increases
in feed water content and decreases in feed density were observed in melter feed samples as a
result of adjusting the solids content of the as­received feed.

 4.1.2 Chemical Composition

 The methods used for analysis of feed sample chemical compositions are described in
Section 1.4. The boron and lithium oxide concentrations from the DCP­AES analysis were used
for normalizing the XRF data since their concentrations were not determined by XRF. The
analyzed compositions of the as­received feed and feed sampled during melter tests are
compared to the target compositions in Tables 4.2 ­ 4.4. The results for the as­received feed
samples show good agreement with the target compositions and corroborate the consistency of
the feed for the major elements. All the constituents with target concentrations greater than one
percent deviated by less than nine percent and all but bismuth oxide deviated by less than five
percent from the target concentrations. Measured phosphorus concentrations were 2.85% below
target concentrations and therefore small quantities of mono basic sodium phosphate were added
to the feed to ensure the phosphate target concentration was achieved. The measured
concentrations of constituents with target concentrations greater than one percent in the feed
sampled from melter tests were all within ten percent of the target; measured phosphorus
concentrations were at or above target concentrations. Analyzed boron and lithium
concentrations were within six and two percent of target, respectively, for all as­received and
melter test feed samples, validating the use of target values to normalize XRF values in product
glasses. Low concentrations (up to 0.05 wt%) of chlorine and titanium oxide were found in each
feed sample, even though they are not included in the target compositions. These constituents,
presumably originating from trace level contamination of feed additives and chemicals used to

 23

produce the waste simulant, are not expected to have any significant impact on the glass or
processing properties of the melter feed.

4.2 Analysis of Glass Samples

 Over eight hundred and fifty kilograms of glass was produced in these tests. The glass
was discharged from the DM100 melter periodically into 5­gallon carbon steel pails using an air
lift system. The discharged product glass was sampled by removing sufficient glass from the top
of each pail for total inorganic analysis. Product glass masses and discharge date are given in
Table 4.5. Glass samples were also taken by inserting a threaded metal rod directly into the glass
pool. These “dip” samples serve to document the composition of the glass pool before and after
each test. No macroscopic secondary phases were observed in any of the discharged glasses or
dip glass samples. If any separate sulfate phase is present on the melt pool surface, it will adhere
to, and will be visible both on the surface of the threaded metal rod and on the surface of the
glass that covers the metal road as glass pool dip samples are taken. The absence of any such
observation on the dip samples indicates the absence of a separate sulfate phase on the melt pool
surface. SEM and XRD analyses of the crucible and melter glasses for crystalline secondary
phases are discussed below in Section 4.2.3.

calculate boron and lithium concentrations for discharge glasses. The XRF analysis results
compare very favorably to their corresponding target values and feed sample analyses (see

less than 10% deviation from the target values in discharge glasses from the end of each test.

analyzed compositions of dip samples are similar to contemporaneous discharge glasses.

 4.2.2 Chemical Durability of Discharge Glasses

 Samples of glass discharged from the end of the DM100 tests with full and partial sulfur
contents were evaluated for chemical durability using the PCT and TCLP methods. The PCT
results are compared to those for the benchmark DWPF­EA and the comparable crucible glasses
in Table 4.10; the TCLP results are compared to the WTP delisting limits [46, 47] and Universal
Treatment Standard (UTS) limits in Table 4.11. The chemical durability determined for the
melter glass by both of these methods is excellent. All measured normalized PCT releases for the
discharge glass samples are greater than an order of magnitude lower than the corresponding
values for the DWPF­EA glass except for lithium, which is about one­fifth of the DWPF­EA
release. All regulated TCLP leachate concentrations are less than 0.25 mg/l and well below the
WTP delisting and UTS limits. Measured PCT and TCLP releases were similar for melter and
crucible glasses, as expected. The slightly lower measured releases for lithium, sodium, boron,
chromium, and nickel for the melter glass solutions may be related to the mineral phases detected
in those glasses (see Section 4.2.3). The chemical durability of these glasses is within the range
measured on glasses produced from wastes limited by bismuth, chromium, aluminum, and
aluminum plus sodium [3], chromium and iron [20, 16], strontium [21], and zirconium [22].
These results confirm that glasses can be formulated with higher phosphorus contents and with
faster processing rates without compromising the quality of the glass product.

 4.2.3 XRD and SEM Analysis of Melter Glass Sample

 Discharge glass samples from the end of DM100 tests with each of the two glasses with
full and partial sulfur contents were subjected to XRD and SEM analysis to determine the extent
of crystal formation. The results are summarized in Table 4.12. SEM micrographs of discharge
glass samples are given in Figures 4.2 and 4.3. Both the XRD and SEM analyses indicate the
glasses contain about three volume percent apatite (Ca5(PO4)3(OH)). This amount of either
calcium or sodium phosphate was detected only in the canister cooled crucible scale glass

samples suggesting that the phosphate minerals formed during cooling. Similarly, lithium
phosphate, which was detected at 5.6 volume percent by XRD analysis in the discharge glasses,
was detected only in appreciable quantities in heat treated or canister cooled crucible glasses,

5.1 Particulate Sampling

 The melter exhaust was sampled for metals/particles according to 40­CFR­60 Methods 3,
5, and 29 at steady­state operating conditions during each test segment. The concentrations of
off­gas species that are present as particulates and gaseous species that are collected in impinger
solutions were derived from laboratory data on solutions extracted from air samples (filters and
various solutions) together with measurements of the volume of air sampled. Particulate
collection required isokinetic sampling, which entails removing gas from the exhaust at the same
velocity that the air is flowing in the duct (40­CFR­60, Methods 1­5). Typically, a sample size of
30 dscf was taken at a rate of between 0.5 and 0.75 dscfm. Total particulate loading was
determined by combining gravimetric analysis of the standard particle filter and chemical
analysis of probe rinse solutions. An additional impinger containing 2 N NaOH was added to the
sampling train to ensure complete scrubbing of all acid gases. The collected materials were
analyzed using DCP­AES for the majority of the constituents and ion chromatography (IC) for
anions. Melter emission fluxes are compared to feed fluxes and emission samples taken while
processing the same glass compositions in Table 5.1. Notice the distinction that is made between
constituents sampled as particles and as "gas". The "gaseous" constituents are operationally
defined as those species that are scrubbed in the impinger solutions after the air stream has
passed through a 0.3 µm heated filter. All samples were well within the 90 ­ 110% limits for
isokinetic sampling.

 Particulate emissions while processing the HLW­HP­16 composition constituted 0.34 and
0.23 percent of feed solids for the optimized bubbling and fixed bubbling tests, respectively.
These levels of carryover are considerably lower than the range measured while processing
various feeds containing high iron HLW simulants processed on the same melter at a temperature
of 1150oC: AZ­102 (0.57 ­ 1.47 percent) [15]; C­106/AY­102 SIPP (0.61 to 0.81 percent) [12];
the former C­106/AY­102 baseline (0.3 ­ 0.74 percent) [25]; the C­106/AY­102 high waste
loading formulation (0.66 and 0.71 percent) [16]; high chromium HLW­HCr­16 formulation
(0.70 and 0.62 percent) [20]; high strontium HLW­HSr­7 formulation (1.14 and 0.58 percent)
[21]; high zirconium HLW­ZrA­07 formulation (1.57 and 2.51 percent) [22]; high zirconium
HLW­Zr­11 formulation (3.05 and 1.77 percent) [22]. However, the emissions from the present
tests were within the range of measured carryover for previous tests with chromium, bismuth,
aluminum, and aluminum plus sodium limited HLW wastes (0.04 – 0.57) [3, 26, 27], high
bismuth HLW­Bi­F3 formulation (0.30 and 0.39 percent) [17], and high bismuth HLW­Bi­F9
formulation (0.18 percent) [17] when processed under the same melter conditions. Carryover is a
function of the composition of the feed, particularly the amount of volatile constituents in the
feed, and the conditions in the melter, particularly the completeness and uniformity of the cold
cap. The relatively low particulate carryover in the present tests, the tests with high bismuth
formulations [3, 17], and the tests with high aluminum formulations [3, 26, 27], were a result in
part of the low volatile content of the feed. Much higher levels of carryover were observed in
tests with high volatile constituents such as rhenium, cesium, and chlorine that are emitted

 27

primarily as fine particulate [29]. The volatile constituents in the HLW­HP­16 composition,
fluorine and sulfur, are emitted in larger proportion as gaseous species and therefore do not
contribute as significantly to the calculated carryover of feed solids. No clear trend of increased
emissions while processing feed with higher sulfur content was discernable. Conversely,
particulate carryover was higher while processing at higher bubbling rates, as expected.

 The composition of the particulate in the melter exhaust is reflective of the feed
composition, with some enrichments or depletions based on elemental volatility. The elements
that were present in the particulate in proportions lower than in the feed are aluminum, nickel,
silicon, and zirconium, which constitute about half of the glass on an oxide basis. As expected,
the feed elements emitted at the lowest total (particulate and gas) melter decontamination factor
(DF) were clearly sulfur and fluorine. Other elements exhibiting some volatile behavior were
boron, chromium, and potassium. The relative carryovers of magnesium and potassium are
difficult to evaluate due to their low target concentrations, trace contamination in the feed, and
high concentrations in the melt pool prior to testing. Boron, fluorine, and sulfur were the only
elements detected in the impinger solutions collected downstream of the heated particle filter in
the sampling train, which constitutes the “gas” fraction of the melter emissions.

 From Table 5.1, the sulfur and fluorine emissions are lower in Test 1 as compared to the
emissions in Tests 2 and 3. This is probably because the glass pool started with almost no F or S
(see Table 4.6). Under such conditions, more of the F and S are retained in the glass pool (and
therefore less are emitted) than when the concentrations of these species are higher, as is the case
when the glass pool reached stead­state with respect to these components. By the end of the first
test the glass pool reached close to the steady­state values for S and F, and the emission rates
from Tests 2 and 3 appear to be steady­state values. In the last two tests, the mass balance for S
is close to 100%, but is only about 66% for F (see Table 5.1 and Tables 4.6 to 4.8). This is most
likely because the feed concentration of F was lower than target. In other melter tests with
Hanford feeds the mass balance for F has generally been better.

5.3 Gases Monitored by FTIR

 SECTION 6.0
 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

 A number of HLW batches to be processed at the WTP have P2O5 concentrations that
extend up to about thirteen weight percent on a calcined solids basis. These high­phosphorus
HLW streams frequently contain other components such as bismuth, iron, calcium, sulfur, and
various rare earth elements, which when combined with phosphorus, can form secondary phases
that can limit waste loading or lead to poor cold cap behavior and slow processing rates.
Previous studies for ORP have explored some of the HLW streams that contain relatively high
concentrations of bismuth [17] or sulfur, both of which also contained significant amounts of
phosphorus [18]; however, these studies did not focus on HLW streams for which glass waste
loading is limited primarily by phosphorus. Consequently, the present work focused on
developing high waste loading glass formulation with good processing characteristics to treat
selected high phosphorus Hanford HLW stream. Future studies will address the effects of various
forms of phosphate (e.g., aluminum, bismuth, calcium, iron, sodium) on waste loading,
secondary phase formation, and processing characteristics of other high phosphorus HLW
streams [23]. A high­phosphorus HLW composition derived from the inventory data on insoluble
components from the BBI HTWOS model run (April 17, 2011) [24] was used as the basis for the
present glass formulation development and melter test work. Testing was designed to identify
glass and melter feed formulations that optimize waste loading and waste processing rate while
meeting all processing and product quality requirements. The work included preparation and
characterization of crucible melts to evaluate the effects of waste loading on glass properties and
the maximum waste loadings that can be achieved. The testing included an assessment of melt
rate using a VGF system to develop new formulations with both high waste loadings and high
melt rates. The results from crucible­scale testing supported subsequent DM100 tests designed to
further assess the enhanced glass and feed formulations with respect to processing rate and
product quality. The DM100­BL, which is one of two DM100 melter systems installed at VSL,
was selected as the platform for these tests due to its extensive previous use in processing rate
determination for various HLW streams and glass compositions [3, 11­17, 20­22, 25­35].

 Glass formulations were developed and tested for waste Batch 4089 with waste loadings
ranging from 36.50 wt% to 52.00 wt%, which corresponds to P2O5 loadings in glass of 5.00 wt%
to 7.12 wt%. Crucible scale testing was used to collect various property data on these
formulations. The results suggested that in addition to crystallization of secondary phases (as is
the case with many other HLW glass formulations), sulfate solubility was a primary waste
loading limiting factor. The major secondary phases observed in these tests were spinel and
calcium/sodium phosphate. Another secondary phase that is observed much less frequently in
HLW glasses, lithiophosphate, was identified by XRD. High resolution scanning electron
microscopy showed that this phase occurred as nano­sized particles. The presence of
lithiophosphate is unlikely to affect melter processing significantly, as confirmed during melter
testing. The two candidate glasses considered for melter testing have rather different waste
loadings. The waste loading in HLW­HP­08 is only 37 wt%, among the lowest in the glasses
tested. However, not only does this glass meet all processing and product quality requirements, it

 30

The Catholic University of America High Waste Loading Glass Formulation Development for High-P HLW
Vitreous State Laboratory Final Report, VSL-17R3700-1, Rev. 0

also has a sulfate solubility (0.98 wt% SO3 in glass) that can easily incorporate all of the sulfur in
the waste (0.66 wt% SO3 in glass). In contrast, the waste loading of HLW­HP­16 is much higher
(51 wt%, 6.99 wt% P2O5 in glass) but its sulfate solubility is lower than the nominal SO3 target
(0.91 wt% SO3 in glass), which is higher than in HLW­HP­08 because of the increased waste
loading. In addition, crucible scale melt rate screening tests using the VGF showed that the
formulation HLW­HP­16 was fast melting; the conversion of feed to glass was essentially
complete in about 30 minutes. Based on these collected data and the consideration that the
objective was to process a high phosphorus loading formulation, HLW­HP­16 was selected as
the target glass to support melter tests. Further, it was decided that an initial series of melter tests
would employ a sulfur target of 0.5 wt% (as SO3 in glass). This target would be raised to the
nominal 0.91 wt% in subsequent tests to investigate the retention of sulfur in the melt.

 Melter tests were conducted on the DM100 with the new HLW­HP­16 glass formulation
using the Batch 4089 high­phosphorus HLW simulant. Tests on the DM100 were conducted at
1150oC at the nominal bubbling rate of 9 lpm and also with optimized bubbling to achieve
maximum production rates; these conditions were selected to allow comparison to results
obtained previously with other HLW simulants [16, 17, 20­22, 27]. The feeds processed well as
the feed rate was adjusted to provide the desired complete cold cap. Glass production rates of
1200 and 1600 kg/m 2 /day were achieved with nominal and optimized bubbling, respectively.
 P P

The results from the melter tests confirmed those from the VGF testing, demonstrating high glass
production rates with the high phosphate HLW stream. The results demonstrate that processing
rates can exceed the WTP HLW baseline requirement by a factor of two using the HLW­HP­16
formulation in combination with optimization of melt pool bubbling.

 The melter exhaust was sampled during each test for particulate and gaseous species to
determine the effect of glass composition, waste sulfur content, and bubbling rate on emissions.
Particulate emissions while processing the HLW­HP­16 composition constituted only 0.34 and
0.23 percent of feed solids for the optimized bubbling and fixed bubbling tests, respectively.
These levels of carryover are considerably lower than the range measured while processing
various feeds containing high iron HLW simulants processed on the same melter at a temperature
of 1150oC but comparable to measured carryover for previous tests with chromium, bismuth,
aluminum, and aluminum plus sodium limited HLW wastes. The relatively low particulate
carryover in these tests was a result in part of the low volatile content of the feed. The
composition of the particulate in the melter exhaust is reflective of the feed composition with
some enrichments or depletions based on elemental volatility. The feed elements emitted at the
lowest total (particulate and gas) melter decontamination factor were clearly sulfur and fluorine
with boron, chromium, and potassium exhibiting some volatile behavior. Particulate collected
from cyclones downstream of the transition line largely confirm the trends observed in the
particulate emission samples. No phosphate minerals were detected by XRD analysis in the
cyclone deposits. Boron, fluorine, and sulfur were the only elements detected in the impinger
solutions collected downstream of the heated particle filter in the sampling train, which
constitutes the “gas” fraction of the melter emissions. Gaseous emissions of nitrogen oxides and
byproducts of incomplete combustion, such as carbon monoxide and ammonia, were very low
due to the low levels of nitrates and organic carbon in the feed. The concentrations of measured
species, most notably water, carbon dioxide and nitrogen monoxide, were higher with optimized
bubbling as compared to fixed bubbling in response to the higher feed rates with optimized

 31

bubbling in tests with both of the compositions, as expected. Hydrogen fluoride was an
exception, increasing in exhaust concentration as the glass reaches steady fluorine concentration.

 Glass samples from the crucible and melter tests were subjected to leach testing using the
PCT and TCLP methods in order to evaluate product quality. The glass products significantly
out­performed the DWPF­EA benchmark glass on the PCT leaching procedure by more than an
order of magnitude and exhibited TCLP leachate concentrations that are well below the WTP
delisting limits.

 Discharge glass samples from the end of DM100 tests with each of the two glasses with
full and partial sulfur contents were subjected to XRD and SEM analysis to determine the extent
of crystal formation. Both the XRD and SEM analysis indicate that the glasses contain about
three volume percent apatite (Ca5(PO4)3(OH)). This amount of either calcium or sodium
phosphate was detected only in the canister cooled crucible scale glasses suggesting that the
phosphate minerals formed during cooling. Similarly, lithium phosphate, which was detected at
5.6 volume percent by XRD analysis in the discharge glasses, was detected in appreciable
quantities only in heat treated or canister cooled crucible glasses, again suggesting that the
lithium phosphate forms during cooling. Detection of the lithium phosphate was challenging by
SEM analysis due to the submicron size of the phase. Spinels were detected in the discharge
glasses at about 0.4 volume percent based on the SEM analysis and 0.8 volume percent based on
the XRD analysis. Again, these levels of spinel formation are consistent with heat treated
crucible glasses and not as­melted samples, suggesting formation during cooling. Spinel
formation may also be encouraged by the partitioning of lithium to mineral phases leaving less
lithium in the glass to suppress spinel formation. No clear differences can be discerned between
the two glasses with different concentrations of sulfur; no sulfur bearing secondary phases were
detected.

 The results of the testing presented herein further demonstrate the viability of the
enhancement and optimization strategies that have been developed in previous work for ORP [3,
16, 17, 21­22, 26, 27, 32­35, 45]. The results extend the potential for enormous savings in cost
and schedule that result from that work. It is recommended that testing and evaluation of these
very successful strategies be continued in order to provide a solid basis for their broad
implementation in order to maximize the cost and schedule benefits while minimizing technical
risk. The formation of secondary phases containing phosphorus in the glasses from the present
tests suggests that further work is needed on the formulation of glasses with high phosphorus
HLW in order to develop strategies for the suppression of crystallization. This is being addressed
in part by the development of glasses and testing with another high phosphorus HLW
composition [23].
