Joint trajectories of physical frailty and social relationships and associated long-term care needs among Japanese community-dwelling older adults: a 6-year longitudinal study
Authors: Mengjiao Yang, Yang Liu, Kumi Watanabe Miura, Munenori Matsumoto, Dandan Jiao, Zhu Zhu, Xiang Li, Mingyu Cui, Jinrui Zhang, Meiling Qian, Lujiao Huang, Tokie Anme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical frailty and social relationships are key factors in older adults’ health. However, their joint trajectories and associations with long-term care needs remain unclear. This study aimed to identify joint trajectories of physical frailty and social relationships among Japanese community-dwelling older adults and evaluate their association with long-term care needs.MethodsThree waves of data were collected between 2014 and 2020 in suburban Japan. Of 1,004 older adults aged ≥ 65 years, 704 initially independent participants with at least two waves of data were included in the analysis. Physical frailty was assessed using the physical strength dimension of the Kihon Checklist. Social relationships were evaluated by Index of Social Interaction, and joint trajectories were identified using parallel process latent class growth models. The associations between trajectories and long-term care needs were examined by Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazard regression models. Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the findings to be robust, including multiple imputations for missing covariates, propensity score matching to address potential bias due to high dropout rates in wave 3, and Fine-Gray competing risk models for considering the competitive risk of mortality.ResultsThree dual trajectories were identified: low frailty and high social relationships (78.0%), progressive frailty and maintenance of social relationships (14.8%), and progressive frailty and rapid social relationships decline (7.2%). Over the 6-year follow-up, 103 participants required long-term care. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed significant differences in independence rates among groups (log-rank P < 0.001). Compared to the low frailty and high social relationships subgroup, the progressive frailty and maintenance of social relationships subgroup exhibited a 133% higher risk of requiring long-term care (adjusted HR: 2.33, 95% CI: 1.33–4.06), whereas the progressive frailty and rapid social relationships decline subgroup had a 285% higher risk (adjusted HR: 3.85, 95% CI: 2.05–7.22). The results of the sensitivity analysis confirmed these associations.ConclusionsThere are distinct joint trajectories of physical frailty and social relationships among Japanese community-dwelling older adults. These trajectories are associated with different risks of long-term care needs, with the progressive frailty and rapid social relationships decline subgroup facing the greatest burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07829-5.Keywords: Frailty, Long-term care, Trajectory, Aging, Social support

Introduction

Global population aging has emerged as a significant demographic trend, particularly in Japan, where the proportion of people aged 65 years and older is projected to reach 40% by 2070 [1]. Health among older adults is multidimensional [2], with physical frailty and social relationships identified as key determinants. Physical frailty is defined as a clinically recognizable state of increased vulnerability resulting from a decline in the reserve and function of multiple physiological systems associated with aging [3]. Notably, 5.6%–11.3% of community-dwelling older adults in Japan experience frailty [4, 5] and are more likely to experience adverse health outcomes, including falls and disability. Concurrently, strong social networks have been associated with reduced mortality, enhanced mental health, and improved quality of life [6, 7]. In Japan, 24.1% of older adults experience social isolation, with higher rates observed among those living alone or in urban areas [8].

Frameworks such as the disablement process model (DPM) [9] and the socioemotional selectivity theory (SST) [10] provide a foundation for understanding the relationships between physical frailty, social relationships, and health outcomes. The DPM conceptualizes disability as a progressive process influenced by risk factors and interventions, wherein social support may assist in delaying progression from impairment to disability [9]. Meanwhile, the SST proposes that advancing age and perception of limited time lead individuals to actively prioritize emotionally meaningful social relationships over other social goals [10]. These frameworks are also related to the concept of resilience in aging, which has been defined as the ability to maintain or recover well-being despite adversity [11]. From this perspective, social relationships may support adaptation to age-related challenges among older adults by providing emotional resources, practical assistance, and opportunities for social engagement [11, 12].

Accumulating longitudinal evidence suggests that social relationships are associated with the onset and progression of physical frailty. Poor social connections, including social isolation and loneliness, have been identified as risk factors for frailty. In a large cohort study in England, loneliness was associated with a higher risk of developing frailty over four years [13]. At the same time, a trajectory analysis in China also documented that higher social isolation was associated with increased risks of transitioning to pre-frailty and frailty [14]. In contrast, greater social engagement has been linked to more favorable frailty trajectories [15]. Evidence from Japanese community-dwelling older adults further indicates that frailty and social relationships are bidirectionally associated [16]. These associations may operate through behavioral, psychological, and physiological pathways, including the promotion of health-related behaviors [17], reduced stress and depressive symptoms [18], and lower inflammatory and cardiovascular risk [19, 20].

As is well documented, frailty is a dynamic condition, with individuals transitioning between robust, pre-frail, and frail states over time [21], and different frailty trajectories have been associated with varying risks of disability and mortality [22, 23]. Similarly, social relationships are not static in later life; sustained social participation has been associated with lower risks of disability and mortality even among frail individuals [24], whereas increasing social isolation has been associated with frailty progression and higher mortality risk [14]. A recent study modeled dual trajectories of social participation and frailty index in Chinese older adults and identified associations between the two trajectories [25]. However, the implications of these joint trajectories for long-term care needs remain elusive.

In Japan, the need for long-term care is determined through the certification process of the Long-Term Care Insurance (LTCI) system, which evaluates functional abilities across physical, cognitive, and behavioral domains to determine eligibility for care services [26]. Noteworthily, physical frailty has been identified as a key predictor of LTCI certification among community-dwelling older adults. Indeed, earlier studies have demonstrated that frailty is associated with an increased risk of long-term care needs [27] and higher long-term care costs [28]. Social participation has also been associated with lower risks of disability even among frail older adults [29]. In addition, older adults with both physical frailty and social isolation have been reported to have higher rates of LTCI use compared with those with either condition alone [30], and overlapping deficits across frailty domains, including social domains, have been associated with incident LTCI certification [31]. These findings collectively suggest that physical frailty and social relationships may jointly influence long-term care needs.

Therefore, the associations between social relationships and a lower risk of long-term care during concurrent progression of physical frailty remain to be elucidated. It is important not only for clarifying the interplay between physical and social aging but also for the early identification of older adults at elevated risk of care dependency. The current study aimed to identify joint trajectories of physical frailty and social relationships among Japanese community-dwelling older adults over a 6-year period. The objectives of this study were as follows: (1) to identify joint trajectories of physical frailty and social relationships among Japanese community-dwelling older adults over the 6-year study period, and (2) to explore the associations between these joint trajectories and long-term care needs.

Methods

Design and participants

The data used in the study were derived from the “Community Empowerment and Care for Well-being and Healthy Longevity: Evidence from a Cohort Study (CEC),” an ongoing community-based longitudinal cohort initiated in 1991 in a suburban municipality in Aichi Prefecture, central Japan. This project surveys approximately 4,500 residents every three years to explore factors associated with well-being and longevity. Data are collected using a drop-off/pick-up method, in which questionnaires are distributed by municipal staff, volunteers, and researchers and collected approximately two weeks later [32, 33]. For participants who had difficulty completing the questionnaire independently, trained researchers or volunteers assisted them through face-to-face interviews. The present analysis employed data from three waves of surveys conducted in 2014, 2017, and 2020, and participants were followed regarding their care needs during this period.

The inclusion criteria comprised community-dwelling older adults aged 65 years and older, whereas the exclusion criteria included baseline receipt of long-term care, a diagnosis of dementia, or availability of fewer than two waves of data on physical frailty or social relationships. As illustrated in Supplement Fig. 1, 1,213 older adults aged 65 years and over were identified at baseline. Among them, 1,004 participants completed the baseline survey (response rate of 82.8%). After excluding 86 participants who required long-term care, 17 with dementia, and 197 with data from only one wave, a total of 704 participants were finally included in the analysis.

Assessment of physical frailty

Physical frailty was assessed using the physical strength domain of the Kihon Checklist (KCL) [16, 34]. Assessments were conducted at three survey waves, Wave 1 (T1, 2014), Wave 2 (T2, 2017), and Wave 3 (T3, 2020), at approximately three-year intervals over the six-year study period. This domain consists of five items, including the ability to rise from a chair without support, the ability to climb stairs without using a handrail or wall for support, the ability to walk continuously for 15 min, a history of falls within the past year, and fear of falling. The total score ranges from 0 to 5, with higher scores reflecting greater physical frailty.

Each item is scored on a binary scale, with 0 indicating better physical function and 1 indicating poorer function. For the first three items, a response of “Yes” is scored as 0 and “No” is scored as 1. Conversely, for the last two items, a response of “No” is scored as 0, while “Yes” is scored as 1.

Assessment of social relationships

Participants’ social relationships were assessed using the index of social interaction (ISI) [35], a validated and reliable instrument [33, 36]. The ISI was administered at T1 (2014), T2 (2017), and T3 (2020). It is composed of 18 items across five domains: social curiosity, independence, interaction, participation, and feeling of safety. The total score ranges from 0 to 18, with higher scores representing a higher level of social relationships.

The social curiosity domain evaluates behaviors such as engagement in reading, willingness to learn new technologies, pursuit of hobbies, and perceived social value. The independence domain evaluates motivation to live and maintain a healthy, active life. The interaction domain measures communication with both family members and other individuals. The participation domain investigates involvement in social and community groups, as well as engagement in active social roles. Lastly, the feeling of safety domain explores access to counseling and emergency support.

Each item is scored on a binary scale, with positive responses (always, often, sometimes) scored as 1 and negative responses (rare) scored as 0.

Outcome measures

The onset of long-term care needs was determined through certification through the Long-Term Care Insurance (LTCI) system, and data were acquired from the local government. The follow-up period started on May 1, 2014, and ended on December 31, 2020.

Covariates

The selection of covariates was informed by previous studies and clinical experience [37–40] and comprise age, gender (male, female), living arrangement (with others, alone), subjective economic status (better-off, poor), smoking status (former or current, never), drinking status (non-daily, daily), physical activity (active, inactive), and chronic disease status.

Subjective economic status was evaluated via self-evaluation with four response options: “Very comfortable,” “Fairly comfortable,” “Fairly difficult,” and “Difficult.” These responses were subsequently grouped into two categories, with “Very/Fairly comfortable” representing better-off status and “Difficult/Fairly difficult” indicating poor economic status. Additionally, participants self-reported chronic conditions, including hypertension, stroke, heart disease, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, respiratory disease, liver-stomach disease, kidney disease, musculoskeletal disorders, blood and immune system disorders, cancer, depression disorder, and eye and ear disorders. The number of disease states was categorized as 0, 1, or 2 and over.

Statistical analysis

The parallel-process latent class growth model (PP-LCGM) was applied to identify subgroups of participants with similar joint trajectories of physical frailty and social relationships. This method estimated trajectories of physical frailty scores and ISI scores across three waves and classified participants into latent classes based on their trajectories. Linear growth functions (intercept and slope) were specified for each process within each class. The optimal number of latent classes was determined by comparing model fit across solutions with increasing numbers of classes using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample size-adjusted BIC (a-BIC), Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT), and Vuong-Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (VLMR) [41]. Models with lower BIC values, entropy values greater than 0.70, significant BLRT and VLMR tests (P < 0.05), and a minimum class proportion exceeding 5% were preferred [42]. Full information maximum likelihood estimation was employed to handle missing data.

Descriptive statistics, including ANOVA (one-way analysis of variance), χ² or Fisher’s exact test, and Kruskal–Wallis test, were performed to compare baseline characteristics. Subsequently, Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curves and Cox proportional hazards models were utilized to explore the relationship between joint trajectories and long-term care needs and to assess the proportionate hazard assumption. Univariate analyses were conducted using the Cox proportional hazards model, and variables showing statistical significance were subsequently included in the multivariate model.

Thereafter, a series of sensitivity analyses was performed. (1) Multiple estimations were performed using chained equations to impute missing covariate data. (2) To address the high dropout rate in wave 3 (2020), propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to mitigate potential selection bias in the longitudinal analysis. Among the included participants (N = 704), 383 participants completed the whole follow-up, whilst 321 participants dropped out at wave 3. Logistic regression was used to calculate propensity scores, employing a 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching strategy with a caliper width of 0.25 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Matching variables encompassed age, gender, subjective economic status, living arrangement, drinking status, smoking status, physical activity, disease status, physical frailty, baseline ISI scores, and joint trajectory class. Standardized mean differences (SMD) were used to evaluate the balance of matched characteristics, with an SMD < 0.1 indicating adequate balance. This matched dataset was then used for further Cox regression analyses to examine the association between joint trajectories and long-term care needs. (3) To account for the competing risk of death, Fine-Gray competing risk models were used to validate the relationship between joint trajectories and long-term care needs.

Mplus version 7.4 was used for parallel-process latent class growth modeling. KM survival curves were plotted using GraphPad Prism version 9.0. SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.3.0 were used for the remaining analyses (R packages included “mice” for multiple imputations, “MatchIt” for PSM, and “riskRegression” for Fine-Gray regression). A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

During the 6-year follow-up period, 103 participants (14.6%) developed long-term care needs. Among them, 45 were from the low frailty and high social relationships group, 23 from the progressive frailty and maintenance of social relationships group, and 35 from the progressive frailty and rapid social relationships decline group. Within each trajectory group, 8.2% (45/549) of participants in the low frailty and high social relationships group, 22.1% (23/104) of those in the progressive frailty and maintenance of social relationships group, and 68.6% (35/51) of those in the progressive frailty and rapid social relationships decline group developed long-term care needs.

As delineated in Fig. 2, significant differences were noted in independent probabilities across joint trajectories (log-rank P < 0.001). The proportional hazards assumption was verified through examination of log-log survival curves and evaluation of time-dependent covariates in the Cox model. Table 3 details hazard ratios (HRs) for each variable relative to the 6-year risk of long-term care needs. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analyses (joint trajectory classes, age, and physical activity) were incorporated into multivariate analyses and further adjusted for gender and disease status. Multivariate analyses revealed a 133% (adjusted HR 2.33, 95% CI 1.33–4.06) increased risk of long-term care needs in the frailty progression and maintenance of social relationships group, as well as a 285% (adjusted HR 3.85, 95% CI 2.05–7.22) increased risk in the frailty progression and rapid social relationships decline group compared to the low frailty and high social relationships group.

The results of Cox regression analyses following multiple imputations are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Compared to the low frailty and high social relationships subgroup, the progressive frailty and maintenance of social relationships subgroup exhibited a 155% higher risk of requiring long-term care (adjusted HR: 2.55, 95% CI: 1.52–4.28), whereas the progressive frailty and rapid social relationships decline subgroup demonstrated a 247% higher risk (adjusted HR: 3.47, 95% CI: 1.92–6.29).

The characteristics of wave 3 participants and dropouts before and after PSM are summarized in Supplementary Tables 3 and Supplementary Table 4, respectively. As illustrated in Supplementary Table 3, significant differences in baseline age, physical activity, physical frailty, ISI scores, and class distribution were observed between the two groups. Following PSM, a balanced dataset (N = 384) was generated, consisting of 192 dropouts and 192 participants. As presented in Supplementary Table 4, most variables achieved SMDs below 0.1, confirming a good balance between the two groups post-matching. Next, Cox regression analyses were conducted using this matched dataset to examine the association between joint trajectories and long-term care needs (Supplementary Table 5). Multivariate modeling uncovered a 169% (adjusted HR 2.69, 95% CI 1.39–5.20) increased risk of long-term care needs in the frailty progression and maintenance of social relationships group, and a 283% (adjusted HR 3.83, 95% CI 1.61–9.15) increased risk in the frailty progression and rapid social relationships decline group compared to the low frailty and high social relationships group.

During the follow-up period, 7.81% of participants (55 of 704) died. Among them, 22 died after experiencing long-term care needs, whereas the remaining 33 died prior to the occurrence of this outcome. Taking into account this competing risk, Fine-Gray analysis identified significant variations in the cumulative incidence function of long-term care needs across joint trajectories (P < 0.001). These variations are depicted in Supplementary Fig. 2. Supplementary Table 6 provides the detailed results of the competing risks models. The Fine-Gray model unveiled a 154% (adjusted HR 2.54, 95% CI 1.46–4.41) increased risk of long-term care needs in the frailty progression and maintenance of social relationships group, and a 247% (adjusted HR 3.47, 95% CI 1.80–6.73) increased risk in the frailty progression and rapid social relationships decline group compared to the low frailty and high social relationships group. Sensitivity analyses, which included multiple imputations, PSM, and Fine-Gray models, yielded findings consistent with the primary analyses, thereby validating the robustness of the results.

Discussion

This study identified three distinct trajectories of physical frailty and social relationships among Japanese community-dwelling older adults: ‘Low Frailty and High Social Relationships’ (78.0% of participants), ‘Progressive Frailty and Maintenance of Social Relationships’ (14.8%), and ‘Progressive Frailty and Rapid Social Relationships Decline’ (7.2%). Differences in long-term care needs were observed across these trajectories, with older adults experiencing declines in both physical frailty and social relationships facing the greatest burden of long-term care needs. The primary finding of this study is that maintaining high social relationships was associated with lower long-term care needs, even among older adults with progressive physical frailty.

The analysis revealed that most participants maintained both low frailty and high social relationships throughout the study period, suggesting considerable resilience in these domains [11, 12, 43]. This may be attributed to their established social networks and support systems, which aid in protecting against life stressors and adverse events [44–46]. Additionally, a distinct group that maintained strong social connections despite increasing physical frailty was identified. This result was consistent with the DPM and SST [9, 10], implying that maintaining social relationships may attenuate the impact of physical decline on care needs.

A comparison of the two progressive frailty groups revealed an unexpected finding. Although the progressive frailty and rapid social relationships decline group showed more rapid frailty progression, the proportion of individuals with two or more chronic conditions at baseline was lower than that in the progressive frailty and maintenance of social relationships group (29.4% vs. 42.3%). Several factors might account for this finding. To begin, individuals who experienced parallel declines in both domains might have faced more rapid disease progression or developed additional health conditions more frequently over time, despite a lower initial disease burden. Moreover, lower baseline social engagement might have limited their capacity to preserve social connections as their health status deteriorated [47, 48]. While previous studies have identified poor social relationships as a risk factor for physical frailty [13], the results of this study indicated that individuals with a decline in both physical and social domains were at a greater risk of needing long-term care, thereby highlighting their cumulative impact [49].

Although Class 1 and Class 2 showed similar levels and trajectories of social relationships, marked differences were noted in physical frailty progression. Of note, the higher LTC risk observed in Class 2 suggests that progression of physical frailty may contribute to LTC dependency even when social relationships are relatively well maintained. In contrast, the progressive frailty and maintenance of social relationships group demonstrated the maintenance of social ties despite progressive frailty, resulting in lower long-term care needs. This may be ascribed to strong pre-existing social relationships, adaptability, and access to supportive resources that allowed the participants to remain socially engaged regardless of functional limitations [50, 51]. It is worthwhile emphasizing that previous studies have identified other potential pathways. For instance, strong social relationships encourage physical activity [5] and adherence to medical treatments [52], while also providing emotional support that minimizes stress and fosters a sense of purpose [48, 53]. At the physiological level, social integration has been associated with lower inflammation [19] and improved cardiovascular outcomes [20], both of which may delay functional decline.

The findings of this study suggest the need for differentiated intervention strategies based on joint trajectories. For the majority group maintaining low frailty and high social relationships, preventive programs focusing on sustaining both domains may be beneficial. For older adults experiencing physical decline while maintaining social relationships, interventions could prioritize physical rehabilitation while supporting their existing social connections. Those experiencing declines in both domains would potentially benefit from comprehensive support, including physical rehabilitation, social interaction opportunities, and environmental modifications to help maintain their independence [54–56]. Integrated community-based programs such as group exercise classes or volunteer activities may offer a practical approach to simultaneously promote physical activity and social engagement [55, 56]. We recommend incorporating social relationship assessments into routine community health screenings, along with measures of physical frailty. Using validated social relationship scales within existing geriatric assessments, or developing novel tools capable of evaluating both physical and social aspects, could facilitate the timely identification of at-risk individuals and more effectively guide care planning [57].

A key strength of this study is its 6-year longitudinal design, highlighting the dynamics of physical frailty and social relationships over time. Furthermore, a parallel-process latent class growth model was employed, providing a novel perspective on the development of these factors. Nevertheless, several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgment. First, participants excluded from the analysis tended to be older, physically inactive, have a poorer status of physical frailty and social relationship at baseline, and were more likely to need long-term care and die during follow-up (Supplementary Table 7). Thus, this selection pattern may have resulted in an underestimation of the associations between physical frailty, social relationships, and long-term care needs. Second, physical frailty was evaluated using a self-reported method, which may not reflect objective physical function accurately. Future studies are warranted to incorporate objective measures such as grip strength and gait speed to provide a more comprehensive assessment of physical frailty [3]. Third, this study was limited to three waves of data collection. Future studies with more frequent assessments could offer a more detailed characterization of dynamic changes in physical frailty and social relationships. Fourth, the study was limited to Japanese community-dwelling older adults and their specific LTCI insurance system, which may limit generalizability to other populations. Finally, validated continuous measures of cognitive function and depressive symptoms were not available. While individuals with dementia at baseline were excluded, cognitive decline during follow-up was not assessed. Depression was included within the disease burden variable; nonetheless, only a minority of participants reported depressive disorders, and no validated symptom scale was available. These factors may not have been fully captured, potentially resulting in residual confounding. Further studies are necessitated to incorporate validated measures of cognitive function and depressive symptoms to comprehensively account for these potential confounders.

Conclusions

This study identified three distinct joint trajectories of physical frailty and social relationships, demonstrating heterogeneity in the development of these factors among community-dwelling older adults in Japan. Overall, these findings highlight the protective role of maintained social relationships against long-term care needs, even among older adults experiencing progressive physical frailty. By simultaneously considering both domains, more effective strategies can be developed to promote healthy aging and reduce long-term care needs of the aging population.
