LaneRoPE: Positional Encoding for Collaborative Parallel Reasoning and Generation
Gabriele CesaQualcomm AI ResearchThomas Hehn, Qualcomm AI ResearchAleix Torres-Camps, Qualcomm AI ResearchÀlex Batlle Casellas, Qualcomm AI ResearchJordi Ros-Giralt, Qualcomm AI ResearchArash Behboodi, Qualcomm AI ResearchTribhuvanesh Orekondy, Qualcomm AI Research

Abstract
Parallel LLM test-time scaling techniques (e.g., best-of- N N ) require drawing N > 1 N>1 sequences conditioned on the same input prompt. These methods boost accuracy while exploiting the computational efficiency of batching N N generations. However, each sequence in the batch is traditionally generated independently and hence does not reuse intermediate generations, computations, or observations from other sequences. In this paper, we propose LaneRoPE to enable coordination and collaboration among N > 1 N>1 sequences at generation time. LaneRoPE involves two key ideas: (a) an inter-sequence attention mask to make sampling of sequences dependent on one another; and (b) a RoPE extension that injects positional information that captures relative positions between tokens, both within and outside a particular sequence. We evaluate our approach on mathematical reasoning tasks and find promising results: LaneRoPE enables collaboration among sequences, yielding additional accuracy gains under limited generated sequence length. Importantly, since LaneRoPE enables coordination with minimal changes to the underlying LLM architecture and introduces a negligible overhead at inference time, it is appealing to rapidly incorporate parallel reasoning into existing LLM inference pipelines.



1
Introduction

Parallel test-time scaling methods improve LLM performance with additional inference-time compute instead of finetuning
[
4
,
2
,
35
,
29
,
32
]
.
The key idea of these methods is generating
multiple
responses (each potentially with its own reasoning trace) for a given input, and optionally, ranking and aggregating responses with a reward model.
The multiple responses are typically generated in parallel during the same forward pass (i.e., batched inference), and benefit from a hardware efficiency standpoint, since this amortizes memory transfers, minimizes kernel launch overheads, and better utilizes compute cores at each generation step.
Despite its efficiency, parallel inference is largely
uncoordinated
: for a given input, the output sequences are generated independently.
This prohibits exploiting inherent decomposable problem structures
[
36
]
, limits the sharing of intermediate outputs between sequences, and the generation of diverse responses.

Figure 1
:

LaneRoPE: High-level introduction and intuition.

(a) We investigate the problem of
collaborative reasoning
, where given a single input prompt, parallel sequences can reason by conditionally attending to other sequences mid-generation;
(b) Our work assumes the tokens across sequences are generated in parallel using the same model, and thereby benefit from
batched efficiency
;
(c) A key contribution of our work is introducing
cross-lane attention
, such that attentions can be calculated across sequences (i.e.,
lanes
). To enable this, we introduce a novel position encoding scheme to account for relative distances between tokens of different sequences.

Building on powerful parallel inference, how can we make multiple sequences coordinate with each other during generation?
Two families of approaches have gained traction, each with their inherent trade-offs.
The first family of approaches leverage ‘explicit branching’
[
9
,
22
,
36
,
38
]
mechanisms: at inference time, a single sequence adaptively fans-out to multiple search paths (e.g., one path per decomposed subproblem) and funnels them into a single response.
Although they encourage the LLM to explicitly decompose tasks, the dynamic nature of how variable number of parallel processes are spawned and merged introduces challenges in predicting runtime compute resources and more importantly, they depend on specialized run-time engines.
The second family of ‘collaborative’ approaches
[
12
,
27
,
7
]
, under which our approach falls, introduce a more flexible means of coordination primarily by enabling shared visibility (Fig.
1
a) into intermediate generations of all parallel sequences.
These techniques are practically attractive, since they rely on a fixed number of generation sequences (i.e., constant batch size; see Fig.
1
b) and can hence largely leverage batching support by both LLM inference engines and the underlying hardware.
In particular, existing techniques
[
12
,
27
]
exploit an interesting insight that multiple sequences can be ‘virtually ordered’ into a single sequence by reparameterizing the positions or positional encodings.
However, we argue that this is inherently a multi-sequence generation problem and inference must accurately reflect positions both along the token dimension (as typically done)
and
sequence (or
lane
) dimension.
In the rest of this manuscript, we use
lane
and
sequence
interchangeably to refer to batch elements generated simultaneously and inter-dependently.

In this work, we propose LaneRoPE to better enable parallel reasoning and generation.
Similar to parallel test-time scaling techniques
[
32
,
35
]
and collaborative reasoning
[
12
,
27
,
7
]
, we assume a constant number of sequences (Fig.
1
b) generated at inference.
Our approach involves two key insights.
First, we make intermediate reasoning traces and outputs visible across sequences by conditioning the next token on previous tokens from
all sequences
.
We achieve this by using causal cross-sequence attention masks and generalizing attention score calculations to extend to inter-sequence tokens.
Second, we extend rotary positional encoding ‘RoPE’
[
30
]
to additionally capture relative distances among sequences (Fig.
1
b), allowing us to learn the attention structure across parallel generations in a dynamic and task-dependent manner.
Our formulation is expressive enough to capture a range of parallel inference techniques, ranging from independent sampling (e.g., self-consistency) to the fully dense attention masks used in GroupThink
[
12
]
.

Our evaluation of LaneRoPE on standard mathematical reasoning datasets (e.g., AIME, MATH500) and two open-weight LLMs shows promising results.
Overall, we find that the LLMs demonstrate a reliable accuracy improvement across multiple benchmarks.
The flexibility in our formulation enables LaneRoPE to be integrated into existing models with minimal modification and, importantly, in contrast to prior work
[
12
,
27
]
, admits finetuning.
Furthermore, finetuning requires very few additional parameters (
<0.5\%
over base models), which can be fit with simple SFT and synthetic data.
In summary, we find LaneRoPE takes a promising step towards enabling parallel
collaborative
reasoning by fundamentally exploiting relative positions of tokens, both within and outside a particular sequence.

Contributions

We summarize our contributions as:

•

A novel method to enable
fine-grained token-level collaboration
during batched parallel generation of language models. Notably, our method encompasses some existing techniques but its superior flexibility and generality allow for further fine-tuning.

•

A recipe to synthesize
collaborative reasoning traces
which can be used for supervised fine-tuning (SFT).

•

A fine-tuning pipeline to unlock and strengthen the parallel reasoning capabilities of pre-trained models.

2
Related Work

LLMs, Reasoning and Test-time Search.

Large language models (LLMs) have attracted significant attention, largely due to their remarkable performance across a range of tasks.
Many of these tasks (e.g., math, coding) test the LLM’s ability for generalization, abstraction, and multi-step problem solving.
Especially for such tasks, reasoning
[
33
,
14
]
has proven to be a powerful paradigm and has significantly improved success rates at solving challenging problems.
These techniques can be further extended to explore various reasoning paths before concluding with a final solution.
The reasoning search paths can take various structures, but can broadly be classified into sequential
[
21
]
, graph-like
[
37
,
1
]
, or parallel
[
32
,
2
]
.
In this work, we primarily study reasoning as a parallel search problem.

Parallel Reasoning: Independent and Inter-dependent.

A popular strategy to scale compute at test-time to improve accuracies is with parallel generations
[
32
,
4
]
.
For a given problem, multiple sequences (potentially chain-of-thought reasoning followed by a solution) from the same LLM are sampled in parallel and ranked (e.g., using a reward model) to determine the final solution.
Prior works
[
32
,
4
,
2
,
29
,
35
]
primarily demonstrate effectiveness where the sequences are sampled
independently
.
This however leads to inefficient use of compute, since there is little collaboration between sequences and risks redundant compute between sequences.
A new recent line of work explores whether the sequences can be sampled
inter-dependently

[
7
]
, such that LLMs can decide to adaptively parallelize and coordinate
during
generation.
This line of inter-dependent generation broadly falls under two categories:
(a) ‘adaptive branching’
[
13
,
22
,
25
,
36
,
3
,
38
,
34
,
15
]
where at generation time, special tokens (e.g.,
fork
and
merge
) signal the inference engine to spawn or merge multiple parallel sequence generations; and
(b) ‘fine-grained collaboration’, as in
[
12
,
27
]
and recently
[
7
]
, where sequence generation is always parallel (constant batch size) and each sequence can attend to part or all of the tokens generated by other sequences using dynamic attention masks.
Our approach falls into the second category, and unlike previous works we introduce a flexible framework to positionally encode cross-sequence tokens which additionally account for relative inter-sequence distances.

Positional Encoding in LLMs

Large language models (LLMs) incorporate
positional encodings
to inject sequence order information into transformer architectures, since the self-attention mechanism alone is permutation-invariant
[
31
]
.
The original Transformer used fixed sinusoidal position encodings
[
31
]
, while subsequent models like BERT adopted learned positional embeddings
[
6
]
, both providing each token with an absolute position bias.
To improve generalization beyond fixed-length contexts,
relative positional encodings
were introduced to encode pairwise token distances directly in self-attention
[
28
]
, allowing the model to focus on relative order.
More recent approaches modify how positional information enters the attention mechanism to enable better extrapolation to longer sequences. For example,
rotary positional encodings
(RoPE) rotate query/key vectors by angle proportional to token index, effectively combining absolute and relative positional information
[
30
]
. Similarly,
ALiBi
(Attention with Linear Biases) eliminates explicit embeddings and instead adds a distance-dependent bias to attention scores, allowing models trained on shorter sequences to generalize to much longer inputs
[
24
]
.
In our work, we extend RoPE
[
30
]
to additionally model relative distances between sequences.

3
LaneRoPE: Cross-lane positional encoding

In this section, we present our approach LaneRoPE to enable collaborative parallel inference.
We begin by covering preliminaries, and then we detail our approach.

Figure 2
:

Comparison of RoPE
[
30
]
and LaneRoPE for parallel inference.
(a) Parallel inference (e.g., best-of-N) relies on generating sequences independently. As a result, positional encodings and attention scores are defined only per-sequence.
(b) With LaneRoPE, tokens are generated by causally attending to tokens from all sequences. We achieve this by introducing cross-sequence attention scores with a novel position encoding scheme to account for inter-sequence relative distances.

3.1
Preliminaries

A standard transformer self-attention layer
[
31
]
takes token embeddings
\{{\bm{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
, computes query, key, value representations (Eq.
1
) and produces corresponding attention logits and outputs (Eq.
2
):

\displaystyle{\bm{q}}_{i}=f_{q}({\bm{x}}_{i},i),\quad{\bm{k}}_{j}=f_{k}({\bm{x}}_{j},j),\quad{\bm{v}}_{j}=f_{v}({\bm{x}}_{j},j)

(1)

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}_{i,j}=\text{softmax}_{j}\!\left({\bm{q}}^{\top}_{i}{\bm{k}}_{j}/\sqrt{d}\right),\quad{\bm{o}}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\alpha^{\prime}_{i,j}{\bm{v}}_{j}

(2)

Positional Encodings and RoPE

Positional encodings injected into
f_{\{q,k\}}
make outputs position-dependent.
RoPE
[
30
]
enforces that attention logits depend only on the relative offset
i-j
by rotating embeddings with a block-diagonal matrix
{\bm{R}}_{\Theta,i}
(see Appendix
A
):

\displaystyle f_{\{q,k\}}({\bm{x}}_{i},i)

\displaystyle={\bm{R}}_{\Theta,i}\,({\bm{W}}_{\{q,k\}}{\bm{x}}_{i}+{\bm{b}}_{\{q,k\}})

(3)

\displaystyle{\bm{R}}_{\Theta,i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}\!\left(R(i\theta_{1}),\,\ldots,\,R(i\theta_{d/2})\right)

\displaystyle\text{with frequencies params.: }\Theta=(\theta_{l})_{l=1}^{d/2}

(4)

\displaystyle R(i\theta_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(i\theta_{\ell})&\sin(i\theta_{\ell})\\
-\sin(i\theta_{\ell})&\cos(i\theta_{\ell})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}

\displaystyle\ell=1,\ldots,d/2

(5)

Parallel Test-time Scaling

Parallel test-time scaling
[
2
,
29
,
35
]
improves LLM accuracy at inference time by sampling
N>1
sequences and selecting the best via majority vote
[
32
]
or a verifier
[
4
,
16
]
.
Most prior work assumes
independent
sampling across sequences.

3.2
LaneRoPE: Sequence-aware Positional Encoding

Multi-sequence generation

We generate
N>1
sequences
\{{\bm{x}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
auto-regressively in parallel for the same prompt:

\displaystyle p({\bm{x}}^{(n)}_{i+1}

\displaystyle\;|\;\texttt{prompt},\;\{{\bm{x}}^{(m)}_{1:i}\}_{m=1}^{N})

(6)

Here, as opposed to
independent
sampling (conditional generation of
{\bm{x}}^{(n)}_{i+1}
on
{\bm{x}}^{(n)}_{1:i}
), we now have
inter-dependent
generation (conditioned on
\{{\bm{x}}^{(m)}_{1:i}\}_{m=1}^{N}
) via
causal inter-sequence attention
, allowing tokens in sequence
m
to attend to tokens in sequence
n
:

\displaystyle{\alpha^{\prime}}_{i,j}^{(m,n)}

\displaystyle=\text{softmax}_{j,n}\!\left({{\bm{q}}_{i}^{(m)}}^{\top}{\bm{k}}_{j}^{(n)}/\sqrt{d}\right)

(7)

However, since tokens at the same time step across sequences share identical positional indices, standard RoPE cannot distinguish their sequence identities.

Cross-sequence Positional Encoding

To distinguish between sequence identities, we introduce a second block-diagonal rotation
{\bm{R}}_{\Omega,m}
that encodes the sequence index
m
:

\displaystyle{\bm{q}}_{i}^{(m)}

\displaystyle=f_{q}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{(m)},i,m),\quad{\bm{k}}_{j}^{(n)}=f_{k}({\bm{x}}_{j}^{(n)},j,n)

(8)

\displaystyle f_{\{q,k\}}({\bm{x}}_{i},i,m)

\displaystyle={\bm{R}}_{\Omega,m}{\bm{R}}_{\Theta,i}({\bm{W}}_{\{q,k\}}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(m)}+{\bm{b}}_{\{q,k\}})

(9)

with lane frequencies
\Omega=\{\omega_{t}\}_{t=1}^{d/2}
.
The associativity
R(\omega_{t}m)R(\theta_{t}i)=R(\omega_{t}m+\theta_{t}i)
lets both rotations be merged and applied together, enabling drop-in integration into any RoPE architecture.
Analogously, while RoPE uses a 1D Fourier basis over the token sequence, LaneRoPE uses a 2D Fourier basis over the joint (token, sequence) grid.
An important effect of
{\bm{R}}(\Omega_{t},m)
being orthogonal is that the (pre-softmax) attention weight between two tokens
i,j
within the same sequence
m
is unchanged since both key and query vectors are rotated by
{\bm{R}}(\Omega_{t},m)
and, therefore, the inner product
{{\bm{q}}_{i}^{(m)}}^{\top}{\bm{k}}_{j}^{(m)}
remains the same.
In particular, we emphasize that for
N=1
lane, LaneRoPE simplifies to standard RoPE.

3.3
LaneRoPE: Properties and Initialization Strategies

Thanks to its flexibility, LaneRoPE can express some existing parallelization strategies, but can also go beyond if trained.
This suggests using one (or a combination) of these strategies to
initialize LaneRoPE from pre-trained models
, in order to leverage their native reasoning and cooperation capabilities.

Special Case: GroupThink

GroupThink
[
12
]
previously proposed causal inter-sequence attention by ‘virtually’ ordering generation of multiple sequences, albeit within a single sequence.
We find this to be a special case of our proposed approach and elaborate further.
GroupThink distinguishes sequences by assigning token
{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(m)}
the virtual position
Km+i
(sequence gap
K
), so that within-sequence tokens appear close in RoPE-space while cross-sequence tokens appear further away.
This is exactly recovered in LaneRoPE via the rotation-matrix decomposition

\displaystyle{\bm{R}}_{\Theta,Km+i}={\bm{R}}_{\Theta,Km}{\bm{R}}_{\Theta,i}={\bm{R}}_{K\Theta,m}{\bm{R}}_{\Theta,i}={\bm{R}}_{\Omega,m}{\bm{R}}_{\Theta,i}

(10)

with
\Omega:=\{K\theta_{t}\}
, i.e. GroupThink is LaneRoPE with lane frequencies
\Omega=K\Theta
.
A
limitation
is that tokens from higher-index sequences (
n>m
) appear at
negative
virtual relative positions when attended by sequence
m
, which is out-of-distribution for causally pre-trained models.
1
1

1

Hogwild!
[
27
]
avoids this by dynamically reordering sequences, but requires custom attention kernels.

Special Case: Parallel Independent Sampling

Independent sampling across
N
sequences is equivalent to adding a sequence-identity bias
\beta(m-n)\propto\delta_{[m=n]}
to the attention logits, so that cross-lane scores are suppressed:

\displaystyle a_{i,j}^{(m,n)}

\displaystyle={{\bm{q}}_{i}^{(m)}}^{\!\top}{\bm{k}}_{j}^{(n)}+\beta(m-n)

(11)

We approximate
\beta
via a
F
-dimensional discrete Fourier basis and incorporate it into LaneRoPE by augmenting the projection layers with
F
additional bias dimensions (see Appendix
B
):

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{\{q,k\}}^{\prime}:=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{W}}_{\{q,k\}}\\
{\bm{O}}\end{pmatrix}\ ,\quad{\bm{b}}_{\{q,k\}}^{\prime}:=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{b}}_{\{q,k\}}\\
\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}\end{pmatrix}

(12)

with
{\bm{O}}\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times d_{\text{emb}}}
zero-initialized.
This leaves all pre-trained weights intact and adds negligible overhead
(
{\approx}0.25
B parameters for
F{=}2
in
Qwen2.5-7B
).
The bias strength is controlled by
|\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}|_{2}
; Fig.
3
shows its effect on the resulting attention pattern.

Initialization Strategy: NTK-aware correction for GroupThink

To mitigate the
negative virtual indexes limitation
of GroupThink, we draw inspiration from the "NTK-aware" interpolation methods like YaRN
[
23
]
which modify RoPE’s frequencies to extend the context length of pre-trained models.
The main idea is that, despite the translation equivariance of RoPE, frequencies with a relatively long period compared to the pre-trained context length enable the model to encode absolute positions, rather than relative ones.
We suspect these low frequencies suffer the most from the negative relative indexes of GroupThink, which fall outside the portion of the period typically observed during pre-training.
Hence, we introduce a scaling function
\gamma(\theta_{t})\in[0,1]
which smoothly decays to
0
the frequencies below a certain threshold, i.e.
\Omega:=\{\gamma(\theta_{t})K\theta_{t}\}_{t}
, such that the lane index has limited effects on them and they maintain their capacity to represent absolute positions; see Appendix
B
for more details.

(a)

\beta(0)=0
, i.e.
|\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}|_{2}=0

(b)

\beta(0)>0
, i.e.
|\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}|_{2}>0

Effect of the attention bias
\beta
on attention scores in LaneRoPE, controlled by

. A GroupThink initialization is used, where tokens from different lanes occupy distinct virtual position indices. The causal mask within each lane is preserved.

In practice, we combine these strategies: lane frequencies
{\bm{\omega}}^{\prime}=K\Theta
(GroupThink or NTK) for cross-lane structure, and the Fourier bias for initialization to independent sampling.
Since
{\bm{W}}_{\{q,k\}}^{\prime}
and
{\bm{b}}_{\{q,k\}}^{\prime}
are fully learnable, training is free to move beyond these special cases.

4
Training LaneRoPE

To elicit cross-lane interactions in a base model, we consider two fine-tuning strategies:
i)
supervised finetuning (SFT) on a dataset that mimics collaborations, and
ii)
optimizing with a training objective adapted from Kahneman-Tversky Optimization (KTO)
[
8
]
on a dataset of (un)successful reasoning traces independently generated by the base model.

4.1
SFT with collaborative data

We construct this dataset using
Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507

[
26
]
, using questions from the
DeepScaleR-Preview-Dataset

[
17
]
.
To simulate collaboration across
N
lanes using a sequential LLM, we prompt the LLM to play the role of
N
different assistants in a step-by-step manner.
An assistant’s message at each step ends after
128
new tokens.
After each step, an assistant is prompted to generate a new message by completing its own answer, pre-filled with the text it generated so far, which follows a user message providing the query and the text previously generated by other assistants.
For the final SFT dataset, we separate the text generated by each assistant into individual traces such that each trace only contains the messages of one assistant.
As a result, a model fine-tuned on this dataset requires a form of cross-sequence interaction to be able to fit the data.
In total, we generate
3
conversations with
N=2,3
or
4
assistants on a subset of questions of the dataset, resulting in about
50\mathrm{k}
raw conversations.
See Apx.
E
for more details.
To focus on quality over quantity, we further filter the original generations based on the following criteria.
First, we only keep samples where all the assistants give the correct answer.
Second, we employ a length limit in range
10-30
messages per lane and remove all samples outside that range.
Third, we use a simple heuristic to filter out conversations with little or no interactions between the assistants.
After this curation process, the final SFT dataset consists of
4797
conversations.
See Apx.
E.1
for an example of the SFT dataset.

4.2
KTO with independent reasoning traces

We sample
8
completions with the base model (either
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B
or
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B
) and a maximum completion length of
4096
, for each of the
40315
questions of the
DeepScaleR-Preview-Dataset

[
17
]
.
Among these, we keep only those queries where
at most half
of the answers were correct, to ensure a sufficiently challenging dataset.
Importantly, we preserve both good and wrong completions to be used for the KTO loss
[
8
]
, which respectively maximizes and minimizes their likelihoods, up to some per-sample saturation.
Hence, we create a dataset by grouping the completions of each query into subsets of
N=2,3,4
lanes, ensuring each subset contains at least one correct answer, for about
15
k total groups; during training, LaneRoPE is able to cross-attend among the
N
lanes within each subset.
While SFT with independently sampled data would force our model to mimic the i.i.d. distribution, batches with mixed good/wrong completions can
encourage a non-trivial joint distribution among parallel lanes via the KTO loss
: e.g. it’s easier to minimize the likelihood of a wrong completion when its final answer doesn’t match that of the other completions in the batch.
See Appendix
D
.

4.3
Other training details

To train our models, we first initialize them from open-source pre-trained LLMs using a combination of the initialization strategies described earlier.
In particular, we use a "sequence gap"
K=8192
to bias the model towards a GroupThink-like behavior but also set a strong positional bias (
|\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}|_{2}\geq 1000.
) to encourage the model to attend to tokens in other sequences only when necessary.
Then, we train our models for one epoch on these curated datasets.
In our experiments with small
1.5B
, we train all their weights while we use LoRA (including trainable biases) for the
7B
ones to reduce memory cost at training time and fit at least
4
lanes within a single GPU.
When indicated with a
*
, we also train the frequencies
\Omega
of the LaneRoPE itself.

5.3
Inference time measurements

An important benefit of LaneRoPE is that it comes with little-to-no overhead over standard transformer architectures.
Indeed, inference can be done by interleaving the input tokens into a
N
times longer sequence (with sufficient care of tracking indexes) and then using standard methods.
This strategy allows for using memory efficient attention back-ends, such as Flash Attention, which minimize the (theoretical) overhead due to the increased context length: in practice, in Tab.
2
we find negligible increments in inference time with respect to the standard model when cross-attending between multiple lanes within a batch.
In particular, we observe no difference when using larger numbers
N
of lanes: the main delay (
\sim 6\%
) in LaneRoPE is observed with
N=1
lane too, despite being logically equivalent to a standard transformer architecture.
This suggests its cause lies in the pre- and post-processing done in our customized "generate" implementation (e.g. reshaping and interleaving the inputs) rather than the attention mechanism.

We also compare the inference time of (our implementation of) Bridge
[
7
]
as an interdependent-generation baseline.
Bridge introduces additional layers (a layer-norm and a attention block with its linear layers, to implement "axial" attention across the tokens within the same time step) after each transformer block, increasing the overall number of sequential operations performed during inference and the number of weights to load in memory during inference.
In Tab.
2
, we find that this architecture choice leads to up to
25\%
increase in inference time with respect to our LaneRoPE models.

Finally, we note that, while these observations might not hold for a much larger number of lanes
N\gg 4
, a small number
N\leq 8
is typically sufficient and larger batch sizes can still benefit by mixing multi-lanes attention with independent sampling strategies as shown in Fig.
4
.
In conclusion, we believe these results justify the adoption of LaneRoPE as an efficient and lightweight solution to enable inter-sequence collaboration.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we addressed the problem of collaborative parallel inference, where multiple sequences (e.g., within the same batch of a forward pass) can access each other’s partial generation to condition future reasoning.
In particular, we proposed a novel and flexible method - which encompasses some previous approaches - to model token-level inter-sequence interactions efficiently, along with a data-generation recipe to create high-quality parallel and collaborative reasoning traces for fine-tuning.
Our empirical results show improved reasoning capabilities in parallel setups and open up opportunities for better parallel scaling strategies beyond independent sampling.

Limitations and Future Work

We do not yet employ a dedicated training scheme for merging outputs from multiple lanes, and developing such mechanisms remains an open area for refinement.
Our current experiments focus on
N\leq 4
collaborating lanes, though the method itself is designed to extend naturally to a larger number of lanes.
While LaneRoPE increases context length by a factor of
N
, we found this to have negligible impact on performance in the small batch regime - relevant for inference on the edge - in Sec.
5.3
.
Finally, while a small SFT stage with synthetic data proved sufficient to unlock parallel reasoning capabilities, an additional refinement stage with Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Feedback (RLVF), as done in
[
7
]
, could unlock novel collaborative behaviors and strategies in an unsupervised way and further strengthen the overall reasoning performance.

Appendix A
Background: Self-attention and Positional Encodings

Self-attention

The self-attention layer of a standard transformer
[
31
]
takes as inputs a set of tokens
{\mathbb{T}}=\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
.
Each token is represented by its corresponding embedding
{\mathbb{E}}=\{{\bm{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
, where
{\bm{x}}_{i}
is a
d^{\text{emb}}
-dim embedding of the
i
-th token without positional information.
The self-attention layer converts embeddings into query, key, and value representations via a linear transformation over positionally-encoded embeddings (Eq.
1
), with attention logits
a_{i,j}={\bm{q}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{k}}_{j}
and output
{\bm{o}}_{i}=\sum_{j}\alpha^{\prime}_{i,j}{\bm{v}}_{j}
(Eq.
2
).

Positional Encodings and RoPE

Positional encodings are injected at the query/key level so that attention outputs are position-dependent.
A traditional choice
[
31
]
uses
absolute
positional encodings

f_{\{q,k\}}({\bm{x}}_{i},i)={\bm{W}}_{\{q,k\}}({\bm{x}}_{i}+{\bm{p}}_{i})
,
with
{\bm{p}}_{i,2t}=\cos\!\left(\frac{i}{10000^{2t/d}}\right)
and
{\bm{p}}_{i,2t+1}=\sin\!\left(\frac{i}{10000^{2t/d}}\right)
.
However, most token interactions are
translation-invariant
, motivating attention scores that depend on relative offsets.
RoPE
[
30
]
enforces this by splitting dimensions into
d/2
independent 2D planes, rotating each with frequency
\theta_{\ell}
:

\displaystyle f_{q}({\bm{x}}_{i},i)^{T}f_{k}({\bm{x}}_{j},j)

\displaystyle=g({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{j},i-j)

(13)

where the rotation matrices (Eq.
4
) use per-plane rotation blocks:

\displaystyle R(i\theta_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(i\theta_{\ell})&\sin(i\theta_{\ell})\\
-\sin(i\theta_{\ell})&\cos(i\theta_{\ell})\end{bmatrix}\quad\ell=1,\ldots,d/2

(14)

such that the key-query inner product satisfies

\displaystyle{\bm{q}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{k}}_{j}=({\bm{W}}_{q}{\bm{x}}_{j}+{\bm{b}}_{q}){\bm{R}}_{\Theta,i-j}({\bm{W}}_{k}{\bm{x}}_{i}+{\bm{b}}_{k})\ .

(15)

Multi-sequence generation notation

We generate
N>1
sequences
\{{\bm{x}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
in parallel, where the
n
-th sequence is an ordered list of tokens
{\bm{x}}^{(n)}=[{\bm{x}}_{1}^{(n)},{\bm{x}}_{2}^{(n)},\ldots]
.
The standard assumption generates each sequence independently from the prompt and its own past tokens.
LaneRoPE instead conditions each token on all sequences’ past tokens (Eq.
6
), implemented via a modified causal attention mask that exposes the full KV cache of all lanes to each query token.

Appendix B
Initialization Strategies: Additional Details

Independent Sampling via Fourier Attention Bias

We approximate the identity-selector
\beta(x)\propto\delta_{[x=0]}
using a
F
-dimensional discrete Fourier basis over
F/2
frequencies
{\bm{\omega}}=\{\omega_{t}\}_{t=1}^{F/2}
:

\displaystyle\beta(x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{t=1}^{F/2}\hat{\beta}^{c}_{t}\cos(2\pi\omega_{t}x)+\hat{\beta}^{s}_{t}\sin(2\pi\omega_{t}x)

(16)

Since
\beta
is symmetric,
\hat{\beta}^{s}_{t}=0
and all
\hat{\beta}^{c}_{t}\geq 0
.
Defining
\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{F}
with coefficients
\{\sqrt{\hat{\beta}_{t}^{\{c,s\}}}\}_{t}
lets us write
\beta(x)=\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}^{\top}R_{{\bm{\omega}},x}\,\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}
, which is an inner product of rotated vectors - exactly the form produced by LaneRoPE on the augmented dimensions.

We incorporate this into LaneRoPE by augmenting query/key projections as in Eq.
12
, with correspondingly enlarged rotary matrices:

\displaystyle R_{\Theta^{\prime},i}:=\begin{pmatrix}R_{\Theta,i}&\\
&{\bm{I}}\end{pmatrix}\ ,\quad R_{\Omega^{\prime},m}:=\begin{pmatrix}R_{\omega^{\prime},m}&\\
&R_{\omega,m}\end{pmatrix}

(17)

where
R_{\Theta,i}
is the pre-trained RoPE matrix and
R_{\omega^{\prime},m}
can be any LaneRoPE matrix (we use
{\bm{\omega}}^{\prime}=K\Theta
following GroupThink).
The resulting projection has the same form as Eq.
9
:

\displaystyle f_{\{q,k\}}({\bm{x}}_{i},i,m)={\bm{R}}_{\Omega^{\prime},m}{\bm{R}}_{\Theta^{\prime},i}({\bm{W}}^{\prime}_{\{q,k\}}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{(m)}+{\bm{b}}^{\prime}_{\{q,k\}})

(18)

Note that LaneRoPE is also sufficiently expressive to represent
\beta
without the additional dimensions, but the augmented form avoids modifying any pre-trained weights.
For
N
parallel sequences,
N+1
additional dimensions always suffice for an exact Fourier representation over the sequence indices.

When combining this strategy with GroupThink, we simply set the lane frequencies
{\bm{\omega}}^{\prime}=K\Theta
(or according to the NTK-aware correction
{\bm{\omega}}^{\prime}=\gamma\cdot K\Theta
, as below).

NTK-aware correction for GroupThink

Portion of period
observed at
pre-training
Negative
relative position

As observed by the authors of YaRN
[
23
]
, some RoPE dimensions have a frequency
\theta_{t}
with a wavelength
\lambda_{t}=\frac{2\pi}{\theta_{t}}
larger than (or close to) the pre-training context size
L
, and, therefore there are rotation angles in these dimensions which were never or rarely observed during training by the model, as represented on the right.
Conversely, higher frequencies have shorter wavelength and the full range of rotations was observed often during training.
We argue these higher frequencies are more robust to GroupThink since they properly model relative positions and since negative relative positions still represent rotations which were sufficiently observed during training.
Conversely, the low frequencies model the distance from the beginning of the sequence and are more sensitive to GroupThink indexing.
YaRN defines a ramp function
\gamma\in[0,1]
to weight and interpolate smoothly between two choices: interpolation by scaling the low frequencies and preserving high frequencies.
Similarly, we use a ramp function
\gamma
to interpolate between zeroing out the low frequencies (s.t. positional embedding depends only on the sequence index, but not on the lane index) and applying GroupThink on the highest ones.
At dimension
t
, define the ratio
r_{t}=\frac{L}{\lambda_{t}}
where
\lambda_{t}
is the wavelength and
L
the pre-traning max context size.
Then, the ramp function is

\displaystyle\gamma(r_{t})=\begin{cases}0\text{ if }r_{t}<\alpha\\
1\text{ if }r_{t}>\beta\\
\frac{r-\alpha}{\beta-\alpha}\text{ otherwise }\\
\end{cases}

and the final LaneRoPE frequency at dimension
t
is

\displaystyle\omega_{t}:=\gamma(r_{t})\cdot K\theta_{t}

where
K
is the sequence gap of GroupThink.
We use
\alpha=4
and
\beta=32
.

Appendix D
Additional Experiments Details

SFT Training

We train all models for one epochs on our collaborative datasets with a total batch size of
60
, (each containing groups of
N=2,3,4
collaborative reasoning traces).
We use
F=2
and
|\beta|_{2}^{2}=1000
to initialize the model to independent sampling.
We initialize the other LaneRoPE frequencies either like GroupThink as
\Omega=K\Theta
or like the NTK-corrected variant, by using a
sequence gap

K=8192
.
We train all our
7
B models using a LoRA adapter (with rank
32
and including the bias of the key and query linear projection layers) to reduce memory cost at training time and fit at least
4
lanes within a single GPU, with a learning rate of 1e-4 and a batch size of
60
.
Due to the large initialization norm
|\beta|_{2}^{2}=1000
in the biases of the attention linear layers, we typically use a larger learning rate of 1e-2 only for these parameters.
Similarly, we adopt a stronger learning rate of 1e-2 for the LaneRoPE frequency parameters
\Omega
, when tuning these parameters.
We also adopt a cosine learning rate scheduler with a warmup ratio of
10\%
and weight decay 5e-2 (excluding the LaneRoPE frequency parameters
\Omega
).

KTO training

The KTO loss has form

\displaystyle v(x,y)=\begin{cases}\lambda_{D}\sigma(\beta(\log\pi-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}-z_{0}))\text{ for positive example}\ y\\
\lambda_{U}\sigma(-\beta(\log\pi-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}-z_{0}))\text{ for negative example}\ y\end{cases}

(19)

where
\pi
is the policy model, i.e. the model we want to train and
\pi_{\text{ref}}
is a reference model.

\lambda_{U}
and
\lambda_{D}
are hyper-parameters controlling the weights of undesirable and desirable examples, for non-balanced datasets (we use
\lambda_{D}=1
,
\lambda_{U}=0.7
).

\beta
is a hyperparameter (we use
\beta=0.1
) that controls the strength of the regularization with the reference model.
With respect to
[
8
]
, we do not use the KL term
z_{0}
since it is more expensive to compute and the models are initialized to independent sampling and, therefore, already similar to the model that generated the data.
In our experiments, whenever we adapt a base model with LaneRoPE, we always use the base model as a
reference
model
\pi_{\text{ref}}
in the KTO loss.
Moreover, we replace the sigmoid
\sigma
with an asymmetric function
\sigma^{\prime}
defined as

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}\sigma(x)&\quad\text{ if }x\geq 0\\
-x+\frac{1}{2}&\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(20)

which ensures saturation only on one side. This is because, despite the independent sampling initialization as in Sec.
B
, LaneRoPE can give slightly lower likelihood estimations than the original model
\pi_{\text{ref}}
(especially the small
1.5
B model).
A symmetric saturating loss there would be undesirable since it would prevent any gradient at the beginning of the training until
\log\pi
matches precisely
\log\pi_{\text{ref}}
.

\boxed{}
.

\boxed{}

Appendix E
Synthetize Collaborative Reasoning Traces

Below are the system prompt used to instruct the model on how the conversation will work and the user prompt used to communicate to the assistant the query and the text generated by the other assistants so far.

At each step, we prompt the model from scratch, pre-filling its own message with the text it generated in previous iterations and adding the text generated by the other assistants in the user prompt.
An assistant’s message at each step ends after
128
new tokens and the assistants generate for up to
30
steps.
Note also that, since the user message is constantly updated, there is no reuse of the Key-Value cache in consecutive messages.
In total, we generate
3
conversations with
N=2,3
or
4
assistants for a subset of questions of
DeepScaleR-Preview-Dataset

[
17
]
., resulting in about
50\mathrm{k}
raw conversations (pre-filtering).
In the final data filtration phase (after filtering out any conversation with wrong answers), we score each conversation by counting the number of occurrences of other assistants’ names in an assistant’s completion, together with few other key phrases that suggest some disagreement happened (e.g. "Alice you are wrong"), and keep those with a count above
2N
