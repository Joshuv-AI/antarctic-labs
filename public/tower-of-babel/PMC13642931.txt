Reprogramming hydrogen metabolism for methane mitigation in dairy cows: mechanistic insights from polyphenols using meta-omics approaches
Authors: Yuchao Zhao, Ying Wang, Sarula Bai, Jian Tan, Haoyu Niu, Ao Zhang, Gang Guo, Luoyun Fang, Linshu Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnteric methane emissions from ruminants contribute significantly to agricultural greenhouse gases. Plant-derived phytochemicals such as grape seed proanthocyanidins (GSP) are promising natural antimethanogenic feed additives, yet their modes of action remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to comprehensively elucidate the microbiological and functional mechanisms underlying phytochemical-induced methane mitigation using integrative meta-omics. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrated that GSP supplementation significantly reduced methane emissions; in lactating dairy cows, GSP decreased methane emission intensity by 16.5% (g/kg energy-corrected milk). Metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analyses revealed a reprogramming of microbial communities, with decreased abundance and transcriptional activity of methanogenic archaea (e.g., Methanobrevibacter) and enhanced activity of alternative hydrogenotrophic bacteria (Selenomonas, Veillonella, Sharpea). Functionally, GSP elevated expression of genes involved in reductive acetogenesis (e.g., acsB), nitrate ammonification (narG, nrfA), and sulfate reduction (dsrA), thereby redirecting hydrogen flux away from methanogenesis. These shifts were accompanied by increased microbial carbohydrate metabolism and antioxidative responses. Our findings provide the first meta-omics-based mechanistic framework for understanding methanogenesis suppression by phytochemicals in ruminants. GSP modulates microbial composition and function to reroute reductant flows and suppress archaeal methanogenesis through enhanced bacterial electron sinks. This work highlights the potential of polyphenols to modulate the rumen microbiome for sustainable methane mitigation, supporting the development of next-generation feed additives.Subject terms: Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

Methane (CH4) is a major anthropogenic greenhouse gas (GHG), significantly contributing to climate change1. Enteric CH4 emissions from livestock represent the largest source of agricultural GHG, accounting for approximately 17% of global CH4 emissions and 3.3% of total anthropogenic GHG emissions2. Research on reducing enteric CH4 production to enhance livestock efficiency has been ongoing since early studies revealed that ruminants lose up to 12% of their gross energy intake through CH4 emissions3. To address this issue, global initiatives have set a target to cut CH4 emissions by 30% by 2030 relative to 2020 levels4. Therefore, identifying strategies that mitigate CH4 emissions while maintaining production efficiency is crucial.

Methanogenesis in the rumen is carried out predominantly by archaea through three main pathways, including hydrogenotrophic, methylotrophic, and acetoclastic, among which the hydrogenotrophic pathway is the most dominant5. Methanogenesis is tightly linked to ruminal fermentation dynamics, especially the interspecies transfer of hydrogen among microbes. Hydrogen production is facilitated by hydrogenases, particularly fermentative and electron-bifurcating [FeFe]-hydrogenases, which reoxidize reduced cofactors generated during carbohydrate fermentation and subsequently convert the released electrons into H26. The use of antimethanogenic feed additives, such as 3-nitrooxypropanol7,8, can elevate rumen H2 partial pressures, leading to cascading effects on rumen metabolism since most rumen microorganisms encode hydrogenases6. Terminal reductases, which are involved in the reduction of substrates like nitrate, sulfate, and fumarate, can act as alternative electron sinks, redirecting hydrogen flow away from methanogenesis and potentially reducing CH4 emissions9. Consequently, strategies that target ruminal hydrogen metabolism by either suppressing methanogenic archaea or promoting alternative hydrogen-utilizing pathways hold great promise for reducing CH4 emissions10–12.

Condensed tannins, or proanthocyanidins, are plant-derived polyphenolic compounds widely present in forages, legumes, and agro-industrial by-products13. These compounds can reduce enteric CH₄ emissions by modulating rumen microbial composition and inhibiting methanogens and protozoa14–16. Meta-analyses and in vivo studies have reported that condensed tannins significantly reduce CH₄ emissions while influencing volatile fatty acid (VFA) profiles and nitrogen metabolism17–19. Among these compounds, proanthocyanidins extracted from grape seed, a readily available by-product of the wine and juice industries, represent a promising and sustainable methane mitigation strategy20. Grape seed proanthocyanidins (GSP) possess high antioxidant capacity and are known to exert antimicrobial and enzyme-modulatory effects, making them suitable candidates for modulating rumen fermentation and CH4 emission16,21,22. However, despite growing evidence for their methane-lowering effects, the precise mechanisms by which GSP affect hydrogen metabolism at the genomic and transcriptomic levels remain poorly understood. In particular, it is unclear how GSP influence the expression and activity of hydrogenases, terminal reductases, and microbial pathways that compete with methanogenesis for reducing equivalents.

In this study, we employed an integrative multi-omics framework to investigate the impact of GSP on rumen fermentation, hydrogen metabolism, and methane emissions in dairy cows. Using a combination of in vitro and in vivo trials, we explored how GSP reshapes the microbial ecosystem, modulates hydrogenase expression, and redirects metabolic hydrogen toward non-methanogenic pathways. Our findings provide mechanistic insights into the capacity of dietary polyphenols to functionally reprogram ruminal hydrogen flow and suppress methane production, offering a promising avenue for sustainable livestock feeding strategies.

Methods

Animal ethics

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the Chinese regulations on animal welfare and received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Beijing University of Agriculture (Approval No. BUA2023023).

In vitro model of short-term rumen fermentation

Three cannulated Holstein cows in mid-lactation [116 ± 8.0 days in milk (DIM) on average, body weight (BW) 682 ± 25 kg] were used as ruminal inoculum donors. These donor animals were maintained on a total mixed ration (TMR) as detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Rumen inocula were manually collected in equal proportions from each cow and strained through 4 layers of cheesecloth within one hour, transferred into prewarmed thermoses, transported to the laboratory, and immediately mixed together. All sampling and handling procedures were performed under anaerobic conditions. A representative sample of the TMR fed to donor cows was freeze-dried and ground to pass a 1-mm screen using a Wiley mill (Standard Model 4, Arthur M Thomas, Philadelphia, PA) for later use as in vitro fermentation substrate.

The in vitro study was designed as a randomized complete block design with 3 independent runs (Fig. 1). Run was used as a blocking factor. Two treatments were used in this study, (1) control group (CON, without additive) and (2) GSP-supplemented group (5 g/kg). The GSP, with a purity of 95.02%, was sourced from Xi’an Xuquan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xi’an, China). The GSP dose of 5 g/kg was selected based on a preliminary dose-response experiment, in which GSP was tested at five concentrations (0, 2.5, 5.0, 7.5, and 10 g/kg) using the same in vitro batch culture system. The 5 g/kg dose was chosen because it exhibited the most favorable balance between methane reduction and maintenance of normal rumen fermentation parameters (i.e., no significant negative effects on total gas production or volatile fatty acid profiles), whereas higher doses (7.5 and 10 g/kg) tended to suppress fermentation without additional methane mitigation benefits (data not shown). The buffered rumen inoculum was obtained by mixing rumen fluid with artificial saliva at a ratio of 1 to 2 (vol/vol). The incubation medium was prepared under CO2 flux and kept in a water bath at 39 °C according to Menke and Steingass23. Each liter of artificial saliva contains 0.013 g of CaCl2⋅2H2O, 0.001 g of CoCl2⋅6H2O, 0.010 g of MnCl2⋅4 H2O, 0.008 g of FeCl3⋅6 H2O, 0.832 g of NH4HCO3, 7.280 g of NaHCO3, 1.966 g of Na2HPO4⋅12 H2O, 0.125 g of MgSO4⋅7H2O, 1.290 g of KH2PO4, 0.390 g of L-cysteine hydrochloride, 0.390 g of Na2S⋅9 H2O, 0.1 mg of resazurin)23. Approximately 0.5 g of TMR substrate was weighed into each 100-mL serum bottle, and 70 mL of buffered rumen inoculum was added under a steam of CO2. The incubation process was carried out using the Automated Trace Gas Recording System (AGRSIII, Beijing, China) for 24 h at 39 °C, following the method outlined by Zhang and Yang24. In brief, serum bottles were connected to the gas inlets of the AGRSIII system. Cumulative gas production (3.0 mL) for each vent was automatically recorded using a differential pressure switch (pressure range: 20–300 Pa, Huba Control Inc., USA) once the internal pressure reached 100 Pa. Gas production was continuously monitored and recorded over the incubation period. Meanwhile, serum bottles were connected to pre-emptied gasbags for later analysis of gas composition.

Experimental design and workflow for in vitro and in vivo studies.

Experiment 1 (in vitro): Rumen fluid was collected from three donor cows and pooled for each of the three independent incubation runs (Run 1, Run 2, Run 3). Treatments included a control (CON, no addition) and grape seed proanthocyanidins (GSP) at 5 g/kg. Parameters measured included nutrient degradation, fermentation parameters, methane production, and microbial community analysis (16S rRNA gene sequencing). Experiment 2 (in vivo): Thirty-two mid-lactation Holstein cows were assigned to either a basal diet (CON) or a diet supplemented with 150 g/d of GSP. Data collected included dry matter intake, milk yield and composition, feed efficiency, rumen fermentation parameters, methane emission, and microbial community and function analysis (16S rRNA sequencing, metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, untargeted metabolomics, GSP metabolites, and carbon metabolome).

Upon completion of the incubation period, bottles were promptly placed in ice water to halt fermentation. The pH of the fermentation broth was measured immediately using a pH meter (model FP20, Mettler Toledo, Columbus, OH). Subsequently, the buffered rumen fluid was centrifuged at 22,000 × g for 20 min to separate the solid and liquid fractions. The supernatant was collected and preserved for subsequent analysis of ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) and VFA. Meanwhile, the microbial pellet obtained from the centrifugation was collected and stored at −80 °C for subsequent microbial composition analysis. Each treatment had 6 serum bottles per run, and each experimental run had 6 blank serum bottles (buffered rumen fluid only) with no substrate to correct for background gas production.

In vivo experiment design, animals, and sampling

A total of 32 multiparous high-producing Holstein cows in mid-lactation were enrolled in a randomized complete block design trial (Fig. 1). The animals had an average (mean ± SD) of 92 ± 12 days in milk (DIM), a body condition score (BCS) of 3.0 ± 0.5, parity of 3.3 ± 1.2, and a BW of 677 ± 27 kg. Cows were blocked according to parity, DIM, and milk yield, assigned to 1 of the 2 treatments: (1) basal diet without GSP (CON) or (2) basal diet with the addition of 150 g/head/d per day of GSP (GSP; purity = 95.02%; Xi’an Xuquan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Xi’an, China). A more detailed analysis of GSP composition is shown in Supplementary Table S2. The dosage of GSP in the in vivo trial (150 g/d per cow) was determined based on our preliminary in vitro dose-response study, which identified 5 g/kg DM (0.5% inclusion level) as the optimal concentration for mitigating CH4 emissions without adversely affecting fermentation characteristics. This daily dose was calculated by assuming an average dry matter intake (DMI) range of 25–30 kg/d for high-producing cows in mid-lactation under the experimental conditions.

The study consisted of a 7-day covariate period, followed by 14 days of dietary adaptation and a 42-day sampling phase, totaling 9 weeks of experimental comparison. Cows were provided a TMR formulated to meet or slightly exceed their nutritional needs as outlined by NASEM25 (see Supplementary Table S1), and were fed twice daily at 0800 and 1730 h with ad libitum access. All animals were housed in a ventilated enclosed barn equipped with individual feeding gates and mattress-free stalls. Daily dry matter intake was determined individually by weighing feed offered and refusals. Feed amounts were adjusted daily to maintain 5–10% orts. Water was freely available throughout the experiment.

Grape seed proanthocyanidin supplementation was split evenly between the morning and evening feedings. Each portion was mixed into 3 kg of TMR and offered for a 2-hour consumption period. The amount consumed was recorded, and any uneaten portion was top-dressed onto the remainder of the daily TMR. Control cows received the same feeding protocol without GSP inclusion. No feed refusals or sorting behaviors specifically related to GSP were observed during the adaptation or experimental periods, and the GSP-laden TMR was typically consumed within the allotted 2-hour window, indicating that the supplement at the tested dosage did not adversely affect the palatability of the diet. Cows were milked at 0630 h, 1300 h, and 2200 h daily. Milk yield was recorded at each milking. Composite milk samples from morning and evening milkings were collected during the final three days of the covariate period and on days 28, 35, 42, 49, 56, and 63 of the trial. Samples were preserved using bronopol and natamycin and analyzed for fat, protein, lactose, total solids, milk urea nitrogen (MUN), and somatic cell count (SCC) using a Milkoscan FT 6000 analyzer (Foss Electric, Hillerød, Denmark).

Approximately 50 mL of rumen fluid was collected from each cow via oral stomach tubing 3 to 4 h post-feeding on the final 3 days of the covariate period and on days 28, 35, 42, 49, 56, and 63 of the trial. To reduce contamination from saliva, the initial 200 mL of fluid was discarded before sample collection. Rumen pH was measured immediately after sampling using a pH meter (model FP20, Mettler Toledo, Columbus, OH). Three 10 mL subsamples were aliquoted into separate containers and stored at −20 °C for subsequent analyses of NH3-N, VFA, and dissolved hydrogen (dH2), respectively. Additionally, three separate 5-mL aliquots of rumen fluid were collected from each cow for independent omics analyses. Individual aliquots were dedicated to DNA extraction for microbial community profiling, RNA extraction for metatranscriptomic analysis, and metabolite extraction for non-targeted and targeted metabolomics, respectively. To ensure the stability of both the microbial transcriptional profiles and the metabolic fingerprints, all three aliquots were immediately transferred into sterile, RNAse-free cryovials and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen within 1 min of collection. These samples were maintained in liquid nitrogen during transport and subsequently stored in an ultra-low temperature freezer (−80 °C) until independent processing for each omics platform.

In vitro and in vivo methane measurements

Fermentation gases of in vitro batch cultures were collected in gas-tight bags and analyzed using a GC-7890B gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) fitted with a thermal conductivity detector and a GS-CarbonPLOT capillary column (30 m × 0.32 mm × 3.00 μm, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) to quantify CH4 and CO2 concentrations. Methane output was expressed as milliliters per gram of degraded dry matter (mL/g DDM).

In vivo CH4 measurements were conducted twice daily—once before feeding (06:00–08:00) and once post-feeding (09:00–11:00)—on the final 2 days of the covariate period and during weeks 4 through 9 of the trial. Methane emissions were quantified using a handheld laser methane detector (LMD; model YC100, Aikesi Electronics Technology Co., Ltd., Changzhou, China), which detects CH4 concentrations via a red laser beam directed into the animal’s nostril, following the method of Kang et al.26 with slight adjustments. Specifically, the original measurement duration of 6 min was extended to 8 min to capture more eructation events. Additionally, methane concentrations were originally reported in ppm-m, but we converted these values into daily methane emissions (g/day) using the equation provided by Chagunda et al.27, which better reflects the total methane production.

For each measurement session, methane concentration in exhaled breath was continuously recorded at 0.5-s intervals over an 8-min period, expressed in parts per million-meter (ppm-m). Two trained technicians operated the LMD, carefully adjusting the device to maintain the laser focus on a consistent location near the nostril while accommodating the cow’s head movement. A fixed distance of 1 m between the detector and the animal was maintained throughout data collection. The 8-min recording duration was chosen to reliably capture 3–4 eructation events, in line with recommendations by ref. 26.

Methane concentration data were analyzed using the approach described by ref. 26. In brief, CH4 peaks recorded by the LMD were extracted using the AMPD package (v. 0.2) in R (v. 4.2.0)26. These peaks were then classified into two distinct types—respiratory and eructation events—by fitting a mixture of two normal distributions using the mixdist package in R. Respiratory peaks occurred more frequently but were of lower amplitude. The mean value of the fitted distribution corresponding to eructation events was taken as the representative CH4 concentration for each measurement session. For each cow, four measurements per day (two in the morning and two in the afternoon) were averaged to calculate the daily mean CH4 concentration. The resulting values, initially expressed in ppm-m, were subsequently converted to daily methane emissions (g/day) based on the equation provided by Chagunda et al.27.

In vitro and in vivo chemical analysis

Volatile fatty acid concentrations were determined using an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a DB-FFAP capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and a flame ionization detector. Ammonia nitrogen levels in the incubation supernatant were analyzed according to the method of Broderick and Kang28. Dissolved hydrogen in in vivo rumen fluid was measured following the protocol described by ref. 29. Details on determination of VFA and dH2 can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Dry matter content of feed and fermentation residues was determined by oven drying at 105 °C (AOAC method 988.05)30, while ash content was measured by incineration at 600 °C (AOAC method 942.05)30. Nitrogen concentration was analyzed using the Kjeldahl method, and crude protein (CP) was calculated as N × 6.25, following AOAC guidelines30. Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) were measured using an Ankom fiber analyzer (ANKOM 200, ANKOM Technology, New York, NY, USA), incorporating the use of thermostable amylase and sodium sulfite as described by ref. 31. The apparent disappearance of DM, NDF, and ADF during in vitro incubation was calculated from the difference between the amount of DM, NDF, and ADF before the incubation and the amount recovered in 24 h samples.

Untargeted rumen metabolome analysis

For metabolomic analysis, rumen fluid samples from the final sampling point of the in vivo trial were analyzed, including 8 samples per group. Metabolome was measured by ultra-performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS). The details on the metabolites extraction and settings of UPLC-MS/MS systems were provided in the Supplementary Material.

Rumen metabolomic raw data were pretreated by the Progenesis QI software (v. 3.0; Milford, MA, USA) for the extraction, alignment, deconvolution, and normalization of peaks. The software was set to automatically search the selected databases, such as METLIN Metabolite Database(https://metlin.scripps.edu/), the Human Metabolome Database (http://www.hmdb.ca/), and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG; https://www.genome.jp/kegg/), to identify the metabolites based on m/z, retention time, and fragment information. Multivariate statistical analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), and corresponding loading plots, were conducted using SIMCA-P software (v. 13.0; Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden). Metabolites were deemed significantly different between groups if they had a variable importance in projection (VIP) score greater than 1 and a Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted P-value below 0.05.

Determination of phenolics and proanthocyanidins in rumen fluid

Rumen fluid samples collected during the in vivo trial were analyzed for phenolic compounds. Total phenolic content was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric method as described by ref. 32. Total flavonoid concentration was measured following the procedure outlined by ref. 33. Proanthocyanidin levels in the rumen fluid were assessed using a commercial assay kit (#AKPL017M; Beijing Boxbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Proanthocyanidin composition in rumen fluid was analyzed using a SCIEX Triple Quad 5500 + LC-MS/MS system (AB SCIEX) equipped with a diode array detector (DAD), following the protocol described by ref. 34. The detailed parameters are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Quantification of individual proanthocyanidin compounds was conducted using calibration curves prepared for reference standards, including catechin, epicatechin, gallocatechin, epigallocatechin, epicatechin gallate, epigallocatechin gallate, procyanidin B1, procyanidin B2, and procyanidin C1 (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

Targeted metabolomics analysis of GSP metabolites in rumen fluid

Rumen fluid samples collected at the final in vivo time point were pre-processed using off-line solid phase microextraction (SPME), following the procedure described by ref. 35. Extraction was carried out with OASIS HLB μ-Elution Plates (30 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The resulting eluates were analyzed for proanthocyanidin-derived metabolites using LC-MS/MS, as outlined by ref. 36. Detailed information about extraction and is listed in Supplementary Material.

To quantify metabolites in rumen fluid, calibration curves were created by spiking blank rumen fluid from the control group with eight standard concentrations (20, 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000, 2500, and 5000 ppb). Metabolite concentrations in the GSP group were calculated by subtracting the control group values. Quantification was based on analyte/IS peak intensity ratios inserted into calibration curves, with method parameters detailed in Supplementary Table S3. LOD and LOQ were defined at signal-to-noise ratios of 3 and 10, respectively. Accuracy, evaluated at 100, 2500, and 5000 ppb, was within 15–20% of guideline limits. Data were processed using MassHunter software (v.12.1; Agilent Technologies, CA, USA).

Targeted rumen carbon metabolome

A comprehensive description of the central carbon metabolite profiling is available in the Supplementary Material. In brief, analyses were carried out using a UHPLC system (1290, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters), coupled to a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Detection was performed in negative ion mode using electrospray ionization, with multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) employed for quantification of 56 selected metabolites (Supplementary Table S4). All the metabolites were quantified by calibration curves established with standard solutions.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

For the in vitro experiment, six representative samples from each treatment in the second run were selected, whereas for the in vivo study, all rumen fluid samples collected at the final time point were analyzed, resulting in 16 samples per group. After thawing the rumen fluid samples at room temperature, total microbial DNA was extracted using the ZR Fecal DNA MiniPrep™ kit (ZYMO Research, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primer pair 338F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806 R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’)37. For methanogenic archaea, the primers were Ar915aF (5’-AGGAATTGGCGGGGGAGCAC-3’) and Ar1386R (5’-GCGGTGTGTGCAAGGAGC-3’)38. PCR amplicons were verified by electrophoresis on 2% agarose gels and subsequently purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Purified products were used to construct sequencing libraries, which were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using paired-end 2 × 300 bp reads. All raw sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the BioProject accession number PRJNA1211180.

Sequence reads were processed using the DADA2 workflow, which included quality filtering, error correction, paired-end read merging, chimera removal, and inference of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), with default parameters implemented in QIIME2 (v.2024.5)39,40. Taxonomic annotation of bacterial ASVs was performed against the SILVA reference database (v.138.2)41, while archaeal ASVs were classified using the RIM-DB database42. To account for differences in sequencing depth across samples, the ASV count table was rarefied to the minimum sequencing depth observed among all samples.

Metagenome sequencing, assembly, and binning

In the in vivo study, metagenomic sequencing was conducted using rumen fluid samples collected at the final time point, with eight biological replicates per treatment group. Genomic DNA extracted from these samples was sheared to an average fragment length of approximately 400 bp using the Covaris M220 system (Gene Company Limited, Hong Kong, China). Library construction followed the dual-index paired-end protocol (2 × 150 bp) and sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Paired-end reads were first aligned to the bovine reference genome using Bowtie2 (v. 2.5.4)43, and reads that did not align were retained as high-quality, host-filtered sequences. MEGAHIT (v.1.2.9) was used to assemble the clean reads with “-min-contig-len 500” parameters44. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted from the assembled contigs using Prodigal (v. 2.6.3)45, and only ORFs ≥ 100 bp in length were translated into protein sequences for further processing. To construct a non-redundant gene catalog, predicted protein sequences were clustered using CD-HIT (v. 4.8.1)46, applying a threshold of > 95% sequence identity and > 90% alignment coverage. The longest sequence within each cluster was selected as the representative sequence. Based on the Non-redundant Protein Sequence Database, species taxonomic analysis of unigenes was performed using DIAMOND software (v. 2.1.11)47. Metagenomic sequencing raw reads have been submitted to the National Center for Biotechnology Information Short Reads Archive database under the project PRJNA1211180.

To reconstruct metagenomic assembled genomes (MAGs) from the rumen microbial community, the assembled contigs were grouped into bins using MetaBAT2 (v. 2.15) with default parameters48. Quality assessment of the resulting MAGs was conducted using CheckM (v. 1.1.2) to evaluate completeness and contamination levels49. MAGs with a completeness ≥ 90% and contamination ≤ 5% were considered high quality. Taxonomic classification of MAGs was performed using GTDB-Tk (v. 2.3.0)50 against the GTDB (release 226) database.

Functional annotation of the gene catalog was conducted using KofamKOALA (https://www.genome.jp/tools/kofamkoala/)51, with particular emphasis on metabolic pathways associated with volatile fatty acid synthesis and methanogenesis. Hydrogenases were identified and classified using HydDB6 using the DIAMOND (v.2.1.11)47, with genes encoding terminal reductases screened as done by ref. 6. A detailed functional annotation protocol can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

Metatranscriptomic sequencing analysis

Metatranscriptomic analysis was conducted using rumen fluid samples obtained at the final sampling point from the in vivo experiment, with eight biological replicates per treatment group subjected to sequencing. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s guidelines, with subsequent removal of contaminating genomic DNA using the RNase-Free DNase Set (Qiagen, Germany). RNA quality was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis, and RNA concentrations were determined using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Library preparation was performed with the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA LT Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), which included RNA fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation, PCR enrichment, and library size selection. The resulting libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Raw RNA-seq reads were initially assessed for sequencing quality using FastQC (v. 0.12.0)52. Adapter contamination and low-quality bases were subsequently removed with Cutadapt (v. 3.7)53. To eliminate host-derived sequences, the cleaned reads were aligned against the Bos taurus reference genome using the STAR aligner (v. 2.7.11b)54, and mapped reads were discarded. Ribosomal and transfer RNA sequences were then filtered out from the remaining reads using SortMeRNA (v. 4.3.7)55, based on SILVA rRNA reference databases (release 138)41. The resulting high-quality, non-rRNA reads were assembled de novo with Trinity (v. 2.5.1)56.

Taxonomic annotation was conducted using Kraken2 (v. 2.1.0)57 with using the complete set of bacterial and archaeal in NCBI RefSeq database as reference. Open reading frames were identified using TransGeneScan (v. 1.2.1)58, and to generate a non-redundant gene set, predicted sequences were clustered with CD-HIT (v. 4.8.1)46 based on a sequence identity threshold of >95% and coverage >90%. All raw data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under BioProject accession number PRJNA1213261.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of both in vitro and in vivo rumen fermentation parameters, as well as milk production traits, were performed using SAS software via the PROC MIXED procedure (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). In vitro data were analyzed using a completely randomized block design, with run as the blocking factor. Run was treated as a random effect, while treatment was considered a fixed effect.

Sample size determination for the in vivo experiment was conducted using an online sample size calculation tool (https://samplesizecalculator.ucdavis.edu/) developed by Ramirez-Agudelo and Kebreab59 at the University of California, Davis. Power analysis indicated that allocating 32 cows to two treatments under a randomized complete block design would provide adequate statistical power. Specifically, this sample size was sufficient to detect a 10% reduction in methane emissions at a significance level of 0.05 with 80% statistical power.

In vivo data were analyzed using a repeated measures approach with the MIXED procedure in SAS 9.4. The statistical model incorporated fixed effects of treatment (Trt), time, and their interaction (Trt × Time), while block and individual cows nested within treatment were treated as random effects. A first-order autoregressive covariance structure [AR(1)] was applied based on model fit using the Akaike information criterion (AIC). Results for performance metrics, fermentation parameters, and methane emissions were expressed as least squares means and adjusted for baseline values recorded during the covariate period. Statistical significance was established at P < 0.05, while values between 0.05 and 0.10 were interpreted as trends.

Microbial alpha diversity was evaluated using the Shannon index, Simpson index, and the abundance-based coverage estimator (Ace), as calculated with the phyloseq package (v. 1.40.0) in R (v. 4.2.0). Differences in diversity metrics between treatments were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, and post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Community dissimilarities were quantified based on Bray–Curtis distances calculated with the vegdist function in the vegan package (v. 2.6-6.1). These distances were visualized by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) using the cmdscale function. Treatment effects on overall microbial community structure were further evaluated by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) implemented with the adonis2 function.

Additionally, Analysis of Compositions of Microbiomes with Bias Correction (ANCOM-BC) was employed, with statistically significant taxa identified based on adjusted P-values < 0.05. To further identify key microbial taxa associated with GSP supplementation, differential abundance analysis was performed using Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe), applying an LDA score threshold > 2 and P < 0.05.

Results

GSP reduced in vitro CH4 production and altered rumen microbial community

There were no significant differences between treatments in total volatile fatty acids (TVFA) or in the molar proportions of valerate, isobutyrate, and isovalerate (P > 0.05; Supplementary Table S5). However, GSP supplementation resulted in a lower acetate molar proportion compared to the control group (P < 0.05) and a higher propionate molar proportion (P < 0.01), leading to a reduced acetate-to-propionate ratio (P < 0.05). Additionally, GSP significantly decreased ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) concentrations (P < 0.05) and showed a tendency to reduce butyrate molar proportion (P = 0.071).

GSP addition had no measurable impact on in vitro ruminal digestibility of dietary nutrients (Supplementary Table S6). Regarding gas production, total gas volume after 24 h of incubation was significantly reduced in the GSP group (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A), and methane output per gram of degraded dry matter (DDM) was 15.9% lower in GSP-treated samples compared to the control (P < 0.05; Fig. 2B). No significant differences were observed in H2 production between treatments (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Following quality control and rarefaction, a total of 21,144 bacterial ASVs and 679 archaeal ASVs were retained. After quality filtering and removal of host-associated reads, a total of 1,287,848,576 high-quality reads were obtained from the metagenomic dataset, averaging 80,490,536 ± 6,311,752 reads per sample (mean ± SD). For the metatranscriptomic dataset, 562,728,228 high-quality reads were retained, with an average of 35,170,514 ± 9,282,256 reads per sample.

Consistent with the findings from 16S rRNA gene sequencing, taxonomic analysis of both metagenomic and metatranscriptomic datasets revealed distinct differences in the composition of bacterial and methanogenic communities between the CON and GSP groups (Supplementary Data 7–10). Alpha diversity metrics derived from metagenomic data, including the Ace and Simpson indices, showed no significant variation between treatments (P > 0.05). However, the PCoA suggested a trend toward group separation (PERMANOVA, P = 0.068; Fig. 4A, B). At the phylum level, the rumen microbiota was primarily composed of Bacteroidota and Bacillota (Fig. 4C). Within these phyla, Prevotella was the dominant bacterial genus, and Prevotella_sp. was among the most prevalent species in both experimental groups (Supplementary Figs. S4A and S4B).

Functional suppression of methanogenesis via disruption of hydrogen metabolism

To elucidate the functional mechanisms underlying GSP-induced methane suppression, we further investigated the expression of key enzymatic genes involved in ruminal methanogenesis and hydrogen metabolism. Metagenomic and metatranscriptomic data revealed that GSP significantly downregulated the abundance and transcription of genes encoding core enzymes in the hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis pathway (MD: M00567), including methylenetetrahydromethanopterin dehydrogenase (mtd, EC:1.5.98.1), 5,10-methylenetetrahydromethanopterin reductase (mer, EC:1.5.98.2), and methyl-CoM reductase (mcr, EC:2.8.4.1), which catalyzes the terminal step of methane formation (Fig. 6A). Similarly, enzyme-coding genes in the methanol-utilizing (MD: M00356; Fig. 6B) and methylamine-utilizing (MD: M00563; Fig. 6C) pathways also exhibited reduced abundance and transcription in GSP-fed cows, indicating a broad inhibition across methanogenic routes.

Consistent with these findings, gene expression analysis of VFA biosynthetic pathways further confirmed the functional shift in carbon flux under GSP supplementation (Supplementary Fig. S5). At both the metagenomic and metatranscriptomic levels, GSP reduced the abundance of genes encoding enzymes involved in the acetate and butyrate production pathways, such as acetyl-CoA synthetase (acs, EC: 6.2.1.13), acetyl-CoA C-acetyltransferase (atoB, EC: 2.3.1.9) and butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase (But, EC: 1.3.8.1). In contrast, key enzymes in the succinate and acrylate pathways for propionate synthesis, including succinyl-CoA synthetase (suc, EC: 6.2.1.5), methylmalonyl-CoA/ethylmalonyl-CoA epimerase (MCEE, EC: 5.1.99.1), lactoyl-CoA dehydratase (lcd, EC: 4.2.1.54), and acrylyl-CoA reductase (acrC, EC: 1.3.1.95) were upregulated. These results suggest that GSP modulates ruminal fermentation toward increased propionate production, a hydrogen-consuming pathway, while suppressing acetate and butyrate pathways that generate reducing equivalents. This shift in VFA profiles at the gene expression level aligns with the observed rumen fermentation patterns and supports a redirection of hydrogen flow away from methanogenesis. Although metagenomic data showed no significant differences in the abundance of some genes involved in methane metabolism and VFA production pathways, the metatranscriptomic data revealed significant changes in gene expression. This discrepancy highlights the superior sensitivity of metatranscriptomic analysis for detecting active gene expression changes compared to metagenomic data.

In addition to direct inhibition of methanogenic enzymes, GSP significantly altered microbial hydrogen metabolism (Fig. 7). Specifically, the abundance of genes associated with alternative hydrogen sinks, including those involved in propionate production from pyruvate, was increased in the GSP group (Fig. 7A), suggesting a redirection of reducing equivalents away from methanogenesis. Classification of hydrogenase types revealed that GSP supplementation led to distinct shifts in the functional composition of hydrogenases (Fig. 7B, C and Supplementary Data 11). Notably, the relative abundance of methanogenic hydrogenases—including NiFe3a, NiFe3c, and NiFe4h—was significantly decreased in both metagenomic and metatranscriptomic profiles, consistent with suppressed hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis. Similarly, key fermentative hydrogenases such as FeFeA1, FeFeA2, and FeFeB were also downregulated. In contrast, respiratory hydrogenases (e.g., NiFe1a, NiFe1d) and sensory hydrogenases (e.g., FeFeC2, FeFeC3) showed increased abundance, particularly at the transcript level, suggesting a shift in hydrogen utilization toward alternative electron-accepting processes. Bifurcating and energy-converting hydrogenases exhibited no significant changes, indicating that the suppression of methanogenesis was not mediated through these classes. These results highlight a selective remodeling of hydrogenase expression under GSP treatment, redirecting hydrogen flow away from methane-producing pathways.

Metabolomic profiling reveals polyphenol-derived remodeling of carbon and hydrogen metabolism

The concentrations of phenolic compounds and proanthocyanidins in rumen fluid were measured during the in vivo trial. Total phenolic and flavonoid concentrations were significantly higher in the GSP group compared to CON (P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S9). The total proanthocyanidin content was also significantly increased in the GSP group (P < 0.001). Specific proanthocyanidin compounds, including catechin, epicatechin, gallocatechin, epigallocatechin, epicatechin gallate, epigallocatechin gallate, procyanidin B1, B2, and C1 showed significant increases in the GSP group (P < 0.001).

Untargeted and targeted metabolomic profiling revealed that GSP supplementation markedly reshaped the rumen metabolic landscape (Supplementary Data 14–S17 and Fig. 9). Non-targeted metabolomics analysis using OPLS-DA identified a distinct separation between groups, revealing 31 significantly altered metabolites under GSP treatment (VIP > 1, P < 0.05, Fig. 9A). Among the 31 differential metabolites, 15 were upregulated and 16 were downregulated, further emphasizing the impact of GSP supplementation on metabolic shifts. Notably, several microbial fermentation products were significantly elevated in the GSP group. Specifically, polyphenol-derived metabolites including hippuric acid, 4-hydroxyphenylacetic acid, and vanillic acid were significantly increased, indicating active microbial biotransformation of GSP-derived polyphenols. Additionally, key intermediates of central carbon metabolism such as pyruvic acid and succinic acid were also elevated in the GSP group, suggesting a broader shift in microbial metabolic activity.

Targeted analysis of proanthocyanidin-derived metabolites further confirmed microbial biotransformation of GSP components (Fig. 9B). Several hydroxyphenylpropionic acids, hydroxybenzoic acids, and phenylvaleric acid derivatives were enriched in the GSP group, indicating active ring cleavage, dehydroxylation, and chain-shortening processes. These metabolites are consistent with known microbial catabolic routes of flavan-3-ols and procyanidins. In parallel, targeted metabolomics of central carbon metabolism showed that GSP shifted the intracellular redox landscape (Fig. 9D). In particular, increased levels of succinate and propionate pathway intermediates (e.g., succinyl-CoA, propionyl-CoA) alongside reduced fumarate and butyrate-related compounds suggest a rerouting of hydrogen flux toward non-methanogenic, hydrogen-consuming pathways. This was further supported by the integrative map (Fig. 9E), which links flavonoid catabolism to key steps in glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and the TCA cycle. Together, these data indicate that GSP-derived metabolites not only serve as substrates for microbial catabolism but also modulate intracellular redox balance and hydrogen utilization, contributing to the suppression of methanogenesis through both structural and functional remodeling of the rumen microbiome.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that GSP markedly suppresses enteric CH4 emissions through a multifaceted reprogramming of rumen fermentation and hydrogen metabolism. In both in vivo and in vitro trials, GSP consistently reduced CH4 production, while in the in vivo trial, dissolved H2 concentration was significantly increased in the GSP group. This agrees with prior reports demonstrating that condensed tannins reduce ruminant CH4 yield and modulate rumen fermentation patterns by shifting acetate and propionate proportions60–62. In our study, the 16.5% reduction in methane intensity observed with GSP (5 g/kg DM) is more pronounced than the 9.3% average reduction reported in a recent meta-analysis of various tannin sources, where effective mitigation typically required much higher inclusion rates (>10 g/kg DM) 63. This discrepancy likely stems from the exceptionally high purity (95.02%) and lower polymerization degree of GSP compared to crude extracts from quebracho or chestnut, which often contain higher proportions of non-bioactive polyphenols64.

In our study, GSP decreased the ruminal A:P ratio, indicating a shift toward propionate production, which serves as an alternative hydrogen sink65. By channeling reducing equivalents into propionate at the expense of acetate and butyrate, the GSP-treated rumen diverted hydrogen away from methanogenesis. This pattern is consistent with previous studies of tannin extracts. Fitri et al.66 reported that increasing dietary condensed tannins significantly decreased the A:P ratio in ruminants. In addition, GSP-treated rumens exhibited lower butyrate and NH3-N concentrations. These findings suggest improved nitrogen utilization and a shift toward H2-consuming fermentation pathways. Similarly, Wang et al.67 found that quebracho-chestnut tannin extract decreased ruminal NH3-N and tended to reduce blood and milk urea-N, indicating enhanced nitrogen efficiency, which likely contributed to the higher milk protein percentage in the GSP group. Condensed tannins can form complexes with dietary proteins, protecting them from excessive ruminal degradation and increasing non-ammonia nitrogen and essential amino acids flow to the abomasum and small intestine14, thus providing more precursors for milk protein synthesis without reducing total milk yield18.

Importantly, TVFA production was not severely depressed at the GSP dose used in vivo, highlighting that optimal dosing can mitigate CH4 emissions without compromising rumen function. This is further supported by our in vitro findings, which showed that GSP supplementation did not negatively affect the digestibility of NDF or ADF. While high concentrations of condensed tannins are often associated with reduced fiber digestion primarily through the formation of complexes with lignocellulose or the direct inhibition of cellulolytic bacteria and enzymes14,15,17, the dosage used in this study (5 g/kg in vitro and 150 g/d in vivo) appears to be within a range that modulates microbial metabolism without over-inhibiting the fibrolytic community. These results suggest that GSP, at these inclusion levels, can effectively redirect hydrogen flow while maintaining the core degradative capacity of the rumen. From a practical standpoint, the dual impact of GSP on methane mitigation and feed conversion efficiency (as indicated by improved FCM/DMI and ECM/DMI ratios) positions GSP as a promising additive for sustainable dairy production.

At the mechanistic level, GSP induced concerted suppression of methanogenic archaea and their hydrogenotrophic activity. Differential abundance analyses using both ANCOM-BC and LEfSe yielded largely consistent results, with taxa identified by both methods considered robustly associated with GSP treatment. Metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analyses revealed that GSP markedly downregulated core methanogenesis pathways. Transcript abundances for key enzymes in hydrogenotrophic, methanol-, and methylamine-utilizing methanogenesis were significantly decreased, indicating broad inhibition across CO2-reducing Methanobrevibacter-type and methylotrophic archaea. In vivo, archaeal relative abundance declined ~45% (methanogen 16S rRNA per total bacteria), although Methanobrevibacter and Methanomassiliicoccus remained dominant. Notably, many enzymes showed non-significant metagenomic changes but significant transcriptional downregulation, suggesting that transcriptional regulation, rather than population size reduction, primarily drives GSP-induced methanogenesis suppression, similar to observations with 3-NOP7. Unlike 3-NOP, which specifically inhibits MCR and transiently increases methylotrophic activity7, GSP uniformly suppressed all methanogens, likely by disrupting archaea directly or via symbiotic protozoa deprivation68–70. These findings align with meta-analyses reporting decreased archaeal counts and methane yield under tannin supplementation18,71. Suppressing methanogens raises the question of how the rumen disposes of reducing equivalents (H2, formate, NADH). Without adaptation, H2 accumulation can inhibit fermentative bacteria and anaerobic digestion72,73, so the microbial community redirects metabolic pathways to maintain redox balance when methanogenesis is inhibited.

A key insight from this study is that GSP “reprogrammed” hydrogen flow through alternative microbial pathways, activating latent hydrogen sinks and altering hydrogenase expression system-wide. Normally, H2 is produced mainly by [FeFe] hydrogenases, which aid H2 production and sensing, and by [NiFe] hydrogenases, involved in H2 uptake6. In the CON group, [FeFe]-hydrogenases (especially group A2) and [NiFe]-hydrogenases (e.g., group 3a) were abundant, supporting H2 production11, which promoted methanogenesis and acetate formation. In contrast, GSP supplementation increased [NiFe]-hydrogenases involved in H2 uptake11, facilitating alternative processes such as nitrate and sulfate respiration, thereby lowering H2 and mitigating methane. Moreover, group C [FeFe] hydrogenases, known for sensing H27, were enriched in GSP-fed cows, indicating their role in regulating H2 dynamics under reduced methane conditions. These results highlight the importance of hydrogenases, particularly group C, in adapting the rumen microbial community to higher H2 resulting from methanogenesis inhibition by GSP. Our results also suggest that H2-producing bacteria responded to elevated ruminal H2 by reducing hydrogen generation. Similar feedback regulation has been reported during methane inhibition, where H2 accumulation suppresses [FeFe]-hydrogenase expression and redirects electrons to alternative pathways6,7. Consistently, GSP supplementation reduced the expression of H2-evolving enzymes, likely helping maintain redox balance and prevent feedback inhibition of NADH reoxidation74,75.

Conversely, GSP stimulated alternative hydrogen-consuming pathways, as evidenced by the increased abundance of respiratory [NiFe]-hydrogenases, including group 1a and 1d enzymes. These hydrogenases are associated with microbial H2 uptake and respiratory processes, such as fumarate reduction and the utilization of alternative electron acceptors76,77. Consistent with this shift, metatranscriptomic analysis revealed enhanced expression of key enzymes in the succinate and acrylate pathways for propionate production, whereas enzymes involved in acetate and butyrate formation were downregulated. Together, these changes indicate a rerouting of reducing equivalents away from H2-generating pathways and toward propionate synthesis, thereby reducing the availability of hydrogen for methanogenesis. This finding supports the concept that propionate production serves as a major alternative sink for metabolic hydrogen in the rumen10, suggesting that GSP mitigates methane formation by promoting endogenous hydrogen-utilizing pathways.

The rise in lactate and succinate supports alternative electron flow: lactate formation from pyruvate consumes NADH, serving as a rapid sink for excess [H]7, while succinate production via fumarate reduction is a hydrogen-consuming pathway leading to propionate78. Fumarate reductase (FrdA) was also increased in metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analyses, highlighting fumarate reduction as an active hydrogen sink. Formate, which can transiently carry hydrogen, increased under GSP supplementation, consistent with prior reports that methanogenesis inhibition elevates formate release7,79,80. Additionally, genes involved in sulfate and sulfite reduction (aprA, dsrA) and nitrate ammonification (narG) were upregulated, representing alternative electron sinks that help stabilize ruminal redox balance and reduce methane emissions81–83. Collectively, these findings indicate that GSP broadly redirects hydrogen flux into multiple non-methanogenic pathways.

Although rumen acetogens are typically outcompeted by methanogens, our data suggest that putative acetogenic and fumarate-reducing bacteria became more important hydrogen sinks under GSP treatment. This inference is supported by the increased abundance of respiratory [NiFe]-hydrogenase transcripts, which are associated with microbial hydrogen uptake, together with enhanced succinate pathway activity6. Consistently, MAG-based analysis showed that Bacillota-associated genomes exhibited enrichment of propionate-producing pathways, whereas fibrolytic taxa such as Ruminococcaceae displayed reduced expression of hydrogen-forming enzymes, which normally generate H2 during acetate and butyrate production84. We also observed phylum-level functional shifts. Verrucomicrobiota showed reduced representation of hydrogen-metabolizing functions under GSP supplementation, suggesting inhibition of some low-abundance hydrogen producers, whereas Prevotellaceae maintained or slightly increased their contribution to rumen fermentation, consistent with their role in propionate production and utilization of fermentation intermediates. These findings indicate that relatively small changes in key microbial populations can drive substantial shifts in ruminal hydrogen flux. Consistent with Li et al.11, who demonstrated that variation in methane yield is linked to distinct hydrogen-disposal routes, our results further support the concept that redirecting hydrogen toward alternative electron sinks is central to methane mitigation6. GSP supplementation appears to induce such a low-methane functional state by favoring microbial populations that channel reducing equivalents toward propionate production rather than methanogenesis.

The targeted and untargeted metabolomic profiling uncovered extensive microbial transformation of GSP into various bioactive phenolic acids. Compounds such as hippuric acid and hydroxyphenylpropionic acids, which were elevated in the GSP group, are known microbial degradation products of flavan-3-ols36. These metabolites may act as modulators of microbial gene expression and redox balance, providing an indirect route by which GSP shapes ruminal fermentation and hydrogen flow. Our proposed integration of polyphenol degradation pathways with central carbon metabolism further suggests that GSP not only serves as a substrate but also contributes to redox modulation by enhancing pathways that consume reducing equivalents, such as propionate biosynthesis and nitrate respiration.

We acknowledge that the laser methane detector (LMD) is not the gold standard for enteric methane measurement, with respiratory chambers providing greater accuracy. However, LMD offers important advantages for large-scale on-farm studies, including its non-invasive nature, operational simplicity, and suitability for measuring multiple animals under commercial conditions26,27. Its reliability has been validated against respiratory chambers, with studies reporting strong agreement between the two methods (R2 ≈ 0.9)85,86. In addition, LMD has been successfully applied in several studies of dairy cow methane emissions26,87,88. Nevertheless, limitations remain, including dependence on conversion equations, diurnal variation, and operator effects. To minimize these sources of variation, we followed standardized protocols recommended by ref. 26, including a fixed measurement distance, 8-min recording duration, twice-daily sampling, and the use of trained technicians. Future studies incorporating chamber-based measurements would further strengthen these findings.

From a practical perspective, the feasibility of GSP supplementation depends on balancing additive costs with production benefits. Although the supplementation rate used in this study (150 g/head/d) may increase feeding costs, it was accompanied by improved feed efficiency, higher milk protein percentage, and a 16.5% reduction in methane emission intensity, all of which may help offset the additional expense. The high purity of GSP (95.02% proanthocyanidins) also allows lower inclusion rates than many crude tannin extracts, potentially reducing the risk of adverse effects on rumen function. Notably, despite reductions in some fibrolytic taxa (e.g., Fibrobacter and Butyrivibrio), fiber digestibility was maintained, likely reflecting the functional redundancy of the rumen lignocellulolytic microbiome89,90. As dairy production systems increasingly prioritize environmental sustainability, GSP may represent a promising strategy for simultaneously improving production efficiency and reducing methane emissions.

In summary, this study provides multi-layered mechanistic evidence that dietary GSP suppresses rumen methane production by targeting hydrogen metabolism at both the taxonomic and functional levels (Fig. 10). Through microbial community restructuring, hydrogenase remodeling, enhancement of alternative electron sinks, and redox modulation via polyphenol-derived metabolites, GSP offers a promising strategy to reprogram rumen microbiomes for lower methane output and improved sustainability in ruminant production systems. Future research should explore whether rumen microbes could adapt to prolonged GSP exposure, thereby reducing its methane-mitigating efficacy over time, and thus the long-term stability of GSP effects requires further investigation. Furthermore, given that tannins are known to influence rumen protozoal populations and their symbiotic relationships with methanogens, future studies incorporating 18S rRNA-based analysis are warranted to elucidate the role of protozoa in GSP-induced methane mitigation.

Multi-omics landscape of ruminal hydrogen metabolism reprogramming by grape seed proanthocyanidins (GSP).

Integrates metagenomic, metatranscriptomic, and metabolomic data to illustrate the mechanistic reprogramming of ruminal hydrogen metabolism in dairy cows supplemented with GSP, comparing control (CON, left) and GSP-treated (right) groups. In CON cows, fermentation favors acetate/butyrate (high A/P ratio). [FeFe]-hydrogenases drive H₂ production, fueling hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis (Mcr ↑ ) and high CH₄ emissions. Methanobrevibacter dominates; alternative H₂-utilizing bacteria and central carbon intermediates (succinate, pyruvate) are low. In GSP-supplemented cows, multi-omics reveals: (1) acetate/butyrate ↓ , propionate ↑ , lower A/P ratio; (2) H₂-producing [FeFe]-hydrogenases ↓ , H₂-uptake [NiFe]-hydrogenases ↑ ; (3) H₂ rerouted to alternative electron sinks; (4) Methanobrevibacter ↓ , alternative H₂-utilizers ↑ ; (5) polyphenol-derived metabolites and succinate/pyruvate ↑ . These changes reduce CH₄ emissions, improve milk protein, and enhance feed efficiency. Red arrows (↑) indicate upregulation or enhancement, green arrows (↓) indicate downregulation or suppression in the GSP group compared to CON. A/P ratio, acetate-to-propionate ratio; NH3-N, ammonia nitrogen; CH₄, methane; H2, hydrogen.
