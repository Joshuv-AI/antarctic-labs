 ABSTRACT
 We report experimental measurements of dimethyl sulfide oxidation, focusing on a major
chemical intermediate, hydroperoxymethyl thioformate (HPMTF). The (photo)chemistry and
transport properties of HPMTF, which are critical to accurate modeling of tropospheric sulfur, are
not known, partly because current analytical methods are not optimized for elusive chemical
intermediates.
 We investigated dimethyl sulfide oxidation using a custom time-resolved photoionization mass
spectrometer, interfaced with pulsed laser photolysis, continuous photolysis, and liquid droplet
reactors. We detected and characterized HPMTF (along with other reaction intermediates),
quantified its yield, and measured its formation rate coefficient. We also developed a clean, intense,
online HPMTF source, suitable for further experimental investigations. Lastly, we measured the
mass accommodation coefficient of HPMTF on water droplets – a critical quantity for modeling its
atmospheric partitioning between the gas and condensed phases. The experimental platform we
developed here enables the exploration of other critical elusive reaction intermediates.

1. INTRODUCTION
 The goal of this report is developing experimental techniques for a detailed understanding of the
gas-phase oxidation of dimethyl sulfide (DMS), an important part of the global sulfur cycle, with a
particular focus on a key intermediate hydroperoxymethyl thioformate (HPMTF). DMS is the most
abundant biogenic sulfur molecule emitted into the troposphere, primarily over the ocean surface.1-3
The atmospheric oxidation of DMS in the gas phase is initiated mainly by OH, NO3, and reactive
halogen species,4 and involves highly complex reaction networks. Atmospheric DMS oxidation has
been investigated for over 40 years, yet many aspects of its detailed mechanism remain poorly
constrained,5 and some of the chemical intermediates in DMS oxidation are not well understood.
Recently, Wu et al.6 identified a new gas-phase pathway from DMS to HPMTF (HOOCH2SCHO).
HPMTF is a closed-shell molecule that is metastable at room temperature, suggesting that it could
be a major previously overlooked reaction product at atmospherically relevant conditions.
Subsequently, HPMTF formation from DMS oxidation was confirmed in laboratory flow tube
experiments7 and HPMTF was detected at high mixing ratios (up to 60 ppt) over the Pacific and
Atlantic oceans,8 confirming that it is a significant reservoir of sulfur in marine air.
 Recent global modeling studies introduced HPMTF reactions into the atmospheric sulfur cycle
and demonstrated high sensitivity to HPMTF sources and sinks, deposition, and photochemistry.9-11
However, many of the critical model parameters related to HPMTF have never been measured and
consequently have large uncertainties. The HPMTF consumption reactions, the optical absorption
spectrum of HPMTF, or the liquid uptake rate of HPMTF are currently only estimated in global
models from analogous compounds – often under uncertain assumptions. As a result, DMS
oxidation is currently an active, yet challenging field: for example, since 2019, at least seven papers
have calculated6,8,12,13 or experimentally measured7,12,14-16 the critically important rate coefficient for
HPMTF formation, but the reported values at 298 K range over almost an order of magnitude: 0.03
– 0.23 s-1.
 One important reason for the lack of reliable information on the fundamental structural
properties, chemical reactivity, photochemistry, and multi-phase transport of HPMTF is that, like
other hydroperoxides, it cannot be easily synthesized and is unstable in the long-term, meaning it
cannot be safely stored. As a result, authentic reference samples of HPMTF cannot be obtained for
analysis by typical laboratory methods, and HPMTF must be characterized in situ, i.e. from the
atmosphere or from chemical reactors, under typically complex reaction conditions. It is therefore of
great interest and practical importance to develop detection methods for HPMTF that can be
applied to a broad array of chemical kinetics and physical chemistry experiments.
 The published reports of HPMTF detection in the laboratory flow reactors,7,16 in atmospheric
chambers,15,17,18 and in field campaigns8,19 did so by using chemical ionization mass spectrometry
(CIMS). CIMS is a well-developed technique that measures the mass-to-charge ratio of an ionic
complex of analyte with a specific reagent ion (in this case, the HPMTF∙I- adduct), providing
unambiguous detection of HPMTF. However, CIMS is inherently not universally sensitive, since the
chosen reagent ions do not typically complex efficiently with all possible reaction products.
Additionally, CIMS is an ex-situ sampling method that requires a flow tube to react the analyte with
the reagent ions. As such, it is not typically sensitive to short-lived reactive species, because they do
not survive transport through the flow tube. Lastly, the sensitivity of CIMS must be calibrated,
which is not possible directly for compounds (like HPMTF), for which authentic samples cannot be
obtained. Therefore, quantification of HPMTF and many other key reaction intermediates often
relies on sensitivity estimates from analogous molecules, which may introduce large systematic
uncertainties.

 13

 The purpose of this project is to develop experimental approaches that enable fundamental
investigations of HPMTF and other important chemical species in DMS oxidation under simplified
and tightly controlled laboratory conditions. To accomplish this goal, we set out three main
objectives, which are discussed in detail below:
 • Section 2 - Direct detection and characterization of HPMTF and other key DMS oxidation
 intermediates by photoionization mass spectrometry (PIMS) – a common, universal, sensitive,
 and structurally selective experimental method.
 • Section 3 – Development of an efficient online source of HPMTF that can supply high
 concentrations of HPMTF for further analysis.
 • Section 4 – Direct measurements of the accommodation coefficient of HPMTF on liquid
 water droplets, a critical parameter for modeling its atmospheric impact.
 It is worth noting that although the bulk of this report focuses on HPMTF, we also characterized
other reaction intermediates, and the methods we developed here are generally applicable to other
complex reaction systems.

2. THE CHARACTERIZATION OF HPMTF AND OTHER PRODUCTS
 AND INTERMEDIATES OF DIMETHYL SULFIDE OXIDATION
 This section reports time-resolved measurements of DMS oxidation using a high-temperature,
high-pressure laser photolysis reactor, coupled to a custom, uniquely sensitive PIMS apparatus,
which were developed at Sandia in 2019.20 This setup allows us to conduct experiments under finely
controlled temperature, pressure, and reactant concentrations, which are not directly relevant to
atmospheric conditions, but which allow us to simplify the chemistry and decouple the key reactions
of interest from secondary reactions. By employing PIMS, a universally sensitive probe method, we
detect all transient chemical species simultaneously and obtain a comprehensive view of the relevant
reaction networks. This in turn enables us to apply a global time-resolved atom balance analysis
method, which we also recently developed, to quantify unknown intermediates without available
reference photoionization cross-sections (PICS). The results in this section have also been submitted
as an article to The Journal of Physical Chemistry A and are currently under peer review.

2.1. Current state of understanding of gas-phase DMS oxidation
 The atmospheric oxidation of DMS in the gas phase is initiated by reactive radicals,4 proceeding
by an H abstraction and an addition channel:
        CH3SCH3 + X ⟶ CH3SCH2 + HX                                                            (R1a)
        CH3SCH3 + X ⟶ (CH3)2S—X,                X = OH, NO3, Cl, BrO                          (R1b)
 The products of DMS oxidation include SO2, OCS, dimethyl sulfoxide, methanesulfonate, and
methanesulfonic acid, which can in turn condense to sulfate aerosols, impacting the Earth’s radiative
balance and acting as cloud condensation nuclei.21,22 Recently, Wu et al.6 identified a new important
gas-phase pathway from DMS to hydroperoxymethyl thioformate (HPMTF, HOOCH2SCHO) via a
series of O2 addition and internal H shift steps, as shown in Scheme 1:
          CH3SCH2 + O2 ⟶ CH3SCH2O2                                                         (R2)
          CH3SCH2O2 ⟶ CH2SCH2OOH                                                           (R3)
          CH2SCH2OOH + O2 ⟶ O2CH2SCH2OOH                                                   (R4)
 O2CH2SCH2OOH ⟶ HOOCH2SCHOOH ⟶ HOOCH2SCHO + OH (R5)

Scheme 1. The pathway to HPMTF from Cl-initiated DMS oxidation.

 Note that this reaction sequence is well known in combustion chemistry as the basis of
autoignition, i.e. the spontaneous oxidation of fuels at gas temperatures below ∼1000 K.23 There, the
intermediate carbon-centered and peroxy radicals are commonly labeled R, RO2, QOOH, and
O2QOOH, and we refer to them in the same way here. The radical di-hydroperoxide species formed
by internal H transfer in O2QOOH is typically not stable, and we therefore lump the last two steps
of this mechanism together as reaction R5.

 15

 The rate limiting step of HPMTF formation is isomerization of RO2 to QOOH (R3).16
Bimolecular reactions of RO2 with other peroxy radicals, HO2, and NO compete with R3,24,25 and
the rate coefficients of these bimolecular reactions are known. Therefore, knowledge of the
isomerization rate coefficient, k3, is a critical parameter in modeling the atmospheric impact of DMS
because it determines the branching to HPMTF formation under different atmospheric conditions.
Experimentally measured7,12,14,15 room temperature values of k3 span the range 0.03 – 0.23 s-1 and
calculated6,8,12,13 k3(298 K) are between 0.04 and 0.21 s-1. Most recently, Assaf et al.16 performed a
careful experimental and theoretical study of k3 over T = 314 – 433 K using CIMS. From an
Arrhenius fit to their temperature-dependent results, they reported a room-temperature value of k4
= 0.06 s-1 with a 2σ uncertainty of ±30%. However, the sensitivity of CIMS to HPMTF is uncertain,
and this uncertainty propagates into quantification of HPMTF by CIMS.
 Here, we report the characterization of HPMTF and other DMS oxidation intermediates by
vacuum ultraviolet (VUV) photoionization mass spectrometry. Using photolytic Cl atom initiation,
we produce HPMTF on millisecond timescales in a flow reactor at temperatures from 400 to 545 K
and detect it by time-resolved VUV-PIMS, along with RO2 and numerous products of competing
secondary reactions. We explore the dependence of the observed product distributions on O2
concentration and propose an expanded mechanism for secondary chemistry, which impacts the
interpretation of other laboratory experiments using Cl atom oxidation of DMS.
 We also fit the RO2 decay and HPMTF rise to obtain k3 at T = 430 – 510 K and combine our
results with those of Assaf et al.16 at 314 – 433 K. Our Arrhenius fit to the combined dataset is
indistinguishable from that of Assaf et al., strengthening the validity of their extrapolation of k3 to
room temperature. Furthermore, we experimentally constrain the yields of HPMTF and RO2 and
determine their absolute PICS from a global time-resolved atom balance analysis. We find that
HPMTF does not have a stable parent cation and instead undergoes dissociative ionization to four
dominant fragment ions. Nonetheless, the absolute, channel-specific partial PICS of HPMTF we
report enable its quantitative detection by PIMS, a common experimental tool in chemical kinetics,
under atmospherically relevant conditions in other laboratory reactor or chamber experiments.

2.2. Methods

2.2.1. Experimental methods
 Our experimental apparatus, which includes a laser photolysis reactor and a photoionization mass
spectrometer, has been described previously.20 Briefly, the reactor has a 0.6 cm inner diameter, 3.6
cm-long flow channel, lined with chemically inert quartz, encased in a heated Inconel chamber,
operating at temperatures up to 800 K and pressures up to 100 bar. Photolysis laser pulses irradiate
the gas mixture through a UV fused silica window at the back of the reactor at repetition rates of 1 –
10 Hz, creating a spatially uniform population of radicals. The photolyzed gas flows forward and is
replaced by a fresh mixture before the next laser shot, and a small (1 – 5 %) central portion of the
gas is continuously sampled by an interchangeable pinhole orifice, with a diameter of 30 or 50 µm
used in this study.
 The reactor is placed in a vacuum chamber, housing an orthogonal ion extraction time-of-flight
(TOF) mass spectrometer. The sampled reaction gas expands into the ion source region with typical
operating pressures of 10-4 Torr and intersects a continuous VUV ionizing beam. Ionization occurs
in a high-density part of the supersonic gas expansion (0.25 cm from the orifice), resulting in high
ionization efficiency and enhanced instrument sensitivity. A calibrated photodiode records the VUV
photon fluence for signal normalization. The ions are focused by electrostatic lenses and guided into
 16

a reflectron TOF mass spectrometer, which is pulsed at 32 kHz here, and detected by an 8 cm × 3
cm microchannel plate detector. Ion signals are amplified and digitized with 0.1 ns TOF resolution,
producing complete mass spectra continuously every 31.25 µs with mass resolution m/∆m = 2000.
 For this study, the reaction gas consisted of N2, O2, DMS vapor (0.1% diluted in N2) and oxalyl
chloride vapor ((COCl)2, 1% diluted in N2), which we mixed on the fly. Typical concentrations were
[DMS] = (4.0 – 20)×1013 cm-3, [(COCl)2] = (8.1 – 65)×1014 cm-3, and [O2] = (7.0 – 7000)×1015 cm-3.
Reactions were initiated at P = 800 Torr and T = 400 – 545 K via oxalyl chloride photolysis at 248
nm: 26,27 (COCl)2 + hν (248 nm) ⟶ 2 CO + 2 Cl. The nascent Cl radicals reacted with DMS within
∼0.15 ms under pseudo-1st-order conditions:
 Cl + CH3SCH3 ⟶ products (R1)
 The photolysis repetition rate was 6.67 Hz. Mass spectra were recorded from t = -20 ms to +125
ms, relative to the initiating laser pulse, and averaged for up to 104 laser shots. Initial Cl atom
concentrations, [Cl]t=0, were determined from the measured photolysis fluence and published oxalyl
chloride absorption cross-section and Cl yields,27 and were varied over (0.7 – 38)×1012 cm-3 by
changing the photolysis pulse energies and oxalyl chloride concentrations. Ionizing radiation came
from Beamline 9.0.2 at the Advanced Light Source, which provides quasi-CW intense (∼1013
photons∙s-1), narrowband (E/∆E ∼1000), tunable (7 – 25 eV) VUV photons. Time-resolved
photoionization (PI) spectra of the detected chemical species were measured at a subset of
experimental conditions by scanning the VUV photon energies, EVUV = 8.5 – 11.2 eV. Product time
evolution was measured at EVUV = 9.7 and 10.95 eV over broad ranges of O2 and initial Cl atom
concentration. Additionally, we performed experiments at select conditions in the presence of CO to
scavenge OH radicals and distinguish secondary OH-initiated reactions from photolytic Cl-initiated
reactions. A complete list of experiments is detailed in the Appendix.
 Chemical species with known PICS were quantified, relative to the known starting concentration
of DMS, from their ion signal ratios, after correction for the mass-dependent detection efficiency, as
described in the Appendix and before.28 As part of this work, we recorded reference PI spectra for
CH3SCH3, CH3SSCH3, CH3S(O)CH3, and CH3SCH2Cl from authentic gaseous samples of these
compounds. These reference spectra are provided in the Appendix.
 Instrument calibrations were made using (i) a commercial mixture of 0.5% Xe/1% ethene/1%
propene/1% 1-butene in Argon, (ii) a homemade mixture of 0.1% CH4/0.1% CO/0.1% CO2/0.1%
Kr/0.1% Xe in nitrogen, and iii) a homemade mixture of 0.1% ethane/0.1% propane/0.1% n-
butane/0.1% n-pentane/0.1% Xe in nitrogen. Ionization energy calibration was done using the well-
known Xe 8s' resonance at 12.575 eV. The photodiode spectral response was validated using known
PI spectra of mixture (i); mass-dependent ion detection efficiency was determined using the known
PI cross-sections of mixtures (ii) and iii) at 14.74 eV.

2.2.2. Theoretical methods
 To help interpret the PI spectra of species for which reference PICS and authentic pure samples
are not available, we calculated the local (i.e. conformer-resolved) adiabatic and vertical ionization
energies (AIEs and VIEs) of candidate compounds. For a subset of species, we also mapped out the
stationary points on the parent cation potential energy surfaces (PESs) to understand their
fragmentation. Both tasks were mostly carried out with our automated PES exploration and gas-
phase kinetics tool, KinBot,29-31 using Gaussian 16 software32 for the L1 and L2 calculations and
Molpro 202333 for L3 calculation.

 17

 For ionization energy calculations, starting neutral conformer geometries were generated by
sampling all rotatable bonds on a 120-degree grid and optimizing the structures, first at the L1 =
B3LYP/6-31G(d) level and subsequently at the L2 = ωB97X-D/6-311++G(d,p) level. Next, unique
conformers were reoptimized on the cation PES at the L2 level, creating a set of neutral-cation pairs.
The differences of the optimized neutral and optimized cation energies are the local AIEs, and we
report them after zero-point energy (ZPE) correction. The neutral – cation energy differences at the
optimized neutral geometry are the local VIEs and are reported without ZPE correction. Lastly, we
refined the AIE and VIE of the lowest-lying neutral conformer at the L3 = CCSD(T)-F12a/cc-
pVTZ-F12//ωB97X-D/6-311++G(d,p) level and corrected the AIEs and VIEs for all other
conformers by the same L2 – L3 correction.
 The species in this study contained many anharmonic vibrations, and we did not attempt to
calculate neutral-cation Frank-Condon overlaps, using instead the calculated AIEs and VIEs as
qualitative guides for ion signal assignments. To compare experimental PI spectra with calculated
ionization energies, we assumed similar PICS for all conformers of a given species. We then assigned
a weight to each AIE and VIE based on the Boltzmann weight of the neutral conformer. Because
the vibrational temperature of the neutral species in the gas expansion is not known, we estimated
the Boltzmann weights at T = 100 – 300 K and found the temperature to have no effect on our
conclusions. These weights were then used to calculate cumulative probability distributions
𝐹𝐹IE (𝐸𝐸) = 𝑃𝑃(IE ≤ 𝐸𝐸) of all calculated AIEs and VIEs. Finally, we took the mid-points of the
distribution rises corresponding to 𝐹𝐹IE (𝐸𝐸) = 0.5 as the most probable observed AIE and VIE,
respectively.
 To calculate the possible dissociative ionization channels of important chemical intermediates
without reference PI spectra, we ran KinBot starting from the optimized structure of the parent
cations. KinBot located the stationary points along cation dissociation pathways at the L1 level and
refined them at the L2 level. Final ZPE-corrected energies were calculated at the L3 level. Because
ionic reactions, especially dissociation reactions can be steered by unexpected long-range effects,34
we investigated some dissociation pathways manually as well at the same levels of theory. Finally, to
estimate fragment branching fractions on the cation PES, we calculated microcanonical rate
coefficients, k(E), including anharmonic effects from hindered rotors and tunneling, using the
MESS code.35,36

Although the absence of parent cations makes our assignment challenging, we present strong
support for it below.
 Support for HPMTF assignment. Our results provide four arguments in favor of the
assignment of the m/z = 46, 47, 75, and 80 peaks as fragment ions of HPMTF.
i. Their time traces and PI spectra were identical (within our noise) at all T and [O2], suggesting one
 common carrier. It is unlikely that multiple neutral products are so strongly correlated.
ii. Their reactivity is consistent with HPMTF production in Scheme 1. Their intensity increased
 linearly with [Cl]t=0 and increased at higher O2, indicating that their source is a product not of
 radical-radical, but of radical + O2 reactions. Reaction R4, which leads to HPMTF, competes with
 unimolecular decomposition of CH2SCH2OOH, so that higher [O2] should favor HPMTF.
 Furthermore, these ion signals increased with temperature up to 475 K and decreased at higher T.
 The activation barrier of R3, which is rate-limiting for HPMTF formation, was calculated to be
 ∼17 kcal/mol.43 At the same time, HPMTF should decompose via O-O bond scission at high T,
 so that its yield should peak at intermediate T.
iii. Quantum chemical calculations of the HPMTF cation PES, shown in Figure 2, support our
 proposed assignments of DI into the m/z = 46, 47, 75, and 80 ion channels. The calculated AIE
 of HPMTF is ∼9.77 eV, and the parent cation is bound by <0.17 eV (∼3.9 kcal/mol). Substantial
 changes in the SCOO dihedral angle upon HPMTF ionization suggest poor Frank-Condon
 overlap near ionization threshold. VIEs of HPMTF conformers span ∼10.3 – 10.55 eV, which is
 above the lowest decomposition barriers and is likely why we did not observe the parent ion of
 HPMTF. The calculated AEs of the m/z = 46, 75, and 80 channels are 10.03, 10.44, and 9.44 eV,

 21

 respectively. One of the possible m/z = 80 fragment ions can further decompose into the m/z =
 47 channel with asymptotic energy of 10.32 eV. These calculated energies agree with the observed
 signal onsets, as we discuss in the following section.
 The calculated PES contains four other energetically accessible DI channels, which we did not
 observe: two isomerizations (with barriers of 9.97 and 10.03 eV), a combined S-C bond
 scission/H transfer, leading to m/z = 79 at 10.13 eV, and a decomposition to m/z = 63 ions at
 9.91 eV. The TSs for the first three pathways are likely to be tight, which would result in low flux.
 This fourth channel may also be dynamically disfavored; furthermore, it overlaps with strong
 signals from the CH3S13CH3 isotopolog of DMS and from the CClO DI channel of oxalyl
 chloride, which could obscure a small DI signal of HPMTF.
iv. We previously characterized an analogous product of dimethyl ether (CH3OCH3) oxidation:
 HOOCH2OCHO.46 The parent cation of this molecule was also negligible, and the major DI
 channels were neutral CO elimination, HO2 elimination, and HO2 + CO elimination, analogous
 to the m/z = 80, 75, and 47 DI channels of HPMTF. The CH2O+ + HOOCHO channel,
 analogous to the m/z = 46 channel of HPMTF, was not energetically accessible.
 In summary, our experimental and computational results strongly support the assignment of the
m/z = 46, 47, 75, and 80 ion peaks to HPMTF, providing a means of direct detection of HPMTF
by photoionization mass spectrometry, a common experimental tool in chemical kinetics.

2.3.2. Chemical networks in DMS oxidation
 Pre-photolysis chemistry . DMS may react with O2 or OxCl without laser photolysis via gas-
phase or wall-catalyzed “dark” reactions, and thus we considered Cl-initiated reactions of the dark
chemistry products. To check for potential effects of dark reactions, we collected mass spectra over
a broad range of residence times without the photolysis laser. Beside the starting reactants, DMS and
OxCl, we observed weak ion peaks due to CH3SCl, CH3SSCH3, and CH3SCH2Cl, as detailed in the
Appendix.
 We do not know the exact nature of the dark reactions, but they are consistent with C—H and
C—S bond activation in DMS, leading to the production of CH3S and CH3SCH2 radicals, which
then react with Cl. The signals of dark chemistry products increased linearly with reactor residence
time. Therefore, during the photolysis experiments we flowed the gas mixture at maximum practical
speed, resulting in residence times of ∼0.11 s. Under those conditions, only 1.8% of DMS was
consumed via dark reactions, and the ratio of DMS to product concentrations always exceeded 50.
Because the Cl + DMS rate coefficient is almost collision-limited, photolytic Cl atoms reacted
mainly (>98%) with DMS and not with the products of dark chemistry. Therefore, we neglect dark
chemistry in our analysis of photolysis-induced reactions.
 Low-O2 chemistry. At low [O2] we detected no HPMTF. Instead, we observed many other
oxygenated and chlorinated products, and we propose a reaction network in Scheme 2 to explain
their formation. The role of Cl addition to DMS (R1b) has been debated in the literature extensively.
Wine and co-workers40,41,58 revealed a pressure-dependent channel in the Cl + DMS reaction, which
became significant at atmospheric pressure, and attributed it to the formation of the Cl—DMS
adduct. Stickel et al.40 and Arsene et al.37 showed that this adduct reacts slowly (k < 4×10-18 cm3
molec-1 s-1) with O2. The branching into R1b (k1b/k1) has not been established at any temperature,
and data at elevated temperatures are particularly scarce.
 The two theory-based equilibrium constants based on high-level calculations for Cl—DMS
formation differ by four orders of magnitude, highlighting the challenges of both electronic
structure and thermochemical calculations in this system. Enami et al.38 reported the equilibrium
constant at 300 K of 2.8×10-12 based on a 17.7 kcal mol-1 binding energy at the
QCISD(T)/6−311++G(2df,2p)// B3LYP/6-311++G(3df,3pd) level. On the other hand, Resende
et al.39 provided an equilibrium constant of 5.5×10-16 cm3 molec-1 based on the 12.32 kcal mol-1
binding energy calculated at the UQCISD(T)//UMP2/DZP level. Extrapolating to our
experimental conditions, the values of Resende et al. result in <<1% of the radical population
existing as Cl—DMS. Enami et al. don’t provide the required information for extrapolating to our
temperatures, but they would likely predict that Cl—DMS dominates our radical population. If a
substantial fraction of the Cl radicals is initially trapped as the Cl—DMS complex, it could provide a
steady source of Cl radicals long after photolysis by dissociating to Cl + DMS, as free Cl are
removed by the H abstraction reaction R1a.
 We observed strong signals from RO2 even at low oxygen levels, demonstrating that RO2 was
efficiently generated. RO2 isomerizes to QOOH with a rate coefficient k3 ∼120 s-1 at 433 K,16 and
this should get even faster at higher temperatures. At low [O2], reaction R4 (QOOH + O2), which
leads to HPMTF, evidently cannot compete with back-isomerization to RO2 (R-3) or decomposition
to CH2S + CH2O + OH (R6). Jin et al.43 calculated R-3 and R6 to be the two fastest unimolecular
reactions of QOOH, and R6 is known to be efficient in analogous dimethyl ether oxidation at
elevated temperatures. In our experiments, R6 likely accounts for the observed CH2S and a portion
of CH2O.

 23

 Self-reaction of RO2 or reaction with Cl (R7) could produce the CH3SCH2O radical, which we
would expect to react rapidly with O2, forming CH3SCHO (R8). Alternatively, CH3SCH2O could
decompose to CH3S + CH2O. In addition to CH2O, we observed abundant dimethyl disulfide
(CH3SSCH3), which is strong evidence of CH3S self-reaction. Further reaction of CH3S + Cl may
explain the formation of CH3Cl. O2 addition to Cl—DMS, followed by ClO loss (R9) was proposed
as the source of CH3S(O)CH3 at atmospheric conditions.40 An analogous reaction sequence (Cl
addition, followed by O2 addition and ClO loss) could lead from CH3S(O)CH3 to CH3SO2CH3 (R10)
and also from CH3SSCH3 to CH3SS(O)CH3 (R12). CH3 loss from Cl—DMS may be an alternative
route to CH3SCl. Zhao et al. estimated the CH3SCl yield in R1 to be <2% at room T;41 however, that
pathway may be more important at our temperatures. Analogous Cl addition/CH3 loss from
dimethyl sulfoxide (collectively shown as R11) may lead to our observed CH3S(O)Cl.
 Urbanski et al.58 suggested that reactions of Cl—DMS with R (or RO2) may produce CH3SCH2Cl
+ DMS (+ O2). Alternatively, a simple reaction of R + Cl may also form CH3SCH2Cl. H abstraction
followed by oxidation may lead from CH3SSCH3 to CH3SSCHO (R13). This pathway is the exact
analog of the reactions leading from DMS to CH3SCHO. Lastly, we detected minor signals from
CH3SH. This product may result from H abstraction by CH3S, although we cannot pinpoint the
route to its formation.

 High O2 chemistry. At oxygen concentrations above ∼7×1017 cm-3, we observed much simpler
product distribution than at low [O2], consisting of four major stable species (CH2O, H2S, ClSCHO,
and HPTMF), minor yields of CH3SCl and CH3SCHO, and the short-lived RO2. We determined the
CH2O concentrations, relative to the pre-photolysis concentration of DMS, using the ratio of their
ion signals, the published PI spectrum of CH2O,50 and the PI spectrum of DMS that we measured
here. However, the other transient species in our high-[O2] experiments do not have available PICS
and could not be quantified in the same way. Therefore, to quantify them we employed the method

 24

of global atom balance fitting, which our group recently developed,28,46,47,49 and which we describe in
greater detail in the Appendix.
 Briefly, our approach leverages the fact that the instantaneous sum of all atoms contained in all
products and intermediates (quantified and unquantified) must equal the instantaneous sum of all
atoms contained in the consumed reactants. This mass balance must be fulfilled at all experimental
conditions (e.g., all temperatures, oxygen concentrations, and initial radical concentrations). Our
experiments do not detect O2, Cl atoms, HCl, or OH, which precludes tracking the O, Cl, and H
atom balance. However, we do detect all C- and S-containing species, allowing us to track the C
atom and S atom balance. The number of C and S atoms consumed by reactions is known from the
quantified depletion of DMS, as is the concentration of CH2O produced. Therefore, we perform a
least-squares optimization by scaling the time traces of each unquantified species (in units of ion
counts) at all experimental conditions, using a single condition-independent scaling factor per
species – effectively, the unknown PICS – until the C and S atom balance is fulfilled at all kinetic
times t and all conditions simultaneously.
 In the present study, the signals of CH3SCl and CH3SCHO were weak. Our fit could not
constrain them individually, but their combined yields were less than 6% at any condition. However,
the fit did constrain the absolute concentrations and, correspondingly, the absolute PICS of
ClSCHO, H2S, RO2, and HPMTF. Representative concentration-time profiles of the main transient
species at T = 470 K, with [O2] = 1.5×1018 cm-3, [Cl]t=0 = 9×1011 cm-3, with and without the OH-
scavenger CO, are presented in Figure 3 (pre-photolysis signals have been subtracted). Similar plots
at other temperatures from 430 to 530 K are shown in the Appendix.

the current consensus that HPMTF is the main product of unimolecular RO2 reactions.

ClSCHO do not arise from RO2, but are instead produced by parallel routes to RO2 formation, with
yields that are determined by the initial Cl reaction branching (R1a vs R1b). The Cl—DMS adduct is
known to react slowly with O2 at 300 K;37,40 however, this reaction may become significantly faster at
our temperatures. The products that we hypothesized in Scheme 2 to arise from Cl—DMS reactions
at low [O2] are virtually eliminated at high [O2], which is consistent with a mechanism switch to Cl—
DMS + O2 pathways at high [O2].

2.3.4. The CH3SCH2O2 → CH2SCH2OOH isomerization rate coefficient
 The path from the CH3SCH2 radical to HPMTF involves two O2 addition steps (R2 and R4 in
Scheme 1) and two internal H atom shifts (R3 and R5). Initial measurements7,12,14,15 of k3 at 293 –
298 K spanned the range from 0.03 to 0.23 s-1, and reported theoretical values6,8,12,13 of k3 ranged
from 0.04 to 2.1 s-1. k5 was calculated by Wu et al.3 and Veres et al.9 as 35 times or 10 times higher
than k3, respectively. Under our high-[O2] conditions, the pseudo-1st order O2 addition rate
coefficients k’2 and k’4 exceed 106 s-1, according to available experimental and theoretical data.16,43
Therefore, R3 is the rate-limiting step in HPMTF formation, and its precise knowledge is critical to
accurate modeling of tropospheric DMS oxidation.
 Most recently, Assaf et al.16 determined k3 experimentally (over T = 314 – 433 K) and
theoretically. Their experimental results fit well to an Arrhenius expression that extrapolated to k3 =
0.06 s-1 at 298 K, with 2σ uncertainty of ±30% throughout the entire temperature range. Their
highest measured k3 value was ∼120 s-1 at 433 K, and theoretical k3 agreed with the experiments.
 Our experiments provide an opportunity to validate the measurements of Assaf et al. and extend
them to higher temperatures. We fit the decay times of RO2 and rise times of HPMTF to
exponential functions at 430 – 510 K, resulting in k4 varying from 120 to 1430 s-1, independent of
the initial Cl atom concentrations. We could not get reliable fits at 530 K, because k3 became too

2.4. Conclusion
 We reported the time-resolved photoionization mass spectrometry probing of Cl-initiated
oxidation of DMS at P = 800 Torr and T = 400 – 545 K, under a broad range of oxygen
concentrations, ranging from 7∙1015 to 7∙1018 cm-3. In addition to closed-shell products, we detected
the peroxy radical, CH3SCH2O2 (RO2) Under low-O2 conditions, we detected 13 oxygenated and
chlorinated products, which suggest participation from the dissociation of QOOH (R6), self- and
cross-reactions of radicals (Cl, R, RO2, and the Cl—DMS adduct), and reactions of radicals with O2.
We detected no HPMTF under our low-O2 conditions, indicating that the QOOH + O2 reaction
(R4) was too slow to compete with R6.
 At high O2 concentrations the product distribution was significantly simpler. Aside from RO2,
the major species we detected were HPMTF, CH2O, H2S, and ClSCHO. Using a global time-
resolved C and S atom balance analysis, we determined the absolute concentrations of RO2 and the
product species and derived the photoionization cross-sections of RO2, HPMTF, H2S, and
ClSCHO. The Cl + DMS branching ratio was ∼70%:30% in favor of H abstraction (R1a), and
correspondingly the HPMTF yield was constant at ∼70% of the DMS consumed, regardless of
temperature or initial Cl concentration. The other products appear to be formed in a parallel
competing pathway to the R + O2 reaction, most likely through the Cl—DMS adduct.
 The rate coefficient for the important atmospheric reaction k3, which is rate-limiting for HPMTF
formation, varied between 120 and 1430 s-1 at 430 – 510 K. These values of k3 matched exactly the
trend, discovered by Assaf et al.16 at lower temperatures, and strengthened their extrapolation to
room temperature, 0.06±0.014 s-1.
 Our experiments characterized many of the detected transient chemical species which are not
commercially available (including CH2SCHO, ClSCHO, CH3SSCHO, RO2, and HPMTF) by mass
spectrometry, including ion fragmentation patterns. Furthermore, our determination of the absolute
PICS of RO2 and HPMTF enables future quantitative detection of these key intermediates of DMS
oxidation in other experimental setups, e.g., atmospheric chambers.

3. DEVELOPMENT OF AN ONLINE SOURCE OF HPMTF
  This section describes the development of a continuous source of HPMTF for further
downstream analysis, using a new continuous photolysis reactor for online synthesis of HPMTF.
The application of this reactor is detailed in Section 4.

3.1. Synthesis of chemical intermediates using continuous UV photolysis
 Section 2 of this report describes time-resolved experiments, in which we produced HPMTF
starting by pulsed 248 nm laser photolysis of DMS/OxCl/O2/N2 mixtures. The laser pulses
irradiated the gas mixture at repetition rates of 5 – 10 Hz with typical fluences of 1 – 10 mJ/cm2,
translating to 1.25×1015 – 1.25×1016 photons∙cm-2. These laser pulse energies were chosen to
produce initial Cl atom number densities in the 3×1011 - 1×1013 cm-3 range. Pulsed laser initiation is
required for such experiments, because the laser starts the reactions at a precise time (within the 8 ns
duration of the pulse), allowing the time evolution of intermediates and products to be observed
directly, with sub-millisecond resolution, using our 32 kHz pulsed time-of-flight mass spectrometer.
Another benefit of the pulsed laser initiation is the ability to produce high levels of initial radicals,
resulting in high number densities of products and strong experimental signals.
 However, pulsed laser initiation also has drawbacks. Most importantly, high radical number
densities can result in significant radical-radical reactions, since the rates of these reactions are
proportional to the concentrations of both reactant radicals and thus scale quadratically with the
photolysis laser flux. In the present case, we varied the laser pulse energies and consequently the
initial radical concentrations by up to factors of ~30. The signal intensities of many secondary
products (e.g., CH3SSCH3 and CH3SCH2Cl) scaled approximately as the square of the laser energy.
This is consistent with our Scheme 2, i.e. we propose CH3SSCH3 to be the product of CH3S radical
self-reaction, and CH3SCH2Cl to result from the CH3SCH2 + Cl radical-radical reaction.
 Unlike the radical-radical reactions, the main pathway of interest here, leading from DMS to
HPMTF, involves only the reactions of initial radicals with O2 and unimolecular isomerizations of
the intermediate species. Because O2 is present in large excess, the rate of these reactions scales
linearly with the radical concentrations, and, consequently, with the photolysis flux. As a result,
higher photolysis energies increase the rates of radical-radical reactions more than those of radical +
O2 reactions and therefore reduce the product branching ratio to HPMTF.
 We attempted to suppress the unwanted radical-radical side reactions by using continuous UV
photolysis from a 350 – watt Hg/Xe lamp instead of pulsed laser photolysis. The lamp has
broadband, relatively flat emission in the 200 – 2000 nm range, with a prominent, sharp atomic Hg
emission band at 254 nm. Importantly, the lamp produces time-averaged photon fluences that exceed
the pulser excimer laser fluence at UV wavelengths relevant to oxalyl chloride photolysis by a factor
of ~10, but with instantaneous photon flux that is lower by a factor of ~1.25×106. This enables
efficient generation of Cl atoms and conversion of DMS to products, but with low instantaneous
radical concentrations that limit the radical-radical reactions.
 Moreover, continuous generation of HPMTF and other intermediates is suited to further studies
that don’t require high time resolution. For example, in section 4 we report experiments aimed at
measuring the liquid water uptake of HPMTF in a droplet train reactor. There, we measure steady
state concentrations of HPMTF as a function of the total area of droplet surfaces in the reactor,
from which we derive surface sticking coefficients. In this and similar experiments, continuous gas

 31

flows with constant HPMTF concentrations are preferable to time-varying HPMTF sources, which
could be produced by pulsed laser photolysis.

3.2. Construction of the CW photolysis reactor
 The continuous-wave (CW) photolysis reactor is shown in Figure 7. It consists of a 6.35 mm
outer diameter (OD), 4.35 mm inner diameter (ID) quartz tube, held between two perfluoroalkoxy
alkane (PFA) blocks, making up the flow channel in which reactant gas is photolyzed. Premixed
reactant gas enters and exits the flow channel via ports with PFA tube fittings, angled at 45°. A
loosely focused UV beam from the Hg/Xe lamp enters the reactor through a UV Fused Silica
window and propagates through the reactor channel, which has a high reflector mirror on the
opposite end. We use a commercial mirror centered at 264 nm with reflectivity >98% at 254 nm,
which is the main wavelength we use here. The mirror reflects the 254 nm beam back through the
reactor and increases the photolysis yield in our experiments by a factor of 2.

Figure 7. CAD drawing (A), photograph (B), and isometric view (C) of the CW photolysis reactor. LR is the overall
effective reactor length, and LH is the heated length.

 PFA and quartz are chemically inert materials, and their use for all wetted surfaces in the reactor
minimizes wall losses of radicals and avoids reactor degradation from oxalyl chloride and other
corrosive reactants. The reactor length is easily adjusted by selecting appropriate lengths of the flow
tube (marked LR in Figure 7). In the present study, LR was 120 mm. Considering the area of the flow
channel (14.86 mm2), the effective reactor volume was approximately 1.8 cm3. Typical reactor gas

 32

flows were in the 0.5 – 2 standard liters per minute (SLM) range, corresponding to average residence
times of 0.1 – 0.4 s.
 The reactant gas mixture was supplied by several mass flow controllers and was mixed on the fly,
as in the experiments in Section 2. A PFA throttle valve at the outlet maintained constant pressures
in the reactor at constant total gas flows. The maximum working pressure of the setup is 100 psi,
limited by the pressure rating of the PFA fittings, and we used pressures up to 3000 Torr here. The
middle portion of the reactor (marked LH in Figure 7) is wrapped by a resistive heating wire. LH was
set at 90 mm for this work. Temperature was in the heated section was measured by three K-type
thermocouples, positioned with equal spacing along LH. Desired temperature was maintained by a
variable AC current supply.
 After passing through the outlet valve, the gas mixture passed through a 1 m long, 6.35 mm
(OD), 4.57 mm (ID) PFA tube, to which we refer as the sampling line. For the present experiments,
we used the same photoionization mass spectrometer as in Section 2, except with the high-pressure
photolysis reactor replaced by a simple fitting, which allowed the gas from the sampling line to
expand into the mass spectrometer vacuum chamber and intersect the ionizing radiation beam. The
pressure in the sampling line was typically maintained at 10 – 30 Torr, and the residence times
between the CW photolysis reactor and the mass spectrometer were on the order of tens of ms.
 We analyzed the output of the CW reactor using fixed-energy ionizing radiation from discharge
lamps in the laboratory at SNL-CA. For the present experiments, we employed H2 discharge, which
emits broadband VUV photons that peak in intensity at 10.2 eV but stretch to both lower and
higher energies at least up to 15 eV. This enabled us to monitor the ion signals of all major chemical
species in that we identified in Section 2, albeit without quantitative yield information because the
lamp emission spectrum was not known exactly. In addition, we also analyzed the CW reactor
output at the ALS, using tunable VUV ionizing radiation similarly to Section 2. The ALS
experiments allowed us to verify the identity of the detected species via their PI spectra.

3.4. Conclusion
 The tunable VUV photoionization experiments at the ALS confirm that the CW photolysis
reactor efficiently generates HPMTF from DMS oxidation, along with CH2O and a small number of
other, minor, competing products. Meanwhile, although the SNL-CA laboratory experiments do not
allow us to quantify the yield of HPMTF directly, they demonstrate that we can consume up to
6×1013 cm-3 of DMS. Furthermore, based on the trends in Figure 9, we estimate that the maximum
yields of HPMTF that we observed at SNL-CA were greater than 90%, relative to the consumed
DMS concentration.
 We must now consider whether CW or pulsed laser photolysis initiation is a better choice for an
online source of HPMTF or other reactive species, which can be used for further downstream
experiments. Notably, the maximum HPMTF yields we measured in the pulsed laser experiments
(Section 2) were only ~70%, relative to the initial DMS concentration. Furthermore, those
experiments were done with low DMS concentrations and the peak absolute number densities of
HPMTF in the reactor were on the order of 1×1012 cm-3 at total reactor pressures of ~1000 Torr
and T ~ 500K. This represents a mole fraction of ~5×10-8, or 50 parts per billion (ppb). Larger total
yields of HPMTF would require higher Cl radical concentrations, which would form unacceptably
high concentrations of unwanted secondary products.
 In contrast, we can operate the CW photolysis reactor with much higher DMS concentrations
and higher total Cl radical generation. Moreover, the selectivity for HPMTF is higher in the CW
reactor. We have demonstrated final HPMTF concentrations of at least 1.7×1013 cm-3 at the ALS
and we estimate HPMTF concentrations of ~6×1013 cm-3 in the laboratory experiments at total P =
2000 Torr, equivalent to molar fractions of ~1500 ppb. The CW reactor is thus a steady-state
HPMTF source that is a factor off at least ~30 more intense than the pulsed laser reactor. This
advantage can likely be increased further by using higher starting DMS and oxalyl chloride
concentrations. Such high HPMTF output levels are critical to enabling further studies of HPMTF
reactivity, photochemistry, and transport properties, which would not be possible with the low levels
of HPMTF, generated via pulse laser photolysis.

4. MEASUREMENTS OF LIQUID UPTAKE OF HPMTF
 This section details measurements of the water accommodation coefficient of HPMTF, using a
newly constructed droplet reactor. Partitioning of HPMTF from gas phase into aerosols is a major
uncertain atmospheric model parameter and, therefore, accurate knowledge of the gas-liquid surface
interactions of HPMTF is needed. However, precise measurements of liquid uptake coefficients are
challenging for reactive chemical intermediates, such as HPMTF, because these compounds often
cannot be easily synthesized and safely stored ahead of time. Our development of the droplet
reactor leverages the new capabilities described above for online synthesis of HPMTF and
demonstrates a generally applicable new laboratory platform for the study of liquid surface uptake
and reactivity of elusive, yet practically and fundamentally important reactive chemical species.

4.1. Accommodation coefficient measurements in a droplet reactor
 Liquid uptake of gas-phase molecules is a complex heterogeneous process, which begins with a
molecule striking a liquid surface and either scattering back into the gas or sticking to the surface.
Initial adsorption is followed by desorption back into the gas, surface diffusion (lateral movement
on the surface), chemical reactions on the surface, or transport into the bulk. Once in the bulk
liquid, the molecule can undergo chemical reactions (which may differ from surface reactions),
diffuse in the bulk without reacting, or transport back to the surface. The concentration of gaseous
molecules in the presence of liquid surfaces is governed by the balance of the above processes, and
the net uptake of molecules into the liquid depends on the conditions, e.g. on the surface or bulk
saturation, rate of liquid or bulk reactions, temperature, rate of gas-phase or liquid diffusion, and
many other factors.
 The multi-phase dynamics of liquid surface uptake, including the mathematical formulation of
the governing equations, were reviewed comprehensively by Davidovits et al.59 A key quantity of
interest is the mass accommodation coefficient, α, which is defined as the probability of a molecule
entering the liquid for each surface encounter. Unlike the net uptake coefficient, α depends only on
the ratio of the adsorption and desorption rate coefficients. Consequently, α is sensitive to the
molecule-surface free energy profile (a function only of the molecule and surface structure), but not
sensitive to the Henry’s law coefficient or reaction rate coefficients (that are also functions of the
bulk liquid composition). Therefore, α is an “elementary” parameter that can be directly predicted
theoretically and is a key component of modeling the overall heterogeneous chemistry of molecules
in the presence of liquid surfaces.
 In laboratory measurements, the net surface uptake of molecules can approach the limiting value,
defined by α, if the effects of gas-phase transport and surface and bulk saturation can be minimized.
Practically, this requires interaction with pristine surfaces in a reactor where wall and gas-phase
losses are known, and gas-phase diffusion is rapid. One reliable experimental method for this is a
droplet reactor,59 in which a train of droplets with tightly controlled frequency, velocity, and size is
propagated down the center of a flow tube. The total surface area of the droplets is typically known
to within a few percent. Gas-phase molecules flow through the reactor with varying total path
lengths, and their concentrations are measured as a function of the gas-liquid interaction (i.e., droplet
area and reactor length). Droplet trains operate at low gas pressures and low droplet loading (i.e. low
accumulation of molecules in the droplets, which helps reduce the effects of gas diffusion and
saturation. This approach has been used to measure the mass accommodation of numerous small
alcohols, acids, and atmospherically important inorganic trace species.59 However, until now it has
not been applied to transient reaction intermediates.

4.2. Construction of the Sandia droplet train reactor

4.2.1. Physical setup and schematic drawings
 As part of this project, we constructed a new droplet train reactor, coupled to a photoionization
mass spectrometer for detection. The droplet reactor input is interfaced with the output of the CW
photolysis reactor, so that the liquid uptake of transient chemical species (in this case, formed via Cl-
initiated DMS oxidation) can be tested.
 The droplet reactor is shown schematically in Figure 11. It consists of a 30 mm OD, 27 mm ID
quartz tube, positioned between top and bottom aluminum blocks. For the present work, the tube
length (LR) was 12.0 inches; however, other tube lengths can be cut if a different reactor length is
desired. The top block contains a piezo-drive liquid drop dispenser (MicroFab Technologies, MJ-
ATP-01 series) on top, two opposing UV Fused silica windows with 20 mm clear apertures on the
sides, and a port for a ¼-inch gas line leading to a pump in the front. The bottom block contains
identical opposing windows and a ¼ gas line port (used as inlet), along with a KF-25 Kwik-flange
style connector on the bottom leading to a liquid catcher vessel. The top and bottom blocks have
mounting hardware on the back, allowing for secure placement on a breadboard. The tube, ports,
and windows are sealed by elastomer O-rings, enabling base reactor pressures below 0.01 Torr.

Figure 11. Schematic drawing (side view) and photograph (front view) of the droplet train reactor. LR and LU are the
total reactor length and the length of the uptake measurement regions, respectively. Insets A and B show photographs of
water droplets, taken through the top and bottom block windows, respectively.

 40

 For the present work, we used droplet dispensers with 40- and 70-μm nozzle diameters, which
formed extremely stable monodisperse droplet trains, directed downward. Distilled water was
supplied from a 1L reservoir, in which we maintained constant headspace pressures in the 750 –
3000 Torr range using house N2 line with pressure and flow control. The dispenser was actuated by
a piezo crystal with maximum rated frequency of 30 kHz, driven by a sine wave AC power supply
with maximum peak-to peak voltage of 30 V. Droplet frequency, size, and velocity were controlled
by the pressure in the reservoir headspace, orifice diameter, and piezo frequency, as described in
detail below.
 The droplets propagated down the centerline of the reactor into a water catcher, which was
emptied periodically. The droplets were imaged through the windows in the top and bottom blocks
using two 1.6-megapixel CMOS cameras (Thorlabs, Zelux), illuminated by a pulsed high-power 625-
nm LED. The LED output was split into approximately equal intensity beams with a 50%
beamsplitter cube and directed towards cameras. The LED was driven by a pulsed driver (Thorlabs,
DC2200) with minimum pulse widths of 1 μs, which was synchronized to the piezo driver. Such
short pulse widths were critical for sharp imaging of the fast-moving droplets, as described in detail
below. We used the camera images for active monitoring of the droplet stability and daily calibration
of the droplet size and velocity.
 The test gas, produced by the CW reactor, was mixed on the fly (if needed) with up to 5
additional gas flows, and entered the droplet reactor through the inlet port in the bottom cube.
Typical inlet flows were 0.4 – 1 SLM. Part of the gas flow (typically 0.1 – 0.3 SLM) was withdrawn
from the reactor through pump port 2, located below the inlet, and the remaining glow propagated
upward. There, another portion of the flow (0.1 – 0.3 SLM) was withdrawn through pump port 1.
Both pump port flows were maintained by mass flow controllers (MKS instruments).
 The remaining gas flow (typically 0.2 – 0.4 SLM) was sampled out of the reactor through a
movable inlet, which could be positioned anywhere along the length of the reactor. The sampled gas
propagated to the mass spectrometer via a ¼-inch OD PFA line. In the mass spectrometer,
approximately 0.003 – 0.005 SLM of the sampled gas was admitted into the vacuum chamber, with
the remainder exiting to a pump. A throttle valve after the mass spectrometer controlled the
pressure in the sampling line and the droplet reactor, which we usually set at 40 – 50 Torr. The
position of the sampling inlet, relative to the entrance of the test gas, determined the total length
over which the sampled test gas interacted with liquid droplets (LU, 0 – 300 mm in the present
study). In this way, the test molecules encountered a known number of liquid droplets of known
size. The mass accommodation coefficient was determined from the measured ion signal intensity
variation as a function of effective reactor length, LU, which is proportional to the total droplet
surface area in the gas-liquid interaction region.

4.2.2. Calibration measurements
 A key component of uncertainty associated with the liquid uptake measurements in the droplet
reactor comes from the total liquid surface area of the droplets. Because of this, we monitored the
stability of the droplet trains in real time using the CMOS cameras, and we independently calibrated
the droplet number and size in the reactor. The theory of jet formation is summarized in the
Appendix (Section B1). To validate the expected droplet parameters, we measured the liquid flow
rate through the dispenser orifice, as well as the droplet size and velocity.

4.2.2.1. Liquid flow through the dispenser orifice
 To predict the total volumetric flow rate of water out of the droplet dispenser, we use Equation
B7 (see the Appendix). However, the pressure dependent discharge coefficient (𝐶𝐶𝑑𝑑 , see Appendix)
must first be measured. This was done by dispensing the water continuously (i.e. without generating
droplets) into a graduated cylinder and noting the change of the water volume with time as a
function of pressure in the water reservoir. In Figure 12, we show the measured discharge
coefficient as a function of the differential pressure for the 70 µm diameter dispenser, with the
reactor held either at 750 Torr or 40 Torr of air. At about 1 bar differential pressure and above, the
discharge coefficient in both, 750 Torr and 40 Torr conditions converges to 0.8. However, notable
differences are apparent below 1 bar differential pressure between the two conditions. This result
indicates that the volumetric flow of water can be predicted accurately, irrespective of reactor
pressure, using 𝐶𝐶𝑑𝑑 = 0.8, as long as the differential pressure of the water dispenser exceeds 1 bar.
However, if differential pressures below 1 bar are used, the water jet flow must be calibrated
separately for each reactor pressure.

4.2.2.2. Droplet imaging
 With known volumetric water flow from the dispensing orifice, we can compute the expected
droplet size, velocity, number and total area (per cm travel in the reactor), as well as the optimal
piezo driving frequency, using Equations B1 – B5 (see Appendix). In order to validate these
calculations, we imaged the droplets at the exit of the dispenser orifice and after transit through the
reactor, using CMOS camera #1 and #2, respectively. We illuminated the droplets using 1 μs long
LED pulses, synchronized to the piezo driver. The camera exposure length was 40 μs, and the
droplet frequency was typically 25 – 30 kHz (i.e. 33 – 40 μs between LED pulses), meaning that
typically each camera frame captured a single image of the droplet train. Representative photographs
of the central portion of camera images are shown as insets A and B in Figure 11.
 The camera optics of both cameras were selected to provide a magnification factor of 1.60±0.01,
resulting in spatial resolution of 2.16 μm/per pixel at the focal plane. To determine the droplet size,
we analyzed the camera images and measured the width of the droplet horizontal and vertical
profiles; to determine the droplet velocity, we measured the distance between adjacent droplets.
Typical droplet velocities in the reactor were ~10 m/s when operating with 750 Torr differential
pressure across the dispenser orifice. This means that the droplets traveled vertically approximately
10 μs (~4.6 pixels) during each LED illuminating pulse, smearing the imaged droplet profile. As a
result, the vertical droplet profiles were typically longer by 4 – 5 pixels than the horizontal profiles.

4.3.2. The mass accommodation coefficient of HPMTF
 Lastly, we measured the liquid uptake and determined the mass accommodation coefficient of
HPMTF. We generated HPMTF in the CW photolysis reactor, as described in Section 3.3. The
measured depletion of the HPMTF ion signal at m/z = 80 is plotted as a function of the gas-liquid
interaction length in Figure 15. From these data, we determined the mass accommodation
coefficient, αHPMTF = 0.018 ± 0.009 (1σ), at T = 290 K. The large error bars are due to the statistical
uncertainty of the measurement and the systematic uncertainty of the main correction factors
(diffusion and surface saturation). These correction factors must be estimated from gas and liquid
diffusion coefficients and the Henry’s law constant, which have never been measured for HPMTF
and are therefore highly uncertain.
 To our knowledge, our measurement of αHPMTF is unique. There are no direct measurements of
the mass accommodation coefficient for HPMTF or any other structurally related compounds (e.g.,
those that contain carbonyl and OOH groups. Similarly, no theoretical calculations of HPMTF
uptake on water or other liquids are available. Therefore, no comparison with prior measurements

or theory predictions can currently be made. However, we hope that the present development of the
droplet reactor, coupled to an online source of reactive molecules, can open the door to such
experimental measurements in the future.

4.4. Conclusion and outlook
 The experimental measurements we report here are the first of their kind – i.e. the first direct
determination of a mass accommodation coefficient of an elusive chemical intermediate under
controlled laboratory conditions. Our value of αHPMTF is a preliminary result at a single temperature
point. Further work should be conducted to confirm it and to elucidate its temperature dependence.
There is also clear practical need to measure the mass accommodation on surfaces with more direct
relevance to the troposphere, such as sea salt, sulfate, and aged organic aerosols. If successful, these
measurements will provide valuable inputs to the atmospheric chemical models and will enable an
accurate assessment of the impact of DMS oxidation chemistry on the global sulfur cycle.
 From a fundamental point of view, temperature dependent experiments can probe the free
energy of accommodation on liquid surfaces. Experimental values of the entropy and enthalpy
change can be directly compared to predictions from high-level theory and test the fidelity of
quantum chemical calculations and molecular dynamics simulations of surface uptake.
Measurements of the mass accommodation of transient, reactive compounds significantly broaden
the range of gas-liquid surface interactions that can be studied. Therefore, we view the development
of the droplet reactor, coupled to an online photolysis source of reactive intermediates, as an
important milestone on the way to a detailed exploration of the transport and reactivity at complex
gas-liquid interfaces.

A.1. Experimental conditions
 Experimental set 1 was used to test the oxygen dependence of reaction products and intermediates
over a three order of magnitude range of O2 concentrations at constant temperatures, 400 – 545 K.
 Experimental set 2 was aimed at distinguishing radical-radical reaction products (which would
depend in a non-linear fashion on initial radical concentration) from radical + O2 reactions (which
would depend linearly on radical concentrations). To do this, we varied the oxalyl chloride
concentration and photolysis laser pulse energy to produce an order of magnitude variation in [Cl]t=0
at T = 475 K and at two representative [O2] concentrations.
 The goal of Experimental set 3 was to collect photoionization spectra for low-oxygen and high-
oxygen reaction products and intermediates for species identification.
 Experimental set 4 aimed to quantify HPMTF using a global C atom and S atom balance fit (see
below). To reduce the chemical complexity and eliminate as many competing side reactions as
possible, we used high O2 concentrations and low initial radical concentrations. We also performed
experiments with and without CO added, under otherwise identical conditions. CO addition
scavenged OH radicals, eliminating secondary consumption of DMS by OH and enabling us to
distinguish the initial, Cl-initiated, reactions from secondary, OH-initiated, reactions.

A.5. Systematic fragment ion search
 We developed a method to systematically explore temporal correlations among transient ion
signals, which helps group parent and fragment ions from the ionization of the same species. The
time evolution of all DI signals must mirror that of the parent cation, although their yield (relative
intensity) may vary, depending on the initial internal energy of the parent cation. To search for
temporal correlations, our automated script plotted the point-wise signals of all detected ion peaks
(with known DI contributions of CH3SCH3, CH3S(O)CH3, CH3SSCH3, and CH3SCH2Cl subtracted)
against each other in pairs. We focused on the time profiles taken at different [O2], but at the same
VUV energy and the same temperature, 475 K, where DI yields should be identical. We performed
linear fits of the resulting plots and evaluated the slopes and R2 values. R2 measures temporal
correlation among plotted signals, and the slope is proportional to the ion yield ratio.
 Figure A34 shows an example of the m/z = 46, 47, and 75 ion signals, plotted point-by-point
against the m/z = 80 ion signals at different [O2]. At the lowest [O2] all the signals are small and
consequently poorly correlated with each other. However, at [O2] ≥ 7×1016 cm-3 all signals are
substantial and show high correlation, suggesting that they arise from a common carrier that
increases in yield at high [O2].

and [O2] = 4×1015 – 4×1018 cm-3. DI contributions were subtracted from m/z = 46 and 47 signals.

A.6. Global atom balance analysis details
 Our experiments measure ion signals of species i, which depend on the photoionization energy E
time after photolysis: 𝑆𝑆𝑖𝑖 (𝐸𝐸, 𝑡𝑡). The intensity if ion signals is given by 𝑆𝑆𝑖𝑖 (𝐸𝐸, 𝑡𝑡) = 𝑁𝑁𝑖𝑖 (𝑡𝑡) ∙ 𝜎𝜎𝑖𝑖 (𝐸𝐸) ∙ Λ 𝑖𝑖 ,
where 𝑁𝑁𝑖𝑖 (𝑡𝑡) is the time-dependent concentration of i in the reactor, 𝜎𝜎𝑖𝑖 (𝐸𝐸) is the PICS of i, and Λ 𝑖𝑖 is
the mass-dependent instrument sensitivity factor, which we measure using calibration mixtures of
gases with known 𝑁𝑁𝑖𝑖 and 𝜎𝜎𝑖𝑖 (𝐸𝐸). The ion time traces are related to the absolute concentrations of
their source chemical species in the reactor by a constant factor. For species with known PICS, this
factor is known, and their concentrations are readily determined by multiplying the ion time traces
by 𝜎𝜎𝑖𝑖 (𝐸𝐸) ∙ Λ𝑖𝑖 . For species without known PICS, the scaling factor must be determined
experimentally. Our global atom balance fitting method accomplishes this, as long as we detect all
relevant chemical species.
 Our reactants are Cl, O2, and DMS. The main products and intermediates we detected at high
[O2] are CH2O, H2S, RO2, ClSCHO, and HPMTF. Minor products were CH3SCHO and CH3SCl.
We do not detect Cl atoms, O2, HCl (from initial H abstraction), or OH, since doing so would
require using high ionization energies and give unacceptably high total ion count rates. Therefore,
we cannot track the H, O, or Cl atom balance. However, we do detect all C- and S-containing
species, allowing us to track the C and S atom balance. We perform a combined least-squares
optimization on all ion time traces in dataset 4 (at 10.95 eV, see section S1), which are multiplied by
the number of C and S atoms (for the C and S balance, respectively) in each neutral species. We
adjust the scaling factors for each atom-weighted ion time trace without known PICS, until the total
number of C and S atoms in the transient intermediates and products equals the number of
respective atoms consumed by the depletion of DMS at all kinetic times t and at all reactor
conditions at once. The details of the fit are given in Table A12.
