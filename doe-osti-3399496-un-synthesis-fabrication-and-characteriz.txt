 ABSTRACT
 Uranium nitride and carbide-based fuels are proposed for use in advanced LEU
fuel systems. This report details recent development work to study synthesis,
sintering and fabrication methods for single phase UN in monolithic geometries.
These samples were intended to support a variety of irradiation testing conditions
by tailoring enrichment, geometry, and compositional requirements. Two methods
were under evaluation for synthesizing uranium nitride powder – carbothermic
reduction-nitridation (CTR-N) of UO2 powder feedstock and hydride-
dehydride/nitridation (HDN) of uranium metal feedstock. Pellets were fabricated
from powder feedstocks via conventional pressing and sintering methods. As-
fabricated feedstocks and pellets were evaluated via X-ray diffraction (XRD) for
phase purity and sintered materials were characterized for density. Both the
CTR-N and the HDN methods were successfully demonstrated as an effective
process for fabricating phase pure UN. Additionally, results from machining
efforts for UN are detailed. This report also contains information on a study trying
to elucidate the oxidation mechanisms of sintered uranium carbide samples
fabricated from feedstock synthesized via an arc-melting method.

 1. INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND
 Uranium nitride (UN) and uranium carbide (UC) continue to remain of interest for use in high
temperature and advanced microreactor concepts because of their high uranium density, high melting
temperatures, and high thermal conductivity as compared to the benchmark UO2 fuel [1-5]. Predicting the
performance of UN and UC fuels is essential in order to progress them towards fuel qualification for
commercial purposes. Much of the irradiation data for UN was generated as part of the SP-100 program or
bounded by high temperature tests for lead fast reactors [1, 6-10], which involve peak inner clad
temperatures (PICT) in excess of 600°C [11]. The Fission-Accelerated Steady-state Test (FAST) in the
Advanced Test Reactor (ATR) at Idaho National Laboratory (INL) was planned under the Advanced Low-
Enriched Uranium Program (ALEU) to evaluate irradiation performance at a PICT of 300-650ºC for UC
and 300-400ºC for UN. Concurrent tests were planned at Oak Ridge National Laboratory’s (ORNL) High
Flux Isotope Reactor (HIFR) and the Argonne Tandem Linac Accelerator System (ATLAS) at Argonne
National Laboratory (ANL) to utilize full testing capabilities to analyze irradiation performance of
monolithic UC and UN fuels in the desired temperature range.
 Fuel specimens for HFIR irradiations were designed to utilize the MiniFuel irradiation vehicle that
allows for rapid experimentation and utilizes similar geometry required for testing in ATLAS. The ability
to fabricate UC and UN of similar geometry was previously demonstrated at Boise State University (BSU)
[12], and as these tests permitted the use of depleted uranium, BSU was selected to fabricate the MiniFuel-
type specimen. The specimen for the FAST experiment needed tailored enrichment and dimensional
specifications that required capabilities for handling and machining at INL to fabricate.
 The fabrication of the UC specimens was previously reported in INL/RPT-23-75699 [13]. However,
significant variations in post-fabrication oxidation behavior were observed in the INL and the commercially
fabricated specimens. Therefore, the mechanism of the oxidation behavior in UC fuels was investigated
prior to irradiation. Fabrication methods for the UN samples were selected based on ease of handling and
synthesizing feedstock materials. The carbothermic reduction-nitridation (CTR-N) method was utilized at
BSU due to reduce risk of handling UO2 powder feedstocks over U metal, and availability of depleted UO2
powder feedstocks. The hydride-dehydride-nitride (HDN) method was utilized at INL to develop a process
for fabricating FAST-type specimens as this method utilizes a metal U feedstock that can be tailored to a
homogenous enrichment. Significant challenges were faced in the development of FAST-type UN
specimens, including multi-programmatic use and competing demands on the sintering furnace, failure of
interior furnace components, and deterioration of the glovebox atmosphere control system. Additionally,
the depleted UN work at BSU was also hindered by attrition of the students with knowledge of the UN
synthesis process.
 This report provides the concluding work for the aLEU programmatic efforts at INL and BSU for UN
synthesized via CTR-N and HDN, as well as refinement efforts towards sintering and machining of UN.
The report summarizes the follow-on work to that documented in INL/RPT-23-75699 [13] and work
completed to date through the termination of programmatic funding in early 2025. Efforts to investigate the
mechanism behind the room temperature oxidation behavior of UC samples, which had previously been
intended for ATR irradiation but were ultimately too degraded for use in assembly, are also included here.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL AND RESULTS
 2.1. UN Synthesis and Fabrication at Boise State University
2.1.1. UN Synthesis via CTR-N
 Uranium mononitride powder was synthesized from UO2 and carbon feedstock powders through
carbothermic reduction and subsequent nitriding within a high temperature tube furnace. The starting
reagents were depleted uranium dioxide powder (UO2) supplied by the Idaho National Laboratory, which
was mixed with spherical glassy carbon in a 2.7 UO2:C molar ratio for feedstock used to make MiniFuel
specimens and a 2.5 UO2:C molar ratio for pellet feedstock. The UO2 + C powders were combined with
yttria stabilized zirconia milling media in a zirconia lined milling vessel in an inert atmosphere glovebox
and milled for 30 minutes at 150 RPM. After ball milling the UO2 + C mixture was retrieved from the ball
mill vessel in an argon glovebox, loaded into a tungsten crucible and transferred into a high temperature
tube furnace. The furnace underwent a series of five vacuum purge cycles down to 6 x 10-3 torr before
starting the furnace profile. During the carbothermic reduction of UO2 to UN the process environment
within the tube furnace was varied from vacuum to 6% H2 in N2, and then ultra-high purity argon during
cooling to reduce the formation of the sesquinitride. The synthesis profile is shown in Figure 1 and images
of the mixed powders and final UN product are seen in Figure 2.

 2.2. UN Synthesis and Fabrication at Idaho National Laboratory
2.2.1. UN Synthesis via HDN
 Initial demonstration of UN synthesis via HDN at INL was completed in August 2023 as described in
INL/RPT-23-75699 [13], using the profile shown in seen in Figure 10. As part of the development efforts
for the fuel fabrication for the UN-FAST experiments, two additional synthesis runs were completed in the
high-temperature refractory metal furnace in order to reduce the time required for synthesis. In the first
attempt in December 2023, the hydride-dehydride portion of the synthesis was shorted by increasing the
ramp time for those segments to 25°C/min, up from 5°C/min. The second refinement attempt for the
synthesis process was completed in March 2025 and involved accelerating the final cooldown step by
allowing the furnace to cool naturally instead of at 10°C/min. As can be seen in the XRD patterns in Figure
11, the powder synthesized 12/23 appears to be phase pure UN (the peak at approximately 26° 2ϴ is an
artifact from the sample stage used to analyze the sample). The 12/23 powder was analyzed again in
November 2024 to assess its suitability for pellet sintering, and the pattern suggests the powder had slightly
oxidized, however it was deemed suitable for a sintering test run. The powder sample synthesized in 3/25
however, indicated significant oxygen contamination, showing a distinct UO2 phase. The program funding
was terminated in early spring 2025, thus no further synthesis attempts were possible. It is recommended
that the synthesis profile not be shortened beyond the hydride-dehydride portions and follows the profile
used for the UN synthesis completed in August 2023.

2.2.2. UN Fabrication and Sintering
 Two UN samples were sintered in October 2023 from the powder synthesized in August 2023, one full
sized pellet and one disk specimen intended for characterization use. The sintering profile used a standard
sintering cycle having a 5-hour dwell at 1900°C in Ar/100 ppm N2. The pellet and disk specimens had
geometric density measurements of 92% and 90% theoretical density (theoretical density for UN is 14.33
g/cm3), respectively. The disk specimen was prepared and analyzed via scanning electron microscopy
(SEM). Significant porosity was observed in the sample from the backscattered electron (BSE) images
(Figure 12). The average grain size was estimated at approximately 13 µm.

 Figure 12: BSE images of 10192023-2 UN Pellet polished surface (1um).

 9

 Two additional pellets were sintered in November 2024 using the powder synthesized in December
2023. As stated above, that powder had oxidized slightly since synthesizing. Due to an issue with the gas
bottles after the samples were loaded to the furnace, the samples sat under a slight vacuum in the furnace
for 5 days. The sintering profile was modified to increase the dwell time at maximum temperature to 7
hours in an attempt to increase sintered density and reduce the porosity. The density of those two pellets,
as determined by Archimedes’ density in ethanol, was only 90% TD. To assess whether the lowered density
was due to the samples sitting under vacuum for several days or the age of the UN powder, another pellet
was sintered a week later under the same sintering conditions. The pellet density was approximately 91%
TD, and it was deemed that a combination of the two issues was the cause of the decreased density. It is
recommended that the thermal profile used follow the prescribed method as reported here, using a minimum
of a 5-hour dwell at the maximum temperature. Additionally, due to the extremely compressed development
schedule, the synthesized powder had not been subjected to a comprehensive optimization protocol. This
factor was later identified as a critical variable. Particle size analysis was conducted via laser diffraction on
the as-synthesized powder used to make the pellets. Five runs for three samplings of the powder were
averaged. The analysis indicated the powder has a tri-modal distribution and an undesirable large particle
size with the majority larger than 300 μm (Figure 13). To increase sintered density, it would be prudent to
incorporate as-synthesized powder conditioning, which could include ball-milling to a desired particle size
distribution, combined with a granulation step, prior to pellet fabrication.

Figure 13: Particle size analysis on the as-synthesized UN powder used for pellet sintering as determined
via laser diffraction.

2.2.3. UN Pellet Machining
 As part of the UN development for the FAST tests, it was necessary to test the machining capabilities
for the UN material. These tests were to ensure that sintered pellets could be centerless ground to exact
diameter dimensions as well as to explore the possibility of fabricating annular pellets for future
instrumented irradiation tests. While centerless grinding of a UN pellet to a tolerance of ±0.001” was
demonstrated successfully, challenges exist for annular fabrication of oxygen sensitive, brittle materials,
like UN. Traditional methods for annular ceramic fabrication incorporate slurries, binders, or additives,
which would introduce undesirable impurities to the fuel form. INL machinists incorporated the use of a

 10

HAAS Mini-mill, equipped with a diamond-tip carbide jobber drill (0.078”) and the use of a non-
chlorinated cutting oil to test annulus drilling on test UN specimens. These samples were UN pellets
sintered in the high-density fuel furnace in 2021 as part of another program. The drilled pellets (seen in
Figure 14) had a nominal outer diameter of 0.288” and an annular inner diameter of 0.077”. Further
refinements were planned to improve reproducibility, concentricity, and to explore various other OD/ID
ratios, as well as the ability to centerless grind.

 Figure 14: Images of the UN pellet used for testing annular drilling capabilities.

Centerless grinding was completed on one sintered UN sample. It was established that the pellet was not
difficult to grind, however, process optimization was recommended to improve on instrument parameters
and sample concentricity.
 2.3. UC Oxidation Investigation
 Fabrication of UC pellets for the FAST test was reported in INL/RPT-23-75699 [13]. However, prior
to test assembly, it was observed that these sintered pellets as well as pellets fabricated under other
programs, being dense (>90%TD), phase pure UC, had varying degrees of surface oxidation. Furthermore,
the oxidation resulted in varying degrees of pellet degradation, including the passive pulverization of
sintered pellets while other pellets remained pristine. The degradation was not unique to the storage
environments or batch. The pellets in Figure 15 were strictly stored in an inert (Ar) atmosphere glovebox,
where the atmosphere was kept below 10 ppm O2, fabricated from the same arc melted ingot and sintered
during the same cycle. The secondary electron SEM images seen in Figure 16 highlight the surface
difference of the pellets circled in Figure 15. Even though numerous theories were considered, three theories
stood out as likely mechanisms for the room temperature oxidation and degradation of the UC pellets: minor
carbide phases, UC stoichiometry, and grain boundary angle.

 11

Figure 15: UC pellets from same sintering cycle showing varying degrees of surface oxidation. A) Pellets
 immediately after sintering, B) pellets 5 months after sintering. The pellets circled in red were analyzed.

  Figure 16: (Left) Secondary electron images of heavily oxidized sample, and (Right) lightly oxidized
 sample.

 While the presence of a minor carbide phase may impact the oxidation behavior, no minor carbide
phases were observed by XRD, but this theory has not been ruled out as a possible mechanism.
Thermodynamically, the minor carbide phases, specifically UC2 and U2C3, are less stable than UC, thus
preferentially oxidizing. The C/U ratio present in the UC samples was not studied during this project. A
lack of carbon in the UC structure (i.e. UC1-x) may impact the oxidation behavior of UC, but further study
is needed to understand the impacts of the U/C ratio on sample oxidation and degradation.

 It has been observed in literature that the presence of high angle grain boundaries in stainless steel
decreased the corrosion resistance of the material [14]. The energy associated with high angle grain
boundaries is significantly higher than that of low angle grain boundaries due in part by the disorder of
atoms along the grain boundary. This higher energy may result in oxygen preferentially diffusing along
high angle grain boundaries, which results in these grain boundaries being ideal nucleation sites for
oxidation to occur. The initial TEM imaging (Figure 17) indicated the presence of both high and low angle
grain boundaries, with an oxide present along the high angle grain boundaries. However, with the use of
4DSTEM imaging (Figure 18), the presence of two grains with a low angle grain boundary in the lamella
was revealed. What had appeared to be high angle grain boundaries initially were, in fact, cracks, which
will oxidize preferentially over low angle grain boundaries. While the lack of observed oxide along the low
angle grain boundary supports the grain boundary theory, it does not definitively prove or disprove this

theory. Further study is needed to better understand the impacts of grain boundary angles on the oxidation
and degradation of UC.

  In addition to the observation of grain and phase orientation in Figure 18, it was found that oxygen
preferentially attacked cracks in the lamella; it is unknown when the cracks and pores formed and oxidized
— before, during, or after the FIB process. Evidence of internal strain is also visible in the 4DSTEM image,
but further analysis is needed to determine what role internal strain plays in the oxidation behavior of the
material.

 Finally, to gain a fundamental understanding of the experimental observations and the impacts of
grain boundary angles, Density Functional Theory (DFT) was utilized to predict the oxygen diffusion along
grain boundaries. Initial calculations indicated that high angle grain boundaries are more energetically
favorable for oxygen diffusion. Further analysis and modeling is required to confirm the energetic
favorability of high angle grain boundaries and to support experimental work.

 3. SUMMARY
 The work summarized here demonstrated successful UN feedstock synthesis (via two different
techniques, CTR-N and HDN), and fabrication of UN samples of varying geometries, as well as machining
capabilities for this highly sought after fuel form. Establishing UN synthesis and fabrication proficiency at
MFC highlights the expertise of the ceramic fuel personnel at INL and expands the catalog of fuel forms
available to our customers, whether that be industry, academia, or other national laboratories.
 Although program termination did not allow for the completion of the fuel fabrication for the UN-FAST
capsule assembly, the development work reported here has provided a solid basis for future UN synthesis
and fabrication at MFC. As demonstrated with the use of UN pellets from another program being used for
the UN machining work here and as observed by the authors in other instances for various other
programmatic work, UN samples remain highly sought after for experimental research and development.
The samples already fabricated, both at MFC and BSU, will remain in inert storage in EFF for future
experimental use. Any future development work should include incorporating a powder conditioning step
for HDN UN feedstock prior to pellet fabrication to increase sintered density, cycling a CTR-N process
through the high-density fuel furnace to ensure high quality UN feedstock powder can be synthesized using
either synthesis method, and an evaluation of light element (C, O) impurities and properties of samples
produced by each method.
 The initial investigation into the oxidation mechanism of UC compacts synthesized using the arc-melt
method revealed interesting insights regarding the role of grain boundary angles. Future work should
include a systematic study in which mass change over time is recorded for samples synthesized from an
arc-melt method and the carbothermic reduction method, including well-characterized samples in terms of
density, U/C ratio, and starting oxygen impurities.
