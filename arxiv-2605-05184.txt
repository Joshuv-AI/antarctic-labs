Abstract
. We consider transcendental entire functions having doubly parabolic Baker domains, such that the Denjoy-Wolff point of the associated inner function is not a singularity. We describe in a very precise way the dynamics on the boundary from a measure-theoretical point of view. Applications of such results lead to a better understanding of the topology and the dynamics on the boundaries. In particular, we improve some of the results in [ 13 ] for the Baker domain of z + e − z z+e^{-z} . In fact, our conclusions are obtained by applying new results established here on the dynamics of the radial extension of one component doubly parabolic inner functions, which strengthen those of [ 14 ] .



1.
Introduction

Let
f\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be a transcendental entire function and denote by
\left\{f^{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
its iterates, which generate a discrete dynamical system in
\mathbb{C}
. Then, the complex plane is divided into two totally invariant sets: the
Fatou set

\mathcal{F}(f)
, defined to be the set of points
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\left\{f^{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
forms a normal family in some neighbourhood of
z
; and the
Julia set

\mathcal{J}(f)
, its complement. The connected components of the Fatou set are called
Fatou components
.

For entire functions, invariant Fatou components are always simply connected
[
4
]
, so the Riemann map can be used as a uniformization. More precisely, let
U
be a invariant Fatou component of
f
and let
\varphi
be a Riemann map from the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}
onto
U
. Then,

g\colon\mathbb{D}\longrightarrow\mathbb{D},\hskip 28.45274ptg\coloneqq\varphi^{-1}\circ f\circ\varphi

is an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{D}
. The function
g
is called the
associated inner function
. Since
f|_{U}
and
g|_{\mathbb{D}}
are conformally conjugate by
\varphi
, the study of holomorphic self-maps of
\mathbb{D}
is a good approach to analyse the dynamics of
f|_{U}
. Moreover, we can extend the conjugacy by using radial limits for both
g
and
\varphi
, i.e.

g^{*}\coloneqq\lim\limits_{t\to 1^{-}}g(t\xi),\hskip 28.45274pt\varphi^{*}\coloneqq\lim\limits_{t\to 1^{-}}\varphi(t\xi),

for
\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}
. Then,
\varphi^{*}\circ g^{*}(\xi)=f\circ\varphi^{*}(\xi)
for
\lambda
-almost every
\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}

[
15
, Lemma 3.11]
, where
\lambda
denotes the normalized Lebesgue measure on
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

Let us focus on
doubly parabolic Baker domains
, i.e. invariant Fatou components in which iterates converge locally uniformly to
\infty
, and for which the hyperbolic distance between iterates converges to zero. Baker domains are exclusive to transcendental functions, and present richer dynamics (both in the interior and on the boundary), affected by the presence of the essential singularity.

Moreover, if
U
is a doubly parabolic Baker domain, there are infinitely many accesses from
U

[
3
]
(see also
[
6
,
15
]
), which form a natural partition of the boundary
\partial U
, in the following way.

Definition
.

(Target)

Let
f\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be a transcendental entire function, let
U
be a Baker domain, and let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{D}\to U
be a Riemann map. Let
I\subset\partial\mathbb{D}
be a closed arc, delimited by
\xi_{1}\neq\xi_{2}\in\partial\mathbb{D}
such that
\varphi^{*}(\xi_{1})=\varphi^{*}(\xi_{2})=\infty
. We say that
\varphi^{*}(I)\subset\partial U
is a
target
on
\partial U
.

See Figure
1.1
for a visual intuition.

Figure 1.1
.
Schematic representation of targets. Indeed, for the Baker domain
U
of
f(z)=z+e^{-z}
, the figure shows the image of two arcs in the unit circle, each of them bounded by two rays landing at infinity in the dynamical plane.

Hence, given such an invariant Fatou component, it is a natural to examine the distribution of orbits on
\partial U
with respect to a given target.

A first step on this direction are the results of Doering and Mañé
[
11
]
(and also
[
7
, Thms. B and C]
), which give a description of
f|_{\partial U}
from a measure-theoretical point of view. More precisely, if
U
is a doubly parabolic Baker domain,
f|_{\partial U}
is always ergodic with respect to any harmonic measure
\omega_{U}
. In the case when
f|_{\partial U}
has finite degree or the Denjoy-Wolff point of the associated inner function is not a singularity, then
f|_{\partial U}
is also conservative, and thus
\omega_{U}
-almost every orbit is dense on
\partial U
.

In contrast with attracting basins, no harmonic measure supported on
\partial U
is
f
-invariant, so one cannot do a straightforward analysis applying classical results from ergodic theory, such as Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem or Kac’s Lemma (compare with the results given in Appendix
A
).

Nevertheless, there exists an infinite
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu
which is
f
-invariant and absolutely continous with respect to
\omega_{U}

[
11
]
. This measure
\mu
allows us to prove more precise statements on the ergodic properties of
f|_{\partial U}
and the distribution of orbits with respect to targets, although the complexity of the arguments is much greater than in the case where an invariant probability measure exists.

Statement of results

For a Baker domain, the convergence point of the interior orbits is placed on the boundary. In terms of the associated inner function, this means that the
Denjoy-Wolff point
(the point of convergence of the interior orbits) is on the unit circle (and can be assumed to be 1, by precomposing the Riemann map
\varphi\colon\mathbb{D}\to U
with an appropriate rotation). Denote by
\omega_{U}
the harmonic measure on
\partial U
obtained as the push-forward of the Lebesgue measure
\lambda
on
\partial\mathbb{D}
under
\varphi^{*}
.

The
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu
on
\partial U
which is invariant under
f
is defined as follows.
Denote by
\psi\colon\mathbb{H}\to U
a conformal map from the upper-half plane to
U
, such that points in
\mathbb{H}
converge to
\infty
when iterating
h\coloneqq\psi^{-1}\circ f\circ\psi
. Radial limits are defined for
h
and
\psi
(denoted by
h^{*}
and
\psi^{*}
, respectively).
Let
\mu
the push-forward by
\psi^{*}
of the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
, which is
T
-invariant. Thus,
\mu
is
f
-invariant.

We make the following distinction between targets.

Definition
.

(Finite target and infinite target)

Let
U
be a doubly parabolic Baker domain, and let
\psi\colon\mathbb{H}\to U
be as above. Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
be a closed interval, delimited by
x_{1}\neq x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\psi^{*}(x_{1})=\psi^{*}(x_{2})=\infty
.

•

If
I
is bounded on
\mathbb{R}
, we say that
\psi^{*}(I)\subset\partial U
is a
finite target
on
\partial U
.

•

If the complement of
I
is bounded on
\mathbb{R}
, we say that
\psi^{*}(I)\subset\partial U
is a
infinite target
on
\partial U
.

See Figure
1.2
for a visual intuition.
We prove the following.

Theorem A
.

Let
f
be a transcendental entire function, and let
U
be a doubly parabolic Baker domain such that the Denjoy-Wolff point of the associated inner function is not a singularity.

(a)

(Occupation times) Let
E\subset\partial U
be a finite target, and let

S_{n}E(x)=\#\left\{0\leq k\leq n-1:f^{k}(x)\in E\right\}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\circ f^{k}(x).

Then, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
,

\lim\limits_{n}\frac{S_{n}E(x)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}}\to\infty,

for
\omega_{U}
-almost every
x\in\partial U
. In particular,
{S_{n}E}/{\sqrt[3]{n}}\to\infty

\omega_{U}
-almost everywhere.

(b)

(First return times) Let
E\subset\partial U
be a finite target, and let

E_{n}\coloneqq\left\{x\in E\colon\tau_{E}(x)=n\right\},

where
\tau_{E}(x)\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the first return time of
x\in E
to
E
. Then,
\omega_{U}(E_{n})\sim\frac{1}{n\sqrt{n}}
.

(c)

(Waiting times) Let
F\subset\partial U
be an infinite target,
F=\psi^{*}(I)
, and assume the complement of
I
in
\mathbb{R}
is large enough. Let

F_{n}\coloneqq\left\{x\in F\colon f(x),\dots,f^{n-1}(x)\in F,f^{n}(x)\notin F\right\}.

Then,
\omega_{U}(F_{n})\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.

.

Inspired by results of Thaler and Zweimüller
[
24
]
on distributional limit theorems in infinite ergodic theory, we prove the following finer results for Baker domains of finite degree (in fact, the assumption of finite degree can be weakened – see Section
5.2
).

Theorem B
.

Let
f
be a transcendental entire function, and let
U
be a doubly parabolic Baker domain of finite degree.

(a)

(Darling-Kac theorem) Let
E\subset\partial U
be a finite target on
\partial U
, and let

S_{n}E(x)=\#\left\{0\leq k\leq n-1:f^{k}(x)\in E\right\}.

Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\omega_{U}\left(\left\{\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2n}}S_{n}E\leq\mu(E)t\right\}\right)\to\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{\pi}}dy,\hskip 14.22636ptt\geq 0.

(b)

(Arcsine law for occupation times)
Let
A\coloneqq\psi^{*}((0,+\infty))
, and let

S_{n}A(x)=\#\left\{0\leq k\leq n-1:f^{k}(x)\in A\right\}.

Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\omega_{U}\left(\left\{\frac{1}{n}S_{n}A\leq t\right\}\right)\to\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\sqrt{t},\hskip 14.22636ptt\in[0,1].

(c)

(Arcsine law for waiting times)Let
E\subset\partial U
be a finite target on
\partial U
, and let
Z_{n}E(x)
be the time of the last visit of the orbit of
x
to
E
up to time
n
.
Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\omega_{U}\left(\left\{\frac{1}{n}Z_{n}E\leq t\right\}\right)\to\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin\sqrt{t},\hskip 14.22636ptt\in[0,1].

Doubly parabolic inner functions

The main theorems stated above are in fact obtained by first proving more general results for the radial extension of the associated inner functions, and then transfering them to the Baker domains, exploiting the compatibility between the targets and the Riemann map.

Inner functions associated to doubly parabolic Baker domains are themselves doubly parabolic (Def.
3.4
). For these inner functions we develop a very precise measure-theoretic description of their boundary dynamics, including the following.

•

We give an asymptotic description of occupation times, first return times and waiting times (Thm.
4.1
), in the same spirit as in Theorem
A
.

•

We consider a specific class of inner functions, namely
one component doubly parabolic inner functions
(Def.
3.2
). We prove that the boundary of such inner functions fits into the framework of AFN-systems (Lemma
4.2
), which form a class of interval maps that preserve infinite measures, widely studied from the point of view of infinite ergodic theory. Thus, we will be able to apply results on AFN-systems concerning the asymptotic distribution of orbits, thereby obtaining finer recurrence properties of one-component inner functions (Thm.
4.3
) that translates into Theorem
B
above.

•

We prove the distribution of periodic points according to their period (Prop.
4.6
). More precisely, for
\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}
and
r>0
, there exist a periodic point
p_{r}\in D(\xi,r)
. The period of
p_{r}
increases asymptotically at most as fast as

\frac{-\ln r}{r^{2}},\mbox{\quad as $r\to 0$.}

(Compare also with Theorem
D
below.)

The previous results for inner functions, presented in Sections
3
and
4
, are of independent interest.

Applications

Even though the previous results are interesting on their own, we apply them to a specific class of Baker domains to deduce some additional properties, which are interesting from a topological and dynamical point of view, and not necessarily related with ergodic theory.

Let us start by defining this specific class of Baker domains, which we call
expanding
.

Definition
.

(Expanding Baker domains)

Let
f
be a transcendental entire function, and
U
be a Baker domain. We say that
U
is an
expanding Baker domain
if there exists
r>0
such that the following properties are satisfied.

(a)

U
is doubly parabolic Baker domain, and the Denjoy-Wolff point of the associated inner function is not a singularity.

(b)

For every
x,y\in\partial U
such that
f(y)=x
there exists a branch
F_{y}
of
f^{-1}
which is well-defined in
D(x,r)
, and

F_{y}(D(x,r)\cap U)\subset U
.

(c)

\left|f^{\prime}(z)\right|>1
,
z\in D(x,r)
, for all
x\in\partial U
; and for every finite target
E
,
\left|f^{\prime}(z)\right|\geq K_{E}>1
,
z\in D(x,r)
, for all
x\in E
.

Although this seems a very restrictive condition, we provide a family of Baker domains of any finite degree satisfying the previous properties, inspired by
[
12
]
. In particular, the Baker domains of degree two of the function
f(z)=z+e^{-z}
, previously studied in
[
3
,
12
,
13
]
, are expanding.

Given an expanding Baker domain, and a backward orbit
\left\{x_{n}\right\}_{n}\subset\partial U
(i.e. so that
f(x_{n-1})=x_{n}
), the branch
F_{n}
of
f^{-n}
sending
x_{0}
to
x_{n}
is well-defined in
D(x_{0},r)
, for all
n\geq 0
(Lemma
5.3
). Applying Theorem
A
, we find precise bounds on the contraction for
\widetilde{\omega_{U}}
-almost every backward orbit (where
\widetilde{\omega_{U}}
is the measure on the space of backwards orbits given by Rohklin’s natural extension
[
23
]
; see also
[
17
]
).

Theorem C
.

(Uniform subexponential contraction of inverse branches)

Let
f
be a transcendental entire function, and
U
be an expanding Baker domain. Then, for
\widetilde{\omega_{U}}
-almost every backward orbit
\left\{x_{n}\right\}_{n}
and
r_{0}\in(0,r)
, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
K>1
such that, for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

F_{n}(D(x_{0},r_{0}))\subset D(x_{n},K^{-\sqrt[3]{n}}r_{0}),

where
F_{n}
is the branch of
f^{-n}
sending
x_{0}
to
x_{n}
.

It is well-known that periodic points are dense on the boundary of expanding Baker domains (compare with
[
15
,
18
]
). However, standard proofs showing density of boundary periodic points
[
22
,
13
,
15
,
18
,
17
,
16
]
do not give control in the period of the periodic point.

Combining the techniques in
[
15
,
18
]
with our results on the inner functions (notably Proposition
4.6
), we prove the following result, which gives a finer description of the distribution of periodic points on the boundary of expanding Baker domains in terms of their period.

Theorem D
.

(Bound on the period of periodic boundary points)

Let
f
be an entire function,
U
be an expanding Baker domain and let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{H}\to U
be a conformal map sending infinity to infinity. Let
z\in\partial U
, and let
E=\varphi^{*}(I)
be target, where
I=(x-r,x+r)
. Then, for all
r>0
small enough, there exist a periodic point
p_{r}\in E
. The period of
p_{r}
increases asymptotically at most as fast as

\frac{-\ln r}{r^{2}}+x^{2},\mbox{\hskip 10.22217ptas $r\to 0$.}

Finally, we apply the obtained results to describe the boundary of the Baker domain of the function
f(z)=z+e^{-z}
. The boundary of such Baker domain has been significantly studied in
[
13
]
, giving a detailed description of both the topology and the dynamics. More precisely, the boundary of such Baker domain is made out of hairs (curves of escaping points), some of them land at a point and others accumulate in indecomposable continua, and that the dynamics of the hairs is encoded by some symbolic dynamics. With the previously developed techniques we are able to prove that almost every of such hairs land, and we prove some estimates on the measure of codes satisfying some specific dynamical properties (see Theorem
E
for a precise statement).

Remark
.

We restricted ourselves to entire functions, since we need Fatou components to have infinitely many accesses to infinity (and preimages of infinity being dense in the unit circle), so that the concept of target makes sense and partititions the boundary in an interesting way. For meromorphic functions, this is no longer the case: there exist doubly parabolic Baker domains with a unique access to infinity, and their boundary may even be a Jordan curve
[
6
,
8
]
.

Structure of the paper

We collect some needed background on Infinite Ergodic Theory and AFN-systems in Section
2
, which will serve as a framework for the ergodic study of our maps. In Section
3
, we study doubly parabolic inner functions (the inner functions that can be associated to the Baker domains considered) and their mapping properties. In Section
4
we prove a finer measure-theoretic description of the boundary dynamics of doubly parabolic inner functions (including estimates on the asymptotic distribution of orbits and periodic points).

Finally, in Section
5
we transfer all the results for doubly parabolic inner functions to the boundaries of Baker domains, using the targets and their compatibility with the Riemann map. The proof of the main theorems can be found in this section, even though they follow as direct applications of the work in the previous sections.

As mentioned earlier, the previous results have analogues in the case of basins of attraction (and, therefore, for centered inner functions), which can be obtained quite easily from classical results in ergodic theory (such as the Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem). We collect these results in Appendix
A
, and invite the reader to consult them in order to appreciate the difficulties that arise intrinsically when an invariant probability measure is not available.

2.
Background on Infinite Ergodic Theory

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space, and
T\colon X\to X
be a measure-preserving transformation. We are interested in the case when
\mu
is an infinite
\sigma
-finite measure.

The main difficulty when dealing with measure-preserving transformations for which the preserved measure is not finite, but just
\sigma
-finite, is that the standard results in ergodic theory (such as the Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem or Kac’s Lemma; see Sect.
A.1
) fail to be true. We refer e.g. to
[
1
]
,
[
25
]
,
[
26
, Chap. 10]
for basic background in this field, and we state here only the results that we need.

When dealing with infinite measure spaces, the use of sweep-out sets (which can be viewed as finite-measure building blocks of the system) is particularly useful as it provides a finite-measure framework to work with.

Definition 2.1
.

(Sweep-out set)

Let
T\colon X\to X
be a measure-preserving transformation of a
\sigma
-finite measure space
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
. A set
A\in\mathcal{A}
, with
0<\mu(A)<\infty
is a
sweep-out set
for the transformation
T
if

\mu\Big(X\smallsetminus\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}T^{-n}(A)\Big)=0.

Note that, if the transformation
T
is ergodic and conservative, then every set of positive measure is a sweep-out set
[
26
, Corol. 10.1.14]
. The following lemma, which is well-known (compare e.g.
[
25
]
), relates first return times and sweep-out sets.

Lemma 2.2
.

(Return times to sweep-out sets)

Let
T\colon X\to X
be a measure-preserving transformation of a
\sigma
-finite measure space
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
. Let
A\in\mathcal{A}
be a sweep-out set,
A^{C}=X\smallsetminus A
, then

\mu(A\cap\left\{\tau_{A}>n\right\})=\mu(A^{C}\cap\left\{\tau_{A}=n\right\}),

where
\tau_{A}
denotes the first return time to
A
.

We now introduce a quantity, the wandering rate, that captures the cumulative time spent outside the sweep-out set before returning.

Definition 2.3
.

(Wandering rate)

For a sweep-out set
A
, the
wandering rate

w_{n}(A)
is defined as

w_{n}(A)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mu(A\cap\{\tau_{A}>k\}).

The following pointwise version of an ergodic theorem applies to measure-preserving transformations of
\sigma
-finite measure spaces.

Theorem 2.4
.

(Hopf Ergodic Theorem,
[
26
, Thm. 10.3.3]
)

Let
T\colon X\to X
be an ergodic and conservative measure-preserving transformation of a
\sigma
-finite measure space
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
. If
f,g\in L^{1}(\mu)
and
\int_{X}gd\mu\neq 0
, then

\lim\limits_{n}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f(T^{k}(x))}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(T^{k}(x))}=\frac{\int_{X}fd\mu}{\int_{X}gd\mu},\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{for }\mu\textrm{-a.e. }x\in X.

In the particular case when
f
and
g
are the indicator functions of two measurable sets
E,F\subset X
of positive finite measure, we get

\lim\limits_{n}\frac{\#\left\{0\leq k\leq n-1:T^{k}(x)\in E\right\}}{\#\left\{0\leq k\leq n-1:T^{k}(x)\in F\right\}}=\frac{\mu(E)}{\mu(F)},

i.e. the occupation times are asympotically comparable.

Finally, let
L^{1}_{+}(\mu)
denote the class of functions
f\in L^{1}(\mu)
such that
f\geq 0
and
\int_{X}fd\mu<\infty
. We need the following result by Aaronson.

Theorem 2.5
.

(Rescaling sequences,
[
1
, Thm. 2.4.1]
,
[
26
, Thm. 10.3.6]
)

Let
T\colon X\to X
be an ergodic and conservative measure-preserving transformation of a
\sigma
-finite measure space
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
. Let
\left\{a(n)\right\}_{n}
be a sequence such that

a(n)\nearrow\infty,\hskip 11.38092pt\frac{a(n)}{n}\searrow 0,\hskip 11.38092pt\textrm{as }n\to\infty.

If there exists
A\in\mathcal{A}
such that
0<\mu(A)<\infty
and
\int_{A}a(\tau_{A}(x))d\mu(x)<\infty
, where
\tau_{A}
denotes the first return time to
A
, then

\lim\limits_{n}\frac{1}{a(n)}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f(T^{k}(x))=\infty\hskip 11.38092pt\textrm{ for }\mu\textrm{-a.e. }x\in X,\textrm{ for all }f\in L^{1}_{+}(\mu).

In our applications (Section
4
),
a(n)
is of the form
n^{\alpha}
, for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
f
is the indicator function of some measurable set
E\subset X
of positive measure. This way we will determine that the occupation times of the set
E
grow slower that the sequence
n^{\alpha}
, almost everywhere.

While there is no hope of pointwise ergodic theorems for infinite ergodic systems, it is often possible to find a limit in a weaker sense. We use the following definition.

Definition 2.6
.

(Strong distributional convergence)

Take a sequence of measurable random functions
R_{n}
(taking values in
\mathbb{R}\cup{\pm\infty}
) defined on a
\sigma
-finite measure space
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
.
We denote the distributional convergence of
(R_{n})
w.r.t.
\nu
as
R_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\nu}}{{\Longrightarrow}}R
.

We say that
R
is a
strong distributional limit
of the sequence
R_{n}
if
R_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\nu}}{{\Longrightarrow}}R
for any probability measure
\nu\ll\mu
. We denote this by
R_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}(\mu)}}{{\Longrightarrow}}R
.

2.1.
Basic AFN-systems

An important family of infinite measure-preserving dynamical systems is given by piecewise-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
interval maps with indifferent (neutral) fixed points, introduced by Thaler and Zweimuller
[
24
]
. As explained in the introduction, we will prove that the maps that we consider are AFN-systems, and thus we can apply Thaler and Zweimuller’s results, which we reproduce here.

The definition of a basic AFN-system is as follows (by
\lambda
we denote here the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
).

Definition 2.7
.

(Basic AFN-system)

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}
be a bounded interval, and let
\mathcal{I}=(I_{k})_{k}
be a countable collection of nonempty disjoint subintervals of
X
, such that
\lambda(X\backslash\bigcup_{k}I_{k})=0
. Let
T\colon X\to X
be a map, such that
T|_{I_{k}}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
and strictly monotonic, for every interval
I_{k}\in\mathcal{I}
. Assume that
T
is conservative and ergodic with respect to
\lambda
.

The triple
(X,T,\mathcal{I})
is a
basic AFN-system
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

Adler’s condition:

|T^{\prime\prime}/(T^{\prime})^{2}|
is bounded on
\mathcal{I}
.

(ii)

Finite image condition:

T\mathcal{I}=\{T(I_{k}):I_{k}\in\mathcal{I}\}
is a finite set.

(iii)

There is a finite, nonempty subset
\mathcal{J}\subset\mathcal{I}
of intervals
(I_{k_{l}})_{l=1}^{j}
having one parabolic fixed point
x_{l}
as an endpoint, and
x_{l}
is a one-sided regular source, i.e.

(x-x_{l})\ T^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq 0,\quad x\in I_{k_{l}},\ I_{k_{l}}\in\mathcal{J}.

(iv)

For
I_{k_{l}}\in\mathcal{J}
,
x\to x_{l}
in
I_{k_{l}}
, there is
p_{l}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
a_{l}>0
, such that

T(x)=x+a_{l}|x-z_{l}|^{1+p_{l}}+o\Big(|x-z_{l}|^{1+p_{l}}\Big).

(v)

The map
T
is uniformly expanding on a set bounded away from
\{x_{l}:l=1,\ldots,j\}
.

See Figure
2.1
for two examples of such maps.

Figure 2.1
.

On the right, a AFN-system composed by five branches, two of them with a parabolic point (at 0 and at 3) –in red–, and the others hyperbolic –in blue–.

It is well-known that, if there exist
p_{l}\geq 1
, which is true in our situation, such maps do not preserve any finite measure absolutely continuous with respect to
\lambda
. However, they do preserve an infinite
\sigma
-finite measure absolutely continuous with respect to
\lambda
. We denote such a measure by
\mu
, which is unique (by the ergodicity of
\lambda
).

The following three general results come from
[
24
]
. As we will use them in a very specific situation, we do not provide all the definitions here, in particular, of the distributions
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,\beta}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}
– if necessary those can be found in the cited paper. However, under each result we will state how this translates into our setting.

For any AFN-system, we can associate three parameters, defined below.

•

The parameter
p
describes the behaviour near the parabolic point. Indeed,
p\coloneqq\max\{p_{l}:I_{k_{l}}\in\mathcal{J}\}
.

•

The parameter
\alpha\coloneqq 1/p
.

•

The parameter
\beta
which describes the behaviour at a parabolic point with respect to all the other parabolic points. The – rather technical – definition is in Theorem
2.10
. Suffice it to say that in our situation
\beta=1/2
, basically because we have two parabolic points with the same Taylor expansion around them.

Notably, for our systems the three parameters used below are
p=2
,
\alpha=1/2=\beta
(cf. Section
3
and Lemma
4.2
– where we prove that our system is indeed AFN).

The first result gives a weak limit version of the Ergodic Theorem, i.e. of the asymptotic distribution of

S_{n}f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}f\circ T^{k}(x).

Theorem 2.8
.

(Darling–Kac theorem for AFN maps,
[
24
, Corol. 8.1]
)

Let
(X,T,\mathcal{I})
be a basic AFN-system, and let
E
be a sweep-out set. Then

\frac{1}{a_{n}}S_{n}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}(\mu)}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\int f\,d\mu\cdot\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}\quad\text{for all }f\in L^{1}(\mu)\text{ s.t. }\int f\,d\mu\neq 0,

where

a_{n}\sim\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\cdot\frac{n}{w_{n}(E)},\qquad n\geq 1.

Remark 2.9
.

For
\alpha=1/2
the Mittag-Leffler distribution
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
becomes
|\mathcal{N}(0,1)|
(the absolute value of the standard normal distribution) with the density function

g(x)=\frac{2}{\pi}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{\pi}}\;\chi_{x>0}.

And
a_{n}\sim\frac{4}{\pi}\cdot\frac{n}{w_{n}(Y)}
. And by the calculation from the proof of Theorem
4.1
we have that for any compact interval
E

w_{n}(E)\sim\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sim\sqrt{n}.

The second law tells us about the number of returns into a neighbourhood of a parabolic point. Stating it requires more notation (for formal definitions we again refer to
[
24
]
). Let us abuse the notation slightly and denote the set of parabolic points as
\mathcal{J}
(previously
\mathcal{J}
denoted the set of parabolic intervals). Let
\mathcal{K}
be a proper subset of
\mathcal{J}
. For every parabolic point
x_{l}\in\mathcal{J}
, which is the endpoint of the interval
I_{k_{l}}
, we call the set
I_{k_{l}}\cap T^{-1}(I_{k_{l}})
the
full neighbourhood
of
x_{l}
. Finally, denote by
A_{\mathcal{K}}
the union of full neighbourhoods of points from
\mathcal{K}
.

Also, denote by
w_{N}(E,A)
the wandering rate of
E\cap T^{-1}(A)
, and by
h_{0}
the density of
\mu
w.r.t.
\lambda
. And finally
a_{x_{l}}
comes from assumption (iv) in the definition of AFN.

Theorem 2.10
.

(Arcsine law for neighbourhoods of neutral fixed points,
[
24
, Corol. 8.2]
)

Let
(X,T,\mathcal{I})
be a basic AFN-system, and let
E
be a sweep-out set. Suppose that
\emptyset\neq\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
.
Then

\frac{w_{N}(E,A_{\mathcal{K}})}{w_{N}(E)}\longrightarrow\beta\coloneqq\frac{\sum_{x_{l}\in\mathcal{K},\;p_{x_{l}}=p}h_{0}(x_{l})\,|a_{x_{l}}|^{-\alpha}}{\sum_{x_{l}\in\mathcal{J},\;p_{x_{l}}=p}h_{0}(x_{l})\,|a_{x_{l}}|^{-\alpha}}\in[0,1]\quad\text{as }N\to\infty,

and

\frac{1}{n}S_{n}(\mathbbm{1}_{A})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}(\mu)}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\mathcal{L}_{\alpha,\beta}

for all
A\in\mathcal{B}
with
\mu(A\triangle A_{\mathcal{K}})<\infty
.

Remark 2.11
.

In our situation
\mathcal{J}
contains only two elements. Thus, we will apply Theorem
2.10
for
\mathcal{K}
equal to only one of those elements. Both
h_{0}(x_{l})
and
a_{x_{l}}
are equal at those two points (because those two parabolic points come from one
split
point), so
\beta=\frac{1}{2}
. The distribution
\mathcal{L}_{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}
is the
arcsine distribution
with the cumulative distribution function given by

f(t)=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin{\sqrt{t}}.

Finally, with the notation to be introduced in Section
3.3
, the set
A_{\mathcal{K}}
by definition is either equal to
(p_{2}^{+},+\infty)
or
(-\infty,p_{2}^{-})
(see Figure
3.3
).

We define
Z_{n}Y(x)
to be the time of the last visit of the orbit of
x
to
Y
before time
n
. The third theorem gives the behaviour of this quantity.

Theorem 2.12
.

(Dynkin-Lamperti law for AFN reference sets,
[
24
, Corol. 8.3]
)

Let
(X,T,\mathcal{I})
be a basic AFN-system, and let
E
be a sweep-out set. Then

\frac{1}{n}Z_{n}(E)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{L}(\mu)}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}.

Remark 2.13
.

For
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
the
Z_{\alpha}
(defined as the
B(\alpha,1-\alpha)
distribution) becomes the arcsine distribution.

Remark 2.14
.

We would like to finish this section by observing that AFN maps are well studied and there are many more useful results concerning their statistical behaviour. For example, in
[
5
, Corollary 6.3.3.]
the author proves that for
x
not being a periodic point nor a point eventually hitting the boundary of intervals from
\mathcal{I}
(in particular not one of the parabolic points) we have the following limit behaviour of the first entrance and first return time statistics:

\tau_{B(x,r)}\overset{\mathcal{L}(\mu)}{\underset{r\to 0}{\Longrightarrow}}H_{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\Gamma\big(\frac{3}{2}\big)\Big)\mbox{\quad and \quad}\tau_{B(x,r)}\overset{\mu_{B(x,r)}}{\underset{r\to 0}{\Longrightarrow}}H_{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\Gamma\big(\frac{3}{2}\big)\Big)

where

is a first type Mittag-Leffler law – defined by its Laplace transform

\mathbb{E}\Big(e^{-sH_{\alpha}(\lambda)}\Big)=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s^{\alpha}}.

In fact the author gives the distribution for
n
-th entry/return time statistics. All of this is proved under additional assumptions of the system being
mixing
and
Markov
, both of which are trivially satisfied for the radial extension of one component doubly parabolic inner functions. For details, we refer to the aforementioned PhD thesis.

3.
Doubly parabolic inner functions

3.1.
Inner functions and one component inner functions

Definition 3.1
.

(Inner function)

A holomorphic self-map of the unit disk
g\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
is an
inner function
if, for
\lambda
-almost every point
\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}
,

g^{*}(\xi)\coloneqq\lim\limits_{t\to 1^{-}}g(t\xi)\in\partial\mathbb{D}.

Given an inner function
g
, we use the following notation.

•

A point
\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}
is called a
singularity
of
g
if
g
cannot be continued analytically to a neighbourhood of
\xi
. Denote the set of all singularities of
g
by
\Sigma
.

•

We denote by
\lambda
the normalized Lebesgue measure on
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

Following Cohn
[
10
]
(see also
[
14
]
), we define one component inner functions as follows.

Definition 3.2
.

(One component inner function)

An inner function
g\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
is a
one component inner function
if for some
0<r<1
the set
\{z\in\mathbb{D}:|g(z)|<r\}
is connected.

An equivalent property, and one that is more interesting from a dynamical point of view, is that the singular values of the function

g
are compactly contained in
\mathbb{D}
, as shown by the following result of Ivrii and Urbański.

Theorem 3.3
.

(Characterization of one component inner functions,
[
14
, Thm. 7.2]
)

Let
g\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
be an inner function. The following conditions
are equivalent:

(a)

The set
g^{-1}(B(0,r))
is connected for some
0<r<1
.

(b)

There is an annulus
A=A(0;\rho,1)
such that

g\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
is a covering map over
A
.

(c)

There is an annulus
\widetilde{A}=A(0;\rho,1/\rho)
such that

g\colon\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\smallsetminus\Sigma\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}

is a covering map over
\widetilde{A}
.

(d)

The singular set
\Sigma\subset\partial\mathbb{D}
has Lebesgue measure
0
,
the derivative
g^{\prime}(\xi)\to\infty
as
\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}\smallsetminus\Sigma

approaches
\Sigma
, and

\left|\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(\xi)}{g^{\prime}(\xi)^{2}}\right|\leq C,\qquad\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}\smallsetminus\Sigma,

for some constant
C>0
.

3.2.
Doubly parabolic inner functions

We now address inner functions that do not have a fixed point in
\mathbb{D}
. In particular, we focus on doubly parabolic inner functions, defined as follows.

Definition 3.4
.

(Doubly parabolic inner function)

An inner function
g\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
is a
doubly parabolic inner function
if
g
is holomorphic in
a neighbourhood of the Denjoy-Wolff point
p\in\partial\mathbb{D}
,

g(z)=p+c(z-p)^{3}+\ldots,\qquad c\neq 0,

near
p
.

Note that, by definition, the Denjoy-Wolff of a doubly parabolic inner function is not a singularity for
g
(this is not standard in the literature, but it is the approach followed in
[
14
]
).

As above, we denote by
\Sigma
the set of singularities of
g
and by
\lambda
the Lebesgue measure on
\partial\mathbb{D}
. Moreover:

•

Let
\Theta
be the set of preimages of
p
, i.e. the set of
\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}
such that
g^{*}(\xi)=p
.

•

Take a Möbius transformation
M\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{H}
which sends
p
to
\infty
. Then,

h=M\circ g\circ M^{-1}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}

is a holomorphic self-map of the upper half-plane. Denote its extension to the real line by
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. With a slight abuse of notation, we denote by
\Sigma
,
\Theta
and
\lambda
the set of singularities, the set of preimages of
\infty
, and the pushforward of
\lambda
to
\mathbb{R}
, respectively.

•

Denote by
\mu
the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. For doubly parabolic inner functions
h\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
, such a measure is
T
-invariant (see
[
11
]
).

Moreover,
h\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
has the expression

h(z)=z+\beta+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+zw}{w-z}\,d\rho(w),

where
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\rho
is a finite positive singular
measure on the real line
[
14
, Proof of Thm. 9.1]
. Differenciating,

h^{\prime}(z)=1+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{w^{2}+1}{(w-z)^{2}}\,d\rho(w).

A
one component doubly parabolic
inner function is, by definition, a one component inner function (in the sense of Def.
3.2
) which is also doubly parabolic. Note that such functions enjoy the properties of Theorem
3.3
.

Finally, note that, when seen in the real line
\mathbb{R}
, doubly parabolic inner functions have two parabolic branches (one at
-\infty
and another at
+\infty
), and the preimages of
\infty
lie in a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
(and there are possibly countably many of them). An example is

x\mapsto\frac{1}{\tan(1/x)},

shown in Figure
3.1
.

(a)

x\mapsto 1/\tan(1/x)

(b)

x\mapsto\tan x

The easiest example of a doubly parabolic inner function is the degree 2 map
h(z)=z-1/z
, whose extension
T(x)=x-1/x
is known as

, which has been widely studied and will play a distinguished role later on. See Figure

3.3.
Mapping structure around the Denjoy-Wolff point

Most of our arguments rely on the linearization around the Denjoy-Wolff point, following the ideas of
[
14
]
.

More precisely, if
h
is a doubly parabolic inner function with Denjoy-Wolff point at
\infty
, this means that the normal form of the associated inner function around
\infty
point is the one corresponding to a parabolic fixed point with two invariant petals. If we place for a moment the Denjoy-Wolff point at 0, this leads to the normal form

z\mapsto\phi(z)\coloneqq z+az^{3}+\dots\hskip 14.22636pta>0,

near 0 (note that, since we are dealing with inner functions, the map fixes the real line – compare e.g. with
[
21
, Chap. 10]
). By the change of coordinates
z\mapsto 1/z
, we get

h(z)=\frac{1}{\phi(1/z)}=z-\frac{c}{z}+\dots,\hskip 14.22636ptc>0,

near infinity.

Moreover, since
\infty
is not a singularity of
h
, preimages of
\infty
do not accumulate to itself. Hence, denote by
p_{1}^{-}
and
p_{1}^{+}
the preimages of
\infty
which are located most to the left and most to the right, respectively. In particular,
h
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus\left[p_{1}^{-},p_{1}^{+}\right]
, and one-to-one in each of the intervals
(-\infty,p_{1}^{-})
and
(p_{1}^{+},+\infty)
. We refer to the intervals
(-\infty,p_{1}^{-})
and
(p_{1}^{+},+\infty)
as the
parabolic branches
of
T
; while any interval delimited by two other consecutive preimages of
\infty
is referred as a
hyperbolic branch
of
T
.

Since
\infty
is repelling, there is a backward orbit of
p_{1}^{-}
in
(-\infty,p_{1}^{-})
converging to
-\infty
(and analogously for
p_{1}^{+}
), which we denote as follows

\dots<p_{3}^{-}<p_{2}^{-}<p_{1}^{-}\hskip 14.22636pt\textrm{and}\hskip 14.22636ptp_{1}^{+}<p_{2}^{+}<p_{3}^{+}<\dots,

with
T(p_{n+1}^{\pm})=p_{n}^{\pm}
.

We denote by
J^{\pm}_{n}
the interval
\left[p_{n}^{\pm},p_{n+1}^{\pm}\right]
. We claim that
p_{n}
is located
\sim\sqrt{n}
away from the origin, and thus
\left|p_{n+1}^{\pm}-p_{n}^{\pm}\right|\sim 1/\sqrt{n}
. Indeed, for the normal form with Denjoy-Wolff point at 0,
z\mapsto z+az^{3}+\dots
,
a>0
, points converge backwards to 0 as
1/\sqrt{n}
(modulo a uniform multiplicative constant which only depends on
c
)
[
21
, Lemma 10.1]
. Therefore,
p_{n}\leq C\cdot\sqrt{n}
, for
n
large enough and
C>0
depending only on
c
, as desired.

Finally, note that there is a preimage of zero placed most to the left, say
z^{-}
, and one placed most to the right, say
z^{+}
.
We define
D
as the set
(z^{-},z^{+})
, which will be especially relevant in the sections that follow.

.

and

p_{1}^{-}

3.4.
Finite degree doubly parabolic Blaschke products

Inner functions of finite degree are finite Blaschke products, i.e. they have the form

g\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D},\ \ \ g(z)=e^{i\theta}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{a_{k}-z}{1-\overline{a_{k}}z},

where
\theta\in\left[0,2\pi\right)
,
a_{k}\in\mathbb{D}
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
is the degree of
g
. The following lemma gives a characterization of doubly parabolic finite Blaschke products when seen as functions of the upper half plane.

Lemma 3.5
.

(Characterization of finite Blaschke products)
The map
h\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
is a doubly parabolic inner function of degree
n\in\mathbb{N}
if and only if

h(z)=z-\frac{b_{1}}{z-a_{1}}-\dots-\frac{b_{n}}{z-a_{n}},

for
b_{k}>0
and pairwise different
a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Functions
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
of the form

x\mapsto x-\frac{b_{1}}{x-a_{1}}-\dots-\frac{b_{n}}{x-a_{n}},

for pairwise different
a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
b_{k}>0
, are usually called
generalized Boole’s transformations
in the setting of infinite ergodic theory (see e.g.
[
19
]
).

Proof.

It is clear that
h
is an inner function of finite degree, and that it fixes infinity. Moreover, since

h^{\prime}(z)=1+\frac{b_{1}}{(z-a_{1})^{2}}+\dots+\frac{b_{n}}{(z-a_{n})^{2}},

h^{\prime\prime}(z)=-\frac{2b_{1}}{(z-a_{1})^{3}}-\dots-\frac{2b_{n}}{(z-a_{n})^{3}},

we have
h^{\prime}(\infty)=1
and
h^{\prime\prime}(\infty)=0
, implying that
h
is doubly parabolic.

It is left to see that any finite degree doubly parabolic inner function can be written in the previous form. Indeed,
from the expression

h(z)=z+\beta+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+zw}{w-z}\,d\rho(w)

and the fact that
h
has degree
n
, one can write

h(z)=z+\beta+\frac{1+zw_{1}}{w_{1}-z}+\dots+\frac{1+zw_{n}}{w_{n}-z},

for
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
are pairwise different since
h|_{\mathbb{R}}
has no critical points. Then,

\frac{1+zw_{k}}{w_{k}-z}=-w_{k}-\frac{1+w_{k}^{2}}{z-w_{k}}=-w_{k}-\frac{b_{k}}{z-a_{k}},

for
a_{k}=w_{k}
and
b_{k}=1+w_{k}^{2}>0
. It is left to see that the additive constant
\beta-w_{1}-\dots-w_{n}
is 0, but this follows easily from the fact that
h
is doubly parabolic.
∎

Note that, if we assume
a_{1}<\dots<a_{n}
, then
a_{1}=p^{-}_{1}
and
a_{n}=p^{+}_{1}
(following the notation above). Then, the parabolic branches are
(-\infty,a_{1})
and
(a_{n},+\infty)
, while the hyperbolic ones are of the form
(a_{k},a_{k+1})
, for
k=1,\dots,n-1
.

Figure 3.4
.
Graph of the function
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
x\mapsto x-\frac{1}{x-1}-\frac{1}{x+1}-\frac{1}{x-2}-\frac{1}{x+2}
, which is the radial extension of a doubly parabolic Blaschke product of finite degree (seen in
\mathbb{H}
). Its hyperbolic branches are drawn in red, while the parabolic ones in blue. Note that
a_{1}=p^{-}_{1}=-2
and
a_{n}=p^{+}_{1}=2
.

Recall that
D
is the set
(z^{-},z^{+})
, where
z^{-}
is the preimage of zero placed most to the left, and
z^{+}
is the one placed most to the right. The following theorem by Li and Schweiger
[
19
]
shows uniform bounds for the distortion of the return map
T_{D}\colon D\to D
(see
[
2
, Lemmas 3 and 5]
for the same statement for Boole’s map).

Theorem 3.6
.

(Distortion for the returns to
D
,
[
19
, Lemma 3]
)

Let
h\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be a doubly parabolic inner function of finite degree with Denjoy-Wolff point at
\infty
, and let
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be its extension to
\mathbb{R}
. Let
D=(z^{-},z^{+})
, and let
T_{D}\colon D\to D
be the return map to
D
. Then, there exists a constant
M>1
such that for all
n\geq 0
,
D_{n}
connected component of
T_{D}^{-n}(D)
and a measurable set
E\subset D
,
\mu(E)>0
, we have

\frac{1}{M}\leq\frac{\mu(D_{n}\cap T^{-n}_{D}(E))}{\mu(D_{n})\mu(E)}\leq M.

Remark 3.7
.

The result in
[
19
, Lemma 3]
is stated for generalized Boole’s transformations. By Lemma
3.5
, equivalently it holds for doubly parabolic inner function of finite degree with Denjoy-Wolff point at
\infty
, as stated in

.

4.
Ergodic properties of the radial extension of doubly parabolic inner functions

We next prove some ergodic properties of the radial extension of doubly parabolic inner functions.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
h\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be a doubly parabolic inner function with Denjoy-Wolff point at
\infty
, and let
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be its extension to
\mathbb{R}
.

(a)

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}
be a compact interval, and let

S_{n}E(x)=\#\left\{0\leq k\leq n-1:h^{k}(x)\in E\right\}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\circ h^{k}(x).

Then, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
,

\lim\limits_{n}\frac{S_{n}E(x)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}}\to\infty,

for
\lambda
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

(b)

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}
be a compact interval, and let

E_{n}\coloneqq\left\{x\in E\colon\tau_{E}(x)=n\right\},

where
\tau_{E}(x)\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the first return time of
x\in E
to
E
. Then,
\lambda(E_{n})\sim\frac{1}{n\sqrt{n}}
.

(c)

Let
E\subset\mathbb{R}
be a compact interval, let
F=\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus E
. Assume
E
is large enough. Let

F_{n}\coloneqq\left\{x\in F\colon h(x),\dots,h^{n-1}(x)\in F,h^{n}(x)\notin F\right\}.

Then,
\lambda(F_{n})\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.

Proof.

Observe that, by Hopf Ergodic Theorem

2.4
, in items
(a)
and
(b)
it is enough to prove the statement for a (well-chosen) set
E
, and then it holds for any compact interval.

Let us prove first
(b)
and
(c)
, for which we rely on the linearization around the Denjoy-Wolff point (Sect.
3.3
).

To prove
(b)
, assume
E=\left[p^{-}_{1},p^{+}_{1}\right]
. By Lemma
2.2
,

\mu(E\cap\left\{\tau_{E}>n\right\})=\mu(E^{C}\cap\left\{\tau_{E}=n\right\}),

where
\tau_{E}
denotes the first return time to
E
. By the definition of
p^{\pm}_{n}
,

E^{C}\cap\left\{\tau_{E}=n\right\}=J^{-}_{n}\cup J^{+}_{n}.

Therefore,

\mu(E\cap\left\{\tau_{E}>n\right\})=\mu(J^{-}_{n}\cup J^{+}_{n})\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}

and

\mu(E\cap\left\{\tau_{E}=n\right\})\sim\frac{1}{n\sqrt{n}}.

Since
\lambda
and
\mu
are comparable at compact intervals of the real line,

\lambda(E\cap\left\{\tau_{E}=n\right\})\sim\frac{1}{n\sqrt{n}},

as desired.
Since it is enough to prove the statement for this specific set
E
, this ends the proof of
(b)
.

To prove
(c)
, assume first
E=\left[p^{-}_{1},p^{+}_{1}\right]
, so
F=\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus\left[p^{-}_{1},p^{+}_{1}\right]
. Since
p^{\pm}_{2}
are the unique preimages in
F
of
p^{\pm}_{1}
, the interval
J_{1}
corresponds to the points in
F
which escape from
F
in one iteration, while points in
J_{n}
are the points in
F
which escape from
F
in
n
iterations. Thus,

F_{n}=(-\infty,p_{n}^{-}]\cup[p_{n}^{+},+\infty).

Finally, we compute the
\mu
-measure of the intervals
(-\infty,p_{n}^{-}]\cup[p_{n}^{+},+\infty)
(in fact, we just do it for the positive one, and we already get the desired result, which is up to multiplicative constant). Indeed,

\int_{\sqrt{n}}^{+\infty}\frac{2}{x^{2}+1}d\mu(x)=\pi-2\arctan\sqrt{n}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}

showing the desired result in the case
E=\left[p^{-}_{1},p^{+}_{1}\right]
. Clearly, the statement holds true for any interval of the form
E=\left[p^{-}_{k},p^{+}_{k}\right]
, and then for any compact interval
E
large enough.
This ends the proof of statement
(c)
.

To prove
(a)
, we work again with the set
E=\left[p^{-}_{1},p^{+}_{1}\right]
. As above, denote by
\tau_{E}
the first return time to
E
. By Theorem
2.5
, it is enough to show that, for
a(n)=n^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
,

\int_{E}a(\tau_{E}(x))d\mu(x)<\infty.

Indeed, by
(b)
, we have

\int_{E}a(\tau_{E}(x))d\mu(x)=\sum_{n}a(n)\cdot\mu(E\cap\left\{\tau_{E}=n\right\})\sim\sum_{n}n^{\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon}\frac{1}{n\sqrt{n}},

and this later sum clearly converges.
This ends the proof of the theorem.
∎

4.1.
AFN-systems and one component inner functions

In this section we prove that the radial extension of a one component doubly parabolic inner function is essentially an AFN-system, which allow us to deduce finer distributional properties for its orbits.

Lemma 4.2
.

(One component doubly parabolic inner functions are AFN)

Let
h\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be a one component doubly parabolic inner function, and let
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be its radial extension to
\mathbb{R}
. Then, there exists an analytic homeomorphism
\varphi\colon(-\pi/2,\pi/2)\to\mathbb{R}
such that
((-\pi/2,\pi/2),S,\mathcal{I})
is an AFN-system, where
S\coloneqq\varphi^{-1}\circ T\circ\varphi
and
\mathcal{I}
is the set of all intervals of
(-\pi/2,\pi/2)\smallsetminus\varphi^{-1}(\overline{\Theta})
,
\Theta=T^{-1}(\infty)
.

Proof.

Take
\varphi(x)=p\tan(x)
, for
p
large enough (the assumption on
p
is at the end of the proof). We have the following commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}

We have to check that the system
((-\pi/2,\pi/2),S,\mathcal{I})
is indeed an AFN system, i.e. check the conditions of Definition
2.7
. First we will show those results for the map
T
, i.e. on the real line, and then show that they are preserved by our conjugation.

Observe that for a parabolic inner map on the upper half-plane we have an equivalent statement of Theorem
3.3
. In particular we will use (c) and (d).

By definition, the set
\overline{\Theta}
is the union of
\Theta
(i.e. the preimages of infinity) and the singular set
\Sigma
(the accumulation points of the preimages of infinity).
By Thm.
3.3
(d) the singular set of

has Lebesgue measure
0
, and
\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus(\Sigma\cup\Theta)
is a union of disjoint open intervals. On each of those intervals the map is strictly monotonic (no critical points on the boundary) and smooth. Any doubly parabolic inner map is conservative and ergodic. None of those properties change when moving from

to

.

There are only two (thus finite and nonempty) elements in
\mathcal{J}
with parabolic points at

and

To get (ii), observe that on every interval (between the points of
\Sigma

is onto the whole real line (thus the set of images has one element). This we know from Thm.
3.3
(c), namely that the map is covering. Moving to

preserves this property (the image of every subinterval is

).

Again, by (d) we have that
T^{\prime\prime}/(T^{\prime})^{2}
is bounded. To show the same for

we need to use word-by-word the same argument as in the proof by Ivrii and Urbański (part
(c)\implies(d)
) giving (i).

It remains to prove (iv).

We start by observing that this is true for the map

and its derivative from Section

\displaystyle T(x)

\displaystyle=x+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+xw}{w-x}\,d\rho(w),

\displaystyle T^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=1+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{w^{2}+1}{(w-x)^{2}}\,d\rho(w),

where
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\rho
is some finite positive singular measure on the real line.

Taking a bounded set of
x

we have to use the construction of

.
A computation of the derivative gives

S^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1+t^{2}}{1+(T(pt))^{2}/p^{2}}T^{\prime}(pt),

where we substituted

and

\frac{p^{2}+s^{2}}{p^{2}+T^{2}(s)}T^{\prime}(s)\geq K>1.

So it suffices to have the following.

Using the Jensen’s inequality we have (for any function

\Big(\int u\,d\rho\Big)^{2}=\Big(\int R\cdot u\,d\frac{\rho}{R}\Big)^{2}\leq\int R^{2}\cdot u^{2}\,d\frac{\rho}{R}=R\int u^{2}\,d\rho.

‣
4.1

\displaystyle T^{2}(s)

\displaystyle=\Big(s+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+sw}{w-s}\,d\rho(w)\Big)^{2}=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{s}{R}+\frac{1+sw}{w-s}\,d\rho(w)\Big)^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}R\Big(\frac{1+sw(1+1/R)-s^{2}/R}{w-s}\Big)^{2}d\rho(w)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(\alpha w+\beta)^{2}}{(w-s)^{2}}d\rho(w),

\displaystyle\frac{p^{2}}{K}\Big(1+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{w^{2}+1}{(w-s)^{2}}\,d\rho(w)\Big)-1\geq\frac{p^{2}}{K}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{w^{2}+1}{(w-s)^{2}}\,d\rho(w).

Thus it suffices to choose

so big such that

for all

(which is trivial to do for quadratic maps). This ends the proof of (iv) and the entire lemma.

Theorem 4.3
.

4.2.
Periodic points for doubly parabolic inner functions

The following propositions allow us to find periodic points for doubly parabolic inner functions, yielding explicit bounds for the period. We first focus on the so-called
Boole’s map

T(x)=x-\frac{1}{x}
,
which serves as a canonical model, and we then generalize the argument.

Proposition 4.4
.

(Periodic points for Boole’s map)

Let
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
T(x)=x-\frac{1}{x}
. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
r\in(0,1/4)
. Then, there exist a periodic point
p\in(x-r,x+r)
of period less than

\left(\frac{-\ln r}{\ln 2}+1\right)\cdot\frac{4}{r^{2}}+x^{2}.

Lemma 4.5
.

(Mapping properties for Boole’s map)

Let
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
T(x)=x-\frac{1}{x}
. Let
D=(-1,1)
.

(a)

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
T^{\prime}(x)>1
, and for all
x\in D
,
T^{\prime}(x)>2
.

(b)

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists
n=0,\dots,\lfloor x^{2}\rfloor
such that
T^{n}(x)\in D
.

(c)

For all
r\in(0,1)
, let
D_{r}=(-\frac{r}{2},\frac{r}{2})
. For all
x\in D\smallsetminus D_{r}
, there exists
n=1,\dots,\lfloor 4/r^{2}\rfloor
such that
T^{n}(x)\in D
.

(d)

Let
B\subset\mathbb{R}
be a compact interval. Then,
T^{-1}(B)
consists of two disjoint compact intervals, not containing
0,1,-1
.

Proof.

Observe first that the image
T^{n}(x)
of any given point is well-defined as long as the point is neither
0
, nor one of the countably many preimages of
0
. Note
T(D)=\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
. Also,

T^{2}(0,1)=T(-\infty,0)=\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},

T^{2}(-1,0)=T(0,+\infty)=\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.

Since
T
is a proper map of degree 2, and we denote as follows the inverse branches:

T_{1,1}\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{-}\coloneqq\left(-\infty,0\right),

T_{1,2}\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}\coloneqq\left(0,+\infty\right).

We also note that the positive (and negative analogously) can be covered by consecutive preimages (using only the branch going to the right half-line) of the set
(0,1]
.

We prove the different statements separately.

(a)

The first item follows from the computation of the derivative:

T^{\prime}(x)=1+\frac{1}{x^{2}}.

(b)

Observe that from the behaviour of
T
around infinity as a parabolic point in Section
3.3
one cannot deduce the desired bound. However, since we have an explicit expression for the map, we can find the desired bound as follows.

We have that for large, positive
k
the map needs at most
k
steps to go from
k
to
k-1
. More precisely,

k-1\leq\min\{n:T^{n}(k)\leq k-1\}\leq k.

Thus, it takes at most

1+2+\dots+k=\frac{k^{2}+k}{2}\leq k^{2}

steps to get from
k
to
(0,1)
. The proof is analogous for negative values of
k
.

(c)

From the behaviour of the map near 0 we see that

T(D_{r})=(-\infty,-\frac{2}{r}+\frac{r}{2})\cup(\frac{2}{r}-\frac{r}{2},+\infty)
. Therefore,

T(D\smallsetminus D_{r})\subset\left[-\frac{2}{r}+\frac{r}{2},\frac{2}{r}-\frac{r}{2}\right],

and applying
(b)
, we get the desired estimate.

(d)

Since
T
is a proper map of degree 2,
T^{-1}(B)
is compact. Moreover,
T_{1,1}(B)\subset\mathbb{R}_{-}
and
T_{1,2}(B)\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
, so the two intervals are disjoint. It is left to show that they not contain 0, 1 nor
-1
. Indeed, if
0\in T_{1,1}(B)
, then
\infty\in B
, which contradicts the fact that
B
is compact. One can prove the remaining part of the statement analogously, taken into account that
T(1)=T(-1)=0
.

∎

.

Proof of Proposition
4.4
.

The strategy of the proof is as follows. If we let
B=(x-r,x+r)
, we want to find the minimal
N
for which we can guarantee that there exists
y\in B
,

\#\left\{n\leq N\colon T^{n}(y)\in D\right\}>-\frac{\ln r}{\ln 2}

and
T^{N}(y)\in(x-r/2,x+r/2)
. We claim that then there is an
N
-periodic point in
B
,
and, to complete the proof of the proposition, we will show that we can take

N=\Big\lfloor\frac{-\ln r}{\ln 2}\cdot\frac{4}{r^{2}}+x^{2}\Big\rfloor.

First let us prove our claim on the existence of the
N
-periodic point in
B
. Consider the intervals
B_{m}
,
m=0,\dots,N
, defined as the connected component of
T^{-m}(B)
containg
T^{n}(y)
,
n=N-m
. According to Lemma
4.5
(d)
, all such intervals are well-defined, and
0,1,-1\notin B_{m}
, for all

. By the estimates on the derivative (Lemma

(a)
),
\left|B_{m}\right|<\left|B_{m-1}\right|
, and
2\left|B_{m}\right|<\left|B_{m-1}\right|
whenever
T^{n}(y)\in D
(note that if
T^{n}(y)\in D
, since
0,1,-1\notin B_{m}
, then
B_{m}\subset D
and the derivative is greater than 2 for all points in
B_{m}
). Since
T^{n}(y)\in D
at least
\lfloor-\ln r/\ln 2\rfloor
times, and
r\in(0,1/4)
, so
\lfloor-\ln r/\ln 2\rfloor>2
, we have

\left|B_{N}\right|<\frac{1}{4}\left|B\right|.

Therefore, since
T^{N}(y)\in(x-r/2,x+r/2)
, there exists an inverse branch of
T^{N}
, say
T_{N,B}
, well-defined in
B
and
T_{N,B}\colon B\to B
. Note that it is strictly contractive. By the Banach fixed point theorem,
T_{N,B}
has a fixed point in
B
, which is an
N
-periodic point of
T
.

Second, let us show that we can indeed take
N
as defined above. To start with
there are two possibilities, depending on the placement of the starting set
B
: either
x\in D
or
x\notin D
. In the second situation we require an additional at most
x^{2}
steps to enter
D
, so actually we can assume
x\in D
. We first assume that
B\subset(0,1)
(or analogously,
B\subset(-1,0)
).

The goal is to show that, with

N\leq\left(\frac{-\ln r}{\ln 2}+1\right)\cdot\frac{4}{r^{2}},

then
T^{N}(B)\supset(0,1)\supset B
(and therefore there is
y\in B
such that
T^{N}(y)\in(x-r/2,x+r/2)
).

As

, then the diameters of the images of
B
grow. Also those images are connected as long as they do not hit
0
, and this leads us to distinguish two cases. First, assume
0\notin T^{n}(B)
, for
n=0,\dots,N^{\prime}
, with

N^{\prime}=\Big\lfloor\frac{-\ln r}{\ln 2}\cdot\frac{4}{r^{2}}\Big\rfloor.

Since
0\notin B
,
B\cap(D\smallsetminus D_{r})\neq\emptyset
, then the after at most
\lfloor 4/r^{2}\rfloor
steps we return to
D
. And because
T^{\prime}|_{D}\geq 2
the new image of
B
within
D
has radius at least
2r
, so it has to intersect
D\smallsetminus D_{r}
. Proceeding in an inductive manner, by the choice of
N^{\prime}
, the radius of
T^{N^{\prime}}(B)
is at least 1, so either
T^{N^{\prime}}(B)\supset(0,1)
or
T^{N^{\prime}}(B)\supset(-1,0)
. In any case,
T^{N}(B)\subset(-1,1)
, as desired.

Now assume otherwise that
0\in T^{n}(B)
, for some
n\leq N^{\prime}
. Then
T^{n}(B)
has to contain either
(-r/2,0)
or
(0,r/2)
– without loss of generality assume it is
(-r/2,0)
. Thus,
T^{n+1}(B)
contains
(\frac{2}{r}+1,+\infty)
. By Lemma
4.5
(b)
, the image of this set covers
(0,1)
after at most
4/r^{2}
steps. Therefore,
T^{N}(B)\supset(0,1)
for

N\leq N^{\prime}+\frac{4}{r^{2}}=\left(\frac{-\ln r}{\ln 2}+1\right)\cdot\frac{4}{r^{2}}.

In both cases we proved that
T^{N}(B)\supset(0,1)
.

Now, assume that
0\in B
. Then, either
B\cap(0,1)
or
B\cap(-1,0)
have length greater that
r
, so we can apply the last argument and deduce the same bound also in this case. This ends the proof of the proposition.

Now we turn to the general one component doubly parabolic inner functions. We want to rewrite the proof above with necessary changes.

Proposition 4.6
.

(Periodic points for doubly parabolic inner functions)

Let
h\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be a one component doubly parabolic inner function, and let
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be its radial extension to
\mathbb{R}
. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
r_{0}>0
small enough. Then, for all
r\in(0,r_{0})
, there exist a periodic point
p\in(x-r,x+r)
of period less than

\Big(\frac{-\ln r}{\ln K}+1\Big)\cdot\frac{W}{cr^{2}}+\frac{x^{2}}{c},

where
c>0
and
W,K>1
are constants that depend only on
h
.

Note that, essentially, this is telling that the asymptotic rate of increase of the period as
r\to 0
is

\frac{-\ln r}{r^{2}}+x^{2},

which asymptotically is the same as for Boole’s map.

Lemma 4.7
.

(Mapping properties for doubly parabolic inner functions)
Let
h\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be a doubly parabolic inner function, and let
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be its extension to
\mathbb{R}
. Then, there exists an open bounded interval
D\subset\mathbb{R}
and a set
D_{r}\subset D
, consisting of a collection of open intervals of length

, such that:

(a)

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
T^{\prime}(x)>1
, and for all
x\in D
,
T^{\prime}(x)\geq K
.

(b)

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists
n=0,\dots,\lfloor\frac{x^{2}}{c}\rfloor
such that
T^{n}(x)\in D
.

(c)

For all
r\in(0,r_{0})
and
x\in D\smallsetminus D_{r}
, there exists
n=1,\dots,\lfloor W/cr^{2}\rfloor
such that
T^{n}(x)\in D
.

(d)

Let
B\subset\mathbb{R}
be a compact interval. Then,
T^{-1}(B)\subset\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus\Sigma
consists of disjoint compact intervals, not containing any preimage of
\infty
.

The constants
c>0
and
W,K>1
depend only on
h
.

Proof.

For the map
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, the orbit of a point may get truncated for two reasons. First, because it hits the set
\Sigma

5.
Boundaries of doubly parabolic Baker domains

5.1.
Partitioning the boundary. Targets

Let
U
be a doubly parabolic Baker domain. Let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{D}\to U
be a Riemann map, and let
g\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
,
g\coloneqq\varphi^{-1}\circ f\circ\varphi
, be the associated inner function. It is clear that
\varphi
is a conjugacy between
f|_{U}
and
g
.

We aim to extend this conjugacy to the boundary, i.e. between
f|_{\partial U}
and
g^{*}|_{\partial\mathbb{D}}
(the radial extension of
g
). Let us note that
a priori
it is unclear how to proceed, since
\varphi\colon\mathbb{D}\to U
does not extend continuously to
\partial\mathbb{D}
(indeed, for the considered Fatou components, its boundary is never locally connected
[
3
]
), and hence it does not provide a topological conjugacy between
f|_{\partial U}
and
g^{*}|_{\partial\mathbb{D}}
. The way of showing that
\partial U
is not locally connected is by showing that points in
\partial\mathbb{D}
whose radial limit by
\varphi
is infinity are dense on the unit circle. Precisely these infinitely many accesses to infinity is what makes a partition of the boundary specially well-behaved with respect to the radial extension of the inner function. Next we make this argument precise.

We consider the radial extension of the Riemann map
\varphi^{*}\colon\partial\mathbb{D}\to\partial U
.
The following is a classical result concerning the relation between
\varphi^{*}
and the topology of the boundaries of Fatou components of entire functions, which collects the results in
[
3
,
9
,
15
]
.

Recall that for
\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}
, the
cluster set

Cl(\varphi,\xi)
of
\varphi
at
\xi
is the set of values
w\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
for which there is a sequence
\left\{z_{n}\right\}_{n}\subset\mathbb{D}
such that
z_{n}\to\xi
and
\varphi(z_{n})\to w
, as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 5.1
.

(Cluster sets and radial limits for Baker domains)

Let
f
be a transcendental entire function, and let
U
be a doubly parabolic Baker domain. Let
\varphi\colon\mathbb{D}\to U
be a Riemann map. Then,

(a)

\{\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}\colon\varphi^{*}(\xi)=\infty\}

is dense on
\partial\mathbb{D}
;

(b)

\partial U
is the disjoint union of cluster sets
Cl(\varphi,\cdot)
of
\varphi
in
\mathbb{C}
, i.e.

\partial U=\bigsqcup\limits_{\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}}Cl(\varphi,\xi)\cap\mathbb{C}.

Moreover, either
Cl(\varphi,\xi)\cap\mathbb{C}
is empty, or has at most two connected components. If
Cl(\varphi,\xi)\cap\mathbb{C}
is disconnected, then
\varphi^{*}(\xi)=\infty
.

As defined in the introduction,
E\subset\partial U
is a
target
on
\partial U
if
E=\varphi^{*}(I)
, for a closed arc
I\subset\partial\mathbb{D}
, delimited by
\xi_{1}\neq\xi_{2}\in\partial\mathbb{D}
such that
\varphi^{*}(\xi_{1})=\varphi^{*}(\xi_{2})=\infty
.

From Theorem
5.1
, we deduce the following basic property of targets, which determines precisely when a point is in a target, and we can codify the orbits of points in the boundary
\partial U
by analizing the radial extension of the associated inner function.

Proposition 5.2
.

(Basic property of targets)

Let
E
be a target on
\partial U
, defined as
E=\varphi^{*}(I)\subset\partial U
, for an arc
I=\left[\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\right]\subset\partial\mathbb{D}

\varphi^{*}(\xi_{1})=\varphi^{*}(\xi_{2})=\infty
. Then,

•

x\in E
if and only if
x\in Cl(\varphi,\xi)
,
\xi\in I
;

•

if
x\in Cl(\varphi,\xi)
,
f^{n}(x)\in E
if and only if
(g^{*})^{n}(\xi)\in I
.

Proof.

For the first statement, denote by
R_{\xi_{i}}
the radial segment at
\xi_{i}
,
i=1,2
. Then,
R_{\xi_{1}}\cup R_{\xi_{2}}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
is a Jordan curve through infinity, which divides the Riemann sphere into two sets. One contains the cluster sets of points in
I
, and the other the cluster sets of its complement. Then, the statement follows.

For the second statement, note that we can think of the target
E
as being delimited by a Jordan arc
\gamma
ending at
\infty
in both directions (that is, a Jordan curve through infinity in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
). The preimages (by
f^{n}
) of such an arc are curves landing at infinity from both ends (since, for entire functions, infinity has no finite preimage). We consider only the preimages of
\gamma
lying in
U
. Then,
x\in f^{-n}(E)
, and
f^{-n}(E)
is a collection of targets delimited by the previous arcs. Applying the first statement, it is clear that
\xi
lies in some interval which projects by
\varphi^{*}
to some target of
f^{-n}(E)
, so
\xi\in(g^{*})^{-n}(I)
. Then, the statement follows immediately.
∎

Finally, recall that sometimes one needs to distinguish between finite and infinite targets, defined as follows. Let
U
be a doubly parabolic Baker domain, and let
\psi\colon\mathbb{H}\to U
be a conformal map from the upper-half plane to
U
, such that points in
\mathbb{H}
converge to
\infty
when iterating
h\coloneqq\psi^{-1}\circ f\circ\psi
. Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
be a closed interval, delimited by
x_{1}\neq x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\psi^{*}(x_{1})=\psi^{*}(x_{2})=\infty
.

•

If
I
is bounded on
\mathbb{R}
, we say that
\psi^{*}(I)\subset\partial U
is a
finite target
on
\partial U
.

•

If the complement of
I
is bounded on
\mathbb{R}
, we say that
\psi^{*}(I)\subset\partial U
is a
infinite target
on
\partial U
.

Now we are in the position of proving the theorems concerning boundaries of doubly parabolic Baker domains stated in the introduction. Mostly, the theorems follow from the analogous ones on doubly parabolic inner functions (via Proposition
5.2
), although for Theorem
C
a finer argument is required.

5.2.
Proof of Theorems
A
and
B

By Proposition
5.2
, Theorem

follows from Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
B
follows from Theorem
4.3
. The proofs are immediate. Note that Theorem

holds not only for Baker domains of finite degree (as stated in the introduction), but also for Baker domains of infinite degree whose associated inner function is one component.
\square

5.3.
Expanding Baker domains. Theorems
C
and
D

In this section, we study
expanding Baker domains
of entire functions, which we defined in the introduction as the ones satisfying the following conditions.

(a)

U
is doubly parabolic Baker domain, and the Denjoy-Wolff point of the associated inner function is not a singularity.

(b)

For every
x,y\in\partial U
such that
f(y)=x
there exists a branch
F_{y}
of
f^{-1}
which is well-defined in
D(x,r)
, and

F_{y}(D(x,r)\cap U)\subset U
.

(c)

\left|f^{\prime}(z)\right|>1
,
z\in D(x,r)
, for all
x\in\partial U
; and for every finite target
E
,
\left|f^{\prime}(z)\right|\geq K_{E}>1
,
z\in D(x,r)
, for all
x\in E
.

Note that iterated inverse branches are well-defined and conformal along all backward orbits, as in the case of expanding basins of attraction.

Lemma 5.3
.

(Inverse branches are well-defined)

Let
f
be a transcendental entire function, and let
U
be an expanding basin of attraction. Let
\left\{x_{n}\right\}_{n}\subset\partial U
be a backward orbit. Then, the inverse branches
F_{n}
of
f^{n}
,
F_{n}(x_{0})=x_{n}
are well-defined in
D(x_{0},r)
.

Proof.

The lemma follows easily from an inductive construction of the required inverse branches (compare with Lemma
A.5
). Indeed, the existence of the first inverse branch
F_{1}
is known by assumption. Note that
F_{1}(D(x_{0},r))\subset D(x_{1},r)
, since
\left|f^{\prime}(z)\right|>1
,
z\in D(x,r)
, for all
x\in\partial U
. Since all inverse branches if
f
are well-defined in
D(x_{1},r)
, one deduces that
F_{2}
is well-defined in
D(x_{0},r)
. The construction of
F_{n}
follows by induction.
∎

Proof of Theorem
C

Theorem
C
asserts that, given a transcendental entire function
f
, and
U
an expanding Baker domain, then for
\widetilde{\omega_{U}}
-almost every backward orbit
\left\{x_{n}\right\}_{n}
and
r_{0}\in(0,r)
, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
K>1
such that, for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

F_{n}(D(x_{0},r_{0}))\subset D(x_{n},K^{-\sqrt[3]{n}}r_{0}),

where
F_{n}
is the branch of
f^{-n}
sending
x_{0}
to
x_{n}
, for some
K>1
.

According to Lemma
5.3
, we only need to prove the estimate of contraction, and it will follow from Theorem
A
(a)
. Indeed, Theorem
A
(a)
asserts, in particular, that for the finite target
E\subset\partial U
where there is expansion by
K_{E}>1
,

\lim\limits_{n}\frac{\#\left\{0\leq k\leq n-1:x_{k}\in E\right\}}{{\sqrt[3]{n}}}\to\infty,

for
\widetilde{\omega_{U}}
-almost every backward orbit
\left\{x_{n}\right\}_{n}\subset\partial U
. Therefore, having fixed a backward orbit
\left\{x_{n}\right\}_{n}\subset\partial U
satisfying the previous condition, we can take

such that for

Thus, at time

, we contracted by the factor

at least

times (and the remaining times

do not increase distance), leading to the desired result (with

).

\square

Proof of Theorem

The proof follows straight from Proposition
4.6
(and using the same notation).

The fact that the periodic point that we found lies on

\square

.

The Baker domain of the function

Proof.

Let

be a Baker domain of degree 2. Then, there exist a Riemann map
\psi\colon\mathbb{H}\to U
such that the associated inner function
h=\psi^{-1}\circ f\circ\psi
is
h(z)=z-\frac{1}{z}
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
A
, the map near infinity is of the form

h(z)=z-\frac{c}{z}+\dots,\hskip 14.22636ptc>0.

However, since the map is of degree two, we have
h(z)=z-\frac{c}{z}
. By a conformal change of variables (i.e. by precomposing the Riemann map by an automorphism of
\mathbb{H}
), we can take
h(z)=z-\frac{1}{z}
, as desired.
∎

If we restrict ourselves to the Baker domain of

Lemma 5.6

Proof of Theorem
E

For the proof of
(a)
, recall that the hair
\gamma_{\underline{c}}
is constructed by the concatenation of infinitely many pieces
\gamma_{n}
, and

\textrm{diam}\gamma_{n}\leq\textrm{diam}F_{c_{1}}\circ\dots\circ F_{c_{n}}(D),

where
D
is a fixed bounded set and
F_{0}
and
F_{1}
are the two branches of
f^{-1}
fixing

[

. Therefore, if

\sum_{n}\textrm{diam}F_{c_{1}}\circ\dots\circ F_{c_{n}}(D)<\infty,

the hair
\gamma_{n}
lands at a finite endpoint. Note that in any finite target
f
is strictly expanding
[
13
, Sect. 3]
; fix such target and let
K
be the constant of expansion. Then, invoking Theorem
C
, we see that for
\nu
-almost every
\underline{c}
,

\textrm{diam}F_{c_{1}}\circ\dots\circ F_{c_{n}}(D)\leq K^{-\sqrt[3]{n}}\textrm{diam}D,

which is summable, as desired.

Items
(b)
and
(c)
are a reformulation of Theorems
A
and
B
in the language of codes. Indeed, let us note that hairs are precisely cluster sets
[
13
, Sect. 6]
, and its dynamics is governed by the inner function. Moreover, there is a distinguished target
E=\varphi^{*}(\left[i,-i\right])=\psi^{*}(\left[-1,1\right])
, characterized by the following property. When a hair hits this target (note that all the hair hit the target at the same time, because points in the hair have all the same symbolic dynamics), it changes from being in
\Omega_{0}\coloneqq\partial U\cap\mathbb{H}_{+}
to
\Omega_{1}\coloneqq\partial U\cap\mathbb{H}_{-}
(or viceversa) in the next iteration. Hairs which are not in the target remain in
\Omega_{i}
. Thus, the target is the ‘changing zone’. In terms of codes. Thus, understanding probabilistically when hairs hit
E
can be translated in understanding the asymptotic distribution of
H_{n}
,
B_{n}
and
L_{n}
, as we show next.

By definition,
H_{n}
is the set of codes whose first block is of length
n
. This is equivalent to ask for the first entrance to the set
E
for hairs which are outside
E
(or, equivalently, how much time remain outside
E
), and this is described by Theorem
A
(c)
.

Moreover,

is the number of blocks of
\underline{c}_{n}
. This is precisely the number of times the hair of code
\underline{c}
visits

up to time

(a)
. Finally

stands for the length of the last block of
\underline{c}_{n}
. The last block is determined by the last time we visited

(

in the notation of Theorem

L_{n}(\underline{c}_{n})=1-\frac{Z_{n}E}{n}

and the distribution is given by Theorem

(c)
.

Appendix A
Centered Inner Functions

An inner function satisfying that
g(0)=0
is called a
centered inner function
. For centered inner functions, the Lebesgue measure
\lambda
is invariant and ergodic under
g^{*}
. As a consequence, the ergodic properties of
g^{*}

can be studied very easily (at least compared with doubly parabolic ones). Establishing these ergodic properties is the goal of the present section.

Note that, by conjugating
g
by an appropriate automorphism of
\mathbb{D}
, we are actually considering all inner functions having a fixed point in
\mathbb{D}
.

Moreover, it is well-known that a centered inner function is uniformly
expanding on the unit circle:

\inf_{\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}}|g^{\prime}(\xi)|>1,

where we use the convention that
|g^{\prime}(\xi)|=\infty
if
g
does not have
an angular derivative at
\xi
(see
[
20
, Thm. 4.15]
).

A.1.
Background on Ergodic Theory

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space, and
T\colon X\to X
be a measure-preserving transformation. Assume
\mu
is a probability measure.

Theorem A.1
.

(Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem,
[
26
, Corol. 8.2.14]
)

Let
T\colon X\to X
be a measure-preserving transformation on a probability space
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
. Assume
T
is ergodic with respect to
\mu
, and
h\in L^{1}(\mu)
. Then, for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
,

\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}h(T^{k}(x))=\int_{X}hd\mu.

Theorem A.2
.

(Kac’s Lemma,
[
26
, Lemma 10.2.6]
)

Let
T\colon X\to X
be a measure-preserving transformation on a probability space
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
. Assume
T
is ergodic with respect to
\mu
. Let
E\subset X
be a set of positive measure, and let

E_{n}\coloneqq\left\{x\in E\colon\tau_{E}(x)=n\right\},

where
\tau_{E}(x)\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the first return time of
x\in E
to
E
. Then,

\sum_{n}n\cdot\mu(E_{n})=\mu(E).

Remark A.3
.

We notice that the statement of Theorem
A.2
is not exactly the one in
[
26
, Lemma 10.2.6]
, but it can be deduced straighforward from it. Indeed,
T
is ergodic and invariant (and hence, conservative) with respect to
\mu
, every set of positive measure is absorbing
[
26
, Corol. 10.1.14]
. Since
[
26
, Lemma 10.2.6]
is stated in terms of absorbing sets for measure-preserving transformations (not assumed to be ergodic), we see that it holds for any set of positive measure, if we assume that the measure is finite and ergodic.

A.2.
Ergodic properties of centered inner functions

The following theorem is analogous to Theorem
A
, but for centered inner functions. Notice the simplicity of its proof compared with the one of Theorem
A
.

Theorem A.4
.

(Ergodic properties of centered inner functions)
Let
g\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
be a centered inner function.

(a)

(Occupation times) For every arc
E\subset\partial\mathbb{D}
, let

S_{n}E(x)=\#\left\{0\leq k\leq n-1:T^{k}(x)\in E\right\}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{E}\circ T^{k}(x).

Then,

\lim\limits_{n}\frac{S_{n}E(x)}{n}=\lambda(E)

for
\lambda
-almost every
x\in\partial U
.

(b)

(First return times) Let
E\subset\partial\mathbb{D}
be an arc, and let

E_{n}\coloneqq\left\{x\in E\colon\tau_{E}(x)=n\right\},

where
\tau_{E}(x)\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the first return time of
x\in E
to
E
. Then,

\sum_{n}n\cdot\lambda(E_{n})=\lambda(E).

(c)

(Waiting times) Let
p\in\partial\mathbb{D}
be a regular fixed point (i.e.
g
is holomorphic on a neighbourhood of
p
, and
g(p)=p
). Let
G\subset\partial\mathbb{D}
be an open arc,
p\in G
, and let

G_{n}\coloneqq\left\{\xi\in G\colon g^{*}(\xi),\dots,(g^{*})^{n-1}(\xi)\in G\right\}.

Then,
\lambda(G_{n})\sim C^{n}
, for some constant
C
,
0<C<1
.

Proof.

Applying Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem
A.1
with
h=\mathbbm{1}_{E}
, we deduce that

S_{n}E(x)=\#\left\{0\leq k\leq n-1:T^{k}(x)\in E\right\}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{E}\circ T^{k}(x),\hskip 8.5359pt\lim\limits_{n}\frac{S_{n}E(x)}{n}=\lambda(E),

for
\lambda
-almost every
x\in\partial U
, as stated in
(a)
. Kac’s Lemma
A.2
gives in a direct way the estimate of the first return times given in
(b)
.

To prove
(c)
, assume without loss of generality that
G\subset\partial\mathbb{D}
is an open arc,
p\in G
, such that
g|_{G}
is holomorphic. Then, the Lebesgue measure of the arcs

G_{n}\coloneqq\left\{\xi\in G\colon g(\xi),\dots,g^{n-1}(\xi)\in G\right\},

decays exponentially (this can be easily seen by using linearizing coordinates around the fixed point), as desired. Note that, since
g
is holomorphic in
G
, the image
g(\xi)
is well-defined for
\xi\in G
, and coincides with
g^{*}(\xi)
. This ends the proof of Theorem
A.4
.
∎

A.3.
Ergodic properties of one component centered inner functions

As usual when dealing with backward orbits, we consider
Rohklin’s natural extension
. The set

\widetilde{\partial\mathbb{D}}\coloneqq\left\{\left\{\xi_{n}\right\}_{n}\subset\partial\mathbb{D}\colon g^{*}(\xi_{n+1})=\xi_{n},n\geq 0\right\}

is the space of backward orbits, and
\widetilde{g^{*}}\colon\widetilde{\partial\mathbb{D}}\to\widetilde{\partial\mathbb{D}}
is defined by

\widetilde{g^{*}}(\left\{\xi_{n}\right\}_{n})=g^{*}(\xi_{0})\xi_{0}\xi_{1}...

Let
\pi_{j}(\left\{\xi_{n}\right\}_{n})=\xi_{j}
; then, one can define a measure
\widetilde{\lambda}
as the one satisfying
\widetilde{\lambda}(\pi_{j}^{-1}(A))=\lambda(A)
, for every
A\subset\partial U
measurable. Since
\lambda
is
g^{*}
-invariant and ergodic, the measure
\widetilde{\lambda}
is
\widetilde{g^{*}}
-invariant and ergodic (see e.g.
[
26
, Sect. 8.5]
).

Note that, for the radial extension of a one component inner function, all backward orbits are conformal, in the following sense.

Lemma A.5
.

(Inverse branches are well-defined)

Let
g
be a one component centered inner function. Let
\left\{\xi_{n}\right\}_{n}\subset\partial\mathbb{D}
be a bakward orbit. Then, the inverse branches
G_{n}
of
g^{n}
,
G_{n}(\xi_{0})=\xi_{n}
are well-defined in
D(\xi_{0},r)
, for some
r>0
.

Proof.

The lemma follows easily from an inductive construction of the required inverse branches. Take
r>0
such that there are no singular values in
\mathbb{D}\smallsetminus D(0,r)
(the existence of such
r>0
follows from the assumption of
g
being one component). Then, the existence of the first inverse branch
G_{1}
in
D(\xi_{0},r)
is given. Note that
G_{1}(D(\xi_{0},r))\subset D(\xi_{1},r)
, since
\left|g^{\prime}(\zeta)\right|>1
,
\zeta\in D(\xi,r)
, for all
\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}
. Since all inverse branches if
g
are well-defined in
D(\xi_{1},r)
, one deduces that
G_{2}
is well-defined in
D(\xi_{0},r)
. The construction of
G_{n}
follows by induction.
∎

It follows from
[
9
, Sect. 2.4]
that, for centered inner functions, periodic points are dense on the unit circle.
Moreover, the following proposition gives a more precise control on the dynamics, from a more quantitative point of view.

Proposition A.6
.

(Backward orbits and periodic points)
Let
g
be a one component inner centered function.

(a)

(Contraction of inverse branches)
There exists
K>1
and
r>0
, such that for
\widetilde{\lambda}
-almost every backward orbit
\left\{\xi_{n}\right\}_{n}
,
r_{0}\in(0,r)
, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

G_{n}(D(\xi_{0},r_{0}))\subset D(\xi_{n},K^{-{n}}r_{0}),

where
G_{n}
is the branch of
g^{-n}
sending
\xi_{0}
to
\xi_{n}
.

(b)

(Bound on the period of periodic boundary points) Assume
g
has degree
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}
and
r>0
. Then, there exist a periodic point
p\in D(\xi,r)\cap\partial\mathbb{D}
of period at most

\frac{-\ln r}{\ln d}+N+1,

where
N
depends only on
g
.

Proof.

To prove (a), note that the fact that the branches
G_{n}
are well-defined folows from the pevious lemma, and it is only left to prove the contraction estimates, which follows from Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem.
Indeed, let
E\subset\partial\mathbb{D}
be an arc in which
\left|g^{\prime}\right|\geq K>1
.
Since
\widetilde{\lambda}
is
\widetilde{g^{*}}
-invariant and ergodic, one can apply Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem
A.1
, as in Theorem
A.4
(a)
, to the indicator function of
E
, to get that for
\widetilde{\lambda}
-almost every backward orbit
\left\{\xi_{n}\right\}_{n}

\lim\limits_{n}\frac{\#\left\{0\leq k\leq n-1:\xi_{k}\in E\right\}}{n}=\lambda(E).

Thus,
\#\left\{0\leq k\leq n-1:x_{k}\in E\right\}\sim n
. Since each time we hit the target
E
, the contraction factor is
K
, we get the desired result.

To prove (b), note that
g|_{\partial\mathbb{D}}
is conjugate to
z\mapsto z^{d}
on
\partial\mathbb{D}
, and
\left|g^{\prime}\right|\geq K>1
on
\partial\mathbb{D}
. Let
N
be such that
K^{N}>4
.
For

n=\frac{-\ln r}{\ln d},

we have

\lambda(g^{n}(D(\xi,r)))=1.

In particular, there exists
y\in\partial\mathbb{D}\cap D(\xi,r/2)
such that
g^{n+N+1}(y)\in D(y,r/4)
(this follows from the conjugacy on
\partial\mathbb{D}
to
z\mapsto z^{d}
and the fact that
\lambda(f^{n}(D(\xi,r)))=1
, so all its images have also full measure, meaning that there are preimages of all points in
D(\xi,r)
).

Then, let
G_{n+N+1}
be the inverse branch of
g^{n+N+1}
sending
g^{n+N+1}(y)
to
y
, which is well-defined in
D(\xi,r)
. Moreover, since
g^{n+N+1}(y)\in D(y,r/4)
and
K^{N}>4
, it follows that

G_{n+N+1}(D(\xi,r))\subset(D(G_{n+N+1}(\xi),r/4))\subset D(G_{n+N+1}(\xi),r/2)\subset D(\xi,r).

This already implies that there is a periodic point of period in
D(\xi,r)
, as desired.
∎
