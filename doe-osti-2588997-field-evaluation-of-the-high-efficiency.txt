Executive Summary
The High Efficiency Dehumidification System (HEDS) technology from Conservant Systems
Inc., installed at the Timken Museum of Art in San Diego, California, was evaluated to
determine its performance relative to appropriate baseline operation. Data was collected for
measurement and verification for several weeks during three evaluation periods: Summer (Aug-
Sep 2023), Winter (Dec 2023-Feb 2024) and Spring (May-Jun 2024). The evaluation included
operating the heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC) system in both constant air
volume (CAV) and variable air volume (VAV) modes, with and without the HEDS energy
recovery and HVAC system optimization technology enabled. The electricity consumption of the
chiller and the gas-supplied reheat energy were measured to characterize savings achieved by the
HEDS operation. Based on these measurements, the HEDS was responsible for chiller electrical
load savings during the summer evaluation period of 39% and 42% for the CAV and VAV
operating modes, respectively; 97% and 100% chiller electrical load reductions were observed
during the winter evaluation for the CAV and VAV operating modes, respectively; and the
corresponding reductions during the spring evaluation were 42% and 52% for CAV and VAV
operation, respectively. The measured reheat energy reductions, which are typically provided by
natural gas, due to the HEDS during summer were 64% and 97% in CAV and VAV operating
modes, respectively, while the corresponding values were 99% and 78% during the winter
evaluation, and 59% and 56% during the spring evaluation.

The measurements indicated that if a conventional CAV system were replaced with a VAV
system configuration with HEDS, the chiller power consumption could be reduced by
approximately 72%, 100% and 80% during summer, winter and spring measurement periods,
respectively. The gas consumption for reheat in this scenario could be reduced by 99% in
summer, 86% in winter and 82% in spring.

Background – Many legacy HVAC systems, which are generally the largest energy consumer in
commercial buildings, have traditionally operated in CAV mode, but most new and retrofitted
HVAC systems today are designed to operate in VAV mode to save energy in low load
conditions. The HEDS, which can be implemented in either CAV or VAV systems, was created
to help the Department of Defense solve HVAC-related problems such as poor indoor air quality
(IAQ), poor environmental control, excessive electrical & fossil fuel energy use, biological
growth and corrosion. The HEDS technology includes two high performance counter-flow heat
exchanger coils to dehumidify air and subsequently recover much of the low-quality heat
generated in the cooling and dehumidification process, reducing the load on the chiller while
simultaneously limiting the need for reheat of the air.

The HEDS also uses advanced HVAC system optimization control strategies designed to
minimize energy waste that is common in many legacy systems that operate the cooling coil to
maintain a fixed supply air dry bulb temperature setpoint. The dehumidification and heat
recovery coils combined with the optimized controls result in precise and efficient humidity and
temperature control that automatically responds to changes in ambient temperature, humidity,
occupancy and other operating conditions to minimize energy use while maintaining occupant
comfort.

 ix

 Field Evaluation of the High Efficiency
 Dehumidification System (HEDS) at the
 Timken Museum of Art
The National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) completed analysis of data collected at the
Timken Museum of Art in San Diego, California as an assessment of the system performance of
the Conservant Systems High Efficiency Dehumidification System (HEDS). Data was collected
for measurement and verification (M&V) for several weeks during three evaluation periods:
Summer (Aug-Sep 2023), Winter (Dec 2023-Feb 2024) and Spring (May-Jun 2024), while
operating the Timken Museum’s HVAC system in several operating modes. This report provides
details of the M&V measurements and analysis of data. Comparisons of the performance of the
HEDS to different baseline configurations are provided to indicate the benefits that can be
expected of the HEDS technology.

1 Description of the HEDS
Figure 1 shows the configuration of the air handling unit (AHU) for the HEDS system. A
primary feature of the HEDS is the use of a specially designed high efficiency cooling coil (CC
in the schematic) and cooling recovery coil (CRC) that operate together to perform
dehumidification and reheat of the supply air stream. HEDS reduces the cooling loads that must
be served by the chiller plant and offers the potential to strongly reduce or eliminate the need for
additional reheat energy while providing effective relative humidity (RH) control.

 Figure 1. Schematic showing the HEDS system configuration

 1

For the conditions shown in Fig. 1, the air is cooled in the cooling coil from 71.06 °F (Air_inlet)
to 53.68 °F (CC Drybulb) while the chilled water is heated from 48.8 °F (CHWS) to 64.5 °F
(CRC_inlet) while passing through the CC. This dehumidifies the air but leaves it in an
overcooled state for temperature control. Both the CC and CRC coils employ a counterflow
configuration for maximum effectiveness, and the CC outlet water temperature is greater than the
CRC entering air temperature. The CRC therefore uses the warmed-up return water from the CC
outlet at 64.5 °F to reheat the air. The chilled water is cooled back to 59.3 °F (CRC_outlet)
while flowing through the CRC, while the outlet air from the cooling coil is reheated from 53.68
°F to 61.3 °F (CRC DA-T).

The normal HEDS operation is similar to a variable air volume (VAV) HVAC system with the
addition of the recovery coil and optimized control strategies described below. The proprietary
control system is designed to control the HVAC operating parameters to maintain temperature
and humidity within specified levels while minimizing energy consumption and maximizing
occupant comfort.

1.1 Controls and Optimization of the HEDS Operation
To significantly increase annual HVAC system savings, the HEDS unit is equipped with a
factory installed, tested and commissioned HVAC optimization software package that resets the
HVAC system setpoints to minimize energy use and promote stable and occupant friendly
operation.

The setpoints being reset include the following:
• supply air dry bulb temperature,
• supply air dewpoint temperature,
• supply air static pressure,
• chilled water supply temperature,
• chilled water distribution loop differential pressure,
• hot water supply temperature,
• hot water distribution loop differential pressure.
The HEDS HVAC system optimization software package continually evaluates the conditions at
the loads and determines whether the loads are increasing or decreasing, evaluates the distance
between the actual conditions and the desired conditions and how long the controlled variables
have been away from their desired conditions, and then creates small changes in the setpoints,
with updates taking place every minute. As a variable moves further away from its desired
value, the changes are larger, as the variable gets closer to its desired value, the changes are
smaller.

As a part of the HEDS HVAC optimization software, there is functionality for “Thermal
Flywheel” operation that contributes to significant chiller and boiler system runtime reductions.
The Thermal Flywheel software evaluates current conditions and those experienced during the
previous day to determine whether it is possible to shut the boiler or chiller systems off and use
stored energy in the building to “float” through a period, by allowing a slightly larger deviation
from the desired conditions for a short period of time. The ability to maintain space conditions

 2

without the chiller or boiler systems running creates significant operational savings, as well as
reducing equipment maintenance needs.

2 Measurement and Verification (M&V) Results
For the performance assessment at the Timken Museum, it is desirable to compare the normal
operation of the HEDS with other conventional HVAC systems. The Timken Museum’s HVAC
system was operated during part of the evaluation in a manner consistent with how a typical
constant air volume (CAV) system would be run, while for other test periods it was run in a
manner consistent with operation of a conventional VAV system.

The HEDS energy recovery coil and HVAC optimization software were alternately enabled and
disabled for periods of both the CAV and VAV system operation to allow comparisons between
different baseline configurations and the optimized HEDS operating modes. Although some
system design features of the HEDS AHU unit are different than what would normally be
implemented in conventional CAV or VAV systems, the comparisons between these four
operating modes (CAV baseline, CAV HEDS, VAV baseline, and VAV HEDS) provide a
reasonable estimation of differences in energy use that can be expected between the operation of
the different HVAC system configurations.

2.1.1.1 Data Validation
Water and air side heat balance

To validate the heat transfer calculations, we compared the air side and water side heat transfer
rates at each time step to validate the recorded values. To quantify the two metrics, we compared
the water side thermal power to air side thermal power for VAV mode and to enthalpy change

 3

for CAV mode as the latter has constant airflow. The normalized air side and water side thermal
powers for the CAV and VAV operating modes are shown in Figure 2. Since thermal losses from
the coils are expected to be very minimal, these heat transfer values should closely match. We
see that the air side thermal power aligns better to the water side in the CAV operating modes,
when the air flow rate is constant, compared to the VAV modes when the air flow is frequently
adjusted. This is likely due to inaccuracies in the air flow rates which are difficult to measure
precisely since there are often significant flow variations through the cross section of the flow
chamber, and calibration of the air flow sensors is also known to be challenging. The water side
heat transfer is considered to be more accurate and is used to characterize the heat transfer
performance of the coils, while the air side heat transfer rates are used only to provide a general
verification of the water side rates. The accuracy in the CAV modes (with higher air flow rates
that are maintained constant) is more stable and matches the water side heat transfer to within
approximately 10%. There are additional uncertainties in the humidity reading (about 5%
accuracy), which impacts the air side enthalpy calculation, so the 10% difference and very
consistent trends in the data give confidence that the measurements are reasonable.

2.1.1.2 Analysis of Load Metrics
Figure 3 shows a comparison of several key performance metrics recorded during the M&V that
characterize the HVAC system operation in the different operating modes. It is evident that the
different operating modes have quite different characteristics, which consequently result in
differences in energy use. A clear difference is apparent in the nature of the zone and supply air
temperatures for the VAV HEDS mode compared to the other three modes. The average zone
temperature is maintained nearly constant at about 70 °F in modes other than VAV HEDS, while
the controls in that mode accept a setback in the range of 67-72 °F. The supply air temperature is
a direct result of the dewpoint set point and CRC operation, so the HEDS supply air temperature
variations are also a result of the control strategy. Note that the estimated VAV box supply air
temperature is calculated based on an energy balance using the HEDS supply air temperature, the
VAV reheat coil load and the total air supply flow rate.

According to Conservant literature, the reheat coil recovers 20-40% of the energy extracted from
the air during the cooling and dehumidification process. The air reheating that occurs using the
energy recovery reheat coil (CRC) directly avoids the need to provide reheating through other
means, which can reduce or eliminate natural gas use for reheat.

Furthermore, the same amount of energy returned to the air stream by the recovery coil is
removed from the chilled water stream, yielding a lower chilled water return (CHWR)
temperature and thus a reduced load on the chiller, which provides additional energy savings.

An important objective of NREL’s research is to quantify the energy savings in the boiler and
chiller due to the HEDS operation. The M&V effort included evaluating several load metrics
that characterize various energy transfers in the HVAC system, and these metrics were calculated
for each operating mode.

2.1.3 Estimated Energy Savings due to HEDS HVAC Optimization Controls

The HEDS operation yields energy savings from both the cooling recovery coil operation and the
optimized control strategies employed. Complex interactions between temperature setpoints at
different locations in the system, variations in air flow rate, changing environmental conditions,
and other factors affecting the loads make it challenging to understand what the relative impacts
are from the HEDS-optimized controls and the energy recovery reheat coil.

To differentiate the savings due to the HEDS HVAC optimization software from those due to
reheat in the cooling recovery coil (CRC), we performed an analysis to estimate operation of the
HEDS in CAV and VAV operating modes with the same optimized control settings but without
the CRC energy recovery activated. This hypothetical operation corresponds to the hardware
configuration used for the CAV and VAV baseline configurations but without the CRC coil, so
comparisons between the “HEDS without CRC” modes and the baseline configurations provide
an indication of the energy savings that result from the automated control strategies employed by
the HEDS operation. With the same control settings, the zone conditions are assumed to remain
the same as in the normal HEDS mode, so the Space_load, Fan_power and Misc_load values
remain identical. With CRC operation deactivated, however, the chilled water load and VAV
reheat coil energy would be increased to achieve the same operating conditions. Consequently,
the chiller electrical power required would also increase proportionally to the chilled water load.
This analysis allows us to estimate the portion of the load reductions that were achieved as a
result of the HEDS-optimized control settings separately from those due to the CRC operation.

2.2.2 Key Observations of Performance Among the 5 M&V Operating Periods
• The HEDS HVAC optimization system creates a significant shift in the HVAC system’s
 behavior in period (2) vs. period (1) when HEDS unit is running based on dewpoint setpoint,
 even though both periods are being operated in the VAV with HEDS mode of operation.
 The optimized transition out of the dehumidification mode (period 1) into the cooling mode
 (period 2) eliminates the need for dewpoint control for the cooling coil. This allows the
 cooling coil and unit supply air temperatures to be increased, reducing, and even eliminating,
 the need for the chiller to operate. This change in operation was due to changing weather
 conditions identified by the logic contained in the HEDS HVAC optimization code while
 operating in an automatic optimization mode. When the mixed air dewpoint remained below
 50 °F and ambient, return air and space conditions allowed, the HEDS optimization code
 switched to mode 8 – cooling without dehumidification. It remained in this state during the
 HEDS operation throughout the remainder of the evaluation period.
• Due to the stark difference in performance between periods (1) and (2), and given that the
 remaining operating modes do not have dehumidification needs, performance comparisons
 are only made using the mode 8 operating period for the VAV HEDS operation.
• Without a dehumidification call, the CRC coil has no impact on the energy usage of the
 system, as seen in periods (2) and (4), during which the CRC_water_reheat is 0.
• At low air flow rates, the VAV reheat coil had to run to compensate for the heat loss during
 M&V operating period (2). However, during M&V operating period (4), the heat gain from
 the fan at full power (18.5 kW) was enough to entirely cover the heat losses from the system,
 so no additional reheat was needed at the zone terminals.

 21

• During M&V operating periods (2) and (4), the cooling coil is rarely turned on since the
 HEDS controller does not request dehumidification, as discussed above, and the supply
 temperature setpoint is maintained above the zone temperature by the HEDS controller. This
 allows recirculated air to reduce the amount of heat required at the reheat coils to control the
 zone temperatures. In the corresponding baseline modes (3 and 5), the supply temperature
 setpoint remains in the 50-55 °F range. This difference is the main reason for significant
 chilled water consumption during baseline modes even though the overall Space_load and
 Misc_load are negative.
 This result shows that almost all the chilled water thermal power provided during M&V
 operating period (3) and about 75% of chilled water usage in M&V operating period (5) are
 not satisfying a required load, i.e. the chiller operation is unnecessary and results in a large
 amount of wasted energy during these times. As evidence, the chilled water energy is almost
 identical to the reheat energy in period (3): 86,929 vs 88,167 W, respectively. In addition,
 during operating period (4), the HEDS supply air temperature (HEDSsupplyAir) is always
 higher than the average space temperature MeanAirTemp, which clearly shows that it is
 effectively in a heating, not cooling, mode.
While the simple control strategies employed in both CAV operating modes (3) and (4) are
typical of most legacy systems, the wasted energy is significant. While it is possible to change
setpoints during a season when dehumidification is no longer needed, this requires regular
monitoring, and random and/or frequent weather changes will not generally result in operational
changes if there is no impact on occupant comfort. This real-world operational data reinforces
the need to equip HVAC systems with optimization systems that can effectively evaluate internal
and ambient conditions and automatically adjust system setpoints to minimize energy waste.

Control strategies that over-emphasize energy savings, however, can have negative consequences
for comfort, so it is critical to ensure a balance between saving energy and meeting the comfort
needs of building occupants. Occupant complaints may be handled by the operating engineers
by manually overriding the fan systems and the cooling and heating system setpoints, which can
eliminate the energy efficiency benefits of an optimization system.

It is noted that the baseline operating conditions used during the M&V were selected to reflect
how real CAV and VAV systems are often operated. If a Building Management System (BMS)
or Direct Digital Control (DDC) system is not in use, or it is not equipped with some form of an
after-market, 3rd party HVAC optimization platform that covers the chillers, boilers, AHUs and
cooling towers (if so equipped), and their associated pumping systems, for example in a legacy
HVAC system, it is not uncommon for facility operators to configure the HVAC system so that it
continues to operate the cooling coil to control to a fixed supply air dry bulb temperature
setpoint, and allow the heating system to control the temperature in the conditioned spaces,
particularly for a temperature and humidity sensitive application such as a museum.

The HVAC operation may be modified manually during different seasons or when there is a
significant and enduring change in weather conditions, but if active controls are not in place to
automatically adjust the setpoints in real time, it is generally considered too burdensome for the
facility operator to regularly make changes to operating setpoints as weather, building occupancy
and other conditions change at the facility.

2.2.4 Creation of Alternate Baseline Metrics
1. CAV alternate baseline metrics

The baseline CAV mode used in this evaluation was selected to represent the operation of many
legacy systems that apply VAV reheat following dehumidification. Based on ASHRAE 90.1-
2013 section 6.5.2, the current baseline for CAV mode (3) does not comply with zone control
requirements. To allow a comparison of the measured performance of the HEDS with CAV
operation that meets current standards, we generated an alternate baseline data set from the
existing data to match 90.1 requirements, as follows:

• A maximum flow rate of 50% of the rated zone supply flowrate is allowed when
 simultaneous heating and cooling take place (i.e. VAV reheat following cooling) as required
 for dehumidification and relative humidity control.
• The supply air temperature should reset at minimum 25% difference between design supply
 and zone temperature setpoints to adjust to changing conditions like outdoor temperature or
 building load.
Note that in several applications, including hospitals, laboratories and many manufacturing
sites, constant airflow is required. The ASHRAE 90.1 standard allows exceptions for cases
where application specific requirements dictate different operating conditions than the standard
specifies, so the alternate baseline case will not be relevant to such applications.

 23

For the alternate baseline cases, the heating and cooling calls are determined based on the value
of Space_load. The originally calculated values of Space_load and Misc_load are used for the
alternate baseline calculations, but Fan_Power, HEDS_water_side_load, and VAV_reheat_load
are recalculated. The calculation workflow for determining the new alternate baseline metrics is
as follows:
• The heating or cooling call is based on whether the Space_load value is positive (cooling) or
 negative (heating).
• During a cooling call, if reheat is employed the following calculation is applied:
 o If the air flow rate is higher than 50% of the rated flow rate of 12,000 cfm, then
 Alt_CC_water_side_load is calculated assuming an air flow rate of 50% of the
 maximum rated flow, and a 25% setback reset of the HEDS_supply_Air_temp is
 applied. This results in the following:
 Alt_CC_water_side_load = 0.5 * (flow_rate_max/flow_rate) *
                                                      (1-0.25) * CC_water_side_load              (16)
 o At 50% flow rate (6000cfm), Alt_Fan_W is approximately 5000 W, as observed
 during other VAV mode measurements:
                                        Alt_Fan_power = 5000.0                                   (17)
 Alt_CC_waterside load is (always) higher than Space_load (see Eq. (15)), so we
 need to add reheat to compensate for any overcooling that takes place. The
 ventilation load (Misc_load) is always negative during winter also.
• During a heating call (if SpaceLoad is negative):
 o The fan can run at full flow rate during a heating call.
 o The cooling coil is turned off unless the fan heat gain is higher than the sum of
 Space_load_loss and Misc_load_loss.
• For both modes:
 o VAV reheat should run to compensate for the remaining heat loss after
 subtracting the Alt_Fan power and Space_load / Misc_load gains.

for the VAV HEDS relative to the alternate CAV-VAV hybrid baseline is unexpected, it is a

1. Water and air side heat balance review

Using a similar validation method as described in the summer and winter data evaluation
periods, we verified that the M&V data gives similar results for the heat transfer calculated using
data for the air side and water side flows. The plots in Fig. 16 show very good alignment of the
trends between the thermal power metrics for the CAV mode, with the calculated heat transfer
for the air and water side flows differing by less than 5%. Very similar to what we observed with
the previous data sets, there were larger discrepancies in the calculated heat transfer for the VAV
mode, as shown in Fig. 17. This is expected, due to the changes in air flow rate through the coils
for the VAV operating mode and the uncertainties of the air flow rate and air humidity
measurements. These results provide confidence that the data measurements used to calculate the
heat flows through the HEDS are reliable.

2. Controllability

As indicated in Table 10, the HVAC system was running with fixed setpoints, without
automatically controlled and optimized dehumidification control during M&V operating periods
2 and 3 (VAV baseline and CAV baseline). The relative humidity (RH) at the zone level always

tolerances, the baseline operation without HEDS was controlled based on a fixed supply air
setpoint of 50.5 °F. This is about halfway between the HEDS dynamically optimized dewpoint
setpoint, which varied between approximately 49 °F and 52.5 °F during HEDS periods, as seen

in Fig. 18. As a result of the baseline operation utilizing fixed setpoints and not including the
HEDS optimized dewpoint and supply air temperature setpoint logic, cooling loads delivered to
the zones during the baseline periods were greater than during the HEDS operating periods even
though the system controller indicates that no dehumidification is required. This is to be
expected, as properly optimized HVAC systems will use less energy than HVAC systems that
are operated without continuous optimization. The zone RH in the humidity-controlled area
(Gallery) was approximately 5% lower in baseline compared to HEDS modes.

3 Conclusions from the Timken HEDS Evaluation
The evaluation of the HEDS at the Timken Museum of Art included running the HVAC system
in both CAV and VAV operating modes, with and without the HEDS energy recovery and
HVAC system optimization software enabled. The data obtained during these measurements

 33

showed significant energy savings relative to the baseline operating modes in both CAV and
VAV operation.
The HEDS optimization software and CRC recovery coil enable highly efficient operation of the
HVAC system. The electricity consumption of the chiller and the gas-supplied reheat energy
were measured to characterize savings achieved by the HEDS operation.
Based on these measurements, the HEDS was responsible for chiller electrical load savings
during the summer evaluation period of 39% and 42% for the CAV and VAV operating modes,
respectively; 97% and 100% chiller electrical load reductions were observed during the winter
evaluation for the CAV and VAV operating modes, respectively; and the corresponding
reductions during the spring evaluation were 42% and 52% for CAV and VAV operation,
respectively. The measured reheat energy reductions, which are typically provided by natural
gas, due to the HEDS during summer were 64% and 97% in CAV and VAV operating modes,
respectively, while the corresponding values were 99% and 78% during the winter evaluation,
and 59% and 56% during the spring evaluation.
The results from this study indicated that replacing a conventional CAV system with a VAV
system configuration with HEDS could reduce the chiller power consumption by approximately
72%, 100% and 80% during summer, winter and spring measurement periods, respectively. The
gas consumption for reheat was reduced by 99% in summer, 86% in winter and 82% in spring.
Although measurements were not performed prior to installation of the HEDS at the Timken
Museum for direct comparison, these results are consistent with energy cost reductions that the
museum experienced following the installation of the HEDS, which provides additional
validation of the performance.
These results clearly indicate consistent and significant energy savings due to the HEDS
operation in a wide variety of conditions at the Timken Museum, and the energy recovery and
HVAC optimization aspects of the HEDS technology can be expected to provide benefits for a
variety of facility types and in a variety of climates. It is recommended that future evaluations be
conducted in other operating scenarios to quantify the benefits in different climates and building
types.
These results highlight the benefits of the HEDS energy recovery system and the need for HVAC
equipment to be furnished with factory installed, tuned, tested and commissioned HVAC
optimization code. The HEDS use of the HVAC optimization system and the thermal flywheel
code allows the “High Efficiency Dehumidification System” to save significant amounts of
energy throughout the year, even when dehumidification is not required.
