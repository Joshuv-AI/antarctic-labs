From Architecture to Output: Structural Origins of Hallucination in Large Language Models and the Amplifying Role of Data
Md. Rejaul Korim Sadi 1 , Toufiqur Rahman Tasin 1 , Golam Mostofa Naeem 1

Abstract
Large language models hallucinate—producing fluent, confident, factually wrong outputs—with a consistency that persists across generations and scales. Existing taxonomies classify hallucination by output type, distinguishing intrinsic from extrinsic failures and faithfulness from factuality divergence. These frameworks are descriptively rigorous but do not identify which internal mechanism produced a given instance. This paper analyses hallucination as a structural consequence of three architectural decisions that together form a compound failure system. Self-attention’s co-occurrence learning substitutes statistical proximity for semantic meaning and produces entity confusion, fact misattribution, and semantic drift. The maximum likelihood estimation training objective optimises next-token probability without factual constraint, rewarding statistically plausible outputs regardless of their truth value. Autoregressive decoding’s permanent left-to-right commitment under exposure bias ensures that a single wrong token cascades forward through the entire output sequence without revision. Dataset pathologies—long-tail deficiencies, training bias, and synthetic pollution—amplify these vulnerabilities but do not independently cause them. We make three contributions. First, we map each mechanism to a specific output category in the Alansari and Luqman taxonomy, locating intrinsic hallucination in self-attention, extrinsic hallucination in MLE, and logical inconsistency in autoregressive decoding. Second, we show that each commonly cited dataset pathology exploits one of these mechanisms rather than originating hallucination independently. Third, we identify the diagnostic limitation of output-type-only classification and contrast it with inference-layer mitigation approaches.



Large language models produce fluent, confident, factually wrong outputs with a consistency that no amount of scaling has resolved. This failure—hallucination—has been documented across model generations, measured across benchmarks, and taxonomised across surveys. Alansari and Luqman

This paper argues that the description is insufficient. Hallucination is not a surface phenomenon that varies randomly across outputs. It is a structural consequence of specific architectural decisions—decisions that create the conditions for error regardless of dataset quality, model scale, or inference strategy. Until the field can identify which architectural mechanism is responsible for a given hallucination instance, intervention will remain necessarily indirect: managing outputs rather than addressing origins.

Three mechanisms constitute the structural origin this paper establishes. The first is self-attention, introduced by Vaswani et al.

I
Prior Hallucination Surveys and Their Shared Limitation

Research into hallucination in large language models has produced a growing body of taxonomic work. Alansari and Luqman
[
1
]
offer one of the most comprehensive recent surveys, classifying hallucinated outputs as either intrinsic—where the model contradicts information present in the input context—or extrinsic—where the output cannot be verified against any external source of truth. Huang et al.
[
2
]
draw a parallel distinction between faithfulness and factuality, identifying where model outputs diverge from the source material and where they diverge from established fact. Both frameworks are valuable. Both share the same structural limitation.

These taxonomies describe what kind of hallucination occurred. They do not identify which internal mechanism produced it. A survey can classify an output as extrinsic hallucination with precision—and still leave entirely open the question of which architectural decision made that output possible. The classification tells you the symptom. It does not locate the cause.

This is not a minor gap. A taxonomy that cannot identify the source of an error cannot guide targeted intervention. A diagnostic framework that identifies the site of failure—not only its visible output characteristics—enables interventions that target the mechanism rather than the symptom. Prior surveys have produced rigorous descriptions of hallucination as an output phenomenon. What remains less developed is the structural question: which architectural mechanisms are the necessary conditions that make hallucination possible in the first place. That is the question this paper addresses.

II
Architectural Mechanisms: A Compound Failure System

Transformer-based large language models are built on three architectural decisions that together constitute the structural origin of hallucination. Each decision carries an implicit assumption about how language and meaning relate. Each assumption breaks under specific conditions. And each failure produces a distinct and identifiable type of hallucinated output.

The first mechanism is self-attention. Vaswani et al.
[
3
]
introduced the attention mechanism as a method of weighting relationships between tokens across an input sequence. In practice, the mechanism learns these relationships from statistical co-occurrence in the training corpus—tokens that appear together frequently develop strong associative weights. The implicit assumption is that statistical proximity approximates semantic relationship. It does not. When two tokens are strongly associated by co-occurrence, but their relationship does not hold in a new context, the model applies the learned association regardless. Liu et al.
[
8
]
demonstrated empirically that this positional and distributional bias produces measurable failures—models lose track of relevant information when it appears in the middle of a long context, attending instead to positional edges. Attention does not read for meaning. It reads for pattern. That structural property is the origin of entity confusion, fact misattribution, and semantic drift.

The second mechanism is the maximum likelihood estimation training objective. Brown et al.
[
4
]
document that language models at scale are trained to maximise the probability of the next token given its preceding context—across the entire training corpus, without factual constraint. The implicit assumption is that a statistically plausible output is a satisfactory output. It is not. Lin et al.
[
9
]
measured the consequence of this directly: larger models trained under MLE are less truthful than smaller ones, not more. As the model becomes better at learning the training distribution, it becomes better at reproducing its falsehoods. High-frequency false patterns in the corpus are learned with greater confidence than low-frequency true ones. MLE does not distinguish between the two. Fluency is optimised. Truth is never the objective.

The third mechanism is autoregressive decoding. The left-to-right token generation process commits permanently to each generated token, which then serves as input context for all subsequent tokens. Ranzato et al.
[
5
]
identified the structural vulnerability this creates—exposure bias. During training, the model is always fed correct ground-truth tokens at each step and never encounters its own errors. At inference, it must consume its own outputs. The moment a wrong token is generated, that token becomes the directional context for everything that follows. The model was never trained to recover from this situation. One committed error does not remain isolated—it propagates forward and compounds, producing outputs that are internally coherent but factually broken from the point of the first error onward.

These three mechanisms do not operate independently. They form a compound failure system, as illustrated in Fig.
1
and Fig.
2
. Attention produces the wrong associative context. MLE trains the model to reproduce statistically frequent patterns regardless of their truth. Autoregressive decoding ensures that once a wrong pattern is committed, the entire subsequent output follows it. Dataset pathologies do not create this system—they exploit it. That causal structure is the central argument of this paper.

III
Self-Attention Diffusion as Structural Hallucination Origin

The first architectural mechanism responsible for hallucination in large language models is the self-attention mechanism introduced by Vaswani et al.
[
3
]
. To understand why attention produces hallucination structurally, it is necessary to understand precisely what it learns—and what it does not.

The self-attention mechanism computes:

\text{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)\!V

(1)

where
Q
,
K
, and
V
denote the query, key, and value matrices respectively, and
d_{k}
is the key dimension used as a scaling factor. The product
QK^{\top}
encodes co-occurrence strength between token pairs. No factual constraint appears anywhere in this formulation.

The full multi-head form, as specified by Vaswani et al.
[
3
]
, extends this to
h
parallel attention heads:

\text{MultiHead}(Q,K,V)=\text{Concat}(\text{head}_{1},\ldots,\text{head}_{h})\,W^{O}

(2)

where each
\text{head}_{i}=\text{Attention}(QW_{i}^{Q},KW_{i}^{K},VW_{i}^{V})
. Multiple heads allow the model to attend to different co-occurrence patterns simultaneously, compounding the structural vulnerability across
h
parallel processes
[
3
,
10
]
.

Self-attention operates by computing a weighted relationship between every token in a sequence and every other token. Those weights are not derived from semantic understanding. They are derived from statistical co-occurrence patterns learned across the training corpus. A token that frequently appears near another token in training develops a strong associative weight with it. The mechanism has no access to causal structure, no representation of real-world relationships, and no capacity to evaluate whether a learned association is contextually valid. It learns what tends to appear together. It applies that learning everywhere.

The implicit assumption embedded in this design is that statistical proximity approximates semantic relationship. In the majority of cases, this assumption holds well enough to produce fluent and apparently coherent output. But it is an approximation—and approximations break. When two tokens share a strong co-occurrence pattern in training but their relationship does not hold in a specific new context, the mechanism applies the learned association regardless. It has no mechanism for detecting the mismatch. It generates from the wrong relationship with the same confidence it would generate from the correct one.

Liu et al.
[
8
]
demonstrated empirically that this distributional bias extends to positional behaviour. Across multi-document question answering and key-value retrieval tasks, model performance followed a consistent U-shaped curve—strongest when relevant information appeared at the beginning or end of the input context, and significantly degraded when it appeared in the middle. The attention mechanism, rather than weighting tokens by semantic relevance, was dominated by positional and distributional frequency. The model could not reliably find what mattered. It attended to what was statistically familiar.

The failures this mechanism produces are identifiable and consistent. Entity confusion occurs when two entities share strong co-occurrence patterns—the model applies an association from one context to another where it does not hold, generating a wrong entity with full confidence. Fact misattribution occurs when a property strongly associated with one subject is transferred to another because both subjects appear frequently in similar contexts. Semantic drift occurs when a chain of plausible co-occurrence steps moves the output progressively further from the correct referent—each individual association defensible, the cumulative result wrong. Fig.
3
illustrates this with a concrete example: tokens for a regional city and a commodity historically associated with that region develop strong co-occurrence weights during training, but when the model encounters a query about the city in a different context, the learned association fires regardless—producing a confident but factually wrong output.

These are not random errors. They are structurally produced by a mechanism that substitutes statistical co-occurrence for semantic understanding. The attention mechanism operates as designed. It was designed to learn patterns, not meanings. What it produces when those patterns do not hold is hallucination—and that hallucination is a structural property of the mechanism, not an artefact of insufficient data.

Right: at inference, a query about Sylhet triggers the high-weight co-occurrence pattern in a context where it does not hold, and the model generates a factually wrong output with full confidence. The mechanism provides no mismatch detection. The mathematical formulation at the bottom confirms that no factual constraint exists in the attention computation.

IV
Autoregressive Decoding: Exposure Bias and Cascade Failures

The second architectural mechanism responsible for hallucination is autoregressive decoding—the left-to-right token generation process that governs how large language models produce output. Where self-attention determines what relationships the model draws on, autoregressive decoding determines how those relationships are committed to in sequence. The structural vulnerability here is not in what the model knows. It is in how permanently it acts on what it knows.

Autoregressive generation factorises the joint sequence probability left-to-right:

P(x_{1:T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}P(x_{t}\mid x_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1})

(3)

Each token
x_{t}
is permanently committed once generated. Exposure bias arises because the training distribution conditions on ground-truth prefixes
x^{*}_{<t}
(where
x^{*}
denotes ground-truth tokens), while inference must condition on the model’s own prior outputs
\hat{x}_{<t}
(where
\hat{x}
denotes model-generated tokens):

P_{\text{train}}(x_{t}\mid x^{*}_{<t})\neq P_{\text{infer}}(x_{t}\mid\hat{x}_{<t})

(4)

This discrepancy is the structural source of cascade failure, as visualised in Fig.
4
.

Autoregressive generation proceeds one token at a time. Each token is selected based on the probability distribution over the vocabulary conditioned on all preceding tokens. Once selected, that token is permanent. It becomes part of the context that conditions every subsequent token. The architecture provides no mechanism for revision, no capacity to look ahead and evaluate whether the current token will lead the output in a correct direction, and no ability to return to an earlier decision point and correct it. Generation is a one-way commitment.

The structural vulnerability this creates was identified by Ranzato et al.
[
5
]
as exposure bias. During training, autoregressive models are trained under teacher forcing—at every step, the model is fed the correct ground-truth token as input, regardless of what it would have generated itself. The model is therefore never exposed to its own errors during training. It never learns to recover from a wrong token because the training procedure ensures it never encounters one. At inference, this changes entirely. The model must consume its own previously generated tokens as input. The training condition and the inference condition are structurally different—and the model was only ever prepared for one of them.

The consequence is cascade failure. When the model generates a wrong token at inference—and given the vulnerabilities of self-attention and MLE, it will—that wrong token immediately becomes the input context for the next prediction. The model does not flag the error. It does not pause. It conditions its next token on the wrong token as if it were correct, because it has no mechanism to do otherwise. The second token, now conditioned on a wrong foundation, is itself more likely to be wrong. Each subsequent token reinforces and extends the direction established by the first error. The output remains internally fluent—the autoregressive mechanism is doing exactly what it was designed to do—but it is built entirely on a wrong foundation from the point of the first committed error onward.

This failure pattern produces what Alansari and Luqman
[
1
]
classify as logical inconsistency—outputs that are internally coherent and linguistically fluent but factually broken in a way that traces back to a single point of failure early in the generation sequence.

This is not a fabricated fact in the way extrinsic hallucination is. It is a structurally coherent wrong direction—a cascade that the architecture cannot interrupt once it has begun. A model trained on a perfectly clean corpus would still carry exposure bias. The cascade mechanism is a property of the decoding architecture itself. Dataset pathologies can worsen the frequency with which the first wrong token appears—but they do not create the cascade. The architecture does.

Holtzman et al.
[
11
]
demonstrate that deterministic decoding strategies compound this cascade—producing degenerate, repetitive outputs that reflect the structural commitment problem at inference scale.

V
The MLE Objective: Fluency Over Factuality

The third architectural mechanism responsible for hallucination is the maximum likelihood estimation training objective. Where self-attention determines what relationships the model learns from context, and autoregressive decoding determines how those relationships are committed to in sequence, MLE determines what the model is fundamentally rewarded for producing. It is the training objective that sits beneath both other mechanisms—and it is the objective that makes dataset pathologies dangerous.

The MLE training objective minimises the negative log-likelihood:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLE}}=-\sum_{t}\log P_{\theta}(x_{t}\mid x_{1},\ldots,x_{t-1})

(5)

where
\theta
denotes the model parameters and
x_{t}
the token at position
t
. The objective maximises next-token probability across all positions. Truth is not present in the loss function.

Maximum likelihood estimation trains a language model to maximise the probability of the next token given all preceding tokens, across the entire training corpus. At every training step, the model receives a reward signal proportional to how well it predicted the actual next token in the training data. The objective is precisely defined and mathematically coherent. What it does not contain—anywhere in its formulation—is a constraint on factual accuracy. A token that is statistically likely given its context receives a high reward whether it is factually true or factually false. A fluent falsehood and a fluent truth are treated identically by the objective, provided both match the training distribution. The model learns to be fluent. It is never trained to be true.

Brown et al.
[
4
]
document this objective at scale in GPT-3—a model trained under MLE across a corpus of such size that the statistical patterns it learned encompass the full breadth of human-generated text, including its errors, its misconceptions, and its falsehoods. The implicit assumption in this design is that a statistically representative sample of human language is a reliable approximation of truth. It is not. Human language contains confident falsehoods, repeated misconceptions, and systematically biased patterns—and MLE learns all of them with the same fidelity it learns correct information. High-frequency false patterns are learned with greater confidence than low-frequency true ones, because frequency is the only signal the objective responds to.

Lin et al.
[
9
]
measured this consequence directly. Across 817 questions designed specifically to surface imitative falsehoods—false answers that exist in the training distribution and were statistically reinforced by MLE—the best available model achieved truthfulness on only 58% of questions, against a human baseline of 94%. The finding that makes this result structurally significant for this paper is the inverse scaling result: larger models trained under MLE were less truthful than smaller ones, not more. As a model’s capacity to learn the training distribution increases, its capacity to reproduce that distribution’s falsehoods increases with it. Scaling the model scales the problem. The model is not failing despite its training. It is succeeding at exactly what MLE specified. Fig.
5
illustrates the core asymmetry: common falsehoods receive nearly equal probability mass as common truths, while rare truths are systematically suppressed. Fig.
6
presents the TruthfulQA result for GPT-3-175B against the human baseline.

This is the mechanism that makes dataset pathologies dangerous—and it is the bridge between this paper’s architecture argument and the dataset amplification sections that follow. MLE does not merely permit dataset pathologies to influence the model. It actively rewards the model for learning them. Long-tail deficiencies in the training data—where rare truths appear with low frequency—are systematically under-weighted by MLE’s frequency-driven objective, producing confident wrong answers where correct ones were statistically scarce
[
12
]
. Training bias in the corpus feeds skewed co-occurrence patterns directly into the MLE reward signal, reinforcing those biases with every training step
[
13
]
. Synthetic pollution from prior model generations introduces hallucinated patterns into the training distribution, and MLE learns those hallucinated patterns as faithfully as it learns anything else, compounding errors recursively across model generations
[
12
]
. In every case, the dataset pathology does not independently cause hallucination. MLE converts it into hallucination by rewarding the model for learning it.

In the taxonomy of Alansari and Luqman
[
1
]
, MLE failures map primarily to extrinsic hallucination—outputs that cannot be verified against external facts because the model learned to reproduce statistically frequent patterns rather than factually grounded ones.

The three mechanisms together—self-attention’s co-occurrence learning, autoregressive decoding’s cascade commitment, and MLE’s frequency-driven objective—do not operate as independent failure modes. They form a compound system. Attention produces wrong associative context. MLE trains the model to reproduce statistically frequent patterns from that context regardless of their truth. Autoregressive decoding ensures that once a wrong pattern is committed, the entire subsequent output is built on it. All three are necessary conditions in the compound system as currently implemented: no deployed large language model removes any of the three, which is consistent with why hallucination persists across model generations and scales. That is the structural argument of this paper.

VI
Empirical Validation of the Three Mechanisms

To test whether the three architectural mechanisms identified in Sections III–V produce measurable failures in practice, we conducted a set of controlled experiments on GPT-2
[
14
]
, a decoder-only autoregressive transformer trained under the MLE objective. GPT-2 was selected because it implements all three mechanisms in their canonical form and its weights are publicly accessible, allowing direct inspection of attention distributions and token-level probabilities. The experiments are not intended as a benchmark comparison; they are designed to surface each mechanism’s structural failure mode under controlled prompting conditions.

VI-A

Mechanism 1: Self-Attention Co-occurrence Misfires

We constructed 15 prompts designed to trigger learned co-occurrence associations in contexts where those associations do not hold. Each prompt paired a correct factual completion with a statistically adjacent but incorrect completion (e.g., “Albert Einstein was born in

”—correct: Ulm, Germany; co-occurrence wrong: Princeton). We used greedy decoding to isolate the model’s highest-probability continuation.

GPT-2 produced factually correct completions in 2 of 15 prompts (13.3%) and co-occurrence misfires in 13 of 15 prompts (86.7%). Representative failures include “Shakespeare was born in 1829 in the village of Bury St.” and “Python programming language was created by the late Paul Graham”—outputs that are fluent, syntactically well-formed, and factually wrong. These are not random errors; they reflect the mechanism’s structural substitution of co-occurrence strength for semantic grounding, as argued in Section III.

VI-B

Mechanism 2: MLE Frequency Over Factuality

To test MLE’s indifference to factual accuracy, we constructed 5 pairs of matched statements—one common falsehood widely repeated in text corpora, one verified factual correction—and computed the negative log-likelihood assigned by GPT-2 to each. If MLE were sensitive to truth, verified facts should receive systematically higher probability mass. If MLE rewards statistical frequency rather than truth, the scores should be comparable or favour the falsehoods.

GPT-2 assigned higher probability to the common falsehood in 3 of 5 pairs, including “We only use 10 percent of our brains” (falsehood preferred by 0.56 log-probability units) and “Lightning never strikes the same place twice” (falsehood preferred by 0.93 units). In the remaining 2 pairs, the verified truth won by margins of 0.03 and 0.48 log-probability units respectively—near-equivalent scoring that is itself evidence of the central claim: MLE does not systematically distinguish truth from falsehood. It distinguishes frequent from infrequent. Figure
9
presents the full result.

The near-equivalent scoring across pairs is consistent with MLE’s structural indifference to factual accuracy, as argued in Section V.

VI-C

Mechanism 3: Autoregressive Cascade Under Exposure Bias

To test whether a single committed wrong token propagates forward through the generated sequence, we paired prompts differing only in a single factual token (e.g., “The capital of France is
Paris
, which…” versus “The capital of France is
Berlin
, which…”) and allowed GPT-2 to complete each under greedy decoding. If autoregressive decoding were robust to local errors, continuations should remain factually anchored to the surrounding context. If exposure bias causes cascade failure, the wrong committed token should redirect the generated sequence into a fabricated but internally coherent trajectory.

Across 5 tested pairs, cascade failure was observed in 4 of 5. With the correct prefix, GPT-2 continued with “Paris, which is home to the French capital’s largest city, the Louvre.” With the single wrong token substituted, the same model continued: “Berlin, which is home to the world’s largest concentration of German Jews.” Similarly, “Earth orbits the Moon” produced “the Moon is the only planet in the solar system that orbits the Sun”—an output that is grammatically fluent, internally coherent, and compounds the original error into further factual breakage. These are precisely the properties Section IV attributes to cascade under exposure bias: the architecture does not flag the wrong token, does not recover from it, and instead builds subsequent tokens on its committed foundation.

In one pair (“Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius” versus “Water boils at 20 degrees Celsius”), GPT-2 produced identical continuations regardless of the prefix—the surrounding context was semantically strong enough that the wrong token did not redirect the trajectory. This observation is itself informative: it is consistent with the compound-system argument of Section II, in which attention-based co-occurrence (Mechanism 1) can in some cases dominate over autoregressive commitment (Mechanism 3). The mechanisms are not independent; they interact, and the interaction can under some conditions suppress individual failure modes.

VI-D

Empirical Summary

Across the three experiments, each mechanism exhibited the structural failure mode argued for in Sections III–V under controlled prompting. The experiments are limited in scope—one model (GPT-2), small prompt counts, and greedy decoding—and are not presented as a large-scale benchmark. Their purpose is to demonstrate that the mechanisms identified theoretically are observable in practice on a transformer architecture where all three are present. A broader empirical programme across model scales and decoding strategies is a direction for future work that this framework would support.

VII
Long-Tail Deficiencies: Rare Facts Lost Under the MLE Objective

The first dataset pathology that amplifies the architectural vulnerabilities established in Section V is long-tail deficiency—the systematic under-representation of rare, low-frequency facts in large language model training corpora. To understand why this pathology produces hallucination, it is necessary to understand how training data is distributed and what the MLE objective does when it encounters that distribution.

Training corpora assembled at scale are not uniform. They are heavily skewed toward dominant sources—majority languages, mainstream publications, frequently discussed entities, and high-volume domains. The facts, entities, and relationships that appear frequently in these sources accumulate high token co-occurrence frequencies across the corpus. Facts that exist at the margins of the distribution—specific events in underrepresented languages, niche domain knowledge, low-profile entities, historically excluded communities—appear with comparatively low frequency. They occupy what is known as the long tail of the data distribution: present, but sparse.

Under the MLE objective, this distributional asymmetry becomes a systematic bias in what the model learns. As established in Section V, MLE trains the model to maximise the probability of the next token across the training corpus. Frequency is the only signal the objective responds to. A fact that appears frequently receives high probability mass—the model learns it confidently. A fact that appears rarely receives low probability mass—the model learns it weakly, if at all. Shumailov et al.
[
12
]
demonstrate that this narrowing is not incidental. As training progresses, the model progressively over-indexes on high-probability patterns and under-weights low-probability ones, causing the tails of the distribution to effectively vanish from the model’s learned representation. The rare fact is not stored incorrectly—it is not stored at all with sufficient confidence to be retrieved reliably.

The consequence at inference is precise and consistent. When a query requires a long-tail fact—a low-frequency truth that the model encountered rarely during training—the model does not produce uncertainty. It does not generate a blank or a refusal. It generates the highest-probability pattern available given the query context, which is a frequent fact that is statistically adjacent but factually wrong. The model produces the wrong answer with the same fluent confidence it produces correct answers. The MLE objective trained it to do exactly this—maximise probability given context—and it does so faithfully, regardless of whether the most probable output is true.

This is the amplification mechanism that Shumailov et al.
[
12
]
describe. Long-tail deficiency does not introduce a new failure mode into the architecture. It exploits the vulnerability that Section V established—MLE’s indifference to factual accuracy in favour of statistical frequency. Without MLE’s frequency-driven objective, a gap in the training data would manifest as uncertainty or low-confidence output. With MLE as the training objective, that same gap produces a confident wrong answer. The dataset pathology supplies the sparsity. The architecture converts it into hallucination. The dataset does not act alone. It amplifies what MLE already structurally enables.

In the taxonomy of Alansari and Luqman
[
1
]
, long-tail failures map directly to extrinsic hallucination—outputs that cannot be verified against external facts because the model learned to generate statistically frequent approximations where factually accurate responses were too sparse to be learned reliably. This is the hallucination type that is most resistant to scaling—because, as Lin et al.
[
9
]
demonstrated, larger models trained under MLE learn the distribution more completely, which means they learn its long-tail gaps more completely too. Scaling does not resolve long-tail deficiency. It inherits it at greater fidelity.

VIII
Training Bias Exploiting Attention Co-occurrence Learning

The second dataset pathology that amplifies the architectural vulnerabilities established in this paper is training bias—the systematic skew toward dominant perspectives, majority cultures, and hegemonic associations that is embedded in large-scale training corpora. Where long-tail deficiency exploits the MLE objective’s indifference to frequency, training bias exploits a different and earlier vulnerability: the self-attention mechanism’s co-occurrence learning established in Section III.

Training corpora assembled from internet-scale sources are not neutral collections of human knowledge. They reflect the distributional reality of who produces text, in which languages, about which subjects, and from which cultural positions. Majority languages dominate by volume. Mainstream perspectives dominate by frequency. The associations embedded in these texts—between concepts, entities, identities, and properties—are not balanced representations of reality. They are statistical artefacts of the corpus composition. When the self-attention mechanism learns co-occurrence patterns from this corpus, it learns these biased associations with the same fidelity it learns any other pattern. Attention does not evaluate whether a co-occurrence is culturally contingent or universally valid. It records what appeared together. It applies what it recorded.

Bender et al.
[
13
]
articulate this concern directly: language models trained on internet-scale corpora inevitably encode the biases present in those corpora, and the sheer volume of training data does not correct for systematic skew—it reinforces it. The model does not see more perspectives at scale. It sees the dominant perspectives more often.

The consequence for hallucination is specific and traceable to the attention mechanism. When a query involves an entity, concept, or relationship that is underrepresented or misrepresented in the training corpus, the attention mechanism retrieves the co-occurrence patterns it learned—which are the biased patterns from the dominant distribution. The model generates output that reflects the training bias, not the queried reality. This is not a failure of the attention mechanism in any engineering sense. The mechanism is operating as designed—learning and applying co-occurrence patterns. The failure is that the patterns it was given to learn were systematically skewed.

This failure mode—where biased pretraining patterns bleed into generated responses through the attention mechanism’s co-occurrence learning—produces outputs that are confidently wrong about underrepresented subjects in ways that contradict what a correctly informed response would contain, because its attention weights were shaped by a corpus that did not represent that information accurately. Huang et al.
[
2
]
identify pre-training architectural vulnerabilities, including what they term attention glitches, as contributors to hallucination in transformer-based language models. The correction required is not architectural—the attention mechanism is operating as designed. The correction is distributional: the co-occurrence patterns that attention learns must reflect the world more accurately. But that correction does not eliminate the structural vulnerability that Section III identifies. It only reduces the frequency with which the vulnerability fires.

IX
Synthetic Pollution and the Model Collapse Cycle

The third dataset pathology that amplifies the architectural vulnerabilities established in this paper is synthetic pollution—the introduction of AI-generated content into training corpora, and the recursive degradation that follows when successive model generations train on data that increasingly reflects prior model errors rather than human-grounded reality. Where long-tail deficiency exploits the MLE objective and training bias exploits the self-attention mechanism, synthetic pollution is the only dataset pathology that exploits all three architectural mechanisms simultaneously—and compounds across generations.

The mechanism begins with a straightforward observation. As large language models are deployed at scale, the volume of AI-generated text on the internet grows. Web-scraped training corpora assembled for subsequent model generations inevitably contain outputs produced by prior models. Those outputs are not neutral additions to the training distribution. They are approximations of reality—shaped by the architectural vulnerabilities already established in this paper. Outputs generated by attention’s co-occurrence learning, committed through autoregressive decoding’s left-to-right cascade, and rewarded by MLE’s indifference to factual accuracy. When those outputs enter the training corpus of the next model generation, their errors enter the training distribution as if they were ground truth.

Shumailov et al.
[
12
]
document the consequence of this process rigorously. When generative models are trained on data that includes outputs from prior model generations, a recursive feedback loop forms. The new model learns the previous model’s approximation errors as valid patterns. Its own outputs then carry those errors forward, compounding them with new approximation errors introduced by its own architectural vulnerabilities. Each successive training generation slightly over-represents the high-probability outputs of the previous generation and under-represents rare content—progressively erasing the tails of the original human data distribution. Shumailov et al. call this model collapse: a recursive narrowing of the model’s learned distribution until it becomes increasingly disconnected from the reality the original training data was drawn from.

The connection to Section IV’s autoregressive cascade is direct and precise. When synthetic training data contains hallucinated sequences—outputs where an early wrong token cascaded forward through the autoregressive mechanism into a coherent but factually broken output—the next model generation trains on those cascades as valid generation patterns. The cascade structure itself is learned. At inference, the new model does not merely reproduce the original hallucination. It reproduces the pattern of cascading—the structural tendency to commit to a wrong direction and follow it fluently to the end. Shumailov et al.
[
12
]
identify this as the critical mechanism: synthetic pollution does not introduce a new architectural failure. It feeds the autoregressive cascade’s structural vulnerability with training data that was itself produced by that same cascade in a prior generation. The architecture learns to cascade from data that was already cascaded.

The connection to the MLE objective compounds this further. As established in Section V, MLE learns every pattern in the training distribution with equal fidelity—it does not distinguish between human-generated truth and model-generated approximation. When synthetic content enters the corpus, MLE assigns probability mass to the errors it contains with the same process it assigns probability mass to correct information. High-frequency errors in synthetic data—patterns that appeared consistently across many model-generated documents—accumulate high probability mass and are learned confidently. Low-frequency truths that were already underrepresented in the original corpus become even more marginalised as synthetic content dilutes their relative frequency further, compounding the distributional skew already present in internet-scale corpora
[
13
]
. This process, which Shumailov et al.
[
12
]
frame as approximation error compounding across generations, produces small errors in the first model generation that become magnified in the second, shifting the model’s internal representation progressively further from the original human data distribution.

The consequence at late-stage collapse, as Shumailov et al.
[
12
]
document, is a model producing high-confidence output from an increasingly narrow, distorted distribution—one that reflects what prior model generations believed rather than what the world contains. Rare facts, minority knowledge, and low-frequency truths are the first casualties. The model does not know they have been lost. It generates from what remains in its distribution with full confidence. In the taxonomy of Alansari and Luqman
[
1
]
, synthetic pollution produces both extrinsic hallucination—outputs unverifiable against external facts because the relevant facts have been erased from the learned distribution—and logical inconsistency—cascade patterns learned from prior model outputs that produce coherent but factually broken generation sequences.

Synthetic pollution is therefore the dataset pathology with the widest architectural reach. It does not exploit one mechanism selectively—it feeds corrupted data into the self-attention co-occurrence lattice, the MLE training objective, and the autoregressive generation process simultaneously. Each mechanism amplifies the corruption according to its own structural vulnerability. Attention learns biased co-occurrence patterns from synthetic outputs. MLE assigns high probability mass to frequently recurring synthetic errors. Autoregressive decoding commits to those errors and cascades them forward. The dataset does not create any of these mechanisms. It gives all three of them progressively worse material to work with across every generation of training.

X
Discussion

The preceding sections have established three architectural mechanisms as the structural origin of hallucination in large language models: self-attention’s co-occurrence learning, the MLE training objective’s indifference to factual accuracy, and autoregressive decoding’s cascade commitment under exposure bias. Sections VII through IX have demonstrated that dataset pathologies do not independently produce hallucination—they amplify vulnerabilities the architecture already contains, through the specific mechanisms those sections identify. What remains is the question the literature has not yet fully addressed: if the mechanisms are known and their failure modes are identifiable, why does the dominant taxonomy of hallucination not reflect them?

The most comprehensive recent survey of hallucination in large language models—Alansari and Luqman
[
1
]
—classifies hallucinated outputs as either intrinsic, where the model contradicts information present in its own input context, or extrinsic, where the output cannot be verified against any external source of truth. Huang et al.
[
2
]
draw a parallel distinction between faithfulness and factuality, identifying failures that diverge from source material and failures that diverge from established fact. Both frameworks are analytically rigorous. Both are consistently applied. And both share the same structural limitation: they describe what kind of hallucination occurred. They do not identify which architectural mechanism produced it.

This is not a minor gap in the taxonomy. It is a diagnostic gap. A classification system that can tell you an output is extrinsic hallucination with precision—and still leave entirely open the question of which architectural decision made that output possible—cannot guide targeted intervention. It locates the symptom. It does not locate the cause. To intervene effectively in a system as complex as a large language model, the diagnostic framework must be capable of pointing to the specific architectural site of failure, not only to its visible output characteristics.

The three mechanisms established in this paper map onto the existing taxonomy in ways that make this gap concrete. Extrinsic hallucination—output unverifiable against external facts—maps primarily to the MLE training objective. As Section V established and Lin et al.
[
9
]
measured directly, MLE trains the model to reproduce statistically frequent patterns regardless of their factual grounding. The model produces unverifiable outputs not because it lacks access to truth, but because truth was never the objective it was trained to optimise. Radford et al.
[
14
]
and Brown et al.
[
4
]
document this objective across successive model generations at increasing scale; Kaplan et al.
[
15
]
demonstrate that scaling the model scales its capacity to learn the training distribution, including its falsehoods, with greater fidelity. Extrinsic hallucination does not diminish with scale. It is inherited at greater precision. The MLE objective is the mechanism responsible—and the existing taxonomy does not name it.

Intrinsic hallucination—output that contradicts information present in the model’s own input context—maps primarily to self-attention’s co-occurrence learning. As Section III established and Liu et al.

measured empirically, the attention mechanism does not weight tokens by semantic relevance. It weights them by positional and distributional frequency. When a learned co-occurrence pattern fires in a context where it does not hold, the model contradicts its own input with full confidence—not from reasoning failure, but from the structural substitution of statistical proximity for meaning. Elhage et al.

provide a mathematical framework for understanding these internal circuits, demonstrating that specific attention heads can be identified as responsible for particular associative behaviours—supporting the observation that the architectural operator can, in principle, be named. The attention mechanism is the responsible operator. The existing taxonomy classifies the output correctly. It does not name the mechanism.

Logical inconsistency—output that is internally coherent but factually broken from a specific point forward—maps to autoregressive decoding’s cascade under exposure bias. As Section IV established and Ranzato et al.
[
5
]
identified, the left-to-right commitment mechanism provides no capacity for revision once a wrong token is committed. The cascade that follows is a structural property of the decoding architecture, not a dataset failure.

The intrinsic/extrinsic classification, faithfulness and factuality distinction—these are valid descriptive frameworks. What they do not provide is what a diagnostic framework requires: the ability to identify the architectural operator responsible for a specific hallucination instance. Without that identification, intervention remains necessarily indirect. Prompting strategies, chain-of-thought elicitation, and retrieval augmentation—approaches documented by Wei et al.
[
7
]
and others—address hallucination at the inference level without engaging the structural mechanisms that make it possible. They manage the symptom. They do not locate the cause.

What this analysis adds is a mechanism-level correspondence: an instance classified as extrinsic hallucination and produced by MLE optimisation is not the same intervention target as an instance classified as intrinsic hallucination and produced by attention co-occurrence failure, even when both outputs look equally wrong to the end user. Recognising this correspondence is consistent with recent work on interpretability—Elhage et al.

demonstrate that specific attention heads can be identified with particular associative behaviours—and provides a basis on which mechanism-level analysis can be combined with output-level classification.

The mechanisms are identifiable. Their failure modes are traceable. A classification that incorporates both the output category and the responsible mechanism offers a more complete picture of how a given hallucinated output was produced than either axis alone.

XI
Conclusion

This paper has argued that hallucination in large language models is not primarily a data problem. It is a structural consequence of three architectural decisions that together form a compound failure system: self-attention’s substitution of statistical co-occurrence for semantic meaning, the MLE training objective’s optimisation of statistical plausibility without factual constraint, and autoregressive decoding’s permanent left-to-right commitment under exposure bias. Each mechanism carries an implicit assumption about how language and meaning relate. Each assumption breaks under specific and identifiable conditions. And each failure produces a distinct, traceable type of hallucinated output.

Vaswani et al.
[
3
]
introduced an attention mechanism that learns co-occurrence, not causation. Liu et al.
[
8
]
measured where it fails. The mechanism does not weight tokens by what they mean; it weights them by how often they appeared together. That is not a semantic operation, however fluent its outputs may seem. It is a statistical one—and statistical operations produce statistical errors when the patterns they learned do not hold. Those errors are what the field calls hallucination. They are not anomalies. They are the expected output of a mechanism that was never designed to distinguish co-occurrence from truth.

The MLE objective, as this paper has established through Brown et al.
[
4
]
and the empirical measurement of Lin et al.
[
9
]
, compounds this. A training objective that rewards the model for producing the most statistically probable next token—without any factual constraint—does not produce a model that knows what is true. It produces a model that knows what is frequent. When what is frequent is also true, the output is correct. When what is frequent is false, the output is a confident, fluent falsehood. MLE does not distinguish between these outcomes. It was never designed to.

Autoregressive decoding, as Ranzato et al.
[
5
]
identified and this paper has extended, ensures that errors produced by the first two mechanisms are not isolated. They cascade. A wrong token becomes the foundation for every subsequent token. The architecture provides no mechanism for revision, no capacity for the model to recognise that it has committed to a wrong direction. The output remains fluent. The error propagates. These three decisions do not operate independently. Attention produces wrong associative context. MLE trains the model to reproduce it with confidence. Autoregressive decoding commits to it permanently and cascades it forward. Dataset pathologies do not create this system. They exploit it. That causal order is the central finding of this paper and constitutes the necessary starting point for any diagnostic framework addressing LLM hallucination.

Our analysis suggests that hallucination, understood structurally, is not a single phenomenon but three distinct failure modes with three distinct architectural origins. Self-attention’s co-occurrence learning produces intrinsic hallucination when a learned associative pattern fires in a context where it does not hold. The MLE objective produces extrinsic hallucination when a statistically frequent falsehood receives the same reward as a factually correct output. Autoregressive decoding produces logical inconsistency when a committed wrong token cascades forward through the remainder of the generated sequence under exposure bias. Dataset pathologies—long-tail deficiency, training bias, and synthetic pollution—amplify these failures without originating them.

This structural view is consistent with, and extends, the output-focused taxonomies that have defined prior work. It also clarifies the limits of inference-time mitigation approaches such as Self-Refine
[
6
]
and chain-of-thought prompting
[
7
]
: these methods operate on generated outputs and can improve surface factuality without addressing the structural mechanisms that produced the underlying error, so their effectiveness is bounded by the same architectural vulnerabilities the outputs reflect.

We note several limitations. Our empirical validation (Section VI) is limited to one decoder-only model (GPT-2) under controlled prompting; broader validation across model scales, decoding strategies, and architectures remains a direction for future work. Our scope is limited to decoder-only autoregressive transformers; encoder-decoder and non-autoregressive architectures may exhibit different failure structures. The three mechanisms we identify are not claimed to be exhaustive—fine-tuning dynamics, positional encoding choices, and tokeniser effects may introduce additional structural contributions that this analysis does not cover. A natural direction for future work is the development of architectures and analysis tools in which module-level activity can be inspected at runtime, enabling empirical attribution of observed output errors to specific operators. Existing interpretability work—circuit analysis
[
10
]
, attention attribution, and logit-lens methods—provides partial tools in this direction; extending them into a principled empirical mapping between output errors and the three mechanisms identified here is a concrete research programme that this paper’s framework would support.
