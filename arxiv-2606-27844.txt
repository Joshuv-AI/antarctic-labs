Critical percolation on preferential attachment graphs with infinite variance
Peter Mörters, Lucas Schätze

Abstract
We study the inhomogeneous random graph with preferential attachment kernel and degree distribution with power-law exponent τ ∈ ( 2 , 3 ) \tau\in(2,3) as a representative of the class of graphs of preferential attachment type with infinite variance degrees. Under bond percolation with a positive retention probability independent of the size n n of the graph there is a unique macroscopic component with high probability. We therefore investigate percolation probabilities p n ↓ 0 p_{n}\downarrow 0 . We identify a moving critical window at p c ∼ β n τ − 3 2 ​ τ − 2 p_{c}\sim\beta n^{{}_{\frac{\tau-3}{2\tau-2}}} . Above this window, when p n ≫ p c p_{n}\gg p_{c} , the maximal component has size of order n p τ − 1 3 − τ n np^{{}_{\frac{\tau-1}{3-\tau}}}_{{}_{n}} and it is unique. Below this window, when n 1 1 − τ ≪ p n ≪ p c n^{{}_{\frac{1}{1-\tau}}}\ll p_{n}\ll p_{c} , it is non-unique, star-shaped and has size of order n 1 τ − 1 p n n^{{}_{\frac{1}{\tau-1}}}p_{n} . In the critical window itself, the largest component scaled by n \sqrt{n} converges in distribution to a positive random variable with a law given in terms of a subcritical Norros-Reittu graph. This behaviour is markedly different from that seen for other classes of scale-free graphs and is conjectured to persist throughout the broad class of growing graphs with infinite variance.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background

It is a by now well-established fact in the random graph literature that locally tree-like scale-free random graphs with power-law exponent
\tau
smaller than three only possess a percolative phase. Despite this absence of a non-degenerate phase transition, Cohen, ben-Avraham and Havlin
[
8
]
argue that for models that can locally be approximated by unimodular Galton-Watson trees, percolation critical exponents are
well defined near the limit of extreme dilution and that these exponents bear a strong dependence on
\tau
. These heuristics have been made rigorous for the configuration model
by Dhara, van der Hofstad and van Leeuwarden
[
12
]
. They show in particular that
when the degree distribution satisfies a power-law with exponent

\tau\in(2,3)
then there is a moving critical window at

p_{c}\sim\beta n^{\frac{\tau-3}{\tau-1}}\quad\text{ for some $0<\beta<\infty$.}

For
p_{n}\gg p_{c}
the largest component is of size
np_{n}^{\frac{1}{3-\tau}}
and for

p_{n}\ll p_{c}
it is of size
n^{\frac{1}{\tau-1}}p_{n}
. In the critical window the largest component has size of order
n^{\frac{\tau-2}{\tau-1}}
.
A unique largest component emerges at the end of the critical window (when
\beta\uparrow\infty
), while components have a trivial star-like structure at the bottom of the critical
window (when
\beta\downarrow 0
). Interestingly, this behaviour is partly due to the occurrence of multiple edges in the configuration model.

Bhamidi, Dhara and van der Hofstad
[
4
]
investigate a class of scale-free graphs with infinite variance and rank-one connection probabilities without multiple edges, including the Norros-Reittu model, Chung-Lu model and inhomogeneous random graphs with a product kernel. Here the critical window is at

p_{c}\sim\beta n^{\frac{\tau-3}{2}}\quad\text{ for some $0<\beta<\beta_{c}$.}

The characteristic features of this class in this window are

•

there is a finite (explicitly computable)
\beta_{c}>0
such that for
\beta<\beta_{c}
the largest
component has size of an order depending on
\tau
but strictly smaller than
\sqrt{n}
,

•

scaled by the order this largest component has a random structure related to a preferential attachment-type graph,

•

above the critical window, when
\beta>\beta_{c}
, a unique largest component of size
\sqrt{n}
, the ‘tiny giant’, emerges.

This critical percolation behaviour is conjectured to be universal for rank-one models with infinite variance in the absence of multiple edges.

1.2
Description of our results

We investigate percolation on a class of dynamically growing graphs with infinite variance caused by a preferential attachment mechanism.
In this class there is, for all percolation probabilities
p>0
, with high probability, a unique macroscopic component, which comprises
\sim\theta(p)n
vertices, for some
\theta(p)>0
. However, the
size of this component decays very quickly when
p\downarrow 0
, namely like

\displaystyle\theta(p)=\exp\left(-\frac{c}{p}\,(1+o(1))\right),

(1.1)

for
c=\frac{\tau-2}{\tau-1}
, as shown by the authors in
[
25
]
. It is therefore interesting to couple the percolation probability
p
to the graph size
n
such that
p_{n}\downarrow 0
and ask for a moving critical window in which we see a qualitative change of behaviour in the structure of the largest component.

We show that our models exhibit such a critical window at

p_{c}\sim\beta n^{\frac{\tau-3}{2(\tau-1)}}\quad\text{ for some $0<\beta<\infty$.}

They are in a new universality class, which has not been uncovered so far,
even in the extensive non-rigorous statistical physics literature, see the surveys
[
14
,
21
]
. This universality class is characterised by the following defining features,

•

the largest component has size of order
\sqrt{n}
throughout the critical window. Note that in all previously known model classes the largest component in the critical window has size of order strictly smaller than
\sqrt{n}
,

•

scaled by
\sqrt{n}
the largest component in the critical window has a novel random structure related to a
rank-one graph,

•

above the critical window the largest component is unique and has deterministic asymptotic size of order
larger than
\sqrt{n}
but smaller than
n
, a
‘tiny giant’,

•

below the critical window the largest components are stars of random size of smaller order
than
\sqrt{n}
.

We conjecture that this behaviour is universal across
the broad class of growing graphs
with infinite variance degree distribution, which often arise through some preferential attachment mechanism.

The model we investigate is an inhomogeneous random graph with a preferential attachment kernel, which we define in Section
2.1
. It is a natural and relatively simple representative of a
dynamically growing graph model where preference is given to connections linking young vertices to old ones. Dynamically growing graph models, and in particular preferential attachment type models, tend to have quite different behaviour at the phase boundary compared to the models in the class of the inhomogeneous random graphs with a product kernel studied in
[
4
]
.
This was explored for graphs with finite variance in a recent paper by Jorritsma, Maillard and Mörters
[
20
]
by means of a detailed analysis of the critical behaviour at the phase transition in growing graph models using branching random walk techniques. Their arguments are particularly useful to investigate subcritical and the most delicate near critical behaviour. In the infinite variance case, in the absence of a nondegenerate phase transition, we have to rely on more robust techniques, exploring a more classical path counting approach instead.

We now informally describe our results for inhomogeneous random graphs of preferential attachment type with
\tau\in(2,3)
. These results will be formalized in Theorem
1
in Section
2.2
.

Below the critical window, when

n^{{}_{\frac{1}{1-\tau}}}\ll p_{n}\ll n^{\frac{\tau-3}{2(\tau-1)}}

the largest component arises from a competition of the initial, most powerful vertices. These vertices are typically not connected and have degree of order
n^{\frac{1}{\tau-1}}p_{n}

and the largest components have the same order and contain at least one of these vertices. For smaller
p_{n}
edges are either isolated, when

n^{-1}\ll p_{n}\ll n^{{}_{\frac{1}{1-\tau}}}

or even completely absent when
p_{n}\ll n^{-1}
. In other words, below the critical window the largest components are star-like and not unique in their size.

Throughout the critical window, i.e. when

p_{n}\sim\beta n^{\frac{\tau-3}{2(\tau-1)}},

the maximal connected component has random size of order
\sqrt{n}
and is also not unique in this size. Scaled by
\sqrt{n}
the size of the largest component is random and can be described as the maximum weight component in a subcritical Norros-Reittu graph
\mathscr{G}_{\text{NR}}
with deterministic vertex weights arising from the expected component size of the vertex in the original graph.

Above the critical window the largest component is unique and the size concentrates at a constant multiple of size
np_{{}_{n}}^{{}_{\frac{\tau-1}{3-\tau}}}
. A unique largest component of asymptotically constant (but not macroscopic) size arising above the
critical window is known as ‘tiny giant’. Here it emerges with a size larger than
\sqrt{n}
, in contrast to the other known graph models of infinite variance studied in
[
4
,
12
]
.

1.3
Overview of the proofs

Results on the emergence of large components in random graphs are most often based
on typical behaviour, either globally in terms of high density approximations given by differential equations
[
22
,
3
,
28
]
, or locally given by an exploration process which in the limit takes the form of a random tree
[
6
,
11
,
26
,
20
]
. Neither of these approaches is promising in the case of infinite variance degree distributions when the connectivity behaviour is governed by a relatively small number of vertices of extreme power. We therefore base our analysis on the identification of scales in which a subgraph of hubs, called the core graph, has good connectivity features. The idea of core graphs has been used so far primarily to study distances in supercritical random graphs, see for example
[
23
]
and
[
19
, Section 8.7.1]
, and in
[
4
]
to analyse critical behaviour in rank-one models. We extend the method to growing graph models with infinite variance, where the structural properties of the core and hence the methods of analysis differ considerably compared to the rank-one case.

We now present the ideas of the proof in detail, but without explicit reference to the graph model we use, in order to emphasise the general nature of our approach. Let
\mathscr{G}_{n}
be the graph with
n
vertices, indexed in order of increasing arrival time, after bond percolation with retention probability
p_{n}
. We use two scales to define the
core graph
: Its vertex set consists of the first
m
vertices, and two such vertices are connected by an edge in the core graph if in
\mathscr{G}_{n}
they are connected by a path of
length two with the index of the middle vertex, called a
one-connector
,
at least
sn
. The scales
m
and
s
are chosen
such that the largest component in
\mathscr{G}_{n}
is always given by vertices connected by a path in
\mathscr{G}_{n}
to the largest (or maximal weight) connected component in the core graph. Our proofs are then based on a combinatorial analysis of the structure of these paths eventually showing that the dominant contribution comes from paths of length one.

1.3.1
Above the critical window.

Let

m=\lfloor\mu n\,p_{n}^{{}_{2\frac{\tau-1}{3-\tau}}}\rfloor

and
s=p_{n}^{{}_{\frac{\tau-1}{3-\tau}}}
. The proof relies on variation of the constant
\mu
in the definition of
m
.
Irrespective of this constant,
the core graph has a giant component
\mathscr{C}
consisting of an asymptotically constant proportion of vertices,
see Lemma
3.2
. This component is of course much smaller than the largest component in
\mathscr{G}_{n}
, but we show in Lemma
3.16
that, for sufficiently large
\mu
,
the component of
\mathscr{C}
in
\mathscr{G}_{n}
is the unique largest component. We use path counting arguments to systematically reduce the possible shortest paths leading to
\mathscr{C}
to be essentially just single edges, see Lemmas
3.8
,
3.10
, and
3.13
. The size of the largest component is then obtained to leading order as the sum over the degrees in
\mathscr{G}_{n}
of vertices in
\mathscr{C}
, see Lemma
3.14
. For a core vertex this degree concentrates on the scale
p{{}_{{}_{n}}^{{}_{\frac{1-\tau}{3-\tau}}}}
,
see Lemma
3.3
, and hence the tiny giant has size of order

mp^{{}_{\frac{1-\tau}{3-\tau}}}_{{}_{n}}
, which equals order

n\,p^{{}_{\frac{\tau-1}{3-\tau}}}_{{}_{n}},

with the exact constant obtained in the limit
\mu\to\infty
.

1.3.2
In the critical window.

We choose
m
of constant order and
sn=m+1
.
The largest component in the graph
\mathscr{G}_{n}
restricted to
\{m+1,\ldots,n\}
is still smaller than the largest degree in
\mathscr{G}_{n}
with high probability, see Lemma
3.18
. Hence the largest component overall must contain a vertex in
\{1,\ldots,m\}
.
We analyse the connection probabilities between vertices
i,j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
in the core graph. We show by Poisson approximation arguments that the numbers of one-connectors between vertices
i,j
in the core graph converge to independent Poisson distributed random variables with expectations proportional to

i^{\frac{1}{1-\tau}}j^{\frac{1}{1-\tau}}
, see Lemma
3.19
.
Hence the core graph, for large
m
, approximates an infinite Norros-Reittu graph, which is subcritical as
\frac{1}{\tau-1}<\frac{1}{2}
and the edge density is small. We then show that the number of vertices in
\{m+1,\ldots,n\}
connected by a path to a given vertex
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
concentrates when scaled by
p_{n}n^{\frac{1}{\tau-1}}
around its expectation, which is of
order
j^{\frac{1}{1-\tau}}
,
see Lemma
3.20
. Showing that these paths do not intersect significantly concludes the proof, in Lemma
3.21
.

1.3.3
Below the critical window.

Below the critical window we show that, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the connected components of the vertices in
\{1,\ldots,m\}
are pairwise disjoint
with high probability, see Lemma
3.22
. These components are essentially the direct neighbours of the vertices and
in the regime when
p_{n}\gg n^{\frac{1}{1-\tau}}
the number of direct neighbours scaled by
n^{\frac{1}{\tau-1}}p_{n}
concentrates at its mean,
as before. Otherwise, this is not the case and the number of direct neighbours is random, see Lemma
3.23
. When
p_{n}\ll n^{\frac{1}{1-\tau}}
a vertex has no more than one direct neighbour.

2
Statement of the results

2.1
Inhomogeneous graphs of preferential attachment type.

Inhomogeneous random graphs, as defined by Bollobas, Janson and Riordan in
[
7
]
, see also
[
19
, Chapter 3.2]
or
[
29
]
, are parametrized by a symmetric, continuous kernel

\kappa\colon(0,1]\times(0,1]\to[0,\infty).

Given the kernel, the graph
\mathscr{G}_{n}
has vertex set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
and any pair of distinct vertices
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
is connected by an edge, independently with probability

p_{ij}:=\frac{1}{n}\kappa\Big(\frac{i}{n},\frac{j}{n}\Big)\wedge 1.

In most preferential attachment models the probability at which a vertex arriving at time
j
connects to an earlier vertex
i
is proportional to its degree. This degree is of order
(j/i)^{\gamma}

for some
0\leq\gamma<1

and the proportionality factor therefore inverse to order
\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(j/i)^{\gamma}\sim j
. This motivates us to choose the preferential attachment kernel
\kappa
as

\kappa(x,y)=\beta(x\wedge y)^{-\gamma}(x\vee y)^{\gamma-1},

for some density parameter
\beta>0
and parameter
0\leq\gamma<1
. The resulting graphs
\mathscr{G}_{n}
are called
inhomogeneous random graphs of preferential attachment type
.
They have essentially the same edge probabilities and a slightly simplified covariance structure compared to the classical preferential attachment models, see
[
18
,
19
]
.
For all

\beta<1
the connection probabilities of two vertices
i,j
are explicitly given by

p_{ij}=\beta(i\wedge j)^{-\gamma}(i\vee j)^{\gamma-1}.

The probabilities
(p_{ij})
do not depend on
n

due to the fact that the preferential attachment kernel is homogeneous of index
-1
, which is a characteristic feature of dynamically growing models and in particular of preferential attachment-type graphs. As a result these graphs can also be defined with infinite vertex set
\mathbb{N}
, and in this form have been studied by Durrett and Kesten in
[
15
]
. A version of their model is featuring in the scaling limits of
[
4
]
.

This model has been studied extensively in the case
\gamma=0
under the name Dubins’ model or uniformly grown graph, see for example
[
6
,
20
]
. For
0<\gamma<1
it is easy to check that the graph thus constructed has a power-law degree distribution with exponent

\tau=1+\frac{1}{\gamma}.

When
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
the power-law exponent
\tau
lies
between two and three, a range which is often desired when modelling scale-free networks. Precisely in this case there is no nontrivial phase transition
as the largest component in the graph has macroscopic size no matter how small the edge density, see Dereich and Mörters
[
11
,
24
]
. We study the inhomogeneous random graphs of preferential attachment type in this regime. Percolating this model with a parameter
p
is equivalent to replacing
\beta
by
p\beta
so that, from now on, we only vary the parameter
\beta
and express our results in terms of the model
parameters
\beta,\gamma
instead of
p,\tau
.

2.2
The main result: Behaviour around the critical window.

For any finite graph
G
we denote by
C_{1}(G)
its largest connected component, when either it is clear that it is unique or one can break ties arbitrarily, and by
\#C_{1}(G)
its size.
Similarly,
C_{i}(G)
is the
i
th largest component and

\#C_{i}(G)
its size.

Equation (
1.1
) implies that the largest component
C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n})
of
\mathscr{G}_{n}
has size of order
n
for all
\beta>0
, but if
\beta=\beta(n)
goes to zero with the number of vertices, the largest component is no longer of macroscopic size. Indeed, by monotonicity we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\#C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n})\leq e^{-\frac{c}{\beta}}
, for all
\beta>0
. We now investigate the size of the largest component in this scenario.

Recall that a sequence of nonnegative random variables
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
tight
if

\lim_{M\to\infty}\,\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\tfrac{1}{M}\leq X_{n}\leq M\big)=1\;.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\tfrac{1}{2}<\gamma<1

and
\mathscr{G}_{n}
the inhomogeneous random graph with the kernel

\kappa(x,y)=\beta(n)(x\wedge y)^{-\gamma}(x\vee y)^{\gamma-1}

for
\beta(n)\searrow 0
.

(I)

Tiny giant regime
: If

\beta(n)n^{\gamma-\tfrac{1}{2}}\to\infty
, then there exists a constant
\theta>0

such that

\displaystyle\frac{\#C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n})}{n\,\beta(n)^{\frac{1}{2\gamma-1}}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\theta\,\;\text{ and }\;\frac{\#C_{2}(\mathscr{G}_{n})}{n\,\beta(n)^{\frac{1}{2\gamma-1}}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0\;.

(II)

Critical window
: If
\beta(n)n^{\gamma-\tfrac{1}{2}}\to\varphi>0
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\#C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n})}{\sqrt{n}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\varphi\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\bigg\{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}(i)}\frac{1}{\gamma}\,j^{-\gamma}\bigg\}\;,

where
\mathcal{C}(i)
is the component of
i
in the random graph

\mathscr{G}_{\text{NR}}
with vertex set
\mathbb{N}
, where two vertices
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
have a
\text{Pois}(\tfrac{\varphi^{2}}{2\gamma-1}(ij)^{-\gamma})
number of edges between them, independently for all pairs of vertices.

(III)

Star component regime
: If

\beta(n)n^{\gamma-\tfrac{1}{2}}\to 0
but
\beta(n)n^{\gamma}\to\infty
,
then

\frac{\#C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n})}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{\gamma}\;.

(IV)

Bounded component regime
:

(i)

If
\beta(n)n^{\gamma}\to c\in(0,\infty)
, then

\big(\#C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n})\big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\text{ is tight}
;

(ii)

if
\beta(n)n^{\gamma}\to 0
and
\beta(n)n\to\infty
, then

\mathbb{P}\big(\#C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n})=2\big)\to 1
,

(iii)

if
\beta(n)n\to 0
, then
\mathbb{P}\big(\#C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n})=1\big)\to 1
.

Remarks:

(i)

In the proof we will see that for fixed
i\in\mathscr{G}_{n}
we have

\frac{\text{deg}(i)}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{\gamma}\,i^{-\gamma}.

While the largest component in regime
(I\!I)
arises from
the direct neighbours of a tight, random number of vertices, the largest
component in regime
(I\!I\!I)
arises from the direct neighbours of the vertex of
maximal degree. It follows that in these regimes
\#C_{2}(\mathscr{G}_{n})/n^{\gamma}\beta(n)
does not vanish, contrary to regime
(I)
.

(ii)

Comparing the behaviour in the critical window for the rank-one class
studied in
[
4
]
and our preferential attachment class there is an interesting duality to be observed. While for a preferential attachment graph the scaling limit of the largest component arises from a core resembling a Norros-Reittu (or rank one) graph, for the Norros-Reittu random graph (and similar models) the largest component arises from a preferential attachment graph; see Theorem 2.3 and Lemma 3.4 in
[
4
]
.

(iii)

When
\gamma<\tfrac{1}{2}
there exists a percolation phase transition at a positive density
\beta_{c}>0
. In this case Mörters and Schleicher
[
26
]
identify the size of the largest subcritical component. Further,
in
[
20
]
Jorritsma et al. identify a critical window

\beta(n)=\beta_{c}+\frac{\alpha}{(\log n)^{2}}

in which the largest component has size of order
\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\log n}
if
\alpha<\pi^{2}
and size of larger order if
\alpha\geq\pi^{2}
.

(iv)

For
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
the preferential attachment kernel has rank one, but the case of
\tau=3
and infinite variance is not covered in the papers
[
4
,
17
]
discussing such models.

Above the critical window

Lemma 3.2

For any

Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(i\overset{1}{\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.75pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\longleftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.75pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\longleftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 2.625pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\longleftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.875pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\longleftrightarrow}}}}j\big)=

\displaystyle\prod_{x=\lfloor sn\rfloor+1}^{n}1-\beta(n)^{2}i^{-\gamma}j^{-\gamma}x^{2(\gamma-1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\exp\Big(\sum_{x=\lfloor sn\rfloor+1}^{n}\log(1-\beta(n)^{2}i^{-\gamma}j^{-\gamma}x^{2(\gamma-1)})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\exp\Big(-\frac{\beta(n)^{2}n^{2\gamma-1}}{2\gamma-1}i^{-\gamma}j^{-\gamma}\big(1+o(1)\big)\Big),

where we estimated the sum by an integral in the last step. Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(i\overset{1}{\longleftrightarrow}j\big)=

\displaystyle 1-\exp\Big(-\frac{\beta(n)^{2}n^{2\gamma-1}}{2\gamma-1}i^{-\gamma}j^{-\gamma}\big(1+o(1)\big)\Big)

(3.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{m}\,\kappa_{\mu}\Big(\frac{i}{m},\frac{j}{m}\Big)\big(1+o(1)\big)\Big)\,.

Note that
\kappa_{\mu}(x,y)=\psi(x)\psi(y)
, with
\psi(x)=cx^{-\gamma}
for a suitable

. This is the kernel of an inhomogeneous random graph of rank one.
By
[

this implies that
\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{{}_{(2)}}
has a component of size of order
m
as

\displaystyle 1<||T_{\kappa_{\mu}}||^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}\psi^{2}(x)\,\text{d}x=\infty,

where
T_{\kappa_{\mu}}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt operator associated with the integral kernel
\kappa_{\mu}

on

.
More precisely,

\frac{\#C_{1}\big(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{(2)}\big)}{m}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\int_{0}^{1}\rho({\kappa_{\mu}},x)\,dx\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{\#C_{2}\big(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{(2)}\big)}{m}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0,

where
\int_{0}^{1}\rho({\kappa_{\mu}},x)\,dx>0
is the survival probability of a branching process associated with the kernel
\kappa_{\mu}
and started in a vertex of uniform random type.
∎

\mathscr{G}_{n}

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\displaystyle\text{\emph{outdeg}}(i):=\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\{j\sim i\}}
and
f(x):=\tfrac{1}{\gamma}x^{-\gamma}
. Then,
for any
\mu>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\,\Big|\frac{1}{sn}\sum_{i\in C_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{{}_{(2)}})}\text{\emph{outdeg}}(i)-\frac{\mu^{1-\gamma}}{m}\sum_{i\in C_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{{}_{(2)}})}f\big(\frac{i}{m}\big)\Big|>\varepsilon\Big)\longrightarrow 0\,.

Proof.

There is
(o_{n})
converging to zero such that, for all
i\leq n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\text{outdeg}(i)\big]=\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\beta(n)j^{\gamma-1}i^{-\gamma}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\beta(n)n^{\gamma}i^{-\gamma}+o_{n}.

Further, for the variance we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\text{outdeg}(i))

\displaystyle=\beta(n)^{2}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\sum_{l=i+1\atop l\neq j}^{n}j^{\gamma-1}l^{\gamma-1}i^{-2\gamma}+\beta(n)\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}j^{\gamma-1}i^{-\gamma}

(3.4)

\displaystyle-\bigg(\beta(n)\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}j^{\gamma-1}i^{-\gamma}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=\beta(n)\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}j^{\gamma-1}i^{-\gamma}-\beta(n)^{2}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}j^{2(\gamma-1)}i^{-2\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\beta(n)n^{\gamma}i^{-\gamma}\;.

Moreover we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{sn}\sum_{i\in C_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{(2)})}\mathbb{E}\big[\text{outdeg}(i)\big]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n\beta(n)^{\alpha/2}}\sum_{i\in C_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{(2)})}\frac{1}{{\gamma}}i^{-\gamma}\beta(n)n^{\gamma}+o_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu^{1-\gamma}}{m}\sum_{i\in C_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{(2)})}f\big(\frac{i}{m}\big)+o_{n}\,.

Thus, by Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\Big[\Big|\frac{1}{sn}\sum_{i\in C_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{{}_{(2)}})}\text{outdeg}(i)-\frac{\mu^{1-\gamma}}{m}\sum_{i\in C_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{{}_{(2)}})}f\big(\frac{i}{m}\big)\Big|\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{sn}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\text{outdeg}(i)-\mathbb{E}\big[\text{outdeg}(i)\big]\big|\Big]+o(1)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{sn}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\text{outdeg}(i)-\mathbb{E}\big[\text{outdeg}(i)\big]\big|^{2}\Big]^{\tfrac{1}{2}}+o(1)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{sn}n^{\tfrac{\gamma}{2}}\beta(n)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}m^{1-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}}+o(1)=c\beta(n)^{\tfrac{1}{2(2\gamma-1)}}+o(1)\,.

The statement now follows by Markov’s inequality.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

We have, for some
C>0
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\#\{x\in\{m+1,\dots,n\}\,|\,\exists i,j\leq m\,:\,i\sim x\sim j\}\big]\leq C\beta(n)^{\alpha}n.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\#\{x>m\,|\,\exists i,j\leq m\,:\,i\sim x\sim j\}\big]\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{x=m+1}^{n}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\beta(n)^{2}i^{-\gamma}j^{-\gamma}x^{2(\gamma-1)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\beta(n)^{2}\beta(n)^{2\alpha(1-\gamma)}n=C\beta(n)^{\alpha}n.

∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f(x)=\tfrac{1}{\gamma}x^{-\gamma}
.
Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n\beta(n)^{\alpha/2}}\#\big\{x\in\{1,\dots,n\}\,:\,x\sim C_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{(2)})\}=\mu^{1-\gamma}\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\rho(\kappa_{\mu},x)\,\text{d}x\,,

(3.5)

in probability, where

is the survival probability of the multitype Galton-Watson tree started in

associated with the kernel

\kappa_{\mu}(x,y)=\mu^{1-2\gamma}(2\gamma-1)^{-1}x^{-\gamma}y^{-\gamma}\,.

We trivially have

\displaystyle\#\big\{x

\displaystyle\in\{1,\dots,n\}\,:\,x\sim C_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{(2)})\}

Lemma 3.6
.

We have

\lim_{\mu\to\infty}\mu^{1-\gamma}\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\rho(\kappa_{\mu},x)\,\text{d}x=:\theta>0\,.

Note that

\displaystyle\mu^{1-\gamma}\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\rho(\kappa_{\mu},x)\,\text{d}x=\frac{\mu^{1-\gamma}}{c\gamma}\underbrace{c\int_{0}^{1}\psi(x)\rho(\kappa_{\mu},x)\,\text{d}x}_{=:\rho(c)}\,.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let

Lemma 3.8
.

Let

\frac{1}{n\beta(n)^{\alpha/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\#\big(\mathcal{N}_{2k}(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{(2)})\setminus\mathcal{N}_{2k+1}(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{(2)})\big)\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{ in probability.}

\frac{\#\mathcal{N}_{2}(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{(2)})}{n\beta(n)^{\alpha/2}}\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{ in probability.}

Proof of Lemma
3.8

The proof uses a path counting argument. Recall

from (

\tilde{s}=\tilde{s}(n)
to be determined, but such that

Let

If

\displaystyle\frac{1}{sn}\#\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{G}_{n,\mu}^{(2)})\leq\frac{1}{sn}\sum_{l\geq k}X_{l}+o(1),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{2}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{x,y=m+1}^{n}\beta(n)^{2}i^{-\gamma}x^{\gamma-1}(x\wedge y)^{-\gamma}(x\vee y)^{\gamma-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{x,y=m+1}^{n}\beta(n)^{2}i^{-\gamma}\big(x^{-1}y^{\gamma-1}+y^{-\gamma}x^{2(\gamma-1)}\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\beta(n)^{2}m^{1-\gamma}\big(\log(\tfrac{n}{m})n^{\gamma}+n^{\gamma}\big)\leq Cn\beta(n)^{\gamma\alpha}\log(\beta(n)^{-\alpha})=o(sn)\,,

as

For

we look at the expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{l}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{0<x_{l}\leq\tilde{s}n}\;\sum_{\tilde{s}n<x_{l-1}\leq n}\cdots\sum_{\tilde{s}n<x_{0}\leq n}\prod_{i=0}^{l-1}\beta(n)(x_{i}\vee x_{i+1})^{\gamma-1}(x_{i}\wedge x_{i+1})^{-\gamma}\;.

Note that in the above we would actually get an equality if we restrict the summation variables to be distinct.

This is a recursion, since

\displaystyle\mu_{k+1}(x)=\sum_{\tilde{s}n<y\leq n}\mu_{k}(y)\beta(n)(x\vee y)^{\gamma-1}(x\wedge y)^{-\gamma}\quad\text{ for all }k\geq 1\;.

(3.10)

Thus we can write for the expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{l}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{0<x_{l}\leq\tilde{s}n}\mu_{l}(x_{l})

(3.11)

and apply Lemma
3.7
. For the involved variables we choose

, with

. This guarantees

.
First we have for the starting value of our recursion

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(x_{1})=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{s}n<x_{0}\leq n}\beta(n)(x_{0}\vee x_{1})^{\gamma-1}(x_{0}\wedge x_{1})^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{1}>\tilde{s}n\}}\frac{\beta(n)n^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\,x_{1}^{\gamma-1}+\frac{\beta(n)n^{\gamma}}{\gamma}\,x_{1}^{-\gamma}\;.

Thus we define
\psi_{1}=\tfrac{\beta(n)n^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}

\phi_{1}=\tfrac{\beta(n)n^{\gamma}}{\gamma}

\displaystyle\psi_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\phi_{k}\beta(n)\frac{(\tilde{s}n)^{1-2\gamma}}{2\gamma-1}+\psi_{k}\beta(n)\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\tilde{s}}\right),

\displaystyle\phi_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\phi_{k}\beta(n)\log\left(\tfrac{1}{\tilde{s}}\right)+\psi_{k}\beta(n)\frac{n^{2\gamma-1}}{2\gamma-1}\;.

Lemma 3.10
.

Define

\mathcal{A}_{\text{\emph{no 1-path}}}:=\Bigg\{x\in\{m+1,\dots,n\}\,:\,{x\leftrightarrow\{1,\dots,m\},x\not\sim\{1,\dots,m\}\atop\text{ \emph{and} }x\overset{1\text{\emph{-path}}}{\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.75pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\longleftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.75pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\longleftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 2.625pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\longleftrightarrow}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 1.875pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\longleftrightarrow}}}}\{1,\dots,m\}}\Bigg\}\,.

Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n\beta(n)^{\alpha/2}}\#\mathcal{A}_{\text{\emph{no 1-path}}}\longrightarrow\,0\qquad\text{ in probability.}

Proof.

By Lemma
3.8
we can neglect vertices at even distance to
\{1,\ldots,m\}
and at distances
2\big\lfloor\tfrac{1-\gamma}{2\gamma-1}\big\rfloor+3

or greater.
For
l\geq 3
and
k\leq l-2
let
X_{l,k}^{\vee},X_{l,k}^{\wedge},X_{l,k}^{\downarrow},X_{l,k}^{\uparrow}
be the number of paths
x_{0}x_{1}\cdots x_{l}
such that
x_{i}>m
for all
0\leq i<l
,

x_{l}\leq m
and

•

x_{k},x_{k+2}>x_{k+1}

and
\big(x_{k+1}>sn
or
\min\{x_{k},x_{k+2}\}<sn\big)
for
X_{l,k}^{\vee}
,

•

x_{k},x_{k+2}<x_{k+1}

and
\big(x_{k+1}\leq sn
or
\max\{x_{k},x_{k+2}\}>sn\big)
for
X_{l,k}^{\wedge}
,

•

x_{k}>x_{k+1}>x_{k+2}
for
X_{l,k}^{\downarrow}
,

•

x_{k}<x_{k+1}<x_{k+2}
for
X_{l,k}^{\uparrow}
.

Also let
Y_{l}
be the number of paths
x_{0}x_{1}\cdots x_{l}
such that
x_{0},x_{k}\in\{m+1,\dots,sn\}
for all even
k
,
x_{k}>sn
for all odd
k\neq l
and
x_{l}\leq m
if
l
is odd and
Y_{l}=0
else. That is, the
Y
-paths follow the one-path pattern only up to the last step.

We now argue that any path which does not follow the one-path pattern fits at least one of the paths described above. First note that any path ending in
\{1,\ldots,m\}
, which is neither a one-path nor a
Y
-path, has a segment
x_{k}\sim x_{k+1}
with either
x_{k},x_{k+1}>sn
or
x_{k},x_{k+1}\in\{m+1,\dots,sn\}
for some
k\leq l-2
. Second, if
x_{k}>x_{k+1}>sn
then the next step is either
x_{k+2}<x_{k+1}
and thus of
X^{\downarrow}
-type or
x_{k+2}>x_{k+1}
and thus of
X^{\vee}
-type. If however
x_{k+1}>x_{k}>sn
then the next step is either
x_{k+2}<x_{k+1}
and thus of
X^{\wedge}
-type or
x_{k+2}>x_{k+1}
and thus of
X^{\uparrow}
-type.

A similar argument for the case that
x_{k},x_{k+1}\in\{m+1,\dots,sn\}
shows, that any non one-path fits at least one of the patterns described above. Thus we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\#\mathcal{A}_{\text{no 1-path}}\big]\leq\sum_{l=3}^{2K_{\gamma}+1}\sum_{k=1}^{l-2}\mathbb{E}\big[X_{l,k}^{\vee}+X_{l,k}^{\wedge}+X_{l,k}^{\downarrow}+X_{l,k}^{\uparrow}\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{l}\big]+o(sn)\,.

(3.14)

Note that
X_{l,k}^{\vee},X_{l,k}^{\wedge},X_{l,k}^{\downarrow}
and
X_{l,k}^{\uparrow}
coincide with
X_{l}
from the proof of Lemma
3.8
, for
\tilde{s}n=m
, up until step
k
.
Thus we can use our recursion (
3.10
) up to step
k
. We start with
X_{l,k}^{\vee}
, for which we may assume that
x_{k+1}>sn
and
x_{k},x_{k+2}
arbitrary. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[X_{l,k}^{\vee}\big]\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{0<x_{l}\leq m}\cdots\sum_{x_{k},x_{k+1},x_{k+2}>sn}\mu_{k}(x_{k})\beta(n)^{2}x_{k}^{\gamma-1}x_{k+1}^{-2\gamma}x_{k+2}^{\gamma-1}\prod_{i=k+2}^{l-1}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}\sim x_{i+1})

Using Lemma
3.7
and estimating the sum by an integral we get for the middle term

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{k},x_{k+1}>sn}

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(x_{k})\beta(n)^{2}x_{k}^{\gamma-1}x_{k+1}^{-2\gamma}x_{k+2}^{\gamma-1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x_{k},x_{k+1}>sn}\big(\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{k}>sn\}}\psi_{k}x_{k}^{2(\gamma-1)}+\phi_{k}x_{k}^{-1}\big)\beta(n)^{2}x_{k+1}^{-2\gamma}x_{k+2}^{\gamma-1}

\displaystyle\leq c\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{k+2}>sn\}}\big(\psi_{k}\beta(n)+\phi_{k}\beta(n)^{2}\log(\tfrac{1}{s})(sn)^{1-2\gamma}\big)x_{k+2}^{\gamma-1}

\displaystyle\leq c^{\prime}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{k+2}>sn\}}\psi_{k}\beta(n)x_{k+2}^{\gamma-1},

where we used (
3.12
) and (
3.13
) in the last step with
\tilde{s}=\beta(n)^{\alpha}
. By the same set of equations one can also see that
\beta(n)\psi_{k}=o(\psi_{k+1})
.
For
X_{l,k}^{\wedge}
we may assume without loss of generality that
x_{k}>sn
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[X_{l,k}^{\wedge}\big]\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{0<x_{l}\leq m}\cdots\sum_{x_{k}>sn\atop x_{k+1},x_{k+2}>m}\mu_{k}(x_{k})\beta(n)^{2}x_{k}^{-\gamma}x_{k+1}^{2(\gamma-1)}x_{k+2}^{-\gamma}\prod_{i=k+2}^{l-1}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}\sim x_{i+1})

and for the middle term

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{k}>sn\atop x_{k+1}>m}\mu_{k}(x_{k})\beta(n)^{2}x_{k}^{-\gamma}x_{k+1}^{2(\gamma-1)}x_{k+2}^{-\gamma}\leq

\displaystyle\,c\big(\psi_{k}\log(\tfrac{1}{s})+\phi_{k}(sn)^{1-2\gamma}\big)\beta(n)^{2}n^{2\gamma-1}x_{k+2}^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,c^{\prime}\phi_{k+1}\beta(n)\log(\tfrac{1}{s})x_{k+2}^{-\gamma}\,.

For the other paths we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[X_{l,k}^{\downarrow}\big]\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{0<x_{l}\leq m}\cdots\sum_{x_{k},x_{k+1},x_{k+2}>m}\mu_{k}(x_{k})\beta(n)^{2}x_{k}^{\gamma-1}x_{k+1}^{-1}x_{k+2}^{-\gamma}\prod_{i=k+2}^{l-1}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}\sim x_{i+1})\,.

Again, by our estimates (
3.13
) we have for the middle term

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{k},x_{k+1}>m}

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(x_{k})\beta(n)^{2}x_{k}^{\gamma-1}x_{k+1}^{-1}x_{k+2}^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{k},x_{k+1}>m}\big(\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{k}>m\}}\psi_{k}x_{k}^{2(\gamma-1)}+\phi_{k}x_{k}^{-1}\big)\beta(n)^{2}x_{k+1}^{-1}x_{k+2}^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c\big(\psi_{k}n^{2\gamma-1}\log(\tfrac{1}{s})\beta(n)^{2}+\phi_{k}\beta(n)^{2}\log(\tfrac{1}{s})^{2}\big)x_{k+2}^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c^{\prime}\beta(n)\log(\tfrac{1}{s})\phi_{k+1}x_{k+2}^{-\gamma}\;.

Similarly one can see, that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[X_{l,k}^{\uparrow}\big]\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{0<x_{l}\leq m}\cdots\sum_{x_{k+2}>m}c^{\prime}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{k+2}>m\}}\beta(n)\log(\tfrac{1}{s})\psi_{k+1}x_{k+2}^{\gamma-1}\prod_{i=k+2}^{l-1}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}\sim x_{i+1})\,.

Thus in any of the four paths we can factor out the
c^{\prime}\beta(n)\log(\tfrac{1}{s})
term and continue the recursion. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[X_{l,k}^{\vee}+X_{l,k}^{\wedge}+X_{l,k}^{\uparrow}+X_{l,k}^{\downarrow}\big]\leq c^{\prime}\beta(n)\log(\tfrac{1}{s})\mathbb{E}\big[X_{l}\big]\,,

for any
k
. Moreover for the paths of uneven length, we get by choosing
\tilde{s}n=m=\beta(n)^{2/(2\gamma-1)}n
in (
3.13
), that

\phi_{2l+1}\leq c\beta(n)^{2l+1}s^{l(2\gamma-1)}n^{\gamma}=c\beta(n)n^{\gamma}\,.

Thus by (
3.11
) and the above we get

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{K_{\gamma}}\beta(n)\log(\tfrac{1}{s})\mathbb{E}\big[X_{2l+1}\big]+o(sn)\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{K_{\gamma}}C\beta(n)\log(\tfrac{1}{s})\phi_{2l+1}m^{1-\gamma}+o(sn)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\beta(n)^{2+(1-\gamma)\alpha}\log(\tfrac{1}{s})n+o(sn)=o(sn)\,.

It remains to estimate the sum over the
Y
-paths in (
3.14
). By the definition of
Y_{l}
we have, for
l\geq 3
odd, that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{l}\big]\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{l}\leq m}\sum_{m<x_{0},x_{2},..,x_{l-1}\leq sn}\prod_{i=0}^{l-3}\mathbb{P}(x_{i}\overset{1}{\longleftrightarrow}x_{i+2})\beta(n)x_{l-1}^{\gamma-1}x_{l}^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{l}\leq m}\sum_{m<x_{0},x_{l-1}\leq sn}\frac{\beta(n)^{3}n^{2\gamma-1}}{2\gamma-1}x_{0}^{-\gamma}x_{l-1}^{-1}x_{l}^{-\gamma}\Big(\sum_{m<x\leq sn}\frac{\beta(n)^{2}n^{2\gamma-1}}{2\gamma-1}x^{-2\gamma}\Big)^{\tfrac{l-3}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,c_{1}\beta(n)^{3}n^{2\gamma-1}(sn)^{1-\gamma}\log(\tfrac{1}{s})m^{1-\gamma}c^{l}_{2}=\,c_{1}c_{2}^{l}\beta(n)^{\tfrac{3\gamma}{2\gamma-1}}n=o(sn)\,.

Since we only sum over finitely many
l
we are done.
∎

At this point we know that the size of the component of
C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n,\mu}^{{}_{(2)}})
is asymptotically the same as the number of vertices connected to it either by an edge or by a one-path. We continue by systematically ruling out further sets of vertices, first those
with index smaller than
sn
.

Lemma 3.11
.

We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n\beta(n)^{\alpha/2}}\#\big\{j\in\{1,\ldots,\lfloor sn\rfloor\}\colon j\sim\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{n,\mu}\text{ or }j\overset{1\text{\emph{-path}}}{\longleftrightarrow}\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{n,\mu}\big\}{\longrightarrow}\,0\quad\text{ in probability.}

Proof.

Note first that we can restrict attention to
vertices
j\in\{m+1,\dots,\lfloor sn\rfloor\}
. If
j
is connected by a one-path to some
i\in\mathcal{G}^{{}_{(2)}}_{n,\mu}
then it is by definition at an even distance to
i
and by Lemma
3.8
this set is negligible. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\#\{j\in\{m+1,\dots,\lfloor sn\rfloor\}\,:\,j\sim\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{n,\mu}\big]\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq m\atop j\in\{m+1,\dots,\lfloor sn\rfloor\}}\beta(n)i^{-\gamma}j^{\gamma-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,C\beta(n)s^{2-\gamma}n=o(sn),

showing that nearest neighbours in this set are also negligible.
∎

Our aim is now to show that, for every
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, an
M
can be chosen so large that with probability exceeding
1-\delta
all but
\varepsilon n\beta(n)^{\alpha/2}
vertices, which are connected by a path to the giant in
\mathscr{G}_{n,\mu}^{{}_{(2)}}
, are connected by an edge to a unique vertex in
\mathscr{G}_{n,M}^{{}_{(2)}}
, see Lemma
3.14
. We prepare this step with a further lemma, comparing connections by one-paths and by one-connectors.

Lemma 3.12
.

There exist
c,c^{\prime}>1
, depending on
\mu
, such that, for any
i,j\in\{1,\dots,\lfloor sn\rfloor\}
and
x\in\{\lfloor sn\rfloor+1,\dots,n\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(j\overset{1\text{\emph{-path}}}{\longleftrightarrow}i)\leq c\,\mathbb{P}(j\overset{1}{\longleftrightarrow}i)\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbb{P}(x\overset{1\text{\emph{-path}}}{\longleftrightarrow}i)\leq c^{\prime}\,\mathbb{P}(x\sim i)\,.

Here
j\overset{1}{\longleftrightarrow}i
stands for the event that
i
and
j
are connected via a single middle vertex in
\{\lfloor sn\rfloor+1,\dots,n\}
, in accordance with Definition
3.9
.

Proof.

From (
3.3
) we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(j\overset{1}{\longleftrightarrow}i)

\displaystyle=1-\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{m}\,\kappa_{\mu}\Big(\frac{i}{m},\frac{j}{m}\Big)\big(1+o(1)\big)\Big)

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\beta(n)^{2}n^{2\gamma-1}}{2\gamma-1}i^{-\gamma}j^{-\gamma}(1+o(1)),

using that
i\vee j\geq m
and hence

\beta(n)^{2}n^{2\gamma-1}(i\vee j)^{-\gamma}\leq\beta(n)^{2-\gamma\alpha}n^{\gamma-1}\to 0\,.

By the definition of a one-path the maximum path length is bounded by some
C=2K_{\gamma}+1
. Therefore, by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(j\overset{1\text{-path}}{\longleftrightarrow}i)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{C}\sum_{m<y_{1},..,y_{k}\leq sn}\mathbb{P}(i\overset{1}{\longleftrightarrow}y_{1})\mathbb{P}(y_{k}\overset{1}{\longleftrightarrow}j)\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}(y_{i}\overset{1}{\longleftrightarrow}y_{i+1})

\displaystyle\leq i^{-\gamma}j^{-\gamma}\sum_{k=0}^{C}\sum_{m<y_{1},..,y_{k}\leq sn}\Big(\frac{\beta(n)^{2}n^{2\gamma-1}}{2\gamma-1}\Big)^{k+1}y_{1}^{-2\gamma}\cdots y_{k}^{-2\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq i^{-\gamma}j^{-\gamma}\frac{\beta(n)^{2}n^{2\gamma-1}}{2\gamma-1}\sum_{k=0}^{C}\Big(\frac{\beta(n)^{2}n^{2\gamma-1}}{2\gamma-1}\sum_{y>m}y^{-2\gamma}\Big)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq c\,i^{-\gamma}j^{-\gamma}\frac{\beta(n)^{2}n^{2\gamma-1}}{2\gamma-1}\,,

for some
c>1
depending on
\mu
.

Now let
x>sn
, then by Definition
3.9
and the above we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(x\overset{1\text{-path}}{\longleftrightarrow}i)=

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{P}(\exists j\in\{m+1,\dots,sn\}\,:\,x\sim j\text{ and }j\overset{1\text{-path}}{\longleftrightarrow}i)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\sum_{m<j\leq sn}\beta(n)x^{\gamma-1}j^{-\gamma}c\mathbb{P}(j\overset{1}{\longleftrightarrow}i)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,c^{\prime}\beta(n)^{3}m^{1-2\gamma}n^{2\gamma-1}x^{\gamma-1}i^{-\gamma}=c^{\prime}\beta(n)x^{\gamma-1}i^{-\gamma}\,,

for a suitable
c^{\prime}>1
depending on
\mu
, which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

(a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{sn}\#\big\{x>sn\,:\,\exists i_{1},i_{2}\leq sn\text{ \emph{with} }i_{1}\sim x\sim i_{2}\big\}\longrightarrow\,0
in probability.

(b)

For every
\mu>0
there exists
C=C(\mu)>0
such that, for any
\varepsilon>0
and
M>\mu
we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\#\big\{x>sn\,:\,

\displaystyle x\overset{1\text{\emph{-path}}}{\longleftrightarrow}\{1,\dots,m\}\text{\emph{ and }}

\displaystyle x\not\sim\{1,\dots,\lfloor M\beta(n)^{\alpha}n\rfloor\}\big\}>\varepsilon sn\Big)\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}M^{1-2\gamma}\,.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\#\{x>sn\,:\,\exists i_{1},i_{2}\leq sn\text{ with }i_{1}\sim x\sim i_{2}\}\big]\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{x>sn}\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}\leq sn}\beta(n)^{2}i_{1}^{-\gamma}i_{2}^{-\gamma}x^{2(\gamma-1)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\beta(n)^{2+\tfrac{2(1-\gamma)}{2\gamma-1}}n=o(sn)\,.

By Markov’s inequality, this already yields statement
(a)
of the lemma.

For the set of vertices in
(b)
we have by definition

\displaystyle\#\big\{x>sn\,:\,

\displaystyle x\overset{1\text{-path}}{\longleftrightarrow}\{1,\dots,m\}\text{ and }x\not\sim\{1,\dots,\lfloor M\beta(n)^{\alpha}n\rfloor\}\big\}

\displaystyle\leq\#\bigg\{x>sn\,:\,{\exists y\in\{\lfloor M\beta(n)^{\alpha}n\rfloor+1,\dots,\lfloor sn\rfloor\}\atop x\sim y,\;y\overset{{}_{1\text{-path}}}{\longleftrightarrow}\{1,\dots,m\}}\bigg\}\,.

Taking expectations, for fixed
x
the event
\{\exists y\in\{\lfloor M\beta(n)^{\alpha}n\rfloor+1,\dots,\lfloor sn\rfloor\}\colon x\sim y,

y\overset{{}_{1\text{-path}}}{\longleftrightarrow}\{1,\dots,m\}\}

is contained in the union of the events that there is a one-path from

\{\lfloor M\beta(n)^{\alpha}n\rfloor+1,\dots,\lfloor sn\rfloor\}
to
\{1,\dots,m\}
using the vertex
x
and the event that there is such a one-path not using the vertex
x
. If a one-path uses vertex
x
, then
x
is a one-connector, the number of which is
o(sn)
in probability by
(a)
. Otherwise, we use a union bound in
y
and observe that for fixed
y
the events
\{x\sim y\}
and
\{y\overset{{}_{1\text{-path}}}{\longleftrightarrow}\{1,\dots,m\}
not over
x\}
are independent.
Thus, by Lemma
3.12
, the expectation of the right hand side can be bounded from above by

\displaystyle\sum_{x>sn}

\displaystyle\sum_{y=\lfloor M\beta(n)^{\alpha}n\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor sn\rfloor}\sum_{z\leq m}\mathbb{P}(x\sim y)\mathbb{P}(y\overset{{}_{1\text{-path}}}{\longleftrightarrow}z)+o(sn)

\displaystyle\leq c\sum_{x>sn}\sum_{y=\lfloor M\beta(n)^{\alpha}n\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor sn\rfloor}\sum_{z\leq m}\beta(n)^{3}n^{2\gamma-1}x^{\gamma-1}y^{-2\gamma}z^{-\gamma}+o(sn)

\displaystyle\leq C\beta(n)^{\tfrac{4\gamma-1}{2\gamma-1}}n^{2\gamma}\big(M\beta(n)^{\tfrac{2}{2\gamma-1}}n\big)^{1-2\gamma}+o(sn)\,,

for some
C>0
depending only on
\mu
and
\gamma
.
As

\beta(n)^{\tfrac{4\gamma-1}{2\gamma-1}}n^{2\gamma}\beta(n)^{-2}n^{1-2\gamma}=sn
,
the statement
(b)
follows by Markov’s inequality.
∎

Let
K
be a component of
\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{n,\mu}
and denote by

its component in
\mathscr{G}_{n}
.

Lemma 3.14
.

For any
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
there exists

such that, for all

,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\#\mathcal{C}(C_{1}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{n,\mu}))\leq\sum_{i\in C_{1}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{n,M})}{\mathrm{outdeg}}(i)\,+\varepsilon sn\Big)\geq 1-\delta,

Let
O_{M}:=\{m+1,\dots,\lfloor M\beta(n)^{\alpha}n\rfloor\}
, roughly speaking

is the set of all vertices of order

, which are not included in
\{1,\dots,m\}
.
By Lemma
3.11
and
3.13
, for any

large enough, such that, for all sufficiently large

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{C}(C_{1}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{n,\mu}))\leq\sum_{i\in C_{1}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{n,\mu})}\text{outdeg}(i)\,+\sum_{j\in O_{M}\atop j\overset{1\text{-path}}{\longleftrightarrow}C_{1}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{n,\mu})}\text{outdeg}(j)+\varepsilon sn

.

Recall the definition of one-paths from Definition
3.9
and

by one-paths using only vertices in

at the even steps.
If for all

large enough, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{{j\in O_{M}\setminus O_{M}^{\prime}\colon}\atop{j\overset{1\text{-path}}{\longleftrightarrow}C_{1}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{n,\mu})}}\text{outdeg}(j)>\varepsilon sn\bigg)<\delta,

then the second sum in (

j\in O_{M}^{\prime}

It remains to show (
3.17
). Let
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
. For

.

where
\circ
denotes the disjoint occurrence
of events. It therefore suffices to show that, for large enough

,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in O_{M}}\sum_{y=\lfloor M\beta(n)^{\alpha}n\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor sn\rfloor}\mathbbm{1}_{\{j\overset{{}_{1\text{-path}}}{\longleftrightarrow}y\}\,\circ\,\{y\overset{{}_{1\text{-path}}}{\longleftrightarrow}C_{1}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{n,\mu})\}}\text{outdeg}(j)\leq\varepsilon sn

with probability

, for all sufficiently large

.

\text{outdeg}(j)=\sum_{x>j}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\sim j\}}
. If

the edge

and
\{j\overset{{}_{1\text{-path}}}{\longleftrightarrow}y\}
, as well as
\{x\sim j\}
and
\{y\overset{{1\text{-path}}}{\longleftrightarrow}C_{1}(\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{n,\mu})\}

If
x>sn
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

Lemma 3.15
.

Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists\mathcal{C}\subset\{m_{n}+1,\dots,n\}\,:\,

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{\mathcal{C}\subset\{m_{n}+1,\dots,n\}\atop|\mathcal{C}|\geq k}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{C}\not\sim\,\{1,\dots,m_{n}\}\big|\mathscr{F}(m_{n},n)\right)\bigg].

Lemma 3.16

In the critical window

In the critical window the core from Lemma

Lemma 3.17

Let

\displaystyle\psi_{l}=

\displaystyle O\big(\beta(n)^{l}m^{(1-2\gamma)\lfloor\tfrac{l}{2}\rfloor}n^{\gamma+(2\gamma-1)(\lfloor\tfrac{l}{2}\rfloor-1)}\log(n)^{\mathbbm{1}_{\{l\text{ odd}\}}}\big)

\displaystyle\phi_{l}=

\displaystyle O\big(\beta(n)^{l}m^{(1-2\gamma)\lfloor\tfrac{l-1}{2}\rfloor}n^{\gamma+(2\gamma-1)\lfloor\tfrac{l-1}{2}\rfloor}\log(n)^{\mathbbm{1}_{\{l\text{ even}\}}}\big)\;.

Equation (

The starting value is given by

\displaystyle\sum_{m<x_{0}\leq n}\beta(n)(x_{0}\vee x_{1})^{\gamma-1}(x_{0}\wedge x_{1})^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{1}>m\}}\underbrace{\frac{\beta(n)n^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}}_{=:\psi_{1}}x_{1}^{\gamma-1}+\underbrace{\frac{\beta(n)n^{\gamma}}{\gamma}}_{=:\phi_{1}}x_{1}^{-\gamma}\;.

q\beta(n)^{2}1^{-\gamma}3^{-\gamma}x^{2(\gamma-1)}&\text{if }k=3,\end{matrix}\right.

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[I_{(1,x,k)}I_{(1,x,k^{c})}]=pq\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbbm{1}_{1\sim x}\mathbbm{1}_{x\sim 2}\mathbbm{1}_{x\sim 3}\big]=pq\beta(n)^{3}6^{-\gamma}x^{3(\gamma-1)}\,.

Hence, by the above for two constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
,

\displaystyle d_{tv}\Big(\mathcal{L}\big(\sum_{\alpha\in\Gamma}I_{\alpha}\big),\text{Pois}(\lambda)\Big)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\Big(c_{1}\beta(n)^{4}\sum_{x=m+1}^{n}x^{4(\gamma-1)}+c_{2}\beta(n)^{3}\sum_{x=m+1}^{n}x^{3(\gamma-1)}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\big(c_{1}\varphi_{n}^{4}n^{-1}+c_{2}\varphi_{n}^{3}n^{-1/2}\big)\to 0\;.

Lastly we compute

\displaystyle\lambda=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\alpha\in\Gamma}I_{\alpha}\Big]=p\sum_{x=m+1}^{n}\pi_{(1,x,2)}+q\sum_{x=m+1}^{n}\pi_{(1,x,3)}\to p\tfrac{\varphi^{2}}{2\gamma-1}1^{-\gamma}2^{-\gamma}+q\tfrac{\varphi^{2}}{2\gamma-1}1^{-\gamma}3^{-\gamma}\,.

∎

We now define
\mathscr{G}_{\text{NR}}=\mathscr{G}_{\text{NR}}\big((\lambda_{ij})_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}\big)
as the random graph with vertex set
\mathbb{N}
, where two vertices have an
\text{Pois}(\lambda_{ij})
amount of edges between them, independent of all other vertices. Moreover let
\mathcal{C}(i)
be the component of
i
in
\mathscr{G}_{\text{NR}}
. By the above lemma we can couple our core graph with
\mathscr{G}_{\text{NR}}
, such that they
agree up to the first
m
vertices with probability going to one as
n\to\infty
.
Moreover we define

\text{outdeg}(i)=\sum_{j>i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{i\sim j\}}\;\text{ and }\;\text{indeg}(i)=\sum_{j<i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{i\sim j\}}\,.

The following lemma shows that the main contributors to
\#C(i)
are the outdegrees in
\mathscr{G}_{n}
of the vertices in the component of
i
in
\mathscr{G}_{\text{NR}}
.

Lemma 3.20
.

If
\varphi_{n}=\beta(n)n^{\gamma-1/2}\to\varphi>0
, then
for every fixed
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\#C(i)}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}(i)}\frac{1}{\gamma}\,j^{-\gamma}\;.

(3.29)

Proof.

For every fixed
j
and
m
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\text{outdeg}^{>m}(j)\big]=\sum_{l=m+1}^{n}\beta(n)l^{\gamma-1}j^{-\gamma}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\beta(n)n^{\gamma}j^{-\gamma}+o(1)\;.

Furthermore, for the variance we have, similar to (
3.4
), that
\operatorname{Var}(\text{outdeg}^{>m}(j))\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\beta(n)n^{\gamma}j^{-\gamma}
.
Thus by Chebyshev’s inequality

\frac{1}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}\text{outdeg}^{>m}(j)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{\gamma}\,j^{-\gamma}\;.

(3.30)

We fix
i
and denote by
C^{\prime}(i)
the component of vertex
i
in the core graph. Note that

\displaystyle\#C(i)\geq\sum_{j\in C^{\prime}(i)}\text{outdeg}^{>m}(j)-\#\bigg\{j\in\{m+1,\ldots,n\}{\exists k\not=l\in C^{\prime}(i)\atop\text{ with }k,l\sim j}\bigg\}.

(3.31)

The subtracted set is not empty, as every edge
\{k,l\}
in the core graph causes the middle vertex to be an element of this set. We now argue that this set is negligible. Note that its expectation can be bounded from above by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{j=m+1}^{n}\sum_{1\leq k<l\leq m}\mathbbm{1}_{\{l\sim j\sim k\}}\bigg]\leq\sum_{j=m+1}^{n}\beta(n)^{2}j^{2\gamma-2}m^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{2\gamma-1}\beta(n)^{2}n^{2\gamma-1}m^{2}.

(3.32)

Hence, scaled by
n^{\gamma}\beta(n)
, the second term in the inequality above goes to 0 in probability. This implies that, for fixed
i\leq m
and
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\frac{\#C(i)}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}\geq\sum_{j\in C^{\prime}(i)}\frac{1}{\gamma}j^{-\gamma}-\varepsilon\Big)=1,

where
C^{\prime}(i)
agrees with the component of
i
in the restriction of

\mathscr{G}_{\text{NR}}
to the first
m
vertices. The lower bound in (
3.29
) follows as
m
was arbitrary.

To prove the upper bound in (
3.29
) note that

\displaystyle\#C(i)\leq\sum_{j\in C^{\prime}(i)}\text{outdeg}^{>m}(j)+\#\bigg\{k\in\{m+1,\ldots,n\}\colon{k\leftrightarrow i,\,\atop k\not\sim j\,\,\forall j\in C^{\prime}(i)}\bigg\}.

(3.33)

It remains to show that the added set on the right is negligible, i.e. that
vertices with a distance greater than two
to
i
, which are not a nearest neighbour of some
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, are negligible. Denote by
C^{>m}_{\geq 2}(i)
the set of said vertices. Then we prove for any
\varepsilon>0
, that

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\frac{1}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}\#C^{>m}_{\geq 2}(i)>\varepsilon\Big)=0\,.

(3.34)

Recall that
X^{(i)}_{l}
counts the paths running of length
l
running above the threshold
m
and ending in
i
and observe that
\#C^{>m}_{\geq 2}(i)\leq\sum_{l\geq 2}X^{(i)}_{l}
. By (
3.25
) we can estimate the expectation of the latter by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{l\geq 2}X^{(i)}_{l}\Big]\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{l\geq 2}\mu_{l}(i)\leq\sum_{l\geq 2}\phi_{l}i^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,c\sum_{l\geq 2}\varphi_{n}^{l}m^{(\tfrac{1}{2}-\gamma)l}\sqrt{n}\leq c\,m^{1-2\gamma}n^{\gamma}\beta(n)\;.

Now (
3.34
) follows from Markov’s inequality. Together with
(
3.30
) and Lemma
3.19
this implies (
3.29
) and hence the lemma.
∎

We now complete the proof of Theorem
1

(I\!I)
.

Lemma 3.21
.

We have

\displaystyle\frac{\#C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n})}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\bigg\{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}(i)}\frac{1}{\gamma}\,j^{-\gamma}\bigg\}\;.

Proof.

Let us first show that the limiting object on the right hand side in the lemma is well-defined.
We identify components by its smallest index vertex, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\leq}(i):=\left\{\begin{matrix}\mathcal{C}(i)\,,&\text{if }\,i=\min\{j\,:\,j\in\mathcal{C}(i)\}\\
\emptyset\,,&\text{ otherwise,}\end{matrix}\right.

similarly we define
C_{\leq}(i)
.
Then it suffices to show that

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Big(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{\leq}(i)}\frac{1}{\gamma}\,j^{-\gamma}\Big)^{2}\bigg]<\infty\,.

The left hand side is equal to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Big(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{\leq}(i)}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\,j^{-2\gamma}+\sum_{j,k\in\mathcal{C}_{\leq}(i)}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\,(jk)^{-\gamma}\Big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\,\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i^{-2\gamma}+\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{\infty}(jk)^{-\gamma}\mathbb{P}\big(j\leftrightarrow k\text{ in }\mathscr{G}_{\text{NR}}\big)\;.

The first term is finite as
\gamma>\tfrac{1}{2}
. For the second term, note that

\mathbb{P}\big(i\sim j\text{ in }\mathscr{G}_{\text{NR}}\big)\leq\lambda_{ij}=\frac{\varphi^{2}}{2\gamma-1}(ij)^{-\gamma}\leq\lambda_{c}\underbrace{(i\wedge j)^{\gamma-1}(i\vee j)^{-\gamma}}_{=:h(i,j)}\;,

where the last inequality follows for all, but finitely many, tuples
(i,j)
and

\displaystyle\lambda_{c}=

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{h(1,y)}{\sqrt{y}}\,\text{d}y\bigg)^{-1}=\bigg(\Big[\frac{1}{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}y^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}\Big]_{0}^{1}+\Big[\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}-\gamma}y^{\frac{1}{2}-\gamma}\Big]_{1}^{\infty}\bigg)^{-1}=\frac{\gamma}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\,.

We define
\mathscr{G}_{\text{DK}}
as the random graph on the vertex set
\mathbb{N}
, where we draw an edge between to vertices
i
and
j
with probability
\lambda_{c}h(i,j)\wedge 1
, independent of all other edges. This random graph is inhomogeneous of rank
-1
, i.e.
h(ti,tj)=t^{-1}h(i,j)
and
\lambda_{c}
is chosen such that the graph is at criticality. For this particular class of random graphs it was shown in
[
30
]
, that

\mathbb{P}\big(i\leftrightarrow j\text{ in }\mathscr{G}_{\text{DK}}\big)\leq c\,\frac{\log(i\wedge j)}{\sqrt{ij}}\;.

We can couple
\mathscr{G}_{\text{NR}}
and
\mathscr{G}_{\text{DK}}
such that every edge that exists in
\mathscr{G}_{\text{NR}}
also exists in
\mathscr{G}_{\text{DK}}
, except for finitely many edges.
Putting everything together, we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Big(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{\leq}(i)}\frac{1}{\gamma}\,j^{-\gamma}\Big)^{2}\bigg]\leq c_{1}+c_{2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{\infty}(jk)^{-\gamma-\tfrac{1}{2}}\log(j\wedge k)<\infty\,.

To prove convergence we want to argue by Lemma
3.20
and Cramer-Wold, that for every fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{C_{\leq}(i)}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}\bigg)_{1\leq i\leq m}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\bigg(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{\leq}(i)}\frac{1}{\gamma}\,j^{-\gamma}\bigg)_{1\leq i\leq m}\,.

(3.35)

Therefore let
(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be arbitrary, recall that
C^{\prime}(i)
is the component of
i
in the core graph. Similar to (
3.31
) we have for all
M
sufficiently large

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}\frac{C_{\leq}(i)}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{t_{i}}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}\bigg[\sum_{j\in C_{\leq}^{\prime}(i)}\text{outdeg}^{>M}(j)-\#\bigg\{j>M\,:{\exists k\not=l\in C^{\prime}_{\leq}(i)\atop\text{ with }k,l\sim j}\bigg\}\bigg].

By (
3.32
) the rightmost term goes to zero in probability and leftmost term on the right hand side converges in distribution to the desired object, since the core graph converges in distribution to
\mathscr{G}_{\text{NR}}
by Lemma
3.19
and the discussion thereafter. The matching upper bound follows by (
3.33
) and (
3.34
). Thus (
3.35
) follows.
Now note that by the remark following Lemma
3.18
, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an
m
, such that

\frac{C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n})}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\frac{C_{\leq}(i)}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}

with probability greater than
1-\varepsilon
. By continuity of the
max
function we conclude

\frac{C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n})}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\frac{C_{\leq}(i)}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\sup_{1\leq i\leq m}\bigg\{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{\leq}(i)}\frac{1}{\gamma}\,j^{-\gamma}\bigg\}\;,

with probability greater than
1-\varepsilon
. Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
and thus
m\to\infty
we therefore obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\#C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n})}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\bigg\{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}(i)}\frac{1}{\gamma}\,j^{-\gamma}\bigg\}\;,

using that the right hand side is well defined.
∎

3.3
Below the critical window

By (
3.30
) the degrees of the first
m
vertices concentrate around their mean and thus by Lemma
3.18
the largest connected component still has to intersect the set
\{1,\dots,m\}
. Lemma
3.19
suggests that the components of the first
m
vertices are disjoint. The next lemma confirms this.

Lemma 3.22
.

Suppose
\beta(n)n^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}}\to 0
. Then, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the components
C(1),\ldots,C(m)
in
\mathscr{G}_{n}
are pairwise disjoint with high probability.

Proof.

We show that the expected number of paths with start and endpoints in
\{1,\ldots,m\}
goes to 0. Let
\tilde{X}_{l}
be the number of paths
x_{0}x_{1}\cdots x_{l-1}x_{l}
of length
l
satisfying
x_{0},x_{l}\leq m
and
x_{k}>m
for all
0<k<l
. We have by Markov’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(

\displaystyle\text{components induced by }\{1,\ldots,m\}\text{ are disjoint})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(\text{no edges in }\{1,\ldots,m\})\big(1-\mathbb{P}\big(\sum_{l=2}^{n}\tilde{X}_{l}\geq 1\big)\big)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\big(-\beta(n)m^{2}\big)\Big(1-\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{l=2}^{n}\tilde{X}_{l}\Big]\Big)\;.

To estimate the expectation above, we use the same recursion as in the last section. Recall (
3.11
) and Lemma
3.7
, here we have for the starting value that

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(x_{1})=\sum_{0<x_{0}\leq m}\beta(n)x_{0}^{-\gamma}x_{1}^{\gamma-1}\leq\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{1}>m\}}\frac{\beta(n)m^{(1-\gamma)}}{1-\gamma}\,x_{1}^{\gamma-1}\;.

Hence we define
\tilde{\psi}_{1}=\tfrac{\beta(n)m^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}
and
\tilde{\phi}_{1}=0
. Now applying Lemma
3.7
with
s_{1}=s_{2}=mn^{-1}
(thus
s_{1}n=m
and
\log(\tfrac{1}{s_{1}})<\log(n)
) we define

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\phi}_{k}\beta(n)\frac{m^{(1-2\gamma)}}{2\gamma-1}+\tilde{\psi}_{k}\beta(n)\log(n)

(3.36)

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\phi}_{k}\beta(n)\log(n)+\tilde{\psi}_{k}\beta(n)\frac{n^{2\gamma-1}}{2\gamma-1}\;.

Similar to the last section, one can prove by induction that for all
k\geq 2

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}_{k}=

\displaystyle O\big(\beta(n)^{k}m^{(1-\gamma)+(1-2\gamma)\lfloor\tfrac{k-1}{2}\rfloor}n^{(2\gamma-1)\lfloor\tfrac{k-1}{2}\rfloor}\log(n)^{\mathbbm{1}_{\{k\text{ even}\}}}\big)

(3.37)

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{k}=

\displaystyle O\big(\beta(n)^{k}m^{(1-\gamma)+(1-2\gamma)(\lfloor\tfrac{k}{2}\rfloor-1)}n^{(2\gamma-1)\lfloor\tfrac{k}{2}\rfloor}\log(n)^{\mathbbm{1}_{\{k\text{ odd}\}}}\big)\;.

By the above estimate and (
3.11
) it follows for a suitable
c>0
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{l}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{0<x_{l}\leq m}\phi_{l}x_{l}^{-\gamma}\leq c\,m\beta(n)^{l}m^{(\tfrac{1}{2}-\gamma)l}n^{(\gamma-\tfrac{1}{2})l}\;.

Summing over all
l\geq 2
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{l=2}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{l}]

\displaystyle\leq c\,m\sum_{l=2}^{\infty}\Big(\beta(n)m^{\tfrac{1}{2}-\gamma}n^{\gamma-\tfrac{1}{2}}\Big)^{l}\leq c\,m^{2(1-\gamma)}(\beta(n)n^{\gamma-\tfrac{1}{2}})^{2}\,,

which goes to zero as
n\to\infty
by assumption. Thus the components of the first
m
vertices are disjoint.
∎

By (
3.34
) the number of vertices in
C(i)
which are not a nearest neighbour of at least one of the vertices in
\{1,\dots,m\}
is of smaller order than
n^{\gamma}\beta(n)
. This implies that

\frac{\#C(i)}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}=\frac{\text{outdeg}(i)}{n^{\gamma}\beta(n)}+o(1)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{\gamma}i^{-\gamma}\,.

As
m
is bounded this completes the proof in the star-component regime (III).

As the largest degree was the main contributor in the star component regime, it is quite intuitive that this should be the case in the isolated edges regime as well.
Let us first consider the case where
\beta(n)n^{\gamma}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Lemma 3.23
.

(a)

Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
be independent random variables where
P_{j}
is Poisson distributed with parameter
\lambda_{j}=\sum_{i=j+1}^{n}\beta(n)j^{-\gamma}i^{\gamma-1}
. Then
\emph{outdeg}(1),

\dots,\emph{outdeg}(n)
can be coupled to
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
, such that

\mathbb{P}\big(\exists j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\,:\,\emph{outdeg}(j)\neq P_{j}\big)\to 0\,.

(b)

Let
\hat{P}_{1},\dots,\hat{P}_{n}
be independent random variables where
\hat{P}_{j}
is Poisson distributed with parameter
\hat{\lambda}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\beta(n)i^{-\gamma}j^{\gamma-1}
. Then
\emph{indeg}(1),\dots,\emph{indeg}(n)
can be coupled to
\hat{P}_{1},\dots,\hat{P}_{n}
, such that

\mathbb{P}\big(\exists j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\,:\,\emph{indeg}(j)\neq\hat{P}_{j}\big)\to 0\,.

Proof.

We do the proof for the outdegrees, the indegrees follow analogously. By Le Cam’s theorem, see e.g.
[
2
, (1.23)]
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\big|\mathbb{P}\big(\text{outdeg}(j)=k\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(P_{j}=k\big)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=j+1}^{n}\beta(n)^{2}j^{-2\gamma}i^{2(\gamma-1)}

\displaystyle\leq C\beta(n)^{2}n^{2\gamma-1}\to 0\,.

∎

Note that the family
\hat{P}_{1},\dots,\hat{P}_{n}
is not independent of the family
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
. Also note that
\hat{\lambda}_{j}\asymp\beta(n)
with
\hat{\lambda}_{1}\leq\hat{\lambda}_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\hat{\lambda}_{n}
. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,\gamma)
be arbitrary, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{j=1,..,n^{\varepsilon}}\hat{P}_{j}=0

\displaystyle,\;\max_{j=n^{\varepsilon},..,n}\hat{P}_{j}\leq 1\Big)=\,\prod_{j=1}^{n^{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{P}_{j}=0\big)\prod_{j=n^{\varepsilon}+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{P}_{j}\leq 1\big)

(3.38)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle e^{-c\beta(n)n^{\varepsilon}}\,\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{P}_{n}\leq 1\big)^{n}\geq\,e^{-c\beta(n)n^{\varepsilon}}\,e^{-c\beta(n)n}(1+c\beta(n))^{n}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\exp(-c\beta(n)n^{\varepsilon}-\tfrac{c^{2}}{2}\beta(n)^{2}n)\to 1\,.

Note that
\lambda_{j}\sim\tfrac{1}{\gamma}j^{-\gamma}\beta(n)n^{\gamma}
. Hence for the outdegrees we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{j=1,..,n}P_{j}\leq 1\Big)=\,e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1+\lambda_{j})\geq\,\exp\big(-c(\beta(n)n^{\gamma})^{2}\big)\to 1\,.

(3.39)

Thus with high probability the largest connected component can at most be a monotone path, that is
C_{1}=(y_{0},\dots,y_{l})
for some
l\in\mathbb{N}
and vertices
y_{0}<y_{1}<\ldots<y_{l}
. Let
Y_{l}
be the number of all such paths for a fixed length
l
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{l}\big]\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{y_{l}=1}^{n}\cdots\sum_{y_{0}=1}^{y_{1}}\beta(n)^{l}y_{l}^{\gamma-1}y_{l-1}^{-1}\cdots y_{1}^{-1}y_{0}^{-\gamma}\leq\sum_{y_{l}=1}^{n}\cdots\sum_{y_{1}=1}^{y_{2}}\beta(n)^{l}y_{l}^{\gamma-1}y_{l-1}^{-1}\cdots y_{2}^{-1}\tfrac{y_{1}^{-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\beta(n)}{1-\gamma}\Big)^{l}\sum_{y_{l}=1}^{n}y_{l}^{\gamma-1}y_{l}^{1-\gamma}=\Big(\frac{\beta(n)}{1-\gamma}\Big)^{l}n\;.

Thus by Markov’s inequality

\mathbb{P}\big(\exists l\geq 2\text{ such that }Y_{l}>0\big)\leq\sum_{l=2}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{l}\big]\leq\,c\,n\Big(\frac{\beta(n)}{1-\gamma}\Big)^{2}\to 0\,.

(3.40)

As the largest connected component has to be a monotone path, but there is no such path with length greater than two, we have shown

\mathbb{P}\big(\#C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n})=2\big)\to 1
.

If
\beta(n)n^{\gamma}\to c
, then
\lambda_{1}\to\tfrac{c}{\gamma}
. Moreover we see that (
3.38
) still holds and (
3.39
) can be weakened to: For every
\delta>0
there exists a
N=N(\delta)
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{j=N,..,n}P_{j}\leq 1\Big)\geq

\displaystyle e^{-\sum_{j=N}^{n}\lambda_{j}}\prod_{j=N}^{n}(1+\lambda_{j})\geq\,\exp\Big(-\sum_{j=N}^{n}c^{\prime}\beta(n)^{2}n^{2\gamma}j^{-2\gamma}\Big)

(3.41)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\exp\big(-c^{\prime\prime}N^{1-2\gamma})\geq 1-\delta\,.

Given
\delta>0
and thus
N
, the family
(\max_{j=1,..,N}P_{j})_{n}
is tight since

\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{j=1,..,N}P_{j}>A\Big)\leq\frac{1}{A}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}\leq\frac{c^{\prime}}{A}<\delta\,,

(3.42)

where the last line follows for
A=A(\delta)
sufficiently large.

We have shown that with probability
1-\delta
the largest connected component contains only one of the vertices of
\{1,\dots,N\}
and hence its nearest neighbours. Since all the indegrees are less or equal one and by (
3.40
) every monotone path has at most length one, the largest connected component has a star shape. Hence

\mathbb{P}\Big(\#C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n})\leq 2\max_{j=1,..,N}P_{j}+1\Big)\geq 1-\delta\;.

With
\delta\to 0
and using that
(\max_{j=1,..,n}P_{j})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight, we obtain that
(\#C_{1}(\mathscr{G}_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight as well.
