Abstract
. Parent–child tutoring is a collaborative learning setting with asymmetric roles, where parents guide children’s problem solving while children engage in understanding and reasoning. However, most LLM-based learning systems are designed for either single users or symmetric collaboration, leaving parent–child tutoring with distinct instructional roles underexplored. Through a formative study, we find that effective parent–child tutoring depends on preserving these distinct roles, with parents guiding the learning process and children remaining actively engaged in reasoning. We also identify recurring challenges when parents struggle to understand problem structure, lack sufficient knowledge to provide support, or encounter communication difficulties that disrupt shared understanding. To address these challenges, we present ParaTutor , a scaffolding system that provides different forms of support to parents and children. ParaTutor supports parents with guidance for tutoring and provides children with visual grounding for problem solving. We evaluate ParaTutor with 23 parent–child dyads (children aged 10–12) under four tutoring conditions that vary how LLM assistance is delivered. Results show that generic LLM assistance tends to reduce the parent’s role in tutoring, whereas ParaTutor better preserves parent-led support and sustains children’s participation in reasoning. These findings suggest that in multi-user learning, the value of LLM support depends not only on model capability but also on how support is distributed across users with different roles. Our work contributes design implications for LLM systems that support family learning.

We propose an LLM system that aims to facilitate the interactions between parents and their children. We hope to have a system that can recognize the pedagogical value of parental involvement but also streamlines parental tutoring strategies. Borrowing ideas from the education field, a scaffolding framework is helpful to facilitate the system design. •

How can the LLM system be implemented to support parent–child tutoring?

•

Can our LLM system facilitate parent–child tutoring interaction?

We conducted a formative study to first establish in the Chinese home tutoring context to identify locally common math word problem-solving procedures, common challenges in parental tutoring, and seven identified strategies that were commonly adopted by the parents. We then conducted a user study with 23 pairs of parents and children to evaluate this prototype. This evaluation demonstrates that parental tutoring is more effective when the tutoring strategies selected in our system align with parental tutoring style.
The study protocol of this project was approved by the university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB), and informed consent was obtained from all participating parents.

2.1.
Challenges in Parent–Child Math Tutoring

Parental tutoring refers to home-based instructional support in which parents assist children with academic tasks such as homework
(

(

(

These challenges become especially salient in cognitively demanding tasks such as math word problems

In practice, however, it can be difficult to determine what kind of help is appropriate at a given moment, how much guidance to provide, and how to keep children engaged without taking over the task. This challenge motivates us to address parent-child tutoring through the context of math word problems.

By contrast, parent-child tutoring is an asymmetric and relational setting, where scaffolding must support parents in guiding children’s reasoning across different tutoring phases without taking over the problem-solving process. In response, we examine how scaffolding can be organized across tutoring phases in parent-child learning, and present a system that supports parents in providing timely, adaptive guidance while keeping children actively engaged in problem solving.

3.
FORMATIVE STUDY

To address RQ1, we conducted a formative study to understand how parent and child roles are organized during math word problem tutoring, and what challenges disrupt this role structure.

3.1.
Participants and Procedure

We recruited eleven parents (P1–P11) and two experienced primary school teachers (T1, T2). Participants were recruited through online parent communities, including
Xiaohongshu
, a popular social media platform in China, as well as local after-school institutions. All participating parents were primary caregivers of children in Grades 4–6 (ages 10–12) and regularly involved in home tutoring. The two teachers had 26 and 38 years of teaching experience in upper-elementary mathematics. Appendix
A
presents background information about the parent participants and their children.

We conducted semi-structured interviews with teachers and parents to examine instructional practices in school and home settings. Each interview lasted for 20 to 30 minutes. Interview questions included how tutoring was structured across problem-solving phases, how problem-solving responsibilities were distributed between parents and children, and where difficulties or breakdowns occurred. We also explored the strategies parents used to guide learning and how interaction unfolded in practice, including how turns were coordinated, how intervention timing was decided, and how artifacts (e.g., drawings, phone searches, scratch paper) were used to maintain shared understanding. All interviews were audio-recorded with consent and anonymized for analysis.

3.2.
Data Analysis

We conducted a thematic analysis. Two researchers independently reviewed the transcripts and generated initial codes capturing phenomena such as role responsibilities, intervention timing, phase transitions, artifact use, and strategy mismatch. Through iterative comparison and discussion, the researchers developed a shared codebook with definitions and representative excerpts. Discrepancies were resolved in team meetings to ensure analytic consistency. Our analysis aimed to catalog tutoring strategies and identify recurring breakdown patterns and coordination mechanisms in the parental tutoring process. The notion of instructional asymmetry emerged through iterative coding of role distribution and phase transitions.

3.3.
Findings

Across interviews, we identified recurring patterns in how parents and children maintain distinct instructional roles during math tutoring, as well as situations where these roles become blurred or disrupted.
Overall, effective tutoring relied on parents guiding the learning process while children remained responsible for reasoning and solution generation.

3.3.1.
Distinct Roles Are Maintained Through Staged Tutoring

Across both teacher and parent interviews, word problem solving was described as a process that embodies three recurrent phases, namely
understanding
,
calculation
, and
summarization
, and aligns with classical problem solving models
(
40
)
. Each phase establishes a temporary focus of attention and a shared expectation of what counts as progress, thereby regulating when reasoning advances and how instructional authority and responsibility are distributed in interaction.

The
understanding
phase begins with the parent and child working toward shared interpretive alignment. As T1 stressed, students are required to "read the question three times without adding or omitting any words" before attempting computation. Parents similarly reported authorizing phase transitions, deciding whether comprehension was adequate to proceed (P5, P10, P11). Some described asking children to restate the problem in their own words before any calculation (P4, P7).
Calculation
typically proceeds after shared
understanding
is established. When errors occur, T2 reported that students are instructed to "return to the question instead of correcting numbers blindly", thus going back to the understanding phase. Parents described similar strategies (P2). The
summarization
phase extends beyond answer verification. Teachers and parents both described prompting children to articulate underlying principles and consider how the method applies to other similar problems (P3, P6).

Based on accounts from teachers and parents, math word problem solving is organized around these clearly defined stages. These phase boundaries are actively enforced rather than implicitly assumed. Parents often require children to proceed through these stages in sequence, using them as an interactional script to guide instruction and regulate problem-solving behavior. This staged progression enables parents to assert pedagogical authority by directing how the problem should be approached.

3.3.2.
Distinct Roles Are Maintained Through Scaffolding Strategies

We identified recurring scaffolding moves that parents use. Rather than isolated techniques, these strategies shape turn-taking, pacing, and the distribution of responsibility during problem solving.

Understanding Phase.

Task simplification
breaks down lengthy or complex problem statements into smaller, more manageable units. Parents noted that children often struggle with questions containing dense wording (P2, P5, P7, P9). As P2 described, "For example, use a pen to circle parts of the problem on paper, so they can gradually understand it better and eventually grasp it fully". He further explained, "Sometimes I would ask him to read out the question part by part, according to the commas." These actions make the structure of the problem more visible to the parent and child, supporting shared understanding before moving forward.

Situational guidance
refers to mapping abstract numerical relationships onto familiar real-life contexts. As P7 explained,
"If a child does not know much about the specific scenarios or applications, he may not understand the meaning."
Several parents also described drawing diagrams to clarify relationships. As P3 noted,
"If the question is too long, I will draw it so my child can see the relationships."
However, some parents found visualization challenging. P2 admitted,
"I know drawing can really help, but I’m not good at it."
Parents sometimes replaced the original problem context with more common examples. As P8 illustrated,
"For certain questions… we will use real-life examples with food… to help him understand better."

Calculation Phase.

The most common strategy was
feedback and correction
. This strategy can effectively prevent children from forming wrong cognitive patterns due to deviations in thinking when solving problems for the first time (P4). Especially when children have relatively weak foundations, this step-by-step explanation method is particularly important (P10). During these interactions, parents monitored not only correctness but also their child’s understanding, adjusting subsequent explanations based on the child’s responses (P7). However, this approach was not universal. Two parents preferred allowing children to complete all problems before conducting an overall review (P2, P6), believing that a holistic assessment better revealed recurring reasoning patterns (P2).

A second strategy involved is
demonstration and imitation
. Four parents (P2, P3, P8, P11) described modeling a solution process when independent reasoning stalled. As P8 explained, he would first demonstrate how to solve a similar problem and then modify its values. Parents who adopted this strategy viewed imitation as a means of stabilizing reasoning patterns (P11). However, P4 expressed concern that their problem-solving approach might differ from the teachers’ approach.
As a regulation strategy, demonstration allows parents to take greater control temporarily and then return the task to the child when reasoning needs support.

Several parents adopted an
independent-thinking strategy
to redistribute problem-solving responsibility. Rather than immediately identifying errors, they prompted children to re-examine their reasoning. As P5 stated firmly,
"children must think first."
Others described asking children to articulate their solution process before offering help (P2, P6). Parents viewed this strategy as a way to strengthen independent reasoning. However, they also acknowledged that its effectiveness depends on the child’s competence. As P4 noted, even after prompting, some children struggle to progress, requiring renewed intervention. This strategy shifts more responsibility to the child while maintaining parental supervision.

Summarization Phase.

The first was
review and consolidation
, in which children revisited incorrect problems and articulated underlying principles. As P5 explained, he emphasized the importance of understanding "the reasons behind each type of mistake rather than just memorizing the specific errors." Rather than treating mistakes as isolated events, parents asked their children to review the question to stabilize reasoning patterns across similar problems (P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, P9, P10, P11). However, several noted that systematic review could be too time-consuming (P4, P9).

A second strategy involved
practical application
. Some parents extended solutions into familiar real-life contexts, such as paper cutting (P3) or buying fruit (P8), encouraging children to apply learned methods beyond the original problem. By connecting abstract reasoning to lived experience, parents reinforced reasoning coherence.

3.3.3.
Distinct Roles Break Down Under Recurring Tutoring Challenges

Parents often encounter challenges when assisting their children with math. They tend to forget important concepts, use outdated problem-solving methods, and have difficulty communicating effectively. We summarize the key challenges below:

C1: Limited Visualization Proficiency.
Although many parents acknowledged the importance of visual representations in helping their children understand math word problems, several noted difficulties in expressing mathematical relationships accurately through drawings. P2 admitted, “I know drawing can really help, but I’m not good at it.” Parents were generally aware of whether their drawings were effective. Many could observe that their children became even more confused with their visual explanations.

C2: Knowledge Gaps.

Some parents (P2, P11) encounter knowledge gaps. They have to put in additional effort to align their understanding with the educational content being taught in schools. P2 said,
” I also have some concepts that are not clear, and I have to look them up because it has been so long. When I am tutoring my child, I have to look up the book myself or search on my phone.”

6 out of 11 parents reported that when tutoring their children, they tend to use the methods of solving equations that they are familiar with, which are far beyond the methods taught at school (P1, P2, P4, P5, P6, P7).
“ My child still prefers the method taught by the teacher, which sometimes I find difficult to accept. Sometimes, I can only use my original method to explain it to him.”
P2 added. This shows that parents have certain difficulties in understanding and accepting new problem-solving methods, which can confuse children.
For example, Parent P5 worries that exposing their child to various methods could lead to confusion. This concern highlights parents’ frustration with the differences between their own methods and what is taught in schools, as well as their fear that using a different problem-solving method could confuse their child.

C3: Communication Conflict
. When parents help their children with math, they often encounter significant communication challenges (P3, P9, P10). Parent P10 observed that children often think differently from their parents. This difference can make it hard for children to understand their parents’ intentions and methods. Many parents believed that they had explained things clearly, but children remained confused and struggled to follow their reasoning.

These conflicts largely stem from parents’ difficulty in accurately gauging their children’s zone of knowledge, sometimes compounded by parents’ resistance to or distrust of unfamiliar problem-solving approaches. Although some parents, such as P3 and P10, acknowledged that their own methods might not be very smart, they still viewed them as more reliable than alternative approaches. Although some parents claimed respect for their children’s choices, their attitudes revealed an underlying superiority. Specifically, they tended to honor their familiar approaches and remained cautious, if not skeptical, toward potentially more efficient or innovative strategies brought about by their children (P10).

Conflicts also arise from parental observation of learning habits or attitudes. For example, Parent P9 said,
“Sometimes he shows no attitude, no patience, or carelessness, and starts answering questions without fully reading them."
When children display impatience or carelessness in their studies, parents may respond harshly instead of offering further guidance and corrections. This approach can lead to resistance from the children and further strain the communication between them.

4.
PARATUTOR SYSTEM

Instead of directly delivering answers to the child, ParaTutor is designed to support the parent’s tutoring role while keeping the child actively engaged in reasoning. The system therefore provides different forms of support to parents and children, helping parents guide tutoring without bypassing parental involvement or taking over the child’s reasoning process. This section answers RQ2.

4.1.
Design Requirements

Our design is grounded in the formative finding that successful parent–child tutoring depends on maintaining distinct roles during interaction, in which parents guide the tutoring process and children remain engaged in reasoning. We therefore derive the following requirements directly from the recurring challenges identified in the formative study.

R1: Provide Child-Facing Visual Grounding for Problem Understanding.

When parents struggle to clearly externalize mathematical relationships (C1), shared understanding can break down during early problem solving. The system should therefore generate child-facing visual representations that help make key entities and relationships visible, reducing reliance on parents’ drawing skills and supporting shared problem understanding.

R2: Provide Parent-Facing Tutoring Guidance That Adapts to Different Solution Methods.

When parents encounter knowledge gaps or differences in solution methods (C2), they may find it difficult to explain the problem or guide the child effectively. The system should therefore provide parent-facing, strategy-based guidance that helps parents support children’s reasoning and adapt to different valid ways of solving the problem.

R3: Support Parent–Child Alignment During Tutoring Progression.

When communication difficulties arise between parents and children (C3), shared understanding can become difficult to maintain. The system should therefore support staged progression with shared confirmation, helping parents and children stay aligned, slowing down misunderstanding, and reducing premature correction or emotional escalation.

4.2.
Role-Separated Interfaces and Interaction Flows

4.2.1.
Role-Separated Interfaces

Guided by these requirements, ParaTutor separates system support by user role. Rather than presenting explanations directly to the child, the system supports parents with tutoring guidance while providing children with visual grounding for problem understanding. This design helps parents guide tutoring, helps children remain engaged in reasoning, and reduces the risk that system support bypasses parental involvement.

As shown in Fig.
1
, the workspace is divided into two synchronized panels with different functions. The
Parent Operation Panel
(C) provides strategy-based guidance that helps parents interpret the problem, choose suitable scaffolding strategies, and support the child across tutoring phases. The
Child Operation Panel
(D) provides visual grounding of key entities and relationships in the problem, helping children build understanding without revealing procedural solution steps or final answers. A shared
phase progress control
(E) supports parent–child alignment by requiring confirmation before moving to the next stage, helping both users remain synchronized during tutoring.

Through role-separated surfaces and staged progression, the interface provides different forms of support to parents and children while preserving parent-led tutoring. The system operates as a tutoring scaffold rather than as a parallel instructor for the child.

4.2.2.
Interaction Flows

ParaTutor organizes tutoring into three phases:
Understanding
,
Calculation
, and
Summarization
. For each problem, the parent begins by selecting scaffolding moves and entering the word problem. The system then provides two forms of support for the current phase: child-facing visual grounding and parent-facing scaffolding prompts. The parent uses this support to guide the interaction, while the child works through the problem with visual support and dialogue. Within each phase, parent and child interact iteratively, and the parent may query the system on demand for additional help. Moving to the next phase requires joint confirmation, which helps both users remain aligned and prevents premature advancement. This design preserves parent-led tutoring while keeping the child engaged in reasoning. Fig.
4
in Appendix
B
provides an overview of this flow.

This interaction is implemented through phase-specific agents whose outputs are constrained by both role and tutoring stage. In
Understanding
, agents prioritize segmentation and grounding prompts while withholding solution procedures. In
Calculation
, agents support error localization and guided questioning. In
Summarization
, agents support reflection, consolidation, and transfer prompts. Across phases, the orchestrator routes outputs to the appropriate parent- or child-facing surface and enforces answer-disclosure constraints.

4.3.
Phase-Gated Scaffolding Architecture

Although ParaTutor is implemented using multiple agents, the architectural novelty lies in how the system coordinates role-separated support across tutoring phases rather than in the agent decomposition itself. As shown in Fig.
2
, ParaTutor consists of a
Main Agent
, a set of
phase-specific scaffolding agents
, and a
visual grounding module
. At each interaction turn, the Main Agent maintains a shared internal state that stores the current word problem, the active tutoring phase, recent interaction history, the selected tutoring mode, the child’s intermediate progress, and the current visualization state. Based on this shared state, the Main Agent invokes only the scaffolding agents relevant to the current phase, and does so sequentially rather than in parallel. For example, in the understanding phase, it prioritizes agents for task simplification and situational guidance, whereas in the calculating phase it prioritizes agents for mistake detection, feedback correction, and prompts that encourage independent thinking. Each scaffolding agent takes the updated shared state as input and returns a structured tutoring move, including both its instructional intent and its parent-facing surface realization. The Main Agent then integrates these sequential outputs into a single response shown in the parent-facing interface.

The same shared state also drives child-facing support. When the current problem state contains entities, quantities, or relations that should be externalized, the Main Agent invokes a visual grounding module implemented as a rule-based state-to-diagram generation pipeline. Specifically, the module reads a structured problem state consisting of extracted entities, numerical attributes, and relational constraints, maps these elements to graphical primitives and layout rules, and then renders the resulting diagram as an interactive visualization. Because the visualization is generated from the evolving shared state rather than revealed as a worked solution, the child-facing interface supports interpretation and representation of the problem while withholding procedural solution steps that remain reserved for parent-facing scaffolding. Finally, phase transitions are controlled by explicit rule-based conditions over the shared internal state. Rather than allowing the LLM to decide when to move on, ParaTutor advances between the understanding, calculating, and summarizing phases only when predefined completion criteria are met, such as confirmation of problem interpretation or completion of an intermediate reasoning step.

5.
USER STUDY

RQ3 examines how
ParaTutor
’s role-separated and phase-gated LLM support reshapes parent-child tutoring interaction in practice. We evaluate
ParaTutor
not only as a tutoring aid, but as an interaction guide for parent-child tutoring. In particular, we examine whether it changes how support is delivered, how parents remain involved, and how children engage in reasoning across problem-solving phases.

5.1.
Experiment Design

Each parent-child dyad completed four tutoring
Sessions
at home. In each
Session
, the parent tutored their child using one assigned tutoring
Mode
to complete one math
Test
. To remove order effects, we used a two-level Latin Square design
(
42
)
to counterbalance tutoring
Modes
and math
Tests
. The final
Modes
and
Tests
combinations are reported in Appendix
E
.

We compared four tutoring modes: Conventional Tutoring (Mode A),
DeepSeek
Tutoring (Mode B),
ParaTutor
Aligned Tutoring (Mode C), and
ParaTutor
Complementary Tutoring (Mode D). Mode A served as the no-AI baseline. Mode B provided support from a general conversational LLM that returned explanations, hints, and worked examples in response to queries from either the parent or the child, rather than delivering role-structured support. Modes C and D used
ParaTutor
’s role-separated scaffolding support, with Mode C aligned to parents’ existing practices and Mode D introducing less familiar, complementary strategies. This design allowed us to compare not only whether AI helps, but also how different ways of delivering LLM support reshape parent-child tutoring interaction.

The four test sets (Test 1-4) were designed by the two school teachers in the formative study to assess the same underlying mathematical logic through different real-world scenarios (Appendix
C
). Each test included three word problems that reflected the same three problem types, namely perimeter-based counting, two-category constraint problems, and age-related reasoning, while varying the surface context, entities, and numerical values.

5.2.
Participants and Procedure

We recruited parent-child dyads through
Xiaohongshu
and local after-school institutions between August 2024 and May 2025. Eligible participants were primary caregivers of children aged 10-12 who were actively involved in home tutoring and had access to an internet-connected computer for the remote study. The final analysis included 23 parent-child dyads, labeled E1-E23, with each family receiving 50 RMB for participation. Table
3
summarizes participant demographics, with parents averaging 39.1 years of age, including 6 fathers and 17 mothers. Most held a Bachelor’s degree, and families varied in perceived tutoring difficulty, children’s math performance, and prior familiarity with LLMs.

Before the study, parents attended an online orientation session covering system use and study procedures. Informed e-consent was obtained. During the study, parents were asked to accompany their child throughout all sessions and prepare basic materials such as paper and pens. Each dyad completed four tutoring sessions. Each session was limited to 30 minutes, with a 5-minute break between sessions. After each session, parents submitted a brief questionnaire reporting the tutoring strategy used, time spent, and photographs of the child’s handwritten solutions (Appendix
F
). Upon completion of all four sessions, each parent participated in a semi-structured interview. The interview focused on (1) how the system influenced their tutoring approach to math problem-solving, (2) the effectiveness of different tutoring strategies, and (3) the overall user experience. The full interview guide is provided in Appendix
D
.

The analysis of the parental interview scripts followed the same procedure as in the formative study. Three researchers reviewed the entire meeting transcript to gain a general understanding. Two researchers then coded the transcript separately, categorizing the content. Any discrepancies were discussed with a senior researcher.

5.3.
How ParaTutor Reshapes Parent-Child Tutoring Interaction

5.3.1.
Role-Separated Support Changes How Guidance Is Delivered

Parent-Facing Scaffolds Enhance Parents’ Confidence.

AI-supported tutoring led to significantly more effective tutoring experiences than traditional, unaided tutoring, boosting parents’ confidence. When tutoring without AI support, two parents (E2, E6) expressed a strong sense of helplessness, often lacking the mathematical knowledge or pedagogical strategies needed to support their children. E6 noted,
“I could understand the questions, but I didn’t know how to do the calculations, and when I tried to explain, we ended up arguing (in Mode A, no AI)
.” In contrast, with AI-supported systems, parents reported greater confidence and success in guiding their children. Beyond improvements in confidence, AI support also enhanced the learning experience and children’s engagement. As E2 explained,
“After communicating with the AI (in Mode B)
, I actually learned a lot myself and helped him (her child) understand the questions faster.” E13 shared,
“My child used to give up the moment he saw a word problem. Now he starts by trying to understand the meaning of the sentences with me (during Mode C, ParaTutor-Aligned)
.” These accounts suggest that our AI tools not only bridge gaps in parents’ instructional abilities but also encourage more active parent-child interactions.

Adaptive Scaffolds Tailor Guidance to Parents’ Understanding.

AI offered adaptive support that adjusted to the parents’ level of understanding, allowing them to better support their children. Four parents (E10, E12, E14, E23) noted that the system did not simply offer a fixed explanation, but could adjust its response when users encountered difficulties. E10 shared,
“I told the system I didn’t understand that method, and it immediately gave me another one—simpler and easier explanation to follow (in Mode C ParaTutor-Aligned)
.” Similarly, E14 recalled using Mode D
ParaTutor
-Complementary,
“I was struggling to follow the first explanation, but then it rephrased it more simply, and I suddenly got it.”
E23 added,
“Sometimes I approach problems differently, and the system (in Mode D ParaTutor-Complementary)
actually offered an explanation that fit my way of thinking. That was surprisingly helpful.” By tailoring both the content and explanation style to meet the diverse needs of different parents, the system fostered a more inclusive learning process.

Child-Facing Visual Grounding Supports Understanding.

ParaTutor
’s visual representations—especially the dynamic diagrams—made abstract problems more intuitive. E15 commented,
“The diagrams helped me and my child see what the problem was really asking. It made things clearer right away.”
Because some of the word problems included interactive visual representations, parents consistently noted that the interactive diagrams were especially engaging for children compared to static ones. The ability to manipulate elements—such as dragging points, adjusting shapes, or revealing hidden steps—gave children a greater sense of control and agency. As E16 shared,
“My child loved dragging and adjusting the diagram—he felt like he was figuring it out himself.”
This interactive format kept children focused and made learning feel like a shared discovery.

LLM as a Mediator between parent and children.

LLMs, working as a “fair” third party in this process, also helped resolve conflicts derived from the different problem-solving approaches. Even those who knew how to solve word problems found that the system offered fresh perspectives. For instance, in a problem about planting rose bushes along the edges of a square garden, the LLMs suggested an elegant strategy of “unfolding” the square into a straight line to simplify the counting. E4 shared that such Mode D not only helped their children grasp the logic more quickly and inspired them to think more flexibly about mathematical structure.

And more, several parents (E5, E7, E11, E17) mentioned that conflicts often arose when their child used a different method than theirs, each insisting they were right. With LLMs being the mediator, parents are more likely to acknowledge the existence of different problem-solving approaches. As E11 reflected,
“The Mode D helped us realize that different paths could lead to the same solution. It turns out we were both right—we just saw it differently.”
These experiences promoted mutual understanding and made learning a more enjoyable process at home.

Taken together, these findings suggest that ParaTutor did not provide undifferentiated assistance. Instead, it separated support by role, routing strategic guidance to the parent and visual grounding to the child, while also creating space for alternative explanations to be discussed rather than resisted.

5.3.2.
ParaTutor Keeps Parents in the Instructional Loop

Structured guidance helped parents lead the tutoring process.

Compared to
Deepseek
,
ParaTutor
provided a more step-by-step, structured, and engaging tutoring experience (E7, E10, E12, E19). E19 compared the two systems,
“With Deepseek, it just gives you the whole solution at once. But with ParaTutor, it breaks things down step by step, so that we can really work through it together.
E5 echoed this sentiment,
“With ParaTutor, I finally understood how to guide him step by step. It’s like I could see the logic unfolding with him.”
Similarly, E7 noted,
“With ParaTutor, we could stop at each step and talk about it. Deepseek just gave the whole answer.”

In addition, many (E10, E12, E20, E21) noted a shift in their role, from passive observers to active participants. As E12 described,
“I felt like I was doing it with him (the child) using ParaTutor. With Deepseek, I was just watching.”
This greater involvement not only helped parents better support their children but also strengthened the sense of collaboration in this process. E10 echoed this point, stating,
“ParaTutor helped me ask better questions. Deepseek didn’t really teach—it just showed the solution.”
Taken together, this design of a structured output not only improved comprehension, but also promoted shared thinking and deeper engagement between the parent and the child.

Parent-facing language reduced tension during correction.

In addition to providing practical assistance, the system also influences the emotional dynamics of parent-child interactions during tutoring. Many parents noted that the system’s tone and language helped to reduce tension and conflict, fostering a calmer and more supportive learning environment. For example, E23 shared,
“Now that my child is in adolescence, she often finds me annoying. But with the system, things feel less tense—it creates some distance so she can think without us getting into arguments.”

To support emotional buffering, the system includes design features like gentle reminders for parents, suggestions for positive reinforcement, and neutral phrasing. For example, sentence starters such as “You’re on the right track,” “let’s think it through together” or “That’s okay, let’s try another way”) help parents maintain a constructive tone, especially when children struggle or make mistakes. Parents, like E15, noted that the system’s phrasing made a significant difference: instead of saying “wrong,” it would suggest “let’s try a different way,” which helped both parent and child stay calm. This indicates that emotionally supportive language can enhance persistence and reduce frustration, fostering greater learner confidence and encouraging problem-solving without anxiety.

Compared with general conversational LLM support, ParaTutor kept parents centrally involved by making guidance something they delivered and adapted in interaction, rather than something they merely retrieved from the model.

5.3.3.
Phase-Gated Support Sustains Child Reasoning

Phase-Gated Support Helps Dyads Progress Without Jumping to Answers
Encouraging independent thinking is a key tutoring strategy aligned with the scaffolding principle. Parents of higher-performing children more frequently chose this approach, reflecting their belief that their role is to support rather than control the learning process. Instead of intervening directly, these parents see their support as supplementary, stepping in only when necessary. As E7 explained,
“If he can already understand the concept at school, I prefer to let him try first without too much interference. I just step in when he’s stuck.”

This belief was especially evident when parents relied on the system to provide guidelines for independent learning. For example, E1 shared,
“My son was guided effectively, with detailed problem-solving strategies and hints, especially on more challenging problems, allowing him to operate independently through each problem-solving step.”
This emphasizes the value of guided independence, where the system offers support only when necessary, allowing the child to take primary responsibility for their own reasoning. E14 also noted,
“The system’s structured guidance during mode selection provided a clear framework for approaching the problem, allowing the child to engage in the computational process more independently.”

Rather than accelerating children toward answers, phase-gated support helped dyads maintain progress while preserving space for independent reasoning.

5.4.
How Strategy Alignment Shapes Tutoring Trade-offs

5.4.1.
Aligned Strategies Support Faster Coordination

Our system allows for testing different combinations of tutoring strategies. We also asked parents to choose the strategy they rarely use when tutoring word problems. A Latin Square experimental design was employed to ensure that each parent–child pair experienced all four sessions in a balanced sequence, thereby minimizing order effects and controlling for prior knowledge or task difficulty variations across sessions. Across sessions, aligned Mode C had a shorter average question completion time (328.02 seconds) than Mode D (364.93 seconds). A paired-sample t-test showed this difference was significant (
p\approx 0.000012
). This result suggests that when the system’s tutoring strategy aligns with parents’ existing approaches, it can support smoother real-time coordination and faster progress through problems.

5.4.2.
Complementary Strategies Expand Problem-Solving Paths but Increase Difficulty

Although complementary strategies can encourage flexible thinking, unfamiliarity also increased cognitive burden for both parents and children during real-time problem solving. Several parents noted that adjusting to a new explanation style while guiding their child often led to confusion and hesitation. For example, E12 reflected,
“It was hard to change how I usually explain things. I wasn’t sure if I was helping or making it more complicated.”
At the same time, complementary strategies sometimes introduced new and unexpected ways of solving word problems, helping parents recognize alternative solution paths and become more open to different forms of reasoning. Taken together, these findings point to a trade-off between coordination efficiency and perspectival expansion.

5.5.
Practical Outcomes and Usability.

5.5.1.
Interface Clarity and Accessibility

Qualitative feedback suggests that the system interface was generally easy to understand and supported smooth tutoring interactions. Parents noted that mode selection, prompt presentation, and feedback display were intuitive. For example, E13 noted,
“We found the interface intuitive, especially when selecting different modes. The seamless integration of mode selection and feedback display contributed to her learning.”
E22 expressed similar appreciation,
“Once I showed my child how to use the first question, she could continue the rest independently—because the prompts were so clear.”
These accounts suggest that interface clarity itself functioned as a form of scaffolding by reducing the need for constant parental intervention.

5.5.2.
Perceived Usability

User-Friendly Interface:

The system demonstrates strong usability in terms of interface clarity and accessibility, supporting scaffolding-oriented interaction. As shown in the System Usability Scale (SUS) boxplot (Fig.

), participants rated highly on items related to ease of use and clarity. In particular, Item 3 ("The system interface prompts are clear and easy to understand") and Item 1 ("I am willing to use this system frequently to tutor my child") received consistently high scores, with low variance and mean scores above 4. These results suggest that the system interface successfully reduces cognitive load, which is essential for maintaining engagement during home-based tutoring.

5.6.
Limitations and Design Boundaries

Keeping parents in the loop also preserves parental burden.

A central goal of ParaTutor is to keep parents actively involved rather than allowing the model to take over tutoring. While this design helped sustain parent-child engagement, it also meant that parents still needed to interpret the system’s guidance, decide when to intervene, and regulate the emotional tone of interaction. This ongoing responsibility remained effortful for some parents. For example, E12 reflected that
“I wasn’t sure if I was helping or making it more complicated,”
while E6 described unaided tutoring as difficult because
“I could understand the questions, but I didn’t know how to do the calculations.”
These accounts suggest that, for parents with limited time, low confidence, or limited pedagogical patience, a role-preserving design may remain demanding.

Strategic novelty can come at the expense of real-time coordination.

Although complementary strategies sometimes expanded how parents and children approached a problem, they also increased coordination difficulty during live tutoring. In practice, introducing unfamiliar explanations may slow interaction, create hesitation, or reduce parents’ confidence in whether they are guiding effectively. As E12 reflected,
“It was hard to change how I usually explain things.”
This suggests that not all pedagogically valuable strategies are interactionally appropriate in the moment, especially when parents must simultaneously learn a new strategy and use it to guide their child.

The current phase-gated design is task-specific.

ParaTutor was designed around math word problems, where tutoring can be organized into relatively clear phases of interpretation, representation, and solution. This structure worked well in our study because both the instructional flow and the visual grounding could be aligned to these stages. However, this design may transfer less directly to more open-ended learning activities, where progress is harder to segment and where instructional support may need to remain intentionally ambiguous.

Role-separated scaffolding depends on family relational conditions.

Our design assumes that parents are willing to take an active tutoring role and that children are willing to engage with that role. While some parents felt that the system reduced tension, as E23 noted,
“with the system, things feel less tense,”
this benefit may not generalize to all families. In families where parent-child tension is already high, or where children strongly resist parental involvement, role-separated support may be harder to sustain. In such cases, preserving parental involvement may not always lead to smoother tutoring interaction.

6.
DISCUSSION

6.1.
Scaffolding as Role Coordination in Family Tutoring

Our findings suggest that the contribution of
ParaTutor
lies not simply in providing more help, but in restructuring how help is delivered in parent-child tutoring. Prior work in HCI has operationalized scaffolding through structured prompts, decomposition, annotation, feedback, and dialog guidance to support users in writing, programming, literature review, and healthcare communication
(
19
;
32
;
38
;
18
)
. These systems show that scaffolding can help users stay oriented in complex tasks, externalize intermediate structure, and receive context-sensitive support without immediately giving final answers. However, most such work focuses on single-user learning or human-AI collaboration, where the primary goal is to improve an individual user’s task performance, reflection, or engagement.
Our study suggests that in parent-child tutoring, scaffolding functions differently. Here, support must be coordinated across two human participants whose responsibilities are unequal but interdependent. Rather than delivering tutoring directly to the child,
ParaTutor
distributes support by role, giving parents strategic guidance while providing children with visual grounding and space for independent reasoning. In this sense, scaffolding becomes not only a way of helping a learner progress, but also a way of coordinating participation, pacing, and responsibility across the dyad.

This perspective extends prior work on family-oriented AI systems. Recent HCI studies have shown that AI can support parent-child co-reading, storytelling, and in-home co-learning by facilitating shared interaction and communication
(
62
;
29
;
12
;
15
)
. These systems position AI as a third party in family learning, but they primarily support co-experience or child-facing interaction. Our findings suggest a different role for AI in family education: not as the main tutor or conversational partner, but as a mediator that helps parents remain instructional participants while supporting children’s reasoning.

6.2.
Phase-Gated Guidance Supports Progress Without Replacing Parents

A second design insight from our study is that effective AI support for family tutoring may need to be organized across tutoring phases rather than delivered as a single, undifferentiated response. Prior scaffolding research emphasizes contingent support, fading, and alignment with the learner’s current level of understanding
(
31
;
44
)
. Our results suggest that, in family tutoring, these principles may be better enacted when support is phase-gated. In
ParaTutor
, this meant helping dyads first interpret the problem, then work through representation and solution, and finally consolidate understanding, rather than moving directly to a completed answer.

7.
Limitations and Future Work

Contextual scope.

This work is shaped by the context in which
ParaTutor
was designed and studied. The formative study and system design were informed by Chinese parents’ and teachers’ practices around math word problem tutoring, which often emphasize correct problem solving, procedural clarity, and staged support. Although some of these interaction patterns may generalize to other family learning settings, tutoring practices are also shaped by local educational cultures, parental expectations, and classroom norms. Future work should examine how
ParaTutor
and similar systems may need to adapt to settings that place greater emphasis on exploratory discussion, open-ended reasoning, or student-led learning.

Interactional scope.

Our evaluation focused on short-term use of
ParaTutor
as a research prototype. While the study shows that the system can reshape parent-child tutoring interaction, it does not establish longer-term effects on children’s mathematical reasoning, strategy transfer, or sustained changes in family tutoring practices. Future longitudinal deployments are needed to understand whether these interactional benefits persist over time.

System boundaries.

The current design assumes that parents are willing to remain active tutoring participants and that children are willing to engage with that role. In families with high parent-child tension, low parental confidence, or limited time for tutoring, role-separated support may be harder to sustain. In addition, the current phase-gated design was developed for math word problems, where progress can be segmented relatively clearly. Extending this approach to more open-ended tasks will likely require different forms of coordination and support.

Technical constraints.

The current prototype also has technical limitations.
ParaTutor
runs through an LLM-agent pipeline built on the
DeepSeek
API, and response latency is noticeably higher than direct interaction with the base model. These delays can interrupt the pace of parent-child tutoring and weaken the sense of conversational flow. Future versions could reduce these constraints through faster orchestration, caching mechanisms, and partial offline support.

8.
CONCLUSION

This study presents the design, implementation, and system evaluation of
ParaTutor
, a multi-agent LLM-based system addressed key challenges in parental involvement in tutoring upper elementary children on mathematical word problems at home. By drawing on formative research with teachers and parents, the system integrates structured scaffolding strategies into an interactive parent-child interface. The evaluation results demonstrate that
ParaTutor
enhances parents’ confidence, facilitates more collaborative and less conflict-prone tutoring interactions, and supports more effective engagement in problem-solving activities. Structured guidance in LLMs can significantly improve the effectiveness of parental tutoring. The findings indicate promising improvements in how parents guide their children’s problem-solving processes. This research contributes a comprehensive framework for future developments in HCI-based tutoring systems facing parents and relatively young children, providing valuable insights for designing interactive educational tools that integrate family involvement into the learning process.
