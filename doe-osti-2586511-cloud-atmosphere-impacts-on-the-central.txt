Abstract

This project investigated how clouds and atmosphere-sea ice interactions shape the central Arctic
surface energy budget using observations from the MOSAiC expedition and coordinated model
experiments. Analyses focused on three themes. First, a new method applied to thermistor string data
revealed that snow thermal conductivity is systematically higher than assumed in most models, strongly
influencing conductive heat flux and sea ice growth, especially under clear skies. Second, a full annual
cycle of surface energy budget observations, combined with detailed cloud microphysical retrievals,
demonstrated how cloud regimes regulate radiative, turbulent, and conductive fluxes, driving seasonal
contrasts in sea ice energy balance: in winter, radiative forcing elicits strong turbulent and conductive
responses, while in summer excess energy is primarily partitioned into surface melt. Third, these
observations were used to evaluate and improve regional and global forecast models. While some
models captured key Arctic cloud-radiation states, most exhibited persistent deficiencies in representing
liquid-containing clouds, snow-on-sea-ice processes, and flux responses to radiative forcing. Together,
these results provide unprecedented benchmarks for understanding coupled Arctic processes, improve
parameterizations of snow, sea ice, and cloud interactions, and highlight critical pathways by which
atmospheric variability drives sea ice change in a rapidly evolving Arctic system. Lastly, the project was a
resounding success, resulting in 38 peer-reviewed publications and dozens of presentations, while also
supporting an early career scientist.

Review of Project Activities

As the Arctic system goes through rapid transitions, and many of these are driven at the top level by
changes in the global atmosphere, this project aimed to examine some of the key interactions through
which the Arctic atmosphere interacts with the sea ice surface. The project’s goal was to address two
related questions:

 • How does the partitioning of surface energy budget terms (conductive, turbulent, and radiative
 heat fluxes) over sea ice vary with cloud state, atmospheric stability, season, snow depth, and
 sea-ice thickness?
 • How can model representations of Arctic cloud and precipitation processes be optimized to
 simultaneously and consistently simulate observed relationships between cloud radiative effects
 and net precipitation?

To answer these questions, the project leveraged a wide range of observations made during the
Multidisciplinary drifting Observatory for the Study of Arctic Climate (MOSAiC) expedition in the central
Arctic in coordination with a coupled, regional forecast model to examine critical processes and
interactions in the Arctic system. The associated research activities fall into three primary categories.

1) Role of sub-surface processes in the surface energy budget (Sledd et al. 2024). The surface energy
budget is comprised of two atmospheric components, namely the radiative and turbulent heat fluxes.
The third component needed to balance this energy budget is the sub-surface heat flux, which
represents the interaction of the atmosphere with the sea ice and snow. To quantify the temporal and
vertical evolution of snow and sea ice heat flux, thermal conductivity, and density, a new method was

 2

adapted to MOSAiC thermistor string measurements. Many measurement sites were used to quantify
the spatial and temporal variability of these processes on model grid-box scales, revealing important
dependencies on sea ice thickness, snow depth, and other related properties that impact density. The
median of all derived thermal conductivities for snow was 0.39 W/m/K, which is significantly higher than
the value of 0.3 W/m/K that is typically assumed in most models. Model sensitivity studies performed
using the Coupled Arctic Forecast System (CAFS) regional model were used to explore the implications of
variability and/or misrepresentation of snow thermal conductivity. These simulations showed that the
assumed value of snow thermal conductivity directly impacts the sub-surface conductive heat flux,
which then must modulate the relationship between surface radiative and turbulent heat fluxes. The
impact is largest under clear sky conditions, when the other flux terms are typically the largest, and can
have implications for sea ice growth. Overall, this research helps us better constrain the representation
of snow and sea ice properties in models, parameters that are being increasingly recognized as
influential for accurate representation of the central Arctic.

2) Cloud impacts on the surface energy budget. The full annual cycle of the surface energy budget at
MOSAiC was fully characterized by optimally combining observations from different stations, resulting in
the most continuous and comprehensive data set of its kind over the Arctic sea ice (Shupe et al. 2025).
In parallel, the ShupeTurner cloud microphysics product was derived using combined cloud radar, lidar,
microwave radiometer, and radiosonde data. With these data sets, we characterized the opaque cloudy
and semi-transparent states of the Arctic atmosphere and quantified their net impacts on the annual
variability of the surface energy budget. Results reveal how under semi-transparent skies the radiative,
turbulent, and conductive heat fluxes are all at maximum magnitude, while under cloudy skies the full
system tends towards a near-equilibrium state with weaker fluxes. A closure analysis was conducted
wherein the net energy change of the sea ice was shown to be remarkably consistent with the net
change in sea ice mass (i.e., growth or melt). A second component of research quantified the response
of surface energy budget terms to changes in radiative forcing and showed a marked shift between
winter and summer (Sledd et al. 2025). In winter, when the surface skin temperature can respond, there
are strong turbulent and conductive heat responses to radiative forcing. However, in the summer melt
season the surface skin temperature cannot vary substantially from the melting point, such that
turbulent and conductive heat fluxes are also relatively invariant. Instead, surface melt is the primary
response to summer radiative forcing. These analyses provide a new generalized understanding of Arctic
energy budget variability, and unique benchmarks with which to assess model capabilities over sea ice.

3) Process-based model evaluation. The surface energy budget observations described above were used
to directly evaluate operational and experimental model performance in the central Arctic. We have
assessed the CAFS model and seven other international operational models as part of the World
Meteorological Organization’s Polar Prediction Project. Process relationships were developed that relate
different energy budget parameters, such as the relationship between surface longwave radiative flux
and the near-surface scaled sensible heat flux. By examining process-relationships in this way, it is
possible to more clearly delineate problems in model parameterizations that are largely independent of
errors in representing the large-scale state of the Arctic system. Two model assessment papers were
published. The first (Solomon et al. 2023) found that only two of the tested models reasonably
reproduced the fundamental bi-modal state of Arctic winter net surface longwave radiation, and of
these only CAFS produced this state by reasonably representing liquid water clouds. All other models
struggled to represent liquid water clouds and thus the all-important net surface longwave radiation.
Yet, even if the models were able to produce the correct radiative forcing, the assessment revealed that
responses to radiation were also not described correctly. For example, most models failed to shut down
negative sensible heat fluxes as atmospheric stability increased, leading to unrealistic surface warming

 3

under high static stability. Lastly, models with crude, or no, representation of snow on sea ice were
shown to poorly represent skin temperature variability. In the second study (Sledd et al. 2025), the
annual variation of energy budget responses to radiative forcing was evaluated, showing that CAFS is
very successful at capturing the annual shifts in the primary relationships. Many other models tend to
have key failures where, for example, excess radiative energy can lead to overestimated conductive heat
flux response but underestimated sensible heat flux response. Collectively, these model assessment
activities have demonstrated the long-standing challenge in representing Arctic liquid-containing clouds
and the resultant implication on surface radiation. They have reached well beyond to further
demonstrate how problems in representing surface and boundary layer processes then alter other key
surface energy balances that can have implications for sea ice mass changes.

In addition to these core activities, the team has engaged extensively with MOSAiC colleagues to
support research on many other related themes, resulting in a wide range of additional publications and
public presentations. Some key topic areas include:
 • Cloud and cloud radiation properties (Silber and Shupe 2022; Lonardi et al. 2022; Lonardi et al.
 2024; Barrientos-Velasco et al. 2025; Dahlke et al. 2025; Achtert et al. 2025; Lac et al. 2025)
 • Aerosols and atmospheric composition (Creamean et al. 2022; Dada et al. 2022; Gong et al.
         2023; Pilz et al. 2024, Kanaya et al. 2025; Mavis et al. 2025; Xu et al. 2025)
 • Surface energy budget processes (Smith et al. 2022; Huang et al. 2022; Calmer et al. 2023; Liu et
         al 2023; Perovich et al. 2023; Egerer et al. 2023)
 • Snow and freshwater (Hames et al. 2022; Wagner et al. 2022; Smith et al. 2025; Robinson et al.
 2025)
 • Modeling and assessment (Heinemann et al. 2022; Hermansdoerfer et al. 2023; Pithan et al.
 2023; Svensson et al. 2023; Zampieri et al. 2023; Zampieri et al. 2024)
 • Program overviews (Shupe et al. 2022; Nicolaus et al. 2022; Rabe et al. 2024)

Overall, this project is important for Arctic coupled‐system understanding, serving to link processes that
occur in the atmosphere with their impacts on the sea‐ice and snow surface. Within the atmosphere
itself, this project helped to summarize and characterize atmospheric processes at MOSAiC and to put
these within the context of long‐term Arctic atmospheric observations made at ARM’s North Slope of
Alaska site and elsewhere. This effort generalized our knowledge of cloud‐atmosphere processes across
the pan‐Arctic environment. With regard to surface interactions, this project provided key insights into
ongoing variability of the Arctic sea ice, and specifically the role that clouds play in rapid ice change.
MOSAiC observations are unique in this regard, extending over a full annual cycle to allow investigation
of impacts on both ice growth and melt periods. The research conducted here built on initial
perspectives developed more than 20 year ago at the SHEBA project in the Arctic ice, but expanded
upon them in critical ways and with new observing and modeling technologies. Importantly, these
studies have also given us new insight into the current Arctic, which is vastly different from the Arctic of
20 years ago. Lastly, a major thrust of this project has been to use unique observations and observation‐
based understanding in close coordination with models to examine processes at a level that is not
possible with observations alone, and to also assess and improve modeling tools. This last point is
essential for developing the “coupling” aspects of models that link processes in the atmosphere and the
surface and are important for improved representation of the rapidly evolving Arctic system.
