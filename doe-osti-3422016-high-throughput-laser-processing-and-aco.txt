 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) will conduct electrochemical testing and
 characterization on the partners lithium ion cells as well as provide and validate laser-ablated
 electrode materials for enhancing cell rate performance.

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 • Adds new capability to the laboratory’s core competencies
 • Enhances the laboratory’s core competencies

 Summary of Research Results:

 PURPOSE:

 As part of a cost-share award from the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Advanced
 Manufacturing Office (AMO), NLR will perform work equaling the cost-share value committed
 by the partner. This will involve receiving the partner’s lithium-ion cells, testing and
 characterizing their materials, and modifying their materials for enhanced rate performance
 through laser-ablation of electrodes. NLR will prepare the testing apparatus, conduct the tests,
 validate performance improvements, and share the data with the partner for their interpretation.

 STATEMENT OF WORK:

 Statement of Work is divided into three parts, referring to Tables 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 below.

 NLR and its project partners created a general project plan for development of battery electrodes
 laser-ablation and roll-to-roll scale up demonstration (see Figure 1). The project was divided into
 4 main approaches. 1. The project began with predictive models of laser ablation to engineer
 microstructures into battery electrodes and simulation of their effects on lithium-ion battery
 performance. 2. A benchtop ultrafast laser system with galvo-mirror beam steering was used to
 explore different laser ablation patterns. 3. The laser ablated electrodes were extensively
 characterized, then tested in coin cell batteries. 4. After optimization of the laser ablation
 patterns, scale up was demonstrated for proof of concept on NLR’s roll-to-roll processing tool.

 2

 Finally, large format prismatic cells using laser ablated graphite electrode were fabricated and
 tested at Clarios’ manufacturing facility.

 Figure 1. Overall poject approach.

 To integrate the laser scribing onto its existing roll-to-roll machine, NLR designed and installed
 an interlocked Class IV laser safety enclosure that required no direct modification of the R2R
 (see Figure 2).

 Figure 2. A custom interlocked Class IV laser enclosure was fitted over a portion of the R2R
 machine.

 4

 The Amplitude Satsuma HP2 laser system with harmonic module was adapted onto the
 “backside” of the safety enclosure (see Figure 3).

 Figure 3. An Amplitude Satsuma HP2 air-cooled diode-pump ultrafast laser with multi-wavelength
 harmonic generation module was adapted onto the R2R enclosure.

 A custom dual-axis galvo-mirror beam steering system (ThorLabs XG220-Y1) with 206 mm f-
 theta lens (VONJAN F-206H-1030-1080) was designed and built into the R2R enclosure (see
 Figure 4). The beam steering was used to raster the laser across the width of the electrode web.

 5

 Figure 4. Custom dual axis beam steering and focusing, allowing rastering of the laser across the
 width of the electrode during R2R, was integrated inside the laser safety enclosure.

 To test the processing throughput, several trials were performed at various processing speeds to
 examine the fidelity of the large-area laser scribing pattern (see Figure 5). With the 20W time-
 average power of the laser and single beam steering module, it was determined that slower
 processing speeds (0.3m/min) were necessary for demonstration of the laser ablation on a
 graphite electrode roll that was subsequently used in prismatic lithium-ion batteries.

 Figure 5. R2R laser ablation of graphite battery electrode was tested at various processing
 speeds.

 6

 At the end of the project, NLR and the Partners published a summary of the R2R battery laser
 processing, see Tancin, R., et al., Demonstration of and future perspective on scaling ultrafast-
 laser-ablation microstructuring of Li-ion battery electrodes to roll-to-roll production and large-
 format cells. Within the publication, Section 6 was dedicated to describing options and strategies
 to scale laser processing to a benchmark throughput of 50 m min−1.

 For lab-scale prototyping and validation of laser-ablated electrodes in coin cell batteries, Clarios
 provided NLR with smaller samples of cathode and anode electrodes (approximately 1-5 meters
 in length) and their proprietary electrolyte. For R2R scale up demonstration, Clarios shipped
 NLR large rolls of electrodes (approximately 700 meters in length). After R2R laser ablation of
 the graphite electrode, NLR shipped the roll back to Clarios’ manufacturing facility where it was
 later used in 27 Ah prismatic cells.

 27Ah prismatic cells were tested to determine the electrochemical performance with laser ablated
 anode versus baseline, non-ablated anode, see Figure 6. Compared to the baseline, laser ablated
 cells demonstrated a reduction in soaking time of at least 60%, an improvement in fast charge
 capability with >30% more capacity accepted during 6C charging, and an extension of cycle life
 of >40% during 0.5C cycling. These results were detailed in a published article, see Tancin, R.,
 et al., Demonstration of and future perspective on scaling ultrafast-laser-ablation
 microstructuring of Li-ion battery electrodes to roll-to-roll production and large-format cells,
 https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlepdf/2025/eb/d5eb00149h

 Figure 6 (a) Discharge capacity vs. voltage during formation; (b) Cycle-life capacity fade data
 during 0.5C cycling between 2.7 V and 4.1 V showing the drop-o in capacity for non-ablated cells
 after around 2000 cycles.

 Feasible (now called Liminal Insights) specializes in non-destructive acoustic characterization of
 batteries. They worked with NLR and other project partners to coordinate measurements of 27Ah
 prismatic cells with and without laser-ablated graphite anodes. After discussions, it was
 determined that a team from Liminal Insights should meet collaborators from NLR and Clarios at
 the Clarios manufacturing facility rather than provide equipment to NLR as originally planned.
 At Clarios, measurements of electrolyte spatial distribution immediately after filling the cells
 was recorded with their EchoStat technology. Preliminary evidence suggested that cells with
 laser-ablated electrodes exhibited faster electrolyte infiltration. This was corroborated with
 electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements recorded concurrently, the results
 of which can be found in Figure 7.

 from fitted circuit model.
