 ABSTRACT
 This report serves as the deliverable for Milestone M4FT-26AN080502015
(Report on methodology for elemental/isotopic analysis of fluoride fuel salts). The
review within this document is a summary of the current literature identified by
Argonne regarding fluoride salt dissolution and digestion methods and provides
guidance on selecting optimal fluoride dissolution and digestion methods for future
fuel salt qualification studies. Methods are compared based on completeness of
digestion, chemical hazards, and tradeoffs such as equipment needs or potential to
introduce impurities. Recommendations are provided for best dissolution and
digestion methods for uranium- and thorium-bearing salts, and ideas for key future
experiments are described.

 Best Methods for Fluoride Fuel Salt Dissolution and

 1. Introduction
 Monitoring of molten salt composition is important for fuel qualification of advanced molten salt
reactors, which will include both actinide and carrier eutectic salt components. Some of the most precise
and sensitive methods for fuel qualification include inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-
MS) and inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) which enables accurate
determination of elemental compositions, including major and trace constituents.1 This process involves
dissolving the salt in aqueous media for subsequent dilution and introduction into the instrument for
ionization and analysis. While effective methods exist for chloride salt dissolution, fluoride-containing salts
tend to be less soluble, and this leads to complications in effective salt dissolution and digestion. For fuel
salts, it is critical to have digestion methods that will effectively solubilize not just the eutectic components
(often Li, Na, K, Be) but also actinide (U, Th) and impurity constituents.
 Although some fluoride salt digestion methods have gained popularity in literature recently, there is
not a true standard method for this analytical task. One of the more common dissolution methods is the use
of an acid cocktail consisting of concentrated nitric (HNO3), hydrochloric (HCl), and hydrofluoric (HF)
acids.2,3 While this method has yielded good results, it introduces the significant hazard of concentrated
HF. From the laboratory operator’s point of view, avoiding concentrated HF is preferable, but it is still
important to choose procedures that achieve complete salt solubilization. Overall, ideal salt digestion
methods should:
 1. Achieve complete solubilization of eutectic salt components
 2. Fully solubilize actinide (U, Th) and impurity elements in the salt
 3. Avoid introduction into the sample elements that are analytes of interest
 4. Present no operator hazard beyond that which is justified by effectiveness
 5. Avoid use of highly specialized equipment that might preclude wide method adoption
 Thus, in FY26 we have reviewed existing literature on fluoride salt digestion methods and compared
their merits, both in areas of effectiveness and safety/practicability. This work took place under Work
Package FT-26AN08050201.

 Certain findings are immediately notable from the summary presented in Table 1. First, microwave
digestion appears to be the most common digestion method, though the exact acid combination and
digestion parameters vary within that category. Additionally, nearly all reported instances of microwave
digestion resulted in complete dissolution of the salt sample. This is likely due to the substantially elevated
temperatures that are achievable within the closed pressure vessel used for microwave-assisted reactions
such as digestion or synthesis.
 Beyond the common mode of microwave digestion, a handful of studies compared dissolution results
for acid cocktails containing HF versus those excluding HF. While HF does possess strong dissolving power
it is uniquely dangerous among the mineral acids in that it is acutely toxic. Even relatively minor skin
contact will result in deep burns and may be fatal, necessitating rigorous safety precautions for use. Most
authors in the results shown in Table 1 found that HF was not a critical component of their salt digestion
method,2,3,7,18 and one report achieved good dissolution without trying HF at all.4 One study presents a
comprehensive table of ICP-MS analysis of purified FLiBe, looking at 63 elemental concentrations between
two digestion methods, one using the HCl-HNO3-HF cocktail, and one using just HCl-HNO3.2 At the 95%
confidence level, comparison of the results shows only two significant differences in measured
concentration. Hydrofluoric acid enhanced Li dissolution, but it repressed La dissolution. 2,18 Lithium
fluoride is sparingly soluble in water, so the added HF may aid in its dissolution. However, the lanthanides
are known to form relatively insoluble complexes with fluoride, and lanthanum itself has even been studied
for fluoride removal from wastewater.19 Thus, especially when considering analysis of fuel salt for neutron-
absorbing lanthanides, HF may not be beneficial for digestion methods.

 4

 It is likely that the nearly identical performance between acid cocktails including HF and those
excluding it stems from HF from another source: in-situ generation. Another report, focused on particulate
matter rather than salt as the analyte, identified that siliceous matter in their samples could be effectively
digested by HF made in-situ during the microwave-assisted digestion from the reaction of NaF and HNO3.20
This may explain the minimal differences seen in the literature reviewed here and would eliminate the need
for direct handling of concentrated HF. As a final point of evidence, the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment
(MSRE) salt dissolution procedure used a mixture of boric, nitric, and sulfuric acids and did not require HF
for successful analysis of fuel salt from the operational reactor.17

 3. Method Recommendations
 3.1 Salts Containing Uranium
 For fluoride-based salts laden with uranium (whether as UF4 or UF3), the literature suggests microwave
digestion to be the most effective digestion method. Although most of the reviewed studies in this report
only tested digestion of neat UF4, one study investigated the UF4-KF-NaF salt mixture. They successfully
analyzed their samples for bulk salt composition as well as metallic impurities, performing ICP-MS to track
chromium concentration increases due to corrosion.14 For their work, 1 M HNO3 was sufficient to digest
samples for ICP-MS. Other studies used heated nitric acid for neat UF4 digestion with good results, whether
microwave-assisted or not. One study showed good results for 3 M HNO 3 heated to 80°C (also noting
dissolution of Al, Ca, Fe, K, Si, and Na impurities).16 Another obtained complete solubilization (including
lanthanide impurities) with microwave-assisted digestion using concentrated HNO3 at 180°C.15 While the
aforementioned studies used only HNO3 in their dissolutions, one patent mentions the use of alumina as a
dissolution aid paired with HNO3.8 In this arrangement, the aluminum ions complex to free fluoride,
preventing reprecipitation of UF4. However, this patent also notes difficulties in filtering the suspension
under certain conditions, and even with that issue solved, there remains the need for high-purity alumina to
avoid introducing impurities into the analysis.
 Studies have also demonstrated the effectiveness of nitric acid in dissolutions of the carrier salt eutectic.
For FLiNaK, both 9.8 M and 15.4 M HNO3 have shown complete dissolution of the bulk elements (Li, Na,
K) as well as certain impurities (Mn, Ni, Co, Fe) with microwave-assisted heating to 230°C. 7 Another study
showed similarly good results for FLiNaK dissolution with 12.3 M HNO3 in a microwave-assisted setup.5
Although that study achieved higher calculated fluoride ion recovery by including boric acid (H 3BO3) in
their acid mix, the recoveries were substantially greater than 100%, which creates doubt as to the precise
role of the boric acid. The FLiBe salt has been less frequently studied, but one publication noted similar
results when using a HCl-HNO3-HF cocktail compared with HCl-HNO3.2 It is not known how HNO3 on its
own (without HCl) performs in dissolution of FLiBe.
 As such, it is our recommendation that microwave-assisted HNO3 digestion be considered as the
method of choice for analysis of uranium-bearing FLiNaK salt, and HCl-HNO 3 for FLiBe containing
uranium. While HNO3 alone might be sufficient to solubilize FLiBe using the microwave digestion method,
recent experimental work has used HCl-HNO3.
 3.2 Salts Containing Thorium
 For salts containing substantial amounts of thorium, digestion is significantly more difficult, as ThF 4 is
notoriously insoluble. Indeed, formation of insoluble ThF4 is the basis for gravimetric analysis of fluoride
ions.21 However, certain methods of chemically reacting ThF4 to form more soluble compounds show
promise for salt analysis digestion. First is pyrohydrolysis, where extremely hot steam is passed over heated
ThF4, forming ThO2 and HF:

 5

 𝑇ℎ𝐹 + 2𝐻 𝑂 → 𝑇ℎ𝑂 + 4𝐻𝐹

The thorium dioxide produced may then be easily dissolved for analysis in HNO3 using a microwave
digester.10 While this method has the advantage of separating the thorium and fluoride (and thus avoiding
reprecipitation later), it does have greater equipment needs than simple “heated acid” methods.
Additionally, it is not known how pyrohydrolysis would perform for analysis of mixed thorium-eutectic
salts, as LiF, NaF, and KF may not readily pyrohydrolyze.
 Another promising digestion method for ThF4-containing salts involves dissolution with an ammonium
carbonate solution, followed by addition of hydrogen peroxide.10 This makes a soluble thorium
pentacarbonato complex and reprecipitates it as thorium peroxide, allowing it to be separated from the
fluoride-containing liquid:

                          𝑇ℎ𝐹 + 5(𝑁𝐻 ) 𝐶𝑂 → 𝑇ℎ(𝐶𝑂 )                + 10𝑁𝐻 + 4𝐹
                            𝑇ℎ(𝐶𝑂 )      + 2𝐻 𝑂 → 𝑇ℎ(𝑂 ) + 5𝐶𝑂              + 4𝐻

Then, under acidic conditions, excess ammonia and fluoride may be evaporated off. Once the thorium
peroxide is redissolved in HNO3, the fluoride-free liquids may be recombined for analysis. This method
has the advantage that it removes fluoride via evaporation of HF; that said, this method is also unproven on
samples containing carrier salt.
 Overall, both methods hold promise for digestion of thorium-bearing fluoride salts and are
recommended for further use. Notably, pyrohydrolysis has been proven to be effective on both UF4 and
ThF4, indicating that it may be useful for situations involving mixed U/Th salt samples. 22
 3.3 Ineffective Methods
 Certain methods from the literature have been clearly shown to be ineffective, and it is important to
briefly note these to prevent future studies with low probability of success. First, water at neutral pH,
whether heated, unheated, or assisted by gamma radiation, is not sufficient to achieve high dissolution
recoveries.9 This has been verified on FLiNaK as well as LiF-ThF4 and LiF-ThF4-UF4, and simple low
solubility of the constituent salts (especially LiF and ThF4) in water is to blame
 Sonication-assisted methods that employ ultrasonic baths to speed digestion are also not optimally
effective. Two studies investigated FLiNaK digestion with sonication-assisted HNO3 or HNO3 + HCl
mixtures; both resulted in incomplete dissolution of even the bulk elements comprising the salt. 5,7 As such,
we believe future work should focus on microwave-assisted digestion.

 4. Recommended Investigations
 Based on the findings of this study on published results, we have recommendations for future
experiments. First, the microwave-assisted digestion using HNO3 (for FLiNaK) or HCl-HNO3 (for FLiBe)
should be tested on UF4 in the appropriate carrier salt to verify that dissolution recoveries are still high for
the actinide/carrier combination. For purely HNO3 dissolutions, it may be necessary to test several
concentrations, as reported concentrations in the literature vary widely (1 M to 15.4 M). Potentially, one
concentration could work better for dissolving impurity components and another might be best for
dissolving bulk constituents.

 6

 Second, we recommend testing the functionality of the pyrohydrolysis and ammonium carbonate
digestion methods on samples that include the carrier salt and not only an actinide tetrafluoride. Although
the pyrohydrolysis method could have utility for UF4-containing salts, the equipment needs of this method
would likely limit it to analysis of more difficult ThF4 salts. We specifically recommend testing both these
methods on ThF4-FLiNaK or ThF4-FLiBe samples, but understanding the wider applicability of these
methods would be useful as well.
 Finally, we recommend investigation of microwave-assisted digestion for uranium-bearing salt samples
using the HCl-HNO3 mixture, and the HCL-HF-HNO3 mixture, in the event that HNO3 on its own results
in insufficient impurity element dissolution. It may also be useful to test microwave-assisted digestion of
salt samples (especially those containing thorium) with acid cocktails containing boric acid, as two studies
have highlighted potentially promising results there.
