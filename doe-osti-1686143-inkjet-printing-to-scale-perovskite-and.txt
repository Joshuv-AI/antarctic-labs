 Abstract of CRADA Work:

 The proposed project seeks to develop a method for inkjet printing of the active layer in
 perovskite solar cells for application in stand-alone products or added to conventional silicon
 solar cells in a mechanically-stacked tandem configuration. This technology presents significant
 advanced manufacturing challenges and opportunities in getting to scale, including development
 of novel uses for printing technologies and development of predictive quality control techniques.

 Summary of Research Results:

 For this project the individual tasks that where laid out at the beginning of the project were all
 accomplished and most of the goals have been achieved. The individual tasks will be and the
 associated results will briefly be discussed below.

 1

 1. Formulation of inks with suitable rheology for inkjet process

 In order to inkjet print an ink the viscosity of the ink, in general, has to be in the range of
 8-12 cP. Most inks used for perovskite film formation by spin coating or blade coating
 have a viscosity lower than this. Therefore an investigation was started to formula inks
 with suitable rheology for inkjet, e.g viscosity >8cP. Several routes where explored and
 the one ultimately chosen was the one in which the concentration was modified and
 methylammoniumchloride (MACl) was added to an ink that had been successfully used
 for spin coating and blade coating (Yang et al., Nature Comm 2, 17038). The basis of
 the ink is methylammonium iodide (MAI) and lead iodide (PbI2) dissolved in a mixture
 of DMF and NMP. Figure 1, shows that changing the concentration and adding the
 MACl can result in a viscosity that is within the inkjet requirements. The surface tension
 of the ink for this inkjet printable ink turned out to be good for inkjet printing and
 surface wetting as well.

 Figure 1: Ink viscosity as function of sheer rate for various ink concentrations and MACl
 additions.
 2. Prototyping of and optimization of perovskite solar cells on a standard coupon
 size area (<1cm2) using inkjet printing for the absorber layer and established
 processes for other layers

 Once an ink was identified to use for inkjet printing, the printing parameters had to
 be optimized. Aside from the jetting parameters that control the formation of the
 drops coming out of the inkjet nozzles, the drop spacing, printing frequency and
 number of nozzles to use had to be optimized in order to create good, homogeneous
 and continuous films. Figure 2 shows some of this optimization. In the top row of
 images 16 nozzles were used at different drop spacings. It can clearly be seen that the

 film forms stripes which are the direct result of material drying before additional
 material is being added. In the bottom row these stripes cannot be seen, regardless of
 drop spacing. Integrating these inkjet printed films into photovoltaic test cells
 resulted in cells with efficiencies approaching 18%.

 Figure 2: Perovskite inks inkjet printed using various printer settings.
 The inkjet printing approach was only developed for deposition of the perovskite
 absorber layer. For the other layers, more conventional spin coating was used for
 HTL and ETL on small area (1”x1”) substrates. For large area substrates blade
 coating was used for the deposition of the HTL and ETL. Conductive contact layers
 were deposited by evaporation and sputtering, regardless of size.

 3. Development and evaluation of perovskite layer quality and homogeneity by
 inkjet printing on larger area coated glass substrate, ideally on a size relevant
 for silicon solar cells (156mm x 156mm).

 The work shown in figure 2 was done on a tabletop inkjet printer (Dimatix Material
 Printer), which enabled us to print small areas up to 1”x1”. Unfortunately to avoid
 these stripes from forming on larger areas, the printing had to be done on a larger
 system with more jets and faster printing speeds. Therefore the developed process was
 transferred to the large area printing system (Dimatix/Spectra Apollo with 128
 nozzles) located in a glovebox as part of the Atmospheric Processing Platform (APP)
 in the Process Development and Integration Laboratory (PDIL) at NREL. Although
 that inkjet system had no real issues with printing the developed ink, it required a re-
 optimization of the printing parameter. Figure 3 shows a cross sectional SEM of an
 inkjet printed perovskite device using the large area printing system. It can be seen
 from this image that inkjet printing perovskite film, the film in the middle, is dense
 and relatively smooth. Both properties required for making good perovskite devices.

 3

 Figure 3: Cross sectional SEM of inkjet-printed perovskite device.
 In order to test the large area printing quality, processing quality and uniformity of the
 materials, the films had to be converted into a mini-module. This required the
 optimization of the scribing needed for making a monolithic interconnect. This
 interconnect devices the large area deposited material in smaller strip cells that are
 connected in series through the interconnect. Figure 4 shows one of the minimodules
 that are made using this technique. The absorber of the minimodule (6”x3.5”) shown
 in Figure 4 was inkjet printed. The ETL and HTL were blade coated and the top contact
 was evaporated and sputtered. Using the developed approach, semitransparent
 perovskite minimodules with efficiencies well over 10% were achieved. These
 semitransparent minimodules could be use in tandem photovoltaic applications that
 are using silicon solar cells as the other part of the tandem.

 Figure 4: Semitransparent minimodule using an inkjet printed absorber layer.
 The image in Figure 4 shows a homogenous reflection, which is indicative of a
 homogeneous film. Imperfections in film quality, i.e. homogeneity, would have led to
 a much more radical reduction in efficiency when scaled to a larger area.

 4

 4. Development of evaluation methods for determining perovskite layer quality
 and homogeneity ideally mapping optical profilometry and photoluminescence
 mapping.

 When the project was started, it was believed that optical profilometry and
 photolominescense mapping would be the best approach to determine the perovskite
 layer quality and homogeneity. Where profilometry will provide information on the
 thickness of the film in various locations on a substrate, it does not provide any
 information regarding the quality of the material. During the project it was found that
 Time-Resolved Microwave Conductivity (TRMC) was a faster and easier way to
 determine the intrinsic film quality and its homogeneity. Testing the inkjet printed
 material using this technique showed that, once all parameters were optimized, the
 material had high carrier mobilities (>25 cm2/Vs) and long lifetimes (> 800 ns), very
 similar to spin coated materials using the same perovskite composition. The
 measured parameter values predict that devices can have high device fill factors and
 power conversion efficiency, which was achieved after extensive optimization of the
 deposition parameters for the absorber and other layers of the device stack.

 5. Development and evaluation of scalable processing options for drying the
 perovskite wet layer, including immersion in diethyl ether.

 As stated before, the basis for our ink was an ink formulation developed for spin and
 blade coating that was published in Nature Communication in 2017. Part of the
 processing of that ink involved dipping the deposited film in a diethyl ether (DEE).
 For the modified version used for this work, this dipping is still required. For the
 small area samples it was possible to use the same technique and equipment as
 before. However, since much larger cells (i.e. minimodules) where produced, this
 dipping process had to be scaled to larger area. For this a larger dipping vessel was
 acquired, setup and used. It was found that the process does not need any major
 modification and that dipping times are unchanged. The drying process for the wet
 films was developed as part of the inkjet printing process. The image shown in
 Figure 4 clearly demonstrates that the perovskite film deposition and processing can
 be scaled to larger area without significant impact to appearance, quality and
 performance.
