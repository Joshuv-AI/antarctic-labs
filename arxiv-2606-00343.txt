Polar Depth for Potentially Heavy-Tailed Data
Stephan Clémençon, Carlos Fernándes, Pavlo Mozharovskyi, Anne Sabourin

Abstract
Motivated by the analysis of the behaviour of extremes from multivariate heavy-tailed distributions, we introduce a novel notion of statistical depth, referred to as polar depth . The polar depth function is naturally expressed in polar coordinates, as is the limiting distribution of a regularly varying random variable, beyond asymptotically large thresholds, once its marginals have been appropriately normalized. Not only does the polar depth function make it easy to order the extreme values taken by a heavy-tailed random variable X X and finds natural applications in anomaly detection, but it is also possible to show, as we prove it under appropriate assumptions in this article, that the polar depth of the largest observations, i.e. , observations X X such that ‖ X ‖ > t ||X||>t , converges to the polar depth of the limiting distribution as t → ∞ t\to\infty . Although designed to quantify the depth of multivariate extremes, the polar depth is interesting in its own right, insofar as this notion is more relevant for distributions whose support is included in a halfspace than the alternatives proposed in the literature, the halfspace depth in particular. Here, we demonstrate its properties and analyze statistical issues related to its estimation from both finite-sample and asymptotic points of view. We present numerical results to empirically demonstrate its relevance, particularly for the statistical analysis of extreme observations and more specifically for the identification of anomalies among them.



Keywords:

Data depth, Multivariate Extremes, Extrapolation, Non-asymptotic guarantees, Anomaly Detection.

Introduction

Since the seminal work of
67
, the notion of
statistical depth has received increasing attention in the
statistical literature and is at the origin of many tools for
multivariate data analysis. Considering a probability distribution

P
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d>1
, a statistical data depth function

D(.;\;P):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
transfers the
natural order on the real line to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, thus providing an
order from the center outwards of the points in the support of
P
.
The tools of (signed) rank or order statistics, so useful for
analyzing univariate data, can then be immediately extended to
multivariate observations. Applications in multivariate statistics
are too numerous to list exhaustively here (see
e.g.

49
and
48
), but we can mention
hypothesis testing
22
, robust
inference
41
,
classification
40
;
50
, imputation of missing data
51
or anomaly
detection
52
;
64
;
68
,
among others. Many alternative definitions to the original concept
introduced in
67
, the
halfspace
depth, have been
proposed in the literature. Examples include the simplicial
depth
43
, the projection depth
44
, the majority
depth
42
, the Oja depth
58
, the
zonoid
36
, the spatial depth (
11
;
69
), the Monge-Kantorovich depth
12
or
the IRW depth
55
and its affine invariant version

16
. The axiomatic nomenclature proposed in

72
(see also
48
, for a comparison)
enables a systematic comparison of their advantages
and drawbacks. It lists four key properties that should be (ideally)
possessed by a ‘proper’ depth function (see Section

2
for a detailed formulation): affine
invariance, maximality at center, vanishing at infinity and
monotonicity relative to deepest point. In addition to checking
these properties, the advantages and disadvantages of any statistical
depth are also examined in terms of the possible existence of
computational algorithms in the case of empirical distributions

23
and of theoretical guarantees for the latter
54
.

A central challenge in statistics and machine learning is the
capacity of methods and algorithms to extrapolate beyond the range of
observed data, or to perform out-of-domain generalization. This
concern also arises in depth estimation. Extreme value theory (EVT)
provides a rigorous framework for such extrapolation, offering strong
theoretical guarantees under weak tail homogeneity assumptions.
Incorporating EVT principles into the analysis of tail properties is
a natural approach, as explored in

29
;
33
, where the focus lies on
the halfspace depth under the assumption of joint regular variation.
However, two main limitations persist.
First, the geometric structure of heavy-tailed multivariate
extremes—specifically, the product-form limit distribution
involving radial and angular components, as described in
Section
2
—is not reflected in the family of sets
(halfspaces) used to solve the minimization problem. For asymmetric
point clouds extending far in only one halfspace (
e.g.
, when a coordinate at least is strictly positive, as in many real-world applications if we consider the analysis of quantities such as rainfall, pressure, or volatility for instance), the estimated depth
becomes unstable for test points that are extreme both in magnitude
and direction, namely when their norm is large, while their
direction lies near the boundary of the ‘angular’ support. Further
discussion and illustrative examples are deferred to
Section
6
, which presents numerical experiments.
More generally, the classical requirement of shift-invariance for
depth functions has been recognized as potentially inappropriate in
certain contexts, including directional data

53
;
57
.
Shift-invariance, a property satisfied by the halfspace depth, is
undesirable when the origin holds a special status and should be
considered the most central point, as is often the case in
applications. In particular, if one of the components is almost
surely positive, the asymmetric structure of the point cloud suggests
that the origin should not be treated as a ‘shallow’ point, despite
its halfspace depth being zero.
Second, and more critically, the assumption of multivariate regular
variation imposes uniform tail behavior across all components, which
is rarely satisfied outside the study of log-returns in Finance. In
environmental applications, for instance, different components may
exhibit distinct tail indices, with some being short-tailed. While
relaxing this assumption through preliminary marginal standardization
is intuitive, the authors do not provide a clear path forward, and
the technical challenges posed by such standardization leave the
possibility of theoretical guarantees in this setting as an open
question—one we do not address here.

In this paper, we introduce a new notion of depth, the
polar
depth
, defined on the entire input space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This notion
is naturally aligned with the limit structure of multivariate
extremes and permits out-of-domain generalization with guarantees
valid in the classical framework of multivariate extremes. The polar
depth of a test point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as the minimum
probability mass contained in
polar regions
of the form

\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|y\|\geq t,\langle y,u\rangle\geq 0\},\quad t\geq 0,\,u\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

that contain
x
, where
\|\,\cdot\,\|
denotes the Euclidean norm.
These regions are intersections of directional halfspaces orthogonal
to the vector
u
—whose boundaries pass through the origin—and
complements of balls centered at zero with radius
t
.
We derive non-asymptotic statistical guarantees in the form of
uniform error bounds in probability, both in the bulk and in the
tails, under suitable joint regular variation assumptions. These
theoretical guarantees also accomodate marginal standardization to
unit Pareto margins.
Our analysis builds on recent computational and theoretical advances
in the
angular halfspace depth
, a depth notion tailored to
directional data

(
56
;
53
;
57
)
,
as well as non-asymptotic guarantees for the empirical angular
measure
(
15
)
.

Our contributions are as follows. After having briefly recalled the
elements of theory relating to multivariate regular variation and
those concerning the concept of statistical depth in Section

2
, we rigorously define the polar depth, a
novel notion of depth particularly suited to data supported on a
halfspace going through the origin. Therein, we establish its
properties and study the problem of its estimation from both an
asymptotic and non-asymptotic point of view in Section

3
. Section
4
aims to show that this
notion of statistical depth applied to the limit law of a
heavy-tailed random vector is quite relevant from a statistical point
of view insofar as it can be estimated in a natural way in a
pre-asymptotic regime by means of a fraction of the largest
observations. Asymptotic and non-asymptotic results are proved for
this purpose, and the difference with the approach developed in

(
29
;
33
)
, an alternative to
defining the depth of extremes, is also discussed in detail.
Comparable results are next developed in the marginally standardized
framework in Section
5
.
Numerical experiments are presented in Section
6
,
with the empirical results obtained confirming the relevance of the
theoretical concepts and analyses developed previously. Finally, in
Section
7

and several avenues for further research are outlined. Proofs are deferred to the Appendix.

2.1
Statistical Depth

Order and (signed) rank statistics are valuable tools for univariate
statistical analysis and are used to perform a wide range of tasks,
from robust statistical inference to effective statistical hypothesis
testing.
Since
\mathbb{R}^{d}

2.2
Multivariate Extremes and Regular Variation

We review here key concepts from multivariate extreme value theory and present the classical assumptions—some stronger than others—used in the related works and in Sections
4

5

We recall that a function a function

\ell(st)/\ell(t)\to 1
as

A function

Multivariate Regular Variation

A standard assumption, although sometimes debatable in practice,

Marginal Standardization

A common assumption for handling different tail behaviors

\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}(A)=\mu\{tw,w\in A,t\geq 1\}.

(2.10)

The measure

2.3
Statistical Depth and Multivariate EVT - Related Works

As mentioned in the Introduction, the previous works

29
;
33
aimed to define the
concept of depth of extreme observations by focusing on the tail
properties of the
halfspace depth (
2.1
).
The main finding
regarding the structural
asymptotic properties of

as
||x||\to\infty
is that the halfspace depth of points

relative to
the supposedly regularly varying distribution
P
with limit measure
\nu
in
the tail regions converges to the depth of their rescaled versions,
relative to the limit measure
\nu
. Specifically (cf.

(
33
, Proposition 2)
), if the strong condition
(
2.6
) holds
true, together with minor continuity assumptions, then the halfspace
depth converges uniformly on regions bounded away from the origin:

\sup_{x:\|x\|>\varepsilon}|t^{\alpha}D_{H}(tx;\;P)-D_{H}(x;\;\nu)|\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}0,

(2.11)

for all
\varepsilon>0
. Observe that homogeneity of
\nu
implies
that of
D_{H}(\cdot;\;\nu)
with the same index
\alpha>0
,

i.e.
,
D_{H}(tx;\;\nu)=t^{-\alpha}D_{H}(x;\;\nu)
,
t>0
.
A natural idea behind both

29
;
33
is to construct estimates
of the limit depth
\widehat{D}_{H}(\cdot\mid\nu)
based on an
i.i.d. sample
X_{1},\;\ldots,\;X_{n}
drawn from
P
by leveraging the
homogeneity property of
\nu
and the approximation (
2.11
):
a ’plug-in’ estimator of the approximant of
D_{H}(x;\;\nu)
for
large

is obtained by combining an estimator of the tail index

\alpha
with an empirical version of
\nu
based on the
k

observations with largest norm.

The approach we propose in the following section for quantifying the
statistical depth of extreme data is quite different and addresses
certain limitations of the definition outlined above. First, it
should be noted that only weak guarantees (weak consistency of
quantile regions based on the estimator of
D_{H}(\cdot;\;\nu)

under appropriate conditions for
k=k_{n}
in particular, see Theorem 1
in
33
) under the restrictive condition
(
2.6
) are established for the latter. More importantly,
it does not cover the case, which is very common in practice, where
the coordinates of the heavy-tailed random vector
X
do not all have
the same tail index (non standard multivariate regular variation).
Finally, in many situations where multivariate extreme value analysis
is applied, the random vector
X
under study takes its values in a
halfspace. In many applications, where the data corresponds to
physical measurements, it is very common for at least one of its
components (
e.g.
, thermodynamic temperature, pressure) to
take only positive values, for example. In EVT especially, the
positive orthant has a particular status, as one is often interested
in joint tail events associated with large, positive values (when
examining both positive and negative extreme values, one usually
“doubles” the number of features by analyzing their negative and
positive parts, see
e.g.

13
). In these
situations, the notion of halfspace depth (
2.1
), on
which the approach promoted in

29
;
33
are based, is not
relevant anymore. First because the ’center’ (
i.e.
the mode)
of the mass distribution is often near the origin, which is located
at the boundary of the distribution’s support in this case), has then
small halfspace depth, see
Fig.
1
. Second, the halfspace
depth is not suitable for the tensor product form of the law of
extremes, involving independent radial and angular components: here
too, the extreme points occurring in the most probable directions may
be assigned a halfspace depth close to zero.

As shall be seen, the approach we develop in the following section
addresses all of these issues. The definition we propose in Section

possibly distinguishes between radial depth and
angular depth, which makes it possible to assign a significant depth
to points located near the boundary of the (empirical) distribution’s

asymptotic guarantees for the empirical version can be established
under the weaker condition (
2.3
), see subsection

and our approach can be extended to the non
standard multivariate regular variation case, as shown in Section

.

3
The
Polar Depth
Function

In this section, we introduce the polar depth, based
on the transformation to pseudo-polar coordinates as its name suggests, and
on the notion of angular halfspace depth recalled in Subsection
2.1
. Its main properties are established, and
the theoretical and computational issues involved in its
statistical estimation are also addressed. While this notion is of
interest in its own right, we shall see in the next section that it
is the key to quantifying and estimating the depth of the limit law
of a multivariate heavy-tailed random variable.

3.1
Definition and Main Properties

Here and below, for any positive measure
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the notation
\pi_{r}
stands for the push forward measures of
\pi
by the mappings
r
, while
\pi_{\theta}
is the pushforward of the restriction of
\pi
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, by the mapping
\theta
, namely

\pi_{\theta}(\,\cdot\,)=\pi\{x\neq 0:\theta(x)\in\,\cdot\,\}
. More generally for
t\geq 0
, and for any positive Borel measure
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write

\pi_{\theta,t}(\,\cdot\,)=\pi\{x\neq 0:\theta(x)\in\,\cdot\,,r(x)\geq t\}.

so that by definition
\pi_{\theta}=\pi_{\theta,0}
. For
t\geq 0
such that

0<\pi_{r}([t,\infty)\setminus\{0\})<\infty
, we denote by
\pi_{\theta|t}
the distribution of the angular component of a random variable with distribution proportional to
\pi_{\theta,t}
, namely

\pi_{\theta|t}(\,\cdot\,)=\frac{\pi_{\theta,t}(\,\cdot\,)}{\pi_{r}\big([t,\infty)\setminus\{0\}\big)}=\frac{\pi\{x\neq 0:\theta(x)\in\,\cdot\,,r(x)\geq t\}}{\pi\{x\neq 0:r(x)\geq t\}}.

(3.1)

We sometimes refer to
\pi_{\theta|t}
as a
conditional angular distribution
. Note that the set condition ‘
x\neq 0
’ in the above definition is weaker than the condition ‘
r(x)\geq t
’ for
t>0
, not for
t=0
.

While allowing for a (potentially infinite) positive measure
\pi
may seem
unnecessary in the current section, it will prove particularly useful
in subsequent Sections
4
and
5
when discussing the tail properties of the
polar depth. This approach facilitates a connection with multivariate EVT.
In this context we shall consider limit measures that arise in the context of regular variation. These measures are traditionally defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, not
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The notations, definitions, and results in the present section extend immediately by identifying such a limit measure, say
\nu
, with its extension
\tilde{\nu}
to the full space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defined as
\tilde{\nu}(A)=\tilde{\nu}(A\setminus\{0\})
, for any Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This amounts to assigning the origin a null mass.

We now define the notion of depth that is central to our work.

Definition 3.1

(Polar depth)
.

Let
\pi
be a Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for
t>0
,

\pi(\mathbb{B}_{t}^{c})<\infty
(this is the case for any probability
distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and fo limit measures in multivariate EVT). We define the
polar depth

of any point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
relative to
\pi
as

\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{\pi}\right)=\inf_{u\in\mathbb{S},\;x\in H_{0,{u}}}\pi(\mathbb{B}_{\|x\|}^{c}\cap H_{0,{u}})=\inf_{u\in\mathbb{S},\;x\in H_{0,{u}}}\pi(\{y:\;\|y\|\geq\|x\|,\;y\in H_{0,{u}}\}).

(3.2)

For
x=0
by convention we let

\mathrm{pD}\left({0}\;;\;{\pi}\right)=\inf_{u\in\mathbb{S}}\pi(H_{0,{u}}\setminus\{0\})

Recall the definition of the angular depth in (
2.2
).
Wih the notations introduced at the begining of this section, we have

\mathrm{pD}\left({0}\;;\;{\pi}\right)=\pi(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})~\inf_{w\in\mathbb{S}}{\mathrm{aD}}\left({w}\,;\,{\pi_{\theta|0}}\right)
.

Remark 3.1

(Homogeneity of basic sets)
.

For any
t>0
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\|u\|=1
, we have

x\in H_{0,{u}}\iff tx\in H_{0,{u}},

(3.3)

as well as

\mathbb{B}_{\|tx\|}^{c}\cap H_{0,{u}}=t\Big(\mathbb{B}_{\|x\|}^{c}\cap H_{0,{u}}\Big).

Remark 3.2

(Special case: independent radial and angular
components)
.

When
P
is a probability measure defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
as a product measure in polar coordinates,
namely when
P\{x\neq 0:r(x)\in A,\theta(x)\in B\}=P_{r}(A)P_{\theta}(B)
for any Borel sets
A,B
in
(0,\infty)
and
\mathbb{S}
respectively, where both
P_{r}
and
P_{\theta}
are probability measures, then Definition
3.1
simplifies as follows. We have for
x\neq 0
,
P\{y:\;\|y\|\geq\|x\|,y\in H_{0,{u}}\}=P_{r}[r(x),\infty)\,P_{\theta}(H_{0,{u}}\cap\mathbb{S})
, hence the connection
between the polar depth and the angular depth in this situation:

\forall x\neq 0,\;\;\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)=P_{r}[r(x),\infty)\,{\mathrm{aD}}\left({\theta(x)}\,;\,{P_{\theta}}\right).

(3.4)

This special case will be of particular interest for the tail
properties of polar depth investigated in
Section
4
, as limit distributions of extremes
satisfy this independence property under minimal assumptions.

The following lemma extends the simple observation made in
Remark
3.2
.

Lemma 3.1

(Connection between polar and angular depths)
.

For any positive measure
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\pi(\mathbb{B}_{t}^{c})<\infty
for any
t>0
, and
x\neq 0
such that

\pi(\mathbb{B}_{r(x)}^{c})>0
, it holds that

\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{\pi}\right)=\pi_{r}[r(x),\infty)\,{\mathrm{aD}}\left({\theta(x)}\,;\,{\pi_{\theta|r(x)}}\right).

(3.5)

Lemma
3.1
shows that for fixed
t>0
, the polar depth of points on the sphere of radius
t
is proportional to their angular depth relative to the conditional angular distribution
\pi_{\theta|t}
. As discussed in Subsection
2.1
, the latter depth is necessarily constant over a hemisphere. As a consequence, just like the angular depth, the polar depth is most relevant in
the case where at least one component of
X
is either nonnegative,
or nonpositive, so that the distribution of
X
is concentrated on a halfspace. This property is shared in common by many
geophysical data (
e.g.
, pressure, precipitation, water flow, …).

Our continuity results below concerning the polar depth relative to a probability distribution
P
require additional assumptions. Although not all of them are systematically necessary, we state them together here.

Assumption 3.1

(Radial continuity)
.

The probability distribution
P
has continuous radial distribution
P_{r}
.

The following mild assumption regarding continuity on hyperplanes is required in
57
to ensure continuity properties of the angular depth. It comes at no surprise, considering Lemma
3.1
, that it is also a convenient condition to ensure continuity of the polar depth.

Assumption 3.2

(Smoothness)
.

The probability distribution
P

places no mass on the frontiers
of the angular halfspaces, that is,
P(\partial H_{0,u})=0
for
all
u\in\mathbb{S}
.

We now list the desirable ‘depth properties’ satisfied by
\mathrm{pD}
.
Obviously, the standard shift invariance property is not satisfied,
nor should it be in a context where the origin plays a special role,
as mentioned in the Introduction. However, many other standard
properties of depth hold true, see
72
;
71

Proposition 3.1

(Properties of the polar depth)
.

Let
P
be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
P(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})>0
.

\textbf{D'}_{1}

(Rotational invariance)
For
any orthogonal matrix

and all

in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have:
\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)=\mathrm{pD}\left({Ox}\;;\;{O_{\#}P}\right)
.

\textbf{D}_{3}

(Monotonicity along rays that pass through 0)
For
all
t\geq 1
and
x\neq 0
, it holds that
\mathrm{pD}\left({tx}\;;\;{P}\right)\leq\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)
,
while

\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)\geq\mathrm{pD}\left({tx}\;;\;{P}\right)
when
0<t\leq 1
. In addition,
under Assumption
3.2
, we have
that for any sequence
(t_{n})
of nonnegative numbers such
that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{pD}\left({t_{n}x}\;;\;{\pi}\right)=P_{r}(0,\infty){\mathrm{aD}}\left({\theta(x)}\,;\,{\pi_{\theta|0}}\right)
.

\textbf{D}_{4}

(Vanishing at infinity)
We have
\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)\to 0

as
r(x)\to+\infty
.

\textbf{D}_{5}

(Upper semicontinuity)
Suppose that
P
satisfies

3.1

\mathrm{pD}\left({\,\cdot\,}\;;\;{P}\right)
is upper semicontinuous, i.e.
if
x_{n}\to x\neq 0

\limsup_{n}{\mathrm{pD}\left({x_{n}}\;;\;{P}\right)}\leq\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right).

If additionally
P
satisfies Assumption
3.2
and

is continuous,
then
\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)
is a continuous function of

.

\textbf{D}_{6}

(Continuity in measure)
Suppose that
P
places no mass on the boundary of affine halfspaces and that

is a continuous probability distribution. Then, for any sequence of
probability
measures
(\pi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that weakly converges to

as

, we have

\sup_{x\neq 0}|\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)-\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{\pi_{n}}\right)|\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty.

(3.6)

Remark 3.3
.

For simplicity and concreteness we focus on probability measures in Proposition

\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
equipped with the topology of

(

, which is appropriate in the context of regular variation. It turns out that our developments in Sections

and

do not require such a formalism, so that we leave this topic to the interested reader.

Example 3.1

(Normal

Suppose that

(3.7)

For

When

3.2

A natural statistical counterpart of the polar depth

of

(Non-asymptotic bounds)

Let

(3.11)

For

where

(2\delta)^{d}\leq 1/8

p_{t}^{-1}\sup_{x:\,r(x)\geq t}|\widehat{\mathrm{pD}}(x)-\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)|\leq 3\sqrt{\frac{d\log((np_{t}+1)/\delta)}{np_{t}}}+\frac{2}{3}\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{np_{t}}.

(3.12)

Based on the classic

d
namely, as expected. The bound

Algorithm 1

Computation of the Empirical Polar
Depth

4
Extrapolation under Regular Variations Assumptions

In this section, we study the tail behavior of the polar depth
\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)
and explore
estimation strategies in extrapolation regimes,
i.e.
in out-of-sample
regions. More precisely, we focus on test
points
x
such that
\widehat{P}_{r}[r,\infty)\leq k/n
,
where
k\geq 1
is significantly smaller than the sample
size
n
and place ourselves in the case where the
data distribution
P
satisfies a multivariate regular variation property. This
assumption is legitimate in certain applications, particularly in finance,
when analyzing the logarithmic returns of multiple assets.
The more complex case in which marginal standardization is necessary, and
the assumption of regular variation applies to the standardized data, is analyzed in Section
5
.

4.1
Regularly Varying Distributions - Tail Properties of the
Polar Depth

Turning to the polar depth properties in the tails, we now assume
that the probability distribution
P
is regularly varying with index

\alpha>0
, as described in Subsection
2.2
, where
the notation used here is introduced. Key to the subsequent analysis, an immediate consequence
of the homogeneity
property (
2.5
) of the limit measures
\nu
and
\nu_{1}
in
(
2.3
), (
2.4
),
and Definition
3.1
, is
that the polar depth functions
\mathrm{pD}\left({\,\cdot\,}\;;\;{\nu}\right)
and

\mathrm{pD}\left({\,\cdot\,}\;;\;{\nu_{1}}\right)
are homogeneous as well, namely
for all
x\neq 0
and any
t>0
,

\mathrm{pD}\left({tx}\;;\;{\nu}\right)=t^{-\alpha}\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{\nu}\right)\text{ and }\mathrm{pD}\left({tx}\;;\;{\nu_{1}}\right)=c\,\mathrm{pD}\left({tx}\;;\;{\nu}\right)=t^{-\alpha}\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{\nu_{1}}\right).

(4.1)

Furthermore, since the limit measure

4.2
Statistical Inference in Tail Regions - The
\mathrm{XpD}

Approach

In this section, we develop a statistical procedure for estimating

\mathrm{pD}(x;P)
in tail regions where standard empirical methods fail due
to data scarcity. Specifically, we focus on test points
x
such
that
\|x\|
is so large that the number of training
observations
X_{i}
satisfying
\|X_{i}\|\geq\|x\|
is too small for
reliable empirical estimation. Our approach, referred to as

eXtrapolated Polar Depth
(
\mathrm{XpD}
in abbreviated form),
leverages the theoretical results from
Subsection
4.1
. The key insight is the
asymptotic relationship (
4.6
), which
claims that for large
x
, the polar depth
\mathrm{pD}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)
can be
approximated by
\mathbb{P}(r(X)>r(x))\,{\mathrm{aD}}\left({\theta(x)}\,;\,{\Phi}\right)
. This
decomposition allows us to estimate the radial and angular
components separately, following established practices in
multivariate extreme value theory.

The inference method we propose thus proceeds in two steps. First, we estimate
the tail probability
p_{r(x)}=\mathbb{P}(r(X)>r(x))
. It is a very
classic problem in
univariate extreme value analysis, for which many statistical
techniques are available. For
t>1
, set
U(t)=F^{\leftarrow}(1-1/t)

where
F^{\leftarrow}
denotes the left continuous generalized
inverse of the cdf
F(v)=\mathbb{P}(r(X)\leq v)
. For
r(x)\geq U(n/k)
where
k\ll n
, they rely on the approximation:

p_{r(x)}=\mathbb{P}(r(X)>U(n/k))\times\frac{\mathbb{P}(r(X)>U(n/k)\frac{r(x)}{U(n/k)})}{\mathbb{P}(r(X)>U(n/k))}\approx\frac{k}{n}\Big(\frac{r(x)}{U(n/k)}\Big)^{-\alpha}.

Second, we estimate the angular half-space depth

{\mathrm{aD}}\left({\theta(x)}\,;\,{\Phi}\right)
by means of a plug-in strategy using the
angles corresponding to the
k

largest radii among the
n
observations.

Precisely, given an i.i.d. sample
X_{1},\;\ldots,\;X_{n}
drawn from

P
, a test point
x
, and
an integer
k
satisfying
1\ll k\ll n
, we construct our
estimators as follows. Let
R_{k}
denote the
k
-th largest radius
among the sample,
i.e.
,
r(X_{(1)})\geq\ldots\geq r(X_{(n)})
and

R_{k}=r(X_{(k)})
. Provided that an estimator
\widehat{\alpha}
of the tail
index
\alpha
is available, we
define the estimator
\widehat{p}_{r(x)}
of
p_{r(x)}
as

\widehat{p}_{r(x)}=\frac{k}{n}(r(x)/R_{k})^{-\widehat{\alpha}}.

(4.7)

For the angular component, consider the truncated angular sample

\mathcal{T}_{k}=\{\theta(X_{(1)}),\ldots,\theta(X_{(k)})\}
and compute the
empirical angular distribution given
by
\widehat{\Phi}_{k}=(1/k)\sum_{i\leq k}\delta_{\theta(X_{(i)})}
, which approximates the conditional
distribution of
\theta(X)
given
r(X)\geq q({1-k/n})
, where

q(x)=\inf\{t\geq 0:P_{r}([0,t)]\geq x)\}
for
x\in(0,1)
. The plug-in angular depth estimator on the region
\{x:r(x)\geq R_{k}\}
is then written as

\widehat{\mathrm{aD}}_{\infty}(\theta(x))={\mathrm{aD}}\left({\theta(x)}\,;\,{\widehat{\Phi}_{k}}\right).

(4.8)

Combining both components, our estimator
\widehat{\mathrm{XpD}}\left({x}\,;\,{P}\right)
is
eventually defined as

\widehat{\mathrm{XpD}}(x)~=~\widehat{p}_{r(x)}~\widehat{\mathrm{aD}}_{\infty}(\theta(x))~=~\frac{k}{n}~\Big(r(x)/R_{k}\Big)^{-\widehat{\alpha}}~{{\mathrm{aD}}\left({\theta(x)}\,;\,{\widehat{\Phi}_{k}}\right)}.

(4.9)

Algorithm 2

\mathrm{XpD}
: Polar Depth Extrapolation
in Tail Regions

Input
: Data set
\mathcal{D}=\{X_{1},\;\ldots,\;X_{n}\}
, integer

k
satisfying
1\ll k\ll n
, (large)
test point

in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
.

1.

Compute an estimator
\widehat{\alpha}
of the regular
variation index based on

.

2.

Sort the data by decreasing order of magnitude

r(X_{(1)})\geq\dots\geq r(X_{(n)})
. Denote by

R_{k}=r(X_{(k)})
the
k^{th}
largest radius and by
\Theta_{(k)}=\theta(X_{(k)})
the corresponding angle. Form the truncated
angular sample
\{\Theta_{(1)},\ldots,\Theta_{(k)}\}
and, based on it,
compute the empirical angular distribution:

\widehat{\Phi}_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{\Theta_{(i)}}.

3.

Compute the angular half-space depth of
\theta(x)

relative to
\widehat{\Phi}_{k}

\widehat{\mathrm{aD}}_{\infty}(\theta(x))={\mathrm{aD}}\left({\theta(x)}\,;\,{\widehat{\Phi}_{k}}\right).

4.

Estimate the quantity

by

\widehat{p}_{r(x)}=\frac{k}{n}(r(x)/R_{k})^{-\widehat{\alpha}}.

(4.10)

Output

(4.11)

Before establishing guarantees for the accuracy of the method
sketched above and summarized by Algorithm

order. First, as shown by (

Second, we point out that any consistent estimator

of the tail index of

5
Standardized Polar Depth and Extrapolation

In multivariate analysis, marginal transformations aimed at standardizing the components of a dataset are a common practice. This approach is particularly relevant when the objective is to perform out-of-domain generalization using EVT tools. In such contexts, the probability integral transform emerges as a natural and standard choice, both in multivariate EVT and copula analysis.
Standardization is especially useful when different components exhibit varying scales or, in heavy-tailed settings, when they possess distinct tail indices. Beyond facilitating interpretation within the observed domain, this standardization serves as a convenient and widely adopted tool for extrapolation. It also enables the separation of two key analytical challenges: the study of the dependence structure and the characterization of marginal behaviors.

In this section, we introduce a notion of depth that incorporates such a marginal standardization. We establish theoretical guarantees—both probabilistic and statistical—concerning the tail behavior of the proposed
standardized polar depth
, as well as that of its estimator. We define the standardized polar depth
in terms of marginally standardized variables with unit Pareto
marginal distributions,

\mathrm{pD}_{s}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)=\mathrm{pD}\left({v(x)}\;;\;{v_{\#}P}\right),

(5.1)

where
v(x)=(v_{1}(x_{1})\ldots,v_{j}(x_{j}))
,
v_{j}(y)=(1-F_{j}(y))^{-1}
, see the background subsection
2.2
.
In this section, we focus on the multivariate tail behavior on the standardized scale, specifically
\mathrm{pD}_{s}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)
when
\|v(x)\|
is large.

5.1
The Standardized Margins Framework

We assume throughout this Section that the vector
V=v(X)
satisfies (
2.9

\mu

2.2

\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}
necessarily concentrates on

. In the sequel we let

denote the surface measure on

, with total mass

\sigma_{d-1}(\mathbb{S})=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}:=s_{d-1}.

(5.2)

By symmetry, the surface measure of the intersection of the positive orthant with the unit sphere is
\sigma_{d-1}(\mathbb{S}_{+})=2^{-d}s_{d-1}
.

Tail equivalent of the standardized polar depth.

for large

(and the corresponding angular depth), under the RV assumptions outlined above.

Proposition 5.1

(Limit behaviour of
\mathrm{pD}\left({y}\;;\;{v_{\#}P}\right)
for large

)
.

Let

B_{1}(t)=\sup_{y:\|y\|\geq t}\Big|~\|y\|\mathrm{pD}\left({y}\;;\;{v_{\#}P}\right)-{\mathrm{aD}}\left({\theta(y)}\,;\,{\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)~\Big|

Under Assumption

, we have

.

An important interpretation of Proposition

, analogous to the results in Section

for the original scale, is that for large

\mathrm{pD}\left({y}\;;\;{v_{\#}P}\right)\approx_{y\to\infty}\frac{1}{\|y\|}{\mathrm{aD}}\left({\theta(y)}\,;\,{\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)=\mathrm{pD}\left({y}\;;\;{\mu}\right),

(5.3)

where the latter identity derives immediately from the homogeneity of the polar depth and the fact that
\mu\{\|x\|>t,\theta(x)\in A)=t^{-1}\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}(A)
for

and

a Borel subset of
\mathbb{S}
.

5.2
Statistical Inference

Given the
definition of the standardized polar depth
\mathrm{pD}_{s}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)
in (
5.1
) and
Heuristic (
5.3
), we consider
an estimator of
\mathrm{pD}_{s}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)
, designed for a new input
x
such that
\|v(x)\|
(or an estimation thereof) is large, of the form

\widehat{\mathrm{XpD}}_{s}\left({x}\right)=\frac{1}{\|\tilde{v}(x)\|}{\mathrm{aD}}\left({\tilde{v}(x)}\,;\,{\widehat{\Phi}_{\mathrm{s},k}}\right),

(5.4)

where
\tilde{v}(x)
and
\widehat{\Phi}_{\mathrm{s},k}
are estimators of the Pareto transformation
v(x)
and the angular measure
\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}
, respectively.
In this paper, we derive non-asymptotic guarantees for a specific version of
\widehat{\mathrm{XpD}}_{s}\left({x}\right)
where
\widehat{\Phi}_{\mathrm{s},k}
is chosen as the empirical estimator of the angular
measure, as in
15
, see
also
27
;
28
,
constructed using a marginal
rank-transformation of the input. Namely, with
P_{n,j}(\,\cdot\,)=n^{-1}\sum_{i\leq n}\delta_{X_{i,j}}(\,\cdot\,)
,

\hat{v}_{j}(x)=\frac{1}{1-\frac{n}{n+1}P_{n,j}(-\infty,x_{j})},

and
\hat{V}_{i}=\hat{v}(X_{i})
, we consider here

\widehat{\Phi}_{\mathrm{s},k}(\,\cdot\,)=\frac{n}{k}\sum_{i\leq n}\mathbf{1}\{\theta(\hat{V}_{i})\in\,\cdot\,,~\|\hat{V}_{i}\|\geq n/k\}.

Our main result Theorem
5.1
encompasses in principle any estimator
\tilde{v}
in the definition of
\widehat{\mathrm{XpD}}_{s}\left({x}\right)
in (
5.1
), of the kind

\tilde{v}(x)=(\tilde{v}_{1}(x_{1}),\ldots,\tilde{v}_{d}(x_{d}))
where
\tilde{v}_{j}(x_{j})=1/\tilde{p}_{j}(x_{j})
and
\tilde{p}_{j}(t)
is an estimator of the marginal survival function
p_{j}(t)=\mathbb{P}[X_{j}>t]
. It is important to consider estimators
\tilde{v}
that are different from
\hat{v}
used in the empirical angular measure, in order to allow extrapolation, namely, meaningful estimation of
v(x)
(and thus of
\mathrm{pD}_{s}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)
) even when some component
x_{j}
is as large as, or larger than, the largest observation
X_{(1),j}
in direction
j
.

The upper bound on the error includes a term
h
representing the maximum relative deviation
|\tilde{p}_{j}(x_{j})/p_{j}(x_{j})-1|
. For concreteness we focus further our analysis in Proposition
5.2
and Corollary
5.1
below, on a widely used semi-parametric version of
\tilde{p}_{j}
, see
e.g

17
,
using an empirical estimator below a high threshold and (a variant) of a Pareto model above the same high threshold, namely we take
\tilde{p}_{j}(t)=\tilde{p}_{sp,j}
where

\tilde{p}_{sp,j}(t)=\begin{cases}P_{n,j}(t,\infty)&\text{ if }t\leq X_{(\lfloor k/2\rfloor),j},\\
\frac{k}{n}\Big(\frac{t}{X_{(k)}}\Big)^{-\hat{\alpha}_{j}}&\text{
if }t>X_{(\lfloor k/2\rfloor),j}.\end{cases}

(5.5)

There is no reason why the same integer
k
should be used both for estimating the angular measure and the margins. However, since we are not examining the theoretical implications of the choice of
k
here, it seems simpler to us to use a single index
k
.

Guided by (
5.3
), our analysis is structured around the following error decomposition,

|\widehat{\mathrm{XpD}}_{s}\left({x}\right)-\mathrm{pD}_{s}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)|\leq|\widehat{\mathrm{XpD}}_{s}\left({x}\right)-\mathrm{pD}\left({\tilde{v}(x)}\;;\;{\mu}\right)|~+~|\mathrm{pD}\left({\tilde{v}(x)}\;;\;{\mu}\right)-\mathrm{pD}\left({v(x)}\;;\;{\mu}\right)|\\
+|\mathrm{pD}\left({v(x)}\;;\;{\mu}\right)-\mathrm{pD}\left({v(x)}\;;\;{v_{\#}P}\right)|=\Delta_{s,1}(x)+\Delta_{s,2}(x)+\Delta_{s,3}(x).

(5.6)

Controlling the first term
\Delta_{s,1}(x)
involves adapting some results in
15
regarding the deviations of the empirical angular measure, and Lipschitz continuity properties.
The middle term
\Delta_{s,2}
requires a control of
\|\tilde{v}-v\|
, which is shown to be of the same order as the relative deviations
\tilde{p}_{j}(x_{j})/p_{j}(x_{j})-1
.
The last term
\Delta_{s,3}(x)
above is a bias term which vanishes when
v(x)
is large, under the conditions of Proposition
5.1
.
These controls of
\Delta_{s,i},i=1,2,3
, permit to prove the following main result of this section.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let Assumptions
5.1
and
5.2
hold true, and suppose that
\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}
has a bounded density
\phi_{s}
w.r.t. the surface measure
\sigma_{d-1}
on
\mathbb{S}
, i.e.
\|\phi_{s}\|_{\infty}<\infty
.
Let
k
be the number of extreme observations involved in the construction of the estimator
\widehat{\mathrm{XpD}}_{s}\left({\,\cdot\,}\right)
through the empirical angular measure. Let
\tilde{p}_{j},j\leq d
, be some estimators of the marginal survival functions
p_{j}
,
\tilde{v}_{j}(t)=1/\tilde{p}_{j}(t)
and
\tilde{v}(x)=\big(\tilde{v}_{1}(x_{1}),\ldots,\tilde{v}_{d}(x_{d})\big)
.
Let
(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\in(0,1)^{2}
such that
\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}\leq 1
,
0<h<1/8
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that, on an event

of probability at least

\max_{j\leq d}\sup_{x\in\Omega}|\tilde{p}_{j}(x_{j})/p_{j}(x_{j})-1|<h

Then for any

\sup_{x\in\Omega,\|\tilde{v}(x)\|\geq t}\|\tilde{v}(x)\|~\Big|\widehat{\mathrm{XpD}}_{s}\left({x}\right)-\mathrm{pD}_{s}\left({x}\;;\;{P}\right)\Big|\leq D(k,\delta_{2})+B(k,n)\\
+\frac{h}{1-h}\Big(\Phi_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathbb{S})+\|\phi_{s}\|_{\infty}\frac{64\sqrt{2}d^{3/2}}{3\pi}\sigma_{d}(\mathbb{S}_{+})\Big)+\frac{1}{1-h}B_{1}(t(1-h)),

where

is a universal constant, and

, and

.

(on Corollary

The first statement in Corollary

above threhsold

7
Conclusion

The ultimate goal of this paper was to define a concept of statistical depth applicable in practice to the extreme values of a multivariate regularly varying distribution. The essential property of homogeneity that characterizes their distribution, which is fundamental to statistical extrapolation in extreme value analysis, led us to propose a new notion of depth, which we have named ’polar depth’ and which, by construction, inherits the homogeneity property. As we have demonstrated theoretically and illustrated numerically, this definition, which allows us to view the (appropriately rescaled) values taken by the depth of the extreme limit distribution as the limits of the depth of extreme points, offers considerable advantages in terms of the guarantees that can be provided regarding statistical estimation, in addition to its interpretability. Beyond its initial motivation, polar depth has intrinsic value, as we have shown: not only does it satisfy many desirable axiomatic, statistical, and numerical properties, but it also resolves the problems encountered by Tukey’s depth when applied to distributions whose support is contained in a half-space (
i.e.
the occurrence of points with low depth even though they are located near the center of mass). We therefore hope that this novel concept will find many statistical applications in the future, similar to its use for anomaly detection (in extreme regions) discussed in this article.

\left|A_{+}-A_{-}\right|\leq\frac{2\beta\sqrt{2Q}}{|u_{1}|^{2}-\beta^{2}}\leq\frac{8\beta\sqrt{2Q}}{3|u_{1}|^{2}}\leq\frac{8\beta d\sqrt{2Q}}{3}.

(B.31)

Taking expectations in (
B.30
) and using
(
B.31
) yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\left\langle u,X\right\rangle}{||X||_{2}}\right|\leq\beta\right)\leq\frac{16\sqrt{2}\beta d}{3\pi}\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{Q}]\leq\frac{16\sqrt{2}\beta d^{3/2}}{3\pi},

where the last inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality and the that fact
that
Q
follows a Chi-squared distribution with
d-1
degrees
of freedom. The result now follows from
(
B.24
) and (
B.26
).
∎

Lemma B.8

(Surface area of
\mathbb{S}^{\tau-\varepsilon}\setminus\mathbb{S}^{\tau+\varepsilon}
)
.

Let
\tau<1/2
and
\varepsilon\leq 1
. Then

\sigma_{d-1}(\mathbb{S}^{\tau-\varepsilon}\setminus\mathbb{S}^{\tau+\varepsilon})\leq\frac{d^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\max\Big(\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-4\tau^{2}}},\pi\Big)\sigma_{d-1}(\mathbb{S}_{+})\varepsilon.

Proof.

Let
\tau<1/2,\epsilon\in(0,1),j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
be fixed, and let

M_{j}=\{x\in\mathbb{S}_{+}:x_{j}\in[\tau-\varepsilon,\tau+\varepsilon]\}
. Then

M_{j}=\{x\in\mathbb{S}_{+}:x_{j}\in[\max(\tau-\varepsilon,0),\min(\tau+\varepsilon,1)]\},

and

\mathbb{S}^{\tau-\varepsilon}\setminus\mathbb{S}^{\tau+\varepsilon}\subset\cup_{j\leq d}M_{j}
, so that

\sigma_{d-1}(\mathbb{S}^{\tau-\varepsilon}\setminus\mathbb{S}^{\tau+\varepsilon})\leq d\sigma_{d-1}(M_{j}).

(B.32)

Now for fixed
j
, let
\Delta_{j}
denote the subset of
\mathbb{S}
comprised between the parallel circles

\mathcal{C}_{\max(\tau-\varepsilon,0)}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\min(\tau+\varepsilon,1)}
, where
\mathcal{C}_{b}=\{x\in\mathbb{S}:x_{j}=b\}
. Then
M_{j}=\Delta_{j}\cap\mathbb{S}_{+}
, and by symmetry with respect to the
d-1
coordinates different from
j
, we have

\sigma_{d-1}(M_{j})=2^{-d+1}\sigma_{d-1}(\Delta_{j}).

(B.33)

We are left with computing the surface measure
\sigma_{d-1}(\Delta_{j})
.
For
0<b<1
, the circle
\mathcal{C}_{b}
is isometric with the sphere in the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
with radius
r_{b}=\sqrt{1-b^{2}}\leq 1
. Recall from (
5.2
) that that the surface of the
d-2
-dimensional unit sphere is

s_{d-2}=\frac{2\pi^{(d-1)/2}}{\Gamma((d-1)/2)},

so that

\displaystyle\frac{s_{d-2}}{s_{d-1}}=\frac{\pi^{-1/2}\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma((d-1)/2)}\leq\sqrt{\frac{d}{2\pi}},

where we have used Gautschi’s inequality to bound from above the ratio of Gamma’s.
Whence, for
0\leq b\leq 1
, we have

\sigma_{d-2}(\mathcal{C}_{b})=r_{b}^{d-2}s_{d-2}=\leq\sqrt{\frac{d}{2\pi}}\sigma_{d-1}(\mathbb{S}).

Finally, for
\varepsilon<\tau<1/2
, the geodesic distance between the two circles
\mathcal{C}_{\tau-\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\tau+\varepsilon}
is

\rho(\mathcal{C}_{\tau-\varepsilon},\mathcal{C}_{\tau+\varepsilon})=\arcsin(\tau+\varepsilon)-\arcsin(\tau-\varepsilon)\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-(\tau+\varepsilon)^{2}}}\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-4\tau^{2}}}.

Also for
\varepsilon\geq\tau
,

\rho(\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{\min(\tau+\varepsilon,1)})\leq\rho(\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{\min(2\varepsilon,1)})\leq\arcsin(\min(2\varepsilon,1))\leq\pi\varepsilon,

where the last inequality is obtained by studying the variations of the functions
x\mapsto\arcsin(\min(2x,1))/x
on
(0,1)
.
Combining the latter three displays, we obtain

\sigma_{d-1}(\Delta_{j})\leq\sup_{0\leq b\leq 1}\sigma_{d-1}(\mathcal{C}_{b})\rho(\mathcal{C}_{\max(\tau-\varepsilon,0)},\mathcal{C}_{\min(\tau+\varepsilon,1)})\leq\sqrt{\frac{d}{2\pi}}\sigma_{d-1}(\mathbb{S})\max\Big(\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-4\tau^{2}}},{\pi}\Big)\varepsilon.

(B.34)

Finally, combining Equations (
B.32
), (
B.33
), (
B.34
) and using
\sigma(\mathbb{S}_{+})=2^{-d}\sigma_{d-1}(\mathbb{S})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sigma_{d-1}(\mathbb{S}^{\tau-\varepsilon}\setminus\mathbb{S}^{\tau+\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq d\sigma_{d-1}(M_{j})\leq d\frac{\sigma_{d-1}(\Delta_{j})}{2^{d-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\max\Big(\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-4\tau^{2}}},\pi\Big)\frac{\sigma_{d-1}(\mathbb{S})}{2^{d-1}}\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\max\Big(\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-4\tau^{2}}},\pi\Big)\sigma_{d-1}(\mathbb{S}_{+})\varepsilon.

∎

Lemma B.9

(Controlling the error of
\tilde{v}
from that of the
\tilde{p}_{j}
’s)
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that with

h=\max_{j}|\tilde{p}_{j}(x_{j})/p_{j}(x_{j})-1|,

we have
|h|<1
. Then

\|\tilde{v}(x)-v(x)\|\leq\min\Big(~\frac{h}{1-h}\|v(x)\|~,~h\|\tilde{v}(x)\|~\Big)\leq\frac{h}{1-h}\min\big(\|v(x)\|,\|\tilde{v}(x)\|\big).

Proof of Lemma
B.9
.

For
j\leq d
we have by hypothesis,

v_{j}(x_{j})/\tilde{v}_{j}(x_{j})\in[1-h,1+h],

(B.35)

so
|{v}_{j}(x_{j})-\tilde{v}_{j}(x_{j})|\leq h\tilde{v}_{j}(x_{j})
, or equivalently

|{v}_{j}(x_{j})-\tilde{v}_{j}(x_{j})|^{2}\leq h^{2}\tilde{v}_{j}(x_{j})^{2}.

Summing over
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and taking the squared root yields

\|\tilde{v}(x)-v(x)\|\leq h\|\tilde{v}(x)\|
.

Now by inversion of (
B.35
), also

\tilde{v}_{j}(x_{j})/{v}_{j}(x_{j})\in[(1+h)^{-1},(1-h)^{-1}]
, thus

|\tilde{v}_{j}(x_{j})/{v}_{j}(x_{j})-1|\leq h/(1-h).

Repeating the previous argument with
\tilde{v}
and
v

interchanged and
h
replaced with
h/(1-h)
yields
\|\tilde{v}(x)-v(x)\|\leq\|v(x)\|h/(1-h)
.
The result follows.
∎
