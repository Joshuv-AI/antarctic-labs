 ABSTRACT

Most nuclear fuels irradiations at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) over the past decade have been
conducted using MiniFuel—a static capsule design employing subscale fuel specimens to collect
separate-effects irradiated fuel performance data. Irradiation conditions for MiniFuel experiments are
predicted pre-test using reactor physics, and thermal models are verified post-irradiation via SiC
dilatometry and various spectrometry methods. Relevant fuel performance parameters are also observed
post-irradiation in a hot cell, thereby providing a single data point for each parameter representing the
cumulative effects of the irradiation conditions. Substantially more data can be harvested from a single
test and within a shorter duration by instrumenting irradiation vehicles and measuring desired quantities
in situ. This report presents the design and analysis of the MiniFuel INstrumented Irradiation Test
Apparatus for Understanding Radiation Effects (MINITAURE)—an instrumented test rig based on the
separate-effects MiniFuel concept that aims to capture fission gas release (FGR) and thermal conductivity
degradation of fuel specimens during irradiation in the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR). MINITAURE
will be integrated with the Materials Irradiation Facility (MIF) located in the HFIR building outside the
reactor containment. The MIF will act as the instrumentation and control center for the experiment,
enabling real-time feedback from in situ sensors and control of irradiation temperatures via a gas delivery
system. Two unique capsule designs were developed to capture each phenomenon: the thermal
conductivity capsule, which uses a thermopile method to estimate fuel specimen thermal conductivity,
and the fission gas release capsule, which will have continuous flowing gas communication to high-purity
germanium detectors that are housed in the MIF for monitoring FGR. This report details the reactor
physics and heat transfer modeling activities that were used to inform the experiment design and predict
capsule performance. It also describes out-of-pile activities conducted to stand up this new capability and
verify the measurement techniques. Modeling efforts to date have demonstrated the feasibility of the in
situ measurement techniques and supported the development of the MINITAURE assembly
configuration. Out-of-pile testing of the thermal conductivity measurement shows promise in capturing
relative changes in thermal conductivity. However, significant errors exist in the measured absolute value,
posing a need for further refinement.

 1. INTRODUCTION

As nuclear fuel undergoes irradiation in a reactor, radiation damage and the accumulation of fission
products drive microstructural evolution and degradation of fuel thermophysical properties.
Understanding the evolution of these properties is crucial in predicting fuel performance and for eventual
qualification of fuel concepts for commercial use. Fuel performance is a primary focus of many separate-
effects and integral irradiation experiments in materials test reactors. Data collected from these tests form
the technical basis for qualifying fuels as well as for developing and validating computer models. Most
nuclear fuel irradiation campaigns follow the sequential process of irradiating specimens in passive
capsules, transferring radioactive materials to a hot cell, and then performing examinations to assess fuel
performance phenomena. While effective, this approach requires long irradiation periods and produces
post-irradiation measurements that capture only the cumulative effects of the test conditions. Passive
methods for post-irradiation verification of experiment conditions, namely temperature and burnup, are
often integrated into capsule designs, but these techniques provide time-averaged or terminal values that
do not fully capture evolving or local conditions. Instrumenting irradiation vehicles overcomes these
limitations by extracting substantially more data from a single test while also providing information on
the evolving irradiation conditions and fuel behavior as they occur. Although such instrumentation has
been leveraged extensively in the past, recent initiatives to accelerate nuclear fuel qualification [1] have
brought in situ measurement techniques to the forefront of nuclear fuel research and development.

 1

For nearly a decade, most passive separate-effects fuels irradiations at Oak Ridge National Laboratory
(ORNL) have been conducted via the MiniFuel irradiation platform [2]. MiniFuel employs subscale
geometry (<4 mm3) fuel samples within a larger structural containment to maintain tight burnup control
and a near-constant specimen temperature over the course of irradiation, relying on nuclear heating in the
structural material to drive temperatures in lieu of fission heating in the fuel specimen. Dozens of
MiniFuel targets bearing a wide variety of fuel forms have been deployed in the High Flux Isotope
Reactor (HFIR) to obtain commercially relevant discharge burnups in short test durations. These tests
have successfully isolated experimental variables to better understand their influence on fuel irradiation
performance. To improve the fidelity of these experiments and collect real-time fuel performance data, a
new test apparatus has been designed to accommodate thermocouple and gas line instrumentation while
still leveraging the separate-effects MiniFuel concept. The design and analysis of this new capability—
deemed the MiniFuel INstrumented Irradiation Test Apparatus for Understanding Radiation Effects
(MINITAURE)—are detailed in this report.

The first iteration of MINITAURE aims to capture two critical fuel performance phenomena: thermal
conductivity degradation and fission gas release (FGR) with the possibility of incorporating temperature
transients in the future via variable sweep composition and flow rate. In commercial reactors, thermal
conductivity governs peak fuel temperatures and how efficiently heat generated in the fuel is transferred
to the coolant. As radiation damage and fission products accumulate within the fuel, its thermal
conductivity degrades, hindering reactor performance and safety [3]. Gaseous fission product release from
the fuel is also a key performance characteristic because it can reduce the thermal conductivity of radial
fuel–cladding gas gaps and increase the internal pressure of sealed fuel rods. Thermal conductivity
evolution and the release process of gaseous fission products—primarily Kr and Xe isotopes—are
complex phenomena that depend on several variables, including burnup, temperature, fission density,
microstructure, and the stoichiometry of the fuel [3, 4, 5]. The MINITAURE experimental objectives will
be met by measuring these quantities separately in two unique capsule designs that are described later in
this report. In addition to the fuel-bearing capsules, MINITAURE will be equipped with numerous gas
lines for temperature control with the goal of maintaining constant specimen temperatures while capturing
the desired quantities as a function of burnup.

 2. EXPERIMENT DESIGN AND MEASUREMENT TECHNIQUES

This section details the designs of the thermal conductivity capsule (TCC), thermal conductivity
measurement technique, fission gas release capsule (FGRC), fission gas monitoring system (FGMS), and
the overall layout of MINITAURE. Instrumented HFIR experiments are most readily conducted in the
removable beryllium (RB) reflector because these positions have direct line-of-sight access to
penetrations in the pressure vessel quick access hatch. Therefore, MINITAURE will be deployed in the
RB reflector and will have much of the same ex-core architecture as previous instrumented HFIR
experiments, the most recent of which was WIRE-21 [6]. Monolithic UO2 fuel discs are the planned fuel
form for the initial iteration of the experiment to benchmark the capability against existing data. All
designs and analysis in this report therefore assume UO2 as the fuel material, but the concepts are
applicable to any fuel type.

2.1 THERMAL CONDUCTIVITY CAPSULE (TCC)

The basic elements needed to measure thermal conductivity are a known heat source that drives a
temperature gradient across a test specimen, a means for measuring the temperature gradient, precisely
known geometries, and a model that relates these known quantities, which in this case is Fourier’s law.
Accurate measurements require unidirectional heat flow with negligible losses to the environment,
particularly for low thermal conductivity materials like UO2. In irradiation experiments, heat is generated

 2

internally in all experimental components via various nuclear and gamma reactions, and temperatures are
measured using thermocouples. The extreme neutron flux in HFIR and the separate-effects objectives of
the experiment necessitate small fuel specimens, which in turn precludes joining thermocouples directly
to the fuel. Therefore, substrate materials are required to hold the specimen, and thermocouples will be
joined to these substrates.

The TCC, shown in Figure 1, was designed with consideration of these constraints. A series of three
thermocouples referred to as a thermopile are integrated into the capsule to provide temperature
measurements, and the capsule is designed so that heat transfers vertically upward. The fuel specimen
will be a natural UO₂ disc with an approximate outer diameter of 5–7 mm and a thickness of 0.5 mm.
Because the primary goal is to measure thermal conductivity at temperatures and burnups representative
of commercial light-water reactor fuel conditions, the capsule was designed to achieve specimen
temperatures of at least 600°C to approximately 1,000°C. Stainless steel (SST) was selected as the
housing material for its weldability and weld compatibility with vanadium (V), which is used for the
upper substrate material. Thermal contact resistances are reduced by welding the V slug to the SST
housing under a preload condition that applies significant contact pressure to all internal interfaces.
Additionally, the weld between the capsule and centering thimble creates a critical heat transfer pathway
to the test rig’s outer containment tube, which contacts HFIR’s coolant on the outer wall. Lastly, all three
thermocouples will be tack-welded in place near their installation sites.

 Figure 1. Depiction of the TCC.

The lower substrate material is Mo. Molybdenum was chosen for its high melting point, mechanical
strength at elevated temperatures, and thermal conductivity, which minimizes the difference between the
measured temperature and the true bottom surface temperature of the specimen. The upper substrate
material is V, which was selected for its moderate thermal conductivity that promotes 1D heat transfer
while maintaining a sufficient temperature difference (Δ𝑇) between its two embedded thermocouples.
This Δ𝑇 is expected to be roughly one order of magnitude greater than the measurement uncertainty of
N-type thermocouples. The V component and upper part of the Mo piece were sized to adequately secure
the thermocouples while allowing a large gas gap between the SST housing to promote 1D heat flow. The
Mo part also serves the additional functions of generating gamma heat to drive high temperatures and to

 3

maintain contact pressure in the thermopile; the narrow shaft region of the part is sized to balance
mechanical strength and total heat generation. The shaft length is intended to be a variable length
dimension that can be reduced the closer the capsule is located to the HFIR midplane. It should be noted
that the thermocouple embedded in the Mo substrate enters the capsule through a window machined in the
SST housing, so the capsule is not hermetically sealed.

To control heat flow and protect structural materials, an alumina spacer is positioned near the bottom of
the capsule to reduce heat conduction from the Mo component—which is expected to be the hottest
element—to the SST housing, thereby mitigating risks of creep or strength loss. Grafoil discs are also
included in the lower assembly to accommodate thermal expansion, maintain mechanical preload between
internal components, and assist in controlling temperature gradients. Large internal gas gaps, along with a
direct conduction pathway from the primary heat source to the coolant, support predominantly 1D upward
heat transfer with minimal radial losses. All critical dimensions of the capsule, including the precise
locations of the installed thermocouples, will be inspected prior to assembly. Heat losses due to thermal
contact resistance between bodies are mitigated by welding the capsule under a compressive load,
polishing all fuel and substrate interfaces to <1 µm surface roughness, and operating the experiment in
inert gas pressurized to ~1–2 atm. These conditions promote solid spot and gas gap conductance and
thereby minimize temperature gradients across the material interfaces that could induce error in the
measurement. Nonetheless, very high heat fluxes through the thermopile are expected, which will cause
some heat loss to occur. The temperature gradients at material interfaces can be accounted for using
thermal contact conductance equations, such as those described by Toptan et al. [7].

2.3 FISSION GAS RELEASE CAPSULE (FGRC) AND FISSION GAS MONITORING
 SYSTEM (FGMS)

Online monitoring of radioactive fission products has been implemented at numerous materials test
reactors worldwide. Notable examples include the Halden Reactor, which operated such systems for
decades [8], the DIDO reactor at Harwell [9], the Advanced Test Reactor [10], and several experimental
campaigns at HFIR [11]. The concepts and engineering solutions developed for these facilities informed
both the design and modeling of the instrumented FGRC and its supporting instrumentation. This section
outlines the architecture of the FGRC and FGMS.

 7

FGR measurements will be conducted via two primary elements: an in-core subcapsule containing
miniature fuel samples and the FGMS, which comprises an external high-purity germanium (HPGe)
detection system situated in the HFIR MIF. The MIF, positioned adjacent to the reactor, is a dedicated
laboratory space for managing HFIR’s instrumented experiments. It is equipped with multiple inert gas
supply banks, mass flow controllers, thermocouple logging systems, and lead-shielded exhaust gas
conduits. A programmable control module in the MIF reads thermocouple data from experiments and
adjusts sweep gas compositions to maintain desired temperature setpoints. During the FGRC design
phase, a site survey was conducted in the MIF to identify the optimal location for the out-of-pile HPGe
detection system. This evaluation included background gamma measurements, taken with a portable
HPGe detector during reactor operation, and structural load assessments to verify that the HPGe system
and its lead shielding could be adequately supported.

The installation site, shown in Figure 3, is located on the east wall of the MIF’s shielded valve box. A
lead-shielded conduit will link the valve box to two HPGe detectors and enclose four gas lines (1/16 in.
diameter each) that transport sweep gas from the in-core capsule to the MIF. Upon arrival at the MIF, the
sweep gas enters a dwell chamber within a 4 in. thick lead shield. This chamber, with an internal free
volume of roughly 50 cm3, contains baffles that promote gas mixing and retention for a fixed period. The
dwell chamber is enclosed within a secondary containment vessel for leak monitoring of the primary
sweep lines. The entire assembly is positioned above an HPGe detector and is aligned with a 1 in.
diameter lead aperture in the shielding. This aperture serves to collimate gamma radiation emitted from
radioactive gases in the dwell chamber, thereby moderating detector count rates and reducing dead time.
A detailed description of the operating principles of Ge detectors is given by Eberth and Simpson [12].

  Figure 3. Fission gas release detection system located in the HFIR MIF, with the HPGe detectors located

The in-core capsule will be integrated into MINITAURE as described in the following section. For the
current configuration, two in-core capsules will be located near the reactor midplane. This placement is

MIF and the reactor pool, as well as the availability of two HPGe detector systems for gamma

The FGRC retains key aspects of the established MiniFuel subcapsule design as well as targeted
modifications to enable continuous monitoring of FGR, real-time thermal control, and neutron flux

two 1/16 in. gas lines connected at the top endcap. Internally, the fuel specimen is held between a Mo
filler and a Mo fuel cup, supported by a zirconia (ZrO₂) spacer for thermal insulation. Molybdenum was
selected for its high heat generation rate (HGR) in the HFIR environment—it serves as the dominant heat
source for the assembly—and for its compatibility with UO₂ fuel specimens. Two SST thermowells,
which house N-type thermocouples for temperature measurement, extend from the capsule’s top endcap
into the Mo filler. The thermowell design and choice of SST for the capsule housing improves weldability
while allowing in-capsule instrumentation. Test parts were fabricated and welded and successfully
demonstrated that the gas lines and thermowells could be welded to the endcap while maintaining a
hermetic seal. To facilitate fission gas transport to the capsule plenum and into the sweep gas system,
shallow grooves are machined into the Mo components in contact with the fuel. A cross-sectional

2.4 MINITAURE DESIGN

MINITAURE is intended to be a flexible capability that allows for variable capsule configurations based
on the goals of each iteration of the experiment. Each capsule is on the order of 25–50 mm tall, meaning

be installed is predominantly limited by the 46 mm diameter of RB irradiation positions. The first

 3. COMPUTATIONAL ANALYSIS

3.1 METHODOLOGY

A multistep reactor physics and heat transfer analysis approach was used to determine the expected
operating conditions of the in-core capsules. First, a coupled radiation transport and isotopic depletion
simulation was performed to determine experiment HGRs and fuel specimen fission and burnup rates.
These HGRs were input into 3D finite element analysis models of the in-core capsules along with
prototypic boundary conditions and material properties to determine the anticipated fuel temperature in
the capsule. For the TCC, the predicted time-dependent burnup of specimens was used to degrade the
thermal conductivity of the specimen and simulate the thermal conductivity measurement technique. For
FGR detection, a basic diffusion model was applied to calculate the expected gaseous fission product
release based on fission product inventory and the modeled temperature of the fuel. The released gaseous
fission product inventory was corrected to account for decay during transit to the out-of-pile detectors,
and the gamma spectra were modeled using the decay-corrected source term with the Gamma Detector
Response and Analysis Software (GADRAS) simulation tool [13]. Additional details of these analyses are
provided in the following sections.

3.2 RADIATION TRANSPORT

Scoping neutronics simulations were conducted using the HFIRCON framework [14], a code developed
at ORNL to simplify the process of modeling cycle behavior of HFIR. This code employs the use of
several pre-existing software and tools with specially written Python scripts and C plugins to automate the
interaction between them. For radiation transport, the ORNL Transformative Neutronics (ORNL-TN)
patch for MCNP Version 5.1.60 is used. ORNL-TN was developed to increase MCNP’s applicability to

 10

fusion application neutronics analyses, and it has introduced fixes to many bugs present in the MCNP
Version 5.1.60 software and increased its overall efficiency. HFIRCON also employs the use of
LAVAMINT to calculate cell volumes and bounding boxes, the Automated Variance Reduction
Generation (ADVANTG) code for generating weight windows and variance reduction parameters, and
the msx-deplete solver for depletion calculations with ORIGEN.

Flux spectra and isotopic data are passed between ORIGEN and MCNP to enable time-dependent
calculations of internal HGRs in all experiment components. Fuel specimen fission rates and fission
product isotopic inventories can also be computed by the code, and burnup can be calculated from the
fission rates. Simplified models were developed for the scoping calculations, in which duplicates of the
capsules were placed axially along the length of the in-core portion of an RB irradiation position. The
SST tube that will house the experiment was included in the model, and open space between the capsules
and tube inner wall was filled with graphite. Figure 6 shows a lateral cross-sectional view of the FGRC
capsule, along with a contextual representation of its location within the HFIR model. A similar
representation was used for the TCC.

 Figure 6. A portion of the MCNP model containing the FGRC experiment.

The same methodology for calculating the relevant nuclear responses was applied to both the FGRC and
TCC capsules. This methodology consisted of the following steps: (1) converting a CAD model of each
capsule to a corresponding consolidated solid geometry (CSG) model, (2) integrating the resulting CSG
model into a reference MCNP model of the HFIR geometry at a location in the RB reflector, and (3)
running a simulation of multi-cycle behavior with HFIRCON.

CAD models were available for all the capsule types considered and were the primary reference for the
geometry because no drawings were available at the time of this work. Because the CAD models of the

 11

capsules represented only a single capsule, the capsule geometry was replicated and stacked, each capsule
separated by an equivalent-length, equivalent-diameter cylinder of solid graphite. Once the CAD was
prepared, the model was converted into CSG using the CAD-to-CSG conversion software GEOUNED
[15]. The resulting model was then integrated into the HFIR Representative Model [16], which is
currently considered the reference model for HFIR calculations, using a universe and a transformation
card to rotate the geometry into the appropriate RB location.

The materials used in the analysis (Table 1) were based on information provided by the primary
investigators of the project as well as publicly available information about the elemental composition for
common materials. The parameters of a HFIRCON calculation are defined in the controller, a JSON file
that dictates the values for cycle length, control cylinder movement, depletion time steps, variance
reduction calculation time steps, location of fuel in the MCNP input deck, computational resources, and
many others. Table 2 shows some of the various parameters specified for a calculation and the values
chosen for this analysis. A cycle length of 26 days was chosen because it gives the most conservative
results for heating. In reality, HFIR cycles vary in time and are generally shorter than 26 days, but the
length depends on the target loading and whether there are any issues throughout the cycle that require
shutdown and restart. A decay time of 15 days between cycles was modeled in the calculation, and a total
of 4 cycles were modeled for each capsule.

A 3D, half-symmetry finite element thermal model was constructed using ANSYS to simulate an axial
portion of MINITAURE containing a single TCC, graphite holder, and SST tube (Figure 7). Python-based
automation was used to import HGR outputs from HFIRCON into the ANSYS model, scaling them to
user-specified axial positions and solving for corresponding temperature distributions.

To establish the sensitivity of thermal behavior to experiment positioning, a parametric study was
performed. In the study, the average fuel temperature and maximum SST housing temperature were
calculated as functions of axial elevation relative to the HFIR midplane. These initial simulations
assumed a static He fill gas and included only conductive heat transfer (neglecting convection to flowing
gas). Results, shown in Figure 8, indicate that positioning the capsule approximately 200 mm away from
the midplane is necessary to keep both the fuel and SST within acceptable temperature limits. Some
degree of convective cooling is expected from the sweep gas lines in MINITAURE and may allow the
capsule to be placed closer to the core midplane, but the potential of this modification requires further
assessment.

Additional ANSYS analyses were conducted to demonstrate the thermal conductivity measurement
procedure. For these runs, the capsule was fixed at 210 mm above the midplane, and irradiation time was
extended from 1 day to 104 days (equivalent to four HFIR cycles). In these simulations, convective
cooling was artificially introduced by applying a heat transfer coefficient at the capsule’s exterior surface,
and both the convection coefficient and the He/Ar gas mixture were adjusted to maintain an average fuel
temperature near 800°C throughout the irradiation. The convection coefficient was varied as needed to
maintain constant fuel temperature and may not be reflective of actual values. Detailed thermal hydraulics
simulations are outside the scope of this work and will be addressed in future work. The specimen thermal
conductivity decreased as burnup increased, according to the HFIRCON predictions of burnup as a
function of irradiation time.

3.4 FGRC AND FGMS ASSESSMENT

3.4.1 FGRC Thermal Modeling

Finite element simulations were carried out using the ANSYS software to represent a 76.2 mm axial
section of the MINITAURE assembly (equivalent to one-eighth of the total experiment length). Like in
the TCC assessment, this was a 3D, half-symmetry model that included the SST housing tube, graphite
support structure, and an embedded FGRC to evaluate heat transfer behavior. The same Python interface
was used to import spatially and temporally varying HGR data from HFIRCON and apply them to the
relevant components of the ANSYS model. Gaussian curve fits of the HFIRCON results were used to
interpolate HGRs and scale them to the specified axial position of interest within the model. Preliminary
analyses were performed to refine internal geometries so that specimen temperatures of at least 1,000°C
could be sustained while limiting the SST capsule housing temperature to less than ~750°C. Locating the

 15

capsule close to the HFIR midplane was preferred to maximize specimen fission rates and thereby
promote higher FGR.

In addition to the dedicated sweep gas lines, independent gas circuits will deliver variable He/Ar mixtures
to adjust heat transfer from the experiment and regulate capsule temperature. Gas composition and flow
rates will be managed remotely from the MIF; adjustments will be guided by thermocouple measurements
from the capsule. Once the geometry was finalized, the ANSYS Direct Optimization toolkit was
employed to identify the required sweep gas mixtures and outer-wall heat transfer coefficients (controlled
by the sweep gas flow rate in practice) necessary to hold the specimen temperature at 1,050°C throughout
irradiation. Without this control, temperature drift would occur due to evolving specimen isotopics
(notably Pu buildup) and HFIR control element motion, both of which influence HGRs. Maintaining a
stable fuel temperature is essential for enabling a separate-effects interpretation of FGR as a function of
burnup.

Figure 10 presents the calculated HGRs for the fuel specimen and SST capsule in a midplane
configuration over four consecutive 26-day HFIR cycles. The natural UO₂ specimen shows an increase in
HGR during the initial phase as Pu accumulates, eventually plateauing once Pu production and depletion
balance. By contrast, the SST capsule and surrounding structures display relatively steady HGRs with
only small fluctuations caused by control element repositioning. This lower initial fuel HGR complicates
temperature control, as it requires reduced cooling capacity early in the irradiation, which can lead to SST
housing temperatures exceeding 750°C.

Figure 11 illustrates the average fuel temperature and maximum SST capsule temperature, where the goal
was to stabilize the average fuel temperature at 1,050°C for the first three cycles. The predicted fuel
burnup is also plotted for context. Because the fuel HGR reaches a cyclic steady state between the third
and fourth irradiation cycles, the fourth cycle was not considered in the optimization. The reduced cooling
needed at the beginning of the test caused SST temperatures to overshoot the target but eventually
converge to ~750°C as the specimen HGR increased. Figure 12 shows an example of the modeled
temperature field during the third HFIR cycle, highlighting the resulting steady-state distribution across
the experiment.

There are several ways to combat the excessive SST temperatures at the start of the irradiation. One
approach would be to add an additional gas gap between the fueled region of the capsule and the capsule
housing and then rely on greater gas flow rates to cool the outside of the capsule. A second way to
mitigate SST overheating would be to move the capsule away from the midplane. However, doing so
would reduce the specimen fission rate, and the fuel HGR would decrease proportionally, likely requiring
very low cooling capacity on the capsule to reach temperatures >1,000°C. The specimen could be slightly
enriched to raise the initial HGR. Enriching to ~1–2% 235U/U would cause the specimen HGR to initially
be greater than the equilibrium value, and increased cooling could maintain more reasonable SST
temperatures. Alternatively, it may be acceptable to allow the temperature to be lower than the 1,050°C

 17

target early in the irradiation. However, previous BISON analyses of UO2 MiniFuel experiments [17, 18]
indicate that significant FGR is expected almost immediately for fuel temperatures >1,000°C, whereas the
threshold burnup at which FGR above recoil (i.e., the kinetic ejection of fission gas atoms from free
surfaces) occurs is very sensitive to fuel temperatures in the range 800°C–1,000°C. Allowing the fuel to
initially operate within this range may confound results.

 4. OUT-OF-PILE TESTING AND SUPPORTING ACTIVITIES

4.1 OUT-OF-PILE THERMAL CONDUCTIVITY TESTING

To verify the thermal conductivity measurement technique implemented in the TCC, a series of out-of-
pile experiments were conducted to simulate the expected performance of the in-pile design. The primary
difference between the two designs lies in the heat generation method: the in-pile capsule relies on
internal nuclear and gamma heating, whereas the out-of-pile capsule is heated externally. The out-of-pile
capsule was designed to closely replicate the in-pile capsule, with only minor modifications to
accommodate the external heating approach. The only notable change between the in-pile TCC design
and the out-of-pile test capsule is that the shaft diameter of the Mo substrate was increased to make
contact with the SST housing inner wall. This change allows externally applied heat to transfer inward to
the capsule internals before flowing upward through the thermopile system.

Nuclear fuel was not used in the out-of-pile test, and yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) was deemed a
suitable surrogate since it is a ceramic with a thermal conductivity similar to that of UO2. An
experimental facility was developed to house the capsule, control heat flow, maintain an inert gas or
vacuum environment, and establish a temperature gradient across a thermopile system using the surrogate
material. This setup enabled calculation of the surrogate specimen’s thermal conductivity over a range of
temperatures. Multiple iterations of the experiment were performed, with design and procedural
improvements informed by each previous iteration’s results. Due to the sensitivity of the measurements to
small variations in geometric dimensions and thermal contact conductance, careful attention was given to
the fine details of the experimental design. This necessitated additional supporting activities such as
radiography and X-ray computed tomography (XCT) analysis, finite element analysis verification, and
precise dimensional inspections. The final results demonstrate a promising correlation with the expected
thermal conductivity trends, although a consistent and significant error relative to expected values
remains for the surrogate material. The detailed calculation method of thermal conductivity and
postulated sources of error are given in Section 4.1.1, the experimental facility and experimental methods
are described in Section 4.1.2, and detailed results are presented in Section 4.1.3.

where 𝑡𝑠 is the specimen thickness, 𝐴𝑠𝑝𝑒𝑐𝑖𝑚𝑒𝑛 is the cross-sectional area of the specimen, and 𝐴𝑠𝑢𝑏𝑠𝑡𝑟𝑎𝑡𝑒 is

As with the thermal conductivity measurement technique for the in-pile TCC described in Section 2.2,
this method relies on several key assumptions to ensure the calculated specimen thermal conductivity is
accurate and representative of actual heat transfer conditions. First, it is assumed that heat flows one-
dimensionally along the axial direction of the capsule, with negligible radial or bypass heat losses to the
surroundings. This requires that the specimen and slug geometries be well aligned, that lateral conduction
paths through the capsule housing are minimized, and that any radiative or convective losses are
insignificant compared to conductive transfer through the thermopile stack.

Second, the thermal conductivities of the Mo and V substrates are assumed to be accurately known over
the temperature range of interest. These values are used to calculate both the axial heat flux from the
thermocouple measurements and the surface temperatures of the specimen. Any deviation in these
reference values, whether due to material variability, irradiation effects (in the in-pile case), or
temperature-dependent uncertainties, will directly propagate into the calculated specimen thermal
conductivity. Third, it is assumed that the location of all thermocouples with respect to each other and to
the specimen are precisely known and that these distances remain constant during heating. Even small
errors in the measured or assumed distances can introduce significant bias in the calculated surface
temperatures.

Fourth, the thermocouple measurements are assumed to accurately represent the local bulk material
temperature at their intended locations. In practice, variability can arise due to construction differences
between thermocouples, such as the position of the sensing junction within the SST sheath and the
thickness and density of the packed powder insulation. These variations can shift the actual measurement
point relative to the assumed location and introduce additional uncertainty. Furthermore, the SST sheaths
themselves may provide a parallel heat conduction path, potentially bypassing the surrounding material
and creating a small but non-negligible “short-circuit” heat loss that affects the local temperature reading.

Finally, the method assumes that thermal contact resistance at the specimen–slug interfaces is either
negligible or has been minimized to the extent that it does not significantly alter the surface temperature
estimates. In practice, microscopic surface roughness, imperfect flatness, or insufficient contact pressure
can create temperature drops at the interfaces, leading to errors in the thermal conductivity estimations.

 21

For this reason, contact quality, applied preload, and surface finish are carefully controlled during
assembly, and the impact of these variables is further examined in Section 4.1.3.

The calculation method discussed here demonstrates that determination of specimen thermal conductivity
is feasible when these assumptions are carefully addressed during design and testing. By maintaining
strict control over the heat flow geometry, material property inputs, thermocouple placement, and
interface quality, the measurement approach can provide accurate and repeatable results. The viability of
the technique is further strengthened when supported by detailed finite element modeling to evaluate the
sensitivity of each potential error source and to develop strategies for mitigating or compensating for
them. These modeling results, along with targeted experimental practices, ensure that the influence of
each identified phenomenon is either minimized or accounted for in the data analysis process.

4.1.2 Experimental Methods

The out-of-pile capsule was constructed to closely replicate the in-pile TCC design. The purpose of the
out-of-pile experiment was to reproduce the in-pile thermal and geometric configuration as closely as
possible while replacing nuclear heating with external resistive heating of the Mo slug. Further, it allows
researchers to verify and quantify sensitive system parameters to mitigate undesired performance of the in
situ thermal conductivity measurement device. This approach allows the thermopile method to be
validated in a representative capsule configuration, supports refinement of the design, and enables
identification of measurement uncertainties and methodological corrections for future in-pile irradiations.
The overall capsule layout, materials, and thermocouple (designated as TC) positions are shown in Figure
15.

 Figure 15. Out-of-pile TCC cross-section.

The capsule assembly consists of a Mo substrate on the heat input side, the ceramic surrogate specimen at
the center, and a V alloy heat sink on the opposite side. Both metallic slugs were machined with flat,
parallel interfaces and polished to submicron surface roughness to promote uniform thermal contact and
minimize interfacial resistance. The Mo slug included an enlarged base section to provide direct contact
with the SST capsule housing, enabling efficient conduction from an external band heater. The in-pile
TCC will be welded under a preload condition that mitigates thermal contact losses, which also means a
one-time use for each capsule. To facilitate efficient testing, the out-of-pile capsule was not welded, and

 22

the contact pressure on the system is maintained by a wave spring external to the capsule. Therefore, the
grafoil spacers were replaced by an SST spacer sized so as to create a small axial gap between the SST
housing and the V slug at the top of the capsule and thus eliminate the possibility of thermal expansion in
the housing causing lift-off of the V substrate from the specimen. This approach was taken because the
external heater causes the SST housing to reach higher temperatures than the capsule internals, which is
not the case for the in-pile capsule.

The capsule was mounted inside an SST ConFlat-flanged test chamber capable of operating under high
vacuum (<10 mTorr) or in inert gas up to ~3 atm. In overpressure conditions, a low He purge flow was
maintained to offset minor leakage. At the bottom of the chamber, an insulating base was designed to
minimize conductive heat loss downward. Inserted in the base is a steel pedestal that was also designed so
that its geometry further restricted direct downward heat flow while promoting efficient heat transfer into
the Mo slug. Heating was supplied by a 120 V AC band heater clamped around the pedestal. The chamber
arrangement, heating components, and preload system are shown in Figure 16.

At the top of the capsule, the V substrate was coupled to a Cu heat sink using graphite paste. The Cu heat
sink aligned the V substrate lid with the Ti shroud and was supported against the chamber’s top flange
through the wave spring. Contact pressure was applied to the system by the deflection of this wave spring
when the top flange was tightened. Combined with the gravitational load of the Cu heat sink assembly,
the contact pressure at the specimen-substrate interfaces was determined to be at least 14 MPa. To
enhance heat rejection and provide additional contact pressure throughout the system, a compressible
graphite rope was sandwiched between the Cu heat sink and the flange, providing both compliance and
thermal contact. The thermocouple leads were routed upward through the top flange to the data
acquisition system. The assembled capsule, including a 0.1 mm thick molybdenum foil retainer clip used
to center the specimen during assembly, is shown in Figure 17 (left), with the unit installed in the heating
pedestal prior to chamber closure (right).

Three K-type thermocouples sheathed in SST with MgO insulation were installed in the capsule. K-type
thermocouples were used instead of N-type thermocouples for out-of-pile testing due to their lower cost
and the lower temperature regime expected for preliminary tests. For reference, the thermocouple in the
Mo slug is designated as TC1, the lower thermocouple in the V slug is TC2, and the uppermost
thermocouple is designated TC3. The thermocouples were fixed in place using graphite paste to improve
thermal contact with the substrates. XCT imaging was performed on the capsule using a Zeiss Versa 620
system to identify the exact position of the two thermocouples. This inspection revealed that TC2 was
bonded without full insertion, introducing an offset from its intended position. This offset was corrected
for in the thermal conductivity estimation, but the small distance between thermocouple probes induces
uncertainty in the measurement from a reduced signal-to-noise ratio in the computed heat flux. A single
2D tomogram through the center of the full 3D XCT image is shown in Figure 18, which reveals the
partial insertion during graphite pasting, which was corrected for in post-test analysis but increased
measurement uncertainty.

 Figure 18. Tomogram of the V substrate showing thermocouple placement depths (TC2 and TC3).

Prior to conducting experiments, laser flash analysis (LFA) was performed on samples taken from the
material used to create the Mo and V components, as well as several samples of the YSZ specimens. This
was done to characterize the thermal diffusivity, and therefore the thermal conductivity, of each
component. LFA measurements were conducted from room temperature up to 500°C for all samples. In
the case of the YSZ samples, specific heat capacity data were not available. Instead, this value was
obtained by using the manufacturer’s recorded room temperature thermal conductivity and solving for the
specific heat capacity using the measured thermal diffusivity from LFA. This specific heat capacity was
assumed to remain constant with temperature. This assumption introduces potential error into the
experimental validation and is intended to be corrected in future work with the use of a differential
scanning calorimeter to properly identify the temperature-dependent specific heat of the YSZ samples.
Precise inspections of all relevant dimensions and parameters for each component in the thermopile
system were conducted, including roughness measurements of each contacting surface. These surfaces
were polished to less than 0.6 µm average roughness to increase the thermal contact conductance between
part interfaces.

Temperature readings were recorded in differential mode using a LabJack T7 data acquisition system,
with a custom Python script managing real-time monitoring and data logging. For each run, the variable
AC transformer supplying the heater was set to a fixed output until steady-state conditions were reached.
After steady state was confirmed, heater power was increased in controlled increments to collect
measurements at progressively higher temperatures. Multiple experiments were conducted to assess
repeatability and the effects of system pressure. Between experiments, the capsule was disassembled to
inspect contact surfaces and document any changes from repeated thermal cycling.

4.1.3     Results

Two series of measurements were conducted in a He environment at 8 and 10 psi. This configuration
closely replicates the anticipated gas environment in MINITAURE, which will utilize a sweep gas
mixture of Ar and He to control the specimen temperature. The two gas pressures were used to determine
the sensitivity of the system response. Results show a strong match to the expected trend of conductivity
for YSZ, but with significant overall error. Additionally, an abnormal data point occurs in specimen

 25

temperatures between room temperature and 500 K; this anomaly creates an out-of-trend thermal
conductivity data point. It is currently believed to be due to differential thermal expansion between the
thermocouple sheath and Mo and V components. At higher temperatures, the sheath should expand into
the walls of these components, creating greater thermal contact conductance and more accurate
measurements.

A third series of measurements were conducted in vacuum to better understand the sensitivity of the
measurement to the ambient gas species and pressure. These results yielded better correlation to the LFA
data, but only low temperature data points could be collected due to the lack of cooling on the heater,
causing it to fail. It is believed that two mechanisms contributed to the deviations observed in the tests
with pressurized gas: (1) the gas species aids thermal conduction directly between the Mo and V slugs
through the gas gap created where the sample is not present and (2) a possible natural convection loop is
created inside the system, resulting in a heat loss path between the thermocouples. Both mechanisms
would contribute to an artificially high thermal conductivity measurement of the sample. The results for
both series of measurements are given in Figure 19. An uncertainty range of ±5% was assigned to the
LFA data based on ASTM Standard E1461 [19], although the LFA measurements were not conducted to
this standard and the actual error may be greater than 5%.

4.2 ADDITIONAL ACTIVITIES SUPPORTING MINITAURE

4.2.1 MIF Integration

The MIF, a laboratory space adjacent to the HFIR reactor pool, serves as a control room for performing
instrumented irradiation experiments [20]. The MIF consists of two touchscreen computers running
Allen-Bradley PLC control software; several panels of automated mass flow controllers, valves, pressure
transducers, and thermocouple readers; high-purity gas bottle arrays for supplying temperature control gas
to the reactor experiment; a shielded valve box for handling radioactive effluent gases; and a data server
with RAID 5 architecture for storing experiment data and system conditions—known as the Historian.
Configuration control of the MIF has previously been maintained by the Research Reactor Division
(RRD) at ORNL, which has a series of standard operating procedures for upgrading or replacing
components in the MIF, modifying software, or performing any other modifications to the facility. To
accelerate the software modification process necessary for MINITAURE, management of the MIF
configuration control and software quality assurance plans have been transferred to the Nuclear Energy
and Fuel Cycle Division (NEFCD). This will allow for necessary experiment-specific changes to be made
in the control software without tying up valuable RRD operational resources. This transfer process is
ongoing and is expected to be complete by the end of 2025.

Several new components have been identified to replace the aging computational resources in the MIF.
These include new servers for the touchscreen controllers and a new data server to replace the current
Historian. The new Historian server will allow for faster access to experiment data and SCADA variables,
enabling near-real-time access to data for potential AI or ML applications. These new components will be
procured after the MIF configuration control has been transferred to NEFCD.

4.2.2 FGMS Installation

Two new HPGe detectors with 4 in. thick lead shields were purchased for performing real-time fission
gas gamma spectrometry measurements in the MIF. The detectors are coaxial P-type HPGe “PopTop”

 27

detectors equipped with ultra-high-rate, transistor reset preamplifiers. The detectors have 20% relative
efficiency (NaI) and are cooled using an electro-mechanical integrated cooling system. The system is
coupled to a DSPEC-502 (Ortec) dual-channel, multichannel analyzer running the GammaVision
spectroscopy software. A 152Eu calibration source was used to perform energy and efficiency calibration
of the detectors for acceptance testing and for use in detector response modeling with GADRAS. Both
detectors were found to have an FWHM resolution of <1.7 keV at 1.3 MeV, which is sufficient for the
intended application.

A floor loading analysis was performed for installing the two HPGe detectors, Pb shields, and the
necessary Pb shielded gas lines in the MIF. Because the detectors will be installed adjacent to the shielded
valve box—and not next to the pool wall, which is supported by the reactor building concrete monolith—
the floor structure required analysis for safety. HFIR structural engineers confirmed the floor can support
the approximately 12,000 lb. of equipment needed for the fission gas measurement system. Relocation of
the HPGe detectors to the MIF is ongoing at the time of this writing and is expected to be complete by the
end of 2025.

 5. FUTURE WORK

Ongoing efforts related to MINITAURE thus far have focused on the development of measurement
techniques, specifying critical design characteristics, and refining specific concepts. With the
configuration of the first iteration of MINITAURE now complete as described in this document, future
efforts will involve safety qualification of the experiment for deployment in HFIR, MIF integration, and
error and uncertainty mitigation. HFIR safety calculations are required to ensure that operating
MINITAURE will have no adverse effects on the reactor or pose safety risks to the public. At a high
level, the calculations must ensure that under conservative, worst-case scenario conditions, the
experiment will not cause the HFIR coolant to boil, the experiment containment tube will not be
breached, and staff members will not receive radiation dose in the vicinity of the MIF and fission gas
detection equipment. These calculations must be based on conservative estimates of HGRs for all
components predicted in RRD-approved reactor physics simulations.

At a minimum, new radiation dose and thermal safety calculations are needed for MINITAURE to ensure
safe operation of the MIF and that the SST containment tube is not breached due to melting or mechanical
loads induced at operating temperatures. It may be possible to leverage existing safety calculations for
other experiments (e.g., RB MiniFuel, WIRE-21) to obtain bounding HGRs and thermal hydraulic limits
that can be applied to MINITAURE to establish a safety basis without additional calculations. This
possibility is yet to be confirmed and is the subject of ongoing conversations with RRD experiment safety
staff. Engineering drawings and assembly procedures for MINITAURE will be developed in parallel with
safety qualification activities.

The results shown in this report demonstrate proof-of-concept for both capsule designs. From a technical
perspective, the FGR concept is relatively simple: continuous gas flow over a nuclear fuel specimen
transports gaseous fission products from inside the capsule to dedicated detectors to quantify the amount
of FGR. The analysis presented in this report indicates that the amount of FGR from the miniature fuel
specimen will be detectable. The primary area of uncertainty regarding the FGRC pertains to the ability to
simultaneously maintain constant fuel temperature and acceptable SST housing temperature. Quantifying
the cooling capacity of multiple gas flow streams is complex due to the small geometries and is further
convoluted by thermal hydraulic entrance effects and natural circulation. Targeted analysis is required to
determine whether the gas flow both internal and external to the capsule can be configured to achieve the
desired irradiation conditions or whether other design changes are necessary. Other focus areas for the

 28

FGR measurement are the refinement of release models and the development of a methodology to convert
measured gamma spectra to nuclide R/B ratios and cumulative FGR.

For the TCC, a critical focus for future work will be to reduce the error between measured and reference
values. The out-of-pile test results shown in this report indicate that the current approach can capture
relative changes in thermal conductivity at temperatures >500 K but does so with error on the order of
~30%–40% when conducted in a pressurized gas environment. Measuring relative thermal conductivity
degradation in situ is itself valuable since the initial and terminal values will be measured out-of-pile
using traditional means (i.e., LFA). However, the capability would be significantly more attractive if
absolute values could be accurately measured in situ. Several sources of error have been hypothesized and
can be systematically addressed via additional testing and simulation.

Measurement accuracy would likely be improved if the thermocouples were fully installed in their
respective positions. The small distance between the thermocouple probes in the V slug (Figure 18)
enhances the sensitivity of the measurement to uncertainty in other parameters, such as the V thermal
conductivity or thermocouple readings. The sensitivity of the measurement may decrease with properly
installed sensors. Additionally, a better understanding of how accurately the thermocouple measurements
represent the bulk substrate temperatures is needed. The thermocouples are sheathed in SST and have a
porous MgO insulator between the sheath and probe. The temperature difference between the probe and
surrounding substrate temperature may induce some error into the measurement and should be quantified.
Combined with the manufacturer-specified ±2°C uncertainty on thermocouple measurements, any
additional temperature difference between the readings and surrounding substrate can propagate to
meaningful error in the final thermal conductivity estimation. Improper binding of the thermocouples to
the substrates could exacerbate this error even further. The graphite paste used in tests so far is preferable
over leaving a small gap between the sensors and the substrates, but its thermal properties are not well
characterized and may contribute to some error in the experiments. Careful selection of a binder material
and high-fidelity simulations are planned to quantify and mitigate this potential source of error. A key
takeaway from the tests is that the location of the probes within the thermocouple sheath and insulation
should be determined prior to installation and testing.

Another focus area for error mitigation is understanding heat loss pathways. The two most probable
sources of error are thermal contact resistance losses and heat transfer to the surrounding gas
environment. These phenomena have opposing effects on the thermal conductivity estimation.
Unaccounted-for contact resistance leads to an overprediction of the specimen hot side surface
temperature and an underprediction of the cold side surface temperature. In other words, the Δ𝑇 across the
specimen is overpredicted, which causes an underprediction of the thermal conductivity. Design choices
were made to mitigate thermal resistance losses. Namely, sub-micron surface finishes and applied contact
pressure improve solid spot conductance, and applying gas pressure improves gas gap conductance. These
features drive the temperature drop at material interfaces to near zero, but totally eliminating the thermal
resistance is impractical for the high heat fluxes expected in HFIR.

Although increasing gas pressure improves the gas gap conductance, doing so creates an additional heat
transfer pathway that wicks heat away from the thermopile system. Heat transferred to the surrounding
gas via conduction and natural circulation reduces the Δ𝑇 across the specimen, causing an overprediction
of the thermal conductivity. It is possible that some heat is conducting across the gas gap between the Mo
and V substrates since the specimen is of a smaller diameter, but natural circulation within the capsule is a
more probable source of error. Natural circulation within the capsule would predominantly cool the Mo
substrate since it is the hottest portion of the thermopile, thereby reducing the specimen hot side
temperature more than the cold side temperature. The out-of-pile tests assert thermal contact resistance
and heat loss to the gas environment as likely sources of error since the tests conducted in pressurized He
exhibited gross overestimation of thermal conductivity, and the vacuum tests exhibited underprediction.

 29

Operating the in-pile capsule in vacuum is impractical, so understanding and mitigating these effects is a
critical path forward.

Simultaneous management of the fuel specimen and SST capsule temperatures is as important for the
TCC as it is for the FGRC. In the MINITAURE configuration presented in this report, the TCCs were
placed far enough from reactor midplane to prevent excessive SST temperatures. However, it is desirable
to move them closer to the midplane to increase the fuel fission rates. Future work will aim to identify a
strategy to maintain acceptable SST temperatures while enhancing the fuel fission rate. It is worth noting
that all of the uncertainty mitigation activities mentioned will at most lead to targeted design refinements
that will improve confidence in the capability rather than requiring new measurement concepts.

Significant upgrades are needed to further modernize and enhance experiment controls with the MIF. This
facility was last upgraded in 2016 [20]; aging equipment and limited software upgrading capabilities must
be addressed prior to the execution of MINITAURE. The shifting of hardware and software quality
assurance process ownership to NEFCD is an important first step to ensure the successful deployment of
this experiment, but additional work is required to organize the systems into reactor safety critical and
experiment design critical categories. Currently, all systems are considered reactor safety critical, which
makes modifying any aspect of the MIF prohibitively time consuming and expensive. Making the
distinction between the design and safety critical categories will allow irradiation experimenters to make
hardware and software changes/upgrades to the design critical components while also avoiding any
changes to the more challenging safety critical systems. New RRD-accepted work control procedures and
specifications for the MIF are therefore needed that clearly outline the functional requirements of all
aspects of the facility.

Lastly, future out-of-pile experiments will extend the operating temperature range toward the in-pile
target range of 600°C–1,000°C to evaluate the influence of elevated temperature on measurement
accuracy. At these conditions, heat losses from radiation, natural convection, and gas-gap conductance are
expected to become more significant and will be systematically quantified. Sensitivity tests with varying
pressure, gas species, and surface roughness conditions will be performed to isolate the contribution of
each mechanism, enabling the development of mitigation strategies and calibration procedures using
empirically derived correlations. These corrections will be incorporated into the analysis to ensure that
high-temperature measurements yield the true specimen thermal conductivity required for confident in-
pile experiment deployment. Although UO2 is the intended fuel form for the first iteration of
MINITAURE, the capability aims to accommodate a variety of fuels in future iterations. Therefore,
additional out-of-pile tests using surrogate specimens of greater thermal conductivity are planned to
define the performance envelope of the technique.

 6. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

A novel instrumented irradiation capability is being developed at ORNL to monitor various fuel
performance phenomena in situ using unique capsule designs. The first iteration of the capability—known
as MINITAURE—aims to capture thermal conductivity degradation and FGR from subscale UO2 fuel
specimens as they are irradiated in HFIR. This report presents the cross-disciplinary efforts that have been
conducted in fiscal year 2025 that informed the design of each capsule and the configuration of
MINITAURE. The experiment will contain four TCCs, two FGRCs, four SPNDs, and up to five sweep
gas lines for temperature control. The TCCs will monitor thermal conductivity degradation at constant
fuel temperature and increasing burnup, and each capsule will have a different setpoint temperature
ranging from 600°C to 1,000°C. Both FGRCs are intended to operate at fuel temperatures of ~1,000–
1,050°C to facilitate substantial FGR as burnup increases. The FGMS, consisting of two HPGe detectors
and a lead-shielded gas delivery system, will be installed in the HFIR MIF to record the evolving gamma

 30

signature of gas flowing directly through the capsules. The SPNDs will be installed at different elevations
in the experiment to verify the axial power distribution and fuel specimen burnup predictions.

Proof-of-concept for both capsule designs was established in this work. The FGR monitoring approach is
technically straightforward, and the primary questions regarding its feasibility concerned the ability to
detect the small amounts of fission gas generated by MiniFuel-style specimens, hermetically weld gas
lines and thermowells to the capsule, and to operate the capsule at desired temperatures. The assessments
summarized in this report showed that a gamma spectrum above the MIF background will be observable,
and test welds were conducted on the capsule to verify that a hermetic seal could be acquired. The two-
step reactor physics and thermal analysis showed that operating the capsule at >1,000°C without the SST
housing exceeding ~750°C appears feasible, but additional design refinements are needed to maintain
these temperatures early in the irradiation when the fuel internal heat generation is low.

An analytical approach to estimate specimen thermal conductivity based on thermocouple measurements
was developed for both in-pile and out-of-pile configurations. Simulations of the in-pile TCC in which
basic rods were used as thermocouple surrogates showed that the thermal conductivity calculation
approach is valid and accurate when all relevant inputs (geometry, heat generation, and temperature
measurements) are precisely known. A test chamber and modified TCC was developed to verify the
measurement technique out-of-pile using an externally applied heat load. The results from those tests
showed that the approach captures relative changes in the thermal conductivity of YSZ surrogate
specimens, but errors in the absolute values remain. The ability of this approach to measure effective
change in thermal conductivity as a function of temperature and burnup would still provide value, but the
uncertainty inherent to such an approach must be well understood to defend the experiment cost. Tests
conducted under vacuum conditions exhibited much lower error, suggesting natural circulation occurring
in pressurized gas environments is likely a significant contributor to error. The series of tests conducted
to-date provide insights into the sources of error, and a significantly expanded test matrix is planned for
the future.

Based on the computational and experimental assessments conducted so far, several strategies for
mitigating error and uncertainty have been identified and will be pursued in future work. These strategies
are intended to highlight opportunities for minor but effective design modifications that will improve
confidence in the capability without major revisions to the capsule designs or measurement techniques.
Safety analyses will be conducted in parallel to qualify the experiment for irradiation in HFIR, and
together these activities will be the focus of work conducted in fiscal year 2026.
