Prevalence and risk factors for refractory and untreated chronic cough in the Canadian Longitudinal Study of Aging
Authors: Wafa Hassan, Alexandra J. Mayhew, Nazmul Sohel, Kieran J. Killian, Paul M. O'Byrne, Parminder Raina, Imran Satia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic cough, defined as a persistent cough lasting >8 weeks, affects 16% of Canadian adults in the community. Chronic cough can be classified as refractory chronic cough when cough persists despite treatment of any identifiable underlying condition. The prevalence of refractory chronic cough is unclear, as is the number of patients who remain untreated despite having a persistent cough.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 26 606 adults of 45–85 years old from the Canadian Longitudinal Study of Aging (CLSA) with available chronic cough data at baseline and first follow-up at 3 years. Chronic cough was defined by self-reported daily cough for ≥12 months.ResultsIn this cohort, 4059 participants had a cough at baseline. Among them, 26.2% had refractory chronic cough on medications, 27.4% had chronic cough without medication (untreated chronic cough), 19.3% had resolution of chronic cough with medication, and 27.1% had resolution of chronic cough without medication. Overall, the prevalence of refractory chronic cough was 4.0% (1063 out of 26 606) among patients on medication and 4.2% among the untreated chronic cough group. Refractory chronic cough was more common in men, persistent smokers and those with asthma, COPD, migraine and inflammatory bowel disease or irritable bowel syndrome. Smoking cessation and discontinuation of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors were associated with resolution of cough. Despite guideline-recommended therapies, neuromodulators were infrequently prescribed by physicians.ConclusionThis population-based study was able to explore the outcomes of chronic cough over 3 years and identify risk factors with its development. Nearly one third of cases resolved without new medication, but likely by targeting treatable traits such as smoking and stopping angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor use.

Introduction

Chronic cough, defined as a cough lasting >8 weeks, is a common and often debilitating condition that poses a substantial burden on patients and the healthcare system [1, 2]. Data from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA) indicate that 16% of Canadians 45–85 years old living in the community have chronic cough [3], a figure higher than the average global estimate of 10% [4–6]. Chronic cough is often associated with an underlying condition, and in some cases, treating the underlying condition can lead to full or partial resolution of symptoms [7]. If chronic cough persists despite treatment, this is classified as refractory chronic cough (RCC), indicating its refractory nature and lack of response to therapy targeting identifiable conditions or treatable traits. In some cases, no cause is identified for the cough and it is termed unexplained chronic cough. RCC is a highly burdensome condition, associated with marked impairments in physical, social and psychological quality of life, and in many cases, significant social isolation [1, 8, 9]. Participants with RCC are frequently referred to specialist tertiary care centres [10], where treatment often relies on off-label use of neuromodulators owing to the absence of approved therapeutic options [11–13].

Despite many studies describing the prevalence of chronic cough, there is a paucity of data estimating the prevalence of RCC. Uncertainties persist regarding its prevalence and risk factors, as well as the heterogeneity of treatment response among patients seen in clinical practice [7]. The limited recognition of chronic cough and RCC and patient-specific risk factors may also contribute to the lack of awareness among healthcare professionals on how to appropriately identify patients for investigations and treatment [14–17]. Furthermore, understanding the prevalence of RCC is important to healthcare regulators, payers and healthcare providers to allocate relevant resources and develop pathways of care appropriate for this condition.

The primary objective of this analysis was to estimate the prevalence of RCC and identify demographic and clinical factors associated with its persistence or resolution. We used a large, national cohort of community-dwelling adults 45–85 years old from the CLSA. The CLSA allows for a prospective follow-up of participants and allowed us to examine whether risk factors were different in those participants who had RCC on medication and those who had persistent chronic cough over 3 years and were not taking any medication. Identifying risk factors for both groups is valuable so that potentially modifiable risk factors can be targeted, as along with health awareness and healthcare education.

Methods

Study design and population

The CLSA is a large, nationally generalisable, stratified random sample of 51 338 Canadian men and women 45–85 years old at baseline (2011–2015) from the 10 Canadian provinces [18]. Participants were eligible if they were capable of understanding and providing consent, could complete data collection in English or French, were not living in the three territories and First Nations reserves, were not in the Canadian army and were not living in institutions. Of the 51 388 in the tracking cohort, 30 097 participants were randomly selected from within 25–50 km of 11 data collection sites located in seven provinces for comprehensive assessment. In addition to completing interviews in person, the Comprehensive Cohort participants completed in-depth physical assessments and provided blood and urine samples. Details on the study design have been described elsewhere [19]. Each participant is followed every 3 years for 20 years or until death. The first follow-up was conducted between 2015 and 2018 with a retention rate of 95%. The Comprehensive Cohort data from baseline and follow-up 1 (FU-1) were included in the current analyses; eligible participants were those who had chronic cough at baseline and completed FU-1 data collection. This study was approved by the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board and by the CLSA scientific advisory board (Project ID: 1909024). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Definition of chronic cough and outcomes

Chronic cough was defined as a self-reported cough persisting on most days over the previous 12 months. Although clinical guidelines often use >8 weeks for the definition of chronic cough [3], there is no established duration definition for RCC. However, 12 months is consistent with the inclusion criteria used in most clinical trials of RCC [14, 15]. Medication classification

Medications were classified using the World Health Organization's Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification System (supplementary table S1). Medications were categorised as follows: 1) nasal preparations, 2) respiratory inhalers, 3) over the counter cough and cold preparations, 4) antihistamines, 5) antacids and gastrointestinal dysmotility drugs and 6) centrally acting cough neuromodulators that include morphine sulfate, pregabalin and gabapentin. Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi) were classified separately and not included within the main medication categories. Given their known association with cough, ACEi use was included as a covariate in our model.

Classification of smoking

Smoking is associated with coughing [3] and classification of smoking for this study was based on self-reported behaviour of whether participants were smoking at baseline and FU-1. Participants were classified into 1) never-smokers: did not smoke at baseline and FU-1; 2) persistent smokers: smoking at baseline and FU-1; 3) stopped smoking: smoking at baseline but not at FU-1; and 4) started smoking: not smoking at baseline but were at FU-1.

Classification of chronic conditions

Prior chronic conditions are associated with chronic cough [3, 20–22] and we have reported on the impact of these conditions in previous studies [3, 23]. Respiratory conditions such as asthma and COPD were based on self-reported physician diagnosis at the time of conducing face-to-face assessment. Non-respiratory comorbidities were grouped into three major categories: neurological, gastrointestinal and psychiatric conditions. Neurological conditions included previous strokes, transient ischaemic attacks, Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, epilepsy and migraines. Gastrointestinal conditions included intestinal or stomach ulcer, bowel incontinence and bowel disorders such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). Psychiatric conditions included anxiety disorders. Previous literature has reported a higher association of these comorbidities with cough [1, 20].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for each of the four categories are presented for age, sex, smoking status, province, associated respiratory conditions and non-respiratory conditions (table 1). The analysis was stratified based on medication status. Participants with chronic cough taking medications at baseline and/or FU-1 were included in the RCC with medication group. Univariate logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for individual risk factors for RCC with medication (Group 1), comparing RCC to resolved chronic cough with medication as the reference group (Group 2). We also estimated ORs for untreated chronic cough (Group 3) compared to resolved chronic cough without medication (Group 4). We used a set of covariates (age, sex, education and smoking) as the core model and tested each covariate separately, comprising provinces, underlying respiratory and non-respiratory conditions, and use of ACEi, because these have previously shown associations with chronic cough [3]. All analysis was performed using SAS software (version 9.4, window version 6.2.9200; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Chronic cough outcomes

Of the 30 097 participants in the CLSA Comprehensive Cohort, 3491 were excluded because data for cough were unavailable at either baseline or FU-1. Of the 26 606 participants with available data at both time points, 4059 had chronic cough at baseline. Within the whole Comprehensive Cohort, the prevalence of RCC on medication was 4.0%, 4.2% had untreated chronic cough, 3% had resolved chronic cough on medication and 4.1% had resolved chronic cough without medication (figure 1 and table 1). Among the 4059 with chronic cough at baseline, 1063 (26.2%) had RCC, 785 (19.3%) had chronic cough on medication, 1112 (27.4%) had untreated CC not on medications and 1099 (27.1%) reported resolution of CC without medication.

Risk factors for refractory and untreated chronic cough

Among participants with RCC on medications, persistent smoking was associated with increased odds of RCC (OR 1.86, 95% CI 1.34–2.58) (figure 3), as was asthma (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.07–1.71), COPD (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.15–2.00), IBD/IBS (OR 1.25, 95% CI 0.94–1.65) and migraine (OR 1.25, 95% CI 0.96–1.64). For participants with untreated chronic cough, cessation of smoking (OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.27–0.71) and discontinuation of ACEi (OR 0.54, 95% CI 0.31–0.94) were associated with a lower likelihood of persistent chronic cough (figure 4). This group also had a higher odds of chronic cough if they had anxiety (OR 1.55, 95% CI 1.07–2.24) and greater odds of migraine (OR 1.40, 95% CI 1.04–1.88). It was noteworthy that despite evidence of the association between ACEi and cough, this medication was started in participants and associated with a higher odds of having cough (OR 1.77, 95% CI 1.11–2.82).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective, longitudinal, population-based study to assess the prevalence and risk factors associated with RCC. The main findings of our study are 1) the overall prevalence of RCC with medication use is approximately 4.0% in the Canadian population; 2) 4.2% of participants had untreated chronic cough at both baseline and follow-up but had not received any pharmacological treatment during that period; 3) both RCC and resolved chronic cough were more commonly observed in English-speaking provinces, mainly Ontario, than in predominantly French-speaking Quebec; 4) smoking cessation and discontinuation of ACEi were both associated with significantly lower odds of RCC; and 5) there were notable associations between RCC and non-respiratory conditions, particularly IBD/IBS and migraine.

These findings have several important clinical implications. First, the natural history of chronic cough in the general population remains poorly characterised, and our results offer some insights into its potential trajectories. A substantial proportion of individuals (27.1%) experienced resolution of chronic cough without the use of medications. Based on our multivariable modelling, we speculate that this improvement may be attributable to modifiable factors such as smoking cessation and discontinuation of ACEi. This cohort of participants could also reflect those individuals that used non-pharmacological methods to control cough, such as cough control therapy or undergoing behavioural modifications (such as avoiding triggers) to alleviate their cough. These data were not captured in the CLSA; however, there is a relative lack of speech and language therapists, physiotherapists and respiratory therapists available to perform cough control therapy.

Conversely, a significant number of participants had chronic cough over a 3-year period but were not receiving any pharmacological treatment. This observation points to potential gaps within the healthcare system that can be attributed to limited access to appropriate care, under-recognition or misdiagnosis of RCC by healthcare providers, or a lack of clear treatment pathways. It may also reflect patient-related barriers such as hesitancy to seek care or initiate therapy. We have previously shown that men with chronic cough are less likely to seek healthcare and this may explain the greater proportion of men in this untreated chronic cough group [1]. Another possibility is that some patients with RCC have less severe coughing that they do not feel warrants treatment, i.e. lower cough frequency, intensity and disruption, and thus further healthcare was not accessed and treatment was not initiated. Nevertheless, these findings highlight the need for improved awareness and accessible treatment strategies for individuals with chronic cough, particularly those with refractory symptoms. This gap also presents an opportunity to educate primary and secondary care physicians. Currently, there are no therapies approved by the US Food and Drug Administration or Health Canada specifically for the treatment of RCC [24]. Neuromodulators such as morphine, pregabalin and gabapentin are commonly used off-label in clinical practice [11–13]; however, our findings indicate that only a minority of individuals with RCC or resolved chronic cough had received these agents. This low utilisation may reflect physician hesitancy, either due to concerns of over-prescribing opioids or uncertainty regarding the diagnosis of RCC, lack of awareness of guidelines, and lack of evidence. This highlights the need for higher quality trials and the need for safer therapies. It is also possible that only those with the most severe coughing have initiated neuromodulators.

Antacid therapies and inhaled respiratory medications were the most frequently prescribed treatments across both groups. Notably, both RCC and resolved chronic cough groups were prescribed these therapies in similar proportions, yet outcomes differed because some responded to treatment and others did not. This variability reflects the heterogeneity of treatment responses and highlights the urgent need for more precise diagnostic frameworks, possible development of biomarkers [25] and evidence-based therapeutic options.

Geographic and linguistic disparities in cough outcomes also emerged from our analysis, which is similar to what was seen in our prior study [26]. Individuals residing in English-speaking regions had higher odds of RCC. These differences may be driven by a range of factors, including cultural or linguistic variations in symptom perception and reporting, disparities in healthcare access and utilisation, or regional differences in climate and indoor and outdoor pollution. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms contributing to these regional variations.

Our mutually adjusted models identified several modifiable risk factors for RCC. Smoking cessation and discontinuation of ACEi were both associated with significantly lower odds of RCC, reinforcing the importance of recognising and addressing these contributors in routine clinical practice [27, 28]. These findings support existing recommendations to evaluate cough-inducing medications and smoking behaviour as part of the initial assessment and management strategy for chronic cough.

In addition, our analysis revealed associations between RCC and non-respiratory chronic conditions, particularly IBD/IBS and migraine. These findings are consistent with prior literature highlighting the multisystemic nature of RCC and support the hypothesis of a distinct disease with a neuronal hypersensitivity component underlying its persistence [20, 29]. The involvement of multiple organ systems suggests that RCC may, in part, reflect a systemic disorder of sensory processing, warranting a broader, interdisciplinary approach to diagnosis and treatment.

Although many studies have examined the prevalence of chronic cough in the general population or healthcare settings, there is a large, unmet need to report the prevalence of RCC. Both the Rotterdam Study and the Tasmanian Longitudinal Health Study are longitudinal cohort studies that have estimated the prevalence of chronic cough at 10.9% and 10%, respectively [30, 31], but not RCC. The Tasmanian study was unique in following participants from age 7 to 53 years, collecting data on cough and associated conditions across the life course. Among those with chronic cough, 46.4% were classified as having unexplained chronic cough. Although longitudinal, the study did not capture medications or data for individuals >53 years old, which is often the most common group for patients with RCC [31]. The Rotterdam Study focused on an older population (>45 years old) and found unexplained chronic cough prevalence to be 7.7%, using a 3-month duration cut-off to define chronic cough. This study did not specifically report on RCC but suggested unexplained chronic cough and RCC may represent a single entity characterised by cough hypersensitivity [32]. In Canada, one cross-sectional online market survey that attempted to identify individuals with RCC and unexplained chronic cough demonstrated a point prevalence of 3%, but no evidence was collected on whether any trials of treatment were offered [33]. A study conducted in Poland reported that 30% of patients presenting with chronic cough had RCC, but this study was limited to 201 patients attending specialist cough clinics over a 3-year period and figures may not be generalisable to the larger population [32]. In contrast, our dataset provides the first national-level estimate of RCC prevalence in Canada and offers unique insights into its clinical trajectory and associated risk factors in a general population sample.

There are limitations to this study. First, estimates of RCC and untreated chronic cough in this study come from a community-based sample, not from a hospital clinic population. While these data allow us to estimate the risk of RCC and untreated RCC, the true prevalence may differ. Establishing a more accurate population estimate would require field testing within the CLSA cohort and validation of our RCC diagnosis using standardised assessment methods to assess cough severity, cough-specific quality of life and investigations and treatments trialled and failed. Second, the CLSA was not specifically designed to study chronic cough, but to examine the aging Canadian population; hence, we do not have data on participants 18–44 years old. Because RCC is a disease of older individuals, our population estimate of 4% in adults is likely higher than the overall adult population >18 years old. Third, our definition of chronic cough relied on a self-reported question about symptom duration, with no information on frequency, severity or impact on cough-specific quality of life. Fourth, the cohort reflects a largely urban, community-dwelling and predominantly white population; findings may not be generalisable to more diverse clinical settings. Fifth, medications were recorded at baseline and follow-up 3 years apart, and there is a possibility of trials of medication for shorter periods in between which have not been captured. Sixth, our definition of RCC includes the use of medications and, therefore, we were unable to investigate the effects of a specific class of medication on the outcome of RCC or resolved chronic cough because we were unable to ascertain which treatment was started at which time point and in which order. We also were unable to capture non-pharmacological treatments in this study. Finally, the CLSA does not capture prior diagnostic work-up or past treatment trials; therefore, “untreated chronic cough” is an operational term reflecting the absence of reported medication use in the study time period, while acknowledging the potential for misclassification (e.g. prior therapy discontinued or normal investigations) or cough which is not severe enough to seek healthcare.

Conclusion

This study represents the first population-based investigation into the prevalence and outcomes of chronic cough. We found nearly one third of chronic cough cases resolved without medical treatment, highlighting the heterogeneity of the disease outcomes. Our findings emphasise the need to address modifiable risk factors, such as smoking and ACEi use, and reveal potential gaps in the current pharmacological management of chronic cough. These results provide important insights for clinicians and point towards the need for more education on, awareness of and novel therapies for RCC.
