Global trends in Alzheimer's and dementia research (2019–2024): A machine learning-based bibliometric analysis
Authors: Anthony Scerri, Kenneth Scerri, Charles Scerri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDementia, including Alzheimer's disease, represents one of the fastest-growing global health challenges. To coordinate research priorities, the World Health Organization (WHO) introduced the Blueprint for Dementia Research in 2022. This framework outlines six research themes spanning disease mechanisms, diagnosis, treatment development, risk reduction, epidemiology and health economics, and care and support. However, empirical evidence assessing whether global research activity aligns with these priorities remains limited.ObjectiveTo analyse global trends in dementia and Alzheimer's disease research and examine how research output is distributed across the thematic priorities defined in the WHO Blueprint for Dementia Research.MethodsA total of 206,636 unique peer-reviewed primary care studies published between 2019 and 2024 were retrieved from databases on the EBSCOhost platform. A machine-learning–assisted natural language processing pipeline classified publications into the six WHO research themes. Temporal trends were assessed and differences in thematic distributions before and after publication of the WHO blueprint were evaluated.ResultsAnnual dementia research output increased from 27,248 publications in 2019 to 39,147 in 2024. Research activity was consistently dominated by studies on disease mechanisms and diagnosis. Risk reduction and drug development demonstrated the strongest growth. Research related to dementia care and support, epidemiology and health economics increased more slowly and declined following publication of the WHO blueprint.ConclusionsGlobal dementia research continues to expand but remains unevenly distributed across research priorities. While progress in biomedical discovery is substantial, comparatively limited growth in care-related research highlights the need for stronger investment in studies addressing implementation and real-world dementia care.

Introduction

Dementia represents one of the most pressing public health challenges of the twenty-first century. Globally, more than 55 million people are estimated to be living with dementia, and this number is projected to rise substantially in the coming decades due to population ageing and demographic change. 1 Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause of dementia, accounting for approximately 60–70% of cases worldwide. Dementia is associated with progressive cognitive decline, loss of functional independence, and increasing care needs, creating profound social, clinical, and economic consequences for individuals, families, and healthcare systems.

The societal burden linked to dementia is substantial and continues to grow. The worldwide cost of dementia was estimated to exceed US$1.3 trillion in 2019, reflecting the combined impact of direct medical costs, social care expenditure, and informal caregiving. 2 Projections suggest that the economic burden will continue to increase markedly in parallel with rising prevalence and longer survival of affected individuals. 3 These trends have intensified international efforts to strengthen dementia research and accelerate the development of effective diagnostic tools, treatments, and care models.

Recent years have witnessed rapid advances in Alzheimer's disease research, particularly in the areas of disease mechanisms, biomarker-based diagnosis, and therapeutic development. Progress in neuroimaging and fluid biomarkers has improved the ability to detect Alzheimer's disease pathology in vivo and has facilitated earlier diagnosis and more precise disease staging. 4 At the same time, increasing attention has been directed toward disease-modifying therapies targeting underlying pathological processes such as amyloid and tau accumulation. 5 These scientific developments have contributed to a significant expansion in global research activity across multiple domains of dementia science.

Despite these advances, questions remain regarding how research activity is distributed across different domains of dementia research. 6 Previous analyses of dementia research priorities and funding patterns suggest that scientific attention may not be evenly distributed across biomedical, clinical, and care-related domains, highlighting the importance of monitoring how research portfolios evolve over time. 7

In response to the need for improved coordination of research efforts, the World Health Organization (WHO) published the Blueprint for Dementia Research in October 2022, which proposed a strategic framework for guiding global research priorities. 8 The blueprint identifies six key research domains: Disease Mechanisms and Models, Diagnosis of Dementia, Drug Development and Clinical Trials, Risk Reduction, Epidemiology and Economics, and Care and Support. These domains span the continuum from basic science and translational research to clinical care and public health. However, empirical evidence examining whether global research activity reflects these priorities remains limited.

Bibliometric analyses have increasingly been used to map research activity and identify emerging trends across biomedical fields including Alzheimer's disease..9,10 Recent studies have applied bibliometric approaches to examine global research patterns in specific areas of neurological science, including investigations of the relationship between Alzheimer's disease and type 2 diabetes mellitus 11 and other specialized topics within dementia research. 12 Such analyses provide valuable insights into publication growth, thematic development, and knowledge gaps within research domains. However, many existing bibliometric studies focus on relatively narrow topics, specific subfields, or limited datasets, and therefore do not provide a comprehensive overview of how dementia research activity is distributed across the broader spectrum of scientific priorities. Little empirical evidence exists on whether the rapidly expanding global dementia research literature aligns with international strategic frameworks designed to guide research investment and coordination. The WHO Blueprint for Dementia Research provides such a framework by defining the six major thematic priorities. Therefore, systematic monitoring of research output is important for understanding whether global research activity reflects these priorities and for identifying potential imbalances within the dementia research landscape.

Advances in natural language processing and machine learning now offer new opportunities to analyze large volumes of scientific publications and to classify research outputs into meaningful thematic domains. These approaches enable more comprehensive mapping of research landscapes and can provide insights into how scientific activity evolves across different areas of dementia research. Moreover, machine learning approaches offer advantages over traditional bibliometric analyses by enabling semantic analysis of large textual corpora and automated classification of research themes at scale. The present study therefore aimed to analyze global trends in dementia and Alzheimer's disease research between 2019 and 2024 and to examine how research output is distributed across the thematic domains outlined in the WHO Blueprint for Dementia Research. Using a machine-learning-assisted bibliometric approach, this study maps a large corpus of dementia research publications to identify dominant research themes, examine temporal trends, and explore whether shifts in research activity are observable following publication of the WHO blueprint.

Methods

Study design

This study used a bibliometric research design combined with machine-learning–assisted natural language processing (NLP) to analyze global dementia research publications between 2019 and 2024. The objective was to evaluate whether the distribution of dementia research output aligns with the thematic priorities defined in the WHO Blueprint for Dementia Research. The analytical workflow consisted of five main stages: literature retrieval, data preprocessing and filtering, machine-learning–assisted thematic classification, clustering and keyword extraction, and statistical trend analysis.

Data sources and search strategy

The literature search was conducted using the EBSCOhost platform, which aggregates several biomedical and social science databases. The EBSCOhost platform was selected because it provides access to multiple large multidisciplinary databases with extensive coverage of biomedical, nursing, and public health literature. This approach enabled retrieval of a broad corpus of dementia-related publications across clinical, epidemiological, and health systems research domains. The following databases were searched: Academic Search Ultimate, AgeLine, CINAHL Complete, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, MEDLINE Complete, and APA PsycInfo.

The search strategy used the query: “dementia OR Alzheimer* OR cognitive impairment.” The wildcard term “Alzheimer*” was used to capture variations such as “Alzheimer's disease” and “Alzheimer disease”. Search expanders (“All fields” and “Apply related words”) were enabled to maximize retrieval. Results were limited to peer-reviewed journal articles published in English with an available abstract. A deliberately broad search strategy was used to maximize sensitivity and capture the full spectrum of dementia-related research, including biomedical, clinical, and public health studies. Additional terms such as “neurodegenerative disease” were not included to avoid retrieving large numbers of publications unrelated to dementia. Searches were conducted separately for each year between 2019 and 2024, and results were exported in CSV format for further processing.

Eligibility criteria

Publications were considered eligible for inclusion if they met several predefined criteria. First, records had to be peer-reviewed journal articles published between 1 January 2019 and 31 December 2024. Second, the publications needed to address dementia, Alzheimer's disease, or cognitive impairment as a central focus of the study. Third, only articles that included an abstract were retained, as the machine-learning classification pipeline relied on textual information from titles and abstracts to perform thematic analysis. Studies were also required to contain sufficient bibliographic information (e.g., title, author details, publication year, and abstract) to enable accurate processing within the analytical workflow.

Review articles, including systematic reviews, scoping reviews, and meta-analyses, were excluded to ensure that the analysis focused on primary research output rather than evidence synthesis studies that summarize existing literature. Editorials, commentaries, perspectives, and opinion papers were also excluded because these publication types typically do not report original empirical findings. To assess whether this decision materially influenced thematic distributions, a sensitivity analysis was conducted on a 2024 EBSCO subset in which the thematic classification pipeline was repeated both with and without inclusion of these publication types. In addition, records lacking adequate bibliographic or textual information were removed, as incomplete entries could not be reliably processed by the natural language processing pipeline.

Data preprocessing

Exported records were processed using a Python-based NLP pipeline implemented in a Jupyter Notebook environment. For each publication, a composite text field was created by combining the title, abstract, and subject terms. Duplicate records were removed prior to analysis. To reduce inclusion of studies unrelated to dementia, records referring exclusively to delirium or acute confusion were excluded unless dementia-related terminology appeared in the surrounding text.

Machine-learning thematic classification

Publications were classified according to the six research themes defined in the WHO Blueprint for Dementia Research, namely: Epidemiology and Economics, Disease Mechanisms and Models, Diagnosis of Dementia, Drug Development and Clinical Trials, Care and Support, and Risk Reduction.

Sentence embeddings were generated using the transformer model all-MiniLM-L6-v2 implemented in the sentence-transformers library. This model produces dense semantic representations of text that enable similarity-based comparison of research abstracts.

Seed phrases representing each WHO research theme were encoded to create thematic reference vectors. Publications were scored according to their semantic similarity to these vectors. High-confidence classifications were used to train a multinomial logistic regression classifier with balanced class weighting. Publications with lower confidence scores were assigned themes using combined semantic similarity and lexical matching.

Clustering and keyword extraction

To explore thematic structure within the dataset, publication embeddings were grouped using K-means clustering. Cluster-level keyword profiles were generated using a CountVectorizer bag-of-words representation, which identifies frequently occurring terms within each cluster.

Clusters were then mapped to WHO research themes based on the majority classification of publications within each cluster.

Statistical analysis

Temporal trends in research output across the WHO research themes were examined using two complementary approaches. First, least-squares linear regression was applied to estimate the annual rate of change in publication counts for each research theme and to calculate coefficients of determination (R2). Regression slopes were estimated using the linregress implementation in the SciPy statistical library. Second, Mann–Kendall tests were used to detect monotonic trends in publication counts over time. To assess whether the publication of the WHO blueprint in 2022 was associated with changes in research priorities, thematic distributions were compared between two time periods: pre-blueprint period [2021–2022] versus early post-blueprint transition period [2023–2024]. Differences between periods were evaluated using chi-square tests of independence. Because multiple thematic comparisons were conducted, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate.

Robustness, validation, and reproducibility

To assess the stability of the classification results, the analytical pipeline was repeated using multiple random seeds. The yearly distribution of publications across WHO research themes remained highly consistent across runs. To further evaluate the plausibility of the automated classification, a random subset of publications was manually reviewed by the authors. To assess classification validity, a stratified random sample of 479 publications from the 2024 dataset was independently manually coded according to the six WHO blueprint research themes using a structured coding framework derived from the blueprint's definitions and predefined decision rules for overlapping domains. Manual coding was compared against automated thematic classification outputs. Manual validation demonstrated 80.6% overall agreement between automated and manual classification. Inter-rater agreement between manual coding and automated classification was substantial (Cohen's κ = 0.77; weighted κ = 0.76). Theme-specific F1 scores ranged from 0.74 to 0.86, with highest performance observed for Care and Support, and Disease Mechanisms and Models. Most disagreements occurred between conceptually adjacent domains, particularly Disease Mechanisms and Models versus Diagnosis of Dementia, reflecting overlap between mechanistic biomarker studies and diagnostic applications.

All analyses were conducted using Python in a Jupyter Notebook environment. Machine-learning procedures were implemented using the sentence-transformers, scikit-learn, RapidFuzz, and SciPy libraries, and visualizations were produced using Matplotlib. The analytical pipeline and processed datasets are publicly available in a Zenodo repository to facilitate reproducibility. An analytical workflow is presented in Figure 1. Moreover, the thematic seed phrases and classification decision rules used can be found in Supplemental Material 4 [https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20645601].

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate whether exclusive reliance on EBSCOhost may have underrepresented biomedical engineering and technology-oriented dementia research, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a Scopus sample of dementia-related studies published during January–February 2024. The same preprocessing, topic modelling, and thematic classification pipeline was applied to both datasets. Of 2883 Scopus records, 77.13% overlapped with EBSCOhost coverage, while 658 records were unique to Scopus. Comparative analysis demonstrated broadly similar thematic distributions across databases, although Scopus showed greater representation of Diagnosis of Dementia (+8.13 percentage points), likely reflecting stronger indexing of biomedical engineering, computational, and diagnostic methodology research. Despite these differences, the dominant thematic profile remained consistent across datasets, with Disease Mechanisms and Models and Diagnosis of Dementia continuing to predominate.

A further sensitivity analysis was carried out to examine whether the exclusion of non-primary publication types, disproportionately affected the thematic distribution. The classification pipeline was repeated on a 2024 EBSCO subset with and without inclusion of reviews, commentaries, editorials, and perspectives. Inclusion of these publication types resulted in only modest shifts in proportional theme distributions, with a maximum absolute percentage-point difference of 1.10 across WHO themes. Care and Support increased slightly from 12.16% to 13.00% (+0.84 percentage points), while Disease Mechanisms and Models remained comparatively stable (33.75% vs 33.71%). Absolute proportional changes were small, suggesting that exclusion of non-primary publication types did not materially influence the principal thematic findings.

Trend analysis of research themes between 2019 and 2024

Table 3 summarizes the results of the least-squares linear regression and Mann–Kendall trend analyses for each WHO research theme. Linear regression analysis indicated increasing publication counts across most themes between 2019 and 2024. The strongest linear growth was observed in studies related to Risk Reduction (slope = 334.8 publications per year; R2 = 0.891), followed by Drug Development and Clinical Trials, Diagnosis of Dementia, and Disease Mechanisms and Models. In contrast, themes related to Care and Support and Epidemiology and Economics demonstrated more modest growth, with weaker regression model fits (R2 = 0.305 and 0.501, respectively). The Mann–Kendall analysis, which evaluates monotonic trends independent of linear assumptions, identified statistically significant increases only for Risk Reduction (Tau = 1.00, p = 0.009) and Drug Development and Clinical Trials (Tau = 0.867, p = 0.024).

Table 4 compares the distribution of publications across WHO research themes between the pre-blueprint period (2021–2022) and the early post-blueprint transition period (2023–2024). Significant proportional increases were observed for Risk Reduction research (8824 to 9627 publications; χ2 = 42.42, p < 0.001) and Drug Development and Clinical Trials (7055 to 7556 publications; χ2 = 20.58, p < 0.001). These increases are indicative of a relative expansion of research activity in these areas during the later period. In contrast, the proportional share of publications related to Care and Support declined significantly between the two periods (9145 to 8222 publications; χ2 = 52.81, p < 0.001). A smaller but statistically significant decline was also observed for Epidemiology and Economics (7228 to 6894 publications; χ2 = 7.77, p = 0.005). No statistically significant changes were observed for Disease Mechanisms and Models or Diagnosis of Dementia, indicating that these themes maintained relatively stable shares of the overall research portfolio across the two time periods. Discussion

The present study provides a comprehensive analysis of global trends in Alzheimer's disease and dementia research between 2019 and 2024 and examines how research activity aligns with the thematic priorities outlined in the WHO Blueprint for Dementia Research. Using a machine-learning–assisted bibliometric approach applied to more than 200,000 publications, the study offers a large-scale overview of how dementia research output is distributed across six major research domains spanning biomedical discovery, clinical research, and care. Overall, the findings indicate that global dementia research output increased substantially during the study period, but that research activity remains unevenly distributed across the blueprint themes.

The analysis showed that global dementia research output increased by approximately 44% between 2019 and 2024, rising from 27,248 to 39,147 publications. This expansion reflects growing scientific and clinical attention directed toward dementia in response to its increasing global burden. Across the study period, research activity was dominated by publications related to disease mechanisms and models and dementia diagnosis, which consistently accounted for the largest proportion of dementia research output. This pattern reflects the continued expansion of biomedical research in Alzheimer's disease, particularly advances in neuroimaging and biomarker-based diagnostics. 4 Such developments have facilitated the emergence of biologically defined disease frameworks and have supported the design of more targeted clinical trials aimed at modifying disease progression. Notably, this trajectory mirrors trends identified in earlier bibliometric analyses of Alzheimer's disease research, which documented a progressive shift toward molecular mechanisms, biomarker discovery, and translational neuroscience over the past several decades. 10 The present findings suggest that this biomedical emphasis continues to shape the contemporary dementia research landscape.

Significant growth was also observed in research related to risk reduction and drug development and clinical trials. In the present study, both linear regression and Mann–Kendall analyses indicated statistically significant increases in publication output for these themes. These findings suggest that prevention research and therapeutic development represent areas of sustained expansion within the global dementia research portfolio. The increasing prominence of risk-reduction research reflects growing recognition that dementia risk is influenced by multiple modifiable factors across the life course. 13 At the same time, renewed momentum in Alzheimer's disease drug development, including the emergence of disease-modifying therapies targeting amyloid pathology, has stimulated increased activity in clinical trials and therapeutic evaluation. 5

Within the limitations of this study, the findings suggest that the ambition outlined in the WHO Global Action Plan on the Public Health Response to Dementia 2017–2025 to double global dementia research output by 2025 may be challenging to achieve within the remaining timeframe. 14 Although this study demonstrates a steady increase in primary dementia research publications between 2019 and 2024, the observed rate of growth may still be insufficient to meet this target. A temporary decline in publication output observed in 2023 may partly reflect disruptions associated with the COVID-19 pandemic, which affected research activity across many scientific fields. Surveys of health researchers indicate that numerous projects experienced delays or interruptions during this period. 15 Similar patterns were reported within dementia research, where a multinational survey of early-career dementia researchers identified widespread research delays, project modifications, and funding challenges. 16 The recovery in publication output observed in 2024 suggests that research activity has resumed momentum following these disruptions.

Despite the overall expansion of dementia research, the results of this study highlight comparatively limited growth in research related to dementia care and support, and epidemiology and economics. Although the absolute number of publications in these areas increased modestly over time, their proportional share within the global research portfolio remained relatively small and declined following publication of the WHO blueprint. These findings raise concerns regarding progress toward research priorities that emphasize health-system evidence, including the development of models of care, implementation strategies, and economic evaluation of interventions. Without stronger evidence in these domains, there is a risk that advances in biomedical discovery may not translate fully into improvements in clinical practice and service delivery.

The imbalance between biomedical discovery and applied research has been highlighted in previous analyses of dementia research funding. Pickett and Brayne reported that global dementia research investment remains heavily concentrated in basic and translational science, while comparatively fewer resources are allocated to studies addressing care systems and service delivery. 6 The findings of the present study suggest that this imbalance continues to be reflected within the global research literature. Achieving greater balance across research domains may therefore be important for ensuring that scientific advances translate into improvements in real-world dementia care.

Interestingly, the priorities observed in the global research literature differ from those identified by key stakeholders. Studies examining stakeholder perspectives have consistently found that people living with dementia and their caregivers prioritize research related to post-diagnostic support, caregiver wellbeing, workforce training, culturally competent services, and equitable care delivery. 17 These priorities reflect the everyday challenges experienced by individuals affected by dementia and highlight the importance of research addressing practical aspects of care and support. The divergence between stakeholder priorities and the distribution of research output may partly reflect structural incentives within research systems that favor biomedical innovation.

The pattern observed in this study also aligns with the concept of an “implementation cliff” in dementia innovation described by Boccardi and colleagues. 18 According to this perspective, advances in discovery science may fail to translate into routine practice when implementation considerations are not incorporated early in the research process. Strengthening implementation science and health-services research may therefore be essential to ensure that emerging innovations generate tangible benefits for people living with dementia and their caregivers.

From a research governance perspective, these findings highlight the importance of maintaining a balanced dementia research ecosystem that supports both biomedical discovery and improvements in care delivery. Continued monitoring of research portfolios may help identify gaps in global research investment and guide strategic allocation of funding across research domains. Importantly, the relevance of such monitoring has been reinforced by the recent decision of the World Health Assembly to extend the Global Action Plan on the Public Health Response to Dementia 2017–2025 to 2031, providing an extended timeframe for countries and research systems to strengthen dementia research capacity and accelerate progress toward global dementia research priorities. 19

Strengthening patient and public involvement (PPI) in dementia research may also improve the relevance and implementation potential of future studies. Meaningful engagement of people living with dementia and caregivers throughout the research process can help ensure that research priorities reflect lived experiences and practical care needs. However, organizations such as Alzheimer Europe emphasize that such engagement should be meaningful rather than tokenistic and should include appropriate safeguards and support mechanisms for participants. 20 Embedding robust PPI requirements within research funding and ethics processes may therefore represent an important mechanism for strengthening patient-centered research and improving the translation of evidence into practice. 21

Comparison of the pre-blueprint (2021–2022) and early post-blueprint transition (2023–2024) periods identified statistically significant proportional increases in research related to Risk Reduction, and Drug Development and Clinical Trials, alongside significant declines in the relative share of publications focused on Care and Support, and Epidemiology and Economics. While these findings suggest shifts in the thematic composition of dementia research during the later period, they should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct impact of the WHO blueprint document. The latter was published in October 2022, and the period examined after its publication represents only an initial transition phase. Research funding cycles, grant approvals, study development, ethical review processes, data collection, manuscript preparation, and publication delays frequently span several years. Consequently, a substantial proportion of studies published during 2023–2024 were likely conceived, funded, and initiated before the release of the blueprint. The observed changes in thematic distributions should therefore be interpreted as descriptive indicators of evolving research priorities rather than causal effects attributable to the blueprint itself. Continued monitoring over a longer timeframe will be necessary to determine whether the strategic priorities outlined by the WHO are associated with sustained changes in the global dementia research landscape.

More broadly, this study highlights the value of systematic monitoring of research portfolios as a tool for evaluating alignment between scientific activity and global health priorities. Bibliometric analyses can provide important insights into how research activity evolves over time and may help funding agencies and policymakers identify under-represented areas of investigation. In addition, advances in machine learning and natural language processing enable automated analysis of large bodies of scientific literature, allowing more comprehensive mapping of research landscapes than traditional bibliometric approaches. 22 Such analytical methods may support ongoing monitoring of global dementia research trends and contribute to more evidence-informed research governance. Finally, an understanding how research output is distributed across thematic domains is particularly relevant for Alzheimer's disease, where advances in biomarkers, disease-modifying therapies, and prevention strategies require complementary progress in health systems research and implementation science.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. The analysis was based on a large dataset of more than 200,000 publications retrieved from multiple biomedical and social science databases, providing broad coverage of global dementia research output over a six-year period. The use of machine-learning–assisted text classification also enabled systematic thematic mapping of a large corpus of scientific literature, allowing identification of research patterns that would be difficult to detect using traditional bibliometric approaches.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis was restricted to English-language publications indexed in selected EBSCOhost databases and containing abstracts. Consequently, some relevant research published in other languages or in non-indexed sources may not have been captured. Second, the classification of publications into WHO research themes relied on automated text-analysis methods. Although validation procedures indicated high consistency in thematic assignments, multidisciplinary studies that span multiple research domains may not be fully captured within a single category. Third, although robustness analyses demonstrated consistent results across multiple random seeds, some themes—particularly those related to dementia diagnosis, and epidemiology and economics—showed borderline statistical significance, indicating that trends in these areas should be interpreted cautiously. Moreover, bibliometric analyses quantify research output but do not directly measure the quality or impact of individual studies. Consequently, the thematic distribution identified in this study reflects publication trends rather than the relative scientific importance of specific research areas.

Although the search strategy included multiple EBSCOhost databases, Scopus and Web of Science were not searched. Because Scopus indexes additional biomedical engineering and computational science journals, the sensitivity analysis showed that the overall thematic profile and dominance of major WHO blueprint themes remained stable, supporting the robustness of the primary findings. Moreover, although the search strategy was intentionally broad and incorporated commonly used umbrella terms for dementia research (e.g., dementia, Alzheimer's disease, and cognitive impairment), it is possible that some publications focusing primarily on specific dementia subtypes or emerging terminology were not captured. For example, studies referring exclusively to conditions such as frontotemporal dementia, Lewy body dementia, vascular cognitive impairment, posterior cortical atrophy, or other less common neurodegenerative syndromes without explicit reference to dementia-related keywords may have been underrepresented. Similarly, evolving terminology and disease-specific nomenclature may not have been fully captured by the search strategy. However, the large dataset analysed and the consistency of findings across multiple sensitivity analyses suggest that any such omissions are unlikely to have materially altered the overall thematic distribution of dementia research observed in this study.

A further limitation relates to the temporal interpretation of pre- and post-blueprint comparisons. Although the WHO Blueprint for Dementia Research was published in October 2022, the comparative periods used in this study (2021–2022 versus 2023–2024) do not perfectly align with the timing of policy dissemination and research implementation. As previously highlighted, research funding cycles, grant approvals, ethics procedures, study conduct, and publication delays mean that any genuine response to the blueprint's priorities would likely emerge gradually and may not be fully observable within the 2023–2024 publication period. Consequently, findings should be interpreted as reflecting an early post-blueprint or transition period rather than definitive evidence of its impact. Continued monitoring of research portfolios over a longer timeframe will therefore be necessary to determine whether the blueprint leads to measurable shifts in global dementia research priorities.

Conclusions

Overall, the findings of this study highlight both the rapid expansion and the persistent imbalances within the global dementia research landscape. While substantial progress has been made in advancing biomedical understanding and therapeutic development, comparatively less growth has occurred in research addressing dementia care, health systems, and implementation. Achieving a balanced research ecosystem will be essential to ensure that scientific advances translate into meaningful improvements in prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and care for people living with dementia. The importance of sustained research investment has been reaffirmed by the recent decision of the World Health Assembly to extend its Global Action Plan to 2031, providing an extended timeframe to strengthen global dementia research capacity and accelerate progress toward international research priorities. Continued monitoring of research portfolios may therefore play an important role in guiding strategic research investment and ensuring that future scientific advances translate into improved outcomes for people affected by dementia.
