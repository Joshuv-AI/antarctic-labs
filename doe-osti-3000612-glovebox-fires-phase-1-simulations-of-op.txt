ABSTRACT
Understanding the duration of a fire required to breach a glovebox via glove ports and/or windows
within a facility is important in developing mitigation strategies for safety. To facilitate this
understanding, both simulation and experiments are utilized. The approach is to first validate the
Computation Fluid Dynamics (CFD) code, Fire Dynamics Simulator (FDS), with experimental data
collected by New Mexico Tech (NMT) and then perform simulations of full-scale rooms containing
gloveboxes to assess numerous scenarios which would otherwise be cost-prohibitive experimentally.
This report provides comparison to first-phase experiments involving a fire without a glovebox. A
subsequent report will provide comparison to experiments involving a glovebox. The results indicate
good agreement with FDS tending to over predict the pre-mixed and diffusion-mode tests by 6%
and 10%, respectively.

1. INTRODUCTION
The objective of this project is to determine through testing and simulation the size and duration of a
fire required to first cause a glovebox to breach via glove ports and/or windows and then activate
nearby fire sprinklers within a facility. The project is comprised of an experimental and simulation
effort in which experiments are performed by New Mexico Tech (NMT) and simulations performed
by Sandia National Laboratories. The work described and discussed in this report pertains to the
simulations carried out using the Fire Dynamics Simulator (FDS), a Large Eddy Simulation (LES)
based computational fluid dynamics code developed by National Institute of Standards and
Technology (NIST) to model fires. This report provides a description of the geometry, mesh, and
boundary conditions used for each test series, as well as comparison between simulation and
experimental data.
The experiments involve several series, the first involving a fire using a gas burner without a glovebox,
the second involving a fire using a gas burner within a glovebox, and the third involving burning
combustibles in one glovebox which is to be completed. The objective of using simulation is to
evaluate fires within gloveboxes in full-scale rooms to assess numerous scenarios which would
otherwise be cost-prohibitive experimentally. In applying simulation to model full-scale rooms,
confidence is first gained through validation which is carried out by comparison to the three
experimental series at relatively small-scale with respect to room size.
The experiments are performed in a bunker that has been modified to resemble a room with
ventilation. Dry sprinklers are installed at the same height representative of a glovebox room to
determine the temperature and time of activation resulting from a fire with a given heat release rate
which is based on the fuel flow rate and heat of combustion. The burner used for the first and second
experimental series can operate in a pre-mixed or a diffusion mode.
This report presents comparison of simulations and experimental results from the open burner tests.
In the second test series, measurement of the airflow into the glovebox was not successfully obtained.
This is a key input parameter for simulations since it can significantly affect the temperature field and
hence comparison to thermocouple measurements. Thus, comparison to only the first test series is
presented.
Section 2 provides a description of the method used to quantify model performance, while Section 3
provides the simulation specifications and validation comparison to the open burner experiments, and
section 4 provides the conclusions that can be drawn from this comparison.

2. VALIDATION QUANTIFICATION
The approach taken to quantify the accuracy of model predictions of experimental results follows the
recommendation provided in [1] [2]. The approach presented in [1] [2] addresses how to identify
model uncertainty and provides performance measures for the degree of under or over-prediction and
scatter. The degree of under- or over-prediction is measured by the bias factor, δ, and the degree of
scatter by the model relative standard deviation, 𝜎𝜎
 � 𝑀𝑀. It is assumed that model predictions are normally
distributed about true values multiplied by the bias factor. To assess this, a histogram and normality
test of the natural log of the ratio of model predictions to experimental measurement is performed.
This method considers three sources of uncertainty, namely, from experimental measurement, model
parameter inputs from experimental measurement, and those arising directly from the model.
Model input uncertainties based on experimental measurement may include quantities such as air
inflow conditions and heat release rate for example. These sources of uncertainty are termed parameter
uncertainty and are used to determine the propagated input uncertainty. Both the propagated input
uncertainty and measurement uncertainty must be identified to determine a combined uncertainty
which is then used to determine the model uncertainty.
The measurement uncertainty pertains to measurements used for model comparison. In reference [1],
measurement uncertainty has been determined for certain measured quantities of interest (e.g. flame
temperature, heat flux, gas concentrations, velocity etc.) from a range of experiments that have
measured these quantities. Pertinent values from reference [1] are provided in Table 2-1.

3.1.1. Simulation specifications
Section 3.1.1.1 provides a description of the mesh and domain used for all simulations. Section 3.1.1.2
provides a description of the boundary conditions used for the pre-mixed and diffusion flame
simulations.

3.1.1.1. Mesh and Domain
FDS requires a text input file in which the geometry, mesh, and domain are created through associated
command lines. The text input for the geometry in the FDS format is created using Blender, an open-
source code. Since FDS has only a Cartesian mesh capability, creating a non-linear structure can be
quite involved since the x, y, and z locations and dimensions for the numerous blocks comprising a
structure must be specified in the input file. Blender allows automation of this process. A solid model
with an ‘stl’ format is first created and imported into Blender which then decomposes the geometry
into blocks. The geometric information for the numerous blocks is then outputted into a text format
suitable for an FDS input file.

A relatively fine mesh compared to the size of the facility is required to resolve the fuel inlet boundary
since a single burner head was used for the experiments which has a width of 0.05 m. Note that the
burner consists of 6 individual burner heads which can be operated independently of each other.
Thus, the total cell count is approximately 5.4 million. A stretched grid can be used in FDS but as
mentioned in the FDS user’s manual for the LES model, eddy formation responsible for mixing is
based on the largest length scale of the cell size so that if the mesh is stretched, the longest dimension
of the cell will be used to determine the eddy size. Thus, eddy resolution to capture mixing will not be
better for the stretched cells compared to uniform cells. Also, the manual mentions that stretching the
grid reduces the computational efficiency, especially if the grid is stretched along two coordinate
directions. Thus, a uniform mesh was used for these simulations. The run time for these simulations
was almost 4 days using 84 processors. A grid refinement of this mesh would require 43M elements
and about 656 processors. Given the real time of 1300 seconds over which the simulations were
performed, such a refinement would be computationally cost prohibitive.

Note that the temperature of the incoming air was measured at the entrance to the test facility and not
at the diffuser. The experiments involving a glovebox performed after this test series indicates that
the air entering at the diffuser increased by approximately 41oF above the measured outside
temperature. This is due to the duct leading to the diffuser being exposed to the heated environment
during testing. Thus, for the open burner simulations, the air temperature at the diffuser is specified
41oF (23oC) higher than the measured outside temperature.
For the experiments with the burner operating in diffusion mode, a heat release rate per unit area was
specified based on the measured average fuel flow rate and the heat of combustion to represent the
fire. For the premixed mode experiments, a surrogate representation of the fire was required since
FDS has been designed to model diffusion flames and does not have a direct capability to model
premixed flames. Thus, the approach to represent the flame is to release a stoichiometric mixture of
combustion products at a temperature corresponding somewhere between the lower flammability
limit and stoichiometry. A temperature of 1500oC is used for the simulations. The stoichiometric
balance for propane is the following.
C3H8 + 5O2 + 18.8 N2 => 3CO2 + 4H2O + 18.8 N2
By using respective molecular weight and the above stoichiometric coefficients, the mass of each
product component can be determined. Then based on the fuel release rate, the product release rate
and ultimately mass flux can be determined as provided in Table 3-5. The same value for product mass
flux is used for each experiment since the measured flow rate is approximately 0.93 g/s for all
premixed tests. The product mass flux for the 100 kW experiments is about 2.16 g/s.

3.1.2.3.   Variations
Simulations are performed varying the direction and temperature of the air entering the test facility.
One case specifies the direction of the incoming air as perpendicular to the vent while another
implements a directional flow of the incoming air accounting for the effect of the diffuser as described
in section 3.1.1.2. The variation for the heating of the air duct utilizes data from glovebox experiments
subsequently performed. The heated duct simulation specifies the air temperature at the diffuser as
41oF (23oC) higher than the measured outside temperature. The diffusion mode test, T1, is used for
these cases. Table 3-10 provides the temperature and direction of the incoming air.
Figure 3-12 shows a comparison of temperatures averaged over 15-20 minutes at sprinkler location E
for the variational cases. The comparison indicates that having the incoming air louvered versus
entering normal to the surface is the most impactful variation. This indicates the importance of
characterizing the velocity profile of the incoming air experimentally to provide a representative
boundary condition for simulations. The louvered representation of the incoming air presented in
section 3.1.1.2 is an approximation since the velocity was not measured across the intake vent. Thus,
the effect of a more accurate representation is unknown.
Including both the heating and the louver for the incoming air results in significantly higher
temperatures compared to not taking them into account. This is also true of the centerline
temperatures above the burner shown in Figure 3-13 which indicates a much greater effect than the
comparisons at location E. Thus, based on these findings the results presented in sections 3.1.2.1 and
3.1.2.2 include the heating and directional aspects of the incoming air.

3.1.2.4. Sustaining a fire within a glovebox
In addition to performing simulations of the open burner experiments, simulations are performed to
investigate scenarios involving the ability of a fire to be sustained within a glovebox, specifically, FDS
is used to address the following questions.
 1. Can two open glove ports support a 100-kW fire for 20 minutes with and without air being
 supplied to the glove box?
 2. What is the maximum heat release rate that can be sustained with an air supply of 40 acfm
 and no glove ports open?

Figure 3-14 and Figure 3-15 show time-averaged O2 concentration contours from simulation results

APPENDIX A. DESCRIPTION OF OPEN BURNER EXPERIMENTS
The following description of the open burner experiments was provided by New Mexico Tech who
performed the experiments.

A.1. Room Layout
The test room at the 2-Ton site is the location of the open burner tests. The general layout of the
room including the installed ventilation layout is shown in Figure A-1. The floor of the room is 17’2”
wide by 31’ 7” deep. The floor in the rear of the room is poured concrete. The floor in the front of
the room is suspended over a basement is constructed of extruded steel with plywood and cement
backer board overlays. The stairwell on the right side of the room has also been covered with plywood
and cement board. The room ceiling is constructed of hemicylindrical corrugated steel. A T-bar
suspended “drop” ceiling has been installed at a height of 12’6” from the floor to provide a flat ceiling
spanning the full length and the majority of the width of the room. A portion of the corrugated ceiling
is exposed at the edges of the drop ceiling. Rockwool insulation has been used to fill the gaps between
the edge of the drop ceiling and the corrugated steel.

 front
  Figure A-1. Schematic top view of the room detailing the geometry and the ventilation layout.

 37

A.2. Ventilation Layout
The test room has been outfitted with independent intake and exhaust systems as detailed in Figure
A-1. The intake system brings air from outside of the room via 12” diameter round ductwork and
delivers air via a ceiling mounted 2’ x 2’ square diffuser located near the front of the room (2’ from
the front wall, 7’ 6” from the left wall). The exhaust system removes air from the bunker via two 12”
diameter round ducts located at the rear floor of the room. The face of the left exhaust is located
15” from the rear wall, and the center line of the duct is 9” from the left wall. The face of the right
exhaust is located 17” from the rear wall and the centerline of the duct is 8” from the right wall.
From each of these exhaust intakes ductwork extends vertically and then horizontally joining at a tee
before joining the vent stack passing through the corrugated ceiling.

A.3. Burner layout
The burner consists of 6 individual burner heads which can be operated independently of each
other. The arrangement is detailed in Figure A-2. Each burner is 20” long and 2” wide, and a gap of
2” is between individual burners. The schematic only shows the portions of the burners that have
holes for propane to flow out of.
