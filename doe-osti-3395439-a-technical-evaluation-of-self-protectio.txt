ABSTRACT
U.S. nuclear security policy uses radiation self-protection as a basis for reducing material
attractiveness, but existing dose-rate criteria may not reflect the time scales of theft or sabotage. This
report evaluates whether the commonly cited 1 Gy/h at 1 m criterion can plausibly cause adversary
task failure during short-duration malicious acts. Published security timeline studies were reviewed
to establish a working adversary action window of approximately 10 to 30 minutes, and clinical
radiobiology, accident evidence, and controlled animal studies were assessed to relate acute dose to
early functional impairment. The analysis shows that 1 Gy/h produces only about 0.17 to 0.5 Gy
over this window, well below doses associated with rapid incapacitation. A revised screening
criterion in the range of 25 to 100 Gy/h at 1 m would better align self-protection with minutes-scale
incapacitation objectives.

1. INTRODUCTION
U.S. domestic nuclear security policy uses the concept of “self-protection” to denote nuclear
material whose radiation field provides an inherent barrier to unauthorized access and removal.
Existing guidance referenced by the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) and the International
Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) treats a dose rate on the order of 1 Gy/h at 1 m as providing some
degree of self-protection for nuclear material [1, 2].
IAEA Nuclear Security Series No. 13 (INFCIRC/225) notes that nuclear material with an
unshielded radiation level above 1 Gy/h at 1 meter, classified as Category I or II by fissile content,
may be downgraded by one category due to self-protection. However, the guidance explicitly states
that if the threat assessment includes adversaries willing to carry out malicious acts, States should
carefully consider whether such a reduction is appropriate, as radiation may not reliably incapacitate
determined adversaries before task completion [2].
In the Department of Energy (DOE) safeguards framework, the graded safeguards table similarly
recognizes that sufficiently irradiated material may present a high probability of task failure for an
adversary and therefore has reduced attractiveness for theft or diversion [3]. However, policy
documents and derivative guidance do not consistently articulate what dose-rate level is sufficient to
ensure adversary task failure on time scales relevant to theft or sabotage. This report evaluates the
technical basis and practical adequacy of the dose-rate criteria currently associated with self-
protection, with the goal of providing a clearer reference for policy discussions.
A key issue is that the numerical self-protection threshold for self-protection appears to be rooted in
eventual lethality rather than rapid incapacitation. Although this approach may be meaningful for
emergency planning, the current self-protection criterion is based on lethality in days from an hours-
scale exposure, but theft or sabotage can occur on minutes-scale timelines [4]. A meaningful self-
protection threshold must therefore address incapacitation on the time scale of the attempted act.
This distinction matters because some adversary actions can be completed quickly once access is
obtained. For example, the Sandia Proliferation Vulnerability Red Team Report estimates that a
successful overt theft could take only ten to twenty minutes [4]. Other studies using scenario
simulation and security timeline analysis likewise support that certain sabotage actions against
research reactor and commercial light water reactor fuel could be completed on the order of minutes
to tens of minutes under assumed access conditions [5, 6]. If a consequential act can be completed
within roughly 10 to 30 minutes, then a self-protection criterion intended to ensure adversary task
failure should be tied to health effects that manifest on the same time scale, not solely to effects that
occur hours or days later.
This report therefore evaluates self-protection through the lens of acute radiation effects that
plausibly interfere with sustained task performance during short-duration actions. The analysis draws
on clinical descriptions of acute radiation syndrome (ARS) to relate dose and dose rate to the timing
of early symptoms. Found-source and accident reports are also used to provide qualitative
corroboration that severe or even lethal exposures can be compatible with meaningful activity for
substantial periods, depending on dose, dose rate, shielding, and source geometry. Controlled animal
studies are included as supporting evidence where human data are sparse. These evidence streams

 8

are synthesized to assess whether the current 1 Gy/h at 1 m convention is aligned with a minutes-
scale adversary timeline and to identify directions for updating policy criteria.
The scope of this report is intentionally first-order. It focuses on photon-dominated external
radiation fields and treats “dose rate at 1 m” as an unshielded screening metric, consistent with how
the criterion is commonly stated in existing guidance [7]. Detailed modeling of specific facility
layouts, protective force response, adversary path analysis, and shielding tactics is outside the scope
of this report. Mixed neutron and gamma fields, including criticality events, are discussed only for
contextual insight into very high dose-rate consequences and are not used as the primary basis for a
general dose-rate threshold. Within these bounds, the report aims to clarify the origin of the existing
threshold, connect dose-rate criteria to symptom timing that is relevant to short-duration malicious
acts, and provide a technically grounded starting point for future work on more detailed, scenario-
specific measures of self-protection.
This report is organized as follows. Section 2 summarizes how self-protection dose-rate thresholds
are stated in current guidance and reviews the historical basis for the commonly cited 1 Gy/h
criterion. Section 3 reviews evidence on the time scales associated with theft or sabotage attempts,
with emphasis on minutes-scale timelines reported in prior vulnerability and security timeline
studies. Sections 4 and 5 summarize clinical and experimental evidence on the timing of acute
radiation effects to short-term functional impairment, supplemented by qualitative timelines from
accident and found-source reports. Section 6 reviews experimental studies of radiation effects on
animals, filling gaps left by limited human data. Section 7 integrates these findings to assess the
adequacy of current dose-rate thresholds and to identify policy updates and limitations.

2. DEFINITION OF SELF-PROTECTION DOSE-RATE CRITERIA AND
 TECHNICAL BASIS
The concept of self-protection appears in several safeguards and physical protection contexts as a
way to distinguish material that is inherently more difficult to handle because of its radiation field. In
NRC and IAEA guidance, self-protection is commonly expressed in terms of a dose rate measured
at a fixed distance from the unshielded material [1, 2]. In U.S. domestic policy, the DOE graded
safeguards table guidance similarly recognizes that sufficiently irradiated material can impart a life-
threatening dose to an adversary, leading to reduced attractiveness and potentially reduced control
requirements relative to more accessible forms of the same material [3].
Policy guidance requires a practical way to represent the radiation barrier across many material forms
and configurations. Dose rate at 1 m is therefore used as a practical screening metric rather than a
complete description of the exposure that an individual would receive while performing a task. Even
for sources with relatively simple geometry, the dose an adversary would receive during an attempt
depends on details of how the material is approached and handled. Time spent near the source,
distance to the source during key tasks, and any shielding or remote handling methods can all change
the accumulated dose. A single measurement point at 1 m also does not capture non-uniform
irradiation when hands and arms are closer to the source than the torso. Despite these limitations,
the 1 m dose rate remains attractive because it is straightforward to state, relatively easy to measure
or calculate, and provides a consistent basis for categorization.
To evaluate whether that screening metric is meaningful for self-protection, it is necessary to
distinguish between radiation effects that occur too late to affect task performance and those that
can impair function during the attempted act. The International Commission on Radiological
Protection defines deterministic effects as “Injury in populations of cells, characterized by a threshold
dose and an increase in the severity of the reaction as the dose is increased further.” Deterministic
effects are caused by high doses, often over a short time period, referred to as an acute exposure.
Stochastic effects are defined, “malignant disease and heritable effects for which the probability of an
effect occurring, but not its severity, is regarded as a function of dose without threshold ” [8]. In this
work, stochastic effects, due to their probabilistic nature and time to become apparent are
unimportant and do not result in inability to function. Deterministic effects impair physical
capabilities; therefore, those effects discussed here are deterministic in nature.
When expressing exposure to radiation, two quantities are considered: absorbed dose and equivalent
dose. Absorbed dose is energy absorbed per mass of tissue and units are the SI unit gray (Gy) and
the traditional unit rad. This quantity is best used to describe deterministic effects and, as such, is
used here. The equivalent dose quantity is used to describe stochastic risk by applying a radiation
weighting factor to absorbed dose that is based on the radiation type. The corresponding units are
sievert (Sv) for the SI system and the historical unit rem. This quantity will not be used further. For
reference, 1 Gy = 100 rad and 1 Sv = 100 rem.
The original DOE graded safeguards table addressed radiation exposure for two attractiveness
levels. “Moderately irradiated” material was considered low grade attractiveness, and “highly
irradiated” material was a catch-all group for the lowest attractiveness level. In the early 1990s, the
accompanying guidance document for the order containing the graded safeguards table specified
numerical thresholds of 0.15 Gy/h (15 rad/h) and 1 Gy/h (100 rad/h) for moderately and highly
irradiated material, respectively. Moderately irradiated material was removed from the graded
safeguards in 2011, along with the numerical thresholds for both moderately and highly irradiated
materials in the accompanying technical standard.

 10

The accompanying guidance from the 1990s states that the highly irradiated threshold is based on an
individual receiving a dose equal to a lethal dose (LD50) of 3.5 Gy (350 rad) in 3 hours, and the
moderately irradiated threshold was based on a 350 rad LD50 received over 24 hours. An LD50 is a
dose of radiation expected to cause death to 50 percent of an exposed population within 60 days [9].
A dose-rate criterion derived from days-scale lethality does not necessarily ensure adversary task
failure on the minutes-scale timelines relevant to theft or sabotage. Early symptoms of ARS can be
delayed, vary widely across individuals, and depend on exposure geometry. For these reasons, a
threshold motivated primarily by eventual lethality can still allow an adversary to remain functional
long enough to complete an attempted act. The remainder of this report evaluates this mismatch by
combining evidence on adversary task-time scales with evidence on the timing of acute radiation
effects relevant to short-term functional impairment.

3. ADVERSARY TIME-TASK BASIS
This section establishes a working time window for adversary actions relevant to self-protection.
Because this report evaluates whether radiation can cause task failure during an attempted theft or
sabotage, the focus here is on minutes-scale timelines once access is obtained. The intent is not to
develop a facility-specific adversary model or to provide a detailed sequence-of-events for theft or
sabotage. Instead, this section compiles published estimates and scenario-based analyses indicating
that certain theft or sabotage actions can be completed on the order of minutes to tens of minutes
once access is obtained. This time window is used later in the report to evaluate whether a self-
protection dose-rate criterion can plausibly induce incapacitating effects before an adversary
completes key tasks.

3.1. Theft Timeline Estimate
A clear statement of minutes-scale task times appears in the Sandia Proliferation Vulnerability Red
Team Report [4]. The report estimates that a successful overt theft could take only ten to twenty
minutes [4]. Although the Red Team report focuses on spent fuel as an illustrative example, the key
point for this report is the time scale. If an adversary can complete an attempt within roughly 10 to
20 minutes, then a radiation barrier that relies on symptoms appearing hours later is unlikely to
prevent task completion.
The Red Team report makes a practical point that health effects arise not from the dose rate at 1 m,
but how much dose the adversary accumulates while carrying out the task [4]. That depends on how
long they spend near the source and how close they need to get during key steps. The report also
treats dose to the midline of the body as a useful proxy for whole-body exposure during task
execution, which is consistent with the idea that systemic acute effects are driven more by torso dose
than by brief extremity exposure [5]. This framing supports the simple dose-over-time comparisons
used later in this report.
Theft and sabotage are distinct objectives. Theft requires removal of material from the protected
area, whereas sabotage requires causing damage or release and may not require removal. However,
both types of objectives can involve short sequences of tasks that must be completed before
effective interruption, and both are therefore relevant when deciding whether a self-protecting dose
rate should be tied to minutes-scale incapacitation.
Wadoud et al. present a physical protection evaluation approach using sabotage scenarios and a
single-path intrusion analysis model to compute the probability of interruption for a hypothetical
research reactor facility [5]. In their sabotage scenario, they report a total adversary mission time on
the order of 928.5 seconds (approximately 15 minutes and 30 seconds) [5]. This provides a scenario-
based example in which sabotage can be completed on a time scale comparable to the 10–20 minute
window stated in the Sandia Red Team report. For the purposes of this report, the result is used as
an order-of-magnitude indicator of how quickly certain sabotage actions could be executed under
assumed access conditions, not as a universal sabotage time applicable to all facilities.
More recent work also supports minutes-scale timelines. The Idaho National Laboratory report
“Physical Security Timeline Analysis in Support of Advanced Reactor Demonstration and
Deployment” applies dynamic modeling to theft and sabotage scenarios for a generic sodium fast
reactor facility using publicly available information and generic assumptions [6]. In the example
sabotage scenario summarized in Table 5 of that report, the final sabotage action (detonation step) is
associated with a mean delay time on the order of 1200 seconds (20 minutes), with additional
preceding actions required to reach and access the target [6]. The report also provides representative

 12

theft scenario timelines with multiple mission elements occurring on the order of minutes [6]. While
the INL report is aimed at demonstrating analysis methodology rather than establishing universally
valid timeline constants, it provides a modern, open, and methodologically transparent reference that
treats theft and sabotage mission completion times as minutes-scale quantities.
Taken together, the Sandia Red Team report, the Wadoud et al. sabotage scenario analysis, and the
INL timeline study support using a working adversary time window of approximately 10-to-30-
minutes once access is obtained [4, 5, 6]. These sources differ in objective, target type, and
assumptions, but they consistently treat theft or sabotage task completion as a minutes-scale
problem rather than an hours-scale one. This time window is used as a first-order basis for
evaluating whether a radiation field can realistically prevent task completion through prompt
incapacitation, rather than through delayed health effects. Representative task-time estimates
supporting this working window are summarized in Table 1. In the remainder of this report, the 10-
to-30-minute window is used to compare dose accumulation under current and candidate self-
protection dose-rate criteria to the timing of acute radiation effects relevant to short-term functional
impairment. Site-specific timelines and protective strategy details are outside the scope of this paper.

4. RADIATION HEALTH EFFECTS FOR RAPID INCAPACITATION
This section summarizes human clinical and epidemiological evidence relevant to rapid functional
impairment following acute radiation exposure. The purpose is to connect dose accumulated over
minutes to a practical proxy for loss of capability during a theft or sabotage attempt. The focus is on
early, short-term effects rather than delayed outcomes, because a self-protection dose-rate criterion
intended to prevent adversary success must act on the same minutes-scale time window established
in Section 3.

4.1. Incapacitation Proxy
Incapacitation can be defined in multiple ways, and the literature does not provide a single
operational endpoint for the ability to complete a specific task. For the first-order evaluation in this
report, incapacitation is therefore treated as the onset of acute prodromal effects that are likely to
prevent sustained, coordinated task performance. The primary proxy used is severe nausea and
vomiting (emesis), with particular emphasis on cases where vomiting is early, severe, or repeated.
Military Radiobiology emphasizes that emesis is one of the most important responses to irradiation that
can impact performance, and that its consequences can range from minimal to almost total
debilitation depending on circumstances [10]. For this reason, this report uses severe nausea and
vomiting as the primary practical proxy for loss of functional capability during a short-duration theft
or sabotage attempt.
This endpoint choice is intentionally pragmatic. Long-term endpoints such as hematopoietic injury,
infection, and eventual mortality are not decisive for whether an adversary can complete a task
within 10 to 30 minutes. In contrast, prodromal effects such as nausea, vomiting, profound fatigue,
and early prostration can appear on time scales that overlap with the operational window of interest.
For this reason, the remainder of this section emphasizes the timing and variability of prodromal
effects as a function of acute dose.

4.2. Prodromal Timing Versus Dose
Clinical descriptions of ARS include an initial prodromal phase, followed by a latent phase, and then
manifest illness [11]. The timing and severity of the prodromal phase depend strongly on dose, dose
rate, and individual radiosensitivity. Military Radiobiology notes that even after doses of several Gy,
exposed individuals may remain normal and asymptomatic for one to two hours after irradiation
[11]. The prodromal syndrome may then begin with increasing fatigue that is often described as
feeling “washed out” or “worn out,” with gastrointestinal symptoms developing depending on dose
and individual response [11].
As absorbed dose increases, the onset of severe prodromal symptoms becomes much faster. Multi-
gray exposures can be compatible with an hours-long delay before vomiting, while very high acute
doses can shift severe symptoms into the tens-of-minutes range and may be associated with early
prostration and minimal latent period [11]. Figure 4-1 provides a visual summary of the dose
dependence of early symptom timing used in this report.

These dose-dependent timing trends support a key point for self-protection criteria. If the dose
accumulated over a 10-to-30-minute attempt is only on the order of a few gray or less, severe
prodromal symptoms are unlikely to occur during the attempt, even if the exposure could contribute
to later illness. In contrast, symptom onset within minutes to tens of minutes generally corresponds
to acute doses in the multi-gray to tens-of-gray range, implying substantially higher dose rates over
the operational window.
Individual variability remains an important limitation. Military Radiobiology emphasizes that prodromal
onset and severity vary widely with radiosensitivity and that the largest differences are often seen in
the earliest reactions [11]. For example, within a Hiroshima dose group with severe ARS, only 32%
vomited during the prodromal phase [11]. For this reason, a self-protection criterion based on early
symptoms should be interpreted probabilistically. In addition, because real-world exposures are
often non-uniform, the incidence and timing of prodromal symptoms can differ substantially for
partial-body irradiation, which is addressed in the next section.

4.2.1. Combat Effectiveness
While emesis and prodromal symptoms are commonly used proxies for incapacitation, military
performance studies provide an additional look at how acute radiation exposure affects the ability to
perform physically demanding tasks. Figure 19-1 of Military Radiobiology and the supporting
information in Chapter 7 of the Textbook of Military Medicine present estimates on the duration for
which individuals remain “combat effective” after receiving an acute dose, based on a combination
of human and animal data [12, 13]. Figure 4-2 is adapted from Figure 19-1 of Military Radiobiology.
For physically demanding tasks, the threshold between combat effectiveness and ineffectiveness or
performance degraded is observed at doses of approximately 2000–7000 rad (20–70 Gy), with
individuals able to perform for up to an hour post-exposure before significant performance
degradation occurs. This threshold is particularly relevant when considering adversary actions that
must be completed within 10–30 minutes, as previously discussed.

4.3. Whole-body Versus Partial-body Irradiation
The preceding dose-to-symptom timing relationships are most directly applicable to acute whole-
body irradiation. In practical situations, exposures are often non-uniform. Military Radiobiology notes
that responses to whole-body irradiation can differ substantially from responses to partial-body
irradiation [11]. Based on radiation therapy experience, it reports that the abdominal region is
particularly sensitive for inducing prodromal reactions, with decreasing sensitivity for exposure
toward the head and toward the thighs [11]. This would be expected, because tissues are observed as
more prone to effects from radiation exposure if they are less well-differentiated (more specialized
like neurons or muscle cells), must reproduce due to metabolic processes, and divide more rapidly.
Cells of the intestinal lining meet these criteria [14]. It further reports that the same dose applied to
the abdomen can induce prodromal reactions in a larger fraction of patients than when the dose is
applied to the thorax or head and neck, and that exposure limited to extremities may produce little
to no prodromal incidence [11].
This has two implications for a first-order self-protection dose-rate criterion. First, a single dose-rate
value at 1 m is, at best, a screening metric, because it cannot capture the range of distances and body
orientations that occur during handling. Second, exposures dominated by extremity dose may not
produce the same prodromal response as exposures that irradiate the torso, even if local tissue injury
is severe. For the purposes of this report, the prodromal timing evidence is therefore interpreted as
most relevant to scenarios in which the adversary receives substantial torso dose during the attempt,
and it is used to motivate conservative discussion of variability and limitations rather than to claim a
universal incapacitation guarantee.

5. HUMAN ACCIDENT AND ORPHAN-SOURCE EVIDENCE
This section provides qualitative corroboration from real-world events that lethal-range exposures
do not necessarily produce immediate incapacitation. These cases help illustrate why a self-
protection criterion based primarily on eventual lethality can be misaligned with the minutes-to-tens-
of-minutes time window relevant to theft or sabotage attempts. The intent is not to derive a
quantitative dose-rate threshold from accident narratives. Instead, these events are used to show that
symptom onset and loss of functional capability are often delayed relative to the exposure and that
victims may remain ambulatory and capable of purposeful action for hours or days even after severe
exposures.
Accident reports are not controlled experiments. Dose reconstructions can be uncertain, exposures
are often non-uniform, and timelines are frequently reconstructed from interviews and medical
notes. Accordingly, the cases discussed here are used to illustrate qualitative timelines of symptom
onset and functional capability rather than to establish a precise dose-rate criterion.

5.1. Orphan-source events
Orphan-source events provide some of the clearest real-world examples of severe external photon
exposure from sources that were not under effective control. These events are relevant to self-
protection criteria because they often involve sources that produce dose rates at 1 m that meet or
exceed the current self-protection screening threshold, yet the exposed individuals may remain
capable of purposeful action for hours to days before becoming incapacitated. Table 3 summarizes
the key quantitative data and timelines for the cases discussed below.

The Tammiku, Estonia accident involved an orphan 137Cs source encountered during scrap metal
activities. Activity estimates for the source range from 150 GBq to 7.4 TBq, and an activity of 3.33
TBq (90 Ci) was ultimately used in retrospective dose estimates. In the accident documentation, the
dose rate is reported at a distance of 5 to 7 cm from the source, with values on the order of 1.5 to
1.8 Gy/h [16]. The geometry and available measurement equipment limited the characterization of
the source dose. Although the dose rate at 1 m cannot be inferred for this source, the case remains
relevant because the most exposed individual reportedly kept the source in his pocket for a period of
time, implying extended close proximity and a strong whole-body component in addition to severe
localized exposure [16]. Dose reconstruction indicates a dose on the order of several gray
(approximately 4 Gy is commonly cited), followed by acute radiation syndrome and death within
days to weeks [16]. The timeline narrative indicates that the individual was able to obtain the source
and return home before vomiting and other prodromal symptoms became incapacitating [16]. This
supports the qualitative conclusion that severe exposure and eventual fatality do not necessarily
translate to immediate task failure on minutes-scale timelines.

In the Samut Prakarn, Thailand accident, a 15.7 TBq (425 Ci) cobalt-60 teletherapy unit was
dismantled and the source was removed from shielding, leading to severe irradiation of several
individuals [17]. Although no unshielded dose rate measurement is available, a dose rate of ~10
Gy/h was measured while the source was shielded behind a pile of scrap metal. The accident report
indicates that at least three individuals received very high doses, commonly reported as exceeding 6
Gy, and multiple fatalities occurred following a period of progressive illness [17]. Reported symptom
onset and clinical progression occurred over subsequent days rather than as immediate collapse
during the initial handling period [17]. This timeline again reinforces that an unshielded gamma
source can be lethal while still allowing substantial time between exposure and incapacitating illness,
particularly when exposure is non-uniform, intermittent, and influenced by how the source is
approached and handled.

5.2. Criticality Accidents
Criticality accidents provide a contrasting class of events in which extremely high dose rates can
produce rapid incapacitation. The exposure time of criticality incidents is typically on the order of
seconds to minutes, which aligns with the minutes-scale tasks of interest in this report. However,
these events are not the primary basis for a general self-protection criterion because normal facility
operations are assumed during an adversary task. They are included here for contextual insight into
the regime where minutes-scale collapse can occur.

In the Tokaimura criticality accident, workers in a fuel processing operation inadvertently initiated a
critical excursion, producing an intense mixed neutron and gamma field [18]. Three workers were
exposed, with dose reconstructions indicating very high acute doses for the two most exposed
individuals (commonly reported on the order of 16 to 20 Gy for the most exposed worker and
roughly 6 to 10 Gy for the second), while the third worker received a substantially lower dose [18].
The accident documentation describes rapid onset of prodromal symptoms. At least two of the
workers developed vomiting soon after the event, and one experienced loss of consciousness within
minutes, indicating early functional collapse on a time scale far shorter than the orphan-source
events discussed in Section 5.1. Although medical care prolonged survival for weeks to months, the
two most exposed workers ultimately died from complications of the exposure [18].
The Wood River Junction criticality accident provides a similar example in the United States. An
unintended critical excursion occurred during processing operations, producing an intense mixed
neutron and gamma field [19]. One worker received a very high acute dose over a very short time
interval, with post-accident dose reconstruction commonly reported on the order of several gray
(approximately 8 Gy) [19]. The event is notable for rapid onset of prodromal symptoms and early
functional impairment, including vomiting and marked neurologic symptoms within minutes after
the excursion, followed by rapid clinical deterioration [19]. Despite medical care, the victim died
within a few days of the exposure [19].
As another example, a 1958 Los Alamos criticality accident occurred during plutonium processing
[20]. During processing in an organic treatment tank, stirring created a critical mass of plutonium,
which resulted in an excursion lasting 1 s [20]. The worker looking through a sight glass received 120
± 50% Gy [20]. Military Radiobiology notes that the worker exhibited transient behavioral
deterioration characterized by incoherence, ataxia, and general incapacitation shortly after irradiation,
which lasted on the order of hours before partial recovery [10]. The worker died 36 hours later.

Taken together, these criticality accidents are not representative of the photon-dominated source
scenarios that motivate most self-protection discussions in policy guidance. However, they do
demonstrate an important bounding point. When dose rates are sufficiently extreme, functional
incapacitation can occur on a minutes time scale. This supports treating minutes-scale incapacitation
as a plausible endpoint only in very high dose-rate regimes, and it motivates the report’s later focus
on what dose rates would be required to drive early symptoms within the 10 to 30 minute
operational window. The next section supplements this human evidence base with controlled animal
studies that provide additional insight into early incapacitation endpoints under high-dose-rate
conditions.

6. SUPPORTING EVIDENCE FROM CONTROLLED ANIMAL STUDIES
This section summarizes controlled animal studies on early functional impairment following acute
irradiation. The purpose is to provide supporting evidence where human time-to-incapacitation
datasets are limited, and to bound the dose regimes in which rapid incapacitation has been observed
under controlled conditions. This section does not attempt to build a new predictive model. Instead,
it compiles the key endpoints reported in the animal literature and relates them to the operational
incapacitation proxy used in this report. In animal experiments, operation incapacitation is often
referred to as early transient incapacitation (ETI), which is discussed further in Section 6.1.
Controlled animal studies offer two advantages that accident narratives and most clinical datasets do
not. First, they can use known and repeatable radiation fields, which improves the interpretability of
dose and dose-rate relationships. Second, they can assess performance endpoints that are difficult to
quantify in human cases, such as standardized task performance.
At the same time, animal-to-human extrapolation has limitations. Species differ in radiosensitivity,
symptom presentation, and behavioral response. Experimental tasks may not reflect real-world
human behavior, and experimental conditions may not capture factors such as stress, motivation, or
purposeful risk-taking. Accordingly, this report uses animal studies as supporting evidence and
bounding information rather than as the primary basis for setting a policy threshold. This approach
is consistent with the discussion in Military Radiobiology Chapter 16, which notes that while
extrapolation from animals to humans is difficult, such studies provide a useful first approximation
and can inform the initial formulation of casualty criteria [10].

6.1. Performance Decrement at High Dose in Controlled Primate Studies
As previously stated, controlled animal studies, particularly in nonhuman primates, have been used
to quantify rapid performance loss after acute radiation exposure under conditions where dose and
task performance can be measured directly. These studies are useful here because they provide
experimentally defined functional endpoints that are difficult to obtain from human clinical data.
Military Radiobiology also emphasizes that emesis is one of the most important responses to irradiation
that can affect performance and, depending on circumstances, can range from minimal to nearly
total debilitation [10]. In the primate literature summarized in that chapter, the ED50 (dose
producing emesis in 50% of subjects) is reported as about 500 rad in rhesus monkeys, compared to
about 180 rad in humans [10]. This difference highlights an important limitation of animal-to-human
extrapolation, but it also supports the use of controlled primate studies as one line of evidence for
bounding early performance effects when human data are sparse.
In addition to emesis, controlled primate studies have evaluated early performance degradation at
very high acute doses using trained task paradigms. These studies are useful because they define loss
of function in a repeatable way, even though the specific tasks are not intended to replicate real-
world adversary actions. Military Radiobiology summarizes that irradiation with supralethal doses can
produce marked but sometimes transient behavioral decrements in rhesus monkeys, and that the
dose required to induce these decrements depends on radiation quality and on the physical and
cognitive demands of the task [10].
A commonly used experimental endpoint in this literature is early transient incapacitation (ETI),
defined as a complete cessation of trained task performance for at least 1 minute [10]. Military
Radiobiology reports ETI results for monkeys performing a visual discrimination task, where ETI can
develop within minutes after supralethal whole-body irradiation and persist on the order of tens of
minutes before partial recovery. For one set of mixed neutron-gamma conditions (neutron/gamma

 22

ratio of 0.4), it reports an ETI ED50 of about 16 Gy, with few monkeys exhibiting ETI below about
1000 rad under those conditions [10]. In a separate physical activity task, it reports an ETI ED50 on
the order of 2000 rad in a mixed spectrum field with a higher reported neutron/gamma ratio
(approximately 3.0), and notes that multiple ETI episodes and time lost can occur depending on the
task [10].
The 1958 Los Alamos criticality accident is a rare documented human accident where transient
behavioral decrements resembling ETI have been observed [10]. While such cases are rare and often
have uncertainties, they provide qualitative human corroboration that rapid neurobehavioral effects
can occur in the very-high-dose regime. This pattern suggests that, under some conditions, high-
dose irradiation does not cause an immediate all-or-nothing failure, but instead degrades
performance through intermittent incapacitation and increasing time lost. In operational terms, this
is consistent with the possibility that an adversary could continue some physical actions after
receiving a significant dose, but with degraded capability and reduced effective work rate [10].
In the context of this report, the implication is that an emesis-based proxy is appropriate for a first-
order evaluation of near-term loss of capability at moderate to high doses, while ETI serves
primarily as a bounding endpoint for extreme dose-rate conditions where rapid neurobehavioral
collapse is possible [10]. This section therefore supports the approach taken in Sections 4 and 5, to
use human prodromal timing and incident narratives as the primary basis for evaluating whether
current dose-rate criteria can plausibly cause task failure during a 10 to 30 minute attempt.
Controlled animal studies serve as secondary sources to bound the high-dose regime and highlight
task degradation in a controlled setting. Section 7 integrates these lines of evidence with the
adversary time window to evaluate the adequacy of the current self-protection threshold and to
identify candidate updates and limitations.

7. INTEGRATING EVIDENCE INTO FIRST ORDER SELF-PROTECTION
 CRITERION
This section integrates the expected 10 to 30 minute adversary time window (Section 3) with the
timing of acute radiation effects (Sections 4 through 6) to evaluate whether the current self-
protection dose-rate convention is likely to cause task failure during an attempted theft or sabotage.
The intent is to provide a transparent, first-order assessment. Detailed scenario modeling, shielding
analysis, and site-specific path analysis are outside the scope.

7.1. First Order Dose Accumulation
For a first-order evaluation, the relevant quantity is the dose accumulated over the duration of the
attempted act. The dose received by an adversary is the time integral of the dose rate during task
execution. If the dose rate is treated as approximately constant over a short interval, the accumulated
dose can be approximated as:

Under the current self-protection convention of approximately 1 Gy/h at 1 m, the dose
accumulated over the time scales of interest is small. For example, 20 minutes at 1 Gy/h
corresponds to about 0.33 Gy, and 30 minutes corresponds to about 0.5 Gy. These doses are well
below the multi-gray range associated with severe nausea and vomiting on a tens-of-minutes time
scale (Section 4 and Table 2), and Military Radiobiology notes that even after doses of several Gy,

exposed individuals may remain asymptomatic for 1 to 2 hours [11]. The military view on combat
effectiveness further reinforces this point by demonstrating that the onset of acute symptoms
associated with radiation sickness is estimated to occur in the multi-gray range and that at lower
doses accumulated within the operationally relevant window, affected individuals are likely to remain
able to complete physically demanding tasks. This supports the conclusion that the current threshold
is unlikely to produce incapacitating symptoms within a 10-to-30-minute window.
If the intent of self-protection is to cause task failure during the attempt, the threshold must be tied
to dose accumulation over 10-to-30 minutes rather than hours. Figure 7-1 shows that dose rates on
the order of 25 Gy/h to 100 Gy/h would accumulate 10 Gy in less than 30 minutes, which is more
consistent with producing severe prodromal effects before key tasks could be completed. This does
not imply a single “correct” number, but it illustrates the order-of-magnitude gap between the
current 1 Gy/h convention and a minutes-scale incapacitation objective.

7.2. Applicability and Limitations
This report provides a first-order screening evaluation intended to inform policy discussions. It is
not a substitute for facility-specific security analysis, and several limitations should be stated
explicitly. Real-world exposures during handling are typically non-uniform. Dose rate at 1 m does
not capture changes in distance or shielding during key tasks, and it does not represent the
distribution of dose between the torso and the extremities. The prodromal timing evidence
summarized in Section 4 is most directly applicable when the adversary receives substantial torso
dose during the attempt. Scenarios dominated by extremity exposure may produce severe local
injury without producing the same systemic prodromal response on the same time scale (Section
4.3).
Prodromal onset and severity vary widely across individuals, due to the wide range of radiosensitivity
observed in humans [11]. The earliest reactions can be especially variable. A motivated adversary
may be able to continue purposeful action through nausea for some period. Accordingly, an emesis-
based incapacitation proxy should be interpreted probabilistically, not as a guarantee of immediate
task failure for all individuals under all exposure conditions.
This report focuses primarily on photon-dominated external fields so that dose-rate thresholds
expressed in rem, rad, and Gy can be compared on a consistent first-order basis. Mixed neutron and
gamma fields, including criticality events, are more complex and require more sophisticated analysis
than presented here. The dose-rate threshold analysis in this report is meant as a general screening
criterion because biological effectiveness and the relationship between absorbed dose and health
effects depend on radiation type and energy. Criticality accidents are discussed only for contextual
insight into very high dose-rate regimes.
Controlled animal performance studies provide useful bounding information, but reported
thresholds for performance decrement depend on the task being performed and the experimental
conditions. Extrapolation from animal studies to human operational capability is inherently
uncertain (Section 6) [10]. For this reason, animal data are used in this report to support qualitative
conclusions about the high-dose regime rather than to define a precise policy threshold.
A self-protection dose-rate criterion is a screening construct. It does not replace physical protection
system design, protective force response, detection and delay measures, or other safeguards and
security requirements. A revised self-protection criterion, if adopted, should be treated as one
element within a broader graded safeguards and security framework. The next section summarizes
the main findings, states the recommended path forward for updating self-protection criteria in a

regulator-friendly way, and identifies specific areas where additional work would be most valuable if
a revised threshold or a two-tier definition is pursued.

8. CONCLUSIONS
This report evaluated the technical basis and practical adequacy of the self-protection dose-rate
convention commonly expressed as approximately 1 Gy/h (100 rem/h) at 1 m. The review indicates
that the origin of this criterion is largely lethality-based and hours-scale, rather than tied to rapid
incapacitation during an attempted sabotage or theft act. In particular, the commonly cited 100
rem/h threshold traces to an assumption that a nominally lethal whole-body dose on the order of
350 rem would be delivered over roughly three hours and was then rounded to a convenient
screening value. A lethality-based threshold does not, by itself, establish that an adversary would
experience task failure within the 10-to-30-minute window relevant to theft or sabotage.
Published security timeline references support that consequential theft or sabotage actions may be
completed on a minutes-to-tens-of-minutes time scale once access is obtained. Human radiobiology
evidence further indicates that the doses accumulated under the current 1 Gy/h convention over a
10-to-30-minute interval are too small to reliably produce incapacitating symptoms during the
attempt. Military Radiobiology notes that even after doses of several Gy, individuals may remain
asymptomatic for one to two hours, and it describes dose regimes in which severe nausea and
vomiting commonly occur on hours time scales rather than minutes [11]. Accident narratives
involving orphan gamma sources provide qualitative corroboration that severe, and even fatal,
exposures do not necessarily produce immediate incapacitation. Taken together, these lines of
evidence support the conclusion that the current self-protection dose-rate convention is unlikely to
ensure adversary task failure within an operationally relevant time window.
For implementation, this report recommends retaining a single dose-rate-at-1 m screening threshold
but increasing it to align with a minutes-scale incapacitation objective. Using the first-order dose
accumulation relationship developed in this report, a dose rate of approximately 25 Gy/h would
accumulate 10 Gy in about 24 minutes, which is consistent with entering a regime where severe
prodromal effects and emesis may occur on a tens-of-minutes time scale. A higher threshold of
approximately 100 Gy/h would accumulate 10 Gy in about 6 minutes and therefore provides a
criterion for rapid incapacitation within the 10-to-30-minute window. On this basis, 25 Gy/h is
identified as a plausible lower-bound screening value for incapacitation within 30 minutes, while 100
Gy/h represents a more conservative screening criterion that provides additional margin for
variability in symptom timing, individual sensitivity, and exposure geometry. The 100 Gy/h
threshold aligns with recent recommendations made for a rewrite of DOE-STD-1194, which
provides guidance on consideration of radiation as a mitigating factor for material attractiveness.
The study recommended that a dose of 100 Gy/h be considered incapacitating and cautioned that
the effects of radiological decay, and the adversary’s ability to minimize time, utilize shielding, and
maximize distance should be considered in any formal dose assessment [21].
Additional work would strengthen both the technical defensibility and implementation of an
updated criterion. A limited study that quantifies how simple, practical shielding changes the dose to
an adversary during a short handling period would help connect a dose-rate threshold to real-world
feasibility and would strengthen the technical basis for any updated criterion. Finally, a focused
source-type benchmarking effort would improve clarity on applicability by compiling representative
unshielded dose rates at 1 m for major material forms and devices, including high-activity sealed
sources and irradiated materials, and by documenting typical configurations of those sources.
Together, these efforts would provide a stronger basis for adopting and applying a revised self-
protection criterion as a screening construct within a broader safeguards and security framework.
