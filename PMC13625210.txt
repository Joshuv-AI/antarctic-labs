Optimizing multi‐trait selection for profitability in Illinois wheat using a factor analytic mixed model and an economic selection index

Lucas Berger Munaro, Daniel J Tolhurst, Jessica E Rutkoski

Abstract

Economic selection indices can improve the efficiency of selection for multiple traits, but they are underutilized in plant breeding due to limited research on the economic values of traits and a lack of well‐documented case examples employing economic indices. This study evaluated a genomic‐based Smith–Hazel index incorporating empirically derived economic weights for grain yield, test weight, and maturity in soft red winter wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) in Illinois. Economic weights used in the index were estimated using a function describing expected profit considering the predominant cropping system and grain quality pricing scheme of the region. Data from 2995 genotypes, five traits, and 12 environments (2022–2024) were analyzed together with genome‐wide marker data using a factor analytic (factor analytic model with k factors [Fak]) model fitted to the environment‐by‐trait combinations to obtain predictions of breeding value. The optimal multi‐trait multi‐environment (MTME) model (FA5) explained over 91% of the additive genetic variance and achieved high prediction accuracy (≥0.95). Compared with a single‐trait multi‐environment (STME) model, use of an MTME model for index selection increased predicted economic return by 3.4% ($120 ha−1 vs. $116 ha−1 for STME). Furthermore, index‐based selection under MTME yielded a predicted net merit gain of $120 ha−1, compared with $97.5 ha−1 when selecting on grain yield alone, demonstrating the economic value of multi‐trait selection. Covariance estimates from the MTME model indicated that the genetic correlations between traits depended upon the environment, likely due to environment‐specific stressors. Overall, we determined that an optimal economic selection index can be constructed for a regionally focused plant breeding program using an FAk model and economic weights.
The use of economic selection indices can improve the effectiveness of multi‐trait selection for the purpose of improving profitability; however, these indices are underutilized in plant breeding programs. This study determined the economic weights for three key traits in soft red winter wheat in Illinois and used these weights with predicted breeding values to compute an economic selection index. Predicted breeding values suitable for the index were obtained using University of Illinois wheat breeding program data. Two statistical models were compared for their impact on gain from index selection. Response to index and single‐trait selection was also compared. Overall, we found that selection based on the index and modeling traits as genetically correlated improved expected response in profitability.

INTRODUCTION

The primary goal of an applied plant breeding program is to develop and release improved commercial varieties or hybrids. This objective is typically achieved through two processes: population improvement and product development (Gaynor et al., ). Population improvement focuses on increasing the population mean breeding value for traits of importance, while product development focuses on identifying and advancing superior breeding materials for cultivar release. Population improvement is achieved by conducting multiple cycles of breeding in which the breeding materials are evaluated, and individuals identified to be the best parents are intermated to generate new breeding materials that themselves become candidates for parent selection or commercial release. Successful population improvement is necessary to ensure that the product development pipeline remains competitive. Genotype‐by‐environment interaction (GEI) and the need to improve multiple traits are two factors that can limit a breeding program's success if not carefully addressed.

Differential responses of genotypes to changes in their environment result in GEI. Breeders often conduct multi‐environment trials (METs) and fit multivariate linear mixed models to evaluate breeding candidates’ performance across the target population of environments (TPE). Single‐trait multi‐environment (STME) models capture GEI for each trait analyzed separately; however, because traits are modeled independently, they cannot exploit or account for genetic covariances between traits. Integrating multi‐trait multi‐environment (MTME) data further increases model complexity, especially when traits are not uniformly phenotyped across environments, a common occurrence due to cost constraints. As a result, many breeding programs simplify selection models by omitting one or more of these key aspects. Among the various statistical approaches for the analysis of MET data (Bančič et al., ; Montesinos‐López et al., , ; A. B. Smith & Cullis, ; A. Smith et al., ; A. B. Smith et al., ), the factor analytic (FA) linear mixed model is especially attractive due to its parsimony, flexibility, interpretability, and ability to efficiently capture genetic covariance heterogeneity between environments (A. Smith et al., ; A. B. Smith et al., ). While Bayesian hierarchical MTME models offer greater flexibility (Montesinos‐López et al., , ), they are computationally intensive and scale poorly with large datasets (Angelino et al., ; Wu et al., ; Zhou et al., ). In contrast, FA models strike a favorable balance between model complexity and computational feasibility. Recent advancements have also extended breeders’ selection tools for the FA linear mixed model, providing informative measures of overall performance and stability across traits and environments (Bančič et al., ).

Selection methods in breeding programs must not only address GEI but also address the need to improve the population for multiple traits. This can be especially challenging when traits are genetically and unfavorably correlated because selection for one trait will lead to a correlated response to selection in another trait due to pleiotropy or linkage disequilibrium (Falconer & Mackay, ). Traditionally, procedures such as tandem selection and independent culling levels have been used for multi‐trait selection, but these methods are suboptimal for parent selection because they do not account for genetic correlations between traits and because extra merit in one trait will not compensate for a lack of merit in another. For optimal multi‐trait selection, the Smith–Hazel index (SHI) (Hazel, ; H. F. Smith, ) is available. In its original form, the SHI linearly combines phenotypic data on multiple traits using optimal weights computed using economic weights along with genetic and phenotypic covariances to maximize gain in total net merit. Net merit denotes the aggregate economic breeding value of a genotype, which is its expected overall economic return across all traits given the defined economic weights. Henderson () showed that predictions of breeding value using all phenotypic information in best linear unbiased prediction can be directly weighted by economic value (EV) to compute a SHI (Hazel et al., ). Subsequent extensions to genomic selection have adopted a similar approach by considering genomic predictions of breeding value (e.g., Ceron‐Rojas et al., ; Dekkers, ). For a given trait, the economic weight quantifies how much a one‐unit change in trait value impacts the total profit earned by the producer in monetary units per acre, holding all other traits constant. Economic weights are specific to a breeding program's TPE and market segments. To estimate economic weights for multiple traits, profit functions (Moav & Moav, ) and other methodological approaches can be used, as reviewed by Nielsen et al. (). It is important to emphasize that linear selection indices are optimized to maximize gain in the next generation; thus, these methods are directly applicable to parent selection within breeding programs, whereas selection for product development requires different methods.

In animal breeding, economic indices have long been used to achieve genetic gain across multiple traits. Successful applications have been reported in cattle (Bos taurus) with weight traits (Fernandes et al., ) and the interval between first and second calving (Amaya et al., ). Examples in pigs (Sus scrofa) include meat quality traits with intermediate optima (Hovenier et al., ) as well as reproductive, growth, and carcass traits (Dube et al., ). In contrast, most crop breeding programs rely on heuristic methods for multi‐trait selection, or they may employ weights assigned based on the perceived importance of each trait due to a lack of research on the EVs of traits of interest in the regions of interest. Unlike in animal breeding, the economic importance of traits in crop breeding is expected to vary widely across and even within regions due to the diversity of crop production systems, markets, and environmental conditions found within a breeding program's TPE. When explicit EVs are unavailable, or unreliable, the desired gains index provides a practical alternative by solving for index coefficients that deliver user‐specified proportional responses across traits (Pešek & Baker, ). Recent work formalizes its connection to the Smith–Hazel solution (Werner et al., ) and demonstrates balanced responses under price uncertainty in animal breeding (Sánchez et al., ), with maize (Zea mays L.) studies similarly moving toward indices that reduce reliance on ill‐defined weights (Olivoto et al., ). Although the desired gains index provides a good solution for multi‐trait selection, in some cases, economic weights can be readily obtained. In these instances, determining and utilizing empirically derived economic weights would guide selection decisions toward farmer and industry priorities.

Soft red winter wheat (SRWW; Triticum aestivum L.) is an important cereal crop in the central US Midwest, including Illinois, where it is commonly grown as part of a wheat–soybean (Glycine max L.) double‐crop system. In this system, wheat is planted from late September to early October, harvested in late June to early July, and soybean is planted immediately afterward. Grain yield (GY) of both wheat and soybean is a primary determinant of crop revenue because it directly influences gross returns per unit area. Timely wheat maturity (MAT) is critical because delayed soybean planting shortens the soybean growing season, reduces vegetative and reproductive development, and ultimately decreases yield potential (Hu & Wiatrak, ). Several studies in the US Midwest have reported soybean yield penalties when planting is delayed beyond the optimal window (Bastidas et al., ; De Bruin & Pedersen, ; Egli & Cornelius, ; Loman et al., ; Pedersen & Lauer, ; Rowntree et al., ). In wheat breeding programs, heading time (HD) is often used as a proxy for maturity date because the two traits are highly correlated and because HD can be evaluated more quickly and accurately.

In addition to GY and MAT, test weight (TW) is a key quality trait in wheat breeding. TW is defined as the weight of grain that fills a standardized volume and serves as a rapid, indirect measure of grain bulk density and soundness. High TW values are generally associated with plump kernels, superior milling properties, and higher flour extraction rates. Low TW can result from Fusarium head blight (FHB) caused by Fusarium graminearum, poor grain development, or pre‐harvest sprouting. FHB infection can also lead to the accumulation of deoxynivalenol (DON), a mycotoxin that poses food safety risks and triggers additional price discounts or load rejection at grain elevators when concentrations exceed market thresholds. In the US grain marketing system, TW is a federally recognized grading factor, and most grain elevators enforce discount schedules when TW falls below a threshold (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Agricultural Marketing Service [USDA‐AMS], ). A “discount” refers to a price deduction applied per unit of grain sold when quality specifications are not met. These deductions are proportional to the deviation from the standard threshold, with steeper penalties for more severe deficiencies. In some cases, loads with extremely low TW may be rejected outright due to processing inefficiency or poor end‐use quality. For producers, discounts can represent substantial economic losses, as they directly reduce the final sale price received for the crop. Consequently, maintaining adequate TW is a critical breeding objective, both to meet federal grade requirements and to protect farmer profitability.

Munaro et al. () evaluated 21 years of SRWW breeding progress at the University of Illinois and found that genetic trends for GY, TW, and HD were favorable, but due to the influence of the environment, genetic trends in GY were not enough to generate favorable phenotypic trends. Greater rates of genetic gain would be needed to ensure that the breeding program has a visible impact. Given the complexity of multi‐trait selection in this environment, a genomic‐based SHI that incorporates a single‐step MTME model with empirically derived economic weights has the potential to increase rates of genetic gain and enhance breeding efficiency. With this goal in mind, the objectives of this research are to (i) implement a single‐step MTME model and SHI to rank genotypes for parent selection based on total net merit; (ii) confirm the superiority of this approach compared to the standard STME analysis; (iii) assess the accuracy of MTME genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs); and (iv) explore genetic correlations among traits and environments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Phenotypic data and experimental design

The dataset comprises 38,792 phenotypic observations collected from the SRWW yield trials at the University of Illinois wheat breeding program over 3 years (2022–2024), across four Illinois locations: Addieville (Adv), Neoga (Neo), St. Peter (Stp), and Urbana (Urb) (Figure ). This results in a total of 12 different environments, each defined by a unique year–location combination.

Each trial employed a partially replicated design, ensuring at least 20% replication, with replicated genotypes being tested twice, in a rectangular row‐column arrangement (Table ). A sparse testing strategy was used across environments, where genotypes from all stages of selection were randomized together to avoid genotype and environment confounding (Ackerman & Rutkoski, ). Genotypes in the early stages of selection were evaluated in fewer locations and with fewer replications due to the large number of individuals and the limited seed availability at this stage. In contrast, more advanced genotypes were evaluated in more environments and served as replicates, in addition to commercial checks. Each year, approximately 30% of lines were selected for continued evaluation in subsequent trials. The number of genotypes assessed per environment varied from 486 to 1337 (Table ), while the number of shared genotypes between environments ranged from 35 to 855 (Figure ).

Five traits were measured: GY, TW, HD, plant height (HT), and maturity (MAT). GY and TW were measured on whole plots using a HarvestMaster H2 weigh system (Juniper Systems). GY was then standardized to 13.5% moisture. The HD of each plot was determined by assessing each plot on alternating days and recording the Julian date when at least 50% of the wheat heads had emerged. HT was evaluated as an overall average plant height across entire plots using a measuring stick. Likewise, for MAT, plots were assessed on alternating dates to determine when at least 50% of the peduncles and wheat heads had lost all green color. GY and TW were recorded across all environments. HD, HT, and MAT were assessed in six, seven, and two environments, respectively (Table ). The phenotypes for each trait were then scaled to unit variance for model fitting, with model parameters transformed back to their original scale after estimation.

Marker data

For each genotype, desiccated leaf‐tissue samples were submitted to the USDA‐ARS Eastern Regional Small Grains Genotyping Lab for genotyping via genotyping‐by‐sequencing (Elshire et al., ) using the protocol reported by Poland et al. (). The resulting marker data included marker information for 2980 out of the 2995 genotypes, coded as 0, 1, or 2 based on a set of 9588 SNPs with less than 50% total missing data. Missing marker data were imputed with Beagle 5.4 using default parameters (Browning et al., ).

Markers were filtered using the ASRgenomics package in R (Gezan et al., ), applying a minor allele frequency threshold >5%, which retained 9210 markers for analysis. To estimate genetic relationships among the genotypes, the genomic relationship matrix, G, was constructed using the first VanRaden () method. Three potential duplicate genotypes with pairwise kinship values greater than two were identified and subsequently removed to avoid singularities during model fitting. To ensure positive definiteness and prevent computational issues, the G matrix was adjusted using the G.tuneup function. This “bending” process modifies the matrix's eigenvalues, improving its numerical stability. Following this adjustment, the G matrix was inverted to obtain the inverse matrix, G−1, using the G.inverse function.

Statistical models

The multi‐trait MET dataset comprised nt = 5 traits and ne = 12 environments with nte=39 observed trait–environment combinations and ng = 2977 genotypes with nm = 9210 markers for n = 38,792 plots in total. The traits were recorded in at least two environments, with yield and TW recorded in all 12 environments. The environments contained field trials laid out as a rectangular array comprising cj columns and rj rows, with nj=cjrj plots in total. A partially replicated design was used for each trial following Cullis et al. (), with replication ranging from 20% to 41% (Table ). MTME versus STME

The multi‐trait MET dataset was analyzed using two linear mixed models. First, the MTME model, implemented as described in Section , incorporates all traits simultaneously. Then, the STME model was applied, where each trait was analyzed independently using the same model structure but assuming independence across traits. Under the STME model, the SHI was computed by applying the same vector of economic weights to GBLUPs obtained from the independent single‐trait analyses. That is, GBLUPs for GY, TW, and HD were extracted from their respective STME models and combined post‐hoc using the economic weights to compute a SHI for each genotype. Therefore, the genetic variance matrix for STME model is given by var(u)=Int⊗Ge⊗G, where Int is a nt×nt diagonal matrix between traits, Ge is a ne×ne variance matrix between environments, and G is as previously defined.

Response to selection

To evaluate the impact of different models and selection criteria, where selection criteria refer to selecting the top genotypes based on their average SHI or GY across all environments, we compared the MTME‐predicted values for SHI and other traits of the selected individuals (top 10%) to those of the full candidate population. Additionally, we identified the top 10% of individuals using the STME model and calculated the expected response to selection relative to MTME. The response to selection was quantified as the difference between the mean performance of the top 10% selected genotypes and the mean performance of all selection candidates, excluding genotypes discarded prior to 2024.

Software, code, and data availability

All data manipulation and analysis were conducted in the R Environment version 4.4.2 (R Core Team, ) using the R packages Tidyverse (Wickham et al., ) for data manipulation, ASRgenomics to create the genomic relationship matrix (Gezan et al., ), and ASReml‐R (Butler et al., ) for data analysis. The R scripts used to perform the analyses are available in the GitHub repository mtme‐gsi4wheat (https://github.com/lbmunaro/mtme‐gsi4wheat).

RESULTS

Model selection and accuracy

A series of factor analytic model with k factors (Fak) models (k=1,...,9) were compared to determine the optimal balance between model complexity, proportion of additive genetic variance explained, and computational cost (Table ; Figure ). Across all traits, the proportion of additive genetic variance explained (V ̄a
%) increased from k = 1 to k = 9, with diminishing returns for k > 5 (Figure ). GY exhibited the largest gain in mean Va
%, rising from 18% (FA1) to 87% (FA5) and plateauing thereafter. HD was well captured even at low rank, with >96% of Va explained at k ≥ 1. HT and TW required moderate factor complexity (k ≥ 3 and k ≥ 2, respectively) to exceed 90%Va, whereas MAT remained moderately explained (76%–80% Va) across all k. Model fit statistics further supported k = 5 as a pragmatic choice while ensuring all trait–environment combinations had at least 60% explained for yield (Table ). Log‐likelihood values increased, and Akaike information criterion values decreased with increasing k, but the rate of improvement diminished, and computational time increased substantially beyond FA5. Therefore, FA5, capturing >91% of additive variance on average while maintaining manageable runtime (∼53 h), was selected for all subsequent analyses.

The model‐based accuracy of the total GET effects remained uniformly high across all five traits (Figure ). Across 116,103 GET effects, the mean accuracy was 0.95 for GY, 0.97 for TW, 0.98 for HD, 0.97 for HT, and 0.96 for MAT. HD exhibited the highest average accuracy and the narrowest spread, whereas GY showed the lowest mean accuracy with a slightly wider spread.

Correlation among GEBVs from STME and MTME

We assessed selection outcomes based on GEBVs and the SHI predicted using MTME and STME models. Strong linear relationships were observed between MTME and STME predictions for all traits (Figure ), with Pearson correlation coefficients (r) ranging from 0.86 to 0.97. Traits such as HD and HT exhibited the highest correlations (r = 0.97), indicating nearly identical predictions from both models. Lower correlations were found for maturity (MAT; r = 0.86) and GY (r = 0.89), suggesting greater differences between MTME and STME model predictions for these traits, likely reflecting enhanced modeling of GEI and trait correlations in the MTME framework.

Expected response to selection

Selecting the top 10% of genotypes based on either SHI or GY resulted in distinct shifts in the distributions of GEBVs for all evaluated traits (Figure ). For both selection criteria, the mean SHI and GY of the selected groups exceeded those of the full candidate population. GY‐based selection produced the largest mean increase in GY, whereas SHI‐based selection generated higher gains in TW, with no observable changes in MAT or HD.

Under the MTME model, SHI‐based selection achieved a predicted economic gain of $120 ha−1, compared with $116 ha−1 for STME‐based SHI selection (Table ). For GY selection, MTME yielded a gain of 409 kg ha−1, exceeding the 390 kg ha−1 gain from STME. TW increased by 19.3 g L−1 with index selection under MTME and by 18.7 g L−1 under STME. In contrast, GY‐based selection resulted in smaller increases in TW: 5.7 g L−1 for MTME and 6.0 g L−1 for STME. For HD, SHI selection slightly reduced mean values under both MTME (−0.1 day) and STME (−0.3 day), whereas GY selection increased HD by 0.4 days (MTME) and 0.2 days (STME). HT increased by 0.4 cm for MTME and 0.2 cm for STME when selection was based on SHI, while GY‐based selection decreased HT by 1.0 cm for both models. For MAT, the mean changes resulting from index selection were −0.4 days under MTME and −0.6 days under STME, whereas GY selection produced negligible changes (0 days for MTME and −0.3 days for STME).

Genetic correlation among trait–environment combinations

Genetic correlations among trait–environment combinations, estimated from the MTME FA5 model, varied substantially, indicating the presence of both trait‐dependent GEI and environment‐dependent genetic covariances between traits (Figure ). Within a single trait, genetic correlations across environments were consistently high for HD, MAT, and HT (Figure ). TW tended to be highly correlated with itself except for a few environment combinations. In contrast, correlations between GY across environments varied widely, reflecting a stronger GEI effect. While most cross‐environment correlations for GY were positive, exceptions included negative correlations between GY‐24‐Urb and several environments, such as GY‐22‐Stp, GY‐22‐Urb, GY‐23‐Adv, GY‐23‐Neo, GY‐23‐Stp, and GY‐23‐Urb.

Across traits, correlation values varied considerably, with some trait pairs exhibiting strong positive correlations in specific environments and weak or even negative correlations in others. For instance, HD and MAT were consistently strongly correlated across all environments, while HT showed moderate positive correlations with both HD and TW. In contrast, correlations between GY and other traits were less consistent. For example, GY‐23‐Urb was positively correlated with HD in all environments, whereas GY‐23‐Adv, GY‐24‐Neo, and GY‐24‐Urb showed negative correlations with HD. Similarly, HT generally showed weak or negative correlations with GY, except for GY‐22‐Adv, where the correlation was moderately positive.

Mean within‐trait genetic correlations across environments were moderate for GY (r = 0.54), and high for other traits, with correlations ranging from 0.79 (TW) to 0.97 (HD) (Figure ). Strong positive correlations were observed between HD and MAT (r = 0.84), with moderate associations between HT and HD (r = 0.20) and between HT and TW (r = 0.25). GY had relatively weak correlations with all other traits, ranging from −0.08 (HT) to 0.15 (MAT) (Figure ).

Within GY across locations, average genetic correlations indicated moderate to strong within‐location consistency, with self‐to‐self estimates ranging from r = 0.44 (Urb) to r = 0.76 (Neo) (Figure ). Cross‐location correlations ranged from r = 0.34 (Adv vs. Urb) to r = 0.62 (Adv vs. Neo and Neo vs. Stp). Neo consistently showed higher genetic correlations both within and across locations, while Urb exhibited weaker correlations.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates the application of a genomic‐based SHI with MTME data and empirically derived economic weights to enhance genetic gain in net merit in a wheat breeding program. The methods we applied address GEI and incorporate EVs of traits to ensure parent selection decisions will improve the total net merit of the breeding population. While this study applies the MTME approach for SRWW breeding in the central US Midwest, it can be applied in other crops and geographies to facilitate population improvement.

The optimal model identified, FA5, provided an efficient compromise between complexity, predictive power, and computational practicality, explaining over 91% of additive genetic variance. This efficiency is critical for practical breeding applications where computational resources and turnaround times can constrain breeding decisions (Bančič et al., ; A. B. Smith & Cullis, ). Moreover, consistently high accuracies of predicted GET effects across traits validated the robustness and reliability of this modeling approach. The number of common factors required (k = 5) to explain most of the additive genetic variance indicates that the genetic covariance structure between trait‐environments is potentially complex (Meyer, ).

Predictions from STME and MTME models were highly correlated, indicating strong agreement overall. However, this level of agreement can diminish under stronger GEI or when traits are more highly correlated, conditions under which MTME more efficiently borrows information across traits to improve prediction accuracy (Jia & Jannink, ). Direct comparisons of the expected response to selection for the top 10% of genotypes relative to the full candidate population indicated that the MTME‐derived index would lead to higher economic returns than the STME‐derived index if implemented. However, these economic differences were modest, and in situations where computational time is constrained, STME may provide a suitable compromise.

A distinct advantage of MTME models is their capacity to estimate genetic correlations and thus borrow information among trait‐environment combinations, unlike the STME. This is especially beneficial when a trait of low heritability is genetically correlated with another trait of higher heritability (Shahi et al., ). Another advantage is the ability to predict performance for all environment‐trait combinations for genotyped lines, even when phenotypic data are missing. This is important when different environments are to be weighed differently. For example, trial sites that are less representative of the TPE can be assigned lower weights, while those that more closely match target conditions can be emphasized in the selection index.

In our case, genetic correlations among economically important traits were generally weak, particularly between GY and other traits, indicating that simultaneous improvement of these traits is readily attainable. Nevertheless, other wheat breeding programs frequently encounter antagonistic correlations. The most notable example is the negative association between GY and grain protein content, which can slow genetic gain when both traits are under selection (Simmonds, ; Terman et al., ). In such scenarios, MTME models are advantageous because they jointly model trait covariances and facilitate the use of empirically derived economic weights to balance opposing selection pressures and optimize expected economic gain (Bernardo, ).

In some cases, the genetic correlations we estimated reflected our firsthand observations of the environmental conditions. For example, HD was consistently positively correlated with GY‐23‐Urb, an environment where a late frost occurred during the boot and early heading stages. Wheat is especially vulnerable to freezing injury during heading and anthesis (Frederiks et al., ), and we observed that early‐heading genotypes experienced extensive frost damage and yield loss, leading to a positive correlation between later heading and higher yield. Similarly, in 2024, a severe FHB epidemic occurred in parts of central and southern Illinois, and that year, we observed severe FHB symptoms in our trials, particularly in Urb. This epidemic resulted in strong positive correlations between TW and GY in 2024, particularly in Urb. FHB is known to reduce both GY and TW through kernel damage and mycotoxin contamination (Buerstmayr et al., ; McMullen et al., ). The reduced correlations between GY‐24‐Urb and other environments in 2022–2023, as well as between TW‐24 and TW in other environments, are also attributable to the severe FHB epidemic we experienced. These examples illustrate how specific biotic and abiotic stresses can modify the expression of genetic variance and covariances among traits, leading to environment‐dependent changes in their apparent genetic architecture (Des Marais et al., ; El‐Soda et al., ).

While this research represents an important step toward the routine application of economic genomic selection indices in wheat, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, to obtain a single linear economic weight for TW, we assumed an average yield for the TPE and TW below the market standard. Because these assumptions affect the economic weight assigned to TW, this weight would be inaccurate if either assumption is inappropriate. Second, our index assumes no price discount for DON contamination caused by FHB. In practice, DON concentrations exceeding market thresholds lead to price penalties or outright rejection; therefore, DON concentration is an economically important trait that should ultimately be included in our index. Third, lodging tolerance was excluded, although severe lodging is known to cause substantial yield losses, delayed harvest, reduced grain quality, and increased drying costs (Berry & Spink, ). Furthermore, if double‐crop soybeans are to be planted, wheat lodging can hinder the establishment of the soybean crop, ultimately reducing its yield. Similarly, we excluded height from our index, although it can affect profitability. Straw yield is a function of height (Engel et al., ); thus, for growers who profit from straw, height would have a positive EV. Conversely, for growers who plant double‐crop soybeans and do not use or sell wheat straw, height would have a negative EV due to the impact of wheat residue on double‐crop soybean establishment.

Comprehensive EV tables, like what is available in dairy cattle breeding (VanRaden et al., ), could be developed for wheat breeding with additional research. As demonstrated for TW, an economic weight for DON could be derived using the discount schedules that grain buyers impose. However, it is important to bear in mind that DON phenotyping is typically conducted within an inoculated and misted nursery representing a severe FHB epidemic. Studies to quantify the relationship between genetic variation in DON concentrations observed in such nurseries and that expressed under production conditions may be needed to derive an appropriate economic weight. For lodging tolerance, an economic weight could be derived based on research examining all costs associated with different degrees of lodging across a representative set of environments. This may not be practically feasible given the sporadic nature of lodging and difficulty of quantifying some of its economic impacts. Traits for which EVs cannot be readily determined could be assigned a weight based on desired gains (Pešek & Baker, ) to provide an immediate solution.

Regardless of the type of weight used, defining a breeding objective requires a nuanced understanding of production practices and scenarios within the TPE. In wheat, production and pricing systems are region‐specific and can vary widely even within a region. This can result in system‐specific EVs for the same trait (Wolfová et al., ), potentially requiring multiple sets of economic weights to adequately represent a given TPE. Economic weights will also vary temporally due to the influence of genetic and nongenetic trends caused by genetic improvement, changes in the environment, and improvements in management. Knowledge of these trends could inform economic weights to ensure that genetic improvement is directed toward future production needs.

In this study, the selection index was developed for parent selection, where the objective is to maximize expected genetic gain in the next generation based on additive genetic merit. In contrast, when the objective is to advance lines as future commercial products, total genetic value based on additive + nonadditive genetic merit should be considered to evaluate product performance. The selection procedure would then involve a combination of index selection and independent culling to meet required thresholds (Werner et al., ). Given that our work aims to optimize parent selection for improving the breeding population, a next step would be to integrate complementary strategies such as optimum contribution selection (Woolliams et al., ), with MTME modeling to maximize genetic gain while maintaining genetic variation in the key traits over time. This would enhance and help secure the long‐term sustainability of genetic gain for the breeding program.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates that combining a genomic‐based SHI with an MTME model and empirically derived economic weights can improve selection efficiency in wheat. The optimal MTME model explained over 91% of the additive variance and achieved high prediction accuracy for all traits. The advantage of the MTME model over the STME model was clear based on outcomes of index selection. Compared to the STME, the MTME‐based index selection delivered higher predicted economic returns and achieved genetic gains in GY, TW, and maturity that were more consistent with the breeding objectives. Use of an MTME model also captured environment‐specific genetic correlations between traits, aiding our understanding of how local stress conditions impact trait relationships. Overall, this study serves as a useful case example for how a regional breeding program can optimize multi‐trait selection to accelerate genetic gain in total net merit.
