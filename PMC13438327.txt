Expanding the phenotypic spectrum of COL6-related diseases: Motor neuropathy-like and neuromyopathy associated with COL6A3 c.7447A>G
Authors: Rocio Nur Villar-Quiles, A Reghan Foley, Corinne Metay, Rotem Orbach, Sandra Donkervoort, Beatrice Labella, Daniel Natera-de Benito, Andres Nascimento, Berta Estevez-Arias, Cecilia Jimenez-Mallebrera, Carlos Ortez, Cristina Domínguez-González, Alejandro Horga, Maria Itxaso Marti Carrera, Roberto Fernandez Torron, Sergei Kurbatov, Paulina Chausova, Aysylu Murtazina, Dmitrii Subbotin, Anna Kuchina, Rodrigo Frezzati, Alzira Carvalho, Anne Waschbisch, Valerie Allamand, Yaqun Zou, Pascale Richard, Carsten G Bönnemann, Tanya Stojkovic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCollagen VI is a key component of the extracellular matrix. Murine models suggest that it regulates Schwann cell differentiation, peripheral nerve myelination and regeneration after injury. Neuropathic findings in COL6-related dystrophies (COL6-RDs) have been rarely reported, often based on electromyography findings without nerve conduction abnormalities. We report 37 patients (1.5-79 years), from 32 different families, carrying the COL6A3-c.7447A>G variant, presenting with neuropathic/neuromyopathic findings. Most patients presented in the first decade of life with an abnormal gait and frequent falls (54%), delayed motor milestones (32%) and foot deformities (19%). Predominant distal weakness was present in 46% of patients, whereas 27% exhibited proximo-distal involvement. Foot deformities were present in 32%; joint contractures were present in 83%, exclusively affecting lower limbs in 49%. At last evaluation, mild restrictive respiratory insufficiency was diagnosed in 1 patient and nocturnal hypoventilation requiring non-invasive ventilation in 2. Most patients (89%) remained ambulant at last assessment. Nerve conduction studies (n=29) revealed reduced lower-limb CMAP amplitudes in 79%. Needle EMG (n=31) revealed chronic neurogenic-like features in 84% and mixed neuromyogenic features in 13%. Muscle biopsy (n=12) demonstrated features suggestive of neuropathic involvement in 4 (33%) and mixed neuropathic-myopathic features in 5 (42%). Muscle imaging (n=23) revealed a typical COL6-RD pattern in 48% (with severe distal lower limb atrophy in 65%), and isolated or predominant distal atrophy in 30%. Inheritance was autosomal recessive in all cases. The COL6A3-c.7447A>G variant was present in homozygosity in 24 (65%) and in compound heterozygosity with a second COL6A3 variant in 13 (35%), including 8 previously unreported. We report an expanding phenotypic spectrum associated with COL6-RD, underscoring its relevance in the diagnostic evaluation of distal motor neuropathy-like or neuromyopathic presentations.Keywords: COL6, collagen VI, neuropathy, muscle MRI, EMG

Introduction

Collagen type VI is a key component of the extracellular matrix (ECM) of muscle, the so-called ‘myomatrix’ and is also present in in the ECM of skin, tendons, cartilage, intervertebral discs, lens, cornea and blood vessels. 1 Collagen VI plays a major role in modulating the ECM’s stability and mechanical properties.2,3 It is also implicated in different cellular pathways, including regulation of apoptosis and oxidative damage, autophagy, cell differentiation and tumor progression.2,4,5 The expression of collagen VI in axons of nerves in humans, however, has remained an area of investigation. 6 Human peripheral nerves studies suggest that collagen VI is synthesized by Schwann cells and perineural cells. 7 Studies in Col6a1 (-/-) mice suggest that collagen VI may play a critical role in Schwann cell differentiation, peripheral nerve myelination, function, and structure. 2

Autosomal dominant or recessive causative variants in each of the genes encoding the collagen VI α-chains (COL6A1, COL6A2, COL6A3) underlie collagen VI-related dystrophies (COL6-RDs). COL6-RDs are typically characterized by early onset muscle weakness and connective tissue involvement including proximal joint contractures, distal joint hyperlaxity, characteristic cutaneous abnormalities (i.e. keloid scars, keratosis pilaris and soft/velvety skin) and respiratory muscle weakness resulting in respiratory insufficiency.3,8,9 Characteristic muscle MRI findings in COL6-RD include a pattern of abnormal T1-weighted signal around the fascia surrounding the muscle (“outside-in” pattern) of the vastus lateralis and the presence of the so-called “central cloud” or “central shadow” of the rectus femoris. 10

Interestingly, neuropathic findings have been only rarely reported in COL6-RD, and the clinical phenotype of these atypical forms have been described with few details.11–13 Along these lines, the COL6A3 c.7447A>G; p. (Lys2483Glu) variant has been recently reported in isolated cases, in homozygosity or compound heterozygosity, associated with atypical phenotypes, including a pattern of predominantly distal weakness and potential neuropathy-like features or neuromyopathy not fully characterized at that time.11,12,14,15 This discrepancy between the atypical phenotypes observed in some patients harboring this variant and the clinical presentations typically seen in the classical forms of COL6-RD, including Ullrich muscular dystrophy, Intermediate COL6-RD or Bethlem myopathy,9,16 raised questions about its pathogenicity and led us to the initial suspicion that COL6A3 c.7447A>G; p. (Lys2483Glu) could act as a phenotype-modulating variant. However, to date, there has been no solid evidence that this variant is associated with a neuropathic-like or neuromyopathic phenotype. 14 Here, we report 37 patients from 32 families presenting with clear evidence of motor neuropathy-like features or neuromyopathy, the largest series reported to date of highly suggestive evidence of motor nerve involvement in c.7447A>G associated COL6A3-RD, thus expanding the clinical spectrum of the COL6-RDs.

Methods

Patients

Through an international collaboration we identified 37 patients, from 32 different families, harboring the c.7447A>G; p. (Lys2483Glu) variant in the COL6A3 gene. Clinical data of patients 2 (local ID B16-58), 3.1 (local ID UC552.1), 3.2 (local ID UC552.2) and 32.1 were reported by our group in a previous work. 14 Clinical data and ancillary tests (including serum CK levels, electroneuromyography (ENMG) studies, muscle imaging, muscle histopathology, fibroblasts immunolabelling) were retrospectively retrieved and analyzed. All clinical data were collected anonymously from the patient medical records. All patients were examined by at least one of the coauthors in specialized neuromuscular departments.

Genetics analysis and variants interpretation

Genetic analyses were performed from peripheral blood using whole-exome sequencing (WES) or whole-genome sequencing (WGS) in 30 patients, and next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based myopathy panel in 7. NGS-based neuropathy panels were performed in 6 of these patients. Direct sequencing of the SMN1 gene was performed in 5 patients.

All variants considered as pathogenic and likely pathogenic were confirmed by a second independent method (Sanger sequencing). Variants were reported according to Human Genome Variation Society recommendations (https://varnomen.hgvs.org/) using the complete COL6A1-2-3 transcript ((NM_001848.4, NM_001849.4 and NM_004369.4, GRCh37 genome). Genome Aggregation (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/), Clinvar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/), LOVD3 and HGMD Pro databases were interrogated to determine variant frequency in the population and to identify previously-reported pathogenic variants. We evaluated each variant considering a review of the literature, the location of the variant in the gene and the corresponding protein and the in-silico prediction tools (CADD/MPA/Revel, SPiP/SpliceAI). Pathogenicity of variants was determined according to current ACMG guidelines (Supplementary table 1) 17 using the following codes: PM1: located in a mutational hotspot and/or critical and well established functional domain without benign variation; PM2 code: population frequency threshold: absent from controls or at extremely low frequency if recessive (GnomAD V4 with <2 homozygotes); PP3 code: REVEL score threshold (>0,5) or CADD score threshold (>25); PM3 code: code for variant in trans of a pathogenic variant in an recessive inheritance disease; PP4: functional studies (COL6 immunostaining); PVS1: null variant (nonsense, frameshift, canonical +/-2 splice sites, initiation codon, single or multiexon deletion) in a gene where LOF is a known mechanism of disease; PP5: the majority of the variant has been reported thus this ACMG code has been used to classify them. This code indicates that reputable source recently reports variant as pathogenic, but the evidence is not available to the laboratory to perform an independent evaluation. The cases have been submitted to LOVD database (LOVD entries can be found in Supplementary table 2).

Collagen VI immunolabelling

Skeletal muscle cryosections were co-stained with monoclonal antibodies against COLVI (MAB1944, Chemicon) and perlecan (MAB1948, Chemicon). Dermal fibroblasts from patients and control individuals were cultured to confluency in DMEM glutamax (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Biosera), antibiotic/antimycotic solution (1/100; Gibco), and 50g/ml L-Ascorbic acid-2- phosphate (Sigma). Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma) and subsequently immunostained using the polyclonal antibody ab6588 (Abcam) and a monoclonal antibody against fibronectin (HFN7.1, DSHB), Staining was visualized with Zeiss Axio Observer Apotome inverted microscope. Z-stacks were obtained using a 1µm step, with a 40x/1.30 NA objective. Images were acquired with the same exposure setting, using the Zen Pro software (Zeiss), and subsequently analyzed using Fiji. Stacks were merged and Z projections (Max intensity slices) were obtained.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed with R 4.0. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The frequency of each finding was calculated over the number of patients from whom this data was available.

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

Written informed consent according to local ethical committees in all participating centers was obtained for all patients. [NIH, National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS), Institutional Review Board (Protocol 12-N-0095)].

Clinical and genetic data were anonymized and recorded in a secure database, with access restricted exclusively to the first and last authors.

Results

Thirty-seven patients from 32 different families were identified through an international collaboration. Twenty-three patients were male (62%) and 14 (38%) were females. Patients were aged 1.5-79 years at the time of last examination (mean 26.4±23 years).

Clinical phenotype

Clinical features are summarized in Table 1. First symptoms were noticed during the first decade of life in 92% of patients and within the first two years of life in 41%. Two patients were diagnosed during adolescence, while one patient presented in adulthood with gradually progressive mild distal lower limb weakness beginning after the third decade of life. Reduced fetal movements were reported in two cases. Arthrogryposis, congenital contractures and torticollis were not reported in any patient. Neonatal hypotonia was reported in 3 patients (8%), and congenital hip dislocation/dysplasia were reported in two patients (5%). Delayed motor development was present in 32%, in particular delayed walking. Independent ambulation was acquired by all patients except for two siblings (30.1 and 30.2), monozygotic twins, who presented a severe psychomotor global delay and were diagnosed with an autistic spectrum disorder (ASD). The most common initial symptoms were an abnormal gait, inability to run or frequent falls (54%). Pes cavus was the presenting symptom in 7 patients (19%), isolated or prominent distal weakness in five patients (14%), generalized muscle weakness in five patients (14%) and toe-walking in 2 patients (5%).

Distribution of skeletal muscle involvement

Nine patients (24%) had weakness in the lower extremities only and 27 patients (73%) had both upper and lower extremity weakness. Predominantly distal weakness with striking distal amyotrophy was present in 17 (46%) and proximal and distal weakness was present in 10 (27%). Predominantly axial and proximal weakness, as classically seen in COL6-RDs, was observed in only 9 patients (24%). In one patient evaluated at age 1.5 years, formal manual muscle strength testing was not feasible; mildly decreased appendicular tone was observed.

Joint contractures

Most patients (84%) exhibited joint contractures upon presentation, with lower limb involvement, specifically Achilles’ tendon contractures, observed in 18 cases (49%). Thirteen patients (35%) had multiple joint contractures, including long finger flexors, wrists, elbows, and knee contractures along with Achilles’ tendon contractures. The occurrence of joint contractures appears to correlate with age; among patients presenting with multiple contractures, 62% (8 out of 13) were over 20 years old. In contrast, most younger individuals exhibited only Achilles’ contractures, with 68% (13 out of 19) affected. Distal joint hyperlaxity was observed in 17 patients (46%) and seen in association with upper and lower extremity joint contractures.

Orthopedic features

Striking foot deformities were present in 12 patients (32%), manifesting with pes cavus and hammer toes (Figure 1). Two patients had been diagnoses with clubfoot in relation to prominent distal weakness and contractures, which required specialized foot surgery. One patient required triple arthrodesis. Of note, spinal rigidity was observed in 5 patients (14%) and was associated with scoliosis in 3 (8%). None of the patients required spinal fusion surgery for scoliosis.

Dermatologic findings

Cutaneous involvement in the form of follicular keratosis pilaris and/or hypertrophic/keloid scars were observed in 11 patients (30%).

Respiratory and cardiac involvement

There was no evidence of cardiac involvement in any patient. Mild restrictive respiratory insufficiency was present in one patient, who had a forced vital capacity (FVC) of 60% predicted at age 54 years. Two patients had significant nocturnal apneas or hypoventilation documented, requiring nocturnal non-invasive ventilation, initiated at age 5 years and 39 years.

Clinical course

Weakness was slowly progressive for most patients. Loss of ambulation occurred in one patient at the age of 12 years, who harbors the c.7447A>G variant in compound heterozygosity with a c.6359_6364del COL6A3 variant. The monozygotic twins with autism spectrum disorder and comorbid intellectual disability did not achieve independent ambulation, separate from their neuromuscular issues. Details of the genetic investigations for this developmental delay are provided in the genetics section. Most patients (89%) remained independently ambulant with only two needing assistance devices (e.g., cane, walker). Intermittent use of a wheelchair was reported in one patient. Interestingly, nine patients maintained independent ambulation beyond the age of 40, including two patients over the age of 70 years.

Creatine kinase (CK) levels

CK levels were available for 25 patients and were mildly elevated (<500 IU/L) in 28% (Table 2). Thirty percent of patients had creatine kinase (CK) levels between 500 and 1000 IU/L, while three patients (12%) had CK levels above 1000 IU/L. Four patients (16%) had normal CK values.

Electrodiagnostic studies

Nerve conduction studies performed in 29 patients showed absent or reduced distal compound motor action potentials (CMAPs) in the lower limbs—especially in the fibular and posterior tibial nerves—without sensory involvement, suggesting pure motor neuropathy in 23 cases (79%) (Table 2). Reduced CMAP amplitude in lower limbs was found even in patients from very young ages without significant muscle atrophy, exhibiting mild weakness, including thirteen cases in which EMG was performed during the first decade of life. Interestingly, in cases where longitudinal studies were performed at different ages, progressively reduced CMAP amplitude was noted (Supplementary table 3). Motor nerve conduction velocities were normal in all cases, as well as sensory nerve action potentials (SNAPs). Needle EMG was performed in 31 patients and revealed chronic neurogenic features (high-amplitude or giant motor unit action potentials (MUAPs) of increased duration, poor recruitment pattern without spontaneous activity) in 26 (84%). Altogether, these findings are compatible with a pure motor neuropathy. Interestingly, neurogenic features were not only present in distal muscles (i.e., tibialis anterior) but also in proximal muscles (i.e., quadriceps). In four patients (13%), there were mixed neuro-myogenic features, with needle EMG revealing a rather myopathic pattern in proximal muscles (i.e., quadriceps) contrasting with a neurogenic pattern of giant MUAPs and poor recruitment in distal muscles such as tibialis anterior.

Muscle imaging

Muscle MRI was performed in 23 patients (Table 2, Figure 2), revealing typical COL6-RD patterns of abnormal T1-signal in the lower extremities, namely a “central-cloud” pattern in the rectus femoris and an “outside-in” pattern in the vastus lateralis in 11 (48%). In 7 of these 11 patients (30% of the cohort), significant distal atrophy and fatty infiltration of muscles was noted, contrasting with a relatively milder proximal muscle involvement. Predominantly distal lower extremity muscle atrophy (in the absence of typical COL6-RD muscle MRI pattern) was noted in 7 cases (30%).

Indeed, peroneus and tibialis anterior were particularly affected. All but one of the patients exhibiting isolated or predominant distal involvement (86%) were homozygous for the c.7447A>G variant, whereas the COL6-RD pattern of abnormal T1-signal was observed both in homozygous and compound heterozygous patients. Non-specific diffuse lower limbs muscle atrophy and fatty replacement was observed in two patients (9%). Muscle MRI was reported as normal in three pediatric patients (ages 5, 6 and 7 years, respectively). Interestingly, the typical COL6-RD pattern of abnormal T1-signal was observed in older patients (43.3 ±21.2years, ranging 14-70 years), in comparison with patients with normal muscle MRI (ages 5, 6 and 7 years) or global lower limb atrophy (ages 8 and 21 years).

Muscle ultrasound was conducted in four patients and evaluated using the Modified Heckmatt Scale (0 = normal; 1= mildly increased echogenicity while bone echo is still distinct; 2= moderately increased echogenicity with reduced bone echo; 3 = severely increased echogenicity with complete loss of bone echo). There was increased echogenicity, consistent with a chronic myopathic process (modified Heckmatt grade 2-3 proximally, grade 2 distally) in two individuals (ages 5 and 10 years). Clear involvement of the rectus femoris muscle with increased echogenicity consistent with a “central cloud” pattern and mild evidence of an “outside-in” pattern of increased echogenicity in the vastus lateralis was seen, in addition to nonspecific distal chronic involvement. In a third patient, muscle ultrasound revealed increased echogenicity in the rectus femoris and tibialis anterior (grade 3), and gastrocnemius (grade 2) consistent with a chronic myopathic process. In the remaining patient, aged 1.5 years-old, there was normal appearance and echogenicity of the imaged muscles (grade 0).

Muscle biopsy

Muscle biopsy was performed in 12 patients. Histopathological features suggestive of neurogenic involvement were observed in a subset of biopsies. Specifically, four biopsies (33%) showed findings compatible with neuropathic changes, including angular atrophic muscle fibers, patterns suggestive of fiber type grouping, and occasional target fibers. These features were observed in biopsies from both proximal muscles (quadriceps and deltoid) and distal muscles (tibialis anterior) (Figure 3). In 5 patients (42%), muscle biopsies revealed mixed neuropathic and myopathic features [quadriceps (N=2), tibialis anterior (N=2) and deltoid (N=1)], with patterns suggestive of fiber type grouping (sometimes associated with target fibers) along with fiber size variation, internalized nuclei and mild endomysial fibrosis. In one of the patients with a mixed neuropathic and myopathic findings who had a tibialis anterior biopsy at 38 years, rimmed vacuoles were observed. It is also striking that a patient with a deltoid biopsy performed at 16 years of age had evidence of fiber type grouping. A subsequent biopsy in this same patient at age 47 years revealed continued fiber type grouping, as well as the presence of angular fibers, along with a more dystrophic pattern, with endomysial fibrosis and split fibers. The remaining three patient biopsies, performed in deltoid muscles, revealed either non-specific myopathic features or severe muscle atrophy and fatty replacement.

Collagen VI immunolabelling

Co-immunolabelling using specific antibodies was performed on muscle cryosections from a control individual and patient 1.1, demonstrating rather preserved COLVI staining and colocalization with perlecan (Figure 3(b)). On the other hand, immunolabelling performed on cultured dermal fibroblasts obtained from 6 patients revealed reduced secretion/expression of collagen VI and intracellular retention in three of fibroblast cultures, which were from three patients homozygous for the COL6A3 c.7447A>G variant (Figure 3(c) and data not shown).

Genetics

In all cases inheritance was autosomal recessive. In 28 cases, familial segregation confirmed that the heterozygous parents were healthy carriers. In 24 cases (65%), the COL6A3 c.7447A>G variant was identified to be homozygous (Table 3, Figure 4). In one patient, an additional third splice variant was identified in the COL6A1 gene (c.2435-2A>G). Of note, this patient’s mother and one sibling were both heterozygous for the COL6A3 c.7447A>G variant and heterozygous for the COL6A1 c.2435-2A>G variant. Both individuals were reported as asymptomatic and had normal neuromuscular examinations. In two siblings from the same family, a variant of unknown significance (VUS) in COL6A3 c.5867A>G p. (Asp1956Gly), was found in addition to the homozygous COL6A3 c.7447A>G variant, which was unlikely related to the clinical phenotype. In the remaining 13 patients (35%), the COL6A3 c.7447A>G variant was compound heterozygous with a second recessive variant in the COL6A3 gene (Table 3, Figure 4).

The c.7447A>G variant is located within the C1 von Willebrand factor A domain (VWF) of COL6A3. Despite a high prevalence in GnomAD V4.1.0 (7.1 10-4) consistent with a recessive mode of inheritance, the bioinformatic prediction tools were all in favor of a pathogenic effect (CADD: 24.5, MPA: 10 and Revel: 0.827). The variant was classified as likely pathogenic in the HGMD Pro-database and likely pathogenic or a variant of unknown significance (VUS) in LOVD and Clinvar.

Eight previously unreported COL6A3 variants were identified (Table 3, Supplementary table 1), six located within the VWF and two within the triple helix (TH) domains. Based on current ACMG criteria, six of these variants were classified as probably pathogenic. The remaining two variants were classified as VUS: c.5867A>G p. (Asp1956Gly) is a missense variant located within the VWF domain and the in-frame deletion c.6359-6364del p. (Asp2120_Lys2121del) is located within the C-terminal part of the TH domain and is predicted to disrupt the structure of the TH region. However, the latter is harbored by one of the unaffected parents of the index case.

In the two patients presenting with ASD, WES was performed and identified the so-far unreported c.2941C>A, p. (Leu981Ille) variant in JMJD1C (also known as JARID1C or SMCX), a key epigenetic regulator in neuronal development. Heterozygous mutations in JMJD1C are associated with global developmental delay, autism, and intellectual disability.18,19

Misdiagnosis and prior diagnostic tests

At least 20 patients (54%) were initially misdiagnosed as having a neurogenic disorder, mostly distal hereditary motor neuropathy (dHMN) or spinal muscular atrophy (SMA). In six patients (16%), NGS-based panels including hereditary peripheral neuropathy genes were performed as the first diagnostic test, since the initial clinical suspicion was dHMN. In five patients (14%), SMN1 direct sequencing was also performed among the first investigations, prior to WGS or WES, due to the suspicion of a SMA based on proximal-distal weakness and pure motor neurogenic involvement in ENMG. In five patients, the presence of diffuse joint contractures along with a typical MRI COL6 pattern, arose suspicion of diagnosis leading to performing an NGS-muscular genes panel including the COL6 genes despite the clinically distal or neuropathic presentation.

Discussion

Due to detailed natural history studies performed across international cohorts, the classification of the COL6-RDs as Ullrich congenital muscular dystrophy (UCMD), intermediate COL6-RD and Bethlem myopathy (now referred to as Bethlem muscular dystrophy) can be well-recognized and even predicted from a young age based on early maximal motor milestones.3,9,16 Historically, electrophysiologic findings indicative of neurogenic discharges in the COL6-RDs have been interpreted as a manifestation of atrophic muscle fibers, reflecting this muscle histology finding.11–13 Fiber atrophy is commonly observed in COL6-RDs, especially early in during the disease course where this can be evident in the absence of dystrophic feature, 20 and the loss of fibers can result in shorter MUAPs, which may thus present as neurogenic-like. Furthermore, the early muscle histology pattern in the COL6-RDs which is congenital fiber type disproportion (CFTD)-like may also be interpreted as neurogenic in appearance. 20

Recently, however, atypical cases of COL6-RD have been reported presenting with a distinct phenotype of predominantly distal weakness and electromyographic and/or histological findings suggestive of nerve involvement.11–15 Here, we report a remarkably consistent association of neuropathic-like and neuromyopathic features in this cohort of 37 patients carrying the c.7447A>G; p. (Lys2483Glu) variant in recessive COL6A3 associated disease, either in homozygosity or compound heterozygosity. These patients exhibit either distal weakness or proximal and distal weakness as the most salient feature, associated with joint contractures mainly affecting the distal lower extremities. In many cases, pes cavus and hammer toes were a presenting symptom, and these foot deformities were a common reason for a referral to a neurologist. This phenotype of predominantly distal muscle weakness, distal contractures and pes cavus and hammer toes, diverges from classical phenotypes of the COL6-RDs which present with more proximal muscle weakness. Joint contractures were present in many of the patients presented here, although they predominantly affected the lower limbs in contrast to the classical COL6-RD phenotypes in which joint contractures are present in both upper and lower extremities relentlessly progressive. Scoliosis and spinal rigidity, features that often dominate the natural history of UCMD an intermediate COL6-RD, were uncommon. Additionally, respiratory insufficiency varied among patients; only two individuals required nocturnal non-invasive ventilatory support, consistent with the pulmonary progression seen in Bethlem muscular dystrophy patients. 9

Electrodiagnostic studies provide compelling evidence of neurogenic involvement in this COL6A3 c.7447A>G,_p. (Lys2483Glu)-specific cohort. More than 70% of the patients showed motor conduction abnormalities, which would be consistent with a pure motor neuropathy, including reduced distal CMAP with normal conduction velocities and no sensory involvement. Notably, a marked reduction of motor CMAP amplitudes in the lower limbs was observed even in very young patients, including individuals in the first decade of life who did not yet show advanced disease, severe weakness, or significant distal muscle atrophy. In these cases, the degree of CMAP reduction appeared disproportionate to the degree of muscle involvement, further supporting the presence of a neuromyopathic component. Moreover, needle EMG revealed chronic denervation changes in the vast majority, suggesting involvement of the motor nerves. It is important to highlight that most patients initially underwent electrophysiological testing focused on distal muscles, as they presented with symptoms suggestive of a neuropathy and the studies were therefore primarily conducted in a neuropathy-oriented diagnostic context. While neurogenic-like electrophysiological changes may occasionally be observed in longstanding myopathies with marked distal atrophy and muscle loss, the overall pattern in our cohort suggests that this does not fully account for the findings. Indeed, in patients who underwent electrophysiological testing of both proximal and distal muscles, a mixed pattern was observed, with myopathic features proximally and neurogenic changes distally. This proximal–distal dissociation supports the presence of a neuromyopathic phenotype rather than a purely myopathic process. This combination of both myopathic and neurogenic features was further confirmed on muscle biopsy, where one third of samples showed findings suggestive of neuropathic involvement and nearly half exhibited a mixture of myopathic and neuropathic hallmarks, including fiber type grouping, angular fibers, and target fibers, sometimes even in proximal muscles despite a clinical phenotype of distal muscle weakness. These findings mirror those reported in smaller studies. Quijano-Roy et al. 21 described borderline slowing of motor and sensory conduction in children with COL6A2 pathogenic variants. Verma et al. 13 observed neuropathic features in biopsies of two children with COL6A1 pathogenic variants, with needle EMG revealing large MUAPs distally despite a proximal myopathic pattern. Similar observations were later echoed by other studies.12,14,15 Hunter et al. 11 reported two patients homozygous for COL6A3 c.7447A>G with proximal and distal weakness along with ENMG evidence of motor neuropathy in one patient and spontaneous activity in distal lower limb muscles in the second patient. Our present report has focused on the largest cohort of patients harboring the COL6A3 c.7447A>G variant either in homozygosity or compound heterozygosity and highlights that neurogenic electrophysiologic findings – including nerve conduction studies consistent with a pure motor neuropathy - and neuropathic muscle histologic findings are a consistent feature of these patients’ phenotype. Neuropathic or neuromyopathic involvement in COL6-RD has not been widely recognized until now, despite multiple reports in the literature of large international cohorts encompassing a broad spectrum of disease severity. This under-recognition may reflect several factors: electroneuromyography is not routinely performed in patients with muscular dystrophies, variability in cohort selection may also bias detection of these findings, and interpretation of electrodiagnostic results can be challenging when underlying muscle disease is present. In addition, allele-specific effects, such as those potentially associated with c.7447A>G, may influence the presence or severity of the neuropathy of neuromyopathic findings.

The diagnostic implications of this overlap between myopathic and neuropathic features are significant. Patients with distal weakness and electrophysiological evidence of motor neuropathy are typically directed towards differential diagnoses such as distal hereditary motor neuropathies (dHMNs), Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (CMT), or even spinal muscular atrophy (SMA). In fact, many of the patients we studied initially underwent a diagnostic odyssey. Some patients underwent targeted genetic testing for SMA or CMT (in more than 50% the initial clinical diagnosis was that of a neuropathic condition, and more than 20% of the patients underwent NGS-based neuropathy panels or direct SMN1 testing) yielding negative results and thus leading to delayed recognition of the COL6A3 causative variants. In most cases, WES eventually uncovered the causative COL6A3 c.7447A>G variant. Interestingly, in certain patients, muscle MRI suggested the diagnosis by showing the distinctive COL6-RD muscle imaging pattern, thereby guiding the genetic work-up. Conversely, muscle MRI also proved highly useful for the clinical validation of COL6A3 c.7447A>G finding obtained through WES or WGS analysis. Indeed, muscle MRI revealed typical COL6-RD findings in the lower limbs in 48% of patients, frequently associated with marked distal lower limb atrophy. Strikingly, distal involvement was even more pronounced than proximal involvement in most of these patients, a pattern that contrasts with the classical phenotype of COL6-RD, in which distal muscles are usually relatively preserved until advanced disease stages. Indeed, in a significant proportion of patients, there were significant muscle atrophy and fatty infiltration affecting muscles below the knee, such as peroneus and tibialis anterior, which is be very supportive of a neurogenic involvement. In our cohort, however, distal weakness and atrophy were consistently prominent both clinically and radiologically from disease onset.

After a detailed review and a sub-analysis comparing patients homozygous for the COL6A3 c.7447A>G variant, compound heterozygous with loss-of-function variants, and compound heterozygous with missense variants, no clear genotype–phenotype correlation was found regarding the clinical presentation (neuropathic or combined neuromyopathic findings) or electrodiagnostic findings. Regarding imaging studies, pure or predominant distal atrophy and fatty infiltration was more commonly found in homozygous patients compared with the rest of the cohort, but further studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm this observation.

All patients in our cohort follow a recessive mode of disease causation with the COL6A3 c.7447A>G p. (Lys2483Glu) variant in homozygosity in the majority (65%) while the remaining 13 individuals (35%) harbor it in compound heterozygosity. The interpretation of this variant, located in the VWF domain, is complex to interpret since it is reported with a high allele frequency (7.2x10-4 and with one homozygote occurrence in GnomAD V4.1.0). However, COL6-RDs are associated with a large clinical spectrum including variable disease severity and variable penetrance, with substantial differences in disease expression reported even among individuals from the same family carrying the same pathogenic variant.22–24 Therefore, mildly affected or paucisymptomatic individuals may remain undiagnosed, and population databases may include individuals with unrecognized or very mild disease.23,25 Our team previously studied this variant and reported in 2021 a cohort of 16 patients (12 compound heterozygotes and 4 homozygotes) along with a literature review. 14 The COL6A3 c.7447A>G p. (Lys2483Glu) variant has been documented in compound heterozygosity in relation to limb-girdle forms of COL6-RD; furthermore, when present in the homozygous state, it has been particularly associated with atypical phenotypes.11,12,14,15 This led us to hypothesize that this variant was a phenotype modulator. 14 Interestingly, a retrospective analysis of our previously reported cohort, as well as the reported patients in the literature with this specific COL6A3 variant confirmed distal predominant symptoms or proximal and distal involvement in certain cases, with some patients demonstrating evidence of a motor neuropathy on nerve conduction studies and/or neuropathic features on muscle biopsies.11,12,14 To date, 27 patients carrying the COL6A3 c.7447A>G variant have been reported in the literature to our knowledge. As mentioned before, Hunter et al., reported two patients with neurogenic involvement associated with this variant in homozygosity. 11 Stavusis et al. reported three homozygous patients from two families, two of them presenting with mild-girdle weakness and the remaining patient exhibiting an atypical phenotype with proximo-distal weakness associated with axonal polyneuropathy and neurogenic recruitment on ENMG studies. 12 Panades et al. described four patients, including homozygotes and compound heterozygotes, two of them showing a classical Bethlem phenotype with proximal weakness and joint contractures, one compound heterozygote patient with an atypical phenotype exhibiting distal lower limb weakness and the last of them was an unaffected sibling presenting only with hyperCKemia. 15 Fichna et al. reported one heterozygous individual with a limb-girdle phenotype, without further details. 26 Finally, our team reported 16 patients from 14 families, of whom four were included in the present series and displayed atypical phenotypes with distal or proximo-distal involvement and neurogenic changes on EMG. 14 Brinas et al. reported one compound heterozygous patient presenting with COL6-RD without any specific clinical details. 27 This variant is associated with 25 entries in LOVD, including one of our previously reported patients, and 14 cases in ClinVar. Clinical features are generally not described in detail; however, the reported phenotype is predominantly myopathic. Most patients with the c.7447A>G variant show classic Bethlem or limb-girdle symptoms, but some display unusual distal or proximo-distal involvement and neurogenic features. Overall, while the majority of the reported patients harboring the c.7447A>G variant seems to present with classical Bethlem or limb-girdle features, a subset exhibits atypical distal or proximo-distal involvement and neurogenic findings.

Regarding the localization of the COL6A3 variants associated in compound heterozygosity with COL6A3 c.7447A>G, no particular protein domain appeared, as the majority of the COL6A3 variants are spread along the VWF domain and 3 variants in the triple helical domain. Except for one in-frame deletion, all variants identified in trans with the c.7447A>G COL6A3 variant are truncating variants (nonsense or frameshifting) with predicted nonsense mediated decay (NMD).

Of note, recent studies have identified recessive variants in the VWA1 gene, encoding a von Willebrand factor A–domain containing protein (WARP), leading to neuromyopathic phenotypes.22–24 VWA1 is expressed in both muscle and peripheral nerves and interacts with collagen VI. Knockout models reveal altered peripheral nerve structure and function, with disrupted motor axon growth and synapse formation in zebrafish and locomotor abnormalities. 28 Patients with causative variants in VWA1 exhibit distal neuromyopathy with EMG evidence of non-length-dependent motor neuropathy or mixed patterns,29–32 similar to what we have observed in our COL6A3 c.7447A>G-specific cohort. Biochemically, WARP binds directly to collagen VI with high affinity, via an interaction within the triple-helical domain of collagen VI. WARP-null mice exhibit reduced and disorganized collagen VI in peripheral nerve ECM, underscoring a structural and functional partnership.33,34 Disruption of this interaction may compromise collagen VI microfibril organization and matrix stability, potentially explaining the clinical overlap with COL6-RD and the early prominent distal involvement observed in our cohort. Notably, the GC-rich 3′ region of WARP may represent a lost triple-helix domain since WARP shares an evolutionary relationship with the FACIT and FACIT-like collagens, including collagen VI, likely deriving from a common collagen ancestor.35,36

Potential pathomechanisms as to how this COL6A3 c.7447A>G p. (Lys2483Glu) variant specifically results in neuromyopathic involvement remain to be fully explored. There is evidence supporting a role of collagen VI in peripheral nerve physiology in animal models, whereas its role in human pathophysiology remains less explored. Studies in the Col6a1 (-/-) mouse model suggests that collagen VI plays a role in Schwann cell differentiation and the myelination, structure, and function of peripheral nerves.4,6,37 Zebrafish models provide further evidence of the role of collagen VI neuronal development, as morpholino-mediated knockdown of col6a2/col6a4a/col6a4b leads to axonal growth defects. 38 Taken together, animal model studies of collagen VI support its role as a regulator of peripheral nerve development, regeneration, and pathology. The mechanism by which a recessively acting specific missense variant promotes neuronal involvement in homozygous and compound heterozygous states, but not in carriers, remains an intriguing area for study. This observation distinguishes it from other recessive variants in COL6A3, as well as those in COL6A1 and COL6A2, which do not exhibit such a clear correlation. Further clinical and experimental research is necessary to elucidate this phenomenon.

Interestingly, an expanding spectrum of neuromuscular disorders is now being recognized as neuromyopathies, reflecting the growing awareness that single gene pathogenic variants can affect both muscle and nerve. 39 In addition to the VWA1 gene, pathogenic variants in CRYAB, which is typically associated with myofibrillar myopathy and cataracts, can also result in mixed phenotypes. These may include axonal peripheral neuropathy as well as multisystem involvement affecting skeletal and cardiac muscles and the central nervous system.40,41 Similarly, HSPB8 pathogenic variants are linked to both distal hereditary motor neuropathies and Charcot-Marie-Tooth type 2L, as well as myopathies, particularly myofibrillar forms with vacuoles and protein aggregates.42,43

In conclusion, our findings expand the phenotypic spectrum of COL6-RDs by providing compelling evidence of motor neuropathy-like and neuromyopathic findings in patients harboring the COL6A3 c.7447A>G; p. (Lys2483Glu) variant. Our findings highlight that COL6-RD should be considered in the differential diagnosis of neuromuscular diseases presenting with distal weakness and/or mixed myopathic-neuropathic features, especially when associated with early distal contractures, pes cavus and/or skin involvement. Our results are relevant for diagnosis, as patients may be incorrectly identified as having distal motor neuropathy, leading to targeted NGS panel genetic testing for neuropathy-related genes. Moreover, this so-far unrecognized COL6-RD phenotype can also hinder the interpretation of WES/WGS thus resulting in a diagnostic odyssey.

Our findings also emphasize the need for careful integration of genotype and clinical phenotype data including muscle imaging and muscle pathology findings when evaluating patients with symptoms which may be atypical in relation to the phenotypes historically associated with variants in particular genes. Muscle MRI may play a pivotal role in the diagnostic journey of patients with unexplained distal weakness, neuropathic or neuromyopathic findings by revealing typical COL6-RD muscle imaging patterns, thus guiding genetic testing and the interpretation of genetic testing results. Muscle MRI has an increasingly established role in the differential diagnosis of primary muscle disorders, where specific imaging patterns can narrow the differential of potential muscle diseases. In contrast, the use of muscle MRI application remains underdeveloped in neuropathies and neuromyopathies. Expanding the use of muscle imaging in these contexts could facilitate diagnosis in overlap syndromes or atypical neuromuscular presentations, helping to improve diagnostic accuracy and reduce the diagnostic delay. More research is needed to clarify how the COL6A3 c.7447A>G p. (Lys2483Glu) variant causes neuropathic and neuromyopathic features and to determine whether other COL6 genotypes contribute to overlapping muscle and nerve phenotypes.
