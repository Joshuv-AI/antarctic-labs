Improved synthetic CT generation using surface scan information integrated into a deformable registration algorithm for limited Field of View data☆
Authors: Joachim Marichal, Stina Svensson, Geert De Kerf, Ola Weistrand, Mattias Nilsing, Michaël Claessens, Pelle Jansson, Dirk Verellen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeCone-Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) based synthetic CT is being increasingly used for post-delivery dose computation or adaptive workflows in radiotherapy. However, the limited Field-of-View of the CBCT can cause inaccuracies when tissues fall outside the Field of View. This study evaluates the integration of surface-guided radiotherapy information to enhance synthetic CT generation for breast cases with missing tissues on CBCT.Materials and MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 20 breast patients. CBCT volumes were acquired on a linac, with simultaneous surface scans from three cameras. A full Field of View CBCT was used to generate a reference synthetic CT. A smaller Field of View CBCT was reconstructed from the same raw data to create two synthetic CTs: a standard synthetic CT and a surface-guided synthetic CT, for which surface scan information was incorporated into the algorithm to guide reconstruction outside the Field of View. We compared the image similarity, external contour geometry, and dose distribution.ResultsThe surface-guided synthetic CT showed superior agreement with the reference for nine tested metrics. Specifically, it showed an average Mean Squared Error reduction of 1963 HU2 (p=0.002), revealing hot spots in some cases.ConclusionIntegrating surface scan information into deformable registration improves synthetic CT generation for CBCT with limited Field of View, yielding more accurate images and enhanced dose distribution precision for breast cases. Future work will explore other sites, and potential applications for adaptive radiotherapy.Keywords: Synthetic CT, Surface scan, SGRT, Daily dose computation, Inter-fraction motion management, Breast radiotherapy

Introduction

Image Guided Radiation Therapy (IGRT) and Surface Guided Radiation Therapy (SGRT) systems have become standard components of modern radiotherapy workflows. IGRT enables accurate position adjustment in 3D through linac-integrated Cone-Beam CT (CBCT) imaging [1], [2], which has long replaced 2D X-ray projections or MV imaging for most treatment applications. In the last decade, SGRT has allowed for precise patient positioning and monitoring, replacing lasers and tattoos in many clinics worldwide [3]. In SGRT systems, a portion of the patient’s surface is captured by in-room cameras, processed, and compared to a baseline. It has recently been made possible to import surface scans in treatment planning systems (TPS), but its application remains mainly limited to collision prevention [4].

More recently, synthetic Computed Tomography (sCT) images have been increasingly used in the RT clinics for dose recomputation or adaptation [5]. An sCT is a medical volumetric image mimicking the Hounsfield Unit (HU) values of a CT scan, generated from another modality such as a CBCT or an MRI scan [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]. Various approaches for generating sCT from CBCT have been explored; in some workflows, the strategy consists of deforming the planning CT to the daily CBCT [12], [13]. Other strategies use a two-stage method which alternates between converting CBCT intensities to planning CT HU and applying a low-frequency shading correction map iteratively until convergence [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]. Additionally, other approaches employ deep-learning (DL) techniques [21], [22], [23], [24].

The improved HU accuracy of the sCT better represents the electron density of the imaged tissues, while reduced imaging artifacts enable improved accuracy in dose calculation and target and organs-at-risk delineation compared with the original CBCT. However, one of the main shortcomings of CBCT-based sCT images is the CBCT’s limited Field-of-View (FoV) [25], [26]. In most sCT generation methods, the tissues falling outside the FoV are copied over from the planning CT via a registration [12]. This is an approximation made with the available data, but the simulation CT will most likely not reflect the current anatomy or positioning of the patient outside the FoV. This is particularly important when tissue lies outside the FoV in the beam path, potentially causing dose calculation errors. In some cases, such misrepresentation may influence replanning decisions, including dose recomputation for large breast patients or head-and-neck patients with tumors spanning a long cranio-caudal range.

Previous studies have focused on DL approaches to generate images from incomplete data [25]. Others have integrated surface information in DL algorithms to generate volumetric images, with promising results [27], [28]. However, DL approaches for medical image generation require a significant amount of time, computational resources and data for their training [26]. To our knowledge, no deterministic sCT generation approach integrating volumetric imaging and surface scanning data has yet been proposed.

The aim of this paper is to investigate the integration of surface scan information to improve deformation-based sCT images for breast RT outside the FoV. For this purpose, we conducted a phantom and a retrospective clinical study. In the phantom study, breast swelling was simulated with a bolus, comparing standard and surface-guided sCT generation. In the clinical study, sCTs from truncated CBCT scans were generated with both methods and compared to a reference sCT from full FoV data. We also illustrate the proposed method with a use case.

Materials and methods

For both the phantom and retrospective clinical studies, synthetic CT images were generated using the Virtual CT algorithm implemented in a research version of RayStation (v2025 ReC, RaySearch Laboratories, Stockholm, Sweden) [29]. This approach was selected over corrected CBCT because it is less susceptible to the streak artifacts commonly observed in thoracic CBCT imaging. To generate the surface-guided sCT, the algorithm was modified to integrate the imported surface data.

Surface capture and conversion

The phantom and patient surfaces were acquired using the Catalyst+ HD system (C-RAD) in the treatment room, yielding high-resolution 3D surface reconstructions derived from the combined input of three structured light scanners. The scan volume was configured to encompass a volume of 72.0×36.0×30.0 cm3 (in lateral, longitudinal, and vertical directions, respectively), exceeding the extent of the full CBCT FoV. To enable integration into the subsequent processing pipeline, a prototype conversion tool supplied by C-RAD was used to transform the Catalyst-internal surface format into a standard triangulated STL mesh format. The surface scan was taken in the same frame of reference as the CBCT, and was therefore directly registered at the correct position on the external contour upon import, although the registration could be manually corrected if needed (for example, to correct for patient motion).

Algorithm description

In the conventional Virtual CT algorithm, the first step is to perform a deformable registration between the CBCT and the planning CT. This is done using the ANACONDA algorithm [30] accounting for intensity information inside the FoV of the CBCT. The second step is to map over the intensities from the planning CT to the CBCT anatomy, while preserving local anatomical differences such as presence of bowel gas through an iterative conversion algorithm [15]. The result of this conversion is a type of sCT named virtual CT, which has been validated for daily dose computation [14], [15]. One of the main disadvantages of this algorithm is that, outside the FoV, only information from the planning CT is used. Although a smooth CBCT–planning CT transition is ensured by forcing the deformation vector field to fade to zero, regions beyond the FoV resemble a rigid copy of the planning CT.

In this study, the patient surface scan was incorporated as a spatial constraint in deformable registration beyond the FoV to achieve more realistic mapping. To this aim, the surface scan was converted to an STL surface and added as a region of interest in the CBCT image. First, the external contour of the planning CT was converted to a triangular mesh representation. Then, for each vertex of the external mesh, a search in the normal direction to the surface was performed to find corresponding points on the surface scan. The search was constrained to an interval. The original mesh was then deformed onto the surface scan, using the found intersection points as a driving force. A non-linear optimization method incorporating a shape constraining energy term was used to achieve overlapping surfaces while keeping the external mesh anatomically correct. The described search followed by the described deformation was iteratively applied until no significant change occurred to the external mesh.

Phantom study

A thorax model (CIRS SBRT Spine) on which a breast prosthesis was mounted on the right side was used to acquire two CT scans: the planning CT (pCT), consisting of the phantom and the prosthesis only, and the replanning CT (reCT), defined as the reference, and consisting of the phantom with a 5 mm-thick bolus laid on the breast prosthesis to simulate breast swelling. The acquisition was performed on a SOMATOM go.Open Pro (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen).

The two CT scans were imported into the TPS and rigidly registered. A planning target volume (PTV), excluding the bolus, consisting of the breast prosthesis cropped at 3-mm from the external contour, was manually contoured on pCT, and rigidly copied to reCT. An Intensity Modulated Radiation Therapy treatment plan was created, with a prescription of 5×5.2 Gy median dose to the PTV. A CBCT with tissue outside the FoV was acquired on a Varian TrueBeam (Varian, Palo Alto) C-arm linear accelerator. A conventional sCT (sCTstand) was created using the standard tools available in the TPS. Additionally, a surface scan was acquired using the SGRT system and imported into the TPS, and used to create a surface-guided sCT (sCTsurf). More details on the acquisition parameters are provided in the supplementary material Table S3.

Retrospective clinical study

A retrospective analysis on 20 anonymized breast patients was performed using data acquired on a Varian TrueBeam. Two CBCT reconstructions were generated from a single image acquisition: a standard CBCT with a FoV diameter of 46 cm (the standard used in our clinics) and a CBCT with a limited FoV diameter of 30 cm, shifted by 2 to 5 cm if necessary. An sCT was created using the full CBCT and defined as the reference sCT (sCTref). Using the CBCT with small FoV, two sCTs were created, namely sCTstand and sCTsurf, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Following [31], the lungs and heart were used as guiding structures. Those structures were contoured on the reference via deep learning segmentation (DLS), and then used as controlling Regions of Interest (ROI) to create a deformable image registration (DIR), which was used to propagate the CTV contours from pCT to sCTref. The DIR was created using the ANACONDA algorithm [30]. The CTV was then rigidly copied from sCTref to sCTstand and sCTsurf. The PTV was generated using a margin of 6 mm, in the left, right and inferior directions; and 3 mm in the superior, anterior and posterior directions, respectively, and cropped at 3 mm from the external contour. This study was registered and approved under the reference CTO26014ZAS-260306RETRO.

Workflow of the clinical retrospective study. Creation of the reference based on the CBCT with full FoV data, and creation of the standard synthetic CT, and surface-guided synthetic CT based on truncated CBCT data.

Image and dose comparison

The RT plan was recalculated on each sCT, and the images were compared against the reference, first qualitatively through visual inspection, then quantitatively using three metric groups:

•Image similarity metrics: mean absolute error (MAE), mean squared error (MSQE), mean error (ME), structural similarity image metrics (SSIM), peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), calculated on a ROI consisting of the bounding box of an expansion of the PTV.

•Region of Interest (ROI) overlap metrics: dice similarity coefficient (DSC), precision, sensitivity, specificity, maximum distance to agreement, also known as Hausdorff distance (maxDTA), mean distance to agreement (meanDTA), calculated on a ROI consisting of the intersection of the external contour and the bounding box containing the PTV.

•Dose distribution comparison: we calculated the difference between the dose at 2% volume (D2%), the dose at 98% volume (D98%) and the average dose (Dmean) of the PTV, in absolute value, and normalized it to the prescription dose. Additionally, the gamma passing rate (GPR) was calculated on the entire dose grid with a tolerance of 2%−2mm and a threshold of 10%, using the gamma function from the pymedphys python library [32].

For a comprehensive description of the image and contour overlap metrics, we refer to [33], [34]. A two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for statistical analysis, with p-values < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

The median values of the metrics tested in the retrospective study were computed for both methods, as well as the p-value of the Wilcoxon signed-rank test performed on this metric. We observed significantly improved performance of sCTsurf for nine metrics, and no statistically significant differences for the other seven metrics. Specifically, sCTsurf showed a median MSE reduction of 1963.7 HU2 (p=0.002), an increase in median PSNR of 2.4 dB (p=0.004), and a 1.2% increase in average GPR (p=0.004). The proposed method was then illustrated for a use case. For this patient (prescription: 15×2.67 Gy, treated with Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy), the CBCT was acquired in a centered position to allow collision-free full-arc acquisition. A large part of the right breast then fell outside the standard FoV, which limited the dose evaluation accuracy on CBCT. Therefore, sCTstand and sCTsurf were generated. As shown in Fig. 5, sCTsurf showed hot spots that were not visible on sCTstand. The evaluated dose-volume constraints also exhibited clinically relevant differences: On sCTstand, all dose-volume constraints were fulfilled, however, on sCTsurf, D0.03cc exceeded 107% of the prescribed dose and Dmean to the heart exceeded 1 Gy. The target coverage was not compromised, and the dose to the lung and contralateral breast were acceptable for both sCTstand and sCTsurf (see supplementary material Table S5). No action was taken as this case was retrospectively evaluated for one fraction.

Discussion

The proposed method, tested on a cohort of 20 patients, shows a statistically significant improvement for nine of the sixteen metrics tested. In particular, sCTsurf shows a significant improvement in MSE (−1963 HU2, p=0.002) and RMSE (−16 HU, p=0.002), while ME and MAE do not show any significant differences. As MSE and RMSE are based on squared intensity differences, this suggests that mostly large deviations in intensity are corrected. However, the similar SSIM indicates no improvement in perceived quality. All segmentation metrics indicate a significant improvement for sCTsurf except for specificity. The inspection of the dose distributions suggests that these improvements in image quality and geometry translate into better dose distribution agreement. All improved metrics also demonstrate a smaller range for sCTsurf, suggesting that integrating the surface information in the sCT leads to a more consistent image similarity to sCTref. The improved agreement in dose distribution may reveal hot spots that are less apparent with the standard approach and could influence clinical review or replanning decisions in selected instances, as illustrated in the use case. The phantom study shows the same tendency, and illustrates an important limitation of the standard algorithm: The external contour of the planning CT is registered to the external contour of the CBCT (here the bolus), using only the information from inside the FoV, which creates a shift with respect to the reference or the surface outside the FoV, more pronounced on the lateral side. This effect is corrected for in the new algorithm. We validated this method for breast RT, however, other indications could benefit from it. It is a known issue that the dose evaluation on CBCT for head and neck RT is hampered by the limited size of the FoV in cranio-caudal direction [35]. Including the surface scan information in the sCT generation could help solve this issue if the mask is removed, which is a possibility, considering the recent work done in this field [36], [37], [38].

Although the proposed method is novel, the general problem of completing missing CBCT data or generating images from surface scans has been researched. Namely, Shields et al. demonstrated that it is possible to generate tissue outside the FoV from breast CBCT images and improve their quality using DL techniques, combining streak artifact removal and image completion [25]. They report an SSIM of 0.9993 ± 0.0001 and an MAE of 72.8 ± 1.79 between the reference and the generated images. Pan et al. developed an imaging system to synthesize a CBCT for real-time tumor tracking using low-frequency single-angle X-ray projections and a surface scan as input. They designed a patient-specific diffusion model, trained with prior 4DCT of the patient [27]. The same group also developed another patient-specific DL model, this time to generate synthetic-CT from the surface scan only [28], also trained with the patient’s 4DCT as prior knowledge. They report an average MAE of 34 ± 7.9 HU, PSNR of 36.7 ± 2.3 dB, and SSIM of 0.949 ± 0.02. The SSIM and PSNR reported in those studies are similar to our results, but the MAE we obtained is about 18 units smaller, most likely due to our close-up view approach. Those methods leverage state-of-the-art generative DL techniques to create volumetric data from the surface scan and minimal radiation dose and yield excellent results. However, a new model must be trained for each patient, which limits their potential for a rapid clinical implementation. The method we propose requires no training phase, is less resource intensive and can be easily implemented. Another advantage of using a deterministic method is that its failure modes can be traced to the input data, which makes it more explainable and interpretable than DL approaches.

One limitation of this method for thoracic application is that the lung should be entirely within the FoV. Indeed, using the surface scan data does not provide additional information on the tissue type or air cavities, resulting in potential undesired stretching of the lung, as observed in the phantom study in Fig. 2. It is also important to stress that, in the retrospective study, the reference image is a synthetic CT made with the full FoV data. Although it has been shown that the virtual CT is similar to a replanning CT in terms of MAE and dosimetry [15], [19], it does not suffer from positioning deviations, which can explain that the image metrics show better agreement than those of our phantom study or the other mentioned studies. However, in the phantom study, the bolus does not have the same electronic density as the breast prosthesis, which likely leads to an overestimation of the intensity difference with respect to the reference for both sCTsurf and sCTstand, and probably explains the low gamma pass rates obtained. We should also stress that our phantom setup was designed for validating the method against swelling, but not for arm-positioning deviations, which are common in breast RT. Finally, this method depends on the quality of the surface scan, which may decline, mostly in posterior areas, if, for example, one of the cameras is obstructed by the gantry.

In conclusion, we proposed a method to generate a synthetic CT from a CBCT with missing data, using the information from the surface scan. It shows improved image quality, contour accuracy, and dosimetric agreement in this cohort of breast patients, suggesting potential utility for selected clinical applications pending further validation. In future works, other synthetic CT generation methods such as corrected CBCT should be validated, other sites such as head-and-neck should be investigated, and applications to dose accumulation or plan adaptation should be explored. Moreover, the robustness to the surface scan or CBCT quality should be quantified, as well as the behavior for larger anatomical changes.
