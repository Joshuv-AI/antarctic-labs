Clinical outcomes of hyaluronic acid–sorbitol injection in athletes with patellofemoral pain: Evaluation of the potential influence of body mass index

Julio Carlos Vélez de Lachica, Susana Serrano, Eduard Vidovic, Antonio Farfan

ABSTRACT
Background: Patellofemoral pain (PFP) is a frequent cause of activity-related knee pain and functional limitation in athletic populations. Elevated body mass index (BMI) may contribute to patellofemoral joint loading, but evidence on hyaluronic acid–sorbitol injection outcomes in athletes with elevated BMI remains limited. Objective: To describe longitudinal changes in knee function and symptoms over 6 months after intra-articular hyaluronic acid–sorbitol injection in athletes with PFP, and to explore whether baseline BMI was associated with post-injection improvement. Methods: This prospective, single-arm, non-randomized interventional study included 60 athletes aged 30–55 years who received a single 4 mL intra-articular hyaluronic acid–sorbitol injection. IKDC and KOOS scores were assessed at baseline, 15 days, 3 months, and 6 months. Longitudinal changes were evaluated using linear mixed models, with generalized estimating equations as a sensitivity analysis; exploratory regression and subgroup analyses assessed the association between BMI and improvement. Results: Mean IKDC and KOOS scores increased from baseline to 6 months, with estimated changes of +18.58 points for IKDC (95% CI 16.81–20.36) and +19.28 points for KOOS (95% CI 17.52–21.04; both p < 0.001). At 6 months, 75.0% of participants reached the predefined IKDC threshold for clinically meaningful improvement and 83.3% reached the KOOS threshold. Participants with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 also showed within-group improvement. BMI was not independently associated with improvement rate after adjustment for baseline score; however, the small elevated-BMI subgroup (n = 22) limited these exploratory comparisons, and a clinically relevant difference could not be excluded. Minor local adverse events occurred in 4 of 60 participants and resolved with cryotherapy. Conclusion: Athletes with PFP, including those with elevated BMI, showed consistent improvement in knee function and symptoms over 6 months following hyaluronic acid–sorbitol injection. These findings support intra-articular hyaluronic acid–sorbitol as a feasible, well-tolerated option for athletes with PFP, regardless of body weight. Controlled trials are needed to confirm the treatment’s contribution, given the single-arm design. Trial Registration: CEI/0504/PI/2022; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05450458.

Introduction Over the past few decades, a high incidence of knee pain has been documented secondary to new high-intensity sports trends characterized by longer durations and increased joint loading [ 1 – 4 ]. Improper training methods and suboptimal equipment also contribute to these disorders. Athletes who push their physical limits are particularly prone to overuse-related anterior knee pain [ 5 – 10 ]. Consequently, patellofemoral pain (PFP) is commonly associated with chronic, repetitive sports injuries [ 11 – 13 ]. This condition arises when repetitive microtrauma accumulates without adequate recovery, typically because knee loading exceeds the mechanical tolerance of the affected tissues [ 14 ]. Such injuries are a leading cause of attrition among elite athletes and substantially limit performance and training continuity. A high body mass index often coexists with increased body fat and reduced lean mass, which impairs isometric hip and knee strength and thereby increases both the risk and severity of patellofemoral pain in athletes [ 15 ]. Their debilitating nature frequently forces athletes to reduce or suspend competitive participation. Consequently, prevention strategies are increasingly critical in high-performance athletic populations [ 16 ]. Accurate diagnosis of PFP relies primarily on a thorough clinical history and physical examination, with symptoms correlated to the specific sport or activity involved; imaging is reserved for excluding alternative diagnoses rather than establishing PFP itself [ 5 , 8 ]. Currently, there are no established treatment guidelines for most PFP-related conditions in athletes [ 7 , 17 ]. Consequently, initial management typically focuses on conservative approaches including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), reducing mechanical overload, correcting gait abnormalities, improving training techniques, incorporating strengthening and stretching exercises, and optimizing footwear [ 18 ]. Furthermore, physical therapy modalities such as cryotherapy, low-level laser therapy, therapeutic ultrasound, and electrotherapy are commonly used as first-line treatments, particularly for tissue repair and inflammation reduction [ 19 – 21 ]. Evidence supporting the use of hyaluronic acid (HA) in athletes remains limited [ 22 , 23 ]. While HA is well-documented for osteoarthritis (OA), its efficacy in sports-related overuse conditions remains unproven [ 24 , 25 ]. Similarly, conclusive studies have not demonstrated that HA combined with sorbitol, a proposed antioxidant additive, accelerates pain relief or prolongs therapeutic effects in this population [ 26 ]. To date insufficient data exist on HA–sorbitol and antioxidative formulations in athletes with elevated body mass index, despite the biomechanical impact of increased weight on knee joints [ 27 , 28 ]. In patients with high mechanical joint loading or obesity, oxidative stress has been implicated as part of the underlying pathophysiology [ 29 – 32 ], and HA–sorbitol combines viscoelastic and proposed antioxidant properties that may be biologically relevant in this context, although clinical evidence remains preliminary. This prospective, single-arm, non-randomized interventional study aims to describe longitudinal within-participant changes in IKDC and KOOS scores during 6 months of follow-up after HA–sorbitol injection in athletes with PFP. As a secondary exploratory objective, we examined whether athletes with elevated BMI (≥25 kg/m 2 ) — including overweight and obesity — also showed clinically relevant within-participant improvements while maintaining usual sport activity. Because elevated BMI increases patellofemoral joint loading, this subgroup was of particular interest; although oxidative stress has been proposed as a contributing mechanism in this context, it was not directly assessed in the present study, and findings are intended to be hypothesis-generating and to inform future controlled trials. Methods Study design and ethics This prospective, single-arm, non-randomized interventional study was designed and reported in accordance with the TREND (Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomized Designs) guidelines for non-randomized interventional studies. The study was conducted at the ISSEMyM Tlalnepantla Orthopedics Department after obtaining ethical approval (CEI/0504/PI/2022), and all participants provided written informed consent. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05450458), and the authors confirm that all ongoing and related trials for this intervention are registered. Participant recruitment and enrollment took place between 1 February 2023 and 30 October 2024, with final follow-up completed on 30 April 2025. Participants Initially, 64 consecutive athletes (39 women and 25 men) with a clinical diagnosis of PFP were enrolled. PFP was diagnosed clinically based on anterior or retro-patellar pain aggravated by activities that load the patellofemoral joint (stairs, squatting, running, prolonged sitting). Imaging (ultrasound/X-ray) was used only to exclude alternative diagnoses, not to establish PFP. Symptom behavior was documented, including pain during stair ascent/descent, squatting, running, and prolonged sitting. Pain severity, symptom duration, and unilateral/bilateral involvement were recorded according to REPORT-PFP recommendations [ 33 ]. This study focused on adult athletes aged 30–55 years, who frequently present with activity-related anterior knee pain in sports-medicine practice and often present with elevated BMI, which increases joint loading and is therefore relevant to the study aims. Four participants were excluded during follow-up: two failed to attend any post-baseline evaluation, one sustained a meniscal injury, and one discontinued due to high-risk pregnancy ( Fig 1 ). The final complete-case analysis included 60 participants who completed the single injection and all four assessment points (baseline, 15 days, 3 months, 6 months). Eligibility criteria included age 30–55 years and engagement in moderate- to high-impact knee-loading sport at least three times per week, with sessions lasting a minimum of one hour. Athlete was therefore defined broadly as active adults meeting this frequency/duration threshold, and competitive level was further classified by the Tegner activity scale as professional [ 9 , 10 ], amateur [ 7 , 8 ], or recreational [ 5 , 6 , 34 , 35 ]. This broad definition includes recreational athletes and limits generalizability to elite competitive populations; external validity is discussed in Limitations. Additional eligibility required no history of surgical intervention, ligament or meniscal injury, acute or chronic infection, or lower-limb sequelae, and ability to complete 6-month follow-up without NSAID use and with full adherence to protocol. A minimum sample size of 58 was determined a priori, assuming an expected responder proportion of ~75% based on the author’s prior clinical experience with this patient population, and a two-sided 95% confidence interval half-width of ±11%. Sixty-four participants were enrolled to allow for anticipated attrition; 60 completed follow-up and were analyzed. Procedures After enrollment, each participant received a single sport-specific injury-prevention education session delivered by a physiotherapist, aimed at reducing the risk of secondary soft-tissue injury during the follow-up period; no other physiotherapy, exercise, bracing, or pharmacological intervention was provided beyond the study injection. All participants received a single intra-articular sub-patellar injection of HA–sorbitol (4 mL, 80/160; Synolis VA®, Aptissen, Switzerland), administered by a single orthopedic surgeon (principal investigator). With the knee kept fully extended, the injection site was cleaned with chlorhexidine. A 21-gauge, 1.5-inch needle was inserted just below the patella; aspiration confirmed intra-articular placement and absence of vascular entry before slow, steady injection. Light pressure was applied after withdrawal. Participant characteristics were recorded with a standardized form, including sport type and Tegner level [ 34 , 35 ]. Knee function and pain were assessed with IKDC and KOOS at baseline (day of injection), 15 days, 3 months, and 6 months. Adverse events (pain, swelling, erythema) were monitored at each visit [ 36 ]. Follow-up adherence was reinforced with scheduled phone/email reminders. Statistical analysis All analyses were performed on patient-level data. IKDC and KOOS were analyzed on a 0–100 scale; source values recorded on a 0–1 scale were multiplied by 100 before analysis. The KOOS aggregate was the mean of the five subscales (Pain, Symptoms, Activities of Daily Living, Sport/Recreation, Quality of Life). Continuous variables are presented as mean (SD); 95% CIs were calculated with the t-distribution. Categorical variables are n (%). Missing-data handling was complete-case analysis without imputation, as no IKDC/KOOS values were missing among the 60 completers. Primary repeated-measures analysis used a linear mixed model (LMM) with random intercept for participant and categorical time (baseline as reference; 15 days, 3 months, 6 months), estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Mean changes vs baseline with 95% CIs and overall time effect (likelihood-ratio χ² vs intercept-only model) were derived from the LMM. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) with exchangeable working correlation and robust standard errors served as sensitivity analysis. Descriptive paired changes (mean change, SD change, 95% CI, paired t-test, exact p, and Cohen’s dz) are also reported for comparability. Cohen’s dz (mean within-participant change/ SD of the change) is a descriptive standardized measure of within-participant change that should not be interpreted as a causal treatment effect in this single-arm study, as it may be influenced by regression to the mean, natural recovery, and selection. Clinically meaningful improvement at 6 months was a secondary responder analysis using a priori thresholds of ≥11.5 points for IKDC and ≥10 points for the KOOS aggregate used in this study. The IKDC threshold was based on published MCID estimates in PFP populations [ 37 ]. The KOOS threshold of ≥10 points was defined a priori as an approximation, consistent with published PFP subscale MCIDs of 9.9–10.2 [ 37 ] and with reported KOOS MCID ranges in knee populations [ 38 ]. Because KOOS MCID values vary by subscale, population, and clinical context, this aggregate threshold was interpreted with caution and may overestimate response in athletes with mild–moderate PFP. Responder rates are reported as proportions with exact binomial 95% CIs. Secondary analyses characterized individual post-injection trajectories after the early period. For each participant, a post-injection improvement slope (points/month) was estimated by linear regression across the three post-injection points coded as 0.5, 3, and 6 months. Baseline scores were not included in slope estimation precisely so they could subsequently be evaluated as predictors of slope; slopes are thus simplified linear summaries of the 0.5–6-month trajectory and assume approximate linearity, whereas actual recovery may be non-linear. Simple linear regression modeled post-injection slope as a function of its baseline score (IKDC slope vs baseline IKDC; KOOS slope vs baseline KOOS). Multiple linear regression then evaluated whether baseline BMI (continuous) was independently associated with slope after adjustment for baseline score (IKDC slope ~ baseline IKDC + BMI; KOOS slope ~ baseline KOOS + BMI). Coefficients, 95% CIs, and exact p-values are reported. Regression diagnostics included Shapiro–Wilk normality of residuals, Breusch–Pagan heteroscedasticity, variance inflation factor (VIF) for multicollinearity, and Cook’s distance for influence. BMI subgroup analyses (BMI < 25 vs ≥ 25 kg/m 2 ) were exploratory and descriptive: within-group mean change with 95% CI, between-group mean differences with Welch t-test (and standardized difference), and responder proportions with Fisher exact test, interpreted alongside the primary continuous BMI models. Safety was summarized descriptively ( n , %) without hypothesis testing. All tests were two-sided, exact p -values reported where appropriate (very small as p < 0.001), α = 0.05. Given the exploratory nature of secondary analyses, emphasis was placed on effect estimates and 95% CIs rather than significance alone. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied; the primary pre-specified contrast was baseline–6 months, and all other contrasts are hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory. Safety and adverse events Minor local adverse events occurred in 4 of 60 participants (6.7%): transient injection-site pain, swelling, or erythema, all resolved with local cryotherapy for 1–2 days. No pharmacological treatment was needed, no participant withdrew due to an adverse event, and no serious adverse events occurred. Discussion Principal findings In this prospective single-arm study of athletes with PFP, IKDC and KOOS scores increased progressively during 6 months of follow-up after HA–sorbitol injection when analyzed with an appropriate repeated-measures model (LMM/GEE). LMM estimated mean improvements vs baseline of +18.58 points for IKDC and +19.28 for KOOS at 6 months (both p < 0.001), with most gain by 3 months. Using predefined a priori approximations, a substantial proportion reached clinically meaningful thresholds at 6 months (75.0% for IKDC, 83.3% for KOOS). These within-participant improvements were observed consistently across both instruments and exceeded the predefined thresholds used to characterize clinically meaningful change. In keeping with the single-arm design, these time trends are described as improvements observed during follow-up after treatment, not as demonstrated treatment efficacy. Interpretation of longitudinal improvement and TREND bias considerations The temporal profile, early gains at 15 days, larger gains by 3 months, then stabilization, may reflect short-term changes in pain perception and joint tolerance followed by gradual functional recovery, potentially facilitating return to activity and confidence in sport-specific movements. However, without a placebo/sham, rehabilitation only, or no-treatment comparator, we cannot attribute improvements solely to the injection. As TREND emphasizes for non-randomized interventional studies, observed changes may include placebo/expectation effects, regression to the mean (higher scores on repeat testing among those with initially lower scores), natural symptom fluctuation, and unsupervised load modification. PFP is known for a fluctuating, often self-limiting course, so regression to the mean is a particularly relevant alternative explanation. The consistency of the LMM and GEE sensitivity analyses, together with the observed inter-time correlations, supports the robustness of the longitudinal pattern, while causal inference remains precluded by the single-arm design. Baseline function and improvement rate Lower baseline IKDC/KOOS scores were associated with greater post-injection improvement rates, likely reflecting greater room for measurable change among those with worse baseline status and possibly more noticeable functional gains among more symptomatic athletes. Baseline functional status was the strongest predictor of post-injection slope in the regression models. This may inform future stratification strategies and interpretation of expected recovery patterns in active patients but should not be interpreted as a causal effect of baseline status on treatment response. Influence of elevated BMI A secondary aim was to explore whether elevated BMI was associated with different longitudinal trajectories. Participants with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m 2 showed clinically relevant within-group improvements, but mean changes and responder proportions were numerically lower than in BMI < 25 kg/m 2 . However, exploratory between-group differences, especially KOOS 4.47 points, 95% CI −0.20 to 9.13, p = 0.060, should be viewed as a non-significant trend with substantial uncertainty. With n = 38 vs 22, post-hoc power for observed IKDC (d = 0.41) and KOOS (d = 0.51) differences was only 32% and 46%, and a Type II error cannot be excluded; 62–97 participants per group would be needed for 80% power to detect differences of this magnitude, whereas power was > 98% to detect the full MCID of 10–11.5 points. Given this limited power, the appropriate interpretation is that this exploratory analysis did not detect a statistically significant BMI association, rather than that BMI has no effect on treatment response. This is clinically relevant because BMI is often considered a negative prognostic factor via mechanical load and metabolic inflammation. Yet in athletic populations BMI is an imprecise proxy for adiposity: elevated BMI may reflect greater muscle mass rather than excess fat [ 39 , 40 ]. Ferreira et al. have shown that body fat and skeletal muscle mass, but not BMI alone, relate to pain mechanisms and functional capacity in young adults with PFP [ 15 , 39 , 40 ]. Future studies should therefore include direct body composition measures (fat mass %, waist circumference, imaging) to clarify whether adiposity, muscle mass, or loading patterns modify outcomes. Biological plausibility and potential mechanisms The rationale for HA–sorbitol combines HA’s viscoelastic/lubricating properties with sorbitol’s proposed antioxidant action via reactive oxygen species scavenging, relevant in mechanically stressed joints and in elevated BMI states where low-grade inflammation may heighten vulnerability [ 29 – 32 ]. This is plausible for athletes with repetitive patellofemoral loading and high training demands. The longitudinal improvements observed are consistent with this rationale, but without synovial biomarkers, imaging, or biomechanical endpoints, mechanisms remain speculative and require direct evaluation in future studies. Psychological and functional considerations PFP is increasingly recognized as multifactorial, involving local mechanical/biological factors and psychological/behavioral influences — pain self-efficacy, fear of movement, symptom persistence, training modification, and confidence during activity. Glaviano et al. reported associations between pain self-efficacy and physical activity in PFP [ 41 ], Pazzinatto et al. noted that kinesiophobia may emerge as symptoms persist [ 42 ], and a critically appraised topic found hip/knee strength changes were not consistently associated with PFP improvement despite functional gains [ 43 ]. In this context, the randomized trial by Bagheri and Naderi et al. offers a relevant comparison [ 44 ], where adding an 8-week mindfulness program to exercise therapy improved pain, knee function, fear of movement, pain catastrophizing, and coping versus exercise alone in recreational female runners with PFP. That trial illustrates that behavioral/psychological adjuncts can influence the same IKDC/KOOS domains we measured. Although we did not assess psychological variables, strength, or return-to-sport confidence, our IKDC/KOOS gains may reflect changes in pain, function, confidence, activity tolerance, or their combination. Future comprehensive assessment in athletes with PFP should integrate patient-reported outcomes, physical performance, training load, psychological factors, and return-to-sport metrics. Safety and tolerability The single HA–sorbitol injection was well tolerated over 6 months. The 6.7% rate of minor, self-limited local reactions, managed with cryotherapy and without pharmacologic treatment or discontinuation, supports feasibility in active individuals, while larger studies are needed to detect uncommon adverse events. The absence of systemic or serious events is reassuring for athletic populations where downtime and delayed return to sport are key considerations. Limitations First, the single-arm design without a placebo, sham injection, rehabilitation-only, or untreated comparator precludes causal inference; observed improvements are longitudinal within-participant changes after treatment and may reflect placebo, regression to the mean, natural fluctuation, expectation, and unsupervised load modification. Second, follow-up was limited to 6 months, useful for short- to intermediate-term description but insufficient for durability and recurrence during continued athletic participation; ≥12 months is needed. Third, the sample was modest, particularly for BMI subgroups ( n = 22 with BMI ≥ 25), limiting power to detect clinically relevant differences (KOOS p = 0.060 trend; post-hoc power 32–46% for observed differences; possible Type II error). Fourth, BMI is an imprecise adiposity measure in athletes and may reflect lean mass; future work should include body composition as noted. Fifth, the single standardized preventive education session (injury-prevention, not therapeutic PFP rehabilitation) was delivered to reduce secondary exclusions, but any therapeutic co-intervention, bracing/taping, or unsupervised activity modification was not controlled and remains a potential confounder separable only with a randomized design. Sixth, we did not include objective biomechanical, imaging, return-to-sport, analgesic-use, rehabilitation-adherence, strength, psychological, or inflammatory/oxidative biomarker endpoints. Seventh, post-injection slopes are simplified linear summaries across 0.5, 3, and 6 months and assume approximate linearity; true recovery may be non-linear, as suggested by larger early mean gains. Finally, MCID thresholds were a priori approximations extrapolated from subscale and surgical (tibial tubercle osteotomy) populations [ 37 ] and may overestimate responder proportions in athletes with mild–moderate PFP. Future directions Randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these descriptive findings and isolate the contribution of HA–sorbitol beyond natural history, placebo, and standard rehabilitation. Comparator groups should include sham injections, structured exercise-based rehabilitation, HA without sorbitol, and other injectables, with ≥12-month follow-up. Larger samples would support robust evaluation of BMI, body composition, sex, sport type, baseline severity, training load, psychological factors, strength, biomechanics, and return-to-sport as modifiers, reflecting PFP’s multifactorial nature and helping identify athletes most likely to benefit from intra-articular therapies. Conclusion Athletes with patellofemoral pain, including those with elevated body mass index, showed consistent improvement in knee function and symptoms over the six months following hyaluronic acid–sorbitol injection. These findings support intra-articular hyaluronic acid–sorbitol as a feasible and well-tolerated option for athletes with patellofemoral pain, regardless of body weight. As a single-arm study, this work should be followed by controlled trials to confirm the treatment’s contribution to these outcomes. Supporting information S1 Dataset Anonymized patient dataset.
