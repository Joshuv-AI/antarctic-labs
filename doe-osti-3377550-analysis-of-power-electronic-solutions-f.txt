Executive Summary
Wave energy is a largely untapped energy source with the potential to provide 290 TWh per year to the conti-
nental United States (Kilcher, Fogarty, and Lawson 2021). As a new technology, wave energy converters are
less efficient and reliable compared to established energy sources, leaving the vast energy of the sea largely
unutilized. Wave energy is uniquely inconsistent, with large fluctuations. Paired with harsh operating envi-
ronments and difficulty in repair, the power electronics designed to harvest wave energy need to be reliable,
cost-effective, and able to work in a wide operating band. The literature on existing power electronics in wave
energy converters and lessons learned across other industries provide a framework of topologies to simu-
late. WEC-Sim, an open-source hydrodynamic platform in MATLAB Simulink developed by the National
Laboratory of the Rockies, was used to simulate a wide range of power electronics under a variety of wave
conditions. This paper provides a comprehensive investigation into existing wave energy power electronics
converters, state-of-the-art topologies that can be applied to wave energy, and novel solutions to wave conver-
sion.

1 Introduction
Wave energy is a largely untapped energy source with the potential to provide 290 TWh per year to the con-
tinental United States (Kilcher, Fogarty, and Lawson 2021). The inclusion of wave energy can diversify
and provide additional reliable power to the energy portfolio. In their current state, wave energy converters
(WECs) are a nascent technology that researchers and industry are actively working toward improving. The
availability and maturity of power electronics technology is a significant barrier to effectively harvesting
usable electrical power from wave energy.
The purpose of this paper is to investigate the state of power electronic converters used in existing WECs
and perform analyses on optimal topologies, both novel and existing, to reduce bottlenecks economically and
efficiently in WEC power converter designs.
Among water power technologies, wave energy can support the ocean economy by providing alternative,
resilient energy solutions to remote communities in coastal areas and islands, providing power to far-offshore
applications and quickly deploying energy for life-saving equipment in disaster response. On a larger scale,
wave energy has the potential to be a meaningful part of diversified grid generation sources as a reliable energy
source.
Large-scale grid and microgrid integration of WECs must be stable and predictable. Due to the pulsating
nature of the resource, WECs greatly benefit from incorporated storage elements to provide stable energy
output to the grid. Interconnection between grid assets is a critical consideration in the design of WECs.

 1

2 WEC Structure
A commonly studied WEC structure works by actuating a hydrodynamic body or prime mover that drives
a traditional generator to produce highly variable alternating voltage (Czech and Bauer 2012; Yaramasu et
al. 2015). To support power quality management alternating voltage is typically converted to DC voltage,
which is then used for DC/AC applications. Power electronic converters are needed for two to three stages
of power conversion in WECs. The rectifier can be either an active rectifier that uses power semiconductor
switches or a passive rectifier that uses power diodes. A DC/DC converter regulates the input voltage, which
can be constant or varying depending on the rectifier choice, to constant DC voltage. Figure 1 shows a block
diagram of a common WEC power take-off (PTO) system. The WEC system, in addition to the turbine, gener-
ator, and power conversion units, may contain a storage system on the output of the second stage. The storage
system can be a short-duration storage—such as a small battery—or an ultracapacitor bank, required for de-
livering a shortfall of energy, considering the extreme intermittent nature of wave energy. The short-duration
energy can be replaced or further supported by long-term energy storage solutions depending on individual
device operation or specific application.

 Figure 1. Typical WEC PTO system involving a rotary generator, conversion stages, and energy storage

2.1 WEC Power Converter Requirements
Significant challenges arise in the electrical conversion stages because of the pulse-based, irregular nature
of waves. Although wave resources are reliable and predictable over long periods of time, the energy poten-
tial is instantaneously highly variable, constantly accelerating, changing direction, and crossing through zero
power. Furthermore, WECs experience significantly higher peak-to-average ratios in voltage and power input
compared to other renewables. The maximum input of solar power may exceed the rated power by only 1.4x,
compared to 10x for waves. Figure 2 shows the high variability typically seen in the speed (top) and voltage
(bottom) waveforms from WECs, simulated in 10-s wave conditions. This variability increases the challenges
of component sizing and survivability strategies. To achieve reliable operation in both small-scale and grid
scale applications, converter topologies to meet these challenging requirements need to be thoroughly investi-
gated, built, and tested. Moreover, because WECs operate in harsh marine environments that are challenging to
complete repair work in, the power electronics must be ultra-reliable, redundant, and fault-tolerant.

3 Literature Review of Power Electronics Topologies for WECs
The design of power electronics topologies for WEC PTO systems should prioritize low capital costs, high
reliability, and modularity with redundancy to minimize maintenance requirements. Additionally, a simple
structure utilizing commercially available components is essential for broader industry adoption, along with
high efficiency across a wide operating range. Based on these design objectives, conventional and potentially
applicable power electronics topologies for WEC systems are reviewed in this section. Key comparison met-
rics of the topologies selected for simulation are given in a table in Section 4.

3.1 Conventional Topologies
A widely used topology in WEC PTO systems is the back-to-back converter, which consists of a three-phase,
two-level voltage source converter (2L-VSC), a DC-link capacitor, and a three-phase, two-level voltage source
inverter, as shown in Figure 3a. This topology offers a simple structure, ease of control, and high industry
adoption, as demonstrated in several WEC systems, such as LIMPET and Wave Dragon (Czech and Bauer
2012). Redundancy is typically achieved by paralleling switches or converter modules; however, this approach
increases both hardware and control complexity (Yaramasu et al. 2015). Additionally, the high input power
fluctuation inherent in WEC applications necessitates oversizing the energy buffer and semiconductor devices,
adversely affecting cost-effectiveness and reducing efficiency at low power input levels (Veurink et al. 2023;
Kovaltchouk et al. 2015). Research advancements for this topology often focus on novel control strategies or
the integration of additional energy storage or buffer circuits to enhance performance (Xiao, Huang, and Kang
2016; Kovaltchouk et al. 2015).

Another commonly utilized power electronics topology is the passive diode rectifier with a DC/DC conversion
stage, as illustrated in Figure 3b. This topology features reliability and efficiency by reducing the number of

active switches and DC-link capacitors compared to the 2L-VSC (Artal-Sevil et al. 2019). A inverting single-
switch SEPIC converter can be used in applications with a floating DC bus. It is widely employed in small-
scale wind energy systems and certain WEC applications, such as the PowerBuoy (Yaramasu et al. 2015).
However, similar to the 2L-VSC, its components must be rated for peak power production and offer limited
redundancy. Moreover, the unidirectional power flow characteristic of this topology restricts the capability for
reactive wave loading control (Artal-Sevil et al. 2019).

3.2 Current Source Converters
Current source converters, shown in Figure 4, are a duality of voltage source converters, replacing the DC-
link capacitor with a DC-link choke. In practical applications, most current source converters operate at the
megawatt scale and utilize high-power thyristors as active switches that feature a higher lifetime than IGBTs
and power MOSFETs. For lower-power applications in WEC systems, reverse-blocking silicon and wide-
bandgap devices present potential alternatives; however, these technologies are not yet widely commercialized
(McDonald et al. 2019). Current source converters offer high reliability and low complexity, but the low
switching frequency of high-power thyristors could limit their dynamic response, posing challenges for ad-
vanced grid support (McDonald et al. 2019; Czech and Bauer 2012). Current source converters in wave energy
remain relatively unexplored, with one notable example being the Archimedes Waveswing as a grid-connected
converter (Prado and Polinder 2013).

 Figure 4. Current source converter for WEC systems

3.3 Synchronous Rectifier
Also known as “unfolding converters,” synchronous rectifiers, illustrated in Figure 5, utilize semiconductor
switches acting as diodes, maintaining their switching state for about π radians in single-phase systems and
about π/3 in three-phase systems (Bubovich et al. 2022; Yoo et al. 2009). Unlike back-to-back converters,
which employ a stabilized DC bus, synchronous rectifiers are connected to the inverter with a full-wave rec-
tified unregulated DC bus, eliminating the need for bulky DC-link capacitors. This topology offers improved
efficiency by reducing the number of switching compared to active rectifiers and by having lower forward
voltage and on-state resistance of the active switch compared to diode rectifiers. Also, this topology features
higher reliability because semiconductor switches with higher reliability but lower switching dynamics can
be applied. Additionally, synchronous rectifiers enable bidirectional power transfer, facilitating reactive load
control in WEC systems, allowing to control for optimal hydrodynamic efficiency. This topology has been ap-
plied to photovoltaic inverters and battery storage applications connected to a single-phase grid with relatively
low power ratings to achieve high efficiency (Bubovich et al. 2022; Cho et al. 2016). Furthermore, research
efforts have explored the potential applications of this topology in three-phase grid-connected systems (Yoo
et al. 2009; Yelaverthi et al. 2019).

3.4 AC/AC Converters
AC/AC converters, such as matrix converters or cycloconverters, directly generate AC voltage from an AC
input without a DC link, as shown in Figure 6. These converters offer advantages such as compact system

volume, low current harmonics, and high efficiency. However, a key drawback is the large number of semicon-
ductor switches and gate drivers required for their operation (Kang et al. 2011). AC/AC converters address one
of the primary challenges in wave energy conversion: the need for frequency conversion. Their ability to gen-
erate a stable output frequency from a variable-frequency input is particularly beneficial for WEC generators,
which experience continuous speed variations due to fluctuating wave profiles. Although AC/AC converters
effectively regulate frequency, they lack the ability to boost voltage during low-energy wave conditions with-
out incorporating energy storage, thereby limiting their capability to provide grid support and restricting them
to grid-following operation (Burhanudin et al. 2022). To overcome this limitation, research has explored the
integration of cascaded inductive energy storage to enable voltage boost functionality (Chauhan et al. 2016).
Although this approach enhances the feasibility of AC/AC converters for wave energy applications, it also
introduces increased harmonic distortion, necessitating additional filter capacitors. Consequently, the require-
ment for bulky inductors and capacitors diminishes some of the inherent advantages of AC/AC conversion.

3.5 Multilevel Converters
Multilevel converters (MLCs) extend the conventional 2L-VSC by introducing multiple voltage levels through
additional switching devices. The primary MLC topologies include neutral point clamped, flying capacitor,
and modular/cascaded structures (Poorfakhraei, Narimani, and Emadi 2021). Figure 7 shows example config-
urations of MLCs in a WEC system. Compared to two-level converters, MLCs offer advantages such as lower
current and voltage harmonics, higher efficiency, and reduced voltage stress on individual switches (Sadowski,
n.d.; Müller et al. 2017). These characteristics are particularly beneficial for WEC applications, where wide
operational voltage ranges require high efficiency and low total harmonic distortion. However, MLCs also
present challenges, including an increased component count, which leads to higher costs, reduced reliability,

 6

and more complex control requirements. A high capacitor and switch count leads to lower reliability, higher
cost, and complex control. As the number of levels increases, MLCs become more susceptible to faults, with
a single device failure potentially rendering the PTO inoperable unless fault-tolerant control mechanisms are
implemented (Poorfakhraei, Narimani, and Emadi 2021). MLCs are commonly employed in medium- to high-
power applications. In electric vehicles, they are utilized to minimize conduction losses as battery voltages
continue to increase (Poorfakhraei, Narimani, and Emadi 2021). MLCs are also widely adopted in photo-
voltaic systems, particularly for DC-link voltages exceeding 1.5 kV and power ratings in the tens of kilowatts
(Ghosh and Parzhuber, n.d.). In wind power applications, megawatt-scale wind turbines frequently incorporate
neutral point clamped rectifiers and inverters for efficient power conversion (Yaramasu et al. 2015).

3.6 Modular Converters
Modular multilevel converters (MMCs) are a subset of MLCs that consist of multiple independent submod-
ules, offering high reliability through modularity and redundancy. Moreover, MMCs can withstand peak-
energy wave conditions without requiring oversized components, as voltage is distributed among the sub-
modules (Pacheco, Brito, and Oliveira 2015). However, a higher number of submodules increases control
complexity and implementation costs, necessitating a trade-off consideration during system design.
A widely adopted MMC topology in industry utilizes series-connected half- or full-bridge submodules, com-
monly employed in megawatt-scale wind turbines at medium- to high-voltage transmission levels. Although
MMCs provide benefits such as high redundancy and low switching losses, these advantages diminish in low-
power applications due to the limited number of submodules, leading to lower adoption in such scenarios
(Catalán et al. 2023).
Research on MMC applications in WEC PTO systems has primarily explored configurations where multiple
PTO-rectifier-inverter blocks are connected in parallel, either directly to the grid or via a central grid-interface
inverter (Artal-Sevil et al. 2019; Mantellini et al. 2020; Czech and Bauer 2012; Jafarishiadeh, Farasat, and
Mehraeen 2017). Figure 8 shows the modular power converter configuration that can be employed in WEC
systems. A promising MMC topology for WEC systems is the input-parallel-output-series (IPOS) or input-
series-output-parallel (ISOP) configuration with isolated DC/DC submodules, such as dual active bridges
(DABs) and LLC resonant converters. These architectures incorporate medium- to high-frequency transformer
links, enabling galvanic isolation between the PTO and the grid while significantly reducing the system foot-
print. Additionally, they facilitate a high boost/buck ratio with improved efficiency, making them a viable
solution for WEC applications (Darwish, Holliday, and Finney 2017).

3.7 Split-Phase Rectifiers
Circuit modularity can also come from multiple converters arranged in series or parallel. These can be ar-
ranged to manage three-phase power in unison or with separate winding groups. Paralleled windings give the
power electronics great flexibility and fault tolerance. If one converter fails, the others maintain operability

4 Comparative Analysis With WEC-Sim Simulation
Wave energy research and development is done with the ultimate goal of developing grid applications that
rival other energy sources. Microgrids differ significantly in load demand and renewable availability. Wave
energy can provide an additional energy source for coastal microgrid communities. In its current state, wave
energy is better suited for powering small-scale ocean-based industrial applications (i.e., desalination, ocean
monitoring). For this reason, the following simulations focus on small-scale applications. Although many of
the topologies studied in the literature review were ultimately for grid-tied applications, Numerous existing
and novel power converter topologies were investigated following the framework shown in Figure 9. The
pulsing three-phase output of a WEC generator, known as Wild AC, was fed to the device under test, which
powers a 3.3-kW load and 45-Ahr battery.

 Figure 9. Testbed framework for topologies explored

Existing and novel converter topologies were compared through literature review in Section 3 and analyzed for
simulation based on their technology readiness level, wide-range operability, fabrication cost, and reliability.
The topologies were broken down into rectification and DC/DC conversion. The following topologies were
selected for further simulation:
 • Diode rectifier with non-isolated buck-boost converter
 • Three-phase two-/three-level voltage source rectifier
 • Diode rectifier with a DAB
 • Split-phase diode rectifier configuration

4.1 Simulation Parameters
WEC-Sim, a Simulink-based open-source platform for simulating the hydrodynamic response of a WEC
under various wave conditions, was used to simulate the power conversion topologies connected to a 6-foot
hexagonal body with a winch-style PTO. The PTO was controlled using a consistent damping coefficient
and wave profiles across different topologies to facilitate a comparative analysis of energy generation and
efficiency. The damping coefficient was consistent across all wave profiles and dictated a linear power draw
with the generator velocity. Both the generator and the power electronics were simulated in the Simulink
environment. The parameters of the 10-kW permanent magnet synchronous generator used for this simulation
are shown in Table 1.

target system to achieve optimal damping and maximize energy generation.

4.2 Diode Rectifier and Buck-Boost
A conventional diode bridge rectifier with a buck-boost converter was the simplest topology simulated, as
shown in Figure 3b. The buck-boost converter controls the current drawn from the generator through the
rectifier to keep the damping factor steady as required. Figure 10a and 10b plot simulation results in a low-
energy wave state (1-m height and 5-s period) and a high-energy wave state (4 m height and 5 s period). Table
2 summarizes the results.
The diode rectifier with the buck-boost stage demonstrates high efficiency at low- and high-energy wave states
while operating within a wide range of input voltages. The ability to step down the voltage and the indepen-
dence from DC-link voltage limitations allow the topology to accept a wide range of input voltages, ideal for
wave energy. Additionally, diode rectifiers and buck-boost converters are simple and widely adopted across
industry, making them reliable and cost-effective to implement. Their simplicity comes at the cost of lower
generator efficiency from the high levels of harmonics characteristic of diode bridges. Additionally, diode
bridges are unidirectional, limiting four-quadrant control of the generator. In simple, small-scale applications,
unidirectional power flow is acceptable but may pose a significant limitation in high-power WECs. Diode rec-
tifiers and buck-boost converters also lack modularity, resulting in single points of failure that would require
costly maintenance.

4.3 Active Rectifier
By replacing the diode rectifier with an active pulse-width modulation rectifier, the wild AC from the genera-
tor is controlled without an additional DC/DC stage, as shown in Figure 3a. Simulation results are plotted in
Figure 11a and 11b and summarized in Table 2. Active rectifiers are a well-proven topology, seen in a variety
of industrial applications. They are bidirectional, allowing for four-quadrant control, unlike passive rectifiers.
Active rectifiers are limited in their capability to step-down the input voltage before passing power to the bat-
tery. Deactivation of the device is required for survivability during high-energy conditions. In Figure 11b, the
gray areas represent where the device is deactivated and no power is being generated. In low-energy condi-
tions, the active rectifier improves upon the diode bridge rectifier in efficiency and overall power generation. In
high-energy conditions, power capture is significantly reduced due to the inability to stabilize the DC bus and
capture peak power generated. During these states, the generator freewheels, introducing a source of potential

4.4 Three-Level Active Rectifier
In this study, a three-level neutral point clamped active rectifier was also simulated to assess its feasibility. The
simulation results are presented in Figure 12. Three-level rectifiers are typically employed in systems with
relatively high voltage ratings, generally exceeding 1 kV. Due to the increased number of active (switching)
components and the complexity of control, along with its vulnerability to single-point failures, this topology is
not commonly used in systems operating below this voltage threshold.
The simulation results indicated that energy generation was comparable to that of two-level active rectifiers
but with lower efficiency. Both the two-level and three-level rectifier simulations utilized 600-V rated power
MOSFETs, and at a relatively low DC-link voltage of 400 V, the three-level rectifier did not demonstrate a
performance advantage over the two-level configuration. Additionally, in high-wave-energy-input scenarios,
energy generation was further reduced due to DC-link voltage limitations. However, the three-level topology
offers the potential for higher voltage operation compared to two-level rectifiers, suggesting its suitability for
WEC systems with higher voltage ratings.

4.5 Diode Rectifier With a DAB
The DC/DC conversion stage in the power conversion topology can be replaced with an isolated DC/DC
converter, such as a DAB or an LLC resonant converter. To support a wide operational range, the topology

 11

that incorporates a DAB converter, as shown in Figure 13, was selected for simulation in this work. The power
transferred through the DC/DC stage was determined based on generator speed and a pre-defined damping
factor. The simulation results for two wave input cases are presented in Figure 14, with energy generation and
efficiency outcomes summarized in Table 2.

Although DAB converters offer a wide operating range, achieving high efficiency across this range remains a
challenge. In particular, the DAB suffers from reduced efficiency under light load conditions due to circulating
currents in the high-frequency transformer. Advanced control strategies, such as triple-phase shift modulation
and burst-mode control (Hou and Li 2020; Oggier and Ordonez 2016), can mitigate circulating currents and
enhance soft switching across a wider operational range. The simulation results indicate that the DAB-based
topology demonstrated strong performance, achieving high energy generation with efficiency comparable to
conventional topologies. In addition, this topology provides galvanic isolation, enabling the removal of the
grid-side transformer, which can further reduce the volume of the system.
In terms of reliability, the DAB module supports fault-tolerant operation, ensuring survivability under single-
point failures (Shi et al. 2022). Furthermore, as discussed earlier, the DAB stage can be configured in ISOP or
IPOS architectures, enhancing reliability through redundancy and modularity in high-power WEC systems.

4.6 Split Winding
The generator can be split into multiple winding groupings by designing three smaller generators to share the
same shaft—including multiple ports for each phase—or by other means. In this simulation, three windings
were separated and controlled with independent, connected active bridge pulse-width modulation rectifiers.

4.6.1 Series Configuration
The windings were connected in series as shown in Figure 15. From the simulation results shown in Figure
16a and Figure 16b, connecting split windings in series is more viable in low-energy wave states. To achieve
a DC bus of 400 V, each rectifier has an individual DC bus of 133 V. As with the two-level active rectifier, the
device must deactivate when it is unable to keep the voltage below 133 V. The lowered voltage further reduces
the effectiveness of the topology at high-energy wave states compared with a single active rectifier. The energy
captured at low states is less than that of a single rectifier.

4.6.2 Parallel Configuration
The windings were connected in parallel as shown in Figure 17. In this configuration, each rectifier shares
the same 400-V DC bus. This connection has the same voltage limitation as the single rectifier but is able
to withstand a higher input voltage than the series-connected rectifiers as seen in Figure 18. During points
of high-voltage input, the series configuration does not draw current as the parallel configuration does. Both
the series- and parallel-connected rectifiers have increased switching loss and higher cost/complexity due to
the large number of components. The largest benefit of this topology is the modularity of the design. Failure
of a single semiconductor device does not halt operation of the WEC, which allows the WEC to continue
producing power while in a fault state, increasing its operation time and the cost-effectiveness of long-term
deployments.

5 Conclusion
Wave energy is a nascent technology that has the potential to support diversity and reliability in grid and off-
grid applications. Power electronics design remains a challenge in harvesting the highest and most efficient
energy from the potential of a wave. Ultimately, the solution will be a combination of converter topologies and
will vary based on end use, power levels, and requirements. This paper provides a framework for the analysis
of benefits and drawbacks of several power electronic topologies applied to wave energy based on model
simulation for a WEC design. Low-complexity designs, such as diode and active rectifiers, benefit from low-
cost and robust operation. Modular designs with greater switch count and control complexity capture a larger
portion of the wave potential, however are costly and less robust than low switch count designs. The results
found in this paper should be a starting point for a WEC design. Ultimately, the optimal solution will require
compromises and consider various design constraints driven by cost, tolerance to downtime, and application.
