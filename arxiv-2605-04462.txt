Stabilization by a background magnetic field: global well-posedness of the compressible isentropic ideal MHD equations with velocity damping
Liening Qiao, Jiahong Wu, Fuyi Xu †, Xiaoping Zhai

Abstract
. We study the Cauchy problem for the three-dimensional isentropic compressible ideal (inviscid and non-resistive) magnetohydrodynamic equations with velocity damping on the periodic torus 𝕋 3 \mathbb{T}^{3} . The system admits a steady equilibrium consisting of a constant density ρ ¯ \bar{\rho} and a uniform background magnetic field ω ∈ ℝ 3 \omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3} . We prove that this equilibrium is nonlinearly stable. More precisely, we show that if the initial data are a sufficiently small perturbation of ( ρ ¯ , 𝟎 , ω ) (\bar{\rho},\mathbf{0},\omega) in the Sobolev space H N ​ ( 𝕋 3 ) H^{N}(\mathbb{T}^{3}) with N ≥ 6 ​ r + 4 N\geq 6r+4 , and if ω \omega satisfies a Diophantine condition, then the system admits a unique global smooth solution. Moreover, the perturbations decay algebraically in time. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first global well-posedness result for the multi-dimensional isentropic compressible ideal MHD system. The proof reveals a hidden dissipation mechanism: although neither the density equation nor the magnetic field equation contains explicit diffusion or damping, the coupling between the velocity and the magnetic field through the background field ω \omega , combined with a Diophantine–Poincaré inequality, generates effective dissipation for both the density perturbation and the magnetic field perturbation, which together with the velocity damping yields global regularity and time decay.

1.
Introduction and Main Result

1.1.
Physical background and motivation

The dynamics of compressible inviscid fluids are governed by the
Euler equations for conservation of mass and momentum. In many
physically relevant settings the fluid moves through a porous medium,
or is subject to a linear drag force proportional to the local
velocity, a mechanism referred to as velocity damping.
The resulting system,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\rho+\mathrm{div}(\rho\mathbf{u})=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{u})+\mathrm{div}(\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u})+\kappa\rho\mathbf{u}+\nabla P(\rho)=0,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

arises in the modelling of gas flow through porous media
(Darcy’s law
[
15
,
16
]
), the propagation of acoustic waves in
dissipative media, and the large-time dynamics of cosmological matter
distributions
[
45
]
. The damping term
\kappa\rho\mathbf{u}

(
\kappa>0
) plays a fundamentally regularizing role. It dissipates
kinetic energy and prevents the formation of shocks that would otherwise
develop from smooth data in finite time for the undamped Euler system.
Seminal works by Hsiao–Luo
[
15
]
, Hsiao–Serre
[
16
]
,
Huang–Pan
[
19
]
, and Huang–Marcati–Pan
[
20
]
established
convergence of solutions toward the nonlinear diffusion wave described by
the porous medium equation in the one and multi-dimensional settings.
For smooth small-data solutions on the torus, Sideris–Thomases–Wang

[
45
]
proved global existence and exponential decay for the
three-dimensional problem. Thus, on the periodic domain
\mathbb{T}^{3}
,
the system (
1.1
) is globally well-posed for small
perturbations of a constant state, and the damping is solely responsible
for this regularity.

Electrically conducting compressible fluids such as plasmas, liquid metals,
ionised gases are described by the magnetohydrodynamic (MHD)
equations, which couple the compressible Euler equations with Maxwell’s
equations of electromagnetism via the Lorentz force. In the
ideal regime, where both the kinematic
viscosity
\mu,\lambda
and the magnetic resistivity
\nu
vanish, the
governing system with velocity damping on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
is

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\rho+\mathrm{div}(\rho\mathbf{u})=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{u})+\mathrm{div}(\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u})+\kappa\rho\mathbf{u}+\nabla P=\mathbf{H}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{H}-\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla|\mathbf{H}|^{2},\\
&\partial_{t}\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{H}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{H}\,\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{u},\\
&\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{H}=0,\\
&(\rho,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{H})|_{t=0}=(\rho_{0},\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{H}_{0}).\end{aligned}\right.

(1.2)

Here
\rho
,
\mathbf{u}
, and
\mathbf{H}
denote density, velocity, and
magnetic field,
\kappa>0
is the damping coefficient, and the isentropic
pressure
P=P(\rho)
is a smooth, strictly increasing function. The
right-hand side of the momentum equation is the Lorentz body force
generated by the magnetic field, while the induction equation for

\mathbf{H}
encodes flux freezing property of ideal
(perfectly conducting) MHD, namely in the absence of resistivity the magnetic
field lines are transported with the fluid.

System (
1.2
) models a broad class of physical phenomena including
magnetised plasma dynamics in fusion devices

[
3
,
28
]
, astrophysical accretion discs

[
42
]
, liquid-metal flows in metallurgical
engineering, and geophysical dynamo processes in planetary interiors.
Even though (
1.2
) is the most transparent,
member of the compressible MHD hierarchy, the absence of viscosity and resistivity makes (
1.2
) the most
challenging mathematically.

1.2.
Equilibrium state and the stability problem

A fundamental and physically natural
steady state
of
(
1.2
) is the configuration

(\rho,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{H})\equiv(\bar{\rho},\,\mathbf{0},\,\omega),

(1.3)

where
\bar{\rho}>0
is a constant reference density and

\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
is a constant background
magnetic field. This state represents a perfectly conducting fluid at
rest, threaded by a uniform magnetic field. It is easily verified
that (
1.3
) satisfies (
1.2
) identically.

The central question of this paper is whether the
equilibrium (
1.3
) is nonlinearly stable in the class
of smooth solutions. Given initial data that are a small perturbation of

(\bar{\rho},\mathbf{0},\omega)
in a high-order Sobolev space, does a
unique global smooth solution exist and remain close to the equilibrium
for all time? In the absence of viscosity or magnetic diffusion, this
question is genuinely open in general. The present paper provides an
affirmative answer by showing that the combination of velocity damping
and a background magnetic field satisfying a Diophantine condition
(see (
1.11
) below) is sufficient to guarantee global
regularity and algebraic decay.

1.3.
Perturbation formulation

Setting
\kappa=1
for simplicity (the value of
\kappa
does not affect
the analysis) and writing the perturbation of the magnetic field as

\mathbf{B}:=\mathbf{H}-\omega
, we introduce

a:=\rho-\bar{\rho},\qquad\mathbf{u},\qquad\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{H}-\omega.

(1.4)

Substituting into (
1.2
), the perturbed system reads

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}a+\mathrm{div}(\rho\mathbf{u})=0,\\[4.0pt]
&\rho(\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u})+\rho\mathbf{u}+\nabla P=\underbrace{\omega\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B}-\nabla(\omega\cdot\mathbf{B})}_{\text{linear in }\mathbf{B}}+\underbrace{\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B}-\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla|\mathbf{B}|^{2}}_{\text{nonlinear}},\\[4.0pt]
&\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B}=\underbrace{\omega\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\omega\,\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{u}}_{\text{linear in }\mathbf{u}}+\underbrace{\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{B}\,\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{u}}_{\text{nonlinear}},\\[4.0pt]
&\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{B}=0,\\[4.0pt]
&(\rho,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})|_{t=0}=(\rho_{0},\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{B}_{0}).\end{aligned}\right.

(1.5)

Taking
\bar{\rho}=1
for notational convenience, denoting
\beta:=P^{\prime}(1)>0
,
and separating linear from nonlinear contributions, (
1.5
) can be written as

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}a+\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{u}=f_{1},\\[3.0pt]
&\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\beta\nabla a+\mathbf{u}=\omega\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B}-\nabla(\omega\cdot\mathbf{B})+f_{2},\\[3.0pt]
&\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}=\omega\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\omega\,\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{u}+f_{3},\\[3.0pt]
&\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{B}=0,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.6)

where the nonlinear remainder terms are

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle:=-\mathrm{div}(a\mathbf{u}),

(1.7)

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle:=-a\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}-(P^{\prime}(\rho)-\beta)\nabla a-\rho\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B}-\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla|\mathbf{B}|^{2},

\displaystyle f_{3}

\displaystyle:=-\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{B}\,\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{u}.

1.4.
Main difficulties

The global analysis of (
1.5
) faces several difficulties
that are different from those encountered in the viscous or resistive settings.

The continuity equation
\partial_{t}a+\mathrm{div}(\rho\mathbf{u})=0

contains no diffusive or damping term acting directly on
a
. In the
compressible viscous setting, the combination of viscous stresses
and the pressure gradient yields an effective viscous flux

F=\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{u}-P
with better regularity properties than
its constituents individually
[
13
,
14
]
, and this regularity
is used to extract a damping effect for the density. In the ideal
setting (
\mu=\lambda=0
) this device is entirely unavailable; see

[
9
,
54
]
for discussions of why the effective-flux
method fails here.

The induction equation
is a pure transport-stretching equation with no smoothing in
\mathbf{B}
.
High-frequency oscillations in the magnetic field are neither damped nor
diffused. For the incompressible ideal MHD system, the
Elsässer-variable transformation
\mathbf{z}^{\pm}=\mathbf{u}\pm\mathbf{B}

produces a symmetric hyperbolic system that allows for uniform-in-time energy estimates

[
2
,
4
]
. In the compressible case the divergence of
\mathbf{u}

destroys this symmetry, and no analogous transformation is available.

Because damping acts only on
\mathbf{u}
, a naive energy estimate yields

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(t)+\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}\leq 0
, where

\mathcal{E}
contains contributions from
a
and
\mathbf{B}
that
cannot be controlled by the dissipated quantity
\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}

without additional structure. In addition,
The nonlinear interactions
\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B}
,

\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}
, and
\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B}

couple all three unknowns in a way that makes standard energy methods for
the viscous or resistive MHD systems

[
9
,
29
,
31
,
36
,
37
,
46
,
47
,
53
,
56
,
57
]

directly inapplicable. Cancellations that were exploited in those settings
are no longer accessible.

1.5.
Stabilizing effect of the background magnetic field

It has long been recognized, from both physical experiments and numerical
simulations, that a sufficiently strong uniform background magnetic
field can suppress turbulence and stabilize an electrically conducting
fluid. This phenomenon was first identified theoretically by
Alfvén
[
1
]
, who showed that a uniform magnetic field supports
transverse wave propagation (now known as Alfvén waves), which
transport momentum and energy along field lines without dissipation.

Experimental confirmation of this stabilizing effect is abundant.
Gallet, Berhanu, and Mordant
[
11
]
reported measurements of forced
turbulence in a swirling liquid-metal flow (sodium) subject to an
externally imposed magnetic field. As the field strength was increased,
turbulent fluctuations were progressively suppressed and the flow
became quasi-two-dimensional and laminar along the field direction.
Califano and Chiuderi
[
5
]
demonstrated numerically that resistivity-independent dissipation of MHD waves occurs in inhomogeneous plasmas threaded by a background field, showing that even without resistivity the field geometry alone can transfer energy from large to small scales in a controlled manner.

In engineering practice, the stabilizing effect of an external magnetic
field is exploited routinely in metallurgical continuous casting
processes
[
8
]
, where liquid steel is threaded by a DC
magnetic field to suppress turbulent fluctuations and control
solidification quality; in electromagnetic braking of liquid-metal
flows in nuclear blankets of fusion reactors
[
39
]
; and in
magnetohydrodynamic generators, where the imposition of a strong field
perpendicular to the flow is used to maintain laminar operation.
More recently, magnetic confinement in tokamak devices relies
fundamentally on the idea that a sufficiently strong and appropriately
structured magnetic field prevents the plasma from touching the wall,
a stability problem that is mathematically related to the one we study
here
[
3
,
28
]
.

The mathematical mechanism underlying the stabilising effect is most
transparent at the level of the linearized system of (
1.6
),

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}a+\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{u}=0,\\[3.0pt]
&\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\beta\nabla a+\mathbf{u}=\omega\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B}-\nabla(\omega\cdot\mathbf{B}),\\[3.0pt]
&\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}=\omega\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\omega\,\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{u},\\[3.0pt]
&\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{B}=0,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.8)

Taking the divergence of the momentum equation and using the continuity
equation yields a damped wave equation for the density perturbation,

\partial_{tt}a-\beta\Delta a+\partial_{t}a=-\mathrm{div}\bigl(\omega\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B}-\nabla(\omega\cdot\mathbf{B})\bigr).

(1.9)

The left-hand side describes a damped acoustic wave with
sound speed
\sqrt{\beta}=\sqrt{P^{\prime}(1)}
and damping rate
1
. The
right-hand side is a source driven by the magnetic field perturbation,
revealing the coupling between acoustic and magnetic modes.

Differentiating the induction equation (
1.8
)
3
in time
and substituting the momentum equation yields a wave equation for
the magnetic field perturbation,

\partial_{tt}\mathbf{B}-(\omega\cdot\nabla)^{2}\mathbf{B}+\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}=-\omega\bigl[\partial_{t}\,\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{u}\bigr]-(\omega\cdot\nabla)\bigl(\beta\nabla a+\mathbf{u}\bigr)+(\omega\cdot\nabla)(\omega\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B}-\nabla(\omega\cdot\mathbf{B})).

(1.10)

The operator
(\omega\cdot\nabla)^{2}
on the left-hand side governs
propagation of Alfvén waves along the background field
\omega

at Alfvén speed
|\omega|
. The damping term
\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}

arises indirectly through the velocity coupling and is the mathematical
embodiment of the physical stabilisation mechanism.

The coupled system (
1.9
)–(
1.10
) reveals a
double-wave structure: acoustic waves in the density are driven
by magnetic fluctuations, while Alfvénic waves in the magnetic field are
forced by acoustic and velocity perturbations. It is this
two-way coupling, mediated by the background field
\omega
, that
disperses energy and prevents the concentration of high-frequency modes
that would otherwise lead to finite-time breakdown.

On the periodic torus
\mathbb{T}^{3}
, the wave operator
(\omega\cdot\nabla)

acts on Fourier modes
e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}
as multiplication by

i(\omega\cdot\mathbf{k})
. For the resulting Poincaré-type inequality
to be useful, namely, for
(\omega\cdot\nabla)
to be invertible with
controlled loss of derivatives, one needs the inner products

\omega\cdot\mathbf{k}
to be bounded away from zero for all nonzero
lattice vectors
\mathbf{k}
. The precise condition is the
Diophantine condition introduced by Chen–Zhang–Zhou
[
6
]
:

\exists\;c>0\;\text{ and }\;r>2\;\text{ such that }\;|\omega\cdot\mathbf{k}|\geq\frac{c}{|\mathbf{k}|^{r}},\qquad\forall\;\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}.

(1.11)

By classical results in Diophantine approximation, condition
(
1.11
) holds for Lebesgue-almost every
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

[
6
]
. The key consequence is the following Poincaré-type
inequality with derivative loss (see Lemma
2.1
):
for any zero-mean function
f
on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
,

\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}\leq C\,\|\omega\cdot\nabla f\|_{H^{s+r}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}.

(1.12)

This inequality is the main tool that converts the “hidden” dissipation
generated by the wave structure into quantitative Sobolev control of

a
and
\mathbf{B}
.

1.7.
Main result

We can now state the main theorem of this paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
N\geq 6r+4
with
r>2
, and let
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfy
the Diophantine condition (
1.11
) for some constant
c>0
.
Suppose the initial data
(\rho_{0}-\bar{\rho},\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{B}_{0})

satisfies the mean conditions

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\rho_{0}\,dx=\bar{\rho},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\rho_{0}\mathbf{u}_{0}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\mathbf{B}_{0}\,dx=0,

(1.14)

and belongs to
H^{N}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
with
c_{0}\leq\rho_{0}\leq c_{0}^{-1}

for some constant
c_{0}>0
. Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
if

\|\rho_{0}-\bar{\rho}\|_{H^{N}}+\|\mathbf{u}_{0}\|_{H^{N}}+\|\mathbf{B}_{0}\|_{H^{N}}\leq\varepsilon,

the system (
1.5
) admits a unique global solution

(\rho-\bar{\rho},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\in C([0,\infty);H^{N})
.
Moreover, for any
t\geq 0
and
r+4\leq\gamma\leq N
,

\|\rho-\bar{\rho}\|_{H^{4r+2}}+\|\mathbf{u}(t)\|_{H^{\gamma}}+\|\mathbf{B}(t)\|_{H^{\gamma}}\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{3(N-\gamma)}{2(2r+2)}}.

(1.15)

Remark 1.2
.

To the best of our knowledge, Theorem
1.1
is the first
global well-posedness result for the multi-dimensional isentropic
compressible inviscid, non-resistive MHD equations (
1.2
).
When
\mathbf{B}_{0}\equiv 0
the system reduces to the compressible Euler
equations with velocity damping
[
45
,
52
]
, for which global smooth
solutions near a constant state are known. Our result thus demonstrates
that the addition of a non-zero background magnetic field
\omega
is
compatible with and in fact enhances the stabilizing effect of damping,
bringing the ideal MHD system to the same level of understanding as the
compressible Euler equations.

Remark 1.3
.

Compared with
[
30
,
51
,
53
]
, the present paper is
the first to treat the fully ideal (inviscid and non-resistive) isentropic
compressible MHD system.

Remark 1.4
.

The mean conditions (
1.14
) are preserved under the time
evolution of (
1.5
): for all
t\geq 0
,

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\rho\,dx=\bar{\rho},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\rho\mathbf{u}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\mathbf{B}\,dx=0.

(1.16)

Indeed, integrating the density equation over
\mathbb{T}^{3}
gives

\frac{d}{dt}\int\rho\,dx=0
; integrating the induction equation
and using
\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{B}=0
and
\omega\cdot\nabla(\cdot)

having zero mean on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
gives
\frac{d}{dt}\int\mathbf{B}\,dx=0
;
and integrating the momentum equation gives

\frac{d}{dt}\int\rho\mathbf{u}\,dx=-\int\rho\mathbf{u}\,dx
,
so the mean of
\rho\mathbf{u}
decays exponentially from zero.

1.8.
Strategy of the proof

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is based on a bootstrap argument in
which one propagates a global smallness bound on

\sup_{t\geq 0}\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{H^{N}}
by deriving
time-uniform a priori estimates. The main steps are as follows.

The first step is to exploit hidden dissipation from the background
field (Lemmas
3.2
–
3.3
).
The absence of diffusion in the density and magnetic field equations
means that the naive energy identity

\frac{d}{dt}\int\Bigl(\rho|\mathbf{u}|^{2}+|\mathbf{B}|^{2}+e(\rho)\Bigr)\,dx+\int\rho|\mathbf{u}|^{2}\,dx=0

(1.17)

(where
e(\rho)
is the potential energy density) provides control only
on
\mathbf{u}
and not on
a
or
\mathbf{B}
.
To reveal hidden dissipation for the density
a
: we project the
momentum equation onto
\omega
to isolate the term
\omega\cdot\nabla a
;
applying
\Lambda^{s}
and testing against
\omega\cdot\nabla\Lambda^{s}a

then yields

\|(\omega\cdot\nabla)\Lambda^{s}a\|_{0}^{2}+\frac{d}{dt}\int\omega\cdot\nabla\Lambda^{s}a\cdot\Lambda^{s}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\omega)\,dx\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{s+1}^{2}+\|\mathbf{B}\|_{s+1}^{2}.

(1.18)

The Poincaré inequality (
1.12
) then converts

\|(\omega\cdot\nabla)\Lambda^{s}a\|_{0}
into
\|a\|_{s-r}
, at the cost
of
r
derivatives.
To reveal hidden dissipation for the magnetic field
\mathbf{B}
:
applying
\Lambda^{s}
to the momentum equation and testing against

(\omega\cdot\nabla)\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}
yields

\|(\omega\cdot\nabla)\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}\|_{0}^{2}-\frac{d}{dt}\int\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\cdot(\omega\cdot\nabla)\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}\,dx\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{s+1}^{2}.

(1.19)

Again, (
1.12
) converts this into
H^{s-r}
control
of
\mathbf{B}
. Summing over
s
and iterating in the derivative index
(Lemma
3.4
) produces effective dissipation at the level of

H^{2r}
for all three unknowns.

The second step is to estimate the nonlinear error terms.
The nonlinear terms
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
in (
1.7
) must be
estimated in high-order Sobolev norms. Because no diffusion is available
for
a
or
\mathbf{B}
, the standard techniques for viscous and/or
resistive MHD

[
9
,
29
,
31
,
36
,
37
,
46
,
47
,
53
,
56
,
57
]

are not directly applicable. Instead, we use commutator estimates
(Kato–Ponce type, Lemma
2.4
), integration by parts, and
structural cancellations between the pairs
(I_{5},I_{8})
and
(I_{6},I_{9})

that arise from the divergence-free constraint
\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{B}=0
.

The third step is to close the energy hierarchy.
Combining the basic energy estimate (
1.17
) (which provides
damping for
\mathbf{u}
) with the hidden dissipation estimates from the first step
(at order
4r+2
) and the high-order energy inequality (at order
N
),
we define the Lyapunov functional

\mathcal{E}(t):=A\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{H^{4r+2}}^{2}+\sum_{0\leq s\leq 3r}\int\omega\cdot\nabla\Lambda^{s}a\cdot\Lambda^{s}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\omega)\,dx-\sum_{0\leq s\leq 4r+1}\int\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\cdot(\omega\cdot\nabla)\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}\,dx,

for a sufficiently large constant
A>0
. The cross terms are bounded by
Cauchy–Schwarz and (
1.12
), so that

\mathcal{E}(t)\sim\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{H^{4r+2}}^{2}
.
Under the bootstrap assumption

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{H^{N}}\leq\delta
, one
derives

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(t)+c\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{H^{2r}}^{2}\leq 0.

An interpolation inequality then gives
\mathcal{E}(t)\lesssim(1+t)^{-(N-4r-2)/(2r+2)}
.
This decay of the lower-order energy is fed back into the highest-order
energy inequality to show via Gronwall’s inequality that

\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{H^{N}}^{2}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}
, closing the
bootstrap when
\varepsilon
is small enough. The decay rate
(
1.15
) follows by interpolation between the lower- and
higher-order estimates.

Notations.

We let
C
denote a generic positive constant that may vary from line to
line and write
A\lesssim B
to mean
A\leq CB
. The spaces

L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
and
H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
carry their standard norms

\|\cdot\|_{L^{q}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{k}
, with
H^{0}=L^{2}
. We write

[U,V]W=U(VW)-V(UW)
for the commutator, and use the shorthand

\int f\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}f\,dx
,

\|(f,g)\|_{X}^{2}=\|f\|_{X}^{2}+\|g\|_{X}^{2}
,

\|h(f,g)\|_{X}^{2}=\|(hf,hg)\|_{X}^{2}
.

Section
2
collects the preliminary lemmas, chiefly the
Poincaré-type inequality from the Diophantine condition.
Section
3
derives the time-uniform a priori estimates.
Section
4
completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Preliminaries

This section collects the several analytical tools used throughout the paper.
The first is a Poincaré-type inequality derived from the
Diophantine condition (
1.11
).

Lemma 2.1

(Diophantine–Poincaré inequality,
[
59
]
)
.

Assume that
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfies the Diophantine condition
(
1.11
) with constants
c>0
and
r>2
. Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then for any function
f
with
\nabla f\in H^{s+r}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
and

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}f\,dx=0
, one has

\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}\leq C\,\|\omega\cdot\nabla f\|_{H^{s+r}(\mathbb{T}^{3})},

(2.1)

where the constant
C
depends only on
c
,
r
,
|\omega|
, and

|\mathbb{T}^{3}|
.

The inequality (
2.1
) should be understood as a
Poincaré inequality with derivative loss. It replaces the
standard bound
\|f\|_{H^{s}}\lesssim\|\nabla f\|_{H^{s}}
(which would
require
\omega
to be nonvanishing at every frequency) with a bound
involving
r
additional derivatives on the right-hand side, valid for
almost every
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The loss of
r
derivatives is
precisely what forces the high-regularity requirement
N\geq 6r+4
in
Theorem
1.1
.

The following three lemmas provide the tools needed to estimate the
nonlinear terms. In each case the periodic torus
\mathbb{T}^{3}
replaces

\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and the estimates hold with the same constants as their
classical counterparts.

Lemma 2.2

(Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
,

[
40
]
)
.

Let
0\leq m,s\leq\kappa
. Then

\|\Lambda^{s}f\|_{L^{p}}\leq C\,\|\Lambda^{m}f\|_{L^{q}}^{1-\theta}\,\|\Lambda^{\kappa}f\|_{L^{r}}^{\theta},

where
\theta\in[0,1]
satisfies

\frac{s}{3}-\frac{1}{p}=\bigl(\frac{m}{3}-\frac{1}{q}\bigr)(1-\theta)+\bigl(\frac{\kappa}{3}-\frac{1}{r}\bigr)\theta
.
When
p=\infty
one requires
0<\theta<1
.

Lemma 2.3

(Kato–Ponce product estimate,
[
24
]
)
.

Let
s\geq 0
. For any

f,g\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{3})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
,

\|fg\|_{H^{s}}\leq C\bigl(\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\|g\|_{H^{s}}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f\|_{H^{s}}\bigr).

(2.2)

Lemma 2.4

(Kato–Ponce commutator estimate,
[
24
]
)
.

Let
s>0
. For any

f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{3})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
and

g\in H^{s-1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
,

\|[\Lambda^{s},f\cdot\nabla]g\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\bigl(\|\nabla f\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\Lambda^{s}g\|_{L^{2}}+\|\Lambda^{s}f\|_{L^{2}}\|\nabla g\|_{L^{\infty}}\bigr).

(2.3)

Lemma 2.5

(Sobolev estimate for compositions,
[
49
]
)
.

Let
s>0
,
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{3})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
, and
let
F\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
F(0)=0
. Then

\|F(f)\|_{H^{s}}\leq C\bigl(1+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\bigr)^{[s]+1}\|f\|_{H^{s}},

(2.4)

where the constant
C
depends on

\sup_{k\leq[s]+2,\,|t|\leq\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}}|F^{(k)}(t)|
.

Lemma
2.5
is applied
to handle the nonlinear pressure term
P^{\prime}(\rho)-P^{\prime}(1)
and the factor

\rho^{-1}
, both of which are smooth functions of
a=\rho-1
vanishing
at
a=0
. The uniform bound
\rho\in(\frac{1}{2}c_{0},2c_{0}^{-1})

ensures the
L^{\infty}
hypotheses.

The next lemma controls
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}}
using the conservation
law
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\rho\mathbf{u}\,dx=0
. This is necessary because
the system (
1.5
) conserves the weighted mean

\int\rho\mathbf{u}\,dx
rather than the unweighted mean

\int\mathbf{u}\,dx
.

Lemma 2.6

(Weighted Poincaré inequality,

[
7
, Lemma 5.1]
)
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be a nonnegative, nonzero measurable function on
an open bounded domain
\Omega
with
\partial\Omega\in C^{1}
. There
exists a constant
K>0
depending only on
\Omega
such that for any

z\in H^{1}(\Omega)
,

\|z\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{M}\Bigl|\int_{\Omega}fz\,dx\Bigr|+K\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{M}\|M-f\|_{L^{2}}\Bigr)\|\nabla z\|_{L^{2}},\qquad M:=\int_{\Omega}f\,dx.

(2.5)

Applying (
2.5
) with
f=\rho
,
z=u_{i}
(the
i
-th
component of
\mathbf{u}
),
M=\bar{\rho}=1
, and using

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\rho\mathbf{u}\,dx=0
,
one obtains

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}}\leq K\bigl(1+\|a\|_{L^{2}}\bigr)\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}}.

(2.6)

Under the smallness assumption
\|a\|_{H^{N}}\leq\delta\ll 1
, the factor

(1+\|a\|_{L^{2}})
is bounded, and (
2.6
) reduces to the
usual Poincaré inequality up to a controlled multiplicative constant.
This estimate is used throughout Section
3
to pass between

\|\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}}
and
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{H^{s}}
.

3.
A priori estimates

This section is devoted to deriving a priori estimates for solutions to system (
1.5
). To this end, throughout this section, we assume that
(\rho,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\in C([0,T];H^{N}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
is a smooth solution to system (
1.5
) on
\mathbb{T}^{3}\times[0,T]
for some
T>0
. Moreover,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}c_{0}\leq\rho(t,x)\leq 2c_{0}^{-1},\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,T].\end{split}

(3.1)

For simplicity, we set

Q:=\sup_{0\leq k\leq N+6,s\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2})}|p^{(k)}(s)|,\quad\beta:=p^{\prime}(1),\quad\Lambda:=\sqrt{-\Delta}.

Denote by
e(\rho)
the potential energy density, namely

e(\rho)=2\rho\int_{1}^{\rho}\frac{p(s)-p(1)}{s^{2}}ds.

Recalling that
p(\rho)
is increasing in
\rho
, it is clear that
e(\rho)>0
for
\rho\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)
and
e(1)=0
.
Taking
L^{2}
inner product of
\eqref{MHD1}_{2,3}
with
(u,h)
and using
\eqref{MHD1}_{1}
, one gets by direct calculations that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int\Big(\rho|\mathbf{u}|^{2}\,dx+|\mathbf{B}|^{2}+e(\rho)\Big)\,dx+\int\rho|\mathbf{u}|^{2}\,dx=0.\end{split}

(3.2)

Because of the embedding relation and the smallness of the norm in
H^{N}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
, we easily obtain

\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\,x\in\mathbb{T}^{3}}|a(t,x)|\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Noting
e(1)=e^{\prime}(1)=0
and
e^{\prime\prime}(\rho)=\frac{2p^{\prime}(\rho)}{\rho}>0
and
a\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
,
one obtains by the Taylor expansion that

\begin{split}\Big(\inf_{\rho\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2})}\frac{p^{\prime}(\rho)}{\rho}\Big)a^{2}\leq e(a+1)\leq\Big(\sup_{\rho\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2})}\frac{p^{\prime}(\rho)}{\rho}\Big)a^{2},\end{split}

which together with (
3.2
) implies that

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\int\Big(\rho|\mathbf{u}|^{2}\,dx+|a|^{2}+|\mathbf{B}|^{2}\Big)\,dx+\int\rho|\mathbf{u}|^{2}\,dx\leq 0.\end{split}

(3.3)

Recalling
\int\mathbf{B}\,dx=0
in (
1.16
), Poincaré’s inequality , for an absolute positive constant
C
, gives rise to,

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{0}\leq C\|\nabla\mathbf{B}\|_{0}.\end{split}

(3.4)

Due to
\int\rho\mathbf{u}\,dx=0
and
\int\rho\,dx=1
in (
1.16
), it follows from Lemma
2.6
that

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{0}\leq\big|\int\rho\mathbf{u}\,dx\big|+K\big(1+\|\rho-1\|_{0}\big)\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{0}\leq K(1+\|a\|_{0})\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{0}.\end{split}

(3.5)

Thanks to (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), one gets by Poincaré’s inequality that

\begin{split}\|\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}\|_{0}\leq\|\mathbf{B}\|_{s}\leq C_{s}\|\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}\|_{0},\quad\|\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\|_{0}\leq\|\mathbf{u}\|_{s}\leq C_{s}(1+\|a\|_{0})\|\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\|_{0},\end{split}

(3.6)

for any nonnegative integer
s
, where
C_{s}\geq 1
is a constant depending only on
s
.
This fact will be used throughout this section without further mentions.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(\rho,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\in C([0,T];H^{N})
be a solution to the system (
1.5
). Then, for any
0\leq\ell\leq N
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\big\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\big\|^{2}_{\ell}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\ell}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C(1+\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3})\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3}\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|^{2}_{\ell}+\|a\|_{3}\|\mathbf{B}\|^{2}_{3}\|a\|_{\ell}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\ell},

(3.7)

where the positive constant
C
depends only on
|\omega|,\beta,
and
Q
.

Proof.

Obviously, (
3.1
) with
\ell=0
is the basic energy inequality in (
3.3
). We consider the case when
\ell\geq 1
.
Applying
\Lambda^{s}
with
1\leq s\leq\ell
to
\eqref{MHD1}_{2}
,
\eqref{MHD1}_{3}
and taking
L^{2}
inner product with
\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}
and
\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B},
respectively, then integrating by parts and using
\eqref{MHD1}_{1}
,
\eqref{MHD1}_{4}
yield

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|(\sqrt{\rho}\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u},\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B})\|^{2}_{0}+\|\rho\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\|_{0}^{2}=:\sum_{j=1}^{10}\textrm{I}_{j},\end{split}

(3.8)

where

\displaystyle\textrm{I}_{1}:=-\int[\Lambda^{s},\rho]\mathbf{u}_{t}\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\,dx,

\displaystyle\textrm{I}_{2}:=-\int[\Lambda^{s},\rho\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla]\mathbf{u}\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\,dx,

\displaystyle\textrm{I}_{3}:=-\int p^{\prime}\nabla\Lambda^{s}\rho\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\,dx,

\displaystyle\textrm{I}_{4}:=-\int[\Lambda^{s},p^{\prime}]\nabla\rho\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\,dx,

\displaystyle\textrm{I}_{5}:=\int\Lambda^{s}(\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B})\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\,dx,

\displaystyle\textrm{I}_{6}:=-\frac{1}{2}\int\Lambda^{s}\nabla(|\mathbf{B}|^{2})\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\,dx,

\displaystyle\textrm{I}_{7}:=-\int\Lambda^{s}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{B})\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}\,dx,

\displaystyle\textrm{I}_{8}:=\int\Lambda^{s}(\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u})\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}\,dx,

\displaystyle\textrm{I}_{9}:=-\int\Lambda^{s}(\mathbf{B}\hbox{\rm div}\,\mathbf{u})\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}\,dx,

\displaystyle\textrm{I}_{10}:=-\int[\Lambda^{s},\rho]\mathbf{u}\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\,dx.

In what follows, we shall bound each term above. We first estimate
\textrm{I}_{3}
.
Using
\eqref{MHD1}_{1}
, one has

\displaystyle\rho\Lambda^{s}\text{div}\mathbf{u}=

\displaystyle\Lambda^{s}(\rho\text{div}\mathbf{u})-[\Lambda^{s},\rho]\text{div}\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\partial_{t}\Lambda^{s}\rho+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\Lambda^{s}\rho)-[\Lambda^{s},\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla]\rho-[\Lambda^{s},\rho]\text{div}\mathbf{u}.

Noticing that

\Big(\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}\Big)_{t}+\hbox{\rm div}\,\Big(\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}\mathbf{u}\Big)=\Big(\frac{2p^{\prime}}{\rho}-p^{\prime\prime}\Big)\hbox{\rm div}\,\mathbf{u},

it follows from the integration by parts formula that

\begin{split}\textrm{I}_{3}&=\int\Lambda^{s}\rho\big(p^{\prime}\hbox{\rm div}\,\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}+\nabla p^{\prime}\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\big)\,dx\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}|\Lambda^{s}\rho|^{2}\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\int\big(\frac{2p^{\prime}}{\rho}-p^{\prime\prime}\big)\hbox{\rm div}\,\mathbf{u}|\Lambda^{s}\rho|^{2}\,dx-\int\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}\Lambda^{s}\rho[\Lambda^{s},\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla]\rho\,dx\\
&\quad-\int\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}\Lambda^{s}\rho[\Lambda^{s},\rho]\hbox{\rm div}\,\mathbf{u}\,dx+\int\Lambda^{s}\rho\nabla p^{\prime}\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\,dx.\end{split}

Since
\rho\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2})
and
p(\rho)
is a smooth function in
\rho
, we conclude from Lemma
2.4
, the embedding relation and Young’s inequality, that

\begin{split}\Big|\frac{1}{2}\int\Big(\frac{2p^{\prime}}{\rho}-p^{\prime\prime}\Big)\hbox{\rm div}\,\mathbf{u}|\Lambda^{s}\rho|^{2}\,dx\Big|&\lesssim\Big\|\frac{2p^{\prime}}{\rho}-p^{\prime\prime}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|a\|^{2}_{s}\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{3}\|a\|^{2}_{s},\\
\Big|-\int\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}\Lambda^{s}\rho[\Lambda^{s},\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla]\rho\,dx\Big|&\lesssim\Big\|\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}\|a\|_{s}\|[\Lambda^{s},\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla]\rho\|_{0}\lesssim\|(a,\mathbf{u})\|_{3}\|(a,\mathbf{u})\|^{2}_{s},\\
\Big|-\int\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}\Lambda^{s}\rho[\Lambda^{s},\rho]\hbox{\rm div}\,\mathbf{u}\,dx\Big|&\lesssim\Big\|\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}\|a\|_{s}\|[\Lambda^{s},\rho]\hbox{\rm div}\,\mathbf{u}\|_{0}\lesssim\|(a,\mathbf{u})\|_{3}\|(a,\mathbf{u})\|^{2}_{s},\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\Big|\int\Lambda^{s}\rho\nabla p^{\prime}\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\,dx\Big|&\lesssim\|\nabla p^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\Lambda^{s}a\|_{0}\|\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\|_{0}\lesssim\|a\|_{3}\|(a,\mathbf{u})\|^{2}_{s}.\end{split}

Therefore, we have

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}|\Lambda^{s}a|^{2}\,dx+\Big|\textrm{I}_{3}\Big|\lesssim\|(a,\mathbf{u})\|_{3}\|(a,\mathbf{u})\|_{s}^{2}.\end{split}

For
\textrm{I}_{2}
and
\textrm{I}_{4},
it follows from
Lemma
2.3
, Lemma
2.4
, Lemma
2.5
, (
3.1
), and the embedding relation and Young’s inequality,
that

\displaystyle|\textrm{I}_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq\|[\Lambda^{s},\rho\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla]\mathbf{u}\|_{0}\|\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\|_{0}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+\|(a,\mathbf{u})\|_{2})\|(a,\mathbf{u})\|_{3}\|(a,\mathbf{u})\|^{2}_{s}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{3}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{s},

\displaystyle|\textrm{I}_{4}|

\displaystyle\leq\|[\Lambda^{s},p^{\prime}]\nabla\rho\|_{0}\|\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\|_{0}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\Lambda^{s}a\|_{0}+\|\Lambda^{s}(p^{\prime}(1+a)-p^{\prime}(1))\|_{0}\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}})\|\mathbf{u}\|_{s}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|a\|_{3}\|(a,\mathbf{u})\|^{2}_{s},

and

\displaystyle|\textrm{I}_{10}|

\displaystyle\leq\|[\Lambda^{s},\rho]\mathbf{u}\|_{0}\|\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\|_{0}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\Lambda^{s}a\|_{0}+\|\Lambda^{s-1}\mathbf{u}\|_{0}\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}})\|\mathbf{u}\|_{s}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(a,\mathbf{u})\|_{3}\|(a,\mathbf{u})\|^{2}_{s}.

We now deal with terms
\textrm{I}_{5}
,
\textrm{I}_{6}
,
\textrm{I}_{8}
and
\textrm{I}_{9}.
First, employing the integration by
parts formula and
\text{div}\mathbf{B}=0
, using Lemma
2.3
, (
3.6
), and the embedding relation and Young’s inequality, we infer that

\begin{split}|\textrm{I}_{5}+\textrm{I}_{8}|&\leq\Big|\int[\Lambda^{s},\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla]\mathbf{B}\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\,dx+\int[\Lambda^{s},\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla]\mathbf{u}\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}\,dx\Big|\\
&\quad+\Big|\int\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\,dx+\int\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}\,dx\Big|\\
&\lesssim\big(\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{s}+\|\nabla\mathbf{B}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{s}\big)\|\mathbf{u}\|_{s}\\
&\lesssim\|(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3}\|(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|^{2}_{s}.\end{split}

It follows from the integration by parts formula, Lemma
2.3
, (
3.6
), and the embedding relation and Young’s inequality, that

\begin{split}|\textrm{I}_{6}+\textrm{I}_{9}|&=\Big|-\sum_{i,k=1}^{d}\int[\Lambda^{s},\mathbf{B}^{k}]\partial_{i}\mathbf{B}^{k}\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}^{i}\,dx-\int[\Lambda^{s},\mathbf{B}]\hbox{\rm div}\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}\,dx+\sum_{i,k=1}^{d}\int\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}^{k}\partial_{i}\mathbf{B}^{k}\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}^{i}\,dx\Big|\\
&\lesssim\big(\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{s}+\|\nabla\mathbf{B}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{s}\big)\|\mathbf{u}\|_{s}+\|\nabla\mathbf{B}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{s}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{s}\\
&\lesssim\|(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3}\|(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|^{2}_{s}.\end{split}

For the term
I_{7}
, applying the integrating by parts, Lemma
2.3
, and using the embedding relation and
Young’s inequality, we infer that

\begin{split}|\textrm{I}_{7}|&\lesssim\big(\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{s}+\|\nabla\mathbf{B}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{s}\big)\|\mathbf{B}\|_{s}+\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\mathbf{B}\|^{2}_{s}\\
&\lesssim\|(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3}\|(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|^{2}_{s},\end{split}

Finally, it remains to estimate
\textrm{I}_{1}.
We first bound
\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}.
By (
3.1
)
and the embedding relation, we have

\begin{split}\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}&=\Big\|-(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}-\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}\nabla a+\rho^{-1}\big((\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{B}+(\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{B}-\nabla(\omega\cdot\mathbf{B})-\frac{1}{2}\nabla(|\mathbf{B}|^{2})\big)\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}\\
&\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}+(1+\|\mathbf{B}\|_{L^{\infty}})\|\nabla\mathbf{B}\|_{L^{\infty}}\\
&\lesssim\big(1+\|(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{2}\big)\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3}.\end{split}

(3.9)

Next, we estimate
\|\mathbf{u}_{t}\|_{s-1}.
Note that (
3.1
) implies
a\in(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
. Thus, it follows from Lemma
2.5

that

\begin{split}\Big\|\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}\Big\|_{s-1}&=\Big\|\frac{p^{\prime}(a+1)}{a+1}-p^{\prime}(1)+p^{\prime}(1)\Big\|_{s-1}\lesssim\Big\|\frac{p^{\prime}(a+1)}{a+1}-p^{\prime}(1)\Big\|_{s-1}+1\\
&\lesssim\Big\|\frac{p^{\prime}(a+1)}{a+1}-p^{\prime}(1)\Big\|_{s}+1\lesssim\|a\|_{s}+1\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\Big\|\frac{1}{\rho}\Big\|_{s-1}&=\|(a+1)^{-1}-1+1\|_{s-1}\lesssim\|(a+1)^{-1}-1\|_{s-1}+1\\
&\lesssim\|(a+1)^{-1}-1\|_{s}+1\lesssim\|a\|_{s}+1.\end{split}

Thanks to the above two estimates, and
\rho\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2})
, it follows from Lemma
2.3
and Young’s inequality that

\begin{split}\|\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}\|_{s-1}&=\Big\|-\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}\nabla a+\rho^{-1}\big((\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{B}+(\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{B}-\nabla(\omega\cdot\mathbf{B})-\frac{1}{2}\nabla(|\mathbf{B}|^{2})\big)\Big\|_{s-1}\\
&\lesssim\|\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{s-1}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{s-1}\|\nabla\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\Big\|\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}\Big\|_{s-1}\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}\\
&\quad+\Big\|\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla a\|_{s-1}+\Big\|\frac{1}{\rho}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}}\|(\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{B}+(\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{B}-\nabla(\omega\cdot\mathbf{B})-\frac{1}{2}\nabla(|\mathbf{B}|^{2})\|_{s-1}\\

&\quad+\|\mathbf{B}\|^{2}_{3}+\|\mathbf{B}\|_{3}\|a\|_{s}+\|a\|_{s}\|\mathbf{B}\|^{2}_{3}.\end{split}

(3.10)

This together with (
3.9
), (
3.10

\begin{split}|\textrm{I}_{1}|&=\Big|\int[\Lambda^{s},\rho]\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\,dx\Big|=\Big|\int[\Lambda^{s},a]\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}\cdot\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\,dx\Big|\\

&\quad+\|\mathbf{B}\|^{2}_{3}+\|\mathbf{B}\|_{3}\|a\|_{s}+\|a\|_{s}\|\mathbf{B}\|^{2}_{3}\big)+(1+\|(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{2})\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3}\|a\|_{s}\|\mathbf{u}\|_{s}.\end{split}

Plugging the estimates for

\textrm{I}_{10}

\begin{split}(1+\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3})\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3}\|a\|^{2}_{4r+2}&\lesssim(1+\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{N})\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{2r}\|a\|^{2}_{4r+2}\\
&\lesssim\epsilon\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{2r}^{2}+\|a\|^{4}_{4r+2}\\
&\lesssim\epsilon\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{2r}^{2}+\|a\|_{2r}^{2}\|a\|_{N}^{2}\\
&\lesssim\epsilon\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{2r}^{2}+\delta^{2}\|a\|^{2}_{2r}.\end{split}

(4.9)

For any
{N}\geq 5r+3
, by the interpolation inequality, it holds that

\|\mathbf{B}\|_{4r+2}\lesssim\|\mathbf{B}\|_{3r+1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{N}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(4.10)

It follows from (
4.1
), (
4.10
), the embedding relation and Young’s inequality yields that

\begin{split}(1+\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3})\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3}\|\mathbf{B}\|^{2}_{4r+2}&\lesssim(1+\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{N})\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{2r}\|\mathbf{B}\|^{2}_{4r+2}\\
&\lesssim\epsilon\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{2r}^{2}+\|\mathbf{B}\|^{4}_{4r+2}\\
&\lesssim\epsilon\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{2r}^{2}+\|\mathbf{B}\|_{3r+1}^{2}\|h\|_{N}^{2}\\
&\lesssim\epsilon\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{2r}^{2}+\delta^{2}\|\mathbf{B}\|^{2}_{3r+1}.\end{split}

(4.11)

Define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}(t)}=

\displaystyle A\big\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\big\|^{2}_{4r+2}+\sum_{0\leq s\leq 3r}\int\omega\cdot\nabla\Lambda^{s}a\cdot\Lambda^{s}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\omega)\,dx-\sum_{0\leq s\leq 4r+1}\int\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{u}\cdot(\omega\cdot\nabla)\Lambda^{s}\mathbf{B}\,dx.

Taking
A>1
large enough such that

{\mathcal{E}(t)}\geq\big\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\big\|^{2}_{4r+2}.

Plugging (
4
), (
4.9
), (
4.11
) into (
4.7
), using the assumption (
4.1
) and the embedding relation, and then taking
\epsilon
small enough, we deduce that

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{E}(t)}+C\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{2r}^{2}\leq 0.

(4.12)

For any
{N}\geq 4r+2
, by the interpolation inequality and the assumption (
4.1
), we have

\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{4r+2}\lesssim\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{2r}^{\frac{N-4r-2}{N-2r}}\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{N}^{\frac{2r+2}{N-2r}}\lesssim\delta^{\frac{2r+2}{N-2r}}\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{2r}^{\frac{N-4r-2}{N-2r}},

which along with (
4.12
) implies that

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{E}(t)}+C\mathcal{E}(t)^{\frac{N-2r}{N-4r-2}}\leq 0.

Solving this inequality yields

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}(t)}\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{N-4r-2}{2r+2}}.

(4.13)

Taking
\ell={N}
in (
3.1
) and using the embedding relation gives rise to

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\big\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\big\|^{2}_{N}+\|\mathbf{u}\|_{N}^{2}\leq C(1+\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3})\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3}\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|^{2}_{N}.\end{split}

(4.14)

From (
4.13
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(1+\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3})\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\|_{3}\,d\tau\leq C.

Then, applying Gronwall’s inequality to (
4.14
) yields

\big\|(a,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{B})\big\|^{2}_{N}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathbf{u}(\tau)\|_{N}^{2}\,d\tau\leq C\big\|(a(0),\mathbf{u}(0),\mathbf{B}(0))\big\|^{2}_{N}\leq C\varepsilon^{2}.

Taking
\varepsilon
small enough so that
C\varepsilon\leq\delta/2
, we deduce from a continuity argument that the local solution
can be extended as a global one in time.

Moreover, from (
4.13
), we also have the following decay rate

\displaystyle\left\lVert a(t)\right\rVert_{{4r+2}}+\left\lVert\mathbf{u}(t)\right\rVert_{{4r+2}}+\left\lVert\mathbf{B}(t)\right\rVert_{{4r+2}}\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{N-4r-2}{2(2r+2)}}.

Thus,
for any
4r+2<\gamma<N
, using the following interpolation inequality

\displaystyle\left\lVert f(t)\right\rVert_{{\gamma}}\leq\left\lVert f(t)\right\rVert_{{4r+2}}^{\frac{{N}-\gamma}{{N}-4r-2}}\left\lVert f(t)\right\rVert_{{N}}^{\frac{\gamma-4r-2}{{N}-4r-2}}.

we can get the decay rate for the higher order energy

\displaystyle\left\lVert a(t)\right\rVert_{{4r+2}}+\left\lVert\mathbf{u}(t)\right\rVert_{{\gamma}}+\left\lVert\mathbf{B}(t)\right\rVert_{{\gamma}}\leq C(1+t)^{-\frac{3({N}-\gamma)}{2(2r+2)}}.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.∎
