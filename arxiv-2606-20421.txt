On Ziegler pairs of line arrangements: from non-existence to abundance
Alexandru Dimca and Piotr Pokora

Abstract
. We study Ziegler pairs of line arrangements from both numerical and homological perspectives. First, we show that for arrangements of d < 9 d<9 lines the intersection lattice determines the exponent data considered here. Then we list six distinct Ziegler pair with d = 10 d=10 . In particular, we construct higher-degree examples with the same intersection lattice, the same minimal degree of a Jacobian relation, and the same Hilbert function of the Milnor algebra, but with different minimal graded free resolutions.

1.
Introduction

The purpose of this note is to discuss Ziegler pairs of line arrangements from two complementary points of view. The first one is numerical and concerns the extent to which the intersection lattice of a line arrangement determines the exponents of its module of Jacobian syzygies. The second one is homological and concerns pairs of arrangements with the same intersection lattice and with the same minimal degree of a Jacobian relation, but with different minimal graded free resolutions of the corresponding Milnor algebras.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}:f=0
be a line arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, where
d=\deg f
is the number of lines, and let
m=m({\mathcal{A}})
be the maximal multiplicity of an intersection point of
{\mathcal{A}}
. We write

r=r({\mathcal{A}})={\rm mdr}(f)=\indeg D_{0}(f)

for the minimal degree of a Jacobian syzygy. If

e_{1}\leq e_{2}\leq\cdots\leq e_{s}\leq d-2

denotes the ordered sequence of exponents of
{\mathcal{A}}
, then
r=e_{1}
and the upper bound
e_{s}\leq d-2
follows from
[
20
]
.

A Ziegler pair is a pair of line arrangements
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
such that the intersection lattices
L({\mathcal{A}})
and
L({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})
are isomorphic, but the arrangements are distinguished by their Jacobian-syzygy data, see Definition
2.1
for more details. In the classical examples of Ziegler and Yuzvinsky, the two arrangements have
d=9
lines and the same intersection lattice, but their minimal degrees of Jacobian relations are different. In this sense, the minimal degree itself detects the arrangement in this pair.

The first part of the present paper explains why such examples cannot occur for
d<9
. More precisely, for arrangements with at most eight lines, the intersection lattice determines the relevant exponent data. After the elementary cases are removed, the only cases that require a separate analysis are

(d,m)=(6,3),\quad(7,3),\quad(8,4),\quad(8,3),

and these are treated in Theorems
3.1
,
3.3
,
4.1
, and
4.3
.

In degree
d=9
it seems that only the classical example of Ziegler and Yuzvinsky is known, see
[
22
,
21
]
. The analysis of this border line case is rather long, and we postpone it for a further paper.

On the other hand, for
d>9
the Ziegler pairs seem to abound.
The second part of the paper gives new examples of such pairs in which the difference is subtler. We construct a two-parameter family of arrangements of eleven lines with constant intersection lattice. At a general parameter value, for instance at
(a,b)=(31,17)
, the Jacobian syzygies have degree sequence

(6,7,7,8),

whereas at the special point

p=\left(\frac{3}{10},\frac{2}{7}\right)

the intersection lattice is unchanged and the degree sequence becomes

(6,7,7,8,8).

The two Milnor algebras have the same Hilbert function, but their minimal graded free resolutions are different. Deleting a suitable line from both arrangements then gives a Ziegler pair of ten lines with degree sequences

(6,6,6,7)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(6,6,6,7,7).

Thus the pair has the same value of
{\rm mdr}
and the same Hilbert function, and the distinction is purely homological.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls the algebraic notation and the preliminary tools used throughout the paper. Sections 3 and 4 treat arrangements with at most eight lines. Section 5 introduces the eleven-line family and the rank-drop mechanism producing the homological jump. Section 6 applies deletion to obtain new ten-line Ziegler pairs. Section 7 recalls the known examples with nine and ten lines and places the new examples in this list.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
S=\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]
be the standard graded polynomial ring, and let
C:f=0\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
be a reduced plane curve of degree
d
. In the case of a line arrangement we write

f=\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{d},

where the
\ell_{i}\in S_{1}
are pairwise non-proportional linear forms.

The Jacobian ideal of
f
is

J_{f}=(f_{x},f_{y},f_{z})\subset S,

and the Milnor algebra of
f
is

M(f)=S/J_{f}.

The module of Jacobian syzygies is

AR(f)=\{(a,b,c)\in S^{3}\mid af_{x}+bf_{y}+cf_{z}=0\}.

Equivalently, we use the notation
D_{0}(f)
for the corresponding module of logarithmic derivations annihilating
f
; throughout the paper we identify
D_{0}(f)
with
AR(f)
in the usual way. The minimal degree of a Jacobian relation is

{\rm mdr}(f)=\min\{q\geq 0\mid AR(f)_{q}\neq 0\}=\indeg D_{0}(f).

If
AR(f)
has homogeneous minimal generators of degrees
e_{1}\leq\cdots\leq e_{s}
, we call
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{s})
the degree sequence, or the sequence of exponents, of the arrangement.

For a line arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}
, we denote by
n_{k}=n_{k}({\mathcal{A}})
the number of points where exactly
k
lines of
{\mathcal{A}}
meet. Since all singularities are ordinary quasihomogeneous multiple points, the total Tjurina number is

\tau({\mathcal{A}})=\sum_{k\geq 2}n_{k}(k-1)^{2}.

The intersection lattice
L({\mathcal{A}})
determines all numbers
n_{k}
, but in general it need not determine the module
D_{0}(f)
or the minimal free resolution of
M(f)
.

Definition 2.1
.

Two line arrangements
{\mathcal{A}}:f=0
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
are said to be a Ziegler pair if they have isomorphic intersection lattices
L({\mathcal{A}})
and
L({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})
, obtained from the corresponding central arrangements in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, and distinct sets of graded Betti numbers coming from the minimal resolutions of their Jacobian algebras
M(f)
and
M(f^{\prime})
.

A Ziegler pair
{\mathcal{A}}:f=0
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0
may have one or several of the following additional properties.

(1)

We say that the Ziegler pair
{\mathcal{A}}:f=0
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0
satisfies the condition
{\rm(HF)}
if the Jacobian algebras
M(f)
and
M(f^{\prime})
have the same Hilbert function, that is

\dim M(f)_{k}=\dim M(f^{\prime})_{k}\text{ for any integer }k.

(2)

We say that the Ziegler pair
{\mathcal{A}}:f=0
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0
satisfies condition
{\rm(MDR)}
if the Jacobian algebras
M(f)
and
M(f^{\prime})
have the same minimal degree of a first Jacobian syzygy, that is

{\rm mdr}(f)={\rm mdr}(f^{\prime}).

(3)

We say that the Ziegler pair
{\mathcal{A}}:f=0
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0
satisfies condition
({\rm SPEC}_{0})
if the arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}
is a specialization of the arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
. More precisely, this means that there exists an interval
I=[0,t_{0}]\subset\mathbb{R}
and a smooth family of homogeneous polynomials
f_{t}
, for
t\in I
, such that

f_{0}=f,\qquad f_{t_{0}}=f^{\prime},

and the corresponding family of hyperplane arrangements
{\mathcal{A}}_{t}:f_{t}=0
has constant intersection lattice, i.e.

L({\mathcal{A}}_{t})\cong L({\mathcal{A}}_{t^{\prime}})\quad\text{for all }t,t^{\prime}\in I.

If, in addition, the graded Betti numbers of the minimal resolutions of the Jacobian algebras
M(f_{t})
are constant for all
0<t\leq t_{0}
, then we say that the Ziegler pair
{\mathcal{A}}:f=0
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0
satisfies condition
({\rm SPEC})
.

As an example, the classical Ziegler pair in degree
d=9
satisfies condition
({\rm SPEC})
.

We shall use the following standard bounds and addition-deletion tools.

Let us recall that by
[
5
, Theorem 1.2]
one has

(2.1)

r=d-m\text{ if }2m>d,\text{ or }m-1\leq r\leq d-m\text{ otherwise}.

We list first the cases where
r
is clearly determined by the pair
(d,m)
and some other invariants of the intersection lattice of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Case 1:

2m>d
. Then it follows from (
2.1
)
that
r=d-m
.

Case 2:

When
m=2
, it follows from
[
13
]

that
r=d-2
. In fact, in this case for
d\geq 4
,
{\mathcal{A}}
is a maximal Tjurina curve of type

(d,d-2)
, see
[
14
, Proposition 5.11]
, and hence all the numerical invariants of the minimal resolution of
D_{0}(f)
are determined by
d
.

In the sequel we assume
m>2
and
d\geq 2m\geq 6
, since all the other cases are covered by Case 1 and Case 2 above.
Moreover, it follows from
[
12
, Theorem 2.1]
that

(2.2)

r\geq\frac{2d}{m}-2.

For any reduced plane curve
C:f=0
,
let
J=J_{f}
be the Jacobian ideal of
f
and
I=I_{f}
be its saturation with respect to the maximal ideal
(x,y,z)
. We recall that

N(f)=I/J

is called the Jacobian module of the curve
C
. Then we have the following result, see
[
10
, Theorem 6.2]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
C_{1}:f_{1}=0
be a reduced curve of degree
d_{1}
and
L
be a line.
Assume that all singularities of
C_{1}
and
C=C_{1}\cup L
are quasihomogeneous. Then there is an exact sequence for any integer
k
given by

0\to D_{0}(f_{1})_{k-1}\longrightarrow D_{0}(f)_{k}\to H^{0}(L,{\mathcal{O}}_{L}(k+1-|R|))\to N(f_{1})_{k+d_{1}-2},

where
R=C_{1}\cap L
as a set.

Our first result in this paper is the following.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}:f=0
be a line arrangement with
d\geq 6
and
m\geq 3
. Then
the following hold.

(1)

One has
\dim D_{0}(f)_{d-m}\geq n_{m}
.

(2)

If
\dim D_{0}(f)_{d-m}\leq 3
, then
r({\mathcal{A}})=d-m.

(3)

If
\dim D_{0}(f)_{d-m}\leq\binom{k+2}{2}
for some integer
k\geq 1
, then
r({\mathcal{A}})\geq d-m-k.
In addition, when
m=3
and
k=1
, one has

1\leq\dim D_{0}(f)_{d-3}=n_{3}\leq 3

and the first exponents of
{\mathcal{A}}
are
e_{1}=\ldots=e_{n_{3}}=d-3
.

Theorem
3.3
(4) below shows that this is the best possible result,
namely the inequality
n_{m}\leq 3
is necessary to get
r({\mathcal{A}})=d-m.

Proof.

We use the notation from
[
9
]
, in particular for a multiple point

p=(u:v:w)\in{\mathcal{A}}

we denote by
f_{2p}
the product of the factors in
f
not vanishing at
p
,

D_{p}=u\partial_{x}+v\partial_{y}+w\partial_{z}

and

\tilde{D}_{p}=f_{2}D_{p}-\frac{D_{p}(f_{2})}{d}E,

where
E=x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}+z\partial_{z}
is the Euler derivation.
If
p
is a point of multiplicity
m
, then
\tilde{D}_{p}\in D_{0}(f)_{d-m}
and it satisfies
\tilde{D}_{p}(p)\neq 0
and
\tilde{D}_{p}(q)=0
for any point
q\neq p
in
{\mathcal{A}}
of multiplicity at least 3.
Using
[
9
, Theorem 1.3]
it follows that
\dim D_{0}(f)_{d-m}\geq n_{m}
, since the derivations
\{\tilde{D}_{p}\}_{p}
are linearly independent, with
p
running through the set of points of
{\mathcal{A}}
of multiplicity
m
.
This proves the claim (1).

To prove (2), assume that
\dim D_{0}(f)_{d-m}\leq 3

and that
there is
D\in D_{0}(f)_{d-m-1}
,
D\neq 0
. Then clearly
xD,yD,zD
are linear independent in
D_{0}(f)_{d-m}
. This is already a contradiction when
\dim D_{0}(f)_{d-m}\leq 2
, and hence the proof is complete in this case.
When
\dim D_{0}(f)_{d-m}=3
, it follows that for any point
p
of multiplicity
m
one has

\tilde{D}_{p}\in D_{0}(f)_{d-m}=S_{1}\cdot D.

This is a contradiction, since any derivation
\tilde{D}_{p}
is primitive,
i.e. not the multiple of a strictly lower degree derivation, see
[
5
, proof of Theorem 1.2]
.

The same argument starting with
D\in D_{0}(f)_{d-m-k}
,
D\neq 0
and using the equality
\dim S_{k}=\binom{k+2}{2}
yields the first claim (3).
Note that for
m=3
the equality
\dim D_{0}(f)_{d-3}=n_{3}
always holds by
[
9
, Theorem 1.3]
and hence the hypothesis in (2) above can be reformulated as
n_{3}\leq 3
.
∎

Thus, for
d<9
, after excluding the cases
2m>d
and
m=2
, the only remaining possibilities are

(d,m)=(6,3),\quad(7,3),\quad(8,3),\quad(8,4).

These four cases are treated in Theorems
3.1
,
3.3
,
4.3
, and
4.1
, respectively.

In order to study Ziegler pairs of lines in the last sections of this paper, we shall also use the following elementary criterion.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
m\geq 0
. Consider the multiplication map

\mu_{m}:S_{1}\otimes AR(f)_{m}\longrightarrow AR(f)_{m+1}.

Then the quotient

AR(f)_{m+1}/S_{1}AR(f)_{m}

measures the new minimal generators of
AR(f)
in degree
m+1
.

In particular, their number is

\dim AR(f)_{m+1}-\operatorname{rank}(\mu_{m}).

Proof.

The image of
\mu_{m}
consists exactly of the degree
m+1
syzygies obtained by multiplying degree
m
syzygies by linear forms. Hence the quotient

AR(f)_{m+1}/S_{1}AR(f)_{m}

is the space of degree
m+1
syzygies modulo those generated in lower degree. Its dimension is therefore the number of new minimal generators in degree
m+1
. Since

\dim\operatorname{Im}(\mu_{m})=\operatorname{rank}(\mu_{m}),

the formula follows.
∎

We now introduce a two-parameter family of arrangements of eleven lines in

0&0&1&b(1-a)&b(b-1)(1-a)&b-1&a&0&1-b&b&1\end{array}\right).

We regard the columns of
A(a,b)
as the linear forms

\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{11}\in S_{1}

and set

f_{a,b}=\prod_{i=1}^{11}\ell_{i}.

Thus
f_{a,b}=0
defines an arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{a,b}
of eleven lines.

The arrangement has
13
triple points and
16
double points. The triple points are

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=\{

\displaystyle\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{8}\},\{\ell_{1},\ell_{3},\ell_{7}\},\{\ell_{1},\ell_{10},\ell_{11}\},\{\ell_{2},\ell_{3},\ell_{6}\},\{\ell_{2},\ell_{4},\ell_{5}\},\{\ell_{2},\ell_{9},\ell_{11}\},

The triples listed above correspond to the
3\times 3
minors of

which vanish
identically. In order to keep the intersection lattice fixed, we must require that no
additional triples occur and that no two columns become proportional. Equivalently, all

3\times 3
minors corresponding to triples not contained in
\mathcal{T}
, together with the
pairwise non-proportionality conditions for the columns, must be nonzero. After
factorization, the product of the relevant nonzero factors is

\displaystyle\Delta(a,b)=

\displaystyle a(a-1)b(b-1)(a-b)(ab-1)

\displaystyle\cdot(ab+a-b)(ab-b+1)

\displaystyle\cdot(ab^{2}-b^{2}+b-1)

\displaystyle\cdot(a^{2}b^{2}-ab^{2}-a+b).

We work on the open set

R=\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{A}^{2}\mid\Delta(a,b)\neq 0\}.

For all

For the rest of this section we write

(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4})=(6,7,7,8).

To detect a possible jump in the number of generators of

, we use the map

\mu_{7}:S_{1}\otimes AR(f)_{7}\longrightarrow AR(f)_{8}.

The quotient

AR(f)_{8}/S_{1}AR(f)_{7}

records the new minimal generators in degree

. Hence their number is

\dim AR(f)_{8}-\operatorname{rank}(\mu_{7}).

At the general point

\dim AR(f)_{7}=5,\qquad\dim AR(f)_{8}=13,

of
AR(f)_{8}
. The columns of the matrix of
\mu_{7}
are the coordinate vectors of

xh_{i},\ yh_{i},\ zh_{i}\qquad i=1,\ldots,5.

For a general parameter this matrix has rank
12
. The jumping condition in degree
8
is therefore

\operatorname{rank}(\mu_{7})\leq 11.

The
SINGULAR
script in Appendix A verifies the rank drop at
p
.

Remark 5.2
.

It is natural to ask for the locus in the parameter space
R
for which the multiplication map
\mu_{7}
has rank smaller than expected. Symbolic computations, carried out with the assistance of
Codex
in the preparation and optimization of the computational code, suggest that

p=\left(\frac{3}{10},\frac{2}{7}\right)

is the only special point with this property.

6.
Deletion technique for constructing new Ziegler pairs of ten lines

We now construct a Ziegler pair of ten lines by deleting

\ell_{1}:x=0

from both the generic arrangement and the special arrangement at

p=\left(\frac{3}{10},\frac{2}{7}\right).

For the generic member we use the concrete point
(a,b)=(31,17)
. Let the two eleven-line arrangements be denoted by

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{gen}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{A}_{p},

and define

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{gen}}=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{gen}}\setminus\{\ell_{1}\},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{p}=\mathcal{A}_{p}\setminus\{\ell_{1}\}.

Deleting
\ell_{1}
removes exactly the three triple points

\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{8}\},\qquad\{\ell_{1},\ell_{3},\ell_{7}\},\qquad\{\ell_{1},\ell_{10},\ell_{11}\}.

The remaining ten triple points are

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\ell_{1}}=\{

\displaystyle\{\ell_{2},\ell_{3},\ell_{6}\},\{\ell_{2},\ell_{4},\ell_{5}\},\{\ell_{2},\ell_{9},\ell_{11}\},\{\ell_{3},\ell_{8},\ell_{11}\},\{\ell_{3},\ell_{9},\ell_{10}\},\{\ell_{4},\ell_{7},\ell_{11}\},

\displaystyle\{\ell_{4},\ell_{8},\ell_{10}\},\{\ell_{5},\ell_{6},\ell_{11}\},\{\ell_{5},\ell_{8},\ell_{9}\},\{\ell_{6},\ell_{7},\ell_{9}\}\}.

Thus both deleted arrangements have weak combinatorics

(t_{2},t_{3})=(15,10).

Using
SINGULAR
, we find that the degree sequence of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{gen}}
is

(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4})=(6,6,6,7),

whereas the degree sequence of
\mathcal{B}_{p}
is

(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4},e_{5})=(6,6,6,7,7).

Therefore

(\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{gen}},\mathcal{B}_{p})

is a Ziegler pair of ten lines – you can verify this claim using our
SINGULAR
script, and a procedure indicated therein, that is presented in Appendix A.

Other deletions lead to further examples. The weak combinatorics obtained by deleting
\ell_{i}
are summarized in the following table:

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\text{deleted line}&t_{2}&t_{3}\\
\hline\cr\ell_{1}&15&10\\
\ell_{2}&18&9\\
\ell_{3}&18&9\\
\ell_{4}&15&10\\
\ell_{5}&15&10\\
\ell_{6}&15&10\\
\ell_{7}&15&10\\
\ell_{8}&18&9\\
\ell_{9}&18&9\\
\ell_{10}&15&10\\
\ell_{11}&21&8\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

For
i\geq 2
, set

\mathcal{B}^{i}_{j}:=\mathcal{A}_{j}\setminus\{\ell_{i}\},\qquad j\in\{\mathrm{gen},p\}.

For example,

(\mathcal{B}^{4}_{\mathrm{gen}},\mathcal{B}^{4}_{p})

is another Ziegler pair of ten lines. Its intersection lattice differs from that of
(\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{gen}},\mathcal{B}_{p})
. Indeed, for
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{gen}}
the numbers of triple points lying on the remaining lines are

\begin{array}[]{c|cccccccccc}\text{line}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}&\ell_{4}&\ell_{5}&\ell_{6}&\ell_{7}&\ell_{8}&\ell_{9}&\ell_{10}&\ell_{11}\\
\hline\cr\#\text{ triple points}&3&3&3&3&3&2&3&4&2&4\end{array}

with multiset

\{4,4,3,3,3,3,3,3,2,2\}.

For
\mathcal{B}^{4}_{\mathrm{gen}}
, the corresponding table is

\begin{array}[]{c|cccccccccc}\text{line}&\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}&\ell_{5}&\ell_{6}&\ell_{7}&\ell_{8}&\ell_{9}&\ell_{10}&\ell_{11}\\
\hline\cr\#\text{ triple points}&3&3&4&2&3&2&3&4&2&4\end{array}

with multiset

\{4,4,4,3,3,3,3,2,2,2\}.

Since these multisets are invariants of the intersection lattice and

\{4,4,3,3,3,3,3,3,2,2\}\neq\{4,4,4,3,3,3,3,2,2,2\},

the two deleted arrangements do not have isomorphic intersection lattices.

7.
Known Ziegler pairs with nine and ten lines

Let us briefly recall the known examples of Ziegler pairs with nine and ten lines
which are relevant for the present note.

The classical example is Ziegler’s pair of arrangements of nine lines

[
22
]
. We denote the two arrangements by

\mathcal{A}:g=xyz(x+y+z)(2x+y+z)(2x+3y+z)(2x+3y+4z)(3x+5z)(3x+4y+5z)=0

and

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}:g^{\prime}=xyz(x+y+z)(2x+y+z)(2x+3y+z)(2x+3y+4z)(x+3z)(x+2y+3z)=0.

Both arrangements have the same intersection lattice and weak combinatorics

(n_{2},n_{3})=(18,6),

but their minimal degrees of Jacobian relations are different. With the notation
used here,

{\rm mdr}(g)=6,\qquad{\rm mdr}(g^{\prime})=5.

Geometrically, these arrangements arise from the hexagonal construction: one takes
the six sides of a hexagon and adds the three main diagonals. The value of

{\rm mdr}
depends on whether the six vertices of the hexagon lie on a conic.
This construction was revisited by Dimca and Sticlaru
[
15
]
,
where the conic condition is related to Pascal’s theorem.

There are several natural ten-line extensions of this pair.

First, one can add a line
L
which is generic with respect to both arrangements.
Then

\mathcal{A}\cup L\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cup L

have the same intersection lattice and weak combinatorics

(n_{2},n_{3})=(27,6).

The
{\rm mdr}
-jump persists, shifted by one:

{\rm mdr}(gL)=7,\qquad{\rm mdr}(g^{\prime}L)=6.

Second, one can add a common line through a common double point. For the explicit
classical pair above, take

L_{\rm dbl}:5x+4y=0,

and set

G_{\rm dbl}=g\cdot(5x+4y),\qquad G^{\prime}_{\rm dbl}=g^{\prime}\cdot(5x+4y).

Then the two ten-line arrangements have weak combinatorics

(n_{2},n_{3})=(24,7),

and

{\rm mdr}(G_{\rm dbl})=7,\qquad{\rm mdr}(G^{\prime}_{\rm dbl})=6.

Third, one can add a common line through a common triple point. In the same
coordinates, take

L_{\rm tr}:3x+y+z=0,

and set

G_{\rm tr}=g\cdot(3x+y+z),\qquad G^{\prime}_{\rm tr}=g^{\prime}\cdot(3x+y+z).

Then the added line turns one old triple point into a quadruple point. Thus the
weak combinatorics is

(n_{2},n_{3},n_{4})=(24,5,1).

In this case the two arrangements have the same minimal degree of a Jacobian
relation:

{\rm mdr}(G_{\rm tr})=6,\qquad{\rm mdr}(G^{\prime}_{\rm tr})=6.

Thus this extension is not distinguished by
{\rm mdr}
itself, and we have look at the degree sequences which are
(6,7,7,7,7,7)
and
(6,6,7,7)
, respectively.

There is also a more special ten-line extension described in
Remark 3.1 of
[
15
]
. In that construction one starts with
Ziegler’s arrangement
A_{Z}:f_{Z}=0
, for which the six vertices of the corresponding
hexagon lie on the degenerate conic

Q_{Z}:(x-y-z)(2x-y+z)=0.

One adds the line

L_{Z}:x-y-z=0,

which is one of the two components of
Q_{Z}
. If

B_{Z}=A_{Z}\cup L_{Z}:g_{Z}=0,

then

{\rm mdr}(g_{Z})=5=\frac{10}{2}.

For the corresponding non-conic realization
A^{\prime}_{Z}:f^{\prime}_{Z}=0
, one defines

B^{\prime}_{Z}=A^{\prime}_{Z}\cup L_{Z}:g^{\prime}_{Z}=0.

Then
B_{Z}
and
B^{\prime}_{Z}
have the same intersection lattice, but

{\rm mdr}(g^{\prime}_{Z})=6.

The weak combinatorics of this pair is

(n_{2},n_{3},n_{4})=(18,3,3).

Indeed, the added line
L_{Z}
passes through three old triple points, turning them
into quadruple points, while the remaining three triple points remain triple.

Another ten-line example comes from the orchard construction, equivalently from
the elliptic matroid
T_{10}
, which was studied in
[
3
,
11
]
. It is represented by the one-parameter family

Q_{t}=0
with
t\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\text{forbidden locus}\}
.
The two distinguished realizations are

L_{10}^{1}:Q_{3}=0,\qquad L_{10}^{2}:Q_{\sqrt{5}+3}=0.

They have the same intersection lattice and weak combinatorics

(n_{2},n_{3})=(9,12).

Their modules of Jacobian relations have different minimal resolutions:

0\to S(-8)\to S(-6)^{\oplus 2}\oplus S(-5)\to D_{0}(Q_{3})\to 0,

whereas

0\to S(-8)\oplus S(-7)\to S(-7)\oplus S(-6)^{\oplus 2}\oplus S(-5)\to D_{0}(Q_{\sqrt{5}+3})\to 0.

Thus
L_{10}^{1}
and
L_{10}^{2}
form a Ziegler pair in the homological
sense. In the terminology of
[
1
]
,
L_{10}^{1}
is of type
2A
, while

L_{10}^{2}
is of type
2B
. In this case

{\rm mdr}(Q_{3})={\rm mdr}(Q_{\sqrt{5}+3})=5,

so the difference is not detected by
{\rm mdr}
, but by the higher part of the
minimal resolution.

Our example gives another ten-line Ziegler pair of this homological type. Deleting

\ell_{1}:x=0

from the general arrangement
\mathcal{A}_{31,17}
and from the special arrangement

\mathcal{A}_{p}
, where

p=\left(\frac{3}{10},\frac{2}{7}\right),

we obtain arrangements

\mathcal{B}_{\rm gen}=\mathcal{A}_{31,17}\setminus\{\ell_{1}\},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{p}=\mathcal{A}_{p}\setminus\{\ell_{1}\}.

They have the same intersection lattice and weak combinatorics

(n_{2},n_{3})=(15,10),

but their degree sequences are

(6,6,6,7)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(6,6,6,7,7).

Thus

{\rm mdr}(\mathcal{B}_{\rm gen})={\rm mdr}(\mathcal{B}_{p})=6,

and the difference is visible only in the minimal graded free resolution of the
Milnor algebra.

Summarizing, the relevant examples in degrees
9
and
10
are:

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c}d&\text{arrangement}&\text{weak combinatorics}&\text{difference}\\
\hline\cr 9&\text{classical Ziegler pair}&(n_{2},n_{3})=(18,6)&{\rm mdr}=5\text{ versus }6\\
\hline\cr 10&\text{generic-line extension}&(n_{2},n_{3})=(27,6)&{\rm mdr}=6\text{ versus }7\\
\hline\cr 10&\text{common double-point extension}&(n_{2},n_{3})=(24,7)&{\rm mdr}=6\text{ versus }7\\
\hline\cr 10&\text{common triple-point extension}&(n_{2},n_{3},n_{4})=(24,5,1)&(6,6,7,7)\text{ versus }(6,7,7,7,7,7)\\
\hline\cr 10&\text{Remark~3.1 extension}&(n_{2},n_{3},n_{4})=(18,3,3)&{\rm mdr}=5\text{ versus }6\\
\hline\cr 10&T_{10}\text{-matroid }&(n_{2},n_{3})=(9,12)&\text{type }2A\text{ versus type }2B\\
\hline\cr 10&\text{present deletion example}&(n_{2},n_{3})=(15,10)&(6,6,6,7)\text{ versus }(6,6,6,7,7)\end{array}.

Appendix A

////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////
// Rank test for \mu: S_1 \otimes AR(f)_m ---> AR(f)_{m+1}
// Inspect only the four final variables:
//
// dim_m = dim AR(f)_{m};
// dim_m1 = dim AR(f)_{m+1};
// rank_mu ;
// new_gens;
////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////////

LIB "matrix.lib";

ring R = 0,(x,y,z),dp;

// !Choose parameters!

// Generic point:
// number a = 31;
// number b = 17;

// Special point:
number a = 3/10;
number b = 2/7;

// Lines.

poly L1 = x;
poly L2 = y;
poly L3 = x + y + z;
poly L4 = x + a*y + b*(1-a)*z;
poly L5 = (b-1)*x + (a*b-1)*y + b*(b-1)*(1-a)*z;
poly L6 = (b-1)*x + (a*b-1)*y + (b-1)*z;
poly L7 = x + a*y + a*z;
poly L8 = x + y;
poly L9 = y + (1-b)*z;
poly L10 = x + b*z;
poly L11 = z;

// !Choose arrangement!

// Full arrangement:
poly f = L1*L2*L3*L4*L5*L6*L7*L8*L9*L10*L11;
int m = 7;

// For deletion of L1, use instead:
// poly f = L2*L3*L4*L5*L6*L7*L8*L9*L10*L11;
// int m = 6;

poly fx = diff(f,x);
poly fy = diff(f,y);
poly fz = diff(f,z);

// Coefficient of monomial q in polynomial p.

proc cc(poly p, poly q)
{
number c = 0;
while (p != 0)
{
if (leadmonom(p) == q)
{
c = leadcoef(p);
return(c);
}
p = p - lead(p);
}
return(c);
}

// Matrix whose kernel is AR(f)_d.

proc ARmat(int d)
{
ideal Md = maxideal(d);
ideal Mt = maxideal(d + deg(f) - 1);

int nd = size(Md);
int nt = size(Mt);

matrix A[nt][3*nd];

int i,j;

for (i=1; i<=nd; i++)
{
for (j=1; j<=nt; j++)
{
A[j,i] = cc(Md[i]*fx, Mt[j]);
A[j,nd+i] = cc(Md[i]*fy, Mt[j]);
A[j,2*nd+i] = cc(Md[i]*fz, Mt[j]);
}
}

return(A);
}

// Compute AR(f)_m and AR(f)_{m+1}.

matrix A0 = ARmat(m);
matrix A1 = ARmat(m+1);

module K0 = syz(A0);
module K1 = syz(A1);

int dim_m = ncols(K0);
int dim_m1 = ncols(K1);

// Build the image of S_1 * AR(f)_m in S_{m+1}^3.

ideal Mm = maxideal(m);
ideal Mm1 = maxideal(m+1);

int nm = size(Mm);
int nm1 = size(Mm1);

matrix Images[3*nm1][3*dim_m];

int i,j,k;

for (i=1; i<=dim_m; i++)
{
poly P = 0;
poly Q = 0;
poly H = 0;

for (j=1; j<=nm; j++)
{
P = P + K0[j,i]*Mm[j];
Q = Q + K0[nm+j,i]*Mm[j];
H = H + K0[2*nm+j,i]*Mm[j];
}

for (k=1; k<=nm1; k++)
{
Images[k,3*i-2] = cc(x*P, Mm1[k]);
Images[nm1+k,3*i-2] = cc(x*Q, Mm1[k]);
Images[2*nm1+k,3*i-2] = cc(x*H, Mm1[k]);

Images[k,3*i-1] = cc(y*P, Mm1[k]);
Images[nm1+k,3*i-1] = cc(y*Q, Mm1[k]);
Images[2*nm1+k,3*i-1] = cc(y*H, Mm1[k]);

Images[k,3*i] = cc(z*P, Mm1[k]);
Images[nm1+k,3*i] = cc(z*Q, Mm1[k]);
Images[2*nm1+k,3*i] = cc(z*H, Mm1[k]);
}
}

int rank_mu = rank(Images);
int new_gens = dim_m1 - rank_mu;

// Check these four values.

dim_m;
dim_m1;
rank_mu;
new_gens;
