On Zeckendorf-Niven Numbers and Arithmetic Progressions
Kelly Lao, Steven J. Miller, Nicholas Rosa, Mark Shiliaev, Garrett Tresch, Tony W. H. Wong, Han Zhang

Abstract
A positive integer is Zeckendorf-Niven (respectively, Lucas-Niven) if it is divisible by the number of summands in its Zeckendorf decomposition (respectively, Lucas decomposition). We show that there exist infinitely many Zeckendorf-Niven numbers and Lucas-Niven numbers in every arithmetic progression. Furthermore, we provide bounds on the maximum number of consecutive Zeckendorf-Niven terms in certain arithmetic progressions. MSC: 11B39, 11B25. Keywords: Zeckendorf decomposition, Niven numbers, Zeckendorf-Niven, arithmetic progression.

Introduction

A
Niven number
is a positive integer that is divisible by the sum of its digits in base
10
representation. This definition was first introduced by Niven during a lecture in 1977. Since then, Niven numbers have been studied in the literature in various context
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
12
,
13
,
15
]
. Moreover, the definition of Niven numbers has also been generalized and modified in many different ways. For example, Grundman
[
8
]
defined
b
-Niven numbers as positive integers that are divisible by their digit sums in base
b
, while Gohn et al.
[
7
]
studied
b
-prodigious numbers, i.e., positive integers that are divisible by the product of their nonzero digits in base
b
.

One of the variations that we study are the Zeckendorf-Niven numbers, introduced by Ray and Cooper in 2006
[
16
]
. To understand the definition, we begin with the following theorem by Zeckendorf
[
20
]
. Throughout this paper, we use
\mathbb{N}
to denote the set of positive integers, and for all nonnegative integers
i
,
F_{i}
denotes the
i
-th Fibonacci number, i.e.,
F_{0}=0
,
F_{1}=1
, and
F_{i}=F_{i-1}+F_{i-2}
for all integers
i\geq 2
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a unique sequence
(\zeta_{i}(n))_{i=2}^{\infty}
such that
\zeta_{i}(n)\in\{0,1\}
and
\zeta_{i}(n)+\zeta_{i+1}(n)\leq 1
for all
i\geq 2
, and

n=\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(n)F_{i}.

(1)

Note that the sum in equation (
1
) starts at
i=2
, or else we clearly lose uniqueness (either from adding
F_{0}
as a summand or from switching between
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
.) This sum is called the
Zeckendorf decomposition
of
n
. Although the Zeckendorf decomposition is presented as an infinite sum in equation (
1
), there are only finitely many nonzero summands since all Fibonacci numbers are positive. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the number of nonzero summands in the Zeckendorf decomposition of
n
is defined as
s_{Z}(n)
, i.e.,

s_{Z}(n)\coloneq\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(n).

We say that
n
is
Zeckendorf-Niven
if
s_{Z}(n)
divides
n
.

Similarly, we define Lucas-Niven numbers as follows. For all nonnegative integers
i
, let
L_{i}
denote the
i
-th Lucas number, i.e.,
L_{0}=2
,
L_{1}=1
, and
L_{i}=L_{i-1}+L_{i-2}
for all integers
i\geq 2
. Brown
[
1
]
proved that every positive integer has a unique
Lucas decomposition
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a unique sequence
(\lambda_{i}(n))_{i=0}^{\infty}
such that
\lambda_{i}(n)\in\{0,1\}
and
\lambda_{i}(n)+\lambda_{i+1}(n)\leq 1
for all
i\geq 0
,
\lambda_{0}(n)+\lambda_{2}(n)\leq 1
, and

n=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}(n)L_{i}.

We say that
n
is
Lucas-Niven
if
s_{L}(n)
divides
n
, where

s_{L}(n)\coloneq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}(n).

A common direction of study related to
b
-Niven numbers is their distribution in arithmetic progressions. An
arithmetic progression
is an infinite sequence of positive integers with the same common difference between consecutive terms. If the common difference is
d
, then we call this arithmetic progression a
d
-AP
. Cooper and Kennedy
[
3
]
proved that the maximum number of consecutive Niven terms in a
1
-AP is
20
, while Grundman
[
8
]
and Wilson
[
19
]
generalized this result and proved that the maximum number of consecutive
b
-Niven terms in a
1
-AP is
2b
. Later, Grundman et al.
[
10
]
studied the maximum number of consecutive
b
-Niven terms in a
d
-AP when
d>1
. Harrington et al.
[
11
]
also showed that every arithmetic progression contains infinitely many
b
-Niven numbers.

One of the key results on Zeckendorf-Niven numbers in arithmetic progressions is provided by Grundman
[
9
]
, and she proved that any sequence of consecutive Zeckendorf-Niven numbers greater than
6
has a maximum length of
4
. We show that there are infinitely many Zeckendorf-Niven numbers as well as infinitely many Lucas-Niven numbers in any given arithmetic progression, with the proofs presented in Sections
2
and
3
. We also show in Section
2
that every arithmetic progression contains an arbitrary number of consecutive terms that are all non-Zeckendorf-Niven. In Section
4
, we give upper and lower bounds on the maximum number of consecutive Zeckendorf-Niven terms in a
2
-AP. In Section
5
, we prove that the maximum number of consecutive terms in an
F_{d}
-AP that share the same
s_{Z}
-value is three, and show that there are infinitely many of such occurrences where all three terms are also Zeckendorf-Niven.

2
Infinitely many Zeckendorf-Niven numbers in every arithmetic progression

We begin this section with several preliminary results on Fibonacci numbers.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
14
]
, Theorem 16.1)
.

For all
i,j,\in\mathbb{N}
such that
i\mid j
, we have
F_{i}\mid F_{j}
.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
18
]
)
.

For all
d\in\mathbb{N}
, the sequence
(F_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
is periodic modulo
d
.

The period of the sequence
(F_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
modulo
d
is called the
d
-th Pisano period
and is denoted by
\pi(d)
. Some of the Pisano periods are known.

Proposition 2.3

(
[
6
]
)
.

For all
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\pi(F_{2j})=4j
and
\pi(F_{2j+1})=8j+4
.

The following lemma allows us to focus on arithmetic progressions that have a Fibonacci number as the common difference.

Lemma 2.4
.

For every
a,d\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
F_{2j}\geq a
and
(a+kF_{2j})_{k=0}^{\infty}
is a subsequence of
(a+kd)_{k=0}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

Let
j=a\pi(d)
. Then
F_{2j}\geq F_{2a}\geq a
. The conclusion follows since
F_{2j}=F_{2a\pi(d)}-F_{0}\equiv 0\pmod{d}
by the definition of Pisano period.
∎

The next lemma shows the existence of an appropriate term in the arithmetic progression
(a+kF_{2j})_{k=0}^{\infty}
that achieves a prescribed number of nonzero summands in its Zeckendorf decomposition.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(a+kF_{2j})_{k=0}^{\infty}
be a given arithmetic progression. Then for every integer
m>s_{Z}(a)
, there exists a term
N
in the arithmetic progression
(a+kF_{2j})_{k=0}^{\infty}
such that
s_{Z}(N)=m
.

Proof.

Let
i_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
F_{2i_{0}j}\geq a
, and let

N=a+\sum_{i=i_{0}+1}^{i_{0}+m-s_{Z}(a)}F_{2ij}.

Then
N
is a term in the arithmetic progression
(a+kF_{2j})_{k=0}^{\infty}
by Proposition
2.1
. Furthermore, there are no consecutive Fibonacci numbers inside the summation in the definition of
N
, so
s_{Z}(N)=s_{Z}(a)+m-s_{Z}(a)=m
by the uniqueness of Zeckendorf decomposition.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 2.6
.

Every arithmetic progression contains infinitely many Zeckendorf-Niven numbers.

Proof.

It suffices to show that for every
a,d\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a Zeckendorf-Niven number in the arithmetic progression
(a+kd)_{k=0}^{\infty}
, since the original statement can then be proved inductively by replacing the value of
a
with a larger value at each step. By Lemma
2.4
, we can restrict our attention to arithmetic progressions of the form
(a+kF_{2j})_{k=0}^{\infty}
where
F_{2j}\geq a
. Since
F_{2j+1}>F_{2j}\geq a\geq s_{Z}(a)
, there exists a term
N
in the arithmetic progression
(a+kF_{2j})_{k=0}^{\infty}
such that
s_{Z}(N)=F_{2j+1}
by Lemma
2.5
. To complete the proof, we are going to transform the Zeckendorf decomposition of
N
term-by-term so that each new summand is divisible by
s_{Z}(N)
and yet maintains the same residue modulo the common difference
F_{2j}
of the arithmetic progression.

Let

N=\sum_{i=1}^{F_{2j+1}}F_{r_{i}}

be the Zeckendorf decomposition of
N
, where
r_{1}\geq 2
and
r_{i+1}-r_{i}\geq 2
for all
1\leq i\leq F_{2j+1}-1
. Since
2j+1
and
4j
are coprime, for each
r_{i}
, there exists an integer
0\leq m_{i}<2j+1
such that
m_{i}\equiv-r_{i}(4j)^{-1}\pmod{2j+1}
, i.e.,
2j+1\mid r_{i}+4jm_{i}
. Hence,
F_{2j+1}\mid F_{r_{i}+4jm_{i}}
by Proposition
2.1
. Furthermore, let
t_{i}=r_{i}+4jm_{i}+(2j+1)4ij
for each
1\leq i\leq F_{2j+1}
, and define

N^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{F_{2j+1}}F_{t_{i}}.

Note that the summation provided is the Zeckendorf decomposition of
N^{\prime}
since
t_{1}\geq 2
and

t_{i+1}-t_{i}=r_{i+1}-r_{i}+4j(m_{i+1}-m_{i}+2j+1)>r_{i+1}-r_{i}\geq 2

for all
1\leq i\leq F_{2j+1}-1
, thus
s_{Z}(N^{\prime})=F_{2j+1}
. By Proposition
2.3
, we have
\pi(F_{2j+1})=8j+4
, which divides
t_{i}-(r_{i}+4jm_{i})
for all
1\leq i\leq F_{2j+1}
, so
F_{t_{i}}\equiv F_{r_{i}+4jm_{i}}\equiv 0\pmod{F_{2j+1}}
. Therefore,
F_{2j+1}\mid N^{\prime}
and
N^{\prime}
is Zeckendorf-Niven.

Finally, by Proposition
2.3
,
\pi(F_{2j})=4j
, which divides
t_{i}-r_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq F_{2j+1}
, implying that
F_{2j}\mid F_{t_{i}}-F_{r_{i}}
. Hence,
F_{2j}\mid N^{\prime}-N
, i.e.,
N^{\prime}
is a term in the arithmetic progression
(a+kF_{2j})_{k=0}^{\infty}
.
∎

Before ending this section, we provide a result concerning the distribution of non-Zeckendorf-Niven numbers in arithmetic progressions.

Theorem 2.7
.

Every arithmetic progression contains infinitely many subsequences of
\ell
consecutive non-Zeckendorf-Niven terms, where
\ell
is an arbitrary positive integer.

Proof.

Let
(a+kd)_{k=0}^{\infty}
be a given arithmetic progression. Let
g\in\mathbb{N}
such that
g>a+(\ell-1)d
, and let

j=\lcm\big(\{\pi(s_{Z}(a+kd)+g):0\leq k\leq\ell-1\}\cup\{\pi(d)\}\big).

Note that
j\geq 2
since
\pi(n)\geq 2
for all integers
n\geq 2
. Further let
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
jm\geq r+2
, where
F_{r}
is the largest summand in the Zeckendorf decomposition of
a+(\ell-1)d
.

For each integer
0\leq k\leq\ell-1
, let

a_{k}=a+kd+\sum_{i=1}^{g}F_{j(m+i)}.

If we replace
a+kd
by its Zeckendorf decomposition, then the above summation is the Zeckendorf decomposition of
a_{k}
due to the construction of
jm
and the fact that
j\geq 2
. Hence,
s_{Z}(a_{k})=s_{Z}(a+kd)+g
, implying that
s_{Z}(a_{k})\mid\sum_{i=1}^{g}F_{j(m+i)}
by the definition of
j
. However,
s_{Z}(a_{k})>g\geq a+kd>0
, so
s_{Z}(a_{k})\nmid a_{k}
, i.e.,
a_{k}
is non-Zeckendorf-Niven. The conclusion follows since
a_{k}-a=kd+\sum_{i=1}^{g}F_{j(m+i)}\equiv 0\pmod{d}
.
∎

3
Infinitely many Lucas-Niven numbers in every arithmetic progression

To prove a result analogous to Theorem
2.6
for the Lucas sequence, we need to make small modifications to our technique due to the lack of divisibility properties in the Lucas sequence such as those given by Proposition
2.1
and Lemma
2.4
.

Similar to the Fibonacci sequence, it is known that the Lucas sequence is also periodic modulo
d
for all
d\in\mathbb{N}

[
18
]
. Denote this period by
\pi_{L}(d)
. In the following proposition, we are going to state an interesting relation between
\pi(d)
and
\pi_{L}(d)
as well as some property of
\pi(d)
, which directly implies a subsequent lemma on
\pi_{L}(d)
.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
18
]
)
.

(i)

For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
such that
d\not\equiv 0\pmod{5}
, we have
\pi(d)=\pi_{L}(d)
.

(ii)

For every prime
p
such that
p\equiv-1\pmod{10}
, we have
\pi(p)\mid p-1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For every prime
p
such that
p\equiv-1\pmod{10}
, we have
\pi_{L}(p)\mid p-1
.

The next lemma is an analogue of Lemma
2.5
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(a+kd)_{k=0}^{\infty}
be a given arithmetic progression. Then for every integer
m>s_{L}(a)
, there exists a term
N
in the arithmetic progression
(a+kd)_{k=0}^{\infty}
such that
s_{L}(N)=m
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that there exists a term
N
in the arithmetic progression
(a+kd)_{k=0}^{\infty}
such that
s_{L}(N)=s_{L}(a)+1
, since the original statement can then be proved inductively by replacing the value of
a
with
N
at each step.

Let

a=\sum_{i=1}^{s_{L}(a)}L_{r_{i}}

be the Lucas decomposition of
a
. To simply the notation, let
r=r_{s_{L}(a)}
. Define

N=L_{r-1+3\pi_{L}(d)}+L_{r-2+2\pi_{L}(d)}+\sum_{i=1}^{s_{L}(a)-1}L_{r_{i}}.

Note that the summation provided is the Lucas decomposition of
N
since
(r-1+3\pi_{L}(d))-(r-2+2\pi_{L}(d))\geq 2
and
(r-2+2\pi_{L}(d))-r_{s_{L}(a)-1}\geq(r-2+2\pi_{L}(d))-(r-2)\geq 2
, thus
s_{L}(N)=s_{L}(a)+1
. Furthermore,

N-a=L_{r-1+3\pi_{L}(d)}+L_{r-2+2\pi_{L}(d)}-L_{r}=(L_{r-1+3\pi_{L}(d)}-L_{r-1})+(L_{r-2+2\pi_{L}(d)}-L_{r-2}),

which is divisible by
d
. Therefore,
N
is a term in the arithmetic progression
(a+kd)_{k=0}^{\infty}
.
∎

This leads to the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 3.4
.

Every arithmetic progression contains infinitely many Lucas-Niven numbers.

Proof.

With the same logic presented in the proof of Theorem
2.6
, it suffices to show that for every
a,d\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a Lucas-Niven number in the arithmetic progression
(a+kd)_{k=0}^{\infty}
. By Dirichlet’s Theorem, there are infinitely many primes
p
such that
p\equiv-1\pmod{10\pi_{L}(d)}
. Let
p>s_{L}(a)
be such a prime. Note that
\gcd(p-1,\pi_{L}(d))\leq 2
, which implies that
\gcd(\pi_{L}(p),\pi_{L}(d))\leq 2
by Lemma
3.2
. Since
p>s_{L}(a)
, there exists a term
N
in the arithmetic progression
(a+kd)_{k=0}^{\infty}
such that
s_{L}(N)=p
by Lemma
3.3
. Let

N=\sum_{i=1}^{p}L_{r_{i}}

be the Lucas decomposition of
N
. We are going to complete this proof by considering the following two cases.

Case
1
:
\gcd(\pi_{L}(p),\pi_{L}(d))=1
.

For each
1\leq i\leq p
, there exists an integer
0\leq m_{i}<\pi_{L}(p)
such that

m_{i}\equiv(-r_{i}+1)\pi_{L}(d)^{-1}\pmod{\pi_{L}(p)},

i.e.,
\pi_{L}(p)\mid r_{i}-1+\pi_{L}(d)m_{i}
. Let
t_{i}=r_{i}+\pi_{L}(d)(m_{i}+i\pi_{L}(p))
and define

N^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}L_{t_{i}}.

Note that
\pi_{L}(p)\geq 2
, so the summation provided is the Lucas decomposition of
N^{\prime}
since
t_{1}\geq 2
and
t_{i+1}-t_{i}=r_{i+1}-r_{i}+\pi_{L}(d)\pi_{L}(p)\geq 2
for all
1\leq i\leq p-1
. Consequently,
s_{L}(N^{\prime})=p
. Moreover,
t_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{\pi_{L}(p)}
for all
1\leq i\leq p
, so
N^{\prime}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{p}L_{1}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, implying that
N^{\prime}
is Lucas-Niven. Furthermore, since
\pi_{L}(d)\mid t_{i}-r_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq p
, we have
d\mid N^{\prime}-N
. Therefore,
N^{\prime}
is a term in the arithmetic progression
(a+kd)_{k=0}^{\infty}
.

Case
2
:
\gcd(\pi_{L}(p),\pi_{L}(d))=2
.

Let
E=\{1\leq i\leq p:r_{i}\text{ is even}\}
and
O=\{1\leq i\leq p:r_{i}\text{ is odd}\}
. We consider the following subcases.

Case
2.1
:
|E|\geq|O|
.

Partition
E
into
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
such that
|E_{1}|=|E|-|O|
and
|E_{2}|=|O|
. For each
1\leq i\leq p
, there exists an integer
0\leq m_{i}<\pi_{L}(p)/2
such that

•

m_{i}\equiv-(r_{i}/2)(\pi_{L}(d)/2)^{-1}\pmod{\pi_{L}(p)/2}
if
i\in E_{1}
, i.e.,
\pi_{L}(p)\mid r_{i}+\pi_{L}(d)m_{i}
;

•

m_{i}\equiv-((r_{i}-2)/2)(\pi_{L}(d)/2)^{-1}\pmod{\pi_{L}(p)/2}
if
i\in E_{2}
, i.e.,
\pi_{L}(p)\mid r_{i}-2+\pi_{L}(d)m_{i}
; and

•

m_{i}\equiv-((r_{i}-1)/2)(\pi_{L}(d)/2)^{-1}\pmod{\pi_{L}(p)/2}
if
i\in O
, i.e.,
\pi_{L}(p)\mid r_{i}-1+\pi_{L}(d)m_{i}
.

Similar to Case
1
, let
t_{i}=r_{i}+\pi_{L}(d)(m_{i}+i\pi_{L}(p))
and define
N^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}L_{t_{i}}
. With the same reasoning as in Case
1
, we have
s_{L}(N^{\prime})=p
, and we also know that
N^{\prime}
is a term in the arithmetic progression
(a+kd)_{k=0}^{\infty}
. Moreover,
t_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{\pi_{L}(p)}
if
i\in E_{1}
,
t_{i}\equiv 2\pmod{\pi_{L}(p)}
if
i\in E_{2}
, and
t_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{\pi_{L}(p)}
if
i\in O
, so

N^{\prime}\equiv\sum_{i\in E_{1}}L_{0}+\sum_{i\in E_{2}}L_{2}+\sum_{i\in O}L_{1}\equiv 2(|E|-|O|)+3|O|+|O|\equiv 2p\equiv 0\pmod{p},

implying that
N^{\prime}
is Lucas-Niven.

Case
2.2
:
|E|<|O|
.

Partition
O
into
O_{1}
and
O_{2}
such that
|O_{1}|=|O|-|E|
and
|O_{2}|=|E|
. For each
1\leq i\leq p
, there exists an integer
0\leq m_{i}<\pi_{L}(p)/2
such that

•

m_{i}\equiv-((r_{i}-2)/2)(\pi_{L}(d)/2)^{-1}\pmod{\pi_{L}(p)/2}
if
i\in E
, i.e.,
\pi_{L}(p)\mid r_{i}-2+\pi_{L}(d)m_{i}
;

•

m_{i}\equiv-((r_{i}-1)/2)(\pi_{L}(d)/2)^{-1}\pmod{\pi_{L}(p)/2}
if
i\in O_{1}
, i.e.,
\pi_{L}(p)\mid r_{i}-1+\pi_{L}(d)m_{i}
; and

•

m_{i}\equiv-((r_{i}+1)/2)(\pi_{L}(d)/2)^{-1}\pmod{\pi_{L}(p)/2}
if
i\in O_{2}
, i.e.,
\pi_{L}(p)\mid r_{i}+1+\pi_{L}(d)m_{i}
.

Once again, letting
t_{i}=r_{i}+\pi_{L}(d)(m_{i}+i\pi_{L}(p))
and defining
N^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}L_{t_{i}}
give us
s_{L}(N^{\prime})=p
and that
N^{\prime}
is a term in the arithmetic progression
(a+kd)_{k=0}^{\infty}
. Finally,
t_{i}\equiv 2\pmod{\pi_{L}(p)}
if
i\in E
,
t_{i}\equiv 1\pmod{\pi_{L}(p)}
if
i\in O_{1}
, and
t_{i}\equiv-1\pmod{\pi_{L}(p)}
if
i\in O_{2}
, so by defining
L_{-1}=-1
, we have

N^{\prime}\equiv\sum_{i\in E}L_{2}+\sum_{i\in O_{1}}L_{1}+\sum_{i\in O_{2}}L_{-1}\equiv 3|E|+(|O|-|E|)+(-1)|E|\equiv p\equiv 0\pmod{p},

implying that
N^{\prime}
is Lucas-Niven.
∎

4
Maximum number of consecutive Zeckendorf-Niven terms in a
2
-AP

Motivated by Grundman’s proof
[
9
]
on the maximum number of consecutive Zeckendorf-Niven terms in a
1
-AP, we define
z_{6}(n)=\sum_{i=2}^{6}\zeta_{i}(n)F_{i}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In the following two lemmas, we demonstrate some interesting relation between the values of
z_{6}(n)
and Zeckendorf-Niven numbers.

Lemma 4.1
.

For every integer
n\geq 10
, if
z_{6}(n)=7
and
\zeta_{7}(n)=0
,
z_{6}(n)=4
, or
z_{6}(n)=9
, then
n
and
n+2
cannot be both Zeckendorf-Niven.

Proof.

If
z_{6}(n)=7=F_{3}+F_{5}
and
\zeta_{7}(n)=0
, then
z_{6}(n+2)=9=F_{2}+F_{6}
; if
z_{6}(n)=4=F_{2}+F_{4}
, then
\zeta_{6}(n)=0
, so
z_{6}(n+2)=6=F_{2}+F_{5}
; if
z_{6}(n)=9=F_{2}+F_{6}
, then
\zeta_{7}(n)=0
, so
z_{6}(n+2)=11=F_{4}+F_{6}
. Hence,
s_{Z}(n)=s_{Z}(n+2)
in all cases. Since
n\geq 10
and
z_{6}(n)\in\{7,4,9\}
, we have
s_{Z}(n)\geq 3
. As a result,
s_{Z}(n)
does not divide
(n+2)-n=2
, so
n
and
n+2
cannot be both Zeckendorf-Niven.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

For every integer
n\geq 4
, if
z_{6}(n)=1
or
z_{6}(n)=3
and both
n
and
n+2
are Zeckendorf-Niven, then
s_{Z}(n)=2
.

Proof.

If
z_{6}(n)=1=F_{2}
, then
z_{6}(n+2)=3=F_{4}
since
\zeta_{5}(n)=\zeta_{5}(n+2)=0
; if
z_{6}(n)=3=F_{4}
, then
z_{6}(n)=5=F_{5}
since
\zeta_{6}(n)=\zeta_{6}(n+2)=0
. Hence,
s_{Z}(n)=s_{Z}(n+2)
. If both
n
and
n+2
are Zeckendorf-Niven, then
s_{Z}(n)
divides
(n+2)-n=2
. Since
n\geq 4
and
z_{6}(n)=\{1,3\}
, we have
s_{Z}(n)\geq 2
. Therefore,
s_{Z}(n)=2
.
∎

The following two theorems establish an upper bound and a lower bound for the maximum number of consecutive Zeckendorf-Niven terms in a
2
-AP.

Theorem 4.3
.

The only sequences of eight or more consecutive Zeckendorf-Niven terms in a
2
-AP are subsequences of
2,4,6,8,10,12,14,16,18
.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that
2,4,6,8,10,12,14,16,18
are Zeckendorf-Niven. Now, suppose that
(n+2k)_{k=0}^{7}
is not a subsequence of
2,4,6,8,10,12,14,16,18
and every term is Zeckendorf-Niven. Since
7,19,20
are not Zeckendorf-Niven, we have
n\geq 21
.

By Lemma
4.1
,
z_{6}(n+2k)\notin\{4,9\}
for any integer
0\leq k\leq 6
. We also have
z_{6}(n+2k)\neq 2
for any integer
0\leq k\leq 5
; otherwise,
z_{6}(n+2k+2)=4
since
\zeta_{5}(n+2k)=\zeta_{5}(n+2k+2)=0
. Similarly, we have
z_{6}(n+2k)\neq 0
for any integer
0\leq k\leq 4
; otherwise,
z_{6}(n+2k+2)=2
since
\zeta_{4}(n+2k)=\zeta_{4}(n+2k+2)=0
. If
z_{6}(n)=11=F_{4}+F_{6}
, then the Zeckendorf decomposition of
n+2
is

F_{j}+\sum_{i=j+2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(n)F_{i},

where
j\geq 7
is the smallest integer such that
\zeta_{j}(n)=\zeta_{j+1}(n)=0
. This leads to a contradiction since
z_{6}(n+2)=0
, so we have
z_{6}(n)\neq 11
.

By Lemma
4.1
again,
z_{6}(n+2k)\neq 7
for any integer
0\leq k\leq 6
if
\zeta_{7}(n+2k)=0
. If
z_{6}(n+2k)=3
for some integer
0\leq k\leq 4
, then
s_{Z}(n+2k)=2
by Lemma
4.2
. As a result, we have
\zeta_{7}(n+2k)=\zeta_{7}(n+2k+4)=0
since
n\geq 21
and
z_{6}(n+2k+4)=7
, which is a contradiction. Hence,
z_{6}(n+2k)\neq 3
for any integer
0\leq k\leq 4
. From this, we deduce that
z_{6}(n+2k)\neq 1
for any integer
0\leq k\leq 3
; otherwise,
z_{6}(n+2k+2)=3
since
\zeta_{5}(n+2k)=\zeta_{5}(n+2k+2)=0
.

Next, if
z_{6}(n+2k)=12=F_{2}+F_{4}+F_{6}
for some integer
0\leq k\leq 2
, then the Zeckendorf decomposition of
n+2k+2
is

F_{2}+F_{j}+\sum_{i=j+2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(n+2k)F_{i},

where
j\geq 7
is the smallest integer such that
\zeta_{j}(n+2k)=\zeta_{j+1}(n+2k)=0
. This leads to a contradiction since
z_{6}(n+2k+2)=1
, so we have
z_{6}(n+2k)\neq 12
for any integer
0\leq k\leq 2
. Consequently,
z_{6}(n+2k)\neq 10
for any integer
0\leq k\leq 1
; otherwise,
z_{6}(n+2k+2)=12
since
\zeta_{7}(n+2k)=\zeta_{7}(n+2k+2)=0
. Furthermore,
z_{6}(n)\neq 8
; otherwise,
z_{6}(n+2)=10
since
\zeta_{7}(n)=\zeta_{7}(n+2)=0
.

If
z_{6}(n+2k)=7
for some integer
0\leq k\leq 2
and
\zeta_{7}(n+2k)=1
, then the Zeckendorf decomposition of
n+2k+2
is

F_{2}+F_{j}+\sum_{i=j+2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(n+2k)F_{i},

where
j\geq 8
is the smallest integer such that
\zeta_{j}(n+2k)=\zeta_{j+1}(n+2k)=0
. This again leads to a contradiction since
z_{6}(n+2k+2)=1
, so we have
z_{6}(n+2k)\neq 7
for any integer
0\leq k\leq 2
. Then we also have
z_{6}(n)\neq 5
; otherwise,
z_{6}(n+2)=7
since
\zeta_{6}(n)=\zeta_{6}(n+2)=0
.

The only remaining case is
z_{6}(n)=6=F_{2}+F_{5}
. If
\zeta_{7}(n)=1
, then the Zeckendorf decomposition of
n+2
is

F_{j}+\sum_{i=j+2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(n)F_{i},

where
j\geq 8
is the smallest integer such that
\zeta_{j}(n)=\zeta_{j+1}(n)=0
. This leads to a contradiction since
z_{6}(n+2)=0
. If
\zeta_{7}(n)=0
, then
z_{6}(n+4)=10=F_{3}+F_{6}
, so
s_{Z}(n)=s_{Z}(n+4)
. Since
n\geq 21
and
z_{6}(n)=6
, we have
s_{Z}(n)\geq 3
, thus
s_{Z}(n)=4
. On the other hand, the Zeckendorf decomposition of
n+8
is

F_{2}+F_{j}+\sum_{i=j+2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(n)F_{i},

where
j\geq 7
is the smallest integer such that
\zeta_{j}(n)=\zeta_{j+1}(n)=0
. Hence,
z_{6}(n+8)=1
, which implies that
s_{Z}(n+8)=2
by Lemma
4.2
. The only possibility is
n=F_{2}+F_{5}+F_{8}+F_{10}=82
. However,
s_{Z}(n)=4\nmid n
, contradicting that
n
is Zeckendorf-Niven.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

For every
j\in\mathbb{N}
, let
n=27+F_{120j+17}
. Then
n,n+2,n+4,n+6,n+8
is a sequence of five consecutive Zeckendorf-Niven terms in a
2
-AP.

Proof.

The Zeckendorf decomposition of
n
is
F_{2}+F_{5}+F_{8}+F_{120j+17}
, and it is easy to verify that
s_{Z}(n)=s_{Z}(n+4)=4
,
s_{Z}(n+2)=s_{Z}(n+8)=3
, and
s_{Z}(n+6)=5
. Note that
\pi(3)=8
,
\pi(4)=6
, and
\pi(5)=20
, so
\lcm\{\pi(3),\pi(4),\pi(5)\}=120
, implying that
F_{120j+17}\equiv F_{17}=1597\equiv 37\pmod{3\cdot 4\cdot 5}
. The result follows since
4\mid 27+37
,
3\mid 29+37
,
4\mid 31+37
,
5\mid 33+37
, and
3\mid 35+37
.
∎

5
Consecutive Zeckendorf-Niven terms in an
F_{d}
-AP with the same
s_{Z}
-value

Other than
1
-APs and
2
-APs, the study of consecutive Zeckendorf-Niven numbers in a general
d
-AP is difficult. Here, we choose a special case to consider, namely when the common difference is a Fibonacci number, denoted by
F_{d}
. We further restrict our attention to the scenario when the consecutive terms share the same
s_{Z}
-value.

Generalizing the notation from Section
4
, we define
z_{x,y}(n)=\sum_{i=x}^{y}\zeta_{i}(n)F_{i}
for all
n,x,y\in\mathbb{N}
(if
i<2
, then we define
\zeta_{i}(n)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.) Now, we are ready to present the two main results in this section.

Theorem 5.1
.

For every integer
d\geq 2
, there exists infinitely many
a\in\mathbb{N}
such that
s_{Z}(a)=s_{Z}(a+F_{d})=s_{Z}(a+2F_{d})
. Furthermore, there does not exist
a\in\mathbb{N}
such that
s_{Z}(a)=s_{Z}(a+F_{d})=s_{Z}(a+2F_{d})=s_{Z}(a+3F_{d})
.

Proof.

For every
a\in\mathbb{N}
such that
z_{d-2,d+3}(a)=F_{d-1}
, since
z_{d-2,d+3}(a+F_{d})=F_{d+1}
and
z_{d-2,d+3}(a+2F_{d})=F_{d+2}
, we have
s_{Z}(a)=s_{Z}(a+F_{d})=s_{Z}(a+2F_{d})
.

Now, suppose that
s_{Z}(a)=s_{Z}(a+F_{d})=s_{Z}(a+2F_{d})=s_{Z}(a+3F_{d})
for some
a\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that
z_{d-1,d+1}(a+kF_{d})\neq 0
for all
k\in\{0,1,2\}
; otherwise,
s_{Z}(a+(k+1)F_{d})=s_{Z}(a+kF_{d})+1
. Consequently, we also have
z_{d-1,d+1}(a+kF_{d})\neq F_{d+1}
for all
k\in\{0,1\}
; otherwise,
z_{d-1,d+1}(a+(k+1)F_{d})=0
. Furthermore,
z_{d-1,d+1}(a)\neq F_{d-1}
; otherwise,
z_{d-1,d+1}(a+F_{d})\in\{0,F_{d+1}\}
. If
z_{d-1,d+1}(a+kF_{d})=F_{d-1}+F_{d+1}
for some
k\in\{0,1\}
, then the Zeckendorf decomposition of
a+(k+1)F_{d}
is

\sum_{i=2}^{d-3}\zeta_{i}(a+kF_{d})F_{i}+F_{d-1}+F_{j}+\sum_{i=j+2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(a+kF_{d})F_{i},

where
j\geq d+2
is the smallest integer such that
\zeta_{j}(a+kF_{d})=\zeta_{j+1}(a+kF_{d})=0
. Since
s_{Z}(a+kF_{d})=s_{Z}(a+(k+1)F_{d})
, we have
j=d+2
. As a result, the Zeckendorf decomposition of
a+(k+2)F_{d}
is

\sum_{i=2}^{d-3}\zeta_{i}(a+kF_{d})F_{i}+F_{j^{\prime}}+\sum_{i=j^{\prime}+2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(a+kF_{d})F_{i},

where
j^{\prime}\geq d+3
is the smallest integer such that
\zeta_{j^{\prime}}(a+kF_{d})=\zeta_{j^{\prime}+1}(a+kF_{d})=0
. This leads to a contradiction since
s_{Z}(a+kF_{d})>s_{Z}(a+(k+2)F_{d})
. Thus
z_{d-1,d+1}(a+kF_{d})\neq F_{d-1}+F_{d+1}
for all
k\in\{0,1\}
. Therefore,
z_{d-1,d+1}(a)=F_{d}
.

It is useful to observe that
2F_{i}=F_{i-2}+F_{i+1}
for all integers
i\geq 2
. Next, we have
z_{d-3,d+1}(a)\neq F_{d}
; otherwise,
z_{d-1,d+1}(a+F_{d})\in\{0,F_{d+1}\}
. If
z_{d-3,d+1}(a)=F_{d-3}+F_{d}
, then the Zeckendorf decomposition of
a+F_{d}
is

\sum_{i=2}^{d-5}\zeta_{i}(a)F_{i}+F_{d-1}+F_{j}+\sum_{i=j+2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(a)F_{i},

where
j\geq d+1
is the smallest integer such that
\zeta_{j}(a)=\zeta_{j+1}(a)=0
. Since
s_{Z}(a)=s_{Z}(a+F_{d})
, we have
j=d+1
. This leads to a contradiction since
z_{d-1,d+1}(a+F_{d})=F_{d-1}+F_{d+1}
. Hence, it remains to consider the case when
z_{d-3,d+1}(a)=F_{d-2}+F_{d}
.

Since
F_{d-2}+2F_{d}=2F_{d-2}+F_{d+1}=F_{d-4}+F_{d-1}+F_{d+1}
, we have
z_{d-1,d+1}(a+F_{d})\in\{F_{d-1},F_{d-1}+F_{d+1}\}
. We have shown that
z_{d-1,d+1}(a+F_{d})\neq F_{d-1}+F_{d+1}
, so
z_{d-1,d+1}(a+F_{d})=F_{d-1}
, which implies that
\zeta_{d+2}(a)=1
. Let
j\geq d+3
be the smallest integer such that
\zeta_{j}(a)=\zeta_{j+1}(a)=0
, and let
j^{\prime}\leq d-3
be the largest integer such that
\zeta_{j^{\prime}}(a)=\zeta_{j^{\prime}-1}(a)=0
. If
\zeta_{j^{\prime}-2}(a)=1
, then the Zeckendorf decomposition of
a
is

\sum_{i=2}^{j^{\prime}-4}\zeta_{i}(a)F_{i}+F_{j^{\prime}-2}+\sum_{i=0}^{\frac{d-2-(j^{\prime}+1)}{2}}F_{j^{\prime}+1+2i}+F_{d}+\sum_{i=0}^{\frac{j-1-(d+2)}{2}}F_{d+2+2i}+\sum_{i=j+2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(a)F_{i}

and that of
a+F_{d}
is

\sum_{i=2}^{j^{\prime}-4}\zeta_{i}(a)F_{i}+F_{j^{\prime}}+\sum_{i=0}^{\frac{d-2-(j^{\prime}+1)}{2}}F_{j^{\prime}+2+2i}+F_{j}+\sum_{i=j+2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(a)F_{i}.

This leads to a contradiction since
s_{Z}(a)>s_{Z}(a+F_{d})
. Thus
\zeta_{j^{\prime}-2}(a)=0
, the Zeckendorf decomposition of
a
is

\sum_{i=2}^{j^{\prime}-3}\zeta_{i}(a)F_{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{\frac{d-2-(j^{\prime}+1)}{2}}F_{j^{\prime}+1+2i}+F_{d}+\sum_{i=0}^{\frac{j-1-(d+2)}{2}}F_{d+2+2i}+\sum_{i=j+2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(a)F_{i},

and that of
a+F_{d}
is

\sum_{i=2}^{j^{\prime}-3}\zeta_{i}(a)F_{i}+F_{j^{\prime}-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{\frac{d-2-(j^{\prime}+1)}{2}}F_{j^{\prime}+2+2i}+F_{j}+\sum_{i=j+2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(a)F_{i}.

Since
s_{Z}(a)=s_{Z}(a+F_{d})
, we have
j=d+3
. Then the Zeckendorf decomposition of
a+2F_{d}
is

\sum_{i=2}^{j^{\prime}-3}\zeta_{i}(a)F_{i}+F_{j^{\prime}-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{\frac{d-4-(j^{\prime}+1)}{2}}F_{j^{\prime}+2+2i}+F_{d+1}+F_{d+3}+\sum_{i=d+5}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(a)F_{i}

and that of
a+3F_{d}
is

\sum_{i=2}^{j^{\prime}-3}\zeta_{i}(a)F_{i}+F_{j^{\prime}-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{\frac{d-4-(j^{\prime}+1)}{2}}F_{j^{\prime}+2+2i}+F_{j^{\prime\prime}}+\sum_{i=j^{\prime\prime}+2}^{\infty}\zeta_{i}(a)F_{i},

where
j^{\prime\prime}\geq d+4
is the smallest integer such that
\zeta_{j^{\prime}}(a)=\zeta_{j^{\prime}+1}(a)=0
. This also leads to a contradiction since
s_{Z}(a)>s_{Z}(a+3F_{d})
.
∎

Theorem 5.2
.

For every integer
d\geq 3
, there exists infinitely many
a\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a
,
a+F_{d}
, and
a+2F_{d}
are Zeckendorf-Niven and
s_{Z}(a)=s_{Z}(a+F_{d})=s_{Z}(a+2F_{d})
.

Proof.

Let
m\geq 2
be a factor of
F_{d}
. Then for any nonnegative integer
j
, let

a=F_{d-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}F_{d+2+\pi(m)(i+j)}.

Since
\pi(m)\geq 2
, this is the Zeckendorf decomposition of
a
. Moreover, the Zeckendorf decompositions of
a+F_{d}
and
a+2F_{d}
are

a+F_{d}=F_{d+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}F_{d+2+\pi(m)(i+j)}

and

a+2F_{d}=F_{d+2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}F_{d+2+\pi(m)(i+j)},

respectively. Hence,
s_{Z}(a)=s_{Z}(a+F_{d})=s_{Z}(a+2F_{d})=m
. Since
m
divides
F_{d}
, in order for
a
,
a+F_{d}
, and
a+2F_{d}
to be Zeckendorf-Niven, it suffices to show that
m
divides
a
. This follows since

a\equiv F_{d-1}+(m-1)F_{d+2}=F_{d-1}+(m-1)(F_{d-1}+2F_{d})=mF_{d-1}+2(m-1)F_{d}\equiv 0\pmod{m},

where the first congruence is due to the definition of the Pisano period.
∎
