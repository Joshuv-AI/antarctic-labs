GeoDial: A Multimodal Dialog Tutoring Dataset for Geometry Problem-Solving with Visual Tutor Turns
Sankalan Pal Chowdhury, Junling Wang, Donya Rooein, April Yi Wang, Mrinmaya Sachan

Abstract
Several educational domains rely heavily on diagrams and visual cues, yet most existing tutoring datasets are limited to text-only interactions. This limits the development of AI tutors that can teach in visually grounded ways used by human instructors. Thus, we introduce GeoDial, a multimodal tutoring dataset of over 1.3K teacher-student dialogs in the domain of geometry collected from experienced math teachers, where instructional turns are explicitly grounded in diagram highlights. We propose a scalable annotation protocol that integrates dialog acts, visual highlighting, and feedback, enabling fine-grained supervision of both language and visual tutoring behavior. To illustrate the challenges posed by this setting, we fine-tune several vision–language models on GeoDial and evaluate their ability to generate tutoring utterances and diagram highlights. While supervised fine-tuning substantially improves the quality of generated dialog, it struggles to produce accurate diagram highlights, revealing a key limitation of current methods and highlighting the need for approaches that more effectively integrate visual reasoning with pedagogical interaction.

Introduction

Students are often said to remember 10% of what they read, 20% of what they hear, and 30% of what they see, a widely cited interpretation of Dale’s “Cone of Experience”
(
13
)
.
Although specific percentages can be debated
(
39
)
, a large body of research emphasizes the critical role that visual information plays in learning across subjects
(

48
)
such as mathematics
(
40
)
. Indeed, it is hard to picture a “classroom” without a blackboard, or one of its high-tech replacements. For at least 2 centuries, the blackboard has acted as a visual mirror for mental processes
(
36
)
.

Yet, in the era of rapid adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) in education, their pedagogy largely remains limited to the textual modality.

Indeed, computer-based tutors that existed before LLMs, such as AutoTutor
(
19
)
and Cognitive Tutor
(
38
)
, also used both text and diagrams. However, they were hindered by scaling issues in authoring their scripts
(
25
)
. The ability of LLMs to distill knowledge in fluent natural language is seen as a way around these scaling issues, leading to the creation of several datasets
(
29
;
44
;
14
;
47
;
51
)
recording different types of teacher-student interactions, which could then be used to train LLMs to be better teachers
(
46
)
. However, the initial LLMs had limited visual capabilities.

With the rising capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs), which can work with images in addition to text, there is an opportunity to bring back the visual modality in tutoring.
A first step toward this goal is to develop multimodal tutoring datasets that capture how teachers coordinate language with visual guidance.
In this work, we propose GeoDial

1

The full dataset is made available via
Kaggle
, a collection of over 1.3K richly annotated geometry tutoring dialogs that capture teacher responses to controlled, diagram-grounded student errors, including teachers’ diagram annotations, visual highlights, and turn-level textual feedback.
This provides, to our knowledge, the first dataset for training and evaluating VLMs that support tutoring through coordinated language and visual guidance.

Our experiments show that standard training on GeoDial improves pedagogical tutor-turn generation, but does not produce teacher-like diagram highlights: models learn to abstain more often, yet still struggle to select the correct visual elements. Further analysis suggests that this challenge stems from both highlight sparsity and the tight coupling between visual actions and teacher utterances. Focused highlight experiments show that separating highlight prediction from utterance generation and reweighting positive examples improves performance, suggesting that GeoDial exposes a challenging task for visually grounded pedagogical modeling. We open-source our dataset and models, with the aim of encouraging future work to overcome these limitations through better training and modeling paradigms.

2
Data Annotation Protocol

2.1
Annotation Process Overview

Our goal is to gather tutoring conversations where a teacher is interacting one-on-one with a student. Good quality tutoring is a balancing act: offering a student too little help might lead to them getting bored and disengage, while offering too much support can cause them to become over dependent
(
37
;
8
)
. The goal of the tutor is thus to guide students to the correct solution of a problem, without revealing too much of the solution themselves. Here, we focus on the corrective behaviour more than supportive behaviour.

We assume that the student has already made an attempt to solve a problem, and has gotten it wrong, before approaching the tutor. The conversation starts with the teacher seeing the question, the associated diagram, the correct solution and the student’s solution. The student also has access to all of these, except the correct solution.
The student, in our setup, is played by a VLM. We use VLM students as a controlled elicitation mechanism to collect teacher responses to plausible geometry errors under comparable conditions. While this weakens ecological validity, it avoids privacy and consent issues, speeds up data collection, and limits filler or generic utterances not specific to tutoring. Further, as we discuss in
Section

4.2
, teachers judged the simulated students to exhibit strong fidelity to real student behavior.

Figure

1
shows an overview of our setup. The conversation proceeds with the teacher and the student taking turns, until the teacher decides to end the conversation. Each utterance required the teachers to select an act and a subact, make optional highlights to the diagram and select a feedback type. The act and subact specify the teacher’s strategy while the feedback is usually a single sentence prefix to the utterance, either acknowledging what they did (eg ‘Good job,’ ‘That’s right’ etc.) or encouraging them to reconsider (eg. ‘Not quite.’). We discuss these in
Sections

2.2
and
2.4
respectively. The highlights offer a visual channel of communication, which is the core contribution of GeoDial. We shall discuss them in
Section

2.3
. All these act as guides for the actual utterance.
Hereafter, three VLMs (Gemini-Flash-2.5
(
18
)
, GPT4o
(
33
)
, and Claude-Sonnet-4
(
2
)
) suggest candidate utterances from the teacher-provided guides, leaving the annotator in control of the tutoring strategy. This process is repeated until the annotator wishes to end the conversation. A screenshot of the full annotation interface is provided in
Figure

15
. After each conversation, annotators complete a debrief form (details in
Section

4.2.1
).

Using VLMs to suggest teacher utterances also poses a challenge to ecological validity. However, suggesting potential utterances to the teacher works to reduce their workload and resulting fatigue, and previous work has shown that fatigue can affect tutoring quality to the extent that human tutors are perceived as worse than AI tutors
(
34
)
. We note that these suggestions are still based on the guides provided by the teacher so they remain in control of the overarching strategy. Also, they can always choose to ignore suggestions and write their own utterance.

2.2
Dialog Acts

Dialog acts are the first step to specify the intent of the annotator, and thus need to be specific in order for the VLMs to generate the best possible suggestions. To aid the annotators in easily selecting their intent, we define a two-level hierarchy of
5

16
subacts. Four of our acts are taken from MathDial
(
29
)
, namely
Focus
, which are utterances making direct progress towards the solution,
Probing
, which tests the student’s understanding of underlying concepts,
Telling
, which reveals the solution or parts of it and
Generic
, which covers non-pedagogical conversational elements. To this we add a fifth act,
SeeFigure
, which deals with the visual component of our task. These acts are then split into 16 subacts as shown in
Figure

2
. The subacts for the MathDial acts are mostly sourced from the intents they define in Table 2 of their paper. Each subact is associated with a prompt given to each of the 3 proposer VLMs. The full list of subacts, their descriptions, and examples is provided in
Table

2.3
Highlighting the Diagram

Highlighting the diagram is an important part of the task, and is mainly used to get the student to focus on certain parts of the diagram. Once the annotator has selected the act and subact, they can proceed to highlight the diagram. The diagram is present in the top left corner of their screen (see
Figure

15
), and the annotators are provided with a pen tool with which they can draw on the diagram. The color of this pen is fixed to green, as it is the rarest on the diagrams of the basic English colors, thereby unlikely to be confused with parts of the base diagram by the student model. An Eraser tool is also provided to fix mistakes. Additionally, we allow annotators to clear all their highlights and start fresh, or reuse the highlights from the previous step if they wish.

While annotators are allowed to highlight for all acts and subacts, some subacts, like
Farewell
and
PullBack
, are unlikely to need highlights. On the other hand, the
SeeFigure
act is dedicated to looking at highlights, and therefore must have a corresponding highlight. The
AskRelation
subact is special in that it needs to make two independently identifiable highlights, and for this reason, we provide a second brown pen for this subact. While we provide examples of how to highlight lines, angles, arcs, etc., in the annotator instructions, they are free to choose exactly how to proceed. An example highlight from our dataset is shown in
Figure

4
.

2.4
Feedback Selection

Feedback, both positive
(
30
)
and negative
(
16
)
is an important component in tutoring. However, the feedback component can often be small and inactionable, so making it an independent act or subact might not be the best idea. Therefore, we instead choose to give an option to add a positive or negative feedback to the start of the utterance by selecting the feedback type. There is also an option to give no feedback. The annotators indicate whether the student is on the right path or the wrong path, and the prompt is modified accordingly to provide positive or negative feedback. Note that pure feedback statements are still possible through the
Continue
subact.

Data Collection

3.1
Sourcing Problems and Student Errors

We draw our problems from the Geometry3k
(
27
)
and PGPS9k
(
55
)
. Geometry3k is a dataset of
3,000
geometry problems with associated diagrams and annotations for diagram elements. PGPS9k is an open-source dataset which seeks to extend Geometry3k to
9,000
problems. We make use of the entire training set of Geometry3k and
1,700

We then used
gemini-2.5-flash-preview-05-20

(
18

, hereafter referred to as Gemini, to identify and remove problems whose diagrams lacked point labels, since point labels provide an important mechanism for referring to specific diagram elements in instructional discourse. Although this filtering step may introduce some noise, it provides a practical way to reduce diagrams without usable point references.
Then, we used smaller models (details in App.
J.3
) to generate potential student errors. For each question, we generated
10
potential solutions, and only kept questions where at least two of these had the wrong final answer. Finally, we used Gemini to generate step-by-step correct solutions, dropping any questions whose final answer does not match the official answer. After applying all these filters, we were left with
1,422
problems
2
2

2

The number of problems in the initial set was incrementally increased until we reached about
1,400
problems, given our annotation budget.

3.3
Post Processing

The free form highlights made by the annotators are a convenient way for humans to communicate, but looking at the highlights, it is easy to see that the true message being communicated has significantly fewer degrees of freedom. The vast majority of highlights can be expressed as either line segments, angles, arcs, and, at times, values marked within the diagram or point names.
Moreover, the same target can be highlighted in multiple valid ways. For example, an angle may be indicated by circling it, tracing over its sides, or drawing an arc inside it, and even within each strategy, the exact highlight position may vary.
Since these differences usually carry no information, they are essentially noise, which would make both training and evaluation hard.

To deal with this issue, we standardise all highlights as lines, angles and arcs, identified by the named points forming them. The remaining highlights are consolidated into a fourth category: labels. These annotations are text-based and were produced by the first author.
Since these highlights are text based, they also open up the possibility of using VLMs that can’t generate images. We discuss how we can recover the highlights from these textual annotations in
Appendix

J
.

4.2
Student Simulation Fidelity

Because students are role-played by VLMs, we evaluate whether the collected dialogs reflect realistic tutoring interactions. We use a two-pronged approach: after each conversation, annotators answer debrief questions about the fidelity of different components; we also interview 5 of the 11 main contributors to obtain a higher-level view of AI student behavior and teacher responses.

4.2.1
Debrief Questions

After each conversation, we ask the annotators to rate Q1) Student Simulation Fidelity, Q2) Teacher Message Suggestion Fidelity, Q3) Usefulness of Teacher Message Suggestions, and Q4) Student’s understanding of Diagrams, on a 5-point Likert scale. To reduce directional bias, the scales for Q1 and Q3 went low to high, while Q2 and Q4 went high to low. When reporting, we flip Q2 and Q4 so higher is better for all ratings.

We find that all the 4 questions had a median score of
5
while the
mean scores ranged from
4.28
for Q3 to
4.52
for Q1.
Overall, annotators judged the AI-simulated components to have high fidelity in most conversations.
The full distributions for the ratings, and the full question texts can be found in
Figure

14
.

4.2.2
Annotator Interviews

We interviewed 5 of our 11 main contributors to get a high level view of how realistic the AI students and their confusions felt, how it compared to their real life students, and most importantly, if the student fidelity was good enough such that the collected conversations would reflect their real teaching strategies. All of these teachers had at least three years of teaching experience and had interacted with at least 100 unique students.

A detailed interview analysis is provided in
Appendix

I
.
In summary, the interviewees found that GeoDial students were slightly more engaged than real students, but showed less emotional engagement, consistent with
31
.
In diagram understanding and teacher frustration, GeoDial students were perceived as comparable to human students. Interviewees also reported that initial student confusions were largely indistinguishable from those of real students, and that they only slightly adapted their teaching strategies in GeoDial, suggesting that the dataset captures realistic tutoring behavior.

5.1
Main Experiment

Setup:

We emulate the annotation setup as closely as possible to preserve the original conditional dependencies and avoid circular prediction.
The model is given the context, comprising the question, the diagram (for models which can handle diagrams), the correct answer, the student answer and the conversation so far. Using these, the model must generate, in order, the act, the subact, the highlights, the feedback type and finally the teacher utterance. The prompt explains the setup, followed by brief descriptions of the acts, subacts, and feedback types. It also explains the output format, including how to output highlights in text. The full prompt including the output format is presented in
Appendix

O
).

Metrics:
We propose several metrics to evaluate tutor models.
As act, subact, and feedback are categorical, we report Macro-F1. For the final teacher utterance, we report BLEU
(
35
)
and BERTScore
(
56
)
. For highlights, the output for each category is a set, so we could calculate the F1 score for each output and report the average over all instances. However, this F1 score is undefined when the ground-truth highlight set is empty, which is rather common in our task.
Thus, we report 2 scores:
Prediction
and
Decision
.
Prediction
Accuracy (and
Prediction
F1) looks at whether or not the model correctly predicted that it must make a highlight of the current category. It ignores whether the highlight is correct; e.g., if the ground truth is that line segment AB should be highlighted, the model is considered correct (true positive) as long as it highlights at least one line segment.
If, however, the ground truth has no highlighted line segments, the model also must highlight no line segments to be considered correct (true negative).
Decision
F1, on the other hand, is the average F1 score calculated over the instances where it is defined, i.e., where the ground truth is non-empty. We ignore highlight colors, merging green and brown highlights of the same type.

Models:
We evaluate on 3 closed source models (Gemini, GPT and Claude, same ones used to generate teacher utterances), 3 open source text-only models (Qwen3 8B and 32B, Llama 3.1 8B) and 4 VLMs (Qwen3-VL 8B and 32B, InternVL 8B and Llama 3.2 11B Vision). For the open models, we evaluate them both with and without fine-tuning on GeoDial. We also report a
Random Turn
baseline, which picks a random entry from the dataset as the output. This is a slightly better random baseline, as the outputs match the distribution of the dataset.

Results:

Table

1
reports test-set results, with validation set results in
Table

.
For closed models and open-source models without fine-tuning, outputs sometimes violate the required format and cannot be parsed; in such cases, we assign a score of 0 for categorical variables and teacher utterances, and treat highlight outputs as empty sets.

Categorical variables and teacher utterances follow the expected trends.
Closed models outperform open-source models without fine-tuning, while fine-tuned models perform best overall.
A qualitative analysis comparing utterances from the fine-tuned models with those from the base models shows that fine-tuning improves scaffolding, dialog-flow control, and grounding in the highlighted regions (see
Appendix

M
for details).
Vision-enabled models generally outperform text-only models, although the gap is sometimes small.
Larger models tend to perform better, with Qwen3-VL-32B achieving the strongest overall results.
Most models outperform the Random Turn baseline.

Highlight prediction and selection remain challenging.

Without fine-tuning, lines and angles achieve non-trivial
Prediction
and
Decision
scores, but performance remains weak. Arcs and labels obtain high
Prediction
accuracy because they are rare and empty-set predictions are often correct, yet their F1 scores remain low.
InternVL illustrates this pattern: formatting errors often result in empty-set predictions, inflating Prediction accuracy for rare categories.
As a result, the
Decision
scores for these categories are negligible.

Fine-tuning improves Prediction but can hurt Decision.

Fine-tuning improves Prediction across the board, but lowers Decision scores for lines and angles.
A zero Decision F1 can result either from predicting highlights that miss all correct ones, or from leaving the instance empty.
As shown in
Table

7
, fine-tuning substantially decreases misses, but increases leaves enough to reduce the overall Decision scores.
Thus, fine-tuned models learn to abstain from highlight selection more often, which is unsurprising given the strong empty-set imbalance in the data: the empty set is correct for approximately 77% of line instances and 83% of angle instances, with even higher rates for arcs and labels.
This imbalance also helps explain their weak Decision scores.
Finally, because Decision F1 does not penalize models for predicting highlights when none are needed, it should be interpreted together with Highlight F1, which captures such false-positive cases.

Focused Highlight Model:
In

Since highlights appear particularly challenging, we investigate whether a more focused setup improves highlight generation. We focus on the two most common highlight types in our dataset, lines and angles, and split the task of
Decision
and

Decision
and
Prediction
for lines and angles. The model input consists of the dialogue context, the act and the subact. The
Decision

The results of focused experiments are shown in
Table

. The base
Decision
F1, although substantially higher than in the previous setting, remains relatively low.
Since the highlight model is now separated from utterance generation, we can reverse their generation order.
We observe that highlight performance improves significantly when highlights are generated using ground-truth utterances as input. This behavior is expected, as the ground truth utterances were originally generated
after
the teacher made highlights, and can often make direct reference to what was highlighted. But this same fact makes this setting unrealizable at inference. To avoid this, we instead train a separate utterance generation model and use the utterances from this model instead. The utterances from this model do not have access to the highlights, and thus, even though it performs worse than the ground truth version, it is realistic.

Weighted training:

Next, we explore alternate training strategies for highlight generation.
In
Section

5.1

To test this hypothesis, we adopted a weighted training approach for the highlight model, where each positive sample was repeated three times per epoch, and each negative sample appeared once per epoch.
This generally improved highlighting performance (see Weighted in

7
Conclusion

We introduce GeoDial, to our knowledge the first dataset of geometry tutoring dialogs that tightly integrates conversational teaching strategies with diagram-based visual guidance.
GeoDial captures rich pedagogical behavior, with nearly half of tutoring turns grounded in diagram highlights and a diverse distribution of instructional strategies.
Through fine-tuning experiments, we show that current VLMs can learn to generate higher-quality tutor utterances, but still struggle to produce meaningful diagram highlights, even with specialized training.
This gap highlights visually grounded pedagogical action as a distinct challenge for multimodal tutoring, and positions GeoDial as a resource for developing models that better coordinate instructional language with visual guidance.

Appendix A
Limitations and Societal Impact

In this paper, we propose a novel dataset exploring dialog based tutoring in textual and visual modalities. While GeoDial does make use of visuals, it is still rather limited in the type of visuals. We instruct our annotators to highlight parts of an existing diagram, which leaves out several other visual interactions, even within the limited purview of geometry, like constructions, spontaneous sketching, etc. Furthermore, these interactions are one-way, i.e., the student cannot modify the diagram. It is also worth noting that drawing with a mouse is difficult, which likely discouraged annotators from making complex highlights, something that would not have occurred if they had used pen and paper or a blackboard.

Further, we must also acknowledge that Geometry forms only a small part of mathematics and an even smaller part of education in general. Tutoring techniques vary widely across these subjects, so more datasets need to be collected for other subjects. Visual interactions in other domains, such as Physics may require significant adaptations such as drawing free-body diagrams, etc. which we leave for future work.

Another shortcoming is the fact that the ground truth teacher utterance and highlights are one of potentially more than one equally valid next step at any given point. This limitation, however, is not unique to us, and plagues most work in Natural Language Processing, as there is typically more than one correct way to express something.

Finally, in our approach, both the student errors and student utterances are generated by VLMs, which might not accurately represent real students. We attempt to address this issue by our debrief questions, and interviews, which yield highly positive responses. However, we must note that teachers are not trained to detect fake students, so their judgment is not necessarily absolute. Even if they are right and the models simulate a single student quite well, there is no reason to believe that all types of students are equally represented. Since our dataset is aimed at training teacher models, a reasonable but not perfect student might serve our purposes. However,

GeoDial dialogs should not be used to train student models.

GeoDial is meant to be used to make LLMs better at visual tutoring, a task which has traditionally been the domain of human tutors. While this may lead to concerns about the potential replacement of humans with AI, it is far more likely to slot in to areas where human alternatives do not exist, due to the shortage of good teachers. This is likely to help students significantly. Such methods can also make the job of existing teachers easier by taking over more repetitive tasks.

Appendix B
Issues with Standardising Highlights

While this highlighting makes the task much more structured, we must note that it takes away some amount of nuance. As an example, annotators at times wrote out certain values on the diagram, which do not transfer to these annotations. We also found more creative use of highlights. For example, one annotator marked four different pairs of angles in four different ways to indicate that each pair was equal. While we were able to annotate which angles were highlighted, we could not differentiate between the different styles used. Two more such examples are shown in
Figure

. Some of the more common peculiar highlights were accounted for by using the “Labels” field as a miscellaneous entry. While these were not negligible, they only form a small fraction of all highlight annotations. In particular, of the

1172
label highlights. Of these,
217

49

232

651

2.0\%

0.25\%

Despite our attempts to avoid this in the question setup, there were cases in which highlighted elements could not be annotated because the associated points were not named in the figure. Luckily, in all such cases, the required points had been named correctly in the data source (Geometry3K or PGPS9K), so we simply used these names and marked the points as “Missing”.

Appendix E
A Combined Highlight Score

Let
H_{i}
be the ground truth set of highlights for the
i^{th}
data point. A data point here refers to a single teacher’s utterance to which highlights may be attached. This set can be empty if no highlights are made of the current category for the
i^{th}
data point.
Let
\pi_{i}
be the output of the corresponding
Prediction
model, which is
1
if the model wants to highlight or
0
if it does not. Finally, let
\Delta_{i}
be the output of the
Decision
model for the
i^{th}
data point, which is again a set, but cannot be the empty set as per model instructions.

We start by defining
D_{i}
, the effective set of highlights, which is identical to
\Delta_{i}
if the
Prediction
model outputs a ‘yes’ but the empty set otherwise:

\displaystyle D_{i}=\begin{cases}\Delta_{i}&if\pi_{i}=1\\
\phi&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

For the individual data point, the f1 (note the use of lower case f) as:

f1_{i}=\frac{2*|H_{i}\cap D_{i}|}{|H_{i}\cup D_{i}|+|H_{i}\cap D_{i}|}

Normally, this definition is only valid if
|H_{i}\cap D_{i}|
(i.e. true positives) is non-zero, but here we extend it to all cases where
|H_{i}\cup D_{i}|
is defined and non-zero. If however
H_{i}
is empty,
\Delta_{i}
is undefined, and thus
D_{i}
is also undefined.
In such a case, if
\pi_{i}
is
1
, we are saying that the
Prediction
model decided to make some highlight. If, hypothetically, we did run the
Decision
model on this example, we would still get some output
\Delta^{\prime}_{i}
with non-zero cardinality. Regardless of the actual members of
\Delta^{\prime}_{i}
,
|H_{i}\cup D^{\prime}_{i}|
would be non-zero and
|H_{i}\cap D^{\prime}_{i}|
would be 0, giving an
f1=0
. This leaves out the case where
|H_{i}|=\pi_{i}=0
. There is no realistic way to define it, but we still set the score to
0
to simplify later formulations. So we update our definition to be:

\displaystyle f1_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{2*|H_{i}\cap D_{i}|}{|H_{i}\cup D_{i}|+|H_{i}\cap D_{i}|}&if|H_{i}|>0\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Armed with this definition, we can now proceed to aggregating over data points

We already have the
Decision
Accuracy we defined in
Section

5.1
which aggregates over only those instances where
|H_{i}|>0
.
This is like an overall recall, since we are only aggregating over cases where highlights were needed. Let us call this
R
:

R=\frac{\sum_{i}f1_{i}}{\sum_{i}\mathbb{I}(|H_{i}|>0)}

A corresponding precision score would act over all cases where
\pi_{i}=1
. We call this
P
:

P=\frac{\sum_{i}f1_{i}}{\sum_{i}\pi_{i}}

Note that cases where
|H_{i}|=\pi_{i}=0
never contribute to the denominator, so setting
f1_{i}=0
for these cases effectively excludes them from the calculation, as true negatives usually are. Finally, we can define the Combined Highlight Score
F1
(note the upper case F) as

F1=\frac{2}{\frac{1}{R}+\frac{1}{P}}

Appendix G
Detailed Related Work

G.1
Educational Dialog Datasets

The development of educational tutoring systems has been driven by the availability of educational dialog datasets. Recent efforts have focused on collecting text-only tutoring conversations, such as MathDial
[
29
]
, where teachers interacted with LLM-simulated students to produce pedagogically grounded instructional dialogs.
Several other datasets were created by having crowdworkers role-play both teacher and student, such as CIMA
[
44
]
, by transcribing classroom interactions or logging online conversations
[
45
,
14
,
9
]
, or by using models to simulate teacher and student
[
49
]
.
However, these resources are primarily text-based and do not capture teacher visual actions over diagrams, despite the heavy use of diagrams in math education. In contrast, GeoDial provides dialogs that integrate diagram interpretation and fine-grained diagram highlight annotations by teachers.

G.2
Multimodal Geometry Datasets

Research on multimodal geometry datasets has primarily aimed to enable models to solve geometry problems by integrating textual descriptions with diagrammatic information.
Influential work by Seo et al.
[
41
,
42
]
established diagram understanding as a core challenge in geometry problem solving, introducing structured representations that jointly model visual elements, spatial relations, and their alignment with textual descriptions for geometry problem solving.
This led to several larger datasets and efforts of symbolic and later neural reasoners for geometry problems.
For instance, Geometry3K
[
27
]
introduced the notion of diagram components and solution programs to support rule-based geometry reasoning.
Subsequent works further increased annotation granularity: PGPS9K
[
55
]
introduced fine-grained parsing and structural labels to support reasoning over geometric elements.
Another direction of research in geometry problem solving focused on coordinate geometry driven numerical calculation.
For instance, GeoQA
[
11
]
demonstrated that pairing diagrams with question–answer supervision enables numerical reasoning over visual geometry problems.
UniGeo
[
10
]
spanned both calculation and proving, including tasks such as computing missing angles and proving triangle congruence.
Recent work has also focused on scaling multimodal geometry resources for model training.
Geo170K
[
17
]
, for example, provides a large corpus of automatically generated diagram-instruction pairs, while emerging datasets such as GeoSense
[
54
]
explore conceptual reasoning by evaluating whether models can recognize and apply geometric principles.
Despite these advances, existing multimodal geometry datasets do not capture how teachers interact with diagrams to guide student learning. GeoDial addresses this gap by offering multimodal tutoring dialogs grounded in geometry diagrams, with explicit annotations that connect visual cues to instructional intent.

G.3
Visuals in Mathematics Education

Visuals have long been recognized as critical tools in mathematics education
[
23
,
6
]
. Research indicates that visual representations help students grasp abstract concepts more readily
[
43
,
32
,
15
]
, increase engagement
[
12
]
, and improve learning efficiency
[
3

.
Within geometry education in particular, visual cues such as highlighted segments and annotated angles have been shown to support students in learning geometry concepts
[

and help them grasp geometric relationships
[

.

Despite this pedagogical importance, existing educational datasets and model training pipelines rarely incorporate visual grounding. GeoDial embeds visual feedback directly into the tutoring interaction: teacher utterances reference highlighted regions of diagrams, and the dataset provides structured visual annotations that enable models to learn to generate diagram highlights.

Appendix H
Filtering the Dialogs

We filtered out some dialogs which had quality issues.
Most of the errors in the data collection were due to network issues, causing errors with the order in which utterances were shown to the annotator and the order in which they were presented to the student LLM. While such dialogs usually still make sense, it is unclear what the correct order would be, and hence we decided to drop any conversations where this happened. These errors would manifest as either the interleaving of two conversations, or consecutive student and/or teacher turns. The interleaving happened in 25 of the 237 batches we collected, and these files were manually inspected by the first author to keep only clean conversations. A further 25 conversations were dropped due to consecutive turns. Finally, for one batch, the highlighted diagrams did not get saved on our server due to reasons unclear to us. This was also dropped.

Appendix I
Annotator Interviews for Assessing Faithfulness of Student Simulation

Our interview, inspired by
31
, consisted of 6 qualitative questions, each followed by a rating on a 5-point Likert scale. These questions, in order, dealt with student engagement, student emotions, diagram/highlight understanding, error quality, teacher frustration and teacher strategy (the full text is provided in
Table

16
). Interviewees were asked to compare their experience in teaching and tutoring real students with their experience in GeoDial. For the first 3 questions, the Likert rating went from 1=GeoDial students show better engagement/ more emotions/ better diagram understanding, to 5=Real students show better engagement/ more emotions/ better diagram understanding. For teacher frustration the scale is reversed since it usually hurts teaching
[
7
]
. Error quality discussed how realistic the student wrong solutions were, and the scale went from 1=Indistinguishable from real student errors to 5=Very unrealistic. Finally, the teaching strategy question tried to understand if the general strategy the interviewees used for GeoDial was any different from what they would use with actual students and the Likert rating went from 1=No change in strategy to 5=Very different strategy. The interviews ended with a general feedback question asking if the interviewees wanted to suggest any changes in the setup. The full set of questions is provided in
Table

16
.

All interviewees were initially asked to talk about their teaching experiences. Four of them were regular teachers having 3, 5, 6 and 10 years of teaching experience, having taught approximately 150, 450, 500, and 300 unique students over their career, respectively. The fifth interviewee has worked with a homework support website, where students can upload their homework questions to get paired with tutors who can help them online. He has been working for about 9 years and has had over 30,000 individual sessions. Although this is not the same as classroom teaching, we believe it is rather close to our setup.

The interviewees believed engagement to be slightly better for the AI students (Ratings= 2/2/2/2/3) used in GeoDial, which is not surprising since AI models, unlike real students, cannot opt to disengage or leave the conversation. Our definition of engagement included contesting the teachers’ claims and explanations, which multiple interviewees felt was quite common in the AI. On emotions, however, AI students lose out (Ratings=3/3/4/5/5). We should note here that at least 2 interviewees preferred emotionless students as they felt that the lack of student emotions would make them better teachers. The student confusions were seen as very realistic (Ratings=1/1/1/2/2), though 2 interviewees noted that on some rare occasions, the dialog would sometimes show inconsistencies with the original confusion. All 3 of these findings are consistent with that of
31
. Teacher frustration ratings (1/2/3/4/5) were all over the board, with interviewees being split on whether or not frustration makes them perform better.

Diagram understanding is the primary innovation of our work, and it is important that simulated students demonstrate understanding of teachers’ visual cues.
While the ratings (1/3/3/4/4) say that the AI students matched real students, the discussions in the interviews seemed to lean very slightly towards real students. The vastly different ways diagrams were used by the different interviewees meant that the human reference kept changing.
The interviewees were split on whether or not giving the students the ability to highlight is important, mostly based on whether or not their way of teaching allows for students to interact with the diagram. While this is a limitation we did not address, the interviews show that one-way diagram interaction does happen in the real world.

Finally, the interviewees believed that they only slightly changed their teaching strategy (Ratings=1/2/2/2/3) from their interactions with real students. The main driver behind this seemed to be the uniqueness of the setting, as even in one-to-one tutoring scenarios, solution errors are interspersed with concept revision, and correctly solved questions. The worst rating of 3 came from the interviewee who claimed to use questions only as a diagnostic tool to identify concept gaps, and then addressing those gaps instead of getting the student to fix their solution. As we stated earlier, the entire setup of GeoDial is in service of extracting realistic tutoring strategies from teachers, making this the most important factor behind the fidelity of GeoDial. While it is not perfect, we believe that a mean rating of 2 (Slight change in strategy) shows that we were quite successful in our goal.

Appendix J
Translating Text Annotations into Diagram Highlights

Our proposed GeoDial dataset supports multiple modeling paradigms for building multimodal tutoring systems. The most direct approach is to train a unified model that both understands the diagram and generates a new image containing the highlight. However, this type of full-image generation is typically slow, computationally expensive, and prone to visual hallucinations
[
1
,
28
,
50
]
.
These issues make full image generation unsuitable for real-time tutoring or lightweight deployment.
Thus, as we mentioned in
Section

3.3
, we opt to visual highlights to textual highlight annotations, which we use all across
Section

5
. However, in order to deploy in the real world, we would have to recover the visual highlights from generated textual highlights.
In this section, we introduce an automatic drawing pipeline that achieves this.

J.1
Vertex and Label Detection

Vertex detection identifies the pixel location of every geometric point referenced in a highlight instruction. The system first parses the diagram using GeoSolver
[
42
]
, which extracts structural point candidates from intersections and curve endpoints across all sub-diagram regions.
We then render a temporary version of the diagram that overlays each detected point with a unique numeric marker (see example in
Figure

6
). This is fed to Gemini, which identifies the correspondence between each printed letter in the original diagram and the numeric overlay, establishing a mapping from letter labels to precise pixel coordinates.

For vertices that cannot be resolved through the matching procedure, DeepSeek-OCR
[
53
]
is applied to directly detect printed letter labels on the diagram and provide their bounding box coordinates. DeepSeek-OCR is also used to handle textual references that are not tied to vertices, e.g., side lengths, variable names, etc., which are often marked in the “Labels” field mentioned above.

J.2
Highlights Drawing

Once we have the coordinates and bounding boxes, we can use them to highlight the figure. The renderer supports segment traces for lines, ray-based angle markings, circle-constrained arcs, and circular emphasis for textual labels, as shown in
Section

C.2
.
A set of geometric adjustment procedures manages visual crowding: collinear lines are shifted to avoid overlap, and angles sharing a vertex are displaced along bisectors. These steps produce clear, interpretable highlights, ensuring that the rendered annotations remain legible even under complex instructions.

J.3
Evaluation

We evaluated the automatic drawing pipeline through a human evaluation on 100 randomly sampled utterances from the GeoDial dataset. Two researchers independently reviewed the rendered diagrams and then discussed any disagreements to reach a consensus. Each sample was scored on a three-level scale: a score of 1.0 was assigned when all rendered highlights aligned with the instruction; 0.5 was assigned when the instruction contained multiple required highlights, and only a subset was rendered correctly; and 0.0 was assigned when none of the required highlights were rendered correctly. Under this evaluation protocol, the pipeline achieved an accuracy of 95%, demonstrating that symbolic rendering effectively translates textual highlight descriptions into precise diagram annotations.

Appendix M
Case Study

To better understand how supervised fine-tuning changes model behavior, two researchers manually inspected 100 randomly sampled examples from the test set and compared the next-teacher utterances generated by the original and fine-tuned models. We focused on this field because it directly reflects the model’s ability to generate pedagogically appropriate tutoring responses. The analysis revealed a consistent behavioral shift: the fine-tuned model was less likely to produce generic geometry explanations and more likely to generate teacher-like tutoring moves that were aligned with the dialog context.

From explanation-heavy responses to pedagogical scaffolding.

A prominent pattern is that the original model often produces long, explanation-heavy responses. Even when the gold teacher utterance only provides brief feedback, asks the student to continue reasoning, or closes the conversation, the original model tends to restate the relevant geometry rule or explicitly reveal the answer. While these responses are often informative, they can be pedagogically misaligned because they reduce opportunities for student reasoning. In contrast, the fine-tuned model more often produces concise scaffolding moves that better match the local tutoring context, such as acknowledging a correct step, gently correcting an incorrect one, or asking the student to inspect a specific part of the diagram.

Improved dialog-flow management.

Fine-tuning also improves the model’s ability to manage the flow of the tutoring interaction. The original model frequently treats different tutoring stages in a similar way, continuing to explain the geometry regardless of whether the target teacher move is an introduction, a probing question, a hint, or a farewell. For example, in several closing turns, the original model continues to justify the final answer with additional geometric reasoning, whereas the fine-tuned model produces teacher-like closing utterances that praise the student’s work and invite future questions. Similarly, for introductory turns, the fine-tuned model is more likely to ask the student to explain their approach before giving targeted guidance, while the original model often jumps directly to a specific mathematical assumption or error. This suggests that fine-tuning helps the model learn not only what mathematical content to discuss, but also when and how to deliver it within a tutoring dialog.

Stronger visual grounding.

We further observe that the fine-tuned model shows stronger visual grounding. Its utterances more frequently refer to highlighted or visually salient parts of the diagram, using phrases such as “the angle marked in green,” “the highlighted part,” or “the figure.” This behavior is important in our setting because effective geometry tutoring often depends on directing the student’s attention to specific diagram components. By contrast, the original model more often discusses geometry concepts abstractly, without explicitly connecting its feedback or question to the visual evidence in the diagram. This difference suggests that fine-tuning encourages the model to imitate visually grounded teacher behavior, rather than only producing generic geometry explanations.

Remaining limitations.

The case study also reveals several remaining limitations. The fine-tuned model sometimes becomes formulaic, repeatedly using common teacher-like phrases such as “That’s right,” “Not exactly,” or “Can you walk me through…”. In addition, while its responses are often better aligned with the dialog context, they can occasionally be less conceptually specific than those of the original model. For instance, the fine-tuned model may ask the student to compare two highlighted regions, while the original model more explicitly names the relevant theorem. This suggests that fine-tuning improves conversational and pedagogical alignment, but future work is needed to preserve conceptual specificity and reduce template-like generation.
