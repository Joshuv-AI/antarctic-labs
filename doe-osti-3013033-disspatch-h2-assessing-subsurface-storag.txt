EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This project evaluates the technical feasibility of long-term underground hydrogen storage in
the Anadarko Basin, integrating subsurface science, reservoir engineering, and system-level
analysis to build a cohesive understanding of how hydrogen may be stored and recovered in
porous media. Although salt caverns currently dominate commercial-scale hydrogen storage,
their limited geographic availability necessitates alternative options that can support a more
resilient energy system. Depleted oil and gas reservoirs offer a widely distributed and potentially
high-capacity solution, but their suitability depends on formation-specific geologic,
petrophysical, microbial, and operational factors. This study provides the first coordinated
assessment of these factors within the Simpson Group of Oklahoma.
The technical approach combined literature review, core selection, laboratory characterization,
microbial studies, and multiphase flow experiments with basin-wide geological modeling,
reservoir simulation, technoeconomic analysis, and market assessment. Laboratory results
showed that representative Simpson Group rocks exhibit stable porosity, permeability, and
mineral structure under hydrogen exposure, with gas flow behavior that is consistent with
predictable injection and withdrawal performance. Microbial testing demonstrated that
hydrogen biotransformation is strongly pressure-dependent and minimal at reservoir-relevant
depths, reducing concerns about biological losses in high-pressure environments.
Geological and reservoir models constructed from well logs, core data, and regional mapping
indicate that the Simpson Group provides sufficient reservoir quality and containment for cyclic
hydrogen storage. Simulation studies highlight the importance of pressure management,
reservoir heterogeneity, and cushion gas selection in determining recovery efficiency, while
confirming that technically viable storage performance is achievable under realistic operating
conditions. The technoeconomic and market assessments further illustrate how underground
hydrogen storage can provide system value by absorbing surplus renewable energy, supporting
steady hydrogen demand, and enhancing long-duration balancing across integrated energy
networks.
Taken together, the findings establish a strong technical basis for advancing underground
hydrogen storage in the Anadarko Basin from early-stage concept toward field-scale
demonstration, while also identifying the key uncertainties—such as site-specific heterogeneity,
long-term operational behavior, and evolving market conditions—that warrant continued
investigation. This integrated understanding forms a practical foundation for next steps in
maturing porous-media hydrogen storage as a viable component of clean energy systems.

INTRODUCTION
GTI Energy assembled a multidisciplinary team to assess hydrogen (H2) storage potential for the
region above the Anadarko Basin. The initiative, referred to as DISSPATCH H2 – an acronym for
“The Developing and Investigating Subsurface Storage Potential and Technical Challenges for
Hydrogen” – sought to advance key principles regarding the large-scale, long-term subsurface
storage of hydrogen within depleted oil and gas reservoirs and to evaluate the effects of this gas
on formation fluids and reservoir rocks.

Background
Currently, large-scale geologic storage of H₂ is primarily conducted in salt caverns; however,
these formations are rare across most regions of North America. In contrast, depleted oil and
gas reservoirs are widely distributed and offer significantly larger storage volumes than salt
caverns. However, they present greater risks of H₂ contamination and loss due to residual
hydrocarbons and associated complexities that require further assessment. Given prolific
depleted oil and gas reservoirs in Oklahoma, the Anadarko Basin was selected as the focus area
for this project.

Project objectives
The primary objective of this project was to evaluate the feasibility of long-term bulk H₂ storage
in the Anadarko Basin. This was to be achieved through comprehensive data collection of
reservoir rock core samples, reservoir fluids, regional geology, and natural gas market
information. These data would then inform an integrated modeling of geology, reservoir
performance, and supply-demand dynamics. In addition, the project would conduct a risk
assessment with mitigation and monitoring strategies, field implementation planning (covering
H₂ sourcing and transport), analysis of contractual and regulatory requirements, and technical
and economic feasibility evaluations.
The specific objectives were:
• Characterize H2 movement and resource loss in a representative reservoir and seal rocks in
 the presence of remnant oil and brine and assess the impact of repeated cycles on gas
 transport.
• Assess operational risks including the risk of resource loss and storage underperformance
 due to biogeochemical processes or repeated storage-delivery cycles.
• Assess the infrastructure needs to enable long-term bulk supply for a broad H2 market,
 including infrastructure to produce, transport, and store H2 at scale.
• Assess the technical feasibility for new commercial-scale underground storage sites for
 safely injecting, storing, and withdrawing H2 from porous media in the region overlying the
 Anadarko Basin using technoeconomic analyses, market assessments, and supply chain
 forecasting analyses.

• Offer recommendations that could be applied to a follow-on project focused on field-scale
 testing and demonstration, including risk mitigation strategies.

METHODS

Technical approach
This section outlines the integrated technical framework used to evaluate the feasibility of
underground hydrogen storage in the Anadarko Basin. It brings together multiple scientific
disciplines including geology, petrophysics, microbiology, reservoir engineering, and system
modeling to build a cohesive understanding of how hydrogen behaves in subsurface
environments. The approach begins with a targeted literature review to establish the state of
knowledge and identify promising formations, followed by a structured process for selecting
and characterizing core rock samples representative of storage conditions. Laboratory studies
examine rock properties, multiphase fluid flow, and microbial interactions, providing
foundational insights into storage performance and potential risks.
These experimental findings are paired with basin-wide data integration and detailed geological
modeling to assess spatial variability, storage capacity, and containment. Reservoir simulations
then test operational scenarios, exploring how hydrogen could be injected, stored, and
withdrawn under realistic field conditions. Finally, technoeconomic and market assessments link
the physical science to system-level deployment considerations. Together, these components
form a comprehensive methodology designed to reduce uncertainty and guide future field-scale
demonstration efforts.

Literature review
A literature review was conducted to better understand the current knowledge and research
prospects in the area of underground hydrogen storage (UHS). While there are insights to be
gained from existing natural gas underground storage and the carbon capture and storage
market, there are unique challenges specific to hydrogen that require further investigation. The
review looked at distinct types of UHS: salt caverns, depleted oil and gas reservoirs, and saline
aquifers. Different challenges of UHS were discussed, such as water upconing, structural stability,
leakage risks, and geomechanics. The effect of different variables on the economics of the
process was also reviewed. Key variables included the choice of cushion gas, size of storage, and
number of cycles through a UHS facility. For details, see submitted DOE deliverable “DISSPATCH
H2 Literature Review_04_30_2024.pdf."
This study also undertook a comprehensive literature review to identify geological formations
potentially suitable for underground hydrogen storage (UHS) within the Anadarko Basin in
Oklahoma. It assessed geological and engineering factors influencing the feasibility and
performance of UHS systems, including site selection, storage capacity, containment caprocks,
and withdrawal efficiency. Figure 1 shows the stratigraphic column studied for this purpose.
Additional geologic backgrounds and depositional histories can be found in Appendix A.

Selection of core rock samples
The Oklahoma Geological Survey (OGS) selected, described, and provided core samples from its
repository at the Oklahoma Petroleum Information Center (OPIC) in Norman, Oklahoma, which
contains nearly 5,000 cores, including well logs, photographs, and measurements for many wells
(Figure 2). To identify relevant samples, cores were first sorted by geologic age using a

customized stratigraphic column database that standardized formation names and ages (Figure
1). Key literature sources informed this process (25-26, 28, 44, 46-48).

Figure 2: Map of core available at OPIC in the Anadarko Basin

Geological considerations
Identifying underground rock formations suitable for hydrogen storage involved several steps.
First, cores were pre-screened for the following criteria: 1) geographic location in proximity to
existing infrastructure, 2) location within known depleted hydrocarbon fields to leverage existing
data, 3) proximity to known faults, 4) preview of well log data and core photos (if available) to
detect the presence of reservoir and seal intervals, and 5) preview of archived core plug porosity
and permeability data (where available). Following selection, three sets of cores were examined
(Figure 3).
Next, cores underwent visual lithologic description. Sedimentary facies were classified based on
grain size, fossil content, and relative abundance of siliciclastic to carbonate material. Siliciclastic
grain size was approximated using the Wentworth classification scale (67), and carbonate was
classified by the Dunham classification scheme (32). Facies associations grouped similar facies
within a depositional framework.
Where possible, plugs were taken from cores to undergo any porosity and permeability
measurements that had not already been documented. These were completed at PNNL and

 Page 8

OGS in coordination with the University of Oklahoma (OU) Mewbourne School of Petroleum and
Geological Engineering (MPGE). Taken together, core descriptions combined with well logs and
updated porosity and permeability measurements helped to refine site selection for geological
reservoir modeling.
Finally, caprock – or seal formations – which help keep hydrogen contained underground, were
also evaluated. Because direct samples from the main seal rocks were limited, published data
were used to estimate their properties.

Characterization of core rock samples
The study selected core samples representative of the potential hydrogen storage zones in the
basin. The three teams chose the Simpson Group formation for in-depth analysis. Core samples
from the Red Fork formation were initially considered but excluded due to poor rock quality and
high variability.
PNNL characterized the selected rocks using the following laboratory techniques:

 Page 9

 • Brine vacuum saturation (VS) – for filling rock pores with brine under a vacuum and
 measuring porosity
 • X-ray computed tomography (X-CT) and Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) – for measuring
 surface area and pore structure
 • X-ray diffraction (XRD) – for determining mineral composition
 • Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) –
 for analyzing grain surface morphology and chemical elements
These methods established rock porosity (amount of empty space), permeability (ability to
transmit fluids), pore size, chemical make-up, and other baseline properties.
The study then examined how hydrogen and methane gases, in the presence of brine, moved
through these rocks. "Core-flooding" experiments were performed, where gases and liquids
were pushed through the rock samples under controlled conditions. Gas chromatography (GC),
which measures and identifies gases, monitored how the gases traveled and changed in
concentration. By analyzing pressure differences during these tests, they estimated capillary
pressure—a factor influencing how easily hydrogen could be injected and recovered from the
rock in the presence of multiphase fluids. Together with measurements of how easily each phase
of fluid moved (relative permeability), these results provided insights into hydrogen storage and
retrieval.
To understand how repeated hydrogen injection and withdrawal might alter rock properties,
researchers measured porosity and permeability at various stages of the experiments. This
helped assess whether exposure to hydrogen and brine changed the rocks’ ability to store gas
over time.

Microbial processes involving the biotransformation of hydrogen were studied in samples
collected from candidate underground storage locations in Oklahoma. Bench-scale experiments
used bioreactors (high-pressure vessels) and serum bottles (ambient pressure) to simulate
reservoir conditions and examine microbial activity, specifically the influence of pressure on
hydrogen transformation.
Reservoir fluid samples from multiple Anadarko Basin sources were mixed with synthetic brine
and amended with sulfate to monitor hydrogen sulfide production. Core samples from different
basin locations were incorporated as substrates for biofilm formation and to assess their
suitability as a microbial carbon source. Selection of fluid samples for each experiment was
based on initial bacterial counts determined by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)
analysis; Banks and Tippens samples were used in serum bottles, while Banks and Simpson
samples were incubated in high-pressure vessels.
After incubation, both gaseous and liquid phases were analyzed, focusing on microbial
community composition and changes in methane, and change in hydrogen concentrations.

Data integration and mapping
The study integrated diverse scientific and community datasets to evaluate UHS potential in
Oklahoma. The OGS data library, containing thousands of well core samples, well logs, and
production reports, was used, along with publicly available sources, such as the US Census and
environmental justice indicators. Regional mapping incorporated geological, infrastructure, and
subsurface storage data to guide site selection for UHS feasibility. Well header information and
well logs were compiled and digitized to build comprehensive geological models, with
formation tops interpreted regionally for key stratigraphic units. This integrated, geographic
information systems (GIS)-based approach identified priority areas for future hydrogen storage
(Oklahoma Geological Survey).

The regional feasibility of underground hydrogen storage was assessed using an integrated,
multi-step workflow. This approach combined core data, well logs, and geological modeling to
estimate essential reservoir properties. Core measurements and literature sources were used to
calibrate well log interpretations, enabling accurate determination of porosity, permeability,
lithology, and water saturation. These calibrated properties were incorporated into a three-
dimensional geological model, which defined formation boundaries and stratigraphic zones, and
was populated using geostatistical methods. The resulting model provided a gridded
representation of key reservoir characteristics, guiding site selection and simulation scenarios for
hydrogen storage.
Site selection for UHS considered criteria such as adequate storage capacity, injectivity,
containment security, and operational viability. The Blanchard Northwest Field was chosen due
to its depleted status, historical hydrocarbon production, and favorable geological attributes—
namely, sufficient porosity and permeability, robust caprock, and minimal faulting. Historical
production data from this field established a conservative baseline for hydrogen storage
capacity, supporting its candidacy for simulation. Figure 4 depicts the spatial extent of the
DISSPATCH H2 geologic model.
Simulation studies focused on evaluating seasonal hydrogen injection and withdrawal cycles,
aligning with wind energy production and regional energy demand. Using advanced reservoir
simulation software, the team modeled hydrogen behavior in both averaged and heterogeneous
geological scenarios, incorporating realistic subsurface conditions such as pressure,
temperature, and multiphase flow. The simulations tested operational parameters, storage
capacity, injectivity, and deliverability, while assessing the impacts of cushion gas types and
withdrawal rates. Simplified assumptions were applied for early-stage sensitivity analyses, such
as isothermal reservoir conditions and uniform initial water saturation.
Overall, this technical approach provided the theoretical framework for assessing the feasibility
and performance of UHS in the Anadarko Basin, integrating geological characterization, site

Estimating storage capacity
For simulation purposes, a subset of the regional geological model covering Township 8N 5W,
which includes the Blanchard Northwest Field, was selected due to its production history from
the Simpson Group and the availability of non-commingled well data (Figure 6). The chosen
hydrogen injection site is centrally located within the field, surrounded by multiple oil and gas
wells, ensuring strong operational and geologic relevance. Analysis of historical production data
from nine wells—seven primarily gas producers and two with mixed oil and gas—revealed that
most are now depleted, indicating significant available pore space for potential H2 storage.

 Page 14

Cumulative records show the field has produced approximately 113,055 barrels (bbl) of oil and
825,730 bbl of oil equivalent of gas, totaling around 938,785 bbl. This equates to a minimum
storage potential of 5.3 billion cubic feet (Bcf), based on the conservative estimate that the pore
space previously occupied by hydrocarbons can be re-pressurized and reused for hydrogen
storage, with well-level data further supporting the site's viability for underground hydrogen
storage.

Figure 6: AOR for numerical modeling and simulation

Scenario: H₂ storage source and cycle definition
In 2023, 41% of electricity in Oklahoma was generated from wind, presenting significant
potential for hydrogen production through wind-powered electrolysis. Multiple wind farms in
the study area could support this process. To match seasonal energy dynamics, the project team
established a hydrogen storage cycle with storage during high-wind months (March–August)
and withdrawal during peak energy demand in winter (December–February), as determined by
local natural gas usage trends (U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), 2025).

Simulation Design
The objective of multiple simulations was to evaluate the feasibility of seasonal H2 storage in the
Simpson Group reservoir using operational conditions that accurately reflect the subsurface
geological characteristics of the study area. Specifically, the model aimed to evaluate whether
the reservoir (at a pressure gradient of ~ 0.375 psi/ft and a temperature of ~170 ºF) could
accommodate the injection of 1.6 billion ft³of H2 over a 6-month production period, at a rate of

 Page 15

8.7 million ft³/day. Another goal was to assess the deliverability of the stored H2 during a 3-
month withdrawal phase at 17 million ft³/day, matching peak winter demand, without requiring
cushion gas support. If the cushion gas were needed, the cushion gas type that would deliver
the highest H2 deliverability had to be identified.
Additional simulation goals included evaluating the impact of drawdown on bottom-hole
pressure (BHP) – with a minimum BHP constraint of 3,441 and 3,500 psi – and determining
whether this operation would result in significant water production from the Simpson Group
reservoir. These case studies would establish the technical basis for subsurface H2 storage
performance under cyclic injection and production conditions using two types of subsurface
static models: an averaged model based on average porosity and permeability cores that are
about 5 miles to the east of the core, and a geologic (heterogeneous) model in which porosity
and permeability are distributed statistically using SGS as described in the geological modeling
section.
Nine simulation cases were developed to evaluate hydrogen injection, storage, and withdrawal
under varying operational conditions (with and without cushion gases) and schedule (Table 2).

Numerical simulation and input data
A numerical simulation approach was used to assess hydrogen injection and withdrawal in a
depleted gas reservoir, leveraging the CMG 2024.20 suite for model construction, compositional
fluid modeling, and reservoir simulation. The workflow incorporated porosity and permeability
data from core samples and well logs, with both averaged and heterogeneous models
representing reservoir properties. Rock compressibility for each geological unit was estimated
using the Newman (1973) correlation for consolidated sandstones, which relates compressibility
to porosity:

A uniform porosity value was assumed in the absence of detailed measurements, resulting in an
average compressibility of 19 × 10−6 psi−1, a widely accepted practice for consolidated
sandstones in preliminary simulations.
Relative permeability curves defined multiphase flow behavior during injection and withdrawal,
supporting scenario-based analysis. Pressure and temperature gradients were initialized using
data from Van de Kamp (2019), with an average gradient of 0.375 psi/ft (about 4,000 psi and
170°F). The simulation assumed isothermal reservoir conditions, a no-flow boundary, and
uniform initial water saturation (100%), reflecting typical depleted reservoir characteristics such
as low pressure and aquifer-like behavior (Muhammed et al., 2023; Sadkhan & Al-Mudhafar,
2024). Geomechanical and microbial effects were excluded to streamline early-stage sensitivity
analysis.
This technical approach established a robust framework for evaluating underground hydrogen
storage feasibility and deliverability in the Anadarko Basin, integrating realistic subsurface
properties, operational constraints, and established correlations for preliminary assessment.

This section describes the methodology and calculation approach used for technoeconomic
analysis (TEA) of underground hydrogen storage (UHS) in DISSPATCH H2. Figure 9 shows the
scope of the TEA; production of H2 is not included in the scope.

Figure 9: Scope for technoeconomic assessment

The calculations are performed on a Microsoft Excel-based calculator. The calculator takes user
inputs like the amount of hydrogen to be stored, injection/withdrawal frequency, and choice of
cushion gas, and reports the estimated capital cost required and the levelized cost of
underground hydrogen storage. The inputs used, the calculations, and the TEA model outputs
are detailed below.
Inputs
• Incoming hydrogen conditions – Pressure of H2 gas
 The inlet pressure is used to calculate the compression power needed to compress
 hydrogen to the reservoir storage pressure.
• Amount of hydrogen to be stored
 The amount of hydrogen to be stored is an important input. If curtailed or low-cost
 power is available, it may be converted to hydrogen via electrolysis and stored. Typical
 energy needed for hydrogen production through electrolysis is 55 kWh/kg.1 Otherwise, if
 hydrogen is available from other sources, such as low-carbon hydrogen from steam
 methane reforming (SMR), it can be directly used for storage.
• Frequency of injections and withdrawal

 Page 21

 The frequency of injections and withdrawals is an important input. The number of cycles
 per year is an input in the TEA model that determines how often hydrogen needs to be
 injected and withdrawn, directly affecting revenue potential, system efficiency,
 infrastructure requirements, and alignment with energy system needs. The default
 frequency in the model is set to once per year – 6 months of injection, followed by 3
 months of shut-in and then 3 months of withdrawal.
• Bulk reservoir volume, Mscf
 Bulk reservoir volume refers to the total physical volume of the underground formation
 (e.g., salt cavern, depleted gas field, or aquifer) available for storing hydrogen, including
 both pore space and rock matrix (in porous media) or void space (in caverns).
• Depth of reservoir, ft
 The depth of the reservoir is important to determine the pressure and temperature
 conditions at the UHS conditions. Typical pressure gradient2 (psi/ft) and temperature
 gradient3 (°F/1000 ft) are used from the literature to find P and T at the storage
 conditions. These are essential since they affect the hydrogen gas properties, such as
 density.
• Cushion gas, hydrogen/natural gas/nitrogen
 The user can select to use hydrogen gas itself as a cushion gas. Other options available
 in the model are natural gas and nitrogen. When a gas other than hydrogen is selected,
 the model adds the cost of a pressure swing adsorption (PSA) unit to purify the
 hydrogen gas before it is sent to the end-user. The costs considered for the different
 gases are : Nitrogen: $0.19/kg, Natural Gas: $3/MMBTU, and Hydrogen: $3.92/kg.
• Cushion gas fraction, %
 The fraction of storage volume occupied by the cushion gas refers to the cushion gas
 fraction. For depleted gas fields, the number is usually set to 50%.
• Electricity cost, $/kWh
 The cost of electricity is used to estimate the operating expenses of operating the
 compressor, which is the main source of energy usage in the UHS system.
• Hydrogen loss through leakage or biological activity
 Hydrogen stored underground may be lost to leakage or to biological bacteria that
 convert H2 to other gases. It is important to account for this loss as it affects the overall
 process economics. Current experimental results indicate that hydrogen losses due to
 microbial activity are only an issue at low pressures. At the pressures considered here, at

 Page 22

 ~8000 ft, the pressure is closer to 3500 psi, and hence biological activity is not a concern.
 The losses are less than 1% and are included in the model.
• Financial variables
 The financial variables that affect the discounted cash flow rate of return analysis
 (DCFROR) are also inputs. A 40-year project life is assumed, and a 10% discount rate is
 used.
• Labor cost
 The annual salary of operating staff is an input. The default assumption in the model is
 set to 2 DCS (Distributed Control System) operators and 2 field operators. Since the
 underground storage facilities must remain operational 24/7, 3 sets of operators are
 needed to work in shifts.

In evaluating deployment of UHS within integrated energy systems, a linear optimization model
was used. It was structured to minimize system costs, incorporating technical and economic
constraints, with hourly resolution over a full year and the assumption of perfect foresight. This

 Page 25

approach enabled simultaneous optimization of assets for energy generation, storage, and end-
use, capturing interactions across power and gas systems and performance scenarios under
varying policy, demand, and price conditions.
The design of the model included the following attributes:
• Yearlong hourly resolution: the model uses hourly data throughout the year, capturing
 both short-term operations and long-term seasonal trends. This detail is crucial for
 evaluating UHS, which addresses multi-week or interseasonal supply-demand imbalances
 rather than hourly shifts. Hourly steps also help track curtailment, generator cycling, and
 load-following in the power sector.
• Perfect foresight: the model assumes complete knowledge of future demand, renewable
 output, and system conditions for the year. This idealized approach removes forecast error
 and reveals cost-optimal energy and infrastructure allocation. It also allows consistent
 evaluation of the impact of storage on long-term system performance over short-term
 operations.
• Energy sector integration: the model integrates power, hydrogen, and natural gas sectors
 to represent energy production, conversion, and consumption. It includes electricity from
 dispatchable units and renewables and hydrogen via electrolysis or gas reforming with uses
 for heat or power. By unifying these sectors, the system can dynamically allocate energy and
 assess tradeoffs between direct electricity use and converting it to hydrogen, as well as
 analyzing methods of hydrogen production. This approach highlights growing
 interdependence in energy systems and supports analysis of cross-sector flexibility.
• Storage dispatch with seasonal limits: the model treats UHS as a dispatchable asset with
 variables for injection, withdrawal, and storage volume, enabling simulation of storage use to
 balance production and demand. Seasonal rules limit injection and withdrawal to specific
 windows, reflecting operational constraints like permits and reservoir management. These
 temporal limits emphasize long-duration storage and ensure realistic deployment strategies.
• Infrastructure capacity: the model treats investment in technologies like electrolyzers,
 hydrogen storage, and fuel cells as decision variables, optimizing both system design and
 hourly operation. This allows it to determine not only when storage is needed, but how
 much and in what form. Including levelized capital costs ensures economically sound
 infrastructure choices.
• Adaptive distribution of electricity: the model tracks renewable curtailment and allocates
 renewable electricity between grid supply and hydrogen production. This helps quantify how
 hydrogen infrastructure uses excess clean power, reduces waste, and supports grid stability.
 By costing curtailment and comparing it to the marginal value of hydrogen, the model
 highlights tradeoffs that simpler tools may miss.
• Hydrogen end-use options: in the model, hydrogen serves competing uses such as power
 generation and industrial fuel. The framework evaluates how hydrogen is distributed across

 Page 26

 sectors based on system conditions and storage, while also showing how technology limits
 or policy choices affect the role of hydrogen in the energy system.
• Daily hydrogen demand: unlike electricity, hydrogen demand is modeled daily to reflect
 unique sector consumption patterns and infrastructure. Industrial hydrogen typically uses
 pipelines equipped with line pack which provides short-term storage by maintaining a
 volume of stored gas and smoothing intra-day fluctuations. By simplifying the model
 through daily demand aggregation, usage changes are adequately captured, aligning with
 standard industrial gas sector practices where intra-day supply variations have minimal
 downstream impact compared to electricity.
• Costs: the model addresses main costs: levelized power source costs, technology capital
 expenditures, storage operations, curtailment, and hydrogen via steam methane reforming.
 While it omits transmission and some maintenance, it covers essential categories to compare
 technologies, strategies, and storage options.
• Year-end storage balance: the model starts the UHS asset with a specified state of charge,
 reflecting typical operations where storage is not empty at year start. It enforces that end-of-
 year storage matches this initial value, preventing artificial changes in hydrogen levels that
 could skew sizing or dispatch decisions. These features ensure system performance is
 modeled under repeatable, steady-state conditions.

Structure of model
The research uses a simplified linear model where decision variables interact with costs, fees, or
prices to yield component values that are summed for the total system cost. The model chooses
variables to minimize total cost while meeting predefined constraints.
Key components that govern the system are summarized, with further details in Appendix I.
• Objective function: the objective of the model is to minimize the total system cost across
 the year. It balances infrastructure investment, fuel use, curtailment penalties, and
 operational costs in order to identify the most efficient configuration and dispatch of power
 generation, hydrogen production, storage, and end-use pathways.
• Parameters: fixed inputs like time-series data (hourly electricity demand, renewable
 generation) and static values (generator cost, capacity) define system conditions. These
 guide decisions remain constant during optimization and are usually based on empirical
 data or literature.
• Scalars: single-value constants configure model-wide settings, influencing conversion
 efficiency, costs, and initial conditions. Examples include round-trip storage efficiency and
 electrolyzer capital cost, which affect technology interactions over time. Scalars enhance
 parameters by adding detail without needing time-based changes. Industry averages were
 used for all exogenously defined scalars.
• Decision variables: controllable choices set by the optimization solver to minimize system
 costs. They cover hourly operations like power generation and hydrogen production, and

 Page 27

 infrastructure investments such as sizing electroliers or storage. The model adjusts these
 within constraints to find the lowest-cost solution that meets energy needs.
• Constraints: each constraint ensures the model stays realistic, practical, and economical.
 Together, they help simulate energy production, storage, and use across sectors and
 seasons, clarifying the optimal role of UHS in a decarbonized energy system.
Details of approach are further expanded in Appendix I.
In this model, the notations (g) and (t) indicate that a variable or parameter is indexed over a
particular set, providing structure and granularity to how values are defined and used. The (g)
index refers to the set of dispatchable generators, such as natural gas, coal, imports, or
hydropower. When a variable or parameter includes (g), it means that the model tracks a
separate value for each type of generator, allowing for generator-specific cost, capacity, and
operational decisions.
Similarly, the (t) index refers to the set of hourly time steps across the modeled year. A variable
or parameter indexed by (t) varies over time, capturing the dynamic nature of energy
production, demand, and storage behavior. When both (g) and (t) appear, such as in gen(g,t),
the model is assigning a unique generation value to each generator for each hour of the year,
enabling detailed temporal and technological resolution in system optimization. This structure is
essential to accurately simulate how an integrated energy system operates under different
constraints and conditions.

Pore structure and content
XRD was performed on core chips to characterize the mineral content of the samples. The chips
were crushed and finely powdered using an agate mortar and pestle. Powder XRD scans were
collected using a Bruker D8 Discover TXS-HE A25. Powdered samples were mounted on a low-
background silicon holder and precisely positioned using a laser-video alignment system. The
scans were collected with the instrument in Bragg-Brentano geometry and the detector was
placed in 1D mode with an active area of 65 x 20 mm. Scans were collected from 10-80 °2θ in
coupled 2θ/θ mode with 3501 steps at an increment of 0.02 and continuous stage rotation at
360 °/min. Scans were then imported into Bruker EVA6.0 software for phase identification using
the International Center for Diffraction Database (ICDD) powder diffraction file (PDF) 5+ 2024
database.
BET analyzed surface area and pore structure through N2 physisorption at 77 K using a
Quadrasorb EVO/SI gas sorption system (Quantachrome Instruments, Anton Paar). Prior to
analysis, samples (1 g. rock fragments) were degassed under vacuum (0.4 Torr) at 150 ˚C for 12
hours. The specific surface area was determined using 5 points BET method (Brunaer, Emmett
and Teller 1938), with adsorption data in the relative pressure range of 0.05−0.3. Total pore
volume and pore size distribution were derived from the desorption isotherm using Barrett-
Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method (Barret, Joyner and Halenda 1951), with results presented as
∆V/∆D to quantify pore volume distribution across pore diameters (D).
SEM imaging was performed on core chips to investigate rock surface morphology and mineral
content. Analyses were conducted using a FEI Helios NanoLab 600i field emission electron
microscope. Prior to analysis, a section of the rock sediments was coated with a 20 nm carbon
layer on an aluminum stub by thermal evaporation using a 108 ºC Auto Carbon Coater (Ted
Pella, Inc.). Particle morphology was examined using a secondary electron Everhart-Thornley
detector (ETD) in a field free mode. Higher magnification images were collected in immersion
mode with through-lens-detector (TLD). Energy dispersive x-ray (EDX) analysis was collected
with an X-Max 80mm2 Silicon Drift Detector (SDD) from Oxford Instruments. The EDX analysis
was performed at 20kV voltage and 2.3 nA. Oxford AZtec software was used to collect and
process elemental maps.

sample was placed in a plastic tube on a low-density post and scanned with an 80kVp unfiltered
microfocus W X-ray source. Projections were acquired in a double-helical trajectory with a
detector shift to minimize pixel-specific artifacts. Reconstruction was performed on a GPU
cluster using a filtered back projection algorithm, yielding a digital 3D volume with isotropic 10.4
µm voxel resolution. Post-processing and segmentation were performed in the Avizo software

package. A simple threshold binarization of the rock volume into solid and pore components

Relative permeability
Relative permeability was determined using the unsteady-state method developed by Johnson
et al. (E.F. Johnson 1959) as summarized in Appendix A. This industry-standard approach
calculates gas/water relative permeabilities from displacement experiments in linear porous
media, based on fractional flow theory (Buckley S.E 1942) (Welge 1952). Compared to steady-
state methods, it provides rapid results for standard core plugs but requires strict theoretical
assumptions 1: immiscible and incompressible piston-like displacement, uniform saturation
profiles, and negligible capillary forces (achieved through high injection rates).

The procedure for measurements is as follows:
 a. Core Preparation:
 - Dry cores were weighed, vacuum-saturated (720–746 Torr) with 5000 ppm brine,

RESULTS
This section presents integrated findings from laboratory experiments, geological
characterization, reservoir simulations, and system-level analyses that define the feasibility of
underground hydrogen storage in the Anadarko Basin. The results show how early-stage
concepts progressed into validated subsurface storage. Core analyses established the Simpson
Group as a more promising storage target than initially sampled formations, with petrophysical
properties and rock composition that enable predictable fluid flow and stable behavior under
cyclic hydrogen exposure. Microbial studies revealed that hydrogen biotransformation is
strongly pressure-dependent, highlighting the importance of reservoir conditions in minimizing
biological loss pathways.
Geological modeling and data integration resulted in an understanding of storage capacity,
injectivity, and containment, enabling realistic simulation of seasonal hydrogen cycling. These
reservoir simulations clarified the operational parameters most influential to recovery efficiency,
including bottomhole pressure limits and cushion gas selection. Technoeconomic and market
assessments demonstrated how underground storage performs within broader energy systems
and identified demand conditions under which UHS provides meaningful system value.
Together, these findings establish a technically grounded basis for future field-scale
demonstration.

Literature review
The literature search focused on two key stratigraphic units within the Anadarko Basin—namely,
the Simpson Group and the Red Fork Sandstone Formation—as candidates for underground
hydrogen storage (UHS). These units were selected for further evaluation based on geological
and engineering criteria relevant to UHS feasibility and performance, such as site selection,
storage capacity, containment caprocks, and withdrawal efficiency.

Simpson Group
The Simpson Group consists of five formations including, the Joins, Oil Creek, McLish, Tulip
Creek, and the Bromide (Statler 1965) (Figure 15). All formations of the Simpson Group in
Oklahoma are productive for oil and gas, with the sandstone formations being the most prolific
fields (Suhm, 1997). The Simpson Group of Oklahoma was initially selected for this study for its
high porosity and permeability sandstones. Simpson sandstone reservoirs have been important
hydrocarbon producers for decades within the Anadarko Basin and throughout much of the
state of Oklahoma (Statler 1965). The excellent reservoir potential for the Simpson sandstones
merits further investigation as a potential future success as a H2 storage reservoir. A detailed
geological background and depositional history is included in Appendix A.
The Simpson Group is Middle Ordovician in age, overlying the Arbuckle Group in the Anadarko
Basin, study area. These sediments consist of clean quartzose sandstones interbedded with thick,
shallow-water marine limestones and thin to moderately thick greenish-gray shales (Flores and
Keighin, 1989; Johnson, 1991). Lithologically, the Simpson Group contains sequences of shales,
limestones, and highly porous and clean sandstones (Figure 15).
Reservoirs of the Simpson Group are mature, clean, and well-sorted quartzose sandstones.
Sandstone beds can be either thin bedded or massive. In the Anadarko Basin, Sylvan, and
Woodford Shales function as seals for the Simpson group. The trapping mechanism is primarily
structural (Hill and Clark, 1982). Table 10 summarizes the reservoir system characteristics for the
Simpson Group.

Simpson Group Porosity and Permeability from Cores
Reservoir porosity in producing sandstones ranges from 10% to 30%, with permeabilities
ranging from 15 to 300 (mD) (Ball and others, 1991; Johnson, 1991). High reservoir quality
intervals are derived from well-sorted sandstones which are absent of clay and carbonate
cement (Pollastro, 1989). Simpson Group sandstones within in the Anadarko Basin at depths
between 3,500 and 11,000 feet have a porosity ranging from 10% to 18%, with an average of
15%. The permeability over the same depth range ranges from 15 to 300 millidarcies (mD), with
an average of 120 mD (Harrison and Routh, 1981).
Forgotson et al., 1994, conducted core porosity and permeability measurements on six cores of
the Oil Creek sandstone formation of the lower part of the Simpson Group in McClain,
Cleveland, and Oklahoma Counties (Appendices A and H). Figure 16 shows the crossplot of
permeability vs. porosity for Simpson Group measurements in literature. Three of the cores in
Forgotson et al., 1994 are available at OPIC and were selected for description and sampling in
this study.

Red Fork Formation
The Red Fork Formation, of Middle Pennsylvanian age, has a >100-year history of hydrocarbon
production (Andrews, 1997). Middle Pennsylvanian formations are currently utilized as natural
gas storage facilities in parts of Oklahoma (Wallace, 1963). This long history of successful

 Page 47

hydrocarbon production and natural gas storage in this formation suggests the presence of
good reservoir and seal qualities. The confluence of these attributes suggests the Red Fork
merits further investigation as a potential future subsurface Hydrogen storage reservoir.
On the Anadarko Shelf, the Red Fork is expressed as fluvial systems which fed into the Anadarko
Basin as deltaic and shallow marine depositional systems (Andrews, 1997). Further into the
Anadarko Basin, the Red Fork sands have been interpreted to represent sediment gravity flows
and basin floor fans (Puckette and Al-Shaieb, 2002). The Red Fork is informally subdivided into
two stratigraphic units, named lower Red Fork and upper Red Fork, based on lithology and well
log signatures (Udayashankar, 1985). Reservoirs in both the lower and upper Red Fork are
siliclastic with secondary porosity (Johnson, 1981). Red Fork reservoirs are stratigraphically
trapped, and both the lower and upper Red Fork reservoirs are capped by mudstones which
function as intra-formational seals (Clement, 1991). Reservoir system characteristics are
summarized in Table 11.

Underground hydrogen (H₂) storage is a critical component in scaling up the use of hydrogen as
a clean energy carrier, and depleted natural gas reservoirs represent a logical starting point for
storage assessments. The initial focus on the Red Fork Formation was reasonable given its
existing use for natural gas storage in Oklahoma. However, it primarily serves shelf-channel
systems, which introduced complexity and heterogeneity that proved challenging during
porosity-permeability analysis and log-to-core correlation.
In contrast, the Simpson Group, while also exhibiting variability in porosity and permeability,
presents a more predictable pattern that lends itself better to modeling and evaluation. These
findings underscore the importance of beginning any UHS assessment with a thorough
depositional environment analysis and leveraging high-quality legacy data wherever possible.

Major component analysis
Major component analysis was performed after 6 months of incubation on serum bottle
headspaces containing nitrogen (biotic control), 100% H₂, or a gas mix. The gas mix contained
approximately 50% H₂, 0.65% CO₂, and 49.35% CH₄, with an acceptable error margin of ±10%.
Component concentrations were measured in Mol %.
Overall, Banks bottles with 100% H₂ showed an 18.8% decrease in hydrogen and an 8.9%
increase in methane; with the gas mix, there was a 60.1% loss in hydrogen and a 20.4% methane
increase. Tippens bottles showed similar trends with slightly greater hydrogen loss (22.4%) and a
7.2% methane increase with 100% H₂. For the gas mix, Tippens samples showed 82.9% hydrogen
loss, a 24.4% methane boost, and undetectable CO₂ levels (Table 15). CO₂ decreased from 0.16%

Microbial community analysis
Sulfate-reducing bacteria (Dethiosulfatibacter, Desulfosporosinus, Desulfovibrio) and fermentative
bacteria (Acetobacter, Acidovorax) were found in both original and experimental samples.

observed during incubation. Microbial community diversity decreased after incubation, with
experimental samples dominated by fewer genera. The presence of hydrogen increased the
relative abundance of microbes associated with hydrogen biotransformation (Acidovorax,
Acetobacterium) in Banks and Tippens samples and sulfate-reducing bacteria in Simpson samples.

DISCUSSION

Analysis
This section synthesizes experimental, geological, and modeling results to evaluate what they
collectively imply for underground hydrogen storage in the Anadarko Basin, forming an
integrated understanding of storage behavior and risk. Laboratory studies revealed that
hydrogen interacts with reservoir rocks and fluids in predictable ways, with minimal impact on
porosity or permeability under relevant pressures, and with microbial activity posing limited
concern at depth. These insights build confidence in the physical stability of the storage
environment.
At the reservoir scale, geological heterogeneity emerged as a defining factor in hydrogen
recovery, emphasizing the need for site-specific characterization and careful operational design.
Simulations highlighted the importance of pressure management, cushion gas selection, and
reservoir architecture in determining deliverability and long-term performance. The
technoeconomic and market analyses reinforce these themes, showing that storage viability
depends not only on subsurface behavior but also on broader system dynamics, including
hydrogen demand patterns and the role of storage in balancing renewable generation.
Together, these analyses provide a cohesive interpretation of how technical, biological, and
economic factors converge to shape the feasibility of field-scale deployment.

Results demonstrate that the risk of hydrogen loss due to microbial biotransformation is highly
dependent on pressure and reservoir conditions. Minimal hydrogen conversion at pressures
typical of underground storage (150–800 psi) suggests that microbial activity may not
significantly threaten hydrogen retention in real-world storage scenarios. The increased
biotransformation at ambient pressure—especially with CO2 present—indicates that pressure
management is critical for maintaining hydrogen integrity.
The detection of acetic acid–producing bacteria and sulfate-reducing bacteria across samples
highlights the diversity of microbial communities and their potential roles in hydrogen
transformation. While sulfate-reducing bacteria are known for producing hydrogen sulfide (a
souring agent), their lack of activity during the study suggests that short-term storage at high
pressures may not promote souring, although their presence could pose risks over longer
storage periods.
Water chemistry changes, such as elevated mineral levels and shifts in total carbon, provide
evidence of active microbial metabolism, which could affect reservoir characteristics over time.
The observed reduction in microbial community diversity after incubation implies that hydrogen
storage environments may favor certain microbes, potentially altering ecosystem dynamics and
gas transformation pathways.

 Page 96

Notably, the variable microbial responses among Banks, Tippens, and Simpson samples, as well
as the differences in hydrogen consumption and microbial growth, indicate that site-specific
factors (such as native microbial populations and geochemical conditions) play a crucial role in
determining hydrogen stability and microbial impacts. The ability of piezophilic microorganisms
to survive and even grow under high pressure, without significant hydrogen consumption,
suggests complex adaptive strategies that warrant further investigation.

Pore structure and content
Pore structure analyses reveal that horizontally oriented samples consistently show low
permeability, while vertically adjacent samples exhibit even lower permeability, highlighting
notable vertical heterogeneity, likely due to tight, clay-rich intervals. X-CT and BET methods
together provide a comprehensive understanding of pore size distribution, covering both larger
and finer-scale features. Mineralogical results confirm high quartz content, aligning with
previous studies on Simpson Group rocks. Surface area and pore volume measurements are
consistent with literature values for sandstones and tight sandstones, and indicate most samples
are dominated by mesopores, with some variability in pore size distribution and porosity. Some
of the observed clay minerals, typical of fluvial and deltaic environments, function as grain
coatings, influencing the low cementation seen in these Ordovician Simpson Group reservoir
sands.

Relative permeability
The Nca values found in this study are lower than previous literature suggesting capillary forces
are dominant. The Reynolds number indicates laminar flow in porous media. Aside, because of
hydrogen’s low density, gravity override may occur, so vertical displacements with top-down H2
injection are recommended. Results show that relative permeabilities for all samples reflect
high-water saturation conditions, due to the methodology used and capillary end effects, which
can cause water retention near the outlet and lead to overestimation of water saturation. To
improve accuracy, it is important to account for these effects and use refined experimental
approaches when estimating relative permeability with the JBN method.

Capillary pressure
Capillary pressure is typically determined by reaching a steady state, where no further brine is
produced, hence the wetting phase pressure is considered zero. In this state, capillary pressure
equals the non-wetting phase pressure. Since a complete steady state was not achieved,
pressure data was approximated as capillary pressure when consistent linear behavior was
observed in the method’s diagnostic plots. Alternative experimental techniques, such as porous
plate and mercury injection, could help validate the capillary pressure and water saturation
relationships, which were obtained over a limited range of saturations.

 Page 97

Breakthrough curves
The breakthrough curves reflect differences in gas transport and displacement efficiency within
the core samples. The early methane breakthrough for Sample 3 indicates more favorable
petrophysical properties, such as higher permeability and pore connectivity, allowing for more
efficient hydrogen displacement. In contrast, Samples 1 and 4b show delayed and more gradual
transitions, suggesting more restrictive pore structures. Additionally, residual air in the system
appears to affect measured effluent compositions in all cases.

Cyclic injection impact
Cyclic injection led to minimal changes in porosity among samples, while permeability showed
greater variability, especially in Sample 3. These differences could be partly due to measurement
uncertainty, but other factors may also contribute. The absence of clay peaks in XRD results
indicates clay swelling from brine is unlikely.

Static modeling
The static modeling shows high variability in porosity and permeability across the model area.
Although core data from three wells were integrated into the model, the property models were
heavily dependent on well logs and have a greater range in porosity and permeability values
than the core alone. Additionally, the model highlights the structural variability in the Anadarko
Basin, which guided the selection of a simulation area. Using the heterogeneous model based
on well logs provided a geologically representative property model for fluid flow simulation.

Reservoir modeling: numerical simulation
Early averaged model simulations relied on average porosity and permeability values from three
cored wells. While suitable for preliminary feasibility modeling, this approach overlooks fine-
scale heterogeneities and capillary barriers that influence gas flow and storage. Reservoir
compartmentalization or permeability barriers could further reduce hydrogen deliverability
compared to initial assumptions. To improve model reliability, we evaluated both averaged and
heterogeneous models.
In both models, bottom-hole pressure (BHP) declines sharply at the onset of production,
indicating rapid pressure drawdown near the producer well. This limits flow capacity and
explains the poor recovery observed in cushion gas scenarios. The inability to maintain BHP
above the 3,500 psi constraint confirms insufficient pressure support for high-rate H₂
withdrawal, even with cushion gas injection. Although the reservoir buffers pressure during
injection and shut-in, the steep production-phase decline remains a major constraint. Lowering
withdrawal rates and adding another well would improve the H₂ deliverability.
Cushion gases improve pressure support and allow desirable rates (10 MMscf/day), with H₂
performing best, followed by CH₄, N₂, and CO₂. However, this benefit introduces operational

 Page 98

complexity and economic uncertainty. Across all cases, cushion gas production increases over
time, reaching nearly a 50:50 ratio with H₂ to cushion gas after six months. Water production
further reduces H₂ purity, creating potential challenges for gas separation and downstream
processing. These findings underscore the need to balance enhanced recovery with cushion gas
management and separation costs. Lowering withdrawal rates and adding wells could improve
H₂ purity and deliverability.
H₂ withdrawal rates were consistently higher in the averaged model compared to the geologic
model, regardless of the cushion gas type. This discrepancy underscores the impact of
subsurface rock heterogeneity and compartmentalization on storage performance.
In the averaged model, H₂ recovery ranged from 52% to 57%, whereas the geologic model
achieved significantly lower recovery rates of 6% to 11%, indicating that most injected H₂
remained trapped. These findings emphasize the importance of geologic complexity: while
averaged models tend to overestimate deliverability due to their simplified representation of the
reservoir, geologic models more accurately reflect spatial variability and uncertainty.
Among the cushion gas scenarios, H₂ exhibited a slightly more extensive distribution compared
to N₂, CH₄, and CO₂. These differences are attributed to variations in gas mobility and trapping
mechanisms.
Overall, the averaged model's smoothing of heterogeneity leads to uniform gas distributions
and optimistic performance projections. In contrast, geologically constrained models reveal the
critical influence of stratigraphy and permeability architecture on gas migration and retention.
These results highlight the necessity of incorporating detailed geologic characterization to
reliably assess subsurface hydrogen storage potential.

The TEA results highlight both the promise and the challenges of UHS at commercial scale. The
modeled facility, sized for approximately 6 TWh (~180,000 MT) of storage cycled once annually,
requires a substantial upfront investment of about $1.5 billion, with ongoing operating costs of
about $45 million per year. These figures reflect the capital-intensive nature of subsurface
storage, where wells, compressors, and cushion gas requirements dominate cost structures.
Importantly, the levelized cost of storage, about $1.15/kg when hydrogen is used as the cushion
gas, places UHS within a range that could be competitive under future market and policy
conditions.
The results underscore that capital cost allocation is a decisive factor. As shown in the
breakdown, compressors and wells represent significant cost shares, while the choice of cushion
gas can meaningfully influence both capital and operating costs. For instance, using non-
hydrogen cushion gases introduces the additional burden of purification infrastructure. This
sensitivity to cushion gas selection suggests a need for more robust evaluation of regionally
available options and their long-term economic trade-offs.

 Page 99

Operational assumptions also play a significant role in determining viability. Cycling frequency
was set at once per year in this analysis, and increasing the number of injection and withdrawal
cycles could improve revenue potential and lower the effective cost per unit of hydrogen stored,
though at the expense of higher wear, market mismatches, regulatory friction, and higher
operating costs. Similarly, assumptions about reservoir depth and pressure drive compressor
requirements, which in turn directly affect both energy consumption and capital intensity. These
linkages reinforce the importance of site-specific data in refining the cost profile of UHS.
Overall, these preliminary results suggest that while underground hydrogen storage entails
significant upfront investment, it offers a scalable and potentially cost-effective pathway to
integrate large volumes of hydrogen into future energy systems. Further refinements,
particularly sensitivity analyses on cycling frequency, cushion gas choice, and reservoir
deliverability, will be essential to sharpen the cost estimates and contextualize the
competitiveness of UHS relative to alternative storage pathways.

The primary factor influencing endogenous flexibility—decisions arising within the modeled
system—is the scale and regularity of hydrogen demand. When demand is deeper and more
consistent, the system justifies larger investments in PEM capacity and UHS, with higher
electrolyzer utilization. This highlights that a steady, year-round hydrogen sink is essential for
fully leveraging both conversion and storage capabilities. Regular demand transforms
intermittent renewable surplus into a reliable, time-shifted product through coordinated
expansion of PEM and storage, balancing the value and cost of added flexibility. Without
sufficient demand depth, both PEM and UHS remain limited, even amid high renewable
availability, resulting in increased curtailment.
Curtailment reduction relies on the joint scaling of PEM and UHS with hydrogen demand, not on
electrolyzers alone. Storage extends the time window for electrolyzer operation, aggregating
surplus energy for release during periods of constraint. This system behavior is characterized by
inventories rising in high-resource seasons and declining in low-resource periods, consistent
with long-duration, low-throughput storage designed for seasonal, not short-term, shifts. This
pattern is robust across scenarios, indicating that UHS primarily serves as a seasonal buffer
reallocating energy over months rather than hours.
Average power prices are shaped by how actively the hydrogen system utilizes low-price surplus
energy. Larger, well-used PEM and UHS capacities increase competition for cheap energy,
raising average prices, while shallow demand and smaller capacities result in persistent surplus,
higher curtailment, and lower prices. Notably, moderate changes in UHS cost do not affect
optimal capacity sizing or system operation; these are determined by structural factors such as
demand depth and temporal constraints. Substantial cost or structural changes would be
required to shift the capacity solution.

 Page 100

Overall, UHS acts as a seasonal, system-level flexibility asset, its value unlocked by steady
hydrogen demand and realized through coordination with electrolyzers. Its primary contribution
is to reduce curtailment by storing surplus over long intervals and supplying fuel during
constrained periods, with overall system patterns shaped by demand and temporal dynamics
rather than marginal cost changes.

Recommendations
This section translates the project’s integrated findings into practical guidance for advancing
underground hydrogen storage toward field-scale demonstration. The recommendations focus
on where additional certainty is needed, which operational practices merit refinement, and how
future work can build on the strong technical foundation established in this project. They
emphasize targeted follow-up studies to reduce remaining subsurface uncertainties, particularly
around reservoir heterogeneity, long-term injectivity, and pressure management strategies that
maximize hydrogen recovery. Laboratory results support continued confidence in rock stability
and limited microbial risk at depth, but they also highlight the value of site-specific monitoring
during early operations.
At the modeling and system levels, the recommendations underscore the need for refined
reservoir simulations informed by higher-resolution geological data and for expanded
technoeconomic and market analyses that reflect evolving hydrogen demand and policy
conditions. Collectively, these proposed next steps are designed to de-risk demonstration
activities, strengthen operational planning, and ensure that future field trials are grounded in the
most accurate, integrated understanding of subsurface behavior and system-wide value

Given the reported findings, ongoing microbial monitoring and the development of control
strategies are recommended for future underground hydrogen storage projects. Understanding
and managing microbial communities will help ensure hydrogen purity, minimize souring risks,
and optimize reservoir performance. Specifically, the following actions should be considered:
• Test reservoir fluids from a variety of alternative underground storage sites under different
 pressure and chemical conditions to validate current findings.
• Conduct site-specific evaluations of microbial communities before selecting any location for
 hydrogen storage, due to unique microbial populations at each site.
• Investigate the variability of hydrogen biotransformation and microbial stimulation of
 hydrogen consumption across different reservoirs.
• Extend incubation experiments beyond six months to better understand the long-term
 effects of microbial activity on hydrogen retention and quality.
• Develop and assess hydrogen-compatible and sustainable biocides to mitigate potential
 negative impacts of microbes on stored hydrogen quality.

 Page 101

Based on the findings from core-flooding experiments, it is recommended to target reservoir
units with higher quality pore structures, such as those associated with Sample-3, to optimize
hydrogen injection and storage. Enhanced characterization of sedimentological facies and
hydraulic units should be prioritized in future reservoir modeling to identify zones where
capillary pressure and permeability conditions are most favorable for hydrogen migration and
retention. Additionally, further investigation into clay types and their interactions with brine will
inform effective mud design for future injector wells and help mitigate permeability loss during
operations.
The observed challenges in hydrogen displacement and brine recovery underline the
importance of optimizing mobility contrast and injection rates. Specifically, low relative
permeability to hydrogen and high residual water saturation necessitate the development of
injection strategies, such as higher rate injection protocols and the use of cushion gases, to
maximize storage efficiency. Future laboratory studies conducted under reservoir-representative
conditions—high pressure and elevated temperature—would yield more accurate estimates of
relative permeability and porosity, facilitating better planning for field implementation.
Finally, incorporating cushion gases like methane is recommended to enhance hydrogen
storage performance and facilitate optimal gas recovery. As demonstrated by the breakthrough
curves and recovery rates observed in binary mixture tests, initial methane production may be
significant, necessitating the estimation of ideal hydrogen and cushion gas compositions for
injection. Continued refinement of experimental protocols and modeling approaches will be
crucial in advancing underground hydrogen storage technologies in the studied reservoir
system.

The Bromide Formation of the Simpson Group within the study area appears feasible for hydrogen
storage. To enhance storage and recovery performance, we recommend the following:

 1. Collect Additional Core Data
 Acquire seismic survey and more porosity and permeability measurements from cores
 around the simulation area. Include seismic attributes into static geological model and
 Simpson Group cores and Woodford Shale caprock samples to better understand
 reservoir heterogeneity and seal integrity.

 2. Consider Faults

 o The current model was built without integrating faults in the geocellular grids.
 Fault interpretation in the structural model will improve the predictability of
 simulation and gas migration.

 Page 102

 3. Target High-Permeability Zones
 Select areas with the highest permeability to maximize injection and withdrawal
 efficiency.

 4. Extend Production Duration
 Increase the hydrogen production period by either:

 o Extending production beyond six months, or

 o Implementing multiple injection–withdrawal cycles.

 5. Optimize Well Configuration and Rates

 o Use two production wells instead of one.

 o Reduce production rates to prevent sudden BHP drops, minimize cushion gas, and
 water production, and improve hydrogen recovery.

These strategies aim to improve H₂ deliverability, maintain pressure support, and reduce
operational challenges associated with cushion gas and water handling.

The TEA indicates a sizable impact of the cost of cushion gas, and the cheapest cushion gas that
can be used is natural gas. At $3/MMBTU, it is cheaper than nitrogen obtained from an air
separation unit ($0.2/kg) or hydrogen itself ($4/kg). This model already includes the cost of
pressure swing adsorption to account for non-hydrogen cushion gases. Future work should
focus on the process development of a UHS system with natural gas as the cushion gas to keep
the overall costs as low as possible.

The current modeling framework offers a solid base for assessing underground hydrogen
storage (UHS) in power-hydrogen systems although extending its capabilities could increase its
value. Future improvements should include more detailed and variable hydrogen demand, with
time-varying prices and usage profiles, segmentation by end-use (such as refining, ammonia,
steel, transport, and shipping), and scenario-based demand uncertainty to better support
strategic planning. Integrating dynamic natural gas prices and policy measures like carbon
pricing or renewable energy credits would improve dispatch accuracy and fuel-switching
analysis. Sensitivity studies should go beyond ±25% UHS cost, exploring variations in PEM
efficiency, curtailment costs, and hydrogen prices.
Spatial detail is another critical area: representing multiple UHS sites with unique geologic
features, working limits, and deliverability constraints, alongside hydrogen transmission and
distribution networks, would enable co-optimization of storage locations and energy flows.

 Page 103

Adding zonal or nodal grids can improve alignment between renewable generation, electrolyzer
placement, and storage deployment, reflecting grid congestion more accurately.
Broadening hydrogen production pathways (e.g., autothermal reforming, biomass gasification,
high-temperature electrolysis) and including co-product use from electrolysis could diversify
technology options and competitiveness. Incorporating short-duration storage technologies like
batteries would enable evaluation of flexibility across timescales, while ramp-rate limits and
cycling penalties would clarify conditions for seasonal versus higher-frequency UHS operation.
Participating in markets for reconverting hydrogen to power via turbines or fuel cells, especially
under actual products and prices, can reveal new economic opportunities under varying
reliability scenarios.
Finally, incorporating stochastic or robust optimization to account for weather, load, fuel price,
and outage uncertainties will strengthen model reliability. Calibration and validation against
observed Oklahoma data and historical power trends are recommended to ensure empirical
accuracy.

Conclusions
This section brings together the project’s core insights to present an overarching assessment of
underground hydrogen storage potential in the Anadarko Basin. The conclusions reflect the
cumulative weight of laboratory experiments, geological characterization, reservoir simulations,
and system-level modeling, offering a clear picture of what is now understood and what it
means for future deployment. Collectively, the results indicate that the Simpson Group exhibits
the fundamental attributes required for safe and effective hydrogen storage, with rock
properties that remain stable under cyclic gas exposure and microbial processes that pose
limited risk at representative pressures. Fluid-flow experiments and petrophysical analyses
provide confidence in predictable gas transport within these formations.
At the field scale, integrated geological and reservoir models demonstrate that storage
performance is achievable under realistic operational conditions, though influenced by
heterogeneity and pressure management. The technoeconomic and market assessments further
show that underground storage delivers system value under a range of future energy scenarios,
particularly where hydrogen demand is steady and renewable generation is abundant. Together,
these findings support the technical feasibility of pursuing a field-scale demonstration and
outline a solid foundation for subsequent maturation of underground hydrogen storage in
porous media.

Microbial tests were conducted on reservoir fluid samples incubated with hydrogen gas from
three storage sites. Both liquid components and headspace gases were analyzed.

 Page 104

Hydrogen sulfide remained below detection limits in all cases, with no change in sulfate
reduction. Samples contained various microbes unique to each site; however, ambient pressure
showed little influence on microbial communities, regardless of hydrogen concentration. High-
pressure conditions (150-800 psi) had minimal impact on hydrogen biotransformation across
sample sources. At ambient pressure, more H2 was converted to methane and organic acids,
especially with CO2 present. Hydrogen incubation reduced microbial diversity.
Before storing hydrogen underground, reservoir chemistry and microbial composition should be
evaluated to prevent hydrogen loss. These findings offer insights into chemical changes and
microbial dynamics related to hydrogen storage.

As part of this project, PNNL was tasked with characterizing representative reservoir rocks from
the Simpson Group formation in the Anadarko Basin and assessing H2 fluid behavior through
porous media to evaluate the feasibility of UHS.
To achieve this objective, core samples were selected collaboratively and subjected to
comprehensive petrophysical characterization. Key properties were quantified using a
combination of techniques. These analyses established a fundamental understanding of the
rocks’ pore structure, surface morphology, and mineralogical composition as baseline rock
properties for advanced experiments and modeling efforts.
Fluid flow behavior was investigated under both single-phase (gas or water) and two-phase (gas
and brine) flow conditions. Gaseous H2 was studied as the target storage gas, along with a CH4
and H2 mixture for cushion gas evaluation. Core-flooding experiments were conducted to
determine key transport properties, including relative permeability, capillary pressures, and
residual saturation. The effect of gas composition was also evaluated using in-line GC to
quantify recovery and breakthrough profiles.
Key findings from this study are summarized below:
• Simpson Group rock samples are composed of quartz (>99%), with minimal clay content
 unlikely to impact hydrogen storage or flow.
• Porosity values are between 7.4% and 10.4%; absolute permeability ranges from 2.5 to 19.0
 x10-15 m2, indicating low to moderate reservoir quality.
• Relative permeability to hydrogen is significantly lower than to brine, consistent with
 literature for typical UHS sandstones.
• Cyclic hydrogen drainage did not alter porosity or absolute permeability, suggesting short-
 term exposure does not degrade petrophysical properties.

This study applied geological and reservoir modeling to evaluate hydrogen feasibility,
movement, storage capacity, and potential losses within a subsurface storage complex in the

 Page 105

Anadarko Basin. Dynamic simulations using CMG incorporated actual temperature and pressure
conditions and included both storage and seal formations. Historical production data from nine
wells supported preliminary capacity estimates.
Two reservoir models were developed: an averaged model, based on porosity and permeability
values from three cored wells, for feasibility screening and a geologically heterogeneous model
using sequential Gaussian simulation (SGS) to capture spatial variability. Simulation scenarios
examined injectivity, storage capacity, plume migration, and recovery under varying withdrawal
durations and cushion gas strategies (H₂, N₂, CH₄, CO₂). Sensitivity analyses identified optimal
bottom-hole pressure (BHP) for maximizing recovery.
Key findings include:
• Geologic evaluation: Permeability and porosity significantly vary across the structurally
 complex Anadarko Basin. Detailed geologic models inform the selection of storage locations
 and provides the basis for simulation with higher deliverability.
• Performance ranking: H₂ and N₂ cushions yield the highest hydrogen recovery and lowest
 water production; CO₂ performs worst.
• Operational considerations: H₂ cushions may increase costs due to handling and physical
 properties; cushion gas breakthrough reduces hydrogen purity, requiring additional
 processing.
• Model comparison: H₂ recovery from the averaged model is ~5× higher than from the
 heterogeneous model, indicating that heterogeneity critically influences storage
 performance and deliverability.
• Flow behavior: Gas migrates farther in high-permeability zones, forming irregular plumes but
 remains contained below the caprock.
• Geological suitability: The Bromide Formation within the Simpson Group appears favorable
 for hydrogen storage.
• Overall, these results confirm the feasibility of underground hydrogen storage (UHS) in
 depleted reservoirs and underscore the importance of geological heterogeneity in
 performance forecasts. They also highlight operational factors, such as cushion gas selection,
 purity management, and monitoring strategies that are critical for successful UHS
 deployment and long-term reliability.

For an underground hydrogen storage facility of 180,000 metric tonnes (~6 TWh), cycled once a
year, the total capital cost is $1.5B, and annual operating costs are approximately $45M. After
performing a discounted cash flow rate of return analysis, the levelized cost of UHS is
approximately $1.15/kg with hydrogen as the cushion gas. The sensitivity analysis revealed that
cushion gas has a major impact on the economics, and a cheap non-hydrogen cushion gas, like
natural gas, is suggested to reduce overall costs.

 Page 106

This study applied an integrated power–hydrogen optimization model to evaluate the
operational and economic conditions most favorable for the deployment of UHS in Oklahoma.
Five scenarios were examined to represent a range of future system states, varying in hydrogen
demand intensity, renewable penetration, and UHS cost. The modeling quantified system-wide
outcomes including total cost, dispatchable generation, storage utilization, and renewable
curtailment.
Results show that UHS plays a decisive role in enabling flexibility once hydrogen demand
becomes deep and continuous. Under low-demand conditions, storage and electrolysis remain
modest, and renewable curtailment remains high. When hydrogen demand increases and
becomes more regular, the system endogenously builds more electrolyzer and UHS capacity,
reducing curtailment and stabilizing power system operation. Across all cases, UHS behaves as a
seasonal storage asset with one annual cycle, shifting hydrogen from periods of renewable
surplus to periods of higher demand. Varying UHS cost within a ±25% range affects total system
cost but does not change optimal capacities or operational patterns.
Taken together, these results highlight that the system value of UHS is governed less by
marginal cost assumptions and more by the structural depth and regularity of hydrogen
demand. The model shows that when UHS is combined with large-scale electrolysis, it plays a
key role in supporting renewable energy integration and providing long-term flexibility. This
helps balance the differences between fluctuating energy production and steady hydrogen use
over time.

APPENDIX A: LITERATURE REVIEW

Geological background
Pre-Anadarko Basin tectonics initiated during the Cambrian-PreCambrian breakup of the
Rhodinian super continent, which created a failed rift arm, the Southern Oklahoma Aulacogen
(SOA). This failed rift arm experienced rapid post rift thermal subsidence from the Precambrian
through the end of the Silurian which created a zone of amplified accommodation space
(Feinstien, 1981; Johnson, 1991). From the end Silurian through middle Mississippian, the SOA
became stable, and a broad passive margin developed which accumulated marine black shales
such as the Devonian Woodford Shale and siltstones. By the late Mississippian – early
Pennsylvanian, Gondwana collided with Laurussia during the Alleghenian-Ouachita Orogeny
creating a series of northwest-southeast oriented basement cored uplifts, the Ancestral Rocky
Mountains (ARM) (Kluth, 1986; Kluth and Coney, 1981; Dickenson et al. 2003). In southern
Oklahoma, the Wichita-Amarillo Uplift, (Johnson, 1989) accompanied the the subsidence of the
asymmetrical Anadarko foreland basin (Feinstien, 1981; Soreghan et al. 2012; Sweet et al. 2021).
During the Ordovician, the US midcontinent region of Laurentia was positioned within equatorial
regions (~ -10- -15°S) on a stable craton and wrapped within an arid climate belt (Boucot et al.
2013; Domeier and Torsvik, 2014; Torsvik and Cocks, 2017). The midcontinent epeiric seas
transitioned to a passive margin, facing south-southwest toward the Iapetus Ocean, where the
region of the SOA underwent rapid post-rift thermal subsidence (Feinstien, 1981). Rapid
subsidence created accommodation for thick accumulations of limestones, mudstones, and
sandstones of the Simpson Group to accumulate within the aulacogen during the early
Ordovician (Florian) to late Ordovician (Sandbian). One of the key characteristics of the Simpson
group is its thick accumulation of mature sandstones. The sandstones contain a predominance
of Superior aged grains (~2600 – 2900 Ma) with subordinate contributions from were sourced
from Granite Rhyolite, Granite Rhyolite, Yavapai-Mazatzal and Penokean Trans-Hudson
indicating that they were sourced from the north, recycled from the St. Peter’s Sandstone (Pickle,
2012; Thomas et al. 2016).
Simpson Group
Depositional Environment
The Simpson Group is Middle Ordovician in age, overlying the Arbuckle Group in the Anadarko
Basin, study area. The Simpson rocks mark a shift from carbonate deposition due to the influx of
clastic sediments. These sediments consist of clean quartzose sandstones interbedded with
thick, shallow-water marine limestones and thin to moderately thick greenish-gray shales (Flores
and Keighin, 1989; Johnson, 1991).
Sea-level fluctuations produced regional to local unconformities within the Simpson Group,
leading to a variety of stratigraphic complexities (Gaswirth, and Higley, 2014). As the sea level
rose, sand was reworked into an extensive and sheet-like transgressive deposit in marine
shoreface and tidal flat environments, which is overlain by marine shales and carbonates. Sea-

 A-1

level fluctuations during the remainder of Simpson's time produced a succession of sands,
shales, and limestones (Johnson, 1991).

There are five formations within the Simpson group, the Joins, Oil Creek, McLish, Tulip Creek,
and the Bromide (Statler 1965). The basal sandstones of the Simpson form regionally extensive
sheet-like bodies which can be traced over long distances with thickness ranging from 50 to 200
feet (Gaswirth and Higley, 2014). The Simpson Group records a series of sheet sands that were
deposited within coastal to marine environments and records transgressive and regressive cycles
within the Tippecanoe Sequence. These sands are reworked by eolian processes atop subaerially
exposed carbonates (Shum, 2016). During transgressions, sands were reworked into an extensive
sheet-like transgressive deposit in marine shoreface and tidal flat environments which were then
overlain by marine shales and carbonates (Johnson, 1991). Some sandstones are less continuous
and cannot be traced for long distances laterally. The sand was sourced from the St. Peters
sandstone from the North American Shield and distributed to the southern midcontinent region
within a shallow marine environment (Suhm, 1995).
Reservoir and Seal Properties
Reservoirs of the Simpson Group are from mature, clean, well-sorted quartzose sandstones.
Sandstone beds can be either thin bedded or massive. The porosity of the sandstones range
between 10 – 30 % with permeabilities ranging between 15 – 300 (mD) (Ball et al. 1991; Johnson,
1991). While clays and shales are present within the Simpson Group, the highest reservoir quality
intervals are derived from well sorted sandstone beds with a dearth of clay (Pollastro, 1989).
There are green shales within the Simpson Group which have been suggested to serve as
hydrocarbon sources. The thickness of the Simpson Group varies throughout the Anadarko
Basin. In general, stratigraphic thickness is thinner on the basin shelf but thickens toward the
southeast within the deeper regions of the Anadarko Basin (Figure 57) (Gaswirth and Higley,
2014).

The Oil Creek formation can range ~800 to ~900 ft thick and contains sandstones, thin
limestones, and shales. The sandstone reservoirs are often silica cemented but may contain
occurrences of calcareous cement (Statler, 1965). The McLish formation average ~450 to 500 ft
thick. At the base of the McLish is a sandstone that has minor amounts of clays. Some cement of
the McLish is dolomitic. Above the McLish sands are limestone units. There are no reported
shales within the McLish (Statler, 1965). Bromide contains a basal sand which contains also what
is termed Tulip Creek. The upper part of the bromide is composed of carbonates. Interbedded
shales and limestones overlie the bromide. This formation can range between ~400 to 800 ft
thick (Statler, 1965).
Seal
Simpson reservoir seals are the interbedded Simpson shales and tight sandstones or carbonates
(Gaswirth and Higley, 2014). Tight sandstones within the Oil Creek Formation have also been
documented to function as vertical barriers to fluid movement upward (Smith, 1997). A regional
migration seal for the Simpson Group is the upper Ordovician Sylvan Shale. Above the Sylvan
Shale is the Hunton Limestone, followed by another regional seal of the Late Devonian
Woodford Shale.

Simpson Group Porosity and Permeability from Cores
Forgotson et al., 1994, conducted core porosity and permeability measurements on 6 cores of
the Oil Creek sandstone formation of the lower part of the Simpson Group (Table 25). These
cores were taken from wells located on the eastern side of the Anadarko Basin, parts of McClain,
Cleveland, and Oklahoma Counties. Four of the six cores studied are available at OPIC. Three of
those cores were described and sampled for this study. Simpson core analyses from the 6 cores
() reveal that porosity ranges from 1-20%, with an average of 4%, and a mode of 9%, while
permeability ranges from <1 to 285 mD, with an average of 39 mD, and a mode of 5 mD.
The Oil Creek sandstone cores consist of clean, fine to exceptionally fine sandstone, overlain by
shale, and underlain by wavy laminated fine sandstone, siltstone, and thin carbonate layers
(Forgotson et al., 1994). The sandstone grains are mostly quartz with a few heavy minerals. They
are well-sorted, well-rounded, and bioturbated (Forgotson et al., 1994). The dominant cement in
the Oil Creek sandstones is dolomite with quartz overgrowths. The overgrown quartz cement
constitutes up to 15% of the bulk volume, and dolomite cement constitutes up to 10%. The
dissolution of these cements results in the main reservoir porosity of the Oil Creek sandstone.
The dissolution of dolomite cement and matrix formed the highest values of porosity and
permeability. The Oil Creek sandstone's primary porosity (matrix) is typically less than 12%, while
the secondary porosity (solution), formed by the dissolution of the early matrix and cement, can
reach up to 25%.

Red Fork Formation
Depositional Environments

The Red Fork is a formation in Oklahoma within the middle Pennsylvanian (Desmoinesian)
Cherokee Group (Figure 58). Cherokee Group (and Red Fork) strata were formed by sea-level
fluctuations which generated a series of vertically stacked repeating cycles of sandstones, shales,
limestones and paleosols. During intervals of low sea-level, river systems drained the North
American continent carrying sediments sourced from northeast and southeast where they were
deposited within Anadarko Basin (Kushner et al. 2022). On the Anadarko Shelf, the Red Fork is
expressed as fluvial systems which fed into the Anadarko Basin as deltaic and shallow marine
depositional systems (Andrews, 1997). Further in the Anadarko Basin, the Red Fork sands have
been interpreted to represent sediment gravity flows and basin floor fans (Puckette and Al-
Shaieb, 2002).

Reservoirs and Seals
The Red Fork is informally subdivided into two stratigraphic units, named lower Red Fork and

upper Red Fork, based on lithology and well log signatures (Udayashankar, 1985). Both units
contain siliclastics reservoir units contain detrital quartz silt, feldspars, and rock fragments with
secondary porosity as the primary porosity (Udayashankar, 1985; Johnson, 1981; Cooper, 2017).
Porosity and permeability tend to overall decrease from the shelf to the basin (Figure 59), in part
likely mirroring a fining with distance of grain size. Highest porosity is observed in fluvial
sandstone bodies on the Anadarko Shelf which average 12.1% - 16.38% whereas lower
porosities are within distal basin strata which average ~3.60 – 10.98%. However, there are fewer
published porosity and permeability data points from cores in western Oklahoma, Roger Mills,
Ellis, and Dewey County regions of the Anadarko Basin, as shown in two cores that have only
one sample selected, each at 7.7% and 11.7%. While porosity decreases basin ward with finer
grain sizes, distributary channels and mouth bars will have coarser grains lending to pockets of
elevated porosity.
Red Fork reservoirs are stratigraphically trapped, and both the lower and upper Red Fork
reservoirs are capped by mudstones which function as intra-formational seals (Clement, 1991).
While the strata thickness increases basin ward, the Lower Red Fork reservoir in Dewey County
within the Wessely Clark 1A core is capped by a thick ~25 ft. silty dark shale (Udayashankar,
1985; p. 134 - 135). The upper Red Fork is also overlain by a thick silty shale that transitions into
a thin (~5ft) limestone unit; the Pink Limestone followed by additional mudstone intervals
(Udayashankar, 1985).

1985; Johnson, 1981; Cooper, 2017). This variability is attributed to the high clay input in the
levee versus the channel deposit system.

In this method:
• The pressure gradient must be sufficiently large to minimize capillary pressure end effects
 and, more broadly, to reduce the influence of any capillary pressure gradient.
• The pressure differential across the core should remain small relative to the total operating
 pressure, ensuring that fluid compressibility effects are negligible.
• The rock samples should be homogeneous and initially 100% water saturated to meet the
 underlying assumptions of the analytical models.
• The driving force and fluid properties should remain constant throughout the test to
 maintain consistent flow conditions.
• The flow direction should be horizontal, using a small core sample and a test duration short
 enough to minimize the influence of gravitational forces.
Eq. 1A originates from Welge’s extension of the Buckley–Leverett theory (Buckley Leverett
(1952)). Eq. 2A represents a volumetric fraction, while Eq. 3A estimates the average gas
saturation behind a plane of constant saturation—evaluated at the effluent location in
accordance with the Buckley–Leverett solution.
Eqs. 4A to 6A are derived from Darcy’s law, each incorporating relevant scaling parameters: Eq.
4A introduces a scaling factor that considers the instantaneous volume of gas injected. C1, used

 B-1

in Eqs. 4A–6A, is a scaling constant based on the mean pore pressure in the core, applying
Boyle’s law to account for gas compressibility.

Eqs. 7A to 9A are grounded in fractional flow theory, which describes immiscible two-phase flow
in porous media, fundamental to modeling displacement processes in reservoir systems.
According to Torsaeter and Abtahi (2003), the unsteady-state method offers several advantages:
it is faster than the steady-state technique, experimentally simpler, and more closely mimics
reservoir drainage conditions. It also enables estimation of endpoint relative permeability values.
However, the method often covers only a limited saturation range and typically provides
endpoint data rather than full curvature. A key limitation lies in capillary pressure discontinuities
at the core ends, which can distort pressure measurements and recovery estimates. These
capillary end effects are particularly pronounced under low-pressure gradients or in the
presence of subtle core heterogeneities.

Simpson Group core descriptions
Three cores from the Simpson Group, Berry Unit 1, Czar 2, and Leeper A-1, were described, and

their stratigraphic columns were constructed as shown in Figure 68 to Figure 74. Their lithology
and facies classifications are documented in Table 35. Core descriptions help in understanding
rock fabric, grain sorting, and cementation, all of which provide insights into reservoir quality.
The Bromide Formation of the Simpson Group is characterized by clean, fine- to medium-
grained, well-sorted, and rounded sandstones with low cementation, indicating excellent
reservoir quality. Therefore, the Bromide Formation may be a suitable target for H₂ storage.
