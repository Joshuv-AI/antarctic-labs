Substance use disorder in forensic hospitals: A scoping review of practices and interventions
Authors: Dane Mauer-Vakil, Erin Pereira, Christopher Canning, Bernard Le Foll, Geoff Bardwell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubstance use disorders (SUDs) are prevalent in individuals with mental illness who interact with criminal justice systems, and inpatient forensic hospital care presents a treatment opportunity. Despite recognition that addressing SUD should be a central feature in forensic hospital care, knowledge is limited regarding existing practices. This scoping review addressed the research question: “What is known about the screening, assessment, treatment, and management of SUD in inpatient forensic hospital settings?”.MethodsWe conducted this review in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis and the PRISMA extension for scoping reviews, searching MEDLINE, PsycINFO, PubMed, and Embase databases. Inclusion criteria comprised original, English language, peer-reviewed studies focusing on inpatient substance use practices and interventions in forensic hospitals internationally, and published from database inception to May 13, 2025.ResultsForty-nine articles were included. Most of this research has been conducted in Europe using observational study designs. SUDburden was high, with prevalence estimates varying depending on detection approach. Treatment evidence came mostly from uncontrolled evaluations with small sample sizes of group-based psychosocial interventions. These data show acceptability and improvements in intermediate outcomes, but mixed results on substance use-specific outcomes. Studies highlighted service fragmentation, workforce training gaps, and security-related issues that strained the therapeutic alliance.ConclusionThis review can inform the development of effective SUD treatment pathways and standardized models of care, improving health outcomes and quality of life, and reducing recidivism in this vulnerable population. This work establishes a foundational knowledge base essential for improving forensic hospital services.Keywords: Substance use, Forensic hospital, Psychiatry, Policy, Scoping review, Clinical practice

Introduction

Individuals with concurrent mental illness and substance use disorders (SUDs) are disproportionately represented in criminal justice and forensic mental health systems (Moore et al., 2020; Olagunju et al., 2022a; Penney et al., 2019). This overrepresentation reflects a complex interplay of social conditions, clinical vulnerabilities, and system-level features. Factors such as impulsivity, trauma exposure, and antisocial behaviours are associated with an elevated risk of aggressive or violent behaviour in some individuals with concurrent disorders (Lagerberg et al., 2025, Swanson et al., 1990, Zhong et al., 2020). However, substance use typically acts as an exacerbating factor that interacts with psychiatric symptoms, social stressors, and situational risks (Pickard and Fazel, 2013). Concurrent disorders also frequently intersect with social issues, including housing instability and homelessness, which can further complicate access to and continuity of mental health services (Hodgson et al., 2024). Such precarious living conditions reduce engagement with mental health services (Kaplan et al., 2019) and increase the likelihood of contact with police (Kouyoumdjian et al., 2019, Zakrison et al., 2004).

Previous meta-analyses have shown that the odds of committing an offense is greater for those who use substances compared to those who do not (Sinha and Easton, 1999, Bennett et al., 2008), but these associations weaken when co-occurring mental illness, prior victimization, and socioeconomic disadvantage are accounted for (Pickard and Fazel, 2013). Notably, while violence risk varies considerably across substances, diagnostic groups, and contexts, most individuals with mental illness and SUDs are not violent (Whiting et al., 2021). Importantly, for a subset of people whose mental illness and SUD directly affect their legal capacity, criminal justice involvement may result in designations of Unfit to Stand Trial (UST) or Not Criminally Responsible on Account of Mental Disorder (NCRMD), leading to diversion into secure forensic mental health hospitals (Chaimowitz et al., 2022, Olagunju et al., 2018). People receiving inpatient forensic mental health care frequently arrive with untreated concurrent disorders and extensive histories of trauma, institutionalization, and social exclusion. SUDs are highly prevalent in this vulnerable population (Kivimies et al., 2012, Kraanen et al., 2012, Ogloff et al., 2004, Penney et al., 2019), oftentimes predating an index offence and continuing through hospitalization (van der Kraan et al., 2014).

Forensic mental health systems are comprised of services that assess and treat the mental health and criminogenic needs of individuals who intersect with criminal justice and legal systems (Crocker et al., 2017). It serves individuals whose mental health is pertinent to trial participation and/or criminal responsibility and consists of various services, including psychiatric assessment and evaluation, inpatient psychiatric hospital treatment, and outpatient and community services (e.g., rehabilitation, supportive housing, vocational training). Forensic mental health systems aim to strike a balance between individual treatment and public safety, emphasizing rehabilitation as opposed to punishment. The legal frameworks that guide decisions about mental illness in relation to fitness to stand trial and criminal responsibility vary across jurisdictions and are described elsewhere (Beis et al., 2022, Crocker et al., 2017, Crocker et al., 2022). Forensic mental health systems commonly include independent oversight bodies that review detention, discharge, and community reintegration processes.

One critical branch of forensic mental health systems is that of inpatient forensic hospital care. Practitioners that commonly work in this space are medical doctors and nurses who have undergone subspecialty training in forensic psychiatry. A key role of the forensic psychiatrist is to conduct psychiatric assessments, including evaluations of fitness to stand trial and criminal responsibility. Both forensic psychiatrists and nurses provide treatment in secure forensic hospitals, with various other practitioners assisting with frontline care (e.g., forensic/patient care assistants, therapists, counsellors). In the United States, forensic psychiatry is typically practised within state-based mental health systems, with variation in how services are organized across jurisdictions. The core functions typically include competency-to-stand-trial and criminal responsibility evaluations, alongside treatment delivered in specialized forensic units, state hospitals, general psychiatric facilities, and in some cases correctional settings (Crocker et al., 2017; Wik et al., 2020). In Canada, forensic psychiatry is similarly organized at the provincial and territorial level via service provision in secure forensic units within psychiatric hospitals, although models of service delivery vary across the country (Beis et al., 2022, Crocker et al., 2017). In other jurisdictions, forensic psychiatry services are commonly delivered through secure inpatient hospitals or specialist forensic units, sometimes complemented by outpatient and community-based services (Salize et al., 2023, Völlm et al., 2018).

Globally, the high prevalence of SUD in forensic psychiatry populations (Kivimies et al., 2012, Kraanen et al., 2012, Ogloff et al., 2004, Penney et al., 2019) significantly influences clinical management and legal outcomes (Simpson et al., 2015). A systematic review, whereby 12 of the 14 included studies were conducted in the United States, documented this high prevalence across several jurisdictions, emphasizing their strong association with negative outcomes, including elevated recidivism risk and poor treatment response (Marquant et al., 2021). Diagnosis, treatment, and rehabilitation are complicated when mental illness is comorbid with SUDs in forensic patients. This is evidenced by the association of SUDs with longer hospitalization, more significant criminal histories, and antisocial behaviour (Hilton et al., 2018, Olagunju et al., 2022a, Olagunju et al., 2022b, Olagunju et al., 2024).

In Canada, recent studies have shown elevated rates of SUD among patients under forensic hospital care. For example, an Ontario population-based review of forensic hospital patients reported that 57.2% had a diagnosed SUD, with psychotic spectrum disorders and prior criminal justice involvement commonplace (Chaimowitz et al., 2022). Similarly, Hilton et al. (2018) found that among high-secure forensic inpatients with a major mental illness, 61% met diagnostic criteria for a co-occurring SUD, yet only 19% were diagnosed as such. This underscores a substantial under-recognition, and thus underdiagnosis, of SUD as a concurrent health issue. This may contribute to a lesser likelihood of SUD being considered as a criminogenic need, with care planning implications. Overall, SUDs contribute considerably to the complexity of risk management in forensic psychiatry populations, highlighting the need for integrated treatment approaches.

Substance use plays a role in shaping clinical and criminogenic needs within forensic mental health care. For example, alcohol use is strongly associated with violent crime due to its impact on aggression and impulsivity (Fazel et al., 2009). Alcohol-related offenses are common in forensic psychiatry populations, and those with alcohol use issues experience lengthy hospitalizations (Simpson et al., 2015). Notably, alcohol use is more prevalent compared to other substances; hazardous drinking, as defined by World Health Organization (WHO) criteria has a global prevalence of 39.5% (World Health Organization, 2018). Cannabis, the third most used substance globally after alcohol and tobacco (United Nations, 2020), may contribute to psychosis or behavioural disturbances, and is associated with non-violent offenses (Hoch et al., 2015). Importantly, the American legal landscape surrounding cannabis is evolving, with more states permitting use for medical purposes (40 states) and some also recreationally (24 states; National Conference of State Legislatures, 2025).

Despite recognition that addressing SUD should be a central feature in forensic hospital care, knowledge is limited regarding existing practices. As a result, it is unclear how SUD is managed in inpatient forensic hospital settings. Two evidence syntheses have examined similar topics, but neither map the broader SUD care literature in forensic hospitals. Eagle et al. (2019) reviewed models of care for concurrent mental illness and substance use across inpatient rehabilitation settings, reporting an absence of studies specific to secure forensic settings, thus requiring reliance on evidence from other contexts (Eagle et al., 2019). In contrast, Krishnan and Ireland (2025) focused solely on structured, group-based, psychosocial substance use interventions for forensic inpatients with dual diagnosis, rather than the full range of SUD hospital care. Thus, a scoping review methodology is warranted to map this broader literature. This study addresses the following research question: “What is known about the screening, assessment, treatment, and management of SUD in inpatient forensic hospital settings?”.

Methods

This study employed a scoping review of peer-reviewed literature for knowledge synthesis purposes. This approach was appropriate given the need to broadly map the literature (Arksey and O’Malley, 2005). We conducted this review in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis (Aromataris et al., 2024), which is a widely recognized scoping review framework. Further, we conducted our reporting according to the Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR); see Appendix A for checklist (Tricco et al., 2018). This scoping review provides an inclusive overview of current practices as evidenced in the peer-reviewed literature.

Search strategy and study selection criteria

A preliminary search was conducted and no current or underway scoping or systematic reviews on the topic were identified. We developed the search strategies iteratively. The first author identified five articles as benchmark sources to prepare a shortlist of text and index terms. This list was expanded upon and finalized via discussion between the first, second, and last author. The second author conducted the searches in MEDLINE, PsycINFO, PubMed, and Embase databases on May 13, 2025, adapting the search strategy as per database requirements. Please see Appendix B for the search terms and strategy. The inclusion criteria comprised original, English language, peer-reviewed research studies that focused on inpatient substance use practices and interventions in forensic hospitals internationally, and were published up to May 13, 2025. Please see Appendix C for the detailed eligibility criteria. The review was registered with Open Science Framework on May 31, 2025 (https://osf.io/zjdb2/overview).

Source of evidence selection

After the search was conducted, all identified source citations were uploaded to Zotero and then subsequently to Covidence (a web-based workflow tool for managing evidence syntheses). Duplicates were then removed. The first and second author conducted pilot reviews whereby articles were assessed at the title and abstract stage and discussion ensued with the last author to confirm eligibility- and process-related concerns. Once high agreement (>95%) was attained (two cycles of 10 articles each), the first and second authors independently reviewed all titles and abstracts via application of the eligibility criteria. At the full-text stage, both first and second authors read each article in full to assess compliance with the eligibility criteria. Any disagreements arising at any stage of this review were resolved via consensus discussion with the last author. Reasons for exclusion were selected in Covidence as per standard review methodology protocol (Arksey and O’Malley, 2005, https://jbi-global-wiki.refined.site/space/MANUAL, Tricco et al., 2018). We contacted the authors of papers that could not be retrieved. We also screened the sources in the reference lists of all initially included papers in accordance with JBI requirements (Aromataris et al., 2024). Articles selected for inclusion moved forward to the data extraction phase. Please see the PRISMA Flow Diagram for full search and study inclusion details (Fig. 1).

Data extraction and analysis

The first author used Covidence to facilitate data extraction for the papers included in this review (see Appendix D for study details). A broad and all-encompassing definition of ‘substance use’ was employed in this review (i.e., database search, data extraction, data analysis) given the plethora of terminology used in both the academic literature and in public discourse (see Appendix E for a full list of terms). Regarding data analysis, the first author stratified the dataset by the ‘Focus’ data element, which mapped directly onto the research question: screening, assessment, treatment, and management. Subsequent steps ensued where the extracted results were reviewed, cross-referencing various data elements to organize the data in coherent themes. These are presented in the following section.

Results

Study characteristics

Study publication dates ranged from 1995 to 2024; most were published since 2010 (n = 32). Most studies (n = 39) were based in Europe, with the United Kingdom as the location where most of this research occurred (n = 28). The other European study jurisdictions were Germany, Sweden, Ireland, and the Netherlands. Some studies were conducted in North America (Canada – 4; United States – 2) and Australia (n = 4). Most of the studies focused on management (n = 36) and treatment (n = 32). Other foci included: screening and assessment; staff perceptions and attitudes; implementation; and prevalence. Notably, these categories are not mutually exclusive (i.e., many studies spanned multiple focus areas). Almost half of the studies (n = 22) named a specific forensic hospital where the research took place. Regarding general study design, most were quantitative (n = 36). Qualitative (n = 8) and mixed methods (n = 5) studies were also conducted. The most frequently employed specific study design was cross-sectional observational (n = 16). About half of the studies had sample sizes of less than 50 participants (n = 24) and about three quarters had less than 100 (n = 37). Studies with larger sample sizes were less common (n = 4 - ≥200; n = 2 - 150–199).

Study population characteristics

Most study participants were patients (n = 38), followed by staff (n = 8), and both patients and staff (n = 3). Regarding age, the studies were predominantly adult-focused (n = 35). Age was not reported in 11 studies, and youth-only samples were rare (n = 1). Over half of the studies had mixed sex/gender samples (n = 25), with nearly a third being male-only (n = 15). Female-only samples were rare (n = 1), and several studies did not report sex/gender. Most studies did not report the ethnicity of their sample. When reported, samples were comprised of various ethnicities (n = 10) or majority White/European (n = 8). The studies’ patient diagnoses were mostly categorized as ‘primarily psychotic disorders (± comorbidities)’ (n = 31). The most stated comorbidities were SUDs and personality disorders. In some cases, the diagnoses were not specified (n = 8) or did not apply due to staff-only studies (n = 6). The study institutions’ security-levels were: high (n = 16), medium (n = 15), low (n = 2), combination of low and medium (n = 8), and not specified (n = 8).

Thematic overview

Prevalence

SUD prevalence was estimated in eight observational studies. It was operationalized in three ways: (1) patient-level history and/or diagnosis using standardized assessments and/or medical chart review; (2) period prevalence via urine drug screen testing; and (3) staff perceptions captured via questionnaires/surveys.

A high burden of substance use and psychiatric comorbidity was a consistent finding across studies that quantified patient-level SUD prevalence and substance use history (Bloye et al., 2003; Cappai et al., 2017; Nicholls et al., 2011). Notably, these prevalence estimates varied by measurement approach (e.g., structured diagnostic interviews, clinician ratings/standardized scales, self-report screening tools, routine clinical assessments). Findings suggest that most patients report lifetime substance use, with cannabis often the most reported drug (67% in a medium-secure United Kingdom sample), alongside substance use patterns for other drugs exceeding community comparators (Bloye et al., 2003). Another study showed that patients with pre-admission substance dependence exhibited more in-hospital substance use paralleling behaviour and made more pro re nata medication requests compared to those without such a history (Schaefer et al., 2011). Prevalence estimates were high when screening instruments were used. For example, 89% of patients in a United Kingdom sample reported having a substance use history (Cappai et al., 2017), while 67% of men and 62% of women met criteria on a structured risk/treatability measure in a Canadian sample (Nicholls et al., 2011).

Across studies using urine drug screen testing to estimate in-hospital substance use, positivity rates were highly contingent on testing practice. For example, factors at play included whether testing was random versus risk-based, the consistency of implementation, and the interpretation of results (Kendrick et al., 2002, MacIntyre et al., 2004, Penney et al., 2021). One study suggested that when testing was inconsistently applied, substantial non-completion rates and irregular testing patterns likely underestimated the true substance use prevalence (Kendrick et al., 2002). In contrast, a study with a systematic testing protocol showed meaningful levels of positive screens (most commonly cannabis and alcohol), but cautioned that there were notable proportions of diluted samples which suggest tampering (Penney et al., 2021). Importantly, positivity rates can also be inflated by prescribed medications. In one study, false positive rates were driven largely by medication, while all true positives were the result of substance use prior to hospital admission (MacIntyre et al., 2004).

Staff perceptions of prevalence captured via questionnaires/surveys support substance use as common in secure inpatient forensic settings, even where precise patient-level estimates are not available. Durand et al. (2006) surveyed clinical staff, estimating that a mean of 65% of in-patients had a problem with substance use (range 15–100%), while a questionnaire study described substance use as a “major problem” (Whyte and Harrison, 2004). Staff reported that in-hospital drug use was primarily cannabis-driven, with high estimates of patients using the substance (McKeown and Liebling, 1995). These service-level perspectives indicate less precision regarding SUD prevalence, albeit still pointing toward high burden.

Treatment and management

Thirty-eight studies examined the treatment and/or management of substance use in inpatient forensic hospital settings, most commonly focusing on program characteristics and/or outcomes.

Among program-focused studies (n = 21; Appendix F), structured psychosocial and skills-based approaches were common, usually delivered in group formats, although sometimes alongside individual sessions. Key program elements included cognitive behavioural therapies (CBT), motivational interviewing, cognitive remediation therapy, motivational or stages-of-change approaches, and psychoeducation (Baker et al., 2014, Downsworth and Jones, 2014, Edwards et al., 2011, Glassmire et al., 2007, Long et al., 2010, Marcer et al., 2016, Miles et al., 2007, Miles, 2015, Milosevic et al., 2018, Morris and Moore, 2009, Oddie and Davies, 2009, Parsons et al., 2022, Ritchie et al., 2004, Ritchie et al., 2010, Ritchie et al., 2011, Tibber et al., 2015, Van Der Laan and Janssen, 1996). Programs typically combined multiple modalities and were commonly framed around relapse prevention. Several interventions were explicitly tailored to forensic inpatient contexts by integrating substance use with broader behavioural and risk management practices. These included values-based approaches, skills for managing emotions/urges, and aggression/behavioural regulation techniques (Johnson et al., 2019, Ritchie et al., 2011, Wettermann et al., 2020). Notably, one study employed a dual diagnosis CBT approach that explicitly addressed gender-specific issues related to trauma, mental health, index offence, and lifestyle (Long et al., 2010). Regarding program duration and frequency, the “dose” varied considerably. Some interventions were delivered approximately weekly in groups formats over several months (Miles, 2015, Miles et al., 2007), while others employed higher intensity schedules, for example two sessions per week over several months (Milosevic et al., 2018). Very few studies ran for long periods of time (e.g., 9–14 months).

Evidence on effectiveness was mixed and often limited by small samples and predominantly uncontrolled pre–post designs. Outcomes were mostly intermediate, for example readiness to change (Baker et al., 2014, Long et al., 2010), substance-related knowledge and skills acquisition (Milosevic et al., 2018, Ritchie et al., 2010, Tibber et al., 2015), and self-efficacy/confidence to resist substance use in high-risk situations (Long et al., 2010, Ritchie et al., 2004). Program acceptability and satisfaction were generally high (McFadden et al., 2020, Miles, 2015). Patient engagement was a central theme across various studies, often reporting descriptive results via program attendance and retention. Some interventions achieved reasonably strong engagement, as evidenced by high program attendance and satisfaction scores (Baker et al., 2014, McFadden et al., 2020, Miles, 2015). Qualitative studies emphasized peer support and the therapeutic alliance as central to engagement (Ritchie et al., 2011), particularly when treatment explicitly acknowledged the complexity of dual diagnosis in the forensic inpatient context (Krishnan and Ireland, 2025, Svensson et al., 2024).

The findings were mixed where objective substance use-specific outcomes were available. Some studies reported abstinence among completers, encouraging healthier long-term substance use patterns (Miles et al., 2007, Miles, 2015, Van Der Laan and Janssen, 1996), whereas others found that gains in knowledge and perceived relapse prevention skills did not translate to reduced substance use (Milosevic et al., 2018). Qualitative studies also reinforced perceived benefits (e.g., increased awareness, social and coping strategies, values-based goal setting, positive impact of group work), while highlighting heterogeneity in patient interpretations and intent to change (Edwards et al., 2011, Johnson et al., 2019, Morris and Moore, 2009, Ritchie et al., 2011). Comparative evidence was rare. One study comparing the Reasoning and Rehabilitation Program to a Dialectical Behavioural Therapy group found similar improvements on cognitive outcomes, with minimal differences between the approaches (Wettermann et al., 2020). Although not quite established programs, two studies explored neurofeedback therapy (Fielenbach et al., 2018, Fielenbach et al., 2019). These studies suggested potential positive measurable effects on targeted neurophysiological markers (e.g., drug craving and motor impulsivity), although the broader clinical implications for substance use management remain underdeveloped (Fielenbach et al., 2018, Fielenbach et al., 2019).

Screening and assessment

Nineteen studies focused on screening and/or assessment of substance use in inpatient forensic hospital settings, with emphasis on clinical utility, service-level practice, and the psychometric properties of relevant tools.

Overall, the literature spans brief screeners and dependency measures (Cappai et al., 2017, Ford, 2003), personality- and trait-based measures (Berlin et al., 2023, Lincourt et al., 2020), and structured instruments for characterizing severity, knowledge, confidence, self-esteem, motivation, and relapse prevention skills (Milosevic et al., 2018, Ritchie et al., 2004, Tibber et al., 2015). In one study drawing on a sample of patients primarily with SUD, assessment scores via the Psychopathy Checklist – Revised did not differentiate between completers and non-completers of substance use treatment (Berger et al., 2012).

Substance use detection was also embedded within broader risk assessment tools and service-level practice. For example, the Short-Term Assessment of Risk and Treatability (START) showed strong interrater reliability and, in some studies, predictive validity, but substance use-specific prediction was not consistently supported (Desmarais et al., 2012, Nicholls et al., 2011, O'Shea and Dickens, 2015). At the service level, basic unit surveys show widespread substance use, but variable service provision across settings (Durand et al., 2006, Sandbrook et al., 2015). Furthermore, implementation work emphasized the practical challenges of embedding standardized substance use-specific assessment into routine forensic hospital care (Green et al., 2023). As noted previously, routine urine drug screening was an important clinical practice, although detection and interpretation was impacted by testing coverage, setting, and patient adherence (Kendrick et al., 2002, MacIntyre et al., 2004, Penney et al., 2021, Van Der Laan and Janssen, 1996).

Staff perspectives and attitudes

Thirteen studies examined staff perspectives and/or attitudes toward substance use, patients, and related service provision in inpatient forensic hospital settings, with workforce capability gaps and therapeutic alliance issues emerging as central themes.

Even among qualified staff, substance use training was oftentimes described as inadequate (Barry et al., 2002, Sandbrook et al., 2015), alongside expressed sentiments of uncertainty about “best practice” in day-to-day clinical operations (Dolan and Kirwan, 2001). An earlier staff survey also documented knowledge deficits about substance use, making explicit calls for systematic and coordinated training with clearer guidance (McKeown and Liebling, 1995). Relatedly, a study drawing on a sample of forensic nurses showed their attitudes towards people who use drugs to be suboptimal, reinforcing the need for training that targets substance use knowledge (Foster and Onyeukwu, 2003).

At the service level, staff described heavy reliance on written policies and security procedures, but with variability in how substance use was perceived and the orientation of unit policies (i.e., abstinence versus harm minimization) (Durand et al., 2006). Workforce issues also reflected broader system constraints. For example, staff commonly perceived substance use as a “major problem” regarding its impact on patients’ mental disorders and/or response to treatment. Furthermore, they reported limited treatment options and weak linkages to community addictions services, narrowing what hospital clinicians can feasibly deliver (Whyte and Harrison, 2004). Qualitative work further highlights system-level barriers, including the concern that an “artificial abstinence environment” may limit opportunities for frontline staff to observe substance use improvement on the part of their patients (Krishnan and Ireland, 2025).

A persistent theme across this subset of studies is that substance use care in these specialized settings creates relational strain between patient and clinician whereby control-oriented practices overlap with therapeutic objectives. Staff described concerns that onsite substance use undermines mental health treatment and service operations, and many worried about negative impacts on the therapeutic alliance (Dolan and Kirwan, 2001, Querengässer et al., 2019). Some staff also viewed inpatient substance use as driven by “personal enjoyment” and perceived such drug use as a “form of antisocial behaviour,” which could complicate constructive clinical engagement (Dolan and Kirwan, 2001). Qualitative evidence suggested that intrusive practices, like strip searches, could further damage the therapeutic relationship (Price, Wibberley, 2012). Further evidence suggested some staff reported challenges reconciling their roles of clinician and “warden” (Green et al., 2023). Notably, stigma may also constitute an issue in this context. In one study, patients with histories of amphetamine or opiate use were more likely to be labelled “drug-seeking” (Kaur et al., 2009). In another study, therapists emphasized insufficient effort or motivation on the part of their patients regarding substance use treatment progression (Querengässer et al., 2019).

Discussion

This scoping review maps the literature on the screening, assessment, treatment, and management of SUD in inpatient forensic hospital settings. We reviewed 49 articles and summarized the results into the following themes: prevalence; treatment and management; screening and assessment; and staff perspectives and attitudes. Our findings indicate high prevalence alongside detection challenges, mixed evidence on treatment outcomes, and relational strain in the therapeutic alliance.

Across the included studies, the estimated prevalence of SUD in forensic hospitals varied depending on how it was defined (see Appendix E for the diverse substance use terminology in the included papers) and assessed (e.g., chart diagnosis, structured tools, urine drug screening, staff estimates). This heterogeneity complicates our understanding of the magnitude of the issue. This contributes to under-detection in these clinically complex settings where response bias, obscure symptomatology, and psychometric limitations simultaneously complicate identification. This underdiagnosis presents a serious concern in forensic mental health. If SUD is not accurately detected, it is less likely to be considered a criminogenic need, and integrated into risk assessment and treatment planning. Such underdiagnosis has been clearly shown in a Canadian sample: of 491 forensic inpatients with a major mental illness, 61% met diagnostic criteria for a co-occurring SUD, yet only 19% were formally diagnosed with both conditions (Hilton et al., 2018). Of particular concern in the forensic context is that of cannabis, which has been shown to exacerbate symptoms among individuals with or at high risk of psychotic disorders (Foti et al., 2010, Grech et al., 2005). While most inpatient forensic hospital settings require complete abstinence from substances, select anecdotes in the North American context suggest substance use is common in lower-security settings, especially those where patients have temporary community release privileges. Notably, substance use among forensic patients is also associated with worse illness trajectories and challenges with community reintegration because of poor medication adherence and high rates of hospital readmissions (Penney, Marshall, and Simpson, 2018), which are compounded by a lack of access to social supports. In addition to current substance use, histories of use appear to be associated with patient profiles and illness trajectories relevant for clinical management in forensic mental health, supporting detection as essential (Schaefer et al., 2011).

A considerable gap in this literature is a lack of substance-specific data reporting. For example, population-level research in Ontario, Canada (Chaimowitz et al., 2022) and among Missouri insanity acquittees in the United States (Linhorst, 1997) shows a high burden of SUD, but does not disaggregate by substance type, such as alcohol, cannabis, stimulants, or opioids, each of which carries certain potential clinical and criminogenic implications. This limitation hinders efforts to tailor interventions, train clinical staff, and allocate resources effectively. We recommend future research employ standardized SUD categories aligned with DSM−5 and/or ICD−11 classifications and routinely report substance-specific patterns. Our research group is conducting such research at the institutional level in Ontario, Canada.

The treatment literature we reviewed was dominated by studies evaluating structured, group-based psychosocial programs, which were sometimes paired with individual sessions. These interventions were generally acceptable and often improved intermediary outcomes such as readiness to change, knowledge/skill acquisition, and self-efficacy, although effects on substance use-specific outcomes were mixed. Where such indicators were reported, the overall evidence base was constrained by small samples and mostly uncontrolled pre–post study designs. This evidence mirrors a recent review focused specifically on structured group psychosocial SUD interventions for forensic inpatients with dual diagnosis, highlighting limited effectiveness (Krishnan and Ireland, 2025). Importantly, since our review was much broader in scope, we suggest that intervention studies represent just one aspect of a much larger, fragmented forensic mental health landscape.

Our findings also extend those of Marquant et al. (2021), who conducted a systematic review exploring how SUD is treated in Forensic Assertive Community Treatment (FACT) and how it relates to key outcome measures. Their work found that SUD was highly prevalent among patients receiving FACT, that SUD severity at intake was key in determining whether patients entered FACT directly from prison or via a care continuum, and that SUD was associated with outcomes such as recidivism and hospitalization. While Marquant et al. (2021) focused solely on community-based forensic treatment and the role of SUD in FACT pathway decision-making, our review builds upon this work by mapping how SUD is identified, treated, and managed within inpatient forensic hospital settings. Taken together, these reviews suggest that SUD is not only a post-discharge or community reintegration consideration, but a clinical, criminogenic, and system-level issue requiring standardized detection, integrated treatment, and coordinated transitions between hospital and community services.

An important implication of our findings is the urgency to move beyond a “one-size-fits-all” programming approach toward a more individualized patient-centered model. Theoretically, such an approach would match program content and intensity (i.e., stepped care) to patient readiness, cognitive capacity, and unique substance use symptomatology. The dominance of group-based psychosocial models in the forensic literature stands in contrast with the wider dual diagnosis literature. This evidence base supports the use of various approaches, including group counseling, contingency management, and residential dual diagnosis treatment, showing positive effects on substance use outcomes (Drake et al., 2008). Taken together, the available evidence suggests that forensic hospitals may be drawing, if at all, on a fairly narrow subset of interventions with demonstrated utility in other settings. In the absence of forensic-specific trials with reasonably long-term follow-ups, it will remain unclear which psychosocial approaches are most effective in these highly complex clinical settings. Furthermore, given the lack of longitudinal studies in this literature, it is unclear the proportion of forensic inpatients who return to problematic substance use post-discharge. Future research using large-scale administrative datasets should be conducted to study this phenomenon.

A fruitful avenue for future research centres upon mixed methods evaluations, linking health outcomes to program features and hospital contexts. Regarding the latter, our findings on the therapeutic alliance indicate strained relationships and potentially stigmatizing attitudes on the part of clinicians (Dolan and Kirwan, 2001, Green et al., 2023, Kaur et al., 2009, Price and Wibberley, 2012, Querengässer et al., 2019). This begs the critical question as to whether such perspectives have a deleterious impact of the quality of services provided. Qualitative research on the patient-provider relationship would begin answering some of these questions. Additionally, research employing patient-oriented designs, whereby patients are meaningfully involved in the research process, could yield critical information relevant for improving forensic hospital services (Canning et al., 2025).

There are limitations to our study. Firstly, we excluded studies published in languages other than English given the linguistic limitations of our research team and a lack of resources for hiring translators. This limitation may help explain the narrow geographical coverage of the included studies (i.e., the European over-representation); it is possible that relevant, non-English studies were excluded. For example, there likely exists a robust literature on our topic in Germany, whereby offenders with SUDs related to their index offenses can be involuntarily admitted to forensic hospitals under Section 64 of the German Penal Code (Schalast et al., 2011). Secondly, our eligibility criteria may have excluded important insights from the grey literature (e.g., clinical guidelines). We recognize that such excluded literature could have added value to our findings. Thirdly, while we employed a broad definition of ‘substance use,’ it is possible that some terms across the literature were omitted that may have been relevant. Fourthly, although scoping reviews are suitable for providing a broad overview of literature and are useful for identifying research gaps, they do not provide a depth of information on a given topic. Finally, we did not conduct a quality assessment of the included studies, in alignment with scoping review guidelines (Arksey and O’Malley, 2005; Tricco et al., 2018).

Conclusion

This scoping review mapped what is known about the screening, assessment, treatment, and management of SUDs in inpatient forensic hospital settings. Across 49 included studies, SUD burden was high, but the field lacks a consistent measurement and care framework. Prevalence estimates, and by extension the perceived magnitude of the issue, varied substantially depending on how substance use was defined and assessed. This emphasizes that under-detection is a concern in hospital-based forensic care.

The evidence base on treatment and management demonstrate that interventions were generally acceptable and frequently improved intermediary outcomes; however, the effects on substance use-specific outcomes were mixed. Concurrently, studies described service fragmentation, workforce training gaps, and security-related issues, limiting implementation and placing strain on the therapeutic alliance. Negative impacts on the patient-provider relationship is an issue with serious consequences in these complex settings where engagement, trust, and safety must be balanced.

Altogether, this literature indicates that forensic hospitals are operating within a complex, high-needs environment that employs a narrow and inconsistently evaluated set of approaches. Future research must prioritize: (1) standardized SUD identification aligned with DSM−5 and/or ICD−11 classifications; (2) mixed methods evaluations of inpatient services; and (3) implementation strategies that could improve workforce training and lessen relational strain in the therapeutic alliance. Undertaking this work could support more robust SUD detection processes, higher-quality treatment, improved health and quality-of-life outcomes, and reduced recidivism among forensic psychiatric populations. Our work establishes a foundational knowledge base essential for improving SUD forensic hospital services internationally. This scoping review serves as a resource to build upon for future scholarly work, for health system champions interested in improving forensic services at their organizations, and for policymakers inspired to contribute to forensic mental health system reform.
