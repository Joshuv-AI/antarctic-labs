 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 NLR shall develop a model of an electric thermal energy storage (ETES) system in the System
 Advisor Model (SAM) framework that uses a salt and particle slurry developed by PowderMet as
 the thermal energy storage medium. The ETES shall consist of a recuperated sCO 2 Brayton cycle
 using 2-tank indirect thermal energy storage and an electric heater for heat input. The salt slurry
 uses chloride salt and operates at a maximum temperature of 500-720°C. The ETES uses dry air
 cooling. The salt slurry will be modeled as a sensible heat storage medium and as a latent heat
 storage medium. NLR will use the model to do parametric analysis of the factors affecting the
 performance (ex., round trip efficiency) of the ETES system. NLR will work with PowderMet to
 estimate the costs of system components and total system costs, with the levelized cost of
 electricity (LCOE) as the primary cost metric.

 Background
 Latent heat TES is a technology used to store thermal energy by exploiting the latent heat of
 phase change materials (PCMs). Phase change materials are substances that absorb or release
 heat during the process of melting or solidifying, respectively, at a constant temperature. This
 characteristic makes PCMs ideal for TES applications, as they can store and release large
 amounts of thermal energy at constant temperatures, providing a reliable and efficient source of
 energy for a range of applications.

 Latent heat TES is a key technology in the field of renewable energy, as it enables the storage of
 excess energy generated by renewable sources such as wind and solar power, which can then be
 used to provide electricity or heating when the demand is high. In addition, the technology is also
 used in a range of other applications, such as in buildings, vehicles, and industrial processes,
 where it can be used to provide heating and cooling or to store waste heat for later use.

 The development of latent heat TES technology has been driven by a range of factors, including
 the need to reduce carbon emissions, the increasing demand for reliable and efficient energy
 storage solutions, and the rising cost of traditional energy sources. As a result, the technology has
 undergone significant advancements in recent years, with the development of new PCMs,
 improved thermal storage systems, and the integration of the technology into a range of different
 applications.

 Figure 1. An electric thermal energy storage (ETES) system using a resistive heat element.

 7

 One application of PCM-TES is the use within an ETES system as the storage medium (shown in
 Figure 1). ETES is a type of energy storage system that uses electricity to convert thermal energy
 into a storable form, i.e., increase a storage medium temperature. ETES systems can be used to
 store excess electricity generated by renewable energy sources, such as solar and wind power,
 and release it when needed to provide energy to the grid.

 ETES systems typically use resistive heating elements or heat pumps to convert electricity into
 thermal energy, which is then stored in a thermal energy storage medium, such as molten salt or
 solid particles. The stored thermal energy can be used to generate electricity on demand by
 running it through a heat engine, such as a Rankine cycle or a supercritical carbon dioxide cycle.

 The main advantage of ETES systems is their ability to store large amounts of energy in a
 compact and relatively inexpensive form. Unlike electrochemical batteries, which can be
 expensive as storage capacity increases, ETES systems can be designed to store greater than 5
 hours of discharge capacity, making them ideal for storing excess electricity from intermittent
 renewable energy sources.

 Goals and Objectives

 The goal of the project was to determine the cost savings from using PCM for TES in the ETES.
 The higher energy density of the salt slurry compared to the salt should result in smaller TES
 tank dimensions and therefore lower costs. NREL, through a Small Business Innovation
 Research (SBIR) VELOCTI voucher, was tasked with developing a model of an ETES system
 using the PowderMet Inc. particle/salt slurry as the storage medium in the System Advisor
 Model (SAM) framework. NREL was further tasked with using the model to do parametric
 analysis on the system to determine a range of system costs in terms of LCOE. PowderMet Inc.
 specified an ETES plant configuration of a 10 MWe net power output with 10 hours of thermal
 energy storage capacity. They further specified a 3rd generation molten chloride salt to be the
 heat transfer fluid (HTF) and for a supercritical carbon dioxide power cycle to be used to
 generate power.

 1.1 Modeling Approach
 For this work, PowderMet Inc. was interested in understanding the performance and financial
 viability of an ETES system using a novel molten salt slurry with PCM particles suspended
 within the salt. Additionally, this work focused on an ETES system that uses a supercritical
 carbon dioxide (sCO2) power cycle with a capacity of 10 MWe.

 8

 To accomplish this work, we employ NREL’s System Advisor Model (SAM) new ETES
 modeling capability to evaluate the impact of introducing PCM particles into the 3rd generation
 molten salts (i.e., 45.98% MgCl2, 38.91% KCl, 15.11% NaCl). However, to complete this work,
 we were required to modify SAM’s ETES model to (i) capture the impact of latent heat behavior
 within a TES medium and (ii) enable the use of sCO2 power cycle predicted performance from
 an external Python model.

 1.2 PCMs Latent Heat
 The thermodynamic modeling within SAM’s ETES model uses energy balances to evaluate heat
 and power flows into and out of the system over time. Specifically, the model uses the
 approximate form for the change of enthalpy, i.e., the product of specific heat and change in
 temperature. Before this project, SAM’s ETES model evaluated specific heat at the midpoint of a
 temperature range to characterize energy flows. The midpoint temperature specific heat
 approximation is appropriate for fluids with a linear relationship between specific heat and
 temperature, i.e., molten salts. However, this approach breaks down when the relationship
 between specific heat and temperature is non-linear, i.e., phase change occurs or large
 temperature changes. To address this, we updated the ETES model to evaluate the average
 specific heat using numerical integration over the process temperature range, equation (1).

 1.3 sCO2 Cycle
 For this work, the ETES system used a sCO2 power cycle as the discharge heat engine. Due to
 the immaturity of sCO2 power cycles, SAM does not provide a default model to estimate sCO2
 cycle performance for annual simulations, i.e., of the ETES model. Instead, SAM provides a
 standardized format for importing a user-defined power cycle performance as a function of heat
 input, hot TES temperature, and ambient temperature. Previous work at NREL developed a
 design point and off-design performance models for sCO2 power cycles (Neises et al. 2014,
 Neises et al. 2019), which can be called from Python. Additionally, previous work created an
 sCO2 cycle integration macro within SAM that enables users to import results from the Python
 sCO2 cycle model directly into SAM’s user-defined power cycle. Before this work, this macro
 was only available within SAM’s molten salt power tower concentrating solar power model. As
 a part of this work, we enabled the sCO2 cycle integration macro to be used for SAM’s ETES
 model. This allows us to predict the sCO2 cycle performance using various PCM salt slurries and
 import that performance directly into SAM to estimate the system’s annual performance.

 9

 Currently, there are three major configurations of sCO2 power cycles that are actively being
 researched: (i) simple, (ii) recompression, and (iii) partial cooling, shown in Figure 2 (Neises et
 al. 2019).

 Figure 2. sCO2 cycle configurations

 The simple cycle consists of a turbine (T), a main compressor (MC), a primary heat exchanger
 (PHX), a low-temperature recuperator (LTR), and a cooler. The recuperator transfers heat from
 the turbine exhaust to the high-pressure cold compressor outlet. The heat transfer fluid adds heat
 to the cycle through the PHX. In the recompression configuration, a recompressor (RC) bypasses
 some flow around the cooler, MC, and LTR before mixing with the mainstream after the LTR.
 Additionally, this configuration contains a second recuperator that handles the high-temperature
 recuperation (HTR). This configuration aims to improve on the simple cycle efficiency by more
 effective recuperation in the LTR. In the partial cooling sCO2 cycle configuration, a second
 cooler and first-stage compressor are added after the LTR and before the recompression flow
 spilt. This configuration typically operates over higher pressure ratios and larger temperatures
 difference across the turbine than recompression. For this work, we use the simple cycle

 1.4 Primary Heat Exchanger
 Within the ETES system, the PHX refers to the heat exchanger where the HTF provides heat to
 the sCO2 working fluid of the cycle. The introduction of PCMs within the HTF will impact PHX
 design and performance. Here we discuss our modeling methodology of the PHX, which allows
 us to capture the impact of proposed PCM particles increasing the heat capacity of the HTF
 driving the sCO2 cycle.

 The PHX model assumes a counterflow configuration and uses a sub-heat exchanger modeling
 approach. In this approach, the PHX is discretized into smaller heat exchangers, heat transfer is
 assumed to be constant for each sub-heat exchanger (i.e., total required heat transfer divided by
 the number of sub-heat exchangers), and NTU-effectiveness relationships (conductance ratio
 method) are used to estimate the required heat exchanger conductance (Nellis et al. 2008). By
 discretizing the PHX, this method can capture rapidly changing fluid properties, e.g., phase
 change, as they progress through the heat exchanger. For this work, we discretized the PHX into
 100 sub-heat exchangers to ensure that PCMs latent heat properties were captured during the
 PHX design and performance calculations. For more information about the NTU-effectiveness
 method, please refer to either Nellis et al. (2008) or Bergman et al. (2018).

 1.5 PCM Salt Slurry Properties
 For this work, we assumed the PCM particles were suspended within a salt slurry where the salt
 was assumed to be the 3rd generation molten salts (i.e., 45.98% MgCl2, 38.91% KCl, 15.11%

 1.6 HTF Hot Temperature
 For this work, we varied HTF hot temperature for all PCM salt slurry cases to understand the
 importance of HTF hot temperature on PHX design and cycle cost. HTF hot temperature was
 parameterized between 695°C and 755°C using 5°C increments. During some of the PCM cases,
 specific HTF hot temperatures would result in temperature crossover within the PHX model,
 which would result in the model failing to solve. This modeling failure could be overcome by
 increasing either the hot, cold, or both PHX approach temperatures until the temperature
 crossover no longer occurs. However, for a given HTF hot temperature, increasing the PHX hot
 approach temperature results in a lower sCO2 high temperature, which reduces cycle design
 efficiency. Conversely, increasing the PHX cold approach temperature results in a decreased
 HTF temperature difference which increases required HTF mass flowrate and decreases TES
 energy density. In a real heat exchanger, this crossover event would result in the CO 2 and HTF
 temperature difference decreasing until no heat transfer occurs between the two fluids. This
 would be a poorly designed PHX because the heat exchange surface area is effectively wasted.
 Therefore, for the above reasons, the failed cases are omitted in the results.

 For the HTF hot temperature parameterization, the sCO2 cycle assumptions remained constant
 across the different HTF properties and assumed HTF hot temperatures. As a result, the sCO 2
 simple cycle thermal efficiency was consistent for all HTF properties at a given HTF hot
 temperature, shown. The sCO2 hot temperature is set based on the assumed HTF hot temperature
 and the 20°C PHX hot approach temperature assumption, i.e., the sCO2 PHX outlet temperature
 is set to the HTF hot temperature less the PHX hot approach temperature.

 2 Results
 2.1 Primary Heat Exchanger
 Figure 5 presents the estimated PHX conductance as a function of HTF hot temperature for each
 PCM salt slurry case. Based on these results, there exists an HTF hot temperature that minimizes
 the PHX conductance for each PCM salt slurry. For all cases except for the 3-tier PCM, an HTF
 hot temperature of 715°C minimizes the PHX conductance, which corresponds to the high
 temperature of the PCM 1 phase change range. For the 3-tier PCM, an HTF hot temperature of
 740°C minimizes the PHX conductance. From equation (2), PHX costs scale linearly with
 conductance. For all cases within this study, the cost of sCO2 cycle components downstream of
 the PHX does not change for a given HTF hot temperature. Therefore, PHX conductance directly
 impacts the total sCO2 cycle cost, shown in Figure 6.

 The decrease in PHX conductance is driven by two factors; (i) an increase in temperature
 differences between the two fluids that occurs during phase change of the PCMs and (ii) a
 decrease in capacitance ratio between the two fluids because of high heat capacity of the PCM
 salt slurry during phase change and low salt slurry mass flowrate when phase change is not
 occurring. Figure 7 and Figure 8 presents an estimate of the temperature difference between the
 HTF and CO2 working fluid within the PHX as a function of PHX duty (i.e., cumulative heat
 transfer).

 Figure 7 presents the duty-temperature profile for the 3-tier PCM case where the HTF hot
 temperature is 740°C. Due to the sCO2 PHX temperature increase being set by the turbine
 exhaust temperature and the LTR design, the PHX HTF temperature difference is fixed for a
 given HTF hot temperature. For an HTF hot temperature of 740°C, the HTF temperature
 difference is approximately 214°C meaning that the HTF exits the PHX at 526°C. At this exit
 temperature, PCM 3 has entered the phase change region but only utilizes 13% of the assumed
 temperature range of phase change. If the HTF hot temperature decreases, the PHX pinch-point
 temperatures, occurring before the PCM 2 and PCM 1 phase change region, become a challenge.

 approaches 20°C (the assumed inlet and outlet approach temperature) as the HTF hot
 temperature decreases from 745°C to 715°C. A minimum temperature difference of less than
 20°C indicates that the PHX pinch temperature occurs internal to the PHX and not at either end
 of the PHX. The 2-tier 50/50 PCM case has the greatest sensitivity for HTF hot temperatures
 below 715°C. This is a result of the less latent heat energy during the PCM 1 phase change,
 which causes the HTF temperature to approach the sCO2 temperature before the phase change of
 PCM 2.

 2.3 SAM Analysis
 We used SAM’s ETES system model to determine the annual performance for each HTF case.
 To initialize the ETES model, we had to provide SAM the following information which is
 dependent on the HTF case:

 1. HTF fluid properties table as a function of temperature.
 2. sCO2 cycle performance as predicted by the Python design and off-design model.
 3. sCO2 cycle and TES costs.

 All other parameter values were held constant at their default value for each HTF case. The
 internal rate of return (IRR) estimate is based on an assumed power purchase agreement of
 $75/MWh with a 1% escalation and SAM default time-of-delivery hourly factors.

 This analysis was conducted with the energy arbitrage dispatch optimization model enabled. This
 dispatch approach targets low prices for charging and high prices for discharge to maximize
 system revenue subject to system operational constraints. This approach ensures that the ETES
 system operates only when it is profitable; however, the ETES predicted operations and IRR are
 specific to the time-of-delivery hourly factors used in this study.

 3 Conclusions
 Within this work, we presented a methodology for modeling latent heat of PCMs within SAM’s
 ETES system model; past work focused on sCO2 cycle modeling, including HTF-to-sCO2 PHX
 modeling, and novel PCM properties to be suspended within 3rd generation molten chloride salt.
 Additionally, we presented results from a sensitivity study looking at different HTF
 compositions and assumed HTF hot temperature. NREL provided PowderMet, Inc. with an
 executable file of SAM that includes the ETES model developed in this project.

 Based on our analysis, PCMs within a salt slurry can increase TES energy density from around
 200 kJ/kg to over 500 kJ/kg using salt slurries containing 36% by weight PCM. The higher
 energy density of the slurry leads to decreased TES tank sizes and costs. The salt slurries also
 increase the HTF-sCO2 temperature difference within the PHX, which decreases required PHX
 conductance and hence cost. The bare erected cost for systems with salt slurries was found to be
 up to 40% cheaper than salt-only systems. Finally, using salt slurries with PCM can help
 maintain high HTF temperatures for the power cycle, increasing cycle efficiency. Within the
 results, we showed that the 1-tier PCM case presents the most favorable results; however, there
 are still many unanswered questions.

 To further improve this analysis, the following items should be addressed in future work:

 1. Understand the engineering implications of adding PCM particles into a molten salt
 slurry, including but not limited to, density increase, flow characteristics, charging
 system design considerations, cost of the PCM material, and TES piping design.
 2. Conduct detailed PHX design using higher fidelity modeling methods, i.e., computational
 fluid dynamics and finite element analysis.
 3. Improve PHX cost estimates through a bottom-up cost analysis.
 4. Determine PHX performance experimentally (physical design and performance).
