Prevalence of Kinesiophobia and Associated Factors Among Patients After Percutaneous Coronary Intervention: A Multicenter Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Chunyuan Wang, Xiaoqin Hou, Pian Zhang, Caiyang Liu, Yi Yang, Zhenhua Zhao, Weijun Jiang, Guanfu Su, Xiaofang Jiang, Lijuan Lin, Libing Ning, Lixia Lin, Lechang Zhan, Jie Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo investigate the prevalence and associated factors of kinesiophobia after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).DesignA multicenter cross‐sectional study.MethodsWe collected data of post‐PCI patients who were recruited via convenience sampling from cardiology departments of eight tertiary hospitals between 1 November 2022 and 31 December 2023. Kinesiophobia was assessed by the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia Heart (TSK‐SV Heart). Exercise self‐efficacy, depression, anxiety, stress, and social support were evaluated by validated instruments. Participants' demographic and objective socioeconomic factors were also collected. Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify the associated factors of kinesiophobia.ResultsOf 1145 recruited patients, 970 (84.72%) provided complete data and were included in the final analysis, of which 780 (80.41%) exhibited kinesiophobia. Factors significantly associated with kinesiophobia in the binary logistic regression analysis included: single status (vs. married: OR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.22–0.79, p = 0.008), retired (vs. freelancers: OR = 1.89, 95% CI: 1.22–2.93, p = 0.004), had beyond 3 children (vs. 1 child: OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.46–0.99, p = 0.045), generally familiar with illness (vs. not familiar: OR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.37–2.93, p < 0.001), comorbid hypertension (vs. no hypertension: OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.05–2.13, p = 0.028), participated in cardiac rehabilitation (vs. did not participate: OR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.06–2.19, p = 0.023), moderate physical activity (vs. low: OR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.42–0.97, p = 0.034), and high physical activity (vs. low: OR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.25–0.65, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with kinesiophobia occurrence (p < 0.05). For each one‐point increase in stress, anxiety, and objective support, the odds of kinesiophobia increased by 9% (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.02–1.15, p = 0.008), 21% (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.12–1.29, p < 0.001), and 9% (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.01–1.19, p = 0.027), respectively. For each one‐point increase in depression and SEE‐C score, the odds of kinesiophobia decreased by 11% (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.84–0.96, p = 0.001) and 1% (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–0.99, p < 0.001), respectively.ConclusionKinesiophobia is highly prevalent among post‐PCI patients. Identified associated factors include marital status, occupational status, number of children, illness familiarity, hypertension comorbidity, cardiac rehabilitation participation, physical activity level, levels of psychological distress, objective support, and exercise self‐efficacy. These findings highlight potential targets for intervention.Implications for the Profession and Patient CareNurses should integrate routine kinesiophobia screening using the TSK‐SV Heart scale into post‐PCI assessment protocols. Risk‐stratified screening should prioritize married or retired patients, those with fewer than three children, individuals with hypertension, and those with low baseline physical activity. For patients with low exercise self‐efficacy, nurses can use motivational interviewing; for those with elevated anxiety or stress, collaborative cognitive‐behavioural therapy is recommended. These nursing actions can reduce kinesiophobia and improve cardiac rehabilitation adherence.Impact What problem did the study address? Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are the leading cause of global mortality, with a growing disease burden. In 2019, approximately 523 million individuals worldwide had CVD, doubling the prevalence reported in 1990 (Roth et al. 2020). By 2020, China accounted for 330 million CVD cases (23.3% of its population), including 11.39 million coronary heart disease (CHD) patients (The Writing Committee of the Report on Cardiovascular Health and Diseases in China 2023). CVD remains the top cause of death in both urban and rural China (The Writing Committee of the Report on Cardiovascular Health and Diseases in China 2023). Without strengthened interventions, the World Bank projects that acute myocardial infarction (AMI) cases in China will rise from 11 million in 2010 to 22.63 million by 2030 (Wang et al. 2012).

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has become a primary treatment for AMI and can promptly restore myocardial blood supply, improve myocardial ischemia and hypoxia, and reduce mortality. It is now widely used in clinical practice (Hoole and Bambrough 2020). The proportion of direct PCI in China increased from less than 30.60% before 2013 to 74.52% in 2022, yielding significant clinical benefits (National Center for Cardiovascular Disease 2023). Exercise rehabilitation is an effective strategy for post‐PCI recovery, significantly reducing mortality, rehospitalization rates, and cardiac dysfunction (Suaya et al. 2009; Achttien et al. 2013). Clinical guidelines consistently recommend participation in exercise rehabilitation for post‐PCI patients, confirming its safety and reliability (Levine et al. 2011, 2016).

However, many patients with CHD often fear that physical activity may cause chest distress, dizziness, palpitations, or other discomfort. They question the safety of physical activity, leading to kinesiophobia. Consequently, they avoid exercise and are unwilling to participate in rehabilitation programs, which diminishes physical activity engagement, leads to functional decline, and impairs quality of life. While studies have examined kinesiophobia in other cardiac populations (Dąbek et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2020), data on its prevalence and associated factors specifically among post‐PCI patients, particularly from multicenter studies, are limited. Understanding kinesiophobia in this population is critical for nurses to develop targeted rehabilitation assessments and interventions.

Nurses play a pivotal role in the early identification and management of kinesiophobia following PCI. As the healthcare professionals with the most frequent and sustained contact with patients throughout the care continuum, nurses have a distinct vantage point from which to observe the physical and emotional distress experienced by patients during routine clinical encounters (Mohammad et al. 2023). Nurse‐led interventions, providing information and support especially in relation to symptoms, have been shown to improve patients' physical function, activity levels, and health‐related quality of life, and help reduce adverse psychological sequelae (Perez‐Dominguez et al. 2021; Gray et al. 2013). Furthermore, cardiac nurses can deliver brief psychological support, such as cognitive‐behavioural techniques in relation to cardiac rehabilitation, which are effective in reducing anxiety and depression, improving adherence to cardiac rehabilitation, and reducing cardiovascular readmissions (Holdgaard et al. 2023). Despite these potential roles, current evidence on kinesiophobia in post‐PCI patients remains largely descriptive and limited to single‐centre studies with small sample sizes (Liu et al. 2020). Most existing research has focused on non‐modifiable demographic and clinical factors (e.g., age, sex, comorbidities), offering little guidance for nurse‐led interventions (Goba et al. 2018; Cui and Lei 2019). Importantly, the independent contributions of modifiable psychosocial factors, particularly exercise self‐efficacy and multidimensional social support, have not been systematically quantified in Chinese post‐PCI populations. Moreover, no study has translated these factors into a practical, risk‐stratified screening tool that nurses can readily use at the bedside to identify high‐risk patients and prioritize tailored interventions. This evidence gap directly impedes the development of targeted, nurse‐delivered care plans for Chinese patients after PCI.

Therefore, this multicenter study aims to: (a) determine kinesiophobia prevalence and identify the independent associations between modifiable psychosocial factors and kinesiophobia in Chinese post‐PCI patients; and (b) propose a practical, risk‐stratified screening strategy to help nurses quickly identify patients at elevated risk.

Background

Kinesiophobia is defined as an irrational and debilitating fear of physical activity or movement due to enhanced pain sensitivity caused by painful injury or injury (Kori et al. 1990). In patients with CHD, kinesiophobia significantly reduces cardiac rehabilitation participation rates (Bäck et al. 2016). This avoidance behaviour diminishes physical activity engagement, leading to functional decline and suboptimal rehabilitation outcomes (Rogerson et al. 2012; Knapik et al. 2019). Moreover, it exacerbates patients' self‐perceived burden and impairs quality of life (Sun 2019). Regarding prevalence, a 2020 study reported high kinesiophobia in 81% of hospitalized CVD patients (Dąbek et al. 2020), whereas a Chinese study focusing on post‐PCI patients reported only 20% prevalence, though limited by a small sample size (n = 275) (Liu et al. 2020). Crucially, large‐scale epidemiological data on kinesiophobia specifically in Chinese post‐PCI populations remain scarce. Given its detrimental impact on recovery, this phenomenon warrants greater clinical and research attention.

Clinical observation reveals that patients frequently decline prescribed exercises due to kinesiophobia, often misinterpreting benign symptoms (e.g., muscle soreness) as angina recurrence. Robust evidence confirms kinesiophobia is multifactorial, involving demographic traits, clinical profiles, physical activity levels, exercise self‐efficacy, self‐perceived burden, and social support (Huang et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2021). For instance, women with CHD exhibit higher kinesiophobia than men, while hypertension or stroke history also elevates risk (Goba et al. 2018; Cui and Lei 2019). However, these factors are still not completely clear in Chinese PCI populations.

Identifying modifiable factors of post‐PCI kinesiophobia and implementing early screening are fundamental steps toward effective prevention. As the most frequently accessed healthcare providers, nurses bear critical responsibilities in guiding post‐PCI patients' safe activity resumption and rehabilitation adherence. However, it remains unclear which modifiable psychosocial factors are independently associated with kinesiophobia, and no risk‐stratified screening strategy is currently available for nurses to identify high‐risk patients.

Methods

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangzhou First People's Hospital (Approval No. K‐2022‐140‐01) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki principles. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. Personally identifiable information was de‐identified to ensure anonymity. We stored all data in electronically encrypted databases with access restricted to principal investigators.

Study Design

This study adopted a multicenter cross‐sectional design and was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (von Elm et al. 2014).

Setting and Participants

Participants were recruited via convenience sampling from cardiology departments of eight geographically dispersed tertiary hospitals in Guangdong Province, China, between 1 November 2022 and 31 December 2023. Guangdong Province is located in southern China, with a permanent population of 127.06 million by the end of 2023, making it the largest economic province in China. The population, cultural customs, geographical environment and medical level of the regions of these eight hospitals participating in this study are typical representatives of different regions in Guangdong Province. In addition, the diagnosis and treatment ability of CVD in these eight hospitals is all above regional averages and the number of PCI cases ranks among the top in the local area. Therefore, the eight hospitals were selected as the source of the survey sample, which made the sample more representative and could widely represent the real situation of patients with kinesiophobia after PCI.

Based on pilot data showing 35% kinesiophobia prevalence (n = 150), we calculated the minimum sample size using the formula for proportion estimation in cross‐sectional studies (Figure 1), with Z = 1.96 (α = 0.05), p = 0.35, and ε set as 10% of p (0.035) to ensure relative precision; the initial estimate was 713. Accounting for 10% attrition, the final target sample size was 792.

Inclusion criteria: (a) diagnosis of coronary atherosclerotic heart disease per the World Health Organization or International Society of Cardiology criteria with successful PCI; (b) post‐PCI status (6–48 h); (c) age 18–80 years; (d) well‐consciousness, good communication skills, and able to complete questionnaires. Exclusion criteria: (a) severe communication barriers (aphasia, blindness, and cognitive impairment); (b) active major comorbidities, such as metastatic cancer, psychotic disorders, acute stroke; (c) mobility‐limiting conditions, such as paralysis, fracture, amputation, active deep vein thrombosis; (d) high‐risk cardiac conditions requiring absolute bed rest; (e) pregnancy/lactation; (f) bedridden status.

Data Collection and Measures

We developed an online survey questionnaire in Chinese via the SurveyStar (Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co. Ltd., Changsha, China). Nurse‐led data collection occurred during routine cardiac rehabilitation visits to minimize participant burden. Before filling out the questionnaire, the trained research nurses provided the participants with standardized verbal instructions about the questionnaire. Then, participants completed questionnaires independently using the online questionnaire in clinic rooms. If the participant needed explanation or clarification, the research nurses could be consulted at any time throughout the interview. The responses to the questionnaire were automatically collected by the SurveyStar for further data analyses. The pilot testing was carried out in 30 post‐PCI patients (excluded from main analysis) to test the feasibility and psychometric properties of the questionnaire.

Socio‐Demographic Information

Demographic and objective socioeconomic data were self‐reported by the participants, including their sex (male/female), age (years), marital status, number of children, smoking history, drinking history, education, family monthly income, occupation, and payment method. Marital status was set as married or single (including divorced and widowed). Family monthly income was set as low (< 5,000 RMB), moderate (5,000–10,000 RMB) and high (≥ 10,000 RMB). Education was set as primary school or under, middle school, and high school or above. Occupation was set as freelancers, worker, staff, farmers, or retirees. Payment method was set as new cooperative medical scheme (NCMS), urban residents basic health insurance (URBHI), or other.

Similarly, the disease‐related information was also self‐reported by the subjects, including hypertension history, diabetes history, level of understanding of illness, number of stents inserted, participation in cardiac rehabilitation (yes or no), physical activity level in the past week. The level of understanding of illness was set as not familiar, generally familiar, or very familiar. The physical activity level in the past week was set as low (exercise once per week), moderate (exercise 1–3 times per week), and high (exercise more than 3 times per week).

Level of Kinesiophobia

The TSK‐SV Heart (Bäck et al. 2012) was used to assess the level of kinesiophobia in post‐PCI patients. This scale consists of 4 dimensions including 17 items, namely hazard perception (4 items), motor fear (4 items), motor avoidance (5 items), and functional disorders (4 items). Each item is rated on a 4‐point Likert scale, with 1 score representing “strongly disagree” and 4 scores representing “strongly agree”, while items 4, 8, 12, and 16 are scored in reverse (Bäck et al. 2012). The sum of all 17 items yields the final composite score, which ranges from 17 to 68, with a higher score indicating a higher level of kinesiophobia. The mandarin version demonstrated excellent psychometric properties in Chinese cardiac populations, which is useful to assess the level of kinesiophobia in Chinese heart disease patients (Lei et al. 2019). We categorized PCI patients with a TSK‐SV Heart score ≥ 38 as having kinesiophobia. The cut‐off value (≥ 38) has been used in CVD patients with good reliability and validity (Vlaeyen et al. 1995). This scale enables nurses to identify patients requiring activity fear‐reduction interventions during rehabilitation planning. In present study, the Cronbach's alpha of TSK‐SV Heart was 0.806.

Exercise Self‐Efficacy

The Chinese version of exercise self‐efficacy scale (SEE‐C) (Lee et al. 2009) was used to assess the level of self‐efficacy in post‐PCI patients. The SEE‐C is translated from the English version of SEE by Lee et al., which consists of 9 items, with each item scoring 0–10 points (Lee et al. 2009). It reflects the level of patient's self‐efficacy by evaluating their confidence in overcoming barriers to exercise in different environments. The higher the total score of the scale, the stronger the patient's self‐efficacy for exercise. The SEE‐C is a psychometrically robust tool for evaluating the level of exercise self‐efficacy in Chinese CHD patients (Wong et al. 2020). This scale helps nurses to guide individualized exercise prescriptions based on the confidence thresholds. In this survey, the Cronbach's alpha of SEE‐C was 0.977.

Depression, Anxiety, and Stress

The depression, anxiety and stress scale‐21 (DASS‐21) was validated in clinical populations by Antony et al. (Antony et al. 1998). This scale includes 3 subscales of depression, anxiety, and stress, with a total of 21 items. Each item is scored from 0 to 3 (not at all =0, some of the time = 1, a good part of time = 2, and most of the time = 3). The sum of the 7 items for each subscale multiplied by 2 is the total score for that subscale, ranging from 0 to 42, with higher scores indicating greater depression, anxiety, or stress (Antony et al. 1998). The Mandarin version shows good metric equivalence, which is suitable for the use in Chinese population (Gong et al. 2010). DASS‐21 screening allows nurses to triage patients needing psychological support. In this study, the Cronbach's alpha of DASS‐21 was 0.958.

Social Support

The social support rating scale (SSRS) (Xiao 1994) was used to assess the level of social support in PCI patients. This scale consists of 3 subscales of subjective support (4 items), objective support (3 items), and support utilization (3 items) (Xiao 1994). The total score of this scale is the sum of the ten items. The total score ranges from 12 to 62 points and the higher the total score, the higher the level of social support (Xiao 1994). SSRS identifies social isolation risks requiring nurse‐led family engagement strategies. In present study, the internal consistency was suboptimal (α = 0.692) due to heterogeneous constructs.

Statistical Analyses

Data processing and analysis were performed using SPSSAU (version 26.0, https://www.spssau.com). Continuous variables were described using median and interquartile range (IQR: 1st quartile, 3rd quartile), while binary variables were described using frequency and percentage. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify the strength of observed associations. We used the Shapiro–Wilk test to examine the normal distribution of the data and Cronbach's alpha to evaluate the reliability of the scale. The chi‐square test and Mann–Whitney U test were used to compare patients with and without kinesiophobia after PCI in terms of socio‐demographic characteristics, exercise self‐efficacy, depression, anxiety, stress, and social support. We also conducted Spearman correlation analyses to test the relationship among the scores of DASS‐21, SEE‐C, SSRS, and TSK‐SV heart.

To identify factors associated with the presence of kinesiophobia in patients after PCI, univariate logistic regression analysis was first performed for each potential predictor. Variables with a p value < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were subsequently entered into a binary logistic regression model. A stepwise selection method (forward or backward) was applied to determine the final model. The dependent variable was the presence of kinesiophobia (yes vs. no), defined as a TSK‐SV Heart score ≥ 38. The independent variables initially entered into the binary logistic regression model included: marital status, education level, occupation, payment system, number of children, drinking history, level of understanding illness, hypertension comorbidity, number of stents, participation in cardiac rehabilitation or not, physical activity level in the past week, all DASS‐21 subscales (stress, anxiety, depression), SEE‐C score, objective support, and support utilization.

Multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance. Tolerance values > 0.1 (or VIF < 10) indicated no significant multicollinearity. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test. A non‐significant p value (> 0.05) indicated adequate model fit. The overall model significance was tested using the likelihood ratio test comparing the final model to the null model.

For the logistic regression models, regression coefficient estimates (β), standard errors (SE) of β, and corresponding p values were calculated. Results were presented as ORs with 95% CIs. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

From 1264 screened post‐PCI patients, 1205 met inclusion criteria; 13 declined participation. Of 1192 questionnaires distributed, 1145 (96.06%) were returned. After excluding 175 cases (15.28%) with > 30% missing data, 970 participants (84.72% of respondents) comprised the analytic sample (Figure 2). Prevalence of Kinesiophobia

Kinesiophobia (TSK‐SV Heart score ≥ 38) was identified in 780 post‐PCI participants (80.41%). Significant differences (p < 0.05) existed between PCI patients with and without kinesiophobia for marital status, education level, occupation, number of children, drinking history, level of understand illness, hypertension comorbidity, cardiac rehabilitation participation, and physical activity level in the past week. Clinically, PCI patients with kinesiophobia had a higher score of DASS‐21 than those without kinesiophobia (p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in the scores of the SEE‐C and the total score of the SSRS between PCI patients with and without kinesiophobia (p > 0.05), however, significant differences were found in the dimensions of objective support, support utilization, and subjective support (p < 0.05). Associations of Kinesiophobia With Modifiable Psychosocial Factors

Spearman correlation test revealed significant positive correlations between the TSK‐SV Heart score and DASS‐21 subscale scores for stress (r = 0.240, p < 0.01), anxiety (r = 0.306, p < 0.01), and depression (r = 0.205, p < 0.01). These three DASS‐21 subscales were highly inter‐correlated (r = 0.846–0.868, all p < 0.01). Nevertheless, collinearity diagnostics (Table S1) supported their simultaneous inclusion in the regression model. The total SSRS score (r = −0.159, p < 0.01), support utilization score (r = −0.211, p < 0.01), subjective support score (r = −0.157, p < 0.01), and SEE‐C score (r = −0.128, p < 0.01) were all negatively correlated with the TSK‐SV Heart score. However, the objective support score was not significantly correlated with the TSK‐SV Heart score in these post‐PCI patients (r = 0.009, p > 0.05). Details are presented in Table 2.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first multicenter cross‐sectional study specifically investigating kinesiophobia prevalence and associated factors in post‐PCI patients. Given the rapidly expanding global PCI population, delineating kinesiophobia risk profiles is critical for nurses to: (a) integrate evidence‐based screening into high‐yield care workflows (e.g., perioperative education), (b) develop precision multimodal interventions, and (c) optimize adherence to guideline‐directed cardiac rehabilitation, ultimately maximizing the long‐term therapeutic efficacy of PCI.

Prevalence of Kinesiophobia

This study found an 80.41% prevalence of kinesiophobia among post‐PCI patients in Guangdong Province, China. Notably, this substantially exceeds the 41.76% prevalence reported in young and middle‐aged CHD patients not undergoing PCI using the same assessment tool (Sun 2019). This discrepancy may be attributable to PCI‐specific procedural factors. PCI includes percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty (PTCA) with or without stent implantation. PTCA is performed by puncturing the femoral artery, radial artery or brachial artery. With the development of technology, most patients in China choose radial artery approach because it can reduce patient discomfort, shorten postoperative bed time, and reduce complication rates (Valgimigli et al. 2015; Section of Interventional Cardiology of Chinese Society of Cardiology of Chinese Medical Association 2016). However, all techniques involve postoperative pain (Section of Interventional Cardiology of Chinese Society of Cardiology of Chinese Medical Association 2016). This iatrogenic pain may aggravate the patient's fear and then induce fear of movement, resulting in the patient's unwillingness to move. Non‐PCI patients lack these invasive experiences, likely explaining their lower kinesiophobia prevalence. This underscores the need for nurses to proactively address procedure‐related pain and fear in post‐PCI kinesiophobia management.

Factors Affecting the Prevalence of Kinesiophobia

Our study identified marital status, occupation, and number of children as significant factors associated with kinesiophobia in post‐PCI patients. Unmarried (single/divorced/widowed) patients had significantly lower odds of kinesiophobia than married patients (OR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.22–0.79, p = 0.008), which aligns with previous research linking marital status to kinesiophobia severity in CHD (Cui and Lei 2019). Married individuals may experience greater psychological burden due to concerns about family responsibilities, potential income loss, and the impact of illness on their spouse and children, leading them to avoid physical activity and thereby reinforce fear of exercise. Regarding occupation, retired patients had an 89% higher odds of kinesiophobia compared with freelancers (OR = 1.89, 95% CI: 1.22–2.93, p = 0.004). While a previous study reported that employed individuals have higher kinesiophobia levels than unemployed individuals due to illness‐induced income loss anxiety (Brunetti et al. 2017), our finding specifically highlights retirees, who tend to be older, may have more comorbidities, and often hold stronger beliefs about cardiac fragility after PCI, potentially amplifying fear‐avoidance beliefs through increased rumination on bodily sensations. Additionally, patients with more than three children had significantly lower odds of kinesiophobia than those with one child (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.46–0.99, p = 0.045). This protective effect may be explained by greater emotional and practical support from a larger family network, stronger resilience developed through cumulative life experiences, or enhanced motivation to maintain functional independence for the sake of family. Taken together, these findings suggest that being married, retired, or having only one child are associated with increased kinesiophobia risk. Routine assessment of these sociodemographic factors may help identify high‐risk patients, and tailored interventions—such as family‐inclusive counselling for married patients, graded exercise programs for retired individuals, and leveraging family support for those with fewer children—are warranted to reduce kinesiophobia and promote active participation in cardiac rehabilitation.

Our analysis further revealed that patients with generally disease familiarity had higher kinesiophobia risk than those not familiar with the disease. Hypertension comorbidity also significantly increased kinesiophobia prevalence, aligning with previous study (Cui and Lei 2019). These findings extend existing evidence linking health literacy, professional health education, and education level to kinesiophobia severity (Knapik et al. 2019; Sun 2019; Wang et al. 2021; Brunetti et al. 2017). We posit that patients with partial disease knowledge may misinterpret CHD pathophysiology and PCI outcomes. Common misconceptions, such as “postoperative weakness prohibits exercise” or “activity elevates blood pressure and impairs cardiac recovery”, can aggravate the fear of exercise (Li et al. 2021). Paradoxically, incomplete understanding appears more detrimental than no understanding, as fragmented knowledge fosters inaccurate risk appraisals. These evidence strongly support implementing structured PCI rehabilitation education led by cardiac nurses to correct maladaptive beliefs at teachable moments (e.g., pre‐discharge, rehabilitation intake). Targeted correction may reduce kinesiophobia more effectively than general health education.

Contrary to existing evidence (Liu et al. 2020; Bäck et al. 2013), our study found PCI patients receiving cardiac rehabilitation had higher kinesiophobia rates than non‐participants. We speculate this relates to the compliance of cardiac rehabilitation. While exercise reduces cardiovascular risk and all‐cause mortality, patients' uncertainty about exercise safety during rehabilitation may trigger avoidance behaviours, which reduces the compliance of patients with cardiac rehabilitation. Discomfort during cardiac rehabilitation sessions (e.g., dyspnea, fatigue) may further reinforce movement‐related fear, paradoxically elevating kinesiophobia prevalence despite therapeutic intent. Conversely, patients with moderate or high physical activity levels in past week demonstrated significantly lower kinesiophobia, aligning with established negatively correlated between activity and kinesiophobia (Sun 2019; Dąbek et al. 2020). This likely reflects experience‐based safety awareness and physiological resilience, namely, active patients better understand exercise benefits/cardiac tolerances, and successfully build exercise self‐efficacy, reducing “burden to heart” misconceptions. For practice, nurses should educate on differentiating safe discomfort from warning symptoms, and leverage active patients as peer mentors to model confident movement.

Consistent with previous studies (Huang et al. 2021; Bäck et al. 2013; Farris et al. 2019; Knapik et al. 2020), higher levels of anxiety and stress were independently associated with an increased likelihood of kinesiophobia in post‐PCI patients. Specifically, each one‐point increase in the anxiety score increased the odds of kinesiophobia by 21% (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.12–1.29, p < 0.001), and each one‐point increase in the stress score increased the odds by 9% (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.02–1.15, p = 0.008). From a psychological perspective, anxiety and stress are core emotional components of kinesiophobia (Huang et al. 2021), and in some cases, fear may be mediated by these emotional states. The diagnosis of CHD and the experience of PCI can adversely affect patients' physical, psychological, and social well‐being, giving rise to anxiety and stress. In an effort to reduce perceived harm to themselves and their families, patients may consequently avoid physical activity to prevent disease recurrence. In contrast, a higher depression score was unexpectedly associated with a lower odds of kinesiophobia (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.84–0.96, p = 0.001). This counterintuitive result may be explained by several mechanisms. First, depression often involves psychomotor retardation, apathy, and reduced overall activity; such individuals may not experience strong fear of movement simply because they rarely engage in physical activities that could trigger fear. Second, in the post‐PCI context, patients with predominant depressive symptoms might focus more on mood‐related complaints rather than on cardiac‐specific fears, leading to an underestimation of kinesiophobia. Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of early alleviation of negative emotions to reduce the risk of kinesiophobia after PCI. Nurses should pay increased attention to anxiety, depression, and stress in post‐PCI patients, for example, by conducting routine screening using validated tools such as the GAD‐7 or DASS‐21.

Our study demonstrated that each one‐point increase in the SEE‐C score reduced the risk of kinesiophobia by 1% (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–0.99, p < 0.001), directly supporting the work of Liang et al. (Liang et al. 2022). Exercise self‐efficacy, as a critical cognitive regulator of activity behaviour, can affect individuals' perception of exercise beliefs, determine effort investment, and promote the transformation of patients' exercise motivation into specific activity behaviour (Chai et al. 2021). This evidence mandates several nursing practice innovations. For example, nurses should integrate self‐efficacy enhancement into all patient interactions by utilizing anatomical models or educational videos to demonstrate the cardioprotective benefits of exercise. In addition, exercise self‐efficacy should be measured as a nursing‐sensitive outcome to continuously monitor patients' level of confidence. For those with low self‐efficacy, nurses should implement timely, targeted interventions, such as guided mastery experiences, verbal persuasion, and peer success story videos, to enhance patients' sense of exercise self‐efficacy, thereby mitigating kinesiophobia risk. In contrast, higher objective support was unexpectedly associated with increased odds of kinesiophobia (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.01–1.19, p = 0.027). A possible explanation for this paradox is that overprotective care, although intended to be supportive, may inadvertently reinforce fear‐avoidance behaviours in patients. Alternatively, the possibility of reverse causality, in which kinesiophobia leads to increased support seeking, cannot be ruled out. Nevertheless, the protective effect of self‐efficacy was consistent and robust, highlighting its potential as a key target for intervention in post‐PCI patients.

Implications for Nursing Management

The high prevalence of kinesiophobia (80.41%) and the modifiable factors identified in this study have direct implications for cardiac nursing practice. Nurses should embed the culturally adapted TSK‐SV Heart scale into routine nursing assessment protocols for post‐PCI patients, for example within the World Health Organization PEN (Package of Essential Noncommunicable Disease Interventions) framework. Furthermore, risk‐stratified screening should be implemented, prioritizing married or retired patients, those with fewer than three children, individuals with hypertension, and those with low baseline physical activity. For patients with low exercise self‐efficacy, nurses can use motivational interviewing to reframe maladaptive beliefs about physical activity. For those with elevated anxiety or stress, nurses should facilitate collaborative cognitive‐behavioural therapy (CBT), either delivered by trained cardiac nurses or in partnership with psychologists, to address psychological barriers to exercise. Additionally, nurses should proactively address kinesiophobia‐related safety concerns through pre‐referral education sessions that correct misconceptions (e.g., “Will exercise trigger another heart attack?”) and engage in collaborative goal‐setting with rehabilitation teams to enhance patients' willingness to participate in formal programmes.

Our findings also have important implications for nursing science. Cardiac nursing education programmes should be enhanced to equip future cardiac intervention nurses with three core translational competencies: (a) advanced psychosocial assessment skills, including standardized administration and interpretation of the TSK‐SV Heart scale; (b) kinesiophobia‐sensitive communication techniques that can be taught through simulated patient encounters; and (c) multimodal intervention design proficiency—the ability to develop personalized kinesiophobia mitigation bundles integrating cognitive‐behavioural, physiological, and graded‐activity components based on individual risk profiles. By transforming kinesiophobia from a hidden morbidity into a routinely managed “vital sign” in cardiac nursing, this study provides an evidence‐based framework for future implementation science research, enabling nursing scientists to develop and test theory‐driven interventions that contribute to the growing body of knowledge on fear‐avoidance behaviour in cardiovascular populations.

Advantages and Limitation of This Study

This multicenter cross‐sectional study has some advantages. First, the respondents were from representative hospitals in Guangdong Province, which could widely represent the real situation of post‐PCI patients with kinesiophobia in Guangdong Province. Secondly, this study used the internationally recognized survey scales, which ensure the generalizability of the results. Third, we have conducted training for researchers and developed strict program implementation plans, such as standardized scale management and data quality control procedures, which ensure the credibility of the results.

However, this study also has some limitations. First of all, this study used a self‐report measurement method, so there may be a risk of information bias. Secondly, our study was a cross‐sectional study, which limits our ability to state causality. In the absence of further follow‐up studies, causality should be treated with caution. Third, this study was administered at 6–48 h post‐PCI and did not consider the specific time of disease. Therefore, future large sample size longitudinal studies are needed to conduct serial TSK‐SV heart assessments at different time points post‐PCI and integrate objective activity monitoring with self‐reported measures to evaluate the prevalence and associated factors of kinesiophobia.

Conclusion

This study found that kinesiophobia is very common among post‐PCI patients in southern China, and it is an important factor affecting the prognosis of patients. Marital status, occupational status, number of children, illness familiarity, hypertension comorbidity, cardiac rehabilitation participation, physical activity level, anxiety, stress, depression, objective support, and exercise self‐efficacy were found to be the important factors affecting the occurrence of kinesiophobia in post‐PCI patients. We should conduct routine kinesiophobia screening, precision interventions targeting self‐efficacy and unhealthy emotions, and patient education correcting exercise misconceptions. Implementing this evidence‐based approach represents an essential nursing strategy to reduce kinesiophobia, enhance rehabilitation adherence, and ultimately optimize PCI therapeutic efficacy.
