Failure analysis of coal–rock composites with different coal thicknesses under uniaxial loading based on resistivity response and damage evolution
Authors: Chenglin Tian, Jiali Liu, Jianlei Liu, Wenjie Liu, Wenlong Wang, Bohan Liu, Yong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVariations in coal-seam thickness affect the load-bearing structure and electrical response of coal–rock composites, contributing to dynamic disasters in coal-thickness variation zones. To investigate failure mechanisms and resistivity precursors, conductive coal–rock analogue materials with similar mechanical properties were prepared. Composite specimens with rock-to-coal thickness ratios of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3 were tested under uniaxial compression with synchronous resistivity monitoring, and PFC3D simulations were used to analyze crack evolution and damage accumulation. As the coal proportion increased, peak strength decreased from 18.73 to 15.62 MPa, and failure strain decreased from 2.45 to 2.20%. The total input energy and elastic energy storage capacity also declined, indicating weakened load-bearing and energy storage capacities and earlier damage localization. During loading, resistivity evolves through compaction‑induced reduction, elastic stabilization, pre‑peak rise and failure fluctuation. The failure-stage resistivity increased by 1.27–1.33 times, and the pre-peak resistivity rise preceded the macroscopic stress drop, providing a precursor window for instability identification. PFC3D results reproduced the experimental responses and failure modes, showing that increased coal thickness promoted earlier crack clustering and coalescence, with shear cracks dominating near peak stress. The normalized damage variable based on broken particle bonds corresponded well with resistivity evolution. A resistivity increment index was developed to quantify the transition from stable fluctuation to sustained increase, enabling identification of the precursor stage from stable crack growth to accelerated unstable coalescence. These findings provide a basis for failure-mechanism analysis and resistivity-based warning in coal-thickness variation zones.Keywords: Coal-rock composite, Rock-to-coal thickness ratio, Resistivity precursor, Early warning indicators, Uniaxial loadingSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

As coal mining gradually extends to greater depths, coal–rock masses are increasingly affected by high in-situ stress, intense mining-induced disturbance, and complex geological structures. Consequently, dynamic disasters such as coal and gas outbursts, rock bursts, and large roadway deformation have become more prominent1–3. Ma et al., Tan et al., and Zhao et al. reported that loading-system stiffness, coupled instability between residual coal pillars and overlying strata, and stress concentration under complex geological conditions are important triggers for dynamic coal failure4–6. Wang et al., Zheng et al., and Li et al. further showed, from field testing, FDEM simulation, micro seismic assessment, and fault-slip perspectives, that deep-mining failure is jointly controlled by geological conditions, mining-induced stress, structural breakage, and monitoring responses7–9.

Mining faces and roadway surrounding rocks commonly occur as coal–rock composite structures in which coal seams and roof/floor strata carry loads together10–14 . Their stability is controlled by the coal–rock height ratio, interface effects, and coal strength. Ran et al. used physical similarity simulation and acoustic-emission monitoring to reveal overburden failure during inclined coal-seam mining, indicating that coal occurrence conditions can change crack propagation paths15. In coal-thickness variation zones, abrupt changes in coal-seam thickness can weaken the load-bearing skeleton of the rock layer and intensify deformation incompatibility at the coal–rock interface, causing mining-induced stress to concentrate in the weak coal layer and along the interface. When cracks initiate, propagate, and coalesce in the coal layer or at the interface, disasters such as rock bursts, rib spalling, and roof falls may be induced. Therefore, the mechanical transition from stable load bearing to instability should be analyzed from the perspective of coal-thickness proportion.

Natural coal–rock masses are strongly heterogeneous and contain bedding, joints, and other discontinuities. Direct sampling makes it difficult to ensure consistent specimen dimensions, internal structures, and physical–mechanical parameters, which affects test repeatability. Previous studies have used 3D printing, acoustic-emission monitoring, and rock-like material testing to provide controllable specimens for damage studies of complex structures16–19. Studies on coal-like materials have also shown that cement, sand, gypsum, and other components can be used to regulate strength and deformation parameters and to support solid–gas coupling and gas adsorption simulations20–25. For resistivity-based warning, the analogue material must also exhibit stable electrical conductivity. Li et al. and Hu et al. demonstrated that electrical potential or resistivity during loading can reflect crack initiation, pore evolution, and reconstruction of the conductive network26,27. Thus, preparing coal–rock analogue materials with both mechanical similarity and measurable electrical properties is a prerequisite for investigating coal-thickness-related failure.

Resistivity is sensitive to pore closure, crack propagation, and reconstruction of conductive paths, and has been used for damage identification and precursor monitoring in rock masses. Song et al., Wu et al., and Tan et al. investigated the relationship between coal–rock damage and resistivity variation from the perspectives of hydraulic fracturing, tomographic imaging, and cyclic loading, respectively28–30. Water content, temperature, confining pressure, acoustic emission, crack orientation, and saturation state have also been shown to influence electrical responses31–37. Nevertheless, most existing studies focus on single coal samples, rock samples, or specimens with prefabricated fractures. The correspondence among load-bearing-structure transformation, damage localization, and resistivity precursor transition in composites with different coal-thickness proportions remains insufficiently understood.

Combining field monitoring with numerical simulation helps explain the transition of mining and geotechnical structures from deformation accumulation to instability. Li et al. applied three-dimensional numerical simulation and long-term monitoring to open-pit slope stability analysis, showing that mutual verification between model calculations and field data can improve the reliability of failure-mechanism identification38. For laboratory-scale coal–rock composites, numerical simulation can compensate for the difficulty of directly observing mesoscopic cracks and can clarify the transition from local damage to macroscopic instability. Existing PFC-based studies have analyzed crack interaction, defect-controlled failure, force-chain distribution, and dynamic fracture behavior in rock-like specimens39–47. However, most simulations focus on homogeneous or defective rock masses, and systematic analysis of load-bearing skeleton transformation, crack clustering and coalescence, and their correspondence with resistivity anomalies caused by coal-thickness variation remains limited. In particular, existing studies have not clearly explained how the staged resistivity signature maps to the transition from distributed crack growth to localized damage under different rock-to-coal thickness ratios. The lack of this correspondence makes it difficult to identify whether a resistivity anomaly reflects ordinary conductive-path adjustment or the onset of localized instability in coal-thickness variation zones.

In summary, dynamic disasters in coal-thickness variation zones can be attributed to load-bearing imbalance, damage localization, and rapid crack coalescence in coal–rock composite structures under mining-induced stress concentration. However, the relationships among load-bearing-structure weakening, damage localization, and resistivity precursor response induced by coal-thickness variation remain unclear, limiting effective identification of instability risk. In this study, coal–rock analogue materials with comparable mechanical properties and measurable electrical conductivity were prepared. Composite specimens with rock-to-coal thickness ratios of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3 underwent uniaxial compression testing. During compression, electrical resistivity was continuously monitored. And PFC3D simulations were conducted to characterize crack evolution and damage accumulation during progressive failure. The objectives were to reveal the effects of coal-thickness variation on strength, energy evolution, and failure mode; to clarify crack initiation, clustering, and coalescence mechanisms; and to establish the relationship between resistivity response and damage variables. The results provide theoretical support for failure analysis and resistivity-assisted warning of coal–rock composite structures in coal-thickness variation zones.

Materials and methods

Preparation and testing methods of similar materials for rock assemblages

Because natural rock–coal composite specimens satisfying standard laboratory dimensions are difficult to obtain, cast coal-like and rock-like materials with physical and mechanical properties similar to those of natural coal–rock masses were used in this study. Quartz sand was used as the granular skeleton, ordinary Portland cement and gypsum were used as cementing materials, shell powder was added to increase brittleness, and graphite powder was incorporated to improve the measurability of specimen resistivity. The selected raw materials are shown in Fig. 1a. The coal analogue material was prepared using 425-grade Portland cement, whereas the rock analogue material was prepared using 525-grade Portland cement. Quartz sand with a particle size of 70–140 mesh was used, and the shell powder consisted of large particles of 3–5 mm. The compositions and proportions of the rock and hard-coal analogue materials are listed in Table 1. Three groups of coal–rock composite specimens with different rock-to-coal thickness ratios and dimensions of 50 mm × 100 mm were prepared so that their compressive strengths were comparable to those of natural coal–rock masses. The test scheme is shown in Table 2. To avoid ambiguity, the specimen-grouping term “rock-to-coal thickness ratio” is used consistently; A-1, A-2, and A-3 correspond to ratios of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3, respectively, so the relative coal proportion increases from A-1 to A-3. The material ratios in Table 1 were selected according to similarity-material proportioning principles reported for coal-like and rock-like materials20–25. Cement grade and cement-sand-gypsum proportions were used to control the strength level, shell powder was used to adjust brittleness, and graphite powder was added to build a stable conductive network. The selected ratios were finally retained because the measured compressive strengths of the rock and hard-coal analogue materials were comparable with the target strength range of natural roof rock and hard coal, while maintaining measurable resistivity during loading.

During the tests, a TH28 LCR digital bridge manufactured by Changzhou Tonghui Electronic Co., Ltd., together with the YGJ digital-bridge computer analysis software, was used. Copper sheets were selected as electrodes and placed on the upper and lower surfaces of the coal–rock composite specimens. The two copper sheets were connected to the digital bridge using extended wires. Conductive paste was applied to the upper and lower specimen surfaces to reduce contact resistance. Two insulating plates were placed between the electrodes and the upper and lower platens of the universal testing machine. The materials and equipment are shown in Fig. 1b. The original YGJ acquisition files recorded a bridge frequency setting of 10.0 and an excitation voltage of 1.0 V; the time stamps in the resistance records gave an average sampling interval of approximately 0.376 s, corresponding to a sampling frequency of about 2.66 Hz. The copper-sheet electrodes covered the circular end faces of the 50-mm-diameter specimens, and the effective electrode area used in the resistivity calculation was therefore the specimen cross-sectional area, with the 100-mm specimen height taken as the electrode spacing. Conductive paste was applied and the electrode contact state was checked before loading so that contact resistance remained stable during synchronous mechanical–electrical testing.

Before specimen preparation, molds were prepared, labeled externally, and uniformly coated with lubricating oil on the inner walls to facilitate demolding. The raw materials were weighed according to the designed proportions, mixed thoroughly, cast in layers, and vibrated to ensure compaction. After being covered with film and naturally cured for 24 h, the specimens were demolded, labeled, and placed in a constant-temperature and constant-humidity chamber for 15 d. They were then dried at 105 °C for 24 h and cooled to 25 °C before testing. The preparation process is shown in Fig. 1c.

The experimental procedure involved conducting uniaxial compression tests on the coal–rock composite specimens using a universal testing machine. Mechanical parameters, including load, force, and displacement, were recorded during deformation and failure. Meanwhile, the LCR digital bridge was used to monitor and collect electrical parameters, resistance R and reactance X, in real time. Stress, strain, resistance, resistivity evolution curves, and macroscopic fracture images were then obtained, as shown in Fig. 1d.

Analysis of mechanical response of analogue specimens

Coal–rock composites with different rock-to-coal thickness ratios exhibited different failure characteristics under uniaxial loading, resulting in different uniaxial strengths, as shown in Fig. 2. In the early loading stage, all three groups of specimens showed an approximately linear elastic deformation stage, with stress increasing nearly linearly with strain, indicating good overall integrity. With further strain increase, the specimens gradually entered a nonlinear deformation stage, in which the stress growth rate decreased. After reaching peak strength, distinct post-peak softening occurred.

Mechanical response and typical failure modes of coal-rock composite specimens with different rock-to-coal thickness ratios.

Comparison of peak strength shows that specimen A-1 had the highest uniaxial compressive strength, with a peak stress of approximately 18.73 MPa. A-2 ranked second, while A-3 had a lower peak strength of approximately 15.621 MPa, indicating differences in structure or internal defect distribution among the specimens. In the post-peak stage, the stress of A-1 decreased relatively slowly and the residual bearing capacity remained higher, whereas A-2 and A-3 showed rapid post-peak stress attenuation and more pronounced brittle failure. Combined with the failure patterns in Fig. 2b, the cracks mainly developed along axial and local shear directions, and through-going cracks formed in some specimens during loading, eventually causing overall instability. The differences in stress–strain curves were consistent with the macroscopic failure characteristics, confirming the rationality of the analogue-material proportions and the reliability of the tests.

Numerical simulation method

A PFC3D coal–rock composite model was established based on the discrete element method. A cylindrical specimen with a diameter of 50 mm and a height of 100 mm was generated, with particle radii ranging from 1 to 1.6 mm. The numerical model was divided axially into coal and sandstone material regions. In the grain-based model (GBM), both intra-grain and inter-grain contacts were simulated using the linear parallel-bond model, which has been widely applied and accepted in GBM studies because of its reliability43–45. The microscopic parameters were calibrated using a trial-and-error method, which is widely used in discrete-element numerical studies and has been recognized as effective46,47. The loading process consisted of initial equilibrium, preloading, and uniaxial compression. A confining pressure of 10 MPa was applied during preloading, and displacement control was adopted during uniaxial compression at a loading rate of 5.095 × 10⁻2 m/s. The calculation was stopped when the stress dropped to 60% of the peak value. A monitoring system was established to record axial stress, strain, and crack evolution in real time. The reliability of the model was verified by comparing the numerical stress–strain responses and crack-evolution characteristics with the experimental results. The particle elastic modulus/stiffness ratio, tensile and bond strengths, friction angle, smooth-joint parameters, and damping coefficients were adjusted within the calibrated ranges listed in Tables 3 and 4 until the simulated peak strength, elastic-stage slope, staged stress–strain response, crack-concentration location, and final failure mode were consistent with the laboratory observations. The calibration objectives followed the multi-response validation idea used in recent field-monitoring/numerical-modeling and rock-fracture studies49,50: peak strength and elastic-stage slope were used as quantitative controls, whereas stage division, crack-localization position, and final failure morphology were used as qualitative controls. The accepted calibration required the simulated peak strength to remain within approximately 10% of the experimental value and the elastic-stage slope to remain within approximately 15%, while also reproducing the observed failure mode. The 10 MPa preloading step was used to compact the randomly generated particle assembly and establish initial force equilibrium; after radial/lateral constraints were removed, the model was transferred to the displacement-controlled uniaxial loading path, and no additional residual-stress field was intentionally retained for the formal loading stage.

Figure 3 illustrates the construction procedure of the numerical composite specimen. Step a (model initialization): an initial cylindrical particle assembly was generated. By defining the particle-radius range and porosity, an ungrouped initial geometric configuration was formed randomly within the specified domain. Step b (material grouping): particles were identified and grouped according to their spatial positions. The model was divided axially into coal and rock material groups to establish the basic coal–rock composite framework. Step c (contact-model assignment): differentiated microscopic mechanical parameters were assigned to different material groups. The linear-bond model was adopted, and mechanical parameters were set separately for coal–coal and sandstone–sandstone contacts to represent material heterogeneity. Step d (preloading equilibrium): radial and axial stresses were applied simultaneously to reach initial stress equilibrium and eliminate initial pore effects. The lateral constraint was then removed, uniaxial compression was performed, displacement-controlled loading was maintained at the upper and lower ends to simulate the experimental conditions, and automatic crack tracking was activated. This unloading sequence ensured that the subsequent simulated compaction stage reflected initial particle adjustment and laboratory-like pore closure rather than an artificial lateral-confinement effect.

Numerical modeling procedure for coal-rock composite specimens.

By comparing the crack-development curves from the numerical simulation with the experimentally measured resistivity variation curves, their synchronicity during the compaction, elastic, and failure stages was confirmed, verifying the model reliability in capturing mechanical–electrical coupled responses. Model credibility was therefore evaluated from multiple aspects, including peak strength order, stress–strain stage division, crack-evolution location, and final failure morphology.

Experimental results and failure analysis under loading

Resistivity evolution and instability precursor characteristics of specimens with different rock-to-coal thickness ratios

As shown in Fig. 4, the stress–strain and resistivity curves of the specimens during uniaxial compression exhibited staged variations. The process was divided into four stages: compaction stage (I), elastic deformation stage (II), plastic deformation stage (III), and failure stage (IV). Overall, the resistivity curve displayed a U-shaped trend.

Stress–strain and resistivity evolution curves of specimens A-1–A-3.

Stage I: The stress–strain curve increased nonlinearly. Pores and cracks in the coal–rock mass, especially in the coal component, were gradually compacted. This generated new conductive channels. Under the combined effect of original and newly formed conductive paths, the overall conductivity of the specimen increased, resulting in a gradual decrease in resistivity.

Stage II: The stress–strain curve increased linearly, and the decreasing rate of specimen resistivity gradually weakened. This was mainly because some local conductive channels were damaged during elastic deformation.

Stage III: The stress–strain curve increased nonlinearly and its growth rate gradually decreased, indicating the development of plastic deformation. The stress approached the ultimate bearing capacity of the specimen. The resistivity remained relatively low but began to increase, mainly because continued loading generated macroscopic cracks that severely damaged the conductive channels. Discontinuities in the dielectric structure increased the overall resistivity of the specimen.

Stage IV: After the peak stress, the bearing capacity of the specimen decreased. The stress initially declined slowly and then dropped sharply within a short period. Cracks in the coal–rock composite specimen gradually expanded and coalesced, leading to loss of bearing capacity and severe failure. The resistivity increased with local surface spalling and crack development, accompanied by fluctuations caused by the uncertainty of crack propagation and the diversity of failure modes.

Figure 4 shows that the stress–strain curves, resistivity curves, and failure characteristics of coal–rock composite specimens with different rock-to-coal thickness ratios followed broadly similar trends. The uniaxial compressive strengths of A-1, A-2, and A-3 were 18.73 MPa, 17.17 MPa, and 15.621 MPa, respectively, and the corresponding failure strains were 2.45%, 2.34%, and 2.20%. The compressive strength of the coal–rock composite lay between those of pure coal and pure rock, but was closer to that of coal. With increasing coal thickness, the uniaxial compressive strength decreased, failure occurred earlier, and the damage degree increased. The dominant failure patterns were discontinuous surface spalling and crack propagation. The initial resistivities of A-1, A-2, and A-3 were 3.81 MΩ·m, 3.87 MΩ·m, and 3.96 MΩ·m, respectively, indicating that the initial resistivity increased with coal thickness. At failure, the resistivities reached 4.81 MΩ·m, 5.02 MΩ·m, and 5.28 MΩ·m, corresponding to increases of 1.27, 1.29, and 1.33 times, respectively. These results show that coal thickness significantly affects the coal–rock composite specimens: thicker coal layers lead to lower compressive strength, greater resistivity increase, and more pronounced failure. Compared with A-1, the initial resistivity of A-2 and A-3 increased by approximately 1.6% and 3.9%, respectively, whereas the failure-stage resistivity increased by approximately 4.4% and 9.8%. The larger amplification at failure indicates that increasing coal proportion not only raises the initial electrical resistance of the composite but also intensifies conductive-path destruction during unstable crack coalescence.

The three groups of specimens exhibited consistent staged resistivity evolution during uniaxial compression. In Stage I, resistivity decreased rapidly with loading, reflecting closure of primary pores and microcracks and progressive improvement of contact conductive paths. In Stage II, resistivity changed only slightly and tended to stabilize, indicating a relatively stable internal structure. Notably, when the specimen entered Stage III, resistivity changed from a slow decrease to a sustained increase, and the onset of this increase generally preceded the sharp stress drop in the failure stage (Stage IV), showing a clear instability precursor. This phenomenon can be interpreted as the transition of cracks from stable propagation to accelerated growth and local coalescence, which cuts and reconstructs conductive paths, reduces the equivalent conductive cross-section and connectivity, and consequently causes resistivity recovery and enhanced fluctuation. Therefore, the resistivity transition in Stage III should be regarded not merely as an electrical-response change, but as an external manifestation of internal damage changing from distributed evolution to localized failure.

Definition and calculation of energy parameters

Under uniaxial compression, the energy evolution of coal–rock composites mainly involve energy accumulation, release, transformation, and dissipation. It can generally be divided into input, storage, dissipation, and release stages. In the experiments, external loading induced specimen deformation and failure. The input energy mainly came from work done by the testing machine and was transformed into elastic strain energy and dissipated energy within the specimen. Elastic strain energy is recoverable, whereas dissipated energy includes irreversible forms such as crack surface energy and plastic energy. Coal–rock composites exhibit complex mechanical responses and energy-state transitions during loading. To analyze the energy evolution characteristics under uniaxial compression, representative specimens with strengths close to the average value of each group were selected. The following assumptions were adopted: the material was regarded as ideal elastoplastic, residual energy associated with strain hardening or softening was ignored, and damping dissipation and heat exchange during loading were neglected according to the first law of thermodynamics. Therefore, work done by the testing machine was considered the main energy input. Energy evolution curves of coal-rock composite specimens.

As shown in Fig. 5, the total input energy of the composite increased with strain as external loading continued. The energy curves of specimens with different rock-to-coal thickness ratios showed similar overall trends, but their slopes and pre-peak maximum input energies differed markedly. With increasing coal thickness, the total input energy absorbed at failure gradually decreased, indicating that the load-bearing skeleton of the composite changed from rock-dominated to jointly controlled by the weak coal layer and the coal–rock interface. When the coal proportion was low, the rock layer provided stronger resistance to external loading and the specimen could accumulate more energy. As the coal proportion increased, deformation of the weak coal layer and interface incompatibility more readily induced local damage, reducing the energy accumulation capacity. Thus, increasing coal thickness not only lowers peak strength but also weakens the energy-bearing and energy-storage capacities of the composite before instability.

Once the specimen entered the plastic stage during uniaxial compression, the external input energy was no longer primarily stored elastically, but was gradually converted into crack propagation, coal–rock interface slip, and irreversible plastic deformation. At the same time, the stress–strain curve began to deviate from linearity, indicating accelerated internal damage. As shown in Fig. 5, increasing coal thickness significantly reduced the maximum elastic strain energy and the energy-storage rate during the elastic stage, suggesting that a larger weak-coal proportion weakens the elastic energy-storage capacity of the composite. As shown in Fig. 5, dissipated energy increased rapidly after the peak, indicating that unstable crack propagation, formation of localized failure zones, and crack coalescence consumed a large amount of energy. Therefore, increasing coal thickness changes the energy distribution of the composite: it reduces pre-peak energy accumulation, promotes earlier initiation of dissipation, and makes post-peak damage more likely to concentrate in local regions, thereby accelerating the transition from stable load bearing to instability. This energy redistribution agrees with the macroscopic failure observations: early elastic-energy accumulation corresponds to specimen integrity, rapid dissipated-energy growth accompanies surface spalling and axial/shear crack extension, and the post-peak surge of dissipation reflects crack coalescence and unstable loss of bearing capacity.

Meso-scale failure mechanism

In this section, a cylindrical numerical specimen was constructed using a three-dimensional particle-flow model based on PFC3D, and uniaxial compression simulations were conducted. The calibration was constrained by laboratory peak strength, elastic-stage slope, stress–strain-curve morphology, and the observed locations of crack propagation rather than by a single numerical response, thereby reducing the non-uniqueness of mesoscopic parameter selection. This strategy is consistent with calibration approaches that combine monitored deformation, mechanical response, and failure morphology to validate numerical parameters49,50.

Validation of the numerical model.

As shown in Fig. 6c, the simulated stress–strain curve was generally consistent with the laboratory results and exhibited four similar stages: compaction (I), elastic deformation (II), plastic deformation (III), and failure (IV). Natural rocks contain uncertain internal structures formed by microdefects, which lengthen the compaction stage of the experimental curve. Therefore, although the numerical peak load and elastic-stage slope were broadly consistent with the experimental results, the numerical displacement at peak load was smaller than that measured experimentally. This is a common phenomenon in PFC simulations and does not affect the rationality of the calibrated mesoscopic parameters or the validity of the model. In terms of failure mode, the experimental and numerical results also showed good consistency. Severe failure occurred at the bottom of the specimen, and a secondary macroscopic crack extended from the bottom toward the specimen side, as shown in Fig. 6b. The comparison therefore considered peak strength, stress–strain-curve stages, dominant crack-propagation location, and final failure mode, which strengthens the consistency between the numerical simulation and the indoor tests. Based on calibration strategies that integrate monitored deformation, mechanical response, and failure morphology49,50, the mesoscopic parameters were evaluated using both quantitative and qualitative criteria. Specifically, the simulated peak strength and elastic-stage slope were controlled within approximately 10% and 15% of the experimental values, respectively, and the stress–strain stage division, crack-concentration location, and final failure morphology were required to be consistent with the laboratory observations.

Damage evolution

The failure characteristics of discrete-element particles, including the number of failures and the proportion of failure types, can effectively describe debonding and fracture processes under loading and thereby reveal the mechanical transition from mesoscopic damage to macroscopic fracture.

Figure 7 presents the crack-evolution snapshots obtained from the PFC3D simulations, where the rows correspond to specimens A-1–A-3 and the columns correspond to loading stages I–IV. The coal–rock composites exhibited distinct staged damage characteristics under uniaxial compression. Stage I was dominated by particle compaction and pore closure, with almost no cracks generated. In Stage II, cracks first initiated sporadically within the weak coal layer and along the coal–rock interface. In Stage III, the number of cracks increased rapidly and local crack clusters formed, subsequently extending toward the coal layer. Stage IV was characterized by extensive crack coalescence, formation of a dominant main fracture surface, and eventual overall failure. Comparison among A-1, A-2, and A-3 shows that coal proportion strongly affected crack-evolution rate and failure mode. A-1 showed the latest crack initiation and coalescence, followed by A-2, whereas A-3 showed the earliest crack clustering and rapid formation of through-going cracks in the early part of Stage III. This indicates that increasing coal thickness significantly accelerates the transition from stable damage evolution to unstable failure.

Crack evolution of specimens at different loading stages.

As shown in Fig. 8–c, crack evolution in A-1 to A-3 corresponded well with the stress–strain responses. Figure 8d shows that shear cracks dominated in all three models. At the end of the record, shear cracks accounted for 83–84% of the total crack number and could reach approximately 88–89% near the peak stress, indicating that the loss of peak bearing capacity was mainly controlled by concentrated shear-crack propagation. The proportion of tensile cracks increased after the peak, suggesting further local opening and block separation after formation of the main fracture zone.

Crack evolution of specimens with different rock-to-coal thickness ratios.

As the relative coal thickness increased from A-1 to A-3, crack initiation, accelerated propagation, and coalescence all occurred earlier, and A-3 showed the most pronounced post-peak crack accumulation. This indicates that increasing coal thickness enhances deformation incompatibility between the coal and rock layers, causing stress to concentrate more readily in the weak coal layer and near the interface. As a result, shear cracks localize and coalesce earlier. Therefore, increasing the coal-thickness proportion not only reduces composite strength but also changes the crack propagation path and failure process.

This mesoscopic fracture process corroborates the resistivity response. Rapid pre-peak crack clustering and damage to conductive paths correspond to the change in resistivity from stable variation to sustained increase. Therefore, pre-peak resistivity recovery can serve as an external response of crack localization and provides a mechanistic basis for using the resistivity increment index to identify instability precursors.

Instability criterion and warning significance based on the resistivity increment index

Based on the uniaxial compression tests, PFC3D numerical simulations, and resistivity evolution model described above, the resistivity response shows good correspondence with crack-damage evolution during the transition of coal–rock composites from stable load bearing to instability. The resistivity increment index proposed in this study was not derived directly from field monitoring, but was established from laboratory tests and numerical simulations as an instability identification index. Therefore, its engineering significance mainly lies in providing a criterion reference for identifying instability precursors in coal–rock composite structures in coal-thickness variation zones.

When the resistivity increment index is close to zero or fluctuates slightly, internal crack development is weak, the conductive structure is relatively stable, and the composite remains in a stable load-bearing stage. When the index changes from near zero to continuous positive values, accompanied by a transition of resistivity from stable variation to sustained increase, internal cracks have shifted from stable propagation to accelerated growth, and conductive paths are being continuously cut. This can be used as an identification signal for the instability precursor stage. When the index shows an obvious sudden increase or strong fluctuation, rapid crack coalescence and connection among local damaged regions are indicated, and the composite enters a stage of high macroscopic instability risk.

From the perspective of engineering failure analysis, an increase in coal proportion in coal-thickness variation zones weakens the overall bearing and energy-storage capacities of coal–rock composite structures and promotes earlier crack clustering and coalescence. If continuous resistivity monitoring is available in the field, attention should be focused on coal-thickness transition zones, areas near coal–rock interfaces, and local failure-sensitive zones around roadways. When the monitored curve shows a 'stable–sustained increase–abrupt fluctuation’ pattern, it can be used as a warning signal for accelerated damage and increased instability risk in coal–rock composite structures.
