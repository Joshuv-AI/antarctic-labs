Toward implementation of lactate compared with pH in fetal scalp blood sampling during labor: A clinical comparative single‐center study
Authors: Suzanne J. van Woudenberg, Ivonne Mijnheer, Marcel M. G. J. van Borren, Lian Roovers, Femi Janse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSince 60 years, pH measurement in fetal scalp blood samples (FBS) is used to diagnose fetal hypoxia during labor, although the last three decades lactate measurement in FBS has been increasingly used as a safe alternative with a lower chance of failure. However, studies demonstrating the direct relation between lactate and pH in FBS with the currently used measurement systems are lacking. Therefore, clinical acceptance and implementation is impeded. This study investigated the relation between lactate and pH in FBS and derived an appropriate lactate cutoff value.Material and MethodsThis observational prospective single‐center study included 263 FBS in the period of 8 months. After collection of FBS, pH was assessed first (iSTAT®), after which lactate was assessed (StatStrip® Lactate 2). Clinical decisions were based on pH results only. Our primary objective was to assess the correlation between lactate and pH in FBS and to establish a lactate cutoff value for ruling out a low Apgar score at 5 minutes and neonatal acidosis, with corresponding sensitivity and specificity as secondary outcomes. Diagnostic performance of lactate was assessed by using the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operation characteristic curve (ROC curve), from which cutoff values were derived.ResultsIn FBS, lactate and pH showed a moderate inverse linear correlation (r = −0.53). The AUC of the ROC curve was 0.88 (0.83–0.93), indicating a good diagnostic performance of lactate to identify an abnormal pH (pH <7.20 in FBS). A cutoff value for lactate of ≥5.6 mmol/L seemed to have the most optimal balance between sensitivity (78.1% [60.0–90.7%]) and specificity (79.2% [73.4–84.3%]) for a pH <7.20 in FBS.ConclusionsThis study confirmed that the StatStrip® Lactate 2 is a suitable alternative analyzer for measurement of lactate compared with measurement of pH with the iSTAT in FBS. Despite only a moderate correlation between lactate and pH, a lactate cutoff value of ≥5.6 mmol/L could be proposed for clinical decision‐making. This knowledge increased confidence of our gynecologists in this relatively new method and paved the way for successful and universal implementation of lactate in FBS, eventually without measuring pH.

INTRODUCTION

Fetal monitoring during labor is aimed to provide insight into the risk for fetal hypoxia, reducing later neonatal morbidity and mortality. Cardiotocography (CTG) is one of the best known methods used for intrapartum monitoring of the fetal heartrate. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 However, the specificity of CTG is low and does not reduce the frequency of unnecessary instrumental deliveries. 5 , 6 It is thought that adding fetal blood scalp sampling (FBS) may resolve these pitfalls, however evidence and international guidelines are contradicting. 3 , 7 Nevertheless, the majority of the Dutch hospitals complement CTG monitoring with FBS. This is based on the rationale that FBS improves sensitivity and specificity, respectively for ruling out and confirming fetal hypoxia, and potentially reduces the amount of unnecessary instrumental deliveries. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12

Since 60 years, pH measurement in FBS is used to diagnose fetal hypoxia during labor, although the last three decades lactate measurement in FBS has been increasingly used as a safe alternative, without significant differences in neonatal outcomes and without increasing the amount of medical interventions. 3 , 5 , 6 , 8 , 9 Lactate measurement has practical benefits compared with pH measurement in FBS: the failure rate is lower (1–2% vs. 10–20%), it requires a smaller fetal blood sample (0.5‐1 μL vs. 30‐150 μL) and results are available more quickly (10–20s vs. 190‐300 s). 4 , 7 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Moreover, lactate is specific for metabolic acidosis, whereas pH cannot differentiate between metabolic and respiratory acidosis. Since it is metabolic acidosis that causes neurological damage to the fetus, lactate could theoretically be more specific for fetal hypoxia. 6 , 17

Nevertheless, the clinical adoption of lactate measurement in FBS is lagging behind, mainly because it remains unclear how pH values translate into lactate concentrations and how this relation varies across currently used point‐of‐care testing (POCT) devices. 5 , 15 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Studies examining the relation between lactate and pH in FBS are lacking and many previously published studies have lost relevance, since several analyzers have been updated or are no longer available. 6 , 7 , 12 , 18 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 Therefore, each laboratory has to establish their own lactate cutoff values from their own analyzer. The Dutch Society of Obstetrics and Gynaecology (NVOG) has included this recommendation in their guideline and states that the lactate test in FBS could only be implemented after clinical validation. 3 , 24 Such a clinical validation and implementation requires a dedicated multidisciplinary team of nurses, obstetricians and clinical chemists: a comprehensive process that has to be repeated in every hospital separately. Moreover, following the recommendation of the NVOG guideline leads to individual hospitals creating their own standards, causing undesirable inconsistency in clinical decision making among Dutch hospitals.

The aim of this study was to investigate the correlation between lactate and pH in FBS and to establish an optimal cutoff value for lactate, using the most recent edition of the StatStrip® Lactate 2.

MATERIALS AND METHOD

Study design and inclusion

This observational prospective single‐center study was performed in Rijnstate hospital, the Netherlands, with 2300 deliveries annually. Samples were collected between March 11, 2024 and October 27, 2024. Inclusion criteria were as follows: indication for FBS based on midwife's and/or doctor's interpretation of the intrapartum CTG, singleton pregnancy, gestational age ≥ 32 + 0 weeks, cephalic presentation, reliable pH measurement based on pCO2 > 5.0 kPa and FBS samples in which both lactate and pH were known.

Fetal blood scalp sampling

The intrapartum CTG was interpreted by the midwife and/or doctor according to the International Federation of Gynaecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) guidelines for CTG classification. 2 When the CTG was interpreted as suboptimal or abnormal and the woman gave consent to perform FBS, FBS was performed following the standardized local protocol. At first, the woman was positioned supine and with the legs in stirrups. Then, the fetal scalp was visualized using an amnioscope and cleaned using a sterile gauze held by forceps. After application of sterile petrolatum at the incision site, the scalp was punctuated with a 2‐mm scalp lancet and an incision of 3‐8 mm was made. The blood sample was collected in a capillary of 125 μL. When the capillary was completely full, it was transferred to the iSTAT® POCT analyzer (Abbott) for pH measurement. Pending pH results, another capillary was filled on condition that sufficient blood was flowing from the earlier made incision. Increase of the incision or making a new incision for lactate collection only was not allowed. When 5 μL blood was collected, the capillary was transferred to the StatStrip® Lactate 2 POCT analyzer (NovaBiomedical) for lactate measurement.

Clinical decisions were based on pH results only. The cutoff values with subsequent management for pH were: <7.20 acidotic, immediate termination of labor required; 7.20–7.25 pre‐acidotic, close monitoring advised and repeating FBS every 30 min; >7.25 normal, repeat FBS according to CTG assessment. When pH could not be collected or pH was unreliable based on pCO2 < 5.0 kPa, clinical decisions were based on the gynecologist's judgment. Regardless of pH, no decisions were based on lactate results. Although obstetricians and nurses were not blinded to the lactate values and outcomes, they were strictly discouraged from incorporating the lactate results in clinical decision‐making.

After delivery, an umbilical artery sample was collected within the first 5 minutes after birth from an unclamped umbilical cord. Then the umbilical artery sample was transferred to the laboratory for pH assessment with the GEM4000® (Werfen BV) analyzer, in order to assess asphyxia. For each FBS, the obstetric care provider recorded the patient number, time of sampling, pH and lactate values, clinical decisions, and the time and mode of delivery on a registration list in the electronic health record. All study outcomes were subsequently reviewed in detail by a single clinician using the maternal electronic health record. When necessary, the neonatal record was reviewed as well, particularly to obtain the umbilical artery sample pH.

The StatStrip® Lactate 2 POCT analyzer was calibrated by the NovaBiomedical company. This calibration was implemented in the software. In our hospital, the StatStrip® analyzers were regularly checked by quality control measurements to see whether the analyzers still complied.

Objectives and outcomes

The primary objective was to assess the correlation between lactate and pH in FBS and to establish a lactate cutoff value indicating fetal distress, based on the correlation between lactate and pH. Secondary outcomes were as follows: the sensitivity and specificity to identify cases with low 5‐min Apgar score and acidosis, failure rates for FBS for both lactate and pH, and failure reasons for lactate in FBS. Signs of asphyxia were defined by either a low 5‐min Apgar score based on an Apgar score at 5 minutes <7 or acidosis based on pH in the umbilical artery sample <7.0. Only FBS performed within 30 min before delivery were included for the assessment of the relation with a low 5‐min Apgar score or acidosis.

Statistical analyses

Characteristics of the study population, mode of delivery, failure rates for both lactate, and pH in FBS and failure reasons for lactate in FBS were described quantitatively. Multiple FBS within the same woman were allowed and assessed as independent measurements. Since we did not perform a power analysis, the study population was a convenience sample. A scatter plot was constructed for comparing lactate with pH in FBS, in which a regression line with a corresponding 95% prediction interval was fitted. Besides, a correlation coefficient (r) was calculated by using the Pearson correlation. This correlation coefficient was interpreted as weak, moderate or strong, according to international consensus. 25 In the scatter plot, no distinctions were made for multiple testing within individual women nor for time between FBS and delivery. The diagnostic performance of lactate to predict an abnormal pH (pH <7.2 in FBS) was assessed by calculating the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operation characteristic curve (ROC curve). This assessment was corrected for multiple observations within one woman. The proposed cutoff value for lactate was established based on the most optimal balance between the highest sensitivity and highest specificity, marked by the most upper left corner of the ROC curve. Corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) for the sensitivity and specificity were calculated by using the Clopper–Pearson method. In the subanalysis for signs of asphyxia, the aforementioned statistical analyses and graphs were repeated, although only FBS performed within 30 min before delivery were included. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS, version 22.0 (BM SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY, USA) and R, version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

During the 8‐month study period, 1563 women delivered in Rijnstate hospital. In this period, 349 FBS were taken in 245 women, corresponding with a sampling frequency of 15.7%. Figure 1 presents the course of the inclusions. FBS with unreliable outcomes based on pCO2 < 5.0 kPa (n = 12), missing pH values (n = 43) and missing lactate values (n = 31) were excluded. Reasons for failure of FBS lactate (n = 31 [10.5%]) were as follows: failure of POCT analyzer (n = 5), insufficient blood after pH assessment (n = 3), viscous blood (n = 1), off protocol (n = 1), and undefined (n = 21). After exclusion, 263 FBS were useful for analysis (75.4%). These samples were collected in 192 women. Samples were taken between at least 1 min until 19 h before delivery, of which 66 samples within 30 min (25.1%), 59 samples within 30–60 min (22.4%), and 138 samples within more 60 min (52.5%). In total, 104 women (54.2%) had a spontaneous vaginal delivery, 50 women (26.0%) delivered by instrumental vaginal extraction and 38 women (19.8%) delivered by cesarean section. In total, 15 neonates (7.8%) had a low 5‐min Apgar score. An umbilical artery sample was available in 135 of the 192 women (70.3%) and seven neonates (5.2%) had an acidosis based on the pH in ambilical artery sample.

An overview of the correlation between lactate and pH in FBS is provided in the scatter plot in Figure 2. Lactate and pH showed an inversely linear relation with a moderate correlation (r = −0.53). Lactate values varied between 0.6 and 11.8 mmol/L with a mean of 4.4 mmol/L, whereas pH values varied between 7.06 and 7.40 with a mean of 7.27. Including the inverse linear relation with 95% prediction interval and well‐known cutoff values of pH of, respectively, >7.25 and <7.20, some outliers could be identified. Five FBS exceeded the 95% prediction interval and had a pH >7.25 (7.27–7.40), corresponding with a lactate of 7.3–9.6 mmol/L. No FBS exceeded the 95% prediction interval with a pH <7.20.

The ROC curve for lactate compared with pH in FBS is shown in Figure 3. The AUC of the ROC curve was 0.88 (95% CI 0.83–0.93), which indicates a good diagnostic performance of lactate to predict an abnormal pH (pH <7.2). Based on the coordinates of the curve, cutoff values were assessed. A cutoff value for lactate of ≥5.6 mmol/L was defined to have the most optimal balance between sensitivity (78.1% [60.0–90.7%]) and specificity (79.2% [73.4–84.3%]). DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to present the correlation between lactate and pH values in FBS, by using the StatStrip® Lactate 2 and iSTAT® analyzer, respectively. We showed a moderate inverse linear correlation (r = −0.53). The AUC of the ROC curve was 0.88, indicating a good diagnostic performance of lactate to predict an abnormal pH (pH <7.20). A cutoff value for lactate of ≥5.6 mmol/L seemed to have the most optimal balance between sensitivity (78.1% [60.0–90.7%]) and specificity (79.2% [73.4–84.3%]) for a pH <7.20 in FBS.

Lactate testing in FBS during labor has been studied for almost 50 years. 5 , 6 , 8 , 9 , 14 , 26 , 27 These studies identified that lactate has a few advantages compared with pH. At first, the failure rate is lower (1–2% vs. 10–20%), most probably since less blood is needed (0.5‐1 μL vs. 30‐150 μL). Secondly, the results are available more quickly (10–20s vs. 190‐300 s). 4 , 7 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Lastly, lactate would theoretically be more specific for metabolic acidosis. 5 , 6 , 8 , 17 Besides, these studies have consequently shown that lactate and pH are comparable in preventing neonatal acidemia and asphyxia. 5 , 6 , 8 , 9 , 27 Only a few studies examined and illustrated the relation between lactate and pH in FBS: the higher the lactate, the lower the pH. 12 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 28 In these studies, correlations ranged from weak to strong (r = 0.33–0.85), depending on different sample sizes and different analyzers. Our prospective study in a Dutch cohort of 263 FBS showed an overall moderate correlation between lactate and pH by using the StatStrip® Lactate 2 and iSTAT® analyzer (r = 0.53). Besides, using a cutoff value for lactate of ≥5.6 resulted in less false positive and a comparable amount of false negative results compared with pH in cases with signs of asphyxia. Therefore, our study further supports lactate as a safe alternative for pH in FBS.

Later, studies focused on incorporating lactate assessment in clinical practice using a variety of methods and outcomes. 8 , 9 , 12 , 15 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 27 , 29 , 30 Unfortunately, many previously published studies became less relevant, since the previous studied POCT analyzers are no longer available, have been updated or have not yet been standardized. 8 , 9 , 12 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 It is known that the lactate value measured, is depending on every specific. 5 , 18 , 20 , 21 Therefore, clinical validation with the chosen POCT analyzer is advised prior to implementation of lactate in FBS. 3 In this study, we decided to examine the StatStrip® POCT analyzer. This analyzer is specially developed for clinical use in hospitals, has widely been used and is thus readily available in the Netherlands. 18 Since the StatStrip® Lactate 1 is no longer available on the European market, we used the StatStrip® Lactate 2 with the expectation that lactate values would be different using a different edition of the analyzer. 31 So far, few studies on the use of StatStrip® analyzer in FBS have been published, while all had different study designs. 15 , 16 , 21 , 24 , 27 , 29 Some studies compared the StatStrip® with other analyzerss than the iSTAT. 16 , 21 , 27 , 29 Other studies assessed lactate in umbilical artery samples but not in FBS. 15 , 24 One study assessed lactate in the umbilical artery sample first, thereafter translating this value to a value of lactate in FBS theoretically. 24 However, this translation is complicated due to differences in time between collection and labor, physiological factors (confounders) and analytical (analyzer) factors. 5 , 21 To our knowledge, this is the first study that visualized and correlated exact values of the new lactate test compared with the former and well‐known pH test, assessed with the StatStrip® Lactate 2 and iSTAT®, respectively. With this insight, we aim to augment knowledge and confidence in the lactate test among healthcare professionals, so that further steps of implementation of lactate in FBS are supported.

The next step of implementation will be to define a safe cutoff value for lactate. Based on our findings, we suggest a cutoff value for lactate of ≥5.6 mmol/L for intervention, based on the most optimal balance between sensitivity (78.1% [60.0–90.7%]) and specificity (79.2% [73.4–84.3%]) for a pH <7.20 in FBS. Previous studies suggested varying cutoff values for lactate, that is from 4.2 to 5.7 mmol/L for reassurance and from 4.8 to 7.0 mmol/L for intervention. 9 , 14 , 16 , 21 , 24 , 27 , 30 Unfortunately, these cutoff values cannot be extrapolated to our study since different analyzers and methods were used. Iorizzo et al. recommended one lactate cutoff value of ≥5.2 mmol/L for intervention measured by the StatStrip Lactate 1, based on 800 FBS taken within 25 min before delivery. 27 This cutoff value was later confirmed by Berge et al. in another cohort of 1466 FBS taken within 25 min before delivery. 32 Recently, Gellert et al. technically validated the StatStrip® Lactate 2 with the no longer available StatStrip® Lactate 1. Based on a strong linear correlation between these analyzers (r = 0.94), they recommended an interventional cutoff value of ≥7.0 mmol/L for the StatStrip® Lactate 2. 31 Our proposed cutoff value of ≥5.6 mmol/L is considerably lower than Gellert et al. suggested. However, we compared the StatStrip® Lactate 2 with the iSTAT® analyzer that measured pH, whereas Gellert et al. compared the StatStrip® Lactate 2 with another analyzer, the StatStrip® Lactate 1, that measured lactate only. The use of different analyzers and outcomes could explain the different results. Especially, we need to be aware that lactate measures only metabolic acidosis, whereas pH cannot differentiate between metabolic and respiratory acidosis, which makes comparison of lactate and pH more difficult. 6 , 17 , 28

Therefore, we performed a subanalysis to the presence of a low 5‐min Apgar score and acidosis as signs of asphyxia, since this is the most important clinical marker which FBS intends to predict and prevent. Since the prevalence of this outcome is very low in European countries, it is challenging to study this outcome, as was acknowledged in earlier studies on FBS. 5 , 8 , 9 , 24 , 27 Unsurprisingly, this was also true for our study. Based on 66 FBS taken within 30 min before birth, we identified only seven cases with a low 5‐min Apgar score or acidosis, with varying both lactate and pH values. Of these seven neonates, four neonates had a lactate not only above our proposed cutoff value of 5.6 mmol/L but also above the cutoff value of 5.2 mmol/L that Iorizzo et al. recommended. 27 This small group of 66 FBS suggests that lactate might be an even better indicator for asphyxia, since a cutoff value for lactate of ≥5.6 resulted in less false positive and comparable false negative results compared with pH in cases with signs of asphyxia. However, it is important to keep in mind that lactate values are not used for clinical management in these cases. Further research, ideally nationwide and specific to lactate management, is needed to further examine this valuable clinical outcome.

The main strength in our study is the prospectively, uniformly and systematically collection of FBS. in a relatively large cohort. During this collection, we reduced the influence of lactate assessment in clinical decision making as much as possible, creating an objective comparison of the exact values of lactate and pH in FBS. Besides, since we not only compared the most commonly used POCT analyzer in the Netherlands for both lactate and pH assessment but also used the currently available and mostly used POCT analyzer for lactate: the StatStrip® Lactate 2, our study is useful for daily practice and clinical validation in many different hospitals.

Some limitations were identified in this study. At first, failure rate of lactate measurement could not be assessed properly, since we prioritized adequate pH sampling over lactate sampling. Therefore, FBS could have been falsely marked as failed or could have been completely missed. However, we did show that the failure rate of lactate was at least comparable with the failure rate of pH (10.5% vs. 12.3%, respectively). We hypothesize that the failure rate of lactate would have been lower if pH assessment was no longer prioritized. Moreover, we could not reflect on operative delivery rate using lactate measurement, since we based clinical decisions on pH measurement only. This outcome can only be assessed properly after well‐defined cutoff values of lactate will have been incorporated in clinical practice.

CONCLUSION

This study confirmed that the StatStrip® Lactate 2 is a suitable alternative analyzer for measurement of lactate compared with measurement of pH with the iSTAT in FBS. Despite only a moderate correlation between lactate and pH, a lactate cutoff value of ≥5.6 mmol/L could be proposed for clinical decision making. This knowledge increased the confidence of our gynecologists in this relatively new method and paved the way for successful and universal implementation of lactate in FBS, eventually without measuring pH.

ETHICS STATEMENT

Every woman with an indication for FBS was counseled. The scientific board of the Rijnstate hospital was asked for advice on requirement of further approval for lactate assessment. Further approval for lactate assessment in FBS was considered to be unnecessary for three reasons. At first, this study was considered a validation of the diagnostic FBS test in order to improve the quality of testing. Consequently, laws on scientific research other than quality assurance were not applicable. Besides, no extra intervention was required to get enough material to assess lactate in FBS. Finally, no clinical decisions were based on lactate results. Therefore, the assessment implied no risks to the mother and child. After delivery, every woman in whom FBS had been performed was asked for permission to take umbilical artery samples, regardless of FBS outcomes.
