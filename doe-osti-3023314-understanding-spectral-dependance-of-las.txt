 Understanding spectral dependance of laser-induced damage precursors in dielectric
 materials
 Colin Harthcock
                                          23-ERD-006

Abstract
The performance of high-energy laser systems is constrained by laser-induced damage in dielectric
coatings, particularly those containing hafnium oxide (HfO₂). While thresholds at fundamental Nd
harmonics are well studied, the spectral dependence of damage initiation—especially under dual-
wavelength irradiation—remains poorly characterized. This project provides the first systematic
investigation of wavelength-dependent laser damage in hafnia coatings, focusing on nanoscale
precursors such as craze lines, nanobubbles, stoichiometric variations, nodules, and controlled
crystallization. Coatings were fabricated via ion beam sputtering and electron-beam deposition and
characterized using spectrophotometry, ellipsometry, AFM, GI-XRD, RBS, PCI absorption, and
fs/ns laser damage testing. Results show that craze lines, benign under infrared light, strongly
initiate damage under UV due to wavelength-selective field intensification. Substituting xenon for
argon suppresses nanobubbles and improves UV thresholds by up to 32%. Oxygen modulation
reveals that fully oxidized films maximize UV resistance, though at the cost of porosity and stress
in multilayers. HfO₂–SiO₂ composites resist crystallization and defects while achieving ppm-level
absorption and elevated thresholds, whereas full crystallization of HfO₂ enhances LIDT by
reducing defects and improving thermal transport. Collectively, these findings link photon energy,
defect states, and bandgap collapse, providing a predictive framework for wavelength-dependent
laser damage. The outcomes directly inform the design of durable, multi-wavelength coatings for
facilities such as NIF, MEC, HAPLS, and DPAL, advancing the readiness of next-generation
optics.
Background and Research Objectives
 High-power laser systems are constrained by laser-induced damage in their optical
components. While thresholds at fundamental Nd harmonics are well characterized, much less is
known about material susceptibility at intermediate or simultaneous multi-wavelength irradiation.
Photon-energy effects on initiation are complex, as early-stage absorption is dominated not by
direct band-to-band transitions but by defect-assisted pathways, leaving the links between photon
energy, defect states, and bandgap collapse poorly understood.
 This project conducts the first comprehensive measurements of spectral damage
dependence in the nanosecond regime under both single- and dual-wavelength irradiation,
focusing on hafnia-based coatings—the damage-limiting material in many high-power facilities.
By introducing controlled defects and probing their interaction with varying photon energies, we
aim to clarify mechanisms of absorption, defect activation, and bandgap collapse.
 The framework builds on the role of sub-bandgap states in multiphoton and defect-assisted
absorption, with dual irradiation expanding accessible interactions and representing worst-case
system conditions. Prior studies on KDP and fused silica have shown strong wavelength
dependencies and threshold discontinuities tied to defect populations. Extending this to coatings
will enable predictive rules for wavelength-dependent damage and provide insights into initiation
physics. The outcomes will inform safe operating regimes for next-generation multi-wavelength
systems at NIF, MEC, HAPLS, and DPAL, bridging a critical gap in predictive models for high-
performance optical systems.

Laser damage wavelength-dependance of precursors associated with craze lines and stress
concentration near nodular defects.
Bottom Line – Craze lines contain precursors that are UV-sensitive similar to cracks in
bulk dielectric materials. They appear to be insensitive to IR irradiation.
 This study examined craze lines—stress-induced microcracks—in multilayer dielectric
(MLD) mirrors and their differing responses to 1064 nm versus 355 nm irradiation. Crazing arises
when tensile stress exceeds fracture toughness, commonly in low-energy deposition methods.
While mitigation strategies exist, the critical gap has been why craze lines are benign at 1064 nm
yet strongly limit 355 nm performance.
 HfO₂/SiO₂ high-reflectors deposited by e-beam were designed to produce subtle crazing.
Microscopy revealed nodules with associated craze lines, and FIB-SEM confirmed stress
concentration sufficient to drive substrate cracking. Raster testing showed that at 1064 nm, damage
avoided craze lines, while at 355 nm, initiation occurred preferentially along them, forming
eruption-like pits typical of UV nanosecond mechanisms. These results align with operational
experience: IR transport optics tolerate crazing, whereas UV optics in ICF are strongly limited by
it. Figure 1 shows the damage morphology of the testing.

Figure 1. Micrographs of laser‐induced damage around the MLD craze lines: (a) confocal micrograph and (b) SEM micrograph at
spot irradiation at 1064 nm (24 ± 2 J/cm²) along with an inset displaying the typical 1064 nm damage morphology; (c) confocal
micrograph at spot irradiation at 355 nm (2.0 ± 0.2 J/cm²); (d) an SEM micrograph of damage spots along the craze lines visible in
(c) along with an inset displaying the typical 355 nm damage morphology; (e, f) zoomed in SEM micrographs of the damage in
(d).

 Finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations confirmed negligible field
amplification at 1064 nm but ~3× enhancement at 355 nm, co-located with HfO₂ interfaces rich in
defect states. This optical intensification, combined with crack-tip defect chemistry, explains the
UV-specific vulnerability. Figure 2 shows the simulation results.

Figure 2. Finite-difference time-domain simulations of the maximal electric field intensity for the high index hafnia material for
(a) the 1064 nm and (b) the 355 nm case as a function of craze line thickness for both the T E and TM polarization conditions. The
values are relative to the intensity of light input onto the MLD.

 Overall, this work provides the first evidence of stress-driven substrate cracking in brittle
MLDs, demonstrates that craze lines are selective UV initiators, and establishes a framework
linking mesoscale stress, defect chemistry, and wavelength-dependent field intensification. These
findings underscore the need to minimize nodules and tensile stress and carefully monitor craze
signatures in UV transport optics. The results were published in Applied Optics and selected as
an Editor’s Pick.[1]
Composite, hafnia-silica materials for high peak and average power laser systems.
Bottom Line – Composite HfO2-SiO2 materials show great promise for demanding
applications, arresting crystallinity and allowing for high temperature annealing.
 This work investigates the structure–property–performance relationships of ion-beam–
sputtered (IBS) HfO₂–SiO₂ composites relative to conventional HfO₂ films for high-power laser
optics. Co-sputtering a 50:50 mixture of HfO₂ and SiO₂, with Ar or Xe working gases, exploits the
amorphous-stabilizing role of SiO₂ to suppress crystallization even after 300–600 °C anneals.
Comprehensive metrology linked chemistry and nanostructure to absorption, stress evolution, and
pulsed-damage response in both single layers and HR stacks. This work was conducted during two
LDRDs; 21-FS-040 and 23-ERD-006.
 RBS confirmed near-ideal stoichiometry for pure HfO₂ and consistent Hf₀.₉Si₀.₁O₂
composition in composites. Composites exhibited wider bandgaps and remained amorphous after
annealing, while pure HfO₂ developed monoclinic domains. Xe-based composites achieved the
lowest absorption (~0.2 ppm in HRs after 550–600 °C anneals) and exhibited superior stress
relaxation compared to pure HfO₂, which suffered adhesion failures >350 °C.
 Damage testing revealed a strong decoupling between linear absorption and LIDT. For
nanosecond pulses, composite HRs improved with anneal, while pure HfO₂ degraded despite lower
absorption. Composites produced smaller, more stable craters, contrasting with large scalds in pure
HfO₂. Under femtosecond pulses, composites again outperformed, showing athermal breakdown
rather than defect-seeded ablation. Laser damage results are reported in Figure 3.

Figure 3. (a) Long-pulse (10 ns) laser damage threshold and (b) short-pulse (200 fs) laser damage threshold for high reflectors
made with pure HfO2 and HfO2-SiO2 composite materials.

 Overall, Xe-deposited HfO₂–SiO₂ composites suppress crystallization, minimize
absorption, and elevate both fs and ns LIDT, establishing practical processing windows (Xe gas,
400–550 °C anneals) for ppm-level absorption and durable HR coatings. These results advance

coating design for next-generation high-power laser systems and are in final review for
Optical Materials Express.
Study to understand the role nanobubbles play on laser damage at numerous wavelengths
Bottom Line- Nanobubbles prove to be a potent damage-prone precursor for all wavelengths
 Amorphous hafnium oxide (HfO₂) is a key material for both microelectronics and high-power
laser systems due to its wide bandgap, high refractive index, and strong dielectric properties. In
lasers, HfO₂/SiO₂ multilayer dielectric (MLD) coatings are widely used in optics such as transport
mirrors at NIF, where their damage resistance has supported inertial fusion milestones. However,
performance is strongly influenced by microstructural defects—particularly vacancies,
interstitials, and nanobubbles—that promote absorption and laser-induced damage.
 Ion beam sputtering (IBS) yields dense, high-quality hafnia coatings but can trap inert gas,
especially argon, forming nanobubbles that degrade performance. Substituting xenon suppresses
bubble formation and increases laser-induced damage thresholds (LIDT), especially under UV
nanosecond irradiation, by reducing defect-assisted absorption channels. Figure 4 reports the laser
damage results.

Figure 4: LIDT results at 50% damage probability are presented for wavelengths of 1064 nm, 532 nm, and 355 nm. The relative
difference is also plotted to highlight the variation in damage performance between the two types of hafnia layers .

 In this study, hafnia films deposited with Ar or Xe were systematically tested at 1064, 532, and
355 nm. Structural characterization confirmed amorphous films with fewer crystalline signatures
in Xe-based coatings, while AFM and TEM revealed higher nanobubble density and roughness in
Ar-based films. LIDT testing showed consistent improvements with Xe—~32% at 355 nm, ~21%
at 532 nm, and ~17% at 1064 nm.
 Microscopy of damage sites revealed redeposited crystalline hafnia in Ar-based films,
generating new defect states, whereas Xe-based films exhibited cleaner material removal and
reduced defect-driven absorption. Overall, xenon sputtering significantly improves smoothness,
minimizes defect populations, and enhances LIDT across the spectrum, offering a practical
pathway to more durable coatings for next-generation high-power laser systems.

 The manuscript of these findings is very mature, and we plan to push for publication in
FY26. The efforts here will be supported by Amira’s postdoctoral account and we have high
certainty of this work to be published.
The wavelength dependance and influence of film sub-stoichiometry on optical and laser
damage resistance of hafnia thin films.
Bottom Line - UV applications require highly oxidized films, whereas IR applications are
less reliant.
 This study evaluates the effect of oxygen flow during ion beam sputtering (IBS) of hafnia
(HfO₂) thin films on structure, optics, and laser-damage resistance. Oxygen vacancies in sub-
stoichiometric films create sub-bandgap states that reduce performance, while excessive oxygen
can induce porosity and stress. Films were deposited under flows from 0–45 sccm and tested
under nanosecond irradiation at 1064 and 355 nm.
 All films remained largely amorphous, though low-flow samples showed minor
monoclinic features. AFM revealed pinhole density decreased with oxygen flow, while optical
measurements showed refractive index reduction and saturation at ~25–35 sccm. Bandgaps
remained ~5.7 eV, though higher flows introduced defect states near 3.7 eV, suggesting stress-
driven oxygen interstitials.
 Laser testing showed strong wavelength dependence. At 1064 nm, LIDT was largely
insensitive to stoichiometry. At 355 nm, however, LIDT increased markedly with oxygen flow,
with the highest resistance at 45 sccm. This confirms UV damage is dominated by defect-related
absorption, while IR thresholds are comparatively robust. Figure 5 shows the laser damage
results.

Figure 5. The laser damage performance of films irradiated with (a) 1064 nm and (b) 355 nm (black, square data points) as
compared to the bulk film stoichiometry (red, triangle data points) as a function of oxygen flow rate.

 In multilayer designs, interface effects complicate this trend: low-flow films improve
stack adhesion and reduce interfacial sensitivity, while high-flow films, though optimal in UV
single layers, suffer from rough interfaces and stress that limit multilayer durability.
 In summary, oxygen stoichiometry is a critical parameter for tailoring HfO₂ coatings.
High O₂ flow benefits UV performance, whereas IR optics tolerate a wider range. For
multilayers, optimal flows must balance stoichiometry, stress, and interfacial quality to maximize
durability in high-fluence applications. This work is very close to publication, with likely
submission in Q1 of FY 26 as supported by Amira’s postdoc time account.
The impact of dual irradiation on laser damage resistance of ion beam sputtered hafnia coatings
Bottom Line – Dual irradiation, even with low fluence can greatly impact laser damage
performance.
 This study investigates dual-wavelength irradiation in multilayer dielectric (MLD)
coatings, a key challenge for next-generation laser systems in defense and inertial fusion energy.
In collaboration with CEA, LLNL evaluated ion beam–sputtered hafnia films under simultaneous
1064 and 355 nm exposure, benchmarking results against single-wavelength tests. Baseline
measurements showed excellent agreement between the two laboratories, confirming consistency
in deposition and testing.
 Under dual irradiation, a strong coupling effect emerged: even modest UV fluences
(~300 mJ/cm², ~10% of the UV LIDT) significantly reduced the infrared threshold. This
demonstrates that sub-threshold exposure at one wavelength can sensitize coatings to damage
from another—behavior absent in single-color data. The effect was observed in both directions
but was strongest when small UV fluences preceded IR exposure (Fig. 6).

 Figure 6. A graph summarizing dual-irradiation laser damage performance for hafnia single layers.

 These results establish dual irradiation as a critical factor in modeling laser damage. For
multi-wavelength, high-fluence facilities, harmonic interplay must be explicitly addressed in
both coating development and system-level design to ensure robust optical performance.
The effect of high temperature annealing and crystallinity on LIDT of hafnia-based MLD
coating performance at 355 nm.
Bottom Line – High temperature annealing greatly increases UV LIDT, even past the
crystallinity point.
 This study examines the effect of thermal annealing on hafnia (HfO₂) coatings and their
laser-induced damage threshold (LIDT) under 355 nm nanosecond irradiation. As-deposited IBS

HfO₂ films are amorphous, with high compressive stress but minimal defect absorption.
Traditionally, amorphous films were preferred to avoid grain-boundary scattering; however, our
results show that full crystallization can enhance LIDT by reducing defect density, relaxing
stress, and improving thermal transport. In contrast, partial crystallization increases defects and
stress, lowering LIDT.
 Literature comparisons support these findings: annealing enlarges grain size, raises
thermal conductivity, and improves LIDT in both single- and multilayer coatings, while poorly
controlled crystallization degrades performance. Crystallization temperatures depend on layer
thickness and deposition method, with thinner layers requiring higher activation energies.
 Mechanistically, annealing reduces oxygen vacancies, improves thermal conductivity,
and stabilizes the band structure, all of which suppress damage initiation. XRD confirmed
predominantly monoclinic phases after annealing, with no cubic signatures. Single layers
annealed at 600–700 °C achieved the highest measured UV LIDTs, particularly in Xe-based
coatings (Fig. 7). Multilayer coatings also improved but faced issues such as haze, scattering, and
delamination at 700 °C, partially mitigated by oxygen control and slower cooling.

 Figure 7. The laser damage threshold of hafnia single layer coatings as a function of annealing temperature.

 In summary, carefully tuned high-temperature annealing enhances HfO₂ coating
durability, overturning the assumption that crystallization is always detrimental. Optimized
protocols will be key to balancing laser resistance with optical clarity in next-generation UV
optics.
Wavelength-agile laser damage testing (OPO test stand)
Bottom Line – While the OPO was not installed in time for this LDRD, it is ready for use!
 The original plan for this LDRD was to use an optical parametric oscillator (OPO) laser to
understand the wavelength-dependance of the different classes of laser damage-prone precursors,
however due to unforeseen issues we were unable to use this test stand for any of the above work.
This was mainly due to issues with the procurement, issues with the company making the laser
and the laser used to pump the OPO. Even with all of these issues, the team was able to set up a
new laser damage test stand and have conducted some preliminary laser damage testing. Figure 22

shows recent LID results using the OPO at 532 nm as compared to older 532 nm damage testing.
This data implies strongly that the test stand is ready for use by the broader LLNL community.

 Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory is a leader in high-power laser systems and the
advanced optics that enable them. Maintaining this position requires deeper understanding of
defect-driven performance limits. This research directly advances the Optics and Optics
Manufacturing Technologies and Laser-Material Interaction (LMI) priorities, reinforcing LLNL’s
leadership in next-generation lasers and strengthening the Laboratory’s High Energy Density
Science mission, including inertial fusion energy.
 The project has had broad impact: advancing defect science through publications in high-
impact journals and invited international presentations, supporting major programs such as NIF,
MEC, HAPLS, DOD, and AVLIS, and recruiting two postdoctoral fellows who are now key
members of the OMST team. In addition, the establishment of a wavelength-agile damage test
station provides a unique capability to probe spectral responses of laser-damage precursors,
improving predictive models and mitigation strategies for high-power laser optics.
Conclusion
 This project significantly advanced understanding of wavelength-dependent laser damage
in hafnia-based coatings by examining precursor types, deposition methods, and annealing
treatments. The studies revealed that damage is governed by the interaction of photon energy,
defect chemistry, and microstructure. Craze lines were shown to be benign under infrared
irradiation but strong UV initiators due to field intensification. Xenon sputtering suppressed
nanobubbles, reducing defect populations and raising thresholds across all tested wavelengths.
Oxygen stoichiometry was found to have little effect in the infrared but strongly influenced UV
resistance, with higher oxygen flows improving LIDT.

 Composite HfO₂–SiO₂ coatings maintained amorphous structures during annealing,
reducing absorption and stress, while pure hafnia demonstrated that full crystallization—long
considered detrimental—can instead enhance LIDT by lowering defect density and improving
thermal transport. These findings overturn prevailing assumptions and demonstrate that defect type,
stoichiometry, and crystallinity impose wavelength-specific effects not captured by traditional
models.
 Together, the results provide a predictive framework for tailoring deposition and annealing
to produce durable coatings for high-power, multi-wavelength laser systems. They directly support
critical national facilities such as NIF, MEC, HAPLS, and DPAL, and inform future UV-driven
fusion and high-average-power architectures.
 The project also established a wavelength-agile test stand, though systematic use remains
a future opportunity. Observed blister delaminations during high-temperature annealing highlight
another avenue for follow-on research, with direct relevance to enabling next-generation MLD
optics. By any metric, this LDRD was highly successful.
