Introduction
Refrigerants are found in nearly every building. They are the workhorses of most heating,
ventilating, and air-conditioning (HVAC) and refrigeration systems. They play a crucial role in
air-conditioning systems, refrigeration equipment, water coolers, ice makers, dehumidifiers, heat
pumps, and more. Figure 1 shows a sample of common locations for refrigerants in and around a
facility. Some equipment, such as refrigerators and ice makers, come with refrigerant
preinstalled at the time of equipment manufacture, while other equipment such as variable
refrigerant flow HVAC systems may require the refrigerant to be added during equipment
installation.

 Figure 1. Example refrigerant locations throughout a facility.
 Credit: Marjorie Schott, NLR, with components from Pond5.com
Regardless of the application, all refrigerants share a common purpose—to move heat from one
location to another. In practice, there are a variety of chemicals that do this, and their properties
can be dramatically different. ASHRAE Standard 34 1 defines toxicity and flammability
properties for refrigerants (Figure 2). Some refrigerants have been or are being phased out of use
or are not yet commonly adopted across equipment types. In addition, some refrigerants work
better at certain temperatures than others.

Safety classification. Because refrigerants are used in buildings, their relative toxicity and
flammability are important safety indicators. These parameters have standard classifications
described in Figure 2.

Figure 2. Toxicity and flammability classifications of refrigerants as defined by ASHRAE Standard

Toxicity is described using alphabetical designations. Class A indicates refrigerants with low
toxicity and class B indicates refrigerants with high toxicity. Flammability is classified according
to flame spread speeds, and ASHRAE Standard 34 uses this information to characterize
flammability. One (1) indicates no flame propagation, 2L indicates low flammability, 2 indicates
flammability, and 3 indicates high flammability.

Common refrigerants. There are many common refrigerants, and Table 1 describes a sampling
of them as well as their classifications and types, ozone depletion potential (ODP), global
warming potential (GWP), and current status or use. Natural refrigerants are a class of
refrigerants that are not hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), can exist naturally, and are not derived as a
manufactured product.

GWP values reference the atmospheric heating impact relative to carbon dioxide (CO2), which
has a GWP value of one. In other words, one pound of R-22 has the same impact as 1810 pounds
(approximately one ton) of CO2. Newer refrigerants have GWP values closer to one or zero.

 Because of the wide variety of refrigerant applications and classifications, it is important
 to develop a refrigerant management plan to track locations, types, and quantities of
 refrigerants throughout a facility or a portfolio of facilities.

refrigerants have ODP values of zero or close to zero. Note that the scale of measurement is
different between GWP and ODP.

Reasons for tracking refrigerants. Because of the wide variety of refrigerant applications and
classifications, it is important to develop a refrigerant management plan to track locations, types,
and quantities of refrigerants throughout a facility or a portfolio of facilities. Building owners
track refrigerants for several reasons, including:

 • Reducing cost and optimizing performance. Each piece of equipment is designed to
 use a specific amount of refrigerant. The amount of refrigerant is also referred to as the
 refrigerant charge. The refrigerant is at a different pressure than the air around the unit;
 therefore, it is prone to leak through worn seals, poor piping joints, and valves. Without
 the correct charge, the equipment will not operate at the rated efficiency, which can
 increase operational costs and shorten equipment lifetimes. There is also cost associated
 with adding the lost refrigerant, including both the service to replace and the expense of
 purchasing the added refrigerant.
 • Mitigating risk. Tracking the quantity of refrigerants currently in use and the anticipated
 lifetimes of equipment using them will help reduce risk related to increased maintenance
 and operational costs of refrigerant leakage. Some refrigerants are blends that contain
 different refrigerants. When they leak, the different refrigerants leak at different rates.
 Just adding the original refrigerant may or may not be effective, resulting in more repair
 costs. A refrigerant management plan can help track equipment issues and allow owners
 to be proactive to avoid costly repairs, equipment replacement, and, in some cases, local
 compliance issues, including refrigerant disposal.
 • Complying with protocols. The 1987 Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the
 Ozone Layer is a global agreement that phases out ozone-depleting substances. The most
 recent amendment to the Montreal Protocol, the Kigali Amendment, calls for the
 phasedown of HFCs by 2047. The American Innovation and Manufacturing (AIM) Act
 2020 HFC Phasedown 5 outlines the phasedown of widely used HFCs over a 15-year
 schedule. The Act introduces technology-specific restrictions for the transition. It does
 not require discontinuing systems currently in use, but it does raise awareness about the
 potentially higher cost of legacy refrigerants.

Decisions about equipment design and form factors (the physical design and layout of the
equipment 6) can reduce refrigerant charge without impacting performance, reduce leakage, and
minimize human exposure to dangerous chemicals. 7 For example, a monoblock system (e.g.,
self-contained refrigeration systems that integrate both the condensing and evaporating units
within a single piece of equipment) will have less refrigerant charge, and refrigerant is installed
by the manufacturer at the factory. Conversely, VRF systems will have more refrigerant charge
and will have field-installed equipment that has more opportunity to leak. Knowing where
refrigerants are in the building and the types of refrigerants in each application will contribute to
more efficient operations, lower costs, and overall risk reduction.

How To Use This Guide
This document can help support a portfolio-wide refrigerant action plan. Through a series of
steps, it outlines the information needed to establish a refrigerant inventory, develops refrigerant
management goals, and prioritizes actions to achieve those goals. An effective plan measures the
current state of refrigerants in a building and provides next steps. These next steps move the
organization towards energy goals while strengthening the organization’s business case. The
accompanying document [https://www.nlr.gov/docs/fy26osti/95392ActionPlanWorksheet.docx]
provides a blank worksheet to help stakeholders document the inventory, goals, and suggested
actions in the following five steps:

 1. Provide an organizational profile
 2. Establish refrigerant management goals
 3. Complete a refrigeration equipment inventory
 4. Develop a refrigerant charge maintenance inventory
 5. Take action.

Step 1. Provide an Organizational Profile
Date: April 2026

Organization Name: ABC Investment Trust

Point of Contact (name, email): Jane Smith, Jane@ABCInvestmentTrust.com

Step 2. Establish Refrigerant Management Goals
Describe your refrigerant management goals:

 • What are your organization’s refrigerant-related goals? Why is it important for your
 organization to track refrigerants? How does it improve your organization’s bottom line?
 The best goals can be measured to determine whether they have been met. In addition,
 goals can be time-bound to determine whether a goal has been achieved by a specific
 date. Do you have management support? Do you have partners among your installers and
 suppliers? Who needs to be engaged? Example goals could include:
 o Service equipment annually. Add refrigerant as necessary to maintain the
 specified efficiency. Well-maintained equipment that operates effectively and
 efficiently can:
 ̶ Increase equipment service lives
 ̶ Keep occupants comfortable and productive
 ̶ Reduce operating costs, both in terms of maintenance and operational cost
 ̶ Minimize equipment downtime.
 o Track refrigerant losses and report on the impacts of refrigerant releases as part of
 corporate reporting. Catching leaks quickly saves money and protects equipment.

 5

 o Minimize leaks and use smaller quantities of refrigerant.
 • What methods, tools, processes, and reporting platforms does your organization use to
 track refrigerants? How will data be collected and where will the information be stored?
 Who should have access to this information and in what form? Examples might include:
 o Tracking small appliances that contain refrigerants
 o Tracking maintenance records for equipment servicing, including the amount of
 refrigerant added and the location of leak(s) 8
 o Identifying the person or team responsible for collecting the information
 o Determining what software will be used to track information.
 • How do these goals translate into facility-level targets? What are your implementation
 strategies to achieve these targets? Example goals might include reducing the total
 amount of refrigerant across a building portfolio or reducing refrigerant leaks across the
 portfolio by a certain percentage.

Step 3. Complete a Refrigeration Equipment Inventory
To address refrigerant management goals, conduct a refrigerant inventory of the equipment to
help determine current refrigerant use and future needs. The first and most time-consuming part
of this step is a systematic walk-through of the site with a focus on the equipment, also referred
to as assets, covered in Table 2. Ideally, each asset would have a permanent tag (see Figure 3) to
identify it easily. In addition, as refrigerant consuming equipment is purchased (e.g., a new
HVAC unit), it should be tagged and inventoried, which may require coordination between
departments. This list can be created and maintained by facilities, accounting, or another
appropriate office and be updated by a facilities employee.

The information in Table 2 can be stored in software designed for refrigerant management or
asset management, or in a spreadsheet. Although Table 2 is designed for an individual building, a
column for building names could be added so that assets in multiple buildings can be organized
by, for example, refrigerant type or equipment age.

Some equipment has multiple refrigerant loops, often called refrigerant circuits. For example, a
two-stage air conditioner may have two compressors and two refrigerant circuits—essentially it
is two air conditioners housed in the same unit. Ideally, each circuit would be identified,
maintained, and tracked separately as different maintenance assets, a strategy that also helps
isolate problems when a system component has repeated issues.

For example, if a piece of equipment reports two compressor failures one month apart, it could
be an indication that both compressors failed in the unit or that the same compressor failed twice.
The latter should be studied carefully because it is a premature failure, while the former might be
normal based on the life of the equipment. Tracking by circuit rather than by piece of equipment
can help make this determination.

Step 4. Develop a Refrigerant Charge Maintenance Inventory
Step 4 is the core of the refrigerant tracking process. It describes maintenance functions during
which facility employees or contractors check the equipment refrigerant charge and perform
maintenance if the charge is incorrect. In many cases, refrigerant is only checked when
equipment is malfunctioning. If the refrigerant can be recharged, maintenance personnel can add
refrigerant during regular servicing and make a note of it in Table 3. In addition, equipment can
be routinely checked and a maintenance record created for each piece of equipment.

For equipment that cannot be repaired, workers can make a note in Table 3, dispose of the asset
appropriately, 9 purchase a new one, and add it to the inventory. This is an opportunity to review
goals about refrigerant selection and choose equipment that meets the organization’s refrigerant
goals (see Step 2).

Step 5. Take Action
In Step 5, evaluate the current situation to determine whether it meets the goals identified in
previous steps. This step also contains information to help develop a plan that will help meet the
refrigerant goals set in Step 2.

How can you verify equipment is working properly?
Work with manufacturers to create a checklist for checking and maintaining equipment.

Set up a schedule for checking and maintaining equipment.

Identify personnel to execute checklist.

Do you have equipment that is always problematic (requiring refrigerant to be
added every year, for example)? Are there systems in the portfolio that are more
likely to leak or have recurring leaks or failures?
If so, consider locating the refrigerant leak and fixing it or determining whether it makes sense to
replace the equipment before its end of life.

Do you have equipment that is nearing its end of life? What happens to the
equipment at the end of life? Is the remaining refrigerant in retired equipment
recycled or thrown away?
If you have equipment nearing end of life, evaluate replacements that use refrigerants consistent
with your goals. Also consider equipment based on the volume of refrigerant—different
configurations of equipment will use different amounts of refrigerant. It is common to replace
equipment at end of life. Examples of equipment that are typically replaced include ice makers,
refrigerators, and other small, self-contained equipment, all of which are available with a variety
of refrigerants. If that equipment has field installed refrigerant lines (such as split systems), the
refrigerant lines may need to be replaced or flushed if the new equipment uses a different
refrigerant.

 9

 Much of the system design and its form factor can be done as a long-term planning
 process such that when equipment fails, a replacement plan is available that makes
 implementation easier.

For HVAC equipment, verify that the equipment is properly sized. Oversizing equipment (which
traditionally has been done for many installations) will yield decreased efficiencies, increased
capital costs, and short cycling that increases maintenance costs, in addition to requiring larger
quantities of refrigerant. It also can cause humidity control issues. When replacing assets,
investigate system configurations that use less refrigerant. Much of the system design and its
form factor can be developed as a long-term planning process such that when equipment fails, a
replacement plan is available that makes implementation easier.

Do you have equipment that uses refrigerants that are not hydrocarbons or
carbon dioxide?
If yes, develop a disposal plan to be implemented at the equipment end of life. When the
equipment fails, having a solution mapped out makes decisions easier at a critical time.

Other Resources
Larger systems with ODP refrigerants require federal reporting. Limiting these refrigerants
during the planning process will minimize these reporting requirements. Federal reporting
requirements 10 include that:

 • Owners or operators of appliances that contain 50 or more pounds of ozone-depleting
 refrigerant must keep servicing records documenting the date and type of service as well
 as the quantity of refrigerant added.
 • Owners or operators must maintain records of the leak inspections and tests performed to
 verify repairs of leaking appliances.
 • Owners or operators must submit a report to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency
 for any appliance containing 50 or more pounds of ozone-depleting refrigerant that leaks
 125% or more of the full charge in a calendar year. This report must describe efforts to
 identify leaks and repair the appliance. Reports are due March 1 for an appliance that
 leaked more than 125% of its full charge in the prior year.

Glossary
Term Definition

chlorofluorocarbon Chlorine-containing compound that is used as a refrigerant.
(CFC)
circuit A refrigerant loop consisting of a compressor, condenser, and metering
 device along with associated piping and valves that contain a refrigerant.
 Some equipment that is multistage has multiple circuits—each one is
 operated and maintained separately and therefore should be tracked
 independently even if it is in the same piece of equipment.
form factor The physical design and layout of the refrigeration unit, including its size,
 shape, and how it's accessed.
global warming Measure of warming impact of a compound relative to the same quantity
potential (GWP) of carbon dioxide (CO2) over a certain number of years. A refrigerant
 with a global warming potential of 1,000 will have 1,000 times the heat-
 trapping potential of CO2.
hydrofluorocarbon Fluorinated-based compound that is an effective refrigerant because it has
(HFC) properties that allow the phase change to occur at relatively low pressures
 and temperatures that are appropriate for heating, ventilating, and air-
 conditioning applications.
monoblock system A self-contained refrigeration unit in which all major components are
 housed in a single, integrated piece of equipment.
natural refrigerants A class of refrigerants that are non-synthetic, can exist naturally, and are
 not derived as a manufactured product. Examples include carbon dioxide,
 hydrocarbons, propane, and ammonia.
ozone depletion Measure of ozone degradation of a compound compared to
potential (ODP) trichlorofluoromethane (R-11), which has an ozone depletion potential of
 1.
refrigerant A two-phase working fluid that has thermodynamic properties that allow
 it to move heat from one location to another.
refrigerant charge Amount of refrigerant in a refrigeration system.

refrigeration Process that uses refrigerant to move heat from one space to another.
split system A heating, ventilating, and air-conditioning system that has both an indoor
 and outdoor unit, requiring field-installed refrigerant piping connecting
 the indoor and outdoor units.
working fluid A liquid or gas that transfers heat.
