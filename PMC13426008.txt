Airway Management and Retrieval of a Large Ingested Foreign Body: A Case Report
Authors: Emma De Ravin, Leonard E Estephan, Dev Amin, Kathleen Tibbetts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLarge foreign body ingestion in patients with bulimia nervosa requires rapid airway‐focused retrieval and multidisciplinary care; awake fiberoptic intubation with video‐laryngoscopic extraction can safely avoid invasive surgery while psychiatric evaluation addresses recurrence risk.Keywords: bulimia nervosa, case report, foreign body ingestion, foreign body of the aerodigestive tract

Introduction

Patients presenting with foreign body (FB) aspiration and ingestion are commonly managed by otolaryngologists. Symptoms and clinical acuity vary, ranging from minimal symptomatology to dysphagia, odynophagia, inability to tolerate secretions, or respiratory distress. Prompt intervention is indicated to prevent significant complications such as anoxia and/or death. A unique subset of FB ingestions occurs in patients with psychiatric comorbidities such as bulimia nervosa, in which patients may use objects to induce emesis. While up to 80% of smaller ingested foreign objects pass spontaneously, ingestion of large and/or rigid instruments may require intervention. This occurrence in the setting of bulimia nervosa is rare and presents challenges not only in retrieval for airway protection, but also in psychiatric management [1, 2]. Here, we aim to describe the successful multidisciplinary management of a rare case of large foreign body ingestion in a patient with bulimia nervosa and to highlight key considerations for airway management, foreign body retrieval, and psychiatric care.

Case History and Examination

A 34‐year‐old female with a history of anxiety and bulimia nervosa presented to the emergency department (ED) after ingestion of a spatula during a purging episode. The patient endorsed a longstanding history of restricting and purging behavior with a previous psychiatric hospitalization approximately 10 years prior. The patient's condition was managed with bupropion obtained through an online prescription site. The patient had undergone multiple prior trials of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), lamotrigine, and gabapentin without symptomatic improvement. The patient denied any prior FB ingestions or known gastrointestinal complications related to bulimia. On initial presentation to the ED, the patient's vital signs were stable on room air; however, the patient was unable to tolerate their secretions and maintained a tripod position.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Initial laboratory studies were notable for a leukocytosis to 13 (B/L) and hypophosphatemia to 1.7 (mg/dL), and urine drug screen positive for benzodiazepines; all other laboratory values (including complete blood count, basic metabolic panel, thyroid labs, Vitamin B12 and folate) were unremarkable. An oropharyngeal FB was visualized on examination via the oral cavity (Figure 1). Chest and cervical spine plain radiographs were obtained (Figure 2), which demonstrated “a metallic hyperdense rod projecting over the neck and mediastinum measuring 22.5 cm in the craniocaudal dimension and 8 mm in width. The lower extent of the rod extends to the level of the mid‐mediastinum at the level of T8 from C4 above.” Nasopharyngolaryngoscopy demonstrated the flat end of a spatula lodged in the postcricoid region arising from the esophageal inlet and a widely patent glottic airway.

The patient was emergently taken to the operating room, where they underwent successful awake transoral fiberoptic intubation followed by video‐laryngoscopy‐assisted retrieval of the spatula using alligator forceps (Figure 3). Post‐retrieval flexible esophagoscopy demonstrated no strictures, mucosal abrasions, lacerations, or perforations of the stomach or esophagus. The patient's stomach was suctioned to remove the remaining food matter to prevent vomiting/aspiration during emergence, and the patient was extubated uneventfully. Outcome and Follow‐Up

Postoperatively, the patient was admitted for observation with psychiatry consultation. Psychiatry discussed the risk of refeeding syndrome and malnutrition with the patient, as well as the increased risk of seizures with the concurrent use of bupropion in the setting of recurrent purging behavior. The patient declined inpatient eating disorder unit treatment, but was amenable to and arranged for robust outpatient treatment and follow‐up. The patient was observed closely on postoperative day one and successfully advanced to a soft and bite‐sized diet prior to discharge.

Discussion

Foreign body ingestion in adults is often associated with psychiatric illness, cognitive impairment, or substance use disorders [3]. Among patients with psychiatric comorbidities, those with bulimia nervosa often engage in repetitive purging behaviors, which may include the use of foreign objects to trigger the gag reflex and induce emesis. Prior reports describe accidental ingestion of cutlery and toothbrushes in this setting; however, reports of large, rigid FBs—such as the spatula in our described case—are rare [1, 2].

After diagnostic laboratory and imaging studies (plain radiographs, with or without the addition of computed tomography imaging), the approach to FB removal is largely dictated by the size and type of object, its location, duration of impaction, and presence of symptoms or complications. Emergent endoscopic removal is commonly the first‐line intervention for cases of symptomatic large FB ingestion, and is recommended to occur within two hours (and no later than six hours) in cases such as ours with complete esophageal obstruction [4]. Despite the size of the foreign object, our case was successfully managed with awake fiberoptic intubation and video‐laryngoscopy‐assisted retrieval, demonstrating that careful airway planning and visualization can mitigate the need for more invasive procedures.

From a psychiatry standpoint, obtaining a careful history and close monitoring and follow‐up in the postoperative period are imperative. Patients with bulimia nervosa have a heightened risk of intraoperative complications due to chronic mucosal injury from repeated vomiting, delayed gastric emptying, and possible nutritional deficiencies [5]. Further, the risk of recurrence is high, highlighting the importance of adequate psychiatric input [2, 3].

The management of symptomatic large FB ingestion in a patient with bulimia nervosa requires a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach that addresses the acute airway, medical, and procedural risks, the chronic complications of bulimia and purging behaviors, and the high potential for recurrence.
