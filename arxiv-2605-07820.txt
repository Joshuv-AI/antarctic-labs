Scaling Categorical Flow Maps
Oscar Davis, Anastasiia Filippova, Pierre Ablin, Victor Turrisi, Amitis Shidani, Marco Cuturi, Louis Béthune

Abstract
Continuous diffusion and flow matching models could represent a powerful alternative to autoregressive approaches for language modelling (LM), as they unlock a host of advantages currently reserved for continuous modalities, including accelerated sampling and tilting. Recently, several works have demonstrated the possibility of generating discrete data continuously by a simple flow matching process between a Gaussian and the one-hot encoded data distribution. They have further shown the feasibility of accelerated sampling via Categorical Flow Maps (CFMs), resulting in competitive sample quality in the few-step regime. However, this method had only been evaluated at relatively modest scales ( < 1 <1 B), leaving the question of its scalability completely open. In this article, we train a 1.7 1.7 B-parameter base flow model on 2.1 2.1 T tokens and self-distill it into a CFM that generates diverse, high-quality text in as few as 4 4 inference steps while maintaining near-data-level token entropy. Furthermore, we introduce a likelihood bound for CFMs in the semi-discrete setting, and show that they can be used to score the model on standard LM benchmarks, achieving results in the same range as discrete diffusion methods. Finally, we uncover some of the challenges that arise from training these models at scale, and we provide prescriptive insights on loss weighting and time scheduling.

Background

We denote by
V
the vocabulary of our target distribution,
p_{\mathrm{data}}
, which has support
V^{L}
for a context length
L\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
. For parameters
\theta
,
p_{\theta}
refers to our model distribution.

2.1
Autoregressive Modelling

AR models approach language modelling by decomposing the joint distribution over a sequence into a product of conditional distributions using the chain rule of probability:

p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_{1:L})=\prod_{i=1}^{L}p(x_{i}\mid x_{<i}),

(2.1)

where
x_{<i}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1})
denotes the prefix preceding token
x_{i}
. This factorisation converts the problem of modelling a sequence into a next-token prediction problem. A parametric model
p_{\theta}
is trained to approximate these conditional distributions by maximising the likelihood of observed sequences in the training corpus.

2.2
Stochastic Interpolants

Figure 2
:
Categorical Flow Maps on
\Delta^{d}
, each vertex corresponding to a token. The
ODE trajectory
continuously transports noisy
x_{0}
towards data. The flow map
X_{0,1}(x_{0})
traverses the path in one step.

We model the generative problem as a dynamic transport of measure
(
30
;
1
;
31
)
. Starting from a prior
p_{0}
, we evolve the distribution through the following ordinary differential equation (ODE) on the sample trajectory level:

\dot{x}_{t}=b_{t}(x_{t}),\qquad x_{0}\sim p_{0}

(2.2)

for some drift
b:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
chosen so that
\mathrm{Law}(x_{1})=p_{1}\equiv p_{\mathrm{data}}
, enabling sampling from
p_{1}
by integrating the ODE. To this end, consider a
stochastic interpolant
, which is a map
I
defined by

I_{t}(x_{0},x_{1})=(1-\alpha_{t})x_{0}+\alpha_{t}x_{1},

(2.3)

satisfying
I_{0}(x_{0},x_{1})=x_{0}\sim p_{0}
and
I_{1}(x_{0},x_{1})=x_{1}\sim p_{1}
for some differentiable schedule
\alpha
. It defines a probability path between the two distributions of interest. By defining the drift as

\forall 0\leq t\leq 1,\qquad b_{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\dot{I}_{t}\mid I_{t}=x\right]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\dot{\alpha}_{t}x_{1}-\dot{\alpha}_{t}x_{0}\mid I_{t}=x\Big],

(2.4)

we can ensure that
\mathrm{Law}(x_{t})=\mathrm{Law}(I_{t})
at all times, and in particular at
t=0
and
t=1
. Thus, learning to generate from the data distribution,
p_{1}
, reduces to learning the drift:

\min_{\theta}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert v^{\theta}_{t}(I_{t})-\dot{I}_{t}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t,

(2.5)

where
v^{\theta}
is our modelled vector field, and the expectation is taken over
I_{t}
. We can also directly model an endpoint
denoiser
, since
v_{t}(x)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[x_{1}\mid I_{t}=x]-x}{1-\alpha_{t}}
.

2.3
Flow Map Matching

Integrating the learnt vector field can be costly, requiring many function evaluations (NFEs) to obtain high quality samples. Instead, it is possible to learn a flow map,
X:[0,1]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which brings any solution of the probability flow from any time
s
to another time
t

(
4
;
5
;
18
)
. Formally, the flow map satisfies the following
jump condition
:

\forall 0\leq s,t\leq 1,\qquad X_{s,t}(x_{s})=x_{t}.

(2.6)

Reparameterising
X
as

X_{s,t}(x)=x+(t-s)v_{s,t}(x)

(2.7)

for some two-time vector field
v:[0,1]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can characterise the flow map via three key identities, the presentation of which we defer to
Appendix B
. Alongside those, it can be shown that

\forall 0\leq t\leq 1,\qquad v_{t,t}(x)=b_{t}(x),

(2.8)

which relates the flow map to the drift of the probability flow. By enforcing simultaneously this tangent condition through (
2.5
) together with one of the consistency characterisations, we can learn the unique flow map of the ODE by modelling
v_{s,t}^{\theta}
. Sampling becomes a single pass:
X_{0,1}(x_{0})\sim p_{1}
.

Categorical Flow Maps.

Introduced in
42
;
28
;
39
, CFMs extend the flow map matching framework to the discrete case. Reparameterise the two-time vector field as

v_{s,t}(x)=\frac{\pi_{s,t}(x)-x}{1-s}

(2.9)

where
\pi:[0,1]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a “partial” denoiser. The tangent condition requires enforcing (
2.5
), which reduces to a simple cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diag}}(\theta)\coloneqq-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\lvert V\rvert}I_{1}^{(k)}\log\pi_{t,t}^{\theta,(k)}(I_{t})\right],

(2.10)

and the self-distillation objectives become analogous identities but on the endpoint prediction level
(
28
;
39
)
. We consider in this work the empirically best-performing losses. The first one is the progressive self-distillation loss (PSD):

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\operatorname{KL}\!\left(\alpha_{s,u,t}\pi^{\theta}_{s,u}(I_{s})+(1-\alpha_{s,u,t})\pi^{\theta}_{u,t}(X^{\theta}_{s,u}(I_{s}))\,\middle\|\,\pi_{s,t}^{\theta}(I_{s})\right)\Big],

(2.11)

with
\alpha_{s,u,t}\in[0,1]
, which effectively amounts to a continuous-time shortcut model
(
16
)
. The second objective is the stabilised Eulerian-logit self-distillation (ESD)
(
39
)
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ESD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\operatorname{KL}\!\left(T^{\theta}_{s,t}(I_{s})\,\middle\|\,\pi_{s,t}^{\theta}(I_{s})\right)\Big],

(2.12)

where
T^{\theta}:[0,1]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the teacher distribution, given by

T^{\theta}_{s,t}(I_{s})\coloneqq\mathrm{softmax}\Big(z_{s,s}^{\theta}(I_{s})-\log\left((1-t)\mathbf{1}-(1-s)(t-s)\delta_{s,t}^{\theta}(I_{s})\right)\Big),

(2.13)

with
\delta_{s,t}^{\theta}(x)=D_{s}z_{s,t}^{\theta}(x)-\langle\pi_{s,t}^{\theta}(x),D_{s}z_{s,t}^{\theta}(x)\rangle\mathbf{1}
, and
z^{\theta}:[0,1]^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the logits of
\pi^{\theta}
, for
\pi_{s,t}^{\theta}(x)=\mathrm{softmax}(z_{s,t}^{\theta}(x))
. While ESD produced the best numbers of
39
, our experiments at large scale have exhibited relatively unstable training. Due to that, in the main body of the paper, we focus on the PSD results. The ESD results are deferred to
Section

D.6.2
.

Pretraining a flow

Figure 3

Mixture schedules for varying

Current best-performing approaches for CFMs rely on a two-stage training
(
28
;
39
)
: in the first stage, a simple cross-entropy denoiser is trained (see
equation

2.10
); in the second stage, that denoiser is self-distilled into a CFM (see
equations

2.12
and
2.11
). The two-stage approach is necessary, because the self-distillation objectives rely on a biased signal from the model itself, so applying them from the start degrades final performance. Moreover, self-distillation objectives are computationally demanding (requiring multiple forward passes per gradient step), making their optimisation over the entire training trajectory prohibitively expensive.

In this section, we investigate the importance of three components in the design space of CFM pretraining: the time schedule mixture, the adaptive loss weight, and the random “unmasking” of inputs. Finally, we sweep over optimiser hyperparameters, and discuss the possibility of their transfer to larger scales.

3.1
Design space of CFMs

Time scheduling.

28
;
39
show the importance of the time schedule for CFMs. Instead of the linear interpolant schedule, a monotone map
\gamma:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, coined the “error decoding” schedule
(
44
;
40
)
, significantly improves the final model’s performance. This schedule adapts the time so that the probability of incorrectly decoding the current point,
P(\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}I_{t}\neq\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}I_{1})
, grows linearly in
t
, instead of exhibiting a sharp phase transition of the linear schedule. They report that a convex combination of it with the linear schedule,
\alpha_{t}=(1-\lambda)t+\lambda\gamma_{t}
, further improves the generation quality. While
39
train with
\lambda=0.9
,
28
use it only at inference with
\lambda\in\{0.5,0.75,1\}
. We ablate over this mixture choice.

Adaptive loss weighting.

39
adopt the adaptive loss weight of
19
: for any objective of the form
\operatorname{KL}\!\left(p\,\middle\|\,q\right)
, they compute
\Delta(x)=\lVert p(x)-q(x)\rVert_{2}^{2}
, and reweight by
w(x)=\mathop{\mathrm{sg}}\left((\Delta(x)+c)^{-r}\right)
, with constants
c=10^{-2}
and
r=1/2
on the diagonal loss. The resulting objective is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{w}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\Big[w(x)\operatorname{KL}\!\left(p\,\middle\|\,q\right)\Big].

(3.1)

This upweights low-error regions and downweights high-error ones in order to stabilise training. We also assess the importance of this weight.

Random unmasking of input sequence

The models are by default only trained on generating full, unconditional sequences, but final downstream applications typically require conditional generation given prompts. To match this setting, we follow
13
, and with a probability
p
unmask a random prefix of the text, sampled from
\mathcal{U}[1,L/d]
, with
d\mid L
. We vary both
p
and
d
.

3.3
Hyperparameter transfer

Searching for optimal hyperparameters directly at large scale is expensive. Therefore, it is common to rely on zero-shot hyperparameter transfer rules to identify optimal hyperparameters when scaling the model size, batch size, and token horizon
(
56
;
12
;
33
)
. We attempted to leverage Complete(d)P
(

)
by performing a hyperparameter sweep on a

4.3
Likelihood-based benchmarks

Semi-discrete ELBO.

Standard LM benchmarks require scoring answer choices by likelihood, which is not directly available from a flow-based model. To bridge this gap, we derive the following ELBO for the semi-discrete setting, extended from
27
. We denote by
\mathbf{e}_{x}
the one-hot encoding of
x\in V
.

Proposition 4.1

(Semi-discrete ELBO)
.

Let
x\in V
be a category. Let
\ell_{\mathrm{CE}}
denote the cross-entropy loss. The following bound holds on the log likelihood of the model
p_{\theta}
:

-\log p_{\theta}(x)\leq\mathbb{E}_{I_{t}}\left[\frac{2\dot{\alpha}_{t}\alpha_{t}}{(1-\alpha_{t})^{3}}\ell_{\mathrm{CE}}\left(I_{1},\pi_{t,t}^{\theta}(I_{t})\right)\mid I_{1}=\mathbf{e}_{x}\right]+\ell_{\mathrm{CE}}(x,\pi_{1,1}^{\theta}(\mathbf{e}_{x})).

(4.1)

The proof is deferred to
Section

C.2
. The semi-discrete ELBO spares us the reverse integration due to the instantaneous change of variables
(
20
;
7
;
30
)
, which, in this setting, is particularly costly, as its evaluation additionally requires an integration over
\Delta^{d}_{i}\coloneqq\{p\in\Delta^{d}:\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}p_{j}=i\}
and as the model size is large. We discuss in more detail the issue of likelihood estimation for our setting in
Appendix

C
.

LM Harness.

We evaluate CFMs on standard multiple-choice question answering (MCQA) benchmarks using the ELBO estimator above.
We compare against an autoregressive baseline that we train under the same setup, and against the baselines reproduced by
45

(

Limitations

CFMs require materialising full
L\times\lvert V\rvert
continuous-state trajectory matrices for all steps. At our scale (
L=2048
,
\lvert V\rvert\approx 100\text{k}
), this is manageable given sufficient hardware (
256
H100s), but the cost grows linearly in context length and vocabulary size. We absorb this bottleneck through compute and efficient cross-entropy implementation
(
54
)
, without solving it. Moreover, CFMs also present challenges in principled hyperparameter transfer. Unlike standard transformers, CFMs condition on continuous times via dedicated layers, which fall outside the parametrisations covered by Complete(d)P
(
33
)

\mu

(

)
is left for future work.

7
Conclusion

We have shown that Categorical Flow Maps scale competitively to the
1.7
B-parameter regime. After self-distillation, they produce diverse, high-quality text in as few as
4
function evaluations while maintaining near-data-level token entropy, pushing the quality vs. throughput Pareto front beyond discrete diffusion baselines. Our semi-discrete ELBO places the model in the same ballpark as other non-autoregressive methods, though a gap remains with approaches trained directly on ELBO maximisation. These results suggest that continuous-state generation is a viable path for discrete data at LLM scale, and that the broader flow map toolbox (guidance, tilting, reward fine-tuning) is now within reach for language modelling.

Appendix B
Extended presentation of flow maps

B.1
Formal presentation

As described in the background section, flow maps satisfy the tangent condition and are characterised by three additional equivalent properties. We first begin by proving the tangent condition.

Lemma B.1

(Tangent condition)
.

For any solution of the probability flow,
(x_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
,

\forall 0\leq t\leq 1,\qquad v_{t,t}(x_{t})=b_{t}(x_{t}).

(B.1)

Proof.

Consider the partial derivative with respect to
t
of the flow map applied at a solution of the probability flow:

\partial_{t}X_{s,t}(x_{s})=v_{s,t}(x_{s})+(t-s)\partial_{t}v_{s,t}(x_{s}).

(B.2)

By definition, we also know that

\partial_{t}X_{s,t}(x_{s})=\partial_{t}x_{t}=b_{t}(x_{t}).

(B.3)

Combining the two relations and taking the limit as
s
tends to
t
, we arrive at the desired conclusion.
∎

The tangent condition is not surprising if we consider the two-time vector field to be the integral of the drift of the flow, it can also be derived by using Leibniz’s integral rule.

We can now present the three characterisations from
4
;
5
.

Proposition B.2
.

A flow map of the probability flow satisfies the following three conditions, for any
0\leq s,u,t,\leq 1
and any solution of the probability flow:

•

Lagrangian condition
.
\partial_{t}X_{s,t}(x_{s})=v_{t,t}(X_{s,t}(x_{s}))
.

•

Semigroup condition
.
X_{u,t}\circ X_{s,u}=X_{s,t}
.

•

Eulerian condition
.
\partial_{s}X_{s,t}(x_{s})+\nabla X_{s,t}(x_{s})v_{s,s}(x_{s})=0
.

Proof.

For the Lagrangian condition, it suffices to recall that
X_{s,t}(x_{s})=x_{t}
, which implies, by taking the partial derivative with respect to time, that
\partial_{t}X_{s,t}(x_{s})=v_{t,t}(x_{t})=v_{t,t}(X_{s,t}(x_{s}))
.

The semigroup condition is trivial.

The Eulerian condition comes from the fact that
X_{s,t}\circ X_{t,s}=\mathrm{Id}
, of which one can take the total derivative with respect to
s
, yielding the required identity.
∎

Each of these conditions yields a simple MSE objective. When null, and when the flow matching objective is null too, the learnt map is the unique flow map of the probability flow. Formally, the flow map learning objective amounts to

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left\lVert v^{\theta}_{t,t}(I_{t})-\dot{I}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SD}}(\theta),

(B.4)

where, in practice,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SD}}(\theta)
is either one of

•

Lagrangian self-distillation
.
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LSD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\partial_{t}X^{\theta}_{s,t}(I_{s})-\mathop{\mathrm{sg}}(v^{\theta}_{t,t}(X^{\theta}_{s,t}(I_{s}))\right\rVert^{2}

•

Progressive self-distillation
.
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PSD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left\lVert X^{\theta}_{s,t}(I_{s})-\mathop{\mathrm{sg}}(X^{\theta}_{u,t}(X^{\theta}_{s,u}(I_{s}))\right\rVert^{2}

•

Eulerian self-distillation
.
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ESD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\partial_{s}X_{s,t}(x_{s})-\mathop{\mathrm{sg}}(\nabla X_{s,t}(I_{s})v_{s,s}(I_{s}))\right\rVert^{2}
,

with
\mathop{\mathrm{sg}}
denoting the stop-gradient operator, which is such that
\nabla_{\theta}\mathop{\mathrm{sg}}(f(\theta))=\mathbf{0}
. Note that the placement of
\mathop{\mathrm{sg}}
does not change the minimiser of those objectives, and is put in those positions in practice for stability and memory usage reasons.

Appendix C
Semi-discrete likelihoods

In this section, we detail our attempts at extracting likelihoods out of a CFM. We note that we are interested in the following quantity, for a category
x\in V
,

p_{\theta}(x)=\int p_{\theta}(x\mid x_{1})p_{\theta}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}x_{1}.

(C.1)

With argmax decoding, this reduces to

p_{\theta}(x)=\int\mathbbm{1}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{j}x_{1}^{j}=x\right)p_{\theta}(x_{1})\mathrm{d}x_{1}.

(C.2)

C.1
Reversing the ODE

The continuous likelihood of the endpoint,
p_{\theta}(x_{1})
, is accessible by reversing the ODE
(
20
;
7
)
, as mentioned in
Section

4.3
. Specifically, with the instantaneous change of variables, we have that

\log p_{\theta}(x_{1})=\log p_{\theta}(x_{0})-\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{div}(v_{\theta}(x_{t}))\mathrm{d}t,

(C.3)

where, in our case,

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},0\leq t<1,\qquad v^{\theta}_{t}(x)=\frac{\pi^{\theta}_{t,t}(x)-x}{1-t}.

(C.4)

The divergence can be efficiently estimated using the Hutchinson trace estimator
(
26
)
:

\mathrm{Tr}(A)=\mathbb{E}[z^{\top}Az],\qquad\text{ for }z\text{, s.t. }\mathbb{E}[zz^{\top}]=I.

(C.5)

This avoids the materialisation of the Jacobian matrix through a Jacobian-Vector Product (JVP). While this is true, this computation remains prohibitively expensive in high dimensions. In our case, we operate over
\mathbb{R}^{L\times\lvert V\rvert}
requiring many samples for the convergences of the above estimator, and applying a JVP to a
1.7
B model is also expensive. Besides, this computation has to be carried through time and space in our case, as described in (
C.1
). We therefore cannot leverage this technique.

C.2
Semi-discrete ELBO

We prove, here, the semi-discrete ELBO stated in
Section

4.3
.

See
4.1

Proof.

27
provide the following continuous-time ELBO:

-\log p_{\theta}(x)\leq\operatorname{KL}\!\left(q(I_{0}\mid I_{1}=\mathbf{e}_{x})\,\middle\|\,p(I_{0})\right)+\mathbb{E}_{q(z_{0}\mid I_{1}=\mathbf{e}_{x})}\left[-\log p_{\theta}(x\mid I_{1}=\mathbf{e}_{x})\right]+\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(x).

(C.6)

The first term is the prior loss and is
0
in our case. The second term is a cross entropy loss between the clean point, and our prediction at time
1
, assuming a continuous relaxation of the decoding of our model, replacing the argmax by a sample from
\mathrm{Cat}(x_{1})
, where
x_{1}
is the endpoint of our trajectory. The last term is the continuous-time diffusion loss and is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\infty}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{SNR}^{\prime}(t)\left\lVert\mathbf{e}_{x}-\pi_{t,t}^{\theta}(I_{t})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}t,

(C.7)

where
\mathrm{SNR}(t)=\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{(1-\alpha_{t})^{2}}
, for any
t
, the derivative of which corresponds to twice the weight in the final integral. Moreover, we have, by Pinsker’s inequality, since
\mathbf{e}_{x},\pi_{t,t}^{\theta}(I_{t})\in\Delta^{d}
,

\lVert\mathbf{e}_{x}-\pi_{t,t}^{\theta}(I_{t})\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2\operatorname{KL}\!\left(\mathbf{e}_{x}\,\middle\|\,\pi_{t,t}^{\theta}(I_{t})\right)=2\ell_{\mathrm{CE}}(x,\pi_{t,t}^{\theta}(I_{t}))

(C.8)

∎

The tightness of this bound in the semi-discrete setting remains, however, unknown. We hypothesise that it is possible to extract better likelihoods out of the model.

D.6
Self-distillation divergence ablation

D.6.1
Choice of divergence

In this work, additionally to the typically used forward KL divergence, we also attempted using reverse KL and Jensen-Shannon Divergence. As a reminder, for a model
p_{\theta}
and a target
q
, the forward KL is

\operatorname{KL}\!\left(q\,\middle\|\,p_{\theta}\right)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{q}\log q-\mathbb{E}_{q}\log p_{\theta},

(D.1)

which results in the following gradients with respect to the parameters,
\theta
:

\nabla_{\theta}\operatorname{KL}\!\left(p_{\theta}\,\middle\|\,q\right)=-\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q}\log p_{\theta}=-\mathbb{E}_{q}\frac{\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}}{p_{\theta}},

(D.2)

recovering typical cross entropy training. Since
q_{\theta}
weighs the loss term, it is highest when
p_{\theta}
puts almost no density on a point where
q
does. On the other hand, if
q
puts no mass on a given point but
p_{\theta}
does, the loss is zero. It is said to be “mode-covering”. While this seems favourable, let us rewrite the gradient in clearer way:

\nabla_{\theta}\operatorname{KL}\!\left(p_{\theta}\,\middle\|\,q\right)=-\int\frac{q(x)}{p_{\theta}(x)}\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

(D.3)

Notice that when
q\approx p
, then the gradient under the integral is weighed by approximately
1
: this means that in positions where the model and the target agree already, the model is reinforced in that direction. While this may be favourable on the diagonal part of the loss, this might not hold for self-distillation part: the imperfect, biased teacher might further push the student towards the wrong distribution. It might be favourable that the self-distillation loss minimally alters the base model, to avoid biasing the model while learning the flow map, as we show with JSD.

The reverse KL, on the other hand, is defined as

\operatorname{KL}\!\left(p_{\theta}\,\middle\|\,q\right)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}}\log\frac{p_{\theta}}{q}=-H(p_{\theta})-\mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}}\log q,

(D.4)

where
H(p_{\theta})
is the entropy of
p_{\theta}
. Minimising this objective results in a maximisation of the entropy of the model distribution and a minimisation of the reverse cross-entropy. In this objective, the
\log q
terms prevails in terms of magnitude (remember that
q(x)\in[0,1]
, in the discrete case), and therefore, on any density point where
p_{\theta}
and
q
differ, the model is drawn back strongly towards it. For example, if
p_{\theta}
puts some non-zero mass on a point
x
and
q(x)\approx 0
, then the magnitude of the term may diverge. It is therefore said to be “mode-seeking”. (We also note that the entropy is maximised when it is uniform.) The reverse KL presented the most instabilities as shown in the results.

Finally, the JSD is defined as follows. Let
m_{\theta}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(p_{\theta}+q\right)
be the midpoint distribution between the model and the target. Then,

\operatorname{JSD}\!\left(p_{\theta}\,\middle\|\,q\right)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{KL}\!\left(p_{\theta}\,\middle\|\,m_{\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{KL}\!\left(q\,\middle\|\,m_{\theta}\right)=H(m_{\theta})-\frac{1}{2}\Big(H(q)+H(p_{\theta})\Big).

(D.5)

It is minimising the entropy of the middle point while maximising the entropy of the model. As such, we can already note that it has the property that there may no be points such that
m^{\theta}
and
p^{\theta}
diverge completely on mass, by the middle-point’s definition, therefore acting in some sense as a higher temperature term. Let us inspect its gradient:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}H(m_{\theta})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int\nabla_{\theta}\left[(p_{\theta}(x)+q(x))\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x)+q(x)}{2}\right]\mathrm{d}x

(D.6)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int\left[\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x)+q(x)}{2}+\big(p_{\theta}(x)+q(x)\big)\frac{\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)}{p_{\theta}(x)+q(x)}\right]\mathrm{d}x

(D.7)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}H(m_{\theta})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int\left[\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x)+q(x)}{2}+\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)\right]\mathrm{d}x.

(D.8)

The
p_{\theta}
entropy term’s gradient amounts to:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\theta}H(p_{\theta})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int\left[\log p_{\theta}(x)\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)+p_{\theta}(x)\frac{\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)}{p_{\theta}(x)}\right]\mathrm{d}x

(D.9)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int\left[\log p_{\theta}(x)\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)+\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)\right].

(D.10)

Summing the two terms yields

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\operatorname{JSD}\!\left(p_{\theta}\,\middle\|\,q\right)=\nabla_{\theta}H(m_{\theta})-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\theta}H(p_{\theta})=-\frac{1}{2}\int\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x)+q(x)}{2p_{\theta}(x)}\mathrm{d}x.

(D.11)

Otherwise stated:

\nabla_{\theta}\operatorname{JSD}\!\left(p_{\theta}\,\middle\|\,q\right)=\frac{1}{2}\int\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(x)\log\frac{2p_{\theta}(x)}{p_{\theta}(x)+q(x)}\mathrm{d}x.

(D.12)

When the two distributions concur, the gradient is near zero. When
p_{\theta}
tends to infinity, the
\log
term tends to
\log 2
, therefore bounding gradients in the case of a (large) overestimation by the model. Remark as well that this term is always bounded by
\log 2
. When
p_{\theta}
is small (tends to zero) where
q
assigns mass, the term diverges to infinity. We see that the JSD alters the model’s distributions in less aggressive ways than the forward KL divergence, and show empirically that the base flow is better preserved. Finally, the boundedness of the term, beyond avoiding divergence, naturally balances out the training dynamics between the diagonal
s=t
loss and the self-distillation---an issue in the case of the KL divergences, where the diagonal term is of roughly the same order of magnitude of the self-distillation one, leading to the possible and detrimental domination of the latter.
2
2

2

If the flow term remains high, but the self-distillation remains close to zero, then the model would have effectively learnt a trivial solution of the self-distillation loss landscape.
