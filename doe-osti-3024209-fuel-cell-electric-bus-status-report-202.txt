Executive Summary
The National Laboratory of the Rockies, funded by the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE’s)
Hydrogen and Fuel Cell Technologies Office, presents this report as part of its long-standing
efforts to evaluate and track the deployment of fuel cell electric buses (FCEBs) in the United
States. This report highlights recent changes in the FCEB market, status updates for
deployments, and a detailed performance evaluation of a large fleet of FCEBs operated by
Foothill Transit in California from June 2023 to December 2024.

Key highlights:

 • National FCEB deployment trends:
 o The total number of active FCEBs in U.S. transit fleets is at least 99, with an
 additional 103 buses on order across 14 transit agencies, per annual data from the
 American Public Transportation Association.
 o California leads with 89 active FCEBs and plans for more than 2,600 buses by
 2040 as part of its Innovative Clean Transit regulation.
 o The FCEB market has seen notable orders by transit agencies, including Santa
 Cruz Metropolitan Transit District’s 57 FCEBs (largest acquisition to date) and
 Orange County Transportation Authority’s purchase of 40 FCEBs.
 • Foothill Transit FCEB evaluation results:
 o Foothill Transit operates 33 model year 2022 New Flyer FCEBs, marking North
 America’s largest fuel cell bus fleet at the time of deployment. These buses utilize
 advanced fuel cell and battery systems.
 o Evaluation included comparisons between the FCEB fleet and three baseline
 compressed natural gas (CNG) bus fleets to assess key performance metrics such
 as bus availability, fuel efficiency, maintenance costs, and reliability.
 o Foothill Transit FCEB fleet performance:
 ̶ The fleet of 33 FCEBs achieved a total mileage of 1,195,339 miles during
 the analysis period, with an average availability of 76.8%, higher than the
 North American Bus Industries (NABI) CNG fleet’s 72.4% but below the
 target of 85%.
 ̶ The fuel efficiency for the FCEB fleet averages 9.2 miles per diesel gallon
 equivalent (mpdge), surpassing the ultimate DOE/U.S. Department of
 Transportation (DOT) target of 8.0 mpdge.
 ̶ The monthly fuel cost per mile for the FCEB fleet ranges from $1.00 to
 $1.50, with an average of $1.25 per mile.
 ̶ The fleet achieved a high value for miles between roadcalls, with 21,345
 miles between general roadcalls and 44,272 miles between propulsion-
 related roadcalls, both exceeding performance targets.
 ̶ Maintenance costs for the fleet during the analysis period were $0.72/mile,
 lower than the NABI CNG fleet’s $1.13/mile, with preventive

 v

 maintenance inspections and propulsion systems being primary cost
 contributors. This met DOE’s interim target of $0.75/mile.
• Technological status and challenges:
 o FCEBs have progressed toward meeting DOE/DOT technical targets for fuel
 economy, reliability, and maintenance costs. However, challenges persist with
 high upfront capital costs for buses, hydrogen fuel infrastructure expenses, high
 costs for hydrogen fuel, and hydrogen fuel loss, which hinder wider deployment
 of FCEBs in U.S. transit fleets.

1 Introduction
This report is part of a series of status reports from the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE’s)
National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) tracking the evolution of fuel cell electric bus (FCEB)
technology in the United States. It summarizes the status and recent progress from
demonstrations of FCEBs in public transit and other passenger transport applications. Since
2000, NLR has evaluated FCEB demonstrations at several transit agencies, assessing the buses,
infrastructure, and implementation experience from the partnering transit agencies. These NLR
evaluations have been funded by DOE, the U.S. Department of Transportation’s (DOT’s)
Federal Transit Administration, and the California Air Resources Board.

This status report discusses the achievements and challenges of fuel cell propulsion for transit
and summarizes the introduction of fuel cell transit buses in the United States. It provides an
analysis of the results from the latest FCEB deployment evaluated by NLR—a large fleet of
2022 New Flyer FCEBs operated by Foothill Transit between June 2023 and December 2024.
The report’s intent is to inform DOE and Federal Transit Administration decision makers who
guide future research and funding, state and local government agencies that fund new propulsion
technology transit buses, as well as interested transit agencies, bus manufacturers, and other
industry representatives.

This report is organized into the following sections:

 1. Introduction.
 2. Fuel Cell Electric Buses in the United States: Provides an overview of FCEBs deployed
 in the United States at public transit agencies, as well as other passenger transport
 applications such as universities, airports, and private fleets. This section also provides a
 brief update on notable FCEB deployments and recent changes in the FCEB market.
 3. Update of Evaluation Results – Foothill Transit: Presents the detailed results of an
 evaluation of 33 new FCEBs in service at Foothill Transit, compared to baseline
 compressed natural gas (CNG) bus fleets, including comparisons for availability, fuel
 economy, fuel cost, and reliability.
 4. Current Status of FCEB Technology: Summary of Achievements and Challenges.

2 Fuel Cell Electric Buses in the United States
2.1 FCEB Deployment Status
The American Public Transportation Association (APTA) takes an annual inventory of vehicles
in U.S. transit fleets and publishes detailed information in the APTA Public Transportation
Vehicle Database. This includes both the number of vehicles in operation and planned purchases,
separated by vehicle type, fuel/powertrain type, original equipment manufacturer (OEM), vehicle
model, size, and model year. Many of the records also include the total purchase cost. While the
records in this database are self-reported by transit agencies, it is one of the most detailed
inventories of transit bus purchases in the United States. Figure 1 shows the number of FCEBs
reported to be in operation or on order for U.S. transit fleets, by model year, according to the
2024 APTA vehicle database. 1

 Figure 1. Number of FCEBs in U.S. transit fleets by model year.

Database entries for model year 2022 buses show a large increase in FCEBs deployed, with 58
new active buses. These new buses are divided between three transit agencies, all in California—
Golden Empire Transit (5), Alameda-Contra Costa Transit District (20), and Foothill Transit
(33). This signals a shift toward much larger orders of FCEBs at transit agencies, including the
purchase of 33 FCEBs by Foothill Transit, which was the largest confirmed order in this dataset
and is the focus of NLR’s FCEB evaluation in Section 3.

There was another large increase in FCEB purchases for model year 2024, with 75 buses on
order, collectively, among 11 transit agencies located in seven different states. This year

Another national inventory of zero-emission buses (ZEBs), funded by the Federal Transit
Administration, has been conducted by CALSTART. This annual report, Zeroing in on Zero-
Emission Buses: The U.S. Advanced Technology Transit Bus Index, expands beyond public
transit agencies to also include ZEBs that are in service or on order at universities, airports, and
private bus fleets. 2 The 2025 report calculates a total of 575 full-size (Class 7 or 8) FCEBs either
funded, ordered, delivered, or deployed in service in the United States. This represents a 55%
increase over the previous year’s total. California accounts for 435 (or 76%) of those FCEBs.
The report identifies only nine small FCEBs (Class 3–6, <30 feet) in the United States—five in
Ohio and four in California—and counts zero new small FCEBs purchased since 2022. This
report highlights that FCEBs continue to be deployed in applications beyond public transit
service, but bus types are limited to full-size (“standard”) transit buses. The industry would
benefit from additional FCEB manufacturers and bus sizes and types, especially a fuel cell
powered cutaway shuttle or other style of minibus, which is preferred in numerous passenger
transport applications such as at airports, hotel shuttles, rural transit agencies, and in paratransit
or on-demand services.

Transit agencies in California have developed and submitted ZEB rollout plans that outline their
plans to meet the Innovative Clean Transit regulation, which requires all agencies to transition to
100% transit ZEBs by 2040. 3 An analysis of the submitted rollout plans indicates that agencies in
the state anticipate purchasing more than 2,600 FCEBs by 2040, and potentially more, as some
agencies will decide in the coming years which ZEB technology to adopt—battery electric or
hydrogen fuel cell. 4

2.2 Notable Fuel Cell Bus Deployments
As shown, the 2024 APTA vehicle database documents new orders of FCEBs for a combined
total of 75 buses in 2024. Of all the new orders, the largest single order was for 13 FCEBs from
Victor Valley Transit Authority in California, who is budgeted for another 10 FCEBs. This is a
significant new order for a small transit agency (that has fewer than 100 buses in their fleet), as
most FCEB deployments thus far have been at larger transit agencies that have greater capacity
to pilot and test new ZEB technologies in their revenue service. With the experience gained by
these agencies during previous FCEB demonstrations, and continuous advancements of the
technology, smaller transit agencies are now able to adopt FCEBs with more confidence.
Similarly, Humbolt Transit recently purchased 11 FCEBs and began incorporating into their fleet
of fewer than 50 buses.

The APTA vehicle database reports three other transit agencies having ordered 10 buses each in
2024—San Mateo County Transit District (California), Champaign-Urbana Mass Transit District
(Illinois), and Southeastern Pennsylvania Transportation Authority (Pennsylvania).

In 2023, Santa Cruz Metropolitan Transit District announced the purchase of 57 FCEBs—the
largest FCEB acquisition in North America at the time of the announcement. The order is a
combination of 40-ft and 60-ft articulated buses, with delivery and preparation for service
occurring in early 2025. 5

In late 2024, New Flyer of America announced the company’s largest single order of FCEBs to
date—a contract with San Mateo County Transit District (SamTrans) for an additional 108
FCEBs, to be delivered in the coming years. 6 SamTrans is on track to achieve their transition to a
100% ZEB fleet by 2034. 7 Also in late 2024, Orange County Transportation Authority approved
the purchase of 40 FCEBs in their effort to transition to a 100% ZEB fleet by 2040. 8

3 Update of Evaluation Results – Foothill Transit
This section presents the evaluation results from a deployment of FCEBs operated by Foothill
Transit in the San Gabriel and Pomona valleys in eastern Los Angeles County, California.
Foothill Transit currently operates 33 hydrogen fuel cell buses, which is one of the largest fuel
cell bus fleets in North America. 13 These buses also represent the latest generation of FCEB
technology from the primary domestic manufacturer of FCEBs, which have been operated and
evaluated in service for more than one year. The goal of this evaluation is to compare the FCEB
performance to that of conventional technology and to track progress over time toward meeting
the technological targets set by DOE and DOT, as shown in Table 3. NLR collected data on the
33 FCEBs and three CNG bus fleets covering the period of June 2023 through December 2024
for the analysis. The dataset includes daily mileage and fuel consumption records, maintenance
records, vehicle availability records, monthly hydrogen bills and CNG fuel bills, recorded
roadcalls, and detailed work orders for all non-warranty maintenance performed. NLR’s
standardized evaluation protocol was applied to assess the FCEBs’ vehicle usage, availability,
fuel efficiency, fuel costs, roadcall frequency, and maintenance costs, with performance
compared to the baseline conventional CNG buses.

3.4 Availability
NLR calculates bus availability as the percentage of days buses are available for passenger
service (whether or not they are used) out of days the buses are planned for service. Buses that
are available may be used for passenger service, training, or special events, or they may be
available but not used for any purpose (e.g., spares). Buses unavailable for service may have had
issues with the propulsion system (fuel cell system or electric drive system), had general bus
maintenance, or been undergoing scheduled preventive maintenance. Any issues not covered by
the other listed categories for preventive maintenance or propulsion-related systems fall into the
general “bus maintenance” category—this includes items such as wheels and tires, steering, bus
chassis, windows and mirrors, and seats and interior. The results presented are based on
availability for morning pullout and do not necessarily reflect all-day availability. Transit
agencies typically have a target of 85% availability for their fleets to allow for time to handle
scheduled and unscheduled maintenance. Accidents are removed from the data―the bus is
considered “not planned” during the repair time for accident-related repairs. To calculate
availability for the FCEB and the baseline CNG fleets, NLR used the daily down sheets for the
Pomona and Arcadia facilities, which list each bus that is not available for morning pullout and
provide a general reason for unavailability.

3.6 Reliability: MBRC
A roadcall, or revenue vehicle system failure, is defined as a failure of an in-service bus that
causes the bus to be replaced on route or causes a significant delay in schedule. If the problem
with the bus can be repaired during a layover and the schedule is kept, this is not considered a
roadcall. The analysis described here includes only roadcalls that were caused by “chargeable”
failures. Chargeable roadcalls include systems that can physically disable the bus from operating
on route, such as interlocks (doors and air system), engines, or things that are deemed to be
safety issues if operation of the bus continues. They do not include roadcalls for things such as
problems with radios, fareboxes, or destination signs.

The transit industry measures reliability as mean distance between failures, also documented as
MBRC. NLR tracks MBRC by total roadcalls, propulsion-related roadcalls, and fuel cell system-
related roadcalls. Total roadcalls includes all chargeable roadcalls. Propulsion-related roadcall is
a subset of total roadcalls and includes all roadcalls due to propulsion-related systems including
the fuel cell system (or engine for a conventional bus), electric drive, fuel, exhaust, air intake,
cooling, non-lighting electrical, transmission systems, and hydraulics. The fuel cell system-
related roadcalls, a subset of the propulsion-related roadcalls, and calculated MBRC are also
included for the FCEBs.

Table 7 summarizes the number of total roadcalls, propulsion-related roadcalls, fuel cell system-
related roadcalls, and their corresponding MBRC. This roadcall analysis includes data
accumulated since the clean point of June 2023. For the ENC and New Flyer CNG fleets, fleet
mileage data are unavailable; therefore, only the number of roadcalls is reported. From January
to December 2024, the ENC fleet experienced a total of 52 roadcalls, of which 32 were
propulsion-related (62%). From January to December 2024, the New Flyer CNG fleet recorded a
total of 118 roadcalls, 67 of which were propulsion-related (57%). The NABI CNG fleet
experienced 34 roadcalls between June and December 2023, including 22 propulsion-related

roadcalls were attributed to the fuel cell system. The FCEB fleet achieved a total MBRC of

4 Current Status of FCEB Technology: Summary of
  Achievements and Challenges
This section discusses the progress being made and the challenges that remain to bring FCEBs to
the market.

4.1 Progress Toward Meeting Technical Targets
In 2012, DOE and DOT/Federal Transit Administration established performance and cost targets
for FCEBs. Interim targets were set, along with ultimate targets that FCEBs would need to meet
to compete with current commercial technology buses. Although the targets set in 2012 included
a 2016 interim target, not all FCEBs were expected to reach these targets in that time frame. In
particular, the power plant lifetime requires 6 years/250,000 miles before reaching the target.
Table 10 shows a selection of these technical targets for FCEBs.

 • Bus costs:
 o Previous status reports showed that FCEB capital costs had been decreasing
 steadily since the early demonstrations. Reported costs in the 2024 APTA
 database show average FCEB costs increasing sharply from approximately
               $1,000,000 per bus in 2020 to nearly $1,500,000 by 2025.
 o Foothill Transit reported a capital cost of approximately $1,150,000 per bus for
 the 2022 model year FCEB fleet.
 • Bus lifetime:
 o Assessing the durability and lifetime of a propulsion system takes time, and the
 latest generation of the FCEB technology has not yet acquired enough operation
 time to determine the average expected lifetime of the components or system.
 This will need to be assessed in the coming years as the current fuel cell stacks in
 the buses degrade and are ultimately retired.

 31

 o Previous FCEB status reports demonstrated that the fuel cell stacks on the Van
 Hool FCEBs were meeting, and often exceeding, the ultimate target of 25,000
 hours of operation before being retired from service due to voltage degradation.
 • Fuel economy:
 o Previous FCEB status reports documented the average fuel economy for first- and
 second-generation FCEBs as 5.89 mpdge and 7.94 mpdge, respectively, noting a
 significant improvement in the performance of the technology and approximating
 the ultimate target of 8.0 mpdge. The latest generation of New Flyer FCEBs
 operating at Foothill Transit have achieved an average fuel economy of 9.2
 mpdge in service in Southern California.
 • Bus availability:
 o The FCEB fleet at Foothill Transit demonstrated a fleet average availability of
 76.8%, while the comparative CNG fleets ranged between 72% and 88% during
 the data period. The ultimate target of 90% availability is a high threshold that
 most conventional bus fleets do not consistently achieve. A target of 80%-85% is
 more common at transit agencies.
 • Maintenance cost and MBRC:
 o As with assessing total powerplant lifetime, assessing the overall maintenance
 costs and MBRC for a bus takes time in service. Maintenance costs tend to start
 lower and increase with the age of the bus but can fluctuate over time due to a
 variety of operation and maintenance factors.
 o Overall maintenance cost for the FCEB fleet at Foothill Transit during the data
 period was calculated to be $0.72 per mile, approaching the interim target of
              $0.75 per mile.
 o The MBRC for the FCEB fleet was approximately 21,000 miles for all bus-related
 roadcalls and 44,000 for propulsion-related roadcalls. The fleet has not yet
 experienced any fuel-cell-related roadcalls.
Some of the primary challenges remaining with deploying and operating FCEBs in transit
service include the high upfront cost for purchasing the buses, as well as the cost of installing the
hydrogen refueling infrastructure necessary to operate the buses. The unit price of hydrogen fuel
remains high ($9–$10/kg) for many FCEB fleets in operation today, leading to higher per-mile
operating costs than conventional transit buses. Reliability and durability of the technology are
continuing to improve, as is the fuel economy of FCEBs. Agencies operating FCEBs are still
developing and increasing their in-house technical expertise to efficiently troubleshoot and repair
issues that arise with the fuel cell powerplant and electric powertrain. Continued progress in
costs, performance, and reliability will encourage more transit agencies to deploy FCEBs in their
service.
