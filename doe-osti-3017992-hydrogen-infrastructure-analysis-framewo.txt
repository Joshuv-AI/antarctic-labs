Executive Summary
Although aircraft account for less than 10% of U.S. transportation energy consumption, air travel
is the only mode expected to see sustained growth in energy demand through 2050. Currently,
commercial aircraft rely heavily on either jet fuel or leaded aviation gasoline. Over the past few
decades, many aircraft manufacturers have been pursuing the development of new-generation
aircraft that can leverage alternative, renewable fuels.

Hydrogen has become a fuel of interest because of its abundant availability. Approximately 10
million metric tons of hydrogen are produced annually in the United States, and more than 95%
of the hydrogen produced in the United States comes from steam methane reforming of natural
gas. The primary challenge for these and more traditional hydrogen production is reducing the
costs. With the U.S. Department of Energy’s “Hydrogen Shot” cost goal of $1 per kilogram in 1
decade for production, hydrogen can be produced through a number of pathways that use diverse
domestic resources, including nuclear energy and renewable energy from wind, solar,
geothermal, hydroelectric, and biomass. Aviation should economically benefit from research,
development, and demonstration efforts for other end-use applications such as light- and heavy-
duty vehicles and net-zero industrial processes such as fertilizer (ammonia) production.

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) is presently working toward certifying multiple
novel aircraft and developing an environment to safely support this innovation. For airports and
aircraft original equipment manufacturers that choose to be early adopters of hydrogen
technology, the FAA needs to provide guidance for the associated support infrastructure. The
FAA is collaborating with the National Renewable Energy Laboratory on this research project to
evaluate the impact of the anticipated demand for hydrogen as a fuel source on existing airport
infrastructure. This study is intended to identify and reduce market barriers involving standards
and operational requirements of hydrogen technology and airport infrastructure. By defining
systemwide best practices in refueling systems and facility layouts, manufacturers, airports, and
communities can be better prepared to participate in this emerging industry.

Section 2 of this document is the culmination of a literature review that describes the current
state and the anticipated future of hydrogen-related technology across various government
organizations, industries, and academic institutions. This work identifies existing goals and the
maturity level of the industry to help define a realistic strategy and approach to implement
hydrogen systems into the airport environment within a realistic time frame. The technology
areas cover production, storage, transport, and fueling as a supply chain of hydrogen. This report
also briefly investigates hydrogen propulsion systems, such as fuel cell and combustion engines.
Furthermore, this work organizes safety codes and standards to identify gaps in our current
understanding.

Presently, there are detailed standards regarding hydrogen technology, airport safety, and aircraft
fueling. However, they are not consolidated as a unified set of codes and standards suitable for
the airport environment. Preventive safety measures and emergency procedures must be
identified as hydrogen will begin to be transported to airport sites. These safety requirements
must continue to mature as airports move to adopt hydrogen technology. In addition, before
placing any hydrogen systems at airports, detailed risk assessments and operations planning

 vi

should also be required to ensure safety and seamless integrating, while meeting all statutory
obligations.

Sections 3 and 4 of this study focus on hydrogen aircraft demands and airport infrastructure
needed to support hydrogen technology. The examination of hydrogen production, storage
requirements, delivery methods, refueling services, and safety requirements is critical to
accommodate novel hydrogen aircraft in the airport environment. Lessons learned are applied to
the West Coast region using existing air traffic patterns to gain insight on (1) space requirements
for hydrogen infrastructure, (2) airport hydrogen infrastructure placement, and (3) cost and
economic impacts of hydrogen infrastructure at each airport. Any airports contained within these
traffic patterns are strictly hypothetical and were not active participants in this study. Results
show each airport has a few locations where hydrogen storage and/or hydrogen production
facilities could be located based on the hydrogen aircraft demand scenarios analyzed in this
study. We also find that both hydrogen infrastructure cost and economic impacts become more
favorable as hydrogen demand at the airports scale.

This study is intended to provide a comprehensive understanding of hydrogen infrastructure that
will enable energy savings and emissions reductions while illuminating pathways to transition
from legacy systems to next-generation aviation. The study should be used as a guide by airport
and government officials, as well as industry leaders, to gain insights on how hydrogen and
hydrogen infrastructure can be leveraged for aviation applications.

1 Introduction
Although aircraft account for less than 10% of U.S. transportation energy consumption, air travel
is the mode expected to see the highest growth rate and sustained growth in energy demand
through 2050 (“Use of Energy for Transportation - U.S. Energy Information Administration
(EIA),” n.d.) (“U.S. Energy Information Administration - EIA - Independent Statistics and
Analysis,” n.d.). The first federal office on aviation was established in 1926 (Kraus, n.d.), a
pivotal age for opening air commerce, travel, and technology. Aviation has undergone several
periods of rapid innovation in the last hundred years, and an emerging trend in lower emissions
aircraft is pushing the industry to its next frontier. The U.S. Department of Transportation’s
Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) plays a critical role in certifying airworthiness and
promoting safety in the skies and on the ground. Thus, as new models of novel aircraft come
online, the FAA needs to provide safety guidance for the planning, design, and establishment of
landing areas and on-the-ground infrastructure at public facilities. The National Renewable
Energy Laboratory (NREL) has been asked by the FAA to evaluate how potential airport
facilities can serve novel aircraft, particularly operation via electrification (“Federal Aviation
Administration Partners With NREL for a Holistic Look at Energy Needs of Electrified
Aircraft,” n.d.) and using hydrogen (H2) for aircraft.

Hydrogen is particularly intriguing because of its high gravimetric energy density (roughly three
times higher than aviation fuel) and its use results in nearly zero-carbon generation. However,
the low volumetric energy density of hydrogen must be properly accounted for in use in onboard
aircraft. Overall, increasing the volumetric density via compression or liquefaction can require
5% to 20% and 30% to 40%, respectively, of the energy in hydrogen (Gardiner 2009a).
However, although market price will be a dominant factor going forward, the complex
relationship of overall energy efficiency will ultimately be important considerations.

The FAA is currently assisting multiple parties through the aircraft and aircraft component
certification process (“FAA Reauthorization Opens DC Doors to Hydrogen Aviation,” n.d.) and
also exploring how to safely support this innovation. For airports that choose to be early adopters
of novel aircraft using non-drop-in fuels, the FAA typically provides guidance for the associated
support infrastructure for safe inclusion into the airport system. Because of NREL’s experience
with electrical (Schwab et al. 2021) and hydrogen infrastructure and integration, NREL will
perform analysis that the FAA will use to develop guidance on safety standards and efficient and
resilient airport infrastructure for novel aircraft. This effort is critical for non-drop-in
replacement fuels such as electricity and hydrogen, for which existing electrical systems and
infrastructure at most airports cannot support significant and/or rapid aircraft refueling. In the
near term, these analyses will primarily leverage existing gaseous hydrogen generation and
delivery infrastructure outside of airports. However, as new aircraft push for greater range, both
delivered and ultimately on-site generation of liquefied hydrogen may be needed at airports.
Thus, analyses regarding production, delivery, storage, refueling services, and safety will be
needed, along with measurement of associated factors such as the growth potential for jobs
supporting the airport infrastructure changes. This comprehensive understanding will enable
ambitious energy-saving goals and illuminate pathways to transition legacy systems to support
next-generation aviation.

 1

Constructing the necessary hydrogen infrastructure at airports requires the collaboration and
cooperation of multiple stakeholders, including aircraft manufacturers, utilities, hydrogen
suppliers, hydrogen delivery, potential site property owners, and local communities. The
operational requirements for each of these entities must be evaluated. Through the completion of
this study, market barriers involving uncertainty in standards and operational requirements of
hydrogen aircraft and airport infrastructure aim to be diminished. By defining potential
systemwide best practices in refueling systems and facility layouts, manufacturers, airports, and
communities can be better prepared to participate in this emerging industry. This document is
intended to organize information regarding current hydrogen technology and to identify the on-
ground infrastructure needs to accommodate novel hydrogen aircraft in the airport environment.
The remainder of this document is organized as follows: section 2 describes the current state of
hydrogen technology to identify technological gaps for aviation applications. Section 3 describes
methodologies used to identify the needs of hydrogen infrastructure to support novel hydrogen
aircraft. Section 4 provides those analysis results. Finally, section 5 summarizes findings.

2 Background
Although it is the simplest, smallest element and consists of only one proton and one electron,
hydrogen is typically a diatomic molecule (dihydrogen, or H2) in its pure state. But on Earth it is
almost always found as part of another compound, such as water or methane. Hydrogen is too
light to be gravitationally held in Earth’s atmosphere and eventually can escape to space.
However, because of the oxygen-rich atmosphere, almost all gaseous hydrogen will react to form
other compounds before it clears the atmosphere. The environmental impact, energy efficiency,
and costs of hydrogen depend on how it is produced, transported, and stored. This section
describes the current state of hydrogen technology to identify technological gaps for aviation
applications. Sections 2.1 through 2.4 describe these hydrogen systems from production to end-
use application (e.g., hydrogen refueling) from a supply chain perspective (see Figure 1 for an
illustrative example). Sections 2.5 and 2.6 provide background of hydrogen-based propulsion
technologies. Section 2.7 discusses potential opportunities using hydrogen systems in the airport
environment. Section 2.8 summarizes current hydrogen safety codes and standards for airports.

2.1 Hydrogen Production
Approximately 10 million metric tons (MMT) of hydrogen are produced annually in the United
States (Pivovar, Rustagi, and Satyapal 2018); most of it from nonrenewable and high CO2-
emission processes such as steam methane reforming (Connelly et al. 2020). Hydrogen can be
produced from diverse domestic resources, including fossil fuels, biomass, and water electrolysis

 3

with electricity (“Alternative Fuels Data Center: Hydrogen Production and Distribution,” n.d.).
Presently, 95% of the hydrogen produced in the United States comes from steam methane
reforming (U.S. DRIVE 2017). Furthermore, nearly 70% of the hydrogen is used in the
petroleum refining industry, with another 20% being used for fertilizer production. This section
explores notable hydrogen production technologies and the cost of production from each
pathway.

2.1.1 Production Pathways
Steam methane reforming hydrogen production is achieved by creating synthesis gas from
natural gas and high-temperature steam, resulting in a mixture of hydrogen, carbon monoxide,
and carbon dioxide. This synthesis gas can also be produced by reacting coal or biomass with
high-temperature steam and oxygen in a pressurized gasifier. Thermal conversion pathways
involving steam methane reforming presently dominate production because of their low cost.
However, the methane comes from natural gas, which is nonrenewable, and substantial
infrastructure investment is needed to capture, use, and store the high amount of CO2 emissions
from the process (Lewis et al. 2022).

A key issue in hydrogen production technologies is to achieve lower carbon intensity compared
to fossil resources (e.g., natural gas conversion or coal gasification) with carbon capture,
utilization, and storage, where carbon credits are available through 2032 and can be used to
offset some of the carbon capture, utilization, and storage costs (Congressional Research Service
2023). However, these carbon credits will also assist renewable hydrogen production
technologies, including hydrogen from biomass/waste conversion, water splitting electrolysis
using renewable electricity, and direct solar pathways (“Alternative Fuels Data Center: Hydrogen
Production and Distribution,” n.d.).

Hydrogen can be produced in a variety of ways with different costs and carbon/emission
intensities. DOE has been mandated (“PLAW-117publ58.Pdf,” n.d.) to develop and update a
clean hydrogen production standard that considers the “technological and economic feasibility”
while requiring a carbon intensity less than or equal to 2 kilograms (kg) CO2-equivalent
generated at the production site per kilogram of hydrogen produced.

Although the bulk of hydrogen in the United States is produced using nonrenewable steam
methane reforming because of the relatively low cost, several technologies are being developed
and demonstration projects are underway to decrease hydrogen production costs using renewable
energy and resources. (“Alternative Fuels Data Center: Hydrogen Production and Distribution,”
n.d.)

Electrolyzers (e.g., proton exchange membrane water electrolyzers) can achieve near-zero
emissions using renewable energy because electrolysis is a process to split water into hydrogen
and oxygen using an electrical current (Pivovar 2021). If the electricity is produced by renewable
sources, such as solar or wind, the resulting hydrogen will be considered renewable and near-
zero GHG emissions. However, electrolyzers are currently high cost (Mayyas et al. 2019)
(“Home | Hydrogen from Next-Generation Electrolyzers of Water (H2NEW),” n.d.) (DOE
Hydrogen and Fuel Cells Program Record 2019), especially when operating on low capacity
factor renewables such as solar and wind.

 4

Although biomass to hydrogen can result in clean hydrogen, the biomass transportation fuels
must be considered to determine the overall carbon intensity (Elgowainy 2021). In general, there
are also many pathways for hydrogen production from biomass, including direct biological
conversion (e.g., dark fermentation and photo-fermentation), as well as thermochemical (e.g.,
steam methane reforming or autothermal reforming) and electrochemical processing of microbial
generated hydrocarbons (e.g., methane, methanol, and ethanol) (Lepage et al. 2021). Biomass
pathways include direct fermentation of sugar-rich feedstocks to form hydrogen, and high-
temperature steam reforming of renewable methane or liquid fuels such as biomass ethanol.
There are also photoelectrochemical and photobiological water splitting pathways, in which
sunlight is used by special semiconductors or microbes (e.g., green algae), respectively, to
convert water to hydrogen. Photoelectrochemical water splitting has many challenges, including
poor efficiency resulting from charge carrier recombination, high overpotential, reaction kinetics,
and materials durability in the harsh electrochemical environments (Kumar et al. 2022).
Similarly, presently, photobiological water splitting is inefficient with low hydrogen production
rates (“Hydrogen Production: Photobiological,” n.d.). High-temperature thermochemical water
splitting also has issues with efficiency and durability of reactant materials, robust reactor
designs, and costs, especially for concentrated solar approaches.

In general, hydrogen can be produced through several low-carbon pathways that use diverse
domestic resources, including nearly carbon-free nuclear energy and renewable energy from
wind, solar, geothermal, hydroelectric, and biomass. The primary challenge for these and more
traditional hydrogen production is reducing the costs so that the overall hydrogen technology is
cost competitive with conventional transportation fuels such as aviation fuel. However, aviation
should benefit, especially for costs, from research, development, and demonstration efforts for
other applications such as light- and heavy-duty vehicles (Onorato and Kuroki 2022), and net-
zero industrial processes such as fertilizer (ammonia) production.

2.1.2 Production Cost
DOE (“DOE National Clean Hydrogen Strategy and Roadmap” 2022), International Energy
Agency ((“The Future of Hydrogen – Analysis” 2019), (“Global Hydrogen Review 2021”
2021)), and others (Okonkwo et al. 2021) have recently released “technology roadmaps.” The
DOE roadmap outlines pathways for clean hydrogen based on demand scenarios through 2050. It
estimates clean hydrogen will grow from less than 1 MMT today to approximately 50 MMT
annually by 2050. This substantial increase in hydrogen demand will be driven by industries and
sectors that can initially pay higher prices of delivered hydrogen to support scale-up activities
that ultimately reduce the costs. This initial momentum includes scaling sustainable aviation fuel
(SAF) production to 35 billion gallons, which would use 2 to 6 MMT/yr of clean hydrogen, and
help reduce air quality impacts by 2050. Although DOE’s hydrogen roadmap does not call out
hydrogen production volumes for direct use in aircraft, it does mention aviation as a potential
new end-use application for hydrogen as the technology matures (“DOE National Clean
Hydrogen Strategy and Roadmap” 2022).

Current costs of steam methane reforming with carbon capture and storage are in the range of $1
to $2 per kilogram, whereas current electrolysis costs $4 to $7 per kilogram of hydrogen
produced (“Cost of Electrolytic Hydrogen Production with Existing Technology,” n.d.). DOE’s
“Hydrogen Shot” cost goal is $1 per kilogram in 1 decade for production, which does not include
delivery, compression, storage, or dispensing costs. Note that 1 kg of hydrogen has

 5

approximately the same energy content as 1 gallon of gasoline or approximately 0.9 gallons of
jet fuel. Thus, initial hydrogen prices for fuel for buses and commercial trucks must be between
$4 and $5 per kilogram to be competitive with incumbent diesel fuel. Hydrogen prices must be
substantially lower than this price to be competitive with aviation fuel, which typically has a
lower price than diesel because it is easier to refine and is typically transported to airports
through pipelines that have a lower cost than truck transportation. However, other industries with
hydrogen threshold prices in the $1 to $3 per kilogram range (e.g., chemicals, iron, industrial
heat, and rail) (Ahluwalia, n.d.) may create demand along with aviation to help make hydrogen
prices competitive with standard aviation fuel. This aspect is important because DOE’s hydrogen
roadmap indicates that research, development, and demonstration (RD&D) along with
deployment at scale are needed to reduce hydrogen costs, which will be critical for hydrogen
aircraft to be deployed at scale.

The Inflation Reduction Act included legislation to invest in a broad number of technologies and
strategies to incentivize new energy pathways, including up to a $3.00/kg-H2 production tax
credit (“Section 45V Credit for Production of Clean Hydrogen; Section 48(a)(15) Election To
Treat Clean Hydrogen Production Facilities as Energy Property” 2023) for hydrogen produced
with less than 0.45 kg-CO2e/kg-H2 (calculated using the Greenhouse Gases, Regulated
Emissions, and Energy Use in Transportation [GREET] model (“GREET | Department of
Energy,” n.d.)). Although this tax credit may substantially help hydrogen cost competitiveness
(e.g., against nonrenewable aviation fuels), implementation details are still being worked
through, including “Additionality” requirements (Esposito, Gimon, and O’Boyle, n.d.).
However, these types of incentives and their specific implementation may significantly impact
how quickly green hydrogen is commercialized and implemented for airport and aircraft
applications.

2.2 Hydrogen Storage
This section covers the key types of hydrogen storage typically used for large-scale facilities. For
storage at scale, there are two primary bulk hydrogen storage methods: surface storage and
underground storage. Surface storage includes (1) vessels for the immediate supply of hydrogen
and (2) chemical storage, which covers compounds such as ammonia, liquid organic hydrogen
carriers, and metal hydrides.

Underground storage offers long duration and seasonal options at scale and includes caverns,
which are large, hollow spaces that can be natural or human made, typically in salt deposits;
depleted reservoirs, which are former oil or natural gas fields; and aquifers, which are
underground layers of water-bearing permeable rock that can be repurposed for hydrogen
storage. These underground methods leverage existing geological formations to safely and
economically store large volumes of hydrogen, with the added benefit of natural containment and
the potential for long-term storage solutions.

2.2.1 Surface Hydrogen Storage
Figure 2 categorizes hydrogen storage technologies into physical-based and material-based
methods. Physical-based storage includes compressed gas in high-pressure tanks,
cold/cryocompressed hydrogen, and liquid hydrogen (LH2) stored at low temperatures as

 6

illustrated in Figure 2. These methods rely on the physical state change of hydrogen to store
energy.

Material-based storage is further divided into two groups based on adsorption and absorption
mechanisms. Adsorption-based storage uses materials such as adsorbents that hold hydrogen
molecules on their surface, whereas absorption-based storage involves materials that integrate
hydrogen into their structure, such as liquid organic hydrogen carriers, interstitial hydrides,
complex hydrides, and chemical hydrogen storage compounds. These material-based methods
allow hydrogen to be stored with a higher density form than physical methods. Each hydrogen
storage method has its unique advantages and challenges, and the choice of technology depends
on the specific application and requirements.

High-pressure gas tanks are the most mature and widely used technology for hydrogen storage.
Hydrogen gas storage commonly requires the use of tanks that can withstand high pressures,
ranging from 350 to 700 bar (5,000 to 10,000 pounds per square inch [psi]). Because of the
nature of GH2, it has low volumetric energy density and requires robust, heavy, and expensive
tanks. Generally, there are four types of high-pressure gaseous storage vessels for hydrogen,
categorized based on their construction (Langmi et al. 2022):

 • Type I: All-metal cylinders, which are the most common and currently less expensive
 than other types. They are made entirely of metal and are used for various pressures.
 • Type II: These have a load-bearing metal liner and are wrapped with resin-impregnated
 continuous fiber. They provide a balance between the durability of metal and the weight
 advantages of composite materials.
 • Type III: These vessels have a non-load-bearing metal liner and are both axial and
 wrapped with resin-impregnated continuous fiber. They offer improved performance over
 Type II tanks in weight and pressure capabilities.
 • Type IV: The most advanced, featuring a non-load-bearing, nonmetal liner, is also axial
 and wrapped with resin-impregnated continuous fiber. These tanks are lighter and can
 handle higher pressures but are currently more expensive. However, with advancements
 in carbon fiber and manufacturing methods, the costs may ultimately decrease below
 those of traditional Type I cylinders.
These pressure vessels are used for on-site storage at hydrogen production facilities, transport
terminals, and end-use locations, with the choice depending on factors such as cost, required
pressure and volume, and weight considerations.

 8

Cold or cryocompressed hydrogen storage refers to the practice of cooling hydrogen gas to
temperatures lower than ambient but not low enough to liquefy the gas. The hydrogen remains in
a gaseous state throughout this process. By cooling the gas, its volume is reduced, and density is
increased, which allows for more efficient storage as more hydrogen can be stored within the
same volume. This method is a balance between the high pressure used in typical compressed
hydrogen storage and the extremely low temperatures required for liquefaction. The temperatures
involved in cold compression are specifically chosen to be above the point at which hydrogen
turns into a liquid, which is –252.87°C at standard atmospheric pressure.

Liquid hydrogen can be stored in cryogenic tanks at cryogenic temperatures. In this method,
hydrogen is cooled below its boiling point and becomes a liquid at atmospheric pressure. This
process requires maintaining temperatures at or below –252.87°C to keep hydrogen in its liquid
state and typically stored at 3–4 bar. This storage method offers a higher volumetric energy
density compared to gaseous storage and is more suitable for large-scale storage and
transportation. The main challenges are the energy required for hydrogen liquefaction, which can
be significant, and maintaining cryogenic temperatures, which requires advanced insulation
technologies. The storage tanks must be designed to withstand low temperatures, using materials
such as glass bubbles, multilayer insulation, and vapor-cooled shields to minimize hydrogen
boil-off (Kang, Yun, and Kim 2022).

Chemical hydrogen storage captures hydrogen within chemical compounds that release the gas
upon decomposition or catalytic reaction, offering high storage densities and controlled release
rates (Andersson and Grönkvist 2019). Identifying and regenerating suitable chemical carriers
safely and efficiently is a challenge, with current research aimed at developing new compounds
and catalysts to improve storage and release efficiency. This storage method is particularly
advantageous for safe transportation and situations, in which traditional gaseous or liquid storage
is impractical.

Furthermore, chemical hydrogen storage methods include metal hydrides, complex hydrides, and
liquid organic hydrogen carriers. Each method has unique release mechanisms and suitable use
cases for different infrastructures. These compounds release hydrogen upon heating or through a
catalytic reaction. Liquid organic hydrogen carriers store hydrogen in liquid form, which can be
convenient for existing liquid fuel infrastructure. Metal hydrides, for example, absorb hydrogen
to form a new compound and release it when heated. Chemical hydrides, such as lithium hydride
(LiH), magnesium hydride (MgH2), and sodium aluminum hydride (NaAlH4), can store
hydrogen in a solid-state form.

Adsorption-based hydrogen storage is another technology that employs the physical process of
adsorption, in which hydrogen molecules adhere to the surface of materials such as activated
carbons, zeolites, or metal-organic frameworks. Unlike absorption, by which hydrogen is taken
into the bulk of the material, adsorption relies on the interaction of hydrogen with the surface of
the materials. These materials have high surface areas, which provide numerous sites for
hydrogen to attach, allowing for storage at near ambient temperatures and low pressures, which
is advantageous for safety and energy efficiency. However, the hydrogen storage capacity of
sorbent materials is typically lower than that of other methods, presenting a trade-off between
storage capacity and the energy required for hydrogen adsorption and desorption (Andersson and
Grönkvist 2019).

 9

2.2.2 Underground Hydrogen Storage
Both geological high-pressure storage and underwater tube storage present innovative solutions
to the challenge of storing hydrogen at scale. These underground storage solutions are crucial for
large-scale hydrogen infrastructure, with significant energy storage capacities. Although these
methods are still being developed and face various technical and environmental challenges, they
represent important steps toward a more flexible and resilient hydrogen infrastructure.

Storing hydrogen in geological formations at high pressure is an emerging approach that
leverages natural underground structures, such as depleted oil and gas fields, aquifers, or salt
caverns, for large-scale, long-term hydrogen storage (“Underground Gas Storage,” n.d.). This
method presents a potentially cost-effective and large-capacity solution, essential for balancing
intermittent renewable energy sources and meeting seasonal energy demands.

Nonporous media, such as domal and bedded salt deposits, use a fluid composition that includes
30% cushion gas and the option to use brine to maintain pressure, a method already proven for
pure hydrogen storage (“Natural Gas Storage - Storage Fields | Federal Energy Regulatory
Commission,” n.d.). The advantages of using salt formations include their relative
impermeability to hydrogen and the commercial viability of the approach (“A Comprehensive
Literature Review on the Challenges Associated with Underground Hydrogen Storage -
ScienceDirect,” n.d.). Salt caverns are the preferred and proven means for safely storing large
volumes of hydrogen. The location of the four largest operational commercial underground
hydrogen storage systems globally, situated in either domal or bedded salt deposits (Hévin
2019). These facilities are located mostly in the United States and the United Kingdom, with the
earliest commissioned in 1972 and the most recent in 2014. Of these projects, the storage
pressure and the energy and volume stored are increasing over time.

On the other hand, porous media, such as depleted reservoirs and deep aquifers, use formation
fluid and a larger proportion of cushion gas at 50% (“Natural Gas Storage - Storage Fields |
Federal Energy Regulatory Commission,” n.d.). There are ongoing pilot projects to investigate
storage in these formations. The primary benefits of porous media are their large capacity and
broad geographical distribution. However, these methods are not yet commercially established,
and there are concerns regarding the integrity of the formation and cap rocks.

The primary challenges in geological storage involve ensuring the integrity and safety of the
storage sites. There is a need to thoroughly assess the geological formations to prevent hydrogen
leakage and to ensure the stored hydrogen does not react adversely with surrounding materials
(“A Review on Well Integrity Issues for Underground Hydrogen Storage | J. Energy Resour.
Technol. | ASME Digital Collection,” n.d.). Another challenge is the potential environmental
impact, such as the risk of induced seismicity or ground subsidence, which requires careful
monitoring and management. Furthermore, the infrastructure for injecting, storing, and retrieving
hydrogen from these formations must be developed and optimized for efficiency and safety.

Advancements in geological surveying and monitoring technologies are key to identifying
suitable storage sites and ensuring their integrity. Necessary advancements include advanced
seismic imaging techniques and real-time monitoring systems such as the use of fiber-optic
sensors to detect any structural changes or leakages. Developing materials and technologies that

 10

can withstand the high pressures and potentially corrosive environment of hydrogen storage is
also crucial. In addition, there is ongoing research into developing efficient compressors and
pumps specifically designed for hydrogen service to optimize injection and extraction processes.

As the hydrogen economy grows at scale, the role of geological high-pressure storage is
expected to become increasingly important. This method of storage could provide a cost-
effective way to store large quantities of hydrogen, facilitating the large-scale adoption of
hydrogen as a clean energy carrier. The development of regulatory frameworks and safety
standards specific to geological hydrogen storage will be essential to ensure safe and
environmentally responsible operations.

Storing hydrogen in tubes or containers under water is a novel concept that leverages the natural
pressure of water depths to contain hydrogen at high pressures. Hunt et al. introduced the
Hydrogen Deep Ocean Link, (“Hydrogen Deep Ocean Link: A Global Sustainable
Interconnected Energy Grid - ScienceDirect,” n.d.), a sustainable energy grid concept leveraging
deep-sea hydrogen storage in inexpensive plastic tanks. It is designed to balance with seawater
and is anchored by sand to counteract buoyancy. Compression and descent to the ocean floor are
managed by an isothermal compressor with an 80% efficiency rate. This system allows the tanks
to gradually sink and compress the hydrogen, bypassing the need for expensive compression
methods. The system’s investment and operational costs are substantially lower than current
methods, such as deep ocean hydrogen pipelines and submarines. The evolution of this
technology will be closely linked to advancements in materials, robotics, and environmental
impact assessment methodologies.

However, the challenges of underwater hydrogen storage are significant. These include ensuring
the structural integrity of storage containers against corrosive seawater and fluctuating pressure
conditions. There is also the challenge of developing efficient and safe systems for hydrogen
transportation to and from these underwater storage facilities. Environmental considerations,
such as the potential impact on marine ecosystems, must be carefully evaluated.

2.3 Hydrogen Transport
Most hydrogen used in the United States is produced at or close to where it is used—typically at
large industrial sites. This aspect is due to several factors, including the economies of scale,
benefit of centralized hydrogen production, the poor volumetric energy density of hydrogen and
related cost to compress or liquefy it into a more energy-dense state, and the high cost of delivery
equipment resulting from limited technology development. When hydrogen must be transported,
it is typically delivered via gaseous tube trailers, liquid tanker trucks, or gaseous hydrogen
pipelines. It can also be transported via railcar or barge, but for the purposes of this study, this
document focuses on the more common transport methods, which are suitable methods for
delivering hydrogen to airports.

 11

2.3.1 Trucking Transport
For small hydrogen demand points that are close to production (less than 200 miles), gaseous
tube trailer delivery is most viable. Traditionally, tube trailers carry steel cylinders that hold
hydrogen at 200 bar. These tube trailer trucks carry around 100 to 200 kg hydrogen per
truckload. Compressed hydrogen can also be transported at pressures between 450 and 700 bar if
composite cylinders are used to store the hydrogen. Depending on the composite material used
and the cylinder pressure, these tube trailer trucks can carry up to 700 kg per trip. The energy
requirement to compress hydrogen from production outlet to between 450 and 700 bar is
typically between 2 and 4 kilowatt-hours (kWh) electricity per kilogram H2 (Gardiner 2009b).
For reference, the typical electricity requirement for electrolysis hydrogen production is 55 kWh
per kilogram H2 (“Technical Targets for Proton Exchange Membrane Electrolysis,” n.d.). In the
case of hydrogen usage at an airport, hydrogen delivery via tube trailers will only be viable in the
short term when hydrogen infrastructure buildout is minimal and daily demand is less than or
equal to the hydrogen-carrying capacity of a few tube trailer trucks (roughly less than 1,000 kg
per day). For reference, a small hydrogen-powered aircraft with a range of 250 to 500 nautical
miles is expected to have 100 kg of onboard gaseous hydrogen storage (H2-Aero Team 2023).
Therefore, gaseous tube trailer delivery would make the most sense if about 10 or less total
refueling instances are needed for this aircraft type at an airport each day.

When delivery distance is large (greater than ~200 miles) and, more important, when daily
hydrogen demands begin to exceed the carrying capacity of gaseous tube trailer trucks, liquid
hydrogen trucking becomes an economically viable delivery method. Hydrogen is converted
from a gaseous state to a liquid state using liquid nitrogen precooling and a Joule-Thomson
expansion liquefaction process (Züttel 2003). It can then be transported in a liquid hydrogen
tanker truck, which can hold around 3,800 kg H2 per truck. Unlike compressed hydrogen, LH2
remains close to ambient pressure but is kept at a temperature below –253°C. The electricity
input for hydrogen liquefaction is around 10 to 13 kWh per kilogram H2 (Gardiner 2009a). One
caveat of LH2 delivery is that if the liquefied hydrogen is not used at a sufficiently high rate at
the point of consumption, it boils off (or evaporates) from its containment vessels. As a result,
LH2 delivery and consumption rates must be carefully matched. For airport demand applications,
LH2 delivery is most economical if daily airport demand levels are less than or equal to the
carrying capacity of a few liquid tanker trucks (roughly 1 to 20 metric ton per day) and if the size
of the off-site hydrogen production and liquefaction facility is sufficiently large to consider an
economy of scale benefit that offsets the high cost of liquefaction (Bracci, Koleva, and Chung
2024a).

2.3.2 Pipeline Transport
Because hydrogen demand increases further and delivery via gaseous or liquid trucking would
require several truck trips per day, delivery via pipeline, or on-site production with a
consideration of on-site liquefaction, becomes more attractive. These two delivery options would
be most viable at an airport where a considerable percentage of the aircraft passing through
operate using hydrogen or hydrogen-derived fuels (i.e., demand of approximately 101 to 103
metric tons per day). At this demand level, economy of scale benefits can be realized for either
on-site hydrogen production or hydrogen pipeline delivery infrastructure. In the case where on-
site production is possible, no distribution costs would be incurred.

 12

As of 2023, there are roughly 1,600 miles of gaseous hydrogen pipelines in the United States,
with more than 1,000 miles in the Gulf Coast region (“Hydrogen Hub Proposed for US Gulf
Coast — Chevron,” n.d.) (“Hydrogen Pipelines,” n.d.). These pipelines are used primarily for
delivering hydrogen to high-demand industrial facilities. Key challenges with building new
hydrogen-dedicated pipelines include high upfront capital costs and a long construction time
frame. Technical challenges with pipeline materials and compressor design must also be
overcome to minimize hydrogen permeation and leaks from pipelines.

An alternative pipeline transportation/delivery approach involves using the present natural gas
pipeline infrastructure. Recently, some utilities have initiated demonstration projects to blend up
to 20% by volume of hydrogen with methane in isolated portions of their natural gas distribution
systems (“Hydrogen,” n.d.). Planning is also being done to perform demonstrations in the
transmission pipelines and to increase the hydrogen content. However, analysts estimate that
20% H2 may be the maximum allowable blend before significant costs will have to be incurred to
upgrade the pipelines because of potential impacts on pipeline materials (Energy Futures
Initiative and Energy and Environmental Economics 2020). In addition, hydrogen separation
from natural gas at the airport will require an additional expense that would not be needed with a
dedicated hydrogen pipeline. The airport would also have to make use of the separated natural
gas stream, which is emission intensive.

Although most airports currently use pipelines to bring in aviation fuel, many other factors will
have to be considered to determine if pipelines can deliver enough H2 to airports to meet
hydrogen aircraft needs. These factors include present and future H2 production facility locations
and capacities, H2 demand requirements, costs for new and converting pipeline infrastructure for
H2, electric and renewable resource availability, as well as eventual costs for on-site H2
production, compression, and liquefaction.

Another method of hydrogen delivery that eliminates the need for high-cost compression or
liquefaction is hydrogen-carrier transport. Hydrogen carriers have a high hydrogen weight
percentage and are found in a liquid or solid state at or near ambient conditions. This method
allows for lower-cost, higher-throughput hydrogen transport as compared to pure hydrogen
transport alternatives. Common hydrogen carriers include methanol, ammonia, liquid organic
hydrogen carriers, and metal hydrides. Although transporting hydrogen becomes lower cost
when attached to a carrier, energy input is required to both produce the carrier from pure
hydrogen and dehydrogenate the carrier at the final end-use location. The cost of these additional
steps negates the benefit of lower-cost transport, therefore reducing the attractiveness of the
hydrogen-carrier. However, the idea of using hydrogen carriers is still nascent, and with further
research and development, hydrogen-carrier production and delivery may scale down in cost
enough to become a viable option for many applications.

Continued innovation and advancement in hydrogen delivery technologies will aid in reducing
cost and minimizing technical concerns such as hydrogen leakage. Though it will take time,
these advancements will enable a national hydrogen delivery network to develop that will likely
include combinations of the various technologies described. Delivery infrastructure needs and
resources will also vary by region and type of market (e.g., urban, interstate, coastal, or rural).
Continued research will help to identify the optimal delivery solutions for each region and
market type over the course of the system operating life.

2.4 Hydrogen Fueling Methods
Heavy-duty and medium-duty vehicles emerge as early adopters of H2 fuel because of their large
onboard energy capacity requirements. The low specific energy storage capacity and slow
charging times of batteries make them nonviable for heavy-duty/medium-duty vehicles. With the
rapidly growing demand for low-carbon aviation solutions, hydrogen stands out as a viable
option for powering aircraft. To ensure the seamless utilization of hydrogen, it is crucial to
understand and develop various fueling methods, leveraging the existing fueling protocols used
for on-ground vehicles. This section details lessons learned from on-ground transportation
applications.

2.4.1 Gaseous Fueling
As discussed in section 2.2, on-ground vehicles use onboard high-/medium-pressure tanks to
store hydrogen. These tanks can be filled by using the pressure difference to flow hydrogen gas
from bulk storage tanks at a station to onboard vehicle tanks. To achieve faster fueling
performance, the hydrogen gas is often precooled, mitigating hot spots on the liner material of
vehicle tanks while fueling as a result of the Joule-Thomson effect. This process needs a chiller
to precool hydrogen gas –20 to –40°C.

Another method is the liquid-to-high-pressure gas transfer. In this process, LH2, initially at
cryogenic temperatures of –253°C, is passed through a vaporizer—a device specifically designed
to add heat to the LH2 as a heat exchanger, prompting it to undergo a phase change. As the
temperature of LH2 increases, it transitions from its liquid state to a gaseous state. This high-
pressure, low-temperature GH2 is transferred to vehicle tanks.

To meet the rapid expanse of airports and the growing needs of hydrogen-powered aircraft, the
aviation industry may initially look to a less fixed infrastructure. Mobile refueling vehicles
emerge as a more practical solution in the near-term adaptation compared to traditional
centralized, fixed refueling stations for on-ground vehicle applications. In fact, these mobile
vehicles can directly approach the aircraft, ensuring faster turnaround times and increased
efficiency. This decentralized approach also could be effective for general aviation ramp
configurations and allow for more flexible ramp management.

Although mobile refueling offers swift refueling procedures, there are a few challenges to be
considered. An influx of refueling vehicles might intensify ground traffic, potentially introducing
logistical challenges during peak airport hours. In addition, the fuel capacity of these vehicles
might be limited, which could demand more frequent refills. For example, Linde offers a mobile
hydrogen refueling station capable of storing 500 kg of hydrogen at a storage pressure of 50 MPa
(Cano et al. 2018). Such a unit could refuel small-scale hydrogen-powered aircraft for the near
term. It is important to develop protocols that enhance fueling performance while ensuring safety
of hydrogen fueling in the airport environment.

For mobile refueling application to fuel a smaller aircraft, the J2601/4 protocol can be used. This
protocol is a well-established standard designed to guide the fueling procedures for light-duty
fuel cell electric vehicles when the fuel delivery temperature is not precooled, so called “ambient
fueling” (“J2601/4_202410: Ambient Temperature Variable and Fixed-Orifice Fueling Protocols
for Light-Duty Gaseous Hydrogen Surface Vehicles - SAE International,” n.d.). Its application
becomes particularly significant when a protocol for refueling of hydrogen-powered aircraft is

 14

not yet available. One important aspect in this protocol is the use of a fixed orifice, which plays a
crucial role in regulating the flow of hydrogen during the refueling process. The fixed orifice
serves as a consistent flow-regulating mechanism, ensuring a stable flow of hydrogen throughout
the refueling procedure. By maintaining a predetermined flow rate, the fixed orifice eliminates
potential risks associated with variable flow rates, such as overpressure or exceeded heat
generation from the turbulence flow, thus enhancing safety and reliability. However, the nature
of ambient fueling means it takes time to fuel hydrogen.

For gaseous hydrogen fueling for light-duty fuel cell electric vehicles, SAE J2601 establishes
protocols to ensure safety, performance, and communication between vehicles and hydrogen
stations (“J2601_202005: Fueling Protocols for Light Duty Gaseous Hydrogen Surface Vehicles
- SAE International,” n.d.) (“J2601/2_202307: Fueling Protocol for Gaseous Hydrogen Powered
Heavy Duty Vehicles - SAE International,” n.d.):

 • Fuel delivery temperature: It prescribes fueling conditions in which the hydrogen gas
 temperature is precooled to –20°C (denoted as T20), –30°C (T30), and −40°C (T40).
 • Communication: It supports both communication-based fueling (with communication
 between vehicle and fueling station) and noncommunication fueling.
 • Safety: It emphasizes safety measures such as leak checks and ensuring fuel is delivered
 within certain temperature and pressure limits to prevent overpressurization.
 • Performance: It aims for a consistent fill of the hydrogen tank to the state of charge
 between 95% and 100% of its rated capacity under all operating conditions.
 • Efficiency: It prioritizes quick refueling with an average refueling rate of 2 to 4 kg/min
 based on a peak refueling rate of 3.6 to 7.3 kg/min and typically targets a 3- to 5-minute
 refueling time for light-duty vehicles.
 • Applications: The primary target is passenger vehicles but can also be applied to other
 light-duty vehicles, such as forklifts and other industrial vehicles.
As part of an active area of research resulting from the growing interest in hydrogen as a
transportation fuel, research and development in heavy-duty hydrogen refueling has seen an
uptick. NREL, Toyota, Hydrogen Europe, and Daimler Truck are among pioneers in advanced
fueling technologies and are working to establish standards that ensure efficient and safe
refueling. Despite the potential, the industry faces challenges related to limited research in
fueling rates, equipment reliability, and the technology readiness level of high-flow hydrogen
refueling (Elgowainy 2018). As the demand for larger vehicle operations increases (e.g., aircraft
application), addressing the issues of refueling these vehicles becomes vital. They might demand
flow rates exceeding 10 kg/min to expedite the refueling process. This flow rate target highlights
the necessity to develop a high-capacity liquid to gaseous hydrogen fueling system. Both DOE
and the industry support this direction, targeting fueling rates of at least 10 kg/min and peak rates
between 18 and 20 kg/min (Elgowainy 2018). Although higher flow rates offer the advantage of
fueling performance, they introduce complications such as managing the resulting heat and
maintaining equipment durability under such conditions. Thus, the industry’s primary goal is to
establish reliable, efficient, and safety-focused refueling standards for the next generation of
heavy-duty vehicles and aircraft. Because of the similarity in size of early adapters of novel
hydrogen aircraft, developing and refining protocols are essential to deploying hydrogen-based
aircraft.

 15

By leveraging the existing fueling protocols, the SAE AE-5 Committee has been working on the
development and endorsement of standards related to hydrogen fuel systems specifically for
aviation applications (“AE-5 Aerospace Fuel, Inerting and Lubrication Systems - SAE
StandardsWorks,” n.d.). The AE-5CH Hydrogen Airport Taskgroup has the responsibility for
hydrogen as a fuel at the airport (“AE-5CH Hydrogen Airport Taskgroup - SAE
StandardsWorks,” n.d.). The objective is to develop standardization for hydrogen in aviation and
other aspects at the airport such as fueling, transport, and storage. Bringing together with existing
SAE standards efforts (including SAE, EUROCAE, etc.) and codes (e.g., National Fire
Protection Association [NFPA]) will be a priority for this committee and a potential resource for
aviation regulatory agencies when completed.

2.5 Hydrogen Fuel Cells
Fuel cell technologies has been used for decades for on-ground vehicle applications and can be
integrated to support an aircraft propulsion system. This section categorizes fuel cell technology
primarily in low and high temperature. This section also convers a hybridization approach for
fuel cell integrated systems.

2.5.1 Low-Temperature Fuel Cell
Although the chemical energy in hydrogen can be used for propulsion in an aircraft via a
combustion engine or a fuel cell, this section focuses on the latter. In a fuel-cell-run aircraft
propulsion system, liquid or compressed hydrogen is used to produce electricity, which powers
motors. The motors are connected to the propellers on the aircraft designed to meet the
propulsion demand. Operating a fuel cell system also requires efficient management of auxiliary
systems, which comprise heat rejection, humidity control, stack operating point, fluid
conveyance, and electrical systems. These auxiliary systems are often bundled into the term
“balance of plant.”

There are several promising short- and long-term solutions currently being explored for
propulsion systems. Although solutions around improving aircraft efficiency and facilitating the
use of SAF are promising to de-intensify long-distance flights, hydrogen-powered and electrified
propulsion systems have emerged as leading practical solutions to diversify propulsion systems
using a different source of fuels. There are many challenges associated with the use of fuel cell
systems in aviation, such as sizing the hydrogen storage, optimizing the fuel cell stack
configuration, evaluating optimum balance of plant configuration, and evaluating propulsion
system mass updates because of additional storage requirements and space constraints. In a
recent work by Svensson et al. (“Hydrogen Fuel Cell Aircraft for the Nordic Market -
ScienceDirect,” n.d.), a dynamic model was developed for H2 consumption and tank pressure
control for a hydrogen-powered aircraft using a fuel cell. The study evaluated optimal tank
design for both liquid and compressed gaseous H2 options. The study also optimized fuel cell
stack configuration and evaluated the updates in propulsion system mass. For demand modeling
of H2 consumption during flights, the study used Stanford University Aerospace Vehicle
Environment (SUAVE) software (Botero et al. 2016). The study concluded that the optimized
fuel cell and storage tank design parameters will successfully be able to cover the 648 nautical
miles design range for the projected flight demand of Nordic countries for the year 2045. In
another recent study, Zaghari et al. studied the effect of multi-stack proton exchange membrane
(PEM) fuel cell configurations on system efficiency, total aircraft weight, controllability and

 16

power reduction because of efficiency losses (“The Impact of Multi-Stack Fuel Cell
Configurations on Electrical Architecture for a Zero Emission Regional Aircraft | AIAA SciTech
Forum,” n.d.). Optimum configurations of individual cells connected in series to form a stack
and then multiple stacks connected in parallel to meet the desired power (which is approximately
4 megawatts, MW) were identified along with the ideal power converter design.

A performance analysis study of fuel cell retrofit analysis by Mukhopadhaya models a retrofit
ATR 72 and Dash 8 Q300 turboprop aircraft with both LH2 and compressed GH2 options
(Mukhopadhaya and Rutherford, n.d.). The study analyzed the effect of increased fuel-carrying
capacity of LH2 and GH2 on passenger capacity and aircraft’s maximum range. The study also
conducted a fuels analysis that quantified carbon intensity and cost of fueling a retrofit aircraft
with GH2 and LH2 versus fueling with regular jet fuel (Jet A). To model H2 consumption, the
study assumed a low, central, and high average fuel cell efficiency of 0.45, 0.55, and 0.65,
respectively. These values were based on inputs from industry experts and review of the state-of-
the-art fuel cell technologies. Because GH2 and LH2 are, respectively, seven times and four times
lower in energy density than jet fuel, they require additional space claim to carry enough
chemical energy in the aircraft. In addition to that analysis, they have challenging thermal and
structural constraints to ensure adequate storage on the aircraft. The study also considered
different ranges of relevant thermodynamic and structural parameters to estimate their effect on
life cycle emission values. To estimate H2 consumption during flight path and to optimize
component sizing, the study used a low-fidelity aerodynamic analysis to calculate lift, drag, and
corresponding required thrust force using SUAVE open-source simulation environment (Botero
et al. 2016).

2.5.2 High-Temperature Fuel Cell
Approximately 40% to 60% of the chemical energy is converted to heat in a polymer exchange
membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) (Kazula, Graaf, and Enghardt 2023). Because of their high
technology readiness level, compact build, and high-power densities as compared to other fuel
cell types, low-temperature PEMs are the most commonly used fuel cell type. However, their
low operating temperatures require bulky and efficient thermal management systems because of
a low-grade operating temperature and a smaller temperature gradient between the fuel cell
system and environment to eliminate heat. Moreover, the need for oversizing the cooling system
in low-temperature PEMFCs translates into increased air intake requirements, resulting in higher
drag on the aircraft (Baroutaji et al. 2019).

High-temperature fuel cells, operating above 130°C , have the potential to address the issues
highlighted previously. Operating at a high temperature can result in enhanced rates of chemical
kinetics and additionally allow better exergetic potential of the waste heat recovery (Zhang et al.
2006). Furthermore, membranes in high-temperature PEMFCs do not require enhanced humidity
control, unlike low-temperature PEMs. Because of these factors, the water management systems
and cooling systems are simplified for these fuel cells. High-temperature PEMFCs also have a
higher tolerance to carbon monoxide impurities, which can allow these fuel cells to use lower-
quality reformed hydrogen. However, high-temperature fuel cells suffer from several
shortcomings. High operating temperatures of high-temperature PEMFCs can lead to increased
thermal, chemical, and structural stresses. The use of phosphoric acid in high-temperature
PEMFC membranes can also result in potential acid leakage and durability issues. High-
temperature solid oxide fuel cells suffer from long startup times, sealing issues, complicated

 17

controls, and reduced lifetime (“Introduction to Fuel Cells: Electrochemistry and Materials |
SpringerLink,” n.d.). However, the landscape in high-temperature PEMFCs research is rapidly
evolving because of significant ongoing advances in membrane electrode assembly,
sophisticated flow channels to enhance heat and mass transport, durability, and
commercialization of such systems (Barabanova 2023) (Xiao et al. 2024).

2.5.3 Hybrid Electrification With Fuel Cell System
Several studies in the literature have evaluated the performance of hybrid systems in which an
onboard battery energy storage system is also placed to allow for meeting a surge in power
demand during takeoff or landing (Park et al. 2022) (“Development of Fuel Cell/Battery Hybrid
System Sizing Methodology for eVTOL Aircraft | AIAA AVIATION Forum,” n.d.)
(“Development of a Lumped Parameter Model for an Aeronautic Hybrid Electric Propulsion
System,” n.d.) (Marinaro, Di Lorenzo, and Pagano 2022). Palladino et al. formulated a
conceptual design of a hybrid electric and fuel cell system for a regional aircraft with 70- to 80-
passenger capacity (“Preliminary Studies of a Regional Aircraft with Hydrogen-Based Hybrid
Propulsion | Request PDF,” n.d.). The analysis starts with a baseline conventional turboprop
kerosene-based aircraft and uses a multidisciplinary design analysis approach to determine the
optimal sizing of a fuel cell and battery hybrid propulsion system for the same aircraft. The twin-
propeller aircraft model is developed with a conceptual design platform called FAST-OAD
(Future Aircraft Sizing Tool—Overall Aircraft Design), (Sy et al. 2023) which uses a
combination of physics-based analyses and empirical correlations to update aircraft masses and
optimize geometry and performance.

2.6 Hydrogen Combustion Engine
Retrofitting existing jet combustion aircraft to hydrogen combustion involves substantial
investments in research and development. This transition requires addressing issues such as
mitigating hydrogen embrittlement, managing higher combustion temperatures and flame speeds,
and developing new combustion control mechanisms with innovative fuel injectors
(“Performance Analysis of Evolutionary Hydrogen-Powered Aircraft - International Council on
Clean Transportation,” n.d.). A study by Mukhopadhaya and Rutherford (2022) indicates
hydrogen combustion aircraft could service one-third of the commercial aviation market but are
unlikely to enter service before 2035 because of the need for large-scale hydrogen infrastructure
(“Performance Analysis of Evolutionary Hydrogen-Powered Aircraft - International Council on
Clean Transportation,” n.d.). The following section provides a brief overview of combustion in
turbine engines.

2.6.1 Combustion in Turbine Engines
Traditional turbine engine combustors, both terrestrial and in aircraft, can run on hydrogen in
some cases. However, the systems are optimized for petroleum fuels and therefore see a decrease
in performance when hydrogen is used. This drawback is attributed to the differences in fuel-air
ratio for the petroleum-derived fuels and hydrogen combustion reactions (Amy and Kunycky
2019a). High-bypass turbofan engines used on modern commercial airliners produce thrust based
largely on a mass flow. The bypass ratio is defined as the ratio of the mass flow of air bypassing
the engine core to the mass flow of air passing through the core. Engine cores burning hydrogen
fuel require only 77% of the mass flow to produce the same energy output driving the core,
resulting in lower specific fuel burn rates (by mass) and the potential for greater bypass ratios in

 18

hydrogen engines producing the same thrust as their petroleum-derived fuel-burning counterparts
(“On the Energy Efficiency of Hydrogen-Fuelled Transport Aircraft - ScienceDirect,” n.d.)
(Khandelwal et al. 2013) (“An Approach to the Use of Hydrogen for Commercial Aircraft
Engines - G Corchero, J L Montañés, 2005,” n.d.).

In hydrogen combustion engine operation, the main reaction products are water and a small
amount of nitrogen oxides (NOX). The overall efficiency of the engine is associated with the
concentration of oxygen supplied to the combustor; however, oxygen is typically sourced from
the ambient air. This approach reduces the amount of gas that must be stored onboard,
maximizing the hydrogen fuel carried and thereby increasing the aircraft’s range (Khandelwal et
al. 2013). The production of NOX is minimized through lean burning and lower flame
temperatures, though the water vapor and NOX produced still have environmental impacts. Well-
designed systems can further minimize NOX production, contributing to the overall
environmental benefits of hydrogen fuel (Khandelwal et al. 2013) (Dahl and Suttrop 1998).

With various requirements for fuel capacity and consumption, both hydrogen and combustion
systems share a common layout that includes tanks, boost pumps, heat exchangers, and a venturi
section. The tanks are designed to store liquid (or gaseous) hydrogen and feature a boil-off rate
of about 0.05% to 0.08% per hour (by mass) (Amy and Kunycky 2019a). Insulated supply lines,
designed as double-layer vacuum tubes to minimize heat transfer, carry fuel from the tanks to the
liquid fuel boost pumps. These pumps ensure a steady stream of liquid hydrogen leaves the tank
to meet the fuel consumption rate. Stored and initially pumped at 20.28K, the liquid hydrogen
passes through heat exchangers that raise the temperature and convert it to a gas before it reaches
the combustor (“Dynamic-Stability Characteristics of Premixed Methane Oxy-Combustion | J.
Eng. Gas Turbines Power | ASME Digital Collection,” n.d.) (“Explosion Hazard from a
Propellant-Tank Breach in Liquid Hydrogen-Oxygen Rockets | Journal of Spacecraft and
Rockets,” n.d.).

Combustion in turbine engines burning hydrogen fuel presents unique challenges, such as the
potential for flashback or spread of combustion up the supply line. This risk is low during
operation but presents more significant challenges at startup and shutdown. To mitigate this risk,
supply lines can be flushed with an inert gas such as nitrogen (Dahl and Suttrop 1998)
(“Hydrogen Combustion: Features and Barriers to Its Exploitation in the Energy Transition,”
n.d.). Previous research shows hydrogen has the potential to reduce specific fuel consumption
nearly by a factor of 3 compared to petroleum-derived fuel-burning engines producing the same
thrust (“An Approach to the Use of Hydrogen for Commercial Aircraft Engines - G Corchero, J
L Montañés, 2005,” n.d.) (Tashie-Lewis and Nnabuife 2021).

 Environment

efficiency, economy, and resilience of the entire system. This section describes grid-integration
application, backup and stationary power opportunity, and combined heat and power to design an
airport as a whole energy system.

2.7.1 Hydrogen Energy Storage on the Grid
Managing electrical grid capacity and reliability is a key as airport demand increases. Hydrogen
energy storage has the potential to play a significant role in grid stabilization and the integration
of renewable energy sources. Hydrogen serves as a versatile energy carrier that can store excess
electricity generated during periods of low demand and high renewable output and release it
during periods of high demand or low renewable generation. Energy storage on the grid can have
several different roles, including time shifting, guaranteeing firm capacity, avoiding transmission
line buildout, and providing ancillary services. Presently, grid energy storage is dominated by
pumped hydropower deployments capable of discharging power for up to 12 hours (“Advanced
Hydropower and PSH Capacity Expansion Modeling: Final Report on HydroWIRES D1
Improvements to Capacity Expansion Modeling - National Renewable Energy Laboratory,”
n.d.). Meanwhile, lithium-ion batteries are the fastest-growing mode of energy storage,
commonly for shorter durations of 4 hours or less (Denholm, Cole, and Blair 2023).

Figure 4 illustrates the integration of hydrogen production and usage within the existing energy
storage system (Melaina and Eichman 2015). Melaina and Eichman at NREL suggest two
primary routes for electrolytic hydrogen production: one upstream using electricity from various
sources including renewables, and one downstream, which is closer to the end use. Hydrogen can
be injected into the natural gas network or used directly in various markets, such as fuel cell
vehicles or industrial processes.

The diagram includes methanation, in which hydrogen is combined with carbon dioxide to
produce methane, and hydrogen separation processes. It highlights the versatility of hydrogen as

an energy carrier that can be produced from and integrated into diverse energy systems,
including electricity and natural gas, to provide flexibility and storage options for variable
renewable energy sources, such as solar and wind. The system suggests a future in which
hydrogen plays a central role in balancing and supporting a more energy efficient and resilient
energy grid.

The integration of hydrogen energy storage on the grid presents several challenges. A primary
concern is the round-trip efficiency of energy conversion processes. Energy is lost during
electrolysis, hydrogen storage, and reconversion to electricity, which currently results in a lower
round-trip efficiency compared to other energy storage methods. The cost of the necessary
technologies, including electrolyzers and fuel cells, is also a significant factor. Although costs
have been decreasing, they still represent a substantial investment, especially for large-scale
applications.

Moreover, developing the infrastructure for hydrogen storage and distribution network is a
complex task. It involves considerations for safe hydrogen handling, storage (whether as a
compressed gas, liquid, or solid form), and transportation, possibly through existing natural gas
pipelines with appropriate modifications. This infrastructure development requires substantial
capital investment and regulatory support. Hydrogen storage on the grid offers unique
advantages over other storage technologies. It is highly scalable, meaning it can be used for
small local grids as well as for large regional energy storage. Hydrogen can be stored for longer
periods, making it suitable for seasonal storage, a capability that batteries currently struggle with.
This aspect of hydrogen storage is particularly valuable for regions with significant seasonal
variations in renewable energy generation and demand.

2.7.2 Hydrogen for Backup Power and Stationary Power
Backup power and stationary power from fuel cells can replace diesel generators to provide
resiliency to critical facilities that require 24/7 power; both are currently being deployed in
facilities such as hospitals and data centers. Hydrogen fuel cells, which convert hydrogen into
electricity, offer several advantages over traditional diesel generators, including lower emissions,
quieter operation, and the potential for longer run times. However, the integration of hydrogen
into backup power systems faces challenges. These include the initial cost of fuel cell
technology, which remains higher than conventional generators. In addition, ensuring a reliable
supply of hydrogen, either through on-site generation or delivery, is critical, and this involves
developing an appropriate hydrogen infrastructure. There is also a need to address safety
concerns related to hydrogen storage and handling.

Hydrogen can be used not only as fuel for aircraft but also as backup power for critical loads
such as airports. It is important to consider the airport as a whole energy system and integrate
technologies to build resilient energy systems.

2.7.3 Hydrogen Combined Heat and Power
Large facility campuses such as larger airport facilities have historically benefited from central
heating and cooling facilities that, in some cases, include power generation. Combined heat and
power plants typically use turbines to generate electricity for local use or export to the grid, and
the waste heat from turbine exhaust is then used to service heating loads. Natural gas turbines are
typically used, with existing high volume natural gas pipelines servicing these facilities.

 21

In addition to work by various manufacturers, efforts such as the New York Power Authority's
recent study with GE and EPRI explored blending up to 40% hydrogen into existing turbines as a
means to reduce emissions (“20220923-GreenHydrogen,” n.d.).

A more advanced example of hydrogen combined heat and power is the Fountain Valley energy
station in California, a project that received $2.2 million in sponsorship from the U.S.
Department of Energy (“Tri-Generation Success Story: World’s First Tri-Gen Energy Station -
Fountain Valley,” n.d.). It is the first of its kind to integrate hydrogen production, electricity, and
heat in a single tri-generation system. Powered by biogas from wastewater treatment, the
facility’s molten carbonate fuel cell generates 100 kg of hydrogen daily, fueling public vehicles
(25 to 50 fuel cell electric vehicle fills daily) and providing 250 kilowatts (kW) of power to the
plant. This innovative system highlights the effective use of renewable waste streams and
positions tri-generation as a key player in advancing hydrogen infrastructure.

2.8.1 Hydrogen Equipment
Hydrogen Technologies Code (NFPA 2) (“NFPA 2, Code,” n.d.) is the most comprehensive
standard for hydrogen implementation at airports. It covers the design, construction, and safety
of dozens of hydrogen systems. To consider hydrogen infrastructure at airports, Chapters 6–8
and 10–13 are the most relevant, but the entire code should be taken into consideration. Chapters
6–8 look at hydrogen technology infrastructure components such as piping, ventilation, gas
detection systems, and liquid and gaseous hydrogen storage. Sections 7.3 and 8.3 detail the bulk
hydrogen storage systems that would be needed for hydrogen fueling in the airport environment.
Chapters 10 and 11 discuss the design, installation, dispensing, and maintenance of liquid and
gaseous hydrogen fueling facilities. These sections pertain to stationary road vehicle fueling
facilities. Chapters 12 and 13 cover hydrogen fuel cell and hydrogen generation systems,
respectively. Although Chapter 12 is focused on stationary hydrogen fuel cells, it is still relevant
for aircraft manufacturers looking to develop fuel cell power systems for airplanes as well as
backup power systems at airports for resiliency. Chapter 13 covers three types of hydrogen
generation systems—electrolyzers, catalytic-reforming-based systems, and gasifiers—and
provides information regarding hydrogen generation on-site at airports.

The American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) (“Safety Codes and Standards by
ASME - ASME,” n.d.), the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) (“ANSI Safety
Standards,” n.d.), and the Compressed Gas Association (CGA) (“Home - Compressed Gas
Association,” n.d.) are organizations that produce multiple safety standards that are relevant to
hydrogen equipment and technology in North America.

 24

ASME B31.12 (“B31.12 - Hydrogen Piping & Pipelines | Digital Book - ASME,” n.d.) was first
published in 2008 as an extension of ASME B31.3 (“B31.3 - Process Piping - ASME,” n.d.),
Process Piping. B31.12 addresses hydrogen piping and pipelines, with Part IP discussing piping
materials, welding, testing, inspections, repairs, and decommissioning, and Part PL covering
pipeline design, installation, and testing. During the design, installation, and maintenance of
hydrogen systems at airports, ASME B31.12 should be referenced. Section VIII of the ASME
Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code describes the design, testing, inspection, and material
requirements of pressure vessels that operate at 15 psi or greater. This section is one of the most
important safety standards for the implementation of hydrogen in the airline industry. It applies
to airport hydrogen storage systems, hydrogen fuel tanks on airplanes, and pipelines large
enough to qualify as pressure vessels.

OSHA 1910.103 addresses the installation of gaseous and liquid hydrogen systems on consumer
premises. As a Federal Code of Regulation, it outlines minimum safety requirements for GH2
and LH2 storage, safety relief devices, piping, tubing, fittings, and setback distances. OSHA
1910.103 was last revised in 2007. As noted in Annex G of NFPA 2, the “criteria established in
OSHA’s tables of distances are based on the 1969 edition of NFPA 50A.” NFPA 50A was later
revised prior to integration NFPA 55 and later the hydrogen specific content in NFPA 55 was
moved to NFPA 2 (“NFPA 2, Code,” n.d.). Stakeholders should consult the Federal Code of
Regulations when designing, constructing, and operating hydrogen systems to ensure that
OSHA’s minimum requirements are met.

CGA G-5.6 outlines pipelines that transport gaseous hydrogen between –40°C and 175°C and 1
megapascal (MPa) and 21 MPa. The publicly available ICC International Fuel Gas Code Section
705 (“CHAPTER 7 GASEOUS HYDROGEN SYSTEMS - 2018 INTERNATIONAL FUEL
GAS CODE (IFGC),” n.d.) explains requirements for the testing of hydrogen piping systems.
ANSI/CGA H-5 analyzes hydrogen storage further by discussing the siting, installation,
maintenance, and decommissioning of bulk hydrogen systems. CGA G-5.5 and CGA S-1.3 are
key safety standards that dive into individual hydrogen system components. G-5.5 describes the
installation, design, and operation requirements for hydrogen ventilation systems. S-1.3 outlines
the minimum requirements for pressure relief devices in compressed gas systems and is based on
content in the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code. Vented hydrogen and hazardous
hydrogen leaks cause the area surrounding bulk storage systems to have special requirements.

There are multiple ways to get gaseous hydrogen to airport bulk storage. If stakeholders plan to
generate hydrogen on-site, they should refer to NFPA 2 and CSA/ANSI B22734. NFPA 2
Section 13.3 addresses electrolyzers, which convert water into hydrogen and oxygen using
energy. NFPA 2 also addresses carbon-based hydrogen generation methods, such as gasification
and methane reforming, but these processes generate a high amount of GHG emissions that work
against the goal of clean hydrogen. CSA/ANSI B22734 is a standard that discusses hydrogen
production through water electrolysis for commercial and industrial uses. B22734 and NFPA 2
represent comprehensive system-level recommendations for electrolyzers. For electrolyzer
components, stakeholders should reference CGA G-5.5 for vent systems, ASME B31.12 for
piping systems, NFPA 70 for classification of hazardous areas, and ISO 14687 for hydrogen
quality recommendations.

2.8.2 Energy Systems
Article 500 of NFPA 70 (“NFPA 70 (NEC) Code Development,” n.d.), the National Electric
Code, classifies hazardous locations into different categories, including Classes I–III and
Division I and II. These classes and divisions classify how far electrical systems must be from
flammable gas stores. Articles 501 and 505 further explain classified locations and explain Zone
0, 1, and 2 locations, which address areas with flammable gases. IEC 60079-10-1 also addresses
the classification of hazardous areas, giving recommendations regarding the design, construction,
and maintenance of systems in areas susceptible to explosive gases. NFPA 70 Articles 625, 670,
and 692 address power system requirements for electric vehicles, industrial machinery, and fuel
cell systems, which could be relevant to hydrogen infrastructure and hydrogen-powered aircraft.

NFPA 855, the Standard for the Installation of Stationary Energy Storage, could be referenced
when designing energy storage systems on hydrogen aircraft, but the standard specifies it only
addresses stationary energy storage. Focused standards such as ASTM WK56255 and 14 CFR
27.1353 are helpful for energy storage design and implementation, but they still do not address
how these systems work alongside hydrogen fuel cells.

2.8.3 Hydrogen Aircraft
Moving on from infrastructure, four SAE standards apply to the inner workings of hydrogen-
powered aircraft. SAE AIR6464 discusses design safety, failure analysis, and risk assessment for
hydrogen fuel cell aircraft. SAE AS6858 covers the safe installation of PEM fuel cell power
systems in airplanes. SAE AS6679 and AS7373 address the installation, operation, and
maintenance of liquid and gaseous hydrogen tanks onboard aircraft. SAE standards tend to be
focused on certain processes or components. A combination of SAE standards could provide
safety during design and implementation, but they are not comprehensive. As hydrogen aircraft
continue to be researched and funded, an all-encompassing standard will be published.

2.8.4 Hydrogen Fueling
Fueling is an important aspect of hydrogen implementation at airports. Multiple codes and
standards address carbon-based airplane fueling, such as NFPA 407 and AC 150/5230-4C, but
recommendations for hydrogen fueling are more limited. The 2022 information report SAE
AIR8466 discusses recommendations for gaseous and liquid fueling at airports. It covers process
limits and stationary and portable fueling stations. Hydrogen aircraft are novel. There are no
comprehensive hydrogen aircraft fueling standards. For a general idea of what hydrogen aircraft
fueling standards will look like, stakeholders can consider current hydrogen vehicle fueling
standards. SAE J2601, J2601/3, and J2601/4, J2601/5 discuss fueling methods, parameters, and
safety recommendations for light- and heavy-duty hydrogen vehicles.

SAE J2719, SAE J2719-3, and ISO 14687 address hydrogen fuel quality, including
contamination limits and points of contamination recommendations. ISO 19880-1 discusses the
design, installation, commissioning, maintenance, and inspection of gaseous hydrogen fueling
stations. ANSI/CSA HGV 4.10 describes recommendations for testing and analyzing fittings
used in gaseous hydrogen fuel stations. SAE 2600 includes standards for the design and testing
of nozzles, connectors, and receptacles used to connect the hydrogen fuel to the vehicle’s tank.
SAE J2799 details standards for the communication technology between the dispenser and the
vehicle used at hydrogen fueling stations. ANSI/CSA HGV 4.3 provides the minimum

 26

requirements for test methods and safety at hydrogen fueling stations. Stakeholders should have
a general understanding of current hydrogen vehicle fueling standards, but these documents do
not fully address the complex process of aircraft fueling.

2.8.5 Hazardous Material Transport
If stakeholders decide against a pipeline for hydrogen transport, they must look toward portable
pressurized tanks. There are federal and private voluntary standards that address the transport of
hydrogen via pressurized tanks. DOT 49 CFR Parts 172 and 177 discuss the road transport of
hazardous materials, including recommendations for marking, shipping, labeling, safety, and
security. These standards form the technical basis for the transport of hydrogen by tube trailers
and liquid tankers. DOT 49 CFR Part 175 specifically covers the transport of hazardous
materials by aircraft. Stakeholders can use this standard when designing hydrogen tank systems
onboard hydrogen aircraft, but more targeted standards are required. If the airport owns portable
hydrogen tanks, they should refer to the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code. ASME BPVC
Section XII addresses the construction, inspection, testing, and certification of pressurized
transport tanks. These standards also apply to hydrogen transport within airports, from bulk
storage or tanker trucks to fuel stations.

2.8.6 Environmental Regulation
Another Code of Federal Regulations that applies to hydrogen implementation at airports is EPA
40 CFR Part 98 Subpart P, which addresses hydrogen emissions monitoring, thresholds, and
calculations. EPA 40 CFR Part 98 is a sufficient standard for hydrogen emissions reporting and
calculations, but a more specialized standard regarding the safety of hydrogen releases at an
airport would be helpful, especially with large jet fuel storage and passenger airplanes in the
immediate area. Overall, stakeholders can use the list of safety standards in Table 1 to inform the
design, installation, and operation of hydrogen storage and fueling systems at airports but must
take into consideration that comprehensive safety codes and standards do not yet exist.

The Jet-A combustion system operates on the principle of direct combustion of jet fuel to
produce thrust, which drives the propellers or fans. The hydrogen combustion process does not
produce any carbon dioxide but does produce nitrogen oxides, which are known air pollutants.

In contrast, the hydrogen fuel cell system harnesses the chemical energy of hydrogen by
converting it into electrical energy through an electrochemical reaction in the fuel cell. This
electricity is then used to power electric motors that drive the aircraft’s propellers or fans. The
only emissions for this process are pure water, making it more environmentally friendly than a
combustion system. Notably, a fuel cell system also has a different noise profile than that of a
Jet-A combustion system.

3.3.1 Quantifying Fueling Performance
To better understand fueling performance for aviation application, we leverage the Hydrogen
Filling Simulation (H2FillS) software, developed by NREL. This software is an advanced
thermodynamic model that accurately tracks the transient changes in hydrogen temperature,
pressure, and mass flow during the process of filling onboard hydrogen gaseous tanks for a fuel
cell electric vehicle. It enables the simulation of gas flow from the hydrogen station to the tank of
the fuel cell electric vehicle, and the models have been validated against a range of testing data,
ranging from a light-duty application (“Thermodynamic Modeling of Hydrogen Fueling Process
from High-Pressure Storage Tank to Vehicle Tank - ScienceDirect,” n.d.) to medium-/heavy-
duty application. This tool not only fills knowledge gaps in the interaction between hydrogen
stations and fuel cell electric vehicles but also provides substantial insights to the light-duty and
heavy-duty fueling market. It supports the safe design and operation of station’s components and
fosters the development of new generations of hydrogen stations to minimize capital and
operating expenses for hydrogen infrastructure.

With a user-friendly “drag-and-drop” graphical interface, H2FillS facilitates simulation of station
and vehicle systems with preset parameters for common hydrogen station components, while
offering the flexibility for users to specify custom parameters for unique station or vehicle
configurations. The model exists in two versions: a full-station model including the transport of
hydrogen from high-pressure bank storage through a dispenser to the vehicle tank, and a partial-
station model focused solely on the dispenser components and vehicle tank. Through both
models, H2FillS capably generates fill performance data, capturing pressure, temperature, and
mass flow rates dynamics throughout the fill event. In addition, it permits users to input custom-
designed fill profiles, thereby enabling a diverse range of simulations tailored to specific needs
or investigational purposes.

The modeling philosophy in H2FillS is transferable to the aviation industry for refueling the
aircraft. H2FillS has been developed to support gaseous hydrogen fueling. Figure 16 illustrates a
high-level representation of the refueling process for aircraft using hydrogen as a fuel source.
The tfills represents the time taken for refueling the hydrogen tanks, and tground denotes the total
time spent on the ground for maintenance, boarding, or other ground operations.

The computational flow diagram detailed in Figure 18 describes the structured steps for
simulating hydrogen fueling operations. The workflow begins with the selection of the

The simulation and postprocessing phases of the hydrogen fueling simulation involve setting the
specifications for the high-pressure tank outlet conditions, initializing pressure and temperature
parameters upstream and downstream of the control valve, and applying calculation methods to
different components of the system, such as the high-pressure bank storage outlet, pipes, and
vehicle tank. Upon reaching the predetermined termination conditions, the software generates a
detailed summary and visual plots to assess the simulation’s outcomes.

3.4 Hydrogen Infrastructure Safety Assessment
Hydrogen system safety relies on understanding effective safety planning to identify hazards and
evaluate risks by considering the likelihood and consequence of an incident associated with the
hazards (“Safety Planning | H2tools | Hydrogen Tools,” n.d.). To effectively evaluate risk, the
unique properties of hydrogen should be well understood to facilitate safe system design.
Because hydrogen is the lightest molecule in the universe, colorless, odorless, tasteless, and
flammable and explosive over a wide range of concentrations, subject matter experts, best
practices, and lessons from the past provide valuable information on likelihood and how to
mitigate hydrogen system hazards. Safety codes and standards organizations publish generalized

 42

safety requirements based on safety learnings from subject matter experts, companies, and
research institutions. Hydrogen codes and standards, such as the National Fire Protection
Association (NFPA) 2: Hydrogen Technologies Code and ASME B31.12: Hydrogen Piping and
Pipelines, are mature codes that have undergone multiple revision cycles and cover common
hydrogen systems (e.g., bulk hydrogen storage, piping systems, generation systems) in detail.
The emerging applications, such as hydrogen aircraft, may not be as thoroughly addressed by
current codes and standards. These subject matter areas may require a proper gap analysis and
more thorough risk assessment.

The implementation of hydrogen systems at airports has the potential to combine mature
technologies with novel technologies. Hydrogen storage, transport, production, transfer to
storage, dispensing (fueling) to cars and trucks, and sensor integration for hydrogen safety are
established commercial technologies. Hydrogen as fuel in aircraft and the aircraft fueling process
are more novel applications that are not as thoroughly addressed in current codes and standards.
Hydrogen systems can be safely deployed at airports, given thorough safety planning and hazard
evaluation and mitigation.

The following sections explain how hydrogen systems can be safely deployed at airports. These
sections include a general hazard analysis framework, a discussion of bulk hydrogen storage
setback distances, and a case study for the deployment of hydrogen storage and fueling
infrastructure at an airport.

3.4.1 General Hazard Analysis Framework
When designing new hydrogen systems, stakeholders must consider all potential hazards that
could affect the well-being of the system or the people and structures around the system.
Creating a comprehensive list of possible system hazards allows stakeholders to establish
safeguards and procedures for a multitude of failure scenarios. The most common hazard
identification tool is a hazard and operability (HAZOP) study, which is a risk assessment tool
that uses specific guidewords to analyze potential hazards in a system. HAZOPs help
stakeholders break large systems down into smaller nodes and are used when stakeholders are in
the design phase of a project. A hazard identification (HAZID) study is a less common tool that
is closely related to a HAZOP. HAZIDs use guidewords to identify system hazards without
focusing on specific components, such as valves or piping configurations. HAZOPs and HAZIDs
bring together industry experts, the safety team, and designers to qualitatively assess the hazards
associated with system design and to identify potential hazard mitigation strategies. Depending
on the hazard levels, risk tolerance of a facility, or complexity of a system, a quantitative risk
assessment may be needed to better understand the hazards and assess if appropriate safeguards
have been implemented. HAZOPs are often performed later in the design process, after site
selection, operating limits, and design drawing have been completed. HAZIDs, on the other
hand, are used early in the design process to inform stakeholders of potential hazards, helping
interested parties decide if an idea or design is viable. Therefore, this work is focused on
HAZIDs for selected systems that may be deployed at airports.

3.4.2 Bulk Hydrogen Storage Setback Distances
Minimum setback distances are an important component of safety for hydrogen storage systems
and are highlighted in hydrogen codes and standards. The bulk storage setback distances are the

 43

single largest code-based driver on system footprint impacting where the hydrogen system can
be deployed in a facility. Stakeholders should familiarize themselves with how these distances
were created, the assumptions made, and how they should be applied to the environment around
liquid and gaseous hydrogen storage.

Bulk fuel storage poses a risk to the people and structures around it, no matter the fuel
composition. To mitigate this risk, research-backed minimum setback distances are agreed upon
by codes and standards organizations. NFPA 2 includes the bulk hydrogen setback distances.
NFPA 2’s setback distances are based on research by Sandia National Laboratories (Sandia) and
finalized by NFPA 2 Technical Committees. Sandia has used risk assessment methods to justify
and update hydrogen setback distances in NFPA 2. Sandia’s 2009 study “Analyses to Support
Development of Risk-Informed Separation Distances for Hydrogen Codes and Standards”
(LaChance et al. 2009) was the basis for NFPA 2’s gaseous hydrogen setback distances.

Since 2009, three risk criteria thresholds were reevaluated by the NFPA committee, and the
gaseous separation distances were adjusted accordingly. Sandia’s 2023 paper “Technical
Justifications for Liquid Hydrogen Exposure Distances” (“Technical Justifications For Liquid
Hydrogen Exposure Distances - SAND2023 12548 | PDF | Risk | Risk Assessment,” n.d.)
provided justification for liquid hydrogen setback distances. The 2023 paper used the same
analytical concepts as the 2009 paper but used a more advanced and comprehensive risk
assessment method along with the Hydrogen Plus Other Alternative Fuels Risk Assessment
Model (HyRAM+) tool (“HyRAM+,” n.d.).

3.4.2.1 Bulk Gaseous Storage
LaChance et al. (LaChance et al. 2009) presented NFPA 2 Technical Group 6 with multiple
approaches to determining minimum gaseous hydrogen setback distances. The technical group
selected the risk-informed approach as the optimal way to develop setback distances for
quantitative risk assessments. The first step in determining gaseous hydrogen setback distances
was to collect leak frequency data for components in hydrogen systems. Leak rate data for
hydrogen components, such as compressors, valves, hoses, and pipes, were limited at the time of
publication. A Bayesian statistical method was used to combine general leak frequency data with
the small amount of hydrogen leak frequency data, resulting in a comprehensive and relevant
leak frequency estimate. The general leak frequency data consisted of leakage rates from systems
in nuclear power plants, automotive refueling stations, and compressed natural gas facilities.

Researchers from SNL established five leak size categories based on the percentage of pipe flow
area (0.01%, 0.1%, 1%, 10%, and 100%) and used the Bayesian data to determine the cumulative
probability that a leak will occur at a certain leak size. The team used 3,000- and 15,000-pounds-
per-square-inch gauge (psig) systems to inform NFPA 2 of gaseous separation distances. Sandia
then established risk guidelines based on a few core principles: risk during hydrogen refueling
should be less than or equal to risk during gasoline or compressed natural gas refueling, and
hydrogen accidents should not pose a significantly higher risk than any other type of accident.
These principles were combined with risk criteria from hazardous gas facilities in countries such
as Hungary, the Czech Republic, Australia, the United Kingdom, the United States, and the
Netherlands. The Sandia team only considered component failures that led to hydrogen jet leaks
in their quantitative risk assessment, leaving out overpressure and human error scenarios. Using
the 3,000- and 15,000-psig models, the group focused on the type of accident (jet fire or flash

 44

fire) and the location of the accident (tube trailer, stanchion, pressure control module, instrument
module). NFPA 2 Technical Group 6 set a fatality risk guideline of 2 × 10-5/yr for hydrogen
accidents in the 3,000- and 15,000-psig models. This rate was chosen based on the risk of fatality
that one experiences when fueling at a gas station in the United States. Technical Group 6 chose
a leak size of 3% of the largest flow area downstream of the storage system as the basis of NFPA
2 separation distances.

3.4.2.2 Bulk Liquid Storage
“Technical Justifications for Liquid Hydrogen Exposure Distances” (“Technical Justifications
For Liquid Hydrogen Exposure Distances - SAND2023 12548 | PDF | Risk | Risk Assessment,”
n.d.) expands upon LaChance et al. by including HyRAM+ physical models to determine liquid
hydrogen setback distances. HyRAM+ is a toolkit that uses public data and models to analyze the
safety of liquid and gaseous hydrogen transport, fueling, and storage. HyRAM+ allows users to
perform risk assessment calculations and create heat flux, overpressure, and hydrogen release
behavior models (“HyRAM+,” n.d.). Again, the team used five leak size categories based on
percentage of pipe flow area (0.01%, 0.1%, 1%, 10%, and 100%) and developed leak frequencies
using gaseous hydrogen and liquid natural gas data. They used HyRAM+ to perform a
quantitative risk assessment, which included pipe and system sizes, component leak frequencies,
and probability of ignition. The output of this assessment was a value for the likelihood of
fatality based on location and pipe size. Separation distances were initially found by finding the
location at which the risk criteria from the 2009 paper, 2 × 10-5 fatalities/yr, applied.

Risk-based distance calculations are sensitive to system specifics; thus three physical
consequence models were used alongside the risk assessment: (1) overpressure, (2) heat flux, and
(3) hydrogen concentration. These models were used to calculate equivalent hole sizes. The team
then performed a sensitivity study by changing parameters such as temperature, pressure, and
detection timings to reduce uncertainty in risk calculations. The consequence models were split
into failure groups of varying intensity. The most conservative values were used for Group 1,
followed by 2 then 3, as Group 1 exposures carry the largest risk of fatality during a hydrogen
leak event. Group 1’s concentration by volume of 8%, peak overpressure of 7 kPa (1 psi), and
heat flux of 4.732 kW/m2 represent parameters that would do little damage to structures and pose
a low risk to people. Group 2’s values of 13.7 kPa (2 psi) and 9 kW/m2 represent conditions that
may pose a risk of injury but not fatality. Group 3’s values of 20.7 kPa (3 psi) and 20 kW/m2
represent parameters that would pose a risk of fatality or structural damage. Based on the
sensitivity study, the group went forward with a fractional hole size of 5%, which encompassed
most leak scenarios. To calculate liquid hydrogen setback distances, the Sandia team applied the
5% leak size, the physical models, and the quantitative risk assessment to three different
situations. The largest setback distance was chosen as the group setback distance, giving users a
look at how different physical phenomena affect risk in different scenarios. The Sandia team also
considered risk mitigation factors, such as walls and automatic shutoff valves. These strategies
were shown to substantially lower risk, therefore lowering the minimum acceptable setback
distance. In the future, SNL hopes to apply HyRAM+ and the consequence model process to
gaseous hydrogen setback distances.

The bulk storage hydrogen setback distances have the greatest impact on facility size and

The technoeconomic modeling for this study generates a levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH).
LCOH refers to the constant price at which hydrogen must be sold over the lifetime of a project
to break even on the investment. It is measured in dollars per kilogram hydrogen ($ per kg) and
typically is used to compare costs between different hydrogen production and delivery pathways.
The LCOH of each supply chain component (production, transportation, storage, and dispensing)
can be added together to calculate the total LCOH dispensed at a refueling station, and in this
case, an airport refueling station. It is worth noting that the LCOH dispensed at the airport does
not necessarily translate into a retail price at the pump, as the hydrogen retailer can decide to
include additional markup to ensure a profit is made on the hydrogen sold. Further, the LCOH
results presented in section 4.4 do not consider any incentives for hydrogen infrastructure
development that may become available through the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law or Inflation
Reduction Act in the United States.

For the GHG emission analysis, we again break down results by supply chain component and list
emissions in terms of kilogram CO2e per kilogram hydrogen dispensed. We use an emission
accounting scheme that includes scope 1 emissions (direct emissions from hydrogen supply
chain components) and scope 2 emissions (combustion-related emissions from fossil-based
electricity-generating sources).

3.6 Jobs and Economic Impacts Analysis
The required infrastructure for hydrogen aircraft operations across airports in California, Oregon
and Washington is expected to support local jobs in multiple sectors as well as to stimulate the
local economy of these states during construction and operation phases. In this study, we
estimate these potential economic impacts using an input-output framework, one of the most
commonly used and straightforward methods for estimating economy-wide impacts because of a
change in regional demand (e.g., a new construction project) (Lahr, Michael, Erin Coughlin, and
Frank Felder 2010). Input-output models represent the production structure of a region as a
network of sectors buying and selling to one another, to local households, government, and to

 52

external markets. Their results reflect the supply chain’s responses and the total macro-level
impacts from changes in demand for goods or services in the area.

3.6.1 Modeling Approach
To model costs, construction and operation and maintenance (O&M) costs for each project are
estimated from HDSAM and then allocated by sectors. From the total amount of expenditures in
a region, a portion of the goods and services are provided by local business. The remaining
portion is assumed to be provided by out-of-state companies (nonlocal purchases) that do not
generate local benefits (Figure 26). This breakdown is determined by state-specific regional
purchase coefficients (RPCs) that reflect the percentage of local purchases for each good/service
in the model. RPCs vary by year and state, as a result of the evolving regional economic
structure and particular industrial mix in the region. In this study, we split total expenditures
among those supplied by the state, those supplied by other regions of the United States, and
imports. Local purchases were the basis for a demand shock in the model and to determine the
total economic impact, including jobs created by these investments on a state level.

Input-output models capture “multiplier effects” in the economy that arise from sectorial
interaction across different supply chains. A change in demand acts as a drop of water in a still
pond creating waves (“ripples”) that are large in the beginning and fade over time. The first wave
is the largest: it represents the change in the purchasing pattern of directly affected firms; to meet
the demand for goods and services of this first wave, suppliers of these firms need to change
their purchasing patterns, generating a secondary smaller ripple; then suppliers of these suppliers
also need to change their purchasing patterns, and so on, generating a sequence of fading ripples
in the economy. The sum of all ripple effects is the total economic impact of that initial change
in demand.

 Figure 26. Economic impact analysis overview
Hence, impacts are classified as direct (immediate economic impact from the change in demand),
indirect (from supply chain linkages), and induced (resulting from the spending of wages/salaries

 53

by workers) by region. Our results include total temporary and permanent jobs supported, changes
in gross regional product (GRP, value added), and sectoral output:
 • Jobs are defined as the sum of full-time and part-time workers (measured in full-time
 equivalent [FTE] jobs) employed at the place of business. All jobs supported by local
 companies are accounted for, including those of out-of-state commuters (who might
 spend part of their wages outside the state).
 • Output represents the value of production and includes all sales and purchases of a
 particular sector. It reflects the level of economic activity in the region.
 • Value added represents the wealth generated by an economic activity and includes
 compensation of employees (wages and benefits), profit-type income, property income,
 and taxes on production.
 • GRP measures the monetary value of final goods and services produced in a region and is
 the sum of all sectors’ value added.

3.6.2 Data Preparation
For this analysis, we use economic data from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s
(USEPA) StateIO project, (“GitHub - USEPA/Stateior: Prepares US State Two-Region Make and
Use Tables,” n.d.) which provides input-output tables for each U.S. state at BEA Summary Level
(71 sectors disaggregation) for 2012 to 2021 (Li et al. 2022). From this dataset, we employ the
2019 multiregional input-output tables for California, Oregon, and Washington, which have two
interconnected regions: the state and the rest of the U.S. Data for 2020–2021 are not considered
because of the transient effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on the economic structure of these

As mentioned, construction and operation costs for each project are obtained from HDSAM, and

4.1.1 Jet-A Combustion Engines
Figure 29 and Figure 30 provide a comparative analysis of the jet combustion engines
performance data for sample ATR 72 and Dash 8 flights with key parameters such as altitude,
true airspeed, aircraft mass, thrust, fuel consumption, and total efficiency. The time series data
illustrates the aircraft’s performance during the different flight phases: climb, cruise, and
descent.

Both flights reach their cruising altitudes within 400 to 600 seconds—around 13,000 feet for the
ATR 72 and 11,000 feet for the Dash 8. Each aircraft then maintains a stable cruise phase before
descending. The true airspeed stabilizes at approximately 290 knots for the ATR 72 and around

Because of fuel consumption, the aircraft mass decreases steadily throughout both flights, with
the ATR 72 dropping from about 20,400 kg to 19,800 kg, and the Dash 8 reducing from 25,500

approximately 2,600 seconds.

Thrust analysis shows an initial high thrust during takeoff, peaking at 21,000 newtons (N) for the
ATR 72 and 32,000 N for the Dash 8. During cruise, thrust stabilizes at around 14,000 N for the
ATR 72 and 18,000 N for the Dash 8. During descent, both aircraft exhibit thrust fluctuations
because of adjustments in speed and power required for a controlled landing.

Fuel consumption follows these thrust patterns, with the highest rates during takeoff and climb,
stabilizing during cruise, and decreasing during descent. Fuel consumption fluctuates during
descent as the aircraft adjusts altitude and airspeed.

Total efficiency peaks during the cruise phase, reaching around 34% for the ATR 72 and 31%
for the Dash 8. Both aircraft experience a gradual drop in efficiency during descent, with the
Dash 8 showing a sharper decline. This could be attributed to increased drag from additional
aerodynamic resistance, such as extended flaps and landing gear deployment, which demand
more power to maintain stability and control during descent. The trend highlights the challenge
of maintaining high efficiency during descent, where aerodynamic and power adjustments are

needed for landing. Therefore, optimizing fuel strategies during the less efficient takeoff and
descent phases remains crucial for enhancing overall aircraft performance.

maintain stable thrust during critical phases, such as climb and descent, emphasizes its reliability
in ensuring safe operations. The observed variability in the fuel cell system’s thrust during climb
and descent suggests that, although it offers efficiency benefits, further refinement in power
management and control may be necessary to achieve consistent performance across all phases

4.2 Hydrogen Fueling Analysis
The simulation results in Table 7 highlight two critical factors affecting hydrogen refueling
operations: ambient temperature and refueling scheduling. First, ambient temperature plays a
significant role in the efficiency of the refueling process. At a standard temperature of 20°C
(Cases 1, 2, and 3), the refueling times are relatively consistent, averaging around 3,075 seconds,
with the mass of hydrogen dispensed ranging from 21.1 kg to 23.3 kg. However, in the
precooling condition of –40°C in Case 4, the refueling time nearly doubles to 5,872 seconds (an
increase of 91%), while the amount of hydrogen dispensed rises by approximately 67% (reaching
39.1 kg). At lower temperatures, hydrogen becomes denser, which allows more mass to be
refueled into the tank. However, the refueling process also slows down because of the need for
careful pressure and temperature management (such as minimizing boil-off or excessive pressure
increases) to maintain system stability.

Second, the timing and scheduling of refueling events are crucial for maintaining operational
efficiency. The data in Cases 1, 2, and 3 reveal that the interval between refueling events has a
substantial impact on the amount of hydrogen dispensed, with longer intervals allowing for
greater hydrogen delivery. For instance, in Case 3, in which there is a longer gap between

 65

refueling events, 23.3 kg of hydrogen was dispensed—10% more than Case 1 (22.5 kg) and 8%
more than Case 2 (21.1 kg). Technically, the longer rest period in Case 3 improves cooling and
pressure equalization within the hydrogen storage tank. This suggests allowing sufficient time
between refueling operations enables the system to stabilize, leading to more efficient hydrogen
transfer.

Figure 35 depicts the process of cascade fueling for a hydrogen-powered aircraft, showing both
temperature and pressure changes over a 1,450-second filling time. The solid red line represents
the temperature of the aircraft tank, which increases during the fueling process. The dashed blue
line and solid blue line indicate the pressure in the aircraft and bank storage tanks, respectively.
The background shading indicates the sequential use of five different pressure storage banks
used in the cascade fueling process.

 Figure 35. Cascade filling of the aircraft tank at the ambient temperature of 30oC

 66

The cascade filling process in Figure 35 involves a series of steps that progressively use gas from
multiple banks’ storage to fill the aircraft tank. The sequence of events is as follows:
 1. Initial filling from Bank 1: The process begins with compressed hydrogen being
 released from the first bank or the bank has the highest pressure (50 MPa in this case).
 The transfer of hydrogen to the aircraft tank starts rapidly because of the high differential
 pressure between the bank and the destination tank. During this phase, the aircraft tank
 pressure increases swiftly to 19 MPa, as indicated by the initial steep slope of the blue
 dashed line. Moreover, as the refueling progresses, the temperature within the aircraft
 tank begins to rise from the ambient temperature of 30oC to 61oC because of the
 compression of the gas. This temperature rise must be carefully managed to prevent
 safety issues and to maintain the integrity of the aircraft tank.
 2. Sequential bank switching: After a certain amount of hydrogen has been transferred
 from Bank 1 and the pressure differential decreases, the system switches to the next bank
 (Bank 2) to maintain the pressure differential, which occurs at approximately 690
 seconds. Subsequent switching to Banks 3, 4, and 5 occurs sequentially at 1,060 seconds,
 1,280 seconds, and 1,400 seconds, respectively. It is important to note that each bank
 starts with the same pressure of 50 MPa, helping to manage the filling rate as the pressure
 in the aircraft tank increases. As the refueling progresses, the pressure differential (dP) is
 maintained at about 10 MPa, which is essential to ensure the vehicle tank can be filled to
 the desired pressure without exceeding safety limits. The last bank used in the cascade
 filling (Bank 5) provides the final amount of hydrogen needed to reach the target pressure
 of 35 MPa in the aircraft tank.
 3. End of fueling: The process concludes when the vehicle tank reaches its maximum safe
 pressure of 35 MPa. At this point, the temperature in the aircraft tank has also reached
 76oC; however, it is still lower than the terminating temperature of 85oC set before the
 filling.
In practice, effective cascade fueling requires precise control systems and sensors to monitor and
adjust the flow of hydrogen, temperature, and pressure in real time, ensuring both safety and
efficiency in the refueling of hydrogen-powered aircraft. The sequential activation of banks
suggests an optimization process to ensure the right balance between filling speed (mass flow
rate) and safety (pressure and temperature limits) is maintained.

4.3.2 HAZID Analysis
Building upon methodologies outlined in section 3.4.1, HAZID concepts were used to outline
potential safety events, consequences of those events, and potential safeguards for hydrogen

 72

infrastructure at airports. Framing the safety assessment as a HAZID ensures that all likely
failure scenarios are addressed. This safety assessment goes one step further by showing the
relationship between hazards and their causes, as multiple deviations can lead to the same
hazard. A HAZID was performed on five nodes integral to hydrogen implementation at airports:
bulk gaseous storage, bulk liquid storage, aircraft fueling, pipelines, and hydrogen generation.

Figure 40 through Figure 44 are hazard identification flowcharts that show the relationship
between hydrogen systems’ overarching hazards and their causes. These figures also include a
list of safeguards for each “cause” that acts to reduce the likelihood of the hazard occurring. The
primary hazard for hydrogen systems is a fire or explosion. A hydrogen fire or explosion needs
three things to occur: (1) a hydrogen leakage event (fuel), (2) an ignition source, and (3) an
oxidizing agent. Oxygen in the air acts as an oxidizing agent and is difficult to eliminate;
therefore, the primary focus is on reducing the likelihood of leakage and ignition events. Fire and
explosion hazards must be accounted for in all five systems that would be used at an airport:
liquid and gaseous hydrogen storage, pipelines, generation systems, and fueling stations. Two of
these systems, electrolyzers and liquid hydrogen storage, involve additional high-level hazards.
Liquid hydrogen storage poses a risk to people and structures because of its cryogenic
temperature. Any structure or person near leaking liquid hydrogen is at risk for cryogenic harm.
Electrolyzers pose a significant electrical hazard to operators and the public. Anyone who comes
into physical contact with an electrolyzer is at risk of an electrical shock or arc flash. If properly
mitigated using the listed safeguards, industry best practices, and safety codes and standards, the
hydrogen can and has been used safely in a variety of industrial and commercial applications for
many years.

Excessive overpressure events and material failure lead to hydrogen leakage events. Common
causes of overpressure in a gaseous hydrogen storage system are blockage, equipment failure,
and human error. In the case of PEM electrolyzers, membrane degradation can lead to pipe
blockages, overpressure, or hydrogen leakage. For liquid hydrogen storage systems, heat can
cause an overpressure event. Failures in insulation systems can cause liquid hydrogen to vaporize
and expand, putting extreme stress on the system. Safeguards against overpressure events include
pressure sensors, pressure safety valves, automatic shutoff systems, and redundant design safety
practices that plan for valve and pipe failures.

The most common causes of material failure include corrosion and hydrogen embrittlement. For
pipelines, accidental excavation damage is also a common cause of material failure. Natural
force damage is also highlighted in the pipeline flowchart as a leading factor in hydrogen leakage
because of a pipeline’s susceptibility to soil erosion, earthquakes, flooding, and high wind speed.
All hydrogen systems would be affected by these events, and stakeholders should factor all
extreme weather events into their designs. To protect against material failure and external
weather damage, stakeholders should use properly rated materials to construct their hydrogen
systems. They should also use coatings or materials that protect against corrosion and hydrogen
embrittlement. The soil should be reinforced around hydrogen installations to protect against
wind and erosion. Regular testing and proper operating procedures and training can help protect
against faulty components and human error.

As mentioned previously, for a fire or explosion to occur, there must be fuel, an oxidizing agent,
and an ignition source. If there is a mixture of between 4% and 75% hydrogen in air, then a stray
spark or flame could cause an ignition event. Ignition sources for hydrogen systems can come
from a multitude of places. Lightning or electrostatic discharge could create enough energy to
ignite hydrogen. A spark from an unclassified or malfunctioning sensor could create a fire or
explosion. Finally, human error could create an explosive hydrogen event, whether that be from
a cigarette, a lighter, or from the mishandling of electrical equipment. To help prevent an ignition
event, stakeholders should properly ground their metal components, use appropriate lightning
protection, and abide by NFPA 70’s, National Electrical Safety, electrical classification of areas.
To mitigate instances of human error, proper testing, operating procedures, and safe work
practices should be used.

Some safeguards are operational choices that are not mandatory, but many of the safeguards
listed in the previous figures are required by the current codes and standards. Most of the
safeguards listed in the table are component specific and work to prevent a specific design
failure. Hydrogen setback distances are a broad safeguard that aim to protect structures, vehicles,
and living things in the event of a hydrogen release or ignition.

For general hydrogen system safety, the two easiest mitigation methods are through leakage
prevention and removal of ignition sources. Removal of ignition sources is called out in NFPA 2
setback distances and Class I, Division II, Group B electrical classification zones. Leakage
mitigation goes into the specific design choices, assembly, and material selection and is arguably
harder to control. Safeguards such as leak detection methods are valuable tools in detecting
leakage and mitigating the consequence. Safety codes and standards provide the general
structure, but the risk assessment, HAZID, and HAZOP provide the details.

4.3.3 Airport Operations

4.3.3.1 Hydrogen Supply Methods
The first segment of hydrogen service at airports is transporting the fuel on-site. This can be
accomplished by hydrogen truck delivery, pipeline, or on-site generation. Each of these options
presents different benefits and challenges, with significant design variations. Site-specific
analysis would be required to select the appropriate hydrogen delivery method for each airport
based on the available space, power, funding, timeline, and nearby hydrogen generation
facilities. The least intrusive and delivery option with the shortest timeline is to use hydrogen
tube trailers (truck delivery) to replenish hydrogen reserves. The tube trailer would fill at a local
or regional hydrogen fueling station and deliver hydrogen according to demand.

This method relies on the normal market price and hydrogen availability from a generation or
distribution facility. To combat variability in hydrogen pricing, airports may enter a fuel
purchase agreement with regional hydrogen suppliers. The sizing and configuration of hydrogen
storage at an airport are based on the airplane fueling demand, frequency of hydrogen delivery,
and mass of on-site hydrogen storage. Initial hydrogen deployments at airports could use tube
trailers in place of permanent bulk storage. Once empty, the tube trailer can be swapped,
minimizing the permanent infrastructure, costs, and construction time at the airport. As hydrogen
demand increases, permanent ground storage may be required.

A more intrusive option includes installing a hydrogen pipeline that feeds directly into the
airport’s bulk hydrogen storage. The hydrogen that supplies the pipeline could be generated
either off-site by a third party or on-site by the airport. On-site generation would require the
airport to fund and construct an electrolyzer system that can meet the demand of the airport. The
airport must also meet the electrolyzer’s power demand, which ideally would be generated using
renewable energy. The airport must decide whether to build its own renewable energy sources or
purchase from a local source. Constructing a renewable energy grid would present a significant
upfront cost, but ownership and control of energy and fuel should be evaluated on a life cycle
basis because it is potentially more cost-effective. Privately owned power generation systems are
unaffected by fluctuations in energy prices and widespread power failures.

Using off-site generation and transportation via a pipeline is another viable option that avoids the
challenges of on-site generation. Pipelines are ultimately unintrusive to regular airport operations
because they are buried underground. Hydrogen can be transported quickly through pipelines,
minimizing the amount of on-site storage needed to support the airport. This pipeline could be
supplied by a local or regional hydrogen distribution center directly, potentially leading to
reductions in cost over tube trailer deliveries. Pipelines are generally considered a longer-term
option for hydrogen delivery and could provide enough hydrogen to support a high demand from
a large, centralized distribution or production center.

Tube Trailer Deliveries
Tube trailers are trucks that haul steel hydrogen tanks filled with hydrogen. Tube trailers operate
at a relatively low pressure and are limited to a maximum pressure of 250 bar by the U.S.

 80

Department of Transportation (“Hydrogen Tube Trailers,” n.d.). They have the capacity to
transport up to 900 kg of hydrogen, meaning that multiple shipments must be conducted to refill
the bulk hydrogen storage system. As with any hydrogen system, gaseous tube trailers present a
fire and explosion hazard. Tube trailers can be exposed to a multitude of unforeseen hazards on
public roads, such as collisions and ignition sources. As part of siting, tube trailer movements are
recommended to maintain the gaseous hydrogen setback distance or electrically classified zone
around the storage system. Based on these setbacks, the tube trailer would dispense hydrogen at
a fueling receptacle at least 25 feet away from the bulk storage. The fueling receptacle should be
inspected and maintained regularly to prevent hydrogen leakage.

Liquid hydrogen for aircraft service is produced by first generating gaseous hydrogen, which
then undergoes the process of cryogenic liquefaction, where the gas is compressed, cooled, and
expanded, cooling it further. Liquid hydrogen is loaded onto vacuum-insulated trailers that make
periodic deliveries to the airport. Cryogenic tube trailers typically transport 12,000 to 17,000
gallons of liquid hydrogen at a time and are built to comply with the U.S. Department of
Transportation’s Hazardous Material Safety Code (“Safetygram #9 Liquid Hydrogen,” n.d.). 49
CFR 173.318 gives recommendations for liquid hydrogen in cargo tanks regarding pressure
relief valve settings, filling density, jacket integrity, and safety systems. The main goal of these
trailers is to keep heat from entering the tank, which is achieved through passive thermal
insulation. Passive insulation includes designing containment systems using materials with low
thermal conductivity, whereas active thermal conductivity includes the mechanical
recondensation of evaporated hydrogen (Yatsenko et al. 2022). A thermal leak can cause liquid
hydrogen to rapidly expand, leading to an overpressure event. Cryogenic tube trailers inevitably
encounter solar radiation as they transport cargo. Although the vacuum jacket surrounding the
hydrogen is meant to eliminate heat transfer between the outer shell of the tank and the cargo,
small amounts of heat still make their way through the insulation. To combat this, cryogenic
tanks are outfitted with pressure relief valves that allow for gaseous boil-off.

On-Site Generation
There are multiple ways that hydrogen storage systems can be filled. One alternative to tube
trailer deliveries is on-site gaseous hydrogen generation via a PEM electrolyzer. On-site
generation allows airports better control over the hydrogen that fuels their airplanes. Although
building a PEM electrolyzer comes with a large capital expenditure for the electrolyzer system
and grid upgrades if needed, airports would avoid the increased price of hydrogen that comes
with external vendors. On-site generation also circumvents supply chain issues and the
possibility of late deliveries by directly supplying the storage system with hydrogen as it is
generated. After construction, the only continuous costs are water, power, and maintenance.
There are multiple electrolyzer manufacturers that have designed compact packaged systems at
the megawatt scale that produce dry hydrogen with only standard utility water and power
connections.

4.3.3.2 Aircraft Fueling
Although hydrogen aircraft fueling is a novel concept, fueling methods can be modeled after
current land-based hydrogen vehicle fueling procedures, such as the recommendations in NFPA
407. Today, commercial airports commonly use pipeline fuel delivery with a booster connection
or mobile refueler via a truck. Mobile hydrogen refuelers have been developed and demonstrated

 81

for a variety of applications, primarily cars and trucks, and appear to be a viable solution for
airplane fueling at airports. Alternatively, small airports could use a stationary fueling station,
such as a gas station—the airplane would need to pull up to the stationary hydrogen fueling
station. A mobile hydrogen aircraft fueling station would contain hydrogen storage tanks and
fueling apparatus. These mobile refuelers would fuel the airplane as it prepares for its next flight.
These mobile refuelers would be supplied by the primary bulk hydrogen storage discussed
earlier.

To fuel hydrogen aircraft, mobile refuelers park near a plane’s receptacle, and an operator
connects a hose from the dispenser to the aircraft. Before fueling, the aircraft’s fueling receptacle
should be inspected for deformities. Fueling presents a risk for a hydrogen leakage event, which
could create a hazard. All metal parts in the vicinity should be grounded, and nearby electrical
devices should be appropriately rated for the area, as defined by NFPA 2. Stakeholders should
reference SAE J2601-2, SAE J2601-4, SAE J2601-5, ANSI/CSA HGV 4.10, SAE AIR8466,
SAE J2799, and SAE 2600 for safety recommendations regarding heavy-duty gaseous hydrogen
fueling, fueling communications technology, fueling valves and fittings, and hydrogen fueling
stations for airports.

While passengers are boarding or on board, aircraft may be fueled with kerosene-based fuels
(e.g., Jet A, Jet A1, etc.) but not fuels containing gasoline (e.g., Jet B, JP4, etc.) (“Refuelling
with Passengers on Board | SKYbrary Aviation Safety,” n.d.). This is because gasoline mixtures
have a higher flammability than kerosene-based fuels. Hydrogen is more flammable than Jet B
fuel but also has unique characteristics. Stakeholders should conduct further research into this
subject and assess previous assumptions to determine if aircraft fueling can safely proceed while
passengers are on board.

After a mobile cryogenic refueler fills at the bulk storage receptacle, it travels through the airport
to fill a hydrogen aircraft. SAE AIR8466 is a standard for liquid and gaseous hydrogen aircraft
fueling that was developed as a starting point for aircraft hydrogen fueling protocol. Stakeholders
can refer to this report for recommendations regarding hydrogen fueling station specifications,
fueling process limits, and safety requirements specific to hydrogen aircraft (“AIR8466:
Hydrogen Fueling Stations for Airports, in Both Gaseous and Liquid Form - SAE International,”
n.d.). A cryogenic refueler would likely be classified as a bulk liquid hydrogen storage system.

Mobile Refuelers
A mobile hydrogen refueling station could be used to transport hydrogen from bulk storage to
aircraft all around the airport. A mobile hydrogen refueling station is a vehicle that contains
hydrogen tanks, a compressor, and a dispenser. The Gas Technology Institute designed a model
mobile hydrogen refueling station that features these components and a fuel cell that provides
power to the Ford F-650 chassis that the refueler is built on (Newsom, n.d.). This specific vehicle
was meant to act as a backup source of hydrogen for stationary fueling stations and could
accommodate small hydrogen aircraft with approximate fuel capacities of 70 kg.

The mobile refueler is exposed to hazards similar to a tube trailer. Collisions, exposure to
unintended ignition sources, human error, and vibrations from road travel can cause a hydrogen
leakage event. Airports have many workers and service vehicles that use service roads at all
hours of the day. Mobile refuelers, like all vehicles at the airport, should have a route that

 82

minimizes the hazards to the vehicle and operator. Mobile refueler operators should be trained
and aware of safety protocols in the event of a hydrogen leak or collision. Stakeholders should
perform regular inspections of valves, fittings, and safety systems onboard the mobile refueler.

A mobile cryogenic refueler would be used to transport liquid hydrogen to aircraft. This mobile
refueler will contain a vacuum-insulated hydrogen tank, a compressor or pump, and a dispenser.
Because liquid hydrogen would be used to fuel large aircraft with capacities at and above 850 kg,
the mobile refueler should be able to carry at least 850 kg of LH2. The refueler trailer and tank
could be scaled up to accommodate multiple aircraft fills. Mobile liquid hydrogen refuelers
present unique challenges and hazards compared to gaseous refuelers. To manage boil-off, the
mobile refueler would need a pressure safety vent or advanced design. Fueling vehicles with
liquid hydrogen is currently an area of research, with multiple companies developing and
implementing designs that could safely fuel an aircraft.

Liquid hydrogen’s cryogenic nature presents a hazard to nearby structures and operators. With
just 3.5 kg of liquid hydrogen, a 0.5-inch-wide leak can produce a cold cloud with a harm radius
of around 5 feet, whereas a catastrophic rupture can produce a cryogenic harm radius of almost
70 feet (Zhiyong, n.d.). Direct human contact with liquid hydrogen has the potential to cause
severe frostbite and cell death, so proper personal protective equipment and handling procedures
must be used. Structures or devices located near leaked liquid hydrogen that are not rated for
cryogenic exposure would freeze, causing embrittlement and structural damage. All materials
near the cryogenic tank should be able to withstand a substantial liquid hydrogen leak.

4.3.4.3 Difference Between Gaseous and Liquid Bulk Hydrogen Storage Footprint
Liquid hydrogen has a higher density, which allows for a greater mass of hydrogen to be stored
in a smaller footprint. Because of the density and some of the unique properties of liquid
hydrogen, the setback distances to the different exposure groups are larger. The safety footprint

 86

density of gaseous and liquid hydrogen becomes comparable when the setback distances are
included in the land use footprint. There are different pros and cons associated with gaseous and
liquid hydrogen utilization. One benefit of liquid hydrogen is that fewer tanks leave a greater
amount of land space that could be used by auxiliary or support systems. One benefit of gaseous
hydrogen is that boil-off because of cryogenic warming is avoided. Another benefit of liquid
hydrogen is a much simpler piping system, with fewer potential leak locations resulting from the
use of 1 tank instead of 105 gaseous tanks.

framework. The input-output model employed for this analysis is a static model (representing the

impacts or changes over time. As such, the results do not account for changes in the economic
structure (including electricity grid) over time, that is, there are no economies of scale or
technology changes in any industry. Moreover, the estimates are based on several assumptions
about material sourcing. More accurate results can be obtained once suppliers and labor force
hiring are determined for each project.

5 Discussion and Conclusion
Transitioning the aviation industry from conventional fuel to hydrogen represents a multidecade,
multi-trillion-dollar endeavor. An emerging trend in lower emissions aircraft is pushing the
industry to its next frontier. The FAA plays a critical role in certifying airworthiness and
promoting safety in the skies and on the ground. As new models of novel aircraft come online,
the FAA needs to provide safety guidance for the planning, design, and establishment of on-the-
ground infrastructure at public facilities.

With the collaboration of NREL, section 2 of this report provides a holistic perspective on
hydrogen technology that may be relevant to the airport environment. To identify technological
gaps for aviation applications, section 2 describes hydrogen systems from production to end-use
application (e.g., hydrogen refueling) from a supply chain perspective. Furthermore, hydrogen-
based propulsion technologies are discussed along with potential opportunities using hydrogen
systems in the airport environment. Section 2 also attempts to summarize current hydrogen
safety codes and standards for airports.

With a wide range of technology readiness level for storage technologies, hydrogen can be stored
in many forms at scale. For large-scale facilities, bulk hydrogen storage methods include surface
storage (vessels and pipeline networks) and underground storage (caverns, salt deposits, depleted
reservoirs). It is essential to consider geospatial trade-offs (supply, demand, and transportation
methods) for at-scale applications. Bulk hydrogen storage is vital to the hydrogen economy
because it provides a means for the efficient and secure storage of hydrogen. Despite the
challenges in technology, efficiency, and cost, significant progress is anticipated through
ongoing research and development to expand the application from the current auto industry to
aviation, marine, and heavy industries, and as a means of storing renewable energy.

Most hydrogen used in the United States is produced at or close to where it is used—typically at
large industrial sites. This aspect is due to several factors, including the economies of scale for
the hydrogen distribution network, including tube trailers, tanker trucks, and gaseous pipelines.
An alternative pipeline transportation/delivery approach involves using the present natural gas
pipeline infrastructure. Past research estimates the maximum of 20% of hydrogen may be
allowable blending before significant costs will have to be incurred to upgrade the pipelines
because of potential degradation of pipeline materials.

To support novel hydrogen aircraft, this report provides background and lessons learned from
on-ground transportation and industry. As heavy-duty and medium-duty vehicles emerge to
decarbonize on-ground emissions, the industry needs high-capacity onboard energy storage. The
low specific energy storage capacity and slow charging times of batteries make them nonviable
for heavy-duty vehicles, especially for a longer range of operation. To meet the continued growth
of airports and the growing needs of hydrogen-powered aircraft, the aviation industry may look
to less fixed infrastructure initially. Mobile refueling vehicles emerge as a more practical
solution in the near-term adaptation compared to traditional centralized, fixed refueling stations
for on-ground vehicle applications. This application leverages existing fueling protocols. There
are ongoing efforts to develop aviation-focused fueling protocols for both gaseous and liquid
hydrogen.

 119

There are several promising short-term and long-term solutions currently being explored for
propulsion systems to bring energy innovation to the aviation sector. Hydrogen-powered and
electrified propulsion systems have emerged as leading practical solutions to achieve zero in-
flight emissions for passenger flights. Traditional turbine engine combustors, both terrestrial and
in aircraft, can run on hydrogen in some cases. However, the systems are optimized for
petroleum fuels and therefore see a decrease in performance. A PEMFC has a high technology
readiness level, compact build, and high-power densities. However, its low operating
temperatures require bulky and efficient thermal management systems because of a low-grade
operating temperature and a smaller temperature gradient between the fuel cell system and
environment to eliminate heat. The need for oversizing the cooling system in low-temperature
fuel cells translates into increased air intake requirements, potentially resulting in higher drag on
the aircraft. Although high-temperature fuel cells have shortcomings, including increased
thermal, chemical, and structural stresses, high-temperature fuel cells have the potential to adapt
in the aviation application with lower (and less bulky) thermal management needs.

Integration of hydrogen systems can offer cross-sectoral benefits to increase overall efficiency,
economy, and resilience of the entire energy system. Large facility campuses such as larger
airport facilities have historically benefited from central heating and cooling facilities that, in
some cases, include power generation. The integration of hydrogen energy storage on the grid
presents the challenging round-trip efficiency of energy conversion processes. Though hydrogen
distribution network and on-site storage have primarily economical barriers, hydrogen can be
stored for longer periods, making it suitable for seasonal storage. This aspect of hydrogen storage
is particularly valuable for regions with significant seasonal variations in renewable energy
generation and demand.

Because there is no unified set of codes and standards suitable for the airport environment,
section 2 of this report provides a snapshot of current codes and standards consolidated for the
airport application. Standards such as NFPA 2, NFPA 70, ASME B31.12, and CGA G-5.5 are
commonly used in the industry and can be easily applied to hydrogen infrastructure at airports. It
is important to note that many of the aforementioned standards are not specialized nor
comprehensive enough for hydrogen airport implementation. The field of hydrogen safety is
always expanding, with new developments and standards being published every month.
Stakeholders must stay informed of codes and standards revisions and make an effort to find the
latest standards publications.

Delving further into hydrogen fuel technology, in sections 3 and 4 of this report, we focus on
identifying hydrogen aircraft consumption and hydrogen aircraft refueling patterns and applying
this information as well as that from section 2 to a few case studies. In these case studies, we
identify on-ground infrastructure needs, potential system-level costs, emissions, and economic
impact from deploying novel hydrogen aircraft at four distinct airports in the United States. The
airports in the West Coast region were analyzed because of existing traffic patterns and
proximity to hydrogen aviation startup companies, like ZeroAvia, and their accommodation for
airframes such as the Dash 8 Q400 and Cessna 208 Caravan, which are likely candidates for
retrofitting with hydrogen-operated powertrains.

We developed hydrogen demand scenarios for each airport based on the typical number of flights
each airframe makes to the airport in a day and the fuel consumed during the flight to the airport.

 120

Aircraft fuel economy is derived from specifications from ZeroAvia as well as from the in-flight
hydrogen consumption modeling work described in section 3.2 and section 4.1. In future work,
we would like to continue developing and refining the aircraft hydrogen consumption model so
that the model can support additional airframes and account for liquid hydrogen storage.

Sections 3.3 and 4.2 in this study detail potential hydrogen refueling systems and refueling rates
required to dispense gaseous hydrogen into hydrogen-powered airframes. Based on ZeroAvia’s
aircraft specifications, these refueling configurations and refill rates are most applicable to the
Cessna 208 Caravan, which is expected to operate using gaseous hydrogen. The refueling rate,
refueling pressure, and refueling configuration laid out in section 4.2 are used as input to the
technoeconomic modeling for Demand Case 1, which covers hydrogen demands for Cessna 208
Caravans at each airport of interest. The work performed in section 4.2 on hydrogen refueling
allows us to represent gaseous hydrogen refueling station infrastructure and costs with
confidence. In future iterations of this work, we would like to make a more in-depth examination
of mobile hydrogen refueling stations. At present, most airplanes are refueled using mobile
refuelers instead of fixed stations because mobile refuelers are more space and time efficient. In
addition, we would like to expand H2FillS in the future so that it can consider liquid hydrogen
storage and dispensing and then can be used to model hydrogen refueling characteristics for
larger hydrogen-power aircraft.

We also address hydrogen codes and safety. In section 2.8, we provide context as to the current
hydrogen codes and standards that could apply to airports. In sections 3.5 and 4.3, we build on
these codes and standards by identifying specific safety events, consequences, and potential
safeguards to hydrogen infrastructure at airports using HAZID concepts. In addition, we look at
case studies at airports on the West Coast exploring where hydrogen infrastructure, particularly
hydrogen production and storage infrastructure, could meet all safety codes and standards. We
focus on hydrogen storage and production because of the large setback distances required for
these components. Although we determine that there are spaces around the airports that can
potentially accommodate hydrogen production and storage infrastructure, it may require the
displacement of other infrastructure at the airport. Careful coordination with the airport and FAA
would be needed to identify whether displacement of existing infrastructure is feasible, or
whether there even exists a location on or nearby the airport grounds where hydrogen systems
can be built while meeting federal obligations. In addition, the hydrogen demand level
anticipated at the airport would play an important role in space requirements and placement of
infrastructure. The infrastructure sizing used in section 4.3 (roughly 4 to 5 metric tons of H2
storage) would be most compatible with Demand Case 2, in which Dash 8 Q400 flights are
converted to hydrogen powertrains. For Case 2, we expect hydrogen demand levels between 400
and 1,200 kg per day, resulting in multiple days of on-site storage capacity. Note again that the
airport case studies discussed in this report are strictly hypothetical. The airports were not active
participants in this study and do not endorse any conclusions that are part of this report.

Importantly, there will be other limitations on incorporating hydrogen and its accompanying
infrastructure at airports. Statutory federal obligations such as airport planning requirements,
compatibility with existing infrastructure, safe operations, minimized impact on other
aeronautical activities, and other legal duties must be met as hydrogen is integrated into the
airport. Detailed planning and appropriate risk assessments must be carried out to ensure safe
operations and other statutory requirements are met. For a proper risk assessment to be carried

 121

out, safety standards specific to hydrogen-powered aircraft and aircraft infrastructure operations
should first be developed to remove ambiguity with existing hydrogen safety codes and
standards that may not be acceptable for aviation end-use applications.

In our technoeconomic analysis, we find that, as more airframes can operate on hydrogen fuel,
hydrogen demand and hydrogen station capacity will grow, and the cost of dispensing hydrogen
into the airplanes can be considerably reduced. As noted for Demand Case 1, if only Cessna 208
Caravan airframes are converted to hydrogen powertrains, the demand level is on the order of
100 kg/day, resulting in a levelized cost of dispensed hydrogen of around $15 per kg ($15 per
gallon gasoline equivalent) in the best-case scenario. However, in a future in which turboprops
and regional jets are also operating using hydrogen fuel, hydrogen demand may reach thousands
of kilograms per day at airports, and dispensing costs can fall to as low as $5 to $6 per kilogram.
This would be approaching cost competitiveness with jet fuel, especially considering efficiency
gains with hydrogen fuel cell powertrains compared to conventional powertrains. In the near
term, given low demand levels, it is likely that any hydrogen demand at airports for aviation or
other applications will be delivered via trucks. However, as demand scales, the logistics of
hydrogen transport via trucking become more complicated and either on-site production or
hydrogen pipeline connections to airports may be more viable. Given the opportunity for follow-
on analysis, we would like to explore other delivery pathways, such as LH2 shipping and LH2
rail, and develop a technoeconomic model for mobile refueling stations instead of using
stationary refueling infrastructure costs in HDSAM. It would also be worthwhile to explore how
hydrogen production and delivery infrastructure could be built out to support multiple airports
and end-use applications, instead of each airport building out their own dedicated hydrogen
infrastructure.

The required infrastructure for hydrogen aircraft operations across airports in California, Oregon,
and Washington is expected to support local jobs in multiple sectors as well as to stimulate the
local economy of these states during construction and operation phases. From a national
perspective, projects that only require a refueling station on-site, with hydrogen purchased off-
site (Cases 1, 2 and 3.1), are expected to generate significantly less GDP and job growth
compared to the case in which hydrogen infrastructure for production, storage, liquefaction, and
dispensing are all built directly at the airport (Case 3.2). This is attributed to the higher
infrastructure investment at the airport in Case 3.2, thus requiring more workers to construct and
operate the equipment in comparison to the other demand cases. Noticeably, in section 4.4, we
find that on-site hydrogen production (Case 3.2) may also be both economic and logistically
viable when considering possible hydrogen demands for regional jets at each airport. Another
key finding is that the share of economic benefits that stay in each state versus the RoUS vary
considerably among the three selected regions. California retains most of the job and economic
benefits because of the size and sectoral diversity of its economy, whereas Oregon loses the most
economic gain to other states. As for future goals, our team would like to further explore the
relationship between technoeconomic analysis and job and economic impact analysis to find
hydrogen supply and delivery pathways that minimize cost while maximizing jobs and economic
growth in the airport region.

In sections 3 and 4, we identify fuel consumption patterns, refueling characteristics, and safety
codes and standards that apply to hydrogen-powered aircraft and accompanying infrastructure.
This information was applied to the airports in the West Coast to gain insight on (1) how much

 122

space hydrogen infrastructure could take up, (2) where hydrogen infrastructure could be placed,
and (3) the cost and economic impact of hydrogen infrastructure at each airport. Our results
indicate each airport has a few locations where hydrogen storage and, if needed, hydrogen
production facilities could be located based on the hydrogen aircraft demand scenarios analyzed
in this study. Notably, hydrogen production would be a non-aeronautical activity and would not
be a requirement to have at airport. Airport hydrogen production would likely only be built at an
airport if it abides by all federal regulation and if hydrogen demand at the airport scales to a
magnitude of tens of metric ton per day or higher such that it is cost advantageous. Therefore,
onsite hydrogen production at an airport would not be a viable near-term option, but an option
that could be feasible in a few decades if hydrogen-powered aircraft demand scales.

This study provides an overview of the status of hydrogen technology development and how
hydrogen infrastructure could be leveraged at airports to meet future aviation sector energy
demands. It is intended to be used as a guide by industry, airport officials, and government
agencies to explore the use of hydrogen in aviation and airport infrastructure. Defining potential
systemwide best practices in refueling systems and facility layouts can better prepare
manufacturers, airports, and communities to participate in this emerging industry and diminish
market barriers involving uncertainty in standards and operational requirements of hydrogen
aircraft and airport infrastructure.
