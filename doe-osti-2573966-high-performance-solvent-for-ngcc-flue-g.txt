Executive Summary
Amine-based solvent absorption is the most mature and reliable technology for large scale CO2 capture,
dating back to the 1930s when monoethanolamine (MEA) was used to treat acid gases from oil refineries.
However, while strategic advancements have optimized the CO2 capture process, the cost of capture
remains high, where current estimates suggest that CO2 capture costs are around $72/tonne of CO2. To
address this, solvent development and optimization have become a focus of current research. Key solvent
properties to consider are: (1) CO2 working capacity of the solvent, (2) rate of absorption of CO2, (3) solvent
degradation rate, and (4) enthalpy of CO2 absorption. Ideally, an optimized solvent will have low water
content, viscosity and degradation rates and avoid precipitation.
This project sought to develop a high-performance solvent to reduce the overall cost of CO2 capture from
NGCC flue gas. Here, solvent optimization focused on: (1) reducing the energy required for CO2 desorption
in a reboiler, (2) improving CO2 absorption and desorption reaction kinetics, (3) improving solvent stability,
and (4) reducing environmental impacts. Susteon has developed and evaluated a promoted solvent for
NGCC flue gas CO2 capture. Additionally, a conceptual process design was developed to perform a
preliminary technoeconomic assessment to determine the solvent’s impact on the cost of capture.
Using a design of experiments methodology, Sustenol™ solvent was optimized for a significantly lower
energy for regeneration. Sustenol™ exhibits lower sensible heat and has three times higher absorption
kinetics compared to 30 wt% MEA solvent. The optimized Sustenol™ also shows a higher dynamic CO2
absorption capacity of ~0.5 molCO2/molamine compared to 0.25 molCO2/molamine for 30 wt% MEA.
Additionally, the solvent is oxidatively, thermally and hydrothermally stable, which leads to lower solvent
loss and emissions. These advancements have resulted in a solvent regeneration energy of 2.16 GJ/tonne of
CO2 which is >30% lower than current state-of-the-art commercial and emerging solvents.
To test this solvent under relevant conditions, Susteon designed and built a bench-scale unit to capture CO2
from a simulated NGCC flue gas (~4% CO2). An industrial standard solvent, 30 wt% MEA, served as a
baseline to compare the performance of the Sustenol™ solvent. Studies indicated that the Sustenol™ has
much faster reaction kinetics compared to 30 wt% MEA. These results were then validated in a 1
tonneCO2/day pilot plant, where Sustenol™ reached a 61% reduction in regeneration energy over seven
hours of continuous testing at >96% of CO2 removal.
All physical and thermochemical properties of Sustenol™ were measured at varying temperatures.
Importantly, Sustenol™ demonstrated twice the CO2 loading capacity compared to 30 wt% MEA at the
absorber inlet conditions for a typical NGCC flue gas and the solvent could be regenerated. The heat of
absorption, heat capacity, and the specific reboiler duty of Sustenol™ were all significantly lower compared
to 30 wt% MEA and Cansolv, the reference solvent from Shell used in the NETL baseline report.11 Using
these properties, a rate-based model was developed in Aspen Plus using measured reaction rates, which
accurately predicted CO2 concentration and temperature profiles obtained in Susteon’s bench scale absorber
column at various gas and liquid flow rates. This process model was used to develop a high-fidelity
technoeconomic analysis (TEA) for post-combustion CO2 capture from a 687 MWe NGCC power plant.
The TEA basis was to capture 5,800 metric tons of CO2/day at 97% capture efficiency, potentially
sequestering or utilizing nearly 2 million tons of CO2 per year. Combined with empirical data from the
bench and pilot scale testing, this preliminary TEA study indicated the cost of CO2 capture by Sustenol™
for 97% CO2 removal at $54/tonne and for 90% removal at $49/tonne, with a pathway to achieve $45/tonne
of CO2 with continued process and solvent advancements. Susteon has developed a technology roadmap to
reduce the cost of CO2 capture to <$45/tonne for NGCC flue gas. Susteon plans to derisk this technology
for commercial deployment through comprehensive solvent degradation testing, long-term testing in a pilot
plant at 5 tonneCO2/day and demonstration scale testing at 100 tonneCO2/day with NGCC flue gas and
engineering design studies to qualify Sustenol™ as a drop-in replacement solvent.

Introduction and Objectives
Introduction
Amine-based solvent technology is the workhorse of large-scale CO2 capture and is used in several
commercial solvents for post-combustion CO2 capture, including Mitsubishi Heavy Industries’ (MHI’s)
KS™ series, Shell Cansolv®, Fluor EconamineSM, and SLB Capturi’s green solvents, among many others.
However, current amine-based solvents are unable to cost-effectively reach 97% CO2 capture capacity from
a natural gas combined cycle (NGCC) power plant’s flue gas. The high cost of capture is mainly attributed
to amine degradation and the energy requirement for solvent regeneration as well as large CAPEX for
absorption and desorption columns and associated equipment.1–3 Extensive efforts to develop non-aqueous
and water-lean solvents are underway, with significant investments by the Department of Energy (DOE)
and industry to reduce the overall CAPEX and OPEX of the CO2 capture processes. However, these solvent
systems exhibit technical challenges and require additional equipment to address the issues.4,5
Susteon has developed the Sustenol™ solvent specifically tailored for NGCC flue gas CO2 capture. The
objectives of this bench-scale solvent development project were to validate the design principles and
develop a solvent to cost-effectively capture >97% CO2 with >95% CO2 purity. Utilization of a highly
stable mixed amine solvent containing proprietary additives that have high reaction rates with CO2 was the
key innovation to achieve a 40% reduction in the cost of CO2 capture for NGCC flue gas. Notably,
Sustenol™ has the following attributes:
 • A mixed amine solvent with effective promoters for fast adsorption and desorption kinetics for
 significantly improved CO2 capture efficiency at low CO2 concentrations (~4%), representing an
 NGCC flue gas
 • A water lean solvent with reduced energy requirements for solvent regeneration
 • A solvent with high oxidative, thermal, and hydrothermal stability for reduced solvent loss and
 emissions
 • A solvent requiring reduced CAPEX due to fast kinetics and mass transfer
 • An environmentally safe solvent
 • A solvent that captures >97% of CO2 from an NGCC flue gas with >95% CO2 purity at a low L/G
 ratio
The anticipated benefits of this high-performance solvent process are (1) greater than 40% reduction in the
energy required for solvent regeneration, (2) fast kinetics of CO2 absorption and desorption to reduce the
equipment size, (3) drastic increase in solvent lifetime/stability due to high thermal and oxidative stability,
(4) lower environmental impact, and (5) lower reboiler duty or steam requirement for the regeneration
process. The project goals are shown in Table 1 in comparison with the state-of-art Cansolv® process.

Technical Approach
To achieve the project objectives, Susteon
[1] Screened promoters to enhance the reaction rate of initial Sustenol™ formulation with CO2.
[2] Synthesized, characterized, and evaluated the promoted solvents using a lab-scale test unit (LSTU).
[3] Down selected formulations based on 3 sets of nested design of experiments that incorporated CO2
 capture efficiency (%), viscosity (cP), heat capacity (kJ/kg/K) and working capacity (molCO2/molalk).
[4] Optimized and tested selected formulations in a bench-scale system (BSTU) to obtain the optimum
 process parameters.
[5] Selected two solvent formulations for parametric and long-term testing to estimate the CO2 working
 capacities and relative stripping energy required to regenerate the solvent for target CO2 capture
 efficiency (>97%) in the Susteon’s BSTU.
[6] Produced the final optimized down-selected solvent (Sustenol™) in bulk quantity and validated its
 performance using a continuous 1 tonneCO2 per day pilot-scale test unit (PSTU).
[7] Developed a detailed rate-based thermodynamic process model in Aspen Plus™ simulation platform
 based on characterization and experimental results obtained for Sustenol™.
[8] Performed a technoeconomic analysis (TEA) based on a commercial scale CO2 capture system
 developed for 740 MW NGCC power plant based on Case B31B.97 of the DOE-NETL report (Rev
 4a).
The technical approach utilized by the project team is detailed below.

The primary objective of this project was to develop a high-performance solvent to reduce the overall cost

Measurements of Physical and Chemical Properties
Density Measurements:
The densities of solvents with and without CO2 were measured using an oscillating-tube density meter
(Anton Parr, DMA 4500) at temperatures ranging between 25-75°C. The CO2 loading process, described
in Figure 2, was performed under atmospheric pressure on the prepared solvent. After the desired solution
temperature was reached, CO2 was passed through the solvent for a certain amount of time. A control valve
was used to maintain a constant flow of gas in solution. The measuring cylinder was covered with aluminum
foil to prevent any loss by evaporation. There was no precipitation observed during CO2 loading under the
described experimental conditions. The loading was determined by the density difference method, where
the density of a prepared solution was measured before and after the loading of CO2 gas and any increase
in measured density was attributed to the weight of CO2 in the loaded solution. Measured densities are also
verified with a 10 mL pycnometer for fresh and CO2 loaded solvents.

 Figure 2. CO2 loading process and measurement of density at different temperatures.

Viscosity Measurements:
The kinematic viscosity was measured using a Canon-Fenske viscometer immersed in a constant
temperature viscosity bath using the ASTM D 455 standard. The kinematic viscosity bath consists of an
illumination facility for better visibility, a stirrer for improved temperature uniformity throughout the

 11

chamber, and a multi-span temperature controller (UTC 421P) with an accuracy of ± 0.1 K. The viscosity
of water was measured by the viscometer and used as a standard. The viscometer was filled with 8–10 mL
of water and then immersed in the viscosity bath. The temperature of the bath was set to the desired level.
When the bath reached the desired temperature, suction is applied to the viscometer until the water reached
the upper marking of the lower bulb. The water would then flow from the lower bulb's upper making to the
lower bulb’s lower marking. A stopwatch with an accuracy of 0.1 s was used to record the time of flow.
The experiment was repeated at the desired temperature range of 298–348 K. The measured viscosities for
both fresh and CO2 loaded solvents were validated with measured viscosities from a Bonvoison Digital
Rotary Viscometer (NDJ-8S) using L1 rotor (30-60 rpm) with an accuracy of ± 1 cP.
Surface Tension Measurements:
The surface tension of solutions was measured using a Kyowa DY-300 surface tensiometer, with an
accuracy of 0.2 mN/m for water. Average absolute deviation (AAD%, absolute (Literature Value -
Measured value)/Literature value) *100) was calculated for water from literature and found to be ≅0.1%.

Vapor-Liquid Equilibrium (VLE) Measurements:
VLE experiments of CO2 for the aqueous solutions were performed using a stainless steel (SS) stirred
reaction cell connected to a SS buffer cell represented in Figure 3. Temperature sensors with an accuracy
of ± 0.1 K are mounted at the respective cell to measure the temperature. For each experimental run, both
cells were evacuated simultaneously. Then, the buffer cell was filled with pure CO2, and the reaction cell
was charged with solution and degassed. The reaction cell remained under vacuum to reach vapor pressure.
The vapor pressure (Pv) at the experimental temperature inside the reaction cell and the initial pressure in
the buffer cell (PBi) were recorded and a controlled amount of CO2 was transferred from the buffer cell to
the reaction cell. As CO2 was absorbed by the solution, the total pressure (Pt) of the cell decreases.
Equilibrium between vapor and the liquid phase was established when total pressure remained constant for
1 hour. At equilibrium, the total pressure in the reaction cell, and the final pressure of the buffer cell (P Bf),
were recorded simultaneously. The equilibrium pressure (PCO2) at the experimental temperature was
calculated using the following:
 PCO2 = Pt − Pv Equation 11

Task 6.0: Process Modeling
A thermodynamic model was developed based on an Electrolyte-Non-Random Two Liquid (E-NRTL)
model with Redlich-Kwong (RK) Equation of State16 to represent the non-ideal behavior of the solvent.
Experimental data for physical and chemical properties were measured at different temperatures and CO 2
loadings to update the process model. The Aspen Plus™ platform was used to regress these properties with
known correlations and establish a rate-based process model. Process design for the plant was developed
using the rate-based thermodynamic model for both 30 wt% MEA and the optimized Sustenol™.
Data regression for the Sustenol™ process model includes:
 • Pure component properties as a function of pressure, temperature and mole fractions
 • Binary component properties with water and CO2 as a function of pressure, temperature, and mole
 fractions
 • Ternary component properties with amines, water and CO2 as a function of pressure, temperature
 and mole fractions
 • Sustenol™ mixture component properties with water and CO2 as a function of pressure,
 temperature and mole fractions
The Sustenol™ process model consists of three model components and required properties to represent
mass and heat transfer during CO2 absorption and desorption along the absorber-desorber packing columns:
 1. Thermodynamic Model
 2. Rate-based Model
 3. Transport Model

Transport Model
The Sustenol™ transport process model uses the following properties to determine the mass and heat
transfer in the absorber-desorber columns, which is based on dimensionless numbers such as Reynolds
number (Dvϱ/µ), Prandtl number (Cpµ/k), etc.:
 • Density (ϱ) as a function of temperature, pressure and CO2 loading
 • Viscosity (µ) as a function of temperature, pressure and CO2 loading
 • Surface tension (σ) as a function of temperature, pressure and CO2 loading
 • Thermal conductivity (k) as a function of temperature, pressure and CO2 loading
 • Heat Capacity (Cp, kJ/kg/K) as a function of temperature, pressure and CO2 loading
 • Heat of absorption (Habs, kJ/molCO2) as a function of temperature, pressure and CO2 loading
All properties of the Sustenol™ components were measured and regressed. The model prediction required
for transport properties was used in the mass and heat transfer correlations in the absorber-desorber columns.
A rate-based model was developed based on the CO2 flux measured at different temperatures using the
zwitterionic reaction mechanism of amines and amino-acids in Sustenol™ with CO2.

Process Design for Commercial Scale
The process model was used to scale up to accommodate the large power plant flue gas rate, based on the
design proposed by Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI)22, which consists of two absorber trains with
a diameter of ~20 meters that each treat half of the flue gas. The rich solvent streams then combine into a
single solvent regeneration unit. Two four-stage compressors are used to compress pure CO2 to 151.7 barg
(2,200 psig) for transportation and storage. Figure 23 represents a conceptual commercial scale design
used to develop this TEA. The absorber columns, with conventional intercooling, were sized at 80% of
flooding, all stripper columns were sized at 70% of flooding, and each packed section has a maximum
height of 6 meters. The process was evaluated to calculate the CO2 avoidance cost with a fixed absorber
height of 53 meters based on the design proposed by Moioli and Pellegrini (2019). 23 The CO2 loading in
the lean solvent was maintained at 0.15 mol CO2/molalk with a reboiler temperature of 120°C. The lean/rich
solvent cross exchanger was designed at a 5°C approach temperature. Six cases of Sustenol™ with capture
efficiencies between 90% and 99.95% were evaluated for NGCC power plants and compared with 30 wt%
MEA and CANSOLV processes described in the NETL report (Rev 4a)19. The key equipment sizing
information and total energy requirement in optimized NGCC cases for CO2 capture ranging between 90%
to 99.80% were presented in a separate TEA report24.
The six evaluated cases are as follows:
Case No. 1 (NGCC-1): 90% CO2 capture efficiency with 4,282 ppmv CO2 in exhaust
Case No. 2 (NGCC-2): 95% CO2 capture efficiency with 2,165 ppmv CO2 in exhaust
Case No. 3 (NGCC-3): 97.7% CO2 capture efficiency with 1,019 ppmv CO2 in exhaust
Case No. 4 (NGCC-4): 99.04% CO2 capture efficiency with 420 ppmv CO2 in exhaust (Net-Zero)
Case No. 5 (NGCC-5): 99.77% CO2 capture efficiency with 105 ppmv CO2 in exhaust (Net-negative)
Case No. 6 (NGCC-6): 99.8% CO2 capture efficiency with 90 ppmv CO2 in exhaust

To achieve zero-emissions for NGCC plants (99.04% capture, to attain ~420 ppm in the exhaust gas), an
L/G ratio of 1.2 with an SRD of 2.94 GJ/tonneCO2 is estimated, whereas for 97% capture at an L/G ratio of
1.1 is needed with an SRD of 2.39 GJ/tonneCO2. Process flow diagram (PFD) of the Sustenol™ system used
in the TEA and corresponding heat and material balances with list of equipment for CO2 capture are given
in Appendix C in the TEA report24.

Technical Achievements
Major accomplishments of this 18-month project are the following.
 [1] We successfully scaled up the synthesis process of the promoted solvent from gram to kilogram
 quantities. The large batch of promoted solvent was characterized and compared with small laboratory
 synthesized solvent, and no difference was found in the chemical and physical properties between the
 two samples.
 [2] We successfully formulated an optimized solvent with 47% higher CO2 working capacity than baseline
 solvent and 50% reduction in regeneration energy with higher CO2 capture efficiency >97%.
 [3] We successfully determined all the properties required to update the State-Point table to compare the
 solvent performance with state-of-the-art solvent technologies. Sustenol™ solvent has adsorption and
 desorption rates significantly higher than that of the current DOE’s baseline solvent (Cansolv®).
 [4] We successfully performed a commercial-scale process design using the optimized solvent
 formulation test data from laboratory-scale, bench-scale and pilot scale CO2 capture plants. A high-
 fidelity techno-economic analysis was performed for 650 MW NGCC power plants and compared
 with 30 wt% MEA and Cansolv®) processes described in the NETL report (Rev 4a).
 [5] We have successfully scaled up our technology readiness level (TRL) from TRL-2 to TRL-5 since
 2021. Susteon has signed a solvent manufacturing agreement with a major contract manufacturer who
 has successfully produced 5,000 liters of Sustenol™. The final Technology Maturation Plan was
 prepared and submitted.

Conclusions
Susteon has developed SustenolTM, a drop-in high performance, water-lean, mixed amine solvent with fast
absorption and desorption kinetics at >95% CO2 capture efficiency for an NGCC flue gas (4% CO2). The
composition of the Sustenol™ solvent is optimized to reduce regeneration energy, increase absorption
reaction kinetics, enhance CO2 absorption capacity and reduce thermal and oxidative degradation through
a comprehensive design of experiment testing methodology. This optimization was achieved by conducting
extensive testing in a bench-scale absorber column and characterization of physicochemical properties for
both rich and lean solvent samples. Results obtained from the 15 kg/day CO2 bench-scale column were then
validated in a 1 tonne/day pilot plant.
Results from bench and pilot scale testing were used to develop a comprehensive TEA for SustenolTM. The
cumulative TEA indicated the cost of CO2 capture by Sustenol™ for 97% CO2 removal at $54/tonne and
for 90% removal at $49/tonne. Susteon has developed a technology roadmap to reduce the cost of CO2
capture to <$45/tonne for NGCC flue gas. The cost of 97% CO2 capture from an NGCC flue gas by
Sustenol™ is lower than both 30 wt% MEA and Cansolv®. Susteon plans to derisk this technology for
commercial deployment through comprehensive solvent degradation testing, long-term testing in a pilot
plant at 5 tonneCO2/day and demonstration scale testing at 100 tonneCO2/day with NGCC flue gas and
engineering design studies to qualify Sustenol™ as a drop-in replacement solvent.
