Regularization of the metric generalized inverse in Banach spaces and the dichotomy phenomenon
Peter Mathé, Bernd Hofmann

Abstract
. For a bounded linear operator acting between Banach spaces, its metric generalized inverse is the analog to the prominent Moore-Penrose inverse for operators acting between Hilbert spaces. This generalized inverse is well-defined for Banach spaces that are strictly convex and reflexive. Previous studies had been restricted to closed range situations, where the metric generalized inverse constitutes a continuous homogeneous mapping. The focus of the present study is on the ill-posed situation in the sense of Nashed, when the governing operator has a non-closed range. We define and analyze iterative schemes as the Landweber and the Schulz-Newton method, as well as parametric schemes with focus on a specific Tikhonov method. Both types are called regularizations aimed at approximating the metric generalized inverse. As a fundamental feature of such schemes we observe a dichotomy. This emphasizes that these schemes, when applied to elements of the domain of the metric generalized inverse, approximate the corresponding best approximate solutions well, whereas the resulting approximations will be asymptotically unbounded if they are applied to elements that do not belong to the domain of the metric generalized inverse.

1.
Introduction

We are to consider operator equations

(1)

Ax=y,\quad x\in X,

for a
bounded linear
operator
A\colon X\to Y
, acting between

Banach spaces

X
and
Y
. Our goal is to ‘solve’ the inverse equation, which means
the reconstruction of
x=A^{-1}y\in X
from
y\in Y
.
This problem is called
ill-posed in the sense of Hadamard
if the operator
A
is
not boundedly invertible. One reason may be that a non-trivial
null-space
\mathcal{N}(A)\subset X
is present. The more interesting case is that the
range
\mathcal{R}(A)\subset Y
is not closed. Then the problem is called
ill-posed in the sense of Nashed

[
11
]
.

For operator equations (
1
) in Hilbert spaces
X
and
Y
the most prominent single-valued substitute for
A^{-1}
is the

Moore-Penrose generalized inverse

A^{\dagger}\colon\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})\subset Y\to X
.
This operator
A^{\dagger}
is linear in a Hilbert space setting and
bounded (continuous)
if
the range
\mathcal{R}(A)
of the operator
A
is closed, in which case we have
\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})=Y
. Otherwise, for
\mathcal{R}(A)\not=\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}

the linear operator
A^{\dagger}
is only densely defined on
Y
and
unbounded (not continuous)
, which requires

regularization
for treating the ill-posedness. This means
that we are to approximate the unbounded map
A^{\dagger}
by a family of
bounded maps. While in Hilbert spaces the most studied regularization
is of spectral type, see the monograph
[
4
]
, for problems
in Banach spaces other types of regularization are natural. The most
important one is
Tikhonov regularization
as a variant of variational regularization, see the
monograph
[
16
]
for details.

If
A:X\to Y
is a bounded linear mapping between two Banach spaces
X
and
Y
, different types of (often nonlinear)
generalized inverses

A^{\dagger}
to
A
exist. They also suffer from the possibly occurring ill-posedness of equation (
1
) in the sense that
A^{\dagger}
fails to be continuous. Then the goal of regularization is to build a family of (linear or nonlinear) continuous maps to approximate the respective generalized inverse
A^{\dagger}\colon\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})\subset Y\to X
.

The notion of metric generalized inverse, as an analog to the Moore-Penrose inverse, for operator equations in Banach space
is less studied. In Hilbert space every closed subspace is
complemented, i.e., there is a bounded linear projection onto it.
However, in a Banach space setting, both the closed null-space
\mathcal{N}(A)
of the bounded linear operator
A

as well as the closure of the range
\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}
may be
uncomplemented
,
and we refer to
[
5
]
and
[
8
]
for consequences of the uncomplementedness.
A comprehensive account for concepts of generalized inverse mappings is given in
[
10
]
and
[
12
]
. Still linear projections play a major role.
Here, it is not assumed to have appropriate linear projections. Instead,
metric projections are considered, and our focus is on
metric generalized inverses

A^{\dagger}\colon\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})\subset Y\to X

defined below in Definition
1
.
Given
y\in Y
, and a closed
subspace
U\subset Y
, we are to find
\pi_{U}(y)\in U
which
minimizes the distance
\left\|y-\pi_{U}(y)\right\|_{Y}=\inf_{z\in U}\left\|y-z\right\|_{Y}
. These metric projections exist whenever the subspace
is a
Chebyshev subspace
, which is weaker than requiring the subspace
to be complemented. Metric projections exist whenever the Banach
space under consideration is reflexive and strictly convex. Therefore, we make the
following global assumption with respect to properties of the Banach spaces
X
and
Y
.

Assumption 1

(Banach space geometry)
.

We consider a bounded linear operator
A\colon X\to Y
, where both spaces
X
and
Y
are
reflexive
and
strictly convex
Banach spaces.

Under this assumption, every closed
subspace of such a Banach space is a Chebyshev subspace. We will no further
dwell into this, and refer to the study
[
19
]
,
instead. Specifically we highlight that in this case the (nonlinear, but homogeneous) metric
projections
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}

and
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}
exist and are single-valued.
The
metric generalized inverse
was defined in
[
19
]
. Here we specify this as follows.

Definition 1

(metric generalized inverse)
.

Under Assumption
1
let
\mathcal{N}(A)\subset X

denote the closed null-space and
\mathcal{R}(A)\subset Y
the range of the bounded linear
operator
A:X\to Y
mapping between the Banach spaces
X
and
Y
. Then
\mathcal{N}(A)
and the closure
\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}
of the range are Chebyshev
subspaces of
X
and
Y
with metric projections
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}

and
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}
, respectively. There exists a
homogeneous mapping
A^{\dagger}\colon\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})\subset Y\to X
with the
following properties

(2)

\displaystyle AA^{\dagger}Ax

\displaystyle=Ax,\quad x\in X,

(3)

\displaystyle A^{\dagger}AA^{\dagger}y

\displaystyle=A^{\dagger}y,\quad y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger}),

(4)

\displaystyle A^{\dagger}Ax

\displaystyle=(I_{X}-\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)})x,\quad x\in X,

(5)

\displaystyle AA^{\dagger}y

\displaystyle=\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y,\quad y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger}).

This mapping is called the
metric generalized inverse
.

Remark 1
.

It is interesting to notice, that historically, in finite dimension, or in Hilbert space, the first two properties were
initially used in the original study by R. Penrose
[
13
]
, whereas the
last two properties are due to E. H. Moore
[
9
]
.

The construction of the mapping
A^{\dagger}
can be given explicitly, by using the
decomposition of the space
X
, namely as

(6)

X=\mathcal{N}(A)+C_{X}(A).

The right term
C_{X}(A)
is a closed cone. In general it is not a
linear subspace. Its description is given in Proposition
6
in
Appendix
A
.
Here, it is important to notice that the mapping
A\colon C_{X}(A)\to Y

is injective, such that formally
\left(A|_{C_{X}(A)}\right)^{-1}\colon\mathcal{R}(A)\to X
exists as a homogeneous single-valued mapping. So we can write

(7)

A^{\dagger}y=\left[\left(A|_{C_{X}(A)}\right)^{-1}\circ\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}\right](y),\ y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger}).

Additional details are also given in Proposition
6
of Appendix
A
.

Let us look at the
decomposition of the space
Y
as

(8)

Y=\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}+C_{Y}(A),

where the part
C_{Y}(A)\subset Y
constitutes a closed
cone. Again, further details are given in Appendix
A
.
The domain and range of
A^{\dagger}
attain the forms

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}(A)+C_{Y}(A),

and

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(A^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=C_{X}(A).

The seminal paper
[
19
]
and several follow-up studies focus on
the closed range situation
\mathcal{R}(A)=\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}
, where the operator equation (
1
) is well-posed in the sense of Nashed
[
11
]
, but it still may be ill-posed in the sense of Hadamard if the operator
A
is non-injective. In this case, the metric generalized inverse
A^{\dagger}
is defined on the whole space
\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})=Y
, and under weak additional assumptions it is even
continuous
, see
[
19
, Theorem 4.1]
.

However, in the present study attention is drawn to the case of a
non-closed range
, where

\mathcal{R}(A)\not=\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}
and the operator equation (
1
) is ill-posed in the sense of Nashed.
As seen from (
7
) the mapping
A^{\dagger}
is a homogeneous (in general nonlinear) operator, which is as a composition of a nonlinear (homogeneous) continuous projection operator
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}
, and the inverse of the restriction
of the injective operator
A
to the part
C_{X}(A)\subset X
. This inverse and hence the whole metric generalized inverse
A^{\dagger}
is

not continuous
, due to the non-closed range of
A
.

In this study we are to approximate the metric generalized inverse by
means of a sequence of mappings, or by a parametric family with focus on Tikhonov regularization,
respectively. The imposed conditions will be called regularization. The present setup is in contrast to solve approximately the ill-posed
operator equation (
1
), for which the monographs
[
14
]
and
[
16
]

yield comprehensive details.

When, for a given
y\in Y
, we use a regularizing sequence
X_{k}y,\,k=1,2,\dots
,
or a regularizing parametric family
x_{\alpha}(y),\,\alpha>0
, we
face two problems. If
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
then the approximations should converge to the existing best approximate solutions
x_{\ast}(y):=A^{\dagger}y
. To this end
we have to take care that asymptotically
C_{Y}(A)
is suppressed. On the other hand, for
y\not\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
the approximations cannot converge. Instead, under the made assumptions, they will necessarily ‘explode’ (in norm). We refer to this as the
dichotomy phenomenon
. Such dichotomy cannot be seen for operators
A
with a closed range
\mathcal{R}(A)
, because there are no elements
y\notin\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
. Such dichotomies have been observed for spectral regularization of operators acting between Hilbert spaces, and we highlight
[
18
, Thm. 5.2]
. Within the context of Tikhonov regularization the unboundedness in norm of the parametric approximations
x_{\alpha}(y)
, as
\alpha\to 0
, for data
y
which do not have a minimum norm solution, and hence which are not in
\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
, has been proven in
[
1
, Prop. 3.2]
.

We claim that the approximation of the metric generalized inverse (as a mapping) is
harder than the standard situation of solving (in a stable approximate manner) an ill-posed operator equation (
1
),
where noisy data
y^{\delta}
to an
exact right-hand side

y\in\mathcal{R}(A)
with
\|y^{\delta}-y\|_{Y}\leq\delta
and noise level
\delta>0
can be used. For the metric generalized inverse
A^{\dagger}
, noisy data
y^{\delta}=y+\xi

with
\|\xi\|_{Y}\leq\delta
may perturb
arbitrary elements

y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
that do not necessarily belong to
\mathcal{R}(A)
.

The study is arranged as follows. In
Section
2
we introduce the concept of

sequential regularization
to fix the requirements for the
approximations
X_{k}\colon Y\to X,\ k=0,1,2,\dotsc\,.

Section
3
, however, introduces the counterpart of
parametric regularization
.
For both cases, we highlight that the conal part
C_{Y}(A)\subset Y

will be suppressed, and we exhibit a dichotomy, distinguishing the
behavior for the approximations, depending on
whether
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
or not.
We complement the analysis with some discussion on the special case of Hilbert spaces in Section
4
. Two appendices complete the paper. On the one hand, Appendix
A
provides background information, with links to auxiliary studies. On the other hand, Appendix
B
gives a detailed proof of Theorem
4
.

2.
Sequential regularization: From Schulz to Landweber

There is vast literature for approximating the inverse of a matrix by
a sequence of mappings, probably starting with G. Schulz in 1933,
see
[
15
]
, which we recall
in §
2.1
, below. For a recent survey we refer to
[
17
]
, and we mention
[
2
]
, putting such schemes within the framework for approximation of the (bounded) metric generalized inverse.
To the best of our knowledge, such iterative
schemes were not considered for equations that are ill-posed in the sense of Nashed, except in Hilbert spaces.

Based on the
properties given in the items (
4
) and (
5
) of Definition
1
we
would require that those properties hold
asymptotically. It will be seen that an additional strengthening is
useful. We know from item (
iii
) of Proposition
7

in Appendix
A
that
A^{\dagger}y=A^{\dagger}\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y,\ y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
. For
y\in\mathcal{R}(A)
this trivially
holds true. However, taking into account the decomposition of
Y

from (
8
) we aim to require that the part

C_{Y}(A)
is suppressed, at least asymptotically.
Therefore we propose the following

Definition 2

(sequential regularization)
.

Given a bounded linear operator
A\colon X\to Y
, we call a
sequence
X_{k}\colon Y\to X,\ k=1,2,\dots,
of homogeneous mappings a regularization, if for
k\to\infty

(11)

\displaystyle X_{k}y

\displaystyle\longrightarrow A^{\dagger}y,\quad y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger}),

and

(12)

\displaystyle AX_{k}y

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y,\quad y\in Y.

Remark 2
.

For
y\in C_{Y}(A)
, we have that
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y=0\in\mathcal{R}(A)
and hence
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
as well as
x_{\ast}(y)=0
. Consequently, we have
A^{\dagger}y=0
whenever
y\in C_{Y}(A)
. This is analogous to the phenomenon of the Moore-Penrose generalized inverse

A^{\dagger}:\mathcal{R}(A)+\mathcal{N}(A^{*})\subset Y\to X
in Hilbert spaces, where also
A^{\dagger}y=0
for
y\in\mathcal{N}(A^{*})
.

The following observation shows that regularizations suppress the
information from
C_{Y}(A)
:

Proposition 1
.

Let
X_{k}y,\ k=0,1,2,\dots
be any sequential regularization in the sense of Definition
2
.
For
y\in Y
, we have that
X_{k}\left(y-\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y\right)\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. Thus the conal part is asymptotically suppressed.

Proof.

As mentioned in Remark
2
, we have for all
y\in Y
that
y-\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y\in C_{Y}(A)\subset\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
and that
x_{*}(y-\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y)=0
. From (
11
) we deduce further

X_{k}\left(y-\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y\right)\longrightarrow A^{\dagger}\left(y-\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y\right)=0.

This completes the proof.
∎

Based on this the following result shall be established.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
A\colon X\to Y
is a bounded operator
in a reflexive strictly convex Banach space
Y
. Let
X_{k},\ k=0,1,\dots,
be any regularization.

Either
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
, and there is the
minimum norm solution
x_{\ast}(y)
,
then we have that

X_{k}y\longrightarrow x_{\ast}(y)\quad\mbox{as}\quad k\to\infty;

or
y\not\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
and then

\left\|X_{k}y\right\|_{X}\to\infty\quad\mbox{as}\quad k\to\infty.

Proof.

The part concerning
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
coincides exactly with the
requirement (
11
), because
x_{\ast}(y)=A^{\dagger}y
whenever
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
.

Now, we assume contrarily that the sequence
X_{k}y,\ k=1,2,\dots,
is uniformly norm bounded. Since this space
X
is assumed to be reflexive, it has a weakly convergent
subsequence by the Eberlein-Shmulyan Theorem. Without loss of generality, we assume the weak convergence
X_{k}y\rightharpoonup z\in X
in the Banach space
X
. Since the operator
A
is weak-to-weak continuous, we find that
AX_{k}y\rightharpoonup Az
, weakly in
Y
. But the left-hand side converges by the regularizing property (
12
) to
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y
, which is not in
\mathcal{R}(A)
for
y\not\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
. Since the weak limit is
unique, we deduce that
Az=\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y
. This contradicts
the assumption for the second assertion, and thus completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

To continue, we look at the requirements (
11
)
and (
12
).
Specifically, for
y\in\mathcal{R}(A)
the convergence in (
11
)
is towards
A^{\dagger}Ax_{\ast}=\left(I_{X}-\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}\right)x_{\ast}
.
Thus, we consider the mappings

(13)

\displaystyle S_{k}x

\displaystyle\colon=\left(I_{X}-\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}-X_{k}A\right)x,\quad x\in X,

and

(14)

\displaystyle R_{k}y

\displaystyle\colon=\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y-AX_{k}y,\quad y\in Y.

We state the following properties.

Lemma 1
.

The following holds true.

(i)

We have that
S_{k}\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}x=0
,

(ii)

R_{k}y\in\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}
for
y\in Y
, and

(iii)

we have the identity
AS_{k}x=R_{k}Ax,\ x\in X
.

Proof.

The first two items are seen, immediately.
Finally,

\displaystyle AS_{k}x

\displaystyle=A\left(x-\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}x\right)-AX_{k}Ax=Ax-AX_{k}Ax

\displaystyle=\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}Ax-AX_{k}Ax=R_{k}Ax,\quad x\in X.

The proof is complete.
∎

We are to find conditions on the
sequence
X_{k},\ k=0,1,2,\dots
that allow for the sequences
R_{k},k=1,2,\dots
and
S_{k},k=1,2,\dots
to tend to zero.

Before continuing, we emphasize the following observation. Even if we guarantee that the iterations
X_{k}
map into the conal part
C_{X}(A)=J_{X}\left(\mathcal{N}(A)^{\perp}\right)
, then the misfit
S_{k}
might not tend to zero. This is the case, because this conal part is not a linear subspace, hence having
\left(I_{X}-\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}\right)x\in C_{X}(A)
, and
X_{k}Ax\in C_{X}(A)
will not guarantee that
S_{k}x\in C_{X}(A)
, unless the conal part constitutes a linear subspace. The latter is the case if either the operator
A
is injective, or the space
X
is a Hilbert space.
Therefore, when considering sequential regularization we confine to the case that the projection
x\to\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}x
is linear, covering both cases mentioned before. Under this assumption we have that
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}S_{k}=0
, provided that
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}X_{k}y=0
.

The convergence
analysis will be based on two facts. First,
a certain invariance property of the initial guess
X_{0}
is assumed. Then, we require a smallness
condition for
k=0
. In this case it will be seen that there is a contraction
as
k
grows.

Assumption 2

(Smallness condition)
.

Suppose that
X_{0}
is such that

(i)

(a)

we have that
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}X_{0}y=0,\ y\in Y
, and

(b)

X_{0}\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y=X_{0}y,\ y\in Y
.

(ii)

For each
x\in X,\ \left\|x\right\|_{X}\leq 1
, and
y\in Y,\ \left\|y\right\|_{Y}\leq 1
, there is
0<q<1
, such
that

(15)

\displaystyle\left\|\left(I_{X}-\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}+X_{0}A\right)x\right\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq q

and

(16)

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}-AX_{0}\right)y\right\|_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq q.

Remark 3
.

The first two items are trivially fulfilled whenever the
operator
A
is injective and has a dense range, because in such a case

\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}x=0
for
x\in X
and
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y=y
for
y\in Y
.

Next we will consider specific sequences
X_{k},\ k=1,2,\dots
. Again, we mention
the recent study
[
2
]
, where results, similar to the ones
below are given. However, proved for operators with closed range, and
for
quasi-additive
mappings
A^{\dagger}
. The latter holds true
exactly when the metric projection
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}
is linear.

2.1.
The Schulz-Newton method

The following scheme is classical and had been published by G. Schulz in 1933,

[
15
]
for invertible
matrices (with trivial null space) in the form
X_{k+1}=X_{k}(2I_{Y}-AX_{k}),\ k=0,1,\dots
.
This translates to

(17)

X_{k+1}y:=X_{k}y+X_{k}R_{k}y,\quad y\in Y.

The following lemma holds true.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that the projection
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}
is linear.
Under Assumption
2
the following holds true

(i)

We have, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, that
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}X_{k}y=0,\ y\in Y
; and

(ii)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
that
X_{k}\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y=X_{k}y,\ y\in Y
.

(iii)

Moreover,
S_{k+1}=S_{k}^{2}
and
R_{k+1}=R_{k}^{2},\,k=0,1,2,\dots\,.

Proof.

To verify (
i
) and (
ii
) we use induction. The assertion is true
for
k=0
. Suppose that it holds true for some
k>0
.
Under the hypothesis for
k
we have that
X_{k}y=X_{k}\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y
, which in turn yields that
R_{k}y=R_{k}\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y
.
This implies

X_{k+1}\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y=X_{k}\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y+X_{k}R_{k}\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y=X_{k+1}y.

Similar reasoning applies to
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}X_{k}y
, provided that
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}
is linear. This in turn yields
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}S_{k}x=0,\ x\in X
.

For the assertion in item (
iii
) we use the definition of
S_{k}x
, making use of item (
iii
) of Lemma
1
, that

\displaystyle S_{k+1}x

\displaystyle=x-\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}x-X_{k+1}Ax

\displaystyle=\left(x-\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}x\right)-\left(X_{k}+X_{k}R_{k}\right)Ax

\displaystyle=\left(x-\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}x-X_{k}Ax\right)-X_{k}R_{k}Ax

\displaystyle=\left(x-\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}x-X_{k}Ax\right)-X_{k}AS_{k}x

\displaystyle=S_{k}x-X_{k}AS_{k}x

\displaystyle=S_{k}x-\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}S_{k}x-X_{k}AS_{k}x

\displaystyle=S_{k}^{2}x.

Again, the proof for
R_{k+1}
is similar.
∎

2.2.
Landweber iteration: From Hilbert space to Banach space

The metric generalized inverse in Banach spaces is the extension of the Moore–Penrose
generalized inverse in Hilbert spaces. This means that the metric generalized inverse
A^{\dagger}
coincides
with the Moore-Penrose inverse when
X
and
Y
are Hilbert spaces,
because the defining properties are identical. Regularization is
required whenever the operator equation (
1
) is
ill-posed. Besides spectral regularization, as for instance Tikhonov
regularization, iterative schemes gained interest, see the
monograph
[
4
]
for details. The monograph
[
18
]

is devoted to a systematic study of such
schemes, and Theorem
1
above mimics Theorem 5.2 ibid.

It seems interesting to discuss the relation of those iterative
schemes in Hilbert space with those considered here. To be specific we
consider Landweber iteration for an operator
T\colon H\to G
acting
between Hilbert spaces. Then the iterates, starting with some
initial
x_{0}
, and data
y\in G
, as

(18)

x_{n+1}:=x_{n}+\mu T^{\ast}\left(y-Tx_{n}\right),\quad n=0,1,\dots

We readily see that the residuals
y-Tx_{n}
play a prominent
role. This is in accordance with the mappings
R_{k}
from
above, see the second order scheme, studied before. However, and this seems crucial, the adjoint
operator
T^{\ast}\colon G=G^{\ast}\to H=H^{\ast}
is used to
map the residual information to the space
H
. In order to extend such
iteration to a Banach space setting the duality mappings are used, and
a corresponding iterative scheme was developed and analyzed
in
[
16
, Chapt. 6]
.
Within the present context of strictly convex and reflexive
Banach spaces the dual mapping is continuous and single-valued. Therefore, we propose to replace the adjoint operator
T^{\ast}
of
the Hilbert space setting, using the dual
operator
A^{\prime}\colon Y^{\prime}\to X^{\prime}
of the
present
A\colon X\to Y
, by

(19)

B:=\left(J_{X}\right)^{-1}A^{\prime}J_{Y}\colon Y\to X,

in the Banach space setting. Above, the spaces
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
denote the dual spaces to
X
and
Y
, respectively. Then the version of the Landweber
iteration from (
18
) reads as

(20)

X_{k+1}y:=X_{k}y+\mu B\left(y-A{X_{k}y}\right)=X_{x}y+\mu BR_{k}y.

Remark 4
.

Comparing this iterative procedure with the Schulz-Newton method from (
17
), started with
X_{0}y=\mu By
, we recognize the following: Landweber iteration updates the residuals
R_{k}y
always with the initial
X_{0}:=\mu B
, whereas the Schulz-Newton method uses the current
X_{k}
, instead. This may lead to an improved performance of the Schulz-Newton method, and this will be manifested in Lemma
4
, below.

Therefore, it makes sense to investigate the relevant properties of the mapping
B
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
B
be as in (
19
). The following holds true.

(i)

We have that
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}By=0,\ y\in Y
,

(ii)

B\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y=By,\ y\in Y
.

(iii)

An element
y\in Y
fulfills
ABy=0
if and only if
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y=0
.

Proof.

In the proof we use Proposition
5
.
To prove item (
i
) it is required to show that
B\left(y-\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y\right)=0
. Let
y\in C_{Y}(A)=J_{Y}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{R}(A)^{\perp}\right)
, we have to show that
By=0
.
We notice that
\mathcal{R}(A)^{\perp}=\mathcal{N}(A^{\prime})
, and hence there
is
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(A^{\prime})
with
y=J_{Y}^{-1}(y^{\prime})
.
But

By=J_{X}^{-1}A^{\prime}J_{Y}(y)=J_{X}^{-1}A^{\prime}J_{Y}J_{Y}^{-1}(y^{\prime})=J_{X}^{-1}A^{\prime}y^{\prime}=0,

which proves the first assertion.

Similarly we proceed for item (
ii
). Due to the
decomposition of
Y
it requires to show that
\mathcal{R}(B)\subset J_{X}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{N}(A)^{\perp}\right)=J_{X}^{-1}\left(\overline{\mathcal{R}(A^{\prime})}\right)
.
This is immediate from the definition of
B
in (
19
).

It remains to prove the last equivalence.
Let us first suppose that

\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y=0
. By using the decomposition of
Y
this
means that
y\in J_{Y}^{-1}\left(\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}^{\perp}\right)
, which is
equivalent to
y\in J_{Y}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{N}(A^{\prime})\right)
, and
henceforth
J_{Y}y\in\mathcal{N}(A^{\prime})
. It follows
that
A^{\prime}J_{Y}y=0
, which yields
By=0
.

To prove the converse, let
y
be such that
ABy=0
. This means
that
z:=J_{X}^{-1}A^{\prime}J_{Y}y\in\mathcal{N}(A)
. By definition we have
that

\langle{z},{A^{\prime}J_{Y}y}\rangle=\left\|z\right\|_{X}^{2}\left\|A^{\prime}J_{Y}y\right\|_{X^{\prime}}^{2}=\langle{Az},{J_{Y}y}\rangle=0.

Therefore, it must hold true that
A^{\prime}J_{Y}y=0
, i.e.,
J_{Y}y\in\mathcal{N}(A^{\prime})=\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}^{\perp}
. We see
that
y\in J_{Y}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{N}(A^{\prime})\right)=J_{Y}^{-1}\left(\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}^{\perp}\right)
, and hence in the decomposition
of
Y
its projection
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y
equals zero. The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
X_{0}:=\mu B
, and let

R_{0}:=\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}-\mu AB\;\;\text{ and
accordingly}\;\;S_{0}:=I_{X}-\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}-\mu BA.

Suppose that the projection
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}
is linear.
Then
R_{k+1}:=R_{0}R_{k}
, and correspondingly
S_{k+1}=S_{0}S_{k}
.

Proof.

Applying the operator
A
to both sides in (
20
) yields the identity

(21)

AX_{k+1}y=AX_{k}y+\mu ABR_{k}y,

which implies that

(22)

R_{k+1}=\left(\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}-\mu AB\right)R_{k}=R_{0}R_{k},

since
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}R_{k}=R_{k}
.
A similar reasoning applies when
using
y:=Ax
in (
20
). By doing so, and using the
identity
R_{k}A=AS_{k}
we see that

AS_{k+1}x=R_{k+1}Ax=R_{k}^{2}Ax=R_{k}AS_{k}x=AS_{k}^{2}x.

This yields
AS_{k+1}=AS_{k}^{2}
. Since under the made assumptions both
S_{k}
and
S_{k+1}
map into the conal part
J_{X}(\mathcal{N}(A)^{\perp})
, on which the mapping
A
is injective, we deduce that
S_{k+1}=S_{k}^{2}
.
This proves both assertions, and this completes
the proof.

∎

Remark 5
.

By comparing the alterations of the
R_{k+1}
with respect
to
R_{k}
, we have a constant factor, here, whereas the Schultz-Newton scheme from Subsection
2.1

exhibits a power type
behavior. Thus, under Assumption
2
, the latter shows
higher order decay.

We turn to the main result in this section.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that the projection
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}
is linear.
Under Assumption
2
for the initial approximations
X_{0}
, the sequences
X_{k},\ k=0,1,2,\dots
from (
17
) and (
20
) above constitute regularizations in the sense of Definition
2
.

Proof.

We first observe that under Assumption
2
both
sequences
S_{k}y
and
R_{k}y
for
k=0,1,2,\dots
tend to zero.
Indeed, we have that
\left\|S_{0}\right\|_{X\to X}\leq q<1
. By iterating along the lines of item (
iii
) in Lemma
2
, we find for
x
with
\left\|x\right\|_{X}\leq 1
that we have convergence

\left\|S_{k}x\right\|_{X\to X}=\left\|S_{0}^{2^{k}}x\right\|_{X\to X}\leq q^{2^{k}}\to 0.

For Landweber iteration the contraction is linear, i.e.,
\left\|S_{k}x\right\|_{X\to X}\leq q^{k}
.
The convergence proof for the sequences
R_{k}y,\ k=0,1,2,\dots
is similar, and hence omitted.

This means that we have for
y\in\mathcal{R}(A)
, precisely with
y=Ax
, that

X_{k}y=X_{k}Ax\longrightarrow\left(I_{X}-\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}\right)x=A^{\dagger}Ax=A^{\dagger}y=x_{\ast}(y).

To continue we highlight that the schemes under consideration
obey
X_{k}y=X_{k}\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y,\ y\in Y
.
Then, for
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
and hence with
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y\in\mathcal{R}(A)
, the
above convergence yields, due to item (
iii
) of
Proposition
7
as

X_{k}y=X_{k}\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y\longrightarrow x_{\ast}(\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y)=x_{\ast}(y),

which completes the proof of (
11
). The proof
of (
12
) is immediate from the convergence of the
sequences
R_{k}y,\ k=0,1,2,\dots
to zero.
∎

We mention that other schemes are known to exhibit the regularizing
properties. A general guideline for the construction of such schemes
is given in
[
2
, Thm. 3.13]
.

3.
Parametric regularization: The Tikhonov scheme

In contrast to approximations of the metric generalized inverse
A^{\dagger}:\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq Y\to X
by means of sequences of homogeneous linear operators
X_{k}:Y\to X
presented before,
we consider in this section Tikhonov regularization in Banach spaces as an example of a parametric regularization scheme.
To this end we adopt the notion of regularization from
Definition
2
, accordingly.

Definition 3

(parametric regularization)
.

Given a bounded linear operator
A\colon X\to Y
, we call a
parametric family
x_{\alpha}\colon Y\to X,\ \alpha>0
, regularization, if for
\alpha\to 0

(23)

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}(y)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow A^{\dagger}y,\quad y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger}),

and

(24)

\displaystyle Ax_{\alpha}(y)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y,\quad y\in Y.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that
A\colon X\to Y
acts between Banach spaces that obey Assumption
1
. Let
x_{\alpha}(y)
be a parametric regularization.

Either
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
, and there is a unique minimum norm solution
x_{*}(y)=A^{\dagger}y
, then we have that

(25)

x_{\alpha}(y)\longrightarrow x_{*}(y)\quad\mbox{as}\quad\alpha\to 0,

or
y\not\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
then

(26)

\left\|x_{\alpha}(y)\right\|_{X}\to\infty\quad\text{as}\quad\alpha\to 0.

Theorem
3
represents the counterpart to Theorem
1
, where again the dichotomy phenomenon occurs. The proof of Theorem
3
is analog to the proof of Theorem
1
and was therefore omitted.

In the following we will establish that the Tikhonov scheme, with details given below, constitutes a parametric regularization.
There is vast literature on variational regularization for operators
acting between Banach spaces, and we mention the basic study
[
7
]
and the monographs
[
16
]
as well as
[
14
]
. However, the focus of these works is on the stable approximate solution of equation (
1
) (and extensions to nonlinear forward operators) with
attainable data
, where either the exact right hand side
y\in\mathcal{R}(A)
to Equation (
1
) or noisy data
y^{\delta}
of
y\in\mathcal{R}(A)
with
\|y^{\delta}-y\|_{Y}\leq\delta
are given. Our goal, however, is to recover
the metric generalized inverse
A^{\dagger}
in the sense that elements
x_{*}(y)=A^{\dagger}y
are to be approximated by convergent sequences of Tikhonov-regularized solutions for all elements
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})=\mathcal{R}(A)+C_{Y}(A)
.
This includes the case of
non-attainable
data when
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
, but
y\notin\mathcal{R}(A)
.
In this case,
y
does not play the role of a right-hand side to Equation (
1
), because there is a distance

{\rm dist}(y,\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)})=\|y-Ax_{*}(y)\|_{Y}=\|y-\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y\|_{Y}>0,

in contrast to
y=\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y\in\mathcal{R}(A)
, where
{\rm dist}(y,\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)})=0
.

The present scheme will be based on the specific Tikhonov functional

(27)

T_{\alpha}(x,y):=\|Ax-y\|_{Y}^{p}+\alpha\|x\|_{X}^{q},\quad\alpha>0,

which is similarly discussed in
[
16
, Chapter 5]
. Here, we consider exponents
p,q\geq 1
.
Moreover, we denote by
x_{\alpha}(y)\in X
the minimizers of the functional (
27
) over all
x\in X
,
for fixed
regularization parameter

\alpha>0
.
Under
Assumption
1
, this functional is strictly convex for
all
\alpha>0
, because the misfit term
\|Ax-y\|_{Y}^{p}
is convex due to the linearity of
A
and the part
\alpha\,\|x\|_{X}^{q}
is strictly convex for all
\alpha>0
due to the strict convexity of the Banach space
X
.

For obtaining assertions on norm-convergence for minimizers
x_{\alpha}(y)
of the Tikhonov functional (
27
),
which we call
regularized solutions
, we will need in addition to reflexivity and strict convexity of the Banach space
X
that it is a
Radon-Riesz space
, or in other words,
X
possesses the
property H
(see also item (iii) of Proposition
6
in Appendix
A
). This means that
weak convergence
x_{n}\rightharpoonup x
in
X
and convergence of the norms
\left\|x_{n}\right\|_{X}\to\left\|x\right\|_{X}
yield convergence in norm
\|x_{n}-x\|_{X}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Remark 6
.

We note that
uniform convexity
as
imposed on the Banach space
X
implies reflexivity and strict convexity as well as the property H for that space. Reflexivity occurs as a result of the Milman-Pettis Theorem, see
[
21
, Chapt. 5.2]
. The fact that every uniformly convex spaces has the Radon-Riesz property is due to M. I. Kadets.
Thus, the results in this section hold true for uniformly convex spaces.

Proposition 2
.

Under Assumption
1
there is a uniquely determined minimizer
x_{\alpha}(y)\in X
of the Tikhonov functional (
27
) for all
y\in Y
and
\alpha>0
. If moreover
X
is assumed to be a Radon-Riesz space, then
the minimizer
x_{\alpha}(y)
(regularized solution) is stable with respect to perturbations in
y\in Y
. Precisely, for any
sequence
(y_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset Y
, the condition

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|y_{n}-y\|_{Y}
implies that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|x_{\alpha}(y_{n})-x_{\alpha}(y)\|_{X}=0
.

Proof.

As is well-known, continuous linear operators between Banach spaces
are also weak-to-weak continuous. Moreover, for the reflexive Banach
space
X
, the penalty functional
x\to\|x\|_{X}^{q}
is for
p\geq 1
a
proper convex, lower semi-continuous and stabilizing functional in the
sense of
[
16
, Ass. 3.22]
. The stabilizing property of the
penalty means that sub-level sets
\{x\in X:\left\|x\right\|_{X}\leq c\}
are weakly compact in
X
for all
c\geq 0
. Then Propositions 4.1 and 4.2 from
[
16
]
apply for
F:=A
and yield the assertions of the proposition when taking into account that the uniqueness of regularized solutions is a consequence of the strict convexity of the Tikhonov functional.
The transfer from weak to norm convergence, when applying
[
16
, Prop. 4.2]
, is based on the Radon-Riesz property of the Banach space
X
by reminding us that the occurring penalty term in the Tikhonov functional (
27
) is a power of the norm in
X
.
∎

It was shown for sequential regularization in Section
2
that such schemes suppress the conal part, see Proposition
1
. Similar is valid for parametric regularization. Actually, an even stronger assertion holds true for Tikhonov regularization.

Proposition 3
.

Given
y\in Y
let
x_{\alpha}(y)
be the minimizer of the functional (
27
).
If
y\in C_{Y}(A)
then we have
x_{\alpha}(y)=0
, regardless of
\alpha>0
.

Proof.

It was mentioned in Remark
2
, for
y\in C_{Y}(A)
with
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y=0
, that we have
x_{\ast}(y)=0
.
Using this information we can bound the functional (
27
) as

\displaystyle\left\|Ax_{\alpha}(y)-y\right\|_{Y}^{p}+\alpha\left\|x_{\alpha}(y)\right\|_{X}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|Ax_{\ast}(y)-y\right\|_{Y}^{p}+\alpha\left\|x_{\ast}(y)\right\|_{X}^{q}

\displaystyle=\left\|\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y-y\right\|_{Y}^{p}=\left({\rm dist}(y,\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)})\right)^{p}.

Hence
Ax_{\alpha}(y)
is the unique element in
Y
that approximates
y\in C_{Y}(A)
in the best way over all elements from the range of
A
, which implies the equation
Ax_{\alpha}(y)=\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y=0
. This yields
x_{\alpha}(y)=x_{\ast}(y)=0
for all
\alpha>0

and all
y\in C_{Y}(A)
. Now the proof is complete.
∎

It will be shown in the due course of Theorem
4
that this phenomenon, mentioned above in
Proposition
3
extends
from
y\in C_{Y}(A)
to
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
in an asymptotic manner. Instead of
x_{\alpha}(y)=x_{\ast}(y)
for
y\in C_{Y}(A)
, we have there that
x_{\alpha}(y)\to x_{\ast}(y)
as
\alpha\to 0
for all
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
, not only for
y\in\mathcal{R}(A)
.
This result, as part of Theorem
4
, is novel within the context of the relevant literature, and it adds a new aspect to the properties of Tikhonov regularization.

The following result, the proof of which is given in Appendix
B
,
constitutes that, under additional geometric assumptions, the Tikhonov scheme represents a parametric regularization in the sense of Definition
3
.

Theorem 4
.

For the bounded linear operator
A\colon X\to Y
, let
X
and
Y
be
Banach spaces satisfying Assumption
1
. Moreover,
X
and
Y
are assumed to be a Radon-Riesz spaces. Consider the uniquely determined minimizer
x_{\alpha}(y)\in X
of the Tikhonov functional (
27
) for
fixed
y\in Y
and regularization parameters
\alpha>0.
The following holds true.

(i)

For
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger}),
and hence when there is a unique minimum norm solution
x_{*}(y)=A^{\dagger}y
, we have norm-convergence in
X
of the form

(28)

x_{\alpha}(y)\longrightarrow x_{*}(y)\quad\mbox{as}\quad\alpha\to 0.

(ii)

For all
y\in Y
we have the convergence

(29)

Ax_{\alpha}(y)\longrightarrow\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y\quad\mbox{as}\quad\alpha\to 0.

Thus, the family
x_{\alpha}(y),\ \alpha>0
, constitutes a parametric regularization in the sense of Definition
3
.

Remark 7
.

For attainable data
y
, with
{\rm dist}(y,\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)})=0
, an extension of the result (
28
) to the case of noisy data
y^{\delta}=Ax_{*}(y)+\xi\in Y
with
\|\xi\|_{Y}\leq\delta

and noise level
\delta>0
can be obtained for the related Tikhonov functional

(30)

T^{\delta}_{\alpha}(x,y^{\delta}):=\|Ax-y^{\delta}\|_{Y}^{p}+\alpha\|x\|_{X}^{q},\quad\alpha>0,

with associated minimizers
x_{\alpha}^{\delta}(y):=x_{\alpha}(y^{\delta})
, under Assumption
1
and for Radon-Riesz spaces
X
.
For the a priori choice of the regularization parameter
\alpha=\alpha(\delta)
, with

\alpha(\delta)\to 0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{\delta^{p}}{\alpha(\delta)}\to 0,\;\;\mbox{as}\quad\delta\to 0,

it is shown that

x^{\delta}_{\alpha(\delta)}(y)\to x_{*}(y).

This can even be extended to appropriate nonlinear forward operators
F:\mathcal{D}(F)\subset X\to Y
. We refer in this context for details to
[
7
, Theorem 3.5]
,

[
14
, Theorem 3.26]
and
[
16
, Corollary 4.6]
.

For non-attainable data
y=y_{1}+y_{2}\in Y
with
y_{1}\in\mathcal{R}(A)
,
y_{2}\in C_{Y}(A)
and
{\rm dist}(y,\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)})>0
, convergence results under noise are only known for Hilbert spaces
X
and
Y
. The following is shown in
[
1
, Theorem 3.5]
(again also with possible extensions to appropriate nonlinear forward operators
F
).
For
p=q=2
in (
30
), we have

(31)

x^{\delta}_{\alpha(\delta)}(y)\to x_{*}(y)\quad\mbox{for}\quad\alpha(\delta)\to 0\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\frac{\delta}{\alpha(\delta)}\to 0\;\;\mbox{as}\;\;\delta\to 0.

4.
The case of Hilbert spaces

It is worthwhile to highlight the approximation of the metric generalized inverse in case that the operator
A\colon X\to Y
acts between Hilbert spaces.
This
includes an inspection of the specific behaviour of the Tikhonov regularization, of Landweber iteration and of the Schulz-Newton method in Hilbert spaces.

In this case we know that the duality mappings
J_{X}
and
J_{Y}
, see Appendix
A
for the definition, are the identities in
X
and
Y
, respectively, by identifying the dual space
X^{\prime}
with
X
, and similarly
Y^{\prime}
with
Y
.
By notifying that we have that
\mathcal{N}(A)^{\perp}=\overline{\mathcal{R}(A^{\ast})}
, and similarly
\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}^{\perp}=\mathcal{N}(A^{\ast})
, for the adjoint operator
A^{\ast}\colon Y\to X
to
A
.
The decompositions (direct sums) are thus

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(A)+\overline{\mathcal{R}(A^{\ast})}

and

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}+\mathcal{N}(A^{\ast})

These are even orthogonal decompositions into two closed linear subspaces. Also, the projections
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}
as well as
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}
are orthogonal projections.

The restricted injective inverse mapping
A^{-1}:\mathcal{R}(A)\to\overline{\mathcal{R}(A^{\ast})}
is also linear. Consequently, the Moore-Penrose generalized inverse
A^{\dagger}:\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})=\mathcal{R}(A)+\mathcal{N}(A^{*})\to X
is a linear operator with range
\mathcal{R}(A^{\dagger})=\overline{\mathcal{R}(A^{\ast})}\perp\mathcal{N}(A).

In this case, the metric generalized inverse coincides with the Moore-Penrose inverse
A^{\dagger}\colon Y\to X
with
\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})=\mathcal{R}(A)+\mathcal{N}(A^{\ast})
. For
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
there is a uniquely determined minimum norm solution
x_{*}(y)\perp\mathcal{N}(A)
. The important fact is that for all
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
, with
y=y_{1}+y_{2}
, and
y_{1}\in\mathcal{R}(A)
and
y_{2}\in\mathcal{N}(A^{\ast})
, we have that
A^{*}y=A^{\ast}y_{1}
. Thus, regularization schemes, which are based on using
A^{\ast}y
, automatically suppress the part in
\mathcal{N}(A^{\ast})
.

Let us specify this for Landweber iteration as studied
in Subsection
2.2
. Notice that here the governing map
B\colon Y\to X
equals
A^{\ast}
, and hence in Hilbert spaces this yields reconstructions, linear in the data
y
. Using initially
X_{0}y:=\mu By
, we are in the situation from above. Item (
i
) in Lemma
3
highlights that again we see
By\perp\mathcal{N}(A)
. This led to Theorem
2
, which asserts that Landweber iteration constitutes a sequential regularization in the sense of Definition
2
when started with the mapping
B
, provided that the parameter
\mu>0
is small enough.

Similar reasoning applies to Tikhonov regularization (see, e.g.,
[
6
, Chapt. 3]
).
Hilbert spaces are of Radon-Riesz type, satisfy Assumption
1
, and the uniquely determined minimizer
x_{\alpha}(y)\in X
of the Tikhonov functional

(32)

T_{\alpha}(x,y):=\|Ax-y\|_{Y}^{2}+\alpha\|x\|_{X}^{2},\quad\alpha>0,

is linear and it is explicitly given as

(33)

x_{\alpha}(y)=(A^{*}A+\alpha I)^{-1}A^{*}y,\quad y\in Y.

Taking into account that
A^{*}y=0
for
y\in\mathcal{N}(A^{*})
,
one easily finds from (
33
) the limit statements

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}x_{\alpha}(y)=x_{*}(y)=A^{\dagger}y,\quad y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})

and

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}Ax_{\alpha}(y)=\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y,\quad y\in Y,

which coincide with the conditions (
23
) and (
24
) in Definition
3
.

A proof of the convergence result (
31
) with noisy data in both the attainable data and the non-attainable data case is rather simple for linear operators
A
in the Hilbert space setting, because of having the explicit representation

x^{\delta}_{\alpha}(y)=(A^{*}A+\alpha I)^{-1}A^{*}y^{\delta},

in combination with the fact that using
A^{*}y^{\delta}
automatically suppresses the part of
y^{\delta}
in
\mathcal{N}(A^{*})
.

Overall, the above reasoning applies to general
spectral regularization
in Hilbert space. We will not dwell into this. However, the regularizing properties, as given in Theorems
1
and Theorem
4
, are well known there, and we mention the monograph
[
18
, Thm. 5.2]
.

We conclude this section with a brief discussion, highlighting the fundamental differences between Landweber iteration and the Schulz-Newton method. Landweber iteration is known to be a gradient descent algorithm, i.e., mimimizing the quadratic functional

x\longrightarrow\frac{\left\|Ax-y\right\|_{Y}^{2}}{2},\quad x\in X.

In this case the gradient evaluates to
-A^{\ast}y
, which in turn yields Landweber iteration in the form of (
18
), after introducing an acceleration factor
\mu>0
.
The Schulz-Newton iteration is also a gradient descent algorithm, but based on sequentially minimizing the functional

x\longrightarrow\frac{\left\|\left(I_{X}-X_{k}A\right)x\right\|_{X}^{2}}{2},\quad x\in X.

Notice that we have to constrain minimization to elements
x\in\mathcal{N}(A)^{\perp}
. This corresponds to the conal part in the decomposition of
X
, which however simplifies in the case that the operator
A
is injective.
It is immediately seen that this results in (
17
). The latter scheme is known to be quadratically convergent, provided that the initial guess
X_{0}
fulfills the smallness Assumption
2
.

Appendix A
Fundamentals

In this appendix we collect several facts which are underlying the
considerations in the main body.

Duality mappings

On the Banach space
X
we let
J_{X}\colon X\to X^{\prime}
denote the
map

J_{X}(x):=\left\{x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime},\ \langle{x^{\prime}},{x}\rangle=\left\|x\right\|_{X}^{2}=\left\|x^{\prime}\right\|_{X^{\prime}}^{2}\right\}.

Similar applies to the Banach space
Y
.

Remark 8
.

There is parametric family of duality maps, often denoted
by
J_{p}^{X},p\geq 1
. Here we confine to
p=2
to ensure the

homogeneity
of the duality map, and hence let
J_{X}:=J_{2}^{X}
.
For a comprehensive study on duality maps we refer to
[
3
]
. It is immediate from the definition that the duality map
J_{X}
is the identity when the space
X
is a Hilbert space.

The following is known.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose that the Banach space
X
is strictly convex and reflexive.
The following holds true.

(i)

The duality map is
single-valued, continuous, and homogeneous

J_{X}(\lambda x)=\lambda J_{X}(x),\ x\in X
, and for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

(ii)

We have that
J_{X^{\prime}}(J_{X}(x))=x
, and
also
J_{X}(J_{X^{\prime}}(x^{\prime}))=x^{\prime}
.

(iii)

The inverse
J_{X}^{-1}(x^{\prime})
is single-valued and continuous for
each
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
.

Decomposition of the Banach spaces

Given a closed subspace
L\subset X
with annihilator
L^{\perp}\subset X^{\prime}
, we let
J_{X}^{-1}(L^{\perp})=\left\{x\in X,\ J_{X}(x)\cap L^{\perp}\not=\emptyset\right\}
. Since the duality mapping is homogeneous,
this set constitutes a cone in
X
. It is a closed cone,
because
L^{\perp}
is closed, and the mapping
J_{X}^{-1}
is continuous.
We state the following results, see e.g.
[
20
]
.

Proposition 5
.

Under Assumption
1
the following holds true.
Given the linear operator
A\colon X\to Y
, we have the decompositions

(34)

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(A)+J_{X}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{N}(A)^{\perp}\right)

and

(35)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}+J_{Y}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{R}(A)^{\perp}\right).

The mapping
A\colon J_{X}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{N}(A)^{\perp}\right)\to\mathcal{R}(A)
is injective.

In the introductory discussion we have used the abbreviations

C_{X}(A):=J_{X}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{N}(A)^{\perp}\right),\quad\text{and}~C_{Y}(A):=J_{Y}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{R}(A)^{\perp}\right).

Properties of the metric generalized inverse

Here we collect results from
[
19
, Cor. 3.2 & Thm. 4.1]
.

Proposition 6
.

Under Assumption
1
the following holds true.

(i)

The
metric generalized inverse
A^{\dagger}
exists.

(ii)

Its domain
\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
is given as

(36)

\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})=\mathcal{R}(A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot}}{{+}}J_{Y}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{R}(A)^{\perp}\right).

In particular,

y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
if and only if
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y\in\mathcal{R}(A)
.

(iii)

If the space
X
has the property H (Radon-Riesz property), i.e.,

x_{n}\rightharpoonup x
and
\left\|x_{n}\right\|_{X}\to\left\|x\right\|_{X}
,
yields
x_{n}\to X
in norm, then the metric generalized inverse
is continuous if and only if the range
\mathcal{R}(A)\subset Y

is closed. In this case
\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})=Y
.
If the range of
A
is not closed, then the domain
\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
is dense in
Y
.

The last assertion could not be found in the literature, and we sketch a proof of
\overline{\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})}=Y
, here:
Consider an arbitrary
element
y=y_{1}+y_{2}\in Y
, for which
y_{1}\in\overline{R(A)}
and
y_{2}\in C_{Y}(A)
are uniquely determined. Then there is a sequence
z_{n}\in\mathcal{R}(A)
converging in norm to
y_{1}
as
n\to\infty
. Moreover, the sums
z_{n}+y_{2}
belong to the domain of
A^{\dagger}
, because
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}(z_{n}+y_{2})=z_{n}\in\mathcal{R}(A)
. Finally we have
\|z_{n}+y_{2}-y\|_{Y}=\left\|z_{n}-y_{1}\right\|_{Y}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Minimum norm solutions, best approximate solutions

Let
y\in Y
be any element. An element
x_{0}\in X
is called
best
approximate solution
to
Ax=y
, if

\left\|Ax_{0}-y\right\|_{Y}=\inf\left\{\left\|Ax-y\right\|_{Y},\ x\in X\right\}.

Best approximate solutions need not exist. If they exist, then such a solution
is called
minimum norm solution
whenever it has mimimal norm among all
best approximate solutions.
The following results can be seen from the proof of
[
19
, Thm. 3.2]
, and from the fact
that
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y\notin\mathcal{R}(A)
for all
y\notin\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
.

Proposition 7
.

Under Assumption
1
the following is known for the
elements
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
.

(i)

x_{\ast}(y):=A^{\dagger}y
is a best approximate
solution to the equation
Ax=y
.

(ii)

x_{\ast}(y):=A^{\dagger}y
is the uniquely determined minimum norm solution to the equation
Ax=\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y
.

(iii)

x_{\ast}(y):=A^{\dagger}y

is the only solution to
Ax=\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y
with
x\in J_{X}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{N}(A)^{\perp}\right)
, and hence it satisfies
\pi_{\mathcal{N}(A)}x_{\ast}(y)=0
.
In particular we have that
x_{\ast}(y)=x_{\ast}(\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y)
for
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
, since
\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}
is a projection.

For
y\notin\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
, however, best approximate solutions and minimum norm solutions do not exist.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
4

As basic regularizing property we have, for all
x\in X
and all
y\in Y
, that
T_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha}(y),y)\leq T_{\alpha}(x,y)
, which means

(37)

\|Ax_{\alpha}(y)-y\|_{Y}^{p}+\alpha\|x_{\alpha}(y)\|^{q}_{X}\leq\|Ax-y\|_{Y}^{p}+\alpha\,\|x\|_{X}^{q}.

By omitting the second term on the left-hand side and applying the superior limit for
\alpha\to 0
, we derive from
(
37
), for all
x\in X
and all
y\in Y
, the inequality

(38)

\limsup_{\alpha\to 0}\|Ax_{\alpha}(y)-y\|_{Y}\leq\|Ax-y\|_{Y},

and consequently, for all
y\in Y
, the estimate

(39)

\limsup_{\alpha\to 0}\|Ax_{\alpha}(y)-y\|_{Y}\leq\inf_{x\in X}\|Ax-y\|_{Y}=\|\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y-y\|_{Y}={\rm dist}(y,\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}).

Because we have, for all
\alpha>0
, that
Ax_{\alpha}(y)\in\mathcal{R}(A)\subset\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}
, this gives

\;\,{\rm dist}(y,\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)})\leq\liminf_{\alpha\to 0}\|Ax_{\alpha}(y)-y\|_{Y}\;,

and hence with (
39
) that the limit
\;\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\|Ax_{\alpha}(y)-y\|_{Y}\;
exists for all
y\in Y

and obeys the equalities

(40)

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\|Ax_{\alpha}(y)-y\|_{Y}={\rm dist}(y,\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)})=\|\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y-y\|_{Y}\qquad\forall y\in Y\,.

Evidently, for every
y\in Y
,
Ax_{\alpha}(y)
is bounded in norm for all regularization parameters
\alpha>0
, and thus there exist weak limits
z\in Y
for sequences

Ax_{\alpha_{n}}(y)\rightharpoonup z
. Since the set
\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}
is convex and closed, hence weakly closed in
Y
,
we have
z\in\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}
.
Furthermore
\|z-y\|_{Y}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\|Ax_{\alpha_{n}}(y)-y\|_{Y}={\rm dist}(y,\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)})
.
Since
z\in\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}
, we see that
z=\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y
.

Summarizing, we have weak convergence
Ax_{\alpha_{n}}(y)-y\rightharpoonup\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y-y
, and by (
40
) we also have convergence of the norms

\|Ax_{\alpha_{n}}(y)-y\|_{Y}\to\|\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y-y\|_{Y}
as
n\to\infty
. By the Radon-Riesz property this gives
\|Ax_{\alpha}(y)-\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y\|_{Y}\to 0
as
\alpha\to 0
for all
y\in Y
. The arguments above show the validity of (
29
).

To establish (
28
), we restrict our considerations to the case
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
. There we have a uniquely determined minimum norm solution
x_{*}(y)
to any fixed
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
with
Ax_{*}(y)=\pi_{\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}}y
. Also, taking into account (
40
) we find that

\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\|Ax_{\alpha}(y)-y\|_{Y}=\|Ax_{*}(y)-y\|_{Y}={\rm dist}(y,\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}).

For
x:=x_{*}(y)
, (
37
) attains the form

(41)

\|Ax_{\alpha}(y)-y\|_{Y}^{p}+\alpha\|x_{\alpha}(y)\|^{q}_{X}\leq\|Ax_{*}(y)-y\|_{Y}^{p}+\alpha\,\|x_{*}(y)\|_{X}^{q},

The assertion (
28
)
can be derived from (
41
) by rewriting this as

\alpha\|x_{\alpha}(y)\|^{q}_{X}\leq\left[\|Ax_{*}(y)-y\|_{Y}^{p}-\|Ax_{\alpha}(y)-y\|_{Y}^{p}\right]+\alpha\,\|x_{*}(y)\|_{X}^{q}.

For all
\alpha>0
, the difference in the square brackets is non-positive. This is
a consequence of

\|Ax_{*}(y)-y\|_{Y}={\rm dist}(y,\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)})\leq\|Ax_{\alpha}(y)-y\|_{Y},

which is due to
Ax_{\alpha}(y)\in\mathcal{R}(A)\subset\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}
.
Consequently, we obtain that

(42)

\|x_{\alpha}(y)\|_{X}\leq\|x_{*}(y)\|_{X}\quad\mbox{for all}\;\;\alpha>0,

which in turn yields that

\limsup_{\alpha\to 0}\|x_{\alpha}(y)\|_{X}\leq\|x_{*}(y)\|_{X}
. Since
X
is reflexive, and because
A
is weak-to-weak continuous, for any sequence
\alpha_{n}\to 0
, there is some element
z\in X
such that weak limits in
X
and
Y
, respectively, apply as

x_{\alpha_{n}}(y)\rightharpoonup z\quad\mbox{and}\quad Ax_{\alpha_{n}}(y)\rightharpoonup Az\quad\mbox{as}\quad n\to\infty.

Then the weak lower semi-continuity of the norm provides us with

\|z\|_{X}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|x_{\alpha_{n}}(y)\|_{X}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|x_{\alpha_{n}}(y)\|_{X}\leq\|x_{*}(y)\|_{X}.

In view of (
40
) we see that

\|Az-y\|_{Y}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|Ax_{\alpha_{n}}(y)-y\|_{Y}={\rm dist}(y,\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}).

Since
Az\in\mathcal{R}(A)
, we even have
\|Az-y\|_{Y}={\rm dist}(y,\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)})
. Thus, every such limit element

z\in X
is a best approximate solution, obeying
\|Az-y\|_{Y}=\min_{x\in X}\|Ax-y\|_{Y}
and

\|z\|_{X}\leq\|x_{*}(y)\|_{X}
. This has the consequence that the weak limit elements
z
, for any sequences of
\alpha
tending to zero, coincide with the uniquely determined minimum norm solution
x_{*}(y)
.
We aim at using that
X
is a Radon-Riesz space.
Therefore, to complete the proof of (
28
), we still have to show that
\lim_{\alpha_{n}\to 0}\|x_{\alpha_{n}}(y)\|_{X}\to\|x_{*}(y)\|_{X}
as
n\to\infty
. However, the weak convergence
x_{\alpha_{n}}(y)\rightharpoonup x_{*}(y)
as
n\to\infty
implies with (
42
) that

\|x_{*}(y)\|_{X}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|x_{\alpha_{n}}(y)\|_{X}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|x_{\alpha_{n}}(y)\|_{X}\leq\|x_{*}(y)\|_{X}

and hence
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|x_{\alpha_{n}}(y)\|_{X}=\|x_{*}(y)\|_{X}
, which completes the proof.
