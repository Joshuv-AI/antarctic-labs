Left arm twitching caused by failure of pacemaker lead insulation
Authors: Shunsuke Kido, Junya Aoki, Satoshi Suda
Published: 2026

An 89-year-old woman with dementia was referred to our neurology clinic after presenting to her family physician with progressively worsening involuntary movements over 1 month. She had undergone permanent pacemaker implantation for complete atrioventricular block 14 years earlier, with generator replacement 3 years before presentation. Routine device follow-up had not been performed after she moved into a long-term care facility. On examination, we observed intermittent left arm twitching at approximately 1 Hz (Appendix 1, Video 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260045/tab-related-content). We considered myoclonus, fasciculation, and tremor, but these were inconsistent with the regular, coarse, low-frequency movements. The movements were simultaneous with pacing on monitor electrocardiography, suggesting pacemaker involvement. Chest radiography revealed an abnormal configuration of the atrial pacing lead (Figure 1). We diagnosed pacemaker lead malfunction caused by insulation failure. Reprogramming the pacemaker mode from dual-chamber (DDD) to ventricular (VVI) pacing immediately abolished the involuntary movements by eliminating atrial pacing output.

(A) Chest radiograph of an 89-year-old woman showing an abnormal configuration of the atrial pacing lead near the sternoclavicular region (B, black arrow), consistent with suspected insulation damage.

Pacemaker lead failure accumulates over time, with long-term studies reporting cumulative rates of complications of 28% to 40% by 8 years.1 Lead failure is broadly categorized into 2 types: conductor fracture and insulation failure. Insulation failure results in electrical current escaping from the lead, causing unintended depolarization of adjacent neural or muscular structures. Involuntary movements related to pacemaker malfunction are classically described in twiddler’s syndrome and Reel syndrome. In these syndromes, device rotation from patient manipulation or “twiddling” displaces the lead, and its electrode stimulates the brachial plexus.2 Diaphragmatic pulsations have also been reported when displaced ventricular leads stimulate the phrenic nerve or diaphragm.3

Although insulation failure often warrants lead revision, invasive intervention may pose substantial risk in frail patients or those of advanced age. Given this patient’s age, comorbidities, and lack of dependence on atrial pacing, we selected a conservative management approach. Prompt recognition of this complication is essential, and simple device reprogramming may provide rapid symptom resolution without surgical intervention, particularly in patients with limited access to routine device surveillance.
