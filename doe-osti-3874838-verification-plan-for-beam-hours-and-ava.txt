1 Introduction
1.1 Project Background

The Los Alamos Neutron Science Center (LANSCE) is located at Technical Area 53 (TA-53) at Los
Alamos National Laboratory in Los Alamos, New Mexico. LANSCE is driven by an 800 mega electron
volt (MeV) proton accelerator that delivered first beam in 1972. The LANSCE accelerator is unique in that
it accelerates both H- (to full energy of 800 MeV) and H+ ions (up to 100 MeV currently but has accelerated
high-power H+ beam to 800 MeV in the past) and supports five separate experimental areas that operate
simultaneously with each having different timing and beam current requirements. Many of the LANSCE
accelerator front end components date back to its original commissioning in 1972, including the ion sources,
Cockcroft-Walton (CW) generators, and the Drift Tube LINAC (DTL) which accelerates the beam up to
100 MeV.

The purpose of the LANSCE Accelerator Modernization Project (LAMP) is to replace and modernize
multiple accelerator systems and components to improve beam delivery, reliability and availability. LAMP
modernization will improve system maintainability and prepare the facility for operation through 2050. The
scope of modernization currently includes the LANSCE front end (up to 100 MeV). This scope includes
modernizing the two ion sources, replacing the two CW generators with a modern Radio Frequency
Quadrupole (RFQ), and replacing the DTL, which is exhibiting severe degradation and has significantly
exceeded its expected lifespan. Modernization of accelerator systems beyond the 100 MeV beam energy
are not within the currently approved LAMP scope; however, end-to-end modeling through the entire
accelerator including the Proton Storage Ring (PSR) will need to be performed to verify the project’s
performance requirements for all five experimental areas.

LAMP will be executed as a congressional line-item project under DOE Order 413.3b [1] for the National
Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA). The design will follow the Program Requirements Document
(PRD) for LAMP [2] and other requirements that flow down from the PRD.

1.2 Document Scope

This document lays out a high-level plan for verification of requirements for beam hours and availability
of the LAMP subsystems through 100 MeV. Scheduled beam hours and availability of other LANSCE
subsystems or user facilities are not considered here.

The LAMP components are required to function while exposed to environmental stresses present at
LANSCE, including high altitude, varying humidity and temperature. Failure of beam availability due to
facility systems meant to control the environment or other systems that are not within the LAMP scope will
not be attributed to the LAMP availability though they may result in loss of beam to the sections of the
accelerator downstream from the front end (front end starts with ion sources and ends at the DTL exit).

Availability for the purposes of verification refers to the percentage of time that the proton beam is properly
delivered to accelerator elements downstream from the LAMP design. In the PRD for LAMP [2] (level 0
requirements for LAMP) the stated goals for beam hours and availability are:

Level 0 requirement: The solution must provide operational beam hours to enable NNSA data delivery
needs.
5,300 beam hours per annual run cycle with greater than 90 percent beam availability.

2 Methodology
As stated in the LAMP-PM-MOA-002 [3], availability verification methods for these requirements shall be
analysis and similarity.
Demonstration of operating beam hours and availability is not a practical requirement verification method
due to time constraints for project delivery. For instance, Chi2 analysis with a 90% confidence level was
run for the required beam hours and availability. It indicated that a demonstration test would require 10,983
operating hours with zero failures. This is more than two full years of operating the front end (from ion
sources to DTL exit) at 5,300 hours per year. One failure during testing would increase the required
operating time (to demonstrate the requirements) to 18,554 operating hours, or 3.5 years of running the
front end at 5,300 hours per year. These time durations are deemed to be beyond the allowed design time.
Chi2 analysis may be used to establish statistical confidence in failure rate or MTBF; however, it does not
by itself demonstrate availability, which depends on both reliability and maintainability. Direct
demonstration of availability would require sufficient operating experience to characterize both failures and
repair or restoration times and is deemed impractical within the project schedule. Nevertheless, failures
and associated repair or restoration times will be accumulated and assessed for use in the calculated
availability.
Analysis
Analysis use the available component reliability data, collected from the existing accelerator maintenance
data and obtained from external sources using equipment used in a similar manner to LAMP. After judging
suitability for the comparison with the LAMP design, analysis of the data will be blended to model the
availability of LAMP. This will provide a statistical basis for determining whether the objective availability
goal has been met. The data necessary to calculate beam availability will be obtained and organized as
separate subsystems spanning the project scope and combined to provide an overall availability metric.
Similarity
Similarity includes comparisons of the existing accelerator reliability and data obtained from similar from
external system components, especially if no existing data from LANSCE is available or deemed
sufficiently suitable. In many cases, equipment from the current LANSCE accelerator design will be
sufficiently equivalent to equipment in the LAMP design. The intended data sources used in the analysis
are shown below in Table 1. For components where insufficient data is available, ranges of parameters will

2.1 Data Sources

 2.1.1 Existing LANSCE Maintenance Data

Analysis of existing systems at LANSCE will assess their similarity to the proposed design. Existing
systems data will be obtained from the existing LANSCE accelerator maintenance history records from the
ion sources through the DTLs. Due to differences between the current LANSCE accelerator and the
proposed LAMP design, existing maintenance history has varying levels of applicability. Nevertheless,
many of the current systems in use will have useful parallels to LAMP. Log data will be obtained from
maintenance teams and operations over a certain maintenance period. The advantage of using this data is
its direct correlation to the operational environment and the maintenance processes in use at LANSCE. The
available data will be compared to data from other sources as available. Discussions with design and
maintenance personnel will provide a determination of the applicability of datasets and level of similarity
to the LAMP design.
The proton beam is delivered to five target areas: IPF, Prad, UCN, Lujan, and WNR. Each target area has
a specific beam pulse timing structure and beam current requirement. The detectability of beam downtime
depends on the designed measurement capability. LANSCE downtime measurement capability is typically
greater than 1 second.

 2.1.2 External Data Sources

Reliability and maintainability data will be collected from external users for known failure and repair
experiences. Data for the injector sources and radio frequency quadruple (RFQ), used at the Spallation
Neutron Source (SNS) at ORNL, are similar in design and will be used in a similar manner at LANL. Data
from SNS is likely to be very useful in estimating projected reliability and maintainability parameters used
to estimate the projected availability of these components.
Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory may be a source for data concerning Choppers and Bunchers, two
components used in the existing LANSCE design and in the LAMP proposed design. Discussions with
their personnel would have to be conducted to collect any reliability data available. The lower altitude of
SNS and Fermilab dictate the potential need for adjustment due to LANL’s lower humidity and higher
elevation. These adjustments introduce additional uncertainty.

 2.1.3 Other Data Sources including AI

Vendor information and literature reviews, as well as discussions with additional data owners will be
conducted for other LAMP design components as needed to obtain reliability and maintainability data that
can be used to estimate similarity to the LAMP design. This may result in generic failure and repair data
that may have larger uncertainty than the other methods, given that it may not reflect the exact operating
environment, maintenance practices, and intended use at LANSCE. Discretion must be used in applying
these sources to the LAMP reliability and availability analysis. Research from other organizations will be
reviewed for applicability to LANL.
AI searches for reliability data generate specific information concerning failure modes and failure
distributions but generally only offer wide ranges for mean time between failure (MTBF) and mean time to
repair (MTTR), as this data is usually site and process specific. AI searches may be useful for rough
confirmations of data obtained from other sources. The usefulness of AI searches is in reference to
additional component research that will lead to a fuller understanding of those components’ reliability. AI
obtain data will need to be verified and reviewed by subject matter experts.

2.2 Availability

If scheduled up time and unscheduled downtime are recorded during operations, accurate availability can
be calculated as:

 % Availability 𝑋 100% (1)

Beam scheduled up time, as planned by operations, is managed over the course of an annual calendar run
cycle. The plan may change due to unforeseen circumstances. Matching unscheduled maintenance to the
planned availability will be important to the calculation’s accuracy.
The sum of the downtime of the components within each subsystem allows calculation of the availability
of that subsystem. The sum of the downtime of the separate subsystems, with the caveat that the actual
downtimes do not overlap, can be used to estimate the availability of the entire LAMP system. Downtime
adjustments will be made to account for any overlapping unexpected maintenance actions.
One scenario is when an unexpected failure occurs during scheduled up time. While repairs are in process,
planners decide to simultaneously perform some preventive maintenance tasks that would normally be
scheduled for a later planned downtime. The unexpected failure is repaired, and the equipment is returned
to service, but the preventive maintenance task is not completed until some later time. This extended

maintenance time would not be considered downtime for the calculation of availability. It could be
considered a change in the plan and reduce the uptime schedule.
For these reasons, it is necessary to have a timestamp with date and time when the failures occur and when
components are returned to full service. The total downtime can be determined from the timestamps.
An equally valid way to calculate availability is as follows:

               % Availability                           100%                                             (2)

Collecting the existing LANSCE or other available data in this way will establish baseline reliability and
maintainability data for use in estimating the availability of LAMP components and for comparison with
operational data after turnover to operations.
Mean-Time-Between-Failures (MTBF) can be calculated from the elapsed runtime of the system divided
by the number of failures during that runtime, provided the runtime and number of failures are recorded.
Mean-Time-To-Repair (MTTR) is calculated as the total time spent performing corrective maintenance
divided by the number of distinct repair actions required to restore failed components or systems to service.
This also requires that the downtime be recorded for each maintenance action.
The inverse of MTBF is the failure rate (λ). The failure rates for each subsystem can be summed and the
total failure rate inverted to determine the MTBF of the entire LAMP system. MTTR can be summed over
the subsystems to obtain the MTTR for the entire LAMP system. In this way, the availability of the various
subsystems as well as the overall LAMP can be determined.

2.3 Maintenance Data

The following data should be collected, when available, to enable an accurate calculation of availability.
The first four metrics in Table 2 are the primary inputs to the availability calculation. It is recognized that
data for some failure may not be recorded, such as when a component reset immediately returns the system
to full operation and no work order is generated.
For external data sources, it is likely that only overall availability may be reported. It this case, the reported
availability data will be applied to LAMP’s scheduled operating hours to determine an estimated downtime.
This estimated downtime can then be summed with data from other sources to calculate total downtime and
availability.
The metrics shown below become particularly important when only maintenance data is available. This
data will be used to establish an operational baseline. Work order information should be used to estimate
MTTR. If necessary, maintenance personnel would be interviewed and recorded information reviewed to
develop estimates. MTBF can be calculated from accumulated runtime and the number of failures, while
MTTR is determined from recorded or estimated repair duration.
Though demonstration testing has been shown to not be practicable for determining system availability,
data collect from LAT testing could prove valuable for confirmation of the analysis and similarity result.
The LAMP at ADEF (Accelerator Development and Engineering Facility) Tunnel (LAT) will operate on
its own schedule and provide an important source of operational data for evaluating the reliability of
individual components based on observed failures. It is therefore essential that failures and associated
maintenance activities be accurately and consistently recorded. Because the LAT testbed is intended to
replicate the LAMP front-end design through the first DTL tank, the resulting reliability data will support
the refinement of component reliability estimates and improve confidence in the beam availability analysis.

2.4 Statistical Analysis

Below is a brief overview of potential uses of the collected reliability data in statistical analyses. The
techniques planned are Weibull analysis for MTBF and MTTR if operational failure and maintenance times
are available. If only ranges are known for these parameters, then a PERT distribution and Monte Carlo
simulations can be used to develop a level of confidence in meeting the availability requirement. Chi2
analysis will become useful if operating times for failures and/or maintenance repair times are collected.
The following sections provide context for understanding the planned use of these statistical methods.

 2.4.1 Weibull Analysis

Historical maintenance and failure data must first be analyzed to derive representative failure rates before
they can be used in the beam availability model. Weibull analysis [4] provides a statistically rigorous
method for estimating component reliability by accounting for censored data (components that have not yet
failed) and failure behavior that may deviate from a constant failure rate. The resulting MTBF estimates
become key inputs to the beam availability analysis.
From maintenance history alone, it is generally not known whether the observed failures occurred during
infant mortality (decreasing failure rate), the useful life phase (constant failure rate), or wear out phase
(increasing failures rate) of the components lifecycle. These three regions are illustrated by the reliability
“Bathtub” curve shown in Figure 1 - Reliability Lifetime Characteristics.

 Figure 1 - Reliability Lifetime Characteristics

Weibull analysis fits a statistical distribution from which the characteristic life (scale parameter, η), shape
parameter (β), and, where appropriate, location parameter (γ) are estimated. The Gamma function is applied
to the shape parameter to determine the true MTBF as follows.

 2.4.2 Monte Carlo Analysis

The results of the analysis of existing systems at LANSCE and the inclusion of external data sources will
be point-estimates of MTBF and MTTR for the subsystems and the overall LAMP design. It may be
possible to only establish expected ranges for these parameters. In this case a PERT distribution can be
used to model the variance and used with a Monte Carlo [5] simulation to determine the expected
availability at a 90% level of confidence.

 2.4.3 Chi2 Analysis

Chi2 analysis is useful for demonstrating reliability because it does not require assumptions about the
underlying failure-time distribution and is applicable even when zero or only a few failures have been
observed.

 2.4.4 MTTR and Log-Normal Distributions

Repair time is one of the key inputs to the beam availability analysis and must therefore be represented
using an appropriate statistical model. The lognormal distribution provides a realistic representation of
maintenance duration. When maintenance activities are consistently and accurately recorded, the resulting
data can be used to estimate the MTTR distribution for use in the availability calculation.
