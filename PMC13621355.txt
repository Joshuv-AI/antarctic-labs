Synergistically engineered Ag@NiO–TiO2 ternary nanocomposites as dual-functional photocatalysts for pollutant degradation and antibacterial applications

Iftikhar Ahmad, Muhammad Tahir

Abstract

A synergistically engineered Ag@NiO–TiO2 ternary nanocomposite was synthesized by a hydrothermal method, and its performance was evaluated by organic pollutant degradation and antibiotic removal. The photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue, (MB), crystal violet (CV), and ciprofloxacin (CIP) by the nanocomposite under UV-visible light irradiation was systematically studied for comparison. The photocatalytic removal achieved by the Ag@NiO–TiO2 nanocomposite for MB and CV was 88% and 90%, respectively, under UV-visible light irradiation after 1 h. Similarly, 57% removal of CIP from water was achieved using 20 mg L−1 of solution after 180 minutes of UV-visible light irradiation. The superior UV-visible photocatalytic capacity of the nanocomposite toward organic dyes was confirmed by its high removal efficiencies for MB and CV, and its potential for treating pharmaceutical wastewater was revealed by the moderate removal of CIP. The prepared compound was also evaluated for antimicrobial activities, and significant antimicrobial activities were achieved against bacterial and fungal strains. The results of the Ag@NiO–TiO2 ternary heterojunction nanocomposite showed that it is an effective photocatalyst for degrading organic pollutants under UV-visible light irradiation. Furthermore, the photocatalyst showed enhanced antimicrobial activity against all strains. Therefore, the synthesized Ag@NiO–TiO2 nanoparticles could have beneficial applications in environmental remediation and biological science.

Introduction

Organic pollutants, such as methylene blue and crystal violet dyes, collected from industries contain complex aromatic compounds that are non-biodegradable, environmentally toxic and carcinogenic. It is highly recommended to remove these colored compounds from sewage wastewater before it enters natural habitats due to their toxicity, bioactivation properties, complex chemical nature and cancerous proclivity. Methylene blue is applied for different purposes such as dyeing cotton, silk, wool, etc. Similarly, crystal violet is a triphenylmethane dye that is used in the textile processing industry as well as a biological stain. These dyes are associated with harmful side effects on human health, and hence, it is essential to remove these dyes from wastewater on a priority basis. Similarly, based on their worldwide sales and therapeutic versatility, fluoroquinolone antibiotics have been one of the most widely used medications in veterinary medicine since the 1960s. High quantities of ciprofloxacin (CIP), a significant member of this group, and its products have been detected in water sources in the range of ng L−1 to μg L−1 since it is widely employed to treat illnesses caused by bacterial infections. When this antibiotic medication is present in an aqueous medium, it can become more stable, resulting in harmful effects like causing cancer. Consequently, using a practical and sustainable approach to address this issue is essential. Two of the global concerns for human health are bacterial and fungal infections. The World Health Organization published a report in 2014 that in the face of microbial illness, if the excessive use of antibiotics continues at the current rate, the resulting rise in antimicrobial resistance may severely limit the effectiveness of available treatments for bacterial infections. It is assessed that human mortality rate can increase to 12 million people in the next 30 years as compared with 700 000 people due to such drug-resistant bacterial infections. The World Health Organization has identified fungal infections and antifungal resistance as growing major public health concerns that require urgent research on therapeutic agents. Therefore, it is essential to develop novel chemically altered compounds to respond to these multidrug-resistant bacterial and fungal infections.

Semiconductor-based oxidative photocatalysis technology has shown great progress and attracted attention in the degradation of organic dyes and antibiotics to deactivate bacteria and viruses. Semiconductors such as zinc oxide, titanium oxide, manganese oxide, cadmium sulphide and g-C3N4 are well known for their photocatalytic and antimicrobial activities. These photocatalysts are highly efficient, inexpensive, less toxic, easily available, environmentally friendly, and highly stable in a wide range of pH with high oxidizing photo-generated hole properties. When these materials are used as photocatalysts, they suffer from several drawbacks, such as wide band gap range, incomplete mineralization and fast recombination of electrons (e−) and holes (h+), which decreases their photocatalytic activities. Therefore, to increase the efficiency of these materials, several metal oxide nanomaterials, doped metal oxides and metal oxide nanocomposites were prepared and investigated. The TiO2 photocatalytic activity is improved by doping it with different metals such as Ni, Ag, Pd and Ni nanoparticles. More specifically, the ability of Ag and Au to produce Schottky heterostructures with TiO2 has led to their widespread use. However, compared to Au nanoparticles, Ag nanoparticles are more cost-effective and have a larger work function. Additionally, Ag nanoparticle doping increases the efficiency of electron injection into TiO2 because the Ag nanoparticles' SPR induces charge carriers. As a result, the surface plasmon resonance excitations of Ag nanoparticles in the shorter wavelength area of the visible light spectrum (about 380–450 nm) would increase the TiO2 photocatalytic activity. However, when TiO2 and p-type NiO are combined, a p–n heterojunction is created that reduces photogenerated charge carrier recombination and increases charge separation via an internal electric field. Ag nanoparticles doping further enhances the properties of systems by extending visible-light absorption through localized SPR, creating Schottky barriers that trap electrons, and gives intrinsic antibacterial activity.

The synergistic effect of Ag, NiO, and TiO2 was confirmed by a recent work on ternary Ag@NiO–TiO2 heterojunctions, which showed improved UV-visible light degradation of pollutants and pharmaceutical wastes compared with TiO2, Ag/TiO2, and NiO/TiO2. Ag@NiO–TiO2 nanomaterials are potential multifunctional materials for photodegradation and antimicrobial applications due to the incorporation of TiO2 with the p-type semiconducting material of NiO and the plasmonic/antimicrobial activity of Ag nanoparticles. Ag@NiO–TiO2 is therefore selected as a new photocatalytic-antimicrobial material due to its superior visible-light activation, reduced charge recombination, increased formation of reactive oxygen species, better degradation efficiency, and significant potential for bacterial inhibition. The results might be helpful in the development of advanced nanomaterials for environmental disinfection, dye and pharmaceutical contaminant degradation, and wastewater treatment.

The present work reports the development of Ag@NiO–TiO2 ternary nanomaterials as a dual-functional platform for both the photocatalytic removal of dye/pharmaceutical contaminants and antibacterial activities. The core objective of this work is to synthesize and characterize Ag@NiO–TiO2 nanomaterials and evaluate their efficiency for the UV-visible light-assisted removal of methylene blue, crystal violet, and ciprofloxacin, along with their antibacterial performance against selected pathogenic bacterial and fungal strains. This work offers a versatile and sustainable nanomaterial-based method for environmental disinfection and wastewater treatment.

Experimental

Materials

Titanium isopropoxide (97%), AgNO3 and nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O) were procured from SRLChem USA. Sodium borohydride (NaBH4, Fisher Scientific), ethanol (EtOH, Alfa Aesar Chemicals), methylene blue and crystal violet dyes (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and ciprofloxacin (Pharmaceutical grade, USA) were used as received with any further purification.

Synthetic procedure for TiO2, NiO–TiO2, and Ag@NiO–TiO2 nanocomposites

TiO2 nanoparticles were synthesized by dissolving 10 mL of TTIP into 40 mL of isopropanol to obtain solution A. Then, 5 mL of acetic acid (solution B) was added into 100 mL of deionized water in a separate beaker. Under constant stirring solution A was added dropwise into solution B. The solution was put for aging for 12 hours and then dried at 80 °C. TiO2 nanoparticles were obtained by calcining at 500 °C for 6 h. The collected solid yield was 2.3 g, corresponding to 88% yield.

The synthesis of ternary NiO–TiO2 heterojunction nanocomposites was carried out through hydrothermal methods. NiO–TiO2 nanocomposites were prepared by mixing 2 : 1 ratio of TiO2 (2 g) and NiO (1 g) in ultra-pure water, and then this solution was transferred to an autoclave. At 200 °C temperature, a standard calculated quantity of NiO and TiO2 dispersed in 100 mL of deionized water were subjected to 12 h reaction in the autoclave. The mixture was then filtered, washed several times with deionized water and methanol, and dried at 70 °C for 12 h to obtain the NiO–TiO2 nanocomposite. The collected amount of the NiO–TiO2 composite was 2.9 g, equal to 96% yield.

To synthesize the Ag@NiO–TiO2 photocatalyst, 1.0 g of TiO2 powder was sonicated in 60 mL of deionized water for 20–30 minutes, and then, the mixture was agitated magnetically for 30 minutes to obtain a homogeneous suspension. After dissolving the required quantities of 0.5 g of NiO and 0.03 g AgNO3 separately in deionized water, both precursor solutions were added dropwise into the TiO2 suspension under vigorous stirring. The pH was adjusted to about 9–10 using diluted NaOH and stirring was continued at 60–80 °C for two hours. Then, the solution was transferred to an autoclave and the reaction was carried out for 12 h at 180 °C. The suspension was centrifuged, repeatedly cleaned with deionized water until the Ph is neutral, washed several times with methanol, and dried overnight at 80 °C. The collected amount of the solid was 1.32 g, which was approximately 86% yield. The synthetic method is shown in .

Material characterization

The present work studied the structure, optical properties, morphology, elemental composition, and particular peaks of TiO2, NiO–TiO2, and Ag@NiO/TiO2 nanocomposites using FT-IR spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), UV-vis spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy, PL, SEM-EDX, TEM and TGA. A Jasco Fourier transform infrared (FTIR-4700) spectroscopy instrument was used to examine the presence of bonds between the composite materials. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker Advance D8 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation of λ = 0.154 nm was performed to analyze the crystalline structure of the synthesized titanium oxide nanoparticles and their composites. The UV-vis spectroscopy results of the samples were obtained using an Analytik Jena SPECORD 200 Plus UV-vis spectrophotometer. Raman measurements (λex of 785 nm, 200–2500 cm−1) were carried out using a Renishaw Ramascope. The morphological features and EDS were examined using an FESEM Hitachi SU8020. Similarly, transmission electron microscopy (Thermo Fisher Scientific (TFS) Talos system operating at 200 kV) was used to examine the shape, size and structure of the catalyst samples. The thermal stability was investigated using a TGA Q500 model.

Experimental procedure

MB and CV dyes and ciprofloxacin antibiotic in an aqueous solution were used to assess the efficiency of the catalyst to degrade the pollutants. All photodegradation studies were conducted using a 400 W halogen lamp (metal halide) with a UV cut filter coating (λ > 400 nm, with λmax = 546 nm). Using 20 mg loading of photocatalyst suspended in 100 mL of MB, CV and CIP (10 mg L−1) solution in a 150 mL glass reactor, the light source intensity was measured using a light meter at 15 cm between the lamp and the cup. It was approximately 170 W m−2. The solution was constantly stirred in the dark for fifteen minutes before irradiation to balance the absorption and desorption processes between the photocatalyst and the MB, CV, and CIP solution. To assess the degradation of the MB and CV dye and CIP, absorbances were measured at specified intervals. The aqueous solution was exposed to radiation in the control experiment using a photocatalyst. Antimicrobial activities

Antibacterial activity

The previously reported method was used to examine the susceptibility of the compound to various bacterial species. Three Gram-negative organisms were examined: Enterobacter aerogenes, Salmonella typhimurium, and Bordetella bronchiseptica. The strains were cultivated in a nutrient broth medium and incubated at 37 °C for a whole day. The turbidity was adjusted to 104 CFU mL−1 using sterilized deionized water and compared to McFarland 0.5 BaSO4 turbidity standards. After that, 100 μL of the updated inoculum was swabbed onto Petri dishes with 20 mL of nutritional agar. Sterile filter paper discs (6 mm) were injected with test samples (5 μL of 20 mg per mL DMSO; 100 μg per disc) and put on a seeded nutrient agar plate. Positive and negative controls were DMSO-impregnated discs and cefixime-USP at a dose of 20 μg per disc, respectively.

Antifungal activity

Antifungal activity was assessed using a standard procedure reported previously. SDA was used to cultivate the fungal strains Aspergillus fumigatus, Fusarium solani, and A. niger acquired from the Fungal Culture Bank of Pakistan. The spores were collected in a 0.02% Tween 20 solution, and their turbidity was corrected using the McFarland 0.5 turbidity standard prior to the sensitivity assessment. On plates with 25 mL of sterilized SDA, 100 μL of each extracted spore was swabbed. On seeded SDA plates, filter paper discs containing 5 μL of the test sample (20 mg per mL DMSO; 100 μg per disc), standard antifungal terbinafine (50 μg per disc), and DMSO were added. For 24 to 48 hours, the plates were incubated at 28 °C. A Vernier caliper was then used to measure the clear zones of inhibition surrounding the discs.

Zone of inhibition (ZOI) and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) measurements of the antibacterial tests were carried out in the presence of visible light. Consequently, the material's performance under light-activated conditions, where photocatalytically produced reactive oxygen species (ROS) can considerably contribute to bacterial inactivation, is reflected in the reported antimicrobial activity.

Results and discussion

Characterization

FT-IR analysis

displays the FT-IR spectra for pure TiO2, NiO–TiO2 and Ag@NiO–TiO2 nanocomposites recorded in the 400–4000 cm−1 range. The existence of O–H stretching and bending modes of vibrations resulting from water absorption is responsible for the strong wide band at around 3200–3500 cm−1 in all the synthesized samples. A characteristic vibration mode of TiO2 is shown by the appearance of a wide absorption band at 552 cm−1. For the NiO–TiO2 nanocomposite sample, broad peaks of TiO2 and NiO appear at about 688 cm−1 and 470 cm−1, respectively, as a shift was observed in the peak due to the presence of NiO, which is commonly reported in the literature. For the Ag@NiO–TiO2 nanocomposite sample, a small change appears in the peak position, and no clear new peak is observed for Ag itself, indicating that the ternary heterojunction was successfully formed. The broad absorption band at 3395 cm−1 is attributed to the O–H stretching vibration of surface hydroxyl groups and physically bound water molecules on the surface of the Ag@NiO–TiO2 compound.

XRD analysis

XRD analysis showed that after adding NiO and subsequently Ag, as shown in , the three patterns maintained almost the same primary peak positions, which often indicates that the basic TiO2 anatase crystal structure is still retained. The dominating anatase TiO2 (101) reflection is consistent with the strongest peak at about 25°, and the other recurrent peaks likewise correspond to frequent anatase sites. Metallic Ag diffraction peaks appear near 38°, 44°, 64°, and 77°, while the peaks around 37° and 43° are typically associated with cubic NiO. The NiO–TiO2 and Ag@NiO–TiO2 traces show these additional weak peaks and intensity changes, suggesting the successful loading of secondary phases or surface modification rather than a complete phase transformation of TiO2. Anatase TiO2 continues to be the predominant crystalline phase in all samples, according to the XRD patterns. Furthermore, using Cu Kα radiation with a wavelength of 1.5406 Å and the Debye Scherrer equation (crystallite size (nm) = 0.9 λ/B cos θ) and Williamson–Hall (W–H) analysis was used to determine the sample crystallite size and lattice strain from the XRD data. The major anatase TiO2 diffraction peaks at around 25.28°, 37.78°, 48.02°, 53.87°, and 55.04° were used for analysis. The crystallite size was determined from these peaks, according to the Debye Scherrer equation, with an average of roughly 60.2 ± 6.4 nm. Using the formula, β cos θ = 4ε sin θ + Kλ/D, Williamson–Hall analysis gave an estimated crystallite size of about 59.1 nm and a lattice strain of around −1.54 × 10−5, correspondingly about −0.0018%. The negative sign suggests a tiny compressive strain, and the very small magnitude of the strain showed that the lattice deformation in the sample is almost insignificant.

Raman analysis

The crystalline TiO2 phase was confirmed by the black TiO2 spectrum, which displayed distinctive Raman peaks at 350–400, 460, and 590–650 cm−1, as shown in . The red NiO–TiO2 spectrum retained identical peak positions when NiO was added, but the peaks grew larger and slightly displaced, indicating potential structural distortion and interaction between NiO and TiO2. The Raman peaks are still visible in the blue Ag@NiO–TiO2 spectrum, but they look larger and less strong than those for pure TiO2. This suggests that Ag insertion changes the surface structure and promotes defect formation or charge interaction. Overall, the spectra showed that TiO2 is still the primary phase in all samples, but the addition of NiO and Ag affects the composite material crystallinity, surface defects, and electrical interaction. These Raman spectra variations are frequently linked to strain caused by dopants and heterojunctions, nanoscale effects, and modified charge-transfer behavior in metal oxide nanocomposites. The successful synthesis of NiO–TiO2 and Ag@NiO–TiO2 heterostructures, where NiO and Ag affect the surface structure and electronic interaction of TiO2, is thus supported by the Raman study.

The vibrational modes of TiO2 are primarily responsible for the Raman peaks seen in the TiO2, NiO–TiO2, and Ag@NiO–TiO2 spectra. The crystalline TiO2 structure is confirmed by the Eg mode, which shows stretching vibrations of the Ti–O bond. Ti–O–Ti bending vibrations are associated with the B1g mode, whereas symmetric stretching vibrations of the Ti–O framework are associated with the A1g mode. These modes existence in every sample confirms the TiO2 phase retention following NiO and Ag incorporation. Slight variations in peak intensity, broadening or shifting, however point to defect development, lattice distortion, and interactions between Ag, NiO, and TiO2 in the nanocomposite structure.

SEM analysis

The SEM image of pure TiO2 () shows a porous network formed by densely agglomerated, roughly spherical nanoparticles. Sol–gel-derived TiO2 exhibits such aggregated nanocrystalline TiO2 domains, which offer a high surface area advantageous for photocatalytic applications. The NiO–TiO2 nanocomposite () exhibits a more compact surface, featuring sub-nanometer aggregates made up of finer nanograins. The difference in texture when compared to pure TiO2 suggests that NiO particles/shells are closely deposited on the TiO2 matrix. This aligns with prior findings on NiO–TiO2 heterostructures, where NiO incorporation enhances surface roughness and creates a p–n junction with TiO2. The high-magnification SEM image of Ag@TiO2–NiO () shows fine nanoparticles (8–16 nm) that are uniformly distributed on the NiO–TiO2 aggregates, as indicated by the size markers. Ag nanoparticles that are anchored on the NiO–TiO2 surface are the origin of these nanodots. Similar sizes and distributions of Ag nanoparticles on TiO2 are observed in Ag–TiO2 and Ag@NiO–TiO2 systems, where the Ag NPs create Schottky contacts and enhance visible-light absorption through plasmonic effects.

UV-vis analysis

Using UV-visible (UV-vis) spectroscopy, the energy band gap of the Ag@NiO–TiO2 photocatalyst was examined and compared with those of TiO2 and NiO–TiO2 composites, as seen in . The absorbance values of the samples were measured in the 350–900 nm range. The red shift at 450 nm in indicates that adding Ag and NiO nanoparticles to TiO2 greatly enhanced the composite absorption. Hence, the Ag nanoparticles impact localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), and the shift of the peak to a longer wavelength value (redshift) will result in a decrease in the energy band gap of Ag@NiO–TiO2. In addition, the color shift to brown following the synthesis of Ag@NiO–TiO2 in contrast to the white rutile TiO2 is another indication, which promotes the absorption spectrum of visible light. The Tauc plot of TiO2 is displayed alongside NiO–TiO2 and Ag@NiO–TiO2 composites in . The plot showed that all compounds have band gap energies for TiO2, NiO–TiO2 and Ag@NiO–TiO2 nanoparticles as 3.37, 3.35 and 3.27, respectively, as determined using the Tauc plot method. These findings show that the band gap was lowered by 0.10 eV when Ag and NiO were added to TiO2.

Photoluminescence spectroscopy analysis

The photoluminescence spectra provide insights into the recombination behavior of photogenerated charge carriers in the photocatalysts, as displayed in . Pure TiO2 exhibits the highest PL intensity, which indicates rapid recombination of photoexcited electrons and holes, limiting its photocatalytic activity. After coupling TiO2 with NiO, the PL intensity decreases, suggesting that the formation of a NiO–TiO2 heterojunction promotes interfacial charge transfer and improves electron–hole separation. The Ag@NiO–TiO2 composite shows the lowest PL emission intensity, confirming the most efficient suppression of charge-carrier recombination. Ag nanoparticles function as conductive mediators and electron traps, which promote charge separation and electron movement responsible for this improvement, whereas NiO facilitates the creation of heterojunctions with TiO2 on its surface. As a result, the lower PL intensity of Ag@NiO–TiO2 suggests improved charge-transfer efficiency, a longer carrier lifetime, and increased availability of reactive species such ̇OH and ̇O2−, which improves the photocatalytic degradation performance.

TGA

The thermal stability of TiO2, NiO–TiO2, and Ag@NiO–TiO2 from room temperature to 800 °C is shown in . The materials are thermally stable since all three samples exhibit very little mass loss. With a mass loss of about 99.3%, pure TiO2 exhibits the least amount of mass loss, indicating strong thermal stability. Ag@NiO–TiO2 exhibits a little greater mass loss, close to 98.9%, indicating some surface alteration and adsorbed species removal. The removal of physically adsorbed water, surface hydroxyl groups, or leftover precursor species from the composite surface may be the cause of NiO–TiO2 largest mass loss, which ends at about 97.4%. While slow weight loss at higher temperatures may be linked to structural/surface changes or the breakdown of residual organics, low-temperature mass loss in TiO2-based nanomaterials is often related to adsorbed water and surface hydroxyl groups. TGA is frequently used to assess how the weight of nanoparticles changes and thermal stability is affected as the temperatures increases. The TiO2, NiO–TiO2, and Ag@NiO–TiO2 TGA curves show a slow mass loss as the temperature rises, indicating that all samples have high thermal stability up to 800 °C.

Photocatalytic activity

MB dye photodegradation

The time-resolved UV-vis spectra of MB reveal a gradual reduction in the characteristic band at 665 nm for all catalysts, confirming photocatalytic degradation. Due to its wide band gap and rapid charge recombination, TiO2 demonstrates only moderate MB removal after 60 minutes, as presented in . It showed 51% removal of MB dye after one hour. The decrease in MB absorbance in TiO2–NiO occurs at a significantly accelerated rate, which is attributed to the p–n heterojunction that enhances charge separation and the use of visible light, and the removal of the dye reached 76% after 60 minutes, as presented in . The highest activity is shown by the Ag@NiO–TiO2 nanocomposite that approximately removes 88% of MB in 60 min, as presented in . This improvement is due to the combined effects of the NiO–TiO2 heterojunction and plasmonic Ag, which enhance light absorption and reduce electron–hole recombination.

CV dye photodegradation

The photodegradation studies were carried out utilizing UV-visible light irradiation with photocatalysts, namely, TiO2, NiO–TiO2, and Ag@NiO–TiO2. To establish an adsorption–desorption equilibrium, the CV dye wastewater and a specific photocatalyst were stirred in the dark for 15 minutes before being exposed to UV-visible light. The initial dye concentration used in the experiments was 50 mg L−1, and the catalyst dose was 20 mg L−1 at the original pH 6.5 of dye solution. display the results for the time-dependent dye degradation.

During the entire span of 60-minute testing period, the blank photodegradation run with any catalyst showed little photodegradation performance. Dye degradation, however, varied significantly over time when all photocatalysts were present. The outcomes of the dark condition displayed the improved adsorption capacity of the Ag@NiO–TiO2 photocatalyst, which is essential for the efficient dye degradation under UV-visible light irradiation. The order of several photocatalysts photocatalytic activity was Ag@NiO–TiO2 > NiO–TiO2 > TiO2. After a 60-minute treatment period, the dye removal efficiencies for TiO2, NiO–TiO2, and Ag@NiO–TiO2 photocatalysts were calculated to be 48%, 62%, and 90%, respectively. Ciprofloxacin antibiotic photodegradation

display a comparative plot of relative concentration vs. irradiation duration after the UV-vis monitoring of ciprofloxacin (CIP) degradation using three photocatalysts: TiO2, NiO–TiO2, and Ag@NiO–TiO2. The strong UV absorption of ciprofloxacin is mostly observed around 275–278 nm, with additional bands and shoulders frequently observed at 316 and 331 nm. Since these bands originate from the fluoroquinolone chromophore, it is a routine practice to monitor CIP degradation by observing how they diminish under irradiation. The CIP concentration was measured by UV-vis spectroscopy at around 277 nm in several CIP photocatalysis investigations.

TiO2, NiO–TiO2, and Ag@NiO–TiO2 absorbance spectra showed how the concentration of ciprofloxacin (CIP) varies during the photocatalytic degradation process. The dark spectrum is recorded after the catalyst and CIP solution are kept in the dark, frequently to assess the degree of adsorption on the catalyst surface, whereas the before dark spectrum represents the original CIP solution prior to adsorption equilibrium being established. The spectra obtained between 30 and 180 min represent varying light irradiation times. A drop in CIP concentration is indicated by a decrease in the distinctive CIP absorption peak, which is observed between 275 and 300 nm. Consequently, more effective photocatalytic degradation is suggested by a quicker and larger drop in this peak.

The major ciprofloxacin absorption peak in TiO2 (a) only marginally declines from the dark stage to 180 minutes of light irradiation in , suggesting that bare TiO2 exhibits poor photocatalytic effectiveness under the studied environment. Despite TiO2 stability and widespread usage as a photocatalyst, its activity is limited by the fast recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs and its comparatively large band gap, which is typically between 3.0 and 3.2 eV. These findings weaken the photocatalytic efficiency and visible light usage. TiO2 can therefore produce reactive species, but before many of the photogenerated electrons and holes can react with oxygen, water, or hydroxide ions to efficiently breakdown ciprofloxacin, they recombine.

The ciprofloxacin absorption peak in for NiO–TiO2 is clearly reduced compared to bare TiO2, suggesting that the inclusion of NiO enhances the photocatalytic degradation ability. The development of a NiO/TiO2 heterojunction, in which NiO functions as a p-type semiconductor and TiO2 typically operates as an n-type semiconductor, is most likely the cause of this increase. Combining these two materials can create a p–n junction, which encourages the separation of photogenerated electrons and holes and decreases their recombination. More electrons and holes can take part in surface reactions and produce reactive oxygen species such as hydroxyl radicals ( ̇OH), superoxide radicals (O2 ̇−), and photogenerated holes (h+) to improve charge separation. The piperazine ring, quinolone core, carboxyl group, and other active areas of ciprofloxacin molecules possibly will then be attacked by these reactive species.

Similarly, ciprofloxacin absorbance peak in the presence of catalyst Ag@NiO–TiO2 in lowers the greatest during 180 minutes of light irradiation, suggesting that Ag doping offers the best CIP degradation of the three catalysts. Ag plays many roles in the TiO2-based photocatalytic system, which accounts for the increased activity. By taking in photogenerated electrons, Ag can function as an electron trap, improving charge separation and lowering electron–hole recombination. Ag nanoparticles can also improve visible light absorption through surface plasmon resonance, which makes it possible for the catalyst to use light more efficiently. By helping as a bridge between NiO and TiO2, Ag may also enhance interfacial charge transfer by allowing charges to flow throughout the composite structure. Consequently, additional reactive oxygen species are created, and contribute to the breakdown of CIP, including hydroxyl radicals ( ̇OH) and superoxide radicals (O2 ̇−). This behavior is in line with the findings that show how doping, metal decorating, and heterojunction building improve light absorption, charge separation, and reactive species formation to increase ciprofloxacin photocatalytic degradation.

MB, CV and CIP mineralization

The total organic carbon (TOC) indicates the degree of MB, CV and CIP mineralization. To determine the mineralization of the MB dye, CV dye and CIP antibiotic using the Ag@NiO–TiO2 NPs, TOC tests were carried out. A significant decrease in TOC was found, indicating effective MB, CV and CIP mineralization. The mineralization process of MB, CV and CIP under ideal photocatalytic conditions was examined using TOC analysis. After 1 h of the reaction, the findings displayed TOC removal efficiencies for MB, CV and CIP to be 82%, 65% and 47%, respectively. The formation of persistent intermediates during the degradation processes, each of which follows a different degradation pathway, may be the cause of the difference in mineralization and degradation rates.

Since only the carbon part of each molecule contributes to the TOC measurement, even if the initial concentration of each chemical was 50 ppm, this number does not precisely correlate to 50 ppm TOC. The theoretical initial TOC concentrations for MB, CV and CIP were around 60.1, 73.6 and 61.6 ppm, respectively, based on their molecular compositions. The residual TOC values after treatment were 11.10 ppm for MB, 26.24 ppm for CV, and 31.81 ppm for CIP. These numbers translate into projected mineralization efficiencies around 82.0%, 64.3% and 47.1%, respectively. As a result, MB showed the highest level of mineralization, followed by CV and CIP.

Reusability and stability of photocatalysts

The ideal parameters for the photocatalyst Ag@NiO–TiO2 with a catalyst dose of 0.03 g, a pH of 9, and a concentration of 20 ppm were tested for the reusability and stability. After the tests, the sample was collected and dried in an oven at 70 °C. Even after four cycles, there were no substantial variations in the photocatalyst performance, as shown in . This experiment showed that the Ag@NiO–TiO2 photocatalyst has excellent recyclability and stability, and may be effectively employed in the degradation of organic waste. From the first to the fourth cycle, there is only a little drop in degradation efficiency. CIP degradation decreases from around 64% to 52%, CV degradation from roughly 97% to 88–89%, and MB degradation from 95% to about 82%, whereas the reduction in degradation may result from the catalyst loss during recovery, partial blocking of active sites by adsorbed intermediates, or photocatalyst deactivation following repeated uses. This retention of activity indicates a high photocatalytic stability and recyclability. Reusability is a crucial factor for effective photocatalytic wastewater treatment because catalyst recovery and activity retention have a direct impact on process practicality.

The current photocatalytic degradation studies were carried out in a controlled environment with synthetic single-pollutant solutions. Real industrial wastewater contains a variety of inorganic ions and dissolved organic matter that may affect the photocatalytic efficiency, even though our studies shed light on the intrinsic photocatalytic performance of the given materials. The degradation rate may be altered by species like Cl−, HCO3−, SO42−, NO3−, and natural organic matter that compete with target pollutants for adsorption sites or interact with photogenerated reactive species. Specifically, extremely reactive hydroxyl radicals can be scavenged by bicarbonate and natural organic materials, which could decrease the photocatalytic efficacy. Therefore, to assess the prepared photocatalyst practical use under complex environmental conditions, future research studies would be carried out with real or suggested industrial wastewater matrices.

Effect of pH, initial concentration of MB, CV and CIP solutions and catalyst dose on degradation

Using 10 mg per L MB dye, CV dye and CIP antibiotic as the starting concentration and 0.02 g of the Ag@NiO–TiO2 NPs as the photocatalyst quantity, the impact of pH on the photocatalytic activity of Ag@NiO–TiO2 NPs was examined between pH 3.0 and 11.0. The pH of 10 mg per L MB dye, CV dye and CIP antibiotic was determined to be approximately 6.5, and the pH of this solution was adjusted by using 1 mol per L NaOH or 1 mol L solution. Under UV-vis illumination, the percentage degradation versus time for different pH levels of 10 mg per L MB and CV solutions was measured. At pH 9, the greatest percentage of degradation was achieved in 60 minutes. The Ag@NiO–TiO2 surface charge is known to be pH sensitive, with a negative surface charge at pH 9. Therefore, the Ag@NiO–TiO2 NP composite surface charge is negative. MB and CV are cationic dyes, so the dye and the nanoparticles have strong electrostatic contact. The photocatalytic effect of Ag@NiO–TiO2 NPs was further initiated by this interaction. Similarly, the highest performance was observed for CIP degradation with Ag@NiO–TiO2 at a pH around 7.5, frequently near pH 7, rather than very alkaline pH like 9–11.

The effect of the initial dye concentration of 10, 30, 40 and 50 mg L−1 with the photocatalyst quantity at 0.02 g and the pH of the MB and CV solution at 9 was studied. It was experimentally observed that the percentage of dye removal decreases from 10 to 50 mg L−1. The parts of the Ag@NiO–TiO2 NP surface that are activated by light may be blocked by methylene blue and crystal violet dye molecules. As a result, it may be possible to stop the production of ̇OH radicals, which oxidize and destroy the color. Similar results for CIP degradation at pH 7.5 and the same conditions were observed.

Ag@NiO–TiO2 NPs with various concentrations in the range of 10–50 mg L−1 were used to degrade 10 ppm of MB and CV solution at a pH of 9.5 with a contact period of 60 min in order to investigate the impact of loading ability on the dye degradation efficiency. When the dose of Ag@NiO–TiO2 NPs was increased up to 30 mg L−1, the percentage of dye photodegradation also increased; after that, it became nearly constant. The overall number of active sites on the catalyst surface increases somewhat with the concentration of Ag@NiO–TiO2 NPs. Therefore, for 30 mg of catalyst, the maximum degradation for MB and CV is observed for 10 ppm solution of MB and CV. Ag@NiO–TiO2 NPs nanocomposites (0.02 g L−1) under constant conditions were used to examine the impact of initial ciprofloxacin (CIP) concentration (10 ppm, 20 ppm, 30 ppm, 40 ppm and 50 ppm) on the photocatalytic performance. The degradation efficiency varies greatly with the starting CIP concentration. After 180 minutes of irradiation, the greatest degradation of 64% was achieved at 10 ppm, compared to 57.2%, 48%, 42% and 36% for 20 ppm, 30 ppm, 40 ppm and 50 ppm solutions of the Ag@NiO–TiO2 composite, respectively, as the most efficient catalyst in all.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the Ag@NiO–TiO2 ternary heterojunction nanocomposite was effectively created by using a hydrothermal technique to evenly deposit and distribute NiO and Ag nanoparticles in sphere shape with diameters less than 10 nm on the surface of TiO2 with a size range of 100–200 nm. The material prepared was characterized using FT-IR spectroscopy, XRD, Raman spectroscopy, UV-visible spectroscopy, SEM-EDS mapping, HRTEM, TGA, CV and EIS. On the TiO2 surface, the Ag and NiO nanoparticles are evenly distributed with no other phase. In comparison to the other compounds (NiO–TiO2 and bare TiO2), the larger porosity and decreased band gap caused a higher wavelength shift in the UV-absorption spectra and a high generation of radicals, due to which Ag@NiO–TiO2 exhibited efficient photocatalytic activity. Ag@NiO–TiO2 showed outstanding organic compound photocatalytic degradation efficiency against MB, CV and CIP. Ag NPs considerably increase the catalytic activity and facilitate electron transfer by adding more electrons to the CB of TiO2, hence decreasing the electron–hole recombination. By lowering the energy band gap, moving the absorption to the visible light region, and improving the electron–hole pair separation efficacy, the close contact of TiO2, NiO, and Ag through the creation of Schottky junctions at the Ag–TiO2 and Ag–NiO interfaces and the formation of a p–n junction between TiO2 and NiO within the ternary nanocomposite structure all play critical roles in the photocatalytic activity. This study demonstrates how well the photocatalyst breaks down organic molecules. The antimicrobial activity showed that Ag@NiO–TiO2 is a good agent exhibiting high biocidal activity against all strains compared to TiO2 and NiO–TiO2 compounds. To assess the toxicity of the ternary Ag@NiO–TiO2 nanocomposite against microorganisms (such as bacteria and viruses), more research is needed.
