SSEL‐CPP: A SHAP‐based feature‐selection ensemble learning framework identifies molecular properties of cell‐penetrating peptides
Authors: Chan Woo Kwon, Minjun Kwon, Shaherin Basith, Sampa Misra, Yong Eun Jang, Gwang Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCell‐penetrating peptides (CPPs) facilitate the intracellular delivery of therapeutic molecules. However, their accurate identification and design remain challenging because of the complexity of their structural and physicochemical characteristics. This study aimed to develop an interpretable predictive model that enables reliable CPP discovery and provides interpretable descriptors suggestive of the molecular properties underlying their activity. Peptide samples were represented using two‐dimensional descriptors generated by Mordred. A novel two‐stage feature selection method was introduced, combining a correlation‐based filter with Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). The model was trained on the CPP1708 dataset and built using an ensemble learning strategy integrating multiple machine learning algorithms. The ensemble framework, combining Extreme Gradient Boosting and Light Gradient Boosting Machine, identified five Mordred descriptors—5‐ordered bonding information content (BIC5), Extended Topochemical Atom epsilon 5 (ETA_epsilon_5), averaged and centered Moreau‐Broto autocorrelation of lag 0 weighted by ionization potential (AATSC0i), centered Moreau‐Broto autocorrelation of lag 2 weighted by mass (ATSC2m), and first highest eigenvalue of Burden matrix weighted by gasteiger charge (BCUTc‐1h)—as critical features for CPP prediction. The model achieved an accuracy of 82.0% and an area under the curve of 87.5% on the CPP1708 test set, outperforming existing predictors. This interpretable, high‐performing prediction model supports the rational design of CPPs and advances peptide‐based drug development. The SHAP‐guided feature selection framework improves both efficiency and interpretability, with potential applications across diverse peptide classes. Furthermore, the identification of five mechanistic descriptors offers deeper insight into the structural, electronic, and physicochemical properties underpinning CPP activity.Keywords: cell‐penetrating peptide, ensemble learning, feature selection, filter methods, Mordred, Shapley additive explanations, simplified molecular input line entry system

INTRODUCTION

The therapeutic potential of many drugs is often limited by their poor cellular uptake. Over the past two decades, cell‐penetrating peptides (CPPs) have received significant attention for their ability to transport cargos—including small molecules (Stiltner et al. 2021; Zorko et al. 2022), nucleic acids (Falato et al. 2021), proteins (Qian et al. 2014), liposomes (Jiang et al. 2012; Yang et al. 2014), nanoparticles (Zhang et al. 2021), and oligonucleotides (Margus et al. 2012)—across cellular membranes without disrupting membrane integrity (Ghorai et al. 2023; Hsueh et al. 2023; Thomas et al. 2022).

CPPs, typically 5–30 amino acids in length, are characterized by cationic, amphipathic, or hydrophobic properties (Stalmans et al. 2013). The intrinsic properties of cellular membranes, together with the interactions between CPPs and the membrane—strongly influenced by the structural and electronic properties of the peptides—play a significant role in determining their cell‐penetrating efficacy and the cellular uptake pathways employed (Heitz et al. 2009; Zakany et al. 2023). Identifying the biochemical properties of peptides that should be targeted during drug design enables precise regulation of the factors governing membrane penetration. Therefore, the rational design and accurate identification of CPPs are crucial for effective drug development.

Traditionally, experimental (“wet‐lab”) methods involving peptide synthesis and assays of membrane‐penetrating ability have been employed to discover and characterize CPPs (Madani et al. 2011). Although these approaches offer valuable insights into peptide dynamics and function, they are limited by their labor‐intensive, costly, and technically complex nature (El‐Andaloussi et al. 2005; Su et al. 2020). In contrast, computational (“in silico”) approaches—such as machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL)—offer scalable and cost‐effective alternatives. These approaches leverage large peptide datasets to enable efficient prediction and generalization across diverse sequences (Basith et al. 2020; Gautam et al. 2013; Kwon et al. 2025).

Several existing prediction models, for example, CellCPP (Gautam et al. 2013), SkipCPPred (Wei et al. 2017a), CPPred‐RF (Wei et al. 2017b), CPPred‐FL (Qiang et al. 2020), StackCPPred (Fu et al. 2020), C2Pred (Tang et al. 2016), BChemRF‐CPPred (de Oliveira et al. 2021), MLCPP (Manavalan et al. 2018) use conventional biochemical features with ML classifiers. Furthermore, conventional feature‐based DL models, such as TargetCPP (Arif et al. 2020) and MLCPP2.0 (Manavalan and Patra 2022), have enhanced predictive performance by integrating peptide features into neural network architectures. Feature‐based approaches utilize descriptors such as charge, hydrophobicity, and amino acid composition (Chen et al. 2018). Despite their interpretability, however, these models struggle to capture sequence‐specific structural patterns, thereby limiting their ability to represent the inherent complexity of peptides.

Protein language models (PLMs) have demonstrated the ability to capture biologically relevant features of CPPs, including charge distribution, hydrophobicity patterns, and secondary structure tendencies (Elnaggar et al. 2021). Consequently, many recent studies have leveraged PLM‐based representations, pre‐trained on large‐scale sequence datasets, for CPP prediction. For instance, models such as SiameseCPP (Zhang et al. 2023) and FusPB‐ESM2 (Zhang et al. 2024) rely exclusively on PLM‐derived features, whereas hybrid approaches like PractiCPP (Shi et al. 2024) and EnDM‐CPP (Zhu et al. 2024) integrate both conventional hand‐crafted features and PLM‐derived representations to enhance predictive accuracy. PLM‐based features provide rich contextual information trained on extensive protein sequences, thereby capturing both structural and evolutionary relationships. Nevertheless, these embeddings are often generic and may fail to distinguish subtle variations between highly similar sequences, in addition to offering limited interpretability. CPPpred‐En (Jang et al. 2025) addresses these limitations through an ensemble approach that combines diverse conventional and PLM‐based features to enhance representational power. Likewise, GraphCPP (Imre et al. 2025) introduces an prediction model by utilizing the Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) format (Weininger 1988) instead of the conventional FASTA format, coupled with a graph neural network architecture.

To date, most CPP prediction studies have focused on improving model performance, resulting in the development of various DL architectures that achieve high predictive accuracy. However, these models generally function as black‐boxes, offering limited insight into the underlying mechanisms that govern predict penetration of cell membranes. Furthermore, only a few models—namely CellPPDMod (Kumar et al. 2018), BChemRF‐CPPred (de Oliveira et al. 2021), and GraphCPP (Imre et al. 2025)—are capable of predicting cyclic peptides or peptides containing non‐natural amino acids. Consequently, current approaches remain restricted both in terms of interpretability and in the breadth of chemical space they can accurately predict.

In this study, we developed an ML‐based framework, termed SSEL‐CPP (SHAP‐based Soft Ensemble Learning for Cell‐Penetrating Peptides), to explore a wide range of peptide chemical space and predict CPPs. The model was trained on SMILES format data using two‐dimensional (2D) molecular descriptors generated by Mordred (Moriwaki et al. 2018). To enhance explainability, we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to identify the features that most strongly contributed to the predictions (Lundberg and Lee 2017).

SHAP is now one of the most widely used approaches for model explainability (Lundberg and Lee 2017). Recent studies have shown its utility not only in explaining final model outputs but also in interpretable and informative feature selection (Bhattacharjee et al. 2025; Kraev et al. 2024; Marcílio and Eler 2020). Building on this, we propose a novel SHAP‐based feature selection process that improves interpretability and efficiency for feature selection.

SSEL‐CPP operates in two stages. First, a two‐step feature selection is performed, combining filter‐based redundancy removal with SHAP‐based importance ranking. Second, the selected feature subsets are used to train base models, which are then combined using a soft‐voting ensemble strategy.

This feature selection approach achieved over 80% feature reduction while maintaining or improving classification performance across the base models. The final model achieved strong and competitive predictive performance and highlighted five key features critical for CPP prediction. These features are consistent with established scientific knowledge and provide mechanistic insights into CPP activity. Taken together, the model's predictive accuracy and interpretability suggest that our methodology will be valuable in guiding future research and the development of CPP‐driven drug delivery systems.

Selection of important features

Feature importance was quantified using the mean absolute SHAP values from the final XGB and LGBM models. Averaging the feature importance ranks from both models yielded a consensus ranking (Table S5), with the top five features identified as 5‐ordered bonding information content (BIC5), Extended Topochemical Atom epsilon 5 (ETA_epsilon_5), averaged and centered Moreau‐Broto autocorrelation of lag 0 weighted by ionization potential (AATSC0i), centered Moreau‐Broto autocorrelation of lag 2 weighted by mass (ATSC2m), and first highest eigenvalue of Burden matrix weighted by gasteiger charge (BCUTc‐1h). XGB and LGBM produced near‐identical top five rankings, differing only in the relative order of AATSC0i and ATSC2m. This ranking was consistent across both the CPP1708 test set and the MLCPP2.0 test set, except for LGBM, where GATS3c replaced AATSC0i in the fifth position (Figure S4). Detailed descriptions of the five features are provided in Data S1.

Pairwise Pearson correlations among the top five features were low, indicating minimal redundancy (Figure 4a). Mann–Whitney U tests revealed significant distributional differences between CPPs and non‐CPPs for all five descriptors (p < 0.001). Notably, BIC5, ETA_epsilon_5, ATSC2m, and BCUTc‐1h were lower in CPPs, whereas AATSC0i was higher (Figure 4b).

Further analysis showed that BIC3 and BIC4 were also significantly reduced in CPPs (p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively), whereas BIC2 showed no difference (Figure S5a). To investigate the molecular basis of the lower ATSC2m values observed in CPPs, we analyzed trends of ATSC descriptors based on the same atomic property (atomic mass) across increasing topological distances (lag 0–8), as depicted in Figure S5b. Among the evaluated descriptors (ATSC0m–ATSC8m), ATSC0m, ATSC2m, and ATSC4m were significantly lower in CPPs compared with non‐CPPs (p < 0.01 for ATSC0m; p < 0.001 for ATSC2m and ATSC4m), whereas ATSC6m and ATSC7m were significantly higher (p < 0.001). The remaining descriptors (ATSC1m, ATSC3m, ATSC5m, and ATSC8m) showed no differences.

To further evaluate the robustness of the SHAP‐derived feature importance results, repeated permutation importance analysis was additionally performed using the final ensemble model. The permutation importance results showed partial but meaningful consistency with the SHAP‐derived rankings. BCUTc‐1h and ATSC2m consistently exhibited high importance across multiple evaluation metrics, while BIC5, ETA_epsilon_5, and AATSC0i also remained influential in permutation‐based analyses. DISCUSSION

We developed a transparent and interpretable framework for CPP classification by integrating a correlation‐based filter method with SHAP‐based feature selection in a two‐stage feature selection process, followed by ensemble learning of base ML models. This framework achieved competitive performance on the CPP1708 dataset.

Previous studies have not reached consensus on whether to use absolute or original correlation values when applying filter methods. In this work, we conducted a comparative analysis of alternative approaches. Filter Method 4, which removes one feature from each redundant pair using the absolute value of Kendall's tau and then orders the remaining features according to their original Kendall's tau values, yielded the best performance. This result suggests that the choice of filter method may vary depending on the specific dataset and prediction task (in our case, CPP classification) (Bommert et al. 2020). Accordingly, we recommend conducting dataset‐specific comparative studies when selecting filter methods to enhance predictive accuracy.

Although the use of SHAP values in feature selection remains debated (Fryer et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2024), several studies have demonstrated their effectiveness (Marcílio and Eler 2020; Verhaeghe et al. 2022). Yet, few studies have applied SHAP‐based feature selection to peptide prediction, especially in combination with wrapper methods (Akbar et al. 2022). To the best of our knowledge, no previous CPP prediction model has employed SHAP‐based feature selection. Unlike existing approaches, our method incorporates trend‐line analysis, which is more intuitive and provides greater interpretability while minimizing the risk of overfitting by focusing on the overall patterns rather than individual points. Comparative analysis demonstrated that the proposed two‐step feature selection strategy enhanced both model performance and computational efficiency by substantially reducing the feature set.

Compared with many previous CPP prediction models that primarily function as black boxes, our framework provides both high interpretability in feature representation and strong explainability in model predictions. SHAP analyses performed on the training set, the CPP1708 independent test set, and the MLCPP2.0 benchmark consistently identified five Mordred descriptors as the most influential features across datasets, suggesting relative consistency in descriptor importance patterns. Furthermore, repeated permutation importance analysis demonstrated meaningful consistency with the SHAP‐derived results. Although the exact ranking order differed between SHAP and permutation importance, such differences are expected because the two methods quantify feature relevance using different principles. While residual multicollinearity effects cannot be completely excluded, these findings suggest that the major descriptors identified in this study are not merely SHAP‐specific statistical artifacts and retain meaningful predictive relevance, providing structural and electronic insights into the mechanisms underlying CPP activity.

Lower values of ETA_Epsilon_5 indicate reduced electronic influence from polar or electron‐dense moieties, such as electronegative atoms, which may correspond to increased hydrophobicity and, depending on other molecular properties, enhanced membrane permeability. A higher AATSC0(i) value implies a more homogeneous distribution of ionization potentials, consistent with a balanced overall electronic structure. Similarly, lower BCUTc‐1h values suggest a more uniform partial charge distribution and reduced electronic asymmetry, reinforcing the electrostatic stability of CPP molecular surfaces.

Lower BIC3, BIC4, and BIC5 values point to simpler and more repetitive bonding patterns in CPPs, indicative of uniform molecular connectivity. Mass‐based ATSC descriptors provide further structural insights: CPPs displayed lower autocorrelation values at short topological distances (ATSC0m, ATSC2m, ATSC4m), suggesting high local heterogeneity in their molecular structure. In contrast, higher values at intermediate distances (ATSC6m, ATSC7m) suggest regular mass distribution patterns, potentially associated with structural repetition or amphipathic motifs. These descriptors align with the known structural and electronic characteristics of CPPs. While these findings provide meaningful insights into the structural and electronic characteristics of CPPs, further validation through in vitro and in vivo experiments will be essential to confirm their biological relevance and practical applicability.

2D Mordred descriptors were selected in this study because they provide chemically interpretable and computationally efficient representations while enabling unified feature extraction from structurally heterogeneous peptides, including cyclic and chemically modified peptides. Nevertheless, future studies may benefit from integrating complementary representations such as 3D conformational descriptors and graph neural network embeddings to better capture peptide structural dynamics.

Future work could further explore CPP prediction using PLM‐derived representations rather than relying solely on conventional features. Beyond the ESM1‐based representations adopted in previous CPP prediction models (Bernardes‐Loch et al. 2025), features derived from advanced PLMs such as ESM2 (Lin et al. 2023), ESM‐C (Hayes et al. 2025), and ProtT5 (Elnaggar et al. 2021) could be investigated to assess their potential contribution to predictive performance. However, PLM‐based features are predominantly pretrained on canonical amino acid sequences. Therefore, their direct applicability to the CPP1708 dataset, which includes peptides containing non‐canonical residues, chemical modifications, and cyclic structural representations, may be limited. Also, PLM‐based representations may compromise model interpretability, as their high‐dimensional embeddings are inherently difficult to attribute to specific chemical or structural properties. In contrast, the proposed SSEL‐CPP framework emphasizes interpretability through SHAP‐based feature selection, enabling the identification of chemically meaningful descriptors associated with CPP activity. This makes SSEL‐CPP a complementary tool for both predicting and mechanistically understanding CPPs.

CONCLUSION

We developed a CPP prediction model, called SSEL‐CPP. Despite its strong performance, interpretability, and novelty, our model has three important limitations. First, the base models relied on conventional ML algorithms (e.g., AB, CB, XGB, LGBM, GB, RF, and ERT), whereas recent studies increasingly employ advanced approaches such as contrastive learning (Zhang et al. 2023), graph neural networks (Imre et al. 2025), and protein language models (Jang et al. 2025). Second, only 2D Mordred descriptors were used for feature extraction, while alternative representations such as Morgan fingerprints or PaDEL descriptors (Yap 2011) might provide improved accuracy and interpretability. Finally, our study lacks experimental validation under in vivo conditions.

Nevertheless, descriptor‐based ML models offer advantages in computational efficiency and explainability, particularly when paired with SHAP analysis. This study further demonstrates that descriptor‐based approaches can match or even surpass the prediction accuracy, efficiency, and explainability of graph‐based models (Jiang et al. 2021).

The feature selection and model construction framework proposed is reproducible and domain‐independent, making it broadly generalizable beyond CPP prediction. We envisage its potential application to other peptide‐related domains, such as antimicrobial, anticancer, and multifunctional peptides, as well as to wider areas outside peptide research. Furthermore, the high interpretability of the model and the explanatory insights provided by SHAP analysis not only enhance reliability for future researchers but also facilitate the integration of domain‐specific knowledge during model development. Finally, the insights provided into CPP activity, together with accurate predictive performance, may prove valuable in multiple stages of drug discovery and development.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Modification of the CPP1708 dataset for our analysis

We used the CPP1708 dataset, first introduced in the GraphCPP study (Imre et al. 2025), which integrates peptide sequences from several published sources and public databases. CPP1708, comprising 854 CPPs and 854 non‐CPPs, is currently the largest and most structurally diverse benchmark dataset for CPP prediction. It includes a wide variety of peptides, such as 63 cyclic, 90 synthetic, and multiple linear sequences, which are typically underrepresented or excluded from earlier datasets. This diversity supports the development of predictive models with broader applicability across peptide classes. Each peptide is represented in both FASTA and the corresponding isomeric SMILES format. The original CPP1708 dataset was stratified by molecular weight into training (n = 1465), validation (n = 121), and test (n = 122) subsets to preserve distributional consistency. Since we employed traditional ML models—namely AB, RF, ERT, CB, GB, XGB, and LGBM—with CV, a separate validation dataset was unnecessary. Redundancy in the original dataset was reduced using CD‐HIT with a 70% sequence similarity threshold applied to both CPPs and non‐CPPs, ensuring that highly similar sequences were clustered and deduplicated prior to dataset splitting. Accordingly, we generated a new training set of 1586 samples by combining the original CPP1708 training and validation subsets, while retaining the independent test set of 122 samples.

Data preprocessing

The preprocessing pipeline (Figure 5) was applied to both the training and test sets. During feature extraction, 2D molecular descriptors were calculated using the Mordred package (Moriwaki et al. 2018). The dataset included valid SMILES representations of peptides, including peptides containing non‐canonical or chemically modified residues, allowing Mordred descriptors to be calculated from the corresponding SMILES‐derived molecular graphs. Features containing the keywords “BaryszMatrix,” “BurdenMatrix,” “DetourMatrix,” “SpAbs,” and “Eigen” were excluded because of potential computational errors during descriptor calculation. Infinite and not‐a‐number (NaN) values were converted to “None,” yielding a total of 1495 features for both the training and test datasets.

Schematic representation of data preprocessing. 2D Mordred descriptors are extracted from both the raw training set and the raw test set. Non‐informative features are removed from the training set. Features are considered non‐informative if they: (1) contain only constant values, NULL values, or string‐type values; (2) have less than 10% unique values; or (3) contain more than 15% NaN values. After this filtering, 987 features remain in the training set. These same 987 features are selected in the test set. A KNN imputer is trained on the training set to replace missing values. This trained imputer is then applied to the test set to impute its missing values as well.

Preprocessing of the training dataset was carried out in two steps. The first step involved removing non‐informative features. Features were excluded if they: (i) consisted solely of string values, (ii) had only a single constant value, or (iii) contained only missing values. Additionally, features with a unique‐value ratio below 10% or with more than 15% missing values were removed, resulting in 987 features retained in the training set. The same subset of 987 features was selected from the test set, without performing a separate preprocessing procedure.

In the second step, the remaining missing values in the training set were imputed using k‐nearest neighbors (KNN) imputation (Euclidean distance, neighbors = 5). The KNN model trained on the processed training set was then applied to the test set. Consequently, the final training set contained 1586 samples and 987 complete features, while the test set contained 122 samples with the same 987 complete features.

Proposed framework

In this study, we developed SSEL‐CPP, an ML‐based framework for predicting CPPs using 2D molecular descriptors generated by Mordred (Moriwaki et al. 2018). As illustrated in Figure 6, the SSEL‐CPP architecture comprises two major components: feature selection and model training.

Proposed workflow to construct the SSEL‐CPP model. (1) The model‐independent feature selection process uses a filter method based on Pearson's correlation between two features and Kendall's tau between each feature and the corresponding label. Among a pair of features with a Pearson correlation higher than 0.90, the one which has a lower Kendall's tau value or absolute Kendall's tau value between the feature and the label is discarded. Detailed explanation of whether using an absolute Kendall's tau value is provided in section 2.4.1 and Figure S1. (2) The model‐dependent feature selection procedure employs SHAP values to interpret feature importance. Each machine learning (ML) classifier is trained on the filtered feature subset, followed by SHAP‐based analysis to assess discrepancies between fold‐wise and global SHAP values. If the discrepancies are acceptable, incremental training combined with trend‐line analysis is conducted to identify the optimal feature set tailored to that specific ML model. (3) After completing the two‐step feature selection process for each ML classifier, individual base models are trained using their respective selected feature subsets. Subsequently, a soft voting ensemble strategy is employed to construct the final CPP prediction model (SSEL‐CPP).

The feature selection process was performed in two stages. In the first stage, a model‐independent, filter‐based approach was applied, using Pearson's correlation coefficient and Kendall's tau rank correlation to remove redundant features. In the second stage, a novel model‐dependent method employed SHAP values to identify features with the highest predictive contribution, and this information was incorporated into the selection process.

Model training was also conducted in two stages. First, base ML models were trained on their respective feature subsets. Second, a soft‐voting ensemble learning strategy was employed to integrate the trained base models. The result of this ensemble procedure was the final model, SSEL‐CPP, which predicts whether a given peptide is a CPP.

Model‐independent feature selection (filter methods)

The proposed filter method was based on the principle that an optimal feature subset should exhibit high correlation with the target class while maintaining low inter‐correlation among features (Hall 1999). The training dataset was first normalized to the range [0, 1] to address scaling differences. A Pearson correlation similarity matrix of size N × N was then computed, where N denotes the total number of features. To identify redundancy, pairwise correlations were examined by traversing the matrix. Feature pairs with correlation coefficients greater than 0.90 were considered highly correlated. For each such pair, the Kendall's tau correlation with the target was computed, and the feature with the higher τ value was retained, while the other was discarded. This process effectively eliminated features carrying overlapping information, thereby reducing redundancy in the dataset. However, when applying Kendall's tau correlation for pairwise selection and ranking, no consensus exists on whether to use the absolute value of Kendall's tau or its original signed value.

To investigate the effect of using the absolute value of Kendall's tau (|τ|) in both feature selection and ranking, we developed four filtering strategies (Figure S6a). In Filter Method 1, both selection and ranking were based on |τ|; in Filter Method 2, features were selected on τ but ranked on |τ|; in Filter Method 3, both processes were based on τ; and in Filter Method 4, features were selected on |τ| but ranked on τ.

To compare the effectiveness of these filter methods (Figure S6b), we employed an RF classifier, selected for its efficient training time and relatively small number of critical hyperparameters (Negovetić et al. 2024). Recognizing that model performance may vary because of the randomness of data partitioning and training initialization (Jiang et al. 2021), each experiment was repeated with 50 different random seeds (0–49). For each filtered feature subset, hyperparameter tuning was performed independently using five‐fold cross‐validation under each seed. The hyperparameter configuration most frequently selected across the 50 runs was adopted as the final setting. The resulting classifiers were denoted RF1–RF4, where RF1 corresponds to the feature subset from Filter Method 1, RF2 from Filter Method 2, and so on.

To ensure fairness and reproducibility, grid search was applied uniformly across all feature subsets. Rather than tuning a broad range of hyperparameters, we focused on two key parameters—“max_features” and “min_samples_leaf”—which exert a meaningful impact on RF performance (Probst et al. 2019). We also included “n_estimators” to ensure sufficient model convergence (Negovetić et al. 2024).

The hyperparameter space was deliberately kept narrow to increase the likelihood of identifying a meaningful mode (i.e., the most frequently selected configuration) across 50 random‐seed repetitions. A wider search space would have dispersed the distribution of selected configurations, thereby reducing the reliability of mode‐based model selection. Based on preliminary experiments, “max_features” was fixed to “sqrt,” and grid search was conducted over a reasonable range for the remaining parameters.

Each RF model was evaluated not only on the feature subset on which it was trained, but also on those generated by the other filter methods, to assess generalizability. To investigate the effect of feature ranking, six versions of each subset were generated by selecting the top 50–300 features, in increments of 50, based on Kendall's tau or |τ| scores. Model performance for each subset size was assessed using ACC, MCC, and the area under the ROC‐AUC, with results averaged across RF1–RF4.

To enable statistical comparison between filter methods, two‐sided paired t tests were conducted at each subset size. For each random seed, average performance across RF1–RF4 was computed per filter method, and comparisons were made using identical seed indices. The resulting p‐values indicated whether the observed performance differences were statistically significant. The best‐performing filter method was subsequently selected for downstream analyses.

Model‐dependent feature selection (SHAP selection)

To further reduce the feature space and enhance interpretability, a model‐dependent feature selection approach based on SHAP values was applied. This method integrates model‐specific learning dynamics with post hoc feature attribution, thereby identifying features that contribute most substantially to prediction performance. The SHAP‐based selection process comprised four stages: model training, SHAP value computation and ranking, incremental training, and trend‐based determination of optimal subset.

Model training

Each base classifier was initially trained on the filtered feature subsets obtained from the best‐performing filter method (section 2.4.1). Hyperparameter optimization was conducted using Optuna, an efficient Bayesian optimization framework with 100 trials and five‐fold CV, using a fixed random seed (random_state = 0) to ensure reproducibility (Akiba et al. 2019). The configuration yielding the highest mean CV ACC was selected as the final model for each algorithm. These optimized models were then used to extract SHAP values and guide downstream feature ranking.

SHAP value computation and feature ranking

To quantify the contribution of individual features to model predictions, SHAP values were computed for each trained base model. Two levels of analysis were performed: (i) Fold‐wise analysis, in which SHAP values were calculated on the validation set of each fold during five‐fold CV and averaged across folds; and (ii) Global analysis, in which SHAP values were computed on the entire training dataset using the final model.

The choice of SHAP explainer depended on model architecture. For tree‐based models (e.g., RF, XGB, LGBM, ERT, GB), TreeExplainer was used because of its efficiency and precision. For AB, a non‐tree ensemble model, KernelExplainer was employed, initialized with the model's probabilistic prediction function. A random subset of 100 validation samples served as both the background and evaluation dataset.

Feature importance was calculated as the mean absolute SHAP value for each feature. To ensure the robustness of rankings, we assessed the agreement between fold‐wise and global SHAP attributions using multiple metrics: Spearman's rank correlation and Kendall's tau for ranking consistency; Pearson's correlation coefficient for linear agreement between SHAP magnitudes; MAE, defined as the average absolute difference in SHAP values; and relative MAE (expressed as a percentage of the global mean SHAP) to measure attribution discrepancies.

Models demonstrating high consistency across these metrics were retained and globally analyzed SHAP values from these models were used to rank features for subsequent stages. Models exhibiting substantial inconsistency were excluded from further analysis.

Incremental feature selection and performance evaluation

To investigate how feature dimensionality affected model performance, we adopted an incremental training approach guided by SHAP‐derived feature rankings. Starting with the top five features, we progressively added features in increments of five (i.e., top‐5, top‐10, …, all features). For each subset, the corresponding model was retrained using the previously optimized hyperparameters (section 2.4.2.1).

Model performance was evaluated using five‐fold stratified CV and three evaluation metrics: ACC, MCC, and ROC‐AUC. Average scores across folds were recorded, generating a performance curve as a function of the number of features included. This process enabled an empirical analysis of the relationship between feature dimensionality and classifier performance.

Trend‐based optimal feature subset selection

To determine the optimal number of features for each model, we fitted the performance curves obtained from incremental training using three complementary smoothing techniques: (i) Exponential moving average (EMA; span = 10, equivalent to 50 features), (ii) Third degree polynomial regression, in which the peak was located by solving the first derivative and confirming local maxima with the second derivative, and (iii) LOWESS with smoothing fractions of 0.2, 0.3, and 0.5 (Berger et al. 2004; Cleveland 1981).

For ACC, the feature count corresponding to the highest point on each smoothed curve was identified as a candidate subset size. Among the five estimates (EMA, polynomial, and three LOWESS values), the minimum feature count was selected to ensure a compact yet effective feature set.

Model training pipeline

The training pipeline comprised two key stages: (i) training base classifiers on optimized feature subsets, and (ii) integrating the trained models through a soft‐voting ensemble to improve predictive performance and robustness.

Base model training

Each machine learning model was trained independently on its respective feature subset, obtained through a two‐stage feature‐selection process: correlation‐based filtering followed by SHAP‐based selection. This strategy ensured that each model was exposed to a relevant, non‐redundant set of features tailored to its learning dynamics.

Hyperparameter optimization was conducted using Optuna (Akiba et al. 2019), with five‐fold stratified CV on the training set. The objective was to maximize CV ACC. The hyperparameter configuration that consistently yielded the highest average performance across folds was selected as the final setting for that model.

Ensemble method

To leverage the complementary strengths of the base models, ensemble learning was employed. A decision‐level fusion approach, implemented through soft‐voting, was applied to integrate predictions from the base models. Each base model was trained independently on its corresponding feature subset, and the predicted class probabilities were averaged to determine the final class label. This approach reduces the risk of overfitting—especially when combining diverse models—and capitalizes on the strengths of each model to improve overall performance.

Let i denote the index of a test sample, j the index of the M base classifiers, and c a candidate class (e.g., 0 or 1). Let pi,jc represent the probability assigned to class c by the jth classifier for the ith sample. For each test sample i, the ensemble prediction yi was calculated by averaging of pi,jc across all M classifiers for each class c and then selecting the class with the highest average probability. Ensemble combinations achieving the highest ACC on the training set were selected for further evaluation.

Data and statistical analysis

We analyzed SHAP values from the final base models to identify the most contributing molecular descriptors. The top five descriptors were compared between CPPs and non‐CPPs using the two‐sided Mann–Whitney U test. In addition, related descriptors were examined with the same test to provide further insights into feature‐associated characteristics. Permutation importance analysis was additionally performed to evaluate the robustness of the SHAP‐derived feature importance results. The analysis was conducted using the final ensemble model on the independent test dataset. Single feature values were randomly permuted while maintaining all remaining features unchanged, and the difference in ensemble performance relative to the baseline model was used as the permutation importance value. To reduce stochastic variability, the analysis was repeated 50 times with different random seeds, and permutation importance was evaluated using accuracy, MCC, and ROC‐AUC.

To comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed two‐step feature selection process—comprising an initial filter‐based method followed by SHAP‐based feature selection—we compared model performance at three stages: (i) before and after filter‐based selection, (ii) before and after SHAP‐based selection, and (iii) before and after the entire two‐step process. Performance was assessed using three metrics: ACC, MCC, and ROC‐AUC. Seven classifiers were trained, including CB, AB, GB, XGB, LGBM, ERT, and RF, on both the original feature set and the subsets obtained after each selection step. Hyperparameter tuning was performed with Optuna (150 trials) using internal five‐fold CV (Akiba et al. 2019). Final model performance was evaluated using both five‐fold and ten‐fold CV. Two‐sided paired t tests were then performed on fold‐wise scores to assess the statistical significance of observed differences.

All statistical analyses were performed using the scipy.stats package (version 1.10.1) in Python (version 3.8.20).
