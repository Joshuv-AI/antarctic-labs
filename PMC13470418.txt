External validation and comparative performance of ACLS, SARICA, NULL-PLEASE, BDR scores for in-hospital mortality after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest: a multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Chien-Tai Huang, Cheng-Yi Fan, Sih-Shiang Huang, Chun-Hsiang Huang, Yi-Ju Ho, Ching-Yu Chen, Tzu-Hsueh Wang, Chi-Hsin Chen, Wei-Tien Chang, Edward Pei-Chuan Huang, Chih-Wei Sung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is associated with poor outcomes, with a survival rate ranging from 4.6% to 16.4% across international registries.1, 2, 3 To guide clinical decision-making and optimize resource allocation, multiple predictive models have been developed to estimate in-hospital mortality following OHCA, each offering distinct advantages and predictive performance. These models range from the early simplified ACLS score to more recent developments, such as NULL-PLEASE, SARICA, and BDR scores.4, 5, 6, 7

In the 1980s, the ACLS score focused on core prehospital variables, including witnessed arrest, bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), emergency medical service (EMS) response time, and initial shockable rhythm.4 Contrastingly, the SARICA score uses prehospital return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), age, and initial rhythm as key variables.5 The NULL-PLEASE score further incorporates patient characteristics, such as age and comorbidities, while refining prehospital factors, including no-flow time, low-flow time and prehospital ROSC.6, 8 More recently, the BDR score introduced in-hospital variables, such as computed tomography (CT) findings and the Full Outline of UnResponsiveness (FOUR) score.7

Eventually, these models have evolved from relying solely on pre-hospital factors to integrating increasingly complex in-hospital variables, including clinician-dependent assessments. However, whether this increased complexity and the incorporation of subjective evaluations meaningfully enhances the prediction of in-hospital mortality remains unclear. Notably, although some of these scores have undergone external validation, the existing evidence remains fragmented, and no study has performed a head-to-head comparison of multiple models within a single cohort.6, 8, 9 Their relative predictive performance and clinical use in real-world settings remain unclear.

This study aimed to externally validate the ACLS, NULL-PLEASE, SARICA, and BDR scores in predicting in-hospital mortality in a single Asian cohort, thereby enabling direct comparisons and offering insights into their comparative clinical use.

Methods

Study design, setting, ethics statement

This retrospective multicenter study was conducted using data from the National Taiwan University Hospital Hsinchu and the Yunlin Branch Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest Research Database (NTUH-HYCARD). The NTUH-HYCARD consolidates information from six campuses affiliated with three hospitals; two medical centers, two regional hospitals, and two district hospitals. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Taiwan University Hospital (IRB No. 202401013RINC), and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study included all EMS-attended patients with OHCA who achieved ROSC and were recorded in the database. Patients aged <18 years, those who did not receive prehospital EMS treatment, and those transferred to other hospitals were excluded during the initial screening. To ensure the validity of the predictive scoring, patients without brain CT, venous blood gas analyses, or lactate level measurements were excluded.

The modified SARICA score

The SARICA score includes prehospital ROSC, age, and initial rhythm to predict survival until hospital discharge.5 Prehospital ROSC was defined as the presence of a palpable pulse or measurable blood pressure upon arrival at the ED. Moreover, we calculated the modified SARICA (mSARICA) score by subtracting the predicted survival probability from 1, thereby estimating the probability of in-hospital mortality.

The NULL-PLEASE score

Factors included in the NULL-PLEASE score were age, initial rhythm, witnessed arrest, prolonged no-flow time, prolonged low-flow time, ongoing resuscitation, extracardiac etiology, blood pH, and lactate levels.6, 8 Prolonged no-flow time was defined as the absence of bystander CPR before EMS arrival. Prolonged low-flow time was defined as a CPR duration >30 min. Ongoing resuscitation was defined as continued CPR upon arrival in the ED. Blood pH and lactate levels were obtained from the initial laboratory results recorded in the ED. An extracardiac cause was defined as any etiology besides “presumed cardiac,” in accordance with the 2024 Utstein OHCA registry template used in our database.10

The BDR score

Factors included in the BDR score were age, initial rhythm, evidence of hypoxic-ischemic brain injury on head CT within 24 h post-arrest, etiology of acute intoxication, and the FOUR score.7 As detailed components of the FOUR score, such as eye response, motor response, brainstem reflexes, and respiratory patterns, are not routinely documented in the ED, the earliest available consciousness assessments in the intensive care unit (ICU), along with respiratory therapy records, were used for validation.

Single imputation

Single imputation was used to address the missing values. For continuous variables, such as age, low-flow time and no-flow time, missing data were imputed using the median. For categorical variables, missing values were replaced with default assumptions; “no bystander CPR,” “no witnessed arrest,” “non-shockable initial rhythm,” and “no prehospital ROSC.” For missing components of the FOUR score, imputation was guided by the first documented Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) during hospitalization; E4M6 (spontaneous eye opening with obeying commands) was mapped to a FOUR score >5, E1M1 (no eye opening and no motor response to painful stimuli) to a score of 0, and all other cases were imputed as “null.”

Outcome

The outcome was in-hospital mortality (defined as failure to survive to discharge) among OHCA survivors.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test.11 Variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean with standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages, and comparisons were performed using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

The discriminative ability was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Calibration was performed using a decile-based plot. To assess the diagnostic performance, a comprehensive performance table was constructed reporting the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). In-hospital mortality was treated as the target condition in accordance with ILCOR standards for prognostic accuracy.12 The optimal cut-off threshold for each score was determined using the Youden index. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

The robustness of our imputation strategy was evaluated with a complete-case sensitivity analysis including only patients without missing data. Discrimination and calibration of the four prognostic models were reassessed in the complete-case cohort.

Results

Patient enrollment and screening flow

The flow diagram of patient selection for this study is presented in Fig. 1. We initially identified 1507 patients with OHCA in the database. Patients aged <18 years (n = 23), did not attend the EMS (n = 282), and were transferred from other hospitals (n = 34) were excluded. Before the analysis, an additional 140 patients were excluded because of missing data (133 missing CT images, one missing pH value, and eight missing lactic acid values). The final analysis included 1036 OHCA survivors. Discrimination of the four prognostic scores

The discriminatory performances of the four prognostic models using ROC curves were compared (Fig. 2). The BDR model demonstrated the highest AUC (0.775, 95% CI; 0.745–0.805), followed by the NULL-PLEASE (0.756, 95% CI; 0.723–0.788), mSARICA (0.746, 95% CI; 0.714–0.779), and mACLS (0.662, 95% CI; 0.627–0.697). The DeLong test p-values indicated that all three models significantly outperformed mACLS (all p < 0.001). However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the mSARICA and NULL-PLEASE (p = 0.562), mSARICA and BDR (p = 0.157), or NULL-PLEASE and BDR (p = 0.309). Discussion

We externally validated four prognostic scoring systems for predicting in-hospital mortality among OHCA survivors, using a multi-year, multicenter database. The BDR, NULL-PLEASE, and mSARICA scores demonstrated comparable overall performances and consistently outperformed the mACLS score regarding discriminatory ability. At its optimal cutoff, BDR showed the highest sensitivity and negative predictive value among the evaluated scores. However, its specificity remained moderate, limiting its use as a standalone individual-level prognostic tool.

Cautious interpretation in relation to multimodal neuroprognostication

Although the evaluated scores demonstrated fair discrimination for in-hospital mortality, their clinical role should be interpreted cautiously. Contemporary 2025 ERC–ESICM and AHA guidelines emphasize that neuroprognostication after cardiac arrest should not rely on a single index test, score, or modality, but should be based on a multimodal approach incorporating clinical examination, electrophysiology, biomarkers, and neuroimaging after major confounders have been excluded.12, 13 In the 2025 ERC–ESICM prognostication algorithm, several predictors may be recorded before 72 h after ROSC; however, conclusions regarding prognosis are made only at the time of clinical prognostic assessment at 72 h, and poor outcome is considered likely only when at least two concordant unfavorable predictors are present. Therefore, the scores evaluated in the present study should not be considered alternatives to guideline-recommended multimodal neuroprognostication.

This distinction is particularly important because, at their optimal thresholds, the evaluated scores achieved only moderate specificity despite acceptable AUCs and PPVs. Such performance is insufficient for decisions with irreversible consequences, especially withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (WLST). Accordingly, score-derived estimates should not be communicated to families as deterministic individual predictions. Instead, family discussions should emphasize uncertainty, the need for serial reassessment, and the planned multimodal prognostication pathway. The potential role of these scores, if any, is limited to adjunctive population-level risk stratification, benchmarking, research adjustment, or preliminary resource planning while guideline-recommended assessments are pending or unavailable.

BDR: heavy reliance on post-arrest indicators

Several factors may explain the superior discriminative performance of the BDR scores. A notable distinction is the inclusion of subjective clinician assessment. The FOUR score, developed to replace the verbal component of the GCS and incorporate brainstem reflexes and respiratory patterns, is a valuable tool for evaluating coma severity in critically ill patients14 and has shown excellent prognostic accuracy and inter-rater reliability compared to the GCS.15, 16, 17 Another important element of the BDR score is the head CT findings. Loss of gray-white matter differentiation or a decreased gray-white ratio (GWR) indicates cytotoxic edema from hypoxic injury and may reflect prolonged low-flow or no-flow times.18, 19 However, the 2025 ERC and AHA guidelines do not define the optimal time for CT to assess GWR, resulting in variability in practice.12, 13 Here, the final analysis excluded 133 patients (11.3%) who did not undergo head CT within 24 h post-arrest. Calculating the BDR score requires multidisciplinary collaboration and substantial medical resources, which typically delays its use until ICU admission.7 Furthermore, because the BDR score was originally developed to predict brain death, its use may influence WLST decisions, potentially affecting the observed mortality outcomes.

Complete-case sensitivity analysis yielded similar discrimination performance. The lower mortality observed in the complete-case cohort suggests that missingness may be related to patient severity, likely excluding higher-risk and unstable patients who may have been unable to complete testing or documentation during ongoing resuscitation. Notably, even within this lower-risk cohort, the BDR, NULL-PLEASE, and SARICA scores continued to demonstrate superior discriminative performance compared with the ACLS score. Such findings reduce concerns regarding selection bias due to missing data.

Other considerations: timing and population differences

Patient population characteristics must be carefully considered in applying prognostic scores, as they may significantly impact predictive accuracy. The sequential use of prognostic tools, from early stage ACLS and SARICA scores at ED arrival to later-stage NULL-PLEASE and BDR scores, which require more detailed data, may optimize their clinical use after resuscitation. Although additional clinical data would theoretically improve the prognostic accuracy, the SARICA score achieved comparable performance using only three variables. This may be owing to the strong prognostic significance of prehospital ROSC, observed in 14.9% of patients, with a survival rate of 69.5% and odds ratio for unfavorable outcomes of 0.14 (95% CI; 0.10–0.20).20 Conversely, patients without prehospital ROSC often undergo prolonged resuscitation, implying more severe underlying conditions and prolonged low-flow states.

The NULL-PLEASE score reflects the physiological burden of cardiac arrest and duration of resuscitation, making it suitable for early prognostication immediately following ROSC. Initial serum pH and lactate levels reflect systemic hypoxia and offer prognostic insight, especially in patients without ROSC.21, 22 However, relying on single-point laboratory values may be insufficient to guide complex clinical decisions.23 The serial monitoring and reassessment of these biomarkers may provide comprehensive prognostic strategies.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, owing to its retrospective design and the aim of externally validating multiple prognostic scores, missing data were inevitable, and missingness was unlikely to be completely at random and may have been related to disease severity. Our conservative imputation approach may have inflated the prognostic scores, overestimated the risk, and reduced the specificity, as reflected in our findings. Second, although the NTUH-HYCARD comprised hospitals with varying care levels, all data were obtained from a single healthcare system, potentially limiting generalizability to other healthcare settings. Taiwan’s National Health Insurance, a universal single-payer system, may limit generalizability of our findings.24 Third, selection bias may have affected validation of the BDR score. Patients who survived long enough to receive intensive care and undergo head CT and formal neurologic assessment were more likely to have sufficient data for BDR calculation. Fourth, the timing of score-component measurements was not fully standardized. Blood gas analysis, lactate measurement, neurologic assessment, and brain CT may have been performed at different time points after ROSC, and score performance may therefore vary according to measurement timing. Fifth, self-fulfilling prophecy bias cannot be excluded. Although these scores were not used as formal triggers for WLST, clinicians were aware of score components, including neurologic examination, CT findings, pH, and lactate levels, which may have influenced treatment decisions and affected observed mortality.

Conclusions

The BDR, NULL-PLEASE, and mSARICA scores demonstrated fair discriminatory ability for in-hospital mortality after OHCA. However, their diagnostic performance at clinically practical thresholds was insufficient to reliably identify either non-survivors or survivors for individual-level prognostic decision-making. Their role should therefore be limited to adjunctive risk stratification, benchmarking, or research adjustment, and clinicians should interpret score results cautiously in the context of serial, guideline-recommended multimodal assessment.
