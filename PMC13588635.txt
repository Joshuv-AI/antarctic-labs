Reply to Zhang and Lou: beta-blockers after myocardial infarction should be guided by indication, ejection fraction, and contemporary evidence
Authors: Borja Ibanez, Xavier Rossello
Published: 2026

We thank Zhang and Lou for their thoughtful comments on our landmark analysis from the REBOOT (Treatment with Beta-Blockers after Myocardial Infarction without Reduced Ejection Fraction) trial.1 We fully agree that the generalization of REBOOT, and of other contemporary randomized trials testing beta-blockers after myocardial infarction (MI), must be restricted to the entry criteria of their study populations. These trials systematically excluded patients with an established indication for beta-blocker therapy for reasons other than post-infarction secondary prevention, including patients with heart failure, those with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and those in whom the treating physician considered beta-blockers necessary for another reason, such as rate control in atrial fibrillation, arrhythmias, uncontrolled hypertension, or other specific cardiovascular conditions.

This distinction is clinically important. REBOOT should not be interpreted as supporting discontinuation of beta-blockers in all patients after MI. Rather, it challenges the routine initiation or continuation of beta-blockers when they are prescribed solely because of a recent MI in otherwise stable, revascularized patients without heart failure, reduced LVEF, or another active indication. In this regard, we agree with Zhang and Lou that reassessment of the original indication for beta-blocker therapy should be the first step in any de-escalation strategy.

The low prevalence of atrial fibrillation in REBOOT2 and in similar post-infarction trials3 is also informative. It likely reflects, at least in part, the fact that many patients with atrial fibrillation already have an independent indication for beta-blockers to achieve rate control and were therefore not eligible for randomization. This reinforces the need to distinguish beta-blockers used as post-infarction secondary prevention from beta-blockers used for another therapeutic purpose.

Hypertension deserves specific consideration. Historically, blood-pressure control has been one of the common reasons for long-term beta-blocker use after MI. However, beta-blockers are not generally considered first-line antihypertensive therapy in the absence of another compelling indication. Their use for blood-pressure control after MI was largely justified by the assumption that previous MI itself constituted such an indication. In the light of REBOOT and other contemporary randomized evidence, we anticipate that the use of beta-blockers solely for hypertension in post-infarction patients without reduced LVEF will decrease substantially. Of course, withdrawal should be accompanied by optimization of alternative antihypertensive therapy when required.

We also agree with Zhang and Lou that LVEF above 40% should not be treated as a single therapeutic category. The subgroup of patients in REBOOT who appeared to benefit the most from beta-blockers was those with mildly reduced LVEF, between 40% and 49%. This observation is supported by the individual patient data meta-analysis of contemporary trials,4 including patients from REBOOT, BETAMI-DANBLOCK, and CAPITAL-RCT, which showed that beta-blocker therapy was associated with a reduction in the composite of death, MI, or heart failure in patients with recent MI and mildly reduced LVEF. This population may therefore still be one in which routine beta-blocker therapy should be considered.

In contrast, the evidence is particularly robust for patients with truly preserved LVEF. An individual patient data meta-analysis including patients with LVEF of at least 50% from REBOOT, REDUCE-AMI, BETAMI, DANBLOCK, and CAPITAL-RCT showed no reduction in death, MI, or heart failure with beta-blocker therapy in patients without another indication for these drugs.3 The lack of treatment effect was also consistent across individual components of the primary composite endpoint. Thus, for post-infarction patients with LVEF ≥50%, treated according to contemporary standards of care and without another indication for beta-blockers, the available randomized evidence does not support routine beta-blocker therapy.

We acknowledge that categorizing patients by LVEF is inherently imperfect, particularly near clinically used thresholds (e.g. 50%). A patient with an LVEF of 49% early after MI may subsequently improve spontaneously or after complete revascularization and optimized medical therapy. Whether beta-blockers should be continued or withdrawn in such patients after LVEF recovery remains uncertain and has not been directly answered by the available evidence. However, given the consistency and scale of the recently completed randomized trials and individual patient data meta-analyses, it is difficult to envisage that future large randomized trials will be conducted specifically to resolve this narrow borderline scenario. The current evidence is therefore likely to remain the best available basis for clinical decision-making.

We would also nuance the concern that beta-blocker withdrawal necessarily requires a prolonged or complex tapering pathway in all patients. In REBOOT, approximately 10% of participants were already receiving beta-blockers before randomization for the index MI.2 In those allocated to the no-beta-blocker group, treatment was stopped at hospital discharge, a period of particular vulnerability after MI. Importantly, this strategy was not associated with an excess of adverse events in a broad and clinically relevant composite endpoint including death, reinfarction, or heart failure hospitalization, even within the first 3 months after discharge.5 These findings suggest that abrupt discontinuation at discharge can be safe in appropriately selected patients without heart failure, reduced LVEF, or another active indication for beta-blocker therapy.

Trials evaluating withdrawal later after MI provide complementary evidence. ABYSS6 and SMART-DECISION7 addressed a different clinical question from REBOOT: whether beta-blockers can be stopped in patients with chronic coronary syndrome who had already tolerated long-term therapy post-MI. In ABYSS, patients had been treated for a median of approximately 3 years after MI. Interruption did not meet the prespecified criterion for noninferiority, but the excess appeared to be driven mainly by ‘soft’ cardiovascular hospitalizations rather than by hard clinical events such as death, recurrent MI, or heart failure.6 In SMART-DECISION, which included stable patients treated with beta-blockers beyond the first year after MI, discontinuation was noninferior to continuation for the composite of all-cause death, recurrent MI, or hospitalization for heart failure.7 Collectively, these data suggest that many stable post-MI patients who have been receiving beta-blockers for years solely because of a prior MI, and who have no other indication such as heart failure or arrhythmia requiring rate control, can discontinue beta-blockers without an expected increase in hard clinical events.

The association between higher beta-blocker doses and worse outcomes in the chronic phase of REBOOT should be interpreted cautiously, as dose was not randomized, and therefore the analysis was not designed to establish causality. Nevertheless, a similar direction of effect has been observed in the large meta-analysis of patients with preserved-LVEF.3 Although speculative, these findings might suggest that higher beta-blocker doses may be unnecessary or even deleterious in patients with preserved LVEF. This should not be considered a definitive conclusion, but it reinforces the need to avoid automatic dose escalation in patients without heart failure or another clear indication.

Taken together, the available evidence supports a practical and indication-guided approach. Beta-blockers should be continued in patients with another clear indication, especially heart failure, reduced LVEF, or heart rate control. They should be considered in patients with mildly reduced LVEF after MI, recognizing that the supporting evidence is less extensive than for the preserved-LVEF population. In contrast, in stable post-infarction patients with LVEF ≥50% and no active indication, routine long-term beta-blocker therapy is no longer supported by contemporary randomized evidence.

The implications are substantial. Millions of patients worldwide have received beta-blockers for years for the sole reason of a prior MI. Given the large and consistent randomized evidence now available, particularly from individual patient data meta-analyses,3,4 it is highly unlikely that another large trial will substantially change the overall conclusion. The key message is not universal discontinuation, but a shift away from MI alone as a sufficient reason for beta-blocker therapy in patients with preserved LVEF.
