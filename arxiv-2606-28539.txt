Extremal ranks of unlabeled multifurcating rooted trees in a bijective encoding by the positive integers
Michael R Doboli, Alessandra R P Maranca, Noah A Rosenberg

Abstract




Abstract.
Maranca & Rosenberg
10
devised a ranking scheme for unlabeled multifurcating rooted trees, in which the trees are bijectively associated with the positive integers. Here, generalizing earlier results for bifurcating trees, we determine, for trees with a fixed number of leaves, which multifurcating trees obtain the maximal and minimal ranks. We identify these maximizing and minimizing trees for each of two sets of unlabeled multifurcating rooted trees: strictly
k
-furcating trees, in which each internal node possesses exactly
k
descendants, and at-most-
k
-furcating trees, in which internal nodes possess at least 2 and at most
k
descendants. In both scenarios, we find that a tree that can be regarded as maximally balanced attains the minimal rank, and a minimally balanced tree attains the maximal rank. We deduce recurrences for the maximal and minimal rank for trees with fixed numbers of leaves in both the strictly
k
-furcating and at-most-
k
-furcating cases. The maximal rank on
(n-1)(k-1)+1
leaves grows with
(k!)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\beta_{k}^{(k^{n})}
in the strictly
k
-furcating case, and the maximal rank on
n
leaves grows with
(k!)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\gamma_{k}^{(k^{n})}
in the at-most-
k
-furcating case, where
\beta_{k}>1
and
\gamma_{k}>1
are constants that depend on the value of
k
. We show that
\beta_{k}
decreases as the value of
k
increases, and that
\gamma_{k}>\beta_{k}
for
k\geqslant 3
. The results contribute to the use of tree encodings for empirical characterization of phylogenies and measurement of tree balance.

mathematical phylogenetics, recurrences, unlabeled trees

Introduction

Encodings are a central topic of mathematical phylogenetics
7
;
13
. Given a class of trees, to what extent can a tree from the class be encoded by features such as quartets, splits, or pairwise distances between leaves—or by a single positive integer?

For the set of unlabeled rooted binary trees, a scheme introduced by Colijn & Plazzotta
3
bijectively associates positive integers with trees. The single-leaf tree is assigned rank 1. Each subsequent tree is then associated with an ordered pair
(j_{1},j_{2})
,
j_{1}\geqslant j_{2}\geqslant 1
, where
j_{1}
is the rank of the left subtree and
j_{2}
is the rank of the right subtree; “left” and “right” are used for convenience, but trees are non-plane. The rank of the tree is then computed by finding the position of
(j_{1},j_{2})
in the dictionary ordering of ordered pairs
(a,b)
satisfying
a\geqslant b\geqslant 1
:
(1,1),(2,1),(2,2),(3,1),(3,2)
, and so on. For example, the tree with rank 3 is the tree associated with ordered pair
(2,1)
, or the caterpillar tree on 3 leaves. More generally, we can directly compute that the tree associated with
(j_{1},j_{2})
has rank
j_{1}(j_{1}-1)/2+1+j_{2}
. This map between trees and the positive integers is in fact bijective. Examples of the ranks of small bifurcating trees appear in Table
1
.

In a further analysis of this bijection, Rosenberg
12
identified the trees with the smallest and largest rank among all trees with a given number of leaves. The tree with the smallest rank on
n
leaves is a specific highly balanced tree, with rank
a_{n}
satisfying
a_{n}=\frac{1}{2}a_{\lceil n/2\rceil}(a_{\lceil n/2\rceil}-1)+1+a_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
,
a_{1}=1
. The tree with the maximal rank is the caterpillar, the minimally balanced tree, with rank
b_{n}
satisfying
b_{n}=\frac{1}{2}b_{n-1}(b_{n-1}-1)+2
,
b_{1}=1
. A curious relation
a_{2^{n}}+1=b_{n+2}
holds between the minimal and maximal ranks for all
n\geqslant 0
.

Rosenberg
12
also studied the asymptotic growth of the smallest and largest ranks among
n
-leaf trees, showing
b_{n}\sim 2\beta^{(2^{n})}
, where
\beta\approx 1.05653
. An immediate corollary is
a_{2^{n}}\sim 2\alpha^{(2^{n})}
, where
\alpha=\beta^{4}\approx 1.24602
. Doboli et al.
6
then found
a_{n}\sim 2[2^{P(\log_{2}n)}]^{n}
, where
P
is a 1-periodic function satisfying
1.24602<2^{P(t)}<1.33429
for all
t
. This result improved upon an earlier upper bound
a_{n}<(\frac{3}{2})^{n}
of Rosenberg
12
.

Maranca & Rosenberg
10
have generalized the ranking scheme of Colijn & Plazzotta
3
to provide a scheme for ranking unlabeled rooted
multifurcating
trees. In the Maranca–Rosenberg scheme for
strictly
k
-furcating
trees, where each internal node has exactly
k
descendants, we associate to each tree a
k
-tuple
(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k})
, where
j_{1}\geqslant j_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant j_{k}\geqslant 1
and
j_{i}
denotes the rank of the
i
th subtree (canonically ordered from left to right). Each tuple is then associated to a positive integer corresponding to its position in the dictionary ordering of
k
-tuples
(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k})
, where
j_{1}\geqslant j_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant j_{k}\geqslant 1
. For example, with
k=3
, after rank 1 is assigned to the 1-leaf tree, the dictionary ordering begins
(1,1,1),(2,1,1),(2,2,1),(2,2,2),(3,1,1)
. This generalized scheme provides a bijection between the strictly
k
-furcating trees and the positive integers.

Maranca & Rosenberg
10
also provided a ranking scheme for
at-most-
k
-furcating
trees, where each internal node has at least two and at most
k
descendants. To accommodate the fact that the root might have fewer than
k
immediate descendant nodes, each tree is associated with a tuple
(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k})
, where
j_{1}\geqslant j_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant j_{k}\geqslant 0
and
j_{1}\geqslant j_{2}\geqslant 1
. The latter condition encodes the fact that each internal node must possess at least two descendant nodes. Each tuple is then associated to a positive integer corresponding to its position in the dictionary ordering of
k
-tuples
(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k})
, where
j_{1}\geqslant j_{2},\ldots\geqslant j_{k}\geqslant 0
and
j_{1}\geqslant j_{2}\geqslant 1
. For example, for
k=3
, after the initial 1-leaf tree, the dictionary ordering of these tuples begins
(1,1,0),(1,1,1),(2,1,0),(2,1,1),(2,2,0),(2,2,1),(2,2,2),(3,1,0)
. This labeling scheme provides a bijection between at-most-
k
-furcating trees and positive integers.

In this study, we conduct analogous work to Rosenberg
12
on the maximal and minimal rank, but for the multifurcation schemes of Maranca & Rosenberg
10
. We first reframe the schemes of Maranca & Rosenberg
10
by providing a notion of lexicographically ordering tuples. We use this notion to prove a useful relation between two trees’ ranks and the ranks of their subtrees (
Section

2
). Next, we find the strictly
k
-furcating trees with maximal (
Section

3.1
) and minimal rank (
Section

3.2
). We also compute a recursive formula describing the maximal rank of strictly
k
-furcating trees on
(n-1)(k-1)+1
leaves,
b_{n}
, and the minimal rank of strictly
k
-furcating trees on
(n-1)(k-1)+1
leaves,
a_{n}
. We explore the asymptotic growth of
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
(
Section

3.3
). We then address these same questions in the at-most-
k
-furcating case (
Section

4
), arriving at similar answers. We conclude with a discussion (
Section

5
).

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Lexicographical ordering

We first recall a notion of lexicographically ordering
k
-tuples of non-negative integers.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A_{k}=\{(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}:a_{1}\geqslant a_{2}\ldots\geqslant a_{k}\geqslant 0\}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{k}
for
k\geqslant 1
. For
X,Y\in A_{k}
with
X=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})
and
Y=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{k})
, we say that
X\leqslant_{D}Y
if either of the following holds:

(i)

x_{i}=y_{i}
for all
i
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
.

(ii)

x_{i}\neq y_{i}
for some
i
, and if
j
is the smallest positive integer such that
x_{j}\neq y_{j}
, then
x_{j}<y_{j}
.

If
X,Y\in A_{k}
satisfy
X\leqslant_{D}Y
and
X\neq Y
, then we can also write
X<_{D}Y
.

2.2
Strictly
k
-furcating trees

For
k\geqslant 2
, we use the ranking scheme of Maranca & Rosenberg
10
for strictly
k
-furcating trees. Denote
T_{k}
as the set of strictly
k
-furcating trees, with the single-leaf tree also included. We denote
f:T_{k}\to\mathbb{Z}^{+}
as the map sending each strictly
k
-furcating tree to its
rank
.

For all trees
t
in
T_{k}
other than the single-leaf tree, the
canonical order
of
t
is the ordering of subtrees
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{k}
at the root such that
f(t_{1})\geqslant f(t_{2})\geqslant\ldots\geqslant f(t_{k})
. In a planar representation of
t
, it is convenient to represent the canonical order from left to right, with
t_{1}
as the leftmost and
t_{k}
as the rightmost subtree.

For a given tree
t\in T_{k}
(other than the single-leaf tree), define
K(t)=\big(f(t_{1}),f(t_{2}),\ldots,f(t_{k})\big)
, where
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{k}
is the canonical order of the subtrees of
t
at the root. By definition of the canonical order,
f(t_{1})\geqslant f(t_{2})\ldots\geqslant f(t_{k})
.

Definition 4.2 of Maranca & Rosenberg
10
establishes that if
K(t)=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})
and
t\in T_{k}
, then:

\displaystyle f(t)=2+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\binom{x_{k-i+1}+i-2}{i}.

(1)

We prove a lemma relating the rank of a strictly
k
-furcating tree with the lexicographical order of the canonical ordering. The proof is in Appendix
A
. This lemma confirms the intuition that the ordering of trees in the canonical ordering is the same as the ordering induced by the tree rank. It is used extensively in
Section

3.1
and
Section

3.2
to compare the ranks of trees by instead comparing the lexicographical ordering of the trees’ canonical orderings.

Lemma 2.2
.

For two trees
t_{1},t_{2}\in T_{k}
,
K(t_{1})\leqslant_{D}K(t_{2})
if and only if
f(t_{1})\leqslant f(t_{2})
. Additionally,
K(t_{1})=K(t_{2})
if and only if
t_{1}=t_{2}
.

2.3
At-most-
k
-furcating trees

We extend our results from
Section

2.2
to at-most-
k
-furcating trees (including the single-leaf tree). Let
T_{k}^{\ast}
be the set of at-most-
k
-furcating trees. We denote
f:T_{k}^{\ast}\to\mathbb{Z}^{+}
as the map sending each at-most-
k
-furcating tree to its rank, as in Definition 5.2 of
10
. By Theorem 5.3 of
10
, this map is a bijection. Note that we use the same symbol
f
as in the map
f:T_{k}\to\mathbb{Z}^{+}
in
Section

2.2
; the version of
f
will be clear from the context.

For all trees
t
in
T_{k}^{\ast}
other than the single-leaf tree, we define the
canonical order
of
t
to be the ordering of subtrees
t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{k}
at the root such that
f(t_{1})\geqslant f(t_{2})\geqslant\dots\geqslant f(t_{k})
. For any tree
t\in T_{k}^{\ast},
define
K(t)=\big(f(t_{1}),f(t_{2}),\ldots,f(t_{k})\big)
, where
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{k}
is the canonical order of the subtrees of
t
at the root. By definition of the canonical order,
f(t_{1})\geqslant f(t_{2})\ldots\geqslant f(t_{k})
. The key difference in the at-most-
k
-furcating case compared to the strictly
k
-furcating case in
Section

2.2
is that the at-most-
k
-furcating case allows
f(t_{k})=0
(i.e., if the subtree
t_{k}
has no leaves) for
k\geqslant 3
.

By Definition 5.2 of Maranca & Rosenberg
10
, if
t\in T_{k}^{\ast}
and
K(t)=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})
, then

\displaystyle f(t)=-x_{1}+1+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\binom{x_{k-i+1}+i-1}{i}.

(2)

We show an analogous result to
Lemma

2.2
for at-most-
k
-furcating trees. The proof is in Appendix
B
. This lemma confirms that the lexicographic ordering induced by canonical ordering is the same as the ordering induced by the tree rank, and we use it in
Section

4.1
and
Section

4.2
to compare tree ranks.

Lemma 2.3
.

For two trees
t_{1},t_{2}\in T_{k}^{\ast}
,
K(t_{1})\leqslant_{D}K(t_{2})
if and only if

f(t_{1})\leqslant f(t_{2})
. Additionally,
K(t_{1})=K(t_{2})
if and only if
f(t_{1})=f(t_{2})
.

3
Strictly
k
-furcating trees

3.1
Maximal rank

We now identify the strictly
k
-furcating tree that attains maximal rank among trees with a fixed number of leaves and find a recurrence to compute this rank. Unlike for strictly bifurcating trees (
k=2
), for
k\geqslant 3
, not every positive integer can be the number of leaves of a strictly
k
-furcating tree.

Denote by
m:T_{k}\to\mathbb{Z}
the number of leaves possessed by a strictly
k
-furcating tree. The number of leaves for a tree
t\in T_{k}
satisfies
m(t)\equiv 1\pmod{k-1}
. This result can be seen inductively. A strictly
k
-furcating tree is formed by successive application of a step of replacing a leaf by an internal node with
k
descendant leaves. This step increases the number of leaves by
k-1
, so that beginning with a single leaf, each step produces a number of leaves congruent to 1 modulo
(k-1)
.

Because the number of leaves possessed by a tree in
T_{k}
is congruent to 1 modulo
(k-1)
, we modify the notation from
12
for the minimum and maximum. In particular, for
n\geqslant 1
, we denote by
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
the minimal and maximal rank, respectively, across trees in
T_{k}
with
(n-1)(k-1)+1
leaves. Similarly, we denote by
z_{n}
the tree in
T_{k}
with rank
a_{n}
and by
Z_{n}
the tree in
T_{k}
with rank
b_{n}
. This notation accords with
12
for the case of
k=2
.

The strategy is to construct a collection of trees
\{Z_{n}^{\ast}\}
and to show via induction that
Z_{n}=Z_{n}^{\ast}
. Let
\{Z_{n}^{\ast}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
be the collection of trees defined as follows:
(i)
Z_{1}^{\ast}
is the tree with exactly one leaf;
(ii) for
n>1
,
Z_{n}^{\ast}
is the tree that has
Z_{n-1}^{\ast},Z_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,Z_{1}^{\ast}
as its
k
subtrees descended from the root. Note that
Z_{n-1}^{\ast}
has
(n-2)(k-1)+1
leaves, and
Z_{n}^{\ast}
has
(n-1)(k-1)+1
leaves.

We show that the rank
f(Z_{n}^{\ast})
is strictly increasing with
n
.

Proposition 3.1
.

f(Z_{n}^{\ast})>f(Z_{n-1}^{\ast})
for all
n\geqslant 2
.

Proof.

By the recursive definition of
Z_{n}^{\ast},
we know that
K(Z_{n}^{\ast})=\big(f(Z_{n-1}^{\ast}),1,\ldots,1\big)
. We have
f(Z_{1}^{\ast})=1
, and by
Equation

1
, for
n>1
,

\displaystyle f(Z_{n}^{\ast})

\displaystyle=2+\binom{f(Z_{n-1}^{\ast})+k-2}{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\binom{1+i-2}{i}=2+\binom{f(Z_{n-1}^{\ast})+k-2}{k}.

In Appendix
C
, we prove that
2+\binom{x+k-2}{k}>x
for all nonnegative integers
x
, implying the desired inequality
f(Z_{n}^{\ast})=2+\binom{f(Z_{n-1}^{\ast})+k-2}{k}>f(Z_{n-1}^{\ast})
.

\square

We use Proposition
3.1
and the recursive construction of
Z_{n}^{\ast}
to show that
Z_{n}=Z_{n}^{\ast}
, demonstrating that the tree with maximal rank among strictly
k
-furcating trees with
(n-1)(k-1)+1
leaves is exactly
Z_{n}^{\ast}.

Theorem 3.2
.

For
n\geqslant 1
, the strictly
k
-furcating tree
Z_{n}
with maximal rank is
Z_{n}=Z_{n}^{\ast}
.

Proof.

We induct on
n
. The base case
n=1
is trivial, as only one tree has a single leaf:
Z_{1}=Z_{1}^{\ast}
.

For the inductive hypothesis, suppose that
Z_{\ell}=Z_{\ell}^{\ast}
for each
\ell
with
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant n-1
. In the tree
Z_{n}
, let
j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}
be the subtrees, in canonical order. The idea is to substitute the leftmost tree
j_{1}
with the tree
Z_{[(m(j_{1})+k-2]/(k-1)}
that attains the maximal rank on
m(j_{1})
leaves. Trees
j_{1}
and
Z_{[(m(j_{1})+k-2]/(k-1)}
have the same number of leaves, because
m(Z_{[(m(j_{1})+k-2]/(k-1)})=(k-1)(\frac{m(j_{1})+k-2}{k-1}-1)+1=m(j_{1})
. The substitution does not break the canonical order, because the rank of
Z_{[(m(j_{1})+k-2]/(k-1)}
is at least as large as that of
j_{1}
. It produces the following chain of inequalities:

\displaystyle K(Z_{n})=\Big(f(j_{1}),f(j_{2}),\ldots,f(j_{k})\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant_{D}\Big(f(Z_{\frac{m(j_{1})+k-2}{k-1}}),f(j_{2}),\ldots,f(j_{k})\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big(f(Z^{\ast}_{\frac{m(j_{1})+k-2}{k-1}}),f(j_{2}),\ldots,f(j_{k})\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant_{D}\Big(f(Z^{\ast}_{n-1}),f(Z^{\ast}_{1}),\ldots,f(Z^{\ast}_{1})\Big)

\displaystyle=K(Z_{n}^{\ast}).

The second equality follows from the inductive hypothesis. The last inequality follows from
Proposition

3.1
and the fact that
\frac{m(j_{1})+k-2}{k-1}\leqslant n-1
. By definition of
Z_{n}
,
f(Z_{n})\geqslant f(Z_{n}^{\ast})
, so by
Lemma

2.2
,
K(Z_{n})\geqslant_{D}K(Z_{n}^{\ast})
. Therefore,
K(Z_{n})=K(Z_{n}^{\ast})
, which by
Lemma

2.2
means that
f(Z_{n})=f(Z_{n}^{\ast})
, so that
Z_{n}=Z_{n}^{\ast}
because
f
is a bijection.

\square

With
Theorem

3.2
in hand, we have obtained the maximal-rank tree with
(n-1)(k-1)+1
leaves. For its rank, because
Z_{n}=Z_{n}^{\ast}
and
f(Z_{n}^{\ast})=2+\binom{f(Z_{n-1}^{\ast})+k-2}{k}
for
n>1
, we conclude that

\displaystyle b_{n}=2+\binom{b_{n-1}+k-2}{k},

(3)

with
b_{1}=1
. Note that setting

yields Theorem 9 of
12

is a generalized version of the caterpillar tree, which also agrees with Corollary 10 of

3.2
Minimal rank

Next, we identify the strictly
k
-furcating trees with minimal rank among trees with
(n-1)(k-1)+1
leaves. We employ a similar strategy: we construct trees
\{z_{n}^{\ast}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and use the properties of
z_{n}^{\ast}
and
z_{n}
to prove that
z_{n}=z_{n}^{\ast}
. The construction proceeds as follows:
(i)
z_{1}^{\ast}
is the tree with one leaf.
(ii) For
n>1
,
z_{n}^{\ast}
is the tree whose leftmost
n-2+k-k\lceil\frac{n-2}{k}\rceil
subtrees are
z_{\lceil(n-2)/k\rceil+1}^{\ast}
, and whose rightmost
-n+2+k\lceil\frac{n-2}{k}\rceil
subtrees are
z_{\lceil(n-2)/k\rceil}^{\ast}.

We first verify that
z_{n}^{\ast}
has
(n-1)(k-1)+1
leaves, as it is not obvious from the definition.

Lemma 3.3
.

m(z_{n}^{\ast})=(n-1)(k-1)+1
for
n\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction. In the base case,
m(z_{1}^{\ast})=(1-1)(k-1)+1=1
. For the inductive hypothesis, assume that
z_{j}^{\ast}
has
(j-1)(k-1)+1
leaves for all
j
,
1\leqslant j<n
. Now count the leaves of
z_{n}^{\ast}
using the recursive definition and the inductive hypothesis:

\displaystyle m(z_{n}^{\ast})

\displaystyle=\bigg(n-2+k-k\bigg\lceil\frac{n-2}{k}\bigg\rceil\bigg)m(z^{\ast}_{\lceil\frac{n-2}{k}\rceil+1})+\bigg(-n+2+k\bigg\lceil\frac{n-2}{k}\bigg\rceil\bigg)m(z^{\ast}_{\lceil\frac{n-2}{k}\rceil})

Lemma 4.3
.

m(y_{n}^{\ast})=n
for
n\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

We induct on
n
. For the base case of
n=1
,
y_{1}^{\ast}
has one leaf. For the inductive hypothesis, suppose that
y_{\ell}^{\ast}
has
\ell
leaves for all
\ell
,
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant n-1
. By the recursive definition of
y_{n}^{\ast}
,

\displaystyle m(y_{n}^{\ast})

\displaystyle=\bigg(n-k\bigg\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\bigg\rfloor\bigg)m(y^{\ast}_{\lceil\frac{n}{k}\rceil})+\bigg(k-n+k\bigg\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\bigg\rfloor\bigg)m(y^{\ast}_{\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\rfloor})

\displaystyle=\bigg(n-k\bigg\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\bigg\rfloor\bigg)\bigg\lceil\frac{n}{k}\bigg\rceil+\bigg(k-n+k\bigg\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\bigg\rfloor\bigg)\bigg\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\bigg\rfloor

\displaystyle=n\bigg\lceil\frac{n}{k}\bigg\rceil-k\bigg\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\bigg\rfloor\bigg\lceil\frac{n}{k}\bigg\rceil+k\bigg\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\bigg\rfloor-n\bigg\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\bigg\rfloor+k\bigg\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\bigg\rfloor^{2}.

If

If

, then

Proposition 4.4
.

For

,
f(y^{\ast}_{n})>f(y^{\ast}_{n-1})

Proof.

We induct on

. In the base case of

,

is the tree that has two copies of

as its non-empty subtrees. In particular,

2
, we conclude that

, so that

For the inductive hypothesis, suppose

for

, with

. It follows that

\displaystyle K(y_{n}^{\ast})

\displaystyle=\Big(\overbrace{f(y^{\ast}_{\lceil n/k\rceil}),\ldots,f(y^{\ast}_{\lceil n/k\rceil})}^{n-k\lfloor n/k\rfloor\text{ times }},\overbrace{f(y^{\ast}_{\lfloor n/k\rfloor}),\ldots,f(y^{\ast}_{\lfloor n/k\rfloor})}^{k-n+k\lfloor n/k\rfloor\text{ times }}\Big).

\displaystyle=\Big(\overbrace{f(y^{\ast}_{\lceil(n-1)/k\rceil}),\ldots,f(y^{\ast}_{\lceil(n-1)/k\rceil})}^{n-1-k\lfloor(n-1)/k\rfloor\text{ times }},\overbrace{f(y^{\ast}_{\lfloor(n-1)/k\rfloor}),\ldots,f(y^{\ast}_{\lfloor(n-1)/k\rfloor})}^{k-n+1+k\lfloor(n-1)/k\rfloor\text{ times }}\Big).

Discussion

We have obtained the minimal and maximal ranks of unlabeled multifurcating rooted trees in the bijective schemes of Maranca & Rosenberg
10
that encode such trees with the positive integers. The results generalize corresponding results of
12
on minimal and maximal ranks in the bifurcating case.

For strictly
k
-furcating trees,
k\geqslant 2
, we obtained a recursive equation for the maximal (
Equation

) and minimal ranks among trees with
(n-1)(k-1)+1
leaves,
n\geqslant 1
(

; the growth follows

(

), where

is a constant that depends on

(

). The minimal rank among trees with

leaves is one less than the maximal rank among trees with

leaves (

).

For at-most-

-furcating trees, we have obtained analogous results for the maximal (
Equation

10
) and minimal ranks among trees with
n
leaves (
Equation

11
). Asymptotically, the maximal rank grows superexponentially with
n
; the growth follows
B_{n}\sim(k!)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}\gamma_{k}^{(k^{n})}
(
Theorem

4.7
), where
\gamma_{k}
depends on
k
(
Table

9
). The minimal rank among trees with
k^{n}
leaves is one less than the maximal rank among trees with
n+2
leaves (
Proposition

4.6
).

The at-most-
k
-furcating trees include the strictly
k
-furcating trees, and we have observed numerically that for small
k\geqslant 3
, the growth constants satisfy
\gamma_{k}>\beta_{k}
(

,
Table

), so that the growth of the number of at-most-
k
-furcating trees with
n
leaves empirically exceeds the growth of the number of strictly
k
-furcating trees with
(n-1)(k-1)+1
leaves for
k\geqslant 3
. Indeed, Theorem
4.10
demonstrates that
\gamma_{k}>\beta_{k}
for
k\geqslant 3
. The constants

decrease with

), and we conjecture that the constants
\gamma_{k}
decrease with

as well (
4.8
).

In both types of multifurcating trees, tree ranks can serve as measures of tree balance. In particular, with a fixed number of leaves, a highly balanced tree has minimal rank, and a highly imbalanced tree has maximal rank. This phenomenon was observed by
12
in the bifurcating case, so that a measure
[\log f(t)-\log a_{m(t)}]/[\log b_{m(t)}-\log a_{m(t)}]
can serve as an index that measures increasing imbalance of a tree
t
. An update of this proposal based on Devroye et al.
4
instead suggests the use of
[\log_{2}\log f(t)-\log_{2}\log a_{m(t)}]/[\log_{2}\log b_{m(t)}-\log_{2}\log a_{m(t)}]
, as the numerical values of the ranks of different trees are comparable after the double logarithm is taken. Modifying this suggestion for
k
-furcation, our results here suggests that a comparably sensible index in the setting of multifurcating trees is

\frac{\log_{k}\log f(t)-\log_{k}\log a_{m(t)}}{\log_{k}\log b_{m(t)}-\log_{k}\log a_{m(t)}},

(13)

According to this scheme, the minimally balanced tree has value 1 for the index and the maximally balanced tree has value 0.

In mathematical evolutionary biology, multifurcating trees have the potential to describe biological phenomena with rapid diversification and large numbers of offspring
8
, phenomena that that are not as easily modeled with bifurcating trees. The study adds to recent interest in the mathematical understanding of multifurcating trees in evolutionary biology
5
;
10
;
11
;
14
;
15
, providing further information about encoding schemes for two multifurcating tree classes, strictly
k
-furcating and at-most-
k
-furcating, with potential for application in diverse types of evolutionary studies.
