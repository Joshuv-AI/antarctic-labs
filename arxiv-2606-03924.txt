Knowledge Editing in Masked Diffusion Language Models
Haewon Park, Yohan Jo Graduate School of Data Science, Seoul National University {dellaanima2, yohan.jo}@snu.ac.kr

Abstract
Knowledge editing aims to update or correct factual knowledge in a language model. A widely used approach, locate-then-edit , does this in two steps: it first localizes a fact within the model, then edits the weights there. To date, such methods have been developed exclusively on autoregressive models (ARMs). Whether their underlying assumptions hold for masked diffusion models (MDMs), which model text bidirectionally and generate by iterative denoising rather than next-token prediction, remains an open question. We address it by transferring locate-then-edit to MDMs and comparing two MDMs (LLaDA, Dream) with two ARMs (LLaMA, Qwen) at matched scale. Our central finding has two parts. First, where an edit is applied transfers across paradigms: causal tracing highlights the same early-to-mid-layer MLP at the last subject token in both, and editing is most effective there. Second, this shared location does not guarantee a shared outcome. Single-token edits succeed in both, but as targets grow longer, editing degrades systematically in the MDMs but not the ARMs. The failure stems from how the edited fact is generated: producing a multi-token target requires passing through partially unmasked intermediate states for which the edit was never optimized. Guided by this diagnosis, we introduce a simple correction that optimizes the edit for these states, substantially restoring multi-token performance. 1 1 1 We will release our code upon publication.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) acquire extensive factual knowledge through
pretraining, but not all of it is correct: some facts are simply wrong, a
product of noise in the training data, and others grow stale over
time
(
Ji et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2022
;
Jang et al. 2022
)
.
Fine-tuning can fix them, but doing so over the full model is
computationally expensive and risks overfitting to the correction while
eroding unrelated knowledge through catastrophic
forgetting
(
Bethune et al. 2025
;
Luo et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2020
)
.
Knowledge editing offers an alternative, revising targeted facts while
keeping the rest of the model’s knowledge
intact
(
Yao et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024b
)
.
A fact is typically represented as a tuple
(s,r,o)
—a subject
s
, a
relation
r
, and an object
o
—and an edit replaces the object with a new

target object

o^{*}
for a given
(s,r)
: for example, “Lionel
Messi
(s)
plays for
(r)
FC Barcelona
(o)
” can be edited to “Inter Miami”

(o^{*})
. Among such methods, the
locate-then-edit
approach
identifies where a fact is causally localized by analyzing the model’s
internals, then modifies only the weights at that
location
(
Meng et al. 2023a
;
Meng et al. 2023b
)
. Because the edit is
grounded in an explicit analysis rather than distributed across the whole
model or delegated to an auxiliary
network
(
De Cao et al. 2021
;
Mitchell et al. 2022a
;
Mitchell et al. 2022b
)
, the
edit site is interpretable.

These methods, however, were developed and validated entirely on
autoregressive models (ARMs). Masked diffusion models (MDMs)
(
Nie et al. 2025
;
Ye et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2025
)
have
recently emerged as a competitive alternative paradigm, yet knowledge
editing for MDMs remains largely unexplored. Whether locate-then-edit,
designed for ARMs, also applies to MDMs is the question this work
takes up. The challenge runs deeper than reimplementation: the
location these methods modify is not arbitrary but derived from how
ARMs work. Using causal tracing, ROME
(
Meng et al. 2023a
)

localized a fact’s recall to the early-to-mid-layer MLP of the last
subject token, read this MLP as a form of associative memory, and
made it the edit target; MEMIT
(
Meng et al. 2023b
)
built on the same
site to scale to many edits at once. This grounding, however, comes
entirely from ARMs, which differ from MDMs even in how knowledge is
acquired: an ARM is trained to predict the next token, whereas an MDM recovers masked tokens from bidirectional context. It is
therefore not obvious that an edit site identified in ARMs carries
over to a model trained so differently.

To investigate this, we transfer locate-then-edit to MDMs without
altering its algorithm, applying only the adjustments needed for the
MDM’s masked prediction (§
3.3
), and we compare ARMs
(LLaMA, Qwen) and MDMs (LLaDA, Dream) under matched scale. We examine the
question at two levels: we perform causal tracing to locate where
facts are causally localized, and we measure editing performance
across token positions and layer ranges to identify where editing is
most effective. We find that even in MDMs, where every token attends
to the full subject bidirectionally, the localization of facts
coincides with that in ARMs—the early-to-mid-layer MLP of the last
subject token. The location assumption underlying locate-then-edit is
thus not specific to autoregressive training but holds across both
paradigms.

Sharing a location, however, does not mean sharing an outcome. While
the two paradigms perform comparably when the target object is a
single token, the MDM degrades substantially once the target spans
multiple tokens. The cause lies not in the location but in the
generation process. Locate-then-edit fits the edit on a single state.
An ARM passes through exactly that state as it decodes left to right,
so the edit operates under the conditions it was fit for. An MDM, by
contrast, starts from a fully masked output and unmasks its positions
over several denoising steps, passing through a chain of partial-mask
states the edit was never optimized for. As a result, editing degrades
systematically as the target spans more tokens. Building on this
diagnosis, we propose a simple correction that makes the edit robust
to these intermediate states, and show that it substantially restores
multi-token editing in MDMs.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We present the
first analysis of locate-then-edit
knowledge editing in MDMs
(§
3
), showing that its
core location assumption—that recall is mediated by the
early-to-mid-layer MLP at the last subject token—holds across both
paradigms.

•

We find that in MDMs, locate-then-edit suffers a

multi-token breakdown
(§
4
): editing succeeds
for single-token target objects but degrades systematically as the target object grows longer, which
we trace to a mismatch between single-location editing and iterative
denoising. Standard benchmarks, dominated by single-token targets,
cannot reveal this, so we surface and analyze the breakdown with a
multi-token evaluation adapted from
Kamel

(
Kalo and Fichtel 2022
)
.

•

We propose a
simple correction
(§
4.3
)
that makes the edit robust to the partial-mask states traversed during
generation, substantially restoring multi-token editing performance in
MDMs.

Preliminaries

2.1
Locate-then-Edit Knowledge Editing

Knowledge editing aims to modify a fact triplet from
(s,r,o)
to

(s,r,o^{\ast})
. Locate-then-edit methods
(
Meng et al. 2023a
;
Meng et al. 2023b
)
achieve this by viewing the down-projection layer

\mathbf{W}
of a Transformer MLP as a linear associative
memory
(
Anderson 1972
;
Kohonen 1972
)
that maps a key vector

\mathbf{k}
to a value vector
\mathbf{v}
via
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{v}
. The key
\mathbf{k}
, taken as the MLP up-projection
activation at the last token of the subject
s
, encodes the entity,
while the value
\mathbf{v}
encodes the associated
(r,o)
. Editing a
fact amounts to adding an update
\mathbf{\Delta}
to
\mathbf{W}
so
that the same key maps to a new value
\mathbf{v}^{\ast}
encoding

(r,o^{\ast})
, i.e.,
(\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{\Delta})\mathbf{k}\approx\mathbf{v}^{\ast}
. This choice of where to edit rests on
causal
tracing

(
Meng et al. 2023a
)
, which identifies the components
most responsible for recalling a fact—the early-to-mid-layer MLPs
at the last subject token.

Building on this site, MEMIT
(
Meng et al. 2023b
)
edits a contiguous
range of these MLP layers and, for a batch of facts, solves for the
update
\mathbf{\Delta}
in closed form from the keys
\mathbf{k}
and
the corresponding target values
\mathbf{v}^{\ast}
(full derivation in
Appendix
A
). While the key
\mathbf{k}
is read directly
from the subject, the target value
\mathbf{v}^{\ast}
is obtained by
optimization: it is the value that, when written at the edit location,
drives the model to produce the target object
o^{\ast}
given the
prompt
p
describing
(s,r)
. Concretely,
\mathbf{v}^{\ast}=\mathbf{v}+\boldsymbol{\delta}
, where the residual
\boldsymbol{\delta}
is found
by gradient descent to maximize the probability of
o^{\ast}
under the
model with the edit-site value replaced by

\mathbf{v}+\boldsymbol{\delta}
, denoted

G(\mathbf{v}\leftarrow\mathbf{v}+\boldsymbol{\delta})
:

\boldsymbol{\delta}=\arg\min_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\;-\log\mathbb{P}_{G(\mathbf{v}\leftarrow\mathbf{v}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}})}\bigl[\,o^{\ast}\mid p\,\bigr].

(1)

The optimization details follow MEMIT
(
Meng et al. 2023b
)

(Appendix
A
). Crucially,
\boldsymbol{\delta}
is fit for a single conditioning
state. This implicitly assumes the model encounters that same state
when it generates the target—an assumption we revisit for MDMs in
§
4
.

2.2
Masked Diffusion Language Models

ARMs factorize the joint distribution into left-to-right conditionals,
predicting each token from its left context only through a causal
attention mask. An MDM is trained differently. Given a sequence

x_{0}=(x_{0}^{1},\dots,x_{0}^{L})
, a forward process independently
replaces each token with an absorbing mask state
\mathrm{M}
with
probability
t\in[0,1]
, and the model learns a
mask
predictor

p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{t})
that recovers all masked tokens
simultaneously from the partially masked sequence
x_{t}
. Training
minimizes a cross-entropy loss evaluated only at masked
positions
(
Nie et al. 2025
)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\,\mathbb{E}_{t,\,x_{0},\,x_{t}}\!\Bigg[\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}\!\left[x_{t}^{i}=\mathrm{M}\right]

(2)

\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}\!\left(x_{0}^{i}\mid x_{t}\right)\Bigg],\quad t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1].

Two properties of this formulation are central to our study. First,
the mask predictor attends to the
entire
input without a causal
mask, so every position is predicted from bidirectional context rather
than from a left prefix. Second, the model is trained to reconstruct
masked tokens rather than to predict the next one, so the factual
knowledge that locate-then-edit modifies is formed under a learning
objective different from that of ARMs. We study two MDMs that realize
this objective differently: LLaDA is trained from scratch, whereas
Dream is initialized from the weights of an ARM (Qwen2.5) and trained
with a shifted objective that aligns its prediction with that of an
ARM
(
Ye et al. 2025
)
.

Iterative denoising.

Unlike AR decoding, which produces tokens one at a time, an MDM
generates by
iterative denoising
. Starting from a fully masked
output of length
L
, at each step the model predicts all masked
positions, unmasks a subset of them (e.g., the most confident ones),
and leaves the rest masked for subsequent steps
(
Nie et al. 2025
)
.
Producing a multi-token answer therefore unfolds over several steps
and necessarily passes through
intermediate states
in which
some positions are already filled while others remain masked. As we
show in §
4
, these intermediate states—which have no
counterpart in AR generation—are where an edit optimized for a
single conditioning state can fail to take effect.

The locate-then-edit approach proceeds in two stages: causal tracing
identifies the components most responsible for recalling a fact, and
the weights at those locations are then modified. We test whether each
stage transfers to MDMs. After the setup and our MDM adaptation
(§

), we apply causal tracing to locate
where

(§

) and measure

where editing is most effective
across token positions and

). In both, despite the MDM’s bidirectional
training, it localizes facts to the same site as the ARMs—the
early-to-mid-layer MLP of the last subject token.

3.1
Setup

Models.

We study two ARM–MDM pairs matched in parameter count and layer depth, so each comparison controls for model capacity: LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct
(
Grattafiori et al. 2024
)
(ARM) / LLaDA-8B-Instruct
(
Nie et al. 2025
)
(MDM), and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
(
Qwen et al. 2025
)
(ARM) / Dream-v0-Instruct-7B
(
Ye et al. 2025
)
(MDM). The latter is especially controlled, as Dream is initialized from the Qwen2.5 weights.

Dataset and metrics.

We evaluate on
CounterFact

(
Meng et al. 2023a
)
, the standard
knowledge-editing benchmark, using its three criteria

(
Meng et al. 2023a
;
Meng et al. 2023b
)
:
efficacy
(the rewrite prompt

(s,r)
elicits the new target),
generalization
(paraphrase prompts
elicit it too), and
specificity
(a neighborhood prompt, with a distinct
subject
s^{\prime}
sharing
r
and
o
, still yields the original object
o
).
Following MEMIT’s batched setting, we edit 500 facts in a single batch. We assess all three from the model’s
generation; details are deferred to Appendix
B
.

3.2
Locating Facts via Causal Tracing

Causal tracing.

To localize where a fact is recalled, we follow the causal tracing
procedure of
Meng et al. 2023a
. Since we ask where the

subject
information is processed en route to the prediction, we
corrupt the subject tokens and then measure how much restoring an
individual hidden state to its clean value recovers the correct
prediction. For each fact, we compare three runs:

•

Clean run.
We pass the unperturbed input
x
through
the model and collect all hidden states
\{h_{i}^{(l)}\}
; the model
produces the correct answer
o
.

•

Corrupted run.
To obfuscate the subject, we add
Gaussian noise
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\nu^{2})
to the embeddings
of all tokens spanning the subject entity, with
\nu=3\sigma
,
where
\sigma
is the standard deviation of subject token embeddings,
estimated once per model. The model then runs normally and, having
lost subject information, typically predicts incorrectly.

•

Corrupted-with-restoration run.
We proceed with the
corrupted input but restore clean states at a chosen

(\hat{i},\hat{l})
. Writing each layer’s update as

h_{i}^{(l)}=h_{i}^{(l-1)}+a_{i}^{(l)}+m_{i}^{(l)}
, we vary the
restored mediator: the full hidden state
h_{\hat{i}}^{(\hat{l})}

when tracing hidden states, or only the MLP contribution

m_{\hat{i}}^{(\hat{l})}
or attention contribution

a_{\hat{i}}^{(\hat{l})}
when tracing sub-modules. Since these
per-layer contributions are small, restoring one layer alone has
little effect, so for sub-modules we restore a contiguous window of
layers around
\hat{l}
at token
\hat{i}
, following

Meng et al. 2023a
.

The
indirect effect
(IE) of a mediator is the increase in the
probability of
o
when it is restored, relative to the corrupted run,
and averaging over facts yields the
average indirect effect

(AIE). For comparability across models, we report a
normalized
AIE
: the recovered probability gain,
P_{\text{restored}}(o)-P_{\text{corrupted}}(o)
, as a fraction of the full
corruption-induced gap,
P_{\text{clean}}(o)-P_{\text{corrupted}}(o)
, expressed as a percentage and averaged over
facts. A value of
0
means the restored state contributes nothing
beyond the corrupted run, and
100\%
means it fully recovers the
clean prediction.
Our interest is in
where along the layers
, and in
which
sub-module
(MLP vs. attention), the effect concentrates. We apply
the same procedure to all four models. For the two MDMs, the model
predicts at mask positions rather than at the next token, so we append
a block of mask tokens to the prompt and read the score
P(o)
at the
first mask position, where the first token of
o
is predicted. We
give the noise level, restoration window size, mask block length, MDM
adaptation, and remaining tracing details in
Appendix
C
.

Results.

Figure
1
decomposes the normalized AIE over
token positions and layers for all four models. In every model, both
ARMs and MDMs, the two causal sites reported by

Meng et al. 2023a
emerge: an
early site
at the last
subject token in the early-to-mid layers, carried mainly by the MLP,
and a
late site
at the answer position in the upper layers,
dominated by attention. The late site is expected, since the model
reads out its prediction there; the early site, which

Meng et al. 2023a
interpret as the site of factual recall, is
our focus.

Crucially, this early site holds for the MDMs as well. Despite being
trained on bidirectional context, both LLaDA and Dream localize
factual recall to the same early-to-mid-layer MLP at the last subject
token found in the ARMs.

LLaDA’s early-site signal is visibly weaker than the ARMs’, but this
reflects the distribution of
P_{\text{clean}}(o)
rather than weaker
localization: LLaDA assigns low probability to the correct answer on
more facts, and such facts yield weak peaks that pull down the
average. Stratifying facts by
P_{\text{clean}}(o)
leaves the
location unchanged while only its magnitude grows with probability,
and this pattern holds across all models—the ARMs simply place more
mass at high probability (Appendix
C
). What is
invariant across paradigms is thus not the magnitude but the

location
of recall.

In the early layers of the last subject token, Qwen and Dream show
a clear attention contribution that is largely absent in LLaMA and
LLaDA. For Dream this seems consistent with its initialization from
Qwen. Either way, this concerns a secondary attention signal; the
primary recall site is shared by all four models.

3.3
Editing Facts

Causal tracing (§
3.2
) reveals where a fact is causally
localized; this section asks where editing is actually

effective
. The two need not coincide—the most causally
implicated location is not necessarily the most effective one to
edit
(
Hase et al. 2023
)
—so rather than assuming the tracing result, we
measure editing performance directly. We report three analyses: how
performance varies with the edited
token position
, with the
edited
layer
, and how robust the edits are under generation
settings that reflect the MDM’s decoding process.

Transferring locate-then-edit to MDMs.

We keep the locate-then-edit algorithm (MEMIT) unchanged and apply
only the minimal adjustments needed to match the inference behavior of
an MDM. Both paradigms optimize the same target value
(Eq.
1
); they differ only in the conditioning state
under which
o^{\ast}
is scored.

The ARM appends the answer tokens
directly to the rewriting prompt and reads
o^{\ast}
as the next-token
continuation, as it is trained to do.
An MDM instead recovers masked positions. We place a block of mask
tokens at the answer slot of each rewriting prompt, one for each token
of the edit target
o^{\ast}
, and train the edit so that the model
reconstructs
o^{\ast}
there. For instance, to edit the team Messi plays for to
“Inter Miami”, the answer slot holds two mask tokens,
and the edit is scored on whether the model reconstructs “Inter”
and “Miami” jointly. Because an MDM uses bidirectional attention,
the mask tokens at the object positions can influence the fact-lookup
hidden state even though they appear after the lookup position in the
input. We therefore compute both the edit target and the key vector
(§
2.1
) using the same masked-input format the model
sees at inference. The full procedure is in Appendix
D
.

Robustness to generation settings.

Unlike an ARM, which decodes one token at a time from left to right,
an MDM fills a fully masked output over a number of denoising steps,
so its generation depends on both the generation length and the number
of steps. Since the edit is optimized once at the target length
(§
3.3
), a natural concern is whether it holds under
other settings. We therefore take the edited model as is and vary the
generation length and step count over
\{8,16,32\}
at evaluation
(Table

near
0.85
for LLaDA and
0.90
for Dream across all settings, with generalization and
specificity similarly steady. The edit thus holds across decoding
configurations it was not tuned for.

4
What Breaks: Editing Multi-Token Facts

The previous section showed that locate-then-edit transfers across
paradigms: the MDM localizes facts to the same site as the ARMs, and
editing succeeds there. That success, however, was measured on

CounterFact
, whose targets are almost always a single token.
The edit is optimized for one state—the fully masked answer. A
single-token target is unmasked in one step from exactly this state,
so the edit applies as intended. A multi-token target is unmasked over
several steps, each conditioned on the tokens revealed so
far—intermediate states the edit was never optimized for. An ARM
avoids this, decoding left to right just as its edit is optimized, so
multi-token targets pose no such mismatch. It is here, in the MDM’s
iterative generation, that editing breaks.

In this section we show how this failure grows with target length
(§
4.1
), diagnose its cause
(§
4.2
), and show that a simple correction
restores performance (§
4.3
).

This points to a mismatch between the state the edit is optimized for
and the states it actually operates on. As described in
§
2.1
, MEMIT optimizes a single residual

\boldsymbol{\delta}
so that the model produces the target from one
state: the fully masked answer. But an MDM generates a multi-token
target over several steps rather than from that single state
(§
2.2
). It unmasks one position at a time, passing
through intermediate states in which some positions are already filled
while others remain masked—states
\boldsymbol{\delta}
was never
optimized for. And because the MDM is bidirectional, each position it
unmasks becomes context that reshapes the predictions at the positions
still masked. A single token can therefore be recovered in isolation,
which is why the right tokens do appear; but the moment one is
unmasked, it shifts the conditions for the rest, under which

\boldsymbol{\delta}
was never fit. Over a multi-token target these
shifts compound—the more positions, the longer the chain and the
wider the gap we observe.

This explains our earlier observations. A single-token target has only
one position and no intermediate state, so the edit operates exactly
where it was optimized—which is why single-token edits were
comparable for both ARMs and MDMs. Longer targets traverse more
intermediate states, where failures accumulate. The ARM never faces
this mismatch, as it generates left to right in one pass. The failure
thus stems not from
where
the edit is applied but from

how
the edited fact is generated, and is specific to the
iterative denoising of MDMs.

5
Related Work

Knowledge Editing.

Knowledge editing methods fall into three paradigms.

Memory-based approaches
store edited knowledge externally and
retrieve it at inference, leaving the model untouched

(
Mitchell et al. 2022b
;
Hartvigsen et al. 2023
)
.

Meta-learning approaches
train auxiliary networks to predict weight updates
(
De Cao et al. 2021
;
Mitchell et al. 2022a
)
.

Locate-then-edit approaches
instead identify the parameters
associated with a fact and modify them directly

(
Meng et al. 2023a
;
Meng et al. 2023b
)
. This last family has been
especially influential: by grounding the edit in a causal analysis of
where a fact is localized, it makes the edit site interpretable, and
has since scaled to many simultaneous edits while reducing side
effects
(
Fang et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Park et al. 2026
;
Gupta et al. 2024
)
. We take it as our starting point both for its strong performance at scale and because its edit location is derived from an explicit analysis of the model. This makes it well suited for asking whether such an analysis, developed on ARMs, carries over to MDMs.

ARMs vs. MDMs.

A growing body of work compares the two paradigms. Some studies
contrast their capabilities, finding advantages of MDMs in knowledge
acquisition
(
Pan et al. 2026
)
or differences in long-context
behavior
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
. Closer to our setting,

Piskorz et al. 2025
show that appended mask tokens
distract context comprehension and propose a mask-agnostic loss, and

Wang et al. 2026
interpret MDM representations through sparse
features. These analyses operate on features or inference-time
behavior; none addresses where a fact resides in the weights or how to
edit it, and where they modify training objectives or activations, we
leave the model’s training untouched and intervene only on the edit.
To our knowledge, ours is the first work to bring locate-then-edit
knowledge editing, developed on ARMs, to MDMs, and to compare the two
paradigms from this perspective.

6
Conclusion

We transferred locate-then-edit knowledge editing from ARMs to MDMs.
The two agree on
where
an edit applies: facts concentrate at
the early-to-mid-layer MLP of the last subject token in both, and
editing there matches the ARM on standard benchmarks. They diverge on

how
the edited fact is generated. Because such benchmarks are
almost entirely single-token, this divergence is invisible on them;
evaluating across target lengths reveals that MDM editing degrades as
the target grows, since iterative denoising visits intermediate states
the edit was never optimized for. Including those states in the
optimization restores most of the lost performance, confirming the
cause; a gap at the longest targets remains for future work. Editing
an MDM thus turns not only on where a fact is placed but on how it is
generated—porting an editing method across architectures means
matching the generation process, not only the site it acts on.

Limitations

We instantiate locate-then-edit with MEMIT, the most established
method of its kind. Other methods in this family share the same
locate-and-edit structure and could plausibly transfer to MDMs in the
same way, though we have not verified this. Our analysis also covers
two ARM–MDM pairs at a single scale. We chose these pairs because
each matches an MDM to an ARM of the same architecture and size, which
isolates the effect of the training objective—the cleanest such
pairings currently available; whether the findings hold at larger
scales or for other MDM families remains open.
Finally, we identify and address one cause of multi-token failure—the mismatch between the single state the edit is optimized
for and the intermediate states generation visits. This accounts for
most of the degradation but not all of it: our correction exposes the
edit to how many positions are unmasked, but not to which positions or
in what order, and a gap remains at the longest targets. Closing it
may require accounting for the order in which an MDM unmasks its
output, which we leave to future work.

Ethics Statement

Knowledge editing modifies the facts a model produces, and the same
techniques that correct outdated or erroneous knowledge can in
principle be used to insert false or misleading information. Our work
studies whether and how locate-then-edit transfers to masked diffusion
models, rather than proposing editing for deployment, and our
experiments use standard public benchmarks
(
CounterFact
,
Kamel
) and openly available models. We
nonetheless note that editing methods should be applied with
appropriate safeguards—such as provenance tracking and human
oversight—particularly as they extend to new model families. We see
no additional risks specific to our analysis beyond those already
present in the knowledge-editing literature.

Appendix A
MEMIT Closed-Form Update

This appendix expands the closed-form update summarized in
§
2.1
, following
Meng et al. 2023b
. We use the
notation of §
2.1
throughout:
\mathbf{W}
is the MLP
down-projection viewed as a linear associative memory,
\mathbf{k}
a
key, and
\mathbf{v}
its value.

Single-layer associative memory.

A layer storing a set of key–value pairs
\{(\mathbf{k}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
holds the weights that minimize the squared
recall error,

\mathbf{W}=\arg\min_{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{k}_{i}-\mathbf{v}_{i}\bigr\rVert^{2},

(3)

which, stacking the keys and values into matrices

\mathbf{K}=[\mathbf{k}_{1}\mid\cdots\mid\mathbf{k}_{n}]
and

\mathbf{V}=[\mathbf{v}_{1}\mid\cdots\mid\mathbf{v}_{n}]
, is solved
by the normal equation
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}^{\top}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{K}^{\top}
.

Adding a new association.

An edit asks the same weights to additionally map a set of new keys

\mathbf{K}_{1}
to target values collected in
\mathbf{V}_{1}
, the matrix
whose columns are the
\mathbf{v}^{\ast}
of §
2.1
, while
preserving the existing associations. Writing the updated weights as

\mathbf{W}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}
and solving the corresponding normal
equation in block form yields a closed-form
update
(
Meng et al. 2023b
)
:

\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{K}_{1}^{\top}\bigl(\mathbf{C}+\mathbf{K}_{1}\mathbf{K}_{1}^{\top}\bigr)^{-1},

(4)

where
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{V}_{1}-\mathbf{W}\mathbf{K}_{1}
is the
residual of the new targets under the current weights, and
\mathbf{C}

is the uncentered covariance of the pre-existing keys. Since the
original keys are not accessible,
\mathbf{C}
is estimated as

\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\top}]
over a sample of
inputs, with
\lambda
balancing the new associations against the
preserved ones.

Optimizing the target value.

The target value
\mathbf{v}^{\ast}=\mathbf{v}+\boldsymbol{\delta}

enters Eq.
4
through
\mathbf{V}_{1}
, and its
residual
\boldsymbol{\delta}
is the quantity optimized in
Eq.
1
: it is chosen so that writing
\mathbf{v}^{\ast}

at the edit location drives the model to produce the target object

o^{\ast}
. To make the edit robust across contexts, the objective
averages over prompts formed by prepending random prefixes to the
rewriting prompt
p
. For an ARM these prompts place
o^{\ast}
as the
next-token continuation; for an MDM we instead append
L

[MASK]
tokens and score
o^{\ast}
at the masked positions, as
described in §
3.3
.

Spreading the update over layers.

Rather than editing a single layer, MEMIT distributes the update over
the contiguous range
\mathcal{R}
of critical MLP layers identified
by causal tracing. The full residual is computed at the last layer

L=\max(\mathcal{R})
, and each layer
l\in\mathcal{R}
absorbs a
fraction of it, scaled by
1/(L-l+1)
so that the change is shared
evenly across the range. Because editing one layer shifts the
activations of later ones, the keys
\mathbf{K}_{l}
are recomputed and
the layers are updated in ascending order.

Appendix B
Evaluation Details

We adopt the three standard knowledge-editing
criteria
(
Meng et al. 2023a
;
Meng et al. 2023b
;
Fang et al. 2025
)
,
but assess all of them from the model’s
generation
rather than
from probability comparisons, so that the same protocol applies
uniformly to ARMs and MDMs. For each item
i
, let
x_{i}
be the
rewrite prompt
(s_{i},r_{i})
,
N(x_{i})
its paraphrase prompts, and

O(x_{i})
its neighborhood prompts (a distinct subject
s_{i}^{\prime}
sharing
the relation
r_{i}
and object
o_{i}
). Let
o^{\ast}
denote the new
target object and
o
the original object.

Generation protocol.

Given a prompt, the model generates a continuation of length

T=\max\bigl(\lvert\tau(\sqcup o^{\ast})\rvert,\lvert\tau(\sqcup o)\rvert\bigr)
,
where
\tau(\cdot)
denotes tokenization and
\sqcup
a leading space.
A single generation of this length is scored against both objects,
which lets us track not only whether the new target
o^{\ast}
appears
but also whether the original
o
still does. For ARMs we use greedy
left-to-right decoding of
T
tokens. For MDMs we run denoising over a
fully masked suffix, committing one position per step in descending
order of model confidence, with the number of steps equal to the
suffix length. LLaDA uses a suffix of length
T
; for Dream we extend
it by one mask slot to leave room for its natural generation pattern,
such as an EOS marker or a function word, without which generation
degrades to copying the first token or terminating early.

Scoring.

An edit
hits
a target
t
on a prompt
p
if
t
appears as a
case-insensitive substring of the generated text (after whitespace
stripping):

\mathrm{hit}(p,t)=\mathbf{1}\bigl[\,t\subseteq_{\text{substr}}\mathrm{gen}(p)\,\bigr].

(5)

The three criteria are then

Efficacy

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}\,\mathrm{hit}(x_{i},o^{\ast}),

(6)

Generalization

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}\,\mathbb{E}_{p\in N(x_{i})}\,\mathrm{hit}(p,o^{\ast}),

(7)

Specificity

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}\,\mathbb{E}_{p\in O(x_{i})}\,\mathrm{hit}(p,o),

(8)

where efficacy and generalization check that the new target
o^{\ast}

is produced on the rewrite and paraphrase prompts, while specificity
checks that the original object
o
is
preserved
on
neighborhood prompts.

Appendix C
Causal Tracing Details

This appendix details the causal tracing setup summarized in
§
3.2
, following the procedure of

Meng et al. 2023a
.

Dataset and fact selection.

Tracing is run on the
Knowns
dataset of

Meng et al. 2023a
(1,209 facts), separate from the

CounterFact
and
Kamel
sets used for editing
evaluation. Following
Meng et al. 2023a
, we restrict the
analysis to facts that the clean model already predicts correctly,
i.e., whose first decoded token of
o
matches the full answer string.
Since this filter depends on each model’s own predictions, the
retained subset differs across the four models.

C.1
MDM Adaptation

Because an MDM predicts at mask positions rather than at a next token,
we append a block of mask tokens to the prompt and read the score at
the first of them. The score
P(o)
is the marginal probability of the
first token of
o
at this position, taken as the clean run’s greedy
token. In practice the correctness filter above requires this first
token, once decoded, to match the full answer string, so the trace is
restricted to single-token answers; this matches the ARM case, where

P(o)
is the first-token probability at the last prompt position. We
use a block of
8
mask tokens throughout; since only single-token
answers are retained,
8
positions are more than sufficient and the
answer token is always scored at the first mask position.

Tracing uses a single forward pass over the masked input rather than
the iterative denoising used at evaluation. A single pass exposes each
layer’s contribution directly, which is what layer-by-layer
restoration requires; iterative denoising would instead entangle
contributions across steps.

The corruption is applied exactly as in the ARM case: Gaussian noise
is added to the embeddings of the subject tokens only, leaving the
appended mask block untouched. The noise level
\nu=3\sigma
is
calibrated to each model, with
\sigma
the standard deviation of
subject-token embeddings, estimated separately per model from its own
embeddings.

C.2
Tracing Window

When tracing the full hidden state we restore a single state

h_{\hat{i}}^{(\hat{l})}
. When tracing a sub-module (MLP or
attention), a single layer’s contribution is too small to recover the
prediction on its own, so we restore a contiguous window of
10

layers centered at
\hat{l}
at token
\hat{i}
, following

Meng et al. 2023a
.

C.3
Normalized AIE

We report the
normalized AIE
, the standard fractional tracing
effect of
Meng et al. 2023a
: the recovery a restored state
provides as a fraction of the gap that corruption induces,

\mathrm{normAIE}(\hat{i},\hat{l})=\mathbb{E}_{\text{facts}}\!\left[\frac{P_{\text{restored}}(o)-P_{\text{corrupted}}(o)}{P_{\text{clean}}(o)-P_{\text{corrupted}}(o)}\right]\times 100\%

(9)

where
P_{\text{clean}}
,
P_{\text{corrupted}}
, and

P_{\text{restored}}
are the first-token probabilities of
o
under
the clean, corrupted, and corrupted-with-restoration runs. A value of

0
means no recovery beyond the corrupted run and
100\%
a full
recovery. Reporting it as a percentage places the four models on a
common scale despite their different baseline probabilities. Facts
whose corruption gap
P_{\text{clean}}(o)-P_{\text{corrupted}}(o)

is negligible are excluded, since the ratio is then undefined.

Appendix D

This appendix expands the transfer described in §

A single MEMIT edit runs forward passes on four distinct input
distributions. In the ARM case all of them use the raw prompt (or
prompt followed by the target); in the MDM case we put all of them in
the same form, a prompt followed by a block of mask tokens at the
answer slot, with one mask token per token of the edit target

o^{\ast}

1.

Rewriting prompts.
The main term of
Eq.
1

3.

Key extraction.
The key
\mathbf{k}
for each fact is
read as the subject-last hidden state on the mask-augmented prompt.

4.

Residual baseline.
The baseline value used to form
the closed-form residual is likewise read from the subject-last
hidden state on the mask-augmented prompt.

Under the causal attention of an ARM, the subject-last hidden state is
identical on the raw prompt and on the prompt followed by the target,
since future tokens cannot be attended to; the two hidden states that

coincide automatically. Under the bidirectional attention of an MDM
this coincidence breaks, so we force all four distributions to be

Appendix I
Partial-Mask Augmentation: Details and Ablations

This appendix specifies the partial-mask augmentation of
§
4.3
and ablates its two design choices: how often
each partially unmasked state is visited during optimization, and how
the revealed positions are chosen.

I.1
Method

The correction broadens the states on which the residual

\boldsymbol{\delta}
(Eq.
1
) is fit, leaving
everything else in MEMIT unchanged. For a target of
N
tokens, at
optimization step
t
we reveal
k=t\bmod N
of the target
positions, cycling through
k=0,1,\dots,N{-}1
over training. At
each step we pick
k
positions, fill them with the corresponding
target tokens, mask the rest, and update
\boldsymbol{\delta}
on the

N{-}k
still-masked positions. The fully masked state (
k{=}0
) and
every partially unmasked state thus appear in turn, matching the
progression generation runs through.

Appendix J
Computational resources.

All experiments were run on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU (144GB). The
models we study have 7–8B parameters (§
3.1
). Editing time
scales with the number of denoising steps and is roughly constant per
sample: for LLaDA, a single edit takes about
8.7
seconds per fact
(e.g.,
\sim
29 minutes for 200 facts), and
for Dream about
4.5
seconds per fact (
\sim
15 minutes for 200
facts). A full edit of 500 facts therefore takes approximately

70
minutes for LLaDA and
40
minutes for Dream on one GPU.
