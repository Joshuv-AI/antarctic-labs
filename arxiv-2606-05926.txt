Auxiliary Gradient-Flow Solvers for Generalized Newtonian Models
Stefano Zampini, Daniele Boffi, Gurt Dovletov, Peter Markowich

Abstract
We introduce an auxiliary gradient-flow framework for variational problems with generalized Newtonian structure governed by an N-function. The key idea is to replace the nonlinear constitutive dependence on the gradient, or symmetric gradient, by an auxiliary scalar variable representing its squared magnitude. This shifts the nonlinearity from the state equation to the auxiliary variable, yielding a sequence of uniformly elliptic weighted linear problems. At the continuous level, we construct an auxiliary energy on a metric space adapted to the growth of the underlying N-function. In this topology, we prove lower semicontinuity, geodesic λ \lambda -convexity, and exponential convergence of the associated minimizing-movement scheme. At the finite element level, we derive a metric gradient flow through an explicit Riesz map, prove global well-posedness of the resulting semi-discrete ODE, and establish convergence to the finite element solution of the Euler–Lagrange equations of the generalized Newtonian energy. For the p p -Laplacian and p p -Stokes models, this gives a rigorous convergence result for 4 / 3 ≤ p ≤ 4 4/3\leq p\leq 4 , p ≠ 2 p\neq 2 , with asymptotic rate estimates beyond this range. We also propose practical time discretizations, including an operator-splitting scheme that gives the Kačanov iteration as a special case, and an adaptive pseudo-transient method that can be implemented using scalable linear solvers. Numerical experiments for power-law, Carreau–Yasuda, regularized Bingham, and optimal-design models demonstrate robustness, mesh-independent iteration counts in the tested regimes, and performance that matches or outperforms Newton’s method.

The starting point of the present work is the differential-algebraic system introduced in
[
50
]
for modeling evolution of biological transportation networks

\frac{ds}{dt}=|\nabla u|^{2}-s^{\gamma-1},\quad-\nabla\cdot\bigl((s+r)\nabla u\bigr)=f,\quad\gamma>1,

where
r>0
is an arbitrarily small regularization parameter ensuring the well-posedness of the linear elliptic problem. The steady states of this system solve the Euler–Lagrange equations of the regularized
p
-Laplacian

\int_{\Omega}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}+r)\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}fv\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad p=\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}.

Rewriting
\gamma
in terms of
p
and introducing the change of variables

c=s^{2/(p-2)}
, the dynamics become

\frac{dc}{dt}=\frac{2}{p-2}c^{\frac{4-p}{2}}\bigl(|\nabla u|^{2}-c\bigr),\quad-\nabla\cdot\left(\left(c^{\frac{p-2}{2}}+r\right)\nabla u\right)=f.

If the goal is to recover only the steady state, the prefactor in the ordinary differential equation (ODE) is not essential, since it changes the parametrization of the relaxation in time, but not its equilibrium. One is thus tempted to deliberately drop this factor and use the simpler evolution

\frac{dc}{dt}=|\nabla u|^{2}-c.

(2)

This seemingly minor simplification was the starting point of the present paper: it produces an auxiliary dynamics that is no longer tied to the specific algebraic structure of the power-law model (which is only encoded through the elliptic constraint) and can therefore be extended to more general constitutive laws. Note that, for
r=0
, a forward Euler discretization of (
2
) with time step equal to one gives the Kačanov iteration for the
p
-Laplacian
[
33
, Equation 1.4]
,

c_{n+1}=|\nabla u_{n}|^{2},\quad\int_{\Omega}c_{n+1}^{(p-2)/2}\,\nabla u_{n+1}\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}fv\,\mathrm{d}x.

The aim of this work is not to derive an equivalent minimization problem for (
1
). Rather, we introduce an auxiliary-field formulation in which the nonlinearity is shifted from the state equation to a scalar variable
c
representing, at equilibrium, the squared magnitude
|Du|^{2}
. The resulting formulation provides a variationally consistent framework for designing and analyzing approximation schemes based on a sequence of uniformly elliptic linear state problems of the type

\int_{\Omega}\mu(c(x))\,Du\!:\!Dv\,\mathrm{d}x=F(v),\quad\mu(r):=\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\sqrt{r})}{\sqrt{r}}.

(3)

Note that, in the non-Newtonian fluids literature (see e.g.
[
17
, Section 4.1]
, the viscosity is written as a function
\widehat{\mu}(\dot{\gamma})
, where the shear rate
\dot{\gamma}
is the magnitude of the rate-of-strain tensor

\dot{\gamma}=\sqrt{2}|\varepsilon({\bm{u}})|.

Here the auxiliary variable tracks, modulo the
\sqrt{2}
scaling, the squared modulus
|\varepsilon({\bm{u}})|^{2}
.

In the first part of the paper, we construct an auxiliary energy with domain of arbitrarily truncated functions, i.e.
\epsilon\leq c(x)\leq 1/\epsilon
almost everywhere for an arbitrary
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, and show that, under suitable assumptions, it is lower semicontinuous on a complete geodesic metric space whose structure depends on the first derivative of the viscosity. A central point of the analysis is that the space to which the auxiliary variable
c
belongs is isometric to
{L^{2}(\Omega)}
. We then use the theory of gradient flows in metric spaces
[
2
]
to prove exponential convergence, independent of
\epsilon
, of the method of minimizing movements
[
29
]
toward the minimizers in the truncated space. The convergence mechanism is the geodesical
\lambda
-convexity of the auxiliary energy in this adapted metric. In particular, for the power-law case, this yields a rigorous convergence theory for
4/3\leq p\leq 4
,
p\neq 2
, with
p=2

The second part of the paper connects the continuous formulation with finite element discretizations. Instead of discretizing the method of minimizing movements directly, we derive a finite-dimensional evolution leveraging the theory of metric gradient flows
[
61
]
(see also
[
64

2

3

\mu^{\prime}(r)
has a fixed sign and is nonzero for
r>0

1

4/3\leq p\leq 4
,
p\neq 2
that matches the continuous analysis. The cases
p<4/3
and
p>4
are not covered by the present theory, but the same framework suggests asymptotic convergence-rate estimates near equilibrium that are consistent with our numerical experiments.

We then describe several time-discretization strategies for the auxiliary metric gradient flow, which contain the Kačanov iteration as a special case. Motivated by pseudo-transient continuation methods
[
59
]
and their extensions to differential-algebraic equations
[
26
]
, we also propose a time-adaptive pseudo-transient iteration that can be implemented using scalable linear solvers. A convergence analysis of these fully discrete schemes is not pursued here and is left for future work. Nevertheless, the numerical experiments demonstrate the robustness of the methods across different models, confirm the predicted rates for power-law models and for the Carreau–Yasuda model
[
74
,
75
]
in the theoretically covered range, and support the asymptotic estimates outside that range. Numerical results also show that the methods are mesh-independent and indicate that the proposed strategy is competitive with, and in several cases more robust than, Newton’s method, including solving the
p
-Laplacian equations with
p=100

The paper is organized as follows. Section

Preliminaries

2.1
Orlicz-Sobolev spaces

Definition 2.1

(N-function)
.

We say that
\phi:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
is an N-function, if
\phi
is convex, continuous,

\phi(0)=0
,
\phi(r)>0
, and

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\phi(r)}{r}=0,\quad\lim_{r\to+\infty}\frac{\phi(r)}{r}=+\infty.

An equivalent characterization is

\phi(r)=\int^{r}_{0}\phi^{\prime}_{+}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

(4)

where
\phi^{\prime}_{+}
denotes the right derivative.

The function
\phi^{\prime}_{+}
is non-decreasing, satisfies
\phi^{\prime}_{+}(0)=0
and
\phi^{\prime}_{+}(r)>0
for
r>0
, with
\lim_{r\to+\infty}\phi_{+}^{\prime}(r)=+\infty
, and is strictly increasing on
(0,+\infty)

[
66
]
. For simplicity, we assume throughout that
\phi
has enough regularity for the following derivatives to be well defined:

Assumption 2.2
.

\phi\in C^{1}([0,+\infty))\cap C^{3}((0,+\infty))
.

We also assume the following growth condition:

Assumption 2.3
.

The Simonenko indices of
\phi

[
69
]
are bounded, i.e.

{{R}^{-}_{\phi}}:=\inf_{r>0}\frac{r\phi^{\prime}(r)}{\phi(r)}>1,\quad{{R}^{+}_{\phi}}=\sup_{r>0}\frac{r\phi^{\prime}(r)}{\phi(r)}<+\infty.

This assumption ensures that
\phi
and its complementary function
\phi^{*}
defined as

\phi^{\ast}(r):=\int^{r}_{0}(\phi^{\prime})^{-1}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

(5)

satisfy a
\Delta_{2}
condition
[
60
]
, i.e.

\exists\,C\geq 1:\phi(2r)\leq C\phi(r),
for all
r\geq 0
.
Under Assumption
2.3
, the natural spaces to study energies of the form (
1
) are the Orlicz-Sobolev spaces
[
34
]
. Associated with
\phi
is the Orlicz space
{L^{\phi}(\Omega)}
, which is a separable and reflexive Banach space equipped with the Luxemburg norm

\|v\|_{{L^{\phi}(\Omega)}}:=\inf\Big\{\lambda>0:\int_{\Omega}\phi(|v|/\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq 1\Big\},

and, under the sole
\Delta_{2}
condition, we have the equivalence (see e.g.
[
37
]
)

v\in{L^{\phi}(\Omega)}\iff\int_{\Omega}\phi(|v|)\,\mathrm{d}x<+\infty.

The Orlicz–Sobolev space
W^{1,\phi}(\Omega)
consists of all
v\in{L^{\phi}(\Omega)}

such that
\nabla v\in L^{\phi}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
and the following continuous embeddings hold for bounded domains
[
48
, Lemmas 3.7.7 and 6.16]
)

\displaystyle\phi(r)/r^{2}\text{ non-decreasing}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow{L^{\phi}(\Omega)}\hookrightarrow{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad W^{1,\phi}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow H^{1}(\Omega),

(6)

\displaystyle\phi(r)/r^{2}\text{ non-increasing}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow{L^{2}(\Omega)}\hookrightarrow{L^{\phi}(\Omega)},\quad H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow W^{1,\phi}(\Omega).

(7)

Instead of working with the energy functional (
1
), we consider a reparameterization in terms of
|Du|^{2}
, and introduce the function

\Phi(r):=\phi(\sqrt{r}),

(8)

so that
\phi(|Du|)=\Phi(|Du|^{2})
; from (
4
) we have the following characterization

\Phi(r):=\frac{1}{2}\int^{r}_{0}\mu(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad\mu(r):=2\Phi^{\prime}(r)=\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\sqrt{r})}{\sqrt{r}}.

(9)

The function
\Phi
is continuous, satisfies
\Phi(0)=0
, and
\Phi(r)>0
for
r>0
and it is strictly increasing, thus
\mu(r)>0
for
r>0
; however, since the mapping
r\mapsto\sqrt{r}
is concave,
\Phi
is generally not an N-function. A direct calculation yields

\Phi^{\prime\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{2}\mu^{\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{4r\sqrt{r}}\left(\phi^{\prime\prime}(\sqrt{r})\sqrt{r}-\phi^{\prime}(\sqrt{r})\right)=\frac{1}{4t^{3}}\left(\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)t-\phi^{\prime}(t)\right)=\frac{1}{4t}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\phi^{\prime}(t)}{t}\right),\quad t=\sqrt{r}.

(10)

This leads to the next assumption, which we require to hold throughout this work.

Assumption 2.4
.

The function
\Phi
is either strictly convex (i.e.
\mu^{\prime}(r)>0
) or strictly concave (
\mu^{\prime}(r)<0
) on
(0,+\infty)
.

From (
10
) we thus have that
\Phi
is strictly convex (resp. concave) if and only if
\phi^{\prime}(r)/r
is increasing (resp. decreasing).
and we derive the convenient representation

\mu^{\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{2r}\left(\frac{t\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)}{\phi^{\prime}(t)}-1\right)\mu(r).\quad t=\sqrt{r}.

(11)

Note that the assumed convexity or concavity of
\Phi
guarantees, by a simple application of the monotone form of de L’Hôpital’s rule, that one of the embeddings (
6
)–(
7
) holds. This result is stated without proof in the next Lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
\frac{\phi^{\prime}(r)}{r}
is non-decreasing (resp. non-increasing) then
\phi(r)/r^{2}
is non-decreasing (resp. non-increasing). Therefore

\displaystyle\Phi\text{ convex on }(0,+\infty)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow{L^{\phi}(\Omega)}\hookrightarrow{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad W^{1,\phi}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow H^{1}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\Phi\text{ concave on }(0,+\infty)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow{L^{2}(\Omega)}\hookrightarrow{L^{\phi}(\Omega)},\quad H^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow W^{1,\phi}(\Omega).

For our analysis, we will need two further assumptions. The first is the so-called

assumption introduced in
[
32
]
.

Assumption 2.6
.

Let
{{S}^{-}_{\phi}}
and
{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}
be defined as

{{S}^{-}_{\phi}}:=\inf_{r>0}\frac{r\phi^{\prime\prime}(r)}{\phi^{\prime}(r)},\quad{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}:=\sup_{r>0}\frac{r\phi^{\prime\prime}(r)}{\phi^{\prime}(r)},

0<{{S}^{-}_{\phi}}\leq{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}<+\infty.

Note that the following chain of inequalities holds

1<1+{{S}^{-}_{\phi}}\leq{{R}^{-}_{\phi}}\leq{{R}^{+}_{\phi}}\leq 1+{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}<+\infty.

We state here some useful Lemmas, for which we provide proofs in the Appendix. The first Lemma provides bounds for two-point growth estimates of
\phi^{\prime}
and

A.1
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
0<s\leq t
and
{{S}^{-}_{\phi}},{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}
given in Assumption

(i)

satisfies the two-point growth

\left(\frac{s}{t}\right)^{{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}}\leq\frac{\phi^{\prime}(s)}{\phi^{\prime}(t)}\leq\left(\frac{s}{t}\right)^{{{S}^{-}_{\phi}}},

\left(\frac{s}{t}\right)^{\frac{{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}-1}{2}}\leq\frac{\mu(s)}{\mu(t)}\leq\left(\frac{s}{t}\right)^{\frac{{{S}^{-}_{\phi}}-1}{2}}.

Using Lemma

.

.

Let
{{S}^{-}_{\phi}},{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}
be given as in Assumption

\frac{1}{{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}+1}\,r\phi^{\prime}(r)\leq\phi(r)\leq\frac{1}{{{S}^{-}_{\phi}}+1}\,r\phi^{\prime}(r),\quad\frac{1}{{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}+1}\,r\mu(r)\leq\Phi(r)\leq\frac{1}{{{S}^{-}_{\phi}}+1}\,r\mu(r),

2.2
Metric space

Associated with
\Phi
is the function space
{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
, defined, with some abuse of notation, as

{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}:=\left\{c:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{ measurable}:\sqrt{|c|}\in{L^{\phi}(\Omega)}\right\}.

(12)

Note that
{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
is a reflexive and separable Orlicz space if and only if
\Phi
is convex. When
\Phi
is concave, the Luxemburg norm is not well defined, and
{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
is not an Orlicz space. Nevertheless, the modular is always well defined, and we have the equivalences

c\in{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}\Longleftrightarrow\int_{\Omega}\phi\left(\sqrt{|c|}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x<+\infty\Longleftrightarrow\int_{\Omega}\Phi(|c|)\,\mathrm{d}x<+\infty.

(13)

To prove that
{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
is a function space also in the concave case, we only need to show it is closed under addition, since the scaling by constants clearly yields functions in
{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
. Since
\Phi
is non-decreasing,
\Phi(0)=0
, and it is subadditive on
[0,+\infty)
, we have

\Phi(|r_{1}+r_{2}|)\leq\Phi(|r_{1}|+|r_{2}|)\leq\Phi(|r_{1}|)+\Phi(|r_{2}|)
, for all
r_{1},r_{2}
,
which, when integrated, gives
c_{1}+c_{2}\in{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
for all
c_{1},c_{2}\in{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
.

In order to define a topology on
{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
which is valid in both the convex and concave case, we introduce a further assumption.

Assumption 2.9
.

Let
{{S}^{-}_{\phi}}
and
{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}
defined in Assumption
2.6
be such that one of the following two conditions holds

0<{{S}^{-}_{\phi}}\leq{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}<1,\quad\text{or}\quad 1<{{S}^{-}_{\phi}}\leq{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}<+\infty.

We then introduce the odd function

\psi(r):=\text{sgn}(r)\int^{|r|}_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{2}\mu^{\prime}(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s,

(14)

where

\omega:=\begin{cases}\phantom{-}1,&\Phi\text{ convex},\\
-1,&\Phi\text{ concave},\end{cases}

(15)

and note that
\psi
is a continuous, strictly increasing function under Assumption
2.4
. The following Lemma proves that under Assumption
2.9
it is a homeomorphism and thus its inverse
\psi^{-1}(r)
is also a continuous, strictly increasing function; this allows us to identify functions of
{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
with functions of
{L^{2}(\Omega)}
via the bijective map

\Psi:{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}\rightarrow{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\Psi(c)(x):=\psi(c(x)),

(16)

and obtain the characterization
c\in{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}\iff\Psi(c)\in{L^{2}(\Omega)}
. For the proof, see Lemma
A.3
.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\psi
be defined as in (
14
) and let Assumption
2.9
hold. Then:

(i)

There exist constants
0<C_{1}<C_{2}<+\infty
depending only on
{{S}^{-}_{\phi}},{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}

C_{1}\mu(r)r\leq\psi(r)^{2}\leq C_{2}\mu(r)r,\quad\forall r\geq 0.

(ii)

3
Auxiliary energy reformulation

3.1
The scalar case

We consider the minimization problem

\argmin_{u\in W^{1,\phi}_{0}(\Omega)}\int_{\Omega}\phi(|\nabla u|)\,\mathrm{d}x-F(u),

(19)

where

W^{1,\phi}_{0}(\Omega)
is the closure of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
with respect to the

W^{1,\phi}(\Omega)
-norm and
F
is a continuous linear operator in
W^{1,\phi}_{0}(\Omega)^{*}
.
The Euler–Lagrange equations associated with (
19
) read:
Find
u\in W^{1,\phi}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(|\nabla u|)}{|\nabla u|}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x=F(v)\quad\forall v\in W^{1,\phi}_{0}(\Omega).

(20)

For regularity results of the minimizers of (
19
) under Assumption
2.6
, see
[
23
,
24
]
the references therein.

Using the definition of
\Phi
from (
8
), we rewrite the energy in the equivalent form

E(u):=\int_{\Omega}\Phi(|\nabla u|^{2})\,\mathrm{d}x-F(u),

(21)

yielding the Euler–Lagrange equations: Find
u\in W^{1,\phi}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

\int_{\Omega}\mu(|\nabla u|^{2})\,\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x=F(v)\quad\forall v\in W^{1,\phi}_{0}(\Omega).

(22)

Remark 3.1
.

Assumption
2.9
used in the metric-space construction excludes the cases in which the viscosity law
becomes asymptotically Newtonian approaching 0 or
+\infty
. In such cases, since

\frac{t\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)}{\phi^{\prime}(t)}=1+\frac{2r\mu^{\prime}(r)}{\mu(r)},\quad t=\sqrt{r}

we have

\frac{2r\mu^{\prime}(r)}{\mu(r)}\to 0\Rightarrow\frac{t\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)}{\phi^{\prime}(t)}\to 1.

Therefore, in the convex case, one obtains
{{S}^{-}_{\phi}}=1
, whereas in the concave case
{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}=1
. In this sense, the asymptotically Newtonian cases excluded by Assumption
2.9
are often easier to study, since in the asymptotic regime the coefficient
\mu(r)
approaches
a positive constant and the operator in (
22
) behaves like a uniformly elliptic linear operator with bounded coefficients.

As stated in the introduction, our goal is not to derive an equivalent minimization problem, but
to introduce an auxiliary-field formulation that shifts the nonlinearity from the state equation to a scalar variable, and that provides a variationally consistent framework for designing and supporting approximation schemes through a sequence of uniformly elliptic linear state problems. To this end, we first introduce the extended functional

J:{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}\times{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}\rightarrow[-\infty,+\infty],\quad J(u,c):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Phi(c)+\frac{1}{2}\,\mu(c)\big(|\nabla u|^{2}-c\big)\,\mathrm{d}x-F(u),&c\in{L_{0}^{\Phi}(\Omega)},\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(23)

where

{L_{0}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}:=\left\{c\in{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}:c(x)\geq 0\,\text{ a.e. in }\,\Omega\right\}.

(24)

For a fixed
u\in{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}
, since
2\Phi^{\prime}(c)=\mu(c)
, stationarity of
J
with respect to
c
implies

\mu^{\prime}(c)(|\nabla u|^{2}-c)=0,\quad\text{ a.e. in }\Omega,

and thus, from Assumption
2.4
, we obtain

|\nabla u|^{2}=c\,\text{ a.e. in }\{x\in\Omega:c(x)>0\}.

Stationarity of
J
with respect to
u
instead yields

a[c](u,v)=F(v),\quad\forall v\in{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)},

(25)

where

a[c](u,v):=\int_{\Omega}\mu(c)\,\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x.

(26)

For a general
c\in{L_{0}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
, the variational problem (
25
) is not well-posed on
{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}
and results like the Minty-Browder theorem
[
25
, Theorem 9.14-1]
cannot be applied. In the concave case we can in fact prove the coercivity of the functional in
{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}
, but the bilinear form (
26
) is not well defined since
\mu(c)\nabla\cdot
does not map
{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}
into
{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}^{*}
. On the other hand, when
\Phi
is convex, the linear operator induced by the bilinear form is hemicontinuous, but we lose coercivity instead.

When
\Phi
is concave, we have

\Phi(r)\leq\Phi(c)+\frac{1}{2}\mu(c)(r-c)\quad\forall\,r,c\geq 0.

Integrating on
\Omega
and subtracting
F(u)
from both sides yields

E(u)\leq J(u,c),\quad\forall u\in{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)},\,\forall c\in{L_{0}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}.

and thus

\inf_{u\in{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}}E(u)\leq\inf_{u\in{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}}J(u,c)=\mathcal{J}_{c}(c)+\inf_{u\in{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}}\Big(\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\mu(c)|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-F(u)\Big),

(27)

where the functional

\mathcal{J}_{c}(c):=\int_{\Omega}\Phi(c)-\frac{1}{2}\mu(c)c\,\mathrm{d}x,

(28)

is well defined and finite for all
c\in{L_{0}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
by Lemma
2.8
.
In the case of convex
\Phi
, the comparison with the tangent functional reverses

\Phi(r)\geq\Phi(c)+\frac{1}{2}\mu(c)(r-c)\quad\forall\,r,c\geq 0,

yielding

E(u)\geq J(u,c),\quad\forall u\in{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)},\,\forall c\in{L_{0}^{\Phi}(\Omega)},

and

\inf_{u\in{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}}E(u)\geq\inf_{u\in{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}}J(u,c)=\mathcal{J}_{c}(c)+\inf_{u\in{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}}\Big(\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\mu(c)|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-F(u)\Big).

(29)

We then define an auxiliary energy in
c
by replacing the space
{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}
in the minimization of the Dirichlet integral with
{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}
and constraining
c
to the set

{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}:=\left\{c\in{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}:\epsilon\leq c(x)\leq 1/\epsilon\,\text{ a.e. in }\Omega\right\},

(30)

for an arbitrary
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, which ensures the uniform bounds to hold almost everywhere

0<\mu_{-}\leq\mu(c(x))\leq\mu_{+}<+\infty,\quad\mu_{-}:=\min\{\mu(\epsilon),\mu(1/\epsilon)\},\quad\mu_{+}:=\max\{\mu(\epsilon),\mu(1/\epsilon)\}.

(31)

For the problem to be well-posed, we need a further assumption.

Assumption 3.2
.

The linear functional
F
admits a continuous extension to
{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{*}
.

Note that the above condition is always satisfied in the concave case but not in the convex case. We also point out that, for ease of discussion, from now on, all the inequalities (and equalities) involving pointwise bounds for functions must be understood in the almost everywhere sense.

Under Assumption
3.2
we can consider the well-posed functional for a given
c\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
, see e.g
[
39
, Section 25.2]
,

\mathcal{J}_{u}(c):=\inf_{u\in{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\mu(c)|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-F(u)\right),

(32)

whose Euler–Lagrange equations lead to the weighted Poisson problem: Find
u[c]\in{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}
such that

\int_{\Omega}\mu(c)\nabla u[c]\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x=F(v),\quad\forall v\in{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)},

(33)

yielding the identities

\int_{\Omega}\mu(c)|\nabla u[c]|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=F(u[c]),\quad\mathcal{J}_{u}(c)=-\frac{1}{2}F(u[c])=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mu(c)|\nabla u[c]|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(34)

Combining (
27
) with the embedding (
7
) in the concave case we find

\inf_{u\in{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}}E(u)\leq\mathcal{J}_{c}(c)+\mathcal{J}_{u}(c),\quad\forall c\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}.

Similarly, for the convex case, we combine (
29
) with the embedding (
6
) and obtain

\inf_{u\in{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega)}}E(u)\geq\mathcal{J}_{c}(c)+\mathcal{J}_{u}(c),\quad\forall c\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}.

Using the definition of
\omega
given in (
15
) we arrive at the extended auxiliary energy functional

{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}:{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}\to(-\infty,+\infty],\quad{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}(c):=\begin{cases}-\omega\mathcal{J}_{c}(c)-\omega\mathcal{J}_{u}(c),&c\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)},\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(35)

which is a proper, well-defined functional, and it is bounded from below by the value of the energy of the minimizer of (
21
) multiplied by
-\omega
, since we have

-\omega E(u[c])\leq-\omega J(u[c],c)={\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}(c),\quad c\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}.

(36)

3.2
The incompressible vector-valued case

The extension of the previous results to the vector-valued case

\argmin_{{\bm{u}}\in W^{1,\phi}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\int_{\Omega}\phi(|\nabla{\bm{u}}|)\,\mathrm{d}x-F({\bm{u}}),

is straightforward. In this section, we instead consider the minimization problem

\argmin_{{\bm{u}}\in W^{1,\phi}_{0,\mathrm{div}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\int_{\Omega}\phi(|\varepsilon({\bm{u}})|)\,\mathrm{d}x-F({\bm{u}}),

(37)

where

W^{1,\phi}_{0,\mathrm{div}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{\bm{v}\in{W_{0}^{1,\phi}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}:\nabla\cdot\bm{v}=0\},\quad\varepsilon({\bm{u}}):=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla{\bm{u}}+\nabla{\bm{u}}^{\mathsf{T}}),

and
F\in W^{1,\phi}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\ast}
.
For existence, uniqueness, and regularity of the solutions under Assumption
2.6
see the recent work
[
45
]
and the references therein.

The auxiliary functional is constructed by following verbatim the scalar case, replacing

|\nabla u|^{2}
by
|\varepsilon({\bm{u}})|^{2}
, and the infimum of the variational problem

\inf_{{\bm{u}}\in W^{1,\phi}_{0,\mathrm{div}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\mu(c)|\varepsilon({\bm{u}})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-F({\bm{u}})\right),

is replaced by

\mathcal{J}_{\bm{u}}(c):=\inf_{{\bm{u}}\in H^{1}_{0,\mathrm{div}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\mu(c)|\varepsilon({\bm{u}})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-F({\bm{u}})\right),

(38)

for a given
c\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
under the following assumption:

Assumption 3.3
.

The linear functional
F
admits a continuous extension to
{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{\ast}
.

To model incompressibility, we introduce a Lagrange multiplier through the pressure variable that lives in

Q:=\left\{p\in{L^{2}(\Omega)}:\int_{\Omega}p\,\mathrm{d}x=0\right\},

and obtain the Euler–Lagrange equations for (
38
) given by the weighted incompressible Stokes system: Find
{\bm{u}}[c]\in{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}
and
p[c]\in Q
such that

\displaystyle\bm{a}[c]({\bm{u}}[c],\bm{v})-b(\bm{v},p[c])

\displaystyle=F(\bm{v}),\quad\forall\bm{v}\in{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})},

(39)

\displaystyle b({\bm{u}}[c],q)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall q\in Q,

(40)

where

\bm{a}[c]({\bm{u}},\bm{v}):=\int_{\Omega}\mu(c)\,\varepsilon({\bm{u}}):\varepsilon(\bm{v})\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad b({\bm{u}},q):=\int_{\Omega}q\,\nabla\cdot{\bm{u}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(41)

The well-posedness of the weighted incompressible Stokes problem is standard for a given
c\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
, see e.g
[
39
, Section 53.2]
. Testing (
39
) with
\bm{v}={\bm{u}}[c]
and using the incompressibility of
{\bm{u}}[c]
gives the identities

\int_{\Omega}\mu(c)|\varepsilon({\bm{u}}[c])|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=F({\bm{u}}[c]),\quad\mathcal{J}_{\bm{u}}(c)=-\frac{1}{2}F({\bm{u}}[c])=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mu(c)|\varepsilon({\bm{u}}[c])|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(42)

and the auxiliary functional has the same structure as the scalar case

{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}:{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}\to(-\infty,+\infty],\quad{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}(c):=\begin{cases}-\omega\mathcal{J}_{c}(c)-\omega\mathcal{J}_{\bm{u}}(c),&c\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)},\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(43)

Even in this case,
{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}
is bounded from below by the minimum of (
37
) multiplied by
-\omega
and we have
-\omega E({\bm{u}}[c])\leq{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}(c)
for all
c\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
. Stationarity with respect to a given
c
yields

\mu^{\prime}(c)(|\varepsilon({\bm{u}})|^{2}-c)=0\quad\text{a.e. in }\Omega.

3.3
Lower semicontinuity of the auxiliary functional

We then prove the lower semicontinuity of
{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}
. First we prove the continuity of
\mathcal{J}_{c}
on
{L_{0}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
; then, we prove the continuity of
\mathcal{J}_{u}
and
\mathcal{J}_{\bm{u}}
on
{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
. Note that both spaces
{L_{0}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
and
{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
are closed in the
d-
topology.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let Assumption
2.9
hold. Then, the functional
-\omega\mathcal{J}_{c}
defined in (
28
) is continuous on
{L_{0}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
in the topology induced by the metric (
17
).

Proof:
We use the notation
h(r):=\Phi(r)-\frac{1}{2}\mu(r)r
,
h^{\prime}(r)=-\frac{1}{2}\mu^{\prime}(r)r
; hence

h(r)=\frac{1}{2}\int^{r}_{0}-\mu^{\prime}(s)s\,\mathrm{d}s.

Combining (
11
) with Assumption
2.6
yields

A\mu(s)\leq\omega s\mu^{\prime}(s)\leq B\mu(s),

(44)

with
A=\frac{{{S}^{-}_{\phi}}-1}{2}
and
B=\frac{{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}-1}{2}
when
\Phi
is convex, while
A=\frac{1-{{S}^{+}_{\phi}}}{2}
and
B=\frac{1-{{S}^{-}_{\phi}}}{2}
when
\Phi
is concave.
Integrating both sides from zero to
r
, we obtain

A\Phi(r)\leq-\omega h(r)\leq B\Phi(r)
,
and thus
-\omega\mathcal{J}_{c}(c)
is positive and controlled by the modular of
c
in
{L_{0}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
.

Defining
q(\eta):=-\omega h(\psi^{-1}(\eta))
with
\eta:=\psi(r)
and
\psi
given by (
14
), straightforward calculations yield

\displaystyle q^{\prime}(\eta)=-\omega\frac{h^{\prime}(r)}{\psi^{\prime}(r)}=r\frac{\omega\mu^{\prime}(r)/2}{\sqrt{\omega\mu^{\prime}(r)/2}}=\sqrt{\frac{r}{2}}\sqrt{\omega\mu^{\prime}(r)r}\leq\sqrt{\frac{B}{2}}\sqrt{\mu(r)r}\leq L\psi(r)=L\eta,

(45)

where the first inequality follows from the upper bound in (
44
) while the second inequality follows from the lower bound in statement (i) of Lemma (
2.10
), thus the constant is
L=\sqrt{\frac{B}{2C_{1}}}
.
From (
45
), we obtain

\left|q(\eta_{2})-q(\eta_{1})\right|=\left|\int^{\eta_{2}}_{\eta_{1}}q^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq\frac{L}{2}\left|\eta^{2}_{2}-\eta^{2}_{1}\right|=\frac{L}{2}\left|\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}\right|\left|\eta_{2}+\eta_{1}\right|,\quad\forall\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\geq 0.

(46)

Let
\{c_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in{L_{0}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
such that
c_{n}\to c^{*}
in the
d
-topology; then, since
{L_{0}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
is closed,
c^{\ast}\in{L_{0}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
. Using the notation
\eta_{n}:=\Psi(c_{n})
,
\eta^{*}:=\Psi(c^{*})
, we have, using (
46
), the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, a triangle inequality, and the definition of the metric (
17
)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{J}_{c}(c_{n})-\mathcal{J}_{c}(c^{*})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\eta_{n}-\eta^{*}\right|\left|\eta_{n}+\eta^{*}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L}{2}\|\eta_{n}+\eta^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\eta_{n}-\eta^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{L}{2}(d(c_{n},c^{*})+2\|\eta^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)})d(c_{n},c^{*})\to 0,

and thus
-\omega\mathcal{J}_{c}
is continuous on
{L_{0}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let Assumption
2.9
hold. Under the Assumptions
3.2
and
3.3
, the functionals
-\omega\mathcal{J}_{u}
defined in (
32
) and
-\omega\mathcal{J}_{\bm{u}}
defined in (
38
) are continuous on
{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
in the
d
-topology for all
\epsilon\in(0,1)
.

Proof:
Let
\{c_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
such that
c_{n}\to c^{*}
in the
d
-topology; then, since
{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
is closed in the
d
-topology,
c^{\ast}\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
. By Chebyshev’s inequality,
\Psi(c_{n})\to\Psi(c^{\ast})
in measure on
\Omega
. Since
\Psi^{-1}
is continuous, we also have
c_{n}\to c^{\ast}
in measure, as well as
\mu(c_{n})\to\mu(c^{\ast})
since
\mu
continuous and finite on
{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
for (
31
).

We first give the proof for the scalar case, and show that
u[c_{n}]
converges to
u[c^{\ast}]
in
{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}
; this allows us to prove the statement since we obtain
F(u[c_{n}])\to F(u[c^{\ast}])
given that
F\in{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{\ast}
and, using the identity in (
34
),
-\omega\mathcal{J}_{u}(c_{n})\to-\omega\mathcal{J}_{u}(c^{\ast})
. For convenience, we write
a_{n}:=\mu(c_{n})
,
a^{\ast}:=\mu(c^{\ast})
,
u_{n}:=u[c_{n}]
,
u^{\ast}:=u[c^{\ast}]
,
then, from (
33
)

\int_{\Omega}a_{n}\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x=F(v)=\int_{\Omega}a^{\ast}\nabla u^{\ast}\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\forall v\in{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}.

Subtracting
\int_{\Omega}a_{n}\nabla u^{\ast}\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x
from both sides yields

\int_{\Omega}a_{n}\nabla(u_{n}-u^{\ast})\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(a^{\ast}-a_{n})\nabla u^{\ast}\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x.

Choosing
v=u_{n}-u^{\ast}
we obtain

\int_{\Omega}a_{n}|{\nabla(u_{n}-u^{\ast})}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(a^{\ast}-a_{n})\nabla u^{\ast}\cdot\nabla(u_{n}-u^{\ast})\,\mathrm{d}x.

Using the lower bound
a_{n}\geq\mu_{-}
from (
31
), and a Cauchy–Schwarz inequality argument we get

\|\nabla(u_{n}-u^{\ast})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{\mu_{-}}\|(a_{n}-a^{\ast})\nabla u^{\ast}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

The right-hand side of the above inequality is uniformly integrable since
u^{\ast}\in{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}
and

|(a_{n}-a^{\ast})\nabla u^{\ast}|^{2}\leq 4\mu_{+}^{2}|\nabla u^{\ast}|^{2}.

Therefore, since
a_{n}\to a^{\ast}
in measure, by Lebesgue-Vitali theorem
[
18
, Theorem 4.5.4]
,
\|\nabla(u_{n}-u^{\ast})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\to 0
. By Poincaré’s inequality, convergence of the gradients implies
u[c_{n}]\to u[c^{\ast}]
in
{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}
.

The proof for the incompressible vector-valued case is very similar. Using the shorthand notation

{\bm{u}}_{n}:={\bm{u}}[c_{n}]
,
{\bm{u}}^{\ast}:={\bm{u}}[c^{\ast}]
,

p_{n}:=p[c_{n}]
, and
p^{\ast}:=p[c^{\ast}]
,
from (
39
) and (
40
) we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}a_{n}\varepsilon({\bm{u}}_{n}-{\bm{u}}^{\ast})\!:\!\varepsilon(\bm{v})\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\bm{v}\,(p_{n}-p^{\ast})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(a^{\ast}-a_{n})\,\varepsilon({\bm{u}}^{\ast})\!:\!\varepsilon(\bm{v})\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\forall\bm{v}\in{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})},

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot({\bm{u}}_{n}-{\bm{u}}^{\ast})\,q\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall q\in Q.

Choosing the divergence-free test function
\bm{v}={\bm{u}}_{n}-{\bm{u}}^{\ast}
yields

\int_{\Omega}a_{n}|{\varepsilon({\bm{u}}_{n}-{\bm{u}}^{\ast})}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(a^{\ast}-a_{n})\varepsilon({\bm{u}}^{\ast})\!:\!\varepsilon({\bm{u}}_{n}-{\bm{u}}^{\ast})\,\mathrm{d}x.

Repeating the same arguments, using the symmetric gradient instead of the plain gradient, we arrive at
\|\varepsilon({\bm{u}}_{n}-{\bm{u}}^{\ast})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\to 0
and thus, by Korn’s and Poincaré’s inequalities,
{\bm{u}}[c_{n}]\to{\bm{u}}[c^{\ast}]
in
{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Under the assumptions of Theorems
3.4
and
3.5
, the functional
{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}
defined in (
35
) for the scalar case and (
43
) in the incompressible vector-valued case is lower semicontinuous in the
d
-topology for all
\epsilon\in(0,1)
.

Proof:
Lower semicontinuity accounts for proving that

{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}(c^{\ast})\leq\liminf_{n\to+\infty}{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}(c_{n})

for all sequences
\{c_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
that converge to
c^{\ast}\in{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
. We split the proof into two cases.

Suppose
c^{\ast}\notin{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
, then
{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}(c^{\ast})=+\infty
. Since
{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
is closed with respect to
d
, then eventually
c_{n}\notin{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
because the complement of
{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
is open, and thus
\liminf_{n\to+\infty}{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}(c_{n})=+\infty
.

Suppose now that
c^{\ast}\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
. If
c_{n}\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
only for finitely many terms, then
\liminf_{n\to+\infty}{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}(c_{n})=+\infty
, and thus, for such sequences, the functional is lower semicontinuous. It only remains to consider the case where
c_{n}\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
for infinitely many terms, having indices
I:=\left\{n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}:c_{n_{k}}\in{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}\right\}
. Since the terms of the sequences for which
n\notin I
do not lower the limit inferior, we have

\liminf_{n\to+\infty}{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}(c_{n})=\liminf_{k\to+\infty}{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}(c_{n_{k}}).

From Theorems
3.4
and
3.5
,
{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}
is continuous on
{L_{\epsilon}^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
, and thus
{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}(c_{n_{k}})\to{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}(c^{\ast})
, proving that the functional is lower semicontinuous.

3.4
Gradient flow in metric space

In this section, we study the minimization of the auxiliary energy
{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}
using the theory of gradient flows in metric spaces
[
2
]
. Using the notation
C:={L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
, we consider the metric functional system as in
[
28
]

\displaystyle(C,d,{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}),\text{ where }(C,d)\text{ is a complete metric space},

(47)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}:C\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]\text{ is a proper, lower semicontinuous functional, bounded from below},

and provide sufficient conditions for the existence of Evolution Variational Inequalities (EVI). We first recall the definition of
\mathrm{EVI}_{\lambda}
-gradient flows.

Definition 3.7

(
\mathrm{EVI}_{\lambda}
gradient flows)
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
(C,d,{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}})
a metric functional system as in (
47
). An
\mathrm{EVI}_{\lambda}
-gradient flow of
{\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}}
in
{\text{dom}({\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}})}
is a family of continuous maps
S_{t}:{\text{dom}({\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}})}\rightarrow{\text{dom}({\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}})}
with
t\geq 0
, such that for every
c_{0}\in{\text{dom}({\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}})}

\displaystyle S_{t+s}(c_{0})

\displaystyle=S_{s}(S_{t}(c_{0})),\quad\forall t,s\geq 0,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\downarrow 0}S_{t}(c_{0})

\int_{\Omega}\mu(c_{h})\nabla\zeta_{h}\cdot\nabla v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\mu^{\prime}(c_{h})\nabla u_{h}[c_{h}]\cdot\nabla v_{h}\,w_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x=0,\quad\forall v_{h}\in U_{h},

(67)

that, when tested with
v_{h}=u_{h}[c_{h}]
, gives the identity

\int_{\Omega}\mu^{\prime}(c_{h})|\nabla u_{h}[c_{h}]|^{2}w_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\mu(c_{h})\nabla\zeta_{h}\cdot\nabla u_{h}[c_{h}]\,\mathrm{d}x.

(68)

Substituting (
68
) in (
66
), we obtain

{\mathcal{DE}_{h}}(c_{h})[w_{h}]=\int_{\Omega}\frac{-\omega\mu^{\prime}(c_{h})}{2}\left(|\nabla u_{h}[c_{h}]|^{2}-c_{h}\right)\,w_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(69)

We then compute the first variation of the auxiliary energy in the incompressible vector-valued case

{\mathcal{DE}_{h}}(c_{h})[w_{h}]=\int_{\Omega}-\omega\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mu^{\prime}(c_{h})c_{h}-\frac{1}{2}\mu^{\prime}(c_{h})\,|\varepsilon({\bm{u}}_{h}[c_{h}])|^{2}\right)w_{h}+\omega\mu(c_{h})\,\varepsilon({\bm{u}}_{h}[c_{h}])\!:\!\varepsilon(\bm{\zeta}_{h})\,\mathrm{d}x

where

\bm{\zeta}_{h}:=\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}{\bm{u}}_{h}[c_{h}+\delta w_{h}]\right|_{\delta=0}.

Using the notation

\pi_{h}:=\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}p_{h}[c_{h}+\delta w_{h}]\right|_{\delta=0},

a differentiation of the Stokes constraint (
39
)–(
40
)
yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu(c_{h})\,\varepsilon(\bm{\zeta}_{h})\!:\!\varepsilon(\bm{v}_{h})\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\pi_{h}\,\nabla\cdot\bm{v}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\mu^{\prime}(c_{h})\,\varepsilon({\bm{u}}_{h}[c_{h}])\!:\!\varepsilon(\bm{v}_{h})\,w_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\forall\bm{v}_{h}\in\bm{U}_{h},

(70)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}q_{h}\,\nabla\cdot\bm{\zeta}_{h}\,\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h}.

(71)

Testing the first equation with
\bm{v}_{h}={\bm{u}}_{h}[c_{h}]
and using the incompressibility of
{\bm{u}}_{h}[c_{h}]
, we arrive at

{\mathcal{DE}_{h}}(c_{h})[w_{h}]:=\int_{\Omega}\frac{-\omega\mu^{\prime}(c_{h})}{2}\left(|\varepsilon({\bm{u}}_{h}[c_{h}])|^{2}-c_{h}\right)\,w_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(72)

The same first variation is obtained if homogeneous essential boundary
conditions are replaced by homogeneous or non-homogeneous natural boundary conditions. In that case the state problem is posed on the corresponding
linear space
W_{h}
, normalized, as usual, when the operator has non-trivial kernel, and the right-hand side is replaced by

F_{N}(v_{h}):=F(v_{h})+\int_{\partial\Omega}g_{N}\,v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Since
F_{N}
is independent of
c_{h}
, differentiating the state equation gives the same linearized problems as above, and therefore the same cancellation. We then show how to handle the case of non-homogeneous essential boundary conditions; we just consider the scalar case, since similar reasoning holds for the incompressible vector-valued case and is omitted for brevity. With non-homogeneous essential boundary conditions,
U_{h}
is an affine space and the state problem is

\int_{\Omega}\mu(c_{h})\nabla u_{h}[c_{h}]\cdot\nabla v_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x=F(v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in U_{h,0},

where
U_{h,0}
denote the space with homogeneous boundary conditions. The contribution of the elliptic problem is

\mathcal{J}_{u_{h}}(c_{h})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mu(c_{h})|\nabla u_{h}[c_{h}]|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-F(u_{h}[c_{h}]),

with first variation

\mathcal{DJ}_{u_{h}}(c_{h})[w_{h}]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\mu^{\prime}(c_{h})|\nabla u_{h}[c_{h}]|^{2}w_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\mu(c_{h})\nabla u_{h}[c_{h}]\cdot\nabla\zeta_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x-F(\zeta_{h}).

where
\zeta_{h}\in U_{h,0}
since the lifting is independent on
c_{h}
. Therefore,
\zeta_{h}
is an admissible test function in the state equation, giving the identity

\int_{\Omega}\mu(c_{h})\nabla u_{h}[c_{h}]\cdot\nabla\zeta_{h}\,dx=\mathcal{F}(\zeta_{h}),

and thus yielding the same first variation (
69
).

Remark 4.2
.

The discretization of the method of minimizing movements given in (
50
) reads

c_{h,n+1}\in\argmin_{c_{h}\in C_{h,\epsilon}}\left\{{\mathcal{E}_{h}}(c_{h})+\frac{1}{2\tau}\int_{\Omega}|\psi(c_{h})-\psi(c_{h,n})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right\},\quad C_{h,\epsilon}=\{c_{h}\in C_{h}:\epsilon\leq c_{h}\leq\epsilon^{-1}\}.

A formal derivation of the Euler–Lagrange equations, ignoring the box constraints, leads to

\int_{\Omega}\psi^{\prime}(c_{h,n+1})\left[\frac{\psi(c_{h,n+1})-\psi(c_{h,n})}{\tau}-\psi^{\prime}(c_{h,n+1})\bigl(|Du_{h}[c_{h,n+1}]|^{2}-c_{h,n+1}\bigr)\right]w_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x=0,\quad\forall w_{h}\in C_{h},

which can be formally read as an implicit Euler scheme in the metric coordinate
\eta_{h}=\psi(c_{h})

\frac{\eta_{h,n+1}-\eta_{h,n}}{\tau}=\psi^{\prime}(c_{h,n+1})\bigl(|Du_{h}[c_{h,n+1}]|^{2}-c_{h,n+1}\bigr),\quad\eta_{h,n+1}=\psi(c_{h,n+1}),

tested against variations induced by
w_{h}\in C_{h}
.

Following
[
61
]
, the differential
{\mathcal{DE}_{h}}(c_{h})\in C_{h}^{*}
is turned into a gradient
\nabla_{g_{c_{h}}}{\mathcal{E}_{h}}(c_{h})\in C_{h}
by solving the variational problem

g_{c_{h}}(\nabla_{g_{c_{h}}}{\mathcal{E}_{h}}(c_{h}),w_{h})={\mathcal{DE}_{h}}(c_{h})[w_{h}],\quad\forall w_{h}\in C_{h},

where the inner product arising from the discrete metric tensor is

g_{c_{h}}(z_{h},w_{h}):=\bigl(\mathcal{D}\Psi(c_{h})z_{h},\mathcal{D}\Psi(c_{h})w_{h}\bigr)_{{L^{2}(\Omega)}}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\omega}{2}\mu^{\prime}(c_{h})z_{h}w_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Note that, since
C_{h}
is a finite-dimensional linear space, its tangent space at
each point is canonically
C_{h}
itself. Moreover, under the sole Assumption
2.4
,
\omega\mu^{\prime}(c_{h})>0
on
C_{h}^{+}
, and therefore
g_{c_{h}}(\cdot,\cdot)
defines an inner product on
C_{h}
for all
c_{h}\in C^{+}_{h}
. Note that this distinction between derivatives and gradients is standard in
Hilbert-space PDE-constrained optimization

[
55
]
and
Bayesian inverse problems, where the prior covariance determines the geometry used to identify dual quantities with admissible
directions
[
70
]
.

In the present setting, the metric tensor, equivalently the Riesz map

\mathcal{G}(c_{h}):C_{h}\rightarrow C^{\ast}_{h},\quad\langle\mathcal{G}(c_{h})w_{h},z_{h}\rangle=g_{c_{h}}(z_{h},w_{h}),\quad c_{h}\in C^{+}_{h},\,w_{h},z_{h}\in C_{h}

(73)

is explicitly known, and we do not need to solve any additional variational problem to define the gradient. This is in contrast with many gradient-flow equations, where the so-called Onsager operator
K_{h}(c_{h}):C_{h}^{*}\to C_{h}
, mapping forces to velocities, is specified first and the metric tensor is only implicitly defined as its inverse
G_{h}(c_{h})=K_{h}(c_{h})^{-1}
.

Using the first variation of the functional given in (
69
) for the scalar case, (
72
) for the vector-valued case, and the short-hand notation

to denote
\nabla u
in the scalar case and
\varepsilon({\bm{u}})
in the vector-valued case, the metric gradient flow approach therefore gives

\nabla_{g_{c_{h}}}{\mathcal{E}_{h}}(c_{h})=c_{h}-|Du_{h}[c_{h}]|^{2},

and it allows us to consider the ordinary differential equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{dc_{h}}{dt}&=|Du_{h}[c_{h}]|^{2}-c_{h},\\
c_{h}(0)&=c_{h,0}\in C^{+}_{h}.\end{aligned}\right.

(74)

Assumption
4.1
ensures that the pointwise ODE is equivalent to the semi-discrete problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} \int_{\Omega}\frac{dc_{h}}{dt}w_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x&=\int_{\Omega}(|Du_{h}[c_{h}]|^{2}-c_{h})w_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\forall w_{h}\in C_{h},\\
c_{h}(0)&=c_{h,0}\in C^{+}_{h}.\end{aligned}\right.

(75)

Moreover,
{\mathcal{E}_{h}}
is a Lyapunov function, since

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{E}_{h}}(c_{h}(t))=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\omega}{2}\mu^{\prime}(c_{h}(t))\,\dot{c}_{h}^{\,2}(t)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq 0,\quad\dot{c}_{h}:=\frac{dc_{h}}{dt}.

\lambda=p-1
, since

\left(\frac{\mu^{\prime\prime}(r)}{2\mu^{\prime}(r)}-\frac{2\mu^{\prime}(r)}{\mu(r)}\right)_{+}r=\frac{4-3p}{4}.

Introducing the notation for the squared distance in the discrete metric

d_{h}(t):=g_{c_{h}(t)}(\dot{c}_{h}(t),\dot{c}_{h}(t))=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\mu^{\prime}(c_{h}(t))|(c_{h}(t)-|Du_{h}[c_{h}(t)]|^{2})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(84)

we are then ready to state the last Theorem of this Section. The proof is provided in Lemma

Theorem 4.8
.

Let the assumptions of Theorems

4.2
Choice of the finite element spaces

We first consider the case of a simplicial element
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and we use
\mathcal{P}_{k}(T)
to denote the space of polynomials in
d
variables with degree at most
k
.
Given a polynomial of degree
k
, the choice of the finite element space for the discretization of functions in
{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}
is standard

U^{k,T}_{h}:=\big\{u_{h}\in{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}:u_{h}\in C^{0}(\Omega),{u_{h}}_{|T}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(T)\big\}.

Given
u_{h}\in U^{k,T}_{h}
, we have
\nabla{u_{h}}_{|T}\in\mathcal{P}_{k-1}(T)
, and thus
|\nabla{u_{h}}|^{2}
is a polynomial of degree
2(k-1)
on each
T
; a suitable discretization space for functions in
{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
that satisfies Assumption
4.1
is then

C^{k,T}_{h}:=\big\{c_{h}\in{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}:{c_{h}}_{|T}\in\mathcal{P}_{2k-2}(T)\big\}.

Similar considerations hold using tensor-product cells
K
. In such a case, we use

U^{k,K}_{h}:=\big\{u_{h}\in{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}:u_{h}\in C^{0}(\Omega),{u_{h}}_{|K}\in\mathcal{Q}_{k}(K)\big\},

where
\mathcal{Q}_{k}(K)
denotes the space of polynomials in
d
variables with degree at most
k
for each variable. Since in this case
\nabla{u_{h}}_{|K}\in\mathcal{Q}_{k}(K)
, a sufficient choice for the discretization space for functions in
{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}
is

C^{k,K}_{h}:=\big\{c_{h}\in{L^{\Phi}(\Omega)}:{c_{h}}_{|K}\in\mathcal{Q}_{2k}(K)\big\}.

The same matching conditions hold true when
\nabla u_{h}
is replaced by the symmetric gradient
\varepsilon({\bm{u}}_{h})
; in the case of vector-valued spaces, we will consider the Taylor-Hood pairs with
k\geq 2

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{h}^{k,T}

\displaystyle:=\big\{{\bm{u}}_{h}\in{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}:{\bm{u}}_{h}\in C^{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}),{{\bm{u}}_{h}}_{|T}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(T)^{d}\big\},

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{k,T}

\displaystyle:=\big\{p_{h}\in Q:p_{h}\in C^{0}(\Omega),\,{p_{h}}_{|T}\in\mathcal{P}_{k-1}(T)\big\}.

or, in case of tensor-product cells

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{h}^{k,K}

\displaystyle:=\big\{{\bm{u}}_{h}\in{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}:{\bm{u}}_{h}\in C^{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}),{{\bm{u}}_{h}}_{|K}\in\mathcal{Q}_{k}(K)^{d}\big\},

\displaystyle Q_{h}^{k,K}

\displaystyle:=\big\{p_{h}\in Q:p_{h}\in C^{0}(\Omega),\,{p_{h}}_{|K}\in\mathcal{Q}_{k-1}(K)\big\}.

Remark 4.9
.

The finite element spaces described above are only one possible choice.
Any inf–sup stable pair
\bm{U}_{h}\times Q_{h}
may be used for the
Stokes discretization, provided that
C_{h}
is chosen rich enough to contain

|\varepsilon({\bm{u}}_{h})|^{2}
for all
{\bm{u}}_{h}\in\bm{U}_{h}
.

4.3
Time discretization algorithms

We first describe the time discretization for the scalar problem.
The modifications required for the incompressible vector-valued case are given at the end of the Section. Throughout, the subscript
h,n
denotes finite element functions at the
n
th iteration. Also, we drop the cell and order superscripts and always write
U_{h}
,
C_{h}
,
\bm{U}_{h}
,
Q_{h}
for the finite element spaces, assuming matching order conditions. We do not provide convergence results for the time-advancement schemes described
below; rigorous convergence proofs are left for future work.

For a given initial condition
c_{h,0}\in C_{h}
, we first compute the initial state
u_{h,0}
by solving the weighted Poisson problem: Find
u_{h,0}\in U_{h}
such that for all
v_{h}\in U_{h}

a[c_{h,0}](u_{h,0},v_{h})=F(v_{h}),

where
a[\cdot](\cdot,\cdot)
is given by (
26
).
As in our previous work on biological transportation networks
[
49
]
, we use the backward Euler method to discretize (
75
) in time and consider the nonlinear system of equations: Given
(u_{h,n},c_{h,n})
and a current time step
\tau_{n}
, find
(u_{h,n+1},c_{h,n+1})
such that for all
(v_{h},w_{h})\in U_{h}\times C_{h}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{c_{h,n+1}-c_{h,n}}{\tau_{n}}\,w_{h}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(|\nabla u_{h,n+1}|^{2}-c_{h,n+1})\,w_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(85)

\displaystyle a[c_{h,n+1}](u_{h,n+1},v_{h})

\displaystyle=F(v_{h}).

Note that the backward Euler ensures the strict positivity of
c_{h}
throughout the time iteration, since

c_{h,n+1}=\frac{\tau_{n}}{1+\tau_{n}}|\nabla u_{h,n+1}|^{2}+\frac{1}{1+\tau_{n}}c_{h,n}.

(86)

Although strict positivity of
c_{h}
is preserved throughout the time iteration, the time-step nonlinear system can be difficult to solve directly, because Newton iterates need not preserve such positivity if not at convergence.

When only the steady state is of interest, one may instead consider the split iteration

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{c_{h,n+1}-c_{h,n}}{\tau_{n}}\,w_{h}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(|\nabla u_{h,n}|^{2}-c_{h,n+1})\,w_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(87)

\displaystyle a[c_{h,n+1}](u_{h,n+1},v_{h})

\displaystyle=F(v_{h}),

which completely decouples the two equations and, given our choice of finite element spaces, allows us to replace the first variational equation for the time advancement of
c_{h}
with the exact interpolation formula

c_{h,n+1}=\frac{\tau_{n}}{1+\tau_{n}}|\nabla u_{h,n}|^{2}+\frac{1}{1+\tau_{n}}c_{h,n}.

(88)

Remark 4.10
.

Using
\tau_{n}=+\infty
in (
88
), or equivalently a forward Euler scheme with
\tau_{n}=1
, leads to the Kačanov iteration scheme
[
47
,
58
]
, which, as noted for example in
[
33
, Example 20]
, can fail in some cases for the power-law case and
p>2
; furthermore, using the update
c_{h,n+1}=|\nabla u_{h,n}|^{2}
requires dynamical clipping of the weights of the Poisson equation since the strict positivity of
|\nabla u_{h,n}|^{2}
cannot be guaranteed.

In numerical experiments, we found that the split-iteration scheme does not always converge to the system’s steady state and may require small time steps to avoid divergence.
A robust alternative we have found, for which we do not currently have any proof of reliable convergence, borrows ideas from the pseudo-transient continuation scheme
[
59
]
and its extension to differential-algebraic equations
[
26
]
. In our pseudo iteration scheme, we first compute an intermediate value for
c_{h}
, denoted by
c_{h,n+1/2}
, by using the interpolation formula (
88
), and then perform a single Newton step by linearizing (
85
) around
(c_{h,n+1/2},u_{h,n})

\begin{bmatrix}(\tau_{n}^{-1}+1)M&-2B^{n}\\
C^{n}&K^{n}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta c_{h}\\
\delta u_{h}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
g\end{bmatrix},

where the matrix and vector entries are

\displaystyle M_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle B^{n}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\phi_{i}\nabla u_{h,n}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle C^{n}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mu^{\prime}(c_{h,n+1/2})\,\phi_{j}\nabla u_{h,n}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle K^{n}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mu(c_{h,n+1/2})\,\nabla\varphi_{i}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle g_{i}

\displaystyle=F(\varphi_{i})-\int_{\Omega}\mu(c_{h,n+1/2})\,\nabla u_{h,n}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x,

with
\phi_{i}
and
\varphi_{i}
the basis functions for
C_{h}
and
U_{h}
respectively. Note that the first component of the residual is zero because of the choice of the partial update. Unlike the fully implicit (
85
) and the split (
87
) steps, in this case the new iterate
c_{h,n+1}=c_{h,n+1/2}+\delta c_{h}
is not guaranteed to remain strictly positive and we thus need to backtrack the direction
\delta c_{h}
(together with
\delta u_{h}
) until this condition is met. Note that, in the fully nonlinear case (
85
), we always start the Newton iteration from
c_{h,n+1/2}
.

Remark 4.11
.

The pseudo-time stepping strategy enjoys a very favorable property, since the Schur complement obtained after eliminating the
c_{h}
variable

S^{n}:=K^{n}+2\alpha_{n}C^{n}M^{-1}B^{n},\quad\alpha_{n}:=\frac{\tau_{n}}{1+\tau_{n}},

(89)

is symmetric and positive definite. The proof is given in the Appendix Lemma
A.8
. This implies that we can leverage scalable preconditioning strategies, such as Algebraic Multigrid
[
73
]
or Balancing Domain Decomposition methods
[
76
]
, to efficiently solve the linear problem in the pseudo-time stepping algorithm.

Time advancement for the incompressible vector-valued case is analogous to the scalar case, with the difference that the weighted Poisson problem is replaced by a weighted Stokes problem, and the equations for
c_{h}
involve the symmetric gradient. We state here only the fully implicit backward Euler step: Given
({\bm{u}}_{h,n},p_{h,n},c_{h,n})
and a current time step
\tau_{n}
, find
({\bm{u}}_{h,n+1},p_{h,n+1},c_{h,n+1})
such that for all
(\bm{v}_{h},q_{h},w_{h})\in\bm{U}_{h}\times Q_{h}\times C_{h}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{c_{h,n+1}-c_{h,n}}{\tau_{n}}\,w_{h}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(|\varepsilon({\bm{u}}_{h,n+1})|^{2}-c_{h,n+1})\,w_{h}\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\bm{a}[c_{h,n+1}]({\bm{u}}_{h,n+1},\bm{v}_{h})-b(\bm{v}_{h},p_{h,n+1})

\displaystyle=F(\bm{v}_{h}),

\displaystyle b({\bm{u}}_{h,n+1},q_{h})

\displaystyle=0,

where
\bm{a}[\cdot](\cdot,\cdot)
and
b(\cdot,\cdot)
are given in (
41
). The matrices and the right-hand side defining the pseudo update are the same, except that inner products are replaced by Frobenius inner products, symmetric gradients are used in place of plain gradients, and the matrix blocks accounting for the incompressibility condition of the weighted Stokes problem are included. The velocity Schur complement obtained by eliminating the
c_{h}
variable in the pseudo iteration scheme is also positive definite.

4.4
Adaptive time step selection

Since we are only interested in the steady state of the system, in our numerical experiments we will also make use of a heuristic adaptive time step strategy. The time step is chosen adaptively using numerical residual norms

\rho_{h}^{c}(c_{h},u_{h}):=\|R_{h}^{c,*}(c_{h},u_{h})\|_{\ell^{2}},\quad\rho^{f}_{h}(c_{h},u_{h}):=\|\mathcal{R}_{h}^{f,*}(c_{h},u_{h})\|_{\ell^{2}}

where
\mathcal{R}_{h}^{c,*}(c_{h},u_{h})
is the assembled residual vector corresponding to the right-hand side of the ODE, and
\mathcal{R}_{h}^{f,*}(c_{h},u_{h})
the assembled vector including also the constraint residual.
We reject the time step if

E(u_{h,n+1})>E(u_{h,n})+10^{-4}\text{ and }\rho_{1,n}^{c}>\rho_{1/2,n}^{c},

where
E
is the original energy (
21
) and
\rho_{j,n}^{c}:=\rho_{h}^{c}(c_{h,n+j},u_{h,n+j})
, with the convention
u_{h,n+1/2}=u_{h,n}
.
This means that we accept an increase in the energy
E
if it reduces the residual norm of the right-hand side of the ODE and, vice versa, accept the step if an increase in the ODE right-hand side leads to a decrease in the original energy. In case of rejection, we restore the previous iterate and set
\tau_{n+1}=0.1\,\tau_{n}
.

If the step is accepted, the next time step value is selected according to the rule

\tau_{n+1}=\begin{cases}\tau_{n},&\rho_{1,n}^{c}\leq\rho_{1/2,n}^{c},\\
\displaystyle\tau_{n}\min\left\{\frac{\rho^{f}_{1/2,n}}{\rho^{f}_{1,n}},2\right\},&\rho_{1,n}^{c}>\rho_{1/2,n}^{c},\end{cases}

where
\rho_{j,n}^{f}:=\rho_{h}^{f}(c_{h,n+j},u_{h,n+j})
.
The rescaling factor used after an accepted step is thus based on two
residual indicators. The first is the
c
-component residual, which measures how close the auxiliary variable is to the
fixed-point relation
c_{h}=|Du_{h}|^{2}
, and that is used to decide whether the
adaptive rescaling is activated. The second indicator is the fully coupled
residual as in the conventional pseudo-transient continuation, which contains both the residual of the
c_{h}
-equation and
the residual of the discrete state
equation. Hence, the time step is enlarged up to a conservative factor of two only if the fully coupled residual decreases, but
is reduced if the fully coupled residual increases.

5.1.3
Carreau-Yasuda model for incompressible fluids

For the next experiment, we use the Carreau–Yasuda model
[
74
,
75
]
(see also

[
17
, Section 4.1.a]
), for which the viscosity law is

\mu(r)=\mu_{\infty}+(\mu_{0}-\mu_{\infty})\left(1+\rho^{a}r^{a/2}\right)^{\frac{n-1}{a}},\quad a>0,\quad\rho>0,\quad\mu_{0}>\mu_{\infty}\geq 0,\quad n>0,\quad n\neq 1

where
\mu_{0}
is the low-shear viscosity,
\mu_{\infty}
is the high-shear limiting viscosity,
\rho
is a time-scale parameter,
a
controls the width of the transition between regimes, and
n
is the power-law index. For
0<n<1
, the model is shear-thinning, meaning that the viscosity decreases as the shear rate increases, as for example in polymer solutions, blood, paints, and biological and industrial fluids. For
n>1
, the model is shear-thickening; the viscosity increases with the shear rate, as for example in cornstarch and water mixtures. In the intermediate regime, the model behaves like a power-law fluid.

A direct calculation shows

\phi(t)=\frac{t^{2}}{2}\left[\mu_{\infty}+\Delta\mu\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1-n}{a},\frac{2}{a};1+\frac{2}{a};-\rho^{a}t^{a}\right)\right],\quad\Delta\mu:=\mu_{0}-\mu_{\infty},

where
{}_{2}F_{1}
is the Gauss hypergeometric function. Moreover, setting
z:=\rho^{a}r^{a/2}
and
q:=\frac{n-1}{a}
, we find

\mu^{\prime}(r)=\frac{\Delta\mu(n-1)}{2r}z(1+z)^{q-1},\quad\mu^{\prime\prime}(r)=\frac{\Delta\mu(n-1)}{4r^{2}}z(1+z)^{q-2}\bigl[(a-2)+(n-3)z\bigr],

which gives us, using
t^{2}:=r
and (
11
)

\frac{t\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)}{\phi^{\prime}(t)}=1+\frac{2r\mu^{\prime}(r)}{\mu(r)}=1+(n-1)\frac{\Delta\mu\,z(1+z)^{q-1}}{\mu_{\infty}+\Delta\mu(1+z)^{q}}.

Therefore, for
n<1
,
\Phi
is strictly concave, while for
n>1
,

6
Conclusions

In this work we introduced an auxiliary gradient-flow framework for generalized Newtonian-type variational problems. The main idea is to replace the nonlinear constitutive dependence on the gradient or strain by an auxiliary scalar variable, so that each state solve is a uniformly elliptic weighted linear problem.

At the continuous level, we studied the auxiliary energy in a metric space adapted to the growth of the N-function
\phi
. Under suitable assumptions, we proved lower semicontinuity, geodesical
\lambda
-convexity, and exponential convergence of the corresponding minimizing-movement scheme. At the discrete level, we derived a finite-dimensional metric gradient flow using an appropriate Riesz map, proved global well-posedness of the resulting ODE on the positive cone of the discretized functional space, and established convergence to the finite element solution under additional structural assumptions. At the discrete level, the choice of the Riesz map is not only a technical device for defining gradients: it is precisely what transforms the first variation of the auxiliary energy into the evolution
\frac{dc}{dt}=|Du[c]|^{2}-c
.

A concrete consequence of the theory is obtained for power-law models: the auxiliary gradient-flow framework gives guaranteed convergence in the ranges
4/3\leq p<2
and
2<p\leq 4
, while the regimes
p<4/3
and
p>4
remain outside the present theory. For these latter cases, the discrete formulation nevertheless suggests asymptotic convergence rate estimates near equilibrium, and the numerical experiments are consistent with these predictions.

We also proposed several time-discretization strategies, including an operator-splitting scheme related to the Kačanov iteration and a time-adaptive pseudo-transient method that can be implemented using scalable linear solvers. The numerical experiments show that the proposed approach is robust across several models, including power-law, Carreau–Yasuda, regularized Bingham, and optimal-design
examples, and is competitive with Newton’s method in the tested regimes.

Future work will focus on refining the convergence theory of the ODE, the convergence analysis of the fully discrete algorithms, the development of adaptive mesh-refinement techniques, and possible extensions to a broader, less regular class of models, including those depending on
\varepsilon({\bm{u}})

[
68
]
. The adaptive time stepping procedure can also be improved by extending the techniques in
[
30
, Section 6.4]
and
[
4
]
to the differential-algebraic case.

\displaystyle\mu(\widehat{c}_{h})|\nabla v_{h}|^{2}+2\alpha_{n}\mu^{\prime}(\widehat{c}_{h})(\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}\cdot\nabla v_{h})^{2}
