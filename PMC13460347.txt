Oxidative Damage Induced Disruption of Mitochondrial Quality Control Underlying Abnormal Energy Metabolism in Peripheral Leukocytes of Schizophrenia Patients
Authors: Shuhui Li, Wenli Zhu, Jie Jiang, Yangying Bu, Qian Wang, Minghui Li, Dandan Wang, Yan Gao, Jinfeng Wang, Tianqi Wang, Xiaowen Hu, Chunling Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisMitochondrial dysfunction is increasingly implicated in schizophrenia (SZ) pathogenesis. To maintain mitochondrial homeostasis under cellular stress, a sophisticated mitochondrial quality control (MQC) mechanism has developed, encompassing mitochondrial biogenesis, dynamics, and mitophagy.Study DesignThis study systematically evaluated MQC in peripheral leukocytes of 42 SZ patients and 43 healthy controls through morphological analysis, MQC gene expressions, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) maintenance, and oxidative damage. Besides, we validated the regulatory effects of oxidative stress on MQC in vitro using a neuronal model treated with hydrogen peroxide.Study ResultsWe observed mitochondrial fragmentation in SZ, characterized by increased organelle numbers with reduced sizes. This was supported by imbalanced MQC, with expression of biogenesis-related genes SIRT1 and TFAM upregulated (P = .008 and 0.027, respectively), and mitophagy receptor gene PHB2 suppressed (P = .041), indicating enhanced biogenesis but impaired mitophagy. Despite enhanced biogenesis, mtDNA copy number was lower (P < .001) with more oxidative damage (P = .037). Furthermore, we discovered deficits in antioxidant capacity, including reduced coenzyme Q10 levels and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, with SOD decline correlating with mtDNA depletion. This suggests redox imbalance contributes to MQC dysregulation, supported by findings from an oxidation-damaged neuronal model. Moreover, disrupted MQC functionally impaired energy metabolism, reflected by downregulated NDUFV1 expression (P = .031) and increased lactate-to-pyruvate ratios (P < .001).ConclusionsOur findings demonstrated MQC imbalance in SZ, manifested as mitochondrial fragmentation and mtDNA depletion, probably resulted from oxidative damage. These disruptions may underlie the energy metabolism abnormalities in SZ.Keywords: oxidative stress, mitochondrial biogenesis, mitochondrial dynamics, mitophagy, mtDNA copy number

Introduction

Schizophrenia (SZ) is a severe mental disorder caused by complex genetic and environmental factors, affecting approximately 1% of the global population.1 Despite the distinct mental manifestations, abnormal energy metabolism has been proposed as a systemic feature of SZ patients, as repeatedly evidenced in peripheral blood and postmortem brains.2–4 Reduced cellular respiration and abnormalities in oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) have been documented in SZ across multiple tissue types, including downregulation of OXPHOS gene expression in postmortem brain5–7 and lymphoblastoid cells derived from patients,8 reduced enzyme activity in the postmortem frontal cortex and caudate nucleus,9,10 and decreased respiratory rates in patient-derived lymphoblastoid cell lines,11 collectively pointing to mitochondrial dysfunction.

This growing body of evidence highlights the multifaceted nature of mitochondrial dysfunction in SZ patients, which overlaps with various aspects of neuronal function that are compromised in this disorder,12,13 and may play a role in its pathogenesis.14 Mitochondria are essential organelles involved in various complex cellular processes.15 They serve as the primary source of cellular energy and are also the main site for reactive oxygen species (ROS) production.16 Research has indicated that mitochondrial morphology is closely related to energy demand and supply.17 At the ultrastructural level, mitochondria exhibit morphological features that reflect their functional states. Changes in mitochondrial size, number, and membrane integrity have been associated with cellular stress and mitochondrial dysfunction.17 Some studies have reported smaller sizes and fewer amount of mitochondria in brain tissues18,19 and peripheral lymphocytes20 of SZ patients compared with healthy controls (HC). However, other studies have found no anomalous size or number of mitochondria in brain tissues,21 or reported increased sizes of mitochondria in peripheral lymphocytes22 of SZ patients. These contradictions may be influenced by the effects of antipsychotics,22 which has been reported to directly alter mitochondrial morphology.23 Therefore, it is preferable to study antipsychotic-naïve patients or those who have undergone a drug washout period in mitochondria-related research.

The mitochondrial homeostasis and cellular energy metabolism are maintained through the process of mitochondrial quality control (MQC), which refers to the sophisticated coordination of mitochondrial biogenesis in response to cellular demands, dynamics regulating mitochondrial fission-fusion networks, and mitophagy for clearing damaged mitochondria.24 Some studies have shown that mitochondrial dysfunction would be amplified by failing MQC processes, especially in cells that consume a large amount of energy like neurons.25 Indeed, studies have revealed alterations in MQC among patients with psychiatric disorders.26 In SZ patients, alterations in mitochondrial fusion and perturbations in network connectivity have been observed11,27; however, the overall processes involved in MQC, particularly the roles of mitophagy and mitochondrial biogenesis, have not yet been fully clarified.

The MQC processes sense and respond to various cellular stressors including ROS, to mediate cellular homeostasis.28–30 ROS functions as a signaling molecule that modulates mitochondrial biogenesis,31 dynamics,32 and mitophagy.33,34 Under physiological conditions, these coordinated responses may help maintain mitochondrial homeostasis; however, when dysregulated, they may contribute to mitochondrial damage. Increased ROS levels and decreased antioxidant capacity have been reported in the brain and peripheral tissues of SZ patients, impacting neural activities in this disorder.35 Thus, we propose that the disrupted redox homeostasis in SZ patients may affect the MQC processes and contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction.

Peripheral leukocytes, particularly lymphocytes, have been increasingly recognized as accessible surrogate cells for studying psychiatric disorders. Although lymphocytes and neurons originate from different germ layers, they share many common signaling pathways and biological processes, including neurotransmitter receptor signaling and mitochondrial function.36,37 More importantly, accumulating evidence suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction in SZ is a systemic feature, with abnormalities observed not only in postmortem brain tissue but also in peripheral cells.38,39 Furthermore, gene expression studies have demonstrated that approximately half of SZ-related candidate genes are expressed in both whole blood and prefrontal cortex, with a moderate transcriptome correlation between these tissues.40 While peripheral leukocytes cannot fully capture central nervous system (CNS)-specific pathology, this convergence of systemic mitochondrial alterations supports their use as a minimally invasive window into the disorder. Therefore, investigating MQC in peripheral leukocytes may provide valuable insights into the broader pathophysiology of SZ.

In this study, we aimed to comprehensively evaluate MQC in SZ patients through morphological analysis, gene expression assessment of core MQC regulators, and evaluation of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) maintenance. We further investigated potential contribution of redox equilibrium to MQC. Our objective is to characterize MQC alterations in SZ and explore their potential contribution to the pathogenesis of the disorder.

Methods

Patients with SZ and HC were recruited from the Fourth People’s Hospital of Wuhu, Anhui Province, China, following the guidelines of the local ethics committee.

Inclusion criteria for SZ patients: (1) meeting the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnostic criteria for SZ; (2) aged 18-60, any gender; (3) not using any antipsychotic medication for at least 4 weeks prior to enrollment. Exclusion criteria for SZ patients: (1) metabolic disorders such as hypertension, diabetes, or metabolic syndrome; (2) dementia, brain trauma, or other neurological or psychiatric disorders; (3) pregnancy.

Inclusion criteria for healthy controls: (1) diagnosed with no psychiatric disorders through the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview; (2) aged 18-60, any gender; (3) not using any antipsychotic medication; (4) no personal or family history of psychiatric disorders. Exclusion criteria for healthy controls: (1) metabolic disorders such as hypertension, diabetes, or metabolic syndrome; (2) dementia, brain trauma, neurological or psychiatric disorders; (3) pregnancy.

We used the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)41 to estimate the current severity of illness in SZ. We documented patients’ atypical antipsychotic use prior to the drug washout period.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Medical Ethical Committee of the Fourth People’s Hospital of Wuhu (No. 2020–KY–17). We obtained written informed consent from all participants.

Additional methodological details are provided in the Supplementary material.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

A total of 42 SZ patients and 43 healthy controls were included in this study. The demographic and clinical characteristics of all subjects are shown in Table S3. No significant differences were observed between the 2 groups in terms of sex, age, or BMI. All patients underwent a washout period of at least 4 weeks, during which they did not take any antipsychotic drugs, other psychotropic medications, or non-psychiatric drugs. Prior to the washout period, the patients’ medication regimens were as detailed in Table S3. The medications included olanzapine (n = 12, 28.6%), clozapine (n = 10, 23.8%), risperidone (n = 9, 21.4%), aripiprazole (n = 8, 19.0%), and amisulpride (n = 3, 7.1%).

Dysregulation of Mitochondrial Quality Control in Schizophrenia

We first observed mitochondrial morphology through electron microscopy in 9 SZ patients and 6 healthy controls. Demographic and clinical information for these subjects is shown in Table S4, with no significant differences between the 2 groups in sex distribution, age, or BMI. Representative images are shown in Figure 1A, depicting lymphocytes from a healthy control and a SZ patient. The cell shown in the SZ panel exhibits a deeply invaginated nuclear profile, which may appear as 2 separate nuclear sections due to the plane of sectioning; such nuclear configurations can occasionally be observed in lymphocytes and do not indicate cellular damage or apoptosis.42–44 We found that the number of mitochondria per unit area of cytoplasm each cell (NA) was significantly higher in the SZ patients (P = .034, Figure 1A and B) compared to the HC group, while the mitochondrial cross-sectional area was significantly decreased (P = .018, Figure 1C and D). Additionally, we identified mitochondria with ruptured membranes exclusively in the SZ patients (Figure 1C). These results suggest that the SZ patients exhibited morphological and structural aberrance in mitochondria compared to HCs.

Further, we investigated transcriptional levels of genes involved in the MQC processes that regulate mitochondrial morphology. Both GAPDH and ACTB show good stability with coefficients of variation 2.68% and 3.27%, respectively. GAPDH was chosen for further analysis as its Ct value is closer to the targets than ACTB.

We found significantly increased expression levels of mitochondrial biogenesis-related genes SIRT1 and TFAM (P = .008 and P = .027, respectively, Figure 1E), along with a significantly decreased expression of the mitophagy-related gene PHB2 (P = .041, Figure 1F) in the SZ group compared to the HC group. No significant differences in the expression levels of genes associated with mitochondrial fission and fusion were found between the 2 groups (Figure S1). Nevertheless, this consistent pattern of dysregulation, with upregulation of biogenesis-related genes alongside downregulation of the mitophagy-related gene, together with the morphological evidence of increased mitochondrial number and reduced size, suggests a possible imbalance in MQC processes in SZ patients. This potential dysregulation, characterized by upregulation of biogenesis-related gene expression without coordinated fusion or effective clearance of damaged mitochondria, is consistent with the fragmented mitochondrial morphology observed in the SZ group.

Of note, after applying False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction for multiple testing within the MQC gene family, the differences in SIRT1, TFAM, and PHB2 expression levels were no longer statistically significant (adjusted P = .080, 0.135, and 0.137, respectively), indicating that these individual gene expression findings should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory.

Decreased mtDNA Copy Number, Increased Oxidative mtDNA Damage, and Antioxidant Deficiency in Schizophrenia

Given that mtDNA content is regulated by mitochondrial biogenesis process and is extremely sensitive to mitochondrial damage,45,46 we aimed to evaluate the changes in mtDNA copy number in SZ patients. We found a significantly decreased mtDNA copy number in the SZ group compared to the HC group (P < .001, Figure 2A), suggesting a defect in the MQC where mtDNA replication was insufficient to support the increased mitochondrial biogenesis observed in SZ patients. Furthermore, increased degree of 8-OHdG in mtDNA was observed in the SZ group compared to the HC group (P = .037, Figure 2B), suggesting a severe oxidative mitochondrial damage in SZ patients.

Further, the deficits in antioxidant capacity were found in SZ patients, revealing decreased CoQ10 levels (P = .022, Figure 2C), an endogenous mitochondrial antioxidant,47 and reduced activity of antioxidant enzyme SOD (P = .0021, Figure 2D). Notably, the SOD activity decline was correlated with mtDNA depletion in the SZ group (r = 0.403, P = .008, Figure 2E), suggesting that the defects in antioxidant capacity may contribute to the defects in maintaining mtDNA and the subsequent dysregulation of MQC.

Oxidative Stress-Induced Dysregulation of Mitochondrial Quality Control In Vitro

To elucidate the potential regulatory relationship between oxidative stress and the altered MQC in SZ patients, we used the model of oxidation-damaged neurons (PC12 differentiated cells) that we previously established.48 PC12 differentiated cells were treated with 40 or 80 μM H2O2 for 24 h to assess the mtDNA copy number and oxidative mtDNA damage, and expression levels of genes related to mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy, which showed significant differences in SZ patients.

We found that the mtDNA copy number was significantly decreased in PC12 differentiated cells treated with 40 and 80 μM H2O2 for 24 h (P = .0049 and P = .0017, respectively, Figure 3A), together with the increased oxidative mtDNA damage in PC12 differentiated cells treated with 80 μM H2O2 (P = .0499, Figure 3B). Notably, the correlation between decreased mtDNA copy number and increased oxidative mtDNA damage showed a trend toward significance (r = −0.650, P = .067, Figure 3C). These findings suggest that the mtDNA depletion and its oxidative damage observed in SZ patients could be partially simulated by oxidative stimulation in vitro, with oxidative damage to mtDNA potentially contributing to its depletion.

Furthermore, the gene expression levels of SIRT1 and TFAM were both significantly increased in PC12 differentiated cells treated with 80 μM H₂O₂ for 24 h (P = .029 and 0.020, respectively, Figure 3D), consistent with the direction of change observed in SZ patients. The expression levels of 3 genes related to mitophagy also exhibited significant changes in PC12 differentiated cells treated with H₂O₂ (Figure 3E), although the specific pattern of alterations differed from that observed in SZ patients.

Taken together, these results provide supportive evidence that oxidative stress may contribute to the MQC dysregulation observed in SZ patients, while the discrepancies in mitophagy-related gene expression highlight the limitations of an acute oxidative model in recapitulating the chronic pathophysiology of the disorder.

Abnormal Energy Metabolism in Patients with Schizophrenia

To further explore the potential impact of aberrant MQC, we analyzed OXPHOS-related gene expression and plasma lactate/pyruvate levels that reflect energy metabolism in SZ patients. Given that complex I is the primary site of the OXPHOS defects in SZ patients,49 and studies have also identified abnormalities in complexes IV9,10 and V,50 we focused primarily on the expression levels of key genes in these 3 complexes. We found a significantly decreased expression level of the mitochondrial complex I gene NDUFV1 in the SZ group compared to the HC group (P = .031, Figure 4A), while no significant differences in the expression levels of genes related to complex IV and V were observed (Figure S1). Of note, after FDR correction within the OXPHOS gene family, this difference did not retain statistical significance (adjusted P = .124). suggesting that this transcriptional finding should be interpreted with caution. To further assess mitochondrial functional status, we measured plasma lactate and pyruvate levels, as the lactate-to-pyruvate ratio reflects the NAD+/NADH cytoplasmic redox and energy states.2,51 We found significantly increased lactate concentrations (P = .0012, adjusted P = 0018), decreased pyruvate concentrations (P = .026, adjusted P = .026) and increased lactate-to-pyruvate ratios (P < .001, adjusted P < .001) in the SZ group compared to the HC group (Fig. 4b), robustly supporting abnormal energy metabolism in SZ patients.

Besides, we analyzed the relationships between the above mitochondrial measures and course of disease, medication history, and disease severity in SZ patients. No significant correlations were found between course of disease and any mitochondrial measures. Similarly, neither previous medication type nor dose (converted to chlorpromazine equivalents,52 Table S3) was associated with any mitochondrial measures. Regarding disease severity, the expression levels of mitochondrial biogenesis-related genes SIRT1 showed a significant positive correlation with the PANSS-N scores (r = 0.418, P = .014), but not with the total PANSS-TOTAL scores, PANSS-P scores, or PANSS-G scores. No other mitochondrial measures correlated significantly with PANSS scores.

We further explored correlations between ultrastructural and molecular measures in the EM subsample (n = 15). No significant correlations were observed (data were not shown). Given the small sample size, these null findings should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the MQC in SZ patients in terms of the organellar morphology, gene expression of core MQC regulators, and mtDNA maintenance, while exploring oxidative stress as a potential driver of MQC defects and its subsequent impact on energy metabolism. Our findings revealed an increased number of mitochondria with reduced sizes in SZ patients. Furthermore, qPCR showed higher expression levels of genes related to mitochondrial biogenesis, but lower levels of gene related to mitophagy, which is consistent with the morphological observation of increased mitochondrial number and reduced size, suggesting a possible imbalance in MQC processes. Notably, despite enhanced biogenesis, the mtDNA replication was insufficient, as evidenced by decreased mtDNA copy number and elevated mtDNA oxidative damage. Moreover, we discovered deficits in antioxidant capacity that may account for the MQC defects observed in SZ patients. Finally, we explored the potential consequences of aberrant MQC and found abnormalities in energy metabolism.

Our study demonstrated an enhancement in the mitochondrial biogenesis, evidenced by morphological observation of an increased mitochondrial number alongside transcriptional activation of related genes. Importantly, all SZ patients in our study underwent a drug washout period of at least 4 weeks; thus, our results may partially exclude the influence of antipsychotic drugs. Although patients had previously received long-term antipsychotic treatment prior to the washout period, we found no significant correlation between mitochondrial indicators and medication dosage, drug type, or disease duration. Further validation of these results in first-episode, drug-naïve patients is warranted. Mitochondrial biogenesis responds to cellular demands and regulates mitochondrial quantity by generating new mitochondria through mtDNA replication and the synthesis of mitochondrial lipids and proteins.53 SIRT1 serves as a key metabolic sensor that adjusts mitochondrial mass to meet cellular functional requirements and plays a major role in regulating mitochondrial biogenesis.54 TFAM stimulates mitochondrial biogenesis by enhancing the expression of mitochondrial-encoded polypeptides.55 This study showed significantly increased expression levels of SIRT1 and TFAM, together with increased mitochondrial number. While these findings do not directly demonstrate enhanced biogenesis, they are consistent with the possibility that mitochondrial biogenesis may be upregulated in SZ patients, potentially as a compensatory response to cellular stress including oxidative stress.55

Studies have shown that mild oxidative stress would induce mitochondrial biogenesis as a compensatory mechanism, both in vivo and in vitro.56–58 Increased ROS levels have been repeatedly documented in SZ patients and are proposed to be involved in the pathogenesis of SZ.35 Our study illustrated the transcriptional activations of SIRT1 and TFAM in neurons induced by oxidative stress in vitro, which is consistent with the observations in SZ patients. This concordance supports the possibility that the enhancement of mitochondrial biogenesis may be a compensatory adaptive response to oxidative stress in SZ patients, potentially contributing to the higher number of mitochondria observed in these patients (Figure 5).

Higher Quantity but Lower Quality of Mitochondria in Schizophrenia: Pathological Cascade and Functional Consequences. Increased mitochondrial biogenesis drives compensatory proliferation of organelles in SZ patients, likely responding to chronic oxidative stress. However, this maladaptive response fails to restore functional capacity due to oxidative damage-induced mtDNA depletion combined with mitochondrial fragmentation, collectively disrupting OXPHOS efficiency. Additionally, the mitophagy suppression exacerbates the vicious cycle of oxidative damage by impairing clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria. The high energy demands of synaptic transmission render neurons acutely vulnerable to mitochondrial dysfunction; the impaired energy metabolism disrupts neurotransmission, leading to abnormal neural activity implicated in SZ pathogenesis. Abbreviations: HC = healthy controls; OXPHOS = oxidative phosphorylation; SZ = schizophrenia.

In contrast to the increase in mitochondrial number, we observed a significant decrease in mtDNA copy number in SZ patients, indicating mtDNA depletion in this disorder. This result was in line with findings from 2 other studies involving antipsychotic-naïve patients or those who underwent a drug washout period.59,60 Studies suggest that mtDNA replication does not occur simultaneously with organelle proliferation31,61; thus, the mtDNA content may vary independently of the amount of mitochondria, especially under physiological or pathological conditions, such as oxidative stress.31 Our study found that reduced antioxidant capacity was associated with the mtDNA depletion, and found increased oxidative mtDNA damage in SZ patients. These suggest that oxidative stress may induce mtDNA damage and lead to a decrease in its content, which was also observed in the oxidation-damaged neuronal model. These oxidative lesions in mtDNA primarily induce single-strand DNA breaks (SSBs), impairing the integrity of replication templates and leading to a reduction in the availability of mtDNA templates.62 Thus, we speculate that the mtDNA depletion is likely caused by mitochondrial oxidative damage in SZ patients (Figure 5). Additionally, studies have shown that DNA polymerase γ was a target of oxidative stress, thereby affecting mtDNA replication.63 Future research is needed to determine whether DNA polymerase γ is also affected in SZ patients. Importantly, the observed mtDNA depletion aligns with prior reports establishing mtDNA copy number as a biomarker of cellular oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction across various pathological contexts.46,64

Moreover, we found no consistent increase in mitochondrial fusion alongside enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis, suggesting that the newly formed mitochondria failed to mature into larger organelles with adequate mtDNA through sufficient fusion. Fusion promotes the capacity of OXPHOS and facilitates the exchange of metabolites, proteins, and mtDNA, thus maintaining mitochondrial stability and enabling a response to urgent stress events.65,66 Research has shown a decrease in the fusion protein OPA1 in the brains and peripheral tissues of SZ, indicating an impaired fusion process.11 Our transcriptional data revealed no activation of the fusion pathway concurrent with the upregulation of mitochondrial biogenesis. This mismatch between mitochondrial biogenesis and fusion could lead to accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria in SZ patients.

We identified fragmented mitochondria in SZ patients, characterized by smaller sizes and significantly ruptured membranes. These results further indicated damaged mitochondrial structures and potential impairment of energy metabolism, as reflected by decreased expression level of the mitochondrial complex I gene and increased lactate-to-pyruvate ratios. In line with our results, studies have shown that energy metabolism in fragmented mitochondria shifts from OXPHOS towards glycolysis,67 and impaired mitochondrial network dynamics are linked to deficits in cellular respiration and abnormalities in OXPHOS complex I in SZ patients.11 We hypothesize that mitochondrial biogenesis in SZ patients is compensatively enhanced to increase organellar content in response to excessive ROS, but this maladaptive response fails to restore functional capacity of mitochondria. The compromised ability of energy metabolism could be attributed to accumulated oxidative damage and incoordinate MQC. Thus, we observed higher quantity but lower quality of mitochondria in SZ patients (Figure 5). Given the high energy demands of neurons, the mitochondrial dysfunction in SZ may be more pronounced in the CNS, significantly disrupting synaptic transmission and leading to abnormal neural activity, which is implicated in the pathogenesis of SZ12 (Figure 5).

Mitochondrial dysfunction typically triggers mitophagy to remove damaged organelles, thereby ensuring mitochondrial homeostasis for sustained cellular respiration68,69and synaptic integrity.70 However, defective mitophagy may lead to the accumulation of toxic proteins and a decline in mitochondrial function, exacerbating oxidative stress and nerve cell degeneration.71,72 Notably, pharmacological enhancement of mitophagy has been shown to improve synaptic plasticity, redox status, and mitochondrial function.73 PHB2, a highly conserved membrane scaffold protein, has recently been identified as a novel inner membrane mitophagy receptor that mediates mitophagy.74 Our result showed decreased gene expression levels of PHB2 in SZ patients, indicating altered expression of this mitophagy-related marker. However, as mitophagy is a dynamic flux process, transcript-level changes alone are insufficient to infer functional impairment. This transcriptional alteration, together with the presence of damaged mitochondria observed in electron microscopy, raises the possibility of mitophagy dysregulation, while further studies incorporating protein-level and functional readouts are needed to confirm this observation. Research has also identified mitophagy defects in mice with redox dysregulation and in early psychosis patients, highlighting that such defects may contribute to the vicious cycle of oxidative damage.75 Intriguingly, our results showed that oxidative stress induced more pronounced transcriptional changes in mitophagy-related genes in neurons than those observed in SZ patients, potentially reflecting the acute H2O2 challenge in vitro versus chronic, mild oxidative stress in vivo. The causal interplay between oxidative stress and mitophagy dysregulation in SZ patients warrants systematic exploration.

Although the acute H₂O₂ exposure in PC12 cells differs from the chronic oxidative stress thought to occur in SZ, this in vitro model allowed us to test whether oxidative stress alone could reproduce the mtDNA depletion, oxidative damage, and gene expression changes observed in patients. The consistency between the in vitro and human findings supports a potential causal role for oxidative stress in MQC dysregulation, while the discrepancies in mitophagy-related gene expression underscore the limitations of an acute model in fully recapitulating the chronic pathophysiology of the disorder.

Notably, the substantial overlap in values between SZ and control subjects across multiple measures suggests that MQC dysregulation is not a universal feature of all SZ patients but may characterize a specific subgroup. This heterogeneity highlights the complex nature of SZ and raises the possibility that oxidative stress-related mitochondrial dysfunction represents a pathophysiological mechanism relevant to a subset of patients, which could inform future patient stratification and targeted therapeutic approaches.

This study presents several limitations. First, it is a cross-sectional investigation that identified perturbations in MQC, increased oxidative mitochondrial damage, and decreased energy metabolism in SZ patients. Although we have conducted preliminary validation of the potential causal relationship between oxidative stress and MQC dysregulation using an in vitro neuronal model, it is important to note that this acute H₂O₂ exposure differs from the chronic oxidative stress in SZ, and the discrepancies in mitophagy-related gene expression between the model and patient samples highlight this limitation. Therefore, further studies using chronic oxidative stress models or patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived neurons, as well as longitudinal designs, are needed to confirm these findings and better recapitulate disease-relevant conditions. Second, we could not exclude several potential confounders known to impact mitochondrial function, including lifestyle factors, early life trauma, and self-harm behaviors. Additionally, all patients had been on long-term antipsychotic medication prior to the at least 4-week washout period. Therefore, further validation of our findings in first-episode, drug-naïve patients is necessary. Third, we recognize that we did not directly assess mitochondrial oxidative stress and mitochondrial function, such as mitochondrial ROS levels and cellular respiration, respectively, due to sample type and feasibility considerations. Fourth, the lack of leukocyte subtype information restricts our analysis to peripheral total leukocytes; therefore, our conclusions should not be generalized to SZ pathophysiology. Future studies should isolate distinct leukocyte subpopulations to determine whether the observed MQC alterations reflect specific immune cell changes and to explore their relevance to CNS pathology. Fifth, the electron microscopy substudy included 9 SZ patients and 6 healthy controls, resulting in a smaller and imbalanced sample size between groups. This was due to the sample availability at the time of processing, rather than selective inclusion. The total number of mitochondria analyzed was 354 in the SZ group and 169 in the HC group, which reflects the higher mitochondrial density in SZ patients (approximately 8.6 vs. 6.5 mitochondria per cell). Nevertheless, future studies with larger and balanced cohorts are needed to confirm these morphological findings. Sixth, while we implemented strict pre-analytical handling including rapid processing at 4°C and exclusion of hemolyzed samples, the inherent instability of lactate and pyruvate warrants caution in interpreting these metabolic ratios.

Our findings suggest an imbalance in MQC processes in SZ patients, characterized by potential compensatory increases in mitochondrial number and biogenesis alongside impaired mitochondrial quality, manifested through reduced organelle sizes and mtDNA depletion, with altered expression of mitophagy-related markers. This imbalance suggests that mitochondrial proliferation occurs at the expense of functional quality, likely associated with accumulated oxidative damage. Our study reinforces mitochondrial dysfunction as a feature of SZ, with MQC dysregulation potentially linking oxidative stress and bioenergetic deficits. These findings emphasize the need for longitudinal studies to determine whether MQC imbalance represents a primary defect or a secondary consequence of disease progression. Future work using patient-derived neuronal models could clarify the temporal dynamics of MQC disruption and test interventions targeting redox homeostasis or MQC coordination. Advancing our understanding of MQC alterations could provide insights to therapeutic strategies to restore mitochondrial resilience in SZ.
