The complexity of Stoquastic Sparse Hamiltonians
Alex B. Grilo, Marios Rozos

Abstract
Despite having an unnatural definition, 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{StoqMA} plays a central role in Hamiltonian complexity, e.g., in the classification theorem of the complexity of Hamiltonians by Cubitt and Montanaro (SICOMP 2016). Moreover, it lies between the two randomized extensions of 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{NP}} , 𝖬𝖠 {\mathsf{MA}} and 𝖠𝖬 {\mathsf{AM}} . Therefore, understanding the exact power of 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{StoqMA} (and hopefully collapsing it with more natural complexity classes) is of great interest for different reasons. In this work, we take a step further in understanding this complexity class by showing that the Stoquastic Sparse Hamiltonians problem ( 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖲𝖧 \mathsf{StoqSH} ) is in 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{StoqMA} . Since Stoquastic Local Hamiltonians are 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{StoqMA} -hard, this implies that 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖲𝖧 \mathsf{StoqSH} is 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{StoqMA} -complete. We complement this result by showing that the separable version of 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖲𝖧 \mathsf{StoqSH} is 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathsf{StoqMA}(2) -complete, where 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathsf{StoqMA}(2) is the version of 𝖲𝗍𝗈𝗊𝖬𝖠 \mathsf{StoqMA} that receives two unentangled proofs.



1
Introduction

Stoquastic Hamiltonians and
\mathsf{StoqMA}
bridge a surprising connection between quantum Hamiltonian complexity and fundamental questions in classical complexity theory.

We recall that in the
k
-local Hamiltonian problem, we are given the description of a Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{j\in[m]}H_{j}
, where each term
H_{i}
acts non-trivially on
k
qubits of an
n
-qubit system, and two numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
, and the task is to decide whether the ground-state energy of
H
is below
\alpha
or above
\beta
, promised that one of the two cases holds. This is the quantum analogue of classical constraint satisfaction problems and was shown by Kitaev to be complete for
{\mathsf{QMA}}

[
23
]
, the quantum analog of
{\mathsf{NP}}
. The
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-completeness of the local Hamiltonian problem presents a promising way of studying key problems in condensed matter physics, such as entanglement in ground states of Hamiltonians, via a complexity-theoretic lens.

Given the importance of this problem, there has been much effort in understanding the complexity of the local Hamiltonian problem for subfamilies of Hamiltonians of practical interest
[
21
,
2
,
14
,
10
,
27
]
. In
[
7
,
9
,
11
]
, the authors study the complexity of the local
stoquastic
Hamiltonian problem, in which each term
H_{j}
has nonpositive off-diagonal elements. These Hamiltonians have ground states with non-negative amplitudes, which can be interpreted as a probability distribution, and thus they do not suffer from the “sign problem”, being more responsive to classical methods such as Monte Carlo simulations.

[
7
,
9
]
showed that the stoquastic local Hamiltonian problem with inverse polynomial
\beta-\alpha
is complete for
\mathsf{StoqMA}
.
\mathsf{StoqMA}
is defined to be the complexity class where we have a quantum proof, a classical reversible circuit, and a measurement in the Hadamard basis to decide acceptance/rejection. While such a complexity class may seem somewhat artificial, it plays a key role in the classification theorem on the complexity of local Hamiltonian problems
[
15
]
. From a complexity theory perspective,
\mathsf{StoqMA}
lies between the two randomized generalizations of
{\mathsf{NP}}
:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{NP}}\subseteq{\mathsf{MA}}\subseteq\mathsf{StoqMA}\subseteq{\mathsf{AM}}.

(1)

We note that
[
11
]
also showed that the stoquastic local Hamiltonian problem with
\alpha=0
and inverse polynomial
\beta
(also known as
frustration-free
Hamiltonian), is
\mathsf{MA}
-complete.
[
4
]
shows that such a problem actually sits in
{\mathsf{NP}}
if
\beta=\Omega(1)
.
Thus, understanding stoquastic Hamiltonians and
\mathsf{StoqMA}
provides an interesting path toward progress in the fundamental questions
{\mathsf{NP}}
vs.
{\mathsf{MA}}
and
{\mathsf{NP}}
vs.
{\mathsf{AM}}
.

One direction to shed new light on
\mathsf{StoqMA}
is to identify new problems that are complete for this class. Previous progress in this direction has focused on adding more structure to stoquastic local Hamiltonians. These include certain restricted Hamiltonian models – such as the Transverse field Ising Model on degree-3 graphs,
[
10
]
, and the Antiferromagnetic Transverse field Ising Model,
[
27
]
– or geometrically local stoquastic Hamiltonians, such as on 2D-lattices or 1D-lines,
[
28
]
, or on spatially sparse graphs,
[
31
]
.

In this work, we take a complementary approach by proving
\mathsf{StoqMA}
-completeness of a problem with
less
structure. In particular, we examine
sparse stoquastic Hamiltonians
. A Hamiltonian
H
is
d
-sparse, where
d=\operatorname{poly}(n)
, if each row of
H
has at most
d
nonzero entries, which either can be computed efficiently with a circuit or we assume that we have access to them via an oracle. Sparse Hamiltonians form a more general family of Hamiltonians than local Hamiltonians – in fact, every
k
-local Hamiltonian with
m
terms is
2^{k}m
-sparse. The main focus of this paper is understanding the computational complexity of stoquastic sparse Hamiltonians (
\mathsf{StoqSH}
).

1.1
Results

Our main contribution is showing that
\mathsf{StoqSH}
is
\mathsf{StoqMA}
-complete.

Theorem 1.1
.

\mathsf{StoqSH}_{\alpha,\beta}\text{ is }\mathsf{StoqMA}_{a,b}\text{-complete}
, where
a-b\geq 1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
.

The hardness direction follows immediately from the
\mathsf{StoqMA}
-hardness of the local variant
[
9
,
7
]
, since every
k
-local stoquastic Hamiltonian is a special case of a sparse stoquastic Hamiltonian. Our focus here is, therefore, to establish containment in
\mathsf{StoqMA}
.

The core difficulty lies in constructing a stoquastic verification procedure whose acceptance probability is a linear function of the energy expectation value
\expectationvalue{H}{\psi}
of a given witness state
\ket{\psi}
. In the general (non-stoquastic) case,
[
5
]
show that the sparse Hamiltonian problem is in
{\mathsf{QMA}}
by showing that one can use the Phase Estimation algorithm
[
22
,
26
]
to estimate the energy of a sparse Hamiltonian. This verification approach, however, is inherently quantum and cannot be leveraged by stoquastic verifiers. Moreover, the techniques of
[
9
]
to put stoquastic
local
Hamiltonians in
\mathsf{StoqMA}
do not extend to the sparse setting, since their approach consists of converting each local term of the Hamiltonian into a
\outerproduct{+}{+}
projection rotated by a classically reversible circuit.

We resolve this obstacle by exploiting the decomposition of a sparse Hamiltonian into
1
-sparse terms
[
6
]
. For each term in this decomposition, we construct a verifier that runs a “Hadamard-like" test using the witness state and the oracle that outputs the Hamiltonian matrix elements, and whose acceptance probability depends on
\expectationvalue{H}{\psi}
.

A crucial ingredient of our verification procedure is a generalization of
\mathsf{StoqMA}
verifiers that allows measurements on a polynomial number of qubits. We show that such a generalization does not increase the power of the class, incurring just a loss in the completeness and soundness parameters. This generalization of
\mathsf{StoqMA}
defines a class called
\mathsf{gStoqMA}
and showcases this interesting property of multi-qubit measurements in
\mathsf{StoqMA}
, which had not been considered before and may be of independent interest.

Theorem 1.2
.

\mathsf{StoqMA}_{a,b}\subseteq\mathsf{gStoqMA}_{a,b}\subseteq\mathsf{StoqMA}_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}
, where
a^{\prime}=\frac{1+a}{2}
and
b^{\prime}=\frac{1+b}{2}
.

1.2
Related work

Stoquastic sparse Hamiltonians have already appeared implicitly in several contexts, even though the corresponding Hamiltonian problem has not been studied directly. For example, in the proof that
\mathsf{StoqMA}\subseteq{\mathsf{AM}}

[
9
]
, Bravyi et al. observe that the proof does not require the Hamiltonian to be local, but works also in the case of sparsity. Regarding adiabatic evolution,
[
18
,
16
]
exhibited a subexponential oracle separation between classical and adiabatic quantum computation using frustrated (i.e. the ground energy is nonzero) sparse stoquastic Hamiltonians. More recently, building on the fixed-node Hamiltonian construction introduced in
[
30
]
, Bravyi et al.
[
8
]
showed that, given suitable guiding states, one can reduce a general Hamiltonian to a frustration-free stoquastic sparse Hamiltonian and use the latter within classical algorithms.
[
20
]
subsequently used this framework to prove that the Local Hamiltonian Problem with succinctly describable ground states is
{\mathsf{MA}}
-complete, even for general Hamiltonians.

1.3
Conclusions and open questions

In this work, we studied the complexity of the
Stoquastic Sparse Hamiltonian problem
and proved that it is
\mathsf{StoqMA}
-complete. Our approach relies on showing that
\mathsf{StoqMA}
admits certain multi-qubit measurements, which may be of independent interest. These results naturally motivate an investigation of the power of stoquastic verifiers, when the witness is restricted to be separable. To this end, we introduced
\mathsf{StoqMA}(k)
, the stoquastic analogue of
{\mathsf{QMA}}(k)
, and showed that
\mathsf{StoqMA}(2)
is complete for the Stoquastic Sparse Hamiltonian problem, when we have the guarantee of separable witnesses.

This places
\mathsf{StoqMA}(2)
as an interesting intermediate model between single-prover stoquastic verification and general multi-prover quantum verification, and raises several questions about the structure and limitations of stoquastic proof systems, which we mention below.

•

Robustness of
\mathsf{StoqMA}(k)
collapse.

We showed that
\mathsf{StoqMA}(k)\subseteq\mathsf{StoqMA}(2)
for certain values of completeness and soundness parameters. An important open problem would be whether this inclusion can be made more robust under improved values of parameters, or whether tighter error bounds introduce new subtleties in the stoquastic multi-prover setting.

•

Separable Stoquastic Local Hamiltonians.

An interesting problem, which we leave as an open question, is the complexity of the
Separable Stoquastic Local Hamiltonian
problem.
Since we do not have an algorithm for the stoquastic analogue of the Consistency of Local Density Matrices problem (which is of independent interest
[
24
]
), the proof of
[
13
]
that the Separable Local Hamiltonian problem is
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-complete cannot be directly adapted to this setting.

•

Upper bounds on
\mathsf{StoqMA}(2)
.

Perhaps the most intriguing question is to determine a nontrivial upper bound on
\mathsf{StoqMA}(2)
. A trivial bound is
\mathsf{StoqMA}(2)\subseteq{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
, but it is not clear whether this containment can be strengthened.

This question highlights an interesting tradeoff:
\mathsf{StoqMA}(2)
involves two unentangled quantum provers interacting with a highly restricted, nearly classical verifier. While it is known that
\mathsf{StoqMA}\subseteq{\mathsf{AM}}

[
9
]
, and that
{\mathsf{AM}}(k)={\mathsf{AM}}(2)={\mathsf{AM}}
, for
k=\operatorname{poly}(n)

[
1
]
, none of these techniques seem to directly extend to
\mathsf{StoqMA}(2)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\subseteq}}{\mathsf{AM}}
.

1.4
Concurrent work

At the time of submission, we became aware of an independent work
[
25
]
that has as central topic the study of
\mathsf{StoqMA}(k)
. That work develops a broader complexity-theoretic understanding of
\mathsf{StoqMA}(k)
, including showing that
\mathsf{StoqMA}(k)\subseteq{\mathsf{EXP}}
, stronger parameter regimes for simulating
\mathsf{StoqMA}(k)
by
\mathsf{StoqMA}(2)
, and some complete problems for
\mathsf{StoqMA}(k)
. Our results are complementary: while their work focuses on the structural and complexity-theoretic properties of unentangled stoquastic proof systems, we provide a Hamiltonian-complexity perspective by showing that the separable stoquastic sparse Hamiltonian problem is complete for
\mathsf{StoqMA}(2)
. This gives a natural complete problem for
\mathsf{StoqMA}(2)
, analogous to the role of separable sparse Hamiltonians for
{\mathsf{QMA}}(2)
of
[
13
]
.

2
Preliminaries

In this section we introduce the basic notions and tools used throughout the paper.

2.1
The Local Hamiltonian Problem

The local Hamiltonian problem is a quantum analogue of classical constraint satisfaction problems and plays a central role in quantum complexity theory, serving as the canonical complete problem for
{\mathsf{QMA}}
.

Definition 2.1

(
k
-local Hamiltonian)
.

A Hamiltonian
H
on
n
qubits is a Hermitian operator on
\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
, i.e., a complex Hermitian matrix of dimension
2^{n}\times 2^{n}
. A Hamiltonian on
n
qubits is called
k
-local
if it can be written as
H=\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{i}
, where each
H_{i}
acts on at most
k=O(1)
out of the
n
qubits.

Definition 2.2

(
k
-Local Hamiltonian Problem (
\mathsf{LH}_{\alpha,\beta}
))
.

For a
k
-local Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{j=1}^{m}H_{j}
on
n
qubits, where each
0\preceq H_{j}\preceq I
, decide whether
\lambda_{\min{}}(H)\leq\alpha
or
\lambda_{min{}}(H)\geq\beta
, where
\beta-\alpha\geq 1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
.

Kitaev
[
23
]
has shown that for
\alpha\text{ and }\beta
such that
\beta-\alpha=1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
, the
k
-local Hamiltonian Problem is
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-complete.

2.2
Sparse Hamiltonians

Definition 2.3

(Sparse Hamiltonian)
.

A Hamiltonian
H
on
n
qubits is said to be
d
-sparse
, where
d=\operatorname{poly}(n)
, if each row and column has at most
d
nonzero elements.

Definition 2.4

(Sparse Hamiltonian Problem (
\mathsf{SH}_{\alpha,\beta}
))
.

For a
d
-sparse Hamiltonian
H
on
n
qubits, where
0\preceq H\preceq I
, decide whether
\lambda_{\min{}}(H)\leq\alpha
or
\lambda_{min{}}(H)\geq\beta
, where
\beta-\alpha\geq 1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
.

A
k
-local Hamiltonian
H
is a special case of a sparse Hamiltonian, since each term acts only on
k
qubits and is therefore
2^{k}
-sparse, implying that
H
is
2^{k}m
-sparse, where
m
is the total number of local terms.

Furthermore, we suppose that the Hamiltonian is accessed via a pair of oracle unitaries
O_{F}
and
O_{H}
. The
O_{F}
oracle returns the location of the
i
-th nonzero entry
y_{i}
in a given row
x
,

O_{F}\ket{x,i}=\ket{x, y_i},

while the
O_{H}
oracle returns the entry of the Hamiltonian
H
for any given row
x
and column
y
,

O_{H}\ket{x,y, z}=\ket{x,y, z \oplus H_{x,y}}.

We give now a useful lemma regarding the decomposition of
d
-sparse Hamiltonians.

Lemma 2.5

(Sparse decomposition, Lemma 4.4 of
[
6
]
)
.

If
H
is a
d
-sparse Hamiltonian, there exists a decomposition
H=\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}}H_{j}
, where each
H_{j}
is 1-sparse and a query to any
H_{j}
can be simulated with
O(1)
queries to
H
.

Remark 2.6
.

An important property of this decomposition is that each nonzero matrix element of the Hamiltonian is uniquely assigned to one of the terms
H_{j}
of the decomposition. This will ensure that if
H
is stoquastic, then each term
H_{j}
will be stoquastic as well.

For a given
1
-sparse term
H_{j}
with real coefficients and a given row
x
, we denote
x\in H_{j}
, if there exists a nonzero element at row
x\in H_{j}
, and
x\not\in H_{j}
otherwise.

Remark 2.7

(Oracle Simulation)
.

If
x\in H_{j}
, we can access the location
f(x)
and the value
H^{(j)}_{x,f(x)}
of the unique nonzero element by querying an oracle
O_{f,H_{j}}
, which can be simulated with one query from
O_{f}
and
O_{H}
:

\displaystyle\ket{x, 0, 0}

\displaystyle\xmapsto{O_{f}}\ket{x, f(x), 0}

\displaystyle\xmapsto{O_{H}}\ket*{x, f(x), H_{x,f(x)}}.

In the case of 1-sparse Hamiltonians with real elements, it holds that
f(f(x))=x
. This means that starting from
\ket{x}
and with some ancilla qubits, one can produce the state
\ket{f(x)}
as follows:

\displaystyle\ket{x, 0, 0}

\displaystyle\xmapsto{O_{f,H_{j}}}\ket*{x, f(x), H_{x,f(x)}}

\displaystyle\xmapsto{\text{SWAP}}\ket*{f(x), x, H_{x,f(x)}}

\displaystyle\xmapsto{O_{f,H_{j}}}\ket*{f(x), x \oplus f(f(x)), H_{x,f(x)} \oplus H_{x,f(x)}}=\ket{f(x), 0,0},

and finally, we can ignore the ancilla register.

In order to also treat the case of applying
O_{f,H_{j}}
while
x\not\in H_{j}
, we can add an extra flag qubit that becomes
0
in that case and then we can condition the application of SWAP and the second oracle query on that qubit. So, in this case, we have that
f(x)=x
.

2.3
StoqMA and Stoquastic Hamiltonians

We recall now the definition of the complexity class
\mathsf{StoqMA}
, introduced in
[
7
]
, as well as the definition of Stoquastic Hamiltonians. We begin with the definition of stoquastic verifiers.

Definition 2.8

(Stoquastic Verifier,
[
7
]
)
.

A
stoquastic verifier
is a tuple
\mathcal{V}=(n,n_{w},n_{0},n_{+},U)
, where:

•

n
is the number of input bits,

•

n_{w}
is the number of input witness qubits,

•

n_{0}
is the number of input ancillas in the state
\ket{0}
,

•

n_{+}
is the number of input ancillas in the state
\ket{+}
, and

•

U
is a quantum circuit on
n+n_{w}+n_{0}+n_{+}
qubits with X, CNOT, and Toffoli gates.

The acceptance probability of a stoquastic verifier
\mathcal{V}
on input string
x\in\Sigma^{n}
and witness state
\ket{\psi}\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n_{w}}
is defined as
\Pr[\mathcal{V}\text{ accepts }(x,\ket{\psi})]=\expectationvalue{U^\dagger\Pi_\out U}{\psi_\inp}
, where
\ket{\psi_\inp}=\ket{x}\otimes\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{0}^{\otimes n_{0}}\otimes\ket{+}^{\otimes n_{+}}
is the initial state and
\Pi_{\text{out}}=\outerproduct{+}{+}_{1}\otimes I_{\text{else}}
projects the first qubit onto the state
\ket{+}
.

Definition 2.9

(
\mathsf{StoqMA}
,
[
7
]
)
.

A promise problem
\mathcal{L}=(\mathcal{L}_{\text{yes}},\mathcal{L}_{\text{no}})\text{ is in }\mathsf{StoqMA}_{a,b}
if there exists a uniform family of stoquastic verifiers, such that for any input
x\in\mathcal{L}
and for any fixed number of input bits
n
, there exist efficiently computable functions
a(n)
and
b(n)
and the corresponding verifier
\mathcal{V}
obeys:

Completeness
: If
x\in\mathcal{L}_{\text{yes}}
, then there is some
\ket{\psi}
s.t.
\Pr[\mathcal{V}\text{ accepts }(x,\ket{\psi})]\geq a(n)
.

Soundness
: If
x\in\mathcal{L}_{\text{no}}
, then for any
\ket{\psi}
,
\Pr[\mathcal{V}\text{ accepts }(x,\ket{\psi})]\leq b(n)
.

One can observe that, due to the fact the input state is non-negative, the soundness parameter is always at least
\frac{1}{2}
(see Remark 2.4 in
[
3
]
).

It is not known currently how to reduce the error probability for
\mathsf{StoqMA}
, and
[
3
]
has shown that error reduction implies
\mathsf{StoqMA}={\mathsf{MA}}
, as well as that
\mathsf{StoqMA}
with perfect completeness is contained in
{\mathsf{MA}}
. Therefore, one has to be careful on the parameters of completeness and soundness and how they translate on the hardness and containment proofs.

Definition 2.10

(Stoquastic Hamiltonian)
.

A
k
-local Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{j=1}^{m}H_{j}
is called stoquastic in the computational basis, if for all
j\in[m]
the off-diagonal elements of
H_{j}
in this basis are nonpositive. Similarly, a sparse Hamiltonian
H
is stoquastic in the computational basis if all the off-diagonal elements of
H
are nonpositive.

Definition 2.11

(
k
-Local Stoquastic Hamiltonian Problem (
\mathsf{StoqLH}_{\alpha,\beta}
))
.

For a
k
-local Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{j=1}^{m}H_{j}
on
n
qubits, where each
H_{j}
is stoquastic and
\lVert H_{j}\rVert\leq\operatorname{poly}(n)
for all
j\in[m]
, decide whether
\lambda_{\min{}}(H)\leq\alpha
or
\lambda_{min{}}(H)\geq\beta
, where
\beta-\alpha\geq 1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
.

In this work, we will focus on the stoquastic Sparse Hamiltonian, which we formulate below.

Definition 2.12

(Stoquastic Sparse Hamiltonian Problem (
\mathsf{StoqSH}_{\alpha,\beta}
))
.

For a
d
-sparse stoquastic Hamiltonian
H
on
n
qubits, where
0\preceq H\preceq I
, decide whether
\lambda_{\min{}}(H)\leq\alpha
or
\lambda_{min{}}(H)\geq\beta
, where
\beta-\alpha\geq 1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
.

2.4
Other useful lemmas

We conclude this section by stating two standard lemmas that will be used throughout the paper.

Lemma 2.13

(SWAP test for mixed states, based on
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\rho,\sigma
be two
n
-qubit quantum states. Consider the swap test applied to
\rho\otimes\sigma
. The probability that the test accepts is

\frac{1+\Tr(\rho\sigma)}{2}.

Lemma 2.14

(Lemma 22 of
[
17
]
)
.

Let
\ket{\psi},\ket{\phi}
be pure states such that
|\innerproduct{\psi}{\phi}|^{2}=1-\varepsilon
, and let
\Pi
a projector. Then,
|\expectationvalue{\Pi}{\psi}-\expectationvalue{\Pi}{\phi}|\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.

3
The Stoquastic Sparse Hamiltonian Problem is StoqMA-complete

In this section we prove the main theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

\mathsf{StoqSH}_{\alpha,\beta}\text{ is }\mathsf{StoqMA}_{a,b}\text{-complete}
, where
a-b\geq 1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
.

The
k
-local stoquastic Hamiltonian problem is already known to be
\mathsf{StoqMA}
-hard
[
7
]
, and every
k
-local Hamiltonian is a special case of a sparse Hamiltonian. Hence,
\mathsf{StoqSH}
is
\mathsf{StoqMA}
-hard. To complete the proof, it remains to show that
\mathsf{StoqSH}\in\mathsf{StoqMA}
. For that, we introduce in
Section

3.1
a generalization of
\mathsf{StoqMA}
verifiers, that does not change the computational power of the class up to an inverse polynomial decrease of the completeness/soundness gap. In
Section

3.2
, we present the generalized
\mathsf{StoqMA}
verifier for the sparse stoquastic Hamiltonians problem. We then analyze its correctness in
Section

3.3
.

Normalization and Representation Assumptions.

Throughout this section, we assume that the stoquastic sparse Hamiltonians have real entries in
[-1,0]
. We assume this without loss of generality: given the oracle that outputs the entries of a stoquastic
H
such that
0\preceq H\preceq I
, we can provide an oracle that outputs the entries of
\frac{H-I}{2}
, which only incurs a shift in the energy (for both positive and negative instances of the stoquastic sparse Hamiltonian problem).

We also assume that the absolute value of each entry can be computed exactly as
|H_{x,y}|=k/2^{\ell}
, where
k\in\{0,\dots,2^{\ell}\}
and
\ell=\operatorname{poly}(n)
is the maximum number of bit precision and that for a given
x
, we have access to an oracle
O_{H_{j}}
that returns the numerator
k
of the entry
|H_{x,f(x)}^{(j)}|
.

Non-negative states assumptions.

In the analysis, we only consider non-negative states. This can be done without loss of generality because the groundspace of stoquastic Hamiltonians is spanned by nonnegative states. Moreover, there is always a nonnegative state that achieves the maximum acceptance probability of
\mathsf{StoqMA}
verifiers.

3.1
Generalized StoqMA verifiers

We show now that the computational power of
\mathsf{StoqMA}
does not change if we allow the verifier to measure a polynomial number of qubits, at the cost of a change of the completeness and soundness parameters. We start with some definitions about the modifications in the measurement operator.

Definition 3.2

(Generalized stoquastic verifier)
.

A generalized stoquastic verifier is a stoquastic verifier, for which the acceptance probability on input string
x\in\Sigma^{n}
and witness state
\ket{\psi}
is given by
\Pr[\mathcal{V}\text{ accepts }(x,\ket{\psi})]=\expectationvalue{U^\dagger\Pi_\out U}{\psi_\inp}
, where
\Pi_{\text{out}}
projects
\ell=\operatorname{poly}(n)
qubits, the first onto the
\ket{+}
state and the rest onto some of the
\ket{+},\ket{0}\text{ or }\ket{1}
states.

Remark 3.3
.

The choice of measuring the first
\ell
qubits happens without loss of generality, since if one wanted to measure any other
\ell
qubits, they could apply SWAP gates accordingly. Notice that SWAP gates are a legal choice of gates, since each SWAP gate can be simulated with 3 CNOT gates
[
26
]
.

Definition 3.4

(
\mathsf{gStoqMA}
)
.

\mathsf{gStoqMA}_{a,b}
is the same as
\mathsf{StoqMA}_{a,b}
, except the verifier is a generalized stoquastic verifier for every input size
n
with completeness parameter
a(n)
and soundness parameter
b(n)
.

We will show that with the modification in the measurement operator we remain in
\mathsf{StoqMA}
, but with increased completeness and soundness parameters.

Theorem 3.5
.

\mathsf{StoqMA}_{a,b}\subseteq\mathsf{gStoqMA}_{a,b}\subseteq\mathsf{StoqMA}_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}
, where
a^{\prime}=\frac{1+a}{2}
and
b^{\prime}=\frac{1+b}{2}
.

Proof.

The inclusion
\mathsf{StoqMA}_{a,b}\subseteq\mathsf{gStoqMA}_{a,b}
is immediate, since every StoqMA verifier is also a
\mathsf{gStoqMA}
verifier.

For the second inclusion, let
\mathcal{V}_{g}
be a generalized stoquastic verifier that has as input the state
\ket{\psi}
(the witness state along the ancilla), and suppose that
\mathcal{V}_{g}
is measuring with respect to some fixed state, say
\ket{\phi}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{\ell}\ket{\phi_i}\in\mathbb{C}^{\otimes\ell}
, where
\ket{\phi_i}\in\{\ket{+},\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
.

We can now define a stoquastic verifier
\mathcal{V}
that constructs the state
\ket{\phi}
using
\ket{0},\ket{1}
and
\ket{+}
ancilla qubits, runs the circuit
\mathcal{V}_{g}
on
\ket{\psi}
, and then performs a SWAP test between the
\ell
output qubits measured by
\mathcal{V}_{g}
and the prepared register
\ket{\phi}
. The verifer
\mathcal{V}
accepts if and only if the SWAP test accepts.

Let
\ket{\Psi}=\mathcal{V}_{g}\ket{\psi}
. The first
\ell
qubits of
\ket{\Psi}
need not be in a pure state, since they may be entangled with the remaining qubits. We therefore denote their reduced density matrix by

\rho_{\ell}=\Tr_{\text{else}}(\outerproduct{\Psi}{\Psi}).

Then, the acceptance probability of
\mathcal{V}_{g}
can be written as follows:

\Pr[\mathcal{V}_{g}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}]=\bra{\Psi}(\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\otimes I_{\text{else}})\ket{\Psi}=\Tr(\ketbra{\phi}\rho_\ell).

Furthermore, the acceptance probability of
\mathcal{V}
is the acceptance probability of the SWAP test applied to
\rho_{\ell}
and
\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}
, so from
Section

2.4
we get

\Pr[\mathcal{V}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}]=\frac{1+\Tr(\ketbra{\phi}\rho_\ell)}{2}.

Hence,

\Pr[\mathcal{V}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}]=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Pr[\mathcal{V}_{g}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}].

∎

Remark 3.6
.

Notice that the result above holds for a polynomial number of qubits, which will enable us to perform multi-qubit measurements with stoquastic verifiers in the verification procedure of the next section.

3.2
The verification procedure

We now define the verifier
\mathcal{V}
that, with equal probability, runs one of the two verification procedures
\mathcal{V}_{1}
and
\mathcal{V}_{2}
below.

Procedure 3.7
.

Consider a stoquastic verifier
\mathcal{V}_{1}
that has access to the oracles
O_{f},O_{H_{j}}
and receives a witness state
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x}
.

1.

Pick
j\in\{1,\dots,d^{2}\}
uniformly at random.

2.

Start from the state
\ket{+}\ket{\psi}\ket{+}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}
.

3.

Use the oracle
O_{H_{j}}
to construct the state
\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{+}\ket{x}\ket{c_x}
,
where
:

\ket{c_x}=\sqrt{1-|H^{(j)}_{x,f(x)}|}\ket{\chi'_x}\ket{0}+\sqrt{|H^{(j)}_{x,f(x)}|}\ket*{\chi_x}\ket{1},

(2)

for some orthogonal states
\ket{\chi_x}
and
\ket{\chi_x'}
defined in
Eq.

6
.

4.

Use the oracle
O_{f,H_{j}}
to the state
\ket{\psi}
, controlled by the first qubit. This creates the state

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{0}\ket{x}\ket{c_x}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{1}\ket{f(x)}\ket{c_x}

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{0}\ket{x}\ket{c_x}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{f(x)}\ket{1}\ket{x}\ket{c_x}.

(4)

5.

Perform measurement of the first qubit in the
X
basis and of the last qubit in the
Z
basis, and accept if the outcomes are
\ket{+}
and
\ket{1}
respectively.

Procedure 3.8
.

Consider a stoquastic verifier
\mathcal{V}_{2}
.

1.

Pick
j\in\{1,\dots,d^{2}\}
uniformly at random.

2.

Start from state
\ket{+}\ket{\psi}\ket{+}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}
.

3.

Construct the state

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{0}\ket{x}\ket{+}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{1}\ket{x}\ket{c_x}.

(5)

4.

Perform measurement of the last qubit in the
Z
basis and accept if it is
0
.

3.3
Analysis of the verification procedure

We analyze now the acceptance probability of
\mathcal{V}_{1}
and
\mathcal{V}_{2}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

The probability that
\mathcal{V}_{1}
accepts the witness state
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x}
is

\Pr[\mathcal{V}_{1}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}]=\frac{1}{2d^{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}^{2}|H_{x,f(x)}|+\frac{1}{2d^{2}}\expectationvalue{-H}{\psi}.

Proof.

At first, we are going to restrict ourselves to a circuit for a single 1-sparse term
H_{j}
of the decomposition of
Section

2.2
.

The stoquastic verifier
\mathcal{V}_{1}
receives
\ket{\psi}
and prepares the state
\ket{+}\ket{\psi}\ket{+}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}
. For any fixed
x
of the witness, due to the assumptions we have made for the entries of
H
, if
x\in H_{j}
, suppose that
|H^{(j)}_{x,f(x)}|=k/2^{\ell}
for some
k\in\{1,\dots,2^{\ell}\}
. Notice that

\ket{x}\ket{+}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}=\frac{1}{2^{\ell/2}}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}}\ket{x}\ket{y}\ket{0}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0},

so, by applying the oracle
O_{H_{j}}
, our state becomes:

\frac{1}{2^{\ell/2}}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}}\ket{x}\ket{y}\ket{k}\ket{0}.

Using a classical reversible comparator, we can construct the state

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{\ell/2}}\sum_{y\geq k}\ket{x}\ket{y}\ket{k}\ket{0}+\frac{1}{2^{\ell/2}}\sum_{0\leq y<k}\ket{x}\ket{y}\ket{k}\ket{1},

which can be rewritten as

\sqrt{1-\frac{k}{2^{\ell}}}\ket{x}\ket{\chi_x'}\ket{0}+\sqrt{\frac{k}{2^{\ell}}}\ket{x}\ket{\chi_x}\ket{1},

where we define

\ket{\chi_x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sum_{0\leq y<k}\ket{y}\ket{k}\text{ and }\ket{\chi_x'}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{\ell}-k}}\sum_{y\geq k}\ket{y}\ket{k}.

(6)

So, we can apply this procedure coherently to get the state

\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{+}\ket{x}\ket{c_x}.

Using the oracle
O_{f,H_{j}}
conditioned on the first qubit and the procedure described in
Section

2.2
, the state becomes

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{0}\ket{x}\ket{c_x}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{1}\ket{f(x)}\ket{c_x}.

Due to the fact that
H_{j}
is 1-sparse, it holds that
f(f(x))=x
and there exists a one-to-one correspondence between each
x
and
f(x)
. Also, since
|H_{x,f(x)}|=|H_{f(x),x}|
, we have that
\ket{c_x}=\ket*{c_{f(x)}}
. Thus, the state above, which we denote as
\ket{\Phi}
, can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\ket{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{0}\ket{x}\ket{c_x}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{f(x)}\ket{1}\ket{x}\ket{c_x}.

From
Theorem

3.5
, the verifier
\mathcal{V}_{1}
can measure the first qubit with respect to
\ket{+}
and the final qubit with respect to
\ket{1}
, so the projector operator is
\Pi_{\text{acc}}=\outerproduct{+}{+}\otimes I_{\text{else}}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{1}_{\text{final}}
. We can observe that

\bra{c_x}(I_{\text{else}}\otimes\outerproduct{1}{1}_{\text{final}})\ket{c_x}=|H_{x,f(x)}^{(j)}|

and the contribution of the first qubit for a fixed
x
is equal to

\bigg\|\bra*{+}\frac{\big(\alpha_{x}\ket{0}+\alpha_{x}\ket{1}\big)}{\sqrt{2}}\bigg\|^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\big(\alpha_{x}+\alpha_{f(x)}\big)^{2},

so we get

\expectationvalue{\Pi_\acc}{\Phi}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x\in H_{j}}\big(\alpha_{x}+\alpha_{f(x)}\big)^{2}\,|H_{x,f(x)}|.

Notice that we ended up only with the terms of
x\in H_{j}
, since if
x\not\in H_{j}
,
\ket{c_x}
is at the state
\ket{0}
, and thus they do not contribute to the measurement.

Thus, the acceptance probability is

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{V}_{1}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}\mid H_{j}]

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\Pi_\acc}{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x\in H_{j}}(\alpha_{x}^{2}+\alpha_{f(x)}^{2})|H_{x,f(x)}|+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in H_{j}}\alpha_{x}\alpha_{f(x)}|H_{x,f(x)}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in H_{j}}\alpha_{x}^{2}|H_{x,f(x)}|+\frac{1}{2}\expectationvalue{-H_j}{\psi}.

where in the second equality we use the one-to-one correspondence between
x
and
f(x)
.

In the general case, the verifier
\mathcal{V}_{1}
chooses an integer
j
from
1
to
d^{2}
uniformly at random. Thus, by averaging over all
j
, the total probability of
\mathcal{V}_{1}
accepting is

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{V}_{1}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}}\frac{1}{d^{2}}\Pr[\mathcal{V}_{1}\text{ accepts}\ket{\psi}\mid H_{j}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2d^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}}\sum_{x\in H_{j}}\alpha_{x}^{2}|H_{x,f(x)}|+\frac{1}{2d^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}}\matrixelement{\psi}{-H_j}{\psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2d^{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}^{2}|H_{x,f(x)}|+\frac{1}{2d^{2}}\expectationvalue{-H}{\psi},

where in the last step we used the fact that each entry of the Hamiltonian appears only in one term of the decomposition (see
Section

2.2
).
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

The probability that
\mathcal{V}_{2}
accepts the witness state
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x}
is

\Pr[\mathcal{V}_{2}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}]=1-\frac{1}{2d^{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}^{2}|H_{x,f(x)}|.

Proof.

Again, we are going to start by restricting ourselves to the case of a
1
-sparse term
H_{j}
.

The stoquastic verifier
\mathcal{V}_{2}
receives the witness
\ket{\psi}
and prepares the state

\ket{+}\ket{\psi}\ket{+}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}.

Conditioned on the first qubit, we apply the same procedure as in
Section

3.3
to construct
\ket{c_x}
and we get the state

\ket{\Phi'}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{0}\ket{x}\ket{+}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{1}\ket{x}\ket{c_x}.

Now, we are going to measure the last qubit, so the measurement is with respect to the operator
\Pi_{\text{acc}}=I_{\text{else}}\otimes\outerproduct{0}{0}_{\text{final}}
. We can observe that

\Pi_{\text{acc}}\ket{\Phi'}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{0}\ket{x}\ket{+}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}^{\otimes\ell}\ket{0}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\sqrt{1-|H^{(j)}_{x,f(x)}|}\ket{1}\ket{x}\ket{\chi_x'}\ket{0}.

So, the acceptance probability for the
H_{j}
term will be

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{V}_{2}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}\mid H_{j}]

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\Pi_\text{acc}}{\Phi'}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}^{2}(1-|H_{x,f(x)}|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}^{2}|H^{(j)}_{x,f(x)}|.

Notice that
\sum_{x}\alpha^{2}_{x}=1
and that the second term of RHS contains only terms of
x\in H_{j}
, so the above probability can be rewritten as:

\Pr[\mathcal{V}_{2}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}\mid H_{j}]=1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in H_{j}}\alpha_{x}^{2}|H_{x,f(x)}|.

In the general case, the verifier
\mathcal{V}_{2}
chooses an integer
j
from
1
to
d^{2}
uniformly at random. Thus, by averaging over all
j
, the total probability of
\mathcal{V}_{2}
accepting is

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{V}_{2}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}}\frac{1}{d^{2}}\Pr[\mathcal{V}_{2}\text{ accepts}\ket{\psi}\mid H_{j}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in H_{j}}\alpha_{x}^{2}|H_{x,f(x)}|\Big)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2d^{2}}\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}^{2}|H_{x,f(x)}|,

which concludes the proof.
∎

Now, we go on to put everything together as follows.

Theorem 3.11
.

\mathsf{StoqSH}_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathsf{StoqMA}_{a,b}
, where
a=\frac{3}{4}+\frac{|\alpha|}{8d^{2}}
and
b=\frac{3}{4}+\frac{|\beta|}{8d^{2}}
.

Proof.

First we will show that
\mathsf{StoqSH}_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathsf{gStoqMA}_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}
for some values of

. The overall verifier
\mathcal{V}
accepts with probability

\Pr[\mathcal{V}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}]=\frac{1}{2}\Pr[\mathcal{V}_{1}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}]+\frac{1}{2}\Pr[\mathcal{V}_{2}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}].

From
Section

3.3
and
Section

3.3
we have that the total acceptance probability is

\Pr[\mathcal{V}\text{ accepts }\ket{\psi}]=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4d^{2}}\expectationvalue{H}{\psi}.

For completeness, the promise is that
\lambda_{\min{}}(H)\leq\alpha
, so there exists some state for which the verifier
\mathcal{V}
accepts with probability at least
a^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{|\alpha|}{4d^{2}}
. Similarly, for soundness we have that
\lambda_{\min{}}(H)\geq\beta
, so for all quantum states, the verifier
\mathcal{V}
accepts with probability at most
b^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{|\beta|}{4d^{2}}
. It is easy to see that the gap between the completeness and soundness parameters remains at least

. Finally, from

3.5
, we have that
\mathsf{StoqSH}_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathsf{StoqMA}_{a,b}
, where
a=\frac{1+a^{\prime}}{2}
and
b=\frac{1+b^{\prime}}{2}
.
∎

4
Separable Stoquastic Sparse Hamiltonians and StoqMA with multiple provers

In this section, we consider the stoquastic version of the Separable Sparse Hamiltonian problem and we prove that is complete for
\mathsf{StoqMA}(2)
.
In

, we introduce a new complexity class called
\mathsf{StoqMA}(k)

4.1
StoqMA with unentangled proofs

Let us first define the complexity class
\mathsf{StoqMA}(k)
.

Definition 4.1
.

A promise problem
\mathcal{L}=(\mathcal{L}_{\text{yes}},\mathcal{L}_{\text{no}})
is in
\mathsf{StoqMA}_{a,b}(k)
if there exists a uniform family of stoquastic verifiers, such that for any input

and for any fixed number of input bits

, there exist efficiently computable functions

,
b(n)
and

, and the corresponding verifier
\mathcal{V}
obeys:

: If
x\in\mathcal{L}_{\text{yes}}

Soundness
: If
x\in\mathcal{L}_{\text{no}}
, then for all
k
witnesses
\ket{\psi_1},\dots,\ket{\psi_k}
, where each witness
\ket{\psi_i}
consists of
f_{i}(n)
qubits,

\Pr[\mathcal{V}\text{ accepts }\ket{x}\otimes\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\dots\otimes\ket{\psi_k}]\leq b(n).

We can easily see that
\mathsf{StoqMA}(k)
shares some properties with
\mathsf{StoqMA}
. First, one can easily see that there is a non-negative state that maximizes the acceptance probability. And secondly, one can see that the soundness is always greater or equal than
\frac{1}{2}
.

We can also define
\mathsf{gStoqMA}(k)
as follows.

Definition 4.2

(
\mathsf{gStoqMA}(k)
)
.

\mathsf{gStoqMA}_{a,b}(k)
is the same as
\mathsf{StoqMA}_{a,b}(k)
, except the verifier is a generalized stoquastic verifier for every input size

with completeness parameter

and soundness parameter
b(n)
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Notice that
Theorem

3.5
continues to hold for
k
Merlins as well, so we have that
\mathsf{gStoqMA}_{c,s}(k)\subseteq\mathsf{StoqMA}_{c^{\prime},s^{\prime}}(k)
, for

and

.

4.2
Separable Stoquastic Sparse Hamiltonians

We now define the Separable Stoquastic Sparse Hamiltonian.

Definition 4.4
.

(Separable Stoquastic Sparse Hamiltonian Problem
(\mathsf{SepStoqSH}_{\alpha,\beta})
)
For a given Stoquastic Sparse Hamiltonian

on
n
