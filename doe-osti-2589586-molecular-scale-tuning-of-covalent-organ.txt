ABSTRACT
Covalent organic frameworks (COFs) are a type of porous, extended structure material which has
demonstrated utility in numerous applications, including sensing and separations. Judicious choice of
the linker and node not only fine tune the size of the framework, but also the functionality. We seek
to tailor COF materials for applications in gas capture and electrochemical sensing. Herein, we
demonstrate the material properties of COFs can be fine-tuned via post-synthetic metal cation
doping of the parent structures. We have synthesized several COF structures with varying linkers
which allow for the incorporation of metal cations. Structures incorporating Zn2+, Mn2+, and Cu2+
have demonstrated utility in improving the CO2 uptake properties of the COFs, while Ag+ doped
structures show potential as electrochemical sensors for chloride anions.

1. GAS CAPTURE APPLICATIONS OF COVALENT ORGANIC
 FRAMEWORKS

1.1. Background
Covalent organic frameworks (COFs) are a class of extended structure, nanoporous material which
have demonstrated utility in a wide range of applications, including molecular sensing, separations,
and catalysis. They are related to metal organic frameworks (MOFs), but instead of using metal
cations as the “node”, another organic molecule is utilized. As such, the structure of a COF is fully
held together via covalent bonds. The covalent nature of COFs results in these structures being
robust in a wide variety of environments. COF properties are generally tuned via appending
functional moieties into the backbone of the scaffold, imbuing the pores of the scaffolds with
tailored functionalities which can either bind analytes of interest, or function as a transducer to
demonstrate a property change upon addition of a stimuli of interest.
Gas capture applications have been and continue to be of widespread interest throughout Sandia
mission space. Efficient binding of gaseous guests is of paramount interest not only for applications
of gas capture and sequestration, but also for catalytic applications where efficient gas binding is
necessary to achieve efficient catalysis to value added products (i.e. CO2 catalysis to formate etc.). To
decrease the cost and energy demands for CO2 capture, separation, and conversion, targeted
materials development is needed.1 Researchers have explored materials such as zeolites,2, 3 activated
carbon4, 5 and metal—organic frameworks (MOFs)5-7 to capture the CO2, and further work has seen
the development of novel catalysts to transform the adsorbed species into useful products.8, 9 These
materials suffer from challenges such as high cost and/or environmental sensitivities10 (chemistry,
temperature, humidity, etc.). Covalent organic frameworks (COFs) are an appealing option to
mitigate the deficiencies seen in other porous materials classes. Due to the covalent nature of the
backbone structure, COFs are chemically and thermally robust, which allows for deployment in a
variety of environments.10-12 Due to their ease of synthesis, tunability, and stability COFs have
found widespread utility in applications including sensing, separations, switches, and catalysis. Of
particular interest is their gas uptake properties, which can be readily tuned for capture or
separations of specific gas species.
Throughout two decades of exploration of COFs, numerous reports have detailed approaches to
tailor their properties, either by a post-synthetic modification approach, or via bottom-up synthetic
efforts. The result has been a robust library of structures and applications on which current efforts
are based.13-15
An alternative approach to tune the properties of COFs is the addition of metal centers in the
structure, particularly for catalysis.16, 17 Metalated COFs have not only enabled catalyzing reactions
for routine organic synthesis,18, 19 but also in carbon catalysis.20 These results represent a marked step
forward in the field and there is wide agreement that further exploration is necessary to fully
understand the breadth of possible catalysis interactions and the system tunability. While the impact
on CO2 catalysis using COF frameworks has been performed, there is still little published on how
the incorporation of a metal impacts the native gas uptake capacity. However, a recent report by
Chengjun Kang et al. that demonstrated how inclusion of iron sites in an imine-based framework
resulted in a 98% increase in the CO2 uptake capacity.21 Furthermore, limited information to date
has been published on the impact of the metal binding site on the gas uptake, which is necessary for
efficient catalysis.

1.2. Methods

Instrumentation. Powder X-ray diffractions (PXRD) patterns were collected on either an X or a Bruker
D2 Phaser with LYNXEYE XE-T detector and 30 kV Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at room
temperature. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were taken on a Kratos AXIS
Supra operated at a base pressure better than 5x10-9 Torr, using a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray
source (1486.6 eV). Samples were packed densely onto conductive carbon tape. Surveys and high
resolution for elements of interest were taken on each sample. Spectra were obtained using a large
analyzer spot size and a pass energy of 160 eV for all surveys and 20 eV for the high-resolution
elements, with a step size of 0.1 eV. Charge compensation was done using a charge neutralizer with
a filament current of 0.42 A. Spectra were fit using CasaXPS with Shirley backgrounds and corrected
to sp3 C at 285 eV. Both nitrogen (N2) and carbon dioxide (CO2) gas adsorption isotherms were
obtained using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020, at 77 K and 273 K respectively. All COF samples were
activated at 120°C for 8 h before analysis.

Materials. Benzene-1,3,5-tricarbaldehyde and 4,4'-diamino-[1,1-biphenyl]-3,3'-diol were purchased
from Ambeed. Copper (II) acetate, manganese (II) acetate, zinc acetate dihydrate, and p-
phenylenediamine, was purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Solvents were purchased from Sigma
Aldrich. All materials were used without further purification.

Synthesis of 1. To a 50 mL round bottom flask, benzene-1,3,5-tricarbaldehyde (208 mg, 1.28 mmol)
was added and dissolved in 8.75 M acetic acid (8 mL). In a 20 mL vial, p-phenylenediamine (208 mg,
1.92 mmol) was dissolved in 8 mL DI water. Both solutions were stirred for 30 minutes at room
temperature before the p-phenylene diamine solution was added to the aldehyde mixture dropwise.
The reaction was left stirring at room temperature for 4 days. The solid was then washed with water,
acetone, and dichloromethane (2x20 mL each) and dried under vacuum to yield a yellow powder
(280.4 mg).

Synthesis of 2. To a 100 mL round bottom flask, benzene-1,3,5-tricarbaldehyde (375 mg, 2.31 mmol)
was added and dissolved in 8.75 M acetic acid (14.4 mL). In a 20 mL vial, 4,4'-diamino-[1,1-
biphenyl]-3,3'-diol (500 mg, 2.31 mmol) was dissolved in 14.4 mL DI water. Both solutions were
stirred for 30 minutes at room temperature before the 4,4'-diamino-[1,1-biphenyl]-3,3'-diol solution
was added to the aldehyde suspension dropwise. The reaction was left stirring at room temperature
for 4 days. The solid was then washed with water, acetone, and dichloromethane (2x20 mL each)
and dried under vacuum to yield a yellow powder (678.4 mg).

Cation Complexation. COF (1 or 2 100 mg) and acetate salt (25 mg) were combined in a 50 mL round
bottom flask. Ethanol (25 mL) was added to the round bottom, and the suspension was stirred at
60°C overnight. The product was cooled to room temperature before being filtered and washed with
ethanol (2x10 mL). The solid was dried under vacuum.

Computational Methods

Gas-phase electronic structure calculations were performed on molecules that represent the
extended COF structure to evaluate the impact of metal adsorption on CO2 uptake. Structural
relaxations were performed at the density functional theory (DFT) level in the Gaussian16 code 22.
Carbon atoms were frozen to limit unphysical twisting. For the optimization, the hybrid B3LYP
functional (Becke’s three parameter hybrid exchange functional with the Lee–Yan–Parr correlation
function) was used 23-25. Metal cations Cu2+, Mn2+, and Zn2+ were simulated with the
LAN2DZeffective core basis set 26, 27 that includes scalar relativistic corrections and all other atoms
were described using the 6-31G(d,p) basis set 24, 28, 29. This level of theory has been successfully
applied to previous investigations of cluster models including transition metals and f-electrons 30, 31.
The lowest spin state was used for all calculations and all simulations were unpolarized due to the
incorporation of adsorbed metal ions. A natural bond order (NBO) analysis was performed for
calculation of partial atomic charges 32-34.
Results and Discussion
Herein, we have synthesized two imine-based COF structures, with and without an added phenol
moiety to promote “in pore” binding of metals, termed COF 1 and 2 respectively (Figure 1). The
incorporated metals are predicted to facilitate interactions between gas molecules and the COF
framework. We have demonstrated the synthesis of imine-based COFs that have been doped with
Cu2+, Mn2+, and Zn2+ as their acetate salts, and characterized the resultant gas uptake properties.
While the metal@COF structures resulting from complexation of 2 proved to consistently increase
the CO2 uptake capacity of the system, metal@COF structures without a traditional metal binding
site resulted in mixed uptake efficiencies. Interestingly Cu@1 displayed the highest observed CO2
uptake, despite not having the confirmed “in pore” metal binding present in the metal@2 structures
highlighting the intricacies in the structure-property relationships of these porous materials.

Initial synthesis of the desired COFs (1,2) (Figure 1) was performed by stirring 1,3,5-benzene
tricarbaldehyde with the desired diamine precursor in aqueous acetic acid at room temperature for
three days. The resultant yellow powders were isolated by centrifugation and washed with large
amounts of water, acetone, and dichloromethane before being dried under vacuum at 60°C
overnight. The resultant structures displayed PXRD patterns that match previously published
structures.35, 36 Subsequent metal complexation was accomplished by suspending a solution of each
COF with Zn(OAc)2, Mn(OAc)2, or Cu(OAc)2 in ethanol and stirring the mixture for 12 hours at
60°C, followed by washing the resultant powder with ethanol to remove uncomplexed metal salts.
Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) was used to evaluate the
extent of metal species incorporated into the framework (Table 1). The metal binding sites present
in 2 increased the affinity of the framework for metal cations substantially. While metal
incorporation for 1 was less than 1% loading by mass in all cases, inclusion of a phenol moiety (a
metal binding site) into the framework allowed for metal loading as high as 7.4% in the case of
copper (II).

In all metal@COF structures evaluated, the structure of the parent COF is maintained after metal
binding, as indicated by the similarities between the PXRD and Fourier Transform Infrared
Spectroscopy (FT-IR) spectra of the pristine and complexed materials. The robust low angle peak

complexation of the metal cations in the COF frameworks. Similarly, lack of spectroscopic change

Despite lowered surface areas of M-2 COFs , increased in the CO2 uptake were observed compared
to the non-metalated 2 structure (Figures 9, 10). The CO2 uptake of M@2 species was all of higher
magnitude than the undoped 2 structure, despite lowered BET surface area. Direct comparison of
the samples indicate 2<Zn-2<Mn-2<Cu-2, of CO2 quantity adsorbed with increasing pressure, with
Cu@2 displaying a CO2 uptake of 1.6 mmol/g at 1 bar of CO2. Cu@2 exhibits a 33% increase in
uptake capacity compared to undoped 2. This result highlights that while the metal cations do
functionalize the pore, possibly blocking the pore and decreasing porosity, the metal centers still
provide a binding site for the gas molecules to adsorb. This results in a net increase in the overall
CO2 uptake.

Results are included in Table 3. Overall, metal loading of the COF introduces a more
thermodynamically favorable binding site, with CO2 binding energies between -57 to -91 kJ/mol for
1 and -49 to -80 kJ/mol for 2. These reaction free energies are 2-4x higher than those for CO2

The low metalation of 1 resulted in few available metal sites for stronger CO2 adsorption (Table 4),
despite the substantially higher ΔG value for CO2 binding to these sites (Table 3). Still, the overall
effect is an increase in the CO2 isotherms. Conversely, in 2 the higher concentration of metals results
in a wider variety of binding sites and an increase in average ΔG for CO2 binding.

The average CO2 ΔG for 2 aligns well with the adsorbed CO2 concentration at 1000 mbar (Figures
9, 10), indicating that the incorporation of the metal is impacting gas adsorption, which trends with
both the metal loading and the associated increase in reaction free energies. The trend for 1 is less
clear, with a similar variation in CO2 absorption but a narrower distribution of average CO2 ΔG
values. These results suggest that the variation on CO2 absorption seen in the experimental data for
1 is not driven by direct CO2–linker or CO2–metal interaction, the mechanism that is evaluated via
DFT. Instead, a difference in CO2 adsorption is likely caused by changes in the extended crystal
structure from a decrease in pore volume, blocking of internal porosity, or the formation of metal
nanoparticles rather than absorption of single metal cations that may be the driving force for CO2
absorption in 1.

We have demonstrated that doping COF structures with various metal cations has a dramatic impact
on the gas adsorption properties of the resultant complex structures. Incorporation of a traditional
binding site within the COF allows for more cations to be bound within the structure, as indicated
by ICP-OES, and results in a decrease in the BET surface area of the structure due to guest
inclusion within the pore.
Interestingly, the decrease in surface area does not result in a decrease in the CO2 uptake capacity,
but rather an increased CO2 uptake is observed. Data suggests this is due to the cations within the
COF providing robust binding sites which are available for interactions with gaseous guests. By
contrast, complexes formed without this binding site provide less predictable gas binding
efficiencies, highlighting the intricacies in the structure that are controlling the gas adsorption
properties. The results indicate that the gas uptake in COF structures can be tuned by incorporation
of metal cations, with potential for catalytic activity.

2. ELECTROCHEMICAL SENSING APPLICATIONS

2.1. Background
The second area we will investigate tailoring is the electrical conductivity of the COFs. Conductive,
organic materials are of broad interest due to their utility in applications including switches, sensors,
and memory storage devices.39-42 Again, COFs offer an attractive platform for tuning these materials
due to their synthetic variety and modifiable functional groups. Electroactive motifs such as
porphyrin, pyrene, and tetrathiafulvalene can be readily built into the backbone of the COF scaffold
to alter the material’s electronic properties.43-47 There are multitudes of examples of COFs being
used as electrochemical sensors, not only for small molecules, but for gasses of interest as well. The
electrochemical properties of these structures also make them intriguing systems for catalytic
applications. As such, their use has become increasingly popular in applications such as single atom
catalysis.17 Interested in the properties imbued by metal doping in COFs, we explored a bipyridine-
containing COF (3) as a scaffold for metal coordination and electrochemical applications, both
sensing and catalysis. 3 was chosen as our base scaffold as it contains bipyridine subunits, which are
readily complexed with a wide variety of metal cations. This particular structure is also known to
have electrochemical responses upon metal coordination to the bipyridine subunits.48 As such, we
hypothesized that this material could be complexed with silver cations and used as an
electrochemical sensor for chlorine anions. AgCl sensors for anions are a known system, which is
reportedly highly selective for chloride in aqueous media,49, 50 and the technology has been matured
for industrial use in geosciences.51 However, this phenomenon has not yet been documented in a
COF scaffold, and we hypothesize the COF backbone will allow for increases in sensitivity and
reproducibility of the developed sensor systems.

2.2. Methods
Materials: The following chemicals were used as received without further purification: Phosphate
buffer solution (PBS) was made from potassium phosphate dibasic (K2HPO4, ACS Reagent
>99.0%, Sigma Aldrich) and potassium phosphate monobasic (KH2PO4, ACS Reagent >99.0%,
Sigma Aldrich) and had a pH of 7, Nafion (5 wt% Nafion/isopropanol, Fuel Cell Store), IPA
(99.7%, Sigma Aldrich), Ethanol (200 proof, Pharmco-Aaper). The solutions were purged and
blanketed with either argon (Ar, 99.999%, Matheson Tri-Gas, Inc.) or carbon dioxide (O2, 99.9%,
Matheson Tri-Gas, Inc.).All aqueous solutions were made in 18.2 MΩ·cm DI water (Millipore Milli-
Q systems).

Synthesis of 3. 50.0 mg of 2,4,6-Trihydroxybenzene-1,3,5-tricarbaldehyde (T) added to 15 mL 8.75 M
Acetic acid (AcOH). In a separate vessel, 66.48 mg of [2,2’-Bipyridine]-5,5’-diamine (DP) was added
to 15 mL DI water. Both solutions were stirred at room temperature for 30 minutes. The DP
solution was then added dropwise to the T solution and stirred for 3 days at 120 °C. This was then
cooled to room temperature and washed thoroughly with DI water, then with ethanol. Product was
vacuum dried overnight. Average yield was 100.73 mg of purple-brown powder. Product was
characterized via X-ray diffraction and FTIR.

 23

Cation Complexation. 50.0 mg of OH DP COF was added to 12.5 mg Silver acetate and then dissolved
in 12.5 mL of ethanol. This reaction was stirred at 60°C over night. It was then filtered, washed with
ethanol, and vacuum dried. Average yield was 58.3 mg. Product was a dark red/brown coarse
powder. Product was characterized via X-ray diffraction and FTIR.

Salt washes. In total, four washes were done with different ammonium salts. These salts were
ammonium chloride, ammonium nitrate, ammonium acetate, and ammonium sulfate. Each salt wash
was made to a 100 mM solution. 50mg of COF 3 was stirred in the salt solution for two hours at
room temperature. The material was then filtered, and washed with copious amounts of DI water
followed by ethanol, then dried under vacuum.

Impedance testing on IDE: Sensors were fabricated by drop-casting Cu(cyhdc) powder onto
interdigitated electrodes (IDEs) which consisted of Pt electrodes with a 10 um spacing between the
interdigitated electrodes with an active are of 35 mm2 (Metrohm DropSens). In a 10 mL glass vial,
COF powder (25 mg) and ethanol (1 mL) were added and stirred vigorously for 1 h. Drops of 12.5
μL of the COF suspension was then pipetted onto the active area of the IDE. The IDE was allowed
to dry at room temperature for 5 min, followed by additional depositions of 12.5 μL to uniformly
coat the electrodes. Impedance spectra were simultaneously recorded using a Solartron Modulab
with both femtoammeter and reference measurement cards. All measurements utilized the internal
reference capacitors to increase measurement accuracy. The AC waveform was applied at 0 V DC
with a 100mV RMS AC waveform over a range of frequencies (1 MHz to 10 mHz) with 10
points/decade and the resulting impedance response measured.

OER and ORR Electrocatalysis: Experimental Cyclic Voltammetry (CV) scans were taken using a 3 mm
diameter glassy carbon electrode (BASi Research) which had the COF catalyst ink deposited onto it.
Prior to catalyst deposition, the electrode was hand polished on SiC grit paper (1200 grit), rinsed,
and sonicated in water prior to use. Catalyst ink and deposition were taken from the reference paper
Chem. Mater. 2016, 28, 4375−4379 and slightly modified where a conductive carbon (Vulcan
Carbon XC-72) was used. The COF powder was ground using a mortar and pestle to create a fine
powder and 9 mg COF and 1 mg Vulcan Carbon XC-72 were combined in 0.6 mL of IPA and 0.4
mL of DI H2O. Then the COF ink was ultrasonicated for 30 minutes to fully disperse the COF in
the catalyst ink. On the polished and cleaned glassy carbon electrode, 2.5 µL of the COF ink was
deposited and let air dry for a total of 4x (10 µL total deposited). Then, 2.5 µL of 0.5 wt% Nafion
(diluted down from the stock Nafion solution) in IPA was deposited and let air dry for a total of 2x
(5 µL total deposited). The glassy carbon electrode was placed upright in an oven at 60 °C for 1 hour
to fully dry the deposited ink before electrochemical testing. For electrochemical testing, a 3-
electrode system was used with a graphite rod counter electrode and a commercial Ag/AgCl (BASi
Research) reference electrode. The electrochemical tests were performed using a Gamry Reference
620 potentiostat.

2.3. Results and Discussion
Successful synthesis of covalent organic framework 3 (Figure 13) was proved via powder X-ray
diffraction (PXRD, Figure 13). The low angle peak, is consistent with previously reported literature.
Excitedly, metal coordination with 3 did not impact the overall structure of the framework, as noted

 24

by the lack of significant shifting in the PXRD pattern (Figure 13). The Ag-3 sample was
subsequently washed with 100 mM ammonium chloride solution (aqueous) to assess the propensity
of the system to act as an electrochemical sensor for anions in solution.

 Figure 13. COF 3 which was evaluated as an electrochemical sensor system, PXRD analysis of
 COF 3, and its metal complexed analogs.

The Covalent Organic Frameworks (COF) samples were evaluated by measuring the impedance
response of the material through Electrical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) testing. The as made
COFs were modified using different metal ions which could change their electrical properties. To
measure the properties, the different COFs were suspended in an ethanol solution and drop cast
onto some bare InterDigitated Electrodes (IDEs).

  Figure 14. (A) photograph of a Pt IDE with the active area magnified to show the interdigitated
 electrodes. (B) Photograph of an IDE that has the sample COF dropcast onto the active area for
 EIS testing.

The IDEs have separate Pt electrodes that are located on the glass substrate with the active area
located at the bottom of the IDE where the Pt lines are separated by a 10 um spacing, as seen in
Figure 14A. The bare IDEs have a very high impedance because the Pt electrodes are not physically
connected but are located on an insulating glass substrate. Once the COF is dropcast onto the IDE,
the COF material will create a new connection between the Pt electrodes and allow the
measurement of the materials impedance response.

 25

After the COFs were dropcast onto the IDEs, the impedance response as a function of frequency
could be measured from an applied AC waveform. The tested COFs, include the as made COF
(COF 3), a Ag modified COF (Ag COF 3), a Co modified version (Co COF 3), and a different COF
that was initially modified with Ag ions, but was then washed with a NaCl containing solution (Ag
COF 3 (Cl wash)). The washing of the Ag modified COF with the Cl- containing solution should
then pull the Ag out of the COF (as highly insoluble AgCl). The Ag modified COF could be a
candidate for a Cl- sensing material, whereas the Cl- affects the Ag ions in the COF, we should
expect a change in impedance. Figure 15 shows the resulting Bode plots from the different COFs.
The Bode plots shows the impedance magnitude as a function of the AC frequency. All the COFs
showed very high impedance values, which was expected from testing other similar materials (e.g.
MOFs) that are used in sensing applications and typically also have high impedance responses.

At high frequencies, the bare IDEs and COF loaded IDEs all show a linear increase in the measured
impedance with decreasing frequency. However, at lower frequencies, the impedance begins to
show a deviation from the linear response for the COF loaded IDEs, but the bare IDEs still
increase in a linear fashion. The different COFs showed different responses at the lowest
frequencies, with some only slightly deviating from the bare IDE response, but other showing more
significant deviations.

  Figure 15. EIS Bode plots of the bare IDEs with the different COFs dropcast onto the IDES. (A)
 COF 3, (B) Ag COF 3, (C) Co COF 3, (D) Ag COF 3 Cl Wash.

The impedance was observed to decrease for all the COFs, as compared to the bare IDE. The
impedance value, taken from the 0.1 Hz frequency, for each sample is as summarized in Table 5.

 26

Surprisingly, the as made COF (no metal ion modification) showed the lowest impedance value at
0.1 Hz with 3.11 GOhm impedance. The Ag and Co modified COFs had slightly higher impedance
values at the same 0.1 Hz frequency with 7.69 and 6.43 GOhms, respectively. Possibly, this is due
to the metal ions decreasing some electron mobility within the COF as they bond to the COF.
However, the Cl- washed Ag COF had the highest impedance value of all the COFs. It is not clear
why the impedance would increase unless the Cl- washing was removing the Ag ions and forming
resistive AgCl, which is not effectively removed from the COF and this leads to a measured increase
in the impedance.

COF Electrocatalysis Evaluation for OER and ORR

The electrocatalytic activity of metal modified COFs was demonstrated in recent literature for a Co
modified COF.48 This was done for the Oxygen Evolution Reaction (OER) where water is oxidized

 28

at the catalyst surface forming molecular oxygen (O2). This is an important Electrocatalytic reaction
that has many different use cases and applications. Typically, catalysts utilize expensive platinum
group metals, such as Ru and Ir, to efficiently drive this reaction at lower potentials. Besides the
OER, another electrocatalytic reaction was investigated. This was the Oxygen Reduction Reaction
(ORR), that involves electrochemically reducing O2 to H2O (with the reaction intermediate H2O2 as
a potential side product). This reaction is important for fuel cells and metal-air battery systems.

These electrocatalytic reactions were tested by depositing the COFs onto inert Glassy Carbon (GC)
substrate electrodes. Inert GC shows no activity for OER and ORR at the potentials that are being
tested and as such means any observed currents are from the metal modified COF materials
catalyzing the reactions of interest. The COFs were deposited onto the GC by making a catalyst ink
from the COFs and a conductive carbon material. This was needed as the COFs showed very high
resistivities from the initial EIS analysis and the conductive carbon would be needed to aid in the
transport of electrons to and from the metal modified COF catalyst. The use of a Nafion polymer
binder (standard for electrocatalyst testing) would keep the catalyst bound to the electrode surface
but is also a proton conducting polymer that aids in transport of the protons to and from the
catalyst surface. The metal modified COF catalyst activities were evaluated in a 0.1 M phosphate
buffer solution (PBS) electrolyte (with pH 7) which would also buffer against any substantial p
changes during testing. Depending on the tests, Ar and O2 were used to purge the electrolyte and
saturate it with either gas that was being bubbled.

 Figure 18. Experimental CVs for the tested COFs on GC electrodes showing the OER oxidation
activities. OER tested in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7) and CV scans were 50 mV/s using a Ag/AgCl reference
 electrode and a graphite rod counter electrode.

The Co and Ag modified COFs showed promising initial results with a substantially increased
current, attributed to the OER, as compared to the as made un-modified COF 3, which showed very
little oxidation current even up at the 2.1 V (RHE) potential. These overlaid OER CVs are shown
in Figure 18. The Co modified COF showed the largest oxidation currents and also had an onset
potential that was shifted to lower potentials compared to the Ag modified COF, indicating that it
had a higher activity for OER than the Ag modified COF.

 29

With further cycling of the COFs, the currents increased for both the Co and Ag modified COFs.
The Ag COF even showed a much larger current density that was nearly on par with the Co
modified COF. This can be seen in Figure 19A. However, the Co modified COF still had a more
favorable onset potential with it being ~200 mV lower than the Ag COF. An interesting behavior
of the Ag modified COF that became apparent with continued cycling and electrochemical testing
was some new oxidation and reduction peaks that appeared in the CV scan potential window.
These peaks were located centered around 150-200 mV (vs Ag/AgCl), as can be seen in Figure 19B.
This could be indicative of the reduction and oxidation of the Ag ions within the COF. If this is
occurring, Ag nanopartciels could be forming outside of the COF structure due to the continued
testing, leading to degradation of the catalyst. The presence of Ag nanoparticles may be why the
OER currents were higher and more similar to the Co-modified COF.

 Figure 19. Experimental OER CVs from the unmodified and Co and Ag COFs. The CVs show the
 resulting current densities in Ar and O2 purged 0.1 M PBS (pH 7) and were run at 50 mV/s with a
 graphite counter electrode and a commercial Ag/AgCl reference electrode.

Besides OER testing, which was already demonstrated in literature for the Co-modified COF,
further testing of these COFs for the ORR was done. All of the COFs, even the un-modified COF,
showed some activity for the ORR. However, the Ag and Co modified COFs showed ~3x and ~4-
5x increase in ORR currents compared to the unmodified COF ORR current. The COFs in the Ar
purged electrolyte showed little to no current where O2 purged electrolyte showed larger reduction
currents corresponding to the ORR. This confirms the reduction currents observed on the COFs
were indeed due to the ORR. These preliminary studies showed that the COFs were ORR active
catalysts, but more work would need to be done to determine their true activity and stability. These
COFs might be good ORR catalysts and the metal ion compositions could be tailored to be better
for ORR.

 30

Figure 20. Experimental ORR CVs from the (A) as made un-modified COF-DP, (B) Co modified COF
 and (C) Ag modified COF. The CVs show the resulting ORR reduction current densities in Ar and
 O2 purged 0.1 M PBS (pH 7) and were run at 50 mV/s with a graphite counter electrode and a
 commercial Ag/AgCl reference electrode.

These initial results were very promising and showed that the different metal modified COFs could
function as effective OER and ORR electrocatalysts, while avoiding the use of platinum group
metals and using only a very small mass loading of metals. These catalysts may be idealized as single
atom electrocatalysts and promise a path forward to inexpensive electrocatalysts that utilize
abundant transition metal elements and in very small amounts. Further work would be useful to
fully characterize and evaluate these catalysts.
