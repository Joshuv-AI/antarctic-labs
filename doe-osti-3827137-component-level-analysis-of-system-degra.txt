 ABSTRACT
Electric-grid resilience is difficult to quantify because system performance depends on network topology,
component reliability, operating configuration, and restoration time. This project produced a scalable
component-level framework for characterizing and quantifying resilience at grid interconnection points.
The framework converts standard power-system planning models to undirected network graphs and
uses iterative minimum-cut calculations to identify branch combinations capable of disconnecting
each location. Prescribed component failure rates and restoration times are associated with the graph
edges and aggregated across the identified cut sets to calculate nodal availability and unavailability.
A separate connectivity-loss measure records how frequently each node is disconnected across the
enumerated cuts and provides a topology-based indicator independent of outage probability. The
method was verified on the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) 14- and 57-bus test
systems, and the software installation was extended to Eastern Interconnection subsystems. Results show
that expected service continuity varies by location according to source-path redundancy, component
performance, and restoration assumptions. The resulting approach provides an interpretable measure
of expected grid performance and risk to support identification of vulnerable interconnection points,
comparison of infrastructure alternatives, prioritization of component hardening, and resilience-informed
decision-making.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Reliable electric service depends not only on the aggregate performance of the bulk power system but also
on whether individual loads, substations, and interconnection points retain access to available generation
when grid elements or components are out of service. Conventional reliability indexes provide valuable
system-level information, but they do not necessarily show how expected service continuity varies from
one network location to another or identify the specific component combinations that can isolate a
particular location. A nodal availability measure addresses this need by estimating the fraction of time
that a bus or interconnection point remains connected to at least one available source (Billinton and Allan
1996; IEEE 2022).
Availability and reliability are related but distinct concepts. Reliability describes the probability that a
component or system performs its required function for a specified interval without failure. Availability
also accounts for restoration and is commonly expressed using the mean time between failures (MTBF)
and the mean time to restore service (MTTR). For an interconnection point, restoration means restoration
of service; it need not require physical repair of the failed component if switching, isolation, or an alternate
transmission path restores connectivity. This distinction makes availability especially useful for evaluating
network design and redundancy (Rausand and Høyland 2004; IEEE 2007).
Calculating nodal availability in a meshed transmission network is more difficult than evaluating a
conventional series or parallel system. A node may be supplied through several overlapping paths, and
different combinations of line and transformer outages may disconnect it from all generation. Manually
constructing a reliability block diagram or fault tree for every node becomes impractical for a model
containing thousands of buses and branches. Exhaustive enumeration is also computationally expensive
because the number of possible multiple-component outage combinations grows rapidly with system size
(Rausand and Høyland 2004; Billinton and Allan 1996).
Graph-based analysis provides a scalable alternative. A power-system model can be represented as a
graph in which buses, generators, and loads are nodes and transmission lines and transformers are edges.
Generator nodes are connected to a common super-source, allowing standard source-to-node minimum-cut

algorithms to identify combinations of branches that can interrupt service to each location. The resulting
cut sets can be combined with prescribed component failure rates and restoration times to calculate nodal
availability and unavailability. A related connectivity-loss measure records how frequently a node becomes
disconnected across the enumerated cut sets and, therefore, provides a topology-based indicator that is
independent of component outage probability (Biswas et al. 2021; Goldberg and Tarjan 1988).
The Component-Level Analysis of System Degradation, Restoration, and Resilience project developed an
automated implementation of this approach. The work included conversion of standard planning models to
network graphs, enumeration of source-to-node cut sets, treatment of parallel paths, association of graph
edges with component failure information, computation of node-level availability and connectivity-loss
measures, and a software architecture supporting parallel execution. The software was verified using
the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) 14- and 57-bus test systems and extended to
Eastern Interconnection subsystems.
The completed capability is a design-basis, topology-specific planning tool that uses prescribed component
performance and restoration data. The method evaluates source connectivity under modeled component
outages. The results are intended to complement conventional electrical studies by providing a traceable,
location-specific screening tool to identify vulnerable interconnection points and the components that most
strongly influence their expected service continuity.

individual interconnection points (each bus of a network). A node is treated as available when at least one
functioning path connects it to an available source of generation. The implemented workflow combines
network topology with prescribed component failure rates and restoration times to identify disconnecting
element combinations and estimate location-specific availability, unavailability, and additional metrics.

2.3 CONVERSION OF POWER-SYSTEM MODELS TO NETWORK GRAPHS
The workflow imports a standard power-system planning model, including models in the Power System
Simulator for Engineering (PSS® E) raw data (RAW) format, and converts it to an undirected, weighted
graph 𝐺 = (𝑉, 𝐸). Buses, generators, and loads are represented as vertices, and transmission lines and
transformers are represented as edges. The electrical attributes and identifiers associated with each branch
are retained so that a graph result can be traced to the corresponding physical component.
All generator vertices are connected to a common super-generator vertex. Bus interconnection vertices
are retained as candidate destinations. Internal transmission edges are assigned a unit topological weight,
whereas artificial connections to the super-generator and terminal loads receive a large weight. This
weighting prevents an artificial source or terminal connection from becoming the preferred cut and
focuses the calculation on transmission paths within the modeled system. Parallel branches are represented
separately so that distinct physical failure opportunities are preserved (Biswas et al. 2021).
Figure 2 illustrates the conversion for a small example network. Generator buses are connected to the
common super-generator, terminal loads are only retained in node metadata, and each physical line or
transformer is retained as a separate graph edge.

One minimum-cut calculation returns one cut set, although several cut sets may have the same cut value.
Therefore, the project developed an iterative weighting procedure to enumerate alternate sets. After a

 5

set is identified, the weights of its edges are incremented by a small amount, and the minimum cut is
recalculated. For a target cut value 𝑚, the increment is selected so that previously identified sets are
temporarily displaced without moving the search immediately to the next integer cut value. The procedure
continues until the minimum-cut value advances.
A second iteration raises previously identified cut edges to a large weight and searches for cuts at the next
value. The resulting enumeration is complete for the lowest cut value and for the first higher grouping that
does not depend on previously excluded lower-cut edges. At still higher cut values, some valid sets can
overlap lower-order sets and may not be returned. The implemented method therefore provides complete
lowest-order cut sets and a progressively partial representation of higher-order cut sets; this limitation must
be considered when interpreting the calculated failure space.

2.8 SOFTWARE IMPLEMENTATION AND APPLICATIONS
The software workflow imports a network model, constructs the graph, generates source-to-node cut sets,
computes connectivity-loss measures, associates edges with prescribed component data, and calculates
nodal availability and unavailability. The project refined the node and edge scoring, improved the treatment
of parallel paths, incorporated component failure modes, and documented and tested the scripts.
Node-specific searches and cut-set evaluations are naturally parallel because many source-to-node and
component-state calculations are independent. The implementation was therefore structured to support
parallel execution. It was verified using the IEEE 14- and 57-bus test systems, and the software installation
was extended to Eastern Interconnection subsystems. The available test-system results are summarized in
Section 3.
The framework remains complementary to conventional power-system studies. Its primary calculation is
probabilistic source connectivity. It does not determine by itself whether a connected network satisfies
thermal limits, power balance, voltage constraints, stability requirements, or protection criteria.

 3. NODAL AVAILABILITY METRICS AND RESULTS
The project produced two closely related node-level results. Nodal availability and unavailability combine
topology with prescribed component failure and restoration data. Connectivity loss records the number or
fraction of enumerated cut sets that disconnect a node from the common generation source. The first is a
probability-based service-continuity estimate; the second is a topology-based screening indicator. They
should be interpreted together as aspects of resilience, but not used interchangeably.

3.1 INTERPRETATION OF THE METRICS
A high value of 𝐴𝑖 , or an equivalently large number of nines, indicates low modeled unavailability at
node 𝑖. A low connectivity-loss value indicates that the node remains connected to generation under most
of the enumerated cut sets. These two indicators can differ because cut sets have different probabilities
of occurrence. A node may be disconnected by several very unlikely cut sets or by a small number of
comparatively likely component combinations.
The joint interpretation is useful for planning. Nodes with high availability and low connectivity loss
have both favorable modeled component performance and strong topological redundancy, respectively.
Nodes with low availability and high connectivity loss are candidates for more detailed study. Mixed cases
help distinguish whether a result is driven primarily by network structure or by the prescribed failure and
restoration parameters.

3.2 VERIFICATION ON THE IEEE 14-BUS SYSTEM
The availability algorithms were verified on the IEEE 14-bus test system (Texas A&M University
Electric Grid Test Case Repository, n.d.). The model was converted to the undirected graph described in
Section 2.3, source-to-node cut sets were enumerated, and the resulting component unavailabilities were
aggregated for each bus. Table 1 reports the five buses with the lowest availability.

3.4 PLANNING USE AND LIMITATIONS
The results support three related planning decisions. First, nodal availability identifies interconnection
points with comparatively weak expected service continuity. Second, connectivity loss identifies locations
that are topologically exposed across the enumerated cut sets. Third, the underlying edge sets retain the
identities of the lines and transformers whose joint outages disconnect each node. A planner can therefore
examine the cause of a low node-level result, compare alternative network configurations, and screen
candidate hardening or redundancy improvements.
The numerical results remain conditional on the source model and assumptions. Component unavailability
depends on failure-rate, line-length, restoration, and independence assumptions. The iterative algorithm
may omit overlapping higher-order cuts, and direct summation may overcount overlapping outage events
(Rausand and Høyland 2004). Connectivity-loss fractions depend on the selected enumeration scope
and do not include event probability. Finally, maintained graph connectivity does not establish that the
remaining electrical network can serve all load within thermal, voltage, stability, and protection limits.
These limitations make the method a transparent screening and planning tool rather than a replacement
for detailed reliability and power-system studies.

 4. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
This project developed and demonstrated a scalable method for relating transmission-network topology
and prescribed component performance to the expected availability of service at individual buses and
interconnection points. The software converts standard power-system models to graphs, connects
modeled generators to a common source, enumerates source-to-node cut sets, and combines those
sets with component failure and restoration data. The resulting nodal availability, unavailability, and
connectivity-loss measures retain the identities of affected nodes and contributing branches, providing
information that is not available from a single system-wide index.
Verification on the IEEE 14-bus system showed that peripheral nodes with fewer independent source paths
can have lower modeled availability and greater exposure across enumerated cut sets. The additional
IEEE 57-bus demonstration showed that the method can evaluate connectivity across an enumerated set
of network cuts. The software installation was also extended to Eastern Interconnection subsystems, and its
architecture supports parallel execution of independent node and cut-set calculations.
The framework is intended to inform design-basis planning, vulnerability screening, and component-hardening
assessments. It can help identify interconnection points with comparatively low expected service
continuity, trace those results to consequential component combinations, and compare proposed changes
in topology or component performance. Its outputs are conditional on the adopted network, failure-rate,
restoration, independence, and cut-enumeration assumptions. Connectivity alone also does not establish
thermal adequacy, voltage performance, stability, protection performance, or feasibility of serving all
connected load.
Future work should emphasize utility validation of component reliability and restoration data, probability
treatment for overlapping cut sets, more complete enumeration of higher-order cuts, and sensitivity
analysis for uncertain input parameters. Integration with conventional electrical studies would allow
topologically connected outage states to be evaluated for power-transfer and security constraints. Additional
software parallelization and data-quality diagnostics would support routine application to large interconnection
models.
