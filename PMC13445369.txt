CLCF1–ZIC5–CNTFR feedback loop drives cisplatin resistance in cervical squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Qingge Jia, Mingyang Li, Jin Liu, Tianqi Xu, Junpeng Xu, Yuan Yuan, Fang Sui, Xinyuan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•ZIC5 is upregulated in CDDP-resistant cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CESC) and correlates with poor patient survival.•ZIC5 transcriptionally activates CNTFR, while CLCF1-CNTFR-STAT3 signaling induces ZIC5, forming a positive feedback loop that drives CDDP resistance and cancer stem cell properties.•Blocking CNTFR with a neutralizing antibody disrupts this loop and synergizes with cisplatin to suppress tumor growth in CESC PDX and organoid models.Keywords: Cervical squamous cell carcinoma, Cisplatin resistance, CLCF1, ZIC5, CNTFR

Introduction

Cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CESC) remains the fourth most common malignancy among women worldwide, with an estimated 604,000 new cases and 342,000 deaths reported annually [1,2]. Despite substantial advances in screening protocols and human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination programs, cisplatin (CDDP)-based chemotherapy remains the first-line therapeutic option for patients with advanced or recurrent CESC [3]. However, acquired CDDP resistance develops in >50 % of these patients, which represents the primary cause of clinical treatment failure and contributes to a dismal 5-year overall survival rate of <30 % in this patient cohort [4,5]. The mechanisms driving cancer cisplatin resistance are highly complex and multifaceted. Beyond classical drug efflux and apoptosis evasion, enhanced DNA damage clearance plays a pivotal role in cellular survival following CDDP treatment [6,7]. Consequently, DNA repair inhibition has emerged as a critical strategy to sensitize tumors to platinum-based agents [8]. Furthermore, recent therapeutic paradigms have highlighted the potential of redox-based sensitization, which disrupts the oxidative balance in resistant cells [9], as well as the development of platinum nanomedicine, which improves targeted delivery and minimizes systemic toxicity [10]. Despite these advances, further elucidation of the molecular mechanisms underlying chemoresistance in CESC and the identification of potential regulatory targets are of great clinical importance for developing novel therapeutic strategies and improving the prognosis of patients with this disease [11].

Cancer stem cells (CSCs), a unique subpopulation of cells within tumor tissues that possess self-renewal capacity and multi-lineage differentiation potential, are widely recognized as the core cellular mediators of chemoresistance in malignant tumors [12]. These cells can evade the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapeutic agents through multiple distinct mechanisms; notably, CSCs not only survive chemotherapy regimens but also further proliferate and differentiate to form new tumor lesions, thus acting as a key driver of therapeutic resistance and tumor recurrence [13]. The existence of CSCs in CESC has been well confirmed by numerous studies, and the high expression of CSC-associated markers is closely correlated with CDDP resistance in CESC. Such markers serve as important tools for identifying drug-resistant cell populations in CESC and represent potential predictive indicators for evaluating chemotherapy efficacy [14,15]. It is currently well established that CSCs enhance their intrinsic tolerance to CDDP by regulating a spectrum of cellular processes, including drug efflux capacity, activation of anti-apoptotic signaling pathways, and DNA damage repair ability, thereby driving the development of acquired resistance in the entire tumor tissue [16]. Accordingly, targeting and modulating CSC properties has emerged as an important research direction for reversing CDDP resistance in CESC.

The zinc finger of the cerebellum (ZIC) family comprises conserved transcription factors that regulate embryonic development and cell fate. Dysregulation of ZIC proteins is closely linked to tumor pathogenesis in multiple malignancies. As a core member of this family, ZIC5 acts as a critical oncogenic driver and is frequently aberrantly expressed in various malignant neoplasms [17]. In non-small cell lung cancer, melanoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma, ZIC5 promotes tumor progression by regulating cell proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and CSC properties [[18], [19], [20]]. Our previous study confirmed that ZIC5 is highly expressed in CESC tissues, correlates with advanced FIGO stage and poor clinical prognosis, and enhances stemness and invasive potential in CESC cells [21]. Nevertheless, the upstream regulatory factors of ZIC5 and its downstream signaling cascades that mediate chemoresistance in CESC remain largely undefined.

Ciliary Neurotrophic Factor Receptor (CNTFR), a member of the cytokine receptor superfamily, is critically involved in the regulation of cell survival, proliferation, and maintenance of stem cell properties [22]. Cardiotrophin-like cytokine factor 1 (CLCF1), the endogenous ligand of CNTFR, forms a functional complex with CNTFR and leukemia inhibitory factor receptor β (LIFRβ) to activate multiple downstream signaling pathways, including the STAT3 pathway [23]. The STAT3 pathway is frequently hyperactivated in CESC, and this aberrant activation in turn promotes the acquisition and maintenance of CSC properties, ultimately inducing CDDP resistance [24]. Notably, emerging evidence has shown that STAT3 can transcriptionally regulate ZIC family members in gliomas [25]. However, whether the CLCF1‑CNTFR‑STAT3 signaling axis contributes to ZIC5 regulation and CDDP resistance in CESC remains to be further investigated.

Here, we investigated whether a CLCF1–ZIC5–CNTFR positive feedback loop contributes to CDDP resistance in CESC through activation of STAT3 signaling and modulation of CSC properties. To address this question, we performed clinical sample analysis, in vitro functional assays, and in vivo experiments to examine the role and mechanism of this signaling axis in CDDP resistance. The present study aims to identify potential contributors to chemoresistance in CESC and to evaluate CNTFR as a possible therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

Human CESC cell lines (SiHa, C-33A, SW756, CaSki) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Specifically, SiHa, CaSki, and SW756 are HPV‑16‑positive CESC cell lines, while C-33A is an HPV-negative CESC cell line. This panel of cell lines covers both HPV-positive and HPV-negative CESC backgrounds, which well reflects the heterogeneity of clinical CESC tumors and validates the robustness of our findings. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination before use. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO₂. CDDP-resistant sublines (SiHa-R and C-33A-R) were established by stepwise exposure of parental cells to increasing concentrations of CDDP (0.5–5 μM) over 6 months, with maintenance in 2 μM CDDP to preserve resistance.

Patient samples and clinical data

A total of 132 CESC patients who received CDDP-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy before surgery at Xijing Hospital were enrolled in this study. All patients were treated with standardized neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimens, while patients who received concurrent chemoradiotherapy, postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy, or treatment for recurrent/metastatic disease were excluded from this study. Tumor response and therapy resistance were uniformly evaluated according to the RECIST 1.1 criteria. Paired tumor samples from 10 patients before and after chemotherapy were collected for dynamic expression analysis. Clinical data including age, tumor differentiation, pT/pN status, FIGO stage, and survival information were prospectively recorded. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xijing Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Lentiviral production and transduction

The ZIC5, CNTFR, and STAT3 ORF sequence were PCR amplified using specific primers and cloned into the lentiviral expression vector pEZ-Lv201 to develop recombinant plasmid. To construct shRNAs of ZIC5, CNTFR, and STAT3 into shRNA expression vector psi-LVRU6GP, the target sequences (Supplementary Table 1) were synthesized and inserted into the vector. The shRNA with a non-targeting sequence was used as a negative control. The constructed vectors were transfected into 293T cells to produce viruses. Cells were infected with concentrated viruses and the supernatant was replaced with complete culture media after 24 hours, followed by selection with puromycin.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Japan). RT-qPCR was performed on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA) using SYBR Green Master Mix (TaKaRa). GAPDH was used as an internal reference. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. The primer pairs used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, Switzerland). Protein concentrations were determined by the BCA assay (Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA), and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Antibody information, including origins and dilutions, is provided in Supplementary Table 2. Bands were visualized using an ECL Chemiluminescence Kit (Millipore) and quantified with ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections (4 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3 % H₂O₂. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies against ZIC5, CNTFR, Ki-67, and Cleaved Caspase-3 (Supplementary Table 2) at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies. Staining was developed with DAB (Beyotime), and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. The IHC H-score is calculated by adding the multiplication of the different staining intensities in four gradations (0, 1+, 2+, 3+) with each percentage of positive cells. H-score = 1 × (% cells 1+) + 2 × (% cells 2+) + 3 × (% cells 3+). We used the median H-score in the cohort as a cut-off to distinguish between high and low protein expression.

Luciferase reporter assay

The promoter regions of CNTFR and ZIC5 were cloned into the pGL3-Basic vector. Mutant promoters lacking binding sites for ZIC5 or STAT3 were generated by site-directed mutagenesis. HEK293T cells were cotransfected with promoter luciferase plasmids, effector plasmids, and pRL-TK (Renilla luciferase control vector). Luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA) 48 h after transfection.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP assays were performed using the EZ-ChIP Kit (Millipore) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were cross-linked with 1 % formaldehyde, lysed, and sonicated to shear chromatin. Chromatin fragments were immunoprecipitated with antibodies against ZIC5, STAT3, or IgG (negative control). Immunoprecipitated DNA was purified and analyzed by RT-qPCR using specific primers targeting the promoter regions of CNTFR or ZIC5 (Supplementary Table 1).

Cell viability and colony formation assays

For cell viability, cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with different concentrations of CDDP for 48 h. CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime) was added, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) was calculated using GraphPad Prism 8.0. For colony formation, cells were seeded in 6-well plates (500 cells/well) and cultured for 14 days. Colonies were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and counted manually.

Sphere formation assay

Cells were seeded in ultra-low attachment 6-well plates (1000 cells/well) in serum-free DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with B27 (Gibco), 20 ng/ml EGF (PeproTech, USA), and 10 ng/ml bFGF (PeproTech). Spheres with diameter >50 μm were counted after 7 days, and sphere formation efficiency was calculated.

Animal models

Female BALB/c nude mice (5–6 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory. For subcutaneous xenografts, 5 × 10⁶ SiHa-R or C-33A-R cells were injected into the flanks of mice. When tumors reached ∼100 mm³, mice were treated with CNTFR neutralizing antibody (5 or 10 mg/kg, i.p., 3 times/week) or vehicle. For patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, fresh CESC tissues were implanted into mice and passaged twice. PDX-bearing mice were randomized into four groups: vehicle, CNTFR antibody (5 mg/kg), CDDP (5 mg/kg), or combination therapy. Tumor volume was measured every 3 days, and mice were sacrificed after 4 weeks. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xijing Hospital.

Patient-derived organoid (PDO) culture and treatment

Fresh CESC tissues were minced into 1 mm³ fragments, digested with collagenase IV (Sigma, USA), and embedded in Matrigel (Corning, USA). Organoids were cultured in Advanced DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with growth factors and maintained at 37°C with 5 % CO₂. Organoids were treated with CNTFR neutralizing antibody (1 μg/ml), CDDP (20 μM), or their combination for 7 days. Organoid surface area was measured using ImageJ software.

Data mining

The CESC cohort from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) was applied to analyze ZIC5 mRNA expression and its relationships with CSC markers and clinical parameters. CDDP sensitivity data from CESC cell lines were obtained from the GDSC (www.cancerRxgene.org) database, which is the largest public database on molecular markers of cancer drug sensitivity and drug response.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Differences between groups were compared using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA. Correlations between gene expression and clinical parameters were analyzed by χ² test. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards regression models. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ZIC5 upregulation correlates with CDDP resistance and poor prognosis in CESC

Our previous study verified the aberrant upregulation of ZIC5 in CESC tissues, which contributes to malignant progression and poor patient prognosis [21]. Consistently, bioinformatics analysis of the TCGA CESC dataset further indicated that high ZIC5 expression is correlated with unsatisfactory therapeutic response and lymph node metastasis, strongly suggesting a potential regulatory role of ZIC5 in CESC malignant evolution (Supplementary Figure 1A and 1B). To establish the clinical relevance of ZIC5 in CDDP resistance of CESC, we first profiled ZIC5 expression in a cohort of 132 CESC patients treated with CDDP-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining revealed that ZIC5 expression was significantly higher in CDDP-resistant tumors compared to chemotherapy-sensitive counterparts (Fig. 1A). Patients with high ZIC5 expression were significantly associated with CDDP resistance, that is, the higher the expression level of ZIC5, the lower the complete response rate (Fig. 1B). To further capture the dynamic changes of ZIC5 expression during chemotherapy exposure, we performed paired IHC analysis on tumor specimens collected from 10 patients at baseline and after CDDP treatment, which showed a marked upregulation of ZIC5 in post-chemotherapy tissues, suggesting that ZIC5 activation is induced by CDDP and may be involved in acquired resistance (Fig. 1C and 1D). Clinically, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of CESC patients receiving CDDP-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy demonstrated that those with high ZIC5 expression exhibited significantly shorter overall survival (OS) than patients with low ZIC5 expression (Fig. 1E).

To further verify the association between ZIC5 expression and CDDP resistance in heterogeneous CESC cells, we first interrogated the GDSC database covering 10 CESC cell lines and observed a positive correlation between ZIC5 expression and CDDP IC₅₀ value (Supplementary Figure 1C). Subsequent in vitro assays yielded consistent results, showing that ZIC5 protein levels were positively correlated with CDDP IC₅₀ values across CESC cell lines (Fig. 1F). To consolidate the above observations, we further established CDDP-resistant sublines (SiHa-R and C-33A-R) via stepwise dose escalation of CDDP exposure. These resistant cells exhibited markedly reduced CDDP sensitivity (Fig. 1G), enhanced colony formation ability (Fig. 1H), and significantly upregulated ZIC5 mRNA and protein expression compared to their isogenic parental cells (Fig. 1I and 1J). These clinical findings establish an important correlation between ZIC5 upregulation and CDDP treatment failure in CESC, prompting us to interrogate the causal functional role of ZIC5 in chemoresistance.

ZIC5 promotes CDDP resistance and CSC properties in CESC

Having established the clinical link between ZIC5 overexpression and poor chemotherapy response in CESC, we next performed gain- and loss-of-function studies to define the causal role of ZIC5 in CDDP resistance. We silenced ZIC5 using a specific shRNA, and efficient knockdown at the protein level was confirmed by western blot in CDDP-resistant SiHa-R and C-33A-R cells. Cell viability assays across a gradient of CDDP concentrations revealed that ZIC5 knockdown significantly shifted the dose-response curve leftward, reducing the IC₅₀ of CDDP in SiHa-R cells and C-33A-R cells, indicating an important restoration of CDDP sensitivity (Fig. 2A). Conversely, we stably overexpressed ZIC5 in parental CDDP-sensitive SiHa and C-33A cells, which led to a rightward shift in the CDDP dose-response curve, with an increase in CDDP IC₅₀, recapitulating the chemoresistant phenotype of the isogenic resistant sublines (Fig. 2B). Clonogenic survival assays, which measure the long-term proliferative capacity and clonogenic potential of tumor cells following chemotherapy exposure, further validated these findings. ZIC5 silencing in SiHa-R and C-33A-R cells resulted in a > 70 % reduction in colony formation capacity under CDDP exposure, compared to non-targeting shControl cells (Fig. 2C). In contrast, enforced ZIC5 expression in parental SiHa and C-33A cells enhanced colony formation by >4‑fold under CDDP treatment (Fig. 2D).

It is widely acknowledged that CSC properties play a vital role in driving CDDP resistance. To uncover whether ZIC5 participates in this process by regulating CSC phenotypes, we first analyzed the TCGA CESC dataset to explore the correlation of ZIC5 with canonical CSC markers. The results revealed that ZIC5 expression was positively correlated with multiple CESC CSC-related markers, including ABCC5, CD44, TP63, SOX2, ALDH1A3 and BMI1 (Supplementary Figure 2). We then performed RT-qPCR and western blot to confirm the regulatory effect of ZIC5 on CSC markers. In SiHa-R cells, ZIC5 knockdown robustly downregulated CD44, SOX2 and BMI-1 at mRNA and protein levels. Conversely, ectopic ZIC5 expression increased their abundance in parental SiHa cells (Fig. 2E and 2F). To functionally assess the impact of ZIC5 on self-renewal, we utilized sphere formation assays, a well-established method for evaluating this property in vitro. ZIC5 knockdown in SiHa-R cells significantly reduced sphere number and average sphere diameter compared to shControl cells, indicating impaired self-renewal capacity. Conversely, ZIC5 overexpression in parental SiHa cells markedly increased sphere number and average diameter, demonstrating enhanced stem cell activity (Fig. 2G and 2H).

To assess the impact of ZIC5 on in vivo tumor-initiating capacity, a defining feature of CSCs linked to therapeutic failure and tumor relapse, we performed in vivo limiting dilution assays with serial dilutions of SiHa-R cells with or without ZIC5 knockdown, and SiHa cells with or without ZIC5 overexpression. Limiting dilution analysis of xenografted tumors developed from different numbers of injected cells showed that ZIC5 overexpression increased the tumorigenic capacity of CESC cells, whereas ZIC5 knockdown had the opposite effect (Fig. 2I and 2J). Collectively, these results establish that ZIC5 is a functional driver of CDDP resistance and CSC maintenance in CESC. These findings prompted us to delineate the downstream transcriptional program through which ZIC5 exerts these oncogenic effects.

ZIC5 transcriptionally activates CNTFR in CESC

To identify the direct transcriptional targets of ZIC5 that mediate its pro-resistance and pro-stemness functions, we performed comparative RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on ZIC5-overexpressing SiHa cells and empty vector control cells. Differential gene expression analysis identified CNTFR, the receptor for the IL-6 family cytokine CLCF1, as one of the most significantly upregulated genes upon ZIC5 overexpression (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table 3). To validate the clinical relevance of the ZIC5-CNTFR regulatory axis, we first examined their expression correlation in primary CESC patient specimens. Western blot analysis of 26 CESC tissues revealed a significant positive correlation between ZIC5 and CNTFR protein levels (Fig. 3B and 3C), which was further validated by IHC co-staining in the full patient cohort (Fig. 3D and 3E). Consistent with the prognostic value of ZIC5, high CNTFR expression was also associated with significantly shorter OS in CESC patients treated with CDDP-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy (Fig. 3F). To validate the clinical significance of CNTFR in CESC, we analyzed its expression in relation to clinicopathological characteristics in our patient cohort. High CNTFR expression was significantly correlated with advanced pT status (p = 0.0414), lymph node metastasis (p = 0.0329), and advanced FIGO stage (p = 0.0185) (Supplementary Table 4). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses further identified high CNTFR as an independent unfavorable prognostic factor for OS in CESC patients (HR = 2.504, 95 % CI: 1.571–3.965, p = 0.042; HR = 2.855, 95 % CI: 1.861–6.471, p = 0.036) (Supplementary Table 5).

In parallel functional experiments, we found that ZIC5 overexpression significantly upregulated CNTFR mRNA and protein levels in parental SiHa cells, while ZIC5 knockdown markedly reduced CNTFR expression in CDDP-resistant SiHa-R cells (Fig. 3G and 3H). Bioinformatics analysis predicted a potential ZIC5-binding motifs in the proximal promoter region of the CNTFR gene (Fig. 3I). Luciferase reporter assays showed that ZIC5 overexpression significantly enhanced the activity of the wild-type (WT) CNTFR promoter, but had no effect on the mutant (MUT) promoter with deleted ZIC5-binding sites (Fig. 3J). Exogenous and endogenous chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays further confirmed that ZIC5 directly bound to the CNTFR promoter in both ZIC5-overexpressing SiHa cells and CDDP-resistant SiHa-R cells (Fig. 3K). These results demonstrate that CNTFR is a direct transcriptionally activated target of ZIC5 in CESC, prompting us to further investigate whether CNTFR serves as the key functional effector mediating ZIC5-induced CDDP resistance and cancer stemness.

CNTFR mediates ZIC5-induced CDDP resistance and cancer stemness

To verify that CNTFR is a key downstream effector of ZIC5, we performed bidirectional rescue experiments by altering CNTFR expression under ZIC5 gain‑ or loss‑of‑function conditions, and examined changes in CDDP sensitivity and cancer stemness. CCK-8 cell viability assays across a CDDP concentration gradient showed that exogenous CNTFR expression substantially reversed the leftward shift in the dose-response curve induced by ZIC5 knockdown, restoring the CDDP IC₅₀ to levels comparable to those of shControl SiHa-R cells. Conversely, we silenced CNTFR using a validated shRNA in ZIC5-overexpressing SiHa cells. Knockdown of CNTFR markedly reversed ZIC5-induced CDDP resistance and restored IC₅₀ to control levels (Fig. 4A). We next validated these findings in clonogenic survival assays. In ZIC5-silenced SiHa-R cells, exogenous CNTFR expression markedly rescued the impaired colony formation capacity, restoring colony number to levels comparable to shControl SiHa-R cells. In contrast, CNTFR knockdown in ZIC5-overexpressing SiHa cells substantially abolished the enhanced colony formation induced by ZIC5 overexpression, reducing colony number to baseline control levels (Fig. 4B).

We further investigated whether CNTFR mediates ZIC5-driven CSC maintenance by assessing stemness factors SOX2 and BMI‑1 in rescue models. Western blot analysis showed that the downregulation of SOX2 and BMI-1 induced by ZIC5 knockdown was substantially reversed by exogenous CNTFR expression in SiHa-R cells. Conversely, the upregulation of SOX2 and BMI-1 induced by ZIC5 overexpression was markedly abrogated by CNTFR silencing in parental SiHa cells (Fig. 4C). In ZIC5-silenced SiHa-R cells, which exhibited a marked reduction in sphere number and average diameter, exogenous CNTFR expression markedly rescued the impaired sphere formation capacity, restoring both sphere number and size to control levels (Fig. 4D). In ZIC5-overexpressing SiHa cells, which displayed enhanced sphere formation, CNTFR knockdown markedly abolished this effect, reducing sphere number and average diameter back to baseline control levels (Fig. 4E). Taken together, these rescue experiments establish that CNTFR is the essential downstream effector mediating ZIC5-driven CDDP resistance and CSC maintenance in CESC.

CLCF1 activates ZIC5 via CNTFR-STAT3 signaling in CESC

Given that ZIC5 was significantly upregulated in CESC and promoted CDDP resistance, we sought to investigate the molecular mechanisms facilitating ZIC5 upregulation in CESC. Since CNTFR contributed to the aggressive phenotypes of ZIC5-overexpressing CESC, its canonical ligand CLCF1 attracted our attention. Previous studies have shown that the CLCF1/CNTFR axis is closely linked to poor prognosis in multiple solid tumors and drives malignant phenotypes in tumor cells via activating the PI3K/AKT, ERK1/2, c-JNK and STAT3 signaling pathways [26]. Based on these studies, we hypothesized that the CLCF1/CNTFR axis regulates ZIC5 expression through the implicated signaling pathways. To test our hypothesis, we treated SiHa-R and C-33A-R cells with different concentrations of CLCF1 and observed that CLCF1 induced ZIC5 expression in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5A and 5B), confirming that extracellular CLCF1 upregulates ZIC5 expression in CESC cells. Given that CNTFR acts as the transmembrane receptor for CLCF1, we investigated whether CNTFR is essential for CLCF1-induced ZIC5 expression. SiHa-R and C-33A-R cells were transfected with CNTFR-targeting shRNA before CLCF1 stimulation, and CNTFR knockdown almost substantially abrogated CLCF1-induced ZIC5 upregulation (Fig. 5C).

To elucidate the intracellular signaling cascade connecting CLCF1‑CNTFR activation to ZIC5 upregulation, we examined key downstream pathways using specific pharmacological inhibitors. Only the STAT3 inhibitor reversed CLCF1-induced ZIC5 upregulation, while inhibitors of ERK, JNK, and AKT had no significant effect (Fig. 5D). This finding was further confirmed by genetic knockdown of STAT3, which substantially abolished CLCF1-mediated ZIC5 activation (Fig. 5E). Bioinformatics analysis predicted a conserved STAT3-binding motif in the proximal promoter region of the ZIC5 gene (Fig. 5F), and luciferase reporter assays showed that STAT3 overexpression significantly enhanced the activity of the WT ZIC5 promoter, but had no effect on the MUT promoter with deleted STAT3-binding sites (Fig. 5G). STAT3 inhibition also blocked CLCF1-induced ZIC5 promoter activation (Fig. 5H). Exogenous and endogenous ChIP assays further confirmed that STAT3 directly bound to the ZIC5 promoter in both STAT3-overexpressing SiHa cells and CDDP-resistant SiHa-R cells (Fig. 5I). To explore whether ZIC5 mediates CLCF1‑driven CDDP resistance and cancer stemness, we performed rescue experiments to verify its requirement in these oncogenic phenotypes. CLCF1 treatment significantly increased CDDP IC₅₀ values, enhanced colony formation, upregulated SOX2 and BMI-1 expression and strengthened sphere formation capacity in SiHa-R and C-33A-R cells, while all these effects were substantially reversed by ZIC5 knockdown (Fig. 6A-6F). These findings demonstrate that CLCF1, the cognate ligand of CNTFR, induces ZIC5 transcription through STAT3-mediated promoter binding, thereby forming a positive feedback loop that reinforces pro‑resistance signaling.

Targeting CNTFR reverses CDDP resistance in CESC

To translate our mechanistic findings into a potential therapeutic strategy, we evaluated the efficacy of a CNTFR neutralizing antibody, which can block ligand binding and disrupt the positive feedback loop, in reversing CDDP resistance. Treatment with the CNTFR neutralizing antibody significantly reduced ZIC5 and CNTFR mRNA and protein levels, as well as p-STAT3 (Y705) expression, in CLCF1-treated SiHa-R and C-33A-R cells (Fig. 7A and 7B). In vitro functional assays showed that the antibody reversed CLCF1-enhanced CDDP resistance, as evidenced by decreased IC₅₀ values (Fig. 7C), suppressed colony formation (Fig. 7D), and reduced sphere formation efficiency (Fig. 7E). Additionally, the antibody downregulated the expression of CSC markers SOX2 and BMI-1 (Fig. 7F), consistent with the role of CLCF1-CNTFR signal in maintaining CSC properties.

Following validation in vitro, we evaluated the in vivo antitumor efficacy of CNTFR blockade in CDDP-resistant CESC xenograft models. Treatment with a CNTFR-neutralizing antibody suppressed tumor growth in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 7G and 7H). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of CNTFR reverses CDDP resistance in CESC, supporting its potential use in combination with standard first‑line CDDP treatment.

CNTFR blockade sensitizes CESC to cisplatin in PDX and organoid models

Given that single-agent targeted therapy seldom yields durable clinical responses, we further investigated the antitumor efficacy of CNTFR blockade combined with CDDP in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, which better reflect the heterogeneity and molecular characteristics of primary human CESC. Three PDX models were established using high ZIC5/CNTFR-expressing CESC specimens, and mice were divided into four groups: vehicle control, CDDP, CNTFR antibody, and combination therapy. Combined treatment markedly suppressed tumor growth compared with monotherapy groups, as evidenced by reduced tumor volume over time and lower tumor weight (Fig. 8A-8C). IHC analysis revealed that the combination therapy significantly elevated cleaved caspase‑3 levels (Fig. 8D) and reduced Ki‑67 expression (Fig. 8E). To further validate the translational potential of this combination strategy in a physiologically relevant model, we established patient-derived organoids (PDOs) from fresh CESC surgical specimens. The combination treatment led to significant shrinkage of PDOs, with a marked reduction in organoid surface area compared to monotherapy (Fig. 8F). Collectively, these data from PDX and PDO models provide preclinical evidence that combining a CNTFR neutralizing antibody with CDDP results in a greater antitumor effect than either monotherapy alone. This suggests a potential therapeutic strategy for overcoming CDDP resistance in CESC patients.

Discussion

CESC remains a leading cause of gynecological cancer mortality globally, largely due to the development of cisplatin resistance and the subsequent metastatic progression, which severely limits the efficacy of conventional chemotherapy and leads to poor clinical outcomes [27]. Our study uncovers a CLCF1-ZIC5-CNTFR positive feedback cascade that facilitates the acquisition of CDDP resistance, maintenance of CSC phenotypes and progression of aggressive malignancy in CESC. The identification of this regulatory network not only explains key molecular mechanisms driving chemoresistance, but also lays a theoretical and experimental groundwork for designing alternative therapeutic regimens against CDDP-resistant CESC.

A central finding of this study is that ZIC5 is both a clinical biomarker and a functional driver of CDDP resistance and poor prognosis in CESC. Consistent with previous reports linking ZIC5 to oncogenicity in melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer [19,20], our clinical data demonstrate that ZIC5 upregulation correlates with acquired CDDP resistance and shortened OS. Functionally, gain- and loss-of-function assays confirmed ZIC5’s role in promoting CSC traits. These results extend our prior work on ZIC5 in CESC aggressiveness [21], positioning ZIC5 as a core regulator of both stemness and chemoresistance in this malignancy.

Mechanistically, we identified CNTFR as a direct transcriptional target of ZIC5, a regulatory interaction that has not been described in CESC before. As reported by Kim et al., the CLCF1–CNTFR signaling axis facilitates proliferation and survival of lung cancer cells. Their group also generated a high-affinity soluble receptor eCNTFR Fc, which suppresses oncogenic signaling by binding to and neutralizing CLCF1 [28]. Song et al. demonstrate that CAF-secreted CLCF1 triggers a positive feedback loop via CXCL6/TGF-β, enhancing hepatocellular carcinoma stemness and N2 neutrophil polarization, and correlates with advanced disease and poor prognosis [29]. Our rescue experiments confirmed that CNTFR is essential for ZIC5-induced CDDP resistance and CSC traits, confirming its role as a key downstream mediator. Clinically, the co-expression of ZIC5 and CNTFR correlates with unfavorable clinical outcomes, further highlighting the pathological significance of this regulatory axis in CESC.

We further identified the CLCF1-STAT3 pathway as the upstream activator of ZIC5, completing the positive feedback loop. CLCF1, a ligand of CNTFR, signals through STAT3 to transcriptionally activate ZIC5—an interaction supported by ChIP and luciferase assays showing direct STAT3 binding to the ZIC5 promoter. STAT3 hyperactivation is a well-documented driver of chemoresistance [24], and our findings link this pathway to ZIC5 regulation for the first time in CESC. The resulting CLCF1-ZIC5-CNTFR feedback loop creates a self-reinforcing signaling cascade that amplifies stemness and resistance, offering a plausible explanation for the persistence of aggressive CESC cells after chemotherapy.

Therapeutically, our preclinical data highlight the potential utility of targeting CNTFR. In vitro, CNTFR neutralizing antibody disrupted the feedback loop, reversed CDDP resistance, and suppressed CSC properties. In vivo, monotherapy with the antibody inhibited tumor growth in subcutaneous xenografts, its combination with CDDP resulted in enhanced antitumor effects in PDX and PDO models. These models are considered highly relevant for evaluating clinical response potential [30]. Moreover, the observed increase in cleaved caspase-3 and reduction in Ki-67 in tumors treated with the combination therapy confirm that this chemosensitizing effect arises from enhanced tumor cell apoptosis and diminished proliferation. Collectively, these preclinical findings provide a rationale for further investigation into the efficacy and safety of combining CNTFR blockade with CDDP-based chemotherapy for patients with CESC.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the clinical cohort was single-centered, and validation in multi-institutional cohorts is needed to confirm the prognostic value of the CLCF1-ZIC5-CNTFR axis. Second, the PDX and PDO models focused on ZIC5/CNTFR-high tumors; further studies are required to assess efficacy in low-expression tumors. Finally, the specific role of LIFRβ—an essential co-receptor for CLCF1 signaling—was not markedly elucidated and merits future investigation. While our current findings highlight the potential of targeting the CLCF1–ZIC5–CNTFR axis to overcome CDDP resistance, the clinical management of chemoresistant CESC will likely require multifaceted strategies. Future studies should explore the integration of CNTFR blockade with other emerging therapeutic paradigms. For instance, combining our targeted approach with DNA repair inhibition could further prevent the NER-mediated clearance of platinum-DNA adducts in resistant cells [[6], [7], [8]]. Additionally, exploiting redox-based sensitization to disrupt the oxidative balance of CSCs [9], or utilizing advanced platinum nanomedicine delivery systems [10], may offer superior strategies to maximize the efficacy of CDDP while minimizing systemic toxicity. Exploring these combinatorial approaches represents a critical direction for our future research.

In conclusion, this study identifies the CLCF1-ZIC5-CNTFR positive feedback loop as a contributor to CDDP resistance in CESC. By connecting cytokine signaling, transcription factor activity, and the maintenance of CSC properties, these findings advance our understanding of CESC biology and suggest a new therapeutic avenue for overcoming chemoresistance. Targeting this loop, particularly via CNTFR neutralization, represents a potential strategy that may improve outcomes for patients with advanced CESC.
