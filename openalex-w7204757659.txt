Biominerals in human bones and teeth are composed primarily of calcium phosphate (CaP) compounds, predominantly apatite. Biomineralization in human hard tissues proceeds under supersaturated conditions in body
ﬂuids. This review summarizes biomineralization-inspired technologies that use supersaturated CaP solutions
for the synthesis and functionalization of CaP particles and coatings, highlighting the author’s contributions. In
biomimetic processing, functional cargo such as proteins, nucleic acids, antibacterial agents, and inorganic
nanoclusters can be loaded into CaP via coprecipitation in supersaturated CaP solutions, allowing the fabrication of CaP-based particles and coatings with diverse functions. The fundamental principles of this biomimetic
approach and its biomedical applications are described, with emphasis on our studies on gene delivery and
periodontal tissue regeneration. The review also highlights a laser-assisted biomimetic process that enables
rapid, region-speciﬁc CaP coating and its application to dental surface functionalization. These studies demonstrate the versatility of biomineralization-inspired CaP processing as a platform for next-generation functional
bioceramics.

regeneration, Scaffolds

1. Introduction
Biominerals in vertebrate bones and teeth are primarily
composed of calcium phosphate (CaP) compounds, predominantly apatite. In human cortical bone, apatite nanocrystals are deposited on collagen ﬁbrils to form a hierarchical organic–inorganic composite, whereas human
enamel is highly mineralized and constructed of c-axisoriented, needle-like apatite nanocrystals. Biomineralization of these tissues proceeds in body ﬂuids that are
supersaturated with respect to apatite.
Hydroxyapatite [Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2] and some other CaP
ceramics (e.g., ¢-tricalcium phosphate and octacalcium
phosphate) are chemically similar to human biominerals
and exhibit good biocompatibility and osteoconductivity,
enabling bonding to host bone in vivo.1) Therefore, these
CaP ceramics have been widely used as hard-tissue substitutes in orthopedic and dental practice. Nonetheless, many
bulk CaP ceramics are brittle, which limits their use in loadbearing and ﬂexible devices. This limitation has driven the
development of CaP-based particles and coatings.
This review focuses on biomineralization-inspired synthesis and functionalization of CaP-based particles and
coatings using supersaturated CaP solutions, highlighting

2. Nano-integration of functional
cargo and CaP
Among various synthesis approaches for CaP-based particles and coatings, biomimetic processes that use supersaturated CaP solutions as reaction media are distinguished
by their mild synthetic conditions, including near-neutral
pH, low temperature (typically near-physiological temperature or lower), and physiological ionic strength. Such
conditions enable the integration of a wide range of functional cargo types within the CaP matrix via coprecipitation
in supersaturated solutions while retaining their biological
functions (Fig. 1).2–4) Representative cargo types include
proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides, inorganic nanoclusters, nutrients, antibacterial agents, and functional
elements. They can be loaded into the CaP matrix through
elemental substitution or by entrapment at grain boundaries or within intracrystalline spaces.

3. Synthesis of CaP particles loaded
with functional cargo
3.1

Process fundamentals

CaP particles loaded with functional cargo can be synthesized using highly supersaturated, labile CaP solutions
via homogeneous CaP nucleation (amorphous phase nucleation) and subsequent growth in the presence of the target cargo, resulting in cargo–CaP coprecipitation throughout the bulk solution (Fig. 1, left). The as-formed CaP
particles (without aging) are generally in a metastable
amorphous state and can transform into the thermodynamically more stable apatite upon aging.9) For example, in
CaP particles loaded with ciproﬂoxacin (CIF), a ﬂuoroquinolone antibacterial agent, extending the reaction time
from 2 to 24 h caused transformation of the particle matrix
from amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) to apatite
[Fig. 3(a)].10) Consequently, CIF release from the particles
was delayed despite the increased CIF loading [Figs. 3(b)
and 3(c)].10) This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact
that cargo release from CaP proceeds in concert with phase
transformation and dissolution of the CaP matrix (in either
particle or coating form), and that apatite is less soluble
than ACP under physiological conditions.9) Notably, apatite (and other CaP compounds) synthesized from supersaturated solutions is generally bioresorbable, whereas
sintered hydroxyapatite is typically nonresorbable and
may persist in the body over the long term. This makes
biomimetically synthesized apatite useful as an immobilization matrix and delivery vehicle for cargo. The particle
size and dispersibility can be controlled by synthesis conditions, including solution composition, pH, temperature,
reaction time, and the mixing protocol, and most eﬀectively by adding dispersants that provide electrostatic and/
or steric repulsion.

4. Synthesis of CaP coatings loaded
with functional cargo
4.1

Process fundamentals

In a biomimetic process, CaP coatings loaded with functional cargo can be synthesized even on thermally sensitive
polymeric substrates.3,27) Such coatings can be formed by
inducing CaP growth at the substrate surface in metastable,
supersaturated CaP solutions containing the target cargo,
thereby allowing cargo–CaP coprecipitation at the solid–
liquid interface (Fig. 1, right). To induce interfacial CaP
growth, polymeric substrates typically require prior surface modiﬁcation with speciﬁc functional groups (e.g., Si–
OH, Ti–OH, and COOH) and/or CaP seeds. However,
early surface-modiﬁcation strategies often required complex and time-consuming procedures, such as the glasscontacting process, silane-coupling treatment, sol–gel
processing, and photografting.28–31) To address this limitation, we established a simple surface-modiﬁcation
strategy3) that chemically mimics the initial biomineralization steps on certain osteoconductive materials, namely,
calcium-ion adsorption, phosphate-ion uptake, and deposition of ACP, followed by spontaneous CaP growth and
transformation into crystalline apatite.32,33)
In our biomimetic process, a polymeric substrate is ﬁrst
treated with NaOH34) or oxygen plasma35–39) to increase its
surface wettability. It is then alternately dipped in calciumion and phosphate-ion solutions several times (typically
three cycles) to deposit ACP on the surface, in a procedure
simpliﬁed from Taguchi’s method.40) Subsequently, the
ACP-precoated substrate is immersed in a metastable,
cargo-containing supersaturated CaP solution for a few
days. During this immersion period, the precoated ACP
grows into a micrometer-thick apatite coating while incorporating the cargo. This process can be used to coat a wide
variety of polymeric substrates, including those with
complex geometries (e.g., ﬁbers,37) sponges,38) and porous
blocks34,35)). The coating–substrate adhesion can be tuned
by adjusting the coating thickness and composition (e.g.,
content of loaded cargo)39) as well as the surface roughness
and chemistry of the substrate.36,37) For example, oxygenplasma treatment increased surface roughness and surface
density of polar functional groups of a poly(lactic acid)

substrate in a power-density-dependent manner, thereby
increasing coating adhesion up to approximately 6 MPa

Owing to their good biocompatibility and osteoconductivity, apatite coatings have long been applied to orthopedic and dental implants mainly by thermal processes such
as plasma spraying. The biomimetic process described
above provides a non-thermal route for producing apatite
coatings loaded with a variety of cargo, including thermally labile organic molecules, thereby imparting diverse
functions to coated implants in addition to biocompatibility and osteoconductivity. For instance, loading antibacterial agents into apatite coatings confers antibacterial
activity,41) whereas loading cell-adhesive proteins such as
laminin42–44) and ﬁbronectin17,45) promotes cell adhesion to
the coated implant. Moreover, loading regenerative proteins such as bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2)46)
and ﬁbroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2)47) enhances bone
tissue regeneration around the coated implant. With such
an approach, the surface functionality of coated implants
can be tailored to target applications by selecting an
appropriate cargo. This represents a major advantage of
the biomimetic coating process over conventional apatite
coating processes, including plasma spraying, ﬂame
spraying, and sputtering. Among the many reported applications of biomimetic coating, this review focuses on our
studies of gene delivery (Section 4.2) and periodontal
tissue regeneration (Section 4.3).

Surface-mediated
applications

gene

delivery

As described in Section 3.2, conventional CaP-based
transfection systems use dispersed CaP particles (Fig. 4,
left). In 2004, Shen et al. proposed a surface-mediated
system using culture plates coated with DNA-loaded CaP
(Fig. 4, right).48) We extended this surface-mediated concept to create multifunctional gene-delivery devices capable of modulating cellular and tissue responses through
the co-loading strategy based on our biomimetic process.
We synthesized apatite coatings co-loaded with DNA (a
plasmid encoding the luciferase reporter gene) and celladhesive proteins (laminin or ﬁbronectin) on polymeric
substrates, and cultured cells on their surfaces for gene
delivery. Transfection eﬃciency increased with increasing
protein loading [Fig. 6(a)], most likely due to enhanced
cell adhesion and an enlarged cell–coating interface
[Fig. 6(b)].44,45,49)
The biomimetic synthesis enables straightforward control over coating composition and structure, thereby allowing precise tuning of transfection performance. For
instance, transfection eﬃciency can be regulated by the
amounts of DNA and cell-adhesive proteins loaded into
apatite coatings.44,45,49) In addition, apatite solubility can
be modulated through ion substitution—carbonate substitution increases solubility, whereas ﬂuoride substitution
decreases it—thereby aﬀecting not only transfection eﬃciency but also the timing of gene expression.50) Antibody
co-loading in the coating enables antigen-speciﬁc, targeted

4.3

tissue

Bioresorbable, infection-resistant, and osteogenic porous scaﬀolds are needed to promote periodontal tissue

bioresorption [Fig. 7(b)] in a rat calvarial defect model.55)

5. Laser-assisted biomimetic coating
5.1

Process fundamentals

In a conventional biomimetic process, a surfacemodiﬁed substrate typically requires immersion in supersaturated solutions for periods ranging from one day to
several days. To overcome this limitation, we developed a
laser-assisted process, based on Lee’s technique,61) to accelerate pseudo-biomineralization on substrates. Using this
process, we achieved rapid CaP (mainly apatite) coating
within only 30 min.62–70) In this process, a nanosecond
Nd:YAG pulsed UV laser is used without focusing to irradiate a substrate immersed in a metastable, supersaturated
CaP solution [Fig. 8(a)]. After 30 min of irradiation, CaP
forms selectively on the irradiated region of the substrate
[Fig. 8(b)].63) This laser-assisted process requires neither
hours of immersion in the supersaturated solution nor prior
surface modiﬁcation, and it also enables region-speciﬁc
coating. These features represent clear advantages over
conventional biomimetic processes.
A possible mechanism underlying laser-assisted pseudobiomineralization is as follows. The supersaturated solution used in this process does not induce homogeneous
CaP nucleation in the bulk solution. Because the solution
absorbs little UV light, the incident laser light mostly
reaches the substrate and modiﬁes its surface. In the case
of polymeric substrates, this modiﬁcation includes surface
roughening, bond breaking (with formation of functional
groups), and enhanced surface wettability.62–64) The modiﬁed surface, with its increased surface free energy, pro-

The local increase in solution temperature promotes mass

This laser-assisted coating process can be applied to a
wide variety of substrate materials, including polymers,62–64) metals,66–68) ceramics,69) and their composites.70) For titanium metal, not only UV but also nearinfrared (NIR) and visible laser irradiation was eﬀective in

depending on the optical absorption characteristics of the

The apatite coating obtained by the laser-assisted process exhibited good cytocompatibility and promoted cell
proliferation on polymer substrates.63) Functional cargo
can also be loaded into the apatite coating,66) as in the conventional biomimetic synthesis. However, thermally labile
organic cargo may undergo inactivation or denaturation
during irradiation.66) Therefore, inorganic cargo (discussed
below) is more suitable for this laser-assisted process.

5.2

Dental coating applications

As noted above, the laser-assisted process can induce
rapid pseudo-biomineralization on a desired region of a
substrate and is therefore well suited for dental surface
functionalization. We ﬁrst conducted preliminary studies
using sintered hydroxyapatite as a model tooth material
and demonstrated rapid, region-speciﬁc pseudo-biomineralization on its surface within 30 min of irradiation.69)
Addition of ﬂuoride, as a functional element, to the supersaturated solution resulted in the formation of ﬂuoridesubstituted apatite (F-Ap) with enhanced acid resistance.
We then extended this approach to human dentin substrates, with appropriate ethical approval, and demonstrated successful dental surface coating ex vivo.72)
More recently, we further reﬁned the process by precoating the dentin substrate with a light-absorbing paste containing indocyanine green (ICG), an NIR absorber, and
irradiating it with an NIR dental diode laser in a metastable,
ﬂuoride-containing supersaturated CaP solution.73) This
reﬁnement enabled even faster pseudo-biomineralization
(F-Ap formation) within only 30 s of irradiation.73) The
resulting F-Ap coating consisted of an enamel-like nanostructure, i.e., needle-like nanocrystals with preferential caxis orientation approximately perpendicular to the substrate surface [Fig. 9(a)].73) In a similar process with a
longer irradiation time of 3 min, a ﬂuoride-loaded miner-

6. Summary
Biomineralization-inspired processing using supersaturated CaP solutions enables the fabrication of functional
CaP-based particles and coatings under mild aqueous
conditions. A variety of functional cargo types can be loaded into CaP particles and coatings, making them useful for
diverse biomedical applications, including gene delivery,
scaﬀold-based tissue regeneration, and dental surface functionalization, as exempliﬁed in this review. These studies
highlight the potential of biomineralization-inspired CaP
processing as a versatile platform for next-generation functional bioceramics.
