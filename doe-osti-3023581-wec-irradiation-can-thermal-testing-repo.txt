1 Background
With renewed interest for the next generation of small modular reactors and space-based nuclear
reactors, metal hydrides have been a focus of research for moderating materials to reduce the
need for highly enriched fuel to maintain small core sizes. Yttrium hydride (YH) is an attractive
candidate for these metal hydrides due to its ability to retain hydrogen at elevated temperatures
as well as the low neutron absorption cross section of yttrium metal. Given the necessity for
reactors to operate at elevated temperatures for extended periods of time, understanding
hydrogen retention in moderator channels in simulated operating thermal conditions is
paramount in predicting reactor performance and the long-term stability of YH. Additionally,
coating strategies on the moderator encapsulating will be explored to ascertain if it aids in
retention of hydrogen within the capsule and moderator.

Together with Idaho National Laboratory (INL), Westinghouse and Los Alamos National
Laboratory (LANL) were awarded funding for YH moderator and encapsulation coating
development under Department of Energy (DOE) Funding Opportunity Announcement
(FOA) 2271. This report documents out-of-pile thermal cycling trials that LANL performed on
integral encapsulated YH samples as part of the FOA-2271 project.

2 Sample Prepara>on
2.1 YH Fabrica-on
YH specimens were fabricated for the WEC ATR irradiation capsule testing campaign1. Yttrium
metal cylinders were machined at the Prototype Fabrication (PF) facility at Los Alamos National
Laboratory (LANL) from a K902 yttrium ingot via electrical discharge machining (EDM), which
were then subsequently cleaned with Scotch-Brite pads on a lathe to remove surface scale from
the EDM process. The metal specimens went through a final cleaning step of ultrasonication for
15 minutes in isopropanol before being allowed to air dry.

Crack-free bulk YH specimens were fabricated from these yttrium metal cylinders using a direct
hydriding process. The yttrium metal is first heat treated under a hydrogen gas flow profile
informed by the Y-H binary pressure-composition-temperature (PCT) profile. For the targeted
hydrogen/metal (H/M) ratio of 1.94, the furnace profile consists of a 10°C/min ramp to 1100 C
with a one hour hold, a 10 C/min cooling rate to 900°C followed by a 0.1°C/min cooling rate to
600°C with a 10-hour hold, ending with a 10°C/min cooling rate to reach room temperature. An
argon gas mixture containing 6% hydrogen is introduced once the initial heating profile reaches
600°C and is switched to pure argon at the final 600°C dwell to prevent the formation of YH3
which leads to severe cracking. Specimens for this thermal testing campaign were originally
witness specimens for the ATR campaign, specifically the #8 specimens (h: 10mm, Ø: 12.5mm)
which deviates slightly from the requested geometries from the test plan [Appendix II, 3.b.i.] of
10mm tall, 10mm diameter YH specimens. Geometries for these specimens can be found in

 1

Appendix IV from the WEC ATR test campaign and the specimen geometries used here (Detail
8).

2.2 Sample Cans
Cans were fabricated from FeCrAl tube and disks provided by WEC. The FeCrAl tube was
procured from Kanthal (APM) and turned down to a wall thickness of 0.5mm. The FeCrAl disks
was received with an as-fabricated, powder metallurgical surface finish which was not conducive
to the adhesion of the desired coatings. They were therefore cleaned with a degreaser and an HCl
acid dip to remove the residue. The canister body and lids were fabricated from stock material
into the two component geometries detailed in Appendix IV. The first FeCrAl lid was welded
onto the FeCrAl tube using an automated electron beam welder (EBW). The remaining lid and
the can + lid assembly was subsequently oxidized to produce a protective surface coating on the
interior and exterior of the cans. FeCrAl components were set on top of a tabular alumina bed
and oxidized in an air muffle furnace (Thermolyne F48055-60), where they were heated at
5°/min to 1200°C where they dwelled for 2 hrs before cooling at 3°C/min to room temperature.
With these surface oxidized FeCrAl assemblies and lids, only the top edge of the can was sanded
with 400 grit SiC paper by hand to expose bare metal at the closing weld interface. The lid edge
was placed on the can with diamond paste in the joint and rotated by hand in an attempt to polish
the mating surface. For clarification, a models of the canister components and the affected
regions of this polishing are included in Appendix V. The can and lid were cleaned in an
ultrasonic bath with DI water and rinsed. One YH specimen was inserted into each of five cans
and welded and sealed using the same EBW procedure used on the first weld.

2.3 Coa-ng
To assess coating impact on hydrogen retention within the FeCrAl can and YH moderator, two
cans had Al2O3 coatings applied via atomic layer deposition (ALD). The full production report
can be found in the deposition summary report [Appendix III], however the coating on both
cans was produced in a reactor vessel heated to 150°C under vacuum (10-6 Torr). Coatings were
deposited in ~0.3 nm layers from precursor gases of H2O and trimethylaluminum with total
coating thicknesses derived from the average known deposition thickness and the number of
coating layers as determined via literature evaluation. Two specific thicknesses were targeted for
the test campaign, i.e.100 nm on one can and 200 nm on another can both to be thermal cycled at
the highest temperature of 900°C. These two cans were coated together for the first ~100 nm,
with the 100 nm can removed from the reaction vessel and the second had another ~100 nm
applied for a total of ~200 nm. This interrupted coating cycle was not expected to impact the
coating thickness or integrity, due in part to the low operational temperature of the vessel, inert
nature of the coating, as well as the vacuum level and cleanliness of the reaction vessel.

3 Experimental Plan
3.1 Thermal Cycling
Thermal cycling on the respective test cans was performed in accordance with the Irradiation
Can Thermal Testing Work Plan [Appendix II], section 6. This consisted of two 500 hr cycles at

 2

900˚C for the uncoated YH can and the two ALD coated cans. In addition, two uncoated cans
experienced two 500 hr cycles and five 100 hr cycles respectively at 800°C. These thermal
cycles were conducted in vacuum tube furnaces held under a turbopump vacuum at 10-5-10-6 Torr
for the duration of the runs. After the appropriate in-process masses were measured and the
thermal cycles were completed, cans were opened using a lathe and rotary cutting tool inside an
argon atmosphere glovebox to prevent oxygen pickup or other environmental interactions with
the YH specimens while they were excised and prepped for characterization.

4 Characteriza>on
4.1 Mass Measurements
Mass measurements were taken at several points during the specimen lifecycle. Initial weights
were measured of the pure yttrium metal stock as well as post-hydriding to determine hydrogen
pickup and initial stoichiometry. Weights of the sealed sample can assemblies were collected
prior to the start of thermal cycling as well as at each designated test interval to assess in-process
hydrogen loss (designated indirect or in-can measurements). After the YH specimens were
extracted from the cans, weights of just the samples were taken again to compare stoichiometry
as determined during testing to a more accurate measurement of the specimen itself (designated
direct or out-of-can measurements). The scale used to measure these weights was accurate to
±0.0001 g and frequently checked against calibration weights. This would be equivalent to a
~0.002% error possible with the measurements or a deviation of ~0.002 in the reported H/Y
ratio.

4.2 X-ray Diﬀrac-on
X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD) was performed to determine bulk phase content of the
specimens post-test. Samples were fractured after mass measurements were taken, with a portion
of the fractured specimen further pulverized to a powder using an alumina mortar and pestle.
This powder was loaded into an air-protective domed sample carrier and inserted into a benchtop
powder diffractometer (D6 Phaser, Bruker AXS, Billerica MA). Diffraction patterns were
resolved from Cu Kα radiation (40kV, 30 mA) scanning from a 2θ of 20 to 90°. Steps were
0.005° with a dwell of 500 ms per step. Patterns were subsequently refined and background
corrected in TOPAS (Bruker AXS, Billerica MA).

4.3 Microscopy
YH specimens and their respective FeCrAl cans were cross sectioned and mounted for both
optical and scanning electron microscopy. For YH specimens the fractured portion was mounted
in epoxy and ground to 800 grit with SiC paper before moving to diamond suspension polishing.
Suspensions of 9 µm, 3 µm, and 1 µm were used for 5 minutes each at a 50 rpm co-rotation
before finishing with a colloidal silica final polish. For the cannisters, a similar polishing
procedure was used to produce the final surface. An additional electrochemical etch was
performed using oxalic acid at 6V for 1 min to expose the grain boundaries.

4.4 LECO O/N/H Analysis
Hydrogen quantification of YH was conducted by gas fusion analysis in a LECO ONH836
Analyzer. Field preparation included inspection of He and N2 gas lines, coolant levels, and
purifiers; and replacement of the disk filter. Then, the reagents and heaters are allowed to
stabilize while a system leak check is performed prior to sample analyses. Instrument parameters
were applied according to the LECO ‘Titanium Hydride’ method (203-821-458). The
background signal is established by measuring blanks in triplicate. The blanks are prepared by
adding Alphacel tin flux (Alpha Resources AR242) accelerant to a nickel capsule (LECO 502-
822) with the high temperature graphite crucibles (LECO 782-780).

After establishing a baseline, a standard (Sigma Aldrich titanium (II) hydride -325 mesh, 98%
CAS 7704-98-5) is used to create a single-point calibration curve for O and H. Standards are
prepared in triplicate by weighing 15 ± 5 mg of material into the nickel capsule with pre-tared
and co-located accelerant. Next, the nickel capsule is loaded, and the instrument is initiated for
analysis. After each analysis, the furnace and electrode are cleaned of any residual material. The
determined concentrations for the standard measurements are verified against the certified
values. A zirconium hydride (ZrH2, 99.7% CAS 7704-99-6) control check is also run before and
after the analysis of samples of interest. Samples are run in duplicate and averaged.

5.1 YH
Through this testing campaign, five YH specimens were thermally cycled to determine the
impact of time, temperature, and can coating on hydrogen retention. At the end of the test,
coatings appeared to be intact with no visible signs of degradation, however, no test was
performed to confirm integrity. The YH specimens with their production naming can be found in
Table 1 alongside their respective test conditions for this study.

The in-situ/indirect measurements using the total can mass indicate that they achieve close to a
steady state H/Y ratio after ~500 hrs. This was supported by the direct measurement made on the
sample after removal from the can indicating a ratio of ~1.90 to 1.91. However, the LECO
measurement resulted in a ratio of ~1.81. The cause of this inconsistency is not known. As it is
an outlier, we consider this point to be unreliable. All other measured values found good
agreement between LECO and direct mass measurements however these differed significantly
from the in-can or indirect mass measurements and slightly from the SWIFT modeled values.
Our interpretation is that the indirect measurements are imprecise because they convolute
potential oxidation during the thermal treatment as discussed in detail below. However, they may
indicate the correct trends if the oxidation is consistent. With this interpretation in mind, we
believe that the direct mass measurements of 1.85-1.86 (mass measurement and LECO)
accurately represent the hydrogen loss due to the thermal treatment.

The results are qualitatively similar for the 900°C specimens as seen in Figure 2. Specifically,
the indirect H/Y loss ratios are high initially but trend towards a plateau at 500 hrs (~1.86) and

 5

drop to ~1.85 at 1000 hrs total time. Both the direct measurements and LECO data show a
significantly larger hydrogen loss than the indirect measurements while the SWIFT model is
consistent with the in-can measurements.

Notably, the ALD Al2O3 coatings do not have a clear effect on H loss. Final H/Y ratios show no
distinct trend, with the indirect measurements indicating 200 nm ALD > native > 100 nm ALD
while the direct measurements and LECO data indicating native > 200 nm ALD > 100 nm ALD.
This suggests that there is unlikely a significant impact of the ALD coating on internal hydrogen
retention.

Figure 2: H/Y results using in-can, out-of-can, and LECO measurements on the 900°C specimens with
LECO results were mass corrected for the reported oxygen and nitrogen content.

There are several possibilities that explain the slowdown in mass loss for these specimens. The
first is that there is possible oxidation of the exterior of the can. While preoxidized, continued
oxidation is a known phenomena even at the reduced pO2 of vacuum. Second, the surface of the
YH specimens may scavenge oxygen from the native oxide coating present on the inside of the
can. Yttrium has a higher oxidation potential than iron, chromium, or aluminum so there is a
thermodynamic driver for the formation of yttrium oxides that could subsequently inhibit further
hydrogen loss. The last explanation (and desired outcome) is that the cans are well sealed and the
evolved hydrogen gas partial pressure builds sufficiently to achieve an equilibrium of hydride
formation and decomposition at these temperatures. While these each explain the discrepancies
 6

in H/Y ratio from the indirect total can weight measurements to the direct ones, the evidence
supports additional oxidation as described below although a combination of phenomena is also
possible.

The results from LECO O/N/H analysis, XRD, and SEM/EDS were used to explore the first
option and the potential for local oxidation. The LECO results can be found in Table 2, with the
oxygen, nitrogen, and hydrogen contents reported in ppm weight. There are two major takeaways
from the results, first and foremost, there is oxygen pickup into the specimens as the oxygen
content is higher than the initial oxygen content from the yttrium feedstock source of 2700 ppm
[Appendix I]. The oxygen pickup during specimen fabrication is typically very low (although
this was not measured for these samples) so it is likely this is sourced from the specimen cans.
Additionally, the 800°C specimens had a higher oxygen content than those tested at 900°C with
between 300-700 ppm more oxygen.

5.2 FeCrAl Cans
Visual inspection of the cans was performed prior to thermal cycling. Figure 9 presents two
cans, 8witness1 and 8witness4 which represents one of the cans (1) that received no additional
coating after they were welded shut, and another (4) that has the additional ALD coating on the
surface. Notably, the top weld for both, which was the final weld and completed after thermal
oxidation, is more lustrous than the bottom weld which would have been thermally oxidized with
the rest of the can. Additionally, there does not appear to be a noticeable visual difference with
the 200 nm ALD coating that is present, which is not unsurprising given that Al2O3 would likely
be optically transparent at such a thin coating depth. From the outside, both welds on top and
bottom look quite regular with no obvious defects, gaps, or features that would stop the thermal
cycling test from being performed.

Figure 9: Top and bottom welds for 8witness1 (left) and 8witness4 (right)

Once the thermal cycling was completed, cans were cut open in an argon glovebox to excise the
YH specimen from inside. The cut cans can be seen in Figure 10, with the portions of the can
upside down on the top and bottom sides of the image so that the welds for the cans can be seen
next to each other. The 800°C cans still have a shine to the top weld while the 900°C cans show a
more matte finish in the post-test condition on the upper weld. These cans were thermal cycled in
a vacuum environment, however exposing the cans to air for weighing and any minor leaks in
the vacuum system would provide additional exposure to oxygen. This optical difference could
be due to roughening of the smooth weld surface from thermal etching, minor direct oxidation or
even oxygen migration from the native oxide to the surface of the weld. Higher magnification
microscopy was subsequently performed on cut cross-sections of the can to not only understand
the surface coating better but to also inspect the integrity and quality of the sealing welds.

Starting with the polished cross-sections of the cans under optical microscopy, there are some
features worth noting immediately. Figure 11 shows the weld cross-sections for the top and
bottom welds, un-etched in this figure. Weld fusion is only expected through the edge of the tube
wall into the top of the lid can, with no weld metal penetrating into the step of the lid. In some of
the welds such as tops of 8witness4 and bottom of 8witness1’s cans, there does not seem to be
continuous fusion across the top lip of the lid and can wall. The etched images of these can cross
sections can be seen in Figure 12, which provides more context on the lack of fusion in some of
these areas. Weld pools on average look to penetrate 200-300 μm in many of the welds, with
some penetrating deeper with full engagement of the top and tube wall. Another observation is
that in the case of many of the welds, the weld pool does not seem to be centered on the lid/tube
interface, with them frequently having more tube engagement than lid, with the center of the
weld pool offset away from the desired weld interface. This leads to a shallower engagement
than anticipated at the edge of the weld, contributing in part to the lack of fusion across the entire
interface.

Interestingly enough, the etched micrographs in Figure 12 show evidence of what looks to be
debris in the tube wall of the can. Scanning electron micrographs were taken of 8witness1, 3, and
4 which are shown in Figure 13. The debris within the tube wall seems to only be present in the
top welds, which were those welded after the native oxide coating was thermally prepared. It is
suspected that this debris was pickup into the FeCrAl as a portion of the native oxide that was
distributed in and around the weld. SEM/EDS analysis indicates that the debris is primarily
silicon bearing, though no other major elements were found in or around debris in the elemental
mapping which suggests it could be sanding debris from the SiC paper that was used to abrade
the coating off the weld area. Figure 12 shows a distinct grain size difference between the lid
and the tube wall, indicating different fabrication methods for the feedstock used in the different
can components.

6 Summary
 Isothermal testing of yttrium hydride fully encapsulated in FeCrAl cans was performed. The
 FeCrAl cans were all preoxidized to create an aluminum oxide hydrogen permeation barrier
 prior to testing; however, final welding of the cans was expected to disrupt the oxide barrier
 resulting in a fast diffusion path for hydrogen. To attempt to mitigate this, two of the cans
 were coated with ALD aluminum oxide to provide a more continuous coating. Tests were
 performed at 800˚C and 900˚C for up to 1000 hours. Hydrogen loss occurred at both
 temperatures with H/Y dropping from an initial 1.94 to ~1.85 at 800˚C and ~1.75 at 900˚C.
 The presence of the ALD coating did not appear to impact the hydrogen loss. Weld quality
 varied between the cans and less than full penetration was observed. It is not clear how
 complete the weld was throughout the circumference of the can. SWIFT modeling3 (not
 discussed in this report) was performed to provide an estimate of expected behavior relative
 to the experiment. This model predicts that H loss is completely governed by the thickness of
 the alumina layer while the FeCrAl has almost NO impact on H loss. For example, at 800˚C
 after 1000 hours, the expected H loss for a FeCrAl can with a 0.5mm wall thickness (no
 native oxide) is H/Y <1.4. However, an alumina thickness of only 1 µm increases this to
 1.88. At 900˚C, the anticipated H/Y is 1.66 after 1000 hours. Doubling the alumina thickness
 to 2 microns increases the anticipated H/Y to 1.77 after 1000 hours. This compares well with
 the experimental value of ~1.75 regardless of continuity of alumina coating or weld quality.

 Al2O3 Atomic Layer Deposition onto FeCrAl Welded Cannisters
 Timothy J Gorey

  Executive Summary: A 100 nm Al2O3 ALD coating was requested for a welded FeCrAl
  sample named “8WITNESS3”, and a 200 nm Al2O3 ALD coating was requested for a
  second, identical FeCrAl welded cannister names “8WITNESS4”. Both cannisters were
  loaded into the LANL R&D scale reactor, which was then evacuated and leak checked to 10-6
  Torr. The reactor was wrapped in resistive heating tape and insulated with fiberglass ribbon.
  Overnight, the reactor was brought to uniform temperature of 150 oC as measured by
  internal thermocouple. Coating thickness was approximated by cycle number, with 0.3 nm
  assumed per cycle. 333 deposition cycles were dosed onto both cannisters; a dose consists of
  trimethylaluminum and water vapor dosed using equilibrium vapor pressure. (no carrier
  gas). Nitrogen was used as the purge gas in between chemical doses. Post deposition,
  heating elements were de-energized, and the reactor was briefly vented to remove sample
  “8WITNESS3”. The reactor was then re-evacuated and brought back to 150 oC overnight.
  The following day, an additional 333 cycles were dosed as described above, such that
  “8WITNESS4” received a total of 666 cycles. ~200 nm.
