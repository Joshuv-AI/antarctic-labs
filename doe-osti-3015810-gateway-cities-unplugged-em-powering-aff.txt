Executive Summary
The Gateway Cities Unplugged: (em)Powering Affordable Housing (the “Project”) was
launched to address a critical challenge in the U.S. building and energy sectors: enabling
multifamily buildings especially those serving low and moderate‑income (LMI)
communities to actively support grid modernization through Grid‑Interactive Efficient
Buildings (GEB). Multifamily housing represents a large share of national building energy
consumption, yet the sector faces persistent barriers to adopting advanced load flexibility
technologies, including capital constraints, aging infrastructure, and limited access to
demand‑side management tools. This Project sought to overcome those barriers by
defining, evaluating, and designing commercially viable GEB technology packages for six
representative multifamily properties across Massachusetts, Connecticut, and New York.
The Project significantly advanced understanding of how energy efficiency, distributed
energy resources (DERs), and load flexibility measures can be integrated and optimized
within multifamily portfolios. Through detailed building audits, technology vendor
evaluations, and the development of portfolio wide system architecture, the team
documented how supervisory controls, smart thermostats, battery energy storage
systems (BESS), and solar PV interact within real world operating conditions. This
analysis also produced the GEB Calculator, a portfolio screening tool that quantifies
costs, savings, and potential revenue across many utility programs. This effort provides
new insight into technical feasibility, financial effects, and practical steps for replicable
adoption of GEB technologies in multifamily housing.
Technical findings from Budget Period 1 demonstrated that GEB solutions are both
effective and economically promising. Across the six buildings, the Project identified 1,516
to 1,639 kW of load flexibility which is equivalent to 78% of peak summer demand,
substantially exceeding the Projects Go/No‑Go threshold of 500 kW (30‑minute) and 175
kW (four‑hour) load flexibility. Most buildings can feasibly support solar PV and BESS
installations, with BESS providing two‑thirds of total load flexibility and cooling‑based
measures contributing another quarter. Financial analysis revealed stackable revenue
streams of $210–$470/kW, reinforcing the economic case for integrating BESS,
thermostatic control, and point‑load management where technical conditions allow.
Though some challenges like high BESS installed costs, limited thermostat vendors
suitable for multifamily, and switchgear constraints remain, the results confirm that a
GEB‑enabled multifamily portfolio can deliver meaningful grid services while generating
owner revenue and avoiding peak demand charges.

Beyond technical and financial contributions, the project provides substantial benefits to
the public. The work supports national goals for decarbonization, grid reliability, and
energy equity by demonstrating practical strategies that reduce peak demand, improve
building performance, and support renewable energy integration. By defining scalable
GEB packages, creating a portfolio valuation tool, and improving understanding of
resident engagement needs, the project positions multifamily housing providers to adopt
advanced energy technologies that lower operating costs and improve comfort for
residents.

A. Background
Electricity consumption in multifamily buildings has been rising and now represents a
substantial and growing share of total system load in dense urban areas, particularly as
electrification of appliances, heating, and cooling accelerates and overall power use in
the U.S. continues to reach new highs. National electricity demand is projected to set
successive record highs through 2026 and 2027, with residential electricity sales climbing
in line with broader electrification trends in homes and buildings (1). While single-family
homes typically register higher per-household totals, multifamily buildings collectively
account for a significant portion of residential energy use, and electrification in these units
including space conditioning, appliances, and electric water heating is increasing overall
building loads. In Massachusetts and similar regions, the shift toward more electricity-
intensive end uses and higher occupancy density means that the aggregate energy drawn
by multifamily buildings contributes meaningfully to distribution feeder peaks and capacity
pressures even if per-unit averages vary by household type and tenure. This evolving
consumption landscape underscores the importance of demand flexibility in buildings as
a critical alternative for managing peak demand, accommodating distributed resources
such as rooftop solar and energy storage, and mitigating constraints on overloaded urban
distribution networks.

Prior research establishes both the magnitude and characteristics of residential load
shapes relevant to demand response and flexibility programs. Urban et al. (2016) and
Navigant Consulting (2018) document baseline residential load profiles and end use
contributions, providing empirical foundations for estimating peak coincidence and
available flexibility (2). German and Hoeschele (2014) further demonstrate that mechanical
precooling can shift cooling loads away from system peaks while maintaining occupant
comfort, forming a basis for thermostat-based flexibility strategies in residential buildings
(3). These studies collectively inform the assumptions that a meaningful portion of

residential load particularly cooling, water heating, and selected plug loads can be
modulated or shifted during critical periods without adverse impacts on tenants when
events are properly designed and communicated.

Distribution system constraints further shape the role and value of distributed energy
resources in multifamily settings. In many dense urban service territories, feeder and
substation capacity limitations constrain both incremental load growth and export from
onsite generation, limiting the ability to interconnect new electrified loads or solar
photovoltaics without triggering costly infrastructure upgrades. National Grid’s Active
Resource Integration framework highlights the role of coordinated photovoltaic and
energy storage controls in mitigating these constraints by eliminating net export,
managing coincident peak demand, and effectively increasing host capacity on
constrained distribution networks (4). Within this framework, energy storage systems
function not only as demand management assets, but also as grid-enabling infrastructure
that supports higher penetrations of onsite solar and electrification while deferring or
avoiding traditional distribution system investments.

 Page 5 of 23

Quantifying and scheduling flexibility requires appropriate metrics and forecasting
approaches. Liu et al. (2020) develop and evaluate demand flexibility metrics that
compare simulated and field measured building performance, demonstrating the
importance of grounding flexibility estimates in observable operational behavior (5). This
work is directly aligned with this project in its emphasis on measurable, event-based
demand response outcomes and realistic operational constraints. Complementary work
by Kromer and Roth (2020) evaluates time series load prediction methodologies for
optimal energy storage dispatch in solar dominant distribution systems, demonstrating
that accurate short-term load forecasting significantly improves storage dispatch
effectiveness and enables greater value stacking across peak reduction, energy shifting,
and renewable integration objectives (6). Together, these studies inform the project’s
approach to DER coordination in urban distribution networks by emphasizing that credible
flexibility value depends on both accurate measurement and realistic prediction of building
load behavior.

Against this technical backdrop, broader regional system conditions further heighten the
need for deployable demand flexibility resources. In the ISO New England region,
planned and announced retirements of conventional generating capacity are expected to
tighten capacity margins and increase dependence on demand-side and distributed
resources to maintain system reliability during peak conditions (7). These changes amplify
the value of resources that can be mobilized quickly, operate locally within constrained
distribution networks, and reduce coincident peak demand without requiring major
infrastructure investment. Multifamily buildings, which concentrate load in urban areas
already facing distribution constraints, are therefore well positioned to contribute
meaningfully to regional reliability and capacity needs when equipped with coordinated
HVAC controls, energy storage, and onsite generation.

Within this evolving system context, the project addressed a critical gap between
emerging demand flexibility research and practical implementation in affordable
multifamily housing. This project, which concluded after Budget Period 1, focused on
design level analysis and planning informed by recent literatures, studies and Project
team’s experience with residential load behavior, flexibility metrics, forecasting methods,
and DER coordination strategies. Completed work included development of load and
flexibility assumptions, preliminary control concepts for HVAC and energy storage
systems, and evaluation of distribution level constraints and operational strategies. While
field deployment was not executed, the design work establishes a technically grounded
foundation for future implementation of demand flexibility and distributed energy
resources in affordable multifamily housing.

B. Project Objectives
Key project goals focused on empowering affordable housing communities by defining,
evaluating, installing, and optimizing real world Grid Interactive Efficient Building (GEB)
packages tailored to existing multifamily properties. The team identified optimal
technology combinations across a portfolio and evaluated them against cost
effectiveness, grid challenges addressed, technical feasibility, and resident satisfaction.
In practice, this work converts multifamily portfolios into actively managed demand assets
that shift consumption from peak to off peak hours, relieve stress on local distribution
systems, and improve reliability and resilience during disturbances and emergencies. At
scale, a larger pool of grid interactive buildings provides diversified flexibility that stabilizes
system operations, creates local jobs in installation, commissioning, and analytics, and
expands access to energy savings for housing that has been historically underserved,
delivering durable economic benefits alongside cleaner and more reliable power. The
project is ambitious and transformative, advancing resilience as a service, resident
engagement, data acquisition and telemetry, software and controls development,
revenue sharing structures, and portfolio level optimization to enable repeatable
deployment across multiple properties.

Specific Project objectives defined in the Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO) include:

1. Coordinate interaction of GEB measures from various manufacturers and data
 platforms to co-optimize value generated across multiple use cases.
2. Analyze and document interaction between energy efficiency and demand flexibility
 measures both within a building and across a group of buildings, from the standpoint
 of energy performance as well as financing, first cost, operating costs, and total
 revenue generated.
3. Develop and implement utility data acquisition strategies along with a light-touch
 screening tool to help multifamily portfolio owners develop GEB scopes.
4. Develop and pilot Resiliency as a Service model for LMI multifamily affordable
 housing.
5. Transform the interaction between residents, building management staff, and the grid
 responsive buildings through real-time communication, engagement, and DER
 education.
6. Support future financeability of multiple GEB Solutions Packages, providing a cost-
 benefit analysis for GEB measures to help inform property owners, lenders, investors,
 regulators, and the design of incentives to drive commercial opportunities and market
 transformation.

The Project established a strong foundation and generated actionable insights that show
how GEBs can transform multifamily housing into active partners in grid modernization.

  C. Project Results
  1. Coordinate interaction of GEB measures from various manufacturers and data
 platforms to co-optimize value generated across multiple use cases.

   The Project Team conducted a review of WinnCompanies’ portfolio of multifamily
 properties to identify representative sites for inclusion in the Connected Communities
 project (Appendix A.2). Based on this review, we identified a portfolio of six buildings
 to use as case studies for evaluating and designing GEB-enabling technologies and
 technology packages. We then conducted detailed site assessments of the six
 buildings to document existing conditions, understand how these buildings operate,
 and understand how energy is used in these buildings. In parallel, we conducted a
 broad-based review of currently available GEB-enabling technologies to consider
 implementing in these buildings. Using preliminary findings from the site audits and
 the technology review, we prioritized GEB-enabling technologies according to their
 suitability for adoption in multi-family housing, based on factors such as cost
 effectiveness, impact, technical feasibility, and acceptance within the connected
 community. (Appendix A.1)
   The Project Team evaluated a variety of GEB-enabling technology providers and
 manufacturers to understand product functionality, limitations, and cost. Based on this
 research, the team selected vendors to provide supervisory controllers, thermostat,
 EV charging stations, and solar design and engineering services.
   With all project partners engaged, the Project Team prepared an integrated system
 architecture, detailed in Figure 1: GEB System Architecture, that optimizes the
 interaction between GEB measures to maximize value generation across use cases.

4. Develop and pilot Resiliency as a Service model for LMI multifamily affordable
 housing.
  The Project Team evaluated the resiliency potential of Battery Energy Storage
 Systems (BESS) at the six selected buildings. While BESS can clearly provide grid
 benefits, energy savings, and revenue, BESS can also provide back-up power
 during a grid outage. When connected to solar PV, BESS could theoretically be
 recharged during an outage for long-term resiliency. However, there are limitations

                                                    Page 13 of 23

 to the resiliency applications and benefits, and it proved to be challenging to prove
 Resiliency as a Service (RaaS) model, especially in low-income apartment
 communities with existing diesel or gas generators capable of powering significant
 emergency loads during long-term outages. Our approach considered integrating
 BESS as a secondary non-critical back-up source to existing emergency
 generators, providing enhanced resiliency beyond typical emergency loads like
 lighting and elevators.
  BESS design is complicated and making it operable during a grid outage adds
 complexity. The Project aimed to identify and quantify the benefits to justify the
 added cost and complexity. Total BESS capacity for the buildings is limited by
 existing switchgear, which is often 40+ years old with limited spare capacity to
 accommodate new electrical loads. Battery sizing limitations reduce the potential
 emergency loads that could be supported by BESS. Further, implementing site-
 wide resilience for multi-family properties is expensive, requiring more complex
 utility reviews and interconnection points, with no model to recoup the investment;
 moreover, the sites we investigated have zero or very few outages per year,
 resulting in a limited owner appetite to pay for resiliency as a service.
  To make BESS deployment economically viable, the systems should be designed
 to allow participation in demand response programs in addition to providing
 resiliency to maximize the savings and revenue potential. This dual-purpose
 approach enables BESS to not only enhance resiliency but also generate financial
 benefits, improving the overall value proposition for property owners. This Project
 seeks to compare BESS design with and without back-up power functionality to
 put a price to resiliency, which is an effort still underway.

 C.1. Significant findings, conclusions, or developments
The Project Team has:
  Evaluated GEB implementation at six target properties located in MA, CT, and NY.
  Defined GEB packages and implementation strategies optimized for deployment
 in multi-family buildings consisting of solar PV, BESS, cooling/heating load shift,

                                                Page 15 of 23

 DHW load shift, and point-load shifting.
  Supervisory control: no existing commercial offerings that are optimized for the
 multi-family sector. Identified a vendor (Elexity) that has worked in buildings
 adjacent to this space and worked with them to understand the potential of adding
 feature sets (thermostat API integration, new battery vendor, optimize for MA value
 streams)
  Identified multiple stackable value streams in CT and MA, ranging from $210 to
 $470 per kW load flexibility. Due to first costs, combining on-bill savings with
 demand response revenue is critical for undewriting GEB projects.
  Completed design studies of BESS and solar PV at all six sites. We found that
 BESS and solar PV are technically feasible at all six sites. (One site already has
 existing PV.)
  Approximately 1,516-1,639 kW of flexible load is technically feasible at the portfolio
 properties from a combination of demand-side measures and BESS. This
 represents approximately 78% of the summer peak load. BESS accounts for
 approximately 2/3rds of this total, with cooling-based load shift accounting for 25%
 and assorted point loads and electric DHW accounting for the remaining 10-15%.
  Challenges associated with thermostat-based load shifting including: (1) load
 increases due to pre-cool and rebound can potentially shift, rather than reduce, a
 building’s demand peak, thereby eroding the value of demand reduction for
 demand charge management; and, (2) there are limited vendors that carry
 products suitable for multi-family GEB deployment, and all require expensive
 ongoing fees, in addition to high up-front cost compared to typical thermostats,
 resulting in a break-even point for thermostat deployment on the order of $30 per
 unit per year. This level of value generation is feasible, particularly in (a) electrified
 buildings, with (b) high demand charge, and where (c) BESS is challenging
 because of switchgear limitations.
  Installed costs for BESS are challenging, e.g., installed costs per kWh are higher
 than we had expected for commercial applications, which presents a challenge:
 we speculate that products are not optimized for multi-family properties. There
 are two specific issues: first, most battery packs are designed for 2-hour discharge
 (footnote – 4-hour packs are available but generally implemented as a down-rated
 inverter paired with a 2-hour discharge pack) which is higher than needed for the
 value streams we have targeted, and more expensive on a per-kWh basis.
 Second: 125kW rated packs tend to be the smallest light-commercial batteries that
 are currently widely available – i.e., there is a gap in product availability between
 ~10kW and 125kW packs. At 5 of 6 properties, this pack size is higher than optimal.

6. Mission Main
Space heating and cooling loads are served by in-unit water source heat pumps (WSHPs)
on a water loop cooled by a rooftop cooling tower and heated by gas-fired hot water
boilers. Hot water demand is served by two gas-fired water heaters. The WSHPs in each
apartment are controlled by Embue thermostats. Note that the building is served by two
different electrical services: the bulk of the load is on the tenant meter (approximately
110kW out of 150 kW peak load), and the balance is on a common area meter. The
flexible cooling load is divided between the two bills: the cooling tower and common area
WSHPs are served by the common meter, while the in-unit WSHPs are served by the
aggregated tenant meter.
