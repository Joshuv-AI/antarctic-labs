Methods of incisor inclination control during the non-accelerated retraction of upper anterior teeth with fixed orthodontic appliances: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Majd Abdullah Kalloumeh, Mohammad Y Hajeer, Ahmad S Burhan, Mohammad Khursheed Alam, Huda Abutayyem, Samer T Jaber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProtrusion of the maxillary incisors represents a frequent clinical challenge in orthodontic treatment. Current systematic reviews are limited in range and methods employed, while individual trials lack consensus on the most effective mechanics for retraction.ObjectiveThis systematic review aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of various approaches used to control anterior tooth inclination during non‑accelerated retraction with buccal fixed appliances.MethodsA search of databases was conducted in September (2025) to identify randomized controlled trials using various methods and techniques to retract the upper anterior teeth. Eligible RCTs enrolling patients aged 12–35 years who underwent extraction of upper first premolars were included. Meta‑analyses used random‑effects models (RevMan 5.3). Certainty of evidence was appraised with GRADE.ResultsTwenty RCTs with 568 patients in total were included. Risk‑of‑bias was low in 11 trials, some concerns in 4, and high in 5. Pooled analyses showed that retraction produced clinically and statistically significant palatal crown inclination of the upper incisors across techniques: sliding en‑masse (MD ≈ − 9.8°; moderate certainty), loop‑based en‑masse (MD ≈ − 13.3°; moderate certainty), and two‑step retraction (MD ≈ − 8.9°; moderate certainty). Loop‑based mechanics were associated with slightly greater retroclination than sliding (MD = 2.49°; statistically significant but not clinically meaningful; moderate certainty). Comparisons of en‑masse versus two‑step retraction showed no significant difference (very low certainty). Vertical incisal changes were inconsistently reported and supported by very low‑certainty evidence. Heterogeneity varied from low to considerable across analyses.ConclusionAnterior teeth retraction is generally associated with clinically meaningful incisor retroclination. Loop-based mechanics appear to produce slightly greater retroclination than sliding, but the clinical relevance is limited. Evidence quality ranges from moderate to very low, and heterogeneity could not be avoided; high‑quality RCTs with standardized inclination measurements are needed to define optimal inclination‑control strategies.Trial registrationThe review protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251167359) during the early stages of the review process.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09162-0.Keywords: En-masse retraction, Incisor retraction, Class II division 1 malocclusion, Retraction of upper anterior teeth, Upper incisor inclination

Introduction

Maxillary incisor protrusion is one of the most common malocclusions in permanent dentition that patients request orthodontic treatment, demanding the retraction of their protruded incisors, this includes Class II division 1 and bimaxillary dentoalveolar protrusion cases [1]. These cases can be treated in several methods, such as growth modification with fixed functional appliances [2], teeth extraction (usually the first premolars) [3], and maxillary arch distalization [4], as well as orthognathic surgery in severe cases [5]. Recurrently, the treatment plan for these cases includes the extraction of the first upper premolars as an approach to creating space needed for retraction, followed by the retraction of the anterior teeth, with medium to maximum anchorage [6].

Several methods have been developed to close extraction spaces. The two‑step retraction technique is often selected to preserve anchorage, although it typically requires a longer treatment duration [7]. En‑masse retraction is preferred by many clinicians because it is straightforward and relatively fast; however, it places greater demands on posterior anchorage [3].

These two methods of retraction can be used in two different mechanics. While the sliding technique is widely used because it is simple and is applied with minimum chair time, the loop-based technique allows for inclination control of anterior teeth during space closure [8].

During retraction, it is critical to control protruded incisors to obtain a good treatment result from the functional and esthetic points of view [9]. One of the most important factors of the retraction control efficiency is the incisor inclination control [10]. To this purpose, several methods have been utilized such as increasing built-in incisors brackets torque, adding torque to the retraction wire, using a long retraction hook and employing additional intra or extraoral retraction inclination control devices [11].

Given the importance of evaluating different retraction methods and their ability to control the inclination of anterior teeth, a previous systematic review was published by Kuc et al. [12], which evaluated the effectiveness of the methods used to control torque during anterior teeth retraction. However, this review had some shortcomings, as it only included studies published between 2011 and 2021, and excluded clinical trials that employed loop-based techniques for anterior teeth retraction. In addition, both randomized and non-randomized clinical trials were included as well as one retrospective study with the expected high risk of bias. This heterogeneity of included studies could have affected the estimated outcomes. Therefore, the conclusions cannot have a high level of certainty.

Another systematic review carried out by Kheshfeh et al. [8] assessed the effectiveness of the sliding technique compared with the loop-based technique in the retraction of the upper anterior teeth, but the focused review question led to only include studies that compared the two techniques directly, which means the exclusion of many studies concerning incisor inclination change during and the additional methods or auxiliaries used to control incisor torque during retraction. In addition, this review assessed torque control as a secondary outcome.

Khlef et al. [6] evaluated change in incisor inclination between en-masse retraction with temporary anchorage devices (TADs) and two-step retraction with conventional anchorage. But the reliance on TADs as an inclusion criterion for that SR limited the number of included trials to 2 RCTS and 2 CCTs only. Another systematic review by the same team (i.e. Khlef et al. [3]) assessed en-masse retraction using temporary anchorage devices in comparison with conventional anchorage, but data synthesis seemed unclear with an absence of forest plots, heterogeneity assessment, and quality of evidence for this outcome.

Accordingly, as no sufficient evidence concerning the most effective method to control incisor inclination during retraction in the existing systematic reviews, the need for a new systematic review has emerged. Thus, the current systematic review aimed to evaluate the different methods used to control anterior teeth inclination during their retraction in the sliding technique as well as the loop-based technique, whether en-masse or two-step retraction was utilized. The focused review question was: “What are the effects of non-accelerated retraction methods of upper anterior teeth on their inclination? And which method provides the most effective control on upper incisor inclination when using buccal fixed orthodontic appliances?”

Materials and methods

Registration and protocol

This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered prospectively in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Registration No. [CRD420251167359]). The review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. All methodological steps, including search strategy, study selection, data extraction, risk of bias assessment, and synthesis, followed the PRISMA framework to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Scoping search

Before initializing this systematic review, a PubMed scoping search was performed to ensure there were no similar systematic reviews and to check for potentially eligible trials. The result indicated the presence of four systematic reviews that discussed the same topic but had some shortcomings [3, 6, 8, 12], and the presence of eight potentially eligible trials [13–20].

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were established on the base of the PICOS framework.

Population

Patients with class II division 1 malocclusion or class I bimaxillary dentoalveolar protrusion who had a complete permanent dentition and between 12 and 35 years that required the extraction of the upper first premolars and the retraction of the anterior teeth, either in one step (i.e., en-masse retraction) or in two steps (i.e., canine retraction followed by incisor retraction).

Intervention

Any sliding or non-sliding method to retract the six anterior teeth or the canines followed by the retraction of the incisors using the buccal orthodontic treatment with/without an additional means to support inclination control, with/without any anchorage device. If a trial compared accelerated and non-accelerated methods, only non-accelerated groups were included.

Comparison

Any other method to retract the six anterior teeth or the canines followed by the retraction of the incisors using the buccal orthodontic treatment with/without an additional means to support inclination control, with/without any anchorage device, that is different from the intervention group or no intervention at all. If a trial compared accelerated and non-accelerated methods, only non-accelerated groups were included.

Outcomes

The primary outcome: the change in incisor inclination during retraction. The secondary outcome: the vertical change in the incisal edge of the incisors.

Study design

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with no time-period or language restrictions.

Exclusion criteria

Controlled clinical trials (CCTs), case reports, case series reports, finite element analysis studies, personal opinions, reviews, technique description articles, and animal studies were excluded, in addition to studies included less than 10 patients in each group. This threshold was selected because groups smaller than 10 are highly vulnerable to random variation and baseline imbalances, which compromise the stability of effect estimates. Randomized controlled trials with acceleration method were also excluded.

Information sources, search strategy, and study selection

Supplementary Table 1 provides the keywords used for the electronic search, while Supplementary Table 2 outlines the search strategy details. In September 2025, two reviewers (MAK and MYH) conducted the initial search across PubMed®, Scopus®, Web of Science™, and the Cochrane Library, without any time restrictions. Additionally, a manual review of the reference lists from all included articles was performed to identify further relevant studies. Google™ Scholar was searched to identify relevant grey literature as well as published literature; the first 200 results were screened to capture potentially relevant indexed and non‑indexed studies.

The literature search was conducted manually by two reviewers (MAK and MYH) across the selected databases. References were imported into EndNote 21 software (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, Pa) for management, and duplicate records were identified and removed automatically using the software’s duplicate detection function, followed by manual verification.

The article selection process was carried out in two stages. In the first stage, two reviewers (MAK and MYH) independently screened the titles and abstracts of articles identified through electronic searches. In the second stage, the two reviewers assessed the full texts of potentially relevant articles. Articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria were then excluded. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers, with input from a third author (ASB) if necessary.

Data collection process

Two reviewers (MAK and MYH) extracted data from the selected studies and arranged them into tables. The collected information included general details (such as author name and publication year), study design, patient count, average age, type of malocclusion, type of intervention and outcomes. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion, and if needed, a third author (ASB) was consulted.

Risk of bias evaluation in individual trials

The two reviewers (MAK and MYH) independently evaluated the included studies for risk of bias using the RoB-2 tool [21]. Their evaluations were then compared, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with input from a third reviewer (ASB) if needed. The risk of bias was classified as low, high, or some concerns across the following domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results.

The overall risk of bias for each study was determined based on these criteria: studies were rated as having a “low risk of bias” when all domains were classified as low risk, “some concerns” when at least one domain raised issues but none were considered high risk, and “high risk of bias” if at least one domain was rated high risk or if multiple concerns significantly affected confidence in the results.

Quantitative synthesis of the collected findings

A meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.3 (The Nordic Cochrane Centre for the Cochrane Collaboration in Copenhagen, Denmark). Heterogeneity was initially assessed through visual inspection, followed by statistical analysis. Two reviewers (MAK and MYH) examined the graphical representation of treatment effects, along with 95% confidence intervals. The P-value for heterogeneity was calculated, with significance considered at P < 0.05. The I² statistic was used to quantify heterogeneity among studies, interpreted as follows: low (0–25%), moderate (26–50%), substantial (51–75%), and considerable heterogeneity (76–100%). Data were included in the meta-analysis when the studies involved similar interventions, participant characteristics, and outcomes. For continuous data, mean differences (MD) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported, while risk ratios (RR) were used for dichotomous outcomes. Treatment effects were weighted using the weighted mean difference (WMD), based on a random-effects model to account for variability across study settings and populations. The inverse-variance method was applied for continuous outcomes, and the Mantel–Haenszel method was used for dichotomous outcomes. Means and standard deviations were directly obtained from the studies. Missing raw data were first sought by contacting the corresponding authors of the included studies. When no response was obtained, missing values were estimated when feasible; otherwise, the study was excluded from the meta‑analysis. Supplementary Table 3 details the procedures used to derive missing values for each individual study. Forest plots were used to visually display the results of the analysis. If ten or more studies were available for quantitative synthesis, publication bias was to be assessed using funnel plots. To evaluate how each individual trial affected the pooled effect size and heterogeneity, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted. Each study was sequentially excluded, and the meta-analysis was re-run to observe changes in the pooled estimate and heterogeneity statistics. When the leave-on-out analysis failed to reduce heterogeneity, a subgroup comprising methodologically robust groups was constructed. The discussion focused primarily on the results derived from sensitivity analyses, as they provided more stable and reliable estimates. The certainty of the evidence was evaluated using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) system [22], categorizing evidence as high, moderate, low, or very low certainty. GRADEpro GDT software was used to assess the evidence and generate the ‘Summary of Findings’ table.

Results

Study selection and literature review

A total of 1,044 articles were identified through electronic and manual searches. After removing duplicates, the number was reduced to 532. Following a review of titles and abstracts, 27 articles were considered potentially relevant, while those that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. Upon full-text evaluation of these 27 studies, 7 were further excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria, with the reasons detailed in Supplementary Table 4. Ultimately, 20 studies were included in data synthesis, of which 19 were used in at least one meta-analysis, while one trial was excluded from meta-analyses due to lack of raw data [23]. The number of studies consisting of comparable groups in which data was extracted and analyzed for all the groups was 12 [13–20, 23, 29, 32, 34]. While 8 studies contained two groups each; an accelerated group, and a non-accelerated group [24–28, 30, 31, 33]. The data was collected and analyzed for the non-accelerated groups only while the accelerated groups were excluded.

Based on the retraction method in dependence on the number of teeth being retracted, the 20 studies can be classified into three groups. A comparison between en-masse retraction of upper anterior teeth and two-step retraction, and this group can be divided into four subgroups according to the method of anchorage. In one subgroup, two studies evaluated direct temporary anchorage devices (TADs) based en-masse retraction versus traditional anchorage two-step retraction [13, 20]. The second subgroup included one study which compared direct anchorage with TADs during en-masse retraction with two-step retraction by adding an intrusion arch to canine retraction, followed by incisor retraction using a continuous mushroom loop archwire [29]. The last two subgroups contained one study each which evaluated en-masse retraction and two-step retraction, the third subgroup was without using any auxiliary anchorage device in either group [18], while the fourth one utilized various anchorage methods based on the requirements of each case in both groups [34].

The second group of studies comprised trials in which en-masse retraction was investigated in all of the comparable trial arms, wherein the same retraction technique was applied, but varying forms of anchorage were employed. These studies lie within six distinct subgroups. The first subgroup consisted of studies that compared an acceleration method against a non-accelerated control group using direct TADs anchorage, four studies were during en-masse retraction [24, 28, 30, 33], and one was during sectional en-masse retraction [26]. The second subgroup contained three studies which compared the sliding technique with the loop-based technique during en-masse retraction [14, 16, 17]. One study compared the use of direct TADs against headgear anchorage [32], and one compared infrazygomatic anchorage against interradicular one [19], which composed the third and fourth subgroups respectively. The three-arm study that assessed direct TADs anchorage in two groups and indirect TADs anchorage in one group during en-masse retraction formed the fifth subgroup [23]. The last subgroup consisted only of a single study that evaluated the use of rapid distraction of the periodontal ligament (the Hycon device) on the rate of en-masse retraction using conventional anchorage in comparison with a control group [25].

The third group of studies included those trials in which the treatment provided in all compared groups were based on two-step retraction. One study evaluated sliding against loop-based techniques during two-step retraction [15]. Two trials evaluated the accelerated two-step retraction against the traditional non-accelerated two-step technique. one of them used direct TADs anchorage [31], and one used conventional anchorage [27].

According to the presence or absence of friction during the retraction of upper anterior teeth, there were different designs employed in the collected studies. Four studies compared the sliding technique with the loop-based technique. Three of them were during en-masse retraction [14, 16, 17], and one was during two-step retraction [15]. 16 studies used the sliding technique during retraction, eight of them were during en-masse retraction [19, 23–25, 28, 30, 32, 33], and two were during two-step retraction [27, 31]. Four studies compared en-masse retraction with two-step retraction using the sliding technique [13, 18, 20, 34]. While one trial assessed en-masse retraction using the loop-based technique [26]. Finally, one study compared en-masse retraction using the sliding technique with two-step retraction using the loop-based technique [29].

Depending on the force delivery system, several means were utilized. Closed coil spring was used in 9 trials [18, 24, 26–28, 30–33], while elastic chains were used in three studies [13, 19, 23]. Three studies utilized a T-loop fabricated using titanium molybdenum alloy (TMA) wire in one group. While in the other group, elastic chains were used in two studies [14, 15], and closed coil springs were used in one [16]. One study compared elastic chains with mushroom loops fabricated using Connecticut new archwire (CNA) [17]. One study used closed coil springs for en-masse retraction in one group, while closed coil springs were used to retract canines in the other group and incisors were retracted with mushroom loops fabricated using CNA archwire [29]. One study compared the use of Hycon device with closed coil springs [25]. The force delivery system was not mentioned in two studies [20, 34].

Risk of bias of the included trials

A total of 20 randomized controlled trials were included in this systematic review [13–20, 23–34]. Of these, 11 were assessed as having a low risk of bias [13, 15–17, 20, 23–25, 27, 28, 30], four were deemed to present some concerns [14, 26, 33, 34], and five were classified as having a high risk of bias mainly due to the lack of concealment and insufficient blinding during measurement assessment [18, 19, 29, 31, 32]. Changes in incisor inclination following retraction

The change in the inclination of the upper central incisor was the main outcome investigated in all the 20 trials included. Regarding the sliding en-masse retraction of the upper anterior teeth, 15 groups from 13 trials investigated incisor inclination change pre- and post-intervention [13, 14, 16, 17, 19, 20, 24, 25, 28–30, 32, 33]. The pooled estimate revealed a statistically significant decrease in the inclination of the upper incisors post-intervention in comparison with pre-intervention, with considerable heterogeneity (MD=-8.78; 95% CI: -11.04,-6.51; P < 0.00001; Tau2=15.63; X2=172.01; P < 0.00001; I2 = 92%; Fig. 4A). According to the GRADE approach, the strength of evidence regarding this outcome was moderate. When sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding studies judged as having a high risk of bias, similar result was found but the heterogeneity remained considerable (MD=-7.83; 95% CI: -10.60,-5.06; P < 0.00001; Tau2 = 15.81; X2 = 120.54; P < 0.00001; I2 = 93%; Supplementary Fig. 1). Several study groups were excluded due to methodological concerns that could introduce heterogeneity. Al-Sibaie and Hajeer (Group 1) [13] were excluded because, although they described the upper anterior teeth as moderately to severely protruded before treatment, the reported mean value of the U1-SN angle indicated only mild protrusion, and the subsequent decrease to a normal value after levelling and alignment although upper first premolars extracted after leveling and alignment, raising sensitivity issues in heterogeneity analysis. Davoody et al. (Group 2) [29] were excluded since their use of a 0.016 × 0.022 stainless steel base archwire in a 0.022 bracket slot produced an unusually large decrease in upper incisor inclination. Dora et al. [25] were excluded because the reference line used to measure incisor inclination seemed inconsistent across cephalometric images. Finally, Hatrom et al. [30] were excluded as they employed a bidimensional multibracket system unique to their study, which contributed to heterogeneity sensitivity. Consequently, a new subgroup was introduced. I2 was reduced to 59% with substantial heterogeneity. The pooled estimate showed a statistically significant reduction in the inclination of the upper incisors after retraction (MD=-9.79; 95% CI: -11.54,-8.04; P < 0.00001; Tau2=4.19; X2 = 24.43; P < 0.007; I2 = 59%; Fig. 4B).

When the loop-based en-masse retraction was employed, 4 groups from 4 trials studied changes in incisor inclination [14, 16, 17, 26]. The pooled estimate indicated to a statistically significant increase in the palatal inclination, with substantial heterogeneity (MD=-10.72; 95% CI: -15.05,-6.38; P < 0.00001; Tau2=13.82; X2 = 12.23; P < 0.007; I2 = 75%; Fig. 5A). According to the GRADE approach, the strength of evidence concerning this outcome was moderate. The leave-one-out analysis revealed that exclusion of the control group of Al-Bozaie et al. [26] reduced I² from 75% to 0%, indicating the absence of heterogeneity and this study contributed substantially to heterogeneity. The new pooled estimate referred to a statistically significant increase in the palatal inclination (MD=-13.31; 95% CI: -15.01,-11.61; P < 0.00001; Tau2=0.00; X2 = 0.88; P < 0.64; I2 = 0%; Fig. 5B).

Change in incisor inclination was evaluated during the two-step retraction in 7 groups from 6 studies [13, 15, 20, 27, 29, 31]. The pooled estimate showed a statistically significant decrease in the inclination of the upper incisors post-intervention, with considerable heterogeneity (MD=-7.77; 95% CI: -11.27,-4.27; P < 0.0001; Tau2=18.65; X2=130.9; P < 0.00001; I2 = 95%; Fig. 6A). According to the GRADE approach, the strength of evidence supporting this finding was moderate. The exclusion of studies with a high risk of bias resulted similar findings but did not reduce the heterogeneity (MD=-9.09; 95% CI: -12.45,-5.73; P < 0.00001; Tau2 = 11.87; X2 = 40.79; P < 0.00001; I2 = 90%; Supplementary Fig. 2). A subset of studies including group 2 of the study by Bakhit et al. [15] and group 1 of the study by Davoody et al. [29], which represents loop-based two-step retraction groups, was identified with low heterogeneity. This subgroup was analyzed separately, and the pooled estimate showed a statistically significant reduction in incisor inclination after retraction (MD=-8.94; 95% CI: -13.09,-4.78; P < 0.0001; Tau2=0.00; X2 = 0.27; P = 0.6; I2 = 0%; Fig. 6B).

Schneider et al. and Xu et al. found no statistically significant differences in the upper incisor inclination after retraction between the en-masse retraction group and the two-step retraction group (P = 0.11), (P = 0.68), respectively [18, 34].

The results of the three-arm trial by Zhang at al. revealed that the use of direct TADs anchorage with high crimpable anterior hooks 8 mm in height during en-masse retraction showed statistically significant lower changes in the palatal crown inclination of the upper central incisors, in comparison with low crimpable hooks 2 mm in height, whether direct or indirect TADs anchorage was used (P = 0.004), (P = 0.014), respectively. While no statistically significant changes were observed between the two low crimpable hooks groups (P = 0.648) [23].

Three studies compared sliding en-masse retraction with loop-based en-masse retraction [14, 16, 17]. The pooled estimate showed that loop-based retraction caused a slightly more palatal crown inclination which was statistically significant, and the heterogeneity was moderate (MD = 2.49; 95% CI: 0.71,4.27; P = 0.006; Tau2=1.12; X2 = 3.66; P = 0.16; I2 = 45%; Fig. 7). According to the GRADE approach, the strength of evidence regarding this finding was moderate.

The vertical variations in the incisal edge of the upper central incisor during retraction

Vertical changes were described in 13 trials [13, 15–19, 23, 25, 26, 29, 32–34]. Statistically significant differences between pre- and post-retraction measurements were observed across all groups in six studies [13, 16, 18, 25, 26, 34]. In addition, significant changes were reported in specific groups of three trials: group 2 in Bakhit et al. [15], group 1 in Ma et al. [32], and group 3 in Zhang at al., [23]. Intergroup comparisons yielded statistically significant differences in 4 trials [13, 23, 32, 34]. According to the GRADE approach, the strength of evidence supporting this finding was very low.

Discussion

Changes in incisor inclination following retraction

Eight trials compared accelerated and non-accelerated arms, from which only the non-accelerated arms were selected as standalone groups and included in the pre- and post-intervention analyses [24–28, 30, 31, 33].

For the purposes of this systematic review, changes in upper incisor inclination were considered clinically meaningful if they exceeded 5°. This threshold was determined by consensus among the review team. While some studies discuss inclination changes without specifying a numeric cutoff, the team agreed that adopting a 5° threshold provides a pragmatic and clinically relevant benchmark for distinguishing statistical from clinical significance.

All retraction methods resulted in retroclination, with pooled mean differences ranging from 8.94° to 13.31°. Sliding en-masse retraction groups demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in incisor inclination (MD=–9.79), exceeding the threshold of 5°. The residual heterogeneity (I2 = 59%) after subgrouping likely reflects genuine differences in patient populations and clinical protocols. The quality of evidence was moderate due to high risk of bias in two trials and some concerns in two others.

Similarly, loop-based en-masse retraction produced a statistically and clinically significant anterior retroclination (MD=–13.31). The exclusion of Al-Bozaie et al. [26] in the sensitivity analysis was due to its use of sectional en-masse retraction, which resulted the absence of heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). Furthermore, loop-based two-step retraction resulted in a statistically and clinically significant retroclination (MD=–8.94). The moderate quality of evidence and the absence of heterogeneity observed for these two outcomes may highlight the importance of following the biomechanical principles when loops are employed and maintain the differential moments when loops are reactivated, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of incisor inclination management during retraction.

Overall, upper incisor retraction led to a statistically and clinically significant reduction in inclination, regardless of the retraction technique. This result can be attributed to the moment generated by the distance between the line of force application and the center of resistance of the incisors, which consistently induces retroclination. Baseline incisor inclination is a critical factor in interpreting the outcomes of retraction. In cases where incisors are excessively proclined prior to treatment, the anticipated retroclination during retraction may contribute to achieving an ideal inclination. Conversely, when incisors present with normal or retroclined positions at baseline, retraction should be undertaken with caution, as further retroclination may have deleterious effects on dental esthetics and facial harmony.

Differences between retraction techniques in incisor inclination change

When comparing sliding versus loop-based en-masse retraction, the meta-analysis revealed that loop-based mechanics induced a greater reduction in upper incisor inclination. However, this difference was statistically significant but not clinically meaningful. Moreover, the moderate heterogeneity for this outcome limits the generalizability of the finding. A previous systematic review by Kheshfeh et al. [8] reported no significant differences between loop-based en-masse retraction and sliding en-masse retraction in two trials. In contrast, Goyal et al. [35] found a statistically significant difference, concluding that the loop‑based technique provided superior control of incisor inclination. This discrepancy, when compared with the findings of our meta-analysis, may be explained by the mechanics employed in the sliding group, where closed coil springs were attached directly to canine hooks during retraction, thereby increasing the distance between the point of force application and the center of resistance of the anterior teeth.

The comparison between en-masse and two-step retraction showed no statistically or clinically significant differences. Nevertheless, this result should be interpreted cautiously due to the substantial heterogeneity among included studies and the very low quality of evidence. Notably, Khlef et al. [6] reported a slightly better, statistically significant control of incisor inclination when en‑masse retraction was performed with TADs, compared with conventional anchorage in two‑step retraction.

Vertical variations in the incisal edge of the upper central incisor

Retraction effects on incisors varied across studies, with reports of intrusion, no change, or extrusion. Incisor vertical position was maintained in four trials [17, 19, 29, 33], while intrusion was observed in two trials [16, 26], and extrusion was noticed in three trials [18, 25, 34]. Four trials showed differences in incisor vertical position between study groups [13, 15, 23, 32]. Differences in incisor vertical position across studies likely stem from factors such as the direction of force application and differential moments. Generally, extrusion was observed with conventional anchorage [13, 18, 25, 34], while intrusion was more common when temporary anchorage devices (TADs) were used [13, 15, 16, 23, 26, 32]. It is important to note that the vertical movement of incisors may influence posterior teeth, an effect not evaluated in this review. The quality of evidence for this outcome was rated very low, primarily due to high risk of bias. Khlef et al. [6] reported intrusive forces on incisors with en-masse retraction using TADs, and extrusive forces with two-step retraction using conventional anchorage, with a statistically significant mean difference between the two techniques (SMD=-2.48 mm, P < 0.00001). In a separate study, Khlef et al. [3] assessed vertical changes of upper incisors during en‑masse retraction and similarly found intrusion with TADs and extrusion with conventional anchorage, with a statistically significant difference (SMD=–1.87 mm, P = 0.04). Taken together, these findings suggest that TADs may be advantageous when incisor intrusion is indicated, though careful construction of force systems remains essential. In contrast, conventional anchorage do not consistently counteract extrusive forces, highlighting the importance of caution when planning retraction in patients with deep bite malocclusion.

Limitations of this review

The included studies showed clinical and methodological differences—such as variations in retraction mechanics, anchorage systems, force delivery, hook height, wire type, and activation protocols—making pooled results less comparable and increasing uncertainty. As the non-accelerated arms were included and the accelerated ones were excluded, these extracted arms could not contribute to between-group comparisons and were therefore limited to pre-post analyses. Measurement methods were inconsistent, ranging from two‑dimensional cephalometry to limited three‑dimensional assessments, often with different reference lines, which may have introduced bias. Several trials had issues with allocation concealment and blinding, and many had small sample sizes, reducing precision and increasing the chance of misleading results. Follow‑up periods were generally short, so long‑term stability, relapse, or late side effects could not be assessed. No previous study has tested the clinical relevance of incisor inclination change during retraction, which means that the suggested 5° threshold remains insufficiently validated. Future research should aim to establish a validated cutoff limit for clinical relevance. Finally, attempts to contact the corresponding authors of potentially eligible studies to obtain missing raw data were unsuccessful, as no responses were received.

Conclusions

On the basis of the current findings, retraction of maxillary incisors is generally linked with retroclination across different retraction techniques. Loop‑based en-masse mechanics showed bigger pooled change in inclination than sliding en-masse and two-step mechanics, while differences between sliding and loop‑based en‑masse retraction were small and of uncertain clinical relevance. No clear advantage was observed for inclination preservation when comparing en‑masse with two‑step retraction. Given the heterogeneity although sensitivity analysis was performed, variable measurement methods, and risk of bias, these results should be interpreted cautiously. Clinicians should expect retroclination during anterior retraction and apply active inclination‑control measures—such as bracket prescription, auxiliary torqueing mechanics, wire adjustments, appropriate hook height, and careful anchorage selection—when inclination preservation is a treatment goal. Future randomized trials should directly measure torque with standardized three‑dimensional protocols, provide detailed reporting of mechanics and activation schedules, include longer follow‑up periods, and ensure rigorous randomization and blinding to improve the reliability and clinical applicability of the evidence.
