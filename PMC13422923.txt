Teleconsultation and joint consultation across primary–secondary care in a Dutch urbanised area: an exploratory qualitative study of healthcare professionals’ experiences of implementation barriers and facilitators
Authors: Malou Crasborn, Maarten O van Aken, Sam van Smoorenburg, Bas L van der Hoeven, Sytske van Bruggen, Mattijs E Numans, Rianne M C Pepping, Hedwig M M Vos, The Hague Consortium Of Network CarE Redesign and Transition, (The Hague CONCERT), Petra G van Peet, Rimke C Vos

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study explored healthcare professionals’ experiences with (asynchronous) teleconsultations and (synchronous) joint consultations in primary and secondary care as well as perceived implementation barriers and facilitators to their use in clinical practice.DesignAn implementation-focused qualitative study employing semi-structured interviews guided by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). A systematic examination of implementation barriers and facilitators using deductive and inductive approaches, followed by thematic analysis that identified patterns, developed conceptual categories and synthesised these into overarching themes and thematic areas.SettingTeleconsultation and joint consultation used to support collaboration and continuity of care between general practitioners and medical specialists in a Dutch urbanised area.Participants37 participants were interviewed between August 2021 and October 2022, including 14 general practitioners and 23 medical specialists.ResultsFour thematic areas were identified: (1) intervention, (2) interprofessional collaboration, (3) perceived patient perspective and (4) prerequisites for implementation and sustainability. Teleconsultations were highly valued by healthcare professionals, as they enhanced the quality, efficiency and flexibility of their work and improved access to care and the overall care experience for patients. Joint consultations were particularly appreciated for their interactive nature, which strengthened interprofessional relationships and supported their educational value while facilitating delivery of the right care in the right place. However, next to these facilitators several challenges were identified. Specifically, teleconsultations were hindered by variation in use, unclear expectations, a lack of dialogue and limited IT interoperability. Furthermore, scaling up joint consultations was deemed challenging due to volume requirements, time inefficiencies and organisational constraints.ConclusionsTeleconsultation and joint consultation were valued by healthcare professionals as tools that support continuity and quality of care. While serving distinct purposes, their combined use strengthened collaboration between general practitioners and medical specialists. However, sustainable implementation was hindered by organisational, technical, financial and governance-related barriers, as well as limited interoperability and lack of shared clinical ownership. Addressing these barriers is essential to enable scalable and routine use of digital interprofessional consultation models.Keywords: Clinical Decision-Making, eHealth, Organisation of health services, HEALTH SERVICES ADMINISTRATION & MANAGEMENT, Interprofessional Relations, Primary Health Care

Introduction

Continuity of care is a core component of high-quality, patient-centred healthcare and is associated with improved health outcomes and patient satisfaction.1–5 In the Netherlands and many other countries (eg, Denmark, England, Norway), healthcare systems are structured around primary and secondary care. General practitioners (GPs) provide first access to care and function as a gatekeeper to secondary (specialist) care, which requires a referral. While this system supports efficiency and appropriate use of specialist care, it specifically requires effective collaboration and communication between primary and secondary care to ensure continuity of care. This is especially relevant given that referral to secondary care does not replace primary care, and patients continue to consult their GP alongside specialist treatment.

Traditionally, communication between GPs and medical specialists (MS) has been limited to telephone consultations or written (electronic) referral letters. Increasing healthcare demand, rising care complexity, overlapping care pathways between primary and secondary care and the growing prevalence of multimorbidity place additional pressure on collaboration across care settings.6–10 These developments challenge traditional models of collaboration and highlight the need for new forms of interprofessional collaboration that support communication across care settings.

In recent years, alternative consultation models have been developed to improve collaboration and to bridge gaps between primary and secondary care by facilitating more efficient and timely specialist advice prior to a referral.11–14 Key examples include asynchronous teleconsultations where GPs consult MS via secure digital platforms, and synchronous joint consultations where GPs and MS jointly assess patients in primary care with or without the patient present (table 1).

Since 2018, teleconsultation has been implemented on a large scale in The Hague region of the Netherlands, alongside joint consultations in several specialities, especially cardiology and orthopaedics. However, evidence regarding healthcare professionals’ experiences with these consultation models remains limited.15 16 In addition, little is known about factors influencing their integration into routine clinical practice or the clinical contexts in which they are most appropriately applied. Therefore, this study aimed to explore healthcare professionals’ experiences with teleconsultations and joint consultations in primary and secondary care, and to identify perceived barriers and facilitators to their use in clinical practice.

Methods

Study design

This paper presents a secondary analysis of a previously conducted study, addressing a different research question. This study was based on an implementation-focused qualitative exploratory study using semi-structured interviews guided by the Consolidated Framework of Implementation Research (CFIR).17 The study design, participants and data collection procedures were identical to those reported in Crasborn et al,18 described here in brief, with reference to online supplemental file 1 for more comprehensive detail to minimise the risk of redundancy or textual overlap. The current analysis focused on experienced barriers and facilitators with two interventions of interprofessional collaboration to support continuity of care. The study’s interview guide can be found in online supplemental file 2. The ‘Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act’ does not apply to this study. The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting checklist for qualitative research was used when writing this article.

Context/definition

Clear differentiation between teleconsultation and joint consultations is essential, as their purposes and indications differ. Inconsistent terminology, both nationally and internationally, creates confusion among professionals, policymakers, insurers and researchers. Teleconsultations are variably labelled as e-consults, advice and guidance, remote consultation, pre-referral e-consult, advisory consult and are asynchronous. Joint consultations are variably labelled as support consultations or video-based consultations and are synchronous. Thus, many terms are used and confused with digital consultations between doctors and patients. Although national initiatives aim to harmonise terminology, discrepancies persist between Dutch practice and international literature.19

The present study specifically focused on two newly implemented consultation services: (1) teleconsultation (asynchronous consultations between GP and MS) and (2) joint consultations (synchronous consultations between GP and MS, with or without a patient, via video call or at the primary care practice), primarily in the fields of cardiology and orthopaedics. Definitions of these consultation formats are provided in table 1.

Framework

The study was guided by the CFIR as explained in figure 1. To analyse health services that bridge the gap between primary and secondary care, we used the CFIR. This determinant framework is designed to systematically identify barriers and facilitators to the implementation of healthcare innovations. It addresses factors across five core domains. In contrast to the previous study18 which included only two CFIR domains (Individual Characteristics and Inner setting—tension for change) and focused on how to improve collaboration between primary and secondary care defining four key domains, this study incorporates all five CFIR domains: (1) Intervention characteristics, (2) Inner setting, (3) Outer setting, (4) Individual characteristics and (5) The process of implementation. These domains were applied to examine two collaborative interventions: teleconsultation and joint consultation (table 1).

Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), adjusted to this study17: Intervention domain comprises teleconsultation and joint consultations. Individual characteristics include general practitioners and medical specialists. Inner setting refers to hospitals and general practice settings. Outer setting encompasses patient needs, health insurers and other external stakeholders. Process reflects the implementation process.

Participants

Participant recruitment (GPs and MS of different hospitals in The Hague) was based on stratified purposive and snowball sampling methods. 37 healthcare professionals were interviewed, comprising 14 GPs (10 female, 4 male) and 23 MSs (10 female, 13 male) (online supplemental file 1). Face-to-face interviews were conducted when possible; otherwise, telephone or video call. Researcher MC, a female GP in training, conducted all the interviews. She introduced herself and explained the aim of the study while conducting the interviews. Because the researcher had the same experience as the people they interviewed, they understood the data better. However, they had to be careful not to let their own opinions change the results. For further details, see online supplemental file 1.

Data collection

Data were collected in The Hague region through semi-structured interviews with GPs and with MS at two hospitals with different specialties, between August 2021 and October 2022. The interviews were audio-recorded, and the pilot data were included in the main analysis. Data collection ended once saturation was reached, meaning no new themes appeared in two consecutive interviews. To ensure complete heterogeneity as described earlier, the research group confirmed that adequate representation was achieved. The participants did not receive their transcripts for feedback. For further details, see online supplemental file 1.

Data analysis

The initial stages of data analysis were identical to those reported in Crasborn et al18 (online supplemental file 1). This included verbatim transcription, pseudonymisation and iteratively analysing the data. These data were initially deductively coded in Atlas.ti 22 (MC and SvS) and organised by CFIR domains. Subsequently, for the purposes of this study, data were extracted that specifically related to the interventions (teleconsultation and joint consultation). For content that fell outside the established CFIR framework, an inductive approach was used. This allowed us to capture new insights through subthemes that still mapped onto the overarching domains. This was followed by a thematic analysis focusing on barriers and facilitators of the two newly implemented consultation formats. This process involved identifying patterns, developing conceptual categories and synthesising these into overarching themes. Two researchers (MC and SvS) coded all transcripts independently, with meetings at the beginning of the process, in between and at the end. After coding independently, themes were formulated together and fine-tuned by MC and eventually subsequently reviewed jointly with SvS for final changes. Finally, to enhance coherence and readable presentation of the results, the research team (RMCP, PGvP, SvS, MOvA, MC) collaboratively refined, merged and structured the themes into four thematic areas according to their content during several sessions.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Thematic area 1: intervention

Theme 1.1 teleconsultations: enhance quality, efficiency and flexibility of work for healthcare professionals (facilitator)

Asynchronous communication

It was broadly appreciated that teleconsultations facilitated asynchronous communication at a self-selected moment. Both GPs and MS reported that teleconsultations led to higher-quality responses compared with telephone consultations, as clinical questions were formulated (by the GP) and answered (by the MS) in writing at a time chosen by themselves, rather than being discussed by phone while performing routine hospital activities (Intervention characteristics—relative advantage). One MS explained:

Look, it’s never convenient if two healthcare providers have to call each other. One of us will always have a patient in front of them or about to arrive; this (the Teleconsult) is something you can arrange at a convenient time. Then you can just sit down calmly for a moment and time to analyse and contemplate the case. (MS 14)

Another MS emphasised the reduction in untimely telephone interruptions due to the implementation of teleconsultations (Intervention characteristics—relative advantage):

I think all my colleagues view this positively. Especially because it avoids receiving calls at inconvenient moments […] (MS 26)

Similarly, GPs valued the scheduled nature and convenience of written communication (Intervention characteristics—relative advantage):

It’s less stressful when I’m not put on hold and perhaps might bother someone at an inconvenient moment (for example, during lunch or in the emergency room with a patient). So yes, I’m perfectly happy to put my question in writing (via teleconsultation). (GP 1)

These findings indicate that the primary benefit of asynchronous communication lies in its ability to bridge the conflicting operational workflows of primary and secondary care. Synchronous contact, such as traditional telephone consultation, disrupts clinical routines, forcing clinicians to balance real-time patient care with interprofessional dialogue. By shifting to an asynchronous format, the teleconsultation addresses this relational and structural friction. It transforms a potentially intrusive disruption into a controllable task that fits into the daily schedules of both GPs and MS, thereby establishing a significant relative advantage over existing communication methods.

Improved documentation

Participants highlighted that both the GP’s clinical question and the MS’s response were documented more formally in teleconsultations than in telephone contacts, enhancing clarity, traceability and accountability, as well as supporting financial registration. Written communication was also perceived to stimulate more detailed and considered responses compared with telephone consultations (Intervention characteristics—relative advantage):

I always appreciate a teleconsultation, with internal medicine for example, when you get a more detailed answer. It allows them calm consideration, and you often get a better answer than from a quick call to a specialist. (GP 4)

I think the answers are of a better quality than when the GPs call. (MS 35)

The transition from verbal exchange to a digital record enhances clinical accountability and systemic traceability. This means the innovation not only improves individual decision-making quality but also fulfils critical structural and financial requirements within the wider organisation.

Filling knowledge gaps

Teleconsultation enabled specialist input within primary care without the need for immediate referral, thereby strengthening collaboration between primary and secondary care. The low-threshold opportunity to seek advice was perceived by GPs as a facilitator to address knowledge gaps that might otherwise remain unaddressed, contributing to improved continuity and quality of care (Intervention characteristics—relative advantage). GPs regarded this as a key facilitator and added value of teleconsultation:

I sometimes have questions, for example concerning complicated lab values, or I don’t quite understand the lab values. And then I think, ‘Okay, it’ll probably be fine, I’ll manage’, but then I realize that it is more appropriate to just ask anyway. (GP 12)

However, as discussed in the subsequent theme, MS also identified this as a boundary, expressing concerns that teleconsultations should not be used solely as a general ‘question-and-answer service’ (see Thematic area 2: Interprofessional collaboration).

This facilitator highlights how lowering the professional threshold changes how clinical knowledge is shared between primary and secondary care. By providing an easy way to fill immediate knowledge gaps, the innovation helps GPs manage complex cases within primary care. However, this shift reveals a tension regarding professional boundaries and expectations.

Additionally, lowering the threshold for consultation might lead GPs to seek advice earlier for cases they used to manage on their own, which could lower their clinical self-confidence. While specialists worry that this misaligns with the innovation’s goal, fearing it might shift basic teaching responsibilities to secondary care, this dynamic also creates a paradox for healthcare delivery. Although it may improve the immediate quality of care, a major disadvantage is the potential increase in workload for both specialists and GPs. Ultimately, this duality shows how a single structural feature can improve care quality for primary care while simultaneously causing relational and operational strain within professional collaboration.

Theme 1.2 teleconsultations: lack of dialogue and inconsistent information limits teleconsult quality (barrier)

Variability in quality and completeness of information

Both GPs and MS reported that teleconsultations often contained incomplete or insufficiently detailed clinical information and/or images (Individual context—expectations, Intervention characteristics—relative disadvantage). This variability in information quality sent from primary to secondary care generated uncertainty in clinical decision-making and often resulted in specialist responses characterised by conditional reasoning or hypothetical scenarios (Individual context—knowledge and beliefs). MS described this challenge as follows:

The only thing you have to realize is that you’re managing with the information you have. […] that the GP setting up a teleconsultation perhaps doesn’t always understand that certain information can be very relevant. And we have to appreciate that this could be the case, and that this means you have to write, well, with plenty of ‘ifs’ and ‘buts’. So yes, then I think it’s perfectly okay. (MS 29)

Contrary to what everyone always thinks: that dermatology is perfectly suited to teledermatology, we really get very little information from the patient history, poor photos or detail photos rather than an overview. And when you finally see, for instance the patient the teleconsultation was about, and perhaps because you use more of your senses, your ideas suddenly head off in a completely different direction. (MS 17)

This gap not only hampered decision-making but also reinforced perceptions of teleconsultation as a less reliable or efficient mode of communication in certain situations, such as dermatology consultations lacking or with poor images, or when questions were not accompanied by complete anamnesis or diagnostic information (Individual context—knowledge and beliefs, Intervention characteristics—relative disadvantage).

GPs further reported that MS responses to teleconsultations were sometimes delayed, exceeding the recommended 3-day timeframe. When responses were received on time, they were sometimes incomplete or did not fully address the original question, necessitating follow-up telephone contact for clarification. One GP described such an experience (Intervention characteristics—relative disadvantage):

[…] (name of colleague) had asked a very wide-ranging question via a teleconsultation. And the only response she got was: send them in. Okay, but that wasn’t the question. So then I think, ‘that specialist really wants to finish their teleconsultations quickly’. This is just an easy answer then or something like that. We looked at each other and just sort of laughed, thinking, ‘I prepared that long teleconsultation for this?’ (GP 2)

From the perspective of MS, the limited time allocated and scheduled for responding to teleconsultations may influence the depth and comprehensiveness of the responses provided.

If it’s going to cost me more than 20 minutes to answer, I think: just let the patient come to see me. (MS 20)

GPs also noted that teleconsultation workflows were not uniformly embedded across specialities, resulting in inconsistent accessibility and responsiveness (Intervention characteristics—relative disadvantage):

You already know, because the surgeon department hardly ever responds. […]. And I know they’ve got it, but it hasn’t been properly organized. That’s the problem, they don’t check it every day. (GP 9)

Furthermore, although teleconsultations were not intended for patients already receiving secondary care, they were frequently used for this purpose (Intervention characteristics—relative disadvantage, adaptability).

We’re also noticing more, actually really a lot of teleconsultations coming in for ongoing cases, people who are already seeing a hospital specialist, but where there’s, well, another question from primary care. That was never really the intention, but it is a convenient way to have a short consultation and to document everything properly at both ends … (MS 32)

In such cases, GPs and MS noted that the responding specialist was not always familiar with the patient (Intervention characteristics—relative disadvantage):

Of course, if it’s about an outpatient that someone knows and that person is away for a week, then someone else will just have to answer. That can be a bit difficult sometimes. (MS 25)

Teleconsultations were also considered less suitable for patients with complex multimorbidity, as limitations in the volume and depth of transferable information constrained adequate clinical assessment (Intervention characteristics—disadvantage):

[…] but I sometimes find it less suited for the elderly with complex issues. (GP 12)

[…] at the same time we also recognize that it’s difficult, right? They often have multimorbidity, so they’re seeing cardiologists, nephrologists and other specialists, and they’re on a lot of medications. It’s also very difficult for primary care to deal with. So yes, we’re trying teleconsultations to see if they’re feasible but there are limits, I’m slowly coming to realize that. (MS 32)

The widespread variability in information quality, such as incomplete medical histories, unclear images or missing test results, creates a significant conflict between the tool’s limitations and the requirements for safe clinical decision-making. When GPs provide fragmented data, MS are forced into ‘conditional reasoning’, which lowers the perceived reliability of the tool and reduces its efficiency. However, this digital approach should be compared with traditional telephone consultations, teleconsultations still offer a structural advantage over brief verbal exchanges, even when the shared data is imperfect.

Furthermore, a lack of system integration between primary and secondary care workflows creates challenges. Brief specialist responses (eg, ‘send them in’) reflect a deeper structural constraint: specialists must balance time-consuming digital reviews against rigid institutional schedules. Consequently, when a teleconsultation takes too long to decrypt, specialists often prefer face-to-face visits to save their own time. This professional trade-off directly shifts the logistical and financial burden back to the patient through potentially unnecessary travel, work absence and mobility challenges.

Lack of interaction

Another barrier experienced with teleconsultations was the limited opportunity for dialogue inherent to current teleconsultation formats. Both GPs and MS indicated that teleconsultations facilitated one-way communication from primary to secondary care, limiting interactive discussion that would typically occur during telephone or face-to face contact. They both expressed a desire for more interactive consultation (Intervention characteristics—relative disadvantage, adaptability).

The communication with general practitioners could be considerably more personal and optimal. (MS 17)

Actually, a teleconsultation would really benefit from some follow-up. For instance, via a chat that we could have about it with each other. (GP 3)

These insights reveal that while the asynchronous format of teleconsultation offers practical efficiency, it also imposes structural limits on communication by removing the personal elements of dialogue. This creates a significant conflict between the tool’s design and the communication needs required for complex clinical cases. By reducing communication to a rigid, one-way exchange of information, the tool unintentionally weakens the collaborative trust traditionally built through real-time verbal discussion.

This structural limitation creates a compounding effect: when combined with variable data quality, the lack of an immediate feedback loop (such as a chat or follow-up feature) prevents clinicians from quickly clarifying ambiguities. Consequently, healthcare professionals view the technology as a rigid tool suited only for straightforward, linear cases, rather than a collaborative platform capable of supporting the nuanced, shared decision-making required for complex clinical scenarios.

Theme 1.3 joint consultations: strong educational character (facilitator)

Educational character

Both GPs and MS described joint consultations as having a clear educational character (Individual setting—educational). This applied to both formats (table 1): (1) in-person consultations in which one MS visits one GP at the practice to treat patients together and (2) video-based joint consultations in which one MS discusses cases with multiple GPs without the patient present. Despite this educational component, participants noted that a hierarchical relationship remains, with the MS primarily adopting an advisory role (Individual setting—knowledge and beliefs). In the video-based format (version 1, table 1), GPs reported learning not only from their own cases but also from the cases presented by colleagues (Individual setting—educational). GPs characterised these sessions as ‘mini training sessions’ that contribute to improved quality of care. MS observed a learning curve among GPs over time, which was reflected in changes in the types of questions posed by GPs (Individual setting—educational):

I have to say that even in those sessions I already notice that at a certain point general practitioners just go, ‘Oh yeah, I had to do it like this, right?’ Well, eventually they stop asking that question and the subject shifts to completely different things, because by then they already understand. (MS 15)

At the same time, GPs emphasised that joint consultations will remain necessary despite these learning effects, as patients continue to value MS opinions, and MS input remains essential for complex decision-making in primary care (Intervention characteristics—relative disadvantage):

Yes, there’s a learning effect, of course. So if at some point we stopped consulting the cardiologist, then maybe we could eventually make our own decisions. But that’s quite difficult, you know. Because the patient also wants to know what the cardiologist thinks about it. And it is of course a genuine specialty, cardiology. Actually, I think that this form of collaboration should be made available to all general practitioners, if possible. (GP 3)

Finally, MS noted that the angle of the learning curve varied, with slower adaptation observed among older GPs. Learning was also influenced by the continuity of collaboration between the same GP and MS, and the extent to which MS were able to align their advice with the primary care context (Intervention characteristics—educational).

These findings demonstrate that joint consultations serve as a powerful tool for continuous learning between professionals. However, their success is limited by structural hierarchies and the characteristics of individual practitioners. The innovation does not replace the need for specialist input, but rather reshapes the connection between primary and secondary care. This emphasises that long-term implementation requires specialists to move beyond traditional top-down advice and actively adapt their expertise to the daily practice of primary care.

Theme 1.4 joint consultations: time inefficiency and restricted options for specialty implementation (barrier)

Time inefficiency

One MS indicated that online joint consultations drained their energy, as they were required to spend an extended period of time (eg, an hour) in front of a screen engaging in discussion. In addition, in-person joint consultations were perceived as time-inefficient due to the travel time required when the MS visited GP practices. As one MS stated:

But it’s also expensive, because I’m out of the hospital for a while. (MS 31)

Both healthcare professional groups described joint consultations as a substantial time investment. GPs as well as MS reported that considerable time had to be blocked in their schedules to discuss only a limited number of patients per session (Process—bottle neck implementation). Consequently, the model was experienced as time-inefficient, particularly when only a small number of patients could be discussed per session.

In addition, GP workloads increased because they remained responsible for requesting diagnostic tests, coordinating follow-up and implementing MS advice, tasks that would normally be transferred to secondary care after referral (Intervention characteristic—disadvantage):

It costs you a lot of time—really a lot of time—and quite a bit of time is spent, well, with having to put in orders, etc. And particularly: you have to constantly check that it actually happens. And you remain responsible for that patient, of course. Look, normally when you refer someone to a specialist they’ll be there for a while. (GP 7)

The intervention has a severe operational bottleneck as it demands a disproportionate investment of time and energy. For MS, the physical travel or prolonged screen-time conflicts with organisational performance indicators, creating a financial and logistical drain on hospital resources. For GPs, the innovation creates a shift in accountability and administration.

Restricted to certain specialties

GPs also expressed a desire to expand joint consultations to additional medical specialties. At the time of the study, consultations were limited to orthopaedists and cardiologists. Prolonged collaboration within a single specialty may ultimately lead to a learning curve plateau and consequent reduced value. Variation and rotation across different specialties could serve as a potential alternative to mitigate this effect:

To improve that process my idea would be […] to arrange alternating joint consultations. I think that after a while you’ll get the hang of it. For instance, how the cardiologist treats heart palpitations, or how the orthopaedist treats knee problems. (GP 2)

In addition, GPs indicated a need to apply joint consultations in other care domains. During the interviews, midwifery, youth health services and psychiatry were mentioned as potential areas for further implementation.

Thematic area 2: interprofessional collaboration

Theme 2.1 teleconsultation: insufficient alignment of agreements, high variability in indications and unspoken expectations among professionals hinders effective use (barrier)

No clear agreements and unclear expectations

Although teleconsultations were generally positively valued by healthcare professionals (Intervention characteristics—relative advantage), participants reported limited feedback mechanisms and a lack of structured dialogue between GPs and MS regarding appropriate use (Inner setting—lack in consultation, resources). Consequently, expectations about the purpose, scope and boundaries of teleconsultations often remained unclear (Individual context—expectations). As one GP explained:

In principle, it’s not very clear in the sense that you’re not really limited to a specific issue. Of course, I don’t know how it goes with other colleagues; they might use it in a slightly different way, I wouldn’t like to say. (GP 6)

GPs indicated that teleconsultations were less suitable for very brief clinical questions requiring only a yes/no answer, for which they preferred telephone contact. At the same time, they expressed a real need for MS advice tailored to the primary care context. MS responses were sometimes perceived as overly extensive or too highly specialised to be implemented directly in primary care, causing frustration:

What often happens is that you receive a very detailed response that makes you think: do I really need to do all of this? Wouldn’t it have been easier just to refer the patient? Then I sometimes think, okay, with that workload I would have been better off just referring the patient. (GP 4)

In contrast, MS expressed distinct expectations regarding appropriate use. They indicated that teleconsultations should not be initiated by general practice nurses (caring for patients with chronic care problems and older adults) without prior discussion with the supervising GP, and it was often unclear whether GP approval was obtained (Intervention characteristics—relative disadvantage):

When I look at the number of teleconsultations for this type of patient, a lot of them come via practice nurses. And okay, that’s fine, but then of course I feel like it’s a new situation and I no longer know where the GP stands. (MS 32)

MS also emphasised that teleconsultation should not function as an unrestricted helpdesk and should not be too easily accessible (Intervention characteristics—relative disadvantage). As one MS explained:

[…] questions from GPs who seem to think, ‘Well, that’s great, I can just ask that too’. (MS 35)

Conversely, this low threshold was described by GPs as a facilitator, as it enabled them to address knowledge gaps that might otherwise remain unresolved.

MS further stressed that GPs should first follow relevant primary care guidelines before requesting MS input via teleconsultation (Individual context—expectations):

[…] that guideline (of Dutch College of General Practitioners) isn’t being followed in primary care, so a lot of unnecessary questions end up in secondary care. (MS 24)

GPs, however, reported feeling underestimated by MS, perceiving that their prior diagnostic and therapeutic efforts were sometimes insufficiently recognised (Individual context—expectations):

I think that sometimes the specialist, […] underestimates what we as GPs have already done. Then I get an answer that makes me think ‘Yes, I’d already thought of that’. (GP 12)

As a result of unclear agreements about the use of teleconsultations and different expectations, professionals described feeling uncertain about which clinical questions are appropriate for teleconsultation. This lack of clarity resulted in considerable variation in reported indications, with both overlap and discrepancies between GPs and MS (online supplemental file 3). Examples of indications were: to confirm diagnoses or treatment plans, for legal reassurance, an unsuccessful telephone contact, to bypass waiting lists or to avoid hospital visits for older patients or assessing the necessity for such a visit (Individual context—indication). One illustration of these practical applications:

And it can be a way to protect oneself and, of course, also to reassure patients. So I can say, ‘Okay, I’ll talk to the specialists but I’ll do that by email [uses the term email with the patient to keep it relatable]. I think I actually know the answer, but I want to double-check. You can use that as a way to avoid a referral or to protect yourself legally. (GP 9)

The absence of formalised protocols and feedback loops creates a profound relational and operational friction, rooted in conflicting professional role expectations across the primary-secondary care interface. This structural ambiguity stems from a lack of systematic alignment within the organisation governance, causing clinicians to individually construct the purpose and boundaries of the innovation based on their immediate workplace needs. For GPs, the low-threshold innovation functions as an expansive resource for continuous education, diagnostic reassurance and so on. However, for specialists, the tool is conceptualised as a highly restrictive, secondary triaging mechanism that should only be activated after primary care guidelines have been fully exhausted. This difference triggers mutual frustration. This mismatch in expectations demonstrates that without explicit, co-designed agreements and workflow compatibility, a low-threshold digital innovation will organically generate high clinical variation.

Theme 2.2 joint consultations: consultations strengthen relationships, trust and interprofessional understanding (facilitator)

Positive experience and strengthening relationships

Joint teleconsultations were reported as a positive experience (Individual setting—knowledge and beliefs) and were perceived to strengthen the relationship between GPs and MS (Intervention characteristics—advantage). Professionals valued the opportunity for MS to ‘look over the GP’s shoulder’ and jointly consider the most appropriate approach for a patient in primary care. In cases where the GP did not have a clear reason to refer but was uncertain about the best course of action, this collaborative discussion was experienced as particularly helpful (Intervention characteristics—relative advantage).

This absence of uncertainty may be explained by clearer structuring within certain specialties, such as cardiology, where predefined indications guided their use:

And sometimes I think, ‘Okay, not really suitable for referral, but I’d like to do more with this but I don’t really know what’. Then I sometimes think it would be nice if that cardiologist could, well, just take a look at what could be done better and differently. Heart failure patients, in particular, aren’t easily described in one easy teleconsultation. It’s not about just one question; you often need the entire file. (GP 10)

Professionals also emphasised the relational benefits of repeated interaction. GPs and MS reported that familiarity and mutual understanding fostered through joint consultations improved collaboration and facilitated more efficient and comfortable communication (Intervention characteristics—relative advantage):

It’s also great for the patient if it does come to surgery; then the three of us are on the same page, more united. Yes, knowing each other is good. (GP 12)

On the other hand, you develop a rapport with the GP because you know one another, so you can easily contact each other. And it’s a bit like you can spar a bit about the profession and how things work. (MS 15)

Overall, joint consultations were perceived as enhancing professional relationships and promoting collaborative decision-making across care settings.

The joint consultation format creates a robust collaborative space that positively shapes the organisation’s implementation climate. The repeated interaction inherent to joint consultations fosters a sense of familiarity and mutual understanding.

Thematic area 3: perceived patient perspective

Theme 3.1 teleconsultation: patient benefit by improving access, efficiency and experience (facilitator)

Positive patient experience

GPs reported that patients appreciated receiving earlier advice and answers regarding their health concerns. Patients experienced shorter periods of uncertainty compared with standard referrals, as they received timely guidance without waiting for specialist appointments. Teleconsultation allowed GPs to address patients’ questions more promptly, support triage, and, when necessary, facilitated more timely access to specialist care. Additionally, this approach reduced the burden on older patients who would otherwise need to travel to hospital. MS noted that fewer patients were unnecessarily referred to secondary care as a result of teleconsultation:

If the patient needs to be referred, it’s immediately clear that the specialist agrees. So then I can refer, and I usually know the timeframe and whether it should be urgent, semi-urgent or just the regular waiting list. (GP 3)

Certainly older patients who are already seeing four specialists tend to think, ‘Do I really need to go to the dermatologist for this?’ Then it’s really a solution that you’re able to try and help someone in this way. (GP 4)

Patients reportedly felt they were taken seriously through this process. Many MS in secondary care expressed uncertainty regarding patient’s experiences with teleconsultations, as they rarely received direct feedback from patients. Nonetheless, speculation about patient perceptions tended to align with GP reports, who consistently indicated that patients felt reassured by GP consultations with an MS. Teleconsultations were perceived by patients as convenient and positive, particularly compared with traditional referrals, because they eliminated waiting times, reduced travel requirements and avoided deductible excess fees:

I explain your whole story (how teleconsultation works) and tell them: ‘and then you (the patient) get an answer of the cardiologist’. Most patients really love that, because then they get a quick answer from me and it doesn’t cost them anything. (GP 2)

It also frequently occurred that patients presented to the GP with concerns or questions for which they were already under specialist care. MS reported that teleconsultations often involved questions regarding patients currently undergoing treatment in secondary care. Addressing such queries digitally via the GP enables more patient-centred care.

From the patient’s perspective, the digital innovation ‘teleconsultation’ successfully optimises the healthcare delivery model by directly addressing core patients needs and systemic barriers within the wider healthcare ecosystem. Teleconsultation functions as an accelerated triaging mechanism that significantly compresses the ‘window of clinical uncertainty’ providing patients with rapid reassurance and clearer timelines for specialist care.

Theme 3.2 joint consultations: promote the right care in the right place and contribute to patient-centred care (facilitator)

Right care in the right place

Joint consultations offered patient benefits by ensuring care was delivered in the most appropriate setting. Improved alignment between GPs and MS enabled patients to reach the right level of care earlier, either through timely referral or by initiating additional diagnostics sooner than would occur in a standard referral process. Shared assessment of urgency also supported a more appropriate distribution of waiting times, allowing prioritisation based on clinical need (Intervention characteristics—relative advantage):

Sometimes the cardiologist says, ‘Wait a minute, the waiting list could be three months, but I’ll arrange a quicker appointment because I think this patient should be seen earlier’. So that’s better for everyone involved. (GP 8)

Patient-centred care

In addition, joint consultations promoted more patient-centred care by improving the quality of diagnostic decision-making and treatment planning (Intervention characteristics—relative advantage). GPs mentioned that patients valued MS input for complaints that might not strictly require secondary care but nonetheless caused considerable concern. Both GPs and MS indicated that patients appreciated the visible collaboration between care levels and felt reassured by shared decision-making (Outer setting—patient needs):

That uncertainty, that can sometimes remain an issue, and for a lot of patients you can manage it like this. Secondary care then becomes actually quite a big step. (MS 31)

But the patient is happy with that. We’re happy with that. We save on referrals. And I think it works very well. (GP 3)

By integrating specialist expertise directly into the accessible environment of primary care, the joint consultation accommodates patients who do not strictly meet the criteria for secondary care referral but still require specialist validation to minimise clinical risks. The shared alignment between GPs and MS transforms secondary care from an automatic next step into a selective, secondary resource, successfully reducing unnecessary referrals while reinforcing patient trust and satisfaction.

Thematic area 4: prerequisites for implementation and sustainability

Theme 4.1 teleconsultation: lack of non-interoperable IT systems, financial and legal uncertainty and organisational embedding (barrier)

IT system-level barriers

System-level barriers to teleconsultation primarily concerned difficulties embedding it within existing care pathways, referral software and electronic health record (EHR) systems in both primary and secondary care (Inner setting—resources). When a teleconsultation results in advice to refer, GPs are required to repeat administrative steps, including rewriting referral letters and re-uploading attachments within referral modules. MS were unable to convert teleconsultations directly into formal referrals or initiate appointments themselves. These inefficiencies were described as frustrating and unnecessary, as illustrated by one GP:

Sometimes you’re teleconsultation includes the question, ‘Should I refer this?’ If upon reflection the answer is “yes”, this means you have to redo the referral letter. That always annoys me […] Everything’s ready, the question is clear, then the specialist says: ‘We are going to handle this’. Then you have to redo the letter, as that’s a question of an insurance box that has to be ticked […], and maybe even in the system. I honestly think that’s creating unnecessary work. (GP 1)

Because a completely new referral had to be created, patients were registered as ‘new’ patients and were not consistently scheduled with the MS who had responded to the teleconsultation. MS perceived this as detrimental to continuity of care (Intervention characteristics—relative disadvantage).

In addition, teleconsultation responses sometimes ended up in a ‘black box’, with instances where responses were provided but missed or not followed up in primary care due to the need for manual scheduling of follow-up and manual documentation of specialist advice. Conversely, some teleconsultations remained unanswered by MS. One explanation mentioned was that responding to teleconsultations was not consistently embedded within the organisational workflows of all specialities, despite the functionality being technically available within the referral platform for nearly every specialty (Intervention characteristic—relative disadvantage). When this occurred, GPs reported that they did not receive automatic notifications indicating that a response was missing. As a workaround, they manually set reminders to check for replies, introducing an additional administrative burden (Intervention characteristics—adaptability):

So we’ve now implemented a new policy where, if we have scheduled a teleconsultation at a later date, we have to check it again to prevent this kind of thing [not getting a teleconsultation answer]. […] So it’s not automatic, with some sort of safeguard in place [to prevent an unanswered teleconsultation]. (GP 7)

Teleconsultations were processed manually by the GP in charge of administration. As a result, other GPs within the same practice were exposed to colleagues’ clinical questions and specialist advice. While this shared visibility supported knowledge transfer and professional learning within primary care (Outer setting—peer pressure), participants noted that it also increased the risk of miscommunication during handover to the patient or the requesting GP.

The lack of interoperability between primary and secondary EHRs creates severe operational friction. Instead of enabling a seamless digital pathway, the system forces clinicians into redundant administrative workarounds, such as recreating referral letters to satisfy insurance requirements or manually tracking missing responses to avoid ‘black box’ data loss.

Financial ambiguity

GPs reported substantial uncertainty regarding teleconsultation financing and reimbursement procedures. There were no clear agreements on what qualified as a teleconsultation, which codes should be used, or which activities were included. As a result, registration varied. Many GPs indicated limited understanding of the financial structure underpinning teleconsultation (Outer setting—financial):

I don’t know exactly how it works financially. (GP 2)

While teleconsultation fees were generally assumed to cover the processing of specialist advice and communication with the patient, some GPs mentioned that these fees do not adequately reflect the effort required for every teleconsultation because of the substantial variation in time investment per teleconsultation. Some consultations required minimal effort, whereas others involved extensive patient interaction and follow-up comparable to services traditionally delivered in secondary care. Both professional groups indicated that current reimbursement fees did not adequately reflect the time required. They also noted challenges in explaining the billing structure to GP practice assistants and locum GPs (Outer setting—financial). Both GPs and MS emphasised the absence of financial incentives to actively use teleconsultation. Engagement was primarily driven by perceived clinical and organisational benefits rather than reimbursement (Outer setting—financial):

It is not the case that our motivation is financial, absolutely not, because we invest many hours of our own time of course, so it’s not from a cost perspective. (GP 6)

This complexity of registration and reimbursement processes contributed to both over- and under-registration of teleconsultation services.

Organisational embedding

Organisational variation in policies, support structures and contractual arrangements further hindered sustainable implementation. Although teleconsultation was perceived as an effective communication tool, uncertainty remained regarding its long-term positioning within agreements between hospitals and health insurers (Outer setting—external policies):

It’s a great way to organize care as well as possible. It’s still a bit of a question whether this can be implemented long-term, and how it might be incorporated into the agreement hospitals make with insurers. (MS 21)

Legal uncertainty

GPs and MS reported legal ambiguity regarding responsibility for clinical decisions following teleconsultation. Responsibility was variably attributed to the GP, the MS or shared between both parties. The absence of clear agreements, as noted earlier, contributed to uncertainty and hesitancy regarding which party is accountable for the content of recommendations provided through a teleconsultation (Individual context indication, Outer setting—external policies).

[…] because at that moment the specialist is a co-practitioner. So if you find yourself in front of the disciplinary court, I ask myself whether the specialist might also be partly liable under disciplinary law if there’s a conflict. (GP 9)

Who bears responsibility after we’ve sent the response? It’s the GP then, right? You see, the patient isn’t ours and doesn’t have a treatment relationship with us, so we don’t have any, we’ve only given the GP advice in response to a question. (MS 20)

The conflicting professional perceptions of liability, where MS view themselves as detached advisors and GPs fear joint disciplinary liability, create a defensive clinical climate. This underscores that a digital interprofessional innovation cannot achieve sustainability if it is merely superimposed onto an antiquated healthcare architecture.

Theme 4.2 joint consultation: logistical and financial constraints hinder sustainability (barrier)

Differences in guidelines and professionals

Differences between primary and secondary care guidelines occasionally resulted in specialist advice that did not align with GP guidelines, complicating implementation in primary care and sometimes leading to frustration or misunderstanding between professionals (Individual setting—knowledge and beliefs). For example, orthopaedic specialists were reported to routinely request imaging for knee complaints, which contrasts with GP guidelines that discourage imaging for conditions such as osteoarthritis:

I often think it’s funny; okay, then an x-ray at least (request of the orthopaedic). It is secondary care after all. Because an x-ray is always necessary (due to the orthopaedic), even though we’ve just learned new stuff about osteoarthritis in primary care, and then it isn’t necessary; I sometimes find that difficult. (GP 12)

Participants further noted that the success and sustainability of joint consultations were highly dependent on individual professionals (Individual setting—educational). MS reported substantial variation in GP practices with regard to available resources and diagnostic capacity, which influenced the type of advice that could realistically be provided. Conversely, GPs highlighted differences between MS in terms of subspecialty background and their awareness of what is feasible within primary care (Intervention characteristics—relative disadvantage). This mutual variability made joint consultations highly person-dependent, limiting consistent implementation and long-term sustainability.

The intervention exposes friction between the distinct clinical guidelines governing primary and secondary care. When MS issue recommendations based on secondary protocols that directly contradict primary care guidelines, it creates cognitive dissonance and operational paralysis for the GP. This friction is compounded by a high degree of person-dependency, the success of joint consultations relies entirely on the individual specialist’s empathy toward primary care limitations and the individual GP’s baseline diagnostic capacity. Without standardised interprofessional guidelines, the innovation remains a person-dependent practice rather than a reliable, reproducible care pathway.

Policy agreements and financial insecurity

Fragmented policy agreements further hindered implementation, as individual contracts are often required between GP practices, hospitals and insurers, generating an administrative burden for all parties (Outer setting—financial, Process—bottle necks implementation). Insurer-imposed restrictions, such as limits on repeat consultations or exclusion of certain patient groups, further constrained applicability (Intervention characteristics—relative disadvantage). In addition, the absence of clear financial incentives and declining tariffs contributed to uncertainty and reduced motivation for sustained implementation. Several participants perceived the model as increasingly cost-inefficient, particularly as MS remuneration remained fixed while GP compensation decreased (Outer setting—financial, external policies):

The health insurer is slowly nibbling away at the rates. Rates are decreasing, while the fee—the specialist’s share—stays the same. So yet again the GP is the one having to make sacrifices. (GP 8)

Joint consultations were often organised through ad hoc calendar entries and informal communication channels, while existing IT systems inadequately supported shared viewing, communication and documentation. This was particularly true for video-based consultations which often required professionals to manage logistics themselves. This lack of structural digital support resulted in workarounds that were experienced as inefficient and unnecessarily complex (Intervention characteristics—adaptability):

Personally I still think the infrastructure is poor. I have to call from the hospital via intranet, so the GPs can’t see my screen. So to be practical, I just call using my own phone, which allows them to see my screen when I share it via Teams. But I can’t get audio like that. I often find these basic kinds of things—when anyone can Zoom with their mother or grandmother—are all very complicated in a hospital. (MS 31)

Financially and politically, the innovation suffers from systemic fragmentation, where short-term, ad-hoc contracting models and insurer-imposed restrictions inflate the administrative burden while directly undermining financial sustainability. The asymmetric reduction in tariffs directly suppresses the GP’s motivation. The utilisation of complex, informal communication workarounds indicates that the existing technological infrastructure fails to accommodate the core interactive demands of the innovation. This underscores that while the concept is powerful, there are structural barriers.

Theme 4.3 joint consultation: scalability constrained by volume requirements, organisational fragility and individual engagement (barrier)

Lack of scalability

A major barrier to scaling joint consultations was insufficient patient volume, particularly for solo or small GP practices. Although there was a strong demand to make joint consultations accessible to small practices, where opportunities for collegial consultation are often limited, both GPs and MS indicated that efficiency requires organising these consultations at a network or neighbourhood level, involving multiple practices. This raises the question of whether this still constitutes a joint consultation (Intervention characteristic—adaptability):

Well, to do it structurally for such a small patient volume, then you’d really have to do it with GP-federation, or district-wide with multiple practices, something like that. Then it might work. But is it then really a joint consultation? The patient wouldn’t be seen by the specialist in my practice but somewhere else. So then it’s more like a secondary care consultation location within a GP’s practice, really. An external outpatient clinic, in other words. (GP 4)

Beyond patient volume, organisational fragility further constrained scalability. Implementation was highly dependent on the individual motivation of GPs and MS. While early adopters were generally positive, other participants perceived limited added value or were discouraged by organisational complexity and time investment. Several respondents noted that when a key figure who drove the initiative withdrew, the programme ceased entirely, indicating a lack of structural and institutional embedding:

Well, speaking for myself, I’m not so active that I’ve really felt the need or anything like that. […] And then I’m literally thinking about the time required for Organisation, yes. (GP 13)

Together, these factors illustrate that although the model is transferable in principle, its scalability remains constrained by volume requirements, organisational fragility and dependence on individual engagement.

The sustainability of joint consultations is heavily undermined by their dependence on individual champions and a lack of systemic institutionalisation. The current model relies almost exclusively on the intrinsic motivation of early adopters; it remains organisationally fragile. This vulnerability underscores that the innovation has not yet transitioned from a person-dependent project into a routine, structurally embedded practice. Attempts to resolve the logistical barrier of low patient volume in smaller practices expose a severe tension within the innovation adaptability. While joint consultation is theoretically transferable, its widespread implementation is blocked by a structural mismatch between volume metrics required for hospital efficiency and the localised, relational infrastructure that gives the joint consultation its unique clinical and educational value.

Discussion

Principal findings

This implementation-focused qualitative study explored healthcare professionals’ experiences of teleconsultations and joint consultations between primary and secondary care and perceived barriers and facilitators to their use in clinical practice. The perspectives expressed by GPs and MS were largely shared regarding both implementation facilitators and barriers to facing these consultation models.

Teleconsultations were valued for their low threshold of use, efficiency and perceived improvement in continuity of care. Importantly, due to their asynchronous nature, teleconsultations were perceived as more structured and deliberate than telephone consultations, which are often interruptive and have the sole virtue of a rapid response. Joint consultations were, due to their synchronous nature, valued for their interactive character, strengthening interprofessional collaboration and fostering mutual trust and understanding between GPs and MS. Both professional groups also described educational benefits, although these were perceived as secondary depending on the continuity of collaboration.

Both types of consultation were considered beneficial for patients, providing faster access to advice, reducing uncertainty, avoiding compulsory deductibles and enhancing patients’ sense of being taken seriously. However, expectations regarding the appropriate content, scope and execution of teleconsultations were rarely made explicit between professionals, resulting in behavioural barriers. This lack of dialogue contributed to variation in use and uncertainty about responsibilities.

Healthcare professionals also identified important limitations on structural, organisational and behavioural levels. Teleconsultations were perceived as lacking nuance and dialogue due to their one-question–one-answer format, which highlights a clear structural limitation. At the same time, joint consultations were experienced as time-inefficient, pointing to an organisational issue. Moreover, a major structural problem arose because responsibility for governance, medical liability, optimisation and long-term embedding of both types of consultation was unclear. This organisational lack of ownership, combined with structural barriers like organisational fragmentation and heterogeneous practice needs, hindered sustainable implementation.

Complementary roles of teleconsultations and joint consultations

Our findings indicate that teleconsultations and joint consultations fulfil different but complementary roles in bridging the gap between primary and secondary care in order to ensure continuity of care. Professionals reported submitting different types of questions to each type of consultation, based on their clinical judgement. Teleconsultations were used for relatively straightforward questions, while joint consultations were used for complex cases requiring dialogue and shared reasoning. These findings suggest that the two models should not be viewed as competing alternatives but as complementary tools within a broader collaboration framework.

Comparison with existing literature

Our findings align with previous qualitative research whereby teleconsultations are perceived as efficient and effective by both healthcare professionals and from the patient perspective, often leading to faster decision-making and improved quality of care compared with telephone consultations or standard referrals.13 20 21 They enabled timely specialist input in situations of uncertainty, potentially preventing unnecessary referrals, again consistent with earlier findings.22 23 Prior research has also demonstrated improvements in interprofessional collaboration and potential cost savings.16 24 25 In contrast to much of the existing literature, this study examined teleconsultation and joint consultation in a broader context, encompassing both primary and secondary care and including teleconsultations across a wide range of medical specialties. This approach revealed substantial variability in indication for use, a pronounced need for dialogue and a lack of structured collaboration between professionals to optimise these consultation models. Regarding joint consultations, previous studies have similarly shown promise in improving efficiency and care quality.26 27 Early studies, including joint consultations with orthopaedic surgeons in the mid-1990s, already reported positive outcomes.28 More recent regional evaluations in the Netherlands and Norway further support these findings.29 30 However, we identified only one study that explicitly examined implementation barriers and facilitators of joint consultations for frail older adults.31 Main barriers identified in this previous study were mainly related to tool characteristics and context of use. Our study adds to this previous study by providing insight into implementation barriers and facilitators from both primary and secondary care professionals and helps explain why consultation models that are experienced so positively struggle to scale and embed in routine practice. Behavioural barriers identified were loss of motivation due to unfair reward, dependence on the motivation of the caregiver, cognitive dissonance regarding the discrepancy between guideline and the specialist’s advice. Moreover, most studies on teleconsultations and joint consultations devote limited attention to more structural barriers like accountability and responsibility for clinical advice and subsequent management. Our findings demonstrate considerable uncertainty among professionals regarding where liability lies and how this is formally arranged. Recent work has provided general guidance on improving clarity around accountability in digital healthcare innovations,32 but practical implementation remains challenging.

Strengths and weaknesses

A key strength of this study was the use of CFIR, which enabled a structured exploration of implementation determinants across all relevant domains beyond the clinical domain and provided a comprehensive understanding of professionals’ experiences at the organisational, policy and financial level. In addition, interviewing both GPs and MS from multiple disciplines strengthened the analysis by incorporating perspectives from both primary and secondary care. Several limitations should be considered. First, only healthcare professionals were interviewed, while patient perspectives were inferred indirectly through GPs. Although it was a deliberate choice to first understand how end users experience the interventions, inclusion of patients and other stakeholders through interviews or focus groups would have provided a more comprehensive view and supported the development of targeted implementation strategies. Second, as joint consultations in particular were less often used, not all participating GPs and MS had experience with both consultation types and could therefore reflect on only a single type.

Implications for clinicians and policymakers

To develop and sustain teleconsultations and joint consultations, attention should be paid to the behavioural, organisational and structural barriers. For instance, to overcome the organisational barrier of a lack of clear agreements and unclear expectations between GPs and MS, a possible solution is the formulation of well-defined working agreements, as well as focusing on behavioural barriers by building personal relationships and mutual respect between professionals.33 34 This process can begin during interprofessional education, which has expanded substantially in recent years.35 36 However, it is particularly important that healthcare systems actively facilitate opportunities for professionals to articulate expectations towards one another regarding roles, responsibilities and modes of collaboration. Unclear responsibilities could affect the adoption of new innovations.

Limited organisational support remains a major barrier to scaling joint consultations. Overcoming this requires close collaboration with professionals to identify practical, workflow-compatible implementation strategies and system-level changes of appropriate reimbursement. One potential solution to the challenge faced by smaller general practices, where case volumes are often insufficient to justify dedicated joint consultations, could be the organisation of centralised joint consultation time blocks at the specialist level. In such a model, GPs could digitally schedule short time slots, for example, an initial 10 min session to discuss the case and determine an appropriate diagnostic work-up, followed by a second 15 min session to review results and formulate advice. This approach could enable multiple, including smaller, practices to benefit from digital joint consultations while maintaining efficiency for specialists. Both teleconsultations and joint consultations are hindered by the siloed organisation of primary and secondary care, including divergent policies and financing arrangements. More structural changes, including alternative payment models that prioritise continuity of care rather than siloed service delivery may help address these barriers. In addition, teleconsultations are often facilitated through external platforms, making adaptations complex or costly, and their effectiveness is constrained by limited access to shared patient information. A shared patient record could offer a potential solution, although such systems also introduce challenges, as described previously.37 The formal embedding of teleconsultation (in Dutch: meedenkadviezen) as a nationwide healthcare service with uniform reimbursement by the Dutch Healthcare Authority (NZa) in January 2026 constitutes a policy, laying the groundwork for strengthening continuity of care.38 Nevertheless, the newly introduced eligibility and exclusion criteria are overly restrictive and fail to encompass several clinically relevant indications identified in this study, including the evaluation of additional diagnostic needs and the management of patients with chronic conditions receiving care across multiple healthcare settings. Moreover, limiting teleconsultation to a single interaction for each care episode fundamentally contradicts the prescribed need in this study for interactive consultation.39 Collectively, these regulatory constraints may compromise the accessible and collaborative nature of teleconsultation, thereby limiting its capacity to optimise continuity of care. These consultation models have largely evolved from the bottom up. However, for their broader standardised implementation, as well as for the development of supporting technical infrastructure and sustainable financing mechanisms, a shared vision and a system-level cultural shift are required. The need for digital collaborative models, such as teleconsultation and joint consultation, that promote continuity of care is widely recognised; the primary challenge now lies in effective implementation.

Unanswered questions

Sustainable implementation strategies need to be identified that address primary barriers, including organisational barriers (eg, moving beyond the distinction between primary and secondary care patients, and embedding training on how to use and integrate these care modalities within professional education), financial barriers (such as alternative payment models including shared savings models40), technical barriers (including real-time shared patient data) and relational barriers (developing personal relationships between professionals). Finally, alternative communication models should be explored that facilitate asynchronous, low-threshold real-time interactive dialogue and enable easy coordination around shared patients and information exchange. Such models could help bridge current gaps concerning clinical questions and coordination needs that fall outside existing eligibility criteria for teleconsultation and joint consultations.

Conclusion

Teleconsultations and joint consultations are positively perceived by GPs and MS and address a real need for accessible, low-threshold professional communication to support continuity and quality of care. Although these consultation types serve different purposes, together they contribute to strengthening collaboration between primary and secondary care to ensure continuity of care. Despite these benefits, sustainable implementation and scaling remain constrained by organisational, technical, financial and governance-related barriers. Moreover, the wide variety of questions being asked highlights a shortage of effective communication opportunities. To fully leverage the potential of digital collaborative consultation models, healthcare systems should invest in clearer agreements on roles and responsibilities, interoperable information systems, flexible reimbursement models and organisational support that enables professionals to collaboratively shape and embed these consultation models in daily practice.
