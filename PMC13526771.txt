The m⁶A reader YTHDF2 regulates mRNA transport and axon outgrowth in developing neurons
Authors: Bonsang Koo, Ajeet Kumar, Huiseon Hwang, Minyeong Park, Sung-Min Lee, Sung Ho Boo, Ki-Heon Lee, Ji-Hoon Moon, Jaewon Yang, Chan-Woo Park, Saebom Lee, Hee-Jung Jo, Woo-il Kim, Eunji Cho, Hagyeong Lee, Chang-Won Lee, Jae-Sung Woo, Jong Hyuk Yoon, Yoon Ki Kim, Ki-Jun Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProper nervous system development is critical for brain function, and deficits in neural development are implicated in many brain disorders. Neurons are distinctly polarized cells where mRNA can be transported to distal structures like axons and dendrites. Recent discoveries of widespread mRNA chemical modifications raise the question of their post-transcriptional regulatory role in brain development and function. N6-methyladenosine (m6A), installed by the METTL3/METTL14 methyltransferase complex, is the most prevalent internal mRNA modification, influencing stability, translation, splicing, and localization. However, the impact of m6A modification on RNA transport in developing neurons is not well understood. In this study, we find that the ablation of Mettl14 in postmitotic neurons leads to impaired axonal projection during corticogenesis. RNA-seq and single-molecule in situ hybridization reveal mislocalization of mRNAs in neurites of neurons with m6A loss-of-function. Furthermore, m6A-SAC-seq to identify a single nucleotide resolution m6A maps in the perinatal brain uncovers m6A-tagged transcripts associated with synapse organization, mRNA processing, and axonogenesis. We also identify YTHDF2 as the reader protein responsible for mRNA transport in callosal projection axons. YTHDF2 interacts with motor proteins, translational regulators, and microtubules to facilitate distal transport of m6A-tagged mRNA. Our data suggest that FMRP may serve as a context-guiding interactor that reshapes the YTHDF2 complex by recruiting specific cofactors and motor proteins, thereby promoting transport rather than degradation of m⁶A-tagged transcripts. Together, these findings provide insight into the epitranscriptomic mechanisms governing axon projection and guidance during mammalian cortical neurogenesis.Subject terms: RNA modification, Neuronal development, Developmental neurogenesis

Introduction

Neurodevelopment is a precisely orchestrated process wherein neural stem cells sequentially give rise to various types of neurons and glia, which then migrate and integrate into distinct regions of the nervous system. Precise regulation of gene expression is crucial for many steps of developmental processes, where mRNA modifications, particularly N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification, play a pivotal role in post-transcriptional regulation. m6A modification, the most prevalent internal mRNA modification in eukaryotic organisms, is involved in various aspects of neurodevelopment1,2. For example, the absence of m6A induces the accumulation of m6A-tagged mRNAs related to temporal and cell-type specific transcription factors, leading to delayed transitions in developmental competency3. Additionally, spatiotemporal progression of neurogenesis is significantly impaired in knockout mice lacking YTHDF2, an m6A reader4,5. These results suggest that dynamic mRNA m6A modification adds an additional layer to gene expression regulation during neurodevelopment, with dysregulation contributing to various neurological problems6,7.

Neurons are highly polarized cells with extensive neuronal processes including dendrites and axons. Recent studies with advanced sequencing technology and high throughput mRNA imaging have revealed a myriad of different mRNA molecules in neuronal processes, suggesting that RNA localization is a prevalent method to sort out proteins to distinct subcellular compartments of neurons8–10. In neurons, long-distance transport of mRNAs to distal subcellular compartments allows dynamic local protein translation upon external stimuli, which is essential for activity-dependent neuronal wiring during neurodevelopment and synaptic plasticity in the adult brain. Many studies have also shown that local translation of specific mRNA subsets at the axon terminal is required for axonal growth during development11,12 as well as in mature brains10. The general principles for targeting an mRNA to a specific subcellular location are still elusive, but several molecular mechanisms suggest that cis-elements of mRNA serve as critical signals for trafficking to dendrites and axons. For example, regulatory elements depending on primary sequence or secondary structure in 3’UTRs of mRNA are essential for active transport by facilitating the anchoring of RNA and associated RNA-binding proteins to cytoskeletons13. It has also been suggested that m6A modification may serve as a cis-regulatory element for mRNA distal trafficking in neurons. Notably, a recent study reported that m6A-modified mRNAs are restricted to the soma due to m6A-mediated enhanced degradation (passive regulation)14. In contrast, other studies have proposed that m6A modification in the 3’UTR facilitates the distal translocation of these mRNAs (active regulation)15,16. Therefore, further mechanistic investigations are necessary to understand m6A-mediated regulation of mRNA transport in light of these contradictory observations in diverse physiological contexts. In addition, the precise m6A-mediated mechanism for recruiting RNA binding proteins (RBPs) and motor proteins to regulate mRNA trafficking processes, remains complex and not fully elucidated.

In this study, we elucidate the critical role of m6A modification in axon projection and neurite development during corticogenesis. Employing a single nucleotide resolution m6A profiling using m6A-selective allyl chemical labeling and sequencing (m6A-SAC-seq), we unveil the quantity and stoichiometry of m6A modification in the developing brain in vivo. Investigation into the stability and axonal localization of m6A-tagged mRNAs suggests that m6A modification in 3’UTR, along with its reader YTHDF2, facilitates the distal translocation of target mRNAs in neurons. Through analysis of YTHDF2 interactome in the perinatal brain, we identified the YTHDF2-transport complex, which includes motor proteins and FMRP, as functionally essential for transporting target mRNAs bearing m6A tagging.

Results

Mettl14 ablation leads to impaired axon projection and reduced growth cone advance

A previous study using Nestin-Cre; Mettl14f/f (M14 cKO) mice demonstrated the critical role of m6A methylation in mammalian cortical neurogenesis3. While gene expression regulation by m6A mRNA modification in neural progenitor cells has been extensively studied, the in vivo functional roles of m6A in differentiating newborn neurons have remained unexplored. To investigate this, we performed in utero electroporation of plasmid vectors expressing Cre recombinase (hCRE-IRES-GFP-RV)17 and RFP at E14.5 into the somatosensory cortex and monitored differentiating neurons at P5 (Fig. 1a). Electroporated neurons were mostly SATB2+ upper layer neurons and migrated into layer 2/3 at P5 both in control (Mettl14+/f) and M14 cKO (Mettl14f/f), suggesting that METTL14 depletion did not affect neuronal subtype specification and neuronal migration in this experimental setting (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c). Callosal axons originating from Cre-electroporated projection neurons traversed the corpus callosum (CC) to reach the contralateral hemisphere, forming axonal arbors. However, axon projections of M14 cKO neurons exhibited reduced axon projections in cortical layers and arborization on the contralateral side compared to the control group at P5 (Fig. 1a–c). Additionally, to mitigate potential secondary effects resulting from m6A depletion in neural progenitor cells (NPCs), we conducted in utero electroporation at E14.5 using a neuron-specific Cre driver plasmid under the control of the Doublecortin (Dcx) promoter, driving the expression of both Cre recombinase and EGFP (Dcx-CRE-IRES-GFP)18, and analyzed at P5. The results are consistent with our findings using hCRE-IRES-GFP-RV, showing reduced axon projection and disrupted arborization during the corticogenesis upon m6A depletion (Supplementary Fig.1d–f). Furthermore, we found that axon projections and arborization were more dramatically reduced contralaterally after the Mettl14 depletion in postmitotic neurons compared to the control at P14 (Fig. 1d, e).

To further examine the effects of m6A depletion on postmitotic differentiating neurons, we cultured primary cortical neurons after acute Mettl14 deletion. Following complete cell adhesion and the initiation of polarization (day in vitro 1, DIV1), we infected the neurons with AAV-mCherry-IRES-Cre viruses and analyzed at DIV6, a time point when neurons typically display well-defined axon-dendrite polarity19. We confirmed a similar population of neuronal subpopulation between control and cKO primary neurons (Supplementary Fig. 1g, h), despite efficient depletion of Mettl14 and m6A by AAV-mCherry-IRES-Cre virus infection in the primary culture (Supplementary Fig. 1i-j). We observed a significant reduction in the longest neurite length of M14 cKO neurons and an increase in axon collateral branching compared with control neurons at DIV6 (Fig. 1f–h), suggesting that the neurite development is impaired after m6A depletion. Last, we also examined the dynamics of growth cones by live imaging, which revealed that Mettl14 deletion significantly reduced the velocity of growth cone advance and increased transient protrusion formation compared to the control at DIV6 (Fig. 1i–k, and Supplementary Movie 1,2). Collectively, these findings indicate that m6A RNA modification plays an essential role in axon projection, neurite development, and branching, as well as normal growth cone dynamics during neuronal differentiation.

A single nucleotide resolution, quantitative m6A mapping reveals transcripts related to axon development with high m6A stoichiometry

To investigate the landscape of the m6A epitranscriptome in the brain at single nucleotide resolution, we used m6A-SAC-seq on the P0 mouse forebrain (Supplementary Fig. 2a)20. To identify METTL14-dependent m6A sites, we filtered out peaks according to the previous protocol21, using m6A-SAC-seq data from WT forebrain and M14 cKO forebrain with 2 biological replicates for each (Supplementary Fig. 2b). We identified 10,304 m6A sites in 3,830 genes, which showed enrichment at the DRACH motif and a stop codon-proximal 3’UTR distribution, consistent with previous reports (Fig. 2a, b, and Supplementary Data 1). We also confirmed that our m6A-modified transcripts highly overlapped with previously reported m6A-modified transcripts in a neuronal context16, suggesting the consistency of m6A targets across different methods (Supplementary Fig. 2c). The m6A-modified transcripts showed strong enrichment for genes associated with synapse organization, axonogenesis, positive regulation of cell projection organization, and regulation of synapse, implying that m6A-modified RNAs play a key role in neuronal differentiation during early postnatal cortical development (Fig. 2c).

We further investigated the m6A-SAC-seq result, including characteristics of stoichiometries and the position of m6A sites. Interestingly, the average m6A stoichiometry in 3’UTR is higher than in the CDS and the 5’UTR (Fig. 2d). This higher methylation stoichiometry in 3’UTR is a unique characteristic of the brain epitranscriptome compared to previously reported m6A-SAC-seq results in HeLa and HEK293 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2d)20. We also performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis22 with genes categorized by m6A position or stoichiometry. Although intronic and long non-coding methylated transcripts showed enrichment for genes associated with RNA processing, other positions, including CDS, 5’UTR, and 3’UTR, did not show differential enrichment (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Furthermore, average methylation stoichiometry among transcripts also did not display differential enrichment between different transcripts set (Supplementary Fig. 2f). However, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed that high m6A stoichiometry was significantly and positively related to regulation of synapse assembly, nervous system development, and axon development (Fig. 2e, and Supplementary Fig. 2g).

Next, we performed an RNA stability assay to determine the effects of m6A methylation in a neuronal context (Supplementary Data 2). As previously described3, cultured primary cortical neurons from WT and M14 cKO mice were treated with Actinomycin D, and RNA-seq was performed at 0 and 5 h to estimate the RNA stability of each transcript. Across the transcriptome, m6A-modified transcripts showed increased relative stability in the M14 cKO condition compared to WT (Supplementary Fig. 3a), and the number of m6A sites affected the stability (Supplementary Fig. 3b). We utilized m6A position data and found that depletion of m6A in the 3’UTR stabilized transcripts more than depletions in the CDS and 5’UTR (Fig. 2f). Moreover, the stability of transcripts with m6A only in the CDS changed slightly less, while the stability of transcripts with m6A only in the 3’UTR or with m6A in both regions increased more significantly, suggesting that m6A in the 3’UTR has a more pronounced effect on mRNA stability (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Interestingly, based on m6A stoichiometry data, we found that transcripts with high m6A stoichiometry were less stabilized than transcripts with low m6A stoichiometry in the M14 cKO neurons (Fig. 2g). This tendency became more evident when comparing the top 10% of m6A stoichiometry transcripts to the bottom 10% (Supplementary Fig. 3d). Altogether, these results support that m6A-tagged transcripts are directly related to neurodevelopmental processes and axon development in the postnatal brain, which are dysregulated in the M14 cKO neurons by increased mRNA stability. Of note, the results of m6A-SAC-seq and mRNA stability assay imply that high m6A stoichiometry transcripts related to axon development (Fig. 2h, and Supplementary Fig. 3e) are not solely regulated at the level of RNA stability as previously described14. This suggests that m⁶A may exert additional functions beyond promoting mRNA degradation, potentially influencing mRNA localization, translation efficiency, or RNP complex stabilization in neurons.

Mettl14 loss-of-function reduced a subset of transcripts in neurites in differentiating neurons

To examine the functional contribution of m6A modification in developing axons, we conducted RNA-seq using microdissected tissue from the axon-enriched CC of cortical projection neurons (layer 2/3) at P4 (referred to as CC-seq), a time-point at which the maximum number of axons are distally projected in the callosal region and remain in the white matter before entering the cortical plates (Supplementary Fig. 4a)23. The enrichment of previously reported axonally localized mRNAs, such as Abce1, Gtpbp4, and Npm124, was confirmed by qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 4b). As a result, we identified 1,864 decreased transcripts and 1,761 increased transcripts in the CC of M14 cKO compared to that of WT (Fig. 3a, and Supplementary Data 3).

To further investigate the relationship between RNA stability and localization, we compared our axon-enriched CC-seq results with the RNA stability assay in WT and M14 cKO primary neurons. We categorized the mRNAs in each quadrant (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4; Fig. 3b) based on changes in stability and axonal localization upon Mettl14 ablation. For example, Q1 represents mRNAs that are more stabilized, but their axonal localization is also increased in the M14 cKO condition. Q2 represents mRNAs that are more stabilized, and their axonal localization is significantly reduced in the M14 cKO condition. mRNAs in Q1 and Q2 groups (stability difference > 0.5) are both related to axonogenesis and synaptic development (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). However, while GO analysis of the Q1 group showed similar GO terms regardless of m6A tagging as revealed by m6A-SAC-seq, the Q2 group displayed distinct GO terms depending on the presence of m6A tagging, including axonogenesis and growth cone (Fig. 3c). These findings suggest that m6A modification may not only regulate stability but also the axonal localization of mRNAs related to axonogenesis and growth cone.

To examine whether m6A-modified transcripts fail to localize to neurites under the M14 cKO condition, we selected target transcripts by comparing our CC-seq, m6A-SAC-seq, and RNA stability assay dataset to previous datasets of axonally localized RNA (Fig. 3d)25. For the control experiments, we selected Gap43 and Camk2b mRNAs, which have m6A peaks but whose mRNA abundance in axons was not significantly changed by Mettl14 ablation in CC-seq (Fig. 3a). To investigate whether m6A tagging plays a role in the localization of our target transcripts during neuronal differentiation, we performed single-molecule in situ hybridization (smFISH) to visualize endogenous Gap43, Camk2b, Ncam1, Cntn1, Ank2, and Actb mRNAs (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 3e) in control and M14 cKO conditions (Fig. 3e). We measured the distance from the soma and the relative density of smFISH signals in neurites, and found that Ncam1, Cntn1, Ank2, and Actb mRNAs were reduced in neurites in M14 cKO compared to the control at DIV6. However, levels of Gap43 and Camk2b mRNAs remained unchanged (Fig. 3e–g, and Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). Consequently, the axonal protein levels of NCAM1 and ANK2 were significantly reduced at growth cones, indicating that m6A-mediated mRNA transport is essential for proper axonal expression of these target proteins (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b).

To directly visualize RNA transport through neurites, we employed the MS2 system26. We used HA-tdMCP-GFP and BFP–actin–3′UTR–24 × MS2 reporters containing a conserved m⁶A site in the 3′UTR (at the 1217th position, located 5nt downstream of the stop codon) (Fig. 3h). RNA dynamics were monitored in control and Mettl14 cKO neurons using a spinning-disk microscope. Interestingly, anterograde RNA movement was significantly reduced under M14 cKO conditions (Fig. 3i, j, and Supplementary Movie 3,4). Moreover, the total motile time of RNA was also reduced under M14 cKO conditions (Fig. 3k). To examine the direct role of m⁶A modification in RNA transport, we generated a mutant reporter, BFP–actin–3′UTRΔm⁶A–24 × MS2, by introducing an A → G substitution at position 1217 to prevent m⁶A modification. RNA granule movement was then compared between the wild-type and Δm⁶A reporters in mouse primary neurons. As a result, anterograde RNA movement was markedly reduced in the Δm⁶A reporter (Fig. 3l, m, and Supplementary Movie 5,6). Also, the total motile time of RNA was reduced in the Δm⁶A reporter (Fig. 3n). These findings demonstrate that a single m⁶A modification within the 3′UTR is critical for efficient anterograde transport of Actb mRNAs in developing neurons.

We also conducted live imaging to examine the transport of lysosomes through neurites in M14 cKO and control groups at DIV6. We did not observe a significant alteration in lysosome transport, indicating that the general transport machinery was unaffected by m6A depletion (Supplementary Fig. 5e–h, and Supplementary Movie 7,8).

Taken together, although m6A tagging generally restricts the stability of target mRNAs, m6A modification also has an additional function to positively promote neurite localization of mRNAs related to axonogenesis and growth cone regulation, which is essential for normal axonogenesis.

YTHDF2 is an essential reader that regulates the transport of m6A-tagged mRNA during axon development

The fates of m6A-tagged mRNAs largely depend on the functions of reader protein27. The YTHDF reader protein family, which is the most well-studied, interacts with m6A through their conserved YTH domain, with all three members (YTHDF1, 2, and 3) regulating the translation or decay of target mRNAs in the cytosol28. In non-neuronal cells, it has been shown that YTHDF reader proteins regulate subcellular mRNA localization, including P-bodies and stress granules29. To investigate which reader protein directly engages with m6A-tagged transcripts in axon projection, we evaluated the expression patterns of YTHDF1, YTHDF2, and YTHDF3 during corticogenesis. Although YTHDF2 is abundantly expressed in the embryonic cortex, the expression levels of YTHDF1 and YTHDF3 are very low according to the single-cell transcriptome atlas (Supplementary Fig. 6a)30. Moreover, Ythdf1 or Ythdf3 knockout mice did not exhibit obvious developmental abnormalities4. In contrast, Ythdf2 null knockout mice exhibited embryonic lethality and impaired cortical development5. Furthermore, YTHDF1 and YTHDF3 knockdown did not alter the dynamics of growth cones and protrusion formation in cultured primary cortical neurons (Supplementary Fig. 6b–e). Therefore, we generated Ythdf2 conditional knockout mice by deleting Ythdf2 in the developing mouse nervous system using the Nestin-Cre; Ythdf2f/f cKO model (DF2 cKO) (Supplementary Fig. 6f). YTHDF2 depletion was confirmed by Western blot analysis of protein lysate from DF2 cKO and control mice at P0 (Supplementary Fig. 6g). DF2 cKO mice exhibited decreased body weight and sub-viability, with ~30% failing to survive beyond P30 (Supplementary Fig. 6h, i). Additionally, we observed a significant decrease in forelimb grip strength in DF2 cKO mice compared to controls at P30 (Supplementary Fig. 6j), indicating that YTHDF2 is crucial for normal postnatal development of the nervous system.However, we found that the cytoarchitecture of cortical layer-specific neurons appeared grossly normal compared to control mice (Supplementary Fig. 6k).

To track the axonal projection of DF2 cKO neurons in detail, we performed in utero electroporation of plasmid vectors expressing Cre recombinase with GFP at E14.5 into the somatosensory cortex and monitored differentiating neurons at P5 (Fig. 4a). Electroporated neurons were primarily SATB2+ upper-layer neurons and migrated into layers 2/3 at P5 in both cKO (Ythdf2f/f) and control (Ythdf2f/+) mice. We observed that the axonogenesis of cortical projection neurons was significantly reduced in the white matter and cortical layer entry on the contralateral side (Fig. 4b-c).

Moreover, to validate the influence of Ythdf2 loss-of-function in postmitotic neurons, we infected primary neurons from Ythdf2f/f or Ythdf2+/f mice with AAV-mCherry-IRES-Cre viruses and analyzed them at DIV6, respectively. We performed live imaging experiments to investigate growth cone mobility and dynamics, and found that Ythdf2 deletion notably reduced growth cone dynamics and increased transient protrusion formation compared to the control at DIV6 (Fig. 4d-f, Supplementary Fig. 7a, Supplementary Movie 9,10). These results suggest that YTHDF2 is crucial for axon projection, neurite development and branching, and growth cone dynamics during corticogenesis31.

It is well known that YTHDF2 specifically interacts with the m6A-tagged mRNA and leads to its degradation. YTHDF2-depleted zebrafish embryos have an extended lifetime of the YTHDF2-targeted mRNAs32. Therefore, we examined the RNA stability of m6A-tagged RNAs in primary cortical neurons3. Unexpectedly, m6A-modified transcripts showed only a slight stability change in the DF2 cKO condition (Fig. 4g, Supplementary Data 2). Altogether, we hypothesized that YTHDF2 does not simply degrade RNAs in neuronal context and may protect and promote neurite localization of selected mRNA.

We used smFISH to visualize the localization of Gap43, Ncam1, Cntn1, Ank2, and Actb mRNAs in both control and DF2 cKO conditions (Fig. 4i, and Supplementary Fig. 7b). In the DF2 cKO condition, Ncam1, Cntn1, and Ank2 mRNAs were found to be reduced in neurites compared to the control, while Gap43 mRNA remained statistically unchanged (Fig. 4j-k, and Supplementary Fig. 7c). Additionally, we observe Ythdf1 or Ythdf3 knockdown does not affect RNA localization (Supplementary Fig. 7d, e). These findings indicate that the loss of YTHDF2 function has a negative impact on selected mRNA localization in neurites, consistent with previous results observed in m6A depletion conditions (Fig. 3f, g).

Next, we used HA-tdMCP-GFP and BFP–actin–3′UTR–24 × MS2 reporters described above to visualize the RNA transport (Fig. 3h). The dynamics of reporter RNA granules were monitored in control and DF2 cKO neurons using a spinning-disk microscope. As a result, anterograde RNA movement was significantly reduced under DF2 cKO conditions (Fig. 4l–n, and Supplementary Movie 11, 12). Moreover, the total motile time of RNA granules was also reduced under DF2 cKO conditions (Fig. 4n), consistent with previous results in the m6A depletion condition and the m6A mutant reporter (Fig. 3k, n).

Collectively, the DF2 cKO model shows significant neurodevelopmental defects at the in vivo and in vitro levels. m⁶A-modified mRNAs exhibit transcript-specific changes in stability in the absence of YTHDF2, and YTHDF2 promotes the anterograde localization of specific target mRNAs to neurites. Therefore, YTHDF2 is a key reader protein that selectively protects m6A-modified transcripts and localizes them to neurites.

YTHDF2 is associated with RNA trafficking machinery in the developing brain

To identify the m6A-dependent protein-interacting complex of YTHDF2 in the developing brain, we conducted co-immunoprecipitation with YTHDF2 in the P0 forebrain, comparing WT and M14 cKO mice, followed by mass spectrometry analysis (Fig. 5a). Upon depletion of METTL14, 281 proteins showed reduced interaction, while 109 proteins exhibited increased interaction (Fig. 5b, and Supplementary Data 4). Gene ontology-based cluster comparison analysis and gene-concept network analysis revealed that m6A-dependent protein interactors are involved in cytoskeleton constitution, cytoskeleton binding, and mRNA 3’UTR binding. Notably, KIF5C, a kinesin protein specifically expressed in the nervous system33, was identified as one of the interacting proteins. Additionally, microtubule components, including TUBB2B, TUBB3, and MAP2, were found to be associated with YTHDF2 in an m6A-dependent manner. In contrast, proteins that interact with YTHDF2 independently of m6A are primarily associated with mRNA binding or transcription corepressor binding (Fig. 5c, d). To validate our LC-MS/MS results, we performed co-immunoprecipitation with YTHDF2, and examined the pull-down affinity of associated proteins, TUBB3, KIF5C, and FMRP (Fig. 5e–g). We performed Co-IP with anti-YTHDF2 antibody in P0 forebrain lysate of Mettl14 WT and cKO to validate the interaction with TUBB3 (Fig. 5e). We found that TUBB3 interacts with YTHDF2 with the presence of m6A specifically. Also, we performed Co-IP with anti-GFP antibody to pulldown GFP fused with YTHDF2 after co-transfection with KIF5C-myc and FMRP-HA in Neuro2A cells and HEK293T cells respectively (Fig. 5f, g). These experiments showed that both KIF5C and FMRP are interacting with YTHDF2 clearly. With these results, we validated that our LC-MS/MS data (Fig. 5a–d) is well representing the interactome of YTHDF2 with m6A.

To further investigate the association between YTHDF2 and microtubules, we conducted proximity ligation assay (PLA) experiments between YTHDF2 and TUBB3, a neuron-specific tubulin, in primary cortical neurons from WT and M14 cKO mice. The results revealed a significant reduction in PLA signals in the axons of M14 cKO neurons compared to those in the axons of WT neurons. This observation suggests that m6A modification is crucial for the interaction between YTHDF2 and microtubules in the axons (Fig. 5h, j). Notably, we did not detect an interaction between YTHDF1 and microtubules in Tau-positive axons, in contrast to YTHDF2 (Supplementary Fig. 8a–b). We next hypothesize this interaction is mediated by neuron-specific motor proteins such as KIF5C. To address this, we performed YTHDF2-TUBB3 PLA after the knockdown of Kif5c in primary cortical neurons. As a result, PLA signals in the neurites were significantly reduced in the Kif5c knockdown condition compared to the control condition (Fig. 5k–m, Supplementary Fig. 8g, h). Next, we also performed YTHDF2-KIF5C PLA in WT and M14 cKO neurons, observed reduced interactions between YTHDF2 and KIF5C in axons (Fig. 5n, o). Again, we did not detect a robust interaction between YTHDF1 and KIF5C in neurites (Supplementary Fig. 8c). These results suggest that the neuron-specific motor protein KIF5C connects YTHDF2 with microtubules to promote mRNA transport in a m6A-dependent manner.

Taken together, these findings suggest that YTHDF2 interacts with RNA trafficking machinery and microtubules in an m6A-dependent manner to facilitate mRNA transport to the neurites.

FMRP is the context-guiding interactor for YTHDF2 for axonal RNA transport in neurons

Next, we hypothesized that YTHDF2 associates with other RBPs and selectively localizes mRNA to the neurites depending on target RNA characteristics, including m6A and 3’UTR sequence. When we examined m6A stoichiometry across different RBP target mRNAs, FMRP target mRNAs exhibited significantly higher m6A stoichiometry than non-target mRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 9a). To further evaluate our model, we performed motif enrichment analysis on sequences proximal to m6A sites within the 3′UTRs of the Q2 and Q1 groups (Fig. 3b)31. As a result, we found FMRP binding motifs were enriched in Q2 3’UTR sequences, particularly near m6A sites, compared with Q1 3′UTR sequences (Fig. 6a, and Supplementary Fig. 9b).

FMRP, an RNA-binding protein and translation repressor, is also known to regulate RNA transport25,34,35. It has been reported that YTHDF proteins interact with FMRP to translationally suppress target mRNAs crucial for proper synaptic function and axonal growth6,31,36. As FMRP has been identified as an m6A-dependent protein interactor of YTHDF2 (Fig. 5b), we validated the interaction between YTHDF2 and FMRP in primary cortical neurons using PLA. Previously, YTHDF2 was shown to interact with the mRNA deadenylase complex, including CNOT1, to promote mRNA degradation37. Intriguingly, PLA signals for YTHDF2-FMRP were observed in both the soma and neurites, whereas signals for YTHDF2-CNOT1 were exclusively detected in the perinuclear soma (Fig. 6b-c). In addition, knockdown of Fmr1 significantly increased the interaction between YTHDF2 and CNOT1 (Fig. 6d–e). These results indicate the presence of two distinct YTHDF2-containing complexes: an RNA transport complex with FMRP and an RNA degradation complex with CNOT1, with FMRP competitively inhibiting the association of m⁶A-tagged mRNA/YTHDF2 with the RNA degradation machinery.

To evaluate the functional significance of FMRP in the association of YTHDF2 with microtubules, we conducted a PLA experiment between YTHDF2 and TUBB3 in control shRNA (shNS) and shFmr1-treated mouse primary neurons. The results revealed a significant decrease in YTHDF2-TUBB3 PLA signals in the axons of Fmr1 knockdown neurons compared to those in control neurons (Fig. 6f, g, and Supplementary Fig. 9d, e), suggesting that FMRP facilitates the microtubule association of YTHDF2. Conversely, FMRP-TUBB3 PLA signals were markedly reduced in the axons of Ythdf2 knockout neurons compared to those in control neurons (Fig. 6h–I, and Supplementary Fig. 9f, g). In addition, the YTHDF2–KIF5C PLA signal was reduced in shFmr1-treated neurons, indicating that FMRP facilitates the association of YTHDF2 with motor proteins in axons (Fig. 6m–n). Collectively, these findings suggest a cooperative interaction between YTHDF2 and FMRP that links m⁶A-tagged mRNAs to microtubules and motor proteins within neurites.

Next, smFISH was employed to investigate the mRNA localization of Ncam1, Cntn1, Ank2, and Actb in primary cortical neurons derived from both WT and Fmr1 knockout mice. Indeed, these experiments revealed a notable reduction in the neurite localization of m6A-tagged Ncam1 mRNA and Ank2 mRNA in Fmr1 knockout neurons compared to control neurons (Fig. 6j-k). We note that we do not exclude the possibility that FMRP partially regulates the localization of these mRNAs in a YTHDF2-independent manner, as FMRP is well known to participate in multiple RNA regulatory processes in the neuron6,25,31,34–36.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that FMRP cooperates with YTHDF2 and plays a crucial role in facilitating the efficient transport of m6A-tagged mRNAs to neurites.

Discussion

RNA transport in neurons is a fundamental process critical for dynamically regulating gene expression within different subcellular compartments. Unlike most other cells, neurons often exhibit extensive cellular architecture with long processes, such as axons and dendrites, which can span considerable distances. Various RNA-binding proteins and molecular motors, such as kinesins and dyneins, facilitate the transport of RNA granules along the microtubule within neurites. This transport mechanism delivers mRNAs to specific subcellular locations where protein synthesis is required38. In this study, we revealed that m6A mRNA modification is essential for proper axon projection and neurite development of cortical projection neurons. A single nucleotide resolution m6A mapping and microdissected axon RNA-seq identified m6A-tagged mRNAs related to cytoskeletal regulation. The m6A reader YTHDF2 is critical for promoting the translocation of these m6A-tagged mRNAs to neurites. Mechanistically, the YTHDF2-FMRP transport complex, distinct from the YTHDF2-CNOT1 degradation complex, interacts with motor proteins to facilitate the trafficking of m6A-tagged mRNAs along microtubules in developing neurons. These findings suggest that YTHDF2-mediated switching between m6A effector complexes regulates the stability and localization of target mRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 10).

In general, m6A-tagged mRNAs are subjected to faster degradation by YTHDF proteins28,39. Recently, it has been reported that highly m6A methylated mRNAs are more unstable and less neurite-localized compared to low m6A methylated mRNAs in neurons14, suggesting that m6A acts as a determinant to restrict distal mRNA localization (passive regulation of transport on non-m6A mRNAs). In contrast, other reports suggest that m6A methylation promotes the localization of a subset of neuronal mRNAs to dendrites and axons (active regulation of transport on m6A mRNAs)16,40. In our study, a group of mRNAs displayed both increased stability and axonal localization upon the depletion of m6A in M14 cKO (Fig. 3b, Q1). However, other group of mRNAs showed reduced axonal localization despite their increased stability, suggesting the presence of additional active regulation of transport on these target mRNAs (Fig. 3b, Q2). Surprisingly, high m6A stoichiometric mRNAs displayed less alteration of stability compared with low m6A mRNAs in M14 cKO. Furthermore, DF2 cKO neurons displayed reduced neurite mRNA localization without significant alterations in mRNA stability, implying that YTHDF2 has additional roles beyond passively regulating mRNA stability for mRNA localization (Fig. 4g, h). Indeed, YTHDF2 interacts both with the mRNA degradation complex, including CNOT1, and with the mRNA transport complex, including FMRP and kinesins, but in distinct subcellular compartments (Figs. 5b, f, g, and Fig. 6b, c). Furthermore, FMRP inhibits the interaction of YTHDF2 with the RNA degradation machinery (Fig. 6d, e). These results suggest that m6A-tagged mRNAs are selectively recognized either by the degradation or the transport complex, with FMRP playing a pivotal role in regulating the degradation and transport of m6A-tagged mRNAs. Specifically, FMRP protects a subset of m⁶A-tagged mRNAs from degradation by inhibiting the interaction between YTHDF2 and the RNA degradation machinery, while simultaneously promoting their transport by enhancing the association of YTHDF2 with microtubules and motor proteins (Fig. 6f, g and m, n). Indeed, known FMRP target mRNAs have higher m6A stoichiometry compared to FMRP non-target mRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 9a), and high m6A stoichiometric mRNAs are less affected in their stability compared to low m6A stoichiometric mRNAs upon Mettl14 depletion (Fig. 2g). Consistently, Drosophila YTHDF directly interacts with FMRP to cooperatively inhibit the translation of mRNAs involved in axonal growth regulation31.

Whereas the previous report showed mossy fiber overgrowth in hippocampal neurons lacking Ythdf241, we observed impaired axonal growth in layer 2/3 cortico-cortical projection neurons. This discrepancy may arise from differences in axonal projection length, as long-range projection neurons require extensive mRNA transport to support growth cone navigation, whereas short mossy fibers may not depend on long-distance mRNA trafficking. Consistently, in M14 cKO and DF2 cKO cortical neurons, we observed increased proximal axon collaterals and transient protrusions, but reduced longest-neurite length and slower growth cone navigation (Fig. 1 and Fig. 4). These findings suggest that YTHDF2 plays a predominant role in coordinating mRNA transport and translational repression in long-range projection neurons, thereby promoting efficient growth cone advance while limiting proximal branching. In contrast, YTHDF2 may function more prominently in RNA degradation pathways in short-range projection neurons, where its loss could increase axonal growth by elevating mRNA abundance or protein synthesis in proximal neurites. Together, these observations indicate that YTHDF2 exerts cell-type-specific control over axonal development, warranting further investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying such diversity.

The three YTHDF paralogs—YTHDF1, YTHDF2, and YTHDF3—share highly conserved protein sequences and have been reported to exhibit functional redundancy in several cellular contexts39,42,43. However, emerging evidence suggests that differences in their low-complexity domains and post-translational modifications confer distinct physiological functions44–46. Consistent with this, our data indicate that YTHDF proteins are not functionally redundant in regulating mRNA transport during early neuronal differentiation. Instead, YTHDF2 appears to play a selective and direct role in promoting mRNA localization in neurites. For example, although all three YTHDF proteins are expressed at comparable levels in the postnatal brain (data not shown), DF2 cKO mice exhibit subviability, neurological deficits, and impaired axon projection (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 6), suggesting that YTHDF1 and YTHDF3 do not compensate for the loss of YTHDF2 in vivo. In addition, we find that YTHDF2, but not YTHDF1, associates with neurite microtubules and motor proteins in an m6A-dependent manner, suggesting that the subcellular compartment of YTHDF2 function differs from that of YTHDF1 (Supplementary Fig. 8). Consistent with this, loss of Ythdf2—but not Ythdf1 or Ythdf3—disrupts neurite localization of target mRNAs and impairs growth cone dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 7). A recent study demonstrating that YTHDF1 regulates APC mRNA translation in soma and indirectly modulates axonal mRNA transport via APC-associated ribonucleoprotein complexes7. In contrast, our data support a model in which YTHDF2 directly engages the cytoskeletal transport machinery to mediate the localization of m6A-tagged mRNAs. Notably, neither YTHDF1 nor APC was identified in the YTHDF2 interactome in our in vivo co-immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry analyses (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Data 4), suggesting YTHDF2-transport complex mediates the transport of a distinct set of mRNAs via a separate transport protein complex from the APC-dependent transport machinery. Together, these findings support a model in which YTHDF family proteins function through mechanistically distinct, non-redundant pathways, with YTHDF1 proposed to regulate APC translation in the soma, whereas YTHDF2 directly controls mRNA transport in neurites.

The m6A-modified RNAs and YTHDF reader proteins have recently been suggested as key stickers in liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), generating RNA granules that play a crucial role in neuronal RNA transport29,47–49. Our study shows that the localization of YTHDF2 along the neurite significantly decreases under m6A depletion conditions (Fig. 5h–j), suggesting that YTHDF2 and mRNA may have a lower tendency to be recruited into neuronal RNA granules without m6A. Moreover, the depletion of m6A and the subsequent failure to recruit YTHDF2 may alter the physical properties of neuronal transport granules, hindering efficient granule transport, proper localization, and translation, which requires further investigation in the future. Our research suggests that m6A modification not only regulates gene expression levels through stability control, but also by modulating subcellular localization in neurites during cortical development. Additionally, we identified the transport machinery involving YTHDF2, motor proteins, and the RNA-binding protein FMRP as key players in m6A-mediated mRNA transport. These findings provide insights into how m6A reader proteins differentially interpret m6A tags through reader-complex switching mechanisms, revealing novel epitranscriptomic mechanisms that govern nervous system development and function.

Methods

Mouse models

Mice with a conditionally removable Mettl14 allele3,50 were crossed with a Nestin promoter-driven Cre recombinase transgenic line (B6.Cg-Tg(Nes-cre)1Kln/J; Jackson Laboratory stock: 003771) to conditionally delete Mettl14 in the nervous system. The mice with a conditionally removable Ythdf2 allele51 were received as a gift from Dr. Chuan He and Dr. Xiaoxi Zhuang at the University of Chicago. Fmr1 allele (B6.129P2-Fmr1tm1Cgr/J; Jackson Laboratory stock:003025) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. For embryo stage counting, plugs were checked in the morning, considered as E0.5, and transferred to the surgery room on the day of surgery. For in utero and in vitro electroporation studies, timed pregnant C57BL/6 J (wild type) mice were commercially obtained from KRIBB (Taconic), South Korea. Mice were housed in standard conditions on a 12-h light/dark cycle in a temperature- and humidity-controlled barrier facility and allowed ad libitum access to standard chow and water. The primers used for genotyping are listed in Supplementary Table 1. All procedures were in accordance with the guidelines of and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at KAIST.

Plasmid information

To knockout in vivo and in vitro, hCRE-IRES-GFP-RV18 (From Dr. William Paul at the National Institute of Health), Dcx-CRE-IRES-GFP17 (From Dr. Ulrich Mueller at Johns Hopkins University), CAG-RFP (From Dr. Hongjun Song at the University of Pennsylvania), and pAAV-mCherry-IRES-Cre (Addgene, #55632) were used. To knock down the YTHDF1, YTHDF3, KIF5C, and FMRP, the sh sequence was cloned into the pUEG vector. Further, shFmr1 sequence was cloned into the pUEtdTomato vector (From Dr. Hongjun Song at the University of Pennsylvania)52. For the Co-IP, pT7-EGFP-HsYTHDF2 plasmid (Addgene, #148310) and each of pFRT-HA-FLAG-hFMRP (Addgene, #48690) and pRK5-myc-KIF5C (Addgene, #127618) were used. For the RNA live imaging, we used phage-ubc-nls-ha-tdMCP-gfp (Addgene #30649), and pcDNA-puro-TagBFP-β-ACTIN-3′UTR-24xMS2 (Addgene #205161). For the point-mutant reporter (BFP-Actb-3′UTRΔm⁶A-MS2x24), we used Site-directed Mutagenesis kit (Enzynomics, EZ004S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, the reporter plasmid was amplified with a pair of primers substituting the conserved m6A site with a cytosine residue. Next, PCR product was ligated and transformed into E. coli for the selection and amplification. All detailed sequence information is written in Supplementary Table 2.

Mammalian cell culture and transfection

HEK293T and Neuro2A cell lines were maintained with DMEM high glucose (Welgene, LM 001-05) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, #12483020) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Welgene, LS 202-02) and subcultured every 3-4 days. All the plasmids were transfected with polyethylenimine MAX (PEI; Polysciences, 24765-1) at DNA (μg) to PEI (μl) ratio of 1:3. Cells were harvested 48 h after transfection for RNA or protein to validate knockdown efficiency or perform immunoprecipitation. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Primary culture of mouse cortical neurons

Before processing cortical neuron cultures, glass coverslips (12 mm) were coated with Poly-D-lysine (Gibco, 0.1 mg/ml) for 2-4 h. Subsequently, laminin (Merck) was added, and the coverslips were incubated for an additional 2 h. Mice cortices were isolated from embryos at the P0 stage, using HBSS buffer (Welgene). The meninges were removed from the tissues, which were then washed twice in HBSS buffer. The cortices were triturated and digested in accutase with DNase I for 30 min at 37 °C. Neurobasal media (Gibco) supplemented with B-27 (Gibco), GlutaMAX (Gibco), penicillin-streptomycin (10,000 U/mL, Gibco), and gentamycin (0.01 mg/mL, Sigma) were added. The mixture was centrifuge at 400 g, and the pellet was resuspended in media, filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer (SPL Life Sciences), and the cells were counted and plated on the prepared coverslips. The media was initially changed after 8 h and subsequently every three days. For virus infection, AAV-mCherry-IRES-Cre virus was added to the new culture media, and the plate was returned to the incubator. For further experiments, we fixed the infected neurons with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, GeneAll) at DIV6.

In utero electroporation

In utero electroporation experiments were done following the method previously described3. In brief, pregnant mice at E14.5 were anesthetized with isoflurane for laparotomy and the uterine horns were exposed to manipulate embryos. A 1 µl solution of endotoxin-free plasmid DNA with fast green (20X) at a final concentration of 1.5 µg/µl was injected into the lateral ventricle of the embryos using a calibrated microcapillary in a Pneumatic PicoPump setup (WPI). Electroporation was performed using a Super Electroporator Nepa21 Type II (Nepa Gene), which delivered five transfer pulses at 43 V, with a pulse length of 50 msec, an interval of 450 msec, and a decay rate of 40. The electrodes were positioned over the embryo’s head atop the uterus. After electroporation, the uterus was repositioned in the abdominal cavity and sutured with W581 Mersilk (Ethicon), and povidone-iodine was applied to prevent infection. At P5, the pups were perfused and incubated overnight with 4% PFA. The following day, the 4% PFA was replaced by 15% sucrose in PBS and 30% sucrose until the tissues were completely submerged. Cryosectioning was then performed on coronal sections with a thickness of 30 µm. All procedures involving the handling of mice and pups were conducted following the protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at KAIST.

in vitro electroporation in primary cortical neurons

We have isolated the primary cortical neurons as described in the primary culture of mouse cortical neurons section. After counting the cells, we washed twice with Opti-MEM to remove serum and collected the fixed number of cells (1.0 × 106) in 100μl of Opti-MEM and mixed with 10μg of plasmid DNA. Cells were transferred in EC-002S (green) cuvette (2 mm gap) to proceed with electroporation. Culture plates were ready and placed in an incubator with the neurobasal media with various compositions as described in the primary culture of mouse cortical neurons section. Electroporation was performed with poring pulse voltage 275.0 V, pulse length 0.5 msec, pulse interval 50.0 msec, number of pulses 2, and decay rate 10% together with transfer pulse voltage 20.0 V, pulse length 50.0 msec, pulse interval 50.0, no. of pulses 5 with decay rate 40%. Culture media was immediately added to the cuvette, and the cells were collected and transferred to culture plates. They were then cultured using the standard method, with the media being changed every three days. We observe expression of the reporter protein in polarized cortical neurons after 2 days.

Immunohistochemistry and confocal imaging

The pups were perfused, and their brains were immediately isolated for overnight fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The next day, the PFA was replaced by 15% sucrose in DPBS (Welgene), followed by 30% sucrose until the tissue completely sunk in the solution. The brain was then embedded in an OCT mold and stored at −80 °C until needed. Coronal brain sections with a thickness of 30 µm were obtained using a cryostat from Leica (CM1860). Antigen retrieval was performed with sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) before blocking. The fixed slide for immunocytochemistry was blocked with a blocking buffer for 1 h at RT as described in publication3. The primary antibody in the blocking buffer was then added and left overnight at 4 °C. After two washes with PBST, the secondary antibody was added for 1 h at RT. Subsequently, the nuclei were stained with Hoechst buffer for 5 min, washed twice with PBST, and finally, mounting media was applied to fix the coverslips. The slides were then stored in the dark at 4 °C. Images were captured with a confocal microscope (Nikon TI2) using 20X, 40X, and 60X objective lenses. All the images from the same experimental condition were captured with the same microscope settings.

Live imaging

Cortical neurons were cultured on a confocal glass-bottom dish (SPL 101350) until DIV6. For the axon growth live imaging, we conducted confocal imaging for 3 h with 5-min intervals. For the lysosome live imaging, we introduced the cathepsin D-binding natural product pepstatin A-based SiR Lysosome tracker (Spirochrome) in M14 cKO with control for 1 h with verapamil, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Confocal imaging was then conducted for 30 min with 10-second intervals.

For the RNA live imaging, we co-electroporated phage-ubc-nls-ha-tdMCP-gfp, with pcDNA-puro-TagBFP-β-ACTIN-3′UTR-24xMS2 reporters which retains the endogenous 3′UTR m⁶A motif (the 1217th position, 5nt after the STOP codon) to cortical neurons on a confocal glass-bottom dish and culture to the DIV5 ~ 6. Live images were acquired with a Nikon Eclipse Ti inverted microscope equipped with a CSU-10 spinning disk (Yokogawa, Tokyo, Japan), an EMCCD camera, and a 100× oil immersion objective (NA 1.49) for 5 min with 500 ms intervals.

RNA-seq library preparation and sequencing

For the corpus callosum (CC) RNA-seq, the mouse P4 brain was isolated and frozen in OCT, and 60 µm thickness sections were cut using a cryostat. Axon projections from the superficial neurons (layer 2/3) across the CC were manually microdissected and collected in TRIzol (Invitrogen) to store at −80 °C until use. Total RNA was isolated from each sample, and further RNA cleanup was performed with the RNAeasy Mini kit (Qiagen). After a total RNA quality check was analyzed from a bioanalyzer (Agilent), a Dyna beads mRNA DIRECT purification kit (Invitrogen) was employed to isolate mRNA from the samples. NEBNext UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) was employed, and the instructed protocol was followed to generate the library. In brief, mRNA was fragmented and reverse-transcribed to generate cDNA. Synthesized double-strand cDNA was purified by Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and ligated with an adapter. Index primers and universal primers were used to amplify the library after adaptor ligation. Further, AMPure beads were used to purify the cDNA library. Finally, cDNA library quality and concentration were assessed on an Agilent Bioanalyzer.

Quantitative RT-PCR analysis

For gene expression level quantification, real-time PCR was performed. TRIzol reagent (Ambion) was used to isolate total RNA, and purified with a QIAGEN RNA purification kit (DNaseI was included). Purified mRNA was reverse-transcribed into the first strand of cDNA with (TAKARA), and further SYBR-green-based qRT-PCR was performed to quantify with qPCR machine (CFX Opus 96, Bio-Rad). All the primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

RNA stability assay

Mouse cortical neurons were cultured in an approximately equal number of cells after counting in standard 6-well plates until DIV7. Actinomycin D (Sigma) was added at 5uM concentration and neurons were collected at two time points (0 and 5 h) after washing with DPBS, cells were collected in TRIzol by scraping each well. Total RNA was isolated, and the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit was used to generate the library. As described the protocol in RNA-seq library preparation section. Linear regression was performed for time, and decay rates were determined.

smFISH

For mouse cortical neuron culture, smFISH was carried out with the RNAscope Fluorescent Detection Multiplex Kit (Advanced Cell Diagnostics Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions as previously described with minor changes53. Briefly, primary cortical neurons were cultured on a 12 mm coverslip until DIV6, then washed with DEPC-PBS and fixed in 4% PFA for 10 min at RT. The neurons were serially dehydrated with ethanol in DEPC-PBS (50%, 70%, and 100%), pretreated with protease III for 15 min after washing with DEPC-PBS, and then treated with customized, previously designed probes for 2 h at 40 °C. All solutions, including the RNAscope wash buffer, were used from the provided kit. Following two washes of 2 min each at RT with the wash buffer, a fluorescent amplification assay was performed by hybridizing with Amp 1-FL for 30 min at 40 °C. After washing, the samples were hybridized with Amp 2-FL for 15 min at 40 °C. The next step involved hybridization with Amp 3-FL for 30 min at 40 °C, followed by the addition of Amp 4-FL using Alt-A or -B for 15 min at 40 °C. Finally, Hoechst solution was added for 3 min after washing, and mounting media was applied to the slides to fix the coverslips for storage in the dark at 4 °C. If required, immunocytochemistry was performed after the smFISH. Confocal imaging was performed to acquire images from more than 5 ROIs from three independent experiments for each probe. Quantification was then conducted to proceed with statistical analysis.

Proximity ligation assay

The NaveniFlex MR PLA kit was employed to perform a proximity ligation assay (PLA) between the following protein pairs: YTHDF2-TUBB3, YTHDF2-FMRP, YTHDF2-CNOT1, FMRP-TUBB3, GFP-KIF5C, and negative controls with IgG. Cortical neurons were prepared on 12 mm coverslips and fixed with 4% PFA. After three washes with TBS-T, Naveni blocking buffer was added to the coverslips and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Primary antibodies were introduced at the specified dilution and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with TBS-T, NaveniBody was diluted in the provided diluent and added to cover the sample area, followed by incubation for 1 h at 37 °C. After three washes with TBS-T, reaction A mix was added to the samples and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in a preheated humidity chamber. After three washes with TBS-T, reaction B mix was added to the samples and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in a preheated humidity chamber. Following two washes with TBS-T, reaction C mix was added to the samples and incubated for 90 min at 37 °C in a preheated humidity chamber. After a TBS wash, nuclear staining was performed with Hoechst buffer. After two washes with TBS, mounting media was applied to the slides to fix the coverslips for storage in the dark at 4 °C. If required, immunocytochemistry was performed after the PLA.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For Co-IP with endogenous protein lysates, the forebrain was dissected from P0 C57BL/6 mouse brains and dissociated using dissociation buffer (0.1% Triton-X 100, 0.5% protease inhibitor cocktail in DPBS) with a tissue homogenizer. The tissue was sonicated under the following conditions: ON 30 sec, OFF 30 sec, for 15 cycles at medium strength using a Bioruptor (Diagenode). To obtain the protein lysate, the sonicated tissue was centrifuged at 15,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatant was collected, and the pellet discarded.

For Co-IP from cells with overexpression of target proteins, pT7-EGFP-HsYTHDF2 plasmid and each of pFRT-HA-FLAG-hFMRP and pRK5-myc-KIF5C plasmid were transfected into HEK293T and Neuro2A cells using PEI. Cell pellets were lysed with lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCL pH 7.4, 1 mM EDTA, and 1% Triton-X-100) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (200:1, Millipore, 535140) and sonicated. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 15,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C.

For antibody binding, 5 µg of antibody was incubated with a total of 30 µL of Dynabeads Protein A (Invitrogen) and Dynabeads Protein G (Invitrogen) in a 1:1 mixture at 4 °C for 3 h with rotation. Meanwhile, 1 mg of protein lysate was pre-cleared with 10 µL of Dynabeads Protein A and Dynabeads Protein G at 4 °C for 1 h with rotation. The pre-cleared lysate was then added to the Dynabeads-conjugated antibody and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rotation. After incubation, the protein-bound Dynabeads were washed six times with dissociation buffer for forebrain derived samples, and washing buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.1% Triton-X-100) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (200:1) for cell derived samples. To elute the protein from the beads, it was heated for 5 min at 95 °C within 5X SDS-PAGE loading buffer (LPS solution) and subjected to western blot.

Western blot

20 µg of proteins per sample were loaded onto a 12% acrylamide gel (LumiBio) and run at 90 V to 120 V in 1X Tris-Glycine SDS buffer (LPS solution). After the gel run, proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane at 300 mA for 90 min in 1X Tris-Glycine buffer (LPS solution) with 20% methanol. The membrane was then blocked with blocking buffer (5% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100 in 1X TBS buffer) for 1 h at room temperature (RT). The primary antibody was applied at a 1:500 dilution in blocking buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was washed three times with wash buffer (0.1% Triton X-100 in 1X TBS buffer) for 10 min each. The secondary antibody was applied at a 1:2000 dilution in 1:5 diluted blocking buffer and incubated for 90 min at RT. The HRP signal was detected using SuperSignal™ West Dura Extended Duration Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and imaging was performed with a Chemi-Doc (Bio-Rad).

Peptide preparation and LC-MS/MS analysis

Co-IP products with internal protein from forebrain conducted followed the protocol described above were used for LC-MS/MS. For final extraction, 50 µL of 2X SDS-PAGE loading buffer (LPS) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 95 °C for 10 min. The interacting proteins were loaded onto a 12% acrylamide gel (LumiBio) and run at 60 V. To extract total protein for proteomic analysis, the gel was excised and washed three times with HPLC-grade water (Fisher) and 30% acetonitrile (Fisher). After incubation with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT, Sigma-Aldrich) at 56 °C for 20 min, 100% ACN was added to the gel and then discarded. Next, 100 mM iodoacetamide (IAA, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the gel and incubated at RT in the dark for 20 min. 100% ACN was added again and discarded. The gel was dried using a lyophilizer. A 1:50 ratio of trypsin/Lys-C mixture (Promega) solution was added to the dried gel and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The supernatant containing the extracted peptides was collected in a new E-tube. After drying, the peptides were applied to a C18 spin column (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the eluents were dried using a lyophilizer.

For LC-MS/MS analysis, the extracted peptides from in-gel digestion were analyzed using a Q-Exactive™ Plus hybrid quadrupole orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) interfaced with an EASY-Spray source. Chromatographic separation of the injected peptides was performed on an UltiMate™ 3000 RSLCnano System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an Acclaim PepMap™ 100 (75 µm × 2 cm, 3 µm, NanoViper, Thermo Fisher Scientific) as the loading column and an EASY-Spray column PepMap™ RSLC C18 (75 µm × 50 cm, 2 µm, Thermo Fisher Scientific) as the separation column. The extracted peptides, dissolved in HPLC-grade water containing 0.1% formic acid, were loaded via an RS autosampler and separated using a linear gradient of acetonitrile (ACN)/water containing 0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The LC eluent was electro-sprayed from the analytical column, and a voltage of 2.0 kV was applied via the liquid junction of the nanospray source. The peptide mixtures were separated using a 5% to 40% ACN gradient over 40 min. The total chromatographic run time was 135 min for each sample.

The analysis method involved a full mass spectrometry (MS) scan over a range of 350–2000 m/z, followed by data-dependent MS/MS (MS²) on the ten most intense ions from the full MS scan. The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent acquisition mode and calibrated using the proposed calibration solution according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Full MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 70,000, followed by HCD MS/MS scans at a resolution of 17,500. The AGC target was set to 1 × 106 for MS2 scans, with maximum injection times of 100 ms, respectively. Precursor ions were isolated using a 2.0 m/z isolation window and fragmented using a normalized collision energy (NCE) of 27. The MS2 scan range was automatically determined based on the precursor m/z, with a fixed first mass of m/z 50. For the database search, tandem mass spectra were processed using Proteome Discoverer software version 2.4 SP1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Spectral data were searched against the mouse Uniprot database (release version 2022_05). The analysis workflow included four nodes: Spectrum Files (data input), Spectrum Selector (spectrum and feature retrieval), Sequest HT (sequence database search), and Percolator (peptide spectral match (PSM) validation and FDR analysis). All identified proteins had an FDR ≤ 1%, calculated at the peptide level and validated based on the q-value. Search parameters allowed for tryptic specificity of up to two missed-cleavages, with carbamidomethylation of cysteine as a static modification and oxidation of methionine as a dynamic modification. The mass search parameters for +1, +2, and +3 ions included mass tolerances of 20 ppm for precursor ions and 0.5 Da for fragment ions.

Each LC-MS/MS sample was analyzed as a single replicate. Protein enrichment was calculated by comparing normalized PSM counts between anti-YTHDF2 immunoprecipitation samples and IgG controls. Proteins exhibiting an absolute log2 fold change greater than 0.5 relative to IgG controls were defined as differentially associated proteins. Gene ontology-based compare cluster analysis and gene-concept network analysis were conducted with the R package clusterProfiler (v 4.6.2)22. Statistical analyses were performed as indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

m6A-SAC-seq analysis

P0 forebrains from Nestin-Cre; Mettl14f/+ pups and Nestin-Cre; Mettl14f/f pups were dissected. Total RNA was extracted using TriZol (Invitrogen). mRNA was purified using the Dynabeads mRNA Purification Kit (Invitrogen). The poly(A) tail of each mRNA sample was removed with oligo(dT) and RNase H (NEB), then fragmented with 10x zinc fragmentation buffer, followed by treatment with PNK enzyme (NEB). Samples were cleaned up with silane beads, and a 2% calibration spike-in mix was added to the reaction, which was then treated with T4 RNA ligase 2, truncated KQ (NEB), and a 3′-adapter, followed by overnight incubation. The excessive RNA adaptor was digested by adding 5′ Deadenylase (NEB) and RecJf (NEB). Then, Dynabeads C1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) resuspended in 2x binding/wash buffer was added to ligation mix to purify the sample with washing the beads in 0.1% PBST (v/v) and 1× binding/wash buffer and twice in Tris–HCl. Beads were resuspended in RNase-free H2O and then, one-third of sample was separated as RNA-seq input and stored at −80 °C. With the tube in magnetic rack, the half of water was removed. The rest of resuspended beads were linearized and added with 10× buffer, SUPERase In RNase Inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific), allylic-SAM, and MjDim1 enzyme for m6A labeling. The supernatant was removed, and the beads were resuspended in water. Labeling reaction was repeated once more. Beads were washed sequentially with 0.1% PBST, 1× binding/wash buffer, and Tris–HCl. Iodine(I2) was added and kept in the dark at RT for 1 h, then Na2S2SO3 was added to quench I2. Beads were washed sequentially with 0.1% PBST, 1× binding/wash buffer, and Tris–HCl. To both samples of SAC-seq and RNA-seq, RT primer and dNTP was added. Then, AMV reverse transcriptase reaction buffer (NEB), RNaseOUT, Actinomycin D, and HIV RT enzyme (Worthington Biochemical) were added for reverse transcription. After RT reaction, RNase-free H2O and 10% SDS were added. Resuspended samples were cleaned with Silane beads washed with buffer RLT, then extracted with RNase-free H2O. For cDNA adaptor ligation, cDNA adaptor was added and linearized. 10× T4 RNA ligase reaction buffer, ATP, 50% PEG8000, DMSO, and Co(NH3)6Cl3 were added, then T4 RNA ligase 1 was added followed by overnight incubation. Ligated samples were cleaned with silane beads washed with buffer RLT, followed by extraction with RNase-free H2O. For library construction, NEBNext Ultra II Q5 Master Mix and NEBNext adaptors were used. Amplified libraries were purified with Ampure XP beads. The purified libraries were sent to Macrogen for next-generation deep sequencing with Illumina HiSeq X in paired-end 2 × 100 bp read length. The adapters of all raw m6A-SAC-seq data fastq file is trimmed with Cutadapt (version 3.4). Filtered fastq files were aligned to the mouse genome (mm10) with STAR (version 2.7.10b). To detect the mutation sites on the transcriptome, the mapping results were filtered by masking flags to eliminate unmapped, secondary, low-quality, or duplicate reads for downstream analysis. Then, the bam file was splitted into forward and reverse reads. The mutations of each site are detected with customized tool, cpup (version 0.1.0, https://github.com/y9c/cpup) Sites with more than one mutation reads in any library are reported in the output file. Detected sites are filtered with their mutation ratio between mutation occurred samples and untreated samples. Following criteria is applied to identify appropriate site; Average mutation ratio of the untreated input libraries should be less then 2.5% or the maximum number of mutations is 1, average mutation ratio of the treated libraries should be more than 5%, There should be 3 or more mutations in the treated libraries, The minimum sequencing depth of all the libraries should be more than 20. To annotate the gene or transcript name, relative position to transcript and motif sequence at RNA level, python package variant (https://github.com/y9c/cpup) were used for final m6A sites. SAC gene body plot was generated through R package Guitar (v2.14.0). Integrative genomics viewer (IGV, version 2.15.2) was used to visualize m6A-SAC-seq results.

Image analysis

For the acquisition and quantification of images, we utilized the open-source online tool Fiji54. To ensure linearity and consistency across samples, minimum and maximum threshold signals were determined. Quantification was performed to obtain binary values from pixel intensity. Callosal axon projection values were normalized by RFP-positive soma (grey zone) and binned by axon projection before and after midline. Contralateral fluorescence intensity of the layer 1 ~ 6 values were normalized to relative RFP-positive soma at the ipsilateral position with the S1 region z-stacked image12. For tracking in vitro primary neuron samples, the Fiji plugin NeuronJ was used55. The neurite length was measured as the distance between a clearly identifiable neurite tip and the center of the nucleus along axonal tracks labeled with Tau. The collateral branching was measured as the number of branching points in a neuron and normalized by total neurite length. The protrusion transiently disappeared within 15 min were counted during neuron live imaging. Quantification of smFISH single molecules and PLA analysis were conducted using NeuronJ by measuring the distance between a single RNA molecule and the center of the nucleus along axon tracks labeled with fluorescent proteins or Tau. The normalized density of smFISH and PLA was quantified by calculating the ratio of neurite signal per total signal and normalizing it with the control ratio. For representative images in figures, maximum intensity projections of Z-stacked images were processed using Fiji. All analyses were performed on uncropped images. For RNA live images, RNA molecules observed > 60 frames were selected for consistency. Anterograde and retrograde movement were counted to calculate the fraction of transport and motile time. Movements longer than 2 µm were categorized into the antero or retro group, and those shorter than 2 µm were categorized as the interrupted group56.

RNA-seq data analysis

The RNA-seq data from CCseq and RNA stability assay were aligned to the mm10 mouse genome, computed expression level with mm10 mouse annotation from illumina iGenomes database. All procedure was done by the RSEM (v1.3.3) and STAR (v2.7.10b) with default parameters. Downstream analysis was done with the DEseq2 (1.38.3) package in R. Transcripts with normalized count below 10 were removed from downstream analysis. Differential expression was done by “DESeq” function with default parameters. On CCseq, low expressing transcripts with base mean below 70 were removed from downstream analysis. Significantly localized transcripts were identified by Log2foldchange > |0.5 | , and p-adjust value < 0.05. On stability assay, relative stability difference was estimated by [5 hr Log2foldchange – 0 hr Log2foldchange] between each experimental condition and significantly stability altered transcripts identified by dLFC > |1.0 | . Selected gene sets were used gene ontology (GO) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) with R package ClusterProfiler (v 4.6.2). Selected gene set 3’UTR sequence were analyzed by AME (v5.5.5).

Statistical analysis

All quantified data were analyzed using either Microsoft Excel or R(4.2.2). Data distributions were assumed to be normal, although this was not formally tested. Sample sizes were not pre-determined based on statistical methods but were consistent with those reported in previous publications3,27. For comparisons of genotype or knockdown samples, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons of two distribution sets, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was employed. No data were excluded from the analysis. All visualized plots were generated using the R package ggplot2(3.4.4) or individual tools described on each method parts. All image analyses were replicated in at least three independent samples, and all experiments showing representative figures were replicated with similar results.
