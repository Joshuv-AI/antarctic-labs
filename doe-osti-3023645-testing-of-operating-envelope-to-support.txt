 SECTION 1.0
 INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background

 About 50 million gallons of high-level mixed waste is currently stored in underground
tanks at the United States Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Hanford site in the State of
Washington. The Hanford Tank Waste Treatment and Immobilization Plant (WTP) will provide
DOE’s Office of River Protection (ORP) with a means of treating this waste by vitrification for
subsequent disposal. The tank waste will be separated into low- and high-activity waste
fractions, which will then be vitrified respectively into Immobilized Low Activity Waste
(ILAW) and Immobilized High Level Waste (IHLW) products. The ILAW product will be
disposed in an engineered facility on the Hanford site while the IHLW product is designed for
acceptance into a national deep geological disposal facility for high-level nuclear waste.

1.2 WTP Baseline LAW Glass Formulations and Correlation

 Acceptable glass formulations for vitrification of Hanford low activity waste (LAW)
must meet a variety of product quality, processability, and waste loading requirements. To this
end, a large number of formulations were investigated during Part A [1], Part B1 [2] and Part B2
[3, 4] of the WTP development program. The testing resulted in the selection of target glass
compositions for the processing of eight of the Phase I LAW tanks. The selected glass
compositions were tested at the crucible scale to confirm their compliance with ILAW
performance requirements. DuraMelter 100 (DM100) [5-16] and LAW Pilot Melter [17-28] tests
were then conducted to demonstrate the viability of these glass compositions for LAW waste
vitrification at high processing rates.

 In support of the WTP Project, The Vitreous State Laboratory (VSL) at The Catholic
University of America (CUA) developed and tested feed and glass formulations to provide data
to meet the WTP contract requirements and to support system design activities. Glass
formulation development work for the WTP has been based on Hanford tank waste composition
data [29-32] that have evolved over time. As a result, the target glass compositions for waste
processing also were adjusted to accommodate the changes in LAW waste compositions. The
major components of interest in the LAW waste streams, from a waste loading perspective, are
sodium, sulfur, and to a lesser extent potassium. Melter testing included composition variations
involving the nominal feed and  15% variations in the amount of waste simulant added to the
melter feed. As a result of these efforts, the WTP has a series of glass compositions with
different sodium, potassium, and sulfur concentrations that have been tested and validated over a
range of melter scales, including the LAW Pilot Melter.

 The work has also included developing property-composition models for Product
Consistency Test (PCT) releases, Vapor Hydration Test (VHT) alteration rates, melt viscosity,

 11

and melt electrical conductivity [33, 34] and glass forming chemical (GFC) additive blends for
each LAW waste stream to generate melter feeds with acceptable rheological properties for
processing at the WTP [35, 36].

 This extensive glass formulation development work underpinning the WTP baseline
LAW glasses was captured in a correlation developed by VSL and Atkins that permits the
calculation of the glass formulation required for each particular batch of LAW delivered to the
LAW vitrification facility [37]. The correlation is based on the baseline LAW glasses developed
for the WTP with particular emphasis on those subjected to melter testing, especially in the LAW
Pilot Melter. The composition outputs from the LAW correlation are designed such that they can
tolerate expected process variations without adversely affecting properties relevant to processing
and product quality. This LAW correlation, along with the LAW glass property-composition
models, will be used to control glass compositions for LAW vitrification at the WTP. Given the
composition of any LAW stream, the correlation permits the calculation of a suitable glass
composition and the required GFC additives such that all processing, waste loading, and product
quality requirements are met. Confirmation of this correlation was based on a set of seven glass
compositions tested in the DM100 melter [38]. The formulation selection also included an
assessment of the process control uncertainties (in terms of composition variations) expected for
the LAW vitrification process and the melter testing strengthened the correlation by
demonstrating the production of LAW glasses that extend from the alkali-limited region through
the sulfate-limited region with decreasing alkali contents. The DM100 melter testing provided
reliable information on sulfate incorporation and secondary phase accumulation in the dynamic
melter environment that could not be obtained using laboratory crucible melts alone.

 The development of the LAW Glass Correlation involved definition of mathematical
relationships that relate the amount of each GFC additive to the LAW to be treated [37],
preparation and characterization of compositions defined by the correlation at the crucible scale,
and melter tests using these compositions once they are confirmed to comply with all processing
and product quality requirements for the WTP. The glass compositions used in these tests span
the range of alkali and sulfur concentrations in Hanford LAW and can be applied to specific tank
waste composition in support of Direct Feed LAW (DFLAW) treatment at WTP. Such is the case
for the LAW from Hanford tank AP-107, which is the likely startup feed for DFLAW operations
at the WTP [39]. The glass composition for treating this waste was defined by the LAW glass
correlation [37]; however, natural process control errors arising from uncertainties in the results
from LAW sampling and analysis, measurement of the volume of LAW transferred, amount of
glass former additives, etc., can lead to variations in the glass composition that is processed
through the melter. Consequently, for a given waste feed source, the WTP LAW melters could
process a range of glass compositions rather than a single composition. It is important to
understand the impacts of variations across this process envelope to ensure that they do not lead
to unacceptable processing or product quality issues. To better define the impact of such
expected process variations in the WTP LAW vitrification facility, a matrix of glass
compositions was defined around the nominal composition to account for likely process
variations. This report presents work responding to the primary objectives listed in a Test Plan
[40] that is responsive to an ORP statement of work (SOW) [41].

 12

1.3 Test Objectives

 The primary objective of the present work was to investigate the potential impact of
likely process variations on the nominal glass composition for LAW AP-107 waste that is
determined by the LAW glass correlation (AP107WDFL). This was accomplished by evaluating
glass and feed variations at crucible scale and DM100 melter tests as follows:

 • Define a matrix of glass compositions and feed variations around the LAW AP-107
 glass composition (AP107WDFL) [39] that was determined by the WTP LAW Glass
 Correlation [37] to account for likely process variations and predict the impact on
 glass properties.

 • Determine the effects of process variability, such as variations in LAW sampling and
 analysis, volume of LAW transferred, amount of glass former additives, etc., on glass
 composition.

 • Select approximately 15 compositions from the process variability evaluation for
 crucible melt preparation and characterization of the glass properties affecting
 processability and product quality including melt viscosity, melt electrical
 conductivity, VHT alteration, PCT releases, crystallization, refractory corrosion, and
 sulfate incorporation.

 • Evaluate the potential processing characteristics of selected compositions among the
 15 compositions by crucible scale testing in a vertical gradient furnace (VGF).

 • Select four compositions estimated to have the poorest processing characteristics
 from the VGF testing for processing on the DM100 melter system for a total test
 duration of 200 hours.

 • Characterize melter feeds processed on the DM100 for physical and rheological
 properties and compare to the range of properties acceptable for melter feed
 processing at the WTP.

1.4 Test Overview

 The objectives of this work were accomplished through a combination of crucible scale
tests, melter feed tests, and DM100 melter tests. The glass composition defined by the WTP
LAW Correlation [37] for the LAW AP-107 waste is shown in the plot of the Na2O-SO3
composition space in Figure 1.1. The composition and measured properties of this glass
(AP107WDFL) are presented in Table 1.1. All measured glass properties are within acceptable
ranges and the glass was tested in a DM10 melter system equipped with an SBS, WESP,
evaporator, and recycle system [39]. During that test, the glass production rate was maintained
at 2,000 kg/m2/day with no processing difficulties over the 200-hour test duration. In the present

 13

work, selected variations around the AP107WDFL glass composition were tested to assess the
impacts of natural process control variations. An initial screening of processing performance was
performed by VGF testing and the results were used to select a smaller subset of compositions
for testing on the DM100 to determine processability under nominal melter operating conditions.

 Crucible scale testing was used to identify the variations causing the largest processing
difficulty on the DM100 melter. Crucible melts were prepared and characterized with respect to
properties affecting processability and product quality (melt viscosity, melt electrical
conductivity, VHT alteration, PCT releases, crystallization, refractory corrosion, and sulfate
incorporation). VGF testing was used to assess the processing rate of the new compositions.
Fifteen crucible melts were generated for testing and selected feed compositions generated from
this process were evaluated for rheological properties prior to melter testing.

 The DM100 melter tests were conducted for a total duration of about 200 hours, divided
into four test segments. All of the tests were performed under nominal melter processing
conditions, which include a nominal melt pool temperature of 1150ºC, near complete cold-cap,
and fixed bubbling rate while obtaining the optimal feed rate. Samples of the glass product were
collected and characterized to provide comparisons to the results obtained from the crucible

1.5 DM100 Melter System Description

 1.5.1 Feed System

 A schematic diagram of the DM100 vitrification system is shown in Figure 1.2. The
melter feed is introduced in batches into a feed container that is mounted on a load cell for
weight monitoring. The feed is stirred with a variable speed mixer and constantly recirculated
except for periodic, momentary interruptions during which the weight is recorded. The
recirculation loop extends to the top of the melter where feed is diverted from the recirculation
loop through a peristaltic pump and into the melter through a Teflon-lined feed line and vertical
water-cooled feed tube.

 1.5.2 Melter System

 Cross-sectional diagrams of the DM100-BL melter are shown in Figures 1.2.a-c. The
DM100-BL unit is a ceramic refractory-lined melter fitted with five electrodes: two pairs of
opposing Inconel 690 plate electrodes and a bottom electrode. Power can be supplied in either
three-phase or single-phase configurations. All of the tests in the present work were performed
with the upper and lower electrodes on each side connected together and powered by a
single-phase supply; the bottom electrode was not powered. Melt pool agitation is achieved by
either a removable lance entering from the top of the melter or a permanent bubbler installed
through the bottom electrode. In these tests, the lance bubbler was used. The glass product is

 14

removed from the melter by means of an airlift discharge system. The melter has a melt surface
area of 0.108 m 2 and a variable glass inventory of between 110 kg, when only the bottom pair of
 P P

electrodes is used, and about 170 kg when both pairs of electrodes are used, which was the case
in the present tests.

 1.5.3 Off-Gas System

 For operational simplicity, the DM100-BL is equipped with a dry off-gas treatment
system involving gas filtration operations only. Exhaust gases leave the melter plenum through a
film cooler device that minimizes the formation of solid deposits. The film-cooler air has
constant flow rate and its temperature is thermostatically controlled. Consequently, under
steady-state operating conditions, the exhaust gases passing through the transition line (between
the melter and the first filtration device) can be sampled at constant temperature and airflow rate.
The geometry of the transition line conforms to the requirements of the 40-CFR-60 air sampling
techniques. Immediately downstream of the transition line are cyclonic filters followed by
conventional pre-filters and high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters. The temperature of the
cyclonic filters is maintained above 150°C while the temperatures in the HEPAs are kept
sufficiently high to prevent moisture condensation. The entire train of gas filtration operations is
duplicated, and each train is used alternately. An induced draft fan completes the system.

 SECTION 3.0
 DETERMINATION OF GLASS COMPOSITION VARIATIONS

 The process by which the melter feed is made up in the WTP LAW vitrification facility
involves a number of measurements, each of which has an associated measurement uncertainty.
The combined effects of these measurement uncertainties results in real variations in the
composition of the melter feed, and therefore in the glass composition, above and beyond
variations that are due to actual variation in the waste composition. These effects can be
calculated by error propagation through the mass balance equations for the process. This requires
a mass balance across the various process steps and estimates of the uncertainties associated with
each measurement. The essential process steps for LAW feed make up can be summarized as
follows:
 • Pretreated LAW is received into the concentrate receipt vessel (CRV) in the WTP
 LAW vitrification facility and the level of the tank contents is measured to
 determine the volume.
 • The vessel contents are then mixed, sampled, and analyzed.
 • The requisite volume of LAW is then transferred from the CRV into the melter
 feed preparation vessel (MFPV) to prepare the next melter feed batch.
 • Knowing the volume and composition of the contents of the MFPV, the mass of
 each GFC required to achieve the target glass composition is calculated using the
 LAW Glass Formulation Correlation.
 • The GFCs are then weighed, blended, and transferred into the MFPV.
 • The level of the melter feed in the MFPV is measured and a sample is taken and
 analyzed for confirmation of composition.

 In the present work, the mass balance equations and WTP LAW measurement
uncertainties summarized in [52, 53] were used to determine the resulting variations around the
nominal glass composition. However, in addition to factors that affect glass composition, the
effects of factors that could affect feed properties and processing behavior, such as feed water
content and sugar content, were also included. As in [52], a Monte Carlo approach was used to
perform the error propagation using the assumed probability distributions for each of the
uncertain variables.

 Glass property composition models were used to estimate the corresponding distributions
of glass properties. These results were, in turn, used to select 15 glass compositions for crucible
scale fabrication and testing and for melter testing. The list of glass compositions selected for
testing was submitted to ORP for review and approval. The details of the glass selection process
are provided below.

4.1 Glass Batching and Preparation

 The target glass compositions (see Table 3.4) were used to create the batching sheets
providing information on the required starting materials and their weights for glass melting. These

 The crucibles were placed inside a Deltech DT-29 furnace with a Eurotherm 2404

of 1200°C. Mixing of the melt was accomplished mechanically using a platinum stirrer, beginning

minutes. At the end of melting, the molten glass was poured onto a graphite plate to cool. The
glass sample was collected and analyzed for composition before distribution for property

4.2 Analysis of Glass Compositions

 The compositions of the glasses were determined using X-ray fluorescence (XRF)
spectroscopy and direct current plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (DCP-AES), as described
below.

 The primary method used for glass composition analysis was XRF spectroscopy on
powdered glass samples. Powdered samples of the glasses (–200 mesh) were analyzed with a

 23

PANalytical AxiosmAX-Advanced XRF spectrometer. The spectrometer was calibrated over a
range of glass compositions using standard reference materials traceable to National Institute of

not included in the VSL procedure because boron is normally one of the analytes. The resulting

4.4 Sulfate Solubility

 Sulfate solubility was determined by over-saturation tests on all fifteen crucible glasses. In
this method, sulfate solubility is determined by re-melting a small amount of the glass with an
excess of sulfate such that a molten salt phase forms on the surface of the glass melt. The sulfate

 24

solubility is then determined by analyzing the chemical composition of the glass melt in
equilibrium with the molten sulfate phase at the designated test temperature. The glass sample is
ground and sieved to pass 40-mesh and then thoroughly mixed with reagent grade sodium sulfate.
The amount of sulfate added was equivalent to 4 wt% SO3 in the glass if all of the sulfur was
retained in the glass. The glass/sulfate mixture was loaded into a Pt/Au crucible with a cover and
re-melted at 1150°C for 1 hour. The crucible was then cooled naturally to room temperature and
the glass recovered for examination. Washing of glass pieces to remove the salt phase was
followed by grinding (<200 mesh) and then washing of the glass powder to ensure removal of all
sulfate salts. Analysis of SO3 in the powdered glass samples (denoted S4 after grinding and S4W
after powder-washing) provides an estimate of sulfate solubility. Results are provided in Table 4.2
and shown with the results for the nominal glass in Figure 4.2. The measured sulfate solubility is
generally at or above the target value; in fact, it is much higher than target in the seven glasses
containing at least 1 wt% Li2O. Therefore, sulfate solubility is unlikely to be exceeded and cause
the formation of a separate sulfate layer in melter tests based on the composition variability
represented by these 15 glasses.

suspended from a hanger in the pressure vessel and enough de-ionized water was added to the

pressure vessels were removed and immediately partially immersed in an ice/water bath to
condense the water vapor near the bottom of the vessel. Once cool and dry, the vessel was
weighed. If the difference in the mass of the sealed pressure vessel before and after the test
indicated a water loss in excess of 50% of the original amount, the test results were discarded
(none were discarded in the set reported here). If the weighing indicated an acceptable test, the
pressure vessel was then opened and if the coupon had not fallen from its hanger during the test, it
was removed and weighed. Post-VHT coupons were placed on an optical scanner and the scanned
images examined and stored for future reference. Coupons were mounted whole in epoxy in a
standard 1-inch diameter scanning electron microscope (SEM) mounting cup with the broad
surfaces supported vertically so that subsequent grinding and polishing would produce a
representative cross-section of the reacted layer and the remaining glass for SEM examination and
measurement. For consistency with existing data, the nominal test duration was 24 days.

 For an average reacted layer thickness greater than 100 microns, the layer thickness
(which can be uneven) was determined by measuring the remaining glass thickness at ten points
throughout the cross-section of the coupon and subtracting the average remaining thickness from
the original thickness of the coupon and dividing that value by 2. For average layer thicknesses
less than or equal to about 100 microns, the thickness of the altered layer was measured directly at
3 points in each of 6 evenly spaced regions of the coupon using the digital caliper in the SEM
software package and the resulting set of 18 measurements was averaged.

 WTP Contract Specification 2 [51] requires that the VHT alteration rate determined from
tests of seven days or longer duration be below 50 g/m2/day. If it is assumed that the altered layer
density is not appreciably different from that of the glass, the mean glass alteration rate over the
test interval (r in g/m2/d) is related to the measured altered layer thickness D in microns by:

                                         r = D/t,                                                  (4.1)

where  is the glass density in g/cm3 and t is the test duration. Under this assumption, for a typical

4.7 Melt Viscosity and Melt Electrical Conductivity

 The melt viscosity (η) of each glass was measured using a Brookfield viscometer with a
platinum-rhodium spindle and crucible. The relative torque of a rotating spindle immersed in
molten glass was measured as a function of rotational velocity (revolutions per minute (RPM)) at
temperatures around 950, 1050, 1150 and 1250ºC. The viscosity of the molten glass was then
calculated from the collected data of torque versus RPM. The equipment was calibrated using
viscosity standard oils and checked periodically using a NIST traceable standard reference glass.
To facilitate comparison, the viscosity data were interpolated to standard temperatures (e.g., from
950ºC to 1250ºC) using the Vogel-Fulcher equation:

                                     ln η = [A/(T-To)] + B,                                          (4.2)

where A, B, and To are fitting parameters.

 Per current WTP requirements, glass melts should satisfy the viscosity limits of 10 to 150
poise at 1100ºC, with the preferred range being 40-80 poise at 1150ºC [50].

 The electrical conductivity (EC) of each glass was determined by measuring the
impedance of the glass melt at temperatures around 950, 1050, 1150 and 1250ºC as a function of
AC frequency using a calibrated platinum-rhodium electrode probe attached to a Hewlett-Packard
model 4194A impedance analyzer. The collected impedance data were analyzed to obtain the DC
electrical conductivity. The probe (analyzer along with the crucible to assure that the geometry is
replicated) was calibrated and checked periodically using NIST traceable standard reference
materials. To facilitate glass to glass comparison, the electrical conductivity data were
interpolated to standard temperatures (e.g., 1150ºC) using the Vogel-Fulcher equation:

                                     ln EC = [A/(T-To)] + B                                          (4.3)

where A, B, and To are fitting parameters.

 The current WTP requirement is that glass melt EC be in the range of 0.1 - 0.7 S/cm at
1100 - 1200ºC [50].

4.8 Refractory Corrosion

 The K-3 refractory corrosion tests were conducted using a modified ASTM refractory
corrosion test procedure (ASTM C621 [58]), building on experience gained from extensive
refractory corrosion tests at the VSL. The primary modification is the addition of gas bubbling
during testing in order to better represent the conditions in the bubbled WTP melters.

 For this test, Monofrax K-3 test coupons are cut from K-3 refractory bricks. Since the
material that forms fused-cast K-3 varies from the surface of the brick to its interior (e.g., the
interior material tends to contain larger and more numerous pores), the test coupons are cut from
material within one inch of the brick surface. All sides of the K-3 coupons are ground parallel
with a precision of better than 2 mil (0.002”). A typical K-3 coupon measures 0.6  0.4 inches in
cross-section and is long enough (~ 3 inches) to be immersed in the molten glass to a depth of one
inch.

 For each test, the K-3 test coupon is first cemented to a crucible cover made of Zirmul and
baked. Before starting the corrosion test, the baked coupons are preheated and then positioned in a
200-ml platinum crucible containing 170 grams of pre-melted glass. A platinum sheet covers the
glass to ensure that it cannot be contaminated by Zirmul. The platinum crucible containing the K-
3 coupon and molten glass is set inside a quartz crucible holder, which is then placed in a box
furnace preheated to about 800C. After the furnace reaches the designated test temperature
(nominally 1208C), a platinum bubbling tube is introduced into the molten glass from above
through a slot in the Zirmul/platinum cover. Dry air is bubbled through the molten glass at a
constant rate of 8 cc/minute (at room temperature) controlled by a precision flow meter. The
bubbling rate, i.e., the number of gas bubbles generated inside the melt per minute, is monitored
using a pressure transducer interfaced to a computer via an A/D converter. The temperature of the
furnace is monitored using an S-type thermocouple positioned above the crucible inside the
furnace and checked before each test against a calibrated S-type thermocouple. The standard
glass-contact corrosion test is run for six days at 1208C with continuous air bubbling. All K-3
corrosion tests are performed at the same refractory surface area (S) / melt volume (V) ratio of
about 0.20 cm-1, which is 74% less than the S/V ratio specified by the ASTM C-621 for static
glass contact corrosion tests. Fresh K-3 test coupon is used for each corrosion test. At the end of
each test, the K-3 coupon is removed from the melt and cooled to room temperature in a clean
quartz crucible. The coupon is then sectioned lengthwise to facilitate measurement of dimensional
changes. Per ASTM C-621, the dimension losses at the “neck” (the glass-air interface) and the
“half-down” (half of the immersed length of the coupon below the neck) locations are reported.

 28

 The acceptability of the corrosion characteristics of a glass composition is somewhat
subjective because a glass composition that shows slightly higher K-3 corrosion, but which allows
higher waste loading, may be a more economical choice than one with lower K-3 corrosion and
lower waste loading. However, for WTP LAW glass formulation development, a neck corrosion
of 0.035 inches on a 6-day K-3 coupon corrosion test at 1208C has been used as an acceptance
limit. A temperature about 50°C higher than the nominal melter operating temperature of 1150°C
was selected for these tests so that a measurable amount of corrosion will be observed on a 6-day
test.

 K-3 corrosion tests were conducted on eight AP107DFLO glasses and the results are given

 4.9.1 Organic Reductant Additions

 With high nitrate feeds, the addition of reductants is necessary in order to control melt
foaming. Sugar, which was used for this purpose at West Valley, has also been selected as the
baseline reductant for the WTP. The amount of sugar required increases with the amount of
nitrates present in the feed and decreases with the amount of waste organics present in the feed,
which themselves act as reductants. Excessive additions of reductants can be deleterious, leading
to over-reduction of the melt and formation of sulfides and molten metals. Melter tests while
monitoring the iron redox state have shown that carbon additions greater than the targeted amount
created overly reducing conditions in the glass [5, 6, 16, 59]. Consequently, the oxidants and
reductants in the feed must be suitably balanced. The basis for achieving this balance was
developed by VSL and Atkins for the vitrification of high-sodium-nitrate feeds at Savannah
River's M-Area and has been successfully applied to the processing of a wide variety of simulated
WTP feeds over many years [5-28]. In developing this approach, we elected to conservatively
adopt the most reducing potential reaction as the basis for the definition of a “sugar ratio” of 1.0

 29

as a result of concerns for over-reducing the melt. Such a reaction, using sodium salts as an
example, is:

                    C12H22O11 + 8NaNO3 = 8CO2 + 4CO + 4N2 + 11H2O + 4Na2O

Fundamentally, the basis that is selected is simply a convention, since the precise stoichiometry of
the reactions involved is neither known nor constant under the conditions prevailing in the melter.
However, with this convention, a sugar ratio of 1.0 corresponds to one mole of sucrose per eight
moles of nitrate or, more generally, 1.5 moles of organic carbon per mole of nitrate. It is then
expected that significantly less sugar than this will be required in practice. The empirically
determined amount required to successfully control melt foaming without significantly reducing
the glass melt was found to correspond to a ratio of 0.5 when any nitrites present were counted as
nitrates (i.e., 0.75 moles of organic carbon per mole of nitrate + nitrite). This approach has been
employed for all WTP melter testing. It is, however, expected that slight variations around the
nominal value of 0.5 may be necessary to account for differences in the reducing power of waste
organics in comparison to sugar, particularly for LAW streams that are high in organics [16].
Testing with starch in place of sugar has shown that, like waste organics, starch has less reducing
power than sugar [59].

 The calculation of the amount of sugar needed to achieve a stoichiometric sugar ratio of
0.5 for LAW AP107 proceeds as follows:

 • One liter of simulant contains 119,641 mg nitrate and 51,107 mg nitrite (see Table
 4.8), which corresponds to 1.930 moles of nitrate and 1.111 moles of nitrite, giving a
 total of 3.041 moles of NOx
 • Required total amount of organic carbon for a sugar ratio of 0.5 is
             3.041 x 0.75 = 2.281 moles
 • One liter of simulant contains 1,834 mg or 0.153 moles of organic carbon (see Table
 4.8)
 • Therefore, 2.281 - 0.153 = 2.128 moles of organic carbon must be added.

 Since the molecular weight of sucrose is 342 g per mole, 2.128 x 342/12 = 60.64 g sugar
must be added per liter of simulant, as shown in Table 4.9. Variations in sugar addition was one
of the factors evaluated during testing, as discussed in Section 5.0.

4.10 Feed Processing Rate by VGF Testing

 Vertical gradient furnace testing has proved to be a useful tool for melt rate screening in
previous work with WTP HLW compositions [60-66]. The method was subsequently adapted for
the intrinsically faster melting LAW compositions to provide relative melt rates of feeds to assess
the effects of GFCs on the glass production rates [67].

 The results from crucible tests of the fifteen AP107DFLO glasses showed two glass melts
with viscosity values greater than 200 poise at 1100°C: AP107DFLO1 and AP107DFLO5. The
very high viscosities of these glasses have the potential to affect processing rate. These two feeds

 30

were, therefore, evaluated for processing characteristics using the VGF system shown
schematically in Figure 4.7. The temperature gradient inside the VGF is maintained by two
separate sets of heating elements, both of which are arranged in cylindrical form and aligned
along their axes. A ceramic crucible (3.5 inches tall) is used to contain the reacting melter feed.
The temperature gradient in the furnace is shown in Figure 4.8. For a typical feed conversion test,
10 grams of glass of identical chemical composition to the test feed (expressed on an oxide basis)
is preheated in the ceramic crucible positioned in the inner heater before the dried melter feed (to
yield 17 grams of glass) is introduced. Feed reactions under the controlled temperature gradient
are allowed to continue for the designated test duration (typically, from 5 to 60 minutes) and then
stopped by rapid cooling in room temperature air. The top surface of the reacted feed material is
then inspected and photographed. The crucibles with their feed contents are then cross-sectioned
to reveal the conversion progress of feed blends. To characterize the reacted feed material, visual
inspection and digital imaging of the top (by photography) and cross section (using an optical
scanner) of the reacted sample are performed. The results are assessed by comparison to results
obtained previously from a wide range of other feeds that have known processing rates from
continuous melter testing [68]. This comparison is done qualitatively on a scale of 1 to 6, where 1
is a very fast melting feed and 6 is the slowest. All VGF tests were conducted at a minimum of
two time periods, all starting with fresh feed samples.

 Five feeds were tested using the VGF. These included the nominal formulation
AP107WDFL (feed sample AP107DFL5p6), AP107DFLO1, AP107DFLO5, and two variations
of AP107DFLO5 with 15% lower sucrose addition. The AP107DFLO5 variations were one with
15% excess nitrates and nitrites (sample ID AP107DFLO5S) and 15% lower sucrose addition,
and a second with nominal nitrates and nitrites but 15% low sucrose (sample ID
AP107DFLO5N). The target compositions of the AP107WDFL, AP107DFLO1, and
AP107DFLO5 feeds are given in Table 4.9. All VGF test feeds were based on the composition of
LAW from Hanford tank AP-107 given in Table 4.8.

 Images of the samples after 15, 30, and 60-minute VGF testing are given in Figure 4.9.
Differences between the feeds appear more evident in the VGF tests after 30 minutes. Images of

The amount of foaming observed in cross sectional images of the 30-minute samples also is the

increases. In addition, the combustion of sugar in the cold-cap, which is exothermic, aids in

therefore expect reduced amounts of sugar addition, especially at amounts much lower than the

6.1 Analysis of Feed Samples

 6.1.1 General Properties

 Feed samples from each test were analyzed to confirm physical properties and chemical
composition. Samples were taken from as-received drums and at the beginning and end of each
melter test from an inline sampling port in the feed line. Sample names, sampling dates, measured
properties, and comparison to target glass yield values are given in Table 6.1. Glass yield analysis
results for the as-received feeds were used to determine the amount of water to be added to the
feed to achieve the target water concentrations. The as-received feed was determined to contain a
little more than half the target amount of water and therefore large amounts of water were added
to each batch along with the sugar. The measured conversion ratios were between 2.4 and 8.4
percent below the target values for melter feed samples from the four tests. The closeness of the
measured and target glass yields for the as-received feed samples led to the use of target glass
yield values for the purposes of calculating glass production rates over the course of each test.
Production rates were also calculated using the measured conversion ratio of the melter test
samples, which show the corresponding up to eight percent lower glass production rate as a point
of comparison (see Table 5.1). The amount of glass produced in each of the melter tests was
generally consistent with the amount of feed consumed and the measured glass yield, confirming
the feed solids content analysis. Measured feed density, pH, and water content for melter test
samples taken from each feed formulation varied within narrow ranges, illustrating the uniformity
of the feed between each of the two tests. Measured melter test feed pH values fell in the narrow
range of 9.2 and 9.5, primarily due to the waste type and amount being the same for each of the
four feeds.

 6.1.2 Chemical Composition

 The chemical compositions of the feed samples were determined by first making a glass
from the feed via crucible melt. The glass was subsequently crushed and analyzed directly by
XRF spectroscopy. Boron and lithium concentrations were determined by DCP-AES analysis of
solutions generated by microwave aided acid dissolution of glass samples. Feed sample data are
compared to the target compositions in Tables 6.2 and 6.3.

 The results corroborate the consistency of the feed oxide composition and show good
agreement with the target composition for the major and minor components. In particular, major
components, boron, silicon, and sodium, targeted at greater than ten percent oxide were all within
ten percent of the target values in the as-received feed samples. Relative deviations of greater than
10% from the target composition for constituents with greater than or equal to one percent in
as-received feed samples were measured for oxides of magnesium (-38 and -42 relative % or

-0.56 and -0.65 absolute %), for titanium (20 and 14 relative % or -0.28 and -0.21 absolute %),
zinc in samples from the AP107DFLO5 composition (-15.3 relative % or -0.21 absolute %), and
lithium in samples from the AP107DFLO9 composition (19 relative % or -0.34 absolute %). The

6.2 Compositional Analysis of Discharged and Dip Glass Samples

 Over seventeen hundred kilograms of glass was produced during the four tests. The glass
was discharged from the DM100 melter periodically into 5-gallon carbon steel pails using an air

and end of tests. These “dip” samples serve to document the composition of the glass pool before

glass in the melt pool. All of the "dip samples" underwent visual examination to detect the
presence of a separate sulfate phase on the glass surface; select samples were also subjected to
complete chemical analysis. No macroscopic secondary phases were observed in any of the
discharged glasses or dip glass samples.

 All discharge glass samples were crushed and analyzed directly by XRF. Since boron and
lithium are not determined by XRF, for all but the terminal glasses from each test, boron and
lithium concentrations were calculated from the target concentrations, the initial concentrations
measured in the melt pool, and the nominal glass volume of the melter. A separate XRF analysis
for fluorine on selected, polished solid samples was performed and no fluorine was detected in
any of the analyzed samples. The XRF analyzed compositions of discharged glass samples are
provided in Tables 6.8 - 6.11. The analyzed composition of the terminal glass from each test is
compared to the target composition in Tables 6.12 and 6.13. The vast majority of the XRF
analysis results compare favorably to their corresponding target values and feed sample analyses
(see Section 6.1.2). All oxide values from glass discharged at the end of each test with a target
concentration of greater than one weight percent showed less than 10% deviation from the target

the glasses were of similar magnitude in feed samples and reflect the deficiency (magnesium in

 SECTION 7.0
 MONITORED OFF-GAS EMISSIONS

7.1 Particulate Sampling

 The melter exhaust was sampled for metals/particles/acid gases according to 40-CFR-60
Methods 3, 5, 26, and 29 at steady-state operating conditions during each test segment. The
concentrations of off-gas species that are present as particulates and gaseous species that are
collected in impinger solutions were derived from laboratory data on solutions extracted from air
samples (filters and various solutions) together with measurements of the volume of air sampled.
Particulate collection required isokinetic sampling, which entails removing gas from the exhaust
at the same velocity that the air is flowing in the duct (40-CFR-60, Methods 1-5). Typically, a
sample size of 30 dry standard cubic feet (dscf) was taken at a rate of between 0.5 and 0.75 dry
standard cubic feet per minute (dscfm). Total particulate loading was determined by combining
gravimetric analysis of the standard particle filter and chemical analysis of probe rinse solutions.
An additional impinger containing 2 N NaOH was added to the sampling train to ensure complete
scrubbing of all acid gases and to distinguish between halogens in the form of acids or
homonuclear molecular gases. The collected materials were analyzed using DCP-AES for the
majority of the constituents and ion chromatography (IC) for anions. Melter emission fluxes are
compared to feed fluxes in Table 7.1 for the four tests. Notice the distinction that is made between
constituents sampled as particles and as "gas". The "gaseous" constituents are operationally
defined as those species that are scrubbed in the impinger solutions after the air stream has passed
through a 0.3 µm heated filter. All samples were well within the 90 – 110% limits for isokinetic
sampling.

 Solids carryover from the melter was 1.46, 0.61, 0.19, and 0.25 percent of feed solids,
listed in chronological sampling order. These values are mostly within the range measured during
Enhanced LAW Glass Correlation testing, which resulted in solids carryover from the melter as
follows: Phase 1 - 0.53 to 0.61 percent of feed solids [73], Phase 2 - 0.17 to 0.36 percent of feed
solids [74], Phase 3 - 0.15 to 0.34 percent of feed solids [75], and Optimized 0.17 to 0.83 percent
of feed solids [76]. All of the Enhanced LAW Glass Correlation testing feeds had a lower feed
water content, which would reduce particle emissions, and contained rhenium, which would
increase particulate emissions. No tests have been conducted on the DM100 with the
AP107WDFL nominal composition; however, DM10 tests were conducted with recycling of the
off-gas effluents back to the melter feed [39]. The measured solids carryover of 0.53 and 0.75
percent of feed solids does not take into account the volatiles recycled back to the melter feed and
therefore the percent carryover would be significantly less without the recycle. Also, the rhenium
and technetium included in the DM10 tests would also contribute to higher solids carryover as
compared to the present DM100 tests. Solids carryover from the melter was anticipated to be
higher while processing the AP107DFLO9 composition as compared to the AP107DFLO5
composition due to the higher volatile and alkali content; however, the magnitude of the
difference suggests anomalous, transient conditions during the initial sampling period. That
sample is also unique in that almost half a percent of feed silicon is carried over into the exhaust

 43

as opposed to 0.03 percent or less for the other tests, suggesting bulk entrainment of feed
components during the sampling period. The near one and a half percent solids carryover
measured during the initial test is higher than the carryover in any of the Correlation tests, tests
with rhenium, and tests with recycle using a similar composition, and therefore is not considered
representative of the feed composition over the entire melter test. There is no obvious trend with
feed sugar content; emissions were higher with nominal sugar while processing the
AP107DFLO9 composition and were higher with reduced sugar while processing the
AP107DFLO5 composition. Alkali halide and sulfate compounds constitute the majority of the
particulate matter sampled during the tests.

 As expected, the feed elements emitted at the lowest melter decontamination factor (DF),
in ascending order, were chlorine, fluorine, potassium, sulfur, chromium, and lead, mostly
consistent with previous tests processing LAW simulants on the DM100 [5-16, 72-76]. Alkali
carryover showed the expected trend of increasing with increasing atomic number. Emissions of
chlorine were mostly particulate for all test samples while most fluorine was emitted as
particulate while processing the AP107DFLO9 composition and gaseous while processing the
AP107DFLO5 composition. Sulfur emissions were mostly particulate with smaller portions (0.58
– 3.3% of feed) detected as a gaseous species. Chlorine, fluorine, sulfur, and boron were the only
elements detected in the impinger solutions collected downstream of the heated particle filter in
the sampling train, which constitute the “gas” fraction of the melter emissions. There is no clear
effect of feed sugar content on the magnitude of emissions or partitioning between particulate and
gaseous constituents.

7.2 Particulate Sampled from the Off-Gas System

 Material accumulated in the cyclones downstream of the film cooler was removed,
quantified, and analyzed after testing intervals. The amounts and analysis of material removed
from the cyclone are provided in Table 7.2. The amount of material removed from the cyclone is
not indicative of the amount of particulate emitted from the melter for any one test since much of
the particulate emitted is fine particulate, which is not captured in the cyclones. Pre-filters for
HEPA filters downstream of the cyclones are routinely changed three times a day while
processing high alkali LAW feeds. This finding is consistent with tests processing high alkali
LAW on the DM1200 in which significant amounts of fine alkali salts are captured downstream

fine particulate from the exhaust stream [78-80]. Analysis of material accumulated in the cyclones
shows components present in similar proportions to the feed for many of the elements,
enrichments over feed for volatile elements, most notably chlorine and sulfur, and to a lesser
degree boron and sodium, as well as depletions of non-volatile silicon, and magnesium.
Enrichments of iron in the cyclone particulate were observed due presumably to corrosion of
stainless steel off-gas system components. Chlorine, which had the lowest measured DF across
the melter, had the greatest enrichment (10X or more) in the cyclone powders over the amounts in
the target glass.

7.3 Mass Balance of Volatile Constituents

 Table 7.3 provides the elemental mass balance around the melter for sulfur and chlorine

 The total recovery of sulfur was excellent at 91.1 – 99.9% and well within the range
achieved in previous tests for sulfur [38, 74, 75]. As illustrated in Figure 6.6, sulfur retention
increased to 90% of the target concentration while feeding the AP107DFLO9 composition and

often have lower retention of sulfur in the glass product. Like most previous test samples, sulfur
in the exhaust stream was all or mostly particulate.

 The total recovery of chlorine was 115 – 128% with a mass balance surplus that is
common for glass formulations targeting low levels of chlorine. Mass balance closure is typically
very difficult to achieve for halides due to the ubiquity of these constituents as trace level
contaminants in glass forming additives and chemicals used to produce the waste simulant [38,

compositions is typically about 50%, with the balance being emitted as particulate [8, 10, 12, 38,
75, 81, 84]; higher measured chlorine retention is often attributable to trace level contamination of
the feed, particularly when targeted at a low concentration. Although the majority of the chlorine
in the exhaust stream was particulate, significant amounts of chlorine were detected as a gaseous
component in each of the tests.

 SECTION 8.0
 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

 The objective of the present work was to investigate the potential effect of likely process
variations on the nominal glass composition for LAW AP-107 waste that is determined by the
LAW glass correlation (AP107WDFL), and its impact on glass properties and feed processing.
This was accomplished by evaluating the effect of glass and feed variations at crucible scale and
DM100 melter tests.

 Mass balance equations and WTP LAW measurement uncertainties summarized in [52,
53] were used to determine the resulting variations around the nominal glass composition,
AP107WDFL. In addition to factors that affect glass composition, the effects of factors that could
affect feed properties and processing behavior, such as feed water content and sugar content, were
also included. A Monte Carlo approach was used to perform the error propagation using the
assumed probability distributions for each of the uncertain variables and generate a set of 40,000
glass compositions that are variations around the nominal AP107WDFL glass formulation for the
AP-107 waste. Glass property composition models were used to estimate the corresponding
distributions of glass properties. There were 2337 glasses for which at least one property fell
outside of the corresponding WTP limits; in all cases, these properties were either viscosity (1818
glasses) or K-3 corrosion (519 glasses). These results were, in turn, used to select 15 glass
compositions, including those with the highest predicted viscosity and the highest predicted K-3
corrosion, for crucible scale fabrication and testing. No analytical uncertainty on nitrate, nitrite,
and organic constituents was available to evaluate any propagated error on the amount of sugar
added; nonetheless, an evaluation was made using the same strategy as for GFC weights, and
starting with an estimated 10% RSD in the analysis of NO2, NO3, acetate, formate, and oxalate.

 Crucible samples of the 15 glasses were prepared and characterized for glass composition,
secondary phases, sulfate solubility, PCT releases, VHT alteration, melt electrical conductivity,
melt viscosity, and K-3 refractory corrosion. All measured PCT releases and VHT alteration rates
are below the WTP product quality requirements for LAW glasses. The nominal formulation
AP107WDFL, and two feed formulations with the highest glass melt viscosities (AP107DFLO1
and AP107DFLO5) were tested for feed processing rate by the VGF. Variations of the
AP105DFLO5 formulation with the highest glass viscosity were tested with higher nitrate and
nitrite content and lower sugar addition. The nominal feed, AP107WDFL showed the highest
processing rate, with the other feeds showing lower but similar melt rates. Since VGF tests with
15% variation in sugar addition did not show significant effects on processing rate, melter tests
were conducted with larger variations in sugar addition.

 Based on the crucible-scale test results, two formulations, AP107DFLO5 and
AP107DFLO9, were selected for DM100 melter tests with the rationale that these are glasses at
the high-viscosity limit and at the high K-3 limit, respectively, and also at the two extremes in
viscosity. Glasses with the largest difference in melt viscosities are expected to show the largest
differences in processing rates. Feeds of AP107DFLO5 and AP107DFLO9 were prepared and

characterized for physical and rheological properties. Properties of feed AP107DFL09 are within

 A series of four tests were conducted on the DM100-BL melter to assess processing
behavior for variations around the AP107WDFL glass formulation developed for the AP-107
waste composition. Each feed was processed at the nominal glass temperature of 1150C with
bubbling fixed at 14 liter per minute achieving the highest attainable feed rate while maintaining a
near complete cold cap. This same approach was used to determine ease of processing LAW
compositions with different amounts of water [72] and different glass forming additives [67].
Attempts were made to replicate the melter configuration and operating conditions used in
previous LAW tests [5-16, 38, 67, 72-76] on the DM100-WV melter. These conditions include a
near complete cold cap, which is between 80-95% melt surface coverage for the DM100 since a
100% cold cap tends to lead to "bridging" in smaller melters. The level of glass in the melter was
maintained between 18 and 20 inches from the bottom of the DM100-BL throughout the majority
of the testing to mimic operation with the DM100-WV melter. Both compositions tested used the
same AP-107 waste composition. The low viscosity AP107DFLO9 glass formulation targeted
18.22 wt% Na2O and 0.47 wt% SO3 in the product glass and the high viscosity AP107DFLO5
glass formulation targeted 15.29 wt% Na2O and 0.39 wt% SO3 in the product glass. The tests
were further distinguished by using the nominal and half the nominal amounts of sugar. These
four tests were nominally 50-hour test segments and produced over 1700 kilograms of glass from
nearly four metric tons of feed.

 Both feed variations were readily processed with cold cap conditions mostly similar to
those experienced in previous DM100 tests with LAW simulants. Deposits formed along the
walls of the melter after discharging glass, which lowered the glass level in the melter leaving
material adhering to the walls out of contact with the molten glass in all but one instance these
deposits incorporated into the glass pool without any manual intervention. In an effort to
maximize the feed rate while processing the AP107DFLO5 composition with 0.5 Sugar, manual
methods were required to remove deposits, which were readily removed as soft deposits and
returned them to the cold cap surface. This occurred during a pause in melter feeding to transfer
feed; when feeding resumed the feed rate was decreased by about ten percent which obviated the
need for further manual intervention. This level of manual intervention was significantly less than
earlier tests with optimized glass formulations [73-76]. No foamy glass or secondary phases were
observed in any of the glass discharges or melt pool samples. Based on the measured glass
conversion ratios, steady state production rates were within the narrow range of 2015 to
2078 kg/m 2 /day for the first three tests, suggesting little difference between the ease of processing

the three feeds. These rates are comparable to those obtained under the same conditions (fixed
bubbling at 14 lpm) with the ORPLA20 glass composition with similar water content
(2000 kg/m 2 /day) [72] and different additives (2000-2100 kg/m 2 /day) [67]. Conversely, the last
 P P P P

test conducted with AP107DFLO5 and 0.25 sugar processed at a slower rate of 1561 kg/m 2 /day. P P

Collectively, the results indicate that lower than nominal sugar concentration can lead to
significant reductions in processing rates for some feed compositions (23% for AP107DFLO5).

 The primary reason for the addition of sugar to Hanford LAW feeds with high
concentrations of oxidants (nitrates and nitrites) is mitigation of foaming that can disrupt melter

 48

operations. Addition of sugar at about 0.5 stoichiometric ratio has been demonstrated to
effectively mitigate foaming during vitrification of Hanford LAW feeds without over-reducing
the glass, which can lead to deleterious sulfide formation. As the amount of sugar addition is
reduced to below the 0.5 sugar ratio, the tendency for melt foaming increases. Gas bubbles that
are formed and get trapped below the cold-cap can form a thermally insulating layer that can
negatively impact feed processing rate; this tendency increases as the glass viscosity increases. In
addition, the combustion of sugar in the cold-cap, which is exothermic, aids in releasing gas
bubbles trapped below the cold-cap, thus improving the processing rate. We therefore expect
reduced amounts of sugar addition, especially at amounts much lower than the 0.5 sugar ratio, to
negatively impact processing rate, especially for glasses with high viscosity. In the present case,
the production rate decreased from 2015 to 1561 kg/m2/day as the sugar ratio was reduced from
0.5 to 0.25 for AP107DFLO5; this glass has a high measured viscosity of 138 poise at 1150 °C.

 There are few test results on LAW melter feed processing rates with varying sugar
stoichiometry, especially in the low sugar range, and no other test results with low sugar and a
high viscosity glass melt. Testing of LAW C2 feeds with sugar ratios in the range of 0.5 to 0.7
did not show significant differences in processing rates [16]. LAW A3 feeds showed a processing
rate of 2000 kg/m2/day at 0.5 sugar ratio and 1800 kg/m2/day at 0.25 sugar ratio in DM100 melter
testing [12]. However, the same LAW A3 feed processed in the LAW Pilot Melter did not show
any difference in processing rate at 0.25 and 0.5 sugar ratios [24]. The LAW C2 and LAW A3
glasses used in the above tests had low measured glass viscosities of 35 poise at 1150 °C.

 Product glass samples from the end of each test were analyzed for composition, melt
viscosity/conductivity, and various measures of chemical durability; feed samples were analyzed
to confirm the composition of feed used in each test. Feed and glass samples were about forty
percent low in magnesium due to a magnesium deficiency in the additive olivine. Glass samples
were about five percent low in sodium due to a small deficiency in the feed and loss during the
vitrification process. Glass chemical durability and melt properties measured on four glasses
taken at the end of each of the tests compare favorably with the corresponding crucible melt
results. Measured PCT releases (Na, B, Si) and VHT alterations are marginally lower for melter
glasses than for the corresponding crucible glasses due in part to melter glasses being about five
relative percent below target in sodium. However, such differences were not observed in the
measured viscosity and electrical conductivity of the crucible and melter glasses. All of the
measured values of the crucible and melter glasses are within acceptable limits.

 The melter exhaust was sampled during each test for particulate and gaseous species to
determine the effect of different glass formulations and sugar content on emissions as well as to
enable mass balance determinations for select elements of interest. Solids carryover from the
melter was 1.46, 0.61, 0.19, and 0.25 percent of feed solids, listed in chronological sampling
order. These values are mostly within the range measured during Enhanced LAW Glass
Correlation testing, which gave carryover from the melter from 0.15 to 0.83 percent of feed solids
[73-76]. All of the Enhanced LAW Glass Correlation testing feeds had a lower feed water content
that would reduce particle emissions and contained rhenium that would increase particulate
emissions. No tests have been conducted on the DM100 with the AP107WDFL nominal
composition for comparison. Solids carryover from the melter was anticipated to be higher while
processing the AP107DFLO9 composition as compared to the AP107DFLO5 composition due to

 49

the higher volatile and alkali content; however, the magnitude of the difference suggests
anomalous, transient conditions during the initial sampling period. That sample is also unique in
that almost half a percent of feed silicon was carried over into the exhaust as opposed to 0.03
percent or less for the other tests suggesting bulk entrainment of feed components during the
sampling period. The near one and a half percent solids carryover measured during the initial test
is higher than the carryover in any of the Correlation tests, tests with rhenium, and tests with
recycle using a similar composition and therefore is not considered representative of the feed
composition over the entire melter test. There is no obvious trend with feed sugar content;
emissions were higher with nominal sugar while processing the AP107DFLO9 composition and
were higher with reduced sugar while processing the AP107DFLO5 composition. Alkali halide
and sulfate compounds constitute the majority of the particulate matter sampled during the tests.
The observations from the melter exhaust samples were confirmed by the analysis of particulate
material accumulated downstream in cyclone filtration units, which contained all of the feed
components in similar proportions to the feed for many of the elements, enrichments over feed for
volatile elements chlorine and sulfur, as well as depletions of non-volatile aluminum, silicon, and
magnesium. The most abundant nitrogen species monitored was NO followed by NO2, which was
about 14 times lower in concentration at nominal sugar content and about 7 times lower in
concentration when half as much sugar was added. Like the preceding LAW tests, the
concentrations of nitrogen and carbon oxides in the exhaust are dependent on the concentrations
of nitrates and nitrite in the feed and the feed rate. Nitrogen oxide concentrations were higher in
tests with reduced added reductants, as expected. In keeping with processing LAW simulants
containing nitrates, nitrites, and sugar added as a reductant, significant amounts of ammonia,
carbon monoxide, and hydrogen cyanide were measured as byproducts of reactions between sugar
and nitrogen oxides. Concentrations of byproducts of complete and incomplete combustion in the
exhaust streams were significantly less in the tests conducted with half the amount of added
reductant. Manual intervention was required in three of the four tests to remove deposits from the
film cooler, as indicated by a high pressure differential across the film cooler.

 The total recovery of the volatile feed constituents for the present tests was 91.1 – 99.9%
for sulfur and 115- 128% for chlorine. Chlorine and sulfur retentions in the glass were 45-63%,
and 82-90%, respectively, with no obvious influence of the other feed constituents including
reductant concentrations. Chlorine retention while vitrifying high alkali LAW compositions is
typically about 50% with the balance being emitted as particulate; higher measured chlorine
retentions and therefore total recoveries are often attributable to trace level contamination of the
feed. Sulfur retention in glass was between 80-90%, consistent with previous tests with high
alkali LAW compositions.

 Crucible and melter tests were completed with glass formulations that could result from
process variations around the nominal glass formulation for LAW AP-107 (AP107WDFL).
Sulfate solubility measurements on the 15 crucible glasses indicate little likelihood of secondary
sulfate phase formation. All 15 crucible glasses met the product quality requirements for WTP
LAW glass. The measured feed viscosity and yield stress for formulation AP107DFLO5 were
higher than the WTP limits but could probably be brought within limits through slight dilution of
the feed. Glass formulations with the highest and lowest melt viscosities and nominal sugar
additions, processed in the melter at a rate above 2000 kg/m 2 /day of glass. Although decreasing
