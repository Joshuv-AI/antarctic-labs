 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Indoor air quality (IAQ) is critical for human health. Poor IAQ is linked to respiratory issues,
cardiovascular diseases, and cancer. Indoor pollutants are emitted by typical household items such as
cleaning products, personal care items, building materials, and tap water - an understudied volatile
organic compound (VOC) source. This document presents the Residential Water Inhalation Risk
Calculator (RWIRC), which estimates daily VOC exposure concentrations from various household water
uses, such as showering and dishwashing, to assess exposure risks for the most vulnerable occupant.
Integrated into the Risk Assessment Information System (RAIS) and sponsored by the US Department of
Energy (DOE), the calculator divides a house into three compartments: shower, bathroom, and other
spaces, accounting for daily water usage patterns and calculating VOC concentrations. Exposure data
generated using the calculator can assist health assessors in estimating excess lifetime cancer risk (ELCR)
and hazard index (HI) from VOC inhalation. Unlike traditional exposure models that utilize Andelman’s
constant, the RWIRC continuously assesses variability in VOC concentrations and environmental
conditions using differential equations to track VOC concentrations and air exchange between
compartments. The calculator also provides unique volatilization fractions for each chemical and
appliance, enhancing accuracy of the exposure concentration estimation. The RWIRC is accessible online
and allows users to customize parameters (i.e., number of bathrooms, water temperature, and exhaust fan
conditions) and input VOC characteristics (i.e., tap water and ambient air concentrations). This document
provides a step-by-step guide on implementing the calculator. It also provides comparisons with the
ATSDR-SHOWER calculator, using eight VOCs with varying physicochemical properties to reveal
differences in algorithms and output concentrations. Simulations also assess how bathroom door positions
and exhaust fan usage affect VOC exposure. The calculator results can enhance EPA risk screening levels
for inhalation exposure to VOCs from tap water, offering a sophisticated tool for assessing inhalation
risks and improving public health protection.

  1. INDOOR AIR QUALITY AND IMPACT OF VOLATILES FROM TAP WATER USAGE

Indoor air quality (IAQ), which represents the impact of indoor air pollution on human health, gained
significant attention from the scientific community in particular during the COVID-19 pandemic, when
people spent most of their time indoors (Jones 1999, Abouleish 2021, Morawska et al. 2024). Poor IAQ
leads to deterioration of occupant health, including respiratory problems, introduction of allergens,
cardiovascular diseases, and even cancer (Seguel et al. 2017, Kumar et al. 2021, Salonen et al. 2024).
Sources of indoor air pollutants include cleaning products, personal care products, furnishings,
combustion/cooking appliances, and building materials, all of which can release several chemical and
biological components that are linked to a range of diseases (Weschler 2011, Habre et al. 2014). Humans
themselves can also contribute to indoor air pollution and impact indoor air chemistry through the
reaction of oxidants such as ozone with squalene and other human skin lipids (Tang et al. 2016, Weschler
2016). With six carbon–carbon reactive double bonds, the ozonolysis of the squalene generates significant
concentration of oxygenated volatile species, including acetone, geranyl acetone (GA), 6-methyl-5-
hepten-2-one (6-MHO), and 4-oxopentanal (4-OPA) at a rapid rate (Salvador et al. 2019). Since emission
of such volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from different indoor sources can substantially impact
human health, careful understanding of the occupant-associated VOC exposure is necessary.

One of the prevailing but understudied sources of VOCs in indoor environments is the evaporation of
gases from tap water. Previous research highlighted the importance of VOC inhalation from the
evaporation of VOCs contained in tap water, which can exceed other exposure routes such as ingestion
and dermal absorption (Chen et al. 2003, Weisel et al. 2020). Among indoor activities related to the use of
tap water, showering contributes the highest concentration of indoor air VOCs due to the amount of water
used during the activity (McKone 1987). Conditions during showering such as elevated water
temperature, turbulent flow of water and mode of delivery from the showerhead contribute substantially
to the release of VOCs from water (Chen et al. 2003).

Assessment of indoor air VOC concentrations released by tap water usage is necessary to account for the
overall exposure of household occupants to compounds that can deteriorate human health. The

2. GENERAL DESCRIPTION OF RESIDENTIAL WATER INHALATION RISK
 CALCULATOR

The RWIRC provides environmental risk assessment information from household tap water usage and
provides insight for US Environmental Protection Agency Superfund risk assessors on the general indoor
VOC concentration profile. There is a potential for several compounds to enter households via tap water
usage. Unwanted VOCs may enter surface water or groundwater sourced for tap water from many
activities, including manufacturing, industrial, agricultural, and residential use of chemicals, as well as
environmental pollution and natural sources and emissions. The modeled information can be utilized for
evaluation of excess lifetime cancer risk (ELCR) and hazard quotient (HQ) from inhalation of VOCs
during household use of tap water. Briefly, the indoor air concentration is based on the instantaneous air
exchange between three compartments of a typical house: shower, bathroom, and other spaces in the
house such as dining areas, bedrooms, and living rooms. The calculator considers the different daily
household tap water usage time and volume from showers, bathrooms, kitchen sinks, etc. The output of
the calculator estimates the concentration of VOCs in the different areas of the house and the integrated
daily exposure concentration to household occupants. The calculated exposure concentrations can be used
by risk assessors to determine the ELCR and HQ in households with unwanted VOCs in tap water. The
RWIRC also uses the same database of physicochemical parameters and toxicity values that is currently
used by multiple EPA risk assessment calculators. This decision was intentional to promote unity for
DOE when submitting risk assessments for EPA review.

One of the primary differentiators of the RWIRC from other tap water use calculators is the improved
capability to continuously assess variability in volatility and concentrations of chemicals throughout the
day as changes occur in the indoor air environment (i.e., air flow, temperature, humidity), tap water
parameters, and various household tap water VOC emission sources (i.e., appliances). In comparison,
current transport models are based on Andelman’s Constant (0.5 L/m3) for the prediction of VOC release
from tap water into indoor air. In essence, Andelman’s model assumes that all compounds with Henry’s
law constants of 2×10−6 atm⋅m3/mol or greater will be volatilized, and VOCs with values below such
constants will remain in the water (Andelman 1990). Likewise, simplified models misrepresent the
exposure when evaluating semi-volatile compounds. Other limitations of Andelman’s model include lack
of differentiation of VOC emission sources (i.e. appliances), disregard of the variability of compound
volatility and water temperature, and assumption of constant contaminant concentration throughout the
day. The RWIRC offers improvements beyond the use of Andelman’s constant by increasing the
confidence (i.e., reducing uncertainty) in modeling inhalation exposure concentrations of VOCs from
household tap water use. An application of the RWIRC is visualized in Figure 1, which showcases the
diurnal variation of the ethyl acetate and vinyl chloride exposure with different showering frequencies.
The four plots of each compound represent the variation in the frequency of showering. Included also
here is the exposure concentration using Andelman’s constant (orange line). Initial tap water
concentration: 100 µg/L. The calculated household average daily exposure concentration simulated using
the RWIRC are also included (red). Ethyl acetate and vinyl chloride represent a wide range of volatility
(see section 4 for VOC properties), making them good VOC representatives. Evident in the plots with
different numbers of daily showers (1, 4, 8, and 16) are the fluctuating exposure across the day,

highlighting the ineffectiveness of the utilization of Andelman’s constant in the assessment of indoor air
VOC exposure.

The inhalation unit risk (IUR) is defined as the upper-bound excess lifetime carcinogenic risk estimated to
result from continuous exposure to an agent at a concentration of 1 µg/m3 in air. IUR toxicity values are
expressed in units of (µg/m3)−1.

A reference concentration (RfC) is an estimate (with uncertainty spanning perhaps an order of magnitude)
of a continuous inhalation exposure to the human population (including sensitive subgroups) that is likely
to be without an appreciable risk of deleterious effects during a lifetime. Various types of RfCs are
available depending on the exposure route (oral or inhalation), the critical effect (developmental or other),
and the length of exposure being evaluated (chronic or subchronic).

The RfC and IUR values used in the RWIRC are selected from a hierarchy of sources. The hierarchy is

7. COMPARISON WITH ATSDR: USER EXPERIENCE AND EQUATION BASIS

Besides the RWIRC, the Shower and Household Water-Use Exposure (SHOWER) calculator was also
developed to capture the inhalation exposure of the occupants from compound emissions from showering
and due to their presence in different areas of the house (Mellard et al. 2019). The calculator was crafted
by the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) to assess the chemical exposure at
Superfund sites, particularly from the volatilization of chemicals from household water. First released in
May 2018, the SHOWER calculator can provide real-time exposure concentration of the residents to
different water sources such as clothes washers, dishwashers, toilets, and faucets.

The RWIRC and SHOWER calculators provide different user capabilities and output results, even with
the same input parameters—such as initial concentration of compounds both in water and ambient air. For
instance, the RWIRC can be accessed online, whereas SHOWER requires local installation; thus, the
calculations on the RWIRC can be performed on any computer with an internet connection. Moreover,
batch processing of several VOCs can be performed on the RWIRC, which substantially reduces the
routine time needed for multiple indoor VOC analyses.

 8. CONCLUSION

The Residential Water Inhalation Risk Calculator presented here was developed to simulate real-time
concentrations of indoor VOCs. Inorganic and organic gases emitted from household activities, such as
showering and bathroom usage, affect indoor air quality, underscoring the need to monitor daily
variations in exposure concentrations. Unlike typical models that rely on a fixed Andelman’s constant, the
RWIRC can simulate real-time exposure concentrations for occupants within a three-compartment system
comprising the shower, bathroom, and main living room. Both calculators present risk results; however,
these results are not compared herein because they are linearly proportional to the air concentrations.

The RWIRC is accessible online, requiring no prior installation for users or health evaluators.
Furthermore, users have significant flexibility to customize household parameters, including the duration
of showers, dimensions of indoor spaces, and the temperature and flow rates of water from fixtures.
Chemical parameters, particularly Henry’s law constant, can also be adjusted to reflect the environmental
conditions in indoor households. The calculator utilized eight VOCs with varying volatilities to assess its
sensitivity and to compare it with a similar calculator from the ATSDR (the SHOWER model).

Default runs of both calculators indicated that the RWIRC exhibited deviations of 44% compared to the
reasonable maximum exposure (RME) scenario of the ATSDR calculator. One of the key differences
between the two calculators lies in their calculation of molecular diffusivity in fractional volatilization of
each contaminant; using the same values for both reduced the average deviation by 38%. For both
calculators, sensitivities to various door conditions (i.e., open versus closed) and the use of exhaust
ventilation were also examined. Results showed that door conditions had a minimal impact on VOC
exposure concentrations. However, activating the exhaust fan during showering and bathroom activities
resulted in a 15% and 34% decrease in exposure concentrations, respectively; these results highlight the
importance of ventilation in reducing indoor air pollution.
