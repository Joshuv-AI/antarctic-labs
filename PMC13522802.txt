Therapeutic Gfral silencing via antisense oligonucleotides ameliorates cancer-associated cachexia and extends survival in tumor-bearing mice
Authors: Hyun Jung Hong, Min Hee Lee, Minsung Park, Jung Tae Kim, Jaehun Kim, Sang-Hyeon Ju, Ju Hee Lee, Jong-Woo Sohn, Koeun Shong, Daiki Setoyama, Ho Min Kim, Jinkuk Kim, Minho Shong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCancer cachexia is a life-threatening wasting syndrome that can be driven by tumor-derived GDF15 signaling through the brainstem-restricted receptor GFRAL. Despite its clinical importance, central genetic interventions targeting this axis remain limited. Here, we develop a potent antisense oligonucleotide (Gfral-ASO, A427) that specifically silences Gfral expression in the central nervous system. Using orthotopic mouse models of severe colon (MC38) and pancreatic (KPC and MPC1) cancers, we show that a single intracerebroventricular administration of Gfral ASO markedly blunts GFRAL expression and pathogenic c-Fos neuronal activation in the area postrema and nucleus tractus solitarius. This central blockade reverses cachectic phenotypes, restoring body weight, muscle mass, and adipose depots. Notably, Gfral ASO achieves superior restoration of muscle (grip) strength compared with systemic anti-GDF15 neutralizing antibody. Crucially, silencing central GFRAL signaling profoundly extends survival of tumor-bearing mice. Our findings highlight central GFRAL antagonism via ASO as a promising therapeutic strategy for cancer cachexia.Keywords: GFRAL, GDF15, antisense oligonucleotides, cancer-associated cachexia, muscle wasting, metabolic dysfunction

Introduction

Cancer-associated cachexia (CAC) is a multifactorial and debilitating wasting syndrome characterized by progressive loss of systemic energy reserves, primarily manifested through involuntary skeletal muscle wasting and adipose tissue depletion.1,2,3 Prevalent in over 50% of patients with advanced malignancies, CAC compromises physical function and quality of life while diminishing therapeutic tolerance and limiting overall survival.4,5,6 CAC also impairs the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), accelerating clearance of therapeutic antibodies and fostering a pro-tumorigenic, immunosuppressive microenvironment.7,8 Despite this clinical significance, pharmacological interventions capable of reversing or arresting CAC remain elusive, representing a critical unmet medical need.9

Growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) has emerged as a pivotal endocrine mediator of CAC.10,11,12,13 In patients with various malignancies, circulating GDF15 is markedly elevated, primarily through tumor-derived secretion, and correlates strongly with disease progression and poor clinical outcomes (Figure S1A).14,15,16,17 GDF15 signals exclusively through glial-cell-derived neurotrophic factor family receptor alpha-like (GFRAL), which exhibits neuroanatomically restricted expression in the area postrema (AP) and nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) of the hindbrain.18,19 Activation of the GFRAL-RET (rearranged during transfection) signaling complex triggers central neural circuits that drive anorexia and systemic catabolism, ultimately manifesting as progressive wasting.18,19,20,21,22,23,24,25

Recent preclinical and clinical investigations targeting the GDF15-GFRAL axis have demonstrated marked efficacy against CAC-associated phenotypes. Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) against GDF15 restore body weight and preserve skeletal muscle mass while ameliorating systemic wasting in both murine models and cancer patients.26,27,28,29,30 While these findings validate the axis as a therapeutic target, current approaches rely on systemic neutralization of the circulating ligand, leaving the central receptor itself—and whether silencing it can reverse cachexia—unaddressed. Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) offer an established platform for sequence-specific downregulation of disease-associated mRNAs, with several therapies approved for central nervous system (CNS) indications.31,32,33 Their high target specificity, chemical stability, and capacity for regional gene modulation render ASOs well suited for CNS-directed interventions.32,34 Consequently, ASOs targeting Gfral mRNA (Gfral-ASOs) are designed to enable selective depletion of GFRAL within hindbrain neurons, providing a CNS-directed approach to intercept the axis.

In this study, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of chemically modified Gfral-ASOs in disrupting the GDF15-GFRAL axis. Using orthotopic murine models of severe colon (MC38) and highly aggressive pancreatic (KPC and MPC1) cancers, we show that Gfral-ASOs suppress hindbrain Gfral mRNA, reversing core CAC manifestations, including progressive weight loss and skeletal muscle atrophy. This central intervention provides superior restoration of muscle grip strength compared with systemic anti-GDF15 neutralizing antibody. Critically, ASO-mediated GFRAL depletion significantly extends overall survival in these models. Collectively, our findings establish central Gfral silencing as a robust and translationally viable modality to address the critical therapeutic gap in the management of CAC.

Results

Gfral-ASOs selectively suppress Gfral expression in vitro and in vivo

To develop effective Gfral-ASOs for treatment of CAC, we designed a panel of 16 ASOs targeting exon 5 of the Gfral gene. We prioritized conserved sequences shared between humans and mice to ensure translational relevance. All candidates utilized a gapmer configuration capable of recruiting RNase H for target mRNA knockdown. They employed 2′-O-methoxyethyl (2′-MOE) sugar modifications along with mixed phosphorothioate and phosphodiester linkages, mimicking the chemical backbone of tofersen—a gapmer ASO recently approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for SOD1-ALS (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis). This backbone enhances nuclease resistance and binding stability.34,35,36 Among these, four lead candidates—A427, A428, A429, and A430—were prioritized based on in vitro screening of their knockdown efficacy (data not shown; Figure 1A).

To assess knockdown efficacy, we utilized a HEK293T-derived reporter cell line stably expressing RET and GFRAL, alongside a serum response element (SRE)-driven luciferase reporter (SRE-Luc2-RET-GFRAL). Quantitative RT-PCR analysis following ASO transfection revealed a robust depletion of Gfral expression. Specifically, treatment with 50 nM of A427 reduced Gfral levels by 43.9% at 24 h and 60.9% at 48 h. Furthermore, exposure to 100 nM of A427, A428, or A429 for 48 h suppressed expression by 71.3%, 68.7%, and 45.0%, respectively (Figure 1B).

To comprehensively evaluate the safety profile of the lead candidate A427, we conducted toxicity assessments in adult C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old), comparing it to PBS (vehicle) and control ASO (nontargeting ASO).37 Two independent experimental sets were performed via intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection (Figure 1C). Mice were administered PBS, control ASO, or A427 at concentrations of 100, 300, or 500 μg, and dose-dependent toxicity was assessed 2 weeks post-administration. For time-dependent toxicity, mice received a single dose (500 μg) and were assessed at 3, 4, and 5 weeks post-injection.

Throughout the experimental period, no significant differences in body weight were observed among the groups (Figures 1D and G). Serum analysis revealed no signs of systemic toxicity across all experimental sets. Specifically, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels remained within normal ranges, specifically 30–40 IU/L (Figures 1E and H) and 110–180 IU/L (Figures 1F and I), respectively, while all other toxicity markers remained within normal physiological limits (Figures S1B–S1I). Consistent with these findings, behavioral assessments showed no functional deficits; the latency to fall in the rotarod test and the immobility duration in the tail suspension test were comparable to those of the control groups (Figures 1J and 1K).

We further assessed acute toxicity by monitoring survival following ICV injection of ASOs into neonatal mice (postnatal days 2) (Figure S1J). While the toxicity control ASO induced severe mortality (>90% lethality), both the PBS and control ASO groups exhibited 100% survival. Similarly, mice injected with the candidate ASOs (A427, A428, A429, and A430) showed robust survival rates of 85.7%, 100%, 100%, and 100%, respectively (Figures S1K and S1L). Collectively, these data indicate that Gfral-targeting ASOs possess a favorable safety profile comparable to that of control ASO (nontargeting ASO).

To investigate the duration of the inhibitory effect of the Gfral ASO, we administered a single dose of A427 (500 μg) in adult C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old). The suppression of GFRAL expression in the AP was evaluated at 2, 3, and 4 weeks post-injection. At 2 weeks, the number of GFRAL+ neurons was reduced by 47.1%, with a 36.8% decrease in signal intensity (Figures 1L–1N). This inhibitory effect was sustained for up to 4 weeks, with significant reductions observed in both neuron count (41.5% at 3 weeks; 34.9% at 4 weeks) and signal intensity (51.2% at 3 weeks; 41.7% at 4 weeks) (Figures 1M and 1N). These findings demonstrate that a single ICV administration of A427 is sufficient to maintain significant GFRAL suppression for at least 4 weeks in vivo.

Collectively, these findings establish Gfral-targeting ASOs as efficacious and well-tolerated tools for receptor inhibition. The sustained downregulation of Gfral expression both in vitro and in vivo settings renders these Gfral-ASOs suitable for evaluating therapeutic efficacy within GDF15-GFRAL-driven murine models of CAC.

Establishment of an orthotopic CAC model with peripheral wasting and central GFRAL activation

ELISA screening of conditioned media from five murine cancer cell lines (MC38, KPC, MPC1, C26, and CT26) identified MC38 and KPC as high-GDF15 secretors (89.0 and 56.88 pg/mL, respectively), versus negligible secretion by MPC1, C26, and CT26 (Figure S2A). MC38 and KPC were therefore selected as high-GDF15 models and MPC1 as a low-GDF15 control. To test whether elevated GDF15 drives systemic cachexia in vivo, we established an orthotopic colon cancer model by cecal implantation of MC38 cells, monitoring circulating GDF15 and cachectic parameters (body weight, food intake, and grip strength) weekly, with terminal tissue harvest at weeks 3–5 (Figure S2B). Plasma GDF15 rose progressively, from ∼2-fold at week 1 to >35-fold (>3,000 pg/mL) by week 5 relative to sham controls (Figure S2C), accompanied by attenuated body weight gain, reduced cumulative food intake, and impaired grip strength throughout the 5-week period (Figures S2D–S2F).

Tumor-bearing mice developed progressive mass depletion of inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT) and gastrocnemius (GA) muscle (Figures S2G and S2H). Morphometric analysis revealed marked cellular remodeling: by week 5, mean lipid droplet (LD) area had decreased by 78.3% in iWAT and 52.9% in epididymal WAT (eWAT) (Figures S3A and S3B), while mean myofiber cross-sectional area (CSA) had decreased by 26.5% in GA, 15.3% in tibialis anterior (TA), 14.1% in extensor digitorum longus (EDL), and 30.8% in soleus (Figures S3C–S3F). Both adipose and muscle showed a consistent distribution shift toward smaller LDs and a reciprocal loss of large-diameter myofibers (Figures S3G–S3L).38,39

We next asked whether this peripheral wasting was accompanied by central GFRAL activation. By immunofluorescence, we examined GFRAL-expressing (GFRAL+) neurons in the AP and NTS and their co-localization with the immediate-early gene product c-Fos (Figure S3M). Although GFRAL expression and GFRAL+ neuron counts were comparable between tumor-bearing and sham mice (Figures S3M and S3N), c-Fos was markedly elevated in tumor-bearing animals (Figures S3M–S3O). The proportion of c-Fos+ cells among GFRAL+ neurons rose sharply with tumor progression: at week 3, 51.2% (AP) and 63.02% (NTS) of GFRAL+ neurons co-expressed c-Fos versus 1.6% and 2.2% in controls (Figures S3M–S3P) and remained elevated at weeks 4 and 5. Conversely, among AP c-Fos+ neurons, 33.2%, 26.4%, and 28.2% were GFRAL+ at weeks 3, 4, and 5, respectively (Figures S3M–S3Q).

Collectively, elevated circulating GDF15 in orthotopic MC38-tumor-bearing mice drives profound peripheral remodeling—accelerated adipose depletion and progressive skeletal muscle wasting—alongside sustained activation of hindbrain circuits spanning both GFRAL+ and GFRAL− neuronal populations, validating this model as a robust platform for investigating GDF15-mediated cachexia.

ASO-mediated inhibition of Gfral alleviates CAC by improving metabolic and muscle function

To exploit the durability of A427 knockdown, which persists for at least 4 weeks, we first evaluated a pretreatment strategy, administering a single ICV injection 2 weeks before orthotopic MC38 implantation and analyzing mice four weeks post-injection (Figure S4A). Although total body weight gain trajectories were comparable across tumor-bearing groups, A427-pretreated mice retained significantly higher terminal body weight, lean mass, and grip strength than the PBS and control ASO groups (Figures S4B–S4E). This protection was achieved despite persistently elevated plasma GDF15 (Figure S4F), indicating that central GFRAL silencing decouples tumor-derived signals from host catabolism.

Target engagement was confirmed by significant depletion of AP GFRAL+ neurons to 50.4% of control ASO levels and of fluorescence intensity to 42.1% (Figures S4G–S4J). Consistent with our baseline characterization (Figure S3N), hindbrain GFRAL expression was unaffected by tumor progression (Figures S4G and S4H). Although tumor-bearing mice showed marked c-Fos induction across the AP-NTS (Figures S4G–S4J), A427 selectively attenuated this activation in GFRAL+ neurons while leaving neighboring GFRAL− neurons unchanged (Figures S4G–S4J), indicating selective disruption of the GDF15-GFRAL axis without compromising GFRAL-independent hindbrain circuits.

At the tissue level, pretreatment with A427 significantly attenuated the loss of iWAT mass relative to the control ASO group (Figure S4K), whereas the partial preservation of GA muscle mass did not reach significance (Figure S4L). A427 maintained adipocyte morphology and LD size, preempting the severe adipocyte shrinkage observed in tumor-bearing controls, and preserved GA myofiber CSA (Figure S4M). Accordingly, A427 induced a rightward shift in the iWAT LD distribution, reducing small-diameter adipocytes (<800 μm2) and increasing larger cells (1,200–2,800 μm2) toward the Sham control pattern (Figure S4N), and the GA myofiber distribution shifted similarly, with fewer atrophic fibers (400–1,600 μm2) and an enrichment of larger fibers (>2,000 μm2) (Figure S4O).

To extend these findings to a therapeutic setting, we administered A427 after the onset of established cachexia, initiating a single ICV injection at week 3, when tumor burden and cachectic manifestations were evident (Figures S2, and S3), and assessing rescue at week 5 (Figure 2A). A427 significantly slowed weight loss relative to the control ASO group: although terminal body weights (unadjusted for tumor mass) were comparable across tumor-bearing groups, A427 yielded a superior longitudinal weight trajectory (Figure 2B), and tumor-free body mass was significantly higher than in control ASO mice (Figure 2C). A427 also maintained higher cumulative food intake (Figure 2D) and improved grip strength from 1 week post-injection through the 5-week endpoint (Figures 2E and 2F). Terminal plasma GDF15 remained comparable across tumor-bearing groups (Figure 2G), confirming that these benefits arose from central receptor silencing rather than reduced circulating ligand.

At the 5-week endpoint, hindbrain GFRAL and c-Fos were characterized immunohistochemically (Figure 2H). Baseline GFRAL+ neuron counts and staining intensity in the AP and NTS were comparable between Sham and tumor-bearing control groups, whereas A427 significantly depleted both GFRAL+ neuron number (to 58.1% of control ASO) and fluorescence intensity (to 49.6%) (Figures 2I and 2J). c-Fos was robustly induced across the AP and NTS in all tumor-bearing groups and was not altered by A427 (Figure 2K), indicating that A427 selectively suppresses GFRAL within target neurons without disrupting the broader, tumor-induced activation, or integrity of the hindbrain.

Therapeutically, A427 attenuated the depletion of adipose and skeletal muscle mass, preserving greater iWAT (0.15 vs. 0.09 g) and eWAT (0.18 vs. 0.10 g) mass and higher terminal GA weight (0.16 vs. 0.14 g) than the control ASO group (Figures 2L and 2M). Histologically, A427 preserved adipocyte architecture and LD size, attenuating the profound LD shrinkage seen in PBS and control ASO mice (925.58 vs. 651.78 μm2; Figures 2N and 2O); the LD-size distribution shifted from small (200–800 μm2) toward larger droplets (>1,200 μm2), approaching the Sham control profile (Figure 2P). Consistently, mean GA myofiber CSA was significantly preserved (1,204.57 vs. 1,010.72 μm2; Figures 2N and Q), with a rightward shift toward larger fibers that recapitulated the Sham distribution (Figure 2R).

Collectively, ASO-mediated Gfral silencing in the orthotopic MC38 model mitigates key cachectic phenotypes—adipose depletion, muscle atrophy, and impaired physical performance—demonstrating that central inhibition of the GDF15-GFRAL axis can arrest established wasting independent of circulating GDF15.

Efficacy of Gfral-ASO on CAC is dependent on circulating GDF15 level

To validate the GDF15 dependency of Gfral-targeted ASO efficacy, we established orthotopic pancreatic cancer models using KPC and MPC1 cell lines, which exhibit high and low circulating GDF15 levels, respectively. Following the therapeutic regimen, mice received a single ICV injection of either Gfral-ASO (A427), a control ASO, or PBS at 3 weeks post-implantation. Interventional efficacy was evaluated by longitudinal monitoring of cachectic parameters, specifically body weight gain, daily food intake, and grip strength, throughout the 5-week study period (Figure 3A).

Longitudinal analysis of total body weight trajectories showed that gain remained largely comparable across experimental groups over the 5-week study period (Figure 3B). However, terminal quantification of tumor-free body mass revealed significant preservation of host tissues in the KPC-A427 group relative to KPC-control ASO mice (Figure 3C). Conversely, no such therapeutic benefit was observed in the MPC1 model, which exhibited no significant difference compared to its respective control ASO group (Figure 3C).

Following ICV administration of the ASO, both the KPC-A427 and MPC1-A427 groups exhibited a similar trend toward recovered food intake compared to their respective control ASO groups (Figure 3D). Despite this similar orexigenic response, functional outcomes diverged significantly between the two models. Specifically, Gfral-ASO treatment significantly improved grip strength at the 5-week endpoint only in the high-GDF15 KPC model (approximately 246.18 g vs. 160.06 g in KPC-control ASO), whereas no functional rescue was observed in the low-GDF15 MPC1 model (194.12 g vs. 181.88 g in MPC1-control ASO) (Figures 3E and 3F).

As expected, terminal plasma GDF15 analysis confirmed the distinct secretory phenotypes: KPC-bearing mice exhibited significantly elevated levels (>1,000 pg/mL), while concentrations in MPC1 mice remained within a physiologically low range comparable to Sham controls (Figure 3G). Notably, these circulating GDF15 levels were unaffected by ASO treatment, demonstrating that the anti-cachectic efficacy of Gfral silencing is strictly contingent upon pathological GDF15 elevation rather than a reduction in ligand availability.

To verify central target engagement and evaluate downstream neuronal activation, we performed immunofluorescence staining for GFRAL and c-Fos within the AP and NTS (Figure 3H). Quantification of GFRAL-expressing neurons revealed a significant reduction in both models following A427 administration. Relative to their respective control ASO groups, the number of GFRAL+ neurons decreased to 71.5% in the KPC group and 73.6% in the MPC1 group (Figures 3H and 3I). Consistent with these counts, GFRAL immunofluorescence intensity in the AP was significantly attenuated, reducing to 42.0% in KPC mice and 61.9% in MPC1 mice compared to control ASOs (Figures 3H and J). These parallel reductions demonstrate that the central knockdown efficiency of the Gfral-ASO is robust and independent of the divergent circulating GDF15 levels observed between the two models. Notably, the total number of c-Fos+ neurons in the AP and NTS showed no significant differences in all tumor-bearing mice (PBS, control ASO, and A427) groups in either model (Figure 3K). This suggests that ASO-mediated suppression is specifically restricted to the GFRAL-positive subpopulation rather than inducing a generalized dampening of hindbrain neuronal activity.

Terminal analysis confirmed that while both MPC1 and KPC models exhibited profound depletion of systemic adipose depots, the therapeutic efficacy of Gfral inhibition was strictly contingent upon the tumor’s GDF15 secretory phenotype. Quantification of iWAT mass in the high-GDF15 KPC model revealed significant preservation following A427 administration relative to the control ASO group (0.1186 g vs. 0.074 g; Figure 3L). Conversely, no significant differences in iWAT mass were observed in the low-GDF15 MPC1 model (Figure 3L). Similarly, both models displayed hallmark skeletal muscle wasting, with KPC-bearing mice exhibiting more pronounced atrophy. In alignment with the adipose tissue observations, A427 treatment significantly attenuated terminal GA muscle loss in the KPC model (0.166 g vs. 0.136 g), whereas these protective effects were entirely absent in the MPC1 model (Figure 3M).

Histological characterization of iWAT and GA muscle confirmed that the structural benefits of Gfral silencing were selectively restricted to the high-GDF15 KPC model. In the high-GDF15 KPC model, A427-mediated Gfral depletion effectively preserved adipocyte architecture and mean LD area, (Figures 3N and 3O) as evidenced by a significant rightward shift in the LD-size distribution profile (Figure 3P, left panel). Conversely, low-GDF15 MPC1 model exhibited pervasive adipocyte shrinkage regardless of interventional status (Figure 3O), with no discernible difference in the LD-size distribution profile between treated and control groups (Figure 3P, right panel). Similarly, morphometric analysis of the GA muscle revealed a robust preservation of mean myofiber CSA (Figures 3N and 3Q) in the high-GDF15 KPC model and a concomitant rightward shift in myofiber-size distribution (Figure 3R, left panel). Conversely, the low-GDF15 MPC1 model exhibited pervasive myofiber atrophy regardless of interventional status (Figure 3Q), with no discernible difference in the myofiber-size distribution profile between the treated and control groups (Figure 3R, right panel). The complete absence of structural rescue in the low-GDF15 MPC1 model reinforces the conclusion that peripheral tissue preservation via central GFRAL inhibition is strictly contingent upon pathological ligand elevation.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that while ASO-mediated Gfral silencing achieves robust and uniform central target knockdown across diverse malignancy models, its capacity to arrest cachectic wasting and functional deterioration is strictly contingent upon pathological GDF15 elevation. Consequently, these findings establish the GDF15-GFRAL signaling axis as a ligand-dependent driver of host catabolism, wherein receptor antagonism is therapeutic only in the context of its cognate pathological signal.

Comparison of the effects of a Gfral-ASO and anti-GDF15 antibody in cachexia models

To elucidate the relative functional contributions of central versus peripheral components of the GDF15-GFRAL axis to CAC, we performed a head-to-head comparative assessment of central Gfral silencing via A427 and peripheral ligand neutralization using anti-GDF15 Ab, Ponsegromab, a clinical-stage humanized mAb.27,29,30 Interventions were initiated in an orthotopic MC38 tumor model at 3 weeks post-implantation—a stage characterized by established tumor burden and the emergence of hallmark cachectic manifestations (Figure 4A). Mice were administered either a single ICV injection of A427 (500 μg/mouse) or weekly subcutaneous injection (SC) doses of anti-GDF15 Ab (10 mg/kg). Hallmark cachectic phenotypes, including body weight trajectory, cumulative food intake, and functional grip strength, were monitored longitudinally through the 5-week study endpoint (Figure 4A).

Longitudinal analysis of body weight revealed divergent outcomes between the two therapeutic modalities. At the 5-week endpoint, anti-GDF15 Ab administration resulted in a significant increase in body weight gain compared to the control Ab group, whereas A427 treatment exhibited an upward trend that did not reach statistical significance relative to the control ASO group (Figure 4B). However, a more definitive therapeutic impact was revealed through the assessment of tumor-free body weight. While all tumor-bearing control groups (MC38-control Ab and MC38-control ASO) exhibited a significant reduction in weight compared to Sham controls, both A427 and anti-GDF15 Ab successfully preserved host body mass to levels comparable to the Sham control groups (Figure 4C). Notably, both therapeutic groups maintained significantly higher tumor-free body weights compared to their respective controls (control Ab and control ASO). Coupled with the sustained restoration of food intake observed in both treatment arms (Figure 4D), these findings demonstrate that both central Gfral silencing and peripheral GDF15 neutralization are equally potent in arresting cancer-associated wasting.

Assessment of physical performance revealed a distinct divergence between the two therapeutic modalities. Central Gfral inhibition via A427 significantly mitigated the progressive decline in grip strength compared to the control ASO group, maintaining superior functional values through the study endpoint (Figures 4E and 4F). In contrast, anti-GDF15 Ab administration failed to elicit a statistically significant rescue of grip strength relative to the control Ab, with both tumor-bearing groups exhibiting a comparable longitudinal deterioration in muscle function (Figures 4E and 4F). This functional superiority of A427 was most evident at the 5-week endpoint, where it achieved markedly higher preservation of grip strength compared to all other tumor-bearing groups, including those receiving anti-GDF15 Ab. Although the anti-GDF15 Ab group displayed numerically higher grip strength than its control at the study conclusion, the difference remained statistically insignificant (Figure 4F).

Plasma GDF15 levels were significantly elevated in all tumor-bearing mice. While concentrations in the A427-treated group remained comparable to control ASO mice, anti-GDF15 Ab administration resulted in a pronounced elevation of circulating GDF15 (Figure 4G). This marked increase is likely attributable to Ab-mediated stabilization and reduced clearance of the ligand-Ab complex, a common pharmacokinetic phenomenon associated with neutralizing Ab therapies.

To compare the central mechanisms of A427 and anti-GDF15 Ab, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of GFRAL and c-Fos in the AP and NTS across all experimental groups (Figure 4H). Quantitative analysis revealed that the number of GFRAL+ neurons was comparable among the Sham control, control ASO, control Ab, and anti-GDF15 Ab groups, indicating that systemic GDF15 neutralization does not affect receptor density. In contrast, A427 treatment significantly reduced the number of GFRAL+ neurons to 58.14% of the levels seen in the control ASO group (Figure 4I). Furthermore, the staining intensity in the A427 group was also markedly decreased to 49.64% compared to the control ASO group (Figure 4J). In addition, we quantified the number of c-Fos+ neurons within the AP and NTS to assess overall neuronal activation (Figure 4K). Interestingly, all tumor-bearing groups (control ASO, control Ab, A427, and anti-GDF15 Ab) exhibited comparable levels of c-Fos expression, with no significant differences observed regardless of A427 or anti-GDF15 Ab treatment (Figure 4K). To specifically assess the activation of the GDF15-GFRAL axis, we quantified the proportion of GFRAL-positive neurons co-expressing c-Fos (merged GFRAL+/c-Fos+ cells). In the tumor-bearing control groups (control ASO and control Ab), approximately 60% of GFRAL-expressing neurons were activated, reflecting the robust stimulation of this pathway by elevated systemic GDF15. In contrast, anti-GDF15 Ab administration effectively suppressed this activation, maintaining the proportion of activated GFRAL neurons below 20% (Figure 4L). These results, together with the reduced GFRAL density observed in the A427 group, demonstrate that both therapeutic strategies—one via direct receptor knockdown and the other via ligand sequestration—successfully blunt the functional output of the GDF15-GFRAL circuitry in the hindbrain.

At the endpoint, tissue weight analysis corroborated the functional superiority of central receptor silencing over systemic ligand neutralization. Both A427 and anti-GDF15 Ab treatments significantly increased iWAT mass compared to their respective control groups (Figure 4M). However, the response in other adipose depots diverged; A427 treatment resulted in a significant preservation of eWAT mass, whereas anti-GDF15 Ab-treated mice exhibited only a numerical trend toward increased eWAT mass that did not reach statistical significance (Figure 4M). Critically, significant preservation of skeletal muscle mass was observed exclusively in the GA of the A427 group. In contrast, anti-GDF15 Ab treatment failed to elicit a significant increase in GA mass compared to controls (Figure 4N). No significant differences were observed in the mass of Soleus, TA, or EDL muscles across the tumor-bearing groups (Figure 4N).

Histological characterization of peripheral tissues confirmed that central Gfral silencing and systemic GDF15 neutralization equally preserved tissue architecture against tumor-induced wasting (Figure 4O). In iWAT, both A427 and anti-GDF15 Ab successfully preempted the severe adipocyte shrinkage observed in tumor-bearing controls, maintaining mean LD CSA at significantly higher levels (925.58 and 1,037.11 μm2 vs. 651.78 and 445.93 μm2; Figures 4O and 4P). This structural preservation was further reflected by a distinct rightward shift in the LD-size distribution, characterized by a reduced frequency of small-diameter adipocytes (<800 μm2) and an enrichment of large-diameter adipocytes (>1,200 μm2), thereby recapitulating the homeostatic profile of Sham control (Figure 4Q).

Similarly, both therapeutic modalities mitigated skeletal muscle atrophy by preserving myofiber dimensions in the GA (Figure 4O). Mean myofiber CSA was significantly augmented in both A427- and anti-GDF15-Ab-treated mice relative to control ASO and control Ab groups (1,204.5 and 939.0 μm2 vs. 1,010.7 and 881.6 μm2; Figure 4R). Morphometric analysis of fiber-size distribution confirmed this protective effect, showing a marked reduction in atrophic fibers (<800 μm2) and a concomitant increase in larger myofibers (>2,000 μm2), effectively aligning the muscle profile with the Sham distribution (Figure 4S).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that while both central Gfral-targeted ASO and systemic GDF15-neutralizing Ab therapies are equally potent in maintaining systemic tissue integrity and restoring cumulative food intake, central intervention provides a distinct functional advantage. Although both modalities conferred robust protection against early functional decline, A427 administration drove a statistically significant, superior preservation of terminal grip strength and skeletal muscle mass (Figures 4F and 4N), underscoring the enhanced therapeutic efficacy of targeting the central GDF15-GFRAL axis.

Therapeutic Gfral silencing extends survival and preserves physical function

To assess the impact of central receptor-level inhibition on overall survival and disease progression, we utilized two distinct MC38 tumor models. We first examined whether pretreatment with A427 could provide a protective buffer against cancer-induced mortality using an intraperitoneal (i.p.) tumor model. Mice pretreated with a single ICV injection of A427 1 week prior to tumor inoculation exhibited a significantly prolonged median survival compared to the control ASO group (Figures S5A and S5B). Consistent with this survival benefit, A427 pretreatment effectively preserved hallmark cachectic parameters at week 5, significantly maintaining fat mass, lean mass, and functional grip strength compared to tumor-bearing controls (Figures S5C–S5E). This preventive intervention effectively delayed the onset of severe cachexia, suggesting that early blockade of the GDF15-GFRAL axis can intercept the progression of terminal wasting.

Having established the preventive efficacy, we next evaluated the therapeutic potential of Gfral silencing in an established disease setting using the orthotopic tumor model. At 3 weeks post-implantation, plasma GDF15 levels were quantified to ensure comparable baseline disease burden; subsequently, mice were stratified and randomized into treatment groups based on these concentrations. Consistent with our therapeutic protocols, mice received a single ICV injection of either Gfral-ASO (A427) or control ASO at week 3, with survival and clinical parameters—including body weight, food intake, and grip strength—monitored until the 7-week study endpoint (Figure 5A).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that the survival probability was significantly improved in the A427 group (n = 10) compared to the control ASO group (n = 10). While the control ASO group exhibited a survival rate of 0% at 7 weeks, the A427-treated group maintained a 90% survival rate (p < 0.0001), indicating a robust protective effect against cancer-associated mortality (Figure 5B).

Regarding physiological and metabolic parameters, plasma GDF15 levels measured at week 3 showed no significant difference between the A427 and control ASO groups (Figure 5C). This lack of divergence reflects the successful randomization of mice based on their systemic GDF15 concentrations prior to treatment, ensuring that both experimental arms were subjected to a comparable ligand burden at the onset of the intervention. Similarly, weekly body weight measurements revealed no significant differences between the two groups throughout the study period (Figure 5D). However, functional and metabolic outcomes were markedly improved following intervention. Food intake in A427-treated mice significantly increased relative to the control ASO group post-injection, with this elevation sustained through week 6 (Figure 5E). Furthermore, weekly assessments of grip strength demonstrated that Gfral inhibition effectively preserved muscle function. Post-injection grip strength values (expressed as a percentage of initial baseline) were consistently and significantly higher in the A427 group compared to control ASO mice (Figure 5F). Collectively, these findings establish that a single administration of Gfral-ASO is sufficient to extend survival and maintain physical performance in an established model of CAC.

Discussion

This study provides robust preclinical evidence establishing centrally targeted Gfral ASOs as a potent therapeutic modality for cancer cachexia. We developed lead candidates (A427, A428, and A429) targeting exon 5 of the Gfral gene, achieving substantial downregulation of GFRAL expression specifically within the AP and NTS. A single ICV administration sustained pharmacological efficacy for over 4 weeks in both prophylactic and therapeutic regimens. Beyond preserving tissue architecture across multiple severe orthotopic models of colon and aggressive pancreatic cancer, this central genetic intervention restored functional muscle strength and, most importantly, extended overall survival.

Central Gfral silencing rescued tumor-induced loss of body weight and lean mass, supported by restored cumulative food intake, consistent with the established role of the GDF15-GFRAL axis in driving cancer-induced anorexia and disrupted energy homeostasis.23,40 Beyond weight stabilization, central GFRAL suppression conferred structural protection against tissue-specific wasting: A427 preempted severe atrophy of both adipose tissue and skeletal muscle, normalizing lipid droplet and myofiber CSA distributions toward sham-like profiles. Thus, like systemic GDF15-neutralizing antibodies.22,26,29,30,41 direct central Gfral antagonism achieves comprehensive preservation of systemic tissue integrity.

To establish that this efficacy is contingent on pathological activation of the axis, we compared orthotopic models with divergent GDF15 profiles: MC38 (colon; high GDF15).42,43 KPC (pancreatic; high GDF15),44 and MPC1 (pancreatic; negligible GDF15).45 In the high-GDF15 MC38 and KPC models, which exhibit profound systemic wasting, ASO administration preserved muscle and adipose mass as well as functional grip strength, whereas it elicited no functional or structural improvement in the low-GDF15 MPC1 group. This divergence confirms that central receptor silencing acts as a target-specific intervention against pathological receptor activation rather than a non-specific metabolic effect.

The GDF15-GFRAL axis drives systemic wasting through a hindbrain-sympathetic nervous system (SNS)-muscle circuit. Antagonistic antibodies such as 3P10 prevent RET recruitment to the GDF15-bound receptor, reversing lipid oxidation and preserving tissue mass even under caloric restriction.27 Whereas such extracellular blockade competes for signaling epitopes, Gfral-ASOs silence receptor mRNA, thereby dismantling this cascade at its source within the brainstem.32,34 To test whether this distinction is functionally meaningful, we performed a head-to-head comparison of A427 with a systemic anti-GDF15 neutralizing antibody. Although both improved cumulative food intake and maintained early-stage grip strength, only A427 significantly increased skeletal muscle (GA) mass and produced superior restoration of terminal grip strength, despite comparable caloric recovery in both arms. This functional advantage, achieved independently of feeding, suggests that direct central receptor downregulation affords more durable modulation of the hindbrain-SNS-muscle axis than transient peripheral ligand neutralization.

Safety considerations must distinguish developmental from adult therapeutic contexts. Although basal GDF15 signaling is required for the development and maturation of sympathetic neurons,46 adult CAC is driven by a pathologically hyperactivated SNS fueled by tumor-derived GDF15. Our strategy therefore aims to attenuate this pathological overdrive while sparing basal metabolic function. Consistent with this rationale, toxicity assessments in adult mice showed no systemic or behavioral impairment: serum markers of hepatotoxicity (ALT and AST; Figures 1E, 1F, 1H, and 1I), nephrotoxicity (BUN, creatinine), and muscle damage (CK) (Figure S1) remained within normal ranges across groups, and behavioral assays revealed no deficits (Figures 1J and 1K). These findings indicate that central Gfral silencing has a favorable safety profile, supporting its feasibility as a long-acting therapy for CAC.

The translational potential of this platform is reinforced by its pharmacokinetic stability.32,34 A427 uses a 2′-MOE-modified gapmer chemistry that confers nuclease resistance and high target affinity, such that a single administration sustains therapeutic efficacy for over 4 weeks. Clinically, this durability could reduce dosing frequency for patients with debilitating CAC.

A key finding of this study is the marked extension of overall survival following a single therapeutic dose of A427. Whereas the control ASO group declined to approximately 20% survival by day 50, the A427 group maintained 90% survival (p < 0.0001; Figure 5B). This benefit demonstrates that central Gfral silencing extends survival beyond symptomatic relief or weight stabilization. Prolonging survival remains the central, yet elusive, objective in CAC management, for which effective pharmacological options are currently lacking.9 By attenuating the GDF15-driven catabolic state, a single Gfral-ASO dose was sufficient to extend lifespan in an established model of advanced malignancy, establishing a strong rationale for targeting central GFRAL signaling to address this critical unmet need.

In conclusion, this study establishes central Gfral silencing as a robust, target-specific strategy against the multifactorial pathophysiology of CAC. Using chemically modified 2′-MOE gapmers, we achieved sustained central receptor downregulation that reversed established tissue wasting, restored functional muscle strength, and reestablished metabolic homeostasis without disrupting basal physiological function. Most notably, the pronounced improvement in survival in aggressive high-GDF15 models indicates that this central genetic blockade offers clinical benefit beyond weight stabilization, supporting central GFRAL antagonism as a promising strategy to preserve function and extend life in cancer cachexia.

Limitations of the study

First, although ICV administration enabled precise initial validation of central Gfral silencing, this invasive route limits clinical translation. Future work should therefore pursue non-invasive, systemic delivery, such as antibody-oligonucleotide conjugates (AOCs) that exploit receptor-mediated transcytosis to reach hindbrain signaling centers,47,48 providing a scalable approach while minimizing the procedural burden in patients with advanced disease.49

Second, all experiments used mice of a single sex and age, so we could not assess sexual dimorphism or age-related effects on cachexia progression and treatment response.

Finally, efficacy was validated across multiple aggressive cancer models (MC38, KPC, and MPC1) but only on a single C57BL/6 genetic background; these findings may therefore not capture the genetic heterogeneity of human populations, and validation across diverse backgrounds is warranted.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines and culture conditions

The murine colorectal carcinoma cell lines C26 (RIKEN, Tsukuba, Japan), CT26 (American Type Culture Collection [ATCC], Manassas, VA, USA), and MC38 (Kerafast, Shirley, MA, USA) were used in this study. The murine pancreatic cancer cell line MPC1, a KPC-derived line harboring the genotype KrasG12D; Trp53fl/fl; Pdx1-CreERT2 (Ku et al., 202445), was kindly provided by Professor Lim, and the KPC cell line was purchased from CancerTools.org (London, UK). The 293T-SRE-Luc2-RET-GFRAL cell line, a human embryonic kidney cell line (HEK293T) stably expressing luciferase under the control of the serum response element (SRE) along with exogenous RET and GFRAL genes, was obtained from Kyinno Biotechnology (Beijing, China).

All murine cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Hyclone, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) or RPMI 1640 medium (HyClone, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA). The 293T-SRE-Luc2-RET-GFRAL cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1 μg/mL puromycin (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), and 150 μg/mL hygromycin B (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

While formal cell line authentication was not performed, the cell lines—particularly MC38, MPC1, and KPC—were obtained from verified commercial or academic repositories and strictly maintained at low passage numbers (<20 passages) to minimize genetic drift and phenotypic variation. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and confirmed to be negative.

Animal models

All animal experiments were carried out in strict adherence to the guidelines and protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST). Male C57BL/6NTac mice (8–10 weeks old) were purchased from DBL (Eumseong, Korea). As experiments were conducted in a single sex, we could not assess potential sexual dimorphism in cachexia progression or treatment response, which represents a limitation of this study.

Mice were primarily housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions within the KAIST Laboratory Animal Resource Center. For surgical interventions and specific experimental procedures, mice were housed in an IACUC-approved satellite facility maintained under controlled environmental conditions. All mice were acclimatized for at least one week prior to experimentation.

To minimize experimental bias, mice were randomized into treatment groups based on their initial body weights to ensure equal baselines.

To mimic tumor growth in its native environment, we generated two orthotopic models. Cell suspensions were prepared by mixing cells with an equal volume of Matrigel (Corning, Tewksbury, MA, USA) and DMEM (1:1 ratio). For the colon cancer model, 1 × 105 MC38 cells were surgically injected into the cecum. For the pancreatic cancer model, 1 × 105 MPC1 or KPC cells were implanted directly into the pancreas.

For the intraperitoneal (i.p.) tumor model, 1 × 105 MC38 cells suspended in 100 μL of a 1:1 mixture of Matrigel and DMEM were injected directly into the peritoneal cavity of the mice to establish disseminated peritoneal tumors. The use of this model was strictly limited to the pretreatment survival experiments.

Cachexia was defined based on significant tissue wasting; specifically, cachexia was established when the weight of the gastrocnemius (GA) muscle decreased by 10% or more, and the weight of white adipose tissue (WAT), particularly inguinal WAT (iWAT), decreased by 20% or more compared to control mice. All experiments and analyses were conducted based on these criteria.

Study approval

All animal experiments were carried out in strict adherence to the guidelines and protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST), (Approved protocol numbers: KA2023-115).

Method details

Antisense oligonucleotide design, screening

ASOs were designed to bind conserved sequences within the human GFRAL pre-mRNA and mRNA (NM_207410.2) sequences. Candidate sites were chosen using the following criteria: preference for regions conserved across human, mouse, rat, and crab-eating monkey with no more than one mismatch; exclusion of areas containing common single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs); minimization of motifs known to trigger immune response, such as GGGG, CCCC, CCGG or CpG. Each ASO followed a 5-10-4, 5-10-5, or 6-10-6 gapmer architecture. The terminal wings contained methoxyethyl (2′MOE)-modified nucleotides, while the middle stretch of 10 bases formed an unmodified DNA gap. Within the wing regions, 4–6 internal linkages (not including the ends) used phosphodiester (PO) rather than phosphorothioate (PS) chemistry. Sequence identities and chemical modifications are denoted as follows in accordance with the notation used in the key resource table: A = MOE-A, C = 2′MOE-5′-methyl-C, G = 2′MOE-G, T = 2′MOE-T, 5 = dnaA, 6 = DNA-5′-methyl-C, 7 = DNA-G, 8 = DNA-T, and ∗ = PS. All ASOs were synthesized by Chemgenes.

For in vitro screening, ASOs were transfected into HEK293T SRE-Luc2-RET-GFRAL cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The efficacy and toxicity of ASOs were evaluated following 24 to 48 h of incubation. Cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), and Gfral mRNA expression was quantified by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR).

Intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection and toxicity assessment

Adult mice

Adult male C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and positioned in a stereotaxic frame. A single ICV injection was delivered into the lateral ventricle using the following coordinates relative to the bregma: anteroposterior (AP) −0.2 mm, mediolateral (ML) +1.0 mm, and dorsoventral (DV) −2.0 mm. The injection was administered at a controlled rate, and the needle was left in place for 5 min post-injection to prevent backflow. Following the procedure, the incision was closed, and mice were monitored on a heating pad until full recovery.

To evaluate toxicity profiles, two independent experimental sets were conducted using 8-week-old mice. First, for dose-dependent toxicity assessment, mice were administered ASO at varying concentrations of 100, 300, or 500 μg. Second, for time-dependent toxicity assessment, mice were administered a single high dose of 500 μg and assessed at 3-, 4-, and 5-week post-injection. At the respective endpoints, serum samples were collected to assess systemic toxicity. Hepatotoxicity was evaluated by measuring aspartate aminotransferase (AST/GOT), alanine aminotransferase (ALT/GPT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total bilirubin (T-BIL), and albumin (ALB) levels. Nephrotoxicity was assessed via blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine levels, while muscle toxicity was determined by creatine kinase (CK) levels. These blood analyses were performed by the Laboratory Animal Resource Center at the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology (KRIBB, Ochang, Republic of Korea). Furthermore, behavioral toxicity was examined by measuring the latency to fall and immobility duration to assess motor coordination and general activity, respectively.

Neonatal mice

To assess potential toxicity in neonates, C57BL/6 pups (postnatal day 2, P2) received a single bolus ICV injection. ASOs were dissolved in sterile 1× PBS, and concentrations were verified by measuring absorbance at 260 nm. To visualize the injection spread, the dosing solution was mixed with 0.05% (w/v) Fast Green dye (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Injections were performed using a 10 μL NanoFil syringe equipped with a 33-gauge needle (World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, FL, USA). The needle was inserted approximately 1 mm lateral to the sagittal suture and 2 mm ventral to the skull surface, targeting the lateral ventricle. A total volume of 2 μL (20μg) containing the ASO or vehicle (PBS) was injected slowly. Following injection, pups were allowed to recover on a 37°C heating pad before being returned to the maternal nest. Survival rates and potential adverse effects were monitored daily for 7 days post-injection.

Survival analysis

To comprehensively evaluate the efficacy of Gfral-targeting ASO, survival analysis was performed using two distinct experimental protocols: a pretreatment model and a therapeutic model.

In the pretreatment setting, mice were randomized to receive a single intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection of either Gfral-targeting ASO (500 μg in 5 μL) or control ASO. 1 week post-ICV injection (designated as Week 1), tumors were established via intraperitoneal (i.p.) inoculation of MC38 cells (1 × 105 cells in 100 μL, suspended in a 1:1 mixture of Matrigel and DMEM). Following tumor implantation, animal survival was monitored for up to 8 weeks.

In the therapeutic setting, tumors were established first to mimic a clinical intervention scenario. Three weeks post-tumor implantation—corresponding to the onset of cachexia—mice were stratified based on plasma GDF15 levels to ensure equivalent disease burden. Subsequently, they received a single ICV injection of either Gfral-targeting ASO (500 μg in 5 μL) or Control ASO. These animals were monitored for survival for up to 7 weeks post-tumor implantation. Through both experiments, body weight and food intake were recorded weekly, and animals were observed daily.

Mice surviving to the end of the observation period or reaching humane endpoints (e.g., severe lethargy or weight loss exceeding ethical limits) were euthanized. Overall survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves, and differences between groups were determined by the log rank (Mantel-Cox) test.

Comparative analysis of Anti-GDF15 antibody and ASO treatments

In the therapeutic setting, treatment was initiated three weeks post-tumor implantation to target established cachexia. To ensure equivalent baselines, mice were stratified into treatment groups based on plasma GDF15 levels. They subsequently received either a single ICV injection of Gfral-targeting ASO (500 μg) or subcutaneous injections of the anti-GDF15 antibody (Ponsegromab; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) (10 mg/kg) once weekly. Treatments continued for two weeks, after which all animals were sacrificed for comparative analysis (5 weeks post-implantation).

Grip strength measurement

Grip strength was assessed using a grip strength meter (Bioseb, Vitrolles, France). Mice were allowed to grasp a metal grid with their forelimbs while being gently pulled back horizontally by the tail until they released the grid. The peak force exerted by the forelimbs before release was recorded in grams. Each mouse underwent five consecutive trials, and the highest value was recorded as the grip strength for that session. Grip strength measurements were performed at the same time of day to minimize variability and ensure consistency.

Rotarod test

Motor coordination and balance were assessed using a rotarod apparatus (Ugo Basile, Gemonio, VA). The rod was elevated 30 cm above the base and featured a knurled surface to prevent slippage. Mice were acclimated to the experimental room for 5–20 min before testing to ensure normal arousal levels. The rotarod was set to a starting speed of 4 rpm. After placing the mouse on the rod, the rotation speed was maintained at 4 rpm for 10 s to allow the animal to stabilize, followed by an acceleration rate of 20 rpm/min until a maximum speed of 40 rpm was reached. The speed at which the mouse fell off the rod was recorded. If a mouse fell within the initial 10 s (pre-acceleration phase), the trial was repeated up to a maximum of three times. In such cases, the final score was calculated as the mean speed of the attempts, assigning a value of 4 rpm to falls occurring before acceleration.

Tail suspension test (TST)

The tail suspension test was performed to assess depression-like behavior, following the protocol described by Can et al.51 Mice were acclimatized to the testing room for at least 1 h prior to the experiment. Adhesive tape was wrapped around the tail approximately 1 cm from the tip, and mice were suspended 30 cm above the floor using a horizontal bar. To prevent visual contact between animals, acoustically and visually isolated compartments were used. The test was recorded for a duration of 6 min. The total duration of immobility was measured during the 6-min period. Immobility was defined as the complete absence of any limb or body movement, except for those caused by respiration. Mice that climbed their tails were excluded from the analysis.

GDF15 measurement

The concentration of GDF15 in cell culture supernatants and mouse plasma samples was quantified using the mouse GDF15 Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, Catalog # MGD150, Minneapolis, MN, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Metabolic monitoring

To assess gross metabolic changes, body weight and food intake were continuously monitored throughout the experimental period. To minimize confounding effects from circadian variations, all measurements were consistently performed at the same time of day. Specifically, body weight was recorded once weekly, while food intake was measured three times per week to accurately calculate the average daily food consumption per mouse. Furthermore, body composition, specifically fat mass and lean mass, was determined using a Minispec time-domain nuclear magnetic resonance (TD-NMR) analyzer (Bruker Optics, Billerica, MA, USA).

Histological and immunofluorescence analyses

Tissue Preparation and Staining: Mouse iWAT and eWAT were harvested, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with H&E. Mouse GA, soleus, EDL, and TA muscles were embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature compound (Leica Biosystems, Buffalo Grove, IL) and cryosectioned at 7 μm thickness using a cryostat (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). For the histological quantification of adipose and muscle tissues, three representative sections per mouse were stained, and the analyzed values were averaged to yield a single biological replicate.

For central nervous system tissues, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and perfused with 20 mL normal saline followed by 20 mL 4% PFA via the left ventricle of the heart. Whole brains were collected and fixed with 4% PFA for 24 h at 4°C, before being dehydrated in a PBS-based 30% sucrose solution until the tissues sank to the bottom of the container, usually after 48 h at 4°C. The tissues were then embedded in OCT compound. Coronal brain sections were collected at a 30 μm thickness using a cryostat. To encompass the entire area postrema (AP) region, five coronal sections per mouse were stained, and the quantified values from these five sections were summed for the final analysis.

Immunofluorescence Staining: Tissue sections were blocked for 1 h at room temperature; brain slices were blocked with 5% donkey serum (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA) in 0.3% PBS-T, whereas other tissue sections were blocked with CAS-Block Histochemical Reagent (Invitrogen, Frederick, MD, USA). Sections were then incubated in primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Specifically, brain slices were incubated with a sheep anti-GFRAL antibody (1:500, Invitrogen, Cat. PA5-47769) and/or a rabbit anti-c-Fos antibody (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. 2250S) whereas other tissue sections were incubated with a anti-laminin antibody (1:500, Abcam, Cat. Ab11575). Sections were washed and incubated in the corresponding fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1:2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 1 min. Slides were mounted with fluorescence mounting medium (Dako, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Imaging and Analysis: Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss LSM780 or LSM980). For brain sections, images were captured using z stack imaging, and Maximum Intensity Projections (MIPs) were generated. All images were then analyzed with Fiji software to quantify fluorescence intensity, cell counts, and morphological parameters. To ensure accurate and unbiased quantification, background subtraction was uniformly applied to all images to eliminate unspecific fluorescence.

For the quantification of GFRAL-positive cells, manual counting was initially performed by visually confirming the colocalization of GFRAL immunofluorescence with DAPI-stained nuclei. To cross-validate these manual counts and ensure the robust reliability of the data, the mean fluorescence intensity of the GFRAL-immunoreactive areas was subsequently measured and quantified using Fiji software.

To determine the cross-sectional area (CSA) of skeletal muscle fibers, muscle sections were immunostained with an anti-laminin antibody (1:500, Abcam, Cat. Ab11575) and analyzed using Fiji software. The area enclosed within the laminin-positive boundaries was measured to quantify individual myofiber size. To ensure robust statistical representation, a minimum of 200 muscle fibers were measured per tissue section. Furthermore, to eliminate technical artifacts and sectioning errors, atypical fibers with an area smaller than 50 μm2 or larger than 8,000 μm2 were systematically excluded from the final analysis.

To assess morphological changes in white adipose tissue (iWAT and eWAT), the cross-sectional area of individual adipocytes was quantified from H&E-stained sections. Using Fiji software, the boundaries of the optically empty unilocular lipid droplets, delineated by the eosinophilic cytoplasm, were traced and measured. To guarantee robust statistical power, a minimum of 200 adipocytes were analyzed per tissue section across multiple randomly selected fields of view. Furthermore, to eliminate technical artifacts—such as tangentially sectioned cells or artificially merged droplets due to disrupted cell membranes during tissue processing—atypical structures with an area smaller than 50 μm2 or larger than 10,000 μm2 were systematically excluded from the final analysis.

Gene and protein expression analysis

Quantitative Real-Time PCR: Total RNA was isolated from target tissues and cells using the TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA was synthesized using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Promega) to quantify the expression of target genes [hGFRAL]. Relative gene expression was normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Analysis of public clinical datasets (or pan-cancer transcriptomic analysis)

To evaluate the transcriptomic landscape of GDF15 across human malignancies, we utilized the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics (https://www.cbioportal.org). The TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas dataset, comprising 10,967 samples from 32 distinct cancer types, was queried. GDF15 mRNA expression levels were obtained as RSEM (RNA-Seq by Expectation Maximization) values. Datasets were extracted and preprocessed using R software (version 4.4.1) to handle batch normalization and log2-transformation (log2(Value + 1)). The processed data were then imported into GraphPad Prism (version 10). Data were batch-normalized from Illumina HiSeq_RNASeqV2 aligned reads and log-transformed (log2(Value + 1)) for analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All data are presented as the mean ± SEM. For comparisons between two independent groups at each specific time point, an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test with the Holm-Šídák correction for multiple comparisons was employed. Statistical significance for multiple groups at a single time point was determined by ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Dunnett’s multiple comparisons tests. For longitudinal data (e.g., body weight gain, food intake) and frequency distribution analysis, ordinary two-way ANOVA was utilized, followed by multiple comparisons to evaluate treatment effects across different time points or size bins. Survival curves were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences were compared using the log rank (Mantel-Cox) test. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In all figures, pairs or data points without indicated asterisks are not significantly different (p > 0.05). Detailed statistical methods, including specific post-hoc tests, and the exact value of n (representing the number of independent biological replicates, i.e., individual mice or independent cell cultures), are provided in each figure legend. The graphical abstract and schematic diagrams were generated using BioRender.com.
