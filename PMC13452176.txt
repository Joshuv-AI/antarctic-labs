Long-term adherence to glucose-lowering therapy and cardiovascular risk in type 2 diabetes: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Lise B Nielsen, Andreas Bartholdy, Tri-Long Nguyen, Majken K Jensen, Simon Rasmussen, Marta Guasch-Ferré, Jordi Merino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundType 2 diabetes is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease, and reducing this excess risk is a central goal of clinical management. Sustained adherence to glucose-lowering medication is considered a key component of this strategy. However, long-term patterns of medication-taking behavior in real-world settings, and their relationship with cardiovascular outcomes, remain insufficiently characterized.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide cohort study of Danish adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes who initiated non-insulin glucose-lowering medication between 2005 and 2012. Medication adherence was estimated from pharmacy dispensing data, and group-based trajectory modeling was used to identify longitudinal adherence patterns. The primary outcome was a composite cardiovascular outcome including ischemic heart disease, stroke, or heart failure. Associations between adherence trajectories and cardiovascular outcomes over 10 years of follow-up were assessed with Cox proportional-hazards models.FindingsAmong 79,510 individuals with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes (median age, 61 (IQR: 52–69); 57% men), we identified three long-term adherence trajectories defined by high (n = 32,912; 41%), intermediate (n = 25,822; 33%), and low (n = 20,776; 26%) adherence. During follow-up, 15,276 cardiovascular events occurred. Compared with the high-adherence group, adjusted hazard ratios for the composite cardiovascular outcome were 1.07 (95% CI, 1.03–1.12) and 1.27 (95% CI, 1.21–1.33) in the intermediate- and low-adherence groups, respectively. Participants with high adherence remained free of cardiovascular events for an average of 220 more days (95% CI, 204–237) over 10 years. Associations were largely driven by ischemic heart disease, with no significant associations for heart failure.InterpretationLong-term adherence to glucose-lowering medication was associated with lower cardiovascular rate and longer event-free survival time among individuals with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes.FundingNovo Nordisk Foundation and the European Innovation Council.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes, Medication adherence, Cardiovascular disease, Epidemiology

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for peer-reviewed human studies published from database inception to November 1, 2025. The literature search was conducted on November 1, 2025, using the terms “type 2 diabetes”, “medication adherence”, “proportion of days covered”, “medication possession ratio”, and “group-based trajectory modelling”, without language restrictions. We identified 96 relevant studies. These studies primarily rely on self-reported adherence, or summary measures such as proportion of days covered, which provide aggregated assessments of medication-taking behavior and preclude a clear understanding of how sustained or changing adherence influences cardiovascular risk over time. In addition, prior work has largely focused on specific drug classes, with follow-up limited to five years or less, and selected populations that may underrepresent younger individuals. Consequently, the long-term cardiovascular implications of real-world adherence patterns from treatment initiation remain poorly defined.

Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this is the first nationwide study to characterize decade-long trajectories of adherence to glucose-lowering therapies from treatment initiation in individuals with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes and to examine their association with major cardiovascular outcomes. Using comprehensive linkage of health and administrative registries and longitudinal dispensing data, we identified distinct adherence patterns over 10 years and observed clinically meaningful differences in cardiovascular risk across these trajectories. Compared with individuals with sustained high adherence, those with persistently low adherence had a higher rate of major cardiovascular disease (adjusted HR 1.27, 95% CI 1.21–1.33), corresponding to approximately 220 fewer event-free days over a decade. Associations were particularly pronounced for early-onset cardiovascular events (<55 years), indicating that adherence patterns early in the disease course are strongly associated with long-term cardiovascular outcomes. By combining a large, unselected, nationwide cohort with detailed longitudinal adherence modelling, this study provides strong real-world evidence that long-term adherence patterns to glucose-lowering therapy are closely associated with cardiovascular outcomes in type 2 diabetes.

Implications of all the available evidence

Taken together, the available evidence indicates that medication adherence is an important marker of long-term outcomes in type 2 diabetes, yet conventional adherence metrics provide limited insight into how adherence evolves and how different longitudinal patterns relate to cardiovascular risk. Our findings suggest that distinct adherence trajectories after treatment initiation are associated with major cardiovascular outcomes over a decade, including in individuals with early-onset events. These results support the clinical value of monitoring adherence longitudinally, particularly in the early years after diagnosis, and of developing targeted strategies for individuals with persistently low or declining adherence.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is associated with a substantially increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), which remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide.1 The growing availability of multiple effective glucose-lowering medication classes and the rising use of combination therapy have transformed diabetes care,2 but their benefits depend critically on sustained adherence. Non-adherence to medication is common in chronic diseases and is associated with higher complication rates and increased health care utilization.3, 4, 5, 6 Recent studies have examined cardiovascular outcomes among individuals with T2D treated with different glucose-lowering therapies.4,7, 8, 9 In pooled analyses from retrospective cohort studies or randomized clinical trials, good adherence to prescribed glucose-lowering medications has been linked to a 28% lower risk of all-cause mortality compared with poor adherence, underscoring adherence as a key determinant for long-term outcomes.4

Despite the recognized importance of medication adherence, most prior studies have relied on short-term or self-reported measures of adherence, or on static, aggregate indicators of adherence such as the proportion of days covered or medication possession ratio.10 While informative, these measures capture only a snapshot of medication-taking behavior and cannot distinguish intermittent lapses from permanent discontinuation or reflect changes that occur over the course of chronic disease management.11 As a result, they provide limited insight into how adherence develops over time and how these patterns influence cardiovascular outcomes.12

Trajectory-based approaches can identify longitudinal medication adherence patterns, offering an avenue to better study adherence patterns and their implications for health outcomes.13,14 However, applications of trajectory modeling in T2D have been limited, and the long-term association between adherence trajectories and CVD risk among individuals with newly diagnosed T2D remains uncertain. Addressing this gap has important clinical and public health implications, as it may guide strategies to maximize therapeutic benefit and reduce cardiovascular risk in people with T2D.5

Here, we conducted a nationwide cohort study using comprehensive Danish registry data in nearly 80,000 individuals with newly diagnosed T2D to characterize long-term trajectories of adherence to non-insulin glucose-lowering medications and to evaluate their association with 10-year risk of major cardiovascular events.

Methods

Study population

We included all Danish residents aged 18 years or older with newly diagnosed T2D, who filled a first-ever prescription for a glucose-lowering medication between January 1, 2005, and January 1, 2012. T2D was defined using a validated algorithm based on diagnostic codes recorded in the National Patient Registry (ICD-10: E10–E14) in combination with dispensing records for glucose-lowering medications from the National Prescription Registry (ATC: A10).15 As the Prescription Registry captures all reimbursed prescriptions dispensed at community pharmacies, this approach enables the identification of individuals treated in primary care who may not appear in hospital-based registries.

We excluded individuals with type 1 diabetes, pregnancy-related dysglycemia, or polycystic ovary syndrome. We further excluded individuals with a diagnosis of T2D more than one year before the first prescription (baseline), those with prevalent CVD at baseline, or those with less than 6 months of follow-up (Fig. S1). Restricting the study population to individuals who initiated glucose-lowering therapy within one year of their first recorded T2D diagnosis allowed us to facilitate the characterization of longitudinal medication-taking patterns from near the time of diagnosis and treatment initiation, while reducing heterogeneity related to unobserved prior treatment exposure or long-standing disease.

Participants were followed from baseline until emigration, incident CVD, death, or 10 years of follow-up, whichever came first. Data linkage was performed using the unique personal identification number assigned to all Danish residents, with access provided through the secure server environment at Statistics Denmark. According to Danish law, retrospective register-based studies do not require individual informed consent or separate ethical approval.16 The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Medication adherence

Dispensing data from the Danish National Prescription Registry include the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) Classification code, dispensing date, package size, and strength for all filled prescriptions at community pharmacies. As prescribed daily dose was not available, we used the World Health Organization's Defined Daily Dose (DDD) to estimate treatment duration.17 For each individual and each glucose-lowering medication class (Table S1), we constructed treatment episodes by estimating the duration of each prescription based on the number of DDDs dispensed. Overlapping prescriptions for the same medication class were assumed sequential, whereas overlapping prescriptions for different classes were considered concurrent. Treatment episodes across non-insulin glucose-lowering medication classes were then combined to define overall exposure.

Adherence trajectories were identified using group-based trajectory modelling (GBTM)13,18 applied to repeated monthly measures of the proportion of days covered (PDC). PDC was defined as the number of days supplied by filled prescriptions divided by the total number of days in the month, with monthly adherence defined as PDC ≥80%.19 Accordingly, trajectory groups represent longitudinal patterns of medication-taking behaviour following treatment initiation, rather than defining exposure status at baseline or assigning individuals to treatment groups at time zero. Individuals contributed adherence data from the index date until censoring or occurrence of the outcome.

Outcomes

The primary study endpoint the first-time event from a composite of any CVD events that included ischemic heart disease (ICD-10: I20–I25), stroke (ICD-10: I60–I64, I69), and heart failure (ICD-10: I50). Secondary outcomes included the individual components and early-onset CVD. Early-onset CVD was defined as cardiovascular events occurring before age 55 at the time of the event during follow-up.

Covariates

Information on age, sex (assigned at birth; male/female), working status, education level, and cohabitation was obtained from the Danish Civil Registration System. Comorbidities were based on Charlson Comorbidity Index,20 excluding T2D, identified from hospital discharge diagnoses within the 5 years before baseline, and summarized as an unweighted count (Table S2). Equivalized disposable income was retrieved from the Danish Personal Income Registry and categorized into national quartiles. Education level was classified according to the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED 2011). These covariates were updated annually on January 1 and included as time-varying variables. Information on diagnosis of hypertension, dyslipidemia, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease was obtained from the National Patient Registry and identified during one-year preceding baseline. Information on antithrombotic medication use, smoking cessation medication, blood pressure lowering medication, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease medication was obtained from the National Prescription Registry and classified as yes with a minimum of one purchased prescription in 1 year prior to baseline (Table S2).

Statistical analysis

We summarized continuous measurements by using medians (interquartile range, IQR) and presented categorical observations as frequencies and percentages. Medication adherence trajectories were identified using GBTM. Model fit was assessed using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).21 Although BIC decreased as the number of groups increased, the largest improvement was observed when moving from two to three groups, with only marginal gains thereafter (Table S3). We defined these categories as high, intermediate, and low medication adherence.

Associations between medication adherence trajectories and CVD outcomes were assessed using Cox proportional hazards models with time since baseline as the underlying timescale. Model 1 was adjusted for age (spline) and sex (male vs. female); Model 2 additionally adjusted for cohabitation (cohabitation/living alone), income (quartiles), education (categorical), number of comorbidities (0, 1, ≥2), and statin use one-year preceding baseline (continuous PDC (%)). These covariates, except for statin use, were updated annually on January 1 and included as time-varying variables. Model 3 was further adjusted for diagnosis of hypertension (yes/no), diagnosis of dyslipidaemia (yes/no), smoking cessation medication (yes/no), blood pressure lowering medication (yes/no), antithrombotic medication (yes/no), diagnosis of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease medication use (yes/no) (Table S2). Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations, with five imputed datasets generated. As this was a registry-based study, missingness was limited, with only one covariate (education) exhibiting incomplete data (Table 1). All covariates included in the primary analysis were incorporated into the imputation model, and estimates from each imputed dataset were combined using Rubin's rules. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and was not violated for the variables included in the models. Restricted mean survival time (RMST) was used to estimate CVD-free survival time over 10 years, with pairwise comparisons of adherence groups.

Secondary analyses examined each CVD component separately. We also conducted a secondary analysis for early onset CVD defined by CVD events occurring before the age of 55 years. In subgroup analyses, we assessed the association between medication adherence trajectories and CVD risk stratified by demographic and social characteristics or presence of comorbidities. A sensitivity analysis included inverse probability of treatment weighting to account for potential selection bias due to baseline exclusions. For these analyses, the reference population was the 136,146 individuals initiating non-insulin, glucose-lowering medication between Jan 1, 2005, and Jan 1, 2012, from the Danish National Prescription Registry, and model 3 covariates with baseline values were used for weighting. Balance metrics (standardized mean differences) before and after weighting are provided in Table S6. In separate sensitivity analyses, we forced four and five medication adherence trajectory groups to assess the robustness of the three-group results. Further, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis adjusting for statin use as time-varying covariate to account for differences in adherence to other cardiovascular risk reduction medications during follow-up.

A two-sided α-level of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. All analyses and data handling were performed using R version 4.5.0 and SAS version 9.4.

Results

From the Danish National Prescription Registry, we identified 136,146 individuals with a first prescription for a glucose-lowering medication between January 1, 2005, and January 1, 2012. Of those, we excluded 30,500 individuals (22.4%) because of no prior T2D diagnosis (n = 13,424) or because medication initiation occurred before or more than one year after T2D diagnosis (n = 17,076). An additional 23,978 individuals (17.6%) with known CVD at baseline, 1702 (1.3%) with follow-up shorter than six months, 336 (0.2%) not residing in Denmark at medication initiation, and 120 (<0.1%). The final study cohort comprised 79,510 participants (Fig. S1). The median age was 61 (IQR: 52–69) years, and 56% were men (Table 1), consistent with the sex distribution of people living with T2D in Denmark.22

Group-based trajectory modeling identified three patterns of adherence (Fig. S2). The largest group (41%) maintained high adherence, with a decline beginning four years after initiation of the medication. A second group (33%) showed stable but moderate adherence. The remaining 26% exhibited an early decline with persistently low adherence to prescribed medications. Compared with the low-adherence group, individuals with high adherence were younger (median age, 59 (IQR: 51–66) vs. 63 (IQR: 53–72) years), more frequently male (60% vs. 50%), and more often free of comorbidities other than diabetes at baseline (87.8% vs. 83.7%). They were also more likely to live with others, to have higher socioeconomic status, and to use multiple glucose-lowering medication classes during follow-up (Table 1).

Over a median follow-up of 10 years (IQR: 6.8–10.0), a total of 15,276 cardiovascular events occurred, including (49%) cases of ischemic heart disease, 4610 (30%) strokes, and 3225 (21%) cases of heart failure. Crude CVD incidence rates per 1000 person-years were 30.1 in the low-adherence group, 23.3 in the intermediate-adherence group, and 19.7 in the high-adherence group (Table 2a). In minimally adjusted analyses, the hazard ratio (HR) for the composite cardiovascular outcome was 1.11 (95% CI, 1.07–1.15) for intermediate adherence and 1.39 (95% CI, 1.33–1.44) for low adherence, as compared with high adherence. After adjustment for demographic, clinical, social, and behavioral factors, the associations remained consistent, with an adjusted HR of 1.07 (95% CI, 1.03–1.12) for intermediate adherence and HR of 1.27 (95% CI, 1.21–1.33) for low adherence (Table 2a, Fig. S3). In analyses of restricted mean survival time, individuals in the high-adherence group remained free of cardiovascular events for an average of 220 additional days (95% CI, 204–237) compared with those in the low adherence group (Table S5).

Stratified analyses showed consistent associations across most demographic and clinical subgroups (Fig. 1). The strongest association of medication adherence was observed among younger individuals (<55 years), in whom low adherence was associated with a 51% higher rate of cardiovascular events (HR, 1.51; 95% CI, 1.38–1.65). In analyses restricted to early-onset CVD (events before age 55 years), low adherence was associated with an 73% higher rate compared with high adherence (HR, 1.73; 95% CI, 1.54–1.94) (Table 2b).

Sensitivity analyses supported the primary findings. Estimates obtained from inverse probability-weighted Cox models were consistent with those from the main analysis (Table S6). When the adherence trajectory model was forced to identify four or five latent patterns, the results remained materially unchanged. In the four-group model, the incidence rates were 34 and 20 events per 1000 person-years in the lowest and highest adherence groups, respectively (adjusted HR, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.32–1.47) (Table S7, Fig. S5). In the five-group model, the corresponding incidence rates were 37 and 20 per 1000 person-years (adjusted HR, 1.46; 95% CI, 1.38–1.55) (Table S8, Fig. S6). Adjustment for statin use as a time-varying covariate to account for differences in adherence to other cardiovascular medications did not materially alter the results (Table S9).

Discussion

In this nationwide study of nearly 80,000 individuals with newly diagnosed T2D, we identified three distinct long-term trajectories of adherence to glucose-lowering medication and evaluated their relationship with cardiovascular outcomes. Individuals with persistently low medication adherence over the years had 27% higher rate of major cardiovascular events and, on average, experienced 220 fewer event-free days over a decade compared with those with sustained adherence.

Our findings extend prior research by moving beyond short-term or static adherence measures, which provide only a limited snapshot of medication-taking behavior. Previous observational studies have suggested that better adherence is associated with reduced cardiovascular events and mortality,4,7,9 but most have relied on single-point metrics and datasets that may underrepresent younger or socioeconomically disadvantaged populations.8 By leveraging comprehensive population-based registries and trajectory modeling, we were able to characterize real-world adherence patterns over 10 years and demonstrate how changes in adherence over time relate to cardiovascular risk.

The relationship between glycemic management and cardiovascular outcomes has been complex. Early trials of intensive glucose control reported modest or inconsistent effects on macrovascular disease risk,23,24 whereas more recent studies of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists have demonstrated clear cardiovascular benefits.25, 26, 27 Our findings extend this body of evidence by showing that in routine clinical practice, sustained adherence to glucose-lowering therapy is associated with meaningful cardiovascular benefit, irrespective of medication class or intensity. However, the observed adherence trajectories in our study may partly reflect differences in medication type, including variation in dosing regimens, tolerability, and the uptake of newer therapies. While our approach captures overall exposure to pharmacological treatment in real-world settings, it does not allow attribution of adherence patterns to specific medication classes. Further studies incorporating more granular prescribing data will be important to disentangle the relative contributions of treatment type and adherence behavior. Overall, these findings reinforce the importance of sustained engagement with pharmacological therapy for long-term cardiovascular risk protection.

Findings from our study were primarily driven by ischemic heart disease, with no clear association observed for heart failure. These differences likely reflect variation in underlying pathophysiology. Whereas ischemic heart disease and stroke are largely driven by atherosclerotic processes that may be influenced by glycaemic control and related metabolic pathways,28 heart failure is a more heterogeneous condition involving additional mechanisms, including myocardial dysfunction, structural cardiac remodelling, and haemodynamic stress.29 In addition, we acknowledge that statistical power may have been lower for heart failure due to fewer events, potentially limiting our ability to detect modest associations. Together, these findings highlight the need for further research to better understand the determinants of heart failure risk in T2D and the role of treatment adherence across different cardiovascular complications.

Our results also have implications for early-onset T2D, an increasingly prevalent form of the disease with disproportionate cardiovascular burden.30 Younger adults with T2D have higher lifetime CVD risk and more years of potential life lost. In our study, the relative impact of low adherence was greatest among younger adults, and low adherence was associated with a markedly higher rate of cardiovascular events occurring before 55 years of age. These findings underscore the importance of early and sustained adherence support to mitigate cumulative vascular risk in this vulnerable population.

From a clinical and public health perspective, our findings highlight opportunities to improve long-term outcomes. Medication adherence is influenced by a range of sociodemographic and clinical factors, including comorbidity burden, socioeconomic status, and social support, factors that are also linked to cardiovascular risk.31 Consistent with this, individuals in the low-adherence trajectory were older and had a higher burden of comorbidities and adverse socioeconomic characteristics. Because medication-taking behaviour evolves over time, proactive identification of individuals at risk of declining adherence, together with ongoing monitoring beyond the early treatment period, may be critical for preventing cardiovascular complications. In practice, integrating adherence patterns into risk stratification frameworks, alongside efforts to address broader drivers of poor adherence, could help guide individualized follow-up strategies and better target preventive efforts toward those most likely to benefit.

Strengths of our study include its nationwide scope, near-complete follow-up, and comprehensive linkage of prescription and clinical data, which enabled long-term trajectory modeling. Several limitations warrant consideration. First, as in other registry-based studies,32,33 prescription redemption was used as a proxy for medication intake, which assumes that filled prescriptions reflect actual use. In addition, medication exposure was estimated using DDD from prescription dispensing data, which may not perfectly reflect prescribed dosing in clinical practice, particularly when therapies are individualised or used in combination. Second, adherence trajectories were derived from longitudinal adherence measurements accrued during follow-up. Differences in follow-up duration across trajectory groups may partly account for the lower incidence of cardiovascular disease observed among individuals in higher adherence groups, as these individuals may have had greater opportunity to remain event-free over time. This pattern, however, was not observed for early-onset events. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive patterns of medication-taking behaviour rather than as evidence of causal associations based on exposure groups defined at baseline. Further, adherence was assessed using the PDC, where a threshold of ≥80% is commonly used to indicate adequate adherence.19 However, this cut-off is largely pragmatic, and adherence exists on a continuum. Accordingly, the identified trajectory groups reflect sustained patterns of high, intermediate, and low medication use over time, rather than strict or universally applicable clinical thresholds. Third, adjustment for time-varying variables affected by prior exposure may induce collider-stratification bias. Accordingly, Models 2 and 3 reflect alternative assumptions and are intended as complementary analyses. As the causal role of these variables cannot be determined with certainty, results from these models should be interpreted as complementary rather than causal. Methods specifically designed to address time-varying confounding such as marginal structural models, g-computation, or longitudinal targeted maximum likelihood estimation would be required for causal inference. Fourth, although misclassification of T2D cannot be completely excluded, diagnoses were based on validated algorithms derived from national hospital and procedure registries.15 The study included only individuals who initiated glucose-lowering medication and therefore does not generalize to those with more advanced disease or who never initiated treatment. However, this restriction aligns with our aim of characterizing adherence trajectories from the point of therapy initiation. Finally, residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as glycemic control, body mass index, or lifestyle behaviors is possible, although we adjusted for socioeconomic indicators and performed multiple sensitivity analyses. In addition, the study was conducted in Denmark, a setting with universally accessible, tax-funded healthcare and relatively low cardiovascular disease risk, which may limit generalizability to populations with different healthcare systems, access to care, or baseline risk profiles. Race and ethnicity were not assessed, as these data are not routinely collected in Danish registries, limiting evaluation of generalizability across racial and ethnic groups.

In conclusion, in this nationwide cohort of individuals with newly diagnosed T2D, long-term adherence to glucose-lowering medication was associated with a lower rate of cardiovascular events and a clinically meaningful longer event-free survival over a decade. These associations were primarily driven by ischemic heart disease, with no clear associations observed for heart failure. Together, these findings underscore the importance of sustained patient engagement and support the need for tailored follow-up strategies to promote durable medication adherence as part of routine care for cardiovascular risk reduction in T2D.
