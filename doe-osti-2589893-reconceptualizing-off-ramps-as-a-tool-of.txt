“Thinking About Off-Ramps as a Tool of U.S.-China Escalation Management”

Mike Albertson1
Introduction

Great power rivalry, at any point on the competition spectrum, involves assessments of
risks and decisions on where to take them. Assessing risk versus reward is also integral
to the decision to cooperate in the form of arrangements like arms control. As Donald
Brennan wrote more than sixty years ago, the value in arms control lies in whether it
“reduce[s] the hazards of the present armament policies by a factor greater than the
amount of risk introduced by the control measures themselves.”2 States can assume in
peacetime the risks inherent in cooperating with an adversary, to attempt to mitigate
risks likely to arise in crisis or conflict. Or they can choose to forgo cooperation and
assume risks later, which will complicate their ability to manage crises, war, and war
termination.

As Wu Riqiang noted in a recent article in International Security, China seems to think
that it can downplay the risks of misperception, inadvertent escalation, and arms races,
instead choosing to prioritize the development of military capabilities.3 It believes it does
not need to accept risk in peacetime by cooperating in any meaningful way. It has
assessed it will not run any risk in executing its desired theory of victory (ToV). At the
same time it avoids taking risk, it believes it can successfully manipulate risks in the
minds of the U.S. and its allies in its effort to change the status quo. In short, it
calculates it can have its cake and eat it too, plus have ice cream on the side.

The reality is likely different. As China doubles down on its military buildup, the U.S. and
its allies will likely continue their strategy of denying China its objectives. Given these
countervailing trends, a quick and smooth victory on either side would appear less likely.
This presents interesting questions of how stakes and objectives will change if the
status quo changes in more limited ways. One example might be if there is a protracted
war with no clear winner and loser. Another would be if China has achieved limited
goals (such as seizing Taiwanese controlled islands but not Taiwan itself) but not
complete victory. These challenges of war termination will require the creation of limited

The present challenge, in the current environment of peacetime competition, is

management is likely to be messy, conflict is likely to be costly, war is likely to lack a

with the likely need for off-ramps and war termination - and then working backwards is a
potentially useful approach to this problem. This requires presenting Beijing with risks in
the form of outcomes unacceptable and unthinkable to its current assessment of a risk-
free risk management strategy.

The main conclusions of this chapter, which will diagnose the problem given the lack of
cooperative progress, answer the questions posed by Project Atom on escalation
management with China. It will propose a three-fold pathway which could yield some
new thinking in how we see “off-ramps” in this context. The first step is a focus on U.S.-
China scenarios short of total victory, which are both likely and could form the “change
points” where ad hoc measures are needed. The second step would be a significant
revision in how we think about “off ramps” as a possible substitute organizing concept
for loaded terms like arms control and risk reduction. The third step is an attempt to
identify and explore potentially useable future off-ramps through looking more closely at
factors of identification, selection, construction, communication, and agency.
Diagnosing the Problem

The primary research question posed by Project Atom - what tools and approaches can
the United States use to “manage” escalation risks with China in the Indo-Pacific across
a range of scenarios to include acute incidents at sea, Chinese threats on Taiwan, and
other contingencies? – boils down to fundamental questions of whether escalation risks
can be 1) identified (either in advance or in the moment) and 2) managed successfully
(likely in a cooperative manner) toward a mutually acceptable outcome. Both are hard
questions, and identification and management of risks have been hot topics in the policy
and expert communities.

Identification has proven more straightforward. Potential problem areas like
entanglement4 or miscommunication5 are well-studied. Numerous ideas on cooperative

Management however – be in under the terminological mantle of arms control, risk

reduction agenda. Even when zooming out further and taking a broader conception of
risk reduction to include unilateral measures and perceived unilateral or allied restraint,
the results seem inadequate compared to the decades-plus erosion, the existing
problems, and the assessed risks.

Why has this been the case with both Russia and China, and why should we believe a
new approach to engagement-based or unilateral risk reduction vis-à-vis China can be
more successful going forward? To explore these questions, Project Atom asked the
authors to explore four key issue areas – U.S. and allied strategic objectives, escalation
risks, military tools for escalation management, and tools for escalation management –
and this paper will do so in turn.

converge and diverge in their thinking on several core issues.

The Sticking Point and the Looming Problem

So where does this leave us in terms of a diagnosis? Both sides likely see risk and
escalation as “manageable.” To admit otherwise would invite undesirable policy
prospects of either backing down in the face of aggression over fears of uncontrollable
escalation or maintaining the detested status quo. Both sides have quasi-existential
views of the impact of victory and defeat in a China-Taiwan scenario on the battlefield in
the realm of broader regional and global geopolitics and in their domestic political
worlds. Both are learning lessons on risks and escalation from recent and ongoing
conflicts using new technologies. The key sticking point to substantive cooperative
progress is disparate views on the allure and desirability of risks and escalation. The
U.S. seeks to reduce risks that could emerge from attempts to change the status quo.
China wants the other side to feel uncertainties and risks. Given this difference, an
extensive menu of mechanisms and pre-established options designed to reduce risk, as
least as it has been marketed so far, is thus unlikely to exist in an upcoming U.S.-China
crisis over Taiwan.
But should China to initiate a conflict over Taiwan, some degree of risk reduction
measures will likely be needed. This is inevitable given how the crisis and conflict will

“on the eve of each war, at least one nation misperceives another’s power. In that

realities is echoed by Geoffrey Blainey, “the start of war is – almost by the definition of
warfare – marked by conflicting expectations of what that war will be like. War itself then
provides the stinging ice of reality. And at the end of the war those rival expectations,
initially so far apart, are so close to one another that terms of peace can be agreed

It is at this point of Blainey’s “the stinging ice of reality” where U.S.-China measures will
be needed and should be considered. This is the point where misperceptions about
stakes and capabilities are corrected, where escalation prove to be difficult if not
impossible to “manage” completely, where military options become more expensive or
costly, where domestic and policy divisions emerge, and where limited objectives are
attained but total victory remains distant. These are the “change points” identified by
Richard Neustadt and Ernest May in Thinking in Time, the “relatively fixed and limited
periods along an issue’s timeline” where the trajectory of issues can be affected.17
Something will likely be needed here in these windows of cooperative opportunity, which
could be brief or lengthy. If one cannot convince the adversary to build something on
arms control or risk reduction in advance they see as unnecessary, how could one
create things quickly midstream at the change points when emotions are highest?
Answering the Topics Posed by Project Atom

U.S. and allies’ strategic objectives in the event of a crisis in the Indo-Pacific

Regarding blue strategic objectives, the key challenge lies in differentiating stakes from
objectives. These should be mutually reinforcing – stakes should shape objectives, and
vice versa. Instead, they sometimes become confused, synonymous, or unbalanced.

Discussions on the stakes regarding Taiwan have been largely cast as quasi-existential.
Existential framing is expected, as advocates attempt to draw attention and resources to
the challenge and policymakers try to signal to the other side that there is not an
asymmetry of stakes to be tested and exploited by an adversary. For the United States,
the “loss” of Taiwan is framed as both a significant regional setback in the Asia-Pacific, a

Risks of Escalation in the Indo-Pacific

The second key area tasked for analysis was on escalation risks. As many of the other
authors in this volume note, escalation in the Indo-Pacific could take many forms. It
could be automatic or voluntary, symmetric or asymmetric, linear or circular, fast or slow.
Likely it will be some formulation of all of these at different points in the conflict
spectrum. This places enormous burdens on policymakers and analysts to identify and
manage risks.
Case in point is a quick tally of anticipated crisis/conflict scenarios and thus potential
escalation pathways. U.S.-China scenarios generally fall into three camps – China
initiates (either purposefully or inadvertently), the U.S. initiates (either purposefully or
inadvertently), or some sort of external catalyst initiates a crisis or conflict (either
purposefully or inadvertently). How the scenario starts has important implications for
whether one side or the other believes itself to be more prepared for hostilities (at least
in the initial stages), or whether neither side is fully prepared for conflict. In examining
the literature, there are a myriad of potential scenarios on how a U.S.-China
crisis/conflict could unfold:

variety of ToV paths available as it pursues a change to the status quo regarding

Military Tools for Escalation Management

The third key area requested for analysis is military tools for escalation management.
Given the uncertainties discussed in the previous section, deterring Chinese initiation of
a conflict remains the most effective U.S. tool for escalation management. Should
deterrence fail, the U.S. military approach is fairly clear: it will likely be on the defensive,
it will likely be reacting to Chinese first moves to deny China its objectives, and it has
more limited goals to achieve. Given this approach, U.S. military tools ideally would
inflict more human casualties on the adversary than would put at risk, would provide
options for both symmetrical and asymmetrical responses at every rung in the
escalation ladder and in various warfighting domains, and would be cost-effective.
Favorable ratios in these areas would enhance defense-dominance, would allow for the
ability to blunt initial attempts at a Chinese fait accompli, and would attrit adversary
offensive assets quickly and cheaply.

This would suggest that as the status quo protector, a U.S. escalation management
strategy would need to rely on greater transparency to show clearly and openly U.S.
and allied capabilities and resolve. These capabilities would be communicated through
unambiguous signals sent with military forces such as exercises and deployments,
through displays of technological superiority via testbeds and demonstrations, and
through clear public messaging plans signaling allied cohesion and military
effectiveness.

As possible given fiscal and industrial restraints, the U.S. and its allies must build a
portfolio of capabilities to address anticipated risk areas across the deterrence
spectrum.22 They rightly want to alter Chinese perceptions of the conventional

The capabilities are thus fairly clear. The US and allied military strategy for escalation
management, however, is far less clear should China choose to initiate a crisis or
conflict. Military and political science often operates around the assumption that
escalation risks can be understood, identified, calculated, and managed through the use
of rational actor models, game theory, case studies, and quantitative analysis. This is
prevalent in the Russian military science approach to escalation management.23
Chinese military science writing on escalation management is particularly problematic,
particularly given China’s inexperience with both nuclear crises and major conventional
conflict.24 This disconnect means that US and allied strategies must be prepared, once
initial Chinese aggression is checked, to steer the conflict towards a new status quo that
is acceptable to China but advantageous to the US and its allies.

when it would be easiest but less pressing to do so, they will not be available in crisis
and conflict when they are most needed. Is there a potential for creating these
measures while in a crisis or conflict, at these inflection points when stalemate and
realities have sobered policymakers and military officials up after their initial
enthusiasm? Or must they be built in advance? We have already explored the
challenges in mutually reducing risks given disparate views on the benefits of risks, so
let us turn next to arms control.

Arms Control

At first glance, arms control remains appealing, as one of the principal roles of arms
control is how to reduce the damage from war should it occur. This has traditionally
been done through setting in advance numerical limitations of certain types of weapons
or creating normative behaviors regarding the use of weapons in a conflict. There is
however little meaningful in place on arms control between the United States and China
to limit military capabilities or behaviors. As discussed, this is unfortunate but
unsurprising as neither side wants to limit its emerging capabilities or take potential
actions off the table. Neither side wants to increase confidence or reduce risks for the
other side. Particularly given the variety of pathways, neither wants to assume potential
risk in trading something of known assessed value away. Both sides doubt adversary
commitments would hold in a high-stakes situation.

This is not to say that future arms control with China is impossible, or that it needs to be
significantly reimagined as something wholly different than the legally binding treaties of
the past. But it likely must wait until a future point at which both the United States and
China either grow more comfortable with their capabilities vis-à-vis one another, more
certain as to what will be needed and unneeded (and thus bargained over without
significant risk), or more comfortable with a mutually agreed status quo.

Off-Ramps

Understanding the problem and acknowledging that risk reduction and arms control
have limited opportunities in the present moment, can off-ramps be useful? Perhaps,
but their utility likely requires a better understanding of escalation management, change
points, and off-ramps.

To begin, there needs to be a deeper exploration of crisis and conflict scenarios, how
they might unfold, and the potential challenges these pose to desired blue and red
theories of victory. There has been a useful expansion in recent years of the U.S.-China
crisis and conflict spectrum beyond the cross-strait invasion scenario to incorporate

 12

challenges of asymmetric attacks, maritime blockade, and political/economic coercion.
The more pathways that can be explored and considered in advance, the better.

One area deserving more analysis is where scenarios bog down and players grow
frustrated, which should increasingly be seen as a feature rather than a bug. Given
scarcity factors of money, sponsor interest, and human participation, exercises typically
are driven to escalate. They do not bog down, often simply ending when the exercise
reaches its conclusion or the participants run out of time. Less often explored is when
participants on both sides get “stuck” - when they have to endure several frustrating
rounds of tit-for-tat with no decision outcome in place, when they face growing domestic
pressures to end or win the war, when they begin to see only limited aims are
achievable. This is an unsatisfying result to a game designer or participant, but it could
reflect realities of how crisis and conflict unfolds.

This is the critical pre-identification of points in a potential crisis or conflict when “change
points,” or as Thomas Schelling and Richard Smoke might call them “equilibrium” or
“salencies,” occur. 25 This has an important human dimension: when participants attempt
to reconfigure the dynamics of an unfolding crisis or conflict, when military operations go
off autopilot, political leadership is reasserted, and factors like emotion and frustrating
come into the equation.

A number of useful dimensions which can be explored on these critical moments. Time
is one: how long do we expect these to last? Actions are another: how do people
attempt to unstick the situation? Collaboration and signalling are critical factors: how do
parties communicate with one another? This also tests the value of pre-established
measures. Strong counterpart ties emerge to be used in a crisis or conflict. Established
mil-to-mil communication lines may finally be used. Backchannel interlocutors may
quietly communicate changing stakes and objectives.
Analysis of stuck moments helps identify potentially needed ad hoc at these points.
Alexander George, writing in the early 1980s when détente had collapsed and the
prospects for future U.S.-Soviet arms control seemed dim, wrote about how “when
norms and rules of engagement do not exist to regulate superpower involvement in a
contested area, the task becomes one of improvising an ad hoc set of ground rules for
managing that particular competition.”26 Managing the competition then becomes largely
about limiting things: limiting escalatory potential, limiting objectives to be achieved in a
particular conflict, and limiting the means employed to achieve that objective. George
further delineates the limiting of means into factors of types of weapons, targets,

Off-Ramps as Tools of Escalation, De-Escalation, and Termination

“Off-ramps” is one of the most overused terms in the deterrence lexicon, where
policymakers and analysts often talk of the need to building off-ramps in the midst of a
crisis or a conflict. Off-ramps however are often unappealing to the aggressor, untaken
by the aggressor, and thus frustrating and escalatory to the constructor of the off-ramp.
Why is this the case?

The Misleading Visualization of Off-Ramps
The term “off-ramps” triggers distinct memories. A family sets off for some distant
destination, the driver focuses on the road, and the passengers watch the scenery out
the window. Road signs and billboards highlight what’s coming up. Periodically the car
pulls off the road. There are scheduled stops get gas. There are unscheduled stops to
visit some local roadside attraction. There are evening layovers at motels with a
morning resumption of the drive to the final destination.

Off-ramps are often frustrating when they are envisioned but not taken in crisis and
conflict scenarios. The complex reality does not conform to the image of their pre-
constructed clarity and pre-planned simplicity in a road trip. They are instead missed or
lay half-finished. “Why didn’t they stop? It was perfectly obvious where the off-ramp
was!” They take on a negative connotation, as unhelpful appeasement short of desired
military outcomes. They become counterproductive, spurring further military action.

Looking closer, it makes perfect sense why off-ramps are often not taken based on
simple factors like the driver, the destination, the road, the signage, the passengers, the
vehicle, and the appeal to pull off the road. Drivers are in control, but they are all
different individuals. Destinations can change. Roads can be wide and straight or dark
and curvy. Signage can be clearly marked or easily missed. Passengers can have a
great deal of power or no power in the journey. The vehicle can be well-maintained or
ready to fall apart. Pulling off the road short of the destination can be a pre-planned
decision or an event compelled by internal or external factors.

Thinking More Deeply about Off-Ramps

This practical reconceptualization of off-ramps provides value when we think about tools
that can be applied across the spectrum of conflict, from peacetime to crisis and war.

Escalation is not inevitable. Escalation does not move in a single direction, solely
upwards. Escalation often is not rapid, nor does it grow necessarily more rapid as the
crisis or conflict unfolds. Escalation can be better viewed as a trip by a driver
undertaken with a particular destination in mind, but one which likely has pauses –
planned and unplanned – built into the journey. For these pauses to become meaningful
off-ramps as we understand it in escalation management, analytic work needs to be
done involving identification, selection, construction, signage, and agency.

Identification is the first step. Structured analytic techniques used for exploring
challenges of escalation across the spectrum – wargames, table-top exercises,
scenario-based discussions – seldom allow for pauses. They are driven toward a
particular end, centered on a particular research question, or focused on a particular
hypothesis. Where they are lacking is in finding these “change points” and sticking on
them. Players say “shouldn’t we be thinking of off-ramps?” as they are shunted along to
the next move in the exercise. Finding and circling change points for further analysis
would be useful. Emerging technology tools can play some role here. But ultimately
these are humans making decisions. Artificial intelligence can map out a perfect car trip,
but it cannot account for the personal whims of the driver or the passengers once the
trip is underway.
Not all change points will ultimately be off-ramps. The point immediately after one side
has chosen to dramatically escalate, in terms of the aforementioned George factors of
weapons, targets, participants, and geographical boundaries, is perhaps not ripe for an
off-ramp. The initiator may see every incentive to push forward and exploit their
advantage. The consideration of off-ramps here by the recipient, rather than reciprocal
action to correct misperceptions of will and stakes in the conflict, may be dismissed as
capitulation or acceptance of the adversary’s attempt to terminate conflict on favorable
terms. These are the kinds of exercise discussions that give off-ramps a whiff of
surrender and diminish their perceived utility as viable tools of escalation management.

Therefore, some process of selection in scenarios featuring the U.S. and China is
necessary. Selection might show that off-ramps would be useful in points where neither
side can gain a perceived advantage, where several rounds of tit-for-tat strikes have
gained either side little, where the aggressor is starting to see the flaws in their initial
assumptions, where nuclear use is threatened but unlikely to change the conventional
battlefield, where domestic support on both sides for a continuation of the conflict is
waning. Exercises that feature these kinds of dynamics at particular points in play may
allow for better exploration of whether off-ramps at these potential stages would have
better odds of success.

Work can then be done on construction, attempting to pre-experience and pre-contruct
off-ramps that are assessed to be more important or more likely to occur. Most of these

 15

involve bargaining or communication mechanisms to convey messaging and suggest
alternatives. This might be done in peacetime by senior policymakers, by military
officials in the midst of an accident or crisis, or by back channel interlocutors in
moments of tension. In this light, many of the legacy mechanisms mentioned at the
beginning of the paper - crisis communication mechanisms, hotlines, mutual signaling
frameworks, strategic stability dialogues, air/sea incident mechanisms – are essentially
off-ramps when reconceived. But taking an off-ramp implies there is a mutual desire by
both parties – one to construct or highlight the off-ramp, the other to pull off the road
either temporarily or for good. If there is not mutual desire, then we are talking less
about off-ramps than roadblocks, which is a completely different discussion on coercion
rather than communication and influence.

Pre-construction also involves thinking hard about signage, consistent with the earlier
analogy of driving down the road and seeing billboards and road signs indicating when
and where offramps occur and what is waiting at that offramp. How to communicate
these in the midst of a crisis or conflict is a difficult problem that warrants attention when
focusing on the change points and the construction of off-ramps. Conversations on the
shortfalls of Chinese military literature on the topic are useful for highlighting the
problem but unlikely to be convincing to the Chinese. Track 1.5 and Track 2
conversations can highlight the problem, but they must try and break new ground as
opposed to being dueling speeches on perceptions of reality.

Agency is the final factor: why do people decide to take off-ramps? Likely because they
see greater value in taking an off-ramp than proceeding down the road. But this
calculation could be an independent decision, or one shepherded or compelled by an
adversary. Academic and think tank work would be useful in providing more historical
analysis of off-ramps – when did they succeed and fail? why? how and when were off-
ramps constructed? how were they communicated and perceived by the players? This
would help the discussion go beyond the standard Cuban Missile Crisis analogies of
two sides working together behind the scenes to get out of a problem.

The Need for Uncomfortable Experiences in Peacetime

While conversations and historical examples are useful, experiential processes likely
hold the most potential, simply because of their power to force players to experience the
challenge firsthand in an immersive environment. But this requires an increased focus in
structural analytic games to less glamorous problems: of players getting stuck for
multiple rounds of unfruitful tit-for-tat, of building in meaningful and time-consuming
communication mechanisms into games, of not forcing escalation, or exploring
uncomfortable scenarios of multiple rounds of limited nuclear use, of not allowing
players to push the cyber button when faced with a lack of real options of what to do
next.

 16

Both U.S. and Chinese players have to experience this situation for themselves and
draw conclusions about whether pre-existing beliefs about stakes, capabilities, and
capacities to successfully manage escalation are valid. This approach has proven to be
have some utility in India-Pakistan related exercises.28 But this exploration of where the
two sides get stuck or get bogged down, and thus where to think about conceiving or
pre-constructing off-ramps, could be more valuable than another exercise that talks
about who wins or loses bases on estimates of capabilities and operations.

How does this help the U.S. and China get to concrete solutions regarding the
challenges posed by escalation management? At present, there is a serious disconnect
in the value of a discussion on the term “escalation management.” Right now, the United
States is attempting to have a conversation on the dangers of escalation management
with a China who feels comfortable in its ability to manage escalation. The same is true
on the term “risk reduction,” with the United States attempting to reduce risks that the
Chinese side sees value in creating and promoting. Putting aside the theological
discussion of whether escalation can be managed or risk needs to be reduced, some
mutual recognition and agreement on pathways and problem sets – how things could
unfold, where things might get messy, where new ad hoc rules might need to be created
- allows the two sides to think about areas where some degree of communication and
cooperation might be useful. This thinking will be aided by some degree of identification,
selection, pre-constructions, and communication of off ramps to show why there is
potential mutual interest in thinking of the need for building and utilizing ad hoc
arrangements. If discussion on these ad hoc rules of the road gains traction, this could
serve as the basis for additional measures that could agreed in peacetime.
