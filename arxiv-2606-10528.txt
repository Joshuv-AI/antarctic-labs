Representation-Aware Advantage Estimation: Your Reward Model Provides More Than A Scalar Output
Guozheng Li, Xiyan Fu, Yiwen Guo

Abstract
Current reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) methods primarily rely on scalar rewards from a trained reward model (RM). While effective, scalar rewards are often noisy and fail to capture fine-grained preference differences, whereas RM hidden states encode richer semantic and preference information. We introduce the representation-aware advantage estimation, which leverages RM hidden states and models them as auxiliary signals for better advantage estimation. Specifically, we propose the Graph-based Advantage Estimation (GraphAE), treat each sampled group as a graph, where nodes correspond to responses and edges capture their similarity in the RM hidden space. Then advantages are computed via graph propagation, enabling each sample to incorporate contextual information from its neighbors. GraphAE is lightweight and can be seamlessly integrated into existing group-based RL algorithms. We apply GraphAE to GRPO, GSPO and RLOO, and conduct extensive experiments on different models and benchmarks. Empirical results show consistent improvements across three benchmarks, with gains of up to + 6.3 on Arena-Hard-v0.1, + 8.27 on AlpacaEval 2.0, and + 0.22 on MT-Bench. These results demonstrate that leveraging RM representations leads to more sample efficient and robust RLHF 1 1 1 https://github.com/1iguozheng/graphae .

Introduction

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

9

. The standard RLHF pipelines rely on a scalar reward produced by a reward model (RM) to guide policy updates via policy gradient methods
25
. Many policy optimization methods have been developed to improve the stability and efficiency of RLHF, including PPO
21
, group-based variants such as GRPO
22

Although these methods have achieved strong empirical performance, they heavily rely on the scalar output of the RM. However, we find different responses may receive identical or very similar scores despite exhibiting clear semantic differences, as shown in Figure
1
. That is, a scalar reward is often insufficient to capture fine-grained differences between responses. As a result, the advantage estimates computed in many online RL algorithms based on such reward signals can be inaccurate or noisy

To address this limitation, we aim to identify a more expressive signal that can better capture fine-grained differences among responses. Recent studies on interpretable RMs suggest that intermediate representations encode rich, fine-grained information related to preference and reward signals that is not fully reflected in the final scalar output

28

37

In specific, we propose the Graph-based Advantage Estimation (GraphAE), a structured advantage estimator built upon RM representations. Given a set of sampled responses, we construct a similarity graph based on their RM representations, where edge weights reflect pairwise similarity. We then estimate advantages by solving a graph-regularized objective that balances fidelity to the original scalar rewards with smoothness over the graph

23
;
41
)
. This yields a closed-form solution that can be efficiently solved in practice. The resulting rewards integrate both individual reward signals and local structural information, leading to a more informative and robust estimator. It is lightweight and can be seamlessly integrated into existing group-based RL algorithms by replacing the original advantage estimator, such as GRPO
(

)
, GSPO
(

)
, and RLOO
(

)
.

, AlpacaEval 2.0
(

, and MT-Bench

Moreover, GraphAE reduces within-group reward variance and accelerates convergence. GraphAE is robust across hyperparameter choices and adds minimal computation during advantage estimation, showing that leveraging RM representations leads to more stable and efficient RLHF.

Related Work

2.1
Reinforcement Learning for LLMs

Reinforcement learning has emerged as a prevalent method for fine-tuning LLMs, with PPO
21
and its variations
20
;
30
being the most prevalent methods. These techniques largely adhere to the actor-critic paradigm
24
, which significantly increases training complexity and computational resource consumption. Many recent studies have sought to mitigate this limitation. Methods such as DPO
19
and its variants
17
;
7
;
11
bypass reward modeling and the actor-critic learning framework by directly learning from preferences. However, existing research indicates that due to their offline nature, these approaches exhibit a performance gap compared to online RL
31
. In contrast to actor-critic and offline methods, recent work explores
group-based policy optimization

22
;
34
;
16
, which removes the need for a value function by leveraging multiple responses per prompt. For example, GRPO
(
22
)
computes advantages via group-relative rewards, avoiding critic training while remaining efficient. GSPO
(
39
)
improves stability by moving from token- to sequence-level optimization, and RLOO
(
1
)
uses a leave-one-out baseline for variance reduction. Despite these differences, they all rely on scalar rewards and treat samples independently, ignoring their structural relationships.

2.2
Reward Models

Reward models (RMs) are central to RLHF, serving as learned proxies for human preferences and providing scalar rewards for policy optimization
(
4
)
. The standard formulation follows the Bradley–Terry model
3
, which assigns scalar scores based on pairwise preference data
2
;
9
. Recent work extends this paradigm with more fine-grained supervision, such as process reward models (PRMs) that provide step-level signals
14
. To address the limitations of raw rewards, a related line of work explores reward shaping techniques that modify or augment reward signals to improve training stability, credit assignment, and robustness to reward hacking
12
;
8
.

Despite these efforts, most RLHF pipelines ultimately rely on a single scalar reward derived from the final hidden state, compressing rich internal representations into a point estimate
26
. This overlooks the information encoded in RM representations, which capture semantic and preference-aligned relationships among samples
33
;
27
. In this work, we instead leverage RM hidden states as reward features to refine advantage estimation.

3
Preliminary

Consider a large language model (LLM)
\pi_{\theta}
parameterized by
\theta
, which takes an input sequence
x
and generates an output sequence
y
according to the conditional distribution
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) aims to optimize
\pi_{\theta}
to maximize expected rewards aligned with human preferences. In practice, such rewards are provided by a reward model (RM) trained from human preference data. Specifically, given a dataset of preference comparisons
\mathcal{D}_{\text{pref}}=\{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\}
, where
y_{w}\succ y_{l}
indicates that
y_{w}
is preferred over
y_{l}
for prompt
x
, preferences are commonly modeled using the Bradley–Terry (BT) model
3
with an underlying ground-truth reward function
r^{\star}(\cdot,\cdot)
:

P(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)=\sigma\big(r^{\star}(x,y_{w})-r^{\star}(x,y_{l})\big),

(1)

where
\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})
denotes the sigmoid function. A parametric RM
r(x,y)
can then be learned via maximum likelihood estimation:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(r)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{pref}}}\left[-\log\sigma\big(r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l})\big)\right].

(2)

In RLHF, the RM provides a scalar reward for each response:
r_{i}=r(x,y_{i})
, where
y_{i}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
. Based on these rewards, group-based RL algorithms such as GRPO
22
estimate advantages based on a global baseline:

A_{i}=r_{i}-\bar{r},\quad\text{where}\quad\bar{r}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}r_{j},

(3)

for a group of
G
sampled completions. Then RLHF formulates policy optimization as maximizing the expected advantages under the policy while constraining deviation from a reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
:

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\ \mathcal{J}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\big[A(x,y)\big]

(4)

\displaystyle-\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})\big].

where
\beta>0
controls the strength of KL regularization. This objective is typically optimized using policy gradient methods
25
.

4
Representation-Aware Advantage Estimation

We introduce a representation-aware advantage estimation that leverages reward model representations to refine advantage signals via graph regularization. We formalize the estimation objective in §
4.1
and derive its closed-form solution in §
4.2
. Finally, we show that the refined advantages can be seamlessly integrated into existing group-based RL algorithms in §
4.3
.

4.1
Graph-Regularized Advantage Formulation

Existing advantage estimation relies on independent scalar rewards. However, prior work
(
36
)
suggests that reward signals are inherently noisy, as responses with similar quality can still receive noticeably different scores. This issue causes high-variance advantage estimates. To address this issue, we refine the reward signal by introducing two complementary objectives:

Representation-aware smoothness.

We encourage similar responses to have consistent reward estimates by enforcing smoothness in the reward model representation space. To this end, we construct a similarity graph over sampled responses. Each node corresponds to a response
y_{i}
, represented by:

z_{i}=f_{\phi}(x,y_{i}),

where
z_{i}
is the hidden representation before the reward head. Edges encode pairwise similarity between responses, with weights defined by a Gaussian kernel:

K_{ij}=\exp\left(-\frac{\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

(5)

This yields a fully-connected weighted graph that captures local relationships in the representation space, and induces a smoothness constraint
\sum_{i,j}K_{ij}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}
that penalizes discrepancies between similar responses.

Fidelity to the original reward.

To prevent over-smoothing, the refined reward should remain close to the original reward signal produced by the reward model. We therefore impose a fidelity constraint of the form
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{r}\|^{2}
, which anchors the optimized rewards to the original values.

Combining the above two objectives, we obtain the refined reward
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}
as:

\tilde{\mathbf{r}}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}}\underbrace{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{r}\|^{2}}_{\text{fidelity}}+\lambda\underbrace{\sum_{i,j}K_{ij}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}}_{\text{smoothness}}

(6)

where
\lambda
controls the trade-off between fidelity and smoothness.

4.2
Solution and Properties

The objective in Eq.
6
corresponds to a standard graph Laplacian regularization problem. Let
L=D-K
denote the graph Laplacian, where
D_{ii}=\sum_{j}K_{ij}
. The optimization problem can be rewritten as:

\tilde{\mathbf{r}}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{r}\|^{2}+\lambda\mathbf{x}^{\top}L\mathbf{x}.

(7)

Taking derivatives with respect to
\mathbf{x}
and setting to zero gives:

2(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{r})+2\lambda L\mathbf{x}=0,

(8)

which yields the linear system:

(\mathbf{I}+\lambda L)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{r}.

(9)

Since
\mathbf{I}+\lambda L\in\mathbb{R}^{G\times G}
is symmetric positive definite, the solution is unique and can be computed efficiently. In RLHF, the group size
G
is typically small (e.g.,
G\leq 32
), making direct solvers both efficient and numerically stable. The computational cost is
O(G^{3})
and negligible in practice. Therefore, the optimal solution admits a closed form:

\tilde{\mathbf{r}}=(\mathbf{I}+\lambda L)^{-1}\mathbf{r}.

(10)

This solution can be interpreted as graph-based smoothing of the reward signal, where each entry aggregates information from similar responses while remaining anchored to its original value.

4.3
Compatibility with Group-based RL

Our method can be seamlessly integrated into a wide range of group-based RL algorithms. Specifically, we replace the original reward
r_{i}
with the refined reward
\tilde{r}_{i}
in the advantage estimator.
For example, in GRPO
22
:

A_{i}^{\text{GRPO}}:\quad\frac{r_{i}-\mu_{r}}{\sigma_{r}+\epsilon}\;\rightarrow\;\frac{\tilde{r}_{i}-\mu_{\tilde{r}}}{\sigma_{\tilde{r}}+\epsilon}.

(11)

This modification only affects the advantage estimation step, while leaving the policy optimization procedure unchanged.

Experimental Setup

5.1
Datasets and Evaluation

We evaluate the performance of GraphAE on three widely adopted RLHF benchmarks: Arena-Hard-v0.1
13
, AlpacaEval 2.0
6
, and MT-Bench
40
. These benchmarks require the model to generate answers to a wide range of open-ended questions.

We follow the standard evaluation protocol of each benchmark. For Arena-Hard-v0.1, we report the win rate (WR) against GPT-4-0314 with the 95% confidence interval (CI), using GPT-4.1 as the judge. For AlpacaEval 2.0, we report both WR and length-controlled win rate (LC WR) against GPT-4-Turbo. For MT-Bench, we report two turns and average scores using GPT-4-Turbo as the judge. More details are provided in the Appendix
A
.

5.2
Baselines

GraphAE is designed as a plug-in advantage estimator for group-based RLHF. We apply it to three representative group-based RL algorithms, including GRPO
22
, GSPO
39
, and RLOO
(
1
)
. For each method, we replace the original scalar reward based advantage estimator with GraphAE while keeping all other components unchanged.

We compare GraphAE against RL methods that use scalar rewards for advantage estimation. To avoid the effect of reward regularization, we further include reward smoothing baselines of the form
\tilde{r}_{i}=(1-\alpha)r_{i}+\alpha\bar{r}
, where
\bar{r}
denotes the mean reward within each rollout group and
\alpha
controls the smoothing strength.

The
\alpha

For a more comprehensive evaluation, we also include classical baselines such as PPO

5.3
Implementation Details

Reward Model.

We adopt a pre-trained reward model Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B
15
given its superior performance across multiple benchmarks. The pre-trained model allows us to isolate the contribution of representation advantage estimation without confounding factors from training. In GraphAE, we extract the representation of the last token from the final layer for graph-based advantage estimation.

Policy Training.

We conduct RL training on two widely used instruction-tuned LLMs: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
32
and Llama-3-8B-Instruct
10
. Specifically, we use a learning rate of 2e-6 and set the maximum generated sequence length to 1024. We employ a batch size of 1024 and a mini-batch size of 8, with 1 epoch and 128 gradient accumulation steps. The sampling temperature is set to 0.7 and the KL coefficient is set to 0.01. We roll out 8 responses for each prompt. We set the smoothing coefficient
\lambda
to 1 for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and 0.5 for Llama-3-8B-Instruct. For the Gaussian kernel bandwidth
\sigma
, we adaptively set it to the median of all non-zero pairwise distances within each group, allowing it to adjust to different group scales.

6
Results and Analysis

6.1
Main Results

Overall Results.

Table
1
summarizes the main results across three evaluation benchmarks. Compared with the original group-based RL algorithms, GraphAE consistently improves GRPO, GSPO, and RLOO across both model backbones and all evaluation benchmarks. This demonstrates the effectiveness and generalization ability of GraphAE, compared to original scalar based advantage estimation methods. We also observe that the uniform reward smoothing baseline (+Smooth) yields only marginal or inconsistent improvements, suggesting that the gains of GraphAE are not attributable to reward regularization, but rather to better exploitation of preference information encoded in RM representations. Moreover, all RL methods substantially outperform the w/o RL baselines, while PPO achieves only moderate gains despite requiring an additional value network.

GraphAE achieves its largest gains on Arena-Hard and AlpacaEval, improving over GRPO by up to +6.3 and +8.27 points, respectively, while the gains on MT-Bench are comparatively smaller. This pattern is consistent with the characteristics of the benchmarks. In Arena-Hard and AlpacaEval, the quality differences between responses are often subtle, making scalar rewards more susceptible to noise and leading to less reliable advantage estimates. GraphAE alleviates this issue by leveraging representation aware advantage estimation, which provide richer and more stable signals. In contrast, MT-Bench exhibits a narrower score range among strong models, which limits the headroom for further improvement.

Feature Ablation.

In this section, we explore whether the improvements of GraphAE come from the preference relevant information encoded in RM hidden states, rather than merely from introducing a graph structure. To examine this, we conduct a feature ablation by keeping the underlying Qwen + GRPO optimization pipeline unchanged and varying only the feature source used for graph construction. We compare GraphAE with three controlled variants: i)
Scalar-Graph
, which constructs graph edges from scalar reward differences; ii)
Policy-Graph
, which uses the last-token hidden states of the policy model; and iii)
Shuffled RM-Graph
, which randomly permutes the correspondence between responses and RM features.

As shown in Figure
3
,
Scalar-Graph
performs similarly to vanilla GRPO, with slight drops on Arena-Hard and only marginal gains on AlpacaEval. This suggests that graph construction based solely on scalar rewards provides little additional information beyond the reward signal already used by GRPO.
Policy-Graph
achieves moderate improvements, indicating that policy-model representations capture some local semantic structure among responses. However, the remaining gap to GraphAE suggests that semantic similarity alone is insufficient for constructing an effective preference graph. In contrast,
Shuffled RM-Graph
degrades performance below GRPO across all metrics, demonstrating that an incorrect neighborhood structure can actively harm optimization. Overall, these results show that the gains of GraphAE primarily stem from the preference-relevant local geometry encoded in RM hidden states, rather than from graph connectivity, feature dimensionality, or the use of a graph structure alone.

6.2
Mechanism Analysis

To better understand why GraphAE improves performance, we conduct a series of analyses on its underlying behavior:

Variance Reduction.

We compare training steps of GRPO under the same training settings. For each training step, we compute the standard deviation of rewards within each rollout group and track its moving average throughout training. A lower value means that rewards within the same rollout group are less variable. As illustrated in Figure
4
, we observe that GraphAE reduces the within-group reward standard deviation, compared with the original scalar reward based optimization. This suggests that GraphAE provides smoother and less noisy rewards, enhancing the training stability.

Figure 4:
Same budget training dynamics of group reward std. over the first 30k training steps on GRPO.

Denoising Without Losing Preference Structure.

After observing more stable within-group rewards during training, we further ask whether GraphAE preserves the underlying preference structure while reducing reward noise. We analyze 120 rollout groups with RM features. For each group, we compare the original reward signal with the GraphAE signal from Eq.
10
using i) centered-signal standard deviation,
\mathrm{Std}(s)=\left(\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\left(s_{i}-\bar{s}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}
, where
\bar{s}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}s_{i}
; and ii) graph total variation,
\mathrm{GTV}(s)=\frac{1}{G^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}K_{ij}\left(s_{i}-s_{j}\right)^{2}
, where
K_{ij}
denotes the RM hidden-state graph edge weight. We report both metrics after normalization by the corresponding value of the original scalar reward signal.
Figure
5
shows ten randomly sampled results, and the average value across 120 groups.

GraphAE substantially reduces both quantities, showing that it effectively suppresses local reward noise. However, the denoised signal still remains highly aligned with the original reward structure: the average pairwise sign-flip rate is only 6.25%, and the cosine alignment between the original rewards and GraphAE remains 0.905. These results suggest that GraphAE reduces noisy local fluctuations without collapsing the preference geometry.

Stronger Benefits for High-Variance Group.

Since GraphAE reduces local reward fluctuations without destroying preference structure, we next examine where this denoising effect brings the largest benefit. We bucket matched rollout groups by their baseline reward standard deviation under vanilla GRPO, and measure the change in group mean reward after applying GRPO + GraphAE on the same prompts. Table

This suggests that GraphAE is most useful when scalar rewards are noisy and credit assignment is unreliable, rather than uniformly smoothing all groups.

Robustness to Hyperparameters.

We evaluate the robustness of GraphAE to its main hyperparameters on Arena-Hard under GRPO. Figure

varies the Laplacian strength
\lambda

Larger groups bring slightly stronger improvements, where richer local neighborhoods make graph-based credit assignment more effective. linear system after reward computation, its cost is negligible relative to policy and RM forward passes. Taken together, these results show that GraphAE is both more sample efficient and lightweight in practice.

Checkpoint Efficiency.

To investigate whether GraphAE accelerates policy convergence, we save checkpoints every 10k training steps and evaluate them on Arena-Hard. As shown in Figure
7
, GraphAE improves alignment quality throughout the entire training trajectory: it robustly outperforms vanilla GRPO at every evaluated checkpoint. For Qwen, GraphAE reaches the final GRPO peak score of 51.5 at merely 30k steps, whereas vanilla GRPO requires 50k steps to reach this level, corresponding to a 40% reduction in optimization steps. This accelerated convergence pattern generalizes remarkably to Llama, where GraphAE attains the GRPO peak in fewer than 30k steps and eventually climbs to an unprecedented 33.9. Moreover, both models show that our method improves both final performance and convergence speed under the same compute budget.

7
Conclusion

We propose to leverage reward model hidden states as graph signals to compute advantages. By propagating rewards over graphs, our method GraphAE captures local correlations, reduces variance, and generalizes group-based RL algorithms. It bridges the gap between scalar reward estimation and structured inference, offering a more information rich, stable, and effective RLHF paradigm.

Limitations

GraphAE relies on the quality of the reward model representations to construct the similarity graph. When the RM hidden states contain weak or noisy preference structure, the effectiveness of graph-based propagation may be reduced. In addition, GraphAE introduces a small amount of extra computation for graph construction and matrix solving within each rollout group. Although this overhead is lightweight in our experiments, scaling to substantially larger group sizes may require more efficient approximations in future work.

Appendix C
Effect of Generic Reward Smoothing

To further distinguish GraphAE from generic reward regularization, we study the effect of the smoothing coefficient
\alpha
in the reward smooth baseline (
\tilde{r}_{i}=(1-\alpha)r_{i}+\alpha\bar{r}
) on final performance. The Qwen + GRPO results are shown in Table
5
. We observe that a small amount of smoothing brings only limited benefit, while stronger smoothing consistently hurts final performance. As
\alpha
increases, the scalar rewards within each rollout group become less distinguishable, which weakens useful preference contrasts among responses. This suggests that indiscriminately suppressing reward variance is not sufficient, and can even be harmful when the original reward differences contain meaningful preference information.

In contrast, GraphAE improves performance substantially over both vanilla GRPO and all reward smoothing variants. The key difference is that GraphAE does not collapse all rewards toward the group mean. Instead, it propagates reward information locally according to the similarity graph induced by RM hidden states. Therefore, responses that are close in the RM representation space can share information, while unrelated responses are not forced to receive similar rewards. This selective, structure-aware denoising allows GraphAE to reduce noisy local fluctuations without destroying useful preference distinctions, explaining why it outperforms generic reward smoothing.

Appendix D
Generalization of Variance Reduction

We further examine whether the variance-reduction effect of GraphAE generalizes beyond GRPO. Specifically, we compare the training dynamics of GSPO and RLOO under the same training settings, and track the moving average of the within-group reward standard deviation throughout training. As shown in Figure

The reduction is especially clear in the early and middle stages of training, where vanilla GSPO and RLOO exhibit larger fluctuations in group rewards. By propagating rewards over the RM representation graph, GraphAE produces smoother within-group reward signals while keeping the underlying optimization procedure unchanged. These results complement the GRPO analysis and suggest that GraphAE provides a general variance-reduction mechanism for group-based RLHF algorithms.

Appendix E
Case Study

We further provide several qualitative examples to show how GraphAE changes individual rewards. Table
6
reports four Qwen + GRPO rollout cases. Each case contains a target response, its nearest neighbor in the RM hidden space, the original scalar RM reward, and the refined GraphAE reward. These examples reveal a clear pattern. The scalar RM sometimes gives a reward that is inconsistent with nearby responses. In the first two cases, the target response receives a relatively high scalar reward, but its nearest neighbors suggest that this reward is over-estimated. GraphAE therefore pulls the reward down. In the last two cases, the target response receives a very low scalar reward, although it is close to higher-reward responses in the RM hidden space. GraphAE then raises its reward toward the local neighborhood.

This shows why GraphAE works. It does not simply smooth all rewards toward the group mean. Instead, it uses the RM hidden space to identify which responses should influence each other. If a response is close to strong neighbors, GraphAE can recover an under-estimated reward. If a response is close to weaker or more cautious neighbors, GraphAE can suppress an over-estimated reward. In this way, GraphAE reduces local reward noise while keeping the preference structure encoded by the RM representations. The examples therefore provide direct evidence that RM hidden states contain useful information beyond the scalar reward, and that GraphAE can use this information to produce more reliable advantage estimates.
