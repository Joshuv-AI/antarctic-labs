Executive Summary
Biological processes are increasingly important to the domestic production of chemicals.
The separation and purification of the desired product from the complex mixtures
produced via fermentation and other biological processes is a frequent barrier to the
scale-up and commercialization of such approaches. Further, physical property data are
scarce, and modeling tools are rarely capable of accurately predicting the separation of
multi-component mixtures. This project addressed all of these challenges by 1)
collecting experimental data for adsorption, capacitive deionization, and membrane
separation of organic acids at both laboratory and pilot scales; 2) developing enhanced
thermodynamic models to predict the separation performance of these processes for
both single- and multi-component mixtures; 3) demonstrating the augmentation of
experimental and literature data via machine learning (ML) to improve model
predictions; 4) incorporating enhanced thermodynamic models into process models
using commercially-available software; and 5) exploring the technoeconomic analysis,
life cycle assessment, and technology-to-market analysis of adsorption and membrane
separation technologies for biological processes.

The project established a rigorous and comprehensive methodology for process
development of biochemical processes. The methodology includes five steps: 1) proper
experimental design and measurement of the lab-scale separation data that covers both
single and binary solutes, 2) parameterization and validation of the thermodynamic
models that accurately correlate and predict separation performance data for both
single- and multi-component mixtures, 3) incorporation of the thermodynamic models
into process simulation models, 4) validation of the process simulation models with pilot
plant data for both single- and multi-component mixtures, and 5) application of the
process simulation models in techno-economic analysis and life cycle analysis.

The TEA and LCA work completed during this project provides an example of how to
establish a baseline case for separation technologies. These analyses were limited to
the separation operations of a larger biochemical process and were restricted to a
single component (acetic acid) in aqueous solution. During the intermediate verification
process, the verification team recommended discontinuing further TEA and LCA
evaluation in favor of continued development of the process models. The T2M work
completed in this project provides a framework that can be used for future evaluation of
separation technologies for biochemical processes.

The work supported by this project led to more than three inventions, 12 articles
published or in preparation, 24 presentations and posters, and supported four graduate
student theses. The enhanced thermodynamic models, machine learning approaches,
and process modeling implementations developed during this project expand the

 Page 4 of 81

available body of knowledge and improve model quality, which can accelerate the
evaluation and implementation of separation technologies in biological manufacturing
processes.

Given the limited project resource and schedule, some of the tasks for thermodynamic
models and process simulation models have not been fully completed. To fully
demonstrate the process development methodology, these remaining tasks must be
completed. Furthermore, to fully realize the impact of this work, it is recommended that
these thermodynamic models should be implemented in commercial process simulators
accessible to process engineers developing these biochemical processes. Moreover, it
is recommended that a new biochemical process development project be initiated to
benchmark the proposed process development methodology.

Background
Cost-effective separations are known to be a key barrier in the scale-up of bio-
processing technology pathways to industrially-relevant volumes. Process simulation
can reduce the cost and technical risk associated with failure during trial-and-error
experimentation and mitigates design errors associated with process scale-up.
Successful process simulation relies on physical property data and thermodynamic
models to describe process units.

At the start of this project, the State of Technology (SOT) in bioseparation process
modeling was described as follows:
 1. Limited single component data measured and fitted to simplified thermodynamic
 models for single component systems,
 2. Simplified thermodynamic models incapable of reliably predicting the targeted
 bio-separation phenomena for multicomponent systems, and
 3. Unreliable process models incapable of accurate predictions to support process
 scale-up.

This project was intended to make the following advances:
 1. Generate essential data for single, binary, and multicomponent component
 aqueous organic acid systems for adsorption, capacitive deionization, and
 membrane separations to support development of rigorous thermodynamic
 models,
 2. Develop rigorous thermodynamic models for single, binary, and multicomponent
 aqueous organic acid systems for adsorption, capacitive deionization, and
 membrane separations,
 3. Demonstrate reliable process models of separations for bio-processes with
 proven value for scale-up of targeted bio-separations
 4. Illustrate best practices in experimental measurement of physical properties and
 model development for bioprocessing separations.

The Department of Energy’s Alternative Fuels and Feedstock Office’s Bioprocessing
Separations Consortium R&D portfolio includes adsorption, electrochemical, and
membrane technologies for the separation and recovery of aqueous and vapor phase
bio-products. The remainder of this section provides brief descriptions of these
separation technologies.

Adsorption
Bioprocesses provide a sustainable pathway to produce chemicals with lesser carbon
footprints and reliance on conventional fossil fuels and fuels [1]. Fermentation is an
established process to produce organic acids such as acetic acid, butyric acid, and lactic
 Page 8 of 81

acid worth over US $17 billion in 2024 [2] [3] [4]. However, these organic acids are highly
soluble and exist in dilute concentrations in aqueous solutions, posing a separation
challenge. Adsorption, a process in which certain species selectively and temporarily
adhere to the surface of a material known as an adsorbent, is one strategy for separating
organic acids from their dilute solutions. Adsorption processes allow for the selective
recovery of these acids from dilute aqueous solutions across a broad range of solution
pH values, temperatures, and concentrations [5], without the need for toxic chemicals or
excessive energy consumption [6]. With little to no literature experimental data available,
it is imperative to conduct experiments on adsorption equilibrium, kinetics, and column
breakthrough. To provide a comprehensive picture on the adsorption processes, the data
must cover the single- and multicomponent adsorption at a wide range of initial pH,
concentrations, composition, and temperature. The collected data shall serve as a
baseline for the development of thermodynamic and process models, and subsequent
design of adsorption processes for organic acids in aqueous solutions [7].

To overcome the difficulty involved in conducting time-consuming experiments at a range
of conditions, rigorous first principles-based thermodynamic models for adsorption
equilibria facilitate accurate correlation and robust extrapolation or prediction of
multicomponent adsorption equilibria data. In fact, these thermodynamic models form the
scientific foundation of adsorption process simulation models. There are a few well-
established adsorption equilibria models reported in the literature for adsorption equilibria
[8]. For instance, the classical Langmuir and extended Langmuir isotherms are widely
used to represent monolayer adsorption isotherm data but are limited to only ideal
adsorption [9] [10]. The Sips and loading ratio correlation [6] [11] can capture the nonideal
adsorption behavior for single and multicomponent monolayer adsorption, respectively,
but the associated parameters lack physical meaning, which makes their predictions
questionable for multicomponent adsorption equilibria and rigorous process simulation
[10]. Ideal adsorption solution theory [12] [13] and overloading model [12] enable the
predictions of multicomponent adsorption from single components, but they do not
account for the nonideal adsorption due to adsorbent surface heterogeneity [14]. In short,
the existing models for single- and multicomponent adsorption equilibria either are limited
to ideal adsorption or are empirical in nature [8]. Therefore, the development of rigorous
thermodynamic models is essential that can accurately represent the single-component
adsorption isotherms and predict the multicomponent adsorption equilibria at a wide
range of initial pH, concentration, composition, and temperature while providing insights
into the underlying monolayer and multilayer adsorption phenomena. Such rigorous
thermodynamic models can ensure accurate process simulation and accelerate the
efforts to scale up the adsorption process for bioprocessing separation.

CDI
Electrochemical separation technologies, particularly capacitive deionization (CDI), offer
a promising platform for ion removal by applying an electrical potential across porous
electrodes [15] [16]. During charging, ions migrate toward oppositely charged electrodes
and are stored within the high surface area of the porous electrodes, forming electric
double layers [17] [18]. During discharge, the opposite occurs, and the collected ions can
be concentrated. CDI has been successfully applied in desalination, wastewater
treatment, and the recovery of inorganics, heavy metals, and nutrients [19] [20] [21].
However, the application of CDI for separating carboxylates like acetate and butyrate
remains limited. The voltage-controlled and modular nature of CDI enables tunable
separation performance through key operating conditions, such as applied voltage and
flowrate, positioning CDI as an energy-efficient approach for recovering short-chain
organic anions [15] [16].

Membrane
Membrane separation involves the use of porous materials to facilitate separation of
materials from their solutions. Nanofiltration (NF) is one type of membrane separation
and has emerged as a promising alternative for the recovery of low concentration and
highly soluble carboxylic acids from fermentation broth [22]. NF offers advantages like
low energy consumption, minimal chemical use, and no phase change. The separation
performance of NF membranes is strongly influenced by factors such as applied
pressure, solute composition and concentration, and solution pH, due to the
membrane’s amphoteric nature and the pH-dependent dissociation of organic acids [5].
A thorough understanding of the underlying transport phenomena requires
systematically designed experiments with a specific NF membrane to generate a
comprehensive permeate flux and solute rejection data set conducted over a wide
range of operating conditions including applied pressure, solute composition and
concentration, and feed solution pH.
To develop a mechanistic model for NF performance, several theoretical frameworks
have been reported in the literature. Solution-diffusion (SD)-based models provide a
simple and widely used approach, describing transport through sorption and diffusion
mechanisms [23] [24]. Derived from the Nernst-Planck equation, competing pore flow-
based approaches hypothesized steric, electrostatic, and dielectric effects [25] [26].
However, the requirement of numerous membrane-specific parameters greatly reduced
their practicality in industrial applications. Other frameworks like Maxwell-Stefan [27]
and Kedem-Katchalsky [28] models exist but have their own limitations. Overall, from
process simulation and design perspective, the desired modeling approach should
balance simplicity and predictive accuracy, particularly for multicomponent systems
involving concentration- and pH- dependent transport in nanofiltration membranes.
Aimed at developing a predictive nanofiltration modeling framework supported by an
extensive experimental dataset spanning a wide range of operating conditions, this
                                      Page 10 of 81

project introduces a comprehensive Solution-diffusion model that simultaneously
captures solute and solvent transports. The model further incorporates pH-dependent
speciation and solute adsorption mechanism to accurately describe NF membrane
separation for multicomponent systems.

Project Objectives
The objectives of this project were to develop a systematic approach to collect
component data and to develop and incorporate these data into advanced
thermodynamic models applicable for both single and multicomponent mixtures. These
advanced thermodynamic models were included in unit and process modeling for
targeted adsorption, CDI, and membrane separation. This project sought to
demonstrate that the models resulting from this work are suitable for extension to
multicomponent mixtures by validating model predictions using pilot-scale data and
demonstrating the value of more accurate and predictive models for the scale-up and
design of commercial processes for organic acids separation.

The data and modeling workflow designed for the project is illustrated in Figure 1.
Laboratory scale experimental data was generated and used to determine
thermodynamic model parameters, inform machine learning efforts, and provide
transport model parameters for process modeling. Pilot scale experimental data was
used to validate the performance of the process models when scaled after development
using laboratory scale data. Due to a limitation on available federal funding, pilot-scale
data for CDI was removed from the scope but is of interest for future validation at larger
scales. All project team members met regularly to share progress and discuss
challenges. Figure 2 depicts a high-level Gantt chart for the project.

Task 1: Initial Verification
The Initial Verification task included demonstrating the data, capabilities, and resources
available and committed to this project to determine whether the background and
results to date proposed in the application were accurate:
BP1 Go/No-Go Decision Point: Demonstrate existing benchmark data, capabilities, and
resources for the proposed separation process in the proposal: 1) Demonstration of
experimental equipment and resources for adsorption/electrosorption and membrane
processes and 2) Demonstration of preliminary adsorption and membrane models

                                       Page 12 of 81

based on currently and publicly available data. Specific separation performance metrics
to be evaluated include adsorption capacity, membrane permeate flux, and membrane
solute rejection. As a metric for the quality of model predictions, the error of the fit of the
experimental data will be determined to highlight the gap between the current state-of-
the-art in physical property data and models and the project targets. Success will be
indicated by a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.9 or larger.

The strong-base anion-exchange resin Amberlite IRN-78 was chosen as the adsorption
baseline and NF90 membranes were selected as the baseline membrane. The
Langmuir isotherm model was highlighted as a suitable model to describe IRN-78 ion
exchange adsorption with an R2 value greater than 0.99 for acetic acid at initial pH 6.3
at 25 °C [5]. The solution-diffusion transport model was highlighted as a suitable model
to describe NF90 membrane separation [5]. Based on these demonstrated references
and capabilities, the project team successfully achieved the BP1 Go/No-Go milestone.

Pretreated activated carbon cloth electrodes (washed with 3M nitric acid) were used to
achieve enhanced capacity for separation, following a previous ANL study [16]. The
experimental setup is shown in Figure 11. A cycle time of 80 min, including a charging
step (voltage applied) and a discharging step (zero voltage), was chosen for all
experiments to ensure that the anode gets fully saturated to study the entire adsorption
and desorption profiles. Additionally, each experiment was continued for multiple cycles
to achieve dynamic equilibrium, defined as the condition in which the amount adsorbed
during charging equals the amount desorbed during the subsequent discharging step.

Model Framework
To perform rigorous thermodynamic modeling study for the single and mixed-acid
adsorption isotherm experiments taken in this project, the adsorption modeling team
developed the first rigorous thermodynamic model for multicomponent multilayer
adsorption equilibria. To support process simulation and design of adsorptive separation
processes, the TTU team has been developing rigorous thermodynamic adsorption
isotherm models for mixed-gas adsorption equilibria during the recent years. By
substituting adsorbate and vacant site concentrations in the classical Langmuir isotherm
with adsorbate and vacant site activities, the team first presented a generalized
Langmuir (gL) isotherm for rigorous thermodynamic modeling of mixed-gas monolayer
adsorption equilibria [14]. The team showed gL is superior to the various empirical
Langmuirian models and Adsorbed Solution Theory in current practice [10]. The current
study further extended gL and presented a generalized Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (gBET)
isotherm for mixed-gas multilayer adsorption equilibria by treating the multilayer
condensation phenomenon as multicomponent phase equilibria at dew point conditions.
gBET is the first and only rigorous thermodynamic isotherm ever formulated for
multicomponent multilayer adsorption equilibria.

In the project, the team compared the performance of gBET for the single and mixed-
acid adsorption isotherms against three well-known adsorption thermodynamic models
in literature: Brunauer-Emmett-Teller isotherm (BET), overloading model, and ideal
dilute solution theory (IDST). The details of each model can be found from the literature
listed in publication session.

Adsorption on Amberlite IRN-78 resin involves only carboxylic acids molecular species.

Subtask 3.4: Membrane Process Models
Membrane process models were developed using both CHEMCAD and gPROMS. Both
implementations are described below.

CHEMCAD Membrane Model
The following sections describe the model development and results of the CHEMCAD
modeling approach.

Model Development
A membrane system model was developed and applied for a single acid mixture
separation scenario. The model was developed to simulate a steady state acid
separation membrane system, including lumped parameter steady state mass transport
phenomena relationships. The model was integrated in CHEMCAD comprising mass
balance equations of the feed/retentate flow and in the permeate flow.

The model, based on the Kedem-Katchalsky theory-model, includes the following
constitutive relationships, integrated in the mass balance equation:
 • Solvent (or permeate, water) flux, as a function of transmembrane pressure and
 osmotic pressure, which is, in turn, depending on concentration gradients,
 • Solute (or acid) flux, as a function of the acid concentration gradient between
 membrane wall concentration and bulk permeate flow concentration,
 • Film theory model applied to assess the membrane wall concentration, as a
 function of the feed flow bulk concentration and solute bulk concentration in the
 permeate side.

The solution can be found iteratively, given the non-linear nature of the relationships,
with an initial guessed value for the solvent (water) flux and with the relationships solved
iteratively.

The model was integrated in CHEMCAD, by using the Datamap environment. The E-
NRTL model, integrated in CHEMCAD, is currently available to assess the electrolytic
dissociation of an acid mixtures. For the project scenarios, the E-NRTL model includes
the acetic acid ion dissociation, while the dissociation for butyric acid and lactic acid
flows is not currently available in the model.

The CHEMCAD Datamap model was applied to identify the membrane separation of the
three acids (acetic acid, butyric acid and lactic acid) from the experimental tests (lab
scale tests) in single acid mixture flows.

Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
To meet the challenge of rigorously modeling the multicomponent multilayer adsorption
equilibrium experiments of aqueous carboxylic acids on the ion-exchange resin
Amberlite IRN-78, the project team carried out more than 2000 lab-scale and pilot scale
experiments to obtain data for adsorption equilibrium, kinetics, and column
breakthrough for aqueous solutions with single and mixed-carboxylic acids including
acetic acid, butyric acid, and lactic acid on Amberlite IRN-78. The effects of acid
concentrations, compositions, temperatures, and initial pH have been systematically
examined. The team further developed a generalized Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (gBET)
isotherm model for mixed-gas multilayer adsorption equilibria and then, by calculating
the carboxylic acids fugacities in the aqueous solutions, successfully applied the gBET
isotherm model to the multicomponent multilayer adsorption equilibrium problem of
aqueous carboxylic acids on the resin. Representing the first and only
thermodynamically rigorous formulation for accurate correlation and prediction of
multicomponent multilayer adsorption equilibria, the gBET model outperforms both the
Overloading model and the Ideal Dilute Solution Theory model in correlating and
predicting the adsorption equilibrium data measured in this project. The thermodynamic
model meets the goal that it consistently agrees with the experimental adsorption
equilibrium data (lab and pilot) with errors less than ±20%. Although not yet fully
validated fully with the pilot plant data due to software limitations with Aspen
Chromatography, the gBET model has been validated against the extensive lab-scale
data sets measured and compiled in this project for aqueous carboxylic acids and their
mixtures. The gBET model is recommended for future modeling efforts for
multicomponent multilayer adsorption equilibria commonly encountered in the
separation of biochemicals from fermentation broth.

As a side project to improve the understanding of aqueous phase adsorption, the team
further examined the recent efforts to develop adsorption equilibrium models for CO2
capture in moist air and for water-ethanol separation with hydrophobic adsorbents. The
adsorption of moisture often exhibits Type V isotherm behavior, and there are no viable
engineering adsorption thermodynamic models available in the literature for Type V
adsorption isotherms. For example, process engineers have to resort to highly
cumbersome and totally empirical “multiple-site and high-order” Langmuir isotherm
                                       Page 71 of 81

models for Type V water adsorption isotherms [34]. By accounting for both the weak
adsorbate-adsorbent van der Waals interactions and the strong lateral adsorbate-
adsorbate associations due to hydrogen bonding, the association-induced Langmuir
isotherm model represents the first successful engineering adsorption thermodynamic
model to correlate Type V adsorption isotherms. This association-induced Langmuir
isotherm is currently being extended for multicomponent adsorption equilibrium.

To systematically evaluate the separation of acetate and butyrate with CDI, a series of
experiments were conducted across a range of applied voltages and flowrates at a fixed
initial concentration of 10 mM and pH 6.2. High-resolution transient effluent conductivity
and pH were continuously monitored to capture transient state separation behavior over
multiple charging–discharging cycles until dynamic equilibrium was achieved. Results
indicate that higher applied voltages and lower flowrates enhance adsorption capacity
and overall separation performance. On the modeling side, a unified, physics-based
modeling framework was developed to simulate the CDI experiments. The model
combines pore-scale thermodynamic equilibria, described using the modified Donnan
approach, with ion transport governed by the Nernst–Planck equation. To accurately
represent electric double layer formation, a voltage-dependent capacitance formulation
was introduced, capturing the influence of applied voltage and ion properties on the
electric double layer capacitance. This integrated framework successfully predicts
transient effluent profiles and adsorption capacities across a range of applied voltages
while slightly underpredicts the effect of flowrates. Overall, the model provides
mechanistic insight into ion transport, electrosorption and desorption dynamics, electric
double layer, and capacitance behavior, bridging system-level performance with pore-
scale phenomena. At the time of report preparation, the team continues to improve
hydrodynamic descriptions to better capture the effect of flowrates. The CDI model will
also be extended for mixed-acid solutions.

For NF membrane separation, the team generated a comprehensive lab-scale and pilot-
scale dataset comprising of more than 650 data points using a NF90 membrane. The
study encompassed single acid, binary mixed-acid, and ternary mixed-acid solutions of
acetic acid, butyric acid, and lactic acid. The effects of acid concentrations,
compositions, applied pressures, and feed pH on solute rejection, permeate flux, and
permeate pH have been systematically examined. In addition, a rigorous form of the
solution-diffusion model has been implemented in both Python and Aspen Custom
Modeler to correlate and predict the membrane separation performance for both solute
transport and solvent transport. Highlights of the model include the introduction of
Poynting correction for pressure effect and the solution chemistry calculation in the
concentration polarization layer for the pH effect. At the time of report preparation, the
model has been successfully applied to correlate lab-scale data for solute rejection and

                                       Page 72 of 81

permeate pH at natural pH (~3) and alkaline pH (~10) and to predict membrane
performance at neutral pH (~6). The team is confident that the model captures all the
fundamental science of NF membrane separation for organic acids from fermentation
broth. The model validation work continues for lab-scale mixed-acid solutions data and
for pilot plant data.

The data and the models implemented as part of the project have been (or will be)
made available to the public as supplementary information of the publications.

Path Forward
At the time of project closure on December 31, 2025, the goals to generate single, binary,
and multicomponent experimental data to support development of rigorous
thermodynamic models for adsorption, capacitive deionization, and membrane
separation have been completed. On the modeling side, the goal to develop and validate
rigorous thermodynamic model for multicomponent systems for adsorption has been
completed while the goal to demonstrate reliable pilot plant models for adsorption has
only been partially fulfilled due to the lack of software support to incorporate the newly
developed gBET model and the pseudo-second-order kinetic model into Aspen
Chromatography process simulator. The goal to develop rigorous thermodynamic and
transport model for capacitive deionization has been largely achieved. However,
additional work remains to fine tune the model parameters for single acid systems and to
validate the model predictions against data for mixed-acid systems. The goal to develop
rigorous thermodynamic model for membrane separation is also largely achieved while
additional work remains to validate the model predictions against data for mixed-acid
systems and to fully validate the model predictions against pilot plant data. The team will
continue to pursue these goals.
Separately, the team would like to see technology transfer and commercialization of IPs
related to key technology breakthroughs developed in this project. These breakthroughs
include the gBET isotherm model for mixed-gas multilayer adsorption equilibria, the
association-induced Langmuir isotherm model for Type V adsorption isotherms, the
physics-based CDI process model, and the rigorous solution-diffusion model for reverse
osmosis membrane model. Target companies include software vendors, major oil & gas
and petrochemical companies, and major biochemical companies with significant assets
in adsorption and membrane separation technologies.
