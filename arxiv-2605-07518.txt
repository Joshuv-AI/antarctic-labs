Loop Composition in Quantum Algorithms
Stacey Jeffery, Manideep Mamindlapally, Alex Baudoin Nguetsa Tankeu

Abstract
The quantum circuit model essentially treats every quantum algorithm as a straight-line program . While this view is universal, recent work has shown that it is inconvenient for using different-length quantum subroutines in superposition. Using the quantum walk formalism of quantum algorithms, it is possible to model such branching behaviour, and get better composition in this setting. We apply the above branching composition to Grover’s algorithm, which gives a variable-time quantum search algorithm that is worse than previous work. The reason it is worse is because branching composition does not take into account another deviation from straight-line programs: looping. We show that by modifying branching composition to also include looping, we can get a complexity that matches previous work. This highlights the importance of properly modeling the program control flow when designing quantum algorithms.

Introduction

Any deterministic classical algorithm can be modeled as a
straight-line program
, which is a sequence of operations, applied one after the other, starting from some initial state, and ending in some final state, which should encode the program’s output. From this description, it is also clear that a (unitary) quantum circuit, which is the most commonly used model of quantum algorithms, is also a straight-line program, but one where we allow quantum operations, on quantum states.

While the quantum circuit model is universal, one drawback is that it hides basically all structure of the program, aside from its length. One example of where this can give rise to a concrete disadvantage is in subroutine composition. If one of the operations represented by a node in the graph in

One way to think of such a subroutine is as
N

\{U_{T_{i}}^{i}\dots U_{1}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
where we want to apply the

i
-th superposition branch.
The superposition structure is completely lost in the straight-line program picture. To use this subroutine, we need to express it as a single straight-line program, that calls
U_{T_{i}}^{i}\dots U_{1}^{i}
controlled on some particular register containing value
{\lvert}i\rangle
. Even in the quantum random access model, this represents a straight-line program of length
\max_{i}T_{i}
.

To get around this, we need to enhance our straight-line programs to allow them to express superposition branching behaviour, which is quite easy to do visually – see

(Informal)

Fix any quantum algorithm

This is the quantum analogue of what one takes for granted in classical composition of randomized programs: when a subroutine’s running time depends on some random variable, the complexity of a subroutine call scales with the average (expected) running time of the subroutine, not the maximum.

Variable-time quantum search.

In fact, the use of quantum subroutines where the precise running time depends on which branch of the superposition you are in had previously been studied in the context of quantum search. In standard
black-box search
, one is given an oracle
f:[N]\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, and wants to decide if there exists
i\in[N]
such that
f(i)=1
. The setting of
variable-time
(quantum) search models the case where this oracle is instantiated by a subroutine for
f
that takes time
T_{i}
on input
i
. Specifically, it is assumed that the subroutine is given via an operator that can apply any
U_{t}^{i}
controlled on
{\lvert}t\rangle{\lvert}i\rangle
in some auxiliary registers. We describe this more precisely in
Section

2.2
, but it is similar to the quantum random access model (see also
[
8
, Section 2.2]
). For simplicity, throughout this introduction, we will consider a promise version of variable-time search, where in the case that there exists
m\in[N]
such that
f(m)=1
, it is unique.

Naively, if we simply take Grover’s algorithm and instantiate the oracle with the given subroutine, the complexity of variable-time search is:

\textrm{Naive variable-time search}:\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}\left(\sqrt{N}\max_{i\in[N]}T_{i}\right).

(1)

However, in
[
2
]
, Ambainis showed how to accomplish this task in complexity:

\textrm{$\ell_{2}$-variable-time search}:\widetilde{O}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[N]}T_{i}^{2}}\right).

(2)

Throughout this work, we let
\widetilde{O}(g(N))=g(N)\cdot\text{poly}(\log g(N))
.

The expression in (
2
) is better than the naive expression in (
1
),
since
\sqrt{\sum_{i}T_{i}^{2}}=\sqrt{N\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}T_{i}^{2}}
, and
\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}T_{i}^{2}}
is the
\ell_{2}
-average of the running times, which is always at most the maximum, and can be arbitrarily smaller. An alternative algorithm achieving (
2
), with a matching lower bound, can be found in
[
1
]
.

Our contribution.

The results of
Theorem

1.1
apply to a much wider class of quantum algorithms than the variable-time search result, but is the variable-time search upper bound in (
2
) a special case of this theorem, where the outer algorithm is simply Grover’s optimal quantum algorithm for search
[
6
]
? In
Section

3
, we prove that it is not, by analyzing the complexity of variable-time search if we address it using
Theorem

1.1
on Grover’s algorithm. Our analysis, which is tight up to constants, gives an upper bound no better than the naive upper bound in (
1
).

This leads us to ask the question: Why does
Theorem

1.1
get a suboptimal result in the context of search? Is this because it’s suboptimal in general? We theorize that what is actually suboptimal is
treating Grover’s algorithm as a straight-line program
. Grover’s algorithm essentially loops through the same two reflections some sufficiently large number
O(\sqrt{N})
of times, so it is not very natural to view it as a line of this length, and in fact, unrolling it into such a line appears to make composition work worse, by not allowing interference to occur between different iterations of the loop.

To support this speculation, in
Section

5
, we show how to modify the program composition of
[
9
]
described in
Theorem

1.1
– which we call
straight-line composition
– to what we call
loop composition
, where we compose subroutines with an outer algorithm that is viewed, not as a straight line (with branching), but a kind of loop (also incorporating branching). In this way, we recover the variable-time search upper bound of (
2
), as well as several other known upper bounds
[
9
]
.

Our variable-time search upper bounds are not new, and not a contribution in and of themselves. We feel the contribution of loop composition is instead a conceptual one.
[
9
]
showed that if one always views quantum algorithms as straight-line programs, one fails to adequately capture their superposition branching behaviour, and shows how to capture this in the context of program composition. We add to this picture by showing that it is also sub-optimal to view all quantum programs as straight lines with superposition branching: one also sometimes needs to capture looping behaviour, and we show how to capture this within the same model as
[
9
]
.

2.1
Black-box search

The abstract problem
black-box search
models virtually any unstructured search problem. Its input is a
black box
or
oracle
,
f:[N]\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, by which we mean, we assume the ability to evaluate
f
on any input
i\in[N]
. For quantum algorithms, we assume this means we can implement the unitary:

U_{f}:=2\sum_{i\in[N]:f(i)=0}{\lvert}i\rangle{\langle}i\rvert-I:{\lvert}i\rangle\mapsto\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\lvert}i\rangle&\mbox{if }f(i)=0\\
-{\lvert}i\rangle&\mbox{if }f(i)=1.\end{array}\right.

Then our goal is, using calls to such a unitary, to decide if there exists
i\in[N]
such that
f(i)=1
. This is the
decision version
of black-box search, which we will consider in this paper. Alternatively we could consider the problem of
finding
a marked element if it exists, but it is not difficult to reduce one version of the problem to the other, so we lose little by restricting to the simpler decision version.

Variable-time quantum algorithms

Here we define variable-time algorithms following the notation of

Quantum algorithms.

A quantum algorithm is defined by a sequence of unitaries
U_{1},\dots,U_{\sf T}
acting on a space

H_{I}\otimes H_{A}\otimes H_{Z}=\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}i\rangle{\lvert}a\rangle{\lvert}z\rangle:i\in{\cal I},a\in{\cal A},z\in{\cal Z}\}

for some finite sets
{\cal I}
,
{\cal A}
, and
{\cal Z}
.

The space
H_{I}
is the
input
space, and we assume each unitary is controlled on this space, but does not change it, meaning that for each
t\in[{\sf T}]
, we can express

U_{t}=\sum_{i\in{\cal I}}{\lvert}i\rangle{\langle}i\rvert\otimes U_{t}^{i}

for some unitaries
U_{t}^{i}
acting on
H_{A}\otimes H_{Z}
.
The unitaries
U_{t}^{i}
are assumed to be taken from some set of “basic” unitaries – for example, 2-local gates from some finite gate set, or the set of all constant depth circuits in some fixed gate set – such that each unitary represents a single “step” of the algorithm.
Throughout this paper, we will always have
{\cal I}=[N]^{\prime}=\{0\}\cup[N]
.

The space
H_{A}
is the
answer space
, and throughout this paper, we will always have
{\cal A}=\{0,1\}
. We say the algorithm computes
f:{\cal I}\rightarrow{\cal A}
with bounded error
\varepsilon
if for all
i\in{\cal I}
, the probability of measuring
f(i)
in register
A
after running the algorithm on input
i
is at least
1-\varepsilon
:

\left\|(I_{I}\otimes{\lvert}f(i)\rangle{\langle}f(i)\rvert\otimes I_{Z})U_{\sf T}\dots U_{1}{\lvert}i,0,0\rangle\right\|^{2}=\left\|({\lvert}f(i)\rangle{\langle}f(i)\rvert\otimes I_{Z})U_{\sf T}^{i}\dots U_{1}^{i}{\lvert}0,0\rangle\right\|^{2}\geq 1-\varepsilon.

The space
H_{Z}
is the workspace of the algorithm, and it can be somewhat arbitrary.

Model.

As is standard in the setting of variable-time algorithms, we assume that we can implement the unitary
\sum_{i,t}{\lvert}i\rangle{\langle}i\rvert\otimes{\lvert}t\rangle{\langle}t\rvert\otimes U_{t}^{i}
in unit cost. This is not generally true in the gate model – except for extremely structured cases, it is not possible to implement such a unitary in
O(1)
local gates. However, this is reasonable in the quantum random access model, with some minimal structure on the
\{U_{t}^{i}\}_{i,t}
. A detailed discussion can be found in
[
8
, Section 2.2]
.

Variable-time quantum algorithms.

Informally, a
variable-time
quantum algorithm is a quantum algorithm where, at each step between
1
and
{\sf T}
, there is some probability of the algorithm terminating. For the algorithm to have the possibility of terminating at some intermediate step
t
, it is necessary to perform a measurement at this step, to get some result that would indicate that the answer has been found, and there is no need to run the remaining computation steps. This is in contrast to the standard paradigm, where we usually run the algorithm for the full
\sf T
steps before measuring the answer.

Thus, formally, a variable-time quantum algorithm is a quantum algorithm that is augmented with two-outcome measurements
\{\Pi_{\leq t}\}_{t=1}^{{\sf T}}
, where if we measure
\Pi_{\leq t}
after applying
U_{t}
, then we know the algorithm is “done”, and we can measure the answer right away. In order to ensure that these measurements don’t change the behaviour of the algorithm, we define them from subspaces

\{0\}=H_{0}\subseteq H_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq H_{\sf T}=H_{Z}

(3)

such that
U_{t}
leaves
H_{t-1}
invariant:

U_{t}\Pi_{\leq t-1}=\Pi_{\leq t-1},

where
\Pi_{\leq t-1}
is the orthogonal projector onto
H_{t-1}
.
Since future unitaries don’t change the “done” part of the state, the output behaviour of the algorithm is the same whether you perform the intermediate measurements or simply measure the answer register at the end – though of course, if you perform the intermediate measurements, you might halt after significantly less than
{\sf T}
steps.

We will assume that the measurements commute with the computational basis of
H_{Z}
, meaning that for each
t\in[{\sf T}]^{\prime}
, there exists a set
{\cal Z}_{\leq t}\subseteq{\cal Z}
such that

H_{t}=\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}z\rangle:z\in{\cal Z}_{\leq t}\}.

Then by (
3
), we must have

\emptyset={\cal Z}_{\leq 0}\subseteq{\cal Z}_{\leq 1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq{\cal Z}_{\leq{\sf T}}={\cal Z}.

Defining
{\cal Z}_{t}={\cal Z}_{\leq t}\setminus{\cal Z}_{\leq t-1}
, we get
\overline{H}_{t}:=H_{t}\cap H_{t-1}^{\bot}=\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}z\rangle:z\in{\cal Z}_{t}\}
. Finally, define
{\cal Z}_{>t}={\cal Z}\setminus{\cal Z}_{\leq t}
.

Example.

For clarity, we give an example of the form a variable-time algorithm might take. Imagine a quantum algorithm consisting of multiple blocks, where after each block you measure, and if your measurement is successful, you halt, and otherwise, you move on to the next block. An example of this is a quantum search algorithm where you don’t know the number of marked elements, so you first run Grover’s algorithm repeatedly using about
\sqrt{2},\sqrt{4},\sqrt{8},\dots,\sqrt{N}
iterations in successive rounds. After each round, you measure, and if the measurement result is a marked element, you halt, but otherwise, you continue with the next round. After the last round of
\sqrt{N}
iterations, if you have still not found a marked element, you conclude there is none.

More generally, suppose you have a quantum algorithm consisting of
B
blocks:

\underbrace{\tilde{U}_{N_{1}+\dots+N_{B}}\dots\tilde{U}_{N_{1}+\dots+N_{B-1}+1}}_{\mbox{Block $B$}},\dots,\underbrace{\tilde{U}_{N_{1}+N_{2}}\dots\tilde{U}_{N_{1}+1}}_{\mbox{Block 2}},\underbrace{\tilde{U}_{N_{1}}\dots\tilde{U}_{1}}_{\mbox{Block 1}}

where
{\sf T}=N_{1}+\dots+N_{B}
, and each
\tilde{U}_{t}
acts on a space

H_{I}\otimes H_{A}\otimes H_{Z^{\prime}}=\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}i\rangle{\lvert}a\rangle{\lvert}z\rangle:i\in{\cal I},a\in{\cal A},z\in{\cal Z}^{\prime}\},

and the idea is to run Block 1, perform some measurement
\{\Pi,I-\Pi\}
of
H_{Z^{\prime}}
, and then if the outcome is 1, measure
H_{A}
and output the resulting answer, and otherwise continue with Block 2, repeat the same measurement, etc. This is not a variable-time quantum algorithm as described above, because the measurement generally changes the outcome. We can make it into a variable-time quantum algorithm by adding two extra registers, to get

H_{Z}=H_{Z^{\prime}}\otimes H_{F}\otimes H_{C}=H_{Z^{\prime}}\otimes\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}0\rangle,{\lvert}1\rangle\}\otimes\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}k\rangle:k\in\{0,\dots,B\}\}.

Both additional registers are initialized to
{\lvert}0\rangle
. The register
F
is a flag register to indicate that the algorithm is done, and the register
C
is incremented at each block, until the algorithm is done, at which point it stops being incremented. That is, for each
t\in[{\sf T}]
, we will replace
\tilde{U}_{t}
as follows. If
t
is not equal to
N_{1}+\dots+N_{k}
for any
k\in[B]
, meaning
\tilde{U}_{t}
is not the last unitary in a block, then
U_{t}
will simply apply
\tilde{U}_{t}
to
IAZ^{\prime}
, controlled on
{\lvert}0\rangle
in the flag register
F
.
Otherwise, if
t=N_{1}+\dots+N_{k}
,
U_{t}
will consist of several sub-operations:

1.

Controlled on
{\lvert}0\rangle
in
F
, apply
\tilde{U}_{t}
to
IAZ^{\prime}
.

2.

Controlled on
{\lvert}k-1\rangle
in register
C
, coherently perform the measurement
\{\Pi,I-\Pi\}
, and xor the result into
F
.

3.

Controlled on
{\lvert}0\rangle
in
F
, increment the register
C
.

An important observation is that for any
t\in\{N_{1}+\dots+N_{k-1},\dots,N_{1}+\dots+N_{k}-1\}
for
k\in[B]
, after applying
U_{t}
, the state of the algorithm in the last two registers is in

\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}0\rangle_{F}{\lvert}k-1\rangle_{C}\}\oplus\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}1\rangle_{F}{\lvert}k^{\prime}\rangle_{C}:k^{\prime}<k-1\}.

That is, either the flag is 0, and the block counter register carries the number of completed blocks,
k-1
, or the flag is set to 1, meaning the algorithm is finished, and the block counter contains some
k^{\prime}<k-1
, indicating the last block to terminate before setting the flag (i.e. halting).

Define, for all
k\in[B]
, for all
t\in\{N_{1}+\dots+N_{k-1},\dots,N_{1}+\dots+N_{k}-1\}
,

H_{t}=\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}z\rangle_{Z^{\prime}}{\lvert}1\rangle_{F}{\lvert}k^{\prime}\rangle_{C}:z\in{\cal Z}^{\prime},k^{\prime}<k-1\}.

Finally, define:

H_{\sf T}=H_{N_{1}+\dots+N_{B}}=H_{Z}.

Then we have:

\{0\}=H_{0}=H_{1}=\dots=H_{N_{1}-1}\subset H_{N_{1}}=\dots=H_{N_{1}+N_{2}-1}\subset\\
\dots\subset H_{N_{1}+\dots+N_{B-1}}=\dots=H_{N_{1}+\dots+N_{B}-1}\subset H_{N_{1}+\dots+N_{B}}=H_{Z}.

(4)

To see that
U_{t}
leaves
H_{t-1}
invariant, note that
\tilde{U}_{t}
is never applied, since the flag is always set to
1
in
H_{t-1}
. Moreover, for
t=N_{1}+\dots+N_{k}
, any state in
H_{t-1}
is supported on values
k^{\prime}<k-1
in register
C
, so the coherent measurement that sets the flag is not performed (i.e. sub-operation 2 does nothing) and thus the flag remains set at sub-operation 3, which also then does nothing.

Thus, this is a variable-time algorithm, and it is also easy to check that it has the same input-output behaviour as the original algorithm.

2.3
Straight-line subroutine composition

Imagine a randomized algorithm that computes a function
g
on input
x
by making calls to an oracle that, on input
i
, computes
f_{x}(i)
.
Throughout most of this paper, we leave the input
x
implicit, but in this section, we want to make it explicitly clear that
x
is the input to the algorithm, and
i
is generally an input to the oracle/subroutine. The subroutine’s behaviour can depend on both of these, but because of their different statuses, we put
x
in the subscript, and
i
in the parentheses.

Having fixed the oracle’s behaviour, we can analyze the algorithm assuming the oracle behaves as expected, but if we ever want to actually run the algorithm, we would need to instantiate the oracle by a subroutine. Suppose we have a subroutine that implements the oracle (let us assume it does so perfectly) in
T_{i,x}
steps on inputs
i
and
x
, where
T_{i,x}
is a random variable. Let
Q_{i,x}
be the random variable representing the number of times the algorithm, with input
x
, queries the subroutine on input
i
, and assume this is independent of
T_{i,x}
. Then if
L_{x}
is the number of operations made by the algorithm outside of subroutine calls, the expected running time of the algorithm on input
x
can be computed as:

\sum_{i}\E[Q_{i,x}]\E[T_{i,x}]+\E[L_{x}].

(5)

Let us phrase this same statement in a slightly different way. Suppose there are
{\sf Q}
steps at which the randomized algorithm has a nonzero probability of making a subroutine call
1
1

1

We assume that this is finite – this need not be the case, even when the expected running time is finite, but we can always truncate the algorithm to run in time at most its expected running time up to a constant, introducing only bounded error. This follows from Markov’s inequality, and is standard.
. For any
i
and any

, let

\sum_{i}\sum_{t=1}^{\sf Q}p_{i,t}(x)\E[T_{i,x}]+\E[L_{x}]={\sf Q}\sum_{i}\bar{p}_{i}(x)\E[T_{i,x}]+\E[L],

where
\bar{p}_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{\sf Q}\sum_{t=1}^{\sf Q}p_{i,t}(x)
is the average probability of querying the subroutine on input

(averaged over all steps at which subroutines are called with non-zero probability).

Something similar happens in a

U_{f}=\sum_{i\in[N]}{\lvert}i\rangle{\langle}i\rvert_{I}\otimes U_{f}^{(i)}+{\lvert}0\rangle{\langle}0\rvert_{I}\otimes I

]
. Consider a quantum algorithm that makes
{\sf Q}

, and
{\sf L}
additional basic operations. Let
{\lvert}\psi_{t}(x)\rangle
be the state of the algorithm just before the

\Pi_{i}
be the projector onto

. Define:

(see
Section

2.1
) we are given a variable-time subroutine implementing
U_{f}
, as described in
Section

2.2
, then it is not difficult to see that by running Grover’s algorithm on this instantiation of the oracle, we can solve the search problem in complexity
\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}(\sqrt{N}\max_{i\in[N]}T_{i})
. In this section, we investigate the complexity of an algorithm derived from instead composing Grover’s algorithm with the variable-time subroutine using
Theorem

2.1
.

For completeness, we start by describing and analysing Grover’s algorithm in
Section

3.1
. Then we apply
Theorem

2.1
to Grover’s algorithm in
Section

3.2
. For simplicity, we restrict our attention to the setting of a unique marked vertex, as this is already enough to illustrate our point.

3.1
Grover’s algorithm

Grover’s search algorithm aims to decide the existence of a marked item among
[N]
, where the property of being “marked” is determined by an oracle
f:[N]\rightarrow\{0,1\}
.

The initial state is the uniform superposition over
[N]
:

{\lvert}\pi\rangle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\in[N]}{\lvert}i\rangle,

which can be generated by applying a Hadamard gate to each of
n=\log N
fresh qubits
{\lvert}0\rangle
.
The algorithm then consists of alternating two reflections:
the oracle
U_{f}
, which is the input to the black-box search problem; and the diffusion operator
U_{\pi}
. The first is defined in
Section

2.1
as:

\displaystyle U_{f}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i\in[N]:f(i)=0}{\lvert}i\rangle{\langle}i\rvert-I

and we define the second:

\displaystyle U_{\pi}

\displaystyle=2{\lvert}\pi\rangle{\langle}\pi\rvert-I=H^{\otimes n}\big(2{\lvert}0^{n}\rangle{\langle}0^{n}\rvert-I\big)H^{\otimes n}.

We recall that

U_{f}{\lvert}i\rangle=\begin{cases}\phantom{-}{\lvert}i\rangle,\hskip 5.69046pt\text{if }f(i)=0\\
-{\lvert}i\rangle,\hskip 5.69046pt\text{if }f(i)=1\end{cases}

(6)

and the action of
U_{\pi}
can be deduced:

\displaystyle U_{\pi}{\lvert}\pi\rangle

\displaystyle={\lvert}\pi\rangle

\displaystyle U_{\pi}{\lvert}\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=-{\lvert}\psi\rangle\text{ if }{{\langle}\pi|}\psi\rangle=0.

The algorithm will apply
U_{\pi}U_{f}
some number
\sf T
of times, to get the state
(U_{\pi}U_{f})^{\sf T}{\lvert}\pi\rangle
, and then measure to get some
i\in[N]
. If
f(i)=1
, it will output
1
(there exists a marked element) and otherwise it will output
0
. If
\sf T
is chosen appropriately, this will give a one-sided error algorithm for black-box search.

Case 0: No marked item.

We first make a somewhat simple observation. If there is no marked element, then
U_{f}=I
, and so every application of
U_{\pi}U_{f}
acts on
{\lvert}\pi\rangle
as the identity. Thus, for any choice of
\sf T
,
(U_{\pi}U_{f})^{\sf T}{\lvert}\pi\rangle={\lvert}\pi\rangle
, and so upon measuring this state, we will get a uniform random
i\in[N]
, and of course, we will have
f(i)=0
, since no
i
is marked, and so we will output 0.

Case 1: Marked item.

Suppose there is a unique
m\in[N]
such that
f(m)=1
. We will argue that applying the rotation
U_{\pi}U_{f}
moves the state towards a good state defined as:

{\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle={\lvert}m\rangle.

(7)

We can define the state corresponding to the superposition of all the unmarked items as:

{\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle=\sum_{i\neq m}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N-1}}{\lvert}i\rangle.

(8)

Let
a
be the unique angle in
(0,\pi/2)
such that
\sin a=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
. Then
\cos a=\sqrt{\frac{N-1}{N}}
, and so

\displaystyle{\lvert}\pi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sin{a}{\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle+\cos{a}{\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle.

(9)

That is,
a
is the angle between
{\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle
and the initial state
{\lvert}\pi\rangle
. Then we have the following.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any angle
\theta\in\big[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\big]
, define a corresponding state
{\lvert}\varphi_{\theta}\rangle=\sin{\theta}{\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle+\cos{\theta}{\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle
. Then
{\lvert}\varphi_{a}\rangle={\lvert}\pi\rangle
, and for any
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
,
U_{\pi}U_{f}{\lvert}\varphi_{\theta}\rangle={\lvert}\varphi_{\theta+2a}\rangle
.

Proof.

The first claim,
{\lvert}\varphi_{a}\rangle={\lvert}\pi\rangle
, is clear from (
9
).
For the remainder of the claim, we will prove that
U_{f}{\lvert}\varphi_{\theta}\rangle={\lvert}\varphi_{-\theta}\rangle
(Claim 1) and
U_{\pi}{\lvert}\varphi_{\theta}\rangle={\lvert}\varphi_{2a-\theta}\rangle
(Claim 2), from which the result follows.

Claim 1:

For any
i\in[N]
,
U_{f}{\lvert}i\rangle=(-1)^{f(i)}{\lvert}i\rangle
. We compute:

\displaystyle U_{f}{\lvert}\varphi_{\theta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sin{\theta}\hskip 5.69046ptU_{f}{\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle+\cos{\theta}\hskip 5.69046ptU_{f}{\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sin{\theta}\hskip 5.69046ptU_{f}{\lvert}m\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N-1}}\cos{\theta}\hskip 5.69046pt\sum_{i\neq m}U_{f}{\lvert}i\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sin{\theta}\hskip 5.69046pt{\lvert}m\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N-1}}\cos{\theta}\hskip 5.69046pt\sum_{i\neq m}{\lvert}i\rangle

\displaystyle=\sin{(-\theta)}\hskip 5.69046pt{\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle+\cos{(-\theta)}{\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle={\lvert}\varphi_{-\theta}\rangle,

where we used
-\sin(\theta)=\sin(-\theta)
and
\cos(\theta)=\cos(-\theta)
for all
\theta
.

Claim 2:

For the second claim, we recall that
U_{\pi}=2{\lvert}\pi\rangle{\langle}\pi\rvert-I
, from which we can compute:

\displaystyle U_{\pi}{\lvert}\varphi_{\theta}\rangle

\displaystyle=(2{\lvert}\pi\rangle{\langle}\pi\rvert-I)(\sin{\theta}{\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle+\cos{\theta}{\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\sin{\theta}(2{\lvert}\pi\rangle{\langle}\pi\rvert-I){\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle+\cos{\theta}(2{\lvert}\pi\rangle{\langle}\pi\rvert-I){\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sin{\theta}(2{\lvert}\pi\rangle{{\langle}\pi|}\mathrm{good}\rangle-{\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle)+\cos{\theta}(2{\lvert}\pi\rangle{{\langle}\pi|}\mathrm{bad}\rangle-{\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\sin{\theta}(2\sin{a}{\lvert}\pi\rangle-{\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle)+\cos{\theta}(2\cos{a}{\lvert}\pi\rangle-{\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}2\sin{\theta}\sin{a}(\sin{a}{\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle+\cos{a}{\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle)-\sin{\theta}{\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle\\
+2\cos{\theta}\cos{a}(\sin{a}{\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle+\cos{a}{\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle)-\cos{\theta}{\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}(2\sin{\theta}\sin^{2}{a}+2\cos{\theta}\cos{a}\sin{a}-\sin{\theta}){\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle+(2\cos{\theta}\cos^{2}{a}\\
+2\sin{\theta}\cos{a}\sin{a}-\cos{\theta}){\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=(-\sin{\theta}\cos{2a}+\cos{\theta}\sin{2a}){\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle+(\sin{\theta}\sin{2a}+\cos{\theta}\cos{2a}){\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sin{(2a-\theta)}{\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle+\cos{(2a-\theta)}{\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle={\lvert}\varphi_{2a-\theta}\rangle.

Combining these two calculations, we can deduce the following:

U_{\pi}U_{f}{\lvert}\varphi_{\theta}\rangle=U_{\pi}{\lvert}\varphi_{-\theta}\rangle={\lvert}\varphi_{2a-(-\theta)}\rangle={\lvert}\varphi_{\theta+2a}\rangle.\qed

We have all the tools to compute an appropriate value for
{\sf T}
.
After
{\sf T}
iterations, we have:

\displaystyle(U_{\pi}U_{f})^{\sf T}{\lvert}\pi\rangle=(U_{\pi}U_{f})^{\sf T}{\lvert}\varphi_{a}\rangle

\displaystyle={\lvert}\varphi_{a+2{\sf T}a}\rangle={\lvert}\varphi_{(2{\sf T}+1)a}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sin{((2{\sf T}+1)a)}{\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle+\cos{((2{\sf T}+1)a)}{\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle.

(10)

To minimize the distance from the
{\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle
state, its corresponding amplitude should be close to 1. In other words, we want
\sin{(2{\sf T}+1)a}\approx 1
which can be achieved by ensuring
(2{\sf T}+1)a\approx\frac{\pi}{2}
.
As
\sin{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
, for large
N
,
a\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
. Thus, we take
{\sf T}=\lfloor\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}\rceil
.
By the Maclaurin series expansion of
\sin a=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}=\sin a\leq

\displaystyle a\leq\sin a+\frac{a^{3}}{3!}={\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}}+o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{2{\sf T}+1}{\sqrt{N}}\leq

\displaystyle(2{\sf T}+1)a\leq\frac{2{\sf T}+1}{\sqrt{N}}+o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{2(\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}-\frac{1}{2})+1}{\sqrt{N}}\leq

\displaystyle(2{\sf T}+1)a\leq\frac{2(\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}+\frac{1}{2})+1}{\sqrt{N}}+o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{N}}\leq

\displaystyle(2{\sf T}+1)a\leq\frac{\frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{N}+2}{\sqrt{N}}+o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}\leq

\displaystyle(2{\sf T}+1)a\leq\frac{\pi}{2}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right).

Then we have:

\displaystyle|{\langle}m\rvert(U_{\pi}U_{f})^{\sf T}{\lvert}\pi\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sin^{2}((2{\sf T}+1)a)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-(2{\sf T}+1)a\right)\geq 1-\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-(2{\sf T}+1)a\right)^{2}\geq 1-O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

Thus, with this choice of
{\sf T}
, we will measure
m
, and therefore output 1, with probability very close to 1.

3.2
Grover’s algorithm with composition

We now apply
Theorem

2.1
to Grover’s algorithm, and a variable-time subroutine instantiating the oracle
U_{f}:{\lvert}i\rangle\mapsto(-1)^{f(i)}{\lvert}i\rangle
. To get an upper bound on the resulting quantum algorithm for variable-time search, we need to upper bound three quantities. First,
{\sf Q}
is the number of queries made by the outer algorithm, so in the case of Grover’s algorithm, we have
{\sf Q}=O(\sqrt{N})
. Second,
{\sf L}
is the number of basic operations outside of queries. In the case of Grover’s algorithm, we need to count the
O(\sqrt{N})
calls to the non-query reflection,
U_{\pi}
, each of which can be implemented in
O(\log N)
basic operations, so
{\sf L}=O(\sqrt{N}\log N)
. Finally, and most complicated, we need to upper bound
{\sf T}_{\mathrm{avg}}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider Grover’s algorithm, where the input contains a single marked element
m
, as described in
Section

3.1
, with the oracle instantiated by a variable-time quantum subroutine. Then
\sum_{i\in[N]}\bar{q}_{i}\E[T_{i}]=\Theta\left(\sum_{i\in[N]\setminus\{m\}}\frac{1}{N-1}\E[T_{i}]+\E[T_{m}]\right)
, where
\E[T_{i}]
is the expected complexity of the subroutine on input
i
, and
\bar{q}_{i}
is the average query weight, defined in
Section

2.3
.

It follows that if we want a value
{\sf T}_{\mathrm{avg}}
that upper bounds
\sum_{i}\bar{q}_{i}\E[T_{i}]
for
all

m\in[N]
, it is necessary and sufficient to take

{\sf T}_{\mathrm{avg}}=\Theta\left(\sum_{i\in[N]}\frac{1}{N}\E[T_{i}]+\max_{m\in[N]}\E[T_{m}]\right)=\Theta\left(\max_{i\in[N]}\E[T_{i}]\right).

Note that we could not get an asymptotically better bound here, since the upper bound
{\sf T}_{\mathrm{avg}}
must hold for
any
input; the marked vertex could be anything. We discuss this further in
Section

4
.
Then by
Theorem

2.1
, we get an upper bound of

\widetilde{O}({\sf Q}\cdot{\sf T}_{\mathrm{avg}}+{\sf L})=\widetilde{O}\left(\sqrt{N}\max_{i\in[N]}\E[T_{i}]\right)

(11)

on variable-time search, which is no better than the naive upper bound of (
1
).

Proof of
Section

3.2
.

Fix any oracle
f
such that there is a unique marked
m\in[N]
.
As in
Section

2.3
, for any
t\in[1,\dots,\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}]
, let
{\lvert}\psi_{t}\rangle
be the state right before the
t^{\mathrm{th}}
call to
U_{f}
in Grover’s algorithm (which depends on
f
). Then:

\displaystyle{\lvert}\psi_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=(U_{\pi}U_{f})^{t-1}{\lvert}\pi\rangle={\lvert}\varphi_{a+2(t-1)a}\rangle={\lvert}\varphi_{(2t-1)a}\rangle=\sin((2t-1)a){\lvert}\mathrm{good}\rangle+\cos((2t-1)a){\lvert}\mathrm{bad}\rangle

by
Section

3.1
. Thus, for any
i\in[N]
:

\displaystyle q_{i,t}

\displaystyle=\left\|\Pi_{i}{\lvert}\psi_{t}\rangle\right\|^{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sin^{2}((2t-1)a)|{{\langle}i|}\mathrm{good}\rangle|^{2}&\mbox{if }f(i)=1\\
\cos^{2}((2t-1)a)|{{\langle}i|}\mathrm{bad}\rangle|^{2}&\mbox{if }f(i)=0.\end{array}\right.

Thus, if
i=m
is the unique marked element, then:

q_{m,t}=\sin^{2}((2t-1)a),

and if
i\in[N]\setminus\{m\}
,

q_{i,t}=\frac{\cos^{2}((2t-1)a)}{\sqrt{N-1}},

by (
8
). Thus, we can compute the average query weight, first, for
i=m
:

\displaystyle\bar{q}_{m}=\frac{1}{\sf Q}\sum_{t=1}^{\sf Q}q_{m,t}=\frac{4}{\pi\sqrt{N}}\sum_{t=1}^{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}}\sin^{2}((2t-1)a)

and then for
i\in[N]\setminus\{m\}
:

\displaystyle\bar{q}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sf Q}\sum_{t=1}^{\sf Q}q_{i,t}=\frac{4}{\pi\sqrt{N}}\sum_{t=1}^{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}}\frac{\cos^{2}((2t-1)a)}{N-1}.

Then we can upper bound the average complexity of a subroutine call, as follows:

\displaystyle L_{f}:=\sum_{i\in[N]}\bar{q}_{i}\E[T_{i}]

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\pi\sqrt{N}}\left(\sum_{i\in[N]:i\neq m}\sum_{t=0}^{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}-1}\frac{\cos^{2}{[(2t+1)a]}}{N-1}\E[T_{i}]+\sum_{t=0}^{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}-1}\sin^{2}{[(2t+1)a]}\E[T_{m}]\right).

Since
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\cos^{2}{x}=\frac{1+\cos{2x}}{2}
and
\sin^{2}{x}=\frac{1-\cos{2x}}{2}
, we can continue:

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}L_{f}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[N]:i\neq m}\frac{\E[T_{i}]}{N-1}\sum_{t=0}^{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}-1}\frac{1+\cos{[(4t+2)a]}}{2}+\E[T_{m}]\sum_{t=0}^{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}-1}\frac{1-\cos{[(4t+2)a]}}{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[N]:i\neq m}\frac{\E[T_{i}]}{2(N-1)}\Bigg[\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}+\sum_{t=0}^{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}-1}\cos{[(4t+2)a]}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\E[T_{m}]}{2}\Bigg[\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}-\sum_{t=0}^{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}-1}\cos{[(4t+2)a]}\Bigg]

from which we have:

\displaystyle L_{f}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[N]:i\neq m}\frac{\E[T_{i}]}{2(N-1)}+\frac{\E[T_{m}]}{2}+\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt{N}}\Bigg[\sum_{i\in[N]:i\neq m}\frac{\E[T_{i}]}{N-1}-{\E[T_{m}]}\Bigg]\sum_{t=0}^{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}-1}\cos{[(4t+2)a]}.

(12)

Using the identity
\cos(x+y)=\cos x\cos y-\sin x\sin y
, we can compute:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}-1}\cos{[(4t+2)a]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}-1}\big(\cos{4ta}\cos{2a}-\sin{4ta}\sin{2a}\big)

\displaystyle=\cos{2a}\sum_{t=0}^{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}-1}\cos{4ta}-\sin{2a}\sum_{t=0}^{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}-1}\sin{4ta}.

From here, we can use Lagrange’s trigonometric identities, which state that for
\theta\neq 0\mod 2\pi
:

\sum_{k=0}^{T}\sin{k\theta}=\frac{\cos{\frac{1}{2}\theta}-\cos{\big((T+\frac{1}{2})\theta\big)}}{2\sin{\frac{1}{2}\theta}},\mbox{ and }\sum_{k=0}^{T}\cos{k\theta}=\frac{\sin{\frac{1}{2}\theta}+\sin{\big((T+\frac{1}{2})\theta\big)}}{2\sin{\frac{1}{2}\theta}}

to get:

\begin{split}&\sum_{t=0}^{\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}-1}\cos{[(4t+2)a]}\\
={}&\frac{\cos(2a)\left(\sin(2a)+\sin\left(\left({\pi}\sqrt{N}+2\right)a\right)\right)-\sin({2a})\left(\cos({2a})-\cos\left(\left({\pi}\sqrt{N}+2\right)a\right)\right)}{2\sin({2a})}\\
={}&\frac{\cos(2a)\sin\left(\left({\pi}\sqrt{N}+2\right)a\right)+\sin({2a})\cos\left(\left({\pi}\sqrt{N}+2\right)a\right)}{2\sin({2a})}\\
={}&\frac{\sin\left((\pi\sqrt{N}+4)a\right)}{2\sin({2a})}=\frac{\sin\left(\pi-(\pi\sqrt{N}+4)a\right)}{2\sin({2a})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\pi-\pi\sqrt{N}a-4a|}{4a/\pi}\\
&=\frac{\pi}{8}|\pi/a-\pi\sqrt{N}-4|\end{split}

where we used
\sin(x+y)=\cos x\sin y+\sin x\cos y
, followed by
\sin x=\sin(\pi-x)
, and
\sin x\leq|x|
and
\sin x\geq 2x/\pi
(whenever
x\in[0,\pi/2]
). Since

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}=\sin a\leq a\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\sin a=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}},

we can upper bound this by
O(\sqrt{N})
, which is sufficient for our purposes. Plugging this into (
12
) gives:

\displaystyle L_{f}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[N]:i\neq m}\frac{\E[T_{i}]}{2(N-1)}+\frac{\E[T_{m}]}{2}+\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt{N}}\Bigg[\sum_{i\in[N]:i\neq m}\frac{\E[T_{i}]}{N-1}-{\E[T_{m}]}\Bigg]O(\sqrt{N})

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(\sum_{i\in[N]\setminus\{m\}}\frac{1}{N-1}\E[T_{i}]+\E[T_{m}]\right),

as claimed.
∎

Comparison of Variable-Time Search Upper Bounds

In this section, we consider a more general setting for search. We no longer restrict to a single marked element. Instead, we let
P_{1}
be
any
subset of all
f:[N]\rightarrow\{0,1\}
such that there exists
i\in[N]
with
f(i)=1
, and we suppose the input
f
is promised to be the all-0s function, or a function from
P_{1}
.
Moreover, we let
\pi
be any distribution on
[N]
from which we can easily generate a coherent quantum sample, and we let
\varepsilon=\min_{f\in P_{1}}\sum_{i:f(i)=1}\pi(i)
.
In this more general setting, the naive upper bound on variable-time search becomes:

\textrm{Naive variable-time search}:\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\max_{i\in[N]}\E[T_{i}]\right),

(13)

and Ambainis’ upper bound
[

]
becomes:

\textrm{$\ell_{2}$-variable-time search}:\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi(i)\E[T_{i}^{2}]}\right)=\widetilde{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi(i)\E[T_{i}^{2}]}{\min_{f\in P_{1}}\sum_{i:f(i)=1}\pi(i)}}\right).

(14)

Ref.
[
9
]
gave two alternative upper bounds for variable-time search that are generally incomparable to (
14

\displaystyle\textrm{$\ell_{1}$-variable-time search}:\;

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i\in[N]}\pi(i)\E[T_{i}]}{\min_{f\in P_{1}}\sum_{i:f(i)=1}\frac{\pi(i)}{T_{i}}}}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle\textrm{$\ell_{0}$-variable-time search}:\;

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\min_{f\in P_{1}}\sum_{i:f(i)=1}\frac{\pi(i)}{\E[T_{i}^{2}]}}}\right).

(16)

and furthermore, we are promised that

for some fixed upper bound

. This is similar to the case we considered in the introduction, and

, except for the upper bound

5
Loop Composition

The straight-line composition,
Theorem

2.1
, applied to Grover’s search algorithm as done in
Section

3.2
treats Grover’s algorithm as a straight-line program, with branching in the subroutine calls. However, this ignores a key structural property of Grover’s algorithm: that every step is a repetition of a single Grover iterate. As we saw in
Section

4
, this results in a suboptimal bound.
In this section, we exploit this
loop
ing structure.
Rather than composing variable-time subroutines into a straight-line unrolling of Grover’s algorithm, we instead compose the subroutine into a more loop-like structure. We call this
loop composition
, and it achieves the following, which matches the upper bound of Ambainis from (
2
).

Theorem 5.1

(Informal)
.

Fix a variable-time quantum algorithm that computes
f:[N]\rightarrow\{0,1\}
using
T_{i}
basic operations on input
i\in[N]
, for random variable
T_{i}
. Then there is a quantum algorithm that decides if there exists
i\in[N]
such that
f(i)=1
with bounded error in complexity
\widetilde{\mathrm{O}}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[N]}\E[T_{i}^{2}]}\right)
.

For a formal version of this statement, see
Theorem

5.1
Phase estimation algorithms

Loosely speaking, phase estimation
[
10
]
is a quantum primitive that makes controlled calls to some unitary
U
, and, given as input some eigenstate of
U
, estimates the phase
\theta
of the corresponding eigenvalue
e^{i\theta}
.
In this section, we review a specific type of phase estimation algorithm, where the unitary is a product of two reflections, and we are only interested in distinguishing a zero phase from non-zero phases. An algorithm of this specific structure can be built from any straight-line quantum algorithm and variable-time subroutines to prove the straight-line composition result described in
Theorem

2.1
, and we will use a similar technique to prove our loop composition result.

We borrow the specific formulation of such a phase estimation algorithm from
[
8
]
and for the remainder of this paper, we let the phrase “phase estimation algorithm” refer to this specific formalism. Refer to
[
8
, Section 3.1]
for intuition about why such an algorithm works.

A phase estimation algorithm is defined by a set of parameters, which we formalize in the following definition.

Definition 5.2

(Parameters of a Phase Estimation Algorithm)
.

Fix a finite-dimensional complex inner product space
H
, a unit vector
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle\in H
, and sets of pairwise orthogonal vectors

\Psi^{\cal A},\Psi^{\cal B}\subset H
(a pair of vectors
{\lvert}\psi_{A}\rangle\in\Psi^{\cal A}
and
{\lvert}\psi_{B}\rangle\in\Psi^{\cal B}
need not be orthogonal). We further assume that
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle
is orthogonal to every vector in
\Psi^{\cal B}
. Let
\Pi_{\cal A}
be the orthogonal projector onto
{\cal A}=\mathrm{span}\left(\Psi^{\cal A}\right)
, and similarly for
\Pi_{\cal B}
.

These parameters define a product of reflections:

U_{\cal AB}=(2\Pi_{\cal A}-I)(2\Pi_{\cal B}-I)
.
The algorithm defined by
(H,{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle,\Psi^{\cal A},\Psi^{\cal B})
performs phase estimation of
U_{\cal AB}
on initial state
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle
, to sufficient precision that by measuring the phase register and checking if the output is 0, we can distinguish between a
negative case
and a
positive case
. For analyzing such an algorithm, it turns out to be sufficient to prove that in the positive case, there exists a
positive witness
, and in the negative case, there exists a
negative witness
– we define these below.

Definition 5.3

(Negative Witness)
.

A
negative witness
for
(H,{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle,\Psi^{\cal A},\Psi^{\cal B})
is a pair of vectors
{\lvert}w_{\cal A}\rangle,{\lvert}w_{\cal B}\rangle\in H
such that

{\lvert}w_{\mathcal{A}}\rangle\in\mathrm{span}\left(\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}\right)
,
{\lvert}w_{\mathcal{B}}\rangle\in\mathrm{span}\left(\Psi_{\mathcal{B}}\right)

and
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle={\lvert}w_{\cal A}\rangle+{\lvert}w_{\cal B}\rangle
.

Definition 5.4

(Positive Witness)
.

A
positive witness
for
(H,{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle,\Psi^{\cal A},\Psi^{\cal B})
is a vector
{\lvert}w\rangle\in H
such that
{{\langle}\psi_{0}|}w\rangle\neq 0
and

\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}{\lvert}w\rangle=\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}{\lvert}w\rangle=0
.

Then the following theorem states the existence of a general type of algorithm – what we are calling a phase estimation algorithm – that we will use to prove
Theorem

5.1
.

Theorem 5.5

(
[
8
]
)
.

Fix
(H,{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle,\Psi^{\cal A},\Psi^{\cal B})
as in
Section

5.1
.
Suppose we can generate the state
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle
in cost
{\sf S}
, and implement
U_{\cal AB}=(2\Pi_{\cal A}-I)(2\Pi_{\cal B}-I)
in cost
{\sf A}
.
Let
c_{+}\in[1,50]
be some constant, and let
{\cal C}_{-}\geq 1
be a positive real number (that may scale with some implicit input size).
Suppose we are guaranteed that exactly one of the following holds:

Positive Condition:

There is a positive witness
{\lvert}w_{+}\rangle
s.t.
\frac{|{{\langle}w_{+}|}\psi_{0}\rangle|^{2}}{\left\|{\lvert}w_{+}\rangle\right\|^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{c_{+}}
.

Negative Condition:

There is a negative witness
{\lvert}w_{\cal A}\rangle,{\lvert}w_{\cal B}\rangle
s.t.
\left\|{\lvert}w_{\cal A}\rangle\right\|^{2}\leq{\cal C}_{-}
.

Then there is a quantum algorithm that distinguishes these two cases with bounded error in cost

\mathrm{O}\left({\sf S}+\sqrt{{\cal C}_{-}}{\sf A}\right).

5.2
The loop structure, without composition

In this section, we present a phase estimation algorithm for black-box search, in the sense of
Section

5.1
and
Theorem

5.5
. This does no better than Grover’s algorithm, but its particular structure facilitates later replacement of the oracle query with a variable-time subroutine (in
Section

5.3
). The results of this section are not required for
Section

5.3
, but the reader may find the next section easier to follow after reading this section.

Recall from
Section

2.1
that in the problem of black-box search, the input is some
f:[N]\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, which we assume we can access via queries to a unitary
U_{f}
. To use such an algorithm in practice, this unitary would need to be instantiated by a subroutine, which we consider in the following section. For now, we will treat
U_{f}
as a black box, that can be implemented in unit cost.

Theorem 5.6
.

Fix
\mu\geq 1
. Let
f:[N]\to\left\{0,1\right\}
be accessible through an oracle
U_{f}
, and let
M_{f}:=\left\{i\in[N]:f(i)=1\right\}
be the set of marked elements.
There exists a quantum phase estimation algorithm that decides whether
M_{f}
is empty or
|M_{f}|\geq\mu
– i.e. that decides a promise version of black-box search – with bounded error, in complexity

\mathrm{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{\mu}}\log N\right).

In fact, it is well known how to solve black-box search by doing phase estimation of the Grover iterate –
U_{\pi}U_{f}
in the notation of
Section

3
– which is a product of two reflections. The difference here is that we construct a product of two reflections in a way that will be amenable to composition with a subroutine in
Section

5
.

Proof.

To design a phase estimation algorithm, we need to fix parameters as in
Section

5.1
, starting with a space

H=\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}d,i,b\rangle:d\in\{0,1,\circ,\bot\},i\in[N],b\in\{0,1\}\}.

Next we need a pair of subspaces
{\cal A}
and

of

\displaystyle\Psi_{\bullet}

\displaystyle=\left\{{\lvert}\circ,0,0\rangle-\sum_{i\in[N]}\sqrt{\tfrac{\omega}{N}}\,{\lvert}0,i,0\rangle\right\},

\displaystyle\Psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.7}{$\blacklozenge$}}}

\displaystyle\Psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.6}{$\blacksquare$}}}

\displaystyle=\left\{{\lvert}\bot,i,b\rangle-{\lvert}1,i,b\rangle:i\in[N],\,b\in\left\{0,1\right\}\right\},

\displaystyle=\left\{{\lvert}1,i,0\rangle:f(i)=0,\,i\in[N]\right\}.

\displaystyle\left\|{\lvert}w_{+}\rangle\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\left\|{\lvert}w_{\mathcal{A}}\rangle\right\|^{2}

Conclusion.

implies an algorithm that decides if
|M_{f}|=0
(negative case) or
|M_{f}|\geq\mu
(positive case) in complexity
\mathrm{O}\left({\sf S}+\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{-}}{\sf A}\right)=\mathrm{O}(\sqrt{N/\mu}\log N)
.

5.3
Loop composition, with subroutines

We next turn to the setting where the oracle is implemented by a quantum subroutine – specifically a variable-time subroutine, as described in
Section

2.2
. Recall that such an algorithm consists of a sequence of unitaries
U_{1},\dots,U_{\sf T}
, where we can imagine running the algorithm by applying
U_{1}

\{\Pi_{1},I-\Pi_{1}\}
to see if we’re done, then if we’re not yet done, apply
U_{2}
and then measure
\{\Pi_{2},I-\Pi_{2}\}

U_{\sf T}
(then we are definitely done). The number of steps we run the algorithm for on any input
i
is thus a random variable from
[{\sf T}]^{\prime}

T_{i}
. For simplicity, we will assume this subroutine computes
f
exactly, so with error
\varepsilon=0

.

Consider the problem of
variable-time search

1.

Costs of steps of phase estimation.

In order to be able to use
Theorem

5.5
we need to analyze the cost
{\sf S}
, of preparing our initial state
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle
, which is easily seen to be at most
O(\log N{\sf T})
; and the cost
\sf A
, of performing a single application of
U_{\cal AB}=(2\Pi_{\cal A}-I)(2\Pi_{\cal B}-I)
. The latter is also
O(\log(N{\sf T}))
, as we show in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.9
.

Each of
2\Pi_{\cal A}-I
and
2\Pi_{\cal B}-I
can be implemented in
O(\log(N{\sf T}))
cost.

Proof.

To derive the cost of performing a reflection around the spaces
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, it is enough to just look at the costs of reflections in the individual orthogonal subspaces.
If
\Pi_{\text{\textbullet}}
,
\Pi_{\text{\scalebox{0.6}{$\blacksquare$}}}
and
\Pi_{\mathrm{even}}
are the projectors onto the spans of
\Psi_{\text{\textbullet}}
,
\Psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.6}{$\blacksquare$}}}
and
\Psi_{\mathrm{even}}
respectively, the projector onto
\mathcal{A}
,
\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}
can be written as

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\text{\textbullet}}+\Pi_{\text{\scalebox{0.6}{$\blacksquare$}}}+\Pi_{\mathrm{even}}

which then allows us to write the reflection around
\mathcal{A}
as

\displaystyle 2\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}-I

\displaystyle=\left(2\Pi_{\text{\textbullet}}-I\right)\left(2\Pi_{\text{\scalebox{0.6}{$\blacksquare$}}}-I\right)\left(2\Pi_{\mathrm{even}}-I\right)

because the product of projectors onto orthogonal spaces is zero. To perform a reflection around
\mathcal{A}
therefore it is enough to perform individual reflections in the spans of
\Psi_{\text{\textbullet}}
,
\Psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.6}{$\blacksquare$}}}
and
\Psi_{\mathrm{even}}
sequentially.
Reflection around
\mathrm{span}\left(\Psi_{\text{\textbullet}}\right)
can be performed by implementing the unitary
G_{\text{\textbullet}}:{\lvert}\circ\rangle{\lvert}0,b\rangle{\lvert}a,z\rangle{\lvert}t\rangle\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\omega_{i}/N}}{\lvert}\circ\rangle\left({\lvert}0,b\rangle-\sum_{i\in[N]}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{i}}{N}}{\lvert}i,b\rangle\right){\lvert}a,z\rangle{\lvert}t\rangle
controlled on
{\lvert}\circ\rangle
in the first register,
and performing the reflection around the zero state in the second register. More precisely we perform

\displaystyle 2\Pi_{\text{\textbullet}}-I

\displaystyle=G_{\text{\textbullet}}(I\otimes\left(2|{0}\rangle\!\langle{0}|-I\right)\otimes I)G_{\text{\textbullet}}^{\dagger}.

This involves
O(\log N)
basic operations. Similarly the reflection around
\mathrm{span}\left(\Psi_{\text{\scalebox{0.6}{$\blacksquare$}}}\right)
can be performed by implementing unitary
G_{\text{\scalebox{0.6}{$\blacksquare$}}}:{\lvert}0,i,b,a,z,t\rangle\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}({\lvert}\perp\rangle-{\lvert}1\rangle){\lvert}i,b,a,z,t\rangle

and a reflection about the zero state in the first register. More precisely we do

\displaystyle 2\Pi_{\text{\scalebox{0.6}{$\blacksquare$}}}-I

\displaystyle=G_{\text{\scalebox{0.6}{$\blacksquare$}}}((2|{0}\rangle\!\langle{0}|-I)\otimes I)G_{\text{\scalebox{0.6}{$\blacksquare$}}}^{\dagger}.

This costs
O(1)
basic operations. The reflection around
\mathrm{span}\left(\Psi_{\mathrm{even}}\right)
can be implemented in
O(\log{\sf T})
cost using
[
9
, Lemma 3.3]
and our ability to perform
U_{t}
in unit cost for superposition over
t\in[{\sf T}]^{\prime}
. This results in a combined cost of
O(\log(N{\sf T}))
for the reflection around
\mathcal{A}
. A similar analysis holds for
\mathcal{B}
, where an additional control on sixth register being
t=0
for the reflection around
\mathrm{span}\left(\Psi_{\rightarrow}\right)
and
\mathrm{span}\left(\Psi_{\leftarrow}\right)
incurs an additive
O(\log{\sf T})
cost.
∎

(i a).

First, set
