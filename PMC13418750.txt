Diffraction-quality, ultraflexible protein single crystals engineered with DNA
Authors: Zhenyu Han, Chad A. Mirkin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
DNA-functionalized colloidal nanoparticles assemble through flexible, nanoscale DNA hybridization interactions that limit atomic-level structural order. Here, we report a valence-centric strategy that enables DNA-bonded, protein single crystals with unconventional mechanical properties. An octameric enzyme, glutarate L-2-hydroxylase, was site- and number-selectively conjugated with eight self-complementary single-stranded DNA, yielding octavalent molecular bonds. The resulting conjugate assembled into the designed body-centered tetragonal crystals that diffracted to 1.42- to 2.61-angstrom resolution, with contacts mediated by B-form DNA helices spanning 17 to 25 angstroms. Increasing oligonucleotide length induces anisotropic lattice expansion while preserving atomic periodicity, even with partial DNA occupancy. Mechanistic studies suggest that the dynamic motion of unhybridized DNA facilitates crystallization, analogous to fluctuating electron clouds in atomic bonding. Compared with native protein crystals, DNA-hybridized crystals are 23-fold softer. These results challenge the assumption that flexibility is incompatible with structural order and establish a programmable framework for biomolecular crystallization and nanomaterials engineering with atomic precision.

INTRODUCTION

The organization of matter has long been understood through the classical concept of chemical bonding, in which the sharing or transfer of valence electrons generates directional, short-range interactions that determine the structure of crystalline solids (1). Atomic crystals formed via ionic, covalent, or metallic bonds constitute the foundation of most functional materials, and molecular crystals and crystalline porous polymers exhibit tunable properties that depend on bond types (2, 3). For example, covalent crystalline frameworks offer thermal stability, whereas hydrogen-bonded crystals are stimuli responsive, enabling applications in separations, catalysis, sensing, drug delivery, and molecular encapsulation (4–6).

Over the past three decades, DNA, the genetic blueprint of life, has been repurposed as a molecular bond for constructing nanomaterials (7–11). Guided by highly specific and programmable Watson-Crick base pairing, DNA-grafted inorganic and polymeric nanoparticles have been assembled into an extensive library of colloidal crystals, with tunable bonding distances and strengths dictated by DNA length and sequence (12–19). However, DNA hybridization occurs over nanometer-scale distances and relies on flexible, noncovalent interactions that lack the rigidity and directionality characteristic of atomic bonds. While single crystals composed of DNA crossover motifs have been reported (8), hybrid DNA-nanoparticle superlattices directed by DNA interactions remain atomically disordered, precluding high-resolution structural characterization of the nanoparticle constituents and direct visualization of the DNA bond. Such structural precision is critical to bottom-up nanomaterials design for applications in electronics, photonics, biomedicine, sensing, and catalysis but, so far, has remained elusive (20).

Proteins are atomically precise nanoparticles that perform essential biological functions and have been used as building blocks for porous crystalline biomaterials (21–28). Traditionally, protein crystallization relies on trial-and-error screening, and the resulting crystals are stabilized by complex and heterogeneous interfaces involving multiple types and numbers of atomic bonding interactions (29). In contrast, diffraction-quality protein crystals assembled solely through highly predictable and programmable DNA-DNA interactions have long been envisioned for applications in structural biology and biomaterials science (30). However, these crystals have remained out of reach, leaving DNA-directed biomolecular crystallization an unsolved challenge for decades. Existing single crystals of protein-DNA conjugates (31, 32) and proteins noncovalently complexed with surface-bound DNA ligands (26) have been dominated by native protein-protein interactions rather than directed by DNA hybridization. In addition, protein superlattices can assemble solely through DNA hybridization by thermally annealing proteins densely modified with DNA shells (33, 34) or encapsulating them within DNA crystalline scaffolds (14, 35). However, these assemblies remain atomically disordered because of the surface heterogeneity of protein-DNA conjugates, and their structural order can be assessed only using low-resolution techniques, such as small-angle x-ray scattering.

Achieving DNA-directed biomolecular crystallization with atomic precision could transform protein assembly from an empirical process into a programmable platform (Table 1 and fig. S1). Such a strategy would require (i) overriding native protein-protein interactions with a sufficient number and surface coverage of DNA strands and (ii) minimizing kinetic traps and structural defects arising from nonuniform and heterogeneous DNA shells to enable the deliberate design of DNA-bonded crystals with angstrom precision. Here, we develop a valency-directed approach to modify protein nanoparticle surfaces with discrete DNA molecules in a number- and site-selective manner. As a proof of concept, an octameric, cube-shaped enzyme was surface modified with eight single strands of DNA, thereby creating an octavalent structure that enforces nearest-neighbor connectivity in the form of a designed body-centered lattice. The crystals were grown using vapor diffusion, and, despite the intrinsic flexibility of DNA, the resulting body-centered tetragonal crystals diffracted to 1.42- to 2.61-Å resolution under single-crystal x-ray diffraction (XRD). These experiments revealed structures with DNA-hybridized crystal contacts previously inaccessible using conventional structural preparation and characterization techniques (13, 34).

Mechanistic studies identify four design parameters critical for accessing these crystals: the chemical linkage between the protein surface and DNA, the selection of DNA attachment sites, the DNA sequence, and the bonding helicity. Contrary to conventional crystallization processes that rely on rigid atomic interactions, structural analyses show that the flexible DNA bonds exhibit alternative conformations, analogous to electron clouds in atomic systems, before hybridizing to direct lattice formation. Unexpectedly, atomic-resolution crystals formed even without full DNA occupancy at all attachment sites, revealing an error tolerance intrinsic to this valency-directed crystallization approach. Furthermore, intermolecular distances were programmable by DNA length and helicity, enabling tunable control over crystal packing and porosity. Compared with protein crystals stabilized by atomic bonds, DNA-bonded macromolecular crystals are mechanically soft and compliant. These results challenge the long-standing assumption that flexibility is incompatible with structural order and establish a general framework for designing atomically precise nanomaterials with noncanonical bonding architectures and programmable properties.

RESULTS

Design of DNA-bonded single crystals

To override complex protein-protein interactions with DNA-mediated ones, we developed an interface design strategy to selectively modify protein nanoparticles. Because protein monomers can naturally assemble into higher-order oligomers in solution via biological interfaces, the resulting quaternary structures were determined using a suite of structural and biophysical techniques (Fig. 1A). As a model system, the enzyme glutarate L-2-hydroxylase (GlaH) was selected, and its monomeric structure was identified through computational prediction and found to be consistent with experimentally determined structures (Fig. 1B) (36, 37). Size exclusion chromatography coupled with multiangle light scattering (SEC-MALS) showed that wild-type GlaH eluted as a single peak with a measured molar mass of 294 ± 1 kDa, corresponding to an octameric assembly (Fig. 1C). Interface analysis of the putative GlaH oligomers indicated that a tetrameric assembly has a total buried surface area (BSA) of 1654 Å2 per monomer, whereas an octameric assembly has a total BSA of 3242 Å2 per monomer (Fig. 1D and tables S1 and S2). The 1588-Å2 BSA difference at the D4 interface exceeds the 700- to 800-Å2 threshold typically associated with a true biological interface (29). Together, these results demonstrate that GlaH forms homo-octameric complexes in solution.

Distinct from the biological interfaces that stabilize solution-state oligomers, crystallographic interfaces dictate intermolecular contacts between protein oligomers during crystal formation. To evaluate the potential crystallographic interfaces of GlaH octamers by design, geometric and bonding analyses were performed using a simplified stick-and-ball model (Fig. 1E). In principle, functionalizing each GlaH monomer with a single DNA molecule would yield eight DNA bonds (approximated as “sticks”) per GlaH octamer. At the nanoscale, the GlaH octamer can be approximated as a spherical nanoparticle (i.e., “ball”). To maximize the number of DNA hybridization events, this octavalent nanoparticle can connect to eight nearest neighbors via eight DNA bonds, making a body-centered structure the optimal three-dimensional geometry. Alternatively, one- or two-dimensional assemblies with lower coordination numbers are also feasible.

To further evaluate the feasibility of the three-dimensional crystal design, atomic models of the protein-DNA conjugates were built incorporating four critical design parameters (Fig. 1F). First, the DNA attachment sites on surface-exposed residues define the orientation and directionality of the DNA bonds. Second, the optimal flexibility of the protein-DNA chemical linkages provides the mobility required for intermolecular DNA recognition, but not so much that excessive motion disrupts lattice formation and impedes crystallization. Third, the DNA sequence governs the specificity and strength of intermolecular interactions. Fourth, the intrinsic helicity of DNA duplexes, unlike the one-dimensional connectivity of conventional atomic bonds (as represented in the stick-and-ball model), must be accounted for in the lattice design.

The surface-exposed residues along the edges and corners of the GlaH octamer were examined, and putative sites located on loop regions amenable to DNA functionalization were identified and modeled to cysteine. To predict the crystal packing of the octavalent protein-DNA conjugates, single-stranded DNA was manually positioned near the conjugation sites; because cysteine side chains adopt two rotameric conformations, two protein-DNA conjugate models were constructed corresponding to each side-chain orientation (fig. S2A). The monomeric conjugate models were then symmetry-expanded to represent the biological assembly of the protein (fig. S2B), and the resulting octameric models were duplicated into nine copies and randomly positioned in space to approximate the unassembled state in solution. Last, the relative orientations and coordination of each conjugate were manually adjusted based on intermolecular hybridization into ideal B-form helices (fig. S2C). Structural designs based on these expanded models that exhibited neither steric clashes between proteins nor packing disorder were selected (Fig. 1G). The final candidates for experimental validation included oligomeric conjugates attached via Asn182, Gln184, or Lys270 residues.

DNA-directed biomolecular crystallization

The GlaHQ184C-DNA design was first experimentally investigated by mutating Gln184 to cysteine and conjugating the resulting GlaHQ184C mutant with maleimide-modified single-stranded DNA bearing a two-carbon linker between the maleimide and the 5′-phosphate (5′-Mal-C2) (Fig. 1, F and G; table S3 for DNA sequences; and fig. S3 for characterization of the protein-DNA conjugates). Crystallization of the GlaHQ184C–C2-AGCT self-complementary conjugate via vapor diffusion resulted in a 2.61-Å structure with I422 symmetry (Fig. 2A). Unexpectedly, omit electron density maps revealed B-form DNA helices with resolved nucleobases and deoxyribose moieties, while the phosphodiester backbone showed partial density (Fig. 2, B and C, and fig. S4). Notably, DNA hybridization links proteins diagonally within the unit cell, and a total of eight DNA bonds were observed per octamer (Fig. 2B; octamers 1, 2a, and 3). Similar crystal structures obtained from the same GlaHQ184C–C2-AGCT conjugate at 2.17- and 1.89-Å resolution exhibited progressively weaker electron density for DNA, consistent with the inherent flexibility of the DNA bond (fig. S5A). Despite this variability, proteins remained ordered within the lattice, and no additional protein-protein interactions were observed at the DNA-mediated interface. Furthermore, GlaHQ184C conjugated to 5′-Mal-C2–GTAC, a different self-complementary DNA sequence, crystallized into a near-identical structure with electron density observed for the DNA helices, confirming that DNA hybridization within crystals is not specific to the AGCT sequence (fig. S5B). As a control, unmodified GlaHQ184C did not crystallize under identical conditions. Similarly, GlaHQ184C-DNA conjugates bearing 5′-Mal-C2–GC, 5′-Mal-C2–GCGC, 5′-Mal-C2–TTTT, or 5′-Mal-C2–AAATTT sequences failed to yield crystals, indicating that orthogonal (i.e., producing a single intermolecular hybridization outcome), fully complementary sequences of at least four base pairs are required for DNA hybridization.

To determine whether base pairing alone drives the crystallization of GlaHQ184C–C2-AGCT and GlaHQ184C–C2-GTAC, the remaining crystal contacts were analyzed, and two additional intermolecular hydrogen bonds were identified: Asn182⋯Asn182 and Lys270⋯Ile272 (fig. S5C). Because these discrete interactions are absent in the wild-type GlaH crystals that exhibit highly conserved crystal interfaces (Supplementary Text), we hypothesized that they did not contribute to crystal formation but instead arose after DNA hybridization. To test this hypothesis, we mutated Asn182 to alanine to eliminate the hydrogen bond-forming side chains, and the mutant was conjugated with 5′-Mal-C2–AGCT. The resulting GlaHQ184C, N182A–C2-AGCT conjugate crystallized with the same packing arrangement as GlaHQ184C–C2-AGCT and showed clear electron density for the DNA helices but lacked the Asn182 hydrogen bonds (Fig. 2D and fig. S5D). To eliminate the Lys270⋯Ile272 interaction, we synthesized a GlaHQ184C, K270A–C2-AGCT conjugate, which also crystallized into a near-identical structure with resolved DNA helices (fig. S5E). In this case, the interface was stabilized by water molecules and glycerol (present in the crystallization buffer) bridging adjacent Asp195 residues (Fig. 2E). Last, both Asn182 and Lys270 were mutated to alanine to prepare GlaHQ184C, N182A, K270A–C2-AGCT, which formed faceted crystals (Fig. 2F). These crystals diffracted to 1.42-Å resolution, representing the highest-resolution GlaH structure obtained to date. The refined structure revealed hybridized DNA and confirmed the absence of both hydrogen bonds (fig. S5F). Together, these findings demonstrate that the observed atomic interactions do not drive crystallization and are merely consequences of DNA-imposed crystal packing.

Crystal packing driven by DNA helicity

With a longer DNA sequence, the GlaHQ184C–C2-AGGCCT crystal at 2.05-Å resolution exhibits a unit cell volume of 2,209,013 Å3, ∼28% larger than that of GlaHQ184C–C2-AGCT crystals (average volume: 1,720,218 Å3; Fig. 2G). This increase arises from a ∼15- to 16-Å expansion along the a and b axes, accompanied by a ∼6- to 8-Å contraction along the c axis. Although electron density was insufficient to model the DNA duplex due to increased flexibility, the intermolecular distance, measured between the Sγ atoms of Cys184, increased from 17.3 to 25.3 Å, consistent with the expected separation between 5′ phosphate atoms for a DNA duplex extended by two base pairs (Fig. 2H). Because the DNA helix is oriented nearly parallel to the ab plane in the GlaHQ184C–C2-AGCT crystals, the longer strand here likely pushes adjacent proteins farther apart in the lateral directions (a and b axes) while pulling them slightly closer along the c axis due to its helical pitch, resulting in anisotropic unit cell expansion (fig. S6). Interface analysis reveals no intermolecular bonding interactions (interatomic distances of <5 Å) between proteins, confirming that DNA hybridization alone likely mediates crystallization. These findings suggest that nanoscopic DNA bonds exhibit directionality determined by the helical shape of DNA.

Tolerance to partial DNA occupancy

Given that the number of DNA bonds can be precisely controlled through chemical functionalization, we next investigated the minimum extent of DNA modification required to direct crystallization. We synthesized GlaHQ184C-DNA conjugates A to G by reacting GlaHQ184C with increasing molar equivalents (0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 5, and 6, respectively) of 5′-Mal-C2–AGCT DNA per conjugation site. Following purification, absorption spectra of the conjugates exhibited a hypsochromic shift in peak maxima from 276 nm (conjugate A) to 269 nm (conjugate G), consistent with increasing DNA content (Fig. 3A). The absorbance ratio at 260/280 nm was used to estimate the average number of DNA strands attached per protein octamer, which ranged from ∼2 strands in conjugate A to ∼8 strands in conjugate G (fig. S7).

Conjugates A-C did not yield crystals under the screening conditions (Fig. 3B). In contrast, conjugates D-G, which carried ∼5 to 8 DNA strands per octamer, consistently produced crystals, all of which indexed to the same space group and unit cell. Overall, diffraction resolution generally improved with increasing DNA content: 2.27 Å for conjugate D, 2.38 Å for conjugate E, 2.07 Å for conjugate F, and 2.01 Å for conjugate G. In addition, the number of crystallization hits correlated positively with DNA conjugation density. Superposition of each protein structure onto that of conjugate G revealed all-atom root mean square deviations of 0.61 Å (conjugate D), 0.49 Å (conjugate E), and 0.44 Å (conjugate F), indicating minimal structural perturbation to the protein despite variable DNA loading (Fig. 3C). Increased DNA density correlated with stronger and more continuous omit electron density for the DNA helices, enabling more precise modeling of nucleobases and double-stranded regions (Fig. 3D). These results demonstrate that complete DNA conjugation at all surface-accessible cysteines is not necessary for DNA-directed crystallization of GlaH, likely due to the multivalent nature of the conjugates. While monodispersity is typically essential for biomolecular crystallization, these findings show that polydisperse protein-DNA conjugates with partial DNA occupancy can still yield diffraction-quality crystals suitable for atomic-resolution structural analysis.

Mechanism of DNA bond formation

Additional conjugate designs with DNA functionalized at different positions and with varying linker flexibilities were crystallized to determine whether DNA bond formation can direct the crystallization of conjugates other than GlaHQ184C–C2-DNA. When a more flexible linker, 5′ 4-(N-maleimidomethyl)cyclohexane-1-carboxyl-amino-C6 (5′-Mal-C6), was used (Fig. 1F), GlaHQ184C–C6-AGCT crystallized with a similar packing arrangement and unit cell parameters as GlaHQ184C–C2-AGCT, although weak electron density precluded DNA modeling (Fig. 4A and fig. S6D). In addition to I422 crystals, GlaHQ184C–C6-AGCT formed crystals in the P4212 space group (Fig. 4B and fig. S8A), exhibiting packing similar to that of wild-type GlaH (without DNA modification; fig. S8B). These P4212 structures retained few native protein-protein interactions observed in wild-type GlaH crystals at the crystal interface, suggesting that the presence of 5′-Mal-C6 DNA did not disrupt them (Supplementary Text). Omit maps at the conjugation sites showed two Cys184 rotamers with attached maleimide moieties, but the remainder of the DNA was not resolved (Fig. 4C and fig. S8C). Furthermore, GlaHQ184C functionalized with 5′-Mal-C6–GC, 5′-Mal-C6–TTTT (nonhybridizing), or 5′-Mal-C6–AAATTT sequences also formed P4212 crystals with similar packing structures, confirming the absence of DNA hybridization. The formation of both DNA-bonded I422 and nonhybridized P4212 crystals from the same GlaHQ184C–C6-AGCT conjugate suggests that increased linker flexibility reduces the directional constraint of DNA bonding, allowing competition between atomic and DNA bonds during crystal formation and leading to multiple packing outcomes.

Next, the effect of conjugation sites on DNA bond formation was examined (Fig. 4D). GlaHN187C conjugated with 5′-Mal-C6–GC, 5′-Mal-C6–AGCT, or 5′-Mal-C6–TTTT produced P4212 crystals, with omit maps revealing the Cys187–cross-linker adducts in two alternative conformations (Fig. 4E and fig. S8D). These adducts adopt parallel but opposing orientations, consistent with nonhybridized DNA within the crystals. In contrast, GlaHN187C conjugated with 5′-Mal-C2–GC or 5′-Mal-C2–AGCT did not yield diffracting crystals, suggesting that both bond location and orientation must be precisely controlled. Furthermore, GlaHK270C–C2-DNA conjugates crystallized in both P4212 and I422 space groups, the latter corresponding to the wild-type, nonhybridized crystal form (Fig. 4F and fig. S8E). These results indicate that DNA bonds located at distal sites away from Gln184 can hinder proper bond configuration and prevent hybridization-mediated crystallization.

Given that alternative conformations of cysteine-maleimide adducts were observed in crystals formed from different DNA attachment sites, these findings suggest that, at the onset of protein assembly, unhybridized DNA strands explore a broad conformational space across both cysteine rotamers through a windshield wiper–like roaming motion (Fig. 4G). A plausible assembly pathway for DNA-bonded crystals involves the intermolecular hybridization of DNA strands that nucleates oligomerization between adjacent protein-DNA conjugates. Although the formation of double-stranded DNA increases rigidity, the cysteine-maleimide linkages likely retain sufficient flexibility to sample different rotamer conformations. As additional protein-DNA building blocks associate, the resulting pseudo-crystalline intermediates reorganize into a crystalline state with unified bonding conformations. In this process, a moderate degree of flexibility imparted by the 5′-Mal-C2 linker appears necessary for proper alignment, whereas excessive flexibility from the 5′-Mal-C6 linker disrupts DNA bond directionality and promotes native protein-protein interactions, as observed in unhybridized I422 crystals. The presence of alternative conformations thus supports an unconventional crystallization mechanism mediated by flexible, rather than rigid, bonding interactions.

Programmable material properties

Owing to the macromolecular nature of DNA bonds, we hypothesized that protein crystals stabilized by DNA hybridization would exhibit greater mechanical compliance than those stabilized by atomic interactions. To probe this, we performed nanoindentation experiments on wild-type GlaH crystals, stabilized by native protein-protein interactions, and on GlaHQ184C crystals functionalized with 5′-Mal-C2–DNA. Crystals stabilized by atomic interactions exhibited steeper force-displacement curves than DNA-bonded crystals, indicating higher stiffness (Fig. 5A). Crystals bonded with 5′-Mal-C2–AGGCCT showed lower resistance to indentation than those with 5′-Mal-C2–AGCT, suggesting that longer DNA duplexes confer reduced stiffness. Correspondingly, the measured reduced moduli and hardness values decreased progressively from wild-type GlaH to GlaHQ184C–C2-AGCT and GlaHQ184C–C2-AGGCCT crystals, indicating a systematic reduction in resistance to deformation (Fig. 5, B and C). The reduced moduli for GlaHQ184C–C2-AGCT and GlaHQ184C–C2-AGGCCT crystals were 175 ± 27 and 15 ± 2 MPa, respectively, comparable in magnitude to those of polymers such as low-density polyethylene and soft tissues such as cartilage (38, 39).

To understand how bonding characteristics influence mechanical properties, we determined the solvent content of the I422 crystals. GlaHWT, GlaHQ184C–C2-AGCT, and GlaHQ184C–C2-AGGCCT crystals contain 66, 58, and 67% solvent, respectively, reflecting differences in protein packing density (fig. S9). The markedly lower stiffness of GlaHQ184C–C2-AGGCCT crystals compared with that of wild-type GlaH crystals, despite similar solvent content, indicates that the mechanical differences arise from bond type rather than packing density. This is consistent with the greater flexibility of DNA molecules relative to short-range atomic interactions. Furthermore, the shorter 5′-Mal-C2–AGCT duplex likely increases intermolecular contact area under load and facilitates more effective stress transfer, thereby enhancing the stiffness of GlaHQ184C–C2-AGCT crystals. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the porosity and mechanical properties of protein crystals can be programmed by the DNA bond.

DISCUSSION

The connectivity of atoms and molecules is fundamentally governed by chemical bonds operating over distances of several angstroms. Here, we have shown that at larger length scales, protein nanoparticles can be precisely connected via DNA hybridization, which operates over nanometer-scale distances. The use of nucleic acids to organize biomolecules into diffraction-quality crystals has represented a three decades-long challenge in DNA-programmable assembly. This problem was addressed via three key innovations: (i) a valency-directed interface design strategy that integrates computational and experimental insights to define the connectivity and directionality of protein building blocks within a lattice; (ii) site- and number-selective DNA functionalization that eliminates the surface heterogeneity and kinetic traps characteristic of nanoparticles densely functionalized with DNA; and (iii) the use of vapor diffusion techniques to prepare single crystals of DNA-modified nanoparticles, enabling high-resolution structural characterization by single-crystal XRD. The formation of DNA-directed, atomically precise protein crystals despite the intrinsic flexibility of single-stranded DNA was unexpected. Alternative conformations of DNA linkages observed in unhybridized crystals further confirmed the mobility of the DNA strands, revealing an unconventional crystallization process driven by the dynamic motion of Cys-DNA rotamers, analogous to an electron cloud in atomic systems. Contrary to the prevailing assumption that rigid molecules and linkages are required to form ordered materials, these findings demonstrate that flexibility does not preclude atomic order.

Interfacial analyses of both DNA-bonded and unhybridized crystals have revealed four design principles for DNA-directed biomolecular single crystals:

1) selective functionalization of DNA strands on the protein surface improves structural order by minimizing kinetic traps and maximizing the number of hybridization events;

2) the number, spatial arrangement, and helicity of DNA strands, together with the intrinsic quaternary structure of the protein core, determine the valency and bonding configurations within an atomic lattice;

3) nonorthogonal DNA sequences lead to nonspecific hybridizations that inhibit single-crystal formation; and

4) short cross-linkers (e.g., 5′-Mal-C2) enhance DNA bond directionality, enabling the DNA-mediated interactions to override native biomolecular contacts during crystallization.

These principles move beyond the traditional DNA-programmable nanoparticle assembly paradigm, which produces atomically disordered superlattices, and underscore how atomic modeling and site-selective DNA modification can generate atomically precise crystals defined by discrete molecular bonds.

While this workflow successfully predicted the crystallization outcomes of GlaHQ184C-DNA conjugates, it still relies on manual modeling and empirical assessment of molecular packing. Advances in computational methods for predicting protein-protein interactions, as well as refinement tools tailored for protein-DNA conjugates, could improve design accuracy (40, 41). Nevertheless, the design strategy described here offers a rational blueprint for extending this approach to other inorganic, polymeric, and biological nanoparticles (fig. S2).

From a structural biology perspective, these results show that DNA functionalization preserves the overall protein structure (Fig. 3C) and enables high-resolution structures that provide more detailed information than existing experimental or predicted models (fig. S10). Notably, unlike traditional crystallization, which often demands extreme monodispersity, polydisperse protein-DNA conjugates with partial DNA occupancy can still support crystal formation suitable for high-resolution analysis. Given the empirical and often unpredictable nature of protein crystallization, an early application of this approach may involve using computational structure prediction tools, such as AlphaFold (42, 43), to guide DNA modification at sites likely to promote DNA-directed crystallization. This strategy could offer a route for determining the structures of previously uncharacterized proteins.

From a materials engineering standpoint, these findings demonstrate that programmable DNA interactions enable precise control over crystal packing and porosity (figs. S1 and S9). In contrast to wild-type GlaH crystals formed through conventional chemical bonds, DNA-bonded crystals exhibit remarkable mechanical flexibility, opening previously unexplored avenues for engineering elasticity and controlled deformation in crystalline materials. Moreover, integrating intrinsic protein functions with properties of DNA-based assemblies, such as stimuli-responsiveness (44) and cellular uptake (45–47), may enable the creation of atomically precise biomaterials for applications in sensing, catalysis, encapsulation, robotics, bioelectronics, and biomedicine. For example, DNA-guided protein crystals with uniform spacing and orientation would allow systematic and precise tuning of cooperativity between neighboring catalytic centers in catalysis, or multivalent interactions with receptors in molecular recognition. Furthermore, atomic-level control over pore size, local charge distribution, and dynamic accessibility could enable selective partitioning and controlled release of cargo relevant to drug delivery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

DNA synthesis

Oligonucleotides on UnyLinker CPG solid supports (ChemGenes) were synthesized using a MerMade 12 synthesizer (BioAutomation). Standard reagents (Glen Research) and coupling procedures were used, with 4,5-dicyanoimidazole as the activator and iodine as the oxidant. Oligonucleotides were cleaved from the solid support using standard ammonium hydroxide–methylamine (AMA) deprotection [a 1:1 mixture of 30% aqueous ammonium hydroxide and 40% aqueous methylamine for 25 min at 55°C] and purified using reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (Agilent 1260 Infinity) equipped with an Agilent Dynamax Microsorb C18 column, using a gradient of 0 to 75% acetonitrile in triethylammonium acetate buffer over 45 min. The collected fractions were treated with 20% (v/v) acetic acid for 1 hour to cleave dimethoxytrityl protecting groups and subsequently lyophilized overnight. The identities of the purified oligonucleotides were confirmed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) mass spectrometry using a Bruker MALDI Rapiflex Tissue Typer in linear negative mode, with 2′,6′-dihydroxyacetophenone as the matrix and diammonium hydrogen citrate as the comatrix.

For the retro-Diels-Alder deprotection of 5′-maleimide-C2 (5′-Mal-C2) DNA, oligonucleotides were coupled with 5′-maleimide-modifier phosphoramidite (Glen Research), purified using standard procedures, and then lyophilized to dryness. A retro-Diels-Alder reaction was performed to deprotect the maleimide prior to bioconjugation. Specifically, the dried DNA strands were dissolved in anhydrous methanol and dried by rotary evaporation. The oligonucleotides were further dried through two coevaporations with anhydrous acetonitrile, followed by three coevaporations with anhydrous toluene. The DNA was then suspended in 50 ml of anhydrous toluene, and the reaction was conducted at 90°C for 6 hours. After evaporating the toluene, the deprotected oligonucleotides were obtained as a white powder. For long-term storage, the deprotected strands can be resuspended in nuclease-free water and stored at −80°C for up to 3 months.

For the synthesis of 5′-Mal-C6 DNA, oligonucleotides modified with a 5′-amino-modifier C6 phosphoramidite (Glen Research) were synthesized and purified using standard procedures. Following lyophilization, 0.1 μmol of DNA was dissolved in 150 μl of nuclease-free water and then mixed with 900 μl of succinimidyl 4-(N-maleimidomethyl)cyclohexane-1-carboxylate cross-linker at a concentration of 11.1 mg/ml in dimethyl sulfoxide (fig. S11). The mixture was vigorously shaken at 1200 rpm for 30 min, after which 5 μl of 1.0 M sodium bicarbonate was added. The reaction proceeded for an additional 5 min before being quenched with 400 μl of 1.0 M tris buffer (pH 7.0). The reaction was further diluted with water to precipitate any unreacted cross-linker, filtered through 0.2-μm filters, and buffer exchanged with water using ENrich SEC 70 columns (Bio-Rad). The eluent containing the maleimide-modified oligonucleotides was dried using a CentriVap centrifugal vacuum concentrator (Labconco) and stored at −20°C until use.

Protein expression

Genes encoding glutarate 2-hydroxylase [Protein Data Bank (PDB) ID: 2R6S] were truncated at the C terminus after His320 to create a 6× His tag. The codon-optimized gene fragment was obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies, inserted into a pET28a vector using Gibson assembly, and transformed into BL21 (DE3) electrocompetent cells (Sigma-Aldrich) via electroporation. The cells were grown overnight on agar plates containing kanamycin (50 μg/ml). Single colonies were selected to inoculate 7-ml cultures in LB broth, and the plasmids were extracted for sequence confirmation via Sanger sequencing using T7 and T7 reverse primers. The starter culture (7 ml) was added to 2× yeast extract/tryptone/potassium phosphate broth containing kanamycin (50 μg/ml) and grown at 37°C until an optical density of 0.4 to 0.8 was reached. Protein expression was induced with 0.1 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (Invitrogen), and the culture was incubated with shaking at 200 rpm overnight at 25°C. The cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in 20 mM Hepes buffer (pH 7.0) (buffer A), and lysed using a high-pressure homogenizer. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 16,000g for 25 min, and the supernatant was loaded onto Profinity nickel-charged immobilized metal affinity chromatography cartridges (Bio-Rad) preequilibrated with buffer A. The column was washed with five column volumes of buffer A, and the protein was eluted with 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.0) and 250 mM imidazole (buffer B). The collected fractions were buffer exchanged into 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.0), 20% ethylene glycol, 5 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (buffer C) using Mini Bio-Gel P-6 desalting cartridges (Bio-Rad) and stored at −20°C.

Site-directed mutagenesis

Mutants of glutarate 2-hydroxylase were generated through site-directed mutagenesis. Primers were designed using NEBaseChanger and obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies. The plasmids were amplified with Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity Master Mix (New England Biolabs), using 0.5 μM primers and 25 ng of template DNA. The amplified plasmids were treated with 1× Kinase, Ligase, and DpnI (New England Biolabs) and then transformed into NEB 5-alpha competent Escherichia coli via heat shock. The sequence of the mutated plasmids was confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

DNA conjugation

Protein was purified and desalted using ENrich SEC 650 columns (Bio-Rad) with buffer A as the mobile phase. The eluent was pooled, and the concentration was adjusted to 50 μM monomer. Maleimide-modified DNA was thawed or dissolved in nuclease-free water to a concentration of 500 μM and then immediately mixed with the protein. Typically, DNA was added in a fourfold molar excess relative to the concentration of free cysteines, with sodium chloride added to a final concentration of 100 mM. For experiments aimed at varying the degree of DNA functionalization, the DNA molar excess and sodium chloride concentration were adjusted up to a tenfold molar excess and 500 mM sodium chloride, respectively. The reaction was incubated overnight at room temperature and then analyzed using 4 to 15% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) or 7.5% native PAGE. Excess DNA was removed by two rounds of spin filtration using a 100-kDa molecular weight cutoff ultracentrifugal filter (Amicon), and the reaction was further purified using ENrich SEC 650 columns (Bio-Rad) equilibrated with buffer A.

Modeling protein-DNA conjugates

The starting GlaH protein structure (PDB ID: 2R6S) was mutated to cysteine at the designated sites using PyMOL 3.1. Maleimide-terminated DNA strands (5′-Mal-C2–AGCT) were constructed in Coot 0.9.8 using a B-form single-stranded DNA model and attached to the engineered cysteine residues. Owing to alternative cysteine side-chain conformations, two monomeric protein-DNA conjugate models were generated for each design. Biological assemblies of the octameric conjugates were generated by symmetry expansion and subsequently duplicated into nine octamers in PyMOL to represent unassembled protein-DNA conjugates in solution. The coordinates and orientations of individual conjugates were then manually adjusted to enforce intermolecular connections through canonical DNA hybridization (Watson-Crick base pairing). In each iteration, steric clashes between proteins and packing defects were evaluated after full hybridization of all DNA strands within an octamer. Models exhibiting severe configurational misalignment were discarded, whereas those with minor steric clashes or packing disorder were retained. The orientations of the maleimide linker and DNA strands were iteratively refined in Coot, maintaining appropriate chemical geometries, and then the refined and expanded conjugate models were evaluated in PyMOL in subsequent cycles. After three to four iterations, the final candidates were selected for experimental crystallization.

Biomolecular crystallization

Proteins and protein-DNA conjugates were crystallized using the sitting-drop vapor-diffusion method. In a typical experiment, a solution containing the proteins or protein-DNA conjugates was prepared in buffer A and then concentrated. Crystallization screening was conducted in a 96-well plate (Intelli-Plate 96-3 well, Art Robbins Instruments) using a Crystal Gryphon liquid-handling robot (Art Robbins Instruments). The Helix and JCSG+ screens (Molecular Dimensions) were primarily used to determine crystallization conditions. Each reservoir in the 96-well plate was filled with 70 μl of the crystallization solution, whereas each sitting drop consisted of 1 μl of the crystallization solution mixed with 1 μl of the protein or protein-DNA solution. The plates were incubated at 22°C, undisturbed, for 2 to 4 weeks, and crystal formation was monitored under an optical microscope. Crystals were transferred to nylon loops and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Diffraction data were collected at facilities including the Advanced Photon Source (Argonne National Laboratory, USA), the National Synchrotron Light Source II (Brookhaven National Laboratory, USA), the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (France), SOLEIL PROXIMA (French National Synchrotron Facility), MAX IV Laboratory (Sweden), and the Diamond Light Source (United Kingdom). Within the same crystallization trials, multiple crystals formed under different conditions. Many of these crystals were selected for diffraction experiments, and all datasets were indexed, scaled, and merged. The highest-quality data, based on overall completeness, resolution, R factors, and I/sig(I) for structures with nearly identical unit cell parameters, were selected for model building and refinement. Crystallization conditions and data collection source are shown in tables S7 to S9.

Structure resolution and model refinement

Diffraction data were indexed, integrated, and scaled using autoPROC (Global Phasing Limited), xia2, DIALS, XDS (MPI for Medical Research, Heidelberg), and/or the CCP4 suite of programs (48) (Pointless and Aimless) depending on the source of data collection. Data were merged using Aimless with a resolution cutoff criterion of CC1/2 > 0.30 for most datasets. Structures were determined by molecular replacement with Phaser MR, using the structure of GlaH (PDB: 2R6S) as the template. The initial molecular replacement structures were refined with Refmac, and the protein models were further refined via successive rounds of manual model building in Coot and Refmac refinements until Rwork/Rfree were unchanged. Last, metal coordination sites, water molecules, and ligands (if applicable) were manually built in Coot, and amino acids were trimmed in regions with poor electron density. Diffraction data collection and refinement statistics are shown in tables S10 to S17.

Size exclusion chromatography with multiangle light scattering

Proteins (15 mg/ml) were injected into a Superdex 200 column (GE Healthcare) with a flow rate of 0.4 ml/min in 1× phosphate-buffered saline. Samples were detected by an Agilent LC 1260 Infinity diode array detector at 260 and 280 nm, a DAWN Heleos (Wyatt Technology) with a fused silica cell at 658 nm for MALS, and an Optilab rEX (Wyatt Technology) concentration detector for refractive index measurements.

Nanoindentation

The mechanical properties of native GlaH crystals and DNA-bonded GlaH crystals were characterized using a Hysitron TI 900 Triboindenter (Bruker). To ensure a stable surface for reliable indentation, all crystals were transferred from the growth solution and air-dried immediately prior to indentation experiments. For all measured crystals, no macroscopic collapse or morphological degradation was observed. A Berkovich probe (tip radius, 0.6 μm; angle, 142.3°) was used for all measurements. Indentation experiments were performed using a trapezoidal load function, with a loading time of 5 s, a holding time of 2 s at a maximum indentation depth of 1000 nm, and an unloading time of 5 s. This loading program was selected to minimize short-term creep effects in biological crystalline samples. For each group, reduced Young’s modulus and hardness were measured from at least three different crystals, with three technical replicates performed per crystal.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 9. The specific statistical tests used are detailed in the corresponding figure caption. Normality was assessed via Q-Q plots, and Brown-Forsythe and Welch’s corrections were applied when unequal variances were detected. For reduced modulus measurement (Fig. 5B), F = 268.6, DFn = 2.000, and DFd = 17.57 for Brown-Forsythe analysis of variance (ANOVA) test; and W = 404.5, DFn = 2.000, and DFd = 14.62 for Welch’s ANOVA test. For hardness measurement (Fig. 5C), F = 44.71, DFn = 2.000, and DFd = 13.18 for Brown-Forsythe ANOVA test; and W = 46.02, DFn = 2.000, and DFd = 14.76 for Welch’s ANOVA test.
