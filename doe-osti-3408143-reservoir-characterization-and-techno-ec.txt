Preface
This report provides details of reservoir characterization and techno-economic analysis for
enhanced and closed-loop geothermal systems in the Denver-Julesburg Basin Wattenberg area.
In this study, NLR developed and applied slender-body theory modeling tools and the
GEOPHIRES simulator to support techno-economic analysis. The slender-body theory wellbore
simulator evaluated thermal performance in U-loop, closed loop multilateral, and enhanced
geothermal system well configurations. Results indicated that an optimized single U-loop design
delivers an outlet temperature of 168.8°C, stabilizing at a final outlet temperature of 135.3°C.
Heat production also improved significantly, with an average thermal output of 16.9 MWth and a
final thermal output of 11.0 MWth. These results provided critical baseline data for selecting
optimized systems for techno-economic analysis.

Building on these outputs, NLR applied the GEOPHIRES techno-economic simulator to estimate
the levelized cost of electricity for both the closed loop multilateral and enhanced geothermal
systems. The sensitivity analysis showed that drilling costs exert a particularly strong impact on
the levelized cost of electricity of multilateral systems. Therefore, advances in drilling
technology and improved drilling rates could yield significant gains in the economic
performance of these systems. In this context, the study directly supports the U.S. Department of
Energy’s mission to reduce the cost of geothermal energy and accelerate the deployment of next-
generation geothermal technologies.

Executive Summary
This study aims to assess the techno-economic feasibility of both enhanced geothermal system
(EGS) and closed-loop geothermal system (CLGS) configurations in the Denver-Julesburg (DJ)
Basin in Colorado. In this effort, NLR applied the slender-body theory wellbore simulator in
combination with the GEOPHIRES techno-economic tool framework. These tools were used to
simulate the thermal performance of U-loop, closed loop multilateral, and EGS well designs,
followed by parametric sensitivity analyses to optimize geometry, well spacing, injection
conditions, and working fluid selection. The results demonstrated that increasing the number and
length of laterals substantially enhanced heat extraction, whereas spacing beyond 25 m yielded
diminishing returns. System performance was found to be highly sensitive to injection and
reservoir temperature, with water circulation in granite providing the most favorable outcome,
whereas lower conductivity lithologies and the use of supercritical CO₂ reduced efficiency.

The techno-economic assessment revealed clear differences between system configurations. The
EGS design achieved the highest technical and economic performance, producing 8.46 MWe at a
levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) of 80.7 $/MWh and a net present value of $68.7 M.
However, these outcomes are contingent on favorable permeability and stress conditions, and the
approach carries higher capital investment and geotechnical risk. By comparison, the closed loop
multilateral closed-loop system produced 5 MWe at an LCOE of 151.1 $/MWh but with a
negative $ 8.54M. Although its internal rate of return was relatively low due to drilling
complexity, this configuration represents a scalable and lower-risk option, as it does not require
stimulation. The simpler U-loop design showed lower performance overall, but it could become
cost-effective in certain contexts if lateral lengths are extended or additional branches are added.

The results confirm that EGS offer the highest potential for power generation in favorable
geological settings, whereas closed-loop configurations provide a more predictable, lower-risk
alternative, particularly in regions where permeability or stress conditions limit the feasibility of
EGS. These findings highlight the critical role of drilling costs and lateral complexity in
determining system economics and underscore the importance of continued innovation in drilling
technology. By demonstrating both the technical feasibility and economic potential of next-
generation geothermal designs, the project directly supports DOE’s mission of reducing the cost
of geothermal energy and accelerating broader adoption of next-generation geothermal
technologies.

Geology of the Denver-Julesburg Basin
The study area is within the Greater Wattenberg area of the Denver Basin. The basin is a large
asymmetric syncline containing Paleozoic, Mesozoic, and Cenozoic sedimentary sequences
dominated by subaerial deposits with limited carbonates. The Wattenberg area is a prolific oil
and gas field with extensive subsurface temperature data. It is also associated with a thermal
anomaly, exhibiting higher geothermal gradients than the surrounding areas. Hydrocarbon
production occurs from units such as the Pierre shale, Lower Cretaceous sandstones, and Lyons
sandstone.

The targeted geothermal reservoir is Precambrian basement rock, encountered below ~3.0-km
depth. We used the following thermophysical properties for the representative lithologies of
granite in our reservoir model: average density of 2,540 kg/m³, specific heat capacity of 1,185

J/kg/K, and thermal conductivity of 3.1 W/m/K. We assume very low permeability in the
basement rocks, whereas the Pierre shale within the Dakota Group sandstones is relatively
higher. Regional geothermal costs average 30°C–40°C/km, with localized anomalies up to
50°C/km. The regional stress regime analysis indicated normal faulting with maximum
horizontal stress (SHmax) oriented northwest-southeast.

1 Introduction
1.1 Project Overview
The project evaluated two model high-temperature geothermal wells (twin wells) located in
Colorado’s Denver-Julesburg (DJ) Basin (Figure 1). The wells design assumes true vertical
depths (TVDs) of up to 6 km (~20,000 feet). It is assumed that a significant portion of the well
trajectory, approximately 2.7 km (~9,000 ft), intersect the hard granitic basement rock. This
NLR’s study focuses on advancing geothermal system modeling and economic evaluation, with
a particular emphasis on wellbore thermal performance and future site development options. The
integration of well test data with simulation tools, coupled with geological analysis and techno-
economic modeling, positions NLR to support both real-time drilling insights and resource
assessment going forward.

2 Geology of the Denver Basin
The DJ Basin is one of the largest basins in the Rocky Mountain region. It lies along the eastern
flank of the Colorado Front Range and extends into parts of Colorado, Nebraska, and Wyoming
(Crowell et al., 2012; Martin, 1965). During the Late Cretaceous, the basin formed part of the
Western Interior Seaway floor and remained submerged until approximately 69 Ma (Cobban et
al. 2006). Its evolution records a complex geologic history shaped by repeated marine incursions,
tectonic uplift, sedimentation, and erosion over the past ~75 million years (Figure 2).

Figure 2. Conceptual model depicting the topographic evolution of the Colorado Front Range and
 Denver Basin.
  From 75 Ma to ~68 Ma, the region was occupied by the Interior Seaway under high sea levels, after
 which abrupt, episodic uplifts occurred to the west. Between ~50 Ma and ~40 Ma, the area experienced
  orogenic collapse and regional beveling, followed by late-stage regional uplift and basin exhumation.
 Figure from (Raynolds et al., 2007).

2.1 Geological Evolution
The Denver Basin’s complex geology is intricately tied to the Laramide orogeny. The Laramide
orogeny, spanning the Late Cretaceous to the Paleocene (ca. 80–55 Ma), was a major tectonic
event that produced the Laramide block uplifts in the United States, the Rocky Mountain fold-
and-thrust belt across Canada and the United States, and the Sierra Madre Oriental fold-and-
thrust belt in east-central Mexico (English & Johnston, 2004). It resulted in uplift and faulting in
regions like Colorado, Wyoming, and New Mexico. The orogeny eventually partitioned the
southern Rocky Mountain foreland basin into many small sedimentary basins (Figure 3),
including the Piceance, Uinta, and San Juan basins (Phillipson 2005), and created basement-
cored uplifts, broad arches, and deep adjacent sedimentary basins like the DJ Basin.

The DJ Basin is situated in a pericratonic area. It is categorized as a foreland basin between the
Rocky Mountains in the west and the large craton beneath the United States and Canada to the
east (Davalos-Elizondo et al., 2024a). This location has allowed for cumulative deposits of
sediments over time, which have built up to form a deep (~3-km) basin of sediments, underlain
by a granitic or gneissic metamorphic basement. The sedimentary sequence consists of Upper
Cretaceous and Lower Cretaceous rocks; Jurassic and Triassic rocks; Permian rocks;
Pennsylvanian rocks; and Mississippian and Ordovician in the upper ~3 km overlying the

Precambrian metamorphic basement rocks. The Precambrian rocks have been identified as either
granite or gneiss (Figure 4).

2.2 Structural Geology
Structures within the Denver Basin are interpreted from surface lineaments and magnetic
surveys. The present-day stress regime is compressive (Figure 5), with maximum horizontal
stress oriented to the north-northwest (Davalos-Elizondo et al., 2024b; Heidbach et al., 2018;
Lundstern & Zoback, 2020). The western part of the basin is characterized by northwest-
trending, high-angle reverse faults that dominate (Erslev & Selvig, 1997) and have been

 6

periodically reactivated in response to shifting stress fields, brittle deformation, and basin
buckling. The Wattenberg area, by contrast, reflects a relaxed compressive regime with strike-
slip characteristics, particularly in the upper crust (Lundstern & Zoback, 2020). This regime
promotes the development of detachment-related wrench faults as localized structures (Figure 6).
These include the five major wrench fault zones (WFZs) that have been identified in the Denver–
Golden–Greeley area: Johnstown (J. WFZ), Longmont (Lo. WFZ), Lafayette (La. WFZ),
Windsor (W. WFZ), and Cherry Gulch (CG. WFZ; sometimes referred to as Cherry Creek)
(Fishman, 2005; Weimer & Sonnenberg, 1996).

3 Subsurface Characterization
Structural and lithological characteristics are fundamental to the geothermal modeling
undertaken in this study. Existing exploration data indicate a granitic-to-gneissic basement at
depths below 3,000 m (Porro et al., 2012). However, because most boreholes terminate above
3,000 m, critical properties such as thermal conductivity, specific heat capacity, and reservoir
porosity remain uncertain below this depth. Significant uncertainty also exists regarding the
continuity and behavior of deep fault networks. Although subsurface parameters are often
uncertain, a comprehensive evaluation of geothermal potential depends on their systematic
characterization, as these parameters directly govern the reservoir’s thermal performance, well
design, and overall techno-economic feasibility. They are critical inputs for both the SBT model
analysis and the GEOPHIRES techno-economic simulator used in subsequent sections. The
following subsections provide an integrated assessment of the subsurface parameters most
relevant to reservoir evaluation and techno-economic analysis, including stratigraphy and
temperature distribution.

3.1 Stratigraphy at the Wattenberg Anomaly Area
The asymmetric geometry of the Denver Basin results in significant variation in sedimentary
thickness, with the basin deepest near its western margin, adjacent to the Front Range Uplift. The
representative cross section in the west-east direction. Figure 7 illustrates the stratigraphy, where
the top succession is sedimentary layers overlying the basement.

 Figure 7. Representative cross section for the Denver Basin along the A-A’ line in Figure 1. The
 Denver Basin is a large asymmetric syncline with its deepest point about 3,900 m below the
 surface.
 Figure from Nelson and Santus (2011).
The sedimentary layers reflect depositional environments characterized by fluvial, deltaic, and
shallow marine processes that controlled basin infill from the Paleozoic through the Cenozoic.
The upper-3-km sedimentary sequence is rich in oil and gas that occurs at multiple stratigraphic
intervals, including the Upper Cretaceous Pierre shale; the Lower Cretaceous sandstones, such as

 9

the Dakota Group; and the Permian Lyons sandstone (Figure 7). These formations also serve as
important stratigraphic markers for understanding reservoir quality, permeability trends, and
thermal maturity. Several formations are recognized as hydrocarbon source rocks (e.g., the Pierre
shale and the Niobrara Formation), whereas others have the potential to yield unconventional
resources, such as coalbed methane. These aspects underscore the basin’s long history as an
energy-producing province, while also providing context for subsurface heat extraction potential.
The nature of the Precambrian basement is of particular significance for geothermal exploration.
The assumption is that the basement is composed of granites and gneisses, which are typically
characterized by high strength, low porosity, and very low permeability. This makes them prime
candidates for EGS or closed-loop geothermal technologies rather than conventional
hydrothermal systems. At the same time, their relatively high thermal conductivity and favorable
heat retention properties contribute to the overall geothermal potential of the basin.

3.2 Temperature Distribution and Geothermal Gradient
A comprehensive dataset of subsurface temperatures has been compiled from drillers’ logs
across Colorado, with the majority sourced from the Colorado Geological Survey. The dataset
includes over 13,000 bottom-hole temperature (BHT) measurements, approximately 9,000 of
which are from Weld County alone (Berkman & Watterson, 2010). These data offer valuable
insights into the thermal regime of the Greater Wattenberg area and its geothermal potential.
Because raw BHT values typically underestimate true formation temperatures, corrections were
applied following the methodology of Crowell et al. (2012), resulting in estimated geothermal
gradients of approximately 50°C/km (Figure 8). Correlation with observations from Tester et al.
(2021) further supports the presence of localized thermal anomalies, with geothermal gradients
approaching 50°C/km and BHTs exceeding 150°C at depths shallower than 3 km. In addition,
complementary datasets from the U.S. Geological Survey—particularly fluid chemistry
analyses—further strengthen subsurface characterization by linking thermal data with reservoir
geochemical properties. For example, the calcium–magnesium geothermometer suggests
reservoir temperatures of ~167°C at depths of around 3 km, under the assumption that sampled
fluids originate from near-total drilled depth.

  Figure 8. The uncorrected BHT and corrected BHT using a correction factor from Crowell et al.
 (2012), indicating an estimated gradient of ~50°C/km

The heat flow map developed by Brokaw (2017) reveals a thermal hotspot within Wattenberg
that might be associated with the Colorado Mineral Belt (CMB) (Figure 9). From the Late
Cretaceous to the Middle Cenozoic (~70–40 Ma), the CMB likely introduced subbasin magmas
and highly conductive metallic intrusions. The basinal wrench faults, on the other hand, which
are oriented northeast and were activated during the Laramide orogeny (Weimer and Sonnenberg
1996), likely acted as conduits for heat transport. High heat flow values exceeding 85 mW/m²
have been observed in the Wattenberg hotspot and cannot be explained by variations in sediment
thickness alone.

3.3 Reservoir Basement Temperatures
Reservoir temperature is the primary control on geothermal resource quality, and multiple
independent studies provide constraints on the expected temperature profile in the study area.
National-scale temperature models (Blackwell et al., 2011) predict temperatures of 175°C–
225°C at depths of approximately 5.5 km, consistent with a geothermal gradient of 30°C–
40°C/km. The NLR assessment indicates modeled temperatures of 110°C–130°C at ~3 km depth
(Mullane et al., 2016). Similarly, the sedimentary basin assessment suggests that the depth to the
Precambrian basement ranges from ~3,000–3,800 m, with predicted bottom-of-basin
temperatures of 115°C–140°C (Porro et al. 2012).

To further constrain these estimates, we developed a one-dimensional steady-state conductive
model, assuming a regional heat flux of ~80–90 mW/m² and a corrected geothermal gradient of
~50°C/km for the basin (Figure 8). Using a representative surface temperature of ~10°C and a
basement depth of 3.0–3.8 km, we obtained modeled temperatures at the basement interface in
the range of ~160°C–200°C. Extending this gradient into the crystalline basement
(granite/gneiss) projects temperatures exceeding ~260°C at ~6-km depth, with upper-bound
cases approaching ~300°C (Figure 10). These results are consistent with corrected BHT datasets

 12

and are only moderately sensitive to variations in thermal conductivity between sedimentary and
crystalline units. Collectively, the regional assessments, corrected wellbore data, and conductive
modeling converge on a robust prediction of >260°C reservoir temperatures at 6-km depth,
reinforcing the feasibility of targeting high-enthalpy resources suitable for both EGS and
advanced closed-loop development at the Wattenberg site.

3.5 Rock Permeability
Permeability governs fluid circulation potential and directly impacts the choice of geothermal
technology. Hydrothermal systems require naturally high permeability, whereas EGS or closed-
loop concepts require low-permeability settings. The Denver Basin contains several oil- and gas-
producing formations, with permeabilities ranging from very low values in the Upper Cretaceous
Pierre shale (~0.1–10 mD; Higley et al. 2006) to highly permeable sandstones in the Dakota
Group (>100–2,000 mD). In contrast, the Precambrian granite and gneiss exhibit low
permeability (<0.01 mD). This means that the basement could be suitable for EGS or closed-loop
concepts but unsuitable for hydrothermal exploitation. The upper, highly permeable sedimentary
section could be suitable for pump wells if hot water exists, but for deep geothermal drilling, the
sedimentary section will not matter as it is cased off.

3.6 Stress Field and Faults and Structural Considerations
The in situ stress regime influences wellbore stability, fracture stimulation, and reservoir
productivity. According to Lundstern and Zoback (2020), the Denver Basin is characterized by a
relaxed compressive regime with a strike-slip component. The relaxed stress regime may induce
a normal faulting regime (overburden stress > horizontal stresses), whereas the strike-slip is
associated with the observed wrench faults. The maximum horizontal stress (SHmax) in the
basin is oriented northwest-southeast, indicating the trend of main faults. This stress orientation
is critical for planning geothermal drilling, including fracture stimulation in EGS and well
trajectories, as it governs preferential fracture propagation directions. Faults represent both an
opportunity and a risk for geothermal development. They can enhance permeability and fluid
circulation in hydrothermal reservoirs but may complicate drilling, induce lost circulation, or
increase induced seismicity risks in EGS or closed-loop systems. Section 2.2 and Figure 6
suggest that the localized faults in the Wattenberg area are dominated by wrench faults. This
indicates the possibility of natural permeability enhancements from fault structures and informs
risk mitigation considerations in different stages of geothermal development.

loop system, based on the thermophysical property parameters described in Section 3 and the
literature data for granite and gneiss (Table 2). All the SBT simulations assumed a conduction-
only reservoir and constant material properties, with no advective flow in the formation.
Working fluids simulated included both water and supercritical CO₂. The properties of water and
supercritical CO₂ (e.g., temperature-dependent specific heat and thermal conductivity) were
sourced from MATLAB CoolProp tables (Bell et al. 2014). All properties were held constant in
each simulation but were varied across scenarios S13 and S14 to compare relative performance.
Each scenario was simulated over a 25-year lifespan with a temporal discretization of four steps,
assuming continuous injection and extraction. Boundary conditions were defined by a surface
temperature of 20°C and linear conductive gradients extending to the target reservoir depth.
Outlet temperatures were simulated at the production wellhead. The model domain was
constrained to a single lithology—granite or gneiss—across scenarios. A total of 14 U-loop
parametric scenario configurations (S1–S14) and a base case were simulated, consisting of

4.2.2 Results of Closed-Loop Simulations
To synthesize the modeling results and provide interpretability across varying conditions, we
developed several figures that visualize the thermal response of the system (Figure 14–Figure
18). These comparative graphics also support deeper thermodynamic and physical interpretations
of trends across scenarios and frame the current findings within the context of prior geothermal
simulation studies. The results have also been summarized in Table 5. The base case represents a
reference configuration with an average outlet temperature of 96.4°C and a final outlet
temperature of 66.7°C, yielding an average thermal output of 9.5 MWth and a final output of 4.4
MWth. This establishes the benchmark performance against which alternative scenarios can be
compared. Scenarios S3, S4, and S13 demonstrate nearly identical results to the baseline,
suggesting that the system behavior remains largely unchanged under these configurations.

Scenarios S5, S6, and S12 show incremental improvements relative to the baseline. They show
slightly higher outlet temperatures (101°C–111°C) and increased thermal output, in the range of
11–12 MWth average and 5.0–5.6 MWth final. These scenarios indicate modest enhancements in
reservoir heat exchange efficiency. A cluster of lower-performing cases is represented by S7–
S11. These scenarios exhibit reduced average outlet temperatures (77°C–90°C) and
corresponding decreases in sustained thermal output. In particular, S11 produces only 7.0 MWth
average and 3.3 MWth final output, substantially below baseline performance. S14 is a notable
outlier; the final outlet temperature remains moderate (71.2°C), but the thermal output is limited
to 3.6 MWth average and 2.7 MWth final. The significantly elevated outlet pressure of 154.5 bar
suggests excessive hydraulic impedance, which likely constrains fluid circulation and suppresses
thermal extraction.

By contrast, scenarios S2, S15, and S16 clearly outperform the baseline and all other cases. S2
yields an average outlet temperature of 160.9°C and a final temperature of 117.5°C,
corresponding to a sustained thermal output of 20.6 MWth average and 13.0 MWth final. S15

6 Conclusions
This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the geological, structural, and thermal
characteristics of the Denver Basin, with a specific focus on the Greater Wattenberg area, to
evaluate its suitability for deep geothermal development. The basin’s thick sedimentary sequence
overlying a granitic–gneissic basement, combined with a corrected geothermal gradient of
approximately 50°C/km, results in favorable subsurface temperatures that exceed 260°C at
depths of ~6 km. These high temperatures, influenced by the Wattenberg thermal anomaly, the
Colorado Mineral Belt, and conductive heat transfer in crystalline basement rocks, underline the
region’s strong potential for advanced geothermal applications.

Reservoir simulations conducted using the SBT tool demonstrate that both rock types of granite
and gneiss exhibit adequate heat retention and conductive properties to support long-term
geothermal operations. A series of closed-loop U-loop scenarios were evaluated, testing
variations in lateral spacing, number of laterals, injection temperatures, injection rates, and
thermal gradients. The optimized configuration, incorporating 12 deep laterals of significant
length, yielded the highest thermal performance among the closed-loop designs. When compared
to the closed-loop multilateral configuration, the optimized U-loop geometry offered competitive
thermal output suitable for techno-economic analysis in GEOPHIRES. Although most of the
Denver Basin lacks the natural permeability required for hydrothermal systems, the low-
permeability crystalline basement is well suited for closed-loop and EGS concepts. The regional
stress field—characterized by a relaxed compressive to strike-slip regime along with wrench-
fault structures may locally enhance permeability, but overall conditions favor engineered rather
than naturally convective systems.

Techno-economic evaluation using GEOPHIRES further contextualizes the viability of these
technologies. Closed-loop configurations achieved moderate electricity production
approximately 5 MWe, production temperatures of ~207 °C and LCOE of $151.1/MWh.
Sensitivity analysis indicated that thermal conductivty and horizontal drilling length have the
greatest influence on cost-effectiveness, whereas vertical drilling costs are less impactful. EGS
configurations consistently outperformed closed-loop systems, delivering net electricity outputs
of 8.46 MWe, production temperatures of 245°C. and LCOE ($80.7/MWh) demonstrating
superior economic and thermal performance. While EGS introduces technical and
geomechanical risks such as induced seismicity and fluid loss these can be mitigated through
careful operational design, enabling higher heat extraction from the stimulated reservoir volume.

These findings provide a quantitative basis for guiding the design and selection of geothermal
system architectures in future deployment efforts. The key takeaways from this study are the
following:

 • EGS systems represent the upper boundary of geothermal heat extraction potential under
 favorable geological conditions but are associated with increased technical uncertainty
 and induced seismicity risk due to their reliance on stimulation and engineered fracture
 network circulation.
 • The closed loop multilateral geometric configuration exhibits superior performance
 among closed-loop systems, primarily due to its extensive lateral development and
 favorable flow distribution, which allow for efficient thermal energy harvesting.

 32

 • U-loop systems, while structurally simpler and potentially more cost-effective in certain
 geologic contexts, may underperform relative to closed-Loop multilateral and EGS
 system designs in the context of power generation, unless they are modified to include
 additional lateral branches or increased lateral lengths. Regardless, they do not compare
 to the multilateral geometric configuration and EGS in the realm of power generation.

In summary, the Denver Basin exhibits strong potential for both closed-loop and EGS
geothermal development. EGS offers the highest thermal and economic performance, whereas
closed-loop systems provide a scalable alternative with lower operational complexity and
environmental footprint. The integration of geological, reservoir, and techno-economic data
confirms the feasibility of next-generation geothermal deployment in the Greater Wattenberg
area and provides a robust foundation for field-scale system design and optimization.
