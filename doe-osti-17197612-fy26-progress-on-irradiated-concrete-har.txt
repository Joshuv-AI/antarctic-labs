 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
In fiscal year 2026 (FY26), the Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) research team planned next
steps for harvesting irradiated concrete at two candidate facilities: the National Research Universal
(NRU) research reactor at Chalk River Laboratories in Canada and the decommissioned Kewaunee
Power Station (KPS) pressurized water reactor (PWR) site in Wisconsin. Based on FY26 evaluation,
concrete harvesting at KPS will not be pursued. This report documents the basis for that decision while
summarizing progress at both sites. The work focused on defining a practical decision path at each site,
reducing key uncertainties, and staying aligned with site priorities and schedules. This report is limited
to FY26 concrete harvesting progress at NRU and KPS. FY26 progress related to the separate Ringhals
Nuclear Power Plant opportunity in Sweden will be reported separately later this year.
At NRU, FY26 work clarified that shield plug retrieval is feasible but operationally complex and must be
integrated into Canadian Nuclear Laboratories (CNL) work planning. The ORNL research team reviewed
CNL’s radiation transport packages and confirmed that strong gamma dose gradients exist across plug
depth and that the dose rate profile varies by beamline location, and these findings can guide targeted
sampling if retrieval proceeds. FY26 outcomes pointed to three near-term needs that should be addressed
before committing to plug extraction and shipment: obtain an unirradiated NRU concrete specimen for
baseline paste characterization; pursue limited, measurement-based checks to build confidence in modeled
dose attribution where feasible; and define a staged approach for receiving and characterizing a small
number of high-value specimens tied to distinct exposure levels. These activities will reduce risk.
At KPS, FY26 work established the operational constraints that would govern any concrete biological
shield coring campaign, including schedule protection, funding boundaries, access windows, and
documentation requirements for traceable sample provenance. In parallel, the ORNL research team
screened an unirradiated concrete specimen using micro X-ray fluorescence mapping to gauge whether
KPS concrete is susceptible to radiation-induced volumetric expansion (RIVE). The screening showed that
Ca-bearing aggregates dominate the coarse fraction, which suggests reduced likelihood of pronounced
silica-driven RIVE compared with silica-rich systems. When considered together with the operational
and radiological constraints, these results indicate limited scientific and practical value relative to
implementation burden. Accordingly, it has been decided that concrete harvesting at KPS will not be
pursued.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Harvesting irradiated concrete from reactor biological shields can provide rare, high-value material
for studying long-term radiation effects in realistic service conditions. These samples support direct
measurement of irradiation-relevant degradation mechanisms and provide benchmarks for models used
to interpret dose, microstructural change, and property evolution in thick concrete sections. Because
harvesting activities occur within active decommissioning programs, the work must also be performed in
such a way that minimizes disruption to site schedules, maintains strict traceability of specimen location
and orientation, and accounts for radiological handling and shipping constraints.
This report summarizes fiscal year 2026 (FY26) progress on a concrete harvesting effort focused on two
facilities with distinct opportunities and constraints: the National Research Universal (NRU) research
reactor at Chalk River Laboratories and the Kewaunee Power Station (KPS) commercial pressurized water
reactor (PWR) site. FY26 work involved practical planning and early screening activities that will inform
whether harvesting should proceed and, if so, how to execute it responsibly.
At NRU, the concrete harvesting opportunity is associated with irradiated shield plugs in the concrete
biological shield (CBS). FY26 work focused on coordination with Canadian Nuclear Laboratories
(CNL), review of available radiation transport analyses, and definition of risk-reducing near-term actions
that improve confidence in dose attribution and specimen selection before any plug extraction and
shipment. At KPS, FY26 work focused on two parallel needs: (1) establishing the operational constraints
associated with a potential CBS coring effort under the decommissioning schedule and (2) screening
an unirradiated concrete sample to assess whether aggregate chemistry suggests a reasonable chance of
observing irradiation-driven degradation signals in harvested material. These activities supported a final
determination that concrete harvesting at KPS will not be pursued. The sections that follow document
the facility context, FY26 activities and findings, and the recommended next steps for NRU and the
documented basis for the decision not to pursue concrete harvesting at KPS. Note that this report addresses
only the NRU and KPS opportunities. FY26 progress for the separate Ringhals Nuclear Power Plant
opportunity in Sweden will be presented in a later report.

 2. NATIONAL RESEARCH UNIVERSAL (NRU) REACTOR
2.1 FACILITY OVERVIEW
The NRU reactor was a high-power, multi-purpose research reactor at Chalk River Laboratories in Ontario,
Canada. The site is owned by Atomic Energy of Canada Limited (AECL) and managed and operated by
CNL; in 2014, the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission (CNSC) transferred the site operating licenses
from AECL to CNL under the Nuclear Safety and Control Act [1, 2]. NRU first achieved criticality on
November 3, 1957, and was designed to provide a high neutron flux for irradiation, isotope production, and
experimental research. In its original configuration, NRU used heavy water (i.e., 𝐷 2 𝑂) as both coolant and
moderator and employed natural uranium metal fuel bars clad with aluminum. Design documentation from
early operation describes a large, high-flux core with extensive irradiation capability, including in-pile
experimental loops and the ability to replace fuel during full-power operation [3]. Early design basis
documentation reports a maximum thermal neutron flux of approximately 3 × 1014 n · cm−2 · s−1 [3]. This
flux supported both high-throughput irradiation services and neutron beam science. The reactor ultimately
served as the neutron source for Canada’s National Neutron Scattering Program, with a suite of six neutron
beamlines arranged around the core region and associated shielding structures [4].
NRU’s history includes multiple fuel and operating configuration transformations driven by mission needs
and evolving fuel cycle constraints. A mid-life transition occurred in November 1963, when the reactor
was shut down for conversion from natural uranium to highly enriched uranium (HEU) fuel. Following
conversion, operating power was reduced to approximately 60 MWt (from an original level of ∼220
MWt) while maintaining comparable thermal neutron flux [5]. Later, NRU’s conversion to low-enriched
uranium (LEU) fuel began in 1991 and was completed in 1993. AECL documentation describes LEU
fuel fabrication at Chalk River and extensive operating experience with uranium silicide dispersion fuel,
including irradiation of hundreds of fuel rods and thousands of fuel elements in NRU operation [6].
NRU produced isotopes at an industrial scale throughout its operating life. CNL identifies NRU as a major
global supplier of molybdenum-99 and documents production of a broad isotope portfolio, including
cobalt-60, carbon-14, iridium-192, xenon-133, iodine-131, and iodine-125, for nuclear medicine and
industrial applications [7]. Independent industry reporting also highlights NRU’s role as a leading Mo-99
source and notes that the reactor operated at power levels up to 135 MWt during its later years [8].

2.2 DECOMMISSIONING STATUS AND SCHEDULE
The CNSC defines the current post-shutdown period as a transition to permanent safe shutdown under
the ten-year Chalk River Nuclear Research and Test Establishment Operating License (NRTEOL)
(i.e., April 1, 2018 through March 31, 2028). Following this period, CNL will prepare the facility for
decommissioning [9]. CNL is carrying out post-operational activities in defined phases to position NRU
for dismantlement [9, 10]. The reactor is presently in a clean-out phase, during which personnel are
characterizing materials and equipment used in reactor operations [10]. This phase creates an opportunity
to harvest irradiated materials from NRU systems and structures. Over its operating life, NRU exposed
materials and components to a heavy-water environment across wide temperature ranges (35°C to 310°C)
and varied neutron flux conditions [11]. These service conditions provide a basis for examining long-term
irradiation effects in a range of materials, including metals, concretes, polymers, cables, and reactor
internals [11].
Decommissioning of NRU depends on the broader waste management and remediation infrastructure at
Chalk River. CNL continues to carry out decommissioning and environmental cleanup activities across

 2

the site, including demolition of legacy facilities and removal of hazardous and radioactive waste between
2015 and 2025 [12]. During this period, the CNSC amended the Chalk River NRTEOL to authorize
construction of the Near Surface Disposal Facility (NSDF), an engineered disposal mound designed
to permanently contain up to 1 million cubic meters of solid low-level radioactive waste, along with
associated treatment and support systems [13]. Although the NSDF serves the entire site rather than NRU
alone, it directly affects disposal pathways for the large volumes of low-level material expected from NRU
decommissioning [13].

2.3 FY26 PROGRESS
In FY26, the Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) research team focused on maintaining close
technical coordination with CNL, strengthening confidence in available radiation transport results,
and defining near-term actions that support harvesting decisions while remaining consistent with CNL
decommissioning priorities. Work proceeded along two main tracks: (1) clarifying feasibility and
operational constraints through direct engagement with CNL in October 2025 and (2) reviewing the CNL
technical packages transmitted in May and December 2025 to determine the extent to which the existing
transport analyses can reliably support experimental planning.

2.3.1 October 2025 CNL meeting
On October 29, 2025, ORNL and CNL held a technical discussion to address practical considerations
for accessing NRU shield plugs, sequencing activities to avoid interference with facility priorities, and
defining the information needed to support a clearly documented harvesting plan. Some key topics are as
follows.
 • Access and handling constraints for shield plugs. CNL outlined practical limitations associated
 with removing plugs from the CBS and stressed that any retrieval effort must be integrated into
 ongoing facility work planning. Plug removal differs significantly from routine coring and may
 require specialized handling, shielding, and dedicated staging space for cutting operations.

 3

 • Staged approach to reduce commitment. Both teams agreed that early efforts should concentrate
 on verification and planning tasks that do not commit the project to full plug extraction. These tasks
 include confirming plug identifiers, accessibility, and current installation or storage status, as well as
 assessing onsite cutting capability and required radiological controls.
 • Onsite size reduction and shipping practicality. The discussion addressed the likely need
 to segment plugs onsite to simplify transport and match specimen geometry to available
 post-irradiation examination capabilities. Key considerations include minimizing mechanical
 damage during cutting, preserving specimen orientation and depth information, and preparing
 packaging that meets regulatory transport requirements.
 • Specimen traceability. CNL emphasized the need to preserve a clear record linking each specimen
 to its original location, depth, and orientation. This traceability is essential for relating observed
 material changes to modeled dose gradients and for benchmarking transport calculations.
 • Dose model confidence and validation. While modeled dose fields support initial planning, the
 teams agreed that additional confidence-building steps are needed before selecting high-value
 irradiated regions for harvesting. Measurement-based checks to corroborate modeled fields were
 discussed, and their implementation will be subject to feasibility and radiological constraints.
 • Near-term coordination. The teams identified immediate actions, including follow-up technical
 exchanges, refinement of transport verification needs, and identification of an unirradiated concrete
 specimen for baseline characterization.
The October 2025 discussion reinforced that the NRU opportunity remains technically compelling but
operationally complex. Near-term efforts will therefore focus on reducing execution risk and improving
confidence in the dose estimates before commitment to shipment of irradiated concrete. Immediate
priorities include confirming plug identifiers and accessibility, defining a practical onsite segmentation
concept, identifying feasible validation approaches for dose estimates, and establishing baseline material
characterization.
2.3.2 Receipt and Review of CNL Technical Packages
CNL provided technical files that enabled an internal ORNL review of the radiation transport methodology
and supported development of targeted follow-up questions. The documentation shows that gamma
dose varies significantly by plug location and by depth from the inner face of the CBS. In one beamline
plug region, gamma dose is very high near the inner surface and decreases rapidly with depth, whereas
other beamline locations exhibit substantially lower peak dose. This pattern is consistent with expected
attenuation behavior in thick biological shielding. The information allowed the ORNL research team to
confirm meaningful exposure gradients across plug depth, preliminarily rank candidate plug locations
by relative exposure, and identify specific technical issues that require clarification before committing to
harvesting.
2.3.3 Radiation Transport Review
ORNL radiation transport subject matter experts conducted a screening review of the CNL methodology
and results to evaluate whether the reported gamma dose levels and gradients are reasonable at an order
of magnitude level and to identify key open questions. The review highlighted the need for clearer
documentation of Monte Carlo convergence and uncertainty metrics, clarification of regions flagged as low
accuracy, and confirmation of whether variance reduction or other efficiency techniques were applied. The
team also identified opportunities to strengthen confidence in the calculations through measurement-based
validation, if feasible. Potential approaches include activation-based checks on selected metallic
components associated with the plug assembly; these approaches are subject to confirmation of material
composition and practical sampling and counting constraints.

 4

2.3.4 Baseline Concrete Characterization
In FY26, the ORNL research team identified the need to obtain an unirradiated NRU concrete specimen
for laboratory characterization. This baseline specimen will support confirmation of binder and aggregate
chemistry and guide selection of paste-focused test methods that are well suited to long-term aging and
irradiation-related mechanisms. The team selected characterization methods based on prior experience
with thick-section concrete investigations in Japan, where researchers evaluated depth-dependent paste
properties using complementary thermal, mineralogical, and microstructural techniques [15]. The
immediate objective is to establish a well-documented unirradiated reference dataset for NRU concrete
paste—including measurements of bound water, portlandite content, amorphous hydrate fraction, and key
microstructural descriptors—before proceeding with a more complex irradiated material campaign.

2.4 RECOMMENDATIONS AND NEXT STEPS
FY26 results define a path forward that reduces technical uncertainty and keeps the harvesting effort
aligned with CNL logistics and planning constraints. The following actions are recommended.
 1. Obtain an unirradiated NRU concrete specimen and complete baseline paste characterization.
 Work with CNL to secure an unirradiated concrete core or equivalent specimen from a
 well-documented location. Conduct baseline testing focused on cement paste condition and
 variability using methods that are sensitive to long-term paste evolution in thick concrete.
 Recommended techniques include the following.
 • Quantitative paste mineralogy and hydrate inventory by X-ray diffraction (XRD).
 • Thermal analysis (e.g., thermogravimetric analysis) to quantify bound water and portlandite
 trends.
 • Microstructural imaging of paste and paste–aggregate interfaces using scanning electron
 microscopy (SEM) (back-scattered electron (BSE) imaging and energy-dispersive X-ray
 spectroscopy (EDS) as needed).
 Document the baseline dataset in sufficient detail to support direct comparison with any future
 irradiated specimens.
 2. Assess the feasibility of measurement-based validation of modeled dose fields.
 Develop a validation approach that minimizes disruption to site operations. Determine whether
 suitable metal components are available near the plug region of interest, confirm their composition,
 and evaluate whether activation products can be measured within handling and radiological
 constraints. If feasible, define a limited validation set tied directly to plug geometry and exposure
 history to strengthen confidence in dose attribution.
 3. Maintain readiness to receive and characterize irradiated plug material.
 When CNL access and logistics permit, implement a staged harvesting approach that limits scope to
 a small number of high-value specimens that represent distinct exposure levels. Define specimen
 geometry and depth targets that capture the steep near-face gradient and specify packaging,
 shipment, and documentation requirements that preserve orientation and location data. This
 approach will minimize handling burden while ensuring that received material can be interpreted
 reliably against the modeled dose gradient.

 3. KEWAUNEE POWER STATION (KPS)
3.1 FACILITY OVERVIEW
KPS is a permanently shut down and de-fueled commercial nuclear power plant located in the Town of
Carlton, Kewaunee County, Wisconsin, on the western shore of Lake Michigan. The facility consists of a
two-loop PWR designed by Westinghouse Electric Corporation and licensed at a maximum thermal power
of approximately 1,772 MWt [16]. The plant had a gross electrical output of about 590 MWe. Pioneer
Service and Engineering Company was the original architect–engineer for balance-of-plant design and
construction [17].
In 2011, the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) approved a license renewal extending the
operating term to December 21, 2033, although the plant later shut down for economic reasons [16].
As part of life extension activities documented in NRC records, KPS replaced its steam generators in
2001 with Westinghouse Model 54F units [17]. Following shutdown, site personnel completed fuel
management on an accelerated schedule compared with many decommissioning sites. NAC International
expanded the on-site Independent Spent Fuel Storage Installation (ISFSI) and installed 24 MAGNASTOR
dry cask systems for interim spent fuel storage. In total, personnel loaded 887 spent fuel assemblies
into MAGNASTOR canisters, and the remaining assemblies are stored in legacy NUHOMS (NUclear
HOriZontal Modular Storage) systems [18].

3.2 DECOMMISSIONING TIMELINE
KPS permanently ceased power operations on May 7, 2013. Dominion Energy Kewaunee, Inc. certified
permanent removal of all fuel from the reactor vessel on May 14, 2013 and transferred the fuel to the
spent fuel pool. Pursuant to 10 CFR 50.82(a)(2), these certifications terminated the plant’s authority
to operate the reactor or retain fuel in the vessel [16, 19]. In February 2013, the licensee submitted its
Post-Shutdown Decommissioning Activities Report (PSDAR), identifying SAFSTOR (safe storage) as
the selected decommissioning strategy, as required by 10 CFR 50.82(a)(4) [19]. Under SAFSTOR, the
facility remains in a safe, monitored condition prior to final dismantlement. The licensee submitted a
revised PSDAR (Revision 1) in April 2014 [16]. By 2017, site personnel had transferred all spent fuel to
the on-site ISFSI, and they completed spent fuel pool activities, including rack removal, in 2018 [16].
The ownership and decommissioning strategy shifted during 2021–2022. On March 31, 2022, the
NRC approved the indirect transfer of Renewed Facility Operating License DPR-43 and the general
ISFSI license under 10 CFR Part 72 from Dominion to Kewaunee Solutions, Inc., a subsidiary of
EnergySolutions, LLC [20]. The NRC concluded that the transferee met applicable qualification
requirements and imposed conditions to ensure continued implementation of the quality assurance
program during transition [21]. EnergySolutions subsequently stated its intent to pursue prompt and safe
decommissioning [22]. In January 2024, the NRC granted an exemption from 10 CFR 50.82(a)(8)(i)(A),
allowing use of the KPS nuclear decommissioning trust fund for site restoration activities without prior
notification, subject to specified conditions [19]. Supporting analysis for the exemption identified 2055 as
the anticipated license termination year under a decontamination and dismantlement (DECON) scenario
[23].
As of February 10, 2026, the NRC site status page reports that major dismantlement activities began
in 2022 and are scheduled to conclude by 2055, including decommissioning of the on-site ISFSI [16,
20]. This schedule reflects a common constraint in US decommissioning: final site closure depends on
establishing an off-site disposal pathway for spent nuclear fuel and any remaining greater-than-Class-C

 6

(GTCC) waste. Under 10 CFR Part 61, GTCC waste exceeds Class C low-level waste limits and requires
deep geologic disposal rather than emplacement in commercial low-level waste facilities [24]. As a result,
even after removal of major structures such as the reactor and turbine buildings, license termination may
depend on resolution of fuel and GTCC disposal [23]. In parallel with decommissioning, EnergySolutions
announced plans in May 2025 to pursue an NRC Early Site Permit for potential new nuclear generation
at the Kewaunee site, in collaboration with WEC Energy Group [25]. In January 2026, the company
indicated its intent to submit a major licensing action application by June 2028 [26]. These activities
remain separate from the ongoing decommissioning program.

3.3 FY26 PROGRESS
In FY26, the ORNL research team evaluated the feasibility and potential value of concrete harvesting
at KPS through two primary activities: (1) development of a notional harvesting plan consistent with
decommissioning constraints and (2) screening of an unirradiated concrete sample to assess expected
irradiation-induced degradation. These activities were conducted to support a final go/no-go decision.

3.3.1 Stakeholder Coordination and Planning
On October 7, 2025, ORNL met with EnergySolutions and Dominion representatives to clarify
expectations and define constraints that govern any potential harvesting effort.
Key outcomes include the following.

 7

• Protect the decommissioning schedule. Harvesting activities must not affect the decommissioning
  critical path. The ORNL research team would need to incorporate contingency measures to avoid
  schedule impacts if access or staffing constraints arise.
• Funding constraints. Decommissioning trust funds cannot support research sampling. The ORNL
  research team must secure independent funding and finalize a complete harvesting plan before
  execution.
• Required planning timeline. EnergySolutions requested a finalized and fully funded harvesting
  plan by approximately October 2027, roughly six months before reactor vessel removal, to allow for
  integration into work packages.
• Access constraints vary by location. Outer diameter coring may be possible earlier, whereas inner
  diameter access depends on completion of reactor pressure vessel (RPV) segmentation and removal.
• Cavity conditions. The reactor cavity will be flooded beginning in 2026. The activity will last
  approximately 13 months and will restrict work in the cavity region during that period.
• Inner diameter access window. Planned dry segmentation beginning around Q2 2028 will provide
  a potential inner diameter coring window that lasts through approximately Q1 2029.
• Core size considerations. Four-inch diameter cores are preferred for mechanical testing; two-inch
  cores are acceptable for characterization. Final dimensions must account for aggregate size to ensure
  representative specimens.
• Core length constraints. Inner diameter cores on the order of 20 in. may be feasible depending on
  physical and scheduling limitations.
• Sampling density. A preliminary plan included six coring locations with three replicate cores per
  location (approximately 18 total cores), spanning beltline and other near-cavity regions as well as
  containment locations suitable for unirradiated reference material.
• Unirradiated reference material. The ORNL research team identified an unirradiated concrete
  sample as a near-term priority to support screening and refine the harvesting decision framework.
• Location selection. Site personnel will assist in identifying constructible coring locations. Final
  selections must reflect physical constraints and work control requirements.
• Coordinate traceability. The plan must preserve precise coring coordinates and elevations so that
  transport calculations and data interpretation remain tied to actual exposure history.
• Work control. Execution will require detailed work orders specifying exact coring locations and
  stepwise procedures to ensure traceability across organizations.
• Technical points of contact. The ORNL research team would designate primary technical leads to
  streamline communication, document exchange, and work package development.
• Schedule transparency. A more detailed and current decommissioning schedule is needed to refine
  access windows and anticipated durations.
• Engage dismantlement leadership early. Early coordination with the site task lead responsible for
  coring and dismantling will confirm feasibility, tooling needs, and duration.
• Contingency planning. If direct coring cannot align with schedule constraints, site personnel may
  set aside a segment of the CBS for later coring to preserve flexibility.

 • Shipping and storage. The site indicated that it can fabricate a dedicated container to support

 • Radiological considerations. The ORNL research team must evaluate radiation levels and potential

The meeting clarified that, while concrete harvesting at KPS may be technically feasible, it would require
tight integration with site operations and would introduce nontrivial schedule, funding, and logistical
constraints. These considerations contributed to the final determination that harvesting at KPS will not

3.3.2 Screening Characterization
Unirradiated KPS concrete was characterized to establish a baseline for evaluating potential
irradiation-induced degradation and to inform the harvesting decision. Micro-X-ray fluorescence
(XRF) mapping provides rapid, spatially resolved information on aggregate chemistry, which strongly
influences radiation-induced volumetric expansion (RIVE). Because aggregate mineralogy governs
expansion behavior under fast neutron exposure, early screening helps determine whether irradiated
concrete is likely to yield meaningful degradation signals.
Figures 1 and 2 show the elemental maps for the unirradiated KPS concrete. In both figures, panel (a)
presents the sulfur (S) map, which is identical in each set. Sulfur appears primarily in the cement paste
and interfacial regions rather than in coarse aggregates, allowing for clear discrimination between paste
and aggregate phases for segmentation.

Figure 1. Micro-XRF maps of unirradiated KPS concrete – calcium-focused set. (a) sulfur map used
for paste–aggregate discrimination, (b) raw calcium intensity map, and (c) classified aggregate map based
 on dominant elemental signature for area quantification.

 9

Figure 2. Micro-XRF maps of unirradiated KPS concrete – silicon-focused set. (a) sulfur map, (b) raw
  silicon intensity map, and (c) classified aggregate map based on dominant elemental signature for area
 quantification.

Panel (b) in Figures 1 and 2 displays the raw elemental intensity maps for calcium (Ca) and silicon (Si),
respectively. Bright regions in the Ca map indicate calcium-rich aggregates, whereas bright regions in the
Si map indicate silicon-bearing aggregates. Visual comparison shows that Ca-bearing aggregates occupy a
larger fraction of the coarse aggregate area than Si-bearing aggregates. Panel (c) in Figures 1 and 2 shows
the classified aggregate map derived from segmentation of the elemental intensity maps. Unlike panel (b),
which shows raw continuous signal, panel (c) represents a processed interpretation in which aggregate
particles are assigned according to their dominant elemental signature. This classification enables
quantitative area estimation. The results indicate that Ca-bearing aggregates comprise approximately
69% of the coarse aggregate area in the Ca-focused map, whereas Si-bearing aggregates comprise
approximately 34% of the coarse aggregate area in the Si-focused map (paste excluded). Because these
values are derived from separate elemental classifications, they are not expected to sum to 100%. This
distribution confirms that calcium-rich aggregates dominate the coarse fraction.
Because silica-rich aggregates such as quartz and certain feldspars are more susceptible to
irradiation-induced expansion, the predominance of Ca-bearing aggregates suggests a lower likelihood of
pronounced RIVE-driven degradation at KPS relative to silica-dominated systems. These findings do not
rule out irradiation effects, but they indicate that the aggregate composition is less aligned with concrete
systems known to exhibit significant silica-driven expansion. Confirmatory XRD and petrographic
analyses would further refine mineralogical understanding; however, the current results are sufficient to
support the decision not to pursue concrete harvesting at KPS.

3.4 DECISION AND CLOSEOUT
FY26 results establish a clear basis for decision-making regarding KPS. The combination of: (1)
operational constraints associated with decommissioning activities and (2) screening results indicating
limited susceptibility to RIVE demonstrate that the expected scientific value of harvested CBS material
does not justify the required operational and radiological effort. Based on these findings, it has been
determined that concrete harvesting at KPS will not be pursued. This site is therefore considered closed
with respect to the current concrete harvesting program. No further concrete harvesting-related planning or
analysis activities are recommended.

 4. CONCLUSIONS
FY26 work advanced the concrete harvesting effort by clarifying what can realistically be done at the
two candidate sites. At NRU, coordination with CNL and review of the available radiation transport
documentation confirmed that shield plug regions exhibit meaningful gamma dose gradients that can
support targeted sampling. However, it was determined during discussions in FY26 that plug retrieval and
size reduction are not routine operations and must be staged and integrated into site work planning. The
recommended path forward is to reduce uncertainty before committing to plug extraction and shipment:
secure an unirradiated NRU concrete specimen and complete baseline paste characterization; pursue
limited validation checks, where feasible, to strengthen confidence in modeled dose attribution; and define
a staged plan for receiving and characterizing a small number of high-value irradiated specimens tied to
distinct exposure levels and preserved provenance. At KPS, FY26 stakeholder coordination defined the
operational constraints associated with any potential CBS coring campaign, including schedule protection,
funding boundaries, access conditions, and traceability requirements. Screening of an unirradiated
specimen using micro-XRF showed that Ca-bearing aggregates dominate the coarse fraction, indicating a
reduced likelihood of a strong silica-driven RIVE response. When considered alongside the operational
and radiological constraints, these findings indicate limited scientific and practical value for harvested
concrete. Accordingly, it has been determined that concrete harvesting at KPS will not be pursued, and
this opportunity is considered closed. The findings and observations generated by FY26 work support a
staged and resource-efficient approach focused on advancing harvesting activities at NRU while formally
closing out the KPS opportunity.
