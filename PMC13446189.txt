Mucopolysaccharidosis type IIIA and IIIC phenotypic progression: A case series
Authors: Paola Naal-Chan, Ermilo Echeverria-Ortegon, Jary-Davis Couoh-Castañeda, Jose-Luis Millet-Herrera, Fabiola-Concepcion Solis-Baeza, Myrna-Edith Perez-Garcia, Jorge-Santiago Rivera-Lavalle, Addy-Manuela Castillo-Espinola, Olga-Berenice Reyes-Flores, Felix-Julian Campos-Garcia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMucopolysaccharidosis type III (MPS III), or Sanfilippo syndrome, is a group of rare autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorders caused by deficiency of enzymes involved in heparan sulfate degradation, encoded by SGSH (type A), NAGLU (type B), HGSNAT (type C), or GNS (type D). Progressive lysosomal accumulation leads to neurodegeneration, cognitive decline, behavioral disturbances, and premature death. Despite a global pooled birth prevalence of ∼0.76 per 100,000 live births, MPS III remains substantially underdiagnosed in Latin America. To date, only a single MPS IIIB case has been molecularly confirmed in Mexico, with no reported cases of MPS IIIA or IIIC.MethodsTwo adolescent patients with progressive neurodevelopmental deterioration were evaluated at a tertiary genetics center in Yucatán, Mexico. Assessment included structured clinical history, neuroimaging, conventional karyotyping, and whole-exome sequencing (WES), with variants classified per ACMG/AMP guidelines.ResultsCase 1 (MPS IIIC): a 15-year-old male presented with speech delay, progressive loss of ambulation and self-feeding, sleep disturbances, and hyperactivity. WES identified a homozygous pathogenic HGSNAT variant (NM_152419.3): c.234 + 1G > A, a previously described Iberian-associated allele. Case 2 (MPS IIIA): a 15-year-old female presented with neurological regression from age 4, refractory generalized seizures, and profound intellectual disability. WES identified two compound heterozygous SGSH variants (NM_000199.5): c.233 T > G (p.Leu78Arg) and c.368 A > G (p.Lys123Arg), both classified as likely pathogenic and, to our knowledge, not previously reported in the literature or genomic databases.ConclusionsThese cases are consistent with the first reported diagnoses of MPS IIIA and MPS IIIC in Mexico, supported by concordant clinical and molecular findings and indicating the presence of at least three MPS III subtypes in the country. The two SGSH variants represent candidate novel alleles, with enzymatic and functional confirmation pending. The identification of these variants and of a recurrent Iberian-associated HGSNAT allele in non-consanguineous patients underscores the likely underdiagnosis of MPS III in Mexico and the diagnostic value of WES in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Sanfilippo syndrome, or mucopolysaccharidosis type III (MPS III), is a group of rare autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorders caused by deficiencies in enzymes that degrade heparan sulfate, a ubiquitous glycosaminoglycan [9]. The resulting intralysosomal accumulation produces progressive cellular dysfunction that predominantly affects the central nervous system [6], making MPS III the most neurologically aggressive of the mucopolysaccharidoses [15].

Four subtypes are recognized, each caused by deficiency of a specific lysosomal enzyme: MPS IIIA (OMIM: 252900; SGSH), MPS IIIB (OMIM: 252920; NAGLU), MPS IIIC (OMIM: 252930; HGSNAT), and MPS IIID (OMIM: 252940; GNS) [1], [3], [4], [7]. Despite these distinct molecular mechanisms, all subtypes share a clinically similar phenotype of progressive neurocognitive decline. The global birth prevalence is estimated at approximately 0.76 per 100,000 live births, varying by region and subtype, with MPS IIIA the most common and typically most severe form and MPS IIIC and IIID considerably rarer [6], [9], [16].

Clinically, MPS III follows a progressive neurodegenerative continuum: early developmental and speech delay, followed by behavioral disturbances and cognitive decline, and ultimately severe motor impairment and loss of independence [7], [9]. Seizures are common, particularly in MPS IIIA, and survival is typically limited to the second decade in severe cases. Somatic features, when present, are generally less prominent than in other MPS disorders [10]. Diagnosis rests on biochemical testing and molecular confirmation, with whole-exome sequencing (WES) increasingly enabling diagnosis in resource-limited settings [9].

Data on MPS III in Latin America remain scarce; in Mexico, only a single molecularly confirmed case of MPS IIIB has been reported to date [4], although diagnostic capacity and case reporting for congenital metabolic diseases are beginning to expand [13], [14]. We report the first clinically and molecularly characterized cases of MPS IIIA and MPS IIIC in Mexico, describing their phenotypic evolution and molecular findings and their implications for diagnosis in underrepresented populations.

Methods

Clinical evaluation

Two patients presenting with suspected lysosomal storage disease were assessed at the Department of Genetics of the General Hospital “Dr. Agustín O'Horan” (Mérida, Yucatán, Mexico), followed from 2022 to 2025. Clinical evaluation consisted of a structured interview covering pregnancy and perinatal history, attainment and subsequent regression of psychomotor developmental milestones, functional status, behavioral manifestations (including hyperactivity, aggressiveness, and sleep disturbances), and the chronological progression of neurological symptoms. Physical examination included measurement of anthropometric parameters (weight, height, and head circumference expressed as Z-scores for age and sex), systematic assessment of craniofacial morphology with attention to dysmorphic features associated with MPS III. Musculoskeletal examination addressed joint range of motion and the presence of skeletal abnormalities. Ophthalmological and audiological assessments were performed as clinically indicated.

Neuroimaging

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed in both patients using a standard clinical protocol with T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and FLAIR sequences. Patient 1 underwent two cranial MRI examinations, at 11 and 14 years of age, allowing longitudinal assessment of disease progression. Findings on both studies included cortico-subcortical atrophy, periventricular gliosis, ventriculomegaly, and pituitary hyperplasia, with more pronounced changes observed on the second examination. Patient 2 underwent MRI at 10 and 14 years of age, with subcortical atrophy identified on both studies and progression documented on follow-up imaging. Neuroimaging reports were reviewed by a board-certified radiologist.

Ethical considerations

This study was reviewed and approved by the Research and Ethics Committee of the General Hospital “Dr. Agustín O'Horan” (approval number CI-0067-1-2026) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of both patients prior to initiation of any study-specific procedures, including genetic testing and publication of clinical photographs.

Molecular analysis

Molecular analysis. Genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral blood leukocytes, and WES was performed on a NovaSeq X platform (Illumina) at the Rare Disease Unit of the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México (UNAM), achieving ≥20× coverage for at least 99.5% of targeted regions. Reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38), and variants were called, annotated, and filtered using standard bioinformatic pipelines.

Variant prioritization and classification. Variants were prioritized based on each patient's clinical phenotype, family history, and results of prior investigations, following a phenotype-driven approach. Only variants considered clinically significant and relevant to the patient's presentation at the time of analysis were reported. Variant classification followed the standards and guidelines jointly established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) [11], [12]. In silico pathogenicity prediction was performed using multiple algorithms, REVEL, AlphaMissense, Mutation Taster, BayesDel. Population frequency of identified variants was assessed against the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v4.1) and the ClinVar, LOVD, and HGMD databases. Variants of interest were named according to HGVS nomenclature recommendations, using NM_152419.3 as the reference transcript for HGSNAT and NM_000199.5 for SGSH.

Cytogenetic analysis

Cytogenetic studies. Conventional G-banded karyotyping was performed on peripheral blood lymphocyte cultures for both patients following standard cytogenetic protocols. A minimum of 20 metaphases were analyzed per patient. Results are reported according to the International System for Human Cytogenomic Nomenclature (ISCN).

Results

Case 1: mucopolysaccharidosis type IIIC

After an initial period of near-normal early motor development, developmental arrest became evident from approximately 2 years of age, followed by progressive loss of previously acquired skills: speech was lost first (∼8 years), self-feeding by 12 years, and independent ambulation by 14 years. Aggressiveness and severe insomnia emerged from 12 years onward, and serial photographs documented progressive coarsening of the facial phenotype (Fig. 1). Case 2: mucopolysaccharidosis type IIIA

A 15-year-old female (Table 1) was referred at 14 years for intellectual disability with progressive neurological regression and refractory seizures. Written informed consent for publication was obtained from her legal guardians. In contrast to Case 1, early development was followed by neurological regression beginning at approximately 4 years, coinciding with the onset of generalized seizures that responded poorly to pharmacotherapy and ultimately required up to three concurrent antiepileptic agents for partial control. Cognitive deterioration progressed to profound intellectual disability with complete caregiver dependence. The facial phenotype coarsened progressively (Fig. 3). Karyotype was 46,XX[20], routine studies were unremarkable, and brain MRI showed subcortical atrophy with radiological progression between 10 and 14 years. Discussion

This series reports the first molecularly characterized cases of MPS IIIA and MPS IIIC in Mexico. Together with the previously reported MPS IIIB case, these findings establish that at least three of the four MPS III subtypes are present in the country and underscore the need for systematic diagnosis and surveillance of lysosomal storage disorders in Latin America. [4].

Clinical phenotype

The course of both patients is consistent with the natural history of MPS III in international cohorts. Patient 1 (MPS IIIC) showed early speech delay with initially normal motor milestones, followed by progressive regression with loss of acquired skills, hyperactivity, sleep disruption, and loss of ambulation in adolescence, closely mirroring the three-stage course described for MPS IIIC [7]. Speech loss at approximately 8 years is concordant with the large international cohort of Martins et al., in which the mean age of speech loss was 10 years [8]. Patient 2 (MPS IIIA) followed a similarly classical course, with regression and refractory seizures from around 4 years progressing to profound intellectual disability; epilepsy is a recognized and often treatment-resistant feature of MPS IIIA, reported in half of one Turkish cohort and accompanied by universal cognitive impairment and sleep disturbance in an Egyptian series [3], [10]. The subcortical atrophy with radiological progression seen in both patients is well documented across MPS III series [9], [16].

Both patients were born to non-consanguineous parents. Whereas consanguinity is a recognized risk factor associated with higher MPS III incidence in some populations [1], [10], its absence here suggests the responsible alleles segregate within the general Mexican population rather than arising through inbreeding, reinforcing the likelihood that MPS III is underdiagnosed in non-consanguineous families.

Molecular findings

Patient 1 carries a homozygous canonical splice-donor variant, HGSNAT (NM_152419.3):c.234 + 1G > A, classified as pathogenic (Table 2) and predicted to abolish functional enzyme activity through aberrant splicing [8]. This allele has been reported across geographically diverse populations, Spain, Portugal, Morocco, Algeria, Brazil, and South Korea, where haplotype analysis links the Ibero-Atlantic and North African cases to a shared founder haplotype, leading Martins et al. to propose dissemination from the Iberian Peninsula to Latin America during the colonial period [8]. Consistent with this, c.234 + 1G > A reaches its highest reported frequency in Latin American gnomAD data. Its identification in a homozygous, non-consanguineous patient from Yucatán plausibly reflects this Iberian-origin founder allele, a mechanism analogous to that proposed for the NAGLU p.(Arg234Cys) variant in Mexico's first MPS IIIB patient [4], and suggests c.234 + 1G > A may be a recurrent allele worth prioritizing in targeted screening for MPS IIIC in Mexico. Haplotype context was not assessed here, which remains a limitation.

Patient 2 harbors two compound heterozygous missense variants, SGSH (NM_000199.5):c.233 T > G (p.Leu78Arg) and c.368 A > G (p.Lys123Arg), both classified as likely pathogenic (Table 2). To our knowledge, neither has been previously reported, identifying them as candidate novel SGSH alleles. SGSH (17q25.3) displays marked allelic heterogeneity, with over 145 reported pathogenic variants and no single global hotspot, though population-specific enrichment occurs [3], [10]. Both substitutions affect residues predicted by multiple in silico tools to be functionally important, supporting their classification (Table 2). Functional studies would be required to establish the molecular consequences formally; however, in a patient with a classic MPS IIIA phenotype and biallelic SGSH disruption, the clinical and molecular evidence is concordant. The discovery of novel alleles in the first Mexican MPS IIIA case fits the broader pattern by which first investigations of MPS IIIA in a given population, as in Egypt and Turkey, recurrently reveal variants absent from predominantly European and East Asian databases, underscoring the value of comprehensive sequencing over panels restricted to known variants in underrepresented Latin American populations [3], [10].

MPS III, its context and implications for underdiagnosis

Before this report, confirmed MPS III in Mexico was limited to the single MPS IIIB patient noted above [4]. The present cases extend the same conclusions to MPS IIIA and IIIC: both were diagnosed by WES only at around 15 years, well after symptom onset. This delay mirrors global trends, mean diagnosis near 11 years in MPS IIIC [8] and 6.6–10.8 years across Turkish MPS IIIA/B cohorts [10], and is likely compounded in Mexico by limited access to enzyme assays, absent newborn screening, and frequent misattribution to more common conditions such as autism spectrum disorder or idiopathic intellectual disability, with which early MPS III overlaps. Progressive neurocognitive decline, behavioral disturbance, and sleep disruption following a period of normal development should prompt consideration of MPS III [9].

No confirmed MPS IIID case has been identified in Mexico, consistent with that subtype's extreme global rarity (∼1 in 1,000,000) [16]. Applying the pooled MPS III birth prevalence of 0.76 per 100,000 [6] to approximately 1.82 million annual births in Mexico [5] yields an estimated 14 affected births per year; assuming median survival into the second decade, this corresponds to a prevalent population on the order of one to a few hundred living patients nationwide, the majority likely undiagnosed. Although approximate, this estimate conveys the scale of the diagnostic gap.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. Biochemical enzymatic confirmation or glycosaminoglycan (GAG) analysis were not performed for either patient, as WES was the primary diagnostic modality available at the reporting institution. While the molecular findings are highly compelling and consistent with the clinical phenotypes, enzymatic confirmation of HGSNAT deficiency in Patient 1 and SGSH deficiency in Patient 2 would provide an additional layer of diagnostic certainty and is recommended as a complement to molecular diagnosis whenever feasible [1], [9]. Also, haplotype analysis was not performed for the variants identified in either patient, limiting the ability to draw definitive conclusions about population-specific founder effects or migration history. Such analysis would be particularly informative for c.234 + 1G > A in Patient 1 and for the novel SGSH variants in Patient 2. Furthermore, parental testing was not available for either patient, and the trans (biallelic) configuration of the compound heterozygous variants could therefore not be experimentally confirmed. Although the clinical, biochemical, and molecular features are fully concordant with the respective diagnoses, and each variant was independently classified as likely pathogenic, phasing remains inferred rather than confirmed; parental segregation analysis is recommended to establish compound heterozygosity definitively whenever family members are available. As a case series limited to two patients from a single institution in the Yucatan Peninsula, the findings cannot be generalized to represent the full spectrum of MPS III in Mexico. The possibility that the genetic landscape of MPS III in other Mexican regions, with their distinct demographic, ethnic, and historical profiles, differs substantially from what is observed in Yucatan cannot be excluded.

Future perspectives

The findings of this report have several implications for future research and clinical practice in Mexico and Latin America. The identification of c.234 + 1G > A as the causal allele in the first confirmed Mexican MPS IIIC patient suggests that targeted genotyping for this variant may be a cost-effective first-line screening strategy in Mexican patients suspected of MPS IIIC, potentially prior to comprehensive sequencing. Systematic enzymatic screening of patients presenting with progressive intellectual disability, behavioral regression, or neurodevelopmental deterioration of unclear etiology would facilitate earlier diagnosis, enable appropriate counseling for affected families, and reduce the substantial diagnostic delay currently observed. Importantly, recent initiatives are beginning to address this gap in Mexico. The Mexican Rare Disease Patient Registry (Registro Mexicano de Enfermedades Raras, ReMexER), an open, online, self-reporting patient registry, has reported its first-year results[2], and a single-center catalog of 605 molecularly confirmed rare diseases, the first such catalog in Mexico, was recently assembled from two decades of genetic diagnoses at a tertiary center affiliated with the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México (UNAM) [17]. Resources of this kind would enable the epidemiological characterization of MPS IIIC in the Mexican population, facilitate the identification of recurrent alleles such as c.234 + 1G > A, and assemble the patient cohorts necessary for future therapeutic trials. Finally, with several disease-modifying strategies for MPS III in various stages of clinical development, including gene therapy, enzyme replacement therapy and substrate reduction therapy, establishing confirmed diagnoses is increasingly urgent to ensure that Mexican patients are considered for emerging therapeutic opportunities as they become available [9], [10].

Parents perspective

Case 1 mother: As his mother, I first sensed something was wrong when he began losing his balance and falling from the smallest stumble; for fifteen years his diagnosis had been severe autism, but this did not seem normal to me. What alarmed me most was when he stopped eating, drinking, and sleeping for five days, which finally led us to the emergency department. The path to a diagnosis was very difficult. As a single mother and full-time caregiver, I cannot work, and I often felt the genetic testing he needed was beyond what I could afford. Receiving the diagnosis was overwhelming: I went alone to hear that, after fifteen years, he actually had a rare disease with little awareness in Mexico and no cure. I am still learning to come to terms with it. Yet finally having an answer brings relief and lets me focus on his health and quality of life rather than endless uncertainty. The empathy of his specialists has meant a great deal; they see not only a disease but my son, a child fighting every day. To other parents just receiving such a diagnosis, I would say: cry when you need to, but never stop fighting. Our children are far more than a diagnosis, they are people full of love and hope who transform our lives.

Case 2 parents: As the parents of our daughter, we first noticed something was wrong when she was about six and a half and had begun first grade; she was losing ground in her memory, behavior, and language, and that frightened us. Reaching a diagnosis was a long and exhausting road: despite many tests, there were no answers for a long time, and we felt deep frustration. When genetic testing finally gave us an explanation, our feelings were mixed, anxiety while awaiting the result, and relief at having a definite diagnosis at last. The news was devastating, yet it also brought a measure of peace, because we finally had a foundation from which to help her alongside her medical team. Our greatest wish is that the diagnosis had come earlier. Over time our daughter gradually lost her abilities, eventually becoming bedbound, and the toll on our family has been heavy; we have coped by working as a team and seeking support. The empathy of the specialists who cared for her has meant a great deal. To other families facing a similar situation, our message is: do not give up, keep fighting, these children are gifts who deserve love, care, and a dignified life. We hope sharing her case helps improve early recognition of this disease.

Conclusion

This case series documents the first cases consistent with MPS IIIA and MPS IIIC reported in Mexico, supported by concordant clinical and molecular findings and expanding the known spectrum of MPS III in the country beyond the single previously reported MPS IIIB case [4]. Both patients reached diagnosis in adolescence after a prolonged period of diagnostic uncertainty, reflecting the difficulty MPS III poses in settings where enzymatic and molecular confirmation is not readily available. The homozygous HGSNAT variant c.234 + 1G > A identified in the patient with MPS IIIC is a known pathogenic allele previously reported in Mediterranean, Iberian, and Latin American populations, consistent with introduction to Mexico through historical Spanish migration, as detailed in the Discussion. By contrast, the compound heterozygous SGSH variants c.233 T > G (p.Leu78Arg) and c.368 A > G (p.Lys123Arg) identified in the patient with MPS IIIA have not, to our knowledge, been previously reported, and represent candidate novel alleles pending the enzymatic, urinary GAG, and functional studies necessary to confirm their pathogenicity.

Together, these findings underscore the probable underdiagnosis of MPS III in Mexico and Latin America and highlight the value of WES as a diagnostic tool where biochemical testing infrastructure is not universally available [9]. They reinforce the need for systematic clinical surveillance, molecular characterization, and a national registry for rare lysosomal storage disorders, both to enable appropriate genetic counseling of affected families and to ensure that Mexican patients are considered for emerging disease-modifying therapies as they advance through clinical development [10].
