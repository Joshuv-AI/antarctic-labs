Beyond the Transplant: Chronic Kidney Disease in Pediatric Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplant Survivors‐A 20‐Year Single‐Center Cohort Study
Authors: Alcotzer Inbar, Talgam‐Horshi Efrat, Sidlik Muskatel Rakefet, Kraus Aviva, Yanir Assaf, Yahel Anat, Stein Jerry, Levy‐Erez Daniella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPediatric hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is curative for malignant and non‐malignant diseases, yet survivors remain at substantial risk for late renal complications. Data on chronic kidney disease (CKD) incidence and its determinants in pediatric HSCT recipients are limited.MethodsWe conducted a single‐center retrospective cohort study of 261 pediatric patients (aged 0–21 years) who underwent HSCT at Schneider Children's Medical Center, Israel, between 2000 and 2020. Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined per KDIGO creatinine criteria. CKD was defined as eGFR < 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 sustained ≥ 3 months and/or proteinuria or hypertension. Given the limited number of CKD events, multivariable logistic regression identifying factors associated with CKD was treated as exploratory. Overall survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methodology.ResultsAKI episodes occurred in 20.5% of patients during the first three months post‐HSCT. CKD incidence increased progressively: 6.0% at one year, 8.6% at three years, and 16.5% at five years‐substantially exceeding the ~5% background prevalence reported in the general Israeli pediatric population. Older age at transplantation was the only variable significantly associated with CKD in this exploratory model (mean 11.96 vs. 7.12 years in the CKD and non‐CKD groups, respectively; p = 0.008). A higher proportion of patients who developed CKD had undergone four or more transplantations compared with those who did not (31.6% vs. 3.7%), though this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.22) and should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating. Patients with a borderline baseline creatinine in the early post‐transplant period had lower overall survival than those with a normal baseline creatinine (log‐rank p = 0.05), an association whose biological basis requires further study.ConclusionsCKD affects a substantial and growing proportion of pediatric HSCT survivors in this cohort, particularly those transplanted at older ages. These findings are associative rather than causal, given the retrospective, single‐center design and limited number of CKD events. They nonetheless support systematic long‐term renal surveillance and nephrology referral as part of post‐HSCT survivorship care, with prospective, adequately powered studies needed to confirm risk factors and clarify mechanisms.Keywords: acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, long‐term outcomes, pediatric, survivorship

Introduction

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is a potentially curative treatment for a wide spectrum of malignant and non‐malignant conditions in children, including hematologic malignancies, immunodeficiencies, inherited metabolic disorders, and bone marrow failure syndromes [1]. As transplant‐related mortality has declined over recent decades, the focus of post‐HSCT care has shifted increasingly toward long‐term morbidity in survivors [2]. Renal complications represent a significant source of this morbidity, with acute kidney injury (AKI) reported in 17%–60% of pediatric HSCT recipients depending on patient population, conditioning regimen, and AKI definition used [3, 4, 5].

AKI after HSCT is multifactorial, arising from nephrotoxic conditioning agents, calcineurin inhibitor exposure, infections, sinusoidal obstruction syndrome, graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD), and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) [3]. Recurrent or severe AKI episodes are hypothesized to promote maladaptive repair, nephron loss, and progressive decline in renal function, ultimately manifesting as chronic kidney disease (CKD) [6]. Published CKD rates following pediatric HSCT range from 11% to 41%, with considerable heterogeneity across studies in follow‐up duration, CKD definition, and patient selection [7, 8]., Reported associated factors include older age at transplant, hematologic malignancy as the primary diagnosis, prior AKI with eGFR < 60mL/min/1.73 m [2] sustained beyond 28days, and high‐dose radiation or nephrotoxic agent exposure [7, 9, 10].

Despite this growing evidence base, no published data exists on CKD incidence or its associated factors among pediatric HSCT recipients in Israel. CKD prevalence in the general Israeli pediatric population has been estimated at approximately 5%, with congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract as the predominant etiology [11]. The extent to which HSCT further amplifies renal risk in Israeli children‐a population with distinct genetic backgrounds, healthcare utilization patterns, and transplant protocols‐is unknown.

The primary aim of this study was to determine the cumulative incidence of CKD in pediatric HSCT survivors at a large national pediatric transplant center over a 20‐year period. Secondary aims were to explore clinical factors associated with CKD development and to characterize the trajectory of mean renal function over five years post‐transplant. Given the retrospective design and the anticipated small number of CKD events, this study was designed as hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Population

This single‐center retrospective cohort study included all patients aged 21years or younger who underwent HSCT at Schneider Children's Medical Center (SCMCI), Petach Tiqva, Israel, between January 2000 and December 2020. SCMCI is one of the largest national pediatric transplant centers for Israel. Patients were excluded if they had [1]: pre‐transplant CKD, defined as eGFR < 90mL/min/1.73 m [2]; pre‐existing structural kidney anomalies; or [3] insufficient longitudinal renal function data (fewer than two creatinine measurements post‐HSCT). Patients who died before reaching a given follow‐up timepoint were not evaluable for CKD at that timepoint and were excluded from the corresponding CKD denominator (rather than analyzed using formal competing‐risk methodology); all patients, including those who died, were included in AKI and overall survival analyses. The implications of this approach for interpreting CKD incidence are discussed below. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Rabin‐Schneider Medical Center (Protocol 0071–21).

Data Collection

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from the Clalit Health Services electronic medical record (EMR) database, the largest health maintenance organization in Israel, which captures comprehensive longitudinal data including laboratory results, vital signs, medications, imaging, and clinical outcomes. Variables collected included age and sex, primary diagnosis, transplant type (allogeneic, autologous, cord blood), number of transplants, donor type (related/unrelated), stem cell source (bone marrow, peripheral blood stem cells, cord blood), exposure to nephrotoxic agents (calcineurin inhibitors, cisplatin, methotrexate), serum creatinine at baseline and longitudinally, and vital status. Granular data on cumulative nephrotoxic drug dosing, conditioning regimen intensity, the specific clinical indication for each sequential transplant (e.g., relapse, graft failure, or planned tandem transplantation), and renal function immediately preceding each sequential transplant were not available for structured analysis in this dataset.

Definitions

AKI was ascertained using standard KDIGO serum creatinine criteria (a ≥ 50% rise from baseline within the defined KDIGO observation window, consistent with Stage ≥ 1 AKI), using pre‐transplant creatinine as baseline [12]. Rather than a single ascertainment at a fixed timepoint, patients were monitored throughout the first three months post‐HSCT, and any discrete episode meeting KDIGO creatinine criteria for AKI during this three‐month period was recorded as an AKI event. Because urine output data were unavailable in this retrospective, EMR‐based cohort, AKI ascertainment relied on the creatinine criterion alone, without the urine output component of the full KDIGO definition; this is a recognized limitation of creatinine‐only AKI ascertainment and likely underestimates true AKI incidence, as discussed further below.

CKD was defined as eGFR < 90 mL/min/1.73 m [2] sustained for ≥ 3 months, calculated using the CKiD formula, and/or the presence of proteinuria or hypertension, consistent with KDIGO guidelines [13]. However, because more than 80% of patients lacked longitudinal proteinuria and blood pressure documentation beyond one year post‐HSCT, CKD ascertainment in this cohort was, in practice, based predominantly on reduced eGFR alone. The reported CKD incidence should therefore be interpreted as primarily reflecting reduced filtration rate rather than the full multicomponent KDIGO definition; true CKD incidence, inclusive of proteinuric and hypertensive phenotypes, is likely underestimated.

Baseline serum creatinine was categorized relative to age‐ and sex‐based normative reference ranges as “borderline” or “normal.” This terminology (borderline vs. normal baseline creatinine) is used consistently throughout the manuscript, including in Figure 1, to avoid ambiguity with creatinine elevation indicating impaired renal function.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data, and median with interquartile range (IQR) for non‐normal distributions, assessed by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Group comparisons used Student's t‐test or Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for continuous variables, and chi‐square or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression examined factors associated with CKD, including age at transplant, sex, transplant type, AKI occurrence, calcineurin inhibitor exposure, and number of transplants. This model included six candidate predictors against only 19 CKD events, yielding approximately three events per variable‐well below the conventionally recommended threshold of ten events per variable for stable multivariable modeling. We retained all pre‐specified, clinically relevant covariates rather than reducing the model post hoc; however, given this low events‐per‐variable ratio, results of the multivariable analysis should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory, and odds ratios are not reported given the limited precision achievable with this sample size.

Because mortality following HSCT is unlikely to occur at random with respect to renal risk‐patients with the most severe transplant‐related complications, nephrotoxic exposure, or comorbidity may be simultaneously more likely to die and, had they survived, more likely to have developed CKD‐excluding these patients from CKD denominators (as described above) may bias CKD incidence estimates in ways that are difficult to predict, and the reported incidence should be understood as describing risk among survivors rather than the full transplanted population. Formal competing‐risk regression (e.g., Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard modeling) was not performed and is discussed as a limitation below. Overall survival stratified by baseline creatinine category was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method with log‐rank testing. All analyses were performed in RStudio 2024.04 and SPSS 30.x. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Claude AI was utilized for language editing and styling.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

Between January 2000 and December 2020, 535 HSCT procedures were performed at SCMCI. After applying exclusion criteria (pre‐transplant CKD, structural kidney anomalies, or insufficient follow‐up data), 261 patients with 326 transplant procedures were included in the final cohort (Figure 2). Mean age at first transplant was 7.38 ± 5.70 years, with a male predominance (54.8%). Single transplantation was performed in 87.4% of patients. The most common indication was malignancy (61.3%), followed by bone marrow failure (12.6%), immunodeficiency (10.3%), genetic/metabolic disorders (9.6%), and benign hematological conditions (6.1%). Allogeneic transplantation comprised 67.0% of procedures. Detailed cohort characteristics are provided in Table 1. During the follow‐up period, 50 patients (19.2%) died; as noted above, these patients were included in AKI and overall survival analyses but were not evaluable for CKD beyond the timepoint at which they died.

Renal Function Trajectory

Mean eGFR at baseline was 132.85 ± 37.50 mL/min/1.73 m [2]. Mean eGFR declined progressively over the follow‐up period: 131.95 ± 34.57 mL/min/1.73 m [2] at one year, 124.77 ± 32.52 mL/min/1.73 m [2] at three years, and 121.21 ± 29.41 mL/min/1.73 m [2] at five years post‐HSCT (Table 2), a reduction of approximately 11.6 mL/min/1.73 m [2] from baseline to five years. Notably, mean eGFR remained well above the 90 mL/min/1.73 m [2] threshold used to define CKD at every timepoint. Because these values are cross‐sectional means computed across the subset of patients with data available at each timepoint, rather than within‐patient longitudinal trajectories, this population‐level decline should be interpreted cautiously; it does not, by itself, establish clinically significant deterioration at the individual‐patient level, and longitudinal modeling of within‐patient trajectories would be required to do so.

AKI Incidence and Early Survival

AKI within the first three months post‐HSCT occurred in 64 patients (20.5%). Only two patients (0.8%) required renal replacement therapy (dialysis), lower than the 2%–10% reported in prior literature [9, 14]., In Kaplan–Meier analysis, patients with a borderline baseline creatinine had significantly lower overall survival than those with a normal baseline creatinine (log‐rank p = 0.05; Figure 1). Because low serum creatinine in children can reflect lower muscle mass, younger age, or poorer nutritional status rather than preserved renal function, we interpret this association cautiously: It may reflect a frailer, younger, or more heavily pretreated subgroup rather than a direct renal mechanism. This finding is discussed further, including its limitations, below.

CKD Incidence and Associated Factors

Among patients with adequate follow‐up, CKD developed in 13 patients (6.0%; denominator 217) at one year, 19 patients (8.6%; denominator 221) at three years, and 19 patients (16.5%; denominator 115) at five years post‐HSCT. The number of patients evaluable for CKD ascertainment differed across timepoints because eGFR ascertainment at each timepoint required an available creatinine measurement within a predefined window around that timepoint; patients without a qualifying laboratory value at a given timepoint (e.g., due to a missed clinic visit) were not evaluable at that timepoint even if data were available earlier or later, which accounts for the one‐year denominator being slightly smaller than the three‐year denominator. Both denominators are substantially larger than the five‐year denominator, which reflects the smaller number of patients who had reached five years of follow‐up by the close of the study period, together with loss to follow‐up over time. These rates are substantially higher than the ~5% CKD prevalence reported in the general Israeli pediatric population [11].

In univariate analysis, AKI within the first three months post‐HSCT showed a numerical association with CKD development (18.6% vs. 36.8%; p = 0.07); this association did not reach significance in the exploratory multivariable model (Table 3). Age at transplantation was the only variable significantly associated with CKD in this exploratory model: Patients who developed CKD were older than those who did not (mean 11.96 ± 6.18 vs. 7.12 ± 5.56 years, in the CKD and non‐CKD groups, respectively; p = 0.008).

A higher proportion of patients with CKD had undergone four or more transplantations compared with those without CKD (31.6% vs. 3.7%; Table 3); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance across transplant‐number categories overall (p = 0.22), and with only 19 CKD events, we regard this as a preliminary, hypothesis‐generating observation rather than evidence of a causal or dose‐dependent relationship. The clinical circumstances of repeat transplantation (e.g., relapse, graft failure, or planned tandem transplantation) and pre‐transplant renal function before each sequential transplant were not characterized in the present analysis and represent an important direction for future work in this cohort.

Sex, transplant type, donor type, stem cell source, primary diagnosis, and individual nephrotoxic agent exposures‐including cisplatin, methotrexate, and calcineurin inhibitors‐were not significantly associated with CKD development (all p > 0.05; Table 3).

Given the small number of CKD events (n = 19), the multivariable model is acknowledged as limited in power to detect associations for all covariates; findings should be interpreted as exploratory and validated in larger, ideally multicenter, cohorts.

Discussion

In this 20‐year retrospective cohort of 261 pediatric HSCT survivors at a national transplant center, we observed a progressively increasing incidence of reduced eGFR meeting CKD criteria‐6.0%, 8.6%, and 16.5% at one, three, and five years post‐transplant, respectively. These rates are consistently higher than the estimated ~5% CKD prevalence in the general Israeli pediatric population, suggesting that HSCT is associated with substantially increased long‐term renal risk, and are broadly consistent with the 17% five‐year CKD rate reported by Lugthart et al. in a comparable European pediatric HSCT cohort [13].

Our observed AKI rate of 20.5% falls at the lower end of the reported 17%–39% range across pediatric HSCT studies [4, 5]. This may reflect close inpatient renal monitoring, the relatively high proportion of non‐malignant indications (38.8%) in our cohort, and evolving conditioning practices over the 20‐year study period, though we were not able to formally test these explanations. Only 0.8% of patients required dialysis, compared with 2%–10% in prior series [9, 14, 15]. The reasons for this low dialysis requirement are not entirely clear from our data. Specific institutional criteria for dialysis initiation (e.g., thresholds for refractory fluid overload, severe electrolyte derangement, or severe azotemia) were not systematically recorded in this retrospective analysis, and we cannot exclude that differences in patient population, conditioning intensity, or under‐ascertainment of milder AKI (given our creatinine‐only AKI definition) contributed to this finding.

Borderline baseline creatinine, rather than elevated creatinine, was associated with inferior overall survival in Kaplan–Meier analysis (p = 0.05; Figure 1). Because low creatinine in children more often reflects lower muscle mass or nutritional status than renal function and given the overlapping confidence intervals and borderline significance of this comparison, we regard this association as hypothesis‐generating; it may reflect a frailer or more heavily pretreated subgroup (e.g., younger or more cachectic patients) rather than a specific renal mechanism. A formal survival model adjusting for age, weight‐for‐age, and nutritional status would be needed to clarify the basis for this association, and we present it here descriptively rather than as evidence of a causal or renal‐specific effect.

Older age at transplantation was significantly associated with CKD in our cohort. It is biologically plausible that this reflects factors such as reduced renal regenerative capacity following injury [6], but this remains a hypothesis rather than an established mechanism in our data, since we did not have granular measures of nephron mass, cumulative nephrotoxic drug dosing, or conditioning intensity by age with which to test it directly. Age may instead act as a surrogate for other correlated but unmeasured factors‐including underlying diagnosis, cumulative chemotherapy exposure, conditioning regimen intensity, radiation exposure, or transplant era‐any of which could independently contribute to renal risk. Whether renal recovery capacity following AKI differs by age in this population is an open question that our data cannot resolve, as we did not perform an age‐stratified analysis of AKI incidence or recovery; this would be a valuable focus for future work rather than a conclusion we draw here.

In contrast to Lugthart et al., who reported hematologic malignancy as an independent CKD risk factor, [16] we found no significant association between primary diagnosis and CKD. This discrepancy may reflect differences in treatment protocols between Israel and the Netherlands, the relatively small number of CKD events in our cohort, or competing mortality risk that disproportionately affected the malignancy subgroup and may have attenuated the apparent renal risk associated with malignant disease in our data‐an example of the broader competing‐risk limitation discussed below. We also did not find a significant independent association between AKI and CKD in multivariable analysis, despite a univariate signal (p = 0.07); given the exploratory nature of this model (approximately three events per variable), this more likely reflects insufficient statistical power than a true absence of association and should not be interpreted as evidence against a relationship between AKI and CKD.

A numerically higher proportion of patients with CKD had undergone four or more transplants compared with those without CKD (31.6% vs. 3.7%), though this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.22) given the small number of CKD events. We describe this as an association rather than evidence of a dose‐dependent causal relationship. Each additional HSCT could plausibly expose the kidneys to repeated conditioning‐related nephrotoxicity, calcineurin inhibitor exposure, and hemodynamic stress, but confirming this would require data not available in the current analysis—specifically, the clinical indications for repeat transplantation (e.g., relapse of malignancy, graft failure, or planned tandem autologous transplantation, such as for high‐risk neuroblastoma or CNS tumors requiring platinum‐based conditioning) and renal function immediately prior to each sequential transplant. We regard the association between repeat transplantation and CKD as a hypothesis warranting dedicated evaluation, ideally with detailed treatment and renal‐function data at each transplant episode, in larger or multicenter cohorts, and to our knowledge this numerical association has not been previously reported in the pediatric HSCT literature.

Our study has several important limitations. First, the retrospective design and reliance on the Clalit EMR resulted in missing data, most critically the absence of urine output data for AKI staging and the lack of proteinuria and blood pressure monitoring in over 80% of patients beyond one year; as noted above, CKD ascertainment in this cohort therefore predominantly reflects reduced eGFR, and true CKD incidence, inclusive of proteinuric and hypertensive phenotypes, is likely underestimated. Second, AKI was ascertained using the KDIGO creatinine criterion alone, without the urine output component of the full KDIGO definition, which likely underestimates true AKI incidence and may have biased downstream CKD risk estimates. Third, patients who died before reaching a given follow‐up timepoint were excluded from the corresponding CKD denominator rather than analyzed using formal competing‐risk methodology (e.g., Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard models). Because death after HSCT is unlikely to occur at random with respect to renal risk, this approach means our reported CKD incidence describes risk among survivors rather than the full transplanted population and may over‐ or under‐estimate true population‐level CKD risk in ways we cannot fully characterize with the current data; formal competing‐risk analysis was not feasible within the scope of this study but is a priority for future analysis of this cohort. Fourth, the multivariable logistic regression model was substantially underpowered, with approximately three events per variable against a conventionally recommended threshold of ten; our identification of age as the only significant covariate should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating, and odds ratios are not reported given the limited precision achievable with this sample size. Fifth, this 20‐year cohort spans substantial changes in HSCT practice, including the introduction of reduced‐intensity and reduced‐toxicity conditioning regimens, evolving antifungal and GVHD prophylaxis, and growing recognition of TMA as a distinct nephrotoxic entity; we were unable to perform an adequately powered era‐stratified analysis (e.g., before vs. after 2010) given the limited number of CKD events, and we cannot exclude that temporal changes in practice influenced our findings. Sixth, the single‐center design may limit generalizability, though SCMCI is Israel's national pediatric transplant center. Finally, the small number of CKD events (n = 19) limits statistical power throughout and precludes simultaneous evaluation of all candidate risk factors; several of our observations, including the number‐of‐transplants finding, require confirmation in larger, ideally multicenter or prospective, cohorts.

Despite these limitations, this is the largest reported pediatric HSCT cohort from Israel and provides the first data on long‐term renal outcomes in this population. Our findings are best regarded as hypothesis‐generating: They demonstrate a high and progressively increasing incidence of reduced eGFR meeting CKD criteria [13], an association between older age at transplantation and CKD, and a preliminary, non‐significant signal linking repeat transplantation to CKD that warrants further study. We believe these findings nonetheless support systematic long‐term nephrology follow‐up for pediatric HSCT survivors‐particularly structured monitoring of blood pressure and proteinuria, which was markedly underutilized in this cohort—while acknowledging that confirmation of specific risk factors and underlying mechanisms will require prospective, adequately powered, multicenter studies incorporating competing‐risk methodology, detailed treatment‐exposure data, and standardized renal surveillance protocols.

Conclusions

This 20‐year single‐center cohort study demonstrates a high and progressively increasing incidence of reduced eGFR meeting CKD criteria among pediatric HSCT survivors in Israel, reaching 16.5% at five years post‐transplant. Older age at transplantation was associated with CKD in this exploratory analysis, and a numerically higher, though not statistically significant, proportion of patients with CKD had undergone repeat transplantation‐an association warranting prospective investigation. Given the retrospective design, limited number of CKD events, and inherent competing‐risk bias arising from the exclusion of patients who died, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory. They nonetheless underscore the potential value of systematic long‐term renal surveillance, including proteinuria and blood pressure monitoring, and nephrology referral as components of post‐HSCT survivorship care. Prospective, multicenter studies with formal competing‐risk analysis and adequately powered multivariable modeling are needed to confirm risk factors and inform evidence‐based renal monitoring guidelines for this growing survivor population.
