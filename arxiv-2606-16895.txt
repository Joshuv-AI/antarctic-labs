Introduction

The so-called stable Lévy distribution, for
0<\alpha<1
, is defined by

(1)

f_{t,\alpha}(r):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}e^{zr-tz^{\alpha}}\,dz,\qquad\sigma>0,\quad t>0,\,\,r\geq 0,

where the branch of
z^{\alpha}
is chosen so that
{\Re}(z^{\alpha})>0
whenever
{\Re}(z)>0
. It is well known that, in general, no explicit representation is available for the Lévy distribution, except in the particular case
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
, where one has

f_{t,\frac{1}{2}}(r)=\frac{t}{\sqrt{4\pi r^{3}}}e^{\frac{-t^{2}}{4r}},\qquad t,r>0.

These functions were introduced by S. Bochner in the study of certain stochastic processes. They constitute the density functions associated with stable Lévy processes and are closely related to fractional Brownian motion (
[
5
]
).

In
[
27
, Section IX.11]
, K. Yosida employed these functions systematically in the study of
C_{0}
-semigroups of operators generated by fractional powers of infinitesimal generators of uniformly bounded
C_{0}
-semigroups of linear operators on a Banach space
X
. More precisely, let
(T(s))_{s>0}
be a uniformly bounded
C_{0}
-semigroup generated by
A
. Then the fractional power
-(-A)^{\alpha}
generates a uniformly bounded
C_{0}
-semigroup
(e^{-t(-A)^{\alpha}})_{t>0}
given by

(2)

e^{-t(-A)^{\alpha}}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{t,\alpha}(s)\,T(s)(x)\,ds,\qquad x\in X.

We now consider the Lebesgue Banach space
(L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+}),\|\cdot\|_{1})
of absolutely integrable functions on
(0,\infty)
, endowed with the norm

\|f\|_{1}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(\lambda)|\,d\lambda<\infty.

It holds that
f_{t,\alpha}(\lambda)\geq 0
for
\lambda>0
,
\|f_{t,\alpha}\|_{1}=1
, and

f_{t+s,\alpha}(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\lambda}f_{t,\alpha}(\lambda-\mu)f_{s,\alpha}(\mu)\,d\mu,\quad\lambda>0,\quad t,s>0.

See, for instance,
[
27
]
.

Let
\ell^{p}
denote the Lebesgue sequence space consisting of complex sequences
f=(f(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
such that

\|f\|_{p}:=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lvert f(n)\rvert^{p}\right)^{1/p}<\infty,\qquad 1\leq p<\infty.

The space
\ell^{\infty}
consists of bounded complex sequences endowed with the supremum norm, and
c_{0,0}
denotes the set of sequences with finite support. It is well known that
\ell^{1}\hookrightarrow\ell^{p}\hookrightarrow\ell^{\infty}
. The convolution product
\ast
of two sequences
f=(f(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
g=(g(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is defined by

f\ast g(n)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}f(n-j)g(j),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

With this operation,
(\ell^{1},\ast)
is a Banach algebra, and
(\ell^{p},\ast)
is a Banach
\ell^{1}
-module for
1<p\leq\infty
. We write
f^{1\ast}=f
and
f^{n\ast}:=f\ast f^{(n-1)\ast}
for
n\geq 2
.

The main motivation of this work is to identify sequences, specifically polynomial sequences
(p_{(\cdot);a}(t))_{t\in\mathbb{C}}\subset\ell^{1}
, that play a role analogous to that of
(f_{t,\alpha})_{t>0}
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
(see Corollary
4.4
). As is often the case, the discrete setting (involving polynomial sequences) exhibits a richer and more intricate structure than its continuous counterpart. Applications to power-bounded operators on Banach spaces are also provided (see Section
5
).

The paper is self-contained and includes the necessary preliminary material. Its structure is as follows.

In the first section, we consider the following recurrence relation: for
a\in\mathbb{C}
, define
b_{1,n;a}=(a)_{n}
;
b_{n,n;a}=a^{n}
, and

(3)

b_{j,n+1;a}:=(aj-n)b_{j,n;a}+ab_{j-1,n;a},\qquad 2\leq j\leq n,

(Definition
1.1
). Here, for
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
(z)_{n}
and
z^{(n)}
the falling and rising factorials, respectively:

\displaystyle(z)_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z(z-1)\cdots(z-n+1),

\displaystyle z^{(n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z(z+1)\cdots(z+n-1),

with the usual conventions
(z)_{0}=z^{(0)}=1
and
(0)_{n}=0^{(n)}=0
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that
(z)_{n}=(-1)^{n}(-z)^{(n)}
. These quantities are also commonly referred to as Pochhammer symbols.

For particular values of
a
(e.g.,
a=-1,{1\over 2},1,2,\dots
), the sequences
(b_{j,n;a})_{1\leq j\leq n,n}
are well known and appear in
[
25
]
, including, for instance, signed Lah and Bessel numbers. In particular, the Lah numbers
(L(n,j))_{n\geq 1,1\leq j\leq n}
are given by

L(n,j):={n-1\choose j-1}{n!\over j!},\qquad n\geq 1,\,1\leq j\leq n,

and count the number of ways to partition a set of
n
elements into
j
nonempty linearly ordered subsets. They were introduced by I. Lah in 1954 (
[
19
]
) and satisfy

L(n+1,j)=(n+j)L(n,j)+L(n,j-1),\quad 1\leq j\leq n,

which corresponds to the recurrence (
3
) for
a=-1
and then
b_{j,n;-1}=(-1)^{n}L(n,j)
.

Moreover, Lah numbers provide the connection between rising and falling factorials:

(4)

\displaystyle z^{(n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}L(n,j)(z)_{j},

\displaystyle(z)_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-j}L(n,j)z^{(j)}.

In Theorem
1.6
, we establish the multiplicative relation

(az)_{n}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}b_{l,n;a}(z)_{l},\qquad n\geq 1,\,z\in\mathbb{C},

which appears to be new.

In the second section, we consider the Cesàro numbers defined by
k^{\alpha}(0):=1
and

k^{\alpha}(n):={\alpha^{(n)}\over n!}=(-1)^{n}{(-\alpha)_{n}\over n!},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},

and prove that
k^{\alpha}\in\ell^{1}
if and only if
\Re\alpha<0
, together with an explicit computation of
\|k^{\alpha}\|_{1}
(Theorem
2.2
).

The study of higher-order derivatives of functions of the form

{d^{n}\over dz^{n}}\left(e^{-z^{a}}\right),\qquad a\in\mathbb{C},

has played a significant role in the historic development of orthogonal polynomials and special functions. Early contributions in this direction were made by R. Hoppe (1846), E.T. Bell (1934), and later H.W. Gould and A.T. Hopper (1961). For
a=m\in\mathbb{N}
, the Gould–Hopper polynomials
(H_{n}^{(m)})_{n\geq 0}
are defined by

H_{n}^{(m)}(z)=(-1)^{n}e^{z^{m}}{d^{n}\over dz^{n}}(e^{-{z^{m}}}),\qquad n\geq 0,\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

and generalize the classical Hermite polynomials.

In the third section, we derive an explicit expression for the
n
-th derivative of
F_{t,a}(z):=e^{-tz^{a}}
. Although it seems to be known, at least it is given for
a\in\mathbb{N}
in
[
4
]
, we have decided to include to avoid the lack of completeness. In particular, we show that

F^{(n)}_{t,a}(z)=e^{-tz^{a}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j}b_{j,n;a}\,t^{j}z^{ja-n},

and express the coefficients
b_{j,n;a}
in terms of Bell polynomials (Theorem
3.1
).

In the fourth section, for fixed
a\in\mathbb{C}
, we consider the polynomial sequence
(p_{n;a})_{n\geq 0}
defined by
p_{0;a}(t)=1
and

p_{n;a}(t):={(-1)^{n}\over n!}\sum_{l=1}^{n}b_{l,n;a}(-t)^{l},\qquad n\geq 1,\quad t\in\mathbb{C}.

These polynomials arise as the Taylor coefficients of the holomorphic function
z\mapsto e^{t(1-(1-z)^{a})}
on the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n;a}(t)z^{n}=e^{t(1-(1-z)^{a})},\qquad|z|<1.

Furthermore, they satisfy the convolution identity

p_{n;a}(t+s)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}p_{j;a}(t)p_{n-j;a}(s),\qquad t,s\in\mathbb{C}.

In fact,
(n!p_{n;a})_{n\geq 0}
forms a family of Sheffer polynomials, which can be studied via umbral calculus
[
6
,
23
]
.

For particular values of
a
, such as
a=-1
or
a=m\in\mathbb{N}
, we recover classical generating functions for Laguerre, Hermite, and Gould–Hopper polynomials. Moreover, we establish the connection with Lévy functions:

\int_{0}^{\infty}{r^{n}\over n!}e^{-r}f_{t,\alpha}(r)\,dr=e^{-t}p_{n;\alpha}(t),\qquad t>0,

for
0<\alpha<1
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
(Corollary
4.4
).

In the fifth and sixth sections, we present several applications of our result in operator theory, Banach algebras and complex analysis.

Given a power-bounded operator
T
on a Banach space
X
, we consider the fractional power
(I-T)^{a}
,
a\in\mathbb{C}
. In Theorem
5.2
, we prove that the entire group generated by
I-(I-T)^{a}
admits the representation

e^{t(I-(I-T)^{a})}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n;a}(t)T^{n},\qquad t\in\mathbb{C},

under suitable assumptions on
T
and
a
.

In the last section, we describe the semigroup generated by
(\delta_{0}-\delta_{1})^{a}
in
\ell^{1}
, study multiplication operators on Hardy spaces
H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
, and propose extensions of our results to
(C,a)
-bounded operators.

Proposition 1.3
.

Take

\displaystyle a\sum_{l=0}^{j-1}(-1)^{j-1-l}{(a(l+1))_{n}\over l!(j-1-l)!}-nc_{j,n;a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{a\over(j-1)!}\Delta^{j-1}((a(m+1)_{n})(0)-nc_{j,n;a}.

Since

, we obtain

\displaystyle c_{j,n+1;a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{a\over(j-1)!}\Delta^{j-1}((am)_{n})(0)+{a\over(j-1)!}\Delta^{j}((am)_{n})(0)-nc_{j,n;a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ac_{j-1,n;a}+ajc_{j,n;a}-nc_{j,n;a}=(aj-n)c_{j,n;a}+ac_{j-1,n;a}.

By Definition

.

Theorem 1.6
.

Take

(az)_{n}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}b_{l,n;a}(z)_{l}

Proof.

2.
Asymptotic estimation for Cesàro numbers

The Euler Gamma function
\Gamma
is defined by

\Gamma(z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}{t^{z-1}}e^{-t}dt,\qquad\Re z>0.

The extension of the Gamma function primarily involves analytic continuation to the entire complex plane, allowing definition for negative non-integers via the rising factorial

\Gamma(z)=\Gamma(z+n)/z^{(n)}.

Handling poles at non-positive integers the following identity with the falling factorial holds

(z)_{n}={\Gamma(z+1)\over\Gamma(z-n+1)},\qquad n\geq 0.

The following reflection formula holds for Euler Gamma function,

(6)

\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)={\pi\over\sin(\pi z)},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{Z}.

Although the next lemma is known, we include the proof to avoid the lack of completeness.

Lemma 2.1
.

Take
0<\Re\alpha<1
. Then

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\Gamma(n-\alpha)\over n!}={\Gamma(1-\alpha)\over\alpha}.

Proof.

Note that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\Gamma(n-\alpha)\over n!}=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\alpha-1}e^{-t}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{t^{n}\over n!}dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-\alpha-1}(1-e^{-t})dt={\Gamma(1-\alpha)\over\alpha},

where we have applied the equality
[
15
, 3.434(1)]

∎

For any complex number
\alpha,
we denote by
k^{\alpha}(0):=1
and

(7)

k^{\alpha}(n):={\alpha^{(n)}\over n!}=\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)\cdots(\alpha+n-1)}{n!}=(-1)^{n}{(-\alpha)_{n}\over n!}\quad\text{for }n\in\mathbb{N},\,

the known Cesàro numbers which are studied deeply in
[
28
, Vol. I, p.77]
and are denoted by
A_{n}^{\alpha-1}
. Note that
k^{0}(0)=1
,
k^{0}(n)=0
for
n\geq 1
and

k^{-m}(n)=(-1)^{n}{m\choose n},\qquad 0\leq n\leq m.

The kernels
k^{\alpha}
have played a key role in results about operator theory and fractional difference equations, see
[
1
,
21
]
. Note that the sequence
k^{\alpha}
can be written as

k^{\alpha}(n)=\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(n+1)}={n+\alpha-1\choose\alpha-1},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, the kernel
(k^{\alpha}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
could be defined by the generating function, that is,

(8)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}k^{\alpha}(n)z^{n}=\frac{1}{(1-z)^{\alpha}},\quad|z|<1.

As usual, we define
z^{\alpha}=e^{\alpha\log z}
for a branch of the complex logarithm on
\mathbb{D}
.

Note that these kernels satisfy the semigroup property,
k^{\alpha}*k^{\beta}=k^{\alpha+\beta}
for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
. As a function of
n,

k^{\alpha}
is increasing for
\alpha>1
, decreasing for
1>\alpha>0
and
k^{1}(n)=1
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
(
[
28
, Theorem III.1.17]
). Furthermore, it is straightforward to check that
k^{\alpha}(n)\leq k^{\beta}(n)
for
\beta\geq\alpha>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

For
\alpha\in\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}
,
k^{\alpha}(n)=0
for
n>-\alpha,
i.e.,
k^{\alpha}\in c_{0,0}
. In addition, for real
\alpha\notin\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\},

(9)

k^{\alpha}(n)=\frac{n^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}(1+O({1\over n})),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(
[
28
, Vol. I, p.77 (1.18)]
). Moreover this property holds in a more general context. Let
\alpha,z\in\mathbb{C},
then

(10)

\frac{\Gamma(z+\alpha)}{\Gamma(z)}=z^{\alpha}(1+\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)}{2z}+O(|z|^{-2})),\quad|z|\to\infty,

whenever
z\neq 0,-1,-2,\ldots
and
z\neq-\alpha,-\alpha-1,\ldots,
see
[
11
]
. Then
k^{\alpha}\in\ell^{1}
if and only if
\Re\alpha<0
or
\alpha=0
(see (
9
) and
[
2
, Lemma 5.3(i)]
. In the next result we estimate the norm.

Theorem 2.2
.

Take
\Re\alpha>0
and
k^{\alpha}
given in (
7
).

(i)

\|k^{-\alpha}\|_{1}=2
for
0<\alpha<1
,
\|k^{0}\|_{1}=1
and

\|k^{-\alpha}\|_{1}\leq 2{|\alpha|\over\Re\alpha}{\Gamma(1-\Re\alpha)\over|\Gamma(1-\alpha)|},\quad 0<\Re\alpha<1.

(ii)

\|k^{-m}\|_{1}=2^{m}
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

(iii)

\|k^{-\alpha}\|_{1}\leq 2^{[\alpha]+1}
for
\alpha>0
and

\|k^{-\alpha}\|_{1}\leq 2^{[\Re\alpha]+1}{|\alpha-[\Re\alpha]|\over\Re\alpha-[\Re\alpha]}{\Gamma(1-(\Re\alpha-[\Re\alpha]))\over|\Gamma(1-(\alpha-[\Re\alpha])|},\quad 0<\Re\alpha.

Proof.

(i) Take
0<\alpha<1
. Then

\|k^{-\alpha}\|_{1}=|k^{-\alpha}(0)|+{1\over|\Gamma(-\alpha)|}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\Gamma(n-\alpha)\over n!}=1+{1\over|\Gamma(-\alpha)|}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)\over\alpha}=2,

where we have applied Lemma
2.1
. Now, let
0<\Re\alpha<1
and

\|k^{-\alpha}\|_{1}=|k^{-\alpha}(0)|+{1\over|\Gamma(-\alpha)|}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{|\Gamma(n-\alpha)|\over n!}\leq 1+{|\alpha|\over\Re\alpha}{\Gamma(1-\Re\alpha)\over|\Gamma(1-\alpha)|}\leq 2{|\alpha|\over\Re\alpha}{\Gamma(1-\Re\alpha)\over|\Gamma(1-\alpha)|}.

(ii) For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, it is straightforward to check

\|k^{-m}\|_{1}=\sum_{n=0}^{m}{m\choose n}=2^{m}.

(iii) Take
\alpha=[\alpha]+\{\alpha\}
with
0<\{\alpha\}<1
. Then

\|k^{-\alpha}\|_{1}=\|k^{-[\alpha]}\ast k^{-\{\alpha\}}\|_{1}\leq 2^{[\alpha]+1},

where we have applied (i) and (ii). Finally, we write
\alpha=[\Re\alpha]+\{\Re\alpha\}+i\Im\alpha
and

\|k^{-\alpha}\|_{1}\leq\|k^{-[\Re\alpha]}\ast k^{-(\{\Re\alpha\}+i\Im\alpha)}\|_{1}\leq 2^{[\Re\alpha]+1}{|\alpha-[\Re\alpha]|\over\Re\alpha-[\Re\alpha]}{\Gamma(1-(\Re\alpha-[\Re\alpha]))\over|\Gamma(1-(\alpha-[\Re\alpha])|}

where we have used again (i) and (ii).
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Take
r>0
,
\Re\alpha>0
and
k^{\alpha}
given in (
7
).
Then the series

\sum_{n,j=0}^{\infty}|k^{-\alpha j}(n)|{r^{j}\over j!}\leq e^{c_{\alpha}r}<\infty,

where
c_{\alpha}:=2^{[\Re\alpha]+1}{|\alpha-[\Re\alpha]|\over\Re\alpha-[\Re\alpha]}{\Gamma(1-(\Re\alpha-[\Re\alpha]))\over|\Gamma(1-(\alpha-[\Re\alpha])|}
.

Proof.

Note that

\sum_{n,j=0}^{\infty}|k^{-\alpha j}(n)|{r^{j}\over j!}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}{r^{j}\over j!}\|k^{(-\alpha)^{j\ast}}\|_{1}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}{r^{j}\over j!}\|k^{-\alpha}\|_{1}^{j}\leq e^{c_{\alpha}r}<\infty,

where
k^{0\ast}:=k^{0}
and we have applied Theorem
2.2
(iii).
∎

where the sum is over all nonnegative integers

(j_{1},\dots,j_{n-k+1})
satisfying

\sum_{m=1}^{n-k+1}j_{m}=k,\qquad\sum_{m=1}^{n-k+1}mj_{m}=n.

.

. If

for all

We consider the function

F_{t,a}(z):=e^{-tz^{a}},

which is holomorphic on a simply connected domain

. Functions of the form
e^{-tz^{a}}
appear naturally in complex analysis,

Understanding the structure of their higher-order derivatives is useful for
studying growth properties, singularities, and asymptotic expansions.

A direct computation shows that each derivative of

P_{n,t,a}

P_{n+1,t,a}(z)=\left({d\over dz}-taz^{a-1}\right)P_{n,t,a}(z)=P_{n,t,a}^{\prime}(z)-taz^{a-1}P_{n,t,a}(z),\qquad P_{0}(z)=1.

The first derivatives illustrate the general pattern:

\displaystyle P_{1,t,a}(z)

\displaystyle=-taz^{a-1},

\displaystyle P_{2,t,a}(z)

\displaystyle=t^{2}a^{2}z^{2a-2}-ta(a-1)z^{a-2},

\displaystyle P_{3,t,a}(z)

\displaystyle=-t^{3}a^{3}z^{3a-3}+3t^{2}a^{2}(a-1)z^{2a-3}-ta(a-1)(a-2)z^{a-3}.

and then

P_{n,t,a}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j}t^{j}z^{aj-n}\,B_{n,j}\!\big((a)_{1},(a)_{2},\dots,(a)_{n-j+1}\big).

A straightforward consequence of the analyticity of the function

(15)

e^{-t((z+\lambda)^{a}-\lambda^{a})}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{P_{n,t,a}(\lambda)\over n!}{z^{n}},\qquad z\in D(\lambda,\Re(\lambda)):=\{w\in\mathbb{C}\,\,:\,\,|w-\lambda|<\Re(\lambda)\},

where this power series converges absolutely to the function throughout the interior of its radius of convergence.

Theorem 3.1
.

For every integer
n\geq 1
,

(16)

P_{n,t,a}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j}b_{j,n;a}\,t^{j}z^{ja-n}.

Consequently,

b_{j,n;a}=B_{n,j}\!\big((a)_{1},(a)_{2},\dots,(a)_{n-j+1}\big).

Proof.

We compute directly
F^{\prime}_{t,a}(z)=\frac{d}{dz}e^{-tz^{a}}=-atz^{a-1}e^{-tz^{a}}.

The formula gives

\sum_{j=1}^{1}(-1)^{j}b_{j,1;a}t^{j}z^{aj-1}F_{t,a}(z)=(-1)^{1}b_{1,1;a}tz^{a-1}F_{t,a}(z)=-b_{1,1;a}tz^{a-1}F_{t,a}(z),

and since
b_{1,1;a}=a
, it matches the derivative.

Assume for some
n\geq 1
:

F^{(n)}_{t,a}(z)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j}b_{j,n;a}t^{j}z^{aj-n}\right)F_{t,a}(z).

We derive
F^{(n)}_{t,a}(z)
to set,

F^{(n+1)}_{t,a}(z)=\frac{d}{dz}F^{(n)}_{t,a}(z)=\frac{d}{dz}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j}b_{j,n;a}t^{j}z^{aj-n}F_{t,a}(z)\right].

We use the product rule and obtain that

\frac{d}{dz}\big(z^{aj-n}F_{t,a}(z)\big)=(aj-n)z^{aj-n-1}F_{t,a}(z)-atz^{a(j+1)-(n+1)}F_{t,a}(z);

substitute back and,

F^{(n+1)}_{t,a}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j}b_{j,n;a}t^{j}\left[(aj-n)z^{aj-n-1}-atz^{a(j+1)-(n+1)}\right]F_{t,a}(z).

We reindex the second sum
j\mapsto j-1
to get

\sum_{j=2}^{n+1}(-1)^{j}ab_{j-1,n;a}t^{j}z^{aj-(n+1)}F_{t,a}(z).

The coefficient of
t^{j}z^{aj-(n+1)}F_{t,a}(z)
is

(-1)^{j}\big[(aj-n)b_{j,n;a}+ab_{j-1,n;a}\big]=(-1)^{j}b_{j,n+1;a},

which exactly matches the recurrence for
b_{j,n+1;a}
for
2\leq j\leq n
. For
j=1
,

and for
j=n+1
,

. By the Faà di Bruno formula (
14
), we identify

b_{j,n;a}=B_{n,j}\!\big((a)_{1},(a)_{2},\dots,(a)_{n-j+1}\big),\qquad 1\leq j\leq n,

and the proof is concluded.
∎

6.2.

Given

As usual we denote by

(see, for example
[

, Chapter 17]

We consider the function

if and only if
\Re\alpha\geq 0
. In this case, we define the multiplication operator

M_{h}(f):=hf
for

and
\|M_{h}\|=\|h\|_{\infty}
. Then it is clear that

and

for

and
\Re a\geq 0
(Theorem
4.3
(ii)); in particular

\|e^{th}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|p_{n;\alpha}(t)|^{2},\qquad t\in\mathbb{C},\qquad\Re\alpha\geq 0.

Moreover, given

, the solution of Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\partial\over\partial t}u(t,z)=M_{h}(u(t,z)),&t\geq 0,\\
u(0,z)=f(z),&\end{cases}

is given by

, see similar multiplication semigroups on

spaces in
[
10
, Section I.4]

Now we characterize directly when the analytic function

, i.e.,

where

In fact we need to study the behaviour of

in the neighborhood of the critical point

. We write

(

);

and

Since

For

, and

and we conclude

, as we have commented previously. Finally for

and

and

and

Similarly we prove that

for

if and only if

Cesàro sum and Cesàro mean of

of order
\alpha
, respectively.

The operator
T
is called Cesàro bounded of order
\alpha
, or simply
(C,\alpha)
-bounded, if it satisfies

\sup_{n}\|M_{T}^{\alpha}(n)\|<\infty
. Thus

(C,0)
-boundedness is the same as
power-boundedness, that is,
\sup_{n}\|T^{n}\|<\infty
.
For
\alpha=1
the operator
T
is called Cesàro mean bounded (or Cesàro bounded).
If
T
is
(C,\alpha)
-bounded
then it is
(C,\beta)
-bounded for every
\beta>\alpha
but the converse does not hold true in general;
e.g.
[
9
, Section 4.7]
and
[
26
, Remark 2.3]
.

Now fix
T\in{\mathcal{B}}(X)
a
(C,\alpha)
-bounded operator. In
[
1
, Corollary 3.7]
, a linear homomorphism
\Psi_{T}:\tau^{\alpha}(k^{\alpha+1})\to{\mathcal{B}}(X)
is defined by

\Psi_{T}(f)(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}W^{\alpha}f(n)\Delta^{-\alpha}\mathcal{T}(n)(x),\qquad x\in X,

where
W^{\alpha}
is a fractional discrete derivation,
W^{1}f(n)=f(n)-f(n+1)
,
W^{m+1}f=W^{m}(W^{1}f)
for
m\geq 1
, and see definition for
\alpha>0
in
[
1
,
2
]
. Note that
\Psi_{T}(\delta_{1})=T
where
\delta_{1}(n):=\delta_{1,n}
for
n\geq 0
is the Kronecker delta. The Banach algebra
\tau^{\alpha}(k^{\alpha+1})\subset\ell^{1}
and
k^{\beta}\in\tau^{\alpha}(k^{\alpha+1})
for
\Re\beta<0
(
[
2
, Lemma 5.3]
). Then it seems natural to conjecture

\displaystyle(I-T)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Psi_{T}(k^{-\alpha}),

\displaystyle e^{t(I-(I-T)^{\alpha})}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Psi_{T}(p_{(\cdot);\alpha}(t)),

for
\Re\alpha>0.
These ideas could be the starting point of a new research.
