Evolutionarily conserved peptidoglycan recognition proteins mediate antibacterial immunity in planarians
Authors: Sahil Kumar, Puja Bharti, Pooja Dudeja, Aman Sharma, Prasad Abnave
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPeptidoglycan recognition proteins (PGRPs) are conserved pattern recognition receptors that mediate bacterial sensing and clearance in vertebrates and arthropods, but their roles in lophotrochozoans remain largely unexplored. Here, we identify and characterize seven PGRPs in the planarian Schmidtea mediterranea, including three secreted (PGRP-S1–S3) and four membrane-associated (PGRP-L1–L4) proteins. Structural modeling demonstrated conserved PGRP domain architecture, and molecular docking predicted interactions with peptidoglycan-derived ligands. S. mediterranea PGRPs (Smed-PGRPs) are enriched in the intestine and epidermis, and are dynamically regulated following injury. Infection with Staphylococcus aureus induced route- and tissue-specific PGRP expression responses. RNA interference-mediated depletion of Smed-PGRPs impaired bacterial clearance, with PGRP-S3 loss resulting in the strongest increase in pathogen persistence and tissue invasion. Together, these findings demonstrate that PGRPs are essential mediators of planarian antibacterial immunity and support the evolutionary conservation of PGRP-mediated antibacterial mechanisms across bilaterian lineages.Keyword: PGRP, planarian, Schmidtea mediterranea, PRR, infection, bacteria, Staphylococcus aureus

Introduction

Innate immunity constitutes the first line of host defense against microbial infection and relies on pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) to detect conserved pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as lipopolysaccharides, nucleic acids, and peptidoglycan. Engagement of PRRs initiates downstream immune signaling pathways and effector mechanisms that restrict microbial growth, promote pathogen clearance, and maintain tissue integrity.1 Understanding how PRR families have diversified and been retained or lost across animal evolution is essential for reconstructing the origins and core principles of innate immune defense.

Among these receptors, peptidoglycan recognition proteins (PGRPs) represent a conserved family of PRRs that bind bacterial peptidoglycan and mediate immune activation or direct bactericidal activity through amidase-dependent mechanisms.2,3,4,5,6,7 In vertebrates, PGRPs contribute to immune homeostasis at epithelial barriers, regulate inflammation, and shape host-microbe interactions.6,7,8 In arthropods, PGRPs play central roles in activating antimicrobial pathways such as Toll and immune deficiency (IMD), as well as in bacterial clearance.4,9,10,11,12,13,14,15 Although PGRPs have been extensively characterized in vertebrates and arthropods, their distribution, structural conservation, and functional roles in other invertebrate lineages, particularly basal bilaterians, remain poorly understood. Notably, PGRPs are absent from the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans,5 indicating lineage-specific loss of this receptor family and underscoring the evolutionary plasticity of innate immune recognition strategies across metazoans. This raises fundamental questions regarding when PGRPs emerged during bilaterian evolution, how their functions diversified across lineages, and whether they represent a conserved immune module retained in some invertebrate phyla but lost in others. Addressing these questions requires investigation of PGRPs in phylogenetically informative organisms outside of the well-studied ecdysozoan and vertebrate models.

Planarians such as S. mediterranea provide a powerful system to investigate the evolution and function of innate immune mechanisms in this context. As free-living lophotrochozoans, planarians occupy a key phylogenetic position within Bilateria and offer an opportunity to explore immune strategies in a lineage that diverged early from ecdysozoans and deuterostomes. Planarians lack an adaptive immune system and therefore rely entirely on innate defenses to survive in microbe-rich aquatic environments.16,17,18 At the same time, they exhibit remarkable regenerative capacity driven by a population of pluripotent adult stem cells known as neoblasts, allowing direct examination of how immunity intersects with tissue homeostasis and regeneration.16,18,19

Previous studies have revealed multiple antibacterial strategies in planarians,18,20,21,22,23,24 including phagocytosis by intestinal cells and LC3-associated phagocytosis mediated by MORN2, which promotes bacterial degradation within phagolysosomes.25 Additional immune regulators such as GRP78, LTA4H, and Tim modulate immune responses, while signaling components of the RIG-I and partial Toll-like receptor pathways contribute to pathogen sensing.16,18,23,26,27,28,29 Moreover, planarians exhibit stem cell-mediated trained immunity, enabling accelerated pathogen clearance upon reinfection,27 and immune gene expression is dynamically regulated during regeneration to protect injured tissues.28 Despite these advances, the identity and functional relevance of canonical PRRs, such as PGRPs, in planarians have not been systematically explored.27,30,31

In this study, we identify and characterize the PGRP family in S. mediterranea and investigate their structural features, spatial expression patterns, regulation during infection and injury, and functional roles in antibacterial defense. Using an integrated approach combining transcriptomic analyses, AlphaFold-based structural modeling, in situ hybridization, RNA interference (RNAi)-mediated gene silencing, and immune challenge assays, we demonstrate that planarian PGRPs function as essential mediators of antibacterial immunity at epithelial and intestinal barriers. Together, our findings establish PGRPs as conserved components of innate immunity in lophotrochozoans and provide new insight into the evolutionary landscape of peptidoglycan recognition mechanisms across animal phyla.

Results

Planarian PGRPs form distinct functional classes

Using homology-based searches with vertebrate (human and mouse) and invertebrate (Drosophila) PGRP sequences, we identified seven transcripts encoding PGRP domain-containing proteins in S. mediterranea from the PlanMine transcriptome database32 (Figure 1A; Table S1). Based on the predicted protein length, these candidates segregated into two major classes: three short PGRPs (PGRP-S1–S3; <200 amino acids) and four long PGRPs (PGRP-L1–L4; ∼800 amino acids) (Figure 1A).

InterPro and NCBI conserved domain analyses confirmed the presence of a single PGRP domain in all seven proteins. In contrast, predicted membrane topology differed substantially between the two groups. PGRP-L1, L2, L3, and L4 contained one or more transmembrane domains, whereas no transmembrane regions were detected in PGRP-S1, S2, or S3 (Figure 1B). These predictions were independently validated by TMpred analysis, indicating that the long PGRPs likely function as membrane-associated sensors, while the short PGRPs represent soluble effector molecules.

Signal peptide prediction using InterPro and SignalP-5.0 revealed N-terminal secretion signals in all Smed-PGRPs except PGRP-L3, which instead harbored two N-terminal transmembrane domains (Figure 1B). Notably, all three short PGRPs possessed signal peptides, supporting their classification as secreted effector PGRPs.

To assess functional conservation, we aligned PGRP domains from planarian, human, mouse, and Drosophila proteins. This analysis revealed strong conservation of residues required for peptidoglycan binding, zinc binding, and amidase catalytic activity (Figure 1C). While all Smed-PGRPs retain conserved substrate-binding motifs, catalytic residues associated with amidase activity were selectively preserved in the secreted PGRP-S1, S2, and S3, suggesting functional specialization within the family.

Phylogenetic reconstruction using 28 PGRP proteins from 4 species showed that Smed-PGRP-S1, S2, and S3 cluster together and form the closest invertebrate orthologs of mammalian PGRP2 (Figure 1D). This relationship was further supported by an expanded phylogeny including 112 PGRP domain-containing proteins from 27 species (3 vertebrates and 24 invertebrates), which confirmed that planarian short PGRPs represent the nearest invertebrate orthologs of mammalian PGRP2 (Figure S1). Multiple sequence alignment of these 112 PGRP proteins from diverse taxa revealed strong conservation within the core PGRP domain (Figure S2). Several residues known to be critical for peptidoglycan binding and amidase catalytic activity were highly conserved across species, indicating preservation of key functional features. In contrast, regions outside the core domain exhibited substantial sequence divergence, including insertions and deletions, consistent with structural variability among PGRP family members.

Planarian PGRPs display conserved structural features and predicted binding to bacterial ligands

All seven Smed-PGRPs were subjected to structure prediction using AlphaFold. Structural comparison revealed a high degree of conservation of the PGRP domain architecture between planarians, Drosophila, and humans, with robust structural superposition across species (Figures 2A, S3A, and S3B). The predicted PGRP domain adopts a conserved fold composed of three peripheral α-helices and a central β-sheet core that together form a surface groove consistent with a ligand-binding pocket.

Docking analysis further indicated that the PGRP domains of all seven planarian PGRPs are likely capable of accommodating multiple bacterial peptidoglycan-derived ligands, including tracheal cytotoxin (TCT) and muramyl tripeptide (MTP). In each case, the ligands were positioned within a defined groove of the PGRP domain (Figures 2B and S3C). Residue-level analysis of the predicted binding interface identified amino acids lining the binding pocket that are positioned in close proximity to the ligands (Figure 2C; Figure S3C). The predicted docking pose of Smed-PGRP-S3 with TCT is stabilized by hydrogen-bond interactions involving charged and polar residues, including Arg and His, at the ligand-protein interface, together with additional backbone-mediated contacts that contribute to ligand positioning within the groove (Figure 2C; Figure S3C).

Notably, residues contacting TCT are concentrated within a defined region of the PGRP domain, indicating that ligand binding may occur at a conserved recognition interface. Collectively, these structural and docking analyses support a possible interaction between Smed-PGRPs and peptidoglycan-derived ligands and provide a structural framework for bacterial ligand recognition by planarian PGRPs.

Smed-PGRPs show distinct and tissue-enriched expression patterns

To determine the cellular and tissue-level expression patterns of Smed-PGRPs in S. mediterranea, whole-mount fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed on intact animals. All seven Smed-PGRPs displayed distinct and non-uniform spatial expression patterns across the planarian body, with several family members showing prominent enrichment in the intestinal region (Figure 3A).

To further resolve the cellular identity of PGRP-expressing cells, we performed double-FISH analyses using established cell-type-specific markers. Consistent with the broad tissue distribution of PGRPs reported in vertebrates, Smed-PGRPs exhibited diverse expression profiles across multiple planarian cell populations (Figures 3B and S4). Individual Smed-PGRPs were predominantly detected in one to two major cell types, with the majority showing enrichment in intestinal tissue. PGRPs were also expressed by epidermal, parenchymal, and secretory cells. These tissues represent primary interfaces with the external environment and luminal microbes, suggesting a spatial association between PGRP expression and potential sites of bacterial exposure.

Notably, Smed-PGRP-L3 exhibited strong co-localization with the neoblast marker H2B, indicating expression within the stem cell compartment (Figures 3B and S4). This observation indicates that antimicrobial recognition machinery is present in proliferative stem cells and may contribute to immune surveillance during tissue homeostasis and regeneration.

Smed-PGRPs are induced in response to bacterial infection and tissue injury

To assess whether Smed-PGRP expression is modulated by bacterial challenge, intact planarians were infected with the Gram-positive bacterium S. aureus using two distinct routes: direct injection into the intestinal lumen to model enteric infection and waterborne exposure to model epidermal infection (Figure 4A). This experimental design enabled route-specific analysis of PGRP induction in tissues that directly encounter bacteria.

Quantitative expression analysis revealed tissue-associated and route-dependent upregulation of Smed-PGRPs following infection. Several Smed-PGRPs exhibited transient increases in expression following infection, with peak induction typically observed at intermediate time points. PGRP-L2 and PGRP-L3 showed significant upregulation following intestinal infection (Figure 4B). In contrast, epidermal exposure resulted in selective upregulation of PGRP-S3, which displayed robust and sustained induction from 6 to 24 h post infection (Figure 4C). These expression dynamics indicate that distinct subsets of Smed-PGRPs respond to bacterial exposure in a tissue- and time-dependent manner.

We next examined whether PGRP expression is also regulated during tissue damage and regeneration. Animals were subjected to injury and analyzed at multiple time points (0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post injury; Figure 4D). Several PGRPs, including PGRP-S2, L2, and L3, showed preferential upregulation following injury (Figure 4E), suggesting that a subset of Smed-PGRPs is dynamically regulated during regenerative responses and may contribute to tissue protection and immune surveillance during wound healing.

Smed-PGRP silencing impairs bacterial clearance and promotes pathogen persistence

To determine whether Smed-PGRPs contribute to antibacterial defense in planarians, gene-specific RNAi was performed by delivering double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) into the intestinal lumen (Figures 5A and 5B). Quantitative analysis confirmed efficient transcript depletion for all Smed-PGRPs, with an average knockdown efficiency of approximately 65% (Figure 5C). Following RNAi treatment, animals were challenged with S. aureus to evaluate the impact of PGRP depletion on bacterial persistence (Figures 5A and 5B).

Consistent with previous reports demonstrating the strong intrinsic resistance of planarians to bacterial pathogens,25,27 including S. aureus, control (GFP RNAi) animals efficiently cleared the infection over time. In contrast, colony-forming unit (CFU)-based quantification revealed significantly elevated bacterial loads in Smed-PGRP-silenced animals at 4 days post infection, with PGRP-S3 knockdown exhibiting the most pronounced effect (Figures 5D and 5E). These findings were further supported by immunofluorescence analysis, which demonstrated increased bacterial signal within the intestinal lumen of PGRP RNAi animals at 3, 5, and 7 days post infection compared to controls (Figures 5F and 5G). Together, these results indicate that Smed-PGRPs contribute to efficient bacterial clearance and restrict pathogen persistence during infection.

To investigate the cellular localization of bacteria following intestinal infection, we performed combined FISH using intestinal cell-type-specific markers together with immunofluorescence-based bacterial detection. This analysis revealed that bacteria predominantly localize within enterocytes, which represent the major phagocytic cell population of the planarian intestine (Figure 6A).

Given the strong and sustained induction of PGRP-S3 following infection, we next examined its functional contribution in regulating bacterial elimination. Silencing of PGRP-S3 resulted in increased bacterial accumulation within the intestinal lumen, as well as enhanced intracellular persistence within enterocytes (Figures 6B and 6C). Furthermore, following epidermal infection, PGRP-S3-depleted animals exhibited elevated bacterial burden at the epidermal surface (Figures 6D and 6E) accompanied by increased bacterial penetration into underlying tissues (Figures 6F and 6G). Together, these results indicate that PGRP-S3 contributes to bacterial clearance and limits tissue invasion, establishing Smed-PGRPs as key components of epithelial and intestinal antibacterial defense in planarians.

Discussion

In this study, we show that PGRPs constitute a functional antibacterial defense system in the planarian S. mediterranea. By integrating comparative analyses with in vivo functional experiments, we demonstrate that planarians encode a diversified PGRP family that is structurally conserved, dynamically regulated during infection, and required for efficient pathogen clearance. These findings extend experimentally validated PGRP function to lophotrochozoans and provide insight into how bacterial sensing mechanisms have been maintained across bilaterian evolution.5,33

Our phylogenetic and domain analyses indicate that planarian PGRPs fall into short secreted and long membrane-associated classes, similar to the organizational patterns observed in insects and vertebrates.5,6,33 The close relationship between Smed-PGRP-S proteins and mammalian PGRP2, together with the retention of conserved catalytic motifs in secreted family members, suggests that core antibacterial effector functions have been preserved across large evolutionary distances.34,35 At the same time, the absence of PGRPs in nematodes such as Caenorhabditis elegans highlights that this receptor family has not been universally retained and instead reflects lineage-specific loss or replacement by alternative immune strategies.5,36,37 These observations support a model in which PGRPs emerged early in bilaterian evolution and subsequently underwent differential retention and specialization.

Structural modeling further supports functional conservation at the molecular level. Despite substantial sequence divergence, the planarian PGRP domain adopts a fold that closely resembles that of insect and mammalian homologs, including a conserved surface groove predicted to accommodate peptidoglycan-derived ligands.38,39 Docking analyses suggest that bacterial ligands can occupy this groove in a manner similar to previously characterized PGRP-ligand interactions.40,41 Although direct biochemical binding measurements will be required to validate these predictions, the convergence of structural and functional data argues that planarian PGRPs may engage bacterial cell wall components using a shared recognition framework.

The spatial expression patterns of Smed-PGRPs are consistent with their proposed immune function. Most family members are enriched in tissues that directly interface with microbes, particularly the intestine and epidermis. This mirrors epithelial PGRP expression patterns reported in insects, mollusks, and mammals, where PGRPs contribute to barrier immunity and regulation of host-microbe interactions.42,43 The detection of PGRP expression in secretory cells further suggests that soluble antimicrobial factors may contribute to luminal defense. Notably, the expression of PGRP-L3 in neoblasts indicates that stem cells themselves may participate in immune surveillance. This is a distinct feature from most other invertebrates, where PGRP expression is largely confined to differentiated epithelial cells or hemocytes. This stem cell-linked immune function suggests that in planarians, antibacterial recognition may be integrated with tissue regeneration and homeostasis, representing a lineage-specific adaptation within lophotrochozoans.27

Planarian antibacterial defense involves multiple cellular and molecular mechanisms that together contribute to pathogen clearance and maintenance of tissue homeostasis. Previous studies have shown that factors such as MORN2 promote bacterial clearance through LC3-associated phagocytosis, while stress-activated kinase pathways, including TAK1-MKK-p38 signaling, contribute to immune responses during microbial imbalance and tissue injury.25,30 In addition, epigenetic mechanisms in neoblasts have been shown to regulate antibacterial responses during reinfection.27 Although the precise relationships between these pathways and PGRP-mediated sensing remain to be defined, our findings raise the possibility that PGRPs may function as upstream recognition molecules that detect bacterial cell wall components and initiate or modulate downstream immune responses within this broader immune network.

Our infection experiments demonstrate that Smed-PGRPs are rapidly and differentially induced depending on the route of bacterial exposure. Intestinal infection triggered broad PGRP induction, whereas epidermal exposure resulted in a more selective response. These tissue-specific expression dynamics suggest that planarians tailor immune gene activation to the anatomical context of infection, a feature observed in other epithelial immune systems.33 In addition, the induction of several PGRPs following injury indicates that antibacterial defense programs are activated during wound repair, a period associated with increased vulnerability to microbial invasion. In this study, S. aureus was used as a representative pathogen because it is a well-established experimental infection model in planarians and reliably triggers antibacterial immune responses.25,27 Future studies examining Gram-negative bacteria and diverse environmental microbes will be important to determine whether individual PGRPs exhibit pathogen-specific recognition or broader antibacterial activity.

Functional depletion experiments establish that PGRPs play an active role in restricting bacterial persistence in vivo. Animals subjected to PGRP RNAi exhibited elevated bacterial loads and delayed clearance of S. aureus, demonstrating that these receptors contribute to host defense. Among the family members, PGRP-S3 emerged as a key effector, as its silencing resulted in increased bacterial accumulation in the intestinal lumen, enhanced intracellular persistence within enterocytes, and greater tissue invasion following epidermal infection. These phenotypes suggest that PGRP-S3 may function at multiple epithelial barriers to limit both luminal colonization and tissue penetration. This is consistent with previously described roles of secreted PGRPs as antibacterial effectors that restrict bacterial survival at host-microbe interfaces.5,44 Similar functions have been reported in other invertebrates, particularly insects, where secreted PGRPs directly limit bacterial growth and contribute to epithelial barrier immunity.33,44,45

Comparative functional studies in other invertebrates provide a framework for understanding planarian PGRPs. In D. melanogaster, short secreted PGRPs such as PGRP-SD act as direct antibacterial effectors, whereas long, membrane-associated PGRPs like PGRP-LC function as PRRs that activate the IMD signaling pathway.15,46,47 Similarly, in mollusks such as Crassostrea gigas, PGRPs contribute to epithelial barrier immunity and are upregulated following bacterial challenge, highlighting a conserved frontline defense role.5,48,49,50,51 Planarian PGRPs exhibit functional parallels: secreted PGRP-S proteins restrict bacterial proliferation at epithelial surfaces, while membrane-associated PGRPs are dynamically expressed in barrier tissues and stem cells, suggesting integrated recognition and effector functions akin to those observed in insects and other lophotrochozoans.

Beyond pathogen recognition, PGRPs have been implicated in broader roles related to immune homeostasis and inflammation across animal systems. In insects and mammals, PGRPs regulate immune tone at epithelial barriers, particularly in the gut, by shaping host-microbiota interactions and limiting excessive inflammatory responses.33,44 Dysregulated PGRP activity has been linked to chronic inflammation and inflammation-associated diseases, including colorectal cancer.43 Although our study focuses on antibacterial defense, the dynamic regulation of Smed-PGRPs during injury and their expression in stem and secretory cell populations suggest that PGRPs may contribute more broadly to immune-tissue crosstalk in planarians, reflecting ancestral functions that have been differentially elaborated in higher organisms.

While our structural modeling and functional RNAi analyses support a conserved role for planarian PGRPs in antibacterial defense, several mechanistic questions remain open. In particular, direct biochemical measurements will be required to determine ligand binding affinities and to establish whether secreted planarian PGRPs possess catalytic amidase activity toward peptidoglycan substrates. Likewise, the intracellular signaling pathways linking PGRP-mediated recognition to antimicrobial responses remain to be elucidated. Addressing these questions will require complementary biochemical and genetic approaches and represents an important direction for future investigation.

Together, our findings support a model in which planarian PGRPs act as conserved mediators of bacterial recognition and clearance at host-microbe interfaces. Comparisons with insects and mollusks highlight both shared mechanisms, such as epithelial barrier immunity and secreted antibacterial activity, as well as unique features, including stem cell linked immune surveillance. By placing functional PGRP activity in a lophotrochozoan context, this work bridges an important evolutionary gap between arthropod and vertebrate immune systems. More broadly, it highlights that core elements of innate immune recognition have been retained across deeply divergent animal lineages, while remaining sufficiently flexible to accommodate lineage-specific adaptations in immune strategy.52,53

Limitations of the study

While our structural analyses and docking predictions support conserved ligand recognition by planarian PGRPs, direct biochemical binding assays were not performed. Future in vitro binding studies will be required to experimentally validate ligand specificity and to determine binding affinities between PGRPs and peptidoglycan-derived ligands. In addition, although sequence analysis indicates that several secreted PGRPs retain conserved catalytic residues associated with amidase activity, direct biochemical measurements will be necessary to establish whether planarian PGRPs possess functional peptidoglycan-hydrolyzing activity.

RNAi in planarians typically results in partial transcript depletion rather than complete loss of gene function. Consequently, the phenotypes observed here may underestimate the full contribution of individual PGRPs to antibacterial defense. Functional redundancy among family members may further buffer phenotypic outcomes following single-gene knockdown. Moreover, due to the current lack of specific antibodies against planarian PGRPs, knockdown efficiency was assessed at the transcript level using RT-qPCR, and future studies will be required to evaluate protein-level regulation.

Our infection assays focused exclusively on S. aureus as a model Gram-positive pathogen commonly used in planarian infection studies. Consequently, the extent to which Smed-PGRPs mediate immune responses against Gram-negative bacteria, commensal microbes, or diverse environmental pathogens remains unclear. Because different classes of bacteria possess distinct cell wall structures and immune-stimulatory molecules, the functional roles and specificity of planarian PGRPs may vary depending on the microbial context. Future studies using a broader range of bacterial species will therefore be important to determine the generality and pathogen specificity of PGRP-mediated antibacterial responses in planarians. Finally, although expression analyses and loss-of-function experiments establish a requirement for PGRPs in bacterial clearance, the downstream signaling pathways and effector mechanisms that link PGRP recognition to antimicrobial responses were not directly investigated in this study. Elucidating these signaling pathways and their integration with other immune mechanisms in planarians represents an important direction for future work.

Experimental model and study participant details

The clonal asexual strain of Schmidtea mediterranea was used for all experiments. The animals were cultured in 1x Montjuic water (1.6 mM NaCl, 1.0 mM CaCl2, 1.0 mM MgSO4, 0.1 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM KCl, 1.2 mM NaHCO3, prepared in sterile distilled water) and kept at 20oC in the dark. Animals were fed goat liver once a week and cleaned every day. Animals were starved for at least 7 days before experiments.

Method details

Identification and in silico analysis of PGRPs

Protein sequences of known PGRPs from different organisms were obtained from UniProt and the transcript sequences of orthologs in S. mediterranea (asexual) were identified using BLAST against Planmine32 dataset of planarian transcripts. InterPro, NCBI Domain Search Tool, PROSITE, TMpred tool and SignalP-5.0 tool were used to identify the PGRP domain and other regions in all the identified planarian PGRP proteins. Uniprot IDs of 105 known PGRPs from 26 different organisms (3 vertebrates and 23 invertebrates) are listed in the Table S1.

Multiple sequence alignment

Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was performed using the MUSCLE algorithm implemented in MEGA (version 11 and 12) with default parameters. Alignments were exported in FASTA format, visualized, and manually curated using Jalview (version 2.11).

For domain-level analysis, PGRP domain sequences from 28 proteins across four species (Homo sapiens, Mus musculus, Drosophila melanogaster, and Schmidtea mediterranea) were aligned to identify conserved residues associated with substrate binding, zinc coordination, and amidase catalytic activity.

For broader evolutionary analysis, 112 PGRP sequences from diverse taxa were aligned and manually curated. Regions containing extensive gaps or low occupancy were removed by column-wise trimming to retain the conserved core regions of the alignment.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analyses were performed using MEGA (version 11 and 12) based on alignments of either 28 PGRPs from four representative species or 112 PGRPs from diverse taxa. Trees were constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method. The evolutionary distances were computed using the Poisson correction method. Pairwise deletion was applied to handle gaps and missing data. Statistical support for the inferred tree topology was assessed using bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates. Trees were visualized and annotated using the MEGA Tree Explorer.

Structural prediction and docking studies

The structures of the planarian, human and drosophila PGRP protein sequences were predicted using AlphaFold 2. To analyze the interaction between planarian PGRPs and different bacterial ligands such as Tracheal Cytotoxin (TCT) and Muramyl Tripeptide (MTP), we used UCSF Chimera and AutoDock Vina software tools.

Gene cloning and RNAi

Primers (Table S2) were designed for all the identified planarian PGRPs using Primer3. Gene cloning of all 7 PGRPs was done into Promega pGEM®-T Easy Vector (Cat. # A1360), followed by transformation into NEB® 5-alpha Competent E. coli (Cat. #C29871) cells. All clones were further used to synthesize single-stranded RNA probes and double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) for RNAi experiments.

For RNAi experiments, dsRNA was synthesized by in vitro transcription reactions using both SP6 and T7 polymerase. For knockdown of genes, animals were injected with ∼100 nL of dsRNA eight times over 2 weeks (Figure 5B). As a negative control for RNAi experiments, dsRNA targeting green fluorescent protein (GFP), which has no homolog in the S. mediterranea genome, was used.

Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH)

RNA probes labeled with digoxigenin and fluorescein were generated by in vitro transcription. In situ hybridization was performed as described previously64 with the following specifications. Briefly, animals were killed with 5% N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) and fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 20 min. Following PBST rinses and dehydration, animals were bleached in 6% H2O2. Bleached animals were hybridized with riboprobe overnight at 56°C, washed with 2× and 0.2× SSCx (Three washes, 20 min each), blocked in 5% horse serum (Gibco 16050130) for 2 h and incubated with anti-dig-AP (Roche 11093274910, 1:2000) and/or anti-FITC-POD (Roche 11426346910, 1:2000) antibody overnight at 4°C. Probes were detected by FastBlue (Fast Blue BB Salt [Sigma F3378] and naphthol AS-MX phosphate [Sigma N4875]) or tyramide amplification developments using FAM (ThermoFisher 46410) or TAMRA (Invitrogen C1171), and subsequently rinsed in PBST and stored in 80% Glycerol.

Immunohistochemistry

Planarian tissue was first treated with 2% HCL for 5 min on ice and fixed in formalin, then dehydrated through a graded series of alcohol solutions (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), and subsequently cleared using xylene (2 × 5 min). The tissue was then embedded in molten paraffin wax (6 × 20 min). Thin sections (∼10 μm thick) were cut using a microtome and used for immunostaining. Sections were deparaffinized with 5 × 3 min washes in xylene, washed 2 × 2 min in 100% ethanol, 90% ethanol, 70% ethanol, and then 1x PBS. Immunofluorescence experiments were performed to detect S. aureus (Invitrogen PA1-7246; 1:1000, overnight at 4°C) inside the planarian tissues such as intestine or epidermis. For experiments involving combined FISH and immunostaining, samples were first processed according to the FISH protocol and subsequently subjected to immunostaining. Hoechst dye (1:5000) was used as counterstain to mark cell nuclei.

Imaging and image analysis

Fluorescence images were taken with Olympus FV3000 laser scanning confocal microscope. All image processing and quantifications were done using the ImageJ, Adobe Photoshop, and Adobe Illustrator software. All images shown are representative of all images taken in each condition, and are maximal intensity projections, except otherwise indicated.

Reverse transcriptase-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Cat. # 15596026). For infection experiments, planarians infected with S. aureus for the different time periods (1, 3, 6, 9, 12, 24 h) were transferred in TRIzol Reagent and stored at −80°C. Uninfected planarians were used as the control. For regeneration experiments, planarians were amputated in 3 fragments. At the different time points (0, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48 h) fragments were transferred in TRIzol Reagent and stored at −80°C. Intact planarians were used as the control.

RNA isolation was performed from frozen samples, followed by TURBO DNase (Cat. # 2238G) treatment as per the protocol mentioned in the kit. RNA quality was assessed using gel electrophoresis and concentration was checked using Nanodrop. Using the isolated RNA, cDNA was synthesized for each sample using BIO-RAD iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Cat. # 1708891), and a 96-well RT-qPCR was performed using TaKaRa TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNase H Plus) reagent (Cat. # RR820A) using the QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Cat. # 4485691). CT values were calculated as the average of three technical replicates. The housekeeping gene gapdh was used to normalize the data. 2−ΔΔCt values are plotted as data in a bar graph, along with the standard deviation for each set of values. The primers used for gene amplification are listed in the Table S3.

Bacterial infection to planarians

Planarians were infected with S. aureus bacteria (ATCC 29213) either through water (108 CFU/mL) and/or into lumen by microinjection (5 × 1010 CFU/mL). For CFU counting experiment, PGRP silenced planarians were first infected by microinjection followed by amputation 24h later and reinfected through water. Infected fragments were homogenized in PBS at 4-day post infection. The lysate was passed several times through a sterile syringe with a 29G needle to disrupt planarian tissue clumps, and CFUs were counted after plating 100 μL serial dilutions onto nutrient agar plates. Plates were incubated overnight at 37oC and bacterial colonies were counted manually.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Prism software package (GraphPad Inc., La Jolla, CA). Comparisons between the means of two populations were done by a Student’s t test. For time-course expression analyses, statistical significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple-comparisons test. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistically significant was defined as a p-value of less than 0.05 (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001). Statistical tests, significance, data points, error bars, and exact value of n (number of animals) for each figure are provided in the legends.
