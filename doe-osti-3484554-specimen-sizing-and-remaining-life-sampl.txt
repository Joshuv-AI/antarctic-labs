Abstract
Advanced high temperature nuclear reactors rely on structural components that will oper-
ate for decades under combined mechanical, thermal, and environmental loading. Materials
surveillance programs are a promising strategy for managing the resulting uncertainty in
long-term structural integrity by monitoring degradation in service using passively actuated
mechanical test articles. Previous reports have developed a simplified, spreadsheet imple-
mentable framework for sizing these test articles and for inferring accumulated creep damage
and remaining life from ex-situ test data. This report advances that work toward practical
deployment by providing a sample problem book: a collection of worked, end-to-end ex-
amples in which the ASME Section III, Division 5 design analysis of a representative high
temperature reactor component is carried through to a sized surveillance article, verified with
detailed finite element analysis, and concluded with a remaining life assessment based on an
assumed ex-situ creep-rate measurement on the retrieved specimen. The report also sum-
marizes ongoing ANL engagement with ASTM Committee E10 toward advancing materials
surveillance guidance for advanced reactors.

1 Introduction
Structural materials in future advanced nuclear reactors will experience a combination of
elevated temperature mechanical loading, thermal cycling driven by normal and off-normal
reactor transients, and exposure to a challenging reactor environment. Depending on the
specific reactor concept, that environment may include high neutron fluxes, corrosive molten-
salt or liquid-metal coolants, or high temperature gas coolants. The mechanical and envi-
ronmental loadings can interact synergistically to produce creep, creep–fatigue, corrosion,
and irradiation damage in the reactor structural components [1, 2, 3]. Individually, each of
these degradation mechanisms has been studied for the candidate advanced-reactor alloys,
but combined effects test data—data that couple mechanical loading with the actual reac-
tor coolant and neutron spectrum—are limited, expensive, and time consuming to generate.
Generating a comprehensive combined effects database that spans all candidate alloys, all
coolant chemistries, and design lives of up to 60 years is not practical [4, 3]. This scarcity of
combined effects data, together with the comparative lack of long-term operating experience
for advanced reactors, introduces significant uncertainty in the long-term structural integrity
of future high temperature reactor components and drives either overly conservative design
margins or the inability to justify long service lives and future life extensions.
 An analogous materials qualification challenge was faced by the light water reactor (LWR)
fleet in the late 1950s and early 1960s. From nearly the beginning of commercial nuclear
energy, engineers understood that neutron irradiation could reduce the fracture toughness of
the steels used to build reactor pressure vessels and other primary components. Test reactor
data established the trend, but long-term, combined effects experiments capable of bound-
ing the in-service degradation for reactors with multi-decade lives were unavailable. The
industry’s response was a materials surveillance program: encapsulated Charpy toughness
specimens placed inside the reactor, periodically withdrawn, tested, and used to establish a
trend of vessel-steel toughness over time. If the measured trend indicated that the toughness
required to maintain adequate design margin would not be met, plant operating conditions
could be adjusted accordingly. This LWR surveillance methodology is now mature and cod-
ified. It is required by the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission through Appendix H to 10
CFR Part 50 [5] and standardized by ASTM E185 [6], first issued in 1961 and updated many
times since. Despite considerable evolution in scope—particularly a gradual narrowing of
the required mechanical tests toward Charpy toughness of the steel—the fundamental ap-
proach has remained constant: monitor the property of interest, in service, on representative
specimens.
 Direct transfer of the LWR surveillance approach to advanced reactors is insufficient for
two fundamental reasons. First, the acceptance criterion evolves with time: LWR surveil-
lance implicitly assumes that the mechanical strength required by design is essentially con-
stant over the plant lifetime and that the reactor environment reduces the actual, in-service
strength over time, so that the acceptance decision is a simple comparison between a fixed
target and a slowly evolving measurement. In high temperature reactors, by contrast, the
required strength itself changes with time because the ASME Section III, Division 5 de-
sign methodology accounts for the ongoing accumulation of creep and creep–fatigue damage
even in the absence of any environmental effect. A surveillance program for high temper-
ature service must therefore track not only the degradation of material properties but also
the evolving acceptance criterion against which those properties are judged [2, 7]. Second,

representative mechanical loading is essential: the dominant damage mechanisms in high
temperature reactor components are creep and creep–fatigue, which are driven by mechan-
ical loading. Unloaded, static specimens exposed only to the reactor environment (as in
LWR surveillance) will miss the damaging effect of cyclic mechanical loading and, more im-
portantly, any interaction between the mechanical loading and the reactor environment [4].
An ASTM standard specifically for high temperature reactor materials surveillance—ASTM
E531 [8]—has existed since 1975 and, in its most recent revision, permits passively loaded
surveillance articles; however, it remains vague on the specific form of the test article, on the
mechanical property measurements to be performed, and on how those measurements are to
be used to evaluate continued acceptability of the corresponding component [4]. Filling that
guidance gap is a central objective of the ongoing ANL/INL surveillance program.
 Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) has developed, in collaboration with Idaho National
Laboratory (INL), a materials surveillance methodology for advanced high temperature re-
actors based on passively actuated mechanical test articles. The concept was introduced in
Ref. [1], extended and analyzed in Refs. [9, 2, 4, 10, 11, 12], and brought to a simplified,
deployable form in Ref. [7]. The test article uses differential thermal expansion between
dissimilar-material bars to drive cyclic mechanical strain in a test section, using only the
reactor’s own temperature transients as the driving force; no active loading hardware is re-
quired. In its simplest representation—the three-bar model used throughout this report—the
article consists of a test section (the material to be surveilled), a driver with a lower coef-
ficient of thermal expansion, and a column that closes the load path. When the assembly
is heated and cooled, the mismatch in thermal expansion between the test section and the
driver imposes a displacement-controlled cyclic load on the test section. Because the load is
displacement-controlled, elastic follow-up of the surrounding structure plays a central role
in setting the amount of stress relaxation that occurs during high temperature holds, and
therefore in setting the creep–fatigue damage accumulated per cycle.
 Over the course of the ANL/INL surveillance program several test article geometries have
been fabricated and tested, including welded flat-plate designs, welded cylindrical designs,
and, most recently, a weld-free cylindrical interlocking design. The worked examples in this
report all assume the cylindrical interlocking geometry shown in Figure 1.1. This choice is
motivated by experimental evidence and fabrication experience accumulated to date. Both
the flat-plate and welded-cylindrical designs use bimetallic welds (for example, an IN718
filler joining an IN617 driver to a 316H specimen) to transfer load between the driver, the
specimen, and the column, and these bimetallic welds have been the principal source of
premature failure of the test article for two compounding reasons: stress concentration is
unavoidable at any weld (raising the local stress well above the nominal test-section stress and
driving crack initiation at the weld before the intended damage accumulates in the specimen
gauge section), and the weld filler and heat-affected zone have a modified microstructure that
offers less environmental protection than the parent metals and is preferentially attacked by
molten-salt coolants [13]. In addition, the welded flat-plate design suffers from an out-
of-plane bending problem: during the cool-down phase of each thermal cycle the driver
contracts and imposes a compressive stress on the thin specimen, and the specimen’s low
compressive stability causes it to buckle out of plane [3]. Bimetallic welding of thin, small test
article components between dissimilar high temperature alloys is also fabrication-intensive
and hard to reproduce reliably across the tens of articles that a full surveillance program

may require. The cylindrical interlocking design shown in Figure 1.1 removes bimetallic
welds from the load path entirely—the driver, specimen, and column are assembled by
interlocking geometry and mechanical fastening rather than by welding—and simultaneously
provides high compressive stability against out-of-plane bending, broader material selection
for driver and column, easier post-exposure dismantling, replaceable driver and column, and
an ASTM-compatible test-section geometry.

 A complete materials surveillance program built around such passively actuated test
articles requires several coordinated technology elements: a specimen sizing methodology
that determines the geometry of the surveillance article so that its test section experiences a
mechanical response representative of the critical location of the corresponding component
under the same thermal cycle; a family of passively loaded test article hardware designs (such
as Figure 1.1) that can survive the reactor environment and be practically manufactured,
retrieved, and post-tested ex-situ; a remaining life assessment methodology that turns ex-situ
test results on retrieved specimens into actionable estimates of the current damage state and
remaining life of the corresponding component, together with associated acceptance criteria;
and codification and standards engagement to translate the methodology into an industry
standard suitable for regulatory acceptance and industrial adoption [2, 4, 7]. The present
report focuses on the ANL contributions to the methodology (specimen sizing and remaining
life assessment) and to codification (ASTM engagement). From the perspective of a future
plant operator, the operational steps of the surveillance program are [7] to size the test
articles so that they mimic the component mechanical loading at the critical location under

ANL-AMMT-029                                     3

the temperature cycle the article will actually experience in the reactor; insert the test articles
into the reactor at appropriate locations and quantities to experience bounding thermo-
mechanical and environmental conditions while minimizing impact on component operation;
periodically remove one or more specimens and perform ex-situ mechanical property testing
and characterization; and apply defined acceptance criteria to the ex-situ test results to
determine whether the corresponding component remains acceptable for continued operation
through the next inspection interval.
 Depending on the plant’s operational needs, the maturity of available material behavior
models, and the required level of accuracy, three complementary surveillance program con-
cepts have been developed under the joint program [2, 4]. In canary surveillance, the test
articles are deliberately sized to accumulate damage faster than the corresponding compo-
nent and the acceptance test is a simple visual or NDE inspection for cracks or flaws on
the retrieved specimen; the article provides early warning of impending component failure,
recalling the canary in the coal mine. In twin surveillance, a second test article is maintained
outside the reactor and subjected to the same thermal history but without exposure to the
reactor environment; comparison of the ex-situ and in-reactor specimens isolates the envi-
ronmental contribution to material degradation and permits its extrapolation into future
operation. In the full remaining life assessment approach, detailed measurements on the
retrieved surveillance article—for example, ex-situ creep tests to determine the current creep
rate—are combined with an analytical model of the component response to infer the current
damage state and to project the remaining life directly. The full remaining life assessment
is the most quantitative of the three and is the primary focus of the worked examples in
this report; Sample Problem 1 (Chapter 4) demonstrates it in detail using the MPC Omega
remaining life expression of Section 2.3 evaluated at an illustrative ex-situ creep-rate mea-
surement, and Sample Problem 2 (Chapter 5) applies the same approach to the same vessel
with liquid sodium coolant.
 This report is organized as a sample problem book. Each worked example applies the
same end-to-end workflow to a different representative high temperature operating enve-
lope, giving a self-contained illustration of how to combine an ASME Section III, Division
5 design analysis, the simplified surveillance article sizing tool, a three-dimensional finite
element verification, and MPC Omega remaining life estimation from an assumed ex-situ
creep-rate measurement, into a coherent surveillance evaluation. The report contains two
worked examples. Sample Problem 1 (Chapter 4) treats a 316H flat head pressure vessel
with FLiBe molten salt coolant subjected to representative thermal cycling; this example is
developed at full length and serves as the reference case around which the remaining chap-
ters are structured. Sample Problem 2 (Chapter 5) treats the same 316H flat head pressure
vessel with liquid sodium coolant, illustrating how the same workflow accommodates a lower-
temperature, longer-hold operational envelope. Chapter 2 summarizes the simplified sizing
framework and the MPC Omega remaining life expression at the level of detail needed to
interpret the worked examples; detailed derivations are not repeated here and the reader is
referred to Ref. [7]. Chapter 3 defines the common four step workflow that is subsequently
applied in each of the two worked examples, together with the ASME Section III, Division 5
time-fraction creep-damage calculation used to compute nominal creep–fatigue life from an
inelastic stress–temperature history (Section 3.3.1). Chapter 6 describes ANL FY26 activi-
ties related to ASTM standards engagement and outlines the path forward for codification.

2 Simplified sizing and remaining life methodology
This chapter summarizes the simplified sizing and remaining life methodology developed in
[7] at the level of detail needed to interpret the worked examples in Chapters 4 and 5. Full
derivations of the closed-form expressions, together with their finite element verification, are
provided in that reference and are not repeated here.

2.1 Three-bar surveillance article concept

The surveillance articles considered in this report are based on the three-bar mechanical
model illustrated in Figure 1.1. The assembly consists of a test section (bar t), a driver (bar
d), and a column (bar c) constrained together at their ends. Differential thermal expansion
between the components during reactor temperature transients drives axial strain in the test
section, producing a cyclic stress–strain response that can be tailored to match that of a
target component location. Because the assembly is displacement-controlled, elastic follow-
up plays a central role in governing stress relaxation and creep damage accumulation in the
test section.
 The mechanical response of the test section is characterized by two parameters derived
from a component design analysis:

  (i) the total mechanical strain range ∆ε after accounting for time-independent plasticity
 and creep, and
 (ii) the elastic follow-up factor q, describing the ratio of creep strain to elastic strain during
 stress relaxation.

 Earlier sizing approaches matched the entire stress–strain hysteresis loop of the test
section to that of the critical component location using numerical optimization coupled to
a detailed inelastic constitutive model of the test-section material. The simplified method
used throughout this report instead classifies the thermo-mechanical response into a small
number of bounding cases and uses closed-form expressions to relate geometry, material
properties, and loading parameters directly to the target strain range and elastic follow-up.
This simplified approach offers several practical advantages over the earlier optimization-
based approach: it eliminates the need for numerical optimization and for a full inelastic
constitutive model during sizing, requiring only temperature-dependent moduli, thermal-
expansion coefficients, yield strengths, and a minimum-creep-rate correlation for the test-
section material; it is spreadsheet implementable rather than requiring complex analysis
software; and its closed-form expressions make the mapping from material properties and
loading parameters to surveillance article geometry directly traceable, so that plant engineers
and reviewers can trace each sizing decision back to its physical origin.

2.2 Simplified specimen sizing framework

The simplified sizing framework represents the three-bar surveillance article as three idealized
bars, models the test section as linear elastic–perfectly plastic with power-law creep occurring
only during the high temperature hold, and constrains the driver and column to remain
elastic and creep-free through appropriate choice of geometry and material. Under these

ANL-AMMT-029                                     7

idealizations the stress–strain response of the test section during thermal cycling can be
classified into four bounding cases, and closed-form expressions relate the geometry and
material properties to the stabilized total strain range ∆ε and the elastic follow-up factor
q. The following subsections describe (i) the modeling assumptions and their geometric
implications, (ii) the material property inputs, (iii) the stress relaxation and “thermal driving
force” concepts on which the case classification is based, (iv) the four hysteresis-loop cases
and their case criteria, (v) the closed-form expression for the elastic follow-up factor, (vi)
the test-section buckling check, and (vii) the resulting spreadsheet workflow.

2.2.1 Model assumptions and geometric constraints
The three-bar idealization used throughout is shown in Figure 1.1. Bart (the test section,
the material of interest) has length Lt and cross-sectional area At ; bard (the driver) has Ld ,
Ad ; and barc (the column) has Lc = Lt + Ld and Ac . The following modeling assumptions
are adopted [7]:

  Creep confined to the hot hold. Creep strain accumulates only during the hold at
 T2 ; creep during the heat-up and cool-down ramps is neglected because the bars only
 briefly reach high temperature during those transients.
  Test-section constitutive behavior. Bart is modeled as linear elastic–perfectly plastic
 with no kinematic or isotropic hardening, plus Bailey–Norton power-law creep active
 during the high temperature hold at T2 (Section 2.2.2).
  Column constitutive behavior. Barc is modeled elastically and is sized so that time-
 independent plasticity does not occur and creep remains negligible over the hold time
 (Section 2.2.1 imposes the corresponding geometric constraint At /Ac ≤ fc ).
  Driver constitutive behavior. Bard is modeled elastically and is selected and sized so
 that time-independent plasticity does not occur and creep remains negligible at the
 hold temperature over the intended hold time. This assumption requires the driver
 material to have a sufficiently high yield strength and creep resistance relative to the
 test-section material over the operating temperature range.
  Same test-section / column material. The closed-form derivation of Section 2.2.5 (Eqs.
 (2.5)–(2.6)) assumes that the column shares the test section’s Young’s modulus and
 coefficient of thermal expansion. This is the setup used in Ref. [7] and in both worked
 examples of this report (316H test section, 316H column). If a different column material
 is required, the closed-form expressions in Section 2.2.5 must be generalized to carry
 separate Ec and αc ; the column-no-creep geometric constraint of Eq. (2.1a) must also
 be re-derived for the different column-material yield strength.

2.2.4 Four hysteresis-loop cases
The four bounding hysteresis-loop cases, as shown in Figure 2.1, are distinguished by which
of σ t , Sy,T1 , and Sy,T2 dominates. In each case the stabilized total strain range ∆ε admits
a closed-form expression involving σ b (Eq. 2.4), σ t (Eq. 2.5), the temperature-dependent
moduli and yield strengths, and αt (T2 − T1 ). The case criteria and qualitative behavior are:

  Case 1: σ t < Sy,T2 (elastic shakedown, no yield anywhere). The first cycle relaxes from
 σ t to σ b ; from cycle 2 onward the test section cycles elastically between σ b and σ b − σ t

 ANL-AMMT-029                                         10

 with no additional creep or plastic strain. The stabilized hysteresis loop degenerates
 to a line and no cyclic creep–fatigue damage accumulates beyond cycle 1.
  Case 2: Sy,T2 ≤ σ t < Sy,T1 + σ b (yield at T2 on cycle 1 only). The first cycle yields
 at T2 then relaxes to σ b ; from cycle 2 onward the response is again elastic shakedown
 between σ b and σ b − σ t .
  Case 3: Sy,T1 + σ b ≤ σ t < Sy,T2 + Sy,T1 (yield at T1 and T2 on cycle 1; from cycle
 2, yield at T1 only, plus stress relaxation at T2 ). From cycle 2 the test section cycles
 between –Sy,T1 (yielding at T1 ) and an intermediate σ a at T2 that relaxes to σ b during
 the hold. A stabilized hysteresis loop with nonzero enclosed area is formed and creep
 damage accumulates each cycle. Both sample problems in this report fall in Case 3.
  Case 4: σ t ≥ Sy,T2 + Sy,T1 (repeated yielding at both T1 and T2 every cycle). Every
 cycle yields at T2 (relaxing from Sy,T2 to σ b ) and at T1 (–Sy,T1 ), giving the largest
 stabilized loop and the highest per-cycle creep–fatigue damage of the four cases.

 Explicit expressions for the total strain range ∆ε for each case are given in Ref.
 [7] and are implemented in the spreadsheet sizing tool.

2.3 Remaining life estimation via MPC Omega

Once a surveillance specimen has been retrieved from the reactor, a short ex-situ creep test
on the specimen provides a direct measurement of the current minimum creep rate εmeas of
the material. The MPC Omega framework of API 579-1/ASME FFS-1 Part 10 [17] converts
this single measurement into an estimate of the specimen’s remaining rupture life at the
operating conditions of the surveillance article. The calculation proceeds in three stages:
the ex-situ test conditions (σ test , Ttest ) are chosen to match the stabilized hold conditions of
the surveillance article; the multiaxial Omega parameter Ωm is evaluated at those conditions
from the API 579 Annex 10B correlations; and the remaining rupture life L is computed
by inserting Ωm and the measured minimum creep rate into the MPC Omega rupture-life
expression. The remainder of this section develops each stage in turn. The generic Step-4
procedure that turns the outputs of this section into a remaining life estimate for a sample
problem — including the closed-form derivation of σ test from the Chapter 2 spreadsheet

outputs, the explicit choice of the Omega adjustment factors, and the cycle-by-cycle iteration

it to specific sample problems.

ature at which the ex-situ creep test is conducted. σ test is taken as the initial stress at

3.1 Step 1: ASME Section III, Division 5 design analysis of the component

The component of interest is first analyzed using ASME Section III, Division 5 design by
elastic analysis rules [18]. The purpose of this step is twofold. First, it establishes a baseline
creep–fatigue design life for the component that will serve as the reference against which the
sized surveillance article is later compared. Second, and more importantly, it produces the
target inputs for the simplified sizing tool at the critical location governing creep–fatigue life:

ANL-AMMT-029                                    17

  The stabilized total mechanical strain range ∆ε, corrected for time-independent plas-
 ticity and single cycle creep in accordance with the elastic-analysis rules;
  The hot and cold coolant temperatures, T2 and T1 , and the hold time at T2 .

 The elastic follow-up factor q at the critical location is a further structural-response
parameter required by the Step-2 sizing calculation. Unlike ∆ε and the temperature/hold
quantities above, q is not a direct output of the ASME design-by-elastic-analysis and must
be estimated separately: either by a dedicated structural analysis of the component, or
bracketed by exercising the sizing calculation over a range of representative q values. Prior
work on materials surveillance for advanced high temperature reactors adopts q = 3.0 as a
representative bounding value for pressure-vessel components of this class [1], and this value
is carried through to the Step-4 remaining life estimation in every worked example of this
report.

3.2 Step 2: Surveillance article sizing using the simplified spreadsheet

The target strain range and elastic follow-up factor from Step 1 are used as inputs to the
simplified spreadsheet sizing tool of Chapter 2. The test-section and column materials are
chosen to match, or be compatible with, the component material. The driver material is
chosen to remain elastic and creep-free over the operating temperature range (Alloy 617 is
used throughout this report). The spreadsheet returns the geometric ratios Ld /Lt and At /Ad
for each prescribed q, along with the corresponding hysteresis-loop case classification and
the buckling and modeling-assumption checks.
 Absolute test-section dimensions (Lt and At ) are then chosen based on manufacturability,
buckling margin, and constraints imposed by the surveillance article housing. Absolute
dimensions of the driver and column follow from the spreadsheet ratios.

3.3 Step 3: Three-dimensional finite element verification

The sized surveillance article is analyzed with a three-dimensional finite element (FE) model
using material-specific inelastic constitutive models for all components. The purpose of
this step is to check whether the actual surveillance article, with its realistic 3D geometry
and constraint, reproduces the target strain range and generates a creep–fatigue damage
accumulation comparable to that of the component under the same prescribed reactor coolant
temperature cycle. Because the simplified sizing framework of Chapter 2 makes several
idealizations (uniform uniaxial stress in each bar, purely elastic driver and column, no local
stress concentrations at the interlocking joints), 3D FE verification is an essential check before
the sized surveillance article is deployed. For each sample problem, FE verification consists
of two inelastic FE analyses and a tabulated three-way comparison of nominal lives. The first
FE analysis is of the component itself and produces the stress–temperature history at the
critical location and the corresponding nominal creep–fatigue life. The second FE analysis is
of the sized 3D surveillance article, using the material-specific inelastic constitutive models
described below, and produces the stress–temperature history at the gauge region and the
corresponding nominal creep–fatigue life. The three nominal lives compared in each sample
problem are: the component (from the vessel FE analysis), the surveillance article test section

 ANL-AMMT-029                                    18

from the inelastic three-bar analytical model, and the surveillance article test section from
the 3D FE analysis. All three are computed with the same nominal-property creep-damage
calculation described in Section 3.3.1; no design margins are applied, and the lives are used
solely for comparison.
 Both the vessel FE analysis and the surveillance article analyses use inelastic constitutive
models implemented in NEML [19] and coupled with MOOSE [20]. The constitutive formu-
lation combines temperature-dependent isotropic linear elasticity with two simultaneously
active inelastic mechanisms: rate-independent Chaboche cyclic plasticity with Voce isotropic
and Chaboche kinematic hardening, and a rate-dependent viscoplastic flow rule representing
creep deformation. The same constitutive formulation is used throughout, with parameters
calibrated separately to the respective material data: the 316H calibration is used for the
vessel, test section, and column, and the Alloy 617 calibration is used for the driver and
end caps. The 3D surveillance article FE analysis additionally performs a coupled thermal–
structural simulation that explicitly resolves contact among the test section, driver, column,
and end caps using the MOOSE contact capability. As shown in Figure 1.1, the top and
bottom end caps integrate the test section, driver, and column and provide the connections
among them. A complete description of the surveillance-specimen geometry is provided in

3.3.1 Nominal creep–fatigue life from the simulated stress–temperature histories

3.4 Step 4: Remaining life estimation from ex-situ creep-rate measurement

The final step turns a single assumed ex-situ creep-rate measurement on the retrieved surveil-
lance specimen into a plant-operator-actionable remaining life estimate using the MPC
Omega framework of Section 2.3. This section defines the generic Step-4 procedure once,
so that the sample problem chapters (Chapters 4 and 5) need only report the component
specific inputs and results.

3.4.1 Spreadsheet closed-form procedure
The spreadsheet closed-form procedure produces the initial hold stress σ test , the relaxed
hold-stress history σ(t) over one operating cycle, and the per-hold creep damage ∆Dc cycle
entirely from the closed-form expressions of Chapter 2. The Step 2 spreadsheet (Section 2.2)
already supplies the thermal driving force σ t (Eq. 2.5), the hysteresis-loop case classification,
and the initial hold stress σ test = σ a at the test temperature Ttest = T2 ; for Case 3, which
applies to both sample problems in this report, the specimen cycles between −Sy ,T1 at T1
and σ a at T2 with σ a = σ t − Sy ,T1 . Step 4 adds two further sub-steps:

 (1) Compute the stress relaxation profile σ(t) over one hold using Eq. (2.4). Starting
 from σ a at t = 0 and integrating the Bailey–Norton creep law of Eq. (2.2) at T = T2
 through the closed-form solution of Eq. (2.4) with the representative elastic follow-up
 factor q gives a monotonically decreasing hold-stress history from σ a to a relaxed value
 σ b at t = thold . Per Section 2.2.3, the same σ(t) profile is taken to apply to every hold
 in the load history.

 (2) Compute the per-hold creep damage from Eq. (2.14b) and iterate cycle by cycle to
 failure. The per-cycle creep-damage increment ∆Dc cycle is given by Eq. (2.14b) with
 both Ωm and εco evaluated at the instantaneous stress σ(t) along the relaxation profile
 of sub-step (1). Per-cycle fatigue damage ∆Df cycle = 1/N nominal(∆ε, Thold ) uses
 the nominal fatigue curve of the test-section material evaluated at the stabilized total
 mechanical strain range ∆ε and the hold temperature. Cumulative creep and fatigue
 damage are accumulated from t = 0 along the full σ(t), T(t) history and are compared
 against the ASME bilinear creep–fatigue interaction envelope of Eq. (3.3); past cycles
 (t ≤ N0 ·t period) use the baseline API 579 εco , and future cycles (t > N0 ·t period)
 use f·εco , where f = εmeas /εco is the ex-situ measurement multiplier. The failure cycle
 n* is the first cycle at which the interaction envelope is violated, and the remaining
 cycles after retrieval are given by Eq. (2.15b) with N0 the number of operating cycles
 completed at the time of retrieval.

3.4.2 Choice of Omega adjustment factors
The creep-ductility adjustment factor ∆cd,Ω in Eq. (2.8) shifts Ωm to reflect the position
of a specific heat, product form, or heat treatment within the material-class creep-ductility
scatter band. API 579-1 permits ∆cd,Ω to be varied between −0.3 (bounding ductile behavior,
smaller Ωm , longer L) and +0.3 (bounding brittle behavior, larger Ωm , shorter L), with
∆cd,Ω = 0 the average of that scatter band. Throughout the sample problem chapters, ∆cd,Ω

ANL-AMMT-029                                    21

= −0.3 (ductile bound) is adopted because the test-section material 316H is a high-ductility
austenitic stainless steel. Choosing ∆cd,Ω = −0.3 shifts the surveillance-workflow prediction
toward the ductile end of the API 579 creep-ductility scatter band.
 The strain-rate adjustment factor ∆sr,Ω in Eq. (2.9a) shifts the API 579 baseline creep-
rate correlation to reflect where a specific heat falls within the correlation’s inherent scatter
band. API 579-1 permits ∆sr,Ω to be varied between −0.5 and +0.5. Because ∆sr,Ω enters the
correlation with a leading minus sign, ∆sr,Ω = −0.5 corresponds to the upper scatter band
(faster minimum creep rate at a given (σ, T), shorter remaining life — the conservative
end for a safety-critical remaining life estimate) and ∆sr,Ω = +0.5 corresponds to the lower
scatter band (slower creep rate, longer remaining life — the non-conservative end); ∆sr,Ω =
0 is the midpoint average behavior. Because ∆sr,Ω has no effect on Ωm or on the closed-form
relaxation profile σ(t), a shift in ∆sr,Ω is numerically equivalent to a proportional shift in
the surveillance-measurement multiplier f = εmeas /εco : the creep-damage rate scales exactly
by a factor of 10ˆ(+∆sr,Ω ), and in the creep-dominated large-life limit Nrem therefore scales
approximately by the same factor, i.e., ≈ 3.16× shorter (longer) at ∆sr,Ω = −0.5 (+0.5) than
at ∆sr,Ω = 0. Small deviations from this proportionality arise from the whole-cycle failure
criterion, the subtraction of N0 , and cumulative-fatigue interaction.
 The geometry factor αΩ in Eq. (2.11) is set to αΩ = 1 throughout the sample problem
chapters per API 579-1 Annex 10B.8, which specifies αΩ = 1 for uniaxial specimens and “all
other components and stress states” when a small-strain (small-displacement) computational
treatment is used, as is the case for the finite element analyses in this report. For sources
P1 and P2 the specimen is uniaxial by construction and the Prager triaxiality parameter
δ Ω in Eq. (2.13) is zero, so Ωm = Ωn + nBN . For sources P3 and P4 the specimen and
vessel critical locations are multiaxial; δ Ω at each source is evaluated from the (σ vM , σ Hudd )
invariants of the stabilized hold state and held constant during the damage integration.
This stabilized-hold triaxiality approximation is used in place of a pointwise δ Ω (t) evaluation
because δ Ω is dominated by the long high temperature hold portion of each cycle, over which
the principal-stress ratios change only weakly; a pointwise δ Ω (t) treatment is identified as a
refinement in Section 7.3.

 (P2) Inelastic three-bar analytical: tabulated σ(t), T(t) history at the test-section mid-

 (P3) 3D FE surveillance article: tabulated stress–temperature history at the gauge-region

3.4.4 Sensitivity to the ex-situ measurement and to the baseline scatter band
Sensitivity to the surveillance measurement is handled at the workflow level: the creep-
damage rate scales exactly with f = εmeas /εco per Eq. (2.14b), and in the creep-dominated
regime of the ASME creep–fatigue envelope (which holds for every entry of the multi-source
tables in Chapters 4 and 5) Nrem therefore scales approximately inversely with f. An ex-situ
measurement returning εmeas = 2 εco approximately halves the Nrem values; εmeas = 3 εco ap-
proximately reduces them by a factor of three; small deviations from perfect proportionality
visible in Tables 4.6 and 5.7 reflect the whole-cycle failure criterion and the fatigue-damage
interaction. The API 579 baseline scatter itself contributes an additional ±3.16× bracket
via ∆sr,Ω as described in Section 3.4.2; this bracket is displayed as an error bar on every
MPC Omega entry of the multi-source figures and represents the API 579 minimum-creep-
rate correlation scatter band around the mean-property estimate. It is one component of
the overall remaining life uncertainty; other contributions include the choice of σ(t) source,
the choice of creep-rupture correlation (MPC Omega vs. Larson–Miller), the choice of ∆cd,Ω ,
the geometry and axial-to-multiaxial mappings, and the ex-situ measurement itself. For a
safety-critical remaining life assessment, ∆sr,Ω = −0.5 is used as the conservative bound of
the API 579 minimum-creep-rate correlation scatter, corresponding to a faster baseline creep
rate and therefore a shorter predicted remaining life; this is material-property conservatism,
independent of any deliberate canary margin introduced through surveillance article sizing.
Whether the surveillance article provides a deliberate canary margin should be assessed
separately from the specimen-to-component damage or life relationship under a common

material-property basis, and can be imposed explicitly through the sizing target if required.

3.4.5 Discussion of workflow-consistency choices
Three internal inconsistencies of the spreadsheet + MPC Omega workflow are recognized and
are common to every sample problem in this report; the quantitative impact is illustrated
once in Section 4.5 and reported for the second sample problem in Section 5.5.
 (a) Two creep-rate models within one workflow. The closed-form relaxation profile σ(t) of
sub-step (1) is generated from the Bailey–Norton creep law of Eq. (2.2) via the closed-form
solution of Eq. (2.4); the damage integrand of sub-step (2) uses the API 579 baseline εco of
Eq. (2.9a). The two correlations are calibrated to different 316H creep-strain databases and
do not in general agree at the stabilized hold conditions. Eq. (2.2) enters Eq. (2.4) because
the closed-form solution of Eq. (2.4) requires a constant Bailey–Norton stress exponent,
which Eq. (2.2) satisfies and the API 579 cubic in log σ does not.
 (b) Two rupture-time correlations for nominal and remaining life. The nominal creep–
fatigue life reported in Step 3 uses the average Larson–Miller correlation of Section 3.3.1.
The remaining life estimate developed here uses either the API 579 MPC Omega correlation
or that same Larson–Miller correlation. The two correlations are independently calibrated
and give different Nrem at the same σ(t), T(t) history; the multi-source comparison reports
both variants so the plant engineer can see the correlation-choice sensitivity directly.
 (c) API 579 Ω and εco are jointly calibrated. The API 579 Ω and εco polynomial coef-
ficients (Section 2.3) are calibrated to the same underlying 316H rupture and creep-strain
database. Substituting a different creep-rate law (e.g., Eq. 2.2) into the damage integrand
of Eq. (2.14b), while keeping the API 579 Ω unchanged, breaks the framework’s intended
self-consistency. In each sample problem chapter this effect is quantified by recomputing the
source P1 remaining life with the damage integrand along σ(t) evaluated from Eq. (2.2)
instead of API 579 εco (Tables 4.5 and 5.6).
 These inconsistencies are pragmatic features of a spreadsheet-executable workflow, not
defects of the underlying frameworks. Unifying the creep-rate model in Eq. (2.4) with the
API 579 εco is identified as a future-work item in Section 7.3.
 The remainder of the report applies this workflow to two worked examples: a 316H flat
head pressure vessel with FLiBe molten salt coolant (Chapter 4) and the same 316H flat
head vessel with liquid sodium coolant (Chapter 5).

3.5 Scope and limitations

The sample problems in Chapters 4 and 5 are illustrative methodology demonstrations, not
a codified acceptance procedure. Key assumptions and limitations of the present treatment
are:

  The elastic follow-up factor q is not a direct output of the ASME design-by-elastic-
 analysis; a representative or bracketing value (here q = 3.0, following Ref. [1]) is
 adopted (Section 3.1).
  The ex-situ measurement multiplier f = εmeas /εco is a single ex-situ test point and is
 assumed to apply uniformly across the future in-reactor (σ, T) history (Section 2.3).

  The thermal boundary conditions used in the FE analyses are simplified representations
 of the coolant, cover-gas, and environment thermal paths (Sections 4.1 and 5.1).
  The MPC Omega implementation choices adopted here — ∆cd,Ω = −0.3, αΩ = 1,
 stabilized-hold δ Ω held constant during damage integration, ±0.5 ∆sr,Ω scatter-band

     (Section 3.4.2).
  The inelastic constitutive simulations do not explicitly couple irradiation-induced or

4.1 Component description and loading

The component considered is a 316H flat head pressure vessel containing FLiBe molten salt
coolant at a nominal pressure of 0.2 MPa. A nearly stationary helium cover gas at 0.2 MPa
occupies the region above the salt. In the finite element model both the coolant pressure
and the cover-gas pressure are applied as uniform pressure boundary conditions on their
respective wetted surfaces; hydrostatic variation with elevation is neglected. The vessel is
assumed to be perfectly insulated along the vertical outer surface. The outer surface of the
vessel head is not insulated and loses heat to argon at 100 ◦ C. The salt temperature cycles
between 500 ◦ C (cold) and 650 ◦ C (hot) on a total cycle period of 1000 h, consisting of a
10 h heat-up ramp, a 980 h hold at 650 ◦ C, and a 10 h cool-down ramp. The geometry and
thermal boundary conditions are shown schematically in Figure 4.1.
 Three distinct thermal boundary conditions are applied to the vessel in the finite element
model, one on each of the three externally exposed surfaces. Each is described below with
the corresponding numerical values used in the analysis.

4.6 Discussion

Sample Problem 1 exercises the four step common workflow end-to-end for a 316H flat head
pressure vessel with a 1000-h FLiBe cycle at 500–650 ◦ C. The ASME Section III, Division
5 design-by-elastic-analysis evaluation of the vessel gives a creep–fatigue design life of 66
cycles (≈7.5 yr) and a target total mechanical strain range ∆ε = 0.115% at the vessel
knuckle. Sizing the surveillance article at the representative elastic follow-up factor q = 3.0
produces a Case-3 hysteresis and a three-bar analytical nominal life of 5063 cycles, and 3D FE
analysis of the sized surveillance article gives 6338 cycles; both are on the same order as the
vessel nominal life of 6469 cycles (Table 4.4). Figure 4.6 shows that at the stabilized cycle
the analytical three-bar test-section, 3D FE surveillance-article gauge, and vessel critical-
location Huddleston effective stresses all fall within an approximately 3 MPa band, with the
analytical stress running slightly above the vessel and the 3D FE gauge stress between the
two. The small specimen–vessel life difference reflects two effects examined in Section 4.4: the
mapping from the bulk cross-sectional dimensions produced by the spreadsheet sizing tool
to the full 3D specimen geometry (Figure 4.3 convention), and the mapping from the axial
three-bar sizing target onto the multiaxial vessel stress state. For Sample Problem 1 both
effects are small. Parametric studies of the spreadsheet-to-3D geometry mapping convention
and of the axial-to-multiaxial stress mapping are identified as principal future-work items in
Section 7.3.
 The Step-4 remaining life results in Figure 4.7 illustrate the two structural features of the
workflow discussed generically in Section 3.4: the MPC Omega and Larson–Miller correla-
tions give Nrem estimates that differ by a factor of 2–3 within any given σ(t) source, and the
±0.5 ∆sr,Ω scatter-band bracket ≈ 3.16× either way about the MPC Omega average spans
this correlation gap. Across σ(t) sources, the P1–P4 ordering reflects the combined effect of
stabilized-cycle stress magnitude, stress-state triaxiality (through δ Ω for MPC Omega and
through the Huddleston effective stress for LMP), and the selected material correlation; for
comparable uniaxial sources (P1, P2) the ordering tracks the stabilized-cycle initial hold
stress directly, while at the multiaxial P4 vessel source the triaxiality contribution can be
significant. Substituting the Bailey–Norton alternative of Eq. (2.2) for the API 579 base-
line εco in the damage integrand shortens the source P1 MPC Omega estimate noticeably
(Table 4.5), a consequence of the workflow-consistency issue discussed in Section 3.4.5 (c).

5.1 Component description and loading

The component considered is a 316H flat head pressure vessel containing liquid sodium
coolant at a nominal pressure of 0.12 MPa. A nearly stationary argon cover gas at 0.02 MPa
occupies the region above the sodium. As in Sample Problem 1, both the coolant pressure
and the cover-gas pressure are applied as uniform pressure boundary conditions on their
respective wetted surfaces; hydrostatic variation with elevation is neglected. As in Sample
Problem 1, the vessel is assumed to be perfectly insulated along the vertical outer surface.
The outer surface of the vessel head is not insulated and loses heat to air at 30 ◦ C. The
sodium temperature cycles between 400 ◦ C (cold) and 550 ◦ C (hot) on a total cycle period
of 10 000 h (approximately 1.14 years), consisting of a 10 h heat-up ramp, a 9980 h hold at
550 ◦ C, and a 10 h cool-down ramp.
 The vessel geometry is identical to Sample Problem 1, and the same three-surface ther-
mal boundary-condition treatment described in Section 4.1 is used, with numerical values
updated for the sodium coolant, argon cover gas, and ambient air environment of the present
problem:

 (i) Sodium-wetted vertical wall. Convective boundary condition of the Newton form of Eq.

6 ASTM standards engagement and path forward
The sample problems developed in the preceding chapters are intended not only as technical
demonstrations of the simplified surveillance methodology but also as supporting material
for codification of a surveillance program in a formal industry standard. This chapter sum-
marizes ANL activities in FY26 that support that codification path and outlines the path
forward for standards development.
 The most mature standards home for a passively actuated surveillance program is ASTM
Committee E10 on Nuclear Technology and Applications, in particular Subcommittee E10.02
on Behavior and Use of Nuclear Structural Materials. E10.02 already maintains two directly
relevant standards: ASTM E185 [6], covering surveillance of light water reactor pressure-
vessel steels via Charpy-based specimens, and ASTM E531 [8], which permits passively
loaded surveillance article designs but does not currently prescribe rules for geometry, load-
ing, or remaining life assessment.
 ANL along with INL participated in the February 2026 ASTM Symposium on Structural
Material Challenges for Long-Term Operation, held in Atlanta, GA. A joint INL/ANL pre-
sentation covering the passively actuated surveillance methodology and supporting combined
effects data was delivered to the symposium audience, introducing the ANL/INL surveillance
program to the broader ASTM structural-materials community and providing an early op-
portunity for feedback in advance of the subsequent E10.02 Subcommittee engagement.
 ANL staff also attended the June 2026 ASTM E10 Committee Week and presented an
overview of the ANL simplified surveillance methodology to Subcommittee E10.02. The
purpose of this engagement was twofold: to familiarize the committee with the technical
basis for a surveillance standard for advanced reactors, and to familiarize ANL staff with
ASTM policies and procedures in preparation for drafting standard language in the outyears.
 The ASTM engagement summarized above has established both a reception of the ANL
passively actuated surveillance methodology within Subcommittee E10.02 and a concrete
direction for near-term standards development. Discussion indicated strong interest in DOE-
NE’s leadership in high temperature surveillance technology development, and participants
noted that they were not aware of another active E10 effort currently focused on this topic.
Members agreed that ASTM E531-23 [8] provides useful general guidance on surveillance-
program design but lacks detailed implementation procedures for passively loaded surveil-
lance articles of the type developed under this program.
 Building on this reception, the near-term path forward for ASTM standards development
is to work with Committee E08 on Fatigue and Fracture to develop consensus test article
geometry and testing procedures for passively actuated surveillance articles, and to engage
ASME on the corresponding acceptance criteria, including remaining life-based criteria of
the type demonstrated in Chapters 4 and 5 of this report.

7 Conclusions and future work
7.1 Summary

This report advances the passively actuated materials surveillance program by demonstrating
the methodology through worked sample problems and supporting ongoing efforts toward
practical implementation and codification. The vehicle for that advancement is a sample
problem book : a collection of end-to-end worked examples that apply the simplified sizing
and remaining life methodology of [7] to representative high temperature reactor operating
envelopes using a common four step workflow — ASME Section III, Division 5 design-by-
elastic analysis of the component, spreadsheet-based sizing of a three-bar surveillance article,
3D inelastic finite element verification of the sized article, and MPC Omega remaining life
estimation from an assumed ex-situ creep-rate measurement.
 Two worked examples are developed on the same 316H flat head pressure vessel geometry:
Sample Problem 1 (Chapter 4) with FLiBe molten-salt coolant on a 1000-h cycle at 500–
650 ◦ C, and Sample Problem 2 (Chapter 5) with liquid sodium coolant on a 10 000-h cycle
at 400–550 ◦ C. Together they demonstrate application of the common workflow across two
operating envelopes and show that the simplified sizing method produces surveillance-article
responses comparable in scale to those at the vessel critical location, with close agreement
in Sample Problem 1 and a larger specimen–vessel difference in Sample Problem 2. The
examples also identify two residual modeling issues — the axial-to-multiaxial mapping and
the spreadsheet-to-3D geometry mapping — that are addressed as dedicated future-work
topics in Section 7.3. The two examples are intended to provide a technical reference for
future implementation activities, standards development, and regulatory discussions.

7.2 Observations across the sample problems

Several observations emerge across the worked examples that are relevant both for future
methodology development and for standards development.

  Elastic follow-up is a key design parameter. The strain range required by the sizing
 tool is obtained from the ASME analysis, whereas the surveillance article geometry is
 strongly sensitive to the selected elastic follow-up factor q. Because q at the component
 critical location depends on structural constraint and is generally not known with
 high precision, the selected value should represent or conservatively bracket the elastic
 follow-up at the critical location of the component being surveilled. Where practical,
 q should be estimated through a dedicated structural analysis; otherwise, the sizing
 calculation should be exercised over a representative range.
  Verification does not equal margin. FE verification evaluates whether the sized surveil-
 lance article produces a response comparable to that of the component; it does not by
 itself establish design margin. If a deliberate surveillance margin is required, it should
 be introduced explicitly during sizing, for example by selecting a target strain range
 somewhat greater than the component strain range, and then demonstrated under a
 common material-property basis.
  The stress-history comparisons show close agreement in Sample Problem 1 and larger,
 but systematically interpretable, differences in Sample Problem 2. The inelastic three-

ANL-AMMT-029                                    61

 bar test-section stress, the 3D FE surveillance article gauge stress, and the vessel
 critical-location stress are very close for Sample Problem 1. For Sample Problem
 2, the differences are larger, and the 3D FE surveillance article is appreciably more
 highly stressed than the vessel. Two modeling steps contribute to these differences: the
 geometry-mapping step, which translates the bulk cross-sectional dimensions produced
 by the spreadsheet sizing tool into a full 3D specimen geometry using the convention
 illustrated in Figure 4.3; and the axial-to-multiaxial mapping step, which relates the
 uniaxial three-bar sizing target to the multiaxial vessel stress state at the critical loca-
 tion. The contribution of these two steps varies between the two problems and merits
 dedicated parametric study (Section 7.3). The observed specimen–vessel life difference
 does not by itself demonstrate a deficiency in the underlying three-bar sizing method;
 the individual contributions of the geometry and axial-to-multiaxial mappings should
 be isolated before assigning cause.
  For the At /Ac values examined, nominal life is only weakly sensitive to At /Ac when
 ∆ε and q are fixed. Table 4.2 shows that the two At /Ac values examined in Sample
 Problem 1 produce three-bar analytical nominal lives that differ by no more than about
 2.4%. Provided At /Ac is selected so that the column remains free of significant creep
 and time-independent plasticity (Section 2.2.1), the simplified sizing framework retains
 one geometric degree of freedom for each prescribed (∆ε, q). Within the assumptions
 and parameter range examined, At /Ac may therefore be selected primarily based on
 practical considerations such as manufacturability and envelope constraints.
  Estimated remaining life scales approximately as the inverse of the surveillance-derived
 creep-rate multiplier in the creep-dominated regime. Within the assumption embedded
 in Eq. (2.14b) that the ex-situ measured creep rate departs from the API 579 baseline
 by a constant multiplier f = εmeas / εco across the future (σ, T) history, the estimated
 remaining life scales approximately as 1/f in the present creep-dominated examples
 (Tables 4.6 and 5.7). This relationship provides a simple first-order way to communi-
 cate remaining life sensitivity. A single-point ex-situ measurement, however, provides
 information on the current creep response at the prescribed test condition; extending
 that result over the future operating stress–temperature history through a constant
 multiplier is a working assumption of the present framework rather than a demon-
 strated material property. A more refined interpretation of the ex-situ measurement
 may therefore be required where that assumption is expected to break down.

7.3 Future work

Priorities for future work are as follows.

  1. Parametric study of the spreadsheet-to-3D specimen-geometry mapping. The present
 examples indicate that translating the bulk cross-sectional dimensions produced by the
 spreadsheet sizing method into the full 3D interlocking geometry can shift the gauge
 stress and corresponding nominal life. Parametric studies of the geometry-mapping
 convention — including the local test-section profile, interlocking-notch design, and
 driver–test interface geometry — should quantify the contribution of this mapping to

 ANL-AMMT-029                                      62

 the specimen–vessel life difference and determine whether geometric refinements can
 improve consistency across loading regimes.
 2. Axial-to-multiaxial mapping study. The current workflow maps the equivalent total
 mechanical strain range ∆ε obtained from the ASME Step-1 analysis at the multi-
 axial vessel critical location onto a uniaxial three-bar sizing target, and the resulting
 three-bar response is then compared with the nearly uniaxial 3D FE specimen gauge
 response. A systematic study of these mapping conventions is required. As one possi-
 ble alternative, the uniaxial three-bar target could be selected to reproduce the vessel
 nominal creep damage per cycle calculated using the Huddleston effective stress and
 the nominal time-fraction procedure of Section 3.3.1, rather than matching only the
 equivalent strain-range target. Such a damage-based mapping would provide an ex-
 plicit link between the uniaxial surveillance article response and the multiaxial vessel
 response.
 3. Integrate experimental validation data. Incorporate available combined effects, strain,
 and other relevant measurements from ongoing surveillance article validation cam-
 paigns into the sample problems as those data become available, providing experimen-
 tal validation alongside the analytical and FE verification of Step 3.
 4. Advance standards-development activities. Develop proposed additions or revisions to
 ASTM materials-surveillance guidance for passively actuated test articles, drawing on
 this sample problem book as one supporting technical input and coordinating with the
 appropriate ASTM committees and ASME as described in Chapter 6. The objective
 is to establish a consensus path for test article geometry, testing procedures, damage
 inference, reporting, and corresponding acceptance criteria without presupposing the
 specific final standards vehicle.
 5. Develop a creep-rate-consistent stress relaxation treatment for MPC Omega. Develop
 a stress relaxation formulation consistent with the API 579 baseline εco correlation, us-
 ing numerical integration or a suitable validated approximation, so that the relaxation
 and MPC Omega damage calculations can be based on a common creep-rate calibra-
 tion. Because the API 579 Ω and εco correlations are jointly calibrated, retaining that
 common calibration would preserve the internal consistency of the MPC Omega for-
 mulation. This work should also establish a reproducible benchmark calculation for
 the implemented API 579 equations before the remaining life procedure is used as a
 standards or acceptance basis.
 6. Develop and validate a technical basis for surveillance margin selection. Evaluate
 candidate over-strain factors, elastic-follow-up bracketing approaches, and other sizing
 margins under a common material-property basis. The objective is to determine how an
 intentional specimen-to-component damage or life margin can be imposed, quantified,
 and verified before prescriptive guidance is recommended for deployment.
