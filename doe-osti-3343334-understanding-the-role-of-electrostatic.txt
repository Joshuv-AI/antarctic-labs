In both the 2D and 3D cases, we found that solitons formed along the beam axis, with the biggest
pair of solitons forming of both sides of the electron source, as shown in Figs 2
(RHS) and 3. The largest soliton in the 3D case appears in the upstream direction of the beam,
as shown in Fig. 3. This is because a 3D plume expansion creates a relatively higher potential near
the ion source (at z = 0 cm) than the 2D case. This causes more electrons to become attracted
and move upstream of the electron source to form a soliton. Since the solitons, by trapping the
electrons in a loop in phase space, hinder the neutralization of the beam, the understanding of
these solitons and their relative positions with respect to the electron source is necessary for
understanding beam neutralization.

In addition to the solitons, we also found long-wavelength waves traveling along the beam axis
in our 3D simulations, as shown in the inserts of Fig. 4. To our knowledge, these are the first time
such waves have been seen in a PIC simulation and reveal the power of the ultra-high spatial and
time resolution that we are able to obtain. These waves appear in the time-accurate simulation
as the beam is being neutralized (i.e. 𝑡𝑡 < 4.5 𝜇𝜇𝜇𝜇) and then persist even beyond 𝑡𝑡 = 4.5 𝜇𝜇𝜇𝜇. The
presence of long wavelength waves, however, was not observed in the 2D case, shown in Fig. 2.
Plasma waves characterized by the term “long” wavelength means that their wavelength is a
large fraction of the domain length and they are much larger than the Debye length of the plasma
beam.

Since these waves had not been observed in such a simulation previously, we spent a great deal
of effort to understand why we see them and their origin. From the analysis of the timescale and
wavelength, we ruled out multi-stream and Bunemann instability mechanisms for this wave.
Another peculiar thing about this wave was its high phase speed of about 2 × 10V m/s, which is
higher than most of the electron present in the beam (as can be seen from phase plots in Fig. 3).
While there is no physical limit that waves in a plasma should have phase speeds smaller than
the electron particle speeds, such waves are rare as they must be initially excited by the high
velocity particles in the tail of the distribution.
