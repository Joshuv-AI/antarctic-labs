The tumor suppressor menin is a key scaffold mediator for homologous recombination repair
Authors: Aobo Lian, Xingwen Zhu, Bangming Jin, Guixin Gu, Bin Xu, Li Zhang, Xiao Lin, Xuan Zhu, Guanghui Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSETD2-mediated H3K36me3 reprogramming is involved in homologous recombination (HR) repair; however, the dynamics of this repair process remain unclear. Here, we report that the scaffold protein menin, encoded by the Men1 gene, plays a crucial bridging role in H3K36-mediated HR repair. Genetic ablation of Men1 resulted in spontaneous lung carcinogenesis and was accompanied by severe genomic instability. Mechanistically, menin directly recognizes histone H2A.X via its Val371–Gln400 motif, and DNA damage markedly promotes the specific interaction between menin and γH2A.X. Subsequently, SETD2 is recruited to DNA damage sites by menin and acts as a writer to catalyze H3K36me3 reprogramming at local chromatin. LEDGF, as a reader, recognizes the H3K36me3 marker and further recruits CtIP as an effector to perform DNA end resection, ultimately activating the RPA2/Rad51 pathway. Restoring H3K36me3 via pharmacological intervention rescued impaired HR repair in Men1-deficient cells, further validating that menin governs HR repair in an H3K36me3-dependent manner. The results of the present study provide the detailed molecular mechanism underlying the previously described functions of H3K36me3 remodeling in HR repair.

Introduction

The persistent accumulation of DNA damage has a range of molecular consequences, including genome instability, which is a major driver of cancer and genetic disease. Cells utilize either homologous recombination (HR) or nonhomologous end-joining (NHEJ) pathways to repair DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) [1]. NHEJ is an error-prone repair mechanism that directly re-ligates broken DNA ends at the cost of potential mutations [1]. HR utilizes sister chromatids as templates to mediate faithful repair and serves as the principal mechanism for preserving genomic integrity [1]. During the initiation of HR repair, the MRE11–Rad50–NBS1 (MRN) complex detects DSBs and recruits ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM), which is subsequently activated by the tat-interactive protein of 60 kDa (TIP60) to phosphorylate H2A.X [2]. In the later stages of HR repair, breast cancer type 1 susceptibility protein (BRCA1) facilitates C-terminal binding protein-interacting protein (CtIP)-mediated DNA end resection and, in complex with PALB2–BRCA2, promotes radiation-sensitive protein 51 (Rad51) filament assembly to drive error-free DNA repair through strand invasion [3]. Recently, emerging evidence has revealed that histone reprogramming, particularly SET domain-containing 2 (SETD2)-mediated histone 3 lysine 36 trimethylation (H3K36me3), may constitute a critical step in HR repair [4]. H3K36me3 deposition near DSBs is specifically recognized by lens epithelium-derived growth factor (LEDGF, encoded by Psip1), which recruits activated CtIP to enhance DNA end resection and Rad51 filament assembly [5]. Currently, the dynamic mechanism through which H3K36me3 remodeling regulates the HR process remains incompletely understood.

Menin, encoded by the multiple endocrine neoplasia type (Men1) gene, acts as an essential scaffold protein that interacts with epigenetic writers to orchestrate histone modifications, including the trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine residues K4 (H3K4me3), K9 (H3K9me3), and K36 (H3K36me3) [6–8]. The best-characterized model involves the recruitment of mixed-lineage leukemia 1/4 (MLL1/MLL4; encoded by Kmt2a/Kmt2d) by menin to specific chromatin loci, which activates the transcription of Hoxs and Meis genes via H3K4me3 in leukemia [9]. Recently, we reported that menin directly interacts with SETD2 to maintain the homeostasis of myeloid cells through H3K36me3 remodeling [8]. Menin can also act as a reader that specifically recognizes H3K79me2 marks on chromatin [10]. We have previously demonstrated that menin plays a critical tumor-suppressive role in lung cancer [11–15]. Men1 deficiency induces spontaneous lung cancer, accelerates KRasG12D mutation-driven lung cancer progression, and is accompanied by severe accumulation of DNA damage in mice [11]. Although our previous studies revealed that menin suppresses malignant phenotypes of lung cancer through the regulation of oncogenes, such as pleiotrophin [13], how menin functions as a driver gene to control lung tumorigenesis has not been fully elucidated. Men1 deficiency leads to HR repair defects and compensatory enhancement of NHEJ in melanocytes [16]. Menin also interacts with replication protein A (RPA), Fanconi anemia group D2 (FANCD2), and checkpoint kinase 1 (Chk1), whose functions are linked to homology-directed DNA repair [17–19]. These findings suggest that menin controls lung carcinogenesis through its potential role in DNA damage repair.

Here, we describe a molecular mechanism by which menin, acting as a scaffold protein, assembles the menin–SETD2–LEDGF–CtIP complex to promote HR repair.

Materials and methods

Mouse models

All mouse experiments were performed in compliance with ethical regulations and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xiamen University (approval number: XMULAC20230278). Animal welfare was monitored throughout the study. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-h:12-h light:dark cycle, 21.1 ± 1°C ambient temperature, and 30%–70% relative humidity. All mice were maintained on a C57BL/6 background. The following genotypes were generated using previously described methods [11, 20]: WT, Men1f/f, Men1f/f; Ubc-Cre, Kmt2af/f, Kmt2af/f; Ubc-Cre, Setd2f/f, Setd2f/f; Ubc-Cre, LSL-KRasG12D/+; Men1f/f; Sftpc-Cre, LSL-KRasG12D/+; Setd2f/f; Sftpc-Cre, and LSL-KRasG12D/+; Men1f/f mice. Mice were randomized into groups with equal numbers of males and females; sample sizes are indicated in the figure legends. At 6–8 weeks of age, all conditional KO mouse models received daily i.p. injections of 100 mg/kg tamoxifen (TAM; Sigma) dissolved in corn oil containing 10% ethanol for five consecutive days. For survival studies, mice were humanely euthanized when they exhibited predefined humane endpoint criteria, including labored breathing, >20% weight loss, or significantly decreased mobility [11]. Any deaths not related to the disease, such as infighting, were excluded from the analysis as pre-established. Sources of all mouse strains are detailed in the Supplementary Table S1.

Cell lines and culture

A549, NCI-H1975, A375, HaCaT, PLC/PRF/5, and HT-29 cells were obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures. NCI-H157 and G401 were obtained from the Cell Culture Center of the School of Basic Medicine; GP2-293 was obtained from Takara. HeLa (CCL-2, original strain), MCF7, HEK293T, and SH-SY5Y cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The cells were maintained at 37°C, 5% CO2, and 95% humidity and cultured in the following media (BasalMedia): A549 in F-12K medium; NCI-H157 and NCI-H1975 in RPMI-1640 medium; GP2-293, HEK293T, HaCaT, and A375 in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM); PLC/PRF/5, HeLa, and MCF7 in MEM medium; SH-SY5Y in DMEM/F12 (1:1) medium; and G401 and HT-29 in McCoy’s 5A medium. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; VivaCell), 100 U/ml penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco). All cells were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling within six months prior to experimentation and were routinely confirmed to be mycoplasma-free using the Myco-Lumi Luminescent Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Beyotime). The resources of cell lines are detailed in the Supplementary Table S1.

Primary mouse embryonic fibroblast cells

Primary mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) were isolated from E13.5 embryos of genetically engineered mice with the specified genotype, as previously described [11]. Pregnant mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Uterine horns were dissected, and embryos were separated, then digested in 0.25% trypsin supplemented with 0.02% EDTA overnight at 4°C. Dissociated cells were centrifuged at 1500 × g for 5 min, washed with DMEM, and plated. To induce gene KO, first-passage MEFs were treated with 1 µM 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT; Sigma) for 48 h. Genomic DNA was then extracted, and successful recombination was confirmed by genotypic analysis. MEFs were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (VivaCell), 100 U/ml penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco), and 0.1 mM non-essential amino acids (NEAAs; Gibco). All MEFs were used at low passages (2–4) and routinely screened for mycoplasma contamination, with all results being negative.

Gene knockdown (KD) and overexpression

The siRNAs targeting Men1, Setd2, Psip1, and Ctip were synthesized by Sigma–Aldrich. A549 cells were transfected with 50 nM siRNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (Yeasen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For stable knockdown of Men1 and Setd2, lentiviruses were produced by transfecting HEK293T cells with shMen1, shSetd2, or shLuc (control) plasmids using linear polyethyleneimine (PEI; MW 25 kDa). The siRNA and shRNA sequences are listed in the Supplementary Table S2. For Men1 overexpression, retroviruses were produced by transfecting GP2-293 packaging cells with the pLNCX2-Men1 or the empty pLNCX2 vector (control) plasmids using linear PEI. In both lentiviral and retroviral systems, viral supernatants were collected at 48 h post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane, and used to infect target cells in the presence of 10 µg/ml polybrene (Sigma). Infected cells were selected with 1 µg/ml puromycin (Sigma) for stable KD or 1 mg/ml G418 (Amresco) for overexpression, for a duration of two weeks.

Ionizing radiation treatment

Cells were cultured for 24 h and pretreated as described in the figure legends. Following pretreatment, cells were exposed to specified ionizing radiation (IR) doses via an X-ray irradiator (RAD SOURCE) at a dose rate of 2.03 Gy/min and harvested at designated timepoints for subsequent analyses as detailed in figure legends.

Men1 f/f and Men1∆/∆ mice received 10 Gy total-body IR and were euthanized at the indicated post-IR timepoints. Lung tissues were isolated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for IF assays.

Peptide synthesis

All peptides were synthesized by Sangon Biotech with a purity of over 95%. Briefly, Pep242 [8] (covering amino acids 231–248 of menin) and Pep-MH (covering amino acids 371–400 of menin) were fused with the cell-penetrating octa-arginine sequence (RRRRRRRR) to enable efficient cellular internalization. Biotinylated Pep-MH and its corresponding mutant control peptide (Pep-Mut) were also generated, and their detailed sequences are listed in Supplementary Fig. S3C. All peptides were dissolved in sterile double-distilled water before experimental use.

Plasmid construction

Plasmid construction was performed as previously described [8]. DNA constructs expressing Men1, Setd2, or H2A.X fragments were generated by PCR and inserted into pLVX-EGFP-HA, pLNCX2-Flag, or pLNCX2-HA vectors. Plasmids were amplified in DH5α cells and extracted using the FastPure EndoFree Plasmid Maxi Kit (Vazyme) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

HR and NHEJ reporter assays

DR-GFP reporter plasmids (a gift from Dr. Enrico V. Avvedimento [21]) and pimEJ5-GFP reporter plasmids (Addgene) were used to assess HR and NHEJ, respectively. To establish A549 DR-GFP and A549 EJ5-GFP cells, the pDR-GFP or pimEJ5-GFP plasmid was stably integrated into A549 cell lines as previously described [22, 23]. Following 24 h of siRNA transfection, reporter cells were transfected with pBSK (vector, as a negative control) or pCBASceI using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (YEASEN), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with the addition of drugs where indicated. Cells were then incubated for 48 h to permit DSB repair. GFP-positive cells were quantified by flow cytometry (FACSCalibur; ∼20 000 events per sample), and the data were processed using FlowJo software (v.10.7.1).

Apurinic/apyrimidinic site assay

Apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP) sites were quantified using the DNA Damage-AP Sites Assay Kit (Abcam). Genomic DNA was isolated by phenol–chloroform extraction. For each sample, 5 µg DNA was incubated in the binding buffer with the aldehyde-reactive probe, followed by washing and proteinase K (Beyotime) treatment. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and AP sites per 100 000 nucleotides were calculated based on the standard curve of the kit.

Immunofluorescence assay

Immunofluorescence (IF) assays were performed as previously described [11]. Following treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, permeabilized, and blocked in phosphate-buffered saline(PBS) containing 5% goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100, then incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. Species-matched secondary antibodies were applied, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI-containing mounting medium (VECTASHIELD). Images were acquired using a Nikon A1R laser scanning confocal microscope or a Leica DM2700 P fluorescence microscope, with all images in a single figure panel captured under identical exposure settings. Positive cells were defined as cells with >10 foci per nucleus. Colocalization and fluorescence intensity were analyzed with FV10-ASW and ImageJ software. The resources of antibodies are listed in the Supplementary Table S1.

UV laser micro-irradiation

A549 cells expressing EGFP-tagged constructs were plated on 35-mm glass-bottom dishes (NEST) and pre-sensitized with 10 µM BrdU (Sigma) for 24 h to enhance DNA photosensitivity. Prior to irradiation, nuclei were visualized by staining with 5 µM Hoechst 33258 (MCE) for 10 min at 37 °C. Micro-irradiation was performed on an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope fitted with a 60× oil-immersion objective and an on-stage incubator maintaining 37°C and 5% CO2. Using ZEN software, approximately 5–8 µm strips were manually drawn across roughly 50 nuclei per condition and irradiated with a 405 nm laser. The laser was set to deliver 50 mW at the focal plane with an exposure time of approximately 120 ms per strip. Time-lapse imaging commenced immediately, capturing EGFP fluorescence every 10 s for 5 min.

Alkaline comet assay

MEFs were harvested at indicated times post-IR, resuspended in ice-cold PBS, and adjusted to 1 × 105 cells/ml. Slides were pre-coated with 90 µl of 1% normal melting point agarose and solidified at 4°C. A total of 10 µl of cell suspension (1000 cells) was mixed with 80 µl of 0.5% low melting point agarose at 37°C, layered onto the pre-coated slides, covered, and gelled at 4°C for 10 min. After coverslip removal, a third layer of 60 µl low melting point agarose was applied. Slides were immersed in ice-cold lysis buffer containing 2.5 M NaCl, 100 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris, and 1% Triton X-100 (pH 10) for 2 h at 4°C in the dark, then transferred to alkaline electrophoresis solution (300 mM NaOH, 1 mM EDTA, pH >13) for 40 min. Electrophoresis was conducted at 25 V (0.86 V/cm) for 25 min at 4 °C. Slides were neutralized in 0.4 M Tris–HCl (pH 7.5) for 15 min, stained with 20 µg/ml ethidium bromide for 5 min, and visualized using a fluorescence microscope with excitation at 515–560 nm and emission above 590 nm. Fifty non-overlapping comets per slide were analyzed with CASP software (v1.2.2) to determine tail DNA percentage and tail moment. All procedures were performed under dim light to prevent additional DNA damage.

Chromosome conformation capture assay

Chromosome conformation capture (3C) assays were performed as described previously [24] with minor modifications. MEFs were crosslinked in 10 ml of serum-free DMEM containing 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. The reaction was quenched with 125 mM glycine. Cells were lysed in ice-cold lysis buffer (10 mM Tris pH 8.0, 15 mM NaCl, 0.2% NP-40, and 1 mM PMSF) for 15 min on ice. Nuclei were pelleted, washed with restriction enzyme buffer, and resuspended in 500 µl of the appropriate digestion buffer (TaKaRa; H Buffer for BamHI or K Buffer for EcoRI). Chromatin was permeabilized with 0.5% SDS for 15 min at 37°C, followed by SDS sequestration with 1% Triton X-100 for 15 min at 37°C. A 10% aliquot of crosslinked cells was reserved as an input control and processed without enzymatic digestion or ligation. The remaining chromatin was digested with 200 U of BamHI or EcoRI (TaKaRa) for 6 h at 37°C with gentle rotation. Digestion efficiency was monitored by removing a 5% aliquot prior to ligation. Restriction enzymes were inactivated by adding SDS to 2% and incubating at 65°C for 45 min. Ligation was performed in a 2 ml volume containing 500 U T4 DNA Ligase (TaKaRa) at 16°C for 4 h. Reactions were stopped with 10 mM EDTA and treated with 200 µg/ml Proteinase K (Beyotime) at 50°C for 2 h followed by 65°C overnight to reverse crosslinks. DNA was purified by phenol/chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation, treated with 20 µg/ml RNase A (Beyotime), and quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo). Ligation products and digestion efficiency were quantified by qPCR using SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche) on a LightCycler 96 system with primers (listed in the Supplementary Table S3) spanning restriction sites.

Cell proliferation detection

Cells stably expressing shLuc, shMen1, or shSetd2 were plated in 96-well plates and treated with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or increasing doses of specified drugs for 72 h. Cell viability was assessed using CCK-8 assays (OBIO) following the manufacturer’s protocol; absorbance at 450 nm was measured via a microplate reader, and relative viability was calculated by normalization to DMSO-treated controls. IC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism 8.

For colony formation assays, cells were plated in six-well plates and treated with DMSO or specified drugs at designated concentrations, either alone or in combination, for 24 h, followed by replacement with drug-free medium. Colonies were allowed to form over an 8–10 days incubation period, with the medium refreshed every 48 h. At the end, colonies were fixed and stained with 0.5% crystal violet, washed, dried, and imaged.

Chromatin extracts isolation

Chromatin extracts isolation was performed as previously described [25]. A549 cells (4 × 106) were irradiated with 5 Gy and harvested at the indicated time points. Cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and resuspended in 200 µl of Buffer A (10 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 10 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.34 M sucrose, 10% glycerol, 1 mM DTT, 10 mM NaF, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1× phosphatase inhibitor cocktail, and 1 mM PMSF) for 10 min on ice. Permeabilization was performed with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min on ice. Nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation at 1300 × g for 5 min at 4°C, and the cytoplasmic supernatant was discarded. The isolated nuclei were lysed in 200 μl of solution B (3 mM EDTA, 0.2 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT, 1× phosphatase inhibitor cocktail, and 1 mM PMSF). The chromatin was collected by centrifugation at 1700 × g for 5 min at 4°C, washed once with solution B, and then centrifuged at 10 000 × g for 1 min at 4°C. The chromatin pellet was resuspended in micrococcal nuclease (MNase) buffer, mixed with 50 U of MNase (Beyotime), and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The reaction was terminated by adding 5 mM EGTA and 5 mM EDTA. After centrifugation at 3000 × g for 30 min at 4°C, the supernatant was passed through a WGA agarose column (Vector). The flow–through was collected as the chromatin extract. Finally, boiled at 100°C for 10 min with 5× SDS–PAGE loading buffer.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed as previously described [8]. Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with 1 µg/ml leupeptin (MCE), 1 µg/ml pepstatin (MCE), 1 mM PMSF, and 1× phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. Cell lysates were incubated on ice for 20 min, sonicated, and clarified by centrifugation at 13 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Protein concentration was quantified using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo). Denatured proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE, transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore), and probed with indicated primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and immunoreactive bands were detected using ECL reagent (Thermo) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Immunoprecipitation assay

Immunoprecipitation (IP) assays were performed as previously described [8], using either the Immunoprecipitation Kit with Protein A+G Magnetic Beads (Beyotime) or Flag-tag/HA-tag Protein IP Kits (Beyotime), following the manufacturer’s instructions. For whole–cell lysates, cells were lysed in IP lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors. When DNase I treatment was applied, lysis buffer was supplemented with 1 M MgCl2 to a final concentration of 5 mM, and samples were incubated with 20 U/ml DNase I (Sigma) for 20 min at 37°C to eliminate DNA-mediated indirect protein-protein interactions [26]. The lysates were centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to remove debris. For each reaction, 1 mg of total protein was incubated overnight at 4°C with rotation either with Protein A+G magnetic beads pre-bound with 1 µg of the primary antibody or IgG control, or with anti-Flag or anti-HA magnetic beads. The beads were then washed three times with cold lysis buffer. For chromatin extracts, the flow through from the WGA column (prepared as described in “Chromatin extract isolation”) was directly incubated with the indicated beads overnight at 4°C with rotation [25]. The beads were then washed three times with wash buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.9, 0.2 mM EDTA, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM KCl, 0.05% NP–40, 10% glycerol, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM benzamidine, 0.2 mM PMSF, 1× phosphatase inhibitor cocktail, and 1 mM STZ). 2% of each preparation was saved as input, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 1× SDS–PAGE sample loading buffer for 5 min.

Pull-down assay

Pull-down assays were conducted as described previously [8] with minor modifications. Men1 cDNA was cloned into pET-28a to produce a C-terminal Flag- and N-terminal His-tagged fusion protein. The SET domain of SETD2, full-length H2A, H2A.X, and the H2A.X-S139E mutant were individually cloned into the pET-32a vector. This cloning strategy yielded recombinant proteins with an N-terminal His tag; the SET protein harbored a C-terminal Trx tag, whereas H2A, H2A.X, and H2A.X-S139E were fused with C-terminal EGFP-HA and Trx dual tags. Recombinant proteins were expressed in BL21 cells induced with 0.1 mM IPTG at 16°C for 24 h with shaking and purified by nickel-chelating affinity chromatography using a His-tag Protein Purification Kit (Beyotime). For each pull-down, 20 µg of purified protein was incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-Flag or anti-HA magnetic beads (Beyotime) that had been prewashed. For competition assays, synthetic peptides or competitor proteins were added at increasing concentrations during the binding step. For biotinylated peptide pull-down, the biotinylated peptide was pre–incubated with streptavidin beads for 2 h at 4°C, and then incubated with the indicated purified protein overnight at 4°C. Beads were washed three times with PBS containing protease inhibitors, and bound proteins were eluted with 1× SDS loading buffer, resolved by SDS–PAGE, and visualized with Coomassie blue staining or immunoblotting.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed as previously described [8] using the ChIP Assay Kit (Millipore). Cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 20 min at 37 °C, then lysed in ChIP lysis buffer and sonicated. Lysates were centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was divided: one portion served as input control, while the remainder was equally aliquoted and incubated overnight at 4°C with specific antibodies or normal IgG as the negative control. Immune complexes were washed sequentially with low- and high-salt buffers, eluted, reverse-crosslinked in 200 mM NaCl at 65°C for 4 h, and treated with proteinase K (Beyotime). DNA was purified by phenol/chloroform extraction and quantified by qPCR using the Roche LightCycler 96 system. The resources of antibodies and primer sequences are listed in the Supplementary Tables S1 and S3.

Adenovirus (Ad) production and tracheal instillation

Adenoviral vectors were generated using the Adeno-X Adenoviral System 3 (Takara). The target gene promoter was amplified from mouse genomic DNA using a high-fidelity polymerase (Vazyme). The CMV promoter and Cre recombinase coding sequence were amplified from plasmid pOG231 (Addgene). The primer sequences are listed in the Supplementary Table S4. Purified DNA fragments (TIANGEN) were assembled with the linearized adenoviral backbone using a seamless cloning kit (Vazyme). The resulting construct was transformed into Stellar competent cells, and positive clones were selected and expanded for plasmid extraction (TIANGEN). Plasmid sequences were verified by Sanger sequencing. Verified plasmids were digested with PacI (Yeasen), purified, and transfected into HEK293T cells using the calcium phosphate method for adenovirus packaging. Viruses were harvested, purified, and concentrated using an adenovirus purification kit (Biomiga). LSL-KRasG12D/+; Men1f/f mice were anesthetized with 2.5% Avertin. Recombinant adenovirus was delivered via orotracheal intubation. Immunosuppression was achieved with cyclosporine A (MCE), administered one week before and two weeks after viral instillation. Cell-specific KO efficiency was validated by crossing LSL-KRasG12D/+; Men1f/f mice with R26CAG-LSL-luci-tdTomato/+ reporter mice to generate LSL-KRasG12D/+; Men1f/f; R26CAG-LSL-luci-tdTomato/+ compound mutants, in which Cre-mediated recombination was reported by tdTomato expression.

In vivo treatment with compound JIB-04

KMS and KSS mice received TAM to induce lung tumorigenesis, with Men1f/f and Setd2f/f mice serving as WT controls. Beginning on day 2 after TAM injection, mice receivedi.p. injections every 3 days with either 20 mg/kg JIB-04 (formulated in 10% DMSO and 90% corn oil) or vehicle (10% DMSO in corn oil) for a total of 12 doses. Tumor burden was monitored by microcomputed tomography at the time points specified in Supplementary Fig. S8G.

Micro-computed tomography

Micro-computed tomography (microCT) detection was performed as previously described [11]. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and lungs were scanned using a Quantum FX microCT scanner (Siemens Inveon PET/CT; Germany) at 1.4 mm resolution, 80 kV, 500 µA current, with CT values calibrated to -1000 to -800 Hounsfield Units. Images were acquired with Inveon Research Workplace IRW4.2.

Immunohistochemistry staining

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining were performed as previously described [11]. Isolated lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 3 µm. Tissue sections were subjected to HE staining or immunostaining, with primary antibodies listed in the Supplementary Table S1. Stained sections were imaged and quantitatively analyzed as previously established [11]; whole-slide scanning was performed using a Motic microscope with Motic DSAssistant Lite software.

Whole-exome sequencing analysis

At 12 months post-TAM induction, normal lung tissues from Men1f/f mice, adjacent nontumor tissues, and tumor tissues from Men1∆/∆ mice were collected and subjected to whole-exome sequencing (WES) and subsequent bioinformatic analysis by Novogene (Beijing, China). Library preparation involved fragmentation using a Covaris M220 system to generate 180–280 bp inserts, followed by end repair, adenylation, adapter ligation, and hybridization capture using the SureSelect All Exon V5/V6 kit (Agilent) targeting 20965 genes. Bioinformatic processing included alignment with BWA (v0.1.22) and Samblaster, duplicate marking with Sambamba (v0.4.7), SNP/INDEL detection with SAMtools (v1.0), somatic variant calling with muTect (v1.1.4) and Strelka (v1.0.13), copy number variation (CNV) analysis with control-FREEC (v6.7), functional annotation with ANNOVAR, Mutational spectrum and inter-tumor heterogeneity analysis with SigProfiler, and mutational signature analysis with MuSiC (v0.04). Six biological replicates were analyzed per group.

RNA-sequencing analysis

At 4 months post-TAM induction, the lung tissues from Men1f/f, Men1∆/∆, Kmt2af/f, and Kmt2a∆/∆ mice were isolated for RNA-sequencing and subsequent data analysis by Novogene (Beijing, China). Briefly, the lung tissues were delivered to the company in dry ice according to the sample delivery instructions. cDNA library construction and sequencing were performed using HiSeq of Illumina. High-quality reads were aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome [GRCm38 (mm10)] using Hisat2. Genes with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 found by DESeq2 were assigned as differentially expressed. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the OECloud tools (https://cloud.oebiotech.com). There were at least three biological replicates for each group.

Statistics and reproducibility

Sample sizes were chosen based on our previous studies [11, 12] and common practices in the field. For both in vitro and in vivo studies, samples and animals were randomly allocated to experimental or control groups. Specifically, in vitro experiments involved random allocation of cell cultures, while age- and sex-matched mice were randomized for in vivo studies prior to treatment. Blinding was implemented during outcome assessment and data analysis. Although the investigators responsible for drug administration were not blinded to group assignment due to the nature of the intervention, all subsequent data collection, outcome assessments, and statistical analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the group allocation. No data points and no animals were excluded from the analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Student’s t-tests or ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons were applied as appropriate, unless otherwise specified. Data are reported as mean ± SD or as mean ± SEM; P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All in vitro experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Results

Loss of Men1 results in genomic instability

Analysis of the cBioPortal database revealed multiple point mutations of menin in lung cancer specimens (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Patients harboring Men1 mutations exhibited a high frequency of co-mutations in genes such as p53 (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Patients with Men1 mutations had significantly reduced overall survival, with a 2-year survival rate of 0%, whereas those with p53 mutations had moderately lower survival than their wild-type (WT) counterparts in lung cancer (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Similarly, Men1 mutations were also significantly associated with a reduced survival probability among patients with pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PanNETs), cutaneous melanoma, and breast cancer (Supplementary Fig. S1D).

In Men1flox/flox; Ubc-CreERT2 mice (Men1f/f), TAM-induced homozygous knockout (KO) of Men1 (Men1Δ/Δ) resulted in spontaneous DNA damage accumulation in the lungs at 4–12 months, as evidenced by increased phosphorylation of histone H2A.X at serine 139 (γH2A.X) (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. S1E and S1F). Men1Δ/Δ enhanced the sensitivity of lung tissues to DNA damage induced by IR (Supplementary Fig. S1G). Men1-KO promoted, whereas supplementation with menin suppressed, IR-induced γH2A.X focus formation in MEFs (Fig. 1B and C; Supplementary Fig. S1H). Following exposure to IR, the DNA tail length and tail moment were significantly greater in Men1Δ/Δ than in Men1f/f MEFs (Fig. 1D). Men1-KO also increased the frequency of genomic instability markers, including micronuclei and nuclear buds (Fig. 1E).

We performed WES on normal lung tissues (N) from Men1f/f mice and tumor tissues (T) and adjacent nontumor tissues (AT) from Men1Δ/Δ mice, which to our knowledge has not been previously reported. Consistent with previous findings [11], Men1-KO resulted in obvious lung cancer development at 12 months. The mean sequencing depth was approximately 200×, ensuring accurate detection of low-frequency mutations and 100% coverage of target exon regions (Supplementary Fig. S1I). Quality control via cumulative base distribution confirmed sample homogeneity (Supplementary Fig. S1J). Control-FREEC software was used to analyze B-allele frequency (BAF) and the log ratio of genomic coverage depth (logR), revealing genome-wide biallelic or heterozygous deletions in tumor tissues (Fig. 1F). A total of 1439 somatic genetic alterations in 328 genes, including 618 single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), 21 insertion-deletion mutations (indels), and 800 copy number variants (CNVs), were identified in all Men1Δ/Δ tumor tissues (n = 6) (Fig. 1G). This mutational burden was markedly higher than that in AT or N tissues, which showed negligible background alterations. CNVs drive genomic instability by activating oncogenes and inactivating tumor suppressor genes [27]. Men1Δ/Δ lung tumor tissues exhibited 800 CNVs in 283 genes, including 384.0 Mb of gains and 21.2 Mb of losses (Fig. 1H; Supplementary Fig. S1K and L). Missense mutations were more frequent than synonymous mutations were (Supplementary Fig. S1M), indicating functionally impactful mutations. The tumor tissues exhibited enrichment in indels and deletions but not insertions (Supplementary Fig. S1N). MuSiC analysis revealed that there were more significantly mutated genes (SMGs) in tumors (Fig. 1I). The majority of SNVs were private events, with only 10 SNVs present in two or more lung tumor samples. These included the epigenetic reader Sp110 and the gene of unknown function RIKEN, both of which were somatically mutated in all tumor samples. Notably, Men1 deletion resulted in mutations in key tumor-associated genes, including KRas, Cdkn2a, Stat3, and Braf (Fig. 1J). The mutational spectrum of Men1Δ/Δ lung tumors was dominated by C > T/G > A and T > C/A > G transitions (Fig. 1K), indicating distinct substitution biases. The C > T pattern in mouse tumors paralleled that of human non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), whereas the T > C pattern resembled that of small cell lung cancer (SCLC) (Supplementary Fig. S1O), which is consistent with the pathological features of mixed-lineage neuroendocrine lung cancer caused by Men1 deletion [11]. Variants predominantly localized to intergenic (38.98%) and intronic (37.60%) regions (Fig. 1L). Analysis of AP sites revealed that the frequencies of AP sites were 1.3, 4.4, and 14.3 per 105 nucleosides in N, AT, and T, respectively (Fig. 1M). Methylnitronitrosoguanidine (MNNG)-induced AP site frequency was also elevated in Men1Δ/Δ MEFs (Fig. 1N), suggesting increased sequence instability and mutagenic potential [28]. Our results confirm that loss of menin results in genomic instability.

Menin directly binds to H2A.X

Exon 5 of the Fhit gene in both the human and mouse genomes contains common fragile sites (CFS) and is highly sensitive to DNA damage signals [29]. ChIP assays revealed that enrichment of both γH2A.X and menin was stimulated by exposure to aphidicolin (APH) at the CFS in A549 cells (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S2A). In MEFs, Men1-KO resulted in a reduction in menin binding but an increase in the level of γH2A.X accumulation at the CFS (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S2B). Ultraviolet laser microirradiation triggered robust recruitment of menin to laser tracks within 10 s (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Menin directly interacts with nucleosomes [10]. Endogenous-IP assays revealed that menin interacts with γH2A.X but not Rad51, and the interaction was activated by exposure to IR (Fig. 2C). Reciprocal-IP assays confirmed the interaction between menin and WT H2A.X (Fig. 2D). DNase I treatment did not disrupt the co-precipitation of menin with γH2A.X, indicating that this protein-protein interaction occurs in a DNA-independent manner (Supplementary Fig. S2D and E). IP assays using chromatin extracts further showed that IR strengthened the association between menin and γH2A.X while diminishing menin binding to canonical H2A, with only a modest elevation in the menin–H2A.X interaction (Supplementary Fig. S2F–H). To preclude artefactual overestimation of signals from co-precipitated interactors resulting from IR-induced upregulation of bait protein abundance, we carried out co-IP assays using chromatin extracts prepared from A549 cells stably expressing HA-tagged menin. Consistently, IR specifically promoted the menin-γH2A.X interaction in A549 cells stably expressing menin–HA (Supplementary Fig. S2I). Notably, pre-treatment with the selective ATM inhibitor KU-55933 markedly impaired the IR-induced menin–γH2A.X interaction (Supplementary Fig. S2J). Pull-down assays revealed a direct interaction between menin and WT H2A.X (Fig. 2E). Menin displayed preferential binding to the phosphomimetic H2A.X-S139E mutant, a commonly used surrogate for the negative charge introduced by H2A.X S139 phosphorylation [30, 31], relative to WT H2A.X and canonical H2A (Supplementary Fig. S2K). Consistent with this finding, competitive binding assays demonstrated that H2A.X–S139E dose-dependently attenuated the binding of menin to both WT H2A.X and canonical H2A (Supplementary Fig. S2L). Together, these results demonstrate that menin is recruited to DNA damage sites through direct recognition of γH2A.X.

Co-IP assays revealed that menin interacts with γH2A.X through its Palm and Fingers domains (Fig. 2F). We generated truncations of the Palm/Fingers domains (Supplementary Fig. S2M and N), and co-IP revealed that menin interacts with γH2A.X via its 371–400 region (Fig. 2G and H). Reciprocal co-IP assays revealed that menin interacts with both the N- and C-terminal domains of H2A.X (Fig. 2I; Supplementary Fig. S2O and P). Importantly, deletion of residues 371–400 in menin (Δ371–400) abolished its binding to γH2A.X (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Functionally, reconstitution with WT-menin alleviated IR-induced γH2A.X foci formation in Men1-KD A549 cells, whereas the Δ371–400 mutant failed to exert such a protective effect (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Sequence analysis revealed evolutionary conservation of the menin Val371–Gln400 motif (Supplementary Fig. S3C). We next synthesized a peptide spanning the 371–400 residue region of menin (designated Pep-MH), which corresponds to the core menin–γH2A.X interaction domain. A mutant control peptide (Pep-Mut) with alanine substitutions at key interfacial residues was also generated for control comparison [32] (Supplementary Fig. S3C). Streptavidin pull-down assays demonstrated that Pep-MH efficiently precipitated both WT H2A.X and the H2A.X–S139E mutant, while Pep-Mut lost the capacity to interact with either protein (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Consistently, Pep-MH dose-dependently inhibited the interaction of menin-H2A.X (Fig. 2J). Moreover, cellular treatment with Pep-MH, but not Pep-Mut, markedly augmented IR-triggered γH2A.X foci accumulation and effectively disrupted the interaction of menin with either H2A.X or H2A.X–S139E (Supplementary Fig. S3E and F). ChIP assays revealed that treatment with Pep-MH inhibited the APH-induced binding of menin at the CFS (Fig. 2K). Pep-MH dose-dependently increased the IR-induced levels of γH2A.X and Rad51 (Fig. 2L). In summary, menin directly binds histone H2A.X through its Val371–Gln400 motif, and DNA damage triggers a specific interaction between menin and γH2A.X.

Chromatin looping facilitates HR repair by shortening the physical distance between homologous templates and directionally recruiting repair factors to DSBs [33]. 3C assays detected spatial interactions between DNA fragments within the mFhit locus using locus-specific primers directed against EcoRI- or BamHI-digested fragments (Supplementary Fig. S3G). ChIP assays mapped menin binding to two regions corresponding to EcoRI fragments F and E, located upstream (506–649 bp) and downstream (27 044–27 182 bp) of the Fhit promoter, respectively (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Fig. S2B and S3G). qPCR analyses with primer pairs 1R/2R, 1R/4R, and 2R/4R confirmed the interactions between regions F-E, F-C, and E-C, respectively (Fig. 2M). Consistent results were obtained with forward primer combinations (Fig. 2M). Notably, Men1-KO reduced the frequency of interactions between region C/F and the menin-binding region E (Fig. 2M and Supplementary Fig. S3H). Similar results were obtained by independent 3C assays using BamHI-digested chromatin (Supplementary Fig. S3I and J). These results suggest that menin acts as a molecular bridge to maintain the spatial conformation of chromatin adjacent to DSBs.

Menin regulates HR repair during DNA end resection

We used the DR-GFP or pimEJ5-GFP reporter systems to investigate the potential effects of menin on HR and NHEJ repair [22, 23] (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Men1-KD with siRNA significantly inhibited HR repair but moderately enhanced NHEJ efficiency (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S4B). Consistently, Pep-MH treatment, but not Pep-Mut, efficiently suppressed IR-triggered HR repair, and concomitantly potentiated NHEJ repair (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Functional rescue assays demonstrated that Men1 siRNA-mediated HR deficiency could be fully rescued by WT-menin but not by the Δ371–400 mutant (Supplementary Fig. S4D). Treatment with APH enhanced the recruitment of Rad51, a downstream effector of HR repair, to the CFS in A549 cells (Supplementary Fig. S2A and Fig. 3B). Men1-KO reduced Rad51 recruitment to the CFS in MEFs (Supplementary Fig. S2B and Fig. 3C). In HR repair, pRPA2 accumulation indicates the extent of resection, and its phosphorylation triggers partial dissociation of RPA from ssDNA, resulting in the formation of Rad51 nucleoprotein filament assembly sites [34]. Using the DR-GFP reporter system, we found that menin, Rad51, and pRPA2 were colocalized at regions 200 and 400 bp from the DSBs and that enrichment was diminished by Men1-siRNA (Fig. 3D). Men1-KD increased the proportion of S–phase cells at 8 h following serum release (Supplementary Fig. S4E), thus ruling out the possibility that HR deficiency in Men1–KD cells arises from a decreased S–phase population [35]. Men1-KO markedly enhanced IR-elicited γH2A.X foci formation, robustly impaired nuclear Rad51 foci assembly and reduced Rad51–γH2A.X colocalization (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. S4F), while promoting foci formation of the NHEJ regulator 53BP1 and enhancing its colocalization with γH2A.X (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. S4G). Men1-KO did not affect the nuclear localization of BRCA1 or NBS1 (Supplementary Fig. S4H). IP assays revealed no detectable interactions between menin and NBS1, BRCA1, DNA-PKcs, or Ku80 (Supplementary Fig. S4I), suggesting that menin regulates HR repair independently of these canonical pathways. Western blotting results revealed that Men1-KO inhibited IR-induced phosphorylation of Chk1, RPA2, and ATR, as well as Rad51 expression, whereas ATM phosphorylation and BRCA1 levels were unaffected (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. S4J). Additionally, Men1-KO did not affect the protein levels of NHEJ factors such as 53BP1, Ku80, or DNA-PKcs (Supplementary Fig. S4J). Hydroxyurea (HU) induces replication fork stalling and indirect oxidative damage by depleting dNTPs, whereas camptothecin (CPT) triggers replication fork collapse and DSB formation by inhibiting TOP1; both of these processes specifically activate the HR repair pathway [5]. Consistent results were observed in A549 cells exposed to HU or CPT (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. S4K). Exposure to CPT promoted the nuclear translocation and colocalization of Rad51 with menin, which was abrogated by Men1-siRNA in A549 cells (Fig. 3I). These results indicate that menin participates in HR repair during the DNA end resection stage mediated by RPA2/Rad51 rather than during the early BRCA1/MRN-mediated DNA damage recognition stage.

Menin facilitates HR repair by recruiting SETD2 to deposit H3K36me3 at DSBs

IR activated the expression of γH2A.X, Rad51, and 53BP1 but concurrently induced a time-dependent increase in the nuclear levels of menin, SETD2, and its mediated H3K36me3 without affecting other histone modifications (Fig. 4A). Exposure to APH increased the enrichment of SETD2 and H3K36me3 at the CFS in A549 cells (Supplementary Fig. S2A and Fig. 4B). Men1-KO reduced the enrichment of SETD2 and H3K36me3 at the CFS in MEFs (Supplementary Fig. S2B and Fig. 4C), whereas it did not affect MLL or H3K4me3 (Supplementary Fig. S5A). EZM0414, a selective SETD2 inhibitor [36], enhanced IR-induced γH2A.X activation (Supplementary Fig. S5B). Men1- or Setd2-siRNA KD, as well as treatment with Pep242, a peptide that specifically disrupts the menin-SETD2 interaction [8], enhanced the formation of IR-induced γH2A.X foci in the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. S5C–E). Treatment with Pep242, Setd2-siRNA, or EZM0414 significantly inhibited HR repair efficiency (Fig. 4D–F). However, exposure to MI-3, an inhibitor of the menin/MLL interaction [37], had no effect on HR repair efficiency (Supplementary Fig. S5F).

ChIP assays revealed that Setd2-KO inhibited H3K36me3 modification at the CFS but promoted the enrichment of γH2A.X (Fig. 4G). Following exposure to IR, Setd2-KO increased the DNA tail length and tail moment in MEFs (Fig. 4H and I). Setd2-KO also increased the frequency of micronuclei, nucleoplasmic bridges, and nuclear buds post-IR (Fig. 4J). KO of either Men1 or Setd2 increased IR-induced γH2A.X accumulation and suppressed the expression of Rad51, pATR, and pRPA2 (Fig. 4K). The levels of pATM, BRCA1, 53BP1, DNA-PKcs, Ku70, and Ku80 were unaffected (Fig. 4K and Supplementary Fig. S5G). In contrast, Kmt2a-KO did not obviously affect these pathways (Fig. 4K and Supplementary Fig. S5G). Pep-MH inhibited the APH-induced modification of H3K36me3 at CFS (Fig. 4L). Pull-down assays revealed that the SET domain of SETD2 could not bind H2A.X directly in vitro but that menin mediated this interaction (Supplementary Fig. S5H), identifying menin as a critical scaffold that recruits SETD2 to DSBs. 3C assays revealed that Setd2-KO reduced the interaction frequency between regions C/F and the menin/SETD2-binding region E (Supplementary Fig. S5I). In summary, we demonstrated that menin-regulated HR repair is associated with local H3K36me3 deposition at DSBs.

We performed gene expression profiling analysis on transcriptomic sequencing data from the lung tissues of Men1Δ/Δ and Kmt2aΔ/Δ mice (GEO: GSE230855). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed significant enrichment between menin expression and DNA damage repair-related pathways, including UV response and DNA repair, and MLL exhibited a nonsignificant trend toward enrichment in these pathways (Supplementary Fig. S5J). We identified 15 DNA repair-associated genes that are coregulated by menin and MLL. Strikingly, only five of these genes were consistently downregulated upon KO of Men1 and Kmt2a (Supplementary Fig. S5J), and their functional roles in HR repair remain to be fully validated. Collectively, these data suggest that menin maintains HR repair independent of MLL-mediated H3K4me3.

Menin maintains the HR program through recruiting LEDGF/CtIP

LEDGF recognizes the chromatin H3K36me3 mark via its PWWP domain, thereby promoting DNA end resection through the recruitment of CtIP [5]. IR exposure enhanced the binding of LEDGF to chromatin (Fig. 4A). ChIP assays revealed that exposure to APH promoted the enrichment of LEDGF and CtIP at the CFS in A549 cells (Supplementary Fig. S2A and Fig. 5A). Men1-KO reduced the binding of LEDGF and CtIP at the CFS in MEFs (Supplementary Figs S2B and S6A). IP assays clearly confirmed the interactions between menin and SETD2/LEDGF/CtIP, and that exposure to IR enhanced these interactions in both whole-cell lysates and chromatin extracts (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S6B–D). Setd2-siRNA reduced the interactions between menin and SETD2/LEDGF/CtIP; Psip1-siRNA inhibited the interactions between menin and LEDGF/CtIP but not with SETD2; and Ctip-siRNA had no effect on the interactions of menin with SETD2 or LEDGF (Fig. 5C). Men1-KO inhibited the individual interactions of menin, SETD2, LEDGF, and CtIP with γH2A.X; Setd2-KO did not affect the interaction between menin and γH2A.X but inhibited the interactions of SETD2, LEDGF, and CtIP with γH2A.X in MEFs (Fig. 5D). However, Kmt2a-KO did not affect the interactions of these proteins with γH2A.X (Fig. 5D).

IF staining revealed that Setd2-siRNA or treatment with EZM0414 inhibited the formation of LEDGF and CtIP foci in the nucleus (Fig. 5E and Supplementary Fig. S6E). ChIP assays revealed that Setd2-siRNA reduced the binding of SETD2, LEDGF, and CtIP, but not menin, to the CFS (Fig. 5F). Treatment with EZM0414 inhibited the binding of LEDGF and CtIP at the CFS without affecting the binding of menin or SETD2 (Fig. 5G). EZM0414 inhibited the interactions of γH2A.X with LEDGF or CtIP but not with menin or SETD2 (Supplementary Fig. S6F). Consistent with menin/SETD2, both Ctip-siRNA and Psip1-siRNA inhibited CPT-induced activation of Rad51, pATR, and pRPA2, as well as the formation of Rad51 foci in the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. S6G and H). These results support a molecular model in which menin, bound to γH2A.X, acts as a scaffold protein to sequentially recruit SETD2, LEDGF, and CtIP, forming an orderly assembled functional complex that promotes HR repair (Supplementary Fig. S6J).

Menin maintains HR repair in cells of multiple origins

Lung cancer has a complex pathological morphology and originates from diverse cell types [11]. We therefore investigated which types of cells in the lung are sensitive to Men1 depletion. Antibodies specific for calcitonin gene-related peptide 1 (CGRP), pulmonary-associated surfactant protein C (SPC), club cells 10 kDa secretory protein (CC10), and keratin 14 (KRT14) were used to label NE, ATII, Clara, and basal epithelial cells, respectively. IHC analysis revealed that lung tumors that developed in Men1Δ/Δ mice were strongly positive for CGRP, SPC, CC10, and KRT14 (Supplementary Fig. S7A), suggesting that lung tumors induced by Men1-KO developed from multiple cell types. We established cell-specific KRasG12D combined with Men1-KO in NE, ATII, Clara, or basal epithelial cells, and CMV promoter-driven Cre activation was used as the positive control for whole-lung KO (Fig. 6A). The cell-specific KO efficiency of each adenovirus (Ad) was validated by crossing the above mice with R26-CAG-LSL-luci-tdTomato mice. IF staining revealed colocalization of the tdTomato signal with specific antibody staining, indicating that gene recombination occurred in the indicated cells (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Survival analysis revealed that the median survival times in the NE, ATII, Clara, and basal epithelial cell-specific gene KO groups were 224.5, 144.5, 149.5, and 143 days, respectively, whereas that in the CMV group was 143 days (Fig. 6B). Necropsy of the mice revealed visible lung tumors in all groups (Fig. 6C). HE staining confirmed that the tumor location and histopathology were consistent with the anatomical origins of the targeted cells (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S7C). The tumors that arose from NE cells were located mainly in the central part of the lungs and within the airway epithelium. The tumors arising from ATII cells were sporadically distributed within the lungs and bronchi. The tumors arising from Clara cells were located mainly within the bronchi, with an irregular scattered distribution. The tumors arising from basal epithelial cells were located mainly outside the bronchi. The tumors in the Ad-CMV-Cre group exhibited a range of locations consistent with the anatomical positions of the cells of various origins in the lungs. There were no significant differences in the lung/body weight ratio or tumor burden among the groups (Supplementary Fig. S7D). IHC analysis revealed strong staining for transcription termination factor 1 (TTF1), a marker for primary lung adenocarcinoma, and for E-cadherin, a marker for epithelial cells, in all groups (Fig. 6D). Importantly, in tumors with four distinct cellular origins, Men1 deficiency resulted in lower positivity rates for H3K36me3, LEDGF, CtIP, pRPA2, and Rad51, and a higher positivity rate for γH2A.X, compared with adjacent nontumor tissues (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. S7E). Furthermore, correlation analysis showed that menin positive rates correlated positively with H3K36me3, LEDGF, CtIP, pRPA2, and Rad51, and negatively with γH2A.X (Supplementary Fig. S7F). IHC staining revealed positive staining for the proliferation marker Ki67 and the metastasis marker HMGA2 in all groups, with stronger signals in ATII- and basal cell-derived tumors (Supplementary Fig. S7G). These pathological features indicate that the combination of KRasG12D and Men1-KO induces the initiation of adenocarcinoma, adenocarcinoma with NE differentiation, and mixed-type lung cancer (adenocarcinoma mixed with squamous cell carcinoma and accompanied by NE differentiation) (Supplementary Fig. S7H). These findings indicate that menin broadly regulates HR repair in the lungs in a cell type-independent manner. Finally, exposure to IR enhanced the interactions between menin and SETD2/LEDGF/CtIP in multiple types of cell lines, including NCI-H1975, NCI-H157, SH-SY5Y, G401, A375, PLC/PRF/5, HeLa, MCF7, HaCat, and HT29 cells (Supplementary Fig. S7I), suggesting that menin/SETD2/LEDGF/CtIP complex-regulated HR repair is a universal mechanism.

Pharmacological rescue of H3K36me3 promotes HR repair

Through small-molecule drug screening, we observed that Men1-deficient lung cancer cells are highly sensitive to JIB-04 [11], a pan-selective Jumonji histone demethylase inhibitor. Treatment with JIB-04 restored the nuclear accumulation of LEDGF and CtIP, which was inhibited by Men1-KO (Fig. 7A). Men1- or Setd2-siRNA exacerbated IR-induced DNA damage, and this effect was reversed by JIB-04 treatment (Fig. 7B). JIB-04 restored the decreased frequency of HR repair caused by Men1 or Setd2 deficiency but had no effect on that induced by Psip1, Ctip, or Rad51 siRNA (Fig. 7C). ChIP assays revealed that JIB-04 effectively reversed the Men1-KO-induced reduction in H3K36me3 modification and LEDGF recruitment at the CFS (Fig. 7D). 3C assays revealed that Men1-KO inhibited the interaction frequency between region C/F and menin-binding region E, whereas treatment with JIB-04 restored these interactions and the chromatin conformation at menin-binding sites (Supplementary Fig. S8A). These results suggest that menin/SETD2 may maintain chromatin conformation at DSBs through H3K36me3 remodeling. Overexpression of Men1 inhibited CPT-induced DNA damage in A549 and NCI-H157 cells (Supplementary Fig. S8B). A549, NCI-H1975, and NCI-H157 cells with Men1 or Setd2 shRNA-KD exhibited increased sensitivity to JIB-04 (Fig. 7E; Supplementary Fig. S8C and D). However, exposure to JIB-04 had no obvious effect on DNA damage in A549 cells treated with Psip1- or Ctip-siRNA (Supplementary Fig. S8E). Men1 overexpression increased the sensitivity of A549 cells to CPT (Supplementary Fig. S8F). Moreover, the combined treatment of JIB-04 with CPT or HU clearly inhibited colony formation in A549 cells (Supplementary Fig. S8F). These findings indicate that pharmacological restoration of chromatin H3K36me3 effectively rescues the altered HR program caused by Men1/Setd2 deficiency.

We generated lung cancer models by inducing ATII cell-specific Men1- or Setd2-KO combined with KRasG12D/+ activation in LSL-KRasG12D/+; Men1f/f; Sftpc-Cre (KMS) and LSL-KRasG12D/+; Setd2f/f; Sftpc-Cre (KSS) mice via TAM injection [11]. MicroCT scanning revealed that KMS and KSS mice displayed visible dot-like markings and high lamellar density in lung regions on day 40; those treated with JIB-04 showed normal lung markings that were similar to asymptomatic mice (Fig. 7F and Supplementary Fig. S8G). The mice were euthanized on day 43. Dissection revealed that the lungs of KMS and KSS mice were enlarged, with grossly visible tumor nodules and consolidated areas on the surface; in contrast, the lung tissues from JIB-04-treated KMS and KSS mice exhibited normal lung morphology similar to that of the WT group (Fig. 7G). The lung weights of mice in the KMS and KSS groups were greater than those in the WT group, while compared with the control group, the JIB-04 treatment group presented significantly lower lung weights, with no significant differences in body weight observed among the groups (Supplementary Fig. S8H). Treatment with JIB-04 dramatically increased TUNEL staining in lung cancer cells (Fig. 7H). Consistent with these findings, Men1- or Setd2-siRNA inhibited the IR-induced expression of the pro-apoptotic factors Bax, Cytochrome C, and cleaved Caspase-3 in A549 cells, and treatment with JIB-04 promoted their expression (Supplementary Fig. S8I). These results indicate that JIB-04 restores the sensitivity of menin/SETD2-deficient lung cancer cells to IR through the activation of HR and apoptosis. IHC staining revealed strong positive staining for SPC but negative staining for CC10, CGRP, and KRT14 (Supplementary Fig. S8J), indicating that lung tumors with the KMS or KSS genotype originate from ATII cells. HE staining revealed that the lungs of KMS and KSS mice were filled with severe tumor nodules (100%), whereas JIB-04-treated KMS (4/9) and KSS (5/10) mice developed only a few small tumor nodules, with the remaining mice exhibiting nearly normal lung morphology (Fig. 7I). IHC staining revealed prominent γH2A.X staining in the lungs of KMS and KSS mice, whereas JIB-04 treatment markedly reduced the level of γH2A.X staining with restored H3K36me3 (Fig. 7I). Moreover, JIB-04 treatment potently suppressed the expression of the cell proliferation marker Ki67 and enhanced the expression of the cellular senescence marker P21 (Fig. 7I), which aligns with our previous observation that Men1 deficiency impairs the cellular senescence program [11]. JIB-04 suppressed vimentin expression while promoting E-cadherin expression without affecting staining for neural cell adhesion protein 1 (NCAM1) or neuron-specific enolase (NSE) (Supplementary Fig. S8J). Finally, Men1-KD markedly sensitized A549, NCI-H1975, and NCI-H157 lung cancer cells to the PARP inhibitor olaparib, as evidenced by reduced cell viability and impaired clonogenic survival (Supplementary Fig. S8K), further supporting the presence of an HR–deficient state in Men1–deficient cells. These findings confirm that loss of Men1 impairs HR repair and that pharmacological restoration of H3K36me3 levels efficiently rescues HR repair in lung cancer cells.

Discussion

The H3K36me3 residue is associated with active chromatin [38]. Recently, emerging evidence has suggested that H3K36me3 mediates HR repair to maintain genomic stability [4]. However, the dynamic process of H3K36me3-mediated HR repair and the composition of the repair complex remain uncharacterized. Here, we report that menin sequentially recruits SETD2, LEDGF, and CtIP to form a complex, serving as a key scaffold protein that promotes H3K36me3-mediated HR repair. Menin binds to nucleosomes through its Palm and Fingers domains [10]. Here, we precisely identified that menin directly binds to histone H2A.X through the Val371–Gln400 motif of the Palm and Fingers domains, and DNA damage signals rapidly trigger the direct recruitment of menin to DSBs. Subsequently, SETD2 is recruited to DSBs by menin and acts as a writer to catalyze H3K36me3 reprogramming on local chromatin. LEDGF, as a reader, recognizes H3K36me3 and further recruits CtIP as an effector to perform DNA end resection at damage sites, ultimately activating the pRPA2/Rad51 pathway (Supplementary Fig. S5G). Inhibition of the interaction between menin and H2A.X suppressed the recruitment of SETD2, LEDGF, and CtIP to DSBs, causing obvious DNA damage and HR pathway defects and thus confirming the critical bridging role of menin in H3K36me3-mediated HR repair. The process appears to be independent of the canonical ATM/NBS1/BRCA1 pathway, indicating that the menin complex is primarily involved in HR repair at the level of DNA end resection. Pharmacological restoration of chromatin H3K36me3 by JIB-04 reinstated the recruitment of LEDGF and CtIP to DSBs and rescued the HR repair program, ultimately facilitating cancer cell apoptosis. In vivo, JIB-04 treatment efficiently impeded tumor progression of lung cancer driven by Men1/Setd2-KO combined with KRasG12D mutation, and diminished the accumulation of genomic DNA damage. Nevertheless, JIB-04 acts as a pan-selective inhibitor of the Jumonji histone demethylases and exerts broad epigenetic regulatory effects. We therefore cannot exclude the possibility that JIB-04 modulates DNA damage repair through effects on histone modifications other than H3K36me3; further studies will be necessary to explore this possibility. On the other hand, chromatin loops reduce the search time for DNA end rejoining and homology search or concentrate repair factors [33, 39]. Our results show that menin/SETD2-mediated local H3K36me3 remodeling contributes to chromatin loop formation, thereby potentially facilitating HR repair processes. This study comprehensively clarifies the dynamic process of H3K36me3 reprogramming-mediated HR repair; identifies key molecules involved in the entire process, including DNA damage recognition, local chromatin remodelling, and DNA end resection; and confirms their biological functions and biochemical binding patterns.

We found that the inactivation of menin expression occurs in most human primary lung cancers, among which approximately 26.8% of cases exhibit barely detectable levels, and this inactivation is associated with poor overall survival [11, 14]. The mechanism underlying the role of menin in lung carcinogenesis remains incompletely understood. Here, we explored mainly the molecular mechanism by which menin inhibits lung cancer from the perspective of DNA damage repair. Multiple point mutations in menin have been identified in lung cancer patients, and patients who carry Men1 gene mutations frequently harbor mutations in classic tumor suppressor genes such as p53. WES revealed that lung cancer tissues from Men1Δ/Δ mice exhibit genome-wide biallelic or heterozygous deletions, increased CNVs and SNVs, and mutations in key oncogenes (such as KRas) or tumor suppressor genes (such as Cdkn2a) (Fig. 1). Various cancers harboring Men1 mutations are highly malignant. For example, lung cancer patients with menin mutations have a particularly poor prognosis, with an observed 2-year survival rate of 0%. Consistent with these findings, we confirmed that Men1-KO dramatically promoted the progression of lung carcinogenesis induced by KRasG12D mutation in mice, with increased malignancy [11]. We conclude that loss of menin results in the inactivation of HR repair, whereas error-prone NHEJ undergoes compensatory activation. Ultimately, this genomic toxicity accumulates at key oncogene/tumor suppressor gene loci, leading to the occurrence of lung cancer. Here, we explored mainly the molecular mechanism by which menin inhibits lung cancer from the perspective of DNA damage repair. We previously reported that menin and SETD2 colocalize on gene bodies and that H3K36me3-dependent transcription of Csf2 is essential for alveolar macrophage development [8]. Hence, we cannot exclude the possibility that menin exerts its tumor-suppressive function by regulating target gene transcription through H3K36me3. The present results provide important genetic evidence supporting the lung cancer-suppressive role of menin and offer novel insights into the treatment of tumors driven by menin/SETD2 mutation or inactivation.

Extensive research has shown that menin is a key scaffold protein through which MLL performs its biological and epigenetic functions. For example, menin recruits WT and fusion-type MLL proteins to activate oncogene expression and promote leukemia development [6]. Surprisingly, pharmacological or genetic targeting of MLL does not affect DNA damage repair, indicating that the HR repair function regulated by menin is independent of MLL-mediated H3K4me3 reprogramming. Unlike Men1, Kmt2a-KO does not promote but rather inhibits the progression of lung cancer induced by KRasG12D mutations [40]. However, consistent with Men1, Kmt2a-KO can promote NE differentiation in lung cancer cells [11, 40]. Mechanistically, the menin/MLL complex maintains p53 protein stability and NE differentiation in lung cancer through the regulation of βTrCP by H3K4me3 [11]. The DNA damage repair and lung cancer-suppressive functions of SETD2 are highly comparable to those of menin. Moreover, similar to Men1, Setd2-KO leads to HR repair defects and promotes the progression of lung cancer induced by KRasG12D mutation. However, Setd2-KO did not affect the NE differentiation of lung cancer cells. These findings indicate that the ability of menin to suppress lung cancer depends on SETD2-mediated H3K36me3 reprogramming, whereas its ability to control NE differentiation in lung cancer cells relies on MLL-mediated H3K4me3. Our findings provide important epigenetic evidence for elucidating the role of menin in regulating lung cancer pathogenesis, advancing the understanding of the diversity in the biological functions of menin.

This study reveals a refined molecular mechanism whereby H3K36me3 epigenetic remodelling governs HR repair efficiency. Furthermore, our findings provide robust epigenetic evidence illustrating the critical tumor-suppressive role of menin in lung cancer pathogenesis.
