 Background and
 Motivation
Resource adequacy (RA) is a component of bulk power system reliability that addresses whether there
are sufficient resources available to meet electricity demand at all times.1
RA analysis is used to assess whether the planned power system will provide reliable electricity to consumers
while accounting for:
  • Equipment outages
  • Weather variability
  • Load uncertainty.

Coordination between regions can enable resource sharing to meet RA needs.
  • The high-voltage transmission network can be used to share resources across planning regions.
  • Transmission’s role in RA coordination can be particularly pronounced for large power systems like the U.S.
 electricity grid, which involves geographically diverse demand and weather-dependent resources.2,3
  • Depending on the level of coordination desired, existing interregional transmission capacity may not be
 sufficient.

This study is designed to assess optimal pathways for the development of interregional transmission in the
United States under varying levels of RA coordination. Results can inform long-term grid infrastructure planning
and provide insights into potential benefits of greater coordination for generation and transmission planning
between regional U.S. power systems.                                                         National Laboratory of the Rockies | 4

  Objective: Identify future transmission needs to support resource adequacy
requirements under scenarios with demand growth and future generation mixes

 Existing interregional transfer capability [GW] in 2025. Image by Billy Roberts, NREL

  Questions addressed in this study:
  • How much transmission is beneficial for RA?
  • Where is the RA-driven transmission built?
  • How sensitive is estimated RA-driven transmission to various drivers?
  • How does RA coordination between planning regions impact transmission expansion?

 Models
• Regional Energy Deployment System (ReEDS )4,5
 • ReEDS, NREL’s flagship capacity expansion model for the U.S. power sector, is used to
 optimize generation, storage, and transmission capacity investment for the contiguous
 United States.
• Probabilistic Resource Adequacy Suite (PRAS)6,7
 • PRAS performs hourly chronological modeling over multiple weather years and a Monte
 Carlo assessment of generator outages to assess resource adequacy metrics of the system
 design provided by ReEDS.

 Methods
• RA coordination limits the amount of power imports a region can use to meet RA requirements.
 • ReEDS implements RA coordination by specifying the maximum amount of hourly net imports that each
 region can rely on during stressful periods.
 • Larger limits represent greater interregional RA coordination.
• All RA coordination scenarios are subject to the same RA target of 2 parts per million (ppm) in each planning
 region.
 • Reliability target is based on normalized expected unserved energy (NEUE) and can be equated to
 systems that are designed to meet ≥99.9998% of each region’s electricity demand.

 System Costs

 • Improving degree of interregional coordination results in increasing system cost savings, primarily driven by avoided
 generation and storage capital and operations and maintenance (O&M) costs, which more than offset additional
 investment in transmission.
 • Medium coordination scenario results in $200B to $220B net savings (2.5% to 3.2%) relative to no coordination.
 • Largest savings are realized in high coordination case ~$260B (3.3% to 3.8%) relative to no coordination.

 Max Net Imports by Region

• Import limits are enforced at the NERC region level in the model.
• Under no coordination, each region must meet their RA requirements entirely with local resources and cannot
 import from neighbors.
• Under high coordination, the regions with the highest max net imports are also regions with the greatest
 increase in interface transfer capability (NYISO, SPP, SERTP).

 Key Takeaways
• Interregional transmission expansion occurs in all scenarios, but greater expansion is found with
 higher RA coordination.
 • Across the Core, High-Demand, and HVDC scenarios, the increases in transfer capability by
 2040 are:
 • Between 4% and 11% (7 and 19 GW) under no RA coordination
 • Between 21% and 32% (38 and 58 GW) under medium RA coordination
 • Between 47% and 68% (84 and 121 GW) under high RA coordination.
• Expansion of transfer capability is valuable for nearly all regions, though some connections are
 more prominent: NYISO ISONE and MISO-C SERTP.
• As more interregional coordination is allowed, the system achieves the same reliability levels at
 lower costs.
• Increasing coordination between planning regions has a greater impact on transmission
 expansion than other sensitivities.
 • For the High-Demand sensitivity, the increases in transfer capability relative to the Core
 scenario across the coordination levels range between 7% and 11% (13 and 30 GW).
 • For the HVDC sensitivity, the increases in transfer capability relative to the Core scenario
 across the coordination levels range between 2% and 14% (3 and 37 GW).

 OBBBA in ReEDS

• OBBBA Ref: Reflect federal tax incentive changes introduced by the One Big Beautiful
  Bill Act (OBBBA). The reference scenario updates the safe harbor periods for utility-
  scale PV, CSP, land-based wind, and offshore wind so that plants must come online by
  2029 to be eligible for tax credits.

• OBBBA Con: Conservative scenario updates the safe harbor periods for these
  technologies so that plants must come online by 2027 for eligibility.
