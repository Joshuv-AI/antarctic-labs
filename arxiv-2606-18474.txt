Tuples as sets
Adrian Ducourtial

Abstract




1
Introduction

Fix an infinite domain
D_{0}
and recursively define
D_{n+1}
as the set of finite non-empty subsets of
D_{n}
for
n\geq 0
. For
k\geq 1
,
n\geq 0
, we define the set
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
, whose elements are functions
D_{0}^{k}\to D_{n}
, by recursion on
n
, as follows.

1.

\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{0}
is the set of
k
-ary projections on
D_{0}
, i.e., those functions
D_{0}^{k}\to D_{0}
that are of the form
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\mapsto x_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq k
.

2.

For
n\geq 0
,
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n+1}
is the set of those functions
D_{0}^{k}\to D_{n+1}
that are of the form
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\{g(\mathbf{x}):g\in\mathcal{G}\}
for some non-empty
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
.

Example 1.1
.

The projections
(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto x_{1}
and
(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto x_{2}
on
D_{0}
are in
\mathcal{F}^{(2)}_{0}
. Therefore, the functions
(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto\{x_{1}\}
and
(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
of type
D_{0}^{2}\to D_{1}
are in
\mathcal{F}^{(2)}_{1}
. It follows that the function
f_{K}:D_{0}^{2}\to D_{2}:(x_{1},x_{2})\to\{\{x_{1}\},\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\}
is in
\mathcal{F}^{(2)}_{2}
.

The function
f_{K}
of Example
1.1
, originally considered by
2
, is injective. This allows us to identify the ordered pair
(x_{1},x_{2})
of objects
x_{1},x_{2}\in D_{0}
with the set
f_{K}(x_{1},x_{2})\in D_{2}
; for then, the injectivity of
f_{K}
amounts to the characteristic property of ordered pairs (of objects in
D_{0}
). This identification is standard in classical set theory
(
1
, 291)
, where, generalizing to classes,
D_{0}
is the universe of sets and
D_{n}\subseteq D_{0}
for all
n
.

More generally, for
k\geq 1
,
n\geq 1
, an injection
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
provides a definition of
k
-tuples over
D_{0}
as sets in
D_{n}
. In this paper, we ask the following.

Problem
.

Let
k\geq 1
,
n\geq 1
be given. Does
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
contain an injection?

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
develops the basic theory, leading us to restate our problem as the study of a certain integer function. Sections
3
,
4
, and
5
then initiate this study, giving qualitative results, function values for small arguments, and quantitative results, respectively. Finally, Section
6
discusses several variants of the problem.

2
Good pairs

Denote by
\mathbb{N}
the set of positive integers. Call a pair
(k,n)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}

good
if
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
contains an injection.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
n\geq 0
,
k,\ell\geq 1
,
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
, and
\pi_{(i)}\in\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}_{0}
for
1\leq i\leq k
be given. The function
f^{\prime}:D_{0}^{\ell}\to D_{n}
given by

f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=f(\pi_{(1)}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,\pi_{(k)}(\mathbf{x}))

is in
\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}_{n}
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on
n
.

•

Base case.
Since
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{0}
, we have that
f=\pi_{i}:(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\mapsto x_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq k
. Hence,

f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=\pi_{i}(\pi_{(1)}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,\pi_{(k)}(\mathbf{x}))=\pi_{(i)}(\mathbf{x})

for
\mathbf{x}\in D_{0}^{\ell}
. In other words,
f^{\prime}=\pi_{(i)}\in\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}_{0}
, and we are done.

•

Inductive step.
Let
n\geq 0
be given. The function
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n+1}
is given by
f(\mathbf{x})=\{g(\mathbf{x}):g\in\mathcal{G}\}
for some non-empty
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
. For each
g\in\mathcal{G}
, the function
g^{\prime}:D_{0}^{\ell}\to D_{n}
given by

g^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=g(\pi_{(1)}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,\pi_{(k)}(\mathbf{x}))

is in
\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}_{n}
by the inductive hypothesis. Together, this entails that

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=f(\pi_{(1)}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,\pi_{(k)}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=\{g(\pi_{(1)}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,\pi_{(k)}(\mathbf{x})):g\in\mathcal{G}\}

\displaystyle=\{g^{\prime}(\mathbf{x}):g\in\mathcal{G}\}

for
\mathbf{x}\in D_{0}^{\ell}
. In other words,
f^{\prime}
is determined by the set
\{g^{\prime}:g\in\mathcal{G}\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}_{n}
, and since this set is non-empty, we have
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}_{n+1}
as desired.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Definition 2.2
.

For
n\geq 0
, recursively define
\iota_{n}\in\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{n}
by letting
\iota_{0}:x\mapsto x
and
\iota_{n+1}:x\mapsto\{\iota_{n}(x)\}
for all
n
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
n\geq 0
, the function
\iota_{n}
is injective.

Proof.

By induction on
n
.

•

Base case.
The function
\iota_{0}
is trivially injective.

•

Inductive step.
For
x,y\in D_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\iota_{n+1}(x)=\iota_{n+1}(y)

\displaystyle\iff\{\iota_{n}(x)\}=\{\iota_{n}(y)\}

\displaystyle\iff\iota_{n}(x)=\iota_{n}(y)

\displaystyle\iff x=y

\displaystyle\text{by ind.\ hyp.},

as desired.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\sigma
be a permutation on
D_{0}
, i.e., a bijection
D_{0}\to D_{0}
. We define the permutation
\hat{\sigma}_{n}
on
D_{n}
by recursion on
n\geq 0
. Let
\hat{\sigma}_{0}=\sigma
, and for
n\geq 0
, let
\hat{\sigma}_{n+1}
be given by
\hat{\sigma}_{n+1}(S)=\{\hat{\sigma}_{n}(s):s\in S\}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
k\geq 1
,
n\geq 0
, and
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
be given, and let
\sigma:D_{0}\to D_{0}
be a permutation. For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in D_{0}^{k}
, we have

f(\sigma\mathbf{x})=\hat{\sigma}_{n}(f(\mathbf{x})),

where
\sigma\mathbf{x}=(\sigma(x_{1}),\dots,\sigma(x_{k}))
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on
n
.

•

Base case.
Since
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{0}
, we have
f=\pi_{i}:(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\mapsto x_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq k
. Therefore,

f(\sigma\mathbf{x})=\sigma(x_{i})=\sigma(f(\mathbf{x}))

for
\mathbf{x}\in D_{0}^{k}
, and since
\sigma=\hat{\sigma}_{0}
, we are done.

•

Inductive step.
The function
f
is given by
f(\mathbf{x})=\{g(\mathbf{x}):g\in\mathcal{G}\}
for some non-empty
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
. Hence,

\displaystyle f(\sigma\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\{g(\sigma\mathbf{x}):g\in\mathcal{G}\}

\displaystyle=\{\hat{\sigma}_{n}(g(\mathbf{x})):g\in\mathcal{G}\}

by ind. hyp.

\displaystyle=\hat{\sigma}_{n+1}(f(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle\text{by def.\ of }\hat{\sigma}_{n+1}

for
\mathbf{x}\in D_{0}^{k}
, as desired.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Definition 2.6
.

We define the function
\atom_{n}:D_{n}\to D_{1}
by recursion on
n\geq 0
. Let
\atom_{0}:x\mapsto\{x\}
and let
\atom_{n+1}:S\mapsto\bigcup_{s\in S}\atom_{n}(s)
for
n\geq 0
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
k\geq 1
,
n\geq 0
, and
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
be given. For
\mathbf{x}\in D_{0}^{k}
, writing
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
, we have

\atom_{n}(f(\mathbf{x}))\subseteq\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}.

Proof.

By induction on
n
.

•

Base case.
We have
f=\pi_{i}:(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\mapsto x_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq k
. Hence,
\atom_{0}(f(\mathbf{x}))=\atom_{0}(x_{i})=\{x_{i}\}\subseteq\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
, as desired.

•

Inductive step.
The function
f
is given by
f(\mathbf{x})=\{g(\mathbf{x}):g\in\mathcal{G}\}
for some non-empty
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
. Therefore,

\atom_{n+1}(f(\mathbf{x}))=\bigcup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\atom_{n}(g(\mathbf{x})).

By the inductive hypothesis,
\atom_{n}(g(\mathbf{x}))\subseteq\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
, whence
\atom_{n+1}(f(\mathbf{x}))\subseteq\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
as desired.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

We have
\atom_{n}(\iota_{n}(x))=\{x\}
for all
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

By induction on
n
.

•

Base case.
We have
\atom_{0}(\iota_{0}(x))=\atom_{0}(x)=\{x\}
as desired.

•

Inductive step.
We have

\atom_{n+1}(\iota_{n+1}(x))=\atom_{n+1}(\{\iota_{n}(x)\})=\atom_{n}(\iota_{n}(x)),

which is equal to
\{x\}
by the inductive hypothesis.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
be given. If
(k,n)
is good, then
(k+1,n+1)
is good.

Proof.

Suppose some
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
is injective. We have that
f
is given by
f(\mathbf{x})=\{g(\mathbf{x}):g\in\mathcal{G}\}
for some non-empty
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n-1}
.

Consider the function
f^{\prime}:D_{0}^{k+1}\to D_{n+1}
given by

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1})\,=\

\displaystyle\big\{\left\{g(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\right\}:g\in\mathcal{G}\big\}\ \cup

\displaystyle\big\{\left\{g(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}),\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1})\right\}:g\in\mathcal{G}\big\}.

By use of Lemma
2.1
, it can be seen that
f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{(k+1)}_{n+1}
. We will show that
f^{\prime}
is injective.

Let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in D_{0}^{k+1}
be given. Write
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1})
,
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{k+1})
, and suppose
f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=f^{\prime}(\mathbf{y})
. We wish to show that
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}
, i.e., that
x_{i}=y_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq k+1
.

Notice that, for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
, we have
\{g(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\}\in f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})
, and this set is a singleton. We claim that the converse holds as well: If
s\in f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})
is a singleton, then
s=\{g(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\}
for some
g\in\mathcal{G}
. To see this, note that
s
is either of the form
\{g(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\}
for some
g\in\mathcal{G}
(in which case we are done) or of the form
\{g(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}),\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1})\}
for some
g\in\mathcal{G}
. In the latter case, since
s
is a singleton, we have
g(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1})
, and hence
s=\{g(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\}
as desired. An analogous argument shows that the singletons in
f^{\prime}(\mathbf{y})
are exactly the sets in
D_{n}
that are of the form
\{g(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})\}
for some
g\in\mathcal{G}
.

Denoting by
S_{\mathbf{x}}
(respectively,
S_{\mathbf{y}}
) the set of singletons in
f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})
(respectively,
f^{\prime}(\mathbf{y})
), we thus have

\displaystyle\bigcup S_{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}),

\displaystyle\bigcup S_{\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=f(y_{1},\dots,y_{k}).

Since
f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=f^{\prime}(\mathbf{y})
, we have
S_{\mathbf{x}}=S_{\mathbf{y}}
by definition. Therefore,
\bigcup S_{\mathbf{x}}=\bigcup S_{\mathbf{y}}
, i.e.,
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=f(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
; and since
f
is injective, this means that
x_{i}=y_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. It remains to show that
x_{k+1}=y_{k+1}
.

Writing
S=S_{\mathbf{x}}=S_{\mathbf{y}}
, define

\displaystyle T_{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})\setminus S,

\displaystyle T_{\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(\mathbf{y})\setminus S.

Note that the elements of
T_{\mathbf{x}}
are exactly those sets in
D_{n}
that are of the form
\{g(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}),\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1})\}
for some
g\in\mathcal{G}
with
g(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\neq\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1})
; similarly,
mutatis mutandis
, for
T_{\mathbf{y}}
. Moreover, since
f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=f^{\prime}(\mathbf{y})
, we have
T_{\mathbf{x}}=T_{\mathbf{y}}
. We now split into cases.

•

Suppose
T_{\mathbf{x}}=T_{\mathbf{y}}
is empty. This means that for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
, it is the case that
g(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1})
. Therefore,
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\{\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1})\}=\iota_{n}(x_{k+1})
. A similar argument shows
f(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})=\iota_{n}(y_{k+1})
. Since
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=f(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
, it follows that
\iota_{n}(x_{k+1})=\iota_{n}(y_{k+1})
; hence, by Lemma
2.3
, we have
x_{k+1}=y_{k+1}
as desired.

•

Otherwise, let
t\in T_{\mathbf{x}}=T_{\mathbf{y}}
be given. We seek to derive a contradiction. Notice that every member of
T_{\mathbf{x}}
contains
\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1})
; likewise, every member of
T_{\mathbf{y}}
contains
\iota_{n-1}(y_{k+1})
. Therefore, in particular,
\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1}),\iota_{n-1}(y_{k+1})\in t
. Since
t\notin S
, it must be the case that
t
has exactly two elements; therefore,
t=\{\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1}),\iota_{n-1}(y_{k+1})\}
with
\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1})\neq\iota_{n-1}(y_{k+1})
. Notice, however, that
t
was chosen arbitrarily; and so

T_{\mathbf{x}}=T_{\mathbf{y}}=\big\{\left\{\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1}),\iota_{n-1}(y_{k+1})\right\}\big\}.

This implies, on the one hand, that
\iota_{n-1}(y_{k+1})
is the unique member of
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
that is distinct from
\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1})
, and, on the other, that
\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1})
is the unique member of
f(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
that is distinct from
\iota_{n-1}(y_{k+1})
. As previously established,
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=f(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
, and so together, we have

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=f(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})=\{\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1}),\iota_{n-1}(y_{k+1})\}.

Now, let
\sigma:D_{0}\to D_{0}
be the permutation
(x_{k+1}\ y_{k+1})
. We have

\displaystyle f(\sigma(x_{1}),\dots,\sigma(x_{k}))

\displaystyle=\hat{\sigma}_{n}(f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}))

by Lemma
2.5

\displaystyle=\hat{\sigma}_{n}\big(\{\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1}),\iota_{n-1}(y_{k+1})\}\big)

\displaystyle=\{\hat{\sigma}_{n-1}(\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1})),\hat{\sigma}_{n-1}(\iota_{n-1}(y_{k+1}))\}

\displaystyle\text{by def.\ of }\hat{\sigma}_{n}

\displaystyle=\{\iota_{n-1}(\sigma(x_{k+1})),\iota_{n-1}(\sigma(y_{k+1}))\}

by Lemma
2.5

\displaystyle=\{\iota_{n-1}(y_{k+1}),\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1})\}

\displaystyle=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}).

Since
f
is injective, this means that
\sigma(x_{i})=x_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
.

Now, because

\displaystyle\atom_{n}(f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}))

\displaystyle=\atom_{n}\!\big(\{\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1}),\iota_{n-1}(y_{k+1})\}\big)

\displaystyle\supseteq\atom_{n-1}(\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1}))

\displaystyle=\{x_{k+1}\}

\displaystyle\text{by Lemma~\ref{lem:atomiota}},

it follows by Lemma
2.7
that
x_{k+1}\in\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
, i.e.,
x_{k+1}=x_{j}
for some
1\leq j\leq k
; and since
x_{j}=\sigma(x_{j})
, this means
x_{k+1}=\sigma(x_{k+1})=y_{k+1}
. However, we established earlier that
\iota_{n-1}(x_{k+1})\neq\iota_{n-1}(y_{k+1})
, implying that
x_{k+1}\neq y_{k+1}
, a contradiction.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
be given. If
(k+1,n)
is good, then
(k,n)
is good.

Proof.

Suppose some
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(k+1)}_{n}
is injective. Then the function
f^{\prime}:D_{0}^{k}\to D_{n}
given by

f^{\prime}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},x_{k})

is in
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
by Lemma
2.1
and is clearly injective.
∎

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
be given. If
(k,n)
is good, then
(k,n+1)
is good.

First proof.

If
(k,n)
is good, then
(k+1,n+1)
is good by Theorem
2.9
, whence
(k,n+1)
is good by Theorem
2.10
.
∎

Second proof.

Suppose
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
is injective. Then the function
f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n+1}
given by
f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=\{f(\mathbf{x})\}
is easily seen to be injective.
∎

Lemma 2.12
.

The pair
(1,1)
is good.

Proof.

The function
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{1}
given by
f(x_{1})=\{x_{1}\}
is injective.
∎

Theorem 2.13
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there is
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(k,n)
is good.

Proof.

By induction on
k
. In the base case, apply Lemma
2.12
; for the inductive step, apply Theorem
2.9
.
∎

We are led to consider the following.

Definition 2.14
.

The function
\mu:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
sends
k
to the least
n
such that
(k,n)
is good, for all
k
.

By Theorem
2.13
,
\mu(k)
is well-defined for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
; and by Theorem
2.11
,
(k,n)
is good just in case
n\geq\mu(k)
for all
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, the course-of-values of
\mu
provides complete information as to which pairs
(k,n)
are good. Until Section
6
, we will henceforth devote our attention to the study of
\mu
.

3
Qualitative results

In this section, we establish a couple of qualitative results concerning
\mu
.

Theorem 3.1
.

The function
\mu
is nondecreasing.

Proof.

Suppose, aiming for a contradiction, that
\mu(k)>\mu(\ell)
for some
k<\ell
. By Theorem
2.10
, it follows that
(k,\mu(\ell))
is good, whence
\mu(\ell)\leq\mu(k)
, a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

The function
\mu
is unbounded.

Proof.

Recall that
D_{0}
is infinite. Let
h\geq 2
,
a_{1},\dots,a_{h}\in D_{0}
be given with
a_{i}\neq a_{j}
for
i\neq j
. Define
D_{0}^{\prime}=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{h}\}
, and for
n\geq 0
, recursively define
D_{n+1}^{\prime}
to be the set of non-empty subsets of
D_{n}^{\prime}
. Note that
D_{n}^{\prime}\subseteq D_{n}
and
D_{n}^{\prime}
is finite for all
n
; moreover, the value
|D_{n}^{\prime}|
is strictly increasing in
n
.

Now, let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be given. We wish to show that
\mu(\ell)>\mu(k)
for some
\ell>k
. Writing
n=\mu(k)
, let
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
be injective. Consider the restriction
f^{\prime}
of
f
to
(D_{0}^{\prime})^{k}
. By Lemma
2.7
, we have
\atom_{n}f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq D_{0}^{\prime}
for
\mathbf{x}\in(D_{0}^{\prime})^{k}
, whence
f^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})\in D_{n}^{\prime}
for all
\mathbf{x}
. Since
f
is injective, so is
f^{\prime}
, implying that
|D_{n}^{\prime}|\geq|(D_{0}^{\prime})^{k}|
. But since
|D_{0}|=h\geq 2
, we may choose
\ell>k
so that
|(D_{0}^{\prime})^{\ell}|>|D_{n}^{\prime}|
. Write
m=\mu(\ell)
, let
g\in\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}_{m}
be injective, and consider the restriction
g^{\prime}
of
g
to
(D_{0}^{\prime})^{\ell}
. Analogously to
f^{\prime}
, we have
\ran(g^{\prime})\subseteq D_{m}^{\prime}
, whence
|D_{m}^{\prime}|\geq|(D_{0}^{\prime})^{\ell}|
by injectivity of
g^{\prime}
. Together, this gives
|D^{\prime}_{m}|>|D^{\prime}_{n}|
, implying that
m>n
, i.e.,
\mu(\ell)>\mu(k)
, as desired.
∎

4
Determination of values

In this section, we determine
\mu(k)
exactly for
k\leq 5
. This will involve the use of three programs: one for enumerating the members of
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
, one for deciding whether some
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
is injective, and one for proposing possible injections
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
, all for given
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
. The programs, as well as the theory behind their design, are available in a repository.

Theorem 4.1
.

The following hold.

(i)

\mu(1)=1
.

(ii)

\mu(2)=2
.

(iii)

\mu(3)=\mu(4)=\mu(5)=3
.

Proof.

(i) It suffices to show
1\leq\mu(1)\leq 1
. The first inequality is trivial, while the second follows from Lemma
2.12
.

(ii) It is easy to show that no function in
\mathcal{F}^{(2)}_{1}
is injective, whence
\mu(2)>1
; on the other hand,
f_{K}\in\mathcal{F}^{(2)}_{2}
from Example
1.1
is injective, so
\mu(2)\leq 2
.

(iii) It suffices to show that
\mu(k)>2
for
k\geq 3
while
\mu(k)\leq 3
for
k\leq 5
, which, by Theorem
3.1
, amounts to showing that
\mu(3)>2
while
\mu(5)\leq 3
. For the former, an exhaustive search of
\mathcal{F}^{(3)}_{2}
yielded no injections, indicating
\mu(3)>2
as desired. For the latter, the function
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(5)}_{3}
given by

\displaystyle f(x_{1},\dots,x_{5})=\Big\{

\displaystyle\{\{x_{1},x_{2}\},\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{4},x_{5}\},\{x_{1},x_{3}\},\{x_{1},x_{4},x_{5}\},\{x_{5}\}\},

\displaystyle\{\{x_{1},x_{4}\},\{x_{3}\}\},\phantom{\Big\}}

\displaystyle\{\{x_{2},x_{3}\},\{x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\}\},\phantom{\Big\}}

\displaystyle\{\{x_{4}\}\}\Big\}

has been verified to be injective, so that
\mu(5)\leq 3
as desired.
∎

5
Quantitative results

In this section, we mention some quantitative properties of the function
\mu
.

Lemma 5.1
.

We have
\mu(k+1)\leq\mu(k)+1
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Since
(k,\mu(k))
is good, we have that
(k+1,\mu(k)+1)
is good by Theorem
2.9
, whence
\mu(k+1)\leq\mu(k)+1
as desired.
∎

Simple bounds on
\mu
can be given. On the one hand, the proof of Theorem
3.2
can be adapted to give a sublogarithmic lower bound; on the other, one can combine injections of low arity to obtain a logarithmic upper bound. Beyond this, the growth rate of
\mu
remains to be determined.

6
Variations on the problem

In this section, we briefly discuss some variants of our problem. The variations may be combined; here, we consider them individually.

The empty set.
Our definition of the
D_{n}
for
n\geq 1
excludes the empty set as a member. One may consider including it for every
n\geq 1
and admitting functions that return it. This would make available the binary function
f_{W}
given by

f_{W}(x_{1},x_{2})=\{\{\emptyset,\{x_{1}\}\},\{\{x_{2}\}\}\},

shown to be injective by
3
prior to the work of Kuratowski
(see
1
, 290)
. Here, the empty set serves to “tag” the argument
x_{1}
; more generally, one may study how the empty set, and derived sets such as
\{\emptyset\}
, allow for new injective functions.

Cardinality.
Conversely, one can consider restricting
D_{n+1}
to those non-empty subsets of
D_{n}
that are of cardinality at most
c\geq 1
, for
n\geq 0
, and restricting the function classes accordingly. One may ask how the choice of
c
affects the values of the function
\mu
, and study the special case
c=2
.

Atoms.
We have taken
D_{0}
to be an infinite set. One may instead take
|D_{0}|=c
for some fixed
c\in\mathbb{N}
and study whether this decreases
\mu(k)
for
k>c
compared to the infinite case.

Total orders.
Rather than having the functions in
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{n}
be defined on general
k
-tuples over
D_{0}
, we may restrict them to those tuples whose components are pairwise distinct, for
k\geq 1
,
n\geq 0
. The latter correspond, in effect, to totally ordered
n
-element subsets of
D_{0}
. In fact, in his
(
2
)
, Kuratowski proposed a set-theoretic definition of ordering relations, which we can adapt to our context in the following manner. For
k\geq 1
, denote by
(D_{0}^{k})^{*}
the set of
k
-tuples over
D_{0}
having pairwise distinct components. Then, the function
f:(D_{0}^{k})^{*}\to D_{2}
given by

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\{\{x_{1}\},\{x_{1},x_{2}\},\dots,\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\}

is injective for all
k
. Notice that the codomain is independent of
k
, and that for
k=2
, we recover (in restricted form) the function
f_{K}
. As a historical note, although Kuratowski suggested to define ordered pairs as sets by means of
f_{K}
, he did not establish the injectivity of this function on general pairs, but only on pairs whose two coordinates are distinct. The former does not directly follow from the latter, however.

Multisets.
Instead of having the members of
D_{n+1}
be sets, we may take them to be multisets, i.e., sets to which their elements may belong “with multiplicity”, for
n\geq 0
. Denote by
\widetilde{D}_{1}
the set of non-empty finite multisets of objects in
D_{0}
. Then, for
k\geq 1
, the function
f:D_{0}^{k}\to\widetilde{D}_{1}
given by

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=[\underbrace{x_{1},\dots,x_{1}}_{2^{0}},\underbrace{x_{2},\dots,x_{2}}_{2^{1}},\dots,\underbrace{x_{k},\dots,x_{k}}_{2^{k-1}}]

is injective. To see this, let
a\in D_{0}
be given and consider the number of times
a
is a member of
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
; the binary expansion of this number tells us which
x_{i}
, if any, are equal to
a
.

Cumulativity.
We have
D_{n+1}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(D_{n})
for all
n\geq 0
, where
\mathcal{P}(\cdot)
denotes the powerset operation. One could more generally take
D_{n+1}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\!\left(\bigcup_{i\leq n}D_{i}\right)
for
n\geq 0
. This setting, however, requires us to settle on a notion of heterogeneous equality. For instance, one will have to decide whether
D_{0}
is disjoint from
D_{1}\setminus D_{0}
. If no member of
D_{0}
is a set, then this statement holds; but if some members of
D_{0}
are sets, and especially if non-well-founded sets are admitted, then this may fail to be the case.
