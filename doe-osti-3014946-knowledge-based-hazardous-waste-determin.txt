Foreword
The National Science Foundation (NSF) Convergence Accelerator’s 4-year, $5.75M-funded
SOLAR project (Securing critical material supply chains by enabling photovoltaic circularity) is
converging research across institutions and disciplines to provide a comprehensive toolkit that
will deliver solutions to circularity barriers throughout the supply chain. SOLAR is led by
Battelle Memorial Institute with partner institutions including National Laboratory of the
Rockies (NLR), the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI), Texas A&M University’s Energy
Institute, Equitable Solar Solutions, and Southern Company. SOLAR will proactively ensure
circularity of solar photovoltaic (PV) modules before waste accumulates at large rates in several
decades. The SOLAR project will provide a toolkit to stakeholders by innovating in three
outcome areas: (1) reduce module end-of-life management costs by developing key tools for
detection and sortation, streamlining hazardous waste determinations, and training a new
workforce for PV decommissioning and repowering, (2) develop upcycling technology for
silicon and critical materials to improve recycling costs and help stabilize domestic supply
chains, and (3) build a groundbreaking modeling tool to account for PV recycling impacts across
supply chains and communities. The SOLAR toolkit will be constructed through a variety of
methods such as working groups, industry-driven integration council feedback, model
development and user testing, and technology development. In alignment with the broader goals
of the NSF Convergence Accelerator, SOLAR can be used as a case study and blueprint for
circularizing other material economies.

Executive Summary
This report explores the use of “generator knowledge” to determine whether a solar photovoltaic
(PV) module must be managed as hazardous waste prior to recycling or disposal.1 Generator
knowledge is a legal term and existing regulatory pathway for making a hazardous waste
determination that has been used by other industries but is a relatively unknown option to the PV
industry (EPA 2025a).2

In the United States, unless an exclusion or an alternative management standard applies, a
hazardous waste determination is required before recycling or landfilling a PV module. The
results of the hazardous waste determination dictate whether the PV module must be managed as
hazardous or nonhazardous solid waste (Section 1). The person or persons who decide to remove
a PV module from service and recycle or dispose of it are the “generator(s)” and regulated
person(s) who have generated waste and are subject to U.S. federal and state solid and hazardous
waste law pursuant to the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act of 1976 (RCRA) and RCRA
regulations (EPA 2025a; 2025b).3 Absent an applicable exclusion or alternative management
standard, the regulated person (e.g., asset owner) must either:

 • Manage the PV module as hazardous waste in compliance with U.S. federal and state
 hazardous waste law or
 • Conduct a hazardous waste determination to demonstrate whether the PV module is safe
 to manage as nonhazardous solid waste or whether it must be managed as hazardous
         waste (EPA 2025a).4
Managing a PV module as hazardous waste requires compliance with U.S. federal and state
hazardous waste laws. Hazardous waste regulations, in every U.S. jurisdiction, mandate
accumulation, handling, storage, transport, and treatment requirements and restrictions prior to
recycling or landfilling a PV module.5 Hazardous waste regulations, for example, set weight-
based accumulation limits and time constraints on how long PV modules may be accumulated
before being transported to a recycler or landfill.6 Hazardous waste law may also mandate

To demonstrate that a PV module can be safely managed as nonhazardous before recycling or
landfilling, the regulated person must make an accurate hazardous waste determination to assess
whether the PV module exhibits any hazardous characteristics (i.e., ignitability, corrosivity,
reactivity, or toxicity).11 The most common reason a PV module is regulated as hazardous is the
presence of a regulated metal at a leachable concentration that equals or exceeds the toxicity
characteristic regulatory limits (EPA 2025a; Curtis 2021a; Curtis 2021b). Today, commercially
available crystalline silicon (c-Si) and cadmium telluride thin-film (CdTe) PV modules may
contain trace amounts of regulated metals, with lead (Pb) in c-Si technologies and cadmium (Cd)
in thin-film modules the most likely to be detected in waste characterization tests, with some
modules exceeding regulatory thresholds (TamizhMani et al., 2021; 2018; Libby and Shaw 2018;
2019; Li et al. 2024; 2025).

When available knowledge is inadequate to make an accurate determination, the PV module
must be tested using Test Method 1311 TCLP or an equivalent EPA-approved method (EPA
2025a).14 Although Test Method 1311 is the primary regulatory compliance pathway currently
used to determine whether PV modules are hazardous waste, three main challenges raise both
practical and potential legal liability concerns. First, there is no U.S. federal or state regulatory
guidance that describes how to develop a scientifically credible sampling plan specific to PV
modules––such as specifying how many modules must be tested to characterize the average
properties of a batch or installation.15 Under this regulatory pathway (40 C.F.R. 262.11(d)(2)), a
sampling plan would be expected to define the purpose of the data and the PV modules to which
the results apply, so a hazardous waste determination could be construed by a regulator to apply
only to the PV modules and TCLP results covered by that sampling plan (EPA 2025l; 2002;
1986).16 Second, conducting TCLP testing on even a fraction of the modules within a single
large-scale project may not be practical (PVQAT 2023; Siegfried et al. 2024). At approximately
$550 to $3,000 per module, TCLP testing even 5%–10% of PV modules in a 100-megawatt
(MW) utility-scale system installed in the early 2000s could cost on the order of $10 million to
more than $100 million.17 Third, variability in TCLP results for PV modules––exceeding 50% in
some studies––has raised concerns for those legally responsible for making accurate hazardous
waste determinations (TamizhMani et al. 2021; 2018; Libby and Shaw 2018; Warren 2022).18 As

Given the challenges associated with Test Method 1311, solar industry members are calling for a
regulatory solution that is both reliable and scalable (PVQAT 2023; Siegfried et al. 2024).20 21
Although PV module hazardous waste determinations are a relatively new regulatory
consideration in the United States—because 72% of all installed modules are fewer than 7 years
old—these challenges are escalating quickly (Wood Mackenzie 2025d). The number of PV
modules reaching an end-of-life management decision is projected to increase nearly 31-fold,
from fewer than 1 million in 2024 to 31 million by 2035, and to 157 million by 2045 (Mirletz
and Ovaitt 2025). Regulators are beginning to prepare for this surge. At the same time, U.S.
recyclers caution that ongoing uncertainty over whether PV modules must be managed as
hazardous waste prior to recycling—and the potential resulting compliance burdens—may
impede domestic resource recovery opportunities (PVQAT 2023; Siegfried et al. 2024).22

This study finds knowledge-based hazardous waste determinations are an existing regulatory
compliance pathway that may offer a scalable framework to address current challenges in
making hazardous waste determinations for PV modules. 23 24 Under this approach, a regulated
person may rely on multiple lines of evidence to characterize PV modules over time, drawing
from diverse sources and forms of information rather than relying solely on contemporaneous
Test Method 1311 laboratory testing. For example, knowledge-based determinations may
incorporate previously conducted TCLP results or other relevant and verifiable data—such as
information from published studies, databases, or product labels—and apply those results to
other PV modules with comparable designs or material compositions. This approach may allow
PV modules to be characterized using representative data collected at different points in time,
across installations or batches, or among identical or substantially similar PV module types,
provided the information is relevant, reliable, and verifiable. It may also reduce the need for

As a case of first impression for the solar industry, the authors conducted legal research to
determine whether “generator knowledge” could serve as a viable regulatory compliance
pathway for end-of-life PV modules (i.e., PV modules with no potential for reuse that will be
recycled or landfilled). The team then developed and tested hypotheses through interviews with
more than 45 experts, including regulators from EPA and state agencies in Colorado,
Connecticut, Minnesota, North Carolina, New York, Ohio, Washington, and Wisconsin (see
Appendix A for Methodology details). Insights from these interviews—and input from additional
subject matter experts (see Acknowledgments)—informed the analysis of how “generator
knowledge” may be applied to PV modules to conduct a hazardous waste determination. This
work establishes an initial framework for interpreting and applying knowledge-based
determinations for PV modules, identifies potential constraints and limitations to this compliance
approach, and highlights areas requiring additional research.

The study’s findings provide an initial foundation for evaluating knowledge-based hazardous
waste determinations for PV modules. As the first legal analysis of its kind, this research
indicates “generator knowledge” may offer a viable regulatory pathway to streamline
compliance, support safe and responsible end-of-life management, and strengthen domestic
recycling efforts for PV modules. Improving the ability to accurately determine whether PV
modules exhibit hazardous characteristics at scale could also help address broader toxicity
concerns impacting the solar industry.25 However, as a foundational study, additional research,
stakeholder engagement, and consensus building are needed to further assess whether
knowledge-based hazardous waste determinations are a desirable regulatory compliance
approach for the solar industry. Key findings derived from the legal hypotheses developed in this

1 PV Modules Managed as Solid and Hazardous
  Waste
In the United States, a PV module that is “accumulated, stored, or treated before recycling or
disposal or is disposed of” is a “solid waste”48 pursuant to the Resource Conservation and
Recovery Act of 1976 (RCRA) and may be subject to federal and state hazardous waste
regulations (EPA 2025a; EPA 2025h). In practice, this means that a hazardous waste
determination serves as a prerequisite for recycling or landfilling a PV module, unless an
exclusion or an alternative management standard applies. The results of a hazardous waste
determination dictate whether the PV module must be managed as hazardous waste or may be
managed as nonhazardous waste prior to recycling or disposal.

The person who decides to remove a PV module from service and recycle or dispose of it is
considered a “generator”49 of solid waste and is the regulated person subject to U.S. federal and
state hazardous waste law (EPA 2025a; 2025b). Importantly, if more than one person is involved
in the decision to remove a PV module from service and recycle or dispose of it, they may be
considered co-generators (EPA 2025h; EPA 1980; EPA 1996).50 For example, an asset owner
that decides to remove from service and recycle a PV module, and an operations and
maintenance (O&M) contractor that removes the PV module from service prior to recycling, may
be considered co-generators and jointly and severally liable to comply with U.S. federal and state
hazardous waste law (EPA 2025h).51

Hazardous waste law in every U.S. jurisdiction mandates accumulation, handling, storage,
transport, and treatment requirements and restrictions prior to recycling or landfilling a PV
module.52 For example, hazardous waste regulations may constrain PV module management
options by limiting the number of modules and the length of time they may be accumulated on-

1.1 Is the PV module a solid waste?
A PV module becomes a solid waste and is subject to RCRA and potentially hazardous waste
regulation when it is removed from service and it is decided that it will be recycled or disposed
of (EPA 2025a).58 California’s Department of Toxic Substances Control (DTSC) guidance
provides that “PV modules become a waste when they are disconnected/removed from service
[and] not reused” (DTSC 2025a).

A PV module that is directly reused in another application or is sent to a reseller for reuse or
repair is not a solid waste (EPA 2025a; DTSC 2025a). In a reuse evaluation, a PV module is
considered a solid waste when it is determined that it cannot be reused, and the decision is made
to recycle or dispose of it. EPA recommends keeping PV modules with a reasonable expectation
of reuse separate from those that are not fit for reuse (EPA 2025a). Any person(s) managing PV
modules for reuse should maintain documentation to demonstrate that they were reused rather
than reclaimed or discarded, and therefore not solid waste (EPA 2025a; Straus and Bello 1987).59

1.2 Does an exclusion or alternative management standard apply?
If an exclusion60 or alternative management standard61 applies, a hazardous waste determination
is not required under U.S. federal or state law.62 In some cases, a recycled63 PV module may be
excluded from the definition of solid waste and therefore not subject to hazardous waste
regulation, or an alternative management standard may similarly eliminate the need for a
hazardous waste determination. Importantly, most U.S. jurisdictions (48 states, the District of
Columbia, and Guam) manage their own hazardous waste programs, meaning exclusions and
alternative management standards are jurisdiction-specific and vary by state or territory (EPA
2025b; EPA 2025c; EPA 2025d).64 Therefore, the federal RCRA exclusions and alternative
management standard examples outlined below may differ, or may not be available, in all
jurisdictions.

1.2.2 Alternative Management Standard Examples
In California69 and Hawaii,70 there is also an option to manage a PV module as universal waste
prior to recycling or disposal. Universal waste is an optional alternative management standard
created for certain types of hazardous waste that are commonly generated and have relatively
low risk during collection and transport compared to other hazardous waste (EPA 2025g).
Although still regulated as a subset of hazardous waste, PV modules managed as universal waste
are subject to less stringent restrictions and requirements than fully regulated hazardous waste. In
practice, a PV module regulated as universal waste eliminates the need for a hazardous waste
determination. The EPA71 and New York72 are also considering adopting universal waste
regulations for PV modules.

nonhazardous prior to recycling or disposal, the regulated person(s) must make an accurate
hazardous waste determination to see whether the PV module exhibits one or more hazardous
characteristics (i.e., ignitability, corrosivity, reactivity, or toxicity).74 The most common reason a
PV module would be regulated as hazardous is if it contains a regulated metal in a leachable

2 Hazardous Waste Determination Methods
To conduct a hazardous waste determination, the regulated person must either “apply
knowledge” or “test” the PV module using Test Method 1311 (Toxicity Characteristic Leaching
Procedure [TCLP]), or an equivalent EPA-approved method, or use “a combination of both”
testing and knowledge (EPA 2025a).88 In some jurisdictions, such as California, both TCLP and
a state-specific test (e.g., waste extraction test [WET]) may be required.89 90

2.1 Knowledge-Based Hazardous Waste Determinations
The regulated person(s) “must apply knowledge of the hazardous characteristic of the waste…in
light of the materials or processes used.”91 Acceptable knowledge for a hazardous waste
determination can include:

 • “Process knowledge (e.g., information about the chemical feedstocks and other inputs to
 the production process)
 • Knowledge of products, byproducts, and intermediates produced by the manufacturing
 process
 • Chemical or physical characterization of the waste
 • Information on the chemical and physical properties of the chemicals used or produced
 by the process or otherwise contained in the waste
 • Testing that illustrates the properties of the waste
 • Other reliable and relevant information about the properties of the waste or its
 constituents.”92

 Consideration: Process knowledge is recognized as “a substitute for physical
 testing that can be gleaned from data and records produced at some point when
 there was reliable knowledge as to the contents of the waste” (U.S. v. Hoffman,
         154 Wash.2d 730, 116 P.3d 999 (Wash. 2005)).93

hazardous waste determination for PV modules (EPA 2024a). However, U.S. federal and state

2.1.2 Information used to make a knowledge-based hazardous waste
 determination for PV modules must be detailed
Federal regulations and state regulatory guidance specify information used to make a knowledge-
based hazardous waste determination must be detailed and fully illustrative of the waste’s (PV
module) properties and constituents (IN DEM 2014; OR DEQ 2017; WI DNR 2023).98 In
practice, this means the information must be sufficient to determine:

98
     40 C.F.R. § 262.11(d)(1).

 11

 • Whether the PV module contains a regulated metal(s) and, if so,
 • Whether the concentration of those metal(s) equals or exceeds U.S. federal and/or state
 toxicity limits.
Determining whether a PV module contains regulated metals is relatively straightforward and
could be demonstrated through product labels, manufacturer data, or diagnostic testing using
standardized methods such as X-ray fluorescence (XRF) or Raman spectroscopy.99 For example,
a product label stating “100% lead free” or a complete BOM may demonstrate a PV module does
not contain Pb or other regulated metals.100 Studies have also used XRF analysis to characterize
elemental composition and identify regulated metals such as Pb in PV modules (Li et al. 2024;
Lee 2014; Lin et al. 2011).

If a PV module contains a regulated metal, determining whether its concentration equals or
exceeds U.S. federal or state toxicity limits may require additional compositional or analytical
data (IN DEM 2014; OR DEQ 2017; WI DNR 2023; EPA 1992; 2016 NC DEQ 2021). For
example, if XRF analysis confirms the presence of Pb in a c-Si PV module, the regulated person
may use TCLP results—whether from verified third-party sources or conducting Test Method
1311 directly—to determine whether Pb concentrations in the leachate meet or exceed applicable
toxicity characteristic limits.

As an alternative to laboratory testing, such as Test Method 1311, TCLP, a regulated person may
use manufacturer data (e.g., a BOM) or other compositional information to perform a total
constituent analysis (TCA; EPA 1992; 2016; DTSC 2025b; Li et al. 2024). The TCA estimates
the maximum theoretical leachate potential of a constituent (e.g., Pb) by dividing its total mass
concentration in the PV module by 20. If the calculated value is below the applicable regulatory
limit (e.g., 5 mg/L for Pb), no further analytical testing is required because the limit cannot be
exceeded even if all the regulated metals were to leach out (EPA 1992; DTSC 2025b; Li et al.
2024).101 For example, if a PV module contains less than 0.01% Pb by mass (100 ppm), the
module could not exceed the toxicity characteristic regulatory limit of 5 mg/L after leaching a
100% solid sample at a 20:1 solution to solid ratio. Therefore, the PV module could not exceed
the regulatory limit, and it could not exhibit the toxicity characteristic for Pb.

Notably, current manufacturer data, such as SDSs and BOMs, are often not detailed enough to
serve as the sole basis for determining 1) whether a PV module contains a regulated metal or 2)
whether its concentration exceeds regulatory toxicity limits. SDSs, for example, typically list
only constituents comprising 1% or more of a material by mass (IN DEM 2014; OR DEQ 2017;
WI DNR 2023). However, studies show Pb in c-Si and Cd in thin-film PV modules are each
typically less than 1% by total weight, meaning SDSs may not capture their exact concentrations
(Fiandra et al. 2019; Latunussa et al. 2016; Libby and Shaw 2018; Maani et al. 2020).
Accordingly, SDSs and BOMs may need to be supplemented with additional data sources when

2.1.3 Information used to make a knowledge-based hazardous waste
 determination for PV modules must be reliable
In addition to being detailed, information used to make a knowledge-based hazardous waste
determination must be reliable—that is, conclusive and verifiable (EPA 2025h; IN DEM 2014;
OR DEQ 2017; WI DNR 2023). Federal and state regulators interviewed for this study
emphasized confidence in the source of information and the ability to verify data are critical in
establishing reliability. U.S. federal and state regulators noted third-party sources, such as peer-
reviewed publications102 or databases103 that provide PV module compositional information or
analytical test data, may be considered reliable if the methodology and results are transparent and
verifiable. Similarly, a product label stating a PV module is “100% lead free” may support a
knowledge-based hazardous waste determination for PV modules if verified by a reputable
industry standards organization or other reliable methods.104

Experts interviewed noted obtaining reliable compositional data from PV module manufacturers
may be challenging. Although manufacturers possess detailed material data, they may be
reluctant to share full constituent information for specific PV modules because of proprietary
concerns.105 Data may also be more difficult to obtain for older PV modules or those produced
by manufacturers no longer in business.

Emerging supply chain transparency initiatives may provide a mechanism to improve access to
manufacturing data for PV modules. Examples include the Solar Energy Industries Association’s
Solar Supply Chain Traceability Protocol and the Global Electronics Council’s EPEAT Ecolabel
for PV Modules, both voluntary, consensus-based programs promoting product traceability and
data veracity (SEIA 2025; GEC 2025). These initiatives could be leveraged to inform
knowledge-based hazardous waste determinations for PV modules. For instance, the EPEAT
ecolabel––an independent third-party-verified environmental certification––requires
manufacturers to provide a substances inventory (e.g., metals and chemicals) for their PV
modules, and future updates could expand to include total regulated metal concentrations (GEC
2025). Similarly, the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA) of 2021 requires companies
importing goods produced in Xinjiang, China, to disclose the sources of their raw materials.106

2.1.4 Information used to make a knowledge-based hazardous waste
 determination for PV modules must be relevant
In addition to being detailed and reliable, federal and state regulatory guidance provides that
information used to make a knowledge-based hazardous waste determination must be relevant to
the specific “waste” (PV module) being analyzed (EPA 2015a; IN DEM 2014; OH EPA 2020;
OR DEQ 2017). Findings from this study indicate information about one PV module may be
used to inform a determination for another module if the modules are substantially similar—that
is, if there are no meaningful differences in design or composition that could affect the accuracy
of the determination (e.g., the presence or concentration of a regulated metal).107 For instance, if
differences in material composition could alter the results of a TCA calculation (e.g., total metal
content) or laboratory testing (e.g., leaching potential), the information would not be considered
relevant.

Historical data—information generated before a hazardous waste determination rather than
contemporaneous or newly produced data—may also be relevant if it accurately represents the
PV modules being analyzed (IN DEM 2014; OH EPA 2020; OR DEQ 2017; EPA 2015a).108
When relevant, such data can allow the same information to support knowledge-based hazardous
waste determinations for multiple PV modules. However, federal and state regulators cautioned
that relevancy may be limited by differences in module design, composition, age, or condition.109

2.2 Testing That Illustrates the Properties of the Waste
When adequate acceptable knowledge is unavailable, the PV module must be tested using EPA
Test Method 1311 TCLP124 or another EPA-approved method (EPA 2025a).125 126 Test Method
1311 evaluates the leaching potential of a solid material, such as a PV module, under simulated
landfill conditions and is the primary regulatory compliance method used today for determining
whether PV modules exhibit hazardous waste characteristics (EPA 2025a; EPA 1994).

Knowledge-based hazardous waste determinations may include “testing that illustrates the
properties of the waste,” such as previously conducted TCLP results or other analytical data.
Under RCRA, hazardous waste determinations based on testing—whether conducted under 40
C.F.R. § 262.11(d)(2) or relied upon as part of an acceptable knowledge determination under §
262.11(d)(1) (and state equivalents)—are expected to be based on representative samples of the
waste, as defined at 40 C.F.R. § 260.10. SW-846 Chapter 9 describes strategies for obtaining
representative samples and discusses statistical approaches to reduce the risk of decision errors
(EPA 2025l; 1986).

This section summarizes key considerations for applying previously conducted TCLP results and
other analytical approaches to support a knowledge-based hazardous waste determination for PV
modules under 40 C.F.R. § 262.11(d)(1) and equivalent U.S. state regulations.

2.2.2 Variations in PV module sampling and preparation methods may produce
 inconsistent Test Method 1311 TCLP results
Performing Test Method 1311 TCLP on PV modules can yield inconsistent results, with some
studies reporting variability ranging from approximately 30% to more than 50% when testing the
same module (Warren 2022; Nain and Kumar 2020; TamizhMani et al. 2018; 2021; Libby and
Shaw 2018; 2019). Experts attribute this variability primarily to:

 • Challenges and differing approaches for obtaining a representative PV module sample
 (i.e., which portions of the module are selected for testing)
 • Variations in sample extraction and preparation methods applied (e.g., mechanical vs.
 waterjet cutting and differences in particle size reduction), which can influence leaching
 behavior and test outcomes (Libby and Shaw 2018; 2019; TamizhMani et al. 2018;
 2021).134

This variability has raised concerns among those legally responsible for making accurate PV
module hazardous waste determinations.135 Anecdotal evidence suggests some asset owners—
preferring to recycle despite higher costs—opt instead for disposal in hazardous waste landfills
because of uncertainty about the reliability of TCLP test results (PVQAT 2023; Siegfried et al.
2024). 136 Industry experts have similarly noted that concerns about potential liability associated
with inconsistent TCLP results may lead to over-management of PV modules as hazardous
waste, regardless of test outcomes (PVQAT 2023; Siegfried et al. 2024).137 138 Although a
knowledge-based hazardous waste determination may rely on previously conducted TCLP test
results, it is not limited to them, allowing for the use of alternative analytical methods and
innovation in PV module–specific waste characterization.

 • Sampling from defined module areas (e.g., silicon cell, cell ribbon, noncell areas) in

 • Use of waterjet cutting to minimize sample fragmentation
 • Specification of target particle size
 • Instructions for laboratories not to further reduce the size or number of extracted samples

Test Method 1311 TCLP was designed to estimate potential leaching from materials disposed of
in unlined municipal landfills (EPA 1994; EPA 2016; Li et al. 2024). However, it has been
criticized as overly aggressive for PV modules because the required particle size reduction more
closely resembles recycling conditions than realistic breakage scenarios (Sinha and Wade 2015).
For instance, experimental evidence shows that crushed PV modules compacted under 50-ton
loads maintained encapsulation and structural integrity (Sinha et al. 2019b), and that toxic
constituents are unlikely to be released unless modules are “ground into a fine dust” (Fthenakis
and Zweibel 2003; OH DOH 2022). Because Test Method 1311 TCLP and California’s WET
test simulate acidic landfill conditions rather than rainwater exposure, they may overstate
leaching potential under realistic PV module field conditions and management scenarios (Li et
al. 2024; Sinha and Wade 2015).

In contrast, several countries have adopted more representative leaching procedures. Japan’s
JLT-13 method uses pre-cracked PV modules and a water-based leachate to simulate rainwater
exposure, whereas Germany and South Korea have implemented similar approaches (Sinha and
Wade 2015; Li et al. 2024). These methods offer potential models for developing more realistic,
PV-specific testing approaches in the United States.

may not pose significant human health or environmental risks. Using the EPA Method 1312
Synthetic Precipitation Leaching Procedure (SPLP), researchers found Pb and Cd concentrations
leached from weathered c-Si and CdTe modules were below EPA screening levels, even after 1
year of full submersion in a water-based solution (Sinha et al. 2019b). Similarly, a Test Method
1311 TCLP-based study found leachate concentrations from both c-Si and CdTe modules below
EPA human health and environmental thresholds (Sinha et al. 2019a).

Collectively, these findings underscore the value of evaluating alternative leaching tests—such
as SPLP and JLT-13—that better reflect realistic rainwater field exposure and end-of-life
management conditions for PV modules. These methods could be leveraged to inform the
development of approaches tailored to PV modules and representative in-field exposure and end-
of-life management conditions, helping support reliable and scalable knowledge-based hazardous
waste determinations for PV modules.

3 Conclusion
In the coming years, millions of PV modules in the United States are expected to reach end-of-
life, requiring decisions about whether to recycle them or send them to a landfill. Unless an
exclusion or alternative management standard applies, these modules must be managed under
U.S. federal and state hazardous waste law–or verified as nonhazardous through a hazardous
waste determination.

NLR’s PV in the Circular Economy (PV ICE) model finds approximately 35 GWdc of installed
capacity—representing 25–32 million PV modules—will be decommissioned in the United
States by 2035. By 2045, nearly 80 GWdc (126–157 million modules) are expected to retire,
increasing to roughly 130 GWdc (269–336 million modules) by 2050 (Mirletz and Ovaitt 2025).

published between 1995 and 2024 (Ovaitt et al. 2022; Mirletz and Ovaitt 2025).

With hundreds of millions of PV modules expected to reach end-of-life over the next 2 decades,
this is a critical period for addressing a key regulatory compliance challenge: making accurate
hazardous waste determinations. Application of Test Method 1311 TCLP to PV modules
presents practical and potential liability concerns, including temporal limitations and the absence
of federal or state sampling guidance specifying how many modules must be tested to represent a
batch or installation. Under this regulatory compliance pathway (40 C.F.R. § 262.11(d)(2) and
state analogs), regulators may interpret a hazardous waste determination as applying only to PV

 26

modules included in the sampling plan and associated with contemporaneous TCLP results (EPA
2025l; 2002; 1986).

Testing even a fraction of PV modules within a large-scale project using Test Method 1311
(under 40 C.F.R. § 262.11(d)(2) and state equivalents) is likely both cost- and time-
prohibitive,158 and reported test-result variability—exceeding 50% in some studies—has raised
concerns among parties legally responsible for making accurate hazardous waste determinations
(TamizhMani et al. 2021; 2018; PVQAT 2023; Siegfried et al. 2024).159 As a result, some asset
owners elect to manage PV modules as hazardous waste despite TCLP results that would
otherwise allow management in compliance with less stringent and more cost-effective
nonhazardous waste regulations––an outcome that has been anecdotally reported to negatively
affect domestic PV module resource recovery.160

 Consideration: PV module recycling presents a significant economic opportunity
 for the United States and is recognized as a key strategy to strengthen domestic
 manufacturing and alleviate supply chain constraints (DOE 2022). NLR’s PV ICE
 model estimates that by 2050, 5 million tonnes (Mt) of recycled PV modules in
 the United States could yield 3.63 Mt of glass (72.2%), 0.64 Mt of aluminum
         (12.8%), 0.18 Mt of silicon (3.6%), 0.003 Mt of copper (0.1%), and 0.002 Mt of
         silver (0.04%).

Knowledge-based hazardous waste determinations are an existing regulatory compliance
pathway under 40 C.F.R. § 262.11(d)(1) and state equivalents that may offer a scalable
framework for addressing current challenges associated with making hazardous waste
determinations for PV modules. This approach allows a regulated person to rely on multiple lines
of evidence—such as compositional data, prior TCLP results, published studies, databases, or
product labels—rather than laboratory testing alone. In doing so, PV modules may be
characterized using representative, verifiable data collected across different time periods,
installations, or batches or among identical or substantially similar module types.

By reducing the need for repetitive, generator-specific (e.g., batch-by-batch) testing and
supporting the use of broader, industry-level datasets, this regulatory compliance approach may
lower costs and improve scalability across assets, products, and markets. Because knowledge-
based hazardous waste determinations are not required to use Test Method 1311, this regulatory
compliance pathway may also encourage innovation through tailored analytical methods, shared
databases, or decision-support tools specific to the solar industry. Collectively, these findings
suggest knowledge-based hazardous waste determinations could enhance transparency and
scalability in PV module end-of-life management. However, as a foundational study, additional

Understanding the scalability of a knowledge-based compliance approach for PV modules is
particularly important. Regulators from eight state agencies interviewed for this study generally
agreed information used to make a verifiable hazardous waste determination for one PV module
could be applied to identical modules—those produced by the same manufacturer, with the same
model number and production year. However, perspectives diverged regarding whether that
same information could be applied to substantially similar modules, such as those from the same
manufacturer but with different model numbers or production years. Regulators and technical
experts also cautioned the age and condition of PV modules may influence the relevance of
historical compositional or analytical data, underscoring the need for further study to define the
boundaries of this approach and its potential for scalable regulatory compliance.

Knowledge-based hazardous waste determinations may also help address public concerns about
PV module toxicity, which have increasingly influenced permitting decisions, siting delays, and
local moratoria.161 Some communities now require evidence that proves PV modules are
nonhazardous prior to project approval (PVQAT 2023). Regulators and policymakers are
similarly seeking credible, publicly accessible data to inform decisions. Establishing a
transparent, third-party-verified database of compositional and analytical information could help
counter misinformation and support consistent regulatory decisions across jurisdictions.

Appendix A. Methodology
The authors conducted legal research, qualitative analysis, and application testing to assess how
“generator knowledge” may be used to make a hazardous waste determination—a case of first
impression for the photovoltaic (PV) industry. The objective was to understand how knowledge-
based determinations have been applied in other industries and approved by U.S. federal and
state regulators to ensure accuracy and regulatory defensibility for application to PV modules.

The legal analysis examined U.S. federal and state statutes and regulations, federal district and
state court decisions, administrative proceedings, U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)
and state guidance documents, and Federal Register notices to evaluate:

 • The legal standard for making a knowledge-based hazardous waste determination
 • The forms (e.g., peer-reviewed studies, databases) and sources (e.g., national laboratories,
 manufacturers) of information that may be used
 • The information requirements (e.g., constituent concentrations by weight) necessary to
 ensure an accurate determination
 • The potential limitations of applying knowledge to PV modules, including whether the
 timing or age of information affects the validity of a knowledge-based hazardous waste
 determination.
The authors then applied these legal findings to PV module management scenarios to identify
sources and forms of information that currently exist—or could be developed—to support
accurate, knowledge-based hazardous waste determinations for PV modules.

Building on the legal and application analysis, the authors conducted semi-structured interviews
to explore how regulators and technical experts interpret what constitutes an “adequate”
knowledge-based determination for PV modules, considering both information quality and
practical limitations.

More than 45 experts participated in semi-structured interviews, including U.S. federal and state
solid and hazardous waste regulators and compliance officers; environmental scientists and
engineers; testing laboratory specialists; environmental and sustainability professionals;
recycling standards auditors; PV module durability and materials scientists; manufacturers; asset
owners; and recyclers. Additional U.S. state regulators, industry representatives, and technical
experts contributed through follow-up communications and served as expert reviewers of the
draft findings (see Acknowledgments and References for details).
