Tyr–Mpa Proximity-Induced Atypical tert-Butyl
Intramolecular Migration in Desmopressin: Mechanistic
Elucidation of a Site-Selective Impurity and Its Suppression Strategy

Xin Lu, Fangfang Yuan, Xiaojie Han, Tingting Qin, Qianqian Wang, Zengli Li, Chuanjun Ge, Wenqing Shi, Haijiao Bai, Pei Wang

Abstract

Desmopressin, a synthetic
analog of vasopressin widely used in
the treatment of diabetes insipidus and bleeding disorders, is a representative
disulfide-containing peptide therapeutic prepared by Fmoc-based solid-phase
peptide synthesis (SPPS). Its unique sequence architecture, particularly
the spatial proximity between the N-terminal mercaptopropionic acid
residue Mpa(1) and the Tyr(2) residue, provides a distinct structural
environment for investigating sequence-dependent side reactions during
peptide manufacturing. The tert-butyl (t-Bu) protecting group is widely employed for side-chain protection
in Fmoc-based solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS). However, under
certain reaction conditions, the highly reactive t-Bu+ carbocation generated during acidic deprotection can
participate in nonspecific alkylation, leading to challenging process-related
impurities. In this study, we investigated a unique and highly site-selective
+56 Da impurity observed during side-chain deprotection. High-resolution
MS/MS analysis unambiguously identified this species as a site-selective
product arising from the migration of t-Bu from the
Tyr(2) side chain to the thiol group of Mpa(1). Based on the structural
features of desmopressin, in particular, the spatial proximity between
Tyr(2) and Mpa(1), we propose that the formation of this impurity
most plausibly proceeds via a vicinal-thiol-induced intramolecular
migration mechanism, which competes significantly with the conventional
diffusion-controlled carbocation intermediate intermolecular pathway.
Strengthening the scavenger conditions failed to eliminate this impurity,
further supporting the intramolecular migration mechanism induced
by vicinal thiol. This finding accounts for the observed site selectivity
that cannot be explained by the conventional carbocation intermediate
pathway alone. Guided by these mechanistic insights, we re-evaluated
the necessity of Tyr side-chain protection and proposed a “source-elimination”
strategy. Experimental validation demonstrated that the use of unprotected
Fmoc-Tyr-OH (free phenolic hydroxyl) completely suppressed the formation
of the t-Bu migration impurity and improved the crude
peptide purity to 93.5%. Overall, this study elucidates the formation
mechanism of t-Bu migration impurities and provides
a broadly applicable, mechanism-driven strategy for process optimization
in solid-phase peptide synthesis. While this study focuses on the
desmopressin system, the findings highlight a proximity-driven reactivity
pattern that may be relevant to other thiol-containing peptide sequences.

Introduction

Solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) has become the cornerstone
technology for the preparation of modern peptide therapeutics owing
to its operational simplicity, high degree of automation, and precise
control over sequence assembly. Nevertheless,
SPPS still faces numerous practical challenges. Side reactions may
occur during deprotection and resin cleavage, leading to the formation
of structurally diverse and process-dependent impurities.
,

Traditionally, such impurities can be removed via preparative
chromatography.
However, this downstream strategy exhibits significant limitations
during the scale-up. The chromatographic resolution between impurities
and the target peptide may vary unpredictably with process scale,
loading capacity, or batch-to-batch fluctuations. Additional purification
steps also impose substantial increases in solvent consumption and
processing time. More critically, product
loss during purification is inevitable, thereby compromising the overall
yield and economic efficiency. Consequently, minimizing or eliminating
impurities through upstream process optimization represents a more
sustainable and scalable approach, aligning with the ongoing trend
in peptide manufacturing toward higher efficiency, lower cost, and
greener production paradigms.
,

Among the many
steps in SPPS, the removal of side-chain protecting
groups represents one of the most critical challenges in process control.
This step is typically conducted under strongly acidic conditions,
where highly reactive intermediatessuch as carbocations or
radical speciesare transiently generated. Simultaneously,
nucleophilic or electrophilic functional groups on the partially deprotected
peptide are exposed to these high-energy environments. Together, these
factors create a favorable setting for nonspecific alkylation driven
by carbocationic or radical pathways. In practical process development,
common mitigation strategies include tuning the ratios of cleavage
reagents, scavengers, and reductants to balance deprotection efficiency
against suppression of side reactions, and incorporating functional
auxiliary additives when needed.

Desmopressin is a synthetic analogue of vasopressin widely used
in the treatment of diabetes insipidus and bleeding disorders. Structurally,
it contains a modified N-terminal mercaptopropionic acid (Mpa) residue
and a tyrosine residue at position 2, which together creates a unique
spatial arrangement involving a thiol and a phenolic functional group.This
structural feature provides a relevant model system for investigating
proximity-driven side reactions during peptide synthesis.

The t-Bu group is one of the most widely used
protecting groups for masking the phenolic hydroxyl of tyrosine (Tyr).
Removal of the t-Bu group under strongly acidic conditions
proceeds predominantly through a classical carbocation intermediate
pathway. It is well established that t-Bu, as an acid-labile protecting group, typically undergoes
protonation of the phenolic oxygen, followed by heterolytic cleavage
to generate a t-Bu+ that is subsequently
quenched. However, if scavenger levels are inadequate, t-Bu+as a highly potent electrophilemay
undergo nonspecific alkylation with nucleophilic side chains exposed
on the peptide, including residues susceptible to electrophilic attack
such as Cys, Trp, and Arg.

In the
desmopressin system investigated in this study, we identified
a t-Bu-migration impurity that exhibits an unexpected
level of site selectivity that deviates from the classical nonselective
behavior typically associated with a conventional carbocation intermediate
pathway. Only a single, site-specific +56 Da impurity was observed
in our system, thereby suggesting the involvement of an alternative,
more selective reaction pathway.
,

Further
investigation revealed that the vicinal arrangement of
Tyr and Mpa in desmopressin provides a feasible intramolecular geometric
context for the unexpected reaction. The thiol of Mpa, being a neutral
nucleophile, is capable of engaging in a vicinal-thiol-induced intramolecular
nucleophilic substitution onto the tert-Bu moiety
under strongly protic and polar cleavage conditions. This process
competes with the conventional diffusion-controlled carbocation intermediate
intermolecular processes and induces highly site-selective t-Bu migration. These findings not only highlight that analogous
nonclassical migration events may warrant attention in peptide systems
containing thiol functionalities but also suggest that sequence-specific
geometry can override classical protecting group logic.

Building
upon this mechanistic insight, we further reassessed the
necessity of Tyr side-chain protection to prevent the formation of
a reactive substitution window altogether. Ultimately, we demonstrated
that employing unprotected Fmoc-Tyr-OH completely suppresses the tert-Bu migration impurity and markedly improves crude-peptide
purity.

This study aims to provide a systematic solution for
controlling tert-Bu migration impurities through
a closed-loop workflow
comprising structural identification, mechanistic elucidation, process
optimization, and experimental validation. Moreover, the findings
offer a generalizable paradigm for mechanism-driven process development
in complex peptide syntheses.

Global Deprotection and Resin Cleavage

The fully assembled
desmopressin peptidyl resin was treated with
cleavage cocktail (100 mL) consisting of TFA/TIS/TAO/PhOH/H2O = 80:6:5:5:4 (v/v/v/v/v) supplemented with DTT (2 equivalents).
The cleavage reaction was carried out at 25 °C for 120 min under
gentle agitation. After completion, the cleavage solution was filtered
to remove the spent resin and slowly poured into cold diethyl ether
(500 mL) with vigorous stirring to precipitate the crude peptide.
The resulting suspension was filtered, and the solid was washed five
times with diethyl ether. The crude peptide was then dried under vacuum
in a desiccator to afford 2.105 g of crude product.

Results
and Discussion

Observation of a Distinct
+56 Da Impurity
and Preliminary Hypothesis of Its Origin

Under the standard
cleavage conditions (TFA/ triisopropylsilane (TIS) /thioanisole (TAO)/PhOH/H2O = 80/6/5/5/4 with added DTT, 25 °C, 2 h), a prominent
impurity was consistently observed in the crude HPLC profile of the
target peptide. (In addition, the front peak (retention time: 18.830
min) observed preceding the main peak (retention time: 19.203 min)
corresponds to an oxidized form of the target peptide. As the subsequent
step involves oxidation of the main component, this species becomes
the desired product and is therefore not considered an impurity at
this stage.) This impurity appeared at approximately 4.5%, substantially
exceeding the acceptable level for routine SPPS processes and markedly
higher than any other coexisting byproducts. As a consequence, the
crude purity decreased to 77.52%, leading to a significant reduction
in the overall synthetic yield. High-resolution mass spectrometry
revealed that the impurity exhibited a molecular mass 56.06266 Da
greater than that of the target peptide (See ). This mass shift is consistent with the
involvement of a t-Bu group (theoretical molecular
weight 56.06260), consistent with a transformation originating from
the Tyr(2) side-chain protecting group.

In Fmoc-SPPS, the t-Bu protecting group is removed under strongly acidic conditions
through a classical carbocation intermediate pathway, which may generate
two general classes of process-related impurities. (1) Incomplete deprotectioninsufficient acidity,
inadequate reaction temperature, or shortened reaction time may result
in retention of the original t-Bu protecting group
on Tyr; (2) Nonselective alkylationwhen scavengers are insufficient,
the t-Bu+ may act as a potent electrophile,
reacting with exposed nucleophilic side chains such as Mpa, Cys, or
Arg, leading to a variety of nonspecific alkylated impurities.

Strengthening the Cleavage Conditions Fails
to Reduce the Impurity Level: Excluding Incomplete t-Bu Deprotection as the Source

If the +56 Da impurity originated
from incomplete cleavage of the Tyr side-chain t-Bu protecting group, then applying more forcing acidic conditions
should reduce its abundance. To test this hypothesis, we intensified
the original cleavage cocktail (TFA/TIS/TAO/PhOH/H2O =
80:6:5:5:4 with 2 eq DTT, 25 °C and 2 h) and systematically evaluated
several modified conditions, including (1) A stronger acidic system,
increasing TFA content from 80% to 90% (TFA:TIS: ethanedithiol (EDT):
PhOH: H2O = 90:4:2:2:2 with added DTT and TCEP); (2) Elevated
reaction temperature, increasing from 25 to 30 °C; and (3) Extended
reaction time, lengthening from 2 to 2.5 h.

The resulting crude
products were analyzed by HPLC. The chromatograms are shown in and the data are
summarized in . The corresponding raw chromatograms are provided in the Supporting
Information (Figures S2–S4).

Despite
substantially strengthened cleavage conditions, the impurity
level did not decrease. Instead, a slight increase was observed in
all cases, and the overall crude purity showed no meaningful improvement.
These findings are inconsistent with the typical behavior of incomplete t-Bu deprotection and are consistent with the fact that
the impurity does not arise from insufficient cleavage of the Tyr
protecting group. Rather, its formation must involve a mechanistic
pathway distinct from that of conventional t-Bu removal.

Structural Elucidation of the Impurity by
MS/MS Fragmentation: t-Bu Migration to Mpa(1)

The standard peptide cleavage cocktail is dominated by trifluoroacetic
acid (TFA), a highly acidic and strongly protic polar medium. Under
such conditions, t-Bu–based protecting groups
(e.g., the phenolic t-Bu ether on Fmoc-Tyr­(t-Bu)-OH) are typically removed through a classical carbocation
intermediate pathway. In this pathway, the t-Bu–O–Ar
moiety is first protonated at the phenolic oxygen, significantly weakening
the C–O bond. Subsequent heterolysis of this bond generates
a t-Bu+ and liberates the free phenol.
The resulting t-Bu+ is rapidly trapped
by nucleophiles present in the scavenger system (e.g., water, thiols,
or other nucleophilic additives), thereby terminating the reaction.

This process represents a canonical carbocation intermediate-type
deprotection mechanism with the following defining features: (1) bond
cleavage precedes nucleophilic capture; (2) formation of a carbocation
intermediate leads to poor positional or regiochemical selectivity,
consistent with the formation of comparable levels of Mpa­(t-Bu), Cys­(t-Bu), and Arg­(t-Bu) adduct impurities; and (3) the product distribution is highly
sensitive to the concentration of scavengers.

To elucidate the
precise structure of the +56 Da impurity, which
appeared as the sole significant byproduct in the chromatogram, we
performed high-energy collision dissociation (HCD) MS/MS analysis
on the corresponding peak. The fragment
ion pattern revealed that all t-Bu–containing
diagnostic ions unambiguously corresponded to a modified form of Mpa(1).
MS/MS spectrum of the target impurity is shown in .

Notably, no additional
+56 Da species were detected apart from
Mpa-t-Bu, nor were any secondary fragments observed
that would originate from t-Bu attachment at alternative
nucleophilic sites such as Tyr(2) (y8
+ 1039.514),
Cys(6) (y4
+ 729.382), or Arg(8) (y2
+ 287.219). Structural assignments
corresponding to the diagnostic fragment ions (Tyr(2), Cys(6), and
Arg(8)) are provided in the Supporting Information (Figures S5-S7). This outcome is clearly inconsistent with
the expected signature of “nonselective carbocation capture”
(see ).

Repeated analyses across multiple batches consistently demonstrated
a high degree of positional selectivity for this impurity, further
confirming that it does not arise from incomplete deprotection of
the Tyr(2) tert-Bu group. At the same time, such
strict site specificity contrasts sharply with the stochastic alkylation
typically associated with a classical carbocation intermediate pathway.This
pronounced site selectivity cannot be readily explained by a diffusion-controlled
mechanism and, therefore, motivates the consideration of an alternative,
intramolecular reaction pathway.

Proposed
Mechanism of Impurity Formation:
Vicinal-Thiol-Induced Intramolecular Migration

Based on the
above evidence, we propose that the impurity most plausibly arises
from a vicinal-thiol-induced intramolecular migration pathway, which
is consistent with the experimental observations and competes with
the classical carbocation intermediate pathway. This mechanistic hypothesis
is supported by three structural and chemical considerations:

- (1)The weak protonation
propensity of
the tyrosine phenolic oxygen disfavors a carbocation intermediate
pathway.In t-Bu–O–Tyr, the phenolic
oxygen is conjugated with the aromatic ring, leading to partial delocalization
of its lone pair. As a result, its proton affinity in TFA is significantly
weaker than that of aliphatic alcohols, reducing the likelihood of
facile C–O bond cleavage and diminishing the formation of a t-Bu+. The chemoselectivity of modern coupling
reagents heavily favors amide-bond formation. With the carbocation
intermediate pathway disfavored, the system is more likely to proceed
through alternative, mechanistically feasible competing reactions.
- (2)Spatial proximity between
the Mpa
thiol and Tyr­(t-Bu) enables an intramolecular nucleophilic
attack.Within the structure of desmopressin, the thiol group
of Mpa(1) lies in close spatial proximity to the O-t-Bu moiety of Tyr(2)­(t-Bu). The predicted 3D structure
showing the spatial proximity of the thiol and t-Bu-O-Tyr is provided
in the Supporting Information (Figure S8). This geometry provides a highly accessible nucleophilic site and
gives rise to a locally elevated effective molarity. In this process, departure of the phenolic oxygen with
the bonding electron pair occurs concomitantly with nucleophilic attack
by the thiol sulfur via lone pair donation to the reaction center.
As a result, C–O bond cleavage and C–S bond formation
proceed in a concerted manner. While the main product is still predominantly
formed through diffusion-controlled intermolecular pathways, the probability
of intramolecular migration is significantly enhanced, rendering its
contribution non-negligible. This vicinal-thiol-induced intramolecular
migration proceeds with minimal entropic penalty. As a result, it
can effectively compete with diffusion of the reactive intermediate
away from the site of generation.
- (3)The thiol group, as a “soft”
nucleophile, more readily promotes an intramolecular migration in
protic, highly polar mediacompared with hydroxyl groups, thiols
(−SH) exhibit greater nucleophilic softness and higher polarizability. In strongly acidic cleavage media, thiols predominantly
exist in a neutral yet sufficiently nucleophilic form, enabling them
to stabilize the developing transition state during backside attack
on the t-Bu moiety. In contrast to classical charged
nucleophiles such as OH–, the neutral thiol can
directly approach the Tyr–O–t-Bu bond.
This process represents a shift from a neutral to a charge-separated
state. Protic, highly polar solvents effectively stabilize such charge
development, thereby facilitating the intramolecular migration.

This mechanism provides a coherent explanation
for the
above experimental observations: (1) strict site selectivity; The
reaction does not originate from simple competition between reactive
side chains and external scavengers for carbocations. Instead, it
is governed by a kinetically controlled competition between a conformation-induced
intramolecular migration process and escape of the carbocation from
the local microenvironment, thereby resulting in pronounced site selectivity
(2). The slight increase in impurity levels observed under reinforced
deprotection conditions (e.g., an elevated temperature and prolonged
reaction time) can be rationalized by a greater fraction of molecules
overcoming the activation barrier associated with the intramolecular
transfer process. This behavior cannot be adequately explained by
a typical diffusion-controlled intermolecular quenching mechanism.

Validation of the Proposed Mechanism:Strengthening
the Scavenger Conditions Failed to Eliminate the Impurity

According to the proposed mechanism, under conditions where the scavenger
is present in a large excess, carbocation intermediates that successfully
escape the intramolecular microenvironment predominantly undergo intermolecular
quenching to form the main product. In contrast, impurity formation
arises primarily from those carbocations that fail to escape and instead
undergo an intramolecular migration. Therefore, the impurity level
is fundamentally governed by the probability of intramolecular capture
of the carbocation immediately after its generation, which is largely
dictated by the molecular spatial arrangement and local conformational
preorganization. Under such conditions, further strengthening of the
scavenger conditions (e.g., increasing scavenger loading or reaction
severity) should not be expected to significantly alter this partitioning
and thus has minimal impact on the impurity level.

Carbocation-intermediate-type
pathways are highly sensitive to the composition and strength of the
scavenger system. If a substantial amount of t-Bu+ were indeed participating in electrophilic attack, then increasing
the equivalents of scavengers or introducing more nucleophilic thiol-based
traps (such as EDT or 3-Mpa) should markedly suppress the formation
of the byproduct.

To validate our
proposed mechanism, we tested several strategies,
including varying the ratios of scavengers and expanding the set of
nucleophilic trapping reagents. The resulting crude-product profiles
after these optimized treatments are shown in and summarized in . The corresponding raw chromatograms are
provided in the Supporting Information (Figures S9–S12).

In the validation studies, particular attention was
given to EDT
and 3-MPa. EDT is widely recognized as an efficient scavenger for
carbocationic species, especially in protecting thiol functionalities,
whereas 3-Mpa can likewise compete for highly reactive cationic intermediates
through its thiol group, thereby safeguarding the thiol on Mpa(1)
from electrophilic attack. Consequently, if the classical carbocation
intermediate pathway was operative, incorporation of these trapping
reagents should substantially reduce the likelihood of t-Bu+ reacting with thiols to generate alkylated byproducts.
However, the impurity remained well above the typical reporting threshold
for peptide-related impurities (0.5%) and continued to significantly
impair overall yield. These findings further demonstrate that the
impurity is not formed through a carbocation intermediate-type nonselective t-Bu alkylation, and the available validation experiments
are inconsistent with a carbocation intermediate pathway and validate
our mechanism.

Mechanism-Driven Process
Optimization: From
“Stepwise Deprotection” to “Upstream Avoidance”

On the basis of the proposed reaction mechanism, a hierarchical
evaluation of potential process-engineering strategies was undertaken
to suppress impurity formation, including stepwise deprotection, ensuring
that tyrosine deprotection occurs before liberation of the Mpa thiol,
thereby preventing the coexistence of a free thiol with t-Bu-protected Tyr. Two potential approaches can be envisioned: (1)
replacing the Tyr protecting group with a more labile one (e.g., BrZ)
and (2) substituting the Mpa protecting group with a more robust variant
(e.g., Acm).

However, introducing
an additional deprotection step would expose the peptide to new acidic
or basic conditions, extend the overall synthesis, increase solvent
and reagent consumption, and increase the risk of incomplete deprotection
or undesired side reactions. In solving one problem, such modifications
may inadvertently introduce new challenges, including an elevated
side-reaction risk, greater process complexity, and increased manufacturing
cost.

Thus, although these strategies are theoretically viable,
they
should be regarded as options of last resort to be employed only when
alternative process solutions prove insufficient.

It was essential
to reassess the actual necessity of employing
a t-Bu protecting group on the Tyr side chain. In
the original synthetic design, this protection was introduced to prevent
undesired esterification between the phenolic hydroxyl and activated
carboxylate intermediates during subsequent coupling steps. However, under standard SPPS conditions, amide-bond
formation overwhelmingly outcompetes ester formation: the nucleophilicity
of the α-amino group is substantially higher than that of the
phenolic hydroxyl, and commonly used coupling reagents generate activated
esters that intrinsically favor reaction with amines. Although the use of unprotected Fmoc-Tyr-OH has been described,
prior reports
−

 are confined to specialized sequences or nonstandard reaction conditions
(e.g., specific solvents or coupling reagents). Its application in
a commercial, platform-compatible synthetic process for desmopressin
remains unreported.

From an electronic standpoint, the lone
pair on the Tyr phenolic
oxygen is delocalized into the aromatic ring, further reducing its
nucleophilicity and diminishing its propensity to undergo side reactions
with activated carboxyl groups. Moreover, Tyr occupies the second
position from the N-terminus in the target peptide, which means that
only one downstream coupling step remains. This greatly limits both
the exposure time and the cumulative probability of any phenolic side
reactions. Collectively, these factors suggest that the omission of
the t-Bu group on Tyr is theoretically safe. In addition,
it may be preferable for this specific sequence.

Guided by this
rationale, we evaluated the use of unprotected Fmoc-Tyr-OH
as a starting amino acid. Experimental outcomes confirmed that this
modification did not generate any phenol-related byproduct. More importantly,
it eliminated the structural prerequisite for the intramolecular tert-Bu migration, thereby completely suppressing the +56
Da impurity. Analytical data demonstrated that the t-Bu migration species was entirely absent, and the crude purity increased
significantly to 93.5%. These findings highlight that removing an
unnecessary side-chain protecting group not only prevents the atypical
migration pathway but also simplifies the synthesis and improves both
the product quality and process robustness.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive structural
and mechanistic
analysis of a persistent site-selective tert-Bu migration
impurity in the synthesis of desmopressin. By integrating high-resolution
MS/MS characterization with systematic process stress tests, we demonstrated
that this impurity is not consistent with canonical incomplete deprotection
or a diffusion-controlled carbocation intermediate alkylation pathway.
Instead, it most plausibly arises from a vicinal-thiol-induced intramolecular
migration, a pathway facilitated by the specific spatial proximity
of the Mpa and Tyr residues. Further strengthening of the scavenger
condition experiments validates our proposed mechanism.

This
study does not merely identify a process impurity, but reveals
a proximity-governed selectivity, highlighting that intramolecular
spatial arrangement can significantly bias carbocation capture pathways.
These findings highlight a critical oversight in standard protecting
group strategies: sequence-specific geometry can override general
reactivity trends. While tert-butyl cleavage in peptide
synthesis is generally described as a nonselective carbocation intermediates
process, the present study reveals that such reactions can exhibit
pronounced site selectivity when governed by intramolecular proximity.
This observation challenges the conventional assumption of stochastic
carbocation capture and highlights the importance of sequence-dependent
spatial organization in directing side reactions. The findings provide
a new perspective on impurity formation in peptide synthesis and emphasize
the need to consider local geometry, in addition to intrinsic reactivity,
in protecting group design. For peptides containing thiol residues
adjacent to t-Bu-protected side chains, the risk
of intramolecular migration is significant. We validated a mechanism-based
solutionthe use of unprotected Fmoc-Tyr-OHwhich proved
to be a robust, scalable, and cost-effective method
to eliminate this impurity. This work establishes a clear precedent
for ′′structure-mechanism-process-verification′′ optimization,
offering a valuable reference for controlling atypical side reactions
in complex peptide synthesis.
