Surgical site infection incidence and surgical antibiotic prophylaxis in the ICU patient: a multicenter observational study
Authors: Bruno Pastene, Nyl Chemli, Emmanuel Dudoignon, François Depret, Minh-Pierre Lê, Nicolas Mongardon, Anne-Claire Lukaszewicz, Romain Ronflé, Marie Le Baron, Laurent Zieleskiewicz, Ignacio Martin-Loeches, Gary Duclos, Marc Leone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical site infection (SSI) is a major cause of postoperative morbidity. Surgical antibiotic prophylaxis (SAP), defined as the pre-incision administration of an antimicrobial agent targeting likely organisms in clean or clean-contaminated procedures without active infection at the operative site, is a cornerstone of prevention in surgery. However, critically ill patients represent a distinct population with profound susceptibility to infection. Data on SSI incidence and SAP practices in patients undergoing surgery during an intensive care unit (ICU) stay remain scarce.MethodsRetrospective multicenter cohort study in five French ICUs (March 2022–March 2023). Adult patients (≥18 years) with an ICU stay ≥ 48 h who underwent surgery during their ICU stay were included. Primary outcome was SSI incidence after surgery. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) with a prespecified adjustment set were used to estimate the adjusted association between antimicrobial spectrum and SSI, accounting for clustering within centers.Results338 patients were included (median age 56 years, IQR 35 – 67; 72% male). Overall SSI incidence was 21%. Procedures with suspected infection present at time of surgery (PATOS) (n = 52, 15%) had an SSI incidence of 37%, against 18% in PATOS-negative patients. In the primary adjusted GEE analysis, broad-spectrum vs. narrow-spectrum antimicrobials (OR 3.06; 95%CI 1.63–5.76; P < 0.001) and no antimicrobial vs. narrow-spectrum antimicrobials (OR 2.49; 95%CI 1.01–6.15; P = 0.048) were independently associated with SSI. Stratified analysis showed that antimicrobial spectrum also had significant effect on SSI risk on PATOS-positive and trauma patients. SSI was associated with prolonged ICU/hospital length of stay, increased organ support use, and worse outcomes at day 28 and day 90.ConclusionsSSI affects more than one in five critically ill patients. After adjustment for prespecified confounders, the association with broad-spectrum therapy likely reflected confounding by indication, whereas the absence of any antimicrobial coverage was independently associated with increased SSI risk. Conventional SAP guidelines derived from non-ICU populations may not apply to critically ill patients. ICU-specific prophylactic strategies, improved coordination between ICU and operating room teams, and prospective studies are needed to enhance SSI prevention in this high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40560-026-00916-0.

Introduction

Surgical site infection (SSI) is a major postoperative complication, with reported incidences ranging from 0.5% in knee prosthetic surgery to more than 10% in colorectal procedures [1]. SSIs are consistently associated with prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare resource utilization, and excess mortality [1–3]. In the operating room, international guidelines frame the use of surgical antibiotic prophylaxis (SAP), a cornerstone intervention for SSI prevention [4]. SAP refers to the prophylactic administration of an antimicrobial agent targeting the most likely organisms for a given surgical site in clean or clean-contaminated procedures [1, 5]. In most cases, a single pre-incision dose is recommended. Contaminated and dirty-infected surgeries are generally excluded from SAP recommendations, except in specific settings such as trauma surgery with contaminated open fractures.

The intensive care unit (ICU) represents a unique clinical environment where the most severely ill patients are managed, and a substantial proportion undergo surgical procedures during their ICU stay. These patients frequently present with multiple risk factors for infection, including prior or ongoing exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics, colonization with multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms, infection present at time of surgery (PATOS), organ dysfunction, and physiological frailty, all of which may profoundly modify SSI risk and SAP effectiveness [6, 7].

Despite these risks, data on SSI occurrence in ICU patients undergoing surgery during their ICU stay remain scarce. Available evidence is limited to selected populations, including a retrospective study reporting a high SSI incidence following surgery for intra-abdominal infection [8], and a narrative review highlighting the challenges of SAP stewardship in cardiac surgery ICU patients [9]. Importantly, while some guidelines recommend adapting SAP based on patient colonization status for specific procedures [10], there are currently no dedicated recommendations addressing SAP strategies in critically ill patients requiring surgery during their ICU stay. Preliminary single-center observational data suggest marked heterogeneity in SAP practices in this setting, underscoring significant uncertainty regarding optimal management [11].

We therefore hypothesized that ICU patients undergoing surgery during their ICU stay experience a substantial burden of SSIs and that SAP practices in this population are highly variable and frequently deviate from standard guideline recommendations. The primary objective of this multicenter retrospective observational study was to determine the incidence of SSIs in patients requiring surgical procedures during their ICU stay. The secondary objectives were to describe the current SAP strategies in this population, to assess their compliance with published guidelines [5] and the causal relationships between antimicrobial practices and SSI, thereby providing a foundation for future optimization of SAP stewardship in critically ill surgical patients.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective, multicenter cohort study across five academic and general intensive care units (ICUs): Marseille Hospital Nord, Aix-en-Provence Hospital, Paris Hospitals Saint Louis and Henri Mondor, and Lyon Hospital Edouard Herriot, from March 2022 to March 2023. In the Marseille center, all eligible patients were consecutively included, whereas in the other four centers, predefined cohorts of 20 to 31 consecutive patients were collected to ensure feasibility of data collection while maintaining multicenter participation.

Adult patients (≥ 18 years) were eligible if they had an ICU stay of at least 48 h and underwent any surgical procedure for any indication during their ICU stay.

Patient data were collected and processed in strict compliance with the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the French reference methodology MR-004. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, registered in the GDPR/APHM registry under number PADS23-112, and received institutional review board (IRB) approval (Ethics Committee for Research in Anesthesia and Intensive Care IRB 00010254–2023-071). All patients were adequately informed of the use of their data [12]. The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE statement for strengthening the reporting of observational studies [13].

Data collection

Data were extracted retrospectively from the electronic medical records of each participating center using a standardized data collection form.

Baseline patient characteristics included age, sex, comorbidities, documented beta-lactam allergy, Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS) II, and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score at ICU admission [14, 15]. Admission diagnosis and baseline risk factors for MDR bacteria were also recorded [16]. Preoperative microbiological data included the presence, site, and microbiological profile of MDR bacterial colonization.

Perioperative variables included the delay between ICU admission and surgery, PATOS status, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Surgical Wound Classification (SWC), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score on surgery day, type of surgery, duration of the surgical procedure, and SOFA score on the day of surgery [17].

Perioperative antimicrobial management variables included the type of antimicrobial strategy used (SAP, new antimicrobial therapy, or ongoing antimicrobial therapy), antimicrobial spectrum, and adequacy of treatment according to published guidelines [5, 18].

The occurrence and characteristics of SSIs were collected during the ICU stay, including delay between surgery and SSI diagnosis, CDC SSI classification, microbiological findings, and the presence of MDR bacteria or polymicrobial infection [4].

Postoperative outcomes included new MDR bacterial colonization (site and microbiology), requirement for renal replacement therapy (RRT), duration of invasive mechanical ventilation and vasopressor support during the ICU stay. ICU and hospital length of stay, ICU mortality, and administrative patient status at day 28 and day 90 following surgery were also collected.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of SSIs. Secondary outcomes included identification of potential risk factors for SSI and the association between SSI occurrence and postoperative outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR, 25th–75th percentile), depending on distribution assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics.

Univariate analyses were performed to compare preoperative characteristics and postoperative outcomes between patients with and without SSI. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared using two-tailed Student’s t-tests, while non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To contextualize the SSI rate and assess clinically important confounders on the SSI risk, descriptive sensitivity analyses stratified the cohort by PATOS status and SAP spectrum within PATOS-negative patients. All sensitivity analyses were descriptive, and no formal subgroup testing was conducted.

The primary confounder-adjusted analysis was performed using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) using the prespecified adjustment set, with an exchangeable correlation structure accounting for patient clustering within centers. Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

During the study period, 338 patients were included. Median (IQR) age was 56 years (35–67), and 245 (72%) were male. Trauma accounted for 52% of ICU admission diagnoses. Median (IQR) SAPS-II and SOFA scores at ICU admission were 35 (22–48) and 4 (2–7), respectively. A documented beta-lactam allergy was reported in 14 (4%) patients.

Risk factors for MDR bacteria colonization were identified in 60 (18%) patients. Surgical characteristics

Surgery was performed at a median (IQR) of 1 day (1–3) following ICU admission, with a median (IQR) operative duration of 120 min (75–190). PATOS-positive procedure accounted for 52 (15%) cases. On the day of surgery, median (IQR) SOFA score was 4 (1–7). According to the CDC SWC, 196 (58%) procedures were classified as clean or clean-contaminated (SWC 1–2) (Table 1).

Perioperative antimicrobial management

Overall, 248 (73%) patients received SAP, 79 (24%) were receiving ongoing antimicrobial therapy at the time of surgery, and 11 (3%) received a new antimicrobial therapy initiated intraoperatively. During surgery, 147 (43%), 97 (29%), and 94 (28%) patients received narrow-spectrum antimicrobials, broad-spectrum antimicrobials, no antimicrobial, respectively.

SAP administration was considered compliant with guidelines in 184 (55%) patients and non-compliant in 64 (19%). Management of ongoing antimicrobial therapy was compliant in 41 (12%) patients and non-compliant in 38 (11%). All patients receiving a new antimicrobial therapy intraoperatively were managed in compliance with guidelines (n = 11 (3%)) (Table 1). Detailed antimicrobial strategies are presented in Fig. 1.

Primary outcome

SSI occurred in 70 patients, corresponding to an incidence of 21%. SSI features are presented in Table 2. Median (IQR) time for SSI diagnosis was 7 days (1–16). SSI were mainly represented by organ/space SSI (n = 48, 68%), followed by deep incisional (n = 20, 29%) and superficial (2, 3%). Discussion

In this multicenter cohort of 338 critically ill patients undergoing surgery during their ICU stay, we observed a high incidence of SSI, affecting more than one in five patients. This burden substantially exceeds that reported in most non-ICU surgical populations and underscores the profound susceptibility of critically ill patients exposed to surgical interventions in an already high-risk environment. Despite widespread use of perioperative antibiotics, SSI occurrence was strongly driven by surgical contamination severity and, notably, by the complete absence of antimicrobial administration during surgery, rather than by deviations from standard SAP guidelines. These findings challenge the direct transposability of conventional SAP recommendations to the ICU setting and suggest that the pathophysiology of SSI in critically ill patients may differ fundamentally from that of elective surgical populations, warranting ICU-specific risk stratification and prophylactic strategies.

Our study highlights several important points regarding SSI and SAP in ICU patients. The incidence of SSI was 21%, which was higher than the incidences reported for non-ICU patients (0.5–10%) [1]. However, previous studies reported a 23% incidence of SSI in severe trauma patients [21] and in tertiary peritonitis patients admitted to ICU [8]. Our results are consistent with current literature and confirmed that ICU patients are at high risk for SSI. Nevertheless, the PATOS-stratified sensitivity analysis suggests that SSI risk is most likely attributable to case-mix complexity, notably the high prevalence of highly contaminated (CDC-SWC ≥ 3) and PATOS-positive procedures. Among PATOS-negative patients receiving appropriate narrow-spectrum prophylaxis, SSI rates approached 8%, directly comparable to non-ICU general surgery benchmarks [1, 21]. Our findings also suggest that SSI is associated with impaired outcomes, notably prolonged hospital stay, underlining the susceptibility of ICU patients and the need to define preventive strategies.

In the confounder-adjusted analysis, the elevated SSI risk in patients receiving broad-spectrum SAP (OR 3.06) presumably reflects residual confounding by indication rather than causality [22, 23]. However, patients receiving no SAP had significantly higher SSI risk (OR 2.49, P = 0.048), compared to those receiving narrow-spectrum SAP. In this critically ill population, the absence of any perioperative prophylaxis, even guideline-concordant, is associated with impaired outcomes, suggesting that the prophylactic action of SAP might be of greater importance here. In stratified analysis on admission diagnosis, the adverse effect of the absence of any perioperative prophylaxis seems more important in trauma patients when compared with non-trauma patients. These new findings highlight a critical discrepancy between existing prophylactic frameworks and the biological susceptibility of critically ill patients, suggesting that strict adherence to standard SAP recommendations may be insufficient in selected ICU populations.

Our findings are in contrast with findings in general surgery where strict adherence in SAP guidelines typically improves outcomes [24]. The increased susceptibility to infection of ICU patients, likely related to immunosuppression, presence of invasive devices, organ dysfunction, and frequent exposure to broad-spectrum antimicrobials, may partly explain our findings and suggests that the risk–benefit balance of SAP in this population may differ from that observed in general surgery patients [25]. Dedicated randomized controlled studies may be required to further evaluate this issue. Of note, 10% of the entire cohort did not receive their ongoing antimicrobial therapy during surgery, highlighting the need to improve communication between ICU and operating room teams.

This study has several notable strengths. Its multicenter design, including both academic and general ICUs, provides a comprehensive overview of SSI risk factors and SAP practices in critically ill patients. By capturing a wide spectrum of patient profiles, surgical indications, and antimicrobial strategies, the study reflects real-world practice and enhances the external validity and generalizability of the findings. In addition, data collection was standardized across participating centers, limiting inter-center variability and improving the internal consistency of the analyses.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. Owing to its retrospective observational design, the study is susceptible to missing data, unmeasured confounding, and information bias. As a result, some causal relationships, particularly regarding the effect of SAP strategies on SSI occurrence, cannot be definitively established. Although efforts were made to harmonize data collection across centers, residual heterogeneity in clinical practice and documentation may persist. Next, more than half of the cohort consisted of trauma patients, representing a specific case mix that may influence SSI risk. Also, postoperative antimicrobial therapy may have treated early SSIs, therefore leading to an underestimation of their true incidence.

Finally, although crude SSI rates varied across participating centers, we used GEE with center-level clustering to appropriately account for within-center correlation. However, this approach does not eliminate the observed between-center heterogeneity in baseline SSI risk, which may reflect differences in practices or case-mix factors not captured in our adjustment set. Center-level variations therefore remain a potential source of residual confounding.

Conclusion

In this multicenter cohort of critically ill patients undergoing surgery during their ICU stay, SSIs were frequent, affecting more than one in five patients and confirming SSI as a major and underappreciated complication in this population. PATOS and antimicrobial spectrum stratification showed that overall SSI incidence was largely explained by case-mix complexity and surgical contamination severity. After adjustment for center and key confounders, the absence of any perioperative antimicrobial administration was independently associated with SSI occurrence when compared with administration of a narrow-spectrum SAP. These findings suggest that, in general, the main principles of standard SAP remain valid, and failure to administer prophylaxis (i.e., not using antimicrobials) is a key preventable cause of SSI. Further research is needed to establish whether SAP should be administered to ICU patients undergoing interventions for which the SAP guidelines recommend against antibiotic prophylaxis. Together, our results highlight a critical gap between existing guidelines and real-world ICU practice and underscore the need for ICU-specific prophylactic strategies, improved coordination between ICU and operating room teams, and prospective studies to define optimal antimicrobial approaches tailored to this high-risk population.
