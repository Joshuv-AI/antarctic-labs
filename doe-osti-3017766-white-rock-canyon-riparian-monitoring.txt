Executive Summary
Lands at Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) are owned and managed by the Department of
Energy, National Nuclear Security Administration (DOE/NNSA). The Laboratory’s eastern
boundary is in White Rock Canyon (WRC) along the Rio Grande within Technical Areas (TAs) 70
and 33, with approximately 4 miles of property along the river (Figure 1). Feral cattle have been
documented along the Rio Grande in WRC since the 1980s. Cattle activity is known to be
especially harmful to riparian ecosystems, which are hubs for biodiversity and crucial habitat
used by multiple endangered species in this region, including the Southwestern Willow
Flycatcher and Yellow-billed Cuckoo on LANL property (Poessel 2020, Sanchez 2021).

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the status
of riparian ecosystems within WRC and the damage
feral cattle populations are imposing, ecologists at
LANL conducted a month-long vegetation monitoring
project in September 2025. Riparian vegetation
monitoring protocols and rapid assessments of cattle
impacts were used to quantify the intensity and
extent of cattle damage. The data collected will be
used as a baseline for comparison following the
removal of feral cattle from the canyon.

Introduction
Cattle grazing poses a signiﬁcant threat to the health of riparian ecosystems. Patterns of long-
term grazing have been linked to alterations in landscape composition, loss of native species,
soil erosion, and water contamination (Fleischner 2013). Excessive grazing and browsing
decimate native plant populations allowing for an inﬂux of non-native species. This ultimately
results in habitat loss and unnatural ecosystem succession. Feral cattle also pose a potential
threat to human activity due to their unpredictable and aggressive behaviors.

LANL biologists published a report in 2021 documenting feral cattle activity in WRC based on
data from game cameras deployed from 2018–19. Based on the camera data, the researchers
concluded that there is a population of about 20 feral cattle in WRC, likely the maximum
carrying capacity for the overgrazed and degraded environment (Sanchez 2021). In 2023, a
contract oﬃce direction letter from NNSA directed Triad National Security, LLC’s Environmental
Stewardship staﬀ to develop an implementation plan for the removal of feral cattle on DOE
property. The plan entitled “White rock canyon feral cattle removal plan management options
and considerations for the removal of feral cattle in white rock canyon” (LA-CP-23-20452) was
ﬁnalized in 2023. Further NNSA interest in chronicling the eﬀects of feral cattle presence arose
once more in 2025 resulting in this data collection eﬀort.

Methods
Data was collected during ﬁve diﬀerent ﬁeld days throughout September 2025. The main area
of interest for the study was the corridor of WRC within the boundaries of TA-33 and TA-70. This
location was found to have the most cattle activity and falls within LANL boundaries (Sanchez
2021). Twelve plots were established: four near spring locations, four along the streambank of
the Rio Grande, and four upland locations, higher up the canyon further from water sources
(Figure 1).

Plot layouts were adapted from the AIM National
Aquatic Monitoring Framework (BLM 2024; Figure
2). Vegetation species and litter were recorded
along a line-point intercept every 1 meter on each
of the three transects.

To record evidence of cattle disturbances, the
scope and intensity of various disturbances were
recorded within each plot according to the Rapid
Assessment of Cattle Impacts in Riparian

Surveyors visually assessed the area and accounted for six diﬀerent types of cattle impact
categories (grazing evidence, browse pressure, ground cover disturbance intensity and extent,
and streambank degradation intensity and extent) and noted the disturbance on a scale of
absence to signiﬁcant. Impact categories were then averaged for each plot to determine an
overall impact level (light, moderate, or signiﬁcant; Figure 1).

Results
The species with the highest percent cover (over 15%) were blue grama (Bouteloua gracilis),
desert olive (Forestiera pubescens), sand dropseed (Sporobolus cryptandrus), and Russian thistle
(Salsola sp.). There were more non-native invasive trees in the Rio plots along the streambank
and signiﬁcant signs of cattle use near the Rio Grande, including wallows, patties, and
vegetation disturbances (Photo 1). The plots closest to springs had the highest count of native
trees and densest canopy coverage with fewer signs of cattle browsing and grazing but wallows
and patties were present (Photo 2). Upland plots had signiﬁcant signs of cattle use, including
signiﬁcant browse activity on cacti (Photo 3). Denuded vegetation was typical in Rio and upland
plots (Photo 4).

A total of 8 out of the 12 plots had signiﬁcant overall signs of cattle damage (Figure 1). This
damage was consistent throughout plots with an abundance of tracks, trails, wallows, and
browse pressure on native woody regeneration (Photos 1–4).

A biodiversity analysis to determine diﬀerences in plant composition was conducted to evaluate
the diﬀerence in abundance and diversity of native and non-native plant species in the diﬀerent
plot types in White Rock Canyon. Native species were signiﬁcantly more abundant than non-
native species in the spring (p < 0.0001) and upland (p = 0.005) plot types (Figure 3), whereas
the diﬀerences within the river plots were smaller and not signiﬁcant. Additionally, the Shannon
Diversity Index shows diﬀerences in plant diversity between native and non-native communities
across the diﬀerent habitat types (p = 0.045; Figure 4).

Discussion
Removal of cattle from this location is the ﬁrst step towards pursuing restoration eﬀorts. In
depth management options and considerations that are speciﬁc to WRC can be found in the
White Rock Canyon Feral Cattle Removal Plan (LA-CP-23-20452). In especially impacted areas,
native seed dispersal and native tree revegetation through pole planting may also be necessary
to provide understory regeneration and soil stabilization. A long-term monitoring procedure
should be implemented to document ecosystem changes following the removal of the cattle.

According to the USDA ecological site description, our plots in WRC are composed of two main
ecological sites: shallow sandstone and riparian. While we documented the presence of some of
the predicted dominant species, several of the expected species were missing from the sites.
The diﬀerence in species composition suggests a transition from the reference state; site
transitions such as these are often the product of unmanaged grazing (USDA 2025).

In October we recently deployed 7 game cameras in WRC to track cattle movement until a
drone ﬂight that is planned in 2026. The cameras were set up in locations where we previously
witnessed the most cattle activity (Sanchez 2021). During the ﬁrst camera check in November,
we captured images of feral cattle on 4 of the 7 cameras. The cameras will be checked monthly
leading up to the drone ﬂight and then transition to checks every 3 months post-ﬂight.
