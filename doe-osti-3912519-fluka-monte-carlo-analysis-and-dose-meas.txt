 support of R&D on permanent magnet quadrupoles (PMQs) for the National Synchrotron Light

 to approximately 100 keV by measuring the potential degradation of its magnetic field as a function
 of absorbed dose.

 I. INTRODUCTION

 As part of the ongoing R&D carried out in preparation for the National Synchrotron Light Source II (NSLS-II)
 upgrade requiring the use of Permanent Magnet Quadrupoles (PMQs), detailed FLUKA Monte Carlo simulations were
 conducted to evaluate particle spectra and dose levels at the 20-ID Instrumentation Front End (IFE) (see Figure 1),
 where a PMQ prototype is expected to be irradiated in the coming months.
 The 20-ID front end, commissioned in 2023, is dedicated to testing new design concepts and characterizing acceler-
 ator materials, including their thermal fatigue and radiation resistance [1]. It provides a photon spectrum extending
 up to approximately 100 keV and can deliver around 2 kW of power (at 500 mA) at the endstation. The radiation
 tolerance of a samarium cobalt (Sm2 Co17 ) magnet will be assessed by measuring the potential degradation of its
 magnetic field as a function of absorbed dose in the photon field present near the endstation.

 Absorbed dose measurements were also performed at the 20-ID IFE, upstream of the beam stop, where the PMQ

 II. RADIATION EFFECTS IN MATERIALS AND ELECTRONIC DEVICES

 When exposed to the mixed radiation fields present in high-energy accelerators, materials and electronic devices are
 subject to cumulative and stochastic radiation effects. Cumulative effects include ionizing dose effects and displacement
 damage, while stochastic effects are associated with single-event phenomena in electronics (see Figure 2) [2, 3]:

 • Ionizing dose effects are cumulative effects arising from energy deposition through ionization and excitation
 processes, leading to changes in the electrical, mechanical, and optical properties of electronic devices and
 materials such as polymers and glasses. While charge build-up (trapped holes) in silicon dioxide (SiO2 ) insulators
 can result in gradual parameter degradation over time, such as threshold voltage shifts in MOS-based devices,
 ionizing radiation can also induce chemical degradation through bond breaking and free radical formation,
 leading to absorption bands (color centers from defect formation and charge trapping) in glasses and crystals,
 and to embrittlement and hardening of polymers. Ionizing dose effects are evaluated in terms of the Total
 Ionizing Dose (TID);

 4

 • Non-ionizing dose effects are cumulative effects resulting from energy transfer to recoil nuclei, leading to atomic
 displacements and subsequent defect accumulation and lattice damage in a wide range of materials, including
 semiconductors, metals and alloys, and ceramics. Displacement damage can be quantified by displacements per
 atom (dpa), while in high-energy accelerators, nuclear facilities or space environments it is often expressed in
 terms of 1 MeV neutron-equivalent fluence (1 MeV n-eq), defined as the fluence of 1 MeV neutrons that would
 produce the same displacement damage in a given material (usually silicon) as the actual mixed radiation field
 and energy spectra;
 • Single Event Effects (SEEs) are stochastic effects caused by direct or indirect energy deposition from a single
 energetic particle in electronic devices. The energy deposited give rise to a transient current pulse in the sensitive
 volume of the device, which can lead for instance to a memory state flip (Single Event Upset (SEU)) or even
 destructive failures such as Single Event Latch-up (SEL). The representative quantity used for SEEs is the
 High Energy Hadron (HEH) fluence, defined as the fluence of hadrons above 20 MeV, since nuclear reaction
 cross sections for charged hadrons decrease rapidly below this energy. The contribution from intermediate-
 energy (0.2–20 MeV) neutrons must also be taken into account, as they remain effective in inducing SEEs. The
 FLUKA estimator for SEEs, which includes this neutron contribution is expressed as the High Energy Hadron
 equivalent (HEH-eq) fluence [4].

 As described in [5, 6], a wide range of experiments have exposed permanent magnets to diverse incident particles,
including electrons, gamma-rays and neutrons, although mostly at energies well below those in high-energy accel-
erators. These studies showed that the demagnetization of permanent magnets cannot be evaluated solely by the
integrated dose over time, but also depends on the radiation fields and energy spectra to which they are exposed.
While the neutron energy spectrum near the vacuum chamber at the NSLS-II still remains to be characterized, its
upper end is expected to reach hundreds of MeV, as shown in [7], where 5 GeV electrons were simulated impinging
on an iron block.
 As previously mentioned, the hadronic contribution to cumulative radiation effects can be significant and must
be evaluated to fully assess the possible demagnetization of permanent magnets under prolonged exposure to mixed
radiation fields. Accordingly, further tests on permanent magnets directly installed at the NSLS-II are planned
following the current tests at the 20-ID front end.
 Several mechanisms for the demagnetization process have been proposed, relating it to the energy imparted to recoil
nuclei in the lattice and the resulting local temperature increase, which ultimately leads to a reduction of the local
magnetization. Based on experimental results, it was shown that a minimum fluence of 10¹¹–10¹² neutrons/cm² is
required to observe any effect on magnetization. [5, 6]
 Figure 3 summarizes the magnetization loss of neodymium–iron–boron (Nd2 Fe14 B) and samarium–cobalt (SmCo5 ,
Sm2 Co17 ) magnets as a function of neutron fluence. The radiation resistance of Sm2 Co17 magnets is shown to be
consistently higher than that of Nd2 Fe14 B magnets. The reported damage thresholds are of the order of 1013 n/cm2
for Nd2 Fe14 B and around 1019 n/cm2 for (Sm2 Co17 ), although a decrease in magnetization has already been observed
at fluences of about 1014 n/cm2 , as shown in Figure 3.

 III. INSTRUMENTATION & METHODS

 1. Passive Dosimeters and Placement

 Preliminary dose measurements were performed at the intended permanent magnet location for the planned irradia-
tion test at the 20-ID front end (position 2 in Figure 1). The main passive dosimeters selected for these measurements
were radiochromic films, as they feature a relatively high upper dose limit and their small thickness could allow
measurements to be performed between the vacuum chamber and the magnet:
 • FWT-60 series radiochromic dosimeters (Far West Technology) – passive film dosimeters consisting of a nylon
 matrix (∼50 µm thick) with hexa(hydroxyethyl) aminotriphenylacetonitrile (HHEVC) dye, featuring a dose
 range from 0.5 kGy to 200 kGy, with an expected1 low energy dependence above ∼100 keV and a reduction in
 sensitivity at lower energies. [9–11]

 3. Radiation Source Term

 The synchrotron radiation source at the 20-ID front end is an out-of-vacuum planar undulator (U68) that produces
a photon spectrum extending from soft to hard energies, as shown in Figure 7. Two irradiation conditions were
carried out and reproduced in the FLUKA simulations: (i) of the 5.362 kW provided by the U68 undulator, 0.569
kW was deposited in the upstream absorber mask and 1.178 kW in the final beam stop, with a beam footprint of
10.1 mm (H) × 3.5 mm (V); (ii) the power deposited in the beam stop was reduced by half (0.585 kW), with a beam
footprint of 10.1 mm (H) × 1.5 mm (V) and no losses at the absorber mask [15]. At the beam stop, the beam profile
was modeled as a Gaussian distribution in both transverse dimensions, with ±3σx = 10.1 mm and ±3σy = 3.5 mm for
configuration (i), and with the vertical extent reduced to ±3σy = 1.5 mm for configuration (ii). The power dissipated
in the absorber mask for configuration (i) was considered to be equally distributed between two points at the aperture
center, located at the top and bottom of the aperture.

 Due to the ∼10–MeV threshold required to open the photonuclear channel, only bremsstrahlung photons above
this energy can contribute to a hadronic component at the 20-ID front end. Given the much lower power of gas
bremsstrahlung (eight orders of magnitude below the synchrotron contribution) and its characteristic spectrum,
strongly weighted toward low photon energies with an approximately 1/E dependence, this component was not
evaluated in the present study. Off-orbit electrons entering the front end during top-off injection can also contribute
to bremsstrahlung production and induce electronuclear reactions; however, both contributions are neglected in this
work, since electron losses in this portion of the storage ring are not expected to be of particular concern, as indicated
for example by the periodical radiological surveys of pentant five [17].

FIG. 13: Absorbed dose comparison between radiochromic film measurements and FLUKA calculations above the
vacuum chamber for campaigns #1 (top), with 376.5 h of exposure time; #2 (center), with the duration reduced by a
factor of ∼10 (40 h); and #3 (bottom), with a further reduction in duration (20.8 h), and in both the dissipated power
and beam footprint at the endstation (see Table I). Left: comparison of the results along the longitudinal direction
(Z-axis) at the central film position (x = 0 cm), using the values from Tables II and III. Right: comparison between
the three transverse measurements (X-axis) at the downstream end of the film (z = 2286.5 cm), shown in Figure 8,
and the simulated results reported in Table III. The shaded horizontal region represents the dose measurement range
of the radiochromic films.

 From Figure 13, the following observations can be made:
 • despite an irradiation time ten times shorter than in the previous test, most of the dose levels obtained during
 campaign #2 show only a few-fold reduction, and some are even higher than those measured in campaign #1.
 However, as shown in Figure 14, the downstream end of the films used during campaign #1 were damaged,
 probably due to excessive temperature or radiation exposure, since according to the FLUKA simulation the
 absorbed dose deposited in the last centimeter of the film reached 10 MGy after 376.5 hours of exposure. This
 might have affected the measured optical absorbance over a significant area of the film, and hence the derived
 absorbed dose;

 13

 • the radiochromic film measurements are systematically lower than the FLUKA estimates, consistent with a
 reduced response of the films to low-energy photons (see Subsection III 1), while a significant fraction of the
 photons reaching the films lies in this part of the spectrum, as shown in Figure 9; The systematic offset with
 respect to the simulation predictions reaches almost one order of magnitude for campaign #1 and becomes more
 pronounced toward the damaged downstream end of the film; for most measurements in campaigns #2 and #3,
 the offset decreases to a factor of 2–3.
 • The measurements follow the same spatial profile predicted by the simulations in both dimensions, longitudinally
 along the film (Figure 13, left) and in the transverse direction (Figure 13, right), despite the systematic offset
 mentioned above.
 • the dose distribution along the film for campaign #3 (Figure 13, bottom-left) changed significantly with respect
 to the previous tests, since all primary photons were intercepted by the extended nose of the beam stop, as
 clearly observed in both simulation and measurement results, especially at the upstream end of the film.

FIG. 14: Radiochromic film irradiated during campaign #1 and subsequently cut into 1 × 1 cm2 pads for the readout
procedure. The last pads at the downstream end of the film are clearly damaged, while the enhanced bluish coloration
reflects the increased absorbed dose in the film.

  The observed differences between the measurement and simulation results shown in Figure 13 may also be influenced
by uncertainties associated with the radiochromic film dosimetry and by simulation-related factors, including discrep-
ancies in the beam phase-space description, mismatches in the beam loss pattern in the vicinity of the endstation,
and possible approximations in the geometrical model.

 VI. CONCLUSION

  Absorbed dose measurements were performed at the 20-ID IFE (NSLS-II) using FWT–60 radiochromic films (10
cm × 3 cm × 50 µm) from Far West Technology and compared with FLUKA Monte Carlo simulations. A beam
stop incorporating a nose extension was used at the endstation, with its tip reaching the downstream end of the
radiochromic films, which were horizontally oriented and positioned 0.5 mm above and below the vacuum chamber.
This configuration generated a significant dose gradient spanning more than three orders of magnitude over only 5
cm along the films.
  According to the FLUKA simulations, the absorbed dose at the downstream end of the films was of the order
of 10 MGy after 376.5 hours of irradiation (∼2 weeks of accelerator operation). However, due to the strong dose
gradients between the upstream absorber mask and the beam stop, the energy imparted to a permanent magnet at
this location would strongly depend on its exact placement. Therefore, dedicated simulations including the magnet
in the implemented geometry will be performed for the upcoming PMQ irradiation test at the 20-ID front end to
properly quantify the dose deposited in the magnet.
  The radiochromic films were found to exhibit a systematic under-response in the absorbed dose compared with the
FLUKA estimates. This may be due to a reduced sensitivity to photons below ∼100 keV, leading to a corresponding
deficit in the measured response.
 While the current test at the 20-ID IFE focus on ionization effects in permanent magnets, it should be noted that non-ionizing
effects from the neutron flux present in the storage ring may also affect the PMQs. All cumulative effects from a mixed field
representative of the storage ring environment should therefore be evaluated, for instance through dedicated irradiation tests in

 14

the storage ring or at mixed-field facilities such as the CERN CHARM Facility [19]. Additional FLUKA simulations to evaluate
the particle field in the storage ring and compute the representative quantities of cumulative and stochastic radiation effects,
namely the 1 MeV neutron-equivalent and HEH fluences, are highly recommended to complement the current photon-field
exposure studies.
