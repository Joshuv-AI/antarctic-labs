Introduction

Transplant nephrectomy (TN) is indicated for various graft complications, including rejection, infection, vascular issues, or malignancies [1, 2]. Graft intolerance syndrome (GIS), characterized by pain, fever, and inflammatory symptoms, is the most common indication [1, 2]. Due to persistent perirenal inflammatory changes, TN for GIS is often accompanied by dense adhesions, increasing the risk of intraoperative bleeding and prolonged operative time [3, 4].

Open surgery (intracapsular or extracapsular) remains the standard approach [5]. Although minimally invasive approaches, including laparoscopic and robot‐assisted techniques, are increasingly applied [6, 7, 8], the optimal laparoscopic strategy for TN has not been fully established. Herein, we report a case of pembrolizumab‐induced GIS safely managed with laparoscopic TN using a combined intracapsular and extracapsular dissection approach, demonstrating a feasible strategy for managing complex adhesions.

Case Presentation

A 49‐year‐old man with graft failure at age 41 underwent staged laparoscopic bilateral radical nephrectomy for native renal cell carcinomas (pT3a and pT1a). Adjuvant pembrolizumab was initiated for the locally advanced left renal carcinoma. Six days later, the patient developed GIS, manifesting as high fever, chills, and graft‐site pain. Symptoms improved with steroid pulse therapy, but computed tomography (CT) showed enlargement of the previously atrophic graft (Figure 1). Laparoscopic TN was planned to enable safe continuation of immunotherapy. The temporal association strongly suggested that pembrolizumab‐induced immune activation triggered GIS.

TN was performed laparoscopically using four ports in the left lateral position (Figure 2a). Intraperitoneal ultrasonography identified the vascular anastomosis, but severe adhesions limited further hilar dissection (Figure 2b–d). The peritoneum was incised along the clearly visible graft outline. Due to dense adhesions between the capsule and abdominal wall muscles, an extracapsular approach was employed to dissect circumferentially along the abdominal wall, delineating the hilum (Figure 3a–e). Subsequently, intracapsular dissection of the renal hilum was performed. Intraoperative exploration revealed multiple feeding vessels supplying the allograft, all of which were successfully divided using a vessel‐sealing device. The graft ureter was clipped and transected at the lower pole, and the kidney was removed (Figure 3f–j). Additional hemostasis was achieved using soft coagulation, and external iliac flow preservation was confirmed via ultrasonography (Figure 3k–o). The operative time was 3 h 15 min, with an estimated blood loss of 100 mL; no blood transfusion was required. Pathology revealed T‐cell‐mediated rejection.

Steroid therapy was discontinued, and the patient was discharged on day 5. Pembrolizumab was resumed on day 18. Follow‐up CT at 3 months confirmed no radiologically evident residual allograft tissue and preserved external iliac flow (Figure 4).

Discussion

TN for GIS is technically challenging because chronic inflammation results in dense perirenal adhesions, increasing bleeding risks. Major complications occur in up to 21% of late TNs [1], reflecting high morbidity. Therefore, the choice of dissection strategy is critical. In this case, laparoscopic TN was performed using a combined approach for GIS following immunotherapy, demonstrating the safety of this strategy.

The intracapsular approach is often selected for late cases with severe adhesions, whereas the extracapsular approach is preferred in early cases [1, 2, 4]. Here, direct hilar dissection was unsafe due to dense adhesions. Therefore, we initiated an extracapsular approach from the abdominal wall, establishing a safe dissection plane that avoided capsular tearing and reduced bleeding. After outlining the graft, selective intracapsular resection was performed at the hilum, avoiding direct dissection of densely adherent major vessels and reducing vascular injury risks.

Regarding the vascular dissection strategy in the robotic approach, identifying the common iliac artery first and subsequently proceeding toward the vascular anastomosis has been recommended [7]. Although this strategy facilitates proximal vascular control, we intentionally limited the extent of dissection in this case. Dense perienteric adhesions from previous surgery and persistent inflammation made extensive retroperitoneal vascular dissection likely to increase the risk of injury to adjacent vessels and organs, including the small intestine and colon. Therefore, we minimized the dissection area to prioritize procedural safety.

With respect to arterial stump management, conventional open intracapsular TN often requires vascular clips or suture ligation to prevent delayed hemorrhage or pseudoaneurysm formation. In our case, however, intraoperative exploration identified multiple feeding vessels to the allograft rather than a single dominant artery. These vessels were safely divided using a vessel‐sealing device, followed by soft coagulation to ensure hemostasis. This approach avoided the need for mechanical clips or sutures in a densely adherent and fragile operative field, and postoperative imaging demonstrated no vascular complications.

Despite severe adhesions, the operative time (195 min) and blood loss (100 mL) compare favorably with previous series. Embolization or open TN may be considered as alternatives; however, embolization may not provide definitive symptom control, while open surgery has higher morbidity.

Continuation of ICI therapy was essential in this case. Minimally invasive TN reduces surgical stress, facilitating early return to systemic therapy [6, 7, 8]. As ICI‐induced GIS can recur even in nonfunctioning allografts, definitive and minimally invasive removal is clinically valuable. This report is limited by its single‐case design.

In conclusion, laparoscopic TN using a combined extracapsular and intracapsular approach is a safe, effective option for managing GIS with severe adhesions. This strategy minimizes vascular dissection, achieves complete resection without radiologically evident residual allograft tissue, and facilitates rapid postoperative recovery.
