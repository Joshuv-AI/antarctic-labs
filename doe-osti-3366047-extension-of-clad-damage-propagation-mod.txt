 ABSTRACT
 This report presents an extension of the Clad Damage Propagation (CDAP) model implemented in the
MOOSE SubChannel Module (SCM) to capture post-failure fission-gas dispersal and two-phase flow
effects in sodium-cooled fast reactor assemblies. The extended model tracks discharged gas axially and
radially, computes channel-averaged flow quality and void fraction using a Lockhart–Martinelli
framework, evaluates two-phase frictional pressure-drop multipliers, determines inlet mass-flow
degradation under fixed core pressures, and applies an intensified-void-based heat-transfer degradation to
affected fuel pins. Radial plume expansion is parameterized using mineral-oil jet experiments mapped to
sodium conditions via Reynolds–Weber similarity.
 Implementation details are documented, along with the new methods and user inputs needed to control
plume mapping and two-phase behavior. Demonstration simulations for 19- and 37-pin bundles show that
breach size and inlet velocity strongly influence propagation potential: small breaches (≤0.5 mm) produce
limited degradation while larger breaches (~1 mm) can drive oscillatory temperature spikes and enhanced
failure propagation, especially at higher velocities. These results demonstrate that the extended CDAP
model provides a more complete framework for quantifying cladding damage propagation and evaluating
propagation potential in transient scenarios. The approach remains computationally efficient, consistent
with subchannel-level analysis, yet incorporates sufficient physics to bridge localized post-failure effects
with bundle- and assembly-scale degradation.

1 Introduction
 The Clad Damage Propagation (CDAP) model was originally developed for (U,Pu)O₂ fuel lead-cooled
fast reactor (LFR) conditions [1–2] in the SAS4A/SASSYS-1 code [3]. In a previous report [4], CDAP
was adapted and documented within the Subchannel (SCM) module of MOOSE up through localized post-
failure phenomena, namely pin depressurization and fission-gas jet impingement on neighboring cladding
surfaces that cause surface blanketing and spray cooling. That initial development did not include
subsequent transport of released gas into the coolant channel.
 CDAP in the SCM module of the MOOSE framework was originally formulated to address the
stochastic nature of fuel pin failures in metallic-fueled sodium fast reactors, where uncertainties in
manufacturing, irradiation effects, and operating conditions can produce adverse local combinations that
trigger early failures. A probabilistic sampling approach based on the Cumulative Damage Fraction (CDF)
model is applied to quantify the likelihood of failure for each pin, determine the timing of the next failure,
and identify the affected subchannel. Following a fuel pin failure, the baseline CDAP computes pin
depressurization and the resulting fission-gas jet impingement on neighboring fuel pins. This impingement
produces localized gas blanketing and spray cooling phenomena, significantly degrading heat transfer at
the impacted cladding surface and reducing safety margins.
 The present work extends CDAP beyond those localized effects to capture fission-gas dispersal and
two-phase flow formation within coolant channels. The extended model tracks entrained gas following
pin failure and accounts for its impact on coolant mass flow rate, which decreases as two-phase pressure
drop increases under fixed core pressure boundary conditions, and heat transfer degradation, reflecting the
reduced liquid–clad contact area and altered convective flow distribution in the presence of gas.
 By extending CDAP to include both localized impingement effects and global channel degradation,
this work provides an improved framework to quantify stochastic fuel failures and evaluate propagation
potential during transient scenarios such as Unprotected Loss of Flow (ULOF) and Unprotected Transient
Overpower (UTOP) events. This enhancement improves predictive capability for fuel performance and
safety assessments of advanced sodium-cooled fast reactors.
 The original CDAP formulation was validated against separate-effect submerged jet impingement
experiments, where measured cladding temperature rises following fission-gas release into sodium coolant
were compared with CDAP predictions [4]. The present extension builds on that foundation. In addition,
ongoing Nuclear Energy University Program (NEUP) work with Oregon State University will generate
high-quality experimental data to further validate CDAP under controlled conditions, enabling improved
confidence in its application to reactor safety analysis [5].
 The remainder of this report is organized as follows: Section 2 describes the overall structure of the
extended CDAP model and provides a high-level view of how the new elements fit within the existing
framework. Section 3 presents the individual modeling components of the extension, including treatment
of fission-gas dispersal and two-phase flow effects. Section 4 discusses the implementation of the
extended CDAP model within SCM. Section 5 demonstrates the extended model through representative
test cases. Section 6 provides conclusions and discusses future validation and application activities.

2 Structure and High-Level Description of Extended CDAP Model
 The Clad Damage Propagation (CDAP) module in SCM represents the sequence from initial pin
failure to post-failure fission-gas release, channel-scale degradation, and potential propagation of
additional failures. CDAP can operate in two modes: normal mode for transient analysis, and separate-
effect test mode for validation of submerged gas jet impingement heat transfer.
 At each time step, CDAP updates plenum pressure, cladding hoop stress, wastage, temperature, and
cumulative damage fraction (CDF) for each pin in a subassembly. Using user-specified statistical inputs,
it evaluates the probability of failure for every pin. If a pin’s CDF exceeds a user-defined upper limit, that
pin fails deterministically. Otherwise, when the accumulated failure probability crosses a threshold, biased
sampling selects the failed pin and the subchannel that receives the fission-gas discharge. The overall
CDAP execution and its coupling to the SCM fuel-pin heat-transfer and flow solvers are summarized in
Figure 1.
 Figure 2 outlines post-failure model algorithm. Once post-failure is triggered, the failed pin is paired
with two neighbors most affected by the jet. Local impingement and gas blanketing reduce the effective
heat-transfer coefficient on those cladding surfaces. In parallel, discharged fission gas disperses into the
coolant channel and forms a two-phase region with lower and upper axial boundaries that migrate upward
as gas is transported. Within this region, the two-phase pressure drop increases channel hydraulic
resistance; under fixed core inlet/outlet pressure, the sustained mass flow rate decreases. The degraded
heat-transfer coefficients computed by CDAP are passed to the fuel-pin heat-transfer solver, and the
reduced inlet mass flow rate is passed to the flow solver, maintaining consistency across modules.
 Radially, the post-failure gas plume expands laterally as it rises, producing a directional, widening
footprint that blankets additional subchannels and exposes more pins to degraded cooling. To parameterize
this radial expansion, submerged mineral oil jet experiments performed at Oregon State University [5]
were analyzed and then mapped to sodium conditions using property-based scaling and dimensionless
similarity (Reynolds and Weber numbers). This provides a mechanistic basis for estimating the lateral
spread and axial growth of the two-phase envelope in liquid-metal coolant.
 These feedbacks establish a closed loop between local failure events and the system-level thermal-
hydraulic response. Elevated cladding temperatures and reduced coolant flow diminish the remaining
failure margin, increase CDF rates, and can lead to additional pin failures, enabling simulation of failure
propagation. Multiple simultaneous failures are supported and treated consistently within this framework.
 As depressurization proceeds and jets deflect or the gas source is exhausted, the lower two-phase
boundary moves upward and the channel is cleared of gas. At that point the post-failure event for the given
pin terminates, and the analysis resumes tracking subsequent failure probabilities under single-phase
conditions. Through this structure, CDAP connects localized jet impingement physics with global coolant
degradation to describe clad failure progression in sodium-cooled fast reactors.

3 Description of Individual Modeling Components of Extended CDAP
 Section 3 presents the new models added in the extended CDAP framework. Only the recent additions
are described here; models already documented in the previous report [1] are not repeated. The subsections
focus on: (i) tracking of discharged fission gas mass flow rate and the corresponding lower and upper two-
phase boundaries in a channel, (ii) calculation of coolant quality, void fraction, and friction pressure drop
multiplier, (iii) evaluation of inlet mass flow rate degradation caused by increased hydraulic resistance,
(iv) radial expansion of the fission gas plume as a function of axial location, and (v) the resulting
degradation of heat-transfer coefficients on affected fuel pins.

3.1 Tracking Fission Gas in Coolant Channel
 After a pin failure, discharged fission gas forms a two-phase region in the coolant channel. This
subsection defines how the model advances that region axially in time, from the breach elevation to the
channel exit, and how it clears after jet deflection. The present model adopts a channel-integral approach
similar in spirit to the Channel Integral (CI) method described in Ref. [6]. In the CI formulation, mass and
energy balances are integrated axially over the channel length, yielding relations in terms of inlet and
outlet fluxes. Here, a comparable strategy is applied to track fission gas transport and define effective flow
quality and void fraction, allowing for simplified treatment of two-phase flow without resolving detailed
subchannel distributions.

3.2 Two-phase Flow Quality, Void Fraction and Pressure Drop multipliers
 This section describes the treatment of two-phase flow effects in the channel model. When fission gas
is released into the coolant channel, the local mixture becomes a sodium–gas two-phase flow. To capture
its impact on pressure drop and heat transfer, the model first computes the instantaneous flow quality
based on the gas and liquid mass flow rates. The Lockhart–Martinelli (LM) framework is then applied to
evaluate the void fraction, and to derive appropriate pressure-drop multipliers that account for two-phase
frictional losses [7-8]. Their applicability to liquid-metal systems has been assessed in several sodium
studies [9-10], which found that LM-type approaches, with suitable parameter adjustments, provide an
adequate engineering approximation for sodium two-phase pressure drop and void fraction prediction.
These parameters are subsequently used in the degradation models described in Sections 3.3–3.5.

3.4 Radial Expansion of Fission Gas Plume

3.4.1 Introduction
 Following a pin breach, the discharged fission gas forms a jet that penetrates the coolant channel and
expands radially as it rises. Accurately capturing this plume expansion is essential for predicting which
subchannels become engulfed in two-phase conditions and therefore experience heat transfer degradation.
The radial spread depends not only on the breach location and momentum flux of the jet but also on
coolant properties such as viscosity and surface tension, which govern jet breakup and bubble dispersion.
In the current implementation, axial coolant velocity effects are not explicitly included in the plume
expansion model.
 To develop a predictive model, mineral oil experiments were performed at Oregon State University to
visualize and quantify gas jet expansion under controlled conditions [4]. Figure 3 shows a representative
experiment, including the raw visualization and the extracted radial distribution of bubble edges as a
function of elevation. The plume initially remains collimated near the breach but spreads laterally with
height, producing an angle-dependent expansion envelope. These measurements provide the experimental
basis for defining an effective initial radius and slope of expansion, which are then mapped to sodium
coolant conditions through scaling relations based on Reynolds and Weber similarity.

4 Extended CDAP Model Implementation in SCM
 The CDAP model has been implemented in SCM within the LiquidMetalCDAP class. In the previous
report [1], the description of CDAP focused on the base framework: biased sampling of fuel pin failures,
plenum pressure evolution, clad wastage, hoop stress, and cumulative damage fraction (CDF) tracking.
That implementation also included coupling of the post-failure jet model and fission gas blanketing to the
heat transfer and coolant flow solvers. The present work extends the CDAP framework with additional
routines that explicitly model fission-gas tracking within the coolant, inlet mass flow rate degradation,
radial expansion of the fission gas plume, and the resulting voiding and heat transfer degradation. These
extensions provide a more mechanistic representation of post-failure conditions and allow CDAP to be
used not only for probabilistic propagation studies, but also for direct coupling to subchannel-scale
thermal-hydraulics.
 Implementation of the extended CDAP is again performed in the LiquidMetalCDAP class, with the
new methods listed in Table 1. Each routine is designed to operate at every time step, in coordination with
other classes of SCM. Correspondingly, several new user inputs are required to control these extended
models, particularly those tied to radial expansion of the gas plume and its mapping from mineral oil
experiments. These are summarized in Table 2.
 In practice, these new methods are called from the Postfail_driver routine whenever a post-failure jet
is active. They operate in conjunction with the existing CDAP framework to provide fission gas deposition
into channels, to track the axial and radial evolution of two-phase regions, and to compute the feedback
on coolant flow and fuel pin heat transfer. The added inputs allow users to adapt the scaling of mineral
oil-to-sodium mapping correlations and to test sensitivities to plume growth behavior.

5 Demonstration Test Cases
5.1 Introduction
 To demonstrate the extended CDAP implementation in SCM, a series of bundle-scale transients were
simulated under conditions designed to stress cladding integrity and enable observation of post-failure
propagation. In all cases, bundle power and inlet flow were held constant, but the power-to-flow ratio was
adjusted such that the peak cladding temperature was sufficiently high to activate creep rupture or to probe
the potential for cascading failures.
 Two additional boundary conditions were imposed: (i) the plenum gas pressure was set at 10 MPa (at
800 K) to mimic high-burnup fuel behavior and increase the driving force for fission gas release, and (ii)
breach sizes were selected in the 0.5–1.0 mm range, consistent with historical furnace-test breach data
[16].
 The matrix covers both low inlet velocity (0.5 m/s) and high inlet velocity (4.0 m/s) cases. The low-
velocity simulations correspond to lower linear heat rates and weaker two-phase resistance, such that
cladding damage propagation occurs primarily at elevated peak temperatures. Conversely, the high-
velocity cases correspond to higher linear heat rates (similar to UTOP-like scenarios), where larger system
pressure drops enhance inlet mass flow rate degradation and amplify post-failure temperature excursions.

5.2   D1 – 19-Pin Bundle
 The first demonstration case corresponds to a 19-pin bundle with a nominal peak cladding temperature
(PCT) of 851 °C, an imposed 1 mm breach, and a coolant inlet velocity of 0.5 m/s. The transient was run
under constant power-to-flow conditions such that the cladding temperature remained sufficiently high to
allow evaluation of creep rupture initiation and subsequent propagation.
 The simulation results (Figure 4 through Figure 7) show a progressive increase in CDF. The peak CDF
rises continuously, reaching nearly unity by the end of the transient, while the mean CDF grows more
gradually. Stepwise increases in the number of failed pins correspond directly to new rupture events. As
each new failure occurs, the peak cladding temperature exhibits a marked increase, reflecting local heat
transfer degradation due to two-phase fission gas–coolant interaction. These spikes are transient in nature
and partially relax as the degraded coolant flow stabilizes. Figure 7 illustrates the coupling between void
fraction, normalized coolant inlet mass flow rate, and heat transfer coefficient degradation. With each pin
failure, void fraction within affected channels rises sharply, reducing the effective coolant flow and
thereby degrading surface heat transfer. These oscillatory patterns persist through successive failures,
creating alternating periods of elevated temperature and partial recovery.

5.5   D4 – 37-Pin Bundle
 Case D4 represents a 37-pin bundle operated at a coolant inlet velocity of 4.0 m/s, with a 1 mm
breach and a nominal peak cladding temperature of 820 °C. Figure 16 through Figure 19 show the

 Despite these sensitivities, the relatively low initial PCT limits the extent of propagation in this case.

failure produces sharper, more distinct spikes in peak cladding temperature, reflecting the stronger
coupling between void fraction, pressure drop, and mass flow reduction at high velocities.
 The flow degradation plots confirm this behavior: even isolated failures produce noticeable reductions
in normalized inlet mass flow rate due to the high baseline pressure drop. Heat transfer degradation follows
the same pattern, but the lower starting cladding temperature prevents these excursions from reaching
propagation thresholds.
 Overall, Case D4 shows that at high coolant velocity, individual failures have a stronger thermal-
hydraulic impact, but propagation may not occur unless the initial cladding temperature is closer to creep

6 Conclusions and Future Work
 The extended Clad Damage Propagation (CDAP) model has been developed and implemented in the
MOOSE SCM subchannel code to provide a more mechanistic representation of post-failure behavior in
metallic-fueled sodium fast reactors. Building upon the original CDAP formulation, which focused on
probabilistic fuel pin failures and localized jet impingement effects, the extension incorporates fission gas
tracking, two-phase flow modeling, plume radial expansion, and corresponding impacts on coolant flow
and cladding heat transfer.
 The extension improves the ability of CDAP to capture the closed-loop feedback between fuel failures
and thermal-hydraulic degradation. Released gas is now tracked axially and radially, creating local two-
phase regions that increase hydraulic resistance and degrade surface heat transfer. These effects feed
directly into the fuel failure probability calculations, enabling the model to simulate failure propagation
during transients such as unprotected loss of flow (ULOF) and unprotected transient overpower (UTOP).
 Validation of the extended framework remains ongoing. Previous submerged jet impingement tests
have already provided support for the baseline CDAP formulation. The new radial expansion model has
been grounded in oil experiments and mapped to sodium conditions using Reynolds- and Weber-number
scaling arguments. Additional high-quality data from the ongoing NEUP collaboration with Oregon State
University will provide the next step toward quantitative validation, particularly for plume expansion,
two-phase boundary tracking, and heat transfer degradation at off-axis and downstream locations.
 Demonstration simulations of 19-pin and 37-pin bundles have illustrated the robustness of the
extended model. Breach size was shown to be a critical parameter: small breaches (≤0.5 mm) resulted in
limited degradation, while larger breaches (~1 mm) produced strong oscillations and, under higher power-
to-flow ratios, full bundle propagation. Inlet velocity strongly influenced system response, with high-
velocity conditions creating greater hydraulic sensitivity to gas release and larger temperature surges,
especially in UTOP-like scenarios.
 Taken together, these results demonstrate that the extended CDAP model provides a more complete
framework for quantifying cladding damage propagation and evaluating propagation potential in transient
scenarios. The approach remains computationally efficient, consistent with subchannel-level analysis, yet
incorporates sufficient physics to bridge localized post-failure effects with bundle- and assembly-scale
degradation.
 Future work will focus on continued validation against experimental data, refinement of oil-to-sodium
mapping exponents for plume expansion, and assessment of model sensitivity across broader operating
and accident scenarios. Exploring better coupling strategies between subchannel and CDAP models would
possibly improve predictions and may further reduce uncertainties in predictions. These steps will increase
confidence in CDAP as a predictive tool for fuel performance and safety evaluations in advanced sodium-
cooled fast reactors.
