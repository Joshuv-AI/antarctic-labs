1. Introduction
With the closure of the Halden Boiling Water Reactor and its associated hot cell facilities, the U.S.
Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy has made significant investments to establish a domestic
test bed capable of supporting the light-water reactor (LWR) industry as well as international
stakeholders. Many of these investments have focused on enabling in-pile testing under both steady-state
and transient conditions at Idaho National Laboratory (INL). In parallel, investments at Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL) have concentrated on materials and separate effects testing, ranging from
evaluating fundamental postirradiation material properties to studying complex transient behaviors such
as loss-of-coolant scenarios and aerosol testing of high-burnup fuels.
A core requirement for supporting these efforts has been the development of ORNL’s capability to
receive commercially irradiated fuel and, more critically, to enable interlaboratory shipments between
ORNL and INL. This milestone report describes ORNL’s ability to receive commerically irradiated fuel
from utilities and demonstrates ORNL’s readinees to ship to and receive commercially irradiated fuel
from INL. Finally, the LWR test bed capabilities developed and deployed at ORNL are briefly
summarized.
2. Fuel Shipment Capabilities
2.1 Battelle Research Reactor Cask
ORNL is working to establish the capability to receive and transfer irradiated fuel segments using the
Battelle Research Reactor (BRR) cask at the Irradiated Fuels Examination Laboratory (IFEL) hot cells.
This cask is capable of shipping 30, 60, or 120 cm long irradiated LWR fuel segments. Using this cask at
IFEL requires mating the BRR cask to the dry transfer system (DTS) that has historically been used at
several university research reactor sites around the country. The DTS uses a transfer cask to move fuel
from the main IFEL hot cells to the BRR cask. This process limits the fuel segment length to about 60 cm
when transferring material in the BRR cask. The internal configuration of the cask can accommodate
twelve 60 cm segments of irradiated fuel that are loaded three segments at a time. The DTS with a BRR
cask is shown in Figure 1.

Procedures to handle the BRR cask and DTS have been developed and are ready to use. To validate the
procedures and increase personnel familiarity with the BRR and DTS, a dry-run activity with this
equipment is scheduled for October 2025. This dry run will ensure IFEL is ready to ship irradiated fuel
segments when shipments are fully approved. Some additional tooling to facilitate fuel sample handling is

2.2 NAC International Legal Weight Truck Cask
The ORNL IFEL hot cells also have experience receiving full length irradiated fuel rods in the NAC
International (NAC) Legal Weight Truck (LWT) cask. In a NAC-LWT shipment, up to 25 full-length fuel
rods can be loaded directly into the IFEL East cell. Most recently, two full-length BWR fuel rods were
received to support collaboration with Global Nuclear Fuels. Previously, a shipment of seven pressurized
water reactor fuel rods was received to support collaboration with Westinghouse. Earlier shipments
include the Sister Rod campaign material and the LWR MOX irradiation material. When the NAC-LWT
is received, the cask is lifted off the transport trailer and placed on an air pallet (Figure 2). The cask is
then maneuvered to an insertion port that connects directly to the IFEL East cell (Figure 3). A shielded
adapter is placed on the cask, and the cask is then mated to the hot cell. In-cell manipulators open the
basket inside the NAC-LWT and then unload the fuel rods (Figure 4). The long continuous length of the
IFEL East cell with the insertion port adapted to the NAC-LWT cask allows full-length rods to be
transferred and handled in the East cell without the need to subsize the fuel as it enters the hot cell. This
capability is rare among hot cells.

3. Light Water Reactor Test Bed
3.1     ADEPT-2
The ADEPT-2 system (Figure 5) is used to conduct visual exams, perform eddy current measurements of
the oxide thickness (planned future capability), obtain profilometry data with linear variable differential
transducers (LVDTs), conduct gamma spectroscopy exams, puncture the rods for fission gas release
(FGR) measurement, and precisely section the rods. This system consists of a trolley-based rod
transportation system, allowing precise axial and rotational positioning of a specimen. Moreover, the
support trolleys contain ball bearings, permitting rod motion along the trolleys without marking or
damaging the rod. Moreover, the system is extremely flexible, consisting of four instrument slots along
the instrument bay. These bays are configured with rapid-transition mating units, permitting changeout of
instrumentation and technique within a few minutes with minimal need for adjustment. This upgraded
configuration allows ADEPT-2 to be agnostic not only to instrumentation type but also to specimen type.

Figure 5. Image of the ADEPT-2 system where the various measurements are made to support industry
needs.

Updates from the original ADEPT system include a light curtain micrometer for highly precise diameter
measurements, an adapter that can host any sample up to 1 in. in diameter, and a calibration and backing
plate for visual examinations. Additionally, several upgrades are being planned, including a confocal
micrometer for local defect analysis, an upgraded eddy current system that allows for replaceable sensor
heads to prolong the life of the unit, gamma ray tomography, an air-mic system for additional
profilometry tools, and adapters to permit visual and gamma data collection on post–loss-of-coolant
accident (LOCA) test rods. Moreover, the instrument is intended for use with samples coming from the
MiniFuel experimental system, permitting in-cell collection of swelling and activity data; this capability
will improve throughput and reduce dose to personnel.
The ADEPT-2 system has been used to collect nondestructive and gas puncture examinations on two
BWR fuel rods and has been successfully used to begin data verification on pressurized water reactor fuel
rods examined using the original ADEPT system.

3.3 Destructive Examinations
3.3.1 LECO Analyzer
A LECO Model 836 ONH analyzer is located in a walk-in contamination area enclosure on the first floor
of IFEL. The instrument is housed in a second enclosure, within the surrounding walk-in enclosure, to aid
in contamination control. This system uses the inert gas fusion technique to analyze elemental oxygen,
nitrogen, and hydrogen content in a specimen. For characterization of hydrogen pickup in irradiated LWR
cladding, defueled cladding segments are placed in the graphite crucible and melted in an impulse furnace
to release the trapped hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen. The released gases are swept out of the furnace by
an inert gas carrier to a series of detectors to determine the hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen content in the
sample.
3.3.2 IFEL Microscopy Capabilities
Optical microscopy

After nondestructive examination and FGR measurement or following other testing, fuel samples are
often sectioned for microscopy using a hot-cell modified slow-speed saw. Fuel cross sections are mounted
in epoxy and polished using a hot-cell modified Allied MetPrep 3 grinder/polisher in the IFEL West cell.
An in-cell inverted optical microscope to characterize irradiated specimens is located in a separate hot cell
called Met2, which is connected to the IFEL West cell via a sample elevator. The microscope system is a
Leica DMI5000 with a DFC490 camera and a Leica CTR6500 controller. It is co-located with a Beuhler
Minimet polisher that is typically used for individual tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) particles or other
small fuel samples. The co-location of the instruments in the hot cell allows for rapid verification of

polishing quality with high-resolution imaging. Additionally, the microscope can be used to image
irradiated specimens whose doses are so high that they cannot be removed from the hot cell, such as full-
diameter fuel cross sections. The system has multiple imaging modes, including bright field, dark field,
and differential interference contrast methods, which can aid in viewing various microstructural features
like porosity, texture, and surface roughness. Samples can also be etched in the West cell and then
examined in MET2 on the optical microscope, which is often used to visualize hydrides in irradiated
zirconium alloys.
Electron microscopy: focused ion beam scanning electron microscopy

On the second floor of IFEL is a Thermo Fisher Scios 2 DualBeam focused ion beam (FIB)/scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) instrument, shown in Figure 7. The microscope is positioned on a vibration
isolation plate to reduce noise visible at high magnification due to environmental factors. The system is
equipped with the following detectors, which provide various characterization techniques for irradiated
samples:

  Retractable directional backscatter detector: shows microstructural variations with grain and
 phase contrast
  Energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) detector: provides qualitative analysis of chemical
 variations
  Electron backscatter diffraction detector: for characterizing grain structure, grain orientation,
 grain boundary character, microstructural strain, and other crystallographic information
  Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry detector: provides spatially resolved elemental

The FIB can be used to mill out lamella for scanning/transmission electron microscopy (S/TEM) analysis
or blocks for 3D feature characterization via FIB tomography in the Scios 2. A unique aspect of this
instrument is that it can handle alpha-contaminated materials and allow for the milling (sputtering away)
of those materials, providing an avenue to characterize irradiated fuels with advanced microscopy
techniques.

3.3.3 Burnup Determination
Gamma spectrometry
On the east side of the main hot cell in IFEL, where full-length fuel rods are handled and examined, a
high-purity germanium detector can be used to analyze various fission products that are present in the
rods that are on the ADEPT-2 system. In the IFEL East cell, the ADEPT-2 system positions fuel rods in
front of a collimator to enable gamma-scanning of irradiated fuel. The collimator passes through the hot-
cell wall and is sealed with a sapphire window on the cell side. The ADEPT-2 motor control is coupled
with gamma spectrometry software that collects an individual gamma spectrum from each specified
position along the rod. Several different gamma-ray detectors have been used for gamma scanning,
including NaI and LaBr3 scintillator detectors and high-purity germanium detectors. The type of detector
is determined by research needs. The spectra collected during this analysis can be used in conjunction
with the known rod average burnup to determine local burnup of specific axial locations in the rod by
comparing differences in the signal given off by different gamma-ray emitting radionuclides. Typically,
cesium-137 is used to estimate relative changes in burnup axially across a full length fuel rod.
Analysis of dissolver solutions
In addition to the nondestructive, semi-quantitative burnup analysis that can be conducted with gamma
spectrometry on ADEPT-2, a more quantitative burnup analysis can be conducted on solutions of
dissolved nuclear fuel. This process involves dissolving segments of irradiated fuel in acid in the hot cell
and then transferring those solutions to the Radioactive Materials Analytical Laboratory for analysis by
inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry. This technique can determine elemental and isotopic
concentrations in the specimen for atomic masses 7–250. Burnup is typically calculated from the actinide
and lanthanide content of dissolved fuel.

3.4 Semi-Integral Testing and Mechanical Properties
3.4.1 Severe Accident Test Station
The Severe Accident Test Station (SATS) at ORNL was originally designed to evaluate high-temperature
steam corrosion in accident-tolerant fuel (ATF) concepts and evaluate LOCA testing of irradiated fuel
segments. The application of SATS has expanded to both low- and high-burnup fuels, including the
world’s first LOCA test on commercially irradiated ATF fuel. An image of the system during an ongoing
LOCA test is shown in Figure 9. SATS has developed and integrated advanced in situ monitoring
capabilities, significantly enhancing the quality and depth of data collected during each experiment. These
capabilities have been demonstrated in a variety of complex transient fission gas release (tFGR)
experiments on high-burnup fuel. In these tests, fuel samples were either pressurized or maintained at
atmospheric conditions and heated under prototypical LOCA scenarios. More recently, SATS has
introduced a first-of-its-kind capability for performing biaxial mechanical testing at high temperatures
using digital image correlation (DIC) in hot cells. This technique enables researchers at ORNL to
precisely track cladding deformation under LOCA or creep conditions, providing critical data to advance
material model development, improve understanding of fuel behavior during accident scenarios, and
support the validation of predictive simulation codes. Finally, the out-of-cell SATS test station has
established the ability to conduct both internally and externally heated tests at pressures up to 10,000 psi,
with potential for even higher-pressure operation.

Figure 9. In-cell SATS system during operation, as evidenced by the glow emitted by the furnace.

 1. LOCA Testing: To date, ORNL has conducted 13 LOCA tests, 3 of which were performed on
 commercially irradiated ATF fuels. The data generated from these tests are actively being used by

 both the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission and industry vendors to support the extension of
 fuel burnup limits and the licensing of ATF fuels. One of the most impactful tests conducted at
 ORNL investigated the influence of spacer grids on fuel fragmentation, particularly on relocation
 effects. The results demonstrated that the spacer grids not only inhibit cladding deformation but
 also prevent fuel fragmentation. Additionally, FGR data were collected during this LOCA test, a
 first-of-its-kind achievement. The findings provided further evidence that tFGR is highly
 influenced by the ambient pressure conditions of the fuel before burst. Furthermore, the first
 LOCA test on commercially irradiated ATF material revealed a significant increase in LOCA
 burst temperatures and a decrease in ballooning strain and rupture opening compared to expected
 performance for an uncoated sample of the same zirconium alloy.
 2. tFGR: The SATS tFGR system is a unique technique for in situ tFGR detection under a variety
 of complex conditions (Figure 10). To date, four tests have been conducted, each one pushing the
 system’s capabilities to its full extent. The first tFGR test assessed tFGR under stepwise heating
 conditions in atmospheric environments. The results were compared to historical French data,
 which involved heating a sample at a rate of 0.1°C/s. The ORNL test system produced
 comparable results, validating its performance. The second test involved a similar sample, heated
 to 1,000°C at a rate consistent with prototypical LOCA conditions. The test once again yielded
 expected results, aligning with historical data. The third test was a first-of-its-kind prototypical
 LOCA test using a pressurized rodlet. The final test combined elements of the previous tests: a
 pressurized rodlet with stepwise heating. This test aimed to clearly identify both the amount of
 gas released and the temperature at which it was released. The results helped refine the
 understanding of tFGR behavior under specific thermal and pressure conditions. The testing done
 to date has fully demonstrated the system’s capabilities under a range of testing conditions, and
 the SATS tFGR system is now available for all ORNL LOCA tests.

 3. In Situ Mechanical Testing: DIC has been developed and applied to unirradiated cladding for
 various mechanical tests, including ring tensile, axial tensile, LOCA, and creep testing. Recently,
 the technique has been refined to a level where it can reliably estimate key mechanical properties
 such as yield stress and ultimate tensile stress, identifying the points at which the material
 exceeds these limits. DIC has also demonstrated the ability to measure parameters such as
 activation energy and other coefficients used in the creep equation. By enabling precise, localized
 strain measurements, DIC allows for fewer tests to achieve the same statistical confidence and
 uncertainty levels as traditional testing methods, significantly improving experimental efficiency.
 Building on this expertise, DIC has been deployed within the in-cell SATS test station to study
 the ballooning and burst behavior of ATF cladding. The objective is to better understand the
 reasons behind the smaller balloon sizes and reduced rupture openings observed in ATF cladding
 and to assess the influence of commercial irradiation on ATF performance under LOCA
 conditions. This test marked the first successful in-cell demonstration of the DIC system and will
 now serve as a foundation for future LOCA and creep testing to support postirradiation
 examination (PIE) programs at ORNL.
 4. FRIAR: The Fission Gas Release and Inspection Apparatus (FRIAR) system (Figure 11) expands
 upon the regular SATS testing capabilities by testing additional post-burst dispersal, which may
 occur due to rod vibration. The most likely source of such vibration is the quenching water
 rapidly injected into the core. A wide range of loading vibrations can be studied using the FRIAR
 system, from 0.3 to 25 mm peak-to-peak amplitudes and up to 25 Hz frequencies. Following a
 LOCA burst test, the rod is transported carefully from the SATS furnace to the FRIAR testing rig.
 The rod is then secured to the rig and loaded with the appropriate frequency and amplitude

 combination. All fuel that disperses during loading or during vibration testing is collected,
 weighed, and sieved. Resulting data are compared against dispersible fuel removed from the rod
 during posttest examination as well as against the burst-induced dispersal. To date, two in-cell
 tests and numerous out-of-cell tests have been conducted using the FRIAR system.

The Advanced Fuels Campaign–supported SATS capabilities and testing, including those highlighted
here, play a crucial role in addressing key knowledge and licensing gaps related to the performance of
ATF and high-burnup fuel during LOCA events. The resulting data are actively used by the U.S. Nuclear
Regulatory Commission and fuel suppliers to extend burnup limits beyond current conditions and to
develop next-generation ATF cladding concepts. These advancements not only enhance safety margins
but also drive significant economic benefits within the nuclear industry, aligning with and supporting the
U.S. Department of Energy’s mission.
3.4.2 Fatigue Testing
The Cyclic Integrated Reversible Bending-Fatigue Tester (CIRFT) was proposed for fatigue testing of
chromium-coated cladding in this project. The system (Figure 12) was developed for studying the
vibration integrity of SNF rods in a hot cell. The system is set up on a testing bench and uses a U-frame to
convert the linear motion into a bending moment in the rod specimen being tested. The linear motion is
supplied by the dual linear motors aligned on the bench. The U-frame is made of two rigid arms with
specially designed specimen grips. The system measures the deformation of the rod by using three
LVDTs installed in the gauge section. Relevant details of the system components, design considerations,
and calibration are given in previous publications [1-7]. The application of the system for testing a variety
of SNF rods and relevant data is discussed in additional references [8-16]. Recently, the CIRFT was
successfully used to test and characterize the fatigue response of cladding tubes with no pellets involved.
Figure 12 illustrates the arrangement of three LVDTs with respect to the bending rod; the deflections are
designated as d1, d2, and d3, and the sensor spacing is denoted as h.

3.4.3 Mechanical Testing on Commerically Irradiated Materials

Mechanical testing of commercially irradiated materials involves several key steps: sample harvesting,
defueling, machining, decontaminating, and finally removal from the hot cell for testing at the required
temperature. Samples are first identified and sectioned from a specified axial region using a slow-speed
saw. They are then transferred to the defueling station, where they are submerged in an acid bath to
remove any remaining fuel material. After defueling, samples are machined using a computer numerical
control machine—a Tormach PCNC 440 end mill with computer numerical controls modified for hot-cell
use—allowing high-precision machining of axial tensile test (ATT) and ring tensile test (RTT) specimens
from commercially irradiated cladding. The overall system is illustrated in Figure 13a. ATT specimens
are mounted inside the stage using a custom collet with expanding fingers. Once positioned, a conical-
head screw is tightened by the fourth axis motor to expand the collet fingers and securely grip the
specimen’s inner diameter. Figure 13b shows a surrogate steel specimen mounted in the ATT fixture
(without the fines collection bowl); the tool is performing a finishing pass on the first machined pocket.
RTT specimens are mounted via a custom fixture that compresses each ring along its axial length. Each
station accommodates ring lengths between 2.5 and 3.7 mm, consistent with the precision of the slow-
speed saw used for cutting. Figure 13c shows the RTT specimens mounted in the fixture, which contains
three stations, each holding one ring. All RTT stations can be machined under a single G-code program,
enhancing throughput efficiency. Rings are loaded into the fixture using dental picks or tweezers. The
RTT fixture features a V-slot between the compressing sides to maintain ring height, and a designed gap

20                                                                                        09/19/2025

between the V-slot and work-holding sides to minimize the impact of burrs left by the slow-speed saw
cuts.

Figure 13. (a) Overview of Tormach PCNC 440 in the hot cell and the work holding for (b) ATT and (c)
RTT specimens.

Once machining is complete, the samples are decontaminated and removed from the hot cell. Mechanical
testing is performed using an Instron 5966 equipped with an enclosed forced-air furnace operating at
approximately 350°C. Tests are conducted under crosshead displacement control, with crosshead speeds
of 0.2 mm/min and 0.09 mm/min, corresponding to a nominal strain rate of 1 × 10−3 s−1. Specimen
temperature is measured using calibrated Type-K thermocouples installed in accordance with ASTM
E1363. The thermocouples are tack-welded directly to each test fixture to provide accurate temperature
monitoring. The reported specimen temperature is the average of the top and bottom thermocouple
readings; the control thermocouple temperature is also recorded. Before testing, specimens are soaked at
the target temperature for a minimum of 15 min to ensure thermal equilibrium. Figure 14 shows the ATT
and RTT test fixtures mounted on the Instron 5966, including the thermocouple weld locations. For RTT
tests, mandrel diameters were measured at 7.962 mm and 7.955 mm using a Keyence VR-5000
microscope; corresponding chord heights of 3.00 mm and 2.99 mm were measured by micrometer. The
load cell was calibrated by Instron in accordance with ASTM E4-21, and the crosshead displacement
system was calibrated per ASTM E2309/E2309M-20.

3.4.4 Nanovea Hardness
The Nanovea PB100 machine (Figure 15) is capable of both micro- and nanoindentation (based on the
load cell) as well as scratch testing. The system is currently equipped with a micro-module 20 N load cell.
The indenter tips available for indentation testing include spherical conical (5, 10, 50 µm diameters), flat
top (50, 200 µm diameters), a 200 µm conical diamond, spherical diamond, and micro Vickers. The
spherical conical, conical, and spherical tips can also be used for scratch testing. Optical microscopes (5×
and 20×) can be used for imaging. For high-temperature testing, a heat stage can be used for tests up to
450°C. The system also allows researchers to determine the stress–strain curves and the yield stress of

3.4.5 Aerosol Collection System
An aerosol collection capability was developed to collect and characterize respirable aerosols (10 μm
aerodynamic equivalent diameter or less is generally considered to be respirable) released during a
mechanical fracture of a high-burnup spent fuel rod [20]. Under the scope of the project, a quasi-static four-
point bending (4PB) test was used for the fuel rod fracture and thus, the aerosol collection system discussed
herein was designed specifically for this test series.
The basic test setup for includes the load frame with its four-point bend fixture that is used to fracture the
specimen, an enclosure that limits broad dispersion of the particulate released during fracture, and the
cascade impactor with its pump to collect respirable aerosols, as shown in Figure 16. The cascade impactor
used in the project was a modified version of the commercially available Sioutas Cascade Impactor. A
detailed description of the modifications and calibration testing is given by Sasikumar et al. [21].

Figure 16. In-cell 4PB aerosol sampling setup (on the left) and an exploded view of the modified Sioutas
Cascade Impactor (on the right).

A typical test involves running the impactor pump during the 4PB test and continuing after fracture until
the sampling period is deemed sufficient (~10 min). Following the 4PB test and aerosol collection, the
cascade is moved to a glovebox and disassembled. All inner surfaces of the enclosure are wiped, and the
media sent for inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis. Likewise, the tubing is

rinsed with HNO3, and the resulting liquid is sent for ICP-MS. Figure 17 shows the beige SEM tabs from
a disassembled cascade after an aerosol collection test. The collected SNF aerosol particles are visible as
faint gray lines across the beige-colored SEM tabs. The tabs are sent for ICP-MS or SEM analysis. A dual-
sampling approach was developed with two cascades sampling from one test to allow for simultaneous
characterization where one set of cascade tabs are sent for SEM and another for ICP-MS.

A detailed description of the sampling setup, test protocols, and aerosol characterization is given by
Montgomery et al. [21]. and Sasikumar et al. [22]. Although the collection system was designed
specifically for the 4PB testing fixtures, the system can easily be integrated into any other collection
configuration with slight modifications.
3.5 High Flux Isotope Reactor MiniFuel Separate Effects Irradiation
The High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) MiniFuel platform enables separate effects irradiation testing to
assess critical performance parameters of nuclear fuel under controlled conditions [23]. By isolating fuel
performance variables such as burnup, temperature, and microstructural changes, the platform provides
detailed data to support the qualification and development of advanced nuclear fuels. These outcomes are
achieved by leveraging PIE capabilities located at IFEL.
MiniFuel involves irradiating miniature fuel specimens, typically less than 4 mm3, allowing accelerated
burnup accumulation rates beyond those achievable in commercial LWR environments [23,24].The small
size of these specimens simplifies their design and subsequent PIE, making the process efficient and cost-
effective. MiniFuel experiments primarily rely on gamma heating from structural materials rather than
direct fission within the fuel, decoupling fuel temperature from fission rate and enabling the study of
diverse fuel compositions, enrichment levels, geometries, and irradiation conditions with reduced
complexity [23,24].
MiniFuel campaigns have investigated various fuels, including standard UO2, chromia-doped UO2,
uranium nitride, and uranium silicide. Recent experiments have specifically targeted high-burnup UO2
specimens to study phenomena such as high-burnup structure formation, dark zone evolution, FGR, and
swelling. These experiments use advanced fuel performance simulation tools such as the BISON fuel
performance code and ANSYS finite element thermal modeling to control experimental conditions
accurately, including time-averaged, volume-averaged fuel temperatures and target burnups [24,25].
PIE campaigns at ORNL’s IFEL hot-cell facility currently include disassembly of irradiated capsules
(Figure 18), puncture-based gas release measurements, gamma spectrometry for burnup validation, X-ray
computed tomography, and electron microscopy (SEM and TEM). These techniques are in active use, but

the PIE workflow for MiniFuel is still under development. Throughput is currently limited due to the
early-stage implementation of handling and examination procedures tailored for sub-sized specimens.
Ongoing work aims to refine methodologies, improve reproducibility, and strengthen the validation of
measurements. Passive SiC thermometry bars are analyzed via continuous dilatometry to verify in-core
irradiation temperatures, providing benchmark data against preirradiation simulations [25,26].

Summary of PIE Capabilities at IFEL Supporting MiniFuel Campaigns:
 1. Subcapsule Disassembly and Handling: Hot-cell disassembly using low-speed saws for retrieval
 of internal components such as fuel specimens and passive SiC thermometry [25,26].
 2. FGR Measurement: Hot-cell puncture apparatus and gamma spectroscopy using charcoal traps
 for precise quantification of krypton-85 released from fuel specimens [24,25,26].
 3. SiC Passive Thermometry Analysis: Samples are transferred to the LAMDA laboratory for
 postirradiation continuous dilatometry to back-calculate irradiation temperatures of fuel
 specimens, validating simulation results [24,25].
 4. Gamma Spectrometry: High-purity germanium detectors measure isotopic fission product
 distribution, validate burnup calculations, and characterize irradiated fuel specimens and internal
        components [25,26].
 5. Mass Spectrometry: Destructive chemical analysis using ICP-MS to confirm fuel burnup and
 validate neutron transport simulation accuracy [25].
 6. X-ray Computed Tomography: Nondestructive volumetric and structural integrity assessment to
 quantify swelling and assess fuel pellet integrity post-irradiation [2][4].
 7. Optical and Electron Microscopy: Detailed grain structure, porosity, fuel kernel integrity, and
 secondary phase analyses using optical microscopy, SEM, EDS, and TEM [25,26].
 8. Thickness-Based Swelling Measurement: Precision measurement techniques (dial gauges,
 profilometry) to evaluate irradiation-induced dimensional changes in fuel disks [24,26].
