 Abstract

Predictability of wind resource conditions is critical for offshore wind design and operations.
While many studies of extreme wind conditions focus on specific events such as low-level jets or
ramps, these rely on threshold definitions that limit generality. Here we present a data-driven
framework that combines principal component analysis (PCA), self-organizing maps (SOM), and
k-means clustering to classify wind resource conditions as typical and anomalous from
climatological data. Anomalies are defined not by fixed thresholds but by flagging samples located
far from SOM node centers inside the baseline SOM structure. This reframes extremes as rare
ebents and hence, likely difficult to anticipate by numerical weather prediction models.

We applied this approach to 23 years (2000–2022) of hourly profiles from the NOW-23 hindcast
model at the Humboldt Wind Energy Area. Classification is conducted on a feature space
consisting of 10 m wind speed and direction, bulk shear and veer across 30–270 m, and a low-
level jet index. Dimensionality reduction is achieved through PC. A 2 × 3 SOM lattice trained on
the PCA vectors identified six baseline regimes spanning weak to strong flow states. High
quantization-error profiles are identified and re-clustered into four anomalous regimes.

The baseline regimes exhibited clear seasonal and diurnal cycles. Meanwhile, the anomalous
regimes represented <10 % of all hours but showed distinct combinations of speed, shear, and
veer, when compared to the baseline regimes. Anomalous regimes are typically short-lived (~few
hours), yet their transitions can lead to hub-height wind changes of −18 to +9 m s⁻¹. For a
representative 15 MW turbine, these shifts imply rapid swings in capacity factor from near-full
output to negligible generation. Validation with lidar buoy data showed 51% agreement in SOM
labels across ~6,000 overlapping hours, with most mismatches confined to adjacent speed classes.
HRRR comparisons further revealed that anomalous regimes were disproportionately associated
with forecast biases exceeding 5 m s⁻¹. Together, these results reframe extremes in offshore wind
from absolute maxima or minima to weather states that are difficult to anticipate from models.

1. Introduction

Predictability of wind conditions is central to the design and operation of offshore wind farms.
Turbines are built to withstand a wide range of atmospheric states, but operators need to know not
only what the typical wind climate looks like, but also when conditions deviate from the expected.
These deviations matter because they affect energy yield, structural loading, and maintenance
planning (Pinson, 2013; Browell et al., 2020).

Much of the research on wind resource extremes has focused on specific meteorological events.
Examples include low-wind “droughts” that limit production (Olauson, 2018), sudden ramps that
challenge grid stability (Gallego-Castillo et al., 2015), or strong low-level jets that create shear and
loading risks (Kelley et al., 2006; Nunalee and Basu, 2014). Such studies usually define extremes
with event-specific thresholds, such as jet maxima or ramp durations (Lima et al., 2018). While
this approach has improved understanding of particular events, it ties analysis to pre-defined
categories. In practice, what matters most is whether an event was foreseen by the forecast system
(Pinson, 2013; Grams et al., 2017). A severe shear episode is less disruptive if predicted than a
modest but unanticipated lull.

This motivates a different perspective. Rather than define extremes by physical thresholds, we can
frame them as conditions that are unlikely relative to the historical multivariate distribution of the
wind resource. In other words, extremes are events that lie far from the central tendency of the
climatology (Chandola et al., 2009). This perspective reframes “extremes” as unexpected
conditions, rather than simply very high or very low values. Such conditions are most likely to
challenge model skill and operational readiness.

The challenge is that climatological wind profiles form a high-dimensional dataset. Each hour of
data includes information on speed, direction, shear, veer, and other variables across heights.
Statistical learning tools can reduce this space while keeping its essential structure. Principal
component analysis (PCA) is a standard way to compress correlated variables into a smaller set of
independent modes (Jolliffe and Cadima, 2016). Self-organizing maps (SOMs) can then project
the reduced data onto a two-dimensional lattice, preserving the topology of the variable space and
grouping similar states together (Kohonen, 2001; Sheridan and Lee, 2011). Points that fall far from
the dense, “typical” clusters of samples can then be flagged as anomalous. SOM quantization error

 2

provides a quantitative measure of this distance and is widely used in environmental anomaly
studies (Vesanto and Alhoniemi, 2000; Concetti et al., 2023). A further clustering step, such as k-
means, can be used to categorize these anomalies into distinct types. By following this framework,
the problem of extremes shifts from identifying high or low values of a given metric to recognizing
when the atmosphere enters a regime that is unusual compared with the bulk climatology. This
reframes risk in a way that better reflects operational needs. The greatest concern is not necessarily
the magnitude of wind speeds, but the occurrence of conditions that are unexpected and thus less
likely to be well captured by forecasts.

In this study, we implement such a PCA–SOM–k-means framework using 23 years of the NOW-
23 hindcast dataset (Bodini et al., 2024) over the U.S. West Coast. To illustrate the method, we
focus on the ORACLE buoy (Krishnamurthy et al., 2023) location at the Humboldt Wind Energy
Area (WEA). This buoy record provides an independent, though limited, dataset for validation.
We compare the SOM-based regime labels derived from NOW-23 to those assigned to the buoy
profiles over their common period of operation.

The rest of this report is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the data and methods, including
preprocessing, PCA variance retention, SOM sensitivity, and clustering. Section 3 presents the
results: composites of typical and anomalous regimes, their seasonal and diurnal occurrence,
persistence and transitions, and a comparison of hindcast and buoy labels. Section 4 summarizes
findings and discusses implications for offshore wind resource assessment and operational risk.

2. Data and Methods

The analysis focuses on the U.S. West Coast offshore region, with a case study at the Humboldt
Bay Wind Energy Area (WEA). Humboldt lies off northern California, an area of strong coastal
upwelling and frequent marine boundary-layer jets (Dorman, 1985; Zhang et al., 2016). It has been
identified as a prime location for floating offshore wind development, though it also presents
complex meteorological and oceanographic conditions (Musial et al., 2020; Doubrawa et al., 2025;
Mitra et al., 2025a). To support pre-lease characterization, DOE, BOEM, and PNNL deployed
research buoys in California waters, including at Humboldt (Sheridan et al., 2024; Krishnamurthy
et al., 2023). The Humboldt Bay WEA has recently been the focus of resource assessments and
observational campaigns (Mitra et al., 2025a; Mitra et al., 2025b). The central buoy deployment

coordinates define the reference location for both the buoy observations and the NOW-23 profiles

2.1 Datasets

2.1.1 NOW-23

The National Offshore Wind dataset 2023 (NOW-23) is a 23-year hindcast constructed from the
Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model, dynamically downscaled from the ERA5
reanalysis (Hersbach et al., 2020). The dataset spans 2000–2022 at hourly resolution and covers
all U.S. offshore planning areas (Bodini et al., 2024). WRF was run with parameterizations tuned
for marine boundary-layer representation and validated against coastal and buoy observations.

For this study, we use the NOW-23 vertical wind profiles at the Humboldt buoy location. Profiles
extend from the surface to 500 m above sea level at ~20–50 m vertical spacing. Variables include
wind speed and wind direction, decomposed into zonal (u) and meridional (v) components for the
machine-learning framework. Derived features such as bulk shear, directional veer, low-level jet

 4

indices, and variance are calculated from these profiles (see Section 2.2.1). The full 23-year record
provides the climatological baseline from which both typical and anomalous regimes are learned.

2.1.2 Lidar Buoys

To provide an observational reference, we use data from two DOE/PNNL floating lidar buoys
deployed in California beginning in 2020, one of which was moored at the Humboldt WEA
(Krishnamurthy et al., 2023; Sheridan et al., 2024). Each buoy carried a ZX300M scanning
Doppler lidar, met-ocean sensors, and wave instrumentation. The lidars retrieved wind profiles
from 10 m up to 250 m above sea level in 20 m increments, at 10-minute resolution.

For comparison with NOW-23, buoy profiles are averaged to hourly means and expressed as u and
v components. The overlap period with NOW-23 is July 2020 to December 2021 (with January-
July 2021 missing), yielding about one year of data. This record allows for a limited validation of
SOM regime labels, though its duration is short compared to the full NOW-23 climatology.

2.2 Methods

Our workflow was designed to identify typical and anomalous offshore wind profile conditions
without assuming in advance the nature of anomalous wind resource conditions. The approach
consists of three main stages:

 i. feature engineering of hourly profiles,
  ii. dimensionality reduction with principal component analysis (PCA), and
 iii. regime mapping with a self-organizing map (SOM), followed by anomaly detection and
 clustering of the anomalous subset.

All analyses were performed at the NOW-23 grid cell coincident with the ORACLE buoy location
in the Humboldt WEA (Mitra et al., 2025a, 2025b).

2.2.1 Feature engineering.

Hourly vertical wind profiles from NOW-23 (2000–2022) were extracted from the grid point
coinciding with the Humboldt buoy location (49.5° N, 124.6° W). Each profile provided wind
speed and wind direction from the surface to 500 m at ~20–50 m vertical resolution depending on

 5

height (Bodini et al., 2024). To match buoy observations, only levels within 0–270 m were used
to construct the input vectors to the unsupervised learning workflow and its later diagnostics.

For each hour, an input vector was prepared for unsupervised learning consisting of:

 i. 10 m wind speed (m s⁻¹).
  ii. 10 m wind direction (degrees).
 iii. Bulk shear (m s⁻¹), defined as the wind-speed difference between 30 and 270 m, to capture
 vertical velocity gradients relevant to typical turbine rotor layers.
 iv. Directional veer (degrees), defined as net rotation of wind vector across the same layer.
  v. Low-level jet (LLJ) index (m s⁻¹), defined as the difference between the hourly value of
 wind at 200 m and the monthly climatological mean wind speed at 200 m.

This compact set of features was chosen to represent near-surface forcing, vertical structure, and
jet occurrence, all of which are known to strongly affect wind-energy production and forecasting
skill (Nunalee and Basu, 2014; Svensson et al., 2019; Kalverla et al., 2019).

2.2.2 Principal component analysis (PCA)

All features were standardized to zero mean and unit variance, and PCA was applied to reduce
redundancy and compress the feature space (Jolliffe and Cadima, 2016). Components were
retained until the cumulative variance explained reached 90%. In practice, this resulted in four
principal components (PC1–PC4), which together accounted for 90.3 % of the total variance (PC1:
28.5 %, PC2: 24.7 %, PC3: 20.2 %, PC4: 16.9 %).

Inspection of the loading vectors shows that each PC is influenced by a different aspect of the wind
profile (Fig. 2). PC1 is shaped primarily by the LLJ index, PC2 by directional veer, PC3 by 10 m
wind direction, and PC4 by bulk shear. Importantly, no single variable dominates across all PCs;
rather, each component reflects a distinct physical dimension of variability. This ensures that the
reduced feature space retains information about near-surface conditions, vertical shear and veer,
and jet signatures in balanced form. Each hourly profile was thereafter represented in terms of its
four PCA scores, which served as the input to the SOM.

2.2.3 Self-Organizing Map (SOM)

The PCA score vectors were then used to train a self-organizing map (SOM) to identify baseline
(“typical”) wind-profile regimes (Kohonen, 2001). A SOM projects high-dimensional data onto a
low-dimensional lattice of nodes, each representing a prototype vector. Profiles that are close in
PCA space map to nearby nodes, while profiles that differ substantially map to more distant nodes.
Each sample is assigned to its best-matching unit (BMU), which is simply the prototype node that
most closely resembles that sample. In practical terms, the BMU can be thought of as the “closest
archetype” among the available regimes, providing a qualitative sense of which broad regime each
hourly profile most resembles.

We tested a range of SOM lattice sizes, from 2 × 2 up to 6 × 6, and evaluated each configuration
using two metrics: the total variance explained by the regime composites and the fraction of the
dataset accounted for by the two most common regimes (Fig. 3). Performance across the different
shapes was broadly similar, with the 2 × 3 map providing one of the most interpretable solutions.

 7

Although it was not uniquely optimal in the sensitivity tests, the 2 × 3 configuration was
retroactively selected for this analysis because it strikes a reasonable balance: it explains ~13.2 %
of the variance in the profiles while the two dominant regimes capture ~48–49 % of all hours,
ensuring parsimony without losing coverage.

Figure 3: Sensitivity of self-organizing map (SOM) performance to lattice size, expressed as (a) total
variance in wind profiles explained by the regime composites and (b) the fraction of hours accounted for
by the two most common regimes.

Final SOM training was performed on the four-dimensional PCA score vectors. The map was
initialized with prototype vectors drawn from a uniform distribution spanning the PCA ranges.
Training ran for 1000 iterations, during which the learning rate decayed linearly from 0.5 to near
zero and the neighborhood radius contracted from 1.5 lattice units to 0.5. At each iteration, an
hourly profile was selected, its BMU identified by Euclidean distance in PCA space, and the BMU
and its neighbors adjusted incrementally toward the sample. This process allows the SOM to
approximate the structure of the dataset while preserving topological relationships.

The final 2 × 3 lattice yielded six prototype SOM nodes, each representing an baseline wind-profile
regime derived from the NOW-23 record. Profiles assigned to a given node can be interpreted as
hours when the wind field most closely resembled that regime. Collectively, these six baseline
regimes capture the range of typical variability at the Humboldt site and provide a reference
framework against which anomalous conditions can be defined. Henceforth, these archetypal SOM
nodes are referenced as “typical” regimes for the rest of the study.

 8

2.2.4 Anomaly detection through k-means Clustering

In addition to the six typical regimes identified by the SOM, we sought to isolate cases that are
poorly represented by those regimes. For this purpose, we used quantization error (QE), which is
computed for every profile as the Euclidean distance in PCA space between the sample and the
prototype vector of its assigned SOM node. The QE provides a quantitative measure of how well
a profile “fits” into its cluster: lower values indicate a close match to the baseline, while higher
values indicate a poorer fit.

To identify anomalous conditions, we examined the QE distribution within each of the six SOM
regimes. Profiles with QE values above the 98th percentile of their cluster-specific distribution
were flagged as outliers. In practical terms, these are hours that had been provisionally assigned to
a regime but differ enough from the cluster prototype to be considered atypical. This approach
avoids bias toward regimes with inherently higher or lower QE and ensures that anomalies are
detected relative to the variability of each cluster.

All profiles flagged as anomalous were then pooled together across the six baseline regimes. This
combined set was subjected to a second round of clustering using k-means, applied directly to the
PCA score vectors of the anomalous samples. To determine the appropriate number of anomalous
groups, we examined solutions with k ranging from 2 to 6. Based on visual inspection of cluster
separation and interpretability, k = 4 was selected as the most parsimonious choice.

The result is a set of four “anomalous regimes”, representing qualitatively distinct types of outlier
wind profiles. These regimes capture conditions that the baseline SOM cannot represent well -
profiles that are atypical compared to the dominant climatological states. Together, the six SOM
baseline regimes and the four anomalous (ANOM) regimes provide a ten-category classification
framework that can be used to study both the typical structure and the rare extremes of the offshore
wind resource.

2.2.5 Post-processing & Validation

After classification into six baseline SOM regimes and four anomalous regimes, the sequence of
hourly labels was further analyzed to quantify temporal patterns and impacts relevant to wind-

 9

energy operations. These post-processing steps provide context for how often regimes occur, how
long they persist, and what changes are associated with transitions into anomalous states. We study
the seasonal and diurnal occurrence, and persistence of the regimes and analyze the frequency and
nature of regime transitions. For every regime and every regime-pair transition, we quantified the
associated change in hub-height conditions. Specifically, we extracted the difference in wind speed
at 150 m, chosen as a representative turbine hub height. These wind-speed changes were then
mapped through a 15 MW reference turbine power curve to estimate the corresponding relative
change in power production. This allowed transitions to be interpreted not only in terms of
meteorological regime shifts but also in terms of their likely impact on wind energy generation.

To evaluate the labels derived from the classification of hourly NOW-23 data, the same PCA–
SOM clustering framework was applied to lidar profiles from the ORACLE buoy during its
deployment period. The buoy operated from July 2020 to December 2021 (missing months from
January-July 2021), yielding nearly 10321 hourly profiles over the deployment duration (Sheridan
et al., 2022). Profiles were standardized with the same means and variances used for NOW-23 to
ensure comparability. Buoy profiles were then assigned regime labels based on their best-matching
units in the SOM trained on NOW-23. A confusion matrix between NOW-23 and buoy labels was
constructed to assess agreement. This validation provides an independent measure of how well the
hindcast-based classification captures observed variability at the Humboldt site.

3. Results

The PCA–SOM framework applied to 23 years of NOW-23 profiles at the Humboldt site yields a
ten-state classification of offshore wind conditions: six baseline regimes that represent the typical
climatological variability, and four additional regimes that capture anomalous, poorly represented
profiles. In Section 3.1, we describe the vertical structure and seasonal–diurnal occurrence of the
six baseline regimes. In Section 3.2, we turn to the anomalous regimes, which are defined by their
high quantization errors and re-clustering into four distinct groups. Finally, we present post-
processing diagnostics that examine regime persistence, transition probabilities, and the power
implications of transitions in Section 3.3, followed by validation against buoy observations in
Section 3.4.

3.1 Typical Regimes

The six SOM baseline regimes derived from the 2 × 3 map capture a progression from weak to
strong wind conditions, with additional differentiation provided by vertical and directional
structure (Fig. 4). Within each speed class, the paired nodes exhibit similar wind-speed magnitudes
but differ systematically in directional profiles. For example, the two high-speed regimes both
show mean speeds exceeding 12 m s⁻¹ near 250 m, yet one regime exhibits a veering profile with
height, while the other maintains nearly uniform westerly flow. Similarly, the medium-speed pair
reaches 8-10 m s⁻¹ at 250 m but diverges in directionality, with one regime showing strong veer
and the other more directional consistency. Even in the low-speed group, where near-surface winds
average only < 5 m s⁻¹, one regime shows a northerly orientation and the other a more southerly
component. This demonstrates that the SOM is partitioning not just on speed intensity but also on
the vertical evolution of wind direction, which is critical for turbine loading and wake interactions.

The seasonal and diurnal occurrence patterns (Fig. 5) further distinguish the regimes. Low-speed
regimes dominate in winter, particularly during nocturnal and early morning hours, reflecting the
prevalence of stable, weakly forced conditions. Medium-speed regimes peak in spring and autumn,
occurring throughout the day but with elevated frequencies in the morning and evening transitions.
The high-speed regimes are most common in summer, with maximum occurrence during afternoon
and early evening, consistent with enhanced diurnal mixing and strong coastal winds.
Quantitatively, low-speed regimes account for ~35–40 % of all hours, medium-speed regimes for
~20–25 %, and high-speed regimes for ~25–30 %. Together, these patterns show that the baseline
SOM classification captures both the intensity spectrum of winds and their seasonal/diurnal
organization, providing a climatological framework against which anomalous events can be
contrasted.

The medium-speed regimes, SOM (0,1) and SOM (1,1), show mean speeds of ~7.3 m s⁻¹ below

conditions, one tends to be more directionally consistent, while the other exhibits greater
variability in vertical turning.

The low-speed regimes, SOM (0,2) and SOM (1,2), are the weakest overall, with mean speeds of
3–4 m s⁻¹ below 100 m, minimal bulk shear (<1 m s⁻¹), and negligible LLJ signals (<1 m s⁻¹). Their
veer is small on average but highly variable (standard deviations exceeding 40–50°), indicating
that these regimes correspond to weak, often disorganized flow patterns. This aligns with their
wintertime prevalence and nocturnal dominance, when stratification and weak forcing favor
sluggish, variable winds.

Across all six regimes, the speed variance and skewness metrics further highlight contrasts: high-
speed regimes show the largest variance (σ² ~6 m² s-2), reflecting strong temporal fluctuations,
while low-speed regimes exhibit both lower variance (<1 m² s-2), consistent with symmetric but
weak flows. These diagnostic metrics confirm that the SOM has not only separated regimes by
wind-speed class but also preserved differences in jet strength, shear, and directional structure—
features with direct implications for turbine performance, wake dynamics, and forecasting skill.

3.2 Anomalous Regimes

The anomalous regimes differ sharply from the six SOM baseline regimes in both intensity and
directional structure (Fig. 6). The low-speed anomalies (A0, A2) exhibit weak mean winds below
100 m (~3–5 m s⁻¹) and minimal bulk shear but are separated by their vertical turning. A0 shows
modest clockwise veer with height, while A2 exhibits larger directional variability, with profiles
rotating counterclockwise in the mean.

The high-speed anomalies (A1, A3) represent rare but intense events. Both reach >15 m s⁻¹ at 200–
250 m, with strong shear through the rotor layer. A1 maintains a nearly uniform westerly
orientation with modest veer, whereas A3 shows pronounced directional rotation with height,
indicating dynamically complex flow. These contrasts show that anomalous clusters are not simply
speed extremes but combinations of intensity and directional structure that fall outside the range
captured by baseline regimes.

Seasonal and diurnal occurrence patterns (Fig. 7) highlight the distinctiveness of these states. Low-
speed anomalies are most frequent in winter and early spring, often during nocturnal or early-
morning hours, consistent with stable conditions and weak synoptic forcing. High-speed anomalies
occur primarily in summer and autumn, peaking in afternoon to evening hours when coastal
pressure gradients are strongest. Although each cluster accounts for only a small fraction of total
hours (<5 % individually), their directional and temporal separation emphasizes that anomalies
represent specific, rare flow situations rather than random noise around the baseline regimes.

The feature diagnostics in Table 2 confirm these distinctions. The low-speed anomalies (A0, A2)
exhibit near-surface winds below 6 m s⁻¹, negligible LLJ signals, and bulk shear of less than 4 m
s⁻¹. Their veer statistics, however, are extreme: both clusters rotate by more than 150° on average,
with very large variability across events. In contrast, the high-speed anomalies (A1, A3) are among
the most energetic profiles in the dataset, with mean near-surface winds of 10–23 m s⁻¹ and bulk
shear exceeding 8 m s⁻¹. A1 is associated with exceptionally strong LLJ signatures (LLJ index
~19.5 m s⁻¹), while A3 couples moderate jet strength (~7 m s⁻¹) with pronounced directional veer
of ~109°. These quantitative metrics emphasize that anomalies are defined as much by vertical
structure as by magnitude, and hence, they lie outside the variability captured by the baseline SOM
regimes.
Together, the four anomalous regimes account for less than 10 % of all hours but encompass some
of the most operationally challenging states, ranging from weak, highly veering flows to intense,
strongly sheared jets. Their distinct profile structures, seasonality, and rarity highlight why they
are important to isolate in the climatology. In the following sections, we examine their persistence,
transitions, and associated changes in hub-height wind speed and turbine power to assess the
potential risks these anomalous states pose for offshore wind operations.

3.3 Regime Characterization & Transitions

The persistence distributions (Fig. 8) show that anomalous regimes are generally short-lived, in
contrast to some of the more persistent SOM baseline regimes. A0 and A3 occur frequently, with
more than 800 and 1000 episodes respectively, but their typical durations are short: the majority
of episodes last only 1–3 h. A1 also appears in over 1000 episodes, but with a broader tail extending
to ~12 h, reflecting its nature as a strong jet regime that can persist through part of a diurnal cycle.

 16

A2 is the rarest anomalous regime, with only ~120 episodes, but individual events can extend to
20–25 h, consistent with the very weak-flow, highly rotated profiles highlighted in Section 3.2.
Together, these persistence patterns reinforce the earlier findings: the high-speed anomalies (A1,
A3) are frequent but short-lived, while the low-speed anomalies (A0, A2) are less common and
A2 in particular can occasionally lock in for longer periods.

episode durations. Each panel shows the number of episodes (y-axis, log scale) as a function of duration in
hours. Sample counts per cluster are shown in the titles. Only events which lasted for 3 consecutive hours
were considered, so number of samples are different from Figures 6 and 7.

The transition matrix (Fig. 9) provides additional insight into how anomalous regimes connect to
the climatological baseline. A0 transitions most often to and from SOM 0, the most weak-flow
baseline node, consistent with its profile characteristics of low speeds and modest shear. A1 is
most strongly connected to SOM 5, a high-speed baseline regime, confirming that it represents an
extreme extension of strong-flow states with amplified jet strength. A2 transitions primarily from
SOM 2, a medium-flow, moderately sheared baseline regime; the strong veer and weak near-
surface flow of A2 highlight how a subset of those SOM 2 conditions depart far enough to register
as anomalous. A3 connects most frequently with SOM 0, despite being a high-speed anomaly; this

 17

indicates that strong counterclockwise rotation with height can transform otherwise moderate-flow
baseline states into anomalous structures.

Figure 9: Transition counts between SOM baseline regimes (SOM 0–5) and anomalous regimes (ANOM0–
ANOM3), shown as a heatmap of hourly transitions. Color scale is logarithmic. Rows indicate the
originating regime, columns the subsequent regime.

These transition pathways show that anomalous regimes are not isolated random occurrences but
arise from well-defined neighborhoods of the baseline climatology. Their distinct persistence and
transition signatures strengthen the interpretation from Section 3.2: anomalies are not random
outliers, but rare states emerging at the margins of common regimes, carrying implications for
both short-term predictability and turbine loading.

Transitions between baseline SOM regimes (Fig. 10) typically involve modest changes in hub-
height winds (±2 m s⁻¹). In contrast, anomalous regimes drive the largest shifts. Low-speed
anomalies (A0, A2) are marked by sharp decelerations: entering A0 reduces wind speeds by up to
9 m s⁻¹, while transitions into A2 can exceed 18 m s⁻¹, the strongest signal in the matrix. High-
speed anomalies (A1, A3) show the opposite tendency. A1 is linked to moderate accelerations of
~1–2 m s⁻¹, whereas A3 produces abrupt increases of 7–9 m s⁻¹ when entered from SOM 0 or SOM
5. These patterns confirm that although anomalies are rare, they dominate the most pronounced
hub-height wind changes, reinforcing their role as critical markers of unexpected variability in the
offshore wind climatology.

 18

Figure 10: Mean changes in 150 m wind speed associated with regime-to-regime transitions. Positive
values (red) indicate acceleration, negative values (blue) indicate deceleration.

For a representative 15 MW offshore turbine, such shifts translate into sizable capacity factor
impacts. A drop of 9–18 m s⁻¹ when entering low-speed anomalies (A0, A2) corresponds to a
reduction of 40–70 % of rated output, effectively driving the turbine from near full production to
partial or near-zero operation within a single regime transition. Conversely, accelerations of 7–9
m s⁻¹ into high-speed anomalies (A3) can raise production from sub-rated to near-rated levels in
less than an hour, producing equally abrupt increases in capacity factor.

3.4 Validation of SOM labels against Lidar Buoy

The buoy data provide an independent reference to evaluate the fidelity of the NOW-23 regime
classification (Fig. 11). Across the 11,765 overlapping hours, SOM labels from the model and
buoy agree just over half the time (5,977 hours, 50.8 %). This moderate agreement reflects both
the inherent differences between modeled and observed profiles and the challenge of reproducing
detailed directional and vertical structure. Much of the disagreement is concentrated in the
September period, especially between 08–18 UTC, when coastal transition dynamics are strong
and small model biases can shift a profile across regime boundaries. Outside of these hours,
agreement rates generally exceed 50–60 %, with several months showing considerably higher

 19

alignment. This indicates that while regime misclassifications do occur, particularly in certain
seasons and times, the SOM framework applied to NOW-23 captures the bulk of the buoy-
observed climatological structure and provides a consistent basis for identifying anomalous events.

Figure 11: Mean changes in 150 m wind speed associated with regime-to-regime transitions. Positive
values (red) indicate acceleration, negative values (blue) indicate deceleration. The comparison covers
11,765 overlapping hourly profiles.

Most mismatches arise from confusion between neighboring speed classes. For example, buoy-
identified low-speed regimes are often labeled by NOW-23 as medium-speed nodes, and medium-
speed buoy regimes are sometimes mapped into high-speed nodes. A smaller fraction of
mismatches involves the anomalous clusters, typically when buoy profiles show strong shear or
directional veer that NOW-23 does not reproduce with the same magnitude. In other words, the
disagreements mainly reflect differences in wind speed intensity and vertical turning between the
modeled and observed profiles, rather than a complete misrepresentation of the flow.

4. Summary & Discussion

This study developed a data-driven framework that combines principal component analysis (PCA),
self-organizing maps (SOM), and secondary clustering to identify typical and anomalous offshore
wind-profile regimes. Unlike approaches that rely on fixed thresholds for specific events such as
jets or ramps, the method defines anomalies as profiles that are poorly represented within the
climatological baseline states. Applied to 23 years of NOW-23 hindcast profiles at the Humboldt

 20

WEA, the analysis produced six baseline regimes capturing the dominant variability in speed and
direction, and four anomalous regimes defined by large quantization error. The baseline regimes
exhibited well-organized seasonal and diurnal cycles, while the anomalous regimes corresponded
to rare states marked by unusual combinations of speed, shear, and veer.

Persistence and transition diagnostics showed that anomalous regimes are generally short-lived yet
associated with the sharpest shifts in hub-height winds. Transitions into low-speed anomalies
produced drops of up to 18 m s⁻¹ at 150 m, while transitions into high-speed anomalies yielded
accelerations exceeding 9 m s⁻¹. For a representative 15 MW turbine, such transitions imply rapid
swings in capacity factor from near full production to partial or negligible output, or vice versa.
Thus, the operational importance of anomalies lies not in their overall frequency of occurrence
(<10 % of hours), but in their capacity to drive abrupt, unexpected changes in turbine inflow.

Validation against buoy lidar observations supports the robustness of the classification. Across
11,765 overlapping hours, NOW-23 and buoy-based SOM labels agreed in ~51% of cases, with
most disagreements confined to adjacent speed classes. Discrepancies involving anomalous
regimes occurred mainly when NOW-23 underestimated shear or veer relative to observations.
Outside of September daytime hours, agreement typically exceeded 50–60%, indicating that the
SOM-based framework applied to NOW-23 is broadly consistent with observed climatological
structure while still reflecting known model–observation differences in vertical gradients.

The relationship between anomalous regimes and forecast skill was further examined with the the
Hourly Rapid Refresh (HRRR) model 80 m winds, comparing 0 h analyses with 12 h forecasts
(Fig. 12). Baseline SOM regimes showed narrow error distributions centered near zero, with
interquartile ranges of 3–5 m s⁻¹. By contrast, the anomalous regimes exhibited broader spreads
and systematic biases. ANOM1 and ANOM2 were consistently underestimated, while ANOM3
showed a median positive bias of more than 5 m s⁻¹ and tails extending beyond +15 m s⁻¹. These
results confirm the central premise of this study: anomalous regimes are rare in the climatology
but disproportionately associated with reduced forecast fidelity, amplifying their operational risk.

Taken together, the results reframe the concept of extremes in offshore wind resource assessment.
Rather than focusing on absolute maxima or minima, the framework highlights anomalies as states
that deviate from the climatological baseline and are thus difficult to anticipate. By coupling
dimensionality reduction with topology-preserving clustering, the approach offers a systematic
way to isolate such states directly from long-term datasets. Future extensions should expand the
framework across multiple WEAs, integrate additional physical drivers such as boundary-layer
stability and synoptic circulation, and test performance using higher-resolution reanalyses and
next-generation AI weather models. Such efforts would enable development of a broader
climatological atlas of anomalous regimes, linking them more directly to forecast uncertainty and
operational risk in offshore wind energy.
