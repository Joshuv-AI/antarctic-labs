Urothelial Carcinoma With Pericardial Involvement: Report of Two Rare Cases and Literature Review
Authors: Cláudia Agostinho, Ana Sofia Patrão, Filipa Carneiro, Catarina Castelo Branco, Harleen Khatra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrothelial carcinoma (UC) is the ninth most diagnosed cancer worldwide, and pericardial dissemination is uncommon. In this case report, we describe the case of a patient in a complete response after chemotherapy with cisplatin/gemcitabine and maintenance avelumab that presented with cardiac tamponade, posteriorly confirmed to be caused by pericardial involvement by UC, and the case of a patient with UC with peritoneal metastasis at diagnosis under carboplatin/gemcitabine treatment, whose disease progressed with pericardial metastasis. The clinical documentation of pericardial metastasis in living patients is rare, and the management of this condition is still not well defined. Therefore, the description of these cases is important to raise awareness of pericardial involvement as a possible manifestation of UC.Keywords: cardiac tamponade, metastatic urothelial carcinoma, pericardial metastasis, urothelial carcinoma

1. Introduction

Urothelial carcinoma (UC) is the ninth most diagnosed cancer worldwide [1]. The most frequent sites for metastasis in UC are lymph nodes, bone, lung, and liver [2].

In general, pericardial malignant involvement and malignant pericardial effusion are often asymptomatic, most of the time only identified postmortem [3], and their true prevalence is uncertain. Large autopsy studies have shown cardiac involvement by tumors in 2%–18% (the pericardium being the most frequent location) [4–8]. They may arise through direct extension of the primary tumor, via hematogenous or lymphatic dissemination [3, 9, 10]. With regard to cardiac metastasis, the most frequently reported primary malignancies include lung cancer, lymphoma, breast cancer, leukemia, gastric cancer, melanoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, and colorectal cancer [8–11]. UC accounts for only approximately 1.8% of the primary tumors associated with cardiac involvement [11].

Additionally, there are no data on the frequency of pericardial involvement in patients with metastatic UC.

2. Clinical Case 1

We report the case of a man in his late 50s, with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG‐PS) of 1, with a medical history of obesity, hypertension, and smoking (50 pack‐years), currently medicated with telmisartan, diagnosed with Stage IV (cTxN3M1a) UC in July 2022 (supra‐ and infradiaphragmatic lymph node involvement).

He started first‐line palliative treatment with cisplatin 70 mg/m2 (Day 1) and gemcitabine 1000 mg/m2 (Days 1, 8, and 15) every 28 days in August 2022, with a complete response after six cycles, and then underwent maintenance avelumab (800 mg every 14 days) that began in January 2023, without significant adverse events.

In March 2023, he was admitted to the emergency department with progressive worsening dyspnea and dry cough. He was tachycardic with no other abnormalities on physical examination. Laboratory evaluation revealed nonoliguric acute kidney injury with proteinuria on urinalysis. Computed tomography (CT) scan of the thorax showed significant pericardial and bilateral pleural effusions, without pneumonitis (Figure 1).

CT scan of the thorax showing pericardial effusion (thick arrow) and bilateral pleural effusion (thin arrows).

At that time, given that the patient was receiving avelumab, immune‐mediated pericarditis was considered the most likely diagnosis, and methylprednisolone at a dose of 1 mg/kg/day was initiated.

An echocardiogram was performed and showed a large‐volume pericardial effusion and the presence of pericardial metastasis (Figure 2).

Echocardiogram showing a large‐volume pericardial effusion (star) and the presence of pericardial metastasis (thick arrow).

The patient rapidly developed cardiac tamponade with obstructive shock, requiring a pleuropericardial window, which drained 800 mL of dark blood. During the procedure, a thickened and constrictive pericardium was observed. The histopathological analysis of the pericardial fluid cytology and pericardial biopsy was compatible with pericardial dissemination of UC.

He was admitted to the intensive care unit for vasopressor support and monitoring. Renal function improved after fluidotherapy, but the patient developed signs of malperfusion of the left lower limb. CT angiography demonstrated findings consistent with arterial embolism. The abrupt clinical onset further supported an embolic etiology, although the precise source remained unconfirmed. Given residual diagnostic uncertainty regarding a potential thrombotic component, antiplatelet therapy with aspirin 100 mg once daily was initiated in addition to therapeutic anticoagulation with enoxaparin 80 mg twice daily.

The patient’s condition deteriorated, with lividity of the left leg and persistent pain, requiring transfemoral amputation. Following lower limb amputation, the patient showed favorable clinical evolution, with full recovery of functional capacity and independent mobility, and was discharged home. In view of the patient’s current clinical status, second‐line therapy was not pursued and symptomatic management was preferred.

Four months later, in August 2023, he was readmitted with psychomotor slowing and reduced strength in the right upper limb that was found to be due to brain metastasis. The patient was then submitted to whole‐brain radiotherapy (five fractions of 4 Gy), but his clinical condition deteriorated further, and he passed away in November 2023. 3. Clinical Case 2

We present the case of a man in his late 60s, with an ECOG‐PS of 1, diagnosed with Stage IV UC (cT2N+M1) with lymph node and peritoneal involvement in February 2023. He had a medical history of chronic renal disease Stage 3b, bilateral nephrostomies due to obstructive nephropathy, Type 2 diabetes mellitus under gliclazide, and former smoking (50 pack‐years).

He began first‐line palliative chemotherapy with carboplatin AUC 4 (Day 1) and gemcitabine 1000 mg/m2 (Days 1, 8, and 15) every 28 days in April 2023, without significant adverse events, with stable disease on CT scan in August 2023 (after C5). Chemotherapy was completed in September 2023, and maintenance avelumab was proposed and scheduled to start in October 2023. The clinical course was complicated by progressively worsening asthenia, precordial chest pain in the left hemithorax, and generalized nonspecific abdominal discomfort. The patient was admitted to the emergency department, and the electrocardiogram at admission showed low‐voltage tachycardia (Figure 4).

Electrocardiogram showing low‐voltage tachycardia (133 bpm).

An echocardiogram was performed, documenting a large circumferential pericardial effusion with partial collapse of the right chambers and fibrin deposition (Figure 5).

Echocardiogram showing a large circumferential pericardial effusion (star) with fibrin deposition (thin arrows).

Pericardiocentesis was performed with evacuation of 1000 cc of serosanguineous content and placement of a pericardial drain. The cytology analysis confirmed UC pericardial carcinomatosis. Reassessment CT scan at this point showed generalized serous disease involvement, with new bilateral pleural effusions and worsening ascites, with heterogeneous densification of the peritoneal layers.

Second‐line palliative treatment with pembrolizumab was proposed, but the patient did not start the treatment because he was hospitalized due to an arrhythmic event and his condition progressively worsened with an ECOG‐PS of 3. He was readmitted 3 weeks later with anasarca and subocclusive syndrome in the context of peritoneal carcinomatosis and ultimately passed away in December 2023. 4. Discussion

UC is the ninth most diagnosed cancer worldwide, presenting with metastasis at diagnosis in ≈6% of cases [1, 12].

Patients with cardiac metastasis are often asymptomatic and are primarily noted on postmortem examination [3]. Symptoms of other organ metastases are usually more prevalent by the time metastasis occurs to the heart, and for this reason, the latter often remains undiagnosed [13].

Pericardial involvement by UC is rare, with few cases reported and only six cases with cardiac tamponade described in the literature [14–20].

There is no established diagnostic approach for such a condition, but imaging is essential, and pericardiocentesis is generally required to control symptoms and document the malignant etiology.

The first case presented in this article illustrates a rare pattern of UC recurrence, as the patient was admitted with clinical signs of cardiac tamponade while receiving maintenance therapy with avelumab, after having achieved a complete response to chemotherapy. Although avelumab‐related pericarditis is extremely rare and not quantified in regulatory labeling or pivotal trials, with an estimated incidence of < 1% [21], this case shows the diagnostic challenge of cardiac tamponade in patients receiving immunotherapy, in whom immune‐mediated pericarditis must be considered. Like other immune‐related adverse events, immune‐mediated pericarditis can occur early or late, and even after treatment discontinuation, although most cases occur within the first few months [22].

The association of pericarditis with pleural effusion and impaired renal function raised the hypothesis of a systemic immune‐mediated process, namely, serositis and nephritis, and supported the decision to initiate corticosteroid therapy, as early immunosuppression can be lifesaving. As illustrated by this case, pericardial fluid cytology is key to a correct diagnosis and appropriate treatment and follow‐up. In the absence of pericardial fluid cytology or pericardial biopsy, the differential diagnosis between immune‐mediated pericarditis and metastatic pericardial disease remains presumptive. The diagnosis should be based on the overall clinical context, including timing relative to immunotherapy exposure, associated immune‐related adverse events, inflammatory markers, imaging findings, oncologic disease status, and treatment response.

Another notable aspect of this case was its unfavorable progression, with the development of severe complications, including ischemia. A multidisciplinary approach was essential for managing complications and allowed the patient to maintain an acceptable quality of life for several months, despite the absence of systemic antineoplastic treatment.

In cancer patients, pericardial effusions can have different etiologies, including pericardial involvement by cancer, inflammation, adverse treatment events, idiopathic pericarditis, or opportunistic infections [8]. In the checkpoint‐inhibitor era, distinguishing pericardial metastasis from immune‐mediated pericarditis is essential, as the management strategy and expected outcomes diverge.

According to current literature, the median overall survival in cancer patients with pericardial effusion requiring pericardiocentesis is 3.9 months [23]. This patient represents an exceptional case, with a survival of 8 months following the diagnosis of pericardial metastasis, presenting cardiogenic shock complicated by ischemia.

In the second case presented, the initial manifestation of UC was peritoneal carcinomatosis, a rare metastatic site associated with poor prognosis [24–26]. Available data about peritoneum involvement in UC are limited and based on small series, indicating a frequency of approximately 7.6% [25].

Despite systemic treatment, the disease later progressed with pericardial metastasis, which is another exceptionally rare site of dissemination [11].

Considering that UC rarely has peritoneal carcinomatosis at presentation and that cardiac involvement is even more uncommon, we believe that reporting such cases is crucial to improve clinical awareness of atypical metastatic patterns. However, it remains unclear whether patients with UC and peritoneal metastases are at increased risk of developing pericardial metastases, given the possible tropism of the disease for serous membranes in these cases.

The approach of pericardial effusion is primarily symptomatic, focusing on symptom management [8], and this was the strategy in both cases, as it is imperative to control myocardial compression to prevent collapse and hemodynamic consequences.

Although cardiac metastasis is rare, it carries substantial morbidity and mortality [8]. As reported in both patients, systemic treatment was not feasible following the diagnosis of pericardial involvement, and they experienced a rapidly deteriorating clinical course, reinforcing the poor prognosis associated with this type of metastasis.

5. Conclusion

Pericardial involvement by UC is rare and is associated with significant morbidity and mortality, and its approach is still not well defined.

These cases underscore the challenges of establishing a differential diagnosis in life‐threatening situations. In the era of checkpoint inhibitors, where immune‐mediated toxicities are more often considered, clinicians should not overlook the possibility of atypical metastatic sites in advanced UC. They further illustrate the severity of such involvement and the potential for serious complications.

Considering the rarity of documented pericardial metastases in living patients, we believe that describing such cases and the management strategies employed is important for increasing knowledge about this condition in the scientific community.
