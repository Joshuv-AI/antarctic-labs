 1. INTRODUCTION

As artificial intelligence (AI) computing densities continue to increase, industry is pursuing megawatt-
scale rack architectures that require tightly coordinated advances in electrical power delivery, thermal
management, operations, and infrastructure integration. This document summarizes the primary technical
challenges for realizing these architectures.

The challenge of scaling AI data centers has shifted from managing steady operation at limited compute
capacity to addressing extreme power densities and rapid transient dynamics across power, thermal, and
workload domains. As training and inference workloads concentrate on accelerator fabrication, power
requirements are moving from conventional 20–50 kW racks to 200 kW–1 MW integrated platforms. At
these levels, the rack must become a tightly engineered, dynamic infrastructure system. The central risk of
this change lies in the decoupling of system responses. Legacy subsystems operate within independent
envelopes. However, synchronized AI/high-performance computing transients create software-driven
power excursions that trigger localized heat pulses faster than traditional facility-level controls can react.
This creates a physics gap in which a software event can lead to coolant-flow imbalances, electrical
transients and mechanical stress. The magnitude of such imbalances varies across scales, from chip to
rack to facility level. Consequently, the megawatt-class rack must be treated as a coupled thermal,
electrical, and mechanical environment in which observability and validation are as critical as cooling
capacity is to overall system reliability.

 2. POWER DENSITY AND DELIVERY: HIGH-VOLTAGE DC POWER
 DISTRIBUTION AND POWER CONVERSION

Future AI infrastructure is converging toward 800–1,500 VDC distribution architectures to reduce
conversion losses and to enable scalable delivery of megawatt-class power with localized single-stage
conversion to lower voltage at the load [1], [2], [3]. However, major technology gaps remain in high-ratio
power conversions, medium voltage (MV)-to-DC conversion, and ultra-fast fault protection. At 800
VDC, a 1 MW rack requires around 1.25 kA, such that even modest workload fluctuations can generate
unprecedented transient power swings. As AI data centers move toward megawatt-class power blocks,
conventional transformer-rectifier architectures face limitations in footprint, controllability, and
integration. Compact MV-to-DC platforms capable of delivering multimegawatt power with transformer-
class reliability are critical. However, enabling technologies including solid-state transformers and MV
rectifiers remains insufficiently demonstrated for insulation integrity, partial discharge behavior, rapid
voltage transients, modular redundancy, and fault tolerance. Protection presents additional barriers. Future
800–1,500 VDC systems require ultra-fast fault isolation beyond conventional electromechanical
breakers. Unlike AC systems, DC architecture lacks natural zero crossing, making mitigation of the fault
substantially more difficult.

 3. THERMAL MANAGEMENT AND COOLING INFRASTRUCTURE

Thermal management is a notable constraint because next-generation AI accelerators generate highly
localized heat fluxes exceeding 100–500 W/cm2, with projections approaching 1 kW/cm2, making the
heat loads nonuniform and transient at component and rack scales. Conventional air cooling begins to
reach practical limits at approximately 20–30 kW/rack, whereas highly engineered hybrid approaches
may extend operation toward 50 kW/rack. Emerging AI infrastructure already exceeds 300 kW/rack,
driving adoption of liquid cooling [4], [5]. Enormous amounts of heat must move through interconnects,
cold-plate interfaces, coolant distribution units (CDUs), and facility heat rejection systems. Although
direct-to-chip cooling extends the operating envelope, megawatt-scale racks expose constraints not solved
by only increasing coolant flow. At these scales, cold-plate transfer capacity, manifold pressure drops,

coolant-flow imbalances, CDU scaling, pump dynamics, two-phase cooling stability, and transient
thermal-control instability become coupled, creating a dynamic rather than steady-state cooling challenge.
Faster coolant flow can reduce coolant dwell time, amplify pressure imbalances, and starve cold-plate
microchannels while other paths flood. Two-phase, direct-to-chip, and immersion cooling concepts may
increase heat-removal capability but introduce new requirements for fluid compatibility, pressure control,
serviceability, leak detection, and fault containment. Cooling systems must therefore be evaluated under
repeated workload transitions, facility-water temperature variations, chiller constraints, and ambient
conditions to determine whether sufficient operating margin exists.

 4. INFRASTRUCTURE INTEGRATION, RELIABILITY,
 AND OPERATIONAL RESILIENCE

As AI infrastructure scales toward megawatt-class racks, reliability depends on tight integration across
electrical, thermal, mechanical, and control systems rather than on optimization of individual components.
High-density graphics processing unit trays, busbars, CDUs, coolant manifolds, sidecar power cabinets,
and localized energy storage increase rack mass and required coolant inventory, challenging floor loading
limits, vibration tolerance, seismic qualification, and maintenance access. Operational reliability therefore
depends on blast-radius management in which localized failures (e.g., cooling disruption, power faults, or
control instability) must be rapidly isolated and mitigated before cascading effects outpace traditional
facility telemetry. Cross-vendor fragmentation compounds the problem. Proprietary manifold geometries,
configurable liquid connectors, CDU interfaces, rack power-distribution schemes, and telemetry protocols
make qualification and maintenance system-level concerns rather than component-level tasks. A future
data center will need evidence that a rack can be installed, serviced, fault-isolated, upgraded, and
integrated across vendors without becoming a custom facility project.

Additionally, data center development is facing supply chain bottlenecks driven by the surge in demand
for AI-ready infrastructure, causing significant delays and cancellations of planned projects. How much
this will influence the move toward 1 MW racks is unclear, but shortages in electric grid components,
computer components, and material costs could affect timelines for the industry to move toward higher
power density systems.

Despite these challenges, industry is expected to aggressively pursue the development and deployment of
1 MW racks in the near term to maintain competitiveness and meet rapidly growing AI demand [6], [7]. It
will be imperative that data center project developers and operators work closely with electric grid
utilities and other energy stakeholders to ensure adequate power infrastructure, grid integration, and
operational reliability. ORNL’s reconfigurable megawatt-scale hardware-in-the-loop research platform
will play a critical role in accelerating deployment by providing an independent environment to validate
integrated power, cooling, controls, and workload management strategies under realistic transient
conditions. Coupled with advanced digital twins and physics-informed AI, the platform enables rapid de-
risking of emerging architectures, reduces time to deployment, and supports development of resilient,
grid-aware next-generation AI infrastructure.
