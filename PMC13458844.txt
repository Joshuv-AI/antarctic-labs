Comparative analysis of autograft vs. synthetic bone graft in spinal fusion: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ahmed Onayzan Alshammari, Saadeldin Ahmed Idris, Turki Alhumaidi Dhawi Alshammari, Refal Mohammed Algahtani, Hatim Salem Alanazi, Thayid Binyah Alshammari, Yousef Hulayyil Alanazi, Obaid Muslm Alrashedi, Rabah Abdulaziz Alshuhail
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutografts are regarded as the gold standard for spinal fusion owing to their capability to promote bone formation. However, synthetic bone grafts are gaining popularity for their consistency and avoidance of donor site complications. This meta-analysis compares the treatment results of autografts and synthetic bone grafts in spinal fusion procedures and evaluates their clinical effectiveness.MethodsThe study adhered to PRISMA guidelines and conducted a comprehensive literature search. It compared autografts and synthetic bone grafts in spinal fusion by employing a random-effects model for meta-analysis, assessing publication bias, and evaluating the certainty of evidence using Grading, Reporting, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) guidelines, along with methodological quality through the risk of bias-2.0 (RoB-2.0) and Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tools.ResultsFollowing a thorough screening, nine studies fulfilled the inclusion criteria. In the subgroup analysis, the pooled effect size for the fusion rate between different types of synthetic bone graft [ceramics, i-Factor, demineralized bone matrix (DBM)] and compared with autograft was 1.24 odds ratio (OR) [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.77–2.02; P=0.38; I2=0%], success rate for ceramics, i-Factor, and bioactive glass was 1.78 OR (95% CI: 1.28–2.48; P=0.0006; I2=0%). The blood loss had an −0.88 standardized mean difference (SMD) (95% CI: −1.90 to 0.15; P=0.09; I2=87%). For reoperation, the effect size was 0.78 (95% CI: 0.37–1.68; P=0.53; I2=0%), and in the complications subgroup analysis (i-Factor, ceramic, bioactive glass), the pooled effect size was 0.82 (95% CI: 0.54–1.24; P=0.35; I2=0%). A funnel plot revealed publication bias, while most studies demonstrated minimal risk of bias. However, the certainty of evidence was low because of this bias and wide variance.ConclusionsSynthetic bone grafts can be a reliable alternative to autografts in spinal fusion, and the recommendation is weak due to low-certainty evidence. Meanwhile, further longitudinal studies are required to validate these findings.Keywords: Synthetic bone graft, autograft, spinal fusion, bone substitute, fusion rate

Highlight box

Key findings

• Synthetic bone grafts demonstrated a significantly higher clinical success rate than autografts while achieving comparable fusion, reoperation, and complication rates.

• Synthetic bone grafts avoided donor-site morbidity associated with autograft harvesting.

What is known and what is new?

• Autografts remain the gold standard for spinal fusion because of their osteogenic potential; however, donor-site morbidity and limited graft availability have driven the development of synthetic bone graft substitutes

• This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates that synthetic bone grafts provide comparable fusion, complication, and reoperation rates while achieving a higher clinical success rate than autografts. However, the certainty of the evidence was low, indicating that these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Synthetic bone grafts may be considered a suitable alternative to autografts in selected patients undergoing spinal fusion, particularly when avoiding donor-site morbidity is desirable. Additional high-quality randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up are needed to strengthen the evidence and guide clinical practice.

Introduction

The global prevalence of spine-associated diseases is increasing with the growing aging population, which signifies the need for more targeted therapeutic interventions (1). Spinal fusion is a standard treatment employed to address deformities, alleviate pain, and stabilize spinal pathologies by promoting bone healing between two or more vertebrae (2). The fundamental aim of spinal fusion involves establishing a solid and immobile union between the vertebral bodies. This is typically accomplished using bone grafts, which maintain structural integrity and provide a scaffold for bone fusion (3). Bone grafts usually provide a framework for new bone growth and possess osteo-inductive, osteogenic, and osteo-conductive properties (4). Bone grafting materials are essential in ensuring the success of the fusion procedures, influencing the potential for complications, overall fusion rate, and reducing the frequency of subsequent surgeries or interventions (5,6).

Bone grafts are classified into several types based on their origin (allografts, autografts, and synthetic bone grafts) and the materials utilized in surgical procedures. Among all these bone grafts, autograft remains the benchmark for bone grafting (7). Typically, the iliac crest serves as the primary source for these bone grafts, though other donor sites may also be used. However, other bone grafting techniques, such as synthetic bone grafts, can substitute for autograft due to certain challenges, including patient donor site morbidity and insufficient training for young surgeons in the autograft harvesting technique (8). Likewise, allografts are devoid of osteogenic properties and pose a potential risk of disease transmission (9). Additionally, allografts have shown lower efficacy in bone regeneration and raised concerns about immune rejection (10).

Meanwhile, over the last few decades, synthetic bone grafts have emerged as an alternative to autografts and allografts. These synthetic bone grafts include a variety of materials, such as ceramics, calcium phosphate (CaP), recombinant human bone morphogenetic proteins (rhBMPs; rhBMP-2 and rhBMP-7), and hydroxyapatite (HAp) (5,11). Moreover, synthetic bone grafts have a great impact on the care of patients and surgical outcomes, offering an alternative to autografts, which have known complications of harvesting and require a high dose of growth factors (5). Furthermore, synthetic bone grafts have various other advantages, such as ease of use, lower cost, lower rates of infection, lower rates of complications associated with donor site morbidity, and properties like osteo-inductive, osteogenic, and osteo-conductive (1). However, disadvantages include a lack of the same level of biological activity as autografts and the limited ability to promote osteogenesis (1).

Despite the widespread use of autografts and synthetic bone grafts, comparative effectiveness remains unclear. Numerous studies have been conducted to assess the clinical outcomes, radiological findings, and complication rates associated with these bone grafts; however, results are inconsistent. A systematic review published in 2016 compared autografts and allografts with synthetic bone grafts; however, it did not include a meta-analysis. Moreover, optimal selection and utilization of bone grafts are essential for achieving improved, reliable radiographic and clinical outcomes related to spine fusion. Meanwhile, available studies had short follow-ups and lacked information regarding the type of surgical techniques used for spinal fusion and the types of grafts used. Moreover, there is a need to assess the impact of these grafts on the adjacent level of the spine. Therefore, this review centered on comparing the treatment results of autografts and synthetic bone grafts in spinal fusion procedures. It aimed to assess the effectiveness of autograft compared to synthetic bone graft in achieving spinal fusion success, as well as to evaluate the safety profiles and complication rates associated with each type of graft. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0072/rc).

Methods

Literature search

Using various databases, including Cochrane Library, PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar, a comparative search was conducted to identify the most relevant studies through April 2025. The last search was conducted on 30th April 2025. Various keywords were utilized, including autograft, autograft bone, autologous bone, autologous bone graft, bone autograft, synthetic bone grafts, bone substitutes, alloplastic bone graft, bone graft substitute, synthetic bone substitute, demineralized bone matrix (DBM), spinal fusion, cervical fusion, lumbar fusion, and spinal surgery (see Table S1).

Inclusion criteria

PICO framework was used, focusing on P (participants): human patients of any age who were undergoing spinal fusion surgery, specifically primary posterolateral thoracic and/or lumbar fusion alone or anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF). I (intervention): implementation of an autograft bone strategy for spinal fusion. C (control/comparator): use of synthetic bone graft, which includes ceramic-based or other commercially available synthetic options. O (outcomes): fusion rate (it is a strictly biological, radiological measurement of bone healing), success rate (it is a clinical, patient-centered measurement of symptom relief and quality of life), blood loss, reoperation rate, complication rate, and other key findings. Both randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and non-RCTs (retrospective, cross-sectional, observational) published in English in peer-reviewed journals, featuring clearly defined control groups, were deemed eligible for inclusion.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they did not provide sufficient data or control groups, or included interbody fusion, or were commentaries, reviews, case reports, or editorials. Additionally, studies published in languages other than English were not considered for inclusion.

Study selection and assessment

A total of 1,002 studies were assessed by two independent reviewers, who separately evaluated the titles and abstracts based on the study’s objectives. After initial screening, 28 studies met the eligibility criteria and were subjected to a full-text assessment. The reviewers then discussed their perspectives to reach a consensus. If any discrepancies or concerns arose, they were addressed with a third independent reviewer, leading to resolution. This process resulted in the inclusion of 9 studies in this systematic review. The other 19 studies were excluded for various factors, as outlined in Figure 1.

PRISMA flow diagram illustrating the process of the study inclusion. PICO, Participants, Intervention, Control/Comparator, and Outcomes.

Data extraction

Information was gathered from the studies that met the inclusion and exclusion criteria using a pre-established Excel sheet. Two reviewers worked separately to document each study’s details, including author ID, country, study design, and sample size. They also recorded participant information such as age, sex, and diagnosis. Additionally, the reviewers noted surgical details like spinal level, surgical approach, and instrumentation, along with aspects related to the interventions and controls, and the observed outcomes (fusion rate, success rate, blood loss, reoperations, and complications) in each study.

Quality assessment

The quality of RCTs was evaluated using the Cochrane Collaboration tool. It focused on aspects such as randomization, alteration from planned intervention, data appraisal, absence of outcomes, and reporting. In studies that were non-RCTs, the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was employed (12).

Statistical analysis

The gathered data were evaluated for the articles incorporated in the systematic review. The qualitative outcomes were displayed in tables and graphs. RevMan 5.4 was employed for the meta-analysis to evaluate the overall effectiveness of the intervention, while the heterogeneity was measured using I2 statistics and classified as low, moderate, and high, corresponding to <25%, 25–75%, and >75%, respectively. Utilizing a random effects model, a significance level of 0.05 was applied. For publication bias, a funnel plot was constructed.

Certainty of evidence

The Grading, Reporting, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework provides a method for evaluating the certainty of the evidence. Each domain was categorized as very low, low, moderate, or high certainty of evidence based on the publication bias, precision, indirectness, and heterogeneity level among the studies.

Results

General characteristics of studies

Two studies (22.22%) were reported from the USA (13,14), 2 (22.22%) from the Netherlands (15,16), 2 (22.22%) from France (17,18), 1 (11.11%) from Japan (19), 1 (11.11%) from China (20), and 1 (11.11%) was collaborative study between UK and France (21). Most studies followed a randomized study design, except for two that used a retrospective study design (18,19). Variation in the sample size was noted across the studies, with a maximum of 170 participants belonging to the synthetic bone graft group and 171 in the autograft groups (21). Minimum sample sizes were 28 and 30, owing to the synthetic bone graft and autograft groups, respectively (17). Patient ages ranged from adolescence, with values of 14.8 and 15.2 years for synthetic and autograft participants, respectively (18), to older individuals aged 72 and 73 years for the synthetic bone graft and the autograft groups, respectively (20). Variation in gender distribution was observed, and female patients were included in a greater number, as described in Table 1. The primary diagnosis included idiopathic scoliosis, cervical radiculopathy, degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis, spondylolisthesis, and other spinal deformities (Table 1). Symptom duration ranged from a few months to 20 years (20). Fusion types differed, with posterior spinal fusion and posterolateral spinal fusion being the most common types, except for three studies that used ACDF (13,14,19).

Outcomes

Overall, operating times and blood loss were comparable in the synthetic bone graft and autograft groups; however, the synthetic bone graft group required slightly less operating time than the autograft group (17,20). Similarly, more blood loss occurred in the autograft than in the synthetic bone graft group (17,18,20). Donor site pain was consistently observed in the autograft group, highlighting a key disadvantage of autograft harvesting (17,18,20,21). Complication rates were low in both groups, featuring minor issues such as mild pain, infections, and instrument-associated difficulties (Table 3). Fusion rates were similar or slightly elevated in the synthetic bone graft category (13-16). There was a significant variation in the follow-up minimum of 12 months (14-16) and a maximum of 40 months (18). In short, the qualitative findings support synthetic bone grafts as a safe, effective, and often preferable alternative to autograft in spinal fusion surgeries (Table 3).

Meta-analysis

Fusion rate

The pooled effect size of the fusion rate between the synthetic bone graft and autograft was 1.24 odds ratio (OR) [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.77–2.02], with a non-significant difference (P=0.38) and low heterogeneity (I2=0%). In the subgroup, Dai et al. [2008] (20) reported 100% fusion in both groups; therefore, the OR was not calculated, whereas a single study (17) indicated 0.93 OR (95% CI: 0.32–2.75). Likewise, in the DBM group, a single study had required data and reported an OR of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.27–2.88). The pooled effect size for the i-Factor subgroup was 1.49 OR (95% CI: 0.81–2.75), with a non-significant difference and low heterogeneity (P=0.20; I2=0%), as described in Figure 2.

Forest plot for fusion rate after the treatment of spinal fusion with different types of synthetic bone graft and compared with autograft. CI, confidence interval; DBM, demineralized bone matrix; M-H, Mantel-Haenszel.

Success rate

A significant difference (P=0.001), with low heterogeneity (I2=0%), was observed in the success rate among different types of materials used in synthetic bone grafts compared with autograft, with an effect size of 1.78 OR (95% CI: 1.28–2.48). In the subgroups, like ceramics and bioactive glass, single study had required data, which had effect size of 2.10 OR (95% CI: 0.68–6.47) and 1.94 OR (95% CI: 0.51–7.43), while i-Factor had pooled effect size of two studies, which was 1.74 OR (95% CI: 1.22–2.49), with significant difference and low heterogeneity (P=0.002; I2=0%), as described in Figure 3.

Forest plot for success rate after the treatment of spinal fusion with different types of synthetic bone graft compared with autograft. CI, confidence interval; M-H, Mantel-Haenszel.

Blood loss

The effect size between the synthetic bone graft and autograft was −0.88 standardized mean difference (SMD) (95% CI: −1.90 to 0.15), with a non-significant difference (P=0.09) and high heterogeneity (I2=87%) (Figure 4). This high heterogeneity may be due to wide variation in surgical approaches, such as ACDF and posterolateral, within the study population.

Reoperation

The effect size between the synthetic bone graft and autograft was 0.78 OR (95% CI: 0.37–1.68), with non-significant differences (P=0.53) and low heterogeneity (I2=0%) (Figure 5).

Complications

The overall effect size between different types of synthetic bone graft and autograft was 0.82 OR (95% CI: 0.54–1.24), with a non-significant difference (P=0.35) and low heterogeneity (I2=0%). Meanwhile, in the i-Factor subgroup, effect size was 0.81 OR (95% CI: 0.39–1.66), with non-significant difference and moderate heterogeneity (P=0.56; I2=47%), While, ceramics and bioactive glass subgroups had one study each indicating effect size of 0.90 OR (95% CI: 0.47–1.72) and 0.30 OR (95% CI: 0.006–1.66), as described in Figure 6.

Certainty of evidence

Overall, five outcomes were evaluated for the certainty of evidence, and all were rated as low. This rating is primarily attributed to significant concerns regarding publication bias and, in certain outcomes, substantial inconsistency and low precision across studies (Table 4).

Discussion

Autografts have long been viewed as the optimal choice because of their natural osteogenic capabilities. Different types of synthetic bone grafts, like bone morphogenetic proteins, synthetic scaffolds, demineralized bone matrices, and other viable bone matrices, are also frequently used. Each synthetic bone graft material has demonstrated promising potential to enhance fusion rates and reduce the pain associated with autografts during procedures. Thus, the current study was designed to assess the clinical outcomes of synthetic bone grafts compared to autografts in association with spinal fusion procedures.

Our findings demonstrated non-significant differences in the fusion rate, blood loss, reoperation, and complications. However, synthetic bone grafts were significantly associated with a better success rate compared with autografts, supporting their use as a reliable alternative in spinal fusion procedures. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the presence of low-certainty evidence. Meanwhile, this higher success rate may be due to certain key factors. For instance, synthetic bone grafts are often engineered to have consistent osteoconductive properties, reducing variability in patient outcomes, whereas autografts can vary in quality depending on the donor site and patient health (22,23). In addition, synthetic bone grafts eliminate the need for a second surgical site, thereby minimizing complications, such as donor site pain or infections, which can lead to improved postoperative recovery and clinical outcomes (24). Meanwhile, the findings in the current study are consistent with those of another systematic review, which also found no differences in fusion rates, complications, or other functional outcomes between patients receiving synthetic bone grafts and those receiving autografts or allografts (25). Likewise, in an RCT, 45 patients were treated with commercial ceramic bone graft substitutes and autografts, and the fusion rates after 24 months of follow-up were 96.4% and 100%, respectively (26). Moreover, in another prospective study, 40 patients underwent PLIF with i-Factor and autografts; after 6 months of follow-up, the i-Factor group (97.73%) demonstrated a higher success rate than the autograft group (59.09%). However, after 24 months, a non-significant difference was observed in the success rate (95.5% vs. 93.33%) (27). A retrospective study was performed on the efficacy of a novel integrative bone matrix, and after 12 months of postoperative follow-up, 100% fusion was achieved with no adverse events (28). In contrast, another meta-analysis demonstrated a significantly (P=0.03) higher fusion rate in cage utilization than bone graft [96.3% vs. 90.8%; risk ratio (RR) =2.74] (29). Likewise, the meta-analysis noted a notable variance in fusion rates between local bone graft and autograft (95.3%, 95% CI: 89.7–98.7% vs. 88.6%, 95% CI: 84.8–91.9%) (6). Furthermore, another meta-analysis was performed, and the outcomes indicated no significant difference in failure risk and Lysholm scores between the groups. A significant difference was observed in complications with 2.54 OR (95% CI: 1.26–5.14) and Tegner score (−0.31 OR; 95% CI: −0.52 to −0.10) (30). Although synthetic bone graft scaffolds are used alone and provide better and more consistent structure with a fusion rate of 77–90%, during comparison with iliac crest autograft, the fusion rate was 78% to 100% (25,31). Our findings also indicated that synthetic bone graft reduced operating time by 14.38 minutes. The possible explanation is that synthetic grafts eliminate the requirement for harvesting autologous bone, usually from the iliac crest. As iliac harvesting requires additional surgical exposure, hemostasis, graft collection, and wound closure, all of which increase operating time (32). In addition, shorter operating time can also provide shorter anesthesia exposure, decreased surgeon fatigue, lower operating room utilization, and lower perioperative morbidity. However, whether this reduced operating time translates into meaningful improvements in long-term clinical outcomes remains uncertain.

Meanwhile, discrepancies among studies comparing these bone grafts arise due to differences in the study setting, design, patient demographics, health conditions, surgical techniques, and most importantly evaluation criteria. In the case of synthetic bone grafts, their efficacy depends on the type of material, composition, and whether used alone or in combination with other biologics. Similarly, autografts also depend on the quality and quantity of harvested bone, and patient-specific factors, like age, gender, and comorbidities. Moreover, donor site-associated problems, such as pain and infections, are most notable.

A key strength of this review is the inclusion of a diverse range of patient populations, from adolescents to elderly patients and graft materials, which allows for a broader understanding of clinical outcomes across different clinical settings. Additionally, statistical analysis provides a comprehensive and reliable conclusion. However, several limitations should be taken into account, including the inclusion of a limited number of studies, which may influence the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, publication bias may affect the robustness of the study’s results. Despite these limitations, this study provides meaningful evidence to inform clinical decision-making in spinal fusion surgery. However, future multicenter and longitudinal studies should be designed to validate the findings of the present study.

Conclusions

Although synthetic bone grafts demonstrated a significantly higher success rate and shorter operating time compared to autografts, both groups showed no differences in fusion, reoperation, or complication rates. Synthetic bone grafts had numerous benefits over autografts, including no donor-site pain or infections, ease of use, and consistent quality. Additionally, synthetic bone grafts can be used according to the patient demographics and surgical techniques. However, these differences are very slight; therefore, further high-quality and long-term RCTs are required to validate the outcomes.
