PLCG2 exon-skipped variants: insights into their potential role in chronic lymphocytic leukemia∗

ABSTRACT
Key Points•PLCG2 SSM or deletion led to exon-skipped variants with enhanced phospholipase activity and BTKi resistance.•PLCG2 exon-skipped variant expressions were predominantly unaltered after ibrutinib-based regimen but decreased after venetoclax treatment.

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is the most common adult B-cell malignancy in Western countries, characterized by the aberrant accumulation of clonal B lymphocytes.1 Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors (BTKi), such as ibrutinib, have significantly improved CLL treatment outcomes, offering superior efficacy and tolerability compared to traditional chemotherapy. However, the emergence of acquired resistance remains a major challenge. Resistance to BTKi is frequently associated with acquired mutations in BTK and phospholipase C gamma 2 (PLCG2). BTK mutations typically disrupt protein-inhibitor binding2 or impair kinase activity.3,4 PLCG2 mutations enhance intrinsic phospholipase activity, as observed in R665W and L845F variants.5,6 Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of cases at relapse lack detectable BTK/PLCG2 mutations. Analysis of samples from ALPINE7 and ELEVATE8 trials identified no BTK and/or PLCG2 mutations in 80.5% and 33% of cases progressing on BTKi, indicating the presence of alternative resistance mechanisms. This mechanistic uncertainty presents immediate clinical challenges by limiting the effectiveness of current biomarker-driven therapeutic strategies.

RNA splicing, in addition to processing pre–messenger RNA (mRNA) to mature mRNAs, generates proteomic diversity by selectively including or excluding exons.9 Aberrant splicing is increasingly recognized as a key pathogenic process in CLL, frequently associated with spliceosome mutations10 and dysregulated splicing complexes.11 Alternative splicing modulates many signaling pathway proteins in CLL, including CD79b,12 B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2) family members,13 and NOTCH1.14 In-frame PLCG2 variants, such as Δ19 and Δ20-22, which lead to truncation of the autoinhibitory domain,15 have been reported in PLCγ2-associated antibody deficiency and immune dysregulation.16,17 Similar to L845F and R665W PLCγ2 point mutations, the Δ19 and Δ20-22 variants also exhibit increased phospholipase activity.16,18 However, the existence and potential roles of PLCG2 exon-skipped variants in CLL remain unknown.

Using a dose-escalation strategy, we previously generated resistance to various BTKi in vitro.4 In REC-1 cells that acquired resistance to the covalent BTKi tirabrutinib, we identified a splice site mutation (SSM), c.2236-1G>T, in PLCG2. Since PLCG2 SSM has never been implicated in BTKi resistance, we aimed to study this further. Here, we analyzed the functional consequences of the novel PLCG2 SSM in the context of response to BTKi and PLCγ inhibitors and studied the occurrence of similar PLCG2 variants in primary samples from patients with CLL treated with BTKi.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and reagents

REC-1 and COS-7 cells were maintained at 37°C in 5% carbon dioxide, in RPMI-1640 with 10% (volume-to-volume ratio [v/v]) fetal bovine serum (FBS), or in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium with 10% (v/v) FBS, respectively. The culture media were supplemented with 1% (v/v) penicillin (10 000 U/mL) and streptomycin (10 mg/mL), as well as 1% (v/v) 200mM l-glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, vecabrutinib, pirtobrutinib, branebrutinib, zanubrutinib, NX-2127, NX-5948, U73122, CCT129957, and 3-nitrocoumarin (3-NC) were purchased from MedChemExpress. Tirabrutinib, nemtabrutinib, fenebrutinib, and RN-486 were obtained from Selleck Chemicals.

Flow cytometric analysis of intracellular Ca2+ flux

To minimize technical variability, different groups of REC-1 cells were labeled with CellTrace Violet, CellTrace Far Red, or a combination of both for 10 minutes and subsequently mixed after 3 washes. Cells were then resuspended in Hank's balanced salt solution with 20 nM Hepes (HHBS, AAT Bioquest) containing 5% (v/v) FBS, 3μM Fluo-4 AM (reconstituted in dimethyl sulfoxide), and 0.1% Pluronic F-127 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After incubation at 37°C in 5% carbon dioxide for 45 minutes, intracellular calcium concentration ([Ca2+]ᵢ) was measured using a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Briefly, the cells were equilibrated at a density of 2 × 109/L in 0.5 mL HHBS at 37°C for 10 minutes. Baseline [Ca2+]ᵢ levels were recorded for 1 minute before stimulation with 10 μg/mL anti-human immunoglobulin M (IgM) (Jackson ImmunoResearch). After 3.5 minutes, 2μM ionomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to induce maximal [Ca2+]ᵢ responses. Data were analyzed using the kinetics platform in FlowJo version 10.10 (BD Biosciences). The gating strategy for Ca2+ assay is shown in supplemental Figure 1. Data normalization was conducted relative to maximal ionomycin responses, with basal fluorescence corrections applied for controls lacking anti-IgM. Normalized calcium response curves were further analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.0.

IP3 formation assay

COS-7 cells were seeded in 500 µl media per well in 24-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 109/L and cultured for 24 hours. Subsequently, 500 ng of plasmids encoding either an empty vector or various PLCγ2 complementary DNA (cDNA) constructs were transfected using 1 μL of jetPRIME reagent (Polyplus) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Twenty-four hours after transfection, COS-7 cells were washed once with phosphate-buffered saline and incubated with 0.2 mL per well of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium containing 2.5 μCi/mL myo-[2-3H] inositol (PerkinElmer) and 10mM lithium chloride. After incubation at 37°C for 20 hours, cells were washed once with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline and lysed with 0.2 mL per well of 10mM ice-cold formic acid on ice for 30 minutes. Then, 0.3 mL per well of 10mM ammonium hydroxide was then added for neutralization, and the samples were centrifuged at 20 000g for 5 minutes. The supernatants were loaded onto columns containing 0.5 mL AmberChrom 1×8 100–200 mesh ion exchange resin (Sigma-Aldrich). The columns were washed once with 3 mL of H2O and twice with 3.5 mL each of 60mM sodium formate and 5mM sodium tetraborate. Inositol phosphate (IP3) was eluted with 3 mL of 1M ammonium formate and 100mM formic acid. The eluate was mixed with 15 mL scintillation fluid (Zinsser Analytic), and radioactivity was measured using liquid scintillation counter. The construction of vectors encoding c-myc-tagged PLCγ2 wild-type (WT) and variants, as well as the detailed assay procedure, have been previously described.19

RNA sequencing and transcriptomic analysis

RNA sequencing of REC-1 was performed using the INVIEW Transcriptome Discover service from Eurofins Genomics (Ebersberg, Germany). High-quality RNA was used for library preparation, which included mRNA fragmentation, random primed cDNA synthesis (strand-specific), adapter ligation, and adapter-specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina platform, generating paired-end reads (2 × 150 bp), with a total of ∼30 million read pairs (± 3%). High-quality sequence reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 using Spliced Transcripts Alignment to a Reference20 and processed through Sentieon (https://www.sentieon.com/) framework, along with the known gene models. Raw gene read counts were used for differential gene expression analysis with the edgeR21 package in R/Bioconductor. Only high-quality reads with counts per million >1 across all batches were included for analysis. Z scores were calculated from log2-transformed counts per million using the pheatmap22 package, and unsupervised heat maps were generated using Euclidean distance. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the gseKEGG function from the clusterProfiler package.23 False discovery rate was calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Figures were generated using the ggplot224 and enrichplot25 packages following standard protocols. RNA sequencing was performed on primary CLL samples (n = 5 paired baseline and post-ibrutinib relapse) and healthy donor (HD) B cells (n = 6) by BMKGENE (Münster, Germany). Libraries were prepared using the Hieff NGS Ultima Dual-mode mRNA Library Kit (Yeasen Bio) and sequenced (paired-end 150 bp) on an Illumina NovaSeq X. Sashimi plots were generated from GRCh37-aligned data using Integrative Genomics Viewer (version 2.19.1).

Software

R and RStudio were used for the transcriptomic analysis of RNA sequencing data. Chromatograms from Sanger sequencing were visualized using Sequence Scanner version 1.0 (Applied Biosystems). GraphPad Prism version 10.4.0 was used for statistical analysis across different experiments. The statistical tests used for comparisons are mentioned in the figure legends. P values <.05 were considered statistically significant.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ulm University. All patients provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Further materials and methods used are described in the supplemental methods.

Results

SSM in PLCG2 contributes to tirabrutinib resistance in REC-1 cells

We have previously shown that REC-1 cells have exceptional sensitivity to BTKi in vitro compared to different CLL and mantle cell lymphoma cell lines. Therefore, we performed long-term dose-escalation treatments of REC-1 cell lines for generating resistance to a series of covalent and noncovalent BTKi. The BTK and PLCG2 mutations acquired in the resistant REC-1 model recapitulated the mutation landscape observed in patients with CLL who were resistant to BTKi.4 As an extension of this work, we generated resistance to the covalent BTKi tirabrutinib in 3 parallel REC-1 lines. Treatment was continued until a 50% inhibitory concentration of >10-fold was achieved compared to dimethyl sulfoxide treatment. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) identified mutations in BTK cysteine 481 in 1 line with a variant allele frequency (VAF) of 35%, whereas the other 2 lines acquired a PLCG2 SSM (c.2236-1G>T) with a VAF of 42% and 44%, respectively. All the cells exhibited significant resistances to tirabrutinib compared to controls (Figure 1A). Analysis of the product of reverse transcription PCR using high-resolution agarose gel electrophoresis revealed 2 distinct PLCG2 variants in resistant REC-1 cells. Gel extraction and Sanger sequencing identified deletion of exon 21 (Δ21) and deletion of exons 21-22 (Δ21-22), which were absent in PLCG2 WT REC-1 (Figure 1B; supplemental Figure 2A). In addition, RNA sequencing was performed to validate the findings. The relative expression level of Δ21 variant was 30% of WT PLCG2 in SSM REC-1, whereas the Δ22 and Δ21-22 variants resulting in premature stop codon (supplemental Figure 2B-D) occurred at lower frequencies and were also present in WT cells (Figure 1C-D). Western blot analysis demonstrated a low expression of a single truncated PLCγ2 protein corresponding to the Δ21 variant in resistant cells (Figure 1E). To understand if BTK and PLCγ2 can be activated in SSM cells, we performed western blotting after stimulation with anti-IgM for 1, 5, and 10 minutes. SSM REC-1 cells showed a prolonged activation of PLCγ2 phosphorylation compared to WT cells, but the differences were not statistically significant (Figure 1F-G).

SSD in PLCG2 in a patient with disease progression under ibrutinib

Of interest, we identified a novel PLCG2 splice site deletion (SSD) spanning c.2236-23_2238 with a VAF of 17% in CLL from a patient progressing 2 years after continuous ibrutinib treatment (Figure 2A). The variant was not identified in the CD19- germ line fraction. Similar to the PLCG2 SSM previously described in REC-1 cells, this SSD also resulted in the generation of Δ21 and Δ21-22 variants (Figure 2B-C). However, clonal origin tracking was limited because of the lack of baseline samples before ibrutinib treatment. We performed targeted NGS on sequentially collected and CD19-enriched samples to study clonal shifts during venetoclax-based therapy. The patient exhibited a drastic decrease in the leukocyte count from 143 × 109/L1 week before treatment start to 25 × 109/L within 20 days of treatment with venetoclax. Interestingly, whereas the VAF of NFKBIE and RPS15 mutations remained stable, the PLCG2 SSD VAF progressively decreased from 17% to 5.5% over the course of 102-day treatment with venetoclax (Figure 2D). These findings suggest that PLCG2 SSD might be counterselected in the absence of BTKi drug selection pressure.

PLCG2 SSM REC-1 cells exhibit enhanced phospholipase activity

To characterize the biological function of PLCG2 SSM c.2236-1G>T, we evaluated enzymatic activity using IP3 formation assays in COS-7 cells transfected with equal amount of WT or mutant PLCG2 constructs (Figure 2E). COS-7 cells were selected because of their low basal PLCγ2 activity, and hence, any increase in IP3 formation corresponds to the function of the transfected PLCG2 variant.26 Similar to PLCγ2-activating mutations R665W and S707Y,5,6 PLCγ2 Δ21 demonstrated significantly increased phospholipase activity (Figure 2F) compared to WT. We then examined whether this would translate to altered [Ca2+]ᵢ in PLCγ2-mutant REC1 cells. Flow cytometry analysis showed significantly enhanced and more sustained [Ca2+]ᵢ in PLCG2 SSM and L845F cells (previously4 acquired in REC-1 resistant to vecabrutinib) upon stimulation with anti-IgM (Figure 2G-H). Our results show that acquired PLCγ2 SSM has enhanced enzymatic activity, leading to increased concentrations of intracellular calcium.

Anti-IgM–mediated [Ca2+]i is not suppressed by BTK inhibition in PLCG2-mutant REC-1 cells

Treatment of leukemia/lymphoma cells with BTKi results in impaired anti-IgM–mediated [Ca2+]i since its effector PLCγ2 is downstream of BTK in the B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling cascade.15,27 Since SSM led to enhanced PLCγ2 activity, we investigated the effect of BTK inhibition or degradation on [Ca2+]i in mutant vs WT REC-1 cells. In PLCγ2 WT REC-1 cells, treatment with 1μM tirabrutinib or ibrutinib for 1 hour strongly suppressed anti-IgM–induced [Ca2+]i, whereas PLCG2 SSM and L845F cells showed no decrease in calcium responses despite BTKi treatment (Figure 3A-C,G). Similarly, 24-hour treatment with 10nM BTK degraders NX-2127 or NX-5948 effectively inhibited [Ca2+]i in WT cells but had minimal effect on PLCG2-mutant cells (Figure 3D-G). To evaluate if this loss of inhibitory effect on calcium signaling in the PLCG2 mutants translated to differential cellular response to BTKi, we studied the sensitivity of PLCG2 mutants to a panel of 5 noncovalent BTKi, namely, pirtobrutinib, nemtabrutinib, vecabrutinib, fenebrutinib, and RN486, as well as 6 covalent BTKi: ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib, tirabrutinib, spebrutinib, and branebrutinib. Both mutants exhibited resistance to all the BTKi evaluated in the study after 4 days of drug treatment. PLCγ2 mutants were also resistant to proteolysis-targeting chimera–based degraders NX-5948 and NX-2127 (Figure 3H). Since PLCG2 SSM, similar to L845F, resulted in BCR pathway activation independent of BTK, these cells showed impaired signaling and cellular response to BTKi and BTK degraders.

PLC inhibitors effectively attenuate calcium flux in PLCG2-mutant REC-1 cells

Given the inability of BTKi to reduce viability and suppress calcium signaling in PLCG2 SSM cells, we next evaluated whether these cells are sensitive to a direct inhibition of PLC. We therefore treated PLCγ2 mutants with the PLCγ inhibitor 3-NC for 3 hours at the concentrations of 5μM, 10μM, and 20μM. Anti-IgM–mediated [Ca2+]i was analyzed in these cells using flow cytometry. Treatment with 3-NC resulted in a more pronounced, dose-dependent reduction in [Ca2+]i in PLCG2 SSM and L845F compared to WT (Figure 4A-C). Comparison of area under the curve confirmed significantly greater inhibition of anti-IgM–induced [Ca2+]i increases in PLCG2-mutant cells at all tested concentrations (Figure 4D) compared to WT. Furthermore, we evaluated the sensitivity of PLCγ2-mutant cells to treatment with the pan-PLC inhibitor U-73122 and the PLCγ inhibitors 3-NC and CCT129957 for 96 hours. Cell viability analysis showed that PLC or PLCγ inhibition was equally effective in WT and PLCG2-mutant cells (Figure 4E). The results indicate that PLCγ2 splice site mutant cells, similar to L845F mutants, bypass BTK function but are dependent on PLCγ2-mediated BCR signaling. These findings imply that direct PLCγ2 inhibition could serve as a potential strategy to counteract resistance to BTKi mediated by PLCG2 mutations.

Distinct transcriptomic profiles and enriched NF-κB and OXPHOS pathways in PLCG2-mutant REC-1 cells

To explore additional mechanisms contributing to BTKi resistance beyond PLCG2 mutations, we performed RNA sequencing on WT (n = 3), SSM (n = 2), and L845F (n = 2) REC-1 cells. Differential expression analysis showed PLCG2-mutant cell lines to exhibit a distinct transcriptomic profile compared to WT with 2104 upregulated and 938 downregulated genes (false discovery rate <0.05; log2 fold change >1) (Figure 5A,E; supplemental Table 1). Gene set enrichment analysis identified multiple upregulated pathways previously implicated in BTKi resistance, such as oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)28 and proteasome29 signaling (Figure 5B; supplemental Table 2).

Notably, NF-κB signaling was significantly upregulated in PLCG2-mutant cells, with an overexpression of genes such as CCL4, ICAM1, NFKB2, and NFKB1 (Figure 5C-D). Since NF-kB signaling is activated downstream of BCR, we investigated whether this was because of an increase in BCR signaling proteins. However, the members of the canonical BCR signaling pathway, including CD79A, CD79B, LYN, SYK, BLNK, BTK, and PLCG2, showed only minimal expression changes (Figure 5E) compared to WT, again hinting on the role of a constitutively active PLCγ2 mutation.30,31 In addition, recent findings suggest that increased PLCγ2 activity could contribute to mitochondrial respiration in non–small-cell lung cancers,32 which aligns with the upregulation of OXPHOS pathway genes observed in our model (Figure 5C-D). These findings indicate that observed pathway dysregulations may be linked to the presence of activating PLCG2 mutations in these cells.

PLCG2 exon-skipped variant expression in samples of patients with CLL

To investigate whether PLCG2 exon-skipped variants occur in primary human CLL cells, especially in the context of ibrutinib treatment, we screened cDNA samples from 27 patients with CLL who were treated with ibrutinib, using primers covering exons 18-24 of PLCG2. Two truncated bands were detected in addition to the WT PLCG2 band in at least 6 cases independent of their BTK/PLCG2 mutational status (supplemental Figure 3A). The truncated bands were excised, and Sanger sequencing was performed. The obtained sequences corresponded to the Δ22 and Δ20-22 variants, respectively (supplemental Figure 3B); however, NGS data showed no SSM or SSD in the PLCG2 autoinhibitory region in these samples. We selected 2 samples with high expression of the deletion variant and performed long-range PCR on the DNA, but no corresponding genomic deletions were observed (supplemental Figure 3C). Together, these findings suggest a potential posttranscriptional mechanism of exon skipping in these CLL cases.

Interestingly, the Δ22 variant, but not Δ20-22, was detected in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from all HD (n = 8) (supplemental Figure 3D). Although Δ20-22 has been previously reported to enhance PLCγ2 enzyme activity because of loss of the inhibitory domain,16 Δ22, similar to Δ21-22, introduces a premature stop codon, likely resulting in a nonfunctional protein. These findings were validated using RNA sequencing of 6 HD-derived CD19+ B cells and 5 paired patient samples from baseline and relapse after ibrutinib-based treatments. The active Δ20-22 variant was found to be expressed at lower levels than the inactive Δ22 variant. Nevertheless, the Δ20-22 variant was predominantly observed in primary CLL samples at relapse (Figure 6A; supplemental Table 3). A quantitative reverse transcription PCR assay with improved sensitivity was used to study the expression of the exon-skipped variants (supplemental Figure 4A) in a cohort of 40 baseline and relapse samples from patients treated with ibrutinib or venetoclax-based therapy. Reverse transcription quantitative PCR confirmed that both PLCG2 WT and variants were expressed at significantly higher levels at baseline in CLL compared to HD (Figure 6B). In HD, the expression range of Δ22 was 2.01% to 5.07% (median, 3.01%), whereas for Δ20-22 it was 0.12% to 0.58% (median, 0.34%) of WT PLCG2; in CLL at baseline, the expression range of Δ22 was 0.23% to 36.83% (median, 6.2%), whereas for Δ20-22 it was 0% to 4.78% (median, 1.3%) of WT PLCG2. A significant linear correlation between Δ22 and Δ20-22 expression was observed in CLL samples (coefficient of determination [R2] = 0.679; P < .0001), but not in HD samples (R2 = 0.212; P = .252) (Figure 6C). On the other hand, the expression of Δ22 correlated with WT PLCG2 expression in HD samples, which was not observed in CLL (supplemental Figure 4B). These findings suggest that the generation of these exon-skipped variants may be governed by distinct regulatory mechanisms in CLL vs healthy individuals.

Next, we compared expression levels of the Δ20-22 PLCG2 variant in paired CLL samples at baseline and at relapse from patients treated with either ibrutinib or venetoclax (n = 20 each). The relative expression of Δ20-22 (% of WT PLCG2) was unchanged at relapse after ibrutinib, whereas it was significantly decreased after venetoclax-based treatments (Figure 6D; supplemental Figure 5). To evaluate if cells with PLCG2 exon skipping have differential priming toward apoptosis, we performed BCL-2 homology 3 (BH3) profiling on WT and SSM REC-1 cells. SSM mutant REC1 cells showed increased mitochondrial priming to apoptosis as observed by responses to the activator peptides BCL2 interacting mediator of cell death (BIM) and BH3 interacting domain death agonist (BID) peptides. Though functional dependencies on BCLxL and MCL1 were unchanged between WT and SSM, PLCG2-mutant cells showed significantly increased dependency on BCL2, as observed upon exposure to the BCL2 sensitizer peptide: BCL2-associated agonist of cell death (BAD). A difference in mitochondrial sensitivity to venetoclax was observed at high concentrations for venetoclax in the SSM PLCG2 mutant REC1 compared to WT (Figure 6E). These findings suggest that, although exon-skipped variants persist in the presence of BTKi, they may be highly susceptible to apoptosis, a phenomenon that could be therapeutically exploited.

Discussion

Targeting BTK with small-molecule inhibitors33,34 or degraders35 has revolutionized CLL treatment, yet acquired resistance remains a significant challenge. BTKi resistance mechanisms, particularly in cases lacking conventional BTK/PLCG2 driver mutations,2,3,29 remain incompletely understood.7,8 This study aimed to explore the occurrence and biological relevance of PLCG2 splicing variants in the context of resistance to BTKi.

In the REC-1 cell line–based in vitro dose-escalation model,4 which effectively recapitulates mutations detected in patients, we identified a novel PLCG2 SSM c.2236-1G>T acquired upon resistance to tirabrutinib. The relatively high VAFs of the PLCG2 SSM observed in the tirabrutinib-resistant cell line was likely the result of prolonged in vitro drug selection and clonal expansion, differing from that of the clinical setting, where PLCG2 mutations typically occur at lower VAFs.8 Of importance, we also found a novel PLCG2 SSD spanning c.2236-23_2238 in a patient sample that is ibrutinib resistant. Both alterations resulted in 2 exon-skipped variants: Δ21 and Δ21-22. In a small cohort of primary CLL samples, we identified a widespread expression of 2 additional variants: Δ22 and Δ20-22 in the absence of PLCG2 SSDs or SSMs. Similar to PLCG2 point mutations,6,36 the Δ21 and Δ20-2216 variants were previously described to exhibit enhanced phospholipase activity, potentially enabling cells to bypass BTK dependency (Figure 7). The PLCG2 SSM demonstrated cross-resistance to various BTKi and degraders while retaining sensitivity to direct PLC inhibition. This finding is consistent with the structural consequences of loss of the autoinhibitory SH2 domain, as previously observed in PLCγ2-associated antibody deficiency and immune dysregulation syndrome with Δ19 and Δ20-22 variants.15,16 Loss of autoinhibition appears to be a shared mechanism driving constitutive activation across different exon-skipped events.17,19

Exon-skipped variants and common point mutations clustered within the autoinhibitory domain are summarized in distinct boxes and labeled in different colors, as indicated. Exon-skipped variants that introduce a premature stop codon and produce truncated proteins are marked with an asterisk (∗).

Transcriptomic analysis of REC-1 cells harboring splicing or the active L845F mutations revealed significant enrichment of the NF-κB pathway. In addition, the upregulation of OXPHOS and proteasome pathways in PLCG2-mutant cells aligns with previous studies28,29 that established their roles in BTKi resistance. However, these may either be adaptive resistance mechanisms in addition to PLCG2 mutations or a direct consequence of the presence of these mutations.30,32 Our data may also support the latter since we observed similar sensitivities of PLCG2 mutant cells compared to WT when treated with single-agent PLC or PLCγ inhibitors. Our findings thus reveal potential therapeutic vulnerabilities of PLCG2-mutant cells. However, the lack of selective PLCγ2 inhibitors limits our ability to precisely target this enzyme.

Interestingly, the presence of PLCG2 Δ22 in HD suggests that this variant may result from normal PLCG2 splicing mechanism, whereas the disease-specific enrichment of Δ20-22 suggests a pathological shift in splicing regulation in CLL. The recurrence of SF3B1 mutations in CLL highlights the importance of dysregulation of splicing machinery in the pathogenesis and progression of CLL.10,14 Though SF3B1 mutations did not co-occur with Δ20-22 PLCG2, other mechanisms9,11,12 altering RNA splicing may play a role in the generation of the pathological Δ20-22 variant in CLL and not in HD. However, the mechanisms underlying Δ20-22 expression in CLL remain to be solved, and long-read sequencing may be necessary to identify possible roles of deep intronic variants. In ibrutinib-treated CLL, the presence of PLCG2 exon-skipped variants was independent of SSMs or genomic aberrations or gene mutations. However, the differential expression patterns of Δ20-22 in patients progressing on ibrutinib vs venetoclax suggest a potential selective advantage under BTK inhibition. A more direct clinical contribution of these variants to BTKi resistance cannot be verified in the present study because of the lack of clinical data. A possible supportive role of the exon-skipped transcript variants in BTKi resistance therefore needs to be established by analyses of serial samples from BTKi-based clinical trials. On the other hand, the increased apoptotic priming and BCL2 dependency in REC-1 SSM, and the decrease in exon-skipped variants upon change of treatment in primary CLL, may indicate a therapeutic vulnerability that needs to be further explored.

Amplicon-based targeted NGS has been used in different mutational studies on CLL progressing under BTKi treatment.8,37 However, this approach has limitations when it comes to detecting large intronic deletions that span splice sites, particularly when these alterations occur outside the boundaries of predesigned amplicons. This could be addressed by using hybridization-capture–based NGS, which provides broader intronic coverage. Such an approach may potentially reveal previously undetected PLCG2 SSMs and deletions that contribute to BTKi resistance.

This report focusing on PLCG2 SSM, SSD, and exon-skipped variants in CLL expands our knowledge of the spectrum of PLCG2 alterations in BTKi resistance beyond canonical BTK/PLCG2 mutations. Our findings from the REC-1 model indicate that splice sites in PLCG2 should be included in NGS panels in clinical diagnostics to study resistance to BTKi and degraders. Developing selective PLCγ2 inhibitors may be valuable for overcoming resistance to BTKi. Future studies with larger patient cohorts are warranted to establish their potential as biomarkers for treatment selection and resistance prediction.
