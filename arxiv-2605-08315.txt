Reflective Prompted Policy Optimization: Trajectory-Grounded Revision and Salience Bias
Rahaf Abu Hara, Vaibbhav Murarri, Claudio Zito

Abstract
Existing LLM-based policy optimizers see only scalar rewards: that a policy scored 0.45, but not whether the agent got stuck in a loop, fell into a hole on the third step, or performed well on 19 out of 20 rollouts and failed catastrophically on one. We propose Reflective Prompted Policy Optimization (R2PO), a two-stage LLM framework for policy search over compact policy classes that augments scalar reward feedback with trajectory-level behavioral evidence. A Search-LLM acts as a global policy optimizer and proposes candidate policy parameters; the environment executes them; a Critic-LLM then inspects the resulting rollouts and proposes targeted parameter revisions grounded in observed states, actions, and rewards. Across ten environments, ablations show R2PO’s gains arise from a design that explicitly separates global search from behavior-grounded revision and uses selection to filter high-variance edits. We further identify a dominant failure mode, salience bias . When presented with multiple rollouts, the Critic-LLM fixates on improving a single failure even when most trajectories succeed. In a three-trajectory variant, where the Critic-LLM is shown the best, worst, and median rollout from each evaluation, this behavior explains 76.6% of regressions on CartPole. R2PO mitigates this by reasoning over aggregate rollout statistics, median-trajectory selection, and a revision rule. Using a relatively small open-weight 20B-parameter model, R2PO achieves the highest mean best reward across all ten environments, while reaching near-optimal performance substantially earlier in training (e.g., near-maximum CartPole reward within ∼ \sim 500 episodes), and training far more stably than both deep RL and prior LLM-based methods. Together, these results show that treating trajectories as first-class in-context evidence, rather than background artifacts reduced to scalar returns, fundamentally changes how even comparatively small LLMs can search over policy spaces, enabling them to learn faster, diagnose more precisely, and reliably improve external controllers rather than tune them by trial and error.



1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning has made substantial progress on policy optimization, but standard algorithms require careful hyperparameter tuning and millions of environment interactions to converge, with performance sensitive to implementation details. Recent work has shown that large language models can serve as black-box optimizers for policy search, consuming histories of parameter-reward pairs and proposing new candidate policies through in-context learning
[
22
,
23
]
. These methods treat the LLM as an optimizer over the policy parameter space, requiring no task-specific algorithmic design.

However, existing LLM-based policy optimizers operate on scalar reward feedback alone. The optimizer sees
that
a policy scored 0.45, but not
why
: whether the agent got stuck in a loop, fell into a hole on the third step, or performed well on 19 out of 20 rollouts but catastrophically failed on one. Scalar feedback provides no diagnostic signal about what went wrong, limiting the optimizer to trial-and-error tuning rather than targeted correction.

We introduce
Reflective Prompted Policy Optimization (R2PO)
, a two-stage LLM framework for policy search over compact policy classes (linear policies for continuous control and tabular policies for discrete tasks). A
Search-LLM
acts as a global policy optimizer: given the history of evaluated policies and their scalar rewards, it proposes a candidate policy parameter vector. The candidate is executed in the environment, producing rollout trajectories that capture states, actions, and rewards. A
Critic-LLM
then inspects this evidence and proposes a targeted parameter revision. Finally, a selection step compares the original and revised candidates and keeps whichever achieves the higher reward, limiting damage from over-corrections or LLM hallucinations.

Trajectory-grounded diagnosis is a powerful tool that enables better decisions than scalar-only feedback, but it can also cause sharp regressions when the trajectories used for diagnosis are unrepresentative. We identify a recurring failure pattern,
salience bias
: when the Critic-LLM is shown multiple trajectories from the same policy, it treats the worst-case trajectory as representative, even when the policy is working well. We mitigate salience bias by feeding the Critic-LLM a more representative view of policy behavior: (i) aggregate rollout statistics that summarize overall performance, (ii) a median trajectory that reflects typical rather than worst-case behavior, and (iii) a revision rule that preserves policies that already perform well.

Our contributions are:

•

R2PO
, a two-stage LLM policy-optimization framework that combines scalar reward search with trajectory-grounded behavioral diagnosis, treating rollouts as first-class in-context evidence.

•

A systematic ablation
across ten environments over search budget, prompt design, two-stage architecture, selection, and trajectory evidence, showing that R2PO’s gains cannot be explained by more LLM calls or richer prompts alone, but require the combination of trajectory-grounded revision, two-stage design, and keep-best selection.

•

A mechanistic analysis
identifying and quantifying a dominant failure mode of trajectory-grounded LLM revision,
salience bias
, in which the Critic-LLM overweights vivid worst-case rollouts when multiple trajectories are available, accounting for 76.6% of regressions in a three-trajectory setting on CartPole.

•

Empirical validation
using a 20B open-weight LLM: R2PO achieves the highest mean reward on 9 of 10 environments and ties on the 10th, matches or exceeds all baselines on mean best reward, reaches near-optimal performance substantially earlier in training, and trains more stably than both deep RL and prior LLM-based baselines.

Related Work

Recent work integrates LLMs into RL pipelines in two main ways. One line uses LLMs to shape components of the RL loop, such as reward functions, action selection, or auxiliary policy guidance, while leaving optimization to conventional RL algorithms
[
6
,
8
,
4
,
21
,
24
]
. In these approaches, the LLM acts as an auxiliary module rather than as the optimizer itself.

A second line places the LLM at the center of optimization. OPRO
[
22
]
uses LLMs to optimize arbitrary objective functions through iterative prompting; ProPS
[
23
]
extends this to RL by consuming parameter-reward histories and proposing policy updates directly. Our work operates in this regime, but differs fundamentally: rather than optimizing from scalar feedback alone, we introduce a second LLM stage that reasons over the proposed policy’s actual rollout behavior, enabling targeted diagnosis of failure modes that scalar rewards cannot reveal.

Self-improvement and reflection methods for LLMs are also related. Self-Refine
[
9
]
performs iterative generate-critique-revise loops for single outputs; Reflexion
[
18
]
extends this to agents by converting task feedback into verbal reflections stored in episodic memory. In Reflexion, the LLM
is
the policy and iteratively retries the same task using verbal reflections from prior attempts.
In R2PO, the LLM is an
optimizer
of separate policy parameters: the Critic-LLM diagnoses an external policy’s rollout behavior and produces a concrete parameter vector evaluated against the original.

A recent direction uses trajectories as supervision for LLM
training
via gradient updates: TreeRL
[
5
]
adds on-policy tree search with process supervision, and TSR
[
1
]
constructs higher-quality training trajectories through lightweight tree-style search. R2PO instead uses rollout trajectories as
in-context evidence
to revise external policies, with no gradient computation or fine-tuning.

3
Methodology

3.1
Problem setting

We consider episodic policy search with parameterized policies
\pi_{\theta}
and the objective of maximizing expected return
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[R(\tau)]
, where
\tau
is a trajectory generated by
\pi_{\theta}
. We focus on compact policy classes whose parameter vectors fit within an LLM context window: linear policies
\pi_{\theta}(s)=\theta^{\top}\phi(s)
for continuous-state tasks (
\arg\max

over action logits for discrete actions) and tabular policies
\pi_{\theta}(s)=\theta_{s}
, with
\theta_{s}\in\mathcal{A}
, for discrete-state tasks. Extending R2PO to high-dimensional neural policies is an important direction for future work (Section
5
).

Given a parameter vector
\theta
, we evaluate it by running
K
independent environment rollouts and averaging their returns. We denote this evaluation

\textsc{Eval}(\theta)\rightarrow(\bar{R},\mathcal{T}),

where
\bar{R}
is the mean return and
\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{K}\}
is the set of recorded trajectories.

3.2
The R2PO framework

R2PO uses two LLMs in distinct roles (Figure
1
). The Search-LLM is the
global policy optimizer
: given the history of previously evaluated policies and their scalar mean rewards, it proposes where to move next in the parameter space. Scalar history suffices at this global level, where candidates are compared on a uniform ranking signal; trajectory-grounded reasoning is reserved for the local revision step. The Critic-LLM is the
trajectory-based reviser
: given a calibrated summary of the behavior observed when the Search-LLM’s proposal was executed, it inspects what went wrong and proposes a targeted revision. In practice, both roles use the
same
underlying 20B model, but via two independent calls with distinct prompts tailored to their respective roles.

Stage 1: Search-LLM proposal.

At iteration
t
, the Search-LLM is prompted with the optimization task and a reward-only history
H_{t}=\{(\theta_{i},\bar{R}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
, and proposes an initial parameter vector
\theta_{t}^{(0)}
, which the environment evaluates as
(\bar{R}_{t}^{(0)},\mathcal{T}_{t}^{(0)})
.

Stage 2: Critic-LLM reflection.

The Critic-LLM is prompted with a natural-language environment description, the proposed parameters
\theta_{t}^{(0)}
, the same reward-only history
H_{t}
, and a calibrated trajectory evidence package built from the
K
rollouts in
\mathcal{T}_{t}^{(0)}
. From this evidence, it proposes a revised parameter vector
\theta_{t}^{(1)}
. The evidence package has three components.

(i) Median trajectory.
From the
K
rollouts, we select the rollout whose return is closest to the median of the
K
returns (interpolated when
K
is even). This provides a concrete example of typical policy behavior, avoiding both best-case and worst-case outliers.

(ii) Aggregate rollout statistics.
A structured statistical report computed over all
K
rollouts: reward mean, minimum, and maximum; episode length mean, minimum, and maximum; and success and failure rates. These aggregates convey how often the policy fails and by how much without placing a specific failed execution trace in front of the Critic-LLM.

(iii) Revision rule.
We add an explicit abstention instruction to the Critic-LLM’s prompt, controlled by a threshold
\tau_{c}
. If the initial mean reward
\bar{R}_{t}^{(0)}
exceeds
\tau_{c}
and the rollout statistics do not indicate a consistent failure pattern, the Critic-LLM is instructed to keep the parameters unchanged or make only a very small, strongly justified edit. The threshold
\tau_{c}
is set per environment based on the observable reward scale (see Appendix
F
).

Selection.

We evaluate the revised parameters and keep whichever candidate performs better:

\theta_{t}=\begin{cases}\theta_{t}^{(1)}&\text{if }\bar{R}_{t}^{(1)}\geq\bar{R}_{t}^{(0)},\\
\theta_{t}^{(0)}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(1)

The selected parameters and their mean reward are appended to the history for subsequent iterations. Algorithm
1
in Appendix
E
summarizes the full R2PO loop.

4.3
Ablation study and decomposition

R2PO combines (i) a two-stage LLM pipeline, (ii) trajectory-grounded behavioral evidence, (iii) a reward-only replay history, (iv) a revision rule, and (v) a keep-best selection mechanism. We ask two questions:
(1) which components are actually necessary for the observed gains?
and
(2) given a two-stage architecture, how should rollout evidence be summarized for the Critic-LLM?
We address (1) using architectural ablations (Section
4.3.1
) and (2) using trajectory-evidence variants that differ only in what the Critic-LLM sees (Section
4.3.2
).
Table
3
reports mean reward across all ten environments; mean best reward (Appendix
H
) shows the same overall pattern.

4.3.1
Decomposition: are all components of R2PO necessary?

We design four ablation variants, each removing or altering a single aspect of R2PO.

Is it just extra budget?

PureSearch
draws two proposals from the same Search-LLM context and keeps the better one, doubling the search budget without structural change.
ActorSecondPass
runs the scalar-only optimizer twice in sequence: the second call sees the first call’s result and reward but no trajectory information. Both variants test whether R2PO’s gains reduce to additional LLM calls or a generic sequential structure. Both are consistently among the weakest methods, failing systematically on Maze and Nim. Extra budget, whether parallel or sequential, does not explain R2PO’s gains.

Is it just the richer prompt?

CriticOnly
replaces the two-stage loop with a single rich Critic-style prompt that internally performs draft–critique–revise within one LLM call, receiving scalar history and environment description but never actual rollouts. This isolates prompt design from the effect of having a separate reviser that reasons over realized behavior. CriticOnly is the strongest single-factor variant, yet R2PO outperforms it on all 10 environments, with large gaps on Swimmer (
\sim
260 vs.
\sim
55) and InvertedPendulum (
\sim
756 vs.
\sim
127). A richer prompt helps but cannot substitute for a two-stage design in which the reviser accesses execution behavior.

Is selection necessary?

AlwaysCritic
is identical to the two-stage variant RepTraj (Section
4.3.2
, which uses a single representative rollout as trajectory evidence), except that it always commits the Critic-LLM’s revision without selection. RepTraj outperforms AlwaysCritic on all 10 environments, often by large margins (InvertedPendulum:
\sim
380 vs.
\sim
141; MountainCarContinuous:
\sim
-94
vs.
\sim
-131
). With architecture and evidence held constant, committing all revisions unconditionally introduces large variance and frequent regressions. We investigate why trajectory evidence fails to help when revisions are unconditional in Section
4.4
, showing that it reflects high-variance, trajectory-conditioned edits often based on unrepresentative rollouts.

Summary.

No single ablation recovers R2PO’s performance. Extra LLM calls (PureSearch, ActorSecondPass), richer prompts alone (CriticOnly), and two-stage reasoning without selection (AlwaysCritic) are each insufficient. R2PO’s gains arise from the
combination
of (i) a two-stage architecture separating global proposal from local revision, (ii) trajectory-grounded reasoning in the reviser, and (iii) a keep-best selection rule that filters destructive edits.

4.3.2
Trajectory representation: how should the Critic-LLM see rollouts?

With the two-stage architecture and selection rule fixed, the remaining design question is how to summarize
K
rollouts into a compact representation that fits in context and remains interpretable to the Critic-LLM. Feeding all
K
trajectories as raw traces would exceed typical context limits and, in our pilots, led to incoherent edits; we therefore focus on compact summaries that preserve the key behavioral patterns. Providing only the scalar mean discards the behavioral information that motivates R2PO. We compare three structured representations, which differ only in how they summarize
\mathcal{T}_{t}^{(0)}
.

RepTraj: single representative trajectory.

RepTraj
selects the rollout whose return is closest to the mean across the
K
rollouts and passes it as a raw trajectory trace, together with the full reward and trajectory history. The Critic-LLM is always asked to revise, with no revision rule. This tests the simplest use of trajectory evidence: one approximately typical example.

ThreeTraj: best, worst, and median trajectories.

ThreeTraj
aims to improve representativeness by exposing the Critic-LLM to the range of behaviors observed: it passes the highest-, lowest-, and median-reward trajectories from the
K
rollouts, with reward-only history
H_{t}
. The Critic-LLM is again always asked to revise. This tests whether richer within-policy evidence yields better corrections.

R2PO: median trajectory + aggregate statistics + Revision rule.

R2PO shows the Critic-LLM (i) the median-reward trajectory, (ii) aggregate statistics over
K
rollouts (reward mean/min/max, length mean/min/max, success/failure counts), and (iii) a revision instruction (threshold
\tau_{c}
) that preserves the policy when performance is already strong. Unlike RepTraj, R2PO does not show extreme rollouts; unlike ThreeTraj, it communicates failure frequency numerically rather than by foregrounding a worst-case trace.

Empirical comparison.

As Tables
3
and
11
show, R2PO outperforms RepTraj on all 10 environments in mean reward. ThreeTraj achieves slightly higher mean reward than R2PO on 2 discrete stochastic environments (Nim and FrozenLake) but is clearly worse elsewhere, especially on continuous-control tasks (Swimmer:
\sim
110
vs.
\sim
260
; InvertedPendulum:
\sim
194
vs.
\sim
756
). Section
4.4
shows why naive multi-trajectory conditioning can hurt: when rollouts disagree, the Critic-LLM over-focuses on vivid worst-case traces, a phenomenon we call
salience bias
. R2PO’s evidence design preserves the benefits of trajectory-grounded diagnosis while reducing this vulnerability.

4.4
Mechanistic analysis

The ablations raise a key question:
why
is trajectory-grounded revision so high-variance that it needs both selection and calibration? AlwaysCritic and CriticOnly perform similarly even though AlwaysCritic sees more information, and ThreeTraj, which sees
three
rollouts, does worse. To investigate, we focus on CartPole and Pong—where CriticOnly outperforms AlwaysCritic—and use AlwaysCritic as our main diagnostic, since it isolates trajectory-based revision without selection or calibration. For each environment, we analyze 1,000 revision decisions (10 runs, 100 iterations).

Trajectory-grounded diagnosis is high-variance.

On Pong, 31.6% of AlwaysCritic iterations are regressions; on CartPole, 34%. The same diagnostic mechanism, the Critic-LLM reading a trajectory, identifying a failure point, and making a fix, produces both the largest improvements and regressions.

The root cause: unrepresentative trajectories.

Under AlwaysCritic, the Critic-LLM sees only one rollout out of
K{=}20
. In stochastic environments, a single rollout is a
sample
of policy behavior, not the complete picture. When the trajectory reflects typical behavior, the Critic-LLM’s diagnosis generalizes and the fix improves the policy. When the trajectory shows an atypical outcome (an unlucky termination, an uncharacteristic failure), the Critic-LLM diagnoses a non-existent problem and destroys behavior that was working in the 19 unseen rollouts. The Critic-LLM cannot distinguish these cases without information about how often the observed failure actually occurs.

Failure patterns.

We categorize AlwaysCritic regressions into four patterns (full descriptions in Appendix
I.1
):
surgical fixes that backfire
,
compulsive revision of near-optimal policies
,
full redesigns on uninformative trajectories
, and
pure stochasticity
. The dominant pattern, compulsive revision, accounts for
12.6\%
of episodes on Pong and
14.2\%
on CartPole; pure stochasticity accounts for
<1\%
, confirming noise is not the main driver.

Three trajectories and salience bias.

We tested giving the Critic-LLM the best, worst, and median rollouts (the ThreeTraj variant) to address representativeness. This should, in principle, help distinguish systematic failures from outliers. On Pong, ThreeTraj achieves the worst mean delta per iteration (
+0.16
) despite the largest edits (mean edit distance
8.4
), versus RepTraj’s
+0.64
at distance
6.2
. On CartPole, the same pattern holds: ThreeTraj
+5.6
at distance
4.6
vs. RepTraj
+16.0
at distance
4.1
.

To understand why, we coded all 2,000 ThreeTraj decisions on CartPole
and Pong by scanning the Critic-LLM’s reasoning text
(Appendix
I
). We define a
salience-problem
regression
as an episode where (i) the Critic-LLM’s reasoning explicitly
references the worst rollout, (ii) the worst rollout’s return is strictly
lower than the median (so the worst is genuinely an outlier), and (iii)
the revision hurt performance (
\Delta<0
). On CartPole, 233 of 304
regressions (
76.6\%
) meet this strict definition. On Pong, where
rollouts more often agree on a failure (worst
=
median), ThreeTraj
instead produces a positive mean delta of
+0.62
on those episodes.

Salience bias.

When shown multiple rollouts of a policy, the Critic-LLM systematically overweights the worst trajectory in its diagnosis and edits, even when most rollouts indicate good performance.

We observe this directly. In a Pong episode, rollout rewards were
2.0
,
3.0
,
3.0
(mean
2.95
). The Critic-LLM organized its revision around the worst rollout: “
From the worst rollout we saw that the policy never moved…this indicates that the do-nothing weights are too dominant.
” It changed six parameters; reward dropped to
0.15
. Better-performing rollouts existed among the median and best, yet the Critic-LLM anchored on the worst trajectory. This pattern motivates R2PO’s evidence design: failure frequency is conveyed through aggregate statistics rather than a worst-case trace.

R2PO produces focused, high-quality revisions.

Table
4
reports revision outcomes across the three trajectory-evidence variants; full edit-pattern frequencies are in Appendix
I.2
. R2PO achieves the highest mean improvement per iteration (
+45.3
on CartPole,
+0.87
on Pong), substantially outperforming RepTraj (
+16.0
,
+0.64
) and ThreeTraj (
+5.6
,
+0.16
), while making the
smallest
edits (mean edit distance
1.6
on CartPole,
4.5
on Pong). It also achieves the highest acceptance rate (
86
–
88\%
) and lowest regression rate (
12
–
14\%
). The pattern breakdown attributes these gains: Near-Optimal Break drops
6\times
on CartPole and
17\times
on Pong (Revision rule); Surgical Backfire drops
7\times
on CartPole and
3.5\times
on Pong (rollout statistics preventing unrepresentative-trace fixes); productive Fine-Tune edits nearly triple on CartPole (
4.8\%\to 13.4\%
). Consistency across both environments confirms these mechanisms are not environment-specific.

The broader implication is that the main challenge in leveraging trajectories is not
whether
to use them, but
how
to summarize them so the LLM can distinguish systematic failures from rare salient mistakes.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

We introduced R2PO, a two-stage LLM framework that separates global policy proposal (Search-LLM) from local behavior-grounded revision (Critic-LLM), together with a calibrated trajectory evidence package and a selection rule. Our central finding is that rollout trajectories provide a fundamentally richer feedback channel for LLM-based policy optimization than scalar rewards alone, but only when presented carefully: naively passing trajectories to the reviser triggers
salience bias
, in which the LLM overweights vivid worst-case rollouts and over-corrects already-strong policies. R2PO’s calibrated evidence package—median trajectory, aggregate statistics, and revision rule—mitigates this bias and yields a more stable and effective optimizer than scalar-only prompting-based search. These results are achieved using a 20B open-weight model, indicating that the benefits of behavior-grounded reflective policy search are not limited to large proprietary systems.

Limitations.

We focus on compact policy classes (tabular and linear); extension to high-dimensional neural policies remains untested. R2PO also uses an environment-dependent threshold
\tau_{c}
that currently requires coarse task-specific calibration. Although Appendix
B
shows robustness to
\pm 50\%
variation and competitiveness without this rule, automatic calibration remains unresolved.

Future work.

A first priority is extending R2PO beyond compact controllers. Promising directions include low-dimensional projections of neural network parameters (cf.
[
23
]
, App. E) and structured policy classes such as compositional or hierarchical controllers, where parameter substructure could provide the Critic-LLM with semantically meaningful revision targets. A second direction is adaptive calibration of
\tau_{c}
from rollout statistics and uncertainty signals, eliminating even the coarse manual setting required today. More broadly, salience bias may affect other settings in which LLMs reason over multiple evidence sources, such as multi-document QA, code review, and multi-turn agents; characterizing this bias beyond policy optimization remains open.

Appendix A
Significance Tests for Mean-Reward Comparisons

To complement the descriptive comparison of reward across methods, this appendix reports significance tests for both
mean reward
and
mean best reward
.

For each environment
e
and method
m
, let

x^{(e)}_{m,1},x^{(e)}_{m,2},\ldots,x^{(e)}_{m,n_{m}}

denote the
per-run
mean rewards, where each
x^{(e)}_{m,i}
is the mean reward of run
i
for method
m
on environment
e
, averaged across all training iterations, and
n_{m}=10
in our experiments. Thus, the test compares the 10 run-level summary values for R2PO against the 10 run-level summary values for a baseline. The sample mean and (Bessel-corrected) sample variance for method
m
are

\bar{x}^{(e)}_{m}=\frac{1}{n_{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{m}}x^{(e)}_{m,i},\qquad\left(s^{(e)}_{m}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{m}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{m}}\left(x^{(e)}_{m,i}-\bar{x}^{(e)}_{m}\right)^{2}.

The standard deviations reported in the main-paper descriptive tables (e.g., Tables
1
and
2
) use this same Bessel-corrected sample-variance convention. The reported performance gap is

\Delta^{(e)}=\bar{x}^{(e)}_{\text{R2PO}}-\bar{x}^{(e)}_{\text{baseline}}.

Because the methods were evaluated with independent runs (rather than paired across methods) and exhibited unequal variances in several environments, we use two-sided Welch’s t-tests.

t^{(e)}=\frac{\bar{x}^{(e)}_{\text{R2PO}}-\bar{x}^{(e)}_{\text{baseline}}}{\sqrt{\frac{\left(s^{(e)}_{\text{R2PO}}\right)^{2}}{n_{\text{R2PO}}}+\frac{\left(s^{(e)}_{\text{baseline}}\right)^{2}}{n_{\text{baseline}}}}},

with Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom

\nu^{(e)}=\frac{\left(\frac{\left(s^{(e)}_{\text{R2PO}}\right)^{2}}{n_{\text{R2PO}}}+\frac{\left(s^{(e)}_{\text{baseline}}\right)^{2}}{n_{\text{baseline}}}\right)^{2}}{\frac{\left(\frac{\left(s^{(e)}_{\text{R2PO}}\right)^{2}}{n_{\text{R2PO}}}\right)^{2}}{n_{\text{R2PO}}-1}+\frac{\left(\frac{\left(s^{(e)}_{\text{baseline}}\right)^{2}}{n_{\text{baseline}}}\right)^{2}}{n_{\text{baseline}}-1}}.

The corresponding raw two-sided p-value is

p^{(e)}_{\mathrm{raw}}=2\,\Pr\!\left(T_{\nu^{(e)}}\geq\left|t^{(e)}\right|\right).

Intuitively, a large positive
t
means that the R2PO runs, viewed as one group, have a higher mean than the baseline runs, viewed as another group, and that this gap is large relative to how much the runs fluctuate within each method. A negative
t
means the baseline group’s mean is higher.

We compare R2PO against
all
core baselines for each environment. That
is, for every environment we test R2PO against ProPS, ProPS+, and the corresponding SB3 baseline. Holm correction is then applied
separately within each baseline family across the 10 environments
; that is, one Holm correction for the 10 R2PO-vs-ProPS comparisons, one for the 10 R2PO-vs-ProPS+ comparisons, and one for the 10 R2PO-vs-SB3 comparisons. If the raw p-values within one such family, sorted in ascending order, are

p_{(1)},\ldots,p_{(10)}
, the Holm-adjusted values are computed as

p^{\mathrm{Holm}}_{(k)}=\max_{j\leq k}\left((10-j+1)\,p_{(j)}\right),

clipped to
[0,1]
. The raw p-value answers whether a single comparison looks significant on its own; the Holm-adjusted p-value answers whether that comparison remains significant after accounting for the 10-environment family of comparisons against the same baseline type.

Table
5
reports the resulting comparisons for
mean reward. The results are strong. After Holm correction, R2PO significantly
outperforms 28 of the 30 core baseline comparisons. The only non-significant
cases are MountainCarContinuous against SB3 (SAC), where SB3 remains slightly
better in mean reward, and Maze against SB3 (A2C), where the two methods are
effectively tied in mean reward. In contrast, R2PO significantly outperforms ProPS and ProPS+
on all 10 environments.

Table
6
reports the corresponding
comparisons for mean best reward. Although only 10 of the 30 mean-best-reward comparisons remain significant after Holm correction, R2PO still matches or exceeds all core baselines descriptively, with positive gaps in most non-significant cases.

These tests reinforce, rather than replace, the descriptive reward tables. The
main value of the significance analysis is to show that R2PO’s higher mean
reward is usually not an artifact of run-to-run noise.

Salience analysis details

To quantify the salience bias observed in the ThreeTraj variant, we
systematically analyzed all 2,000 ThreeTraj episodes across CartPole
and Pong (1,000 each, 10 independent runs per environment). For each
episode, we parsed Critic-LLM’s reasoning text from the logged output,
checking whether the worst rollout was explicitly mentioned, and computed
the reward delta
\Delta=R_{\text{revised}}-R_{\text{initial}}
.

Operational definition.

An episode is classified as a

salience-problem regression
if all of the following hold:

1.

Critic-LLM’s reasoning explicitly references the worst rollout.

2.

The worst rollout’s return is strictly lower than the median
(
strict
criterion), so the worst is genuinely an outlier rather
than the modal outcome.

3.

The revision hurt performance:
\Delta<0
.

We also report a
permissive
variant that relaxes condition (2)
to require only that the worst be no greater than the median and
strictly less than the best. The strict criterion isolates
outlier-driven misdiagnosis; the permissive criterion additionally
counts cases where the worst trajectory reflects a
\geq 50\%
failure
mode but is still cited by the Critic-LLM as the central evidence for
revision.

I.1
Failure pattern definitions

We categorize AlwaysCritic regressions into four patterns:

•

Surgical fixes that backfire
: a 1–3 parameter edit is locally correct for the observed trace but disrupts behavior in other rollouts.

•

Compulsive revision of near-optimal policies
: the Critic-LLM perturbs a high-reward policy citing risks that have not manifested (
12.6\%
of episodes on Pong,
14.2\%
on CartPole).

•

Full redesigns on uninformative trajectories
: near-zero policies whose short rollout traces prompt the Critic-LLM to redesign the policy from scratch.

•

Pure stochasticity
: identical revisions yield different rewards due to rollout variance (
<1\%
of episodes).

I.2
Edit pattern frequencies

Table

Appendix L
R2PO Prompt Templates

We provide the prompts used by the Search-LLM and the Critic-LLM. The Search-LLM prompt closely follows the ProPS prompt design
[
23
]
, which we adopt as the global-optimizer stage of our two-stage framework. Our novel contribution is the Critic-LLM prompt, which introduces a calibrated trajectory evidence package (median rollout, aggregate rollout statistics) and a revision rule controlled by
\tau_{c}
that instructs the model to preserve already-strong policies. Separate variants are used for continuous (linear policy) and discrete (tabular policy) tasks. Placeholders in angle brackets (e.g.,
<RANK>
,
<HISTORY>
) are filled at runtime.

The Critic-LLM prompt includes an environment description (
<ENV_DESCRIPTION>
). We use the same environment descriptions as
[
23
]
(Appendix L), with minor corrections to typos and, in some cases, to the policy parameterization (e.g., the stated number of parameters).
The custom environments (Maze, Nim, and Pong) follow the specifications of
[
23
]
, which we restate for self-containment in Appendix
K
.

L.1
Fixed-value placeholders

Two placeholders represent fixed optimization conventions held constant
across all environments without per-task tuning:

•

<STEP_SIZE>

=1.0
. Used in the continuous-policy
Search-LLM prompt as a suggested exploration step, and in the
continuous-policy Critic-LLM prompt as a soft upper bound on per-parameter
adjustments unless evidence justifies a larger change. Not used by the
discrete-policy prompts.

•

Continuous parameter range
[-6.0,6.0]
with one-decimal precision,
hard-coded in both continuous prompts. This range comfortably contains
the optimal parameter scales observed in preliminary runs across all
continuous-state environments and is held fixed across all methods,
including the ProPS and ProPS+ baselines, for matched-budget comparison.
