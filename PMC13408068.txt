Technology Access, Pattern of Use, Interest in Digital Health, and Barriers to Tobacco Treatment Among HIV-Infected Smokers From Under-Resourced Communities: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Olusanya J Oluwole, Tulay Koru-Sengul, Deborah L Jones, Maria L Alcaide, David J Lee, Adam Carrico, Taghrid Asfar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCigarette smoking remains a major public health concern among people living with HIV (PLWH), particularly those from under-resourced communities. Digital health may improve access to cessation support, yet its potential among this population remains understudied.ObjectiveThis study explored barriers to quitting smoking and evaluated the potential of digital health for tobacco treatment among PWLH smokers.MethodsWe surveyed 205 PLWH who smoked cigarettes (45.4% female; mean age 51.2 years; response rate 92.3%) to examine smoking behaviors, cessation barriers, technology access, use pattern, and interest in digital health for tobacco treatment. Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to assess how demographics and smoking behaviors influence interest in using digital health (phone calls, text messages, smartphone applications, or combinations) for tobacco treatment.ResultsParticipants were primarily racial and ethnic minorities, including 55.1% non-Hispanic Black and 34.2% Hispanic individuals, with 74.4% earning less than $10,000 annually. Most participants reported previous quit attempts (76.5%). Common barriers to quitting included difficulty managing cravings (45.4%), cost of treatment (28.8%), and limited access to care (25.9%), while depression (58.9%) and risky alcohol use (39.6%) were also prevalent. Access to technology was high: 91.7% owned a cell phone, 61.0% owned a smartphone, and 63.9% reported internet access. Among smartphone owners, 74.0% used mobile applications. Phone calls were the most preferred single digital health modality for tobacco treatment (51.7%), followed by text messaging (43.9%), smartphone applications (39.0%), and video conferencing (38.5%). Preferred intervention methods included mindfulness strategies (73.0%), nicotine replacement therapy (71.1%), and counseling (63–65%). Non-Hispanic Black and non-heterosexual participants showed significantly higher interest in digital health for tobacco treatment, with aOR 5.49 (1.44 – 20.90) and 6.02 (1.46 – 24.82), respectively.Practice ImplicationsDespite barriers, PLWH smokers are interested in digital health for tobacco treatment. Incorporating digital tools could improve access and reduce disparities in this population.Keywords: HIV infections, health services accessibility, healthcare disparities, tobacco use cessation, and digital health

Introduction

Due to advances in HIV treatment, life expectancy and overall quality of life for people living with HIV (PLWH) have improved significantly.1-4 However, cigarette smoking remains a serious public health problem among PLWH in the United States (US), with a prevalence two to three times higher than the general population (36% - 65% vs. 15%, respectively).2,5-9 This is concerning because smoking accounts for 24% of the overall deaths among PLWH.10-12 In particular, socially disadvantaged PLWH are at higher risk for smoking.2,7,9 Compared to the general population, PLWH were more likely to live below the poverty line (31.9% vs. 25%) or come from a low-income background.12,13 The prevalence of cigarette smoking was higher among Black PLWH compared to Blacks who do not have the infection (47% vs. 13%). 12 Cravings, exposure to smokers, lack of education, unemployment, other substance use, and limited access to treatment are key factors driving the high smoking rate in this group.14,15

Although technological advancements have made digital health tools such as the internet, video conferencing, cell phones, text messaging, smartphone apps, and websites widely available, under-resourced minorities who represent most PLWH might face significant barriers to accessing these technologies.16-18 Smoking cessation interventions employing mobile health (mHealth), a subset of digital health that uses mobile devices and wireless technologies to deliver health services, have demonstrated higher short-term smoking cessation rates than traditional face-to-face methods among PLWH. 19 Furthermore, strong evidence shows that combining digital tools with face-to-face support dramatically enhances outcomes among individuals with psychiatric and substance use disorders.20-25 These digital tools are important because they can improve access to tobacco treatment, reduce financial barriers, and offer scalable, personalized, low-cost support options that complement traditional behavioral interventions and are relevant to underserved communities.26-28 However, the digital divide can exacerbate existing health disparities, as those who need support most may face structural barriers to accessing these tools. 16

While digital health interventions offer an attractive alternative to traditional face-to-face counseling, their uptake may be highly variable, particularly among under-resourced PLWH. In general, studies have demonstrated increased motivation to quit among PLWH with the use of technology. For example, Brinker et al found that individuals who were exposed to a face-aging app – which projected how their face might appear with or without cigarette smoking over the next 1 to 15 years – were more motivated to quit. 29 Also, a randomized controlled trial by Gritz et al found that cellphone-delivered counseling was over twice as effective as usual care in helping PLWH quit smoking, highlighting the potential of tailored mobile interventions in this population. 30

Research on the accessibility and usage of digital health for tobacco treatment is still emerging among under-resourced PLWH smokers. Therefore, understanding this population’s access to digital technologies, patterns of use, interest in digital health interventions for tobacco treatment, motivation to quit, and barriers to treatment is crucial for assessing the potential reach and impact of these initiatives. This study explores three areas: smoking behavior, including motivation to quit and perceived barriers to treatment; access to digital technology, usage patterns, and interest in digital health for tobacco treatment; and interest in evidence-based tobacco treatment methods. It also assesses whether interest in digital health interventions and barriers to treatment differ between male and female smokers in this high-risk, under-resourced population. 31 We hypothesized that under-resourced PLWH smokers would (1) report substantial access to digital technologies and interest in digital tobacco treatment, (2) experience multiple barriers to tobacco treatment, and (3) exhibit sex-based differences in treatment interest and perceived barriers.

Methods

Study Design, Participant Recruitment, and Procedures

We conducted a cross-sectional survey among 205 PLWH who smoke cigarettes between March 2019 and April 2020. Eligible participants were at least 18 years old, fluent in English or Spanish, current cigarette smokers, and living with HIV. Individuals were excluded if they were younger than 18 years of age, unable to provide informed consent, or incarcerated. Potential participants were identified through an institutional HIV registry, affiliated research databases, and electronic medical records within an academic medical center in the United States. Individuals with HIV and documented smoking histories were considered as potential participants.

Potential participants were contacted by trained research staff to assess interest and eligibility. Individuals who had previously consented to be contacted for research were approached by telephone. Additional potential participants identified through electronic medical records were mailed study information and subsequently contacted by telephone by study staff. During the telephone contact, study staff described the study, screened interested individuals for eligibility, and invited eligible participants to enroll. Of the 222 individuals screened for eligibility, 205 were enrolled and completed the survey, yielding a response rate of 92.3%. Participants who agreed to participate provided verbal informed consent and authorized access to relevant medical records to confirm eligibility and access viral load and CD4 counts. Because all study procedures were conducted by telephone, verbal informed consent was obtained using an IRB-approved consent script. Participants were assured of confidentiality and that their information would be used solely for research purposes.

Following consent, participants completed a structured telephone-administered questionnaire, and responses were recorded in REDCap, a secure, HIPAA-compliant electronic data capture system.32,33 The survey included validated measures where available, as well as study-specific items assessing tobacco treatment preferences, cessation barriers, and digital health access, use, and interest. The full questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Material. Interviews lasted approximately 15–20 minutes, and participants received $15 for participation.

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. Instead, all eligible participants recruited during the study period were enrolled. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the University of Miami Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 20180269; approval date: February 11, 2019).

Measures

Socio-Demographic and HIV Clinical Characteristics

Socio-demographic information was collected and recorded, including age, gender, race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, educational attainment, marital status, household income, employment, health insurance coverage, and perceived overall health status (excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor). HIV diagnosis status was determined based on self-report. Viral load and CD4 count information were obtained from the medical record or confirmed by clinic staff if participants were recruited from the local clinic. Participants’ HIV viral loads were categorized as undetectable if <200 copies/mL and suppressed/unlikely to spread/detectable if >=200 copies/mL. The CD4 counts were categorized as normal if >=500 cells/µL and AIDS/compromised/slightly affected immune system if <500 cells/µL. Adherence to ART was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), with optimal adherence defined as ≥ 90% in the past 30 days.16,34,35

Depression, Alcohol, and Marijuana Use

Depression was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9).21,36,37 Based on the total score, participants were grouped into two categories: no depression to minimal depression (PHQ-9 score 0 to 9) and moderate to severe depression (PHQ-9 score 10 to 27). Alcohol use was assessed using The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test-Concise (AUDIT-C).38,39 The AUDIT-C has 3 questions, each with five options (0–4), yielding a total score of 0-12. Scores of three or higher in females and four or higher in males are regarded as optimal for detecting risky (hazardous) alcohol consumption.38,40 Participants were also asked if they use marijuana with tobacco or use marijuana alone.41,42

Smoking Behavior and Barriers to Tobacco Treatment

The survey collected information on cigarette smoking frequency (daily, occasional), age at smoking initiation, use of other tobacco products (e.g., e-cigarettes, chewing tobacco, cigars/cigarillos, waterpipe), nicotine dependence as measured by the Fagerström test, 43 motivation to quit, previous quit attempts, reasons for quitting, and treatment methods used (e.g., nicotine replacement therapy, behavioral counseling, use of tobacco Quitline, switching to e-cigarettes). Other variables include perceived smoking-related health risks and barriers to treatment (e.g., cravings, financial problems, lack of access to treatment, lack of social support, HIV-related stigma and illnesses, traumatic life events, and living with someone who smokes).

Access to Digital Technology and Usage

Participants were asked whether they or their household owned any of the following devices: a desktop/laptop, a landline, a tablet, a cell phone, a smartphone, or a phone with a video camera. They were also asked whether their devices had capabilities such as internet access, texting, emailing, video recording, and application downloads. Additionally, they were asked about the frequency of their internet use.

Interest in Digital Health for Tobacco Treatment

Participants’ interest in receiving tobacco treatment via digital technologies was assessed. This includes phone calls, text messaging, smartphone apps, websites, and combinations of phone calls, texts, and apps, as well as group or individual videoconferencing. Participants who reported being “extremely interested,” “very interested,” “moderately interested,” or “somewhat interested” were categorized as “yes,” while those not interested were categorized as “no.”

Interest in Evidence-Based Tobacco Treatment Methods

Participants were asked to identify their preferred tobacco treatment options. These include mindfulness training, nicotine replacement therapy, brief and intensive counseling (both individual and group), support from a friend, and contingency management (such as cash or voucher rewards for quitting). They were allowed to choose multiple preferred methods from these evidence-based tobacco treatment interventions.

Analysis

Data were summarized descriptively for: 1) demographic characteristics, and clinical characteristics such as CD4 count, viral load, alcohol and substance use, depression, and overall health status (Table 1); 2) smoking behavior, cessation history, barriers, and treatment preferences (Table 2); and 3) access to digital technologies and interest in digital health for tobacco treatment (Table 3), with all results stratified by gender (male and female). For each table, we presented frequencies and proportions for categorical variables and means with standard deviations for continuous variables. To assess differences between males and females, we applied the chi-square test of independence for categorical variables and the independent Student’s t-test for continuous variables, as appropriate.

Results

Socio-Demographic and Other Clinical Characteristics

As reported in Table 1, a total of 205 participants were recruited for this study, of whom 54.6% were male. Most participants were aged 40 – 59 years (65.4%), identified as non-Hispanic Black (55.1%), unmarried (62.4%), heterosexual (77.6%), had at least a high school education (64.9%), reported an annual income of less than $10,000 (74.4%), and were unemployed, disabled or enrolled in Medicaid (31.2%, 46.8%, and 47.3%, respectively). Compared to males, females were significantly more likely to be non-Hispanic Black (64.2%; p=0.014), identified as heterosexual (88.1%; p=0.001), have less than a high school education (45.2%; p=0.02), reported an annual income of less than $10,000 (p<0.001), be unemployed (35.5%; p=0.012), and be enrolled in Medicaid (61.3%; p=0.001).

Among participants with available HIV clinical records (n=150; 73.2%), although only 13% reported low adherence to ART use, 44.7% had a CD4 count below 500 cells/mL and 22.7% had viral loads of 200 copies/mL or higher, indicating suboptimal immune status and incomplete viral suppression. Risky alcohol use was reported in 39.6% of participants, and 58.9% reported mild to severe depression. Nearly half of the sample (46.1%) perceived their overall health status as fair or poor, with males more likely than females to report fair or poor health (38.7%; p=0.014).

Smoking Behavior and Barriers to Tobacco Treatment

As shown in Table 2, most participants began smoking in late adolescence with a mean age of 18.2 (SD = 7.6) and reported smoking an average of 12.3 (SD = 9.7) cigarettes daily. Participants had a high-risk social environment, with most indicating having a friend who smoked (88.3%), although most had tried to quit at least once in the past year (76.5%). The most commonly used other tobacco products were cigar/cigarillo (27.8%), followed by e-cigarettes (15.1%). Females were significantly less likely than males to smoke cigars or cigarillos (15.1% vs. 38.4%; p<0.001). Participants also reported using marijuana alone or with tobacco (22.9% vs. 10.2%, respectively). The majority (87.8%) believe smoking poses health risks, with a higher percentage of females than males recognizing this risk (93.6% vs. 83%; p = 0.022). High nicotine dependence was common (34.5%). Participants rated their motivation to quit smoking at an average of 6.3 (SD = 3.2) out of 10 and their confidence in quitting at 6.1 (SD = 3.3). Females showed greater motivation to quit than males (mean score of 6.8 vs. 5.9; p = 0.027).

Approximately three-quarters (76.5%) had attempted to quit cigarettes in the past year, with no gender difference (p = 0.983). The most common reason for quit attempts was health concerns (47.6%), followed by other personal reasons (33.8%). Prior treatments received in the past 12 months included nicotine replacement therapy (19.5%; 13.4% male and 26.9% female, p=0.015), switching to e-cigarettes (7.8%), counseling plus pharmacologic treatment (3.9%), and calling Quitline (2.4%). Common cessation barriers identified included difficulty managing cravings (45.4%), financial problems (28.8%), limited treatment access (25.9%), social/environmental factors such as having a partner who smokes (23.9%), lack of social support and traumatic events (22.9%), HIV-related illness (20%), and HIV stigma (19%).

Access to Digital Technology and Usage

Most participants reported internet access (63.9%), with 38.5% reporting daily use (Table 3). Although not significant, daily internet use was higher among males compared to females (47.3% vs. 28%; p = 0.057). Access to digital devices was high, with most owning a cell phone (91.7%) and a smartphone (61%). Other digital devices owned included desktop/laptop (37.6%) and tablet (28.3%), with males more likely to own a desktop/laptop (43.8% vs. 30.1%; p = 0.045) and a tablet (37.7% vs. 19.4%; p = 0.010) compared to females. Participants reported high engagement with these devices, including the ability to send or receive text messages (97.6%), send or receive emails (96.9%), record videos (60.8%), and download apps (74.0%). None of these digital usage behaviors differ significantly by gender.

Interest in Digital Health for Tobacco Treatment

Interest in digital tobacco treatment was generally high and similar across genders for several digital modalities. Approximately 60% of participants were interested in combined digital approaches (e.g., phone calls, text messaging, or smartphone apps), followed by phone calls alone (51.7%), text messaging (43.9%), smartphone apps alone (39%), website (33.2%), and video counseling (38.5%). Males were more likely to express interest in smartphone app–based (45.5% vs. 31.2%; p = 0.036), website-based (41.1% vs. 23.7%; p = 0.008), and video counseling (45.5% vs. 30.1%; p = 0.024) treatments than females.

In Table 4, the socio-demographic and behavioral predictors of interest in different digital health tools for tobacco treatment were examined using multivariable logistic regression. Non-Hispanic Blacks showed a higher likelihood of interest in receiving tobacco treatments via any of phone calls, text messaging, or smartphone apps (aOR = 5.49; 95% CI = 1.44–20.90) or through text messaging only (aOR = 4.23; 95% CI = 1.08–16.54) compared with non-Hispanic White. Also, participants identifying as non-heterosexual had significantly higher odds of interest in receiving tobacco treatments via any of phone calls, text messaging, or smartphone apps (aOR = 6.02; 95% CI: 1.46–24.82) or through phone calls alone (aOR = 3.75; 95% CI: 1.07–13.08).

Interest in Evidence-Based Tobacco Treatment Methods

Figure 1 shows that participants express substantial interest in various evidence-based tobacco treatment strategies. The most frequently endorsed approaches were mindfulness (73.0%) and nicotine replacement therapy (71.1%), followed by brief counseling (65.4%), intensive individual counseling (63.2%), rewards (62.8%), and intensive group counseling (56.6%). Although counseling from a friend or caregiver was the least preferred option, it was still endorsed by nearly half of participants (49.3%), suggesting that professionally delivered treatments may be more appealing than informal sources of support.

Tobacco treatment preferences among PLWH smokers

Discussion

This study examined smoking cessation barriers and the potential of digital health-based tobacco treatment among under-resourced PLWH smokers. Results indicated that participants faced multiple barriers to quitting, including difficulty managing cravings, financial hardship, limited access to treatment resources, cohabitation with a partner who smokes, insufficient social support, exposure to traumatic events, HIV-related illness or physical weakness, and stigma associated with HIV. Despite these challenges, motivation to quit cigarettes was high, with many prior quit attempts driven largely by health concerns. Participants reported broad access to digital technologies and a strong interest in receiving tobacco treatment through these platforms, particularly behavioral interventions such as mindfulness, and pharmacological intervention such as nicotine replacement therapy. Together, high motivation, widespread access to technology, and interest in treatment highlight the feasibility and promise of digital health interventions for tobacco treatment among this high-risk population.

Similar to our findings, barriers such as cravings, financial constraints, limited treatment access, lack of social support, and HIV-related stressors are commonly reported among PLWH as key obstacles to tobacco treatment in the US.45-51 For instance, Cioe et al. reported that high nicotine dependence and smoking as a coping mechanism for stress are obstacles to cessation. 51 In qualitative studies, Scarinci et al. highlighted cost, travel distance, and confidentiality concerns as barriers to accessing cessation aids like nicotine replacement therapy, 45 while Chockalingam et al. identified low tobacco prices, high smoking prevalence, and cravings as barriers to treatment among PLWH. Many PLWH face challenges in accessing treatment due to low prioritization of tobacco treatment in HIV care settings, while social environments that normalize smoking and experiences of stigma contribute to low treatment success.52,53 These barriers directly affect the success of digital health interventions, which, although feasible and engaging, often fail to achieve sustained quit rates unless they also address underlying social and structural factors. 17

Despite these barriers, the findings indicate that under-resourced PLWH were very motivated to quit and made frequent quit attempts, with health concerns emerging as the primary motivator. This is consistent with prior evidence that reported high rates of past quit attempts and interest in treatment among PLWH who smoke, with factors such as older age, prior use of nicotine replacement therapy, and smoking-related illnesses—particularly pulmonary disease—emerging as strong motivators to seek treatment.54,55 For example, Bean et al. reported that 85% of PLWH smokers at an HIV clinic had tried to quit, with nearly half expressing interest in consulting a physician for tobacco treatment, while Pacek et al. found that older PLWH and those with prior nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) use were more likely to pursue treatment. 54

Our findings indicate substantial digital access among this population, as reflected in high rates of cell phone and smartphone ownership and strong ability to use their core functions such as texting, emailing, video recording, and downloading applications. Prior research supports the acceptability and feasibility of delivering tobacco treatments through digital health technologies in this population.14,17,56 More broadly, digital health approaches have been shown to expand the reach of tobacco treatment and provide scalable, low-cost support options that can complement traditional behavioral interventions.28,57 However, the observed gender differences in technology access and internet use are consistent with evidence that disparities in smartphone ownership and digital connectivity can shape participation in digital health interventions—and, if left unaddressed, may further widen existing health inequities. 58 Studies in other marginalized smoking populations similarly show that although access to digital technology is often high, inconsistent internet access and technological barriers can limit sustained engagement with digital health programs, highlighting the importance of assessing digital readiness when designing tobacco treatment interventions. 59

This study suggests that participants are receptive to using digital technologies for tobacco treatment, with many expressing interest in engaging with each assessed digital modality. Specifically, participants preferred a combination of different digital modalities for treatment. Evidence from systematic reviews also indicates that text-messaging interventions are generally acceptable to smokers and can improve quit outcomes, which may help explain relatively high interest in lower-burden digital strategies compared with more intensive technology formats. 28 Gender differences also exist, with males preferring more digitally intensive formats, such as smartphone apps, web-based programs, and videos. This has been reported in prior research among elderly individuals, with men often showing greater intention to use more technologically intensive medical apps, partly due to stronger perceived usefulness and more favorable attitudes toward technology. 60 Preferences for phone- and text-based support among non-Hispanic Black and non-heterosexual PLWH also reinforce the influence of sociodemographics, with similar reports on treatment engagement in a prior study. 54 Other studies suggest that willingness to use more digital health for tobacco treatment varies across subgroups due to differences in technology access, digital familiarity, perceived usability, and practical concerns such as cost and privacy, all of which may influence interest and sustained engagement with these interventions.14,58

Participants demonstrated receptivity to a broad range of tobacco treatment modalities, encompassing both behavioral approaches—such as mindfulness training, counseling, and contingency management—and pharmacological options like nicotine replacement therapy, consistent with prior research documenting treatment interest among PLWH smokers. 11 The appeal of behavioral interventions may reflect their broader psychosocial benefits: mindfulness, for instance, has been shown to reduce stress and enhance motivation to quit, while financial incentives (contingency management) have demonstrated efficacy in improving smoking cessation outcomes and promoting sustained abstinence. 14 Integrating these behavioral strategies with pharmacotherapy, such as nicotine replacement therapy, has been further shown to increase self-efficacy and reduce cigarette consumption. 61 The preference for individualized over group-based approaches likely reflects well-documented psychosocial barriers among PLWH, including stress, low readiness to quit, and social smoking norms, though tailored group programs have shown promise in enhancing motivation and outcomes for some individuals in this population.51,62 Digital health offers a promising avenue for expanding access to both behavioral and pharmacological treatments by enabling remote delivery, real-time feedback, and reminders28,63; however, where present, disparities in digital access and literacy may limit the reach of technology-enabled cessation strategies and sustain preferences for more traditional approaches among some PLWH smokers. 64

Strengths and Limitations

The key strengths of this study include a focus on under-resourced PLWH smokers, identification of barriers to tobacco treatment, and an integrated examination of access to digital technologies, willingness to use digital tools, and tobacco treatment preferences. By exploring gender and sociodemographic disparities, the study emphasizes intersectional barriers and highlights the importance of equity-focused tobacco treatment strategies for PLWH smokers.

Although the sample largely reflects U.S. cohorts, the findings may not generalize to all PLWH, particularly those in rural or international settings, and may not fully predict future trends given rapidly evolving technology. Participants were recruited from a single academic medical center, resulting in a convenience sample that may not be representative of all PLWH smokers. Additionally, potential biases such as selection, recall, and social desirability biases inherent to clinic-based recruitment and self-reported measures cannot be fully excluded. We attempted to minimize these biases by implementing strict confidentiality procedures for data collection, which may have encouraged honest reporting and contributed to the high response rate. Furthermore, the absence of a formal a priori sample size calculation may have limited the power to detect small subgroup differences. While interest in digital interventions was high, further research is needed to assess engagement and long-term effectiveness among this group.

Conclusion

Under-resourced PLWH who smoke face substantial and intersecting barriers to tobacco treatment, yet demonstrate strong motivation to quit and broad interest in both behavioral and pharmacologic treatment options. High levels of access to and interest in using digital technologies highlight important opportunities to expand the reach of tobacco treatment through scalable, flexible, and patient-centered digital health interventions. However, gender and sociodemographic disparities in digital engagement, along with persistent social and structural challenges such as stigma, financial hardship, and limited treatment prioritization within HIV care, may influence treatment uptake and long-term effectiveness. Future interventions should therefore integrate digital delivery with tailored behavioral and pharmacologic support while addressing underlying psychosocial and structural barriers to maximize engagement, equity, and sustained treatment outcomes among PLWH smokers.
