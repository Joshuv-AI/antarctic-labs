ABSTRACT
The grid is undergoing unprecedented change as utilities power a growing demand driven by the
prevalence of artificial intelligence data centers, manufacturing plants, and more. Data centers
experience rapid demand fluctuations that can push voltage, frequency, and power factor outside
acceptable operating limits. This poses brownout and blackout risks to the grids and microgrids
connected to them.

This project evaluated a 3.6 farad (F), 720 V rated supercapacitor system in a microgrid
environment. The system was built and installed by Capacitech Energy, in partnership with the
Orlando Utilities Commission (OUC) and Sandia National Laboratories (SNL). The demonstration
project focused on power resilience applications. The supercapacitor system used thirty-six of
Capacitech’s C-LinkTM supercapacitor modules, which are collectively capable of 302 kW and 0.26
kWh. Each supercapacitor module had an integrated power management system including
monitoring, cell balancing, passive cooling, over voltage protection, over current protection, and
over temperature protection.

The project evaluated three primary use cases: black start, voltage regulation, and load leveling. The
system successfully performed a microgrid black start, maintaining operations for five minutes until
the onsite generation operation resumed. Additionally, the system prevented microgrid downtime
due to power disturbances from inrush currents when energizing a transformer. It also smoothed
the output of onsite generation during fluctuations.

These results demonstrate that supercapacitor energy storage systems are a viable option to improve
power resilience and quality to reduce downtime and stress on connected equipment for some
microgrid applications.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Background
Modern grids are experiencing a rapid increase in electrical power demand driven by artificial
intelligence data centers and manufacturing. These loads cause millisecond- to several seconds-long
power fluctuations, called transients, that are unpredictable and can destabilize the larger system.
Common power generation, energy storage, and control systems are often not optimized for
millisecond-to-second fluctuations, posing an increased risk of equipment damage, premature
battery degradation, and costly downtime. To ensure power reliability and quality, facility operators
require rapid-response solutions that work with existing generation and storage assets.
Supercapacitor energy storage technology is well suited to address short-duration power fluctuations.
They have the capacity to support black start capabilities, address inrush current from transformers
and industrial equipment, reduce voltage fluctuations that can create brownout conditions, and
protect other equipment (such as batteries, converters, and monitoring devices) from power
disturbances and disruptions. Supercapacitors can charge and discharge quickly to balance power
flow and address sudden surges in demand, providing valuable time for control systems, power
sources, and/or energy storage technologies to respond. The supercapacitor system acts as a sink
when generation exceeds demand and as a source when generation falls short.
Compared to batteries, flywheels, and other energy storage technologies, supercapacitors have a high
power density and few charge and discharge rate requirements. Additionally, they exhibit lower
relative safety risks compared to batteries, have a long operating life, and require relatively little
maintenance. Economically, these characteristics result in a lower system cost (including installation,
operation, and maintenance) in dollars per kilowatt ($/kW) and dollars per kilowatt-year ($/kW-
year) which is highly competitive to other short-duration energy storage technologies.
Project Overview
Capacitech Energy, in partnership with the Orlando Utilities Commission (OUC) and Sandia
National Laboratories (SNL), conducted a supercapacitor demonstration project to evaluate how
supercapacitors can improve microgrid power resilience. For the demonstration project, Capacitech
built the energy storage system using thirty-six C-LinkTM supercapacitor energy storage modules.
These modules have a conduit-type form factor that is flexible and lightweight (15 lbs). Each
module used commercial off-the-shelf supercapacitor components managed by proprietary power
electronics responsible for voltage and temperature monitoring; rapid voltage balancing during
operations; and overvoltage, over current, and over temperature protection. This standardized and
modular design enables efficient fabrication and installation in space constrained environments.
The supercapacitor energy storge system with C-Link modules was deployed at the OUC’s microgrid
test site in Orlando, Florida. This energy storge system connected to a bidirectional AC-DC
converter supporting three primary use cases: black start, voltage regulation, and load leveling.
Results
 • Black Start: The system successfully formed a microgrid for five minutes—long enough to
 resume operation of 30 kW of onsite generation — and balanced supply with demand to
 maintain operations.
 • Voltage Regulation: The system mitigated voltage sags and transient inrush currents
 associated with energizing a 75 kVA transformer, reducing the number of failed re-
 energization attempts by 50% to maximize overall uptime.

 8

 • Load Leveling: The supercapacitor energy storage system absorbed and released energy
 rapidly to smooth +/- 5 kW of onsite generation fluctuations while supporting high-power
 transient loads.
The demonstration shows that integrating supercapacitor energy storage systems with existing grid
assets can:
 • Minimize downtime by managing high inrush currents and transient events.
 • Prevent equipment damage by stabilizing voltage, frequency, and power fluctuations (i.e.,
 improve power quality) to reduce stress on batteries and other generation assets.
 • Enable more efficient system designs and simplified microgrid operation.
 • Provide a cost-effective resilience layer with lower lifetime operating costs compared to
 battery-only approaches.
The demonstration project shows that supercapacitor energy storage systems can enhance power
quality and resilience, reducing operational risk across modern power infrastructure—especially for
utilities, data centers, and other mission-critical facilities.

1. INTRODUCTION
The modern grid is under increasing strain due to a surge in electricity demand, driven by the rapid
growth of data centers and manufacturing plants [1] [2]. Data centers, for example, feature major
fluctuations in demand. A 200-megawatt (MW) artificial intelligence (AI) data center is likely to have
power surges that are +/- 30 MW [3], if not more. These fluctuations occur on the millisecond to
second scale and cause voltage and frequency distortions that challenge acceptable operating limits,
posing reliability risks. A variety of new and existing technologies are essential for navigating this
transition. Both long-duration energy storage, which shifts demand, and rapid-response energy
storage, which offers balance and stability, are needed to support increasingly complex load profiles
observed on grids and industrial facilities.
Microgrids serve an important role in powering AI data centers and, in general, the future grid [2]
[3]. They can help utilities quickly deploy additional electrical capacity, often supporting a larger grid,
and enable commercial companies to have onsite generation to reduce energy costs and even claim
power independence from the grid in case of an outage. However, microgrids are only effective if
the energy produced is compatible with the connected loads. They must maintain sufficient power
quality during transients due to inrush currents, variable generation, and dynamic loads to be a
reliable option for the end users and must be capable of black start operation in case of outages [4]
[5] [6].
Supercapacitors are an energy storage technology that are well suited to address these kinds of short-
duration power fluctuations [7]. They support black start capabilities, address inrush current from
transformers and industrial equipment, reduce voltage fluctuations that can create brownout
conditions, and protect other equipment (such as batteries, converters, and monitoring devices)
from power disturbances and disruptions that would otherwise create downtime. Supercapacitors
can charge and discharge quickly, with few requirements or limits as to how they are charged and
discharged, to balance power flow and address sudden surges in demand. They act as a sink when
generation exceeds demand and as a source when generation falls short. This provides valuable time
for control systems and slower power sources or storage technologies to respond.

1.1. Supercapacitor Overview
Supercapacitors are passive, direct current (DC) energy storage components. They feature a high
power density, meaning they are capable of quickly charging or discharging at very high levels of
power with instantaneous response compared to their overall energy storage capacity [7]. This is due
to their low internal resistance, which also contributes to superior thermal management and small
instantaneous voltage drop when a load is applied. Additionally, supercapacitors offer a long cycle
life and operate with a high stability across a wide temperature range.

These characteristic differences between batteries and supercapacitors position them to serve
different functions in power systems. Batteries, such as those with lithium iron phosphate (LFP)

short-duration applications in the millisecond to minute time scale. As such, they complement one
another well.
For example, supercapacitor energy storage systems can mitigate an AI data center’s peak power
requirement and smooth their short-duration fluctuations. Meanwhile, battery systems could provide
multi-hour backup power during an outage on the grid or to reduce the data center’s demand on the
grid in an emergency. In this example, the supercapacitor systems smooth rapid power fluctuations
while the battery systems support the significantly longer duration, steady-state demand. The hybrid
system allows for the deployment of a smaller battery system—sized to meet the nominal steady-
state load requirements—while the supercapacitor system would meet short-duration, peak power
requirements. The combination would reduce the battery size and cost and allow the system to
operate under normal steady-state conditions, avoiding the rapid charge/discharge cycles for a safer
and longer battery life.

2. SUPERCAPACITOR-BASED ENERGY STORAGE MODULES
Supercapacitors, first commercialized by Nippon Electric Company (NEC) in 1978, are considered a
mature and known energy storage technology. However, as inherently passive components, they
require substantial system-level engineering to be properly integrated into high-voltage, high-power
applications. Commercially available supercapacitor modules and cabinets often lack the level of
sophistication necessary for such applications, while custom engineered solutions are expensive and
do not scale. So, there is a need for the design of a scalable, standardized, and complete
supercapacitor energy storage module for wider adoption of this technology.
Commercially available supercapacitor modules currently tend to focus on the series and parallel
connection of the supercapacitor components inside of them. However, they are rarely optimized
for use in high-voltage, high-power applications due to the limits of integrated cell balancing and
communication technologies. Namely, additional work is often required to balance the voltage from
one module to another—frequently on a custom basis that is not adaptable and cannot be readily
reused for future installations. This kind of intermodular balancing is very important to achieving
the safety, long cycle life, and low-maintenance operation expected with supercapacitor systems.
Additionally, most available modules are heavy and require additional integration prior to
deployment. For example, many modules require cabinet enclosures, cabling between them, and
cooling systems. This makes it difficult for utility, data centers, and commercial or industrial
customers to adopt supercapacitor technology. Figure 1 shows examples of commercially available
supercapacitor modules.

 Figure 1. Examples of supercapacitor modules from different manufacturers [8] [9].

As a part of the demonstration project, Capacitech designed and developed a flexible, rugged,
standardized supercapacitor energy storage module, called C-LinkTM, shown in Figure 2. Appendix A
provides the module’s data sheet. This supercapacitor-based module is designed for indoor or
outdoor installations. Its features are intended to address common integration challenges faced by
utilities, data centers, commercial and industrial facilities, and microgrid developers. For the project,
each module provided 8.3 kW power and 7.2 Wh energy, rated to 60 V. Depending on the system

Section 3.1, Installation Site and System Architecture, describes the configuration used for the

2.1.2. Form Factor
Commercial energy storage systems for high-power, high-voltage applications typically come in
large, metal enclosures that can be as large as shipping containers. These installations are expensive,

2.1.3. Enclosure Durability
The C-Link enclosure is a durable conduit designed for outdoor environments. According to the
conduit’s datasheet, it can withstand UV and weathering for up to 20 years. The extreme ends of the
C-Link feature a hydrophobic vent, allowing air to flow without letting any water into the enclosure.
The outdoor installation was subject to afternoon thunderstorms in the summer with zero signs of

The enclosure is also mechanically durable, with an impact strength of 6 J/m, and a peak load value
of 320 newtons (N). The peak load value was obtained via a test performed by the manufacturer. In
this test, a 50 mm length of conduit was subjected to constant deformation to 75% of the original
outer diameter and held for two minutes. After the two minutes, the reaction force of the conduit
was recorded, and no visual damage, cracks, or openings were observed.

2.2. Cell Balancing and Power Management for Supercapacitors
Typical manufacturer capacitance and equivalent series resistance tolerances for commercial
supercapacitor cells are in the range of +/-10 to +/-20% nominal. These intrinsic differences in cell
characteristics can manifest as voltage variations from cell to cell during operation, which exacerbate
over time and with increased charge or discharge rates. Correcting these voltage variations is known
as “cell balancing.” If these voltage imbalances are not corrected, due to an undersized balancing
system or none at all, then individual cells may operate at different voltages from one another,
causing cells to become overcharged or over-discharged. This is a primary safety concern when
operating supercapacitors and will impact the operating life of the system.
Most commercially available cell-balancing systems are tailored to batteries, aptly called battery
management systems (BMS), or balance slowly at low power levels, which is insufficient for the
dynamic and high-power applications that supercapacitors support. To address these shortcomings,

 17

traditional supercapacitor systems are often oversized to provide a buffer or headroom between the
expected operating voltage and the supercapacitor’s rated voltage. Additionally, they may often be
oversized in terms of capacitance so that the system’s voltage changes slower in case of emergency.
While reducing the likelihood that any cell becomes overcharged, this approach increases system
footprint and costs.
Capacitech designed a proprietary supercapacitor energy management system for C-Link. The
demonstration installation used Capacitech’s energy management system, including each C-Link’s
built-in cooling and power management system. The power management system included high-
power balancing, overvoltage protection, cell-level voltage monitoring, and temperature tracking.
The power management system can be integrated with a site performance monitoring system. Cell-
level voltages and ambient module temperatures can be obtained by the user via controller area
network (CAN) communication.
The following sections provide more information about cell balancing, power management, and the
cell balancing and power management mechanisms employed in the demonstration. These features
were validated as described below prior to the demonstration.

2.2.1. Intrinsic Cell Differences
As previously mentioned, intrinsic differences in cell performance can adversely affect system
performance and operating life if not managed properly. Initial component testing showed that the
capacitance values of the commercially available supercapacitors were within the tolerances specified
in the data sheet (+/-10% from the nominal value). When the capacitors are connected in series and
parallel in the module, the tolerance stack up can magnify the cell imbalance. Equation 3, described
earlier, can show how this difference in capacitance value can manifest as a voltage imbalance and
eventual safety issue. Voltage imbalances can be further increased due to differences in equivalent
series resistance following Ohm’s law.
Assuming that the current applied and the charging time are the same for two supercapacitor cells
connected in series, Equation 3 shows that an ~10% difference in capacitance results in an ~10%
imbalance in the change in voltage, even though the cell is within tolerance. Slight differences in
equivalent series resistance increase these imbalances further, causing a different instantaneous
voltage spike (the moment when charging begins) or drop (the moment when discharging begins) across each cell.
This further illustrates why sufficient cell balancing techniques are required for supercapacitors.

2.2.2. Cell Balancing
Cell-balancing techniques are generally described as either passive or active. Their limitations are
largely related to speed to correct imbalance, or equalize, and their ability to scale or adapt. For
example, balancing systems are often isolated to a single module, or group of cells, instead of
balancing across the entire system. As such, these balancing techniques are sufficient for traditional
supercapacitor applications, such as a small group of cells providing backup power for computer
memory. However, higher power solutions are required to be effective in modern applications like
grid and microgrid support, power quality, and AI training.

2.2.2.1. Passive Balancing
Typical off-the-shelf supercapacitor modules generally add a large resistor in parallel with the cells to
create a small, continuous leakage current path to bleed off excess charge. While this works for low-
energy, cost-sensitive applications, passive balancing alone is insufficient for supercapacitor systems

 18

operating at high levels of power following dynamic loads. Traditional, passive-balancing techniques
are effective for addressing small intrinsic differences between supercapacitor cells, such as slight
variations in their equivalent series resistance (ESR).
To validate the passive balancing system, a single supercapacitor cell in a module was charged such
that there was a 0.15 V imbalance. The voltage of the overcharged cell and a control cell were
recorded every 15 minutes. Results showed that after eight hours, the imbalance had reduced to 0.1 V.
This confirmed that passive balancing would correct slight voltage imbalances while the
supercapacitors are at rest. However, given the time requirement for balancing, passive balancing
alone would not be sufficient in protecting the system from imbalances in high-power scenarios.

2.2.2.2. Active Balancing
Unlike passive balancing, which dissipates excess energy as heat through resistors, active balancing
uses power electronics—such as semiconductors or bidirectional DC-DC to transfer energy from
higher-voltage cells to lower-voltage cells. This approach improves efficiency and offers tighter
voltage control across a wider range of the supercapacitor cell’s operating voltage window. This
increases balancing or equalization speed.
Active balancing is particularly valuable in applications with frequent high-rate charge/discharge
events where voltage imbalances can develop rapidly. Active balancing is faster, more efficient, and
more effective than passive. However, power electronics used for active balancing are generally still
low-power circuits, while many supercapacitor applications often require high power.

2.2.2.3. Cell Balancing Used in the C-Link Supercapacitor Module
Since supercapacitor systems are used to support high power, dynamic load profiles—which
increase voltage imbalances between cells due to their intrinsic differences—both passive and active
balancing systems are imperative for safety and longevity. Additionally, high-power balancing is
required to quickly address imbalances in modern applications.
To complement the passive balancing system, the C-Link features a high power and active balancing
system to correct larger imbalances that follow high-current events. The system employs smart
algorithms to check voltages of every cell within a system and activate high-power balancing only
when necessary. This approach monitors and balances cells across the system and across modules
within the system.
The C-Link’s high-power, active balancing is configured to operate at high currents, capable of
correcting a 1% imbalance within a few minutes whereas a traditional passive balancing design
would require several hours. This is due to the low current ratings of passive balancing systems and
the high capacitance of the supercapacitors used in high-voltage, high-power applications. Figure 8
below demonstrates the C-Link active balancing system addressing a voltage imbalance within two
minutes. This data was from an onsite experiment, obtained via the C-Link built-in CAN data
collection system and plotted using the Matplotlib library in Python.

By monitoring cell-level voltages throughout the system and quickly addressing any imbalances that

2.3. Overvoltage Protection
The C-Link system has built-in overvoltage protection, helping to ensure that the system does not
stay charged past its voltage rating. This may occur for a variety of reasons from human or
communication error to voltage spike across a supercapacitor system due to a current surge when at
a high state of charge.
To verify overvoltage protection, the system was charged to 732 V, which is 12 V higher than the
rated voltage at the OUC test site. As expected, alarms went off once the system was overcharged,
confirming that the power management system recognized that it was over its rated voltage. The
data in Figure 9 was obtained via the C-Link data collection system and plotted using the Matplotlib
library in Python. The “traditional” performance models a passive balancing system.

Figure 9. Voltage of the C-Link system during the overvoltage experiment. The section highlighted
 in red signifies when the system was overvoltage after the charging had stopped.

As shown in Figure 9, the overvoltage protection system dissipated ~2 kW of power, limiting how
much the system was overcharged by and quickly returning it to below its rated voltage. Leaving

 20

supercapacitors overcharged, especially over long periods of time, is dangerous and likely to result in
system failure.
The dotted line in Figure 9 models the response of a traditional passive resistor balancing system
without overvoltage protection in the same scenario. A supercapacitor was modeled in LTSpice to
evaluate discharge behavior. Simulation results show that when the device is allowed to discharge
with only a passive resistor balancing system in parallel rather than an active overvoltage protection
circuit, the voltage decays at a substantially slower rate. A smaller resistor in parallel with a
supercapacitor would accelerate balancing. However, it would also significantly reduce the efficiency
of the supercapacitor system in general as the resistor would dissipate energy even when not
overcharged. According to the model, the passive system would have required two hours to correct
the voltage, whereas, during the overvoltage experiment the C-Link corrected the system’s voltage
imbalance in approximately 2 minutes. Even after the charging was stopped, the C-Link overvoltage
protection continued, dissipating approximately 8 Wh of energy to bring the voltage below its
maximum rated voltage.

2.4. Temperature Monitoring
The C-Link modules contain temperature sensors to ensure that ambient temperature does not
exceed the ratings of the components. Should the ambient temperature approach a high enough
value, then the forced-air cooling system will engage until it returns below a threshold. This
approach is intended to be more energy efficient, helping to ensure the cooling system does not
draw amounts of power from the C-Link when unnecessary.

2.5. Power Consumption of Peripherals
The power required to maintain the peripherals of the C-Link was confirmed via a power
consumption test prior to installation. In this test, a C-Link module was charged to 60 V via DC
power supply. Once fully charged, the power supply was left on and operated in a constant voltage
mode. The amount of current required to keep the system at 60 V was used to calculate how much
power is necessary to keep the system in operation. It was calculated that approximately 2.4 W was
required after one minute of being charged to power the system peripherals and maintain the system
at 60 V. This test was done at room temperature, and the fan remained off for the duration of the
test. With the fan on, the power required for peripherals would be approximately 3.8 W. It was later
observed that significantly less power is required after longer periods of being charged. This is due
to the supercapacitors inside the C-Link requiring less current to maintain the 60 V as it is held at
that voltage for an extended period of time, resulting in less power needed overall.

Capacitech proposed a supercapacitor energy storage demonstration project in a microgrid

 • Operate a supercapacitor system in a microgrid environment.
 • Demonstrate power-resilience use cases including black start, voltage regulation, and load

3.2. Test System Objectives and Results

3.2.1. Objective 1: Operate Supercapacitors in Microgrid
Supercapacitors are most effective in high-power, dynamic scenarios such as those experienced on a
microgrid. They store energy electrostatically, allowing for rapid charge and discharge cycles with
minimal degradation. The role of the supercapacitor system is to smooth power fluctuations, bridge
the supply gaps while slower storage or generation responds, and provide voltage support.
At OUC’s microgrid site, the C-Link supercapacitor energy storage system is integrated via CE&T’s
Model Stabiliti 30C3 bidirectional AC-DC converter. As shown in the datasheet provided in
Appendix B, the Stabiliti can support 30 kW with a DC operating voltage range of 200 to 1000 Vdc.
It has three bidirectional ports—two DC and one AC. The microgrid is comprised of a 16 kW
flywheel connected to DC port 1, the C-Link supercapacitor energy storage system is connected to
DC port 2, and the loads are connected to the AC port. This converter supports multiple operating
modes, including grid-forming and grid-following functions. For the purposes of this research,
unless the C-Link needed to be recharged via the grid, all experiments were conducted in the grid-
forming mode.

3.2.1.1. Operating Modes
The Stabiliti bidirectional AC-DC converter manages power transfer between ports using various
operating modes based on how they are configured by the operator using Modbus registers. By
selecting appropriate control strategies via operator input, the supercapacitor system is set to actively
participate in the microgrid to strengthen it and improve stability, whether by injecting power during
sudden load increases or absorbing excess generation during surpluses. The controls were monitored
and input manually by onsite technicians for testing, but they can be automated with a
programmable logic controller that manages the site controls.
In this research, the primary DC port operating modes used were IDLE, NET, and VOLT modes.

 26

3.2.1.2.   IDLE Mode
IDLE mode disables power conversion on the selected port. For example, in instances where the C-
Link system was used to black start and form the microgrid by itself, the flywheels were set to IDLE.

3.2.1.3.   NET Mode
NET mode is used to import or export the necessary power to maintain operations. When the C-
Link system was set to NET mode, it responded to any imbalance on the other ports of the
converter by injecting or absorbing power.

3.2.1.4.   VOLT Mode
VOLT mode operates by ramping the output to the port up or down based on the voltage setpoint.
This mode is commonly used in flow batteries and flywheel energy storage systems. In instances
where the C-Link system needed to be charged or discharged to a particular voltage, the respective
port was placed into VOLT mode. Otherwise, the flywheel port was set to VOLT mode.

system could black start the microgrid, regulate voltage, and level loads.

As seen in Figure 17b, there are times where the C-Link system switches between charging and
discharging without extended periods of rest most easily seen in the voltage profile. This behavior
highlights the rapid charge–discharge capabilities of supercapacitors that are not well suited for
operation with the conventional batteries. This is a distinguishing factor between supercapacitors
and batteries, as this type of response can accelerate battery performance degradation or requires an
oversized battery to reduce the stress on the battery. In addition, unlike batteries, supercapacitors
can operate over a wide voltage window and tolerate such fluctuations inherently, making them
particularly advantageous in applications with variable load profiles. A supercapacitor can increase or
decrease its state of charge with very little energy compared to a battery so that it can be quickly
charged or discharged in preparation for a particular load profile. For example, to increase the
voltage in preparation of black start or other use cases such as decreasing the voltage for
decommissioning or maintenance needs supercapacitors are better suited.
For context, the wide variability in the C-Link system’s voltage is typical of supercapacitor behavior
and made possible by the AC-DC converter’s operating DC voltage range of 200 V to 1,000 V on
each DC port.

3.2.2.2.   Black Start
Black start is the process of restarting a power system from a completely shut-down state. The
correct technologies need to be installed on a microgrid to ensure it can black start after losing
power from the grid. For instance, it is imperative that emergency services and buildings such as
hospitals are powered and operational during the hurricanes that occur in Florida annually, and—
should they lose power—the restart process should happen instantaneously. Supercapacitors, with
their ability to rapidly deliver high power, are a viable option.
The black start experiments at OUC’s facility provided insight on the ability of the C-Link
supercapacitor energy storage system to power up and maintain the microgrid. The C-Link was

One key observation from black start testing was the overall simplification of controls and
sequencing required to operate the microgrid. The C-Link not only provided the initial source of
power to form the microgrid but also functioned as a controllable load by charging at a high rate

During black start tests, breakers were opened to disconnect nonessential loads, leaving only
auxiliary systems (e.g., communications, safety equipment, fans) energized. This minimized demand,
allowing the C-Link modules to sustain the grid for the five-minute period required before inverters
could begin exporting power. Once this interval passed, onsite generation supported the auxiliary
loads while the C-Link absorbed the remaining power to charge. After charging to approximately
450 V, breakers were closed and site loads were brought online, increasing demand to align with
available generation. Together, the C-Link and onsite generation system successfully energized both
the transformer and connected loads.

3.2.2.3. Voltage Regulation
The site includes a 75 kilovolt-ampere (kVA) step-down transformer between the Stabiliti
bidirectional AC-DC converter and the loads. When a microgrid is formed, inrush currents are
handled by slowly ramping the AC voltage. However, if the transformer is not connected when
forming the microgrid, then significant inrush currents will be observed when energizing the
transformer. These inrush currents are very brief (90% decay within one second) but significant
(they can reach 70 A on a single phase), and they can be inconsistent in magnitude per phase. These
inrush currents can have a destabilizing effect on AC voltage on the microgrid. The goal of the
voltage regulation experiment was to use the C-Link supercapacitor energy storage system to ride
through disturbances in voltage caused by inrush currents and prevent the microgrid from
collapsing.
The voltage regulation experiment was repeated approximately 40 times over 6 days of testing, with
similar results. The results described below are from one of these tests.

3.2.2.3.1. Experimental Procedure
Prior to starting the experiment:
 1. The C-Link was charged to 500 Vdc using VOLT mode.
 2. The C-Link was switched to NET mode.
 3. The Stabiliti formed a microgrid using the C-Link.
To conduct the voltage regulation test:
 1. The 75 kVA transformer was disconnected from the microgrid, via breaker panel.
 2. After 10 seconds, the 75 kVA transformer was reconnected to the microgrid, via breaker
 panel.
 3. The user interface on the Stabiliti was observed to confirm if the microgrid was still formed.
Success was indicated by a successful re-energization of the transformer with the microgrid still
formed. A failure was indicated by the microgrid destabilizing and collapsing.

converter would be expected to significantly improve success rates. Additionally, the converter
should feature a wide voltage range as the Stabiliti did so that the supercapacitors can sufficiently
charge and discharge.

4. ECONOMIC BENEFITS
The C-Link supercapacitor energy storage system is shown to be a solution for microgrid resilience
and reliability due to its rapid response capability. This enables greater operating flexibility to handle
inrush current and ride through voltage transients responsible for costly downtime, protect assets
(generators, batteries, transformers, converters, machinery, and other loads), and increase yield.
Economic benefits of a supercapacitor-based energy storage system for certain applications are
discussed in this section. For reference, all energy storage system pricing described includes
balancing and AC-DC converter costs.
Poor grid and microgrid resilience are costly. Even momentary power quality issues can result in
significantly longer periods of downtime, especially when industrial equipment is involved. Data
center downtime caused by outages can cost businesses an average of $9,000 per minute [14]. Four
in ten enterprises (companies with greater than 1000 employees) say that downtime can cost up to
$5 million per hour [15]. This impacts customer satisfaction, insurance premiums [16] , and brand
reputation. One obvious consequence to poor grid resilience and reliability is that it encourages large
industrial customers to consider onsite generation rather than purchasing power from the utility,
which has a downstream effect on other utility customers. In addition, more than 92% of power
problems experienced are due to sags or undervoltage [17] [18]. While short in duration, voltage sag
or undervoltage can lead to equipment damage and potential downtime.
These sags in voltage can be addressed with supercapacitor systems to protect against downtime for
less than the cost of a battery storage system. According to the 2022 Grid Energy Storage
Technology Cost and Performance Assessment produced by Pacific Northwest National Laboratory
(PNNL) [19], the 2021 installed cost in $/kWh for a 1 MW/2 MWh standalone Li ion (LFP) battery
system is $500 per kWh of energy storage capacity. Thus a 1 MW (2 MWh) storage system would
therefore cost ~$1M.
A C-Link supercapacitor energy storage system would need to be large in physical size and cost to
match a 2 MWh nameplate capacity. It would feature a significantly higher installed $/kWh but
lower $/kW in comparison. However, supercapacitor systems are of interest for short-duration
applications. As such, nameplate energy storage capacity is secondary to peak-power capacity in
terms of importance. Since power quality and instantaneous demand response applications only
require discharge durations of a few seconds, price-per-power capacity ($/kW) is a more relevant
indicator than the price for energy ($/kWh). Specifically, a comparable 1 MW C-Link energy storage
system would cost $ 575,000, about half the cost of a battery system but it would be limited to only a
0.9 kWh of usable energy storage capacity, which is acceptable for short-duration, fast-response
applications.

5. CONCLUSIONS
A flexible, ruggedized, modular supercapacitor module with integrated power controls called C-Link
was designed and used in the fabrication of a supercapacitor energy storage system for the
demonstration project. The C-Link supercapacitor module design included a power management
and monitoring system for individual cell monitoring, cell balancing, over voltage, over current, over
temperature protections, temperature monitoring, and cooling, with plug-and-play connectors. This
design was deployed in a demonstration test system at OUC’s microgrid test site in Orlando, Florida,
to support three primary use cases: black start, voltage regulation, and load leveling. The
demonstration project was intended to help understand the ability of supercapacitor energy storage
systems to enhance power quality, reliability, and operational flexibility in modern microgrids, which
could be used for applications such as AI data center workloads.
The deployed 3.6-farad, 720-volt, supercapacitor energy storage system (302 kW, 0.26 kWh)
successfully supported three critical use cases: black start, voltage regulation, and load leveling in the
current demonstration project with the given transformer and the load.
The system:
 • Independently energized the microgrid and maintained operation for five minutes,
 simplifying the controls and sequencing typically required to operate a microgrid.
 • Mitigated transformer inrush currents, maintaining grid stability 50% of the time under the
 current converter constraints, compared to a 100% failure rate without the system. The
 performance was limited by the converter’s current rating, highlighting the importance of
 over-current ratings on converters.
 • Absorbed and delivered excess power, reducing variability and smoothing output without
 impacting downstream equipment.
Results demonstrate that supercapacitor energy storage systems are a viable option to improve
power resilience and quality to reduce downtime and stress on connected equipment for certain
applications.
Microgrids can help utilities quickly deploy additional electrical capacity, often supporting a larger
grid among other applications. This is of particular interest to help meet surging electricity demand
driven by the rapid growth of data centers and manufacturing plants. However, microgrids are only
effective if the energy produced is compatible with the connected loads. They must maintain
sufficient power quality during transients to be a reliable option for the end users and must be
capable of black start operation in case of outages.
Supercapacitors are an energy storage technology that are well suited to address these kinds of short-
duration power fluctuations. This project demonstrated a 3.6-farad, 720-volt, supercapacitor energy
storage system on a microgrid to improve power resilience, power quality, and reduce system down
time and stress. For broader deployment and confidence in supercapacitor energy systems for grid-
scale and microgrid applications, larger scale pilot projects are needed at the hundreds-of-kWh
range, operating for a longer time.
Supercapacitor energy storage systems in combination with battery storage offer a potential
economic solution to increase power resilience. This solution looks especially interesting for AI data
centers which experience much larger power fluctuations. Currently nickel-zinc (Ni-Zn) batteries are
being investigated for these applications; supercapacitors with much faster response characteristics
and a very long cycle life may offer a better choice for these applications.
