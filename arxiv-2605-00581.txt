Gradient Regularized Newton Boosting Trees with Global Convergence
Nikita Zozoulenko, Daniel Falkowski, Thomas Cass, Lukas Gonon

Abstract
Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDTs) dominate tabular machine learning, with modern implementations like XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost being based on Newton boosting: a second-order descent step in the space of decision trees. Despite its empirical success, the global convergence of Newton boosting is poorly understood compared to first-order boosting. In this paper, we introduce Restricted Newton Descent , which studies convex optimization with Newton’s method on Hilbert spaces with inexact iterates, based on the concepts of cosine angle and weak gradient edge. Within this framework, we recover Newton boosting with GBDTs and classical finite-dimensional theory as special cases. We first prove that vanilla Newton boosting achieves a linear rate of convergence for smooth, strongly convex losses that satisfy a Hessian-dominance condition. To handle general convex losses with Lipschitz Hessians, we extend a recent gradient regularized Newton scheme to the restricted weak learner setting. This scheme minimally modifies the classical algorithm by introducing an adaptive ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -regularization term proportional to the square root of the gradient norm at each iteration. We establish a 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 k 2 ) \mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{k^{2}}) rate for this scheme, thereby obtaining a globally convergent second-order GBDT algorithm with a rate matching that of first-order boosting with Nesterov momentum. In numerical experiments, we show that our scheme converges while vanilla Newton boosting may diverge.

2
Newton Boosting as Hilbert Space Convex Optimization

In this section, we recall Newton’s method in the Hilbert space setting, and introduce the
Restricted Newton Descent
framework where Newton boosting with GBDTs is recovered as a special case.

2.1
Hilbert Space Newton’s Method

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space. In this paper we consider twice Fréchet differentiable convex loss functions
L:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined below.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
L:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
Fréchet differentiable
if there exists a mapping
\nabla L:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
such that for all
h,f\in\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle L(h+f)=L(h)+\langle f,\nabla L(h)\rangle+o(\|f\|).

The element
\nabla L(h)
is called the
gradient
at
h
. A function
L:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
twice Fréchet differentiable
if
L
is Fréchet differentiable, and if for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
there exists a bounded, self-adjoint linear operator
\nabla^{2}L(h):\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
such that

\displaystyle L(h+f)=L(h)+\left\langle f,\nabla L(h)+\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}L(h)[f]\right\rangle+o(\|f\|^{2}),

for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
. The map
\nabla^{2}L(h)
is called the
Fréchet Hessian
(or second derivative) of
L
at
h
.

We aim to minimize
L
with Hilbert space Newton’s method with a fixed learning rate
\eta>0
. At each iteration
k
, we update an iterate
F_{k}=F_{k-1}+\eta f_{k}=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\eta f_{t}
by minimizing a second-order Taylor expansion of the loss:

\displaystyle L(F_{k}+f)

\displaystyle\approx L(F_{k})+\left\langle f,\nabla L(F_{k})+\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}L(F_{k})[f]\right\rangle

\displaystyle=L(F_{k})+\left\langle f,g_{k}+\frac{1}{2}H_{k}[f]\right\rangle

\displaystyle=:L(F_{k})+Q_{k}(f)

where
g_{k}=\nabla L(F_{k})
is the gradient,
H_{k}=\nabla^{2}L(F_{k})
is the Hessian operator, and
Q_{k}(f)
is the local second-order approximation of
L
at the point
F_{k}
, that is after
k
iterations of the optimization scheme. Differentiating
Q_{k}
yields

\displaystyle\nabla Q_{k}(f)=g_{k}+H_{k}[f].

Setting the derivative of
Q_{k}
to 0 and solving for
f
gives the Newton update, assuming that
H_{k}
is invertible at the point
g_{k}
:

\displaystyle f_{k+1}:=-H_{k}^{-1}[g_{k}]=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{H}}Q_{k}(f).

(1)

2.2
Restricted Newton Descent

In the context of gradient boosting, the exact update
f_{k+1}
in (
1
) is generally not attainable since weak learners, as opposed to exact Newton directions, are used to minimize the loss. Consequently, the ensemble
F_{k}
instead takes the form
F_{k}:=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\eta f_{t}^{w}
for some notion of a weak iterate
f^{w}_{t}
. To formalize this, we introduce the
Restricted Newton Descent
framework (not to be confused with constrained optimization). This differs from standard Newton’s method in two ways. Firstly, the gradient
g_{k}
and Hessian
H_{k}
are computed w.r.t. the sum of weak, rather than exact, iterates
F_{k}
. Secondly, we do not minimize
Q_{k}(f)
over the entire space
f\in\mathcal{H}
, but rather restrict the weak update
f_{k+1}^{w}
to belong to some subset
f_{k+1}^{w}\in\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{H}
of admissible weak learners. More specifically, given a family
\mathcal{F}
, we define the weak iterates
f_{k+1}
as any element

\displaystyle f_{k+1}^{w}\in\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}}Q_{k}(f).

(2)

Note that
f_{k+1}^{w}
is not necessarily unique (although it will be for decision trees). We consider general families
\mathcal{F}
satisfying the following scalability condition, similar to
20
:

Assumption 2.2
.

The family
\mathcal{F}
is closed under scalar multiplication. That is, if
f\in\mathcal{F}
then
af\in\mathcal{F}
for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
.

Decision trees, commonly used in boosting, satisfy this assumption. If
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{H}
, then the weak update
f^{w}_{k+1}
coincides with the exact update
f_{k+1}
, and standard Newton’s method is recovered. The full algorithm is detailed in
Algorithm

1
, with the exact step in parentheses.

Algorithm 1

Vanilla Restricted Newton

1:

Input:
Loss
L:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
. Learning rate
\eta
. Weak learner family
\mathcal{F}
. Initial value
F_{0}
.

2:

for

k=0,1,2,\dots

do

3:

g_{k},\>H_{k}\leftarrow\nabla L(F_{k}),\>\nabla^{2}L(F_{k})

4:

\big(
Exact target
f_{k+1}\leftarrow-H_{k}^{-1}g_{k}\big)

5:

Weak learner
f^{w}_{k+1}\leftarrow\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}}Q_{k}(f)

6:

F_{k+1}\leftarrow F_{k}+\eta f^{w}_{k+1}

7:

end

for

Theoretical analysis of any optimization scheme now depends on the error between the chosen weak iterate
f^{w}_{k+1}
and the exact iterate
f_{k+1}
, as this difference introduces an error component orthogonal to the optimal descent direction. As we show later, different schemes rely on different error metrics. For vanilla Newton’s method, the relevant metric is the cosine angle between
f_{k+1}
and
f^{w}_{k+1}
in the induced Hessian norm (see
Section

3
), whereas for the globally convergent gradient regularized scheme, the metric takes the form of a weak learner edge condition between the true gradient and the implied weak gradient (see
Section

4
).

2.3
GBDTs as Restricted Convex Optimization

In this section, we provide an overview of gradient boosting from a functional
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\nu)
perspective
(
14
;
33
)
, in the context of Restricted Newton Descent.

Let
(X,Y)\sim\nu
be a random sample with its corresponding probability measure
\nu
. Denote by
X\sim\nu_{X}
the features in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
, with targets
Y\sim\nu_{Y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
, and
\nu_{Y|X=x}
the conditional law. Let
\widehat{\nu}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(x_{i},y_{i})}
be the empirical measure of
\nu
with respect to a training dataset
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of size
N
. We work in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\nu_{X})
of square integrable functions defined on the feature space, with inner product
\langle f,g\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{X}}[\langle f(X),g(X)\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}]
. When
\nu=\widehat{\nu}_{N}
, this space can be identified with
\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}
using the inner product
\langle f,h\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle f(x_{i}),h(x_{i})\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}
. Let
l
be a twice differentiable convex loss function.

To derive the Newton boosting algorithm used by e.g. XGBoost and LightGBM as a special case of Restricted Newton Descent, we simply let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of weak decision trees up to a fixed depth, let the base space be
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{L}^{2}(\widehat{\nu}_{X,N})
, and the loss function be
L(F)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}l(F(x_{i}),y_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{N}}[l(F(X),Y)]
. In the space
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\nu_{X})
, the Fréchet gradient and Hessian take the form

\displaystyle\nabla L(F)(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{Y|X=x}}\big[\partial_{1}l(F(x),Y)\big],

(3)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}L(F)[f](x)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{Y|X=x}}\big[\partial_{11}l(F(x),Y)f(x)\big],

(4)

and are easily computed for empirical measures
\widehat{\nu}_{N}
as partial derivatives and matrices with respect to
F(x_{i})
. Write
g_{(i)}=\partial_{1}l(F_{k}(x_{i}),y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
and
h_{(i)}=\partial_{11}l(F_{t}(x_{i}),y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
. Following the XGBoost derivation
(
3
)
, the objective becomes to minimize

\displaystyle L(F_{k}+f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}l(F_{k}(x_{i})+f(x_{i}),y_{i})

\displaystyle\approx L(F_{k})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle f(x_{i}),g_{(i)}+\frac{1}{2}h_{(i)}f(x_{i})\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}

\displaystyle=L(F_{k})+Q_{k}(f)

which we see coincides with the Hilbert space derivation using
Q_{k}
. Furthermore, in boosting we minimize over the set of weak learners
f\in\mathcal{F}
rather than over all
L^{2}(\widehat{\nu}_{N})
. Letting
f
be a decision tree of the form
f(x)=\sum_{j}w_{j}I_{j}(x)
where
I_{j}(x)\in\{0,1\}
is a disjoint partition of the feature space, one finds that the optimal weights
w_{j}
minimizing
Q_{k}(f)
are

\displaystyle w_{j}

\displaystyle=-\left(\sum_{i\in I_{j}}h_{(i)}\right)^{-1}\sum_{i\in I_{j}}g_{(i)},

coinciding with the classical XGBoost formula. See
Section

A.2
for more details and full derivation.

3
Restricted Newton for Hessian-dominated Losses

Before introducing the globally convergent scheme in
Section

4
, we first analyze the vanilla Newton update via Restricted Newton Descent. We prove for smooth strongly convex losses satisfying a Hessian-dominance condition, that vanilla Newton’s method with weak learners converges globally with a linear rate. This analysis broadly parallels that of the LogitBoost analysis
(
34
)
, but we analyze it within our Restricted Newton Descent formulation in a more general setting beyond logistic loss. To ensure the Newton update is well-defined on
\mathcal{H}
, we assume the following:

Assumption 3.1
.

We assume that the equation
g_{k}=-H_{k}f
admits a unique solution
f=f_{k+1}=-H_{k}^{-1}g_{k}
.

3.1
is satisfied, for example, when
L
is strongly convex, or in many practical settings when
\mathcal{H}
is a finite dimensional space with a strictly convex loss
L
. Note that this assumption is not required for the gradient regularized scheme in
Section

4
.

3.1
Cosine Angle

Recall that the iterates of Restricted Newton Descent are derived by minimizing
Q_{k}(f)=\left\langle f,g_{k}+\frac{1}{2}H_{k}f\right\rangle
restricted to
f\in\mathcal{F}
at each iteration
k
, yielding
F_{k}=\sum_{t=1}^{k}\eta f^{w}_{t}
. In order to analyze the restricted Newton scheme, we need a way to measure the error between the exact direction
f_{k+1}
and the weak learner
f^{w}_{k+1}
. It turns out that the critical quantity determining the performance of this scheme, is the cosine angle between
f_{k+1}
and
f^{w}_{k+1}
measured in the Newton geometry induced by the Hessian. For this, we use the following norm definition.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
A:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be a self-adjoint positive operator. We define the inner product induced by
A
as

\langle h,f\rangle_{A}:=\langle h,Af\rangle.

(5)

When
A=H_{k}
, we call this the
Hessian-induced inner product
, with induced norm
\|\cdot\|_{H_{k}}
.

The following lemma motivates the use of the Hessian-induced norm and naturally leads to the definition of the cosine angle between weak and exact iterates.

Lemma 3.3
.

Minimizing
Q_{k}(f)
is equivalent to finding a closest element
f\in\mathcal{F}
to the exact Newton direction
f_{k+1}
in the Hessian-induced norm
\|\cdot\|_{H_{k}}
.

Proof.

Using the Hessian-induced norm and the exact Newton update
f_{k+1}
, we can write

\displaystyle\|f-f_{k+1}\|^{2}_{H_{k}}

\displaystyle=\|f\|_{H_{k}}^{2}-2\langle f,f_{k+1}\rangle_{H_{k}}+\|f_{k+1}\|_{H_{k}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle f,H_{k}f\rangle+2\langle f,g_{k}\rangle+\|f_{k+1}\|^{2}_{H_{k}}

\displaystyle=2Q_{k}(f)+\|f_{k+1}\|^{2}_{H_{k}}.\qed

Definition 3.4
.

Consider the iterates produced by vanilla Newton’s method. At each iteration
k
, we define the Hessian-induced cosine angle
\Theta_{k}\in[0,1]
between the exact update
f_{k+1}
and the weak learner
f_{k+1}^{w}
as

\Theta_{k}:=\frac{\langle f_{k+1},f_{k+1}^{w}\rangle_{H_{k}}}{\|f_{k+1}\|_{H_{k}}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{H_{k}}},\qquad\Theta:=\inf_{k}\Theta_{k}.

(6)

In contrast to
34
, which uses a weak learnability assumption specific to binary classification, more recent prior work uses definitions similar in spirit to ours, but defines them by taking suprema and infima over much larger spaces of permissible weak learners and targets
(
21
;
20
;
29
)
; see also
(
14
)
. Our definition is simpler and more direct, capturing the specific two vectors
f^{w}_{k+1}
and
f_{k+1}
determining the cosine angle. Using this definition, we immediately obtain the following properties connecting the weak and exact Newton iterates with the gradient
g_{k}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is closed under scalar multiplication, then

1.

\|f_{k+1}-f_{k+1}^{w}\|^{2}_{H_{k}}=(1-\Theta_{k}^{2})\|f_{k+1}\|^{2}_{H_{k}}
,

2.

\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{H_{k}}=\Theta_{k}\|f_{k+1}\|_{H_{k}}
,

3.

\|f^{w}_{k+1}\|_{H_{k}}^{2}=-\langle g_{k},f_{k+1}^{w}\rangle
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{F}
is closed under scalar multiplication, the objective
Q_{k}(\alpha f_{k+1}^{w})
is minimized at
\alpha=1
. Differentiating the equivalent objective (see
Lemma

3.3
)
\phi(\alpha):=\frac{1}{2}\|\alpha f_{k+1}^{w}-f_{k+1}\|_{H_{k}}^{2}
with respect to
\alpha
gives the derivative
\phi^{\prime}(\alpha)=\langle f_{k+1}^{w},\alpha f_{k+1}^{w}-f_{k+1}\rangle_{H_{k}}
. Setting this to zero at
\alpha=1
yields

\langle f_{k+1},f_{k+1}^{w}\rangle_{H_{k}}=\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{H_{k}}^{2}.

Substituting this into the definition of
\Theta_{k}
, we obtain

\Theta_{k}=\frac{\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{H_{k}}^{2}}{\|f_{k+1}\|_{H_{k}}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{H_{k}}}=\frac{\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{H_{k}}}{\|f_{k+1}\|_{H_{k}}},

(7)

which proves the second identity. The first identity then follows immediately from expanding the norm using inner products. The third identity follows from the fact that
-\langle g_{k},f_{k+1}^{w}\rangle=\langle H_{k}f_{k+1},f_{k+1}^{w}\rangle=\langle f_{k+1},f_{k+1}^{w}\rangle_{H_{k}}
.
∎

3.2
Decrease in Loss for Vanilla Restricted Newton

We now show that the restricted Newton scheme guarantees a strict decrease in loss at each iteration, forming the basis of our convergence analysis. We rely on the following standard result from optimization theory, see
Appendix

B
for more details.

Lemma 3.6
.

If the loss
L
is
S
-smooth, then for all
f\in\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle L(F_{k}+f)\leq L(F_{k})+\langle g_{k},f\rangle+\frac{S}{2}\|f\|^{2}.

(8)

Definition 3.7
.

We say that the loss
L
is
Hessian-dominated
if there exists a constant
c>0
such that the exact iterates
f_{k+1}
satisfy
\|f_{k+1}\|^{2}_{H_{k}}\geq cL(F_{k})
.

Definition

3.7
generalizes the condition derived in
(
34
, Theorem 4)
for LogitBoost, to general losses using the language of Hessian norms and weak iterates. Examples of Hessian-dominated losses include binary and categorical cross entropy, both with constant
c=1
(see
Appendix

F
for more details).

We are now ready to state and prove our main convergence result for Restricted Newton Descent.

Theorem 3.8

(
Global Rate I
)
.

If
L
is
S
-smooth,
\mu
-strongly convex, and
\mathcal{F}
closed under scalar multiplication, then for any learning rate
\eta\in(0,\frac{2\mu}{S})
we have

\displaystyle L(F_{k})-L(F_{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq\eta\left(1-\frac{\eta S}{2\mu}\right)\Theta^{2}\|f_{k+1}\|_{H_{k}}^{2}.

(9)

Consequently, if
L
satisfies
Definition

3.7
, then vanilla restricted Newton’s method converges linearly with rate
(1-\rho)
, where
\rho=c\Theta^{2}\eta(1-\frac{\eta S}{2\mu})
:

\displaystyle L(F_{k+1})\leq(1-\rho)L(F_{k}).

(10)

Proof.

Starting with the standard smoothness upper bound
L(F+f)\leq L(F)+\langle g,f\rangle+\frac{S}{2}\|f\|^{2}
from
Lemma

3.6
, we substitute the weak update
f=\eta f^{w}_{k+1}
. Using strong convexity to write
\|u\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\mu}\|u\|_{H_{k}}^{2}
, and the cosine angle properties from
Lemma

3.5
, we obtain

\displaystyle L(F_{k})-L(F_{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq-\eta\langle g_{k},f_{k+1}^{w}\rangle-\frac{S\eta^{2}}{2}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|^{2}

(11)

\displaystyle\geq\eta\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{H_{k}}^{2}-\frac{S\eta^{2}}{2\mu}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{H_{k}}^{2}

(12)

\displaystyle=\left(\eta-\frac{S\eta^{2}}{2\mu}\right)\Theta_{k}^{2}\|f_{k+1}\|_{H_{k}}^{2}.

(13)

Applying
Definition

3.7
, we get
L(F_{k+1})\leq L(F_{k})-\rho L(F_{k})
, which implies linear convergence.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

Our bound compares favourably to SnapBoost
(
29
)
, which proved a linear rate of
1-\rho_{snap}=1-\Theta^{2}_{snap}\frac{\mu^{2}}{S^{2}}
for a fixed learning rate
\eta=\frac{\mu}{S}
. While our definitions of
\Theta
are comparable, our result yields a better dependence on the condition number
\frac{S}{\mu}
. Furthermore, we obtain essentially the same rate as
34
, where they instead use a change of Hessian lemma, and their probability clamping is replaced by strong convexity in our lemma.

While cross entropy loss itself is not strongly convex, this issue is typically addressed in two ways. First, for practical applications
\ell_{2}
-regularization is almost always applied to the loss function, yielding a strongly convex loss. Second, probability values can be clipped to
[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]
for some
\epsilon>0
as in
(
34
)
, which implicitly implies strong convexity. An alternative approach to
Theorem

3.8
could be to clip the output tree values, in combination with a
change of Hessian Lemma
, similar to
(
34
, Lemma 9)
to obtain a linear rate. However, we believe our approach offers a more concise and simpler theoretical treatment.

4
Gradient Regularized Restricted Newton

Despite the fast quadratic local convergence of Newton’s method in finite-dimensional convex optimization, the method does not have global convergence guarantees for general smooth convex functions. A classical example of this is
L(x)=\sqrt{1+x^{2}}
defined for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, see
Appendix

E
. However, cubic regularized Newton
(
26
)
and the more recent gradient regularized Newton
(
24
;
7
)
achieve a global
O(\frac{1}{k^{2}})
convergence rate. In this section, we extend the latter scheme to Newton boosting with weak learners within our framework of Restricted Newton Descent.

Instead of minimizing the local quadratic approximation
Q_{k}(f)
at each iteration
k
, gradient regularized Newton’s method minimizes the following regularized objective:

\displaystyle Q_{k}^{reg}(f)

\displaystyle=\left\langle f,g_{k}+\frac{1}{2}H_{k}[f]\right\rangle+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\|f\|^{2}

\displaystyle=Q_{k}(f)+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\|f\|^{2},

where
\lambda_{k}\in(0,\infty)
is a scalar regularization parameter. For losses with
2M
-Lipschitz continuous Hessians, we will allow values
\lambda_{k}\geq\sqrt{M\|g_{k}\|}
. Minimizing
Q_{k}^{reg}(f)
yields the exact regularized update

\displaystyle f_{k+1}=-(H_{k}+\lambda_{k}I)^{-1}[g_{k}].

(14)

In other words, gradient regularized Newton is simply an
\ell_{2}
-regularized Newton step where the
\ell_{2}
-regularization is adaptive at each iteration, being proportional to the square root of the gradient norm
\sqrt{\|g_{k}\|}
. Similar to
Section

3
, we consider Restricted Newton Descent using weak learners rather than the exact update. The full gradient regularized scheme is detailed below in
Algorithm

2
(the implicit exact step is denoted in parentheses):

L:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
with
2M
-Lipschitz Hessian. Learning rate
\eta
. Weak learner family
\mathcal{F}
. Initial
F_{0}
.

for

do

4.1
Properties of Gradient Regularization

We begin by establishing some basic properties the gradient regularized scheme. In the subsequent analysis, define
K_{k}:=H_{k}+\lambda_{k}I
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The exact regularized update (
14
) satisfies

\lambda_{k}f_{k+1}=-\left(g_{k}+H_{k}[f_{k+1}]\right).

(15)

Proof.

Multiply the Newton update (
14
) by
(H_{k}+\lambda_{k}I)
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

If
L
is convex, then for all
u\in\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\|u\|_{K_{k}}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\langle(H_{k}+\lambda_{k}I)u,u\rangle=\lambda_{k}\|u\|^{2}+\langle u,H_{k}u\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{k}\|u\|^{2}.

Furthermore, if the weak learner family
\mathcal{F}
is closed under scalar multiplication, then the iterates of
Algorithm

2
satisfy

1.

\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{K_{k}}^{2}=-\langle g_{k},f_{k+1}^{w}\rangle
,

2.

\lambda_{k}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|\leq\|g_{k}\|
.

Proof.

The first result follows by convexity. For the rest, let

\phi(t):=Q_{k}^{reg}(tf_{k+1}^{w})=\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{K_{k}}^{2}+t\langle g_{k},f_{k+1}^{w}\rangle.

Differentiating gives
0=\phi^{\prime}(t)=t\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{K_{k}}^{2}+\langle g_{k},f_{k+1}^{w}\rangle
. Since
\mathcal{F}
was assumed to be scalable, the minimum must be attained at
t=1
, and the result follows. As for the last inequality, this follows by the first inequality and Cauchy-Schwartz:

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|^{2}\leq\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{K_{k}}^{2}=-\langle g_{k},f_{k+1}^{w}\rangle\leq\|g_{k}\|\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|.

∎

We impose a standard regularity condition on the loss
L
in terms of the Lipschitz continuity of its Hessian, as is common for regularized Newton methods
(
26
;
25
;
24
;
7
)
.

Assumption 4.3
.

The Fréchet Hessian
\nabla^{2}L:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{L(H)}
is
2M
-Lipschitz continuous for some constant
M>0
:

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}L(f)-\nabla^{2}L(g)\|\leq 2M\|f-g\|\qquad\forall f,g\in\mathcal{H}.

Before we delve into the convergence rate analysis, we recall the following standard results on Lipschitz Hessians, well known in the finite-dimensional
\mathbb{R}^{d}
setting. See
Appendix

B
for proof.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\nabla^{2}L
is
2M
-Lipschitz, then for all
f,g\in\mathcal{H}

1.

L(f)\leq L(g)+\left\langle\nabla L(g),f-g\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}L(g)(f-g),f-g\rangle+\frac{M}{3}\|f-g\|^{3}
,

2.

\|\nabla L(f)-\nabla L(g)-\nabla^{2}L(g)(f-g)\|\leq M\|g-f\|^{2}
.

The following lemma shows that the weak iterates of
Algorithm

2
guarantee a strict decrease in loss. From this point on, we assume that
\mathcal{F}
is closed under scalar multiplication.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
L
is convex with
2M
-Lipschitz Hessian, and
\eta\leq 2
, then the iterates of
Algorithm

2
satisfy

\displaystyle L(F_{k})-L(F_{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq\left(\eta-\frac{\eta^{3}}{3}\right)\lambda_{k}\|f^{w}_{k+1}\|^{2}

(16)

Proof.

Using
Lemma

4.4
and
Lemma

4.2
, we write

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle(F_{k+1})-L(F_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\eta\langle g_{k},f_{k+1}^{w}\rangle+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\langle H_{k}(f_{k+1}^{w}),f_{k+1}^{w}\rangle+\frac{M}{3}\eta^{3}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|^{3}

\displaystyle=-\eta\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{K_{k}}^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{K_{k}}^{2}-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\lambda_{k}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|^{2}+\frac{M}{3}\eta^{3}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|^{3}

\displaystyle=-\left(\eta-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\right)\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|_{K_{k}}^{2}-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\lambda_{k}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|^{2}+\frac{M}{3}\eta^{3}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|^{3}.

By
Lemma

4.2
and
\lambda_{k}\geq\sqrt{M\|g_{k}\|}
we see that

\displaystyle M\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|^{3}\leq\frac{M\|g_{k}\|}{\lambda_{k}}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|^{2}\leq\lambda_{k}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|^{2}.

Combining the above, and again using
Lemma

4.2
, gives the result.
∎

4.2
Convergence of Restricted GRN

We now require a way to quantify how well the weak updates
f^{w}_{k+1}
approximate the exact updates
f_{k+1}
in the gradient regularized scheme. Previously in
Theorem

3.8
for vanilla Newton, the decrease in loss was determined by the Hessian-induced norm
\|\cdot\|_{H_{k}}
. In contrast, for the regularized scheme the decrease in loss is governed by the quantity
\lambda_{k}\|f^{w}_{k+1}\|
. As such, the cosine angle from
Definition

3.2
is no longer well suited for theoretical analysis of GRN, since the scheme and its convergence proof are fundamentally driven by gradient growth rather than directional alignment. Consequently, motivated by
Lemma

4.1
, we introduce the notion of an implied weak gradient
g^{w}_{k}
, together with a corresponding weak gradient edge condition. This condition essentially states that the weak gradient remains within a relative radius of the true gradient, at each iteration
k
.

Definition 4.6
.

We define the
implied weak gradient
as

\displaystyle g_{k}^{w}:=-(H_{k}+\lambda_{k}I)f_{k+1}^{w}.

(17)

We say that the iterates
f^{w}_{k+1}
have
weak gradient edge

\gamma
if there exists a real number
0<\gamma\leq 1
satisfying

\displaystyle\|g_{k}^{w}-g_{k}\|^{2}\leq(1-\gamma^{2})\|g_{k}\|^{2}.

(18)

The following lemma relates the gradient at consecutive iterations and serves as the primary tool for our convergence analysis.

Lemma 4.7

(
Gradient Growth
)
.

If the iterates of
Algorithm

2
have weak gradient edge
\gamma
, then

\displaystyle\|g_{k+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\eta^{2}+\eta\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}\right)\|g_{k}\|.

(19)

Proof Sketch.

Motivated by
Lemma

4.4
, we use the identity

\displaystyle g_{k+1}

\displaystyle=g_{k}+\eta H_{k}f^{w}_{k+1}+E

(20)

\displaystyle=g_{k}+\eta(-g_{k}^{w}-\lambda_{k}f_{k+1}^{w})+E

(21)

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)g_{k}+\eta(g_{k}-g_{k}^{w})-\eta\lambda_{k}f^{w}_{k+1}+E

(22)

where
\|E\|\leq M\eta^{2}\|f^{w}_{k+1}\|^{2}
. Applying the triangle inequality, the weak learner edge, and
Lemma

4.2
yields the result. In the upcoming convergence rate proofs, both the final bound (
19
) and intermediate expressions using (
22
) play an important role, see
Appendix

D
for proofs.
∎

We now present our main result: establishing the global convergence of gradient regularized Newton’s method with weak learners. Despite the inexact updates inherent to boosting and Restricted Newton Descent,
Algorithm

2
retains the
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{k^{2}})
rate, matching first-order momentum boosting
(
20
)
without the requirement of fitting two trees per iteration. We use the following standard definition from the cubic Newton literature.

Definition 4.8
.

L
has
finite sublevel sets
if a finite optimum
F^{*}\in\argmin_{F\in\mathcal{H}}L(F)
exists, and if there exists a constant
\Lambda>0
such that
L(F)\leq L(F_{0})\implies\|F-F^{*}\|\leq\Lambda
.

Theorem 4.9

(
Global Rate II
)
.

Assume
L
is convex with
2M
-Lipschitz Hessian, finite sublevel sets with diameter
\Lambda
, and weak gradient edge
\gamma
. Let
\lambda_{k}=C\sqrt{M\|g_{k}\|}
for some
C\geq 1
. For any learning rate
\eta\in(0,1]
, the gradient regularized Newton scheme has global convergence rate

\displaystyle L(

\displaystyle F_{k})-L(F^{*})\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\max\left(\frac{C^{2}M\Lambda^{3}}{\eta^{2}k^{2}\gamma^{12}},\>\>\Lambda\exp\left(-k\eta\gamma^{2}\big(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}+2-2\eta\big)/8\right)\right).

Proof Sketch.

We generalize the analysis of
24
to the Hilbert space setting with inexact weak learners. The proof proceeds by analyzing two regimes based on the fraction of iterates where the gradient fails to contract sufficiently, determined by
\eta
and
\gamma
. In the first regime, we derive a recurrence of the form
\alpha_{k+1}\leq\alpha_{k}-\tau\alpha_{k}^{3/2}
for some
\tau>0
, yielding a
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\eta^{2}\gamma^{12}k^{2}})
rate. In the second regime, the cumulative contraction dominates, resulting in a linear rate. The global rate is governed by the maximum of these two bounds; see
Appendix

D
for more details.
∎

Theorem 4.10

(
Local Rate
)
.

Let
\lambda_{k}=C\sqrt{M\|g_{k}\|}
for some
C\geq 1
. If
L
is
\mu
-strongly convex with
2M
-Lipschitz Hessian, weak gradient edge
\gamma
, and if there exists a
k_{0}\geq 0
such that
\|g_{k_{0}}\|\leq\frac{\mu^{2}}{4C^{2}M}\left(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}{1+\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}\right)^{2}
, then for all
k\geq k_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\|g_{k+1}\|\leq\frac{3+\rho}{4}\|g_{k}\|,

(23)

where
\rho=1-\eta(1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}})=\mathcal{O}(1-\eta\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2})
. Hence the iterates of
Algorithm

2
converge linearly locally.

Proof Sketch.

The weak gradient edge implies that
\|g_{k}^{w}\|\leq\left(1+\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}\right)\|g_{k}\|:=C_{\gamma}\|g_{k}\|
, while the
\mu
-strong convexity implies that

\displaystyle\|f^{w}_{k+1}\|=\|(H_{k}+\lambda I)^{-1}g^{w}_{k}\|\leq\frac{1}{\mu}\|g^{w}\|\leq\frac{C_{\gamma}}{\mu}\|g^{w}\|.

Combining these bounds with
Lemmas

4.2
and
4.7
yields the recurrence

\displaystyle\|g_{k+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\rho\|g_{k}\|+\sqrt{C^{2}M\eta^{2}\frac{C_{\gamma}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}}\|g_{k}\|^{\frac{3}{2}}+C^{2}M\eta^{2}\frac{C_{\gamma}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\|g_{k}\|^{2}.

The result follows by solving for the region of strict contraction
\|g_{k+1}\|<\|g_{k}\|
, where the dependence on
\eta
cancels out. See
Appendix

D
for details.
∎

Remark 4.11
.

If
\eta=1
and
\gamma=1
, the coefficient of the linear
\|g_{k}\|
term vanishes, allowing for a superlinear rate with exponent
\frac{3}{2}
, recovering the classical result of
24
. However, when
\eta<1
or
\gamma<1
, this term is non-zero, and we are only able to prove a linear rate. We conjecture that superlinear rates are generally unattainable in Newton schemes involving weak iterates, suggesting an interesting direction for future research.

5
Practical Considerations

In this section, we discuss when the regularity assumptions of
Theorems

3.8
,
4.9
and
4.10
are satisfied in practice for boosting, and how the GRN scheme can be implemented into existing tree boosting algorithms with negligible computational overhead.

5.1
Regularity of the Boosting Objective

Our convergence analysis relies on standard convex optimization assumptions, such as Lipschitz-continuous Hessians, smoothness, and strong convexity. Using the same notation as
Section

2.3
, because the boosting empirical risk
L(F)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}l(F(x_{i}),y_{i})
is constructed from a pre-specified element-wise loss
l
(e.g., MSE, cross entropy, Charbonnier), one can prove that the regularity of
l
carries over to
L
in the boosting space
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{L}^{2}(\widehat{\nu}_{X,N})
. We formalize this result below. Note, however, that one must be careful when dealing with the previously mentioned Lipschitz constants: the regularity conditions of
l
are defined in the Euclidean geometry, while the Lipschitz constants used by
Theorems

3.8
,
4.9
and
4.10
are defined in the boosting space with its own intrinsic geometry and inner products.

Proposition 5.1
.

If the element-wise loss
l(\cdot,y_{i})
has an
M_{0}
-Lipschitz Hessian in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
uniformly across all fixed targets
y_{i}
, then the empirical risk
L
has an
M_{0}\sqrt{N}
-Lipschitz Hessian in the boosting space
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\widehat{\nu}_{X,N})
. Additionally, if
l
is
S
-smooth or
\mu
-strongly convex, then
L
is also
S
-smooth or
\mu
-strongly convex, respectively.

The proof can be found in
Appendix

C
. This result ensures that if the base loss
l
is well-behaved, the boosting objective is as well. Moreover, because
l
is chosen prior to training, its Lipschitz constant
M_{0}
can be computed analytically. This avoids the need for adaptive backtracking line search algorithms to estimate this constant, as is typically required in finite-dimensional convex optimization
(
15
;
24
;
18
)
.

5.2
Implementing GRN for GBDTs

The GRN scheme is very simple to implement in practice and only differs from vanilla Newton boosting by an extra additive
\ell_{2}
-regularization term based on the gradient norm. Both methods use the exact same split finding, gain calculation, and leaf fitting formulas. The only difference lies in the effective
\ell_{2}
-regularization used. Standard tree boosting implementations (e.g., XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) use a static
\ell_{2}
-regularization
\lambda_{\text{base}}
. GRN, on the other hand, uses an adaptive regularization term
\lambda_{k}
calculated via

\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{\text{base}}+\sqrt{M\|g_{k}\|_{\mathcal{H}}}.

(24)

Using the notation of
Section

2.3
, the exact gradient norm in the boosting space is computed from the sample gradients
g_{(i)}
as
\|g_{k}\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|g_{(i)}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}\big)^{1/2}
. Because updating this adaptive
\ell_{2}
term only requires a single vector norm calculation and a scalar addition, and the main computational bottleneck for GBDT algorithms is split finding, the computational overhead of GRN is negligible.

Numerical Experiments

In this section, we empirically validate our theoretical findings regarding the convergence of boosting schemes and the behaviour of weak learner conditions. We implement first-order gradient boosting, Newton boosting, and the Gradient Regularized Newton (GRN) boosting scheme in JAX
(
2
)
, following the standard depth-wise greedy tree construction strategy used by XGBoost
(
3
)
. The code is provided in the supplementary material and at
github.com/nikitazozoulenko/global-newton-boosting
.

6.1
Charbonnier loss divergence and convergence

The Charbonnier loss, defined as
l(\widehat{y},y)=\sqrt{1+(y-\widehat{y})^{2}}

-1
, is a classical example of a loss function where Newton’s method can diverge (see
Appendix

E
). We investigate if this also is true for boosting, using the Wine Quality UCI dataset
(
5
)
with depth 4 trees. For GRN we set
M=1
, and otherwise use a roughly equivalent static
\ell_{2}
-regularization of
1.0
when explicitly stated.
Figure

1
compares the training loss of Newton boosting, first-order gradient boosting, and GRN for different learning rates
\eta
. For
\eta=1
, vanilla Newton boosting diverges, whereas GRN decreases the fastest. For a smaller
\eta=0.1
, GRN initially tracks the trajectory of fixed
\ell_{2}
-regularized Newton boosting but eventually outperforms it once the gradient norm decreases sufficiently. Vanilla Newton boosting minimizes the loss most rapidly in this scenario, but is limited to smaller learning rates. Notably,
\ell_{2}
-regularized models will converge to a regularized optimum, whereas GRN provably minimizes the original non-regularized loss.

6.2
Weak learner conditions and tree depth

To better understand the theoretical constants governing convergence, we train models on a subset of the Higgs dataset
(
36
)
using BCE loss with
\ell_{2}
-regularization. We compute the cosine angle
\Theta_{k}
and weak learner edge
\gamma_{k}
in
Figure

2
at every iteration
k
, via (
6
) and (
18
), by instantiating
f^{w}_{k+1},f_{k+1},g^{w}_{k}
, and
g_{k}
as vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. As expected, increasing tree depth results in stronger weak learners, reflected in higher values for
\Theta_{k}
and
\gamma_{k}
. We observe that these metrics are high during the initial iterations of boosting and eventually plateau at positive values. The trajectories of the cosine angle
\Theta_{k}
and weak gradient edge
\gamma_{k}
are remarkably similar, which is somewhat unexpected given that they are defined in different geometries. Conceptually,
\gamma_{k}
(
Definition

4.6
) can be viewed as an approximate
(H_{k}+\lambda_{k}I)^{2}
-induced cosine angle between
f^{w}_{k+1}
and
f_{k+1}

H_{k}
-induced cosine angle
\Theta_{k}
. If
f^{w}_{k+1}
and
f_{k+1}
were orthogonal under the
(H_{k}+\lambda_{k}I)^{2}
-induced norm,
\gamma_{k}
would constitute a true cosine angle; however, we cannot theoretically guarantee this at present.

A similar relationship to tree depth holds for dataset size; larger datasets generally imply harder optimization problems, resulting in smaller cosine angles for a fixed tree depth. Furthermore, as optimization progresses, fitting the diminishing residuals becomes increasingly difficult for the weak learners, naturally leading to lower cosine angles.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced Restricted Newton Descent to analyze the global convergence of vanilla Newton boosting and a novel gradient regularized Newton boosting scheme, based on the cosine angle
\Theta
and weak gradient edge
\gamma
. For vanilla Newton boosting, we proved a linear convergence rate for Hessian-dominated losses. For the gradient regularized scheme, we established a global
\mathcal{O}(1/k^{2})
rate for general convex losses with Lipschitz Hessians. Our results suggest several promising directions for future research. First, given that finite-dimensional cubic and gradient regularized Newton can be accelerated to
\mathcal{O}(1/k^{3})
, investigating whether such acceleration can be realized in the boosting setting is a natural next step. Second, it would be interesting to study the Restriction operation for other optimization schemes to determine which convergence properties are retained under weak updates, and see if the dependence on
\Theta^{2}
and
\gamma^{12}
can be improved. Finally, investigating the possibility of superlinear rates with weak learners and establishing generalization bounds for Newton boosting remain open problems. Future work will also explore other applications of Restricted Newton Descent beyond the gradient boosting setting.

Appendix A
Discussions on Losses and Gradient Boosting Trees

In this appendix, we provide explicit calculations for the gradients and Hessians of common loss functions used in boosting. We also provide the full derivation of the optimal weight update for decision trees within the Newton boosting framework, recovering the standard XGBoost formula.

A.1
Common loss functions used in boosting

We denote the prediction for the
i
-th data point as
u_{i}=F(x_{i})
. The gradient and Hessian with respect to the prediction are denoted by
g_{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
and
h_{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
, respectively.

Mean Squared Error (MSE):

For the quadratic loss
l(\widehat{y},y)=\frac{1}{2}(\widehat{y}-y)^{2}
, the gradient and Hessian are given by:

\displaystyle g_{(i)}

\displaystyle=\widehat{y}_{i}-y_{i},

\displaystyle h_{(i)}

\displaystyle=1.

Binary Cross Entropy (BCE):

For binary classification, let
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
be the label and
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
be the logit. The loss is defined as
l(u_{i},y_{i})=-[y_{i}\log\sigma(u_{i})+(1-y_{i})\log(1-\sigma(u_{i}))]
, where
\sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}
is the sigmoid function. Using the chain rule, one finds that the derivatives are

\displaystyle g_{(i)}

\displaystyle=\sigma(u_{i})-y_{i},

\displaystyle h_{(i)}

\displaystyle=\sigma(u_{i})(1-\sigma(u_{i})).

Categorical Cross Entropy (CCE):

In the multi-class setting (
K>2
), let
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{K}
be the one-hot encoded label and
u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
be the vector of logits. The loss is
l(u_{i},y_{i})=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}(y_{i})_{k}\log(p_{i})_{k}
, where
p_{i}=\operatorname{softmax}(u_{i})
is the vector of class probabilities. The gradient and Hessian are

\displaystyle g_{(i)}

\displaystyle=p_{i}-y_{i},

\displaystyle h_{(i)}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}(p_{i})-p_{i}p_{i}^{\top},

where
\text{diag}(p_{i})
is the diagonal matrix with elements of
p_{i}
on the diagonal, and
p_{i}p_{i}^{\top}
denotes the vector outer product.

A.2
Newton Tree Boosting Derivation

Let
f(x)
be a decision tree defined by
f(x)=\sum_{j}w_{j}I_{j}(x)
, where
\{I_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L}
is a disjoint partition of the feature space (i.e., the leaves of the tree) and
w_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
are the leaf values. The second-order approximation of the loss,
Q(f)
, is given by

\displaystyle Q(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle f(x_{i}),g_{(i)}+\frac{1}{2}h_{(i)}f(x_{i})\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle f(x_{i}),g_{(i)}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle f(x_{i}),h_{(i)}f(x_{i})\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}.

We analyze the linear and quadratic terms separately. Substituting the definition of the tree
f(x)
, we find that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle f(x_{i}),g_{(i)}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle\sum_{j}w_{j}I_{j}(x_{i}),g_{(i)}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left\langle w_{j},\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{j}(x_{i})g_{(i)}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left\langle w_{j},\sum_{i\in I_{j}}g_{(i)}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}.

For the quadratic term, we use the property that the regions are disjoint, implying
I_{j}(x)I_{k}(x)=\delta_{jk}I_{j}(x)
, where
\delta_{jk}
is the Kronecker delta.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle f(x_{i}),h_{(i)}f(x_{i})\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle\sum_{j}w_{j}I_{j}(x_{i}),h_{(i)}\sum_{k}w_{k}I_{k}(x_{i})\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j}\sum_{k}I_{j}(x_{i})I_{k}(x_{i})\left\langle w_{j},h_{(i)}w_{k}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j}I_{j}(x_{i})\left\langle w_{j},h_{(i)}w_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\sum_{i\in I_{j}}\left\langle w_{j},h_{(i)}w_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left\langle w_{j},\left(\sum_{i\in I_{j}}h_{(i)}\right)w_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}.

The total objective decomposes into a sum of independent objectives for each leaf
j
. Differentiating with respect to a specific weight
w_{j}
, we obtain the local quadratic

\displaystyle q(w_{j})=\left\langle w_{j},\sum_{i\in I_{j}}g_{(i)}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle w_{j},\left(\sum_{i\in I_{j}}h_{(i)}\right)w_{j}\right\rangle.

Taking the gradient with respect to
w_{j}
yields

\displaystyle\nabla q(w_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I_{j}}g_{(i)}+\left(\sum_{i\in I_{j}}h_{(i)}\right)w_{j}.

Setting the gradient to zero gives the optimal weight update

\displaystyle w_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle=-\left(\sum_{i\in I_{j}}h_{(i)}\right)^{-1}\sum_{i\in I_{j}}g_{(i)}.

Substituting
w_{j}^{*}
back into the objective (and defining
G_{j}=\sum_{i\in I_{j}}g_{(i)}
and
H_{j}=\sum_{i\in I_{j}}h_{(i)}
), we recover the standard score used in Newton boosting and XGBoost:

\displaystyle Q(f^{*})=\sum_{j}q(w_{j}^{*})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\left\langle H_{j}^{-1}G_{j},G_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}.

Remark A.1
.

The
\ell_{2}
-regularization used in standard implementations like XGBoost differs slightly from the one suggested by our theory. XGBoost adds a fixed scalar
\lambda
to the aggregated leaf Hessian, yielding the update
w_{j}^{*}=-(H_{j}+\lambda)^{-1}G_{j}
. By contrast, our framework regularizes the weak learner in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\widehat{\nu}_{N})
. In this space, the regularization modifies the Hessian operator pointwise, which is equivalent to replacing each
h_{(i)}
with
h_{(i)}+\lambda
. Summing these over the partition
I_{j}
results in the update
w_{j}^{*}=-(H_{j}+|I_{j}|\lambda)^{-1}G_{j}
. Thus, the theoretically consistent regularization scales proportionally with the number of samples routed to the leaf. This is the formulation we use in our JAX implementation for the numerical experiments.

Appendix B
Some Classical Optimization Results

We collect several standard smoothness and Lipschitz-Hessian definitions and results from optimization theory
(
26
;
27
)
, and present them in the Hilbert space setting.

Definition B.1

(
S
-smooth loss)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a real Hilbert space. A Fréchet differentiable function

L:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is called
S
-smooth
if its gradient is Lipschitz continuous
with constant
S
, i.e.,

\|\nabla L(f)-\nabla L(g)\|\leq S\|f-g\|\quad\forall f,g\in\mathcal{H}.

Equivalently, if
L
is twice Fréchet differentiable, then its Hessian satisfies

\|\nabla^{2}L(h)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq S\quad\forall h\in\mathcal{H},

or equivalently
\nabla^{2}L(h)\preceq SI
as an operator inequality.

Definition B.2

(
\mu
-strongly convex loss)
.

A function
L:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mu
-strongly convex
for some
\mu>0
if one
of the following equivalent conditions holds:

1.

(Quadratic lower bound):

The function
h\mapsto L(h)-\frac{\mu}{2}\|h\|^{2}
is convex.

2.

(First-order condition):

For all
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
,

L(f)\geq L(g)+\langle\nabla L(g),f-g\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|f-g\|^{2}.

3.

(Gradient monotonicity):

For all
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
,

\langle\nabla L(f)-\nabla L(g),f-g\rangle\geq\mu\|f-g\|^{2}.

4.

(Second-order condition):

If
L
is twice Fréchet differentiable, then

\nabla^{2}L(h)\succeq\mu I\quad\forall h\in\mathcal{H}.

Definition B.3
.

The Fréchet Hessian
\nabla^{2}L:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{L(H)}
is
2M
-Lipschitz continuous if there exists a constant
M>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}L(f)-\nabla^{2}L(g)\|\leq 2M\|f-g\|\qquad\forall f,g\in\mathcal{H}.

Lemma B.4
.

If the loss
L
is
S
-smooth, then

\displaystyle L(F_{k}+f)\leq L(F_{k})+\langle g_{k},f\rangle+\frac{S}{2}\|f\|^{2}.

(25)

Proof.

Using Taylor’s theorem with integral remainder, we express the expansion of the loss as

L(F_{k}+f)=L(F_{k})+\langle g_{k},f\rangle+\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\langle f,\nabla^{2}L(F_{k}+tf)f\rangle\,dt.

(26)

The definition of
S
-smoothness implies that the Hessian is bounded in operator norm by
S
, i.e.,
\nabla^{2}L(\cdot)\preceq SI
. Consequently, the quadratic form is bounded by
\langle f,\nabla^{2}L(\cdot)f\rangle\leq S\|f\|^{2}
. Substituting this into the integral yields
\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)S\|f\|^{2}\,dt=\frac{S}{2}\|f\|^{2}
, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma B.5
.

If
\nabla^{2}L
is
2M
-Lipschitz, then for all
f,g\in\mathcal{H}

1.

L(f)\leq L(g)+\langle\nabla L(g),f-g\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}L(g)(f-g),f-g\rangle+\frac{M}{3}\|f-g\|^{3}

2.

\|\nabla L(f)-\nabla L(g)-\nabla^{2}L(g)(f-g)\|\leq M\|g-f\|^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
h=f-g
. We begin with the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and expand the terms:

\displaystyle L(f)-L(g)-\langle\nabla L(g),h\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla L(g+th)-\nabla L(g),h\rangle\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\int_{0}^{t}\nabla^{2}L(g+sh)[h]\,ds,h\right\rangle\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}\langle h,\nabla^{2}L(g+sh)[h]\rangle\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}\langle h,\nabla^{2}L(g)[h]\rangle\,ds\,dt+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}\langle h,(\nabla^{2}L(g+sh)-\nabla^{2}L(g))[h]\rangle\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle h,\nabla^{2}L(g)[h]\rangle+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}\langle h,(\nabla^{2}L(g+sh)-\nabla^{2}L(g))[h]\rangle\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\langle h,\nabla^{2}L(g)[h]\rangle+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}\|h\|\cdot\|(\nabla^{2}L(g+sh)-\nabla^{2}L(g))[h]\|\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\langle h,\nabla^{2}L(g)[h]\rangle+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla^{2}L(g+sh)-\nabla^{2}L(g)\|_{op}\|h\|^{2}\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\langle h,\nabla^{2}L(g)[h]\rangle+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}(2Ms\|h\|)\|h\|^{2}\,ds\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle h,\nabla^{2}L(g)[h]\rangle+2M\|h\|^{3}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}}{2}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle h,\nabla^{2}L(g)[h]\rangle+\frac{M}{3}\|h\|^{3}.

Rearranging the terms yields the desired first inequality. For inequality 2, we express the gradient difference as an integral of the Hessian.

\displaystyle\|\nabla L(f)-\nabla L(g)-\nabla^{2}L(g)[h]\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}L(g+th)[h]\,dt-\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}L(g)[h]\,dt\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{0}^{1}(\nabla^{2}L(g+th)-\nabla^{2}L(g))[h]\,dt\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|(\nabla^{2}L(g+th)-\nabla^{2}L(g))[h]\|\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla^{2}L(g+th)-\nabla^{2}L(g)\|_{op}\|h\|\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}(2Mt\|h\|)\|h\|\,dt

\displaystyle=2M\|h\|^{2}\int_{0}^{1}t\,dt

\displaystyle=M\|h\|^{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix C
Proof of Regularity Conditions in the Boosting Space

Recall from
Section

2.3
that we identify the empirical boosting space
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{L}^{2}(\widehat{\nu}_{X,N})
with
\mathbb{R}^{NK}
, equipped with the inner product
\langle f,h\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle f(x_{i}),h(x_{i})\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}
. The induced norm is therefore
\|f\|=\big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|f(x_{i})\|^{2}_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}\big)^{1/2}
.

Proof of
Proposition

5.1
.

First, we prove the claim regarding the Lipschitz continuity of the Hessian. Suppose the base loss
l(u,y)
has a
M_{0}
-Lipschitz Hessian with respect to the prediction
u\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, meaning
\|\nabla_{11}^{2}l(u,y)-\nabla_{11}^{2}l(v,y)\|_{\text{op}}\leq M_{0}\|u-v\|_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}
. In the gradient boosting space, we minimize the empirical risk
L(F)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}l(F(x_{i}),y_{i})
. Because the Hessian operator
\nabla^{2}L(F)
acts pointwise (i.e., it is block-diagonal over the
N
samples), its operator norm on
\mathcal{H}
is exactly the maximum of the operator norms of the individual
K\times K
blocks. Thus, for any
F,G\in\mathcal{H}
, we can bound the difference in Hessians as follows:

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}L(F)-\nabla^{2}L(G)\|_{\text{op},\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\|\nabla_{11}^{2}l(F(x_{i}),y_{i})-\nabla_{11}^{2}l(G(x_{i}),y_{i})\|_{\text{op},\mathbb{R}^{K}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq N}M_{0}\|F(x_{i})-G(x_{i})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}

\displaystyle\leq M_{0}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|F(x_{i})-G(x_{i})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=M_{0}\sqrt{N}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|F(x_{i})-G(x_{i})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=M_{0}\sqrt{N}\|F-G\|.

This proves that
L
has an
M_{0}\sqrt{N}
-Lipschitz Hessian in the space
\mathcal{H}
.

Next, we prove that smoothness and strong convexity are preserved with the same constants. The Hessian quadratic form in the boosting space is given by
\langle f,\nabla^{2}L(F)[f]\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle f(x_{i}),\nabla_{11}^{2}l(F(x_{i}),y_{i})f(x_{i})\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}
. If the base loss
l
is
\mu
-strongly convex and
S
-smooth, its Euclidean quadratic form is bounded pointwise:

\mu\|f(x_{i})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}^{2}\leq\langle f(x_{i}),\nabla_{11}^{2}l(F(x_{i}),y_{i})f(x_{i})\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}\leq S\|f(x_{i})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}^{2}.

Summing these bounds over
i=1,\dots,N
and dividing by
N
directly yields
\mu\|f\|^{2}\leq\langle f,\nabla^{2}L(F)[f]\rangle\leq S\|f\|^{2}
.
∎

The following global convergence proof of the gradient regularized Newton scheme generalizes the proof of
24
to the setting of Restricted Newton Descent with a constant learning rate
\eta\in(0,1]
and weak learners. The primary challenge lies in handling the additional error terms introduced by the learning rate and the inexact weak iterates
f^{w}_{k+1}
, which we handled in our previous Lemmas in this paper. Additonally, the original proof which uses a fixed subsequence condition and fraction, we must make this dependent on both the learning rate
\eta
and weak gradient edge
\gamma
.

Theorem D.2
.

Assume
L
is convex with
2M
-Lipschitz Hessian, finite sublevel sets with diameter
\Lambda
, and weak gradient edge
\gamma
. Let the regularization be chosen as
\lambda_{k}=C\sqrt{M\|g_{k}\|}
for some
C\geq 1
. For any learning rate
\eta\in(0,1]
, the gradient regularized Newton scheme has the global convergence rate

\displaystyle L(F_{k})-L(F^{*})\leq\max\left(\underbrace{\Lambda\|g_{0}\|\exp\left(-\frac{k\eta}{8}(1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}})(3-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}-2\eta)\right)}_{\text{Linear Regime}},\quad\underbrace{\frac{1024C^{2}M\Lambda^{3}}{\eta^{2}(1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}})^{6}(2+k)^{2}}\frac{(1+\eta)^{4}}{(1-\frac{1}{3}\eta^{2})^{2}}.}_{\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}}\right)\text{ Regime}}\right).

(37)

Proof.

By
Lemma

4.5
we have that
L(F_{k})\leq L(F_{k-1})\leq...\leq L(F_{0})
, implying that the iterates
F_{k}
remain within the sublevel set defined by
F_{0}
. Consequently,
\|F_{k}-F^{*}\|\leq\Lambda
for all
T
. By convexity we have the bound

\displaystyle L(F_{k})-L(F^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\langle g_{k},F_{k}-F^{*}\rangle\leq\|g_{k}\|\Lambda.

(38)

Let
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}:=\{t\in\mathbb{N}:\|g_{t+1}\|\geq(1-\eta a)\|g_{t}\|\}
be the set of indices where the gradient does not contract sufficiently, for some
0<a<1
to be specified later. Consider first any
k\in\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
. It follows from
Lemma

D.1
that

\displaystyle(1-\eta a)\|g_{k}\|\leq\|g_{k+1}\|\leq\left(1-\eta(1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}})\right)\|g_{k}\|+(\eta+\eta^{2})\lambda_{k}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|

(39)

Rearranging the terms we obtain that

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|\geq\frac{1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}-a}{1+\eta}\|g_{k}\|

(40)

For the next argument we require the bound (
40
) to be positive, hence we choose

\displaystyle a:=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}{2}.

(41)

By (
40
) and
Lemma

4.5
we have

\displaystyle L(F_{k})-L(F_{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\eta^{2}\right)\eta\lambda_{k}\|f_{k+1}^{w}\|^{2}

(42)

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\eta^{2}\right)a^{2}\frac{\eta}{(1+\eta)^{2}}\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{\lambda_{k}}

(43)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\eta^{2}\right)a^{2}\frac{\eta}{(1+\eta)^{2}}\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{\frac{3}{2}}}{C\sqrt{M}}

(44)

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\eta^{2}\right)\frac{a^{2}}{C\sqrt{M\Lambda^{3}}}\frac{\eta}{(1+\eta)^{2}}\bigg(L(F_{k})-L(F^{*})\bigg)^{\frac{3}{2}}

(45)

\displaystyle=\tau\bigg(L(F_{k})-L(F^{*})\bigg)^{\frac{3}{2}}

(46)

where
\tau=\left(1-\frac{1}{3}\eta^{2}\right)\frac{a^{2}}{C\sqrt{M\Lambda^{3}}}\frac{\eta}{(1+\eta)^{2}}
. If this recursion held for all
k\geq 1
, the
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{k^{2}})
rate would follow by
Lemma

D.3
. Instead, we consider the subsequence technique as in
(
24
)
. We start by enumerating
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}=\{t_{0},t_{1},t_{2},...\}
. We have that

\displaystyle\alpha_{k+1}

\displaystyle:=L(F_{t_{k+1}})-L(F^{*})

(47)

\displaystyle=L(F_{t_{k}})-L(F^{*})+L(F_{t_{k}+1})-L(F_{t_{k}})

(48)

\displaystyle\leq L(F_{t_{k}})-L(F^{*})+\tau\bigg(L(F_{t_{k}})-L(F^{*})\bigg)^{\frac{3}{2}}

(49)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k}-\tau\alpha_{k}^{\frac{3}{2}}

(50)

Lemma

D.3
implies that
\alpha_{k}\leq\frac{4}{\tau^{2}(2+k)^{2}}
. Consider now any general
k\geq 1
. Define
\mathcal{I}_{k}=\{t\in\mathcal{I}_{\infty}:t\leq k\}
. We consider two cases based on the cardinality of the index set
\mathcal{I}_{k}
, that is,
|\mathcal{I}_{k}|\geq pk
and
|\mathcal{I}_{k}|<pk
, for some fraction
0<p<1
to be specified later in the proof.

Case

|\mathcal{I}_{k}|\geq pk
: Using the fact that both
L(F_{k})
and
\alpha_{k}
are decreasing sequences, we obtain that

\displaystyle L(F_{k})-L(F^{*})\leq L(F_{|\mathcal{I}_{k}|})-L(F^{*})=\alpha_{|\mathcal{I}_{k}|}\leq\alpha_{pk}\leq\frac{4}{\tau^{2}p^{2}(2+k)^{2}}=\frac{4C^{2}M\Lambda^{3}}{\eta^{2}a^{4}p^{2}(2+k)^{2}}\frac{(1+\eta)^{4}}{(1-\frac{1}{3}\eta^{2})^{2}}.

(51)

Consequently in this case we have a global
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{k^{2}})
rate.

Case

|\mathcal{I}_{k}|<pk
:
For indices
t\notin\mathcal{I}_{k}
, we have the contraction
\|g_{t+1}\|\leq(1-\eta a)\|g_{t}\|
. For
t\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
the gradient may grow, but it is bounded by
Lemma

D.1
via
\|g_{t+1}\|\leq\left(1+\eta^{2}+\eta\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}\right)\|g_{t}\|
. Combining both we obtain that

\displaystyle\|g_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\eta^{2}+\eta\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}\right)^{|\mathcal{I}_{k}|}(1-\eta a)^{k-|\mathcal{I}_{k}|}\|g_{0}\|

(52)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\eta^{2}+\eta\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}\right)^{pk}(1-\eta a)^{(1-p)k}\|g_{0}\|.

(53)

Using the bound
1+x\leq e^{x}
and taking logarithms, we see that this scheme produces a contraction if

\displaystyle p\left(\eta^{2}+\eta\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}\right)-(1-p)\eta a<0.

(54)

Rearranging the above, we find that we require

\displaystyle p<\frac{a}{\eta+a+\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}.

(55)

Substituting our choice
a=(1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}})/2
, the denominator simplifies to
\eta+(1+\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}})/2
. Since
\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}\leq 1
, this denominator is strictly less than
\eta+1
. Therefore, for any
\eta\in(0,1]
, the choice
p=\frac{a}{2}
is sufficient. In general, one can show that
a
and
p
need to satisfy the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{p\left(\eta+\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}\right)}{1-p}<a<1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}.

(56)

for the proof in both cases above to be valid. With the choice
4p=2a=1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}
, one finds through elementary algebra and the bound
1+x\leq e^{x}
that the gradient growth can be bounded by

\displaystyle\|g_{k}\|\leq\exp\left(-\frac{k\eta}{8}(1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}})(3-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}-2\eta)\right)\|g_{0}\|,

(57)

yielding a linear rate of convergence.

Final bound:
The final convergence rate is obtained by taking the maximum bound of the two cases above. Note that
1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}+o(\gamma^{2})
. Hence the global convergence rate in the first case is

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{C^{2}M\Lambda^{3}}{\eta^{2}k^{2}\gamma^{12}}\right),

(58)

while for the second case this is

\mathcal{O}\left(\exp\left(-k\eta\gamma^{2}\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}+2-2\eta\right)/8\right)\right).

(59)

This concludes the proof.
∎

The following lemma is a standard result in the convex optimization literature, see e.g.
(
26
)
. We include the general proof here for completeness

Lemma D.3
.

If
\alpha_{k}
is a decreasing sequence satisfying
0\leq\alpha_{k+1}\leq\alpha_{k}-c\alpha_{k}^{\frac{3}{2}}
for some
c>0
, then for all
k\geq 0
:

\alpha_{k}\leq\frac{1}{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{0}}}+\frac{c}{2}k\right)^{2}}\leq\frac{4}{c^{2}(k+2)^{2}}.

(60)

Proof.

Using the hypothesis
\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{k+1}\geq c\alpha_{k}^{3/2}
, consider the increment

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{k+1}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}-\sqrt{\alpha_{k+1}}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{k}\alpha_{k+1}}}

(61)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{k+1}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{k}\alpha_{k+1}}(\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}+\sqrt{\alpha_{k+1}})}

(62)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{c\alpha_{k}^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{k}\alpha_{k+1}}(\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}+\sqrt{\alpha_{k+1}})}.

(63)

Since the sequence is decreasing (
\alpha_{k+1}\leq\alpha_{k}
), we have
\sqrt{\alpha_{k+1}}\leq\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}
, hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{k+1}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}}\geq\frac{c\alpha_{k}^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{k}^{2}}(\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}+\sqrt{\alpha_{k}})}=\frac{c}{2}.

(64)

Summing from
i=0
to
k-1
yields a telescoping sum, from which we obtain that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{0}}}+\frac{ck}{2}.

(65)

Inverting and squaring both sides yields the inequality

\alpha_{k}\leq\frac{1}{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{0}}}+\frac{c}{2}k\right)^{2}}.

(66)

The final bound follows from the fact that
c\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}\leq 1
, which follows directly from the hypothesis
0\leq\alpha_{k+1}\leq\alpha_{k}-c\alpha_{k}^{\frac{3}{2}}
.
∎

Lemma D.4
.

Let the regularization be
\lambda_{k}=C\sqrt{M\|g_{k}\|}
for some
C\geq 1
. If
L
is
\mu
-strongly convex with
2M
-Lipschitz Hessian and weak gradient edge
\gamma
, and if there exists a
k_{0}\geq 0
such that
\|g_{k_{0}}\|\leq\frac{\mu^{2}}{4C^{2}M}\left(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}{1+\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}\right)^{2}
, then for all
k\geq k_{0}
we have that

\displaystyle\|g_{k+1}\|\leq\frac{3+\rho}{4}\|g_{k}\|

(67)

where
\rho=1-\eta(1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}})
. Hence the iterates of
Algorithm

2
converge linearly locally.

Proof.

We have the standard bound

\displaystyle\|g^{w}_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|g_{k}\|+\|g_{k}^{w}-g_{k}\|

(68)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}\right)\|g_{k}\|

(69)

\displaystyle:=C_{\gamma}\|g_{k}\|,

(70)

and since we assumed
\mu
-strong convexity, we have that

\displaystyle\|f^{w}_{k+1}\|

\displaystyle=\|(H_{k}+\lambda I)^{-1}g^{w}\|

(71)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mu}\|g^{w}\|

(72)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\gamma}}{\mu}\|g^{w}\|.

(73)

We combine the above with
Lemmas

4.2
and
D.1
, specifically
Equation

33
, to obtain that

\displaystyle\|g_{k+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq(1-\eta)\|g_{k}\|+\eta\|g_{k}-g_{k}^{w}\|+\eta\lambda_{k}\|f^{w}_{k+1}\|+M\eta^{2}\|f^{w}_{k+1}\|^{2}

(74)

\displaystyle\leq(1-2\eta+\eta C_{\gamma})\|g_{k}\|+\eta\lambda_{k}\|f^{w}_{k+1}\|+M\eta^{2}\|f^{w}_{k+1}\|^{2}

(75)

\displaystyle\leq(1-2\eta+\eta C_{\gamma})\|g_{k}\|+\sqrt{C^{2}M\eta^{2}\frac{C_{\gamma}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}}\|g_{k}\|^{\frac{3}{2}}+C^{2}M\eta^{2}\frac{C_{\gamma}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\|g_{k}\|^{2}.

(76)

Write
\rho=(1-2\eta+\eta C_{\gamma})
and
K=C^{2}M\eta^{2}\frac{C_{\gamma}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}
. We seek the condition for a strict decrease
\|g_{k+1}\|<\|g_{k}\|
. Substituting the above and dividing by
\|g_{k}\|
we obtain that

\displaystyle\sqrt{K\|g_{k}\|}+K\|g_{k}\|<1-\rho.

(77)

Let
\Delta=1-\rho=\eta(1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}})
. Since
\eta\leq 1
, we observe that
\Delta\leq 1
. Let
y=\sqrt{K\|g_{k}\|}
. The condition becomes
y+y^{2}<\Delta
. A sufficient condition for this to hold is
y\leq\frac{\Delta}{2}
, because this implies
y+y^{2}\leq\frac{\Delta}{2}+\frac{\Delta^{2}}{4}=\frac{\Delta(2+\Delta)}{4}
. Since
\Delta\leq 1
, we have
(2+\Delta)\leq 3
, and thus
y+y^{2}\leq\frac{3}{4}\Delta<\Delta
. Expanding the condition
y\leq\frac{\Delta}{2}
yields

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{M\eta^{2}C_{\gamma}^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\|g_{k}\|}\leq\frac{\eta(1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}})}{2}.

(78)

Canceling
\eta
and rearranging for
\sqrt{\|g_{k}\|}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sqrt{\|g_{k}\|}\leq\frac{\mu}{2\sqrt{M}}\frac{1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}{C_{\gamma}}=\frac{\mu}{2\sqrt{M}}\frac{1-\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}{1+\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}.

(79)

Squaring both sides gives the bound in the hypothesis. Under this condition, the sum of the higher order terms is bounded by
\frac{3}{4}\Delta\|g_{k}\|=\frac{3}{4}(1-\rho)\|g_{k}\|
. Plugging this back into the gradient bound we obtain that

\displaystyle\|g_{k+1}\|\leq\rho\|g_{k}\|+\frac{3}{4}(1-\rho)\|g_{k}\|=\frac{3+\rho}{4}\|g_{k}\|.

(80)

Since
\rho<1

∎

Appendix E
Divergence of Vanilla Newton on Smooth Convex Losses

This appendix records explicit smooth convex objectives for which the classical
(unregularized) Newton step may fail to converge globally, even though the loss
satisfies the Lipschitz-Hessian assumption used throughout
Section

4
and

Appendix

D
. These examples show that a fixed damping (constant learning rate)
does not, by itself, globalize Newton’s method, and motivate the adaptive
gradient regularization used in
Algorithm

2
.

E.1
Newton, damped Newton, and gradient regularized Newton

Definition E.1

(Newton-type updates in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)
.

Let
L:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be twice continuously differentiable and assume

\nabla^{2}L(x)\succ 0
at the current iterate.

•

The (classical) Newton update is

N(x):=x-(\nabla^{2}L(x))^{-1}\nabla L(x).

•

Given a fixed learning rate
\eta>0
, the damped Newton update is

N_{\eta}(x):=x-\eta(\nabla^{2}L(x))^{-1}\nabla L(x).

Assume moreover that
\nabla^{2}L
is
2M
-Lipschitz in operator norm:

\|\nabla^{2}L(u)-\nabla^{2}L(v)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 2M\|u-v\|\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then the (exact) gradient regularized Newton (GRN) update is

\mathrm{GRN}_{M,\eta}(x):=x-\eta\big(\nabla^{2}L(x)+\lambda(x)I\big)^{-1}\nabla L(x),\qquad\lambda(x):=\sqrt{M\|\nabla L(x)\|}.

E.2
A canonical 1D counterexample:
L(x)=\sqrt{1+x^{2}}

Proposition E.2

(A smooth convex loss with divergent Newton (1D))
.

Let
L:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by
L(x):=\sqrt{1+x^{2}}
.
Then
L
is convex and smooth, and
L^{\prime\prime}
is globally Lipschitz.
The classical Newton iteration
x_{k+1}=N(x_{k})
satisfies the exact recursion

x_{k+1}=-x_{k}^{3}.

In particular,
|x_{k+1}|=|x_{k}|^{3}
, yielding cubic local convergence for
|x_{0}|<1

and divergence for
|x_{0}|>1
.

Proof.

We compute

L^{\prime}(x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}},\qquad L^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{(1+x^{2})^{3/2}},\qquad L^{(3)}(x)=-\frac{3x}{(1+x^{2})^{5/2}}.

In particular
L^{\prime\prime}(x)>0
for all
x
, hence
L
is convex. Since
L^{(3)}
is bounded,

L^{\prime\prime}
is globally Lipschitz. Moreover,

\frac{L^{\prime}(x)}{L^{\prime\prime}(x)}=\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}\,(1+x^{2})^{3/2}=x(1+x^{2}).

Thus the Newton update is

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\frac{L^{\prime}(x_{k})}{L^{\prime\prime}(x_{k})}=x_{k}-x_{k}(1+x_{k}^{2})=-x_{k}^{3}.

Finally,
|x_{k+1}|=|x_{k}|^{3}
implies
x_{k}\to 0
if
|x_{0}|<1
and
|x_{k}|\to\infty

if
|x_{0}|>1
.
∎

Proposition E.3

(Fixed damping does not globalize Newton)
.

Fix any learning rate
\eta>0
and consider
x_{k+1}=N_{\eta}(x_{k})
for

L(x)=\sqrt{1+x^{2}}
. Define

X(\eta):=\sqrt{\max\Big\{0,\frac{3}{\eta}-1\Big\}}.

Then every initialization with
|x_{0}|\geq X(\eta)
diverges and satisfies

|x_{k+1}|\geq 2|x_{k}|\qquad\forall k\geq 0.

Proof.

From the computations above,
L^{\prime}(x)/L^{\prime\prime}(x)=x(1+x^{2})
, hence

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\eta x_{k}(1+x_{k}^{2})=x_{k}\big(1-\eta(1+x_{k}^{2})\big).

If
|x_{k}|\geq X(\eta)
, then by definition
\eta(1+x_{k}^{2})\geq 3
, so

|1-\eta(1+x_{k}^{2})|\geq 2
and therefore
|x_{k+1}|\geq 2|x_{k}|
.
Induction yields divergence.
∎

E.3
A drift-based generator for smooth convex Newton counterexamples

The previous example is not accidental. We now record a general construction
principle: prescribing a one-dimensional
Newton drift

d(x)
determines a
convex objective
L
whose Newton iteration is exactly
x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\tilde{d}(x_{k})
,
where
\tilde{d}
is the odd extension of
d
. This yields an infinite family of
smooth convex losses on which classical Newton diverges.

Theorem E.4

(Lipschitz-Hessian drift-to-loss generator for 1D Newton dynamics)
.

Let
d:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be continuous and satisfy:

(D0)

d(0)=0
and
d(x)>0
for all
x>0
;

(D1)

there exist
\delta>0
and
C_{0}<\infty
such that

|d(x)-x|\leq C_{0}x^{2}\qquad\forall x\in[0,\delta].

Define, for
x>0
,

\Phi(x):=\int_{1}^{x}\frac{ds}{d(s)},\qquad L_{+}^{\prime}(x):=a\,e^{\Phi(x)}\ (a>0),\qquad L_{+}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}L_{+}^{\prime}(t)\,dt,

and extend evenly by
L(x):=L_{+}(|x|)
. Let
\tilde{d}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the odd extension

\tilde{d}(x):=\mathrm{sign}(x)\,d(|x|)
with
\tilde{d}(0)=0
. Then:

(i)

L
is even, convex, and
C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, with
L^{\prime\prime}(x)>0
for
x\neq 0
.
Moreover, for
x>0
,

L^{\prime}(x)=L_{+}^{\prime}(x),\qquad L^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{L_{+}^{\prime}(x)}{d(x)}.

In particular,
L^{\prime}
is odd and
L^{\prime\prime}
is even.

(ii)

For every
x\neq 0
,

\frac{L^{\prime}(x)}{L^{\prime\prime}(x)}=\tilde{d}(x),

and therefore the classical Newton update for
L
is exactly

N(x)=x-\frac{L^{\prime}(x)}{L^{\prime\prime}(x)}=x-\tilde{d}(x).

(iii)

If there exist
X_{0}>0
and
\beta>1
such that

d(x)\geq(1+\beta)x\qquad\forall x\geq X_{0},

then every Newton iterate initialized with
|x_{0}|\geq X_{0}
diverges and satisfies

|x_{k+1}|\geq\beta|x_{k}|\qquad\forall k\geq 0.

(iv)

(Globally Lipschitz Hessian.)

Assume in addition that
d\in C^{1}((0,\infty))
and that

C_{1}\;:=\;\sup_{x>0}\frac{|d^{\prime}(x)-1|}{d(x)}\;<\;\infty.

(81)

Assume moreover that there exist
\kappa>1
and
X_{1}>0
such that

d(x)\geq\kappa x\qquad\forall x\geq X_{1}.

(82)

(For instance,
Equation

82
holds whenever the hypothesis in (iii) holds.)
Then
L^{\prime\prime}
is globally Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}
, i.e. there exists
M<\infty
such that

|L^{\prime\prime}(u)-L^{\prime\prime}(v)|\leq 2M|u-v|\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathbb{R}.

More precisely, for
x>0
we have

L^{(3)}(x)=\frac{L_{+}^{\prime}(x)}{d(x)^{2}}\,\bigl(1-d^{\prime}(x)\bigr)=L^{\prime\prime}(x)\,\frac{1-d^{\prime}(x)}{d(x)},

(83)

and hence
\sup_{x>0}|L^{(3)}(x)|<\infty
, so one may take

2M=\sup_{x\neq 0}|L^{(3)}(x)|
.

Proof.

We split the argument into four parts.

Part A: construction and smoothness on
(0,\infty)
.

By (D0), the map
s\mapsto 1/d(s)
is continuous on compact subsets of
(0,\infty)
.
Hence
\Phi\in C^{1}((0,\infty))
with
\Phi^{\prime}(x)=1/d(x)
.
Therefore
L_{+}^{\prime}(x)=ae^{\Phi(x)}
is
C^{1}((0,\infty))
and

(L_{+}^{\prime})^{\prime}(x)=ae^{\Phi(x)}\Phi^{\prime}(x)=\frac{L_{+}^{\prime}(x)}{d(x)}.

Thus
L_{+}\in C^{2}((0,\infty))
, with

L_{+}^{\prime\prime}(x)=(L_{+}^{\prime})^{\prime}(x)=\frac{L_{+}^{\prime}(x)}{d(x)}>0\qquad(x>0),

so
L_{+}
is strictly convex on
(0,\infty)
.

Part B: behavior at
0
and
C^{2}
even extension.

Write
d(x)=x(1+\varepsilon(x))
on
(0,\delta]
, where by (D1) we have

|\varepsilon(x)|\leq C_{0}x
, hence
\varepsilon(x)\to 0
as
x\downarrow 0
.
Then on
(0,\delta]
,

\frac{1}{d(x)}=\frac{1}{x}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon(x)}=\frac{1}{x}+g(x),

where
g
is bounded on
(0,\delta]
(since
(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}=1+O(\varepsilon)
).
Integrating gives

\Phi(x)=\int_{1}^{x}\frac{ds}{d(s)}=\int_{1}^{x}\frac{ds}{s}+\int_{1}^{x}g(s)\,ds=\log x+C_{\ast}+o(1)\qquad(x\downarrow 0)

for some finite constant
C_{\ast}
.
Exponentiating yields

L_{+}^{\prime}(x)=ae^{\Phi(x)}=ae^{C_{\ast}}\,x(1+o(1))\qquad(x\downarrow 0),

so
L_{+}^{\prime}(x)\to 0
and
L_{+}^{\prime}(x)/x\to ae^{C_{\ast}}\in(0,\infty)
.
Since
d(x)=x(1+o(1))
, we obtain

L_{+}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{L_{+}^{\prime}(x)}{d(x)}\to ae^{C_{\ast}}\in(0,\infty)\qquad(x\downarrow 0).

Define
L_{+}(0):=0
,
L_{+}^{\prime}(0):=0
, and
L_{+}^{\prime\prime}(0):=\lim_{x\downarrow 0}L_{+}^{\prime\prime}(x)
; then

L_{+}\in C^{2}([0,\infty))
.

Now define
L(x)=L_{+}(|x|)
. This
L
is even and
C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with

L^{\prime}(x)
odd and
L^{\prime\prime}(x)
even, and for
x>0
we have

L^{\prime}(x)=L_{+}^{\prime}(x)
and
L^{\prime\prime}(x)=L_{+}^{\prime\prime}(x)=L_{+}^{\prime}(x)/d(x)
.
Since
L^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq 0
everywhere and
L^{\prime\prime}(x)>0
for
x\neq 0
,
L
is convex.
This proves (i).

Part C: drift identity and divergence.

For
x>0
, by (i) we have
L^{\prime}(x)=L_{+}^{\prime}(x)
and
L^{\prime\prime}(x)=L_{+}^{\prime}(x)/d(x)
, hence

\frac{L^{\prime}(x)}{L^{\prime\prime}(x)}=d(x).

By evenness of
L^{\prime\prime}
and oddness of
L^{\prime}
, the ratio
L^{\prime}/L^{\prime\prime}
is odd, so for
x<0
,

\frac{L^{\prime}(x)}{L^{\prime\prime}(x)}=-\frac{L^{\prime}(|x|)}{L^{\prime\prime}(|x|)}=-d(|x|)=\tilde{d}(x),

proving (ii). The divergence statement (iii) follows exactly as in
Theorem

E.4
:
if
x\geq X_{0}
, then
N(x)=x-d(x)\leq-\beta x
, hence
|N(x)|\geq\beta|x|
, and similarly for
x\leq-X_{0}
.

Part D: globally Lipschitz Hessian.

Assume
Equations

81
and
82
.
For
x>0
, differentiating
L^{\prime\prime}(x)=L_{+}^{\prime}(x)/d(x)
and using
(L_{+}^{\prime})^{\prime}(x)=L_{+}^{\prime}(x)/d(x)

from Part A gives
Equation

83
. Hence, for
x>0
,

|L^{(3)}(x)|\leq\frac{L_{+}^{\prime}(x)}{d(x)^{2}}\,|1-d^{\prime}(x)|\leq C_{1}\frac{L_{+}^{\prime}(x)}{d(x)}=C_{1}L^{\prime\prime}(x).

It remains to show
\sup_{x>0}L^{\prime\prime}(x)<\infty
.
By Part B,
L^{\prime\prime}
extends continuously to
x=0
with
0<L^{\prime\prime}(0)<\infty
.
On
[0,X_{1}]
, continuity gives
\sup_{[0,X_{1}]}L^{\prime\prime}<\infty
.
For
x\geq X_{1}
, using
Equation

82
,

\Phi(x)=\Phi(X_{1})+\int_{X_{1}}^{x}\frac{ds}{d(s)}\leq\Phi(X_{1})+\int_{X_{1}}^{x}\frac{ds}{\kappa s}=\Phi(X_{1})+\frac{1}{\kappa}\log\frac{x}{X_{1}},

so
L_{+}^{\prime}(x)=ae^{\Phi(x)}\leq C\,x^{1/\kappa}
for some
C
.
Therefore,

L^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{L_{+}^{\prime}(x)}{d(x)}\leq\frac{Cx^{1/\kappa}}{\kappa x}=\frac{C}{\kappa}x^{\frac{1}{\kappa}-1},

which is bounded for
x\geq X_{1}
since
\kappa>1
.
Thus
\sup_{x>0}|L^{(3)}(x)|<\infty
, and since
L^{\prime\prime}
is even,
L^{\prime\prime}
is globally Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Appendix F
Newton Boosting for Hessian-Dominated Losses

This appendix proves the claims stated around
Definition

3.7
: binary and
categorical cross entropy are Hessian-dominated with constant
c=1
, and
pointwise Hessian-dominance implies Hessian-dominance of the empirical risk.

F.1
A useful log inequality

Lemma F.1

(A log inequality)
.

For all
p\in(0,1]
,

\frac{1-p}{p}\;\geq\;-\log p.

(84)

Equivalently, for all
p\in[0,1)
,

\frac{p}{1-p}\;\geq\;-\log(1-p).

(85)

Proof.

Define
\phi(p)=\frac{1}{p}-1+\log p
on
(0,1]
. Then

\phi^{\prime}(p)=-\frac{1}{p^{2}}+\frac{1}{p}=\frac{p-1}{p^{2}}\leq 0,

so
\phi
is non-increasing. Since
\phi(1)=0
, we have
\phi(p)\geq 0
for all

p\in(0,1]
, which is (
84
). Substituting
q=1-p
gives
(
85
).
∎

F.2
Binary cross entropy is Hessian-dominated

Proposition F.2

(Binary cross entropy is Hessian-dominated with
c=1
)
.

Let
y\in\{0,1\}
and
u\in\mathbb{R}
, and set
p=\sigma(u)=(1+e^{-u})^{-1}
.
Consider the binary cross entropy

l(u,y)\;=\;-\Big(y\log p+(1-y)\log(1-p)\Big).

Let
g(u,y)=\partial_{u}l(u,y)
and
h(u)=\partial_{uu}l(u,y)
.
Then the (scalar) Newton step
f(u,y)=-h(u)^{-1}g(u,y)
satisfies

\|f(u,y)\|_{h(u)}^{2}:=f(u,y)^{2}\,h(u)=\frac{g(u,y)^{2}}{h(u)}\;\geq\;l(u,y).

(86)

Hence binary cross entropy is pointwise Hessian-dominated with constant
c=1
.

Proof.

A direct computation gives

g(u,y)=p-y,\qquad h(u)=p(1-p)>0.

Thus
\|f\|_{h}^{2}=g^{2}/h
. If
y=1
, then
g=p-1=-(1-p)
, so

\frac{g^{2}}{h}=\frac{(1-p)^{2}}{p(1-p)}=\frac{1-p}{p}.

Also
l(u,1)=-\log p
, and
Lemma

F.1
gives

\frac{1-p}{p}\geq-\log p=l(u,1)
. If
y=0
, then
g=p
, so

\frac{g^{2}}{h}=\frac{p^{2}}{p(1-p)}=\frac{p}{1-p}.

Also
l(u,0)=-\log(1-p)
, and
Lemma

F.1
gives

\frac{p}{1-p}\geq-\log(1-p)=l(u,0)
. This proves (
86
).
∎

Lemma F.3

(Softmax Hessian is positive definite on the zero-sum subspace)
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
satisfy
p_{k}>0
and
\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}=1
, and define

H\;:=\;\mathrm{diag}(p)-pp^{\top}.

Let
V:=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{K}:\mathbf{1}^{\top}v=0\}
. Then
H
maps
V
to
V
and is
positive definite on
V
. In particular, the restriction
H:V\to V
is invertible.

Proof.

First, for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
,

\mathbf{1}^{\top}Hv=\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathrm{diag}(p)v-\mathbf{1}^{\top}p(p^{\top}v)=p^{\top}v-(p^{\top}v)=0,

so
Hv\in V
whenever
v\in V
. Next, for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
,

v^{\top}Hv=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}v_{k}^{2}-\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}v_{k}\Big)^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\big(v_{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{K}p_{j}v_{j}\big)^{2}\;\geq\;0.

Equality holds iff
v_{k}
is constant in
k
, i.e.
v\in\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}
.
Intersecting with
V
leaves only
v=0
, hence
v^{\top}Hv>0
for all nonzero

v\in V
. Therefore
H
is positive definite on
V
and invertible as
H:V\to V
.
∎

Proposition F.4

(Categorical cross entropy is Hessian-dominated with
c=1
)
.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
,
p=\mathrm{softmax}(u)
, and let
y=e_{t}
be a one-hot label.
Consider categorical cross entropy

l(u,y)\;=\;-\sum_{k=1}^{K}y_{k}\log p_{k}\;=\;-\log p_{t}.

Let
g(u,y)=\nabla_{u}l(u,y)=p-y
and
H(u)=\nabla^{2}_{uu}l(u,y)=\mathrm{diag}(p)-pp^{\top}
.
Work on the subspace
V=\{v:\mathbf{1}^{\top}v=0\}
; then
g(u,y)\in V
and by

Lemma

F.3
the Newton direction
f\in V
solving

H(u)f\;=\;y-p

(87)

is unique. Moreover,

\|f\|_{H(u)}^{2}:=f^{\top}H(u)f\;=\;\frac{1-p_{t}}{p_{t}}\;\geq\;-\log p_{t}\;=\;l(u,y).

(88)

Hence categorical cross entropy is pointwise Hessian-dominated with constant
c=1
.

Proof.

Let
H=\mathrm{diag}(p)-pp^{\top}
. Define
s:=p^{\top}f
. Expanding (
87
) yields

\mathrm{diag}(p)f-p(p^{\top}f)=y-p\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p_{k}(f_{k}-s)=y_{k}-p_{k}\qquad\forall k,

so (since
p_{k}>0
) we have

f_{k}=s+\frac{y_{k}}{p_{k}}-1.

With
y=e_{t}
, this becomes

f_{t}=s+\frac{1}{p_{t}}-1,\qquad f_{k}=s-1\quad(k\neq t).

Using symmetry of
H
and (
87
),

\|f\|_{H}^{2}=f^{\top}Hf=f^{\top}(y-p).

Compute

\displaystyle f^{\top}(y-p)

\displaystyle=f_{t}(1-p_{t})+\sum_{k\neq t}f_{k}(0-p_{k})

\displaystyle=f_{t}(1-p_{t})-(s-1)\sum_{k\neq t}p_{k}

\displaystyle=\big(f_{t}-(s-1)\big)(1-p_{t})=\left(\left(s+\frac{1}{p_{t}}-1\right)-(s-1)\right)(1-p_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1-p_{t}}{p_{t}}.

Finally,
Lemma

F.1
gives
\frac{1-p_{t}}{p_{t}}\geq-\log p_{t}=l(u,y)
,
proving (
88
).
∎

Remark F.5

(Relation to Assumption 3.1 (uniqueness of Newton step))
.

On
\mathbb{R}^{K}
one has
H(u)\mathbf{1}=0
(softmax is shift-invariant), so the
Newton equation is not uniquely solvable without fixing a gauge. Restricting to the
zero-sum subspace
V=\{v:\mathbf{1}^{\top}v=0\}
(or equivalently fixing one logit
coordinate) yields uniqueness and matches Assumption 3.1.
The decrement
\|f\|_{H(u)}^{2}
is invariant to the choice of representative.

Remark F.6
.

Binary cross entropy corresponds to the special case
K=2
of categorical
cross entropy (after identifying the scalar logit with the difference of the
two softmax logits). Thus
Proposition

F.4

strictly generalizes
Proposition

F.2
; the latter is
included separately for clarity and because it avoids the gauge-fixing
required in the multiclass setting.

F.3
From pointwise to empirical Hessian-dominance

Lemma F.7

(Pointwise
\Rightarrow
empirical Hessian-dominance)
.

Let
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
be a dataset and define the empirical risk

L(F)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}l(F(x_{i}),y_{i}),

with
F(x_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
(take
K=1
for binary). Work in the empirical Hilbert
space
H=L^{2}(\hat{\nu}_{N})
with inner product

\langle A,B\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle A(x_{i}),B(x_{i})\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}.

Fix
F\in H
and write
u_{i}:=F(x_{i})
. Let

g_{(i)}:=\partial_{1}l(u_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{K},\qquad h_{(i)}:=\partial_{11}l(u_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}.

Assume there exists
c>0
such that for all
i
,

g_{(i)}^{\top}h_{(i)}^{-1}g_{(i)}\;\geq\;c\,l(u_{i},y_{i}),

(89)

where
h_{(i)}^{-1}
is taken on the relevant space (for categorical CE one may restrict
to
V
pointwise as in
Proposition

F.4
).
Let
g=\nabla L(F)
and
H=\nabla^{2}L(F)
, and let
f=-H^{-1}g
be the exact Newton
direction (Assumption 3.1). Then

\|f\|_{H}^{2}\;\geq\;c\,L(F).

In particular,
L
is Hessian-dominated in the sense of
Definition

3.7
with constant
c
.

Proof.

In the empirical setting, the gradient and Hessian act pointwise:

g(x_{i})=g_{(i)},\qquad(H[f])(x_{i})=h_{(i)}f(x_{i}).

Thus the Newton equation
H[f]=-g
decouples over samples:

h_{(i)}f(x_{i})=-g_{(i)}\quad\Rightarrow\quad f(x_{i})=-h_{(i)}^{-1}g_{(i)}.

Therefore, using
Definition

3.2
,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle f,H[f]\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(x_{i})^{\top}h_{(i)}f(x_{i})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g_{(i)}^{\top}h_{(i)}^{-1}g_{(i)}.

Applying (
89
) term-by-term yields

\|f\|_{H}^{2}\geq\frac{c}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}l(u_{i},y_{i})=c\,L(F),

which is exactly
Definition

3.7
.
∎

Corollary F.8

(Empirical cross entropy risks are Hessian-dominated with
c=1
)
.

Let
L(F)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}l(F(x_{i}),y_{i})
where
l
is either binary cross entropy
(
Proposition

F.2
) or categorical cross entropy
(
Proposition

F.4
). Then
L
is Hessian-dominated with
c=1

(in the sense of
Definition

3.7
, with the gauge-fix of
Remark

F.5
