ABSTRACT
New reactor designs, such as pebble-bed reactors, present challenges for the safeguarding of fissile
material. Due to the multi-pass fuel circulation design and the non-uniform path a pebble may traverse
through a pebble-bed reactor, there will be variations in the irradiation history of spent fuel pebbles.
The standard approach of estimating actinide quantities based on fission gamma spectra or neutron
emission paired with depletion modeling may yield uncertainties too large for safeguards and material
accountancy. This project investigated, through modeling and experimentation, the potential of
neutron self-interrogation of spent fuel pebbles as an innovative method to implement materials
accountability. As an example, our feasibility studies indicate that the mass of U-235, U-238, Pu-239
and Pu-241 can be predicted to 4.1, 0.86, 13 and 13 % accuracy, respectively, when measuring 100
closely packed end-of-life spent fuel pebbles over approximately 12 days using a 4π counting geometry.

1. INTRODUCTION
There is increasing interest in small modular reactors (SMRs) and their applications. SMRs are newer
generation reactors designed to generate electric power typically up to 300 MW, whose components
and systems can be shop fabricated and then transported as modules to the sites for installation as
demand arises. SMRs are under development for all principal reactor lines: water cooled reactors, high
temperature gas cooled reactors, liquid metal, sodium and gas-cooled reactors with fast neutron
spectrum, molten salt reactors, and most recently microreactors. The key driving forces of SMR
development are fulfilling the need for flexible power generation for a wider range of users and
applications, replacing ageing fossil-fired units, enhancing safety performance, and offering better
economic affordability [1].
In support of SMR development are emerging pebble-bed reactors (PBR). Advanced Generation IV
reactors promise improvements in reactor safety and efficiency. Worldwide, there are more than a
dozen reactor designs of high temperature gas cooled reactors or molten salt reactors utilizing TRi-
structural ISOtropic (TRISO) fuel pebbles [1]. A unique feature of this reactor is its multi-pass fuel
cycle in which fuel pebbles are randomly loaded and continuously circulated through the core until
they reach their prescribed end-of-life burn-up limit.
The fuel handling system of a PBR includes an on-line burnup measurement system that typically
measures certain fission product gammas and assesses whether a given pebble has reached its end-of-
life burnup. From there, the pebble is either circulated back into the reactor or diverted to spent fuel
storage. Due to the multi-pass fuel circulation design and the non-uniform path a pebble may traverse
through the reactor, variations in the final burnup levels will exist due to the irradiation history
differences of spent fuel pebbles. This will yield variations in the isotopic and actinide content of the
spent fuel pebbles. The burnup level and plutonium isotopic vector may not be directly correlated in
a pebble-bed reactor [2]. As a result, the standard approach of estimating actinide quantities based on
burn-up measurements paired with neutronics/depletion modeling may yield uncertainties too large
for burn-up measurement as well as safeguards and material accountancy of a pebble-bed reactor.
There are additional factors why nuclear material accountancy and control for a PBR will be
significantly different than a standard light water reactor (LWR). The major differences stem from the
fact that LWR fuel assemblies are uniquely identifiable items whereas the thousands of fuel pebbles
are not [2]. As a result, the safeguards approach to a PBR will need to be based on bulk accounting.
Additionally, the threat of theft is a more important consideration for spent fuel pebbles attributed to
the smaller size, weight, and radioactivity of pebbles compared to that of a self-protecting LWR spent
fuel assembly [2].
We hypothesize that the process of neutron self-interrogation of the spent fuel may enable direct
measurement of fissile content through fission-fragment gamma spectroscopy. This will serve as an
improvement to burnup and plutonium content measurements. Additionally, an improvement in the
direct measurement of the plutonium and actinide isotopes will provide utility to reactor safeguards.
This report summarizes our investigation through modeling and benchmarking experiments, if
neutron self-interrogation by actinides in a discharged pebble can induce measurable fission in the key
accountable fissile nuclides of uranium, neptunium and plutonium contained therein.
The study was performed with a multi-pronged approach and included:
 (1) Model generation of simulated induced fission product spectra using MCNP6 with a
 fuel depletion module like CINDER or ORIGEN.
 (2) Neutron interrogation benchmarking measurements.
 (3) Safeguards assessment.

1.1. Neutron Self-Interrogation
The total of spontaneous and alpha-induced neutron emission rates within the fuel will increase with
burnup. It is recognized that the major fraction of the neutron energy spectrum in an irradiated pebble
would be provided by Cm-242 and Cm-244. These isotopes are produced through a series of neutron
captures and beta decays during the multi-pass circulation of the fuel pebble through the reactor core.
In the present fuel loading of a pebble, greater than 90% is the fissionable isotope, U-238. Figure 1,
illustrates the Cm-242 and Cm-244 productions from U-238. An isotope transforms from left to right
by neutron capture and moves to a left-up position by a β- decay.

 Figure 1. Cm-242 and Cm-244 formations from U-238 neutron induced transformations in the
 reactor.

Exploiting the fission-fragment spectroscopy concept, the induced fissions may have a unique
distribution of fission products and hence also characteristic fission gamma rays whose detection can
enable quantifying the fissile content[3]. The principle is illustrated in Figure 2 showing the fission
product distribution for different neutron energies. Signatures in the region above 3 MeV are explored
where interferences from most of the other fission products are expected to be the lowest. The
primary source of background for the energy range being considered is from spontaneous fission
prompt gamma rays, which produces a smoothly varying background continuum. In principle, these
fission fragments of interest are produced in a pebble while circulating through the core but have
short half-lives of the order of a few tens of seconds and will decay by the time a pebble arrives at the
burn-up measurement station, thus making the self-interrogation an attractive approach.

2. MODELING APPROACH

2.1. Pebble-bed Reactor Models
The Pebble-bed Reactor (PBR) modeled in this study follows the design parameters for the modular
PBR designed by Eskom et al. [5],[6]. The modular PBR has a thermal power rating of 265 MW. It
uses spherical fuel pebbles composed of an outer graphite shell (0.5 cm thick) surrounding an inner
fuel region (5-cm diameter), as shown in Figure 3. The fuel region consists of 11,000 TRISO particles
suspended in a graphite matrix. A TRISO particle, also displayed in Figure 3, is a combination of four
different material layers surrounding a spherical fuel kernel: a porous graphite buffer, surrounded by
an inner pyrolytic carbon layer (IPyC), surrounded by a layer of silicon carbide (SiC), surrounded
finally by an outer pyrolytic carbon layer (OPyC). The thicknesses and densities of each TRISO particle
layer used in this study are given in Table 1. TRISO particles are arranged in the graphite matrix using
a lattice configuration. Each pebble is loaded with 9 g of 8% enriched UO2, making the total uranium
in the pebble equal to 7.93 g [6].

2.2. Reactor Physics Simulations
Detailed information about the spent fuel composition of a pebble is required to study its gamma
emissions. A Monte Carlo code can be used to model the PBR and simulate fuel burnup to acquire

 13

such information. The Monte Carlo N-Particle (MCNP) radiation transport code - version 6.2 was
selected for use in this study [7].
Several schemes exist for arranging pebbles in a lattice [8]. Pebbles are typically arranged in either
Body-Centered-Cubic (BCC), Face-Centered-Cubic (FCC), or hexagonal close-packed (HCP) unit
cells. Choosing a lattice configuration depends on volumetric packing fraction requirements
established by designs. For this study, the reference modular PBR calls for a packing fraction of 68%.
A maximum packing fraction of about 68% is possible using a BCC arrangement, so it is acceptable
for this study [6],[9]. Such a packing fraction requires the lattice cube side lengths to be 6.93 cm.
Shown in Figure 4, the BCC cell contains two full volume spheres: one whole pebble in the center,
and one-eighth of a pebble in each corner of the cube. The outer radius of a pebble is 3 cm, giving it
a total volume of 113.1 cm3. He-4 is used as the coolant in the PBR, so the space between the pebbles
in the BCC lattice is filled with He-4 of density 0.000178 g/cm3. The reactor core contains 360,000
fuel pebbles. For the core power of 265 MWth, each single fuel pebble outputs a power of 736 Wth
[6]. To generate data for this study, an infinite BCC lattice of pebbles was used to model the entire
PBR core. This model was chosen for its simplicity and relatively low computational run time when
compared with much more complicated full core models.

Figure 4. Pebble arrangement within BCC cell. There is one whole fuel pebble in the center of the
 cell, and one-eighth of a pebble in each corner, totaling two pebbles in the cell.

TRISO particles were arranged in a simple cubic (SC) lattice with a packing fraction of 51.3%. At low
packing fractions such as this example, Monte Carlo simulations using a SC, BCC, FCC, or a simple
hexagonal lattice structure provide similar results [10], [11]. The SC lattice for the TRISOs can be
created in MCNP through the built-in cubic lattice feature designed to ease the placement of repeated
structures (cells) throughout the geometry. With this configuration, the lattice cubes have side lengths
of 0.1812 cm. A TRISO particle is placed in the center of a simple cubic cell. The space between the
TRISO particle and the edges of the cell wall is filled with graphite of density 1.6 g/cm3. Allowing
infinite repetition of TRISO particle - bearing simple cubes would result in TRISOs being clipped by
the edges of the pebble fuel region, meaning incomplete TRISOs would be present in the pebble. This
artifact does have a noticeable impact on simulation accuracy [10]. To address this issue, a custom
lattice of 27 vertical layers of simple cubes containing only graphite and 11,000 TRISO - bearing simple
cubes was created.
Spent fuel composition data was extracted for burnup values of 11, 33, 55, 77, and 90 GWd/MTU.
Neutron flux experienced by the pebbles was tallied at the various burn-up steps. ENDF/B-VII.0
nuclear cross-section library data at 600 K was used for all materials [12] . MCNP simulations were

 14

run using 24,000,000 (240 cycles with 100,000 particles per cycle) neutron transport histories per
criticality calculation. Fission rates for a single 90 GWd/MTU burned pebble were calculated through
burnup simulation in MCNP and through fission rate tallies in MCNP using neutron source terms
generated by ORIGEN for the spent pebbles [13]. Gamma source definitions were created using the
MCNP Intrinsic Source Constructor (MISC) based on nuclide composition acquired through burn-
up simulation and through nuclide production calculations done based on computed fission rates [14].
MISC is a tool for MCNP that facilitates the construction of source definitions for radioactive
materials. By supplying MISC with a material definition, a source definition for a desired particle type
is created for use in MCNP.

2.3. Gamma-Emission Simulations
For gamma-ray analysis, a homogenized pebble model was created. Such a simplification reduces the
complexity associated with generating radiation source points in MCNP. In homogenizing the fuel
pebble, the TRISO particles and graphite matrix were combined into a single cell of equivalent mass,
nuclide composition, and density to the constituent parts. The 0.5 cm-thick graphite shell was not
modified by this process. The entire homogeneous fuel region was set to be the radiation source. A
flowchart summarizing the modeling and simulation of the pebble is shown in Figure 5.

3. BENCHMARKING EXPERIMENTS
Benchmarking experiments were performed with the goal of preliminary validation of the modeling
framework. It was of interest, to experimentally confirm the high-energy (>3 MeV) fission induced
gamma-ray signatures being considered in the modeling.
Since the neutron energy spectrum of a pebble approximates a Watt fission spectrum (average neutron
energy ~2 MeV), a Cf-252 fission neutron source, was thought to be ideal for benchmarking the
simulations (Figure 6). A U-238 coupon (DU) was neutron interrogated, and gamma ray intensity
ratios of certain fission fragment pairs of interest were determined simultaneously with a HPGe
gamma detector system. Since these decay products have half-lives of only a few seconds, the delayed
gamma-ray intensities will reach an equilibrium state during neutron irradiation for a certain time.
These gamma rays were best measured on-line (i.e. while the sample was being irradiated). The
schematic of the experimental set-up is shown in Figure 7. The ~1 µCi Cf-252 source was housed in
a tungsten cave to reduce its fission product gamma ray background.

3.1. Spectral Analysis
A plot of the fission cross sections of U-235, U-238 and Pu-239 as a function of neutron energy is
shown in Figure 8. As can be seen, at the energy of the source neutrons (neutron energy band from
fission) produced in a pebble, all the fission cross sections are about 1 barn compared to 100s of barns
at thermal energies. Thus, long irradiations (one month) and large sample mass were necessary to
obtain statistically significant fission yields. Due to the implications of safety and security involved,
only U-238 fissions in a 72 g DU (Depleted Uranium) sample were measured.

 Figure 8. Fission cross-section as a function of incident neutron energy.
The spectral analysis focused on the region from 3200 keV to 3700 keV because of the low passive
background above 3 MeV, the greater penetration of gamma rays through the pebble, and the relatively
uniform attenuation. The primary source of background in this energy range is from the Cf-252 fission
gammas, passive decay gammas, and from spontaneous fission prompt gamma rays. The net U-238
fission gamma rays are shown in Figure 9, after the relevant backgrounds were subtracted. The identity
of some of the spectral peaks of an isotope were validated from the ratios of peak counts associated
with their decay intensity ratios. The decay intensities were obtained from available Evaluated Nuclear
Structure Data File (ENSDF) library. Table 2 summarizes the peak count ratios of some of the
isotopes obtained from the experimental data and compared with that obtained from MCNP
simulations and that expected from data reported in the ENSDF files. Results indicate a good match
between the experimental data and the simulated values.

4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

4.1. Pebble Fuel Burn-up
Pebble fuel burn-up was simulated using MCNP to obtain spent fuel nuclide data used in this study.
Results for U and Pu isotopic mass buildup and depletion are shown in Table 3 and Table 4 and are
plotted in Figure 10 for a single spent fuel pebble of initial U-235 enrichment of 8 wt%. U-235 and
U-238 mass decreased with burnup while other U isotopes increased. Pu mass generally increased with
burn-up, with Pu-239 mass decreasing towards the final burn-up level. ORIGEN was used to calculate
the neutron production rates in a single spent fuel pebble at various burn-up levels due to spontaneous
fission of actinides and (α, n) reactions occurring in the pebble[13]. Primary contributing isotopes for
spontaneous fission neutrons are U-238, Pu-240, Pu-242, Cm-242, Cm-244, and Cm-246 [5]. The
primary (α, n) reaction neutron producing isotopes are Pu-238, Pu-239, Pu-240, Am-241, Cm-242,
and Cm-244. Total neutron production rates at various burn-up are plotted in Figure 11. A single
spent pebble at 90 GWd/MTU burnup is expected to produce approximately 23,000 neutrons/s,
which matches expectations from literature of 20,500 neutrons/s [5].

4.2. Pebble Neutron Spectrum, Fission and Gamma Emission Rates
Figure 11 (b) shows the neutron flux spectrum of a spent fuel pebble burned to 90 GWd/MTU from
0.001 MeV to 20 MeV. No variations in this spectrum arising from alternative pebble irradiation
histories were considered. Therefore, this study represents the results for an average spent pebble that
has reached its end-of-life burn-up of 90 GWd/MTU and was burned in the center of the reactor
core. From Figure 11, most neutron energies produced passively by the spent pebble are either
intermediate (100 eV – 200 keV) or fast (200 keV – 10 MeV). For this reason, calculations involving
fission yield data used yields corresponding to fast fission as opposed to thermal fission. After
accounting for the atom density and the total fission cross section of all fuel material in the pebble,
the neutron flux (F4 tally) as calculated by MCNP was converted into fission reaction rate density.
This passive fission reaction rate density, once multiplied by the volume of all fuel material in the 90
GWd/MTU burned pebble and by the neutron source strength of 23,000 neutrons/s, resulted in a
passive fission rate of 4.03 fissions/s.
Using predicted fission rates in each nuclide from the burn-up simulation, the fraction of total
fissions/s contributed by each actinide was determined and applied to give expected fission rates for
each actinide in the single 90 GWd/MTU burned pebble. The twelve gamma emissions of interest to

this study are emitted mainly by the nine fission products given in Table 5. Precursors of these nine
fission product nuclides are short lived, with the longest half-life among the nine isotopes being 618
seconds.

4.3. Actinide Mass Quantification Method
To determine the proportion of U-235, U-238, and Pu-239 in a spent fuel pebble, a numerical method
was developed. The method exploits the unique distribution of the nine nuclides of interest in the
pebble resulting from its self-produced neutrons inducing fission at different rates in actinides. By
utilizing linear combinations of known fission product yields for each actinide isotope and branching
ratios for each of the twelve gammas, the number of fissions of each actinide necessary to result in a
set of detected counts of each gamma can be solved [16]. This system of linear equations was arranged
in matrix form according to the equation:

 Ax = b (1)

, where A is a matrix containing the products of fission yields and gamma branching ratios related to
the nine nuclides of interest resulting from the fission of the actinides U-235, U-238, and Pu-239, x is
a vector describing the number of fissions that took place in the three actinides, and b is a vector
describing the number of counts detected for each gamma of interest. Eq. 1 was solved using the
method of least-square minimization. A Python script was created using the lstsq function of the
linear algebra module from NumPy to calculate the least-squares solution [17],[18]. The number of
fissions predicted to have taken place in each actinide can be used to predict the mass of each actinide
present via the fission reaction rate density equation:

 RRDi = ϕΣi = ϕσiρiNA/Mi (2)

 23

, where RRDi is the nuclear reaction rate density for nuclide i, ϕ is the neutron flux experienced by
nuclide i across the volume, Σi is the macroscopic cross section for a nuclear reaction, σi is the spectrum
averaged microscopic cross section of nuclide i for a nuclear reaction, ρi is the mass density, NA is the
Avogadro’s constant, and Mi is the molar mass of nuclide i. Using Eq. 2, the number of nuclide atoms
present in a material undergoing neutron irradiation can be determined. The determined number of
atoms of nuclide i can then be multiplied by the ratio Mi/NA to calculate the mass of nuclide i present
in the material.
The presence of Rh-106 and La-140 in the spent pebble complicated the use of the twelve gammas as
these nuclides also emit gamma rays in the 3 MeV range. In the 3.20 - 3.41 MeV region, Rh-106 and
La-140 gamma emissions coincide with the first half of the twelve gammas shown in Table 5. The
intensity of the gammas emitted by Rh-106 and La-140 in this region are much greater than the nearby
gammas of interest, rendering them indistinguishable from the background. This interference is not
present in the 3.44 - 3.62 MeV region, making the 3.45 (Rb-91), 3.46 (Rb-93), 3.53 (Rb-90), 3.58 (Y-
95), 3.59 (Rb-91), and 3.62-MeV (Sr-95) gammas suitable for use in calculation of the actinide fission
rates. In calculating the fission rates of U-235, U-238, and Pu-239 in the 90 GWd/MTU pebble, it was
found that only five of these six gamma emissions were needed to come to a solution when solving
for the three actinides simultaneously. The 3.446-MeV (Rb-91) gamma was excluded from the
calculation due to its lower intensity compared to the other five suitable gammas.
From the burn-up simulation, the fission rates of actinides in the pebble are known. The ratios of the
fission rates of U-235 to U-238, Pu-239 to U-238, and Pu-241 to U-238 were taken from the burn-up
results and used to compare with the same ratios predicted using the least-squares method. The fission
rate in Pu-241 was estimated by assuming the constant proportionality of Pu-241 to Pu-239 mass in
all pebbles that reach 90 GWd/MTU burnup (see Figure 10. (a) U and (b) Pu isotope mass versus
burn-up level for a single spent fuel pebble.). The least accurate predicted fission rate ratio was found
to be 0.0786, which is 9.81% larger than the expected value of 0.0716 for the ratio of Pu-239 to U-
238 fission rates. Using Eq. 2, the relative number of atoms of each actinide was solved for and then
turned into a relative mass of each actinide. The burn-up simulation indicated that 92.5% of the initial
7.31-g U-238 remained at the 90 GWd/MTU burnup stage. Assuming this to be a constant across all
pebbles that reach this burnup level would allow prediction of the actual actinide mass present based
on the relative masses. By scaling the predicted relative mass of U-238 to match this expectation, the
relative masses of U-235, Pu-239 and Pu-241 could be adjusted to yield the quantity of each isotope
present in the spent fuel pebble. Table 6 and Table 7 show the results of this process. The largest
deviation from expectations was in the predicted mass for Pu-241 of 0.040 g, which is 10.2% larger
than expected. This inaccuracy may come from the assumption of constant proportionality between
Pu-241 and Pu-239 made when calculating the fission rate of Pu-241.

4.4. Sensitivity Analysis of Fission Rate Calculation
A statistical analysis of fluctuations in the inferred number of induced fissions and hence the actinide
masses was conducted. One-sigma uncertainties in the gamma counts were considered to study the
uncertainty introduced in the prediction of actinide masses. The pebble gamma currents (weighted F1
tally) from Table 5 were taken to be the gamma emission rates. For this analysis, it was assumed that
all gammas emitted from the pebble in the 4π are detected. Such an ideal condition was assumed to
offer an absolute best-case scenario for required counting times. The fission-rate predictions were
averaged, and the standard deviations of the rates for each actinide were then used to propagate error
in the mass predictions for four actinides.
Time steps were set to start at one second of counting time and increase by one order of magnitude
until the errors associated with the least-squares mass predictions were acceptable. Table 8 displays
the average predicted masses resulting from this analysis for each actinide. The average masses are the
same at each time step. Only the relative errors change because of additional counting time. Table 9
displays the changes in the relative errors of the average mass predictions for each actinide at each
time step. At the 1E6 seconds (11.57 days) time step, the average predicted masses are 0.148 ± 0.061
g, 6.77 ± 0.58 g, 0.077 ± 0.100 g, and 0.040 ± 0.053 g for U-235, U-238, Pu-239, and Pu-241,
respectively. At the 1E8 seconds (1,157 days) time step, the average predicted masses are 0.148 ±
0.006 g, 6.77 ± 0.06 g, 0.077 ± 0.010 g, and 0.040 ± 0.005 g for U-235, U-238, Pu-239, and Pu-241,
respectively. Increasing count times reduces the error associated with the average mass predictions.

 25

The only way to reduce count times in this analysis is to increase the gamma emission rates by
including additional pebbles. As an example, assuming the fission rate of 100 spent pebbles packed
closely is approximately equal to the fission rate of 100 lone spent pebbles, then the time required to
count the pebbles until a desired level of accuracy is reached can be reduced by 100. This reduction
means the 4.10% (U-235), 0.857% (U-238), 13.0% (Pu-239), and 13.0% (Pu-241) accuracies of
counting a single pebble for 1E8 seconds (1,157 days) could be achieved by counting 100 pebbles for
1E6 seconds (11.57 days). Note that the self-attenuation and subcritical multiplication effects for a
bulk quantity of pebbles were not considered in this analysis.

5. CONCLUSIONS
The goal of this project was to investigate neutron self-interrogation as an innovative non-destructive
assay method for the direct measurement of actinide content in spent pebble bed reactor fuel. A
method that can predict the relative fission rates for U-235, U-238, Pu-239, and Pu-241 that occur in
a spent fuel pebble based on the detection of five gamma rays above 3 MeV was developed. These
relative fission rates were then converted into mass ratios of the actinides present in the spent pebble.
As an example, our study shows that the mass of these four actinides can be predicted to 4.10%,
0.857%, 13.0%, and 13.0% accuracy for U-235, U-238, Pu-239, and Pu-241, respectively, when
measuring 100 end-of-life spent fuel pebbles for approximately 12 days. Utilizing 4π detection to count
pebbles is imperative to approach this ideal scenario in real-world applications. The practicality of
such an approach warrants further study.
