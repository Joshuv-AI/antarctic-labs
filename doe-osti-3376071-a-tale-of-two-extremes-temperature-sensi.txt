I. ACCOMPLISHMENTS

Major goals of the project: Soils represent the largest terrestrial carbon (C) pool, and
the flux of carbon dioxide (CO2) from soils to the atmosphere is ~ 6-10 times more than
anthropogenic emissions. Understanding responses of soil CO2 emissions to warming is
crucial for evaluating feedback to ongoing environmental changes. The relationship
between microbial respiration and temperature is typically modeled using a Q10 function.
Generally, observations of the apparent Q10 of soil respiration are higher for cold vs.
warm ecosystems, reflecting expected biophysical controls of Arrhenius kinetics.
However, results from two field warming experiments in the tropics contradict this
expectation, both observing extraordinarily high soil respiration responses to in situ
warming.

Our overall objective for the proposed work is to reduce uncertainty in temperature
sensitivity of soil C loss by systematically synthesizing underlying mechanisms related
to soil C turnover and stabilization. We are evaluating the temperature sensitivity of soil
respiration in ecosystems across temperature extremes (e.g., arctic/boreal and tropical
systems) by integrating data collected from field warming experiments with machine
learning and biogeochemical models. Specifically, this synthesis will allow us to test the
following hypotheses:

 (1) Biogeochemical responses in cold ecosystems are primarily driven by
 temperature constraints on microbial enzyme kinetics, while these responses
 under warm ecosystems are controlled primarily by microbial community function
 and substrate supply.
 (2) In cold ecosystems, warming at temperatures too low for macromolecule
 decomposition (<5 degree C) exacerbate microbial C limitation within days-
 weeks, while warming at moderate-warm temperatures (>5 degree C) increase
 substrate availability directly via increased decomposition (also within days-
 weeks) and indirectly via greater plant productivity (over weeks-months). In warm
 tropical ecosystems the indirect temperature effects of soil drying and possible
 declines in new plant inputs of substrate will reduce substrate availability relative
 to consumption.
 (3) The effects of warming on activities of extant soil microbial communities may
 be very rapid, whereas the impact on microbial community composition,
 seasonality of plant inputs and soil moisture, and annual ecosystem productivity
 may not become apparent for weeks, months or even several years, depending
 on the system.

Our data synthesis from sites with high and low temperature extremes are providing
generalizable insights for future models. Mechanistic soil respiration models, such as
DAMM (Dual Arrhenius and Michaelis-Menten), often rely on enzyme kinetics or
substrate supply to capture temperature responses. Data streams are being readily
available from this synthesis project for model parameterization and evaluation. We
predict that modeling the biochemical mechanisms controlling the temperature
responses of soil respiration, particularly interactions between respiration, enzyme

activities, and substrate availability to microbes, greatly improve our capacity to predict
soil respiration. A comprehensive understanding of soil respiration-temperature
relationships in regions across temperature extremes should identify emergent patterns
across ecosystems and reduce uncertainties in temperature sensitivity of soil respiration
in other regions.

Modeling Temperature Sensitivity of Soil Respiration from Two Field Warming
Experiments in Tropics: We systematically represent the potentially confounding
effects of temperature sensitivity of enzymatic reactions, changes in the microbial
enzymatic capacity, and substrate supply as they affect microbial decomposition of soil
C in tropical forests under warming. The DAMM (Dual Arrhenius Michaelis-Menten)
model was optimized using data generated from the Soil Warming Experiment in
Lowland Tropical Forest (SWELTR) in Panama and Tropical Responses to Altered
Climate Experiment (TRACE) in Puerto Rico. By including simple representations of
measured warming-induced changes in microbial biomass and changes in soil moisture
that affect substrate diffusion, we show that the observed increased soil respiration with
warming does not reflect a change in activation energy, which remained at reasonable
values (Ea: ~62 kJ mol-1 in SWELTR and 73 kJ mol-1 in TRACE) for both warmed and
control plots in optimized model parameterization. In contrast, optimization of the model
without representation of changing microbial biomass required a higher activation
energy (Ea: ~82 kJ mol-1 in SWELTR and 87 kJ mol-1 in TRACE) in the warmed plots,
which is inconsistent with kinetic theory. Hence, the higher soil respiration observed
under warming could be explained by the increased enzymatic capacity of the microbial
biomass rather than an increased activation energy of the enzymatic reactions.

The seasonal nature of the lowland tropical forest in Panama (for SWELTR) also
influences soil warming responses, with greater increases in soil respiration observed
during the wet season due to enhanced microbial activity in wet seasons over dry
seasons. On the other hand, we did not find any seasonal response in the rainforest in
Puerto Rico (for TRACE). Instead, landscape topography likely introduced variations
that further modulate soil moisture distribution and thermal regimes, thereby influencing
the spatial heterogeneity of soil warming responses. For instance, warming responses
were strongest in ridge, where warmer plots were also wetter than control plots.
However, the tendency of warming-induced soil drying was observed in slope and valley
(slope>valley). Thus, seasonal soil warming responses in Panama's tropical forest are
driven by enhanced wet season microbial activity, whereas Puerto Rico's aseasonal
rainforest exhibits spatially heterogeneous warming responses primarily influenced by
landscape topography's impact on moisture and thermal regimes across ridges, slopes,
and valleys.

These findings underscore the importance of using parsimonious yet mechanistic
representations in biogeochemical models to accurately predict future carbon fluxes
from tropical soils under changing environmental conditions. We show that Q10-based
models may misrepresent these dynamic and nuanced ecosystem responses, thereby
highlighting that the inherent complexity of soil carbon dynamics necessitates
mechanistic modeling beyond simple Q10 relationships by systematically representing

potential confounding factors for accurate projections of future soil carbon loss. This
nuanced response is crucial for understanding the true impact of warming on the
microbial engine of soil carbon cycling. Our modular approach allows us to attribute
agreement or disagreement of model outputs with measured respiration rates and
specific model functions, as well as identify model structures necessary for representing
soil temperature responses in broader Earth System Models. This will enable us to
increase both the sophistication and capability of models to represent these processes
from individual to Earth-System scales. We are currently writing this manuscript for a
peer-review journal article.

AquaMEND Integration and Arctic Soil Warming Insights: We synthesized model-
based and empirical insights into the interactions between soil warming, moisture, and
microbial dynamics using the AquaMEND framework. AquaMEND framework has shed
light on the intricate relationship between soil warming, moisture, and microbial
dynamics, particularly in cold, carbon-rich soils like those in the Arctic. This framework
integrates the Microbial ENzyme Decomposition (MEND) model with PHREEQC (a
geochemical modeling program) to simulate how warming impacts microbial respiration,
carbon use efficiency (CUE), and enzyme dynamics. Simulations applying a 2°C
warming scenario revealed that microbial growth and respiration increase under
moderate conditions. However, under extreme moisture limitations, respiration becomes
constrained. This highlights the complex, non-linear ways in which warming affects soil
C cycling.

Empirical data from Arctic soils showed that even with similar overall organic C pools,
the depletion of labile carbon (easily accessible carbon for microbes) can suppress
temperature-driven respiration. This was particularly evident in samples pre-incubated
at -2°C versus -6°C. This finding emphasizes that both temperature and substrate
availability must be explicitly represented in models to accurately predict C cycling
responses. Soil C cycling is governed by tightly linked processes, including enzyme
activity, microbial growth, and CUE. These processes are further influenced by complex
interactions among temperature, moisture, and substrate availability, making
straightforward interpretations of soil respiration's temperature sensitivity challenging.
The AquaMEND model, by incorporating geochemical and microbial processes like
sulfate reduction and methanogenesis within a redox-sensitive environment,
demonstrated the critical yet indirect role of moisture in regulating decomposition. While
warming can enhance microbial activity, extreme moisture conditions can significantly
dampen these effects. These results again underscore the necessity for models to go
beyond simple temperature dependencies and explicitly account for substrate
availability and the nonlinear interactions between temperature, moisture, and microbial
physiology. A more comprehensive understanding of these processes, as provided by
frameworks like AquaMEND, is crucial for improving predictions of soil C feedbacks
under changing environmental conditions, particularly in vulnerable Arctic ecosystems.

Synergistic Activities: Leveraging activities from this project, PI Sihi also mentored a
master's student (Yaxi Du), who completed her master's thesis research at Emory that
utilized another long-term field warming experiment in a subtropical forest (Whitehall
Forest) in Athens, GA. We isolated confounding elements and elucidated the principal
mechanisms underpinning soil organic carbon (SOC) dynamics under diverse
environmental scenarios: warming (ambient, +1.5°C, and +2.5°C) and substrate (carbon
and nutrients) addition treatments. Our systematic evaluations helped separate the
direct and indirect effects of warming (e.g., the inherent and apparent temperature
sensitivity of SOC formation and loss). Our findings revealed that warming alone did not
sustain increased soil respiration or microbial biomass, underscoring the dominant role
of C limitation in regulating soil respiration. Conversely, substrate (labile C) addition
treatments, either alone or with nutrients, significantly boosted microbial metabolism,
supporting a co-limitation framework where nutrient amendments became impactful only
after alleviating C scarcity. Enzymatic assays further indicated that substrate depletion,
not enzyme denaturation, constrained prolonged warming effects, emphasizing the
need for continued investigation into SOC dynamics and microbial adaptation,
particularly in underrepresented nutrient-poor ecosystems. These findings shed light on
the delicate balance between underlying mechanisms that control SOC dynamics under
changing environmental conditions, emphasizing the nuanced interdependence of
temperature, substrate resource availability, and soil C dynamics.

IV. IMPACTS

Impact on the development of the principal discipline(s) of the project: The global
soil organic carbon stock is approximately twice as large as the carbon contained in the
atmosphere and plants combined. Thus, a small change in soil carbon loss could have
a significant effect on the fate of land-based carbon cycle under future environmental
conditions. However, soil carbon cycle modules in existing Earth system models (ESMs)
do not accurately represent soil warming responses, which is the primary reason for
observed large uncertainty in the dynamics of terrestrial carbon storage across models
used for coupled model intercomparison projects (CMIPs). We are leveraging an
explainable machine learning approach coupled with a modular framework to represent
underlying mechanisms and potential confounding effects of soil carbon response to
warming. Our synthesis work will benefit model benchmarking and evaluations. Thus,
our findings will likely improve representations of carbon cycle processes in ESMs
including DOE’s E3SM land model (ELM).

Impact on other disciplines: Soil organic carbon is a key component of soil organic
matter, which is known to directly influence many ecosystem services, including
production of food and fiber, provision of nutrients, habitat for beneficial microorganisms
used in pharmaceuticals, and other sectors. A better understanding of soil soil response
to warming can have diverse applications for these different sectors and help solve
other disciplinary challenges. For example, robust estimates of soil carbon loss under
future environmental conditions in natural and managed landscapes can help prioritize
targeted areas for projects related to energy investments in the US and beyond.

Impact on the development of human resources: Multiple early career researchers
are being attracted to the Environmental System Science program through our
synthesis work. Undergraduate and graduate student training were enhanced through
research and teaching activities and building networks through participation in lab
meetings, project meetings, workshops, and conferences.

A direct impact of student training can be seen in successful completion of a master
thesis project based on a long-term warming experiment in a subtropical forest site
(Whitehall Forest in Athens, GA) from PI Sihi’s Lab at Emory. Additionally, this research
also attracted an undergraduate student to Sihi Lab to conduct honors thesis research
on soil carbon cycle processes at Emory. PI Wood further mentored two PhD students
to work on synthesizing warming response from TRACE site. Both PI(s) Sihi and Wood
are in the process of hiring a postdoctoral researcher to work on this project. Two
postdocs are being directly trained through this project.

Impact on physical, institutional, and information resources that form
infrastructure: This does not apply to our synthesis project!

Impact on technology transfer: This does not apply to our synthesis project!

Impact on society beyond science and technology: Reducing soil (and ecosystem)
carbon loss in land-based ecosystems (e.g., forest, wetland, and agricultural lands) is
gaining attention as a natural solution to ongoing environmental change (or extreme
weather events). An improved understanding of temperature sensitivity of soil
respiration under current and future environmental conditions is a prerequisite to
accurately quantify the amount of soil carbon lost, especially when it is treated as a
commodity in the voluntary carbon market. Our robust method of AI-enabled
biogeochemical modeling approach will be useful in quantifying the fate of soil carbon
under different land use and land management systems, and restoration efforts at the
regional to national scales. With a clearer picture of how soils contribute to the carbon
cycle, DOE can prioritize areas where energy investments are most likely to yield
tangible environmental and societal benefits.
