Goniothalamin as a Styryl‐Lactone Toxicophore in Cancer Models: Electrophile‐Driven DNA Damage, Reactive Oxygen Species–Endoplasmic Reticulum Stress Signaling and Detoxification‐Relevant Safety Considerations
Authors: Nataša Joković, Strahinja Pešić, Marija Novaković, Anđela Dubroja Krstić, William N. Setzer, Javad Sharifi‐Rad, Daniela Calina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGoniothalamin (GTN), a natural styryl‐lactone from the Goniothalamus genus, has demonstrated cytotoxic properties against a variety of human cancer cell lines with minimal effects on normal cells. Its traditional medicinal use and preliminary preclinical evidence suggest potential as a selective anticancer agent. The purpose of this review is to summarize the preclinical anticancer activity, molecular mechanisms, and therapeutic potential of GTN and its semi‐synthetic derivatives across cancer cell lines and animal models. A comprehensive literature search was conducted on the anticancer effects of GTN using databases including PubMed, Scopus, and ScienceDirect. Studies reporting in‐vitro cytotoxicity, half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values, mechanisms of action, synergistic drug effects, and in‐vivo antitumor efficacy were included. Data on GTN enantiomers and semisynthetic derivatives were also analyzed. GTN exhibited potent, dose‐ and time‐dependent cytotoxicity in breast, colorectal, hepatoma, leukemia, and other cancer cell lines (IC50 in the low micromolar range), while sparing normal cells. Mechanistically, GTN induced DNA damage, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, cell cycle arrest, endoplasmic reticulum stress, apoptosis, autophagy, necroptosis, and anoikis. The anticancer activity of GTN enantiomers appears to be cell‐line dependent: although the (R)‐enantiomer showed higher potency in several cancer models, the (S)‐enantiomer displayed greater activity in selected cancer cell lines. GTN synergized with chemotherapeutics such as paclitaxel, vinblastine, and cisplatin, enhancing apoptosis and reducing cell viability. Semi‐synthetic derivatives, including methoxy‐ and nitro‐substituted analogs, demonstrated enhanced potency and selectivity. In animal models, GTN exhibited antitumor activity without detectable toxicity. GTN is a promising natural anticancer agent with multimodal mechanisms of action and selective cytotoxicity. Semisynthetic derivatives further improve its potency and specificity. Further in‐vivo studies, bioavailability, and pharmacokinetic investigations are warranted to advance GTN towards clinical application.

Introduction

Cancer is a group of diseases characterized by the uncontrolled growth and spread of abnormal cells within the body [1]. These cells escape normal regulatory processes, such as cell death, and can spread to other areas, leading to the formation of new tumors, a process known as metastasis. Cancer is a major global health concern, with 20 million new cases diagnosed and 9.7 million deaths reported worldwide in 2022 [2]. The most common types of cancer include breast, lung, colorectal, and prostate cancer. Cancer can affect nearly any part of the body and is often caused by genetic mutations, environmental factors, or a combination of both [3]. Current anticancer drugs have several limitations, including low efficacy due to tumor heterogeneity, treatment resistance, and inadequate targeting of cancer stem cells, and their administration often results in side effects and toxicity [4, 5]. The discovery of new drugs that can be effectively used in cancer treatment remains a significant challenge [6, 7]. One promising approach is exploring natural plant‐derived compounds identified in ethnobotanical studies as drugs for traditional healing practices [8]. Goniothalamin (GTN) is a styryl lactone primarily synthesized by plants of the Goniothalamus genus (Annonaceae), which are traditionally used in Southeast Asia for the treatment of body pains, rheumatism, skin conditions, typhoid fever, tympanites, stomach aches, and fever, as well as for inducing abortion and preventing aging [9]. Studies on the various biological activities of GTN have shown that it possesses antimalarial, trypanocidal, antifungal, insecticidal, antiprotozoal, and antiviral activities, as well as immunosuppressive, anti‐inflammatory, antinociceptive, and anticancer properties [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. The selective cytotoxic activity of GTN, against various cancer cell lines with minimal or no effect on normal cells, positions this compound as a promising candidate for anticancer therapy [19]. To better understand the mechanism underlying the anticancer effects of GTN and facilitate the development of targeted therapies, in‐depth studies on cancer cell lines have provided insights into its action. GTN exerts its cytotoxic effect by inducing DNA damage and generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), primarily leading to apoptosis [20]. However, autophagy, necroptosis, and anoikis have also been detected as forms of cell death in certain cancer cell lines [21, 22]. The application of GTN induces cell cycle arrest at different phases, depending on the cell type, and modulates key signaling pathways involved in cell differentiation, proliferation, and survival [23, 24, 25, 26]. However, animal studies confirming the antiproliferative effect of GTN in vivo are still very limited. Nonetheless, the limited available animal studies suggest that GTN was relatively well tolerated under the tested experimental conditions, with no significant or observable toxicity reported at the investigated doses [27, 28]. The enhancement of the cytotoxic activity of GTN has been explored through the design and synthesis of various analogs which were tested for their antiproliferative activity and compared to GTN to evaluate their effectiveness. Several of these semisynthetic derivatives demonstrated superior anticancer properties, showing greater potency in inhibiting cancer cell growth than GTN [18, 29]. A review of the literature on the anticancer activity of GTN reveals that only one in‐depth review, published in 2014, specifically addresses the anticancer potentials of GTN and the molecular mechanisms underlying this activity [20]. Other review articles tend to focus on the cytotoxic effects of GTN only in a limited context, often considering it as part of the broader category of styryl lactones or all phytochemicals derived from Goniothalamus species [18, 19, 30, 31, 32]. While these articles contribute valuable insights, they do not provide a full exploration of the unique anticancer properties of GTN. Given the significant potential of GTN as an anticancer agent and the growing number of studies published on its effects, this review aims to summarize critical data on multiple aspects of GTN including its phytochemical source, the development of semisynthetic derivatives, detailed anticancer studies conducted on both cell lines and animal models and the molecular mechanisms underlying its antitumor action.

To improve reporting transparency, the manuscript was also evaluated using the SANRA checklist for narrative review articles. The completed SANRA checklist is provided as suppupporting material. This assessment was used to document the clarity of the review rationale, aims, literature basis, referencing, scientific reasoning, and data presentation (Supplementary file). This review will facilitate a deeper understanding of GTN's potential as a therapeutic agent and emphasize the need for further investigations to fully explore its application in cancer treatment.

Review Methodology

The inclusion criteria for this review primarily focused on the anti‐cancer effects of GTN and its extracts, while other biological effects were not considered in detail. The search was conducted using the following keywords: “goniothalamin,” “goniothalamus extract,” and “goniothalamin cancer.” Several databases were queried, including PubMed, ScienceDirect, Scopus, Taylor & Francis Online, and Wiley Online Library. Initial searches identified 1,825 works. After applying the exclusion criteria—such as removing duplicates, abstracts, conference papers, graphs, encyclopedia entries, non‐English articles, book chapters, and works where GTN was mentioned but not in the context of cancer, the total number of studies included in the review was reduced to 143. Of these, 73 studies focused specifically on the anti‐cancer effects of GTN. The publication dates of the studies were not used as exclusion criteria. Sources, Phytochemistry, Chemical Structure‐Anticancer Activity Relationship

GTN is a low molecular weight phenolic styryl lactone predominantly biosynthesized by plants of the genus Goniothalamus (Annonaceae) as a lipophilic secondary metabolite. Initially, it was isolated from the dried bark of Cryptocarya caloneura (Scheff.) Kosterm. (Lauraceae), and its occurrence was subsequently confirmed in Goniothalamus andersonii J. Sinclair, G. macrophyllus Hook.f. and Thomson, and G. malayanus Hook.f. and Thomson [33]. Further studies have documented its presence in various plant organs within the Goniothalamus genus and in some species of other genera (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). Quantitative analyses indicate that GTN amounts vary across plant tissues, with its levels significantly influenced by plant age, exhibiting higher accumulation in older plants [34].

GTN is synthesized in plants via the shikimic acid pathway, where phenylalanine transforms into cinnamic acid (C6–C3 unit), which subsequently integrates two acetate‐malonate (C4) units activated as coenzyme‐A [35]. This metabolic route positions GTN as a biogenetic precursor to diverse styryl lactone derivatives, including linear, epoxy, and cyclic analogs, which are distributed across various Goniothalamus species [30]. Although the precise biological function of GTN in plants remains to be fully elucidated, its documented antibacterial, antifungal, and insecticidal properties suggest a role in plant defense mechanisms. Furthermore, its phytotoxic activity, characterized by the inhibition of plant growth [36], implies potential allelopathic functions that may confer a competitive advantage against surrounding vegetation. GTN (5,6‐dihydro‐6‐styryl‐2‐pyranone, C13H12O2) belongs to the class of styryl‐pyrone compounds characterized by a fundamental C6–C3–C4 skeleton (Figure 2). Structurally, GTN comprises an α,β‐unsaturated δ‐lactone (cyclic ester) conjugated to a styryl moiety consisting of a benzene ring and a vinyl chain (Figure 2). Extraction of GTN from plant material can be achieved using various solvents, including methanol, ethyl acetate, petroleum ether, and hexane. Iskandar et al. demonstrated that nonpolar solvents yield higher GTN amounts than polar solvents such as ethyl acetate and methanol [37].

Chemical structure of (R)‐ and (S)‐enantiomers of goniothalamin.

The synthesis of GTN analogs and evaluation of their antiproliferative properties have facilitated the elucidation of structure‐activity relationships (SARs) relevant to its anticancer activity. The lactone moiety is crucial for its bioactivity, as the carbonyl group in the lactone ring makes GTN an electrophilic molecule capable of acting as a Michael acceptor, thereby undergoing nucleophilic attack by amino acid residues (cysteine, lysine, serine, or threonine) in proteins and N7 of guanine in nucleic acids [38]. Structural modifications replacing the α,β‐unsaturated δ‐lactone with α,β‐unsaturated δ‐lactam, dihydro‐γ‐pyrone, or γ‐pyrone motifs have resulted in significantly reduced cytotoxic potency. Additionally, the synthesis of aza‐goniothalamin, where oxygen in the lactone is substituted with nitrogen, has been shown to decrease antiproliferative activity due to increased electron density from carbonyl resonance with the nitrogen lone pair [38]. Collectively, these findings indicate that preservation of the electrophilic α,β‐unsaturated δ‐lactone scaffold is a primary SAR requirement for GTN cytotoxicity. Modifications that reduce Michael acceptor reactivity generally diminish antiproliferative potency, whereas derivatives that preserve or enhance this electrophilic center tend to retain stronger anticancer activity.

The styryl group is commonly found in pharmacologically significant compounds, contributing to enhanced lipophilicity, improved oral absorption, and increased biological activity [39]. The vinylic double bond in styryl moiety is nonessential but enhances molecular rigidity and strengthens the antiproliferative effects of GTN, whereas the lactone ring double bond is crucial for its cytotoxic activity [40]. Comparative studies by De Fátima et al. (2006) indicated that the cytotoxic activity of GTN enantiomers is strongly cell‐line dependent. The (R)‐enantiomer showed greater activity in NCI/ADR‐RES breast, NCI‐460 lung, HT‐29 colon, and UACC‐62 melanoma cancer cells, whereas the (S)‐enantiomer displayed higher potency in 786‐0 renal cancer cells and slightly greater activity in MCF‐7 breast, OVCAR‐03 ovarian, and PC‐3 prostate cancer cells [41]. However, Mohideen et al. (2013) reported that the (Z)‐isomer of GTN exhibited higher cytotoxicity against the Jurkat E6.1 lymphoblastic leukemia cell line compared to the naturally occurring and synthetic (E)‐isomer [42]. This apparent discrepancy suggests that the contribution of double‐bond geometry may be context‐dependent and influenced by the cellular background, thereby warranting further validation in broader experimental systems. Naturally occurring GTN is predominantly found as the (R)‐enantiomer, although the (S)‐enantiomer has also been reported in certain plant species. FOURNIER et al., [43, 44]. The spatial configuration of atoms around the C6 chiral center influences GTN's biological activity, with enantiomers exhibiting differential cytotoxicity against various cancer cell lines. Comparative studies by De Fátima et al. (2006) revealed that the (R)‐enantiomer demonstrated superior activity against breast (NCI/ADR‐RES), lung (NCI‐460), colon (HT‐29), and melanoma (UACC‐62) cancer cell lines, whereas the (S)‐enantiomer exhibited 1600‐fold better cytotoxicity against 786‐0 kidney cancer cells and was slightly more potent than the (R)‐form against breast (MCF) ovarian (OVCAR03) and prostate (PCO‐3) cell lines (de Fátima et al., 2006a). Analysis of (S)‐GTN analogs revealed that the presence of endo‐ and exo‐conjugated double bonds and aromatic moiety in (S)‐GTN is essential for its antiproliferative effects, as they interact with hydrophobic domains within cellular targets, ultimately inhibiting cell growth. A study on renal cancer cells demonstrated that the stereoisomers exhibited distinct cytotoxic mechanisms, with the (R)‐enantiomer inducing apoptosis, whereas the (S)‐enantiomer exerted its effects primarily through autophagy [45]. Furthermore, Weber et al. (2017) designed and synthesized 29 GTN analogs in either (R)‐ or (S)‐stereoisomeric forms with structural modifications in the lactone ring, vinylic double bond, and aromatic segment [40]. Their findings demonstrated that (R)‐GTN exhibited significantly greater efficacy than the (S)‐form against cancer cell lines tested. The racemic mixture displayed significant antiproliferative activity exclusively against the triple‐negative breast cancer cell line HBL‐100. Taken together, the available SAR evidence supports four main principles: (i) the α,β‐unsaturated δ‐lactone is the key toxicophoric unit; (ii) preservation of conjugation and molecular rigidity generally favors antiproliferative activity; (iii) substitution patterns on the aromatic ring can modulate potency and selectivity without uniformly improving activity; and (iv) stereochemistry at C6 significantly influences both potency and mechanism of cell death. Nevertheless, some SAR conclusions are still based on a limited number of analog series or single‐study observations, and therefore require broader validation before being used to guide definitive lead‐optimization strategies.

Semi‐Synthetic Derivatives

Natural products represent an important source of anticancer lead compounds because of their structural diversity and ability to modulate multiple cancer‐related pathways. However, their clinical translation is often limited by poor bioavailability, low solubility, limited selectivity, metabolic instability, and potential toxicity. To overcome these limitations, the parent compound undergoes chemical modifications to generate semisynthetic derivatives with enhanced properties, including improved bioactivity, pharmacokinetics (e.g., increased bioavailability and plasma half‐life, solubility, lipophilicity, selectivity, and reduced resistance [46]. The well‐documented antiproliferative activity of GTN across various cancer cell lines has driven the development of numerous synthetic strategies, including chemical synthesis, enzymatic catalysis, and microbial transformations [47]. These advancements have facilitated targeted modifications at key functional centers of GTN, the lactone moiety and the aromatic ring.

The most active semisynthetic GTN derivatives and their chemical structures are provided in Supporting Information S1: Table 2. Since the lactone moiety is essential for GTN's biological activity, modifications at this site frequently result in diminished cytotoxicity. However, one successful strategy involved the synthesis of N‐acylated aza‐goniothalamin analogs [48]. The N‐crotonyl derivative (1‐(E)‐but‐2‐enoyl‐6‐styryl‐5,6‐dihydropyridin‐2(1H)‐one) exhibited greater potency than GTN against most tested cancer cell lines, except for the non‐small cell lung carcinoma NCI‐H460. This derivative, along with its N‐acryloyl counterpart, demonstrated significant antiproliferative activity against glioblastoma (U251), breast (MCF‐7), kidney (786‐0), and prostate (PC‐3) cancer cells, surpassing the cytotoxic efficacy of the well‐established agent piplartine. Given that aza‐goniothalamin itself displayed weak cytotoxic activity due to the reduced electrophilicity of its lactone unit, N‐acylation introduced an additional electrophilic site, thereby enhancing cytotoxicity. However, in‐vivo studies using the ascitic and solid Ehrlich tumor model in mice revealed that the N‐crotonyl derivative exhibited the lowest tumor inhibition (42.9%) compared to GTN (83.7%) and aza‐goniothalamin (77.7%), suggesting that the biotransformation of aza‐goniothalamin into a more potent metabolite may have contributed to its enhanced in‐vivo efficacy [29].

Further modifications of N‐acetylated aza‐goniothalamin, such as N‐cinnamoyl substitution with three methoxy groups, yielded a derivative exhibiting remarkable cytotoxicity against the resistant pancreatic cancer cell line (Panc‐1) at a 20‐fold lower concentration than GTN [48]. This derivative displayed minimal cytotoxicity against normal human keratinocytes (HaCaT) and induced cell cycle arrest and apoptosis through distinct molecular signaling pathways. The antiproliferative effect of the newly synthesized derivative was not associated with reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation.

A predominant strategy for generating semisynthetic GTN derivatives has involved modifications at the aromatic ring, a structural component not critical for its antiproliferative activity. De Fátima et al. demonstrated that the presence of the phenyl group in (S)‐GTN was not essential for cytotoxicity, as its replacement with a cyclohexyl moiety resulted in enhanced antiproliferative activity against several cancer cell lines [18].

Introducing electron‐withdrawing nitro groups into the aromatic ring significantly enhanced GTN's antiproliferative activity. For instance, 12‐nitro‐goniothalamin exhibited threefold greater cytotoxicity against human promyelocytic leukemia (HL‐60) cells compared to GTN. Another nitro‐substituted derivative (10‐nitro‐goniothalamin) displayed slightly better potency against human lung carcinoma (A549) cells [49]. Weber et al. confirmed that nitro substitution at the C‐12 position significantly increased cytotoxic activity against human breast adenocarcinoma (MCF‐7) and triple‐negative breast cancer (HBL‐100) cell lines, whereas the parent GTN remained more potent against lung adenocarcinoma (A549) cells [40].

A series of 19 nitrogen‐containing GTN derivatives was also synthesized to enhance the cytotoxic potency of GTN. Among them, isobutyramide GTN and chloroacrylamide GTN exhibited superior cytotoxicity against all tested cancer cell lines [50]. Isobutyramide GTN was 10‐fold more potent than GTN against colorectal adenocarcinoma (Caco‐2), whereas chloroacrylamide GTN exhibited approximately 26‐fold lower IC50 values for MCF‐7 and PC‐3 cells. Both derivatives demonstrated increased selectivity by displaying significantly lower cytotoxicity against normal prostate epithelial cells (PNT2).

Methoxy substitutions on the aromatic ring have also been explored for synthesizing GTN derivatives with enhanced cytotoxicity. The (S)−4‐methoxy analog of GTN demonstrated approximately 600‐fold greater potency than (R)‐GTN against Adriamycin‐resistant cancer cells (NCI/ADR‐RES) and exhibited superior antiproliferative activity across multiple cancer cell lines (De Fátima et al. 2006). (R)‐ and (S)‐enantiomers of the 4‐methoxy derivative acted as substrates for P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp), an ATP‐binding cassette (ABC) transporter associated with multidrug resistance in cancer. These derivatives inhibited ABC transporter function, leading to increased intracellular accumulation of doxorubicin [51].

Structural modifications involving the addition of multiple methoxy groups significantly improved the antiproliferative efficacy of GTN. The 2,4‐dimethoxy analog exhibited superior potency compared to GTN against all tested cancer cell lines except multidrug‐resistant breast cancer (NCI/ADR‐RES). It surpassed doxorubicin in efficacy against ovarian (OVCAR‐03) and colorectal (HT‐29) cancer cells while exhibiting reduced toxicity to normal keratinocytes (HaCaT). Similarly, the 3,5‐dimethoxy and 2,4,5‐trimethoxy derivatives demonstrated greater cytotoxicity than GTN across most cancer cell lines. Although these derivatives displayed slightly weaker activity against multidrug‐resistant breast cancer cells, their total growth inhibition (TGI) values were significantly lower than those of doxorubicin [38].

Based on our findings, only one study has evaluated the antiproliferative activity of GTN derivatives featuring modifications to the lactone and aromatic moieties of the molecule. Bruder et al. modified the lactone moiety of GTN by replacing it with 5‐methyl‐2,5‐dihydrofuran‐2‐one, while introducing mono‐, di‐, and trimethoxylated, fluorinated, and hydroxylated groups at the aromatic ring. Among 17 analogs tested against 8 cancer cell lines, only ortho‐ and para‐trifluoromethyl derivatives exhibited greater cytotoxicity than GTN against multidrug‐resistant ovarian carcinoma (NCI‐ADR/RES) and leukemia (K‐562) cell lines. Their effects on other cancer cell lines remained comparable to those of the parent compound. Other derivatives in which a methyl group was introduced into the lactone ring exhibited weak antiproliferative activity. This suggests that the trifluoromethyl groups effectively activate the molecule's electrophilic site by electron withdrawal, enhancing its reactivity.

A distinct group of derivatives, subsequently termed klavuzons, was synthesized based on the core structure of GTN. Substituting the styryl moiety of GTN with a naphthyl group resulted in increased cytotoxicity against prostate (PC‐3) and breast (MCF‐7) cancer cell lines by restricting rotational flexibility between the two molecular fragments. The 1‐naphthyl substitution conferred a greater enhancement in cytotoxicity than the 2‐naphthyl analog, while additional methylation at positions 2 and 4 of the naphthalene ring led to a dramatic increase in antiproliferative activity. The 4‐methylnaphthalen‐1‐yl derivative exhibited the highest potency, displaying 80‐fold and 40‐fold greater cytotoxicity against PC‐3 and MCF‐7 cells, respectively, compared to (R)‐GTN [52]. Furthermore, the klavuzons demonstrated potent cytotoxic activity against pancreatic cancer cells (MIA PaCa‐2) with superior selectivity over healthy pancreatic cells (HPDEC) [53]. They also exhibited efficacy in hepatocellular carcinoma spheroids (HuH‐7), functioned as topoisomerase I inhibitors, and exhibited CRM1 inhibitory properties in cervical carcinoma (HeLa) cells [54].

Preclinical Anticancer Studies

In Vitro studies

Numerous studies on various cancer cell lines were conducted to assess the anticancer potential, minimal inhibitory concentration, and underlying mechanisms of GTN anticancer activity (Supporting Information S1: Table 4, Figure 3) [8, 12, 17, 18, 19]. These studies collectively indicate that GTN exerts concentration‐ and time‐dependent cytotoxic effects across several tumor models, including breast, colorectal, hepatoma, leukemia, lung, and renal cancer cell lines. Importantly, the available data also suggest a degree of selectivity toward malignant cells, as GTN generally showed lower toxicity in non‐malignant cell models. For example, GTN markedly reduced HepG2 hepatoblastoma cell proliferation to 19.8%, whereas proliferation of Chang normal liver cells remained at 97.6% [18].

In Vivo

A deeper understanding of the anticancer activity of GTN has been achieved through in vivo studies, which have contributed to determining effective therapeutic doses and elucidating its mechanisms of action. Cancer type, GTN dose and administration method, and the experimental design, can significantly affect the outcomes of these studies. An overview of the tested concentrations, model organisms, and outcomes is summarized in Table 1. To date, in vivo research on GTN remains limited, with only a few studies addressing its antitumor effects in animal models. Nevertheless, the available evidence consistently indicates that GTN exhibits significant anticancer efficacy with minimal to no observable toxicity in vivo [29, 55].

The anticancer effect of GTN on breast cancer was evaluated using SKBR3 xenografts established in athymic nude mice, which were subcutaneously injected in the left inguinal area [55]. Once tumors reached a volume of approximately 40 mm3, the mice were randomized into treatment groups receiving daily intraperitoneal (IP) injections of GTN (40 mg/kg), cisplatin (3 mg/kg), or a combination of GTN and cisplatin. Cisplatin administered alone failed to significantly delay tumor growth. In contrast, administration of GTN resulted in a pronounced inhibition of tumor progression compared to both cisplatin‐treated and control groups. Furthermore, the combined administration of GTN and cisplatin exhibited synergistic effects, leading to an even greater reduction in tumor growth compared to either treatment alone.

The potential of GTN in preventing the initiation and progression of colorectal cancer has been evaluated in mouse models of both colitis‐associated and sporadic colorectal cancer [26]. Long‐term treatment with varying doses of GTN resulted in a significant reduction in tumor multiplicity, load, and size of colitis‐associated tumors induced by chemical carcinogens. Based on these findings, the authors emphasized that early intervention with GTN is crucial for achieving its chemopreventive effects. In the sporadic colorectal cancer model, GTN administration reduced tumor multiplicity by approximately 50%, although no significant effect on tumor size was observed. Interestingly, GTN treatment altered tumor morphology, leading to the development of flatter and more spread lesions, suggesting an influence on tumor architecture and invasiveness.

In both models, GTN‐mediated tumor suppression was associated with downregulation of gene expression and protein levels of key pro‐inflammatory cytokines implicated in colorectal carcinogenesis, including IL‐6, IL‐17, TNF‐α, and S100A9 in tumor tissue. This effect is likely mediated through inhibition of NF‐κB/p65 nuclear translocation in immune cells within the tumor microenvironment, highlighting a potential mechanism by which GTN exerts its anti‐inflammatory and anticancer effects. Additionally, GTN treatment has been shown to restore normal tissue architecture, enhance epithelial cell proliferation within colonic crypts, and reduce epithelial damage in a chemically induced colitis model. The primary mechanism underlying these protective effects involves the downregulation of pro‐inflammatory gene expression, including interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), S100A9, interleukin‐23A (IL‐23A), interleukin‐22 (IL‐22), and interleukin‐17A (IL‐17A). Given that chronic colitis represents a major risk factor for the development of colitis‐associated colorectal cancer, these findings underscore the significant preventive potential of GTN in inflammation‐driven colon carcinogenesis.

The antiproliferative effects of racemic GTN and its enantiomers, (R)‐GTN and (S)‐GTN, were evaluated in BALB/c mice bearing Ehrlich solid tumors [56]. Administration of racemic GTN at doses of 30, 100, and 300 mg/kg resulted in tumor volume reductions of 48.5%, 40.9%, and 66.7%, respectively, with corresponding reduction in relative tumor weight of 36.7%, 31.6%, and 41.8%. The (S)‐enantiomer exhibited slightly greater anticancer potency compared to the (R)‐enantiomer and the positive control, doxorubicin, achieving a tumor volume reduction of 48.7% and a relative tumor weight decrease of 39.7% by the end of the treatment period. However, statistical analysis revealed no significant differences between the treatment groups. The anticancer activity of (R)‐GTN was further confirmed in mouse models of solid and ascitic Ehrlich tumors [29]. In these models, (R)‐GTN demonstrated a comparable tumor growth inhibition rate to that of fluorouracil used as a positive control, highlighting its potential efficacy as an anticancer agent.

The effect of GTN treatment on biochemical parameters, morphology, peripheral blood, and spleen dimensions was investigated in BALB/c mice with induced leukemia [57]. The GTN‐treated mice exhibited significant differences compared to the leukemia mice without treatment, particularly in the reduction of spleen size, which approached the dimensions seen in normal mice. Additionally, the serum biochemical markers associated with kidney and liver function in the GTN‐treated mice, such as urea, creatinine, total bilirubin, alanine transaminase, and aspartate aminotransferase, were comparable to those observed in normal mice, suggesting that GTN treatment helped normalize these vital functions. Furthermore, the peripheral blood of the GTN‐treated mice showed a decrease in the number of immature granulocytes and monocytes, alongside an increase in the apoptotic index. These findings underscore the potential of GTN in mitigating the effects of leukemia, demonstrating its positive impact on both the morphology and biochemical parameters associated with the disease.

GTN treatment in male Wistar albino rats with hepatocellular carcinoma induced by diethylnitrosamine (DEN) resulted in significant improvements in body weight and the preservation of normal liver histology, while reducing liver mass [58]. The formation, incidence, and volume of tumors were notably decreased. Furthermore, hepatic enzyme markers such as ALT, ALP, AST, γGT, and LDH were normalized in the GTN‐treated rats, indicating restoration of liver function compared to the untreated group. GTN treatment also restored normal oxidative status in liver cells by enhancing the activity of key antioxidant enzymes (CAT, SOD, GPx, and GR), while reducing the levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines that contribute to liver inflammation. The antiproliferative effect of GTN was evident from the decreased expression of PCNA, which was elevated in the liver cells of DEN‐induced tumor rats. Additionally, GTN inhibited liver cancer growth by promoting apoptosis, as shown by a reduction in Bcl‐2 expression and increased caspase‐3 activity. This anticancer effect was further supported by the attenuation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, a key factor in the progression and development of liver cancer.

The potential of GTN as a therapeutic agent in the treatment of prostate cancer was evaluated in different animal models. The Transgenic Adenocarcinoma of the Mouse Prostate (TRAMP) model was used to assess the effect of GTN on the development of prostatic cancer in immediate‐response groups and late‐response groups, where animals were sacrificed at 12 and 22 weeks of life [59]. The administration of GTN led to a slower progression of low and high‐grade intraepithelial neoplasia, with undifferentiated adenocarcinoma being identified 40% less in the treated GTN groups compared to the untreated control groups at the end of the study period. In the groups treated with celecoxib, the reduction was only 20%. The reduction of proliferation in the GTN treatment groups was shown by a decrease in PCNA antigen in both response groups, while an increase in apoptotic cells was observed only in immediate‐response groups. Western blotting analysis revealed changes in the protein levels of ventral prostate pro‐inflammatory mediators, which were different between the two observed age groups. The administration of GTN reduced the level of cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX2) in both the early and late responses, while the level of nuclear factor kappa B (NFκB) was lower in the GTN‐treated groups compared to control groups only during the early response. The level of phosphorylated signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (pSTAT3) was reduced only in the late response in the GTN groups. Western analysis also showed that the administration of GTN decreased the level of insulin‐like growth factor receptor 1 (IGFR1) in the younger group of animals. The administration of GTN also led to decreased plasma levels of IL‐1β and TNF‐α during the early response. These results indicate that the inflammatory process in the early stages played a key role in suppressing signaling pathways related to cell proliferation in the later, more advanced stages of cancer. Another study on the TRAMP model showed that the administration of GTN slowed down the progression of prostate cancer, resulting in a decrease in the levels of Androgen Receptor (AR) and Estrogen Receptor (ERα) in prostate tissue, indicating the involvement of the androgenic and estrogenic hormonal pathway mechanism of action [61]. The chemopreventive properties of GTN were confirmed in the prostate of senile 52‐week‐old FVB mice [60]. Treatment with GTN led to a reduction in inflammation through the decrease in the levels of inflammatory mediators TNF‐α, COX‐2, and iNOS, along with a simultaneous reduction in the protein levels of NF‐κB and p‐NF‐κB.

The investigation of the anticancer potential of encapsulated GTN in TRAMP mice also demonstrated its potential in treating prostate cancer [62]. Acetylated Dextran (Ac‐Dex) nanoparticles with GTN exhibited the same effect as pure GTN in delaying the development of prostate cancer in mice. The antiproliferative activity of both agents was shown by the reduction of proliferating cellular nuclear antigen (PCNA) in the analyzed prostate tissue.

Mechanism of Antitumor Action of Goniothalamin

The anticancer activity of GTN is based on several mechanisms that have been extensively documented across various human cancer cell lines and animal models. It can be achieved through the direct and indirect effects of GTN on cancer cells. GTN can directly interact with cellular components such as proteins and DNA, causing modifications that interfere with cancer cell growth, proliferation, and survival. These direct molecular interactions can lead to the activation of cell death pathways, such as apoptosis, thereby reducing tumor viability [31].

On the other hand, GTN also affects several key signaling pathways that regulate crucial processes in cells involved in cell cycle progression, angiogenesis, and metastasis [32]. These pathways are critical for the proliferation, growth, and spread of cancer cells, and their modulation by GTN suggests that it may inhibit tumor progression at multiple stages. Moreover, the anti‐inflammatory properties of GTN further contribute to its chemopreventive effects. Chronic inflammation is widely recognized as a key factor in the initiation and progression of many types of cancer [63]. By modulating inflammatory responses, GTN alters the pro‐tumorigenic microenvironment created by inflammatory mediators [26]. This anti‐inflammatory activity may limit the chronic inflammatory processes that facilitate tumorigenesis, providing a protective mechanism against cancer development.

The initial direct effect of GTN on cells is its genotoxic action, leading to alterations in DNA molecules. Treatment of CHO cells with GTN resulted in the detection of various chromosomal aberrations, including breaks and gaps in chromatids or whole chromosomes, interchanges, endoreduplications, and ring chromosomes. This established GTN as a direct mutagen with high cytotoxicity [64]. Genotoxicity testing of (R)‐GTN and (S)‐GTN at concentrations of 10 μM and 50 μM, using the Comet Assay, revealed that the (R)‐enantiomer induced DNA damage in both normal HB4a and cancerous MCF‐7 cells, while (S)‐GTN only affected the cancerous cells [65]. GTN also induced DNA damage in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells, leading to an increase in pH2AFX(Ser139) protein levels, a key marker of DNA damage. The presence of pH2AFX nuclear foci in the cells indicated double‐strand breaks in the DNA [66]. A significant, dose‐dependent increase in tail DNA was observed in non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) H1299 cells after treatment with GTN [67]. In Jurkat T cells, DNA damage observed 30 min after GTN treatment occurred via a topoisomerase II‐independent pathway, suggesting that oxidative stress was responsible for the genotoxicity [68]. Elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) as inducers of oxidative stress, along with a reduction in glutathione, indicated a disruption of the cellular oxidative balance in these cells. The cytotoxicity of GTN in the SKBR3 breast cancer cells was attributed to ROS induction and glutathione depletion resulting from its conjugation with GTN [55]. ROS induction, DNA double‐strand breaks, and mitochondrial membrane depolarization were identified as the primary mechanisms driving apoptosis in Ca9‐22 oral squamous cell carcinoma cells following GTN administration [69]. In MDA‐MB‐231 breast cancer cells, GTN increased the levels of hydrogen peroxide and superoxide anion radicals, leading to lipid peroxidation and membrane rupture [22, 70]. Disruption of the oxidative balance caused by GTN has also been observed in the SAOS‐2 human osteosarcoma cells and HCC cells [66, 71].

Excessive levels of ROS in cells induce oxidative modifications to cellular membranes, proteins, and DNA [72, 73]. Because GTN contains an electrophilic α,β‐unsaturated δ‐lactone moiety, its activity is likely shaped by cellular detoxification systems, particularly glutathione‐dependent redox buffering, glutathione S‐transferase (GST)‐mediated conjugation, and ATP‐binding cassette (ABC) transporter activity. Available evidence indicates that GTN can induce glutathione depletion and oxidative imbalance, whereas methoxy‐substituted derivatives may interact with P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp), supporting a role for detoxification and drug‐efflux pathways in determining GTN sensitivity, resistance, and safety‐relevant behavior. Although these mechanisms have not yet been systematically characterized for GTN, they represent important determinants of selectivity and translational potential. This, in conjunction with the primary DNA lesions induced by GTN exposure, initiates the activation of intracellular signaling cascades that regulate cellular repair processes and restore homeostasis. However, when the extent of cellular damage exceeds the reparative capacity, the cells undergo one of the death pathways to eliminate the compromised cells, thereby preventing the propagation of dysfunctional cellular components. There are three types of cell death pathways. Apoptosis and autophagy are forms of programmed cell death, occurring through a series of controlled processes within the cell, while necrosis is an accidental, uncontrolled cell death pathway [74]. Studies examining the mechanism of action of GTN in cancer cell lines and animal models demonstrate that apoptosis is the most common mechanism underlying GTN's antiproliferative activity [48, 56]. Apoptosis induced by GTN mainly occurs via the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway as confirmed at both the cellular and molecular levels. The intrinsic apoptosis pathway is triggered by internal cellular stress, such as DNA damage or oxidative stress. and involves the activation of pro‐apoptotic proteins like Bax and Bak, which cause mitochondrial membrane permeabilization [75]. This leads to the release of cytochrome c into the cytoplasm, where it forms the apoptosome. The apoptosome activates initiator caspase‐9, which subsequently activates executioner caspase‐3 and caspase‐7, leading to cell death. Cytochrome c release and caspase‐3 activation were documented in MDA‐MB‐231 breast cancer cells, while in HepG2 hepatoblastoma cells, alongside increased caspase‐3 activity, decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential and exposure of a well‐known marker of apoptosis, phosphatidylserine (PS) on the plasma membrane were observed [23, 76, 77]. The increased PS expression, reduced mitochondrial transmembrane potential, and activation of all three caspases (caspase‐9, −3, and −7), crucial in the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway, were observed in HL‐60 leukemia cells [78]. Activation of caspase‐9, −3, and −7 was also detected in H400 oral cancer cells after GTN treatment [79]. In addition to the increased activity of caspases involved in the intrinsic apoptosis pathway, elevated caspase‐8 activity was recorded in breast (MCF7), ovary (HeLa), colorectal (LoVo and HT29), hepatoma (DCC), and leukemia (HL‐60 and U937) cancer cell lines [25, 26, 66, 76, 80]. Caspase‐8 is an initiator caspase that plays a critical role in the extrinsic apoptosis pathway, where apoptosis is triggered in response to signals from death receptors on the cell surface, such as Fas (CD95) and TNF receptors [81]. The combination of TNF‐related apoptosis‐inducing ligand (TRAIL) and GTN in colorectal cancer LoVo cells enhanced both mitochondrial and death receptor‐mediated apoptosis [80]. GTN increased death receptor DR5 expression and its translocation to the cell surface, while simultaneously reducing the antiapoptotic regulator cFLIP, which inhibits caspase‐8. In this way, in LoVo cells treated with GTN, apoptosis was induced through the extrinsic apoptosis pathway, which was further confirmed by the increased activity of caspase‐8.

Apoptosis via the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway at the cellular level can be monitored by determining the levels of pro‐apoptotic proteins involved in initiating apoptosis and antiapoptotic proteins, which prevent apoptosis by inhibiting pro‐apoptotic proteins [82]. Pro‐apoptotic proteins can be divided into activator and effector proteins. Activators belong to the BH3‐only proteins group, including Noxa, PUMA, Bim, Bad, and Bid proteins [83]. They either inhibit antiapoptotic proteins or directly activate effector proteins Bax and Bak, which cause damage to the mitochondrial membrane, triggering apoptosis. Antiapoptotic proteins like Bcl‐2 and Bcl‐xL prevent apoptosis by inhibiting the action of pro‐apoptotic proteins, thereby maintaining cell survival under normal conditions. In cancer cells, the balance of these proteins is disrupted, with antiapoptotic proteins being overexpressed to promote cell survival. Treatment of various cancer cell lines with GTN, increased the levels of Bax protein, along with a simultaneous decrease in the anti‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 protein [26, 80, 84, 85]. Khaw‐On et al., 2018 further provided deeper insight into the relation of pro‐apoptotic and anti‐apoptotic proteins following the treatment of breast cancer MDA‐MB‐231 cells with GTN [70]. In these cells, an increase in pro‐apoptotic proteins such as Noxa, PUMA, Bax, Bim, and Bad was observed, while the levels of anti‐apoptotic proteins Bcl‐2 and Bcl‐xL were reduced. The expression of apoptotic‐related genes, including PMAIP/Noxa, BBC/PUMA, and BAD, was also upregulated. An increase in the pro‐apoptotic Bax protein was also detected in SKBR3 xenografts in nude mice, while a decrease in Bcl‐2 protein along with activation of caspase‐3 was observed in chemically induced hepatocellular carcinoma in male Wistar albino rats [55, 58]. In addition, the increased cytoplasmic amount of DIABOLO (Direct IAP‐Binding Protein with Low pI) protein and upregulation of the correspondent gene were detected in MDA‐MB‐231, HL‐60 and U937 cells treated with GTN [25, 70]. This protein is located in the mitochondrial intermembrane space and induces apoptosis by blocking the activity of inhibitors of apoptosis proteins (IAPs), which inhibit caspase activation [86]. A decreased concentration of another IAP, survivin, and reduced expression of its gene (baculoviral inhibitor of apoptosis repeat‐containing 5, BIRC5) following treatment with GTN was observed in lung cancer NCI‐H460 cells [87]).

The treatment of cancer cells with GTN also leads to the activation of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress response, which is triggered when there is an accumulation of misfolded or unfolded proteins [88]. Activation of this response can also lead to apoptosis. Upon administration of GTN to RT4 bladder cancer cells, a decrease in the level of the anti‐apoptotic protein Seladin‐1 was observed [89]. A dose‐dependent increase in the concentrations of cytoplasmic Ca2+ and ER‐associated proteins (GRP78 (glucose‐regulated protein 78), GADD153 (growth arrest‐ and DNA damage‐inducible gene 153), as well as expression of ER stress genes HSPA/GRP and CALR was observed in breast cancer MDA‐MB‐231 cells after treatment with GTN [70]. Although CALR expression was upregulated, the amount of the calreticulin protein did not change after GTN treatment, likely due to rapid protein degradation and a short half‐life. Calreticulin is a calcium‐binding chaperone primarily located in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) that has multiple functions in cells, including the regulation of apoptosis [90]. In HeLa cervical cancer cells, the antiproliferative activity of GTN was achieved via the induction of mitochondria‐mediated apoptosis, which was associated with ER stress‐induced activation of JNK (c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase) as a part of mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade [84]. Increases in the amount and gene expression of C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP), a critical molecule in ER stress‐induced apoptosis, as well as two other significant ER stress‐associated proteins, ERdj4 and GADD34, and the spliced XBP1 transcription factor, were observed, indicating that GTN triggered ER stress. Phosphorylation of SAPK/JNK and p38 increased in HeLa cells treated with GTN in a dose‐dependent manner, leading to inhibition of Bcl‐2 via phosphorylation, which ultimately resulted in apoptosis. GTN directly and specifically binds to peroxisomal multifunctional enzyme type 2 (MFE2) in HeLa cells, inhibiting its activity, which leads to disruptions in the peroxisomal β‐oxidation of very long‐chain fatty acids [91]. These lipid metabolism disturbances may trigger independent activation of the ER stress response which was demonstrated in HeLa cells through the increased expression of CHOP protein, other sensor transmembrane proteins such as PERK and IRE1α, and XBP1 mRNA splicing.

In addition to apoptosis, GTN treatment can also activate autophagy as a mechanism of programmed cell death. Upon examining the antiproliferative activity of (R)‐ and (S)‐enantiomers of GTN in the human renal cancer cell line 786‐O, different mechanisms of programmed cell death activation were observed [45]. Administration of (R)‐GTN resulted in a reduction of the anti‐apoptotic protein Bcl2 and led to the cleavage of PARP (Poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase), which is an initial signal for the initiation of apoptosis. On the other hand, (S)‐GTN led to a decrease in the amount of the apoptotic protein Bax and stimulated the conversion of LC3‐I to LC3‐II, suggesting that cell death occurs primarily due to autophagy. Since (S)‐GTN also caused PARP cleavage, the (S)‐enantiomer can likely induce apoptosis as well, but to a significantly lesser extent. In SK‐BR‐3 human breast cancer cells, the cytotoxic activity of GTN occurred through both apoptosis and autophagy [21]. An increased level of Bax protein relative to Bcl‐2, enhanced activity of caspase‐7 and caspase‐9, increased PARP expression, and decreased Bcl‐2 expression indicated apoptosis, while markers of autophagy, such as Atg7, Atg12‐Atg5 conjugation, and LC3II, were simultaneously increased. GTN can also induce cell death through two other forms of programmed cell death, necroptosis and anoikis, as demonstrated in MDA‐MB‐231 breast cancer cells treated with a pan‐caspase inhibitor to suppress apoptosis [22]. Necroptosis, a type of programmed cell death that shares characteristics of both necrosis and apoptosis, was triggered in MDA‐MB‐231 cells via endoplasmic reticulum and oxidative stress. This process was associated with increased expression of the rip1, rip3, and mlkl genes, which are key regulators of necroptosis. Anoikis occurs when cells detach from the extracellular matrix or their cellular surroundings [92]. Treatment with GTN in MDA‐MB‐231 cells resulted in an increased level of anoiks marker Bim‐EL, the reversal of epithelial‐mesenchymal transition protein expression, inhibition of the EGFR/FAK/Src survival signaling pathway, and decreased secretion of matrix metalloproteinases 2 and 9, all of which contributed to the induction of anoikis.

Cell cycle arrest, or the halting of the normal cell cycle, is another mechanism through which GTN exerts its anticancer activity. The normal progression of the cell cycle involves four distinct phases, during which specific cellular and molecular changes occur, leading to cell division and replication. These phases are G1 (Gap 1), S (Synthesis), G2 (Gap 2), and M (Mitosis) while G0 is the quiescent phase in the cell cycle, where cells are resting and not actively dividing. The proper transition between these phases is tightly regulated at several checkpoints. These checkpoints are controlled by complex signaling pathways, often involving proteins such as cyclins, cyclin‐dependent kinases (CDKs), and cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitor (CDKI) (Ding et al., 2020). The application of GTN in various cancer cell lines leads to cell cycle arrest at distinct checkpoints, with the most frequent arrest occurring at the G2/M checkpoint [70, 71, 84, 93, 94]. G2/M cell cycle arrest in GTN‐treated MDA‐MB‐231 breast cancer cells was associated with the degradation of CDC25C, a phosphatase that activates Cyclin B/CDK1, a key complex that enables the G2/M transition into mitosis [23]. However, cell cycle arrest can also occur at other transition points throughout the cell cycle following GTN treatment. Cell cycle arrest in MCF7 breast cancer cells occurs at the G0/G1 and G2/M checkpoints, in HeLa and H400 oral cancer cells at the S phase, and in colorectal LoVo cells and leukemia HL‐60 and U937 cells at the G1 phase [24, 25, 79, 80, 95]. Investigation of the effect of GTN enantiomers on MCF7 breast cancer cells and HB4a epithelial mammary cells revealed that (R)‐GTN leads to cell cycle arrest in the G1 phase in cancer cells and in the G2/M phase in normal HB4a cells. (S)‐GTN induced G0/G1 cell cycle arrest in both cell types [65]. The changes in the levels of cyclins, CDKs, and cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitors in both cell types after treatment with (R)‐GTN were similar, but a decrease in CCND1 and CDK2 levels, along with an increase in CDKN1C, was observed only in MCF7 cells. Treatment with GTN on hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines Huh‐7 cells, which are TP53‐mutated (Y220C) and retinoblastoma 1 (RB1)‐positive, and Hep‐3B cells, which are TP53‐ and RB1‐negative, induced G0/G1 cell cycle arrest in both cell lines [96]. Cell cycle arrest was accompanied by upregulation of CKIs and downregulation of several positive cell cycle regulators, including CDC28 protein kinase regulator subunit 1B, cyclin E1 and D1, cyclin‐dependent kinase 2 (CDK2), CDK4, CDK6, E2F transcription factor 1, and/or transcription factor Dp‐1. Additionally, GTN treatment in Huh‐7 cells stabilized the level of CDKN1B protein by degrading its specific E3 ubiquitin ligase (S‐phase kinase‐associated protein 2), while in Hep‐3B cells, GTN led to an increase in the expression and protein level of CDKN1C by acting as a histone deacetylase inhibitor. Treatment with GTN may also affect the activity of the tumor suppressor protein p53, which plays a crucial role in regulating the cell cycle, maintaining genomic stability, and preventing the formation of tumors. In breast cancer SKBR3 cells harboring the R175H mutant p53, treatment with GTN restored p53, resulting in G2/M arrest, senescence, reduced cell migration and invasion, and increased cell death [55]. An increase in the CDKI p21 after treatment with GTN was observed in SKBR3 xenografts in nude mice [55].

Another mechanism underlying the anticancer activity of GTN is its effect on signaling pathways that are crucial in the regulation of the cell cycle, differentiation, and cell proliferation. In SK‐BR‐3 breast cancer cells, GTN exerts its cytotoxic effect via the mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway [21]. Upregulation of p‐JNK1/2 (phospho‐c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase) and p‐p38 (phospho‐p38) triggered apoptosis and autophagy in SK‐BR‐3 cells, while p‐ERK1/2 (phospho‐extracellular signal‐regulated kinase) was downregulated. An increase in the levels of p‐JNK1 and p‐P38 was also detected in colorectal cancer HT29 cells and HeLa cervical cells, while in A375 melanoma cells, GTN treatment led to the upregulation of p‐ERK1/2 and c‐Jun [26, 84, 85].

The effect of GTN on the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway through the downregulation of p‐Akt (also known as protein kinase B or PKB) has been demonstrated in SK‐BR‐3 cells and A375 melanoma cells, in which the activity of another enzyme in this pathway, p‐PDK1 (3‐Phosphoinositide‐Dependent Protein Kinase‐1), was also reduced [21, 85]. Downregulation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway in cancer cells can effectively reduce cell survival, growth, and proliferation, and increase apoptosis [97]. PI3K/Akt pathway attenuation has been demonstrated in an in vivo study of hepatocellular carcinoma in male Wistar albino rats [58].

The PKC signaling pathway, essential for processes such as cell survival, migration, and gene expression, can also be targeted by GTN. Overexpression of Receptor for Activated C‐Kinase 1 (RACK‐1) and its regulator PC3N3 in both Jurkat and W7.2 T cells reduced the cytotoxic effect of GTN [98].

GTN may also affect the activity of the NF‐κB (Nuclear Factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells) protein complex, a transcription factor crucial for the expression of genes involved in immune response and inflammatory processes, cell survival, proliferation, apoptosis, and stress responses [99]. Treatment with GTN at sub‐cytotoxic concentrations in Jurkat and K562 leukemia cells resulted in NF‐κB inhibition through the inhibition of tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) and the translocation of the p50/p65 heterodimer of NF‐κB to the nucleus [100]. Additionally, GTN reduced TNF‐α‐activated interleukin‐8 (IL‐8) expression, exerting an anti‐inflammatory effect on leukemia cells. A decrease in the nuclear translocation of NF‐κB was observed in H400 oral cancer cells treated with GTN [79]. In the human renal cancer cell line 786‐0, treatment with only (R)‐GTN led to a decrease in NF‐κB activity, while the levels of Ras and PP2A were reduced upon treatment with both enantiomeric forms of GTN [45]. The inhibition of NF‐κB by GTN has also been established in in‐vivo animal studies. Decrease of NF‐κB level, along with reduced levels of TNF‐α and pro‐inflammatory interleukins IL‐1β and IL‐6, were detected in diethylnitrosamine (DEN)‐induced hepatocellular carcinoma in male Wistar albino rats [58]. The significance of the anti‐inflammatory potential of GTN in its anticancer activity has been demonstrated in preventing the development of colitis‐associated cancer and sporadic colorectal cancer in mice through the reduction of TNF‐α, pro‐inflammatory interleukins IL‐17 and IL‐6, and S100A9 protein levels [26]. Synergistic Effects of Goniothalamin With Conventional Chemotherapy, Radiotherapy, and Other Anticancer Compounds

Goniothalamin (GTN), a natural styryl lactone derived from Goniothalamus species, possesses significant anticancer potential through its ability to induce oxidative stress, DNA damage, and both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways. In various cancer models, GTN decreases intracellular glutathione (GSH) concentrations, leading to an accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondrial dysfunction, activation of the ATM/CHK signaling pathway, formation of γ‐H2AX foci, and cell‐cycle arrest at the G2/M phase, culminating in apoptosis [101]. In non‐small‐cell lung carcinoma (NCI‐H460) cells, the (S)‐enantiomer of GTN induces DNA strand breaks and decreases survivin (BIRC5) expression, linking genotoxic stress to programmed cell death [87]). In breast cancer SK‐BR‐3 cells, GTN triggers chromatin condensation, DNA fragmentation, a rise in the Bax/Bcl‐2 ratio, mitochondrial depolarization, activation of caspases 9 and 7, PARP cleavage, p38 and JNK phosphorylation, and downregulation of Akt and ERK phosphorylation. It also induces autophagy through LC3II and Atg7 upregulation [21]. These overlapping mechanisms of oxidative stress, DNA damage, and apoptosis activation complement those of conventional anticancer treatments, supporting the rationale for combination approaches.

In breast cancer SK‐BR‐3 (HER2‐positive) cells, (R)‐goniothalamin shows a highly synergistic cytotoxic interaction with cisplatin in vitro, while in xenograft models the combination significantly delays tumor growth compared to monotherapy. Mechanistically, GTN amplifies cisplatin‐induced DNA damage through oxidative stress, glutathione depletion, and p53 reactivation, resulting in increased apoptosis [55]. Similarly, in MDA‐MB‐231 triple‐negative breast cancer cells, GTN combined with cyclophosphamide, 5‐fluorouracil, paclitaxel, or vinblastine demonstrated significant synergy, enhancing apoptosis through caspase activation, while its combination with methotrexate showed only additive effects [102]. These data confirm that GTN sensitizes tumor cells to chemotherapeutic agents by lowering antioxidant defenses and enhancing apoptotic responses.

Although no direct studies have been published combining GTN with ionizing radiation, its mechanistic profile suggests a potential radiosensitizing role. By inducing oxidative stress, DNA double‐strand breaks, checkpoint kinase activation, and apoptosis, GTN could theoretically potentiate the effects of radiotherapy. Its capacity to impair DNA repair and augment apoptosis may help overcome radioresistance, a major limitation in solid tumor control. This hypothesis warrants further experimental investigation in preclinical and clinical models. Beyond conventional agents, GTN enhances the activity of bioactive anticancer compounds. In TRAIL‐resistant colorectal cancer LoVo cells, GTN restores TRAIL sensitivity by upregulating death receptor DR5 and downregulating the anti‐apoptotic protein cFLIP, resulting in enhanced activation of caspases 8 and 9 and cleavage of PARP [103]. Similarly, the combination of GTN with bioactive glass 45S5 (GTN‐BG) in MCF‐7 breast adenocarcinoma cells produces stronger growth inhibition than GTN alone. The combination increases cell‐cycle arrest at both G0/G1 and G2/M phases and elevates caspase‐8 and caspase‐9 activation, indicating dual activation of intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways [104]. Together, these studies demonstrate that GTN potentiates diverse anticancer modalities through convergent mechanisms involving oxidative stress, ER stress, DNA damage, and apoptotic signaling, underscoring its potential as an effective adjuvant molecule in multimodal cancer therapy (Table 2 and Figure 4).

Toxicity, Side Effects and Safety

Studies investigating the potential toxicological effects of GTN remain scarce and are primarily limited to a small number of in‐vivo animal models. In a study conducted by [28], 300 µg of GTN was administered daily to healthy mice over 14 days [28]. The treatment induced no significant changes in body weight, hematological indices, or biochemical parameters, thereby confirming the absence of toxic effects at the applied dose. Further evaluation of GTN's acute toxicity at higher doses (100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 mg/kg), administered intraperitoneally (IP) to male Sprague‐Dawley rats, determined that the median lethal dose (LD50) of GTN is approximately 420 mg/kg, while doses up to 200 mg/kg were well tolerated and did not induce observable toxicity [27]. Remarkably, even at the highest administered doses, no significant alterations were detected in hematological, biochemical, or histopathological parameters. The morbidities and mortalities observed at these elevated doses were likely attributable to respiratory complications triggered by GTN. Moreover, subacute administration of 42 mg/kg of GTN daily for 14 days did not result in any discernible changes in hematological and biochemical markers, nor did it cause toxic effects in major organs, including the liver, kidneys, lungs, heart, spleen, and brain of treated mice [27]. In addition, a single dose of 50 μM GTN solution, administered to female BALB/c outbred mice, did not induce any morphological alterations in hepatocytes when evaluated 4, 8, and 12 days post‐treatment [105]. Interestingly, GTN treatment activated NADPH‐d‐mediated nitric oxide (NO) signaling, which may contribute to hepatoprotective effects. Vendramini et al. assessed the antitumor activity of GTN in solid Ehrlich tumors using BALB/c mice. The study found that intraperitoneal administration of GTN at doses of 50, 100, and 300 mg/kg showed no signs of acute toxicity or weight loss in the mice after 4 h, and these effects were not observed during the subsequent 15 days [56]. General toxicity indicators such as body weight loss, changes in locomotion, behavior (agitation, lethargy), respiration, salivation, tearing eyes, cyanosis, and mortality were not observed. Additionally, the administration of GTN at a dose of 40 mg/kg for 4 weeks did not lead to toxicity or adverse effects on the organs and tissues in mice with SKBR3 xenografts [55].

Limitations and Translational Challenges

Despite the encouraging preclinical evidence, several important limitations still constrain the interpretation and clinical translation of GTN. First, most available data derive from in vitro studies performed in a limited number of cancer cell lines under heterogeneous experimental conditions, which reduces comparability across studies and limits robust assessment of therapeutic relevance. Although GTN has shown selective cytotoxicity in some models, this selectivity has not yet been comprehensively validated across a broader panel of malignant and non‐malignant cells, and its therapeutic index therefore remains insufficiently defined. In parallel, in vivo validation remains comparatively sparse. Additional well‐designed animal studies are needed to confirm antitumor efficacy, characterize safety and pharmacokinetic behavior, and assess the translational relevance of GTN under biologically and clinically meaningful conditions. Second, several mechanistic conclusions rely on single studies, particularly those involving enantiomer‐specific effects, combinational regimens, and less frequently investigated tumor types, and should therefore be interpreted cautiously until independently replicated. Some variability in reported potency, dominant cell death pathways, and comparison with standard anticancer agents further indicates that parts of the current evidence base remain fragmented and require additional validation. Consistent with this, the reproducibility of the available evidence is uneven. Several core observations, including GTN‐induced apoptosis, ROS generation, cell‐cycle arrest, and antiproliferative activity, have been reported across multiple cancer models and by different research groups, supporting their general relevance. However, other findings, especially those related to enantiomer‐specific mechanisms, combination effects, and less frequently investigated signaling pathways, remain supported by limited studies or specific experimental systems and therefore require further independent replication. Third, pharmacokinetic and bioavailability data remain scarce. The physicochemical properties of GTN, including its lipophilic structure and electrophilic α,β‐unsaturated δ‐lactone moiety, may influence its absorption, distribution, metabolic stability, and systemic exposure, but these parameters have not yet been adequately characterized in vivo. Moreover, the statistical rigor of the reviewed studies was variable, and many reports provided limited information on sample size justification, replication strategy, or the robustness of the statistical analyses used. This heterogeneity further constrains cross‐study comparisons and should be considered when interpreting the strength of the available evidence. Finally, the absence of standardized formulation strategies, long‐term toxicity studies, dose‐optimization studies, and well‐designed in vivo efficacy models further limits translational progress. Therefore, despite promising mechanistic and antiproliferative findings, GTN has not yet advanced to clinical trials, largely because its safety margin, pharmacokinetic behavior, bioavailability, and therapeutic window remain insufficiently established. Addressing these limitations through reproducible experimental designs, broader biological validation, and rigorous in vivo pharmacology, toxicology, and formulation‐oriented studies will be essential for future clinical development.

Future Prospects

Future research should focus on addressing the main gaps that currently limit the translational development of GTN. Priority areas include broader validation of selectivity in malignant and nonmalignant models, well‐designed in vivo studies of efficacy and safety, and deeper characterization of pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and long‐term toxicity. Additional work is also needed to identify direct molecular targets, expand structure–activity relationship studies, and optimize semisynthetic derivatives with improved drug‐like properties. Finally, formulation strategies and combination approaches with established anticancer agents may help enhance systemic exposure, therapeutic performance, and overall translational potential. In addition, future clinical development will also require attention to regulatory expectations, including standardized characterization of compound purity and formulation, reproducible manufacturing procedures, comprehensive preclinical safety evaluation, and robust pharmacokinetic and toxicological documentation prior to clinical testing.

Conclusion

GTN has shown promising anticancer activity across a broad range of preclinical models, supporting its value as a candidate scaffold for anticancer drug development. Its biological activity appears to involve multiple interconnected mechanisms, including oxidative stress induction, DNA damage, endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and activation of several cell death pathways. In addition, some semisynthetic derivatives have demonstrated enhanced antiproliferative activity and, in certain models, improved selectivity relative to the parent compound. Overall, the available evidence supports GTN as a mechanistically versatile natural product with relevant preclinical anticancer potential, although its current evidence base remains predominantly experimental and preclinical.
