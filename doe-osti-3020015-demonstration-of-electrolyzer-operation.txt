1. Executive Summary

This document details the execution of Cooperative Agreement DE-EE0008849, “demonstration
of electrolyzer operation at a nuclear plant to allow for dynamic participation in an organized
electricity market and in-house hydrogen supply” during the performance period of 10/1/2019 –
9/30/2024. The project was funded by the U.S. Department of Energy’s Office of Energy
Efficiency and Renewable Energy (EERE). Constellation Energy Generation, LLC (formerly
Exelon Generation Company, LLC) is the prime recipient of the award. Other members of the
project team are INL, NREL, ANL and Nel Hydrogen.
The main project objective was to demonstrate an end-to-end integrated grid-scale carbon-free H2
production, storage and utilization pilot plant at a nuclear generating facility. The project also
aimed to evaluate market opportunities and regulatory requirements related to the participation of
integrated hydrogen production and nuclear plant facilities in organized power markets, by
demonstrating dynamic control and operation of the electrolyzer and assessing the economics of
dynamic participation combined with the revenue streams from hydrogen production.
On March 7th, 2023 Constellation started hydrogen production at it’s Nine Mile Point Nuclear
Plant in Oswego, New York. The PEM electrolyzer operating at Nine Mile Point uses 1.25
megawatt of nuclear electricity to produce 560 kilograms of clean hydrogen per day, more than
enough to meet the plant’s operational hydrogen use. It will also help set the stage for possible
large-scale deployments at other clean energy centers in Constellation’s fleet that would couple
clean hydrogen production with storage and other on-site uses.
Employing the lessons learned from the 1.25 MW demonstration-scale, nuclear-powered clean
hydrogen production facility at Nine Mile Point, Constellation was a major participant in the
MachH2 hydrogen hub recently selected for up to $1 billion by the Department of Energy (DOE)
as part of the bipartisan Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act. Constellation will use a portion of
the hub funding to build the world’s largest nuclear-powered clean hydrogen production facility
at its LaSalle Clean Energy Center in Illinois.
The project was featured in a number of news articles and press releases and received 2 awards.
At the 2023 DOE HFTO’s Annual Merit Review meeting, the P.I. Dr. Uuganbayar Otgonbaatar
and project manager Robert Beaumont were recognized for “outstanding achievements in the
development and demonstration of a first-of-a-kind clean hydrogen production facility, powered
by carbon-free nuclear energy, at the Nine Mile Point Nuclear Station in Oswego, New York.”
The project was also awarded 2023 Nuclear Energy Institute’s Top Innovative Practice award.

2. Project Objectives
The proposed project will demonstrate an end-to-end integrated grid-scale carbon-free H2
production, storage and utilization pilot plant at a nuclear generating facility. It will also evaluate
market opportunities and regulatory requirements related to the participation of integrated
hydrogen production and nuclear plant facilities in organized power markets, by demonstrating
dynamic control and operation of the electrolyzer and assessing the economics of dynamic
participation combined with the revenue streams from hydrogen production. The project achieves
the following high-level objectives:
 • Successful installation and operation of an integrated hydrogen production, storage and
 utilization system at a scale of ~200 Nm3/h using a 1 MW PEM electrolysis system
 powered by carbon-free nuclear energy to supply in-house hydrogen need and to serve as
 a foundation for future scale-up.
 • Perform factor acceptance testing and demonstrate dynamic operation of a ~1 MW
 electrolyzer. These tests include (1) operating the system at steady state for 24 hours with
 observation of minimal degradation (<0.1%); (2) from steady operation at 100% power,
 suddenly cut power to 0% and then restore power and hydrogen production to 100% within
 30 minutes; and (3) verifying that the power cycling did not cause greater than 0.1%
 efficiency degradation.
 • Conduct a simulation of a scaled electrolyzer (200 or 500 MWe). Simulate a 20-cycle three-
 phase balance fault in the distribution system surrounding the installed 1 MW electrolyzer
 at a nuclear power station. Show that the system increases grid stability by reducing the
 maximum frequency deviation due to the fault by more than 50% compared to a simulation
 without an electrolyzer.
Demonstrating that hydrogen can be economically produced at large scale using curtailed or low-
cost electric power from nuclear power will advance the H2@scale concept, which is to enable
affordable and reliable large-scale hydrogen generation, transport, storage, and utilization in the
United States. Validated simulations of hybrid nuclear energy production and hydrogen generation
will identify scale-up opportunities to reduce curtailment of nuclear and renewable energy, support
grid stability and resiliency, increase energy security, and promote greater use of an under-utilized
domestic resource.

Factory Acceptance Testing for a 750-kW Nel Stack at NREL
National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) hydrogen researchers conducted two factory
acceptance tests and completed Milestone 4.1. Using a 750-kW Nel stack installed at NREL, test
results showed that a 24-hour steady-state operation at 100% power (first test) and a stop/start
operation (second test) had minimal impacts on stack efficiency/voltage degradation and
demonstrated the stability of an electrolysis system based on various stack-related parameters.
Since multiple parameters affect stack voltage, the testing strived to hold these as constant and
similar as possible, while gathering voltage measurements before and after each test, for an

accurate degradation calculation. Both an average voltage calculation over a time window and
individual voltage data points were selected where these parameters were near identical. The first
testing was conducted for more than 28 hours, and the results of this testing showed less than
0.03% of voltage degradation for four sets of single datapoints and a set of averaged datapoints
over a 10-minute time window. The second testing was performed three times, and all the
degradation percentages of this testing showed negative values. A negative degradation calculation
means that the voltage is going in the opposite direction of degradation which for the stop/start
testing, over a short period of time, means no calculated degradation. The voltage degradation
based on averaged datapoints over 5-minute time windows before and after stop/start testing was
higher than single-point comparisons. By considering the similar cathode pressure condition, the
highest degradation percentage throughout these two tests was -0.013%. Having negative
degradation percentages could mean negligible degradation during testing.
These findings verify stack voltage degradation less than 0.1% for the two factory acceptance tests,
passing the Go/No-Go criteria for Milestone 4.1. This leads to the next phase of this project which
will include dynamic controls on the electrolysis system at NREL and further verify its stability to
inform the demonstration at Constellation’s nuclear facility.
Factory acceptance was conducted at Nel on October 5-7 of 2022 and at PDC on August 8-9 of
2022. In both cases, all equipment was tested and performed according to the equipment
specifications. The safety systems, auxiliaries, and control systems were all tested and all minor
issues were fixed prior to shipment to site. Final integrated testing was completing during system
startup in February and March of 2023.

As shown in Figure 3, the FEC provides the control signals to the other systems to optimize the
hydrogen production operations. Effectively, this optimization problem is equivalent to
minimizing the cost of the electricity that is used for hydrogen production while also meeting the
hydrogen demand and without exceeding the available hydrogen storage capacity. This simple
optimization problem does not consider the costs of the electrolyzer, hydrogen storage, or
operating costs other than electricity because those costs are considered during the design phase
of the integrated system. The purpose of the FEC controller is to optimize the operation of the
PEM system in real time during operations. The capital costs of the electrolyzer and hydrogen
storage as well as non-electric operating costs of those systems can be easily incorporated into the
FEC as deemed appropriate, but determining those costs are outside the scope of the FEC
development.

Develop a model of the local electrical grid

This section summarizes work for Subtask 4.2: Develop a model of the local electrical grid. A
basic grid model of the region surrounding the Nine Mile Point Generating Station has been
developed. It is based on a synthetic grid model representing the northeast and mid-Atlantic
regions of United States available from Texas A&M University. This synthetic model has no direct
co-relation with the actual grid in that region, except that the generation and load profiles
developed for the synthetic model are similar to those of actual grid. The synthetic model is
available as a Power System Simulation for Engineering (PSS/E) file in either .raw and .dyr format.

This model must be converted to RSCAD for real time digital simulations using RTDS®
equipment at INL. In this process, the network allocation must be determined to reduce the model
to accommodate the simulation capacity of available RTDS® equipment. One NovaCor chassis
can simulate two network solutions each with not more than 100 buses. The reduced model was
selected by (1) identifying the region in the model that corresponds to western and northern New
York; (2) identifying the nuclear power generating station close to the city of Oswego; and (3)
creating a subsystem with up to 100 buses in the vicinity of the nuclear generating station that
captures the functionality, topology and characteristics of the power grid in the region of interest.

Generating Synthetic Locational Marginal Pricing (LMP) Profiles
To generate synthetic locational marginal prices (LMP), we start with historical LMP data from
2019 and use Fourier series to simulate the diurnal trends and the autoregressive moving average
(ARMA) model to simulate the random behaviors of the LMP profiles [3]. We use RAVEN to
perform our study [4]. After the synthetic time series is generated, we apply a k-means method to
group daily LMP profiles into representative clusters. The data is drawn from New York
Independent System Operator (NYISO). We select the Nine Mile Point as our proposed grid tie-
in point, which belongs to the Central Zone in NYISO. We therefore download the 2019 day-ahead
(DA) LMP prices of the Central Zone from NYISO, which are hourly data and span from January
1, 2019 to December 31, 2019.

Demand Response Participation
The US electricity grids face increasing challenges in matching power supply and demand due to
growing electricity demand and penetration of intermittent renewable generation [1]. A key
solution to the problem is to encourage demand response (DR) as demand-side participation in the
electricity market. Due to their fast response time, high ramp rate, and large capacity, PEM
electrolyzers can provide flexible power demand response (DR) to assist in matching power supply
and demand on the bulk electric grid. PEM electrolyzers can participate in DR programs with two
separate objectives:
 • Emergency demand response: rapidly reduce power consumption during grid
 contingencies to assist in maintaining grid frequency and voltage with acceptable limits.
 • Economic demand response: follow a dynamic power consumption schedule based on
 electricity markets to reduce the cost of electricity used for hydrogen production.
Section 2 and 3 present simulation methodologies and results of commercial PEM systems
providing emergency and economic demand response, respectively.

2.Simulations of Commercial PEM Systems Providing Emergency Demand Response
Frequency response under contingency conditions
The sudden loss of a generator or transmission line can instantaneously create a large imbalance
between generation and load. The power system is designed to recover from these types of events;
however, the grid frequency can deviate substantially for sudden, large imbalances.
Under normal conditions, grid frequency is tightly controlled near 60 Hz with a control band of
± 0.05 Hz. These tight tolerances are generally relaxed somewhat when contingencies occur.
Nevertheless, frequency deviation should be minimized since it can degrade load performance,
damage equipment, and interfere with system protection schemes which may ultimately lead to
system collapse [2].The magnitude of frequency deviation under contingency conditions is

dictated by the system inertia and the generation-load imbalance level. Since the system inertia is
primarily determined by the number of synchronous rotating machines connected to the system, it
is usually difficult and costly to improve. On the other hand, the generation-load imbalance level
can be alleviated by reducing the power consumption from the load side, which can be efficient
and relatively inexpensive. Such demand-side participation is called emergency demand response
and is recognized as one of the most effective ways to improve frequency response under
contingency conditions.
PEM systems for emergency demand response
Due to the transient nature of frequency response, a load participating in emergency demand
response must have fast response time and high slew rate to be able to respond to the contingencies.
Furthermore, the load must have significant capacity with respect to the grid, so that it can affect
grid frequency. Large-capacity PEM electrolysis systems can act as smart loads in the grid with
typical response times that are less than 1 s. Additionally, the capacity of the system ranges from
a few MW to hundreds of MW [3]. As a result, commercial PEM electrolysis systems are well
suited for emergency demand response participation.
Emergency Demand Response Controller Design
A commercial PEM electrolysis system participating in emergency demand response adjusts its
power consumption autonomously based on grid frequency to support balancing the grid power
consumption with power generation. Grid frequency increases if power generation exceeds the
grid load and decreases if power generation is less than the system load. In this work, a droop-
based controller is proposed that adjusts hydrogen production and corresponding power
consumption when the grid frequency deviates outside a defined deadband (db) of 60 +/- 0.05 Hz
[4]. As shown Figure 13, the proposed controller regulates the PEM electrolysis system to operate
at a nominal power 𝑃𝑛𝑜𝑚 while the grid frequency is within the deadband, such that small
frequency changes do not trigger emergency load demand responses.
When frequency drops below the deadband lower limit, the proposed controller triggers emergency
demand response by curtailing hydrogen production. Similarly, the controller increases hydrogen
production to consume power when the grid frequency increases above the upper deadband limit.
The hydrogen production power consumption set point is limited to a range defined by Pmin and
Pmax., and within that range the target power consumption change is proportional to the deviation
of the frequency from the deadband limit. As a result, the dynamic operation of the PEM system
facilitates balancing the generation-load ratio to support maintaining the grid frequency within an
established range. The amount of demand response power curtailment depends upon the starting
power consumption of the PEM system compared to its maximum rating power capacity. Both the
efficiency and lifetime of the electrochemical cells in the PEM system tend to decrease with
increasing power consumption, especially for power consumption above the nominal power
consumption capacity. For these simulations, it is assumed that the PEM systems begin at their
maximum rated power capacities, although in practice, the PEM systems may be routinely
operated at lower power, such as 90% of their nominal rated capacities, which would reduce the
amount of available power curtailment. Operating the PEM systems below their rated capacities
at start-of-life allows them to maintain constant hydrogen production over a longer time period as
the systems experience aging and degradation. It also enables the systems to increase power
consumption to respond to grid events in which power generation exceeds power demand.

A further assumption in the simulations is that during routine demand response, the power
consumptions of the PEM systems are not allowed to decrease below 20% of their nominal rating.
For these simulations, a limit is not placed on the power curtailment rates of the PEM systems,
although in practice, the power curtailment rate will likely be constrained to avoid oscillations or
other unstable power transients. In extreme emergencies, power to PEM systems can be shut off
almost instantaneously by opening a breaker or switch.

Simulation Studies
Simulation setup
Real-time simulations are performed using Real Time Digital Simulator (RTDS®) software and
hardware to validate the performance of the proposed droop-based controller. Simulation results
demonstrate that large-capacity PEM electrolysis systems with emergency demand response
participation can significantly reduce both transient and steady-state frequency deviations during
contingencies.
The IEEE 39-bus system, which is based on the renowned New England 10 generator power
system, as shown in Fig. 2, is used to perform real-time simulations. This test system has been
widely utilized to address small-signal stability in conventional power systems and fits the
purposes of the current study. The system is modified by adding PEM systems with capacities of
100, 300, or 500 MW to bus 39 (coupled to the 1,000 MW generator G1). Frequency transients
are induced in the form of N-1 generator fault contingencies by suddenly taking the 250 MW of
generation at bus 30 (G10) offline. The generator G1 is controlled with constant field voltage and
constant mechanical torque over short time scales to emulate a nuclear power station. The results
are compared with a base case that does not include a PEM system to evaluate the performances
of the proposed controller and the effectiveness of emergency demand response during

contingencies. For these simulations, the PEM systems and inertia response are the only
mechanisms that act to restore grid frequency (there is no primary or secondary frequency control).

3. Economic Demand Response
Introduction
Large-capacity PEM electrolysis systems can also participate in economic demand response by
shifting power consumption among different units to minimize the use of high-cost electricity. To
maintain the stability of the grid and without large-scale local battery energy storage, any
electricity that is being produced must be used immediately or stored. When demand is low,
electricity supply comes from relatively inexpensive base load generation, such as coal, natural
gas or nuclear power. When demand is high and base load generation is exhausted, supply comes
from relatively expensive peaking generators. Additionally, electricity supply can also come from
renewable energy sources, such as wind and solar, depending on prevailing wind speeds and solar
insolation. The relative supply of baseload generators, peaking generators and renewable energy

varies over time, such that the cost of electricity generation is not a constant. Economic demand
response can lower the average cost of electricity generation by reducing the generation from
expensive peaking plants. Time-varying electricity prices encourage utilization during periods of
relatively low cost [5], and economic demand response takes advantage of these incentives to
decrease the cost of electricity that can be used for hydrogen production as an example.
PEM systems for economic demand response
A load participating in economic demand response must follow a dynamic power command and
adjust its power consumption hourly or semi-hourly based on electricity locational market price.
To take maximum advantage of varying electricity prices, the load needs to be able to operate
efficiently over a wide power range and also have low fixed costs when not operating. Of course,
the load also needs scheduling flexibility. For hydrogen production plants, this requirement
typically means that some type of hydrogen storage is required, so that hydrogen delivery quotas
can be met even when the hydrogen production system is operating at low power.
Large-capacity PEM electrolysis systems consists of multiple PEM modules. Each PEM module
contains a plurality of PEM stacks that are arranged into a matrix and connected to each other
electrically in a serial-parallel manner. Since each PEM module can operate independently, many
options exist to meet hydrogen production targets that are below the capacity rating of the plant.

 Figure 16. PEM module and PEM system.

To ensure the optimal operation of a PEM system participating in economic demand response, the
coordination among the PEM modules within the system is critical. The coordination problem is
twofold: power allocation and switching arrangements.
 • Power allocation: the power distribution of PEM modules for a given power command.
 • Switching arrangements: the switching of PEM modules when power command changes.

Methodology
The optimal coordination problem formulated above is a mixed integer nonlinear programming
(MINLP) problem due to the nonlinear production curves 𝑓𝑖 (𝑃𝑖,𝑡 ) and the integer decision
variables 𝑢𝑖,𝑡 for switching arrangements. In general, an MINLP problem is hard to solve
efficiently, and usually must be linearized to a mixed integer linear programming (MILP) problem.
However, MILP has high computational requirements, and the complexity of the problem
increases exponentially with the increase of decision periods and number of PEM modules.
Furthermore, due to the uncertainty of load forecast and renewable generation, the dynamic power
command 𝑃𝑡DR for economic demand response changes in real-time, and only a forecast 𝑃̂𝑡DR is
available in advance:
                               𝑃̂𝑡DR = 𝑃𝑡DR + 𝜖𝑡 ,    ∀𝑡                                 (8)
where 𝜖𝑡 is the forecast error. The forecast uncertainty complicates the problem and results in sub-
optimal solutions.
Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have demonstrated promising results in learning
optimal control strategies in complex domains by deep neural network (DNN) and repeated trial-
and-error in simulated environments. This approach, referred to as deep reinforcement learning
(DRL), is capable of converging to optimal behavior in complex domains with high uncertainty,
and much of the computational expense can be conducted ‘offline’ during training, in advance of
the decision period. Consequently, this approach is adopted here to solve the optimal coordination
problem.

action. A decision-making agent acts according to a policy which maps states to actions, and the
task of DRL is to determine a policy which maximizes the long-term expected return (sum of
rewards) in the MDP [7].

Having formulated the optimal coordination problem as an MDP, the agent’s objective is to select
a sequence of actions that maximizes the expected return from the initial state. In the next section
we will describe our methodology for solving the MDP with guided tree search.
Guided tree search algorithm
The guided tree search algorithm augments a tree search algorithm with a policy trained by model-
free DRL. The policy can intelligently choose a subset of actions for tree search algorithm to
consider. This reduces the branching factor of the tree, thereby making it more tractable in large
action spaces with more PEM modules.
Tree search
A search tree is used to represent the MDP, where nodes represent states and edges represent
actions. Traversing an edge (a state–action pair) incurs a cost, which is the negative reward in the
MDP. We calculate the least cost path through the tree up to a limited depth, representing the
lookahead horizon, to save computation [8].
Once the least cost path is found from a root node at state 𝑺𝑡 up to search depth 𝐻, the first action
𝒖𝑡 is chosen for the least cost path. We then solve again for the new root at 𝑺𝑡+1 , retaining the
search tree.
Tree search minimizes the operating cost by exhaustively searching for the least cost path through
MDP. In the worst case scenario, each node in the search tree is visited exactly one time. It
therefore has a time complexity of 𝑂(2𝑁𝐻 ) for 𝑁 PEM modules. The exponential time complexity
in 𝑁 makes this method unscalable to large PEM electrolysis systems. In addition, it risks making
shortsighted decisions if there are long time dependencies due to constraints.
Guided tree search
To make the tree search tractable, we use an ‘expansion policy’ 𝜋(𝒖𝑡 |𝑺𝑡 ) to reduce the breadth of
the search tree. The policy gives a probability distribution over actions for a given state, which can
be used to intelligently reduce the branching factor of the tree.
This probability distribution represents the relative value of different actions, with high probability
actions being more promising than low probability ones. Instead of adding all available actions to
the tree from state 𝑠, we instead only add those actions which satisfy 𝜋(𝒖𝑡 |𝑺𝑡 ) ≥ 𝜌, where 0 ≤
𝜌 ≤ 1 is a branching threshold. The maximum number of nodes 𝑀 that can be added to the tree is
therefore limited to 𝑀 ≤ 1⁄𝜌, since ∑ 𝜋(𝒖𝑡 |𝑺𝑡 ) = 1. Using this expansion policy prevents the
exponential time complexity of the tree search algorithm in the number of PEM modules. We call
this method guided tree search.

Proximal policy optimization
For our research we use proximal policy optimization (PPO) [9] with entropy regularization to
learn the expansion policy 𝜋(𝒖𝑡 |𝑺𝑡 ) that is used in the guided tree search. PPO uses a clipped
objective that prevents large policy updates, which can cause large temporary performance
decreases resulting in insecure operational strategies. Entropy regularization promotes more
stochastic policies and is widely used in policy gradient RL to prevent premature convergence. In
our case it also favors policies which will propose a diverse pool of actions satisfying 𝜋(𝒖𝑡 |𝑺𝑡 ) ≥
𝜌, when used in guided tree search.
PPO is an algorithm with actor-critic learning architecture. Apart from the expansion policy
(actor), we simultaneously train a critic to learn the state-value function 𝑉 (𝑺𝑡 ) =
𝔼[∑∞ 𝑘
 𝑘=0 𝛾 𝑅𝑡+𝑘+1 ], where 𝛾<1 is the discount facto that determines the present value of future
rewards. The state-value function 𝑉 (𝑺𝑡 ) is a measure of expected total reward value of an agent in
a certain state, and using it reduces the variance of policy gradient updates [9].
The expansion policy is parametrized using a multilayer perceptron (MLP) feedforward artificial
neural network (ANN). The input layer of the policy consists of 𝑁 + 𝑇 continuous input nodes
that corresponds to the observable states (𝑺𝑡 ). The output layer of the policy consists of 𝑁 binary
output nodes that corresponds to the action space (𝒖𝑡 ). There are two hidden layers with 64 nodes
between the input layer and output layer. The critic has a similar structure to the expansion policy,
but they do not share neural network weights due to the different dimensions of the input and
output vectors.

Training setup
PEM system setup
In order to train the DRL-based controller for economic demand response, we consider a large-
capacity PEM electrolysis systems with 10 PEM modules. The production and efficiency curves
of representative PEM modules are shown in Figure 17. Each PEM module consists of 4 stacks of
50 cells, and the active surface area is 1000 cm2 . As a result, the maximum operating power of
each PEM module is around 1 MW, and the total capacity of the PEM system is 10 MW. The
optimal coordination problem comprised of 48 decision periods of 30 min (𝑇 = 48), and the
switch-on delay is chosen as 30 min (Δ𝑡 = 1).

Training and testing data
For dynamic power command 𝑃𝑡DR we use 2018-2022 renewable generation data from California
ISO (1500 days), which is publicly available. Similar optimization can be performed using data
from the New York ISO, if the necessary data is made accessible. The renewable generation data
is linearly scaled to be between 10%-100% of the total capacity of the PEM system. Furthermore,
Gaussian distributed white noise (𝜖𝑡 ~𝒩 (0, 𝜎 2 )) are added to model the forecast error 𝜖𝑡 . To test
the trained expansion policy, 20 days were randomly selected and withheld from the data set, they
are shown in Figure 22.

Training Result
The DRL environment used for training and testing is constructed in Python using the OpenAI
Gym API [10]. During training, the DRL agent samples a random day from the training data and
the initial PEM on/off time 𝜏0 are set randomly to encourage exploration of the state space. The
agent operates the PEM system ‘online’ and observes a single sample of the reward after each
action. The episode ends when all decision periods are completed.
The expansion policy is trained using PPO over 4 parallel workers, with weights of the ANN
periodically updated and synchronized [11]. The policy is trained for a total of 4 million decision
periods (time steps), which takes approximately 18 h. Figure 23 demonstrates the convergence of

 ● Milestone 10.1 (End of Project Deliverable): A report documenting a project specific
 assessment of cyber security aspects in accordance with recommendations from cyber
 security-related projects at INL in the LWRS and NEET programs.

To meet Task 10, Constellation performed a cyber security assessment of the project assets in
accordance with its Constellation’s nuclear Cyber Security Plan. The assessment was conducted
in June of 2021 by a cyber security team with expertise in conducting nuclear cyber security
evaluations. Although the assessment determined the project assets were not classified as Critical
Digital Assets per the CSP, Constellation implemented certain cybersecurity best practices to
secure the project Assets. These included:
 • Requiring passwords to access all software parameter changes
 • Coding safety system functions into the PLC directly
 • Locking all digital interface connections to the PLC
 • Air gapping the electrolyzer and compressor systems
 • Scanning all software updates for viruses prior to installation

16. Conclusion
Constellation has successfully completed the objectives of the project by installing 1.25MW PEM
electrolyzer system at it’s Nine Mile Point Nuclear Plant in Oswego, New York. The system has
started producing 560 kilograms of clean hydrogen per day on March 7th, 2023. The project set the
foundation for potential large scale hydrogen production using nuclear power. Constellation was
a major participant in the MachH2 hydrogen hub recently selected for up to $1 billion by the
Department of Energy (DOE) as part of the bipartisan Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act. The
project was recognized at the 2023 DOE HFTO’s Annual Merit Review meeting and received
2023 Nuclear Energy Institute’s Top Innovative Practice award.

2023 TIP award was given to “Demonstration of Clean Hydrogen Production at Nine Mile

Issue Solution Benefit
 • Issue: Hydrogen gas used for chemistry control and turbine generator cooling. Currently,
 hydrogen is made from fossil fuels and sold by industrial gas companies at expensive price
 due to long distance transportation
 • Solution: The project leverages $5.8M in DOE funding to produce hydrogen in-house using
 a PEM electrolyzer connected behind the meter at NMP station to supply the plant’s
 hydrogen consumption
 • Benefit: The electrolyzer saves the plant more than $800k annually in external hydrogen
 purchase cost. The project is first-of-a-kind demonstration of nuclear hydrogen production
 in the U.S. and establishes a precedent for scale-up projects
