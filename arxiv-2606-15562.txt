Quantum Corrections to Page Curve of Charged Near-AdS 2 Black Holes
Zi-Qing Xiao

Abstract
We study the evaporation and Page curve of charged near-AdS 2 black holes coupled to a non-gravitating bath at fixed temperature and chemical potential. The low-energy dynamics is governed by the Schwarzian reparametrization mode together with a U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) phase mode. We average the boundary energy and the outgoing flux over these two soft modes and obtain corrected balance equations for the temperature and chemical potential. We then use the corrected background to calculate the no-island and island entropies and the Page time shifts. We find that the two soft sectors affect the Page transition in our low-temperature semiclassical regime. The U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) phase mode correction delays the Page transition, while the Schwarzian correction tends to move it earlier. The total Page time shift is therefore determined by the competition between the Schwarzian and U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) sectors.

We then evaluate the no-island and island entropies on this quantum-corrected

relative position of the two entropy branches near their crossing.

Schwarzian mode.

Consider Euclidean AdS
2
in Poincaré coordinates,

ds^{2}=\frac{d\tau^{2}+dZ^{2}}{Z^{2}}\,.

(6)

We denote a curve to regulate the asymptotic boundary

(\tau,Z)=(F(u),Z(u)),

(7)

with

\frac{F^{\prime}(u)^{2}+Z^{\prime}(u)^{2}}{Z(u)^{2}}=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}},\qquad\Phi\big|_{\partial M}=\Phi_{b}=\frac{\phi_{r}}{\epsilon},

(8)

where

Z(u)=\epsilon F^{\prime}(u)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}),

so the boundary curve is determined by a single reparametrization function
F(u)

K=1+\epsilon^{2}\{F(u),u\}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4}),

(10)

where

\{F,u\}\equiv\frac{F^{\prime\prime\prime}(u)}{F^{\prime}(u)}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{F^{\prime\prime}(u)}{F^{\prime}(u)}\right)^{2}

(11)

is the Schwarzian derivative. Substituting (
8
) and (
10

S_{\rm GH}+S_{\rm ct}

I_{\rm Sch}[F]=-C\int du\,\{F(u),u\},\qquad C\equiv\frac{\phi_{r}}{8\pi G_{2}}.

(12)

Thus the gravitational boundary dynamics is encoded by a single reparametrization
F
. See the relevant review
49
for more details. For the black hole studied here, it is convenient to write the Schwarzian action in the standard thermal form with a circle of length
\beta

I_{\rm Sch}[F]=-C\int d\tau\,\{F(\tau),\tau\}\equiv-C\int d\tau\,\left\{\tan\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta}f(\tau)\right),\tau\right\},

(13)

with the following periodicity

f(\tau+\beta)=f(\tau)+\beta.

(14)

U(1)
phase mode.

The gauge sector behaves similarly. In two dimensions, the Maxwell equation

\nabla_{\mu}\!\bigl(Z(\Phi)F^{\mu\nu}\bigr)=0

(15)

does not describe a local propagating degree of freedom. The only physical information are a conserved electric flux and a global Wilson-line degree of freedom along the Euclidean boundary circle, i.e., a boundary holonomy. In the grand-canonical ensemble, this holonomy is conveniently described by a compact boundary phase field
\sigma(\tau)

62
;
30
, defined up to a constant
shift. Gauge invariance then implies that the phase mode can only enter through the combination

\sigma^{\prime}(\tau)-i\mu f^{\prime}(\tau)
, and its effective action is given by

I_{U(1)}[\sigma;f]=\frac{K}{2}\int d\tau\,\bigl(\sigma^{\prime}(\tau)-i\mu f^{\prime}(\tau)\bigr)^{2}.

(16)

Here
K
is the charge susceptibility (or compressibility) of the near-extremal black hole. The
phase mode is compact,

\sigma\sim\sigma+2\pi,

(17)

and therefore on the Euclidean thermal circle one must sum over winding sectors,

\sigma(\tau+\beta)=\sigma(\tau)+2\pi m,\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z}.

(18)

In order to decouple Schwarzian mode and
U(1)
phase mode, it is often convenient to remove the explicit
\mu
-dependence by the field redefinition

\tilde{\sigma}(\tau)\equiv\sigma(\tau)-i\mu f(\tau)\,.

(19)

The corresponding twisted thermal boundary condition becomes

\tilde{\sigma}(\tau+\beta)=\tilde{\sigma}(\tau)+2\pi m-i\mu\beta,\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z}.

(20)

In terms of
\tilde{\sigma}
the Euclidean boundary action becomes

I_{\rm bdy}[f,\tilde{\sigma}]=-C\int d\tau\,\left\{\tan\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta}f(\tau)\right),\tau\right\}+\frac{K}{2}\int d\tau\,\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}(\tau)^{2}.

(21)

In the following we will drop the tilde and still denote the phase mode by
\sigma
. In the Lorentzian signature, the low-energy boundary theory is therefore

I_{\rm bdy}[F,\sigma]=-C\int dt\,\left\{F(t),t\right\}+\frac{K}{2}\int dt\,\sigma^{\prime}(t)^{2},

(22)

This gives the boundary effective theory used in the following sections.

2.3
Static charged black hole solution and thermodynamic dictionary

For later use, we also describe the static charged saddle and the corresponding thermodynamic dictionary. In Euclidean signature, after the field redefinition in (
19
), the action factorizes, and the static saddle is simply

f(\tau)=\tau,\qquad\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}(\tau)=0.

(23)

Equivalently, in the original variables this corresponds to a constant chemical potential
background,

\sigma^{\prime}(\tau)=i\mu f^{\prime}(\tau)=i\mu.

(24)

After analytic continuation to Lorentzian time, the corresponding static charged black hole
is described by

F(t)=\frac{\beta}{\pi}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta}t\right),\qquad\dot{\sigma}(t)=\mu,

(25)

where
T=\beta^{-1}
is the Hawking temperature and
\mu
is the chemical potential.

The boundary reparametrization (
25
) lifts to the
two-dimensional bulk in the standard way by introducing null coordinates

X^{+}=F(u),\qquad X^{-}=F(v).

(26)

The metric is the universal near-AdS
2
metric associated with the frame
F
,

ds^{2}=-\frac{4\,F^{\prime}(u)F^{\prime}(v)}{\bigl(F(u)-F(v)\bigr)^{2}}\,du\,dv.

(27)

For the static saddle (
25
), this becomes

ds^{2}=-\frac{4(\pi T)^{2}}{\sinh^{2}\!\bigl[\pi T(u-v)\bigr]}\,du\,dv,

(28)

which is the standard AdS
2
black hole metric at Hawking temperature
T
. The associated
dilaton profile is

\Phi(u,v)=2\phi_{r}\pi T\coth\!\bigl[\pi T(u-v)\bigr],

(29)

or, equivalently, in Poincaré coordinates,

\Phi(X^{+},X^{-})=2\phi_{r}\,\frac{1-(\pi T)^{2}X^{+}X^{-}}{X^{+}-X^{-}}.

(30)

The future and past horizons are located at

u=+\infty,\qquad v=-\infty,

(31)

or equivalently at

X^{\pm}=\pm\frac{1}{\pi T}.

(32)

The metric in (
28
) and the dilaton profile in (
29
) have the same form as
in neutral JT gravity. This is because, in the low-temperature near-AdS
2
regime, the
geometry remains locally AdS
2
, while the charge sector mainly changes the relation
between
(T,\mu)
and
(E,Q)
. Away from this regime, the full charged solution does
depend on the detailed form of
Z(\Phi)
.

To extract this thermodynamic dictionary, it is convenient to pass from a single classical saddle to the grand-canonical partition function of the same low-energy theory. This use of the exact or semiclassical low-energy density of states follows the standard JT gravity description of near-AdS
2
quantum gravity
61
;
39
;
28
, now supplemented by the
U(1)
charge sectors of the boundary gauge theory
29
;
36
. Since the JT model coupled to 2d Maxwell theory can also be described by the 2d
U(1)
BF model, the charged thermal partition function takes the following form

Z(\beta,\mu)=\sum_{Q\in e\mathbb{Z}}\int_{Q^{2}/2K}^{\infty}dE\,e^{-\beta(E-\mu Q)}\sinh\!\left(2\pi\sqrt{2C}\sqrt{E-\frac{Q^{2}}{2K}}\right)\,,

(33)

up to an overall
(\beta,\mu)
independent normalization constant. In the semiclassical regime, one may approximate
\sinh x\sim\tfrac{1}{2}e^{x}
and write

Z(\beta,\mu)\sim\sum_{Q}\int dE\,\exp\!\Bigl[S(E,Q)-\beta(E-\mu Q)\Bigr],\qquad S(E,Q)=2\pi\sqrt{2C}\sqrt{E-\frac{Q^{2}}{2K}}.

(34)

The saddle point equations are therefore

\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}=\beta,\qquad\frac{\partial S}{\partial Q}=-\beta\mu,

(35)

which give

\beta=\frac{\pi\sqrt{2C}}{\sqrt{E-Q/2K}},\qquad\mu=\frac{Q}{K}.

(36)

It is therefore natural to identify the thermodynamic variables through

E(T,\mu)=2\pi^{2}CT^{2}+\frac{K\mu^{2}}{2},\qquad Q(T,\mu)=K\mu.

(37)

Equivalently, one can express
E
in terms of
Q
as

E(T,Q)=2\pi^{2}CT^{2}+\frac{Q^{2}}{2K},\qquad T=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{2C}}\sqrt{E-\frac{Q^{2}}{2K}}.

(38)

The Bekenstein–Hawking entropy then follows from the first law,

dE=TdS_{\rm BH}+\mu dQ,

(39)

which yields

S_{\rm BH}(T,\mu)=4\pi^{2}C\,T,

(40)

or, equivalently,

S_{\rm BH}(E,Q)=2\pi\sqrt{2C}\sqrt{E-\frac{Q^{2}}{2K}}.

(41)

In particular, the extremal limit is given by

E=\frac{Q^{2}}{2K},\qquad T=0.

(42)

Two conditions should be distinguished here. The first is the low-temperature, or near-extremal regime,

TL\ll 1.

(43)

The second is the validity of the semiclassical saddle, which requires

S_{\rm BH}\gg 1.

(44)

Using (
40
), this becomes

4\pi^{2}CT\gg 1.

(45)

Hence, we will work in the overlap region

TL\ll 1,\qquad 4\pi^{2}CT\gg 1.

(46)

This completes the thermodynamic dictionary of the black hole side. We next turn to the real-time evaporation process.

3
Evaporation dynamics of the charged black hole

3.1
Matter fluxes and classical balance equations

Let us now study the real time evaporation. The black hole exchanges energy and charge with the bath through transparent boundary conditions at the interface
u=v=t
. To obtain the balance equations, it is not necessary to know the full bulk solution. We only need the renormalized chiral one-point functions of the charged matter fields in the background fixed by the boundary modes
(F,\sigma)
.

For the charged scalar introduced in section
2
, the reparametrization mode determines the
chiral coordinates through

X^{+}=F(u),\qquad X^{-}=F(v)\,,

(47)

while boundary phase mode gives the gauge dressing of charged operators. After the normal ordering defined with respect to the Poincaré vacuum, the renormalized outgoing one-point functions are
3
3

3

These expressions follow from the standard conformal transformation of the chiral stress tensor and the gauge dressing of charged matter
operators. A more explicit point-splitting derivation can be found in section 5 of

.

\langle:T_{uu}(t):\rangle_{\rm out}=-\frac{c}{24\pi}\{F(t),t\}+\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\dot{\sigma}(t)^{2},\qquad\langle:J_{u}(t):\rangle_{\rm out}=\frac{q^{2}}{2\pi}\dot{\sigma}(t).

(48)

The same expressions hold in the
v
sector. In the net flux through the interface, the
frame-dependent and gauge-dependent inhomogeneous terms cancel between the two chiral
components. The balance equation can therefore be written directly in terms of the
normal-ordered fluxes,

\langle T_{vv}(t)\rangle-\langle T_{uu}(t)\rangle=\langle:T_{vv}(t):\rangle-\langle:T_{uu}(t):\rangle,

(49)

and similarly for the charge current.

The black hole energy and charge in the classical boundary theory are

E_{\rm BH}(t)=-C\{F(t),t\}+\frac{K}{2}\dot{\sigma}(t)^{2},\qquad Q_{\rm BH}(t)=K\dot{\sigma}(t).

(50)

For the static saddle

F(t)=\frac{\beta}{\pi}\tanh\frac{\pi t}{\beta},\qquad\dot{\sigma}(t)=\mu,

(51)

this reproduces the thermodynamic dictionary

E_{\rm BH}=2\pi^{2}CT^{2}+\frac{K}{2}\mu^{2},\qquad Q_{\rm BH}=K\mu,\qquad T=\beta^{-1}.

(52)

The bath is kept in a stationary grand-canonical state with temperature

T_{b}=\beta_{b}^{-1}
and chemical potential
\mu_{b}
. Its incoming one-point
functions at the interface are

\langle:T_{vv}(t):\rangle_{\rm in}=\frac{\pi c}{12}T_{b}^{2}+\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\mu_{b}^{2},\qquad\langle:J_{v}(t):\rangle_{\rm in}=\frac{q^{2}}{2\pi}\mu_{b}.

(53)

Transparent boundary conditions then give the classical balance laws

\frac{dE_{\rm BH}}{dt}=\langle:T_{vv}(t):\rangle_{\rm in}-\langle:T_{uu}(t):\rangle_{\rm out},\qquad\frac{dQ_{\rm BH}}{dt}=\langle:J_{v}(t):\rangle_{\rm in}-\langle:J_{u}(t):\rangle_{\rm out}.

(54)

Using (
48
) and
(
53
), we obtain the classical balance equations with fixed
(F,\sigma)
background:

\frac{d}{dt}\left[-C\{F(t),t\}+\frac{K}{2}\dot{\sigma}(t)^{2}\right]=\frac{\pi c}{12}T_{b}^{2}+\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\mu_{b}^{2}+\frac{c}{24\pi}\{F(t),t\}-\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\dot{\sigma}(t)^{2},

(55)

and

K\ddot{\sigma}(t)=-\frac{q^{2}}{2\pi}\left(\dot{\sigma}(t)-\mu_{b}\right).

(56)

It is convenient to rewrite these equations in terms of the effective temperature
and chemical potential of the black hole,

\{F(t),t\}=-2\pi^{2}T(t)^{2},\qquad\mu(t)\equiv\dot{\sigma}(t).

(57)

The balance laws then become

\frac{d}{dt}\left(2\pi^{2}CT(t)^{2}+\frac{K}{2}\mu(t)^{2}\right)=-\frac{\pi c}{12}\left(T(t)^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\right)-\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\left(\mu(t)^{2}-\mu_{b}^{2}\right),

(58)

and

K\dot{\mu}(t)=-\frac{q^{2}}{2\pi}\left(\mu(t)-\mu_{b}\right).

(59)

These coupled first order equations can be solved explicitly. Let us denote the initial data when the black hole is put in contact with the bath

T(0)=T_{0},\qquad\mu(0)=\mu_{0}.

(60)

The charge equation is solved by

\mu(t)=\mu_{b}+(\mu_{0}-\mu_{b})e^{-\gamma_{Q}t},\qquad\gamma_{Q}\equiv\frac{q^{2}}{2\pi K}.

(61)

Equivalently,

Q_{\rm BH}(t)=K\mu_{b}+\bigl(Q_{\rm BH}(0)-K\mu_{b}\bigr)e^{-\gamma_{Q}t}.

(62)

We interpret
\gamma_{Q}
as the relaxation rate of the charge sector. It is convenient to eliminate
\dot{\mu}(t)
and rewrite the energy equation as a closed equation for
T(t)^{2}
. Taking the time derivative in (
58

2\pi^{2}C\,\frac{d}{dt}T(t)^{2}+K\mu(t)\dot{\mu}(t)=-\frac{\pi c}{12}\bigl(T(t)^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\bigr)-\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\bigl(\mu(t)^{2}-\mu_{b}^{2}\bigr).

(63)

Using (
59
) to eliminate
K\dot{\mu}(t)
, one finds a closed equation for
T(t)^{2}

2\pi^{2}C\,\frac{d}{dt}T(t)^{2}=-\frac{\pi c}{12}\bigl(T(t)^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\bigr)+\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\bigl(\mu(t)-\mu_{b}\bigr)^{2}.

(64)

Substituting (
61
) into (
64
), and defining

\delta T^{2}(t)\equiv T(t)^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\,,\qquad\delta\mu_{0}\equiv\mu_{0}-\mu_{b},

(65)

we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\delta T^{2}(t)+k\,\delta T^{2}(t)=\frac{q^{2}}{8\pi^{3}C}\,\delta\mu_{0}^{\,2}\,e^{-2\gamma_{Q}t}\,,\qquad k\equiv\frac{c}{24\pi C}\,.

(66)

For the generic case
k\neq 2\gamma_{Q}

T(t)^{2}=T_{b}^{2}+\left[T_{0}^{2}-T_{b}^{2}-\frac{q^{2}\,\delta\mu_{0}^{\,2}}{8\pi^{3}C\,(k-2\gamma_{Q})}\right]e^{-kt}+\frac{q^{2}\,\delta\mu_{0}^{\,2}}{8\pi^{3}C\,(k-2\gamma_{Q})}\,e^{-2\gamma_{Q}t}.

(67)

Here

is the evaporation rate in the neutral case. For the resonant case

T(t)^{2}=T_{b}^{2}+\bigl(T_{0}^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\bigr)e^{-kt}+\frac{q^{2}\,\delta\mu_{0}^{\,2}}{8\pi^{3}C}\,t\,e^{-kt}.

(68)

•

\mu_{0}=\mu_{b}

T(t)^{2}-T_{b}^{2}=\bigl(T_{0}^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\bigr)e^{-kt}.

(69)

Thus, at the classical level, the charge sector affects the energy evolution only through the chemical potential mismatch
\mu(t)-\mu_{b}
.

•

\mu_{0}-\mu_{b}\neq 0

For nonzero chemical potential mismatch, the charge sector can change the temperature evolution. This is obvious in the right hand side of classical equation (
64
). To see this more clearly, let

Y(t)\equiv T(t)^{2},\qquad B\equiv T_{0}^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\,,

(70)

and define

A\equiv\frac{q^{2}(\mu_{0}-\mu_{b})^{2}}{8\pi^{3}C},\qquad k\equiv\frac{c}{24\pi C}.

(71)

Then the classical temperature equation can be written as

\dot{Y}+k(Y-T_{b}^{2})=Ae^{-2\gamma_{Q}t}.

(72)

Here we already substitute the charge solution (
61
). The positive source term on the right hand side can temporarily increase the near-extremal thermal energy
2\pi^{2}CT^{2}
, even while the total black hole energy is decreasing.

In particular, under our black hole evaporation setup
T_{0}>T_{b}
, the temperature can initially increases rather than decreases if

A>kB,

(73)

which means

\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}(\mu_{0}-\mu_{b})^{2}>\frac{\pi c}{12}(T_{0}^{2}-T_{b}^{2}).

(74)

When this condition is satisfied, there is a unique turning time
t_{M}
at which

\dot{T}(t_{M})=0
. For
k\neq 2\gamma_{Q}
, it is given by

t_{M}=\frac{1}{k-2\gamma_{Q}}\log\left[\frac{k\bigl(A-(k-2\gamma_{Q})B\bigr)}{2\gamma_{Q}A}\right].

(75)

In the resonant case
k=2\gamma_{Q}
, the corresponding expression is

t_{M}=\frac{A-kB}{kA}.

(76)

Thus, for
0<t<t_{M}
, the temperature monotonically increases , while for
t>t_{M}
the temperature monotonically decreases toward the bath temperature
T_{b}
.

•

\gamma_{Q}\gg k

In this parameter regime, the chemical potential equilibrates rapidly. Once this fast charge relaxation stage is over, the temperature approximately follows the neutral relaxation law:

T(t)^{2}\simeq T_{b}^{2}+\bigl(T_{0}^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\bigr)e^{-kt},\qquad t\gg\gamma_{Q}^{-1}.

(77)

•

T_{b}=0\,,\mu_{b}=0

For this purely absorbing bath, the classical solution reduces to

\mu(t)=\mu_{0}e^{-\gamma_{Q}t},

(78)

and for
k\neq 2\gamma_{Q}

T(t)^{2}=\left[T_{0}^{2}-\frac{q^{2}\mu_{0}^{2}}{8\pi^{3}C\,(k-2\gamma_{Q})}\right]e^{-kt}+\frac{q^{2}\mu_{0}^{2}}{8\pi^{3}C\,(k-2\gamma_{Q})}\,e^{-2\gamma_{Q}t}\,.

(79)

It should be emphasized that the late-time limit of the classical solution must be treated with care. The thermodynamic dictionary used in this paper relies on the semiclassical near-extremal black hole saddle, whose regime of validity requires

4\pi^{2}C\,T(t)\gg 1,\qquad T(t)\ll 1.

(80)

Therefore, although the purely absorbing bath solution formally drives
T(t)
towards zero, the effective description we used here should only be trusted while the evolution remains inside this overlap regime. Once

T(t)\sim\mathcal{O}(C^{-1}),

(81)

the black hole entropy is no longer parametrically large, and the present semiclassical calculation is no longer quantitatively controlled. For this reason, the solution should be understood as an effective description of the near-extremal region not a controlled way to exact extremal region.

3.2
Quantum-corrected balance equations

The next step is to include the quantum fluctuations of the two boundary soft modes. We work in the adiabatic regime. At each time the black hole is described by an effective temperature and an effective chemical potential. The classical balance equations (
58
) (
59
) are promoted to expectation-value equations by replacing the classical boundary energy and fluxes with their averages over the soft modes. We take the resulting expectation-value equations as the quantum-corrected balance equations. The corresponding quantum-corrected energy is obtained from the exact soft-mode partition function,

E_{\rm BH}^{\rm q}(\beta,\mu)=-\partial_{\beta}\log Z_{\rm soft}(\beta,\mu)+\mu\,\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{\mu}\log Z_{\rm soft}(\beta,\mu)\,.

(82)

Since the Schwarzian mode and the
U(1)
phase mode are decoupled from each other, we can write

Z_{\rm soft}(\beta,\mu)=Z_{\rm Sch}(\beta)\,Z_{U(1)}(\beta,\mu)\,,

(83)

and

E_{\rm BH}^{\rm q}(\beta,\mu)=E_{\rm Sch}(\beta)+E_{U(1)}(\beta,\mu)\,.

(84)

For the Schwarzian mode, the one-loop exact partition function
63
;
49
is given by

Z_{\rm Sch}(\beta)=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{2\pi C}{\beta}\right)^{3/2}\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi^{2}C}{\beta}\right),

(85)

Using

E_{\rm Sch}(\beta)=-\partial_{\beta}\log Z_{\rm Sch}(\beta),

(86)

one obtains

E_{\rm Sch}(\beta)=\frac{2\pi^{2}C}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{3}{2\beta}=2\pi^{2}CT^{2}+\frac{3}{2}T.

(87)

For the
U(1)
phase mode, the partition function
62
;
30
is given by

Z_{U(1)}(\beta,\mu)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\!\left[-\beta\left(\frac{n^{2}}{2K}-\mu n\right)\right].

(88)

In the regime
\beta\ll K
, or equivalently
T\gg K^{-1}
, Poisson resummation gives

\log Z_{U(1)}(\beta,\mu)=\frac{\beta K\mu^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log\!\left(\frac{2\pi K}{\beta}\right)+O\!\left(e^{-2\pi^{2}K/\beta}\right).

(89)

From the grand canonical thermodynamic relations

Q_{U(1)}=\frac{1}{\beta}\partial_{\mu}\log Z_{U(1)}=K\mu,

(90)

and

E_{U(1)}(\beta,\mu)=-\partial_{\beta}\log Z_{U(1)}+\mu Q_{U(1)},

(91)

we obtain

E_{U(1)}(\beta,\mu)=\frac{K}{2}\mu^{2}+\frac{1}{2\beta}=\frac{K}{2}\mu^{2}+\frac{1}{2}T.

(92)

Combining (
87
) and (
92
), we obtain

E_{\rm BH}^{\rm q}(T,\mu)=2\pi^{2}CT^{2}+\frac{3}{2}T+\frac{K}{2}\left(\mu^{2}+\frac{T}{K}\right)=2\pi^{2}CT^{2}+2T+\frac{K}{2}\mu^{2}.

(93)

Compared with the classical result, the quantum-corrected energy contains an additional
term linear in
T
. The Schwarzian sector contributes
\frac{3}{2}T
, while the
U(1)

sector contributes
\frac{1}{2}T
.

We now turn to the fluxes. In subsection
3.1
, the outgoing one-point functions were evaluated in a fixed background
(F,\sigma)
. The quantum-corrected fluxes are obtained by taking the soft-mode average of the same observables. In particular, the outgoing energy flux is

\langle{:}T_{uu}(t){:}\rangle_{\rm out}^{\rm q}=-\frac{c}{24\pi}\,\langle\{F(t),t\}\rangle+\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\,\langle\dot{\sigma}(t)^{2}\rangle.

(94)

We now evaluate the two expectation values separately.

For the Schwarzian part, the exact thermal energy of the reparametrization mode is

E_{\rm Sch}(T)=-C\,\langle\{F(t),t\}\rangle=2\pi^{2}CT^{2}+\frac{3}{2}T.

(95)

Therefore,

\langle\{F(t),t\}\rangle=-2\pi^{2}T(t)^{2}-\frac{3}{2C}T(t),

(96)

and hence

-\frac{c}{24\pi}\,\langle\{F(t),t\}\rangle=\frac{\pi c}{12}T(t)^{2}+\frac{c}{16\pi C}T(t).

(97)

For the
U(1)
sector, the outgoing flux depends on
\dot{\sigma}^{2}
. In the quantum theory,
the phase mode is treated as a thermal rotor. Writing the charge as

Q=K\dot{\sigma},

(98)

one has

\langle\dot{\sigma}(t)\rangle=\mu(t),\qquad\langle Q\rangle=K\mu(t).

(99)

Moreover, in the grand-canonical ensemble the charge fluctuations are characterized by

\mathrm{Var}(Q)=KT,

(100)

so that

\langle Q^{2}\rangle=\langle Q\rangle^{2}+\mathrm{Var}(Q)=K^{2}\mu(t)^{2}+KT(t).

(101)

Dividing by
K^{2}
, we obtain

\langle\dot{\sigma}(t)^{2}\rangle=\mu(t)^{2}+\frac{T(t)}{K}.

(102)

Therefore the
U(1)
contribution to the outgoing flux is

\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\,\langle\dot{\sigma}(t)^{2}\rangle=\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\left(\mu(t)^{2}+\frac{T(t)}{K}\right).

(103)

Combining (
97
) and (
103
), we finally arrive at

\langle{:}T_{uu}(t){:}\rangle_{\rm out}^{\rm q}=\frac{\pi c}{12}T(t)^{2}+\frac{c}{16\pi C}T(t)+\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\left(\mu(t)^{2}+\frac{T(t)}{K}\right).

(104)

This is the quantum-corrected version of the classical outgoing flux. Since the current is linear in
\dot{\sigma}
already at the classical level, its quantum-corrected form is obtained simply by replacing
\dot{\sigma}(t)
with its expectation value
\mu(t)
, namely

\langle{:}J_{u}(t){:}\rangle_{\rm out}^{\rm q}=\frac{q^{2}}{2\pi}\mu(t).

(105)

The first term in (
104
) is the classical outgoing energy flux, the second is the Schwarzian correction, and the last term comes from the
U(1)
sector after replacing the classical quantity
\dot{\sigma}^{2}
by its quantum average,

\langle\dot{\sigma}^{2}\rangle=\mu^{2}+\frac{T}{K}.

(106)

For the bath, we keep the same grand-canonical state with fixed
(T_{b},\mu_{b})
. Therefore the incoming fluxes are

\langle{:}T_{vv}(t){:}\rangle_{\rm in}^{\rm q}=\frac{\pi c}{12}T_{b}^{2}+\frac{c}{16\pi C}T_{b}+\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\left(\mu_{b}^{2}+\frac{T_{b}}{K}\right),

(107)

and

\langle{:}J_{v}(t){:}\rangle_{\rm in}^{\rm q}=\frac{q^{2}}{2\pi}\mu_{b}.

(108)

We now impose the same balance laws as before, but with the quantum-corrected quantities:

\frac{d}{dt}E_{\rm BH}^{\rm q}(t)=\langle{:}T_{vv}(t){:}\rangle_{\rm in}^{\rm q}-\langle{:}T_{uu}(t){:}\rangle_{\rm out}^{\rm q},

(109)

\frac{d}{dt}Q_{\rm BH}^{\rm q}(t)=\langle{:}J_{v}(t){:}\rangle_{\rm in}^{\rm q}-\langle{:}J_{u}(t){:}\rangle_{\rm out}^{\rm q}.

(110)

Substituting (
93
)–(
108
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(2\pi^{2}CT(t)^{2}+2T(t)+\frac{K}{2}\mu(t)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi c}{12}\bigl(T(t)^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\bigr)-\frac{c}{16\pi C}\bigl(T(t)-T_{b}\bigr)

(111)

\displaystyle-\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\left[\mu(t)^{2}-\mu_{b}^{2}+\frac{T(t)-T_{b}}{K}\right].

together with

K\dot{\mu}(t)=-\frac{q^{2}}{2\pi}\bigl(\mu(t)-\mu_{b}\bigr).

(112)

Equation (
111
) is the quantum-corrected energy balance equation that replaces the equation (
58
) in the last subsection. The structure here is obvious: the balance law is still valid in form, but the black hole energy and the energy flux are now replaced by their quantum soft mode averages.

We also rewrite (
111
) as an equation for
T(t)
alone. Expanding the time derivative gives

\displaystyle\bigl(4\pi^{2}CT(t)+2\bigr)\dot{T}(t)+K\mu(t)\dot{\mu}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi c}{12}\bigl(T(t)^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\bigr)-\frac{c}{16\pi C}\bigl(T(t)-T_{b}\bigr)

(113)

\displaystyle-\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\left[\mu(t)^{2}-\mu_{b}^{2}+\frac{T(t)-T_{b}}{K}\right].

Using (
112
) to eliminate
\dot{\mu}(t)
, one finds

\bigl(4\pi^{2}CT(t)+2\bigr)\dot{T}(t)=-\frac{\pi c}{12}\bigl(T(t)^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\bigr)-\frac{c}{16\pi C}\bigl(T(t)-T_{b}\bigr)+\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\bigl(\mu(t)-\mu_{b}\bigr)^{2}-\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi K}\bigl(T(t)-T_{b}\bigr).

(114)

The matter sector is still the same quantum matter sector as before. However, the boundary soft modes are no longer treated as a fixed classical background. Once these modes are quantized, they modify both the black hole energy and the outgoing flux, leading to the
1/C
and
1/K
corrections that appear in the balance equation.

3.3
Several physical limits and regimes of the quantum-corrected balance equations

Now we discuss the physical meaning of the corrected equations. Compared with the classical evolution, there are two changes. First, the black hole energy has an additional linear temperature term,

E_{\rm BH}^{q}(T,\mu)=2\pi^{2}CT^{2}+2T+\frac{K}{2}\mu^{2},

(115)

Second, the outgoing energy flux also has new linear temperature terms,

\frac{c}{16\pi C}(T-T_{b}),\qquad\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi K}(T-T_{b}),

(116)

For the late time analysis below, the fixed point is assumed to lie in the controlled window

T_{b}L\ll 1,\qquad 4\pi^{2}CT_{b}\gg 1,\qquad KT_{b}\gg 1\,.

(117)

Here the first two inequalities are the overlap region introduced in subsection
2.3
, while the last one is the regime in which the grand canonical
U(1)
rotor admits the high temperature expansion used in subsection
3.2
.

The corrected equations cannot be solved analytically in a closed form for general parameters. Nevertheless, two useful descriptions are available. First, the asymptotic late time behavior to the bath fixed point can be obtained directly by linearizing the balance equations. Second, for the later Page time analysis, we need a controlled approximation. In our paper’s controlled window, this can be obtained by expanding the temperature profile around the classical solution in powers of
1/(CT)
and
1/(KT)
. The details of deriving this series solution are given in appendix
A
and we only use its final form in the following. We first consider the late-time near-equilibrium regime. To study the asymptotic approach to equilibrium, we write

T(t)=T_{b}+\delta T(t),\qquad\mu(t)=\mu_{b}+\delta\mu(t).

(118)

The charge equation gives

\dot{\delta\mu}=-\frac{1}{\tau_{Q}}\delta\mu,\qquad\tau_{Q}\equiv\frac{2\pi K}{q^{2}},

(119)

while the energy equation takes the form

(4\pi^{2}CT_{b}+2)\dot{\delta T}+K\mu_{b}\dot{\delta\mu}=-\left(\frac{\pi c}{6}T_{b}+\frac{c}{16\pi C}+\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi K}\right)\delta T-\frac{q^{2}}{2\pi}\mu_{b}\,\delta\mu.

(120)

Using (
119
), the
\delta\mu
-dependent terms cancel exactly, and the
temperature and chemical potential decouple already at linear order:

\dot{\delta T}=-\Gamma_{T}\,\delta T,\qquad\Gamma_{T}=\frac{\frac{\pi c}{6}T_{b}+\Lambda}{4\pi^{2}CT_{b}+2},\qquad\Lambda\equiv\frac{c}{16\pi C}+\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi K}.

(121)

Therefore the late-time behavior are given by

\delta T(t)\propto e^{-\Gamma_{T}t},\qquad\delta\mu(t)\propto e^{-t/\tau_{Q}}.

(122)

The formula for
\Gamma_{T}
makes the two quantum effects explicit. The
coefficient
\Lambda
comes from the new linear dissipation channels in the
energy flux, while the extra
+2
in the denominator comes from the
linear-
T
correction to the black hole energy. Hence the late-time
thermal relaxation is not controlled only by the classical quadratic channel; it is modified both by the quantum-corrected flux and by the quantum-corrected heat capacity.

The discussion above concerns the asymptotic approach to the bath fixed point. There is
another effect inherited from the charged classical dynamics, namely the transient
charge-driven growth discussed in subsection
3.1
. This effect is already
present at the classical level. Quantum corrections do not create it, but they shift the
condition under which it occurs.

After eliminating
\dot{\mu}
, the quantum-corrected temperature equation becomes

(4\pi^{2}CT+2)\dot{T}=-\frac{\pi c}{12}(T^{2}-T_{b}^{2})-\Lambda(T-T_{b})+\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}(\mu-\mu_{b})^{2},\qquad\Lambda\equiv\frac{c}{16\pi C}+\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi K}.

(123)

The last term is the same positive charge-mismatch source as in the classical theory,
whereas the term proportional to
\Lambda
is a new quantum linear dissipation channel. For the black hole evaporation setup
T_{0}>T_{b}
, the initial temperature increases only if

\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}(\mu_{0}-\mu_{b})^{2}>\frac{\pi c}{12}(T_{0}^{2}-T_{b}^{2})+\Lambda(T_{0}-T_{b}).

(124)

Compared with the classical condition, the linear dissipation term raises the threshold for
transient heating. If this condition is satisfied, the quantum-corrected turning time
t_{M}^{q}
is defined by

\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\left(\mu(t_{M}^{q})-\mu_{b}\right)^{2}=\frac{\pi c}{12}\left[T(t_{M}^{q})^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\right]+\Lambda\left[T(t_{M}^{q})-T_{b}\right].

(125)

The above argument separates three different regimes. If the chemical potential mismatch vanishes, the charge source in (
123
) is absent and the
black hole simply cools toward the bath. If the mismatch is nonzero but still below
the threshold (
124
), the temperature again decreases monotonically, although the
quantum-corrected temperature trajectory is shifted relative to the classical one. Finally, if the
mismatch is large enough to satisfy (
124
), the temperature first increases
and then it relaxes to the bath later. These three possibilities are illustrated in
figure

.

Page curve on the quantum-corrected evaporating background

Here we study the Page curve on the quantum-corrected evaporating background. We do not modify the island prescription itself. What we want to know is how the corrected real time evaporation law changes the competition between the no-island saddle and the island saddle. We therefore work in the standard black hole+bath setup, shown schematically in figure

There is one subtle point before writing the entropy formulas. The balance equation (

\{F_{\rm raw}(t),t\}=-2\pi^{2}T(t)^{2}-\frac{3}{2C}T(t)\,.

If this raw map were used directly in the entropy formula, the late-time bath fixed point would not reduce to the standard thermal map in the desired way. For this reason we define an effective boundary trajectory
\hat{F}(t)
by subtracting the static bath fixed-point shift from the one-loop Schwarzian part ,

\{\hat{F}(t),t\}=-2\pi^{2}T(t)^{2}-\frac{3}{2C}\bigl(T(t)-T_{b}\bigr)\,.

(128)

With this definition, when the system relaxes to the bath, the right-hand side of
(
128
) reduces to the standard thermal Schwarzian
-2\pi^{2}T_{b}^{2}
.

S_{\rm no}(t)\equiv S_{\rm CFT}(D)=\frac{c}{3}\log\!\left[\frac{\beta_{b}}{\pi}\cosh\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta_{b}}\bigl(t+\hat{\eta}(t)\bigr)\right)\right]-\frac{c}{6}\log\hat{\eta}^{\prime}(t).

This is the standard no-island result evaluated on the effective
evaporating background. We next turn to the island saddle.

Let

p_{3}=(u_{a},v_{a})

(140)

be the right QES point in the gravitating region. Its left reflected
image is denoted by
p_{4}
and will not be written explicitly. The dilaton entering the geometric term should be evaluated on the same effective background as the no island entropy. Before defining the effective
dilation from the effective boundary reparametrization
F_{\rm eff}

As reviewed in subsection

F_{0}(t)=\frac{\beta}{\pi}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\pi t}{\beta}\right),\qquad X^{+}=F_{0}(u),\quad X^{-}=F_{0}(v),

(141)

and the corresponding dilaton profile can be written in Poincaré coordinates as

\Phi(X^{+},X^{-})=2\phi_{r}\,\frac{1-(\pi T)^{2}X^{+}X^{-}}{X^{+}-X^{-}},\qquad T=\beta^{-1}.

(142)

Writing this in the
(u,v)
frame gives

\Phi_{F_{0}}(u,v)=2\phi_{r}\,\frac{1-(\pi T)^{2}F_{0}(u)F_{0}(v)}{F_{0}(u)-F_{0}(v)}.

(143)

Now use the following identities

F_{0}^{\prime}(x)=1-(\pi T)^{2}F_{0}(x)^{2},\qquad\frac{1}{2}\frac{F_{0}^{\prime\prime}(x)}{F_{0}^{\prime}(x)}=-(\pi T)^{2}F_{0}(x).

(144)

Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{F_{0}^{\prime\prime}(v)}{F_{0}^{\prime}(v)}+\frac{F_{0}^{\prime}(v)}{F_{0}(u)-F_{0}(v)}

\displaystyle=-(\pi T)^{2}F_{0}(v)+\frac{1-(\pi T)^{2}F_{0}(v)^{2}}{F_{0}(u)-F_{0}(v)}

(145)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-(\pi T)^{2}F_{0}(u)F_{0}(v)}{F_{0}(u)-F_{0}(v)}.

This motivates the following bulk reconstruction formula

\Phi_{F}(u,v)=2\phi_{r}\left[\frac{1}{2}\frac{F^{\prime\prime}(v)}{F^{\prime}(v)}+\frac{F^{\prime}(v)}{F(u)-F(v)}\right].

(146)

In the low-temperature regime considered here, we use the same reconstruction formula for the effective dilaton, with the classical boundary trajectory replaced by the effective one.

\Phi_{\hat{F}}(u,v)=2\phi_{r}\left[\frac{1}{2}\frac{\hat{F}^{\prime\prime}(v)}{\hat{F}^{\prime}(v)}+\frac{\hat{F}^{\prime}(v)}{\hat{F}(u)-\hat{F}(v)}\right].

(147)

The role of the
U(1)
sector is to modify the boundary thermodynamic relation and hence the
F_{\rm eff}
, rather than to change the relation (
146
) between
\Phi
and
F
itself.

In the late-time factorization channel, the island saddle which involves four-point twist
correlator reduces to two identical boundary–QES two-point

S_{\rm isl}(t)=\underset{p_{3}}{\rm Ext}\left[\frac{\Phi_{0}+\Phi_{\hat{F}}(p_{3})}{2G_{2}}+\frac{c}{3}\log\!\left(-\frac{(w^{+}_{p_{1}}-w^{+}_{p_{3}})(w^{-}_{p_{1}}-w^{-}_{p_{3}})}{\Omega_{p_{1}}\Omega_{p_{3}}}\right)\right].

The QES position is determined by

\partial_{u_{a}}S_{\rm isl}=0,\qquad\partial_{v_{a}}S_{\rm isl}=0.

(149)

The left QES equation is the reflected copy of these two conditions. In evaluating the
matter four-point function, we keep only the leading factorized channel

(p_{1},p_{3})\,,(p_{2},p_{4})
, while the crossed contribution is neglected.

where

4.2
Quantum corrections to the competition between the no-island and island saddles

After obtaining the zeroth-order saddles, we ask how the corrected background changes their relative position. In the present setup, the relevant deformation is

\hat{\eta}(t)=\eta_{0}(t)+\delta\eta(t),\qquad\Phi_{F_{0}}\rightarrow\Phi_{\hat{F}}.

(157)

The problem is then to determine how this deformation shifts the relative entropy of the two branches, and hence the Page curve and the Page time. There is, however, a simple difference between the two branches. The no-island entropy is directly determined once the background trajectory is known. The island entropy is slightly different, since it is defined after
extremizing the generalized entropy with respect to the QES position. We therefore treat the island branch and the no-island branch separately.

We first consider the island saddle. Let

x\equiv(u_{a},v_{a})

(158)

denote the QES position, and write the generalized entropy functional as

S_{\rm isl}(t;x)=S_{\rm isl}^{(0)}(t;x)+\delta S_{\rm isl}(t;x),

(159)

where
S_{\rm isl}^{(0)}
is the zeroth-order functional and
\delta S_{\rm isl}
is the correction
induced by the replacement
\eta_{0}\to\eta_{\rm eff}
and
\Phi_{F_{0}}\to\Phi_{F_{\rm eff}}
.
We first expand the quantum-corrected QES position as

x_{*}(t)=x_{0}(t)+\delta x(t),

(160)

where
x_{0}=(u_{a,0},v_{a,0})
satisfies the zeroth-order extremality condition

\partial_{x}S_{\rm isl}^{(0)}(t;x_{0})=0.

(161)

Next, expanding the corrected extremality condition

\partial_{x}S_{\rm isl}(t;x_{*})=0

(162)

to first order gives

\partial_{i}\partial_{j}S_{\rm isl}^{(0)}(t;x)\Big|_{x_{0}}\,\delta x^{j}+\partial_{i}\delta S_{\rm isl}(t;x)\Big|_{x_{0}}=0,\qquad i,j\in\{u_{a},v_{a}\}.

(163)

For the on-shell island entropy, however, the first-order correction is simpler. Expanding
S_{\rm isl}(t;x_{*})
around
x_{0}
, one finds

\displaystyle S_{\rm isl}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv S_{\rm isl}(t;x_{*})

\displaystyle=S_{\rm isl}^{(0)}(t;x_{0})+\partial_{x}S_{\rm isl}^{(0)}(t;x)\Big|_{x_{0}}\!\cdot\delta x+\delta S_{\rm isl}(t;x_{0})+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

(164)

Since
x_{0}
is already an extremum of the zeroth-order functional, the linear term
proportional to
\partial_{x}S_{\rm isl}^{(0)}(t;x_{0})
vanishes. Therefore,

S_{\rm isl}(t)=S_{\rm isl}^{(0)}(t;x_{0})+\delta S_{\rm isl}(t;x_{0})+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

(165)

In other words, to first order it is sufficient to evaluate the corrected functional on the
zeroth-order QES. Using the zeroth-order on-shell island entropy obtained in section
4.1
,

S_{\rm isl}^{(0)}(t;\eta_{0})=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2G_{2}}+\frac{8\pi^{2}C}{\beta_{b}}+\frac{c}{3}\log\frac{\beta_{b}}{\pi}+\frac{\pi c}{3\beta_{b}}\bigl(t-\eta_{0}(t)\bigr)-\frac{c}{6}\log\eta_{0}^{\prime}(t),

(166)

and expanding it under
\eta_{0}\rightarrow\eta_{\rm eff}=\eta_{0}+\delta\eta
, we obtain

\delta S_{\rm isl}(t)=-\frac{\pi c}{3\beta_{b}}\,\delta\eta(t)-\frac{c}{6}\frac{\delta\eta^{\prime}(t)}{\eta_{0}^{\prime}(t)}.

(167)

The no-island branch is simpler because there is no additional extremization. In this case the correction is obtained directly by expanding the zeroth-order expression

S_{\rm no}^{(0)}(t;\eta_{0})=\frac{c}{3}\log\!\left[\frac{\beta_{b}}{\pi}\cosh\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta_{b}}\bigl(t+\eta_{0}(t)\bigr)\right)\right]-\frac{c}{6}\log\eta_{0}^{\prime}(t).

(168)

Defining

x_{0}(t)\equiv\frac{\pi}{\beta_{b}}\bigl(t+\eta_{0}(t)\bigr),

(169)

one finds

\delta S_{\rm no}^{\rm(dyn)}(t)=\frac{\pi c}{3\beta_{b}}\tanh x_{0}(t)\,\delta\eta(t)-\frac{c}{6}\frac{\delta\eta^{\prime}(t)}{\eta_{0}^{\prime}(t)}.

(170)

Now we can compare the two branches directly. It is convenient to introduce the entropy difference

\Delta S(t)\equiv S_{\rm no}(t)-S_{\rm isl}(t),\qquad\Delta S^{(0)}(t)\equiv S_{\rm no}^{(0)}(t;\eta_{0})-S_{\rm isl}^{(0)}(t;\eta_{0}).

(171)

Subtracting the two branch corrections, the
\delta\eta^{\prime}
-terms cancel identically:

\delta\Delta S(t)=\delta S_{\rm no}(t)-\delta S_{\rm isl}(t)=\frac{\pi c}{3\beta_{b}}\left[1+\tanh x_{0}(t)\right]\delta\eta(t).

(172)

This cancellation is useful because it shows that, at leading order, the shift of the
competition depends only on
\delta\eta(t)
itself, and not on its derivative.

The Page time shift then follows by expanding the crossing condition around the zeroth-order Page time. Let

t_{\rm Page}=t_{0}+\delta t_{\rm Page},

(173)

where
t_{0}\equiv t_{\rm Page}^{(0)}
is the zeroth-order Page time determined by

\Delta S^{(0)}(t_{0})=0.

(174)

Expanding the corrected crossing condition

\Delta S(t_{\rm Page})=0

(175)

to first order gives

\delta t_{\rm Page}=-\frac{\delta\Delta S_{(}t_{0})}{\Delta S^{(0)\prime}(t_{0})}.

(176)

To make this expression more explicit, define

x_{p}^{(0)}\equiv x_{0}(t_{0})=\frac{\pi}{\beta_{b}}\bigl(t_{0}+\eta_{0}(t_{0})\bigr),\qquad\delta\eta_{p}\equiv\delta\eta(t_{0}).

(177)

Then

\delta\Delta S(t_{0})=\frac{\pi c}{3\beta_{b}}\bigl(1+\tanh x_{p}^{(0)}\bigr)\delta\eta_{p}.

(178)

On the other hand, differentiating the zeroth-order entropy difference yields

\Delta S^{(0)\prime}(t_{0})=\frac{\pi c}{3\beta_{b}}D_{p}^{(0)},

(179)

with

D_{p}^{(0)}\equiv\bigl(1+\eta_{0}^{\prime}(t_{0})\bigr)\tanh x_{p}^{(0)}-\bigl(1-\eta_{0}^{\prime}(t_{0})\bigr).

(180)

Therefore, the dynamical contribution to the Page time shift can be written in the compact
form

\delta t_{\rm Page}=-\frac{1+\tanh x_{p}^{(0)}}{D_{p}^{(0)}}\,\delta\eta_{p}\equiv-F_{p}^{(0)}\,\delta\eta_{p},

(181)

where

F_{p}^{(0)}\equiv\frac{1+\tanh x_{p}^{(0)}}{D_{p}^{(0)}}.

(182)

This is the form that we will use below when we discuss separately the
1/C
and
1/K

contributions to the Page time shift.

At this stage it is useful to comment on the sign of the prefactor
F_{p}^{(0)}
.
Since

F_{p}^{(0)}=\frac{1+\tanh x_{p}^{(0)}}{D_{p}^{(0)}},

(183)

the numerator is always positive, and hence the sign of
F_{p}^{(0)}
is entirely
determined by
D_{p}^{(0)}
. Using the definition (
180
), one can rewrite

D_{p}^{(0)}=\bigl(1+\eta_{0}^{\prime}(t_{0})\bigr)\tanh x_{p}^{(0)}-\bigl(1-\eta_{0}^{\prime}(t_{0})\bigr)=\frac{2\bigl(\eta_{0}^{\prime}(t_{0})-e^{-2x_{p}^{(0)}}\bigr)}{1+e^{-2x_{p}^{(0)}}}.

(184)

Therefore,

\mathrm{sign}\!\bigl(F_{p}^{(0)}\bigr)=\mathrm{sign}\!\bigl(D_{p}^{(0)}\bigr)=\mathrm{sign}\!\Bigl(\eta_{0}^{\prime}(t_{0})-e^{-2x_{p}^{(0)}}\Bigr).

(185)

In particular,
F_{p}^{(0)}
is positive provided

\eta_{0}^{\prime}(t_{0})>e^{-2x_{p}^{(0)}}.

(186)

At the zeroth-order Page point
t_{0}
, the crossing condition

S_{no}^{(0)}(t_{0};\eta_{0})=S_{isl}^{(0)}(t_{0};\eta_{0})
implies

\log\cosh x_{p}^{(0)}=\frac{3\Phi_{0}}{2c\,G_{2}}+\frac{24\pi^{2}C}{c\,\beta_{b}}+\frac{\pi}{\beta_{b}}\bigl(t_{0}-\eta_{0}(t_{0})\bigr).

(187)

In the semiclassical regime
4\pi^{2}C/\beta_{b}\gg 1
, the second term on the right-hand
side is parametrically large. Therefore,
\log\cosh x_{p}^{(0)}\gg 1
, which implies

x_{p}^{(0)}\gg 1
. This means
e^{-2x_{p}^{(0)}}
is small, while
\eta_{0}^{\prime}(t_{0})
remains parametrically larger in this semiclassical regime. Hence we have

D_{p}^{(0)}>0,\qquad F_{p}^{(0)}>0.

(188)

This observation will be used in the sign analysis below.

To summarize, the most important result is that at first order the dynamical contribution to the Page time shift can be
written as

\delta t_{\rm Page}=-F_{p}^{(0)}\,\delta\eta_{p},\qquad\delta\eta_{p}\equiv\delta\eta(t_{0}),

(189)

where
t_{0}\equiv t_{\rm Page}^{(0)}
is the zeroth-order Page time and
F_{p}^{(0)}
is
evaluated on the zeroth-order background. Therefore, once the effective map

\eta_{\rm eff}(t)
is known, the dynamical Page time shift follows immediately from its
value at
t=t_{0}
.

For the purposes of the main text, we do not need to repeat the full reconstruction of

\eta_{\rm eff}(t)
and
F_{\rm eff}(t)
. The only input needed for the entropy analysis is
that these quantities are determined by the quantum-corrected temperature profile
T(t)
.
More precisely, appendix
B
starts from

\hat{F}(t)=\frac{\beta_{b}}{\pi}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta_{b}}\hat{\eta}(t)\right),

(190)

and combines it with the Schwarzian equation satisfied by
\hat{F}(t)
to
reconstruct
\hat{\eta}(t)
. This yields the expansion

\hat{\eta}(t)=\eta_{0}(t)+\frac{1}{C}\eta_{C}(t)+\frac{1}{K}\eta_{K}(t)+\mathcal{O}(C^{-2},K^{-2},C^{-1}K^{-1})\,.

(191)

Substituting the expansion (
191
) into (
181
), the Page time shift decomposes into the Schwarzian and
U(1)
channels,

\delta t_{\rm Page}=-\frac{F_{p}^{(0)}}{C}\eta_{C}(t_{0})-\frac{F_{p}^{(0)}}{K}\eta_{K}(t_{0})+\mathcal{O}(C^{-2},K^{-2},C^{-1}K^{-1}),

(192)

where
t_{0}\equiv t_{\rm Page}^{(0)}
is the zeroth-order Page time. It is therefore natural to define

\delta t_{\rm Page}^{(C)}\equiv-\frac{F_{p}^{(0)}}{C}\eta_{C}(t_{0}),\qquad\delta t_{\rm Page}^{(K)}\equiv-\frac{F_{p}^{(0)}}{K}\eta_{K}(t_{0}),

(193)

so that

\delta t_{\rm Page}=\delta t_{\rm Page}^{(C)}+\delta t_{\rm Page}^{(K)}+\mathcal{O}(C^{-2},K^{-2},C^{-1}K^{-1}).

(194)

Using the equations (
266
) and (
267
) in appendix
B
, we have the following integral formulas

\eta_{C}(t_{0})=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}T_{b}}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}ds\,\frac{X_{C}(s)+3\bigl(T_{\rm cl}(s)-T_{b}\bigr)}{T_{\rm cl}(s)},\qquad\eta_{K}(t_{0})=\frac{3q^{2}}{2\pi^{2}c\,T_{b}}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}ds\,\frac{X_{K}(s)}{T_{\rm cl}(s)},

(195)

with

X_{C}(t)=-e^{-\gamma t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,e^{\gamma t^{\prime}}\Bigl[4\dot{T}_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})+3\gamma\bigl(T_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})-T_{b}\bigr)\Bigr],

(196)

and

X_{K}(t)=-e^{-\gamma t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\gamma e^{\gamma t^{\prime}}\bigl[T_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})-T_{b}\bigr].

(197)

To make the sign structure more transparent, let

\Delta(t)\equiv T_{\rm cl}(t)-T_{b}.

(198)

Then the
1/K
channel can be rewritten as

\eta_{K}(t_{0})=-\frac{3q^{2}}{2\pi^{2}c\,T_{b}}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}du\,W_{K}(u)\,\Delta(u),\qquad W_{K}(u):=e^{\gamma u}\int_{u}^{t_{0}}dt\,\frac{\gamma e^{-\gamma t}}{T_{\rm cl}(t)}>0.

(199)

Therefore, whenever
T_{\rm cl}(t)>T_{b}
on the interval
[0,t_{0}]
, one has

\eta_{K}(t_{0})<0
, and thus

\delta t_{\rm Page}^{(K)}>0.

(200)

In other words, the
1/K
correction from the
U(1)
phase mode tends to delay the Page transition.

For the Schwarzian channel, it is convenient to define

I_{1}(t_{0}):=\int_{0}^{t_{0}}dt\,\frac{T_{b}+e^{-\gamma t}(4T_{i}-4T_{b})-T_{\rm cl}(t)}{T_{\rm cl}(t)},\qquad I_{2}(t_{0}):=\int_{0}^{t_{0}}du\,W_{K}(u)\,\Delta(u).

(201)

Then

\eta_{C}(t_{0})=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}T_{b}}\Bigl[I_{1}(t_{0})+I_{2}(t_{0})\Bigr].

(202)

Hence,

\delta t_{\rm Page}^{(C)}<0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad I_{1}(t_{0})+I_{2}(t_{0})>0.

(203)

This is the general sign criterion for the Schwarzian contribution.

If
T_{\rm cl}(t)>T_{b}
on the interval
[0,t_{0}]
, then
\Delta(u)>0
and therefore

I_{2}(t_{0})>0
. In addition, the sufficient condition

T_{\rm cl}(t)<T_{b}+e^{-\gamma t}(4T_{i}-4T_{b}),\qquad 0\leq t\leq t_{0},

(204)

implies
I_{1}(t_{0})>0
, and hence guarantees

\delta t_{\rm Page}^{(C)}<0.

(205)

We stress, however, that (
204
) is only a sufficient condition, and is not implied by
monotonic cooling by itself. Therefore, for the Schwarzian channel, the general statement is the criterion (
203
), rather than the stronger sign claim (
205
). In the numerical
examples studied in section
4.3
, the Schwarzian contribution is indeed found to advance the
Page transition.

We thus arrive at the following analytic picture. For the
1/K
channel, one obtains a direct
sign result whenever
T_{\rm cl}(t)>T_{b}
on
[0,t_{0}]
, namely

\delta t_{\rm Page}^{(K)}>0.

(4.80)

For the Schwarzian channel, by contrast, the sign is controlled by the criterion
(
203
). The numerical results of section
4.3
show that, in the parameter range studied
in this paper, the Schwarzian contribution is negative and hence tends to move the Page time
earlier. Therefore the sign of the total Page time shift is determined by the competition
between the two soft sectors. Decreasing
C
strengthens the Schwarzian channel, whereas
decreasing
K
strengthens the
U(1)
phase mode channel.

Before turning to the total shift on the full
(C,K)
plane, we check the sign of the Schwarzian contribution itself. From section
4.2
, this sign is controlled by

\Sigma_{C}(t_{0})\equiv I_{1}(t_{0})+I_{2}(t_{0}),\qquad\delta t_{\rm Page}^{(C)}<0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Sigma_{C}(t_{0})>0\,.

(207)

Here
I_{2}
is positive as long as the classical trajectory stays above the bath temperature.
The more delicate term is
I_{1}
. From (
201
),
I_{1}
compares the actual charged temperature profile
T_{\rm cl}(t)
with the simple cooling profile

T_{b}+e^{-\gamma t}(4T_{i}-4T_{b})

that appears in the Schwarzian part of the first-order correction. If
T_{\rm cl}(t)

stays below this curve, the integrand of
I_{1}
is positive. If the charge sector produces
a strong enough heating stage,
T_{\rm cl}(t)
can rise above this curve for part of the
evolution, and that part gives a negative contribution to
I_{1}
.

This explains the formal
\Sigma_{C}=0
curve in figure
7
. The charge mismatch source in the classical equation has the form

\dot{X}_{0}+\gamma(X_{0}-T_{b}^{2})=\delta e^{-\lambda t},\qquad X_{0}=T_{\rm cl}^{2},\qquad\delta\propto\frac{(\Delta\mu)^{2}}{C},\qquad\lambda=\frac{q^{2}}{\pi K}\,.

(208)

Increasing
\Delta\mu
increases the size of the source. Increasing
K
makes the
charge relaxation slower. Together, these two effects produce a larger and longer-lived
heating stage. This can make
I_{1}
negative enough to overcome the positive
I_{2}
,
so that
\Sigma_{C}
becomes negative in a formal extension of the scan.

The same mechanism, however, also raises the maximum temperature. For this reason we
must impose the near-AdS
2
low-temperature condition

\max_{0\leq t\leq t_{0}}T_{\rm cl}(t)<T_{\rm cut},\qquad T_{\rm cut}=0.30\,.

(209)

The gray region in figure
7
is the region that fails this test. It is not a new phase of the Schwarzian correction. In particular, the part of the

\Sigma_{C}=0
contour lying in the gray region should not be interpreted as a controlled
sign reversal. Inside the unmasked region shown in the figure, we find
\Sigma_{C}>0
,
and hence
\delta t_{\rm Page}^{(C)}<0
. Thus, within the working regime (
46
) used in this paper, we do not find a reliable regime in which simply increasing
K
and
\Delta\mu
changes the sign of the Schwarzian contribution.

We now turn from the Schwarzian sign check to the total Page time shift. Figure
7
only tested the Schwarzian contribution through

\Sigma_{C}
. Figure
8
shows the total shift

\delta t_{\rm Page}=t_{\rm Page}^{q}-t_{\rm Page}^{\rm cl}\,,

(210)

which includes both the Schwarzian and
U(1)
soft sectors.

Figure
8
shows the result in the
(C,K)
plane for
three representative values of the mismatch,

\Delta\mu=0,\qquad 0.020,\qquad 0.200.

For
\Delta\mu=0
, there is no charge-driven heating and the temperature decreases
monotonically from
T_{0}
to
T_{b}
. The temperature control condition is therefore
automatically satisfied, since
T_{\max}=T_{0}<T_{\rm cut}
. For the small mismatch

\Delta\mu=0.020
, the pattern is only mildly deformed.

The negative region in the upper-left part of the plots has a simple origin. It is not caused
by the sign reversal of
\Sigma_{C}
discussed above. In this region the Schwarzian
contribution still advances the Page transition, while the
U(1)
contribution delays it. Moving to smaller
C
strengthens the negative Schwarzian contribution, since it is a

1/C
effect. Moving to larger
K
weakens the positive
U(1)
contribution, since it is
a
1/K
effect. Therefore, for small
C
and large
K
, the Schwarzian advance can
dominate over the
U(1)
delay, and the total Page time shift becomes negative.

The third panel shows what changes when the mismatch is large enough to produce
transient heating. The same charge source that deforms the
\delta t_{\rm Page}=0

contour can also push part of the evolution outside the near-AdS
2
temperature window.
This happens in the gray region. Those points are not used to infer the sign of the
Page time shift. Inside the unmasked region, the interpretation remains the same as in the
first two panels: the sign of the total shift is set by the competition between the
Schwarzian advance and the delay from the
U(1)
sector.

Taken together, these figures support the analytic picture of section
4.2
. The quantum correction changes the Page time by changing the relative position of the no-island and island branches. The Schwarzian sector tends to move the Page transition earlier, while the
U(1)
sector tends to move it later. The sign of the total shift is therefore not fixed by one sector alone. It is fixed by their competition, within the part of parameter space where the near-AdS
2

5
Discussion

We studied the evaporation and Page dynamics of charged near-AdS
2
black holes coupled to a non-gravitating bath at fixed temperature and chemical potential. The low-energy boundary theory contains two soft sectors: the Schwarzian reparametrization
mode and the
U(1)
phase mode. This gives a simple setting in which the real-time evaporation law and the Page curve can be studied in the same setup.

We first reviewed the charged near-AdS
2
boundary theory and the corresponding thermodynamic dictionary. Starting from the matter fluxes at the interface, we derived the classical balance equations for the effective temperature and chemical potential. We then included the quantum fluctuations of the two boundary soft modes by averaging the boundary energy and the outgoing flux. This led to the quantum-corrected balance equations used in the rest of the paper. Compared with the classical evolution, the corrected equations contain additional linear terms in the temperature. These terms come from the soft-mode correction to the black hole energy and from the corrected outgoing flux. They modify the relaxation of the black hole toward the bath.

We then used the corrected real-time background to compute the Page curve. The island formula itself was not changed. Instead, we asked how the corrected evaporation dynamics changes the competition between the no-island and island branches. At first order, the on-shell island entropy can be obtained by evaluating the corrected generalized entropy on the zeroth-order QES. This allows the Page time shift to be computed without solving for the corrected QES position explicitly. The shift is therefore controlled by how the corrected background changes the two entropy branches near their crossing.

The analytic result gives a clear separation between the two soft sectors. For the correction from the
U(1)
phase mode, one obtains a direct sign result when the classical temperature stays above the bath temperature during the relevant time interval: this correction delays the Page transition. The Schwarzian correction is more subtle. Its sign is controlled by the integral criterion in equation (
203
). In the controlled numerical region studied in this paper, this criterion gives a negative Schwarzian contribution to the Page time shift, so the Schwarzian sector tends to move the Page transition earlier. The numerical results support this picture. The Page curves show that the quantum correction changes the crossing of the no-island and island branches, rather than producing a uniform vertical shift of the entropy profile. The decomposition of the Page time shift shows good agreement between the first-order approximation and the full numerical calculation. In the examples shown before, the Schwarzian contribution advances the transition, while the
U(1)
part delays it.

We also scanned the relevant parameter space. Decreasing
C
strengthens the Schwarzian
1/C
effect, while decreasing
K
strengthens the charge-sector correction from the
U(1)
phase mode. As a result, the total Page time shift can be positive or negative depending on which channel dominates. The negative region at small
C
and large
K
comes from the Schwarzian advance becoming larger than the delay from the
U(1)
sector. For large charge mismatch, the charge sector can also produce transient heating. However, this same effect can push the evolution outside the near-AdS
2
low-temperature window. Those points were excluded in the phase diagram and should not be interpreted as a controlled new regime.

A related recent work studied the same Schwarzian plus
U(1)
boundary effective theory on replica geometries and found a connected–disconnected saddle transition controlled by the temperature and the couplings
C
,
K

53
. Our setup is different: the replica prescription is kept fixed, while the soft-mode corrections enter through the real-time evaporating background and shift the Page-time crossing.

There are several natural directions for future work. First, it would be useful to go beyond the perturbative treatment of soft-mode quantum effects used here. In this paper the Schwarzian and
U(1)
sectors enter through the leading
1/C
and
1/K
corrections to the evaporation background and to the entropy branches. This is sufficient for the first-order Page-time shift, but it is not a full non-perturbative evaluation of the soft-mode path integral in the evaporating replica geometry. Such a calculation would require keeping the Schwarzian and
U(1)
modes directly in the real-time and replica path integrals. A more microscopic treatment of the bath would also start from an influence functional, as in recent work on open JT gravity
1
. It would also be interesting to see whether the same competition between the Schwarzian sector and the charge sector appears in other near-extremal black holes.

Appendix A
Controlled approximation to the quantum-corrected temperature evolution

In this appendix we derive the controlled approximation to the temperature profile used in
subsection
3.3
and later in appendix
B
. The final result is organized by the following equation

T(t)=T_{\rm cl}(t)+\frac{1}{C}\,\delta T_{C}(t)+\frac{1}{K}\,\delta T_{K}(t)+\mathcal{O}\!\left((CT)^{-2},(KT)^{-2},(CT)^{-1}(KT)^{-1}\right),

(211)

where
T_{\rm cl}(t)
is the classical profile, while
\delta T_{C}(t)
and
\delta T_{K}(t)

denote respectively the Schwarzian and
U(1)
first-order corrections.

Throughout this appendix we work in the same working regime as in the main text,

TL\ll 1,\qquad 4\pi^{2}CT\gg 1,\qquad KT\gg 1,

(212)

and we consistently keep only terms to first order in
1/(CT)
and
1/(KT)
.

We start from the temperature equation obtained from the full quantum-corrected balance equation after eliminating
\dot{\mu}
,

(4\pi^{2}C\,T+2)\dot{T}=-\alpha\,(T^{2}-T_{b}^{2})-\frac{c}{16\pi C}(T-T_{b})-\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi K}(T-T_{b})+J(t),

(213)

with

\alpha\equiv\frac{\pi c}{12}.

(214)

The charge equation gives

\dot{\mu}=-\frac{q^{2}}{2\pi K}\,(\mu-\mu_{b}),\qquad\mu(t)=\mu_{b}+\Delta\mu_{0}\,e^{-t/\tau_{Q}},\qquad\tau_{Q}\equiv\frac{2\pi K}{q^{2}},

(215)

where
\Delta\mu_{0}\equiv\mu_{0}-\mu_{b}
. Therefore,

(\mu(t)-\mu_{b})^{2}=\Delta\mu_{0}^{2}e^{-\lambda t},\qquad\lambda\equiv\frac{2}{\tau_{Q}}=\frac{q^{2}}{\pi K},

(216)

and hence,

J(t)=J_{0}e^{-\lambda t},\qquad J_{0}\equiv\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi}\Delta\mu_{0}^{2}.

(217)

To organize the expansion, it is convenient to introduce

X(t)\equiv T^{2}(t),\qquad X_{b}\equiv T_{b}^{2},

(218)

together with the slow time

s\equiv\gamma t,\qquad\gamma\equiv\frac{c}{24\pi C}.

(219)

In terms of
X
, equation (
213
) becomes

X^{\prime}(s)+\frac{1}{\pi^{2}C}T^{\prime}(s)=-(X-X_{b})-\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}C}(T-T_{b})-\frac{3q^{2}}{\pi^{2}c\,K}(T-T_{b})+\frac{J(s)}{\alpha},

(220)

where the prime denotes
d/ds
. We now expand the squared temperature as

X(t)=X_{0}(t)+\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}C}\,X_{C}(t)+\frac{3q^{2}}{\pi^{2}c\,K}\,X_{K}(t)+\mathcal{O}\!\left(C^{-2},K^{-2},C^{-1}K^{-1}\right).

(221)

The leading piece is identified with the classical solution,

X_{0}(t)=T_{\rm cl}^{2}(t).

(222)

With this parametrization, the first-order temperature corrections appearing in
(
211
) are

\delta T_{C}(t)=\frac{X_{C}(t)}{8\pi^{2}T_{\rm cl}(t)},\qquad\delta T_{K}(t)=\frac{3q^{2}}{2\pi^{2}c}\,\frac{X_{K}(t)}{T_{\rm cl}(t)}.

(223)

At leading order, equation (
220
) gives

X_{0}^{\prime}(s)=-(X_{0}-X_{b})+\frac{J(s)}{\alpha}.

(224)

Returning to the physical time
t
, this becomes

\dot{X}_{0}+\gamma X_{0}=\gamma T_{b}^{2}+\delta e^{-\lambda t},\qquad\delta\equiv\frac{J_{0}}{2\pi^{2}C}=\frac{q^{2}(\mu_{0}-\mu_{b})^{2}}{8\pi^{3}C}.

(225)

For
\gamma\neq\lambda
, the solution is

X_{0}(t)=T_{b}^{2}+(T_{0}^{2}-T_{b}^{2})e^{-\gamma t}+\frac{\delta}{\gamma-\lambda}\left(e^{-\lambda t}-e^{-\gamma t}\right),

(226)

and therefore,

T_{\rm cl}(t)=\sqrt{X_{0}(t)}.

(227)

In the resonant case
\gamma=\lambda
, the last term in (
226
) is replaced by

\delta\,t\,e^{-\gamma t}.

(228)

At first order, the
1/C
and
1/K
channels decouple and satisfy

X_{C}^{\prime}(s)+X_{C}(s)=-4\,T_{\rm cl}^{\prime}(s)-3\bigl(T_{\rm cl}(s)-T_{b}\bigr),

(229)

and

X_{K}^{\prime}(s)+X_{K}(s)=-\bigl(T_{\rm cl}(s)-T_{b}\bigr).

(230)

Imposing the natural matching conditions

X_{C}(0)=0,\qquad X_{K}(0)=0,

(231)

these equations integrate to

X_{C}(t)=-e^{-\gamma t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,e^{\gamma t^{\prime}}\left[4\,\dot{T}_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})+3\gamma\bigl(T_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})-T_{b}\bigr)\right],

(232)

and

X_{K}(t)=-e^{-\gamma t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\gamma e^{\gamma t^{\prime}}\bigl[T_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})-T_{b}\bigr].

(233)

Here

\dot{T}_{\rm cl}(t)=\frac{\dot{X}_{0}(t)}{2T_{\rm cl}(t)},

(234)

with
X_{0}(t)
given in (
226
).

Substituting (
232
) and (
233
) into (
223
), we finally obtain the
controlled first-order temperature profile

T(t)=T_{\rm cl}(t)+\frac{1}{C}\,\delta T_{C}(t)+\frac{1}{K}\,\delta T_{K}(t)+\mathcal{O}\!\left(C^{-2},K^{-2},C^{-1}K^{-1}\right),

(235)

with

\delta T_{C}(t)=\frac{X_{C}(t)}{8\pi^{2}T_{\rm cl}(t)},\qquad\delta T_{K}(t)=\frac{3q^{2}}{2\pi^{2}c}\,\frac{X_{K}(t)}{T_{\rm cl}(t)}.

(236)

Equations (
226
), (
232
), (
233
), and (
236
) are the final results of
this appendix. They provide a global controlled approximation to the quantum-corrected
temperature evolution and cleanly separate the Schwarzian
1/C
correction from the

U(1)
fluctuation-induced
1/K
correction.

For completeness, in the late-time near-equilibrium regime one may further set

T(t)=T_{b}+\delta T\,,

(237)

which reduces the full temperature equation to the linear form used in the main text,

\dot{\delta T}=-\Gamma_{T}\,\delta T,\qquad\Gamma_{T}=\frac{\pi c\,T_{b}/6+\Lambda}{4\pi^{2}CT_{b}+2},\qquad\Lambda\equiv\frac{c}{16\pi C}+\frac{q^{2}}{4\pi K}.

(238)

Appendix B
Reconstruction of
\hat{\eta}(t)
and
\hat{F}(t)
from the controlled temperature profile

In this appendix we explain how the effective time variable
\hat{\eta}(t)
and the
corresponding boundary trajectory
\hat{F}(t)
are reconstructed from the controlled
temperature profile obtained from the quantum-corrected balance equation. The purpose of
this appendix is purely technical: it provides the dictionary needed to translate the dynamical
input
T(t)
into the variables entering the entropy formulas in section
4
.

B.1
Exact relation between the map
\hat{\eta}(t)
and
\hat{F}(t)

We introduce a vacuum coordinate
w^{-}
for the outgoing modes by

w^{-}\equiv-e^{-2\pi\hat{\eta}(t)/\beta_{b}},

(239)

where
\beta_{b}
is the fixed inverse bath temperature. In our notation, the boundary reparametrization is denoted by
\hat{F}(t)
, and we choose the convenient gauge

w^{-}(t)=\frac{\pi\hat{F}(t)-\beta_{b}}{\pi\hat{F}(t)+\beta_{b}}.

(240)

Combining (
239
) and (
240
), one obtains the exact dictionary

-e^{-2\pi\hat{\eta}(t)/\beta_{b}}=\frac{\pi\hat{F}(t)-\beta_{b}}{\pi\hat{F}(t)+\beta_{b}},\qquad\hat{F}(t)=\frac{\beta_{b}}{\pi}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta_{b}}\hat{\eta}(t)\right).

(241)

At equilibrium,
\hat{\eta}(t)=t
, and hence

\hat{F}(t)=\frac{\beta_{b}}{\pi}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\pi t}{\beta_{b}}\right),

(242)

as expected for the static thermal trajectory.

It is important that
\beta_{b}
, rather than the instantaneous black-hole inverse
temperature, appears in (
239
)–(
241
). The reason is that
\beta_{b}
is
the fixed thermal-cylinder parameter entering the vacuum-coordinate map, whereas the
dynamical temperature
T(t)
enters only through the Schwarzian equation for
F(t)
.

Writing

\hat{F}(t)=g(\hat{\eta}(t)),\qquad g(\eta)=\frac{\beta_{b}}{\pi}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta_{b}}\eta\right),

(243)

and using the Schwarzian composition rule

\{g\circ\hat{\eta},t\}=\{\hat{\eta},t\}+(\hat{\eta}^{\prime})^{2}\{g,\eta\},

(244)

together with the standard identity

\{\tanh(a\eta),\eta\}=-2a^{2},\qquad a=\frac{\pi}{\beta_{b}}=\pi T_{b},

(245)

we obtain

\{g,\eta\}=-2\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta_{b}}\right)^{2}=-2\pi^{2}T_{b}^{2}.

(246)

Hence, the exact relation between
\hat{F}
and
\hat{\eta}
is

\{\hat{F},t\}=\{\hat{\eta},t\}-2\pi^{2}T_{b}^{2}(\hat{\eta}^{\prime})^{2}.

(247)

The analysis in section
3.2
determines the raw quantum-averaged Schwarzian

\{F_{\rm raw}(t),t\}=-2\pi^{2}T(t)^{2}-\frac{3}{2C}T(t).

(248)

If this raw Schwarzian is inserted directly into (
247
), the resulting map develops
a residual linear drift even after
T(t)\to T_{b}
. To remove this artifact, we impose the
fixed-point normalization that the map should reduce to the standard thermal
map (
242
) when the system relaxes to the bath. This leads to the following definition

\{\hat{F}(t),t\}:=-2\pi^{2}T(t)^{2}-\frac{3}{2C}\bigl(T(t)-T_{b}\bigr).

(249)

Equivalently,

\{\hat{F}(t),t\}=-2\pi^{2}T_{b}^{2}-2\pi^{2}\bigl(T(t)^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\bigr)-\frac{3}{2C}\bigl(T(t)-T_{b}\bigr).

(250)

Combining (
247
) and (
250
), we arrive at the exact equation

\{\hat{\eta},t\}-2\pi^{2}T_{b}^{2}\Bigl[(\hat{\eta}^{\prime})^{2}-1\Bigr]=-2\pi^{2}\bigl(T(t)^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\bigr)-\frac{3}{2C}\bigl(T(t)-T_{b}\bigr).

(251)

By construction, the right-hand side vanishes at the bath fixed point, so
\hat{\eta}(t)=t

is recovered.

B.2
Controlled adiabatic reconstruction of
\hat{\eta}(t)
and
\hat{F}(t)

We now evaluate (
251
) in the controlled regime discussed in appendix
A
, where
we keep only first-order terms in
1/(CT)
and
1/(KT)
, and drop

\mathcal{O}\!\left((CT)^{-2},(KT)^{-2},(CT)^{-1}(KT)^{-1}\right).

(252)

In the controlled quasi-static regime,
\hat{\eta}^{\prime}(t)
is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
function of the slowly
varying temperature profile
T(t)
. Since the latter evolves on the scales
\gamma^{-1}\sim C
and
\lambda^{-1}\sim K
, one has

\dot{T}=\mathcal{O}(\gamma,\lambda),\qquad\ddot{T}=\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2},\lambda^{2},\gamma\lambda).

(253)

Therefore,

\hat{\eta}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\mathcal{O}(\gamma,\lambda),\qquad\hat{\eta}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)=\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2},\lambda^{2},\gamma\lambda),

(254)

and hence, from the definition of the Schwarzian derivative,

\{\hat{\eta},t\}=\frac{\hat{\eta}^{\prime\prime\prime}}{\hat{\eta}^{\prime}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\hat{\eta}^{\prime\prime}}{\hat{\eta}^{\prime}}\right)^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2},\lambda^{2},\gamma\lambda)=\mathcal{O}(C^{-2},K^{-2},C^{-1}K^{-1}).

(255)

To the order of interest, this term may be neglected in (
251
). We then obtain
the algebraic relation

-2\pi^{2}T_{b}^{2}\Bigl[(\hat{\eta}^{\prime}(t))^{2}-1\Bigr]=-2\pi^{2}\bigl(T(t)^{2}-T_{b}^{2}\bigr)-\frac{3}{2C}\bigl(T(t)-T_{b}\bigr).

(256)

Equivalently,

(\hat{\eta}^{\prime}(t))^{2}=1+\frac{T(t)^{2}-T_{b}^{2}}{T_{b}^{2}}+\frac{3\bigl(T(t)-T_{b}\bigr)}{4\pi^{2}CT_{b}^{2}}.

(257)

Choosing the monotone branch
\hat{\eta}^{\prime}(t)>0
and expanding to first order in
1/C
, we
find

\hat{\eta}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{T(t)}{T_{b}}+\frac{3}{8\pi^{2}CT_{b}}\,\frac{T(t)-T_{b}}{T(t)}+\mathcal{O}(C^{-2},K^{-2},C^{-1}K^{-1}).

(258)

With the gauge choice
\hat{\eta}(0)=0
, this integrates to

\hat{\eta}(t)=\frac{1}{T_{b}}\int_{0}^{t}ds\,T(s)+\frac{3}{8\pi^{2}CT_{b}}\int_{0}^{t}ds\,\frac{T(s)-T_{b}}{T(s)}+\mathcal{O}(C^{-2},K^{-2},C^{-1}K^{-1}).

(259)

We now insert the controlled expansion of the temperature profile obtained in appendix (
A
),

X_{0}(t)\equiv T_{\rm cl}(t)^{2}=T_{b}^{2}+(T_{i}^{2}-T_{b}^{2})e^{-\gamma t}+\frac{\delta}{\gamma-\lambda}\bigl(e^{-\lambda t}-e^{-\gamma t}\bigr),\qquad T_{\rm cl}(t)=\sqrt{X_{0}(t)},

(260)

X_{C}(t)=-e^{-\gamma t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,e^{\gamma t^{\prime}}\Bigl[4\,\dot{T}_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})+3\gamma\bigl(T_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})-T_{b}\bigr)\Bigr],

(261)

X_{K}(t)=-e^{-\gamma t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,\gamma e^{\gamma t^{\prime}}\bigl[T_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})-T_{b}\bigr],

(262)

and

T(t)=T_{\rm cl}(t)+\frac{1}{C}\,\delta T_{C}(t)+\frac{1}{K}\,\delta T_{K}(t)+\cdots,\qquad\delta T_{C}(t)=\frac{X_{C}(t)}{8\pi^{2}T_{\rm cl}(t)},\qquad\delta T_{K}(t)=\frac{3q^{2}}{2\pi^{2}c}\frac{X_{K}(t)}{T_{\rm cl}(t)}.

(263)

Substituting (
263
) into (
259
), we decompose

\hat{\eta}(t)=\eta_{0}(t)+\frac{1}{C}\,\eta_{C}(t)+\frac{1}{K}\,\eta_{K}(t)+\cdots,

(264)

with

\eta_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{T_{b}}\int_{0}^{t}ds\,T_{\rm cl}(s),

(265)

\eta_{C}(t)=\frac{1}{T_{b}}\int_{0}^{t}ds\,\delta T_{C}(s)+\frac{3}{8\pi^{2}T_{b}}\int_{0}^{t}ds\,\frac{T_{\rm cl}(s)-T_{b}}{T_{\rm cl}(s)}=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}T_{b}}\int_{0}^{t}ds\,\frac{X_{C}(s)+3\bigl(T_{\rm cl}(s)-T_{b}\bigr)}{T_{\rm cl}(s)},

(266)

and

\eta_{K}(t)=\frac{1}{T_{b}}\int_{0}^{t}ds\,\delta T_{K}(s)=\frac{3q^{2}}{2\pi^{2}c\,T_{b}}\int_{0}^{t}ds\,\frac{X_{K}(s)}{T_{\rm cl}(s)}.

(267)

Finally, using the exact relation (
241
) with
\hat{\eta}
, we obtain

\hat{F}(t)=\frac{\beta_{b}}{\pi}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta_{b}}\hat{\eta}(t)\right).

(268)

If one expands around the zeroth-order trajectory, define

\delta\eta(t)\equiv\hat{\eta}(t)-\eta_{0}(t),\qquad F_{0}(t)\equiv\frac{\beta_{b}}{\pi}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta_{b}}\eta_{0}(t)\right),

(269)

then to first order,

\hat{F}(t)\simeq F_{0}(t)+\delta\hat{\eta}(t)\,{\rm sech}^{2}\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta_{b}}\eta_{0}(t)\right).

(270)
