Final Technical Report NitFix Project (DOE Award No. DE-SC0018247)
The DOE NitFix project aimed to uncover the genetic basis of symbiotic nitrogen-fixing root
nodules and to engineer this trait into non-nodulating plants. We pursued three major objectives:
(1) Phylogenomic discovery of the genetic toolkit of root nodule symbioses, (2) Verification of
nodulation gene function in model plants (legume Medicago and non-nodulating Populus), and
(3) Engineering nodulation in Populus. This report synthesizes key findings across these aims,
organized by topic, to highlight the final scientific progress and discoveries of the project.

Phylogenomic Discovery of the Root Nodule Symbiosis Toolkit
The first aim of the project was to construct an extensive phylogenetic framework for the
nitrogen-fixing clade of angiosperms. We assembled a phylogeny encompassing ~14,000 species
across the Rosid I clade that contains all nodulating lineages – by far the largest phylogenomic
dataset for any plant clade1,2. This effort involved sequencing targeted gene sets from herbarium
specimens of over 15,000 species and assembling alignments for dozens of loci. Using a custom
high-throughput pipeline (aTRAM with project-specific modules)3, all sequencing data were
assembled and quality-filtered to avoid paralogs or contaminants. The resulting phylogeny
provided a robust comparative backbone for analyzing the evolution of nodulation4.
 With this framework, we investigated how often and under what conditions nodulation
evolved. Ancestral trait reconstruction using a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) strongly
supported the classic “predisposition” hypothesis for nodulation4. Under the best-fit model, the
common ancestor of the nitrogen-fixing clade did not nodulate but existed in a hidden
“precursor” state that was ~10 million times more likely to give rise to nodulation than a non-
predisposed lineage4. This suggests that certain genetic preconditions were present in ancestral
non-nodulating plants, priming them for the later evolution of nodules. The analysis indicated
that this predisposed state was evolutionarily unstable – it was lost (reverted to a normal non-
predisposed state) approximately three times faster than it led to gaining nodulation. In practical
terms, a lineage might have the latent capacity for nodulation but still never evolve nodules
before losing that capacity. Trait mapping also revealed multiple independent gains of nodulation
(with gains more frequent than losses), underscoring that root nodule symbiosis is an
evolutionarily labile trait. These results reinforce that nodulation evolved many times, rather than
from a single origin, and provide crucial context for efforts to transfer this symbiosis across
species. The finding that nodulation can arise repeatedly implies that engineering nodulation may
be feasible if the underlying predisposition genes and regulatory changes are in place.
 Beyond trait evolution, the phylogenomic approach was used to systematically identify
the “genetic toolkit” underlying nodulation. The project targeted ~130 known nodulation-related
genes for sequencing across several thousand species and developed pipelines to test for
correlations between gene presence/absence and the nodulation trait on the phylogeny. Early
results identified several genes whose phylogenetic distribution agrees with the distribution of
the nodulation trait – for example, NIN (NODULE INCEPTION) and RPG (Required for Polar
Growth) were present in all nodulating taxa of the clade and absent in non-nodulators5. This
phylogenetic screening informed the selection of genes to test (Aim II). The comprehensive
phylogeny also allowed us to identify optimal sister clade species pairs – closely related
nodulating vs. non-nodulating species – for detailed genomic comparisons. By focusing on such
pairs (where many genes are shared by common ancestry), we were able to more sensitively
detect nodulation-specific genetic differences. These comparative genomic experiments were
initiated in the latter part of the project, alongside continuing sequencing of difficult-to-obtain

taxa. Overall, the phylogenomic component delivered the evolutionary context (number of
nodulation origins, predisposition lineages) but also generated a database of gene sequences and
conserved elements that enabled the downstream functional analyses4,6,7.

Comparative Transcriptomics and Regulatory Network Analysis
To complement the phylogenomic analysis, we examined transcriptional and regulatory
dynamics of nodulation using time-course experiments and network modeling6,8–10. We
performed comparative transcriptomics with the nodulator Medicago truncatula and the non-
nodulator Populus tremula× alba by treating both with rhizobial lipo-chitooligosaccharide
signals (Nod factors) and sampling roots over a time course11,12. For each species, high-
resolution RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) captured gene expression changes, while ATAC-seq
(Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin) profiled chromatin accessibility dynamics as an
indicator of regulatory element activity9.
 Initial analysis of the Medicago time series showed a temporal program: principal
component analysis of the RNA-seq data revealed that samples clustered primarily by time after
treatment, indicating a strong and coordinated time-dependent response to the Nod factor signal.
We identified clusters of co-expressed genes with distinct dynamic expression patterns over the
24 hours. Many of these early-responsive genes corresponded to known nodulation pathways. In
parallel, the ATAC-seq data identified thousands of regions of open chromatin, many near
transcription start sites or known symbiosis genes. A large fraction of the conserved noncoding
regions identified in the phylogenomic analysis overlapped with ATAC-seq peaks, suggesting
they might be functional regulatory sites specific to nodulating species.
 To integrate these complex datasets, we developed a novel computational tool – Dynamic
Regulatory Module Networks (DRMN). This algorithm groups genes into modules based on
expression dynamics and simultaneously identifies putative regulators (cis-elements or
transcription factors) that drive each module’s profile. By applying DRMN to the Medicago Nod
factor response data, we could infer modules of co-expressed genes at each time point and the
key regulatory motifs associated with their expression9. The analysis uncovered specific
transcription factors and cis-elements that likely orchestrate the symbiotic response. For
example, DRMN highlighted regulators such as MTF1, IBM1, and EIN3 as significant drivers of
dynamic gene modules during the first day after infection. The predicted target genes of these
regulators overlapped with known symbiotic genes, and we validated the module assignments
using published legume gene expression datasets. These results provided new candidates (e.g.,
MTF1 and IBM1) for roles in nodulation and confirmed that the experimental approach could
recover known regulators (like EIN3).
 Using the Populus RNA-seq time course, we performed a direct comparison between the
legume and non-legume responses. Despite Populus being a non-nodulator, exposure to rhizobial
Nod factors still triggered changes in gene expression, allowing a unique conserved vs. divergent
pathway analysis. A preliminary comparison using the Arboretum algorithm (which aligns
expression modules across species) identified seven modules of co-expressed genes that were
substantially conserved between Medicago and Populus. This implies that a core set of gene
networks respond to LCO signals in both a nodulating and a non-nodulating plant, possibly
reflecting ancient signaling or defense responses that predate nodulation. However, the analysis
also found several genes that shifted module membership between species, meaning their
regulatory context in the network was different in Populus. Such genes likely underwent
regulatory re-wiring during the evolution of nodulation. Together, the transcriptomic and

network studies determined the early signaling events of nodulation and identified which aspects
of the response are conserved (perhaps part of the predisposition) and which are unique to
nodulating species. This provided evidence for changes were necessary for the evolution of
nodulation – for instance, it suggests that much of the Nod factor signal transduction network
was already present in ancestral non-nodulators, but some key regulatory connections (modules)
were altered or added in the nodulating descendants.

Evolutionary Analysis of Gene Gain/Loss and Divergence in Nodulation
Leveraging the large comparative dataset, we conducted evolutionary genomics analyses to
pinpoint genes associated with the gain or loss of nodulation. One approach was to identify genes
present or absent in a pattern that mirrors the presence or absence of nodulation across species.
Early in the project, we compared the genomes of representative nodulating and non-nodulating
species in the nitrogen-fixing clade (Fabales, Fagales, Cucurbitales, Rosales – collectively
“FaFaCuRo”). We identified orthologous gene groups that are present in all nodulating
FaFaCuRo species but missing from the non-nodulating relatives, on the premise that such genes
might be essential for nodulation. As mentioned earlier, candidate gene families emerged from
this analysis (excluding NIN and RPG families which served as positive controls). These
included several genes not previously characterized in nodulation. To test their importance,
Medicago truncatula Tnt1 insertion mutants were obtained for three of these candidate genes and
crossed to homozygosity. The nodulation phenotypes of these knockout lines were evaluated as a
direct experimental assessment of whether losing those genes impairs nodule formation.
 As more plant genomes became available, we scaled up the presence/absence analysis.
We assembled a dataset of 93 diverse plant genomes and transcriptomes (covering any more
nodulating and non-nodulating species) and used the OrthoFinder algorithm to cluster ~3.4
million protein sequences from these species into orthologous groups. Quality control was
applied, requiring high completeness scores (e.g. ~70% or greater BUSCO scores) for inclusion
of a genome, to ensure reliable orthology inference. This comprehensive orthogroup catalog was
mined for patterns of gene gains and losses correlated with nodulation.
 In parallel, we implemented a forward genomics approach to detect genes with unusual
sequence divergence patterns associated with the nodulation trait. In this approach, genes crucial
for that trait might exhibit accelerated evolution or relaxed constraint specifically in lineages that
lost the trait. To test this, we aligned whole-genome sequences of 42 plant species (25 nodulating
and 17 non-nodulating, spanning the FaFaCuRo clade and outgroups) against the Medicago
reference genome. We examined gene loci for statistical signatures of different evolutionary
rates between nodulators and non-nodulators, controlling for phylogenetic relatedness. Using
branch models and generalized least squares (GLS) frameworks, we identified 562 genes
showing significant rate shifts (p < 0.05) consistent with nodulation-related divergence – the list
was enriched for genes with known roles in nodulation. These candidates also include many
genes not previously linked to nodulation, especially those related to hormone pathways,
transport, and transcriptional regulation.
 As part of this work, we analyzed the evolution of the NFP (Nod Factor Perception) gene
 7
family . NFP encodes a LysM receptor-like kinase that in legumes binds bacterial Nod factors to
initiate symbiosis. The comparative analysis of NFP homologs across dozens of nodulating and
non-nodulating species suggested a complex history: the data indicate multiple independent
duplications or sequence modifications of NFP in different nodulating lineages, implying that
NFP’s role in LCO symbiosis may have evolved convergently more than once. NFP sequences

from 37 diverse species were examined, and a pattern emerged gene copy number changes in
NFP coincided with nodulation status.

Functional Validation of Nodulation Genes in Medicago and Populus
After identifying candidate genes and regulatory elements through computation and evolutionary
analysis, we experimentally validated some of these components in Medicago truncatula
(nodulator) and Populus (non-nodulator). Discoveries from Aim I were functionally verified,
including roles of cytokinin signaling13, novel transcription factors6,10, and conserved regulatory
sequences6.
 Another avenue of functional genomics was the use of transcriptomic data at single-cell
resolution to find regulators of early nodule development10. We isolated single nuclei from
Medicago root cells and performed RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) on these nuclei after rhizobial
inoculation. We focused on the pericycle cell population to trace a developmental trajectory from
pericycle cells to actively dividing ones as nodulation progressed. Along this trajectory, genes
were identified as early responders, including genes from the STY family of transcription factor,
known to positively regulate auxin biosynthesis. Increased auxin production is known to be
involved in lateral organ formation, including nodules. The single-cell data indicated this STY-
like gene is induced as pericycle cells become mitotically activated. At the same time,
components of the two-component cytokinin signaling cascade were upregulated, notably a
previously uncharacterized histidine kinase (HK).
 To facilitate rapid detection of nodulation signaling and to compare hormone responses
between species, we also developed a fluorescent cytokinin sensor for plant roots13. Cytokinin
accumulation in root cortical cells is one of the earliest signals of nodule organogenesis. We
employed the synthetic TCSn promoter (a known cytokinin-responsive promoter element)
driving a nuclear-localized GFP (pTCSn::nls-GFP) as a reporter for cytokinin signaling. In
Medicago hairy root transformations, this sensor behaved as expected: untreated roots showed
baseline fluorescence mainly in root tips and meristem, but upon application of cytokinin the
sensor was strongly induced in cortical cells within 24 hours.
 We also examined the function of nodulation gene candidates in Populus, essentially to
test whether introducing or altering a given gene in a normally non-nodulating plant can induce
nodulation-like outcomes. A major focus was on the NODULE INCEPTION (NIN) gene, a
master regulator of nodule development in legumes12. Poplar had been found to possess multiple
NIN homologs (seven full-length copies in P. trichocarpa) despite not nodulating. We generated
transgenic Populus tremula× alba lines overexpressing these Populus NIN genes under strong
promoters, as well as lines overexpressing the Medicago NIN itself, to test their effects on root
development.

Engineering Root Nodule Organogenesis and Rhizobial Signal Response in Populus
The ultimate goal of the NitFix project was to engineer a functional root nodule symbiosis in
Populus, a woody bioenergy crop that does not naturally form nodules. Significant progress was
made toward creating nodule-like organs in Populus and making them responsive to rhizobial
signals.
 A critical enabling achievement of the project was establishing a robust transformation
and regeneration system for Populus. We optimized Agrobacterium rhizogenes-mediated hairy
root transformation in the Populus tremula×alba clone 717 and regenerated whole plants from
transformed roots. Over the course of the project, we generated a suite of transgenic Populus

lines expressing various key nodulation regulators. These included Populus’s endogenous
candidates, but also legume genes introduced into Populus. Several transgenic Populus lines
developed unusual root structures that were not seen in wild-type plants, or nodule-like structures
(NLS). Thus, certain genetic modifications can trigger organogenetic programs in Populus roots
beyond normal lateral root development. As an example, the constitutive expression of the Lotus
CRE1 cytokinin receptor and cortex-specific expression of MtNIN were capable of inducing NLS
on Populus roots, albeit at low frequency. Additional work is continuing to enhance the
frequency of occurrence of these structures.
 While genetic induction of nodule-like organs in Populus was a major goal of this
project, a functional symbiosis also requires that these organs be infected by nitrogen-fixing
bacteria. A parallel line of work in Aim III aimed to determine how Populus can perceive
rhizobial signals and allow bacterial colonization. We observed that Populus possesses a
working Common Symbiosis Signaling Pathway (CSSP), a pathway that in legumes and
actinorhizal plants mediates communication with symbionts. A project challenge has been
finding rhizobial strains that can infect Populus roots. We evaluated numerous bacterial strains
and co-cultivation methods to establish colonization of Populus. These attempts have not found a
strain that efficiently infects Populus. This line of experimentation is ongoing, as the ultimate
demonstration of engineered nodulation would be a Populus nodule that hosts rhizobia
internally.
