Epidemic of Measles in the Highland Division at Bedford, 1914-15

By W. Kinnear

EPIDEMIC OF MEASLES IN THE HIGHLAND
DIVISION AT BEDFORD, 1914-15.
By W. KINNEAR, M.D., Dundee, Colonel, Army Medical Service,
T.F. (retired), late A.D.M.S. Highland Division.
(.Presidential Address at the Annual Meeting of the Forfarshire
Medical Association at Arbroath, 27th June 1923.)
The Highland Territorial (afterwards 51st) Division as mobilised
in August 1914 was composed entirely of men belonging to
the territorial districts forming the divisional area, i.e., all
Scotland north of the Forth and Clyde, with Renfrewshire
south of the Firth of Clyde. Thus the 8th Battalion Argyll
and Sutherland Highlanders consisted altogether of men
from Argyllshire and its islands?Islay, Jura, and Mull; the
4th Camerons of men from Inverness-shire and Skye; the
4th Seaforths of men from Ross-shire and the Lews; the 5th
Seaforths of men from Sutherland and Caithness, and so on ;
so that a considerable proportion of the strength of the
Division consisted of men from the remote Highlands and
Islands, where epidemics of measles, scarlet fever, and similar
infectious diseases were unknown, and so they were very
liable to contract such a disease as measles in a severe form
should an outbreak occur.
The Division arrived at its War Station?Bedford?complete
in every respect on 17th August 1914, less than a fortnight
after the declaration of war, and considering that so large a
proportion of its members had to come from their civil pursuits
and employment in remote glens and far-away islands, it formed
a very creditable beginning to their war record.
The men settled down to a long period of training, and
for six or seven weeks all went smoothly from a medical point
of view. As autumn drew on we of the Divisional Medical
Staff watched anxiously and carefully for possible epidemic
outbreaks among children usual at that season, measles being
the one we feared most. We were in touch with the Borough
and County Medical Officers of Health, who did all in their
power to assist us in the trying period that followed. The
Borough Isolation Hospital had no accommodation for measles,
but at a pinch could take about six cases ; the County Isolation
Hospital at Oakley, not far from Bedford, could accommodate
twenty.

All went well, however, until the beginning of October,
when the 8th Battalion Argyll and Sutherland Highlanders
were to march to a village a few miles from Bedford and have
a week-end camp there. But a few days before they were
to start I heard that one or two cases of measles had been
reported from that village, so I advised the General Commanding
the Division that the battalion be not allowed to go there.
He at once agreed and another village was selected miles away
in another direction, a place called Eaton Socon, where no
cases of measles had occurred. The Argylls went there and
stayed over the week-end, but on Monday, the day they
returned to Bedford, a case of measles occurring in a child
was reported from Eaton Socon.
Then we knew we were in for it, for it was scarcely possible
that so susceptible a battalion as the 8th Argyll and Sutherland
Highlanders would escape after being two or three days in a place
where measles was about in its most infectious stage. For a week
there was nothing to be done, but Colonel T. F. Dewar, the
Divisional Sanitary Officer, employed the time in taking a Measles
Census of the battalions and other units most likely to contract
the disease. The great majority of the men were able to tell
whether they had had measles or not, and as for the others,
where they were doubtful or did not know, their parents were
communicated with and the necessary information obtained.
Based on these data Colonel Dewar made a careful calculation
and estimated that should an epidemic of measles break
out among the men we should have to deal with from 500
to 600 cases, a prediction that unfortunately proved to be
accurate.
During the second week after its exposure to infection
the battalion was kept under close observation, and on 15th
October, thirteen days after the battalion had gone to Eaton
Socon, one of the men of E Company was reported to be
suffering from measles. The patient was at once removed
to the County Isolation Hospital at Oakley, and the men
who had been in contact with him were segregated and carefully
watched. Sanction was obtained for the erection of wooden
huts at Howbury, four miles from Bedford, to accommodate
contacts for isolation purposes, and for the erection of a wooden
block at Oakley Hospital for overflow measles cases, and the
building of these was immediately proceeded with.

were no more, but on 26th October two cases were discovered
among the contacts and removed to hospital. The whole of
E Company, the infected Company, were then sent to Howbury
for isolation, where they were accommodated in tents pending
the construction of the huts. In this Company fresh cases
now occurred almost daily, and it soon became evident that
the building of huts at Oakley Hospital would not keep pace
with the spread of the epidemic, as in spite of our precautions
the disease broke out in other companies of the 8th Argyll and
Sutherland Highlanders. The whole battalion was sent into
quarantine at Howbury; but it was a forlorn hope, we did not
really expect that the disease could be confined to the one
battalion. To cope with the rapidly increasing number of cases,
ample and quickly improvised hospital accommodation was
necessary, and accordingly a survey of the various public schools
in Bedford was made, the final choice falling on Goldington
Road School, a modern building on the one-story block system
with large and airy rooms.
By the middle of November the available hospitals were
almost full, and the Education Committee of Bedford Town
Council was asked for permission to take over the school in
Goldington Road. Our request was granted at once, and the
school was cleared out and made ready for occupation as a
hospital, being placed under the charge of Major D. Rorie
of the 2nd Highland Field Ambulance, with a staff of trained
orderlies from his unit. The furnishing and equipment of the
hospital, which had to be done speedily, presented some difficulty.
Officially it should have been done through the Divisional
Ordnance Department; but as that would have involved a delay
of a week or two I resolved to purchase locally, and was fortunate
in finding a tradesman who not only supplied our immediate
wants but was able throughout the epidemic to meet all our
demands promptly and efficiently. So the furnishing of the
hospital went on satisfactorily, and meanwhile baths were being
put in and a kitchen was established in a shelter in the play-
ground, with gas cookers which we had connected with a gas
main in the roadway outside, as there was only electric light
in the school.
All initial difficulties and troubles having been overcome,
the Goldington Road Hospital, fully equipped, was opened for
the reception of patients on 21st November 1914, and, as it
happened, just in time. The epidemic had hitherto been

confined to the 8th Argyll and Sutherlands, but on the day
the hospital was opened a case was reported from the 4 th
Camerons, and in a day or two cases came in from other units ;
the measles had taken a firm hold. Still, we were now well
prepared with a good hospital of 150 beds, well equipped,
with baths and kitchen facilities, and with an intelligent staff
of nursing orderlies. Cases now came in in increasing numbers
from various units, especially the Mountain Brigade of Artillery
and the 4th Camerons, and the hospital got busy.
At the end of the first week of December the number of
discharges began to equal that of admissions, and we hoped
the worst was over; but the epidemic was only taking breath
for a bigger effort. In another day or two cases began to
pour in, more and more every day, until on 22nd December
there were 23 admissions, the maximum number for one day,
and the number of patients in hospital was 143. The epidemic
got to be almost pandemic throughout the Division. In the first
week of January there was very little sign of abatement, and
as by this time Goldington Road was nearly full, we resolved
to open another hospital. Again we approached the Town
Council for the use of another school, and again they agreed
without demur and told us to take our choice. This time
we fixed on Ampthill Road School which, although not on
the one-story block system, like Goldington Road, was an
excellent modern building and turned out to be an admirable
hospital. Major A. E. Kidd, 3rd Highland Field Ambulance,
was put in charge; he had it promptly cleared out. It was as
promptly furnished by my excellent tradesman, and was
opened for the reception of patients on nth January 1915,
two days after it was taken over from the school authorities.
It proved to be a better and more compact hospital than
Goldington Road, so all the new cases were taken there and
Goldington Road was gradually closed down. By the end of
January the epidemic was practically over; there were only
five admissions from the Division during the whole of February,
although there were other five from the depot of the Bedfordshire
Regiment?for the epidemic- was all over the place?and the
last admission was on 6th March.
The peculiar interest of this epidemic lay in the fact that
it attacked a specially susceptible body of men brought together
in unusually large numbers in one place. Not only had these
men from the Highlands and Islands never had measles them-

selves, but their forefathers had had no practical acquaintance
with it; they were about as little salted to the disease as the Fiji
Islanders in their great epidemic of last century, and it went
very hard with them. Then, as if the severity of the disease
itself was not enough, it was accompanied by several serious
complications. The first and most serious of these was
broncho-pneumonia of a septic type, which occurred in a
large proportion of the cases, and in the majority of these
instances was concomitant; the pneumonia was present as well
as the measles when the men were admitted into hospital.
These cases were naturally very grave and most of the
fatalities occurred among them.
Matters were further complicated by an outbreak of scarlet
fever. Fortunately it was not very extensive, but a good many
men. had both measles and scarlet fever concurrently, or
following each other. There were also a few cases of
diphtheria along with the measles.
There was one other complication which was rather of
the nature of a neurosis like the " Morbus Shetlandicus."
Many of these poor fellows lost heart, or never seemed to have
any heart, made no effort to get well, lost all interest in life,
and simply turned their faces to the wall and gave up. We
lost several men in this way, and to obviate as far as possible
further similar fatalities, representations were made to the
War Office to allow relatives of the sick men to visit them.
These were successful, and at the expense of the Government
fathers and mothers came from all parts of the Highlands
to Bedford and sat by their boys. This had a very good
effect and was instrumental in saving a number of lives. I
know personally of two cases where the men seemed almost
moribund when their mothers arrived, whereupon they
brightened up, began to take a more cheerful view of affairs,
and ultimately recovered.
Needless to say the epidemic aroused considerable attention
on the part of the Government, and our hospitals were frequently
inspected by various officers. One practical outcome of their
visitations was that, on the suggestion of Colonel Dewar, they
got the War Office to send down a staff of nursing sisters from
the Army Nursing Service, who took up duty on nth January,
just when our second hospital was opened, and added greatly to
the efficiency of the nursing department.

12-3 per cent., which is not very excessive considering the type
of patient and the complications we had to contend with.
Seven of the 65 deaths were due to scarlet fever, accompanying
or following measles, and one to diphtheria.
As was to be expected, the disease attacked with most
severity the troops from the remoter Highlands; the 4th
Camerons (Inverness-shire and Skye) suffered most, with 139
cases and 21 deaths; next, the 8th Argyll and Sutherlands
(Argyll and its islands), 102 cases, 5 deaths; then the 5th
Seaforths (Sutherland and Caithness-shires), 73 cases, 14 deaths.
The largest case mortality was in the 6th Gordons (Donside
and Banffshire), with 44 cases and 11 deaths (25 per cent.);
three of these 11 fatal cases, however, had scarlet fever as a
complication. At the other end of the scale we have the more
Lowland battalions of the Division, such as the 4th Gordons
(City of Aberdeen), with 3 cases and no deaths; the 7th Argyll
and Sutherlands (Stirling, Kinross, and Clackmannan shires), and
the 9th Argyll and Sutherlands (Dumbartonshire), with 2
cases each and no deaths; and the 6th Argyll and Suther-

One last point of interest to be noted about this epidemic is
that it went to confirm the opinion that you do not take measles
twice, a view very prevalent in this country, although I notice
that in America it is claimed that it frequently occurs more
than once in the same individual. Not one of these 529 men
had ever had measles before. We used to compare the list of
admissions with the names on the census taken just before
the epidemic began, and the result was very striking. I
shall give as an example the figures for the 8th Argyll
and Sutherlands which contained, according to the census,
104 men who had never had measles; there were 102 cases
of measles in the battalion and they all came from that list
of 104.
