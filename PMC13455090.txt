Proteomic Biomarker Discovery in Breast Cancer: Advances, Challenges, and Translational Prospects
Authors: Mahan Hassani, Iman Morshedi, K J Matmurotov, Isanova Shoira Tulkinovna, Abdullaev Makhmudjon Mukhamedovich, Mahdieh Khorsandi, Abdiyev Rasul Ernazar Ugli, Tianyao Yang, Xi Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBreast cancer remains a leading cause of cancer‐related morbidity and mortality among women worldwide. Despite major progress in genomic classification, molecular pathology, and targeted therapy, the identification of clinically reliable proteomics‐derived biomarkers for early detection, prognostic stratification, treatment response prediction, and longitudinal monitoring remains an unresolved translational challenge. Recent studies have applied proteomic profiling to breast cancer tissues and clinically accessible biofluids, including serum, plasma, saliva, nipple aspirate fluid, urine, and extracellular vesicle‐enriched fractions, to identify protein signatures with potential diagnostic and therapeutic relevance. Contemporary platforms, including LC‐MS/MS, data‐independent acquisition mass spectrometry, multiplexed quantitative proteomics, targeted proteomics using multiple reaction monitoring and parallel reaction monitoring, spatial proteomics, and affinity‐based high‐throughput assays such as Olink and SomaScan, have expanded the analytical depth and clinical scalability of breast cancer biomarker research. However, many candidate biomarkers remain confined to discovery or early verification stages because of limited analytical sensitivity, insufficient specificity, inter‐platform variability, small and heterogeneous cohorts, incomplete external validation, and uncertainty regarding clinical utility beyond established pathological markers. This review critically evaluates current proteomic technologies and proteomics‐derived biomarker candidates in breast cancer, distinguishes discovery‐level findings from clinically validated evidence, and discusses the major methodological, regulatory, and implementation barriers that continue to limit translation into routine oncology practice. Greater emphasis on standardized workflows, targeted verification, multi‐center validation, and clinically actionable biomarker panels will be essential for integrating proteomics into precision breast cancer care.Keywords: biomarker validation, breast cancer, clinical translation, mass spectrometry, protein biomarkers, proteomics

Introduction

The human genome contains approximately 20,000 protein‐coding genes [1], yet the functional complexity of human biology cannot be inferred from gene number alone. Alternative splicing, protein isoforms, proteolytic processing, and post‐translational modifications, including phosphorylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, glycosylation, and proteolytic cleavage, substantially expand proteomic diversity and generate dynamic molecular states that are not fully captured by genomic or transcriptomic analyses [2, 3]. Proteomics therefore provides a functional layer between genomic information and cellular phenotype, allowing direct investigation of protein abundance, pathway activation, post‐translational regulation, tumor microenvironment signaling, and treatment‐associated molecular adaptation [4]. Breast cancer remains one of the most common malignancies among women worldwide and continues to impose a major clinical and public health burden. In 2022, approximately 2.3 million women were diagnosed with breast cancer globally, and the disease caused about 670,000 deaths [5]. Although screening programs, molecular pathology, endocrine therapy, HER2‐directed treatment, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted agents have improved patient outcomes, breast cancer remains biologically heterogeneous, and a substantial proportion of patients still experience recurrence, metastatic progression, or treatment resistance [6, 7]. Current clinical biomarkers, including estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, HER2, and Ki‐67, are indispensable for routine classification and treatment selection, but they do not fully resolve early detection, dynamic treatment monitoring, minimal residual disease assessment, or precise prediction of therapeutic response across molecular subtypes [8, 9].

This unmet need has stimulated increasing interest in breast cancer proteomics across tumor tissues and clinically accessible biofluids, including serum, plasma, saliva, nipple aspirate fluid, urine, and extracellular vesicle‐enriched fractions [10, 11]. Unlike antibody‐based clinical assays that usually measure predefined targets, discovery proteomics can identify previously unrecognized protein signatures and pathway‐level alterations associated with tumor biology, disease progression, metastatic potential, and therapeutic response [4, 12]. Recent advances in liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry, data‐independent acquisition, multiplexed quantitative proteomics, targeted proteomics, spatial proteomics, and high‐throughput affinity‐based platforms have expanded the analytical depth and translational potential of proteomic biomarker research [4, 13] However, the central challenge has shifted from biomarker discovery alone to reproducible verification, analytical validation, clinical validation, and demonstration of added clinical utility beyond established pathological markers [12, 14].

This review focuses primarily on proteomics‐derived and proteomics‐validated biomarkers in breast cancer. Established markers such as estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, HER2, and Ki‐67 are discussed only as clinical benchmarks for evaluating the translational readiness of newer proteomic candidates, rather than as biomarkers discovered through modern proteomic platforms. The review critically examines current proteomic technologies, evaluates candidate biomarkers according to the strength of proteomic evidence, and discusses the analytical, biological, regulatory, and implementation barriers that continue to limit translation into routine oncology practice. By distinguishing discovery‐level findings from clinically actionable evidence, this review aims to clarify how proteomic biomarker research can move from exploratory profiling toward standardized, validated, and clinically useful applications in precision breast cancer care.

Proteomics Technologies

Proteomic biomarker research in breast cancer relies on a range of analytical platforms with different strengths, limitations, and levels of translational readiness [12, 15]. For clinical biomarker development, it is essential to distinguish discovery platforms from verification and validation platforms [15, 16]. Discovery proteomics is primarily used to identify candidate proteins or protein signatures without requiring prior knowledge of the target, whereas targeted proteomics and antibody‐based assays are used to confirm, quantify, and clinically validate predefined candidates [14, 15]. This distinction is particularly important because a protein identified in an exploratory proteomic experiment should not be interpreted as a clinically actionable biomarker until it has undergone reproducible verification, analytical validation, independent clinical validation, and assessment of added clinical utility beyond established pathological markers [14, 16].

Polyacrylamide Gel‐Based Techniques: 2‐DE and 2‐DE DIGE

Two‐dimensional electrophoresis (2‐DE) and two‐dimensional fluorescence difference gel electrophoresis (2‐DE DIGE) were among the earliest comparative proteomic approaches used to separate complex protein mixtures [17]. In 2‐DE, proteins are first separated according to their isoelectric point and then according to molecular weight, generating a two‐dimensional protein map that can be visualized by staining or fluorescence‐based detection methods [18]. In 2‐DE DIGE, two or three protein samples are labeled with spectrally distinct fluorescent dyes, such as Cy2, Cy3, and Cy5, and separated on the same gel, which improves direct comparison between experimental groups and reduces gel‐to‐gel variation [19]. The introduction of immobilized pH gradients further improved reproducibility and standardization in gel‐based protein separation [17, 20, 21].

Although 2‐DE and 2‐DE DIGE contributed substantially to early cancer proteomics, their current role in breast cancer biomarker discovery is more limited than that of advanced mass spectrometry‐based platforms [22]. Gel‐based approaches are less effective for low‐abundance proteins, hydrophobic membrane proteins, proteins with extreme molecular weights or isoelectric points, and complex biofluid samples with a broad dynamic concentration range [22, 23]. They also require downstream spot excision and protein identification, which limits throughput and complicates inter‐laboratory reproducibility [17]. Therefore, gel‐based proteomics is best regarded as a historical and complementary approach for comparative protein profiling rather than a primary platform for contemporary clinical biomarker discovery [15, 22].

Discovery Mass Spectrometry‐Based Proteomics

Mass spectrometry (MS)‐based proteomics has become central to modern biomarker discovery because it enables large‐scale identification and quantification of proteins and peptides in complex biological samples [15]. A typical MS workflow includes protein extraction, enzymatic digestion, peptide separation, ionization, mass analysis, peptide‐spectrum matching, protein inference, and bioinformatic interpretation [15, 24, 25]. In breast cancer research, liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) has been applied to tumor tissue and clinically accessible biofluids, including serum, plasma, nipple aspirate fluid, urine, and extracellular vesicle‐enriched fractions, to identify protein signatures associated with tumor subtype, progression, metastasis, and treatment response [26, 27].

Contemporary discovery proteomics increasingly uses data‐dependent acquisition (DDA), data‐independent acquisition (DIA), label‐free quantification, and multiplexed isobaric labeling strategies such as tandem mass tag (TMT) and isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantitation (iTRAQ) [28, 29]. DDA can provide deep peptide sequencing but may show reduced reproducibility for low‐abundance peptides across large clinical cohorts. DIA systematically fragments peptides across predefined mass‐to‐charge windows and can improve quantitative consistency, making it particularly useful for cohort‐scale cancer proteomics and biomarker prioritization [29, 30]. TMT and iTRAQ enable multiplexed relative quantification across multiple samples and have been used to compare breast cancer tissues with different clinicopathological characteristics [28, 31]. However, discovery MS remains vulnerable to pre‐analytical variation, batch effects, missing values, false discovery, and overfitting when small heterogeneous cohorts are analyzed [10, 16]. For this reason, MS‐based discovery should be followed by targeted verification and independent validation before candidate proteins are advanced as clinically meaningful biomarkers [15].

Targeted Proteomics for Biomarker Verification and Validation

Targeted proteomics provides a critical bridge between discovery‐based proteomics and clinically deployable biomarker assays [14, 32]. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM), also called selected reaction monitoring, quantifies predefined peptides using triple quadrupole mass spectrometry [15, 32]. Parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) measures predefined precursor ions and records multiple fragment ions using high‐resolution mass spectrometry [32, 33]. These methods are particularly valuable after discovery proteomics has generated a shortlist of candidate proteins that require reproducible quantification across larger and independent sample sets [14, 32].

MRM offers strong quantitative reproducibility, high analytical sensitivity, and compatibility with stable isotope‐labeled peptide standards, allowing precise relative or absolute quantification of candidate biomarkers in complex biological matrices [14, 32]. PRM provides high selectivity and simplified assay development because multiple fragment ions can be monitored with high mass accuracy, reducing the risk of interference from co‐eluting peptides [32, 33]. In breast cancer biomarker research, MRM and PRM are especially relevant for verifying candidate proteins detected by LC‐MS/MS, DIA‐MS, TMT, or iTRAQ workflows before they are advanced to large‐scale clinical validation [14, 33]. Their limitations include the need for careful peptide selection, optimization of transitions or fragment ions, control of matrix effects, harmonization across instruments, and validation of analytical performance parameters such as limit of detection, limit of quantification, precision, linearity, and reproducibility [14].

Affinity‐Based High‐Throughput Proteomics: Olink and SomaScan

High‐throughput affinity‐based proteomic platforms have expanded the ability to measure large numbers of proteins in small volumes of plasma or serum and are increasingly used in translational biomarker research [13, 34]. Olink is based on proximity extension assay technology, in which paired antibody‐linked oligonucleotides generate a DNA reporter signal when both antibodies bind the same target protein [35]. SomaScan uses modified DNA aptamers to bind protein targets and enables broad multiplexed protein measurement across large cohorts [34]. These platforms are particularly useful for population‐scale protein profiling, biomarker panel development, and integration of proteomic signatures with clinical, genomic, imaging, and pathological data [13, 34].

Despite their scalability, Olink and SomaScan should not be interpreted as direct replacements for MS‐based discovery or clinical‐grade validation [13, 34]. Their outputs are influenced by reagent specificity, protein isoforms, epitope accessibility, binding affinity, platform‐specific calibration, and batch effects [34]. In addition, signals from affinity‐based assays may not always correspond directly to absolute protein abundance [13, 34]. Therefore, candidate biomarkers identified by Olink or SomaScan require orthogonal confirmation using targeted MS, ELISA, RPPA, immunohistochemistry, or other validated assays before clinical interpretation [14, 34].

Orthogonal Validation and Tissue‐Based Proteomic Methods

Antibody‐based and array‐based platforms remain important for confirming candidate biomarkers, evaluating tissue localization, and assessing pathway‐level relevance [36]. Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), western blotting, immunohistochemistry (IHC), tissue microarray, protein microarray, and reverse phase protein array (RPPA) are commonly used for targeted assessment of predefined proteins [37, 38, 39]. These methods are not unbiased discovery platforms because they depend on prior target selection and reagent specificity, but they are essential for verification and validation workflows [36]. ELISA is useful for quantitative measurement of soluble proteins in biofluids, western blotting can support mechanistic confirmation of protein abundance or post‐translational modification, IHC provides spatial and pathological context in tissue sections, and RPPA enables high‐throughput assessment of signaling proteins and phosphorylated targets across large sample sets [36, 40].

The major limitation of antibody‐based validation is dependence on antibody quality, assay calibration, scoring reproducibility, and inter‐laboratory standardization. IHC remains indispensable for established clinical markers such as estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and HER2, but these markers should be interpreted as clinical benchmarks rather than proteomics‐discovered biomarkers. Similarly, ELISA and RPPA are best used to validate predefined proteins or pathway states rather than to generate unbiased biomarker discovery claims [40]. A robust translational workflow should therefore move from discovery proteomics to targeted verification, followed by orthogonal validation and testing in independent clinical cohorts [14, 32].

Critical Comparison of Major Proteomic and Validation Platforms

No single technology is sufficient for breast cancer biomarker discovery, verification, validation, and implementation [13, 14]. Discovery MS provides broad proteome coverage but requires targeted confirmation; MRM and PRM provide reproducible quantification but depend on predefined targets; Olink and SomaScan provide scalable multiplexed profiling but require orthogonal confirmation; and antibody‐based methods provide clinical practicality but are restricted by reagent specificity and limited discovery capacity [14, 32, 34]. The selection of a platform should therefore be guided by the intended stage of biomarker development, the biological matrix being analyzed, the required sensitivity and specificity, the need for multiplexing, and the level of clinical readiness expected from the assay [13, 14]. Protein Profiling for Diagnostic Marker Discovery

Mass spectrometry‐based protein profiling has been widely used in breast cancer research to identify diagnostic signatures that reflect tumor presence, molecular subtype, and biologically relevant differences between malignant and non‐malignant tissues [12, 46]. In contrast to routine clinical markers such as estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, HER2, and Ki‐67, which are mainly assessed by immunohistochemistry or fluorescence in situ hybridization, proteomics‐derived diagnostic candidates are generated through unbiased or semi‐unbiased protein profiling workflows and require subsequent verification before clinical interpretation [32, 47]. This distinction is essential because the presence of a clinically established protein marker does not necessarily mean that the marker was discovered through proteomic technologies [8, 44].

Tissue‐based proteomics remains a major approach for diagnostic discovery because tumor specimens contain proteins derived from malignant epithelial cells, stromal compartments, vascular elements, immune infiltrates, and extracellular matrix remodeling [47, 48]. Recent LC‐MS/MS, label‐free quantification, isobaric labeling, and data‐independent acquisition workflows have enabled deeper characterization of breast cancer tissue proteomes and have supported protein‐level stratification of tumors according to subtype, pathway activation, and microenvironmental composition [29, 47, 49]. Proteomic studies have shown that breast cancer subtypes are not only defined by genomic or transcriptomic programs but also by differences in protein abundance, phosphorylation patterns, metabolic regulation, immune‐related proteins, and stromal signatures [47, 49]. These findings suggest that proteomic profiling may complement routine pathology by capturing functional tumor states that are not fully inferred from DNA or RNA‐based classification alone [47, 48].

However, tissue‐based diagnostic proteomics is strongly affected by intratumoral heterogeneity [50]. A protein signal detected in whole‐tissue lysates may originate from cancer cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, immune cells, necrotic regions, or extracellular matrix components [47, 50]. This cellular mixture can reduce diagnostic specificity if candidate biomarkers are not linked to their cellular source or pathological context [50, 51]. Laser capture microdissection, imaging mass spectrometry, spatial proteomics, and integration with histopathology can partly address this issue by enriching tumor‐specific regions or preserving spatial information [50, 51]. These approaches improve biological interpretability but also increase technical complexity, cost, and workflow variability, which limits immediate scalability for routine diagnostic laboratories [50].

Biofluid proteomics provides a complementary route for minimally invasive diagnostic biomarker discovery [12]. Serum, plasma, nipple aspirate fluid, urine, and extracellular vesicle‐enriched fractions may contain proteins released by tumor cells, stromal cells, immune compartments, or systemic host responses [52, 53]. Among these matrices, plasma and serum are clinically accessible but analytically challenging because high‐abundance proteins can mask low‐abundance tumor‐derived signals [4, 12]. Nipple aspirate fluid may more directly reflect local breast ductal biology, whereas extracellular vesicle‐associated proteins may enrich tumor‐relevant information, although isolation and normalization methods remain insufficiently standardized [4, 52, 53]. These differences mean that diagnostic performance cannot be assumed to be equivalent across sample types [12, 53]. Several candidate diagnostic proteins and protein panels have been reported in breast cancer proteomic studies, including proteins involved in cytoskeletal remodeling, cell adhesion, metabolism, stress response, immune regulation, extracellular matrix organization, and vesicle‐mediated communication [47, 53]. Nevertheless, most candidate diagnostic markers remain at the discovery or early verification stage [12, 32]. Their clinical translation is limited by small cohorts, inconsistent sample processing, incomplete external validation, lack of harmonized analytical pipelines, and insufficient evidence that they improve diagnostic accuracy beyond mammography, histopathology, and established tissue markers [12, 32, 54]. For diagnostic application, proteomics‐derived markers should therefore be evaluated not only for statistical significance but also for sensitivity, specificity, assay reproducibility, subtype relevance, and added clinical value in independent patient populations [12, 54].

Among historically reported candidates, proteins such as 14‐3‐3σ illustrate the difficulty of translating biologically plausible findings into reliable diagnostic tools. 14‐3‐3σ has been linked to cell‐cycle regulation and tumor‐suppressive mechanisms, but reported expression patterns in breast cancer have not been fully consistent across studies. Such inconsistency highlights a recurring limitation in diagnostic proteomics: candidate proteins identified in exploratory cohorts may fail to retain robust performance when evaluated using different sample types, analytical platforms, patient populations, or validation assays [12, 54]. Future diagnostic studies should prioritize proteomics‐derived candidates that can be measured reproducibly by targeted MS, ELISA, RPPA, immunohistochemistry, or other clinically adaptable platforms and should report diagnostic performance using standardized metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve, and positive and negative predictive values [32, 54].

Predictive and Prognostic Proteomic Markers

Proteomic studies of predictive and prognostic biomarkers in breast cancer aim to stratify patients according to recurrence risk, survival outcome, treatment sensitivity, therapeutic resistance, and metastatic potential [4, 47]. Unlike diagnostic biomarkers, which focus on distinguishing disease from non‐disease or subtype from subtype, predictive and prognostic proteomic markers must be linked to clinically meaningful outcomes such as pathological complete response, disease‐free survival, overall survival, relapse risk, or benefit from a specific therapy [4, 55]. This requirement makes their validation more demanding because outcome‐associated protein signatures must remain reproducible across treatment regimens, tumor subtypes, sample handling protocols, and independent clinical cohorts [14, 47].

Early proteomic studies identified several proteins associated with breast cancer subtype and outcome, including cytoskeletal proteins, molecular chaperones, immune‐related proteins, adhesion molecules, metabolic enzymes, and signaling intermediates [28, 46]. More recent proteomic and proteogenomic studies have expanded this view by showing that protein‐level and phosphoprotein‐level alterations can reveal pathway activity, immune infiltration, stromal remodeling, and therapeutic vulnerabilities that are not always evident from genomic or transcriptomic data alone [46]. These studies support the biological relevance of proteomic profiling for risk stratification, but many reported candidates remain associative rather than clinically validated [14]. A marker should not be considered prognostic merely because its expression differs between tumor groups; it must demonstrate independent association with outcome after adjustment for established clinicopathological variables and must show reproducibility in external cohorts [47].

Prediction of therapy response remains one of the most clinically important but difficult applications of breast cancer proteomics [4]. Reliable predictive biomarkers could reduce exposure to ineffective therapy, minimize toxicity, and guide the selection of chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, HER2‐directed therapy, immunotherapy, or targeted agents [12]. Proteomic analyses of pretreatment and post‐treatment tumor samples have shown that response and resistance are often associated with coordinated pathway changes rather than single‐protein alterations [28]. These include DNA damage response, cell‐cycle regulation, immune activation, apoptosis, extracellular matrix remodeling, metabolic adaptation, and stress‐response pathways. This pathway‐level complexity partly explains why single candidate proteins have rarely shown sufficient predictive performance for clinical implementation [4, 14].

Triple‐negative breast cancer has been a major focus of predictive proteomic research because of its molecular heterogeneity and variable response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy [55, 56]. Recent proteogenomic analyses have shown that chemotherapy response and resistance in triple‐negative breast cancer are associated with integrated alterations in protein expression, phosphoprotein signaling, copy‐number changes, immune activity, and DNA repair‐related pathways [55, 56]. These findings suggest that predictive models may need to combine proteomic markers with genomic, transcriptomic, immune, and pathological features rather than relying on isolated protein candidates [4, 55]. However, such integrated models require prospective testing, standardized sample acquisition, analytically validated assays, and clear evidence that they can improve treatment decisions [14, 32].

Endocrine response and resistance also remain important areas for proteomic biomarker development [4, 12]. Estrogen receptor status is the dominant clinical predictor of endocrine responsiveness, but ER positivity alone does not fully explain differences in treatment benefit or acquired resistance [8]. Proteomic studies have investigated proteins related to heat shock response, PI3K‐AKT‐mTOR signaling, cell‐cycle regulation, apoptosis, and adaptive stress pathways in endocrine‐treated tumors [57]. For example, altered expression of heat shock proteins has been associated with treatment response and adverse clinicopathological features in some studies, but the direction and strength of these associations appear context‐dependent [58]. HSP‐70, HSP‐60, and HSP‐90 are biologically plausible candidates because they regulate protein folding, stress tolerance, oncogenic signaling, and apoptosis, yet they are not sufficiently standardized as routine predictive or prognostic biomarkers in breast cancer [57, 58].

Several methodological barriers continue to limit the clinical use of predictive and prognostic proteomic markers [14]. Many studies are underpowered, retrospective, and heterogeneous in terms of tumor subtype, treatment exposure, sample processing, proteomic platform, statistical modeling, and validation design [47]. In addition, some candidate signatures are derived from comparisons between tumor and normal tissue rather than from longitudinal treatment‐response cohorts, which weakens their direct predictive relevance [4, 12]. Predictive biomarkers also require higher evidentiary standards than discovery markers because they must influence treatment selection and demonstrate clinical utility [32]. A reproducible association with outcome is not sufficient if the marker does not improve decision‐making beyond established variables such as tumor stage, grade, ER, PR, HER2, Ki‐67, nodal status, genomic assays, and treatment context [4, 8].

The future development of predictive and prognostic proteomic markers should therefore prioritize targeted verification and clinically structured validation [14, 32]. Candidate proteins identified by discovery MS, DIA‐MS, TMT/iTRAQ, spatial proteomics, Olink, SomaScan, or proteogenomic workflows should be advanced through MRM, PRM, ELISA, RPPA, or immunohistochemistry‐based validation in independent cohorts with clearly defined endpoints [14, 32]. Studies should report whether a marker adds independent prognostic or predictive value, whether it performs consistently across breast cancer subtypes, and whether it can be implemented using a reproducible assay compatible with clinical laboratory workflows [4, 14].

Established Clinical Biomarkers as Benchmarks for Proteomic Translation

Established breast cancer biomarkers remain central to routine classification, prognostic assessment, and treatment selection [8]. However, estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, HER2, and Ki‐67 were not primarily discovered through modern proteomic discovery platforms and should therefore be interpreted in this review as clinical benchmarks rather than proteomics‐derived biomarkers [44]. Their inclusion is useful because they illustrate the level of analytical validation, clinical utility, assay standardization, and therapeutic actionability that emerging proteomic candidates must achieve before routine implementation [8, 44, 59].

Estrogen Receptor and Progesterone Receptor

Estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) are routinely assessed by validated immunohistochemistry in invasive breast cancer and remain essential for identifying patients who may benefit from endocrine therapy [8, 60, 61]. ER‐positive tumors are generally associated with endocrine responsiveness and more favorable clinical behavior than ER‐negative tumors, although the magnitude of endocrine benefit may vary according to the percentage of ER‐positive tumor nuclei and broader tumor biology [8, 9]. PR is commonly interpreted together with ER because it reflects functional estrogen signaling and provides additional prognostic information, particularly in hormone receptor‐positive disease [62, 63, 64]. From a proteomic biomarker perspective, ER and PR should not be presented as proteomics‐discovered markers; instead, they provide a clinical reference point for evaluating whether newer proteomics‐derived candidates offer additional diagnostic, prognostic, or predictive value beyond established hormone receptor testing [4, 8].

Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2

HER2 is a clinically actionable biomarker in breast cancer because HER2 protein overexpression or ERBB2 gene amplification identifies patients who may benefit from HER2‐directed therapies [44]. HER2 status is routinely assessed using immunohistochemistry for protein expression and in situ hybridization when gene amplification needs to be clarified [65, 66]. HER2‐positive disease has historically been associated with aggressive tumor behavior, but HER2‐targeted therapies have substantially improved outcomes in appropriately selected patients [9]. HER2 therefore represents a model of successful biomarker translation, linking a measurable molecular alteration to a defined therapeutic strategy. For emerging proteomic biomarkers, the translational standard is not simply differential expression, but reproducible measurement, validated clinical association, and evidence that the biomarker can guide a treatment decision or improve patient stratification [4].

Ki‐67 Proliferation Index

Ki‐67 is a nuclear proliferation marker assessed by immunohistochemistry and is used to support prognostic evaluation and treatment decision‐making in selected breast cancer settings, especially hormone receptor‐positive, HER2‐negative disease [67, 68, 69]. Higher Ki‐67 expression is generally associated with greater proliferative activity and less favorable prognosis, but its clinical use is constrained by pre‐analytical variation, staining differences, scoring variability, and inconsistent cut‐off selection [59]. These limitations are particularly relevant to proteomic biomarker development because they show that even widely used protein‐based markers can face major implementation challenges when assay standardization and reproducibility are incomplete. Emerging proteomics‐derived biomarkers must therefore demonstrate not only biological relevance but also robust analytical performance, reproducible scoring or quantification, and added clinical utility beyond Ki‐67 and other established pathological variables [4, 59, 70].

Emerging Proteomics‐Derived Biomarker Candidates and Protein Signatures

Emerging biomarker research in breast cancer has increasingly shifted from single‐marker discovery toward proteomics‐derived protein signatures that reflect tumor subtype, immune activity, stromal remodeling, metabolic adaptation, extracellular vesicle cargo, and treatment‐associated pathway states [4, 47, 71]. In this review, emerging biomarkers are considered relevant only when they have been identified, prioritized, or verified through proteomic platforms such as LC‐MS/MS, DIA‐MS, TMT, or iTRAQ‐based quantitative proteomics, MRM, PRM, RPPA, Olink, SomaScan, spatial proteomics, or extracellular vesicle proteomics. Biomarkers that are primarily supported by immunohistochemistry, mRNA expression, genetic testing, or routine clinical pathology are not presented here as proteomics‐derived discoveries unless direct proteomic evidence is available.

Tissue Proteome‐Derived Candidates

Tissue‐based proteomic studies have identified candidate protein patterns that distinguish breast cancer subtypes and reveal biological heterogeneity beyond conventional clinicopathological classification [47, 48, 72]. Large‐scale mass spectrometry‐based analyses of formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded and fresh‐frozen breast tumors have shown that breast cancers can be stratified according to protein‐level features related to immune activation, extracellular matrix organization, lipid metabolism, stromal enrichment, epithelial differentiation, and proliferation‐associated pathways. These findings are important because they show that protein‐level classification can provide functional information that is not always captured by DNA or RNA‐based subtyping [73].

Several tissue proteomic candidates are related to cytoskeletal remodeling, cell adhesion, metabolic regulation, molecular chaperone activity, immune signaling, and extracellular matrix remodeling [56]. However, the translational relevance of these candidates depends on whether they remain reproducible across independent cohorts and whether they can be measured by targeted MS, RPPA, ELISA, immunohistochemistry, or another clinically adaptable assay [74, 75]. A candidate protein should therefore be interpreted as discovery‐level evidence unless it has undergone verification in an independent sample set and has demonstrated diagnostic, prognostic, or predictive value beyond established clinical variables [14].

Immune and Stromal Protein Signatures

Immune and stromal protein signatures have become increasingly important in breast cancer proteomics because the tumor microenvironment strongly influences prognosis and treatment response [76]. Proteomic studies have shown that immune‐hot and immune‐cold tumor states, extracellular matrix remodeling, stromal enrichment, and inflammatory pathway activation can separate biologically distinct groups of breast cancer, particularly within aggressive subtypes such as triple‐negative and HER2‐enriched disease [77]. These signatures may help identify tumors with favorable immune infiltration, mesenchymal features, or stromal‐dominant phenotypes [78].

The clinical value of immune and stromal signatures remains under active investigation [4]. A major limitation is that many proteins detected in bulk tissue proteomics may originate from non‐malignant cells rather than tumor cells, which can complicate interpretation if spatial context is not preserved [79]. Spatial proteomics, imaging mass spectrometry, RPPA, and integration with digital pathology may improve localization and biological interpretation. For clinical translation, immune and stromal proteomic signatures should be tested for their ability to improve prognostic stratification or treatment selection beyond tumor stage, grade, receptor status, Ki‐67, and current genomic assays [80].

Biofluid and Extracellular Vesicle Protein Candidates

Biofluid proteomics offers a minimally invasive strategy for discovering breast cancer biomarkers in serum, plasma, nipple aspirate fluid, urine, and extracellular vesicle‐enriched fractions [12, 53]. Compared with tissue proteomics, biofluid proteomics is more compatible with screening, longitudinal monitoring, recurrence surveillance, and treatment‐response assessment, but it faces major analytical challenges because tumor‐derived proteins are often present at low abundance against a background of highly abundant circulating proteins [4, 81, 82]. Depletion strategies, fractionation, extracellular vesicle enrichment, DIA‐MS, targeted MS, and affinity‐based multiplexed platforms may improve detection of clinically informative signals.

Extracellular vesicle proteomics is particularly promising because vesicles can carry tumor‐associated proteins, membrane receptors, adhesion molecules, immune modulators, and signaling proteins that may reflect tumor biology more directly than unfractionated plasma [53, 83]. Nevertheless, extracellular vesicle biomarker studies require strict standardization of vesicle isolation, purity assessment, normalization strategy, storage conditions, and analytical validation. Without these controls, apparent biomarker differences may reflect pre‐analytical variation rather than true tumor‐associated biology [53, 84]. Candidate EV‐associated proteins should therefore be verified using independent cohorts and orthogonal assays before being proposed for clinical use [85].

Proteogenomic and Multi‐Platform Biomarker Candidates

Proteogenomic and multi‐platform studies are increasingly used to connect protein abundance, phosphoprotein signaling, genomic alterations, transcriptomic subtypes, immune activity, and treatment response [86, 87]. This approach is particularly valuable in breast cancer because DNA or RNA alterations do not always predict protein abundance, pathway activation, or therapeutic vulnerability. Proteogenomic profiling can identify protein‐level markers of chemotherapy resistance, immune response, DNA repair activity, cell‐cycle regulation, and metabolic adaptation, especially in triple‐negative breast cancer [55].

Despite this potential, multi‐platform biomarkers are difficult to translate into routine practice [88]. The main barriers include assay complexity, cost, data integration requirements, lack of harmonized computational pipelines, and uncertainty about which platform should be used for clinical‐grade measurement [13, 14, 34]. Therefore, candidate biomarkers generated from proteogenomic, Olink, SomaScan, RPPA, spatial proteomic, or MS‐based discovery workflows should be prioritized according to biological plausibility, reproducibility, assay feasibility, and clinical decision relevance [34, 54].

Prioritization of Emerging Proteomic Biomarkers

Emerging proteomic biomarkers should be ranked according to evidence level rather than biological interest alone [89, 90]. A practical prioritization framework should distinguish discovery‐level candidates, technically verified candidates, independently validated biomarkers, and clinically actionable markers [90, 91]. Discovery‐level candidates are proteins or signatures identified in exploratory proteomic datasets. Technically verified candidates have been confirmed by targeted MS or an orthogonal assay in additional samples. Independently validated biomarkers show reproducible association with diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment response in external cohorts. Clinically actionable biomarkers demonstrate that their use improves decision‐making, patient stratification, or treatment selection [88, 89, 92]. This framework is important because many biologically plausible breast cancer proteins have been reported as potential biomarkers but have not progressed beyond early validation [93]. For emerging proteomics‐derived biomarkers to become clinically useful, future studies should report the proteomic platform used for discovery, the sample type, validation method, sensitivity, specificity, reproducibility, evidence level, and added clinical value beyond ER, PR, HER2, Ki‐67, tumor stage, grade, and existing molecular assays [88, 91, 92, 94]. Translational Challenges and Clinical Implementation

Methodological and Pre‐Analytical Sources of Variability

Proteomic biomarker discovery in breast cancer is highly sensitive to pre‐analytical variation, particularly when serum, plasma, tissue, nipple aspirate fluid, urine, or extracellular vesicle‐enriched fractions are used as discovery matrices [12, 13, 103]. In blood‐derived samples, coagulation activity, platelet activation, complement activation, hemolysis, freeze‐thaw cycles, and delayed processing can alter the measurable proteome and generate protein or peptide signals that do not necessarily reflect tumor biology [82, 83, 84]. Practical factors such as tube type, anticoagulant selection, clotting time, centrifugation conditions, storage temperature, storage duration, and time from collection to freezing can substantially influence protein abundance measurements [103, 104].

These variables are especially problematic for low‐abundance tumor‐derived proteins because small pre‐analytical differences may be amplified during downstream depletion, fractionation, digestion, LC‐MS/MS acquisition, or affinity‐based multiplexed profiling [13, 103]. For this reason, proteomic biomarker studies should report biospecimen collection and processing protocols in sufficient detail and should include pre‐analytical quality‐control metrics before candidate proteins are interpreted as disease‐associated signals [10, 14, 103]. Standardized operating procedures are particularly important for multi‐center studies, where differences in local sample handling can create site‐specific artifacts that mimic biological variation [14, 103].

Analytical Validity and Risk of False Discovery

High‐throughput proteomic discovery generates large and complex datasets, increasing the risk of false‐positive biomarker claims when cohort size is small, clinical groups are heterogeneous, or statistical correction is insufficient [105, 106]. Analytical variability may arise from protein extraction, enzymatic digestion, peptide recovery, chromatographic stability, ion suppression, instrument drift, database search parameters, normalization methods, batch correction, and missing‐value handling [13, 14]. A candidate protein should not be considered analytically reliable solely because it reaches statistical significance in a discovery dataset. Its identity, quantitative robustness, reproducibility, and measurement precision must be confirmed using transparent quality‐control procedures and independent technical or biological verification [14, 54].

For MS‐based studies, reporting should include peptide and protein identification criteria, false discovery rate thresholds, batch structure, internal standards, technical replicate performance, missing‐value strategy, and normalization procedures [13, 14, 107]. For targeted proteomics, analytical performance should be defined using parameters such as limit of detection, limit of quantification, linearity, intra‐assay and inter‐assay precision, carryover, matrix effects, and peptide‐level specificity. For affinity‐based platforms such as Olink, SomaScan, ELISA, and RPPA, validation should also address reagent specificity, calibration, cross‐reactivity, dynamic range, and inter‐laboratory reproducibility [34, 108, 109]. Analytical sensitivity alone insufficient for clinical translation if the detected signal cannot be reproducibly identified, quantified, and interpreted across platforms and patient cohorts [14, 109].

Sensitivity, Specificity, and Tumor Biological Interpretation

The clinical value of a proteomic biomarker depends not only on detectability but also on the balance between sensitivity and specificity [14]. A highly sensitive marker may detect many breast cancer cases but still perform poorly if it also reflects inflammation, tissue injury, coagulation, stromal remodeling, or non‐specific host response [10, 103]. Conversely, a highly specific tumor‐associated protein may be difficult to detect in blood if it is present at very low abundance, released intermittently, or masked by the wide dynamic range of the plasma proteome [53]. This trade‐off is central to breast cancer proteomic biomarker development and should be explicitly evaluated rather than assumed from differential abundance alone [12, 14].

Tumor specificity is further complicated by cellular heterogeneity within breast tumors. Proteins detected in bulk tissue lysates may originate from malignant epithelial cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, immune infiltrates, adipocytes, necrotic regions, or extracellular matrix components [47, 78]. This issue is particularly important for immune and stromal signatures, which may carry prognostic or therapeutic information but should not automatically be interpreted as tumor‐cell‐intrinsic biomarkers [47, 76]. Spatial proteomics, imaging mass spectrometry, laser capture microdissection, RPPA, extracellular vesicle profiling, and integration with histopathology can improve biological interpretation by linking protein signals to cellular or tissue context [78, 96]. For diagnostic use, candidate biomarkers should be evaluated using clinically meaningful performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and performance within molecular subtypes [12, 54].

Staged Validation Pipeline from Discovery to Clinical Utility

Translation from proteomic discovery to clinical application requires a staged validation framework [32, 110]. Discovery platforms such as LC‐MS/MS, DIA‐MS, TMT or iTRAQ‐based quantitative proteomics, Olink, SomaScan, RPPA, and spatial proteomics can identify candidate proteins, protein panels, phosphoprotein signatures, or pathway‐level alterations in exploratory cohorts [86, 87, 88, 89]. However, discovery alone is not equivalent to validation. Candidate biomarkers should first undergo technical verification using targeted proteomics, especially MRM or PRM, or another analytically suitable assay capable of reproducible quantification in the relevant matrix [14, 32].

After verification, biomarkers should be tested by orthogonal methods such as ELISA, RPPA, western blotting, immunohistochemistry, tissue microarray‐based analysis, or clinically adaptable targeted MS workflows [14, 32, 54]. The next step is analytical validation, which confirms that the assay accurately and reproducibly measures the intended analyte [108, 109]. Clinical validation then establishes that the biomarker is associated with a defined clinical state or outcome, such as diagnosis, recurrence risk, pathological complete response, disease‐free survival, overall survival, or treatment benefit [110, 111]. Clinical utility requires a higher level of evidence, demonstrating that biomarker use improves patient management, treatment selection, monitoring, or outcomes beyond existing clinical standards [110, 111]. Many breast cancer proteomic candidates fail because they move from discovery to publication without passing through this full sequence of verification, analytical validation, clinical validation, and utility testing [10, 12, 14].

Regulatory and Clinical Laboratory Implementation Challenges

Clinical implementation requires more than biological plausibility and statistical association. A proteomic biomarker intended for clinical use must be linked to a defined context of use, such as early detection, subtype refinement, prognostic stratification, prediction of therapy response, recurrence monitoring, or selection of a targeted treatment [108, 110]. The assay must also be compatible with routine laboratory requirements, including reproducible sample processing, acceptable turnaround time, cost feasibility, quality assurance, reporting standards, and interpretability for clinicians [109, 111].

Regulatory and implementation challenges are particularly important for multiplexed proteomic panels [34, 110]. Multi‐protein signatures may provide stronger biological information than single markers, but they introduce additional complexity related to assay calibration, cut‐off definition, algorithm stability, lot‐to‐lot variation, external validation, and clinical decision thresholds [108, 109]. For targeted MS assays, clinical deployment requires robust standard operating procedures, stable isotope‐labeled standards where appropriate, instrument harmonization, and performance monitoring across laboratories [14, 32]. For affinity‐based panels, implementation requires careful control of reagent specificity, panel versioning, batch effects, and cross‐platform comparability [34, 109]. Without these steps, promising protein signatures may remain research tools rather than clinically deployable biomarkers [14, 110].

Matrix Selection and Liquid Biopsy Opportunities

The choice of biospecimen matrix should be guided by the intended clinical application rather than analytical convenience alone. Tissue remains the most direct source of tumor‐associated proteins and enables spatial interpretation through immunohistochemistry, RPPA, imaging mass spectrometry, and spatial proteomic methods, but tissue sampling is invasive and affected by intratumoral heterogeneity [78, 96]. Plasma and serum are attractive for longitudinal monitoring, recurrence surveillance, and treatment‐response assessment, yet their broad dynamic range and high abundance of albumin, immunoglobulins, complement proteins, and acute‐phase proteins can obscure low‐abundance tumor‐derived signals [13, 53]. Nipple aspirate fluid and tumor interstitial fluid may be biologically closer to the breast tumor microenvironment, but collection methods, sample volume, patient eligibility, and processing protocols remain less standardized [12].

Extracellular vesicle‐enriched fractions offer a promising liquid biopsy source because vesicles may carry tumor‐associated membrane proteins, adhesion molecules, immune regulators, enzymes, and signaling proteins. However, extracellular vesicle studies require rigorous control of isolation method, purity, particle characterization, protein normalization, storage conditions, and contamination by soluble plasma proteins [53, 85, 112]. Urine is fully non‐invasive and may be useful for selected secreted or metabolically processed proteins, but its role in breast cancer proteomics remains less mature than plasma, serum, tissue, or nipple aspirate fluid. Matrix selection should therefore be matched to the clinical question: tissue may be most appropriate for subtype and pathway mapping, plasma or serum for monitoring, nipple aspirate fluid for local breast biology, and extracellular vesicle‐enriched fractions for tumor‐associated liquid biopsy signatures [12, 13, 53].

Why Proteomic Biomarkers Fail Clinically

Many proteomic biomarkers fail clinically because the discovery signal is not sufficiently robust, tumor‐specific, independently validated, or clinically actionable [10, 14]. Common causes of failure include small discovery cohorts, retrospective study design, inadequate adjustment for tumor subtype and stage, overfitting of multi‐protein panels, lack of external validation, inconsistent biospecimen handling, platform‐specific measurement bias, incomplete protein identification, and weak assay standardization [54, 110]. Some candidates also fail because they reflect general inflammation, coagulation, tissue damage, or host response rather than breast cancer‐specific biology [13, 103]. Another frequent reason for failure is a lack of added value over existing clinical tools [110]. A new proteomic biomarker is unlikely to be adopted if it does not improve decision‐making beyond mammography, histopathology, ER, PR, HER2, Ki‐67, tumor grade, nodal status, genomic assays, and established treatment‐response indicators [12, 110]. Statistical association with diagnosis or prognosis is therefore insufficient. A clinically useful biomarker must change risk classification, guide therapy, reduce unnecessary treatment, improve monitoring, or identify actionable biology in a way that is practical, reproducible, and cost‐effective [108, 111].

Standardization, Data Sharing, and Reproducibility

Cross‐study reproducibility in breast cancer proteomics requires harmonization across biospecimen handling, protein extraction, digestion protocols, chromatographic separation, MS acquisition settings, antibody or aptamer reagents, data processing, database search parameters, false discovery rate control, and statistical modeling [13, 14, 107]. Public deposition of raw and processed proteomic data, detailed biospecimen metadata, transparent computational workflows, and clear reporting of exclusion criteria are essential for independent reanalysis and cross‐cohort comparison [107, 113]. Shared reference materials, inter‐laboratory benchmarking studies, and ring trials can further support assay harmonization and improve confidence in quantitative comparisons across sites [14, 107].

Reproducibility should also be evaluated at the level of clinical interpretation. A biomarker panel that performs well in one cohort may fail in another if subtype composition, treatment exposure, ethnicity, disease stage, sample matrix, or analytical platform differs [10, 12, 110]. Future validation studies should therefore be designed with sufficient sample size, independent cohorts, predefined endpoints, transparent statistical plans, and clinically meaningful performance thresholds [14, 108]. These measures are necessary for moving proteomic biomarkers from isolated discovery reports toward reliable tools for precision breast cancer care [12, 14].

Future Directions

Future progress in breast cancer proteomic biomarker research will depend on moving beyond candidate discovery toward reproducible verification, clinically structured validation, and practical implementation [12, 14, 42]. The next phase should prioritize proteomics‐derived markers and protein signatures that demonstrate added clinical value beyond established pathological variables, including ER, PR, HER2, Ki‐67, tumor grade, nodal status, and currently used genomic assays [114]. This shift is essential because differential protein abundance alone is not sufficient for clinical adoption unless the marker improves diagnosis, prognostic stratification, therapeutic selection, or longitudinal monitoring in a defined context of use [14, 111].

Targeted proteomics should become a central component of future biomarker development [14, 42]. Candidate proteins identified through LC‐MS/MS, DIA‐MS, TMT/iTRAQ workflows, Olink, SomaScan, RPPA, extracellular vesicle proteomics, or spatial proteomics should be advanced through MRM, PRM, or other analytically validated assays before being evaluated in larger clinical cohorts [13, 14, 42]. This approach would reduce the gap between discovery and clinical translation by improving quantitative reproducibility, matrix‐specific measurement, and assay transferability [14, 42]. In parallel, greater use of stable isotope‐labeled peptide standards, shared reference materials, predefined quality‐control metrics, and inter‐laboratory benchmarking will be needed to support harmonized targeted proteomic workflows.

Spatial proteomics, phosphoproteomics, glycoproteomics, and extracellular vesicle proteomics are likely to play increasing roles in clinically meaningful biomarker discovery [53, 96, 102, 115]. Spatial proteomics can help determine whether candidate proteins originate from malignant epithelial cells, stromal compartments, immune infiltrates, or extracellular matrix‐rich regions [96]. Phosphoproteomics can identify activated signaling pathways and therapy‐relevant kinase networks, while glycoproteomics may improve detection of tumor‐associated changes in secreted or membrane proteins [102, 115]. Extracellular vesicle proteomics may strengthen liquid biopsy strategies by enriching tumor‐associated protein cargo, but this field will require stricter standardization of vesicle isolation, purity assessment, normalization, and cross‐platform validation [53].

Future studies should also integrate proteomics with genomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, imaging, digital pathology, and clinical outcome data. Multi‐omic integration may improve biological interpretation and help identify biomarker panels that capture tumor subtype, immune status, stromal remodeling, metabolic adaptation, and therapy resistance more effectively than single analytes [114]. However, integrated models must remain clinically interpretable and technically feasible. A complex biomarker panel will have limited translational value if it cannot be measured reproducibly, interpreted by clinicians, or linked to a practical treatment decision [110]. Well‐powered, multi‐center, and preferably prospective studies will be essential for determining whether proteomic biomarkers can improve patient care [14, 110]. Future validation studies should include diverse patient populations, standardized biospecimen handling, predefined clinical endpoints, transparent statistical analysis plans, independent validation cohorts, and reporting of sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, calibration, and decision impact [12, 111]. Biomarker development should also consider regulatory readiness at an early stage, including assay robustness, reproducibility, cut‐off definition, reporting format, laboratory workflow compatibility, cost, and turnaround time [110, 111]. These priorities will help determine whether proteomic discoveries can move from exploratory research into clinically useful tools for precision breast cancer care [12].

Conclusions

Proteomics has substantially expanded the molecular study of breast cancer by enabling high‐resolution analysis of protein abundance, post‐translational modifications, pathway activity, tumor microenvironment composition, and liquid biopsy‐associated protein signatures. The major challenge is no longer simply the identification of additional candidate proteins, but the selection of proteomics‐derived markers that can withstand targeted verification, analytical validation, independent clinical validation, and assessment of clinical utility. This distinction is critical because many proposed biomarkers remain confined to exploratory datasets and have not demonstrated reproducible performance across platforms, sample types, or patient cohorts.

Established clinical markers such as ER, PR, HER2, and Ki‐67 remain essential benchmarks for breast cancer management, but they should not be presented as biomarkers discovered through modern proteomic platforms. Their relevance to this review lies in the translational standard they set: reliable measurement, clinical interpretability, therapeutic relevance, and integration into routine decision‐making. Emerging proteomic candidates and protein signatures must demonstrate added value beyond these established tools before they can justify clinical implementation. Current barriers to translation include pre‐analytical variability, tumor and sample heterogeneity, incomplete protein identification, small retrospective cohorts, insufficient external validation, platform‐specific measurement bias, and lack of evidence that candidate biomarkers improve clinical decisions. Future progress will require standardized biospecimen protocols, transparent analytical workflows, rigorous quality control, targeted verification using MRM or PRM, orthogonal confirmation with clinically adaptable assays, and prospective validation in well‐defined clinical contexts. Proteomics is most likely to influence breast cancer care when it is used not only to discover proteins, but also to build reproducible, interpretable, and clinically actionable biomarker panels for diagnosis, prognosis, therapy selection, and disease monitoring.
