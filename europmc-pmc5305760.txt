CASE OF TRAUMATIC INVOLVEMENT OF
MECKEL'S DIVERTICULUM.
By J. D. GILRUTH, M.A., M.D., M.R.C.P. Edin.
History.?About a quarter-past eleven on Monday evening, the
5th September 1921, I received a telephone message from the
Arbroath Infirmary stating that an urgent abdominal case had been
admitted which fell to me for treatment. Dr F. J. Charlton of
Friockheim, who had sent in the patient, forwarded a letter of
explanation, part of which I transcribe. " I am sending in D. P.,
aged 16, as an urgent case. On Wednesday last he is said to have
been playing with some other boys when he sustained a severe injury
to his abdomen. On Friday he had a good deal of sickness, and on
Saturday there was a continuance. I saw him on Saturday, but
thought, after being told that he had been eating raw fruit, that it
Was a case of intestinal colic. Monday, vomiting more severe,
nothing retained by the stomach. Pulse 64, temp. 98? F. Enema
of the blood stained return of water, no stool. Pain, tenderness, and
distension of the abdomen; pain referred to umbilical area. The
bowels have not moved since Saturday. I think the case one of
intestinal obstruction."
I found D. P., aged 16, a well-developed adolescent. He had an
anxious expression, the tongue was furred and dry. The abdomen
showed fair distension, slight dullness in the flanks, type of breathing
thoracic. There were no marks of bruises or any kind of discoloration
?n the abdomen. He complained of pain mostly in the epigastric

In view of Dr Charlton's letter and the general condition of the

On opening the abdomen by a right paramedian incision reflecting
the right rectus, a few ounces of thin sanious fluid came away. Almost

this I ligated, thinking it might be the cause of obstruction. But j

to be a dark-coloured piece of the small intestine appeared, and
this was pulled out and examined. It was found not to be the
bowel proper but a definite diverticulum attached to the surface of
the ileum furthest away from the mesenteric attachment, and at its
other end attached to the cellular tissue behind the umbilicus. The
length was about 5 in.; broadest at the bowel end, say 2 in., and
about the centre there was a dark chocolate - coloured part, in the
centre of which a small area about 4 mm. in diameter had become
gangrenous. As we examined this little gangrenous patch the bit
fell out leaving a definite perforation. About an inch above this
perforation there was a definite constriction, and about an inch below
there was a similar one. There were * no adhesions of any kind
surrounding the diverticulum. The band, about 9 in. in length,
which I had earlier ligated was found to be attached near the tip of
the diverticulum to the cellular tissue behind the umbilicus, while the
other end was attached to the root of the mensentery almost opposite
the base of the diverticulum.
The diverticulum itself and the band were both removed and sent
to Dundee Clinical Research Association for examination. The base
of the diverticulum having been removed, the opening in the bowel was
carefully sutured first with catgut then with a double row of peritoneal
sutures, and the abdomen closed, a rubber tube being left deeply placed
in case of soiling of the peritoneum.
The operation was finished about 2 a.m. During the next few
hours pituitrin and liquid paraffin were employed, enemas given on
the second and third day. On the third day flatus passed, and a
motion was procured. The boy made a quite uneventful recovery,
and left the Institution on 5th October, having passed a calendar
month with us.
The report from the Dundee Clinical Research Association was as
follows: "The diverticulum is a Meckel's diverticulum containing all
the coats of the intestine. The tissue round the perforation is very
vascular and cedematous. The lesion suggests a perforation by some
sharp body rather than the result of an infective process. Some gritty
particles and material like potato skins were found in the diverticulum.

Before discussing the etiology of the case under review it
would be well to consider the nature of the structures observed
in this operation with a view to elucidating the problem as to
how they came to be there at all. Although attention had been
called to the condition as early as 1698, it was John Frederick
Meckel who, in 1812, gave a precise account of this diverticulum
with its pathological bearing, which came to be associated with
his name.
The following is a short statement of the embryology 1: "In
the early human embryo the umbilical vesicle or yolk sac is
large and communicates freely with the midgut. As develop-
ment proceeds the communication becomes narrow and
elongated, forming with the omphalo - mesenteric vessels
which pass to the yolk sac the yolk stalk. The sac which
is attached to the chorion of the placenta together with its
stalk becomes gradually smaller, and should finally disappear.
The persistence of the structures constituting the yolk stalk,
that is the omphalo-mesenteric duct and its accompanying
vessels, form a Meckel's diverticulum, and depending on
the relative degree of obliteration, the varying types are
produced."
The following is the classification of the possibilities given
by Eisendrath : (1) A complete canal opening at the umbilicus
at one end and into the ileum at the other, comparatively rare.
(2) A canal opening at the umbilicus but ending blindly at
a variable distance within the abdominal cavity.
(3) An intermediate portion of the duct remains having no
communication externally at the umbilicus or internally into
the gut, but forming a cystoma due to retention or secretion.
(4) The tube is limited to the proximal end of the canal
opening into the ileum?Meckel's diverticulum.
(5) The cord containing the umbilical vessels may persist
as an independent structure and be attached to the umbilicus
separately. This vascular cord may either take an independent
course or the vessels may run along the free border of the
mesenterioleum of the diverticulum, or, finally, they may run
from the tip of the diverticulum and form a cord commonly
known as the terminal ligament.
(6) The diverticulum may be either absent or rudimentary,
and the only evidence of a congenital condition be a cord con-

There is no doubt then that in the case we are presenting
there had existed a true Meckel's diverticulum, the congenital
condition resulting from an embryonic remains of a omphalo-
mesenteric or vitelline duct, and along with it a band stretching
from the loose areolar tissue behind the umbilicus to the root
of the mesentery, representing the embryonic remains of the j
omphalo-mesenteric vessels, one of them persisting.
It has now to be discussed what was the nature of the
condition which resulted from the blow administered to the
boy's abdomen on the Wednesday preceding the Monday
evening of the operation.
Although I confess that I did not see any part of the
diverticulum kinked behind the fibrous band, the constrictions
above and below the perforation made us think that a kind of
Littre's hernia (part of the diverticulum being herniated between
the band and the abdominal wall), might have accounted for the
engorged and cedematous central portion, part of which had
ultimately become gangrenous, finally perforating. At all
events the suggestion given by the pathologist that the
appearances were those of an injury or blow of some sharp
instrument along with Dr Charlton's history of the case make
it probable that trauma must have formed an important part
in the etiology.
So far as possible I have looked into the literature of injuries
involving a Meckel's diverticulum, and while I find that there
has now accumulated a great mass of literature dealing with
all the many pathological conditions in which such a diverti-
culum may be involved, there is very little reference to trauma.
Bearing in mind that it is stated in the text-books that there is
a persistence of Meckel's diverticulum in about two per cent, of
mankind, that is in one or other of its many forms, it is strange
that it should be involved so seldom in injuries to the abdomen.
In the Mayo Clinic, in a series of 10,600 abdominal operations,
Balfour came across a Meckel's diverticulum fifteen times.2
Ten of those were removed to prevent future trouble, and in
only five was it the cause of symptoms for which the patient
was brought to hospital.
Professor Wellington of Washington,3 in 1913, in what he
calls a fairly careful search of the literature was able to collect
an account of 326 cases of operations involving Meckel's

I have emphasised the trauma in this case because it is quite
evident that recorded cases of an injury to the abdomen having
affected a persistent Meckel's diverticulum are few and far
between. But on the other hand there do not seem to be
many records of a complete Meckel's diverticulum attached by
its tip to the cellular tissue behind the umbilicus along with a
complete band stretching from the umbilical area to the root
of the mesentery. The drawing which I have passed round was
made immediately after my operation when I had a rather
vague idea of the variations of this important embryonic vestige,
and may be depended on to be as correct as it was possible to
make it.
I would venture to make one or two observations of a
general kind on the case I have submitted.
(1) It is rather extraordinary that it is generally stated the
about two per cent, of human bodies have vestigial embryonic
remnants of the foetal yolk stalk in the shape of Meckel's
diverticulum or associated band, and yet it is impossible to find
what the ordinary limits of variation are for the many different
types which must persist. Perhaps it is pity that Meckel's
name is associated with the diverticulum for one reason at
least, and that is that the band which may or may not accom-
pany the diverticulum is a potent cause of trouble, being
responsible for a very large number of intestinal obstructions.
If Meckel's diverticulum, why not Meckel's band ?

a paper by Turner Warwick4 of Middlesex Hospital on
" Intussusception supervening on Congenital Stenosis of the
Ileum." You will see from his paper that congenital con-

So that there are many possibilities from the presence of
one or both, or part of one or both, those embryonic curiosities

Mr Warwick finished by saying that " the details of the
processes underlying those departures from the normal are at
present little understood."
(3) In Wellington's series of cases it is mentioned that there
were fifty cases of acute diverticulitis. During the last two
decades the name "diverticulitis" has acquired a very definite
significance. Telling of Leeds and many others have shown
that acquired sacculation of the large bowel, and notably of the
sigmoid flexure, produces a peridiverticular hyperplasia with,
in many cases, results which mimic cancer of the bowel in an
extraordinary way.
