Project Overview
LLNL applications require scientists to predict how materials evolve under various
thermomechanical conditions. While this is achieved through physics-based simulations,
uncertainty in the predictions of mechanical properties remains a serious challenge that limits
the predictive capabilities of models because we lack methods to compare predictions of atomic-
scale defects (dislocations) with experimental measurements. High energy X-ray diffraction
(HEXRD) is the most relevant technique that can provide the necessary statistical information on
dislocations. However, this technique is not yet quantitative because we lack a precise
understanding of the relationship between X-ray diffraction patterns and the underlying material
dislocation content and arrangements. To address this need, we used our novel computational
X-ray diffraction method to simulate the effect of dislocations on the diffraction patterns. We
compared virtual and experimental diffraction patterns. Results allowed us to clearly establish
the relationship between X-ray diffraction patterns and the underlying dislocation structures,
proving that it is feasible to quantitatively measure dislocation statistics with HEXRD. This project
delivered a method that can provide the missing piece to LLNL’s mechanical property simulations
in advanced metals by obtaining experimentally long-needed quantitative dislocation data, which
could fully enable predictive capabilities.

Mission Impact
This project’s results are directly related to SD and PLS under the focus areas “Stockpile and
Enterprise Transformation” and “Integrated Deterrence and Technology Competition”. The
HEXRD simulator can capture essential materials information from experiments already
conducted on stockpile materials (surrogate and real) and allow us to design better experimental
conditions for future testing. In addition, this same capability is directly relevant to the “Advanced
Materials and Manufacturing” core as it can be used to finally understand the novel deformation
mechanisms occurring in additively manufactured metals where dislocation configurations are
unique.

Abstract
Uncertainty in the predictions of physics-based simulations of mechanical properties in advanced
metals and alloys remains a serious challenge that limits the predictive capabilities of models.
This is because we lack methods to compare predictions of dislocations with experimental
measurements required to validate our models. High energy X-ray diffraction (HEXRD) is the most
relevant technique that can provide the necessary statistical information on dislocations.
However, this technique is not quantitative because we lack a precise understanding of the
relationship between X-ray diffraction patterns and the underlying material dislocation content
and arrangements. To address this need, we developed and used a novel computational X-ray
diffraction model to simulate the effect of dislocations on the diffraction patterns. We compared
the virtual and experimental diffraction patterns and validated that it is feasible to quantitatively
measure dislocation statistics with HEXRD. Successful demonstration of this approach in this
LDRD Feasibility Study shows that we could use our new model to make quantitative dislocation
measurements on HEXRD experiments obtained at LLNL on stockpile materials. This project
would provide the missing piece to LLNL’s mechanical property simulations in advanced metals
by providing long-needed quantitative dislocation data and fully enabling predictive capabilities.
Background and Research Objectives
LLNL missions depend on complex computer simulations to predict the mechanical behavior of
advanced metals. While most simulations are capable of accurately reproducing existing
experimental data, they lack predictive capability within controlled uncertainty bounds.
Science and technology gap: Simulations developed at LLNL are state-of-the-art but require
statistical and quantitative experimental data on the nature and density of dislocations as a
function of stress, deformation rate, temperature, etc., to be either calibrated or validated.
Unfortunately, this data is not easily available from experiments due to the small size of
dislocations (atomic scale). This means that the limitation is not theoretical but experimental; we
simply cannot quantitively measure dislocations in metals.
Hypothesis: Electron microscopy methods are powerful for observing dislocations, but they
cannot statistically measure dislocation properties due to sample size limitations. HEXRD is the
only technique capable of measuring dislocations in metals in a statistically significant way
because the X-ray beam interacts with a large volume of bulk material, up to ≈ 1 cm3 depending
on the alloy composition, while the short X-ray wavelength enables interactions with materials
at the atomic scale.
Feasibility question: Individual dislocations affect position and broadening of HEXRD patterns
through perturbation of the atom positions in a crystal lattice, which can be theoretically

described. However, when a dislocation network forms in real materials, the relation between
the dislocation configuration and the X-ray peak broadening becomes too complex to be
extrapolated from the single dislocation theory. Existing diffraction line profile analyses models
are based on simplified approximations that have not been validated. To accurately measure
dislocations with HEXRD, we need a ground-truth model to compare against and quantify the
contribution of dislocations to the X-ray beam diffraction and prove that accurately measuring
dislocations with HEXRD is feasible.
Objectives: The objective of this project was to leverage our multi-scale HEXRD simulator
framework to: (1) accurately model the effect of complex dislocation network on HEXRD patterns
using lower-scale simulations, and (2) use this model to generate virtual X-ray diffraction patterns
from macro-scale crystal plasticity simulations and directly compare them to experimental
HEXRD patterns. The result of this comparison would establish the feasibility of quantitatively
measuring dislocation with HEXRD.

Scientific Approach and Accomplishments

Our methodology is outlined in Figure 1. We began by experimentally mapping the three-
dimensional microstructure of pure aluminum alloys and 316L stainless steel using high-energy
diffraction microscopy (HEDM), as shown in Figure 2. These same samples were subsequently
subjected to in situ mechanical loading during X-ray diffraction experiments, during which
diffraction patterns were collected.

Figure 1: Project framework

The experimentally measured 3D microstructures served as direct input for large-scale crystal
plasticity finite element (CPFE) simulations, which are being conducted by our collaborators at
VTT [1], using their recently developed advanced models for body-centered cubic (bcc) materials
[2]. The output microstructures from the CPFE simulations were then utilized to generate virtual
X-ray diffraction patterns.
Our modeling approach explicitly accounts for the contributions of geometrically necessary
dislocations (GNDs) and statistically stored dislocations (SSDs). At each point within the
microstructure, we consider a local distribution of distortion fields arising from the presence of
SSDs, which are superimposed on the average distortion field at that material point. This
distribution of dislocation-induced displacement gradients is represented as a multivariate

This project’s results are directly related to SD and PLS under the focus areas “Stockpile and
Enterprise Transformation” and “Integrated Deterrence and Technology Competition”. The
HEXRD simulator can capture essential materials information from experiments already
conducted on stockpile materials (surrogate and real) and allow us to design better experimental
conditions for future testing. In addition, this same capability is directly relevant to the “Advanced
Materials and Manufacturing” core as it can be used to finally understand the novel deformation
mechanisms occurring in additively manufactured metals where dislocation configurations are
unique.
Conclusion
This work has significantly advanced the theoretical models used for diffraction pattern analysis
and provides a reliable tool for interpreting experimental HEXRD results. Following our successful
feasibility demonstration, we were awarded an LDRD-ER that will begin on October 1, 2025. The
project will leverage our HEXRD simulator to generate diffraction patterns for hundreds of
dislocation configurations, which will be used to train a machine learning model capable of
extracting statistical and quantitative dislocation data (such as density and character) from
experimental HEXRD patterns. Teams at LLNL and LANL have accumulated extensive HEXRD data
on stockpile materials over many years; with this new approach, we will finally be able to extract
critical dislocation information, enabling predictive simulation capabilities for the understanding
and design of metals relevant to LLNL applications.
