Radiopacity of contemporary flowable resin composites with different material formulations
Authors: Sema Yazici Akbiyik, Sinem Coşkun Albayrak, Elif Pınar Bakir, Ömer Çelli̇k
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate and compare the radiopacity of contemporary flowable resin composites representing different material types using a digital radiographic system and aluminum-equivalent measurements.MethodsSix flowable resin composites, including universal injectable, bulk-fill, ormocer-based, and short-fiber-reinforced formulations, were investigated. Disc-shaped specimens (5 mm × 2 mm) were prepared and radiographed using a photostimulable phosphor (PSP) system together with a 21-step aluminum step wedge and tooth sections containing enamel and dentin. Mean gray values were measured using ImageJ software and converted to millimeter aluminum equivalent (mm Al) values. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s HSD test (α = 0.05).ResultsSignificant differences were observed among the tested composite materials (p < 0.001). GC G-ænial Universal Injectable exhibited the highest radiopacity (5.54 ± 0.09 mm Al), whereas Omnichroma Flow Bulk showed the lowest value (2.77 ± 0.09 mm Al). All tested composites exhibited mean radiopacity values above the dentin reference and met the minimum radiopacity requirement specified by ISO 4049.ConclusionsRadiopacity varied significantly among the tested flowable resin composites, demonstrating that materials with similar clinical indications may differ considerably in their radiographic visibility. These findings suggest that radiopacity should be considered alongside other material properties when selecting restorative materials, particularly in clinical situations where accurate radiographic interpretation during follow-up is important.

Background

Resin-based composites are among the most commonly used restorative materials in contemporary dentistry owing to their favorable esthetic outcomes, adhesive capabilities, and continuously improving mechanical properties [1]. Advances in material science have led to the introduction of highly filled flowable composites, including injectable, bulk-fill, ormocer-based, and short-fiber-reinforced formulations, thereby extending the clinical applications of flowable materials beyond their traditional use as liners and small restorations [2]. These contemporary materials have been developed to improve handling characteristics, facilitate adaptation to cavity walls, and reduce clinical application time while maintaining adequate mechanical and physical performance [3, 4].

Radiographic examination is an indispensable component of restorative dentistry for the detection of recurrent caries, evaluation of restoration margins, assessment of proximal contours, and radiographic follow-up of restorations [5]. In this context, the radiopacity of restorative materials is of particular importance, as sufficient radiographic contrast between the restoration and surrounding dental tissues facilitates accurate interpretation of radiographic images [6]. Materials with inadequate radiopacity may be difficult to distinguish from dentin, whereas excessively radiopaque materials may mask marginal discrepancies or adjacent carious lesions, potentially compromising diagnostic accuracy [7].

According to the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 4049:2019 standard, the radiopacity of resin-based restorative materials should be equal to or greater than that of an equivalent thickness of aluminum [8]. This requirement ensures adequate radiographic visibility and facilitates differentiation between restorative materials and dental hard tissues. Dentin generally exhibits radiopacity values comparable to those of an equivalent thickness of aluminum, whereas enamel demonstrates considerably higher radiopacity [9]. Therefore, aluminum step wedges together with enamel and dentin references are widely accepted as standard tools for evaluating the radiographic properties of dental materials [10].

The radiopacity of resin-based composites is largely determined by their chemical composition and filler characteristics. Previous studies have shown that filler type, filler loading, particle size, and the incorporation of radiopaque elements such as barium, zirconium, strontium, ytterbium, and aluminum significantly influence X-ray attenuation and, consequently, the radiographic appearance of restorative materials [11]. In general, materials containing higher concentrations of radiopaque fillers exhibit greater radiopacity values. However, modifications in filler composition aimed at improving esthetic properties, translucency, color adaptation, and optical performance may also affect radiographic behavior, resulting in considerable variability among contemporary composite materials [12].

Several studies have investigated the radiopacity of conventional, universal, single-shade, and bulk-fill composite resins, reporting substantial variations among materials despite general compliance with ISO requirements [4, 7, 9, 10]. Significant differences in radiopacity have also been reported among universal flowable composites [4]. More recently, advances in restorative material technology have led to the introduction of flowable composites based on different material formulations, including injectable, ormocer-based, bulk-fill, and short-fiber-reinforced systems. However, existing studies have generally compared materials within the same formulation category rather than across different contemporary flowable material concepts. As a result, direct evidence comparing how different formulation approaches influence radiographic appearance under standardized experimental and imaging conditions remains limited.

The six materials selected for the present study have similar clinical indications; however, they represent different material formulations. Evaluating these materials under the same experimental and imaging conditions allows differences in radiographic behavior to be attributed more directly to material formulation rather than methodological variation. Such a comparison may contribute to the existing literature by providing direct comparative data on contemporary flowable composites with different material formulations, as well as clinically relevant information on their radiographic visibility. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate and compare the radiopacity of six flowable resin composites representing conventional flowable, universal injectable, bulk-fill, ormocer-based, and short-fiber-reinforced systems using standardized digital radiography and aluminum-equivalent measurements. The null hypothesis was that no significant differences would be observed in radiopacity among the tested materials.

Materials and methods

The study protocol was approved by the Lokman Hekim University Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2026/176). A single extracted human molar tooth was obtained from a patient who underwent routine tooth extraction for therapeutic reasons at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Faculty of Dentistry, Lokman Hekim University, Ankara, Türkiye. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the use of the extracted tooth for research purposes.

Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). For one-way ANOVA with six independent experimental groups, an alpha level of 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and a large effect size (f = 0.40) were assumed based on Cohen’s conventional benchmark for large effect sizes [13], as pilot data specific to this material set were not available. This resulted in a minimum required sample size of 10 specimens per group, consistent with the sample size used in comparable radiopacity studies evaluating flowable composite resins [14]. Enamel and dentin specimens were incorporated solely as reference standards for radiopacity assessment and were not considered experimental groups in the sample size calculation.

The tested materials were placed into Teflon molds measuring 5 mm in diameter and 2 mm in thickness. A standardized specimen thickness of 2 mm was selected to enable direct comparison among the tested materials under identical experimental conditions and to maintain consistency with previously published radiopacity studies [4, 7]. To prevent the formation of an oxygen-inhibited layer and to ensure a smooth, flat surface, both sides of each specimen were covered with a Mylar (polyester) strip, over which a glass slide was applied to extrude excess material. The specimens were then polymerized using a light-emitting diode (LED) curing unit (Woodpecker LED-B, China) operating at an irradiance of 1000 mW/cm². The curing tip was positioned centrally over each specimen and held in direct contact with the Mylar strip during polymerization. Each material was polymerized according to the manufacturer-recommended curing time. Following polymerization, the specimens were removed from the molds, and their thickness was verified using a digital caliper. Specimens outside the acceptable range of 2.0 ± 0.1 mm were excluded. The specimens were then stored at 37 °C under 100% humidity for 24 h.

For radiopacity reference, a single intact, caries-free extracted human maxillary left first molar was sectioned under water cooling using a low-speed diamond disc to obtain a 2-mm-thick enamel–dentin slice from the central portion of the tooth in the mesiodistal direction. The specimen was stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h prior to radiographic examination.

Radiographic procedure

Radiographic images were obtained using a 21-step aluminum step wedge (99.5% pure aluminum), with each step increasing in thickness by 0.5 mm, positioned alongside the test specimens. A wall-mounted periapical X-ray unit (NewTom RxDC; CEFLA, Imola, Italy) was operated at 60 kVp, 7 mA, and an exposure time of 0.20 s [9]. All radiographs were acquired using photostimulable phosphor (PSP) plates (Carestream Health, Rochester, NY, USA). A customized positioning device was used to standardize the focus-to-receptor distance (30 cm) and the positioning of both the PSP plate and the X-ray tube head, ensuring that the central X-ray beam was directed perpendicular (90°) to the PSP surface. Following exposure, the PSP plates were immediately scanned using a VistaScan Mini scanner (Dürr Dental, Bietigheim-Bissingen, Germany), and digital radiographic images were obtained. The same PSP plate was used throughout the study to minimize potential variability associated with the imaging plates. The radiographic images were analyzed in their original acquired form without any post-processing or adjustment of brightness or contrast.

The prepared composite specimens, a 2-mm-thick tooth section containing both enamel and dentin, and the aluminum step wedge were simultaneously radiographed to allow direct comparison of radiopacity values under identical exposure conditions (Fig. 1).

Measurement of radiopacity procedure

The mean gray values (MGVs) of the aluminum step wedge, flowable composite specimens, enamel, and dentin were measured on the digital radiographs using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Following a previously described radiographic measurement approach [9], ten independent specimens were evaluated for each material. Ten independent radiographic acquisitions were performed, with one different specimen from each material included in each acquisition; thus, each specimen was radiographed only once. For each specimen, three non-overlapping regions of interest (ROIs; 10 × 10 pixels) were randomly selected from homogeneous areas using the selection tool in ImageJ. The mean gray values (MGVs) obtained from these three ROIs were averaged to generate a single representative MGV for each specimen. Consequently, the specimen, rather than the individual ROI, was considered the experimental unit for statistical analysis, yielding ten independent specimen-level values for each material (n = 10). The ROI measurements served solely as repeated technical measurements to improve measurement reliability and were not treated as independent observations.

The same measurement protocol was applied to the enamel and dentin regions of the tooth section, which served as biological radiopacity references, and to each step of the aluminum step wedge. All measurements were performed by a single oral and maxillofacial radiologist with six years of experience who was blinded to the identities of the tested materials to ensure measurement standardization. The radiopacity values of all tested materials were converted to millimeter aluminum equivalent (mm Al) values. For this purpose, the mean gray value of each specimen was compared with the corresponding values obtained from the aluminum step wedge, and the aluminum-equivalent thickness was determined as previously described in the literature [7, 9, 14, 15].

According to ISO 4049, the radiopacity of a restorative material should not be lower than that of an equivalent thickness of aluminum. Therefore, for the 2-mm-thick specimens used in the present study, a radiopacity value of 2 mm Al was considered the minimum reference threshold for ISO compliance [8].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, while homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. As the data were normally distributed and exhibited homogeneous variances, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the radiopacity values of the tested flowable resin composites expressed as millimeter aluminum equivalents (mm Al). Pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test. Enamel and dentin were used as descriptive biological reference standards and were not included in the inferential statistical comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The radiopacity values of the tested materials, expressed as aluminum equivalents (mm Al), are presented in Table 2. A statistically significant difference in radiopacity was observed among the evaluated flowable resin composites (one-way ANOVA, F(5, 54) = 1416.57, p < 0.001, η² = 0.992). GC G-ænial Universal Injectable demonstrated the highest mean radiopacity value (5.54 ± 0.09 mm Al), whereas Omnichroma Flow Bulk exhibited the lowest (2.77 ± 0.09 mm Al).

The mean radiopacity values of the enamel and dentin references were 4.84 ± 0.33 mm Al and 2.19 ± 0.16 mm Al, respectively. All tested composite materials exhibited mean radiopacity values higher than the dentin reference and met the minimum radiopacity requirement specified by ISO 4049. As the specimens were prepared at 2 mm thickness, compliance was evaluated against the equivalent 2 mm aluminum threshold; even the material with the lowest radiopacity, Omnichroma Flow Bulk (2.77 ± 0.09 mm Al), exceeded this threshold. GC G-ænial Universal Injectable exhibited a higher mean radiopacity value than the enamel reference, whereas Grandio SO Light Flow showed a value close to that of enamel. Estelite Bulk Fill Flow, Admira Fusion Flow, EverX Flow, and Omnichroma Flow Bulk exhibited lower mean radiopacity values than the enamel reference.

According to Tukey’s HSD post hoc analysis, statistically significant differences were observed among all tested composite materials except between EverX Flow and Omnichroma Flow Bulk (p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study evaluated the radiopacity of six contemporary flowable resin composites representing different material types, including universal injectable, bulk-fill, ormocer-based, and short-fiber-reinforced formulations. Although clear differences were identified among the tested materials, the underlying factors responsible for these differences could not be directly determined within the scope of the present study. Therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected.

Among the tested materials, GC G-ænial Universal Injectable demonstrated the highest radiopacity. The radiopacity of resin composites is largely influenced by filler composition, filler loading, and the incorporation of radiopaque elements with high atomic numbers [11]. Previous investigations have shown that fillers containing elements such as barium, zirconium, strontium, and ytterbium significantly enhance X-ray attenuation and radiographic visibility [4, 16, 17]. Therefore, the high radiopacity of G-ænial Universal Injectable may be attributed to the incorporation of high-density injectable fillers containing radiopaque elements with high atomic numbers, particularly barium [4]. Although Grandio SO Light Flow and Admira Fusion Flow also contain barium-based radiopaque fillers, G-ænial Universal Injectable exhibited significantly higher radiopacity values. This finding indicates that the observed differences cannot be explained solely by the reported presence of barium and may also be associated with other characteristics of the filler system, including filler concentration and particle properties [4, 10]. However, as no direct compositional or microstructural analyses were performed in the present study, these explanations should be regarded as possible mechanisms rather than confirmed causes of the observed differences.

Grandio SO Light Flow and Admira Fusion Flow also exhibited relatively high radiopacity values compared with the other tested materials. The mean radiopacity of Grandio SO Light Flow was close to that of the enamel reference, indicating a radiographic appearance similar in radiopacity to enamel under the present experimental conditions. Its radiopacity may be associated with the presence of barium–aluminum–borosilicate glass fillers, which have been reported to contribute to X-ray attenuation [11, 12]. Consistent with the present findings, previous studies have shown that resin composites containing barium and zirconium-based fillers generally exhibit higher radiopacity values than materials formulated with less radiopaque filler systems [7, 12]. Although Admira Fusion Flow is based on ormocer (organically modified ceramic) technology and contains radiopaque fillers, its radiopacity was lower than that of Grandio SO Light Flow and GC G-ænial Universal Injectable. Interestingly, this finding does not appear to be explained solely by filler loading, as Admira Fusion Flow reportedly contains a relatively high filler content according to the manufacturer’s specifications. Therefore, differences in filler composition and the distinctive inorganic–organic network structure of ormocer-based materials may have contributed to the lower radiopacity observed in comparison with the other highly radiopaque materials evaluated in the present study [18].

Estelite Bulk Fill Flow exhibited intermediate radiopacity values, ranking below GC G-ænial Universal Injectable and Grandio SO Light Flow but above Admira Fusion Flow, EverX Flow, and Omnichroma Flow Bulk. The radiopacity observed for this material may be associated with its supra-nano spherical silica–zirconia filler system, as zirconium-containing fillers have been reported to enhance radiopacity through increased X-ray attenuation [11, 12]. EverX Flow and Omnichroma Flow Bulk exhibited the lowest radiopacity values among the tested materials and did not differ significantly from each other. The relatively low radiopacity observed for Omnichroma Flow Bulk is consistent with previous studies reporting lower radiopacity values for Omnichroma materials than for several conventional and universal resin composites [4, 7, 10]. This finding may be related to the material’s unique structural color technology and filler design, which were primarily developed to optimize esthetic performance and shade-matching ability [10]. Similarly, EverX Flow demonstrated relatively low radiopacity despite containing short E-glass fibers. Previous studies have reported that E-glass fibers possess limited intrinsic radiopacity because of their relatively low concentration of radiopaque elements [5]. Consequently, fiber reinforcement alone may not necessarily result in increased radiopacity. The present findings suggest that the radiopacity of restorative materials is influenced not only by the presence of reinforcing fibers but also by the radiopaque characteristics and composition of the filler system [19].

Radiopacity measurements may also be influenced by the radiographic system and imaging parameters used. Factors such as detector type, image acquisition settings, and image analysis methodology have been reported to affect radiographic density measurements and, consequently, the calculated radiopacity values of restorative materials [7, 12, 20]. In the present study, all measurements were performed under standardized conditions using the same PSP digital radiography system, exposure parameters, and image analysis protocol in order to minimize methodological variability. Nevertheless, direct comparisons with studies employing different imaging systems should be interpreted with caution.

The differences observed among the tested materials may also have clinical implications beyond compliance with minimum radiopacity requirements. Adequate radiographic visibility facilitates the assessment of restoration margins and may assist in identifying recurrent caries, overhangs, and interfacial defects. Conversely, insufficient radiopacity may make a restorative material difficult to distinguish from adjacent dental tissues, whereas excessive radiopacity may obscure underlying structures or defects [7, 10, 21]. In the present study, the tested materials showed different levels of radiopacity relative to the enamel and dentin references, indicating that their radiographic appearance may vary considerably despite meeting the minimum ISO requirement. These differences may be particularly relevant in deep or interproximal restorations and in restorations with subgingival margins, where radiographic evaluation plays an important role during long-term follow-up. Therefore, radiopacity should be considered together with other material properties during material selection, particularly when radiographic follow-up is anticipated. In such situations, differences in radiographic appearance relative to enamel and dentin may influence image interpretation and the evaluation of restoration margins and adjacent tooth structures. Nevertheless, radiopacity represents only one of several material properties and should not be interpreted as a direct indicator of the overall clinical performance of a restorative material.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. As an in vitro investigation, the study design cannot fully replicate the complex conditions encountered in the oral environment. Enamel and dentin reference values were obtained from a single extracted tooth, consistent with previously published radiopacity studies [14, 22]. However, this approach does not account for the natural biological variability in enamel and dentin among different teeth and individuals and should therefore be considered when interpreting the reference values reported in the present study. Accordingly, enamel and dentin were used as descriptive biological references rather than independent groups for inferential statistical comparisons. Furthermore, the present study evaluated specimens at a standardized thickness of 2 mm to ensure consistent comparison among the tested materials and to follow the methodology used in previous radiopacity studies [4, 7]. However, flowable resin composites may be used at varying thicknesses depending on their clinical application, and radiopacity may be influenced by material thickness. Therefore, the findings of the present study are limited to the thickness evaluated and may not fully reflect the radiographic appearance of these materials when used at different thicknesses in clinical practice. In addition, the tested materials were assessed only under baseline conditions, and no artificial aging procedures, such as thermocycling, were performed. Since changes in the filler–matrix interface and water-related degradation may influence the radiographic properties of restorative materials over time, the long-term radiopacity behavior of the evaluated composites remains unclear.

Another limitation is that all radiographic images were obtained under highly standardized conditions, with the X-ray beam directed perpendicular to the specimen surface. However, clinical radiographic examinations are influenced by variations in beam angulation, differences in tissue thickness, and anatomical superimpositions, all of which may affect X-ray attenuation and alter the radiographic appearance of restorative materials. Furthermore, the use of a single PSP-based imaging system does not allow direct comparison with other digital imaging modalities, such as charge-coupled device (CCD) or complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) sensors, which may produce different radiographic density measurements. The study was also limited to the assessment of radiopacity and did not include elemental or microstructural analyses; therefore, the proposed explanations regarding the possible influence of filler composition and material architecture should be interpreted with caution. Future studies incorporating multiple tooth references, aging protocols, different imaging modalities and specimen thicknesses, clinical simulation models, and compositional analyses may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the factors affecting radiopacity in contemporary flowable resin composites.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, considerable differences in radiopacity were observed among the tested flowable resin composites. Although all materials showed radiopacity values above the dentin reference, their radiographic appearance varied substantially. These findings indicate that flowable resin composites with similar clinical indications may differ in their radiographic visibility. Therefore, radiopacity should be considered alongside other material properties when selecting restorative materials, particularly when accurate detection of restoration margins, recurrent caries, and other radiographic changes during follow-up is required.
