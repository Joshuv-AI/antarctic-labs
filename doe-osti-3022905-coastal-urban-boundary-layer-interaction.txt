1. Summary

The Coastal Urban Boundary-layer Interactions with Convection (CUBIC) project aimed at
deploying three boundary layer profiling systems during the Intensive Observation Period (IOP,
June 01 - September 30, 2021) of the TRacking Aerosol and Convective interactions ExpeRiment
(TRACER) in Houston, Texas. The objective was to better resolve the meteorological conditions
in the TRACER study domain, Houston, TX, which is impacted by complex interactions between
synoptic-scale flows, meso-scale circulations related to sea breeze (SB) and bay breeze (BB)
developments, and micro-scale effects due to urban land-surface modifications. These
interactions strongly affect the transport of pollutants and can trigger local convergence zones
that play a significant role in convection initiation. Our hypothesis was that adding continuous
observations of boundary layer properties at multiple sites during the TRACER IOP will
significantly enhance the understanding of (i) boundary-layer processes in a coastal city, (ii)
evolution of thermally driven circulation patterns, (iii) mechanisms leading to convergence
zones, (iv) vertical and horizontal aerosol transport, and (v) the role of these processes for
convection initiation.

Three integrated boundary-layer profiling systems were deployed and operated along a North-
South transect that complemented the East-West transect of the DOE-ARM TRACER sites. The
three systems included the University of Oklahoma (CLAMPS 1), NOAA’s National Severe Storms
Laboratory (CLAMPS 2), and the University of Wisconsin (SPARC) facilities. Aside from short-
term interruptions caused by instrumentation issues or adverse weather conditions, CLAMPS1,
CLAMPS2, and SPARC operated continuously during the four-month long TRACER IOP. The
collected datasets were processed using different retrieval techniques and shared in the ARM
archive (see references provided in section 6f). They provide high-temporal resolution profiles
about temperature, humidity, all three wind components, turbulence parameters, and aerosol
backscatter in the boundary layer.

The observations were complemented by simulations with the National Severe Storms
Laboratory Warn-on Forecast System (WoFS; Stensrud et al. 2009; Heinselman et al. 2024) that
were run for 11 cases. The model output from the WoFS ensemble predictions provides further
context about the processes supporting SB development and propagation across the domain.
Profile observations in the planetary boundary layer (PBL) from the 3 CUBIC sites have also
been jointly analyzed with data collected near the coast with the OU CopterSonde, an
unmanned aerial systems (UAS) platform designed for PBL measurements. Additionally, high-
resolution reanalysis data were used to investigate nocturnal temperature patterns and urban
heat island (UHI) signatures in the Houston coastal environment.

2. Experimental Setup and Data Sets Collected

The instrumentation and measurement sites used during CUBIC are summarized in Figure 1.
Three boundary-layer profiling systems (Wagner et al. 2019) were deployed along a north-
south transect spanning from the University of Houston Coastal Center (UHCC) to the Aldine
site north of downtown Houston during the TRACER IOP. These systems included the NOAA
National Severe Storms Laboratory CLAMPS2 (C2), which was deployed at UHCC, the University
of Wisconsin Space Science and Engineering Center Portable Atmospheric Research Center
(SPARC) which was deployed at the ARM CSAPR site near Pearland and the University of
Oklahoma CLAMPS1 (C1) which was deployed at Aldine. The CLAMPS and SPARC profiling
systems are self-contained platforms that have benefited from several years of development
and deployment. Instruments and data processing were maintained remotely, which made the
4-month long deployment for the TRACER-CUBIC IOP period possible. The same basic
instrument configuration comprises each system: a scanning Doppler wind lidar for flow
characterization and passive profiler(s) for characterizing PBL thermodynamic properties. Each
platform includes a Halo Streamline Doppler wind lidar, an Atmospheric Emitted Radiance
Interferometer (AERI), and a surface meteorology station. CLAMPS1 and CLAMPS2 each also
include a microwave radiometer (MWR, Fig. 1e). During CUBIC, these systems were running
continuously between June 1 and September 26, 2022. Due to commitments to NOAA projects,
the DL at the UHCC site was not available until June 24, 2022.

At each CUBIC site, horizontal and vertical wind data were collected with the Halo Streamline
lidars, using a scanning strategy consisting of fixed-point vertical stares interlaced every 15
minutes with planned position indicator (PPI) scans at 60° elevation angle. A velocity azimuth
display (VAD) technique is applied to the lidar PPI scans to determine the horizontal wind speed
and direction every 15 minutes with a vertical grid spacing of approximately 26 m. The fixed-
point data were recorded at a 1 s (both CLAMPS) or 20 s (SPARC) resolution, and a simple signal-
to-noise ratio filter was applied (SNR + 1 < X, where X = Doppler lidar noise threshold; C1=1.006,
C2=1.0075, SPARC=1.005). The longer temporal resolution with the SPARC lidar is due to the
difference in configuration between the systems, with the SPARC lidar using a greater number of
pulses per ray. The thermodynamic data are retrieved from the raw AERI and/or MWR radiance
observations using the Tropospheric Remotely Observed Profiling via optimal estimation
(TROPoe) algorithm, which is an updated version of the AERIoe algorithm from (Turner and
Lohnert 2014; Turner and Blumberg 2019). The retrieved profiles are produced every ten
minutes, and the effective vertical resolution varies as a function of observing conditions and
height.

3. WoFS Model Setup

The WoFS is a convection-allowing ensemble (CAE) NWP modeling system that creates an
ensemble of 36 forecasts. The model has high spatial resolution, with a horizontal 750 X 750
grid at 3-km spacing and 51 terrain following vertical levels. The WoFS uses the 36-member
High Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR) Data Assimilation System (HRRR-DAS; Dowell et al. 2022)
to initialize the forecast and a combination of operational HRRR forecasts and perturbations

 4

from the Global Ensemble Forecast System (GEFS) provides the boundary conditions (Zhou et
al. 2022). The WoFS is a mixed-physics ensemble-based system with a combination of 6 unique
physics parameterizations (Table 5.1). The WoFS also has a rapid data assimilation cycle,
assimilating conventional observations, multi-radar/multi-sensor (MRMS) radar reflectivity,
MRMS radial velocity, GOES-16 cloud water path data, and GOES-16 clear sky radiances every
15 mins, allowing for the latest observations to help guide the model forecasts.

The WoFS was created at NSSL and designed specifically to provide probabilistic guidance for
hazards that are associated with individual convective storms (Skinner et al. 2023). The WoFS is
different from traditional CAEs that operate in a ‘next-day’ capacity and provide guidance for
regional convective threats. The WoFS goal is to provide ‘now-casting’, 0–6-hour forecasts
issued every 30 minutes, which aligns with lead times consistent with the National Weather
Service’s watch-to-warning products and can act as the bridge between observations and the
next day model simulations from traditional CAEs. A single 6-hour forecast is used herein,
initialized at 1700 UTC and running to 2300 UTC. To date, the WoFS has been most commonly
applied in severe and impactful weather situations such as potential severe weather outbreak
days, fire weather, or cases in which high flood risks are in place (Jones et al. 2022; Britt et al.
2024; Burke et al. 2024). The WoFS was run post-mortem for this project, with only the first 18
members included in the WoFS-like ensemble due to computational expense. The application
of the WoFS to simulate the SB is outside of its typical use case and the CUBIC simulations can
be considered as a proof of concept, to confirm whether weakly forced mesoscale boundaries
and the associated CI can be accurately forecasted with WoFS. We ran the simulations for a
total of 11 cases. For each case, we ran 14 separate 6-hour forecasts across 18 members. The
total number of simulations was 2,772. More details about the selected WoFS setup and
ensemble member PBL and radiation schemes are given in Storm (2025) and Lappin et al.
(2026).

4. Major Accomplishments and Results

As part of the data analysis of CUBIC, the synoptic regimes were characterized using a self-
organizing map (SOM) technique based on 700 hPa geopotential heights and the identification
of regional flow patterns was refined based on sounding analyses (Storm, 2025). The flow
regime over the Houston area consisted of four regimes: onshore, offshore, northeasterly, and
southwesterly. Days with SB characteristics were identified using a multivariate approach. For a
total of 52 days, or 41% of CUBIC dates, a SB developed in the Houston area. Of the SB cases,
98.2% occurred when land-sea temperature differences were at least 3°C, with signs of
seasonal changes to the SB, with later onset times and faster propagation speeds in September
SB cases, while no clear relationship between SB timing and regional flow regime emerged.

 5

A fuzzy-logic PBL height (PBLH) detection algorithm (Smith and Carlin, 2023), which combines
thermodynamic, wind, and turbulence data from multiple instruments, was used to investigate
the spatial and temporal variability of PBLH across the Houston region (Storm, 2025; Storm et
al. 2026b). Significant differences in PBLH across the coastal-urban were found which could be,
in part, attributed to influences of the SB and UHI effects. The urban site exhibited PBLH values
~480 m higher than at the coastal location, as well as a two-hour temporal difference in when
the maximum PBLH is reached. In the overnight period, the nocturnal PBLH at the urban site
remains elevated by up to 250 m, further indicating that sharp contrasts in surface
characteristics can lead to meaningful differences in PBL structure. SB events took place on days
with deeper PBLs across all sites when compared to non-SB cases.

Storm (2025) also compared two detailed case studies with the composite results to further
investigate if the regional flow plays a significant role in how the PBL responds to the SB
evolution and propagation. This analysis revealed that the depth, structure, and turbulence
signatures of the PBL response to the SB evolve as the circulation moves inland. The urban site
consistently exhibited stronger and deeper wind profiles, with offshore cases producing greater
post-SB wind speeds and a more pronounced jet-like structure. In contrast, thermodynamic
responses varied across space but showed no systematic differences between regional flow
regimes, suggesting that pre-existing thermodynamic structure is a more important
determinant of SB modification than regional flow. It appears that local-scale and surface
processes, such as surface fluxes, cloud cover, and spatial heterogeneity in land cover, act as
key drivers of the observed variability in PBL responses to the SB across Houston.

To better understand interactions between mesoscale flow processes, SB evolution, and UHI
effects, and their role in convection initiation, WoFS ensemble forecasts were generated for 11
selected days as described in section 3. While the observational data only provided insight into
PBL processes at single points, the joint analysis with model data allowed examining the
evolution and PBL impacts of the SB across the domain and continuously through time.

Lappin et al. (2026) presented a comparison between WoFS model output and observations for
June 16, 2022, when the PBL was influenced as a BB followed by a SB moved through the
domain. On that day, any convection was localized and short-lived, and the BB and SB were
uninterrupted by outflow boundaries. In addition to the data from the three CUBIC platforms,
18 vertical profiles collected at UHCC with the OU CopterSonde UAS were also available. The
comparison revealed broad variability across WoFS members in the depth, arrival time, and
intensity of the simulated SB case and while a small subset of members simulated the
preceding BB, none were accurately capturing timing and evolution of the BB. The radiation
scheme affected the skill in simulating a BB and onset time of the SB, while SB depth, intensity,
and evolution depended on the PBL parameterization. Nonlocal mixing schemes allow
baroclinic circulations to develop more readily, thus simulating coastal flows better, but PBL
temperatures and PBLH were overestimated. The complementary value of CUBIC PBL datasets
 6

and CopterSonde profiles is further discussed in Storm et al. (2026a) for a two-day period in
September 2022 when a low-level jet developed overnight and the evening transition of the
convective boundary layer was affected by a late onset SB that was also associated with the
formation of Kelvin-Helmholtz waves.

Additionally, Storm (2025) conducted an integrated analysis of WoFS model output and CUBIC
observations for August 17, 2022, a day that was characterized by exceptionally strong
thermodynamic and kinematic SB responses and CI along the SB front (SBF). The analysis
indicated that persistent ascent along or ahead of the inland-propagating SBF, in combination
with pre-existing moisture, plays a critical role in successful CI. The WoFS ensemble simulations
provided a more complete depiction of the spatial evolution of the SB which revealed the
forced ascent across much of the domain prior to CI.

To further investigate the significance of the Houston UHI, Spicer (2024) created a detailed
Local Climate Zones map for the CUBIC study domain and Spicer et al. (2026) applied the
National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration National Centers for Environmental
Prediction’s Unrestricted Mesoscale Analysis (URMA) high-resolution (2.5 km, hourly) reanalysis
data along with self-organizing maps (SOM) to explore nighttime minimum temperature
patterns in the Houston, TX area. The SOM revealed nodes with strong UHIs, while others
appeared to indicate heat wave conditions. These results are currently being used to identify
possible impacts of the nighttime UHIs on the PBL structure observed during CUBIC.

Information about CUBIC results can also be found in the TRACER overview paper (Jensen et al.
2025) and Lamers et al. (2025) included CUBIC data in the development of a value-added
observational dataset summarizing PBL properties in the Houston metropolitan area.
