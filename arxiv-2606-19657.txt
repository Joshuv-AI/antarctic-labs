K K -Theoretic Obstructions to Linearizing QCA Representations
Mattie Ji, Bowen Yang

Abstract
. Projective representations arise naturally in physics and representation theory, and determining whether they can be linearized has been a fundamental problem. In this work, we study the analogous problem for quantum cellular automata (QCA) representations, which incorporate locality constraints imposed by a metric space X X . Over an arbitrary field 𝔽 \mathbb{F} , we develop an obstruction theory for the linearization of QCA representations, using the algebraic K K -theory spectrum of QCA constructed in previous work of the authors. The resulting obstructions are governed by the homotopy type of the QCA spaces, from which we extract universal obstruction classes to linearization. In the complex algebraic and unitary case, we also fully compute the homotopy types of the QCA spaces over a point, a line, and a plane.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Quantum Physics and Linearization

In his note
[
18
]

“What is an anomaly?”
, Freed writes:

“Quantum theory is projective. Quantization is linear.”

We explain his first point in the familiar setting of single-particle quantum mechanics. Let

V
be a finite dimensional complex Hilbert space. There are two parallel
ways of describing quantum states, called the Schrödinger and
Heisenberg pictures.

Schrödinger Picture.
In the Schrödinger picture, a pure state is a ray in
V
, i.e. a
one-dimensional subspace

[v]\in\mathbb{P}V

spanned by a nonzero vector
v\in V
. Equivalently, the pure state is
represented by the rank-one orthogonal projection
P_{[v]}
onto
[v]
.

Mixed states are probabilistic mixtures of pure states, and are represented by
density operators

\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}P_{[v_{i}]}\in\mathrm{End}(V),\qquad p_{i}\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{i}p_{i}=1.

The pure states are precisely those density operators of rank one.

Heisenberg Picture.
In the Heisenberg picture, one instead starts with the algebra of observables

\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\mathrm{End}(V).

The Hermitian inner product on
V
gives
\mathcal{A}
a
*
-operation.
A state, pure or mixed, is a normalized positive linear functional

\omega:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C},\qquad\omega(I)=1,\qquad\omega(A^{*}A)\geq 0\quad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal{A}.

The set of states is convex, and its extreme points are the pure states.

The two descriptions are equivalent for finite dimensional
V
. From either picture, the action of a group
G
on quantum states (i.e., a
G
-symmetry) is naturally projective.

In the Schrödinger picture, symmetries are encoded through a projective unitary representation

\varphi:G\longrightarrow PU(V)\subset PGL(V).

In the Heisenberg picture, the same symmetry acts on the algebra of
observables by
*
-automorphisms:

\varphi:G\longrightarrow\mathrm{Aut}_{*}(\mathcal{A})=PU(V).

The induced action on a state
\omega
is then given by composition.

To generalize this to an arbitrary field
\mathbb{F}
, we forget the Hermitian structure. This leads us to a projective representation:

\varphi:G\longrightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{A})=PGL(V).

Freed’s second point above leads naturally to the question of linearization.

Question

(Linearization)
.

Does there exist a lift
\tilde{\varphi}
to the following diagram?

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces GL(V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces PGL(V).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{\varphi}}

This question has a well-known answer in terms of an obstruction class in group cohomology.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\varphi:G\to PGL(V)
be a projective representation over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
.
Choose lifts
U_{g}\in GL(V)
of
\varphi(g)
. Then there are scalars

\alpha(g,h)\in{\mathbb{F}}^{\times}
such that

U_{g}U_{h}=\alpha(g,h)U_{gh}.

The function
\alpha
is a
2
-cocycle, and its cohomology class

[\alpha]\in H^{2}(G,{\mathbb{F}}^{\times})

is independent of the choice of lifts. The projective representation

\varphi
is linearizable if and only if
[\alpha]=0
.

So far, we have focused on a
single-particle
system, in the sense that
V
may be regarded as concentrated at a single point in space. We now pass to the many-body setting in the Heisenberg picture, where one assigns endomorphism algebras to points of a lattice
X
and considers tensor product of them. The resulting objects are quantum spin systems
[
38
,
37
]
, and their natural automorphisms are quantum cellular automata (QCA)
[
15
]
.

In this paper, a quantum spin system is specified by a function
q:X\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
, where
q(x)
is the dimension of the local
\mathbb{F}
-vector space at
x
, and its QCA group
\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
consists of the locality-preserving automorphisms of the associated algebra of observables; see Section
2
. Thus a
G
-symmetry of
(X,q)
is modeled by a homomorphism
\varphi:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
. In this setting, linearization has two components: factorization into pointwise projective representations, followed by pointwise lifting to genuine linear representations.

Question

(Linearization for QCA)
.

Does there exist
\beta\in\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
such that the following diagram can be completed?

(1.1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\prod_{x\in X}GL(\mathbb{F}^{q(x)})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\prod_{x\in X}PGL(\mathbb{F}^{q(x)})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{Q}(X,q)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta\varphi\beta^{-1}}

A QCA representation
\varphi:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
fitting in (
1.1
) is
linearizable
. One can also impose a relaxed condition that
\varphi
is
stably linearizable
if there is some linearizable
\psi:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,r)
such that the map
\varphi\otimes\psi:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,qr)
, induced by pointwise multiplying
q
and
r
, is linearizable. One can also specify that
\varphi
is
weakly linearizable
if it is liftable in (
1.1
) after taking the colimit over all quantum spin systems (see Section
2
for details).

To continue the single-particle story in Theorem
1.1
, a natural question to study is to find obstruction classes to (stable/weak) linearizations of QCA representations.

1.2.
Stable Homotopy Theory and Obstruction Classes.

In this work, we develop an obstruction theory for (stable/weak) linearizations of QCA representations over any field
{\mathbb{F}}
and general metric spaces
X
with reasonable assumptions. We first set up the precise definitions of QCA and their representations in Section
2
. We then study linearizations from the perspective of
stable homotopy theory
and
cohomological obstruction theory
.

Stable Homotopy Theory.
In the recent work of
[
26
]
, the authors developed an algebraic
K
-theory spectrum of quantum cellular automata over a fixed commutative ring
R
. For a reasonable space
X
, they constructed a space
K(\mathbf{C}(X;R))
using Segal’s
K
-theory of symmetric monoidal categories
[
43
]
and defined
\mathbf{Q}(X;R)
, the
QCA space of
X
, as the based loop space of
K(\mathbf{C}(X;R))
at the identity component. They then showed the spaces
\{\mathbf{Q}(X\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{n};R)\}_{n\geq 0}
assemble into an
\Omega
-spectrum
\mathbb{QCA}(X;R)
. When
X=*
, we write
\mathbb{QCA}(R)=\mathbb{QCA}(*;R)
. For the unitary case, the authors also constructed the unitary QCA spectrum using a similar space
K(\mathbf{C}^{*}(X))
and its based loop space
\mathbf{Q}^{*}(X)
.

For an
\Omega
-spectrum
X=\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, the based homotopy classes of maps
X^{n}(-)\coloneqq[-;X_{n}]
form a (reduced) generalized cohomology theory, and the correspondence goes backward by Brown’s representability
[
7
]
. Our observation is that the QCA representation
\varphi:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
induces a natural map
\varphi^{st}:BG\to K(\mathbf{C}(X;R))_{1}
and hence defines a
cohomology class
in

[\varphi^{st}]\in[BG,K(\mathbf{C}(X;R))_{1}]=[BG,\mathbf{Q}(X\times{\mathbb{Z}};R)]=\mathbb{QCA}(X;R)^{1}(BG).

When
X
is for example
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
,
[\varphi^{st}]
is identified as a cohomology class
\mathbb{QCA}(R)^{n+1}(BG)
. We can then use the machinery of (stable) homotopy theory to study the class
[\varphi^{st}]
and infer about (stable/weak) linearizations.

We explore this connection in Section
3
. Our first theorem gives a homotopical obstruction to linearization.

Theorem A
.

Let
G
be a finite or, more generally, rationally acyclic group. Suppose a QCA representation
\varphi:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
over any field
{\mathbb{F}}
is stably linearizable or weakly linearizable, then the stabilization
\varphi^{st}:BG\to K(\mathbf{C}(X;{\mathbb{F}}))_{1}
is null-homotopic.

Write
\operatorname{PGL}(X;{\mathbb{F}})
to denote the colimit of
\prod_{x\in X}\operatorname{PGL}_{q(x)}({\mathbb{F}})
over all quantum spin systems
q
. Our second theorem shows that projectivizable null-homotopies are linearizable. When
X=*
, this gives a converse to Theorem
A
for finite groups and is a stable analog of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem B
.

Let
G
be a group. Suppose a QCA representation
\varphi:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
lands in
\prod_{x\in X}\operatorname{PGL}_{q(x)}({\mathbb{F}})
, and the induced map
\varphi^{\prime}:BG\to\operatorname{BPGL}(X;{\mathbb{F}})\to\operatorname{BPGL}(X;{\mathbb{F}})^{+}
is null-homotopic, then
\varphi
is weakly linearizable. If
G
is furthermore finite, then
\varphi
is stably linearizable.

We also develop unitary analogs of Theorem
A
and
B
in Section
3.3
.

Obstruction Classes.
For a map
f:BG\to K(\mathbf{C}(X;{\mathbb{F}}))_{1}
, there are successive cohomological obstruction classes

u_{i}(f)\in H^{i}(BG;\pi_{i-1}(\mathbf{Q}(X;{\mathbb{F}}))),i\geq 1

that are obstructions to
f
being null-homotopic.

In Section
4
, we explain how to sequentially extract these cohomology classes using a universal construction called Dror’s tower
[
12
]
. We specialize to the case of algebraic and unitary QCA spaces to define what we call
universal obstruction classes
in Definition
4.8
(after the Universal Coefficient Theorem used in the construction).

Theorem C
.

Suppose
G
is finite or rationally acyclic and
\varphi
is a (unitary) QCA representation. If
\varphi
is stably or weakly linearizable, then each universal obstruction class
u_{i}(\varphi)
vanishes.

Using this result and computations of the algebraic
K
-theory spectrum of unitary QCA, we obtain an obstruction theory in the unitary case, as anticipated in
[
50
]
.

Corollary D
.

When
G
is finite (or more generally, rationally acyclic) and
X=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, the only potentially nonzero universal obstruction classes occur in

(1.2)

H^{i+1}\bigl(BG;\mathcal{Q}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}^{n-i})/\mathcal{C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}^{n-i})\bigr),

for
0\leq i\leq n+1
,
and in

(1.3)

H^{n+2}(BG;U(1)).

Computations.
From the stable homotopy theory interpretation of linearization, we are motivated to compute the groups
\mathbb{QCA}(X;R)^{1}(BG)
. In Section
5
, we discuss how to compute the structure of QCA spaces for special examples, which sheds light on the structure of their generalized cohomology theories.

Theorem E
.

The following spaces are equivalent to products of Eilenberg-MacLane spaces:

(1)

(Complex Case): For
n=0,1
, the space
K(\mathbf{C}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n};\mathbb{C}))
and hence
\mathbf{Q}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n};\mathbb{C})
.

(2)

(Complex Case): The universal cover of

.

(3)

(Unitary Case): For

, the space

and hence
\mathbf{Q}^{*}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})
.

(4)

, the spaces
\mathbf{Q}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n};{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{k}})

, and the spaces

.

For all spaces in Theorem

(1-3) other than

, this is in fact an equivalence of group-like

-spaces (with respect to their connective deloopings). For Theorem

-spaces when

]
implies

is simply connected. If the analogous

2.
QCA Representation of Discrete Groups

2.1.
QCA and Circuits

To model infinitely many particles in physical space, we imagine them as being
distributed “uniformly” over an unbounded metric space
X
. To impose mild
regularity assumptions on
X
, we always assume that
X
contains a countable, coarsely dense subspace that is uniformly discrete, locally finite metric space of bounded geometry. Note that this does not require
X
itself to be discrete or locally finite. Typical examples include lattices in Lie
groups, such as

\mathbb{Z}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For two such metric spaces
X
and
X^{\prime}
, the space
X\times X^{\prime}
has the
L^{\infty}
-metric.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
q\colon X\to\mathbb{N}
is called locally finite if

\Lambda_{q}:=\{x\in X:q_{x}\coloneqq q(x)>1\}

is a locally finite subset of
X
. That is,
\Lambda_{q}
has finite
intersection with every bounded subset of
X
.
2
2

2

In particular,

\Lambda_{q}
is finite if
X
is bounded.
We often call
\Lambda_{q}
the
lattice associated to
q
. We denote the collection of such functions by

\mathbb{N}^{X}_{\mathrm{lf}}
. This is a partially ordered set under the
relation

q\leq r\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q_{x}\mid r_{x}\text{ for all }x\in X.

If
X
is already locally finite, then every function
q\colon X\to\mathbb{N}

is locally finite.

Definition 2.2
.

A
\mathbb{F}
-quantum spin system
consists of a metric space
(X,\rho)
, and a locally finite function
q\in\mathbb{N}^{X}_{\mathrm{lf}}
. Its
algebra of local observables
is defined as

(2.1)

\mathcal{A}(X,q)=\bigotimes_{x\in X}M_{q_{x}}(\mathbb{F})=\varinjlim_{B\subset X\text{ bounded }}\bigotimes_{x\in B}M_{q_{x}}(\mathbb{F}).

An element
a\in\mathcal{A}(X,q)
can be understood as a finite
k
-linear combination of restricted tensor products

\bigotimes_{x\in X}a_{x},

where
a_{x}\in M_{q_{x}}(\mathbb{F})
and
a_{x}=I_{q_{x}}
outside some bounded subset of
X
. The minimal bounded subset
B\subset X
such that the element lies in
\bigotimes_{x\in B}M_{q_{x}}(\mathbb{F})
is called its support, denoted by
\mathrm{supp}(a)
.

Definition 2.3
.

A
quantum cellular automaton (QCA)
of
\mathcal{A}(X,q)
is a
\mathbb{F}
-algebra automorphism
\alpha
of finite spread. That is, there exists
\ell>0
such that for every
a\in\mathcal{A}(X,q)

the support of
\alpha(a)
is contained in

D_{\ell}(\mathrm{supp}(a)):=\{y\in X:\rho(\mathrm{supp}(a),y)\leq\ell\}.

The smallest such
\ell
, when it exists, is called the spread of
\alpha
. The
group of QCA
of
\mathcal{A}(X,q)
is denoted as
\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
.

For quantum spin systems
q\leq r
, there is a well-defined map

\mathcal{Q}(X,q)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{Q}(X,r)

induced by pointwise Kronecker product with the identity matrices (see Lemma 2.13 of
[
26
]
).

Definition 2.4
.

The
total QCA group
over
X
is the direct limit in the category of groups:

\mathcal{Q}(X)\ :=\ \operatorname{colim}_{q\in\mathbb{N}^{X}_{\mathrm{lf}}}\ \mathcal{Q}(X,q).

Concretely,
\mathcal{Q}(X)
is the set of equivalence classes
[q,\alpha]
with

\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
, modulo the condition that

[q,\alpha]\sim[r,\beta]\text{ if and only if there exists }s\ \text{with}\ q\mid s,\ r\mid s\ \text{such that}\ \iota_{q\to s}(\alpha)=\iota_{r\to s}(\beta).

The product is

[q,\alpha]\cdot[r,\beta]\;\coloneqq\;\big[s,\ \iota_{q\to s}(\alpha)\circ\iota_{r\to s}(\beta)\big],\qquad s_{x}\coloneqq\mathrm{lcm}(q_{x},r_{x}).

When
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
, there is a unitary variant of QCA that commutes with the adjoint operation on elements of
\mathcal{A}(X,q)
. In Section 5 of
[
26
]
, they are referred to as
*
-QCA, to distinguish them from the algebraic version. We denote the unstable
*
-QCA group as
\mathcal{Q}^{*}(X,q)
, and the total
*
-QCA group as
\mathcal{Q}^{*}(X)
. When
X
is a point, the adjoint preserving QCA are precisely the projective unitary matrices. Thus, we refer to this as the
unitary case
and use the word
unitary QCA
interchangeably with
*
-QCA. All of our results and proofs admit straightforward adaptations to this setting. Throughout, we will indicate the corresponding unitary versions where appropriate.

We now discuss an important class of QCA known as
circuits
.

Example 2.5

(Single-layer general, special, and unitary circuit)
.

Let
q\in\mathbb{N}^{X}_{\mathrm{lf}}
be a spin system. A
single-layer circuit
on
(X,q)
consists of the following data.

(1)

First, choose a uniformly bounded partition

(2.2)

X=\coprod_{j}X_{j},\qquad\sup_{j}\operatorname{diam}(X_{j})<\infty.

For each block
X_{j}
, set

(2.3)

q(X_{j}):=\prod_{x\in X_{j}}q_{x}.

(2)

Second, for each block
X_{j}
, choose a local automorphism
\alpha_{j}
of the matrix algebra associated to
X_{j}
. We will consider either of the following cases:

(a)

A
single layer general circuit
chooses

\alpha_{j}\in\operatorname{PGL}_{q(X_{j})}(\mathbb{F}),

corresponding to conjugation by an invertible matrix.

(b)

A
single layer special circuit
chooses

\alpha_{j}\in\operatorname{PSL}_{q(X_{j})}(\mathbb{F}),

corresponding to conjugation by an invertible matrix of determinant one.

(c)

A
single layer unitary circuit
chooses

\alpha_{j}\in\operatorname{PU}_{q(X_{j})},

corresponding to conjugation by a unitary matrix.

Each circuit can be identified as a QCA as follows. Since the subsets
X_{j}
are pairwise disjoint, the automorphisms
\alpha_{j}
act on mutually commuting tensor factors of
\mathcal{A}(X,q)
. Hence their tensor product

(2.4)

\alpha:=\bigotimes_{j}\alpha_{j}

defines a locality-preserving automorphism of
\mathcal{A}(X,q)

X_{j}
implies that the propagation of
\alpha
is uniformly bounded.

2.2.
QCA Representations

For our discussions on linearization, we are interested in a special class of single-layer circuits known as
on-site QCA
.

Example 2.9

(On-site QCA)
.

For each
x\in X
, choose an invertible matrix
u_{x}\in GL_{q_{x}}(\mathbb{F})
. Define

\alpha\left(\bigotimes_{x\in X}a_{x}\right)=\bigotimes_{x\in X}u_{x}a_{x}u_{x}^{-1}.

This is a QCA of spread
0
.

Now we will define QCA representations and their linearization questions. We first do this over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
and then discuss the unitary case later.

Definition 2.10

(QCA representations)
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
denote the group of QCA of
\mathcal{A}(X,q)
. A
QCA representation
of a group
G
on the quantum system parametrized by
(X,q)
is a group homomorphism

(2.5)

\varphi:G\longrightarrow\mathcal{Q}(X,q).

Given QCA representations

(2.6)

\varphi:G\longrightarrow\mathcal{Q}(X,q),\qquad\psi:G\longrightarrow\mathcal{Q}(X,r),

their tensor product is the QCA representation

(2.7)

\varphi\otimes\psi:G\longrightarrow\mathcal{Q}(X,qr),

defined by

(2.8)

(\varphi\otimes\psi)(g):=\varphi(g)\otimes\psi(g).

Here
qr\in\mathbb{N}^{X}_{\mathrm{lf}}
denotes the pointwise product spin system, so that
(qr)_{x}=q_{x}r_{x}
. The same construction applies to unitary QCA representations, giving

(2.9)

\varphi\otimes\psi:G\longrightarrow\mathcal{Q}^{*}(X,qr)

whenever
\varphi
and
\psi
are unitary.

The on-site example above defines a group homomorphism

\prod_{x\in X}GL_{q_{x}}(\mathbb{F})\longrightarrow\prod_{x\in X}PGL_{q_{x}}(\mathbb{F})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{Q}(X,q),

where an element
(u_{x})_{x\in X}
acts by

\bigotimes_{x\in X}a_{x}\longmapsto\bigotimes_{x\in X}u_{x}a_{x}u_{x}^{-1}.

The algebra
\mathcal{A}(X,q)
should be regarded as the infinite-particle analog of
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{End}(V)
. Under this analogy,
PGL(V)
is replaced by
\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
, and projective representations are replaced by QCA representations. Thus the question of linearization generalizes as follows.

Question

(Linearization of QCA Representation)
.

Given a QCA representation
\varphi:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
. Does there exist a QCA
\beta\in\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
such that the following diagram can be completed?

(2.10)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\prod_{x\in X}\operatorname{GL}_{q_{x}}(\mathbb{F})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\prod_{x\in X}\operatorname{PGL}_{q_{x}}(\mathbb{F})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{Q}(X,q)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta\varphi\beta^{-1}}

The question has two parts.

(a)

First, after conjugating by a QCA
\beta
, does the QCA representation land in

\prod_{x\in X}\operatorname{PGL}_{q_{x}}(\mathbb{F})\subset\mathcal{Q}(X,q)?

Equivalently, is it induced by a collection of independent projective representations?

(b)

Second, do these projective representations lift to genuine linear representations, so that the conjugated action factors through

\prod_{x\in X}GL_{q_{x}}(\mathbb{F})?

Definition 2.11
.

We say that the QCA representation
\varphi
is:

(1)

linearizable
if
\varphi
fits into diagram (
2.10
).

(2)

linearly onsite
if
\varphi
fits into diagram (
2.10
) with
\beta
being the identity map.

(3)

stably linearizable
if
\varphi\otimes\psi:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,qr)
fits into diagram (
2.10
) for some linearly onsite representation
\psi:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,r)
.

(4)

trivially stably linearizable
if
\varphi\otimes c_{id}:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,qr)
fits into diagram
(\ref{eq::unstable_lift}
) and
c_{id}:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,r)
is the constant map to the identity element of
\mathcal{Q}(X,r)
.

Note that from Definition
2.11
, we clearly have the sequences of implications

(2)\implies(1)\implies(4)\implies(3).

The four conditions all consider lifts after propagating a finite number of stages along the colimit in
{\mathbb{N}}^{X}_{\mathrm{lf}}
. We can also ask the question of whether or not the QCA representation
\varphi
is linearizable after passing through the entire colimit. For convenience, we introduce the following notations:

Definition 2.12
.

We write:

•

\operatorname{GL}_{\otimes}(X;{\mathbb{F}})
to be
\operatorname{colim}_{q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{X}_{\mathrm{lf}}}\prod_{x\in X}\operatorname{GL}_{q_{x}}({\mathbb{F}})
.

•

\operatorname{PGL}(X;{\mathbb{F}})
to be
\operatorname{colim}_{q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{X}_{\mathrm{lf}}}\prod_{x\in X}\operatorname{PGL}_{q_{x}}({\mathbb{F}})
.

•

When
X=*
, we write
\operatorname{GL}_{\otimes}({\mathbb{F}})=\operatorname{GL}_{\otimes}(*;{\mathbb{F}})
and
\operatorname{PGL}_{\otimes}({\mathbb{F}})=\operatorname{PGL}(*;{\mathbb{F}})
.

Definition 2.13
.

Continuing from Definition
2.11
, we say that the QCA representation
\varphi
is:

(5)

weakly linearizable
if the induced map
G\to\mathcal{Q}(X)
factors through
\operatorname{GL}_{\otimes}(X;{\mathbb{F}})\to\operatorname{PGL}(X;{\mathbb{F}})
up to conjugation.

(6)

weakly linearly onsite
if
G\to\mathcal{Q}(X)
factors through
\operatorname{GL}_{\otimes}(X;{\mathbb{F}})\to\operatorname{PGL}(X;{\mathbb{F}})
.

From Definition
2.11
and Definition
2.13
, we have the following implications:

(4)\implies(5)\impliedby(6)\impliedby(2).

Each condition in the two definitions is a generalization of linearizability of a projective representation. In particular we have

Proposition 2.14
.

When
X=*
, we have
\mathcal{Q}(X,q)\cong\mathrm{PGL}_{q}(\mathbb{F})
, and a QCA representation becomes a projective representation. Each condition in Definition
2.11
and
2.13
is equivalent to linearizability of the projective representation.

Proof.

Let
\varphi:G\to\mathrm{PGL}_{q}(\mathbb{F})
be a projective representation, and let
\alpha_{\varphi}
denote its obstruction cocycle, as in Theorem
1.1
. Conjugating
\varphi
by an element of
\mathrm{PGL}_{q}(\mathbb{F})
clearly does not change
\alpha_{\varphi}
.

The obstruction is also additive under tensor product. Indeed, if
\psi:G\to\mathrm{PGL}_{r}(\mathbb{F})
is another projective representation, then choosing lifts of
\varphi
and
\psi
gives

(2.11)

\alpha_{\varphi\otimes\psi}=\alpha_{\varphi}+\alpha_{\psi},

where the coefficient group
\mathbb{F}^{\times}
is written additively. In particular, tensoring with a linearizable projective representation does not change the obstruction class.

Thus conditions
(1)
–
(4)
are all equivalent to the vanishing of the obstruction index. By Theorem
1.1
, this is equivalent to linearizability.

The same obstruction may be defined for a homomorphism
G\to\mathrm{PGL}_{\otimes}(\mathbb{F})
, using the central extension

(2.12)

1\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}^{\times}\longrightarrow\mathrm{GL}_{\otimes}(\mathbb{F})\longrightarrow\mathrm{PGL}_{\otimes}(\mathbb{F})\longrightarrow 1.

Its vanishing is equivalent to liftability to
\mathrm{GL}_{\otimes}(\mathbb{F})
, by the same argument as in the finite-dimensional case. Moreover, for a projective representation
\varphi:G\to\mathrm{PGL}(V)
, the obstruction class is the same as the obstruction class of the induced map
G\to\mathrm{PGL}_{\otimes}(\mathbb{F})
. Therefore the stabilized conditions
(5)
and
(6)
are again equivalent to the vanishing of the same index, and hence to linearizability.

∎

Remark 2.15

For a general

As a corollary of Lemma
3.9
, we also obtain the following.

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
G
be a rationally acyclic group. Suppose a QCA representation
\varphi:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
is linearizable or trivially stably linearizable, then the stabilization
\varphi^{st}
is null-homotopic.

Proof.

The case for
\varphi
being trivially stably linearizable reduces to the case of being linearizable, as we could just replace
\varphi
by
\varphi\otimes c_{id}
without changing the induced map
BG\to B\mathcal{Q}(X)
.

Now suppose
\varphi:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
is linearizable. Recall that two group homomorphisms
f,g:G_{1}\to G_{2}
induce freely homotopic maps
Bf,Bg:BG_{1}\to BG_{2}
if and only if they differ by conjugations. Thus, the map
B\varphi:BG\to B\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
is freely homotopic to a map
B\varphi^{\prime}:BG\to B\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
such that
\varphi^{\prime}:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,q)
is linearly onsite. Lemma
3.9
then shows that

(\varphi^{\prime})^{st}:BG\xrightarrow{B\varphi^{\prime}}B\mathcal{Q}(X,q)\to B\mathcal{Q}(X)\to B\mathcal{Q}(X)^{+}\xrightarrow{\sim}K(\mathbf{C}(X))_{1}

is null-homotopic. This shows that the map

(\varphi)^{st}:BG\xrightarrow{B\varphi}B\mathcal{Q}(X,q)\to B\mathcal{Q}(X)\to B\mathcal{Q}(X)^{+}\xrightarrow{\sim}K(\mathbf{C}(X))_{1}

is freely null-homotopic. For a space
Y
and a connected
H
-space
Z
, based-homotopy class of maps
Y\to Z
are the same as free homotopy classes
Y\to Z
(see Proposition 1.4.3 of
[
35
]
, for example). Thus,
(\varphi^{\prime})^{st}
is also based null-homotopic. This concludes the proof.
∎

We are now able to prove Theorem
A
.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

Suppose
\varphi
is stably linearizable, then there is a linearizable
\psi:G\to\mathcal{Q}(X,r)
such that
(\varphi\otimes\psi)
is linearizable. Consider the stabilization map

(\varphi\otimes\psi)^{st}:BG\xrightarrow{B(\varphi\otimes\psi)}B\mathcal{Q}(X,qr)\to B\mathcal{Q}(X)\to B\mathcal{Q}(X)^{+}\xrightarrow{\sim}K(\mathbf{C}(X))_{1}.

This is homotopic to the map

BG\xrightarrow{(\varphi^{st},\psi^{st})}B\mathcal{Q}(X)^{+}\times B\mathcal{Q}(X)^{+}\xrightarrow{\mu}B\mathcal{Q}(X)^{+},

where
\mu
is the
H
-space structure on
B\mathcal{Q}(X)^{+}
. In particular, in the group
[BG,B\mathcal{Q}(X)^{+}]_{*}
,
(\varphi\otimes\psi)^{st}
represents the class
[\varphi^{st}]+[\psi^{st}]
. From Corollary
3.10
, we know
[\psi^{st}]=0
in this group. Thus we have that
0=[\varphi^{st}]+0
, so
\varphi^{st}
is also based null-homotopic.

Suppose
\varphi
is weakly linearizable, evidently the argument for null-homotopy only relied on the stable diagram being factorizable, and we analogously can do this modulo conjugations. Thus,
\varphi^{st}
is null-homotopic.
∎

Definition 3.11
.

Let
(\mathcal{C},\oplus,\otimes)
be a category defined as follows:

(1)

The objects of
\mathcal{C}
are assignments to each
x\in X
of a vector space
\mathbb{F}^{f(x)}
, where

.

(2)

The morphisms are all automorphisms given on each

by

\prod_{x\in X}\operatorname{GL}_{f(x)}(\mathbb{F}).

(3)

The operations
\oplus
and
\otimes
are given pointwise by direct sum and tensor product, respectively.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
consisting of those objects for which
f(x)\geq 1
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Observe that
(\mathcal{D},\otimes)
is a skeletal permutative category.

In particular,
B\mathcal{C}
is an example of an
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
-ring space (see the introduction of Chapter II of
[
9
]
). We also observe
\mathcal{D}
fits the description for Proposition 1.5 of
[

]
, which directly yields the following corollary.

Corollary 3.12
.

The commutator subgroup of
\operatorname{GL}_{\otimes}(X;{\mathbb{F}})
is perfect. Furthermore, the plus-construction
\operatorname{BGL}_{\otimes}(X;{\mathbb{F}})^{+}
at its commutator is the component at identity of
K(\mathcal{D})
. In particular,
\operatorname{BGL}_{\otimes}(X;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})^{+}
is a group-like H-space.

By changing the morphisms in
\mathcal{D}
from
\prod_{x\in X}\operatorname{GL}_{f(x)}({\mathbb{F}})
,
f(x)\geq 1
to
\prod_{x\in X}\operatorname{PGL}_{f(x)}({\mathbb{F}})
, Proposition 1.5 of
[

]
also applies to show

has perfect commutator subgroup. This fact combined with Corollary

proves Lemma

.

To prove Lemma

Let

Proof of Lemma
3.8
.

We first note that
\operatorname{BGL}(X;{\mathbb{F}})^{+}
is connected, so there is no issue at
\pi_{0}
or
H_{0}
. Write
\operatorname{QS}(X)
as a poset whose objects are functions
q:X\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
and
q\leq r
if
q(x)
divides
r(x)
for all
x\in X
. In this case, we write
r=q\cdot q^{\prime}
. For a natural number
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
, we also write
n
to denote the constant function
n:X\to\{n\}
.

We note that each
q\in\operatorname{QS}(X)
corresponds to the component
B\mathcal{C}_{q}
corresponding to
q\in\pi_{0}(B\mathcal{C})
. We write
[q]
to denote the corresponding element in
H_{0}(K\mathcal{C})
. Furthermore, for
q\leq r
with
r=q\cdot q^{\prime}
, there is a natural map
B\mathcal{C}_{q}\to B\mathcal{C}_{r}
given by multiplication by
q^{\prime}
.

The set-up above enables us to consider the following strict filtered colimit

L\coloneqq\operatorname{colim}_{q\in\operatorname{QS}(X)}B\mathcal{C}_{q}.

By Corollary
3.12
,
\operatorname{BGL}(X;{\mathbb{F}})^{+}
is the identity component of
K(\mathcal{D})
. Since
K(\mathcal{D})
is the group completion of
B\mathcal{D}
, the axiomatic definition of localization for group completion implies that for any commutative ring
R
,

H_{*}(K(\mathcal{D});R)\cong\pi_{0}(B\mathcal{D})^{-1}H_{*}(B\mathcal{D};R).

In particular, the homology at the identity component corresponds to applying the localization (which is equivalently a colimit) at the homology of the identity component of
B\mathcal{D}
. In particular, this shows

H_{*}(\operatorname{BGL}(X;{\mathbb{F}})^{+};R)\cong H_{*}(K(\mathcal{D})_{1};R)\cong H_{*}(L;R)\cong\operatorname{colim}_{q\in\operatorname{QS}(X)}H_{*}(B\mathcal{C}_{q};R).

For a prime
p
, we wish to show that
\tilde{H}_{*}(L;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})=0
. The universal coefficient theorem for homology will then imply the multiplication by
p
, in the sense of adding an element with itself
p-1
times, is an isomorphism on
\tilde{H}_{*}(L;{\mathbb{Z}})
. If this holds for all primes
p
, then
\tilde{H}_{*}(L;{\mathbb{Z}})
is rational. It then follows from Lemma
3.13
that
\operatorname{BGL}(X;{\mathbb{F}})^{+}
has rational homotopy groups.

Since
B(\mathcal{C})
is an
{\mathbb{E}}_{\infty}
-ring space, the homology group
H_{*}(B\mathcal{C};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})
has two ring structures under the H-space structure given by
\oplus
and the H-space structure given by
\otimes
. We denote products with respect to
\oplus
as
*
and products with respect to
\otimes
as
\bullet
. We now make a subclaim that for any
x\in H_{n}(B\mathcal{C}_{q};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})
for

and

, for
t
large enough,
x\bullet[p^{t}]\in H_{n}(B\mathcal{C}_{q\cdot p^{k}};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})
is
0
. This subclaim will imply that
\tilde{H}_{*}(L;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})=0
for the following reason. Since
\tilde{H}_{*}(L;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})=\operatorname{colim}_{q\in\operatorname{QS}(X)}\tilde{H}_{*}(B\mathcal{C}_{q};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})
is a filtered colimit of abelian groups, we just need to show that any element
x\in\tilde{H}_{*}(B\mathcal{C}_{q};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})
becomes
0
at some point in the colimit stage. By the subclaim, it suffices to find a path in the poset
\operatorname{QS}(X)
that goes through infinitely many
\bullet

[p]

It remains for us to show the subclaim. We first discuss a construction that will be helpful. In general for a space

has a coproduct map
H_{*}(Y;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\to H_{*}(Y\times Y;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\cong H_{*}(Y;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\otimes H_{*}(Y;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})
induced first by the diagonal map
\Delta:Y\to Y\times Y
and then the Kunneth isomorphism. We use
\psi:H_{*}(Y;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\to H_{*}(Y;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})^{\otimes p}
to denote the

\psi
satisfies a co-commutativity condition in the sense that
\psi
is invariant under any permutation
\sigma
on the tensors of
H_{*}(Y;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})^{\otimes p}
(i.e.,
\sigma\circ\psi=\psi
). Write
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}
as a basis of
H_{*}(Y;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})
where each

has pure degree. Write

Proposition II.1.5

computed how

Since

4.
Cohomological Interpretation of Linearization

Adapting a construction of Dror, we define a complete set of universal obstruction classes for the stable linearization of a QCA representation. The construction works over any field and also in the unitary case. These classes take values in group cohomology, with coefficients in the classification groups of QCA, together with
\mu({\mathbb{F}})
(the roots of unity in
{\mathbb{F}}
) or
U(1)
in the top degree. This agrees with predictions from
[
50
]
.

A noteworthy feature of this construction is that it produces a universal class in each degree. Consequently, any otherwise constructed obstruction class with values in the same cohomology group is weaker than or equal to ours: if the corresponding universal obstruction class vanishes, then any cohomology class obtained by other means must vanish as well.

4.1.
Dror’s Thesis

We apply this with

H{\mathbb{Z}}\to H{\mathbb{Q}}\to H\frac{{\mathbb{Q}}}{{\mathbb{Z}}}
. In the proof of Theorem
5.3
, we showed that
(H{\mathbb{Q}})^{k}(H{\mathbb{Z}})=0
for
k>0
, independently of the present lemma. The long exact sequence then shows that

(H\frac{{\mathbb{Q}}}{{\mathbb{Z}}})^{3}(H{\mathbb{Z}})\cong(H{\mathbb{Z}})^{4}(H{\mathbb{Z}})

are the integral cohomology operations in degree
4
. We will now show that
(H{\mathbb{Z}})^{4}(H{\mathbb{Z}})=0
. For convenience, we write
X=H{\mathbb{Z}}
. The proof of why
(H{\mathbb{Z}})^{4}(H{\mathbb{Z}})=0
will follow from a series of sub-statements which we prove one by one.

(a) The Steenrod algebra
H^{k}(H{\mathbb{F}}_{p};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})=(H{\mathbb{F}}_{p})^{k}(H{\mathbb{F}}_{p})
have explicit basis that have been computed. Here we will use the basis description of Serre-Cartan

45

(see also the
Sage

40

•

For any prime
p

H^{1}(H{\mathbb{F}}_{p};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})={\mathbb{F}}_{p}\{\beta\}
, generated by what is called the Bockstein map
\beta
, and
\beta^{2}=0
.

•

For any prime
p>2
, the next
k
for which
H^{k}(H{\mathbb{F}}_{p};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\neq 0
is
k=2(p-1)
.

•

H^{2}(H{\mathbb{F}}_{2};{\mathbb{F}}_{2})
is
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}\{\operatorname{Sq}^{2}\}
and
H^{3}(H{\mathbb{F}}_{2};{\mathbb{F}}_{2})={\mathbb{F}}_{2}\{\operatorname{Sq}^{3},\operatorname{Sq}^{2}\operatorname{Sq}^{1}\}
, where
\operatorname{Sq}^{1}
is identified as the Bockstein map.

(b)
H^{1}(X;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})=0
. Indeed, consider the fiber sequence
X\xrightarrow{\times p}X\to X/p=H{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
. This induces a long exact sequence

...\to H^{0}(X;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\xrightarrow{\times p}H^{0}(X;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\to H^{1}(H{\mathbb{F}}_{p};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\to H^{1}(X;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\xrightarrow{\times p}H^{1}(X;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\to...

Clearly both
\times p

{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
. This gives an exact sequence

0\to H^{0}(X;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\xrightarrow{\beta}H^{1}(H{\mathbb{F}}_{p};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\to H^{1}(X;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})\to 0,

where
\beta
is the Bockstein map. Since
H^{0}(X;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})=[H{\mathbb{Z}};H{\mathbb{F}}_{p}]=\operatorname{Hom}({\mathbb{Z}};{\mathbb{F}}_{p})={\mathbb{F}}_{p}
, the map
\beta
is an isomorphism by (a). Thus,
H^{1}(X;{\mathbb{F}}_{p})=0
.

(c)
H^{2}(X;{\mathbb{F}}_{2})=H^{3}(X;{\mathbb{F}}_{2})={\mathbb{F}}_{2}

H^{k}(X;{\mathbb{F}}_{2})
is 1-dimensional for
k\in\{2,3\}

5.2.
Unitary Case

We now prove that the
*
-QCA spaces
\mathbf{Q}^{*}(X)
(described in Section
3.3
) constructed by
[
26
]
are equivalent to a product of Eilenberg-MacLane spaces when
X={\mathbb{Z}}^{0},{\mathbb{Z}}^{1},{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. This is the unitary analog of the results over
\mathbb{C}
discussed above.

We first note the unitary QCA spaces are related to the following categories.

Definition 5.7
.

We consider three categories associated to unitary matrices

(1)

We let
\mathcal{U}_{\oplus}
denote the groupoid whose objects are
{\mathbb{C}}^{n},n\geq 0
and automorphisms are the unitary matrices
U(n)
.
\mathcal{U}_{\oplus}
is symmetric monoidal under direct sum.

(2)

We let
\mathcal{U}_{\otimes}
denote the groupoid whose objects are
{\mathbb{C}}^{n},n>0
and automorphisms are the unitary matrices
U(n)
.
\mathcal{U}_{\otimes}
is symmetric monoidal under tensor product.

(3)

We let
\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes}
denote the groupoid whose objects are
U(n),n>0
, and automorphisms are the projective unitary matrices
PU(n)
.
\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes}
is symmetric monoidal under tensor product.

Here we do not associate any topology to the set of morphisms.

Note that
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes})
is the group completion of the multiplicative monoid
{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
, which is
{\mathbb{Q}}_{>0}
. The delooping theorem of
[
26
]
implicitly shows that
K(\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes})\cong\Omega K(\mathbf{C}^{*}({\mathbb{Z}}^{1}))
. Taking
\pi_{0}
on both sides gives an isomorphism
{\mathbb{Q}}_{>0}\cong\pi_{1}(K(\mathbf{C}^{*}({\mathbb{Z}}^{1})))=\mathcal{Q}^{*}({\mathbb{Z}}^{1})/\mathcal{C}^{*}({\mathbb{Z}}^{1})
, which recovers the usual GNVW index
[
21
]
.

Proposition 5.8
.

The homotopy groups of
K(U_{\otimes})
fall in the following pattern.

\begin{array}[]{c|cccc}i&0&1&2&\geq 3\\
\hline\cr K_{i}(\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes})&{\mathbb{Q}}_{>0}&0&\frac{{\mathbb{Q}}}{{\mathbb{Z}}}\oplus(\text{rational group})&\text{rational group}\end{array}

Furthermore,
K_{2}(\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes})
can be non-canonically split into
U(1)\oplus(\text{rational group})
.

Remark 5.9
.

The splitting
K_{2}(\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes})\cong U(1)\oplus(\text{rational group})
being non-canonical is expected. The unitary QCA considered do not take into account the topology of the unitary matrices. Without topology, one does not have a canonical ordering on
{\mathbb{R}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\cong U(1)
, so the splitting is expected to be non-canonical.

In the proof of Proposition
5.8
, we adopt the following convention: for a group
G
with a non-discrete topology,
G^{\delta}
denotes its underlying discrete group.

Proof.

The case of
\pi_{0}
had been discussed right before this proposition. For
\pi_{1}
, Bass’s definition of
K_{1}
(see Proposition 1 of
[
53
]
) tells us that
K_{1}(\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes})=\operatorname{colim}_{n\in({\mathbb{Z}}_{>0},|)}(PU(n)^{\delta})^{ab}
. The main theorem of
[
20
]
shows that each
PU(n)^{\delta}
is perfect, so the colimit becomes
0
.

For the higher homotopy groups, we will construct a fibration sequence to compute them. Let
U_{\otimes}=\operatorname{colim}_{({\mathbb{Z}}_{>0},|)}U(n)
and
PU=\operatorname{colim}_{({\mathbb{Z}}_{>0},|)}PU(n)
, where the colimit is taken over the divisibility poset and an arrow
d\xrightarrow{\times k}n
is sent to the Kronecker product
-\otimes I_{k}
.

The short exact sequences
U(1)\to U(n)\to PU(n)
assemble to give a short exact sequence
U(1)\to U_{\otimes}\to PU
, which induces a fibration

(5.1)

BU(1)^{\delta}\to BU_{\otimes}^{\delta}\to BPU^{\delta}.

For a fibration
F\to E\to B
in general, the main theorem of
[
3
]
characterizes when
F^{+}\to E^{+}\to B^{+}
is a fibration: (a)
\pi_{1}(E)\to\pi_{1}(B)
sends the maximal perfect normal subgroup
\mathcal{P}(\pi_{1}(E))\subseteq\pi_{1}(E)
surjectively onto the maximal perfect normal subgroup
\mathcal{P}(\pi_{1}(B))\subseteq\pi_{1}(B)
, (b)
\mathcal{P}(\pi_{1}(B))
acts trivially on
\pi_{*}(F^{+})
.

The sequence in (
5.1
) satisfies condition (b) as
U(1)
is central in
U_{\otimes}
and the plus construction of
BU(1)^{\delta}
is itself. Condition (a) is also satisfied as the maximal perfect normal subgroup of
U_{\otimes}
is
SU_{\otimes}
, which is sent surjectively onto
PU^{\delta}
, which is itself perfect because each
PU(n)^{\delta}
is perfect. Note this is similar to Proposition 6.2 of
[
26
]
. Thus we have that

(5.2)

BU(1)^{\delta}\to(BU_{\otimes}^{\delta})^{+}\to(BPU^{\delta})^{+}

is a fibration. Furthermore, Proposition 3 of
[
53
]
implies that we can identify
(BU^{\delta}_{\otimes})^{+}=K(\mathcal{U_{\otimes}})_{1}
and
(BPU^{\delta})^{+}=K(\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes})_{1}
. Thus, we now have

(5.3)

BU(1)^{\delta}\to K(\mathcal{U}_{\otimes})_{1}\to K(\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes})_{1}

As
\pi_{*}(BU(1)^{\delta})
is concentrated in degree
1
, the long exact sequence in homotopy groups show that
\pi_{i}(K(\mathcal{U}_{\otimes})_{1})\cong\pi_{i}(K(\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes})_{1})
for
i\geq 3
. A similar argument as to that of Lemma
3.8
would show that the homotopy groups of
K(\mathcal{U_{\otimes}})_{1}
are all rational. In fact, one can use Theorem V.5.3 of
[
34
]
directly here to show that
K_{i}(\mathcal{U}_{\otimes})=K_{i}(\mathcal{U}_{\oplus})\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}
for
i>0
. Regardless, we have that
\pi_{i}(K(\mathcal{U}_{\otimes})_{1})
is rational for
i\geq 3
.

Finally for
i=2
, we investigate the long exact sequence:

0\to\pi_{2}(K(\mathcal{U}_{\otimes})_{1})\to\pi_{2}(K(\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes})_{1})\to U(1)\xrightarrow{\phi}\pi_{1}(K(\mathcal{U}_{\otimes})_{1})\to 0.

Since
\pi_{1}(K(\mathcal{U}_{\otimes})_{1})
is rational, the universal property of rationalization shows
\phi
factors through the rationalization
U(1)\cong{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}\to U(1)\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}\cong({\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}})/({\mathbb{Q}}/{\mathbb{Z}})
, which has kernel being the roots of unity
\frac{{\mathbb{Q}}}{{\mathbb{Z}}}
. Thus,
\frac{{\mathbb{Q}}}{{\mathbb{Z}}}\subseteq\ker(\phi)
. Since
\frac{{\mathbb{Q}}}{{\mathbb{Z}}}
is injective, we can decompose
\ker(\phi)=\frac{{\mathbb{Q}}}{{\mathbb{Z}}}\oplus\text{rational group}
. We also know the exact sequence splits off as

0\to\pi_{2}(K(\mathcal{U}_{\otimes})_{1})\to\pi_{2}(K(\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes})_{1})\to\ker(\phi)\to 0.

Since
\pi_{2}(K(\mathcal{U}_{\otimes})_{1})
is rational and hence injective, we have that
\pi_{2}(K(\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes})_{1})\cong\frac{{\mathbb{Q}}}{{\mathbb{Z}}}\oplus\text{rational group}
.

Using the fact that
K_{i}(\mathcal{U}_{\otimes})=K_{i}(\mathcal{U}_{\oplus})\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}
for
i>0
, we can obtain the stronger second statement in the proposition. The split inclusion
\frac{{\mathbb{Q}}}{{\mathbb{Z}}}\subset\frac{{\mathbb{R}}}{{\mathbb{Z}}}
gives
\frac{{\mathbb{R}}}{{\mathbb{Z}}}=\frac{{\mathbb{Q}}}{{\mathbb{Z}}}\oplus V
where
V
is a rational vector space. It suffices to show we can extract
V
out of
\pi_{2}(K(\mathcal{U}_{\otimes})_{1})
. Now we know that
\pi_{2}(K(\mathcal{U}_{\otimes})_{1})=\pi_{2}(K(\mathcal{U}_{\oplus})_{1})\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}
. Furthermore, Proposition 3 and Theorem 4 of
[
53
]
tells us that

\pi_{2}(K(\mathcal{U}_{\oplus})_{1})=H_{2}(BSU^{\delta};{\mathbb{Z}}),

where
\operatorname{SU}=\operatorname{colim}_{({\mathbb{Z}},\leq)}\operatorname{SU}(n)
is the infinite special unitary group.
[
1
]
shows that
H_{2}(BSU^{\delta};{\mathbb{Z}})\cong H_{2}(SU(n);{\mathbb{Z}})
for a finite
n
sufficiently large (see also Section 4 of
[
36
]
).
[
42
]
shows that for any non-trivial and connected Lie group
G
,
H_{2}(BG^{\delta};{\mathbb{Z}})
surjects onto an uncountable rational vector space. Thus,
H_{2}(BSU^{\delta};{\mathbb{Z}})=H_{2}(BSU(n)^{\delta})
surjects onto an uncountable rational vector space. This shows that
\pi_{2}(K(\mathcal{U}_{\otimes})_{1})
contains an uncountable rational vector space, so we can split
V
non-canonically off. This shows that
K_{2}(\mathcal{PU}_{\otimes})\cong U(1)\oplus(\text{rational group})
.
∎

Remark 5.10
.

Let
R
be a ring and
K^{\otimes}(R)
be the group completion of
\bigsqcup_{n>0}\operatorname{BGL}_{n}(R)
under tensor product. Note that the usual K-theory space
K(R)
is the group completion of
\bigsqcup_{n\geq 0}\operatorname{BGL}_{n}(R)
under direct sum. The statement that
K_{i}(\mathcal{U}_{\otimes})=K_{i}(\mathcal{U}_{\oplus})\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}
for
i>0
has an algebraic analog that
K_{i}^{\otimes}(R)=K_{i}(R)\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}
for
i>0
. This is Proposition 5 of
[
53
]
and follows from Theorem VII.5.3 of
[
33
]
. Recently, the expository note
[
13
]
also gave an argument to explain how to prove the algebraic case, which can be adapted to the unitary case.

Using Proposition
5.8
, we can compute the structure of the unitary QCA spaces similarly as in the complex case, which is the statement for Theorem
E
(3).

Theorem 5.11
.

For
n=0,1,2
, the space
K(\mathbf{C}^{*}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n}))

Appendix A
Low Degree Obstruction Classes

Here, we give descriptions of several low-degree obstruction classes that already appear in the literature. In degree
1
, this obstruction agrees with Definition
4.8
. In degree
2
, these obstructions take values in the same cohomology groups as the universal obstruction classes of Definition
4.8
. Their construction depends on the choice of a hyperplane, and the dependence on this choice, as well as the relation between different choices, had remained open. By comparing these obstructions with the universal obstruction class, we resolve this ambiguity: the obstruction class vanishes for one such choice if and only if it vanishes for any other.

In degree
3
, the constructions described here take values in a different cohomology group, but are refined by the universal obstruction classes. Furthermore, the converse is true over finite groups.

From the descriptions, it becomes clear that definitions in this manner would quickly get out of hand for higher cohomological degrees.

A.1.
Degree-1

Given a QCA representation
\varphi:G\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
, the degree-1 obstruction class is simply the composite map

(A.1)

G\xrightarrow{\varphi}\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})/\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})

viewed as a class in
H^{1}(BG;\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})/\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}))
. This is exactly the degree-1 universal obstruction class. The unitary case is also nearly identical.

A.2.
Degree-2

Here we describe a degree-2 obstruction class that first lies in

(A.2)

H^{2}\bigl(BG;\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Z}^{n-1})/\mathcal{C}^{sp}(\mathbb{Z}^{n-1})\bigr)

associated to a QCA representation
\varphi:G\to\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
for
n\geq 1
. Note that for
n>1
,
\mathcal{C}^{sp}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n-1})=\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n-1})
. The following construction is inspired by the
H^{2}
obstruction class in
[
30
]
constructed for
n=2
in the unitary case. Throughout, we assume that the degree-one obstruction vanishes, so that
\varphi
takes values in
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
.

Construction A.1
.

Let
H\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
be an oriented hyperplane, and write
H^{+}
and
H^{-}
for the lattice points lying in the two closed half-spaces, with
H^{+}\cup H^{-}=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. One can define a truncation operation

(A.3)

t_{+}:\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}(H^{+})

by retaining, in each layer of a circuit, only those local automorphisms supported entirely inside
H^{+}
. This operation is not a group homomorphism. It is also not unique because each
\alpha\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
has infinitely many different circuit expressions. However, the difference between any two truncations
t_{+}(\alpha)
and
t^{\prime}_{+}(\alpha)
,

(A.4)

t_{+}(\alpha)^{-1}t^{\prime}_{+}(\alpha),

acts as identity on all matrix algebras sufficiently far from the hyperplane
H
. Thus it is supported in a bounded neighborhood of
H
, and may be regarded as an element of
\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Z}^{n-1})
, after identifying such a neighborhood with
\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}\times[-R,R]
. For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
, the same can be said for

(A.5)

t_{+}(\alpha)t_{+}(\beta)t_{+}(\alpha\beta)^{-1}.

Applying this to
\alpha=\varphi(g)
and
\beta=\varphi(h)
gives a function

(A.6)

\tau_{H^{+}}:G\times G\longrightarrow\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}),\qquad\tau_{H^{+}}(g,h)=t_{+}(\varphi(g))t_{+}(\varphi(h))t_{+}(\varphi(gh))^{-1}.

One may check, using associativity of multiplication, that
\tau_{H^{+}}
defines a
2
-cocycle after passing to the quotient by (special) circuits:

(A.7)

\overline{\tau}_{H^{+}}\in Z^{2}\bigl(G;\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Z}^{n-1})/\mathcal{C}^{sp}(\mathbb{Z}^{n-1})\bigr).

And for different choices of truncations, the cocycles obtained differ by a coboundary. Indeed,

\overline{t^{\prime}_{+}(\varphi(g))t^{\prime}_{+}(\varphi(h))t^{\prime}_{+}(\varphi(gh))^{-1}}=\overline{t_{+}(\varphi(g))t_{+}(\varphi(h))t_{+}(\varphi(gh))^{-1}}\cdot\overline{\mu(g)\mu(h)\mu(gh)^{-1}},

with
\mu(g)\coloneqq t_{+}(\varphi(g))^{-1}t^{\prime}_{+}(\varphi(g))
.
Its cohomology class, denoted by
\alpha_{\varphi}(H^{+})
, is the degree-2 obstruction associated to the chosen oriented hyperplane
H
. This construction also adapts similarly to the unitary case.

Reversing the orientation of
H
exchanges the two half-spaces and changes the resulting class by a sign. Thus the construction depends on the choice of oriented hyperplane. It is less clear how to compare the classes obtained from two non-parallel hyperplanes. However, we will now show that
they are actually all equivalent
to the universal obstruction class
u_{2}
in Definition
4.8
. More precisely,

Proposition A.2
.

Fix an oriented hyperplane
H
with postive half-space
H^{+}
. When
G=\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})
and the representation is the inclusion map
\iota:\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{Q}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})
, the 2-cochain
\bar{\tau}_{H^{+}}
is a 2-cocycle that is a model for the universal central extension of
\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})
. The analogous statement holds for the unitary case.

Here by a model, we just mean that the cohomology class
\alpha_{\varphi}(H^{+})
gives a central extension that has the universal property of being a central extension. Evidently, the 2-cochain
\bar{\tau}_{H^{+}}
is natural with respect to group homomorphisms. Any QCA representation

whose degree-1 obstruction vanish must factor through the circuit group
G\to\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{Q}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})
. The universal obstruction class is also natural with respect to group homomorphisms. Thus,

Corollary A.3
.

The cohomology class
\alpha_{\varphi}(H^{+})
is a model for the universal obstruction class
u_{2}(\varphi)
for any
\varphi:G\to\mathcal{C}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})
. The analogous statement holds for the unitary case.

Corollary A.4
.

Let
\varphi
be a QCA representation over
\mathbb{F}
. Vanishing of the cohomology class

is independent of the choice of hyperplane
H.

Corollary A.5
.

Let
\varphi
be a unitary QCA representation. Vanishing of the cohomology class
\alpha_{\varphi}(H^{+})
is independend of the choice of hyperplane
H.

Remark A.6

(Computation)

A hyperplane

for

Remark A.7

(Dependence on hyperplane)
.

The resulting classes need not be canonically comparable for two hyperplanes

\ell_{t_{+}(g)}\to B\mathcal{C}(X\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0})^{+}
. This is the lift on the 1-skeleton. A 2-cell in
B\mathcal{C}(X\times{\mathbb{Z}})
is given by a triangle whose edges are
g,h,gh
. Going along the boundary of the triangle gives exactly
t_{+}(g)t_{+}(h)t_{+}(gh)^{-1}
, where we need to flip the last edge. The element
t_{+}(g)t_{+}(h)t_{+}(gh)^{-1}
is supported on
X\times\{0,...,m\}
for some
m
sufficiently large and hence naturally restricts to the image of the fiber. Thus,

\gamma^{2}(g,h)=t_{+}(g)t_{+}(h)t_{+}(gh)^{-1}.

This shows that a choice of
\gamma_{2}
could have been
\bar{\tau}_{\iota}
, so their cohomology classes agree. This concludes the proof.
∎

Degree-3

We recall the construction of

Construction A.15
.

Choose a point

and let

(A.9)

Then we again have

Applying this to

\displaystyle=t_{+}(\varphi(g))t_{+}(\varphi(h))t_{+}(\varphi(k))

\displaystyle=t_{+}(\varphi(g))\tau(h,k)t_{+}(\varphi(g))^{-1}t_{+}(\varphi(g))t_{+}(\varphi(hk))

\displaystyle=t_{+}(\varphi(g))\tau(h,k)t_{+}(\varphi(g))^{-1}\tau(g,hk)t_{+}(\varphi(ghk)).

Appendix B
1D Arithmetic QCA Representations

We describe a family of 1D QCA representations over the rationals. They display obstruction classes not found for QCA representations over
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
, or the unitary case. Therefore, they have been missing from the literature.

B.1.
Setup

We place

(B.1)

V_{i}\cong\mathbb{Q}^{2}

to be the local vector space at the site
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, with ordered basis

(B.2)

|0\rangle,\ |1\rangle.

We use the ordered tensor-product basis

|00\rangle,\ |01\rangle,\ |10\rangle,\ |11\rangle

on
V_{i}\otimes V_{i+1}
.
The spin system correspondes to
q\equiv 2
with algebra

(B.3)

\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{Z},q)=\bigotimes_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{End}(V_{i}).

Fix a parameter
s\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
. Eventually we will let

s
a square-free number. Define the two-site diagonal
gate

(B.4)

D_{s}=\operatorname{diag}(1,s,1,1)\in GL_{4}(\mathbb{Q}),

and the onsite gate

(B.5)

X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\in GL_{2}(\mathbb{Q}).

Technically, we are considering the elements they represent in the projective linear group. Nevertheless, we use the representatives for shorthand.

Let
D_{s,i,i+1}
denote
D_{s}
acting on
\mathrm{End}(V_{i})\otimes\mathrm{End}(V_{i+1})
and let

X_{i}
denote
X
acting on
\mathrm{End}(V_{i})
both by conjugation. We define

A_{s}=\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}D_{s,i,i+1}=\left(\prod_{i\text{ odd}}D_{s,i,i+1}\right)\left(\prod_{i\text{ even}}D_{s,i,i+1}\right),\qquad B=\prod_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}X_{i}.

Since the gates
D_{s,i,i+1}
are all commuting, the product is well-defined and

A_{s}
may be implemented as depth two circuit by separating even and odd edges. The circuit
B
is an onsite
depth-one circuit.

The basic calculation is as follows. Define

L_{s}=\operatorname{diag}(1,s)\in GL_{2}(\mathbb{Q}).

Then

D_{s}(X\otimes X)D_{s}^{-1}(X\otimes X)=\operatorname{diag}(1,s,s^{-1},1)=L_{s}^{-1}\otimes L_{s}.

Equivalently, on the edge
(i,i+1)
,

D_{s,i,i+1}(X_{i}X_{i+1})D_{s,i,i+1}^{-1}(X_{i}X_{i+1})=L_{s,i}^{-1}L_{s,i+1},

where
L_{s,i}
denotes
L_{s}
acting on
\mathrm{End}(V_{i})
.

Now let
[m,n]\subset\mathbb{Z}
be a finite interval and set

A_{s,[m,n]}=\prod_{i=m}^{n-1}D_{s,i,i+1},\qquad B_{[m,n]}=\prod_{i=m}^{n}X_{i}.

Using the preceding edge identity and telescoping, one obtains

\displaystyle A_{s,[m,n]}B_{[m,n]}A_{s,[m,n]}^{-1}B_{[m,n]}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=m}^{n-1}L_{s,i}^{-1}L_{s,i+1}

\displaystyle=L_{s,m}^{-1}L_{s,n}.

Thus the commutator is purely a boundary term. In particular,
A_{s}
and
B
commute as the commutator vanishes telescopically.

We now truncate to the right half-line. Write

A_{s,+}=\prod_{i\geq 0}D_{s,i,i+1},\qquad B_{+}=\prod_{i\geq 0}X_{i}.

The discussion above implies

A_{s,+}B_{+}A_{s,+}^{-1}B_{+}^{-1}=L_{s,0}^{-1}.

B.2.
QCA representations

Let

G=\langle\alpha,\beta\mid\alpha\beta=\beta\alpha,\ \beta^{2}=1\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2.

Thus a general
element of
G
is written as

g=(a,\epsilon),\qquad a\in\mathbb{Z},\quad\epsilon\in\{0,1\},

with

(a,\epsilon)(b,\delta)=(a+b,\epsilon+\delta\bmod 2).

We represent
\alpha
by
A_{s}
and
\beta
by
B
to get QCA representations

(B.6)

\varphi_{s}:G\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Z})\subset\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{Z}).

B.3.
Degree-2 obstruction

Equipped with Proposition
A.10
, we compute
u_{2}(\varphi_{s})
through truncation at
0
.

Since
A_{s}
and
B
commute, we fix the ordering for their product to be
A_{s}B
with truncation

(B.7)

t_{+}(BA_{s})=t_{+}(A_{s}B)=A_{s,+}B_{+}.

Then

(B.8)

\tau((1,0),(0,1))=A_{s,+}B_{+}(A_{s,+}B_{+})^{-1}=I,

whereas

(B.9)

\tau((0,1),(1,0))=B_{+}A_{s,+}(A_{s,+}B_{+})^{-1}=L_{s,0}.

Observe that
[L_{s,0}]\in\mathrm{PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{Q})
does not lie in
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{Q})
whenever
s\notin(\mathbb{Q}^{\times})^{2}
, for instance when
s=2
. Indeed,
\det L_{s,0}=s
, while replacing
L_{s,0}
by another representative of the same projective class multiplies the determinant by a square in
\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
. Thus no representative can have determinant
1
unless
s
is a square.

Moreover, this obstruction is not removed by stabilization. For any
k>0
, the class

(B.10)

[L_{s,0}\otimes I_{k}]\in\mathrm{PGL}_{2k}(\mathbb{Q})

has determinant
s^{k}
. If
[L_{s,0}\otimes I_{k}]
were represented by an element of
\mathrm{SL}_{2k}(\mathbb{Q})
, then
s^{k}
would have to lie in
(\mathbb{Q}^{\times})^{2k}
, which again forces
s
to be a square in
\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
. Hence, for
s\notin(\mathbb{Q}^{\times})^{2}
, the stabilized class remains nontrivial in

(B.11)

\mathrm{PGL}_{\otimes}(\mathbb{Q})/\mathrm{PSL}_{\otimes}(\mathbb{Q}).

It follows that the image

(B.12)

\bar{\tau}\in Z^{2}\bigl(G;\mathrm{PGL}_{\otimes}(\mathbb{Q})/\mathrm{PSL}_{\otimes}(\mathbb{Q})\bigr)

is a nontrivial cocycle whenever
s\notin(\mathbb{Q}^{\times})^{2}
. Thus this construction yields an infinite family of QCA representations with nontrivial obstruction class
u_{2}(\varphi_{s})
. This also gives an infinite family of QCA representations that are not stably linearizable.

Of course, examples such as these can be defined for QCA representations over any number field. Therefore, we expect there to be a rich connection between QCA representations and number theory.
