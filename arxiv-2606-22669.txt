Clipping the Price of Adaptivity at the Tail
Itai Kreisler Yair Carmon Oliver Hinder

Abstract
Adaptive stochastic convex optimization (SCO) methods face a fundamental “price of adaptivity” barrier: under the standard set of assumptions, they cannot efficiently adapt to large uncertainty in both the initial distance to optimality and the Lipschitz constant. We circumvent this barrier by requiring a small amount of additional structure common to many learning problems. Specifically, we assume that the objective decomposes into a model and a loss function, enabling us to intervene by modifying the model’s output before it passes to the loss function. Under this assumption, we design a method that clips the learned model output in tail events where it deviates too much from the output of a fixed reference model. Our method matches the optimal bounds for known-parameter SCO up to logarithmic factors in the uncertainty in the distance and Lipschitz parameters, thus efficiently adapting to large uncertainty in both.

Introduction

This paper studies machine learning objectives composed of a model and a loss function.
Specifically, for a model
m
and a loss function
h
, the learning problem can be written as

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm minimize}_{x}F\left(x\right),\quad\text{where}\quad F\left(x\right)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[h\left(m\left(x;s\right);s\right)\right].

(1)

Such model–loss decompositions arise broadly in practice, e.g., neural networks with various loss functions.
However, optimization theory typically analyzes the more general problem of minimizing a stochastic function
f
without assuming this internal structure:

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm minimize}_{x}F\left(x\right),\quad\text{where}\quad F\left(x\right)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[f\left(x;s\right)\right].

(2)

Stochastic convex optimization is a fundamental problem in machine learning and serves as the foundation for classical stochastic gradient methods.
While classical methods can attain the optimal optimality gap, they typically hinge on knowing the Lipschitz parameter
L_{\star}
and the distance
D_{\star}
from the initial point to the optimum.

Parameter-free

relax the requirement for precise knowledge of the problem parameters, aiming to achieve a near-optimal optimality gap using only loose estimates.
Lower bounds

L_{\star}
and

.
For example,

show that if
\rho

\Omega(\min\left\{\ell,\rho\right\}\sqrt{\ln\left(1/\delta\right)}/\sqrt{T})

This shows that it is difficult to adapt to high uncertainty in both the Lipschitz constant and distance to optimality.
However, this lower bound applies to the general stochastic convex structure given in

Our contributions

We identify that the aforementioned lower bound for parameter-free methods is intimately tied to tail events: rare samples for which the optimized function behaves very differently from the bulk of the distribution.
Such rare samples may go unobserved by the optimization algorithm, making it impossible to reliably characterize the tail behavior of the stochastic noise.

However, under the model–loss structure of
Equation

1
, it is possible to detect and mitigate these rare events, circumventing this lower bound.
In particular, under this model–loss structure, our key idea is to clip the output of the model at
inference-time
.
This clipping occurs on rare events in which the model provides an extremely large outlier prediction.
In these cases, clipping prevents us from paying a high price for a wildly incorrect prediction.
We present two methods that use this idea: one that prioritizes computational efficiency and the other that prioritizes sample efficiency.
Both methods build on
Lawrence et al. 2025
, which focused on the standard parameter-free optimization paradigm, i.e.,
Equation

2
.

In the computational setting, we propose a method that performs grid search over hyperparameters, generates candidate weights using a standard optimization algorithm such as SGD, and then selects one of the resulting weights paired with an associated clipping parameter.
We show that this method, with probability at least
1-\delta
, achieves an optimality gap of
\widetilde{O}(L_{\star}D_{\star}\sqrt{\ln\left(1/\delta\right)}/\sqrt{T})
where
T
is the number of gradient and function evaluations made by the method, and
\widetilde{O}(\cdot)
hides poly-logarithmic factors.
Notably, our method only incurs a logarithmic cost in uncertainty in the problem parameters compared with the polynomial dependence exhibited in the lower bounds.
This optimality gap beats the lower bounds proved in
Carmon and Hinder 2024
,
Attia and Koren 2024
,
Khaled and Jin 2024
, which scale linearly with
\min\{\ell,\rho\}
.

In the sample complexity setting, we propose a method that carefully solves a regularized optimization problem and then applies inference-time clipping to the resulting learned model.
This gives a suboptimality gap of
O(L_{\star}D_{\star}\sqrt{\ln\left(1/\delta\right)}/\sqrt{N})
for the Lipschitz case, where
N
is the number of samples made available to the algorithm.
This matches lower bounds for parameter-known stochastic convex optimization
[
6
]
.

Lower bounds

In non-smooth stochastic convex optimization with known Lipschitz constant,
L_{\star}
, and initial distance to optimality,
D_{\star}
, classical results show that the expected loss is
\Omega\left(L_{\star}D_{\star}/\sqrt{T}\right)

[
25
,
10
]
.
For the high-probability case,
Carmon and Hinder 2024
proves the lower bound of
\Omega\left(L_{\star}D_{\star}\sqrt{\ln\left(1/\delta\right)}/\sqrt{T}\right)
. These lower bounds have matching upper bounds
[
27
, e.g.,]
, and consequently define the optimal optimality gaps for non-smooth stochastic convex optimization.

Recently,
Carmon and Hinder 2024
proves that parameter-free methods incur a penalty due to uncertainty in the Lipschitz parameter
L_{\star}
and the distance
D_{\star}
from the initial point to the optimum.
Specifically, they assume that while
L_{\star}
and
D_{\star}
are unknown, they are bounded such that
L_{\star}\in\left[1,\ell\right]
and
D_{\star}\in\left[1,\rho\right]
for some known values
\ell
and
\rho
.
They prove that if stochastic gradients are almost surely bounded by
L_{\star}
, the optimality gap necessarily suffers a multiplicative degradation of at least
\Omega\left(\min\left\{\ell,\rho\right\}\sqrt{\ln\left(1/\delta\right)}/\sqrt{T}\right)
relative to the optimal rate.
Furthermore, they prove that in the second-moment setting, in which
L_{\star}^{2}
bounds the expectation of the stochastic gradient norm squared, the multiplicative penalty is at least
\Omega\left(\min\left\{\ell,\rho\right\}\right)
.
This implies that, in heavy-tailed settings, achieving a near-optimal optimality gap is impossible without prior knowledge of at least one of the problem parameters.

Attia and Koren 2024
and
Khaled and Jin 2024
also provide lower bounds for parameter-free optimization.

Attia and Koren 2024
derives comparable lower bounds to
Carmon and Hinder 2024
in the Lipschitz case.

Khaled and Jin 2024
provides a slightly weaker lower bound than
Attia and Koren 2024
, demonstrating that the multiplicative price incurred due to parameter uncertainty cannot be bounded by any polylogarithmic term.

Critically, all these lower bounds focus on the standard stochastic optimization setting, i.e.,
Equation

2
, allowing us to beat them by considering the model–loss structure, i.e.,
Equation

1
. We note that
some
of the lower bounds presented in
Carmon and Hinder 2024
do transfer to
Equation

1
; we discuss this in more detail in
Section

6
and its supplement,
Appendix

D
.

Reliable Model Selection

Lawrence et al. 2025
demonstrates that the standard holdout model selection approach can fail catastrophically in SCO.
To address this, they introduce a general framework for reliable model selection.
They also propose an optimization method for the sample complexity setting.
However, both methods require a good estimate of the Lipschitz constant (i.e., within a factor of
\sqrt{T}
of the true value).
In this paper, we build upon and extend many of the results and proof techniques introduced by
Lawrence et al. 2025
, eliminating the need for a good estimate of the Lipschitz constant.

Improper learning

In optimization and machine learning theory, one typically fixes a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
(e.g., linear classifiers) as a representation for the target concept class.
A learning algorithm is said to be
proper
if it is required to output a hypothesis from
\mathcal{H}
.
In contrast, an algorithm is called
improper
if it is allowed to output any predictor, possibly outside
\mathcal{H}
, as long as the predictor attains low prediction error under the data distribution.
This relaxation often substantially enlarges the space of candidate predictors and consequently can yield learning algorithms that succeed in settings where proper learning is statistically or computationally infeasible
[
23
,
12
, e.g.,]
.

The algorithms we present in this work can be viewed as improper, as they output a weight
x
and a clipping parameter
c
for model
m_{c}\left(x;\cdot\right)
, rather than just outputting a weight
x
for the model
m\left(x;\cdot\right)
.
Unlike many works in this field that output an ensemble of predictors aggregated via majority vote, our approach outputs a single model, using the initial hypothesis solely to constrain the predictions.

A particularly relevant piece of work in this area is
Vovk 2006
, who provide an online learning method that exploits a loss–model structure in the least squares setup.
However, that work assumes known bounds on the labels.

A computationally efficient parameter-free method that exploits model–loss structure

In this section, we propose a parameter-free method that achieves a near-optimal optimality gap.
To this end, our method operates under the model–loss decomposition described in
Equation

1
and introduces clipping at the model output level.

Our parameter-free method combines grid search, a known optimization method, and a model selection procedure.
By performing grid search over possible hyperparameters and applying a known optimization method, such as SGD, at each grid point, we generate a collection of candidate weights, one of which attains a small optimality gap.
We then introduce a model selection method that, given this collection of candidates, selects a candidate paired with a clipping parameter such that the selected pair achieves a loss close to the minimum loss among all candidates.

The prior work that we leverage

Their algorithm takes as input a reference model

4.4
Proof outline for
Proposition

1

The proof for the case
m\in\mathcal{I}^{\infty,\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,\beta}}_{\text{SM-Lip}}
follows steps similar to the proof for the case
m\in\mathcal{I}^{\infty,\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,\beta}}_{\text{Lip}}
.
However, some changes are needed to adapt the proof to the case
m\in\mathcal{I}^{\infty,\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,\beta}}_{\text{SM-Lip}}
.
We now present an outline of the proof.

Unlike the Lipschitz case, in the second-moment-Lipschitz case the quantity
\|m_{c_{i}}\left(x_{g_{i}};s\right)-m\left(x_{0};s\right)\|_{\beta}
may be unbounded with non-negligible probability.
Thus, for any choice of
c_{g,0}
we cannot guarantee that
c_{g,0}
bounds
\|m_{c_{i}}\left(x_{g_{i}};s\right)-m\left(x_{0};s\right)\|_{\beta}
.
Therefore, unlike what we showed for the Lipschitz case, we cannot conclude that
F_{c_{g,0}}\left(x_{g}\right)=F\left(x_{g}\right)
in the second-moment-Lipschitz case.
Instead, to balance the trade-off between a small value of
\left\lvert F_{c_{g,0}}\left(x_{g}\right)-F\left(x_{g}\right)\right\rvert
and a small risk term
\tau_{g,0}
, we choose
c_{g,0}\coloneqq\text{binary-ceil}\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{\ln\left(4/\delta\right)}}L_{\star}\|x_{g}-x_{0}\|_{\alpha};\underline{\ell}\|x_{g}-x_{0}\|_{\alpha}\right)
.
Leveraging the facts that
h\left(\cdot;s\right)
is 1-Lipschitz, that
m
is
L_{\star}
-second-moment-Lipschitz, and our choice of
c_{g,0}
, we obtain that
\lvert F_{c_{g,0}}\left(x_{g}\right)-F\left(x_{g}\right)\rvert\leq L_{\star}\|x_{g}-x_{0}\|_{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\ln\left(4/\delta\right)}{N}}
, which is sufficiently small.

By using Theorem 10 of
Maurer and Pontil 2009
(see
Theorem

5
), with high probability we bound the empirical variance
\sqrt{v\left(x_{g},c_{g,0}\right)}
using the true variance and the clipping parameter.
Thus, our choice of
c_{g,0}
results in a sufficiently small risk term:
\tau_{g,0}\leq O\left(\overline{L}_{\star}\|x_{g}-x_{0}\|_{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\ln\left(16\tilde{a}/\delta\right)}{N}}\sqrt{\frac{\ln\left(16\tilde{a}/\delta\right)}{\ln\left(4/\delta\right)}}\right)
.

The above suffices to prove that
Algorithm

3
selects a good candidate from
\left\{\left(x_{g},c_{g,i}\right)\right\}_{\left(g,i\right)\in\mathcal{G}\times\left(\left[n\right]\cup\left\{0\right\}\right)}
, similarly to how it was proven in the Lipschitz case.
All that is left is to show that adding the candidate
\left(x_{g},c_{g,0}\right)
does not affect
Algorithm

3
.
We show that since
n=\lceil 4\ln\left(4/\delta\right)\rceil
is sufficiently large,
c_{g,0}\geq c_{g,n}
.
This results in the validation loss for both clipping parameters
c_{g,0}
and
c_{g,n}
being very close:

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{c_{g,0}}\left(x_{g};s_{i}\right)-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{c_{g,n}}\left(x_{g};s_{i}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2c_{g,0}\frac{n-1}{N}\leq 16\overline{L}_{\star}\|x_{g}-x_{0}\|_{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\ln\left(4/\delta\right)}{N}}.

As a consequence, we artificially increase the value of
\tau_{g,0}
by
16\overline{L}_{\star}\|x_{g}-x_{0}\|_{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\ln\left(4/\delta\right)}{N}}
, maintaining a small enough risk term
\tau_{g,0}
.
Thus, we obtain that
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{c_{g,n}}\left(x_{g};s_{i}\right)+\tau_{g,c_{g,n}}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{c_{g,0}}\left(x_{g};s_{i}\right)+\tau_{g,c_{g,0}}
.
This means two things:
(i) The safe set
\mathcal{F}
calculated in
Algorithm

3
remains unchanged, except that
\left(x_{g},c_{g,0}\right)
may also be included in
\mathcal{F}
, even though we added
\left(x_{g},c_{g,0}\right)
as a candidate.
(ii) If the candidate
\left(x_{g},c_{g,0}\right)
is in
\mathcal{F}
, then
\left(x_{g},c_{g,n}\right)
is also in
\mathcal{F}
.
Now, as we can generalize
Lemma

1
to hold for any candidate in
\mathcal{F}
and not only to the result of
Algorithm

3
(see
Lemma

3
), we can assume that the additional candidate
\left(x_{g},c_{g,0}\right)
does not affect
Algorithm

3
.

Finally, by combining all the above results as in the Lipschitz case, we conclude
Proposition

1
.
We present the full proof in
Appendix

H

5
A parameter-free method with the best possible sample complexity

This section focuses on producing a parameter-free method that matches the sample complexity of optimization with known parameters.
Sample complexity refers to the number of function samples that the algorithm uses, and can be contrasted with stochastic gradient evaluation complexity (the subject of the previous section), which focuses on the number of stochastic gradient evaluations.
The sample complexity perspective allows algorithms to perform operations that may be computationally infeasible in practice, such as computing an exact minimizer of the empirical objective over the
N

6
Lower bounds for the model–loss setup

Our algorithms succeed in achieving a near-optimal optimality gap of
\widetilde{O}\left(L_{\star}D_{\star}\sqrt{\ln\left(1/\delta\right)}/\sqrt{N}\right)
,
surpassing the lower bounds in the case of uncertainty in both the Lipschitz constant and the distance from the initial point to the optimum.
We achieve this because we assume that the optimized function can be decomposed into a model and a loss, and introduce a method to clip the model output.
Therefore, at first glance, it is not clear that any of the known lower bounds apply.

However, in some cases, we can decompose a function
f
into a model
m
and a loss function
h
such that all the complexity of
f
is inherited by
h
.
In particular, consider lower-bound constructions in which each function
f_{i}
is
L_{\star}
-Lipschitz for a known constant
L_{\star}
.
For each such
f_{i}
, we decompose
f_{i}
into
m_{i}\left(x;s\right)\coloneqq xL_{\star}
and
h_{i}\left(x;s\right)\coloneqq f_{i}\left(x/L_{\star};s\right)
.
Under this decomposition, each
m_{i}
is
L_{\star}
-Lipschitz, and each
h_{i}
is
1
-Lipschitz.
Moreover, having the ability to modify the output of
m_{i}\left(x;s\right)
is equivalent to modifying the input
x
.
Consequently, any lower-bound argument that applies to the original functions
f_{i}
continues to apply in the model–loss setting.
Therefore, the lower bound
\Omega\left(L_{\star}D_{\star}\sqrt{\ln\left(1/\delta\right)}/\sqrt{N}\right)
established in
Carmon and Hinder 2024
for the case where
L_{\star}
and
D_{\star}
are known also holds in our setting.
Hence, the optimality gaps established in this work are still near-optimal even in the model–loss setting.

In contrast, not all lower bounds carry over to the model–loss setting.
For instance, the lower bounds established in
Carmon and Hinder 2024
no longer apply, as we obtain strictly better optimality gaps in this work.
In
Carmon and Hinder 2024
, the Lipschitz constant
L_{\star}
is unknown, and only coarse lower and upper bounds on
L_{\star}
are assumed.
Their construction involves two types of functions: one with a uniformly small Lipschitz constant, and another that has a small Lipschitz constant for most samples but a large Lipschitz constant on a rare subset of samples.
Consequently, the reduction argument from the previous paragraph does not apply to this setting.
In our framework, however, the ability to clip the model output limits the loss contribution of rare high-Lipschitz samples.
This mechanism prevents the adversarial behavior exploited in
Carmon and Hinder 2024
, enabling us to achieve near-optimal optimality gaps despite the unknown Lipschitz constant.

In
Appendix

D
, we formally state the lower bound
\Omega\left(L_{\star}D_{\star}\sqrt{\ln\left(1/\delta\right)}/\sqrt{N}\right)
that carries over to the model–loss setting.

Formally stated lower bound

Although our method clips the model output to achieve improved optimality gaps, we establish lower bounds that apply more broadly to any method that modifies the model output.
Specifically, we consider algorithms that, instead of outputting a weight
x\in\mathcal{X}
, output a function
q^{m}:\mathbb{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
that represents a generic modification to the model output.
In the finite case, we restrict the function to be of the form
q^{m}(s)\coloneqq U(m\left(x_{1};s\right),\dots,m\left(x_{k};s\right))
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, where
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
and

are output by the optimization algorithm.
More generally, we only require that the output function

is in the set

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{m}\coloneqq\left\{q\in\mathbb{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}~|~\forall s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{S}:\text{if }\forall x\in\mathcal{X}:m\left(x;s_{1}\right)=m\left(x;s_{2}\right)\text{ then }q\left(s_{1}\right)=q\left(s_{2}\right)\right\}.

We now formally define the basic building blocks of this section: stochastic optimization problems, algorithms, error metrics, and minimax rates.

We consider two fundamental classes of convex functions with a minimizer at most
D

The first class contains problems for which

L
-Lipschitz and

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{L,D}_{\text{SOML-Lip}}\coloneqq\left\{\left(m,h,f,\mathcal{P}\right)\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{SOML}}~|~m\in\mathcal{I}^{L,\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,\beta}}_{\text{Lip}}\text{ and }h\in\mathcal{I}^{1,\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\beta*}}_{\text{Lip}}\text{, and }f\in\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{D,\|\cdot\|_{\alpha}}_{\text{Dist}}\right\}.

The second class contains problems for which

is

-second-moment-Lipschitz and

-Lipschitz,

Optimization algorithms.

We define a SOML algorithm as an algorithm that has unrestricted access to the functions
m,h
and
f
, but observes
\mathcal{P}
only through samples

for some

Thus, the unrestricted access to the functions

and

We write

Error metrics.

For an algorithm
\mathsf{alg}

where

denotes the

over distributions.

Given an instance class
\mathcal{I}
and an algorithm

\mathcal{A}
, we further overload our notation to express minimax optimal worst-case error

\displaystyle\mathsf{Err}^{\delta}_{N}\left(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{I}\right)\coloneqq\inf_{\mathsf{alg}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathsf{Err}^{\delta}_{N}\left(\mathsf{alg},\mathcal{I}\right)=\inf_{\mathsf{alg}\in\mathcal{A}}\sup_{\left(m,h,f,\mathcal{P}\right)\in\mathcal{I}}\mathsf{Err}^{\delta}_{N}\left(\mathsf{alg},\left(m,h,f,\mathcal{P}\right)\right).

D.2
Lower bound statement

We now formally establish the lower bound.
The proof of the bound is a reduction to the lower bounds established in
Carmon and Hinder 2024
.

Proposition 3
.

For any
L,D>0
,
\delta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
, and any number of samples
N\geq 1
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathsf{Err}^{\delta}_{N}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SOML}},\mathcal{I}^{L,D}_{\text{SOML-sm-Lip}}\right)\geq\mathsf{Err}^{\delta}_{N}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\text{SOML}},\mathcal{I}^{L,D}_{\text{SOML-Lip}}\right)\geq\Omega\left(LD\min\left\{\sqrt{\ln_{+}\left(1/\delta\right)}/\sqrt{N},1\right\}\right).

Proof.

The proof mostly follows from the proof of
Carmon and Hinder 2024
.
In the proof of
Carmon and Hinder 2024
, they let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}=\left\{0,1\right\}
and the optimized function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be

\displaystyle f\left(x;s\right)=\begin{cases}L\lvert x\rvert&$s=0$\\
L\lvert x-D\rvert&$s=1$.\end{cases}

They consider the distributions
P_{v}:=\text{Bernoulli}\left(\frac{1+(2v-1)\varepsilon}{2}\right)
for
v\in\left\{0,1\right\}
and some
\varepsilon\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
.

We set
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
and decompose
f
into a model
m
and a loss function
h
as follows:

\displaystyle m\left(x;s\right)=Lx,

and

\displaystyle h\left(x;s\right)=\begin{cases}\lvert x\rvert&$s=0$\\
\lvert x-LD\rvert&$s=1$.\end{cases}

For this decomposition it is easy to see that
f\left(x;s\right)=h\left(m\left(x;s\right);s\right)
, that
m
is
L
-Lipschitz,
h
is
1
-Lipschitz, and that
f\in\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{D,\lvert\cdot\rvert}_{\text{Dist}}
.

Let
q_{N}^{m}
be the output of the optimization algorithm after interacting with
m,h
and
f
through
N
samples.
As
m\left(x;s\right)
depends only on
x
and does not depend on
s
,
q_{N}^{m}\left(s\right)
does not depend on
s
.
As such, we can choose
x_{N}=q_{N}^{m}\left(\cdot\right)/L
.
Therefore,
f\left(x_{N};s\right)=h\left(q_{N}^{m}\left(s\right);s\right)
.
Thus, from here we can use the same proof as in the proof of
Carmon and Hinder 2024
.
Equivalently, any optimization algorithm used for optimizing the problems described here can be used to optimize the problems described in the proof of
Carmon and Hinder 2024
, and vice versa.

We note that
\lvert\cdot\rvert
is not differentiable at
0
.
Using a smoothing argument, the lower bound can also be extended to the differentiable setting.
For example, we can replace each occurrence of
\lvert\cdot\rvert
in the construction of
h\left(\cdot;\cdot\right)
with

\displaystyle\lvert y\rvert_{\gamma}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\lvert y\rvert&\text{if }y\geq\gamma\text{ or }y\leq-\gamma\\
\frac{y^{2}}{2\gamma}+\frac{\gamma}{2}&\text{if }y\in\left[-\gamma,\gamma\right]\end{cases},

for some
\gamma>0
.
The modified function is differentiable and differs from the original function by at most
\frac{\gamma}{2}
.
By choosing
\gamma
much smaller than
\frac{LD}{2}\min\left\{\sqrt{\ln_{+}\left(1/\delta\right)}/\sqrt{N},1\right\}
, we obtain that optimizing the modified function is almost equivalent to optimizing the original function.
Thus, the lower bound also holds for differentiable functions.
∎

F.1
Bounding distances between outputs of a Lipschitz function

We now bound
\|m\left(x;s\right)-m\left(y;s\right)\|_{\beta}
under the assumption
m\in\mathcal{I}^{L_{\star},\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,\beta}}_{\text{Lip}}
.

Lemma 5
.

If
m\in\mathcal{I}^{L_{\star},\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,\beta}}_{\text{Lip}}
, then for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and
s\in\mathbb{S}
we have

\displaystyle\|m\left(x;s\right)-m\left(y;s\right)\|_{\beta}\leq L_{\star}\|x-y\|_{\alpha}.

Proof.

For all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
define the line
\theta_{x,y}\left(a\right)\coloneqq(1-a)\cdot y+a\cdot x
.
We conclude that for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and
s\in\mathbb{S}
we have

\displaystyle\|m\left(x;s\right)-m\left(y;s\right)\|_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\|\int_{y}^{x}\nabla_{x}m\left(x;s\right)dx\|_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\|\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{x}m\left(\theta_{x,y}\left(a\right);s\right)\nabla_{a}\theta_{x,y}\left(a\right)da\|_{\beta}

\displaystyle\overset{(1)}{\leq}\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla_{x}m\left(\theta_{x,y}\left(a\right);s\right)\nabla_{a}\theta_{x,y}\left(a\right)\|_{\beta}da

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla_{x}m\left(\theta_{x,y}\left(a\right);s\right)\|_{\alpha,\beta}\|\nabla_{a}\theta_{x,y}\left(a\right)\|_{\alpha}da

\displaystyle=\|x-y\|_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla_{x}m\left(\theta_{x,y}\left(a\right);s\right)\|_{\alpha,\beta}da,

where (1) follows from the triangle inequality for integrals.
Therefore, since
m\in\mathcal{I}^{L_{\star},\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,\beta}}_{\text{Lip}}
, we conclude that for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and
s\in\mathbb{S}
we have

\displaystyle\|m\left(x;s\right)-m\left(y;s\right)\|_{\beta}\leq L_{\star}\|x-y\|_{\alpha}.

∎

F.4
The sample maximum exceeds a high quantile

Lemma 7
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
\delta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{e}\right)
such that
\ln(1/\delta)<n
.
Define

.

and i.i.d. random variables

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\max\left(X_{1},\dots,X_{z}\right)<Q_{p}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)\right]<\delta.

Proof.

Let
p=1-\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{n}
.
For
\max\left(X_{1},\dots,X_{z}\right)<Q_{p}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
to hold, every
X_{1},\dots,X_{z}
must be less than
Q_{p}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
.
The probability of that is less than
p^{z}
.
A well-known inequality states that for any
k>0
we have that
\ln\left(k+1\right)-\ln\left(k\right)=\int_{k}^{k+1}\frac{1}{t}\,dt\geq\frac{1}{k+1}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{n}{n-\ln(1/\delta)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\ln\left(n\right)-\ln\left(n-1\right)\right)+\cdots+\left(\ln\left(\left\lceil n-\ln(1/\delta)\right\rceil\right)-\ln\left(n-\ln(1/\delta)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n-1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{\left\lceil n-\ln(1/\delta)\right\rceil}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{n}.

Therefore, since
z\geq n
,

\displaystyle z\geq\ln\left(1/\delta\right)/\ln\left(\frac{n}{n-\ln(1/\delta)}\right).

As a consequence,

\displaystyle z\ln\left(\frac{n}{n-\ln(1/\delta)}\right)\geq\ln\left(1/\delta\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{n}\right)^{z}\leq\delta,

Finally, this means that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\max\left(X_{1},\dots,X_{z}\right)<Q_{p}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)\right]<p^{z}\leq\delta.

∎

F.5

Lemma 8
.

(9)

Appendix J
Discussion of Lipschitz properties in the model–loss decomposition setting

In this section, we discuss the relationship between the Lipschitz constants of the model
m
, the loss
h
, and the composed objective function
f
.

First, we note that the dual norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\alpha*}
can be viewed as the operator norm induced by the norms
\|\cdot\|_{\alpha}
and
\lvert\cdot\rvert
; both are defined as
\left\|v\right\|_{\alpha*}\coloneqq\sup\left\{\lvert\langle v,x\rangle\rvert~|~\|x\|_{\alpha}\leq 1\right\}
.
Similarly, the dual norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\beta*}
can be viewed as the operator norm induced by the norms
\|\cdot\|_{\beta}
and
\lvert\cdot\rvert
.
Therefore, immediately from the sub-multiplicativity of operator norms, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f\left(x;s\right)\right\|_{\alpha*}

\displaystyle=\left\|\nabla h\left(m\left(x;s\right);s\right)\nabla m\left(x;s\right)\right\|_{\alpha*}\leq\left\|\nabla h\left(m\left(x;s\right);s\right)\right\|_{\beta*}\cdot\|\nabla m\left(x;s\right)\|_{\alpha,\beta}

(27)

For completeness, we provide a short proof:

Proof.

For any
v\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\|v\|_{\alpha}\leq 1
,
if
\nabla m\left(x;s\right)v=0
then

\displaystyle\lvert\nabla h\left(m\left(x;s\right);s\right)\nabla m\left(x;s\right)v\rvert=0\leq\left\|\nabla h\left(m\left(x;s\right);s\right)\right\|_{\beta*}\cdot\|\nabla m\left(x;s\right)\|_{\alpha,\beta}.

Otherwise,

\displaystyle\lvert\nabla h\left(m\left(x;s\right);s\right)\nabla m\left(x;s\right)v\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\nabla h\left(m\left(x;s\right);s\right)\frac{\nabla m\left(x;s\right)v}{\|\nabla m\left(x;s\right)v\|_{\beta}}\rvert\cdot\|\nabla m\left(x;s\right)v\|_{\beta}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\nabla h\left(m\left(x;s\right);s\right)\right\|_{\beta*}\cdot\|\nabla m\left(x;s\right)\|_{\alpha,\beta},

where the inequality follows from the definitions of the dual norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\beta*}
and the operator norm
\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,\beta}
.
Thus, from the definition of the dual norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\alpha*}
, we obtain that
Equation

27
holds.
∎

From
Equation

27
, it immediately follows that if the model
m
is
L
-Lipschitz and the loss function
h
is
1
-Lipschitz, then the objective function
f
is
L
-Lipschitz.
Formally:

Lemma 11
.

Let

.

1.

If

and
h\in\mathcal{I}^{1,\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\beta*}}_{\text{Lip}}
, then
f\in\mathcal{I}^{L,\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\alpha*}}_{\text{Lip}}
.

2.

If
m\in\mathcal{I}^{L,\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,\beta}}_{\text{SM-Lip}}
and
h\in\mathcal{I}^{1,\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\beta*}}_{\text{Lip}}
, then
f\in\mathcal{I}^{L,\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\alpha*}}_{\text{SM-Lip}}
.

Proof.

Proof of
lem: f lip.
1

For every
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
s\in\mathbb{S}
, from
Equation

27
we have

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f\left(x;s\right)\right\|_{\alpha*}\leq\left\|\nabla h\left(m\left(x;s\right);s\right)\right\|_{\beta*}\cdot\|\nabla m\left(x;s\right)\|_{\alpha,\beta}\leq L.

Thus,
f\in\mathcal{I}^{L,\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\alpha*}}_{\text{Lip}}
.

Proof of
lem: f lip.
2

For every

, from
Equation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{P}}\left\|\nabla f\left(x;s\right)\right\|_{\alpha*}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{P}}[\left\|\nabla h\left(m\left(x;s\right);s\right)\right\|_{\beta*}^{2}\cdot\|\nabla m\left(x;s\right)\|_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{P}}[\|\nabla m\left(x;s\right)\|_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}]\leq L^{2}.

.
∎

However, it is possible to construct a model

and

.

J.1
Lipschitz constant under the absolute loss

In this subsection, we discuss the Lipschitz constant of a model with the absolute loss.
Let
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, and let the loss function be the absolute loss:
h\left(y;s\right)\coloneqq\lvert y-q\left(s\right)\rvert
, where
q:\mathbb{S}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is some function.
It is easy to see that for every
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and

such that

\nabla h\left(y;s\right)\in\{-1,1\}
.
Therefore, for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
s\in\mathbb{S}
such that

, we have
\left\|\nabla f\left(x;s\right)\right\|_{\alpha*}=\left\|\nabla h\left(m\left(x;s\right);s\right)\nabla m\left(x;s\right)\right\|_{\alpha*}=\left\|\nabla m\left(x;s\right)\right\|_{\alpha*}
.
Thus, for many models, the Lipschitz constant of

coincides with that of

.

J.2
Linear model with cross-entropy loss

In this section, we discuss the Lipschitz constant of a linear model with cross-entropy loss.
Let

,
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
, and
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
.
We consider the Euclidean norm

on
\mathcal{X}
and on
\mathcal{Y}
.
Define the softmax function by

\displaystyle p_{i}\left(y\right)\coloneqq\frac{e^{y_{i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{d_{2}}e^{y_{j}}}\qquad\boldsymbol{p}\left(y\right)\coloneqq\left(p_{1}\left(y\right),\dots,p_{d_{2}}\left(y\right)\right).

With a slight abuse of notation, let
s
be a vector of size
d_{1}+1
, where
s_{0}
denotes the class label and
s_{1:d_{1}}
the remaining features.

Proposition 5
.

Let the sample set
\mathbb{S}
be a subset of
\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+1}~|~s_{0}\in\left[d_{2}\right]\}
.
Let
m
be the linear model
m\left(x;s\right)\coloneqq\text{mat}\left(x\right)s_{1:d_{1}}
, where
\text{mat}\left(x\right)_{i,j}\coloneqq x_{j+(i-1)d_{1}}

Define the cross-entropy loss

Consequently, the objective function

Equation

27
is tight, i.e., for all

and

\displaystyle\|\nabla f\left(x;s\right)\|_{2}=\|\nabla h\left(m\left(x;s\right);s\right)\|_{2}\cdot\|\nabla m\left(x;s\right)\|_{2,2}.

, and for

Formally, for every

Informally, every row

\displaystyle\nabla m\left(x;s\right)=\begin{bmatrix}s_{1}&\dots&s_{d_{1}}&0&\dots&0&0&\dots&0&0&\dots&0&0&\dots&0\\

Appendix K
Extending the results to non-differentiable functions

In this paper, we do not impose convexity requirements on the model and loss functions, but only on the objective function.
Therefore, subgradients do not necessarily exist at all points.
Instead, for a function
q
, we use the Clarke generalized gradient
[
8
]
:

\displaystyle\partial_{c}q\left(x\right)\coloneqq\operatorname{co}\left\{v~|~\exists\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\Omega_{q}:x=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}x_{i}\text{ and }v=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\nabla q\left(x_{i}\right)\right\},

where
\operatorname{co}
is the convex hull, and
\Omega_{q}
is the set of points where
q
is differentiable.
We note that if
q
is convex and locally Lipschitz, then
\partial_{c}q(x)
coincides with the set of subgradients
\partial q(x)
for every
x
in any open subset of the domain
[
8
, Proposition 1.2]
; see also
Rockafellar 1970
.

We define
\mathcal{I}_{\text{cont}}
as the class of functions
q
such that:
(i)
q\left(\cdot;s\right)
is differentiable almost everywhere for any sample
s
;
(ii) the Clarke gradient
\partial_{c}q\left(x;s\right)
is nonempty for every point
x
and sample
s
;
and (iii) for every pair of points
x,y
, sample
s
, and every continuously differentiable curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to\operatorname{dom}\left(q\left(\cdot;s\right)\right)
satisfying
\gamma(0)=y
and
\gamma(1)=x
, there exists a selection
\nabla_{x}q\left(\gamma\left(a\right);s\right)\in\partial_{c}q\left(\gamma\left(a\right);s\right)
such that

\displaystyle q\left(x;s\right)=q\left(y;s\right)+\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{x}q\left(\gamma\left(a\right);s\right)\nabla_{a}\gamma\left(a\right)\,da.

We further assume that the model–loss decomposition satisfies the chain rule; i.e., for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
s\in\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle\partial_{c}f\left(x;s\right)\subseteq\overline{\operatorname{co}}\left\{vu~|~v\in\partial_{c}h\left(m\left(x;s\right);s\right)\text{ and }u\in\partial_{c}m\left(x;s\right)\right\},

where
\overline{\operatorname{co}}
denotes the closed convex hull.
The purpose of this requirement is to ensure that if
m
is
L
-Lipschitz and
h

1
-Lipschitz, then
f

L
-Lipschitz, as in the differentiable case (see
Lemma

11

We are now able to replace the use of
\mathcal{I}_{\text{diff}}
with
\mathcal{I}_{\text{cont}}
.
For the classes of convex stochastic optimization problems, the definitions remain almost the same:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\overline{\rho},\|\cdot\|_{\alpha}}_{\text{Dist}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{q\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{cont}}~|~\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|_{\alpha}\leq\overline{\rho}\text{ and }\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{P}}q\left(\cdot;s\right)\text{ is convex}\right\},

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\overline{\rho},\|\cdot\|_{\alpha}}_{\text{Dist}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{q\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{cont}}~|~\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\|_{\alpha}\leq\overline{\rho}\text{ and }\forall s\in\mathbb{S}:q\left(\cdot;s\right)\text{ is convex}\right\}.

To account for the entire Clarke generalized gradient, we modify the definitions of the classes of Lipschitz and second-moment-Lipschitz problems:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\overline{\ell},\|\cdot\|}_{\text{Lip}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{q\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{cont}}~|~\forall x\in\mathcal{X};s\in\mathbb{S};g\in\partial_{c}q\left(x;s\right):\|g\|\leq\overline{\ell}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\overline{\ell},\|\cdot\|}_{\text{SM-Lip}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{q\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{cont}}~|~\forall x\in\mathcal{X}:\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\sup_{g\in\partial_{c}q\left(x;s\right)}\|g\|^{2}\right]\leq\overline{\ell}^{2}\right\}.

1
