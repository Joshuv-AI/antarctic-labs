The Impact of Image-Guided Screw Placement on Unexpected Return to the Operating Room: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of 91,285 Patients

Jaskaran Singh, Marion Sylvain, Mikko Vorster, Bona Baissa, Aazad Abbas, Michael Goytan

Abstract

Study designSystematic Review and Meta-Analysis.ObjectivesNavigation-guided screw placement improves screw accuracy and may reduce hardware-related revision, yet its effect on overall unexpected return to the operating room (URTO) remains uncertain. This study compares image-guided (navigation and robotics) with freehand screw placement for hardware-related revision, non-hardware reoperation, and URTO following spinal fixation.MethodsA PRISMA-guided systematic review identified comparative studies evaluating image-guided versus freehand screw fixation. Two reviewers performed screening, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment. Odds ratios (ORs) were pooled using a random-effects Mantel-Haenszel meta-analysis. Subgroup analyses compared navigated, robotic, and freehand techniques.ResultsTwenty-one studies were included, comprising 91,285 patients (8,743 image-guided; 82,442 freehand). Compared with freehand, image guidance was associated with lower odds of hardware-related revision (OR 0.55, 95% CI 0.40–0.75) and lower odds of URTO (OR 0.69, 95% CI 0.50–0.94), with no difference in non-hardware reoperation (OR 1.03, 95% CI 0.67–1.57). Navigation was associated with lower odds of hardware-related revision (OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.35–0.77) and URTO (OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.51–0.77) versus freehand, whereas robotics showed no significant differences across outcomes. Indirect comparison between navigation and robotics was not statistically significant. Risk of bias was overall high because of retrospective study design, confounding, and inconsistent outcome reporting.ConclusionsNavigation was associated with lower odds of hardware-related revision and lower odds of URTO, without a difference in non-hardware reoperation. Robotics showed no statistically significant advantage over freehand fixation, but its underrepresentation and differing case mix limit comparative conclusions.

1. Introduction

Lateral mass and pedicle screws are widely used in spine surgery to provide stability, fixation, and alignment across diverse pathologies, including instability, deformity, trauma, and degenerative disease. Acting as anchors for fusion constructs, these implants help maintain alignment during osseous healing and have been shown to be safe and effective, making internal screw fixation a cornerstone of modern spinal surgery.

Pedicle screw fixation was originally developed using freehand techniques based on anatomic landmarks, with early descriptions emerging from European orthopaedic surgeons in the mid-20th century, including the foundational work of Boucher,

 Roy-Camille,

 and Magerl.

 Later, frameless stereotactic systems were introduced to augment screw placement accuracy, as described by Kalfas et al in 1995.

 With the advancement in intraoperative imaging, CT-based navigation entered routine practice in the early 2000s, giving rise to navigated screw fixation.

 In parallel, the adoption of minimally invasive and percutaneous approaches expanded, motivated by the potential to reduce surgical morbidity.

An unexpected return to the operating room (URTO), whether for revision of mispositioned hardware or for reoperation due to other complications, remains a costly and burdensome outcome for patients and health systems.

 Prior reviews have reported that navigation reduces hardware-related revision, whereas others find no difference between navigated and freehand techniques, leaving uncertainty about clinical impact., Despite the potential benefits of navigation, there are some studies that have raised concern for non-hardware-related complications and caution that these risks must be interpreted alongside any possible benefits.,

Given the growing clinical adoption of navigation and robotics and the importance of URTO as a patient-centered outcome, a focused evaluation is warranted. The objective of this study was to compare image-guided screw placement—including navigation and robotics—with freehand fixation with respect to hardware-related revision, non-hardware reoperation, and URTO, with subgroup analyses evaluating navigation and robotics separately.

2. Materials and methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the 2020 PRISMA guidelines.

 We included human studies comparing image-guided (navigation or robotic) versus conventional freehand/fluoroscopy-guided lateral mass or pedicle screw fixation across the cervical, thoracic, lumbar, or sacral spine. Primary outcomes were unexpected return to the operating room (URTO), hardware-related revision, and non-hardware reoperation.

2.1. Search Strategy and Study Selection

MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), and PubMed were searched from inception to March 31st, 2025 using predefined terms related to spine surgery, navigation, robotics, and freehand screw fixation. Citations were screened independently by two reviewers using Covidence, with disagreements resolved by consensus or a third reviewer.

2.2. Eligibility Criteria

Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials, prospective and retrospective cohort studies, and case-control studies (Level I–III evidence) reporting navigated or robotic-assisted versus freehand screw fixation and at least one outcome of interest. Exclusions included non-human studies, case reports/series, reviews, conference abstracts without full text, non-English studies, and follow-up <1 month.

2.3. Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Data extraction was performed independently in duplicate, including study characteristics, patient demographics, surgical details (region, indication, guidance method, system used, primary vs. revision, open vs. percutaneous), and outcomes (hardware-related revision, non-hardware reoperation, and URTO). Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2 for randomized trials and ROBINS-I for non-randomized studies.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Binary outcomes were pooled as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals using random-effects models. Heterogeneity was assessed with I2. Preplanned subgroup analyses compared navigation versus freehand fixation and robotics versus freehand fixation. An indirect Bucher comparison between navigation and robotics was performed using freehand fixation as the common comparator. Because navigation and robotics were evaluated in substantially different patient populations and surgical indications, this indirect comparison was considered exploratory and susceptible to between-study confounding. Analyses were performed in RStudio using the meta package, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

3.8. Risk of Bias

Overall risk of bias was high, driven by retrospective study designs, confounding, and incomplete outcome reporting, which limited causal inference. The two randomized trials were judged to have some concerns or a high risk of bias, and most nonrandomized studies were judged to be at serious risk of bias using ROBINS-I (Supplemental Table S2). 4. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated navigation, robotics, and freehand screw fixation across more than 91,000 patients, with a focus on URTO as a patient-centered outcome rather than screw-placement accuracy alone. The crude aggregate event rates in and did not consistently favour image guidance, whereas the random-effects meta-analysis showed lower odds of hardware-related revision and URTO. This apparent discrepancy reflects the different analytic approaches: crude rates pool all patients without study-level weighting, whereas the meta-analysis combines study-specific odds ratios using random-effects weighting and incorporates between-study heterogeneity. The pooled estimates nevertheless remain susceptible to residual confounding because most included studies were retrospective and the cohorts differed substantially in baseline characteristics and case complexity. Robotics showed no statistically significant differences compared with freehand fixation, but its underrepresentation and distinct case mix preclude conclusions of equivalence.

URTO represents a valuable patient-centered endpoint that captures the net clinical effect of navigation, integrating both hardware-related failures and broader postoperative complications. Prior work has demonstrated that navigation improves screw placement and reduces revision for malpositioned hardware.,, In the present meta-analysis, navigation was associated with lower odds of hardware-related revision and URTO, with no significant difference in non-hardware reoperation. This pattern is consistent with the mechanism of navigation: it improves technical accuracy but does not directly influence complications such as infection, hematoma, pseudarthrosis, or medical events.

Demographic and procedural differences across groups help explain these results. Navigation was more frequently used in older patients, deformity and trauma indications, thoracic and cervical regions, and multilevel constructs—situations with intrinsically higher risk. Freehand fixation was most common for lumbar degenerative disease and malignancy, often involving shorter constructs. Robotics appeared to be deployed in a narrower clinical context, largely degenerative lumbar disease and single-level instrumentation. These case-mix differences likely contributed to the higher unadjusted complication rates observed in image-guided cohorts. Although the study-level meta-analysis favoured navigation for hardware-related revision and URTO, it could not fully adjust for patient-level differences in age, surgical indication, construct length, or procedural complexity. Residual confounding and selection bias therefore remain, and the observed associations should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a causal protective effect.

The potential benefits of navigation must be interpreted alongside its trade-offs. Navigation requires increased operating room traffic, more equipment, and workflow-related setup which raises the concern for sterile field contamination. Additionally, Katz et al reported higher unadjusted reoperation rates in adult spinal deformity navigated cases (9.9% vs 5.2%, p=0.011), likely reflecting longer operative time in navigated cohorts (405 min vs 320 min, p<0.001), increased transfusion requirements in navigated cohorts (52.2% vs 41.8%, p=0.01), and greater procedural complexity rather than a direct causal effect of navigation itself.

 Similarly, concerns have been raised regarding infection risk. Berman et al observed higher infection-related return to the operating room in patients undergoing intraoperative 3D navigation (4.9% vs 0.6%, p=0.025), although this association did not reach statistical significance after adjustment (adjusted OR 6.49, p=0.073).

 In contrast, Shuman et al found that navigation was associated with decreased odds of return to the operating room for wound-related procedures despite longer operative times, highlighting the inconsistency in current literature.

 Taken together, these findings provide no clear signal of increased non-hardware reoperation with navigation. However, heterogeneity and residual confounding preclude definitive conclusions regarding infection or other non-hardware complications.

Economic considerations may also influence the value of navigation.

 Although navigation entails substantial acquisition and operating costs, avoidance of hardware-related revision could offset some of these expenses; however, formal cost-effectiveness analysis was beyond the scope of this review.

This study has several important limitations. The evidence base was predominantly retrospective, with inherent risks of selection bias, residual confounding, and inconsistent outcome reporting, limiting causal inference. Heterogeneity in study design, outcome definitions, and reporting formats—including per-patient versus per-screw analyses—further reduced comparability across studies. Mean follow-up was relatively short in both groups—6.36 months for image-guided fixation and 7.01 months for freehand fixation—which may have undercaptured reoperations occurring beyond the early postoperative period. Robotics was substantially underrepresented, limiting statistical power and restricting conclusions regarding its comparative effectiveness. Moreover, navigation and robotics were evaluated in markedly different patient populations and surgical indications, and the absence of direct head-to-head comparisons required exploratory indirect analyses that remain susceptible to confounding. Future prospective, multicenter studies with standardized outcome definitions, adequate follow-up, and direct platform comparisons are needed to clarify the role of image guidance in reducing URTO.

5. Conclusion

Navigation was associated with lower odds of hardware-related revision and a modest reduction in URTO, without a difference in non-hardware reoperation. These associations should be interpreted cautiously because the evidence was predominantly retrospective and remained subject to residual confounding. Robotics showed no statistically significant advantage over freehand fixation, but its underrepresentation and differing case mix preclude definitive conclusions regarding comparative effectiveness. Prospective, multicenter studies with standardized outcome definitions, adequate follow-up, and direct platform comparisons are needed.
