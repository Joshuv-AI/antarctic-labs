1.0 Introduction
 The planetary boundary layer (PBL) is fundamental to numerous atmospheric processes, including
 aerosol mixing and transport, cloud evolution, and precipitation formation. A critical parameter in these
 studies is the PBL height (PBLHT). This vertical depth is essential for characterizing PBL structures in
 numerical simulations and serves as a primary metric for estimating flux exchanges between the Earth’s
 surface and the atmosphere.

 Radiosonde (SONDE) observations provide high-vertical-resolution measurements of temperature and
 moisture profiles and are widely used to estimate PBLHT (Liu and Liang 2010, Seidel et al. 2010). The
 U.S. Department of Energy Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) User Facility’s PBLHT
 value-added product (VAP) for radiosonde measurements, known as PBLHTSONDE, applies three
 commonly used methods—the Heffter (1980) method, the Liu and Liang (2010) method, and the bulk
 Richardson number approach (Seibert et al. 2000)—to derive PBLHT. The PBLHTSONDE VAP
 operates routinely at ARM observatories and mobile facilities, with data available from the ARM Data
 Center shortly after sounding observations are collected (Sivaraman et al. 2013). However, radiosonde
 observations are limited by their low temporal resolution. Most stations launch soundings only twice
 daily, which constrains the ability to investigate and characterize the temporal evolution of the PBL using
 radiosonde data alone.

 The use of continuous remote-sensing observations provides high temporal resolution of PBLHT
 estimates. These observations include aerosol lidars (Dang et al. 2019, Su et al. 2020), Doppler lidar (DL;
 Tucker et al. 2009, Krishnamurthy et al. 2021), and water vapor and/or temperature lidars and radiometers
 (Turner et al. 2014). These observations provide valuable data on the PBL’s thermodynamic properties
 (e.g., water vapor and/or temperature lidars and radiometers), dynamic properties (e.g., DL), and
 distribution of tracer substances (e.g., aerosol lidars), all of which can be used to estimate PBLHT.

 ARM developed PBLHT estimates from the micropulse lidar (MPL; PBLHTMPL), Doppler lidar
 (PBLHTDL), and combined Raman lidar (RL)/atmospheric emitted radiance interferometer (AERI)
 thermodynamic profiles (PBLHTTHERMO). Each estimate captures different physical characteristics of
 the boundary layer—aerosol tracers, vertical velocity turbulence, and thermodynamic structure—and
 exhibits distinct strengths and limitations depending on the PBL regime and time of day. In addition, the
 ARM ceilometer (CEIL) provides three potential PBLHT candidates derived from the vendor's built-in
 algorithm. Building on these individual retrievals, ARM developed the PBLHTBEML VAP, which
 combines the four remote-sensing-based estimates with ancillary meteorological variables using the
 machine learning approach of Zhang et al. (2025) to produce a best-estimate PBLHT at 10-minute
 resolution.

2.0 Algorithm and Methodology

 2.1 PBLHTCEIL
 We use CEIL PBLHT estimates directly provided by the vendor’s built-in software, BL-View, which
 provides real-time monitoring of boundary-layer structures and identifies up to three potential

 1

boundary-layer heights (Münkel and Räsänen 2010). For its CEILs, ARM uses the Vaisala CL31 model,
which has a maximum vertical range of 7.7 km (Münkel and Räsänen 2004). The CL31 provides total
attenuated backscatter coefficient profiles at a wavelength of 910 nm, with a vertical resolution of
10 meters and a temporal resolution of 2 seconds. Figure 1a shows the time-height plot of CEIL
backscatter profiles observed on July 12, 2019, at the ARM Southern Great Plains (SGP) Central Facility
(C1).

The CL31 estimates PBLHT using the gradient method, which identifies local gradient minima in the
range- and overlap-corrected backscatter coefficient profiles. First, the data are averaged to a 16-second
temporal resolution to improve aerosol signal reliability. Then, local gradient minima are detected using a
30-minute temporal and 360-meter vertical sliding window average. Furthermore, the enhanced gradient
method applies a cloud and precipitation filter during the averaging process to suppress false layer
identification. This allows for PBLHT estimates under various weather conditions
(Münkel and Roininen 2010). For the three potential boundary-layer heights, the BL-View algorithm
assigns a quality index (ranging from 1 to 3) to each boundary-layer height candidate. A higher quality
index is assigned to stronger gradients, greater distances between minima, and when no clouds are
detected near the boundary layer. We select the boundary-layer height candidate with the highest quality
index as the CEIL-estimated PBLHT (PBLHTCEIL).

2.2 PBLHTMPL
PBLHTMPL is estimated using range and overlap-corrected MPL backscatter profiles through the
wavelet covariance method, as described by Sawyer and Li (2013). The ARM MPL system provides
aerosol backscatter intensity and depolarization ratio profile measurements at a wavelength of 532 nm,
with a vertical resolution of 15 meters and a temporal resolution of 10 seconds. The raw data are
post-processed to subtract background noise and apply deadtime, afterpulse, and overlap corrections
(Campbell et al. 2002). Figure 3a presents an example of MPL backscatter profiles on July 12, 2019, at
SGP C1.

The Sawyer and Li approach uses the Haar wavelet covariance transform on the lidar backscatter intensity
profile to identify the strongest negative gradient with height, which is then taken as the PBL top
(Brooks 2003). This wavelet covariance transform is an advanced edge detection technique well suited for
vertically resolved active remote-sensing data. The method relies only on lidar backscatter intensity
profiles and is independent of absolute lidar calibration, making it adaptable for different MPL systems
across various locations. A key parameter in this approach is the dilation factor, a, of the Haar wavelet,
which usually ranges between 100 m and 1 km. A small value of a may result in noisy PBLHT estimates
due to spurious gradients, while large values increase the minimal detectable PBLHT. The minimal
detectable PBLHT is half of a. Following Sawyer and Li (2013), we set the dilation factor, 𝑎𝑎, to 0.4 km,
corresponding to a minimal detectable PBLHT of 0.2 km above the ground level (AGL). Figure 3b shows
that before 11:00 LT, PBLHTMPL captures the top of the residual layer as the PBLHT, while after
11:00 LT, it accurately tracks the PBLHT of the convective PBL. Compared to CEIL backscatter profiles
shown in Figure 1a, MPL backscatter profiles have large signal-to-noise ratios and, therefore, provide
better PBLHT estimates. However, PBLHTMPL often determines the residual aerosol layer top as
PBLHT during nighttime, similar to PBLHTCEIL.

2.3 PBLHTDL
ARM DLs provide range-resolved measurements of attenuated backscatter intensity at approximately
1.5 µm and radial velocity profiles. Most ARM DLs have full upper-hemispheric scanning capabilities
and typically operate in a vertical staring mode, offering vertical velocity measurements with a resolution
of 30 meters and a temporal resolution of 1 second or less. The Doppler Lidar Vertical Velocity Statistics

 5

(DLPROF-WSTATS) VAP (https://arm.gov/capabilities/science-data-products/vaps/dlprof-wstats) uses
vertical velocity data to calculate 30-minute statistics of vertical velocity variance (σ2w), skewness, and
kurtosis. The vertical velocity profiles are oversampled by a factor of three to produce these statistical
quantities at a 10-minute temporal resolution. Figure 5a shows an example of the time-height plot of σ2w
on July 12, 2019, at SGP C1.

According to Tucker et al. (2009), the depth of the convective boundary layer can be estimated by
identifying the height where σ2w falls below a specified threshold, such as 0.04 m²s⁻², which is the
approach used to derive PBLHT (PBLHTDL) from DLPROF-WSTATS. Figure 5b shows the derived
PBLHTDL and its comparisons with PBLHTSONDE.

Figure 5. An example of PBLHTDL estimates on July 12, 2019, at SGP C1. a) Time-height plot of DL
 vertical velocity variance profiles; b) DL PBLHT estimates (PBLHTDL) and
 PBLHTSONDE.

Figure 6 shows the statistical comparisons of PBLHTDL with PBLHTSONDE at SGP C1, CRG M1, and
BNF M1 using multiple years of data. Data at SGP C1 span between April 2011 and December 2019
(~9 years); at CRG M1 between November 2024 and November 2025 (~1 year); and at BNF M1 between

slightly higher correlation (R) and lower root-mean-square error (RMSE) for the comparisons with
PBLHTSONDE at all three sites.

2.4 PBLHTTHERMO
At night, the presence of residual layers can create strong water vapor and aerosol gradients, making it
difficult to derive PBLHT from water vapor or aerosol lidar backscatter profiles. In such conditions,
PBLHT estimates based on thermodynamic profiles, such as potential temperature (θ), are more reliable

 7

(Seidel et al. 2010). Both the ARM RL and AERI provide height- and time-resolved temperature profile
retrievals that can be used for this purpose. ARM RL systems measure elastically backscattered light from
aerosols at a wavelength of 355 nm, as well as inelastically scattered light from atmospheric molecules at
specifically tuned channels. The ARM Raman Lidar Temperature (RLPROFTEMP) VAP
(https://www.arm.gov/capabilities/science-data-products/vaps/rlproftemp) uses measurements from the
RL’s rotational Raman channels to retrieve temperature profiles (Newsom et al. 2013). These temperature
profiles have a vertical resolution of 60 meters and a temporal resolution of 10 minutes
(Newsom et al. 2018). The uncertainty in the RL’s temperature retrievals is calculated using standard
error analysis, and by default, temperature retrievals with relative uncertainties greater than 0.05 are
excluded. It is important to note that lidar signals and temperature retrievals can be affected by
incomplete overlap between the outgoing laser beam and the receiver’s field of view. As a result, overlap
corrections are typically applied at low altitudes, where retrievals have higher uncertainties.

The ARM AERI instrument measures absolute infrared spectral radiance from 3000 to 520 wavenumbers
(cm⁻¹) with a spectral resolution of 1 cm⁻¹, capturing data every 20 seconds, which can also be used to
retrieve boundary-layer temperature profiles. The ARM TROPospheric Optimal Estimation (TROPoe)
VAP (https://www.arm.gov/capabilities/science-data-products/vaps/tropoe) provides retrievals of
lower-tropospheric temperature and water vapor mixing ratio profiles from AERI infrared spectral
radiance measurements using a physical-iterative retrieval approach, as described by Turner and Löhnert
(2014, 2021). TROPoe retrievals are produced at a 5-minute temporal resolution, with vertical resolutions
starting at approximately 25 meters near the surface, decreasing to 800 meters by 3 km in altitude. The
output file also includes the one-sigma uncertainty for each retrieved variable, derived from the error
covariance matrix within the optimal estimation framework. The mean bias errors relative to radiosonde
profiles are less than 0.2 K for temperatures at heights below 2 km under clear-sky conditions
(Turner and Löhnert 2014).

To address the impact of overlap correction on RL temperature retrievals and leverage the higher vertical
resolution of TROPoe near the surface, we combine the RLPROFTEMP and TROPoe temperature
profiles. Specifically, TROPoe temperatures are used for altitudes below 700 meters. At altitudes between
700 and 1100 meters, RLPROFTEMP temperatures are linearly scaled using the ratio of the mean
TROPoe temperature to the mean RLPROFTEMP temperature within this layer. RLPROFTEMP
temperatures are applied for altitudes above 1100 meters. The θ profiles are then calculated using the
combined temperature profiles, along with pressure profiles from the ARM Interpolated Sonde
(INTERPSONDE) VAP (https://www.arm.gov/capabilities/science-data-products/vaps/interpsonde).
Figure 7a displays the time-height plots of the θ profiles. PBLHTTHERMO is derived from these θ
profiles using the Heffter method, the same approach employed in the PBLHTSONDE VAP. Figure 7b
shows PBLHT estimates from θ profiles (PBLHTTHERMO) and its comparisons with PBLHTSONDE.

RLPROF data are not available, PBLHTTHERMO will use temp and relative humidity (RH) from
TROPoe at all altitudes. A flag will be added to this.

2.5 PBLHTBEML
Each remote-sensing approach from PBLHTCEIL, PBLHTMPL, PBLHTDL, and PBLHTTHERMO
offers unique strengths and faces different limitations. Figure 9a shows PBLHT estimate from the four
remote-sensing approaches on July 12, 2019. Zhang et al. (2025) use machine learning (ML) methods to

 10

produce a best-estimate PBLHT (PBLHTBEML) by integrating the four PBLHT estimates. Three ML
models—Random Forest (RF) Classifier, RF Regressor, and Light Gradient-Boosting Machine
(LightGBM)—were trained on a data set from 2017 to 2023 that included radiosonde data, the four
PBLHT estimates, and atmospheric meteorological conditions at SGP C1. Evaluations indicated that
PBLHTBEML from all three models improved alignment with PBLHT derived from radiosonde data
(PBLHTSONDE), with LightGBM demonstrating the highest accuracy under both stable and unstable
boundary-layer conditions. The trained models provide PBLHTBEML at a temporal resolution of
10 minutes.

Figure 9b shows PBLHT estimates from three ML models: PBLHTBEML_RF_CL, PBLHTBEML_RF,
and PBLHTBEML_LIGHTGBM, and their comparisons with PBLHTSONDE.

3.0 Input Data

 3.1 Input Data for PBLHTMPL (pblhtmpl1sawyerli.c1)
 The input datastreams for PBLHTMPL are the Cloud Mask from Micropulse Lidar (MPLCMASK) VAP
 (30smplcmask1zwang.c1), Active Remote-Sensing of Clouds (ARSCL) product using Ka-band ARM
 Zenith Radars (KAZRARSCL) VAP (arsclkazr1kollias.c0), and the Planetary Boundary Layer Height
 (PBLHTSONDE) VAP (pblhtsonde1mcfarl.c1). The 30smplcmask1zwang.c1 datastream provides
 corrected MPL backscatter profiles that are used to estimate PBLHT. The arsclkazr1kollias.c0 datastream
 provides cloud boundaries that are useful for better understanding PBLHT under cloudy conditions. The
 pblhtsonde1mcfarl.c1 datastream provides PBLHT estimates from sonde measurements. Appendix A,
 Table 1, lists the datastreams and their corresponding input and quality check fields required to run the
 PBLHTMPL VAP.

 12

3.2 Input Data for PBLHTDL (pblhtdl.c1)
The input datastreams for PBLHTDL are the Doppler Lidar Vertical Velocity Statistics
(DLPROF-WSTATS) VAP (dlprofwstats4news.c1) and arsclkazr1kollias.c0. The dlprofwstats4news.c1
datastream provides vertical velocity variance profiles used for PBLHT estimates. The
arsclkazr1kollias.c0 datastream provides cloud boundaries that are useful for better understanding PBLHT
under cloudy conditions. Appendix A, Table 2, lists the datastreams and their corresponding input and
quality check fields required to run the PBLHTDL VAP.

3.3 Input Data for PBLHTTHERMO (pblhtthermo.c1)
The input datastreams for PBLHTTHERMO are Raman Lidar Temperature (RLPROFTEMP) VAP
(rlproftemp2news10m.c0) and Tropospheric Optimal Estimation Retrieval (TROPoe) VAP (tropoe.c1).
The rlproftemp2news10m.c0 datastream provides temperature and pressure profiles at a temporal
resolution of 10 minutes. The tropoe.c1 datastream provides boundary-layer temperature profiles at a
temporal resolution of 5 minutes. Appendix A, Table 3, lists the datastreams and their corresponding
input and quality check fields required to run the PBLHTTHERMO VAP.

3.4 Input Data for PBLHTBEML (pblhtbeml.c1)
The input datastreams for PBLHTBEML include PBLHT estimates from various lidar-based retrievals,
including boundary-layer height data with CEIL (CEILPBLHT) (ceilpblht.b1 or ceilpblht.a0),
pblhtmpl1sawyerli.c1, pblhtdl.c1, and pblhtthermo.c1, as well as atmospheric meteorological condition
data, including ceilometer (ceil.b1), carbon dioxide flux measurement systems (co2flx25m.b1), surface
meteorological instrumentation (met.b1), pblhtsonde1mcfarl.c1, Data Quality Assessment for ARM
Radiation Data (QCRAD) VAP (qcrad1long.c2 or qcrad1long.c1), and tropoe.c1.

The ceilpblht.b1 or ceilpblht.a0 datastream provides PBLHT estimates from the CL31 BL-View
algorithm. The pblhtmpl1sawyerli.c1 datastream provides PBLHT estimates from MPL backscatter
profiles. The pblhtdl.c1 datastream provides PBLHT estimates from DL vertical velocity variance
profiles. The pblhtthermo.c1 datastream provides PBLHT estimates from combined RL and TROPoe
temperature profiles.

From the atmospheric meteorological condition datastreams, ceil.b1 provides cloud base heights from
CEIL measurements. The co2flx25m.b1 datastream provides sensible heat flux, latent heat flux, total
kinetic energy (TKE), friction velocity, and Monin-Obukhov length (MO-Len) from the eddy correlation
flux measurement (ECOR) and carbon dioxide flux measurement (CO2FLX) systems. The met.b1
datastream provides surface relative humidity, temperature, and horizontal wind speed and direction
measurements. The qcrad1long.c2 or qcrad1long.c1 datastream provides upward longwave and shortwave
hemispheric irradiance measurements. The tropoe.c1 datastream provides TROPoe potential temperature
profiles.

to run the PBLHTBEML VAP.

4.0 Output Data

 4.1 Output Data from PBLHTMPL
 The PBLHTMPL VAP outputs PBLHT estimates derived from MPL backscatter profiles using the
 Sawyer and Li (2013) method, as well as pass-through fields of the corrected MPL backscatter and cloud
 base heights from the MPLCMASK VAP. A complete list of all the output fields and a brief description
 of each is given in Appendix B, Table 5. One file is created for each day named with the following
 convention: XXXpblhtmpl1sawyerliFF.c1.YYYYMMDD.hhmmss where: XXX = the location of the
 instrument (nsa [North Slope of Alaska], sgp, ena, etc.); pblhtmpl1sawyerli = identifies that this is
 PBLHT from MPL data following the Sawyer and Li (2013) method; FF = facility (e.g., C1);
 YYYYMMDD = year, month, and day; hhmmss = hour, minute, second.

 4.2 Output Data from PBLHTDL
 The PBLHTDL VAP outputs PBLHT estimates derived from DL vertical velocity variance profiles using
 the Tucker et al. (2009) method, as well as pass-through fields of the corrected vertical velocity variance,
 signal-to-noise ratio, and DL cloud base height from the DLPROF-WSTATS VAP. A complete list of all
 the output fields and a brief description of each is given in Appendix B, Table 6. One file is created for
 each day named with the same convention as PBLHTMPL.

 4.3 Output Data from PBLHTTHERMO
 The PBLHTTHERMO VAP outputs PBLHT estimates derived from combined TROPoe and RL potential
 temperature profiles using the Heffter method, as well as pass-through fields of potential temperature
 from the TROPoe and RL VAPs, the calculated potential temperature inversion layer top and bottom
 heights, and the maximum lapse rate of the inversion layer. A complete list of all output fields and a brief
 description of each is given in Appendix B, Table 7. One file is created for each day, named following the
 same convention as PBLHTMPL.

 4.4 Output Data from PBLHTBEML
 The PBLHTBEML VAP outputs PBLHT predictions from three ML models using the Zhang et al. (2025)
 method. The input PBLHT estimates from various lidar-based retrievals and atmospheric meteorological
 condition variables are also outputted at the same temporal resolution. A complete list of all the output
 fields and a brief description of each is given in Appendix B, Table 8. One file is created for each day
 named with the same convention as PBLHTMPL.

5.0 Summary
 This report describes a set of PBLHT data products that extend the original PBLHTSONDE radiosonde
 retrievals (Sivaraman et al. 2013) to continuous, high-temporal-resolution PBLHT estimates derived from
 ground-based remote-sensing measurements. Four individual PBLHT estimates are produced from CEIL
 (PBLHTCEIL), MPL (PBLHTMPL), DL (PBLHTDL), and combined RL/AERI thermodynamic profiles

 14

 (PBLHTTHERMO). Each of these estimates captures different physical characteristics of the boundary
 layer—aerosol tracers, vertical velocity turbulence, and thermodynamic structure—and each shows
 distinct strengths and limitations depending on the PBL regime and time of day, as illustrated by
 comparisons against PBLHTSONDE at the SGP, CRG, and BNF sites.

 Because no single remote-sensing method consistently outperforms the others across all boundary-layer
 conditions, the PBLHTBEML VAP combines the four individual estimates, together with ancillary
 meteorological variables, using the ML approach of Zhang et al. (2025) to produce a best-estimate
 PBLHT at 10-minute resolution. This best estimate improves agreement with PBLHTSONDE relative to
 any single remote-sensing method under both stable and unstable conditions, and it provides continuous
 PBLHT coverage between radiosonde launch times. Together, the PBLHT products described in this
 report are intended to support studies of boundary-layer evolution, aerosol and cloud processes, and
 evaluation of numerical models at ARM observatories.
