Influence of Harris disorder on quantum-critical superconductivity
Serhii Kryhin, Peter Lunts, Aavishkar A. Patel, Subir Sachdev, Pavel A. Nosov

Abstract
In the Hertz theory, a quantum critical metal is described by the coupling of a Fermi surface to fluctuations of a Landau-damped bosonic field ϕ \phi , which may represent either an order parameter or, more generally, a Higgs field for a transition without symmetry breaking. Scattering from ϕ \phi produces non-Fermi-liquid behavior in the normal state, while the same fluctuations mediate enhanced Cooper pairing. By the Harris criterion, symmetry-preserving disorder couples most strongly to the coefficient of the ϕ 2 \phi^{2} term, locally tuning the system toward or away from criticality. This random mass (“Harris disorder”) leads to a finite density of localized low-energy ϕ \phi modes even when the fermionic states remain extended and continue to provide Landau damping. We study the onset of pairing mediated by these localized overdamped bosonic modes. Starting from a real-space linearized Usadel equation for the two-particle propagator (the Cooperon), we show that the localized bosonic wave functions generate both a spatially random pairing vertex and an effective random potential for Cooper pairs. Numerical solution, a self-consistent Born approximation analysis, and single-puddle/Lifshitz-tail considerations reveal two regimes of the pairing instability. At high temperatures, pairing nucleates compact superconducting puddles on the most localized bosonic modes. At lower temperatures, an extended pairing eigenstate appears, but its transition scale and spatial structure remain strongly affected by mesoscopic correlation effects and enhanced probability of returns to favorable regions of the localized bosonic glue. The resulting distribution of local pairing scales has a power-law tail, in contrast to the stretched-exponential tails of disordered BCS (i.e. non-critical) superconductors. This mechanism provides a route to broad gap inhomogeneity and superconducting puddles in quantum-critical metals, and offers a natural interpretation of scanning tunneling microscopy measurements of the cuprates.

II
Disordered Quantum-Critical Normal State

We begin by reviewing the disordered quantum-critical normal state and the emergence of localized
\phi
modes.
The theory considered here is a two-dimensional Hertz-Millis-type theory
Hertz 1976
;
Millis 1993
;
Löhneysen et al. 2007
for an
N
-component non-conserved order-parameter field
\phi_{i}
coupled via a Yukawa interaction to fermions
\psi
.
The bosons
\phi_{i}
are subjected to a random mass potential, while fermions
\psi
are subjected to a random chemical potential.
The total Lagrangian density is given by
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\psi}+\mathcal{L}_{\phi}+\mathcal{L}_{\psi\phi}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma}\bar{\psi}_{\sigma}(\partial_{\tau}+E(-i\nabla)+v(\mathbf{r}))\psi_{\sigma}\;,

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[(\partial_{\tau}\phi_{i})^{2}+c^{2}(\nabla\phi_{i})^{2}+(\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}))\phi_{i}^{2}\right]+\frac{u}{4N}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}^{2}\right)^{2}\;,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\psi\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{g}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{i}\bar{\psi}\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}\psi\;.

Here
\sigma{=}{\uparrow}{/}{\downarrow}
denotes the spin component,
E(\mathbf{p})=p^{2}/2m-\mu
is the fermion dispersion counted from the Fermi energy. The constant
u
is the coupling for the order-parameter self-interaction,
\lambda
is the bare mass, and
g
is the translation-invariant Yukawa coupling, with
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}
describing the appropriate spatial- and spin-dependent form factor. There are two sources of disorder in this problem: the random chemical potential
v(\mathbf{r})
and the random bosonic mass
\lambda^{\prime}(\mathbf{r})
, which we assume to be uncorrelated Gaussian white-noise fields

\overline{v(\mathbf{r})}=\overline{\lambda^{\prime}(\mathbf{r})}=\overline{v(\mathbf{r})\lambda^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}=0,\quad\overline{v(\mathbf{r})v(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}=\bar{v}^{2}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),\quad\overline{\lambda^{\prime}(\mathbf{r})\lambda^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}=\bar{\lambda}^{2}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\;.

(2)

The random chemical potential
v(\mathbf{r})
produces diffusive dynamics for electrons, whereas the random mass
\lambda^{\prime}(\mathbf{r})
is Harris disorder: it locally shifts the distance to the quantum critical point.

The pairing instability of the theory in Eq. (
1
) is mediated by the near-critical bosons
\phi_{i}
, which generate an effective attractive interaction for many theories of interest (i.e. many
\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{i}
), such as spin-density-wave and nematic order parameters. For simplicity, we will focus on the trivial s-wave form-factor
\hat{\mathcal{D}}=\hat{1}
.
Assuming that the random chemical potential
v(\mathbf{r})
is weak (i.e.
k_{F}l\gg 1
, where
l
is the fermion mean free path), the fermionic degrees of freedom remain extended and metallic. We therefore average over
v(\mathbf{r})
perturbatively, so that it enters the bosonic theory only through self-averaged metallic parameters, while keeping the random mass disorder
\lambda^{\prime}(\mathbf{r})
explicit. Integrating out the disorder-averaged fermions at the one-loop level then gives an effective Landau-damped action for the bosons
\phi_{i}
:

\displaystyle\int d\tau d\mathbf{r}\,\mathcal{L}_{\phi,{\rm eff}}=\int d\tau d\mathbf{r}\,\mathcal{L}_{\phi}+\frac{T}{2}\sum_{\omega_{m}}\int d\mathbf{r}\,c_{d}|\omega_{m}|\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\phi_{i}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})|^{2},

(3)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\phi}
is given in Eq. (
1
),
\omega_{m}
is a bosonic Matsubara frequency, and
c_{d}
is a Landau-damping coefficient determined as
c_{d}\propto\nu g^{2}
, where
\nu
is the electron density of states at the Fermi level
Patel et al. 2023
. We note that Eq. (
3
) assumes an overdamped but non-conserved order parameter, for which disorder-induced diffuson vertex corrections are absent in the long-wavelength theory. This should be distinguished from cases where the order parameter corresponds to a conserved density and directly couples to the soft diffusive modes of the metal: there the resulting Hertz-Millis dynamical term is nonlocal and in momentum space results in a non-analytic form
\propto|\omega_{m}|/q^{2}

Hertz 1976
;
Belitz et al. 2001
;
Brando et al. 2016
;
Nosov et al. 2023
.

We solve the problem defined by Eq. (
3
) using the self-consistent (Hartree) approach of Refs.
Del Maestro et al. 2008
;
Patel et al. 2024
, which can be formally justified in the large-
N
limit (see Appendix
C
). Specifically, we solve for the normal-state boson propagator
D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
while treating the random-mass problem for the bosons nonperturbatively. Assuming the bosonic flavor symmetry remains unbroken, the bosonic propagator can be expressed as

D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\phi_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{\omega_{m}^{2}+c_{d}|\omega_{m}|+e_{\alpha}},

(4)

where
\omega_{m}
is a bosonic Matsubara frequency, and
\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
are the real-space coordinates on the square lattice. The real-space profiles
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
and auxiliary eigenvalues
e_{\alpha}
are self-consistent solutions to a non-linear real-space equation

\left[-c^{2}\nabla^{2}+\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}(\mathbf{r})+u\,T\sum_{\omega_{m}}D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})\right]\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})=e_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r}).

(5)

This equation is formally equivalent to a nonlinear random Schrödinger problem with a non-linearity that depends on the full bosonic eigenspectrum, not just a single eigenstate. We characterize the resulting spectrum using several quantities. The first one is the disorder-averaged local density of bosonic eigenstates (DOS),

n(\mathcal{E})\equiv\left\langle\sum_{\alpha}|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}\delta(e_{\alpha}-\mathcal{E})\right\rangle\;.

(6)

After disorder averaging this quantity is spatially uniform, and summing it over lattice sites gives the corresponding global density of states. The second diagnostic is the localization length
\xi_{\alpha}
of a given eigenmode
\phi_{\alpha}
. In practice, we extract
\xi_{\alpha}
from the inverse participation ratio (IPR),

\xi_{\alpha}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}}},\quad\quad\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{r}}|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{4},\quad\quad|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{\xi_{\alpha}}e^{-|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}|/\xi_{\alpha}}\;,

(7)

where the last expression gives the exponentially decaying envelope used to relate the IPR to
\xi_{\alpha}
, and
\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}
is the localization center. Another important length scale is the bosonic correlation length
l_{\rm corr}
which characterizes the range over which the averaged propagator
\langle D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\rangle\sim\exp\{-|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|/l_{\rm corr}\}
decays with distance. On separation scales large compared with
l_{\rm corr}
, the propagator can be coarse-grained into a local form with
\mathbf{r}\approx\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
that depends only on the center-of-mass coordinate and varies on the scale of the typical localization length.

The action in Eq. (
3
) and closely related random-mass bosonic theories have been studied extensively
Vojta 2006
;
Vojta 2003
;
Hoyos et al. 2007
;
Vojta et al. 2009
;
Del Maestro et al. 2008
;
Patel et al. 2024
. A central result of these works is that the random-mass bosonic theory flows toward an infinite-randomness fixed point for the class of overdamped
O(N>1)
models considered here. The physical origin of this flow can be understood from rare extended spatial regions that support bosonic modes with anomalously small energies (i.e., small
e_{\alpha}
). The dissipative dynamics induced by the
c_{d}|\omega_{m}|
term in Eq. (
3
) plays a crucial role. At low frequencies, the local fluctuation entering the large-
N
constraint is logarithmically sensitive to the boson energy, schematically as
\int d\omega/(c_{d}|\omega|+e)\sim c_{d}^{-1}\log(\Lambda/e)
. A rare region can therefore satisfy the self-consistency condition with an exponentially small energy
e
, and hence with an exponentially long correlation time. Combining the exponentially small probability of finding a large favorable region with the exponentially long time scale of such a region gives the usual quantum Griffiths phenomenology: power-law-in-time averaged correlations and a power-law low-energy density of bosonic modes
Thill and Huse 1995
;
Read et al. 1995
. These localized modes remain dynamic for
N>1
due to the
O(N)
continuous symmetry with a lower critical dimension
d=2
(Mermin-Wagner theorem
Mermin and Wagner 1966
;
Codello and D’Odorico 2013

6
), the asymptotic Griffiths regime is characterized by an upturn of
n(\mathcal{E})
at the lowest energies, which may be parameterized as
n(\mathcal{E})\approx n\mathcal{E}^{\alpha_{G}}
with
\alpha_{G}<0
. This is the asymptotic rare-region regime indicated near
\lambda_{c}
in Fig.
1
(a). The corresponding low-energy modes are associated with increasingly large rare regions, and their localization length grows as their energy decreases.

The pairing problem, however, is sensitive to a broader part of the bosonic spectrum, not only to its asymptotic low-energy tail. Indeed, the pairing kernel contains the full normal-state propagator in Eq. (
4
), so modes with
e_{\alpha}
up to the energy scale relevant for pairing contribute to the effective interaction. Therefore, we need the distribution of bosonic energies and wave functions over the full low- and intermediate-energy window sampled by the pairing equation, which are not attainable in analytical form. For this reason we solve Eq. (
5
) numerically, as was done in Ref.
Patel et al. 2024
, and use the resulting modes as input to the superconducting problem.

An important observation from the numerical solution is that the phase diagram summarized in Fig.
1
(a) contains a broad regime distinct from the immediate Griffiths-critical region. As
\lambda
is lowered from the gapped disordered regime, the lower edge of the localized bosonic spectrum reaches zero at
\lambda_{\rm gapless}>\lambda_{c}
. For
\lambda_{c}<\lambda<\lambda_{\rm gapless}
, the boson spectrum remains gapless and furthermore acquires a buildup of zero-energy states such that
n(\mathcal{E}\approx 0)>0
, but the corresponding low-energy modes remain strongly localized. In this gapless-localized regime, the low-energy DOS is smooth and is well approximated by a constant over the numerical window relevant for pairing. Moreover, the bosonic correlation length
l_{\rm corr}
remains finite and small: the gapless character comes from localized eigenmodes with small
e_{\alpha}
, not from a divergent spatial correlation length.

(b), we plot
\xi_{\alpha}
versus

(a). The localization length is shortest for the smallest

\lambda_{c}
, where the DOS develops an upturn and the lowest-energy states are controlled by increasingly large rare regions with growing localization length.

The origin of this gapless-localized regime can be understood directly from the nonlinear eigenvalue problem in Eq. (
5
). The random mass acts as a spatially inhomogeneous potential for the bosonic modes. Regions, where the local bare potential
\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}({\bf r})
is anomalously small, form potential wells, and these wells can bind localized bosonic eigenstates. As the average tuning parameter
\lambda
(or temperature
T
) is lowered, the distribution of such local wells shifts downward in energy (see Fig.
1
(c)). In the absence of the self-consistent Hartree term, eigenstates with negative energies eventually would appear, signaling a tendency towards local condensation of
\phi
. However, the presence of the non-linearity prevents the deepest states from simply condensing into static order by pushing their energy above zero: any anomalously soft localized mode enhances the interaction-induced part of the potential
T\sum_{\omega_{m}}D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})
, implying that the local well is screened by the bosonic fluctuation that it itself produces. The dissipative dynamics again plays an important role here: because a soft overdamped mode contributes to the local fluctuation with a logarithmic sensitivity to its energy,
\sim c_{d}^{-1}\log(\Lambda/e_{\alpha})
, the feedback becomes increasingly important as
e_{\alpha}
approaches zero. The nonlinearity therefore pushes would-be negative and almost condensed localized states back to small positive energies. In this sense, the self-consistent Hartree term compresses the Lifshitz tail of the underlying random-mass problem and consequently generates a finite eigenmode DOS,
n(\mathcal{E}\approx 0)>0
. We note that this mechanism is not restricted to the asymptotic Griffiths regime: it only requires random-mass wells capable of binding localized bosonic modes, a local repulsion, and dissipative dynamics that makes the local fluctuation strongly sensitive to small
e_{\alpha}
. The result is a broad, de-tuned regime in which compact localized overdamped modes occur with a finite, approximately constant low-energy density of states.

As we discuss in Sec.
III
, these modes from the gapless localized phase are what provide the inhomogeneous pairing glue for electrons. Moreover, at higher temperatures above the “gapless” crossover (dashed orange line in Fig.
1
(a)), the same arguments can apply. This is because the pairing problem receives contributions from a finite window of bosonic energies, not only from the strict
e_{\alpha}\to 0
limit. Therefore the localized-glue description also applies on the gapped side, provided the gap is not too large relative to temperature,
e_{\rm gap}\lesssim c_{d}T
. For the range of temperatures and parameters we use in this work, this is indeed the case.

III
Quantum-critical superconductivity – results

With the properties of the quantum-critical normal state documented in Sec.
II
, we now turn to the superconducting instability. In this section, we summarize the main results, leaving the derivation of the effective pairing equation to Sec.
IV
. The resulting phenomenology is summarized in Fig.
2
.

The key input from Sec.
II
is that in our regime of interest the bosonic spectrum is gapless while the spatial correlations of the bosonic propagator remain short-ranged. More precisely, the bosonic correlation length
l_{\rm corr}
stays finite and small, whereas the low-energy bosonic eigenmodes have localized envelopes whose length scale is set by their localization length
\xi_{\alpha}
. This separation allows us to coarse-grain the bosonic propagator over its short relative-coordinate structure. After this coarse-graining, the relative coordinate of the Cooper pair is integrated out, and the remaining spatial dependence of the linearized Cooper problem is through the slow center-of-mass coordinate
\mathbf{r}
, as in the standard theory of disordered superconducting thin films. The length-scale hierarchy underlying this description is illustrated schematically in Fig.
2
(b).

The normal-state bosonic modes then enter the pairing problem through two local, spatially inhomogeneous quantities:

\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})=\sum\limits_{\alpha}\frac{|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}}{\omega_{m}^{2}+c_{d}|\omega_{m}|+e_{\alpha}},\quad\quad\bar{\Sigma}(i{\varepsilon_{n}},\mathbf{r})=\frac{\bar{g}^{2}}{\pi c_{d}}\sum_{\alpha}|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}\log\left(\frac{c_{d}|{\varepsilon_{n}}|+e_{\alpha}}{\pi c_{d}T+e_{\alpha}}\right).

(8)

Here
\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})
is the local bosonic propagator entering the pairing vertex, while
\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
is the corresponding local normal-state fermion self-energy (already integrated over the internal frequency). Both quantities vary in space through the weights
|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}
, and hence their spatial variation is controlled by the localization lengths
\xi_{\alpha}
of the bosonic modes that dominate at the relevant frequency. The effective local coupling is
\bar{g}^{2}=8\pi^{2}l_{\rm corr}^{2}\nu g^{2}
.

The linearized pairing problem is therefore an eigenvalue problem in Matsubara-frequency and center-of-mass coordinate space. We define pairing eigenstates
\Phi_{\alpha}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
and eigenvalues
\epsilon_{\alpha}
by

\Big[-D\nabla^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|+2\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\Big]\Phi_{\alpha}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})-2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum\limits_{\varepsilon_{m}}\bar{D}(i\varepsilon_{m}-i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\Phi_{\alpha}(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r})=\epsilon_{\alpha}\Phi_{\alpha}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r}).

(9)

Here
D=v_{F}^{2}\tau/2
is the electron diffusion coefficient and
\tau^{-1}
is the elastic scattering rate induced by the random potential
v(\mathbf{r})
.
We note that Eq. (
9
) is the modified version of the standard linearized Usadel equation
Usadel 1970
;
Meyer and Simons 2001
;
Tikhonov and Feigel’man 2020
;
Larkin and Ovchinnikov 1972
for the anomalous pair amplitude.
For instance, in case of a random BCS coupling
\lambda_{\rm BCS}(\mathbf{r})
(which was first studied in
Larkin and Ovchinnikov 1972
), this equation becomes

\Big[-D\nabla^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|\Big]\Phi_{\alpha}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})-2\lambda_{\rm BCS}(\mathbf{r})T\sum\limits_{\varepsilon_{m}}\Phi_{\alpha}(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r})=\epsilon_{\alpha}\Phi_{\alpha}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\;.

(10)

When tuned away from the critical point, all bosonic energies
e_{\alpha}
in Eq. (
8
) become large, and the boson eigenmode DOS opens a gap.
With the low-energy bosonic states absent, the frequency dependence in the denominator of Eq. (
8
) can be neglected, leading to the random-BCS case described by Eq. (
10
) with
\lambda_{\mathrm{BCS}}(\mathbf{r})=\bar{g}^{2}\sum_{\alpha}|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}/e_{\alpha}
.

The superconducting instability occurs when the lowest pairing eigenvalue crosses zero. For
\epsilon_{\alpha}<0
, the pair mode is unstable towards condensation (at the linearized level). If the corresponding eigenfunction is localized in space, then we interpret this mode as a nucleating superconducting puddle, corresponding to the upper inhomogeneous regime in Fig.
2
(a). If the eigenfunction is extended, then the transition corresponds to the onset of a macroscopic pairing state, schematically indicated by the dome in Fig.
2
(a). The numerical evolution from localized pairing zero modes at high temperature to an extended zero mode at lower temperature is shown in Fig.
2
(c), with representative localized and extended eigenfunctions shown in Figs.
2
(f) and
2
(g), respectively.

Equation (
9
) also makes clear the physical competition controlling the transition. The local propagator
\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})
enhances pairing through a retarded attractive interaction. The same localized bosonic modes also generate
\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
, which enters as a dephasing term. Thus favorable regions with strong bosonic glue also tend to have enhanced normal-state scattering. The structure of the pairing eigenstates is determined by the balance between this local attraction, local dephasing, and diffusive spreading of Cooper pairs through the kinetic term
-D\nabla^{2}
.

Let us finally comment on the self-consistency of using the normal-state bosonic propagator as input to Eq. (
9
). First, we treat the superconducting correlations as a linear instability out of the normal state. The anomalous amplitude is infinitesimal at the transition, so its feedback on the bosonic spectrum, including the depletion of low-energy fermionic states, is beyond the linearized approximation. Second, in the regime considered here, the fermionic polarization bubble is self-averaging and remains local on the scale of the electron mean free path,
l
. Since the inhomogeneous self-energy
\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
varies only on the longer bosonic localization scale, it is approximately constant over the region that controls the local polarization bubble. Thus, within the coarse-grained approximation used here, the Landau-damping term
c_{d}|\omega_{m}|
is not affected by the inhomogeneous self-energy background. The effect of
\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
is retained in the pairing problem only as a dephasing term. Finally, we note that the same coarse-grained pairing equation (
9
) can be derived in a large-
N
limit of a modified version of Eq. (
1
), in which the Yukawa coupling is taken to be spatially random with zero mean (see Appendix
C
).

In the rest of this section, we summarize the main properties obtained from Eq. (
9
) in three complementary ways. In Sec.
III.1
, we obtain the spectrum of Eq. (
9
) numerically using the exact bosonic modes obtained from the normal-state problem of Sec.
II
. In Sec.
III.2
, we develop a self-consistent Born approximation that describes the extended (but inhomogeneous) pairing solution and the macroscopic superconducting transition by treating the bosonic statistics phenomenologically. In Sec.
III.3
, we analyze the localized high-temperature tail of the pairing spectrum, corresponding to isolated superconducting puddles nucleated by the most favorable low-energy localized bosonic modes.

III.1
Numerical analysis

We first analyze Eq. (
9
) numerically, using the exact normal state boson eigenmodes described in Fig.
1
. All simulations are done on a square lattice of linear size
L
with periodic boundary conditions and with the following fixed parameters:
L=64,D=0.5,\bar{\lambda}=0.5,c_{d}=1,c^{2}=10,u=1
. The number of disorder configurations is
N_{d}=10
. The results of the numerical analysis are summarized in Fig.
2
, and more details are given in Sec.
V
.

The spatial structure of the superconducting state is contained in the spectral analysis of the linear pairing operator defined in Eq. (
9
). Diagonalizing this operator yields eigenvectors
\Phi_{\alpha}(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r})
and corresponding eigenvalues
\epsilon_{\alpha}
.
In order to characterize the spatial structure of these emergent pairing eigenmodes and their relation to the underlying localized bosonic states
\phi_{\alpha}
, we focus on the static component
\Phi_{\mathrm{stat,\alpha}}(\mathbf{r})\equiv T\sum_{m}\Phi_{\alpha}(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r})
, which enters the static pair susceptibility. Indeed, for a frequency-independent local pairing source (cf. Eq. (
28
)), the non-local zero-frequency pair susceptibility has the spectral representation

\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\sum\limits_{\alpha}\frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{stat,\alpha}}(\mathbf{r})\Phi_{\mathrm{stat,\alpha}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{\epsilon_{\alpha}}\;.

(11)

Thus the divergence of the pair susceptibility is controlled by the eigenmodes with
\epsilon_{\alpha}\approx 0
, while the spatial structure of the pair response is encoded in
\Phi_{\rm stat,\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
.

The relationship between the spatial structure of
\Phi_{\rm stat,\alpha}
and the eigenvalue
\epsilon_{\alpha}
closely resembles that of the localized bosonic modes
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
. Defining its localization length
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
exactly as done in Eq. (
7
) (making sure to normalize
\Phi_{\mathrm{stat,\alpha}}(\mathbf{r})
beforehand), we can see in Fig.
2
(c) that
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
is
\mathcal{O}(1)
for the lowest pairing eigenstates, and then increases linearly to
\mathcal{O}(L/2)
. As the temperature decreases from well above
T_{c}
, the solutions of the gap equation, i.e.
\epsilon_{\alpha^{\prime}}=0
, have a monotonically increasing
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha^{\prime}}
, from highly localized puddles to a fully delocalized solution. From the solution’s localization length we can extract the percentage of area that is ungapped,
\tilde{P}_{U}
(defined in Eq. (
30
)), which is shown as a function of
T
in Fig.
2
(d).

The shape of these curves and their behavior with
\lambda
is consistent with the same curves extracted from scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) measurements of
\text{Bi}_{2}\text{Sr}_{2}\text{CaCu}_{2}\text{O}_{8+\delta}
from Ref.
Gomes et al. 2007
(c.f. Fig.
4
and Sec.
V
). Both the experimental and numerical simulation curves show a very gradual onset of superconductivity as temperature is lowered: the interval of temperatures in which the sample changes from mostly ungapped to mostly gapped is comparable to the macroscopic
T_{c}
of the sample. Moreover, both sets of data are well fit by skew-normal distributions, and
their midpoints shift linearly with a tuning parameter (i.e. doping in experiment and detuning from the critical point in our numerics).

The transition temperature of delocalized solutions has a dependence on the coupling strength
\bar{g}^{2}
that is
T_{c}\sim\Lambda e^{-b/\bar{g}}
, which is a known result in the theory of non-Fermi liquid superconductivity
Son 1999
. This is shown in Fig.
2
(e), where it is compared to the BCS expectation of
T_{c}\sim\Lambda e^{-b/\bar{g}^{2}}
. This result is derived in Sec.
III.2
and
VI
using the self-consistent Born approximation.

Highly localized states with
\epsilon_{\alpha}\leq 0
correspond to isolated condensed puddles that will not result in global phase coherence. Some examples of these are shown in Fig.
2
(f). One important feature is that the most localized superconducting puddles sit almost entirely on the most localized bosonic modes that form the pairing glue (in contrast to the delocalized state in Fig.
2
(g)). In Sec.
V
we show that this feature is generic across
\lambda,\bar{g},T
. Focusing on higher temperature, we analyze these localized solutions in detail in Sec.
III.3
and
VII
, where we manage to reproduce this high overlap of localized states, and make a prediction for the high
T
behavior of
\tilde{P}_{U}(T)
(which we currently lack the numerical precision to confirm).

III.2
Extended pairing instability and mesoscopic gap inhomogeneity

The numerical solution of Eq. (
9
), summarized in Fig.
2
, shows that the pairing equation develops extended eigenstates at sufficiently low temperature. To analyze these extended solutions analytically, we employ a self-consistent Born approximation (SCBA), analogous to the approximation commonly used to study disorder-averaged spectral properties in disordered conductors
Lee and Ramakrishnan 1985
. The strategy is to separate the spatially averaged pairing problem from fluctuations around it. More specifically, we replace
\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
and
\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})
by their spatial averages plus their leading spatial cumulants, and treat the latter as weak disorder in the pairing kernel. Building on the phenomenology of Sec.
II
, we assume that the low-energy bosonic density of states is approximately constant,
n(\mathcal{E})=n=\mathrm{const}
(see Eq. (
6
)), from the lowest energies up to a cutoff
\mathcal{E}\sim\Lambda_{B}^{2}
, while the bosonic localization length
\xi
is roughly energy independent.

Averaging over the bosonic spectrum gives the following low-frequency forms for the spatially averaged pairing interaction and fermion self-energy:

\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\omega_{m})\approx n\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{|\omega_{m}|}\right),\quad\quad\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n})\approx\tilde{g}^{2}|\varepsilon_{n}|\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{|\varepsilon_{n}|}\right),\quad\quad\tilde{g}^{2}=\frac{\bar{g}^{2}n}{\pi},

(12)

where
\Lambda=\Lambda_{B}^{2}/c_{d}
.
The electron self-energy
\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n})
has a marginal Fermi-liquid form
Varma et al. 1989
, implying that electrons form a strange metal with a
T
-linear resistivity and
T\ln T
contribution to the specific heat in a normal state.
The derivation of the averaged expressions for
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\omega_{m})
and
\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n})
can be found in Section
VI
with the result being Eq. (
32
).

Spatial fluctuations of
\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})
generate an additional effective pairing kernel of the form

K_{DD}(\varepsilon_{n},\varepsilon_{m})\simeq\frac{\vartheta n}{2}\,\Theta\left(E_{\mathrm{Th}}-\eta\right)\ln^{2}\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\eta}\right),\quad\quad\eta=\max(|\varepsilon_{n}|,|\varepsilon_{m}|),\quad\quad\vartheta=\frac{3\bar{g}^{2}}{2\pi^{2}c_{d}D}.

(13)

In the expression above,
E_{\mathrm{Th}}=2D/\xi^{2}
is the Thouless energy of a Cooper pair confined in a region of size
\xi^{2}
.
In this case,
\xi=\xi(\pi c_{d}T)
is a characteristic bosonic localization length.
The total pairing vertex in Eq. (
9
) in this case takes a form
\bar{D}(i\varepsilon_{n}-i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r})\rightarrow\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n}-i\varepsilon_{m})+K_{DD}(\varepsilon_{n},\varepsilon_{m})
.
The double logarithm
\ln^{2}(E_{\mathrm{Th}}/\eta)
in Eq. (
13
) has a simple physical origin.
One logarithm comes from the static spatial redistribution of the Cooper pairs away from the homogeneous solution.
Since
\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})
plays the role of an attractive potential, Cooper pairs naturally concentrate in the regions of the stronger attraction, leading to higher values of the Gor’kov function
\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
in those regions. In turn, higher values of
\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
imply that the Cooper pairs experience, on average, a larger potential than
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\omega_{m})
.
The second logarithm originates from the enhanced return probability of diffusive Cooper pairs in two dimensions. Cooper pairs that enter a favorable attractive region are likely to return to it many times before escaping, further increasing the effective attraction.
It also turns out that spatial fluctuations of
\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
produce only subleading corrections, which mainly renormalize the effective coupling constant
\bar{g}^{2}
and do not change the qualitative structure of the pairing kernel.
The derivation of Eq. (
13
) is given in Sec.
VI.1
.

It is important to note that the explicit dependence of
\vartheta
on the Yukawa coupling constant
\bar{g}
in Eq. (
13
) should not be interpreted as an additional small parameter associated with the interaction strength. In a self-consistent treatment, the Landau-damping coefficient
c_{d}
is itself generated by the same electron-boson coupling (e.g., at the one-loop level, we have
c_{d}\sim\nu\bar{g}^{2}
). With this scaling,
\vartheta\propto\bar{g}^{2}/c_{d}D\sim 1/\nu D
, which is proportional to the inverse dimensionless Drude conductance
\mathcal{G}^{-1}\sim 1/k_{F}l\ll 1
. We should therefore regard
\vartheta
as an electronic parameter (independent of
\bar{g}
) controlling the strength of mesoscopic fluctuation effects.

Dimensionless constants
\tilde{g}
and
\vartheta
, along with two energy scales
\Lambda
and
E_{\mathrm{Th}}
, fully define the behavior of the averaged theory.
With the self-energy and the pairing potential given by Eqs. (
12
) and (
13
), Eq. (
9
) attains a superconducting solution at
T_{c}
given by

T_{c}\simeq\begin{cases}E_{\mathrm{Th}}\exp\left[-\#/(\tilde{g}^{2}\vartheta)^{1/3}\right],\quad\tilde{g}\lesssim\tilde{g}_{*},\\
\Lambda\exp\left[-\#/\tilde{g}\right],\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\,\tilde{g}\gtrsim\tilde{g}_{*},\end{cases}\quad\quad\quad\tilde{g}_{*}=\min[\vartheta,\;\ln^{-1}(\Lambda/E_{\mathrm{Th}})].

(14)

Note that above it is assumed that
E_{\mathrm{Th}}\lesssim\Lambda
.
In case of the opposite, one should set
E_{\mathrm{Th}}=\Lambda
in all the equations that involve
E_{\mathrm{Th}}
.
The calculation supporting Eq. (
14
), along with details of the structure of spatially averaged Gor’kov function
\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n})
, can be found in Section
VI.2
.

The two lines of Eq. (
14
) describe two distinct regimes. When
\tilde{g}
is large, the effects of spatial inhomogeneity provided by Eq. (
13
) are negligible.
In this scenario, the Cooper pairs diffuse over many bosonic localization volumes and probe numerous bosons.
This behavior efficiently averages over the inhomogeneity, resulting in the naively expected answer shown in the second line of Eq. (
14
).
For small
\tilde{g}
, the inhomogeneity correction from Eq. (
13
) dominates over the average effects, given by Eq. (
12
).
This signals the importance of the Cooper pair diffusive return physics and therefore predicts a significant gap inhomogeneity.

To further support the described physical picture, we estimate the degree of inhomogeneity of the pairing amplitude in the same formalism.
We evaluate the structure factor of the spatial fluctuations of the Gor’kov function
\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
and the pairing amplitude
\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(\mathbf{r})
, where

\displaystyle\Phi(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})=\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon)+\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r}),\quad\quad\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(\mathbf{r})=\int\frac{d\varepsilon}{2\pi}\Phi(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})=\langle\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle+\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(\mathbf{r}).

(15)

We seek
\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
as a solution to Eq. (
9
), with the fluctuation effects of
\delta\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})=\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})-\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n})
and
\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})=\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})-\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\omega_{m})
accounted up to the leading order. The correlation function of
\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(\mathbf{r})
for momenta above the thermal diffusive scale,
q\gtrsim\sqrt{T_{c}/D}
, takes the form (cf. Eq. (
52
))

\frac{\langle\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\,\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle(\mathbf{q})}{\langle\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle^{2}}\simeq\frac{32\pi^{4}\vartheta}{3\mathbf{q}^{2}}\frac{\ln\left(\frac{D\mathbf{q}^{2}}{\pi T_{c}}\right)}{\left(1+\mathbf{q}^{2}\xi^{2}/4\right)^{3}},\quad D\mathbf{q}^{2}\gtrsim\pi T_{c}.

(16)

The details of this calculation can be found in Section
VI.3
.
The non-integrable divergence of Eq. (
16
) at small momenta implies that the scale of relative spatial pairing amplitude fluctuations have a logarithmic enhancement at large distances.
These fluctuations become of the order of the average quantity at the distance
\xi_{\mathrm{SC}}
, where

\ln\left(\frac{\xi_{\mathrm{SC}}}{\xi}\right)\simeq\begin{cases}1\quad\tilde{g}\lesssim\tilde{g}_{*},\\
\tilde{g}/\vartheta,\quad\tilde{g}_{*}\lesssim\tilde{g}\lesssim\vartheta/\sqrt{\vartheta_{*}},\\
1/\sqrt{\vartheta_{*}},\quad\vartheta/\sqrt{\vartheta_{*}}\lesssim\tilde{g},\end{cases}\quad\quad\vartheta_{*}=\max(\vartheta,\vartheta^{2}\ln^{2}(\Lambda/E_{\mathrm{Th}})).

(17)

At the scale
\xi_{\mathrm{SC}}
, the impact of spatial fluctuations becomes significant, hence the SCBA approximation breaks down in favor of the strongly inhomogeneous profile of the superconducting gap.
For large values of
\tilde{g}
, the inhomogeneity length scale of the superconducting gap is exponentially larger than a typical inhomogeneity length scale of bosons
\xi
.
This conclusion is consistent with the intuition of an extended gap with freely propagating Cooper pairs that efficiently average out the bosonic inhomogeneity.
For small values of
\tilde{g}
, the length scales
\xi_{\mathrm{SC}}
and
\xi
are comparable.
This implies that the Cooper pairs prefer to occupy the regions of higher attraction and strongly prefer to remain in their vicinity due to the increased probability of return.

The effects of bosonic disorder described above are qualitatively different from the effects of potential disorder in BCS in multiple aspects.
First, the BCS problem with the UV scale
\Lambda
and effective BCS coupling
\tilde{g}^{2}
necessarily results in
T_{\mathrm{BCS}}\sim\Lambda\exp[-\#/\tilde{g}^{2}]
, which is parametrically much smaller than
T_{c}
in Eq. (
14
).
The effects of bosonic inhomogeneity should also be contrasted with the role of disorder in conventional BCS mean-field analyses.
For an
s
-wave order parameter, time-reversal-preserving potential disorder is largely inconsequential due to Anderson’s theorem, and substantial spatial variations of the pairing amplitude arise primarily from rare-region effects
Ioffe and Larkin 1981
;
Meyer and Simons 2001
;
Dodaro and Kivelson 2018
. For a
d
-wave order parameter, short-wavelength disorder can additionally act as a pair breaker by scattering quasiparticles between regions of the Fermi surface with opposite signs of the gap, leading to stronger gap inhomogeneity already at the BCS level
Atkinson et al. 2000
;
Nunner et al. 2005
;
Li et al. 2021
. In our problem, the origin of the gap inhomogeneity is different: the overdamped dynamics of localized bosonic modes produces strong long-wavelength variations of the pairing vertex, with a nontrivial dependence on the Matsubara frequency of the Gor’kov function. Rather than acting as a conventional pair-breaking impurity potential, this vertex locally favors pairing, but the presence of a finite density of low-energy localized modes strongly fragments the pairing response in space, as evidenced by Eq. (
16
). We further corroborate the role of such finite-density low-energy localized modes below, where we analyze pairing solutions associated with individual superconducting puddles.

III.3
Statistics of localized superconducting puddles

The large number of localized superconducting solutions displayed in Fig.
2
and the breakdown of SCBA treatment of inhomogeneity calls for a complementary approach to the pairing problem.
Evidently, these superconducting puddles emerge on top of the most localized bosons, which act as strong local mediators of pairing (compare, for example, the low-energy bosonic eigenstates in Fig.
1
(d) with the localized pairing eigenstates in Fig.
2
(f) obtained for the same realization of disorder).
We consider a single strongly localized boson
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
with a localization length
\xi_{\alpha}
.
Since the strongly localized bosons are relatively rare, we assume that this boson is embedded in the background of other localized bosonic states
\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{r})
with localization lengths
\xi_{\beta}\gg\xi_{\alpha}
.
If a superconducting puddle nucleates on top of
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
with local pairing scale
T_{c,\alpha}
, its spatial extent is controlled by the thermal diffusion length of the Cooperon,
L_{T_{c,\alpha}}\sim\sqrt{D/T_{c,\alpha}}
.
We focus on the regime suggested by the numerical solutions (Fig.
2
), in which different puddles do not overlap and the localized pairing solution is comparable in size to the parent bosonic mode. In terms of length scales, this corresponds to

\xi_{\alpha}\lesssim L_{T_{c,\alpha}}\ll\xi_{\beta}
for
\beta\neq\alpha
.
Hence, we are interested in a solution to Eq. (
9
) with the self-energy and pairing potential given by

\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})=\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n})+\frac{\bar{g}^{2}}{\pi c_{d}}|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}\ln\left(\frac{c_{d}|\varepsilon_{n}|+e_{\alpha}}{\pi c_{d}T+e_{\alpha}}\right),\quad\quad\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})=\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\omega_{m})+\frac{|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}}{\omega_{m}^{2}+c_{d}|\omega_{m}|+e_{\alpha}}.

(18)

We solve Eq. (
9
) for the highest
T_{c,\alpha}
that corresponds to an exponentially localized solution for a simplified bosonic mode profile
|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}\sim\Theta(\xi_{\alpha}-|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}|)
, with details of the calculation displayed in Section
VII
.

The “tail” of the distribution of the localized states is of most interest.
As suggested by Fig.
2
, the local pairing scales
T_{c,\alpha}
associated with the localized bosonic modes can be significantly larger than the pairing temperature of the extended eigenstate,
T_{c}
.
Therefore, assuming
T_{c,\alpha}\gg T_{c}
we can neglect the effects of pairing coming from the bulk, and focus on the pairing from the strongly localized part of the bosonic spectrum.
This allows to neglect
\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n})
and
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\omega_{m})
in Eq. (
18
), leaving only terms proportional to
|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}
.
The important consequences of such “puddle” physics are numerous.
Eq. (
18
) with
\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n})
and
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\omega_{m})
neglected immediately implies that
T_{c,\alpha}
is independent of
n
and the energy scale
\Lambda
.
Instead, the values of
D
and
\xi_{\alpha}
play an important role in determining
T_{c,\alpha}
through a strong inverse proximity effect from the surrounding metallic state and diffusive Cooperon physics of a disordered metal – a consequence of the compact spatial support of the pairing kernel.
As a result, the pairing temperature
T_{c,\alpha}
is controlled by
\vartheta
and
E_{\mathrm{Th},\alpha}=2D/\xi_{\alpha}^{2}
rather than
\tilde{g}
and
\Lambda
– quantities controlling
T_{c}
in SCBA-type averaged theory, Section
VI
.
In Section
VII
we solve Eq. (
9
) with the pairing kernel given in Eq. (
18
) and show that the temperature
T_{c,\alpha}
obeys the following equation

\pi T_{c,\alpha}+e_{\alpha}/c_{d}=E_{\mathrm{Th},\alpha}\exp\left[-\frac{3}{4\vartheta}\right],\quad\quad E_{\mathrm{Th},\alpha}=\frac{2D}{\xi_{\alpha}^{2}}.

(19)

Note that
T_{c,\alpha}
depends only on
E_{\mathrm{Th},\alpha}
,
e_{\alpha}
, and
\vartheta
, supporting the intuition presented above.

There are several conclusions to be drawn from Eq. (
19
).
First, only bosonic states that satisfy
e_{\alpha}\xi_{\alpha}^{2}<2Dc_{d}e^{-3/4\vartheta}
are capable of hosting a localized superconducting solution at finite temperature.
Second, the largest values of
T_{c,\alpha}
correspond to the smallest values of
e_{\alpha}
, assuming that
\xi(e_{\alpha})
grows with
e_{\alpha}
(see Fig.
1
).
Such behavior is consistent with the spectrum of Eq. (
9
) in Fig.
2
.
Whenever
\xi_{\alpha}\sim e_{\alpha}^{\gamma}
with
\gamma>0

and
n(e_{\alpha})=\mathrm{const}
for the smallest
e_{\alpha}
(Fig.
1
suggests
\gamma\approx 1
), the probability density
p(T_{c,\alpha})
of the local pairing scale distribution, and the fraction of gapped area
1-\tilde{P}_{U}(T)
of the sample at
T\gg T_{c}
scale as

p(T_{c,\alpha})\sim T_{c,\alpha}^{-1-1/2\gamma},\quad\quad 1-\tilde{P}_{U}(T)\sim T^{-1-1/2\gamma}.

(20)

The details of the calculation are shown in Section
VII
, Eqs. (
67
) and (
68
).

The power-law scaling of
p(T_{c,\alpha})
and
P_{U}(T)
originates from the Harris disorder that leads to the localization of bosonic glue.
The superconducting puddles with the highest transition temperatures are centered on the most localized bosonic states with the lowest values of
e_{\alpha}
, according to Eq. (
19
).
Therefore, the proliferation of strongly localized bosonic states near the critical point causes the appearance of a wide distribution of strongly localized superconducting states.

The behavior described above is qualitatively distinct from the Lifshitz-tail physics of superconducting puddles in a conventional random-BCS problem
Bulaevskii et al. 1987
;
Ioffe and Larkin 1981
;
Vojta 2006
;
Dodaro and Kivelson 2018
.
Eq. (
20
) predicts a power-law-like pairing temperature distribution of superconducting puddles, while the BCS problem conventionally predicts a stretched exponential scaling.
It is worth repeating the argument of
Vojta 2006
;
Dodaro and Kivelson 2018
for the stretched-exponential Lifshitz tails of the random-BCS problem to assess its robustness.
Assuming that the inverse BCS coupling constant
\lambda_{\mathrm{BCS}}^{-1}(\mathbf{r})=\langle\lambda_{\mathrm{BCS}}^{-1}\rangle-\delta\gamma_{\alpha}
with
\delta\gamma_{\alpha}
being Gaussian-distributed, the probability to find a region of size
R_{\alpha}
with coupling enhancement
\delta\gamma
is
p(R_{\alpha},\delta\gamma_{\alpha})\sim\exp[-AR^{d}_{\alpha}\delta\gamma^{2}_{\alpha}]
for
d=2
.
To ensure the condensation of the puddle of size
R_{\alpha}
at a temperature
T_{c,\alpha}>T_{c}
, the coupling enhancement
\delta\gamma_{\alpha}
should be sufficient to overcome both the increased thermal fluctuations and the inverse proximity effect.
It follows from Eq. (
10
) that
\delta\gamma_{\alpha}
can be estimated by

\delta\gamma_{\alpha}\sim\ln\left(\frac{D/R_{\alpha}^{2}+T_{c,\alpha}}{T_{c}}\right),

(21)

which should be considered the BCS analog of Eq. (
19
).
The temperature
T_{c}
is the superconducting onset temperature that corresponds to the averaged BCS coupling
\langle\lambda^{-1}_{\mathrm{BCS}}\rangle
.
Eq. (
21
) implies two distinct scaling regimes for the local pairing scale distribution of puddles
p_{\mathrm{BCS}}(T_{c,\alpha})
:

p_{\mathrm{BCS}}(T_{c,\alpha})\sim\begin{cases}\exp\left[-A^{\prime}\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}\right],\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\;\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}\lesssim 1,\\
\exp\left[-Aa^{\prime 2}\ln^{2}(T_{c,\alpha}/T_{c})\right],\quad\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}\gtrsim 1,\end{cases}\quad\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}=(T_{c,\alpha}-T_{c})/T_{c}.

(22)

Constants
A
and
A^{\prime}
are independent of
T_{c,\alpha}
, and
a^{\prime}
is a UV length scale defined below.
To obtain the first line of Eq. (
22
), Eq. (
21
) is linearized, leading to
\delta\gamma_{\alpha}=\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}+L_{T_{c,\alpha}}^{2}/R^{2}
, where
L_{T}=\sqrt{D/T}
.
For a fixed
T_{c,\alpha}
, probability density
p(R_{\alpha},\delta\gamma_{\alpha})
is strongly peaked for
R_{\alpha}\sim L_{T_{c,\alpha}}\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}^{-1/2}
.
The existence of the characteristic length scale
R_{\alpha}
for a given
T_{c,\alpha}
is what results in the exponential scaling
p_{\mathrm{BCS}}(T_{c,\alpha})\sim\exp[-A^{\prime}\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}]
.
In the regime that describes the second line of Eq. (
22
),
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}\gtrsim 1
, the size of a puddle is reduced to its minimum allowed by thermal diffusion:
R_{\alpha}\sim L_{T_{c,\alpha}}
.
For such short length scales, the coupling enhancement that is required for the puddle condensation is
\delta\gamma_{\alpha}\sim\ln(T_{c,\alpha}/T_{c})
.
For the small puddles, the size of the strongly attractive core of the puddle is no longer important for
T_{c,\alpha}
, as the effect of the inverse proximity effect has reached its maximum.
Therefore, as far as the UV cutoff
\Lambda
of the BCS problem is dominant, the cores of all sizes in the range
a^{\prime}\lesssim R\lesssim L_{T_{c,\alpha}}
contribute to the puddles of similar
T_{c,\alpha}
.
The UV cutoff
a^{\prime}
can be estimated by
a^{\prime}=\max(L_{\Lambda},a)
, where
a
is a characteristic length scale of
\lambda_{\mathrm{BCS}}(\mathbf{r})
disorder.
As a result, for
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}\gtrsim 1
one expects log-exponential scaling
p_{\mathrm{BCS}}(T_{c,\alpha})\sim\exp[-Aa^{\prime 2}\ln^{2}(T_{c,\alpha}/T_{c})]
.

The BCS argument presented above would fail if the distribution of
\delta\gamma_{\alpha}
was non-Gaussian, which would require a failure of the central limit theorem.
The value
\delta\gamma_{\alpha}
is an effective quantity of a large puddle composed of a large number of microscopic fluctuations.
The central limit theorem can only fail if the microscopic fluctuations are strongly correlated at large, mesoscopic distances comparable to the puddle, or if the microscopic fluctuations are drawn from a distribution with infinite variance
Gnedenko and Kolmogorov 1968
.
The former option is the signature of long-range non-Fermi-liquid correlations, and the latter option only occurs in the vicinity of the infinite randomness fixed point.
The importance of the central limit theorem can be seen from a simple example: if the distribution of
\delta\gamma_{\alpha}
is a power-law instead of a Gaussian, it is easy to find relatively small impurities of size
L_{T}\lesssim R\lesssim L_{T}\tau^{-1/2}
.
This, in turn, would lead to a proliferation of small, power-law-distributed puddles with sufficiently large
\delta\gamma_{\alpha}
to overcome the inverse proximity effect, as described in the quantum-critical scenario.

IV
Linearized Usadel Equation

In this section, we provide further details regarding the origin and underlying assumptions of the linearized pairing equation, Eq. (
9
), used in Sec.
III
. The key point is that the random-mass disorder must be treated nonperturbatively in the bosonic sector, since it localizes the low-energy modes that mediate pairing. By contrast, the fermionic states at the Fermi level remain extended and diffusive (provided
k_{F}l\gg 1
), and can be treated within a self-averaging approximation. To capture this regime, we first coarse-grain the non-local structure of both the electronic and bosonic propagators, controlled by
l
and
l_{\rm corr}
, respectively. We then derive a real-space Usadel equation for the anomalous Cooper-pair amplitude, whose remaining spatial dependence is in the center-of-mass coordinate and varies on the scale of the bosonic localization length
\xi
.

We begin by specifying the hierarchy of length scales, which is schematically illustrated in Fig.
2
(b). The disorder-averaged fermionic Green’s function decays on the scale of the elastic mean free path
l
, so
\langle G(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\rangle\propto\exp\left\{-|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|/2l\right\}
.
Within the self-consistent Born approximation (SCBA), the random chemical potential
v(\mathbf{r})
alone leads to
l=v_{F}\tau
, where
v_{F}
is the Fermi velocity, and
\tau=1/m\bar{v}^{2}
is the mean free time.
Length scale
l
is unrelated to localization and only reflects the fact that the phases of the wave-functions become uncorrelated at long distances.
Similarly, the spatial decay of the averaged bosonic propagator
\langle D(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\rangle\propto\exp\left\{-|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|/2l_{\rm corr}\right\}
is characterized by the correlation length
l_{\rm corr}
.
Finally, anticipating localization of the bosonic wave-functions due to random mass disorder, we characterize them by a (low-energy) localization length
\xi
.
Our main physical assumption will be the following hierarchy of scales

k_{F}^{-1}\;\lesssim\;\;l_{\rm corr}\;\ll\;l\;\;\ll\xi\;.

(23)

Let us clarify the physical significance of this hierarchy.
First, the condition
k_{F}^{-1}\ll l
is standard for weakly-disordered metals and translates to a large dimensionless Drude conductivity
\mathcal{G}\gg 1
in the normal state.
In 2D, the fermion localization length is exponentially longer than the mean free path, i.e.
\sim le^{\#\mathcal{G}}
, and thus we can disregard it and treat fermions as delocalized.
In combination with the fact that the order parameter is non-conserved (e.g., Ising-nematic or AFM), the condition
k_{F}^{-1}\ll l
also leads to the absence of disorder-induced vertex corrections (i.e., the bosonic fluctuations are not enhanced by diffusion directly), which otherwise would lead to strong temperature-dependent Altshuler-Aronov corrections to conductivity (for the discussion of these issues see
Nosov et al. 2020
;
Nosov et al. 2023
).

The condition
l_{\rm corr}\ll l
allows us to treat the bosonic propagator as local on the scale over which the electronic motion becomes diffusive. Thus the short-distance relative-coordinate structure of
D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
can be integrated out, leaving a local pairing kernel
D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\simeq 8\pi l_{\rm corr}^{2}\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
,
where
\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})
is given in Eq. (
8
). Finally, the condition
l\ll\xi
implies that the spatial fluctuations of the boson-induced self-energy and pairing interaction occur on scales much longer than the fermionic mean free path. The localized bosons therefore generate random long-wavelength dephasing and pairing potentials for diffusive Cooper pairs. This last assumption is important for our derivation, but is also physically plausible: the electronic mean free path in strongly correlated metals rarely exceeds tens of lattice spacings, while the relevant localization lengths in our model mostly exceed that scale; see Fig.
1
(b).

Making use of the essentially local nature of the bosonic Green’s function on the scale of
l
and
l_{\rm corr}
, we can also approximate the contribution to a fermionic self-energy from scattering off bosons. The full one-loop expression,

\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=g^{2}T\sum_{\omega_{m}}D(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})G(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

(24)

is coarse-grained to

\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=-i\,\mathrm{sgn}(\varepsilon_{n})\,\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),\quad\quad\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})=\bar{g}^{2}\,\mathrm{sgn}(\varepsilon_{n})\,T\sum\limits_{\omega_{m}}\operatorname{sgn}(\varepsilon_{n}+\omega_{m})\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r}),

(25)

where
\bar{g}^{2}=8\pi^{2}l^{2}_{\mathrm{corr}}\nu g^{2}
is an effective coupling constant, and
\nu=m/2\pi
is a fermionic density of states at the Fermi level (see Appendix
A
for details).
At this level of coarse-graining, the local value of the fermionic propagator only depends on the density of states and not on the quasi-particle dynamics.
Hence, the approximation is self-consistent: the electronic part of the system is self-averaging in the
k_{F}l\gg 1
limit.

Figure 3:
The first line is a definition for
\mathcal{C}_{0}
– a “bare” Cooperon – as diagrammatic series. The dashed line represents a conventional impurity scattering induced by a random chemical potential
v(\bm{r})
.
The second line is a diagrammatic series definition for pairing vertex
\Delta
. The wavy line denotes the position-dependent bosonic propagator
\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\bm{r})
.

The pairing problem in this setting is reduced to the calculation of the pairing vertex and the pairing susceptibility, in a conventional way
Abrikosov et al. 2012
;
Levtov and Shytov 2003
.
The construction of a real-space pairing vertex
\Delta(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
induced by the presence of a local source
\Delta_{0}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
is performed with the use of a real-space Cooperon propagator
\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
. This propagator excludes the vertex corrections originating from the inelastic interactions with a boson, and only includes the potential disorder ladder and the self-energy corrections (see Fig.
3
for a diagrammatic representation). The remaining pairing vertex corrections originating from
\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})
can then be conveniently expressed by using
\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
, as also shown in Fig.
3
. The diagrammatic series for
\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
and
\Delta(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
can be resummed (see
Levtov and Shytov 2003
and Sec.
B
for the details) by solving the following equations

\displaystyle\Big(-D\nabla^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|+2\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\Big)\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

(26)

\displaystyle\Delta(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})-2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum\limits_{\varepsilon_{m}}\bar{D}(i\varepsilon_{n}-i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r})\int d\bar{\mathbf{r}}\;\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r},\bar{\mathbf{r}})\Delta(i\varepsilon_{m},\bar{\mathbf{r}},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\Delta_{0}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\;.

Here
D=v_{F}^{2}\tau/2
is the diffusion coefficient, and
\Delta_{0}
is an infinitesimal local pairing source. One can further define the real-space Gor’kov function, which obeys the following equivalent equations

\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\int d\bar{\mathbf{r}}\;\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\bar{\mathbf{r}})\Delta(i\varepsilon_{n},\bar{\mathbf{r}},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\;,\quad\quad\left[-D\nabla^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|+2\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\right]\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\Delta(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\;.

(27)

After substituting the second expression above into the second equation in Eq. (
26
), we find

\Big(-D\nabla^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|+2\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\Big)\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})-2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum\limits_{\varepsilon_{m}}\bar{D}(i\varepsilon_{m}-i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\Phi(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\Delta_{0}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\;.

(28)

Note that this equation is completely local in space, and the second coordinate
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
only reflects the position of the source term on the right-hand side.
If we now set
\Delta_{0}=0
, we find

\Big(-D\nabla^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|+2\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\Big)\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})-2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum\limits_{\varepsilon_{m}}\bar{D}(i\varepsilon_{m}-i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\Phi(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r})=0\;.

(29)

This equation is a special zero mode case,
\epsilon_{\alpha}=0
, of the general eigenvalue problem defined in Eq. (
9
).
Lastly, we note that the ‘static’ terms in the self-energy and the pairing vertex in Eq. (
29
) cancel each other identically – manifestation of Anderson’s theorem.

V
Numerical Results

We first solve Eq. (
9
) numerically, using the boson wavefunctions
\phi_{\alpha}
obtained from the procedure outlined in Sec.
II
. More precisely, we find the entire spectrum of the operator of the pairing equation, rather than solving only for its zero eigenvalue. This allows us to do a more thorough analysis of the inhomogeneous superconducting state. The technical details of how we obtain the numerical solution can be found in Appendix
I
.

The full spectrum of eigenvalues
\epsilon_{\alpha}
behaves as follows. At high temperatures above the pairing instability, all
\epsilon_{\alpha}>0

\epsilon_{\alpha}

As in the SCBA analysis of Sec.

29

\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}

7

\xi_{\alpha}

(highly localized) and

\epsilon_{\alpha}>0
. Upon lowering the temperature, eventually the most localized eigenstate,

VI
Self-consistent Born approximation

We use Eq. (
29
) to analyze how spatial inhomogeneity modifies the linearized Gor’kov function.
We begin by considering the analog of the Born approximation for the spatially inhomogeneous boson-mediated pairing interaction.

To perform disorder averaging, we use the phenomenological assumptions consistent with Section
II
.
In particular, we assume that all the bosonic eigenstates
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
with eigenvalues
e_{\alpha}
are localized and their localization length
\xi_{\alpha}=\xi(e_{\alpha})
is only a function of the eigenvalue.
Adding the assumption of disorder-averaged translation invariance allows to express the average electron self-energy
\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon)
and the effective pairing potential
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\omega)
through
n(\mathcal{E})=\left\langle\sum_{\alpha}|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}\delta(e_{\alpha}-\mathcal{E})\right\rangle
(see Eq. (
6
)).
Averaging of Eqs. (
8
) leads to

\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\omega)\approx\int d\mathcal{E}\,n(\mathcal{E})\,\frac{1}{\omega^{2}+c_{d}|\omega|+\mathcal{E}},\quad\quad\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon)\approx 2\bar{g}^{2}\int_{\pi T}^{\varepsilon}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\omega).

(31)

For low-energy bosonic states distributed according to the power law
n(\mathcal{E})=n\mathcal{E}^{\alpha_{G}}
, as is expected in the Griffiths regime, the average self-energy and the pairing potential scale as

\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\omega)\approx n\begin{cases}\pi\csc(\pi|\alpha|)|c_{d}\omega|^{\alpha_{G}},\quad\alpha_{G}<0,\\
\ln(\Lambda/|\omega|),\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\;\;\,\alpha_{G}=0,\end{cases}\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon)\approx\frac{\bar{g}^{2}n}{\pi}\begin{cases}\pi\csc(\pi|\alpha_{G}|)(1+\alpha_{G})c_{d}^{\alpha_{G}}|\varepsilon|^{\alpha_{G}+1},\quad\alpha_{G}<0,\\
|\varepsilon|\ln(\Lambda/|\varepsilon|),\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\alpha_{G}=0.\end{cases}

(32)

In case of
\alpha_{G}=0
, the cutoff
\Lambda
is defined as
\Lambda=\Lambda_{B}^{2}/c_{d}
, where
\Lambda_{B}^{2}
is the UV cutoff of the bosonic eigenvalues that span
n(\mathcal{E})
.
The derivation above assumes
c_{d}\gg T
,
\varepsilon_{n},\omega_{n}\lesssim\Lambda
, which allows to neglect
\omega_{n}^{2}
term in Eq. (
8
).
From Eq. (
32
), one observes that different values of
\alpha
reproduce different limits of “
\gamma
-model”
Abanov and Chubukov 2020
;
Wu et al. 2020a
;
Wu et al. 2020b
;
Wu et al. 2021a
;
Wu et al. 2021b
;
Zhang et al. 2021
, including it into the perspective of current work.

For a moment, we focus on the case of non-vanishing density of states
n(\mathcal{E})=n=\mathrm{const}
, as it will turn out to be the most physically interesting regime, which is also supported by numerical simulation (see Sec.
II
).
For a moment, we assume that spatial fluctuations of
\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
and
\bar{D}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{r})
are neglected, so
\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n})
and
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\omega_{n})
can be used instead in Eq. (
29
).
With the notation
\Phi_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
reserved for the solution of Eq. (
29
) with the inhomogeneity neglected, the linearized Usadel equation can be written as

\Big(-D\nabla^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|+2\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n})\Big)\Phi_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})-2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum\limits_{\varepsilon_{m}}\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{m}-i\varepsilon_{n})\Phi_{0}(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r})=\Delta_{0}.

(33)

The leading instability at temperature
T_{c0}
in this translationally invariant problem corresponds to a homogeneous solution.
To tackle the problem analytically, we adopt the approach presented in
Abanov and Chubukov 2020
.
First, we approximate the Matsubara frequency sums with integrals by employing the Euler-Maclaurin approximation
Apostol 1999
under the assumption of a small
T_{c0}
.
Then, we estimate the contribution from
\langle\bar{D}\rangle
to Eq. (
33
), by approximating the kernel
{\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i|\varepsilon_{n}-\varepsilon_{m}|)\approx\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\max(|\varepsilon_{n}|,|\varepsilon_{m}|))}
, leading to

\varepsilon\left[1+\tilde{g}^{2}\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\varepsilon}\right)\right]\Phi_{0}(i\varepsilon)=\tilde{g}^{2}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)\Phi_{0}(i\varepsilon^{\prime})+\Delta_{0},

(34)

for
\varepsilon>0
, and
\tilde{g}^{2}=\bar{g}^{2}n/\pi
. Eq. (
34
) can be solved by reducing it to a differential equation. The solution to the integro-differential equation takes the form
\Phi_{0}(i\varepsilon)=[\varepsilon+\tilde{g}^{2}\varepsilon\ln(\Lambda/\varepsilon)]^{-1}F(\varepsilon)
,
and

F(\varepsilon)=c_{1}\sqrt{x}\,J_{1}\left(2\sqrt{x}\right)+c_{2}\sqrt{x}\,Y_{1}\left(2\sqrt{x}\right),\quad x(\varepsilon)=\log\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\varepsilon}\right)+\frac{1}{\tilde{g}^{2}}.

(35)

The substitution of Eq. (
35
) back into Eq. (
34
) fixes
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
up to a multiplicative constant, resulting in
the pair density that for
\tilde{g}\ll 1
can be written as

\Phi_{\mathrm{0,stat}}(\omega=0)=\int\frac{d\varepsilon}{2\pi}\Phi_{0}(i\varepsilon)\approx\frac{\Delta_{0}}{2\pi\tilde{g}}\tan\left(\tilde{g}\,\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\pi T}\right)\right).

(36)

For algebraic details of solving Eq. (
34
) that lead to Eq. (
36
) see Appendix
F
.
The pairing susceptibility in Eq. (
36
) diverges at

T_{c0}=\frac{\Lambda}{\pi}\exp\left[-\frac{\pi}{2\tilde{g}}\right]\gg T_{\mathrm{NFL}}\sim\frac{\Lambda}{\pi}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\tilde{g}^{2}}\right],

(37)

which is a well-known result in the theory of non-Fermi liquid superconductivity
Son 1999
.
By comparing it against the temperature
T_{\mathrm{NFL}}
, at which the effects of
\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(\varepsilon)
become comparable to
|\varepsilon|
on the left side of Eq. (
34
), one concludes that
T_{c0}\gg T_{\mathrm{NFL}}
.
This implies that the effects of the self-energy
\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})
are negligible in Eq. (
29
) for temperatures comparable to and above
T_{c0}
.
This motivates neglecting the
\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
in Eq. (
29
) and considering a simplified problem.
For a more formal treatment and estimates, see Appendix
D
.

VI.1
Effects of boson localization on the pairing interaction

We treat the corrections from spatial fluctuations of
\bar{D}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{r})
by accounting for them perturbatively at the level of the Usadel equation in Eq. (
29
).
The effects of spatial fluctuations of
\bar{D}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{r})
are encoded in
\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{r})
, where

\bar{D}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{r})=\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\omega_{n})+\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{r})

(38)

To capture the effects of
\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{r})
up to second order, we convolve Eq. (
29
) with
\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
, which is given by Eq. (
26
), but with self-energy dropped.
This allows to rewrite Eq. (
29
) as

\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=2\bar{g}^{2}T\int d^{2}\mathbf{x}\;\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{x})\sum_{\varepsilon_{m}}\Big(\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n}-i\varepsilon_{m})+\delta\bar{D}(i\varepsilon_{n}-i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{x})\Big)\Phi(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(39)

After substitution of Eq. (
39
) into itself, averaging over the bosonic disorder ensemble, and acting with
(-D\nabla^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|)
from the left on the resulting equation, we recover

\Big(-D\nabla^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|\Big)\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum_{\varepsilon_{m}}\Big(\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n}-i\varepsilon_{m})+\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon_{n},\varepsilon_{m})\Big)\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

(40)

where
\bar{K}_{DD}
is
the inhomogeneity correction kernel, which can be expressed as

\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon_{n},\varepsilon_{n}+\omega_{m})=2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\neq 0\\
l\neq m\end{subarray}}\int d^{2}\mathbf{r}\,\langle\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{m}-i\omega_{l},\mathbf{r})\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{l},0)\rangle\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m}-i\omega_{l},\mathbf{r}).

(41)

In the process of deriving Eq. (
40
), we neglected all
\delta\bar{D}-\delta\bar{D}
correlations beyond second order.
In particular, we approximated
\langle\delta\bar{D}\,\delta\bar{D}\,\Phi\rangle\approx\langle\delta\bar{D}\,\delta\bar{D}\rangle\langle\Phi\rangle
.
The algebraic details that lead to the Eqs. (
40
) and (
41
) are rather technical and can be found in
Appendix
D
.

The structure of
\bar{K}_{DD}
is determined from the spatial correlations of
\bar{D}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{r})
by Eq. (
41
).
To evaluate the expression for
\bar{K}_{DD}
, we first evaluate
\langle\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{r})\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},0)\rangle
.
This correlation function is most conveniently expressed through
P_{2,2}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{r})=\left\langle\sum_{\alpha}|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}|\phi_{\alpha}(0)|^{2}\,\delta(\mathcal{E}-e_{\alpha})\right\rangle

Evers and Mirlin 2008
;
Cuevas and Kravtsov 2007
:

\langle\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{r})\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},0)\rangle\approx\int d\mathcal{E}\,P_{2,2}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{r})W(\omega_{n},\mathcal{E})W(\omega_{m},\mathcal{E}),\quad\quad W(\omega_{n},\mathcal{E})\approx(c_{d}|\omega_{n}|+\mathcal{E})^{-1}.

(42)

The bosonic states correlation function
P_{2,2}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{r})
in the momentum space takes form
P_{2,2}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{q})=\int d^{2}\mathbf{r}\,e^{-i\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{q}}P_{2,2}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{r})=n(\mathcal{E})(1+\mathbf{q}^{2}\xi^{2}/4)^{-3}
, where
n(\mathcal{E})=n\mathcal{E}^{\alpha_{G}}
.
Finally, in a diffusive metal, the bare Cooperon in momentum space is of the form
{\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{q})=(D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|)^{-1}}
.
For the scaling estimates below, we replace
\xi(\mathcal{E})
by a representative low-energy value
\xi=\xi(\pi c_{d}T)
.
Substitution of Eq. (
42
) into Eq. (
41
) along with the expressions above results in

\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})\simeq\frac{\bar{g}^{2}n}{4\pi^{2}c_{d}D}\frac{\pi\alpha_{G}c_{d}^{\alpha_{G}}}{\sin\pi\alpha_{G}}\begin{cases}\eta^{\alpha_{G}}R(\eta),\quad\quad\quad\quad\alpha_{G}<0,\\
\left|\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\eta}\right)\right|R(\eta),\quad\alpha_{G}=0,\end{cases}\quad\quad R(\eta)=\begin{cases}\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\eta}\right),\quad\eta<E_{\mathrm{Th}},\\
\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{2\eta},\quad\quad\quad\;\;\eta>E_{\mathrm{Th}},\end{cases}

(43)

where
\eta=\max(|\varepsilon|,|\varepsilon^{\prime}|)
and
E_{\mathrm{Th}}=2D/\xi^{2}
.
The algebraic details of the calculation outlined in this section can be found in the Appendices
D
and
E
.

It is interesting to note that the inhomogeneity corrections are rather mild for
\alpha_{G}<0
: the power-law scaling of
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime})
is only logarithmically amplified in
\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
.
To leading order, the effects of these corrections can be absorbed into the homogeneous theory of Eq. (
33
) by adjusting the coupling constant.
The status of the spatial-inhomogeneity corrections is qualitatively different for
\alpha_{G}=0
.
The logarithmic scaling of
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime})
is amplified by the additional square-logarithmic
\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
.
Thus, inhomogeneity provides a much stronger correction to the effective pairing potential of Eq. (
33
) at low energies.

Once more, we narrow our scope to
n(\mathcal{E})=n=\mathrm{const}
(
\alpha_{G}=0
case), as it is most relevant according to the argument above and is supported by numerical results (Section
II
).
Following the structure of Eq. (
43
), we approximate the inhomogeneity kernel as in Eq. (
13
),
where
\vartheta=3\bar{g}^{2}/2\pi^{2}c_{d}D
and
\Theta(\epsilon)
is a Heaviside step-function.

The physical meaning of
\vartheta
can be understood in the context of the Y-SYK model
Patel et al. 2023
.
The combination
\bar{g}^{2}/\nu
should be regarded as a spatially disordered coupling constant in the notation of
Patel et al. 2023
, leading to
c_{d}\sim\nu\bar{g}^{2}
in the framework of Y-SYK.
Consequently,
\vartheta\sim(k_{F}l)^{-1}
, where
l
is an electron mean free path.
The quantity
k_{F}l
is proportional to the number of bosonic states that an electron visits before its phase decoheres due to disorder.
Therefore, the larger
k_{F}l
, the more bosonic states the electron coherently probes, smearing the effects of inhomogeneity and decreasing the effects of
\bar{K}_{DD}
.
The electronic nature of the parameter
\vartheta
does not permit treating
\vartheta
as a bosonic parameter that reproduces the homogeneous limit in the limit
\vartheta\rightarrow 0
.
Instead, one should consider
\xi\rightarrow\infty
as a homogeneous limit: it leads to
E_{\mathrm{Th}}\rightarrow 0
, so the effects of
\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
are negligible when
E_{\mathrm{Th}}<T_{c0}
.
As a reminder,
T_{c0}
is a superconducting onset temperature in the homogeneous limit, which is given by Eq. (
37
).

VI.2
Spatially averaged pairing amplitude and superconducting transition temperature

To estimate the effects of
\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
on the structure of the spatially averaged Gor’kov function
\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n})
, we solve

|\varepsilon|\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon)=2\bar{g}^{2}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}\frac{d\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2\pi}\Big(\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\max(|\varepsilon|,|\varepsilon^{\prime}|))+\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})\Big)\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon^{\prime}),

(44)

where
\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
is given by Eq. (
13
) and, as before, we approximated
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime})
by
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(\max(|\varepsilon|,|\varepsilon^{\prime}|))
.
Eq. (
44
) can be solved exactly by

\displaystyle\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon)=\begin{cases}\left[A_{+}\cos\left(u(\varepsilon)\right)+B_{+}\sin\left(u(\varepsilon)\right)\right]/\varepsilon,\quad u(\varepsilon)=\tilde{g}\ln(\Lambda/\varepsilon),\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\varepsilon\geq E_{\mathrm{Th}}\\
\left[A_{-}\,\mathrm{Ai}^{\prime}\left(h(\varepsilon)\right)+B_{-}\,\mathrm{Bi}^{\prime}\left(h(\varepsilon)\right)\right]/\varepsilon,\quad h(\varepsilon)=-\left(\frac{\tilde{g}}{\vartheta}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\left(1-\vartheta\ln\left(\Lambda/E_{\mathrm{Th}}\right)+(\vartheta/\tilde{g})u(\varepsilon)\right),\quad\;\varepsilon\leq E_{\mathrm{Th}}\end{cases}

(45)

The functions
\mathrm{Ai}\,(u)
and
\mathrm{Bi}\,(u)
are the Airy functions of the first and second kind, respectively, and the constants
A_{\pm}
and
B_{\pm}
are determined self-consistently.
The smoothness conditions implied by Eq. (
44
) at
\varepsilon=E_{\mathrm{Th}}
ensure that the values of

\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon)
and

\partial_{\varepsilon}\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon)
are continuous across
\varepsilon=E_{\mathrm{Th}}
.
The self-consistency conditions at
\varepsilon=\Lambda
ensure that

\langle\Phi\rangle(i\Lambda)=0
.
Substitution of Eq. (
45
) into Eq. (
44
) with the three conditions mentioned above allows to fully determine all the unknown coefficients in Eq. (
45
).
The resulting behavior of the Gor’kov function is displayed in Fig.
6a
and
T_{c}
is shown in Fig.
6b
.
The exact solution to Eq. (
44
) that results in Fig.
6a
is not physically illuminative, so it can be found in Appendix
G
.

T_{c}
as a function of

, as described by Eq. (

). The lower value on the plot implies a higher
T_{c}
. The constant

determines the crossover between the two regimes. In the regime

\pi T_{c}=\Lambda\exp[-\#\tilde{g}^{-1}]
. In the opposite regime,

. In both regimes, numerical values

and

, but always remain of the order of unity.

T_{c}
shown in Fig.

also follows from the exact solution and can be easily understood from the Rayleigh-Ritz method applied to (
44
).
Eq. (
44
) can be understood as the eigenvalue equation
\langle\Phi\rangle=V[\langle\Phi\rangle]
for a linear operator
V
that has a form

V[\langle\Phi\rangle](\varepsilon)=\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{\varepsilon}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\left[\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)+\frac{\vartheta}{2}\;\Theta\left(E_{\mathrm{Th}}-\varepsilon\right)\Theta\left(E_{\mathrm{Th}}-\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)\ln^{2}\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)\right]\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon^{\prime}).

(46)

The system goes through the superconducting transition at the temperature
T_{c}
when the largest eigenvalue of the operator
V
equals unity.
The largest eigenvalue can be estimated from below using the Rayleigh-Ritz method by evaluating

(\Phi_{T},V[\Phi_{T}])/(\Phi_{T},\Phi_{T})
for a test function

\Phi_{T}(i\varepsilon)
.
The brackets
(f,g)
for functions
f
and
g
denote the dot product of the form
\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon\,\varepsilon f(\varepsilon)g(\varepsilon)
.
The solution to the gap equation with no inhomogeneity effects provides a good candidate for a test gap function, so we set
\Phi_{T}(i\varepsilon)\approx\varepsilon^{-1}\cos(\tilde{g}\ln(\varepsilon/\pi T))
.
The lower estimate of the largest eigenvalue of
V
then evaluates to

\frac{(\Phi_{T},V[\Phi_{T}])}{(\Phi_{T},\Phi_{T})}=\frac{2x-\sin(2x)}{2x+\sin(2x)}+\frac{\vartheta}{\tilde{g}}\Theta(x-g_{\mathrm{Th}})\frac{2(x-g_{\mathrm{Th}})^{2}-2\sin^{2}(x-g_{\mathrm{Th}})}{2x+\sin(2x)},\quad x=\tilde{g}\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\pi T_{c}}\right),\quad g_{\mathrm{Th}}=\tilde{g}\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}\right).

(47)

The lower bound on
T_{c}
is determined by the value of
x
that equates to unity the right side of Eq. (
47
).
The expression in Eq. (
47
) contains two regimes that are controlled solely by the ratio
\vartheta/\tilde{g}
.
When
\vartheta/\tilde{g}
is small, the second term in Eq. (
47
) can be neglected.
This corresponds to the regime where the disorder-averaged interaction produced by
\langle D\rangle(i\varepsilon)
dominates over
\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
in Eq. (
44
).
The smallest
x
that equates the right side of Eq. (
47
) to unity is
x=\pi/2
, leading to

T_{c}\sim\Lambda\exp\left[-\frac{\pi}{2\tilde{g}}\right],

(48)

as expected from Eq. (
37
).
This regime is valid when
\tilde{g}\gtrsim\vartheta
and the effects of
\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{Th}}
in the second term of Eq. (
47
) are negligible.
The latter occurs when
\tilde{g}\gtrsim\ln^{-1}(\Lambda/E_{\mathrm{Th}})
, leading to
\tilde{g}_{*}=\min(\vartheta,\ln^{-1}(\Lambda/E_{\mathrm{Th}}))
, as shown in Fig.
6b
.

When
\vartheta/\tilde{g}
is large, the first term on the right of Eq. (

) can be neglected, so
\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
dominates over
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\varepsilon)
in Eq. (

\vartheta x^{3}/\tilde{g}=1
.
The superconducting transition temperature then behaves as

T_{c}\sim E_{\mathrm{Th}}\exp\left[-\frac{\#}{(\vartheta\tilde{g}^{2})^{1/3}}\right],

(49)

which is also indicated in Fig.
6b
.
This behavior is in agreement with the exact solution displayed in Appendix
G
.

VI.3
Spatial fluctuations of the pairing amplitude

Besides the access to the disorder-averaged prediction of
T_{c}
, our approach additionally allows limited access to the inhomogeneity structure of the Gor’kov function
\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
.
In particular, we consider the statistics of the spatial fluctuations of
\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
, which is defined as
\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})=\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})-\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n})
.
The spatial fluctuation
\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
obeys

\Big(-D\nabla^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|\Big)\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})-2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum_{\varepsilon_{m}}\left[\langle\bar{D}\rangle(\varepsilon_{n}-\varepsilon_{m})+\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon_{n},\varepsilon_{m})\right]\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r})=2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum_{\varepsilon_{m}}\delta\bar{D}(\varepsilon_{n}-\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r})\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon_{m}).

(50)

Eq. (
50
) is a result of linearization of Eq. (
29
) in

\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
and
\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{r})
under the assumption of

n(\mathcal{E})=\mathrm{const}
.
The expressions for
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\omega)
and
\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon_{n},\varepsilon_{m})
remain unchanged and are given by Eqs. (
32
) and (
13
) correspondingly.

Eq. (
50
) can be solved by approximating
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n}-i\varepsilon_{m})\approx\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\max(\varepsilon_{n},\varepsilon_{m}))
in the limit of
T_{c}\ll\Lambda
.
We are interested in the solution
\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{q})=\int d^{2}\mathbf{r}\,e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})
at finite momentum
\mathbf{q}
, such that
D\mathbf{q}^{2}\gtrsim\pi T_{c}
.
Such momenta correspond to microscopic gap fluctuations that cannot be reduced to an adiabatic in-space change in the system’s parameters.
Neglecting the effects of interaction in Eq. (
50
) allows to write its solution as

\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{q})\approx\frac{2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum_{\varepsilon_{m}}\delta\bar{D}(i\varepsilon_{n}-i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{q})\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon_{m})}{D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|}.

(51)

The effects of the pairing
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(\varepsilon_{n}-\varepsilon_{m})
and
\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon_{n},\varepsilon_{m})
are to be discarded in this case, since they result in corrections to
\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
sub-leading in
\bar{g}^{2}

(see Appendix
H
for algebraic details).

The strength of the inhomogeneity of the pairing density is characterized by
\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(\omega=0,\mathbf{q})=(2\pi)^{-1}\int d\varepsilon\,\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{q})
.
In particular, we evaluate the static structure factor
\langle\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(\mathbf{r})\,\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\rangle/\langle\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle^{2}
of the pair density fluctuations.
The quantity can be evaluated from Eq. (
51
), given that statistics of
\langle\delta\bar{D}\delta\bar{D}\rangle
and the spatially averaged Gor’kov function
\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon)
were given in Eqs. (
42
) and Eq. (
45
) correspondingly.
The resulting expression in momentum space,
{\langle\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\,\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle(\mathbf{q})=\int d^{2}\mathbf{r}\,e^{-i\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{q}}\langle\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(\mathbf{r})\,\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(0)\rangle}
, takes a form (see Appendix
H
for algebraic details):

\frac{\langle\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\,\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle(\mathbf{q})}{\langle\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle^{2}}\simeq\frac{32\pi^{4}\vartheta}{3\mathbf{q}^{2}}\frac{\ln\left(\frac{D\mathbf{q}^{2}}{\pi T_{c}}\right)}{\left(1+\mathbf{q}^{2}\xi^{2}/4\right)^{3}},\quad D\mathbf{q}^{2}\gtrsim\pi T_{c}.

(52)

Divergence of
\langle\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\,\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle
at small momenta
\mathbf{q}
implies that the spatial fluctuations of the pair amplitude grow at large distances.
The fluctuations grows comparable to
\langle\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle^{2}
at the length scale
|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|\sim\xi_{\mathrm{SC}}
,
which is estimated by

VII
Single-Puddle Approximation

Sec.
V
demonstrated numerically the presence of a large number of localized superconducting puddles.
We explore the structure of localized solutions analytically by considering a single-puddle model.
In particular, we consider a pairing problem induced by a single strongly localized bosonic state
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
with center at
\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}
, localization length
\xi_{\alpha}

e_{\alpha}

We assume that nearby bosonic states have localization lengths much larger than
\xi_{\alpha}

Each localized boson
\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{r})

\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})

Since

In our model we assume that
|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}=\Theta(\xi_{\alpha}-|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}|)/\pi\xi_{\alpha}^{2}

\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})

\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})

\phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{r})
in the vicinity of
\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}

Then,
\bar{\Sigma}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})=\begin{cases}\frac{\bar{g}^{2}}{\pi^{2}\xi^{2}_{\alpha}c_{d}}\log\left(\frac{e_{\alpha}+c_{d}|\varepsilon_{n}|}{e_{\alpha}+\pi c_{d}T}\right),\quad|\mathbf{r}|\leq\xi_{\alpha},\\
0,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,|\mathbf{r}|>\xi_{\alpha},\end{cases}\quad\quad\quad\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\pi\xi^{2}_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{c_{d}|\omega_{m}|+e_{\alpha}},\quad|\mathbf{r}|\leq\xi_{\alpha},\\
n\log\left(\frac{\Lambda}{|\omega_{m}|}\right),\quad\;|\mathbf{r}|>\xi_{\alpha}.\end{cases}

(55)

Above, we assumed that
\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}=0

The pairing for

.

First, we seek to simplify Eq. (

Justification for this step for

Therefore, an additional contribution is present in the right side of Eq. (

(56)

Substitution of Eq. (
56
) into Eq. (

56

\Big(-D\nabla^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|\Big)\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})-2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum\limits_{\varepsilon_{m}}\bar{D}(i\max(\varepsilon_{n},\varepsilon_{m}),\mathbf{r})\Phi(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r})=0.

(57)

We seek a non-trivial solution
\Phi(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})
of Eq. (
57
) with the highest superconducting transition temperature
T_{c,\alpha}>T_{c}
.
This solution is expected to be exponentially localized, thus requiring solving for

The problem defined by Eq. (

The only length scale in the problem that explicitly appears in Eq. (

.
Therefore, it is natural to assume that

Behavior of

and

The first scenario to be considered is when

The second conclusion concerns the high-temperature behavior of the distribution of superconducting puddles.

correspond to the localized bosons with the smallest

is distributed homogeneously at smallest values, it follows that

(67)

until a microscopic cutoff defined by the smallest

The power law behavior in Eqs. (

Dodaro and Kivelson 2018
;
Bulaevskii et al. 1987
;
Ioffe and Larkin 1981
.

VIII
Discussion and outlook

In this work we have shown that disorder affects quantum-critical superconductivity in a way that is qualitatively distinct from its role in conventional BCS theory. The central reason is that the disorder couples directly to the local distance from the quantum critical point (so-called “Harris disorder”), and non-perturbatively reshapes the spectrum and wave functions of the bosonic modes that both mediate pairing and produce non-Fermi-liquid behavior in the normal state. In the regime considered here, the fermionic states remain extended and diffusive, while the low-energy bosonic modes become spatially localized
Patel et al. 2025
;
Patel et al. 2024
. The superconducting problem is consequently controlled by Cooper-pair diffusion through a highly inhomogeneous, strongly overdamped pairing glue. The main results supporting this picture are summarized in Sec.
III
.

This pairing mechanism produces a broad hierarchy of pairing scales. The most localized low-energy bosonic modes act as compact pairing centers and nucleate superconducting puddles at temperatures above the onset of an extended pairing eigenstate. These puddles are not the conventional rare large regions of enhanced BCS attraction. Rather, they are tied to localized overdamped collective modes, and their pairing scales are controlled by the competition between the singular local glue and the inverse proximity effect from the surrounding metal. This leads to a power-law distribution of local pairing scales, in contrast to the stretched-exponential Lifshitz tails expected in weakly disordered BCS superconductors. At lower temperatures, the pairing eigenstate becomes extended, but the localized structure of the bosonic glue continues to affect the transition through the mesoscopic correlation effects and enhanced return probability of Cooper pairs to favorable regions.

Several assumptions underlie this description. We have focused on pairing from bosonic modes with a localization length that is large compared to the single-particle mean free path. In this regime, the boson-induced pairing vertex and self-energy act as long-wavelength inhomogeneities for diffusive Cooper pairs. Relaxing this restriction would allow us to include effective short-wavelength scattering processes that can act as conventional pair breakers, especially for sign-changing order parameters. We have also assumed a regime in which the electronic states remain metallic on the length scales relevant for pairing, while the bosonic modes are already localized. Finally, the transition studied here is the linearized pairing instability. In two dimensions, the ultimate thermodynamic transition into a phase-coherent superconducting state will also depend on phase fluctuations and Josephson coupling between puddles. Thus, the extended zero mode of the pairing problem should be viewed as a mean-field criterion for macroscopic pairing, while the true transition temperature may be reduced by phase-ordering physics.

The broader implication of our results is that strong superconducting inhomogeneity does not require localized electrons or microscopic granularity. It can arise naturally in a metallic quantum-critical system from the localization of the collective modes responsible for pairing. This provides a route by which strange-metal behavior, broad gap distributions, and superconducting puddles can emerge from a common origin: Harris disorder acting on the quantum-critical bosonic pairing glue.

The evolution of the inhomogeneous superconducting states that we find is broadly consistent with STM observations of temperature-dependent gap inhomogeneity in the cuprates
Gomes et al. 2007
;
Pasupathy et al. 2008
. However, the existing data is too sparse to discern the high-temperature tails of these distributions and confirm our prediction. We hope that future experimental works will revisit this important problem of high-temperature gap inhomogeneity in the cuprates with STM.

It is also useful to compare our results with recent theoretical work on superconducting puddles in the overdoped cuprates
Eliashberg 1987
;
Bashan et al. 2024
;
Tulipman et al. 2024
;
Bashan et al. 2026
. Those works postulate a set of “two-level systems” that provide local Andreev scattering centers for itinerant electrons. The closest analog in our theory is the solitary-puddle regime discussed in Sec.
VII
, where pairing is nucleated by an exponentially localized
\phi
mode. The important difference is that in our case the puddles are not introduced phenomenologically; they emerge from the generic effect of Harris disorder on a quantum-critical metal.

The theory developed here suggests several directions for future work, aside from simply relaxing the assumptions about length scales that we used. One important extension is to derive an effective phase-only theory for the superconducting puddles and calculate the resulting superfluid stiffness. This would clarify the relation between the local pairing scale, the onset of an extended pairing eigenstate, and the eventual phase-ordering transition. Another important direction is to treat the bosonic localization problem and the superconducting gap equation fully self-consistently, allowing the pairing gap to feed back on the Landau damping by depleting the low-energy fermionic spectral weight.

Appendix A
Contribution of localized bosonic modes into electronic self-energy

The inhomogeneous Dyson equation for the electronic self-energy can be derived using the Wigner transform expansion technique.
First, we consider the bosonic Green’s function expanded in localized eigenmodes
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
with eigenvalues
e_{\alpha}
.
Such an ansatz phenomenologically accounts for bosonic localization in the presence of electrons:

D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})\phi^{*}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{\omega_{m}^{2}+c_{d}|\omega_{m}|+e_{\alpha}}.

(69)

We assume that each mode consists of a smooth envelope
|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|
are localized at
\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}
with a localization length
\xi_{\alpha}
, while its short-distance phase correlations decay at the length scale
l_{\rm corr}
.
We model the disorder-averaged short-distance phase correlations by

\langle e^{i[\theta_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})-\theta_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})]}\rangle\sim e^{-|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|/2l_{\rm corr}}
, which gives

D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})||\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})|e^{-|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|/2l_{\rm corr}}}{\omega_{m}^{2}+c_{d}|\omega_{m}|+e_{\alpha}}.

(70)

It is convenient to redefine the bosonic Green’s function as a function of the center of mass
\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}^{\prime})/2
and the difference
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
:

D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{y}/2)\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{y}/2)e^{-|\mathbf{y}|/2l_{\rm corr}}}{\omega_{m}^{2}+c_{d}|\omega_{m}|+e_{\alpha}}.

(71)

Above and in the following, we absorb the absolute value of
\phi_{\alpha}
into the definition of the envelope for convenience.
If
l_{\rm corr}\ll\xi_{\alpha}
is assumed, the contribution of
\mathbf{y}
into the argument of
\phi_{\alpha}
can be neglected.
This is equivalent to neglecting the corrections of the order of
(l_{\rm corr}/\xi_{\alpha})^{2}
.
After performing a Wigner transform over the coordinate
\mathbf{y}
, one obtains

D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{q})=\int d^{2}y\,e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{y}}D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{8\pi l^{2}_{\mathrm{corr}}\phi^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{R})f(\mathbf{q})}{\omega_{m}^{2}+c_{d}|\omega_{m}|+e_{\alpha}},

(72)

where

Assuming that

f(\mathbf{q})\approx 1

As a result, the bosonic Green’s function is local after coarse-graining over length scales large compared with

Hence we define

D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\approx 8\pi l^{2}_{\mathrm{corr}}\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})

\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\phi_{\alpha}^{2}(\mathbf{r})}{\omega_{m}^{2}+c_{d}|\omega_{m}|+e_{\alpha}}.

(73)

We proceed by considering the Dyson equation for the electron Green’s function.
We assume that the electron self-energy contribution due to the chemical potential disorder has already been self-consistently accounted for in a “bare” electron Green’s function

G_{0}(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{i\varepsilon-E(\mathbf{k})+i\Gamma\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\varepsilon},

(74)

where
\Gamma=1/2\tau
is the elastic scattering rate.
The Dyson equation for the electron position-dependent Green’s function

\left[G_{0}^{-1}(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{k})-\Sigma(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})\right]\circ G(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})=1,

(75)

where
A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})\circ B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})\equiv A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})e^{\frac{i}{2}(\overleftarrow{\partial}_{\mathbf{x}}\overrightarrow{\partial}_{\mathbf{p}}-\overleftarrow{\partial}_{\mathbf{p}}\overrightarrow{\partial}_{\mathbf{x}})}B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})

Kamenev 2011

Kryhin et al. 2025

In the Dyson equation given by Eq. (
75

\Sigma(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})

\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})=g^{2}T\sum_{\omega_{m}}\int\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}G(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q})D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{q}).

(76)

To access the effects of spatial inhomogeneity on

and
\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})

75

Assume that the electronic Green’s function can be approximately expressed as

G(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{i\varepsilon_{n}-E(\mathbf{k})+i\Gamma\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\varepsilon_{n}-\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})-\delta\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})}.

(77)

The additional term
\delta\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})
is a small correction to
\Sigma
due to spatial inhomogeneity terms arising from the Moyal product.
Indeed,
G(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})
for
\delta\Sigma=0
not an exact Moyal inverse of

One can compute the correction
\delta\Sigma

\partial_{\mathbf{r}}
and
\partial_{\mathbf{k}}

The self-energy correction at energies close to the Fermi surface takes the form

\delta\Sigma\sim\frac{(\mathbf{v}_{F}\partial_{\mathbf{r}})^{2}}{\Gamma^{2}}\Sigma+\frac{\partial_{k_{i}}\partial_{k_{j}}E(\mathbf{k})}{\Gamma}\partial_{r_{i}}\partial_{r_{j}}\Sigma.

(78)

The expression above is a non-singular small correction when
\xi_{\alpha}\gg v_{F}/\Gamma=2l

l
is an electron mean free path.
Note that the leading correction to
G(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})

\Sigma^{\prime}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\equiv\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})+\delta\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})

With the form of the Green’s function confirmed to be approximately described by

G(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{i\varepsilon_{n}-\xi(\mathbf{k})+i\Gamma\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\varepsilon_{n}-\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})},

(79)

one can compute the position-dependent self-energy of the Green’s function.
Substituting Eq. (
79

\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})=-i\bar{g}^{2}T\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{m}\frac{\phi_{\alpha}^{2}(\mathbf{r})\,\mathrm{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{n}+\omega_{m})}{\omega_{m}^{2}+c_{d}|\omega_{m}|+e_{\alpha}}=-2i\bar{g}^{2}T\,\mathrm{sgn}\,(\varepsilon_{n})\sum_{\alpha}\left[\sum_{m=1}^{|n|-\Theta(-n-1/2)}\frac{\phi_{\alpha}^{2}(\mathbf{r})}{\omega_{m}^{2}+c_{d}|\omega_{m}|+e_{\alpha}}+\frac{\phi_{\alpha}^{2}(\mathbf{r})}{2e_{\alpha}}\right],

where
\bar{g}^{2}=8\pi^{2}l^{2}_{\mathrm{corr}}\nu g^{2}
and
\nu=k_{F}/2\pi v_{F}

Note that
\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})=\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{k})
is approximately independent of momentum
\mathbf{k}
, which immediately implies that the electron self-energy
\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
is local in real space at the scales greater than
k_{F}^{-1}
.
Therefore, one can define

\Sigma(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\approx-i\,\mathrm{sgn}(\varepsilon_{n})\,\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

(81)

which reproduces the Eq. (

In the overdamped regime, where the relevant Matsubara frequencies satisfy

so that the
\omega_{m}^{2}

\omega_{m}
in Eq. (

\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})=\frac{\bar{g}^{2}}{\pi c_{d}}\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}^{2}(\mathbf{r})\left[\psi\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{|\varepsilon_{n}|}{2\pi T}+\frac{e_{\alpha}}{2\pi c_{d}T}\right)-\psi\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{e_{\alpha}}{2\pi c_{d}T}\right)+\frac{\pi c_{d}T}{e_{\alpha}}\right],

(82)

which at

\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})=\frac{\bar{g}^{2}}{\pi c_{d}}\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}^{2}(\mathbf{r})\log\left(\frac{c_{d}|\varepsilon|+e_{\alpha}}{e_{\alpha}}\right).

(83)

Appendix B
Derivation of the linearized Usadel equation

We now derive the linearized gap equation using the coarse-grained boson propagator and the electronic Green’s function obtained in Appendix
A
.
We begin by evaluating the Cooperon propagator
\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
, given in Fig.
3
, which is a central quantity to the theory of disordered superconductivity
Abrikosov et al. 2012
;
Levtov and Shytov 2003
.
Here “bare” means that the Cooperon includes potential-disorder ladders and the local electronic self-energy, but not the boson-mediated pairing interaction.
The “bare” Cooperon propagator satisfies the Bethe-Salpeter equation described by the series in Fig.
3
, which can be written explicitly as

\left(2\pi\nu\tau^{2}\right)^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon,i\omega,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\left(2\pi\nu\tau\right)^{-2}G\left(i\varepsilon+i\frac{\omega}{2},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right)G\left(-i\varepsilon+i\frac{\omega}{2},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\right)+\\
+\left(2\pi\nu\tau\right)^{-1}\int d^{2}\mathbf{x}\;G\left(i\varepsilon+i\frac{\omega}{2},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{x}\right)G\left(-i\varepsilon+i\frac{\omega}{2},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{x}\right)\left(2\pi\nu\tau^{2}\right)^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon,i\omega,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(84)

The “bare” Cooperon depends on two spatial coordinates, so the Wigner transform in the real space can be constructed ordinarily.
After applying the Wigner transform, the Bethe-Salpeter equation reduces to

\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon,i\omega,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p})=\tau K_{C}(i\varepsilon,i\omega,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p})+K_{C}(i\varepsilon,i\omega,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p})\circ\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon,i\omega,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p}),

(85)

where

K_{C}(i\varepsilon,i\omega,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p})=\left(2\pi\nu\tau\right)^{-1}\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{w}}{(2\pi)^{2}}G\left(i\varepsilon+i\frac{\omega}{2},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{w}\right)G\left(-i\varepsilon+i\frac{\omega}{2},\mathbf{r},-\mathbf{w}\right).

(86)

Therefore, the equation for the “bare” Cooperon propagator can be expressed as

\tau^{-1}K_{C}^{-1}\circ(1-K_{C})\circ\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon,i\omega,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p})=1,

(87)

where
K_{C}^{-1}
denotes the Moyal inverse of
K_{C}
.
Note that Eq. (
87
) reduces to the corresponding homogeneous expression with ordinary multiplication instead of the Moyal product, if the system is assumed to be translation invariant
Levtov and Shytov 2003
.
Within the usual Fermi-surface approximation, the integral in
K_{C}
can be evaluated analytically by substituting Eq. (
79
) into Eq. (
86
).
This leads to

K_{C}(i\varepsilon,i\omega,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p})=\frac{\theta\left(\varepsilon^{2}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{4}\right)}{\sqrt{\frac{v_{F}^{2}p^{2}}{4\Gamma^{2}}+\left(1+\frac{|\varepsilon|}{\Gamma}+\frac{|\bar{\Sigma}(\varepsilon+\omega/2,\mathbf{r})+\bar{\Sigma}(-\varepsilon+\omega/2,\mathbf{r})|}{2\Gamma}\right)^{2}}},

(88)

where
\bar{\Sigma}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})
is a local electron self-energy defined in Eq. (
82
).
At
\omega=0
and low momentum
pv_{F}\ll\Gamma
, one can expand
K_{C}
near
K_{C}=1
:

1-K_{C}\approx\frac{\frac{|\varepsilon|}{\Gamma}+\frac{\bar{\Sigma}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})}{\Gamma}}{1+\frac{|\varepsilon|}{\Gamma}+\frac{\bar{\Sigma}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})}{\Gamma}}+\frac{p^{2}v_{F}^{2}/8\Gamma^{2}}{\left(1+\frac{|\varepsilon|}{\Gamma}+\frac{\bar{\Sigma}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})}{\Gamma}\right)^{3}}.

(89)

For
|\varepsilon|,\bar{\Sigma}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})\ll\Gamma
, Eq. (
89
) further simplifies to

1-K_{C}\approx\frac{|\varepsilon|}{\Gamma}+\frac{\bar{\Sigma}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})}{\Gamma}+\frac{p^{2}v_{F}^{2}}{8\Gamma^{2}}.

(90)

The inverse of
K_{C}
under the Moyal transform can be written as

K_{C}^{-1}(i\varepsilon,i\omega,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{p})=\theta\left(\varepsilon^{2}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{4}\right)\sqrt{\frac{v_{F}^{2}p^{2}}{4\Gamma^{2}}+\left(1+\frac{|\varepsilon|}{\Gamma}+\frac{|\bar{\Sigma}(\varepsilon+\omega/2,\mathbf{r})+\bar{\Sigma}(-\varepsilon+\omega/2,\mathbf{r})|}{2\Gamma}\right)^{2}}+\delta K_{C}^{(-1)},

(91)

where the correction
\delta K_{C}^{(-1)}
can be estimated in the leading order as

\delta K_{C}^{(-1)}\approx\frac{v_{F}^{2}}{64\Gamma^{2}}K_{C}^{3}\partial_{\mathbf{r}}^{2}\bar{\Sigma}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{r}).

(92)

Since these corrections are parametrically of order
(l/\xi_{\alpha})^{2}
, they are negligible under the assumed hierarchy
l\ll\xi_{\alpha}
.
The approximate Cooperon equation in real space can now be recovered by applying an inverse Wigner transform to Eq. (
87
) with
K_{C}
approximated by Eq. (
89
).
For small pair momentum and arbitrary
|\varepsilon|+\bar{\Sigma}
within the diffusive approximation, the “bare” Cooperon equation takes a form

\left[-\frac{v_{F}^{2}\tau}{2}\frac{1}{1+2\tau|\varepsilon|+2\tau\bar{\Sigma}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})}\partial_{\mathbf{r}}^{2}+2|\varepsilon|+2\,\bar{\Sigma}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})\right]\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(93)

When
2\tau(|\varepsilon|+\bar{\Sigma})\ll 1
, the expression above simplifies to

\left(-D\partial_{\mathbf{r}}^{2}+2|\varepsilon|+2\,\bar{\Sigma}(\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})\right)\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

(94)

where
D=v_{F}^{2}\tau/2
is the electronic diffusion constant.
Eq. (
94
) is the “bare” Cooperon equation used in the main text.

We proceed to evaluate the equation that governs the pairing vertex
\Delta(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
, as defined by the diagrammatic series of Fig.
3
.
We consider the response of the system to a bare pairing vertex
\Delta_{0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\Delta_{0}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
.
The dressed vertex
\Delta(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
defined by the diagrammatic series in Fig.
3
satisfies a Bethe-Salpeter equation of the form

\Delta(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\Delta_{0}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})+g^{2}T\sum_{\omega_{m}}\int d^{2}\mathbf{x}\,\Delta(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})(2\pi\nu\tau)K_{C}(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},0,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r})(8\pi l^{2}_{\mathrm{corr}})\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})\\
+g^{2}T\sum_{\omega_{m}}\int d^{2}\mathbf{x}\,d^{2}\mathbf{y}\,\Delta(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})(2\pi\nu\tau)K_{C}(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},0,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r})K_{C}(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},0,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{r})(8\pi l^{2}_{\mathrm{corr}})\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})\\
+g^{2}T\sum_{\omega_{m}}\int d^{2}x\,d^{2}x^{\prime}\,d^{2}y\,\Delta(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},\mathbf{x})(2\pi\nu\tau)^{2}K_{C}(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},0,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\times\\
\times(2\pi\nu\tau^{2})^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y})K_{C}(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},0,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{r})(8\pi l^{2}_{\mathrm{corr}})\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r}).

(95)

Summing the impurity ladder using Eq. (
85
) gives

\Delta(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\Delta_{0}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})+16\pi^{2}l^{2}_{\mathrm{corr}}\nu g^{2}T\sum_{\omega_{m}}\int d^{2}x\,\Delta(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r})\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r}).

(96)

With the substitution of
\bar{g}^{2}=8\pi^{2}l^{2}_{\mathrm{corr}}\nu g^{2}
into the equation above, the pairing vertex equation is

\Delta(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\Delta_{0}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})+2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum_{\omega_{m}}\int d^{2}x\,\Delta(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\mathcal{C}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r})\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r}),

(97)

which is the pairing-vertex equation used in the main text.

Appendix C
Large-
N
Yukawa-SYK realization of the model

In this section, we extend our model in Eq. (
1
) to a large-
N

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\bar{\psi}_{j\sigma}(\partial_{\tau}+\xi(i\nabla))\psi_{j\sigma}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}v_{ij}(\mathbf{r})\bar{\psi}_{i\sigma}\psi_{j\sigma}\;

(98)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[(\partial_{\tau}\phi_{l})^{2}+c^{2}(\nabla\phi_{l})^{2}+(\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}))\phi_{l}^{2}\right]+\frac{u}{4N}(\phi_{l}^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle=g_{ijl}(\mathbf{r})\phi_{l}\bar{\psi}_{i}\psi_{j}

where
i,j,l=1,..,N
, and
g_{ijl}(\mathbf{r})
is a random coupling with zero mean and variance
\overline{g_{ijl}(\mathbf{r})g_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}l^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}=g^{2}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\delta_{ii^{\prime}}\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\delta_{ll^{\prime}}
. In the large-
N
limit, the normal state saddle point equations read as

\displaystyle G^{-1}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Big(i\varepsilon_{n}-\xi(i\nabla)+\frac{i}{2\tau}\operatorname{sgn}(\varepsilon_{n})+i\operatorname{sgn}(\varepsilon_{n})\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\Big)\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})

(99)

\displaystyle D^{-1}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Big(\omega_{n}^{2}-c^{2}\nabla^{2}+\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}(\mathbf{r})-\bar{\Pi}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\Big)\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\bar{\Pi}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=uT\sum\limits_{m}D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})-g^{2}T\sum\limits_{m}G(i\omega_{n}+i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})G(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})\;,

\displaystyle\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=g^{2}T\sum\limits_{m}G(i\omega_{m}+i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})D(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})

Here

(
D
) is the fermionic (bosonic) Green’s function. The equations in (
99
) are assumed to be exact in a given realization of the random mass
\lambda(\mathbf{r})
, thus capturing certain disorder-induced effects (such as localization of bosonic modes) in a non-perturbative manner. Meanwhile, the averaging over regular potential disorder for fermions has already been carried out exactly due to the particular flavor structure of the random potential
v_{ij}(\mathbf{r})
. In particular, the disorder-induced vertex corrections are

\bar{\Pi}

4

25
), and Eq. (
26

Appendix D
Self-consistent Born approximation for the gap equation

We analyze Eq. (
29
) by expanding around the disorder-averaged problem and retaining disorder correlations to second order self-consistently.
After separating
\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})
and
\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})
into disorder-averaged and fluctuating parts, the gap equation can be written as

\Big(-D\nabla^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|+2\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n})+2\delta\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\Big)\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\\
=2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum\limits_{\varepsilon_{m}}\Big(\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{m}-i\varepsilon_{n})+\delta\bar{D}(i\varepsilon_{m}-i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\Big)\Phi(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})+\Delta_{0}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(100)

In the equation above,
\langle\cdots\rangle
denotes disorder-averaged quantities and
\delta
denotes a fluctuation, so
\langle\delta\bar{\Sigma}\rangle=\langle\delta\bar{D}\rangle=0
.
If the mixed averages
\langle\delta\bar{\Sigma}\,\Phi\rangle
and
\langle\delta\bar{D}\,\Phi\rangle
are neglected, the averaged equation reduces to Eq. (
33
).
To account for the spatial correlations between
\delta\bar{\Sigma}
,
\delta\bar{D}

\Phi

100

One can formally express the solution of Eq. (
100

+2\bar{g}^{2}T\int d^{2}\mathbf{x}\;\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{x})\sum_{\varepsilon_{m}}\Big(\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n}-i\varepsilon_{m})+\delta\bar{D}(i\varepsilon_{m}-i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{x})\Big)\Phi(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

(101)

where
\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})

(105)

\displaystyle\left.\right.\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-2\int d^{2}\mathbf{r}\,\langle\delta\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},0)\rangle\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r}),

(106)

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon_{n},\varepsilon_{n}+\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\neq 0\\
l\neq m\end{subarray}}\int d^{2}\mathbf{r}\,\langle\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{m}-i\omega_{l},\mathbf{r})\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{l},0)\rangle\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n}+i\omega_{m}-i\omega_{l},\mathbf{r}).

(107)

Equation (
104
) is the disorder-averaged gap equation that includes second-order corrections from spatial fluctuations.
Neglecting
\bar{K}_{\Sigma\Sigma}
and
\bar{K}_{\Sigma D}
, which are subleading in the regime considered in the main text, gives Eq. (
40
).

Appendix E
Estimates of disorder corrections to the Usadel equation

To determine how spatial fluctuations affect the superconducting transition temperature
T_{c}
, we estimate the kernels
\bar{K}_{\Sigma\Sigma}
,
\bar{K}_{\Sigma D}
, and
\bar{K}_{DD}
.
We assume that the spatial structure of the wavefunctions is
|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{\xi_{\alpha}}e^{-|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}|/\xi_{\alpha}}
.
A useful quantity to describe the spatial fluctuations of
|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}
is

P_{2,2}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\equiv\left\langle\sum_{\alpha}|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})|^{2}|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})|^{2}\,\delta(\mathcal{E}-e_{\alpha})\right\rangle=n(\mathcal{E})\int d^{2}\mathbf{x}\,\phi^{2}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{x})\phi^{2}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})=\frac{n(\mathcal{E})r^{2}}{\pi\xi^{4}}K_{2}\left(\frac{2r}{\xi}\right),

(108)

where
r=|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|
,
\xi=\xi(\mathcal{E})
and the function
\phi(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{r})
is given by
\phi(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{r})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{\xi}e^{-|\mathbf{r}|/\xi}
.
In writing
P_{2,2}
, we retain only the same-mode contributions: the disconnected part is removed by the definition of the fluctuations.
With the use of Eq. (
108
), the correlation functions of
\delta\bar{\Sigma}
and
\delta\bar{D}
can be written as:

\displaystyle\langle\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{r})\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=B(\omega_{n},\omega_{m},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

(109)

\displaystyle\langle\delta\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\delta\bar{D}(i\omega_{m},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum_{a=1}^{|n|-\Theta(-n-1/2)}B(\omega_{a},\omega_{m},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

(110)

\displaystyle\langle\delta\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\delta\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=4\bar{g}^{4}T^{2}\sum_{a=1}^{|n|-\Theta(-n-1/2)}\sum_{b=1}^{|m|-\Theta(-m-1/2)}B(\omega_{a},\omega_{b},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

(111)

where the kernel
B(\omega_{n},\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})
has a form

B(\omega_{n},\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})=\int d\mathcal{E}\,P_{2,2}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{r})\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{n}^{2}+c_{d}|\omega_{n}|+\mathcal{E}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{m}^{2}+c_{d}|\omega_{m}|+\mathcal{E}}\right).

(112)

The kernel
B(\omega_{n},\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})
is most conveniently evaluated in momentum space.
In the estimates below, we work in the overdamped regime, where

\omega^{2}\ll c_{d}|\omega|
for the relevant frequencies.
As a result, application of Parseval’s identity to Eqs. (
105
)-(
107
) allows us to express
\bar{K}_{\Sigma\Sigma}
,
\bar{K}_{\Sigma D}
, and
\bar{K}_{DD}
through
B(\omega_{n},\omega_{m},\mathbf{q})=\int d^{2}\mathbf{r}\,e^{-i\mathbf{k}\mathbf{r}}B(\omega_{n},\omega_{m},\mathbf{r})
:

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon_{n},\varepsilon_{n}+\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=2\bar{g}^{2}T\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\neq 0\\
l\neq m\end{subarray}}\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon_{n}+\omega_{m}-\omega_{l},\omega_{m}-\omega_{l},\omega_{l}),

(113)

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{\Sigma D}(\varepsilon_{n},\omega_{m})

\displaystyle=-4\bar{g}^{2}T\sum_{a=1}^{|n+m|-\Theta(-n-m-1/2)}\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon_{n}+\omega_{m},\omega_{m},\omega_{a})-4\bar{g}^{2}T\sum_{a=1}^{|n|-\Theta(-n-1/2)}\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon_{n},\omega_{a},\omega_{m}),

(114)

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{\Sigma\Sigma}(\varepsilon_{n})

\displaystyle=8\bar{g}^{4}T^{2}\sum_{a=1}^{|n|-\Theta(-n-1/2)}\sum_{b=1}^{|n|-\Theta(-n-1/2)}\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon_{n},\omega_{a},\omega_{b}),

(115)

where the kernel
\mathcal{K}
is given by

\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon_{n},\omega_{m},\omega_{l})=\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{q}}{(2\pi)^{2}}B(\omega_{m},\omega_{l},\mathbf{q})\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{q}).

(116)

The correlation function
P_{2,2}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{q})
in momentum space has a form
P_{2,2}(\mathcal{E},\mathbf{q})=n(\mathcal{E})(1+\mathbf{q}^{2}\xi^{2}/4)^{-3}
, where
{n(\mathcal{E})=n\mathcal{E}^{\alpha_{G}}}
.
The expression for the bare Cooperon propagator in momentum space is
\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{q})=[D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|+2\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon_{n})]^{-1}
.
For the analytic estimates, we approximate
\xi(\mathcal{E})
by a typical value
\xi=\xi(c_{d}T)
in the relevant low-energy window.
Substitution of Eq. (
112
) into Eq. (
116
) results in

\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon_{n},\omega_{m},\omega_{l})\approx\frac{n}{4\pi c_{d}D}\frac{\pi c_{d}^{\alpha_{G}}}{\sin\pi\alpha_{G}}\frac{|\omega_{m}|^{\alpha_{G}}-|\omega_{l}|^{\alpha_{G}}}{|\omega_{m}|-|\omega_{l}|}R(\varepsilon_{n}),\quad R(\varepsilon_{n})\approx\begin{cases}\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{|\varepsilon_{n}|+\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(\varepsilon_{n})}\right),\quad E_{\mathrm{Th}}\gtrsim|\varepsilon_{n}|+\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(\varepsilon_{n}),\\
\frac{1}{2}\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{|\varepsilon_{n}|+\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(\varepsilon_{n})},\quad\quad\;\;\;E_{\mathrm{Th}}\lesssim|\varepsilon_{n}|+\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(\varepsilon_{n}),\end{cases}

(117)

where
E_{\mathrm{Th}}=2D/\xi^{2}
and
-1<\alpha_{G}\leq 0
.
Eq. (
117
) along with Eqs. (
113
)-(
115
) fully describes the impact of inhomogeneity correlations on the linearized gap equations in Eq. (
104
).

We now estimate the magnitudes of
\bar{K}_{\Sigma\Sigma}
,
\bar{K}_{\Sigma D}
and
\bar{K}_{DD}
.
The Euler-Maclaurin approximation, applied to
\bar{K}_{\Sigma\Sigma}
in Eq. (
115
), results in

\bar{K}_{\Sigma\Sigma}(\varepsilon)=8\bar{g}^{4}\int_{2\pi T}^{|\varepsilon|}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon,\omega,\omega^{\prime})\approx\frac{\bar{g}^{4}n}{6\pi c_{d}D}f_{\Sigma}(\alpha_{G})\begin{cases}|\varepsilon|^{1+\alpha_{G}}\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{|\varepsilon|}\right),\quad|\varepsilon|\lesssim E_{\mathrm{Th}},\\
|\varepsilon|^{\alpha_{G}}\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{2},\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\;|\varepsilon|\gtrsim E_{\mathrm{Th}},\end{cases}

(118)

where

f_{\Sigma}(\alpha_{G})=\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\frac{\pi c_{d}^{\alpha_{G}}}{\sin\pi\alpha_{G}}\frac{2+2\alpha_{G}(\psi(\alpha_{G})+\gamma)}{\alpha_{G}(1+\alpha_{G})},\quad\quad f_{\Sigma}(0)=1.

(119)

For
\alpha_{G}\neq 0
,
\bar{K}_{\Sigma\Sigma}
is a parametrically small logarithmic correction to a power-law scaling of
\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon)
that is suppressed by
\bar{g}^{2}/c_{d}D
.
For
\alpha_{G}=0
,
\bar{K}_{\Sigma\Sigma}
has the same frequency dependence as
\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon)
and therefore only renormalizes effective coupling.
In both cases, the effects of
\bar{K}_{\Sigma\Sigma}
can be neglected.

The mixed correction
\bar{K}_{\Sigma D}
is

\bar{K}_{\Sigma D}(\varepsilon,\omega)=-4\bar{g}^{2}\int_{2\pi T}^{|\varepsilon+\omega|}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon+\omega,\omega,\omega^{\prime})-4\bar{g}^{2}\int_{2\pi T}^{|\varepsilon|}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\mathcal{K}(\varepsilon,\omega^{\prime},\omega),

(120)

after the Euler-Maclaurin approximation is applied to Eq. (
114
).
The leading order behavior of
K_{\Sigma D}
can be summarized by

\bar{K}_{\Sigma D}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon)\sim-\frac{\bar{g}^{4}n}{12c_{d}D}\begin{cases}\eta^{\alpha_{G}}\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\eta}\right),\quad\quad\quad E_{\mathrm{Th}}\gtrsim\max(|\varepsilon|,|\varepsilon^{\prime}|),\\
\eta^{\alpha_{G}-1}E_{\mathrm{Th}},\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\;E_{\mathrm{Th}}\lesssim\max(|\varepsilon|,|\varepsilon^{\prime}|),\end{cases}

(121)

where
\eta=\max(|\varepsilon|,|\varepsilon^{\prime}|)
.
The effects of
\bar{K}_{\Sigma D}
are small logarithmic corrections to
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\varepsilon-i\varepsilon^{\prime})
in the case of
\alpha_{G}\neq 0
and can be absorbed into a coupling constant for
\alpha_{G}=0
.
We therefore neglect
\bar{K}_{\Sigma D}
in the main analysis.

Evaluation of
\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
requires slightly more care.
The internal Matsubara frequency sum in Eq. (
113
) has a non-trivial convolution structure.
To estimate it, we approximate the integrand as

\mathcal{K}(\omega,\omega-\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}-\omega)\simeq\frac{n}{4\pi c_{d}D}\frac{\pi c_{d}^{\alpha_{G}}}{\sin\pi\alpha_{G}}\begin{cases}\frac{|\varepsilon|^{\alpha_{G}}-|\varepsilon^{\prime}|^{\alpha_{G}}}{|\varepsilon|-|\varepsilon^{\prime}|}\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{|\omega|}\right),\quad\quad E_{\mathrm{Th}}\gtrsim|\omega|,\quad\eta\gtrsim|\omega|.\\
\frac{|\varepsilon|^{\alpha_{G}}-|\varepsilon^{\prime}|^{\alpha_{G}}}{|\varepsilon|-|\varepsilon^{\prime}|}\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{2|\omega|},\quad\quad\quad\quad\;\;E_{\mathrm{Th}}\lesssim|\omega|;\quad\eta\gtrsim|\omega|.\\
\frac{\alpha_{G}}{|\omega|^{1-\alpha_{G}}}\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{|\omega|}\right),\quad\quad\quad\;\;\;E_{\mathrm{Th}}\gtrsim|\omega|;\quad\eta\lesssim|\omega|.\\
\frac{\alpha_{G}}{|\omega|^{1-\alpha_{G}}}\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{2|\omega|},\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\;E_{\mathrm{Th}}\lesssim|\omega|;\quad\eta\lesssim|\omega|.\end{cases}

(122)

where
\eta=\max(|\varepsilon|,|\varepsilon^{\prime}|)
.
The integral kernel
\bar{K}_{DD}
in the regime
E_{\mathrm{Th}}\lesssim\eta
involves integrating options (1), (2), and (4) of Eq. (
122
), which results in

\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})=2\bar{g}^{2}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\mathcal{K}(\omega,\omega-\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}-\omega)\simeq\frac{\bar{g}^{2}n}{4\pi^{2}c_{d}D}\frac{\pi c_{d}^{\alpha_{G}}}{\sin\pi\alpha_{G}}\left[E_{\mathrm{Th}}\frac{|\varepsilon|^{\alpha_{G}}-|\varepsilon^{\prime}|^{\alpha_{G}}}{|\varepsilon|-|\varepsilon^{\prime}|}+\frac{\alpha_{G}}{1-\alpha_{G}}\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{2\eta^{1-\alpha_{G}}}\right].

(123)

Above
\omega
denotes the internal Cooperon frequency after the change of variables in the convolution.
In the regime, where
E_{\mathrm{Th}}\gtrsim\eta
, the kernel
\bar{K}_{DD}
is evaluated by integrating options (1), (3), and (4) from Eq. (
122
), which results in

\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})=2\bar{g}^{2}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\mathcal{K}(\omega,\omega-\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}-\omega)\simeq\frac{\bar{g}^{2}n}{4\pi^{2}c_{d}D}\frac{\pi c_{d}^{\alpha_{G}}}{\sin\pi\alpha_{G}}\left[2\eta\,\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\eta}\right)\frac{|\varepsilon|^{\alpha_{G}}-|\varepsilon^{\prime}|^{\alpha_{G}}}{|\varepsilon|-|\varepsilon^{\prime}|}\right.+\\
+2\left.\alpha_{G}^{-1}\left(E_{\mathrm{Th}}^{\alpha_{G}}-\eta^{\alpha_{G}}\left(1+\alpha_{G}\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\eta}\right)\right)\right)+\frac{\alpha_{G}\,E^{\alpha_{G}}_{\mathrm{Th}}}{1-\alpha_{G}}\right].

(124)

For
\alpha_{G}\neq 0
, the leading order behavior of
\bar{K}_{DD}
is

\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})\simeq\frac{\bar{g}^{2}n}{4\pi^{2}c_{d}D}\frac{\pi\alpha c_{d}^{\alpha_{G}}}{\sin\pi\alpha_{G}}\begin{cases}\eta^{\alpha_{G}}\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\eta}\right),\quad E_{\mathrm{Th}}\gtrsim\eta,\\
\eta^{\alpha_{G}-1}E_{\mathrm{Th}},\quad\quad\;\;E_{\mathrm{Th}}\lesssim\eta.\end{cases}

(125)

Value
\alpha_{G}=0
is a special point of Eqs. (
123
) and (
124
), and up to nonlogarithmic terms and order-one constants produces
\bar{K}_{DD}
of the form

\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})\simeq\frac{\bar{g}^{2}n}{4\pi^{2}c_{d}D}\begin{cases}3\ln^{2}\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\eta}\right),\quad E_{\mathrm{Th}}\gtrsim\eta\\
\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\eta},\quad E_{\mathrm{Th}}\lesssim\eta.\end{cases}

(126)

For energies
\eta\gtrsim E_{\mathrm{Th}}
, the scaling of
\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})
is subleading to
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\varepsilon-i\varepsilon^{\prime})
,
so the effects of
\bar{K}_{DD}
can be neglected at energies larger than
E_{\mathrm{Th}}
.
When
\eta\lesssim E_{\mathrm{Th}}
, the kernel is square-logarithmic and therefore dominates over
\langle\bar{D}\rangle(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime})
at lowest energies.
Therefore, this effect cannot be absorbed into
\langle\bar{D}\rangle
by redefinition of the theory parameters.
This leads to a final form of the inhomogeneity-induced correction:

\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})\simeq\frac{\vartheta n}{2}\,\theta\left(E_{\mathrm{Th}}-\eta\right)\ln^{2}\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\eta}\right),

(127)

where
\theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function,
E_{\mathrm{Th}}=2D/\xi^{2}
is the Thouless energy,
\vartheta=3\bar{g}^{2}/2\pi^{2}c_{d}D
, and
\eta=\max(|\varepsilon|,|\varepsilon^{\prime}|)
.

Appendix F
Solution to Usadel equation without spatial inhomogeneity

For
\varepsilon>0
, Eq. (
34
) in the main text becomes

\Big(\varepsilon+\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon)\Big)\Phi_{0}(i\varepsilon)=\Delta_{0}+\frac{\bar{g}^{2}}{\pi}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,\Phi_{0}(i\varepsilon^{\prime})\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})).

(128)

This integral equation can be reduced to the ordinary differential equation

\partial_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\partial_{\varepsilon}\left((\varepsilon+\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(\varepsilon))\Phi_{0}(i\varepsilon)\right)}{\partial_{\varepsilon}\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\varepsilon)}\right)=\frac{\bar{g}^{2}}{\pi}\Phi_{0}(i\varepsilon).

(129)

Substituting

F(\varepsilon)=\left(\varepsilon+\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon)\right)\Phi_{0}(i\varepsilon)
along with
\partial_{\varepsilon}\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\varepsilon)=-n/\varepsilon
into Eq. (
129
) leads to

\partial_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon\partial_{\varepsilon}F(\varepsilon))=-\tilde{g}^{2}\frac{F(\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon+\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon)},

(130)

where

\tilde{g}^{2}=\bar{g}^{2}n/\pi
is an effective dimensionless coupling constant.
It is convenient to define
x=\ln(\Lambda^{\prime}/\varepsilon)
,
\ln(\Lambda^{\prime}/\Lambda)=\tilde{g}^{-2}

leads to

\partial^{2}_{x}F=-\frac{F}{x}

(131)

with an exact solution given by

F(x)=c_{1}\sqrt{x}\,J_{1}\left(2\sqrt{x}\right)+c_{2}\sqrt{x}\,Y_{1}\left(2\sqrt{x}\right).

(132)

We substitute Eq. (
132
) into Eq. (
128
) to determine constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
.
In terms of
F(\varepsilon)
the integro-differential equation can be written as

F(\varepsilon)=\Delta_{0}+\frac{\bar{g}^{2}}{\pi}\left(\int_{\pi T}^{\varepsilon}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\frac{F(\varepsilon^{\prime})\bar{D}(i\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{\prime}+\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon^{\prime})}+\int_{\varepsilon}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\frac{F(\varepsilon^{\prime})\bar{D}(i\varepsilon^{\prime})}{\varepsilon^{\prime}+\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right).

(133)

The integrals on the right-hand side of Eq. (
133
) evaluate to

\frac{\bar{g}^{2}}{\pi}\left(\int_{\pi T}^{\varepsilon}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\frac{F(\varepsilon^{\prime})\bar{D}(i\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{\prime}+\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(\varepsilon^{\prime})}+\int_{\varepsilon}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\frac{F(\varepsilon^{\prime})\bar{D}(i\varepsilon^{\prime})}{\varepsilon^{\prime}+\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)=F(\varepsilon)-F\left(\Lambda\right)-(x_{\varepsilon}-\tilde{g}^{-2})\left[c_{1}J_{0}\left(2\sqrt{x_{T}}\right)+c_{2}Y_{0}\left(2\sqrt{x_{T}}\right)\right],

(134)

where
x_{\varepsilon}=\log(\Lambda^{\prime}/\varepsilon)
and
x_{T}=\log(\Lambda^{\prime}/\pi T)
.
Substituting Eq. (
134
) into Eq. (
133
) imposes the two boundary conditions

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}=F\left(\Lambda\right),\quad\quad 0=c_{1}J_{0}\left(2\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda^{\prime}}{\pi T}\right)}\right)+c_{2}Y_{0}\left(2\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda^{\prime}}{\pi T}\right)}\right),

(135)

which fix
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
for finite
\Delta_{0}
.
The system of linear equations in Eq. (
135
) can be solved exactly and leads to

F(\varepsilon)=\Delta_{0}\frac{y(\varepsilon)}{y\left(\Lambda\right)}\frac{Y_{0}(y(\pi T))J_{1}(y(\varepsilon))-J_{0}(y(\pi T))Y_{1}(y(\varepsilon))}{Y_{0}(y(\pi T))J_{1}\left(y\left(\Lambda\right)\right)-J_{0}(y(\pi T))Y_{1}\left(y\left(\Lambda\right)\right)},\quad\quad y(\varepsilon)=2\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda^{\prime}}{\varepsilon}\right)}.

(136)

To evaluate the static superconducting susceptibility
\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(\omega=0)
, we compute

\Phi_{\mathrm{0,stat}}(\omega=0)=\int\frac{d\varepsilon}{2\pi}\Phi_{0}(i\varepsilon)=\frac{\Delta_{0}}{2\pi\tilde{g}}\frac{Y_{0}(y(\pi T))J_{0}\left(y\left(\Lambda\right)\right)-J_{0}(y(\pi T))Y_{0}\left(y\left(\Lambda\right)\right)}{Y_{0}(y(\pi T))J_{1}\left(y\left(\Lambda\right)\right)-J_{0}(y(\pi T))Y_{1}\left(y\left(\Lambda\right)\right)},

(137)

which is the static pair density response to
\Delta_{0}
source.
For
\tilde{g}\ll 1
, using the large-argument asymptotics of the Bessel functions gives

\Phi_{\mathrm{0,stat}}(\omega=0)\approx\frac{\Delta_{0}}{2\pi\tilde{g}}\tan\left(\tilde{g}\,\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\pi T}\right)\right),\quad\quad\tilde{g}^{2}\ll 1.

(138)

Eq. (
138
) shows that the static susceptibility diverges at
T=T_{c0}
,, where

T_{c0}=\frac{\Lambda}{\pi}\exp\left[-\frac{\pi}{2\tilde{g}}\right]\gg T_{\mathrm{NFL}}\sim\frac{\Lambda}{\pi}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{\tilde{g}^{2}}\right].

(139)

For later use, it is useful to compare
T_{c}
to
T_{\mathrm{NFL}}
–
the scale at which
\langle\bar{\Sigma}\rangle(i\varepsilon)
becomes comparable to
|\varepsilon|
.
Comparing
T_{c0}
and
T_{\mathrm{NFL}}
shows that
T_{\mathrm{NFL}}\ll T_{c0}
, which implies the electronic self-energy is parametrically small at the onset of superconductivity.
Finally, it is interesting to note that neglecting electron self-energy effects in Eq. (
128
) leads to the same effect as the small
\tilde{g}
asymptotic expansion of the exact gap function in Eq. (
137
).

Appendix G
Solution of the Usadel equation with inhomogeneity corrections

In this section, we determine how the inhomogeneity corrections generated by
\delta\bar{\Sigma}
and
\delta\bar{D}
modify
T_{c}
.
The relevant gap equation has been derived in the main text as Eq. (
44
):

|\varepsilon|\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon)=2\bar{g}^{2}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}\frac{d\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2\pi}\left[\langle\bar{D}\rangle(i\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}))+\bar{K}_{DD}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})\right]\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon^{\prime})+\Delta_{0}.

(140)

As argued in Appendix
E
, the
\bar{K}_{\Sigma\Sigma}
and
\bar{K}_{\Sigma D}
terms only renormalize parameters in the regimes of interest and therefore are dropped.
Eq. (
140
) for
\varepsilon>0
can be written out explicitly as

\varepsilon\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon)=\tilde{g}^{2}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon^{\prime})+\frac{\vartheta\tilde{g}^{2}}{2}\;\Theta\left(E_{\mathrm{Th}}-\varepsilon\right)\int_{\pi T}^{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\ln^{2}\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon^{\prime})+\Delta_{0}.

(141)

The integral equation above separates into two frequency regimes:
\varepsilon\geq E_{\mathrm{Th}}
, and
\varepsilon\leq E_{\mathrm{Th}}
.
We write

\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon)=\begin{cases}\Phi_{-}(i\varepsilon),\quad\varepsilon\leq E_{\mathrm{Th}},\\
\Phi_{+}(i\varepsilon),\quad\varepsilon\geq E_{\mathrm{Th}},\end{cases}

(142)

so Eq. (
141
) is equivalent to a system of equations

\displaystyle-\partial_{\varepsilon}\left(\varepsilon\partial_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon\Phi_{+}(i\varepsilon))\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{g}^{2}\Phi_{+}(i\varepsilon),\quad\quad\varepsilon>E_{\mathrm{Th}},

(143)

\displaystyle-\partial_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\varepsilon\partial_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon\Phi_{-}(i\varepsilon))}{1+\vartheta\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\varepsilon}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{g}^{2}\Phi_{-}(i\varepsilon),\quad\quad\varepsilon<E_{\mathrm{Th}}.

(144)

The differential equations above are solved by

\displaystyle\Phi_{+}(i\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[A_{+}\cos\left(u(\varepsilon)\right)+B_{+}\sin\left(u(\varepsilon)\right)\right],\quad\quad u(\varepsilon)=\tilde{g}\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\varepsilon}\right),

(145)

\displaystyle\Phi_{-}(i\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[A_{-}\,\mathrm{Ai}^{\prime}\left(h(\varepsilon)\right)+B_{-}\,\mathrm{Bi}^{\prime}\left(h(\varepsilon)\right)\right],\quad\quad h(\varepsilon)=-\left(\frac{\tilde{g}}{\vartheta}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\left(1+\vartheta\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right),

(146)

where
\mathrm{Ai}\,(x)
and
\mathrm{Bi}\,(x)
are the Airy functions.

Coefficients
A_{\pm}
and
B_{\pm}
are fully determined from the continuity properties of
\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon)
.
At
\varepsilon=E_{\mathrm{Th}}
, Eq. (
141
) dictates

\Phi_{+}(iE_{\mathrm{Th}})=\Phi_{-}(iE_{\mathrm{Th}})
, and
\partial_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon\Phi_{+}(i\varepsilon))|_{\varepsilon=E_{\mathrm{Th}}}=\partial_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon\Phi_{-}(i\varepsilon))|_{\varepsilon=E_{\mathrm{Th}}}
.
For the eigenvalue problem with
\Delta_{0}=0
, Eq. (
141
) gives
\Phi_{+}(i\Lambda)=0
, implying
A_{+}=0
.
The continuity conditions immediately imply

\displaystyle A_{-}

\displaystyle=-\pi B_{+}\sin\left(u(E_{\mathrm{Th}})\right)\mathrm{Bi}(h(E_{\mathrm{Th}}))+\pi B_{+}\left(\frac{\vartheta}{\tilde{g}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\cos\left(u(E_{\mathrm{Th}})\right)\mathrm{Bi}^{\prime}(h(E_{\mathrm{Th}})),

(147)

\displaystyle B_{-}

\displaystyle=\pi B_{+}\sin\left(u(E_{\mathrm{Th}})\right)\mathrm{Ai}(h(E_{\mathrm{Th}}))-\pi B_{+}\left(\frac{\vartheta}{\tilde{g}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\cos\left(u(E_{\mathrm{Th}})\right)\mathrm{Ai}^{\prime}(h(E_{\mathrm{Th}})).

(148)

Substitution of Eqs. (

) –
(

:

A_{-}\,\mathrm{Ai}\,(h(\pi T_{c}))+B_{-}\,\mathrm{Bi}\,(h(\pi T_{c}))=0.

(149)

We first analyze Eq. (

) in the simplest scenario.
We examine the solution in the regime of a fixed
\tilde{g}
and
\gamma=\vartheta/\tilde{g}\lesssim 1
.
In this regime, one can perform the asymptotic expansion of the Airy functions, leading to

\displaystyle A_{-}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi}B_{+}\left(\frac{\tilde{g}}{\vartheta}\right)^{-\frac{1}{6}}\sin\left(\frac{2}{3\gamma}-\tilde{g}\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}\right)+\frac{\pi}{4}\right),

(150)

\displaystyle B_{-}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\pi}B_{+}\left(\frac{\tilde{g}}{\vartheta}\right)^{-\frac{1}{6}}\cos\left(\frac{2}{3\gamma}-\tilde{g}\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}\right)+\frac{\pi}{4}\right).

(151)

Substitution of Eqs. (
150
) and (
151
) into Eq. (
149
) and the asymptotic expansion of the Airy functions leads to

\frac{2}{3\gamma}\left[\left(1+\gamma\tilde{g}\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\pi T_{c}}\right)\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}-1\right]+\tilde{g}\ln\frac{\Lambda}{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}=\frac{\pi}{2}.

(152)

This equation can be solved with the following notation:

\mathcal{A}=\tilde{g}\ln(\Lambda/\pi T_{c})
, and
\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{Th}}=\tilde{g}\ln(\Lambda/E_{\mathrm{Th}})
, which allows to express
T_{c}
as

T_{c}=\frac{\Lambda}{\pi}\exp\left[-\frac{\mathcal{A}}{\tilde{g}}\right],\quad\mathcal{A}=\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{Th}}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\left[\left(1+\frac{3\gamma}{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{Th}}\right)\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}-1\right].

(153)

We next consider
\gamma\gtrsim 1
, which itself contains two sub-cases:
|h(\pi T_{c})|\lesssim 1
and
|h(\pi T_{c})|\gtrsim 1
.
We begin by considering the first sub-case,
|h(\pi T_{c})|\lesssim 1

h=0

\mathrm{Ai}\,(h)\approx\frac{1}{3^{\frac{2}{3}}\Gamma\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)}-\frac{h}{3^{\frac{1}{3}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)},\quad\quad\mathrm{Bi}(h)\approx\frac{1}{3^{\frac{1}{6}}\Gamma\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)}+\frac{3^{\frac{1}{6}}h}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)},

(154)

which is a good approximation as far as
|h|\lesssim 1
.
Using the approximation given in Eq. (
154

A_{-}=1-\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)\tan(\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{Th}})}{3^{\frac{1}{3}}\Gamma\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)\gamma^{\frac{1}{3}}},\quad\quad B_{-}=\frac{1}{3^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)\tan\left(\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{Th}}\right)}{3^{\frac{5}{6}}\Gamma\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)\gamma^{\frac{1}{3}}}

(155)

after dividing both coefficients by a common nonzero factor.
From Eq. (
149

154

h(\pi T_{c})
can be deduced, resulting in

h(\pi T_{c})\approx-\frac{\gamma^{\frac{1}{3}}}{\tan(\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{Th}})},\quad\quad\gamma^{\frac{1}{3}}\lesssim\tan(\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{Th}}).

(156)

The inequality in Eq. (
156
) follows from
|h(\pi T_{c})|\lesssim 1
.
Using the definition of
h(\varepsilon)
then gives

T_{c}=\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\pi}\exp\left[-\frac{\cot(\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{Th}})}{\tilde{g}}+\frac{1}{\vartheta}\right],\quad\quad 1\lesssim\left(\frac{\vartheta}{\tilde{g}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\lesssim\tan(\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{Th}}).

(157)

Proceeding to the second sub-case,
|h(\pi T_{c})|\gtrsim 1
, we use the asymptotic expansion of the Airy functions, simplifying Eq. (
149

\sqrt{3}(1-x)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}+\frac{2}{3}|h(\pi T_{c})|^{\frac{3}{2}}\right)+(1+x)\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}+\frac{2}{3}|h(\pi T_{c})|^{\frac{3}{2}}\right)=0,\quad x=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)\tan(\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{Th}})}{3^{\frac{1}{3}}\Gamma\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)\gamma^{\frac{1}{3}}}\approx 1.37\;\frac{\tan(\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{Th}})}{\gamma^{\frac{1}{3}}}.

(158)

The solution with minimal
|h(\pi T_{c})|
can be expressed as

h(\pi T_{c})=-\left[\frac{3\pi}{8}-\frac{3\alpha(x)}{2}\right]^{\frac{2}{3}},\quad\quad\alpha(x)=\tan^{-1}\left(\sqrt{3}\frac{x-1}{1+x}\right),\quad\quad x\lesssim 1.

(159)

In the range
0<x<1
the value of
h(\pi T_{c})
changes insignificantly:
h(\pi T_{c})\approx-1.12
for

and
h(\pi T_{c})\approx-1.96
for
x=0
.
Therefore, the superconducting transition temperature can be expressed as

T_{c}=\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\pi}\exp\left[-\frac{\mathcal{B}}{\vartheta^{\frac{1}{3}}\tilde{g}^{\frac{2}{3}}}+\frac{1}{\vartheta}\right],\quad\tilde{g}\lesssim\vartheta,\quad\tan(\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{Th}})\lesssim(\vartheta/\tilde{g})^{\frac{1}{3}},

(160)

where
\mathcal{B}
is an order-one constant determined by
x
; in the
x\to 0
limit,
\mathcal{B}=(7\pi/8)^{2/3}
..

Physically, Eq. (
153
) and Eq. (
157
) correspond to the same
T_{c}\sim\Lambda e^{-\#/\tilde{g}}
regime, where the numerical constant and the prefactor slowly flow with
\tilde{g}
.
Eq. (
160

T_{c}\sim E_{\mathrm{Th}}e^{-\#/\vartheta^{1/3}\tilde{g}^{2/3}}

Therefore, the crossover occurs when
\tan(\tilde{g}_{\mathrm{Th}})\sim\gamma^{\frac{1}{3}}
, which occurs for

and when

Thus, the behavior of

as a function of

can be summarized as

T_{c}\sim\begin{cases}\Lambda\exp\left[-\frac{\#}{\tilde{g}}\right],\quad\quad\quad\tilde{g}\gtrsim\tilde{g}_{*},\\
E_{\mathrm{Th}}\exp\left[-\frac{\#}{\vartheta^{1/3}\tilde{g}^{2/3}}\right],\quad\tilde{g}\lesssim\tilde{g}_{*},\end{cases}\quad\quad\tilde{g}_{*}=\min(\vartheta,\pi\ln^{-1}(\Lambda/E_{\mathrm{Th}})/2).

(161)

Appendix H
Solution of the Usadel equation for gap fluctuation

We solve Eq. (
50
), which for
\varepsilon>0
is

\Big(-D\nabla^{2}+2\varepsilon\Big)\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})-2\tilde{g}^{2}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,\left[\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)+\frac{\vartheta}{2}\Theta(E_{\mathrm{Th}}-\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}))\ln^{2}\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)\right]\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{r})=\\
=4\bar{g}^{2}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,\delta\bar{D}(i\varepsilon-i\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{r})\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon^{\prime}).

(162)

We seek a solution in the form

\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})=\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\int d^{2}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}S(\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),\quad\quad S(\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})=4\bar{g}^{2}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,\delta\bar{D}(i\varepsilon-i\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{r})\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon^{\prime}),

(163)

and the Green’s function
\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
satisfies:

(-D\nabla^{2}+2\varepsilon)\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})-\delta(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0})\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\\
=2\tilde{g}^{2}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,\left[\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)+\frac{\vartheta}{2}\Theta(E_{\mathrm{Th}}-\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}))\ln^{2}\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)\right]\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(164)

In the momentum space, the equation for
\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{q})=\int d^{2}\mathbf{r}\,e^{-i\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{q}}\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{r})
takes the form

(D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon)\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})-2\tilde{g}^{2}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,\left[\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)+\frac{\vartheta}{2}\Theta(E_{\mathrm{Th}}-\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}))\ln^{2}\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)\right]\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})=\delta(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0}).

(165)

We write
\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})
in the form
\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})+\delta(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0})(D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0})^{-1}
.
The function
\mathcal{F^{\prime}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})}
satisfies the following equation:

(D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon)\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})-2\tilde{g}^{2}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,\left[\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)+\frac{\vartheta}{2}\Theta(E_{\mathrm{Th}}-\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}))\ln^{2}\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)\right]\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})=\\
=\frac{2\tilde{g}^{2}}{D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0})}\right)+\frac{\vartheta}{2}\Theta(E_{\mathrm{Th}}-\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}))\ln^{2}\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0})}\right)\right].

(166)

Eq. (
166
) is devoid of
\delta
-functions, therefore
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})
is a continuous function of
\varepsilon
in the vicinity of
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}
.
Differentiating Eq. (
166
) with respect to
\varepsilon
across
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}
gives

\partial_{\varepsilon}((D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon)\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q}))|_{\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}-0}-\frac{2\tilde{g}^{2}}{D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}}\left[\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{0}}+\frac{\vartheta}{\varepsilon_{0}}\Theta(E_{\mathrm{Th}}-\varepsilon_{0})\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\right)\right]=\partial_{\varepsilon}((D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon)\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q}))|_{\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}+0}.

(167)

One expects
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})\sim(D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon)^{-1}
from the structure of Eq. (
166
).
Therefore, the divergence of the integral in the second term of Eq. (
166
) is expected to be cut off at
\varepsilon\sim D\mathbf{q}^{2}
instead of
\varepsilon=\pi T
, leading to

\displaystyle\tilde{g}^{2}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)(D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon^{\prime})^{-1}

\displaystyle\sim\tilde{g}^{2}\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\varepsilon}\right)\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{D\mathbf{q}^{2}}\right)\ll 1,

(168)

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}\vartheta}{2}\int_{\pi T}^{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,\ln^{2}\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)(D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon^{\prime})^{-1}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}\vartheta}{2}\ln^{2}\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\varepsilon}\right)\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{D\mathbf{q}^{2}}\right)\ll 1,

(169)

as far as
D\mathbf{q}^{2}\gg\pi T_{c}
, so the logarithmic terms remain small.
Therefore,

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})\approx\frac{2\tilde{g}^{2}}{(D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0})(D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon)}\left[\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0})}\right)+\frac{\vartheta}{2}\Theta(E_{\mathrm{Th}}-\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}))\ln^{2}\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th}}}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0})}\right)\right].

(170)

Since
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})=O(\tilde{g}^{2}
) with no additional divergences,
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is a subleading contribution.
The structure of
\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})
for
D\mathbf{q}^{2}\gtrsim\pi T_{c}
and
T\approx T_{c}
is therefore
\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})=\delta(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0})(D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0})^{-1}
to the leading order in
\tilde{g}
.

From Eq. (
163
), the correlation function
\langle\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})\,\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\rangle
for
D\mathbf{q}^{2}\gtrsim\pi T_{c}
takes the form

\langle\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\rangle=\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon_{0}\,d\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime}\int d^{2}\mathbf{x}\,d^{2}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\;\langle S(\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{x})S(\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{x})\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

(171)

The “source” term correlation function
\langle S(\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{x})S(\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle
follows from Eq. (
163
):

\langle S(\varepsilon,\mathbf{x})S(\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle=16\bar{g}^{4}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon_{0}d\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime}\;\langle\delta\bar{D}(i\varepsilon-i\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{x})\,\delta\bar{D}(i\varepsilon^{\prime}-i\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon_{0})\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime}).

(172)

With the help of Eq. (
42
), the above expressions in momentum space reduce to

\displaystyle\langle\delta\Phi\delta\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon,i\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon_{0}\,d\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime}\;\langle SS\rangle(\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime},\mathbf{q})\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0},\mathbf{q})\mathcal{F}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime},\mathbf{q}),

(173)

\displaystyle\langle SS\rangle(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=16\bar{g}^{4}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon_{0}d\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime}\;B(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0},\mathbf{q})\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon_{0})\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime}).

(174)

where
B(\omega,\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{q})=\int d^{2}\mathbf{r}\,e^{-i\mathbf{q}\mathbf{r}}B(\omega,\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{r})
and
B(\omega,\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{r})
is given by Eq. (
112
).
Assuming
n(\mathcal{E})=n=\mathrm{const}
and
\xi(\mathcal{E})=\xi=\mathrm{const}
,
Eq. (
174
) simplifies to

\langle SS\rangle(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{q})=\frac{16\bar{g}^{4}n}{c_{d}}(1+\mathbf{q}^{2}\xi^{2}/4)^{-3}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon_{0}d\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime}\;\frac{\ln(|\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0}|/|\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime}|)}{|\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0}|-|\varepsilon^{\prime}-\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime}|}\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon_{0})\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime}).

(175)

The main contributions to the integrals over the energies in Eq. (
175

\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0}^{\prime}\lesssim\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}
, since
\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon)\sim\varepsilon^{-1}
up to logarithmic corrections (see Eqs. (
145
) and (
146
)).
Therefore, Eq. (
175

\frac{\langle SS\rangle(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{q})}{\langle\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle^{2}(\omega=0)}\simeq\frac{16\bar{g}^{4}n}{c_{d}}(1+\mathbf{q}^{2}\xi^{2}/4)^{-3}\frac{\ln(\varepsilon/\varepsilon^{\prime})}{\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}}\ell(\varepsilon)\ell(\varepsilon^{\prime}),\quad\quad\ell(\varepsilon)=\int_{\pi T}^{\varepsilon}d\varepsilon_{0}\;\frac{\langle\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon_{0})}{\langle\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle(\omega=0)}.

(176)

Substitution of Eq. (
176

173
) results in

\frac{\langle\delta\Phi\,\delta\Phi\rangle(i\varepsilon,i\varepsilon^{\prime},\mathbf{q})}{\langle\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle^{2}(\omega=0)}\simeq\frac{16\bar{g}^{4}n}{c_{d}}\frac{\ell(\varepsilon)\ell(\varepsilon^{\prime})}{(D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon)(D\mathbf{q}^{2}+2\varepsilon^{\prime})(1+\mathbf{q}^{2}\xi^{2}/4)^{3}}\frac{\ln(\varepsilon/\varepsilon^{\prime})}{\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}},\quad D\mathbf{q}^{2}\gg\pi T_{c},

(177)

A useful quantity that can be evaluated from Eq. (
177

\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(\omega=0,\mathbf{r})=\int d\varepsilon\,\delta\Phi(i\varepsilon,\mathbf{r})

\langle\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\,\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle(\omega=0,\mathbf{q})=\int d^{2}\mathbf{r}\,e^{-i\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{q}}\langle\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(\omega=0,\mathbf{r})\,\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(\omega=0,0)\rangle

\frac{\langle\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\,\delta\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle(\omega=0,\mathbf{q})}{\langle\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}\rangle^{2}(\omega=0)}\simeq\frac{16\pi^{2}\bar{g}^{2}}{c_{d}D\mathbf{q}^{2}}\frac{\ln\left(\frac{D\mathbf{q}^{2}}{\pi T_{c}}\right)}{\left(1+\mathbf{q}^{2}\xi^{2}/4\right)^{3}},\quad D\mathbf{q}^{2}\gg\pi T_{c},

52

Appendix I
Details of the numerical solution and additional figures

I.1
Numerical solution

Here we provide the technical details of the numerical solution to Eq. (
29
), along with some additional data plots that did not fit into the main text.

The dynamical Usadel equation in Eq. (
29
) is an eigenvalue equation, and can be solved efficiently using iterative methods. We write it again below, to make this more transparent,

\sum\limits_{m=-m_{\text{max}}}^{m_{\text{max}}}\left[\Big(-D\nabla^{2}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|+2\bar{\Sigma}(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\Big)\delta_{n,m}-2\bar{g}^{2}T\bar{D}(i\varepsilon_{m}-i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})\right]\Phi(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r})=0

(179)

Here,
m_{\text{max}}
is a necessary “frequency cutoff”. The size of the vector is
N_{\omega}\times L^{2}
, where
N_{\omega}=2\,m_{\text{max}}+1
. As discussed in the main text, due to the strong disorder in the problem, we are interested in the entire low-lying spectrum (tens to hundreds of eigenvalues), beyond just the zero eigenvalue. We therefore use the locally optimal block preconditioned conjugate gradient (LOBPCG) algorithm, since it is particularly well equipped to find these simultaneously, running on graphical processing units (GPUs) (a similar approach was used in Ref.
Huang et al. 2024
to solve an exciton Schrödinger equation).

In order to implement LOBPCG we simply need to efficiently apply the matrix-vector (matvec) multiplication of the operator in Eq. (
179
). We apply the gradient term by a fast Fourier transform (FFT):
\nabla^{2}\Phi(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(2-\cos(k_{x})-\cos(k_{y}))\mathcal{F}\,\Phi(i\varepsilon_{m},\mathbf{r})
. The attractive part of the pairing operator, which contains the boson Green’s function, is Toeplitz in the frequency index, and can therefore be fast multiplied by FFT with a standard procedure. All other matvecs are elements-wise multiplication. LOBPCG is at its best when there is a fast preconditioner
P
available for acceleration. In this case, we simply use the free kernel with a constant shift
\alpha
:
P^{-1}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(2-\cos(k_{x})-\cos(k_{y}))\mathcal{F}+2|\varepsilon_{n}|+\alpha
.

As the temperature
T
is lowered, we keep the actual frequency cutoff in the Matsubara sum fixed, i.e.
T\times N_{\omega}=C
, where
C
is the same constant throughout all simulations.

Appendix J
Large solitary puddle

We consider the regime in which the local pairing kernel is dominated by a single bosonic eigenstate
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{r})
.
As was determined in the main text, in the limit of
T_{c,\mathrm{loc}}\gtrsim E_{\mathrm{Th},\alpha}
, the gradient term in Eq. (
57
) can be neglected, leading to

|\varepsilon_{n}|\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n})=\bar{g}^{2}T\sum_{\varepsilon_{m}}\Phi(i\varepsilon_{m})\bar{D}(i\max(\varepsilon_{n},\varepsilon_{m})),

(180)

where

\displaystyle\bar{D}(i\omega_{n})=\frac{1}{\pi\xi^{2}_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{c_{d}|\omega_{n}|+e_{\alpha}},

(181)

and the Gor’kov function is given by
\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n},\mathbf{r})=\Phi(i\varepsilon_{n})\Theta(\xi_{\alpha}-|\mathbf{r}|)
.
A standard approximation

\bar{D}(i\varepsilon_{n}-i\varepsilon_{m})\approx\bar{D}(i\max(|\varepsilon_{n}|,|\varepsilon_{m}|))
was used in Eq. (
180
).
For this large-puddle estimate, we approximated the mode as roughly uniform over an area
\pi\xi_{\alpha}^{2}
.
Applying the Euler-Maclaurin approximation to the right side of Eq. (
180
)
simplifies the equation to

\varepsilon\Phi(i\varepsilon)=\frac{2\bar{g}^{2}}{\pi\xi^{2}_{\alpha}}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}\frac{d\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2\pi}\frac{\Phi(i\varepsilon^{\prime})}{c_{d}\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})+e_{\alpha}}.

(182)

With a substitution
g_{\alpha}=\bar{g}^{2}/\pi^{2}\xi^{2}_{\alpha}e_{\alpha}
and
x=c_{d}\varepsilon/e_{\alpha}
, Eq. (
182
) becomes dimensionless:

x\Phi(ix)=g_{\alpha}\int_{x_{T}}^{x_{\Lambda}}dx^{\prime}\frac{\Phi(ix^{\prime})}{\max(x,x^{\prime})+1},

(183)

where
x_{T}=\pi c_{d}T/e_{\alpha}
and
x_{\Lambda}=c_{d}\Lambda/e_{\alpha}
.
The solution of Eq. (
183
) satisfies the differential equation

-\partial_{x}\left[(1+x)^{2}\partial_{x}(x\Phi)\right]=g_{\alpha}\Phi.

(184)

The solution to Eq. (
184
) has a form

\Phi(ix)=AC_{1}(x)+BC_{2}(x),

(185)

where

C_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{x(1+x)}F\left(\alpha_{+},\alpha_{-},2;\frac{1}{1+x}\right),\quad\quad C_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{(1+x)^{2}}F\left(1+\alpha_{+},1+\alpha_{-},2;\frac{x}{1+x}\right).

(186)

The constants
\alpha_{\pm}
are given by
\alpha_{\pm}=1/2\pm\sqrt{1+4g_{\alpha}}/2
, and
F(a,b,c;z)
is a Gauss
{}_{2}F_{1}
hypergeometric function.
Using standard hypergeometric identities
Bateman and Project 1953
, one can show that
C_{1}(x)
satisfies the following identity:

\frac{g_{\alpha}}{1+x}\int_{x_{T}}^{x}C_{1}(x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}+g_{\alpha}\int_{x}^{x_{\Lambda}}\frac{C_{1}(x^{\prime})}{1+x^{\prime}}\,dx^{\prime}-xC_{1}(x)=\\
-\frac{1}{(1+x)(1+x_{T})}\left[x_{T}F\left(1+\alpha_{+},1+\alpha_{-},1,\frac{1}{1+x_{T}}\right)-F\left(1+\alpha_{+},1+\alpha_{-},2,\frac{1}{1+x_{T}}\right)\right]+\\
+\frac{1}{1+x_{\Lambda}}\left[\frac{x_{\Lambda}}{1+x_{\Lambda}}F\left(1+\alpha_{+},1+\alpha_{-},1,\frac{1}{1+x_{\Lambda}}\right)-F\left(1+\alpha_{+},1+\alpha_{-},2,\frac{1}{1+x_{\Lambda}}\right)\right].

(187)

The second solution
C_{2}(x)
satisfies an analogous identity:

\frac{g_{\alpha}}{1+x}\int_{x_{T}}^{x}C_{2}(x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}+g_{\alpha}\int_{x}^{x_{\Lambda}}\frac{C_{2}(x^{\prime})}{1+x^{\prime}}\,dx^{\prime}-xC_{2}(x)=\\
-\frac{1}{1+x}\left[\frac{x_{T}}{(x_{T}+1)}F\left(1+\alpha_{+},1+\alpha_{-},2,\frac{x_{T}}{1+x_{T}}\right)-\frac{1}{1+x_{T}}F\left(1+\alpha_{+},1+\alpha_{-},1,\frac{x_{T}}{1+x_{T}}\right)\right]+\\
+\frac{1}{(1+x_{\Lambda})^{2}}F\left(1+\alpha_{+},1+\alpha_{-},1,\frac{x_{\Lambda}}{1+x_{\Lambda}}\right).

(188)

Substituting Eq. (
187
) and (
188
) into Eq. (
183
) reduces to the self-consistency condition for
T=T_{c,\mathrm{loc}}
of the form

\left[(1-z_{T})\tilde{F}_{1}(z_{T})-z_{T}\tilde{F}_{2}(z_{T})\right]\left[z_{\Lambda}\tilde{F}_{1}(1-z_{\Lambda})\right]-\left[(1-z_{\Lambda})\tilde{F}_{1}(z_{\Lambda})-\tilde{F}_{2}(z_{\Lambda})\right]\left[(1-z_{T})\tilde{F}_{2}(1-z_{T})-z_{T}\tilde{F}_{1}(1-z_{T})\right]=0,

(189)

where
\tilde{F}_{n}(z)=F(1+\alpha_{+},1+\alpha_{-},n,z)
, and
z_{T,\Lambda}=1/(1+x_{T,\Lambda})
.
For
e_{\alpha}\ll c_{d}\Lambda
, one has
z_{\Lambda}\ll 1
. For small
z
it is true that

\tilde{F}_{n}(z)=1+\frac{(2-g_{\alpha})}{n}z,\quad\tilde{F}_{1}(1-z_{\Lambda})\approx\frac{1}{z_{\Lambda}^{2}}.

(190)

Substitution of Eq. (
190
) into Eq. (
189
) simplifies the self-consistency condition for
T=T_{c,\alpha}
to be

(1-z_{T})\left(\tilde{F}_{1}(z_{T})+\frac{g_{\alpha}z_{\Lambda}^{2}}{2}\tilde{F}_{2}(1-z_{T})\right)=z_{T}\left(\tilde{F}_{2}(z_{T})+\frac{g_{\alpha}z_{\Lambda}^{2}}{2}\tilde{F}_{1}(1-z_{T})\right).

(191)

We first analyze Eq. (
191
) by considering the regime
g_{\alpha}\lesssim 1
.
In this limit, one would expect
x_{T}\ll 1
, (
z_{T}\approx 1
), so we can approximate
\tilde{F}_{1}(z)
and
\tilde{F}_{2}(z)
as

\tilde{F}_{1}(z)\approx\frac{1}{(1-z)^{2}}-\frac{g_{\alpha}z}{(1-z)^{2}},\quad\tilde{F}_{2}(z)\approx\frac{1}{1-z}-\frac{g_{\alpha}}{1-z}-\frac{g_{\alpha}}{z}\log(1-z).

(192)

so Eq. (
191
) simplifies greatly, leading to
z_{T}=1-e^{-1/g_{\alpha}}
. Therefore, the
T_{c,\alpha}
of the superconducting puddle is

T_{c,\alpha}\approx\frac{e_{\alpha}}{\pi c_{d}}\left(\exp\left[\frac{\pi^{2}\xi_{\alpha}^{2}e_{\alpha}}{\bar{g}^{2}}\right]-1\right)^{-1},\quad\quad\bar{g}^{2}\lesssim\pi^{2}\xi^{2}_{\alpha}e_{\alpha}.

(193)

Since the bosonic density of states
n(\mathcal{E})
is constant in the vicinity
\mathcal{E}=0
, states with arbitrary small
e_{\alpha}
appear with non-zero probability.
For a fixed value of
\bar{g}^{2}
, the value of
T_{c,\alpha}
peaks for
e_{\alpha}\sim\bar{g}^{2}/\xi_{\alpha}^{2}
, leading to maximal
T_{c,\alpha}
to have a scale
T_{c,\alpha}\sim\bar{g}^{2}/c_{d}\xi_{\alpha}^{2}
.

Next, we consider the solution of Eq. (
191
) in the limit of
g_{\alpha}\gtrsim 1
.
To estimate the behavior of the hypergeometric functions in such a limit, we use Watson’s expansion
(
Bateman and Project 1953
, Chap.2.3.2, Eq. 17)
:

\tilde{F}_{1}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\lambda}}\frac{\cos\left(\lambda\theta-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}{\sqrt{\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\cos^{5}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\right),\quad\quad\tilde{F}_{2}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\lambda}\left(\lambda-\frac{1}{2}\right)}\frac{\sin\left(\lambda\theta-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}{\sqrt{\sin^{3}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\cos^{3}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\right),

(194)

where
\theta=\mathrm{Arccos(1-2z)}
and
\lambda=\sqrt{g_{\alpha}}
.
The approximation works particularly well for
g_{\alpha}\gtrsim 5
.
Due to the oscillatory nature of
\tilde{F}_{1}(z)
and
\tilde{F}_{2}(z)
, one should expect to find the first solution at high temperatures
T
.
Such temperatures would correspond to
z_{T}\ll 1
for large enough
g_{\alpha}
.
This allows to approximate
\theta
as
\theta\approx 2\sqrt{z}
with an expectation that
\theta\lesssim 1/\lambda
, leading to

\cos\left(\lambda\theta-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\left[1-\frac{\lambda^{2}z_{\Lambda}^{2}}{\theta\left(\lambda-\frac{1}{2}\right)}\cos\pi\lambda-2\lambda^{2}z_{\Lambda}^{2}\sin\pi\lambda\right]=\frac{2\theta}{\lambda-\frac{1}{2}}\left(1-2\lambda^{2}z_{\Lambda}^{2}\cos\pi\lambda+\frac{\lambda^{2}z_{\Lambda}^{2}}{2\theta^{2}}\sin\pi\lambda\right)\sin\left(\lambda\theta-\frac{\pi}{4}\right).

(195)

In the leading order in
1/\Lambda
, solution of Eq. (
195
) is independent of
\Lambda
, leading to

\theta=\frac{3\pi}{4\sqrt{g_{\alpha}}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{g_{\alpha}}\right)+O\left(\frac{g_{\alpha}^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\right)\right).

(196)

Therefore,
z_{T}\approx 9\pi^{2}/16g_{\alpha}
for
g_{\alpha}\gtrsim 1
, which gives the puddle transition temperature

T_{c,\alpha}=\frac{64\bar{g}^{2}}{9\pi^{5}c_{d}\xi^{2}_{\alpha}},\quad\quad\bar{g}^{2}\gtrsim\pi^{2}\xi_{\alpha}^{2}e_{\alpha}.

(197)

It is interesting to note that Eq. (
193
) provides a useful interpolation for
T_{c,\alpha}
across both regimes, up to order-one numerical factors.

The large-puddle approximation is valid if the spatial gradient in Eq. (
57
) can be neglected.
The order of magnitude of the spatial gradient is
2D/\xi_{\alpha}^{2}=E_{\mathrm{Th},\alpha}
, and the smallest absolute value of
\varepsilon_{n}
appearing in Eq. (
57
) is
\pi T_{c,\alpha}
.
Then, for the “large” puddle picture to be valid, it must follow that

\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th},\alpha}}{\pi T_{c,\alpha}}\sim\frac{g_{\alpha}}{\vartheta}\left(\exp\left[\frac{1}{g_{\alpha}}\right]-1\right)\lesssim 1.

(198)

Therefore, the large puddle picture requires
\vartheta\gtrsim g_{\alpha}(\exp[g_{\alpha}^{-1}]-1)\gtrsim 1
.
This regime lies outside the weak-inhomogeneity limit
\vartheta\ll 1
emphasized in the main text.

Appendix K
Approximate solution of Eq. (
60
)

To obtain Eq. (
60
), one substitutes Eq. (
59
) into Eq. (
57
).
Ensuring that Eq. (
57
) holds for each
C_{\varkappa_{m}}(\varepsilon_{n})
separately after substitution requires it to satisfy

(|\varepsilon_{n}|-\varkappa_{k})C_{\varkappa_{k}}(\varepsilon_{n})=\pi\tilde{g}^{2}T\sum_{\varepsilon_{m}}\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(|\varepsilon_{m}|,|\varepsilon_{n}|)}\right)C_{\varkappa_{k}}(\varepsilon_{m}).

(199)

Applying the Euler-Maclaurin approximation to Eq. (
199
) results in Eq. (
60
) of the main text.

Eq. (
60
) is solved in the form

C_{\varkappa}(\varepsilon)=C^{\prime}_{\varkappa}(\varepsilon)+\delta(\varepsilon-\varkappa)
for
\varepsilon>0
, where
C^{\prime}_{\varkappa}(\varepsilon)
obeys the following integral equation:

(\varepsilon-\varkappa)C^{\prime}_{\varkappa}(\varepsilon)=\tilde{g}^{2}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)C^{\prime}_{\varkappa}(\varepsilon^{\prime})+\tilde{g}^{2}\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(\varepsilon,\varkappa)}\right).

(200)

Eq. (
200
) can be reduced to a differential equation

\partial_{\varepsilon}\left[\varepsilon\,\partial_{\varepsilon}\left(\left(\varepsilon-\varkappa\right)C^{\prime}_{\varkappa}(\varepsilon)\right)\right]=-\tilde{g}^{2}C^{\prime}_{\varkappa}(\varepsilon),

(201)

which is solved by

C^{\prime}_{\varkappa}(\varepsilon)=A_{\varkappa}F_{1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varkappa}\right)+B_{\varkappa}F_{2}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\varkappa}\right).

(202)

Functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
can be expressed in terms of a Gauss Hypergeometric function
F(a,b,c;z)
as

F_{1}(z)=\mathrm{Re}\,F\left(1+i\tilde{g},1-i\tilde{g};\,1;\,z\right),\quad\quad F_{2}(z)=\Theta(-z)\mathrm{Re}\,F\left(1+i\tilde{g},1-i\tilde{g};\,2;\,z\right).

(203)

Function
F_{2}(1-\varepsilon/\varkappa)
is finite for all
\pi T<\varepsilon,\varkappa<\Lambda
, and the
(1-\varepsilon/\varkappa)^{-1}
divergence in
F_{1}(\varepsilon/\varkappa)
for
\varepsilon\approx\varkappa
should be understood in the sense of the principal value.

To determine the coefficients
A_{\varkappa}
and
B_{\varkappa}
, we substitute Eq. (
202
) back into Eq. (
200
).
Substituting
\varepsilon=\Lambda
into Eq. (
200
) gives

C_{\varkappa}(\Lambda)=0
.
With the help of the asymptotic expansion

\displaystyle F(1+i\tilde{g},1-i\tilde{g},1,z)

\displaystyle\sim-e^{\pi\tilde{g}}\frac{\cos\left(\tilde{g}\ln z\right)}{2z}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\tilde{g}}{z}\right)\right),\quad\quad\arg z=+0,\quad z\gg 1.

(204)

\displaystyle F(1+i\tilde{g},1-i\tilde{g},2,1-z)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\sin\left(\tilde{g}\ln z\right)}{2z\tilde{g}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\tilde{g}}{z}\right)\right),\quad\quad\arg z=+0,\quad z\gg 1,

(205)

the relation between
A_{\varkappa}
and
B_{\varkappa}
in case of
\tilde{g}\ll 1
reduces to

B_{\varkappa}=-A_{\varkappa}\frac{F_{1}(\Lambda/\varkappa)}{F_{2}(1-\Lambda/\varkappa)}\approx A_{\varkappa}\,\tilde{g}e^{-\pi\tilde{g}}\cot\left(\tilde{g}\ln\frac{\Lambda}{\varkappa}\right)\approx A_{\varkappa}\,\tilde{g}\tan\left(\tilde{g}\ln\frac{\varkappa}{\pi T_{c0}}\right).

(206)

As before,
\pi T_{c0}=\Lambda e^{-\pi/2\tilde{g}}
.
Substitution of Eq. (
202
) into Eq. (
200
) results in

\displaystyle\tilde{g}^{2}\int_{0}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}\right)C^{\prime}_{\varkappa}(\varepsilon^{\prime})+(\varkappa-\varepsilon)C^{\prime}_{\varkappa}(\varepsilon)=B_{\varkappa}\varkappa\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\max(\varepsilon,\varkappa)}\right)+(\varkappa-\Lambda)C^{\prime}_{\varkappa}(\Lambda).

(207)

The following integral hypergeometric identities are useful in the derivation of Eq. (
207
):

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,F_{1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{\varkappa}\right)

\displaystyle=\left.\varepsilon\,F_{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varkappa}\right)\right|_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\varepsilon_{1}},

(208)

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,F_{2}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{\varkappa}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\tilde{g}^{2}}\left.\varepsilon\,F_{1}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\varkappa}\right)\right|_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\varepsilon_{1}},

(209)

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right)\,F_{1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{\varkappa}\right)

\displaystyle=\left.\left[\varepsilon\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\varepsilon}\right)F_{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varkappa}\right)+\frac{1}{\tilde{g}^{2}}(\varkappa-\varepsilon)F_{1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\varkappa}\right)\right]\right|_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\varepsilon_{1}},

(210)

\displaystyle\int_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\,\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right)\,F_{2}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{\varkappa}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\tilde{g}^{2}}\left[\varepsilon\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\varepsilon}\right)F_{1}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\varkappa}\right)+(\varepsilon-\varkappa)F_{2}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\varkappa}\right)\right]_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\varepsilon_{1}}.

(211)

Comparing Eq. (
207
) to Eq. (
200
) along with the boundary condition
C_{\varkappa}(\Lambda)=0
immediately implies

B_{\varkappa}=-\frac{\tilde{g}^{2}}{\varkappa},\quad\quad A_{\varkappa}=-\frac{\tilde{g}}{\varkappa}\cot\left(\tilde{g}\ln\left(\frac{\varkappa}{\pi T_{c0}}\right)\right),

(212)

which gives the result quoted in the main text.

Appendix L
Solution of Eq. (
63
)

Eq. (
63
) can be written as

\Psi(\varepsilon)=\frac{2\vartheta}{3}\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\frac{\Psi(\varepsilon^{\prime})K_{0}(k(\varepsilon^{\prime})\xi_{\alpha})}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})+e_{\alpha}/c_{d}}+\Delta_{0}

(213)

in the limit of small temperature. The
k(\varepsilon)=\sqrt{2|\varepsilon|/D}
defines a thermal inverse length scale characteristic of the puddle.
The infinitesimal source
\Delta_{0}
is included to fix the normalization of the linear response.
In general, one expects
T\sim e_{\alpha}/c_{d}\ll\Lambda
for most relevant puddle solutions, so Eq. (
213
) can be approximated as

\Psi(\varepsilon)=\frac{2\vartheta}{3}\int_{\pi T+e_{\alpha}/c_{d}}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\frac{\Psi(\varepsilon^{\prime})K_{0}(k(\varepsilon^{\prime})\xi_{\alpha})}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}+\Delta_{0}.

(214)

Assuming
E_{\mathrm{Th},\alpha}
is large, the modified Bessel function can be approximated by a logarithm as

K_{0}(z)\approx-\ln(z/2)
, leading to

\Psi(\varepsilon)=\frac{\vartheta}{3}\int_{\pi T^{\prime}}^{\Lambda}d\varepsilon^{\prime}\frac{\Psi(\varepsilon^{\prime})\ln(E_{\mathrm{Th},\alpha}/\varepsilon^{\prime})}{\max(\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime})}+\Delta_{0},

(215)

where we introduced

\pi T^{\prime}=\pi T+e_{\alpha}/c_{d}

for conciseness.
The replacement of the lower cutoff reflects that the energy term
e_{\alpha}/c_{d}
cuts off the infrared divergence.

The function
\Psi(\varepsilon)
that satisfies Eq. (
215
) also satisfies

-\partial_{\varepsilon}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\,\partial_{\varepsilon}\Psi(\varepsilon)\right)=\vartheta^{\prime}\,\Psi(\varepsilon)\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th},\alpha}}{\varepsilon}\right),

(216)

where

\vartheta^{\prime}=\vartheta/3
.
Solution of Eq. (
216
) can be found exactly:

\displaystyle\Psi(\varepsilon)=\sqrt{\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th},\alpha}}{\varepsilon}}\left[a(T^{\prime})\,\mathrm{Ai}\,(z(\varepsilon))+b(T^{\prime})\,\mathrm{Bi}\,(z(\varepsilon))\right],\quad\quad z(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{\vartheta^{\prime 2/3}}\left(\frac{1}{4}-\vartheta^{\prime}\ln\left(\frac{E_{\mathrm{Th},\alpha}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right).

(217)

The constants
a(T^{\prime})
and
b(T^{\prime})
are determined by substituting Eq. (
217
) back into Eq. (
215
), resulting in

\displaystyle a(T^{\prime})

\displaystyle=2\vartheta^{\prime}\Delta_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda}{E_{\mathrm{Th},\alpha}}}\frac{B_{-}(z(\pi T^{\prime}))}{B_{-}(z(\pi T^{\prime}))\,A_{+}(z(\Lambda))-A_{-}(z(\pi T^{\prime}))B_{+}(z(\Lambda))},

(218)

\displaystyle b(T^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\vartheta^{\prime}\Delta_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda}{E_{\mathrm{Th},\alpha}}}\frac{A_{-}(z(\pi T^{\prime}))}{B_{-}(z(\pi T^{\prime}))\,A_{+}(z(\Lambda))-A_{-}(z(\pi T^{\prime}))B_{+}(z(\Lambda))},

(219)

where
A_{\pm}(z)=\mathrm{Ai}\,(z)\pm 2\vartheta^{\prime 1/3}\,\mathrm{Ai}^{\prime}(z)
, and
B_{\pm}(z)=\mathrm{Bi}\,(z)\pm 2\vartheta^{\prime 1/3}\,\mathrm{Bi}^{\prime}(z)
.
The pairing amplitude at the distance
|\mathbf{r}|=\xi_{\alpha}
away from the center of the localized bosonic mode then becomes

\Phi_{\mathrm{stat}}(r=\xi_{\alpha})\sim\int_{\pi T}^{\Lambda^{\prime}}\frac{d\varepsilon}{2\pi}\Phi(i\varepsilon,r=\xi)=\Delta_{0}\Lambda\frac{B_{-}(z(\pi T^{\prime}))A_{-}(z(\Lambda))-A_{-}(z(\pi T^{\prime}))B_{-}(z(\Lambda))}{B_{-}(z(\pi T^{\prime}))A_{+}(z(\Lambda))-A_{-}(z(\pi T^{\prime}))B_{+}(z(\Lambda))}.

(220)
