Severe Legionella pneumophila pneumonia identified by targeted next-generation sequencing after nondiagnostic conventional testing: A case report

Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) remains a common infectious disease worldwide and is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. In a subset of patients, uncontrolled infection and dysregulated host inflammatory responses may lead to progression to severe CAP (SCAP), which is frequently complicated by sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and multiple organ dysfunction, resulting in poor clinical outcomes [1].

Among the etiological agents of CAP, Legionella spp. are recognized as important pathogens in hospitalized patients, accounting for approximately 2%–15% of SCAP cases [2]. Legionella pneumophila serogroup 1 is the most common cause of human Legionnaires’ disease, although other serogroups and species can also cause infection [3]. Since its first identification following the 1976 outbreak in Philadelphia [4], L. pneumophila has been established as a major cause of atypical pneumonia, with a considerable proportion of affected patients requiring intensive care unit (ICU) admission [5]. Infection may manifest as Legionnaires’ disease, a severe form of pneumonia with substantial mortality, or as Pontiac fever, a self-limiting, influenza-like illness [6].

Despite its clinical significance, Legionella infection remains difficult to recognize in routine practice because its clinical manifestations overlap with those of other causes of severe pneumonia. Conventional diagnostic methods have important limitations. Urinary antigen testing is rapid but mainly detects L. pneumophila serogroup 1, while culture requires specialized media and laboratory expertise and may be delayed or falsely negative [7]. In China, legionellosis also appears to be underrecognized. A multicenter Chinese surveillance study conducted from 2014 to 2016 found L. pneumophila serogroup 1 antigen in only about 3.8% of patients with severe pneumonia of unknown etiology [8]. As a result, negative or unavailable conventional tests do not exclude Legionella infection, particularly in critically ill patients with rapidly progressive pneumonia. These diagnostic constraints may delay pathogen-directed therapy and support consideration of additional microbiological approaches when the initial work-up is nondiagnostic [9].

In recent years, sequencing-based assays, including targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS), have increasingly been used as adjunctive tools for pathogen identification in severe pneumonia, particularly when conventional microbiological investigations are negative, delayed, or not readily available [10]. Here, we report a case of rapidly progressive severe L. pneumophila pneumonia in an elderly woman who developed septic shock and multi-organ dysfunction. In this case, BALF tNGS provided etiological evidence after routine microbiological testing was nondiagnostic and supported subsequent adjustment of anti-Legionella therapy. This case illustrates how sequencing-based diagnostics may inform clinical decision-making when their results are integrated with disease course, specimen source, microbiological findings, and therapeutic response.

Case presentation

A 79-year-old woman was admitted to the ICU of our hospital because of acute fever and rapidly progressive respiratory failure. Her medical history was notable for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and bronchial asthma requiring long-term home oxygen therapy, hypertension, and stage 4 chronic kidney disease. Several days earlier, she had been discharged from another tertiary-care hospital after improvement following treatment for an acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Chest computed tomography (CT) at the referring hospital revealed bilateral pulmonary involvement, predominantly in the right lung, with patchy consolidation and ground-glass opacities (Fig. 1A). These findings suggested extensive pulmonary infection and pre-existing structural lung abnormalities before transfer to our hospital. Two days before admission, she developed recurrent cough with sputum production and high-grade fever, with a peak temperature of 39.0°C. Empirical treatment administered at home did not improve her symptoms. Approximately 11 h before admission, she experienced sudden worsening dyspnea accompanied by cyanosis and was transported to the emergency department by ambulance. Chest CT performed in the emergency department before ICU admission revealed extensive bilateral pulmonary infiltrates with diffuse consolidation and ground-glass opacities, predominantly involving the lower lobes, without evidence of pleural effusion. These findings were consistent with severe pneumonia and acute lung injury (Fig. 1B).

A: Chest CT at the referring hospital on August 30 showed bilateral inflammatory exudative lesions, predominantly in the right lung, with focal consolidation/atelectasis and right upper-lobe volume loss, suggesting severe pulmonary infection on a background of chronic structural lung disease. B: Chest CT on September 12 showed progressive bilateral pulmonary infection, predominantly in the right lung, with extensive consolidation, ground-glass opacities, and coalescent infiltrates involving both lungs, consistent with worsening severe pneumonia.

On presentation, she was in severe respiratory distress, with a respiratory rate of 33 breaths/min, heart rate of 147 beats/min, and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) of approximately 70% on room air. Despite supplemental oxygen and initial medical management, hypoxemia persisted, and she was transferred to the ICU. Arterial blood gas analysis performed during noninvasive mechanical ventilation with an inspired oxygen fraction of 100% demonstrated profound hypoxemia, with a pH of 7.28, partial pressure of oxygen 55.1 mmHg, and a PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio of 55.1 mmHg. Mild respiratory acidosis with metabolic compensation was present (PaCO₂ 47.1 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 20.4 mmol/L, base excess −4.5 mmol/L). These findings indicated failure of noninvasive ventilation and were consistent with severe ARDS; therefore, the patient underwent endotracheal intubation and was initiated on invasive mechanical ventilation in assist-control volume-controlled mode with FiO₂ 1.0, a respiratory rate of 20 breaths/min, tidal volume of 400 mL, and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 8 cmH2O. Despite active fluid resuscitation, her blood pressure remained low, and therefore norepinephrine (0.5 μg/kg/min) was administered to maintain organ perfusion.

Initial laboratory investigations revealed marked leukocytosis with neutrophil predominance, profoundly elevated inflammatory markers (procalcitonin >100 ng/mL, interleukin−6 >5000 pg/mL, and C-reactive protein >250 mg/L), acute kidney injury with a serum creatinine level of 414.9 µmol/L, hyperlactatemia, and progressive liver dysfunction (Table 1).

Empirical antimicrobial therapy with meropenem and caspofungin was initiated on the day of ICU admission, and moxifloxacin was added the following day to broaden coverage for atypical pathogens. The Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score was 14, and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score was 37, indicating extremely severe illness and a high predicted risk of mortality. Despite aggressive supportive management, including lung-protective mechanical ventilation, vasopressor support, and fluid resuscitation, the patient developed progressive multiple organ dysfunction. Because of severe acute kidney injury, markedly elevated inflammatory markers, hyperlactatemia, hemodynamic instability, and persistent anuria despite fluid resuscitation and vasopressor support, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was initiated approximately 5 h after ICU admission.

Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage was performed for airway management and microbiological evaluation. In this case, conventional BALF culture referred to routine bacterial culture and did not include specialized Legionella culture; no bacterial growth was observed. Legionella urinary antigen testing was not performed because it was unavailable at our institution. Given the rapidly progressive course and nondiagnostic initial microbiological findings, lower respiratory tract sampling was pursued to clarify the etiology. BALF was sent for tNGS on September 15, and the result was reported on September 16. The tNGS workflow included DNA extraction from BALF, library preparation using a sequencing reaction preparation kit, high-throughput sequencing on the MGISEQ−200 platform, and bioinformatic analysis. The assay identified L. pneumophila with a normalized read count of 63,814 and an estimated microbial load exceeding 10⁶ copies/mL (Table 2). No other clinically significant pathogens were reported within the assay detection range. The high organism-specific read abundance in a lower respiratory tract specimen, together with rapidly progressive pneumonia, negative routine cultures, absence of alternative pathogens within the assay range, and compatible radiological findings, supported the clinical relevance of this result. Based on these findings, antimicrobial therapy was adjusted to azithromycin combined with moxifloxacin, and unnecessary broad-spectrum antimicrobial agents were discontinued.

Despite pathogen-directed antimicrobial adjustment and ongoing organ support, the patient’s respiratory status continued to deteriorate, with worsening hypoxemia. Because of her critical condition, transportation for CT was not feasible, and a bedside chest radiography was performed on September 16, 2025. The image revealed diffuse bilateral pulmonary opacities, predominantly involving the middle and lower lung fields, with extensive consolidation and coalescent infiltrates, indicating progressive lung injury consistent with diffuse alveolar damage (Fig. 2).

Bedside chest radiograph on September 16, 2025. Follow-up bedside radiography after antimicrobial adjustment showed diffuse bilateral patchy and confluent opacities, predominantly in the middle and lower lung fields, indicating extensive consolidation and progressive diffuse lung injury.

However, despite pathogen-directed antimicrobial therapy and comprehensive organ support, the patient’s condition continued to deteriorate, with persistent hypoxemia, refractory shock, and worsening hepatic and hematologic dysfunction. On September 19, the patient developed deep coma and experienced cardiac arrest. Although return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after cardiopulmonary resuscitation, she remained critically unstable and fully dependent on high-dose vasopressors and mechanical ventilation. After repeated discussions regarding prognosis, the patient’s family elected to withdraw life-sustaining treatment, and the patient was discharged against medical advice on the same day. Discussion

Severe clinical course and host vulnerability in Legionella pneumonia

Legionella pneumophila is an important cause of severe community-acquired pneumonia and may lead to rapidly progressive respiratory failure, septic shock, acute kidney injury, and multi-organ dysfunction, particularly in patients with reduced physiological reserve [9], [11]. Older age, chronic lung disease, and renal dysfunction are clinically relevant risk factors for severe disease and unfavorable outcomes. In the present case, the patient had several predisposing conditions, including advanced age, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease requiring long-term oxygen therapy, bronchial asthma, and stage 4 chronic kidney disease. These comorbidities likely contributed to rapid clinical deterioration after the onset of infection.

The patient developed profound hypoxemia shortly after admission, with a PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio consistent with severe ARDS, followed by circulatory failure requiring vasopressor support and acute kidney injury requiring continuous renal replacement therapy. The markedly elevated inflammatory markers, high SOFA score, and high APACHE II score at ICU admission reflected severe systemic involvement rather than isolated pulmonary infection. Although anti-Legionella therapy was adjusted after BALF tNGS identified L. pneumophila, the patient had already developed severe ARDS, septic shock, and multi-organ dysfunction. This clinical course suggests that, in vulnerable patients with rapidly progressive severe pneumonia, delayed etiological confirmation may limit the potential benefit of pathogen-directed therapy once advanced organ failure has occurred.

Diagnostic challenges of Legionella pneumonia

Early diagnosis of Legionella pneumonia remains challenging in routine clinical practice. Clinical manifestations are often nonspecific and overlap with those of other causes of severe pneumonia, especially in critically ill patients. Conventional diagnostic methods also have important limitations. Culture requires specialized media and laboratory expertise and may be delayed or falsely negative, while serological testing is less useful for early decision-making because diagnostic antibody responses may appear late in the disease course [7], [9], [12]. Urinary antigen testing is rapid and widely used, but it mainly detects L. pneumophila serogroup 1 and may miss infections caused by other serogroups or species [7], [12]. Therefore, negative or unavailable conventional tests do not exclude Legionella infection, particularly in patients with rapidly progressive severe pneumonia.

In the present case, initial microbiological investigations were nondiagnostic. Blood culture, sputum culture, and routine BALF bacterial culture were negative, specialized Legionella culture was not performed, and urinary antigen testing was unavailable. Empirical broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy was initiated for severe CAP, and moxifloxacin was added to cover atypical pathogens. However, before the BALF tNGS result became available, there was no definitive etiological evidence supporting L. pneumophila as the causative pathogen. This diagnostic uncertainty limited early pathogen-specific interpretation of the clinical course and antimicrobial strategy [9]. BALF tNGS subsequently identified L. pneumophila with high read abundance and no other clinically significant pathogens within the assay range, providing microbiological evidence that supported targeted anti-Legionella therapy and de-escalation of unnecessary broad-spectrum agents.

Clinical role of BALF tNGS in severe pneumonia with nondiagnostic conventional testing

In patients with severe pneumonia, timely identification of the causative pathogen is essential for antimicrobial optimization, but conventional microbiological methods may be negative, delayed, or unable to detect atypical and difficult-to-culture organisms. Sequencing-based assays have increasingly been used as adjunctive diagnostic tools in this setting. Compared with conventional culture-based approaches, BALF tNGS may provide rapid microbiological evidence for pathogens included in the assay panel and may be particularly useful when lower respiratory tract infection remains unexplained after routine testing [13], [14].

In the present case, BALF tNGS provided clinically relevant etiological evidence after blood culture, sputum culture, and routine BALF bacterial culture were negative, specialized Legionella culture was not performed, and urinary antigen testing was unavailable. The assay identified L. pneumophila with a normalized read count of 63,814 and an estimated microbial load exceeding 10⁶ copies/mL. The detection of a high organism-specific signal in a lower respiratory tract specimen, together with rapidly progressive pneumonia, compatible radiological findings, septic shock, and the absence of other clinically significant pathogens within the assay range, supported L. pneumophila as the likely etiological pathogen rather than an incidental finding.

The clinical value of this result was not limited to pathogen identification. It provided a microbiological basis for adjusting antimicrobial therapy toward anti-Legionella-active treatment with azithromycin and moxifloxacin and for discontinuing unnecessary broad-spectrum agents. In critically ill patients, earlier use of BALF tNGS may shorten the period of diagnostic uncertainty and help align antimicrobial therapy with the causative pathogen. However, in this patient, severe ARDS, septic shock, acute kidney injury, and multi-organ dysfunction were already present at ICU admission; therefore, it remains uncertain whether earlier etiological confirmation would have altered the final outcome.

In this case, the BALF tNGS result was interpreted as part of the overall diagnostic picture rather than as an isolated laboratory finding [13]. Several features supported the etiological relevance of L. pneumophila: the organism was detected in a lower respiratory tract specimen, the normalized read count was high, the estimated microbial load exceeded 10⁶ copies/mL, the patient had rapidly progressive severe pneumonia with septic shock, the radiological findings were compatible with extensive pulmonary infection, and no alternative clinically significant pathogen was identified by conventional testing or within the assay range. Nevertheless, targeted assays are constrained by their predefined pathogen panels and cannot fully exclude organisms outside the detection range [15]. Therefore, BALF tNGS should be used as a complementary diagnostic tool and interpreted together with conventional microbiological findings and bedside clinical judgment [14], [15].

Clinical implications

This case highlights the need to consider Legionella pneumophila early in patients with severe community-acquired pneumonia who show rapid clinical deterioration, particularly when respiratory failure, septic shock, acute kidney injury, or marked systemic inflammation develops. Older patients with chronic lung disease or renal dysfunction may have limited physiological reserve and may progress rapidly to multi-organ dysfunction. In such patients, empirical antimicrobial therapy should include appropriate coverage for atypical pathogens when Legionella infection is clinically suspected, while microbiological investigations are pursued in parallel [9], [16].

The case also illustrates the practical diagnostic value of lower respiratory tract sequencing when conventional microbiological testing is nondiagnostic. In this patient, routine cultures failed to identify a causative pathogen, urinary antigen testing was unavailable, and specialized Legionella culture was not performed. BALF tNGS subsequently identified L. pneumophila with high organism-specific read abundance and no alternative clinically significant pathogen within the assay range, providing evidence that supported anti-Legionella-active antimicrobial adjustment. Therefore, this case shows that, in severe pneumonia with nondiagnostic conventional microbiological testing, BALF tNGS can provide clinically actionable etiological evidence and support more focused antimicrobial decision-making [13], [14].

At the same time, the unfavorable outcome in this case emphasizes that pathogen identification alone may not reverse the clinical course once severe ARDS, septic shock, and multi-organ dysfunction have already developed. Sequencing-based diagnostics should therefore be integrated into the broader clinical workflow rather than viewed as a stand-alone test. Its results are most informative when assessed together with specimen source, organism burden, radiological findings, conventional microbiology, antimicrobial exposure, and the patient’s overall clinical trajectory [13], [15].

Conclusion

Severe Legionella pneumophila pneumonia can progress rapidly to ARDS, septic shock, and multi-organ dysfunction, particularly in older patients with major comorbidities. This case highlights the importance of considering Legionella infection early in severe community-acquired pneumonia with rapid clinical deterioration and nondiagnostic initial microbiological testing. In this patient, BALF tNGS provided clinically actionable etiological evidence after conventional testing failed to identify the causative pathogen and supported adjustment toward anti-Legionella-active antimicrobial therapy. The unfavorable outcome also suggests that pathogen identification should be integrated early into the clinical workflow, because its potential benefit may be limited once severe organ dysfunction has already developed. BALF tNGS should therefore be regarded as a complementary diagnostic tool that can help reduce etiological uncertainty and support antimicrobial decision-making in severe pneumonia when routine testing does not provide a clear answer.
