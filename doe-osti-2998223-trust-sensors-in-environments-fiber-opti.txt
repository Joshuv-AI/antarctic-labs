1 Introduction
The objective of the Delivery Environments (DE) Testbeds to Reduce Uncertainty in Simulations
and Tests (TRUST) project is to quantify and help increase confidence in specific areas of computa-
tional and experimental capabilities that are applicable to the development, on-target assessment,
and qualification of current and future delivery environments [1]. More complete quantification of
confidence in experimental and computational capabilities and the sufficient increase of confidence
in those capabilities is critical to improving weapons engineering design, qualification, and assess-
ment efforts that are critical to the current and future stockpile. Under the TRUST work package,
engineers use and provide feedback on analysis tools and experimental results and create databases
for efficient and responsive engineering which are currently under development.

This report focuses on experimental, modeling/simulation, and analysis efforts conducted in FY25
relevant to the TRUST Sensors in Environments: Fiber Optic Displacement (SE-FOD) testbed.
This testbed is focused on identifying and reducing sources of uncertainty in in situ displacement
measurements measured via fiber optic cables on long term aging studies conducted by V-14 in
characteristic stockpile-to-target environments. Both the SE-FOD stepped and sliding test stages
were first developed in FY21 [2, 3]. In FY22, it was theorized that the vibration from the thermal
conditioning unit (TCU) could be a source of uncertainty in the displacement measurements [4].
In FY23, experiments were conducted to understand the effect of vibration on the testbed with
preliminary analysis indicating that vibrations caused by the TCU do indeed result in noisier FOD
measurements than when the TCU is turned off [5]. In Fiscal Year (FY) 2024, expanded analysis
was conducted of experimental measurements of the stepped test stage of the SE-FOD testbed. The
primary goal of this analysis was to better understand the variability of fiber optic displacement
(FOD) gauges across environmental temperatures and step distances [6]. FY24 results indicated
that variability of FOD measurements was very low for tests conducted at room temperature or
in a thermal chamber when the unit was turned off, while variability increased dramatically for
experiments conducted in a thermal chamber running at elevated temperatures.

2 Background
To provide a background on how fiber optic displacement sensors work, an independent document
[7] was created. This document describes the physics behind, and the necessary signal processing
for, distance-to-target measurements with fiber optic interferometry. This section consists of a
reproduction of this document

2.1 Basic Overview
The general objective of the approach is a precise, non-contact measurement of distance through
extrinsic Fabry-Perot interferometry (EFPI). To achieve this, a light signal is passed through an
optical fiber, out from a fixed tip in space. This tip acts as a splitter, reflecting some of the light
back through the fiber. The transmitted light then travels across the gap distance of interest and
is reflected back from a planar surface normal to the fiber axis. The light re-enters the fiber tip
and interferes with the light that was previously reflected from the fiber tip. The key to the gap
measurement is the interference of the two reflected waves; one returned internally from the optical
fiber tip and the other (semi) extrinsic wave that has completed the round-trip journey across the
gap. When measuring intensity as a function of optical wavelength, the signal will spike when an
integer number of complete wavelengths can fill the gap distance. Thus, when measuring intensity
as a function of wavenumber (which shall be defined later), a sinusoidal relation appears as seen in
Figure 1.

 Figure 1: Measured intensity vs. wavenumber [9]

I and the gap distance x is formed from the simple interaction of two waves.

2.2 Experimental Setup and Analysis
This section covers the hardware used in the current setup and the analysis algorithm used to deter-
mine the gap distance. A Python code was developed to provide a concrete example for the analysis
algorithm and is available in Appendix A.

The optical interrogator used is the National Instruments PXIe-4844. It provides a sweep over a
wavelength range from 1510 nm to 1590 nm at discrete values of wavelength, uniformly spaced at
∆λ = 4.0pm. The PXIe-4844 is capable of gathering up to 10 full spectrum sweeps per second (10
Hz). PXIe-4844 cards have 4 slots to measure 4 fiber optic readings simultaneously; however, more
sensors are often desired. A fiber optic switch made by Luna Innovations (LUNA FOS) is used
to increase the number of possible sensors. The LUNA FOS allows the user to sequentially cycle
between channels, enabling serial readings of up to 36 fiber optic cables to be routed to a single
port in the PXIe-4844. Multi-channel cable bundles are made by Timbercon, Inc. These cables,
shown in Figure 2, contain multiple fiber optic strands and fan out to individual, single channel
cables near the ends.

  2. Pre-process signal

 (b) Apply window, e.g., Hann

  3. Compute the single-sided and real-valued Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), F{I}

  4. Find the array index of the peak value of I

  5. Calculate x directly from the peak location

To reduce noise [9], signal wavelength, λ is first converted to wavenumber, k, using Eq. 2. How-
ever, since this transformation is non-linear, the uniform sample spacing in wavelength becomes
nonuniform in wavenumber, which will present problems later when calculating the FFT. To force
a uniform sample spacing, the simplest approach is to interpolate the vector I(λi ) to uniformly
spaced wavenumber values, I(ki ). A simple scheme of table-lookup linear interpolation is employed,
however, we want to oversample (i.e. take more points than originally measured) to ensure we are
getting all the information originally presented. Be aware that sampling more points will decrease
the spatial resolution (discussed in Section 2.4). Due to the presence of numerical artifacts, it is
best practice to interpolate using a nonuniform spread of λ values that will become a uniformly
spaced k array (i.e. interpolate in λ-space).

The DC offset is then subtracted from the signal and a Hann window [10] is applied to reduce
spectral leakage and improve spectral resolution. Finally, the end of the signal is zero-padded to
(a) increase signal length and (b) force a length that is a power of 2. Increasing signal length via
zero-padding enables smaller frequency bins in the FFT and acts as a kind of interpolation in the

frequency domain. Note that since no more samples were taken, there will be no reduction in noise
or bias in the measurement. Computationally, FFTs are faster when the signal is a power of 2, so
using this zero-padding to force a specific signal size can speed up the computation.

Mathematically, FFTs are represented on the complex plane. However, for this analysis, the primary
interest is in the apparent power of the signal. From the FFT, the power spectral density (PSD) is
calculated. The PSD shows which frequencies are the strongest in the signal, allowing us to perform
peak finding and determine the dominant frequency. The location of this peak is assumed to be
fgap , from which we can directly calculate the gap distance via Eq. 4. Currently the peak finding
algorithm looks for the maximum value of the PSD.

2.3 Advanced Signal Processing: Waveform Averaging and Filtering Incorrect
 Values
Due to various environmental factors, there can often be a significant amount of noise in the received
signal. If the signal to noise ratio is too small, this can lead to an incorrect peak identified in the PSD
graph. To mitigate that, multiple frequency scans can be taken to create a single measurement.
These scans should be analyzed independently, and their PSD waveforms should be averaged to
minimize the noise. Note that if there is a good signal to noise ratio in the scans, then averaging
multiple scans will not have much effect, since the peak finding algorithm will be able to find the
correct peak with a single waveform. From the raw optical signal (Figure 3(a)), it is observed that
there is the expected sinusoid, an additional low frequency sin wave, and a “slope” of the total
waveform which, when calculating the PSD (Figure 3(b)), results in two peaks at lower frequencies
(in the figure, frequency is calculated as gap distance). This slope is a result of the intensity loss
along a bent optical fiber. Signal loss along bent fiber optic cables is dependent on the optical
wavelength [11, 12]. To remove the peak (which may vary slightly throughout the experiment), a
masking filter is placed on the signal which removes frequencies outside of the expected displacement
range of the test article, therefore hiding this erroneous peak.

 • Further development of the analysis process, including a curve-fitting algorithm based on the

3 Development of LabVIEW Code
As described in Section 2, raw measurements are taken on a National Instruments PXIe device.
Measurement and data processing is performed via LabVIEW code created in-house. As this code
is still in the development stage, there were a number of unresolved issues. The most predominant
issues were:

  1. Inefficiencies which slow down measurement speed

  2. Hardcoded values, which disabled resolution changes

  3. General disorganization, making updates nearly impossible

As a result, efficiency improvements, user interface changes, and function additions were hard to
implement on the previous iteration of the data acquisition code. A complete, ground-up rewrite of
the code was completed in FY25. This rewrite reduced the complexity while maintaining all impor-
tant features of the original code. One feature removed from the previous code was a curve fitting
method that was nominally used for greater precision of the gap distance; however, this method
was not proven and added additional unnecessary complications to the signal processing algorithm.
One consequence of this change is that much of the data now appears to be from a discrete set –
this is due to the Discrete Fourier Transformation applied during signal processing. The sensor res-
olution now matches the theoretical resolution determined in Section 2.4. Additionally, hardcoded
variables were removed, and there is now minimal processing overhead when taking measurements.
This will greatly reduce development time for any additional signal processing steps that need to
be implemented, for example, implementation of the correction polynomial described in Section 5.

This new LabVIEW code was created and implemented in the current FY. Thus, all tests completed
in FY25 use the new code. Removal of the curve fitting method also enabled the distance resolution
to match the calculated resolution from Eq. 6.

4 Variability in Sensor Placement
There is a large amount of variability in the sensor mounting location, which is observed as vari-
ance in measurements between sensors that are measuring the same nominal distance. There are a
few causes for this variability, including a hand-tight threaded region to mount the sensors, and a
quick-disconnect connector between two fiber bundles which make up the entire sensor assembly.

Tests 41–43 were conducted to measure the variability from the setup process. Measurements were
taken from the back side of the testbed, such that every sensor recorded measurements of the same
distance. Figure 4 shows the orientation of the testbed during testing. Sensors are mounted to the
top of the mounting plate (cross section shown in blue), and measure the distance to the target
surface, in this case the back of the testbed. This enables every sensor to read the same nominal
distance, with the only variable being their respective mounting. Multiple measurements, here de-
scribed as different tests, were taken. Between tests, disassembly and reassembly operations were
performed as described in Table 2, which enabled assessment of the variability of measurements due
to assembly.

6 Assessment of Vibrations in the Thermal Chamber
In previous years, sensor noise was observed when making measurements during operation in the
Thermotron SE-600 chambers [4–6]. It has been hypothesized that the source of measurement
spread was due to vibrations in the thermal chamber. To assess the effect of thermal chamber
vibrations on FOD measurement, a triaxial accelerometer (PCB Electronics, Model 356A01) was
placed on the testbed during measurement. The accelerometer model has a broadband resolution
of 0.03 sm2 rms. A thermal cycle in an SE-600 chamber was performed that went between -50℃ and
80℃, which was within the operational limits of the accelerometer. Accelerometer measurements
were taken at 1000 Hz. A moving root-mean-square filter with a window of approximately 5 seconds
was then used on the accelerometer data to show the presence of the acceleration. This signal was
compared to the FOD measurements. Figure 8 shows the measured acceleration and temperature
over the thermal profile as well as a single FOD measurement.

Figure 8: Air temperature measurements plotted with RMS-filtered accelerometer measurements and FOD measurements over
 the duration of Test 57.

If vibrations were the cause of noise in the FOD measurements, we would expect to (a) see noise in
the sensor measurements during chamber cooling, and (b) observe vibrations with the accelerome-

thermal chamber. Based on this preliminary investigation and analysis, we are not able to detect
any specific acceleration signal that correlates to an increase in noise, which is evidence against past
hypotheses about chamber vibration.

7 Conclusion
Fiber optic displacement sensors have seen a lot of turnover during their lifetime at LANL, so this
FY resulted in compiling and reporting on knowledge about the basic operation, signal processing,
etc. In this report, the basic function and signal processing required to operate FOD sensors is
documented. This has resulted in a rewrite of the data acquisition program which simplifies and
expedites measurement, enabling future developments to the acquisition of distance measurements
with this technique. Significant effort has gone into quantifying and eliminating sources of error. It
was shown that there was approximately 0.2 mm variability in sensor placement - the majority of
this error could be corrected by performing an offset correction before measurement. This enables
more accurate distance measurements with the FOD sensors. By performing multiple distance
measurements on the stepped testbed, sensor nonlinearity was observed, leading to bias in mea-
surements. A polynomial correction function is applied to the raw measurements, reduced the bias
uncertainty, and therefore the total uncertainty. Overall, by including both a zero-offset procedure
and this calibration, the error in measurement was reduced from a 0.062 mm standard deviation to
0.013 mm standard deviation. In previous years, it was hypothesized that vibrations in the thermal
chamber were a significant cause of bimodal noise. An initial investigation was unable to prove this
theory, potentially indicating vibrations are not the cause of bimodal noise. It is hypothesized that
the observed noise is instead caused by currents of heated air off the testbed.

Under TRUST, the testbed for SE-FOD testbed is scheduled for completion after FY25. A bulleted
summary of important factors to consider during operation of FOD sensors is below.

 sensor technique requires that the sensor end partially reflect light back into the fiber. The

        2. MB-951012-134-044-343-006F

7.3 Future Works
FOD sensors are most often used in situ under thermal profiles that typically operate between
-50℃ and 80℃; however, the currently proposed calibration method is defined only for a single
temperature. It is expected that the required correction is a function of temperature, so additional
calibration routines will need to be established to encompass the range of operating environments
for FOD sensors. This would likely also include evaluation of uncertainty in the measurements, due
to the increased noise at elevated temperatures.
