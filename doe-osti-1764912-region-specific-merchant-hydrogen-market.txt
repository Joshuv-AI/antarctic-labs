 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Utilities need to recover value from baseload and some renewable assets even when the
 electricity is curtailed. The declining cost of renewables (particularly solar), the persistently low-
 cost of natural gas, and the declining electricity demand, have all worked to lower the price of
 electricity. Furthermore, the baseload assets have a high turndown cost, which is not reflected in
 the low market electricity prices. One option for utilizing the curtailed electricity is hydrogen
 generation. One important aspect of this project will be to provide a “go/no-go” recommendation
 on the economic feasibility of pursuing hydrogen generation vs. other markets that could offtake
 curtailed electricity or nuclear steam.

 This project will specifically assess the opportunities for hydrogen production and use in the
 service territories of Southern Company Services (SCS), Exelon BSC, and Xcel Energy. Each
 utility partner represents a different mix of generation fleets: Exelon has significant nuclear
 assets, Xcel is primarily renewables (wind) with some nuclear, and SCS represents both a
 renewables fleet and a heavily baseload fleet, which includes significant nuclear.

 The Contractors will conduct the study of hydrogen market opportunities by first considering the
 approximate electricity demand and price profiles for the year, and power generation capacity
 that can be used to produce hydrogen. Therefore, a profile for hydrogen generation will also be
 developed to better understand the quantity and timing of hydrogen production. Once the
 hydrogen production schedule is estimated, hydrogen storage requirements can be determined.
 This effort will provide sufficient information to make a go/no-go decision on moving ahead
 with a detailed regional case study for hydrogen production in any of the three utility areas.

 Summary of Research Results:

 TASK 1: Identify, validate, and rank regional hydrogen
 markets (relative to utility service territories.)
 TASK 1 DESCRIPTION:
 This Task will build on three previous efforts conducted by national labs: INL analysis of
 industrial hydrogen markets, NREL nationwide hydrogen potential market study, and ANL
 market assessment for hydrogen with low CO2 footprint. The Task will also build on work by
 Exelon to identify potential hydrogen customers. Deliverables: 1) hydrogen demand curves for
 each utility service territory, and 2) list of market opportunities (quantity, purity, cost, etc.) in
 each utility service territory

 TASK 1 SUMMARY AND OUTCOME:
 ANL led the effort to identify and quantify demand opportunities. A number of options were
 considered and the results for each location were used to develop demand curves. Further details
 follow.

 1.1 Petroleum Refineries
 Petroleum refineries are currently the most significant user of hydrogen in the U.S., consuming
 10 million metric tonnes (MMT) of hydrogen annually (EIA 2018c). Of the 10 MMT of
 hydrogen used by petroleum refineries, approximately 6-7 MMT is provided by dedicated
 hydrogen production sources such as SMR, while the remaining ~3 MMT of hydrogen is
 produced as a by-product mainly from naphtha reformers in refineries. The hydrogen is used
 primarily for hydrocracking and hydrotreating where hydrocracking is used to produce diesel
 from heavy crude, and hydrotreating is used to remove sulfur from feed, intermediate, and
 product streams. Most hydrotreating capacity is used for reducing sulfur in diesel, treating the
 fluid catalytic cracker feed, and treating naphtha. Refinery hydrogen demand is essentially driven
 by the ratio of gasoline to diesel production, API gravity, sulfur content of the petroleum inputs,
 and complexity of refinery processing.

 Elgowainy et al. (2019) estimated future hydrogen demand for petroleum refining based on
 projections of crude inputs and market demand for refinery products from the EIA “Annual
 Energy Outlook” (EIA 2017a), and crude API gravity and sulfur content, based on Han et al.’s
 analysis (2015). The main features are that crude inputs increase from 16 to 18 million barrels
 per day (with a steeper increase of 9% from 2015 to 2021 and then more gradual to 2050),
 gasoline output decreases from 8 to 6 million barrels per day, diesel output increase slightly, and
 average jet-fuel output increases roughly half a million barrels per day from about 1.7 to 2.2
 million barrels per day (EIA 2017a).

 Future refinery hydrogen requirements are estimated based on linear regression of refineries
 characterized by Elgowainy et al. (2014) with crude API, crude sulfur content, gasoline-to-
 distillate ratio, and liquefied petroleum gas to total product ratio as the explanatory variables.
 The dataset includes 43 large refineries (each with capacity >100,000 bbl/d) in four Petroleum

 10

 Administration for Defense districts (PADDs) covering over 70% of U.S. refining capacity.
 Elgowainy et al. (2019) describe the details of the regression model and its valid range.

 Hydrogen demand by refineries is projected to increase through 2030 due to an increased ratio of
 diesel and gasoline demand, stringent sulfur requirements, higher API gravity and sulfur content
 for petroleum feedstocks, and increased petroleum inputs. This demand is estimated using the
 regression model for each of the PADDs and then allocated to individual refineries within each
 PADD based on the petroleum processing capacity of each refinery within the PADD.

 1.2 Ammonia Producers
 Ammonia is produced by the Haber-Bosch process which reacts hydrogen, usually produced
 from natural gas via the SMR process and with nitrogen from the air. In 2016, 14 MMT of
 ammonia were consumed in the United States (USGS 2017), with 12% of this for non-
 agricultural products and the remainder used to produce fertilizer products including anhydrous
 ammonia, urea, diammonium phosphate, monoammonium phosphate, and nitric acid. The
 Haber-Bosch process uses hydrogen in a molar ratio of 3 moles H2 to 2 moles of NH3; therefore,
 0.178 kilograms of hydrogen are required per kilogram of ammonia. As ammonia is the source
 of nitrogen in other fertilizer products, we can generalize this as 0.216 kgH2/kgN2 in fertilizer.

 The locations and scale of ammonia production are estimated using plant capacities (Ammonia
 Industry 2018) and assuming the national average capacity utilization rate of 80%. Future
 production by location through 2024 is estimated based on announced plans for capacity
 expansion by facility, which include a 40% expansion from 2019 to 2024 (Ammonia Industry
 2018). Currently 40% of the ammonia required for fertilizer products used in the U.S. is
 imported, so this expansion reflects the expectation that domestic production will displace
 imports in the U.S. market. At the national level, Elgowainy et al. (2019) estimate that after
 2024, U.S. ammonia production will increase through 2050 at a modest rate of 1% per year. This
 projection is based on decreasing domestic demand for nitrogen fertilizer for corn, relatively
 stable domestic demand for nitrogen fertilizer for other products and increasing domestic
 production displacing nitrogen-fertilizer imports into the U.S. market.

 1.3 Synthetic Fuel Production
 Significant quantities of high-purity carbon dioxide are generated by both the ethanol-production
 process and the SMR process, used to produce hydrogen from natural gas for refining, ammonia
 production, and other purposes. This high-purity carbon dioxide presents an opportunity for the
 production of synthetic fuels such as methanol, Fischer-Tropsch diesel, or dimethyl ether, while
 avoiding the cost and energy penalty to capture CO2 from other sources (e.g., coal and natural-
 gas power plants). In particular, methanol presents an opportunity as its manufacturing process is
 relatively simple, and its global market is expected to grow related to its use as a petrochemical
 feedstock, fuel-blending component, or a blendstock for transportation fuel. Methanol produced
 from waste carbon dioxide streams with renewable hydrogen offers a low-carbon alternative to
 methanol produced via the conventional process using fossil natural gas. The merchant market
 for carbon dioxide is currently saturated. Of the 100 MMT of carbon dioxide generated from
 ethanol production and steam methane reforming, only 14 MMT are currently available to the
 merchant market and 11 MMT are used for food processing, carbonated beverages, and other

 11

 uses (Supekar and Skerlos 2014). This leaves a significant amount that could be used for
 synthetic fuel production.

 The hydrogen demand for synthetic-fuel production can be estimated based on the 1:3 mole ratio
 of CO2 to H2 that is required for the synthesis of Fischer-Tropsch diesel or dimethyl ether. The
 availability of high-purity carbon dioxide from SMR associated with merchant hydrogen and
 ammonia production and the locations of facilities are based on values reported by facilities to
 the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program (EPA 2017).
 High-purity carbon dioxide from ethanol production is estimated based on the 1:1 mole ratio of
 ethanol to carbon dioxide generated during the conversion of glucose and sucrose in the
 fermentation process. In 2017, 15.6 billion gallons of ethanol was produced in the United States
 (EIA 2018d) which generates an estimated 44 MMT of carbon dioxide. The locations and
 capacities of ethanol-production facilities are based on an EIA dataset (EIA 2018d), and
 production by facility is estimated based on the national average capacity-utilization rate. The
 total potential hydrogen demand to produce synthetic fuels from high-purity carbon dioxide
 sources is significant, at 14 MMT, comprising the production of 6.0 MMT of ethanol, 5.9 MMT
 of ammonia, and 2.1 MMT from merchant hydrogen production. While most ethanol production
 is clustered in Midwestern states, ammonia plants are located in a broader area, mainly in the
 Midwest, Gulf Coast, and southeastern states, and other merchant hydrogen plants are located
 near petroleum refineries, mostly along the Gulf Coast, and near San Francisco, Los Angeles,
 Chicago, Detroit, Minneapolis, and St. Louis.

 1.4 Direct Reduction of Iron
 The direct reduction of iron (DRI) is a method for producing high-purity iron from ore at
 temperatures below the melting point of iron by driving off oxygen in a reactor with a hydrogen
 and carbon monoxide gas environment. The DRI process reduces energy use and carbon dioxide
 emissions associated with steel production compared with the basic oxygen furnace (BOF)
 technology, which uses coking coal to smelt iron. In the U.S., the amount of steel produced by
 electric-arc furnace (EAF) has increased and is expected to continue to grow while the amount
 produced by BOF is expected to remain relatively flat due to the increased production of scrap,
 which can be incorporated in the EAF feed. Product quality dictates the amount of scrap which
 can be used in an EAF, and the remainder must be made up with pig iron from a BOF or DRI.
 Due to the high purity of DRI, it has the potential to increase the amount of scrap which can be
 used at an EAF relative to using pig iron from a BOF. The DRI with hydrogen requires
 approximately 100 kg hydrogen per metric tonne of iron—i.e., a mass ratio of 10%.

 Renewable hydrogen could be used to offset natural gas or other fuels in the DRI process. For
 this analysis, we estimate the potential hydrogen demand for DRI based on using 30% hydrogen
 and 70% natural gas on an energy basis (Chevrier 2018). The locations and scale of potential
 hydrogen use for DRI are estimated based on total steel-production capacity, national average
 utilization rates for BOFs and EAFs (USGS 2018), and national average DRI feedstock shares.
 In 2015, BOFs and EAFs used 2.4 MMT and 1.7 MMT of DRI, respectively (USGS 2018). Thus
 we estimate maximum potential of current hydrogen demand for the DRI at 0.24 MMT for BOF
 and 0.17 MMT for EAF (assuming 10% mass ratio of H2/steel). Future demand for hydrogen for
 DRI is based on the assumption of constant production by facility and replacing all pig iron feed
 with DRI. In this case, hydrogen demand sees the greatest increase at the locations of current

 12

 BOFs, where we estimate an additional 2.0 MMT of hydrogen could be used for DRI (assuming
 30/70 H2/natural gas mix for DRI). These estimates are conservative relative to the national
 estimate of potential future hydrogen demand of 4 MMT for 30% replacement of natural gas on
 an energy basis (Elgowainy et al. 2019). Those estimates were based on the Annual Energy
 Outlook projection of 50% growth in U.S. steel production by 2040 and full replacement of iron
 inputs from DRI.

 1.5 Co-combustion with Natural Gas
 One potential use of renewable hydrogen is its injection into natural gas pipelines for use as a
 low-carbon component of a natural gas-hydrogen fuel mix. Elgowainy et al. (2019) discussed the
 potential and barriers to this use of hydrogen. As a scenario for the purposes of this study, we
 estimate potential demand for hydrogen, assuming it can be used by natural-gas electricity-
 generating units with a ratio of 30% hydrogen with 70% natural gas by volume. This is
 consistent with gas turbine OEMs general agreement that current technology can utilize up to
 30vol% hydrogen. Electricity generators were identified using the datasets from the EIA-860 and
 EIA-923 forms describing electricity-generators’ facility locations and fuel use.

 1.6 Fuel-cell Vehicles
 Hydrogen demand for fuel-cell vehicles (FCVs) is expected to increase as FCVs penetrate the
 market. Currently, FCVs are a small share of the vehicle fleet and are concentrated in California
 due to the presence of hydrogen-fueling infrastructure and other state incentives for fuel-cell
 vehicle purchases. Elgowainy et al. (2019) estimate future hydrogen demand for FCVs in three
 steps: 1) apply FCV shares of vehicle sales by class each year to EIA forecasts of vehicle sales
 by class for each year; 2) allocate national FCV sales by class for each year to states based on
 regional vehicle-sales forecasts adjusted to account for regional FCV targets; and 3) apply
 estimates of regional vehicle lifetimes, usage, and fuel economy to estimate FCV stocks and
 hydrogen demand by state. These estimates considered four classes of vehicles: cars, light-duty
 trucks, medium-duty trucks, and heavy-duty trucks. For cars and light-duty trucks, Argonne’s
 VISION model was used to estimate annual vehicle stock by technology and vintage in order to
 estimate regional vehicle-miles traveled and energy use by FCVs. Two scenarios were run for
 cars and light-duty trucks representing low and high potential hydrogen prices, which resulted in
 high and low FCV shares of sales. For this analysis, we used their low-FCV-sales scenario,
 which is based on what they considered a moderate assumption regarding future hydrogen price
 of 2.20 USD/kg for hydrogen production and 5.03 USD/kg for hydrogen at the pump (estimates
 in 2015 U.S. dollars). The regional vehicle forecasts from the VISION model relate to five U.S.
 regions: western, central industrial, eastern/zero emissions vehicle states, central southern, and
 rest of U.S. The Farley Generating Station is located in that “rest of U.S.” region, which includes
 Alabama, Florida, Georgia, and South Carolina, among other states, and is characterized by
 states having no incentives for FCVs or hydrogen-fueling infrastructure and, therefore, low
 projected FCV adoption rates. For medium-duty and heavy-duty vehicles, market share is based
 on the California Air Resources Board’s (CARB’s) 2016 projections of market share of zero-
 emission vehicles, together with assumptions regarding the share of those which are FCVs and
 assumptions regarding the adoption rates in other states. The share’s potential hydrogen demand
 for medium- and heavy-duty vehicles is small compared with that of cars and light duty trucks.
 For this study, we allocated FCV hydrogen-demand estimates by region in Elgowainy et al

 13

 (2019) to states and counties based on population (USCB 2018) and estimated distances from the
 generating stations based on the county center of population (USCB 2015).

 2 ELECTRICITY MARKET
 In the U.S., there are two electricity-market configurations: regulated and deregulated. In
 regulated markets, states oversee utility decisions about the amount of capacity to procure from
 power plants and other resources [7]. In deregulated markets, entities known as regional
 transmission organizations (RTOs) act as electricity suppliers and purchase electricity at
 independently owned power plants to sell to retail customers. Seven RTOs operate across the
 U.S.: the California Independent System Operator (ISO), Southwest Power Pool, Electric
 Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT), Midcontinent ISO, PJM Interconnection (PJM), New
 York ISO, and ISO New England, as illustrated by Figure 7. It should be noted that not all RTOs
 operate in deregulated markets; in other parts of the U.S., the RTOs act only as grid operators
 based on fixed purchase and selling prices.

 For purposes of this study, two key differences are noted. First, for regulated utilities such as
 Southern Company and Xcel in Colorado, the price of electricity is the same regardless of
 location. However, for Xcel in other states and for Exelon, the location as well as the hour must
 be specified to understand the electricity price. Second, regulated utilities work with their states
 to ensure adequate capacity to serve all customers is available. However, in deregulated markets,
 RTOs issue manage a capacity market, in addition to the energy market.

 Historical and projected power prices for specific regions within the operating regions of the
 Southern Company, Exelon, and Xcel Energy were assimilated to allow further analysis into
 business cases for hydrogen produced via both low-temperature high-temperature electrolysis
 (HTE). Through communication with the CRADA partners, a set of scenarios were identified,

 reported price data consist of both NREL-obtained and utility-provided data; details are in

 2.1 Price-duration Curves
 The format identified for electricity prices was a price-duration curve. A price-duration curve
 sorts its data from the largest positive to the most negative numbers. A typical price-duration

 28

 curve has a flat middle section between two tails at the extreme ends of the data set. The data
 collected by NREL was hourly, and each price-duration curve therefore has 8760 data points.
 The PPA-based scenarios did not have a price-duration curve, as the price in each hour was
 equal. Therefore, the formatting of the data into a price-duration curve was only required for
 scenarios which assumed a market-based LMP pricing structure. Because price-duration curves
 are sorted from highest to lowest price hours, the timing and frequency of changes are not
 reported in them. The project team did not use those factors within this analysis.

 2.2 Capacity Market Pricing
 In many restructured electricity markets, capacity payments are made to generators for the
 capacity they have available to produce electricity at key times, regardless of how much
 electricity they actually produce. Such capacity markets are run to ensure reliability of the power
 system by incentivizing entry of new generation and to provide payments to generation that may
 be critical for system reliability but may not be profitable from selling electricity alone.

 Capacity markets are only present in some regions which are part of auction-based wholesale
 electricity markets run by an ISO or RTO. These include, among others, the PJM and
 Midcontinent Independent System Operator (MISO) ISO regions. Exelon and Xcel Energy have
 operating regions that are situated within PJM and MISO controlled regions; therefore, historical
 and recently cleared capacity market prices are available for each and are used in this study. The
 Southern Company is a vertically integrated utility with no auction-based markets (capacity or
 otherwise). Therefore, we excluded capacity payments from all scenarios for the Southern
 Company, as well as from scenarios using a cost-of-service-based PPA structure.

 The annual capacity payment made to generators is based on the amount of capacity the
 generator has agreed to provide to the capacity market (in MW) multiplied by the capacity
 market clearing price (in $/MW-day) times 365 days in the year. Thus, the total capacity
 payment received would be:

 Capacity Submitted × Clearing Price × 365 = Total Capacity Payment

 Generators that provide capacity in the capacity market will receive a daily credit for every day
 of the delivery year, regardless of how much electricity is generated [11].

 Within MISO and other ISOs, variable resources, such as wind, are eligible to bid in a de-rated
 amount of their nameplate capacity because their generation is variable. This de-rating is often
 called the capacity credit and is based on historic performance of wind generators during certain
 peak-demand periods during the year. In MISO, the capacity credit is calculated for each wind
 commercial-pricing node (CPNode) in the MISO system, of which there were 207 at the end of
 2017 (up from 204 in 2016). The historic output at each of these wind CPNodes is tracked over
 the top eight daily peak hours for each year from 2005 to 2017 (or since the windfarm was
 commissioned). The average capacity factor for each CPNode during all 104 hours (8 hours × 13
 years) is then used as a basis for awarding the capacity credit. In the 2018–2019 MISO planning
 year, the average wind capacity credit across the CPNodes within MISO Zone 1 was 18.3% [12].

 The capacity submitted to the capacity markets within PJM and MISO is called unforced
 capacity (UCAP), and it must be less than or equal to the generator’s installed capacity adjusted
 by the equivalent-demand forced-outage rate (EFORd). The EFORd is a measure of the
 probability that a unit will not be available due to forced outages or forced de-ratings when there
 is a demand on the unit to generate [13]. Therefore,

 Unforced Capacity = Installed Capacity × [1 - Equivalent Demand Forced Outage Rate]

 30

 For a nuclear generator the EFORd is usually very small, and in 2015 the U.S. average was 1.1%
 [14]. This small EFORd for nuclear is reflected by historic nuclear UCAP bids. For example, the
 nuclear UCAP submission to the PJM capacity market each year is very close to the actual
 installed capacity of the nuclear fleet, suggesting that most nuclear generators are submitting
 almost 100% of their installed capacity to the PJM capacity market [15].

 Therefore, we recommend using an EFORd of 1.1% for the nuclear generators’ capacity
 payments. Following conversations with Exelon, it was agreed that it can be assumed that any
 unit looking to install hydrogen production will have cleared 100% of bid capacity. For Xcel
 Energy, we recommend using the installed capacity of the Prairie Island Nuclear Power Plant to
 align with Xcel Energy’s high-temperature electrolysis scenario. The installed capacity of the
 Prairie Island plant is 1041 MW.

 4 HYDROGEN PRODUCTION
 Hydrogen can be produced from a number of different technology pathways and diverse
 resources. Today, 95% of the hydrogen produced in the United States is made by natural gas
 SMR in large central plants. SMR is the most economic technology available in most cases,
 primarily owing to an abundance of low-cost natural gas. However, this project strictly covers
 electrolysis—the splitting of water into H2 and O2 using electricity—which is commercially
 available in two technology types, alkaline and PEM. Both of these are low temperature
 technologies. However, the availability of steam connected with a nuclear power plant is driving
 interest in solid oxide electrolysis as it is potentially more efficient when utilizing available
 steam. Thus, this study will look at PEM electrolysis and solid oxide electrolysis.

 These electrolysis types are distinguished by the chemistry and materials of separator in the
 electrolysis cell. Polymer electrolyte membrane (PEM) electrolysis operates at lower

 41

 temperature, whereas solid oxide (ceramic) electrolysis operates at higher temperature. At the
 higher temperatures, less electricity is needed to produce the same hydrogen; however, a high-
 quality heat source must be available. Generally, PEM electrolysis is associated with using grid
 or renewable electricity, while solid oxide electrolysis is assumed to be co-located with a nuclear
 power plant.

 4.1 Steam Methane Reforming

 Figure 15. Generalized Steam Methane Reforming Process Flow Diagram
 Steam methane reforming (SMR) process is the hydrogen production process commonly used at
 the present time for industrial scale hydrogen production. SMR uses hydrocarbon and steam feed
 streams and produces a purified hydrogen product. The carbon and oxygen atoms introduced to
 the process from the hydrocarbon and steam feed streams are released as carbon dioxide. SMR
 process technology is well established and SMR processes can be constructed using conventional
 approaches with capital cost savings achieved from economies-of-scale in large scale
 installations. Further description of the SMR process is provided in the following excerpt from
 [1]:

 The [SMR] process involves a catalytic conversion of the hydrocarbon and steam to
 hydrogen and carbon oxides. Since the process works only with light hydrocarbons that can
 be vaporized completely without carbon formation, the feedstocks used range from methane
 (natural gas) to naphtha to No. 2 fuel oil.

 Natural gas is fed to the plant from the pipeline at a pressure of 450 psia. The gas is
 generally sulfur-free, but odorizers with mercaptans must be cleaned from the gas to prevent
 contamination of the reformer catalyst. The desulfurized natural gas feedstock is mixed with
 process steam to be reacted over a nickel based catalyst contained inside of a system of high
 alloy steel tubes. The reforming reaction is strongly endothermic, and the metallurgy of the
 tubes usually limits the reaction temperature to 1400-1700°F. The flue gas path of the fired
 reformer is integrated with additional boiler surfaces to produce about 700,000 lb/hour
 steam. Of this, about 450,000 lb/hour is superheated to 450 psia and 750°F, to be added to

 42

 the incoming natural gas. Additional steam from the boiler is sent off-site. After the reformer,
 the process gas mixture of CO and H2 passes through a heat recovery step and is fed into a
 water gas shift reactor to produce additional H2.

 The Pressure Swing Adsorption (PSA) process is used for hydrogen purification based on the
 ability to produce high purity hydrogen, low amounts of CO and CO2, and ease of operation.
 Shifted gas is fed directly to the PSA unit where hydrogen is purified up to approximately
 99.6%.

 Life cycle analysis of the SMR process indicates that each kilogram of hydrogen produced in the
 SMR process results in the release of approximately 10 kilograms of carbon dioxide. The total
 carbon dioxide emissions include contributions from natural gas production and transport
 (14.8%), electricity generation (2.5%), SMR plant operations (83.7%), and other minor
 contributions [2]. Eliminating the significant CO2 emissions (and, to a lesser extent, NOx)
 emissions associated with SMR brings an additional cost.

 4.2 Low-temperature Electrolysis
 The study focuses on LTE, based on the emerging PEM technology. Water is split by supplying
 an electrical current to a PEM cell in which the anode and cathode are separated by a solid
 polymer electrolyte [3]. Water is passed over the anode while the anode is supplied with an
 electrical current. The current splits the water into protons (H+) and oxygen (O2). The protons
 pass through the polymer electrolyte to the cathode. At the cathode, the protons reform into
 diatomic hydrogen, H2, and leave the system to be further purified, as needed [3]. The hydrogen
 outlet pressure is 6.9 MPa. Once desired purity is reached, the hydrogen can then be used as fuel.
 Oxygen likewise leaves the electrolysis cell from the anode side of the system, where it may
 have been diluted with air (if a sweep gas is used). For this reason, no practical use for the
 oxygen is considered for LTE hydrogen-production analysis. Figure 16 depicts a generalized
 PEM electrolyzer system.

 Figure 16. Generalized PEM electrolyzer system [3].
 PEM electrolysis has inherent advantages over SMR; however, assigning value to these
 advantages is beyond the scope of this study. As mentioned earlier, hydrogen may be produced
 with lower CO2 or NOx emissions, depending on the emissions associated with the electricity

 43

 generation. Beyond this, electrolyzers may be responsive load on power grids owing to their
 ability to turn on and off within minutes and to cycle output within subseconds [4]. Although this
 study considers centralized hydrogen production, PEM-based LTE is more modular, allowing for
 hydrogen production on-site and avoiding significant infrastructure costs for delivery of
 hydrogen. This feature takes advantage of the near-universal electrical distribution infrastructure.

 The economic potential estimates in this study assumed 50.2 kWh of electricity is required to
 produce 1 kg of hydrogen (46.7 kWh from electrolyzer-stack use and 3.5 kWh from balance-of-
 plant use). These input parameters are consistent with those used in the H2A v3.2019 Future
 Central PEM Electrolysis Case Study [5].

 4.3 High-temperature Electrolysis
 High-temperature steam electrolysis (HTSE) is another type of electrolysis. While LTE uses only
 electrical energy, HTE is able to boost overall efficiencies by utilizing both heat and electricity.
 (Or, considered differently, the energy required to dissociate water is a function of temperature,
 and it requires less energy at higher temperatures typical of solid oxide fuel cells.). Figure 17
 shows a cross-section of a representative planar SOEC stack.

 Figure 17: Cross-section of a cathode-supported planar SOEC stack. The case of co-flow is
                                             depicted [4, 6].
 The cathode-supported cell consists of a three-layer solid structure (composed of porous cathode,
 electrolyte, and porous anode) and an interconnected (separator) plate [7]. An oxygen-ion
 conducting electrolyte (e.g., yttria-stabilized zirconia or scandia-stabilized zirconia) is generally
 used in SOECs [8]. For electrically conducting electrodes, a nickel cermet cathode and a
 perovskite anode such as strontium-doped lanthanum manganite are typically used. The
 interconnect plate separates the process-gas streams; it must also be electrically conducting and
 is usually metallic, constructed, for example, of a ferritic stainless steel. Stack operation typically
 occurs at what is known as the thermoneutral point. At the thermoneutral point, thermal energy
 consumed by the electrolysis reaction precisely matches heat generation via irreversible losses
 [4]. In practical terms, this means that all energy input into the stack is absorbed by the
 electrolysis reaction, meaning zero heat consumption or loss from the environment.

 44

 HTE has an operating range between 600 and 850°C and uses electricity and heat to produce
 hydrogen. Electrolysis efficiency increases at higher operating temperatures, requiring less
 electrical energy. This increased efficiency can lower production costs because the thermal
 energy required is generally less expensive than electrical energy. This assessment focuses on
 oxygen-conducting SOEC-based HTE, which is driven with steam and electricity at a nuclear
 power plant (pressurized light water reactor).

 While no solid oxide electrolysis cell has been deployed at a nuclear power plant, flowsheets
 have been developed previously by INL. Figure 18 is an AspenTech HYSYS [9] process flow
 diagram of the HTE process, with highlights showing low- and high-temperature heat
 recuperation, nuclear process-heat integration, and electric topping heat. Steam would be
 diverted from the nuclear power plant to preheat feedwater for the HTE unit via intermediate
 heat exchangers. Additional electrical heating is used to further boost temperatures. The overall
 energy split of thermal energy vs. electrical topping energy vs. electrical energy for electrolysis
 is generator- and electrolyzer-specific. As a general rule, when working with LWRs, about 7–
 10% of the total energy input into the HTSE process is in the form of thermal energy. Of the 90-
 93% that is electrical energy, about 5–20% may be used for heating, and the remainder of the
 electricity utilized for electrolysis.

 Once feedstock temperature and pressure are sufficient, the feedstock (steam) is run through the
 electrolyzer stacks. In the stacks, electricity is supplied, and the electrolysis process begins to
 take place, splitting the steam into oxygen and hydrogen. During normal operation, a portion of
 the hydrogen produced is recycled back into the stacks to reduce corrosion and pitting inside the
 stacks.

 The SOEC stacks considered in this analysis operate isothermally at 735°C. The stacks as
 assumed to operate at the thermal neutral voltage, which results in adiabatic operation where
 external heat is neither added nor removed to maintain the specified operating temperature. The
 SOEC operating pressure is 0.5 MPa, producing a design capacity of 608 tonnes per day of
 hydrogen at a pressure of 2.1 MPa. Table 7 summarizes the electrolysis-cell conditions applied in
 the analysis.

 Modular Equipment Components
 The SOEC module assemblies, sweep-gas system, hydrogen/steam system, and hydrogen
 purification system are modular system components. The equipment in these sub-process areas
 would be constructed using modular construction techniques in which the modules are mass
 produced at a centralized manufacturing facility, transported to the HTSE process site, and
 connected to the common process and utility equipment. Modular components are installed as
 blocks of fixed capacity units, and large process capacity is achieved through installation of
 parallel units. Use of modular equipment is associated with reductions in engineering,
 installation, and contingency costs [16, 17]. Since capacity increases are achieved through
 installation of parallel units, conventional economies of scale are not realized with modular
 systems, but economies of mass production (cost savings associated with large manufacturing
 volume) can be realized. The cost savings associated with mass production and repeated
 assembly techniques can be quantified using a learning curve relationship:

 𝑃𝑛 = 𝑃1 𝑥 log2 (𝑏)
 Where the unit cost Pn of unit number n decreases from the unit cost of the first unit P1 according
 to the learning rate b, which is expressed as a percentage. In this relation, the cost of unit n
 decreases by an amount equal to (1-b) with every doubling of production. In this analysis a
 learning rate of 90% was assumed for the modular system components; in this case the cost of
 unit number 2 is 90% the cost of unit 1, the cost of unit 4 is 81% the cost of unit 1, etc. The
 assumed learning rate is within the values reported in the literature for the energy sector [18-20].

 Capital cost estimates were prepared for two separate HTSE plant installation cases: First-of-a-
 kind and Nth-of-a-kind. The first-of-a-kind cost estimates were used in the small scale HTSE
 demo scenarios (Exelon Scenario 1) while the Nth-of-a-kind cost estimates were used in the
 large scale HTSE demo scenarios (Southern Company Scenario 2, Exelon Scenario 2, and Xcel
 Energy Scenario 2). The SOEC module (stacks and containing vessels) costs for the first
 modular unit constructed (i.e. modular unit #1 for the first-of-a-kind plant installation) were set

 52

 equal to $50/kWe [21]. The balance of plant equipment costs were added to obtain the full HTSE
 process capital costs.

 First-of-a-kind
 In the First-of-a-kind plant installation case (Exelon Scenario 1), the highest price is paid for the
 first modular HTSE and BoP units purchased and installed. Cost reductions are then realized for
 each subsequent unit installed as a result of learning effects. A larger capacity installation will
 achieve the greatest decreases in the module installed costs since larger installations will benefit
 from increased learning effects associated with the higher number of units installed.

 It is important to note that the total project cost is equal to the cumulative cost of all installed
 units, and in the first-of-a-kind analysis each modular unit has a different cost (each successive
 unit has a lower cost than the previous unit due to learning effects). Mathematical
 approximations of the cumulative cost exist for N>100. However, in the first-of-a-kind HTSE
 analysis the number of units installed is less than 100 so regression-based correlation was used to
 predict the cumulative cost as a function of the number of units installed. The regression
 equation has the same mathematical form as the conventional CAPEX cost scaling equation, so
 the exponent from the regression equation was used to estimate module costs in the existing H2A
 model framework.

 The standard DOE H2A models for LTE and SMR were used for these cases to calculate the
 production costs of hydrogen. The DOE H2A HTE model for large central plants was modified
 to be consistent with the capital and operating cost data presented above in Section 4.

 5.1 Steady-state Hydrogen Plants
 The main assumption for the steady-state hydrogen plant operation is based on a PPA that
 provides a fixed-price of electricity and/or steam from either the grid or coupled to a nuclear
 plant. The U.S. existing fleet of nuclear reactors has been in operation for many years and has
 therefore paid down debt. The cost of producing power with new nuclear reactors, such as the
 Alvin W. Vogtle Electric Generating Plant can be projected by Southern Company.

 In this assessment, CAPEX and OPEX for the nuclear system were not separately estimated
 because of the complexity and uncertainty of such a direct approach. Instead, the purchase price
 of electricity was used parametrically to account for all CAPEX and OPEX of the nuclear
 system. The wholesale prices of electricity considered ranged from $25–50/MWh. Steam
 purchased from the nuclear facility (also referred to as nuclear process heat) was priced at the
 parametric cost of electricity times the 33.3% thermal efficiency expected for the power-
 conversion system at a typical single-unit nuclear power plant. This effectively equates the cost
 of heat to the cost of electricity that could otherwise be produced and sold using this heat.

 The hydrogen-production cost sensitivities to variations in the PEM stack capital costs were also
 assessed. For the LTE plant, a PEM stack cost of $172/kWe (DC power input to PEM or,
 equivalently a total uninstalled capital cost of $400/kWe [total system power usage]) corresponds

 63

 to a Projected Future case4 defined in James, et al. (2016) [1]. However, this stack cost seems too
 conservative based on conversations with industry researchers; based on that input, the baseline
 PEM stack cost considered in this analysis was halved to $86/kWe. The PEM stack costs of
 $130/kWe (+50% baseline) and $60/kWe (-30% baseline) were considered for the LTE cases to
 reflect an expected spread in the cost of PEM cells. Varying only the stack cost was an adequate
 assumption because the stacks account for a large percentage of the overall cost for electrolytic
 hydrogen-production systems [2].

 For the HTE plant, the baseline SOEC module cost considered was $50/kWe (the module includes
 the SOEC stacks, the containing vessel, insulation, gas distribution, heat exchange, electrical
 distribution, and instrumentation components); this cost is based on cost information from
 manufacturers of solid oxide electrolyzer cells [3] and is also recommended by the Pacific
 Northwest National Laboratory based on relevant experience in solid-oxide fuel-cell development.5

 For LTE hydrogen-production analysis, this report considered a 48-tpd plant (or 46 tpd with a
 97.0% annual overall OCF), which requires a total system power input of 100 MWe. It is
 assumed that system power could be supported via a dedicated transmission line connecting the
 nuclear plant to an industrial plant. Such a scale is typical of a merchant hydrogen supply to, for
 example, an average oil refinery and is defined as a small-scale (or low-volume) production case
 in this analysis.

 For HTE hydrogen-production analysis, the report considered a nominal 1,000 MWe LWR
 power plant coupled to a ~608-tpd HTE nameplate-capacity plant (or 562 tpd average output at
 92.4% OCF), which requires the electrical and thermal energy of 940 MWe and 163.3 MWt,
 respectively. In this case, the report considered HTE integrated with the LWR at the nuclear
 power plant site as the source of heat and electricity. Such a world-class scale would provide
 hydrogen to multiple industries and is defined as a large-scale (or high-volume) production case
 in this analysis.

 The cost of producing hydrogen using SMR is highly dependent on the cost of natural gas.
 Similarly, the cost of producing hydrogen by electrolysis (LTE and HTE) is a strong function of
 the cost of electricity. Several case studies were conducted to parametrically analyze the effect of
 such variable operating costs, as well as capital costs (i.e., electrolysis-stack costs), on the
 economic performance of the three hydrogen-production technologies considered.

 The study of hydrogen production via SMR process was based on three natural-gas price scenarios,

 prices), and reference natural-gas price scenarios. The cases considering a reference natural gas
