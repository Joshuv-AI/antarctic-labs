Wafer-scalable artificial synapses and logic gate circuits based on coplanar self-aligned-gate organic transistors for artificial vision applications
Authors: Qi Yuan, Sihai Luo, Zechen Liang, Lijuan Chen, Hao Sun, Hongyi Shen, Yingfang Zhang, Xiaoliang Chen, Jian Lv, Bai Sun, Yi Lv, Guanghao Lu, Fenggang Ren, Jinyou Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wafer-scale and low-voltage operation are essential for practical artificial vision hardware. Here, we report a wafer-scale platform for artificial synapse and logic gate circuits with a yield exceeding 91%, utilizing coplanar self-aligned-gate (SAG) organic transistors that combine the narrow-bandgap semiconducting polymer DPP-DTT with a self-assembled monolayer dielectric. Coplanar SAG electrodes effectively suppress parasitic effects due to the reduction of gate-to-contact overlaps, enabling uniform transistor operation at low voltages (-6 to 2 V) across the wafer, delivering a high rectification ratio of 2.46 × 105. Under near-infrared illumination, the devices exhibit efficient phototransduction and reconfigurable modulation of synaptic plasticity between excitatory and inhibitory modes, with an ultra-low energy consumption of 5.28 fJ per event. In addition, minimal circuit configurations enable electrically driven NOT and NOR logic gates circuits as well as optical-electrical hybrid-input NOR logic operations, enabling maximize and simplifying the functionality of photosensing logic circuits. Furthermore, an artificial neural network constructed based on the SAG transistors further demonstrate the potential applications in handwritten digit recognition with a recognition accuracy of ~96% and image memory behavior. This work provides a scalable and effective path for developing low-power artificial synapses to neuromorphic artificial vision systems.

Introduction

Visual perception stands as a cornerstone of human information acquisition, with vision processing more than 70% of environmental data1,2. As a result, artificial vision systems play a pivotal role in contemporary society, integrating optical sensing, memory, and computation to emulate biological vision3–7. Optically driven artificial synapses are crucial, converting light into electrical signals and modulate synaptic weights8–10, thus reducing the need for photoelectric conversion circuits and simplifying system architecture11,12. In recent years, organic semiconductors like BPE-PDI13, DNTT13, PVP14, PSPMA15, PDPP4T16, and PVDF-HFP17, have emerged as favored materials for optical synaptic devices due to their tunable optoelectronic properties, ease of solution processing, and good compatibility with large-area fabrication processes18,19. However, many of the organic optical synapses reported to date are still hindered by complex device architectures, high operating voltages, and significant parasitic capacitance between the gate and source/drain electrodes, posing challenges for their scalability to wafer-level manufacturing20,21.

To address these issues, various strategies have been proposed, including self-aligned U-type22 or T-type23 top-gate structures and self-aligned bottom-gate transistor24 configurations. The self-aligned-gate (SAG) transistor architecture offers a promising solution to these challenges25–27. By minimizing the overlap between the gate (G) and source/drain (S/D) electrodes, the SAG structure substantially reduces parasitic capacitance and access resistance, facilitating low-voltage operation and enhancing device uniformity—key factors for large-area and wafer-scale integration28–31. However, existing solutions still encounter issues such as performance degradation and limited compatibility with manufacturing processes. Moreover, logic functions necessary for signal processing are usually implemented using separate transistor circuits, leading to an increase in the number of devices and circuit complexity at the system level32. Therefore, an ideal artificial vision system should combine optical sensing, synaptic plasticity, and logic operations within a single, scalable, and low-voltage device architecture to achieve efficient and streamlined visual processing.

In this work, we report a wafer-scalable coplanar SAG organic transistor based on DPP-DTT that integrates optical synaptic plasticity and logic functionality within a single device. These well-defined coplanar SAG transistors are fabricated using a nanogap lithography process, enabling operation at low voltages (-6 to 2 V) and achieving a high rectification ratio of 2.46×105. Under near-infrared illumination (850 nm), the devices demonstrate pronounced optical synaptic plasticity, including paired-pulse facilitation and adjustable synaptic weight modulation. Additionally, conventional electrically driven NOT and NOR logic gates, as well as an optical-electrical hybrid-input NOR logic gate, are realized using a minimal number of circuit components. Finally, the constructed artificial neural network validates the device’s suitability for handwritten digit recognition and image memory behavior, underscoring its potential for artificial vision systems.

Results and discussion

Figure 1a illustrates the fabrication process for a coplanar SAG transistor structure, with further details provided in the experimental section. In brief, an aluminum (Al) gate was prepatterned on a SiO₂/Si substrate using standard photolithography. Afterward, a selected self-assembled monolayer (SAM), octadecylphosphonic acid (ODPA), was conformally coated on the surface of the prepatterned Al gate, rendering its exposed surfaces highly hydrophobic. This reduces the adhesion of any metals deposited subsequently, while leaving the exposed substrate areas SAM-free. Next, a second layer of gold (Au) was deposited over the Al gate stack to function as the source/drain electrodes. Due to the low adhesion of the SAM-modified Al surface, the Au regions atop the SAM-coated Al gate can be easily removed through simple tape stripping. This creates a nanometer-scale gap (nanogap) between the gate stack and the source/drain electrodes on both sides. Since this process is driven by locally varying adhesion on the gate stack, it does not require additional alignment steps, ensuring it is inherently self-aligned and independent of gate dimensions. Finally, any residual SAM on the Al surfaces is eliminated using a brief UV-ozone or plasma treatment for further device fabrication. To implement the coplanar SAG architecture in transistor devices, ODPA was utilized as the dielectric layer, while the organic semiconductor poly[2,5-(2-octyldodecyl)-3,6-diketopyrrolopyrrole-alt-5,5-(2,5-di(thien-2-yl)thieno[3,2-b]thiophene)] (DPP-DTT) served as the semiconductor channel material. DPP-DTT was selected as the semiconductor material due to its excellent electrical and optoelectronic properties. The polymer exhibits relatively high hole mobility and favorable molecular packing, enabling efficient carrier transport and low-voltage operation. Furthermore, the good environmental stability and solution processability of DPP-DTT make it highly suitable for wafer-scale fabrication of optoelectronic synaptic devices33,34. Unlike conventional vertical transistors, which typically feature partial overlaps between the gate and source/drain electrodes, coplanar DPP-DTT SAG transistors were fabricated with no such overlaps. This was achieved through spin coating followed by thermal annealing at 150°C, as depicted schematically in Fig. 2a. The cross-sectional scanning electron microscope image of the coplanar DPP-DTT self-aligned-gate (SAG) transistor is shown in Fig. S3. The transfer and output characteristic curves of all DPP-DTT SAG transistors with different channel lengths ranging from 1 μm to 100 μm (and channel widths fixed at 1, 5, 10, 50, and 100 μm) were measured under dark conditions (Figs. 2b and S4 in the Supporting Information). These transistors exhibit typical p-type behavior with negligible operating hysteresis, attributed to a low density of interfacial trapping states at both the contact and the semiconductor/dielectric interfaces. As shown in Figs. 2b and S4, gate leakage currents in all devices were consistently suppressed below 1 nA. The device characteristics were systematically analyzed, including the on/off current ratio (ION/IOFF), threshold voltage (Vth), subthreshold swing (SS), and carrier mobility (μ). These parameters were extracted from the transfer characteristics measured in the saturation regime at a drain/source voltage (VDS) of -3 V. As shown in Fig. 2c, d, the SAG transistors demonstrated outstanding electrical performance. Specifically, the DPP-DTT SAG transistors with a 1 μm channel length achieved highest on/off current ratios (2.46 × 105), reduced threshold voltages (-0.98 V), and relatively high carrier mobilities (1.83 cm2V-1S-1) (calculation details are provided in Fig. S6 in Supporting Information). These results indicate effective gate control and a high hole carrier concentration within the channel. Unlike traditional vertical designs (in Fig. 2a, right), which often suffer from reduced ION/IOFF ratios at shorter channel lengths due to increased leakage and poorer gate control35, the coplanar SAG transistors maintain a robust ION/IOFF ratio across varying channel lengths. This ensures efficient switching and minimal leakage, even in scaled-down devices. Furthermore, while conventional vertical transistors may exhibit greater variability in threshold voltage (Vth) with changing channel dimensions, the SAG architecture provides remarkably stable and low Vth of -0.98 V, enhancing predictability and reliability in circuit operations. In addition, the SAG transistors achieves notably lower SS (0.42 V/dec) values, especially at shorter channels, indicating superior gate control and reduced short-channel effects compared to vertical structures, which typically struggle with higher SS values due to less effective gate coupling36. In neuromorphic devices, operating at low threshold voltage is crucial for achieving energy-efficient synaptic and neuronal functions. Since switching energy increases with the square of the applied voltage, using lower voltages can dramatically cut energy use per write/read operation37–39. Compared to high-voltage devices, low-voltage operation can reduce energy consumption by several orders of magnitude. Therefore, achieving low operating voltages is of particular importance for future research on bioinspired synaptic devices and neuromorphic systems.

Leveraging the observed near-infrared phototransduction, we systematically investigated the potential of optically modulated channel conductance to emulate synaptic plasticity under dynamic light stimuli. The pronounced conductance modulation suggests a memory-like response, motivating a systematic investigation into the device’s synaptic behavior under optical pulse stimulation. To reliably probe this behavior, we selected a wavelength of 850 nm for illumination. As shown in the wavelength-dependent excitatory postsynaptic current (EPSC) response measurements (Fig. S7), the devices exhibit a clear and reproducible photocurrent response under 850 nm light, while responses at other tested wavelengths were weaker or less stable. Therefore, 850 nm illumination was used in subsequent experiments to investigate the optoelectronic synaptic characteristics.

Figure 4a shows the coplanar organic SAG transistors generate a transient EPSC signal, reaching a peak of 31.4 nA in response to a single optical pulse, which successfully mimics the response of a biological synapse to a single stimulus. In both biological and artificial synapses, the strength of synaptic connections—known as synaptic weights—is fundamental to synaptic function. Synaptic plasticity refers to changes in these synaptic weights, and in artificial synapses, it can be observed as variations in the conductance of the organic SAG transistors. Synaptic plasticity is categorized into short-term and long-term plasticity. The human brain consolidates memories through learning, with memory categorized by retention time into short-term memory (STM) and long-term memory (LTM). In psychology, STM and LTM are considered fundamental to learning and memory, differing primarily in how long memories last. Since memory strength depends on factors such as the duration, intensity, number, and frequency of learning experiences, organisms typically transition from STM to LTM by repeatedly rehearsing or revisiting the learned material. Similarly, by adjusting the number, duration and frequency of light pulses applied to organic SAG transistors, the conversion from STM to LTM can be achieved, further simulating synaptic plasticity. As the frequency, duration and number of stimuli increase, these transistors show significant synaptic plasticity, characterized by a gradual increase in peak current and prolonged current decay time (Figs. 4d-f). The working mechanism of the DPP-DTT SAG optoelectronic synaptic transistor is illustrated in Fig. 4g. In the initial state, the device stays in thermodynamic equilibrium, in which Fermi levels across all layers are aligned, and the valence band (VB) and conduction band (CB) of the DPP-DTT channel remain at stable energy positions. When exposed to illumination under a negative bias, incident photons are absorbed by the DPP-DTT semiconductor, exciting electrons from the VB to the CB and generating electron-hole pairs. Photogenerated holes migrate along the channel towards the drain electrode, giving rise to an increased source-drain current, i.e., the excitatory postsynaptic current (EPSC). Meanwhile, a fraction of photogenerated electrons is captured by trap states at the ODPA dielectric interface. After illumination ceases, these trapped electrons persist at the interface, generating a local built-in electric field that modulates subsequent carrier injection and transport in the channel. This effect enables precise control of synaptic weight, and emulates both short-term and long-term plasticity of biological synapses. Under consecutive light pulse stimulation, the EPSC gradually accumulates, presenting the typical paired-pulse facilitation (PPF) feature. By tuning the width, intensity and interval of input light pulses, multi-level optoelectronic memory states can be reliably achieved. The combination of this light-induced charge trapping/release mechanism and the superior gate-control capability of the coplanar self-aligned structure enables stable, highly controllable synaptic plasticity of the device.

Leveraging the aforementioned near-infrared phototransduction, we further implemented an optical-electrical hybrid-input NOR logic operation as illustrated in Fig. 5c. In this hybrid configuration, one input is controlled electrically, while the other is modulated by optical illumination. This enables logic behavior through the cooperative modulation of the gate bias and the photo-induced channel conductance. For the electrical input, logic “1” and logic “0” correspond to Vin = -8 Vand 0 V, respectively, whereas for the optical input, the logic states are defined by switching the light source on or off. The measured output responses under all possible input combinations agree well with the NOR truth table, thereby validating the feasibility of optical-electrical hybrid logic behavior using a minimal device-level configuration. This demonstration highlights that the coplanar DPP-DTT SAG transistor can effectively process both optical and electrical signals within a unified device architecture. This capability is particularly attractive for mixed-signal operation at the sensor-processing interface in artificial vision systems, where seamless integration of optical sensing and electrical signal processing is essential.

To assess the applicability of the proposed optical synaptic SAG transistor for artificial vision tasks, an artificial neural (ANN) network was constructed with a three-layer architecture: an input layer comprising 784 neurons (corresponding to a 28 × 28 pixel input image), a hidden layer with 50 neurons, and an output layer containing 10 neurons. This network was employed to recognize MNIST handwritten digits, leveraging experimentally derived synaptic plasticity characteristics. The evaluation process utilized the MNIST dataset, as schematically depicted in Fig. 6a. Synaptic weight updates in the network were regulated by the measured long-term potentiation and long-term depression (LTP/LTD) characteristics of the device (Fig. 6b). To evaluate the network’s robustness under noisy visual conditions, salt-and-pepper noise of varying intensities was introduced into the input images (Fig. 6c). Conclusion

In summary, we have developed a wafer-scalable coplanar organic SAG transistor platform that integrates optical synaptic plasticity and logic functionality within a single, unified device architecture. This innovative design is facilitated by nanogap-engineered SAG electrodes, which enable the devices to operate at low voltages while exhibiting highly efficient near-infrared phototransduction capabilities. Additionally, these devices demonstrate optical synaptic plasticity, allowing for dynamic modulation of synaptic weights, as well as optical–electrical hybrid logic behavior, which expands their functional versatility. By incorporating experimentally extracted synaptic characteristics into artificial neural network models, we have further validated the practical applicability of these proposed devices in artificial vision tasks. Specifically, our devices have shown promising performance in pattern recognition, achieving high classification accuracy even under noisy conditions, and in simulating image memory behavior, including processes of image writing, retention, and gradual decay. Owing to the self-aligned coplanar architecture and nanogap-engineered electrode design, the proposed devices also exhibit strong potential for further dimensional scaling. By reducing the channel length and optimizing the dielectric thickness, higher integration density, lower parasitic capacitance, and even lower synaptic operating energy could be achieved, which would be highly beneficial for large-scale neuromorphic artificial vision systems. In addition, owing to the organic semiconductor platform and wafer-compatible fabrication strategy, the proposed devices also exhibit strong potential for flexible and wearable neuromorphic electronics. We further successfully fabricated the devices on flexible polyimide (PI) substrates, where stable electrical characteristics could still be obtained, demonstrating their compatibility with flexible electronic systems. Beyond artificial vision systems, the integration of sensing, memory, and logic functions within a single device architecture may enable broader applications in neuromorphic sensory systems, wearable intelligent electronics, human–machine interaction, and low-power edge computing platforms41,42. This work establishes a direct link between device-level optoelectronic dynamics and system-level visual functions, providing a scalable and multifunctional building block for future neuromorphic artificial vision systems.

Experimental section

Materials

The DPP-DTT (Mw = 250 kDa, PDI = 5) was purchased from Ossila, and octadecylphosphonic acid (ODPA, 97%) was obtained from Macklin. All materials were used as received without further purification.

DPP-DTT SAG transistor fabrication

A silicon wafer (with a 285 nm thick oxide layer) was used as the substrate, which was cleaned by ultrasonic cleaning in ultrapure water, acetone, and isopropanol for 10 min each. First, the positive photoresist (AZ5214E, exposure energy density: 90 mJ/cm², development time: 30 s) was patterned by maskless laser direct-writing (UV Litho-ACA Pro, TuoTuo Technology (Suzhou) Co. Ltd, China), followed by thermal evaporation of a 50 nm aluminum (Al) layer to form the gate electrode (vacuum pressure <5 × 10-⁴ Pa, deposition rate ≈ 1.5 Å/s). Subsequently, the photoresist was lift-off using acetone, and the patterned Al was subjected to an oxygen plasma treatment for 5 min to form a thin Al₂O₃ layer on its surface. The Al/Al₂O₃ stack was then immersed in a 5 mM solution of octadecylphosphonic acid (ODPA) in isopropanol for 5 h, which is a key step for the formation of a self-assembled monolayer (SAM) of ODPA on the Al/Al₂O₃ surface, rendering it hydrophobic while maintaining the hydrophilicity of the silicon oxide surface. After rinsing with isopropanol, the substrate was dried at 80 °C for 10 min to remove any physically adsorbed ODPA and excess solvents on the silicon oxide surface. Next, a second photolithography process was used to pattern the source and drain electrodes. A 5 nm adhesion layer of aluminum was thermally evaporated (vacuum pressure <5 × 10-⁴ Pa, deposition rate ≈ 1.5 Å/s), followed by the deposition of 45 nm of gold (vacuum pressure <5 × 10-⁴ Pa, evaporation rate ≈ 0.6 Å/s). Due to the reduced adhesion on the SAM-functionalized Al/Al₂O₃ surface, the deposited source/drain metals were selectively removed from the regions overlapping with Al/Al₂O₃ using mechanical glass etching. A 5-min UV-ozone treatment was applied to remove the ODPA, exposing the nanogap, which was typically less than 10-nm. Finally, the self-aligned electrode structure was immersed in an ODPA isopropanol solution to allow SAM growth between the gate and source/drain electrodes, acting as the dielectric layer for the transistor. DPP-DTT was then dissolved in o-dichlorobenzene (o-DCB, 7 mg/mL) and spin-coated at 2000 rpm for 30 s. The substrate was annealed in a nitrogen-filled glovebox at 150 °C for 30 min, forming the semiconductor active layer for the transistor.

Characteristics

The electrical properties of the transistor, including transfer and output characteristics, were measured in ambient conditions using an Agilent Keysight B2902A Precision Source-Measure Unit. Capacitance was characterized using an Agilent E4980A Precision LCR Meter. All optoelectronic synaptic properties were measured under ambient conditions.

Image recognition

To evaluate the feasibility of the device for pattern recognition tasks, we first constructed an artificial neural network (ANN) based on a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to classify the standard MNIST handwritten digit dataset. The MNIST dataset consists of 60,000 training images and 10,000 test images, each with a size of 28 × 28 pixels. The network structure used a single hidden-layer MLP: the input layer consists of 784 neurons, the hidden layer contains 50 fully connected neurons (ReLU activation), and the output layer is a 10-dimensional softmax layer representing the probability of digits 0-9. The loss function used is cross-entropy loss, and the optimization algorithm employs either momentum-based stochastic gradient descent or the Adam optimizer. During training, the batch size was set to 64, with 300 epochs, and the classification accuracy for both the training and test sets was recorded in each epoch to monitor the network’s convergence process.
