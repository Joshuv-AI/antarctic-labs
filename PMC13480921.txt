Epidemiology of ischemic stroke in China and the United States
Authors: Xue Ren, Zixuan Lu, Jiaohua Chen, Jiening Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to comprehensively compare the temporal trends, age–period–cohort (APC) effects, and future projections of ischemic stroke burden between China and the United States from 1990 to 2023. This cross-sectional, population-based retrospective study utilized data from the Global Burden of Disease 2023 database (PMID: 41092926). Data on incidence, prevalence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for ischemic stroke were extracted for China and the United States from 1990 to 2023, stratified by sex and 19 age groups. Temporal trends were quantified using estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) through log-linear regression and average annual percentage change (AAPC) through joinpoint regression analysis. APC analysis using the intrinsic estimator method was performed to disentangle age, period, and cohort effects. Bayesian APC models with integrated nested Laplace approximation were employed to project disease burden through 2035. From 1990 to 2023, China demonstrated divergent trends with age-standardized incidence increasing by 5.41% (EAPC: 0.06%, 95% CI: −0.09 to 0.22%) and prevalence by 11.96% (EAPC: 0.44%, 95% CI: 0.41–0.46%), while mortality declined by 58.20% (EAPC: −2.68%, 95% CI: −2.84 to −2.51%) and DALYs by 54.99% (EAPC: −2.48%, 95% CI: −2.61 to −2.36%). The United States exhibited declining age-standardized rates across incidence (−27.17%, EAPC: −0.67%, 95% CI: −0.80 to −0.54%), mortality (−40.40%, EAPC: −2.00%, 95% CI: −2.33 to −1.67%), and DALYs (−28.49%, EAPC: −1.36%, 95% CI: −1.60 to −1.12%), with prevalence increasing by 10.93% (EAPC: 0.18%, 95% CI: 0.07–0.30%). Joinpoint analysis revealed heterogeneous patterns with multiple inflection points in both countries. APC analysis demonstrated exponential age effects, U-shaped period effects in China contrasting with declining then increasing patterns in the United States, and consistent cohort-related risk reductions in both nations. Projections to 2035 indicated that despite potential declines in age-standardized rates, absolute disease burden would escalate substantially: China’s incident cases projected to increase from 2656,475 to 4288,943 (61.46% increase), and the United States from 388,155 to 444,961 (14.64% increase). Despite improvements in age-standardized mortality and DALYs, both China and the United States face increases in absolute ischemic stroke burden through 2035 due to population aging, necessitating enhanced prevention strategies and healthcare resource allocation to address this intensifying public health challenge.

1. Introduction

Stroke is a neurological disorder characterized by sudden neurological dysfunction, encompassing primarily ischemic stroke, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and cerebral hemorrhage.[1] According to the Global Burden of Disease Stroke Working Group study, stroke ranks as the 2nd leading cause of death globally and the 3rd leading cause of death and disability.[2] Ischemic stroke is the most prevalent form, constituting approximately 65% of all strokes worldwide in 2021.[3] Despite advancements in treatments, such as thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy, patients with ischemic stroke continue to experience a high likelihood of poor functional outcomes or recurrence posttreatment. The high incidence, disability rate, recurrence rate, and substantial economic burden associated with ischemic stroke present a significant threat to global public health. Moreover, stroke incidence exhibits considerable variation across countries, with rates increasing significantly in low- and middle-income countries while declining by 42% in high-income nations.[4] As the world’s largest developing and developed countries, respectively,[5] China and the United States serve as quintessential paradigms of these divergent epidemiological trajectories. China faces an escalating stroke burden driven by rapid population aging and lifestyle transitions,[6] whereas the United States provides established insights into chronic disease management and advanced healthcare delivery.[7] Comparing these 2 nations offers a unique opportunity to evaluate the effectiveness of different healthcare models and demographic transitions on stroke outcomes. This disparity highlights the urgent need for cross-national comparative studies. Recently, an increasing number of epidemiological studies have investigated the temporal trends of cerebrovascular diseases. Previous analyses have documented the shifting burden of stroke within individual nations, utilizing older iterations of the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database, such as the GBD 2021 dataset for China[8] or the GBD 2019 dataset for the United States.[9] However, the majority of the existing literature is constrained by traditional descriptive epidemiology confined to single countries, often failing to disentangle the complex underlying drivers associated with historical periods and generational cohorts. Furthermore, studies providing long-term, quantitative future projections remain scarce. Ultimately, the current body of research on the effectiveness of healthcare systems across different countries remains limited, impeding collaborative progress in the global prevention and management of ischemic stroke. Consequently, this study utilizes the latest GBD 2023 dataset to conduct the first systematic comparison of the burden of ischemic stroke in China and the United States. Furthermore, this study combines Joinpoint regression to detect hidden temporal inflection points with an age–period–cohort (APC) model utilizing the intrinsic estimator (IE) method to disentangle the independent etiological drivers of aging, historical periods, and generational cohorts. Finally, by employing a Bayesian age–period–cohort (BAPC) model incorporating integrated nested Laplace approximation, we generate robust projections of disease trajectories up to 2035, thereby providing evidence-based healthcare planning for stroke prevention and the long-term allocation of healthcare resources.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data source and study design

This retrospective population-based study utilized data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023 (GBD 2023) database (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/, PMID: 41092926). The GBD 2023 database provides comprehensive estimates of the burden of 375 diseases and injuries across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2023.

Ischemic stroke data were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes: G45 to G46.8, I63 to I63.9, I65 to I66.9, I67.2 to I67.848, and I69.3 to I69.4. The study included 4 primary epidemiological measures: incidence (new cases), prevalence (existing cases), mortality (deaths), and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). For each measure, we extracted both the absolute number of cases and age-standardized rates per 100,000 population with corresponding 95% uncertainty intervals.

Sex-specific analyses were conducted for males, females, and both sexes combined. Comparative analyses were performed between China and the United States of America to represent contrasting healthcare systems and socioeconomic contexts. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting statement.

2.2. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0) with the following packages: tidyverse (version 2.0.0), dplyr (version 1.1.0), ggplot2 (version 3.4.0), ggsci (version 3.0.0), patchwork (version 1.1.2), readxl (version 1.4.0), writexl (version 1.4.0), openxlsx (version 4.2.5), and scales (version 1.2.1).

2.3. Temporal trend analysis

2.3.1. Estimated Annual Percentage Change (EAPC)

The EAPC was calculated to quantify the average annual rate of change in age-standardized rates over the study period. EAPC was derived from a log-linear regression model:

where y represents the natural logarithm of the age-standardized rate, x represents the calendar year, and ε represents the error term. The EAPC and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated as: EAPC = 100 × (eβ − 1). A positive EAPC indicated an increasing trend, while a negative EAPC indicated a decreasing trend. The trend was considered statistically significant when the 95% CI of EAPC did not include 0.

2.3.2. Joinpoint regression analysis

Joinpoint regression analysis was performed using the National Cancer Institute’s Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.0.2) to identify significant changes in temporal trends. The analysis utilized the Grid Search Method to detect up to 5 joinpoints, with a maximum of 5 potential inflection points. The optimal number of joinpoints was determined using the Bayesian Information Criterion and Monte Carlo Permutation tests (P < .05). The natural logarithm transformation model was applied with constant variance assumption.

For age-standardized rates, the annual percent change was calculated for each identified segment between joinpoints, representing the rate of change within each distinct time period. The average annual percent change (AAPC) was computed as a weighted average of the APCs, with weights proportional to the length of each time interval. Both APC and AAPC were reported with 95% CIs, and trends were considered statistically significant when the 95% CI excluded 0 (P < .05).

For age-specific rates and all-ages rates, separate joinpoint regression models were fitted for each age group within each location, sex, and measure combination. The analysis followed the same methodological framework as described above, allowing for the identification of age-specific temporal patterns.

2.4. APC analysis

APC analysis was conducted to disentangle the independent effects of age, time period, and birth cohort on ischemic stroke burden. The IE method was employed to address the collinearity problem inherent in APC models, where age = period − cohort.

Data were organized into 5-year age groups (ranging from 25–29 to 90–94 years) and 5-year calendar periods (1990–1994, 1995–1999, 2000–2004, 2005–2009, 2010–2014, 2015–2019, and 2020–2023). Birth cohorts were derived by subtracting the median age of each age group from the median year of each period, spanning from approximately 1900 to 1998. The analysis was performed using the apc package (version 2.0.0) in R.

The APC model was specified as:

log(Rate_{ijk}) = μ + α_i + β_j + γ_k

where Rate_{ijk} represents the rate for age group i, period j, and cohort k; μ is the intercept; α_i is the age effect; β_j is the period effect; and γ_k is the cohort effect.

Age effects represent the inherent risk associated with biological aging. Period effects capture the influence of historical events, policy changes, or environmental exposures affecting all age groups simultaneously. Cohort effects reflect the cumulative experiences of individuals born in the same time period. Results were presented as rate ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs relative to reference categories (median age group, median period, and median cohort). The model fit was assessed using deviance statistics and the coefficient of determination (R2).

2.5. Forecasting analysis

Future trends in ischemic stroke burden from 2024 to 2035 were projected using BAPC models implemented through the BAPC package (version 0.0.35) in R. The BAPC framework extends traditional APC models by incorporating Bayesian inference with integrated nested Laplace approximation, providing robust uncertainty quantification.

The BAPC model structure incorporated:

Age effects: modeled using second-order random walk priors to capture smooth age-related variations.

Period effects: first-order random walk priors to account for temporal trends.

Model specifications included 5-year age groups (20–24 to ≥95 years) and 5-year periods (1990–1994 through 2020–2023 for historical data, extending to 2030–2034 for projections). The models were fitted separately for each combination of location (China and United States), sex (male, female, and both), and epidemiological measure (incidence, prevalence, deaths, and DALYs).

Predictions were generated for both age-standardized rates per 100,000 population and absolute numbers with 95% credible intervals. The forecasting horizon extended from 2024 to 2035, providing 12-year projections. Model performance was evaluated using deviance information criterion and posterior predictive checks. The forecasts assumed continuation of current trends without accounting for potential future interventions or major epidemiological shifts.

3. Results

3.1. Temporal trends in age-standardized rates of ischemic stroke burden

From 1990 to 2023, China and the United States demonstrated divergent temporal patterns in age-standardized rates of ischemic stroke burden across all epidemiological measures (Table 1, Fig. 1). In China, the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) increased modestly from 112.33 per 100,000 population in 1990 to 118.40 per 100,000 in 2023, representing a 5.41% increase, while the prevalence rate rose from 851.25 to 953.08 per 100,000, marking an 11.96% increase. Conversely, the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) declined substantially from 94.12 to 39.34 per 100,000 (−58.20%), and the DALY rate decreased from 1783.23 to 802.71 per 100,000 (−54.99%). In contrast, the United States exhibited declining trends across incidence, mortality, and DALYs. The ASIR decreased from 96.17 to 70.04 per 100,000 (−27.17%), mortality rate declined from 29.78 to 17.75 per 100,000 (−40.40%), and DALY rate decreased from 575.17 to 411.32 per 100,000 (−28.49%). Notably, prevalence rates increased in both countries, rising by 10.93% in the United States from 1129.46 to 1252.89 per 100,000.

Sex-stratified analyses revealed substantial gender disparities in temporal trends within both countries (Figures S1 and S2, Supplemental Digital Content 1). In China, males experienced a 12.80% increase in ASIR (from 131.66 to 148.51 per 100,000) and an 18.74% increase in prevalence rates (from 939.75 to 1115.83 per 100,000), whereas females demonstrated a 5.30% decrease in incidence rates (from 96.09 to 91.00 per 100,000) but a modest 3.43% increase in prevalence rates (from 775.23 to 801.83 per 100,000). Both sexes in China showed marked reductions in mortality and DALY rates, with females exhibiting greater percentage declines (−62.97% for mortality and −60.19% for DALYs) compared to males (−54.42% for mortality and −51.06% for DALYs). In the United States, both sexes demonstrated comparable reductions in ASIR (−27.95% for females and −27.80% for males), mortality rates (−36.63% for females and −46.12% for males), and DALY rates (−24.92% for females and −33.30% for males). Prevalence rates increased for both sexes in the United States, with a greater percentage increase observed in females (13.87%) compared to males (6.83%). In terms of absolute case numbers, China experienced dramatic increases across all measures, with incident cases rising by 203.19% (from 876,167 to 2656,475), prevalent cases increasing by 185.47% (from 7436,521 to 21,229,135), deaths rising by 45.71% (from 590,439 to 860,343), and DALYs increasing by 35.23% (from 13,378,094 to 18,090,920), whereas the United States demonstrated more moderate increases in incident cases (27.59%), prevalent cases (91.81%), deaths (18.40%), and DALYs (30.53%).

3.2. Joinpoint regression analysis of temporal trends

Joinpoint regression analysis revealed heterogeneous temporal patterns with multiple inflection points in ischemic stroke burden trends for both countries (Table 1, Fig. 2). In China, the AAPC for ASIR was 0.13% (95% CI: 0.09–0.17, P < .001), with 6 distinct phases identified: an initial increase from 1990 to 1994 (APC: 1.75%, 95% CI: 1.34–2.38), followed by stabilization from 1994 to 2000 (APC: 0.13%, 95% CI: −0.49 to 0.40, P = .423), a subsequent rise from 2000 to 2010 (APC: 1.01%, 95% CI: 0.90–1.19), a sharp decline from 2010 to 2015 (APC: −2.89%, 95% CI: −3.13 to −2.69), an increase from 2015 to 2019 (APC: 1.33%, 95% CI: 0.94–1.78), and a recent decline from 2019 to 2023 (APC: −1.06%, 95% CI: −1.53 to −0.62). The prevalence rates demonstrated consistent upward trends with an AAPC of 0.34% (95% CI: 0.33–0.34, P < .001), though a recent downturn emerged from 2020 to 2023 (APC: −0.41%, 95% CI: −0.48 to −0.34). For mortality and DALYs in China, significant declining trends were observed with AAPCs of −2.52% (95% CI: −2.60 to −2.44, P < .001) and −2.33% (95% CI: −2.41 to −2.26, P < .001), respectively. Notably, both measures exhibited accelerated declines from 2017 to 2020 (mortality APC: −9.90%, 95% CI: −10.82 to −8.86; DALYs APC: −7.71%, 95% CI: −8.60 to −6.42), followed by stabilization from 2020 to 2023 (mortality APC: 0.90%, 95% CI: −0.23 to 2.05, P = .110; DALYs APC: 0.49%, 95% CI: −0.62 to 1.95, P = .264).

Mortality rates declined with an AAPC of − 1.38% (95% CI: −1.48 to −1.27, P < .001), driven primarily by a steep decrease from 1999 to 2011 (APC: −4.35%, 95% CI: −4.87 to −4.01), followed by an increase from 2011 to 2023 (APC: 1.15%, 95% CI: 0.80–1.59). Sex-stratified joinpoint analyses revealed distinct patterns between males and females in both countries (Figures S3 and S4, Supplemental Digital Content 2). In China, males exhibited a higher AAPC for incidence (0.30%, 95% CI: 0.24–0.36) compared to females (−0.19%, 95% CI: −0.22 to −0.16), while females demonstrated greater declines in mortality (AAPC: −2.91%, 95% CI: −2.98 to −2.84) and DALYs (AAPC: −2.71%, 95% CI: −2.78 to −2.66) than males (mortality AAPC: −2.25%, 95% CI: −2.37 to −2.12; DALYs AAPC: −2.08%, 95% CI: −2.17 to −1.99). In the United States, both sexes exhibited comparable declining trends in incidence (females AAPC: −0.97%, 95% CI: −1.00 to −0.94; males AAPC: −0.97%, 95% CI: −1.00 to −0.94), while males showed steeper declines in mortality (AAPC: −1.72%, 95% CI: −1.82 to −1.60) and DALYs (AAPC: −1.10%, 95% CI: −1.17 to −1.02) compared to females (mortality AAPC: −1.23%, 95% CI: −1.33 to −1.13; DALYs AAPC: −0.77%, 95% CI: −0.83 to −0.71).

3.3. Age-specific trends in ischemic stroke burden

Age-stratified analyses demonstrated distinct patterns across the lifespan, with marked differences between China and the United States in both temporal trends and absolute burden (Table 2, Fig. 3). As vividly illustrated by the spatiotemporal heatmaps (Fig. 3), the burden of ischemic stroke strongly positively correlated with age in both nations, with the darkest red regions (highest rates) concentrated in the oldest age groups. In China, incidence rates among younger age groups (<40 years) exhibited predominantly increasing trends, with the most pronounced increases observed in the 30–34 year age group (rate change: 29.54%, AAPC: 0.72%, 95% CI: 0.62–0.80, P < .001) and 35–39 year age group (rate change: 32.62%, AAPC: 0.74%, 95% CI: 0.68–0.80, P < .001). Conversely, middle-aged and older adults demonstrated heterogeneous patterns, with the 55–59 year age group showing stable rates (rate change: 0.71%, AAPC: −0.01%, 95% CI: −0.06 to 0.04, P = .547) and the ≥75 year age groups showing modest increases (75–79 years: rate change 10.54%, AAPC: 0.26%, 95% CI: 0.20–0.30, P < .001). In the United States, incidence rates declined across most age groups ≥50 years, with the steepest declines in the 60–64 year age group (rate change: −32.01%, AAPC: −1.15%, 95% CI: −1.20 to −1.11, P < .001) and 65–69 year age group (rate change: −36.78%, AAPC: −1.37%, 95% CI: −1.40 to −1.33, P < .001), while younger age groups (30–44 years) showed increasing trends (35–39 years: rate change 26.99%, AAPC: 0.73%, 95% CI: 0.71–0.76, P < .001).

3.4. APC effects on ischemic stroke burden

To disentangle the independent contributions of age, time period, and birth cohort, APC analyses were performed for both countries across all epidemiological measures (Fig. 4, Figure S11, Supplemental Digital Content 5). In China, age effects demonstrated exponential increases in risk across all measures, with incidence showing RRs rising from 0.06 (95% CI: 0.06–0.07) for the 15–19 year age group to 7.36 (95% CI: 7.28–7.44) for the ≥95 year age group, and mortality rates escalating from RR 0.03 (95% CI: 0.03–0.03) for 15–19 years to RR 13.10 (95% CI: 12.80–13.39) for 95–99 years, relative to the reference age group of 60–64 years. Period effects revealed U-shaped patterns for incidence and prevalence, with nadir RRs occurring during 2004 to 2008 (incidence RR: 0.91, 95% CI: 0.91–0.91; prevalence RR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.98–0.99) followed by upward trends through 2019–2023 (incidence RR: 1.22, 95% CI: 1.21–1.22; prevalence RR: 1.24, 95% CI: 1.23–1.24). Mortality and DALYs demonstrated declining period effects from 1994–1998 (mortality RR: 0.83, 95% CI: 0.82–0.84) to 2004 to 2008 (mortality RR: 0.91, 95% CI: 0.91–0.92), followed by increases to 2019 to 2023 (mortality RR: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.20–1.22). Cohort effects exhibited consistent monotonic declines across successive birth cohorts for all measures, with incidence RRs decreasing from 2.67 (95% CI: 2.65–2.69) for the 1899 to 1903 cohort to 0.26 (95% CI: 0.26–0.27) for the 2004 to 2008 cohort, and mortality RRs declining from 6.57 (95% CI: 6.14–7.04) to 0.10 (95% CI: 0.07–0.13) across the same cohorts, indicating progressively lower intrinsic risk among more recently born generations.

In the United States, age effects similarly demonstrated exponential increases, with incidence RRs ranging from 0.13 (95% CI: 0.13–0.13) for 15 to 19 years to 4.70 (95% CI: 4.65–4.75) for 95 to 99 years, and mortality RRs spanning from 0.03 (95% CI: 0.02–0.04) to 15.07 (95% CI: 14.22–15.96) across the same age range. Period effects for incidence displayed continuous declines from 1994 to 1998 (RR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.78–0.79) through 2004 to 2008 (RR: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.99–0.99), followed by increases to 2019 to 2023 (RR: 1.26, 95% CI: 1.26–1.27). Prevalence period effects showed initial increases from 1994 to 1998 (RR: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.09–1.09) peaking in 1999 to 2003 (RR: 1.13, 95% CI: 1.13–1.13), then declining to 2014 to 2018 (RR: 0.88, 95% CI: 0.88–0.88) before rising in 2019 to 2023 (RR: 0.90, 95% CI: 0.90–0.90). Mortality and DALY period effects demonstrated marked declines from 1994 to 1998 through 2009 to 2013, followed by recent upward trends. Cohort effects revealed substantial declines for incidence from the 1899 to 1903 cohort (RR: 3.78, 95% CI: 3.68–3.88) to the 2004 to 2008 cohort (RR: 0.33, 95% CI: 0.32–0.35), with similar patterns for mortality (1899–1903 cohort RR: 6.13, 95% CI: 5.65–6.64; 2004–2008 cohort RR: 0.15, 95% CI: 0.12–0.18). Comparative analyses between China and the United States revealed that while both countries exhibited similar age and cohort effect patterns, period effects diverged substantially, with China showing more recent deterioration in mortality and DALY trends beginning from the 2009 to 2013 period, whereas the United States demonstrated earlier improvements followed by recent stabilization or modest increases.

3.5. Projected trends in ischemic stroke burden to 2035

BAPC projections extending to 2035, under the assumption that current trends continue without major policy changes or unforeseen events, suggested contrasting trajectories between China and the United States, with potential implications for future disease burden (Figures 5 and 6, Figures S12 and S13, Supplemental Digital Content 6). In China, the model estimated that ASIR would increase modestly from 118.40 per 100,000 in 2023 to 140.35 per 100,000 in 2035 for both sexes combined, representing an estimated 18.53% increase, with males (185.41 per 100,000) demonstrating higher projected rates than females (90.61 per 100,000) (Fig. 5A). Age-standardized prevalence rates (ASPR) showed a modest upward trend from 953.08 to 982.35 per 100,000 (3.07% increase) (Fig. 5B), while mortality rates remained relatively stable, declining from 39.34 to 36.39 per 100,000 (−7.50% decrease) (Fig. 5C), and DALY rates decreased from 802.71 to 726.05 per 100,000 (−9.55% decrease) (Fig. 5D). However, if current demographic and epidemiological trends persist, the absolute burden was projected to escalate substantially across all measures (Figure S12, Supplemental Digital Content 6). Despite declining ASMR, the absolute number of deaths was projected to increase dramatically from 860,343 in 2023 to 1187,385 in 2035, representing a 38.02% increase (Figure S12C, Supplemental Digital Content 6). Incident cases were projected to rise from 2656,475 to 4288,943 (61.46% increase) (Figure S12A, Supplemental Digital Content 6), prevalent cases to increase from 21,229,135 to 29,407,969 (38.53% increase) (Figure S12B, Supplemental Digital Content 6), and DALYs to grow from 18,090,920 to 22,893,218 (26.55% increase) (Figure S12D, Supplemental Digital Content 6). Sex-stratified projections indicated that males would bear a disproportionately higher burden, with male-to-female ratios projected at 2.05 for incidence rates, 1.53 for prevalence rates, and 1.71 for DALY rates by 2035.

In the United States, ASIR was projected to decline from 70.04 per 100,000 in 2023 to 63.75 per 100,000 in 2035 (−8.98% decrease) (Fig. 6A), while prevalence rates showed an upward trend from 1252.89 to 1454.89 per 100,000 (16.12% increase) (Fig. 6B). ASMR demonstrated a modest increase from 17.75 to 18.36 per 100,000 (3.44% increase) (Fig. 6C), and DALY rates rose from 411.32 to 459.79 per 100,000 (11.78% increase) (Fig. 6D). Critically, despite declining or stabilizing age-standardized rates for incidence, absolute case numbers were projected to increase substantially due to population aging and growth (Figure S13, Supplemental Digital Content 7). Incident cases were projected to rise from 388,155 to 444,961 (14.64% increase) (Figure S13A, Supplemental Digital Content 7), prevalent cases to increase from 6674,890 to 9441,012 (41.44% increase) (Figure S13B, Supplemental Digital Content 7), deaths to rise from 118,039 to 185,873 (57.47% increase) (Figure S13C, Supplemental Digital Content 7), and DALYs to grow from 2456,713 to 3639,368 (48.14% increase) (Figure S13D, Supplemental Digital Content 7). Comparative projections revealed that while the United States was expected to experience more favorable trends in ASIR, both nations faced substantial increases in absolute disease burden, with China experiencing more pronounced increases due to its larger population base and more rapid demographic aging. By 2035, China was projected to account for 4288,943 incident cases compared to 444,961 in the United States (9.6-fold difference), and 29,407,969 prevalent cases versus 9441,012 (3.1-fold difference). These model-based projections suggest that, conditional on the continuation of current trends, despite improvements in age-standardized rates, the absolute burden of ischemic stroke may intensify substantially in both countries, underscoring the need for enhanced prevention strategies, healthcare resource allocation, and public health interventions to address this potential challenge.

4. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs associated with ischemic stroke in China and the United States over the past 30 years, along with projections for the next decade. We examined disparities in ischemic stroke burden across different age groups and genders in China and the United States. The findings indicate that from 1990 to 2023, China witnessed a significant decline in ASMR and DALYs, whereas ASIR and prevalence showed an upward trend. Conversely, the United States exhibited decreases in all age-standardized rate indicators. Furthermore, substantial differences in disease burden distribution by gender and age were observed between the 2 nations. The APC model revealed that despite a reduction in the intrinsic risk among younger generations, population aging is becoming the dominant driver of disease burden in both countries. Projections for the next decade indicate a substantial increase in the burden of ischemic stroke in both nations, particularly in China. Consequently, ischemic stroke poses unprecedented challenges to public health systems, necessitating more robust public health strategies.

Specifically, between 1990 and 2023, China achieved remarkable success in terms of ASMR and DALYs for ischemic stroke. However, this progress contrasts with the rising ASIR and ASPR, indicating that the population affected by ischemic stroke is expanding, which is consistent with previous findings.[8] This diverging trend could potentially reflect the expansion of disease screening and changing diagnostic practices,[8] as well as shifts in health behaviors accompanying economic development.[9] In sharp contrast, although the prevalence rate in the United States has increased, there are favorable trends in ASIR, mortality rates, and DALY rates, which is consistent with previous findings.[10] This suggests that stroke survivors in the United States are living longer, and greater attention should be paid to the long-term care and management of complications in this population. The prevalence of ischemic stroke in China is higher than that in the United States, reflecting the need to further improve the prevention and control of risk factors, such as hypertension, environmental particulate matter pollution, and high LDL cholesterol levels. In the future, efforts should shift from being primarily focused on treatment to prioritizing prevention and management, enhancing tiered diagnosis and treatment as well as community rehabilitation, and improving follow-up systems.[11]

This unequal distribution underscores the urgency to conduct cross-national comparative studies. However, current research evidence on the effectiveness of healthcare systems across different countries remains limited, and this lack of evidence has, to some extent, hindered collaborative progress in the global prevention and management of ischemic stroke.[12] Women demonstrated a more substantial decline in mortality and DALY rates (−62.97% for mortality and −60.19% for DALYs), exhibiting better outcomes than men. This may be attributed to women’s heightened health awareness. Women have been reported to be generally more receptive to health-related information and more likely to modify unhealthy lifestyles. In the United States, improvements in incidence rates were relatively balanced between genders, with men showing greater reductions in mortality rates. This suggests that implementing prevention and control measures tailored to gender differences could be beneficial for reducing the burden of ischemic stroke. Furthermore, the substantial sex disparity in the ischemic stroke burden in China, where men experience a disproportionately higher incidence and prevalence than women, warrants particular attention. This phenomenon strongly correlates with the profoundly gendered patterns of tobacco consumption in the country. Recent nationally representative epidemiological data reveal that while smoking prevalence among Chinese women remains extremely low (approximately 2%), over 50% of adult men are current smokers, accounting for almost all tobacco consumption nationwide.[13] Notably, despite a gradual decline in overall smoking rates, there has been an alarming upward trend among young men residing in rural areas (born after 1990), whose smoking prevalence surged from 40.2% in 2010 to over 52% in 2018.[13] Given that smoking is a potent and modifiable risk factor for cerebrovascular events, this persistent and high exposure among Chinese males – coupled with the rising rates among rural youth – likely serves as a major contributing factor to the escalating ischemic stroke burden in this demographic. Consequently, to effectively mitigate the future threat of ischemic stroke, it is imperative to implement targeted, rigorous tobacco control strategies specifically tailored to male populations and rural communities. Simultaneously, as the world transitions toward aging societies, we observe that the peak prevalence rates in both countries occur among the elderly. Moreover, China’s elderly population exhibits the most rapid growth in absolute case numbers, highlighting the critical role of population aging as an “amplifier” of disease burden. Furthermore, a particularly alarming trend is the rising incidence rates among younger age groups in both countries (defined as 30–39 years old in China and 30–44 years old in the United States). This reveals a global challenge regarding stroke onset at younger ages. Beyond the accumulation of metabolic risk factors during youth, air pollution and longer working hours are also considered emerging risk factors.[14] Both countries should advance cardiovascular risk factor screening and lifestyle interventions in younger populations.

The APC model analysis indicated that across all indicators in both countries, risk increased exponentially with age. Aging is the strongest nonmodifiable risk factor for ischemic stroke.[15] Elderly patients have higher mortality rates and poorer functional outcomes,[16] reflecting irreversible biological processes, such as vascular aging[17] and cumulative atherosclerosis.[18] The age effect analysis revealed a significant finding: after controlling for age structure, the relative risk of mortality across all age groups in the United States was consistently higher than that in China, indicating poorer outcomes for age-matched patients in the United States. Studies suggest this disparity might be influenced by variations in healthcare access and underlying socioeconomic determinants.[19] Period effects revealed distinct public health trajectories between the 2 countries, with China exhibiting a U-shaped pattern. Around 2004 to 2008, China experienced the lowest incidence and prevalence risks for ischemic stroke (RR: 0.91), accompanied by sustained declines in mortality and DALYs. This trend coincided with rapid economic growth and may partially reflect the initial benefits of strengthened public health initiatives during that period. However, all indicators rebounded after 2009 to 2013, with the incidence risk surging to RR 1.22 by 2019 to 2023. This indicates that, compared to the baseline period, the current risk of ischemic stroke among Chinese residents has increased by over 20%. It is plausible that the negative impacts of risk factors such as the rising prevalence of metabolic diseases,[20] air pollution,[21] and shifts in dietary patterns[22] may have begun to offset the earlier gains. In contrast, the United States experienced a sustained decline in period risks for ischemic stroke mortality and DALYs during the earlier period from 1990 to 2010. This earlier phenomenon has often been attributed in the literature to the proactive management of risk factors,[23] advances in acute treatment technologies (such as tissue plasminogen activator),[24] and the implementation of broader public health initiatives.[18] However, this downward trend stalled and reversed after 2010, which may be associated with factors such as the rapid accumulation of metabolic risk factors among younger cohorts[25,26] and population aging.[27] This suggests diminishing marginal benefits from early effective interventions, while responses to new challenges have yet to reverse the overall trend. Regarding cohort effects, both countries observed progressively lower intrinsic risk among more recently born generations, indicating overall declines in incidence and mortality rates across later birth cohorts. This confirms that feasible long-term primary prevention strategies yield cumulative intergenerational protective effects. Across all age cohorts, ischemic stroke incidence, mortality, and DALYs showed consistent downward trends. Notably, the recent decline in ischemic stroke DALYs in the United States has shown signs of stagnation or even reversal. This aligns with findings from U.S. ischemic stroke healthcare cost assessments; as patient survival increases, the healthcare burden of ischemic stroke may increase.[28] In summary, both countries exhibit similar age and cohort effects but divergent period effects. For China, while reaping the dividends of cohort effects, it must immediately curb the prevalence of risk factors through enhanced primary prevention to sustain long-term health gains. For the United States, despite observing the benefits of past public health investments, there is an urgent need for innovative prevention strategies focused on younger populations and health inequities to reverse recent adverse trends.

This study projected the disease burden over the next decade using BAPC models. These conditional forecasts suggest that, if prevailing trends continue, by 2035 ischemic stroke may pose a persistent and significant public health threat to both China and the United States. China may face dual pressures, namely, rising ASIR and absolute case numbers, which would necessitate strengthened primary prevention to reduce population-level risk while expanding healthcare provision. The United States must prioritize long-term care for stroke survivors to mitigate the risk of surging ASPR and DALYs. Finally, our model-based estimates suggest that demographic forces may offset declines in age-standardized rates. Under the assumption of status quo continuation, the projections indicate potentially substantial increases in absolute ischemic stroke cases, deaths, and DALYs in both countries, particularly among men. Given China’s larger population base, its growth will be especially rapid. Therefore, facing this impending crisis, both nations should shift from primarily treatment-oriented approaches to strengthening primary prevention and poststroke rehabilitation as key priorities, while developing targeted interventions for high-risk groups, such as males.

5. Strength and limitations

This study represents the first systematic comparison of differences in the burden of ischemic stroke between China and the United States, revealing the heterogeneity of disease characteristics and highlighting the impact of macro-level factors, such as aging and population growth, on disease burden. It provides epidemiological evidence to inform the development of strategies tailored to the specific circumstances of both nations. Furthermore, utilizing GBD data from 1990 to 2023, we observed long-term trend changes in ischemic stroke. Finally, by analyzing the characteristics of ischemic stroke burden in both nations, this study facilitates proactive planning for healthcare resources and prevention measures, thereby optimizing resource allocation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the reliability of GBD estimates depends on the quality and availability of underlying data sources, including national vital registration systems, hospital discharge records, disease registries, and survey data. The completeness and diagnostic accuracy of these sources vary substantially between China and the United States. China’s vital registration system has historically had incomplete coverage, particularly in rural areas, and variations in diagnostic coding practices may lead to misclassification between ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke subtypes. Although the GBD modeling framework employs sophisticated statistical methods, including spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression and ensemble modeling, to address data gaps and inconsistencies, these modeled estimates inevitably carry greater uncertainty in data-sparse regions. The 95% uncertainty intervals reported in GBD estimates reflect sampling and modeling uncertainty but may not fully capture systematic biases arising from differential data quality between the 2 countries.[29] Consequently, cross-national comparisons should be interpreted with caution, as apparent differences in disease burden may partly reflect disparities in data infrastructure rather than true epidemiological variation. Second, several methodological limitations related to the APC and BAPC models should be acknowledged. The APC model using the IE method, while providing a mathematically unique solution to the APC identification problem, relies on the assumption that the true effects can be adequately represented by a linear combination of age, period, and cohort parameters. This approach may not fully capture nonlinear interactions among these 3 dimensions. Furthermore, the use of 5-year grouping intervals for age and period may obscure finer-grained temporal variations. The BAPC forecasting model assumes that historical trends in age, period, and cohort effects will continue into the future and does not account for potential future policy changes, technological breakthroughs in stroke prevention or treatment, major shifts in risk factor prevalence, or unforeseen events such as pandemics. Therefore, the projections should be interpreted as conditional model-based forecasts representing 1 plausible trajectory under the assumption of status quo continuation, rather than definitive predictions. Additionally, the study lacked granular data on ischemic stroke subtypes or specific risk factor characteristics, limiting our capacity for more targeted etiological interpretations. Moreover, the GBD database does not provide demographic stratifications differentiating urban and rural residency. Consequently, we were unable to conduct a subgroup analysis comparing the stroke burden between urban and rural populations, despite the well-documented disparities in risk factors such as the escalating smoking rates among young rural Chinese males. Finally, the absence of other population-specific analyses, such as those examining young-to-middle-aged individuals with ischemic stroke in both countries, provides avenues for future research.

6. Conclusion

Overall, this study provides a comprehensive analysis and projection of the burden and trends of ischemic stroke in China and the United States. While China has achieved notable success in reducing ASMR and DALYs over the past 30 years, further efforts are required to curb its rising incidence rates. Conversely, although the United States has implemented primary prevention measures effectively, it still faces a significant issue with the large number of stroke survivors. Over the next decade, accelerating population aging may gradually offset the benefits of declining age-standardized rates, leading to a substantial increase in the absolute burden of ischemic stroke in both countries. These findings indicate that both nations must proactively address the growing disease burden. While the elderly constitute the primary high-risk population in both countries, the trend of ischemic stroke burden shifting toward younger age groups also warrants attention. Therefore, beyond prioritizing healthy aging initiatives, both nations must rationally allocate healthcare resources. China should focus on promoting metabolic risk management and healthy lifestyle behaviors to curb rising incidence rates, whereas the United States should enhance supportive care systems for stroke survivors to ensure access to sustained rehabilitation services. Such interventions hold promise for effectively reducing the absolute burden of ischemic stroke in both countries.
