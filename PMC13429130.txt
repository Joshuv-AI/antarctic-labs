Offenders with Autism Spectrum Disorder: A Clinical Case Series and Diagnostic Considerations in Forensic Psychiatry
Authors: Roberto Keller, Giorgia Marino, Donato Liloia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is increasingly recognized in adulthood, including in forensic settings, where its diagnostic and medico-legal implications remain insufficiently defined. Current evidence does not support a simple association between ASD and offending; rather, offender presentations in autistic individuals appear heterogeneous and are likely shaped by the interaction between core autistic features, intellectual and adaptive functioning, comorbidity, developmental adversity, and contextual stressors.Participants and MethodsThis clinical-forensic case series describes seven offenders with ASD assessed within the Italian forensic psychiatric context. The cases encompassed multiple offending domains, including homicide, stalking, and persecutory acts, alleged sexual offending, property-related offending (arson), and domestic abuse. In several instances, ASD was identified for the first time during forensic evaluation in adulthood, often in the context of atypical presentations, variable cognitive functioning, and overlapping psychiatric or personality features.ResultsAcross cases, offending-related conduct appeared to arise through different psychopathological and developmental pathways, including rigidity and restricted interests, social naivety, impaired contextual understanding, concrete or idiosyncratic interpretations of interpersonal situations, emotional dysregulation, interpersonal vulnerability, and co-occurring psychopathology. The series also highlighted substantial diagnostic challenges in forensic practice, particularly where autism had remained previously unrecognized. These findings suggest that the forensic relevance of ASD lies not in diagnosis alone, but in the specific way autistic characteristics interact with cognition, adaptation, and contextual demands in shaping the behaviour under examination.ConclusionThis case series supports the need for a clinically integrated, developmentally informed, and individualized forensic psychiatric assessment of offenders with ASD. Standardized instruments may be informative, but only when interpreted within a broader formulation incorporating developmental history, cognitive and adaptive functioning, psychiatric comorbidity, and contextual factors. Improved recognition of underdiagnosed or atypical adult presentations may enhance diagnostic accuracy and support more appropriate decisions regarding criminal responsibility, risk management, and treatment planning.Keywords: autism, forensic, offenders, criminal responsibility, violence, imputability, social dangerousness

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex group of neurodevelopmental conditions with a multifactorial etiology, involving both genetic and epigenetic factors, and typically emerging during the early years of life.1 Current estimates suggest that ASD affects approximately 1% of the global population, with cases reported across all world regions, among individuals from diverse ethnic backgrounds, and in both low- and middle-income countries.2–5 According to the most recent data released by the Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring (ADDM) Network, the estimated prevalence of ASD in the United States has reached 1 in 36 children aged 8 years.6 These figures, however, should be interpreted cautiously, as they are partly derived from surveillance systems that may not consistently rely on comprehensive diagnostic evaluations. Nevertheless, epidemiological data indicate a progressive increase in the recorded prevalence of ASD over the last two decades.7

ASD is characterized by persistent difficulties in social communication and social interaction across multiple contexts, together with restricted and repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests, or activities.8 These manifestations may involve reduced social-emotional reciprocity, atypical nonverbal communication, difficulties in developing and maintaining interpersonal relationships, behavioural rigidity, circumscribed interests, and atypical sensory responsivity.9 Symptoms must be present during the early developmental period, although they may become fully apparent only later in life, including in adulthood.10–13 A diagnosis of ASD also requires that these features be associated with clinically significant impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning. This criterion is particularly relevant because it distinguishes ASD as a disorder from broader neurodivergent traits or atypical, non-pathological variations in human functioning.11 The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR)8 also identifies three levels of support associated with ASD severity: Level 1, requiring support; Level 2, requiring substantial support; and Level 3, requiring very substantial support. Consideration of these support levels is clinically important, as they provide a framework for describing the extent of assistance required in everyday functioning and for contextualizing the individual’s difficulties in social communication and behavioural flexibility. At the same time, they should not be interpreted as exhaustive descriptors of overall functioning, since adaptive skills, cognitive abilities, and support needs may vary substantially across domains and contexts.14–16 This issue is clinically important because intellectual ability (IQ) and adaptive functioning (ie, the practical, real-world skills required for independent living, social judgement, and self-regulation) do not necessarily covary in ASD, and a substantial IQ/adaptive-functioning gap is common even among individuals without co-occurring intellectual disability;17,18 this discrepancy is directly relevant to forensic evaluations of capacity, since preserved IQ may coexist with markedly limited real-world adaptive competence.18

The relationship between ASD and offending remains complex and incompletely understood. Current evidence does not support the conclusion that autistic individuals, as a group, are at a consistently increased overall risk of criminal offending compared with the general population; rather, the literature is characterized by substantial methodological heterogeneity, variable sampling strategies, and important differences in diagnostic ascertainment and criminal justice outcomes across studies. Systematic reviews have therefore emphasized that findings in this field must be interpreted cautiously and that simple causal inferences between ASD and offending are not justified.19–22 At the same time, autistic individuals may come into contact with the criminal justice system as suspects, defendants, offenders, or detainees, and such contact appears to arise from a multifactorial interaction between core autistic features, co-occurring psychiatric conditions, developmental history, and contextual stressors, rather than from any uniform antisocial propensity.19,23 In particular, difficulties in social reciprocity, atypical interpretation of nonverbal cues and narrative coherence of an experienced event, behavioural rigidity, circumscribed interests, and reduced contextual modulation of behaviour may, under specific circumstances, contribute to socially inappropriate conduct, perseverative or intrusive behaviours, or poorly adapted responses to interpersonal conflict or emotionally charged situations.11,24,25 These features may be especially relevant in forensic contexts when they coexist with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, mood or anxiety disorders, substance misuse, trauma-related vulnerabilities, or impaired adaptive functioning.23,26 Beyond acute situational triggers, chronic stress may also play a cumulative role: autistic individuals are considered particularly vulnerable to “autistic burnout”, a state of pervasive exhaustion, skill regression, and reduced tolerance to stimuli that can result from the sustained effort of navigating a predominantly neurotypical social and occupational environment,27–29 and which may progressively erode coping capacity and behavioural regulation over time, potentially offering a cumulative-stress pathway to offending that is distinct from any single precipitating event. Moreover, these behavioural patterns may be partly related to neurodevelopmental differences within limbic, paralimbic, and frontostriatal circuits, including the amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex, insula, prefrontal cortex, striatum, and hippocampus. Neuroimaging studies in ASD have reported age-dependent differences in amygdala morphology and growth, as well as altered functional connectivity between the amygdala and medial prefrontal, temporal, and striatal regions.30–33 These circuit-level differences have been associated with atypical socio-emotional processing, threat and safety discrimination, and emotion regulation.34–37

Importantly, the forensic relevance of ASD in offender populations also lies in the risk of misinterpretation. Recent research has shown that autistic adults may be erroneously perceived as more deceptive or less credible because of atypical gaze, body movements, reciprocity, or affective presentation, and that autism-characteristic behaviour may bias judgments of guilt in forensic settings.38–42 This issue is particularly relevant in investigative and judicial contexts, where communication style, apparent emotional incongruity, or unusual interpersonal presentation may be incorrectly interpreted as evasiveness, lack of remorse, or manipulativeness.38,43 In parallel, consensus-based guidance now emphasizes that autism is still frequently under-identified within criminal justice settings and that autistic offenders and suspects require more structured recognition and support pathways.26 This risk of misinterpretation may be further compounded by diagnostic overshadowing or diagnostic substitution, whereby autism-related traits and communication differences are attributed to another psychiatric disorder, particularly a personality or psychotic disorder, thereby obscuring an underlying ASD.44,45 This problem may be especially salient in forensic practice, where autism may remain unrecognized until an offence-related forensic psychiatric assessment is undertaken.46,47 The literature also suggests that offending in autistic individuals is not a unitary phenomenon. Rather, different offending-related presentations may be associated with different mechanisms, including social naivety, obsessive or highly restricted interests, social knowledge deficits, interpersonal misunderstandings, emotional dysregulation, and comorbid psychopathology.19–21,48–50 For example, this heterogeneity is particularly evident in the area of sexual offending, where a recent systematic review22 concluded that the evidence remains limited and varied, but sufficient to indicate the need for careful, individualized clinical-forensic formulation rather than stereotyped assumptions.

Against this background, case-based contributions remain particularly valuable, as they can illuminate the different pathways through which autistic characteristics, adaptive functioning, and contextual stressors intersect in offender presentations.19,21 This appears especially relevant in the Italian forensic context, where the intersection between ASD, criminal responsibility, and the implementation of security measures remains largely underexplored. Under the Italian Penal Code, criminal responsibility may be excluded or substantially diminished when, at the time of the offence, an infirmity completely abolished or greatly impaired the individual’s capacity to understand or to will;51 both faculties (ie, the capacity to understand the wrongfulness of the act and the capacity to act in accordance with that understanding) must be preserved for an individual to be considered fully imputable, and are assessed separately. Where one of the two is entirely abolished, criminal responsibility is excluded; where it is diminished, the individual remains imputable but is sentenced with a statutory reduction of up to one third. In this framework, the medico-legal relevance of a psychiatric or neurodevelopmental condition does not rest on diagnosis alone, but on its demonstrated impact on mental state at the time of the offence and on its nexus with the criminal act; this makes structured and individualized insanity evaluations particularly important in complex clinical presentations.51–53 In both scenarios, the court may also address the issue of social dangerousness, which remains central to the Italian system of security measures for mentally disordered offenders. Following the closure of the Ospedali Psichiatrici Giudiziari (the special psychiatric hospitals historically dedicated to mentally disordered offenders) and the reform introduced by Law 81/2014, the Italian model has increasingly emphasized therapeutic and community-based management, while internment in REMS (Residenze per l’Esecuzione delle Misure di Sicurezza) is conceived as an exceptional or last-resort custodial measure rather than the default response.51,54 The assessment of social dangerousness is linked to recidivism risk, is judicially determined, and must be periodically reviewed; moreover, less restrictive measures such as supervised liberty may be preferred when clinically and legally appropriate.51,54 This legal and institutional framework makes individualized forensic psychiatric assessment particularly important in offenders with ASD, because diagnostic labels alone are insufficient to determine imputability, social dangerousness, treatment needs, or the most appropriate placement.19,21,51

In this perspective, case-based contributions may help clarify how ASD is expressed in different offender presentations. In the present case series, we describe offenders with ASD across a range of clinical-forensic domains, including homicide, stalking and persecutory acts, alleged sexual offending, property-related (arson) offences, and domestic abuse. The aim is to provide a varied clinical overview of the psychopathological and forensic issues that may arise in autistic offenders within contemporary Italian forensic psychiatric practice.

Materials and Methods

This study reports a clinical-forensic case series of seven adult individuals with ASD evaluated within the Italian forensic psychiatric system. All cases were assessed by a forensic psychiatrist and trained psychologist at the Adult Autism Center of the Mental Health Department of the Local Health Unit ASL Città di Torino, which provides specialist autism services for the Piedmont region, Turin, Italy. The cases originated from routine forensic psychiatric evaluations requested by different judges or, in one case, by the public prosecutor, in the context of separate criminal proceedings. Taken together, they encompass several forensic domains in which ASD may become relevant, including homicide, stalking and persecutory acts, alleged sexual offending, property-related (arson) offending, and domestic abuse, and illustrate the heterogeneity of clinical-forensic presentations encountered in practice.

Diagnostic Procedures

Diagnostic procedures followed current consensus guidelines for adult ASD assessment.11 They were carried out by a multidisciplinary team comprising adult psychiatrists with expertise in ASD, clinical psychologists, and neuropsychologists. Diagnoses were established through an integrated procedure including (i) clinical interview and direct examination based on DSM-5-TR criteria;8 (ii) reconstruction of developmental history with informants (parents, school records) where available; (iii) administration of the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule – Second Edition (ADOS-2), Module 4; (iv) cognitive assessment with the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale – Fourth Edition (WAIS-IV)55 or, in cases originally assessed in childhood, the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children – Fourth Edition (WISC-IV);56 (v) where clinically indicated, the Autism Diagnostic Interview – Revised (ADI-R),57 the Diagnostic Interview for ADHD in Adults (DIVA)58 and the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Personality Disorders (SCID-5); and (vi) genetic investigations (array-CGH, Fragile-X testing, karyotype) when indicated by the clinical profile.

Forensic-Psychiatric Evaluation

Forensic-psychiatric conclusions on imputability and social dangerousness were derived from court-appointed expert reports (ie, perizia d’ufficio), based on an integrated clinical formulation rather than on stand-alone psychometric instruments. Imputability was assessed by examining, separately, the capacity for understanding and the capacity for volition at the time of the offence, in accordance with Italian Penal Code provisions and current Italian forensic psychiatric standards.51,53 Social dangerousness and the consequent need for security measures were appraised through integrated clinical-forensic judgement.

Ethics and Consent

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was reviewed and approved by the COMITATO ETICO TERRITORIALE (CET) INTERAZIENDALE AOU CITTA’ DELLA SALUTE E DELLA SCIENZA DI TORINO, under approval/reference number 0000959. Personal privacy was protected throughout the handling of personal data, and all case information was de-identified and treated confidentially. Written informed consent to participate in the study and to publish the anonymized case details was obtained from the participants or, where applicable, from their legal guardians; for the forensic-psychiatric cases, participant protection was ensured through rigorous de-identification, with no personal or situational reference retained.

Homicide-Related Cases

Two individuals with ASD, Level 1, were identified among the homicide-related cases. In both cases, ASD had not been previously diagnosed and was first recognized during adulthood in the course of the forensic psychiatric evaluation. As a result, neither individual had previously received autism-specific assessment, treatment, or psychosocial support.

In the first case, the offence was committed at the age of 24 years. The individual was a male living with his parents in a rural area, with no prior criminal record. At the age of 6–7 years, he had been privately followed for selective mutism, in the context of marked impairments in peer relationships and social communication. The selective mutism resolved within approximately two years, but the underlying social and communicative difficulties persisted. The family arranged a privately funded support teacher rather than referring him to child neuropsychiatric services. Since early childhood, he had shown a circumscribed interest in plants, which persisted into adulthood. He had not developed friendships with peers nor any affective or romantic relationships, and he had never displayed aggressive behaviour. At the time of the homicide, the individual lived with his family and was employed in the family business, an activity closely aligned with his circumscribed and longstanding interests. His social functioning was markedly restricted: he had no friendships outside the family context and showed minimal, if any, motivation for social interaction or for the development of intimate or affective relationships. His interpersonal style also included prominent schizoid personality traits. The homicide occurred in a context in which the victim had, in some way, interfered with the activity linked to the individual’s restricted interests, thereby threatening his highly routinized and psychologically protective zone of comfort.

From a diagnostic standpoint, in addition to the lifelong history of impaired social communication and reciprocal social interaction with deficits in non-verbal communication and in establishing peer relationships, the evaluation documented marked cognitive rigidity, insistence on sameness, sensory hypersensitivity, and circumscribed interests. The ADOS-2, Module 4, yielded a combined Communication + Reciprocal Social Interaction score of 7, at the cut-off for the autism spectrum.59 Cognitive assessment with WAIS-IV showed a Full-Scale Intelligence Quotient (FSIQ) of 67, with Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI) = 78, Perceptual Reasoning Index (PRI) = 83, Working Memory Index (WMI) = 63, and Processing Speed Index (PSI) = 75. Additional composite indices showed a General Ability Index (GAI) of 79 and a Cognitive Proficiency Index (CPI) of 60. This profile suggests globally reduced intellectual functioning, with a relatively higher estimate of general reasoning abilities when working memory and processing speed are deemphasized, and a marked weakness in cognitive proficiency, particularly working memory. The ADI-R, administered to the parents to reconstruct early developmental features, returned subthreshold scores, a pattern that may occur in higher-functioning autistic adults.11 Co-occurring schizoid personality traits further complicated the autistic phenotype; even while incarcerated, the individual preferred complete social isolation and spent his time looking exclusively at photographs related to his circumscribed interest.

The forensic psychiatric assessment did not identify any impairment of reality testing of the kind characteristic of psychotic states. He was able to grasp the wrongfulness of the act and was therefore considered to have preserved capacity for understanding. By contrast, his capacity for volition was judged to be diminished by the combination of cognitive rigidity, impaired problem-solving, and intellectual limitation, which markedly constrained his ability to generate alternative responses to the relational conflict with the victim that threatened the continuation of his circumscribed interest. His extreme ideational rigidity confined him within a “behavioural tunnel” in which homicide was perceived as the only available response, while his social and emotional isolation prevented him from seeking external perspectives to challenge his thinking. The clinical picture did not amount to a delusional psychotic state, in which the individual remains entrenched in his ideation despite confrontation, and would be judged totally incapable of both understanding and willing, rather, reflected an absolute lack of dialectical confrontation arising from autistic and schizoid social-relational impairment combined with ideational inflexibility. It should be emphasized that the diagnosis of ASD was made only in the course of the forensic evaluation, so that no prior habilitative or psychological intervention had been provided.

In the second case, the autistic individual took part in a homicide in a passive and subordinate role, appearing unable to oppose the directives of a dominant co-perpetrator, who also represented his only significant friendship. The offence was committed when the individual was 30 years old; he was a male who had lived continuously with his parents and had displayed marked social isolation since childhood, with no friendships and no later affective or romantic relationships. During secondary school, he had been the target of severe bullying, including episodes in which his limbs were burned with a lighter; it was in that period that he met the principal perpetrator of the homicide, who became his sole friend during adolescence and, subsequently, also his employer. This individual likewise lived with his family, had no meaningful social relationships apart from the co-offender, and showed little or no inclination toward romantic or family life, again suggesting schizoid personality traits.

As in the first case, the diagnosis of ASD was made only at the time of the forensic evaluation, no prior educational or psychological support having been provided. Beyond the developmental history of social-communicative deficits and insistence on sameness, the diagnosis was supported psychometrically: the ADOS-2, Module 4 yielded a score of 11 (cut-off for autism: 10), and the cognitive profile on the WAIS-IV (VCI 106, PRI 77, WMI 72, PSI 81; FSIQ 82) was consistent with borderline intellectual functioning and a particular weakness in working memory. No psychotic episodes were documented. The capacity for understanding was again considered fully preserved, as he recognized the wrongful nature of homicide. With respect to the capacity for volition, however, the autistic condition had narrowed his social world to a single friendship; when this “friend” asked him to participate in the homicide, he had been unwilling to take direct part in the act but had also been unable to withdraw from complicity. As in the first case, the clinical formulation pointed to a deficit in social cognition, extreme ideational rigidity, an inability to consider alternative courses of action, and the absence of friendships or affective relationships through which alternatives could have been deliberated. The fact that the bond with the co-offender had originally formed during a period of severe adolescent bullying, as his only available friendship, is likely to have further reinforced the rigidity of this attachment.

From a forensic psychiatric perspective, in both cases, the capacity for understanding was considered preserved, whereas the capacity for volition was judged to be substantially impaired. Both individuals were therefore assessed as partially criminally responsible. Concerning risk management and forensic disposition, the first case was considered to present a high degree of social dangerousness requiring custodial measures, whereas in the second case, social dangerousness was considered manageable within an outpatient treatment program.

Cases Involving Stalking and Persecutory Acts

Two forensic cases involved individuals with ASD who had engaged in stalking or persecutory behaviours.

In the first case, the diagnosis of ASD, Level 1, with intellectual functioning within the normal range, was made only in the context of the forensic psychiatric evaluation. The individual was 22 years old at the time of the offence. Since childhood, he had displayed marked socio-relational isolation despite cognitive functioning fully within the normal range, and he had completed his secondary-school diploma. He lived with his family, had no friendships and no affective relationships, and worked alone as a delivery rider. He had no prior criminal record. The ADOS-2 administered during the forensic evaluation yielded a score positive for the autism spectrum. With respect to the offence, he had developed a highly selective interest in a female peer and engaged in repeated following behaviours, including appearing near her home, outside her school, and remaining for prolonged periods in front of her residence, despite attempts by the victim’s friends and by the police to discourage such conduct. A particularly relevant clinical-forensic aspect of this case was that his preoccupation with the victim did not appear to be motivated by romantic or sexual interest. Rather, it was related to a sensory-based fascination, specifically focused on the woman’s hair and on the way its reflection appeared in a window.

In the second case, the individual, also diagnosed with ASD, Level 1, but with mild intellectual disability (WAIS-IV: FSIQ 59, VCI 67, PRI 60; ADOS-2 Module 4 Language + Reciprocal Social Interaction score 12; cut-off for autism: 10; for autism spectrum: 7), had occasionally witnessed conflicts between a married couple. Motivated by a misguided wish to “protect” the woman in a chivalric and concrete manner, he began leaving on her car dashboard newspaper clippings about femicides, flyers from anti-violence centres, and Bible passages concerning justice. He described these acts as attempts to “educate” the woman to defend herself and to “seek justice”. Although the underlying intention may have been perceived by him as prosocial or protective, the intrusive and inappropriate form of communication caused persistent anxiety and concern in the victim, eventually leading to a police report and a criminal investigation. He was 35 years old at the time of the offence and was living with his family; he had no friendships and had never had any affective relationship. He had consistently refused educational support in the past because he rejected both his disability and his diagnosis of ASD. Although potentially capable of pursuing some form of employment, this denial of his condition had prevented him from accessing work or building relational pathways, and he tended to position himself as superior to other peers with disabilities despite sharing comparable difficulties. He had displayed social-communicative deficits since early childhood and had received a diagnosis of autism in childhood with educational and school-based support activated by child neuropsychiatric services. The family, however, had explained this support to the child as an “administrative error” and reassured him that he was “perfectly equal to his classmates”. Although well-intentioned, this familial stance had ultimately consolidated his denial of his own difficulties and of his need for support, with consequences for adult autonomy.

From a forensic psychiatric perspective, both individuals were assessed as partially criminally responsible. In each case, the capacity to grasp the wrongfulness of the act was diminished, without reaching the level of impairment of reality testing typical of psychotic conditions. In the first case, the individual, absorbed in his sensory-based circumscribed interest, did not fully appreciate the extent to which his behaviour generated anxiety and distress in the other person; this reflected a deficit in theory of mind and in inferring the mental states of others. In the second case, the impairment lay in the comprehension of context and of shared social rules, such that a behaviour intended as protective acquired, in conventional social meaning, the connotation of generating fear, alarm, and concern; again expressing a deficit of social cognition.

In both cases, social dangerousness was considered low and manageable within therapeutic settings. In the first case, risk management was implemented through placement in a residential facility, whereas in the second case, it was addressed through ongoing supervision by community psychiatric services and an autism specialist centre.

Case Involving Alleged Sexual Offending

We describe the case of an adult individual diagnosed with ASD, Level 2, and moderate intellectual disability, who became involved in criminal proceedings for an alleged sexual offence. The diagnosis of ASD had been made in childhood, when he had displayed social-relational isolation together with vocal and motor stereotypies and inattention/hyperactivity across multiple contexts; the ADI-R and ADOS-2 administered during childhood had both been positive, and the WISC-IV had documented a Full-Scale IQ of 40 (PRI 48, VCI 70). Array-CGH and Fragile-X testing were unremarkable, and an educational support plan was in place. He lived with his family and had only limited autonomy of movement, restricted to familiar routes. He was 18 years old at the time of the alleged offence.

While walking alone along an isolated street at dusk, he encountered a girl who was also alone. Reportedly wishing to “get to know” her, he approached her and attempted to “hug” her. The girl became frightened, ran away, and subsequently filed a police report. He was later identified by the police and brought to the station. A particularly relevant element emerged during the psychiatric evaluation requested by the public prosecutor. When the defence psychiatrist asked him, “When you hugged her, did you intend to engage in sexual activity?”, the young man appeared surprised by the question, looked at both clinicians, and replied with evident alarm: “No, those things are for grown-ups, and you have to get married first… I do not want to get married; I want to stay with my mother”. This response was considered clinically significant, as it suggested a markedly immature and concrete understanding of interpersonal and sexual behaviour, as well as limited awareness of the meaning that his conduct could assume from the perspective of the other person and within a broader social and legal context.

From a forensic psychiatric perspective, the individual was considered to be completely lacking both capacity for understanding and capacity for volition at the time of the act. At the same time, social dangerousness was assessed as low and manageable through a legally defined outpatient treatment program under psychiatric supervision.

Case Involving a Property-Related Offence (Arson)

This case concerns an adult individual diagnosed with ASD, Level 2, and moderate intellectual disability, who set fire to his own apartment during the night in a residential building, thereby exposing other occupants to a potentially catastrophic risk. A more serious outcome was prevented only because his sister happened to pass near the apartment and promptly alerted the emergency services, allowing the Fire Brigade to intervene in time and contain the fire. The damage was ultimately limited to the destruction of the family dwelling.

The individual was known to public mental health services: ASD had been diagnosed in childhood, and an educational support plan was in place, although he had repeatedly struggled to accept it, denying that he required support. Cognitive assessment with the WAIS-IV indicated a Full-Scale IQ of 42 (VCI 55, PRI 54, WMI 60, PSI 50). The ADOS-2, Module 4, yielded a Language + Reciprocal Social Interaction score of 15 (cut-off for autism: 10; for autism spectrum: 7). Genetic testing identified a 4p13 microduplication and an XXY karyotype (Klinefelter syndrome). The individual was 21 years old at the time of the offence.

From a clinical-forensic perspective, the act appeared to be associated not only with the individual’s neurodevelopmental condition, but also with the presence of antisocial and retaliatory personality traits, which were considered contributory to the offence. The fire had been set during the night, several hours after a family argument, and a retaliatory motive appeared to underlie the act. The forensic psychiatric evaluation concluded that the individual was partially criminally responsible. Social dangerousness was assessed as low, but sufficiently significant to require structured containment and support within a residential community placement, which was activated as the most appropriate setting for ongoing management and risk reduction.

Case Involving Domestic Abuse

This case concerns a male diagnosed with ASD, Level 1, with mild intellectual disability and borderline personality features. The individual was 19 years old at the time of the offence. Developmentally, his first words appeared at around 12 months of age but were followed by an extended period of impaired language acquisition, with first sentences only at 8 years. Early social-relational isolation, repetitive play during the first years of life, inappropriate conduct toward peers in childhood, and tactile and olfactory hypersensitivity were present, and a diagnosis of ASD was made at 10 years of age. On the WAIS-IV, he obtained a Full-Scale IQ of 59 (VCI 67, PRI 63, WMI 80, PSI 67); the ADOS-2 was positive for ASD, and genetic evaluation identified an Xq13.1q13.2 duplication. He had been the target of bullying at school, completed lower secondary education, attended upper secondary school without obtaining a diploma, and was admitted to a psychiatric inpatient ward at 17 years of age for hetero-aggressive behaviour; in adulthood, he had been placed in a residential community from which he later absconded. A pattern of persistent and escalating conflict within the family context emerged over time, ultimately leading his parents to file a formal complaint. He had also displayed threatening and intimidating behaviours in the school setting.

The broader psychosocial context appeared to be relevant. The individual lived in a peripheral metropolitan environment characterized by cultural dynamics that seemed to reinforce a rigid and confrontation-based code of honour, which he appeared to internalize and reproduce in his interpersonal behaviour. A marked variability in behavioural presentation was observed across contexts: during outpatient clinical contacts, he appeared appropriate, compliant, and cooperative, whereas in family and school settings, he was described as hostile, oppositional, and threatening. In addition to ASD, an attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder was diagnosed based on a positive Diagnostic Interview for ADHD in Adults (DIVA), and a borderline personality disorder was identified using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Personality Disorders (SCID-5).

From a forensic psychiatric perspective, he was assessed as partially criminally responsible. Social dangerousness was considered low, but sufficient to require structured containment and supervision in a residential facility, where placement was subsequently arranged. This case illustrates the interaction between multiple contributing factors. Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a frequent co-occurring condition in ASD and can contribute to emotional dysregulation.60–63 Although less commonly associated with ASD,64 borderline personality disorder may nonetheless be present and can, in turn, aggravate emotional, ideational, and affective dysregulation.65 In the present case, the peripheral urban context further appeared to nurture dysregulated and aggressive behaviour while also imposing the subcultural rules of the local environment.

Discussion

The growing recognition of ASD, including in adulthood,11,66,67 requires increasing attention also within the forensic field. In autistic individuals, the interpretation of interpersonal situations and the modulation of behavioural responses may be shaped by distinctive patterns of cognitive flexibility, emotional regulation, and social understanding, with important variability across individuals and contexts.68–71 From this perspective, the cases presented here suggest that offending in ASD should not be interpreted through a single explanatory model, but rather as the possible outcome of different developmental and psychopathological pathways. In particular, some presentations appear to involve markedly restricted behavioural repertoires, difficulties in shifting perspective, and reduced capacity to generate alternative responses once a maladaptive course of action has been initiated.72,73 Even in the presence of preserved or relatively preserved intellectual functioning, this combination may contribute to contextually inadequate or poorly modulated reactions under conditions of interpersonal stress, frustration, or emotional overload.74–76 It is also noteworthy that several individuals in this series had not received an autism diagnosis before the forensic assessment. This is clinically relevant, as late-recognized autism has been associated with longstanding social vulnerability, frequent diagnostic complexity, and reduced access to timely post-diagnostic support, all of which may further compromise adaptation in complex real-world situations.45,66,67,77–79 The clinical, psychometric and forensic-psychiatric profiles of the seven cases are summarized descriptively in Figure 1, which provides the quantitative background to the case-level interpretation developed in the following subsections.

Clinical Interpretation of the Cases

In the stalking- and persecution-related cases, autistic features appeared to play a central role in shaping the offending presentation. In the first case, the behaviour seemed to be driven primarily by a restricted, highly specific interest, with sensory salience being a particularly important component of the individual’s focus. This is clinically relevant, because restricted interests in ASD are not equivalent to compulsions in the obsessive–compulsive sense.80,81 Whereas compulsions are typically experienced as anxiety-driven and ego-dystonic, restricted interests and repetitive behaviours in autism are often more ego-syntonic, intrinsically rewarding, and linked to sameness, salience, or fascination. Under these circumstances, social rules and interpersonal boundaries may become less salient relative to the motivational pull of the interest itself, particularly when social understanding is limited.82–84

The second stalking-related case illustrates a different but equally relevant mechanism. Here, the behaviour did not appear to arise from hostility or predatory intent, but from a concrete and socially clumsy attempt to “protect” a woman perceived as vulnerable. The individual’s intention may have been subjectively prosocial; however, his poor appreciation of interpersonal boundaries and of the victim’s emotional perspective produced the opposite effect, resulting in repeated intrusive acts that were experienced as threatening. This pattern is consistent with forensic observations that, in some autistic individuals, difficulties in interpreting social reciprocity, understanding the subjective experience of others, and modulating behaviour according to context may contribute to stalking-like or persecutory presentations, even in the absence of conventional malicious intent.85,86

The alleged sexual offending case points to another area of major clinical relevance, namely sexuality, privacy, and boundary education in ASD. The literature does not support simplistic assumptions about sexuality in autistic individuals;22,87–89 however, problematic or inappropriate sexual behaviours have been described in a subgroup of cases, particularly where there is intellectual disability, limited sexual knowledge, poor differentiation between public and private behaviour, or inadequate understanding of consent and relational boundaries.90,91 For this reason, sexuality and affective education should not be neglected or postponed. On the contrary, autistic adolescents and adults require structured, explicit, and developmentally appropriate education on privacy, consent, interpersonal boundaries, and context-dependent social behaviour, ideally involving both the individual and the family system.92 In this respect, over-permissive or inconsistent responses from caregivers, teachers, or other adults to boundary-crossing behaviours may inadvertently reinforce conduct that is later reproduced in inappropriate contexts, especially in individuals with intellectual disability or marked concrete thinking.

Finally, the present case series also highlights the importance of psychiatric comorbidity in forensic assessment. ASD may co-occur with a broad range of psychiatric conditions, including mood and anxiety disorders, obsessive–compulsive symptoms, psychotic spectrum conditions, and personality pathology, all of which may substantially modify both clinical presentation and offending-related risk formulation.65,93–96 This is particularly important because comorbidity may contribute to emotional dysregulation, impulsivity, suspiciousness, trauma-related reactivity, or maladaptive interpersonal strategies that are not fully explained by autism alone. In this regard, bullying victimization and other adverse developmental experiences deserve careful exploration, given their well-documented association with later psychopathology and social vulnerability in autistic people.65 Likewise, the overlap between ASD and personality pathology, including borderline and schizoid traits, remains a clinically important area, especially in individuals whose autism was not recognized early and who therefore lacked timely support and developmental scaffolding.65,95,96 ADHD, which frequently co-occurs with ASD, can further contribute to behavioural dysregulation;60,62,63 in the same vein, the subcultural codes operating in some peripheral urban environments may amplify dysfunctional personality features, as illustrated by the domestic-abuse case in the present series.

Diagnosing ASD in Forensic Settings: Clinical Assessment and Use of Instruments

In the present case series, one of the most relevant findings was that the diagnosis of ASD in forensic settings could not be meaningfully established based on psychometric data alone; however, it required a clinically integrated and developmentally informed formulation. This was particularly evident in cases involving late-recognized autism, intellectual disability, marked adaptive impairment, psychiatric comorbidity, or overlap with personality traits, where the forensic meaning of behaviour could not be understood without reconstructing the broader developmental trajectory of the individual.97–99 Current guidance is consistent with this view, emphasizing that ASD diagnosis in adulthood remains fundamentally clinical and should be based on developmental history, direct examination, and collateral information, with instruments used only as complementary tools within a broader assessment process.11,100

Our cases also suggest that, in forensic practice, the main diagnostic difficulty often lies not only in identifying ASD itself, but in distinguishing autistic features from partially overlapping phenomena such as psychosis, personality pathology, behavioural dyscontrol, trauma-related reactions, or socially maladaptive conduct.65,101–104 For this reason, cognitive assessment is informative only when interpreted beyond overall scores, taking into account the individual pattern of cognitive strengths and weaknesses, executive functioning, and possible discrepancies between intellectual abilities and real-world adaptive functioning.17,18,73 This is especially important in forensic evaluations, where individuals may present with preserved or relatively preserved formal abilities while remaining markedly impaired in social judgement, contextual understanding, or behavioural modulation.40,41,105–107 Particular attention should therefore be paid to social cognition and to the individual’s capacity to adapt to context and to anticipate the consequences of their own behaviour.108

Particular caution is warranted when self-report measures are used in autistic adults. Literal interpretation of items, reduced insight, difficulty generalizing across contexts, and diagnostic overshadowing may all distort questionnaire-based responses, especially in individuals who have never previously received an autism diagnosis or structured support.45,109 This risk of diagnostic overshadowing extends beyond questionnaire-based assessment to the forensic evaluation itself:26,110 behavioural rigidity, restricted affect, unusual narrative style, or intense, circumscribed interests may be misread as personality pathology (eg, paranoid, schizoid, or narcissistic traits)65 or, in more marked presentations, mistaken for prodromal or attenuated psychotic phenomena,111 particularly when the underlying autism has never been previously recognized; disentangling these overlapping presentations requires a longitudinal developmental perspective that purely cross-sectional psychiatric assessment cannot provide.112 In this respect, instruments such as the RAADS-R may still be clinically informative, but only if interpreted within an autism-informed clinical framework rather than as stand-alone indicators.113 Retrospective developmental instruments also remain important, but are not without limitations in adulthood. In particular, the ADI-R57 may provide valuable support in reconstructing early developmental features, yet its usefulness in adult forensic assessment depends heavily on the availability of reliable informants and on the quality of retrospective anamnesis.114 This may be especially problematic in older adults, in complex family situations, or when developmental history is fragmentary. In addition, findings from adult samples with intellectual disability suggest that the performance and feasibility of ADI-R-based assessment may vary substantially across contexts, reinforcing the need to interpret such data within a broader clinical formulation rather than in isolation.114,115 Similarly, structured observational instruments such as the ADOS-259 may provide useful behavioural data, but their results must be interpreted cautiously in complex adult psychiatric populations, where false positives have been documented, including in cases involving psychosis and other severe mental disorders.116

Another implication of the present study concerns under-recognized or atypical adult presentations. Several individuals in this series had not received a diagnosis until the forensic evaluation, suggesting that clinically significant autistic features may remain undetected for years when they are masked by apparently preserved language, partial social adaptation, intellectual variability, or co-occurring psychopathology.45,66,67 This issue is likely to be even more relevant in groups already known to be diagnostically vulnerable, including females and adults with more camouflaged or internalized presentations, for whom conventional diagnostic expectations may be less sensitive.117–119

Future research should build on these observations in several directions. Longitudinal studies following autistic individuals from childhood or early adulthood could clarify which developmental and contextual factors predict later contact with the criminal justice system, moving beyond the retrospective, cross-sectional perspective inherent to case series. Prospective follow-up of forensic dispositions is also needed to examine the clinical, behavioural, and social outcomes associated with custodial measures, residential placements, outpatient programmes, and community-based interventions, and to identify which forms of management are most appropriate for different clinical and risk profiles. Given the marked under-representation of female cases in the present series and in the broader forensic-autism literature, dedicated studies focusing on autistic women who offend, and on how camouflaging and internalized presentations may shape their forensic trajectories, are particularly needed. Finally, comparative studies systematically contrasting autistic and non-autistic offenders on relevant clinical, cognitive, and offence-related variables would help clarify which mechanisms are potentially specific to ASD and which reflect more general risk factors shared across offender populations.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the present case series includes only seven individuals, all evaluated within a single Italian region and through a single specialist autism service; the cases are therefore not representative of the wider population of autistic offenders, and generalization should be cautious. Second, no female cases were included, despite growing recognition that ASD in adult women is frequently underdiagnosed and may have distinct forensic implications.118,119 Although this absence reflects local case ascertainment within a specialist service rather than an explicit selection criterion, it remains an important gap for future case series to address. Third, in several cases, the reconstruction of early developmental history relied on retrospective parental recall, with the known limitations of such reports in adulthood.114 Fourth, no systematic prospective follow-up data are reported; the longer-term effectiveness of the dispositional measures adopted, including the outcomes of residential placement, community-based supervision, and outpatient programmes, remains to be evaluated. Fifth, diagnosis and offending-pathway formulation were not derived from independent sources of evidence: both stemmed from the same forensic-psychiatric evaluation, so the interpretive framework informing the ASD diagnosis inevitably also informed the reconstruction of the offence. Standardized instruments and a multidisciplinary team constrain, but do not eliminate, this circularity, which is structural to qualitative forensic case formulation rather than specific to the present series. Finally, as in any clinical-forensic case series, the descriptions provided are necessarily selective and shaped by the interpretive framework of the assessing clinicians; alternative formulations of the same cases cannot be ruled out. These limitations are intrinsic to the case-series design but do not, in our view, undermine the heuristic value of the cases in illustrating the heterogeneity of clinical-forensic presentations in autistic offenders.

Conclusions

This case series highlights the marked heterogeneity of offender presentations in autistic individuals and does not support simplistic associations between autism and criminal behaviour. Rather, the cases suggest that offending-related conduct may emerge through different pathways, shaped by the interaction of autistic traits with intellectual and adaptive functioning, psychiatric comorbidity, emotional regulation, developmental adversity, and contextual stressors. From a forensic psychiatric perspective, the relevance of ASD lies not in the diagnosis alone, but in its specific role in the meaning, modulation, and contextual understanding of the behaviour under examination. The diagnostic task is therefore not merely to determine whether ASD is present, but to clarify how autistic characteristics contribute to the individual clinical-forensic picture. These findings support the need for a clinically integrated, developmentally informed, and individualized assessment of offenders with ASD. Standardized instruments may be helpful, but only when interpreted within a broader formulation that includes developmental history, adaptive profile, cognitive functioning, and co-occurring psychopathology. Greater recognition of underdiagnosed or atypical adult presentations may improve diagnostic accuracy and support more appropriate decisions regarding criminal responsibility, risk management, and treatment planning.
