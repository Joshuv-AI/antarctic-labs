 ABSTRACT

Westinghouse Electric Company is developing wireless sensors to monitor the centerline temperature and
internal pressure of commercial light-water reactor fuel rods during irradiation. Oak Ridge National
Laboratory and Westinghouse Electric Company developed the Wireless Instrumented RB Experiment
2021 (WIRE-21) to test wireless temperature and pressure sensor technologies in a removable beryllium
position in the High Flux Isotope Reactor. The experiment was irradiated for a total of 75 days, at
temperatures ranging from approximately 150°C to 400°C, resulting in a peak fast (energy > 0.1 MeV)
neutron fluence of about 3 × 1021 n/cm2. The temperature was intentionally cycled multiple times to
compare the response of the wireless temperature sensor to collocated thermocouples. Similarly, the
pressure sensor was actuated in multiple steps to compare the response of the wireless measurement to ex-
core pressure transducers (Petrie et al., 2023). After irradiation, the experiment was disassembled in the
Irradiated Fuels Examination Laboratory (IFEL) hot cell at Oak Ridge National Laboratory with the intent
to recover sensor and dosimetry components, document the as-irradiated condition of the hardware, and
investigate possible causes of the sensor behavior observed during irradiation.

The postirradiation examination successfully recovered and preserved key WIRE-21 components. After
the housing was removed using a milling machine, the internal experiment sections were examined. All
eight fiber-optic sensors were recovered, cut, and stored. The silicon carbide thermometry, temperature
sensor, pressure sensor, lower spacer, and selected pressure and temperature cable sections were also
removed and stored.

The metal bellows of the pressure sensor was found to be plastically deformed, indicating that it had been
properly pressurized during irradiation and generally behaved as expected. X-ray diffraction analysis of a
section of one of the irradiated inductor cores within the pressure sensor was performed and confirmed
the presence of phase-pure alpha ferrite (i.e., no unexpected phase transformations). Inductance testing
was performed on the irradiated pressure sensor cores using an unirradiated test coil, and DC resistance
measurements of the transceiver coils were also performed. The measurements with irradiated inductor
cores assembled inside unirradiated coils showed slightly reduced inductance compared to measurements
made with unirradiated cores, but the difference was not sufficient to explain the more significant
reductions in inductance that were observed in-pile. Therefore, it is suspected that degradation of the
inductor coils (specifically the wire wrapping) is responsible for the reduced inductance observed in-pile.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Westinghouse Electric Company (WEC) is developing wireless sensors to monitor the centerline
temperature and internal pressure of commercial light-water reactor fuel rods during irradiation. These
sensors are intended to transmit measurements through the fuel rod cladding without requiring sensor
leads to enter the fuel rod pressure boundary. Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) and WEC
developed the Wireless Instrumented RB Experiment 2021 (WIRE-21) to test wireless temperature and
pressure sensor technologies in a removable beryllium position in the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR).

Postirradiation examination (PIE) was performed to recover sensor and dosimetry components, document
the as-irradiated condition of the WIRE-21 hardware, and investigate possible causes of the sensor
behavior observed during irradiation. The PIE started in July 2025 and was performed in the Irradiated
Fuels Examination Laboratory (IFEL) hot cell at ORNL. This report describes the WIRE-21 PIE activities
and results. It presents the experiment shipment, disassembly, component recovery and storage, hot cell
observations, and testing and examination of pressure sensor components.

 2. EXPERIMENT CONSTRUCTION

WIRE-21 consisted of several sections, each housing a different portion of the experiment. There were
two holders: the upper holder and the lower holder. The upper holder housed the pressure sensor
experiment, and the lower holder housed the temperature sensor experiment. There were also two spacers,
each containing passive thermometry and flux wires. Additionally, sensor lead cables, SiO2 optical fibers,
thermocouples, and gas lines ran through the experiment terminating at different axial locations. This
report focuses on the wireless sensor portion of the experiment designed by WEC. Figure 1 shows the
basic construction of WIRE-21.

 Figure 1. WIRE-21 experiment basic construction. TM is temperature monitor.

The wireless pressure and temperature sensor sections in the WIRE-21 experiment were not prototypic of
what would be implemented in a commercial light-water reactor. Instead, as shown in Figure 2, the
individual components of each sensor (e.g., wire-wrapped inductors, metal bellows, and platinum wires
for temperature measurement) were packaged inside a ceramic enclosure to evaluate radiation effects on
the individual components at relevant neutron fluence levels. Within each sensor enclosure there are two
sets of coils. The transceiver section is energized, and the coils are inductively coupled together.
Electromagnetic energy is wirelessly transferred to the sensing and reference inductors. The amount of
electromagnetic energy that couples back to the receiving inductor depends on the temperature-dependent
resistance of the platinum wire that is wrapped around the fuel surrogate of the temperature sensor.
Similarly, the amount of energy that couples back to the receiving inductor of the pressure sensor depends

 1

on the position of the inductor core that is mechanically coupled to the metal bellows, which extends
when internally pressurized. The transceiver senses this change in inductive coupling, which can be
related (via calibration) to a change in temperature or pressure. For more information about the
functionality and construction of the WIRE-21 experiment, see Carvajal et al. (2019), Carvajal et al.
(2020), and Mulligan et al. (2020).

 Figure 2. Temperature and pressure sensor basic construction. MI is mineral insulated.

 3. IRRADIATION OVERVIEW

WIRE-21 was irradiated in HFIR in one of the removable beryllium positions. The irradiation ran for 75
effective full-power days across HFIR cycles 498a, 498b, 499, 500a, and 500b from April 5, 2022, to
December 16, 2022, with long outages between some cycle parts. The reported maximum experiment
fluences were approximately 5.8 × 1021 n/cm2 thermal and 3.4 × 1021 n/cm2 fast (Petrie et al., 2023).

During irradiation, temperatures were mostly in the approximately 300°C–400°C range, consistent with
typical light-water reactor operating conditions. Temperatures rose most noticeably during the first cycle,
attributed to changing nuclear heating and irradiation-induced changes in the graphite holders. Five
intentional temperature transients were produced by changing helium/argon gas flow, and pressure
transients were applied to the pressure sensor bellows up to about 600 psi in cycle 498 and 700 psi in
cycle 499.

The wireless temperature sensor remained functional and captured the actuated temperature transients
even after high fast fluence, though its inductors showed drift/reduced inductance likely tied to high flux
rate effects. The pressure sensor did not respond to applied bellows pressurization.

For more information about the irradiation, see Petrie et al. (2023).

 4. SHIPPING AND STORAGE

When WIRE-21 was removed from HFIR and shipped to IFEL, the outer housing was more radioactive
than expected. Overall, this caused problems with shipping, storage, and movement of WIRE-21. The
issue was determined to be an assumption about the cobalt content in the 316 stainless steel housing.

 2

Cobalt is generally considered as a residual impurity in 316 stainless steel, and its presence as a weight
percentage is not tightly controlled in the production of 316 stainless steel. The initial activation
calculations for WIRE-21 were made with the nominal amount of cobalt present in the housing. After the
housing was found to be activated more than expected, mass spectrometry was performed on a sample of
the material which showed a higher percentage of cobalt than anticipated. This discrepancy caused many
issues during the shipping, storage, and PIE process. For future experiments, it is important to perform
material testing before using a batch of 316 stainless steel to prevent these issues. There are specialized
companies that supply Cobalt-restricted grades of 316 stainless steel, and this could also be considered as
an option to greatly reduce operator exposure and transportation issues.

Once WIRE-21 arrived at IFEL, lead shielding blankets were draped over the loop cask while it remained
in the truck bay waiting to be loaded into the hot cell. After being stored in the truck bay, WIRE-21 was
successfully loaded into the hot cell and was stored until PIE could begin.

 5. IN-CELL DISASSEMBLY AND INITIAL POSTIRRADIATION EXAMINATION

5.1 DOSE ISSUES

After irradiation in HFIR, WIRE-21 was transferred to the hot cells at the IFEL at ORNL for PIE. The
purpose of the PIE was to remove the experiment from the housing as well as remove and store sections
of the experiment for further disassembly and examination. The sections were then planned to be sent to
the Irradiated Material Examination and Testing (IMET) facility at ORNL for further disassembly and
examination.

While performing the initial setup for the PIE, WIRE-21 was moved around the north hot cell. At one
point, the experiment was raised up past the top of the north window, and one of the G64 area gamma
monitors alarmed, indicating a gamma dose rate of >7 mrem/h outside the hot cell. At this point, it was
clear that the housing of WIRE-21 was extremely radioactive and was treated with care for the duration of
the PIE.

Additionally, when it was first stored in the northwest corner of the north hot cell, WIRE-21 created a
radiation area with a dose rate of >10 rem/h in the IFEL charging area and the entrance to the airlock.
This made hot cell operations difficult. Once the facilities team figured out that WIRE-21 was the source
of this radiation, the assembly was moved to a different corner which fixed the issue.

milling machine setup in the hot cell. During the machining process, the area monitor in the north hallway

After the housing was removed, the assembly was tipped to remove it from the mill, all 38 silicon carbide
temperature monitors (TMs) poured onto the table. The team was able to recover and store all the TMs in
random groups of 13, 12, and 13 in three pellet cans. The rest of the thermocouples were able to be

 6. FURTHER DISASSEMBLY AND X-RAY DIFFRACTION

6.1 PRESSURE SENSOR EXAMINATION

After the initial PIE, all components were slated to be sent to the IMET facility at ORNL for further
disassembly and testing. IMET is an alpha-free facility, so before shipping the components to IMET, the
alpha and beta contamination of the components were evaluated at IFEL. The alpha and beta
contamination were found to be too high (72,000 and 32 million disintegrations per minute, respectively)
for the components to be sent to IMET, so further examination and testing was completed at IFEL.

During irradiation of the experiment, the pressure sensor did not function as expected, despite having
been functional during pre-irradiation tests (Mulligan et al., 2020). For this reason, there was interest in
inspecting each element of the pressure sensor. First, the bellows of the wireless pressure sensor (Figure
16) was inspected.

 Figure 16. Pressure sensor bellows.

The bellows was observed to still be flexible. The bellows was also measured to determine if it had
plastically deformed during irradiation. The length of the bellows corrugations was measured to be
approximately 16 mm (Figure 17), indicating that it had expanded significantly compared to its
preirradiation length of 11 mm. Based on preirradiation testing performed by WEC, this amount of
expansion (~5 mm) was observed when pressurizing to 450–500 psi, which is significantly less than the
approximately 800 psi maximum pressure that was applied during irradiation. The reason for the lower
deformation is almost certainly related to well-known hardening of 316 stainless steel under this
temperature and dose regime. The bellows primarily deforms via plastic deformation, and the yield stress
of 316L has previously been shown to increase by nearly 80% following irradiation to about 2 dpa at
300°C. Therefore, the observed bellows deformation appears reasonable and there are no indications that
the bellows was not properly pressurized during irradiation.

 11

 Figure 17. Bellows measurement.

The inductor core attached to the bellows was inside the sensing and receiving coil in the pressure sensor
assembly. When it was initially removed from the pressure sensor, the core remained in the coil (Figure
18). After removal from the pressure sensor, the inductor core was easily removed from the wire-wrapped
inductor coil. It was not noticeably stuck in the coil or its alumina insulating tube, although it did not
simply fall out when the assembly was removed from the pressure sensor. The combination of a
reasonable bellows deformation and a lack of any noticeable obstruction of the inductor core (still
attached to the bellows) means that it is not entirely clear why the bellows displacement was not captured
during irradiation.

 Figure 18. Pressure sensor bellows attached to inductor core, still in the sensing/reference coil.

 7. INDUCTANCE/RESISTANCE TESTING

To further investigate the wireless pressure sensor, inductance testing was performed using an irradiated
inductor core of the pressure sensor and two other small sections of inductor core from the transceiver
section of the pressure sensor. To accomplish this testing, a fixture was fabricated to hold a BK Precision
880 LRC meter and provide easy electrical connections to the meter. The fixture was 3D printed and used
spring-loaded, speaker-terminal style connections.

A test coil (unirradiated) and unirradiated surrogate core were provided by WEC to measure possible
changes in the performance of the inductor cores as a result of irradiation. The test coil was connected to
the meter, and the inductance was measured with no core, the surrogate core (Figure 22), and the
irradiated cores (Figure 23).

 Figure 22. Inductance/direct current resistance measurement fixture with test coil.

 Figure 23. Test coil with irradiated pressure sensor core on the inductance testing fixture.

The direct current resistance of one of the coils on the transceiver was also measured on the fixture, as
shown in Figure 24, to determine if it had failed. The direct current resistance measurement for the
transceiver coil was inconsistent and changed when the coil was moved around while connected to the
fixture.

 Figure 24. Manipulators connecting the transceiver coil to the inductance test fixture.

The transceiver section of the pressure sensor contained three separate sections of ferritic inductor core.
The electrical testing fixture was also used to test the inductance of the test coil with these irradiated
sections inserted to further investigate the effect of irradiation on the cores. Figure 25 shows the locations
of these three sections in the transceiver portion in the blue outlines.

Initially, the core sections were stuck in the alumina insulators of the coils. The section that was on the
side of the pressure sensor closest to the top of the reactor came out first. The opposite side of the coil
former was sealed, and the team was unable to retrieve the other two cores out of the top of the coil
insulator. A cutter was used to cut off the sealed side of the coil insulator, and a small portion of the core
was also cut off. The middle core section remained stuck in the transceiver coil. Figure 26 shows the top
and bottom inductor core sections compared to the bellows, which had previously been measured.

 Figure 26. Known-length bellows shown with top and bottom inductor core sections extracted from the
 transceiver.

The inductor core segments were inserted into the test coil and inductance was measured using the
fixture. Figure 27 shows the shorter core section in the test coil on the electrical testing fixture in the hot
cell.

 16

 Figure 27. Short transceiver coil section on the electrical testing fixture.

The inductance measurements of the test coil showed an inductance of 1.397 mH with the unirradiated
core and 0.916 mH with the irradiated core. This 34.5% reduction is significant but pales in comparison to
the nearly 85% reduction in inductance observed in-pile for some of the inductors. Therefore, it is likely
that most of the radiation-induced reductions in inductance were related to degradation in the coils as
opposed to fundamental radiation effects on the inductor cores.

The direct current (DC) resistance of the transceiver coil was tested on the fixture to determine if
degradation had occurred, but it was apparent that there were internal shorts either due to damage from
the PIE or the irradiation. In some places on the coil for which a DC resistance was attempted, the enamel
insulation was rubbed off, and in some places, there was more noticeable physical damage to the wires.
When a measurement was attempted, the resistance changed drastically when the coil was moved,
suggesting multiple internal short circuits. Figure 28 shows the damage to the transceiver coil.

 8. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION

The WIRE-21 PIE was completed at IFEL after irradiation in HFIR. The work required more disassembly
effort than originally expected because the internal experiment assembly was stuck in the 316 stainless
steel housing and could not be pushed out after the ends of the housing were machined. Additional in-cell
machining was performed, including longitudinal cuts along the housing, to open the housing and remove
the experiment sections.

Shipping and handling of WIRE-21 were significantly affected by the amount of cobalt present in the
housing and internal components made of 316 stainless steel. The amount of cobalt was under-estimated,
so the cobalt-60 activity of the irradiated components was higher than was planned. In the future, it is
imperative to test the exact composition of 316 stainless steel components so the dose can be more
effectively managed.

The PIE recovered and preserved the requested WIRE-21 components. All eight fiber optic sensors were
removed, sectioned, identified, and stored. All silicon carbide TMs were recovered and stored in pellet
cans. The temperature sensor, pressure sensor components, lower spacer, and selected pressure and
temperature cable sections were also removed and stored. Thermocouples were removed and discarded as
planned where possible, and the remaining experiment sections were documented during disassembly.

After the initial disassembly, the recovered components were evaluated for transfer to IMET for
additional examination. Because the components had contamination levels that were too high for transfer,
the remaining examination and testing activities were completed at IFEL. This included additional
inspection of the pressure sensor components, removal and preparation of a pressure sensor core section
for XRD, design and fabrication of custom fixtures for handling and alignment, and in-cell inductance
and resistance measurements using equipment adapted for hot-cell operation.

Several important observations were made during PIE of WEC’s wireless sensor components:

 The metal bellows in the wireless pressure sensor expanded as expected based on the applied pressure
 and the well-known hardening of 316 stainless steel during irradiation.
 The inductor core remained attached to the metal bellows and did not show any obvious signs of
 interference inside the alumina insulator of the coil.
 The irradiated inductor core (α-Fe) did not show any signs of radiation-induced phase
 transformations.
 It is not immediately clear why the bellows deformation was not evident from the measurements
 performed in situ.
 When placed in an unirradiated coil, the irradiated inductor core did show slightly decreased
 inductance but not enough to explain the nearly 85% reductions in inductance that were observed in
 situ during irradiation of some of the inductors, suggesting that radiation-induced degradation of the
 coil is the most likely explanation.
 Visual inspection and DC resistance testing of the transceiver coil supports this claim, but it is
 unknown what damage occurred during PIE versus what occurred during irradiation.
