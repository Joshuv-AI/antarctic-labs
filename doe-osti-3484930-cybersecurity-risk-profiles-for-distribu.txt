Executive Summary
Distributed energy resource management systems (DERMS) are an increasingly important
technology designed to manage, monitor, and control the aggregated benefits and challenges
introduced by the expansion of distributed energy resources (DERs). DERMS are control
systems specifically designed for DERs, including solar photovoltaic (PV) systems, bulk and
distributed wind, behind-the-meter batteries, fleets of electric vehicles, and more. As the
penetration of DERs accelerates, DERMS offer a promising solution to effectively manage and
optimize the flexibility and other benefits offered by deploying DER assets. DERMS
technologies can be deployed as part of a decentralized microgrid or a virtual power plant (VPP);
implemented by a VPP owner/operator, a DER aggregator, or third-party service providers;
integrated into a utility-scale advanced distribution management system; or act as a stand-alone
utility-scale DERMS solution. DERMS have the potential to significantly impact electric system
operations. The cybersecurity risks and considerations of DERMS considerably vary, depending
on how these solutions are deployed and at what scale. As DERMS adoption accelerates, it is
important for the electric sector to better understand the cybersecurity implications of DERMS
active management, monitoring, and control of DERs based on the characteristics and the
security, safety, and reliability impacts to their system.

Securing DERMS integration, deployment, and operations is a significant challenge because
DERMS have a wide variety of capabilities, are likely highly interconnected to a diverse array of
electric systems, and can manage large numbers of DERs. Further, highly distributed and
potentially widely geographically dispersed DER installations increase the dependency on
telecommunication technologies for remote monitoring and control. As electric systems
incorporate more DERs, the number and diversity of stakeholders managing different operations
and capabilities, including cloud-based hosting and third-party services, will also increase. These
factors increase the challenges of effectively managing cybersecurity risks. These risks include
device misconfigurations, exposure to the public internet, software vulnerabilities, third-party
dependencies, and more. It is therefore important to understand and address cybersecurity risks
to DERMS based on the specific needs and requirements for DERMS deployment from utility-
scale to small-scale aggregated DERs. Although past research has highlighted the cybersecurity
risks of DERMS, none has specifically addressed the differences in cybersecurity risks based on
how DERMS are deployed and within different kinds of energy systems—from centralized,
utility-scale DERMS to decentralized DERMS aggregating medium- to smaller-scale DERs.

This report describes various cybersecurity attack scenarios on DERMS capabilities in different
operational technology systems with high penetrations of DERs. It also delineates the
cybersecurity risk profiles for centralized utility DERMS deployments and decentralized
aggregator DERMS. To address these risks, high-level mitigations based on each threat scenario
are provided. The threat scenarios and risk profiles in this report are intended to increase
awareness of and consideration toward the variable cybersecurity risks to DER industry
stakeholders when integrating DERMS based on the needs and characteristics of their electric
power systems and the scale of the DER penetration in which the DERMS are deployed. The
report concludes with an overview of various cybersecurity standards that could be applicable to
DERMS functions, mapped to mitigations identified in the threat scenarios.

1 Introduction
The electric power sector is undergoing a fundamental transformation toward greater
decentralization driven by the rapid integration of distributed energy resources (DERs). The term
“DER” is typically used for grid-edge devices, including generation resources (wind, solar),
storage (battery, hydrogen), and energy management systems (demand response, building load
controllers) that are connected to the distribution grid. But definitions of DERs vary across
organizations, reflecting their evolving role in the grid. For example, the Federal Energy
Regulatory Commission (FERC) defines DERs broadly as “any resource located on the
distribution system, any subsystem thereof, or behind a customer meter” (FERC 2020). The
North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) describes DERs as “resources that are
connected to the distribution system or are behind a customer meter,” including solar
photovoltaics (PV), wind, battery storage, and demand response (NERC 2022a). The U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) considers DERs to include grid-edge technologies, such as solar
PV systems, behind-the-meter (BTM) batteries, electric vehicle fleets, and other flexible loads
(DOE 2022c). The Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) 1547.3 defines DERs
as sources of electric power that are not directly connected to a bulk power transmission system,
while UL 2941 focuses on inverter-based resources (IBRs) and DERs that include PV inverters,
energy storage systems, and other inverter-connected devices.

To manage the opportunities and challenges posed by high penetrations of these resources,
distributed energy resource management systems (DERMS) have emerged as critical enabling
technologies. DERMS are control platforms designed to forecast, monitor, coordinate, and
optimize groups of DERs, whether they are deployed as centralized utility systems, deployed as
decentralized aggregator solutions, or integrated within microgrids and virtual power plants
(VPPs).

Although DERMS offer substantial benefits for grid reliability, flexibility, and market
participation, their increasing complexity, interconnectivity, and reliance on telecommunications
and third-party services also introduce variable and significant cybersecurity risks. These risks
considerably differ depending on the scale, architecture, and operational context of the DERMS
deployment. Understanding these risks and related considerations based on the goals and impacts
of different scales of DERMS deployment is a key first step toward improving the security of
DERMS integrations and supporting innovations that build cybersecurity into the design and
manufacture of these solutions.

This report addresses a key research gap by developing cybersecurity risk profiles for utility-
scale and aggregator DERMS deployments in systems with high penetrations of solar PV and
other DERs. It presents three progressively severe threat scenarios, outlines associated
vulnerabilities and their impacts, and provides high-level mitigations mapped to the National
Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) and relevant
standards, including UL 2941 and IEEE 1547.3.

The goal is to equip DERMS vendors, owners, operators, utilities, and regulators with a clearer
understanding of deployment-specific cybersecurity risks, enabling more informed risk
assessments and stronger protection of the evolving electric grid.

2 Distributed Energy Resource Management System
  Deployments
Over the last decade or so, the cost of solar PV systems has dramatically decreased, with an 85%
decline in the price of PV modules (Feldman et al. 2021). From utility-scale to residential
deployments, DERs, including solar PV, can be dispatched relatively quickly and cost-
effectively with a high degree of flexibility (Haegel et al. 2023). The integration of medium- and
large-scale DERs, VPPs, and other aggregated utility-scale and BTM resources has created new
challenges in managing cybersecurity risks for increasingly geographically dispersed and
complex distribution systems.

Although past research has highlighted the cybersecurity risks of DERMS, none has specifically
addressed the differences in cybersecurity risks based on how DERMS are deployed in different
kinds of energy systems. Because DERMS are a rapidly evolving technology, it is important to
think about DERMS in terms of their functions rather than as a physical entity (EPRI 2018).
Although the fundamental function of DERMS solutions—to organize and control DERs—is the
goal of any DERMS deployment, there is a broad spectrum of possible DERMS capabilities
shaped by the type and number of aggregated DER assets the DERMS will manage and the way
the DERMS are deployed within the grid architecture (Ealey 2021). Successful cybersecurity
attacks can have substantially different impacts on system operations depending on how the
DERMS is deployed.

2.1 Background on Distributed Energy Resource Management
 System Functions
Electric distribution and transmission networks are rapidly changing. The vast majority of new
electricity generation capacity comes from DERs, with 56% of that new capacity coming from
solar PV generation (Dreves 2023). Further, in 2020, FERC Order No. 2222 enabled DERs to
participate in wholesale markets through aggregations, significantly expanding the market
opportunities for DERMS solutions (FERC 2020). As DER penetrations and aggregations
increase, grid owners and operators have turned to DERMS solutions to manage the
interconnection and operational aspects of DERs connected to bulk electric systems. Many
DERMS are also highly versatile and can be hosted by third parties or in the cloud, can be
integrated into advanced distribution management systems (ADMS), or can serve as stand-alone
systems.

In a 2018 paper, the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) defined DERMS core capabilities
as follows:

 • Aggregation: to control, monitor, and manage the aggregated power of a multitude of
 grouped DERs that align with the grid’s configuration
 • Translation: to cohesively handle the many communication protocols and languages used
 by individual DERs
 • Simplification: to streamline device-level data and settings in a way that is manageable
 and useful at the power systems level
 • Optimization: to manage, control, and merge the various grid services a group of DERs
 offers in a way that reduces cost and increases value (EPRI 2018).

 2

The IEEE “2030.11-2021 Guide for DERMS Functional Specification” further describes three
general functionalities that DERMS should address to be effective: DER device information,
monitoring/status, and operation/control—with aggregation serving an important, cross-cutting
role in all three areas (IEEE 2021).

2.2 Methods of Distributed Energy Resource Management System
 Deployment
DERMS can be deployed and function in different ways depending on their location and
configuration within the energy system. This high degree of flexibility maximizes the benefits of
aggregated and optimized groups of DERs. Today, DERMS solutions are largely deployed in
one or more instances within an energy network as stand-alone edge systems to manage,
monitor, and control DERs (IEEE 2021); however, DERMS solutions can also work in concert
with, or be fully integrated into, ADMS or distribution management systems on the distribution
side or energy management systems on the transmission side (Gray 2020).

DERMS can also be deployed as physical hardware or integrated into the cloud so that they are
not constrained to a physical location or a specific logical layer (EPRI 2018). The range of use
cases, system capabilities, market participation, and regulatory considerations all affect how
DERMS are integrated and what functions are included (IEEE 2021). As DERMS deployment
grows and their capabilities expand or change, the definition of DERMS might change too.

There have been many examples of how DERMS can increase grid resilience and optimize the
benefits of and limit the challenges from the aggregation of DERs (Ding et al. 2022b; Strezoski
and Stefani 2021; Rahman, Salek, and Paaso 2021). Since FERC Order 2222 allowed DERs to
participate in wholesale energy markets through aggregators, DERMS projects have further
expanded (Da Rocha Albertini et al. 2022). A few examples of DERMS deployments at various
scales and primary use cases follow:

 • Southern California Edison’s Grid Management System is illustrative of how a utility-
 scale, centralized DERMS is being integrated into an ADMS and coordinated with other
 management systems—including distribution management systems, supervisory control
 and data acquisition (SCADA) platforms, or outage management systems—to manage,
 monitor, and optimize the large number of DERs in their electric grid (SCE 2024).
 • North Carolina’s Electric Cooperative, which comprises 26-member not-for-profit
 electric cooperatives across North Carolina, piloted a DERMS software tool to centralize
 the monitoring, aggregation, and dispatch of DERs and demand response programs
 across multiple distribution entities, including many rural communities. This included
 community solar, solar and storage, utility-scale solar, microgrids, and demand response
 programs using customer BTM devices (NCEC and PJM 2020).
 • San Diego Gas & Electric Company has been developing a microgrid to provide energy
 resilience for the residents of the small desert community of Borrego Springs. They are
 using DERMS to support the management and control of the DERs in Borrego Springs as
 well as others within its service area. The DERMS platform will work in coordination
 with the microgrid controller to optimize the economic, environmental, and reliability
 objectives of the microgrid in either islanded or grid-connected mode (Katmale et al.
 2019).

 3

A more comprehensive list of DERMS projects and their capabilities can be found in Da Rocha
Albertini et al. (2022).

Although DERMS hold significant promise as DER aggregations become increasingly important
components of modern electric systems, the complex, interdependent cyber-physical systems that
DERMS manages, monitors, and controls might create new cybersecurity challenges and risks
that need to be properly understood and addressed.

2.3 Deployment Objectives and Security Concerns for Distributed
 Energy Resource Management Systems
DERMS systems span a broad spectrum of deployments—from centralized to decentralized—
with a variety of responsibilities and capabilities at each scale (Strezoski 2022). The number and
scale of DER resources managed by DERMS can also substantially vary. Within this context,
centralized solutions have a high level of awareness over the entire energy system and all assets
connected to its management platform. This awareness allows centralized DERMS to accurately
assess grid conditions in real or near real time. On the other end of the spectrum, decentralized
DERMS are designed primarily to aggregate lower-voltage DERs, often BTM DERs, to
participate in energy markets and to provide demand response, load management, and other
ancillary services (Strezoski et al. 2022). Decentralized DERMS do not have access to the
upstream grid-level network model and technical constraints, such as voltage limits, relay
settings, or reverse power flow constraints (Ding et al. 2022a). This lack of full grid awareness
and a focus on customer-related services led Strezoski (2022) to argue that these decentralized
DERMS should not be called DERMS at all but rather should be referred to as DER aggregators;
however, IEEE 2030.11-2021 identifies DERMS as being any system that will allow an
organization to interact with and model DERs and demand response (IEEE 2021). This includes
third-party aggregator DERMS, which includes DERMS operators and/or service providers
(which could be the same or separate entities); and DERMS functions that aggregate DERs and
demand response either integrated into or working in coordination with a microgrid controller
(IEEE 2021). Regardless of the specific definitions or boundaries of a DERMS, this report uses
the term “aggregator DERMS” to discuss the functions and goals of decentralized DERMS
deployments that aggregate medium- and small-scale mostly BTM DERs without full awareness
of grid constraints (Strezoski et al. 2022).

DERMS cybersecurity incidents can impact operations, service delivery, business interests, and
consumer interests in highly variable ways (NESCOR 2015). Different deployment scales have
unique needs based on the goals and objectives for deploying DERMS; therefore, securing these
solutions can pose unique challenges and potential impacts to both centralized utility DERMS
and decentralized aggregator DERMS.

2.3.1 Utility Distributed Energy Resource Management Systems

2.3.1.1 Overview
Utility DERMS solutions are centralized, enterprise-level solutions within utility control centers
that have situational awareness of grid conditions in real time and an accurate representation of
all assets and grid constraints (Petrovic et al. 2019). Utility DERMS are designed to help
distribution system operators (DSOs) overcome challenges related to the complexity and

 4

variability that DERs introduce to distribution grids, including more precise control and dispatch
of DERs, voltage management, continuous monitoring and analysis of DER data, and optimizing
the integration of multiple DER asset classes, among others (Ealey 2021). This includes the
active management, monitoring, and optimization of technical, operational, and market benefits
from medium- and large-scale DERs as well as aggregated small-scale DERs (Strezoski and
Stefani 2021).

2.3.1.2 Security Concerns With Utility Distributed Energy Resource Management
 Systems
Although compromising a single DER typically poses minimal security risk, coordinated
cyberattacks on large aggregations of utility-scale, medium-scale, and BTM DERs can cause
significant grid disturbances, service outages, and other severe impacts (NERC 2022a). Utility
DERMS process vast data inputs across complex networks. Consequently, they require high
computational efficiency to execute advanced algorithms for system planning, real-time state
estimation, and predictive DER optimization (Strezoski 2022). Maintaining near-real-time
visibility into the DER environment is a critical security priority for utility operators. To safely
manage and optimize these resources within technical limits, utility DERMS depend entirely on
the integrity and accuracy of endpoint telemetry. The denial, degradation, or manipulation of
these data could lead to operational inefficiencies, constraint violations, power quality
degradation, and physical damage to grid assets, ultimately threatening public safety (Joo,
Stewart, and Helms 2020).

According to NERC, the rapid expansion and integration of IBRs—including wind, solar PV,
battery energy storage systems, and hybrid plants—is “the most significant driver of grid
transformation and poses a high risk to [Bulk Power System] reliability” (NERC 2023a). The
NERC Critical Infrastructure Protection (CIP) standards comprise a comprehensive framework
of regulatory cybersecurity and reliability requirements applicable to bulk electric system cyber
systems and assets in North America. Historically, NERC CIP requirements did not apply to
assets connected to the distribution side of the electric system, despite the increasingly critical
role DERs play in evolving energy networks (NERC 2022a). To mitigate the widespread risks
these new resources pose to the bulk power system, NERC developed and finalized the IBR
Registration Initiative. This regulatory framework establishes explicit criteria to identify and
register formerly “unregistered IBRs” that collectively exert a material impact on reliable grid
operations (NERC 2023a). With the registration rules now completed and active implementation
underway, qualifying IBR projects are being systematically brought under mandatory reliability
and cybersecurity oversight, significantly narrowing the regulatory gap for utility-scale
aggregated DERs.

Further, technological advancements, energy policies, and evolving market dynamics have
fundamentally altered how grid reliability, resilience, and security are managed (DOE 2022).
This paradigm shift requires extensive coordination with aggregators, retail energy providers,
prosumers, and third-party vendors to safely integrate DERs; however, these third-party entities
often maintain highly variable security postures and frequently operate outside of mandatory
cybersecurity frameworks. This regulatory gap significantly complicates cybersecurity risk
management across interdependent organizations with divergent operational missions and
business functions.

2.3.2 Aggregator Distributed Energy Resource Management Systems

2.3.2.1 Overview
Aggregator DERMS are decentralized solutions that are used to collect large numbers of BTM
resources and meet customer-related management needs, such as demand response and market
participation (Matsuda-Dunn et al. 2023). DER aggregators are third-party market entities that
serve as facilitators between aggregated small-scale DERs and utilities. These novel entities are
poised to play a large role in energy distribution as DER penetrations accelerate (NERC 2022a).
The main goal of aggregator DERMS solutions is to facilitate the optimal aggregation of DERs
and participate in new market opportunities created by the deployment of DERs. Following
FERC Order 2222, which allows aggregated DERs to participate in wholesale markets alongside
traditional generation resources, DER aggregations below 100 kilowatts can now compete in
these markets and provide additional ancillary services, such as demand response, energy
efficiency, and bidirectional power flows of electric vehicles (FERC 2020). Aggregator DERMS
can also be designed and configured to be interoperable and work in close coordination with
utility DERMS (Matsuda-Dunn et al. 2023).

Aggregator DERMS can be deployed at various scales. On the larger end of the spectrum,
aggregator DERMS can aggregate thousands of BTM resources and potentially some front-of-
the-meter resources over a wide geographic area, working in close coordination with the utility
DERMS operators, DSOs, regional transmission organizations, transmission system operators,
and other operators to provide information on DER capabilities or operations to provide
reliability and/or efficiencies as requested by the utility. At a smaller scale, aggregator DERMS
can interface with or be integrated into facility DERMS to control smaller numbers of localized
BTM DER systems for facilities such as college campuses, shopping malls, medical facilities,
and more (Xanthus n.d.). Another small, decentralized deployment could aggregate the DER
capabilities of a microgrid. Microgrids can be either islanded resources, supporting local capacity
needs and not interconnected to any utility infrastructure, or grid-connected, supporting the
utility DSO’s grid capacity and resilience needs (Jamil et al. 2021). These smaller-scale,
decentralized solutions might not directly interface with DSOs but provide important but more
basic management of aggregated BTM resources and facilitate the market participation of
prosumers (Strezoski et al. 2022).

2.3.2.2 Security Concerns With Aggregator Distributed Energy Resource Management
 Systems
Aggregated DERs and the DERMS that orchestrate them occupy a critical and unique position in
modern grid security. These systems must simultaneously secure thousands of individual
endpoint connections while maintaining highly coordinated, high-integrity links with grid
operators (Carter et al. 2017). Despite this pivotal role, DER aggregators have historically
operated outside the scope of standardized cybersecurity mandates, such as the NERC CIP
standards (NERC 2023a). Although the NERC IBR Registration Initiative recently moved to
bring utility-scale IBR owners and operators under mandatory oversight—with compliance
obligations for Category 2 entities taking effect in May 2026—many third-party aggregators that
primarily focus on market participation and have a total nameplate capacity less than 20 MVA
and 60 kV will not remain in a transitional regulatory state (NERC 2023a).

The operational profile of aggregator DERMS introduces several distinct threat vectors:

 6

 • Communication vulnerabilities: Data exchange between DERs and aggregators
 frequently traverses third-party telecommunication infrastructure or the public internet.
 Without robust encryption and multifactor authentication, these pathways are susceptible
 to interception, man-in-the-middle attacks, and unauthorized remote access (NERC
 2023b).
 • Remote management risks: To maintain systems across broad geographic areas,
 aggregators heavily rely on remote patching and maintenance (SEPA 2019). If not
 governed by strict access controls, these administrative functions can become primary
 entry points for large-scale systemic attacks (Johnson 2019).
 • Resource and training disparities: Many aggregators operate as lean market entities
 and might lack the specialized cybersecurity personnel or the sophisticated, defense-in-
 depth controls that are common at traditional utility-scale operations.
 • Complex interdependencies: DERMS operators are deeply integrated with a diverse
 ecosystem of third parties, including cloud service providers, regional transmission
 organizations, and DSOs. These overlapping layers of digital and operational dependency
 complicate the clear assignment of responsibilities for risk management, incident
 response, and system recovery.
As the regulatory landscape continues to evolve, significant cybersecurity incidents involving
DERMS could stall the adoption of these technologies and negatively influence state and federal
policies regarding the role of aggregators. To enhance the resilience of the sector and to improve
cybersecurity risk management, it is essential that DERMS vendors, owners, and operators
conduct comprehensive risk assessments tailored to their specific deployment models. The
proactive identification of threats and vulnerabilities will allow stakeholders to make informed,
security-conscious decisions during the design and operational phases of these critical grid
solutions.

3 Literature Review of Distributed Energy Resource
  Management Systems Cybersecurity
3.1 Overview of Past Work in the Cybersecurity of Distributed Energy
 Resource Management Systems
A review of relevant literature found several past works that addressed potential cybersecurity
threats, mitigations, and requirements applicable to DERMS.

The National Electric Sector Cybersecurity Organization Resource (NESCOR) developed a
report on Electric Sector Failure Scenarios and Impact Analyses, an extensive list of electric
sector threat scenarios, including threats to DERs and DERMS, that highlights relevant
vulnerabilities, impacts, and potential mitigations (NESCOR 2015).

Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory’s (LLNL’s) Cybersecurity for Energy Delivery
Systems program created 10 cybersecurity threat scenarios and co-simulations to emulate the
disruption or manipulation of DERMS controls or management (Joo et al. 2019). LLNL’s
Cybersecure Interconnection of DERs project then developed a communications scanning and
co-simulation tool to evaluate the impacts of the threat scenarios, deploy mitigation measures,
and analyze system-level cybersecurity risks and DER behavior (Joo, Stewart, and Helms 2020).

The DOE Office of Cybersecurity, Energy Security, and Emergency Response’s report on
Cybersecurity Considerations for Distributed Energy Resources on the U.S. Electric Grid
described the risks of DER aggregations and offered detailed DER threat scenarios, including
several potential attack vectors on DERMS communications and control and risks to utilities and
aggregators (DOE 2022c).

At the PV Systems Symposium in 2019, researchers from Sandia National Laboratories
presented an overview of DERMS capabilities and cybersecurity vulnerabilities in DERMS and
described an experimental attack in Sandia’s testing laboratory. They recommended
cybersecurity practices to protect DERMS (Johnson 2019).

In “Blockchain-Integrated Resilient Distributed Energy Resources Management System,”
Ahmad et al. described cyberattack case studies that target DERMS and used a cyber-physical
co-simulation test bed to validate a blockchain governance system (Ahmad et al. 2022).

The Smart Electric Power Alliance’s Grid Management Working Group and DERMS Task Force
developed a set of common requirements, including cybersecurity requirements, that serve as a
resource and starting point for utilities that are interested in procuring and securing DERMS
(SEPA 2019). These cybersecurity requirements provide a good baseline guide for protecting
DERMS that is agreed upon by a task force of utilities, developers, and solutions providers.

In 2021, the IEEE Standards Association published a guide that offers functional specifications
and requirements to “assure a technically sound operation of DERMS in its interactions with
distribution and transmission system operators and their management systems” (IEEE 2021). The
guide does not directly address cybersecurity. Instead, it describes common DER communication
protocols and their security profiles and points readers toward IEEE C37.240-2014 -

 8

Cybersecurity Requirements for Substation Automation, Protection, and Control System and its
sources for appropriate cybersecurity for DERMS.

The recommendations found in past research on threat scenarios, co-simulations, mitigations,
and recommendations are useful for understanding threat vectors to DERMS and DER
aggregation; however, none of these works include specific information on the variable risks and
other cybersecurity considerations related to the operational environments and scale at which
DERMS are deployed. This report addresses an important research gap that recognizes and
identifies how cybersecurity risks and concerns can differ based on the scale and architecture of
the systems in which DERMS are deployed.

3.2 Related Risk Assessment Models and Frameworks
A variety of risk assessment frameworks help organizations identify and prioritize cybersecurity
risks as a foundational part of the risk management process. There are many guides and
frameworks for generalized enterprise risk management, including NIST’s Special Publication
800-30 (NIST 2012); NIST Interagency Report (NISTIR) 8286A, Identifying and Estimating
Cybersecurity Risk for Enterprise Risk Management (Quinn et al. 2021); International
Organization for Standardization (ISO)/International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) 27001
(ISO 2022); and Operationally Critical Threat, Asset, and Vulnerability Evaluation (OCTAVE)
(Alberts et al. 1999), among others.

There are also several guidelines specific to the electric sector and/or the smart grid. NIST
Technical Note 2051, Cybersecurity Framework Smart Grid Profile, maps five high-level
business functions to the NIST Cybersecurity Framework’s functions (Marron et al. 2019). The
NIST profiles provide cybersecurity risk management guidance to power system
owners/operators to help identify and prioritize opportunities for improving cybersecurity at an
organizational level (Marron et al. 2019).

The DOE Cybersecurity Capability Maturity Model is a self-evaluation tool that helps
organizations measure and improve their cybersecurity practices. The risk management domain
of the Cybersecurity Capability Maturity Model includes processes and practices for managing
and addressing cybersecurity risks, including threat and vulnerability management, third-party
risk management, and cybersecurity architecture risks (DOE 2022). The National Laboratory of
the Rockies (NLR) created the Distributed Energy Resource Cybersecurity Framework, a risk
assessment tool building on the Cybersecurity Capability Maturity Model and NIST’s
cybersecurity framework that is designed to help organizations with DERs understand and
improve their cybersecurity posture (NLR n.d.).

The Smart Grid Information Security toolbox is a methodology tailor-made for assessing risks to
smart grids, including physical consequences to energy delivery or power equipment damage
(Mennella 2013; Langer and Smith 2015).

The DOE Office of Cybersecurity, Energy Security, and Emergency Response published the
report on Risk Assessment Essentials for State Energy Security Plans as guidance for energy
sector stakeholders to “identify, characterize, and assess key risks to energy infrastructure
and cross-sector interdependencies” (DOE 2024).

 9

These methodologies are useful in assessing cybersecurity risks for energy systems and for
DERs; however, none of these risk models or frameworks specifically address the cybersecurity
risks of DERMS deployments at the utility or aggregator scale. The DERMS risk profiles
described in Section 4 fill this important gap in risk assessments and specifically focus on the
variable risks of DERMS deployments. These risk profiles leverage common elements of these
frameworks to develop cybersecurity risk scenarios and potential mitigations.

4 Risk Profiles for Distributed Energy Resource

This section describes cybersecurity risk profiles for DERMS vendors, owners, and operators
when developing, deploying, and operating DERMS solutions within various deployment scales.
According to the U.S. Department of Homeland Security’s DHS Risk Lexicon, a risk profile is a
“description and/or depiction of risks to an entity, asset, system, network, or geographic area”
and can be used to “show how risks vary across comparable entities” (DHS 2010). These risk
profiles do not constitute a full risk assessment but rather serve as a useful starting point to help
DERMS owners, operators, and vendors identify, assess, and prioritize risks and mitigation
strategies. It is incumbent upon entities that are responsible for managing organizational
cybersecurity risks to conduct assessments using organization-specific risk decisions based on
their threat probability, vulnerability, and consequence metrics. Metrics are typically defined
using qualitative scales that can be transformed into quantitative values. These risk assessment
activities would then be used to develop a risk score or risk determination that will inform risk
management decisions (DOE 2024).

This section describes risk profiles for both utility-scale and decentralized, aggregator DERMS
deployments, with special consideration given to systems with high penetrations of solar PV
resources.

4.1 Key Concepts for Risk Profiles of Distributed Energy Resource

This report explores cybersecurity risks to DERMS deployments through three elements—
threats, vulnerabilities, and impacts—and describes the corresponding threat actor profiles.
These key concepts are adapted to address cybersecurity risks to DERMS from resources
including the DHS Risk Lexicon (DHS 2010); NIST Special Publication 800-30 Revision 1:
Guide for Conducting Risk Assessments (NIST 2012); the DOE Office of Cybersecurity, Energy
Security, and Emergency Response’s Risk Assessment Essentials for State Energy Security Plans
(DOE 2024); and the MITRE Corporation’s “How Do You Assess Your Organization’s Cyber
Threat Level?” (Bodeau, Fabius-Greene, and Graubart 2010).

Risk: In this report, cybersecurity risk involves the disruption, loss, or degradation of one or
several DERMS functions (aggregation, simplification, translation, optimization) that impacts
the safety, reliability, resilience, or optimization of power systems with high penetrations of
DERs and solar PV.

Threat: A threat is a cyber circumstance or event that can adversely impact DERMS operations
and the associated energy assets or systems. This report excludes threats related to natural
hazards, human error or accidents, or physical attacks—such as vandalism, ballistics, explosives,
and so on—that could impact DERMS. Adversarial threat actors could include individuals,
groups, organizations, or nation-states that are motivated financially, politically, criminally, or
otherwise seeking to inflict a range of consequences on the electric grid.

Vulnerability: A vulnerability is a weakness or flaw in or a dependency on information
technology, operational technology, communication systems, procedures, or security controls

 11

contained within or related to DERMS that could be exploited by a threat actor. Vulnerabilities
can also include “predisposing conditions” or aspects of the mission, business, information
systems, architecture, or organization that could make a threat event more likely or less likely to
occur (Stouffer et al. 2023).

Impact: A consequence or impact relates to the effects of the loss, degradation, or disruption in
DERMS functions as a result of a cyber event or circumstance. Consequences can range from
loss, disruption, or degradation of operations and/or service delivery, business interests, and
consumer and/or society interests. The impacts described in Section 4.2 are not exhaustive but
are meant to illustrate some potential consequences based on the type of attack.

Threat actor profile: A threat actor profile describes the characteristics of a likely adversarial
actor attempting to leverage a vulnerability to launch a threat event that impacts DERMS and
connected energy systems and components. Threat actor profiles offer an initial step toward
evaluating the likelihood of occurrence of an attack against DERMS deployments from a
probable threat actor. The threat profiles in Section 4.2 describe a likely adversary’s
characteristics. These are adapted from NIST Special Publication 800-30 (NIST 2012), and they
include the following:

 • Threat actor type could include an adversarial insider, an ad hoc or established hacker
 group or individual, an adversarial organization such as a competitor or supplier, or a
 nation-state actor.
 • Threat actor capabilities describe how well resourced the threat actor might be as well
 as the adversary’s level of knowledge, skills, and abilities, spanning from their capability
 to use, discover, or create vulnerabilities to generate multiple, coordinated, and/or high-
 impact cyberattacks. For example, low-capability threat actors likely carry out attacks
 with limited impact, based on minimal expertise and by leveraging public information,
 exploiting known vulnerabilities or weaknesses, and using predictable attack tactics and
 techniques created by more skilled attackers.
 • Threat actor intent is the motivation and the level of desire to create negative effects on
 targets. This includes how motivated the threat actor is to maintain persistent access to its
 targets and how hard the adversary works to avoid detection and hide their tradecraft.
 • Threat actor targeting describes the threat actor’s access to and interest in creating
 detrimental effects on high-value organizational assets, functions, or mission-critical
 operations.
Most full risk assessments estimate the likelihood of occurrence expressed as a probability and
consider not only the characteristics of a threat actor but also other conditions of an
organization’s mission or function that make a successful attack more or less likely. This could
include an organization’s subjective view of the threat level, the timescale of the threat, historical
data, and other factors (Bodeau, Fabius-Greene, and Graubart 2010; NIST 2012). Other
organization-specific conditions or factors that would affect the tailored process an organization
would undertake to estimate the likelihood of attack occurrence are outside the scope of the
threat scenarios in Section 4.2.

4.2 Cybersecurity Threat Scenarios for Distributed Energy Resource

This section describes example threat scenarios to help DERMS vendors, owners, and operators
understand how security incidents could impact DERMS deployments in DER-rich distribution
networks. In each scenario, the types of threats and vulnerabilities remain the same, whereas the
impacts and threat actor profiles are tailored to each level of deployment. By keeping the types
of threats and vulnerabilities consistent, we demonstrate how the same kinds of attacks can have
different outcomes and consequences depending on how a DERMS is deployed. Each scenario
outlines some examples of cybersecurity risks to DERMS. The scenarios in this section focus on
a subset of potential consequences for each type of attack; however this list is not exhaustive—
there could be many possible consequences in addition to those that are described here.

These threat scenarios are ordered from the lowest to the highest impact—from financial loss and
temporary disruptions (lowest) to the degradation of power quality, power outages, and harm to
equipment or personnel (highest). Scenario 1 represents the low end of the impact range, and
Scenario 4 represents the high end. The scenarios were adapted from NESCOR’s Electric Sector
Failure Scenarios and Impact Analyses (NESCOR 2015), LLNL’s “Cybersecurity Scenarios for
Cybersecure Interconnection of DERs” (Joo et al. 2019), and related co-simulations (Duan et al.
2020). They were further informed by threat scenarios from the DOE Office of Cybersecurity,
Energy Security, and Emergency Response’s report on Cybersecurity Considerations for
Distributed Energy Resources on the U.S. Electric Grid (DOE 2022c) and the National
Association of State Energy Officials’ Hypothetical Solar Cyberattacks Scenarios and Impacts
(Verclas et al. 2023).

Following each threat scenario, we outline potential mitigations that are specific to the identified
vulnerabilities and weaknesses. These mitigations are adapted from recommendations by
NESCOR (2015) and selected from the NIST CSF 2.0 (NIST 2024). The NIST CSF is a
voluntary framework for owners and operators of critical infrastructure to manage cyber risks to
critical infrastructure with five high-level functions: identify, protect, detect, respond, and
recover. The mitigations listed in this section are not exhaustive but are meant to cover important
security topics specific to each threat scenario. In Section 5, these mitigations are mapped to
cybersecurity standards and best practices that DERMS owners, operators, and vendors could
consider in the development, design, and deployment of DERMS.

4.2.1 Scenario 1: Denial-of-Service Attack
Description: A misconfigured firewall allows an attacker to launch a denial-of-service attack,
flooding the network with a high volume of traffic to communication channels between DERMS
and DER groups. The network becomes so overburdened that the DERMS is unable to send or
receive monitoring and control messages to or from DERs.

4.2.1.1 Utility Distributed Energy Resource Management System Risk: Scenario 1
Impact: A distributed denial-of-service attack from a large, distributed botnet causes the
DERMS operators to lose real-time visibility, situational awareness, and communications to
high-impact DER groups during peak load, rendering the DERMS unable to send legitimate
commands and obtain state statuses for monitoring and optimization for several minutes. The
traffic volume impedes the ability of utility DERMS to dispatch resources from alternative DER
groups within a localized geographic area.

The attack could also cause incorrect metering, resulting in customers overpaying for electricity,
and financial losses resulting from increased customer service costs, late invoicing, and
personnel time. Depending on the duration of the incident, the loss of visibility could result in the
inability to manage voltage fluctuations and cause inverters to trip. Utility-scale deployments are
more likely to recover faster than smaller installations because they have additional distributed
denial-of-service protections, resources, and personnel to respond.

 14

Threat profile: A small, loosely affiliated political hacktivist group uses publicly available
information on firewall rule configuration to launch an attack causing temporary disruptions but
during a critical time for utility operations. Distributed denial-of-service disruptions do not
require in-depth understanding of the network architecture or specialized knowledge of or access
to targeted cybersecurity vulnerabilities, so the effects are limited in scope to several minutes.
The threat actors have limited ability to conceal or an interest in hiding their traces or their
tradecraft; however, the timing of the disruption to correspond with the peak load demonstrates a
moderate level of intent to disrupt service delivery and cause the loss of communications, which
impacts greater numbers of customer services than more opportunistic hackers.

4.2.1.2 Aggregator Distributed Energy Resource Management System Risk: Scenario 1
Impact: The aggregator DERMS loses real-time visibility and communications to a large group
of DERs, disrupting coordination between the aggregator and the DSO. This causes the loss of
the DERMS optimization benefits, including the loss of timely weather and market information
and an inability to model and predict DER behavior for active management, including key
protection areas, such as voltage support, frequency response, constraint management, resilience
support, and more. This loss of situational awareness, particularly if the attack is targeted to a
specific DER group, can affect power quality because the aggregator DERMS is unable to
communicate with the utility DERMS or other distribution management systems or to send
commands to dispatch alternate DERs. Further, the aggregator DERMS has limited visibility into
the utility’s alternative dispatchable resources. Poorly implemented incident response,
communication plans, and contracting language regarding roles and responsibilities during such
an incident can prolong the disruption and impede recovery, causing several days of disruption to
some groups of DERs. At the extreme, the loss of DER control could prevent the DERMS from
managing voltage fluctuations or reverse power flow, causing damage to customer DERs.
Recovery could include costs of equipment repairs, customer service responses, personnel time
to rectify incorrect meter information and overcharges to customers, and the cost of hardening
systems. Further, damage to the vendor’s reputation with customers could lead aggregators to
drop DERMS vendors, and also decreasing confidence in the role of aggregators with utility
operators and customers.

Threat profile: A group of “script kiddies” that are motivated to opportunistically disrupt
critical infrastructure operations uses public information to exploit known weaknesses in firewall
configurations to disrupt DERMS operations for a temporary period. The threat actors likely
have limited resources to sustain the attack for more than a few minutes and will have little to no
specific expertise of the network or system architecture. The distributed denial-of-service attack
is launched using predictable attack tactics and techniques created by more skilled attackers;
however, vulnerabilities in aggregator DERMS communications and incident response planning
could still impact timely recovery and damage the reputation of the DERMS vendor among
aggregator service providers, customers, and utility partners.

4.2.1.3 Potential Mitigations: Scenario 1
Following are potential NIST CSF mitigations that relate to the risks described in Scenario 1
(NIST 2024):

 • PR.PS-01: Configuration management practices are established and applied.

 15

 • PR.AA (all as applicable): Access to physical and logical assets is limited to authorized
 users, services, and hardware and managed commensurate with the assessed risk of
 unauthorized access.
 • PR.DS-02: The confidentiality, integrity, and availability of data-in-transit are protected.
 • PR.IR-01: Networks and environments are protected from unauthorized logical access
 and usage.
 • PR.IR-03: Mechanisms are implemented to achieve resilience requirements in normal
 and adverse situations.
 • PR.IR-04: Adequate resource capacity to ensure availability is maintained.
 • DE.CM-06: External service provider activities and services are monitored to find
 potentially adverse events.
 • GV.SC-08: Relevant suppliers and other third parties are included in incident planning,
 response, and recovery activities.

4.2.2 Scenario 2: Weather Forecast Data Integrity Compromise
Description: A threat actor takes advantage of unprotected DERMS communication channels to
launch an adversary-in-the-middle attack, intercepting and manipulating weather data from a
third-party service that the DERMS uses to forecast loads and control dynamic DER generation
and dispatch. The attacker changes weather forecast information to overestimate solar PV output,
causing the DERMS to send suboptimal dispatch of DER generation and underdeliver services to
customers.

4.2.2.1 Utility Distributed Energy Resource Management System Risk: Scenario 2
Impact: Manipulated weather data cause the utility to make misinformed decisions during peak
loads to decline to purchase or dispatch additional load to customers who might need those
resources to meet the peak demand. Forecasts that underestimate the need for the dispatch of
DER generation could lead to power quality issues in localized areas. Utilities could incur
financial losses as a result of incorrect metering, inefficient resource planning and dispatch,
increased customer service responses, billing corrections, and additional personnel time to
respond to and recover from the incident. Operators that cannot distinguish between real and
manipulated data could decrease confidence in DERMS real-time and look-ahead functions and
negatively impact the relationship between utility DSOs and DERMS vendors and operators.

Threat profile: A professional, organized crime group that is moderately resourced and capable
uses its general knowledge of the utility DERMS communications and a known vulnerability in
the third-party weather forecast data service provider to target utility DER assets during peak
load seeking to cause negative impacts to service delivery at a critical operational moment. Their
targeting of weather data could indicate that the threat actor either is more limited in capability or
has constrained intent to cause more serious physical damage or widespread disruptions. After a
successful attack, the threat actor could take steps to avoid detection and discovery of their
tradecraft so that they can reuse their tactics, techniques, and procedures on other targets.

4.2.2.2 Aggregator Distributed Energy Resource Management System Risk: Scenario 2
Impact: Without reliable weather forecasting and with overestimations of solar PV output,
aggregators could inaccurately estimate market pricing for DER generation for potentially
thousands of rooftop solar resources. Further, inaccurate forecasts could lead to the suboptimal

 16

dispatch of solar PV resources and power quality issues to DER groups. DER aggregators could
incur financial losses because of incorrect customer metering and invoicing, leading to
significant customer service requests and personnel time dedicated to correcting customer
invoices. Additionally, as a critical link between end customers and utility operators, an incident
involving manipulated data could cause the utility to mistrust the information coming from the
aggregator DERMS, leading to significant reputational damage and causing the utility to
renegotiate contracts with DERMS operators and/or vendors. The attack could also decrease
customer confidence in new energy markets and lessen interest from new prosumers in DER
market participation.

Threat profile: A commercially motivated market competitor/adversary that is moderately
skilled and resourced uses known vulnerabilities in DERMS communications and industry
knowledge of weaknesses in data integrity and the verification of weather data to negatively
affect the DERMS forecast and dispatch optimization functions. A successful attack requires
knowledge of how DERMS solutions use weather forecast data to dispatch DERs, and attacking
during peak load demonstrates that the threat actor has some familiarity with their target. The
adversary’s intent could be to cause disruptions and financial losses to the DERMS service
providers to decrease confidence in their competitor and to market their own solutions to utility
customers and prosumers. After a successful attack, the threat actor would likely be highly
motivated to avoid detection and discovery of their tradecraft so that a competitor is not
attributed to the disruption.

4.2.2.3 Potential Mitigations: Scenario 2
Following are potential NIST CSF mitigations that relate to the risks described in Scenario 2
(NIST 2024):

 • PR.DS-02: The confidentiality, integrity, and availability of data-in-transit are protected.
 • PR.PS-04: Log records are generated and made available for continuous monitoring.
 • PR.IR-01: Networks and environments are protected from unauthorized logical access.
 • DE.CM-06: External service provider activities and services are monitored to find
 potentially adverse events.
 • DE.CM (all as applicable): Assets are monitored to find anomalies, indicators of
 compromise, and other potentially adverse events.
 • DE.AE (all as applicable): Anomalies, indicators of compromise, and other potentially
 adverse events are analyzed to characterize the events and detect cybersecurity incidents.
 • ID.RA-07: Changes and exceptions are managed, assessed for risk impact, recorded, and
 tracked.
 • GV.SC-08: Relevant suppliers and other third parties are included in incident planning,
 response, and recovery activities.

4.2.3 Scenario 3: Malicious Configuration Changes
Description: A threat actor takes advantage of weak access controls of the DERMS interface to
manipulate the DER devices, such as inverters, that are part of the DERMS network. Once the
DERMS is compromised, poor change management procedures could send wrong or even
malicious commands to trip all the inverters controlled by the DERMS at the same time. With
high penetrations of PV power production, significant losses in PV generation and reactive

 17

power loss can cause voltage variability that affects power quality and stability. Because each
manufacturer has slightly different shutdown response times, the command would likely not
trigger instantaneous tripping (Joo et al. 2019). Instead, inverters would trip in groups and in
intervals between 10 and 30 seconds (Joo et al. 2019).

4.2.3.1 Utility Distributed Energy Resource Management System Risk: Scenario 3
Impact: Mass inverter tripping to utility-scale DERs and to aggregated medium- and low-
voltage smaller DERs could cause significant bulk power system instability and power loss on a
rolling basis to affect DER groups. This could take from minutes, to hours, or up to days to
correct. Instantaneous losses of large amounts of solar PV resources could cause surges in load.
Depending on the utility’s proportion of DERs to traditional assets and loads, the loss of the
situational awareness of DER states could also disrupt the DERMS operator’s ability to properly
coordinate and dispatch the proper alternative resources. This scenario could create significant
financial losses, including equipment repair or replacement costs and significant personnel time
to determine how to operate during the compromise and recovery. Other losses include litigation
or regulatory penalties related to power loss and disruption to interdependent critical
infrastructure sectors, such as transportation, telecommunications, emergency responders, health
and medical facilities, homes and businesses, and more (DOE 2022). Sustained or rolling outages
could also lead to significant increased customer service requests and metering corrections. A
power loss of this magnitude could attract significant amounts of negative press coverage of the
utility and of the DERMS vendor and operator, which could result in regulatory inquiries,
reputational damage to the utility from customers, and cancelled contracts from the utility to the
DERMS vendor and/or operator.

Threat profile: An insider threat—either an employee or a contractor—who does not have
permission to access the DERMS but has physical access to the DERMS interface or software
update server leverages their in-depth knowledge of the system’s DER groups, network
architecture, and software updates within the utility environment to cause the greatest disruptions
to the largest number of DERs possible. The threat actor could be motivated by deeply held
personal grievances and possibly be financially supported by other adversarial actors. The threat
actor could be intent on creating as much disruption as possible for as long as possible. They
could seek to hide their unauthorized presence as long as it takes to execute the successful attack.

4.2.3.2 Aggregator Distributed Energy Resource Management System Risk: Scenario 3
Impact: Large-scale inverter tripping on a rolling basis to a significant proportion of the
medium- and small-scale DERs controlled by the aggregator DERMS creates significant
localized disruptions. Because the aggregator DERMS does not have access to the accurate
network model, operators are initially unclear of the impact that the large loss of DER generation
has on utility services. The aggregator DERMS’ loss of real-time DER control and significant
loss of generation affect the DERMS’ ability to assist grid operators with protections, including
reverse power flow, voltage fluctuation, and demand response. Large fluctuations in voltage
could cause power instability and flickering that affect different localized areas on a rolling
basis, depending on the inverter manufacturer. Entire microgrids that supply power to a rural
community could be impacted, islanding resources that could otherwise be used for resilience
during this kind of event. Further, the incident results in the aggregator’s inability to accurately
measure or predict the current state of the DERs and to look ahead, which could significantly

 18

impede service delivery for a longer time than the recovery times for the utility DERMS.
Regulatory fines and civil litigation from customers could create significant financial losses to
the aggregator and/or aggregator DERMS operator. Reputational damage, increased personnel
time for response and recovery, and the potential for cancelled contracts with the utility operators
could cause significant business damage to these entities.

Threat profile: A moderately resourced but highly skilled extremist group uses their knowledge
of the DERMS functions obtained through several weeks of open-source reconnaissance and
knowledge of the DERMS functions to push the malicious update. This group seeks to disrupt as
many DERs as possible to try to decrease public confidence in the safety and reliability of new
energy resources, such as rooftop solar, electric vehicles, smart thermostats, and so on. These
threat actors might seek to avoid detection but are not concerned with hiding their tradecraft to
demonstrate their capabilities and the exploited vulnerabilities.

4.2.3.3 Potential Mitigations: Scenario 3
Following are potential NIST CSF mitigations that relate to the risks described in Scenario 3
(NIST 2024):

 • PR.PS-05: Installation and execution of unauthorized software are prevented.
 • PR.PS-01: Configuration management practices are established and applied.
 • PR.IR-01: Networks and environments are protected from unauthorized logical access.
 • PR.AA (all as applicable): Access to physical and logical assets is limited to authorized
 users, services, and hardware and managed commensurate with the assessed risk of
 unauthorized access.
 • PR.IR-04: Adequate resource capacity to ensure availability is maintained.
 • PR.PS-04: Log records are generated and made available for continuous monitoring.
 • DE.AE (all as applicable): Anomalies, indicators of compromise, and other potentially
 adverse events are analyzed to characterize the events and detect cybersecurity incidents.
 • DE.CM-03: Personnel activity and technology usage are monitored to find potentially
 adverse events.
 • GV.SC-08: Relevant suppliers and other third parties are included in incident planning,
 response, and recovery activities.

5 Related Standards and Guidelines
The threat profiles described in Section 4.2 reveal cybersecurity risks that might be particularly
important for DERMS vendors, owners, and operators to understand when deploying both
decentralized and centralized DERMS. The accelerated penetration of DERs and the increasingly
important role DER aggregators play in evolving electric systems significantly increase the
complexity of coordinating DER resources that are not directly owned or operated by utilities.
Managing the complexities of the new resources and the new players in electric systems requires
close coordination between a growing number of utility, market, and regulatory stakeholders and
the adoption of standards-based communications and controls that improve the security posture
of DERMS deployments (Newton 2021). Although no current standard exclusively focuses on
DERMS cybersecurity, a variety of cybersecurity standards for energy systems exist that
continue to evolve and grow to meet the dynamic security needs of the energy sector’s critical
infrastructure (Vittor et al. 2017).

Each standard and guideline described in this section was developed through diverse cross-sector
working groups—including members from academia, national laboratories, industry, state energy
officials, and standards development organizations—and they are intended to be applicable to the
widest range of deployments (Walker et al. 2021). Table 2 describes the name of each standard,
requirement, or guideline; the DERMS capability covered within each standard; and the security
topics that relate to the high-level mitigations informed by the threat scenarios in Section 4.

6 Conclusions and Future Work
DERMS offer significant potential to optimize the operation, monitoring, and control of high
penetrations of DERs, including solar PV; however, as DERMS deployments increase in scale
and complexity, they also introduce variable cybersecurity risks that substantially differ
depending on whether the system is implemented as a centralized utility DERMS or a
decentralized aggregator DERMS.

This report addresses a critical research gap by developing tailored cybersecurity risk profiles for
utility-scale and aggregator DERMS deployments. Through three progressively severe threat
scenarios, we illustrate how the same underlying threats and vulnerabilities can result in
markedly different operational, financial, and reliability impacts based on the deployment
architecture, system awareness, and stakeholder responsibilities. High-level mitigations that align
with the NIST CSF are provided for each scenario, along with mappings to relevant standards
and guidelines, including UL 2941, IEEE 1547.3, IEEE 2030.5, and others.

These risk profiles are intended to raise awareness among DERMS vendors, owners, operators,
utilities, and regulators of the deployment-specific nature of cybersecurity risks. They serve as a
practical starting point for organizations to conduct more detailed, context-specific risk
assessments and to prioritize cybersecurity investments accordingly. Ultimately, a clearer
understanding of these risks will support the secure integration of DERMS, strengthen the
cybersecurity posture of the broader solar and DER industry, and help ensure the safe, reliable,
and resilient operation of the evolving electric grid.

To build on this foundation, future research should:

 • Develop co-simulations of the described threat scenarios using real-time test beds (such
 as NLR’s Cyber Range) to quantify system-level impacts for both utility and aggregator
 DERMS.
 • Create targeted training and educational resources to help stakeholders better understand
 and apply relevant cybersecurity standards and best practices.
 • Develop cost-benefit analysis frameworks to support informed decision-making on
 cybersecurity investments across different DERMS deployment scales.
By proactively addressing these deployment-specific cybersecurity challenges, the industry can
unlock the full potential of DERMS while safeguarding the reliability and resilience of the
nation’s electric power system.
