Residual stratification and the Cantor-Bendixson structures of dual algebraic coframes
Silvère Gangloff, Alonso Núñez

Abstract
We introduce a notion of residual derivative for elements of a preordered set, a construction that generalizes both the Frattini subgroup in algebra and the Cantor-Bendixson derivative in T1 topological spaces. For dual algebraic coframes with topologies compatible with order, we establish a partial correspondence between the Cantor-Bendixson structure of the lattice and the residual derivatives of its elements. Within this framework, we provide a complete characterization of the first two Cantor-Bendixson levels in terms of the lattice’s residual structure. This provides a unified lens through which to study the Cantor-Bendixson structures of topological spaces across domains ranging from algebra to functional analysis and dynamics, facilitating the transfer of analytic techniques between them.



1
Introduction

Cantor-Bendixson structure

The
Cantor-Bendixson derivative
of a set
X
relative to a topology
\tau
on
X
is the set
X^{\prime}

of the elements of
X
which are non-isolated for the topology
\tau
. For every ordinal
\alpha
, we denote by

\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(X,\tau)
the
\alpha
th derivative of
X
, defined by transfinite induction, as follows:
\mathcal{S}_{0}(X,\tau):=X
, for any non-limit ordinal
\alpha
,
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha+1}(X,\tau):=(\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(X,\tau))^{\prime}
, and for every limit ordinal
\lambda
,
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(X,\tau):=\bigcap_{\alpha<\lambda}\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(X,\tau)
. The
Cantor-Bendixson rank
of
X
, when it exists, is the smallest ordinal
\alpha
such that
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha+1}(X,\tau)=\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(X,\tau)
. We denote it by
\texttt{r}(X,\tau)
. Historically, this structure emerged from the work of Georg Cantor on a question posed by Riemann: whether a trigonometric series that converges to zero everywhere must have all its coefficients equal to zero. Cantor proved that if a series converges to zero outside a “thin” set
, that is, a set whose Cantor-Bendixson derivative vanishes after finitely many iterations, then the coefficients of the series are zero. The idea of classifying such thin sets led to the introduction of transfinite numbers,
which quantify how many derivation steps are required before the process stabilises. In this way, the Cantor-Bendixson process appears as one of the earliest instances of a transfinite construction in analysis. This line of work was further developed by Bendixson, who proved that every closed subset of
\mathbb{R}

admits a unique decomposition into a perfect set and a countable set.
The latter consists precisely of the points removed during the transfinite derivation process. This result, now known as the Cantor-Bendixson theorem, was later extended to more general topological spaces such as Polish spaces, and became a foundational tool in descriptive set theory.

Applications of the Cantor-Bendixson process

More recently, the Cantor-Bendixson process has found applications across several areas of mathematics, including algebra, functional analysis, and dynamical systems. In algebra, it appears naturally in the study of spaces of subgroups endowed with the Chabauty topology. In this setting, it was used to
classify the topological spaces that arise as the space of subgroups of a countable abelian group
[
6
]
, and
studied as an object of independent interest. It was computed for the Grigorchuk group
[
19
]
and virtually metabelian groups with high Cantor-Bendixson ranks were constructed
[
7
]
.
In functional analysis, the Cantor-Bendixson structure of countable compact Hausdorff spaces governs the structure of associated function spaces. The Banach space
\mathcal{C}(K,\mathbb{R})
, the set of continuous functions from
K
to
\mathbb{R}
, is determined, up to isomorphism, by the Cantor-Bendixson rank of
K
together with the cardinalities of its successive derived layers, leading to classification results for spaces of continuous functions
[
4
]
.
In model theory, it is used to prove, for instance, that certain models are almost prime
[
3
]
.

Multidimensional symbolic dynamics and the hyperspace of shifts

In symbolic dynamics, the Cantor-Bendixson process has been used to analyze the structure of multidimensional shifts
[
2
]
, connecting it with a pre-order on configurations that compares their finite parts and with the notion of complexity. It is also studied in the context of countable shifts, through the realization of high Cantor-Bendixson ranks
[
17
]
. More recently, we have proved that in sharp contrast with the case
d=1

[
15
]
, the hyperspace of
d
-dimensional shifts with the Hausdorff topology has infinite Cantor-Bendixson rank when
d\geq 2

[
8
]
.
In the same work, we also characterized the isolated points in terms of maximal subsystems—a purely order-theoretic notion.

The residual derivative

In the present document, we generalize this perspective to dual algebraic coframes equipped with topologies compatible with the order, including in particular the Lawson topology and the Hausdorff topology on the hyperspaces of shifts. To capture the phenomenon that Cantor-Bendixson layers can be expressed in purely order-theoretic notions at an abstract level, we introduce a new order-theoretic operation: the
residual derivative
.
While it can be defined on any complete lower semilattice,
the residual derivative finds a natural setting in dual algebraic coframes, where it provides a powerful
tool to investigate the Cantor-Bendixson process. In particular, it allows us to characterize the second layer of the Cantor-Bendixson process, that is, the set of elements of the poset that become isolated once isolated elements are removed.
Consider a complete lower semilattice
(L,\leq)
.
Let
x
be an element of
L
. We denote by
\mathcal{M}(x)
the set of
maximal subelements
of
x
, that is, the maximal elements of the poset
\{z\in L\mid z<x\}
. The residual derivative of
x
is
defined by:

\mu(x)=\bigwedge_{z\in\mathcal{M}(x)}z,

whenever
\mathcal{M}(x)=\emptyset
, and by
\mu(x)=x
otherwise.

When
L
is the set of closed subsets of a T1 space, this derivative coincides with the Cantor-Bendixson derivative, while when
L

is the set of subgroups of a group
G
, the derivative coincides with the Frattini sugroup

\Phi(G)
of
G
(which is originally defined as the intersection of all maximal subgroups of
G
). By analogy with the Cantor-Bendixson structure, we also define the residual rank and the residual kernel, leading to a whole conceptual structure and a battery of natural results presented in Section
4
. In particular, we prove that every element can be decomposed into its residual core and the co-Heyting subtractions of its maximal elements, which we call
residues
, that every element which is smaller than
x
and larger than its core can be decomposed into the core of
x
and
the elements of its
residual poset
, which are the completely co-irreducible subelements of
x
that are not smaller than its residual core. We show that these elements are exactly the ones obtained by taking residues iteratively. We also show that the residual derivative is a
\vee
-homomorphism.
Our main results exhibit a deep relation between the Cantor-Bendixson structure of any dual algebraic coframe and the residual structure of its elements by characterizing exactly the first two Cantor-Bendixson layers in terms of the residual structure. In themselves, these characterizations are essentially technical and we leave these details in the core of the text. They can be found in Section
5
and Section
6
.

Our results are reminiscent of the work of Simmons
[
18
]
, who proved that on the family of near-discrete lattices, Gabriel derivative and Cantor-Bendixson derivative coincide. However, our results differs from Simmons’ in the sense that we do not prove an identity but a structured relation between two different derivatives.

Significance

During the last decade, following the work of Mike Hochman
[
10
]

[
11
]
, establishing a strong relation between multidimensional symbolic dynamics and computability theory, most of research effort in the field has been devoted to deepen this relation. However multidimensional symbolic systems are still not well understood, and we believe that more conceptual structure is required for this understanding. We hope that this research direction will contribute to this. Recently, Van Cyr, Bryna Kra and Scott Schmieding
[
5
]
defined the notion of chaotic almost minimal shift, which describes a family of shifts that contains the Rudolph’s discrete version of the
\times 2\times 3
system on the circle
[
16
]
, and proved that Furstenberg’s
\times 2\times 3
conjecture does not hold on this family.
Our framework offers another way to explore shifts similar to Rudolph’s one, as it is contained in the
second layer of the Cantor-Bendixson structure of the hyperspace of bidimensional shifts. The comparison with similar systems may ultimately shed some light on the inner structure of Rudolph’s shift.

More broadly, while many natural topologies on ordered structures arise directly from the order and are studied through structural correspondences, the Cantor–Bendixson process is defined independently of any order-theoretic considerations. We show that, in a broad class of ordered topological structures, it can in fact be recovered from a canonical order-theoretic construction, the residual derivative. This yields a conceptual framework linking topological and order-theoretic stratifications and provides a unified perspective on the Cantor–Bendixson structures in diverse mathematical fields.

Dual algebraic coframes and topology

In this section, we recall definitions of order theory in Section

2.1
Background

A
partially ordered set
(poset) is a pair
(L,\leq)
where
L
is a set and
\leq
is a partial order.
Given a poset
(L,\leq)
, for all
x\in L
, we denote by
\mathop{\downarrow}x
the set
\{z\in L:z\leq x\}
and by
\mathop{\uparrow}x
the set
\{z\in L:x\leq z\}
.
We say that a poset
(L,\leq)
is a
lower (resp. upper) semilattice
when for all
x,y\in L
, the set
\{z\in L:z\leq x,z\leq y\}
(resp.
\{z\in L:x\leq z,y\leq z\}
) admits a maximum (resp. minimum) denoted by
x\wedge y
(resp.
x\vee y
). We say that a lower (resp. upper) semilattice is
complete
when for every subset
S
of
L
, the set
\{x\in L:\forall s\in S,x\leq s\}
has a maximum (resp. the set
\{x\in L:\forall s\in S,s\leq x\}
has a minimum), denoted by
\bigwedge S
or
\bigwedge_{s\in S}s
(resp.
\bigvee S
or
\bigvee_{s\in S}s
). A poset is called a
lattice
when it is both a lower semilattice and an upper semilattice. A lattice is said to be lower (resp. upper) complete if it is complete as a lower (resp. upper) semilattice. A lattice
(L,\leq)
is said to be
distributive
when for all
x,y,z\in L
,
x\wedge(y\vee z)=(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

In a lower complete lattice
(L,\leq)
, for every
x\in L
,
(\mathop{\downarrow}x,\leq)
is upper complete.

Proof.

For all
S\subset\mathop{\downarrow}x
, we prove that the set

S^{+}:=\{x\in L:\forall s\in S,x\geq s\}
has a minimum. Since the lattice
(L,\leq)
is lower complete, we know that
S^{++}:=\{x\in L:\forall s\in S^{+},x\leq s\}
has a maximum, denoted by
\bigwedge S^{+}
. Since by definition,
S\subset S^{++}
, we have
\bigwedge S^{+}\in S^{+}
. By definition,
\bigwedge S^{+}
is a minimum of
S^{+}
.
∎

A
directed set
is a poset
(D,\leq)
such that
for all
d,d^{\prime}\in D
, there exists
d^{\prime\prime}\in D
such that
d^{\prime\prime}\geq d^{\prime}
and
d^{\prime\prime}\geq d
. A
filtered set
is a poset
(F,\leq)
such that
for all
f,f^{\prime}\in F
, there exists
f^{\prime\prime}\in F
such that
f^{\prime\prime}\leq f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime\prime}\leq f
. A
net
(resp.
filter
) is a function whose domain is a directed (resp. filtered) set. Note that the image of an increasing (resp. non-increasing) net is a directed (resp. filtered) set and that the image of an increasing (resp. non-increasing) filter
is a filtered (resp. directed) set.

2.2
Dual algebraic coframes

Definition 2.2
.

Consider a poset
(L,\leq)
. An element
x
of
L
is said to be
dually compact
when for all filtered set
F
in
L
which has an infimum
\bigwedge F\leq x
, then there exists
f\in F
such that
f\leq x
. We denote by
\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
the set of dually compact elements of
(L,\leq)
.

Definition 2.3
.

A poset
(L,\leq)
is called a
dual algebraic
lattice when it is a lower complete lattice and for all
x\in L
, there exists a filtered set
F_{x}
in
\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
such that
\bigwedge F_{x}=x
.

Definition 2.4
.

A poset
(L,\leq)
is called a
coframe
when it is an upper lattice, a lower complete lattice, and satisfies the dual infinite distributivity: for all
x\in L
and every subset
S
of
L
,
x\vee\bigwedge_{s\in S}s=\bigwedge_{s\in S}(x\vee s)
.

Note that every coframe is a distributive lattice.
In the following, we will call
dual algebraic coframe
a poset which is both a dual algebraic lattice and a coframe.

Lemma 2.5
.

For every dual algebraic coframe
(L,\leq)
, the ordered set
(\mathcal{K}(L,\leq),\leq)
is a lower semilattice.

Proof.

Fix
x,z\in\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
. We show that
x\wedge z\in\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
. Let
F
be a filtered set with
\bigwedge F\leq x\wedge z
. Since
x\wedge z\leq x
, we have that

x=x\vee(x\wedge z)=x\vee\bigwedge F.

Since
(L,\leq)
is a coframe, we have

x=\bigwedge_{f\in F}(x\vee f).

Since
x
is dually compact, we have
x\in\{x\vee f\mid f\in F\}
. Hence, there exists
f_{x}\in F
such that
x\vee f_{x}=x
, meaning that
f_{x}\leq x
. Symmetrically, there exists
f_{z}\in F
such that
f_{z}\leq z
. Since
F
is filtered, there exists
f\in F
with
f\leq f_{x}
and
f\leq f_{z}
, so that
f\leq x\wedge z\leq\bigwedge F
, hence,
x\wedge z=f\in F
.
∎

[
9
]
.

2.3
Order-compatible topologies

Given a directed set
(D,\leq)
and a topological space
(X,\tau)
, we say that a net
\boldsymbol{x}:D\rightarrow X
converges to a point
x\in X
when for every
o\in\tau
with
x\in o
, there exists
d\in D
such that for all
d^{\prime}\geq d
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{d^{\prime}}\in o
.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a poset. We say that a topology
\tau
on
L
is
order-compatible
when the following conditions hold:

(i)

every non-increasing (resp. non-decreasing) net
\boldsymbol{x}:D\to L
for which
\bigwedge_{d\in D}\boldsymbol{x}_{d}
(resp.
\bigvee_{d\in D}\boldsymbol{x}_{d}
) exists converges to it;

(ii)

the map
\vee:L\times L\to L
is continuous with respect to
\tau
and
\tau\times\tau
;

(iii)

the order
\leq
is closed as a subset of
L\times L
.

The notion of order-compatible topology is naturally related to multiple classical ideas at the interface of order and topology. Condition
(ii)
sets this structure within the framework of topological semilattices
[
9
]
. The closedness of the order in Condition
(iii)
is a standard in ordered topological spaces, in particular, in the sense of Nachbin
[
14
]
. We notice that, under the additional assumption that the space is Hausdorff, Condition
(iii)
is implied by Condition
(ii)
: since
x\leq y
if and only if
x\vee y=y
, the order is the equalizer of the continuous maps
(x,y)\mapsto x\vee y
and
(x,y)\mapsto y
and is therefore closed whenever the topology is Hausdorff. Thus, Condition
(iii)
is relevant in non-Hausdorff spaces. Finally, Condition
(i)
is a compatibility between topological and order-theoretic convergence which is involved in the definition of Lawson topology
[
9
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, these three conditions have not been combined into a single definition in the existing literature. A canonical source of examples is the class of compact Hausdorff topological semilattices.
Definition

2.6
then provides a more general framework than the aforementioned class, as it has been designed to handle non-Hausdorff or non-compact situations.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a dual algebraic lattice and let
\tau
be an order-compatible topology on
L
. Then, the topological space
(L,\tau)
is Hausdorff.

Proof.

Consider two distinct elements
z,z^{\prime}\in L
. Assume that
z\not\leq z^{\prime}
and
z^{\prime}\not\leq z
(the cases
z<z^{\prime}
and
z^{\prime}<z
are processed similarly). Since
(L,\leq)
is dual algebraic, there exist
two non-increasing nets
\boldsymbol{z}:D^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
and

\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}:D\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
such that
\bigwedge_{d}\boldsymbol{z}_{d}=z
and

\bigwedge_{d^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}_{d}=z^{\prime}
. As a consequence of the assumption, there exist
d,d^{\prime}
such that
z^{\prime}\notin\mathop{\downarrow}\boldsymbol{z}_{d}
and
z\notin\mathop{\downarrow}\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}_{d}
. Since
\leq
is closed, for all
x
,
\mathop{\downarrow}x
is closed. Since
\boldsymbol{z}_{d}
and
\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}_{d^{\prime}}
are dually compact,

\mathop{\downarrow}\boldsymbol{z}_{d}
and
\mathop{\downarrow}\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}_{d^{\prime}}
are open,
and thus the sets

\mathop{\downarrow}\boldsymbol{z}_{d}\setminus\mathop{\downarrow}\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}_{d^{\prime}}
and
\mathop{\downarrow}\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}_{d^{\prime}}\setminus\mathop{\downarrow}\boldsymbol{z}_{d}
are both open and separate the two elements
z
and
z^{\prime}
.
∎

Provided a set
S
, we call choice function of
S
a function
\theta:\mathcal{P}(S)\setminus\{\emptyset\}\rightarrow S
such that for all
P\in\mathcal{P}(S)
,

\theta(P)\in P
.

Lemma 2.8
.

For all dual algebraic coframe
(L,\leq)
such that
\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
has a choice function and all order-compatible topology
\tau
on
L
, an element
x\in L
is dually compact if and only if
\mathop{\downarrow}x
is open.

This equivalence provides a direct translation between order-theoretic compactness and a topological property of principal ideals. In particular, the family of dually compact elements can be recovered purely from the topology as those elements whose lower sets are open.

Remark 2.9
.

Note, on the other hand, that since
\leq
is closed,
\mathop{\downarrow}x
is always a closed set.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)

For all filtered set
F
such that
\bigwedge F\leq x
, since
\mathop{\downarrow}x
is open and
F
converges to
\bigwedge F
, there is some
f\in F
such that
f\leq x
. We proved that
x
is dually compact.
(\Rightarrow)
Assume that
\mathop{\downarrow}x
is not open, meaning that there exists
z\in\mathop{\downarrow}x
such that all for
all
o\in\tau
with
z\in o
, the set
o\setminus(\mathop{\downarrow}x)
is not empty. Since
(L,\leq)
is a dual algebraic lattice, there is some filtered set
F_{z}
in
\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
such that
\bigwedge F_{z}=z
. Since for all
f\in F
,

\mathop{\downarrow}f
is open, none of the elements of
F
is in
\mathop{\downarrow}x
. Since the topology is order-compatible,
F_{z}
converges to
z
, meaning that
F_{z}\cap o\neq\emptyset
. Thus
o\setminus(\mathop{\downarrow}x)
has an element in
\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
. Since
\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
has a choice function
\theta
, we can set
z_{o}:=\theta(\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)\cap(o\setminus\mathop{\downarrow}x))
. The set
\tau_{x}:=\{o\in\tau:x\in o\}
is a filtered set for the order
\subset
. We thus have a filter
\boldsymbol{z}:\tau_{x}\rightarrow L
such that for all
o\in\tau_{x}
,
\boldsymbol{z}_{o}\in o\setminus(\mathop{\downarrow}x)
.
By definition, this filter converges to
z
. By continuity of
\vee
, we also have
x\vee\boldsymbol{z}_{o}\rightarrow x\vee z=x
. Since for all
o
,
\boldsymbol{z}_{o}\not\leq x
, we have
x\vee\boldsymbol{z}_{o}>x
. This means that
\bigwedge_{o\in\tau_{x}}(x\vee\boldsymbol{z}_{o})=x
, and
x
is not dually compact.
∎

2.4
Examples

In this section, we present some examples of order-compatible topologies on dual algebraic coframes.

Lawson topology

Definition 2.10
.

Consider a poset
(L,\leq)
. We call
dual Lawson topology
on
L
the topology

\tau^{*}(L,\leq)
generated by the subbase

\left\{\mathop{\downarrow}x:x\in\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)\right\}\cup\left\{L\setminus(\mathop{\downarrow}x):x\in\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)\right\}.

We prove here that when
(L,\leq)
is a dual algebraic lattice,

\tau^{*}(L,\leq)
is order-compatible (stated as Proposition
2.14
).

Lemma 2.11
.

Consider a poset
(L,\leq)
. For all filtered set
F
in
L
such that
\bigwedge F
exists,
F
converges to
\bigwedge F
for the dual Lawson topology. For all directed set
D
in
L
such that
\bigvee D
exists,
D
converges to
\bigvee D
for the dual Lawson topology.

Proof.

Fix some filtered set
F
. For all
x\in\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
such that
\bigwedge F\leq x
, there exists
f_{x}\in F
such that
f_{x}\leq x
.
As a consequence, for all
f\in F
such that
f\leq f_{x}
, by transitivity, we have
f\leq x
. Additionally, for all
x\in L
, if
\bigwedge F\not\geq x
then there exists
f^{\prime}_{x}\in F
such that
f^{\prime}_{x}\not\geq x
. As a consequence, for all
f\in F
such that
f\leq f^{\prime}_{x}
,
we have
f\not\geq x
, otherwise we would have
f^{\prime}_{x}\geq x
by transitivity.
These two facts imply that
F
converges to
\bigwedge F
for the dual Lawson topology. The proof is similar for the second part of the statement.
∎

Lemma 2.12
.

Consider a poset
(L,\leq)
. The operator
\vee:(L\times L,\tau^{*}(L,\leq)\times\tau^{*}(L,\leq))\rightarrow(L,\tau^{*}(L,\leq))
is continuous.

Proof.

For all
z\in L
,

\vee^{-1}(\mathop{\downarrow}z)=\{(x,y)\in L\times L:x\vee y\leq z\}
.
We thus have
\vee^{-1}(\mathop{\downarrow}z)=(\mathop{\downarrow}z)^{2}
, which is open for the topology
\tau^{*}(L,\leq)\times\tau^{*}(L,\leq)
when
z
is dually compact. We also have
\vee^{-1}(L\setminus\mathop{\downarrow}z)=((L\setminus\mathop{\downarrow}z)\times L)\cup(L\times(L\setminus\mathop{\downarrow}z))
, and when
z
is dually compact, since
L\setminus\mathop{\downarrow}z
is open for the dual Lawson topology,
\vee^{-1}(L\setminus\mathop{\downarrow}z)
is open. We just proved that
\vee
is continuous.
∎

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a dual algebraic lattice. The order
\leq
is closed for the dual Lawson topology.

Set
R:=\{(x,z)\in L\times L:x\not\leq z\}
. It is sufficient to see that
R
is open. Observe that for all
(x,z)\in R
, there exists
f\in\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
such that
x\leq f
and
f\not\leq z
, so that
x\in\mathop{\downarrow}f
and
z\in L\setminus(\mathop{\downarrow}f)
. The set
(\mathop{\downarrow}f)\times(L\setminus(\mathop{\downarrow}f))
is open, contains
(x,z)
and is included in
R
.

Proposition 2.14
.

For all dual algebraic lattice
(L,\leq)
,
\tau^{*}(L,\leq)
is order-compatible.

Proof.

This simply derives from Lemma
2.11
, Lemma
2.12
and Lemma
2.13
.

Remark 2.15
.

Hausdorff topology on the hyperspace of shifts

For all
d\geq 1
, a shift of dimension
d
is a subset of some
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is a finite set, which is closed for the infinite product of the discrete topology, and invariant by the shift action. A pattern on alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is any element of some
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{U}}
, where
\mathbb{U}
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We say that a pattern
p\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{U}}
appears in a configuration
x\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
when there exists
\textbf{u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
x_{\textbf{u}+\mathbb{U}}=p
.
For all shift
X
, we denote by
\mathcal{L}(X)
the language of
X
, that is the set of patterns which appear in at least one configuration of
X
.
For a finite set of patterns
\mathcal{F}
, we usually denote by
X_{\mathcal{F}}
the set of configurations in which no element of
\mathcal{F}
appear. A shift
X
is said to be of finite type when there exists a finite set of patterns
\mathcal{F}
such that
X=X_{\mathcal{F}}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{H}^{d}
the set of
d
-dimensional shifts, and by
\tau^{d}_{\mathcal{H}}
the Hausdorff metrics topology on
\mathcal{H}^{d}
. We call
(\mathcal{H}^{d},\subset,\tau_{\mathcal{H}}^{d})
the
hyperspace
of
d
-dimensional shifts. Its dually compact elements are the shifts of finite type, and for all shift
X
which is not of finite type, there exists a decreasing sequence of finite type shifts
(X_{n})_{n}
such that
X=\bigcap_{n}X_{n}
. It is thus a dual algebraic lattice. The set
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H}^{d},\subset)
is countable (as the set of finite sets of patterns is countable), meaning that it has a choice function. The lattice
(\mathcal{H}^{d},\subset)
is clearly
lower complete and satisfies the dual infinite distributivity. It is thus a coframe. It is also clear that the Hausdorff topology is order-compatible.
We believe that this example is a particular case
of the dual Lawson topology, but we do not need to prove this here.

3
Residual derivative

In this section, we introduce the
residual derivative
, an operator that associates to each element the meet of its maximal proper subelements. This construction isolates the part of an element that cannot be recovered from its maximal approximations. Iterating this operator yields a transfinite decreasing sequence, whose stabilization defines a rank and a canonical core. This provides an intrinsic stratification of the lattice, independent of any external topological construction. The derivative and the subsequent residual stratification
4
are used to describe the Cantor-Bendixson structures of dual algebraic coframes.

The section is organized as follows. We first define maximal subelements in Section
3.1
and prove elementary statements about them. The residual derivative itself is defined in Section
3.2
. In Section
3.3
we see that the residual derivative generalizes multiples concepts across algebra and topology.

3.1
Maximal subelements

For this section, let
(L,\leq)
be a partially ordered set and
S
a subset of
L
. An element
z
of
S
is said to be
maximal
when for all
z^{\prime}\in S
,
z^{\prime}\geq z
implies
z^{\prime}=z
.

Notation 3.1
.

For all family
H
of elements of
L
, and for all
x\in S
, we denote by
\mathcal{M}_{H}(x)
the set of maximal elements of
(H\cap((\mathop{\downarrow}x)\setminus\{x\}),\leq)
. Its elements are called maximal
H
-subelements of
x
.

Whenever
H
is equal to
L
, we will drop
H
from the notations.
Note that in the literature,
it it said that an element
y

covers
another one
x
when
x
is a maximal subelement of
y
.

Notation 3.2
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, we denote by
\mathcal{T}_{n}(L,\leq)
the set of elements
x
of
L
such that
\mathcal{M}(x)
has cardinality
n
.

Remark 3.3
.

In this text, we typically take
H
to be
L
or
\mathcal{T}_{0}(L,\leq)
, though other families may be considered as needed.

Definition 3.4
.

An
H
-
outcast
of
x\in L
is some
z\in H
such that
z<x
and that there is no maximal element
m
of
H\cap(\mathop{\downarrow}x)\setminus\{x\}
such that
z\leq m
.

Lemma 3.5
.

When
(H,\leq)
is an upper semilattice, for all
y,z\in\mathcal{M}_{H}(x)
such that
y\neq z
, we have
y\vee z=x
.

Proof.

Since
y\neq z
, we have
y\vee z>z
or
y\vee z>y
. Because
y
and
z
are in
\mathcal{M}_{H}(x)
, we must have
y\vee z=x
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Assume that
L
is a distributive lattice and
(H,\leq)
is a lattice. For all
y,z\in\mathcal{M}_{H}(x)
such that
y\neq z
,
y\wedge z
is a maximal
H
-subelement of
z
and
y
.

Proof.

Consider some
w\in H
such that
y\wedge z\leq w\leq z
.
We have
y\leq w\vee y\leq x
. Since
H
is an upper semilattice,

w\vee y\in F
. Since
y
is maximal, we thus have
w\vee y=y
or
w\vee y=x
.
In the first case,
w\leq y
, which implies
w\leq z\wedge y
, and thus
w=y\wedge z
.
In the second case, by distributivity, we have
(w\wedge z)\vee(y\wedge z)=z
. Since
w\geq y\wedge z
, we have
z=w\vee(w\wedge z)=w
. Since
H
is a lower semilattice,
y\wedge z\in H
. It is thus a maximal
H
-subelement of
z
. We then apply this to
y
as well.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Provided a dual algebraic coframe
(L,\leq)
, for every
x\in\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
and every
m\in\mathcal{M}(x)
,
m\in\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
.

Proof.

Let
m\in\mathcal{M}(x)
, and let
F
be a filtered set with
\bigwedge F=m
. We show that
m\in F
. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
m\notin F
, so that
m<f
for all
f\in F
. Set
F^{\prime}:=\{m\vee f\mid f\in F\}
. Since
F
is filtered,
F^{\prime}
is filtered. For each
f\in F
, since
m<f
we have
m<m\vee f
. Since
(L,\leq)
is a coframe,

\bigwedge F^{\prime}=\bigwedge_{f\in F}(m\vee f)=m\vee\bigwedge F=m\vee m=m.

In particular, since
x
is dually compact and
m=\bigwedge F^{\prime}\leq x
, by definition of dually compactness, there is some
f\in F
such that
m\vee f\leq x
.
There must be such
f
which also satisfies
m\vee f<x
. Otherwise we would have
\bigwedge F^{\prime}=x=m
, which is not possible. However, this contradicts the hypothesis
m\in\mathcal{M}(x)
.
∎

3.2
Definition of the residual derivative

Definition 3.8
.

Let
(L,\leq)
be a complete lower semilattice, and
H
a family of elements of
L
. For all
x
in
L
, we call
residual
H
-derivative
of
x
the element
\mu_{H}(x)
defined as follows. When
\mathcal{M}_{H}(x)\neq\emptyset
:

\mu_{H}(x):=\underset{z\in\mathcal{M}_{H}(x)}{\bigwedge}z.

Otherwise
\mu_{H}(x):=x
.

Notation 3.9
.

For all
x\in L
, we set
x_{H}^{(0)}:=x
, and for all ordinal
\alpha
, we set
x_{H}^{(\alpha+1)}=\mu_{H}(x_{H}^{(\alpha)})
. When
\lambda
is a limit ordinal, we set
x_{H}^{(\lambda)}=\bigwedge_{\alpha<\lambda}x_{H}^{(\alpha)}
.

Theorem 3.10
.

Assume that
(L,\leq)
is a dual algebraic lattice and that
\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
has a choice function. For all
x
in
L
, there exists an ordinal
\alpha
such that

x_{H}^{(\alpha)}=x_{H}^{(\alpha+1)}
. We call the smallest of these ordinals the
H
-
residual rank
of
x
, and denote it by
\texttt{r}_{H}(x)
. We also denote by
\texttt{c}_{H}(x)
the set
x_{H}^{(\texttt{r}_{H}(x))}
and call it the
H
-
core
of
x
. It satisfies
\mathcal{M}_{H}(\texttt{c}_{H}(x))=\emptyset
.

Proof.

By definition, for all
x\in L
,
\mathcal{M}_{H}(x)
is contained in
\mathop{\downarrow}x
, so
\mu_{H}(x)\leq x
. In particular,
for all ordinal
\alpha
,
x_{H}^{(\alpha+1)}\leq x_{H}^{(\alpha)}
, and for all limit ordinal
\lambda
, by definition, we have
x_{H}^{(\lambda)}=\bigwedge_{\beta<\lambda}x_{H}^{(\beta)}\leq x_{H}^{(\beta)}
for all
\beta<\lambda
. Hence,
\alpha\mapsto x^{(\alpha)}
is a non-increasing net. Suppose ad absurdum that for every ordinal
\alpha
, we have
x_{H}^{(\alpha+1)}<x_{H}^{(\alpha)}
.
Denote by
\theta
a choice function
of
\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
. For all ordinal
\kappa
, we thus have an injection from
\kappa
to
\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)\cap(\mathop{\downarrow}x)
which to every ordinal
\alpha
associates

\theta(\{f\in\mathcal{K}(L,\leq):f\geq x_{H}^{(\alpha+1)},f\not\geq x_{H}^{(\alpha)}\})
.
This straightforwardly contradicts Hartogs’ lemma, which implies that there is an ordinal
\kappa
such that there is no injection from
\kappa
to
\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)\cap(\mathop{\downarrow}x)
.
∎

Remark 3.11
.

This actually proves that for all
x\in L
there is an injection from the
H
-residual rank of
x
to
\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
. In particular, when
\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
is countable, every element of
L
has a countable
H
-residual rank.

Remark 3.12
.

Within the hyperspace of shifts, the residual derivative is close to the Cantor-Bendixson derivative. As a matter of fact, provided a
d
-dimensional shift
X
and any isolated point
x\in X
,
X\setminus\mathcal{O}(x)
, where
\mathcal{O}(x):=\{\sigma^{\boldsymbol{u}(x)}:\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
denotes the orbit of
x
, is a maximal subelement of
X
. Note that this implies that every element of
\mathcal{T}_{0}(\mathcal{H})
is perfect.
However they are not identical. For instance, consider the disjoint union of a minimal aperiodic shift with a full shift. Since both are perfect sets, their union is also perfect.
However, it has a unique maximal subelement, which is the full shift, and this maximal subelement is thus equal to its residual derivative. This proves, in particular, that a maximal subelement of a shift
X
is not necessarily of the form
X\setminus\mathcal{O}(x)
, where
x\in X
is an isolated point.

Remark 3.13
.

Note that this does not enter in contradiction with the fact that the residual derivative coincides with the Cantor-Bendison
when the lattice is the set of closed subsets of a T1 space (Example
3.17
) since we consider here the set of subsystems of a shift, which are its closed subsets which are invariant by the shift action.

Remark 3.14
.

Note also that the elements of the residual structure, the residual rank, core and boundary, are all topological invariants.

3.3
Examples

Example 3.15

(Jacobson radical)
.

Let
R
be a ring. The Jacobson radical of
R
is the intersection of the maximal left ideals of
R
. The set of left ideals of
R
form a lower semilattice for the inclusion preorder
\subset
, and for all ideals
I,J
, the meet of
I
and
J
is their intersection.
The ring
R
itself is a left ideal, and its residual derivative is exactly its Jacobson radical. See
[
12
]
for more details.

Example 3.16

(Frattini subgroup)
.

Let
G
be a group. The Frattini subgroup of
G
is defined as the intersection of its maximal subgroups. The set of subgroups of
G
form a lower semilattice for the inclusion preorder, and for all subgroups
H,H^{\prime}
, the meet of
H
and
H^{\prime}
is
H\cap H^{\prime}
. In this setting, the residual derivative of
G
is its Frattini subgroup. See Section
10.4
in
[
13
]
for more details. In this context, our definition of residual rank is reminiscent of the so-called Frattini series, obtained by iterating the operator building the Frattini subgroup.

Example 3.17

(Cantor-Bendixson derivative of T1-spaces)
.

Provided a topological space
X
, its Cantor-Bendixson derivative, usually denoted by
X^{\prime}
, is the topological space which is obtained from
X
by removing its isolated points. When
X
is a T1-space, meaning that every singleton of
X
is closed, it is also the residual derivative of
X
in the lower semilattice of its closed subspaces. This comes from the fact that a closed subspace is maximal if and only if it is of the form
X\backslash\{x\}
, where
x
is an isolated point in
X
. Indeed, it is straightforward that a subspace of the form
X\backslash\{x\}
is closed if and only if
x
is isolated. When it is, it is clearly maximal. On the other hand, for any
x,y\in X
such that
x\neq y
, a closed subspace which does not contain
x
and
y
is not maximal, because
X\cup\{y\}
is also closed, different from
X
and strictly contains
X
.

4
Residual stratification in coframes

In this section, we assume that
(L,\leq)
is a coframe. We refine the structure induced by the residual derivative by introducing the notion of residue, which captures what is “left out” of a maximal subelement (Section
4.1
). This leads to a canonical decomposition of every element into its core and its residues (Section
4.2
). Building on this decomposition, we define the boundary poset of an element
x
as the set of strongly co-irreducible elements above its core
\texttt{c}(x)
(Section
4.3
), and show that every element
z
such that
\texttt{c}(x)\leq z\leq x
can be reconstructed from the core and elements of this boundary. The boundary itself carries a natural stratification reflecting the successive stages of the residual process. As a structural consequence, we prove that the residual derivative is a
\vee
-homomorphism (Section
4.4
), paralleling the behavior of the Cantor–Bendixson derivative. The framework developed in this section will serve as the main tool for the analysis of the Cantor–Bendixson layers in the subsequent sections.

Since
L
is complete lower semilattice, it has a minimum that we denote by
\varepsilon
. It satisfies
\varepsilon\vee x=x
and
\varepsilon\wedge x=\varepsilon
for all
x\in L
, and it is the unique minimal element of
L
.

4.1
Residues

Notation 4.1
.

For all
x,z\in L
such that
x\leq z
, we denote by
z-x
the following element, usually called Co-Heyting subtraction of
x
from
z
:

z-x:=\bigwedge\{y\in\mathop{\downarrow}z:x\vee y=z\}.

It is well defined because
L
is a complete lower semilattice.

For the remainder of this section, we fix
F\subset L
a family which contains
\varepsilon
and such that
(F,\leq)
is an upper semilattice.
We call
H
-
residues
of
x
the elements of
\mathop{\downarrow}x
of the form
x-m
, where
m\in\mathcal{M}_{H}(x)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For all
x\in L
and
z\leq x
, we have
z\vee(x-z)=x
.

Proof.

Let us denote by
S

the set
\{y\in\mathop{\downarrow}x:z\vee y=x\}
. By the dual infinite distributivity,

z\vee\bigwedge_{s\in S}s=\bigwedge_{s\in S}(z\vee s)=x.

By definition of
(z-x)
,
we thus have
z\vee(x-z)=x
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

For all
x\in L
and all
m\in\mathcal{M}_{H}(x)
,
\mathcal{M}_{H}(x-m)
has exactly one element and
(x-m)
has no
H
-outcast. Furthermore,
\mu_{H}(x-m)\leq m
.

Remark 4.4
.

An element
x
is usually called completely co-irreducible when for all family
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
such that
x=\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i}
, there exists
i
such that
x_{i}=x
. One can prove that an element
x
is completely co-irreducible if and only if
\mathcal{M}(x)
has a unique element and for all
z\leq x
,
z\leq\mu(x)
.

Proof.

For all
z<(x-m)
, by definition of
(x-m)
, we have
m\vee z<x
. Since
m\in\mathcal{M}_{H}(x)
and
m\leq m\vee z
, if
z\in H
, which implies
m\vee z\in H
, we must have
m=m\vee z
, which means that
z\leq m
. If
\mathcal{M}_{H}(x-m)
had at least two elements
z,z^{\prime}
, by Lemma
3.5
we would have
z\vee z^{\prime}=x-m
. Since
z\leq m
and
z^{\prime}\leq m
, we would have
x-m\leq m
, which would imply that
m\vee(x-m)=m
, and this, by Lemma
4.2
, is equal to
x
. Thus
x=m
, which is not true. Therefore
\mathcal{M}_{H}(x-m)
has at most one element. Furthermore, there exists some
z\in F
such that
z<x-m
. Otherwise we would have
(x-m)=\varepsilon
, which is not possible, as it would imply that
m=x
.
Thus
\bigvee\{z\in H:z<(x-m)\}
is well-defined, by Lemma
2.1
. Since for all
z<(x-m)
,
z\leq m
,
\bigvee\{z\in H:z<(x-m)\}
is distinct from
x-m
. By definition, it is in
\mathcal{M}_{H}(x-m)
and it is larger than every
z<(x-m)
such that
z\in H
, which implies that
(x-m)
has no
H
-outcast.
∎

4.2
Core-residues decomposition

Lemma 4.5
.

For all
z\in L
and all subset
S
of
\mathop{\downarrow}z
, we have

z=\left(\bigwedge_{s\in S}s\right)\vee\left(\bigvee_{s\in S}(z-s)\right).

Proof.

Let us set

x:=\bigvee_{s\in S}(z-s).
By dual infinite distributivity, we have:

\left(\bigwedge_{s\in S}s\right)\vee\left(\bigvee_{s\in S}(z-s)\right)=\bigwedge_{s\in S}(x\vee s)

For all
s\in S
, we have
x\geq(x-s)
, meaning that

x\vee s=z
. We thus
obtain the equality of the statement.

∎

Lemma 4.6
.

For all
x\in L
,
x=\mu_{H}(x)\vee\left(\underset{{m\in\mathcal{M}_{H}(x)}}{\bigvee}(x-m)\right)
.

Proof.

When
\mathcal{M}_{H}(x)
is empty, this is straightforward, as in this case
\mu_{H}(x)=x
. When
\mathcal{M}_{H}(x)
is not empty, this is a consequence of Lemma
4.5
.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

For all
x\in L
and all
m\in\mathcal{M}(x)
, we have:

m=\mu(x)\vee\left(\underset{\underset{n\neq m}{n\in\mathcal{M}(x)}}{\bigvee}(x-n)\right).

Proof.

For all
m\in\mathcal{M}(x)
,
we set the two following other elements:

m^{*}:=\mu(x)\vee\left(\underset{\underset{n\neq m}{n\in\mathcal{M}(x)}}{\bigvee}(x-n)\right)\quad\text{and}\quad m^{\prime}:=m^{*}\vee\mu(x-m).

We prove the statement in two steps: first, we prove that for all
m
, we have
m=m^{\prime}
; then that for all
m
, we have
m=m^{*}
.

1.

By definition of
\mu(x)
, we have
\mu(x)\leq m
. We also have
x-n\leq m
for all
n\in\mathcal{M}(x)
such that
n\neq m
. This comes from the definition of
x-n
and the fact that
m\vee n=x
. Because
m
is a maximal subelement of
x
, we have
\mu(x-m)\leq m
by Lemma
4.3
. This implies that
m\geq m^{\prime}
. In order to finish the proof of the claim, it is sufficient to see that
m^{\prime}
is a maximal subelement of
x
. Let us consider some element
z\in L
such that
m^{\prime}\leq z\leq x
.
If we have
z\wedge(x-m)=(x-m)
, then
x-m\leq z
, and since
m^{\prime}\leq z
, we have
(x-m)\vee m^{\prime}\leq z
.
By definition of
m^{\prime}
, we have

(x-m)\vee m^{\prime}=m^{*}\vee(x-m)
. By definition of
m^{*}
and Lemma
4.6
,
we have
(x-m)\vee m^{\prime}=x
, which implies that
z=x
. Otherwise,
we have
z\wedge(x-m)<(x-m)
.
Since
\mu(x-m)\leq m^{\prime}\leq z
,
we have
z\wedge(x-m)\geq\mu(x-m)
, and thus
z\wedge(x-m)=\mu(x-m)
. By distributivity and Lemma
4.6
, we have
z=z\wedge x=z\wedge(m^{*}\vee(x-m))=(z\wedge m^{*})\vee(z\wedge(x-m))
.
Since
m^{*}\leq m^{\prime}\leq z
, we have
z\wedge m^{*}=m^{*}
. Since
z\wedge(x-m)=\mu(x-m)
, we obtain
z=m^{\prime}
. We have just proved that
m^{\prime}
is maximal, which means, since
m^{\prime}\leq m
and
m
is maximal, that
m=m^{\prime}
.
2.
As a consequence of the first point,

\left(\underset{{n\in\mathcal{M}(x)}}{\bigvee}\mu(x-n)\right)\leq m

for all
m
maximal subelement of
x
. We thus have:

\left(\underset{{n\in\mathcal{M}(x)}}{\bigvee}\mu(x-n)\right)\leq\mu(x).

In particular, for all
n\in\mathcal{M}(x)
,
\mu(x-n)\leq\mu(x)
. By using the first point again, we have that for all
m\in\mathcal{M}(x)
:

m=\mu(x)\vee\left(\underset{\underset{n\neq m}{n\in\mathcal{M}(x)}}{\bigvee}(x-n)\right).

∎

Lemma 4.8
.

For all
x\in L
and
m\in\mathcal{M}(\mu(x))
, we have
\mu(x)-m\leq\underset{n\in\mathcal{M}(x)}{\bigvee}(x-n)
. Furthermore, when
\mathcal{M}(x)
is finite, there exists
n
such that
\mu(x)-m\leq x-n
.

Proof.

Assume ad absurdum that it is not the case, meaning that there exists
m\in\mathcal{M}(\mu(x))

such that
\mu(x)-m\not\leq\underset{n\in\mathcal{M}(x)}{\bigvee}(x-n)
.
Then

m^{\prime}:=m\vee\left(\underset{n\in\mathcal{M}(x)}{\bigvee}(x-n)\right)

is a maximal subelement of
x
.
Indeed, we have
m^{\prime}<x
. Otherwise, we would have, by distributivity,

(\mu(x)-m)=(m\wedge(\mu(x)-m))\vee\left(\left(\underset{n\in\mathcal{M}(x)}{\bigvee}(x-n)\right)\wedge(\mu(x)-m)\right),

and since
(m\wedge(\mu(x)-m))\leq\mu(\mu(x)-m)
, we must have
\left(\underset{n\in\mathcal{M}(x)}{\bigvee}(x-n)\right)\wedge(\mu(x)-m)=\mu(x)-m
, meaning
\mu(x)-m\leq\left(\underset{n\in\mathcal{M}(x)}{\bigvee}(x-n)\right)
, which was assumed false. Using Lemma
4.6
on
x
and
\mu(x)
, for any
z\leq x
which satisfies
z\geq m^{\prime}
,
z\wedge(\mu(x)-m)=\mu(x)-m
implies
z=x
and
z\wedge(\mu(x)-m)<\mu(x)-m
implies
z=m^{\prime}
. We have proved that
m^{\prime}
is a maximal subelement of
x
. From the definition of
m^{\prime}
, we have
(x-m^{\prime})\leq m^{\prime}
, and thus
m^{\prime}=x
, which is impossible.
When
\mathcal{M}(x)
is finite we thus have, since
L
is distributive:

z-m=\underset{n\in\mathcal{M}(x)}{\bigvee}((x-n)\wedge(z-m)).

Since all the elements of
\mathop{\downarrow}(z-m)
different from
z-m
are smaller than
\mu(z-m)
(by Lemma
4.3
), there is some
n
such that
(x-n)\wedge(z-m)=(z-m)
, which means that
(z-m)\leq(x-n)
.
∎

This follows directly from Lemma
4.6
and Lemma
4.8
applied inductively:

Lemma 4.9
.

For all
x\in L

This decomposition plays a role analogous to decomposition of topological spaces into a perfect kernel and isolated layers.

4.3
The boundary poset

Notation 4.10

For all

Lemma 4.11

For all

\texttt{c}(x)\not\leq\partial x

\partial x<x
. When these conditions are satisfied, the outcasts of

Proof.

(ii)\Rightarrow(iii)
. If
\texttt{c}(x)\not\leq\partial x
, since

, we must have
\partial x<x
.

(iii)\Rightarrow(i)
. If
\partial x<x

\partial x
is an outcast: if there was any

such that
\partial x\leq m
, we would have
(x-m)\leq m
and thus

.

(i)\Rightarrow(iii)
If
\partial x=x
, then for all
z<x
,
there must be some

such that

(otherwise we would have

. This means that

has no outcast.
(iii)\Rightarrow(ii)

, we get

.
Assume that these conditions are satisfied. For all

such that

is an outcast because
\partial x
is an outcast. For all

such that

.
∎

Proposition 4.18

The triplet

Proof.

Let us prove that for all

4.4
The residual derivative is a
\vee
-homomorphism

We prove here that the residual derivative is a
\vee
-homomorphism. The fact that it behaves as an algebraic operator compatible with the lattice structure reinforces the idea that this is a canonical construction rather than an ad hoc definition.

Proposition 4.31
.

For all
x\in L
and all
z\in\mathop{\downarrow}x
, we have
\mu(z)\leq\mu(x)
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to see that for all
s\in\texttt{s}_{0}(x)
such that
s\leq z
, we have
s\in\texttt{s}_{0}(z)
. Indeed, in this case,

\mu(z)=(\mu(x)\wedge\mu(z))\vee\left(\bigvee_{s\in\texttt{s}_{0}(x)}s\wedge\mu(z)\right)
. For all
s\in\texttt{s}_{0}(x)
,
in any case we have
\mu(z)\wedge s\leq\mu(s)\leq\mu(x)
, meaning that
\mu(z)\wedge s\leq\mu(x)\wedge\mu(x)
. This implies that
\mu(z)=\mu(x)\wedge\mu(z)
and thus
\mu(z)\leq\mu(x)
.
∎

Proposition 4.32
.

For all
x,z\in L
,
\mu(x\vee z)=\mu(x)\vee\mu(z)
.

Proof.

We have
\mu(x)\vee\mu(z)\leq\mu(x\vee z)
as a consequence of Proposition
4.31
.
We prove that
\mu(x)\vee\mu(z)\geq\mu(x\vee z)
. From Lemma
4.6
, we have

x\vee z=\mu(x)\vee\mu(z)\vee\left(\underset{{m\in\mathcal{M}(x)}}{\bigvee}(x-m)\right)\vee\left(\underset{{m\in\mathcal{M}(z)}}{\bigvee}(z-m)\right).

We can rewrite this as:

x\vee z=\mu(x)\vee\mu(z)\vee\left(\underset{\underset{x-m\not\leq\mu(z)}{m\in\mathcal{M}(x)}}{\bigvee}(x-m)\right)\vee\left(\underset{\underset{z-m\not\leq\mu(x)}{m\in\mathcal{M}(z)}}{\bigvee}(z-m)\right).

It is thus sufficient to see that for all
m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(x)
such that
x-m^{\prime}\not\leq\mu(z)
,

\mu(x)\vee\mu(z)\vee\left(\underset{\underset{x-m\not\leq\mu(z)}{m\in\mathcal{M}(x)\setminus\{m^{\prime}\}}}{\bigvee}(x-m)\right)\vee\left(\underset{\underset{z-m\not\leq\mu(x)}{m\in\mathcal{M}(z)}}{\bigvee}(z-m)\right)

and for all
m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(z)
such that
z-m^{\prime}\not\leq\mu(x)
,

\mu(x)\vee\mu(z)\vee\left(\underset{\underset{x-m\not\leq\mu(z)}{m\in\mathcal{M}(x)}}{\bigvee}(x-m)\right)\vee\left(\underset{\underset{z-m\not\leq\mu(x)}{m\in\mathcal{M}(z)\setminus\{m^{\prime}\}}}{\bigvee}(z-m)\right)

are maximal subelements of
x\vee z
. This uses similar arguments as the proof of Lemma
4.7
.
∎

5
Characterization of
\mathcal{S}_{0}(L,\tau)\setminus\mathcal{S}_{1}(L,\tau)

For the remainder of this text, we fix a dual algebraic coframe
(L,\leq)
together with an order-compatible topology
\tau
on
L
. We characterize the first Cantor–Bendixson level of the space
(L,\tau)
, namely
\mathcal{S}_{0}(L,\tau)\setminus\mathcal{S}_{1}(L,\tau)
, the set of isolated points.

More precisely, we show that isolation can be expressed purely in terms of the residual structure, using maximal subelements and residual derivatives, without direct reference to the topology. This establishes a concrete link between the Cantor–Bendixson stratification and the order-theoretic decomposition developed in the previous sections.

As an application, we recover Theorem
A
of
[
8
]
for
(\mathcal{H}^{d},\leq)
equipped with
\tau^{d}_{\mathcal{H}}
(Theorem
5.7
). The characterization of the second level
\mathcal{S}_{1}(L,\tau)\setminus\mathcal{S}_{2}(L,\tau)
is carried out in the next section.

Remark 5.1
.

Notice that there is one subtle difference between the framework introduced in the present text and the one of
[
8
]
: here the empty set is considered to be a shift, which implies that minimal shifts have a unique maximal subsystem - while
[
8
]
they are considered to have no subsystem, hence no maximal subsystem. The reason is that while generalizing the results to upper topological lattices, this appeared much more natural, and actually simplifies definitions and some results. Also notice that
(\mathcal{H}^{d},\tau_{\mathcal{H}}^{d})
is a T1 space, which means that the Cantor-Bendixson structure is identical to its residual structure.

Proposition 5.2
.

Every isolated element of
(L,\tau)
is
dually compact.

Proof.

Let us consider any
x\in L
which is not
dually compact. Since
(L,\leq)
is a dual algebraic lattice, there is a non-increasing net
\boldsymbol{x}:D\rightarrow\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
such that
\bigwedge_{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{d}=x
. Since
\tau
is order-compatible, we have
\boldsymbol{x}_{d}\rightarrow x
. Since
x
is not dually compact, for all
d\in D
, we have
\boldsymbol{x}_{d}>x
. This implies that
x
is not isolated.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

If
x\in L
is dually compact,
x
has no outcast and
\mathcal{M}(x)
is finite, then
x
is isolated.

Proof.

Since
x
is dually compact,
\mathop{\downarrow}x
is open. Thus if
x
were not isolated, there would exist a net
\boldsymbol{x}:D\rightarrow L
converging to
x
and such that
\boldsymbol{x}_{d}<x
for all
d\in D
. Since
x
has not outcast and
\mathcal{M}(x)
is finite, without loss of generality we can assume that there exists
m\in\mathcal{M}(x)
such that
\boldsymbol{x}_{d}\leq m
for all
d\in D
. We thus have

\boldsymbol{x}_{d}\vee m=m
.
By continuity of
\vee
, we get
x\vee m=m
, which is impossible since
x\vee m=x
and
m<x
.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

Assume that
(H,\leq)
is an upper semilattice. For all

Proof.

Let us assume that

The elements

,
where

Proposition 5.5
.

Assume that

Proof.

We can assume it to be minimal. We claim that

6
Characterization of
\mathcal{S}_{1}(L,\tau)\setminus\mathcal{S}_{2}(L,\tau)

We extend the previous characterization to the second layer of the Cantor–Bendixson stratification. The analysis reveals additional structural constraints, expressed in terms of the interaction between the residual derivative and local configurations of maximal subelements. This further clarifies how higher-order isolation phenomena are encoded in the lattice structure.

A subset
S
of a poset
(L,\leq)
is said to be
convex
when for all
x,z\in S
and
y\in L
such that
x\leq y\leq z
, we have
y\in S
.
A topology
\tau
on
L
is said to be
locally convex
when it has a base of convex sets.

Lemma 6.1
.

The dual Lawson topology is locally convex.

Proof.

It is sufficient to see that for all
x
, the sets
\mathop{\downarrow}x
and
(\mathop{\downarrow}x)^{c}
are convex, which is a consequence of transitivity of the relation
\leq
.
∎

The topology
\tau_{\mathcal{H}}^{d}
is also clearly locally convex, as the open balls form a base of convex sets.

In this section, we assume that the topology
\tau
is locally convex, and fix
\mathcal{O}
a base of convex sets of
\tau
.

6.1
Locally constant points of the core operator
c

Lemma 6.2
.

For all
z\in L
, the pair
(\mathcal{T}_{0}(L){\cap(\mathop{\downarrow}z)},\leq)
is a complete upper semilattice.

Proof.

It is sufficient to see that for all
S\subset\mathcal{T}_{0}(L,\leq){\cap(\mathop{\downarrow}z)}
,
\bigvee S\in\mathcal{T}_{0}(L,\leq)
. Fix
S\subset\mathcal{T}_{0}(L,\leq){\cap(\mathop{\downarrow}z)}
, and
x<\bigvee S
.
We can assume that for all
S^{\prime}\subsetneq S
,
\bigvee S^{\prime}<\bigvee S
. Since
L
is distributive,
x=\bigvee_{s\in S}(x\wedge s)
. If we had
(x\wedge s)=s
for all
s\in S
, we would have
x=\bigvee S
. Therefore, there is some
s\in S
such that

x\wedge s<s
. Since
s\in\mathcal{T}_{0}(L,\leq)
, there is some
z
such that
x\wedge s<z<s
. Then
x<x\vee z<\bigvee S
. Indeed, if we had
x=x\vee z
, we would have
z\leq x
and thus
z=z\wedge s\leq x\wedge s
, which is false. If we had
x\vee z=\bigvee S
, we would have
(x\vee z)\wedge s=s
, and thus
z=(x\wedge s)\vee(z\wedge s)=s
, which is false.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

For all
x\in L
, we have:

\texttt{c}(x)=\bigvee_{z\in\mathcal{T}_{0}(L,\leq)\cap(\mathop{\downarrow}x)}z.

Proof.

Since
\texttt{c}(x)\in\mathcal{T}_{0}(L,\leq)\cap(\mathop{\downarrow}x)
, we have
\texttt{c}(x)\leq\bigvee_{z\in\mathcal{T}_{0}(L,\leq)\cap(\mathop{\downarrow}x)}z
.
We have to prove that for all
z\in\mathcal{T}_{0}(L,\leq)\cap(\mathop{\downarrow}x)
,
z\leq\texttt{c}(x)
. We do this by proving, using transfinite induction, that
P(\alpha)
: for all
\alpha<\texttt{r}(x)
, we have
z\leq x^{(\alpha)}
for all
z\in\mathcal{T}_{0}(x)
. The base case
P(0)
is trivial. Fix
\alpha<\texttt{r}(X)
such that
P(\alpha)
holds and
\alpha+1<\texttt{r}(X)
. As a consequence of
P(\alpha)
, in order to prove
P(\alpha+1)
it is sufficient to prove that every maximal element of
(\mathop{\downarrow}x^{(\alpha)})\setminus\{x^{(\alpha)}\}
contains every element of
\mathcal{T}_{0}(L,\leq)\cap(\mathop{\downarrow}x^{(\alpha)})
. Assume that it is not the case, and fix
z\in\mathcal{T}_{0}(x^{(\alpha)})
and
m
a maximal element of
(\mathop{\downarrow}x^{(\alpha)})\setminus\{x^{(\alpha)}\}
such that
z\not\leq m
. In particular,

z\wedge m<z
. Since
z\in\mathcal{T}_{0}(x^{(\alpha)})
, there exists
z^{\prime}<z
such that
z\wedge m<z^{\prime}
.
This implies that
m<m\vee z^{\prime}<x^{(\alpha)}
, which is impossible, since
m
is a maximal subelement of
x^{(\alpha)}
. Indeed, if we had
m=m\vee z^{\prime}
, we would have
z^{\prime}\leq m
and thus, since
z^{\prime}\leq z
,
z^{\prime}\leq z\wedge m
, which is not true; if we had
x^{(\alpha)}=m\vee z^{\prime}
, we would have
z=(m\wedge z)\vee(z\wedge z^{\prime})\leq z^{\prime}
, which is not true.
We have now proved
P(\alpha+1)
. Consider a limit ordinal
\lambda<\texttt{r}(X)
, and assume that
P(\beta)
holds for all
\beta<\lambda
. Then
P(\lambda)
holds. This comes directly from the fact that
x^{(\lambda)}=\bigwedge_{\beta<\lambda}x^{(\beta)}
.
∎

Remark 6.4

Lemma 6.5
.

For all

such that
y\leq x\vee z
and

By distributivity of

would be a maximal subelement of

. Symmetrically, we obtain
y=\texttt{c}(x\wedge y)\vee\texttt{c}(z\wedge y)
.
∎

As a consequence:

Lemma 6.6

For all

\texttt{c}(x\vee z)=\texttt{c}(x)\vee\texttt{c}(z)

\texttt{c}(x)\vee\texttt{c}(z)\leq\texttt{c}(x\vee z)

\texttt{c}(x\vee z)\leq\texttt{c}(x)\vee\texttt{c}(z)

∎

1.

For all
z
dually compact in

such that
x<z
, the stratum of index
x
in
\delta(z)
is infinite

Otherwise,
x

Indeed, fix such

in
\delta(z)
is finite. Since

If

There exists a neighborhood

For all

7
Comments

The results obtained in this work show that the Cantor–Bendixson stratification, although defined topologically, can be reconstructed from purely order-theoretic structure in a broad class of structures. This suggests that similar correspondences may hold in other ordered settings. In particular, it raises the question of identifying minimal conditions under which a topological derivative can be represented by an intrinsic order-theoretic operator.

We formulate below some questions that have arisen during the course of this work, providing natural directions for further research.

Realization

The characterization of
\mathcal{S}_{1}(L,\tau)\setminus\mathcal{S}_{2}(L,\tau)
reveals that this set can be
divided into elements are isolated from above, ones that are isolated from below and others. A natural question is whether each of them is non-trivial, in particular for our motivating example:

Question 7.1
.

We know that there exist elements of
\mathcal{S}_{1}(\mathcal{H}^{d},\tau_{\mathcal{H}}^{d})\setminus\mathcal{S}_{2}(\mathcal{H},\tau_{\mathcal{H}}^{d})
which are isolated from above, as they are dually compact (ie shifts of finite type). On the other hand, does there exist a non-finite-type shift (non isolated from above) in
\mathcal{S}_{1}(\mathcal{H}^{d},\tau_{\mathcal{H}}^{d})\setminus\mathcal{S}_{2}(\mathcal{H},\tau_{\mathcal{H}}^{d})
?

More generally, one may ask whether elements in this family can exhibit isolation from below, and how such properties can be detected in terms of residual structure.

Special subsets

The objects introduced in this work - such as the core
\texttt{c}(x)
and the boundary
\partial x
- define natural subfamilies of elements. For instance, one may consider elements with trivial core, or those for which the decomposition
x=\texttt{c}(x)\vee\partial x
is degenerate in a prescribed way.
A systematic study of these subfamilies could refine the understanding of the topological structure of the lattice, in a similar way as characterizing lattices in which certain of these subsets are empty.

Collapse of Cantor–Bendixson hierarchy

The interaction between residual and Cantor–Bendixson derivatives raises the question of how Cantor–Bendixson levels can degenerate. Having characterized the first levels, it is natural to ask when some of them collapse entirely. For instance:

Question 7.2
.

Which dual algebraic coframes satisfy
\mathcal{T}_{0}(L,\leq)={\varepsilon}
?

In such cases, non-isolated elements coincide with
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}(L)
, and under additional assumptions (see below), the Cantor–Bendixson rank is 0 or 1. These extremal cases provide simple models in which the relationship between residual structure and Cantor–Bendixson decomposition becomes particularly transparent.

Perfect kernel

When
\mathcal{K}(L,\leq)
is countable, the topology becomes first countable, and one obtains partial information over the perfect kernel. In particular,
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}(L,\leq)\subset\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{r}(L,\tau)}(L,\tau)
. It would be of interest to determine to what extent this relation depends on the countability assumption.

Another natural research direction consists in interpreting existing results computing the Cantor-Bendixson rank of algebraic structures, such as the Grigorchuk group

19
]
in terms of our framework. Furthermore, it would be interesting to see how the Cantor-Bendixson structure varies depending on the order on the same set. For instance, in the hyperspace of shifts, Nathalie Aubrun and Mathieu Sablik
[

]
defined an order different from the inclusion, based on the algorithmic enumerability of the language.
