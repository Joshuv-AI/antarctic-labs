Singapore First Aid Guidelines 2026
Authors: Andrew Fu Wah Ho, Janice Hui Hong Oh, Jen Heng Pek, Jun Hao Loke, Swee Han Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
First aid is the immediate care provided for acute illness or injury and can be initiated by anyone in any situation. Its aims are the preservation of life, alleviation of suffering, prevention of further harm and promotion of recovery. To reflect evolving medical science surrounding first aid, the standards of care by first aiders must also evolve accordingly. The Singapore Resuscitation and First Aid Council has updated the national practice standards with the publication of the Singapore First Aid Guidelines 2026. The current guidelines incorporate the latest international evidence synthesis while prioritising relevance for Singapore’s first aid training and operational context. The existing guidelines remain relevant unless specifically updated. The main additions and significant revisions in this edition include guidance on pulse oximeters, bronchodilators, supplementary oxygen, repeated doses of adrenaline in anaphylaxis, hypoglycaemia correction, physical counterpressure manoeuvres for presyncope, handling of amputated body parts, rehydration for exertional-related dehydration and treatment of jellyfish stings.

INTRODUCTION

The Singapore Resuscitation and First Aid Council (SRFAC) remains steadfast in its vision of cultivating a nation in which every Singaporean is a first aider, equipped with the confidence and competence to intervene during medical emergencies. First aid is the immediate help and support provided to a person suffering from acute illness or injury. It can be initiated in any situation by anyone, from an untrained bystander to a trained first aider, healthcare professional or even the affected person. Its purpose is to preserve life, alleviate suffering, limit further harm and promote recovery. First aid scenarios vary widely, ranging from minor illnesses and injuries to immediately life-threatening conditions such as heart attack, stroke or major trauma. As the medical science surrounding first aid evolves, so must the standards of care delivered by first aid providers. In line with the 5-year review cycle, the Singapore First Aid Guidelines 2026, developed by SRFAC, represent the latest update to national practice standards, which were last updated in 2021. Existing guidelines remain relevant unless specifically updated. The guidelines aim to provide clear, practical and evidence-informed recommendations for first aid scenarios in residential, workplace and recreational settings. Key topics that are new, substantially revised, or have significant new guidance include pulse oximeters, bronchodilators, supplementary oxygen, repeated doses of adrenaline in anaphylaxis, how to correct hypoglycaemia, physical counterpressure manoeuvres for presyncope, handling of amputated body parts, rehydration for exertional-related dehydration and treatment of jellyfish stings.

Scientific basis

This update is grounded in the 2025 International Consensus on Science with Treatment Recommendations (CoSTR) published by the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR).[1] The ILCOR conducts continuous reviews of new, peer-reviewed published first aid science and publishes a comprehensive review every 5 years, including the 2025 review. The SRFAC First Aid Committee has reviewed the 2025 CoSTR alongside the 2024 Focused Update for First Aid co-released by the American Heart Association and American Red Cross,[2] the 2025 European Resuscitation Council Guidelines,[3] and guidelines from other learned societies and primary literature. The recommendations contained herein have been contextualised for relevance to Singapore’s epidemiology, urban environment, tropical climate and Emergency Medical Services (EMS) operating model (e.g. relatively short response times).

Local context

Singapore’s first aid landscape is set in a densely populated urban environment with a rapidly ageing population. The last nationwide survey by the Health Promotion Board in 2010 revealed that 34% of Singaporeans had been trained in first aid, while only 10% possessed a valid certificate.[4] The main provider of emergency ambulance services is the Singapore Civil Defence Force (SCDF), which is accessible to the public by a single ‘995’ dispatch number.[5] The SCDF aims to dispatch ambulances to an incident location within 11 min for 80% of the calls. In general, the SCDF conveys cases to the nearest of the 11 public emergency departments for further treatment.[6] First aid training is provided by non-profit organisations (e.g. Singapore Heart Foundation, Singapore Red Cross, St John Singapore) and private providers. In 2025, more than 46,000 individuals were certified by around 50 SRFAC-accredited first aid training centres. The main courses include the Standard First Aid Course and the Child First Aid Course, both of which fall under the purview of SRFAC, whereas the Occupational First Aid Course falls under the purview of the Workplace Safety and Health Council. These courses provide learners with a certificate that remains valid for 2 years.

KEY PRINCIPLES

General principles

First aiders vary in age, physical ability, level of acquired and retained skill, and willingness and ability to act. They should provide care within their skill and knowledge set, seek further medical care (e.g. activating the EMS by calling ‘995’) without delay as needed and remain mindful of their own safety. Every member of society, regardless of background, is encouraged to improve and empower their response through training and preparation, including maintaining a first aid kit.

Safety

The need for first aiders to assess the scene for hazards and take actions to protect themselves cannot be understated. Some settings, such as the scene of a building fire, may be dynamic and require the first aider to maintain situational awareness to address evolving hazards. An injured first aider is of no help to a casualty. Examples of scene hazards include slippery, wet or contaminated surfaces; exposed electrical circuits; road traffic accidents where the road is still open to traffic; or objects falling from height. Occasionally, the situation may allow the casualty to be moved to a safe location before first aid is provided. This should not be attempted if the movement could worsen the condition of the casualty. However, if there are hazards that cannot be mitigated, the first aider should remain at a safe distance while concurrently activating the EMS. Infection control principles are as relevant for first aiders as they are for healthcare providers. As it may not be immediately apparent that the casualty is suffering from an infectious condition, universal precautions should be taken at a minimum, with proper personal protective equipment used whenever possible. Additionally, first aiders should follow the following precautions whenever practicable: (a) wash hands with water and soap (or alcohol-based hand sanitiser if hands are not visibly soiled) before and after attending to the casualty; (b) wear gloves, face mask, eye protection and/or full gown if there is a potential risk of contact with bodily fluids; and (c) safely dispose of all contaminated items after use.

First aid kit

All workplaces, leisure centres, public buildings, homes and cars should have first aid kits. As first aid kits may be tailored according to individual or organisational needs based on the setting, expected risks and users, their contents may vary. First aid kits in workplaces should meet local legal requirements where applicable. The kits should be clearly marked, readily accessible, mobile (i.e. not locked up or fixed to a wall), and inspected regularly to ensure that they are properly equipped and all items are usable.

The list of common basic items recommended for inclusion in a first aid kit is unchanged from the previous guidelines (refer to Figure 1 of Zarisfi et al.[7]). These include: 20 mL of sterile water or saline in disposable vials, adhesive dressings (woven, non-woven or transparent), absorbent gauze, eye pads, crepe bandage (5 cm, 10 cm and 15 cm), triangular bandages, hypoallergenic tape, safety pins, tweezers, scissors, pen-light, antiseptic wipes and disposable gloves. Additional items, such as eye shields, splints, digital thermometer, resuscitation mask or face shield, commercial tourniquets, haemostatic and burns dressings, digital blood pressure monitor, pulse oximeter and glucose meter, may be included depending on the intended use for specialised first aid kits. However, additional training must be available if the first aider is expected to use them.

(b) Leg crossing with tensing of the leg, abdominal and buttock muscles while lying down or, if necessary, while standing. As pulse oximeters were distributed for home use during the COVID-19 pandemic[8] and are increasingly included in smartwatches, contemporary guidelines have considered their roles in first aid. First aiders who use pulse oximeters for the assessment of acute illness or injury should be proficient in their use and understand their many limitations, including equipment factors, environmental considerations and patient-specific factors that may produce inaccurate and unreliable readings.[1910] Importantly, the use of a pulse oximeter for first aid assessment should not supersede or replace physical assessment.[1]

FIRST AID FOR MEDICAL EMERGENCIES

Unresponsiveness

If a casualty is unresponsive and not breathing normally, the first aider should first strongly consider whether the person is in cardiac arrest.[11] In such cases, the casualty should be assessed using a Danger, Response, Send for Help, Airway, Breathing, and Compressions/Circulation approach in accordance with the Singapore Basic Cardiac Life Support and Automated External Defibrillation (BCLS+AED) guidelines.[12] Syncope, a transient loss of consciousness, results from global cerebral hypoperfusion. There are many causes of syncope. It may be preceded by presyncope, a prodrome that lasts a few seconds and may include lightheadedness or dizziness, blurred vision, nausea, a sensation of warmth, and signs such as diaphoresis and pallor. As physical injuries commonly occur as the casualty loses consciousness,[13] the first aider should help the casualty assume a safe position, such as assisted sitting or lying down. Once the casualty with presyncope is in a safe position, performing physical counterpressure manoeuvres (PCMs)[1] to prevent syncope can be beneficial.[1415] Figure 1 shows examples of upper-body and lower-body PCMs. The PCMs are postulated to work through vascular compartment compression, causing an increase in systemic vascular resistance and thereby increasing blood pressure.[16]

Breathing difficulties

Many medical conditions can result in breathing difficulties. These include asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and heart failure. Agonal breathing or gasping is common in the initial minutes of sudden cardiac arrest, and should be treated in accordance with the Singapore BCLS+AED guidelines.[12] It is unknown whether bronchodilators will cause harm when administered to individuals with undifferentiated respiratory symptoms.[1] If an individual with known asthma is having difficulty breathing, a trained first aider may help the individual use the prescribed bronchodilator—whether via an inhaler (with or without a spacer) or a nebuliser.[1] We do not recommend the routine administration of supplementary oxygen in first aid scenarios by first aiders, considering the lack of evidence supporting its routine use and the potential harm in some scenarios. In particular, it is potentially harmful for first aiders to administer supplementary oxygen to an individual with known COPD to an oxygen saturation of >92% (see previous discussion on pulse oximeters).[17]

Anaphylaxis

A small proportion of allergic reactions can progress to anaphylaxis, a severe, life-threatening form of allergic reaction. It can be triggered by exposures to food, drugs and insect venom. Signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis include lip and facial swelling, a sensation of the throat closing, difficulty breathing, rash and dizziness. The presence of this condition may be recognised by a casualty who has experienced anaphylaxis before. First aiders should not be expected to recognise the signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis without repeated episodes of training and encounters with persons with anaphylaxis.[1] There is currently insufficient evidence to support educational approaches for training lay providers to recognise anaphylaxis, although this may be reviewed in the future.[118] The mainstay of immediate treatment for anaphylaxis is prompt intramuscular adrenaline, which may be administered in the first aid setting with an adrenaline auto-injector.[19] Persons at risk, such as those with previous anaphylaxis, may carry their own prescribed adrenaline auto-injector for self-administration. A first aider should assist a person experiencing anaphylaxis in using the auto-injector if assistance is required.[119] The EMS should be activated for all cases of suspected anaphylaxis, regardless of the use of an auto-injector. The auto-injector is injected into the muscle of the lateral thigh. If a person with anaphylaxis does not respond to the initial auto-injector and the arrival of EMS is expected to exceed 10 min, a repeat dose may be considered.[2021]

Chest pain

Many medical conditions can present with chest pain, ranging from self-limiting conditions to those that are imminently life-threatening. Differentiating them is often difficult.[22] In adults with acute, severe chest pain, the most important action by the first aider is to activate the EMS. The transfer of casualties with acute, severe chest pain to a healthcare facility should be performed by the EMS and not via private transport, as it substantially reduces reperfusion delay and enables EMS treatment of complications such as cardiac arrest.[23] We recommend administering 300 mg of aspirin to casualties with chest pain that is likely to be cardiac in nature. It is often challenging to differentiate between cardiac and non-cardiac chest pain. Although early administration of aspirin reduces mortality in those with acute myocardial infarction (AMI),[24] we recommend leaving the decision to administer aspirin to the EMS or physician if the cause of chest pain is uncertain. This approach is recommended due to Singapore’s relatively short EMS response times and the potential harm of aspirin to patients without AMI or who have contraindications.

Hypoglycaemia

Hypoglycaemia occurs due to a low blood glucose level, which may manifest as tremors, diaphoresis and confusion. Persons with diabetes mellitus are at risk of hypoglycaemia, and if they exhibit these symptoms, hypoglycaemia should be presumed. The EMS should be activated immediately if the casualty is unconscious, seizing or is unable to follow simple commands or swallow safely. If the casualty is able to follow simple commands and swallow safely, the first aider should encourage the person to swallow glucose tablets (first choice). Other dietary sugars (e.g. small candies, sugar cubes, jelly beans, orange juice) can also be used effectively to resolve hypoglycaemia.[125] We recommend oral glucose administration over buccal glucose administration for individuals with suspected hypoglycaemia who are conscious and able to swallow.[126] We also recommend the use of sublingual glucose administration for suspected hypoglycaemia in children who may be uncooperative with the oral (swallowed) glucose administration route.[12627] The first aider should monitor for resolution of symptoms, which should occur within 10–15 min if they are caused by hypoglycaemia. The EMS should be activated if there is deterioration or persistence of symptoms.

Seizure

Seizures can be caused by epilepsy or other medical conditions. They may be generalised tonic–clonic seizures, which manifest as full-body rhythmic jerking with altered consciousness, although other forms exist. A small number of casualties with cardiac arrest may initially develop anoxic seizures, which should be treated in accordance with the Singapore BCLS+AED guidelines.[12] The first aider’s priorities are to ensure airway patency and to prevent injury. The casualty should be helped to the ground and placed in a left lateral position and the area around them cleared. We recommend against restraining the casualty or placing any object in the casualty’s mouth, as these may lead to injury.[2829] Most seizures are self-limiting and resolve spontaneously within 2 min. Seizures lasting longer than 5 min constitute a medical emergency requiring urgent medical treatment.[30]

Stroke

Since the last local guidelines update, there has been an extensive accumulation of scientific evidence surrounding the hyperacute treatment of stroke, which increases the emphasis on early recognition, conveyance to a comprehensive stroke centre and early hyperacute stroke treatment.[3132] The first aider has a crucial role in early recognition and activation of the EMS. Activation of the EMS is important because of the presence of diversion protocols to bring suitable patients to hospitals that can provide comprehensive stroke treatment at that point in time, thereby reducing treatment delay.[32] We recommend that first aiders use stroke assessment tools for adults with suspected acute stroke.[133] Specifically, among several available tools, we suggest the use of the Face, Arms, Speech, Time (FAST) tool for stroke assessment [Figure 2], as it is simple to use and has high sensitivity for the identification of stroke.[33] If a glucose meter and trained provider are available, it is reasonable to exclude hypoglycaemia, as it commonly mimics stroke symptoms; however, this should not delay EMS activation. As an arbitrary guide, if blood sugar level is below 3.0 mmol/L, it is possible that hypoglycaemia is causing the stroke-like symptoms, and the casualty may be initially treated according to the recommendations for hypoglycaemia.

FIRST AID FOR TRAUMA EMERGENCIES

Amputated or avulsed body parts

Following a traumatic amputation or avulsion, the priority is to care for the patient, including control of life-threatening bleeding (see 2021 first aid guidelines section on ‘bleeding’, which remains relevant[7]). However, care of the amputated or avulsed part is sometimes overlooked or delayed.[1] Successful replantation is time-dependent; completely amputated and avulsed external body parts, such as fingers, hands, arms and legs, should be retrieved and transported as soon as possible, preferably to the same healthcare facility as the injured person.[1] Replantation outcomes may be improved by cooling the amputated or avulsed part without freezing as soon as possible and transportation to a healthcare facility. If feasible, this can be accomplished by wrapping the part in a moist, clean cloth or gauze and sealing it in a watertight bag or container, before cooling it in ice or an ice–water mix.[13435]

FIRST AID FOR ENVIRONMENTAL AND TOXICOLOGIC EMERGENCIES

Exertion-related dehydration

Singapore’s tropical climate increases the risk of exertion-related dehydration and heat disorders,[36] especially during sporting events in unacclimatised athletes.[37] During prolonged exercise, sweat losses generally exceed fluid intake, and even low levels of dehydration can lead to impaired physical and cognitive performance. In such situations, it is of utmost importance to promote drinking fluids post-exercise to restore fluid balance.[38] In the absence of shock, confusion or inability to swallow, first aiders should assist or encourage individuals with exertion-related dehydration to orally rehydrate with any available rehydration drink or potable water.[13940] We suggest rehydration for exertion-related dehydration with a 4%–9% carbohydrate–electrolyte drink.[13940] Alternative rehydration options include 0%–3.9% carbohydrate–electrolyte drinks, water, coconut water or low-fat cow’s milk.[13940] Previous guidelines on heat disorders remain relevant (see 2021 guidelines section on ‘heat disorders’).[7]

Jellyfish stings

Jellyfish are found in local coastal waters. Jellyfish stings can cause pain, and some species—particularly the box jellyfish, which has been sighted along the southern Singapore coast in recent years—may cause life-threatening systemic envenomation. There is limited high-quality evidence to guide the treatment of jellyfish envenomation,[4142] and treatment effectiveness seems to be species-dependent, limiting the applicability of some evidence. Following a jellyfish sting, we suggest treating pain with the use of heated water at 40–45°C (immersion, irrigation or shower) or hot pack application.[1] Embedded tentacular material may be removed carefully with forceps. With the use of heated water, the risks of secondary burns are real, and care should be taken to avoid scalding by avoiding excess temperature or duration. For every 30 min of immersion, the affected limb should be rested and elevated for 30 min. Where heated water is not available, vinegar (4%–6% acetic solution) may be used to wash the body part liberally for at least 30 s.[414243] If the casualty is unwell or has persistent pain beyond the second cycle of warm water immersion, medical attention should be sought. For bites and stings other than jellyfish, previous guidelines remain relevant (see 2021 guidelines section on ‘bites and stings’).[7]

CONCLUSION

The Singapore First Aid Guidelines 2026 provide practical and evidence-informed recommendations for common first aid scenarios contextualised to Singapore. There are evidence gaps in various aspects of first aid, and periodic guideline updates would enable local first aid standards to remain grounded in scientific evidence and best practices.
