1 Executive Summary
Rigorous stakeholder-vetted techno-economic analysis (TEA) was performed to assess the cost of
hydrogen (H2) produced using state-of-the-art Proton-Conducting Solid Oxide (PSO) electrolysis. The
baseline PSO plant assumes that electricity, water, and air are the only system inputs. Projected high-
volume, untaxed and unsubsidized levelized cost of hydrogen (LCOH)1 ranges from 2020 $1.81 to
$2.47/kg H2 depending on technology year, plant design, and project scale, assuming an electricity price
of $0.03/kWh and a capacity factor of 97% (Table 1).2 These LCOH estimates reflect techno-economic
performance assuming consistent supply chain maturity, stack manufacturing scale-up, and Nth-of-a-
kind (NOAK) engineering, procurement, and construction (EPC) cost structures are in place by the
nominal technology year. The analysis is intended to isolate and evaluate the cost potential of PSO
electrolyzer technology, independent of deployment or commercialization timelines. Depending on the
progress of research and development for PSO electrolyzer systems, commercialization of First-of-a-Kind
(FOAK) “Current” PSO electrolyzer technology will likely occur 2030-2035 with FOAK “Future” PSO
electrolyzer technology coming to market around 2035-2045.

2 Analysis Summary
This report documents the projected LCOH for H2 production from Proton-Conducting Solid Oxide (PSO)
electrolyzers based on techno-economic analysis of current and future technology. Results for the LCOH
represent untaxed and unsubsidized costs associated solely with H 2 production and compression to 30
bar (delivery, storage, and dispensing not included). Tax credits are not considered in this analysis. While
technoeconomic performance is assessed for technology years 2025 and 2035, the actual construction
and commissioning of commercial-scale PSO plants will likely occur later, depending on the pace of
research and development, pilot demonstration, and supply chain development.

This study focused on centralized PSO plants with production corresponding to nominal 100 MW to
1 GW input power. For a PSO plant, the 100 MW base case was sized to deliver 50,000 kg/day (50 MTD)
of H2, whereas a 1 GW plant was sized to deliver 500,000 kg/day (500 MTD). The LCOH was evaluated in
this report for two technology cases: Current (2025) plant and Future (2035) plant H2A cases. The
Current (2025) Technology Case is associated with a PSO stack using technology currently demonstrated
on a lab scale with cost estimates assuming a manufacturing scale of 1 GW/year. The Future (2035)
Technology Case is associated with a PSO stack projected to be available in the lab by 2035 in terms of
catalyst materials, construction, and performance. This case also assumes a manufacturing scale of
1 GW/year. The base case process design for the PSO plant assumes electricity, water, and ambient air
are the only feed system inputs.

2.1 Plant Definition
The project cost and LCOH were modelled for a PSO plant assuming electricity is the only energy input,
including boilers and heaters. The project cost includes both the installed direct costs for the hydrogen
plant and the overhead costs associated with developing and deploying the plant. Each hydrogen plant
is assumed to be divided into one or more separate and standalone H2 production systems, supported
by common high-voltage infrastructure that supports the whole plant. Each system is assumed to be a
fully independent electrolysis process train, including water supply and purification, electrolysis stacks
including electrical energy supply and conditioning, heat management, and H 2 processing. See Figure 1
for a visual representation of the plant terminology and the scope of supply included within this project
cost model.

plant definition for a PSO electrolysis system is shown in Table 2.

Module sizing is based on practical electrical limits dictated by commercially available power electronics.
Using ampacity guidelines from NFPA 70 for single conductors and assuming a standard 800 V
commercial rectifier, modules in the 50 MTD Current cases are limited to ~0.43 MW per module. For the
500 MTD Current case, the conductor count is tripled, enabling modules up to ~1.28 MW. The Future
cases assume a higher-power 1600 V rectifier, increasing module capacity to ~0.85 MW for the 50 MTD
case and ~2.55 MW for the 500 MTD case. Block sizing also scales with Technology Case: Current cases
target ~10 MW of hydrogen production per block, while Future cases target ~20 MW, balancing
electrical integration with process redundancy. Stack sizing also reflects achievable design constraints:
Current cases assume up to 200 cells per stack, consistent with existing commercial solid oxide stacks,
while Future cases assume up to 400 cells per stack, matching counts demonstrated in low-temperature
electrolyzer systems.

2.2 PSO Operating Point
Solid oxide electrolysis cells operate at an elevated temperature (300-900 °C) compared to conventional
low temperature electrolysis (60-100 °C), which allows for acceptable electrocatalytic activity without
using platinum group metal (PGM) catalysts and allows for sufficient ionic conductivity across the solid
electrolyte. The typical range of solid oxide electrolysis is 600-800 °C, which favors the reaction
thermodynamics and kinetics, achieving a high energy conversion efficiency. In addition, catalysts can
use readily available raw materials, such as Ni and abundant perovskite oxides, greatly reducing the
dependence on precious metals. Solid oxide electrolysis stacks are typically operated near the
thermoneutral voltage to achieve thermal balance between endothermic and resistive heating, which
allows stack operation at a near constant temperature while maintaining a faradaic efficiency near
100%. Conventional, oxygen-conducting solid oxide (OSO) stacks operate at a thermoneutral voltage of
~1.285 V at temperatures of 750-850 °C. Using a hydrogen electrode (cathode)-supported cell, fully
commercialized OSO stacks in the future (2030-2040) are expected to operate at 1.285 V/cell at 1.5
A/cm2 at beginning of life (BOL).9

Compared to OSO electrolysis cells, PSO electrolysis cells typically operate at an intermediate
temperature (400-600 °C). This is enabled by the lower activation energy associated with conducting
protons compared to conducting oxygen ions. The lower temperature and higher ion conductivity
possible with PSO stacks compared to OSO stacks has the potential to increase current density, increase
stack durability, and enable lower cost stack, piping, and equipment materials. Similarly to OSO, the
baseline thermoneutral voltage for a PSO stack operating at 600 °C is assumed to be 1.285 V/cell. The
rated, beginning of life current density at the thermoneutral voltage for the PSO Current case is
assumed to be 1.00 A/cm2. This gives the PSO stack approximately the same current density as current
commercially available OSO stacks at the same operating cell voltage.

depending on plant operating strategy, which considers the degradation of stack performance over
time. Many hydrogen applications require a constant supply of hydrogen as it is a feedstock for

An extensive literature and patent review was conducted, in addition to discussions with electrolyzer
suppliers on current OSO and PSO cell design, performance, and potential technology advances. Current
and Future cell designs, component material selections, and performance levels were postulated to
serve as the basis for cost estimation. In general, PSO stack designs share many similarities with OSO

stack designs and similar manufacturing methods were assumed. We expect PSO stack design to
continue to evolve as materials research explores avenues to improve performance and durability.

with Current stacks having a cell active area size of 100 cm2/cell (10 cm x 10 cm) and Future stacks

and manufacturing processes, relative to the total cell area including the inactive surface area, the

2.4 PSO Balance of Plant Design
The PSO electrolysis plant houses several subsystems designed to convert water into product grade
hydrogen, while efficiently recuperating as much thermal energy as possible. A simplified process flow
diagram for a PSO plant is shown in Figure 4. The general process is: 1) makeup water is purified and
used to cool down product hydrogen, 2) inlet water is boiled using an electric boiler, 3) steam mixed
with air is brought up to stack temperature through heat recuperation and an anode topping heater, 4)
steam is electrolytically converted into oxygen and hydrogen in a near-atmospheric (~1.2 bar) stack, 5)
oxygen-rich air and hydrogen gas are passed through several heat exchangers to recover as much
thermal energy as possible, 6) cooled hydrogen gas is compressed, and 7) the hydrogen is purified using
a Temperature Swing Adsorption (TSA) subsystem to remove saturated water from the H 2 gas stream.
Two TSA adsorption beds are used to provide continuous water removal, producing an H 2 product
stream with 99.99 mol% H2 purity at 30 bar. Water removed from the H2 stream is fed back into the
stack feed surge drum. Additional cases, such as a fired boiler, are considered in Sections 5 and 6.

3.2 PSO Balance of Plant Cost
The BOP can be divided into two broad categories: the mechanical BOP and the electrical BOP. The
mechanical BOP encompasses four primary elements: process equipment, piping, valves, and
instrumentation including temperature, pressure, flow, and level indicators. The cost model uses the
Aspen Process Economic AnalyzerTM for preliminary cost estimates of the process equipment. Piping and
valve costs are derived from Aspen Capital Cost EstimatorTM. Based on industry heuristics,
instrumentation is assumed to be 20% of installed equipment costs, and is inclusive of fire detection
systems, hydrogen detection systems, and any early warning systems required for hydrogen safety. 24 25

The electrical BOP consists of four primary elements: rectifier, transformer, power substation, and
electrical wiring. The cost model uses vendor quotes for the rectifier and an estimate from a 2013
engineering study for the high-voltage transformer.26 The electrical wiring is estimated from the 2020
National Electrical Estimator published by Craftsman.27 The power substation and overhead power lines
for external transmission are derived from publicly available price estimates.28 Where necessary, cost
values in this study are adjusted to 2020 $ using the Chemical Engineering Plant Cost Index (CEPCI), the
standard index used by chemical process industries.

4 Operating Cost
The operating costs for a PSO plant are composed of utility costs (electricity, process water, and cooling
water), non-electric thermal energy costs (natural gas combustion), maintenance costs (periodic
replacement of stacks, additional annual costs for all other maintenance activities), and operating labor
costs.

4.1 Utility Cost
The utilities for a PSO plant included in this study are electricity, process water, and cooling water.
Additional utilities, such as gases for instrumentation, are not included in this study. Previous LCOH
calculations performed using the H2A model used default values to define these utility costs. In
particular, the electricity was assumed to follow Annual Energy Outlook (AEO) cost projections for grid-
based industrial electricity prices.33,34 However, for this study and based on DOE guidance, a reduced
price of 2020 $0.03/kWh was assumed to represent a nominal low-cost electricity price currently
possible in specific favorable U.S. markets. Specifically, the baseline electricity price case ($0.03/kWh)
corresponds to average wholesale electricity prices currently possible in U.S. markets with plentiful

Water consumption is estimated at 3.78 gallons per kg H2 inclusive of water converted to H2 and O2 and
water lost from the system due to purging during water purification, O 2 gas venting, and as an impurity

water consumption may differ from that reported here. However, likely alternative values for water
consumption and price are not expected to appreciably change the LCOH.

While recuperative heat exchangers are used extensively to recover thermal energy throughout the
system, cooling water is required for operations where heat recovery is insufficient or impractical.
Specifically, cooling water is used to lower the temperature of the hydrogen product gas to near-
ambient conditions before purification and delivery, and to manage thermal loads between compression
stages where the heat of compression must be removed. A nominal cost of $0.00004/gal for cooling
water was assumed, consistent with default values from Aspen Process Economic Analyzer. This cost
reflects a closed-loop recirculating system using evaporative cooling via a cooling tower, with a typical
temperature rise from 25 °C to 35 °C across cooling operations. Since the cooling water is assumed to
operate in a closed loop, no losses are assumed in the process model.

Depending on safety and reliability requirements of the proton-conducting solid oxide stacks, inert
sweep gas may be needed for startup, shutdown, and emergency operations. Additional costs not
quantified in this study will be associated with these subsystems.

4.3 Maintenance Cost
Default H2A model values for the total unplanned replacement capital costs are used in the analysis and

associated with maintenance is captured under Labor Cost.

5.2 Sensitivity to Plant, System, and Module Sizing
The levelized cost of hydrogen is affected by the plant size and the size of the independent systems used
to build up to the plant capacity. Each case assessed in Figure 8 is associated with a plant size, system
size, and module size as described in the Plant Definition (Table 2). To understand the effect of plant
size, system size, and module size, the levelized cost of hydrogen was calculated for key increments
between the baseline 50 MTD case (which assumes 4 systems for the Current Technology Case and 2
systems for the Future Technology Case) and the baseline 500 MTD case (which assumes 3 systems).
Results are shown in Figure 9 for both the Current and Future technology cases. The largest cost
reduction for all sequences is the transition between the 50 MTD, 1 system case and the 167 MTD, 1
system cases where the system size increases from 50 MTD to 167 MTD. This creates an economy of
scale benefit that reduces the capital cost and the associated maintenance costs. This also significantly
reduces the labor cost since the same number of non-stack process equipment is assumed for the 1
system cases. Transitioning from 50 MTD, 4 system cases to 50 MTD, 1 system cases also increases the
system size from 12.5/25 MTD (Current/Future) to 50 MTD and also achieves a benefit from economy of
size scaling. However, the impact is limited since the total plant capacity does not increase. Finally,
transitioning from the 167 MTD, 1 system case to the 500 MTD, 3 system case involves replicating the
167 MTD system 3 times in a single plant which offers only modest LCOH savings associated with
increasing plant size, particularly a reduction in expected labor costs and reduction in overhead costs.

The final transition from the fourth case to the fifth case in each sequence is a tripling of the stacks per
module. The baseline Current 50 MTD case assumes 21 stacks per module with a stack input power per

module of 0.377 MW (BOL Rated), which increases to 66 stacks per module with a stack input power per
module of 1.13 MW (BOL Rated). The baseline Future 50 MTD case assumes 6 stacks per module with a

reduction in system footprint. Additional cost optimization may be possible through more detailed
layout and piping optimization and further refinement of number of blocks and modules.

5.4 Sensitivity to Electricity Pricing and Capacity Factor
Lower cost electricity sources such as wind and solar are associated with lower capacity factors, which
can increase the relative cost of hydrogen produced from a renewable energy integrated electrolyzer.
Understanding the relationship between electricity price, capacity factor, project cost, and levelized cost
of hydrogen may provide a roadmap for unlocking lower-cost hydrogen, especially in regions of high
renewable energy penetration. To bound the possible range of LCOH results for PSO electrolysis, the
LCOH was parametrically calculated for multiple electricity prices ($0.00/kWh to $0.10/kWh) and

 32

capacity factors (10% to 100%) for a 50 MTD plant (assuming 4 modules for the Current Technology Case
and 2 modules for the Future Technology Case).

Unlike low-temperature electrolysis technologies that can ramp up and down relatively quickly, high-
temperature electrolysis technologies require a significant amount of time to reach operating
temperature at startup and shutdown. Therefore, to take advantage of transient electricity sources and
lower capacity factor environments, solid oxide electrolyzer plants must remain at or near operating
temperatures, including blocks and module equipment. For this study, during stand-by operation when
no hydrogen is being produced, the proton-conducting solid oxide system is assumed to maintain near
steady-state temperature by operating all block equipment at 10% capacity with the stack at 0% power
consumption. The hydrogen compressor and temperature-swing adsorption system are shutdown
during stand-by operation. While the average plant electrical usage during normal operation is
unaffected by stand-by operation, the additional electrical usage incurred during stand-by operation
reduces the net electrical efficiency. Sensitivity results for LCOH are shown in Figure 11 for PSO. For
legibility, cases that exceed $10/kgH2 are omitted from the figures. Additional contour plots displaying
associated total installed capital cost, plant power, scaled plant cost, and average plant electrical usage
are shown in Appendix B.

The LCOH results suggest that a hydrogen price target of $2/kgH2 is achievable across a wide range of
capacity factors, as long as the net electricity price falls below $0.02/kWh. Assuming a common
hydrogen production target for all cases, lower capacity factors are associated with higher total project
costs (and total electrolyzer capacity), which will be a barrier to economic feasibility in the short term.
Lower hydrogen prices are only achievable with very low net electricity prices and high capacity factors,
which will likely require a combination of high renewable energy penetration, dynamic electrolyzer
operation, and smart integration with the grid, or, alternatively, with availability of low-cost nuclear
electricity.

6 PSO Plant Operation Using External Thermal Energy
6.1 LCOH Results for Plant Operation Using Natural Gas
One of the advantages of solid oxide electrolysis is that part of the energy used in the electrochemical
reaction may be supplied in the form of thermal energy. How, and at what temperature, this energy is
applied is of great practical importance. Consequently, we restate key aspects of the modeled system.
For proton-conducting solid oxide, the reactants are heated to 600 °C, which lowers the Nernst voltage
and activation overpotential required for the electrochemical reaction, in addition to reducing ohmic
resistance across the electrolyte. The PSO process model for this study assumes the process water boiler

 35

operates at 110 °C and the topping heaters are assumed to operate at 600 °C. A network of recuperative
heat exchangers is used to reduce the thermal energy load required for steady-state operation.

For the baseline cases in this study, external thermal energy is supplied from electric boilers and electric
topping heaters. The use of electrical energy is consistent with the desire for sustainable hydrogen,
which can be produced from electricity generated from renewable sources such as solar and wind or
nuclear. However, thermal energy can be more economically generated from the combustion of fossil
fuels, such as natural gas. In this scenario, the boiler and topping heaters can be operated using natural
gas. For near-term hydrogen production, where cost is the primary concern, natural gas-based solid
oxide systems will likely be more common than electric systems.

Similar to the all-electric proton-conducting solid oxide plant, a bottom-up cost model was developed
for a PSO plant that uses natural gas combustion to provide thermal energy input for process water
vaporization and topping heat. The adjusted plant definition is shown in Table 10. The capital cost for
the PSO plant with natural gas thermal energy input is assumed to be identical to the capital cost for an

7 Conclusions
7.1 Levelized Cost of Hydrogen Outlook
Techno-economic analysis indicates that proton-conducting solid oxide (PSO) electrolysis has the
potential to produce hydrogen at competitive long-term costs under favorable electricity pricing and
mature deployment conditions. For the all-electric baseline cases evaluated in this study, projected
untaxed and unsubsidized LCOH ranges from approximately 2020 $2.47/kg H₂ to $2.09/kg H₂ for the
Current Technology Case systems and $2.05/kg H₂ to $1.81/kg H₂ for the Future Technology Case
systems, assuming an electricity price of $0.03/kWh and a 97% capacity factor. These estimates reflect
Nth-of-a-kind plant assumptions, large-scale manufacturing of PSO stacks, and a mature supply chain,
and are intended to identify the long-term cost potential of PSO electrolysis rather than represent near-
term commercial pricing.

Realization of the lower end of this projected LCOH range depends on continued research and
development progress and successful pilot and early commercial-scale deployment over the coming
decade, with broad commercial availability occurring in the 2035-2045 timeframe. Achieving these cost
levels is primarily dependent on three factors:
  Access to low-cost, high-availability electricity remains the dominant driver of hydrogen
 production cost.
  Improvements in stack durability and lifetime, particularly reductions in degradation rate
 that extend replacement intervals, and materially reduce fixed operating costs and capital
 recovery.
  Manufacturing scale-up and system-level performance improvements, including higher
 current density operation, larger cell areas, and optimized module and system sizing, reduce
 both stack and balance-of-plant capital costs through economies of scale.
For context, current publicly reported estimates for cost of hydrogen production from renewables are
substantially higher than the long-term projections presented here. Recent industry analyses indicate
that cost of hydrogen production from renewables today commonly range anywhere from $4.5-
$12/kg48, reflecting early deployments, higher capital costs, and site-specific electricity pricing. In
addition, the LCOH values reported in this study are sensitive to project financial assumptions. Default
H2A financial parameters are used to enable consistent comparison with analogous hydrogen
production techno-economic analyses; however, these parameters should be adjusted to reflect local
financing conditions, ownership structure, and risk profile when evaluating specific projects.

7.2 Technology Performance Outlook
Proton-Conducting Solid Oxide (PSO) electrolyzers offer several performance advantages over both
oxygen-conducting solid oxide (OSO) electrolyzers and low-temperature electrolyzers. Their
intermediate operating temperature (500–600 °C) enables higher conversion efficiency than low-
temperature electrolysis systems while providing multiple theoretical performance advantages over

conventional high-temperature OSO electrolyzers. Operation in this moderate temperature range also
permits use of lower-cost materials, faster thermal cycling, and improved start-up behavior. These
attributes enhance compatibility with variable renewable energy sources and support more flexible
plant operation compared to traditional OSO electrolysis systems.

Continued advances in polarization performance are expected, both in increased power density at a
given cell voltage, and in similar polarization performance at a lower temperature. Operation at a lower
temperature (450 to 550°C) is expected to significantly increase stack lifetime while enabling lower cost
metals within the stack.

Future PSO stacks may be able to generate fully dry hydrogen without any loss in performance or stack
lifetime. While this capability has yet to be commercially demonstrated, this would provide a significant
advantage over other electrolyzer technologies because it could simplify process design and remove the
need for a hydrogen purification subsystem. Future studies can explore the cost reduction potential of
this capability.

Additionally, further reduction in stack operating temperature opens up the opportunities for thermal
integration with other processes. The thermally integrated process design allows for partial heat
recycling, and future systems could incorporate more advanced thermal management or waste heat
valorization. These functional benefits are not quantified in this analysis but may offer additional value.

7.3 Technology Deployment Outlook
While the modeled costs assume Nth-of-a-kind capital deployment, scaled manufacturing capacity, and
mature supply chains, these conditions are not yet in place for PSO systems. The actual commercial
deployment of PSO systems at industrial scale will lag the technology years presented (2025 and 2035)
due to the time required for continued R&D, pilot demonstration, supply chain buildup, and regulatory
readiness. Based on the current status of PSO research and engineering, the earliest plausible
deployment of first-of-a-kind commercial-scale PSO plants is projected to occur between 2030–2035.
The results presented in this study should be interpreted as indicative of long-term technical cost
potential, not near-term deployment feasibility.

Additional capital cost savings may be achievable if PSO systems are deployed as pre-fabricated modular
units.49 In particular, the “blocks” described in Figure 4 could be candidates for modularization,
potentially reducing engineering, design, procurement, and on-site construction costs. Realizing these
savings would require further assumptions about deployment scale, fabrication standardization, and
labor market-specific cost reductions.
