 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report summarizes FY26 work at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) under the US Department
of Energy’s Office of Nuclear Energy Advanced Fuels Campaign Next Generation Fuels TRISO effort to
develop next-generation coated particle fuel fabrication methods that involve a nonhalogen route for ZrC
deposition. The effort was motivated by growing interest in advanced coated particle fuel architectures
particularly for microreactors and high-temperature gas-cooled reactors. In these applications, ZrC may
provide benefits for high-temperature performance and fission product retention relative to conventional
coating materials. To avoid the hydrochloric acid by-products associated with halogen-based precursors,
ORNL researchers evaluated zirconium acetylacetonate (ZrAcAc) as a metalorganic precursor for ZrC
deposition in a fluidized bed chemical vapor deposition system using tungsten carbide surrogate particles.
Thermal analysis revealed an effective evaporation window for ZrAcAc between about 180°C and 245°C,
and the work showed that successful precursor delivery required heating of the transfer lines to prevent
condensation on cold surfaces. Even with these improvements, only about 36%–39% of the precursor
mass was effectively volatilized and transported, indicating limited precursor utilization. Coating trials
further showed that deposition temperature was critical: low-temperature runs produced nonuniform or
incomplete coatings, whereas deposition at 1200°C yielded continuous coatings approximately 5 µm
thick. However, the resulting coatings were highly carbon rich and contained only limited zirconium.
Alternative introduction of the precursor through the chlorinator line was ineffective because deposition
occurred prematurely in the hot graphite line. Adding hydrogen did not improve coating quality; instead,
it reduced coating thickness and further decreased zirconium content while increasing carbon content.
Overall, the study demonstrated the feasibility of depositing ZrC-containing coatings from a nonhalogen
metalorganic precursor, but it also showed that ZrAcAc is not an optimal precursor for producing high-
quality ZrC coatings under the conditions evaluated. The work nonetheless established important
knowledge on precursor evaporation, transport, and deposition behavior that will inform future
development of improved metalorganic chemistries for advanced coated particle fuel applications.

  Next-Generation Coated Particle Fuels Development
 1. INTRODUCTION
Tristructural isotropic (TRISO)–coated particle fuel technology has been reestablished in the United
States through the US Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE) Advanced Gas Reactor
Fuel Development and Qualification (AGR) Program, which is now managed within the Advanced Fuels
Campaign (AFC). Although TRISO fuel was originally developed for high-temperature gas-cooled
reactor (HTGR) applications, it is now being considered for a broader range of advanced reactor concepts,
including small modular reactors, microreactors, and space power systems [1]. These emerging
applications may require fuel forms capable of operating under a wider range of temperatures, irradiation
conditions, fuel lifetimes, and core configurations than those targeted by conventional TRISO designs. As
a result, alternative coated particle architectures are of growing interest. These may include different
kernel chemistries and modified coating systems involving changes in layer number, thickness, or
material selection.
The need for improved fuel performance over longer lifetimes and at higher operating temperatures has
also motivated interest in alternative coating materials. One such candidate is ZrC, which may provide
benefits both as a replacement for SiC and as a getter to limit fission product transport [2]. Conventional
deposition of SiC and ZrC typically involves halogen-containing precursors such as methyltrichlorosilane
or zirconium tetrachloride. These chemistries generate hydrochloric acid as a by-product, which can
diffuse through coatings and react with uranium-bearing kernels, leading to kernel degradation and
uranium dispersion. As a result, halogen-based deposition limits direct ceramic coating on the kernel and
constrains fuel design flexibility. Alternative precursor chemistries may enable direct deposition of
functional ceramic layers while reducing these fabrication constraints.
Within the DOE-NE AFC Next Generation Fuels TRISO effort at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, one
area of focus has been the development of ZrC-containing coated particle architectures to improve fission
product retention. This need is especially relevant to microreactor concepts targeting fuel lifetimes of up
to 20 years [1]. Although AGR-pedigree TRISO fuel has demonstrated excellent performance within the
HTGR operating envelope, longer service periods may increase vulnerability to fission product attack,
which was identified in the AGR Program as an important degradation mechanism [2]. Fuel designs
incorporating getter layers therefore represent a promising path toward extending performance in
advanced applications.
A historical example is the UO2* TRISO particle design, which includes a protective pyrolytic carbon
(PyC) layer and a ZrC layer deposited on the kernel. This design has shown improved fission product
retention relative to conventional uranium oxide (UO2) and mixed uranium oxide, uranium carbide (UCO)
TRISO fuel [3]. Despite this potential benefit, adding extra coating layers increases fabrication
complexity and expands quality assurance and quality control requirements, potentially limiting practical
implementation. To address this challenge, new deposition approaches are needed that preserve the
advantages of ZrC-containing architectures while minimizing added process complexity.
This report describes efforts to apply and extend previously developed methods for fabricating coated
particle fuels with nontraditional architectures. In a previous report, established techniques were used to
reproduce the UO2* coating systems, whereas this work centered on new approaches for direct deposition
of ZrC using a metalorganic (MO) precursor. This strategy avoids the hydrochloric acid generation
associated with halogen-based processes and may eliminate the need for an intermediate protective PyC
layer.

 2. METHODOLOGY

 2.1 Fluidized bed chemical vapor deposition system
Coatings were deposited by fluidized bed chemical vapor deposition (FBCVD) using a resistively heated
graphite element furnace with a graphite cone with a 60° angle and a graphite tube of 5 mm inner
diameter (Figure 1). Zirconium acetylacetonate (ZrAcAc) was used as a chemical precursor for the
deposition of ZrC. This ZrAcAc precursor was delivered through a central water-cooled stainless-steel
gas injector and a lateral zirconium chlorinator (Figure 1).

 Figure 1. FBCVD system used for the deposition of ZrC from an MO precursor.

In general, ZrAcAc was introduced into the furnace through a stainless-steel 100 mL bubbler attached to
the gas delivery line at the entrance of the water-cooled injector (Figure 2). An MTI Corporation heating
jacket (Model EQ-HC-BL-150 for BL-SS-150) and temperature controller were used to heat the bubbler
to the desired temperature. Temperature inside the bubbler was measured using a thermocouple and set
using a heating controller (Figure 2). Section 3 describes the multiple attempted heating configurations.

 Figure 2. Gas delivery system used to introduce ZrAcAc into the chemical vapor deposition system.

 2.2 Materials
Argon (ultrahigh purity, Airgas), acetylene (ultrahigh purity, Airgas), propylene (ultrahigh purity,
Airgas), hydrogen (ultrahigh purity, Airgas), and zirconium(IV) acetylacetonate [Zr(C5H7O2)4, 97%,
Sigma-Aldrich] were used without further treatment. Tungsten carbide (WC) kernels (WC-YSZ,
Cospheric) with 500 µm diameters were used as substrates.

 2.3 Methods
The evaporation and decomposition of ZrAcAc was studied by simultaneous thermal analysis (STA)
using a Jupiter 499F3 system under an argon atmosphere to support targeting optimal coating conditions.
Approximately 60 g of WC particles were used as coating substrates to support coating studies. An initial
PyC layer was deposited to facilitate visualization. Argon and a mixture of acetylene and propylene with
30 volume % (% v/v) were used as gas precursors for the deposition of the high-density PyC. ZrC was
deposited using Ar as fluidization gas, and hydrogen was introduced in some experiments to reduce
carbon content in the coatings. Table 1 lists the general deposition conditions explored for the MO ZrC
layer depositions.
Coatings were assessed by surface imaging and analysis of cross sections when feasible. For cross-section
imaging, coated samples were mounted in epoxy resin, ground, and polished to midplane using Struers
polishing pads in a sequence that began at 9 µm and ended with a 1 µm diamond suspension polish.
Coatings were imaged using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) in backscattered mode. Imaging was
performed to qualitatively assess variations in coating thickness or surface structure among the batches.
Energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was used to determine the relative composition. The SEM
and EDS analyses were performed using a Hitachi FlexSEM 1000 II and an Oxford Xplore 30 detector.

 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

 3.1 ZrAcAc evaporation
One of the main challenges in using MO precursors is understanding their evaporation and decomposition
mechanism to select the right conditions for delivery into the chemical vapor deposition (CVD) system.
Figure 3 shows the thermogravimetric analysis of ZrAcAc under an argon atmosphere. No significant
mass loss was observed below 170°C. However, at 180°C, an endothermic reaction and the onset of mass
loss are evident. This endothermic reaction had a maximum at 195°C. The endothermic reaction and mass
loss extended up to 245°C, at which point the total mass loss reached 39%. Above 245°C, an exothermic
reaction appears to have occurred along with a slow but continuous mass loss. At 500°C, 46% of the mass
was lost, most likely because of decomposition of the precursor. These results suggest that the
evaporation temperature must be maintained between 180°C and 245°C.

 Figure 3. STA of the evaporation and decomposition of ZrAcAc.

 3.2 Bubbler configuration
Using the temperature range determined from the STA for the evaporation of ZrAcAc, testing of the
delivery system for depositing ZrC coatings was done. In Figure 4, changes determined from initial
testing are shown. Preliminary trials involved heating only the bubbler with a heating mantle shown in
Figure 4a. No deposition on the substrates was observed and most of the MO precursor remained after
testing. An additional thermocouple was included to measure the temperature inside the bubbler during
deposition (Figure 4b). A temperature difference between the set temperature and the interior of the
bubbler was observed. With the addition of the thermocouple the temperature inside the bubbler was
identified, but condensation still occurred in the gas delivery lines and collection chamber due to
differences in temperature of the precursor gas and the gas delivery lines. Figure 5 displays the material
that was deposited inside the collection chamber. Heating tape with a set temperature of 100°C and was
applied to the exterior of all stainless-steel lines to maintain temperature as shown in Figure 4c to mitigate
condensation in these lines. No further condensation was observed in the collection chamber or the gas
delivery lines after this change.

 Figure 4. Configurations used to control evaporation temperature in the bubbler.

Figure 6 shows the effect of bubbler temperature on the interior temperature of the crucible and on the
remaining mass after 2 h of heat treatment under argon flow. As shown in Figure 6a, although the set

 5

temperature was 190°C, the thermocouple inside the bubbler measured 163°C, and the physical
appearance of ZrAcAc did not change. As the set temperature increased to 200°C, the bubbler interior
temperature increased to 168°C. However, when the set temperature increased by 10°C to 210°C, just
above the endothermic region shown in Figure 3, the internal temperature increased to 230°C. Similarly,
when the set temperature increased to 259°C, the internal temperature increased to 280°C. The
differences in internal temperature, sometimes below and sometimes above the set temperature, is
hypothesized to be related to the endothermic and exothermic reactions identified in Figure 3. Overall,
these results show that a set temperature below 200°C was not enough to promote adequate evaporation
of ZrAcAc and that an increase of only 10°C (to 210°C) promoted evaporation of the precursor.
The set temperature also affected the mass transported into the CVD system. Figure 6 shows that
temperatures below 200°C resulted in a mass loss of only 7%, meaning that most of the MO precursor
remained in the bubbler. However, at 210°C, 36% of the mass was transported to the CVD system after
2 h under these conditions. An increase in set temperature to 259°C did not increase the evaporated mass.
The mass loss observed in these experiments was similar to the values measured in the STA tests (39%),
as shown in Figure 3. These results have important implications for the use of ZrAcAc because they
suggest that only about 36% of the initial MO mass is usable for the fabrication of ZrC coatings,
regardless of the evaporation temperature.

 3.3 ZrC deposition

Once the evaporation temperature was identified above 250°C, experiments were performed to select the
deposition temperature inside the coater. Figure 7 shows optical images of the surface of the substrates
with ZrC deposited at 1000°C and 1200°C with bubbler set point 250–259°C, ZrC-7 and ZrC-9
respectively. In Figure 7b, the samples deposited at 1200°C showed a change in color and more reflective
surface compared to the sample produced at 1000°C. Figure 8 shows SEM and EDS maps of the surface
of the particle after deposition of 1000°C. The ZrC layer was not uniform and only some regions
contained zirconium as shown in the Zr map in Figure 8b.

 Figure 7. Optical images of ZrC deposited at (a) 1000°C (ZrC-7) and (b) 1200°C (ZrC-9).

 and (c) carbon element map.

As the deposition temperature increased to 1200°C, a uniform coating was produced (Figure 9). Using the
SEM images, the layer thickness was measured to be around 5 µm thick. EDS analysis showed that this
coating was carbon rich and contained only 6.4 wt % zirconium.

 Figure 9. Cross section of the ZrC-9 coating produced at 1200°C: (a) optical image and (b) SEM
 micrograph.

 8

Due to possible condensation of MO precursor in the water-cooled injector, a series of tests were
performed to study the deposition of ZrC through the zirconium chlorinator. Figure 10 shows the
resulting ZrC coating that was deposited at 1200°C using the zirconium chlorinator. The coating thickness
and Zr wt % was reduced to approximately 2 µm and 2 wt % respectively. Inspection of the graphite
chlorinator showed that ZrC was deposited to the lining of the graphite line. The graphite chlorinator is
located inside the heating element, causing it to reach ZrC deposition temperatures and coat the inside.

 Figure 10. ZrC-10 deposited using the zirconium chlorinator line to deliver the MO precursor into
 the furnace.

Given the large carbon concentration, hydrogen was added to the reaction to reduce that concentration
because hydrogen can reduce gas maturation and C-C heterogeneous reactions. Figure 11 shows
micrographs of targeted ZrC coatings deposited with hydrogen (10%–30% v/v). Overall, the addition of
hydrogen reduced the coating thickness of the material. Additionally, the surface of the particles also
became darker when hydrogen was used in the reaction. This change in color suggests a change in the
compositions of the coatings produced.

hydrogen. Based on EDS analysis of the cross-section, the carbon concentration increased with hydrogen
concentration. Conversely, oxygen and zirconium content decreased as the hydrogen concentration
changed from 10% to 30%. The increase in carbon content would explain the darker coloration observed
in Figure 12.

The origin of this reduction in zirconium concentration and increase in carbon content with the addition of
hydrogen is unclear. In general, ZrAcAc decomposes by detachment of acetylacetone according to
equation (1) [4]:

Zr(C5H7O2)4(s) → Zr[(CH3CO)2CH2]4−x(g) + [(CH3CO)2CH2]x−y(g) + 2y(CH3COOH)(+yCH4)(g)                  (1)

According to a recent study, the use of hydrogen could promote the detachment of acetylacetonate and
H2O and the formation of Zr-OH bonds, forming the intermediate species Zr(C5H6O2)2(g) and
ZrO(C5H6O2)(g) [4]. Carbon originates from the decomposition of the acetylacetonate ligands, whereas
zirconium and oxygen originate from the residual intermediate species mentioned above. During the
deposition of ZrC with hydrogen, coating appeared to be suppressed (thinner coatings), which suggests
that hydrogen promoted homogeneous reactions through the formation of either more reactive species that
resulted in dinuclear fragments or species with a lower sticking coefficient [4]. Although adjusting the
precursor concentration or deposition temperature could mitigate this suppression in general, further work
would be needed to fully resolve the problem supporting deposition of dense, near stoichiometric ZrC.

 4. SUMMARY
This report documents FY26 work at Oak Ridge National Laboratory on developing next-generation
coated particle fuels through a nonhalogen route to deposit ZrC. In this study, ZrAcAc was evaluated as
an MO precursor for ZrC deposition in an FBCVD system. Thermal analysis showed that the precursor
evaporates most effectively between 180°C and 245°C, but successful transport of the precursor required
heated gas lines to prevent condensation. Even with these improvements to the furnace, only about 36%–
39% of the precursor was effectively volatilized, limiting process efficiency. Coating trials showed that
deposition at lower temperatures produced incomplete, nonuniform coatings, whereas deposition at
1200°C produced continuous coatings about 5 µm thick. However, the coatings were carbon rich, and
zirconium concentrations ranged from 1.5 to 6.4 wt %. Alternative precursor delivery through the
chlorinator line led to premature deposition in the hot graphite line. Adding hydrogen did not improve the
coating chemistry; instead, it increased carbon content, most likely by favoring homogeneous reactions
that limited the deposition of zirconium-bearing species. Overall, the report concludes that although
nonhalogen deposition of ZrC-containing coatings is feasible, ZrAcAc is not an ideal precursor for
producing high-quality ZrC coatings under the conditions tested. Future work should include

investigating more reactive MO precursors, such as tetrakis(dimethylamido)zirconium(IV) and
bis(methyl-η5-cyclopentadienyl)methoxymethylzirconium, and developing improved delivery systems.
