Primary MiNEN of the urinary bladder with large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma and enteric-type adenocarcinoma: a case report
Authors: Xiaofei Xiu, Jiajia Li, Lisha Duan, Xiaomei Wang, Zinan Guo, Xuelan Xiao, Qianhui Han, Feng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) is the rarest high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) of the urinary bladder. It can be observed in mixed neuroendocrine/non-neuroendocrine neoplasm (MiNEN), which is predominantly associated with urothelial carcinoma (UC) and less frequently with adenocarcinoma. Here, we report a primary MiNEN of the urinary bladder, composed of LCNEC and enteric-type adenocarcinoma. The patient was a 58-year-old male who initially presented with painless hematuria and dysuria. Transabdominal ultrasonography demonstrated a 4.8 × 2.4 cm hypoechoic lesion arising from the posterior bladder wall. Histologically, the tumor showed distinct yet closely apposed components of LCNEC and enteric-type adenocarcinoma. Immunohistochemically, the LCNEC component was positive for neuroendocrine markers, including CD56, chromogranin, synaptophysin, and INSM1, while the adenocarcinoma component was positive for CK20 and CDX2. Notably, both components showed aberrant P53 expression (P53-null pattern), supporting a shared clonal origin with divergent differentiation. After progression following initial platinum-based chemotherapy, the patient achieved a radiographic partial response (PR) of pulmonary metastases after treatment with enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab administered with radiotherapy. Our case highlights the diagnostic importance of recognizing a high-grade neuroendocrine component in bladder tumors. Furthermore, antibody-drug conjugates and immune checkpoint inhibitors may merit further study in this rare entity.

Introduction

Primary neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) of the urinary bladder are sporadic, accounting for less than 1% of urothelial neoplasms, and are associated with aggressive clinical behavior, particularly in the setting of high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) (1). According to the 5th World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Urinary and Male Genital Tumors, bladder NENs are categorized into well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC), large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC), and mixed neuroendocrine/non-neuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs). The majority of bladder NENs are high-grade NECs, including SCNEC and LCNEC, which exhibit worse outcomes than conventional urothelial carcinoma (UC) (2, 3). Among them, LCNEC is an exceptionally rare subtype. To date, little is known about its clinicopathologic characteristics and optimal treatment schedule due to the limited number of cases. LCNEC frequently presents with other histologic components, most commonly conventional UC, carcinoma in situ, SCNEC, and, less often, adenocarcinoma. The coexistence of LCNEC with enteric-type adenocarcinoma is particularly rare. Here, we describe a primary MiNEN of the urinary bladder, composed of LCNEC and enteric-type adenocarcinoma in a 58-year-old man. We highlight the diagnostic challenges and describe a notable radiographic response in metastatic disease following treatment with enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab and radiotherapy.

Case description

A 58-year-old man with no smoking history initially presented with painless hematuria and dysuria, without a significant past medical history. He underwent a transabdominal ultrasonography that demonstrated a 4.8 × 2.4 cm hypoechoic lesion arising from the posterior bladder.

Subsequently, the patient underwent transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT). Microscopically, the lesion exhibited two distinct components: a high-grade NEC adjacent to an enteric-type adenocarcinoma component. The NEC component was arranged in nests, solid sheet-like growth, peripheral palisading, accompanied by a high mitotic rate and multiple foci of necrosis. The tumor cells were large, pleomorphic with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, large nuclei with coarse chromatin, and variably prominent nucleoli. The morphology was similar to that of pulmonary LCNEC. The enteric-type adenocarcinoma component, comprising approximately 30% of the tumor volume, was arranged in glandular structures with enteric-type differentiation (Figure 1). Extensive invasion of the lamina propria and the muscularis propria was present.

Immunohistochemically, the LCNEC component was positive for neuroendocrine markers, including CD56, chromogranin, synaptophysin, and INSM1. The urothelial lineage markers, including GATA3, P63, and P40, as well as enteric markers, including CK20 and CDX2, were not expressed. In contrast, the adenocarcinoma component diffusely expressed CK20 and CDX2 but was negative for neuroendocrine markers (Table 1). Notably, both components showed an aberrant P53 immunophenotype characterized by complete loss of nuclear staining (P53-null pattern) (Figure 2).

Subsequently, the patient underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy, and no mass lesions or neoplastic changes were discovered.

Postoperatively, the patient received six cycles of gemcitabine plus cisplatin chemotherapy. Nine months later, computed tomography (CT) revealed multiple bilateral pulmonary nodules, consistent with metastatic disease. The largest nodule measured 2.7 × 1.6 cm within the posterior basal segment of the left lower lobe (Figures 3A, B). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed nodular thickening in the right inferolateral wall of the bladder, which was isointense on T1-weighted imaging and hypointense on T2-weighted imaging. The lesion measured 1.8 × 1.6 × 1.2 cm and was suspicious for local recurrence (Figures 3C, D). 18F-FDG PET/CT scan showed increased uptake in the bladder wall lesion and hypermetabolic bilateral pulmonary nodules, consistent with metastases. Multiple hypermetabolic foci were also noted within the prostate.

The patient subsequently received enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab for three cycles in combination with radiotherapy. Three months after therapy, a follow-up CT showed regression of the largest target pulmonary lesion to 1.4 × 1.2 cm, consistent with a radiographic partial response (PR) (Figures 3E, F). Meanwhile, no appreciable thickening of the bladder wall or definite residual lesion was discovered on MRI (Figures 3G, H).

Discussion

Mixed neuroendocrine/non-neuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs) of the urinary bladder are exceedingly rare, and LCNEC is the rarest high-grade NEC phenotype in this setting. LCNEC accounts for <0.5% of all bladder UCs (1). Since its initial description in 1986, fewer than 50 sporadic case reports with either pure or mixed histology (4). LCNEC predominantly affects older adults (typically >60 y), with a wide age range at diagnosis (20 to 84 y, mean: 60.8 y). It shows a strong male predominance and often presents at an advanced stage with muscle invasion and metastatic dissemination (5, 6). Our case is notable for the coexistence of LCNEC with an enteric-type adenocarcinoma component and is classified as MiNEN. These two components showed a clear transition between the two lineages, underscoring both diagnostic complexity and potential pathogenetic significance.

The origin of bladder NECs, particularly when coexisting with an adenocarcinoma component, remains unclear and has been explained by different theories, including.

derivation of the neoplastic neuroendocrine clone from a common multipotential cancer stem cell shared with non-endocrine carcinoma or development from normal or metaplastic neuroendocrine cells of the urothelial mucosa (7). Research on MiNECs in the bladder and other organ systems has revealed shared molecular alterations between these two components, implying a common molecular histogenesis (8). Cheng et al. identified nearly identical patterns of allelic loss of heterozygosity and X-chromosome inactivation in SCNEC and concurrent UC in the bladder, providing compelling evidence for a shared clonal origin (9). Studies in colorectal cancer by Vortmeyer et al. revealed that adenocarcinoma and poorly differentiated NEC shared identical genetic changes of the adenoma-adenocarcinoma sequence involving TP53, DCC, and APC. The progression to highly malignant neuroendocrine carcinoma may occur at an early stage during colorectal carcinogenesis (10). In our case, the LCNEC and adenocarcinoma components shared a common complete loss of P53 expression (P53-null pattern) by immunohistochemistry, supporting the common molecular pathogenesis.

The diagnostic criteria for bladder LCNEC largely parallels that used in the pulmonary LCNEC: (i) large, polygonal tumor cells, with low nuclear/cytoplasmic ratio; (ii) coarse chromatin with prominent nucleoli; (iii) high mitotic activity in excess of 10 mitoses per 10 high-power field (HPF) with multiple areas of necrosis; and (iv) specific growth patterns (organoid nesting, trabecular growth, palisading, or diffuse solid sheets), immunohistochemical or ultrastructural evidence of neuroendocrine differentiation. LCNEC often exhibits a higher proliferation, ranging from 40% to 95%. Immunohistochemically, it expresses neuroendocrine markers including CD56, chromogranin, synaptophysin, and INSM1, with variable sensitivity and specificity. Research revealed that neuroendocrine tumor cells may secrete neurohumoral mediators, such as neuropeptides, thereby establishing autocrine or paracrine growth-promoting loops that may facilitate the expansion of adjacent glandular components and contribute to rapid disease progression (11, 12). Therefore, it is essential to ensure comprehensive sampling to avoid under-recognition of high-grade NEC elements. For MiNEN tumors, the pathologist should explicitly document the proportion and grading of the neuroendocrine component to inform risk-based multimodal management strategies. The differential diagnoses of bladder LCNEC include SCNEC, poorly differentiated or undifferentiated UC, lymphoma, malignant melanoma, lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma, and metastatic LCNEC (13). In practice, strict size-based thresholds that separate LCNEC from SCNEC are unreliable, and chromatin quality, nucleolar prominence, and architecture are more reproducible discriminators than cell size alone. Distinguishing LCNEC from SCNEC is critical, as LCNEC is associated with significantly worse survival (14). Immunohistochemistry is pivotal for excluding mimics, such as lymphoma, melanoma, and UC. However, neuroendocrine markers are not entirely specific, and urothelial-lineage markers may be variable in NECs. For example, GATA3 lacks absolute discriminatory value because it is expressed in approximately half of LCNEC cases, ranging from weak/focal staining to diffuse strong expression. Therefore, a panel-based approach integrating morphology, neuroendocrine markers, and epithelial lineage markers is preferred. Clinical history often provides helpful clues to rule out metastatic LCNEC.

Given the rarity of LCNEC and bladder MiNEN, optimal management remains uncertain, and evidence is primarily derived from small cohorts and case reports. Surgical treatment, including TURBT, radical cystectomy (RC), and other surgical modalities, significantly contributes to the treatment. Among these surgical treatments, RC may present the optimal therapeutic approach to improve prognosis (2). Meanwhile, the combination of RC with perioperative chemotherapy and/or chemoradiotherapy could enhance patient survival (15). Emerging evidence suggests that immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) may confer clinical benefit in high-grade NECs, particularly in cisplatin-ineligible or previously treated patients (16). In our case, after progression following initial cisplatin-based chemotherapy, the patient achieved a RP response of pulmonary metastases with enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab alongside radiotherapy, supporting the potential therapeutic role of ICIs in the metastatic setting. Although causality cannot be inferred from a single case, particularly in the context of concomitant local therapy, the observed clinical response remains noteworthy and hypothesis-generating.

Age, race, TNM stage, surgery, and chemotherapy are independent predictors for overall survival in bladder LCNEC (17). Whether pure LCNEC confers a worse prognosis than mixed forms remain controversial across small series, reflecting limited sample sizes and substantial biological heterogeneity (2, 14, 18, 19). Moreover, transurethral resection (TUR) specimens may under-sample the lesion and miss minor admixed components, potentially contributing to misclassification. The prognostic impact of the non-neuroendocrine element, including its subtype and/or differentiation, remains incompletely defined, as highlighted by reports in which a specific adenocarcinomatous component drove metastatic behavior (20). Extensive multi-institutional cohorts and integrated genomic-pathologic correlation are required to elucidate these issues.

Conclusion

We describe a primary MiNEN of the urinary bladder composed of LCNEC and enteric-type adenocarcinoma, with concordant aberrant P53 immunophenotype (P53-null pattern). Given that NEC is often associated with worse outcomes and may alter systemic therapy selection, it is critical to recognize and explicitly report the presence of high-grade NEC. Although the absence of component-matched molecular profiling limits our report, the shared P53-null pattern supports a biologically linked process with divergent differentiation. Nevertheless, the limitations of this report must be recognized, including the absence of molecular profiling, the difficulty in identifying the primary therapeutic driver responsible for pulmonary metastasis regression within a multimodal regimen, and the limited duration of follow-up. Large cohorts with integrated molecular profiling are needed to clarify histogenesis and therapeutic strategies.
