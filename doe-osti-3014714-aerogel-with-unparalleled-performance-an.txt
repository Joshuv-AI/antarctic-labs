Executive Summary of CRADA Work:
Westwood Aerogel investigated the reproducible production of an ambiently or
electromagnetic spectrum assisted drying of extremely porous aerogel materials. Traditional
aerogel processing is limited by low throughput and scalability due to supercritical drying.
Westwood Aerogel’s advanced manufacturing process enables commercialization and
deployment at scale, making aerogel materials more accessible to a range of industries and
applications. We focused on shifting from the lab scale environment to manufacturing
prototypes. The initial manufacturing model is based on the float glass process, which is the
industry standard for glass window production, and the paper drying process. By incorporating
an ambient drying process a continuous line model can be used from start to finish. The project
team worked to develop a continuous manufacturing system to scale-up and reduce costs while
maintaining the lightweight and insulative properties of aerospace-grade aerogel. While
aerogels are extremely insulative, lightweight, and thin materials (making them ideal insulators
across application spaces), high costs and stagnant innovation have hampered their wider
adoption. Traditional manufacturing of aerogel requires supercritical extraction of pore fluid to
obtain highly insulating materials, which is an energy intensive batch process and limits aerogel
production output. Westwood Aerogel’s proprietary ambient drying process enables the
continuous production of highly insulative aerogel materials without the use of supercritical
extraction.

Summary of Research Results:

The research was aimed at validation of the production of large aerogel monoliths. During the
customer discovery process, it was determined that a preferred form factor for aerogel
production was aerogel granules. Westwood Aerogel shifted focus to scaling the ambiently

dried aerogel granules and developing quality control checks for the scale-up initiative. The
Westwood Aerogel team explored final product designs, which included the ambiently dried
aerogel in a composite formfactor, termed “ZeroTherm”, in order to extend the ease of use for
the proposed thermal insulation barrier. The final product is aimed at improving the thermal
performance for target applications such as electric vehicle battery pack insulation. The project
explored scale-up the size and throughput of extremely porous gels. Efforts were made to
improve efficiency of the ambient drying chamber and procedure for aerogel production. The
team tested design deposition procedures for oxide layers on aerogel substrate. Product
performance validation experiments were conducted, including mechanical durability, thermal
performance/stability, and product effectiveness in improving thermal performance in target
systems.
