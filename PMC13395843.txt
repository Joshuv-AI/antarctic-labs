A novel mathematical model of seasonality in Oropouche virus transmission in Amazonas, Brazil
Authors: Carli Peterson, Darsh Gandhi, Emma Slack, Elizabeth Rubio, Amira Claxton, Christopher M. Kribs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oropouche virus (OROV) is a vector-borne arbovirus that has caused more than 500,000 infections across South America, Central America, and the Caribbean. Its primary vector, the midge species Culicoides paraensis, acquires the virus from wild reservoir hosts and transmits it to humans during blood feeding. As outbreaks within the Amazon basin continue to occur and confirmed cases steadily increase, a proper understanding of the interplay between climatic variables, vector behavior, and OROV transmission is needed to develop effective public health interventions. In this paper, we develop a novel mathematical model of Oropouche virus spread in Amazonas, Brazil using ordinary differential equations (ODEs). First, we derive the basic reproductive number at baseline to assess the potential for disease persistence. We then incorporate seasonal forcing and fit the model to weekly incidence data from the 2023-2024 outbreak using least squares optimization. With our model structure, we evaluated multiple candidate seasonality functions and implementations, including precipitation-based forcing functions and others intended to model general seasonality or the right-skewed incidence curve. Introduction

Oropouche virus (OROV) is a vector-borne disease of growing concern due to its potential for large-scale outbreaks. In the most recent outbreak, there were more than 14,000 confirmed cases between May 28th, 2023 and June 1st, 2024 in South America alone (Pan American Health Organization 2024). The virus was also detected for the first time in Europe, where there were 19 documented cases in Spain, Italy, and Germany (Desai et al. 2024). In 2024, the first disease-induced fatalities were reported, including a case of vertical transmission and fetal death (Pan American Health Organization 2024; Morrison et al. 2024). Although OROV was first isolated in 1955 (Anderson et al. 1961; Da Silva Azevedo et al. 2007; Romero-Alvarez and Escobar 2018), many aspects of the virus and its transmission remain unknown. Its reemergence in recent years raises concern about increased transmissibility and severity, particularly in the Amazon basin.

The virus circulates in two distinct cycles: sylvatic and urban (Gutierrez et al. 2020; Pinheiro et al. 1982). In the sylvatic cycle, midges and mosquitoes bite infected reservoir species, such as sloths (Bradypus tridactylus), primates and birds, and can infect humans living, working or traveling near forested regions. These individuals link the sylvatic cycle to the urban one, where major outbreaks occur (Files et al. 2022). In the urban cycle, the primary vector, Culicoides paraensis, becomes infected when biting an infected human and subsequently transmits the virus to other humans (Nunes et al. 2007). There is no known horizontal transmission between humans (Sciancalepore et al. 2022), so control of the biting midge population is critical for preventing future outbreaks.

Although about 40-80% of infected individuals are asymptomatic (Pastula et al. 2025; ANRS MIE 2024), symptomatic OROV presents as a febrile illness characterized by severe headaches, myalgia, and arthralgia (Sakkas et al. 2018). In humans, there is a 3–8 day latent period followed by a 2–7 day infectious period. Recovered individuals tend to relapse after 2–10 days, with the viral load in the bloodstream peaking on the second day of symptoms (Files et al. 2022; Sakkas et al. 2018). Immunity following OROV infection remains understudied. Although neutralizing antibodies are produced during infection, the duration of protective immunity is not known, and documented cases of reinfection suggest that immunity is not lifelong (Scachetti et al. 2024). In addition, in-vitro studies of the OROV strain that emerged during the 2023-2024 outbreak indicate higher replication efficiency and reduced neutralizing capacity of sera from previously exposed individuals, suggesting a lower likelihood of gaining immunity (Scachetti et al. 2024). Some infections progress to serious neurological manifestations, making OROV a significant public health threat. There are currently no vaccines to prevent or medicines to treat the infection.

Climate variability and the increasing urbanization of the Brazilian Amazon exacerbate the public health challenges that OROV presents. Deforestation and human expansion into peri-urban areas have increased human contact with biting midges, amplifying the risk of widespread outbreaks (Silva-Caso et al. 2019). The state of Amazonas, Brazil, and specifically its metropolitan capital Manaus, has experienced multiple Oropouche outbreaks (Files et al. 2022; Sciancalepore et al. 2022; Mourão et al. 2009). Notably, despite accounting for only 2%2% of Brazil’s total population, Amazonas reported 47%47% of the country’s confirmed OROV cases during the most recent outbreak (Ministério Da Saúde 2024). In addition to urbanization, the climate in this region may also influence OROV transmission. Amazonas experiences a humid, tropical climate with minimal temperature fluctuations throughout the year and has two distinct precipitation seasons: rainy and dry (Nobre et al. 2009). OROV incidence appears to align with the former, as cases increase considerably during the rainy season, which lasts from December to May (NASA 2015). These factors make Amazonas a prime location for investigating the relationship between climate and disease spread.

Previous modeling studies have primarily used statistical and machine learning approaches, such as ecological niche and spatiotemporal suitability models, to predict the geographic spread of OROV and identify regions at risk of outbreaks (Romero-Alvarez et al. 2023; Tegally et al. 2024; Gutierrez et al. 2020). These methods are valuable for informing public health initiatives and preparedness, but they do not capture the process underlying transmission. Comparatively, deterministic models help describe processes that drive these dynamics. For example, early models of the COVID-19 pandemic identified the potential impact of social distancing and lockdown despite limited knowledge of the virus’s biological and epidemiological properties (Liu et al. 2020; Frost et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2020; Bärwolff 2020; Hammoumi and Qesmi 2020). Similarly, deterministic models that incorporate seasonal variation in vector populations have provided insight into how environmental conditions influence transmission dynamics in vector-borne diseases such as Zika and dengue, where changes in mosquito birth, death, and biting rates can drive seasonal outbreaks (Ibrahim and Dénes 2021; Mclennan-Smith and Mercer 2014). Thus, these types of models can provide a better understanding of the OROV transmission cycle, particularly the role of environmental drivers and seasonal variation in midge activity, which is needed to identify when, why, and how outbreaks emerge.

In this paper, we investigate the role of seasonality in OROV transmission by incorporating seasonal forcing in two aspects: midge biting behavior and midge recruitment. We implement four forms of seasonal forcing - exponential decay, periodic seasonality, an asymmetric pulse, and smoothed precipitation data - and compare them with the goal of answering the question “What type of seasonal environmental forcing in midge activity patterns best explains the observed weekly human OROV incidence in Amazonas, Brazil?” The first three forcing functions serve as generic representations of seasonality, capturing the influence of factors such as temperature, precipitation, and humidity, while the latter function explicitly captures the effect of precipitation. We build upon previous OROV studies by incorporating known features of the disease into what is, to our knowledge, the first mathematical model of OROV transmission. Because we employ seasonal forcing, the model is explicitly time-dependent, or non-autonomous, as opposed to an autonomous model, which does not depend explicitly on time and assumes constant parameter values. Focusing on the midge species C. paraensis and its seasonal activity patterns in this region, we develop a system of ordinary differential equations to explore the impact of seasonality on OROV epidemiology.

In vector-borne disease epidemiology, seasonality plays a key role in the magnitude, scale, and timing of an outbreak. Vectors, such as C. paraensis, rely on temperature, humidity, and other weather patterns to reproduce and survive (Sanders et al. 2011). Variations in weather patterns due to El Niño and La Niña oscillations, periods of heavy rainfall, and changes in humidity and temperature all influence the ability of vectors to propagate, meaning the diseases they carry are all dependent on these parameters as well (Nava et al. 2017). C. paraensis thrives in humid, rainy conditions, with population counts peaking during times of heavy precipitation (Feitoza et al. 2023; Hoch et al. 1990). Consequently, OROV outbreaks tend to occur during wetter months due to the increased activity of Culicoides midges (Lorenz et al. 2017). In the autonomous case of the model described by Eq. (1) where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_1(t)=f_2(t)=1$$\end{document}f1(t)=f2(t)=1, we could not capture the dependence of the outbreak magnitude nor timing on the aforementioned seasonal factors (see Online Resource 1 (Figure S1) for simulations of the model without seasonality). We believe precipitation plays an important role in OROV outbreaks, due to the spike in observed incidence in Amazonas during its rainy season (see Fig. 2); however, the connection may be more complex and also depend on other environmental and epidemiological factors. We also explored precipitation-based functions using rainfall data in Amazonas, Brazil. Data were obtained from the United States Geological Survey (USGS) and aligned with the time period of our study. Since the USGS reports data in five-day intervals, we averaged each data point over five days to produce a daily precipitation estimate, then converted these estimates to the epidemiological weeks used by Brazil’s (Ministério Da Saúde 2024), from which we obtained the incidence data. To reduce noise from week-to-week variability and better capture overall seasonal trends, we smoothed the precipitation time series by taking a three-week moving average centered on week n (i.e., averaging weeks n-1n-1, n, and n+1n+1). To ensure consistency with the other seasonality functions, we normalized the data by dividing by the maximum recorded value so that all data were between 0 and 1. From this processed data, we developed and tested three variants: (1) the smoothed, normalized precipitation data directly; (2) the smoothed, normalized precipitation data shifted in time so that the peak of the rainy season aligned with the peak of the incidence data, mimicking a potential lag in vector response to rainfall; and (3) the smoothed, normalized, shifted data with a threshold of 0.5mm, since precipitation above this level appeared to most strongly influence incidence. For each variant, we implemented two common interpolation methods–linear and cubic splines–using MATLAB’s interp1 function. Cubic spline interpolations tend to preserve the shape of the data better than linear interpolations and provide a smoother curve. On the other hand, linear interpolations can be more appropriate for data that is irregular or contains abrupt changes. Thus, our goal was to implement both methods and compare their outputs to determine which provided a better fit to the observed incidence data. Quantitative results

In this section, we detail the methods used to obtain sets of parameter values for our model, calculate relative error percentage between simulated and observed data, and discuss numerical results. Throughout the section, we will consider the three cases of our model described in Sect. 2.2: (M1) seasonality in only the midge biting behavior, (M2) seasonality in only the midge recruitment rates, and (M3) seasonality in both the biting behavior and midge recruitment rates.

Parameter estimates from literature

We estimated many of our parameters and initial conditions from literature. The death rates for all populations were taken as the reciprocal of the average life expectancies. For sloths, this was 25 years (Bradford 2018), for humans, 73.43 years (Smits and Permanyer 2019), and for midges, 25 days (Sciancalepore et al. 2022; Mellor et al. 2000). Due to a lack of evidence indicating otherwise, we assumed the recovery rates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _H$$\end{document}γH and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _A$$\end{document}γA for the human and sloth populations to be the same and take them as the reciprocal of the average length of OROV infection (4.5 days) in humans (Files et al. 2022; Sakkas et al. 2018; Da Rosa et al. 2017). We took the total number of humans in the state of Amazonas (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_H$$\end{document}NH) from the 2022 Brazilian census (Instituto Brasileiro de Geografia e Estatística 2023a). Since sloth populations across Amazonas are unknown, we estimated the total sloth population (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_A$$\end{document}NA) to be the sloth density of 1.7 sloths per square kilometer (Moraes-Barros et al. 2022) times the land area of Amazonas minus the area of Manaus.

Using these values, we estimated the initial conditions as follows: the initial number of infected humans, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_H(0)$$\end{document}IH(0), was equal to the number of reported infections at the start of our simulation (May 28, 2023), which was zero. Due to their short life spans and the fact that May is at the end of the rainy season in Amazonas, we assumed there were no infected forest or city midges at the beginning of our simulation, meaning both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_F(0)$$\end{document}IF(0) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_C(0)$$\end{document}IC(0) were set to zero. The initial infected sloth population, initial susceptible city midge population, and initial susceptible forest midge population were estimated using the data-fitting methods described in Sect. 4.2.

Data-fitting and error calculation

Using a least-squares data fitting approach, we estimated the remaining parameters for Model (1) not described in Sect. 4.1 based on weekly Oropouche incidence data from May 28th, 2023 to June 1st, 2024, corresponding to the timeline of the 2023-2024 outbreak. The data were obtained from Brazil’s Ministério Da Saúde (2024) and were scaled to account for asymptomatic infections. Estimates of the symptomatic fraction of OROV infections vary in the literature, with some studies suggesting approximately 60% of infections are symptomatic and others estimating as low as 20% (Pastula et al. 2025; ANRS MIE 2024). As an approximation for this initial modeling study, we average these and assume that 40% of infections are symptomatic. Therefore, incidence data were divided by 0.4 to estimate the total number of infections, accounting for unreported asymptomatic cases. Because the infectious period for OROV is known to be close to one week (Files et al. 2022; Sakkas et al. 2018; Da Rosa et al. 2017), we can infer prevalence from incidence data, and we therefore use this inferred prevalence to fit our model, which measures prevalence. The parameters estimated include the product of the maximum biting rate and the probability of infection from midge to host, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b\pi _{VH}$$\end{document}bπVH, the product of the maximum biting rate and the probability of infection from host to midge \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b\pi _{HV}$$\end{document}bπHV, midge preference for sloths versus humans, k the proportion of time spent in urban areas by humans, p, the recruitment of forest midges, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Lambda _F$$\end{document}ΛF, and the recruitment of city midges, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Lambda _C$$\end{document}ΛC. Model comparison and forcing function evaluation

When implementing seasonal forcing functions within a mathematical model, it is important to establish criteria for comparing how well different functional forms capture observed outbreak dynamics. Because our data consist of a single outbreak time series, common validation techniques such as split-sampling or cross-application to independent datasets are not applicable. Instead, we focus on direct measures of model fit between simulated and observed data that are both interpretable and compatible with our ODE system.

As noted in Sect. 4.2, we evaluated our model fit using the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l^2$$\end{document}l2 relative error percentage, which measures the squared relative deviation between the observed and simulated data. This choice is appropriate for our system of non-autonomous ODEs and the limited availability of outbreak data. While likelihood-based approaches such as AIC, BIC, or DIC can be applied to deterministic models, their use requires specification of an explicit observation model, which includes assumptions about the distribution in reporting error. Because OROV surveillance data and its reporting processes are limited, we instead used least-squares-based metrics to compare candidate seasonal forcing functions without introducing additional assumptions about the observation process.

Some alternative error metrics to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l^2$$\end{document}l2 relative error that have been applied to deterministic ODE systems include the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l^1$$\end{document}l1 relative error, normalized root mean squared error (NRMSE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE). In our analysis, we focused on the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l^2$$\end{document}l2 relative error because it penalizes large deviations more strongly than small ones, allowing the model to more accurately capture epidemic features such as outbreak timing and peak size. The relative formulation also scaled deviations by the magnitude of the observations, preventing the error from being dominated by periods of high incidence. To further test the robustness of our conclusions, we evaluated the model fit using an altered version of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}R2 formula, which has been shown to be an informative performance measure (Chicco et al. 2021). For consistency with our other percentage-based metric, we report this value as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 \times 100$$\end{document}R2×100 as described in the following equation:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {EP}_{{R^{2}}}=\left( 1-\frac{\sum _{i=1}^{N_t}(X^i-I_H(t^i))^2}{\sum _{i=1}^{N_t}(X^i-\overline{X})^2}\right) \times 100, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}EPR2=1-∑i=1t(Xi-IH(ti))2∑i=1t(Xi-X¯)2×100,where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{X}$$\end{document}X¯ denoted the mean of the observed incidence data. The numerator of Equation 7 measures the model’s residual error, while the denominator represents the total variance of the data around its mean. Thus, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}R2 measures the improvement of the model over the baseline prediction of simply using the mean, providing a measure of how well the model explains the observed incidence dynamics. Higher EP values (closer to 100%) indicate a better fit, while values near 0 indicate performance comparable to the baseline. Negative values are also possible and indicate that the model performs worse than the naïve baseline of predicting the mean.

Both error metrics are based on the model’s residual error (i.e., the discrepancy between observed and simulated values), and therefore capture the same underlying notion of model error. However, they summarize this error in different ways: the relative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l^2$$\end{document}l2 error measures how far the model predictions are from the data in absolute terms, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}R2 measures how well the model explains the variation in the data relative to a baseline of assuming the mean.

Discussion and conclusions

This study presents, to our knowledge, the first mathematical model of Oropouche virus transmission. We developed and analyzed a novel system of non-autonomous ordinary differential equations to capture OROV dynamics between sylvatic and urban transmission cycles. By fitting this model to weekly incidence data from the 2023–2024 outbreak in Amazonas, Brazil, we provide a foundation for evaluating hypotheses about ecological and climatic drivers of OROV dynamics.

We calculated the basic reproductive number for the autonomous case of our model, before implementing seasonality, to assess baseline qualitative behaviors. Our analysis showed that the three linked transmission cycles (sloths and midges, humans and midges in the forest, and humans and midges in an urban area) are less than additive, agreeing with biological intuition, as each cycle depends in part on infections generated in the others.

To evaluate the role of seasonality, we tested three model versions: (M1) seasonality in only the infection terms, (M2) seasonality in only the midge recruitment terms, and (M3) seasonality in both transmission and recruitment. For each version, we applied candidate seasonal forcing functions: three that were not directly tied to precipitation (exponential (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_1$$\end{document}F1), cosine (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_2$$\end{document}F2), and beta distribution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_3$$\end{document}F3) functions), and precipitation-based functions derived from adjusted rainfall data using linear and cubic interpolations. Model performance was assessed using the relative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l^2$$\end{document}l2 error percentage (EP) (6), which is a standard method for comparing data fitting results, as well as the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}R2 error percentage \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {EP}_{R^2}$$\end{document}EPR2 (7). Note, however, that although the definition given for the EP appears to be proportionalized, it tends to increase with the number of data points being fit. Throughout our results, the ranking of the function and model types was consistent across metrics, supporting the idea that both metrics capture similar aspects of goodness of fit.

Our goal was to find a set of parameter values that fit a model version to the observed data with an EP below 10%10%. However, with the given model structure and data, we could not achieve this threshold. While a few model versions and seasonality types came close, the precipitation-based functions produced much higher errors, in some cases approaching 100%100%. Although 100%100% has no special meaning in the EP definition, the errors for the interpolation functions tended to this value because the “best-fit” resulted in the simulated data nearly flatlining, making the radicand in Equation (6) approximately equal to 1. In these cases, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {EP}_{R^2}$$\end{document}EPR2 was negative, indicating predicting the mean of the incidence data provides a better fit than the precipitation functions.

These results suggest that the model’s main conclusion, namely, that seasonal variation in OROV transmission is best represented by an exponential pulse, is robust to observational noise. However, higher levels of error can affect inferences about which biological mechanism seasonality impacts most in our model.

Model versions with precipitation-based forcing produced the worst fits, despite testing multiple variations, including smoothed data, time shifts aligning incidence and rainfall peaks, thresholding to account for any reliance on rainfall being above a certain value, and combinations of these approaches. While at first this result was surprising, further inspection revealed that the discrepancy stems from differences in the shapes of the incidence and precipitation curves: while OROV incidence initially rises alongside precipitation, it later declines despite sustained precipitation levels. This finding suggests that precipitation alone cannot explain outbreak timing or intensity, even though it still likely plays an indirect role in shaping midge behavior and outbreak severity.

The 2023–2024 OROV outbreak in Amazonas was significantly affected by seasonality, and our model suggests the impact is best explained by a single exponentially decaying pulse in the vector recruitment rate. Qualitatively, the exponential function outperformed the others in capturing the peak incidence, the shape of the rise and decline at the beginning and end of the epidemic, and the timing of outbreak onset (just as the driving force hits its peak and begins to decay). This result implies that relatively small environmental changes may substantially amplify midge abundance and, in turn, transmission potential. Such a pulse is not compatible with precipitation, temperature, or humidity data individually, so any environmental drivers must be entangled in a relatively complex way worthy of further study. Moreover, simulations applying seasonality to midge recruitment (model version (M2)) performed better, in general, than when applied to biting behavior ((M1) and (M3)). Although not definitive, previous work has noted that C. paraensis abundance is positively correlated with accumulated rainfall, which may help explain this result (Aybar et al. 2011). While the exact parameters obtained in the best fit simulation are likely not transferable to future outbreaks, these results provide insight into the expected qualitative shape and behavior of potential incidence curves.

The clustering of several of the parameters around identifiable intervals indicates that the data provide some information for narrowing plausible parameter values, and the alignment of the best-fit parameter set with these clusters supports the consistency of our estimation approach. The sensitivity analysis highlights that p, the proportion of time humans spend in the urban area, plays a large role in determining EP, with small perturbations leading to substantial changes in model output. This implies that uncertainty in p could greatly affect model predictions. In contrast, other parameters exhibit relatively little influence on EP within the tested range, suggesting that small errors in their estimation may have limited consequences for the model’s EP.

This work is a first step toward understanding the mechanisms underlying OROV transmission; however, some limitations should be noted. Our model assumes a single representative sylvatic host (sloths) and vector (C. paraensis) species, despite evidence that other wildlife (e.g., birds, monkeys) and vectors (e.g., mosquitoes) may also play important roles (Sakkas et al. 2018). We also omitted temporary immunity in human and sylvatic hosts, though recent evidence suggests it may exist (Scachetti et al. 2024). In addition, whether the exponential-pulse recruitment pattern we observed reflects a general seasonal mechanism or is specific to Amazonas is not clear and will require testing the model against additional outbreaks and in other endemic regions. Relatedly, although the three closed-form seasonality functions each contain the same number of parameters and are therefore equivalently flexible, the precipitation-based functions were constructed by interpolating observed data rather than fitting a prescribed functional form. As a result, their structure may not be directly comparable to the general forcing functions in terms of shape and adaptability. With respect to the outbreak incidence data, the assumption that exactly 40% of cases are reported is a gross simplification that, although appropriate for the purpose of an initial study, could certainly be replaced with a formal observation model in future studies. More broadly, our parameter estimation and model comparison relied on least squares fitting rather than a likelihood-based framework. This was a modeling choice intended to prioritize identification of seasonal drivers of OROV transmission given limited surveillance data and accuracy of the reporting process. Likelihood-based approaches require specifying an explicit observation model, including assumptions about the distribution of reporting error and case detection, which are not yet well characterized for OROV outbreaks. While least squares fitting allowed us to compare candidate seasonal forcing mechanisms without additional assumptions about the observation process, this approach does not provide the same formal statistical basis for model comparison as likelihood-based methods. Finally, we did not perform a formal structural identifiability analysis of the model parameters. A comprehensive identifiability analysis along with reformulation of the model in terms of identifiable parameter combinations would provide a more rigorous assessment of whether parameters can be uniquely determined from the available data and our model structure.

Future work should address these limitations by incorporating a recovered class for humans and potentially sylvatic hosts, which could alter the shape of the simulated incidence curve. Expanding the host and vector community could also be important for capturing incidence trends and predicting the geographic spread of the disease, as birds are known to distribute diseases as they migrate between regions (Rappole and Hubálek 2003; Reed et al. 2003). A third direction is to account for human seasonality. While we did not find concrete evidence supporting this, movement patterns may differ between the rainy and dry seasons, specifically in the time spent in or near sylvatic patches. Incorporating this information into the p parameter (found to be the one to which EP is most sensitive at the best-fit values) could yield new insights into transmission dynamics. Lastly, future studies should incorporate likelihood-based frameworks to enable a more thorough model comparison. Implementing such approaches will require development of an observation model that assumes a distribution for case counts. This will be more feasible as additional outbreak and surveillance data become available. Integrating these methods will strengthen the modeling framework presented here and improve its predictability.

Overall, the model presented in this work provides a foundational framework for understanding the complex ecological and seasonal factors driving OROV transmission and can be applied to other arboviruses with similar sylvatic-urban transmission cycles. As OROV continues to cause large-scale outbreaks, extensive data collection and model refinement will be essential for improving outbreak prediction.
