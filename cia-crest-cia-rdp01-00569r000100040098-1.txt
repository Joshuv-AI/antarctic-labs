World War II Service by OSS Civilians

1. Recruitment

During the growth and development period of the OSS, the pro-
curement and selection of personnel, both, civilian and military , was
a decentralized activity. Each branch recruited its own people by
its own methods without regard to the activities or needs of any
other branch. This decentralized system, however, was considered
inappropriate for the large organization the OSS had become in the
year following its creation.^ Accordingly, on 26 October 1943,
a Personnel Procurement Branch (PPB) was created in order to es-
tablish centralized recruiting procedures, 3 and, per the 26 May
1944 revision of General Order No. 9,* the PPB was charged with the
responsibility for recruiting all OSS personnel. 4 Later, the
December 1944 revision of General Order No. 9 created a Deputy
Director, Personnel, whose responsibilities included the establish-
ment of policies and procedures for personnel procurement and
administration, and the maintenance of liaison with the Civil Service
Commission, The Deputy Director, Personnel, was also charged with
the supervision and direction of the PPB and the Civilian Personnel
Branch (CPB). The CPB, which maintained close relations with the
Civil Service Commission, was responsible for the employment and
classification of civilian personnel. The PPB handled the ^
mechanics of recruitment and enrollment of all types of OSS
personnel. Through this branch, recruiting procedures were cen-
tralized and removed from the jurisdiction of the indiAfidual
branches. 3 On 13 June 1944, the PPB delegated to the CPB re-
cruitment of all civilian personnel, except agents, whose base
Compensation did not exceed $3,800 per annum (grade CAP 11). All
other civilian personnel were recruited by the PPB, unless the PPB
specifically delegated recruitment to the using branch, 6

* General Order No. 9, issued on 3 January 1943, mandated a
general reorganization of the OSS. 7

4 . / 5 . Te r m of E mploy ment

According to contracts located for special employees (agents)
hired in 1942-1943, terms of employment were for six months, unless
sooner terminated under provisions of the contract. Contracts
examined for agents hired in 1944-1945 showed terms extending through
the duration of hostilities and six months thereafter, unless sooner
terminated under contractual stipulations. In both instances,
employees were free to resign upon providing the OSS with 30 days'
written notice of their desired resignation.

Regarding non-agent employees, some served under temporary
appointments limited to one year or less. Others served under
appointments without time limitation. Still others, on a when-
actually-employed (WAE) basis. 8

6 . / 7 . Unifor m

Civilians were occasionally ordered to wear noncombatant
Army or Navy clothing, and office or branch chiefs were authorized
to requisition up to $200 per person from Special Funds to procure
such attire. 9 Civilians were also called upon to wear a military
uniform with rank or rating. The Special Operations Branch (SO) ,
for example, found that special operatives, in most cases, could
best be protected in the field by wearing such a uniform; consequently
SO agents were mostly paid by the Army through OSS detachments .

8 • Pa y Scale

The salary of OSS Civil Service personnel conformed as nearly
as possible to Civil Service salaries for similar duties or positions.
The salary of secret agents, whose salaries were paid by the
Special Funds (SF) Branch, was also paid with some conformity to
Civil Service classifications . ^ ^ Some idea of the pay range for these
latter personnel may be gleaned from the fact that the Secret
Intelligence (SI) Branch (which employed most of the agents paid by
the SF Branch) paid its agents an average of approximately $3,600 per
annum. None received more than $5,000 per annum. 1 ^

9 . Service 0 v e rseas

At the point of its maximum expansion, in December 1944,
the OSS employed some 13,000 personnel, of which approximately
7, 500- -both military and civilian- -served overseas. 14 No breakdown
was located as to how many of these were civilian personnel; nor
was any over -all figure found for the number of OSS civilians who
served overseas.
