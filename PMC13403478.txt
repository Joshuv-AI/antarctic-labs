High Childhood Leukemia Mortality Rates in Mexico Associated to Water Contamination in Areas of Oil and Gas Extraction
Authors: Madeleine Nicolas, Elizabeth Ortiz Gutiérrez, Jesús Andrés Barranco Pérez, Omar Amador‐Muñoz, Jaqueline Calderón Hernández, Elena R. Álvarez‐Buylla, Juan Carlos Martínez‐García
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the leading cause of death in Mexican children, yet water‐mediated environmental pathways remain largely unexplored as contributors to its spatial distribution. We analyzed ALL mortality records for individuals aged 0–19 years across all Mexican municipalities from 2003 to 2023, using publicly available national death statistics and population estimates. Age‐specific mortality rates were calculated at the municipal level and aggregated to the state level to characterize broad geographic gradients. A discrete Poisson spatial scan statistic, implemented in SaTScan, identified 10 statistically significant mortality clusters, five with elevated risk and five with reduced risk, which were then compared against hydrogeological and industrial variables derived from global remote sensing and infrastructure databases. The national mortality rate averaged 1.64 per 100,000 children aged 0–19, with state‐level rates ranging more than two‐fold from 1.01 in Durango to 2.35 in Tabasco. High‐mortality clusters are concentrated along the Gulf of Mexico coast and southeastern states; low‐mortality zones lie predominantly in the arid west and northwest. High‐mortality areas are systematically characterized by greater annual precipitation, lower aridity, permeable sedimentary geology, and higher forest cover—landscape conditions that collectively maximize infiltration and groundwater contamination risk. All five high‐mortality clusters spatially overlap with oil and gas infrastructure, with three coinciding with zones of intensive extraction along the Gulf coast. These findings suggest that groundwater vulnerability and industrial contamination, rather than genetic predisposition, are primary spatial determinants of childhood ALL mortality in Mexico, pointing toward preventable, structurally driven disease burden.

Introduction

Childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) represents approximately one‐third of all pediatric cancers worldwide and accounts for nearly 40% of cancer deaths in children under 15 years (Namayandeh et al., 2020; Siegel et al., 2012). ALL is the predominant childhood leukemia subtype, accounting for approximately 78% of cases. Its incidence is especially high in low‐ and middle‐income countries, where approximately 83% of childhood cancer diagnoses and 94% of childhood cancer deaths occur (Bhakta et al., 2019). The highest global incidence rates have been documented in Honduras, Mexico, and Dominica (Dong et al., 2020). This disproportionate burden suggests contextual factors contributing to elevated disease rates in these regions.

In Mexico, childhood cancer ranks as the leading cause of disease‐related death in children aged 5–14 years and sixth leading cause in children under 5 years (Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, 2019). ALL is the most frequent cancer type in Mexican children, accounting for over 60% of hospital discharges for malignant neoplasms in children and adolescents (INEGI, 2021). Mexico City exhibits the highest documented ALL incidence rates globally (Flores‐Lujano et al., 2022; Pérez‐Saldivar et al., 2011), with national mortality rates having increased from 1.6 to 1.7 per 100,000 inhabitants aged 0–19 years old between 1998 and 2018 (Muñoz‐Aguirre et al., 2022).

The Mexican government has implemented initiatives against leukemia through the National Strategic Program (Pronaces) to improve early diagnosis, treatment access, and clinical follow‐up, achieving sustained mortality reductions (CONAHCYT, 2023b). With adequate treatment and timely diagnosis, ALL can reach survival rates near 90% (American Cancer Society, 2025). However, these interventions have focused predominantly on clinical management, leaving research regarding environmental factors relatively underexplored.

Multiple studies across Spain, Colombia, Iran, and Mexico have identified non‐random spatial patterns of childhood leukemia, with cases frequently clustering in urban and industrial areas, reinforcing hypotheses that environmental exposures may play a more significant role than previously considered (Calderon‐Hernandez et al., 2023; Cárceles‐Álvarez et al., 2017; Duarte‐Rodríguez et al., 2024; Rahimi Pordanjani et al., 2020; Rodriguez‐Villamizar et al., 2020).

Environmental risk factors most frequently studied in relation to childhood ALL include ionizing radiation, volatile organic compounds like benzene and chlorinated solvents, pesticides, air pollution, high‐voltage electromagnetic fields, and viral infections during early development (Calvente et al., 2010; Carlos‐Wallace et al., 2016; Filippini et al., 2019; Freedman et al., 2001; O'Connor & Boneva, 2007; Van Maele‐Fabry et al., 2011).

Despite extensive research on atmospheric and occupational exposures, water as an environmental pathway remains notably underexplored. This omission is significant given that water constitutes a chronic and ubiquitous potential exposure route: dissolved contaminants can be ingested, dermally absorbed, or inhaled during routine activities such as bathing or cooking, allowing continuous organism entry during critical developmental stages. Oil and gas exploitation, particularly unconventional extraction methods (e.g., hydraulic fracturing), represent a particularly concerning exposure pathway. Elliott et al. (2017) identified 49 compounds classified as carcinogenic by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) in hydraulic fracturing fluids and wastewater, including 17 associated with increased leukemia or lymphoma risk, such as benzene, 1,3‐butadiene, cadmium, diesel exhaust, and several polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons.

Additionally, historical episodes demonstrate plausible water‐leukemia linkages. In Woburn, Massachusetts, maternal exposure during pregnancy to municipal wells contaminated with trichloroethylene was associated with increased childhood leukemia risk (Lagakos et al., 1986). In Toms River, New Jersey, decades of industrial discharges were associated with the presence in the water supply of several compounds with carcinogenic potential, including trichloroethylene, styrene‐acrylonitrile trimer, epichlorohydrin, benzidine, and naphthalene (New Jersey Department of Health and Senior Services (NJDHSS), 2003), which had contaminated the local aquifer and were linked to a childhood leukemia cluster, particularly in girls under 5 years of age. In Ellweiler, Germany, exposure to radium‐226 through drinking water contaminated with uranium processing residues generated cumulative doses in children's bone marrow sufficient to explain an observed case cluster (Hoffmann et al., 1993).

In Mexico, structural factors complicate effective contamination prevention. For instance, the Atoyac River basin, one of the country's most polluted fluvial systems, receives industrial and municipal discharges transporting solvents, metals, and organic matter. Local communities have reported elevated rates of chronic diseases, including childhood leukemia (CONAHCYT, 2023a; Montero‐Montoya et al., 2026). Despite these persistent contamination hotspots, very few studies have examined the linkages between childhood leukemia and water quality or vulnerability to water contamination.

Our study addresses this gap by examining spatial patterns of childhood ALL mortality across Mexico at the municipal level, the finest administrative resolution at which consistent mortality and environmental data converge. This scale allows detection of localized exposure‐outcome associations that coarser aggregations would dilute. We evaluate the relationship between these spatial patterns and groundwater vulnerability factors and large‐scale contamination as potential water contamination sources.

Materials and Methods

Study Area and Period

We analyzed childhood ALL mortality across all 2,478 municipalities in Mexico's 32 states for the period 2003–2023, focusing on deaths in individuals aged 0–19 years. Mortality was selected as the primary outcome measure because it is recorded consistently at the municipal level through a single national registry, providing nationwide spatial coverage at fine resolution throughout the study period.

Mortality and Population Data

Mortality records were obtained from the National Institute of Statistics and Geography (INEGI) Registered Death Statistics (EDR), coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD‐10), considering only codes corresponding to ALL (C91.0). Population estimates were obtained from the National Population Council (CONAPO). Municipal geographic boundaries were obtained from INEGI's Geographic Metadata Catalog (Municipal Political Division, 1:250,000, 2023). Death counts and population estimates were joined to their corresponding municipal geometries and mortality rates were calculated at the municipal level using R v4.5.0 (R Core Team, 2025); more detail on the packages used is available in the Data Availability Statement. The resulting georeferenced rates were then mapped in QGIS version 3.34.12 (QGIS Development Team, 2026) to produce the spatial distribution of childhood ALL mortality shown in Figure 1.

Environmental Data

Environmental variables were selected to represent groundwater vulnerability by examining the principal landscape‐scale drivers of the water cycle—land cover, geology, and climate—under the hypothesis that these characteristics may help elucidate the environmental context of areas with particularly high or low ALL mortality. On the other hand, characterizing sources of carcinogenic exposure at a national scale represents a significant methodological challenge, as such compounds are generated by a wide range of industries and are rarely consolidated into centralized databases. Oil and gas infrastructure constitutes an exception: it is a confirmed source of ALL‐associated carcinogens, a well‐documented environmental pollutant, and one for which georeferenced global inventories exist, making it uniquely tractable for large‐scale spatial analysis. This approach enables systematic spatial evaluation that, although not encompassing all potential contamination sources, offers a useful first approximation for exploring how certain territorial configurations may coincide with higher ALL incidence. All data sets were chosen to prioritize global coverage, public accessibility, and high spatial resolution, with the objective of enabling methodological replication in other national or subnational settings. A full description of each data set is provided in Table 1.

All environmental analyses were conducted in Google Earth Engine (GEE), where the frequency distribution of pixel values for each environmental variable was computed within the boundaries of high‐ and low‐mortality clusters and normalized by total pixel count, expressing the proportional area of each value class within each cluster. Results were exported from GEE for visualization: spatial distributions were also mapped in QGIS, frequency distributions of continuous variables were visualized as column charts in Microsoft Excel version 16.78.3 (Microsoft Corporation, 2019), and the proportional composition of discrete variables was represented as pie charts produced in R, with more detail available in the Data Availability section.

Spatial Analysis

Mortality Rate Calculation

Age‐specific mortality rates per 100,000 inhabitants were calculated for each municipality by dividing mean annual ALL deaths among individuals aged 0–19 years by mean annual population estimates for the same age group across the 2003–2023 study period, following the equation (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2012): MeanannualdeathsinspecifiedagegroupMeanannualpopulationinspecifiedagegroup×100,000

Averaging over 20 years produces more stable estimates than single‐year rates, which is particularly important at the municipal scale where annual death counts for a rare disease such as ALL are frequently zero or in the single digits. State‐level rates were obtained by summing municipal mean deaths and mean populations across all constituent municipalities before dividing.

Spatial Cluster Analysis

To identify spatially contiguous areas of statistically significant high or low ALL mortality, we employed the spatial scan statistic (Kulldorff, 1997), implemented in SaTScan® version 10.2 (Kulldorff, 2024). We applied the discrete Poisson model, under which the number of observed cases in each geographic unit is assumed to follow a Poisson distribution with an expected value proportional to the at‐risk population. This model is the standard choice for spatially aggregated count data involving rare events and variable population denominators, and has been widely applied in cancer cluster analyses where expected counts may be small (Meliker et al., 2009; Sherman et al., 2014). The null hypothesis posits spatial homogeneity of risk, such that the ratio of observed to expected cases shows no statistically significant spatial variation. The spatial analysis was structured around a contrast between municipalities where observed ALL mortality exceeded the national expectation and those where it fell below it (i.e., of particularly high and low mortality), with the objective of identifying environmental and hydrological conditions associated with each pattern.

SaTScan® v10.2 received three inputs: annual ALL death counts, annual population estimates, and the geographic coordinates of each municipal centroid derived from the INEGI boundary shapefile, all restricted to individuals aged 0–19 years and disaggregated at the municipal level. The scan statistic evaluates all circular windows of varying radius centered on each municipal centroid, comparing observed cases within each window against the count expected under spatial homogeneity of risk. For each window, a log‐likelihood ratio quantifies whether the observed spatial concentration of cases is more consistent with elevated local risk than with the null hypothesis of no clustering; the window yielding the highest log‐likelihood ratio is identified as the primary cluster. Statistical significance is then determined by comparing this maximum log‐likelihood ratio against a reference distribution generated by 9,999 Monte Carlo replications under the null hypothesis; clusters where p < 0.05 were retained. Both high and low‐mortality clusters were identified, with the maximum cluster size set to 10% of total observed cases and a minimum of five cases required per cluster, with annual data aggregated internally across the full 2003–2023 study period under a purely spatial scanning design.

Results

General Patterns: Municipal and State‐Level Maps

Figure 1 presents the municipal‐level mortality map for ALL in individuals aged 0–19 years, averaged across 2003–2023. The map reveals substantial spatial heterogeneity, but discerning coherent geographic patterns at this resolution is difficult. This is partly an artifact of the small numbers problem: at the municipal scale, ALL is a rare event, and annual death counts are frequently zero or in the single digits, which can produce extreme apparent rates in sparsely populated municipalities that reflect statistical noise rather than true differences in risk (CONAHCYT, 2023c). This limitation motivates two complementary analytical strategies applied in the sections that follow: aggregation to the state level to describe broad spatial gradients, and a formal spatial scan analysis to identify statistically significant zones of mortality higher or lower than expected given the population present.

At the state level (Figure 2), aggregate rates range from 2.35 per 100,000 in Tabasco to 1.01 per 100,000 in Durango against a national average of 1.64 per 100,000, a more than two‐fold difference between the extremes. The three states with the highest mortality (Tabasco, Quintana Roo, and Yucatán) are all located in the southeast, while the three lowest (Durango, Sinaloa, and Guerrero) are in the west and northwest. This broad geographic contrast motivates the environmental comparisons that follow.

Environmental Characteristics of High‐ and Low‐Mortality Zones

Groundwater Vulnerability: Climatic and Geological Conditions

High‐mortality clusters and low‐mortality areas differ systematically across all environmental variables examined, in a pattern consistent with differential groundwater vulnerability, that is, the degree to which landscape conditions facilitate the transport of surface contaminants to the water resources that populations depend on.

Climatically, high‐mortality clusters occupy substantially wetter environments. Mean annual precipitation within these zones has a median of approximately 1,230 mm (IQR: 1,070–1,870 mm), compared to 810 mm (IQR: 490–1,060 mm) in low‐mortality areas. High‐mortality clusters also exhibit lower aridity indices, indicating greater net water availability after accounting for evapotranspiration losses (Figure 4), climatic characteristics typically associated with higher infiltration, aquifer recharge, and surface runoff—the processes that physically connect surface contamination to drinking water sources.

Climatic differences in annual precipitation and aridity between high mortality zones (in red) and low mortality zones (in blue). Precipitation data are from the WorldClim database (Fick & Hijmans, 2017) and aridity data are derived from TerraClimate (Abatzoglou et al., 2018).

Surface conditions reinforce this contrast (Figure 5). High‐mortality clusters are dominated by sedimentary formations, typically porous and permeable, and by forest land cover, associated with greater soil infiltration capacity and aquifer recharge. Low‐mortality areas show higher proportions of extrusive igneous rocks, generally less permeable than sedimentary rocks, and herbaceous covers characteristic of drier environments, where reduced hydrological activity limits contaminant mobility. Cluster 9 (high‐mortality) and Cluster 2 (low‐mortality) are particularly instructive in this regard: both are geographically large zones, but they represent opposite ends of the environmental spectrum. Cluster 9 exhibits high precipitation, low aridity, predominantly sedimentary geology, and substantial forest cover, conditions collectively maximizing infiltration and contaminant transport. Cluster 2, of broadly comparable spatial extent, shows the inverse profile: lower precipitation, higher aridity, less permeable geology, and more arid land cover types. The smaller high‐mortality clusters and low‐mortality areas largely follow the same environmental logic, though at finer spatial scales. Of the variables examined, geological composition and aridity show the most distinct differentiation between high‐ and low‐mortality zones; precipitation is intermediate; and land cover presents the weakest but directionally consistent contrast.

Discussion

Mortality as a Proxy for Incidence

Lack of access to incidence data obliged basing analysis exclusively on ALL mortality records. While incidence data more precisely capture disease magnitude (Bray & Parkin, 2009), mortality records are considered the best indicator of public health progress against cancer (National Cancer Institute, 2025), and their accuracy as epidemiological instruments has been well established (German et al., 2011). However, mortality rates reflect not only disease incidence but also case‐fatality, which is sensitive to the timeliness of diagnosis and quality of treatment access—factors that vary independently of environmental exposure (Abrahão et al., 2015; Castro‐Ríos et al., 2019). Thus, although death data are reliable and offer a valid approximation for analyzing certain spatial patterns, they must be interpreted cautiously and framed within an integrated approach recognizing these structural limitations. These limitations underscore the need for the Mexican government to strengthen its cancer surveillance infrastructure, particularly through the development of a nationwide population‐based cancer registry capable of providing reliable incidence estimates across the country (Lazcano‐Ponce et al., 2016).

Water‐Mediated Pathways in Childhood ALL Risk

Groundwater Vulnerability and Spatial Patterning of Mortality

Three principal mechanisms link hydrology to contaminant transport: advection (bulk movement of dissolved substances with flowing water), diffusion (molecular‐scale solute spreading along concentration gradients), and dispersion (mixing during flow through heterogeneous porous media; Fetter et al., 2018). Together, these processes govern how efficiently a landscape translates a contamination source into a population exposure. In arid settings where potential evapotranspiration exceeds precipitation, limited water availability suppresses all three, constraining both the volume of contaminants mobilized and the spatial extent of their reach. In humid regions, sustained precipitation drives infiltration, aquifer recharge, and surface runoff, amplifying the hydrological system's capacity to dissolve and carry pollutants from source to receptor.

Our cluster analysis reveals that this mechanism operates at a national scale in Mexico. High‐mortality zones exhibit higher mean annual precipitation, lower aridity indices, permeable sedimentary geology, and greater forest cover, conditions that collectively maximize infiltration and groundwater recharge. Low‐mortality clusters, by contrast, occupy more arid environments with sparse vegetation and geologically confined aquifers, where natural barriers suppress contaminant mobilization. Recharge rates—and thus contaminant transport—are known to increase with forest cover and vegetated land surfaces (Robinson et al., 2022; Scanlon et al., 1997), decrease under arid conditions where evapotranspiration limits infiltration (Kumar et al., 2020), and vary with subsurface geology, particularly the porosity and permeability of sedimentary formations (Gleeson et al., 2011). The systematic contrast between high‐ and low‐mortality zones in our data suggests that landscape structure does in fact govern population‐level exposure to environmental carcinogens through its control over hydrological connectivity. This interpretation is supported by broader evidence from other geographic contexts: in the United States, counties with higher precipitation exhibit significantly elevated invasive cancer incidence rates, with precipitation proposed as a pathway governing contaminant transport and bioavailability (Shah et al., 2019). At the global scale, a study of 33 cancer types across 37 countries found that while mean long‐term precipitation showed no direct association with cancer incidence, accelerating rates of precipitation change were associated with higher incidence of several cancer types, with increased water contamination proposed as a plausible mediating pathway (H. Wang et al., 2024).

The southeastern clusters in our analysis represent a particularly acute expression of this vulnerability. Cluster 9—spanning Campeche, Quintana Roo, Chiapas, and adjacent states (RR: 1.33, p < 0.0001)—coincides with the Yucatan Peninsula's karst limestone geology, where highly soluble carbonate formations develop preferential flow pathways through dissolution, enabling contaminants to bypass natural filtration and reach underlying aquifers rapidly (González‐Herrera et al., 2002). A continental‐scale analysis of carbonate aquifer systems found that focused recharge through karst is the primary driver of rapid contaminant transport to groundwater, and that contamination risk in these systems is substantially underestimated by conventional hydrogeological models (Hartmann et al., 2021). Where communities depend on these aquifers for drinking water, as is common throughout the Yucatan, karst hydrology creates conditions for sustained and geographically widespread population exposure.

Contaminant Hazard: Oil and Gas Infrastructure as a Risk Amplifier

The groundwater vulnerability described above takes on particular epidemiological significance when considered alongside the spatial distribution of industrial contamination sources. All five high mortality clusters overlap with oil and gas infrastructure of some kind; three of these—Clusters 6, 8, and 9, located along the Gulf of Mexico—coincide with zones of particularly intensive hydrocarbon extraction, including documented conventional and unconventional (e.g., hydraulic fracturing) operations with known toxic emissions to air, soil, and water (Gold‐Bouchot, 2022; Villalobos‐Hiriart et al., 2020).

Conventional petroleum exploitation generates well‐documented carcinogenic byproducts; unconventional operations further introduce dozens of chemical compounds with documented carcinogenicity (Elliott et al., 2017). Furthermore, the epidemiological evidence linking oil and gas development to childhood ALL risk has accumulated progressively. A systematic review concluded that residential proximity to unconventional oil and gas operations is associated with elevated childhood leukemia risk, with water contamination identified as the plausible primary pathway (Elliott et al., 2017). Population‐based case‐control studies in Colorado and Pennsylvania have since found dose‐response relationships, with children living closest to well sites bearing two‐to nearly three‐fold increased odds of ALL compared to unexposed children (Clark et al., 2022; McKenzie et al., 2025). Regional precedents exist in Latin America: children under 15 in Ecuadorian Amazon counties with active oil exploitation showed leukemia risks approximately 2.5 times those of children in non‐oil‐producing counties in the same region (Hurtig & San Sebastián, 2004). At a broader scale, a nationwide analysis found consistent dose‐response relationships between surface water pollution—including petroleum compounds—and cancer incidence across multiple cancer types, suggesting that water quality itself functions as a spatial determinant of cancer burden (Z. Wang et al., 2023).

How much of a surface hazard ultimately translates into population exposure depends substantially on local hydrogeological conditions: aquifer depth, confinement, recharge rates, and lithology all govern contaminant transport. This has been demonstrated in the context of unconventional oil and gas development in the Appalachian Basin, where aquifer properties were significant determinants of contamination risk at population scale (Soriano et al., 2022), but the principle applies broadly across contaminant types and geologic settings (Fetter et al., 2018). Direct epidemiological evidence linking hydrogeological properties to cancer outcomes remains scarce; however, a groundwater modeling study in Cape Cod found that aquifer flow pathways connecting wastewater effluent to drinking water wells were associated with elevated breast cancer risk—demonstrating that geology can mediate the relationship between a contamination source and a cancer outcome (Gallagher et al., 2010).

Overall, the convergence of Gulf coast oil and gas infrastructure with the high precipitation, permeable geology, and rapid aquifer recharge that characterize our high‐mortality clusters, creates conditions in which industrial contaminants at the surface are efficiently transferred to groundwater resources vulnerable populations depend on.

Nonetheless, our emphasis on water should not obscure the established role of air pollution in childhood leukemia etiology. Benzene and NO2 are well‐documented risk factors (Filippini et al., 2019), and industrial activity elevates contaminant loads across both media simultaneously, with high cancer burden concentrated in areas of highest co‐exposure (Jiang et al., 2024). We focus on water not to displace air pollution as a recognized risk factor but because the two pathways are best understood jointly, and because water‐mediated exposure remains comparatively understudied, particularly in the Mexican context.

Nature Versus Nurture

Elevated childhood ALL rates among Hispanic and Latino populations have frequently been attributed to genetic predisposition, with Indigenous American ancestry proposed as a biological explanation for disparities in incidence observed in these groups and the regions they inhabit (De Smith et al., 2024; Flores‐Lujano et al., 2024). Our findings suggest that environmental factors play a substantially more important role than this framing recognizes.

The genetic argument warrants scrutiny on several grounds. First, genetic variants associated with ALL susceptibility are permissive rather than sufficient causes: disease development requires both an inherited predisposition and an environmental trigger, a gene‐environment interaction framework supported by evidence from theoretical models and Mexican pediatric populations specifically (Greaves, 2018; Medina‐Sanson et al., 2020). Second, the magnitude of the genetic contribution to observed disparities appears to have been substantially overestimated. In the United States, socioeconomic status mediates between 31% and 44% of racial and ethnic ALL survival disparities—accounting for roughly a third to nearly half of the gap between Black and Hispanic children and their White counterparts—indicating that much of what appears to be a biological signal is in fact a social one (Kehm et al., 2018). Most strikingly, a recent large multi‐cohort genetic study of Hispanic and Latino children in the United States found that while Indigenous American ancestry appeared to increase ALL risk, this association disappeared entirely after adjusting for household income and parental education—suggesting that what had previously been interpreted as a genetic signal was largely a reflection of the socioeconomic circumstances correlated with ancestry rather than ancestry itself (Langie et al., 2026).

This reframing has direct spatial implications. Populations identified in genetic studies as high‐risk frequently overlap with communities experiencing socioeconomic disadvantage and its associated environmental burdens: greater proximity to pollution sources, higher toxic exposures across multiple pathways, and reduced capacity to escape or remediate these conditions due to racial segregation and economic marginalization (Brulle & Pellow, 2006). Carcinogenic exposures are not distributed randomly but are inequitably concentrated among low‐income and racialized communities (Larsen et al., 2023), suggesting that socioeconomic position shapes ALL risk primarily through differential environmental exposure rather than genetic ancestry per se. Climate vulnerability operates through the same structural logic: marginalized communities are disproportionately susceptible to climate‐driven contamination pathways, and extreme weather events compound this burden by directly disrupting access to cancer care and worsening survival outcomes (Ashad‐Bishop et al., 2023). Consistent with this, studies in the United States have found larger increases in cancer incidence among Black, Hispanic, and Asian populations in high climate‐impact regions compared to their White counterparts, across multiple cancer types including breast, lung, and melanoma (Johnson et al., 2026), suggesting that the structural concentration of environmental risk, rather than any biological characteristic, is the operative mechanism.

An environmental interpretation also opens a dimension of prevention that a genetic one precludes. Carcinogenic exposures of environmental and occupational origin are, by definition, modifiable, and a substantial proportion of cancers attributable to such exposures are preventable through targeted exposure reduction, regardless of the socioeconomic or ethnic background of the affected population (Espina et al., 2013). Prioritizing environmental determinants of ALL risk therefore shifts the frame from an immutable biological characteristic to an actionable structural one, with implications not only for understanding disease etiology but for averting it.

Limitations and Future Directions

Our study is cartographic rather than statistical: it documents spatial co‐occurrence between mortality clusters, groundwater vulnerability indicators, and oil and gas infrastructure, but does not quantify these associations or control for confounders. The analysis is best understood as a first cartography of a complex problem rather than a definitive conclusion. By demonstrating that spatial patterns of ALL mortality in Mexico align with groundwater vulnerability and industrial contamination rather than with genetic structure, it opens a research route that local and regional investigators are well positioned to deepen. The ultimate objective is to conceive childhood ALL not as genetic fatality or statistical coincidence, but as a concrete and in many cases preventable manifestation of accumulated socio‐environmental inequalities—and to reorient research and public policy accordingly.

Several research priorities follow directly from this work. Direct water quality monitoring targeted to specific carcinogens, including those associated with petroleum operations, would allow the contamination pathways hypothesized in this analysis to be empirically confirmed. Biomarker studies in affected populations would further establish individual‐level exposure, while higher‐resolution analyses in Mexico City, which exhibits among the highest childhood ALL incidence rates documented globally (Pérez‐Saldivar et al., 2011), could disentangle the urban dynamics obscured at national scale. Distinguishing prenatal, perinatal, and early childhood exposure windows represents a critical additional step, given the developmental sensitivity of leukemogenesis.
