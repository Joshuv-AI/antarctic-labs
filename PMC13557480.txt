Establishing a Multicenter Personalized Medicine Program in Childhood, Adolescent, and Young Adult Cancer in Spain: The SEHOP-PENCIL Project

ABSTRACT
Pediatric cancer care in Spain lacked a national personalized medicine program. The SEHOP-PENCIL initiative, established by the SEHOP, was designed to address this gap. A national survey identified the genomic sequencing needs of hospitals treating pediatric patients with cancer. Results showed improved access to targeted next-generation sequencing panels but revealed persistent variability in implementation, limited availability of advanced sequencing (whole-exome sequencing, whole-genome sequencing, RNA sequencing, and DNA methylation profiling), and gaps in cancer predisposition clinics and molecular tumor boards (MTBs). SEHOP-PENCIL is organized as a network of 10 specialized genomic centers serving 45 hospitals through a centralized inclusion system. The program standardizes patient eligibility, genomic workflows, and result interpretation, supported by a national MTB. This framework enables informed clinical decision making, ensures access to molecular diagnosis and innovative therapies, and supports systematic data collection to advance pediatric cancer research and personalized care. SEHOP-PENCIL represents a pioneering national model for integrating precision oncology into pediatric cancer care in Spain. By fostering collaboration, standardizing genomic practices, and promoting equitable access, it aims to reduce disparities and improve outcomes, offering a scalable example for other decentralized health care systems.

INTRODUCTION

The concept of personalized cancer medicine is based on the principle that thorough molecular characterization of each patient's tumor will directly contribute to a more accurate diagnosis, risk classification and open the door to targeted therapies.

Over the past decade, multiple personalized medicine programs for children and adolescents with cancer have emerged around the world,1,2 including MAPPYACTS in France,3 INFORM in Germany,4 iTHER in the Netherlands,5 Ped-MATCH6 and iCAT7 in the United States, SMPaeds in the United Kingdom,8 GMS in Sweden,9 or ZERO in Australia,10,11 with some countries having already implemented comprehensive genomic sequencing as part of the routine clinical health care system.

For example, France was among the first countries to implement genomic medicine within its national health system, through the French government's investment in the French Plan for Genomic Medicine 2025, launched in 201612,13 with pediatric cancer indication incorporated in March 2020.14 Similarly, in England, the National Health Service has offered whole-genome sequencing (WGS) since January 2021 to every child with a suspected neoplastic disorder.15,16 Other countries, including Australia and Sweden, are transitioning from research programs to national standards of care, with costs increasingly absorbed by their health care systems.17-19

A survey conducted in October 2020 by the SEHOP revealed substantial barriers to access advanced sequencing techniques for pediatric patients with cancer in Spain where, for instance, only 41% (12 of 29) of hospitals had access to next-generation panel sequencing (NGS) at diagnosis and 62% (18 of 29) at the time of relapse. The most frequently used technique was NGS panels, and in most hospitals, samples were referred to a reference center for the performance of these studies.20 The survey disclosed a heterogeneous development of precision medicine across hospitals and regions, largely driven by single-institution programs funded primarily through philanthropic initiatives and research grants. In the absence of a coordinated national strategy, these fragmented efforts have resulted in significant inequalities in the implementation of personalized medicine across Spain.

In response to this unmet need, the SEHOP-PENCIL program was established to provide nationwide access to genomic analyses for all children, adolescents, and young adults with cancer in Spain. Developed by the SEHOP and coordinated by the Vall d’Hebron Research Institute (VHIR), this multicenter initiative centralizes patient inclusion, genomic testing, and data harmonization to promote equity in pediatric oncology care across the country.

The aim of this study is to describe the design and implementation of the SEHOP-PENCIL program, detailing the process from the initial needs' assessment through a national survey to the establishment of a multicenter network for the incorporation of precision medicine in all Spanish hospitals.

PERSONALIZED MEDICINE PROGRAM IN CHILDHOOD CANCER IN SPAIN

Research Team Network

To address the lack of a national personalized medicine program comparable with those implemented in other countries, the SEHOP-PENCIL project was awarded €2 million through the Precision Medicine Implementation competitive grant (PMP21), funded by the Carlos III Health Institute (Instituto de Salud Carlos III, ISCIII) under the Ministry of Science, Innovation and Universities of Spain.

In 2022, this project established a cooperative network of 10 genomic centers from SEHOP member institutions, selected for their ability to perform advanced sequencing techniques previously unavailable in the Spanish health care system. A multidisciplinary team of over 100 professionals, including geneticists, molecular biologists, bioinformaticians, pediatric oncologists, pathologists, surgeons, genetic counsellors, radiologists, laboratory technicians, and project managers was assembled to deliver the initiative. In addition, the patient advocacy organization CRIS Cancer Foundation actively participated in the program, contributing to its design, dissemination, and alignment with patient-centered priorities (Appendix Table A1).

National Needs Assessment Survey

Once the network was established, the consortium's first step was to obtain a comprehensive overview of the current state of personalized medicine across Spanish hospitals to serve as a basis for the national personalized medicine program. For this purpose, a survey was distributed between December 2022 and February 2023 to pediatric oncology and hematology professionals working in SEHOP-affiliated hospitals. Using the REDCap data collection platform, information was gathered addressing the following areas: (a) Hospital characteristics; (b) use of NGS panels and exome, genome, and transcriptome sequencing in patients with solid tumors and leukemia, both at diagnosis and relapse; (c) use of DNA methylation profiling in tumors; (d) use of genetic studies to identify cancer predisposition; and (e) availability of molecular tumor boards (MTBs; Data Supplement, Document 1). The final database was analyzed by applying descriptive statistics methods using R software (v4.2.2).

Of the 45 hospitals contacted, 35 (78%) responded to the questionnaire. Of these, 32 (91%) provided care for patients with solid and hematologic tumors, 2 (6%) treated only hematologic tumors, and 1 (3%) hospital treated only solid tumors. Six hospitals (17%) managed more than 80 new patients per year, whereas 19 (54%) reported care for fewer than 30 patients per year.

NGS panels were available in most hospitals for both solid tumors (>76%) and leukemias/lymphomas (>65%) at diagnosis and relapse (Fig 1A). Among hospitals with access to NGS panels, approximately 80% applied them to selected solid tumors types at diagnosis, mainly central nervous system (CNS) tumors and sarcomas. For leukemias/lymphomas, half applied panels to all patients, whereas the other half restricted testing to selected cases. In both settings, there was a clear trend toward extending NGS testing to all patients at relapse (Table 1 and Appendix Table A2).

Most hospitals used commercial panels and performed testing in house although more than 35% continued to outsource the analysis to other centers. A critical limitation identified was the lack of standardization across institutions. For instance, 69% of hospitals used different commercial panels in solid tumors at diagnosis (for instance, Oncomine Childhood Cancer Research Assay [Thermo Fisher], Oncomine Focus Assay [Thermo Fisher], Oncomine Comprehensive Assay [Thermo Fisher], AmpliSeq for Illumina Childhood Cancer Panel [Illumina], SOPHiA DDM for Solid Tumors [Sophia Genetics], Action OncoKitDx [IMEGEN], Archer FUSIONPlex Sarcoma panel [Archer] or FoundationOne Heme [Foundation Medicine]) and 23% used custom-designed panels (Table 1 and Appendix Table A2).

Fewer than 30% of hospitals reported performing whole-exome sequencing (WES), WGS, or RNA sequencing (RNAseq) at either diagnosis or relapse (Fig 1B), and use was generally limited to specific clinical indications. Among hospitals performing deep profiling, RNAseq was the most frequently applied technique at diagnosis, primarily for the detection of gene fusions. At relapse, however, the use of WES and WGS increased (for instance, 11% at diagnosis to 30% at the time of relapse for WES in solid tumors). Testing was either performed in the hospital's own genomic laboratories or outsourced to external centers, but again, was not routinely conducted for all cases in any center (Table 1 and Appendix Table A3).

Funding for NGS studies was heterogeneous (Table 1). Targeted panels were primarily supported by the hospital's own service portfolio (54%-38%) and institutional funds (24%-22%), whereas WES, WGS, and RNAseq relied mainly on research projects and philanthropic support (67%-50%). Many centers used multiple funding sources to perform these analyses (Appendix Tables A2 and A3).

As with WES, WGS, and RNAseq, DNA methylation profiling was performed in few hospitals (33%-30%; Fig 1C), mainly for CNS tumors. Among hospitals that offered this testing, the majority (>64%) outsourced it to external centers and funding sources were highly variable (Table 1 and Appendix Table A4).

Pediatric MTBs were established in 40% of hospitals (14 of 35) (Fig 2A). Among hospitals without an MTB (21 of 35), 57% discussed cases at MTBs of other hospitals (Table 1 and Appendix Table A5).

Regarding studies for cancer predisposition, 60% of hospitals had access to germline NGS panels (Fig 1D). Candidate patients were selected based on clinical suspicion, MIPOGG,21 or Ripperger/Jongmans criteria22,23 without a nationally standardized approach. Commercial panels were used in 52% of hospitals, encompassing a wide range of assays (Appendix Table A6). WES and WGS were available in 26% of hospitals (Fig 1B), mainly for inconclusive cases. NGS panels, WES, and WGS were performed in house (71% and 56%, respectively) although a proportion of WES and WGS studies continued to be outsourced. Funding came from hospital portfolios, research grants, and philanthropic sources (Appendix Tables A6 and A7).

Over half of hospitals (19 of 35, 54%) lacked an in-house pediatric cancer predisposition clinic (ie, hereditary cancer or cancer genetics clinical service). Among the 40% with a clinic (Fig 2A and Table 1), most were combined adult-pediatric (71%) and provided both pre- and post-test counseling (93%). Of hospitals without a clinic, 63% did not refer patients elsewhere (Appendix Table A8). In addition, of the 14 hospitals with cancer predisposition clinics, 6 (42%) had a cancer predisposition board (ie, hereditary cancer or cancer genetics multidisciplinary board), three exclusively pediatric and three adult-pediatric, representing 17% (6 of 35) of the surveyed hospitals (Fig 2A). Among hospitals without a board (28 of 35), 61% did not present cases at boards of other hospitals (Appendix Table A9).

When asked about ranking unmet needs in the field, most hospitals highlighted the need to access advanced sequencing studies (WES, RNAseq, WGS) in patients at the time of relapse (23 of 35), followed by the possibility to discuss results in molecular expert boards (19 of 35) and access to methylation studies (17 of 35; Fig 2B).

In addition to the previously described genomic analysis, the project also includes the establishment of a national MTB for case discussions and a dedicated working group for in-depth evaluation of the clinical benefit of the precision medicine program in patients. Cases for the MTB are selected based on specific criteria, including (i) cases requiring therapeutic recommendations, particularly relapses; (ii) diagnostically challenging cases; (iii) cases of significant scientific or clinical interest; (iv) germline findings requiring discussion regarding referral to genetic counseling for suspected hereditary cancer predisposition; and (v) requests from the treating clinician when clarification of results is needed. Furthermore, two additional working groups are focused on the analysis of outcomes to support the integration of personalized medicine into the Spanish National Health System and on the dissemination of results.

Once the network, participating centers, and selected techniques had been established, the next step was to ensure compliance with the ethical and legal requirements necessary for the implementation of these genomic analyses.

The project obtained ethical approval at each of the participating centers, with VHIR as the coordinating center (PR(AMI)536/2022). In addition, a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) was conducted and approved prior patient recruitment, in accordance with EU general data protection regulation. Patient Information Sheets and Informed Consent forms (including assent forms for minors) were created for parents/legal representatives and patients of adult age (Data Supplement, Document 2).

Inclusion criteria for patients and specific sample requirements were defined for each of the techniques to be performed (Data Supplement, Document 3). Two REDCap databases were also created: one for patient inclusion requests and another for data collection. This separation ensured a streamlined workflow, facilitated data traceability, and reinforced compliance with data protection regulation. Data collected include patient baseline characteristics, sequencing technologies applied, relevant genomic results, clinical utility of the techniques applied, and patient follow-up. The database is also linked with the Spanish national cancer registry (RETI-SEHOP).

Based on the predefined inclusion criteria, a protocol was created with the steps to be followed by each hospital and patient (Data Supplement, Document 4). These steps are detailed below: (1) identification of the candidate patient, (2) review of inclusion criteria, (3) informed consent signature, (4) patient registration and central confirmation of inclusion/exclusion, (5) shipment of the sample to the genomic center and conduct of the sequencing technique, (6) case presentation in the MTB, (7) report of results, and (8) research data collection (demographic information, clinical data with follow-up, and genomic results; Fig 4).

The setting up of the project (2022-2024) was organized through work packages, dedicated working groups, and centralized SEHOP-PENCIL coordination. Regular expert meetings were held both in person and online to facilitate coordination and decision making. Standard operating procedures and study-related documentation were developed and refined through expert consensus, ensuring consistency and methodological rigor across the network. This preparatory work established a solid foundation for the subsequent implementation of the project.

Implementation of the SEHOP-PENCIL Project

Since 2024, the project has advanced to the implementation stage. This phase involves applying all previously established steps to recruit patients and perform the techniques using the circuit developed in the preceding stage. It is organized into clearly defined sequential phases to ensure systematic execution and evaluation.

Phase 1 aimed to recruit patients prospectively in the SEHOP-PENCIL study and to pilot the tools for patient inclusion, data collection, and organizational workflows. Patient recruitment from the 10 hospital-affiliated genomic centers took place from February 2024 to July 2025. This phase allowed for the evaluation and refinement of the study procedures before full-scale implementation.

Phase 2, launched in July 2025, expanded patient inclusion to all Spanish hospitals treating pediatric cancer. The methodology from previous phases was maintained, with criteria established for referring genomic studies from the requesting hospital to the designated genomic center, as well as a staggered rollout of the project across SEHOP hospitals.

As of 31 December 2025, a total of 600 patients have been enrolled in the SEHOP-PENCIL project. Among these, 792 techniques have been performed in accordance with the study protocol (345 NGS panels, 202 DNA methylation profiles, 131 cancer predisposition studies, 110 WES and/or RNAseq, and four liquid biopsy studies). Recruitment is currently active in 16 of 45 hospitals in Spain.

The project is currently pursuing key objectives, including a cost-effectiveness study, a multistakeholder forum to propose strategies for facilitating implementation in the Spanish National Health System, and the harmonization of genomic methodologies and result interpretation.

DISCUSSION

Personalized cancer medicine for pediatric patients has been progressively incorporated into routine clinical care in several countries through international programs, reflecting a growing global recognition of its potential to improve patient outcomes.3-11 Some countries have already integrated precision oncology within their national public health care systems.12-16 However, the extent of implementation varies considerably between countries, depending on available resources, infrastructures, and nationally adopted strategies.24 These disparities underscore the need to adapt precision oncology to each health care system, while simultaneously ensuring equitable access to advanced diagnostics and targeted therapies.

In Spain, pediatric oncology lacked a national precision medicine framework until recently.20 To address this gap, the SEHOP launched the SEHOP-PENCIL project.

The national survey results highlight both progress and persistent challenges in the integration of genomic technologies within Spanish pediatric oncology and hematology services. The expanded access to targeted NGS panels compared with the 2020 survey20 (≈40% at diagnosis in 2020, while ≈70% in 2022) reflects the growing adoption of precision medicine approaches. However, the marked variability in their implementation, panel selection, and funding mechanisms underscores the absence of standardized national protocols. In contrast to NGS panels, more comprehensive techniques, including WES, WGS, RNAseq, and DNA methylation profiling, were available in only a minority of hospitals and frequently depended on research-based funding. Although 60% of hospitals reported access to germline NGS panels, only 40% had an in-house pediatric cancer predisposition clinic, revealing a gap between diagnostic capacity and clinical implementation. This disparity indicates that, while genomic tools are increasingly available, access to specialized genetic counseling and surveillance programs remains limited. Similarly, the availability of pediatric cancer predisposition boards and MTBs was inconsistent across institutions. Together, these findings identify critical areas for harmonization and capacity building within the national personalized medicine strategy.

SEHOP-PENCIL is structured around a network of 10 specialized genomic centers connected through a centralized patient inclusion system that serves 45 SEHOP hospitals. To reduce disparities between hospitals and ensure uniform implementation, the program established clear criteria for patient eligibility and access to genomic techniques. In parallel, multidisciplinary working groups were formed to harmonize technical procedures and standardize result interpretation protocols across participating centers. A key component of the implementation is the creation of a national MTB, which facilitates centralized discussion of somatic and germline genomic findings. This structure supports evidence-based clinical decision making and promotes equitable access to clinical trials involving targeted therapies. In addition, to ensure access to specialized cancer predisposition clinics, all patients with suspected germline alterations identified by the program are reported to the treating clinicians, along with a recommendation that the patient be referred to a cancer predisposition clinic with expertise in childhood cancers. Beyond its clinical function, the program emphasizes systematic collection of clinical and molecular data, building a national research resource to advance understanding of pediatric cancers and support novel therapeutic development.

At the national level, Spain has advanced precision medicine through the National Strategy for Personalized Medicine, led by the Carlos III Health Institute25 and implementation of genomic testing by the Ministry of Health. The former initiative includes the flagship IMPaCT project26 and a competitive funding call for research projects on the implementation of precision medicine, active between 2021 and 2024.27 With the second initiative, in January 2024, the Spanish Ministry of Health incorporated a Common Catalog of Genetic and Genomic Tests into the national public health services portfolio for adult and pediatric oncology and hematology, encompassing both somatic and germline testing, aiming to optimize disease prevention, diagnosis, and treatment.28,29 While these measures represent essential steps toward integrating personalized medicine into clinical practice, their implementation remains uneven across regions.30,31 This variability is partly attributable to the decentralized structure of the Spanish public health system, in which regional governments manage health care policies and budgets independently.32 Currently, Catalonia is the only region that has incorporated NGS panels into the public health system through the Precision Oncology Program (POP) of CatSalut.33,34 By contrast, the use of more advanced techniques including WES, WGS, RNAseq, and methylome profiling, as well as NGS panels in most other regions of Spain, remains predominantly dependent on research grants, philanthropic initiatives, or hospital budgets.

The long-term sustainability of SEHOP-PENCIL and similar initiatives requires stable national funding, integration of Medical Genetics and Laboratory Genetics as recognized specialties within the public health system,35 and investment in highly specialized personnel.36 The first results of the SEHOP-PENCIL project in terms of clinical utility will be analyzed on completion of the current ISCIII funded project (June 2026), hence becoming available by the end of 2026. Moreover, a cost-benefit analysis currently underway within SEHOP-PENCIL will be critical to demonstrating clinical and economic value, particularly in diagnostic refinement, risk stratification, therapy matching, and identification of germline cancer predisposition.

In conclusion, SEHOP-PENCIL represents a pioneering effort to integrate precision oncology into pediatric cancer care at the national level in Spain. By building a collaborative network, harmonizing genomic practices, and ensuring equitable access across regions, the program has the potential to reduce disparities and improve outcomes for children and adolescents with cancer. Its success will depend on sustained commitment from national health authorities to fully integrate precision oncology into the Spanish National Health System, offering a model for other decentralized health care systems facing similar challenges.
