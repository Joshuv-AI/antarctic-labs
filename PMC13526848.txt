Horizontally arranged covalent organic framework nanowires on poly(amidoxime) fibers for high-efficiency uranium capture
Authors: Jiarui Cao, Wanying Chen, Cheng Zhang, Yue Zheng, Yajie Yang, Doudou Cao, Yingbo Song, Lu Luo, Yuxin Chen, Qiaotian Zhang, Sirui Li, Xinbo Li, Yonghao Zhu, Guangshan Zhu, Ye Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The efficient capture of low-concentration uranium (with a concentration range of 10–50 ppm) from nuclear and metallurgical wastewaters is crucial for safeguarding the ecological security of water bodies. Herein, an ion coordination approach was proposed to achieve the controlled arrangement of covalent organic framework (COF) nanowires, with these nanowires being horizontally aligned on the surface of poly(amidoxime) (PAO) fibers. At the interface, the amidoxime groups of PAO and the photocatalytic sites of COF enable synergistic chemical binding and photoreduction of soluble uranium into (UO2)O2·4H2O precipitates. This efficient integration of the two pathways enables an ultrahigh uranium uptake capacity of 1259 mg g−1 within 12 hours (~10 ppm), reducing the extraction time to 1/10 that of the pristine COF nanowires. Notably, in the uranium-spiked metallurgical wastewater with a uranium concentration of ~50 ppm, the COF composite attains a uranium uptake capacity of 9230 mg g−1, establishing a new benchmark for uranium adsorption capacity among existing materials.

Introduction

The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) projects that global nuclear generating capacity will expand to 950 gigawatts (GW) by 2050 under its high-case scenario, over 2.5 times the 371.5 GW operational capacity recorded in 20231,2. This rapid expansion will significantly boost the large-scale mining and smelting of uranium, the primary feedstock for nuclear fuel3–9. Amidst large-scale mining operations and extensive nuclear industry utilization, the treatment of low-concentration uranium solutions (10–50 ppm) from nuclear and metallurgical wastewater systems has garnered significant attention, an issue stemming from its dual role in energy security and environmental stewardship10–13. The issue positions low-concentration uranium capture as a linchpin technology for bridging the gap between the growing nuclear energy demand and sustainable resource management.

Photocatalytic technology is regarded as one of the most promising approaches for uranium capture from wastewater, owing to its low-carbon nature, absence of chemical emissions, low energy input, and high extraction efficiency14–19. Among various photocatalytic materials, covalent organic framework (COF)-based photocatalysts offer distinct advantages, such as high structural order, large specific surface area, and tunable architecture, and thus have demonstrated great potential for uranium separation20–22. Notably, several COF solids with unique nanostructures, including nanowires (N3-COF60)23 and nanosheets (COF 4-Pd-AO)24, exhibit favorable performance in terms of extraction capacity and selectivity. Nevertheless, pristine unfunctionalized COF-based catalysts exhibit weak affinity toward uranyl (UO22+) ions, which cannot substantially enrich uranium species around catalytic sites. In response, a growing body of research has focused on integrating photocatalytic capability with U(VI) chemical enrichment, exemplified by combining adsorbents and photocatalysts to form composites, constructing efficient U(VI)-binding sites within photocatalysts, and introducing photo-responsive components into adsorbents25–28. The spatial segregation of adsorption and photocatalytic sites introduces significant mass transfer resistance, constraining both the kinetics and overall efficiency of uranium capture.

Herein, an ion coordination approach was employed to induce the growth of COF nanowires, in which a morphology that, to our knowledge, has not previously been demonstrated for COFs. Following the coordination of the UO22+ ions onto the PAO fibers, hydrazine hydrate and 1,3,5-trialdehyde phenyltriazine were sequentially introduced into the reaction system. As the UO22+ ions on the surface of PAO fibers bind structural fragments of the COF, the COF nanowires grow horizontally upon the PAO fibers (MICOF/PAO fiber). In contrast, COF nanowires exhibit vertical growth on the PAO fiber surfaces in the absence of ions acting as templates (NICOF/PAO fiber). Based on the in-situ synergistic chemical adsorption of PAO and photocatalysis of COF, MICOF/PAO fiber can achieve high uranium adsorption capacity at low concentrations, with a saturated uranium adsorption capacity of 9.23 g g−1 in the uranium-spiked metallurgical wastewater. This study provides a reliable pathway for advancing the precise control of nanostructures and energy conversion.

Results

Synthesis and characterization

The triazine-linked two-dimensional (2D) COF, N3-COF60, nanowire was one of the best-performing photocatalysts toward uranium recovery23. After exposure to a 10 ppm uranium-spiked solution under simulated sunlight for 7 days, N3-COF60 exhibited an adsorption capacity of 1270 mg g−1. Despite its high uranium uptake capacity, N3-COF60 suffered from slow adsorption kinetics at the early adsorption stage. PAO fibers possess distinct advantages, including low cost, facile preparation, and simple operation. They enriched uranium through coordination binding of amidoxime groups with UO22+ species, and the adsorbed UO22+ ions generated electrostatic repulsion toward subsequent free UO22+ ions, which compromised the adsorption capacity (<0.4 g g−1)29,30. To address the inherent limitations of PAO fibers and N3-COF60 for uranium extraction, we controllably grew N3-COF60 onto the PAO fiber substrate. PAO fibers (25 mg) were immersed in the UO22+ solution (100 mL, 100 ppm) to get the UO22+-loaded PAO fibers, namely U@PAO fiber. Subsequently, the U@PAO fiber was reacted with hydrazine hydrate (N2H4, 5 μL, 50–60 wt% solution) in a mixture of n-butanol/metaxylene/1,4-dioxane (2 mL, 3:1:1) for 1 hour. Followed by the addition of 1,3,5-trialdehyde phenyltriazine (25 mg, 0.065 mmol) and acetic acid (100 μL, 6 M) as the catalyst, the system was heated to 120 °C for 3 days to facilitate the complete Schiff-base coupling reaction (Fig. 1). The product was stirred in the Na2CO3 (500 mL, 0.1 M) solution for 3 hours to remove UO22+ templates, giving the MICOF/PAO fiber. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) depicted that the U = O stretching vibration band was observed at 945 cm−1 in U@PAO fiber, suggesting the coordination of UO22+ ions on PAO fibers (Fig. 2a). After the reaction between U@PAO fiber and N2H4, the U = O characteristic band shifted to 949 cm−1. This phenomenon arose because N2H4 coordinated to the U center, donating electron density and thereby inducing a blue shift in the U = O stretching vibration31,32. Following the Schiff-base reaction, a C = N vibration band (attributed to N3-COF60) at 1619 cm−1 and an N − O stretching band (attributed to PAO fiber) at 921 cm−1 were observed, demonstrating the formation of MICOF/PAO and NICOF/PAO fibers. In the 13C CP/MAS nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra (Fig. 2b), a series of signal peaks at 128, 137, 162, and 169 ppm was assigned to the carbon atoms in the N3-COF60,33, while the characteristic signals of 35 and 178 ppm proved the existence of amidoxime groups. The results above indicate that the COF nanowires were successfully doped on the PAO fibers. N2 adsorption-desorption analyses were conducted on the fully activated samples at 77 K (Supplementary Figs. 5, 6). The Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET) surface areas of MICOF/PAO and NICOF/PAO fibers were 280 and 294 m2 g−1, respectively. Using a nonlocal density functional theory (NL-DFT) method, MICOF/PAO and NICOF/PAO fibers showed 2.6 nm-sized pores consistent with those of N3-COF6023. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to study the morphology of PAO, U@PAO, MICOF/PAO, and NICOF/PAO fibers. The PAO fibers had a smooth surface; after the coordination of UO22+ ions, their fibrous surface became rough (Fig. 3a, b). The growth orientation of over 200 individual COF nanowires from 3 representative SEM fields of view was analyzed. Over 80% of the COF nanowires in the MICOF/PAO fiber exhibited a horizontal orientation (defined as the angle between the nanowire long axis and the substrate plane <15°), with diameters ranging from 270 to 350 nm (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 9). In stark contrast, more than 98% of the COF nanowires in the NICOF/PAO fiber exhibited vertical growth orientation on the PAO fiber surface, with diameters ranging from 80 to 130 nm (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 10). The distinct broadening feature of COF solids on PAO fibers in the MICOF/PAO fiber, as compared with N3-COF60 with an external diameter of 100 nm23 may lie in the COF network containing more N − N groups on the (100) and (110) planes to coordinate with metal ions. Consequently, the ion templates alter the growth mechanism of the COF nanowires, causing them to expand along the (100)/(110) direction and thereby significantly thicken.Fig. 3Microstructural Characterization.SEM images of a PAO fiber, b U@PAO fiber, c MICOF/PAO fiber, and d NICOF/PAO fiber.

To gain in-depth insights into the structural details of MICOF/PAO and NICOF/PAO fibers, powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis was performed. The PXRD patterns revealed diffraction peaks at 2θ = 3.5, 6.0, 7.1, and 26.4°, corresponding to the (100), (110), (200), and (001) planes, identical with those of N3-COF60 nanowires (Fig. 2c). The intensity ratio of PXRD diffraction peaks between the (100) crystal plane and the (001) crystal plane is used to indicate structural changes in the composite materials. The (100)/(001) intensity ratio fell in the range of 12.1–13.5 for N3-COF60 (Supplementary Fig. 11), while this ratio decreased to 8.3–9.7 for the NICOF/PAO fiber (Supplementary Fig. 12), which suggested that the COF networks tended to grow along the (001) direction. Meanwhile, this value increased significantly to the range of 16.3–18.2 for the MICOF/PAO fiber (Supplementary Fig. 13), indicating that the COF networks expanded along the (100) crystal plane, with a pronounced tendency for intra-layer expansion. For the COF nanowires stripped from the MICOF/PAO fiber, the transmission electron microscope (TEM) image indicated that numerous lattice fringes corresponding to the (001) planes were visible at the edges of the N3-COF nanowire (Fig. 2d). The above findings indicate that the N − N groups of the COF networks coordinate with UO22+ ions on the PAO surfaces. Subsequently, the COF layers grow perpendicularly to this surface, collectively forming a composite structure with PAO.

The ultraviolet/visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV/vis DRS) of PAO fiber showed strong adsorption below 380 nm, while the light absorption of N3-COF60 was limited to below 550 nm (Supplementary Fig. 14). In contrast, both MICOF/PAO and NICOF/PAO fibers displayed significant adsorption around 500 nm, indicating that the incorporation of N3-COF60 significantly enhanced the absorption of visible light of PAO fibers. By analyzing the transformed Kubelka−Munk function versus absorbed light energy, the band gaps of the semiconductor photocatalysts were calculated to be 2.63, 3.57, 2.70, and 2.73 eV for N3-COF60, PAO, MICOF/PAO, and NICOF/PAO, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 15). The narrow optical band gaps of MICOF/PAO and NICOF/PAO fibers suggest that the composite photocatalysts are theoretically capable of visible-light-driven photocatalysis34. From the Mott-Schottky curves, the positive slopes indicate that MICOF/PAO and NICOF/PAO fibers are n-type semiconductors, with flat band values (Efb vs NHE) of −0.92 and −0.97 V for MICOF/PAO and NICOF/PAO fibers, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 20). Correspondingly, the conduction band maxima were −1.02 and −1.07 V (vs NHE) for MICOF/PAO and NICOF/PAO fibers (Supplementary Fig. 21), respectively, which are greater than the potential of the UO22+/UO2 redox couple (+0.411 V vs NHE), illustrating the thermodynamically feasible photoreduction of U(VI) to U(IV) 35.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted using a conventional three-electrode system, where platinum served as the counter electrode, Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode, and the target material as the working electrode, with an aqueous 0.1 M Na2SO4 solution employed as the electrolyte. The MICOF/PAO fiber exhibited a smaller semicircle diameter in the Nyquist plot compared to the NICOF/PAO fiber, indicating a lower charge transfer resistance and more efficient charge carrier transport and separation of the MICOF/PAO fiber (Supplementary Fig. 22)36,37. The transient photocurrent response of the photocatalysts was measured without a bias voltage during an “on-off” cycling experiment, with each cycle comprising 40 s of light irradiation followed by 40 s of dark conditions, totaling six cycles (Supplementary Fig. 23). The MICOF/PAO fiber exhibited a stronger photocurrent response, indicating that MICOF/PAO possesses a superior charge separation property to NICOF/PAO fiber.

Adsorption-photocatalysis mechanism

A variety of ancillary techniques were employed to explore the adsorption-photocatalysis mechanism of the MICOF/PAO fiber for uranium capture. The uranium-spiked solution was prepared by dissolving UO2(NO3)2·6H2O in deionized water, with the solution pH adjusted to 2–3 using nitric acid. After collecting the uranium-loaded MICOF/PAO fibers from a uranium-spiked solution (~10 ppm, 500 mL, pH = 2–3) under irradiation with simulated sunlight (λ = 320–780 nm, intensity of 1 kW m−2), the U@MICOF/PAO fiber exhibited a new U = O stretching vibration band at 916 cm−1 in its FT-IR spectrum, with the intensity of this band gradually increasing over time (Fig. 4a). This observation provided compelling evidence that uranium was consistently sequestered within the composite in the form of uranyl species. In the PXRD patterns, no distinct characteristic peaks corresponding to uranium species were detected in U@MICOF/PAO fiber during the initial 1 hour (named as U@MICOF/PAO fiber-1 h) (Fig. 4b). Chemical adsorption thus emerged as the primary pathway for uranium capture by the MICOF/PAO fiber during this period. With increasing irradiation time, new diffraction peaks at 15.1, 21.1, 25.1, 25.5, and 26.2° assigned to (UO2)O2·4H2O (PDF#49-1821) emerged and progressively intensified in PXRD patterns, demonstrating that uranium was gradually converted by photocatalysis into (UO2)O2·4H2O (Fig. 4b).Fig. 4Mechanism study for uranium capture.a FT-IR spectra and b PXRD patterns acquired from the MICOF/PAO fiber following exposure to a ~ 10 ppm uranium-spiked solution (500 mL, pH = 2–3) over the course of 24 h under simulated sunlight. c Binding energies of different binding configurations of MICOF/PAO fiber fragment with UO22+ ion. d U L3-edge EXAFS R-space and corresponding fitting curves of U@MICOF/PAO fiber-1h. e Computational studies of molecular orbital adiabatic potentials for MICOF/PAO fiber.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of the U@MICOF/PAO fiber-1 h revealed that the U 4f5/2 and U 4f7/2 characteristic peaks appeared at the binding energies of 393.01 and 382.30 eV, respectively, corresponding to hexavalent uranium (Supplementary Fig. 28). In the O 1 s XPS spectra, the peak position (532.84 eV) of the N − OH group in U@MICOF/PAO fiber-1 h shifted significantly by 0.10 eV toward the higher binding energy compared to MICOF/PAO fiber (532.74 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 29). This phenomenon was mainly attributed to the transfer of the electron cloud from the N − OH group to U(VI)38. Similarly, in the N 1 s XPS spectrum of U@MICOF/PAO fiber-1 h, the binding energies exhibited similar shifts, exemplified by 399.58 → 399.69 eV for the N − N group and 400.05 → 400.18 eV for the N − OH group, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 30). These results indicate that UO22+ ions coordinate to the N − OH groups of PAO fiber and the N − N sites of N3-COF60.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were employed to investigate the diverse coordination modes between MICOF/PAO fiber fragment and UO22+ ion using the exchange function, with a focus on identifying energetically favorable binding configurations. Detailed computational procedures are provided in the Supplementary Information. As shown in Fig. 4c, various coordination modes were investigated, encompassing η2(No,Oo) + η2(Nhh,Nhh), η2(No,Oo)+monodentate Nt, η2(Na,Oo) + η2(Nhh,Nhh) and, η2(Na,Oo)+monodentate Nt modes. The UO22+ ion coordinated to the synergistic sites formed by the amidoxime group in PAO (via an η2(No, Oo) mode) and the N − N group of the N3-COF60, exhibiting the lowest binding energy of −117.88 kcal mol−1 among all tested binding modes. U L3-edge X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectroscopy was performed to probe the uranium coordination environment in the U@MICOF/PAO fiber-1 h. Fitting the spectrum confirmed that the coordination modes of the U atom were U−Oax (2, 1.79 Å), U−Oeq (2, 2.29 Å), and U−Neq (3, 2.48 Å) (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Table 1). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the chemical adsorption of UO22+ by the MICOF/PAO fiber predominantly occurs at the composite’s interface.

The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of the MICOF/PAO fiber were calculated using the DMol3 module in Materials Studio 2019 with the Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA)/Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional (further details are provided in the Supplementary Information). The HOMO and LUMO values of MICOF/PAO fiber were determined to be −4.57 and −3.37 eV, respectively (Fig. 4e). This would enable efficient separation of photogenerated electrons and holes under sunlight while allowing photocatalytic reduction of adsorbed U(VI) to insoluble uranium precipitates 39.

A plausible mechanism for uranium capture using the MICOF/PAO fiber is proposed herein. Initially, UO22+ ions are rapidly enriched at the interface between N3-COF60 nanowires and PAO fibers, a process that leverages the synergistic affinity of the dual-component matrix for the UO22+ ions. This interfacial enrichment results in a pronounced accumulation of UO22+ ions within the MICOF/PAO fiber, which, in turn, drives the formation of a local high-concentration uranyl microenvironment. Subsequently, the concentrated UO22+ ions undergo photoreduction to U(IV); however, U(IV) cannot persist stably in the system and is rapidly converted into insoluble uranium species ((UO2)O2·4H2O)40–42. This in-situ transformation of UO22+ ions effectively liberates the occupied binding sites43,44, thereby creating available active sites to sustain the subsequent adsorption cycle of UO22+ ions and ensuring the continuous reactivity of the MICOF/PAO fiber.

Uranium capture performance

The uranium capture kinetics were studied by mixing 2.5 mg of photocatalyst in a uranium-spiked solution (500 mL, ~10 ppm, pH = 2–3) under simulated sunlight. As shown in Fig. 5a, the MICOF/PAO fiber exhibited a distinctly unique uranium capture behavior over time: it rapidly sequestered UO22+ ions within the initial 5 minutes, reaching the first equilibrium at 15 minutes with a uranium uptake capacity of 89 mg g−1. Subsequently, the uranium uptake increased gradually and essentially achieved saturation in the 10 ppm solution at 18 hours, with a final uranium uptake capacity of 1805 mg g−1. The pseudo-second-order model, with a higher R2 (0.9931) than that based on the pseudo-first-order model (0.9755), better described the U(VI) uptake process within the initial 1 hour, suggesting that the uranium capture was mainly driven by chemical adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 38, Supplementary Table 2). Beyond 1 hour, the uranium capture behavior was essentially consistent with N3-COF60, confirming that photocatalytic conversion dominated this stage23. The MICOF/PAO fiber (1259 mg g−1, 12 hours) required only 1/10 of the time needed for N3-COF60 (1270 mg g−1, 120 hours)23 to attain the equivalent uranium uptake capacity; the efficiency of uranium capture was markedly enhanced by the MICOF/PAO fiber.Fig. 5Uranium uptake performance.a Uranium adsorption capacities of the MICOF/PAO and NICOF/PAO fibers in a ~ 10 ppm uranium-spiked solution under simulated sunlight. b Removal kinetics and efficiency of the MICOF/PAO fiber in the uranium-spiked solution under simulated sunlight. c Selectivity of the MICOF/PAO and PAO fibers in the uranium-spiked solution containing different competing cations. d Uranium adsorption capacities of the MICOF/PAO fiber and N3-COF (with an external diameter of ~300 nm) in the uranium-spiked solution (~50 ppm, 500 mL) under simulated sunlight. e Comparison of uranium uptake capacity between the MICOF/PAO fiber (red ball) and other materials under the same adsorption conditions (Supplementary Table 4). By stark contrast, the NICOF/PAO fiber also exhibited rapid uranium uptake within 5 minutes, attaining an uptake capacity of 52 mg g−1 by 15 minutes. Kinetic fitting analyses further verified that chemical adsorption governed this process (Supplementary Fig. 39, Supplementary Table 2). With prolonged time, its photocatalytic performance was distinctly different from that of the MICOF/PAO fiber. Notably, the uranium adsorption capacity of the MICOF/PAO fiber (1802 mg g−1) substantially exceeded that of N3-COF60 (460 mg g−1)23, PAO fiber (319 mg g−1) (Supplementary Fig. 40), and NICOF/PAO fiber (1310 mg g−1). Such a high uranium uptake capacity of the MICOF/PAO fiber markedly outperforms that of other photocatalysts, such as TTh-COF-AO (20 ppm, 682 mg g−1), ZFOCN-2 (10 ppm, 383 mg g−1), and PAF-AN2T8-AO (8 ppm, 136 mg g−1) 45–47.

The binding affinity of the MICOF/PAO fiber for UO22+ ions was calculated by exposing 5 mg of the MICOF/PAO fiber to the uranium-spiked solution (200 mL, ~10 ppm, pH = 2–3) under simulated sunlight. After 96 hours of illumination, the aqueous uranium concentration was reduced to 15 ppb (Fig. 5b), far below the acceptable limit of uranium (30 ppb) in drinking water set by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)48. Correspondingly, the removal efficiency of the MICOF/PAO fiber was as high as 99.84%, comparable with MIPAFs-11c (99.15%), 6% SN-ACP (96.17%), and Anti-PAO (92.00%) (Supplementary Fig. 41, Supplementary Table 3)49–51. The distribution coefficient (Kd) value of the MICOF/PAO fiber was determined to be 2.55 × 107 mL g−1, higher than those of classical uranium adsorbents, including PAF-CS (9.37 × 106), COF-SO3H (9.80 × 106), PAO PNMs (8.61 × 105), LDHS@TCN (1.47 × 104), and PN-PCN-222 (3.00 × 102)52–56, indicating its exceptional affinity towards trace UO22+ ions.

The selectivity of the MICOF/PAO fiber (2.5 mg) for U(VI) uptake against interfering species was investigated in a system containing various cations (500 mL, all at ~10 ppm: UO22+, Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Li+, Mn2+, Ni2+, Fe3+, Al3+, and VO2+) under simulated sunlight for 30 hours. The MICOF/PAO fiber exhibited a uranium uptake capacity of 1177 mg g−1 and a U/V selectivity coefficient reaching 16, a value significantly higher than that of the pure PAO fiber (8.4) (Fig. 5c). For typical high concentrations of Mn2+ in wastewater, the selectivity coefficient for U/Mn of the MICOF/PAO fiber exceeded 25757. Meanwhile, upon the introduction of SO42− and Cl− (all at ~1000 ppm) into the uranium-spiked solution (500 mL, ~10 ppm), the MICOF/PAO fiber (2.5 mg) still retained 83% of its uranium extraction capacity (Supplementary Fig. 42). These findings demonstrate that the MICOF/PAO fiber is promising for uranium recovery from nuclear and metallurgical wastewaters.

2.5 mg of the MICOF/PAO fiber was soaked in the uranium-spiked solution (500 mL, ~10 ppm, pH = 2–3) under simulated sunlight for 30 hours. To elute uranium from the uranium-loaded MICOF/PAO fiber, the material was separately added to NaHCO3 (100 mL, 1 M) and H2O2 (100 mL, 0.1 M) solutions, respectively, and stirred for 3 hours in each system. After ten reuse cycles, the MICOF/PAO fiber still retained 87% of its initial uranium adsorption capacity and exhibited considerable reusability (Supplementary Fig. 43). The uranium concentration in each eluate was quantified, and the corresponding uranium recovery efficiency was calculated. The cumulative uranium recovery efficiency across all cycles reached 89%. Meanwhile, the MICOF/PAO fiber retained its structural integrity after 10 adsorption-desorption cycles (Supplementary Figs. 44−46).

To examine the practical applicability of the adsorbent, the breakthrough experiment was conducted. The uranium-spiked solution (~10 ppm) was passed through a column packed with the MICOF/PAO fiber (30 mg) via a peristaltic pump with a flow rate of 1 mL min−1 under simulated sunlight. No U(VI) ions were detected in the effluent even after 30 hours, which was defined as the breakthrough time53,58 (Supplementary Fig. 47). The final uranium extraction capacity of the MICOF/PAO fiber was calculated to be 1434 mg g−1.

To further investigate the synergistic effect of chemical adsorption and photocatalysis, a mixture of metaxylene and 1,4-dioxane with a volume ratio of 1:1 was used as the reaction medium for N3-COF, resulting in the fabrication of porous nanowires with an outer diameter of approximately 300 nm23. The uranium adsorption capacities of the photocatalysts were evaluated using 2.5 mg of the catalysts in a uranium-spiked solution (500 mL, ~50 ppm, pH = 2–3) and irradiating with simulated sunlight. For N3-COF, the adsorption capacity was only 2.46 g g−1 (Fig. 5d). After integrating chemical enrichment, the uptake capacity of the MICOF/PAO fiber exceeded 9.60 g g−1, a value approaching the experimental limit (the theoretical maximum adsorption capacity is 10.0 g g−1 at a uranium removal efficiency of 100%). These results reveal that the enhanced adsorption capacity stems from the synergistic effect of PAO fibers and COF nanowires.

Different dosages of the catalyst were added to 100 mL of uranium-spiked solution, followed by stirring and irradiation under simulated sunlight, to evaluate the uranium extraction performance of the MICOF/PAO fiber at different uranium concentrations. At an identical solid-liquid ratio and initial concentration, the uranium uptake capacity of the MICOF/PAO fiber was higher to varying degrees than that of other UO22+-capture materials, such as TTh-COF-AO (20 ppm, 0.02 g L−1, 38%), IHEP-24 (50 ppm, 0.2 g L−1, 41%), PN-PCN-222 (400 ppm, 0.25 g L−1, 13%), and LDHS@TCN (800 ppm, 0.1 g L−1, 309%) (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Table 4)43,55,56,59. The MICOF/PAO fiber exhibited excellent uranium extraction capacity across a wide concentration range.

Notably, the MICOF/PAO fiber can be fabricated via a facile one-pot reaction method, in which U@PAO fiber/N2H4 (5.20 g) and 1,3,5-trialdehyde phenyltriazine (1.92 g, 4.88 mmol) were mixed to prepare approximately 7 g of the MICOF/PAO fiber in a single batch. The uranium adsorption capacity of the MICOF/PAO fiber was determined in the natural seawater under simulated sunlight (~3.3 ppb, 80 L, 8 mg). The MICOF/PAO fiber showed a uranium adsorption capacity of 15.01 mg g−1 over 2 days (an average of 7.51 mg g−1 per day) (Supplementary Fig. 48). The extraction rate of the MICOF/PAO fiber compared favorably with other high-performing adsorbents or technologies reported in the literature, including COF-4 (20.52 mg g−1, 3 days), DSUP fibers (17.45 mg g−1, 3 days), and SSUP fiber (12.33 mg g−1, 3.5 days) (Supplementary Fig. 49, Supplementary Table 5)60–62. Over a 7-day testing period, the MICOF/PAO fiber demonstrated a uranium uptake capacity of 22.47 mg g−1 and a vanadium uptake capacity of only 4.03 mg g−1 (Supplementary Fig. 50), reaching the current top-tier level for uranium capture from seawater.

Further, the practical application for recovering uranium from metallurgical wastewater was conducted. The uranium adsorption capacity of the photocatalyst (2.5 mg) was evaluated in uranium-spiked metallurgical wastewater (500 mL, 10–300 ppm) under simulated sunlight. The uranium adsorption capacities of the MICOF/PAO fiber in 10, 50, 100, and 300 ppm uranium-spiked metallurgical wastewaters were 1.64, 9.23, 18.45, and 46.87 g g−1, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 51). Given that uranium concentrations in most nuclear and metallurgical wastewaters typically range from 10 to 50 ppm, the uranium extraction capacity of the MICOF/PAO fiber remains above 90% of its initial level within this concentration range, demonstrating its high efficiency and practical applicability for uranium capture from metallurgical wastewater.

Discussion

In summary, we have employed the ion coordination approach to regulate the arrangement of N3-COF60 nanowires, inducing their horizontal growth on the surface of PAO fibers. The as-synthesized sample efficiently synergizes chemical adsorption and photocatalysis for the capture of UO22+ ions. The uranium adsorption performance of the MICOF/PAO fiber in low-concentration uranium solution is substantially higher than that of reported adsorbents to date. Moving forward, a promising research direction may focus on refining the composite’s structural design and scaling up its fabrication process to promote its practical application in large-scale uranium recovery.

Methods

Synthesis of MICOF/PAO fiber

PAO fibers (25 mg) were added to the uranyl ion solution (100 mL, 100 ppm) and stirred for 24 hours to afford U@PAO fiber. A 10 mL pressure glass tube was charged with the aforementioned U@PAO fiber, hydrazine hydrate (5 μL, 50–60 wt% solution), n-butanol (1.2 mL), mesitylene (0.4 mL), and 1,4-dioxane (0.4 mL). The mixture was stirred for 1 hour at room temperature. To the suspension, 1,3,5-trialdehyde phenyltriazine (25 mg, 0.065 mmol) and acetic acid (100 μL, 6 M) were then added. The tube was then sealed and heated in a blast oven at 120 °C for 3 days. Thereafter, the tube was opened, and the suspension was filtered and washed with chloroform (2 × 5 mL), acetone (2 × 5 mL), and tetrahydrofuran (2 × 5 mL). The product was stirred in Na2CO3 (500 mL, 0.1 M) solution for 3 hours. Finally, the solid was dried at 60 °C under a vacuum to afford MICOF/PAO fibers.

Synthesis of NICOF/PAO fiber

A 10 mL pressure glass tube was charged with PAO fibers (25 mg), hydrazine hydrate (5 μL, 50–60 wt% solution), n-butanol (1.2 mL), mesitylene (0.4 mL), and 1,4-dioxane (0.4 mL). To the suspension, 1,3,5-trialdehyde phenyltriazine (25 mg, 0.065 mmol) and acetic acid (100 μL, 6 M) were then added. The tube was then sealed and heated in a blast oven at 120 °C for 3 days. Thereafter, the tube was opened, and the suspension was filtered and washed with chloroform (2 × 5 mL), acetone (2 × 5 mL), and tetrahydrofuran (2 × 5 mL). The solid was dried at 60 °C under a vacuum to afford NICOF/PAO fibers.

Selectivity studies

MICOF/PAO fiber (2.5 mg) was exposed to 500 mL of U(VI) solution with different metal ions at concentrations of 10 ppm (UO22+, VO2+, Mg2+, Ca2+, Na+, Mn2+, Li+, K+, Ni2+, Fe3+, and Al3+), and stirred for 30 hours under simulated sunlight. The concentrations of these ions were detected by ICP-AES.

MICOF/PAO fiber (2.5 mg) was exposed to 500 mL of U(VI) solution with 1000 ppm SO42− and 1000 ppm Cl−, and stirred for 30 hours under simulated sunlight. The concentrations of U(VI) ions were detected by ICP-AES.

Cycling experiment

The MICOF/PAO fiber (2.5 mg) was exposed to 500 mL of uranium-spiked solution (~10 ppm, pH = 2–3) under simulated sunlight. After stirring for 30 hours, samples were taken from the stirring mixture, filtered, and the filtrate was analyzed by a UV spectrophotometer. The uranium-loaded MICOF/PAO fiber was added separately into NaHCO3 (100 mL, 1 M) and H2O2 (100 mL, 0.1 M) solutions, followed by stirring for 3 hours in each case. MICOF/PAO fiber was collected via filtration and dried under a vacuum at 80 °C for 12 hours for the next adsorption. After stirring for 3 hours, 5 mL of the eluents were sampled, and their uranium concentrations were measured by ICP-AES.

Breakthrough experiment

30 mg of the MICOF/PAO fiber was dispersed in 50 mL of water, and the fiber was then loaded onto the sand core filter plate via vacuum filtration. The uranium-spiked solution (~10 ppm) was passed through a column packed with the MICOF/PAO fiber via a peristaltic pump with a flow rate of 1 mL min−1 under irradiation. The residual uranium concentration in the effluent was monitored via ICP analysis.

Uranium uptake from natural seawater

MICOF/PAO fiber (8 mg) was exposed to 80 L of natural seawater (~3.3 ppb) under irradiation for 7 days. Samples were taken at regular intervals from the stirring mixture, filtered, and the filtrate was analyzed by ICP-MS.

After 7 days of uranium capture in seawater, the MICOF/PAO fiber catalyst was collected and then dried in a vacuum oven (80 °C) for 12 hours. The catalyst powder was dissolved in 4 mL of aqua regia, then the collected solution was pre-diluted 10-fold with deionized water, filtered, and tested for uranium content using ICP-AES.

Uranium uptake from metallurgical wastewater

The metallurgical wastewater was obtained from a metallurgical company in Jiangxi Province, China. The solution with uranyl ion was prepared by dissolving UO2(NO3)2·6H2O into real metallurgical wastewater. The photocatalysts (2.5 mg) were exposed to 500 mL of uranium-spiked metallurgical wastewater (10–300 ppm) under simulated sunlight. Samples were taken at regular intervals from the stirring mixture, filtered, and the filtrate was analyzed by ICP-AES.
