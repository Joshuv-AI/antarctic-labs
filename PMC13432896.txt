School nurses’ perceptions of providing the HPV vaccine to adolescents in undervaccinated communities in Stockholm: A qualitative study
Authors: Helena Avermark, Maria Ström, Sibylle Herzig van Wees
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn Sweden, HPV vaccination is freely available to all adolescents aged 10–12 through a national, school-based program, but vaccine confidence and uptake are lower than for other childhood vaccines, with notable disparities between groups. School nurses are uniquely positioned to influence this, and the aim of this qualitative study was thus to understand their perceptions about the challenges associated with providing the HPV vaccine to adolescents in undervaccinated communities in Stockholm, Sweden. Between June and November 2024, we conducted 21 in-depth interviews with school nurses working in undervaccinated communities in Stockholm. The data was analyzed using inductive reflexive thematic analysis, which generated three themes: (1) School nurses are left to manage HPV vaccination by themselves; (2) Individual care and public health – the school nurses’ dual responsibilities; and (3) Navigating sensitivity around HPV and sex. The themes illustrate how school nurses recognize their ability to influence uptake, but that various challenges, ranging from high workload to normative ideas about sex, hinder them. Our results suggest that ensuring high HPV vaccine uptake largely depends on the efforts of individual school nurses, which ultimately challenges the sustainability of the HPV vaccination program, indicating the need for further support for school nurses with regards to communication materials and strategies to addressing HPV vaccine hesitancy.

Background

HPV vaccine decision-making is complex and multifactorial. Low vaccine confidence, i.e., low trust in the vaccine and the individuals and systems that deliver it, contributes to vaccine hesitancy, defined as being opposed to or conflicted about a vaccine.1 Vaccine hesitancy is often influenced by the interplay between historical, political, and sociocultural factors, individual experiences, and the social environment, as well as specific vaccine-related concerns.2 Low vaccine confidence may stem from social norms, cultural and religious beliefs, ideas of increased sexual promiscuity, and worries about side effects and safety.2,3 Hence, focusing solely on vaccine uptake and confidence at the individual level risks overlooking structural and political dimensions.

In 2012, the HPV vaccine was included in the Swedish national vaccination program for girls, and in 2020, this was expanded to also include boys, meaning that it is now freely available to all adolescents through school health services. Nationally, 88% of girls and 83% of boys aged 12 are fully vaccinated against HPV, but uptake varies from below 70% in some municipalities to above 90% in others.4 Reported reasons for HPV vaccine hesitancy have primarily been related to worry about side effects and lack of reliable information,5 but gendered norms about HPV and sex have also been described.6 While integrating HPV vaccination into the school health service has likely increased equitable access and overall vaccine uptake,7 notable disparities remain. Low educational level, low income, and foreign backgrounda are all associated with lower HPV vaccine confidence and uptake.8,9 Low vaccination uptake for a broad range of vaccines has also been found in anthroposophist communities in Sweden and globally.10,11 Lower vaccine uptake has further been described among girls with mental health conditions, neurodevelopmental disorders and intellectual disability.12 To acknowledge the multiple factors influencing vaccine uptake and the interconnectedness of barriers to access and vaccine confidence, we use the term ‘undervaccinated communities’ to refer to groups and areas at risk of HPV undervaccination,13 including schools with low HPV vaccine uptake.

Healthcare workers are crucial for the public perception of vaccines. Their recommendations have been identified as one of the leading factors influencing individuals’ vaccine decision-making.2,14–16 Positive engagements and trusting relations with healthcare workers foster vaccine confidence and increase uptake,16 making it essential that they recognize their role as opinion leaders promoting vaccination.17 Given the school-based delivery in Sweden, school nurses’ perspectives and experiences are pivotal to understanding how the HPV vaccination program is implemented and its associated challenges. Swedish school nurses generally recommend the HPV vaccine,18 but findings are mixed in terms of their perception of their role as opinion leaders. While some express uncertainty about the extent of their responsibility and how to navigate between respecting the autonomy of parents and adolescents and encouraging uptake,19 others perceive HPV vaccine promotion as a key part of their health-promoting role,18 highlighting the need for a more unified approach.

The suboptimal and inequitable HPV vaccine uptake places individuals at risk for HPV-related cancers and threatens to undermine ongoing elimination efforts. Despite this, to the best of our knowledge, no previous study has focused specifically on school nurses working with undervaccinated communities in Stockholm or Sweden. Thus, the aim of this study is to increase understanding of school nurses’ perceptions of challenges associated with the implementation of HPV vaccination programs in schools in undervaccinated communities in Stockholm.

Methods

This is a qualitative study using data from in-depth interviews with school nurses working in undervaccinated communities in the Stockholm region. Reflextive thematic analysis (RTA) was used for analysis, as it is theoretically flexible and thereby suitable considering the critical realist perspective underpinning the study. The RTA Reporting Guidelines20 were used to guide the conduct and reporting of the study. To enhance reflexivity, transparency and trustworthiness, the guidelines (including comments on how they were reflected in this work and further methodological details) are available in Supplementary file A.

This study was conducted as part of the formative phase of a larger project aimed at understanding and addressing inequalities in HPV vaccine uptake in Stockholm, Sweden, by co-designing an intervention to increase vaccine confidence among undervaccinated communities. In parallel, but outside the scope of this paper, vaccine-eligible adolescents and caregivers were also interviewed.

Study setting

This study was conducted in Stockholm, Sweden. With approximately 86% of girls and 81% of boys fully vaccinated, it is one of the regions with the lowest HPV vaccine uptake in Sweden, and the 17-percentage-point gap between the municipalities with the highest and lowest uptake in Stockholm highlights significant variation within the region itself.4 HPV vaccine uptake are lower in geographical areas characterized by low socioeconomic status, lower overall health and higher unmet need for healthcare and medication.21

Study participants

We conducted 21 interviews with school nurses working in five municipalities in Stockholm. Of the 20 schools represented, 11 were public, and nine were private organizations. Six of the schools also provided education for pupils with intellectual disabilities. Most schools were located in areas classified as socioeconomically challenged or mixed. Additional details are available in Supplementary file B.

Participants were selected using a combination of purposive and snowball sampling. To be eligible for inclusion, participants needed to (1) have a minimum of two years’ experience as school nurses, and (2) be employed at a school with at least one of the following: (a) HPV vaccine uptake below 83% (the regional average); (b) high share of pupils with foreign backgrounds; or (c) low socioeconomic status. Socioeconomic status was determined using the socioeconomic index for schools.22 The original index is designed so that 100 represents the national average, and a higher value equals lower socioeconomic status. To make interpretation more intuitive, it was inversed by HA. Participants were recruited using our professional networks, word of mouth, and active outreach to schools. The number of participants was determined based on information power, decided through continuous dialogue between the authors, assessing the quality and richness of the data in relation to the study’s aim.23 In particular, we weighed the strong dialogue and relatively narrow study aim against the sample specificity, acknowledging the limitations of our recruitment strategy.23

Data collection

MS conducted in-depth interviews between June and November 2024, either digitally (n = 9) or in person (n = 12). We developed an interview guide (Supplementary file C) guided by the WHO 3C model.24 It was tested in a pilot interview, after which minor refinements were made. The pilot interview was included in the study.

The interviews lasted, on average, 38 minutes (ranging between 20 and 67 minutes). All interviews were held in Swedish and transcribed verbatim by HA (n = 7) or a professional transcription service (n = 13). MS checked transcripts for accuracy. No repeat interviews were conducted; however, three participants provided additional comments via e-mail after their interviews. These were added to their transcripts after obtaining participant consent. One participant did not agree to be recorded; therefore, the data collected from that interview consisted of the interview notes.

Data analysis

We conducted a RTA as described by Braun and Clarke.25,26 All transcripts were read carefully, and notes were taken of interesting observations before they were imported to NVivo 15.0, where they were coded using an inductive, data-driven approach.25 Passages perceived as confusing or unclear were noted and returned to at a later stage, until all transcripts had been systematically coded. In most cases, such segments appeared clearer once revisited, likely due to deeper familiarity with the dataset as a whole, which enabled these excerpts to be better contextualized within broader patterns across transcripts. The initial codes were primarily descriptive and reflected the semantic content of the data, but as they were revised and refined, codes grew increasingly analytical.25 This was done by HA supported by SHvW. In parallel, MS coded three transcripts to provide an additional disciplinary perspective as a health professional, support reflexive engagement with the data, and enhance the trustworthiness of the analytical process. Discussions of similarities and differences in interpretation were used to critically examine assumptions and explore alternative readings of the data.26 These discussions informed the ongoing refinement of codes and themes. HA generated initial themes by grouping codes using a mind-map technique. These were refined through a series of team meetings, a process that continued throughout the writing phase. For example, the third theme (Navigating sensitivity around HPV and sex) was generated relatively late in the process, as we initially connected these codes to school nurses’ dual responsibilities and their stated efforts to respect cultural and religious beliefs. Through team discussions, however, this interpretation was expanded to encompass broader societal norms and assumptions surrounding sexuality and gender, which ultimately shaped the final theme.

All transcripts were kept in the original language throughout analysis. Extracts included in this report were translated into English by HA during write-up. Translation inevitably risks losing some of the participants’ own ‘voices’; to mitigate that, we maintained a list of both original and translated extracts, allowing us to continuously review and, if needed, revise the translations.

Reflexivity

RTA highlights the active role of the researcher, where their subjectivity serves as a resource inevitably influencing the analytical process and knowledge generation.25,26 Thus, we acknowledge that our beliefs and experiences shaped all aspects of this work. Throughout the analysis, HA kept a ‘diary’ where she noted her thoughts and reflections, whether related to specific data segments or the set as a whole. These notes helped inform the development and revision of themes. We believe that the use of methodologically coherent quality guidelines further supported reflexivity by requiring us to continuously reflect on our positionalities and assumptions, and how they influenced our work, including methodological choices.

HA is a Swedish-born woman who grew up and attended compulsory school in a geographical area of Stockholm similar to those represented in this study. However, she does not consider herself a member of or ‘insider’ to any undervaccinated community as defined here.

MS is a Swedish Midwife who has work experience as a clinician working with undervaccinated communities in southern Stockholm, and communities associated with anthroposophist beliefs in Järna, Stockholm. She has contributed to research projects on vaccine confidences as an interviewer.

SHvW is a European associate professor who has conducted multiple research projects in undervaccinated communities in Sweden and abroad. With training in social sciences, she has guided the project based on that training and previous research experience in Sweden.

Results

Themes and subthemes generated from interviews with school nurses.

School nurses are left to manage HPV vaccination by themselves

Obtaining HPV (vaccine) knowledge requires active inquiry

Most participants perceived their knowledge about the HPV vaccine sufficient to provide basic information to adolescents and parents and expressed confidence in answering potential questions. However, this knowledge was not taught through official training but resulted from their own inquiries, and thus, understanding depended on the school nurse’s ability to access information and level of interest in the topic. Participants generally agreed that their knowledge had increased over time, but some implicitly suggested that they lacked in-depth knowledge, expressing uncertainty about, for example, the added medical benefits of gender-neutral vaccination or whether they could address the concerns of more informed parents. …I felt that this is a vaccine [the HPV vaccine] that I need to read up on a little more […]. So, it was a little lesson for me too when I first learned about this vaccine, because I never really knew about it myself.(SN 20)

…I felt that this is a vaccine [the HPV vaccine] that I need to read up on a little more […]. So, it was a little lesson for me too when I first learned about this vaccine, because I never really knew about it myself.

Knowledge was suggested to be necessary, but not sufficient, for participants to feel confident providing HPV vaccine information. One participant described this in a general sense, saying “I may have medical knowledge, but to actually talk in the right way. That’s the difficult part” (SN 18). Others found certain situations, such as interactions with vaccine hesitant parents, challenging. One participant expressed doubt in her ability to truly inform adolescents, as she described that she would answer questions in a way that satisfied them, but still, “you, yourself, feel that you would like to have more tools to explain, I mean in a simpler way, or I don’t know … ” (SN 1).

Not sufficiently equipped – organizational challenges, lack of support and feelings of inadequacy

All participants expressed some degree of frustration about the lack of support offered to them in their professional roles. Many called for societal efforts to enhance general awareness of HPV and HPV vaccination. They suggested that the public may still perceive the HPV vaccine as ‘new,’ saying that many seem unaware of its inclusion in the national childhood vaccines program, mistakenly believing it is an ‘extra’ offering. They noted that while other vaccines are regularly acknowledged in the media, the HPV vaccine largely remained neglected, adding an unnecessary burden on school nurses. Participants argued that as the elimination of HPV-related cancer is a societal commitment, it is unjust that the responsibility to encourage vaccine uptake falls solely on school nurses: It shouldn’t just be thrown at schools, for example, that we should take responsibility for this and that, because it is something in society, that everyone should work towards together.(SN 8)

It shouldn’t just be thrown at schools, for example, that we should take responsibility for this and that, because it is something in society, that everyone should work towards together.

There was a general acknowledgment of the solitary nature of the role of a school nurse. Although participants generally described their collaboration with other school personnel as successful, their experiences suggested a lack of organizational support structures and high dependency on the individual school nurse. This absence of structure and standardization (including subsequent attempts by the school nurse to make the best of difficult situations) resulted in a variety of work methods and practices, making both specific aspects of HPV vaccination and the program itself vulnerable to staff turnover. One example was provided by a participant talking about her experience with a temporary position, where she came to a new school and learnt that very few adolescents had received all their vaccinations according to schedule: They hadn’t had a school nurse for a long time. And the one who was there before hadn’t been so focused on the vaccinations, so there was a lot that was lagging behind.(SN 9)

They hadn’t had a school nurse for a long time. And the one who was there before hadn’t been so focused on the vaccinations, so there was a lot that was lagging behind.

Some participants were employed by agencies rather than the schools directly, and many held part-time positions. Among these participants, frequent relocations and sharing their time between different schools were common. One participant articulated the precariousness of their situation, saying “it’s all about money, so schools just employ school nurses at as low a percentage as they can” (SN 13). This would often conflict with the needs of adolescents and parents in undervaccinated communities, where “you might have to take even more time to talk and discuss, and maybe even have an interpreter” (SN 1). Instead, time constraints often forced participants to prioritize between tasks, and many felt their time was not enough to provide all the necessary information. Similarly, refusals were handled differently across schools, often depending on the school nurses’ workload. Some participants regularly contacted all parents of unvaccinated adolescents, while others found this unfeasible due to limited time and resources, leading to reliance on adolescents and parents to reach out if they changed their minds.

Individual care and public health – the school nurses’ dual responsibilities

Promoting HPV vaccine uptake – building trust while remaining objective

Participants viewed vaccination as crucial to their health promotion and disease prevention efforts. While there was a general agreement on the importance of remaining objective when talking about the HPV vaccine, they recognized their ability to influence vaccine uptake and stressed the importance of actively fostering vaccine confidence. Working on prevention and promotion is crucial in order to reduce the spread of HPV and other diseases that we work with. So, it’s important to continue with it and not, like, sit back and think that ‘we’re doing enough’. There’s always something that can be done.(SN 2)

Working on prevention and promotion is crucial in order to reduce the spread of HPV and other diseases that we work with. So, it’s important to continue with it and not, like, sit back and think that ‘we’re doing enough’. There’s always something that can be done.

Participants highlighted the importance of continuity of care and strong and trusting relationships between them, parents and adolescents. Participants were often asked about their vaccine decisions and whether they would vaccinate their adolescents if they had any. Opinions differed regarding the appropriateness of disclosing this type of personal information, and some avoided doing so, believing information should not be mixed with personal opinions. In contrast, others felt that sharing personal experiences related to vaccine decision-making and HPV-related diseases added sincerity to their recommendations. Some participants felt that sharing cultural backgrounds or belonging to the same ethnic minority enhanced their trustworthiness in discussions with parents, saying that if parents “hear this, that it [the HPV vaccine] is okay, from someone who has the same background, then that means it’s okay” (SN 12).

Making HPV vaccination a positive, empowering experience for the adolescent

Informing adolescents about HPV vaccination and its purpose was sometimes challenging as participants had to balance between sharing information and the risk of scaring the adolescents. Participants described the 11-year-olds as intelligent and well-informed, yet highlighted how rapidly fear could spread among them. Talking about HPV causing cancer would sometimes result in distress among adolescents whose parents declined the vaccine. More often, however, fear was centered around pain and the size of the needle. Fear was perceived as the primary reason adolescents declined the vaccine: I don’t think that the children don’t want it [the HPV vaccine], but they don’t want the shot. They want to take the medicine, but not the injection. Does that make sense? I think that’s it.(SN 5)

I don’t think that the children don’t want it [the HPV vaccine], but they don’t want the shot. They want to take the medicine, but not the injection. Does that make sense? I think that’s it.

The adolescent could often be persuaded by either the school nurse or its parents. However, when that was not possible, participants said that they neither could nor wanted to force anyone. This was a particularly prominent issue for participants working with adolescents with intellectual disabilities, with some expressing concerns about whether the benefits of vaccination outweighed the stress and distress it caused: Yes, that is another dilemma. Whether you’re going to vaccinate, because these are children with severe disabilities. We always offer it, of course, but sometimes its not ethically right to give it. I mean, it’s perhaps more trouble for a child to take it than not to.(SN 1)

Yes, that is another dilemma. Whether you’re going to vaccinate, because these are children with severe disabilities. We always offer it, of course, but sometimes its not ethically right to give it. I mean, it’s perhaps more trouble for a child to take it than not to.

Generally, participants found it helpful to frame the vaccination as something positive in the eyes of the adolescents. Using positive reinforcement, offering snacks and rewarding adolescents with a sticker or rubber gum, helped reduce anxiety about vaccinations. Distraction techniques, such as allowing adolescents to decorate their band-aids, were thought to make the experience less intimidating. However, some expressed doubt about where to draw the line between using incentives to encourage cooperation and potentially crossing into coercion. Sometimes I think… am I tricking them all? In some way. Somehow… but I think… but it’s for their own good, that they have this vaccine, because I believe in vaccinations. But sometimes I think like… is this really right?(SN 4)

Sometimes I think… am I tricking them all? In some way. Somehow… but I think… but it’s for their own good, that they have this vaccine, because I believe in vaccinations. But sometimes I think like… is this really right?

It was relatively common for adolescents with declining parents to express a will to receive the HPV vaccination.b Some participants found it easier to contact the parents in those cases, as they could frame the conversation around the adolescents’ wishes, highlighting how participants seemed to adopt strategies for non-confrontational engagement with parents. They emphasized the importance of listening to parents’ arguments without belittling them and that they must not be perceived as pushing for the vaccine, demonstrating how participants’ desire to respect the parental decision sometimes limited their ability to advocate for the adolescent.

HPV vaccination as ‘the right thing’

Still, participants generally spoke about HPV vaccine refusals as something to be corrected into the ‘right’ decision and often dismissed those who declined HPV vaccination as either ignorant or uninformed. Parents who had previously declined other vaccines were commonly labeled as being anti-vaccine. Several participants considered it pointless to engage with them, as “you can’t talk to them because you can never convin… you can’t really convince them.” (SN 15). However, most declines were considered the result of insufficient knowledge and participants seemed to think these could be prevented by providing proper information: People listen to the news from their home country and get information from there and stuff like that. And then … well, that’s just how it is. […] So I think that can actually have an impact. If you could somehow get … somehow get them to … that they get access to the right information.(SN 14)

People listen to the news from their home country and get information from there and stuff like that. And then … well, that’s just how it is. […] So I think that can actually have an impact. If you could somehow get … somehow get them to … that they get access to the right information.

Nevertheless, participants also speculated that some parents gave what could be considered ‘uninformed consent’, believing they did not fully understand what they agreed to when they signed the consent form. Participants offered various possible explanations for this phenomenon, including strong trust in the authority of schools and healthcare services, confusion between the HPV vaccine and vaccines against other diseases, and being unaware of the right to decline vaccination. Often, they sign just because it comes from the school. And the school has this mandate, or the school knows what it is that is best for the students. It’s not often that actually read up on it themselves.(SN 6)

Often, they sign just because it comes from the school. And the school has this mandate, or the school knows what it is that is best for the students. It’s not often that actually read up on it themselves.

Some suggested that migrant parents, in particular, may simply accept the vaccine out of gratitude without fully comprehending the details: “people from other countries are usually very grateful that they’ll be getting all these vaccinations and will be protected, so they just agree to everything.” (SN 9). Notably, several participants felt uneasy when they suspected receiving an uninformed consent, highlighting how sentiments regarding the importance of informed decision-making were often contradictory and depending on the situation.

Navigating sensitivity around HPV and sex

Too much, too soon – navigating an unknown area

The link between HPV and sex often made conversations around the vaccine difficult, and many participants felt that it negatively affected HPV vaccine uptake. They suspected that parents equated the vaccine to sexual activity and that they instinctively reacted by thinking that their adolescent was not having sex or that HPV vaccination might lead to earlier sexual activity. Participants believed that some parents considered the vaccine premature or unnecessary at this age, and that they struggled with the idea that protection against HPV is still important even though their adolescents are typically not yet sexually active. Well, as soon as you mention anything about sexual contact, people… sort of panic, and think that their children… so I don’t know if there’s any way you could make it a bit less loaded.(SN 21)

Well, as soon as you mention anything about sexual contact, people… sort of panic, and think that their children… so I don’t know if there’s any way you could make it a bit less loaded.

These discussions were further complicated if the adolescent had an intellectual disability. One participant described how some parents believed their adolescent might not be able to engage in consensual sexual activities, for which the HPV vaccine raised concerns leading to heavy conversations about the risk of acquiring an infection from a sexual assault.

Encountering gendered assumptions

Participants were often confronted with heteronormative and patriarchal ideas about sex and gender. In their experience, parents mainly associated HPV with cervical cancer and perceived it as something affecting girls. Hence, parents of boys would sometimes think that if girls were vaccinated, their sons would be sufficiently protected by herd immunity, thereby implying that their son would not be having sex with other men. Additionally, many parents held cultural and religious views that prioritize abstinence until marriage. They thus argued that “the vaccine is not necessary because their children will not be having sex before they’re married, or they will wait until marriage” (SN 20), and “then they will have the same partner for the rest of their lives” (SN 18).

Many felt it was important to highlight the role of boys and men in HPV transmission when responding to these concerns. However, in their efforts to do so, it was common to confirm and reproduce heteronormative and gendered social norms about sex. One participant recollected a mother stating that her daughter would only ever have sex with her husband: ”… and then I said ‘but the guy, he might be with some other girls before, so he’s the one infecting his wife.’ And we had a long discussion.” (SN 15). Yet, participants appeared aware of these normative assumptions, illustrated by the same participant recognizing that sociocultural expectations dictate that “the man should be experienced sexually and the woman should be inexperienced” (SN 15). Participants acknowledged that these assumptions are often unrealistic and that many adolescents would, for example, engage in sexual activities before marriage, but felt it was beyond the scope of their role and competence to engage in these discussions. …but yes, it’s difficult. I can’t sit and say to parents ‘but your child might be having sex without you knowing about it’. It’s not a discussion I feel I want to get into. I think my job is just to inform and advise.(SN 20)

…but yes, it’s difficult. I can’t sit and say to parents ‘but your child might be having sex without you knowing about it’. It’s not a discussion I feel I want to get into. I think my job is just to inform and advise.

Albeit uncommon, some participants actively omitted information about how HPV is acquired when they informed adolescents and parents about the HPV vaccine. These participants referred to the ‘sensitivity’ of the topic, claiming that the idea of having sex before marriage or with more than one partner is ‘heavily loaded’ and associated with a lot of ‘shame’ and ‘guilt’ for some religious and cultural groups. Notably, contrary to most participants, those who omitted the link between HPV and sex had not experienced that parents declined the vaccine due to the sexual transmission of HPV. Yet, they believed that including this information would negatively influence HPV vaccine uptake.

Discussion

This study sought to understand the challenges associated with implementing HPV vaccination programs, as perceived by school nurses working in undervaccinated communities in Stockholm.

The first key finding was that, despite relatively high confidence in and uptake of HPV vaccination,27,28 school nurses were largely left to navigate implementation challenges on their own while balancing personal insecurities with substantial organizational constraints. This echoes previous research suggesting that implementation of health interventions in schools tends to be contingent on strong support from school personnel, including the principal and school nurse, as well as sufficient time and resources.29–31 Swedish school nurses have reported that administrative burdens and limited time hinder health promotion activities.32–35 While resource constraints in school health services may be difficult to address in the short term, targeted support could facilitate school nurses’ HPV vaccination efforts. This includes access to clear, up-to-date information, tailored promotion materials for adolescents and parents in multiple languages, guides on HPV vaccination practices, and communication aids to support conversations with hesitant parents; approaches that have shown to be effective in other settings.36–38 Communication support is particularly relevant, as Swedish studies show that pupils and parents often rely heavily on school nurses as trusted sources of HPV vaccine information,39–41 while international studies suggest that structured provider communication training and motivational interviewing can improve conversations with HPV vaccine-hesitant parents.42,43 Moreover, we found that school nurses lack not only time but also the confidence to engage with hesitant parents or follow up on unvaccinated adolescents. Given that active follow-up by healthcare workers has been shown to increase the likelihood that parents who initially hesitate or decline the HPV vaccine will later accept it,44,45 providing Swedish school nurses with communication support and clearer guidelines on when and how to follow up on unvaccinated adolescents may improve vaccine uptake, as well as enhance both standardization across schools and the school nurses’ confidence in engaging in these conversations.

Secondly, by illustrating how school nurses tended to think of parents who were hesitant to or refused the vaccine as either uninformed or ‘anti-vaccine,’ we add to previous Swedish findings suggesting that school nurses and healthcare workers more broadly struggle to address these parents.11,19,46 This finding may be understood through the lens of moralizing discourse47 and may lead to vaccine-related stigma,11 as hesitant parents are positioned as ‘bad’ healthcare users,48 either non-compliant and unruly or as lacking sufficient knowledge. Similar profiling has been reported elsewhere, where it has been linked to preconceived notions about ethnicity and religious and socio-cultural beliefs.49–51 Similar to what was described in a recent Italian study, this appeared to result in deficit-oriented assumptions about migrant parents rather than in profound reflection on potential social and structural barriers to information.49 The framing of ethnic minority healthcare users as ‘different’ and ‘unknowing’ is consistent with literature on structural racism in healthcare settings.51–53 A comparable logic appeared in our data when school nurses explained why some parents unquestionably accepted vaccination. Hence, these parents’ knowledge and understanding were questioned even when they conformed to the norm and did the ‘right thing’, and ultimately, they were deprived of their agency in vaccine decision-making as it was assumed that any decision resulted from insufficient knowledge, potentially demonstrating how structural racism may operate as disempowerment of certain healthcare users.54 Moreover, explaining hesitancy by lack of understanding ignores that cultural barriers, past negative experiences with healthcare and other social institutions, and feelings of stigmatization may present as a limited understanding or disinterest.55 In a study on measles, mumps and rubella vaccination perspectives among Somali mothers in Stockholm, some participants described how nurses dismissed and judged them as they delayed vaccination and how, consequently, their questions remained unanswered.56 Such dissatisfactory healthcare experiences can weaken both vaccine confidence and general trust in healthcare systems.54,57,58 Thus, fostering rapport and addressing potential concerns in an open, respectful, and non-judgmental manner may help support positive vaccination experiences, particularly among undervaccinated groups.

Lastly, we found that normative and gendered ideas about sex may hinder HPV vaccination. Lindén59 has previously described how HPV has been framed as synonymous with cervical cancer. Such framings help maintain the image of sexual health as a female responsibility and follow a long tradition of perceiving female sexuality and the female body as something to be governed.60,61 Some school nurses confronted with beliefs about abstinence and purity tended to reinforce these norms. Mostly, they appeared to do so out of concern for the topic’s sensitivity and a strong will not to offend cultural or religious beliefs. This adds to the findings of Odenbring and Lindén, suggesting that implementation of gender-neutral vaccination may not be sufficient to disrupt traditional, gendered perceptions of sexual health and responsibility.6 Not only were perceptions of sexuality strongly heteronormative, with accounts of non-heterosexual relations largely absent, but we found that school nurses working with adolescents with intellectual disabilities faced further difficulties as they had to address parental worry about sexual abuse. It is imperative that school nurses working with adolescents with intellectual disabilities are equipped to handle these difficult conversations. These parental concerns must be acknowledged, as individuals with intellectual disabilities are at substantially greater risk of sexual abuse compared to the overall population.62 Nevertheless, they could also be interpreted as an embodiment of a societal inability to consider persons with intellectual disabilities as sexual beings,63 and HPV vaccination could provide an opportunity to challenge these assumptions.

Limitations

This study has some limitations, the most prominent being that we were unable to obtain any official data on HPV vaccine uptake per school. The numbers presented in this paper were reported by participants. This meant that we could not reach out to schools based on their HPV vaccine uptake. Although this limitation cannot be reduced entirely, it was lessened by using municipality-level uptake data and snowball sampling, asking participants if they knew of schools with low HPV vaccine uptake. Nevertheless, it is reasonable to think that the school nurses included in the study are generally committed to the HPV vaccination program and ensuring high vaccine uptake. While they provided valuable insights, this limited the range of perspectives captured here. Hence, it would have been interesting to also hear from school nurses who either do not recognize their vaccine uptake as low or perceive it as a problem. Similarly, one participant’s decision to decline audio-recording for the interview highlights the sensitivity of this topic and suggests that participants may feel uneasy about expressing differing opinions, illustrating how social desirability likely influenced our findings. Hence, while the findings presented here could have implications for the organization of school health services in similar settings, the transferability of the results must be carefully evaluated.

Conclusion

School nurses are uniquely positioned to influence HPV vaccine confidence and uptake. However, our findings indicate a strong reliance on the individual school nurse. They recognize that HPV vaccination poses greater challenges for some adolescents and parents than others. Despite striving to accommodate these diverse needs, the heterogeneity in practices appeared to result more from time and resource constraints than deliberate, context-specific adaptations. Whereas this can risk the sustainability of the HPV vaccination program, it also suggests that support such as continuous training, clearer guidelines, and additional resources is critical to harness the full potential of their role. With this in place, it may be possible to increase HPV vaccine uptake and further accelerate the elimination of HPV-related cancers.
