 ABSTRACT
This report describes how to produce reference solutions for the equilibrium core search of pebble bed
reactors using the pyrates Python library. The pyrates library uses the kugelpy methodology to perform
running-in calculations to reach an equilibrium core. This approach relies on full-core Monte Carlo neu-
tron transport calculations and the shifting of fuel pebble inventory through the reactor to simulate pebble
movement in the core from one step to the next of a running-in scenario. In addition to the use of Serpent
for the Monte Carlo neutron transport calculations, as part of this work, updates for the use of the Shift
Monte Carlo code were integrated into the official pyrates GitHub repository. Comparisons of running-
in simulations for a generic pebble bed reactor produced by using pyrates with Serpent and Shift are
shown. Although consistent trends are shown between the use of the two Monte Carlo codes, the limiting
factor in either calculation is the computation time due to the need to perform several hundred full-core
neutron transport calculations before an equilibrium core is reached.

 1. INTRODUCTION
The US Department of Energy (DOE) Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) pro-
gram develops advanced modeling and simulation (M&S) tools to accelerate the deployment of advanced
nuclear energy technologies. The reactor physics technical area focuses on the development of M&S tools
for modeling reactor physics phenomena. The specific focus of this work is methods for modeling and
simulating pebble bed reactors (PBRs).
A key aspect of M&S for PBRs is the estimation of fuel inventory. Any other computational analysis (e.g.,
performing accident analysis using multiphysics tools) relies on the accurate prediction of the initial reac-
tor state, including accurate inventory of the reactor. The determination of accurate fuel inventory is non-
trivial because of the continuous operation of PBRs, during which thousands of fuel pebbles travel through
the core. Each fuel pebble’s burnup is measured after each pass through the reactor core, and fuel peb-
bles at the target burnup are replaced by fresh fuel pebbles. The initial years of operation of a PBR add
complexity to the simulation because these reactors are started with a mixture of fresh fuel pebbles and
graphite moderator pebbles. During this so-called running-in phase, the graphite pebbles are slowly re-
moved, and the start-up fuel pebbles (which are potentially at a lower enrichment) are replaced by fuel
pebbles at the target enrichment. After the running-in phase, which can possibly take years, PBRs operate
at a state of equilibrium, in which pebble refueling rates, average pebble discharge burnup, and the average
fuel inventory are expected to be approximately constant over time.
This work leveraged previous efforts at Idaho National Laboratory (INL) to model the running-in of a
PBR. Stewart, Balestra, et al. developed a Python library called pyrates that is hosted on the INL GitHub.
The kugelpy module of the pyrates library performs a running-in calculation to determine fuel inventory
for the equilibrium core of a PBR using the Serpent Monte Carlo (MC) code (Leppänen et al. 2015)
as the neutron transport solver (Stewart, Balestra, et al. 2022; Stewart et al. 2023; Stewart, Balestra, et
al. 2024; Stewart, Cavaluzzi, et al. 2024). Previous work in FY 2023 confirmed the viability of integrat-
ing the Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) Shift MC code into pyrates to produce equilibrium core
solutions using the running-in process (Pandya et al. 2023).
In FY 2025, Shift was properly integrated into the official pyrates GitHub repository. To test the new in-
tegration and to assess its performance on a computing cluster, a reference equilibrium core solution for
the generic pebble bed reactor (GPBR) was produced using Shift via kugelpy. This solution was com-
pared with a solution produced using Serpent via kugelpy.

 2. METHOD
2.1 BACKGROUND
PBRs operate for most of their lifetimes at a state of equilibrium. In an equilibrium core, the entire core
consists of fuel pebbles at different levels of burnup. Fresh fuel pebbles are continuously added to the core,
and fuel pebbles that have reached the target discharge burnup are continuously removed from the core. At
a constant power level and with the locations of the control rods being static (likely fully withdrawn), aver-
age conditions in any given region in the core (e.g., region-average pebble burnup, fuel inventory, temper-
atures) are approximately constant. The pebbles experience minimal radial motion as they move through
the core. Therefore, it is appropriate to assume that pebbles mainly travel axially through the core and that
radial movement can be neglected (Reger et al. 2023). Depending on the geometric detail of the pebble
bed (for example, the presence of conical areas in addition to a main cylindrical core or the presence of
dimples in the outer structural graphite), pebbles in the different radial regions of the core may travel with
different axial speeds.
In an equilibrium core, fuel pebbles have an average number of times that they travel through the core
(i.e., have an average number of passes) before they are discharged because they have reached their tar-
get discharge burnup. In gas-cooled PBRs, pebbles are continuously added to the top of the core, and they
travel to the bottom—the opposite of the direction of travel in fluoride-salt-cooled high-temperature reac-
tors (FHRs). At the bottom of the core, pebbles are unloaded, and their integrity and burnup are evaluated
before they are either reinserted into the core or discharged. Consequently, an axial zone in the core al-
ways includes a mixture of pebbles at these different passes. The closer an axial zone is to the bottom of
the core, the higher the average pebble burnup is in this zone.
The initial loading of a PBR is a mixture of fresh fuel pebbles at potentially different (lower) enrichments
and graphite pebbles that do not contain any fuel. During the running-in phase, the graphite pebbles are
continuously replaced by fuel pebbles, and lower-enrichment fuel pebbles are replaced by fuel pebbles at
the target equilibrium core enrichment. This running-in phase can take months to years depending on the
reactor (Zhang, Li, and Sun 2017).
2.2 MODELING APPROACH FOR EQUILIBRIUM CORES
To model an equilibrium core, either the zone-wise pebble inventories at equilibrium can be estimated di-
rectly using an iterative approach (jump-in approach), or the running-in process can be simulated. INL
has developed Python scripts (called kugelpy) wrapped around the Serpent MC code to allow the deter-
mination of an equilibrium core via both the jump-in and the running-in approaches (Stewart, Balestra, et
al. 2022; Stewart et al. 2023; Stewart, Balestra, et al. 2024; Stewart, Cavaluzzi, et al. 2024). Using a num-
ber of user input parameters (e.g., equilibrium fresh fuel compositions, startup fresh fuel compositions,
power, temperature), these scripts manage the transition of fuel pebbles—or rather, the transition of fuel
compositions—from one zone to another after each depletion step. By continuously running full-core de-
pletion calculations and moving the fuel compositions, the scripts simulate the running-in process. More
details about kugelpy’s method for tracking and shuffling pebbles are available in the referenced litera-
ture (Stewart, Balestra, et al. 2022; Stewart et al. 2023; Stewart, D.Reger, et al. 2022; Stewart, Balestra, et
al. 2024).
In its current implementation, kugelpy generates a simple or complex reactor model for the GPBR with
its structure as well as the tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) fuel particles and fuel pebbles (see model de-
scription in Section 3). This model includes the materials and geometric details of these items but not the
temperatures and fresh fuel compositions that are provided as input parameters to kugelpy. An overview of
all relevant parameters that the user can control is given here.

For the first year of the running-in process, kugelpy manages the transition from cold zero power (CZP)
to hot full power (HFP). During this time, the graphite pebbles are slowly removed, and the start-up fuel
pebbles are slowly replaced by equilibrium fuel pebbles. After this first year, there are only equilibrium
fuel pebbles in the core. The continuous shuffling of the fuel pebbles, the discharge of pebbles that have
traveled a certain number of times through the core, and the addition of fresh equilibrium fuel pebbles to
replace the discharged fuel pebbles allow the core to continuously converge to an equilibrium state.
kugelpy groups pebbles according to their location in the core (inside specific zones) and according to the
number of times that they have traveled through the core. For example, if the maximum number of passes
that a pebble can take through the core is assumed to be six, then each spatial zone includes six pebble
“types.” The pebble compositions are homogenized across the zone and type.
The velocities with which the pebbles travel through the core and, in turn, the pebble circulation and dis-
charge rate are driven by the time steps. The time steps are based on the user-provided time step input as
well as possible refinements based on the target keff criteria.
Pebbles are discharged after they have traveled a certain number of times through the core rather than be-
ing discharged based on burnup. This choice was made because pebble compositions are homogenized
across a group (i.e., the burnup information of individual pebbles is not known) (Nader Satvat et al. 2021).
In order to discharge pebbles based on burnup, a hyperfidelity approach is needed, and that is not imple-
mented in kugelpy (Robert, Siaraferas, and Fratoni 2023).

 4

kugelpy currently does not check equilibrium conditions, but it continues to run until a final provided time
has been reached. During execution, a result file that provides an overview of key parameters that help
users establish whether an equilibrium has already been reached is continuously updated. These key pa-
rameters include keff , pebble discard rates, minimum/maximum/average discharge burnup, average burnup
after each pass, and average power density for each pass. Additionally, the parameters of the power level,
temperatures, graphite fractions, and fuel types (start-up fuel versus equilibrium fuel) are included as input
parameters.
As stated in Stewart, Balestra, et al. (2024), the running-in process is affected by many of the listed pa-
rameters that affect the average converged discharge burnup, isotopic distribution in the spent fuel, pebble
power peaking factors, and how fast an equilibrium state is reached, among other factors. Sensitivity and
uncertainty analyses are required to enable a more fundamental understanding of how the running-in pro-
cess works and where the limitations in the current approach exist.

2.3 IMPLEMENTATION AND SETUP WITH SHIFT
This work built upon the initial investigation of the viability of using Shift with kugelpy to perform running-
in calculations of PBRs (Pandya et al. 2023). In the present work, Shift was formally integrated into
the pyrates GitHub repository, which is hosted at git@github.inl.gov:reactor-multiphysics/pyrates. The
README was updated to include information on setting up the environment needed to use Shift.
Five pull requests were made and merged into the main branch of the pyrates repository to enable Shift
integration with minimal code duplication between the Serpent and Shift methods. The main class used
to perform running-in calculations is the ShiftPebbleSorter. Details on using this new implementation
are provided in Section 2.4.
Before any calculations are run, the proper environment must be set up. Part of this information is included
in the README in the pyrates repository. Detailed instructions are provided here.

The following capabilities are enabled with Serpent in kugelpy but not with Shift. Plans to implement
these missing capabilities are discussed in Section 5.1.
 • Automatic generation of the GPBR structural geometry
 • Integration with Griffin to support multiphysics feedback
 • Tallying of the power for individual pebbles rather than groups of pebbles (a group of pebbles being

One feature added for both Shift and Serpent using kugelpy is the ability to perform a restart with re-
producibility. The restart capability for running-in calculations with Serpent already existed. This work
added this restart capability for Shift running-in calculations. Reproducibility can be achieved by sav-
ing the Python random number generator state for each step of the running-in calculation and writing this
object to the pickle file. This random number generator state is then restored when loading data from this
pickled state to continue the running-in calculation.

2.4 PERFORMING RUNNING-IN
Once the pyrates and Shift environments have been set up on a compute platform, performing a running-
in calculation with kugelpy requires defining the parameters discussed in Section 2.2 in a Python input.
The scripts outlined below are specific to performing running-in calculations for the GPBR with Shift in
kugelpy.
Listing 1 shows the Python script in which all relevant input parameters are set before kugelpy is called to
perform the running-in simulation. There are four main parts:
 1. Create steps: Generate a dictionary to store model parameters as a function of time during the first
 year of the running-in simulation (power level, graphite pebble fraction, temperatures, time stepping)
 2. Initialize ShiftPebbleSorter with:
 • Fuel compositions for start-up fuel and fresh fuel added after start-up
 • Neutron transport settings
 • Simulation time steps and maximum total time
 • Control rod position
 • Parameters for running-in from step 1
 3. Add reactor details:
 • Pebble streamlines to separate pebbles into radial and axial regions, with each radial region
 having its own number of axial zones
 • Pebble distribution file with axial offset
 4. Perform running-in
The step definition only causes an update of the listed parameters during the first year of the running-in.
As described before, parameters like the power level and the graphite pebble fraction are changed during
this year only. After the first year, there are only equilibrium fuel pebbles being transitioned through the
core, and these pebbles are discharged and replaced after they reach their target number of passes.

 3. MODEL
Two models of the GPBR were produced with Shift and Serpent. The full details of the GPBR de-
sign are described in previous publications (Stewart, Balestra, et al. 2022; Stewart et al. 2023; Pandya et
al. 2023).
The pebble bed contains approximately 220,000 fuel pebbles (packing fraction ∼61%), and each pebble
contains 18,687 TRISO fuel particles distributed randomly in a graphite matrix (packing fraction ∼9.344%).
The fuel region is divided into five channels with multiple axial zones, resulting in 59 pebble regions.
Because each fuel pebble travels, on average, six times through the reactor core (i.e., six passes) before
reaching a maximum discharge burnup of 150 GWd/tU, the model contains 354 unique pebble definitions,
with each unique pebble being found in one specific region only. The detailed model includes 18 riser, 9
control, and 9 safety rod channels, and in all the calculations herein, the rods were fully withdrawn and
the riser channels did not include any pebbles. This full-core detailed model also includes dimples on the
graphite structural walls that contain the pebble bed.
The Shift full-core detailed model, shown in Figure 3, was modified from the previously used model
(Pandya et al. 2023) to take advantage of runtime and memory optimizations added to Shift.

 Figure 3. Shift GPBR full-core model.

GPBR model that can be automatically generated by kugelpy. The simple Serpent model was generated
by setting the create_dimples parameter to False and the simple_core parameter to True. Remov-
ing the dimples, risers, control rods, and safety rods from the detailed model and simplifying the pebble

4.1 PERFORMANCE
First, a performance comparison of the Shift and Serpent detailed and simple models is provided. These
new performance calculations were performed on an internal ORNL compute cluster.
For each full-core neutron transport calculation, 2,000 neutron histories per generation in 200 active and
40 inactive generations were simulated, which is sufficient for a performance comparison. For perfor-
mance assessment, the memory and computation time for a single Shift full-core neutron depletion cal-
culation is compared with a corresponding Serpent calculation in Table 1. Five realizations of each single
CPU simulation were run to obtain average memory usage and computation times. To ensure consistency
between the models, the Shift and Serpent models corresponding to the first step in a kugelpy simula-
tion were used. These models only included fresh fuel pebbles and graphite pebbles but avoided potential
differences in pebble inventory and location in the next steps. The models were slightly adjusted to only
tally the flux and power in the fuel materials. Based upon previous calculations, Serpent was run using
optimization mode 1 to reduce the memory usage as much as possible at the cost of performance optimiza-
tion. The default delta tracking setting was also used in Serpent.

4.2 RUNNING-IN
Figure 6 shows the time-dependent change of input parameters as defined via the steps in the Python script
that calls kugelpy, as described in Section 2.4. During the first year of the running-in process, the power
and the temperatures are brought to HFP conditions and subsequently remain constant.
The graphite pebble fraction is best explained via the visualization of the different pebble types in the core
in Figure 7. Initially, the core is filled up to a certain height with only graphite moderator pebbles (Fig-
ure 7a, black). A mixture of start-up fuel pebbles (red) and graphite moderator pebbles is placed above this
level, with graphite pebbles making up 42% of the total pebbles. As the pebbles shift downward, a mixture
of start-up fuel and graphite pebbles is placed into the core, and the volume of only the graphite pebbles
is reduced (Figure 7b). In the fuel and graphite pebble mixture that is placed into the core, the graphite
pebble fraction is continuously reduced over the first ∼60 steps (as indicated for the corresponding input
parameter in Figure 6) such that after a while, the core consists almost entirely of start-up fuel pebbles
(Figure 7c).
Once there are few graphite pebbles in the core, equilibrium fuel pebbles are introduced (Figure 7d). Over
time, the makeup of pebbles in the core transitions to a mixture of start-up and equilibrium fuel pebbles
(Figure 7e). The start-up pebbles are continuously discharged so that the fraction of equilibrium fuel peb-
bles is continuously increasing, and eventually the core consists of only equilibrium fuel pebbles (Fig-
ure 7f). The discharge of start-up and equilibrium pebbles is driven by the number of passes that the peb-
bles have traveled through the core. The start-up pebbles that are initially present in the core are assigned
a randomly sampled pass number. This approach assumes that fuel utilization is not a target metric for the
operation of the running-in but rather that the intention is to remove the start-up pebbles from the core rela-
tively quickly.
These running-in calculations are very computationally intensive. Equilibrium conditions are established
once the fuel inventory across the zones of the reactor are converged such that they are approximately con-
stant over time. In order to achieve this goal, the running-in calculation must be run out to beyond 3000
days (Stewart, Balestra, et al. 2024). The computing cluster details of the simulations performed with
Shift and Serpent through kugelpy to attempt to achieve equilibrium of the GPBR are summarized in
Table 2. All of these simulations used 20,000 particle histories per generation for 200 active and 4 inactive
generations for each neutron transport calculation. Both Shift and Serpent performed a fully-explicit
depletion scheme for each depletion step. Multiple restarts of kugelpy were needed due to the walltime
limit of the computing clusters used.

 5. CONCLUSIONS
The work in FY 2025 involved the integration of ORNL’s Shift MC code into the official pyrates GitHub
repository. Users of pyrates can now use Shift in addition to the MC code Serpent to perform running-
in simulations of PBRs with the kugelpy module. The result of these simulations is the fuel inventory of
the PBR during the running-in phase and for the state of equilibrium operation. Additionally, other pa-
rameters such as keff , pebble discard rates, minimum/maximum/average discharge burnup, average burnup
after each pass, and average power density for each pass are tracked automatically. In its current imple-
mentation, kugelpy with both Serpent and Shift supports simulations of the GPBR only.
To test the new integration and to assess its performance on a computing cluster, a reference equilibrium
core solution for the GPBR was produced using Shift via kugelpy. This solution was compared with so-
lutions produced using Serpent via kugelpy. The computation time of the neutron transport calculation
is the limiting factor for both simulations because a running-in simulation to equilibrium may require sev-
eral hundred neutron transport calculations. The simulations using Shift and Serpent showed consistent
trends in terms of the behavior of the pebble discharge burnups, pass-specific burnups, and other quanti-
ties over time. The time stepping showed some differences as a result of differences in keff from the loose
convergence criteria of each neutron transport calculation and from differences in the applied TSL data.
However, overall, it was demonstrated that Shift’s implementation can now be used to generate equilib-
rium core solutions with kugelpy.

5.1 FUTURE WORK
As described, kugelpy in general and the implementation with Shift have limitations. Addressing these
limitations in future work will involve the following:
 • Enable a test mode in kugelpy to generate input files without actually running the neutron transporta-
 tion to allow testing of kugelpy input parameter settings.
 • Enable kugelpy to automatically generate the GPBR’s core structure for the Shift model similar to
 the implementation for the Serpent model.
 • Enable jump-in calculations with Shift using kugelpy, with the same methodology implemented for
 Serpent.
 • Enable kugelpy to allow the modeling of reactors different from the GPBR.
 • Enable Shift to restart densities for all 1,675 nuclides tracked during depletion.
 • Enable Shift to tally the power for the individual pebbles rather than pebble groups (a group of
 pebbles includes pebbles that are located in the same spatial zone and that are at the same pass through
 the core).
 • Allow Shift to consider power from (n,γ) reactions in addition to power from fission during deple-
 tion calculations.
 • Perform inventory calculations of the gFHR benchmark (N. Satvat et al. 2021; Kairos Power 2021)
 with kugelpy and Shift to allow code-to-code comparison with Kairos Power’s KPACS (N. Satvat
 et al. 2021) and SCALE’s SLICE methodology (Bostelmann, Skutnik, and Wieselquist 2024, 2025)
 for equilibrium core generation.

 36

• Perform sensitivity calculations with respect to kugelpy’s input parameters (e.g., time step, graphite
  pebble removal mechanism, control rod position) to understand the effect of these parameters on the
  running-in phase (e.g., fuel inventory development, pebble refueling rate, duration until equilibrium
  core is reached).
• Based on the sensitivity calculations mentioned above, explore ML algorithms to determine corre-
  lations between relevant parameters driving the running-in phase and correlations to relevant output
  quantities.
• Enable pebble-based depletion and running-in (as opposed to zones of pebbles) with Shift in kugelpy.
• Optimize Shift geometry tracking for surfaces consisting of union shapes to improve the run-times
  for the detailed GPBR.
