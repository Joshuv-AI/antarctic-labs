Chemical generation of superoxide in the co-presence of riboflavin derivatives and NADH: evidence for a non-enzymatic redox pathway
Authors: Rinka Sugita, Sayaka Iida, Midori Nagase, Hitoshi Masaki, Yorihiro Yamamoto, Akio Fujisawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe recently reported that flavin-associated compounds such as riboflavin, flavin mononucleotide, and flavin adenine dinucleotide reduce oxidized coenzyme Q and vitamin K homologues to corresponding reduced forms in the presence of reduced β-nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH). In these processes, coenzyme Q or vitamin K acts as a terminal electron accepter. If oxygen can function as an electron acceptor instead of those quinones, resulting in the formation of superoxide. In this study, superoxide formation from the system of NADH and flavin-associated compounds was examined using a water-soluble tetrazolium salt (WST-1) and 2-methyl-6-(4-methoxyphenyl)-3,7-dihydroimidazo[1,2-a]pyrazin-3-one (MCLA) assays. Formation of WST-1 formazan and chemiluminescence from MCLA, both of which are specific to superoxide, increased during co-incubation with NADH and a flavin-associated compound, and significantly suppressed with the addition of superoxide dismutase (SOD). Superoxide is an important reactive oxygen species (ROS) that is converted to other ROS, such as hydrogen peroxide or peroxynitrite. HaCaT keratinocytes, immortalized human keratinocytes, were then cultivated with NADH and flavin-associated compounds. Cell viability declined with increasing NADH concentration but was significantly recovered following the addition of SOD and catalase. We propose a chemical pathway for flavin-associated compound and NADH-induced superoxide generation in vivo, in which extracellular SOD plays an important role in cell survival.Keywords: NADH, riboflavin, FMN, FAD, superoxide

Introduction

It is generally accepted that oxidative stress caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS) is closely related to the adverse effects of aging,(1–3) some neurodegenerative diseases,(4–8) and etiologic injury such as chronic inflammation(9–12) and ischemia-reperfusion.(13–15) Superoxide (O2•−) is an important ROS that leads to the generation of other more harmful ROS. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is produced by spontaneous dismutation of O2•− or through the activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD). H2O2 subsequently gives rise to the formation of hydroxyl radical (HO•) and singlet oxygen (1O2) via the Fenton reaction,(16) and production of hypochlorous anion (ClO−) via a myeloperoxidase-catalyzed reaction. Furthermore, peroxynitrite (ONOO−) is formed by a diffusion-controlled reaction involving O2•− and nitric oxide.(17) Therefore, O2•− is the most significant ROS in vivo, although it shows relatively low reactivity per se.

Based on the above background, SOD plays an important role in protecting living tissues and organisms from oxidative stress by effectively eliminating O2•− to prevent further ROS formation. Several SOD isozymes have been described, including SOD1 (Cu/Zn-SOD), which is present in the cytosol, SOD2 (Mn-SOD), which is localized in the mitochondrial matrix, and SOD3, also known as EC-SOD, which is distributed in extracellular spaces. Thus, many types of SOD are present in vivo, indicating that elimination of O2•− is exceedingly important for protecting organisms from oxidative stress.

We previously demonstrated that the flavin-associated compounds riboflavin (RF), flavin mononucleotide (FMN), and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) (Fig. 1) reduce coenzyme Q and vitamin K in the presence of reduced β-nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH).(18) As NADH does not reduce these quinones directly, the mechanism was postulated as follows: NADH reduces the abovementioned flavin-associated compounds to the corresponding reduced forms, and these reduced compounds in turn reduce the quinones. We believe that this enzyme-like non-enzymatic quinone reduction mechanism plays an important role in protection from oxidative stress. In this reduction process, NADH and quinone function as an electron donor and an acceptor, respectively. However, NADH spontaneously oxidized even in the absence of quinones and without the flavin-associated compounds decreasing, but NADH was not oxidized in the absence of flavin-associated compounds. These results indicate that another electron acceptor exists instead of these quinones and that flavin-associated compounds act as catalysts, similar to quinone reduction. Furthermore, NADH was not oxidized under oxygen-free conditions even in the presence of these flavin-associated compounds. These results suggested that oxygen serves as an electron acceptor instead of these quinones. If an oxygen molecule receives an electron, O2•− is formed.

Chemical structures of (A) riboflavin (RF), (B) flavin mononucleotide (FMN), and (C) flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD).

In this study, we demonstrated that O2•− is produced in the co-presence of NADH and flavin-associated compounds under aerobic conditions, even in the absence of any enzyme. The amount of O2•− produced is dependent not on the concentration of RF but instead the concentration of NADH, indicating that NADH, flavin-associated compound, and molecular oxygen function as an electron donor, catalyst, and electron acceptor, respectively, to produce O2•−. SOD is ubiquitously present both intra- and extracellularly in vivo, functioning in a multiple-component system to eliminate O2•−. This broad antioxidative system may play an important role not only in controlled enzymatic processes but also in suppressing chemical- (i.e., accidentally) generated oxidative stress.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

NADH, RF, FAD, FMN, 2-methyl-6-(4-methoxyphenyl)3,7-dihydroimidazo[1,2-a]pyrazin-3-one hydrochloride (MCLA), and other chemicals were purchased from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Co. (Osaka, Japan) and Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan) and used as received. 2-(4-Iodophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium, monosodium salt (WST-1) and diethylenetriamine-N,N,N',N'',N''-pentaacetic acid (DTPA) were purchased from DOJINDO (Kumamoto, Japan). Xanthine oxidase (from bovine milk) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Japan (Tokyo, Japan). [2,2'-Biquinoline]-4,4'-dicarboxylic acid (BCA) assay kit was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Tokyo, Japan).

Detection of superoxide using the WST-1 and MCLA assays

WST-1 and MCLA assays were carried out to determine whether O2•− can be generated from NADH and flavin-associated compound. Upon reaction with O2•−, WST-1 is converted into WST-1 formazan, which shows a characteristic absorption pattern (λmax = 438 ‍nm, ε = 3.7 × 104 M−1cm−1). WST-1 (1.0 ‍mM), NADH (1.0 ‍mM or 200 ‍μM), and flavin-associated compound (10 or 20 ‍μM) were dissolved in phosphate buffer solution (pH ‍7.4), and the absorbance of the solution at 438 ‍nm was measured every 10 ‍min from 0 to 30 ‍min using a spectrophotometer (GENESYS 180; Thermo Fisher Scientific). If the measured absorbance became >2.0, the measurement solution was diluted with phosphate buffer so that the absorbance was ≤2.0 and then re-measured.

For further confirmation, the MCLA assay was carried out. MCLA reacts with O2•− to release a typical chemiluminescence at 465 ‍nm. Flavin-associated compound (30 ‍μM) and MCLA (100 ‍μM) were mixed, and the chemiluminescence was measured using a chemiluminescence detector (AB-2270 Luminescencer Octa; ATTO Corp., Tokyo, Japan). NADH (200 ‍μM) and SOD (5.0 ‍U) were added at 2 and 10 ‍min, respectively.

To prevent the photosensitization reaction of RF, all the above experiments were conducted in the dark or in light-impermeable vessels.

Quantification of hydrogen peroxide

H2O2 was measured in order to estimate the amount O2•− produced. One molecule of H2O2 is formed via the dismutation of two O2•− molecules (Eq. 1). This spontaneous dismutation reaction is thought to occur relatively rapidly, with an estimated rate constant of approximately 5 × 105 M·s−1.(19) Furthermore, when SOD acts, disproportionation occurs almost exclusively at the diffusion limit. Therefore, measuring the amount of H2O2 produced enables quantification of O2•− production.

Generated H2O2 was quantified using an HPLC system equipped with an electrochemical detector (HPLC-ECD). An aqueous solution containing Na2SO4 (10 ‍mM), H2SO4 (1.0 ‍mM), and DTPA (10 ‍μM) was prepared as the mobile phase and delivered at 1.0 ‍ml/min. A cation exchange column (Inertsil CX, 5 ‍μm, 4.6 × 250 ‍mm; GL Science, Tokyo, Japan) was used for separation. A platinum electrode was used as the working electrode for the ECD, and the applied voltage was 600 ‍mV. Prior to measurement of samples, multiple concentrations (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 ‍μM) of an authentic H2O2 standard were analyzed for calibration.

Next, RF (200 ‍μM) and NADH (100, 200, 300, 400, or 500 ‍μM) were co-incubated, and SOD (1.0 ‍U) was added to complete the dismutation of O2•−. The H2O2 generated in solution was measured using the HPLC-ECD system. These experiments were conducted in the dark or in light-impermeable containers to avoid photosensitization of RF.

Cultivation of HaCaT cells and measurement of viability

To investigate the physiological significance of chemical O2•− production, HaCaT keratinocytes were co-incubated with NADH and RF. HaCaT cells were a gift from Prof. Akamatsu (Fujita Health University, Aichi, Japan), and the cells were seeded (3.0 × 105 cells/ml) in 5% DMEM/PBS and cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2 under darkness for 1 day. RF and NADH were then added to the cell culture, and the cells were further incubated for 1 day. Catalase (0.05 ‍U) and/or SOD (1.0 ‍U) were also added to the culture at the same time as RF and NADH addition.

Cell viability was evaluated by measuring the protein concentration using a BCA assay kit and TECAN SPARK 10M (Tecan Japan Co., Ltd., Kawasaki, Japan). Cell viability was calculated as the percentage of protein content at each time point, with the protein content at time 0 (when no supplement was added) set to 100%.

Cell viability was assessed by measuring total protein content using the above BCA assay as a surrogate marker for cell number. The BCA assay is widely used as an alternative method for evaluating viability because it offers high reproducibility and a broad linear range, and total protein content correlates well with cell mass.

Results

Superoxide production from NADH and flavin-associated compounds

The formation of O2•− from the combination of flavin-associated compounds and NADH was examined. Detection of O2•− was carried out using the WST-1 assay or MPEC. NADH (1.0 ‍mM) and RF (10 ‍μM) were co-incubated, and O2•− formation was measured using the WST-1 assay. WST-1 reacts with O2•− to form WST-1 formazan. The increase in absorbance at 438 ‍nm derived from WST-1 formazan was observed in the presence of both RF and NADH, whereas no increase in absorbance was observed in the absence of NADH or RF. Furthermore, formation of WST-1 formazan was significantly suppressed by the addition of SOD (5.0 ‍U) (Fig. 2A). O2•− production was also measured in the presence of 20 ‍μM RF, FMN, or FAD and NADH (200 ‍μM). All of these flavin-associated compounds promoted WST-1 formazan production in the presence of NADH; however, the rate of WST-1 formazan formation differed between the compounds, increasing in the order FAD, FMN, and RF (Fig. 2B). No WST-1 formazan production was observed in the absence of NADH, and WST-1 formazan production was significantly suppressed by the addition of SOD, similar to the result with RF (data not shown).

O2•− formation was also confirmed using the MCLA assay. MCLA reacts with O2•− to release a typical chemiluminescence at 465 ‍nm. Chemiluminescence was observed with RF (Fig. 2C), FMN (Fig. 2D), and FAD (Fig. 2E) due to the generation of O2•− in the presence of NADH, but this chemiluminescence was significantly suppressed following SOD addition. However, the intensity of the chemiluminescence was also dependent on the presence of RF-associated compounds, increasing in the order FAD, FMN, and RF, consistent with the results of WST-1 assays (Fig. 2F).

Dependence of superoxide generation on the concentrations of NADH and RF

To determine the stoichiometric number for O2•− formation from RF and NADH, NADH (100, 200, 300, 400, or 500 ‍μM) was reacted with 200 ‍μM RF in the presence of SOD. The amount of O2•− formed was estimated as H2O2. The amount of H2O2 generated was correlated with NADH concentration, with a slope of 0.94 (Fig. 3A). By contrast, the amount of H2O2 generated was not dependent on the concentration of RF (Fig. 3B).

Viability of HaCaT cells exposed to RF and NADH

HaCaT cells were co-incubated with RF and/or NADH, and the effect on cell viability was monitored. Cell viability initially decreased slightly with increasing NADH or RF concentration when NADH or RF was added alone to the cell culture but remained above 90% when the NADH concentration was ≤800 ‍μM (Fig. 4A) and the RF concentration was within ≤100 ‍μM (Fig. 4B). In the presence of both NADH (0–750 ‍μM) and RF (100 ‍μM), cell viability decreased significantly with increasing NADH concentration. However, at an NADH concentration of 750 ‍μM, the addition of SOD (1.0 ‍U) significantly increased cell viability, and the addition of catalase (0.05 ‍U) to this system induced a further significant increase in viability (Fig. 4C).

Discussion

Superoxide production in a system consisting of RF-associated compounds and NADH

O2•− is an important ROS that can be converted to H2O2 and ONOO−, and the mechanisms by which it is generated have been characterized in detail. The mechanisms by which O2•− is generated are primarily enzymatic and biological and include NADPH oxidase and electron leakage from mitochondria. In the current study, we propose an alternative pathway, a chemical system for O2•− generation.

Formation of O2•− was measured using the WST-1 and the MCLA assays. When WST-1 reacts with O2•−, WST-1 formazan is generated, which is yellow in color and can be quantified by measuring the absorption at 438 ‍nm. O2•− production was observed in the co-presence of NADH and RF, although no O2•− was produced in the absence of either NADH or RF (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, the increment in the absorbance at 438 ‍nm was significantly inhibited by the addition of SOD. These results indicate that the combination of NADH and RF leads to the generation of O2•−. Other flavin-associated compounds, such as FMN and FAD, also produced O2•− in the presence of NADH; however, the rate of O2•− generation was lower, in the order FMN and FAD (Fig. 2B).

The oxidation potentials of RF, FMN, FAD, and NADH versus normal hydrogen electrode (NHE) are −0.18, −0.21, −0.22, and −0.32 ‍V, respectively.(20,21) Considering that the estimated one-electron oxidation potential of molecular oxygen is −0.32 ‍V,(22) the order of the rate for O2•− production seems to depend on oxidation potential.

Although reduced riboflavin (RFH2) possesses a more positive redox potential than NADH, our results indicate that RFH2 exhibits markedly higher reactivity toward molecular oxygen. This apparent discrepancy can be rationalized by considering that redox potentials describe thermodynamic favorability, whereas the rate of electron transfer is governed primarily by kinetic factors such as activation barriers and orbital coupling. RFH2 is capable of undergoing single-electron transfer (SET) from its conjugated isoalloxazine ring, a process that generally proceeds with a lower activation energy than the hydride-transfer mechanism characteristic of NADH.(23) Consequently, RFH2 can reduce O2 more rapidly despite its less negative reduction potential.

Furthermore, flavins are known to form weak charge-transfer (CT) complexes with O2, which can facilitate electron transfer by stabilizing the encounter complex and lowering the activation energy for superoxide formation. Such CT interactions have been reported for both oxidized and reduced flavins and may contribute to the enhanced reactivity observed in our system. We therefore propose that RFH2-mediated O2 reduction proceeds through a kinetically favored SET pathway, potentially involving transient RF-O2 CT complex formation, whereas NADH lacks an efficient mechanism for direct SET to O2.(23–25) These mechanistic considerations reconcile the observed reactivity trends and support the interpretation that RF plays an active catalytic role in promoting superoxide generation. However, further investigations are needed to clarify this point.

An MCLA assay was also used to measure O2•− generation. The chemiluminescence of MCLA increased with the addition of NADH to a solution of MCLA and RF and decreased with further addition of SOD (Fig. 2C), FMN (Fig. 2D), or FAD (Fig. 2E). These results also show that a chemical reaction between NADH and RF-associated compounds generates O2•−. Furthermore, the intensity of chemiluminescence increased in the order FAD, FMD, and RF, similar to the results of WST-1 assays.

Stoichiometry of superoxide production relative to the concentration of NADH or RF

The stoichiometry of O2•− formation relative to NADH and RF as substrates was investigated in order to characterize the mechanism of O2•− production via the reaction of NADH and RF-associated compounds. The amount of H2O2 produced was dependent on the NADH concentration (Fig. 3A) and independent of the RF concentration (Fig. 3B). The slope for a plot of H2O2 production versus NADH concentration was 0.94, or approximately 1.0, indicating that one molecule of H2O2 is produced from one molecule of NADH. Two molecules of O2•− undergo dismutation to form one molecule of H2O2.

A previous study determined the stoichiometric number for the reduction of coenzyme Q10 and vitamin K by NADH and RF-associated compounds as 1.0, indicating that one NADH molecule reduces one quinone molecule in the co-presence of RF-associated compounds. These quinones undergo two-electron reduction for conversion into the corresponding hydroquinone. In this process of reduction, the RF-compound acts as a catalyst. One molecule of NADH reduces an RF-associated compound to yield a reduced RF-associated compound (RFCH2) (Eq. 2). Then, RFCH2 reduces one quinone to its hydroquinone (Eq. 3).

In the current study, it was thought that oxygen molecules act as electron acceptors instead of quinones, resulting in the generation of two molecules of O2•− (Eq. 4). As SOD-catalyzed dismutation of these O2•− molecules yields one-half the amount of H2O2 compared with O2•− and H+ (Eq. 5), one molecule of NADH produces one H2O2.

The correlation between NADH concentration and H2O2 production was confirmed as approximately 1.0 (0.94) (Fig. 3A). This indicates that one H2O2 molecule is produced from one NADH molecule. However, the amount of O2•− produced did not depend on RF (Fig. 3B), remaining constant regardless of the amount of NADH supplied. This suggests that RF acts as a catalyst rather than a substrate. Based on these collective results, we propose the following mechanism for O2•− generation in a system in which both NADH and flavin-associated compound are present (Scheme 1): NADH reduces the flavin-associated compound, generating a corresponding reduced product. Next, one molecule of this reduced product reduces two oxygen molecules by one electron, returning the flavin-associated compound to its original oxidized form and generating two molecules of O2•−.

Postulated mechanism for superoxide production in the co-presence of NADH and flavin-associated compounds.

Physiological significance of the chemical superoxide production system

To consider the biological significance of RF- and NADH-dependent chemical production of O2•−, we exposed cultured cells to RF and NADH and observed their effects on viability. HaCaT cells are a line of immortalized human keratinocytes conditioned to survive in an environment with an oxygen concentration higher than that in vivo.(26) As the addition of NADH (0–800 ‍μM) or RF (0–100 ‍μM) alone did not significantly reduce cell viability (Fig. 4A and B), it was thought that these compounds exhibit virtually no cytotoxicity against HaCaT cells at these concentrations. By contrast, the viability of HaCaT cells decreased with increasing NADH concentration in the co-presence of RF (100 ‍μM) (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, this reduction in viability was significantly recovered following the addition of SOD (1.0 ‍U). These results suggest that HaCaT cells are damaged by the O2•− generated in the co-presence of NADH and RF, leading to decreased cell viability. By contrast, the O2•− generated undergoes dismutation and is converted to H2O2, either spontaneously or via diffusion controlled by the presence of SOD. Therefore, it cannot be ruled out that H2O2, in addition to O2•−, is responsible for the observed cytotoxicity. The addition of catalase (0.05 ‍U) significantly increased cell viability, as expected. This suggests that O2•− is chemically generated in the presence of both NADH and RF, in turn leading to the production of H2O2, which is toxic to the cells. On the other hand, cell viability was not fully restored in all systems upon the addition of both enzymes. This is likely attributable to the low dosage of the respective enzymes or a reduction in the activity of the enzymes themselves. While factors other than O2•− may also be involved, further investigation is required to clarify this point.

Oxidative stress has been associated with the effects of aging and with the development of a variety of diseases. Organisms therefore have numerous antioxidative defense mechanisms to overcome these effects. For example, the oxygen concentration is maintained at a low level in vivo, at approximately 2–5%, and various antioxidant enzymes and antioxidants are present to respond to oxidative stress. These enzymes and compounds protect the organism from oxidative stress by either preventing the generation of ROS or immediately eliminating them if they are generated. Thus, the elimination of ROS has great physiological significance. Among ROS, O2•− is particularly important. Although O2•− exhibits limited reactivity per se, it is regarded as an extremely important ROS because it acts as an initiator for the generation of other ROS, for example by reacting with nitric oxide (•NO) to produce ONOO−. Therefore, the elimination of O2•− is essential for survival in the presence of oxidative stress. From this perspective, it is reasonable to assume that various SOD enzymes are ubiquitously expressed in vivo in order to prevent further ROS generation. For example, there are conventional SODs such as Mn-SOD and Cu/Zn-SOD localized in mitochondria and cytoplasm, respectively, and EC-SOD is found extracellularly. Conversely, the ubiquitously presence of these various SODs in vivo suggests that the O2•− generation system is also widely present in vivo. Considering that NADH is present both intra- and extracellularly and that RF is vitamin B2, which is taken up from outside the body and metabolized to FMN and FAD intracellularly, it is possible that these RF and RF-associated compounds also function with NADH during this process. Furthermore, the co-presence of FAD and FMN with NADH both intra- and extracellularly may lead to the chemical production of O2•− at various locations in vivo through chemical reactions between them, as shown in this study. If this is the case, then SOD, which is ubiquitously expressed in vivo, should be considered as holding great physiological significance.

Previous studies have reported that biomembranes, including mitochondrial membranes, generate O2•− in the presence of NADH.(27–29) These phenomena have typically been attributed to enzymatic reactions mediated by membrane-bound oxidoreductases. However, our results demonstrate that NADH and flavin-associated compounds can produce superoxide even in the absence of enzymes. Because biomembranes contain flavins, quinones, and other redox-active molecules, non-enzymatic electron transfer from NADH to O2 may occur in parallel with enzymatic pathways. In addition to providing an alternative interpretation for previous membrane-based observations, our findings offer broader implications for understanding redox processes in biological membranes. Biomembranes are enriched with redox-active cofactors such as RFs, quinones, and nicotinamide nucleotides, and the local microenvironment often supports rapid electron exchange. The demonstration that NADH and flavin-associated compounds can generate O2•− non-enzymatically indicates that biological membranes possess an intrinsic chemical capacity for one-electron reduction of oxygen, independent of protein-mediated pathways. This expands the conventional view that membrane-associated O2•− formation arises solely from electron leakage, suggesting instead that membranes may host a dual system in which enzymatic and non-enzymatic reactions coexist and jointly shape the redox landscape.

Such non-enzymatic routes may become particularly relevant under conditions where the redox state of NADH or membrane-bound flavins is perturbed, thereby influencing oxidative stress, metabolic regulation, and mitochondrial signaling. Recognizing this additional chemical pathway provides a more comprehensive framework for interpreting membrane redox dynamics and highlights the physiological significance of the non-enzymatic O2•− generation mechanism identified in this study.

Conclusion

In this study, we propose for the first time a non-enzymatic system for superoxide production in vivo. This system is considered to be of high importance physiologically.
