Reciprocals of Subsum Polynomials
Cristina Ballantine, George Beck, Brooke Feigon, Kathrin Maurischat

Abstract
. We introduce the subsum polynomial of a partition λ = ( λ 1 , λ 2 , … , λ k ) \lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k}) defined by sp ⁡ ( λ , x ) = ∏ i = 1 k ( 1 + x λ i ) \mathrm{sp}(\lambda,x)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1+x^{\lambda_{i}}) . We study the sum of reciprocals of sp ⁡ ( λ , x ) \mathrm{sp}(\lambda,x) over all partitions of n n . We prove arithmetic properties of related polynomials and offer connections to other combinatorial objects.

1.
Introduction

An integer partition
\lambda
of
n
is a nonincreasing sequence
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
of positive integers that add up to
n
. We write
\lambda\vdash n
to mean that
\lambda
is a partition of
n
. The numbers
\lambda_{i}
in the sequence are called the parts of
\lambda
. We identify a partition with its multiset of parts. We denote by
P(n)
the number of partitions of
n
and set
P(0):=1
. We occasionally use the frequency notation of partitions. Exponents of parts represent their multiplicity. For example,
(3^{2},2^{4},1^{2})
is the partition
(3,3,2,2,2,2,1,1)
. For more on the theory of partitions, we refer the reader to
[
1
]
.

Given a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
, we define the polynomial

\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,x):=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1+x^{\lambda_{i}}).

In analogy with the generating function for the number of partitions with distinct parts, we can view the polynomial
\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,x)
as the generating function for the number of partitions whose parts form a submultiset of
\lambda
. This inspired the name
subsum polynomial
for
\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,x)
. This construction is also reminiscent of Stanton’s polynomial
G_{\lambda}
, the generating function of all partitions that lie inside of
\lambda
(see
[
7
]
).
Clearly, if
\lambda
is a partition of
n
, then
\deg(\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,x))=n
.

If
m_{\lambda}(i)
denotes the number of times
i
occurs as a part in
\lambda
, then

\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+x^{i})^{m_{\lambda}(i)}.

Numerical experimentation led us to define and study the rational function

\mathrm{sr}(n,x):=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\frac{1}{\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,x)}.

For any partition
\lambda\vdash n
, let

h_{\lambda}(x):=\prod_{i\geq 1}(1+x^{i})^{\lfloor n/i\rfloor-m_{\lambda}(i)}\>.

We define

\mathrm{num}^{*}(n,x):=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}h_{\lambda}(x)

and

\mathrm{den}^{*}(n,x):=\prod_{i\geq 1}(1+x^{i})^{\lfloor n/i\rfloor}\>.

Then,

\mathrm{sr}(n,x)=\frac{\mathrm{num}^{*}(n,x)}{\mathrm{den}^{*}(n,x)}.

We can also write

\mathrm{num}^{*}(n,x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\vdash\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{1}(i)-n\\
m_{\mu}(i)\leq\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor\end{subarray}}\mathrm{sp}(\mu,x),

where
\sigma_{1}(i)
denotes the sum of divisors of
i
.

The degree of
\mathrm{den}^{*}(n,x)
is
\sum_{i=1}^{n}i\lfloor n/i\rfloor=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{1}(i)
. This is sequence A024916 in OEIS
[
5
]
.
We have

\displaystyle\deg(h_{\lambda}(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{1}(i)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}im_{\lambda}(i)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{1}(i)-n=\deg(\mathrm{num}^{*}(n,x)).

Thus

\deg(\mathrm{den}^{*}(n,x))-\deg(\mathrm{num}^{*}(n,x))=n.

Next, we define

G(n,x):=\gcd\{h_{\lambda}(x)\mid\lambda\vdash n\}\>,

and let

\mathrm{num}(n,x):=\frac{\mathrm{num}^{*}(n,x)}{G(n,x)}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\mathrm{den}(n,x):=\frac{\mathrm{den}^{*}(n,x)}{G(n,x)}\>.

In Section
3
we prove results about
\mathrm{num}(n,x)
and
\mathrm{den}(n,x)
. Below we list some of them.

A polynomial is
palindromic
if its coefficients form a palindrome. A sequence
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{m}
of real numbers is said to be
unimodal
if, for some
j
,
u_{1}\leq u_{2}\leq\ldots\leq u_{j}\geq u_{j+1}\geq\ldots\geq u_{m}
. A polynomial is unimodal if its sequence of coefficients is unimodal.

Proposition

(
3.9
)
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Then,

(a)

G(n,x)
,
\mathrm{num}(n,x)
, and
\mathrm{den}(n,x)
are palindromic.

(b)

\mathrm{den}(n,x)
and
G(n,x)
are unimodal.

Theorem

(
3.18
)
.

For all
n\geq 1
,

(a)

\mathrm{num}(n,-1)=n!

(b)

\lvert\mathrm{num}(n,\pm\mathrm{i})\rvert=2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\lfloor n/2\rfloor!

(c)

\lvert\mathrm{num}(n,\zeta_{6})\rvert=\begin{cases}3^{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\lfloor n/3\rfloor!&n\not\equiv 2\pmod{3}\\
4\cdot 3^{\lfloor n/3\rfloor}\lfloor n/3\rfloor!&n\equiv 2\pmod{3},\end{cases}

where
\zeta_{6}
is a primitive sixth root of unity.

Proposition

(
3.13
)
.

For all
n\geq 1
,

(a)

\mathrm{den}(n,1)=2^{\mathrm{val}_{2}((2n)!)}
,

(b)

\mathrm{num}(n,1)=2^{\mathrm{val}_{2}(n!)}P(n)
.

Numerical evidence suggests the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

For
n\geq 1
, the polynomial
\mathrm{num}(n,x)
is irreducible over the integers.

We also make a somewhat weaker claim for which we have achieved some partial results:

Conjecture 2
.

For
n\geq 1
,

\gcd\bigl(\mathrm{num}(n,x),\mathrm{den}(n,x)\bigr)=1\>.

Conjecture
1
implies Conjecture
2
(see Remark
5
).

In Section
4
we prove some analogous results for binary partitions, that is, partitions whose parts are powers of two. In Section
5
, we briefly consider a generalization of
\mathrm{sr}(n,x)
, namely
\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,x)^{m}
for
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. In Section
6
, we discuss open questions for sums of reciprocals of subsum polynomials defined on restricted sets of partitions.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we establish useful properties of polynomials of the form
1+x^{i}
. For the convenience of the reader, we give complete proofs.

We write
\mathrm{val}_{2}(n)
for the
2
-valuation of the integer
n
. We denote by
\Phi_{d}(x)
the
d
-th cyclotomic polynomial, which is irreducible in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
.

Remark 1
.

Since
x^{n}-1=\prod_{d|n}\Phi_{d}(x)
for any positive integer
n
, and

x^{2i}-1=(x^{i}-1)(1+x^{i}),

it follows that

(1)

(1+x^{i})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|2i\\
d\nmid i\end{subarray}}\Phi_{d}(x).

Lemma 2.1
.

(a)

1+x^{i}
is irreducible in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
if and only if
i=2^{k}
for some
k\geq 0
.

(b)

\gcd(1+x^{n},1+x^{m})=\begin{cases}1,&\textrm{ if }\mathrm{val}_{2}(n)\neq\mathrm{val}_{2}(m)\\
1+x^{\gcd(n,m)},&\textrm{ if }\mathrm{val}_{2}(n)=\mathrm{val}_{2}(m).\end{cases}

(c)

In particular,
(1+x^{i})\mid(1+x^{m})
if and only if
m=i\cdot j
for some odd
j
.

Proof.

(a)
The statement holds because the right hand side of (
1
) has a single factor if and only if
i=2^{k}
for some
k\geq 0
.

(b)
From (
1
), it follows that

(2)

\gcd(1+x^{n},1+x^{m})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid 2\cdot\gcd(n,m)\\
d\nmid n,d\nmid m\end{subarray}}\Phi_{d}(x).

Clearly,
each
d
that occurs as an index in the product above satisfies
d\nmid\gcd(n,m)
.

Case 1:

\mathrm{val}_{2}(n)\neq\mathrm{val}_{2}(m)
. Assuming
n>m
, let
n=2^{j}\cdot a\cdot\gcd(n,m)
and
m=b\cdot\gcd(n,m)
, where
j>0
and
a,b
are odd. Then, if
d\mid 2\cdot\gcd(n,m)
we have
d\mid n
. Hence, the product in (
2
) is empty and
\gcd(1+x^{n},1+x^{m})=1
.

Case 2:

\mathrm{val}_{2}(n)=\mathrm{val}_{2}(m)
. Let
n=a\cdot\gcd(n,m)
and
m=b\cdot\gcd(n,m)
, where
a,b
are odd. Then for each
d
such that
d\mid 2\cdot\gcd(n,m)
but
d\nmid\gcd(n,m)
we have
\mathrm{val}_{2}(d)=\mathrm{val}_{2}(\gcd(n,m))+1=\mathrm{val}_{2}(n)+1=\mathrm{val}_{2}(m)+1
, that is,
d\nmid n
and
d\nmid m
. We may rewrite (
2
) as

\gcd(1+x^{n},1+x^{m})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid 2\cdot\gcd(n,m)\\
d\nmid\gcd(n,m)\end{subarray}}\Phi_{d}(x)=1+x^{\gcd(n,m)}.

(c)
Notice that
(1+x^{i})\mid(1+x^{m})
is equivalent to
(1+x^{i})=\gcd(1+x^{i},1+x^{m})
, which by part
(b)
holds if and only if
\mathrm{val}_{2}(i)=\mathrm{val}_{2}(m)
and
i=\gcd(i,m)
, that is, if and only if
m=i\cdot j
for some odd
j
.
∎

Remark 2
.

If
i=\ell\cdot j
with odd
j
, we have the factorization

1+x^{i}=(1+x^{\ell})\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}(-x^{\ell})^{k}=(1+x^{\ell})(x^{\ell(j-1)}-x^{\ell(j-2)}+\ldots-x^{\ell}+1).

Corollary 2.2
.

The
\gcd
of any finite collection of products of terms of the form
(1+x^{i})
can be written as a product of terms of the form
(1+x^{j})
.
In particular,
G(n,x)
decomposes into the product

G(n,x)=\prod_{j=0}^{\lfloor\log_{2}(n)\rfloor}\gcd\{\prod_{d\geq 1,d\textrm{ odd}}(1+x^{2^{j}d})^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2^{j}d}\rfloor-m_{\lambda}(2^{j}d)}\mid\lambda\vdash n\}.

We also recall basic facts about palindromic and unimodal polynomials.

Remark 3
.

(a)

Products and quotients of palindromic polynomials are palindromic and sums of palindromic polynomials of the same degree are palindromic.
In particular, for all
i,\ell\geq 1
, the polynomial
(1+x^{i})^{\ell}
is palindromic.

(b)

A product of palindromic, unimodal polynomials is again unimodal
[
2
]
.

3.
Ordinary partitions

Recall that

h_{\lambda}(x)=\prod_{i\geq 1}(1+x^{i})^{\lfloor n/i\rfloor-m_{\lambda}(i)}.

By Lemma
2.1
(or also by Corollary
2.2
) there exists a product decomposition of
G(n,x)=\gcd\{h_{\lambda}(x)\mid\lambda\vdash n\}
as

(3)

G(n,x)=\prod_{i\geq 1}(1+x^{i})^{c_{n,i}}\>.

Note that this is not a decomposition into coprime factors. Our next goal is to understand the exponents
c_{n,i}
. We first introduce more notation. Given a positive integer
n
, for each
i=1,\ldots,n
, we define
\gamma_{n,i}
as the maximal non-negative integer such that

(1+x^{i})^{\gamma_{n,i}}\mid G(n,x)\>.

Clearly,
c_{n,i}\leq\gamma_{n,i}
.

The next lemma can be used to determine the coefficients
c_{n,i}
recursively for fixed
n
.

Lemma 3.1
.

With the notation above, for
n\geq 1
we have

(a)

For all
i\geq 1
,

\gamma_{n,i}=\sum_{j\geq 1\textrm{ odd}}c_{n,i\cdot j}=\sum_{j>1\textrm{ odd}}\lfloor\frac{n}{i\cdot j}\rfloor\>.

(b)

For
i>\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\rfloor
,
c_{n,i}=\gamma_{n,i}=0
.

(c)

For
3i>\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\rfloor\geq i
,
c_{n,i}=\gamma_{n,i}
.

Proof.

These are consequences of Lemma
2.1
: The term
(1+x^{i})
divides
(1+x^{k})
if and only if
k=i\cdot j
for some odd
j\geq 1
, and if so, the multiplicity of
(1+x^{i})
in the decomposition of
(1+x^{k})
is one.

(a)
For any
i
, we have
\gamma_{n,i}=\sum_{j\geq 1\textrm{ odd}}c_{n,i\cdot j}
.
On the other hand, since

h_{\lambda}(x)=\prod_{i\geq 1}(1+x^{i})^{\lfloor n/i\rfloor-m_{\lambda}(i)}\>,

we also have

\gamma_{n,i}=\min_{\lambda\vdash n}\left(\sum_{j\textrm{ odd}}(\lfloor\frac{n}{i\cdot j}\rfloor-m_{\lambda}(i\cdot j))\right)\>.

The sum on the right hand side is minimal for each
\lambda
such that
m_{\lambda}(i)=\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor
and for such a
\lambda
we have
m_{\lambda}(i\cdot j)=0
for all
j>1
. The minimality claim holds because, if
\lambda
has
m_{\lambda}(i)<\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor
, then

\sum_{j>1}m_{\lambda}(i\cdot j)<\sum_{j>1}jm_{\lambda}(i\cdot j)\leq\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor-m_{\lambda}(i).

Partitions
\lambda
with
m_{\lambda}(i)=\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor
exist, for example
\lambda=(i^{\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor},1^{n-\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor})
.
Hence, we obtain

\gamma_{n,i}=\sum_{j>1\textrm{ odd}}\lfloor\frac{n}{i\cdot j}\rfloor.

(b)

If
i>\lfloor n/3\rfloor
, there is no multiple
k
of
i
satisfying
n\geq k=i\cdot j>i
with
j
odd.
So by
(a)
,
0=\sum_{j>1\textrm{ odd}}\lfloor\frac{n}{i\cdot j}\rfloor=\gamma_{n,i}=c_{n,i}
.

(c)
For
3i>\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\rfloor\geq i
by
(a)
and
(b)
,

\gamma_{n,i}=\sum_{j\geq 1\textrm{ odd}}c_{n,i\cdot j}=c_{n,i}
.
∎

For each
i\geq 1
,
(1+x^{i})^{\gamma_{n,i}}\mid\mathrm{num}^{*}(n,x)
and
\gamma_{n,i}
is the largest such power as shown below.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
p(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1+x^{i})^{a_{i}}
and
q(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(1+x^{i})^{b_{i}}
be polynomials with exponents
a_{i},b_{i}\geq 0
. Let
\alpha_{i}
(resp.
\beta_{i}
) be the maximal exponent such that
(1+x^{i})^{\alpha_{i}}\mid p(x)
(resp.
(1+x^{i})^{\beta_{i}}\mid q(x)
). If
\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}
for all
i
, then
p(x)=q(x)
.

Proof.

Because
\alpha_{i}=\sum_{j\geq 1\textrm{ odd}}a_{i\cdot j}
(resp.
\beta_{i}=\sum_{j\geq 1\textrm{ odd}}b_{i\cdot j}
), and because
\alpha_{i}=a_{i}
(resp.
\beta_{i}=b_{i}
) for
i>\lfloor N/3\rfloor
, we obtain the coefficients
a_{i}
(resp.
b_{i}
) from the
\alpha_{i}
(resp.
\beta_{i}
) by recursion. So if
\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}
for all
i
, then
a_{i}=b_{i}
for all
i
.
∎

Using the results of the previous two lemmas, we prove the following about the quotients of
G(n,x)
for consecutive values of
n
. We use the notation

\mathrm{\mathfrak{q}G}(n,x):=\frac{G(n,x)}{G(n-1,x)}\>.

For a positive integer
m
, we denote by
\mathbf{o}(m)
the largest odd divisor of
m
, that is,

\mathbf{o}(m)=\frac{m}{2^{\mathrm{val}_{2}(m)}}.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
n>1
. Then

\mathrm{\mathfrak{q}G}(n,x)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
\mathbf{o}(n)\nmid d\end{subarray}}(1+x^{d})\>,

In particular, if
n=2^{k}
then
\mathrm{\mathfrak{q}G}(n,x)=1
.

Proof.

Define

\chi(n,k):=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{ if }k\mid n\\
0&\textrm{ else, }\end{cases}\>

so that
\lfloor\frac{n}{k}\rfloor=\lfloor\frac{n-1}{k}\rfloor+\chi(n,k)
.
By Lemma
3.1
, for the maximal exponents
\gamma_{n,i}
such that
(1+x^{i})^{\gamma_{n,i}}\mid G(n,x)
we have

\gamma_{n,i}=\sum_{j>1\textrm{ odd}}\lfloor\frac{n}{i\cdot j}\rfloor=\gamma_{n-1,i}+\sum_{j>1\textrm{ odd}}\chi(n,i\cdot j)\>.

For each
1\leq i\leq n
, let
\delta_{n,i}
be the maximal exponent such that
(1+x^{i})^{\delta_{n,i}}\mid\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
\mathbf{o}(n)\nmid d\end{subarray}}(1+x^{d})
.
Then we have

\delta_{n,i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\textrm{ odd }\\
i\cdot j\neq 2^{\mathrm{val}_{2}(i)-\mathrm{val}_{2}(n)}n\end{subarray}}\chi(n,i\cdot j)=\sum_{j>1\textrm{ odd}}\chi(n,i\cdot j).

The last equality holds because both sides have the same number of summands with value
1
(and all other summands are zero).

So the maximal exponents
\eta_{n,i}
such that
(1+x^{i})^{\eta_{n,i}}\mid\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
\mathbf{o}(n)\nmid d\end{subarray}}(1+x^{d})\cdot G(n-1,x)
are

\eta_{n,i}=\delta_{n,i}+\gamma_{n-1,i}=\gamma_{n,i}\>.

By Lemma
3.2
, we have
G(n,x)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
\mathbf{o}(n)\nmid d\end{subarray}}(1+x^{d})\cdot G(n-1,x)
.
∎

Next, we examine the coefficients
c_{n,i}
in

G(n,x)=\prod_{i=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\rfloor}(1+x^{i})^{c_{n,i}}.

Let
c_{n}:=(c_{n,1},c_{n,2},\ldots,c_{n,n})
. By Lemma
3.1
, we have
c_{n,i}=0
if
i>\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\rfloor
.

In the proof of our result we make use of the following identity for
b=2
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For all positive integers
m
and any integral base
b>1
we have

m=\sum_{{j=1,\,b\nmid j}}^{m}\lfloor\log_{b}(\frac{bm}{j})\rfloor.

Proof.

We prove this statement by induction on
m
. When
m=1
, both sides of the equation are
1
. For the induction step notice that

\lfloor\log_{b}(\frac{b(m+1)}{j})\rfloor=\lfloor\log_{b}(\frac{bm}{j})\rfloor
unless
\frac{b(m+1)}{j}=b^{k}
for some
k>0
. But there is a unique
j\leq m+1
with
b\nmid j
that satisfies this condition. So, by the induction hypothesis,

\sum_{{j=1,\,b\nmid j}}^{m+1}\lfloor\log_{b}(\frac{b(m+1)}{j})\rfloor=m+1\>.

∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
n
be a positive integer.

(a)

For
1\leq i\leq n
,

c_{n,i}=\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor-\lfloor\lb(\frac{n}{i})\rfloor-1\>.

(b)

c_{n,i}>0
for
i=1,\ldots,\lfloor n/3\rfloor
, and
c_{n}
is weakly decreasing.

Proof.

(a)
We show that
\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor-\lfloor\lb(\frac{n}{i})\rfloor-1
satisfies
(a)
–
(c)
of Lemma
3.1
. That is,
c_{n,i}
and
\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor-\lfloor\lb(\frac{n}{i})\rfloor-1
satisfy the same recursion with the same initial conditions and thus must be equal. For
i>\lfloor\frac{n}{3}\rfloor
, obviously
\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor-\lfloor\lb(\frac{n}{i})\rfloor-1=0
. Integers
n,i
satisfying
3i>\lfloor n/3\rfloor\geq i
are related by
n=ai+r
with
0\leq r<i
and
a\in\{3,4,\ldots,8\}
. For those

c_{n,i}=\gamma_{n,i}=\lfloor\frac{n}{3i}\rfloor+\lfloor\frac{n}{5i}\rfloor+\lfloor\frac{n}{7i}\rfloor=\lfloor\frac{a}{3}\rfloor+\lfloor\frac{a}{5}\rfloor+\lfloor\frac{a}{7}\rfloor\>,

and

\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor-\lfloor\lb(\frac{n}{i})\rfloor-1=a-\lfloor\lb(a)\rfloor-1\>.

We check case-by-case the claim
c_{n,i}=a-\lfloor\lb(a)\rfloor-1
holds for
a\in\{3,\ldots,8\}
.
So
\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor-\lfloor\lb(\frac{n}{i})\rfloor-1
satisfies
(b)
and
(c)
of Lemma
3.1
.
Next we check that it satisfies the identity in
(a)
of Lemma
3.1
, that is, we show

\sum_{j\geq 1\textrm{ odd}}^{\lfloor n/i\rfloor}(\lfloor\frac{n}{i\cdot j}\rfloor-\lfloor\lb(\frac{n}{i\cdot j})\rfloor-1)=\sum_{j>1\textrm{ odd}}\lfloor\frac{n}{i\cdot j}\rfloor,\ \ \text{for all}\ \ n\geq i\>.

The above identity is equivalent to the following statement.

\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor=\sum_{j\geq 1\textrm{ odd}}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor}(\lfloor\lb(\lfloor\frac{n}{i\cdot j}\rfloor)\rfloor+1),\ \ \text{for all}\ \ n\geq i\>,

which is true by Lemma

.
This finishes the proof for

.

(b)

.

Corollary 3.6
.

The number of
1
’s in
c_{n}
is
\lfloor n/3\rfloor-\lfloor n/5\rfloor
.

More generally,

(a)

if
j=2^{t}-t-1
for some
t\in\mathbb{N}
, then the number of entries equal to
j
in
c_{n}
is
\lfloor\frac{n}{2^{t}-1}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{n}{2^{t}+1}\rfloor
,

(b)

if
j\neq 2^{t}-t-1
for any
t\in\mathbb{N}
, then the number of entries equal to
j
in
c_{n}
is
\lfloor\frac{n}{j+t_{0}}\rfloor-\lfloor\frac{n}{j+t_{0}+1}\rfloor
, where
t_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
is such that
2^{t_{0}}-t_{0}-1<j<2^{t_{0}+1}-(t_{0}+1)-1
.

Proof.

Using Proposition

Proposition 5.1
.

For
n\geq 1
,

(a)

\beta(n,1,m)=P_{2^{m}}(n)
,

(b)

\beta(2n,-1,m)=P_{2^{m}}(n)
and
\beta(2n-1,-1,m)=0
.

Proof.

(a)
First note that
\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,1)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}2^{m_{\lambda}(i)}=2^{\sum m_{\lambda}(i)}=2^{\ell(\lambda)}
, where
\ell(\lambda)
is the number of parts of
\lambda
. Hence,

\beta(n,1,m)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}2^{m\ell(\lambda)}.

To see that this equals
P_{2^{m}}(n)
, notice that from each ordinary partition
\lambda
of
n
(that is, a partition with parts of a single sort), we can create
2^{m\ell(\lambda)}
partitions with
2^{m}
sorts of parts. There are
2^{m}
choices for the sorts for the first part,
2^{m}
choices for the sorts for the second part, etc.

(b)
If
\lambda
has an odd part, then
\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,-1)=0
. If
\mathcal{P}_{e}(n)
is the set of partitions of
n
with all parts even, then

\beta(n,-1,m)=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{e}(n)}\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,-1)^{m}.

Since all partitions of
2n-1
have at least one odd part,
\beta(2n-1,-1,m)=0
.

The transformation
\lambda\to\lambda/2
is a bijection from
\mathcal{P}_{e}(2n)
to the set of all partitions of
n
. If
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{e}(2n)
, we have
\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,-1)=\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,1)=\mathrm{sp}(\lambda/2,1).
Thus
\beta(2n,-1,m)=\beta(n,1,m)
and the result follows from
(a)
.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
and denote by
\beta^{\prime}(n,x,m)
the derivative of
\beta(n,x,m)
with respect to
x
. Then

\mathrm{val}_{2}(\beta^{\prime}(n,1,k))=\mathrm{val}_{2}(n)+\mathrm{val}_{2}(k)+k-1.

Proof.

We have

\beta^{\prime}(n,x,k)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}k\cdot\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,x)^{k-1}\mathrm{sp}^{\prime}(\lambda,x).

Using logarithmic differentiation, we have

\mathrm{sp}^{\prime}(\lambda,x)=\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,x)\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{\lambda}(i)\frac{ix^{i-1}}{1+x^{i}}.

Evaluating at
x=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}(n,1,k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}k\cdot\mathrm{sp}(\lambda,1)^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{\lambda}(i)\frac{i}{2}=\frac{1}{2}nk\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}2^{k\ell(\lambda)}=\frac{1}{2}nk2^{k}\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}2^{k(\ell(\lambda)-1)}.

Since
\lambda=(n)
is the only partition of
n
with
\ell(\lambda)=1
, it follows that
\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}2^{k(\ell(\lambda)-1)}
is odd. Hence,
\mathrm{val}_{2}(\beta^{\prime}(n,1,k))=\mathrm{val}_{2}(n)+\mathrm{val}_{2}(k)+k-1.

∎

Open Questions

Analogously to
\mathrm{sr}_{\mathcal{B}}(n,x)
for the binary partitions of
n
, we can define
sr_{\mathcal{A}}(n,x)
for any subset
\mathcal{A}(n)
of partitions of
n
and study the questions posed in this article. We offer the following conjectures.

Let
\mathcal{O}(n)
be the set of odd partitions of
n

Conjecture 8
.

For
n\geq 1
,
\mathrm{num}_{\mathcal{O}}(n,-1)=\mathbf{o}(n!)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}(n)
be the set of ternary partitions of
n
, that is, partitions whose parts are powers of
3
.

Conjecture 9
.

Let
s(n)=\mathrm{num}_{\mathcal{T}}(n,-1)
. Then
s(1)=s(2)=1
and
s(3n)=s(3n+1)=s(3n+2)=3^{\mathrm{val}_{3}((3n)!)}

Conjecture 10
.

Let
t(n)=\mathrm{num}_{\mathcal{T}}(n,1)
. Then
t(1)=t(2)=1
and
t(3n)=t(3n+1)=t(3n+2)

t(n)=\Delta(t(3n-2)/2^{2n})
.

Finally, we note that one can replace
x^{i}
in
