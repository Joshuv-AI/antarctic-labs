trustworthy laser threat warning (LTW) to protect

4 March 2010 – V28

Executive summary

Comprehensive space force protection must include effective and trustworthy laser threat
warning (LTW). Effective LTW will detect and characterize threats to space assets and thus
enhance space deterrence. LTW must be trustworthy: able to categorize threats and non-threats
by being both sensitive to true events and resistant to false alarms. Outside of the laboratory, the
statistics and even the roles of lasers become unclear. In the chain of events leading to an attack,
the laser may be the last link to be understood. Human situational awareness and informal
reasoning must blend statistics with circumstantial evidence to visualize these chains before they
are clear. This paper1 sets out an industrial model for an enterprise2 that will routinely produce
trustworthy LTW. By incorporating psychology and economics, this enterprise can overcome the
difficulties and perils of cooperation in networked defense and intelligence. This roadmap
suggests how the enterprise can incentivize distracted actors with different goals to share what
they know and coordinate what they do.

To begin, we describe LTW in terms of adversaries, lasers, and targets. Based on interviews with
experts and observational research in similar sensing domains (e.g., Measurement and Signatures
Intelligence or MASINT), we show how distracted actors can work LTW situations by
participating in “markets” for fresh evidence. We model actors using fictional but evidence-
based “Personas” [2] to illustrate the skills, motivations, and goals of the expert practitioners we
studied. Then we animate the personas in three basic but challenging scenarios to show how
actors can think and work both together and apart. We propose three product concepts—
structured message, chat dialogue, and report—to help guide the actors up the learning curves to
trustworthy LTW. We sketch out three tools for actor teamwork that can reduce the labor of
LTW: First Responders, Event Detectives, and Threat Curators.

We begin with what makes LTW hard: seeing a situational chain begin to form from links that
are seldom visible to a single actor or intelligence discipline.

Laser Threat Warning (LTW) Background: The links that may form into an attack

A fire requires a chain of three links: fuel, oxygen, and ignition. In a similar way, a laser attack
requires four things: an adversary, a laser, a pathway, and a target. To detect an attack—and
more importantly the early indicators of an attack—LTW must gather together information about
each of these links. Let us consider each link in the chain, beginning with the laser.

Lasers are machines for making beams of light that are narrow in every sense of the word. Laser
light diverges little from its path. The light is one color (monochromatic) and coherent (all the
waves are aligned in the same phase). Lasers are powerful: they put out many watts. Like
headlights probing down a dark road, a laser can generate enough watts to illuminate a distant
target. But like headlights in fog, the transferred energy is reduced by scattering. Photons of light
tend to bump into things along the way that absorb or divert them from their path to the target.
Possessing a laser and employing it despite scattering and other physics problems requires
capability.

Adversaries are trying to invent laser capability and apply their capability to achieve strategic
goals. Laser capabilities go up a ramp from relatively easy to difficult, and from benign to hostile
[3, 4]. We begin at the base of the ramp.

Lasers are excellent range finders, measuring distance, speed, and direction of objects in orbit.
Knowing these properties lets adversaries build catalogs of space assets, infer purposes and
performance (e.g., what is visible to an asset at a particular time), and potentially to plan laser or
kinetic attacks. Laser systems can “query” materials, analyzing reflected light to determine what
a target is made of. A laser can even be used like a flash lamp to illuminate a dark target for an
imager. None of these capabilities is easy, but all are relatively benign.

Continuing up the ramp, a more powerful laser can temporarily dazzle a sensor on a target by
overloading it with light. Beyond some point, excess light will permanently blind the sensor by
physically damaging its imaging elements. Finally, a laser can disable the target permanently by
overheating it and burning out solar panels, circuitry, etc.

The capability ramp shows us that a laser system has the curious property of being both sensor
and weapon. There is no consistent demarcation between the two purposes in terms of physical
measurements like frequency or power. Like a nudge and a punch, it’s a matter of degree. So the
laser link must be understood in the context of other links to infer intention and target.

Targets for lasers are various types and sizes of spacecraft. Spacecraft are in different orbits,
which means different ranges and scattering conditions for the laser to reach through. A target
may be easy to illuminate during one part of its orbit, difficult a short time later, and impossible
when it has passed from view. A target spacecraft may belong to the adversary or someone else.
An adversary may practice for a long time against their own spacecraft, or surreptitiously against
other’s, to develop capability. If we can detect these practice sessions, then we can understand an
adversary’s capability ramp and potentially encourage lawful behavior.

The illumination that reaches the target is called irradiance. Irradiance is the power incident on a
surface, measured in watts per square meter. To produce a desired effect on the target, like
heating it up, irradiance must persist long enough to transfer energy. Energy is measured as watts
of power over a period of time, with units of joules.

Lasers do not exist in nature, but the light they emit is often indistinguishable from natural
sources. Spacecraft are dazzled by light all the time, either directly from the sun or from sunlight
glinting off water, snow, or ice on the earth. So what a laser can do is not unique from the

perspective of a target—especially down low on the capability ramp. Of course, when bright
light becomes hot damaging light that is a problem. But the reality is that spacecraft heat up and
cool down all the time. They overheat. They degrade and fail without any assistance from
adversaries. To claim that a failure is the result of a laser attack requires extraordinary evidence,
a fact that adversaries understand well. Defenders must construct a web of circumstantial
evidence to interpret the context of an apparent attack. At the center of that web is a laser
detector.

Some targets are equipped with laser detectors in the hope of discovering whether the target is
being occasionally or routinely visited by laser beams. This history is good to know, because
benign visits (like ranging) could be the precursors to an attack. But lasers can be surprisingly
difficult to detect reliably.

Laser detectors. Laser beams are narrow, so they are often only visible from a single sensor
located on the target itself. Lasers are fast; range finding or imaging may occur very rapidly. So
laser detectors must be very sensitive to detect mild threats like ranging, or attacks that barely
missed. Yet they must also be very resistant to false alarms. As a result, laser sensors are what
engineers call “temperamental” machines. This means they experience different “states of
health” (SOH) [5]. Depending on the current health of the sensor, a “detection” could be the
result of a photon of sunlight (energy), a charged particle from the sun (mass), or even a transient
behavior of flakey electronics.

As we have seen, LTW is a chain of very different links, each of which is uncertain. Adversaries
have uncertain capabilities, to say nothing of their intentions. Targets move, which continuously
changes the laser pathway through atmosphere and weather. Along any pathway, a laser beam
can be transformed into something that looks like sunlight or vice versa. This makes detection
difficult, and a detector will be prone to either misses or false alarms depending on its current
state of health.

While the LTW mission is difficult, experience in other sensing enterprises suggests that it is
workable in real-world situations by combining formal statistics with informal team reasoning.
We’ll illustrate this with a fictional but evidence-based exercise where the participants begin
with only statistics, then mix in situational awareness and human reasoning for significant
performance improvements.

A fictitious but realistic LTW tabletop exercise: Statistics and situations

How does any detector decide yes or no?3 All detectors use a threshold that is based on the
statistics of past experience. For example, a residential heat detector typically triggers at 136
degrees Fahrenheit. Long experience has shown that a temperature above 136 degrees in a
dwelling likely corresponds to a fire.

As we saw above, the complex physics of lasers means that a single measurement will rarely
provide a clear threshold. For example, irradiance (watts per square meter) on a spacecraft does
not clearly and promptly separate laser light from natural light. Instead, many different
measurements need to be combined into a multivariate classifier.4 An algorithm like this lets the

 Page 3 of 21

LTW detector, like the heat detector, declare yes or no in advance of complete certainty.
Complete certainty is often too late, either to escape a house fire or to protect spacecraft from
laser attack.

Suppose that an LTW engineering team has developed a classification algorithm that blends
irradiance together with several other measurements to calculate laser detections. From their
database, the engineers determine that when the algorithm scores between 3 and 7, that is a laser
hit. Less or more is not. This sounds simple. But notice the dependence on the database of
experience.

The engineers team up with some operators to test out the forthcoming LTW system in a space
defense exercise. A real but friendly blue force laser will have capabilities to “attack” the sensor.
But will it? Where? When? And will the defenders know?

During the first round of the exercise, a detector event occurs that scores a 2. That’s below the
threshold range, which means no laser. Then another event occurs, scoring 6 which means yes.

After the first round is over, the judges give the verdict. First event: called No, but actually
Present. It was a dazzle attack, but it mostly missed hence the low score.

Second event: called Yes, but actually Absent. Maybe it was sun glint? Results: one miss, one
false alarm equals zero for two.

The team huddles to plan for Round 2. It is clear that the database is inadequate. The engineers
have assumed the statistics are robust: that they form proper distributions from which predictions
can be made.5 But there are not enough samples to fully describe the statistical distributions.
Furthermore, the classification algorithm cannot compensate for a lack of measurement data. It
cannot perform mathematical calculations on intended irradiance that missed. Is there some way
to compensate for lack of experience and measurements?

Someone points out that they are looking at just a single link in the chain of a laser attack. Could
they use contextual information from outside their own sensor? Could they supplement the
statistical score with circumstantial evidence about the other links? The team gets an idea.

In round 2 they will team with other teams. They will collaborate6 to discover evidence about
other links in the chain.

Round 2, first event: The score is 1, which statistically is negative. But the LTW operators have
collaborated, using secure text chat, with others. In a casual exchange, they met another team
operating a communications sensor. During some brief conversations, they told the comms team
just enough about their mission to “prime” them for laser-relevant communications. As the
comms team listens to their sensor, they hear something about a cluster of adversary remarks that
reminds them of LTW. They tip this information, with a caveat that they don’t really know what
LTW needs and this probably is not it. But the LTW team finds the content and timing of the
remarks persuasive evidence of adversary intentions. Combined with the timing of the event,
they call it a Yes and thank the comms team for the assist.

Event 2: The score is 6, which statistically is a Yes. But there is additional evidence available,
because the LTW operators have collaborated in a “public” secure chat room. Their conversation
with the comms team was noticed by an imaging team. When the LTW operators cautiously state
that they might have a hit from a particular location, the imaging team suddenly volunteers to
take a look. Soon, the imagers pass back their call: they do not see any laser source in the general
vicinity. The LTW team thanks the imagers publicly and makes their own call: No, it’s probably
sun glint due to the season and orbital geometry.

Event 3 scores a 7, which statistically is a Yes. Unfortunately, there is no comms available
because that team is focusing their sensor on a sudden priority. But a bystander in the chat room
points out that weather is also a factor in LTW. He does this by simply asking a representative of
an intelligence weather shop if a real-time weather look would be possible. No problem at all,
replies the weather shop. They soon make their call: Moderately cloudy. The LTW team thanks
them and makes their own call: No hit, probable SOH issue with the laser sensor.

Event 4 scores a 9, which is out of range. But the LTW team considers the score in the context of
previous vague communications alluding to a “new system” in a time range. A new system
would be new to the database and algorithm, the overall situation is favorable, and the time is
right. They call it a Yes.

There are more events, and when the judges give the Round 2 results they are completely
different. The LTW team has gone from mostly wrong to more right than wrong. They have done
what collaborating teams can sometimes do: the situationally difficult and the statistically
impossible.

Cooperative behavior to achieve LTW: A microeconomic model

The fictional exercise above illustrated how each score from the laser sensor’s classifier could be
interpreted in specific context. By gathering evidence about other links in the LTW chain, the
team supplemented their sparse database and strong but brittle algorithm with circumstantial
evidence.

We now consider what looked easy in the exercise: the gathering of evidence from busy,
distracted, often parochial actors. The problem is the most useful evidence usually does not exist
—until it is created by a service: an expenditure of labor to solve a specific problem. For
example, the comms analysts did not simply look up the keyword “laser” in a database. They
actively and expertly listened. In an ocean of distracters, they heard a pattern of evocative
statements—which did not even include the word laser—that triggered a linguistic association.
They performed a service that produced a small amount of rather dubious data. But moved into
another context, this data was transformed into valuable evidence.

Services must be discovered, especially when providers don’t know quite what they have to offer
and consumers don’t know quite what they need. The chat room in the exercise served as a
market, a social structure that gathers together producers and consumers to exchange goods (like

 Page 5 of 21

prepackaged data) and services. By bringing together widely dispersed bits of knowledge [9], a
market reduces the costs of solving problems that can’t be fully anticipated.

Any exchange of goods or services entails transaction costs: the time and trouble of moving
something from one party (the producer) across time and space to another party (the consumer).
Transaction costs include search costs: the effort of finding a source of what is needed. It would
have been expensive for the LTW team to find providers for their sudden service needs. Instead,
the market did the finding for them by dramatically reducing transaction costs: the proverbial
invisible hand. A bystander acted as a broker, almost effortlessly using what they knew to
connect parties into a mutually beneficial exchange.

Services are effort, so they are not free. Services entail production costs: the costs of
transforming things into other things. While the comms team and the imaging team made it look
easy to a casual observer in the chat room, many resources like time, training, computing,
management, and so on were consumed to produce the services. None of these production
ingredients are plentiful or free. At one point, the comms team had to turn LTW away because
the opportunity cost of assisting had suddenly become too high. They would have liked to help,
but the resulting cost to a higher priority customer was too great. The comms team was averse to
the risk of criticism, a form of market signal.

During the exercise, the services did not just produce data. The services produced calls:
subjective judgments by people with specialized experience and powerful tools. What makes
calls so powerful is also what makes them so perilous: they may turn out, with the luxury of
hindsight, to be wrong. The LTW team requires calls, because they do not have the luxury of
hindsight. The exercise judges will be unimpressed by any answers of “We’re still thinking
about it.”

But the market for calls is often undersupplied, because intelligence and defense are, for various
historical and cultural reasons, risk averse and punitive. While the exercise is based on real
examples, participants in these chat markets are both organizationally empowered and
personally bold. They must absorb the risks of providing evidence that is low grade (inferior in
relation to other products) but high quality (having situational utility for a consumer)[10].7
When organizations measure their success in terms of products (e.g., volume of data
disseminated), they may see high opportunity costs in personnel engaging in chat or providing
spontaneous services. In the exercise, this was not an issue but service availability will be a
large part of real LTW.

If data goods or judgment services are unavailable or too costly, the chat market can help to find
substitutes: other services or ingredients, available at tolerable cost, that can help to solve a
problem. During the exercise, the cost of comms intel went to infinity—but a broker helped to
find a weather substitute which was quite different but low cost and useful. Substitution is not
just a response to scarcity. For example, sometimes comms sensing is abundant but useless
because a particular activity is silent by design.

We have now looked closer at the exercise to see how the LTW team built up a web of
circumstantial evidence around the crisp numbers of the classifier (Figure 1). The team gathered

decimated their cost of finding expert services that delivered subjective but useful judgment

Risk and vigilance: More behavioral economics of LTW operationalization

Next, we turn to other areas that looked easy in the exercise but must be overcome by the
sustained LTW enterprise. These are the cost of doubt (risk) and the cost of watching nothing
(vigilance).

In real operations, LTW will not deliver crisp numerical answers to be graded by omniscient
judges. Instead LTW will have to assist military watch who know and care little about LTW per
se to allocate attention and concern. There will be real costs and consequences and risks of both
under reaction and overreaction. If LTW doubts their temperamental detector too much, they
could help the military watch to under react. If they doubt their temperamental detector too little,
they could help the military watch to overreact.

 Page 7 of 21

Instead of the motivating energy of a defined exercise, LTW will have to deal in the intel market
for vigilance services. Vigilance services are the endless costly watching for rare valuable
events. These services try to balance the costs of keeping watch that are local and continuous
against the benefits of recognizing the global and rare. Potential providers will protest on the one
hand that “We don’t have time for that” and on the other hand “If anything ever happens, we
won’t get any credit.” As months go by the chat room will become silent. And clues that could
become evidence will pass in the night.

Engineers will need to continuously add to the database and tweak the classification algorithm.
But in the absence of omniscient judges, ground truth will be rare. Without clear feedback on
true and false, learning will be difficult. What is real, what is noise, and what is a key step up an
adversary’s capability ramp? Engineers and operators will need to work together to separate the
true from the false from the “interesting and needs further analysis.”

Operationalization is difficult and the challenges extend far beyond engineering of the detector.
But previous research in analogous sensing domains show they are workable if goals are defined
for teams to solve. To suggest both the power and subtlety of the cooperative behaviors involved
we will turn to scenarios. Here we will see the work in more detail, an unpredictable but
purposeful improvisation of individuals talking, thinking, and cooperating.

Scenarios: Visualizing LTW in action

We now use three short scenarios to explore operationalization challenges and solutions. To
include the skills, motivations, and goals of expert practitioners who participated in our study,8
we constructed fictional yet representative personas to act in the scenarios. Personas are concise
engineering models of “how users behave, how they think, what they wish to accomplish, and
why” [2]. (Details of the personas are in the appendix on p. 17.)

Scenario 1: a nominal shift

Even in a nominal shift during which “nothing much happens” a great deal of activity must occur
[12]. The various actors overlap little in time and space, leaving boundary zones between roles
and time periods where communication breaks down, handoffs fail quietly, and accidents begin
[13]. When things fall through the cracks, the cracks are usually between rather than within
organizational units. Bridging these boundaries are synchronous communication (face-to-face,
telephone, video), asynchronous communication (email, voicemail, blog), and semi-synchronous
communication (person-person instant messaging and group text chat). The asynchronous
methods use time efficiently, but they do not accurately convey subtleties like confidence, doubt,
and concern. They also lack the error correction of back-and-forth dialogue.

Karen, the sensor engineer, assesses the sensor SOH and conveys approval to continue operation
in an email to Alison, the sensor analyst. In turn, Alison requests operations and passes on
caveats and red flags in an email to the sensor lead, Mike.

As sensor lead, Mike straddles the gulf between the cultures of 8X5 (office carpet) and 24X7
(floor tiles). Mike is in effect the business manager of the sensor, scheduling people and
resources to set up shifts. When Carol, the sensor chief, walks onto the ops floor a few hours
later, Mike has gone home but there are delayed (asynchronous) emails and voicemails waiting
for Carol. The departing chief also engages in a live “pass down” dialogue during which Carol
can verbalize her thoughts and ask questions.

Carol the sensor chief and Larry, the sensor operator, exchange pleasantries, status, changes, and
watch-fors. Carol reads through the emails and verbalizes some of the contents. This reminds
Larry of something: he happened to chat with one of Alison the sensor analyst’s colleagues and
heard something that didn’t quite make it into the email. Carol jots a note to follow this up.9

The outgoing crew morphs into the new crew. Larry and/or Carol connect to Lieutenant Lewis,
the military watch officer, to exchange greetings and salutations, status, situational emphasis,
special concerns, and so on.

Hours pass. The crew generates purposeful variability to counteract natural variation in an
uneventful environment [12], and so nothing especially remarkable happens. The crew tunes to
maintain a reasonable threshold, notices a surprising weather nowcast in chat, acts to acquire
additional evidence from a recent comms report, and predicts what will and will not happen next.
As the shift winds down, asynchronous communications carrying history (logs) and questions are
launched back towards the 8X5 world.

Scenario 2: a detect but reject incident

It is another day. Karen the sensor engineer and Alison the sensor analyst have been running
calibrations on the sensor, trending the results, and comparing them to detection incidents. They
are concerned about SOH but cannot draw any firm conclusions. They lack statistical robustness
because they have a small batch not a statistical sample [8]. They send an email with notional
trends and some specific examples on to the sensor lead. Mike the sensor lead thinks about how
to translate from days to ops. He forwards the email onward, prepending remarks on the changes,
suggesting a general emphasis (watch-fors), and giving specific examples from training.

Carol, the incoming sensor chief again, reads this email as the outgoing chief looks over her
shoulder and points out how some of the “specifics” become ambiguous in an actual context.
Carol ponders, and then, hardcopy in hand, verbally reframes the contents into the current
situation and experience of the crew. Larry, again the sensor operator, recalls a previous
experience. That’s the one I was trying to remember, responds Carol.

Time passes. It is looking like another nominal shift when… Detection. Larry cannot accept the
detection as a true positive. But given some situational evidence he cannot reject it either. Larry
recruits Carol into rapid situational dialogue. They consider the general SOH trend and the
specific SOH context. They reason informally about time, position, and location. In effect, they
solve an underdefined simultaneous equation in the shared psycholinguistic workspace between
their heads.10 They reject the detection as a false positive, and make a decision to not alert the
military watch officer.

The non-event has many spreading consequences. It is logged, commented on, passed-down as a
“look at” and “watch for.” It is lateraled into the day world, where it triggers extensive dialogue
and exploratory data analysis. Eventually, the detection becomes an observation in the slowly-
growing statistical database. Statistical resistance has produced another step down the road froj
batch of samples towards statistical robustness.

Scenario 3: a detect and cannot reject incident

It is another shift. Let us suppose the same actors are involved. The day world has worked the
SOH issues encountered in the previous scenario. Things are quiet and then… Detection. Once
again, Larry cannot accept the detection as a true positive or reject it as a true negative. He
reasons quickly with Carol:

 • SOH in general: Been pretty good.
 • SOH in context: Seems pretty good.
 • Sensor position? False positive possible.
 • Ground location? True positive possible.
 • Situation? Elevated concern.
 • Decision: Cannot reject.

Carol calls Lieutenant Lewis the military watch officer on the secure telephone to explain that
she has a detection that she cannot prove is false—but she’s working on it. The live dialogue
helps Carol to convey, in effect, the state of the sensor (potential for real vs. false alarm) and the
prospect theory curve (upside vs. downside risk). Carol must convey just the right level of both
mission concern and sensor skepticism to guide the Lieutenant’s allocation of attention and
choice of actions. Carol and the Lieutenant use sideband channels (voice volume, tone, cadence)
to gauge each other’s level of distraction, understanding, and concern. These feedback signals
allow them to come into reasonable alignment across a professional and cultural gulf.

Meanwhile, Larry types a chat message to a collaboration broker. The broker understands: Larry
needs cloud cover and he needs a re-interpretation of recent comms.11 Not one hour in the past
and not one hour in the future. Now. With a few words, the broker laterals the weather evidence
need to a colleague and pursues the comms evidence need.12 The two needs are less than one
minute old. Each can only be supplied by a just-in-time service that may or may not yield a
return on investment.

Lieutenant Lewis translates the situation up-echelon, explaining that if the detection is valid (and
it may not be) then that would be evidence for a particular event type. Command begins asking
questions that the Lieutenant cannot answer before she even finishes speaking. The Lieutenant
translates and passes some of the questions to Carol. Carol must continue to work the situation
with Larry and is getting overloaded. She signals a colleague who is following the situation: Call
in Alison now. She also alerts the 24X7 Threat Analysis Watch. Based on a terse discussion and
Carol’s intriguing questions, the watch decides that this is the kind of mystery Charles the threat
analyst would find intriguing.

Pagers start going off, recruiting specialists who can study the link budget from the source end
(threat) and target end (sensor). Larry has obtained weather and comms evidence: both are
helpful in placing bounds on the problem but not definitive.

As the sensor analyst and threat analyst roll in and begin to map the new specific evidence into
their older general frameworks (statistical and mental models), they detect discrepancies between

what they expect to see and what they are seeing. This makes them think of new questions about

Summary of the scenarios. Across the three scenarios we see a network of people, organized
both formally and informally, allocate its resources to tune, notice, act, and predict. How can
such activity be trained, organized and equipped? The value of having actors follow checklists or
scripts is limited, because the situations never occur in the same sequences. The actions must be
more like improvisational theatre than an “orchestrated” or “choreographed” procedure that “will
work flawlessly if the enemy does what is expected” [17]. On the other hand, a task aid that
“makes explicit the minimum, expected steps in complex processes” and helps remember
“mundane matters that are easily overlooked” [18] under stress could be helpful.

Product concepts for an LTW market

The scenarios above foresee a future capability that will take many investments to reach. Starting
to build small but tangible things (prototypes) can motivate the incremental steps up this learning
curve. What new and desirable things will the scenarios produce? Let us consider things for the
actors to work with. Based on inspections of nascent LTW products and observations of more
mature analogous enterprises, we suggest three product concepts: structured message, chat
dialogue, and technical report.

Structured Message. A sensor message is a short, fast information signal that invites or requests
attention. It conveys news, preferably before the news event is over or as soon as possible
afterwards. A message conveys information like when, where, and categorical what. Specific
what, and how, and why are usually unknown. The message is thus a call for more evidence. It is
an invitation for other people in other places to contribute attention now and to search their
working memory of the recent past. A message is an invitation to join in a quest for togetherness
of things (qualitative associations), togetherness in time (synchrony), and even covariance (apart
but related measurements).

Because it is timely, a message from one sensor can affect how other sensors are steered, aimed,
and tuned. But if producers send “too many” false alarms, future messages are discounted
because the return on investment of paying attention is doubted and perceived risk goes up.

Chat Dialogue. Messages from various sensors are often posted and discussed in secure chat
rooms. A chat room acts as a service (sometimes called a multi-sided market) that catalyzes
interactions and induces coordination between consumers and producers of different types of
products [19]. Individuals can ask informally, what do we see here? What do we understand and
not understand? What do you think? What evidence is missing, and where might it be found?

Secure chat has become popular because of relatively small downside (most managers at least
tolerate it), a good likelihood of small upside (conversation provides useful professional context),
and occasional large enterprise upside (mission-relevant chat is believed to help decision-makers

                                         Page 12 of 21

avert problems and seize opportunities). The problem with chat is unpredictable value; it has a
signal-to-noise ratio problem. Chat is a flowing potpourri of fluff and nuggets, and what is fluff
to one participant is a nugget to another. Not surprisingly, chat is maddening to some analysts
who must carefully conserve their attention to accomplish difficult tasks over extended periods
of time.

Technical report. A technical report assembles and interprets evidence such as messages.
Reports reconcile the inevitable contradictions of different sensors looking at the same thing. A
report attempts to reconstruct history and to show real causality and refute plausible but wrong
causality. Reports are usually quantitative in sections, but there are many gaps and no “QED”
will be found at the end. Instead there is narrative strung between the pillars of solid, preferably
quantitative, facts. Reports are relatively tangible, helping to reassure managers concerned about
ROI that their organization produced (or contributed to) something durable, measurable, and
useful. The problem with LTW technical reports is the current scarcity of facts and the difficulty,
pre-robustness, of justifying interpretations.

These product concepts sound useful, even important. But they show that LTW has a chicken-
and-egg problem. We don’t know how to make products that are trustworthy, because we are
still in the realm of exploratory data analysis. We have few observations, because the sensors are
not available, because there is not yet demand. And so on. LTW must jumpstart the market by
beginning to craft and show prototypes. To avoid triviality (e.g., pretty but bogus PowerPoint
slides) the prototypes must be crafted by diverse actors: the real people behind the personas
listed above. By definition, these actors are rare, dispersed, distracted, and busy. What can bring
their efforts together across space and time? While not sufficient, simple, powerful teamwork
tools are necessary.

Teamwork tool concepts

The plural names (responders, detectives, curators) emphasize that these are tools for teamwork
not solo work. The tools must help the teams to address the problems included with each product
above: trustworthy messages (balancing sensitivity to truth against specificity against false
alarms), dialogue that can be confusing (“Did anyone let him know that?”) and then ephemeral
(“Does anyone remember that email mentioned in chat sometime last month…?”), and
compelling reports that explain and predict cautiously but still usefully.

First Responders is a tool concept to help actors like Larry and Carol the sensor operators
perform triage: the rapid allocation of resources under stress and uncertainty. The concept is
based on a LTW decision heuristic called the Event Assessment Spreadsheet [20]. The
spreadsheet is a device for, in effect, working the physics and statistics problems both
quantitatively and qualitatively. Some sections are like a calculator, others a multiple-choice
questionnaire. The limitation of the spreadsheet is its binary output: yes or no. In advance of
statistical robustness, we need a tool that generates scores of grayness, not black or white
“answers.”

A design pattern from far afield is the Apgar Test14 for rapidly assessing infant health. The
Apgar is not easy to use; it requires expert perception by the attending physician to judge
criteria—only one of the five is a number than can plugged in. But by generating a single score
that is binned into low, medium, high ranges, the test structures judgments to allocate diverse
resources.15 The score is not an answer, but a guideline.

For LTW, an analogous “First Responders” tool with score ranges blessed by exploratory
statisticians and endorsed by management would help Larry and Carol decide whether to release
a message in a manner that is disciplined and justifiable, but also flexible. The message metadata
might even include the table and score. With training, military watchstanders and other actors
could grasp the structure and implications of the tool.

Event Detectives is a tool concept to help team-of-teams work over dispersed locations and time
periods to track, organize, and make sense of the evidence from an event. Event Detectives is
intended to reduce the costs and risks of cooperative actors, like those in the event scenarios
above, who have minimal overlap and many distractions. It combines the informal interpretation
of chat dialogue with preservation, recall, and editing of information. It supports authoring,
reviewing, and editing of rich long-form documents which will become technical reports. To
attract participants Event Detectives must offer low overhead, a sense of accomplishment, and
credit for effort contributed. If introduced and used wisely, Event Detectives could support
organizational introspection and continuous learning without the typical resentments of “Monday
morning quarterbacking.”

Event Detectives also respects the need of analysts for uninterrupted periods of sustained
concentration. For example, a person acting as team “radioman” [21] could paste relevant chat
into Event Detectives to reduce the need for everyone to follow or review the chat flood.

Threat Curators is a tool concept for managing a history of events and observations. It is a place
to store and revisit “look-ats” and “watch-fors.” Curators would probably be a seamless
extension of Event Detectives that provides a “signal zoo” (a term from another sensor domain
we have studied) or museum of observations and associated information like messages and
technical reports. Participants would have a shared responsibility to populate the zoo, curate the
collection, and apply it to the enterprise. As a community they would vet observations into
legitimate samples, develop heuristics for acquiring evidence, tune sensors, evolve the First
Responders decision categories, pull out real examples for incident-based training, and so on.

The process of curation, interpretation, and re-interpretation would invent an LTW art
(exploratory data analysis) that forms the foundation of science (confirmatory data analysis) [8,
15]. Among other applications, a populated (i.e., statistically robust) Threat Curators repository
could support machine pattern detection that assists operators in noticing and judging events. By
showing resemblances to familiar patterns that the operators themselves curate, this automation
would be more likely to be trusted and thus used in daily work.

Summary

This report has set out a descriptive (observation-based) and prospective (roadmap) model of the
present and future of LTW. We suggest that LTW must solve three nested problems (physics of
light, statistics of signals, economics of effort and decision) through both formal (math and logic)
and informal (situational reasoning) activities that draw on diverse, distracted, and risk-averse

                                            Page 15 of 21

talents. These dispersed actors must work apart but together to perform four actions: tune sensors
to balance sensitivity against false alarms, notice important but statistically unremarkable
changes, act to reallocate resources, and predict the future.

We illustrate the actors through personas, and the actions through three scenarios: nominal
stability, detect but reject, and detect and cannot reject. We suggest the actions will produce and
consume three products: rapid messages to recruit attention, dialogue to interpret and make sense
of different messages in context, and technical reports to combine and deconflict evidence into
narratives of inferred cause and effect. The investments needed to reach this state can be
encouraged by three tool concepts for teamwork that we label First Responders, Event Detectors,
and Threat Curators. This roadmap sets out a direction towards a trustworthy LTW enterprise
that can work the inevitably underdefined equations of LTW to generate “quick responses [that]
shape events before they have become crystallized into a single meaning” [22].

APPENDIX: Personas for Laser Threat Warning

Personas are fictional but evidence-based models of actors for the design of new technology:
“how users behave, how they think, what they wish to accomplish, and why” [2]. The following
personas are based on interviews with LTW experts and observational research in similar sensing
domains (e.g., Measurement and Signatures Intelligence or MASINT). All of the quotations are
from actual people.

 Lieutenant Lewis, military watch officer, serves as a bridge between very diverse sensing
operations and very diverse military commands. During 12-hour shifts, the Lieutenant’s attention
is pulled in four different directions: responding to superiors, supervising subordinates, talking
upstream with sensor producers, and talking downstream with military consumers. The “LT” is a
generalist with a degree in foreign policy and a minor in mathematics. She doesn’t expect a
“Pearl Harbor in space” but believes “a healthy level of paranoia is healthy.” The LT specializes
in quickly reading situations and asking hard questions. She likes to assure analysts that “I can
do the math” then quote General Powell: “I don’t need facts. … People tell me lots of facts. I
need to know what it means, how important it is, what you think about it” [23]. The LT must
read each superior’s personality and mood: some want to know about everything, others consider
this micromanagement. But everything can change with situation and force posture. Many
superior officers like to alternate off-the-wall questions with detailed questions to see if they can
blindside an LT who has become over fixated or trip up another who isn’t following details.

 Alison, sensor analyst, is essentially the front office of the LTW sensor. She is a bridge and
translator between engineering, operations, and information consumers. An engineer by training,
Alison speaks the language of each group and uses their tools. She can read Karen’s software
code (and hack it if necessary), help Mike confirm and document a bug in the operator interface,
and has even worked Satellite Tool Kit (STK) models with Charles the threat analyst. Alison
spends a lot of time tracking and trending sensor SOH. She works 8X5 and tries to review every
detection and some raw data at least once each day.

 Mike, sensor lead, is essentially the business manager of the LTW operation. He does a little
of everything: operator recruiting, training, scheduling, incident reviews, lessons learned. Mike
works 8X5 but is certified as an operator, and still works at least one shift each month. Mike
sometimes refers to himself as a “slide monkey” because he spends so much time briefing
leadership and stakeholders about the value and potential of LTW. Mike wants to showcase
LTW with good catches but is paranoid about crying wolf. Mike struggles with the management
challenge of a quiet but vital sensor: how to keep his crews motivated and sharp through the long
hours of boredom. He tries to keep an eye on detections and events without making his chiefs
and crews feel like he is “Monday morning quarterbacking” them.

 Carol, sensor chief, has crewed several different sensor operations over the years. She doesn’t
mind the long shifts and enjoys being hands-on at the “pointy end” of operations. Carol believes
that it is “just a matter of time” until a laser attack occurs. Carol runs a tight ship and keeps her
crews on their toes. Her philosophy is to “pass the data and let others do their jobs” but hastens
to add that “every situation is different.”

                                         Page 17 of 21

 Larry, sensor operator, is ex-military and working his first operator job. Good with visual
patterns, he learns rapidly from examples and experiences, and has excellent recall of past
events. He is able to tolerate long periods of boredom and stay effective when the adrenaline
flows. Larry also works the chat rooms, trolling for situational awareness and discussion of LTW
needs and products. A bit of a risk taker, he leans towards “having confidence in what we send
out” but in debates with Carol and others will always concede that “every situation is different.”

 Charles, threat analyst, is a physicist with an applied attitude. He likes investigating real-
world mysteries and making insightful but cautious predictions. Charles manages his own time
and is very selective about what he spends it on. He is particularly good at skimming large
quantities of intel and quickly judging “what might not be crap.” Charles assembles bits and
pieces of evidence then makes inferences to write “low-confidence assessments” for leaders and
policymakers. He sees LTW sensors as promising sources of hard numbers but is concerned the
thresholds are set too high to get good exploratory data.

 Karen, sensor engineer, is the front office and technical support desk for the sensor factory.
Karen and her team track and trend SOH. She personally responds to bug reports, and either
fixes the code herself or hands it off to other factory developers. Karen and her team are also
responsible for new development, but can usually diagnose and repair problems quickly if they
can compare “what actually happened with what we expected to happen.” Karen also has project
management responsibilities, so she relies on Alison the sensor analyst and others to send
complete packages of data and respond to questions rapidly. She reminds them that “We can’t fix
what we don’t understand.” Karen is particularly concerned about the productivity and attention
of her developers, so that they are not constantly distracted yet can still divide up and re-combine
efforts.
