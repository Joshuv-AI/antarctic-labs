 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Historically, dams have been constructed for a variety of purposes, such as providing a more secure and
reliable water supply, mitigating impacts from variations in river flow, allowing continuous navigability,
and harnessing mechanical power. A relatively small portion of dams have been designed to store or
regulate flows for the purpose of generating electricity (roughly 3% of nationally inventoried dams in the
US and 17% of the dams included in the World Register of Dams). The remaining population of existing
non-powered dams (NPDs) presents both an opportunity to generate renewable energy and a need to
modernize aging infrastructure. This report describes an assessment of more than 2,600 NPDs in the US
that have a collective potential of nearly 4 GW in new power capacity.

Previous national-scale assessments were aimed at evaluating the theoretical maximum power potential at
existing dams in the United States. These estimates were based on the best available information at the
time for water availability, hydraulic head, and representative regional capacity factors. This study revisits
a subset of 3,299 dams identified in the most recent theoretical resource assessment and uses more
detailed and updated hydrologic data to produce estimates of technical potential. These improvements in
data enable estimates that more realistically reflect what is physically possible given simple assumptions
about the existing structure and constraints on flow and head (Figure 1).

The following improvements were incorporated in the assessment of technical potential:

• Long-term streamflow records from gauges and modeled reanalysis streamflow data. Generation is
 modeled over a period of hydrologic variability that more accurately reflects local dynamic
 hydrologic conditions. The use of observational records and an improved modeling method that
 leverages data assimilation together provide a better representation of existing system conditions.

• Historical records of reservoir elevations. By accounting for the influence of primary operating
 purposes on seasonal hydraulic head variation, some expected constraints on reservoir operations can
 be better reflected in the modeled generation potential.

• Technical factors that impact efficiency, such as turbine selection and head- or flow-dependent
 relationships.

Sufficient flow and hydraulic head data were available at 2,616 NPDs which have a total capacity of
approximately 4 GW. The capacities of NPD retrofits are generally lower than those of existing
hydropower projects in the US; NPDs in this study have a mean capacity of 1.5 MW compared to a mean
capacity of 36 MW across existing hydropower plants. This may result in a shift in development
strategies if smaller facilities are to be developed. Most potential capacity (85%) is in federally owned
facilities, although the greatest number of NPDs are owned by local governments. The modeled capacity
factor for NPDs based on available hydrologic records is generally higher than historical capacity factors
at powered dams and in the same sub-basins.

This study continues to build a foundation for deeper exploration of the opportunity space for NPD
development. Steps to further support site identification and development decisions include (1) assessing
the sensitivity of the estimated generation capacity to a broader set of design assumptions (e.g., selection
of design flow, comparing configurations of turbines); (2) applying the same assessment techniques to
NPDs in states and territories beyond the conterminous US; (3) mapping connections between the
evaluated dams and key grid infrastructure, such as transmission lines; and (4) factoring in environmental
or supply flow constraints such as seasonal minimum or maximum flows.

 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 MOTIVATION

The ongoing evolution of the electricity grid in the United States includes changes in energy demands,
major electrification of transportation and industrial sectors, diversification of the fuel mix, rapid growth
in variable renewable energy sources, and advances in battery and energy storage technologies [1]. Given
all these changes, there is an increasing need to understand the future role of and opportunities for
hydropower. Despite a plateau in the construction of large dams and reservoirs with hydropower
capabilities in the US (Figure 3), a total of roughly 6.9 GW of conventional hydropower capacity has
been brought online over the past 50 years. This expansion in capacity is largely a result of development
that uses existing infrastructure, either through facility upgrades or retrofits of previously non-powered
dams (NPDs) [2]. Since 1975, there have been 2.7 times as many power plants associated with
inventoried dams that have become operational than power plants associated with conduits, canals, and
other water diversion structures that are not inventoried as dams (Figure 4).

The appetite for renewable energy sources—including hydropower—is evident in the recent efforts of
federal agencies and funding/incentives. For example, federal support and coordination among federal
agencies and with non-federal entities for hydropower development is strong, as evidenced by the
following examples:

• Leases of power privilege (LOPPs) for federal facilities have been utilized for several large retrofits
 and additions of generating capabilities. Some examples include dams owned by the US Bureau of
 Reclamation (USBR), such as the Granby and Pueblo Dams in Colorado (which became operational
 in 2016 and 2019, respectively, totaling 7 MW) and the Jordanelle Reservoir in Utah (13 MW,
 operational since 2008).

• The recent memorandum of understanding (MoU) [4] among the Department of Energy, USBR, and
 US Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) is aimed at establishing a collaborative strategy toward
 research and development for hydropower, including the development and deployment of innovative
 hydropower technologies to tackle modernization and environmental challenges.

Other initiatives that are relevant to hydropower development include tax credits that were created,
expanded, or extended by the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) of 2022, including the production tax and
investment tax credits. Congress retained hydropower’s eligibility for these tax credits with the passage of
the One Big Beautiful Bill Act in 2025. Some hydropower projects at non-federal plants may also be
eligible for incentives through Section 242 of the Hydroelectric Production Incentive Program.
Additionally, some states have tax incentives and programs with incentives for renewable energy
development that extend to hydropower (including retrofits of existing dams) [5].

 2

The high capital and environmental costs of greenfield development, vast number of NPDs in the US, and
potential for additional benefits motivate the exploration of opportunities to add energy generation to
existing infrastructure. Although these dams may have been built for flood control, navigation, water
supply, or to provide mechanical power, they are often underutilized and lack direct revenue for their
original purposes. Benefits are quantified instead through measures like avoided flood damages or social
use of the reservoir or downstream recreation. High costs for necessary maintenance and rehabilitation of
aging infrastructure could be addressed through new revenue from generation. The benefits of a
hydropower retrofit extend beyond the increased renewable energy capacity and revenue. Retrofitted
NPDs offer choices to off-takers for sourcing energy and can provide additional benefits through energy
resilience (i.e., an additional asset that provides ancillary services such as frequency regulation or fast
starts and stops). In some cases, the benefits include reduced energy burdens through lower power costs
and less reliance on high-polluting energy sources. Additionally, the retrofit process opens the door for
addressing safety or implementing measures such as sediment management and fish passage that make
operations sustainable in the long term. Therefore, these NPD opportunities are especially attractive
because they balance the needs of aging dams with potential revenue benefits and the range of grid
services provided by hydropower.

Accurate assessment of the potential for retrofit and hydropower development of existing dams is critical
in guiding national or state-level planning and policy measures that facilitate and encourage new capacity
developments. Additionally, from the perspective of the infrastructure owner or energy developer, high-
level estimates of the generation potential can aid in the initial screening or site identification process,
allowing more efficient use of time and energy for detailed analysis at locations with sufficient technical
potential. Beyond the hydropower industry, potential changes to water regulating structures can be
important for multi-sector water resource management (e.g., coordinating uses, ensuring water quality
standards are met, etc.).

 POTENTIAL AT NON-POWERED DAMS

Previous large-scale estimates of NPD capacity potential used a variety of approaches to quantify the
potential generation and nominal capacity, drawing on theoretical limits, simple physical constraints, and

different sources were used in these assessments to estimate key inputs (i.e., hydrologic input and
upstream/downstream elevation or hydraulic head), none of the models of potential generation consider
possible modifications to the structure (e.g., increasing the dam height) or constraints on flow or storage.

In the most recent conterminous US (CONUS) scale resource assessment, the theoretical generation
capacity of NPDs was estimated using an approximation of hydraulic head, and the available flow was
based on monthly mean runoff for the drainage area at a given dam [11]. The hydraulic head estimate was
either based on the dam height or the hydraulic height (i.e., the difference between reservoir elevation and
the lowest point in the original streambed). Runoff was calculated at the HUC06 (basin) level using an
area-weighted average of streamflow gauge observations between 1999 and 2008. The runoff was then
translated to streamflow by multiplying runoff per unit area by the reported drainage area in either the
NHDPlus v1 river network or the National Inventory of Dams (NID). The monthly streamflow and
hydraulic head were used to calculate monthly generation, which was summed to provide estimates of
annual generation (Eq. 1):

                                                 𝑖=1(𝑄𝑖 ∗ ∆𝐻 ∗ 𝜂 ∗ 𝑇)/11800                                (Eq. 1)

where Q (ft3⋅s−1) is the average flow during each month, i; ΔH (ft) is the gross head for hydropower
generation; and T is the total generating period during each month, in hours. η is a scalar representing the
generating efficiency, which was assumed to uniformly be 0.85. This represents the maximum theoretical
annual generation. To reflect these limitations in the potential capacity, a regional capacity factor was
applied to each dam to determine capacity (MW) from computed generation (MWh). This capacity factor
is the ratio of actual energy produced in a given period to the hypothetical energy that could be produced
over that period. This approach assumed that the historical capacity factor of existing hydropower dams in
a given hydrologic region was representative of others in the same region. In reality, actual generation is a
function of several factors, including the local hydrology, operational constraints, and type of dam (i.e.,
used for storage vs. run-of-river, tributary vs. main stem of the river network).

Advances in data sharing overcome several of the limitations and challenges that have acted as
roadblocks for assessing hydropower potential at NPDs (Figure 5). These factors, along with the
assumptions applied, resulted in a range of estimates of hydropower potential at the national scale
(Table 1). Some studies described theoretical potential based on hydrology and topography, rather than
technical potential, which accounts for how the technical components are impacted by the inputs (i.e.,
losses, efficiencies that depend on hydraulic head and/or flow).

The quality and resolution of input data have improved on multiple fronts, resulting in more realistic
estimates compared to those in previous assessments of theoretical capacity [11] or based on coarse inputs
(Table 1). For location and hydrography, the NID is now publicly available and includes coordinates and
supporting information that are increasingly checked for accuracy and completeness. Additionally,
significant improvements were made between the versions of the National Hydrography Dataset (NHD)
river network product (NHDPlus version 1 and NHDPlus version 2). The higher accuracy of the
NHDPlus version 2 river network increases confidence in the matches between dams (based on reported
coordinates) and the river network. The connections to an improved streamflow network are essential for
ensuring that water availability can be assessed using either streamflow models or gauged records.
Previously, available flow had been estimated through coarse means (e.g., mean flow or based on regional
regression models). Another improvement in the quality of information is greater detail for hydraulic head
estimates. In prior studies, hydraulic head was approximated from the dam dimensions in simple terms,
and this approach neglected to account for seasonal variation or storage constraints that are typical for
many multi-purpose dams. However, greater reporting of the primary purposes of dams in the NID makes
it possible to more realistically account for these factors.

Other advances incorporated into the present analysis include a more detailed representation of technical
considerations for output. In previous studies, simple, uniform values of efficiency were applied,
regardless of the size of project or any variation in operations or water availability. In reality, turbine
efficiency depends on the available flow and head relative to the design flow and head.

Although these advances improve estimates of generation potential, some needs remain, especially with
respect to detailed operations and constraints at existing facilities. Limits or targets for storage and
releases through existing dams are critical to understanding seasonal variation of generation potential. In
particular, minimum flows that are required for environmental or social benefits can substantially
influence how much water is available at a given time for generation. Considering only water availability
(inflow) to the dam - rather than explicit requirements for seasonal storage or release constraints - will
often result in overestimation of generation. Though flow requirements were not obtained and
incorporated in the present study, historical gauged streamflow records do reflect the impacts of storage
or operational constraints on releases for energy generation. Finally, in the absence of additional data that
could be used to systematically evaluate alternative configurations or modifications to the dam structure,
these factors are excluded from the present study.

 2. METHODS

2.1 SELECTING NPDS FOR ANALYSIS

The starting point for this analysis is dams with an estimated potential power capacity greater than
100 kW, based on information from the previous CONUS-scale resource assessment [11] (Table 1). This
assessment used average monthly runoff and drainage areas to estimate available inflow at more than
54,000 dams and then estimated hydraulic head from the reported dam height. An initial subset of 3,299
dams was found to have 100kW of potential capacity (Figure 6). Initial dam locations were based on the
USACE NID, updated in February 2024 [15]. Some locations from the NID were updated if they had
been corrected through prior data quality improvement procedures, as documented in Hadjerioua et al.
[11], or if corrections could be made based on OpenStreetMaps and matching to flowlines.

Figure 6. NPDs evaluated in the previous CONUS-scale assessment [11]. Dams with estimated potential power
capacity of at least 100 kW are shown in blue and serve as the initial subset of dams considered in this evaluation.

Geographic Information System (GIS) tools were used to match dams to the nearest flowline in the NHD
river network, medium resolution, NHDPlusV2 [16] and ensure that only “on-network” dams were
included. A series of quality checks were performed to remove dams that are secondary structures (e.g.,
duplicated in the NID or a supporting structure other than a main dam) or that cannot be modeled using
the NHD river network flowlines. These cases are summarized in Figure 7. The checks included the
following:

• Filtering out dams greater than 100 m from a flowline in the NHDPlusV2. Many of these are water
 treatment facilities, retention ponds, debris or emergency flow control structures. Some locations

 9

 could be manually corrected by matching names or other details from the NID to features in
 OpenStreetMaps.

• Identifying and removing dams with names that include phrases such as “sewage lagoon” or
 “retention pond.”

• Comparing flow estimated in 2012 and the average annual flow associated with the nearest NHD
 flowline. If the difference in flow is more than an order of magnitude, the dam was flagged for
 manual verification of the location and NHD flowline match. This helped identify “off-network” and
 secondary structures that were not identified through other checks.

Following initial GIS processing and location corrections, dams were compared against several dam and
power plant databases to determine whether there was a change in status or removal. The American
Rivers Dam Removal Database [17] catalogs dams in the US that have been removed, providing
approximate locations and, in some cases, identifiers that reference the NID. The Existing Hydropower
Assets Plant Database [3] and US Hydropower Development Pipeline Dataset [18] also catalog dams that
have either already been developed or are in the process of being developed—from permitting to
construction. Finally, all dams were indexed to the most recent version of the NID to ensure that the
current dam identifier and most up-to-date information about the dam dimensions were used. A summary
of dams cross-identified in these datasets (or that could no longer be indexed) is given in Table 2; a total
of 2,670 dams could be assessed for technical capacity using daily streamflow and estimated hydraulic
head information.

2.2 KEY INPUTS FOR ESTIMATING GENERATION, CAPACITY, AND CAPACITY
 FACTOR

2.3 HYDRAULIC HEAD

The calculation of hydraulic head is critical in accurately estimating the generating potential of a
hydropower dam. Hydraulic head represents the available energy in the water due to the difference in
elevation behind the dam and the tailwater (Figure 8). Headwater elevation refers to the water level in the
river or reservoir behind the dam, whereas tailwater elevation refers to the water level below the dam.
Hydraulic head is calculated as the difference between the headwater and tailwater elevations; as one of
the key variables of the hydroelectric power equation, higher head results in greater potential energy.

Figure 8. Simplified side view of a dam illustrating the relationship between dam height and hydraulic head.

2.3.1 Headwater Elevation Data

In this analysis, the key datasets used for calculating headwater elevations were the NID [15], ResOpsUS
[19], and DAHITI [20].

• NID

 11

 o The National Inventory of Dams is a collection of dam attributes that span physical characteristics
 (e.g., dimensions, materials, condition, etc.) and sociopolitical aspects (e.g., ownership,
 regulatory authority, etc.). Coordinates of the dams are provided by a variety of state and federal
 agencies, though some coordinates were updated when the dams were linked to the NHDPlusV2.

• ResOpsUS
 o The ResOpsUS database is a standardized, multi-agency collection of historical reservoir
 observations and estimates that are relevant to reservoir operations across the CONUS. This
 dataset is valuable for watershed studies, national-scale hydrologic simulations, and analyzing the
 impacts of human systems and the natural environment. ResOpsUS provides historical time series
 for 679 major dams across the CONUS and 9 dams in Canada, covering 87% of US reservoirs
 with storage capacities greater than 1000 million cubic meters (MCM). Included parameters and
 temporal extents vary from dam to dam; daily records are available for elevation (506 dams),
 storage (653 dams), and outflow (519 dams), spanning 1930 to 2020, and the most complete
 coverage is available for the period between 1980 and 2020.

• DAHITI
 o DAHITI contains time series of water level based on multi-mission-satellite altimetry from
 Envisat, ERS-2, Jason-1, Jason-2, TOPEX/Poseidon, and SARAL/AltiKa. These estimates are
 derived for inland water bodies, including lakes and reservoirs, as well as rivers and wetlands. For
 most cases, DAHITI provides more accurate water-level information compared to other available
 altimeter databases; validation of results showed a root mean square error difference between
 DAHITI estimates and in situ gauge data of 4–36 cm for lakes and 8–114 cm for rivers [20]. The
 compilation of satellite-derived water level estimates is valuable for hydrological studies and
 understanding variability in reservoir operations, especially in areas where in situ measurements
 are scarce or unavailable. In total, there are over 10,000 water level time series that are distributed
 over all continents, of which 8% (n=836) are located in the US.

 o Of the 836 targets available for the US, only 33 targets are accessible through the API for
 extracting the water levels. Time series were aggregated to determine monthly average waterbody
 elevations and then spatially joined to NPDs (based on coordinates reported in the NID) using a
 GIS to determine the nearest neighbor. Initially, a 50-mile threshold was applied to accommodate
 potential discrepancies between DAHITI coordinates and actual dam locations.

• 3DEP
 o The 3D Elevation Program (3DEP) data describes elevation at a high-resolution (1 m and 10 m,
 depending on the location) across the US and territories. 3DEP provides three-dimensional
 representations of both natural topography and built infrastructure (including buildings and dams)
 using lidar technology to create detailed point cloud renderings of various landscapes and
 structures. Based on comparisons to survey control points, 3DEP data have relatively high
 vertical accuracy, with a root mean square error of 0.35 m [21].

To analyze historical reservoir elevation at specific NPDs, which are referenced to the NID, dam features
were linked to the NID using GIS and spatial joins to the nearest NID feature. Each spatial join was
validated against the World Imagery Base layer to ensure that only accurate associations between
waterbodies and dams remained. After this rigorous quality control, the refined ResOpsUS and DAHITI
datasets were seamlessly merged with the NPD dataset to create a new, unified dataset for further
analysis.

Both ResOpsUS and DAHITI describe reservoir conditions on a daily scale; these data were aggregated
to yield a monthly average for each waterbody. For ResOpsUS, values in the upper and lower extremes

 12

(1st percentile and 99th percentile) were excluded to help remove the influence of errors in the time series.
For example, ResOpsUS contains some records with elevation or storage that are orders of magnitude
greater than the average, which is likely an artifact of data entry error. Similar issues were not detected in
the DAHITI time series, so no additional data filtering was performed. As highlighted in Section 1.2,
previous studies relied on assumed relationships between dam dimensions and hydraulic head. However,
hydraulic head varies seasonally, especially in reservoirs that have significant drawdown for flood storage
or water withdrawals for irrigation and consumption.

Tailwater elevations were derived using information from the 3DEP Digital Elevation Model (DEM) data
[22]. Monthly hydraulic head values were calculated by subtracting the 3DEP outflow elevation from the
reservoir headwater elevation (Figure 9). The resulting hydraulic head calculations were then integrated

2.3.3 Modeling Hydraulic Head at Locations without Historical Elevation Data

The majority of NPDs lack historical reservoir elevation data in the publicly available datasets
(ResOpsUS, DAHITI). Previous assessments have assumed a relationship between hydraulic head and
dam dimensions in which the average hydraulic head could be approximated as the minimum value of
either the hydraulic height (difference between headwater elevation and the river bottom) or 70% dam
height (difference in elevation between the top of the dam and the base of the dam). For these locations
without historical reservoir elevation, monthly scaling factors were derived based on the reported dam
dimensions and primary purpose of the dam.

2.4 STREAMFLOW

For hydropower development at existing NPDs, releases and storage are generally constrained by the
current operational objectives of the dam. For example, a dam built for flood control purposes will be
required to draw down reservoir levels prior to winter melt and spring runoff. Reservoirs that supply
irrigation or municipal water will need to maintain certain storage volumes, and releases for generation
purposes would be required to avoid disrupting reservoir recreation (i.e., access to boat ramps) or
downstream fishing and boating. Therefore, these sites will not be governed solely by the water available
at the dam, but also by the flows that are used to meet the current reservoir and dam services.

Ideally, there are gauged records of streamflow directly downstream of an individual dam that reflect
current operations and constraints on flow. Additionally, typical pre-feasibility assessments for
prospective hydropower sites use long-term flow records that span a range of hydroclimatic conditions
including “wet” and “dry” years. This is critical for understanding the variability of water availability and
release regimes. For this assessment, long-term streamflow data were obtained from two sources:
historical streamflow gauge records and modeled streamflow. The sources of data used for individual
dams depend on the availability and completeness of records and are described in the following sections.
These records provide a significant improvement over using average monthly runoff that is scaled to the
drainage area of the dam site by representing the historical variability in hydroclimatic conditions. It also
allows a flexible approach that maximizes the number of dams that are assessed; streamflow gauge
records are used when available and modeled streamflow records are used at remaining sites.

 14

2.4.1 Gauged Streamflow

Following initial GIS processing to spatially join dams to the nearest NHDPlusV2 river network flowline,
the NHDtools package [23] in R [24] was used to find the nearest downstream USGS gauge with
historical daily streamflow data. These streamflow gauging stations are maintained through partnerships
between the USGS and a variety of federal, tribal, state, and private entities. Roughly 8,500 gauges
provide real-time (and historical) streamflow records, and an additional 1,700 gauges track stage or level
[25]. Streamflow records were obtained using the DataRetrieval R package [26].

To ensure that the flow records adequately describe the range of hydrologic conditions, gauge records
were used only for those dams with 80% of data available for the period of 1980–2015 and for which
streamflow observations were reported in every month of the year. Missing values were excluded from
further analysis (i.e., no estimate of generation was calculated for a date without a streamflow
observation) for several reasons. First, the autocorrelation of streamflow records downstream of dams
varies significantly from one location to another, so interpolating missing data or simply substituting with
an average value may not be appropriate for all sites. Secondly, the final results are summarized as
average monthly generation (averaged by month across all years) and annual generation to represent
seasonal variability.

2.4.2 Modeled Streamflow

Most dams do not have stream gauges directly downstream. Therefore, a model is required to simulate
long-term daily records of flow and approximate water available for generation at ungauged locations. To
provide CONUS-wide coverage of streamflow at these ungauged NPDs, time series data were obtained
from the Dayflow dataset, which contains daily streamflow estimates from 1980 to 2015 [14]. The dataset
was generated using daily meteorological forcing inputs to drive the Variable Infiltration Capacity (VIC)
hydrologic model. The resulting runoff was then routed through the NHDPlusV2 river network using a
coupled RAPID routing and data assimilation technique. At stream reaches where USGS stream gauge
records were available, observational data were substituted and the RAPID routing scheme continued to
pass the corrected flow through the network. Streamflow was not simulated on flowlines designated in the
NHD as “artificial paths,” but the majority of the 2.7 million stream reaches in the dataset are included for
a consistent 36-year record. Although complex interactions such as reservoir operations or withdrawals
are not explicitly represented, the data assimilation (substitution of observational data) does reflect some
human alteration to flow at locations downstream of dams or river regulation sites.

2.5 MODELING GENERATION, CAPACITY, AND CAPACITY FACTOR

The primary inputs for modeling potential generation are hydraulic head and streamflow records.
HydroGenerate is an open-source Python library that estimates hydropower generation based on those
inputs for different types of hydropower applications: impoundments, diversions, and hydrokinetic [27].
Figure 11 illustrates the flow of data, various pre-processing steps (described in detail in Sections 2.3 and
2.4), and the key intermediate calculations and outputs of the HydroGenerate modeling approach.

Figure 11. Inputs, processing steps, and outputs of the HydroGenerate tool [27], which calculates a time series

The first calculations in HydroGenerate determine the design flow and nameplate capacity. The design
flow of a hydropower site is the maximum flow at which the construction of the plant would be
cost-effective. The 30th percent exceedance flow, or Q30, from the available flow time series has
commonly been used to estimate the capacity of a given waterway. For example, USBR applied a design
flow of Q30 to assess potential at all USBR-owned facilities [10]. The Q30 was estimated based on the
entire streamflow record from 1980 to 2015, either from a stream gauge or from the Dayflow modeled
streamflow dataset. The Q30 is used to determine the nominal capacity, ensuring the design flow is
consistent across the entire period of simulation. This process was followed for all the dams using Q30 as
the criteria to determine the design flow. It should be noted that other criteria (e.g., Q20) may be used to
size turbines and design the power plant, depending on operational preferences and specific technologies.

This process to estimate power was repeated for each time-step in the flow record (daily). With the

divided by the nameplate capacity. To compute the annual capacity factor, the daily capacity factor is
averaged across the calendar year.

As mentioned in Section 1.2, flow constraints are important for accurately reflecting limitations on the
water available for generation. Although the HydroGenerate tool can apply minimum flow constraints,
this additional feature was not explored in the present analysis due to a lack of consistent data for the
selected subset of NPDs. However, on the upper end of the flow, a default constraint is applied based on
the selected design flow. It is possible to specify a set of flow constraints that mimics reservoir
operational requirements for non-hydropower purposes (e.g., minimum releases for environmental
purposes, flood mitigation, or storage requirements for water supply). In the absence of these constraints
(and if modeled streamflow is used rather than observed downstream flows, which already describe flow
constraints), the resulting generation represents the maximum technical potential.

3.2.1 National and Regional Potential

The total technical potential at the subset of 2,616 NPDs with sufficient data to support daily-scale
analysis is estimated to be approximately 4 GW, and individual power capacity estimates range from

3.3 COMPARISON OF CAPACITY AND CAPACITY FACTOR TO EXISTING
 HYDROPOWER FACILITIES

The installed capacity of operational conventional hydropower (i.e., not pumped storage) is greater than
the estimated technical capacity at many of the NPDs in this study, with a mean of nearly 36 MW
(compared to 1.5 MW). However, this average is skewed by a small number of facilities with very large
capacity, as nearly 40% of the operational capacity is less than 1.5 MW. The range of estimated technical
capacity also mirrors the spread of capacities that have been installed at NPDs in the past two decades
(minimum of 37 kW, maximum of 105 MW at NPD retrofits between 2000 and 2020) [29]. There are
nearly 1,500 NPDs (with a combined total capacity of 2.7 MW) located in sub-basins that do not yet have
any hydropower. These NPDs represent a significant opportunity for growth and extension of hydropower
in new areas.

The median capacity factor for NPDs in this assessment is 40%. Individual capacity factor varies
geographically, and there are substantial differences even between nearby facilities (Figure 21). Historical
records of generation for existing hydropower dams in the same sub-basins were obtained from the
RectifHyd dataset [30]. With coverage from 2001 to 2022, this information was coupled with installed
capacity reported in the Existing Hydropower Assets dataset [3] to obtain the capacity factor over this
time period. When compared to the capacity factors of existing hydropower dams, those for the NPDs in
this assessment are generally much higher. This may represent an opportunity to explore different options
for sizing criteria that would impact the design or nameplate capacity. For example, higher nameplate
capacity would result in lower capacity factor values, given the same flow rates. It can also reflect the
need to consider additional constraints on flow that were not represented in the present analysis.

3.4 CONTEXT OF OPPORTUNITIES FOR HYDROPOWER DEVELOPMENT AT OTHER
 EXISTING WATER INFRASTRUCTURE

This assessment’s findings suggest a wide variety of opportunities for development at NPDs in the US.
While the majority of recent new capacity has come from retrofitting NPDs (505 MW from 2010 to 2022
[2]), there is a greater number of smaller projects utilizing conduits. These projects include agricultural
canals and municipal water conveyance systems, but they may also utilize bypasses at existing dams. In
the same period of 2010–2022, 89 conduit projects were completed that added 140 MW of capacity [2].

According to a recent national-scale assessment, the potential increase in capacity at conduit sites is 1.41
GW (0.662 MW at sites in agricultural canals, 0.378 MW at industrial sites, and 0.374 MW at municipal
sites) [31]. The scale of development opportunity at NPDs varies more widely than these conduit projects;
power capacity at individual dams spans several orders of magnitude. This range in project scale poses a
greater challenge for generalization of technologies and approaches for retrofitting than other types of
projects, such as in-pipe applications in water conveyance systems, that tend to be more similar in their
size and configuration.

Other additions to capacity have come from upgrades to existing hydropower facilities. From 2010 to
2022, refurbishment and modernization of existing plants resulted in a net increase of 1.4 GW [2].

 4. FUTURE RESEARCH NEEDS

4.1 EVALUATE TIES TO OTHER ENERGY INFRASTRUCTURE

In addition to the physical and operational constraints that are most critical in estimating generation or
capacity at a dam, other factors play a role in siting and development. Once development is determined to
be feasible based on these basic criteria, the dam must also make sense from an interconnection
perspective. This means that the dam’s proximity to substations and transmission lines is extremely
important; any length of new transmission lines or new substations incurs additional costs and could pose
challenges for siting as the infrastructure could have its own environmental impacts that must be
accounted for. Another consideration for development is the role that additional power capacity or storage
capabilities could play in connection with other energy sources or with batteries during the
interconnection process. Viewing hydropower as one piece of a generation–storage system, could provide
benefits from a cost and reliability perspective. However, variable flow and constraints on storage from
existing operations at NPDs may limit the scale at which NPD hybrids are feasible [32].

Other energy infrastructure-related details that are critical to a national-scale understanding of site
suitability for development include locations of non-hydropower plants and their proximity to significant
load off-takers. Additionally, the “energyshed,” or geographic boundary describing where the electricity
is distributed from a given source, could be helpful in understanding interconnections and the needs of the
grid. Evaluating connections to these other energy infrastructure parameters requires geospatial data
analysis and nationally consistent information. Methods for assessing proximity are straightforward (e.g.,
least cost path analysis to determine the distance to the nearest transmission line), but some data may be
difficult to obtain due to infrastructure security.

4.2 EXPAND TO ALASKA AND HAWAII

The current assessment is limited to CONUS and does not include any assessment of infrastructure in
either Alaska or Hawaii. Past assessments focused on dams in Alaska and Hawaii used a variety of input
data and methods that are not consistent with those applied in the present study or other recent CONUS
assessments [33, 34]. To improve consistency in estimates and achieve a more comprehensive
understanding of resources in the US, similar modeling efforts—including the use of more complete
infrastructure inventories, detailed hydrologic modeling at the daily scale, representations of dynamic
hydraulic head, and simulations of generation under a variety of technical configurations—are needed.

Specific motivation for extending the resource assessment to Alaska and Hawaii includes the high
dependence on oil and the cost of electricity in those states [33-35]. The environments and infrastructure
systems of Alaska and Hawaii are also unique relative to other regions within CONUS. Alaska has many
rural and remote communities, rugged topography, and harsh weather conditions. Hawaii also has unique
terrain, geology, and ecology that factor into the types of existing dams and reservoir sites. Additionally,
the hydrologic processes in these states are distinct from other regions in CONUS: glacial and permafrost
conditions dominate hydrology in Alaska, and highly variable precipitation and volcanic geology
influence Hawaiian hydrology.

4.3 ESTIMATE POTENTIAL GIVEN CHANGING HYDROLOGIC CONDITIONS

The simulation of generation at each NPD over a long (36-year) period accounts for hydrologic variability
and therefore different levels of water availability for generation. Several studies have noted the impacts
of changing climate and climate variability on the current hydropower fleet. Globally, impacts are likely
to be regionally variable: some regions are projected to experience increases in precipitation and a
corresponding increase in generation, whereas other areas may see a decrease [36]. In the US, decreases

 29

in generation were localized even during a widespread regional prolonged drought; a combination of the
local climate, storage capacities, and operations meant that some facilities experienced curtailments, and
others were minimally affected [37, 38].

The variability in local and regional climate conditions expected in the future highlights the importance of
future projections of streamflow (i.e., water availability for generation). Accurate representations of the
physical system from the watershed to the river network are important, but these projections must also be
robust in considering the uncertainty of future policies and actions that might lead to different climate
outcomes. Recent research funded through the 9505 SECURE Water Act has led to CONUS-scale multi-
model streamflow records [39]. Ensembles of future projected streamflow reflect a variety of models and
scenarios that simulate how climate processes would be expected to play out under different amounts of
radiative forcing. These time series are crucial in understanding the range of potential for NPDs given
uncertainties in future water availability. Given the timeline of development and long-term investment in
hydropower projects, continued assessments should explore how projected hydrologic changes would
impact the potential generation patterns.

4.4 CONSTRAINTS AND PLANT CONFIGURATION

Many additional considerations could be factored into the estimation of generation. Most notably, no flow
constraints or modifications to the dam were factored into the generation modeling process in this work,
other than maximum flows being capped by the design flow of a site. It is expected that constraints
required to meet environmental regulations or other operational objectives (e.g., minimum flow,
maintenance) would be critical limiting factors to the actual storage and releases that would be possible at
any of these NPDs.

Additionally, the simple modeling configurations assumed for this modeling exercise were limited to a
single turbine, penstock, and generator. In reality, there could be a variety of plant setups that could have
higher technical generation potential or a higher financial viability. Future modeling efforts should
explore alternatives for conveyance/placement of penstocks (e.g., using a bypass). Possible alternatives
for turbine configuration include combinations of turbines with different sizes or using variable speed
turbines that have greater range in generation, even at low flows. This could potentially enable generation
over a larger range of release rates, thus increasing the generation potential. HydroGenerate may be used
to explore possible alternatives at individual dams, for example, optimizing the design flow for two
turbines instead of one, based on maximizing both profit and discharge targets. Alternatives for water
conveyance could also be considered. Longer penstocks may be able to achieve greater hydraulic head
than placement of the powerhouse directly at the dam toe. Different scenarios of generation potential
could be explored by coupling generation modeling with a GIS-based path for penstock placement and
cost models due to the large influence of conveyance length on project cost [40].

4.5 ADDITIONAL CONSIDERATIONS FOR DEVELOPMENT

A variety of other factors contribute to the development decisions at NPDs. Some of these factors are
related to the site-specific conditions and characteristics of the dams, and others are related to external
and connected systems. These factors determine whether hydropower development at an NPD is
technically and economically feasible as well as whether the project can be realized. They may play a role
in the planning and installation stages, influencing social perceptions and support for a given project as
well as design and installation strategies. A list of other considerations follows.

• Limitations of aging infrastructure and design constraints. More than 90% of the NPDs analyzed
 in the present study report year of original construction in the NID [15]. Approximately one quarter of
 these dams are more than 100 years old. These tend to be smaller facilities, with a total of 0.3 GW

 30

 and median potential power capacity of about 90 kW. Still, the median age reported at dams with
 > 1 MW of potential power capacity is 75 years. Older infrastructure and design strategies that did not
 consider hydropower generation may pose challenges for major retrofitting or modification of the site
 to include new conveyance systems or add a power plant.

• Changes in storage and operations. Along with concerns about the structural condition and
 constraints that may exist at an older NPD, there may also be challenges posed by reduction in
 storage that naturally occurs over the life of an impoundment. Reservoirs will trap sediment with
 varying levels of efficiency. Dams lose some of their storage capacity each year due to sedimentation,
 with estimates for losses at reservoirs with at least 1,000 acre-ft of storage ranging from 0.02-
 0.8%/year [41, 42]. Older existing dams may have less capacity for water storage than is reported in
 national inventories and face additional flow or storage requirements as they address sediment
 trapping issues.

• Community needs, energy markets, and policy incentives. The energy and water demands of local
 communities, including those who derive benefits from the reservoir and water control infrastructure,
 will influence whether there is motivation for development of an NPD. There must also be
 coordination between asset owner and developers to ensure that existing demands are met and that a
 particular dam will find sufficient energy off-takers.

• Costs for installation and operations. Analysis of costs from existing and planned projects has
 enabled development of empirical models for capital expenditures based on the estimated capacity
 and simple design characteristics of the project (i.e., head, flow, number of units, and water
 conveyance length) [40]. Improvements to cost estimates could be augmented with the more detailed
 and dynamic representation of flow and hydraulic head. Existing national infrastructure inventories
 could also be enhanced with detailed site and layout information (e.g., conveyance length and units)
 that is related to facility operation.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

Development potential at NPDs depends on many factors and requires coordination and support from a
variety of entities to be feasible. This study summarizes the technical potential—what is possible to
generate based on available flow, hydraulic head, and a simplified representation of a generation
scheme—for a subset of CONUS NPDs. The 4 GW of total capacity that was estimated at these dams is
spread throughout the US. The size and scale of generation opportunity also vary, meaning there could be
a variety of suitable applications and power off-takers who might benefit from adding generation
capabilities to these dams. In other words, there may be circumstances where NPDs are more feasible for
development despite having lower capacity or potential generation than other sites. While many NPDs
share the common requirement to maintain or improve existing functions of the dam, a variety of
techniques may be appropriate [43], and each site will require detailed analysis to determine the unique
considerations that are relevant to the design, installation, and operation of new power generation
facilities.

Emerging research into the valuation of retrofitting NPDs points to a variety of benefits beyond revenue
from the sale of electricity. For example, as communities face vulnerabilities to grid outages from natural
disasters, NPDs can offer greater resilience and alternative energy sources [44]. A recent catalog of value
streams from powering NPDs demonstrates other benefits, ranging from providing services to the grid
(e.g., black start capabilities, reserves, etc.) to improved conditions for local property owners and visitors
(e.g., greater energy sovereignty, increased property values, and recreational opportunities) [45]. A more
robust and informed understanding of the technical potential and benefits of power production alongside
these other benefits will enable more efficient decisions and planning for NPD development in the future.
