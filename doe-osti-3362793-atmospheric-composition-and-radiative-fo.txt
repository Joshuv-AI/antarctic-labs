1.0 Summary
 Aerosols, or atmospheric particulates, are long known to be able to seed clouds and modify their
 characteristics (e.g., cloud brightness, coverage, lifetime). However, the impact of aerosols on clouds is
 highly complex and non-linear. As a result, aerosol-cloud interaction remains the largest uncertainty in
 our understanding of the Earth’s radiative balance and in any future climate projections. Aerosols are also
 a principal component of air pollution and are known to adversely affect respiratory and cardiovascular
 health.

 Ships traditionally burn dirty, high-sulfur fuel and emit large quantities of sulfur and carbon-containing
 gases and aerosols. Due to concerns about air quality in coastal regions, the United Nation’s International
 Maritime Organisation (IMO) mandated that in 2015, ships within Sulfur Emission Control Areas
 (SECAs), such as the English Channel in Europe, needed to drastically reduce their emissions of sulfur
 (primarily in the form of sulfur dioxide gas). A follow-up regulation extends a similar sulfur control to
 ships in the open ocean in 2020. In the atmosphere, sulfur dioxide (SO2 ) is oxidized to form sulfate – a
 main component of marine aerosols. We note also that there are other sources of sulfate aerosols,
 including oxidations of the marine biogenic dimethyl sulfide gas (DMS), volcanic emissions, and sulfate
 in sea spray.

 As a part of the ACRUISE project, a high-volume aerosol collector was installed at the U.S. Department
 of Energy Atmospheric Radiation (ARM) User Facility observatory on Graciosa Island, Azores in the
 spring of 2019. The intention was to collect ~24 hour aerosol samples on a ~weekly basis over multiple
 years and analyze the aerosol composition. An impactor was used to only selectively collect the
 submicron fraction on a cellulose filter, and the aerosol filters were stored in -20 deg C freezer before
 analysis. The analytes of special interest include sulfate and sodium from ion chromatography (IC), as
 well as the sulfur isotopes in sulfate based on mass spectrometry. Sea spray aerosol contains essentially
 the same sulfate:sodium ratio as bulk seawater, and so we can subtract the sea salt contribution of sulfate
 based on sodium from the measured sulfate concentration to derive ‘non-sea salt sulfate (nss-sulfate).’
 Assuming that DMS and volcanic emissions do not change substantially from pre- to post-2020, it was
 hypothesized that a decrease in nss-sulfate concentration due to the shipping regulation may be observed.
 It was further hoped that the sulfur isotope analysis can help to tease out the contributions to sulfate from
 shipping and DMS oxidation.

 Thanks to hard work and support from staff on site (especially Bruno Cunha), a large number of aerosol
 samples were successfully collected from about May 2019 to the beginning of 2024, with some
 unavoidable gaps mostly due to instrument failure (e.g., overheating). However, because of loss of
 multiple staff from Plymouth Marine Laboratory related to the COVID-19 pandemic as well as recent
 redundancies and instrument issues in Plymouth, the IC analysis of these aerosol samples was severely
 delayed. Consequently, we have no analyzed IC results to report so far. The sulfur isotope analysis has
 not been started yet, but the samples are stored in the freezer and will hopefully be analyzed in the near
 future.
