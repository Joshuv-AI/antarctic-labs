Executive Summary of CRADA Work:
This research adds to the understanding of building energy management by fundamentally
challenging the long-standing paradigm of controlling an environmental parameter—air
temperature—and assuming occupant comfort will follow. The project developed and demonstrated
a novel approach that directly senses physiological indicators of human comfort to enable closed-
loop, human-centric control of heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems. This work
shifts the focus from open-loop environmental control to a closed-loop system that prioritizes the
occupants' actual thermal state. The primary benefit to the public is the potential for a simultaneous
improvement in the quality of indoor environments, where people spend most of their time, and a
significant reduction in national energy consumption. Overcooling, or the overuse of cooling energy
in a way that discomforts occupants, wastes approximately 0.5 Quads of primary energy annually in
U.S. commercial buildings and creates unproductive indoor environments.

The technical effectiveness of the developed methods was validated through a novel sensor-
controller, nicknamed "Goldilocks," which uses a combination of thermal-infrared (TIR) and color
cameras with machine vision to passively and privately measure occupant skin temperature profiles.
A five-month field study in a commercial office building near Houston, Texas, demonstrated that the
system, using a refined control strategy, reduced the air conditioning (AC) cooling load by 42%
when the room was occupied compared to conventional control, while simultaneously improving the
fraction of "comfortable" occupant votes by 15 percentage points. The system was designed with
low-cost, modern components, such as uncooled microbolometer sensors and open-source machine
vision software, to ensure a viable path to market.

This project benefits the public by establishing a direct and accelerated path for technology transfer
from a federally funded research concept to a viable commercial product. The Cooperative Research
and Development Agreement (CRADA) with Daikin U.S., the world’s largest manufacturer of air
conditioning systems, was central to this effort. Daikin U.S. consulted on the system’s design and led
efforts to estimate its final cost, payback time, and market demand. This collaboration, coupled with
a provisional patent application filed by Berkeley Lab, positions the technology for widespread
adoption, ensuring that its energy-saving and comfort- improving potential can be realized at scale.

Summary of Research Results:

This summary details project activities for the entire period of performance, comparing the actual
accomplishments with the project's goals and objectives as defined in the Statement of Work (SOW).
The central hypothesis was that a closed-loop HVAC sensor-controller, using radiatively detected
occupant skin temperatures, could reduce commercial-building overcooling by at least 50% while
simultaneously improving occupant comfort. The project was structured into four primary tasks: (1)
Sensing, (2) Comfort, (3) Control, and (4) Integration and Demonstration. The project successfully
developed and validated a prototype system that approached its ambitious energy-saving goal and
significantly enhanced occupant comfort, as detailed in the project's final report.

Task 1: Radiative Sensing of Occupant and Room Surface Temperatures
The objective of this task was to create a sensor capable of mapping occupant skin surface
temperatures. The approach involved developing the "Goldilocks" system, which integrates a pan-tilt
platform carrying narrow-view color and TIR cameras with a wide-view stationary camera and
passive temperature references (PTRs) for calibration. A key problem encountered was the inherent
measurement instability of uncooled TIR cameras, which are subject to temporal drift; experiments
showed that absolute temperature measurements could drift by as much as 0.6 °C over 15 minutes
without correction. A pivotal finding, which had a significant positive impact on the project results,
was that differential temperature measurements—such as the temperature difference between two
points on a person's face—remained remarkably stable (to within 0.1 °C) and were largely immune
to this drift. This discovery provided a robust measurement technique for the subsequent comfort
and control tasks. Another challenge was identifying body parts in low-resolution thermal images.
The team overcame this by developing a machine vision pipeline that uses the open-source
MediaPipe Holistic library to identify features in a high-resolution color image and then maps these
features onto the registered thermal image using a pose-keypoint-based homography transformation,
enabling accurate temperature extraction.

Task 2: Predicting Occupant Thermal Comfort

This task aimed to develop an experimentally validated logic to predict comfort from the sensed skin
temperatures. The approach involved extensive human-subject trials where 34 participants were
exposed to a range of thermal environments while their skin temperatures and thermal sensation
votes were recorded. Using this dataset, machine learning models (random-forest classifiers) were
trained to predict a simplified thermal sensation state (“cool,” “neutral,” or “warm”) from various
skin temperature statistics. The success of this comfort model was built directly upon the robust
sensing method developed in Task 1. By using stable differential temperature measurements as
inputs, the resulting comfort prediction algorithm is inherently more reliable for real-world control
applications than a model based solely on less stable absolute temperature readings.

Task 3: HVAC Predictive Control

This task involved a notable departure from the planned methodology. The SOW originally proposed
the development of a complex Model Predictive Control (MPC) algorithm. However, the project
team ultimately implemented a more practical and robust discrete-time Proportional- Integral (PI)
control logic. This pragmatic shift was assessed to have a positive impact on the project's path to
application, as it prioritized a solution that is widely understood and easily deployable in the HVAC
industry. The theoretical benefits of a more complex MPC algorithm were likely rendered

unnecessary by the high-quality, real-time comfort feedback provided by the sensor. The PI
controller uses the probability-weighted simplified sensation value from the comfort model as its
input, targeting a sensation value of 0.5 (on a scale from -1 to +1) to balance comfort and energy use
by adjusting the thermostat setpoint within a predefined range of 70 to 78 °F.

Task 4: Integration and Demonstration of Prototype Sensor/Controlling System

 In this final task, the sensor hardware, comfort models, and PI control logic were integrated into the
complete "Goldilocks" prototype system. Daikin U.S. participated in this task, leading the effort to
estimate the final cost, payback time, and market demand for the system. The prototype's
performance was assessed in a five-month field study in a conference room in an office building near
Houston, TX. Three control strategies were compared: “Conventional Control” (fixed 72.0 °F setpoint),
“TIR A Control” (our system with an aggressive 80 °F unoccupied setpoint), and “TIR B Control” (our
system with a more moderate 76 °F unoccupied setpoint). The TIR B strategy proved highly successful.
It reduced the occupied cooling load by 42% compared to Conventional Control while increasing the
percentage of "comfortable" votes from 61% to 76%.

Products and Technology Transfer

The CRADA resulted in several products, applications, and technology transfer activities:

 • Networks or collaborations fostered: The project was built on the collaborative research
 and development partnership between Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and Daikin
 U.S. Corporation under this CRADA.

 • Technologies/techniques/methodologies: The primary technology developed is the
 "Goldilocks" prototype sensor-controller, an integrated instrument for closed-loop, comfort-
 based HVAC control. Key methodologies developed include the machine vision pipeline for
 mapping features from color to thermal images and the use of stable differential TIR
 measurements to overcome sensor drift and enable robust comfort prediction.

 • Other products: As a direct result of this research, Berkeley Lab filed a provisional patent
 application for the technology in October 2023, representing a tangible step in transferring
 this federally funded research to the marketplace for public benefit.

In conclusion, the project successfully achieved its core objectives. The demonstrated 42% reduction
in occupied cooling load substantially approaches the ambitious 50% goal set in the SOW, while the
simultaneous 15-percentage-point improvement in occupant comfort validates the system's dual-
benefit approach. The collaboration with Daikin U.S. has effectively positioned the technology for
potential commercialization, fulfilling a key objective of the CRADA.
