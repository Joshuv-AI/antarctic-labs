Bioinspired pore engineering in two-dimensional covalent organic frameworks
Authors: Niyati Arora, Jessica Arcudia, Chamila S. Bandara, Katarzyna H. Drewniak, Sumon Chakrabarty, Rebecca M. Johnson, Noora Al-Kharji, G. Andrés Cisneros, Ronald A. Smaldone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Covalent organic frameworks (COFs) are permanently porous, molecularly engineered scaffolds that exemplify the intersection of design, chemistry, and function. They are composed of organic monomers stitched together by covalent bonds into highly ordered two- or three-dimensional architectures. The majority of the studies on two dimensional COFs have focused on eclipsed rather than antiparallel stacked structures. Drawing inspiration from non-covalent bonding motifs in biomolecules, we report the design and synthesis of robust two-dimensional (2D) COFs with amide side chains called aCOFamide-1 and -2, which mimic the hydrogen bonding motifs found in β-sheets. These linker-based sidechains adopt an antiparallel stacked configuration to create binding sites that display excellent iodine adsorption capability (5.9 g g−1 and 6.7 g g−1 for I2 in aCOFamide-1 and -2, respectively). The antiparallel stacked layers of aCOFamides form three-dimensional, multi-N, nanotraps, which can also bind and adsorb anions such as iodide (I−) in aqueous environments, a major challenge in molecular recognition. Computational electrostatic maps reveal localized regions of positive electrostatic potential near the amide functional groups in the aCOFamide, which enhances the iodide uptake up to 1.3 g g−1 and 1.7 g g−1 in aCOFamide-1 and -2, respectively. We also show that the crystalline nature of COFs plays a major role in adsorption capability, when compared to a porous organic polymer (POP) composed of monomers containing the same amide sidechains but is polymerized under kinetic control which produces an amorphous network with lower adsorption performance. These results highlight the potential of biomimetic design strategies in engineering COFs with exceptional host–guest performance and reusability.

Introduction

The structural and functional sophistication of biological macromolecules has long inspired the design of synthetic materials.1,2 Nature assembles intricate, dynamic architectures using interactions, such as hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, π–π interactions, metal coordination, and electrostatics, to build highly ordered secondary and tertiary structures. In many cases, the same noncovalent bonding interactions that produce ordered structural features such as α-helices and β-sheets also participate in ternary interactions like substrate binding or catalysis.3,4 Mimicking such interactions at the molecular level has become a foundation of supramolecular chemistry and material science and is a key challenge in the design of functional porous materials for selective molecular recognition and adsorption.

Covalent organic frameworks (COFs), a family of crystalline porous polymers, stand out as a platform where molecular tunability, structural predictability, and dynamic functionality can be combined to synthesize crystalline materials that resemble natural complexity with synthetic precision.5–7 2D-COFs are made up of covalently linked two-dimensional sheets that assemble through noncovalent interactions,8 usually dipolar or aromatic stacking, to form a crystalline structure. A wide range of topologies can be produced, from interwoven networks to extended two- and three-dimensional structures, enabling applications such as gas separation,9,10 energy storage,11,12 emission,13,14 catalysis,15,16 and water purification.17–23

Adsorption in porous frameworks is often driven by non-specific van der Waals or dipolar forces,24 however, in recent years framework design has become increasingly focused on using more specific recognition elements such as hydrogen bonding, metal coordination or macrocyclic receptors.25–29 For 2D-COFs in particular, incorporating hydrogen bonding moieties, such as ureas, imines, or amides, into the nodes or linkers allows the design of frameworks with directional and cooperative interlayer interactions that mirror those found in the secondary structures of biomolecules.30,31 When strategically positioned, these moieties promote antiparallel stacking of adjacent layers instead of conventional parallel stacking, wherein sheets adopt opposite orientations to maximize hydrogen bonding.32–35 This antiparallel arrangement enhances interlayer cohesion, and resistance to delamination while preserving pore accessibility for guest species.36,37 The emergence of such ordered architectures is closely tied to the distinct polymerization pathways that differentiate COFs from their amorphous counterparts, porous organic polymers (POPs)38,39 as shown in Fig. 1A and B.

(A) Highly ordered COF network (purple) and an amorphous network of a POP (green). (B) Comparison of hydrogen bonding angles in antiparallel stacked COF linkers (top) and parallel/antiparallel β-sheets (bottom).

POPs are generally formed under kinetic control, which limits structural reorganization and results in disordered frameworks with heterogeneous pore environments.38 By contrast, COFs are synthesized under thermodynamic control through reversible dynamic covalent chemistry, allowing for error correction during framework formation.40,41 This process yields crystalline materials with uniform pores and precisely aligned functional sites, directly translating to enhanced diffusion kinetics and selective guest recognition.42 Furthermore, mimicking biomolecular recognition mechanisms in COFs can drastically improve their affinity and selectivity for target species.43–45 In nature, this molecular recognition is empowered by the precise arrangement of hydrogen bond donors/acceptors and hydrophobic or aromatic moieties.

Transferring this principle into COF design, in which adjacent layers adopt an alternating head-to-tail orientation, enables cooperative hydrogen bonding between functional groups (e.g., amides or ureas)46,47 This antiparallel arrangement closely resembles the stabilizing interactions found in antiparallel β-sheets in proteins, as shown in Fig. 1B. Herein, we find that the N–H⋯O hydrogen bonds in our structures exhibit angles of 176.5° and 160.4° for aCOFamide-1 and -2, respectively, and O⋯H distances of 1.90 Å and 1.70 Å values that fall within the ranges reported for antiparallel β-sheets, where hydrogen bond angles range from 163.8° to 168.5°, and O⋯H distances from 1.88 Å to 1.95 Å.48 These structural parallels reinforce the analogy between the hydrogen bonding patterns in our antiparallel COFs and those observed in protein β-sheets. Our studies here show that this arrangement allows for the construction of pores that are geometrical and chemically complementary to specific analytes such as radioactive iodine, organic pollutants, or toxic heavy metals.49–51

Recently, more detailed experimental investigations and density functional theory (DFT) calculations provided support to the understanding that the stacking behavior and growth patterns of 2D COFs are not only governed by the thermodynamic landscape but also by the rotational freedom of intramolecular bonds within the building blocks34,35,52,53 as shown in Fig. 2A. Functionally, antiparallel stacking improves pore alignment and accessibility, leading to more efficient guest diffusion and stronger host–guest interactions. In the context of iodine capture, these features result in markedly improved uptake capacity and kinetics, as the stabilized, polar interlayer environment enhances both the retention and confinement of iodine species. Thus, the antiparallel architecture provides a structural and functional advantage over traditional parallel-stacked analogues, offering a generalizable strategy for improving the performance of layered COFs.

By bridging biomolecular design principles with crystalline framework chemistry, this study demonstrates a general strategy for improving both structural order and functional performance in COFs. The use of antiparallel hydrogen bonded stacking opens new avenues for the design of next-generation adsorbents where nature inspired interactions enable advanced function.54–56

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of aCOFamides 1–2

To address the factors affecting the stacking arrangement and guest recognition in COFs, unique antiparallel stacked COFs (aCOFamide-1 and -2) were synthesized. These imine-linked 2D COF derived from 1,3,5-tris(4-aminophenyl)-benzene (TAPB) were synthesized at 100 °C by condensing monomer 1 and 2 in presence of o-dichlorobenzene (1.9 mL), n-butanol (0.1 mL) with aqueous acetic acid used as a catalyst (6 M, 0.1 mL) and 1,4-dioxane (3.2 mL), mesitylene (0.8 mL) with scandium(iii) trifluoromethanesulfonate used as a catalyst (3.0 mg, 6 µmol) was added for 3 days to yield aCOFamide-1 and -2, respectively (Fig. 2B, Schemes S2 and S3) For both polymerizations, suspensions (bright yellow for aCOFamide-1; off white for aCOFamide-2) formed within 72 h. The solids were collected by filtration, washed by Soxhlet extraction using methanol for 12 h, and activated using a supercritical CO2 dryer. As a control, we have synthesized a 2D and 3D porous organic polymer (POPamide-1 and -2) containing similar functional groups as shown in Fig. 2C. Despite sharing identical functional groups, POPs have amorphous crosslinked networks. This disparity governs critical properties such as pore accessibility, diffusion dynamics, and the relative orientation of the amide groups to one another.

The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface areas of each COF and POP were measured by nitrogen adsorption with values of 1224 m2 g−1 and 1038 m2 g−1 for aCOFamide-1 and -2 respectively and 567 m2 g−1 and 200 m2 g−1 for POPamide-1 and -2 respectively as shown in Fig. 3A. The pore size distributions, determined using the Non-Localized Density Functional Theory NLDFT model, shows the random pore size distribution for POPamide-1 and -2 and narrow pore distributions for aCOFamide-1 and -2 as shown in Fig. 3B, which center around the predicted pore sizes calculated from computational models (Fig. 3C and D) of each aCOF. A discrepancy in the pore size of aCOFamide-2 can be elucidated primarily from the presence of conformationally flexible side chains appended to the framework linkers.

(B) Nonlocal density functional theory (NLDFT) pore size distributions of all aCOFamides and POPamide. (C) and (D) Crystal structure models of aCOFamide-1 and -2 with their predicted pore sizes. These side chains, though not directly involved in the rigid backbone structure, can occupy significant free volume within the pore channels due to their dynamic motion and steric hindrance. As a result, the effective pore size, measured via gas sorption appears smaller than the crystallographically predicted dimensions.57,58 Additionally, the nitrogen isotherms of aCOFamide-1 and -2 have shapes more closely resembling type IV isotherms, indicative of pore sizes >20 Å which is consistent with their mesoporous character. We next performed powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) measurement and carried out structural optimization via density functional theory (DFT) in tandem to verify that the synthesized COF adopts the designed structure (Fig. S2 and S3).

The simulated PXRDs of the antiparallel eclipsed structure of both COFs match well with our experimental data, indicating that aCOFamide-1 and -2 are arranged in an eclipsed fashion rather than staggered shown in Fig. 3E and F. The diffraction pattern of POPamide-1 and -2 (Fig. S1) does not display any major reflections, which is to be expected for an amorphous material. Relative energy analysis (Table S1) confirms the antiparallel eclipsed stacking as the most energetically favorable configuration for both aCOFs. The parallel eclipsed stacking lies 66.0 and 133.4 kcal per mol per two formula units higher in energy for aCOFamide-1 and -2, respectively, with all other configurations being even less favorable. Moreover, no other minima were found upon layer displacement (Fig. S4 and S5). It should be noted that these calculations were performed on idealized periodic models and therefore do not explicitly account for stacking disorder or mixed stacking domains that may exist in real materials. Pawley refinement revealed agreement with the experimentally measured PXRD pattern, revealing an antiparallel AA stacking geometry (Rp, 3.70% and Rwp, 4.81%) and (Rp, 3.89% and Rwp, 4.91%) for aCOFamide-1 and -2, respectively as shown in Fig. S2 and S3.

To further characterize the antiparallel COF arrangement, we performed a qualitative analysis of interlayer interactions by calculating the non-covalent interaction (NCI) index with NCIPLOT4.59,60 The results reveal more pronounced attractive interactions in the antiparallel configuration compared to the parallel one Fig. S6 and S7, consistent with the presence of stronger interlayer hydrogen bonding. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of POP amide and aCOFamide-1, -2 are shown in Fig. S8 and S9.

We subsequently investigated the structural components of aCOFamide-1, -2 and POPamide-1 and -2 using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR). The formation of imine linkages in aCOFamide-1 and -2 was confirmed by FT-IR through the attenuation of the aldehyde carbonyl stretching vibration (1693 cm−1) and the appearance of the imine (1632 cm−1) vibration, as shown in Fig. 4E and F and broad –NH peak was observed for both POPamide 1–2 likely because of the presence of multiple orientations of the amide N–H hydrogen bonding environments (Fig. 4H, S10 and S11). It is important to note that the node geometry does not produce any changes to the amorphous nature of the polymer, or the hydrogen bonding network. In other words, reticular design rules cannot be used to create ordered functional group relationships within the pores of the hypercrosslinked POPamides synthesized under kinetic control.

We synthesized monomer 1 and 2 for the COF synthesis due to the intermolecular hydrogen bonding involving the linker (N–H⋯O), which is expected to play a role in orienting the amide groups on the linkers, thereby favoring the formation of aCOFamide-1 and -2 to crystallize in the antiparallel AA stacking mode as shown in Fig. 4C and D. This preference was further supported by computational modeling of the vibrational behavior of the amide moiety in both parallel and antiparallel arrangements, which predicted specific vibrational modes depending on the stacking arrangement. The predicted vibrational modes were found to be consistent with in-depth experimental FT-IR studies of the aCOFamide materials. Both computational and experimental results show a red shift in the N–H stretching vibration of both aCOFamide-1 and -2 (3269 cm−1) as compared to the linker (3319 cm−1) which correlate to the shorter and stronger hydrogen bonding predicted when the COF sheets adopt an antiparallel orientation61,62 as depicted in Fig. 4E, F and S12. However, the computed shifts are small and within the expected uncertainty of the method, precluding a clear interpretation, whereas the larger N–H shift provides a more reliable qualitative indicator. In an antiparallel vibration, the dipole moments of the individual vibrating bonds are oriented in opposite directions as shown in Fig. 4A which leads to a peak shift towards lower wavenumber, rather than vibration occurring parallel to the principal axis of rotation of molecule as shown in Fig. 4B. This behavior may also be influenced by the hexylamide side chains at the meta positions of the linker, which can restrict intramolecular bond rotation via intermolecular hydrogen bonding.63,64 These observations are supported by studies of vibrational spectroscopy of biomacromolecules.65 Since antiparallel β-sheets have stronger, more linear hydrogen bonds than other types of arrangements (such as α-helices), the N–H stretching frequency is typically observed to red shift. This is in contrast with our previous reports66 of COFamide-1 and -2 which observed that the amide N–H vibration is blue shifted in comparison with the node monomer alone (Fig. 4G). Additionally, in our prior work67 our theoretical studies predicted that defects in the hydrogen bonding network could partially explain the excellent adsorption performance of iodine. We hypothesize that these defects could arise from a less optimal alignment of the amide hydrogen bonds which is supported experimentally by a blue shift of the N–H signals compared with their monomers.

Moreover, as shown in Fig. 5, the variable-temperature infrared (VT-IR) spectra reveal a clear temperature-dependent shift in the N–H stretching vibration for both COFs. For aCOFamide-1, the N–H stretching band gradually shifts from 3286 cm−1 at room temperature to 3318 cm−1 upon heating upto 210 °C, while aCOFamide-2 exhibits a similar shift from 3280 cm−1 to 3315 cm−1. This progressive blue shift of the N–H stretching frequency indicates a weakening of the interlayer hydrogen-bonding interactions as the temperature increases. The elevated thermal energy disrupts the hydrogen-bonding network, resulting in less strongly hydrogen-bonded N–H groups and consequently higher vibrational frequencies. These observations provide strong spectroscopic evidence for the presence of interlayer hydrogen-bonding interactions in both frameworks and demonstrate their temperature-dependent nature. Compared to our previous study,66 where COF formation was accompanied by an initial blue shift in the N–H stretching vibration and the VT-IR analysis further revealed a gradual weakening of the hydrogen-bonding interactions with increasing temperature, this study shows an initial red shift during COF formation. This suggests that the hydrogen-bonding network is organized differently in the two systems. The observed red shift is consistent with stronger interlayer hydrogen-bonding interactions, resulting from an antiparallel arrangement of the amide groups. Finally, we note that the computational model employed here does not explicitly include the full periodic environment of the framework; however, the good agreement between the calculated trends and the experimental FT-IR data highlights this vibrational feature as a useful descriptor of the local stacking environment.

Iodine adsorption studies

The iodine capture ability of the aCOFamides and POPamides was characterized using UV-Vis measurements similar to the previous literature,67 as mentioned in Fig. S13. Since molecular iodine is limited in its water solubility, a solution of potassium iodide and potassium triiodide was used which produces molecular iodine in equilibrium with its anionic species. As I2 is adsorbed, it is replenished under equilibrium until all of the anionic species are consumed. The kinetics and adsorption efficiencies are measured by monitoring the reduction of the UV adsorption peak for I− in the presence of a porous adsorbent.

The iodine concentration was decreased from 40 ppm to less than 7.7 ± 0.4 ppm and 4.3 ± 0.4 ppm in 4 h, for aCOFamide-1 and -2, respectively (Fig. S13), indicating the rapid uptake of iodine in aCOFamides which is very crucial in real-world scenarios such as nuclear accidents. Swift adsorption minimizes the spread of volatile I2 and ensures immediate containment. On the other hand, our control POPamide-1 and -2 was unable to absorb iodine at this rate, which demonstrates the importance of the crystalline framework in facilitating efficient diffusion and uniform binding site accessibility (Fig. S13). Interestingly, both POPamides demonstrated very similar adsorption behavior despite the fact that POPamide-2 has a lower accessible surface area. Since the accessible pore sizes of the amorphous POPamides are smaller than the mesoporous aCOFamides, the density of amide groups within the pores is actually higher than in the COFs. These observations suggest that the organized nature of the hydrogen bonding network plays an important role beyond just improving the accessible surface area through pore reinforcement, or the amount of molecular receptors, in this case, amide groups. Both the aCOFs showed an enhanced rate of iodine adsorption (Fig. 6D), rising steeply from the origin to around 30 min (80 mg g−1). In the first 3 min, 8 mg g−1 of iodine is adsorbed, and by 30 min, 80 mg g−1 is adsorbed. The uptake rate was calculated to be 0.16 g g−1 h−1. This rapid uptake is due to the abundance of vacant adsorption sites in the COF pores. The maximum uptake capacity was calculated to be 5.9 ± 0.2 g g−1 and 6.7 ± 0.2 g g−1 of iodine, respectively (Fig. S13), which can be explained by the presence of exposed binding sites in an antiparallel stack (Fig. 6B), which would be blocked in a parallel stack (Fig. 6A). The removal efficiency of aCOFamide-1 and -2 was calculated to be 93% and 95% respectively, while the POPamide achieved less than 30% efficiency over the same time period. To calculate this, both COFs and POP (10 mg) were packed in the syringe filter, and the iodine solution was passed through it using a syringe pump at a flow rate of 5 mL min−1 for 8 h, as shown in Fig. 6C. These were then flushed with ethanol to evaluate the reusability of the material. The process was repeated up to five cycles, resulting in the same removal efficiency for both aCOFamides, as shown in Fig. S15. Furthermore, both aCOFamide-1 and aCOFamide-2 exhibit noticeable changes in their FT-IR spectra following iodine adsorption (Fig. S19). For aCOFamide-1, the N–H stretching vibration shifts from 3270 cm−1 to 3292 cm−1, while for aCOFamide-2, it shifts from 3277 cm−1 to 3335 cm−1, corresponding to blue shifts of 22 cm−1 and 58 cm−1, respectively. These blue shifts indicate a weakening of the N–H hydrogen-bonding interactions upon iodine uptake, suggesting that the adsorbed iodine species disturb the original interlayer hydrogen-bonding network and modify the local electronic environment around the amide groups. Consistent with these spectroscopic observations, both COF samples retained a distinct orange-purple coloration even after extensive air drying (Fig. S19B), indicating that the iodine species remain confined within the porous framework rather than being merely physisorbed on the particle surface.

Iodide adsorption studies

Due to the high environmental mobility of iodide, these contaminants can quickly migrate into groundwater, where they pose a serious threat to aquatic life and humans.67–69 In general, anion recognition in aqueous environments remains a major challenge owing to their strong solvation interactions with water. In recent years porous polymers and frameworks have demonstrated promise in this application as the high surface area and confined environments can promote adsorption.70–73 Encouraged by the above results, we postulated that aCOFamide-1 and -2, containing exposed amide N–H groups would offer abundant binding sites for iodide.74 Initial adsorption experiments were carried out by adding 3.0 mg of both aCOFs into a solution of 1 M potassium iodide solution in water. Time-dependent UV-Vis measurements were taken at 3 min intervals for up to 30 min and then up to 12 h.

The initial experiments confirmed that both the aCOFs possess rapid iodide adsorption kinetics. There was a visible decrease in the iodide peak at around 230 nm as shown in Fig. 6F. Electrostatic potential maps reveal localized regions of positive potential near the amide functional groups, which serve as favorable binding sites for iodide, as shown in the Fig. S16. These localized cationic environments give rise to adsorption pockets within the framework. We found iodide uptake to be 1.3 ± 0.1 g g−1 and 1.7 ± 0.1 g g−1 in aCOFamide-1 and -2, respectively. On the other hand, POPamide was unable to adsorb any amount of iodide (Fig. S17) presumably because of the absence of the ordered ionic channels, charge-balancing frameworks and strong electrostatic interactions. We compared the iodide adsorption to other porous polymers that are present in the literature and found that these aCOFamides compete with the maximum adsorption of many polymers,35,75–82 as shown in Fig. 6E. This shows that the ability to engineer pore sizes and stacking modes, particularly antiparallel arrangements, maximizes accessible adsorption sites and improves the affinity of host–guest interactions.

To further support these findings, we performed non-covalent interaction (NCI) analyses in addition to computing interaction energies on fully optimized host–guest complex structures for both iodine and iodide, providing molecular-level insight into the adsorption behavior. In all cases, the computed interaction energies are negative, indicating that adsorption is favorable for both aCOFamides. For iodide, the strongest interaction is found in aCOFamide-1 (Ent = −32.7 kcal mol−1), where the anion is stabilized predominantly by the aromatic ring, enabling favorable anion–π interactions as shown in Fig. 7A. In contrast, in aCOFamide-2 (Ent = −17.3 kcal mol−1), the presence of the ethylene linker causes the interaction to be divided between the aromatic benzene ring and the aliphatic chain (Fig. 7C). This redistribution across chemically distinct environments appears to reduce the overall stabilization, possibly due to a less favorable spatial arrangement or weaker local electrostatic interactions compared to the more focused binding in aCOFamide-1.

NCI analysis of iodide (I−) and iodine (I2) adsorption in aCOFamides (A) and (B) aCOFamide-1 with I− and I2 respectively. (C) and (D) aCOFamide-2 with I− and I2 respectively.

Interestingly, a similar redistribution of interactions between the benzene ring and the aliphatic chain is also observed for iodine. In aCOFamide-1 (−18.2 kcal mol−1), iodine interacts with both regions as shown in Fig. 7B, but this divided binding leads to a less favorable interaction energy compared to aCOFamide-2 (−27.9 kcal mol−1), where iodine aligns predominantly along the aliphatic chain as shown in Fig. 7D, likely due to enhanced contact area that supports stabilization. This suggests a structural role for the aliphatic chain in potentially facilitating stronger non-covalent interactions when the binding is more localized and less shared with the aromatic unit.

The computed interaction energies correlate well with the experimental uptake results for iodine, where aCOFamide-2 outperforms aCOFamide-1 (6.7 vs. 5.9 g g−1), supporting the idea that continuous, localized interactions favor adsorption of neutral iodine species. However, for iodide, despite aCOFamide-1 showing a stronger computed interaction energy, its experimental uptake is slightly lower than that of aCOFamide-2. This observation highlights that the calculated interaction energies primarily describe the local energetic preference of an isolated host–guest binding event. In contrast, the experimentally measured uptake capacity reflects a collective adsorption process involving multiple adsorption events, fractional site occupancy, guest–guest interactions, framework heterogeneity, and other many-body effects that are not explicitly captured by the present computational model. Overall, these findings point to the importance of both the chemical nature and spatial arrangement of binding sites in governing host–guest interactions within these frameworks.

Conclusion

In this study, we report the rational design of amide-functionalized 2D covalent organic frameworks (aCOFamide-1 and -2) that adopt an antiparallel stacking arrangement inspired by biomolecular β-sheet motifs. This unique stacking mode, enabled by directional interlayer hydrogen bonding, enhances framework crystallinity, pore accessibility, and guest diffusion factors that collectively drive high iodine and iodide uptake. Compared to their amorphous POP analogue with identical functional groups, these COFs demonstrate dramatically higher adsorption capacities (up to 6.7 g g−1 for iodine and 1.7 g g−1 for iodide) and faster kinetics (0.16 g g−1 h−1), underscoring the critical role of structural order in host–guest performance. Computational and spectroscopic analyses confirm the formation of multi-N nanotraps that facilitate selective halogen binding via cooperative non-covalent interactions.

Furthermore, we show that by simply changing the location of the interlayer hydrogen bonds within the pore, we can cause them to adopt a different hydrogen bonding arrangement (antiparallel vs. parallel) when located on the linker vs. the node monomer, respectively. This is a key fundamental parameter for reticular COF design, that to our knowledge, has not been elaborated previously through synthesis or computational predictions. In most cases, the choice of where to place the pore functional groups within a COF are driven by synthetic accessibility or convenience, rather than the protocols of reticular design. Our findings establish that these design choices can affect the COF structure at the molecular level. In the future, this can be a powerful design principle for engineering next-generation COFs with self-assembled binding pockets inspired by structural motifs found in biomolecules for targeted environmental remediation and selective adsorption.
