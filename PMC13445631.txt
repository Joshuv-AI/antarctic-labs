A Genomic Perspective on Cryptic Species Reveals Complex Evolutionary Dynamics in the Gray Zone of the Speciation Continuum
Authors: Kin Onn Chan, Dario N Neokleous, Shahrul Anuar, Rafe M Brown, Carl R Hutter, Indraneil Das, Stefan T Hertwig, Bryan Carstens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe evolutionary dynamics of cryptic species remain poorly understood, and their detection relies primarily on methods that quantify divergence, assuming that gene flow is absent. Here, we examine how gene flow shapes the evolutionary trajectories and species boundaries in Bornean Fanged Frogs, a renowned example of cryptic diversity where a single species has been split into 18 genetically divergent yet morphologically indistinguishable species. We employed target-capture data from over 13,000 loci to assess lineage independence of 14 nominal species distributed across Malaysian Borneo by evaluating both divergence and cohesion using network multispecies coalescent (NMSC) and MSC + migration approaches. Under the Unified Species Concept, only 6 of the 14 nominal species unambiguously form independently evolving lineages; the remainder represent cohesive metapopulation lineages nested within those six species. While mitochondrial p-distances varied substantially (up to 10%), genome-wide net divergences (Da) were surprisingly consistent, ranging from 0.5 to 2%, placing all the hypothesized “cryptic species” within the empirical gray zone of the speciation continuum. We show that diversification in the gray zone is unpredictable and heavily impacted by gene flow, leading to two key phenomena that confound species delimitation: (1) the artifactual branch effect, where admixed lineages are inferred as long, early-diverging branches, creating an illusion of deep divergence; and (2) the species-definition anomaly zone, where intraspecific pairwise sequence distances exceed interspecific ones. We further demonstrate that divergence in the gray zone varies substantially among metrics and genomic regions, reflecting heterogeneity in evolutionary dynamics across the genome. Different genomic markers also vary considerably in phylogenetic discordance and their ability to retain signatures of gene flow. Loci from anchored hybrid enrichment and ultraconserved elements produced less phylogenetic discordance and retained signals of older introgression but failed to detect recent migration, making them suitable for phylogenetic reconstruction and inferring ancient introgression, but not ongoing gene flow. Recognizing the central role of gene flow reframes our understanding of cryptic species; rather than being considered as genetically distinct units that failed to evolve morphological differentiation, they can be manifestations of continuous diversification in the gray zone. This shift in perspective offers a new and dynamic evolutionary framework for identifying and interpreting cryptic biodiversity across the Tree of Life.Keywords: Amphibians, artifactual branch effect, diversification, gene flow, reticulate evolution, species definition anomaly zone, species delimitation

Cryptic species are recognized to be prevalent across the Tree of Life, representing significant yet often overlooked components of biodiversity (Pfenninger and Schwenk 2007; Trontelj et al. 2009). Over the past two decades, molecular phylogenetic studies have alluded to a staggering proportion of undescribed cryptic diversity, leading to dramatic revisions of global biodiversity estimates. Older models, which did not account for cryptic species, suggested that Earth hosts between 2 and 9 million species (Mora et al. 2011). In stark contrast, more recent models incorporating undescribed cryptic diversity estimate that the total number of species could surpass two billion (Li and Wiens 2023), potentially reshaping our understanding of Earth’s biodiversity. However, despite their purported prevalence, fundamental questions about cryptic species are unresolved. Their evolutionary history, the processes driving their diversification, the mechanisms by which they persist as separate evolutionary units, and the methodologies used to detect them are all subjects of ongoing debate (Trontelj et al. 2009; Fišer et al. 2018; Heethoff 2018; Struck et al. 2018; Chenuil et al. 2019). This uncertainty raises important questions about the raison d'être of cryptic species, their roles in ecosystems, and their broader implications for biodiversity and conservation.

At its core, the definition of cryptic species remains ambiguous and contentious, with no universal consensus. Over time, numerous definitions have been proposed and used interchangeably (Shin and Allmon 2023). Nevertheless, cryptic species are generally understood as genetically divergent evolutionary lineages that are morphologically difficult to differentiate (Chenuil et al. 2019). This dual-criteria framework of genetic divergence and morphological similarity has fueled a surge of studies uncovering hidden cryptic diversity across many organisms (Adams et al. 2014; Struck et al. 2018; Cerca et al. 2020; Wattier et al. 2020). However, this simplistic definition introduces significant challenges, particularly with the inconsistent and subjective quantification of genetic and morphological differentiation. Which metrics should be used to measure genetic divergence? What level of genetic divergence separates populations from species? Which axes of morphological variation should be considered when assessing similarity? Adding to this complexity, speciation is usually viewed as a gradual and continuous process (Sukumaran and Knowles 2017; Stankowski and Ravinet 2021), and thus, cryptic species may also exist at various points along the speciation continuum, exhibiting varying degrees of “crypticity” in genetic and morphological divergence. Adopting this protracted view of speciation complicates the distinction between cryptic, pseudo-cryptic, and noncryptic species (Reis et al. 2020; Korshunova and Martynov 2024). Taken together, these issues underscore the need for a more robust and nuanced understanding of the evolutionary history and dynamics of cryptic species.

Within the framework of the Unified Species Concept (de Queiroz 2005), the definition of cryptic species implicitly assumes that genetically divergent clades represent independent evolutionary lineages, that is, species. However, in most studies, the hypothesis of evolutionary independence is rarely tested explicitly, leading to a disconnect between species concepts and operational methodologies (de Queiroz 2005). In practice, many cryptic species studies focus on quantifying genetic divergence to characterize lineage independence (Rannala and Yang 2020), often relying on proxies such as genetic distance (Puillandre et al. 2021), tree-based species delimitation algorithms (Fujisawa et al. 2016; Kapli et al. 2017; Cerca et al. 2020), or site-pattern differences (Jörger and Schrödl 2013). Diversification is rarely a discrete process, especially at the early stages of population differentiation. Instead, it is shaped by the interplay between two opposing forces: divergence and cohesion. These antagonistic processes jointly influence lineage splitting and fusion, generating a dynamic push-and-pull tension along the speciation continuum where species boundaries remain semipermeable (Seehausen et al. 2014; Stankowski and Ravinet 2021). This permeability can result in a chimeric assemblage of interconnected populations, where elements of divergence are intertwined with gene flow, introgression, and hybridization—hallmarks of the “gray zone” of speciation (Roux et al. 2016). As such, exclusively focusing on divergence produces a one-sided perspective on evolution, often leading to the overestimation of species numbers in empirical studies (Chan et al. 2022b). To overcome this bias, it is necessary to integrate assessments of both divergence and cohesion into species delimitation frameworks (Chan et al. 2022b; Jackson et al. 2017; Singhal et al. 2018, 2025; Prates et al. 2024). This approach not only mitigates the shortcomings of tree/distance-based methods but is also anchored to the Unified Concept of Species by explicitly evaluating lineage independence (de Queiroz 2005). By balancing the quantification of divergence with an assessment of cohesion, and recognizing species as dynamically evolving metapopulation lineages that could be at various stages of speciation (de Queiroz 2005), we can reduce taxonomic inflation and develop a more robust and nuanced understanding of cryptic species—and, potentially, their contribution to the broader diversity of life on Earth.

Assessing cohesion often conflicts with conventional approaches designed to quantify divergence. For instance, tree-based methods and classical multispecies coalescent models assume no gene flow following species divergence (Rannala and Yang 2003), an assumption directly at odds with the central aim of assessing cohesion. When this assumption is violated, phylogenies can exhibit the artifactual branch effect, where admixed lineages are erroneously inferred as distinct species due to their long branches at early-diverging positions of clades (Chan et al. 2020b; Pyron et al. 2022; Sánchez et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025). Genetic distances within species can also exceed those between species when gene flow is present, a phenomenon known as the species-definition anomaly zone (Jiao and Yang 2020). These limitations are further exacerbated by the inconsistent application of genetic distance metrics, which are heavily influenced by the choice of calculation method, distance model, and data set (Chan et al. 2022a; Fregin et al. 2012). Therefore, if most cryptic species studies rely on tree/distance-based methods that are prone to overestimating species diversity, we find it reasonable to question whether quantitative extrapolations and sweeping claims regarding the prevalence of cryptic species across the Tree of Life are tenable. More importantly, if our fundamental understanding of cryptic species and the speciation process in general is clouded by incorrectly applied methodologies, unclear definitions, and inflated estimates, then it becomes imperative to re-evaluate both the methods and assumptions driving cryptic species research. Without this critical reassessment, we risk perpetuating a distorted view of cryptic species, one that conflates methodological artifacts with biological reality.

Fanged Frogs in the Limnonectes kuhlii complex, renowned for their extraordinary genetic diversity but not accompanied by consistent morphological or ecological differentiation (Matsui et al. 2016), represent an ideal empirical system for studying evolutionary dynamics of cryptic species. Once considered a few widespread species distributed widely throughout Southeast Asia, molecular studies revealed extensive hidden diversity within the complex (Emerson et al. 2000; McLeod 2010; Matsui et al. 2016). These studies, primarily based on single-locus mitochondrial DNA phylogenies, sparked a wave of taxonomic revisions, leading to the description of at least 29 new species (Frost 2025). The populations of the L. kuhlii complex on the island of Borneo (hereafter referred to as Bornean Fanged Frogs; Fig. 1a) exemplify the seemingly remarkable cryptic diversity of these frogs, forming a monophyletic group encompassing 20 putative lineages, 18 of which have been formally described as distinct species (Matsui and Nishikawa 2014; Matsui et al. 2014, 2024; Dehling and Dehling 2017; Gonggoli et al. 2025b). Moreover, some localities host up to four sympatric, non-sister “species” (Fig. 1b, c), a pattern that has been interpreted as evidence supporting lineage distinction (McLeod 2010; Matsui et al. 2016).

Bornean Fanged Frogs exhibit limited phenotypic and ecological disparity. Additionally, bioacoustics analysis of mating calls is intractable because they are either voiceless, produce barely audible calls, or call irregularly, making them difficult to record (Emerson and Berrigan 1993). As such, species delimitation of Bornean Fanged Frogs has solely relied on tree/distance-based methods (McLeod 2010; Matsui et al. 2016, 2024), and their species boundaries remain unvalidated by genomic data. Therefore, the purported but unvalidated cryptic species diversity of Bornean Fanged Frogs, combined with their complex, overlapping distributions, that may have created opportunities for gene flow, makes this an ideal system for studying the evolutionary drivers of cryptic species, while serving as a cautionary case study for overdescribing cryptic species (Zachos et al. 2013; Leaché et al. 2019; Mahony et al. 2024; Chambers et al. 2025).

Materials and Methods

Sampling and Genomic Data Collection

Genomic data were obtained from tissue samples of 79 vouchered specimens housed at the Naturhistorisches Museum Bern, Switzerland (Supplementary Table S1). The samples comprised populations and mitochondrial lineages of the L. kuhlii complex across the Malaysian states of Sabah and Sarawak on the island of Borneo. Whenever possible, multiple samples from every clade/nominal species were included, and their identities were verified by incorporating them in a phylogenetic analysis that included sequences of holotypes obtained from GenBank (Supplementary Table S1). Genomic data were obtained via target-capture sequencing using the FrogCap protocol. Our goal was to create a set of universal amphibian markers that could be targeted using a single set of probes. To initially select and design markers, we used the FrogCap marker set V3 (Hutter et al. 2022; https://github.com/chutter/FrogCap-Sequence-Capture). We selected the full FrogCap markers that were successfully captured across Anura, selecting markers captured from the Ranoidea V2 and Hyloidea V2 probe sets as well as the Metazoan Amphibian probe set from Dietz et al. (2023). We also added additional legacy markers, including those from the rapidly evolving long exon capture (RELEC) marker set (Karin et al. 2019) and anchored hybrid enrichment (AHE) markers (Hime et al. 2021) extracted from their respective publications by BLAST-ing to all the genomes in Supplementary Table S2 and extracting the markers from those genomes.

We aimed to add additional universal markers that could potentially be compared and included in phylogenetic analyses with other groups of organisms. We selected the Universal Single Copy Orthologs (USCO) set of markers, which are orthologous genes found across deep evolutionary scales (i.e., Kingdom, Phylum, Class). To identify USCO markers present across Anura, we used all the available amphibian genomes (Supplementary Table S2). We used the program BUSCO v.3.0.2 (Seppey et al. 2019) to locate and identify the Vertebrate, Metazoan, and Tetrapoda USCO markers in these genomes. We included genes where at least half the genomes recovered USCO markers and included at least one Salamander and Caecilian sample.

To create baits from the FrogCap markers, a consensus sequence was created from the alignments of the selected markers, as this has been suggested to improve target capture success across large phylogenetic scales, and methods for bait design largely follow (Hutter et al. 2022). For all the markers, we used BLAST and extracted each orthologous gene from each genome and separated each of the genes into exons based on the boundaries determined by each genome’s annotations. We aligned each exon and created a consensus sequence for that exon from across all the genomes. The consensus sequences were used to design a MYbaits-2 (120,000 baits) custom bait library (Arbor Biosciences), using 120 mer baits to best capture sequences with greater than 5% divergence from the probes. The finalized set of markers was separated into probe sequences following a 2× tiling scheme, starting 20 bp behind the start codon of the exon and tiling 120 bp probes every 60 bp until 20 bp past stop codons. Individual probes were filtered using these criteria: (1) excluded probes that matched 70% or greater of the length to multiple locations in the genomes referenced above (Supplementary Table S2) using BLAST; (2) kept probes with a GC content between 30 and 50%; (3) retained probes if there were no repetitive sequences based on RepeatMasker annotations using the online server (Sun et al. 2015); and (4) kept probes if there were no matches with BLAST to other probes (using a 70% similarity criterion). This resulted in a final set of 34,114 markers from 5,050 genes covering a total of 9,268,697 bp. The total number of markers from each type of data set is shown in Supplementary Table S3. Raw FrogCap data were processed using the accompanying bioinformatic R package PhyloProcessR. Workflow details are available at GitHub: https://github.com/chutter/PhyloProcessR.

Phylogenomics and Network Analysis

Maximum likelihood (ML) consensus trees were inferred using IQ-TREE-2 (Minh et al. 2020) for near-complete mitogenomes and nuclear genomic loci separately. Loci were concatenated and a partitioned analysis by loci was performed. Due to the large number of nuclear loci, substitution model testing was intractable. Therefore, the most parameter-rich model (GTR + F + I + G4) was applied to every partition/locus, a viable strategy for large genomic data sets (Chan et al. 2020a). Branch support was assessed using 1000 Ultrafast Bootstrap replicates. IQ-TREE-2 was also used to infer gene trees for each locus using the best-fit substitution model inferred using ModelFinder. Individual gene trees were then used as input for a species tree analysis using ASTRAL-III (Zhang et al. 2018) and a quartet-based network analysis using the NANUQ algorithm (Allman et al. 2019) implemented in the R package MSCquartets (Rhodes et al. 2021). As recommended by the developers, we used high alpha (1e–70) and beta (0.1) values for the NANUQ quartet hypothesis test to ensure that inferred networks were highly significant. The program SplitsTree (Huson and Bryant 2024) was then used to visualize a splits network, minimum spanning network, and cluster network, all of which were additionally filtered at a weight threshold of 70, ensuring that inferred reticulation was supported by a high number of quartets.

Population Structure

A data set comprising 13,659 unlinked single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs; one SNP per locus; exons from genes were combined into a single locus) was used to infer population structure using the program sNMF (sparse non-negative matrix factorizations) implemented in the R package LEA (Frichot and François 2015). Ancestry coefficients were estimated for 1–20 ancestral populations (K) using 100 replicates for each K. The cross-entropy criterion was then used to determine the best K based on the prediction of masked genotypes. To assess population clustering, we reduced the dimensionality of the SNP data set using the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) algorithm that can capture local structure of high-dimensional data within two or three dimensions. This analysis was performed with the R package Rtsne (Krijthe 2015) using the following settings: perplexity = 11, max iterations = 1,000,000, and theta = 0.0.

Gene Flow and Species Delimitation

The program Dsuite (Malinsky et al. 2021) was used to perform a four-taxon ABBA–BABA test to detect introgression among clades. The Dtrios and f-branch functions were used to calculate the D, f4-ratio, and f-branch statistics. Migration rates were estimated using the MSC-M model implemented in the program BPP v 4.7.0 (Flouri et al. 2018) and performed through the Python pipeline hhsd (Kornai et al. 2024). For computational tractability, we performed this analysis on 100 randomly selected loci. The following settings were used: theta prior = 3, 0.001 e; tau prior = 3, 0.03; burn-in = 125,000; MCMC samples = 500,000. Other parameters were left at default settings. We also employed the hierarchical merge and split algorithms in hhsd to delimit species based on the genealogical divergence index (gdi) that considers population split times, population sizes, and migration rates (Jackson et al. 2017). We employed a threshold of gdi ≥ 0.7, ≥ 0.5 for the split algorithm and gdi ≤ 0.3, ≤ 1.0 for the merge algorithm.

Divergence Profile

Distance-based species delimitation approaches implicitly assume that lineages diverge in a predictable manner, where genetic distance increases linearly as they evolve from populations to species, and that intraspecific distances do not exceed interspecific ones. To test this assumption, we constructed genome-wide divergence profiles using several commonly applied metrics, including Dxy, Fst, Da (net divergence), and uncorrected p-distances. We then compared the distributions and relationships of these metrics with genomic marker type and between populations versus species to test two hypotheses: (1) Divergence patterns based on different metrics are consistent across the genome (using marker type as a proxy for genomic regions) and (2) divergence increases linearly from populations to species.

For Hypothesis 1, we used the program pixy to calculate unbiased estimates of nucleotide diversity (π), Dxy, and Fst by incorporating invariant sites and accounting for missing data (Korunes and Samuk 2021). Briefly, we generated a consensus alignment using EMBOSS to serve as a pseudo-reference. Raw reads that have been quality-filtered and trimmed were mapped to the pseudo-reference sequence using bwa-mem followed by variant-calling using bcftools (Danecek et al. 2021). A BED file was then created to map the starting and ending positions for each locus based on their sequence lengths to ensure that calculations were performed on a locus-by-locus basis as opposed to using arbitrary sliding windows. Net divergence (Da) was calculated using the formula Dxy-(πx + πy)/2, where x and y represent the two populations compared. Correlations and variation in the distributions of Da, Dxy, and Fst among different marker types were calculated to determine whether divergence patterns are consistent across the genome.

To test Hypothesis 2, we compared distributions of pairwise divergences between and within species across Da, Dxy, Fst, and uncorrected p-distances (mitochondrial and nuclear). To test for linearity, we used the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to assess the fit of our data to the following models: linear, polynomial, generalized additive model (GAM), and spline. Finally, because the mitochondrial 16S rRNA marker is ubiquitously used as a proxy for species divergence, we tested whether genetic distances between the mitochondrial and nuclear genomes are correlated.

Results

Our full genomic data set consists of 13,661 highly informative loci derived from several marker types: FrogCap (6453 loci), BUSCO (3626), UCE (3177), AHE (178), Functional (138), Legacy (69), and RELEC (20). After filtering sequences with <500 bps and <100 parsimony-informative sites (Hutter and Duellman 2023), the filtered data set retained 9,968 loci. Detailed summary statistics of the data are presented in Table 1. We refer to putative species by their respective clade numbers rather than species names (Fig. 1c), which are not central to this study and can be difficult to follow. These clade numbers have been well-established and consistently applied in the literature (Matsui et al. 2016, 2024), providing a coherent, standardized reference system. Only two species from Malaysian Borneo were described without an associated clade number: Limnonectes cintalubang (outgroup) and Limnonectes hikidai. Species names and their respective clade numbers and associated metadata are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Phylogenomics and Population Structure

ML phylogenies based on the mitogenome (16,532 bps), concatenated full genomic data set (14,992,489 bps), and the ASTRAL species tree phylogeny (13,661 gene trees) were fully supported at ancestral nodes along the backbone of the phylogenies but showed discordance at the clade and individual levels (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2). Similar to previous studies (Matsui et al. 2016, 2024), L. cintalubang was consistently inferred as the first-branching lineage, serving as the outgroup to root our phylogenies. At the clade level, Clades 1–17 and L. hikidai were monophyletic in the mitogenomic phylogeny, but Clades 12, 13, and 16 were paraphyletic in the species tree (Fig. 1c). At the individual level, several errant samples from Clades 1, 13, 12, 16, and 17 were discordant and placed in other clades. Additionally, a new population from Mount Hose (designated “Hose” Clade) was inferred as the first-branching lineage of Clades 1, 2, and 5. Species trees inferred separately for each marker type were also highly discordant, though mostly well supported (Supplementary Material).

The sNMF population structure analysis yielded ambiguous results regarding the optimal number of ancestral populations, K (Fig. 2a). The cross-entropy criterion indicated that K values ranging from 6 to 14 had similar scores (Fig. 2b). The t-SNE analysis largely reflected the sNMF results, with several clades showing similar ancestries and forming tight, overlapping clusters (Fig. 2c). These include Clade 1 + 2, Clade 7 + 8, Clade 11 + 12, Clade 13 + 14, and Clade 15 + 16 + 17. Due to the lack of evidence supporting their separation, we merged some of these undifferentiated clusters to facilitate downstream gene flow and species delimitation analyses.

Phylogenetic Networks Analysis

The NANUQ analysis of both the full and filtered data sets revealed numerous quartets consistent with a 4-cycle network, even at a stringent threshold of α = 1e−70 (Fig. 3a). A splits graph based on intertaxon distances from the NANUQ analysis showed extensive reticulation, especially among Clades 10–17 (Fig. 3b–d). A minimum spanning network identified six distinct clusters: L. cintalubang, L. hikidai, Clade 5, Clades 6 + 7 + 8, Clade 9, and Clades 10–17. Errant individuals that were discordant in the phylogenies were interspersed between Clade 5 and Clades 10–17 (Fig. 3c). A cluster network further revealed numerous reticulation edges across different evolutionary scales, suggesting both ancestral and contemporary gene flow. Reticulation within Clades 10–17 was more recent, whereas the other clades exhibited older reticulation events (Fig. 3d).

Gene Flow, Introgression, and Species Delimitation

The Dsuite and F-branch analyses detected extensive introgression across the major markers (Supplementary Fig. S3). Ancestral introgression was high between L. hikidai and the ancestral lineage leading to Clade 6–8 and Clades 10–17, as well as between the lineage leading to Clades 5–8 and that leading to Clades 10–17 (Supplementary Fig. S3). These findings are consistent with the NANUQ analysis, which also revealed reticulation between the ancestral branches of these lineages (Fig. 3d). The F-branch analysis further detected more contemporary introgression between Clade 5 and Clades 6–8; Clade 5 and Clade 13 + 14; Clade 7 + 8 and Clade 1 + 2 + 5; and Clade 13 + 14 and Clade 15 + 16 + 17 (Supplementary Fig. S3). Using 100 randomly loci selected from all marker types, directional migration rates estimated from the MSC + migration model in the program BPP indicated high asymmetrical contemporary gene flow within and between major clades (Fig. 4). When migration rates, population split times, and effective population sizes were integrated into a species delimitation framework under the MSC + migration model, six species were supported, excluding the outgroup: Clade 1 + 2 + 5, Clade 6, Clade 7 + 8, Clade 9, Clade 10–17, and L. hikidai (Supplementary Fig. S4 and Table S4). We were unable to adequately assess the specific status of the “Hose” Clade because it comprises a single sample, while preliminary phylogenetic, population structure, and clustering analyses were unable to confidently assign it to any other population. However, the results clearly show that it is a highly admixed lineage most closely related to Clade 1 + 2 + 5; thus, we provisionally assign it to that clade. Due to this uncertainty and its highly admixed genetic makeup, we excluded it from downstream analyses to avoid potential bias.

Consistency across Different Markers

We also performed network and migration analyses on AHE, BUSCO, FrogCap, and UCE markers separately to assess the consistency of results across different marker types (the other markers had <300 loci and were not assessed). The NANUQ analysis showed variable results. Notably, the UCE data set showed no evidence of reticulation, whereas the other marker types showed variable levels (Fig. 5a). Migration rates were also highly variable. No significant migration (M < 0.1) was detected from AHE and UCE markers, while BUSCO and FrogCap markers detected different levels of migration across different population pairs (Fig. 5b). Overall, networks, migration rates, split times (tau), and population sizes (theta) also varied widely across different marker types (Fig. 5; Supplementary Tables S5-S7).

Divergence Profile and the Speciation Continuum

Pairwise population comparisons of net divergence (Da) revealed a three-stage gradient (Fig. 6a). The lowest net divergences (Da < 0.005) were observed between Clades 7 and 8 and among Clades 13–17, followed by a gradual increase between 0.005 and 0.02, a range that corresponds to the gray zone of speciation (Roux et al. 2016). The third stage showed a sharper increase associated with comparisons involving the outgroup (L. cintalubang). Divergence patterns varied across metrics and marker types. UCE and AHE markers exhibited the lowest Da and Dxy values, whereas FrogCap, BUSCO, and functional markers were the highest. However, this pattern was not observed in Fst, which showed the opposite pattern, where FrogCap, BUSCO, and Functional markers had the highest values (Fig. 6a, b). Similarly, Da and Dxy were highly correlated (R = 0.919), whereas Fst was the least correlated with Dxy (R = 0.273) and moderately correlated with Da (R = 0.572) (Fig. 6b). Across all metrics, the only consistent pattern was that Clades 7 and 8, and Clades 13–17 had significantly lower divergences (Fig. 6a).

Discussion

Insights into the Speciation Continuum

Empirical representations of the speciation continuum are often derived from model taxa (Seehausen et al. 2014; Supple et al. 2015) or reconstructed from unrelated taxon pairs at various stages of diversification (Edwards et al. 2016; Roux et al. 2016; McLaughlin et al. 2020). In this study, we demonstrate that Bornean Fanged Frogs encompass the full spectrum of the speciation continuum within a single species complex spanning a large continuous landscape. This system provides a unique opportunity to investigate the gradual process of speciation within a single continuously diverging clade. We identified three distinct stages within the speciation continuum: an initial stage, a gray zone, and a terminal stage. The initial stage comprised early-diverging populations that were clearly not evolving independently and consistently exhibited low divergence across all metrics (i.e., Clades 7 + 8 and 13–17) (Figs. 6a and 7). The terminal stage corresponds to complete speciation, exemplified by the most basal lineage, L. cintalubang—the only uncontroversial species that exhibits clear phenotypic (significantly smaller size, orange hindlimbs, and presence of bluish-white spots) and ecological differentiation (burrow- vs. stream-dwelling) (Matsui et al. 2014). The gray zone lies between the initial and terminal stages of divergence and corresponds remarkably well with the 0.5–2% net divergence range (Fig. 6a) previously identified through comparative genomic analysis of 61 unrelated taxon pairs (Roux et al. 2016). Within this zone, populations and species exhibit unpredictable and nonlinear patterns of divergence (Figs. 6 and 7; Supplementary Table S8), creating considerable challenges for taxonomic inference. Furthermore, divergence profiles vary among metrics, marker types, and taxonomic scales (populations vs. species), reflecting differential evolutionary rates across genes and hierarchical levels (Romeiro‐Brito et al. 2016).

Heterogeneity across Metrics, Methods, and Markers

As expected, Da and Dxy were highly correlated because their calculations are related. However, Da showed less variation across the genome than Dxy (Fig. 6a), likely because Da corrects for within-population diversity while Dxy measures absolute divergence. Fst showed a weaker correlation with Dxy than with Da (Fig. 6b), consistent with the fact that both Fst and Da account for intrapopulation variation, whereas Dxy reflects total divergence, including both fixed differences and shared polymorphisms. Between-species comparisons showed that the relationship between Dxy and Fst becomes decoupled (R = 0.057; Fig. 6c). This pattern suggests that while absolute sequence divergence (Dxy) accumulates over time, population differentiation (Fst) saturates or increases more slowly, potentially due to ancestral polymorphisms shared through historical gene flow that inflate Dxy but contribute less to contemporary differentiation. Alternatively, if gene flow has ceased between species, divergence may accumulate primarily through lineage-specific mutations that increase Dxy while Fst reaches an asymptote apparently determined by effective population sizes. In contrast, within-species comparisons showed a positive correlation between Dxy and Fst (R = 0.267; Fig. 6c). This pattern is consistent with incomplete reproductive isolation and ongoing or recent gene flow, under which divergence accumulates through drift and mutation. However, gene flow remains insufficient to fully homogenize populations, allowing both Dxy and Fst to increase together. Notably, asymmetrical gene flow could create heterogeneous patterns where some population pairs diverge more rapidly than others. The differences in correlation strength and slope between population and species comparisons demonstrate that the dynamics of divergence differ fundamentally across these taxonomic scales. More importantly, we show that divergence in the gray zone is not linear (Fig. 6c, 7; Supplementary Table S8), reflecting the complex interplay of gene flow, drift, and selection during the early stages of diversification.

Different analytical approaches and markers revealed contrasting patterns of gene flow. Quartet-based gene tree analysis (NANUQ) detected gene flow in AHE, BUSCO, and FrogCap markers, but not UCEs (Fig. 5a); site-based ABBA–BABA tests using SNPs showed variable introgression signals across all four marker types (Supplementary Fig. S3); whereas sequence-based migration rate estimates detected strong migration in BUSCO and FrogCap markers but not in AHE and UCEs (Fig. 5b; Supplementary Table S6). These discordant results demonstrate that analytical approach (quartet-, site-, or sequence-based), data type (gene trees, SNPs, or sequences), and genomic target (AHE, BUSCO, FrogCap, or UCE) vary considerably in their utility to detect gene flow across different time scales (historical vs. ongoing). These disparities likely reflect microevolutionary processes acting on different parts of the genome. Selection can generate false positives, leading to elevated estimates of migration rates in non-neutral loci (Smith and Hahn 2024), while the magnitude and presence of gene flow could be associated with recombination rates (Nachman and Payseur 2012; Thom et al. 2024; Burbrink et al. 2025). Furthermore, gene flow itself involves many parameters, including timing, directionality, and rate, and model misspecifications can further lead to erroneous estimates (Thawornwattana et al. 2025). These complexities caution against relying on any single line of evidence, as apparent conflicts among methods may reflect genuine biological heterogeneity. It is thus not realistic to expect that a single marker type or analytical framework can fully capture the temporal and genomic complexity of diversification. Therefore, it is imperative for researchers to carefully match their choice of markers and methods to their specific research questions—whether investigating ancient introgression, ongoing migration, phylogenetic relationships, or species boundaries—while employing integrative approaches that account for methodological limitations and genome heterogeneity.

Notably, our findings demonstrate that UCEs, while highly effective for resolving phylogenetic relationships (Faircloth et al. 2012), are less appropriate for inferring recent demographic processes such as ongoing migration. UCE core regions are under strong purifying selection (Bejerano et al. 2004; Winker et al. 2018; Cummins et al. 2024; Thomas et al. 2025), which reduces genetic diversity not only in the core regions but also in nearby flanking regions through background selection (Charlesworth et al. 1993; Thomas et al. 2025). This limited variation makes UCEs poor targets for methods that rely on polymorphisms to infer recent demographic events but excellent for phylogenetics due to low gene tree discordance (Thomas et al. 2025). Conversely, FrogCap and BUSCO markers target protein-coding genes where synonymous sites can accumulate variation more freely (Simão et al. 2015). In such regions, deleterious mutations are likely removed by purifying selection, but synonymous variation or mildly beneficial alleles are allowed to persist (Charlesworth et al. 1993; Elyashiv et al. 2016). UCE markers may fail to detect ongoing migration using methods that rely on sequence variation such as BPP because little neutral variation is preserved in those regions due to background or linked selection.

The ABBA–BABA test differs from sequence-based methods by operating on biallelic SNP patterns rather than modeling mutation and drift processes. This allows it to detect introgression even in conserved loci like AHE and UCEs by leveraging patterns of shared derived alleles among taxa (Denton et al. 2018; Winker et al. 2018). Introgressed alleles, once established in a genome, can persist and create detectable asymmetries in allele sharing even when overall variability is low. However, ABBA-BABA cannot distinguish between recent and ancient introgression and may fail to detect ongoing migration if gene flow has not yet generated strong asymmetrical allele sharing patterns across the genome. Collectively, our results demonstrate that while AHE and UCEs can detect historical introgression in some cases, they may not be ideal for inferring recent or ongoing gene flow. For fine-scale demographic questions, markers with more polymorphic sites and less severe selection constraints can better capture recent evolutionary dynamics, though researchers must remain cognizant of potential biases introduced by selection at functional loci (Johri et al. 2021)

Recombination rates may also influence gene flow detection across different marker types. Regions with higher recombination rates facilitate detection because recombination breaks up linkage to deleterious alleles and prevents background selection from reducing genetic variation, allowing migrant alleles to persist and remain detectable (Thom et al. 2024; Burbrink et al. 2025). Conversely, low-recombination regions experience stronger effects of linked selection, where large haplotype blocks move together and variation is more readily purged by selection against linked deleterious mutations (Schumer et al. 2018; Tigano et al. 2022). This reduction in variation can make recent gene flow more difficult to detect, though ancient introgression events may still be detectable if introgressed haplotypes became established before variation was lost. UCEs, which likely occur in regions with lower recombination rates and strong purifying selection, may lose variation through persistent background selection, reducing the genetic polymorphism available for detecting recent migration (Thom et al. 2024; Thomas et al. 2025).

Empirical Confirmation of the Species-Definition Anomaly Zone

This study presents the first empirical evidence of the species-definition anomaly zone that was theoretically posited but never conclusively demonstrated empirically. The species-definition anomaly zone refers to a scenario where different population sizes and asymmetrical migration rates cause within-species divergence to exceed between-species divergence, where divergence is measured by gene tree probability or average genetic distance (Jiao and Yang 2020). Our results provide evidence for asymmetrical migration rates (Fig. 5b; Supplementary Table S5), large differences in population sizes (Supplementary Table S7), phylogenetic discordance, and instances where within-species divergences are greater than between-species divergences across different metrics (Fig. 7). Although divergence estimates could be affected by the small sample sizes of certain populations, we do not believe that it has significantly biased the results. If systematic bias were present, we would expect to observe a correlation between sample size and estimations of metrics relevant to calculating divergence. For example, Clade 1 has a sample size of n = 2 but has the second-highest estimate of nucleotide diversity (π), whereas Clade 10, which also has a sample size of n = 2, has one of the lowest. Limnonectes hikidai, which has one of the largest sample sizes (n = 10) has a moderate π, whereas Clade 13 (n = 6), has the highest π among all populations (Supplementary Fig. S5). Therefore, although small sample size could affect the accuracy of estimates, it does not appear to systematically bias broader patterns of divergence.

Except for Fst, the species-definition anomaly zone appears to be restricted to the first half of the speciation continuum, where populations are in the earlier stages of divergence (Fig. 7). By estimating relevant demographic parameters (e.g., migration and population size), assessing gene tree discordance, and characterizing genome-wide divergence profiles, we demonstrate a mechanistic link among several confounding phenomena, such as phylogenetic discordance, the artifactual branch effect, and the species-definition anomaly zone, all of which are driven by gene flow within the gray zone.

Diversification Dynamics in the Gray Zone Create an Illusion of Cryptic Species

The complex diversification dynamics in the gray zone, which can manifest in confounding phenomena such as the artifactual branch effect and the species-definition anomaly zone, create an illusion of high species diversity. Meanwhile, historical and ongoing gene flow can result in continuous variation in morphological traits (Stefanello et al. 2021). This scenario provides a surprisingly straightforward explanation for the evolutionary origins, purported high diversity, and defining characteristics of cryptic species—genetic divergence coupled with morphological similarity. This gene-flow-driven process (herein referred to as the gene flow hypothesis) differs from other proposed explanatory models (Fišer et al. 2018), such as the recent divergence hypothesis: insufficient time for morphological differences to evolve (Egea et al. 2016); the phylogenetic niche conservatism hypothesis: selection maintains morphological stasis (Smith et al. 2011); or the morphological convergence hypothesis—morphological similarity driven by convergent evolution (Stayton 2006). The gene flow hypothesis expands our understanding of cryptic species as possible outcomes of continuous diversification in the gray zone, rather than simply genetically distinct units that failed to evolve morphological differentiation. This new perspective reconciles otherwise anomalous patterns, such as high genetic but low morphological divergence and the occurrence of non-sister lineages in sympatry. Nevertheless, the gene flow hypothesis complements rather than replaces other proposed mechanisms underlying the evolution of cryptic species and offers a novel, nuanced, and dynamic evolutionary framework for identifying and understanding cryptic biodiversity across the Tree of Life.

Cryptic Species Delimitation

Our results show that the evolutionary histories of cryptic species can be heavily shaped by gene flow, confounding divergence estimates and phylogenetic inferences, linking evolutionary trajectories of populations, and blurring species boundaries. Consequently, widely used methods that rely on guide trees and divergence thresholds while ignoring gene flow risk overestimating species diversity through two key mechanisms. First, gene flow creates lineages with mixed ancestry that are inferred as long, seemingly distinct branches, often positioned basally to the parental clade that contributes the most alleles—the artifactual branch effect (Chan et al. 2022b, 2023), which is increasingly being documented in the literature (Dolinay et al. 2021; Pyron et al. 2023; Barley et al. 2024; Sánchez et al. 2024; Waldron et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025). In Bornean fanged frogs, Clades 1, 2, 10, 11, 12, and “Hose Clade” exemplify this phenomenon, highlighting the inability of conventional bifurcating phylogenies to accurately depict reticulate evolutionary history. When applied to downstream species delimitation approaches, artifactual branches are invariably misinterpreted as distinct species due to their long branch lengths and distinct topological placement, generating an illusion of deep divergence.

Second, we demonstrate that different distance metrics, such as Da, Fst, and uncorrected p-distances (mitochondrial or nuclear), do not provide consistent measures of intra- and interspecific divergence (Fig. 7). We showed that Da and nuclear p-distances were the most congruent (albeit not precise) with population and species boundaries, whereas mitochondrial p-distances and Fst were the least congruent and most affected by the species-definition anomaly zone, where multiple within-species divergences exceeded divergences between species. These discordant results demonstrate that widely used divergence metrics can produce erratic results in the face of gene flow and are thus poor proxies for species divergence, further undermining the static threshold approach for species delimitation.

This study illustrates the importance of extensive geographic sampling to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of divergence and cohesion. However, whereas we have sampled the Malaysian region of Borneo extensively, the Indonesian region of Kalimantan remains sparsely sampled. At least four nominal species occur in central and southeastern Kalimantan (Inger et al. 1996; Gonggoli et al. 2025a, 2025b) that were not available for our study. Of these, the mitochondrial phylogeny shows that Clade 3 + 4 from southeastern Borneo (Kalimantan) is sister to Clade 1 + 2 from northeastern Borneo. Given the highly admixed genotype of Clade 1 + 2 that readily exchanges genes with other sympatric lineages, gene flow between Clade 1 + 2 and Clade 3 + 4 is plausible. A tract of potentially suitable habitat along the entire eastern arc of Borneo connects their distributions, creating opportunities for gene exchange. However, the region between their ranges remains unsampled. The widely distributed Clade 5 may also occur there, potentially acting as a conduit for gene flow.

Broader Implications

Our findings reveal that reticulate evolution can play a major role in the evolutionary history of cryptic species, causing tree- and distance-based approaches to overestimate species diversity. Because these approaches are commonly used to detect cryptic and noncryptic species alike (Rannala and Yang 2020), reports on the prevalence of cryptic species across the Tree of Life could be considerably inflated, with profound ramifications for conservation. Misidentifying admixed lineages as distinct species atomizes taxonomy, artificially reducing distribution ranges and estimates of effective population sizes, while increasing perceived extinction risks for “range-restricted, micro-endemic” taxa (Cerca et al. 2020). Such misrepresentation elevates their conservation value, potentially misguiding resource allocation and undermining effective conservation (Prie et al. 2012; Zachos 2013; Duarte et al. 2014; Oates and Ting 2015; Conix 2019; Folt et al. 2019; Robuchon et al. 2019; Berrilli et al. 2024; Clavero et al. 2024; Mahony et al. 2024). The oversplitting of Bornean Fanged Frogs (Matsui et al. 2024) exemplifies this issue, where 14 named entities assessed in this study (eight of which are not distinct species) have been assigned excessively restricted ranges, several of which are erroneously defined outside protected areas, meeting the criteria for conservation threat status.

Ultimately, the prevalence of cryptic species reflects not just biological reality but also the limitations of certain methods when applied to organisms actively diversifying in the gray zone. By recognizing gene flow's central role in shaping cryptic diversity and adopting integrative, hypothesis-driven approaches to species delimitation, we can move toward a more accurate understanding of biodiversity—one that acknowledges the dynamic, continuous nature of diversification while maintaining the taxonomic stability essential for effective conservation and comparative biology.
