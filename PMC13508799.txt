Development and Evaluation of a Custom GPT-Based Artificial Intelligence Clinical Decision Support System for Emergency Department Interdepartmental Consultation Automation
Authors: Boram Yoo, Chan Woong Kim, Jong-Hoon Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to develop a custom GPT-based, knowledge-guided documentation support system that drafts emergency department (ED) interdepartmental consultation requests from minimal free-text input and evaluate its feasibility, usability, and workflow integration.MethodsThe system generates a patient summary, recommended consulting department, structured consultation draft, and clinical reference notes from brief natural language input, including age, chief concern, and key findings. Two public AI Hub datasets, the Essential Medical Knowledge Dataset (Dataset No. 71875) and the Specialized Medical Knowledge Dataset (Dataset No. 71874), were used to guide prompt construction and constrain output structure rather than for model training. Department recommendation concordance was assessed using 22 de-identified ED consultation scenarios purposively selected across multiple specialties. The documented department was removed from the input and compared with the system’s recommendation. A post-use survey of 10 emergency physicians and residents assessed workflow utility, clinical appropriateness, usability, reflection of relevant information, perceived patient safety, adoption intention, and editing requirements using 5-point Likert scales.ResultsIn the 22-case pilot set, the system’s recommended department aligned with the historically documented department in all cases under controlled conditions. Given the small sample size and study design, this finding represents preliminary evidence of workflow alignment rather than definitive validation of clinical performance.ConclusionsThe proposed system demonstrates the feasibility of large language model-enabled documentation support for generating standardized ED consultation requests, potentially reducing documentation burden. The system is intended to support, not replace, clinical decision-making, and clinician review remains essential. With further validation, this approach may be adaptable to other clinical referral contexts.

Introduction

The emergency department (ED) is a high-risk, high-variability clinical environment in which patient assessment, treatment, and decision-making occur under severe time constraints. Electronic health record (EHR) documentation also represents a substantial portion of physicians’ workload and contributes to documentation burden and workflow disruption [1].

Interdepartmental consultation letters are central to ED workflows because they formally communicate patient status, clinical context, and consultation intent to specialty services. Incomplete or non-standardized consultation documentation may delay specialty responses or lead to misinterpretation, potentially affecting patient safety and continuity of care [1].

Clinical decision support systems (CDSSs) are designed to assist clinicians by integrating clinical knowledge with patient data to support diagnostic, therapeutic, and workflow-related decisions. Prior studies have shown that appropriately designed CDSSs can improve aspects of clinical practice and decision-making [2–4]. However, most existing CDSSs are rule-based or optimized for structured data elements and therefore provide limited direct support for free-text clinical documentation tasks such as consultation letter drafting.

Large language models (LLMs) have enabled natural language understanding and generation for clinical text summarization and draft note generation. Several studies have reported that LLMs can encode clinically meaningful knowledge and generate coherent medical text [5,6]. Despite this potential, LLM-based systems pose recognized risks, including the generation of factually incorrect content, or “hallucinations,” and susceptibility to automation bias. These risks underscore the need for explicit safety mechanisms and clear delineation of human responsibility in clinical settings [7–9].

Existing LLM applications in clinical documentation have largely focused on summarization or handoff-style text generation. Less attention has been given to workflow-specific documentation tasks that require structured communication between departments, including ED interdepartmental consultation requests.

The present study addresses a narrower but clinically consequential workflow task: drafting ED interdepartmental consultation requests from minimal natural language input. The proposed system differs from general-purpose note generation tools by combining knowledge-guided prompting with structured output generation in a single pipeline, including a patient summary, recommended department, consultation draft, and clinical reference notes.

Accordingly, this study aimed to develop a custom GPT-based, knowledge-guided documentation support system and conduct a pilot evaluation of its feasibility, usability, and integration into clinical workflow, with a focus on documentation support rather than autonomous decision-making.

Methods

System Overview

The system was designed as a natural language-driven consultation documentation support tool. Clinicians provided minimal natural language input describing the patient, typically including age, chief concern, and key clinical findings, such as “66-year-old female, generalized weakness, creatinine 2.7 mg/dL.”

The system was implemented as a knowledge-guided prompting pipeline using a custom GPT within the OpenAI GPTs platform. Based on the input, structured prompts were constructed using AI Hub-derived mapping cues and clinical reference information and then processed by a GPT-4–series LLM (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA). Because the GPTs platform provides limited low-level parameter control, model behavior was controlled primarily through prompt design. Output consistency was therefore promoted through structured prompt constraints rather than explicit parameter settings, such as temperature adjustment.

The system generated four structured outputs: (1) a patient summary, (2) a recommended consulting department, (3) a consultation draft, and (4) clinical reference notes. These outputs were formatted using predefined templates aligned with Korean ED documentation practices. All generated outputs were intended for physician review and editing before use. A representative prompt structure is provided in Supplement A. The system was designed to support clinical documentation rather than perform autonomous clinical decision-making, and final clinical judgment remained the clinician’s responsibility (Figures 1 and 2).

Knowledge Sources and Mapping

To reinforce clinical consistency and contextual relevance, two publicly available medical knowledge datasets provided by AI Hub were incorporated as reference knowledge sources: the Essential Medical Knowledge Dataset for emergency and critical care (Dataset No. 71875) and the Specialized Medical Knowledge Dataset (Dataset No. 71874) [10,11] (Table 1). These datasets provide structured disease-symptom-department relationships and specialty-specific medical knowledge, which were used to guide prompting and constrain LLM outputs without model fine-tuning or direct retrieval-based lookup. Representative AI Hub JSON-based knowledge examples are provided in Supplement B.

Standards and Interoperability Considerations

To support potential future interoperability, the generated consultation outputs were structured to allow conceptual mapping to HL7 FHIR Release 4 (R4) resources, such as ServiceRequest, including elements for patient context, clinical problem summary, requested department, priority, and rationale [12]. The current prototype was designed for clinician review and copy-and-paste use rather than direct EHR integration, and this mapping was illustrative rather than used for real-time clinical deployment. A representative HL7 FHIR R4 ServiceRequest JSON example is provided in Supplement C.

Validation Dataset

We evaluated 22 de-identified ED consultation cases obtained from a tertiary hospital. The cases represented diverse clinical presentations requiring consultation across multiple specialty departments, including internal medicine subspecialties, surgical specialties, and neurology. The 22 cases covered 12 destination departments, as summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Department information was removed from each case, and the system’s recommended department was compared with the consulting department documented in the original medical record. No independent adjudication was performed; therefore, the reference standard reflects concordance with historical practice rather than validated clinical optimality. The same scenarios were also used for prototype demonstrations and qualitative feedback collection.

Survey Design and Data Collection

A post-use survey was administered to ED physicians and residents after hands-on system use through an anonymous online questionnaire. Ten respondents completed the survey. The questionnaire included 5-point Likert-scale items assessing perceived time savings, clinical appropriateness, usability, reflection of relevant information, patient safety, and intention to recommend or continue use (Table 2).

Open-ended feedback was also collected. Survey data were collected over a 1-week period in December 2025.

Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize Likert-scale responses. Open-ended responses were analyzed thematically to identify common improvement themes. No formal statistical inference was performed because the pilot validation set was small and feasibility-oriented, and department-matching results were summarized as overall concordance rather than by full confusion matrix analysis. An exact 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated for the observed concordance to provide statistical context despite the small sample size.

Ethical Considerations

According to institutional policy, studies involving de-identified retrospective scenarios and anonymous minimal-risk physician surveys qualify for exemption from formal Institutional Review Board review, as no identifiable patient data were collected and no intervention was performed.

Results

Respondent Characteristics

All 10 survey respondents were emergency medicine physicians from a single tertiary hospital. Most respondents were attending physicians (n = 8, 80%), and two were residents (20%). Half of the respondents had more than 10 years of clinical experience since licensure (Table 3).

Department Recommendation Accuracy

In the 22-case pilot validation set, the system’s department recommendation matched the historically documented consulting department in every case (100.0%; exact 95% CI, 84.6%–100.0%) under controlled conditions. Given the small sample size, single-center design, and feasibility-oriented evaluation strategy, this finding should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of workflow alignment rather than definitive validation of clinical performance. This result may reflect the structured knowledge-guided prompting approach and the limited diversity of the dataset.

Survey Findings

Survey responses were generally favorable across evaluation domains (Table 4). Time savings was rated as agree or strongly agree by five of 10 respondents (50%), clinical appropriateness by eight of 10 respondents (80%), and usability by all respondents (100%). Reflection of relevant information was rated as agree or strongly agree by five of 10 respondents (50%), with one respondent indicating disagreement. The perceived patient safety impact was positive for seven of 10 respondents (70%), whereas the remaining respondents reported neutral perceptions.

Intention to recommend and intention to continue use were positive for seven of 10 respondents (70%) and six of 10 respondents (60%), respectively. One respondent expressed no intention to continue use (Table 4). Usage frequency and primary usage scenarios among respondents are summarized in Figure 3. Editing requirements were predominantly minor: seven respondents reported minor edits, two reported moderate edits, and one reported no edits (Figure 4).

Discussion

This study shows the potential of a custom GPT-based documentation support system to standardize ED interdepartmental consultation drafting and support workflow consistency under physician oversight. Accordingly, the system aligns more closely with documentation support than with traditional decision-making CDSSs. By using knowledge-guided prompting, the system supports structured reasoning and consultation rationale generation rather than simple text generation.

By integrating structured clinical knowledge from AI Hub into the LLM pipeline, the system demonstrated feasibility under knowledge-guided prompting while still requiring physician review to address incomplete or context-specific information. These findings support a knowledge-guided documentation support approach rather than autonomous clinical decision-making [10,11].

Unlike prior rule-based CDSSs, which require extensive manual curation of clinical rules, the proposed system uses pretrained LLM capabilities while constraining outputs through knowledge-guided prompting. This approach may reduce development overhead while maintaining clinical relevance. It also represents a pragmatic middle ground between fully autonomous artificial intelligence (AI) systems and traditional rule-based decision support.

The evaluation strategy used in this study adopts a staged, context-sensitive framing rather than immediate large-scale performance claims. Magrabi et al. [13] highlighted key methodological challenges in evaluating AI-enabled CDSSs. Khairat et al. [14] emphasized that physician acceptance and usability are important determinants of CDSS adoption. van de Sande et al. [15] proposed a step-by-step approach to the development, implementation, and governance of medical AI. Accordingly, the observed concordance should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of feasibility under controlled conditions rather than proof of generalizable clinical performance.

Survey findings indicated high perceived usability and overall acceptability among participating physicians, suggesting preliminary practical usability within ED workflows under controlled evaluation conditions. Respondents also emphasized that AI-generated outputs should function as assistive drafts rather than replacements for physician judgment. This observation is consistent with prior CDSS research highlighting the importance of maintaining clinician responsibility and avoiding automation bias in decision-support systems [2,3,8].

However, not all respondents were fully satisfied. One respondent reported inadequate reflection of relevant information, and one expressed no intention to continue use. These responses indicate a need for further refinement of specialty-specific content generation and suggest that individual workflow preferences may influence adoption. Related qualitative themes are summarized in Supplement D. For example, abnormal laboratory trends and longitudinal clinical context were not always fully captured in the generated drafts, although they may be critical for specialty consultation decision-making.

Operational reliability is another critical consideration. In time-sensitive ED environments, system latency, network instability, or downtime may introduce clinically meaningful delays. Prior work has emphasized the limitations of relying solely on clinician vigilance as an AI safety strategy, reinforcing the need for robust fail-safe mechanisms, redundancy strategies, and clearly defined fallback workflows [9]. Participants also identified the need for richer specialty-specific content and tighter EHR integration to improve clinical utility.

Although the present study focused on interdepartmental consultation within the ED, the design principles of the proposed documentation support system are not inherently limited to this setting. The system may be extended to outpatient referral workflows, inpatient ward consultations, and interhospital transfer or repatriation processes, in which standardized documentation and clear communication are critical for continuity of care.

Extension to these additional clinical settings would require contextual adaptation of the knowledge base, workflow integration tailored to each environment, and dedicated validation studies. Nevertheless, the present findings suggest that combining LLMs with knowledge-guided prompting and explicit safety mechanisms may provide a flexible foundation for future studies in broader referral and consultation workflows.

This study has several limitations. First, the validation dataset was small (n = 22 cases) and derived from a single institution, which may restrict generalizability to other clinical settings or patient populations. Second, survey responses were obtained from a small number of physicians (n = 10), limiting statistical power and the ability to detect subgroup differences. Third, all respondents were from emergency medicine; therefore, the perceptions of consulting specialists who receive these AI-assisted requests were not evaluated. Fourth, the retrospective evaluation design does not fully capture the time pressure and decision complexity of real-time ED workflows, and actual time savings were not measured. Finally, long-term reliability, potential automation complacency, and effects on clinical reasoning were not assessed. Future studies should incorporate quantitative evaluation metrics such as confusion matrices and inter-rater agreement analysis.

In addition, the custom GPT-based system operates within a proprietary environment in which explicit control over model parameters, such as temperature, is not available. The exact model version cannot be fixed, and underlying model updates may occur without notice, potentially causing output variability for identical inputs. These factors may affect the reproducibility and long-term stability of the system.

Furthermore, the validation dataset was purposively selected for feasibility evaluation, which may introduce selection bias. It remains unclear whether the included cases fully represent diagnostically complex scenarios, such as patients with multimorbidity or competing specialty considerations. Therefore, the selected cases may not fully reflect the complexity distribution of real-world ED consultation workflows.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the feasibility of a custom GPT-based documentation support tool for ED interdepartmental consultation requests. By combining LLMs with knowledge-guided prompting and explicit safety constraints, the system was perceived by emergency physicians as usable and clinically appropriate for first-draft consultation support in a pilot evaluation.

The current evaluation focused on ED workflows; however, with further validation, the approach may be extended to outpatient referrals, inpatient consultations, and interhospital transfers. Larger, multi-institutional studies are warranted to confirm safety, generalizability, and clinical impact.
