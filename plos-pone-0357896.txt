Impact mechanisms of high-speed railway accessibility on urban expansion in China: An analysis based on the mediating effect model

Dan He, Zixuan Chen, Jing Zhou, Yiman Hou, Qimeng Li, Zihang Qin

ABSTRACT
High-speed railway (HSR) has emerged as a transformative force in China, markedly reshaping urban development and spatial structures. Here, we examine the indirect mechanisms through which enhanced HSR connectivity influences urban land expansion. We construct national HSR networks for 2011, 2014, and 2017, and assess city-level accessibility based on network structure, direct connectivity, and service frequency. Urban expansion is measured using the average annual intensity of urban land growth during the periods 2011–2014, 2014–2017, and 2017–2020,taking data from 278 cities in China. Using a mediation and interaction effect models, we explore how HSR accessibility has influenced urban expansion through multiple pathways, including real estate investment, employment density, and industrial restructuring. The results reveal a fundamental paradigm shift: rather than simply stimulating sprawl, high HSR network centrality acts as a “structural brake valve” that significantly inhibits extensive and disorderly outward expansion, driving cities toward compact development. Mechanism analyses indicate that high internal employment density effectively reinforces this centripetal inhibitory effect. Conversely, increased HSR service frequency triggers a strong “masking effect” by stimulating real estate investment, which drives short-term spatial sprawl and obscures the network’s intrinsic compacting tendency. However, interaction models confirm that as the HSR network matures, frequent services combined with capital investment reverse this sprawl, guiding development toward high-density Transit-Oriented Development (TOD).Our findings underscore that HSR has transitioned from a catalyst for urban sprawl to a key driver of intensive, stock-based urban transformation, offering critical policy insights for coordinating transportation infrastructure with sustainable land-use planning and curbing local “land finance” impulses.

Introduction China has entered a new era of large-scale high-speed railway(HSR) development. No longer merely a symbol of progress, HSR has become an indispensable element of daily inter-city travel for millions of residents. With the rapid expansion of HSR lines, an increasing number of cities are being woven into the network, continuously transforming its structure and redefining urban accessibility [ 1 – 3 ]. Owing to its exceptional speed, HSR generates a space‒time compression effect that markedly reduces the cost of inter-city travel [ 4 ]. Consequently, HSR eliminates barriers to inter-city travel, broadens the travel horizons of residents, augments the frequency of inter-city journeys, and ultimately alters mobility patterns [ 5 , 6 ]. Changes in inter-city mobility resulting from HSR development potentially influence urban economic performance, while simultaneously fostering economic integration among cities along HSR corridors [ 7 , 8 ]. Such shifts in urban economies and demographic dynamics typically precipitate alterations in land use [ 9 ], and improvements in the accessibility of cities and HSR stations stimulate the economic development of cities. Therefore, HSR exerts a profound impact on land use efficiency and, in particular, on urban expansion [ 10 ]. Many countries, including China, are undergoing rapid urbanization. In China alone, the area of urban land expanded fourfold between 1990 and 2020. The continuous expansion of urban land is essential to meet the space demands of both the residential and industrial sectors, as well as to achieve the objective of economic growth. However, this expansion inevitably encroaches upon cultivated land, vegetation and other land types, which may have profound implications for the urban ecological environment. Such land transformations often result in a range of urban challenges, including environmental degradation and land-use conflicts. Consequently, understanding the dynamics of urban expansion has emerged as an important research focus in urban sustainability studies. Population growth, economic development, industrial structure and transportation development are particularly influential drivers of urban expansion [ 11 ]. In particular, transportation accessibility is widely recognized as a fundamental driving force of urban expansion, because changes in accessibility affect location choices, redistributing economic activities and production factor inputs [ 12 ]. High-speed railway is regarded as a transformative force in redefining urban accessibility. Consequently, growing research attention has been directed toward investigating the relationship between HSR development and urban expansion. Current research in this domain primarily focuses on the impact on urban expansion of opening new HSR lines, the frequency of HSR services, and the location of HSR stations in cities. Such studies indicate that both the opening of a new HSR line and its service frequency exert a substantial influence on urban expansion [ 13 – 15 ]. Moreover, the opening time of HSR significantly affects the speed of urban land-use conversion and accelerates the loss of agricultural land [ 16 ]. Although He et al. [ 17 ] explored the characteristics and impacts of the HSR network on the urban structures of major urban agglomerations within China, several knowledge gaps within this field remain. First, previous studies have neglected the perspective of the HSR network when examining the impact of HSR accessibility on urban expansion. Cities are places in which various flow factors are exchanged; the urban spatial structure is reshaped by flow factors, and the land-use system undergoes corresponding changes [ 18 ]. In this context, the HSR network intensifies the movement of factors between cities, links resources that are otherwise scattered, and facilitates resource sharing and collaborative efforts among urban centers [ 19 ]. The accessibility of cities in the HSR network represents the ability of cities to gather dispersed resources and exert spillover effects [ 19 ], potentially affecting the intensity of urban expansion. However, the current literature remains limited in its exploration of this particular influence. Second, there is a notable dearth of empirical research on the impact mechanism(s) of HSR accessibility in relation to urban expansion. Existing studies predominantly rely on statistical analyses and theoretical models to explain the effects of HSR introduction and general accessibility, but they frequently overlook empirical validation of the socio-economic pathways mediating this relationship. This oversight is critical because, beyond its direct effects, HSR accessibility may also indirectly drive urban expansion by modulating other pivotal variables. Here, we aim to bridge the aforementioned knowledge gaps. First, we construct a comprehensive national HSR network and interpret four accessibility indicators(closeness centrality (CC), betweenness centrality (BC), degree (DG), and the frequency of HSR stops in a city (FRE) for each city within the network to explore the impact of HSR accessibility on urban expansion. Second, we build an mediating effect model, and select the mediating variables of employment density (EMP), investment in real estate development (IRE), and proportion of added value of the tertiary industry (TER) to explore the impact mechanism of HSR accessibility on urban expansion. The core problem we aim to solve is to unravel the mechanism by which HSR accessibility drives urban expansion. Our results are expected to provide valuable insights for governmental bodies in forecasting future trends in urban land use and facilitating refined management in the era of the HSR network. Furthermore, this study offers practical guidance for land management and urban planning departments to optimize urban growth boundaries and effectively regulate spatial expansion, facilitating a strategic transition from extensive land consumption toward sustainable, compact urban development. Reviewing the impact mechanism of HSR on urban expansion The proliferation of HSR networks has emerged as a critical driver of urban spatial expansion, operating through interconnected demographic, economic, and related channels. Accessibility improvements induced by HSR development initiate a cascade of spatial‒economic transformations. Reduced inter-city travel costs and time compression effects fundamentally alter the mobility patterns of various factors, enabling accelerated flows of labor, capital, and technology across city networks [ 20 , 21 ]. High-speed railway facilitates urban expansion by attracting people to cities. The introduction of HSR and enhancement of service frequency have markedly reduced travel time and expenses, thereby augmenting urban accessibility and competitiveness [ 13 , 14 , 22 ]. This improved transportation network facilitates the agglomeration of both population and commercial activities, thus generating additional employment opportunities [ 23 ]. In turn, these improved employment prospects draw labor from surrounding regions to cities served by HSR, further driving urban development and expansion [ 5 , 11 , 24 ]. The resulting concentration of population increases the demand for housing and public services, thereby stimulating real estate development and infrastructure construction, which ultimately stimulates further urban expansion [ 14 , 25 ]. Population agglomeration increases the cost of living in city centers causing the population to further concentrate in the suburbs, where land prices are lower. Ultimately, this increases the demand for housing and drives urban expansion in the suburbs [ 16 ]. In addition to fostering population agglomeration, HSR can also promote industrial agglomeration. High-speed railway improves inter-regional accessibility, strengthens urban influence, expands the urban service radius, and integrates internal and external markets, expanding market size and bringing greater market potential. These factors collectively enhance urban competitiveness, attracting companies and fostering the formation of industrial clusters [ 13 , 16 , 26 ]. The time‒space compression effect of HSR contributes to economies of scale and reduces production costs, thereby reducing transaction and communication expenses. This, in turn, enhances the movement of essential production factors, including labor, capital, and technology [ 24 ]. In this way, the reduction in transaction costs further reinforces industrial agglomeration [ 16 , 27 ]. High-speed railway networks connect resources dispersed across different cities; and the accessibility of a city in the HSR network represents its capacity to concentrate resources and generate spillover effects [ 19 ]. As the HSR network continues to evolve, increasing urban accessibility strengthens inter-city synergy and integration [ 28 ], This process fosters investment agglomeration, enhances borrowed size effects, and ultimately promotes industrial agglomeration [ 19 ]. Industrial agglomeration increases the demand for urban construction land [ 13 ], promotes real estate investment, and consequently results in urban expansion [ 5 ]. Structural economic transformations further compound urban expansion. High-speed railway strengthens inter-city flows of production factors [ 29 , 30 ]; notably, the production factors and products of the secondary and tertiary industries have higher mobility than those associated with primary industry [ 13 ]. This mobility differential drives structural transformation towards non-agricultural industries, accelerating the conversion of agricultural land to urban uses and stimulating metropolitan expansion. Service industries particularly benefit from HSR-induced agglomeration economies, resulting in increased service sector shares at the expense of manufacturing [ 31 ]. Manufacturing industries exhibit greater cost sensitivity [ 31 ]; hence, the industrial agglomeration caused by HSR development increases the pressure of competition, with the market crowding effect forcing companies to choose locations with fewer competitors [ 16 ]. This dynamic often directs manufacturing firms towards the urban periphery. Consequently, for HSR stations located in suburban areas, lower factory construction costs in these locations serve as a strong incentive for manufacturers to establish operations nearby, capitalizing on infrastructure advantages while optimizing investment returns [ 24 ]. In short, HSR development directly results in the conversion of agricultural land into urban land through the upgrading of industrial structure. Meanwhile, the expansion of the service sector necessitates the development of complementary real estate and infrastructure. The increasing land costs brought about by agglomeration further promote the spatial relocation of manufacturing and drive urban land expansion in suburbs [ 18 ]. Underpinning these phenomena lies the capacity of HSR to reconfigure spatial‒economic equilibria. High-speed railway promotes urban expansion through economic growth channels, including improved investment efficiency, enhanced enterprise production efficiency, and increased household consumption. It also reduces travel costs and fosters the agglomeration of production factors, such as industry and labor force. This generates agglomeration benefits and also creates a knowledge spillover effect, facilitating knowledge exchange and mutual learning among companies. Consequently, this enhances the innovation capacity and productivity of enterprises and improves the overall competitiveness of cities [ 31 – 33 ]. The cost-reduction effects induced by HSR enable firms to pursue specialized investments across cities, enhancing regional labor division and industrial productivity through strategic market segmentation. Improved inter-city connectivity facilitates rapid information acquisition and face-to-face negotiations between investors and policymakers, significantly optimizing capital allocation and operational decisions [ 34 ]. Hence, HSR enables enterprises to reduce investment costs, improve decision-making efficiency, attract more high-quality investment, and improve urban production efficiency [ 33 ]. Time savings induced by HSR expand the leisure mobility of residents, stimulating cross-city consumption patterns [ 13 , 35 ] that drive the development of consumer service industries through scale effects [ 26 , 36 ]. Ultimately, HSR promotes the development of the urban economy by boosting consumption vitality and production efficiency, then accelerates the demand for urban construction land and the intensity of urban expansion [ 12 , 13 ]. Theoretically, HSR fosters urban expansion by enhancing accessibility and the inter-city flow of production factors. However, although the link between HSR and urban growth is well-established [ 12 , 13 ], the internal transmission mechanisms remain underexplored. The existing literature emphasizes pro-growth effects but largely neglects the ways in which HSR-induced sectoral shifts might also inhibit or reshape urban expansion. This oversight obscures the dual-faceted nature of the impact of HSR, creating a critical knowledge gap. A new perspective on this problem is offered by complex network theory, which uses metrics such as centrality and degree to correlate the network position of a city with its development [ 17 , 19 , 21 , 37 ]; however, the underlying logic remains a “black box.” The specific economic channels, their relative intensity, and the conditions under which network connectivity translates into physical land expansion still require rigorous, model-based validation [ 38 ]. To address these knowledge gaps, we here develop a mechanism-oriented framework. We employ a mediation effect model that integrates HSR network indicators with key economic and demographic variables to systematically test the direct and indirect pathways from network connectivity to urban expansion, thereby quantifying the strength and direction of these transmission mechanisms. Ultimately, this research provides crucial theoretical insights and empirical evidence to support more nuanced and effective coordinated transportation and land-use policies in the context of rapid urbanization. Closeness centrality Closeness centrality measures the extent to which a node is close to all other nodes along the shortest paths, reflecting its accessibility within a given network. The CC of a node is calculated as the inverse of the average shortest distance from that node i to all other nodes in a given network. A larger CC value indicates greater convenience in reaching other nodes [ 40 ]. In a HSR network, a higher CC signifies that the city of interest is more accessible to other cities. C C ( i ) = n- 1 ∑ v j ∈ V , i ≠ j d i j , (2) where d ij represents the shortest path between nodes i and j ; V = {v i : i = 1, 2,···, n}, n = |V|, and n is the number of nodes. Betweenness centrality Betweenness centrality is defined as the probability that a node lies on the shortest path between two other nodes. This index reflects the probability of a city serving as a crucial transfer point between two other cities that are not directly connected, highlighting its mediating role and control within the HSR network [ 40 ]. A higher city BC value of a city indicates that the city is located on the shortest paths connecting numerous city pairs; this demonstrates a significant transit impact and control capacity, as well as high accessibility. C B ( i ) = ∑ k ≠ i ≠ j ∈ N σ k j ( i ) / σ k j , (3) where C B (i) represents the BC of node i ; σ kj represents the sum of shortest paths between nodes k and j ; and σ kj ( i ) represents the number of shortest paths from node k to j through node i . Degree The DG of a node is defined as the number of edges directly connected to that node within a complex network. In the HSR network, the DG of a city represents the number of cities directly connected to it by HSR. A higher DG indicates greater accessibility, meaning that more cities can be reached directly via HSR from the city of interest. D G i = ∑ j = 1 , j ≠ i n L i j , (4) where DG i represents the DG of city i , L ij represents the number of sides directly connected between city i and city j , L ij = 1 indicates that there is a direct HSR link between i and j , L ij = 0 indicates that there is no direct HSR link between i and j ; and n indicates the total number of cities. Weighted degree The DG examines the accessibility of each city within the HSR network by assessing the presence of direct HSR connections between cities, but neglects the frequency of HSR services. In contrast, the WD metric accounts for the existence of direct HSR links and also incorporates the weight of the HSR network, specifically the frequency of HSR services between cities. A higher WD indicates a greater frequency of direct HSR connections between the city of interest and other cities, thereby providing a more comprehensive reflection of the HSR accessibility of a given city. W D i = ∑ j = 1 , j ≠ i n w i j , (5) where WD i represents the WD of city i , w ij represents the frequency of direct HSR services between city i and city j ; and n represents the total number of cities. Mediating effect model The HSR accessibility not only has a direct influence on the intensity of urban expansion, it also has an indirect affect through socioeconomic variables. These socioeconomic variables serve as mediating factors. Hence, the construction of a mediating effect model is essential to understand the impact mechanism(s) of HSR accessibility on urban expansion. Referring to previous literature [ 19 , 41 – 43 ], equations for the mediating effect model are presented as follows: Y i t = β 0 + c X i t + γ C o n t r o l s i t + μ i + λ t + ε i t (6) M i t = β 0 + a X i t + γ C o n t r o l s i t + μ i + λ t + ε i t (7) Y i t = β 0 + b M i t + c ′ X i t + γ C o n t r o l s i t + μ i + λ t + ε i t (8) Equations (7) and (8) together constitute the test model for the mediating effect. Where: the subscripts i and t denote the city and year, respectively; Y i t is the urban land expansion intensity (AUEI); X i t represents the multidimensional indicators of HSR network accessibility (CC, BC, DG, FRE); M i t represents the mediating variables (EMP, IRE, TER); and C o n t r o l s i t denotes the set of control variables. β 0 is the constant intercept term; γ is the coefficient vector for the control variables; μ i represents the city fixed effect, capturing time-invariant, unobservable city-specific characteristics; λ t represents the time fixed effect, accounting for macroscopic shocks common to all cities in specific years; and ε i t is the random error term. The coefficient c represents the total effect of the explanatory variable on the dependent variable (AUEI). The coefficient a represents the effect of the explanatory variable on the mediating variable, while the coefficient b represents the effect of the mediating variable on the dependent variable after controlling for the explanatory variable. The coefficient c’ represents the direct effect of the explanatory variable on the dependent variable after controlling for the mediating variable. The relationship can be expressed as c = c’ + ab , where ab reflects the indirect effects of the explanatory variables on the dependent variables [ 41 ]. To eliminate complex bias interferences caused by random noise, intercept terms, and spatiotemporal heterogeneities in the original algebraic expansion, this model introduces city fixed effects and time fixed effects. By setting a zero intercept and following the statistical principle that the mean of residual terms is zero, the analysis focuses on the path coefficients that reflect marginal changes between variables [ 43 ], thereby ensuring a strict additive equivalence relationship between the total effect, direct effect, and indirect effect ( c = c ′ + a b ), laying a solid econometric theoretical foundation for subsequent parameter estimation and significance testing of effect values. Chang (2022) used a mediating effects model to analyze the impact of digital finance on agricultural carbon emissions, which supports the feasibility of our model design [ 42 ]. Indirect effects encompass both mediating and suppressing effects. The conceptual figure of the mediating-effect model is shown in Fig 3 . 10.1371/journal.pone.0357896.g003 Fig 3 Mediating-effect model conceptual figure. The analysis flow is as follows [ 44 ]: Step 1: The coefficient c of Eq. (6) is checked. If it is significant, the analysis can proceed to the next step; if not, the result is discussed in terms of a suppressing effect. Step 2: The coefficients a of Eq. (7) and b of Eq. (8) are tested successively. If both coefficients are significant, the indirect effect is considered significant, and the analysis proceeds to Step 4. If at least one of the coefficients is not significant, the analysis proceeds to Step 3. Step 3: The bootstrap method is used to test the significance of ab . This method works by repeatedly resampling the dataset (e.g., 5,000 times) to estimate the distribution of ab. On this basis, a confidence interval is calculated. If the confidence interval does not include zero, the indirect effect is significant. Compared with traditional methods such as the Sobel test, the bootstrap method is more accurate and reliable, especially when the sample size is relatively small or the data distribution is non-normal. If the result is significant, the analysis continues to Step 4. If not, it stops because there is no evidence of a meaningful indirect effect. Relevant references are given here to support this approach [ 41 , 45 ]. Step 4: The coefficient c’ of Eq. (8) is checked. If it is not significant, meaning that the direct effect is not significant, this indicates that there is only a mediating effect. If it is significant, indicating that the direct effect is significant, the analysis proceeds to the Step 5. Step 5: The signs of ab and c’ are compared. If the two signs are the same, a mediating effect is indicated, and the proportion of the mediating effect in the total effect ab/c is reported. If the signs are different, a suppressing effect is indicated, and the absolute value of the proportion of the suppressing effect to the direct effect |ab/c’| is reported. These factors may also influence urban expansion; hence, it is inferred that HSR will affect the intensity of urban expansion by influencing the aforementioned factors. Mediating effect of CC on urban expansion Using CC as the explanatory variable, and EMP, IRE, and TER as mediating variables, successive mediating mechanism tests were conducted with the results shown in Table 4 (numbers in parentheses represent standard deviations; same for Tables 5‒7 below). The second column in Table 4 records the total effect of CC on urban expansion, with a coefficient of −0.1497 (p < 0.01), indicating that an increase in HSR network CC can significantly inhibit extensive urban outward expansion. When the mediating variable was EMP, the regression coefficient of CC on EMP is −0.0445, and the regression coefficient of EMP on urban expansion is 0.2575. After adding EMP, the direct effect coefficient of CC on urban expansion is −0.1383, all of which pass the significance test. This indicates that an increase in CC will reduce EMP, and a decrease in EMP will in turn inhibit urban expansion. Therefore, CC can further inhibit urban expansion by reducing the degree of employment agglomeration. Since the direction of the indirect effect is consistent with the direct effect, EMP plays a partial mediating role in this process. When the mediating variable was IRE, the regression coefficients of CC on IRE is −0.0352, and the regression coefficient of IRE on urban expansion is 0.1600. After adding IRE, the direct effect coefficient of CC on urban expansion becomes −0.1441, all of which pass the significance test. This indicates that an increase in CC inhibits IRE, thereby indirectly reducing urban expansion. Since the direction of the indirect effect is consistent with the direct effect, IRE plays a partial mediating role in this process. When the mediating variable was TER, the regression coefficients of CC on TER is 0.0155, which fails to pass the significance test, indicating that the mediating effect of TER is not significant. This implies that the enhancement of HSR closeness centrality does not significantly act on overall urban expansion by influencing industrial structure upgrading. Mediating effect of BC on urban expansion The mediating mechanism of BC on urban expansion was tested using EMP, IRE and TER, with the results are shown in Table 5 . The second column in Table 5 reports the total effect of BC on urban expansion, with a coefficient of −0.0921, passing the 5% significance test, indicating that the enhancement of urban hub control capability helps guide compact spatial development. When the mediating variable is EMP, the regression coefficient of BC on EMP is 0.0147, which does not pass the significance test; therefore, the mediating effect of EMP is not significant. When IRE served as the mediating variable, the regression coefficients of BC on IRE is 0.0806, and the regression coefficient of IRE on urban expansion is 0.2494. After adding IRE, the direct effect coefficient of BC on urban expansion becomes −0.1122, all of which pass the significance tests. This indicates that an increase in BC can significantly promote IRE, and IRE will further promote urban expansion. Therefore, although the enhancement of HSR hub status overall inhibits urban expansion, it also forms a certain expansion effect by stimulating real estate investment at the same time. Since the indirect effect is positive while the direct effect is negative, and their directions are opposite, investment in real estate development exhibits a masking effect. The size of the masking effect is 17.92%, meaning it weakens the negative impact of BC on urban expansion. When TER was the mediating variable, the regression coefficient of BC on TER is −0.0015, which fails to pass the significance test; thus, the mediating effect of TER is not significant. Discussion Influence mechanism of HSR accessibility on urban expansion First, network centrality exerts an overall inhibitory effect on urban expansion. The regression results demonstrate that the total effects of the three HSR network centrality indicators (CC, BC, and DG) on urban expansion are all significantly negative, which indicates that the higher a city’s topological position within the national HSR network, the more pronounced its spatial intensification becomes. High centrality accelerates cross-regional factor flows through space-time compression; when internal urban development matures, the blind flow of factors from the center to the periphery is curbed, constraining disorderly urban peripheral land expansion as a whole. Second, the mediating variables present distinct transmission and regulatory boundaries. EMP features a mechanism of reinforcing agglomeration. Empowered by the HSR network, labor further clusters and undergoes vertical restructuring around stations, amplifying the inhibition of urban expansion. Conversely, IRE exhibits a strong masking effect: an increase in FRE stimulates the growth of IRE, concealing the compact trend. However, along with the maturation of the HSR network, it can significantly constrain the blind expansion of real estate capital into cities, guiding real estate to shift toward high-plot-ratio TOD complexes around transportation hubs. Furthermore, the elevation of HSR centrality does not significantly alter TER, implying that HSR’s shaping of urban form relies more on factors with spatial characteristics rather than simple transformations of the macro industrial structure. Policy implications The result of “HSR centrality and frequency inhibit urban expansion” obtained in this study reflects the Chinese government’s control and adjustment of land use during the city’s transition from “incremental expansion” to “stock intensification.” HSR network centrality possesses a powerful structural containment capability over urban expansion, and the government should fully unleash hub dividends. Especially in core node cities with high network positions, blind and unnecessary planning of peripheral new towns and development zones should be strictly restricted, commercial, business, and public services should be guided to gather around stations, and high-intensity TOD comprehensive development should be conducted around HSR stations, converting transportation nodes into anchors of comprehensive urban economic vitality. While the increase in HSR shifts boosts real estate investment and drives spatial sprawl, local governments should carefully evaluate the short-term benefits brought by “land finance.” The increase in HSR frequency should be combined with total land supply restrictions to guide real estate capital away from low-density expansion toward multi-functional, vertically intensive development. Combining the interaction effect results, relevant industrial and infrastructure resources should be preferentially tilted toward node cities with a certain labor foundation, so as to take the lead in achieving a benign transformation of HSR dividends and maximizing the policy spillover effect of intensive and economical spatial utilization. Future work In this study, we explored the impact mechanism of multi-dimensional accessibility in the HSR network on urban expansion, but the selection of mediating variables presents certain limitations. Changes in HSR accessibility influence the flow of innovation factors, and shifts in innovation capacity can subsequently impact urban expansion. Future research should incorporate relevant indicators of scientific and technological innovation, such as the number of patents granted, investments in scientific research and development, and urban innovation vitality. Meanwhile, longer time series data can be introduced in the future to precisely calculate the non-linear characteristics of HSR’s inhibitory effect on urban expansion at different developmental stages and under different scale thresholds. Conclusion Based on the panel data of 278 prefecture-level cities in China in 2011, 2014, and 2017, this paper constructs mediating effect and interaction effect models to systematically reveal the impact of HSR network characteristics on the intensity of urban spatial expansion and the mediating regulatory paths. The research indicates that: 1. HSR network centrality (CC, BC, DG) constitutes a structural “brake valve” that overall inhibits extensive and disorderly urban expansion. The “opening promotes sprawl” effect identified in traditional literature based on dummy variables has given way to the “centripetal agglomeration” and intensive convergence brought by high centrality during the network deepening period [ 38 ]. 2. This inhibitory path is regulated by the dual boundary conditions of internal employment density and external capital flows. A high level of employment density can effectively undertake and amplify the centripetal pull of centrality; although the increase in HSR frequency induces masking expansion through the mediating channel of real estate investment in the short term, as the operational network advances toward maturity, high-frequency shifts can significantly constrain and reverse the sprawl tendency of real estate investment through efficient interaction with capital. In conclusion, as China’s HSR moves from “single-line opening” to “deep networking,” its spatial effect is undergoing a fundamental shift, having become a key spatial driving force promoting the transition of Chinese urban land factors toward intensive, compact, and stock-based development.
