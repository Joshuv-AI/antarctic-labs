1.0 Executive Summary

This project advances the trapped-ion quantum simulator as a versatile platform for studying
open quantum system phenomena. We aim to contribute to the emerging quantum simulation
toolkits and enable simulation of nanoscale energy processes. Trapped-ion platforms offer
unique capabilities: their vibrational motion can be precisely manipulated, measured, and
coherently coupled to auxiliary qubits. The vibrational mode can function both as a highly
sensitive quantum sensor and a programmable environment bath. Using this platform, we
achieved three major outcomes. First, we demonstrated using the vibrational mode as an
ultrasensitive probe for testing fundamental physics, including possible nonlinear quantum
mechanics effects. Second, we established that these modes can act as controllable baths in
which tunable noise and loss can enhance or modify energy-transfer dynamics, providing the
experimental preparation toward studying mechanisms relevant to chemical reactions and light-
harvesting systems. Third, by introducing controllable nonlinear gain and loss, we
showed theoretically how simulations using trapped ions can model vibrationally-assisted energy
transport in a non‐Hermitian quantum system comprising a chromophore dimer weakly coupled
to a vibrational mode. Exploring the non‐Hermitian dynamics of the whole system including
vibrations, we found that energy transfer accompanied by absorption of phonons from a
vibrational mode can be significantly enhanced near an exceptional point. This theoretical work
on simulation of energy transfer processes in driven non‐Hermitian quantum systems revealed
an interesting novel path to study open quantum systems dynamics under conditions of gain and
loss. We then further explored the benefits of controllable gain and loss with an experimental
realization of quantum analogs of nonlinear oscillators, namely, the van der Pol oscillator. Here
we observed mutual synchronization mediated by collective dissipation between two oscillators.
In parallel, we explored related quantum networking protocols using the same trapped-ion
platform, developing fast, high-fidelity schemes for distributing entanglement. Together, these
achievements show that trapped-ion vibrational modes provide a highly programmable and
high-fidelity platform for investigating complex dissipative quantum behavior, while enabling
new approaches to remote quantum sensing, energy science, and nonlinear quantum dynamics.

3.0 Project Objectives
The goal of this project was to establish a platform for simulating open quantum system
dynamics using trapped ions. In particular, we planned to focus on using the vibrational mode as
a structured and tunable environment with specific spectral densities. Our plan was to develop
the theoretical framework and experimental controls needed for programmable noise
engineering, and then use these tools to experimentally study vibrationally assisted energy
transfer (VAET) and environment-assisted quantum transport (ENAQT) in a controllable setting. A
central objective was to investigate the quantitative and qualitative differences between classical
and quantum noise, since these differences play a key role in energy-transfer processes and
remain open questions in the field of open quantum systems.

To support these goals, we developed methods for synthesizing classical noise with a range of

spectral profiles and created the software infrastructure needed to program our ARTIQ hardware
as an arbitrary waveform generator. Using these tools, we generated white, pink, brown, and
Lorentzian noise in the parameter regimes relevant for implementing a phase-damped oscillator
and applied them to the laser fields driving the ions. Experiments demonstrated how these
engineered noise sources decohere Rabi oscillations, validating the behavior of a two-level
system under controlled noise. We also carried out preliminary studies of the dynamics of a two-
level system coupled to a harmonic oscillator with the presence of both quantum (VAET) and
classical (ENAQT) noise.

 To complement this work in developing engineered noise sources, we also undertook studies of
the effects of non-Markovian noise due to laser and frequency noise, and developed a method to
characterize non-Markovian errors in quantum logic gates, relevant to digital quantum
simulation. In addition, our theory team expanded the study of VAET with trapped ions to a non-
Hermitian quantum system comprising a PT -symmetric chromophore dimer weakly coupled to a
vibrational mode. They found that energy transfer associated with phonon absorption can be
significantly enhanced near an exceptional point where the system loss approaches the coupling
strength. This result shows that non-Hermitian structure can strengthen vibrationally assisted
transport and provides a new route for exploring open-quantum-system dynamics using trapped
ions. Experimentally, we have validated the non-Hermitian dynamics in a two-level system by
performing Rabi oscillations with system loss and implementing post-selection.

Beyond our main objective, we also used the vibrational modes themselves as resources for
quantum-state engineering in open systems. In this direction, we experimentally realized the van
der Pol (vdP) oscillator in both the near-classical and quantum regimes. The limit-cycle behavior
characteristic of the vdP oscillator was confirmed through motional-state reconstruction.
Through time evolution starting from an arbitrary initial state, we showed that the vdP state is
indeed the steady state under the programmed dissipations. Building on this, we prepared two
identical vdP oscillators using the two axial vibrational modes of a mixed-isotope ion chain and
demonstrated that they can be synchronized through the introduction of collective dissipation.
By adjusting the relative phase of the collective dissipative channel, the system could be tuned
continuously between synchronized and the anti-synchronized state, as revealed by joint
measurements of the two oscillators. We further investigated how synchronization responds to
changes in coupling strength and frequency detuning, identifying the characteristic “Arnold
tongue” structure that defines the stability region food synchronization. In addition, we studied
synchronization in the presence of an external electric field, where the resulting phase-locking
behavior suggests possible applications in quantum sensing. Taken together, these results
demonstrate limit-cycle synchronization in the quantum regime and provide a foundation for
exploring more complex synchronization phenomena in larger networks of oscillators, where the
persistence of quantum features remains an open question.

4.0 Description of Activities Performed
We theoretically and experimentally developed and demonstrated a trapped ion quantum
information processor for simulating open quantum system problems. In particular, we focused
on the vibrational degree of freedom in ion crystals. Over the course of the funding period, we
have shown that trapped ion motions can be used for a series of applications. Our main
achievements are threefold.

First, the bosonic motional mode can serve as a sensitive probe for tests of fundamental physics,
including experimental limits on proposed nonlinear extensions of quantum mechanics. Our
experiments published in Phys. Rev. Lett. 130, 200201, represent the most stringent bound on
nonlinear extensions of quantum mechanics that are compatible with causality. In particular, the
experiments constrain the size of a hypothetical interaction between two superposition states
seven orders of magnitude more than any other experiment. These experiments were enabled
by careful control of the motional degree of freedom of the ion and monitoring its energy as a
function of the superposition state.

The second result is that the bosonic degree of freedom can be used as a controllable
environmental bath for studying energy-transfer processes relevant to light-harvesting systems.
Here, we first undertook a theoretical study that explored how non-Hermitian effects and
exceptional points can enhance vibrationally assisted energy transfer using a trapped ion
quantum simulator. Phys. Rev. Research 6, 023149 (2024). Starting with a pair of trapped ions
modeling a chromophore dimer and adding controllable excitonic coupling engineered by a laser
beam along the axis of the trap, we showed that on adding gain and loss processes controlled by
additional laser heating and cooling, the ions constitute a non-Hermitian system with two
degenerate second-order exceptional points. Enabling a weak coupling of the ions to a
vibrational mode localized on the donor by a tightly focused laser allowed control of vibration-
assisted energy transfer from donor to acceptor ion, in which the ions absorb the energy of a
phonon from the vibration. This vibrationally assisted energy transfer (VAET) was shown to be
significantly enhanced for drive and coupling parameters that bring the full dimer-vibration
emulation close to the degenerate exceptional point in the non-Hermitian regime. Detailed
analysis showed that this enhancement of the VAET is a result of enhanced absorption of
phonons by the ions from the vibrational mode near the exceptional point. For this system, the
donor-vibration interaction is maximally favorable at the two-fold degenerate second-order
exceptional point, allowing four simultaneous transitions between eigenstates associated with
otherwise distinct exceptional points to be excited by a single phonon. The resulting energy
transfer efficiency was significantly higher than could be achieved with the corresponding
Hermitian system. This analysis further revealed a unique fluorescence-detected vibrational
spectroscopy approach to probing exceptional points and PT-symmetry phase transitions in non-
Hermitian systems, by monitoring the acceptor population by fluorescence detection at a given
vibrational frequency, i.e., a form of fluorescence-detected vibrational spectroscopy. First steps

 Page 4 of 10

towards experimental implementation of this VAET enhanced by proximity to exceptional points
of non-Hermitian Hamiltonians were made by validation of non-Hermitian dynamics in a two-
level system by implementing Rabi oscillations in the presence of system loss and implementing
post-selection.

Thirdly, we observed for the first time the phenomenon of synchronization of limit-cycles in the
quantum regime. Synchronization is a hallmark of collective behavior that emerges when
nonlinear systems interact, spanning scales from mechanical oscillators to planetary orbits. As a
universal phenomenon, it underpins the study of complex systems and has far-reaching
technological implications, but so far has never been observed in the quantum regime. As a
model system, we chose the well-known van der Pol oscillator that we implemented using
programmable gain and nonlinear loss to the motion of an ion chain. By adjusting the loss/gain
ratio, we could tune the system from the classical to the quantum regime. We were also able to
expand this work to two modes that allowed us to observe synchronization between two
oscillators. With this at hand, we explored phenomena like phase locking and mutual
synchronization. Beyond its fundamental importance for quantum-many body physics, these
phenomena may have applications in quantum sensing. This work is under review in PRX, and is
already available as a preprint: arXiv:2509.18423.

More generally, we established a versatile experimental toolbox to implement programmable
open-quantum-systems using trapped ions: we can apply well-calibrated dissipation to both the
motion and the electronic degree of freedom of ion chains while maintaining coherence of other
degrees of freedom. Further, we have created advanced capabilities to read-out multiple
motional degrees of freedom, including their correlations.

5.0 Conclusions and Recommendations

The project delivered a series of foundational advances in trapped-ion quantum information
science and open quantum system engineering. While these accomplishments are rooted in
fundamental physics, by establishing these new experimental platforms and analytical methods
for understanding complex, noisy, and non-equilibrium systems, the project contributes to a
growing knowledge base needed for next-generation quantum technologies. Specifically, it
improved understanding of open system dynamics, controlled and enhanced energy transfer at
the quantum scale, characterization of non-Markovian noise on trapped ion qubits, and remote
entanglement distribution schemes that can ultimately accelerate the development of advanced
sensing, secure communication networks, and high-efficiency information processing.

6.0 Lessons Learned
The most serious difficulties arose from the reliability of the ion-trap apparatus and unexpected
vacuum behavior. Shortly after the completion of the nonlinear quantum mechanics test, the trap
stopped functioning in early 2023. Months of troubleshooting were required before the team
identified a short to ground inside the vacuum chamber. After opening the chamber, repairing
the short, and rebaking the system, ion trapping was recovered. However, the repaired
apparatus exhibited an unusual behavior in which additional dark ions would intermittently
appear at a rate of roughly one every ten minutes. This phenomenon persisted despite repeated
mitigation efforts and was not observed in other systems. To avoid another lengthy vacuum
cycle, the group relied heavily on prolonged operation of the titanium sublimation pump and on
a high-power, far-detuned laser beam to improve Doppler-cooling robustness against collision
induced ion melting.These measures helped but did not fully resolve the vacuum issue. In late
2023 a further difficulty arose when another RF electrode became shorted to ground after a
vacuum break, reducing the achievable trap frequency by roughly a factor of two. This
constrained some parameter regimes needed for open-system simulations and slowed down
experimental progress. Occasional background gas collisions continued to disrupt long
experiments and added to the instability of the experiment.

A parallel challenge occurred when the graduate student who had developed and maintained
most of the experimental control infrastructure (J. Broz) graduated in June 2023. His departure
left the experiment without a functioning control framework, and substantial effort was required
to rebuild automated calibrations, timing routines, and day-to-day operational workflows.

The combination of hardware instability and the issues of the control system made it difficult to
pursue the originally planned open-system experiments. As a result, in late 2023 the group
shifted its focus toward the temporal-multiplexing experiment for quantum networking on a long
ion chain (arXiv:2405.10501). This direction was particularly suitable for this period because the
networking protocols relied more on precise timing and synchronization between ion-transport
operations and photon-generation attempts, and much less on the existing qubit and vibrational-
mode control infrastructure. The involvement of an intern, David Miron, who brought
professional software-engineering experience, allowed the group to move forward with the
multiplexing experiment while simultaneously rebuilding the control framework. IDuring this

After the multiplexing experiment concluded in June 2024, the lead graduate student, Bingran
You, left to join another project. A new team of Jiarui Liu and Qiming Wu, was formed to continue
the experimental program. The initial plan was to pursue both the experimental implementation
of non-Hermitian VAET (Phys. Rev. Research 6, 023149) and the synchronization of quantum van
der Pol oscillators (arXiv:2509.18423). In the beginning we worked on both in parallel, however,
given limited personnel and ambiguity in the experimental goals for the VAET project, it would be
more effective to first focus on the quantum vdP oscillators. Our original goal was to first
demonstrate the phase locking of a single vdP oscillator to an external drive. The initial goal was
to demonstrate phase locking of a single oscillator to an external drive. However, given
complementing work by another group (Sci. Adv. 11, eady5649) and our interest in pursuing a
more impactful direction, we decided to work directly toward the mutual synchronization of two
oscillators. This required the development of several essential capabilities that the group had not
previously established, including multimode motional-state reconstruction, dissipative
engineering, and precise control of mixed-species ion chains. The project also demanded a tight
calibration of motional frequencies with stability at the 10-Hz level, which required extensive
work for precise calibration procedures and elevate the control system to a state-of-the-art level.
After a year of work, these efforts culminated in a complete manuscript in August 2025.

Overall, while these obstacles slowed some parts of the original experimental plan, they
ultimately strengthened the program by improving the robustness of the experimental
infrastructure, expanding the capabilities of the control framework, and clarifying the research
directions most aligned with long-term goals in open system quantum simulation. The
experience established a strong foundation for future work and positioned the group to work
with a wider range of experiments with trapped ion quantum information processors.
