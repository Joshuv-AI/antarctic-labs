Haplotypes and Machine Learning Improve Genomic Prediction in Brassica napus
Authors: Tessa R MacNish, Hawlader A Al‐Mamun, Thomas Bergmann, Lixi Jiang, David Edwards
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGenomic selection (GS) is a crop and livestock improvement method suited for predicting complex agronomical traits, while genomic prediction (GP) is the development of GS models prior to their practical use in breeding programs. One of the challenges in GP is accounting for how complex genomic interactions, such as epistasis, affect the resulting phenotype. Incorporating haplotypes and Machine Learning (ML) into GP models are two methods for accounting for local epistasis and non‐linear relationships. This study compared linear‐ and ML‐GP models for single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and haplotypes in Brassica napus . A publicly available dataset of 991 B. napus individuals, 4 286 896 SNPs, and the traits flowering time, oil content and oleic acid content was used for all GP models. The ML models improved trait prediction accuracy for all tested traits in both SNP‐ and haplotype‐based GP. While haplotypes did not significantly boost prediction accuracy over SNPs, they captured novel genetic variation and offered a broader diversity of variants for selection, suggesting a qualitative advantage for long‐term breeding goals. Here, we demonstrate how haplotypes and ML can improve GS in B. napus .Keywords: crop improvement, epistasis, feature importance, genomic prediction, haplotype, machine learning

Introduction

Brassica napus (rapeseed) is an ecologically important oil crop with diverse applications in food and industry (Raboanatahiry et al. 2021). The global production of rapeseed between 2024 and 2025 reached 85 649 thousand metric tons (USDA 2025). Improving agronomically important traits in B. napus is important to keep up with increasing demand and changing environmental conditions. Marker assisted breeding (MAB) is a crop improvement method that identifies molecular markers associated with agronomically important traits and uses these markers to select breeding candidates. Genomic selection (GS) is a MAB method suited for predicting and selecting for complex traits (Meuwissen et al. 2001), aiding crop improvement of agronomically important traits with complex genetic architectures and environmental interactions (Hu et al. 2022; Jin et al. 2023). GS has been applied to a wide range of crops at multiple breeding stages, reducing the breeding cycle length, increasing selection intensity and increasing genetic gain (Alemu et al. 2024; Escamilla et al. 2025; Heffner et al. 2010). Genomic prediction (GP) is the technical process of developing trait prediction models using training data. Once trained, these prediction models can be applied in breeding populations to estimate genomic breeding values. When GP models are applied in breeding programs, the process becomes GS.

Incorporating non‐additive genetic effects such as epistasis into GP models is challenging but important for predicting complex traits (Alemu et al. 2024; Escamilla et al. 2025). Epistasis describes the effect of interactions between alleles for two or more genes on the resulting phenotype (Phillips 2008). Conventional linear GP models such as genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP) (VanRaden 2008) and ridge‐regression best linear unbiased prediction (RR‐BLUP) (Meuwissen et al. 2001; Whittaker et al. 2000) assume a common variance for all marker effects, which implies that markers are treated as having equal prior contribution to genetic variance and they do not account for non‐additive genetic effects. Non‐linear Machine Learning (ML) models (like Random Forests, XGBoost, or Neural Networks) are mathematically capable of capturing non‐additive relationships that linear models miss. Although improved prediction accuracy using ML models has been reported for some complex traits such as grain yield, node number and pod number (Sandhu et al. 2021; Yoosefzadeh‐Najafabadi et al. 2022), the best performing models differ between species and traits (Weber et al. 2023). Ensemble‐based ML models such as Random Forest (RF) (Breiman 2001) and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) (Chen and Guestrin 2016) are robust, less prone to overfitting and have been found to outperform linear GP models in traits such as grain yield, disease resistance and protein content (Sandhu et al. 2021; Sirsat et al. 2022; Tomar et al. 2021; Yoosefzadeh‐Najafabadi et al. 2022). While deep learning models such as Neural Networks can also account for non‐linear relationships, they are outperformed by ML models, are hard to interpret and require large high‐quality datasets (Azodi et al. 2019; Gill et al. 2022).

Haplotype‐based genomic prediction extends standard GP methods by accounting for non‐additive genetic effects, such as local epistasis (Azodi et al. 2019). Haplotypes are genomic regions that are inherited together over generations due to high levels of linkage disequilibrium (LD) (Belzile et al. 2020). Haplotypes reduce the computational cost (Marquez et al. 2024) and increase GP accuracy by accounting for local epistatic effects (Alemu et al. 2023; Difabachew et al. 2023; Weber et al. 2023), when compared to single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)‐based GP. Local epistasis refers to epistatic genetic interactions confined to a small genomic region (Akdemir and Jannink 2015). Haplotypes are multiallelic, which enables breeders to select alleles from a greater diversity of genotypes than is possible with traditional MAB markers such as biallelic SNPs. This allows breeders to identify variants that will have the best performance for their crop and remove variants with deleterious effects (Clot et al. 2024). While the multiallelic nature of haplotypes is beneficial for breeding, the associated increase in multicollinearity can decrease GP trait prediction accuracy, especially with increasing haplotype length (Sallam et al. 2020). Multicollinearity is when multiple predictors are highly correlated—such as when genomic markers are in LD—and it increases variance, bias and overfitting of models (Bian and Holland 2015). Removing rare haplotype variants mitigates the accuracy loss from high levels of multiallelism (Hess et al. 2017). Longer haplotypes may capture a greater number of genetic interactions but can also increase multicollinearity. Optimum haplotype length is species‐ and trait‐specific (Weber et al. 2023). Density‐based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) (Ester et al. 1996) is a clustering‐based algorithm that can be used to estimate haplotypes. DBSCAN is robust to outliers and is a non‐linear algorithm, which can better represent the complex LD patterns and the genetic architecture of complex traits (Ester et al. 1996; Khvorykh et al. 2023). Due to these benefits, clustering‐based haplotypes outperform SNPs in MAB studies (Khvorykh et al. 2023; Wu et al. 2022).

Haplotype‐based ML GP has been applied to soybean, rice and wheat and has demonstrated the potential for haplotypes and ML to improve the robustness and prediction accuracy of GP over linear SNP‐based GP models (Heilmann et al. 2025; Kawakita et al. 2024; Yoosefzadeh‐Najafabadi et al. 2022). To our knowledge, the combined benefits of haplotypes and ML have not yet been explored in B. napus . The aim of this study was to systematically compare the performance of (i) GP models using DBSCAN‐based haplotypes and SNPs and (ii) the ML GP models RF and XGBoost to the linear model GBLUP, for three agriculturally important traits in B. napus : flowering time (FT), oil content (OC) and oleic acid content (C18:1). The publicly available B. napus dataset included 991 individuals and 4 286 896 SNPs from 39 countries and represented all three ecotypes (winter, semi‐winter and spring) (Wu et al. 2019). Our results show that ML consistently improved trait prediction accuracy, while haplotypes identified novel genomic regions. This study highlights how haplotypes and ML can improve GP in the economically important crop B. napus .

Genomic Prediction

Previous studies comparing haplotype‐ and SNP‐based GP models using linear and ML models found trait prediction accuracy could be improved using haplotypes, ML and ensemble models in a trait, species and dataset specific manner (Azodi et al. 2019; Heilmann et al. 2025; Yoosefzadeh‐Najafabadi et al. 2022). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that haplotypes, ML and ensemble models would improve GP trait prediction accuracy for the complex traits tested in this study. Improving the accuracy of trait predictions has the potential to increase the efficiency of GS and crop improvement. The Pearson's correlation coefficients for the SNP‐based, the best performing haplotype‐based and the ensemble SNP‐ and haplotype‐based models are shown in Figure 2. Tables S3–S5 display the trait prediction accuracy for all GP models. Model accuracy linear mixed effect models were fitted for all combinations of main effects (genotype, trait, model) and their interactions. The model that best fit the Pearson's correlation coefficient—based on Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC)—included GP model, trait and the interaction between model and trait. Table S6 shows the results of the pairwise comparisons used to calculate significance between model and trait comparisons based on Pearson's correlation coefficient. The XGBoost and RF flowering time and oil content models performed significantly better than the flowering time and oil content GBLUP models with p‐values of < 0.001. Within the XGBoost and RF flowering time and oil content models, the best performing haplotype, SNP and ensemble models were not significantly different from each other. None of the best performing haplotype, SNP or ensemble models performed significantly differently than each other within the oleic acid content models. However, RF and XGBoost performed better than GBLUP when predicting oleic acid content with p‐values of less than 0.001. RF outperformed XGBoost for oil content and ML‐based GP models performed better than GBLUP for all three traits (Table S6). Previous ML‐based GP studies also found that ML models outperformed linear GP models, especially for tree‐ or gradient boosting‐based ML models such as RF and XGBoost (Sandhu et al. 2021; Sirsat et al. 2022). The gain in trait prediction accuracy from using ML‐based models compared to linear GP models was greater for flowering time and oil content than for oleic acid content. The increase in average trait prediction accuracy compared to GBLUP was 0.358 and 0.374 for flowering time and 0.190 and 0.105 for oil content for RF and XGBoost respectively (Tables S3 and S4). While the average increase in trait prediction accuracy was 0.067 and 0.045 when comparing RF and XGBoost to GBLUP (Table S5). Flowering time and oil content are regulated by a complex network of genes that include epistatic and environmental interactions (Dong et al. 2025; Han et al. 2025; Heinrich et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2017; Li et al. 2018). In contrast, the majority of oleic acid phenotypic variation can be explained by a few genes or loci (Hu et al. 2006; Li et al. 2018). The non‐linear relationships and non‐additive genetic effects important for predicting complex traits (Alemu et al. 2024; Escamilla et al. 2025) can be captured by the non‐linear ML models used in this study. This ability to capture non‐linear relationships that are more important for complex than simple traits explains why there was a greater gain in trait prediction accuracy for flowering time and oil content than for oleic acid content when using ML‐based GP models. It has previously been demonstrated that the use of haplotypes can improve trait prediction accuracy in crops over conventionally used SNPs (Alemu et al. 2023; Sallam et al. 2020). However, this potential accuracy improvement is trait, species and dataset specific (Difabachew et al. 2023; Heilmann et al. 2025; Matias et al. 2017; Weber et al. 2023). In this study, the haplotype‐based models were not significantly different from the SNP‐based GP models for any trait or model. However, the use of haplotypes did reduce the computational cost of running the GP models, with haplotype‐based models running in 2 h or less and the SNP models running for 3–4 days. The reduction in necessary computational resources to run haplotype‐based GP models is a benefit that has been identified in other studies (Heilmann et al. 2025; Marquez et al. 2024). Ensemble models have also been found to improve trait prediction accuracy in GP, by reducing the prediction error without increasing bias (Azodi et al. 2019; Chiaravallotti et al. 2025; Tomura et al. 2025). These models and the ensemble models used in this study estimate the trait of interest by averaging trait prediction values between models. The use of ensemble models in this study did not significantly improve trait prediction accuracy compared to SNP‐ or haplotype‐based models. The lack of trait prediction improvement could be due to only averaging the prediction values between two models (haplotype‐ and SNP‐based) for each trait and model combination, which may be too few models to significantly reduce the prediction error.

The trait prediction accuracy for haplotype‐based GP models varied greatly between the different haplotype parameters used. The average Pearson's correlation coefficient ranged from 0.288 to 0.804, 0.119 to 0.452 and 0.527 to 0.883 for flowering time, oil content and oleic acid content, respectively (Tables S3–S5). In this study, haplotypes were defined by three main parameters: minFreq, which removed rare haplotype variants based on their frequency in the population and MGmin and epsilon, which affected the haplotype density. The best performing haplotype for eight out of nine GP models had a MGmin value of 10 (Tables S3–S5), which corresponds to the shortest haplotypes (Table S2). This is consistent with previous studies that found that increasing haplotype length decreases trait prediction accuracy (Sallam et al. 2020; Weber et al. 2023). Multicollinearity gained from multiallelic haplotypes has been associated with a decrease in trait prediction accuracy and can be mitigated by removing rare haplotype variants (Hess et al. 2017; Matias et al. 2017). Eight out of nine of the best performing haplotypes had a minFreq of nine, which removed haplotype variants present in nine or less individuals, compared to a minFreq of two, which removed variants present in two or less individuals. Therefore, a higher minFreq removed a greater proportion of rare variants. The best performing haplotypes in this study indicate that both haplotype length and the frequency of rare haplotype variants affect GP trait prediction accuracy. However, rare haplotypes are of high interest to breeders as rare haplotypes often harbour the novel genetic diversity essential for long‐term crop improvement (Meena et al. 2025). This highlights the trade‐off between trait prediction accuracy and capturing potential genetic diversity. The haplotype parameter combination epsilon = 0.8, MGmin = 10 and minFreq = 9 (ep0.8_MGmin10_minFreq9) was the best performing haplotype parameter combination for six out of nine GP models while the haplotypes ep0.7_MGmin10_minFreq9, ep0.7_MGmin10_minFreq2 and ep0.8_MGmin20_minFreq9 were all the best performing haplotypes in one GP model. These results indicated that the optimal haplotype parameters were trait‐ and model‐specific; therefore, haplotype parameter optimisation should be included as a parameter tuning step in GP studies.

The GP models for flowering time, oil content and oleic acid content had significantly different trait prediction accuracies (Table S6). Oleic acid content had the highest average Pearson's correlation coefficient, ranging from 0.527 to 0.918 (Table S5). On the other hand, oil content had the lowest average Pearson's correlation coefficient, ranging from 0.119 to 0.492 (Table S4). Oleic acid content's high and oil content's low correlation coefficients corresponded with their broad sense heritability of 0.88 and 0.46, respectively. Higher heritability scores correlated with greater trait prediction accuracies (Kaler et al. 2022; Zhu et al. 2024). Flowering time had a range of average Pearson's correlation coefficients of 0.288 to 0.840 corresponding to a heritability of 0.62, which is between oil and oleic acid contents' heritability.

Feature Importance

XGBoost records the relative importance of each feature, used to identify which covariates and genotypes are associated with each trait. The top 10 most important features for each model are shown in Tables S7 and S8. The ‘ecotype winter’ and several countries were in the top 10 most important features for haplotype‐based GP, while only the ‘ecotype winter’ and ‘country Pakistan’ were important in SNP‐based GP in flowering time. These results agree with previous studies that found ecotype to be strongly correlated to flowering time (Dong et al. 2025; Wan et al. 2025). This reflects the historical adaption and selection of B. napus accessions to different climate conditions. Country of origin likely affected flowering time in a similar way, reflecting historical selective pressures and environmental conditions that affected the ancestors of each accession. The importance of ecotype and country of origin in the models demonstrate how environmental adaption due to differing climates is reflected in the genetics of B. napus accessions. Oil content was associated with ecotype and country for haplotype‐based GP, but no covariates were important for SNP‐based oil content GP. The haplotype‐based GP used fewer markers when compared to SNP‐based GP, which could inflate the relative importance of the non‐genetic predictors. Therefore, the disagreement of whether country and ecotype impacted the model output between haplotype‐ and SNP‐based GP may indicate that these features are less important for oil content than they are for flowering time. The top 10 most important features for haplotype‐ and SNP‐based GP indicated that ecotype was not an important factor for oleic acid content. Country was found to be important for haplotype‐based GP but not for SNP‐based GP indicating that country of origin has a minor effect on oleic acid content. These results are expected, given that the majority of phenotypic variance in oleic acid is explained by a few genes (Hu et al. 2006; Li et al. 2018).

All three phenotypes had haplotypes that were important for model performance (Table S7). The haplotype variants that were of high importance the most consistently are summarised in Table 2. While these haplotypes do not overlap with known flowering time, oil content, or oleic acid content genes, the high number of models that independently identified them as important indicated that they may be novel sources of agronomically associated genomic variation. In addition, out of the 10 haplotypes identified as important for model importance in the most models, eight were found to be important for multiple traits and may have pleiotropic effects. Flowering time associated genes and loci have pleiotropic effects with oil content and oleic acid content (Çağlı et al. 2025; Li et al. 2018; Tyagi et al. 2018) and an oleic acid content associated gene affects oil content (Liu et al. 2022). Additionally, oil content is positively correlated with flowering time and oleic acid content, with co‐localised quantitative trait loci (QTL) for oil content and flowering time (Javed et al. 2016). In this study, the haplotypes that were important for multiple traits (Table S7) indicated either pleiotropic effects, such as those that have been previously reported, or loci in linkage disequilibrium with each other. Multiple haplotype variants were important in the top 10 features. The models trained in this study can identify which genomic variants have a large impact on trait prediction accuracy and therefore likely influence the trait of interest. However, the models do not indicate the direction or magnitude of the variant's effect, nor can the models predict how each haplotype variant will affect other agronomically important traits not tested in this study. Therefore, further study into the impact of each haplotype variant should be done so that breeders can choose haplotype variants that improve flowering time, oil content, or oleic acid content without having deleterious effects on other agronomically important traits.

BLAST was used to check for similarity between agronomically important genes and the genomic data used in this study (Altschul et al. 1990). The locations of any known agronomically important genes identified by BLAST were compared with the genetic variants within the top 10 most important features for the XGBoost GP models. Only two genes were found to be close to or overlap with genomic variants with a high impact on model accuracy. The FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) ortholog BnaC06g27090D was located within the haplotype hap_28471579_28613643 on chromosome C06. This haplotype ranked among the top 10 most important features for 28 flowering time models, with an average gain of 0.019. FT (Kardailsky et al. 1999; Kobayashi et al. 1999) is associated with flowering time in many crops and angiosperms, including B. napus (Guo et al. 2014; Lebedeva et al. 2020; Pieper et al. 2021; Wan et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2024). FT acts as a florigen: when triggered by day length, it moves from the leaves to the shoot apical meristem, interacts with other flowering time genes and induces flowering. SNP chrA08_10194057 was located 4836 bp upstream of the FATTY ACID ELONGATION1 (FAE1) ortholog BnaA08g11130D. First identified in Arabidopsis, FAE1 extends the length of fatty acid chains and is required for synthesis of long fatty acids (James et al. 1995). In the SNP‐based oleic acid content model, the SNP chrA08_10194057 had an average gain of 0.384, indicating a large effect on oleic acid content. The importance of FAE1 for oleic acid content is supported by previous studies (Li et al. 2018; Peng et al. 2010). Both haplotype‐ and SNP‐based GP identified known agronomically important genes and novel genomic regions that could be used to improve B. napus crops; however, there was minimal overlap between the haplotypes found to be in the top 10 most important features with the agronomically associated SNPs identified in this study. Out of 674 haplotypes and 171 SNPs found to be important for model accuracy, there were only nine instances where important haplotypes and SNPs overlapped (Table 3). These results are consistent with previous studies that found that haplotype‐based MAB identified novel genomic regions with little to no overlap with SNP‐based MAB methods (Abed and Belzile 2019; Bekele et al. 2018; Helal et al. 2021). SNPs and haplotypes identify complementary genetic variants because they capture different types of underlying genetic architecture. SNPs are suited for capturing additive genetic effects, while haplotypes can better capture complex genetic networks caused by LD (Abed and Belzile 2019; Bekele et al. 2018; Khvorykh et al. 2023; Oppong et al. 2022). Both haplotypes and SNPs should be used in GP analysis to identify the greatest diversity of genomic variants for crop improvement.

Conclusion

Machine learning GP models consistently outperformed GBLUP across all three B. napus traits, while the integration of haplotypes enhanced computational efficiency without compromising accuracy. Notably, both SNP‐ and haplotype‐based GP models identified critical genomic regions; however, these genomic regions showed minimal overlap between the two methods. Demonstrating the need to use both SNPs and haplotypes to maximise the amount of genomic variation that could be used for crop improvement. Some of the most important haplotypes for model performance affected multiple traits, indicating pleiotropic effects or linkage. Many of the important haplotypes had multiple variants that affected the model performance. The multivariate haplotypes provide an opportunity for breeders to identify haplotype variants with the optimal combination of agronomically important phenotypes for their crops. Haplotypes and ML can improve the performance of GP and can therefore improve the accuracy and efficiency of GS in B. napus .

Experimental Procedures

Dataset

The B. napus dataset published by Wu et al. (2019) and downloaded from CropGS‐Hub (Chen et al. 2024, https://iagr.genomics.cn/CropGS/#/Datasets?species=Rapeseed) included 4 286 896 SNPs in VCF format, two covariates (ecotype and country of origin) recorded for each of the 991 individuals and 10 phenotypes recorded for a subset of the population. The phenotypes used in this study include flowering time (926 individuals), oil content (279 individuals) and oleic acid (C18:1) content (279 individuals). Trait heritability was calculated using GCTA (v1.94.1 Linux) and the –make‐grm function and parameters –autosome and –autosome‐num 19, to calculate the genetic relationship matrix. The genetic relationship matrix was used to estimate the SNP heritability using the parameters ‐‐grm ‐‐pheno –reml (Yang et al. 2010).

SNP Preparation

The VCF file was filtered for minor allele frequency (MAF) (‐‐maf 0.05), minimum depth (‐‐minDP 5), maximum missingness (‐‐max‐missing 0.5) and insertions and deletions were removed (‐‐remove‐indels) using VCFtools version 0.1.16 (Danecek et al. 2011). The filtered VCF contained 2 222 720 SNPs and was phased and imputed using Beagle version 5.4 with the default settings (Browning et al. 2018, 2021). The genotypes within the filtered and imputed VCF file were numerically encoded using PLINK v1.90b6 (Purcell et al. 2007, https://zzz.bwh.harvard.edu/plink/ld.shtml) and the ‐‐recode A. The genotypic matrix was converted to a CSV file and used in the GP models.

Haplotype Preparation

The filtered, phased and imputed VCF was separated by chromosome using BCFtools version 1.15 (Danecek et al. 2021) and the view function with default settings. PLINK generated pairwise linkage disequilibrium matrices using the parameter –r 2 square (Purcell et al. 2007, https://zzz.bwh.harvard.edu/plink/ld.shtml) for each chromosome VCF. Haplotype variants were calculated and formatted for each chromosome VCF using HaploVar version 0.1.1 (MacNish et al. 2025) and the haplotype_variants function. HaploVar generates a haplotype‐based genotype matrix where each column represents a unique haplotype allele and each row represents an individual. Each entry in the numeric matrix denotes the dosage of a specific haplotype (0, 1, or 2 copies), allowing the matrix to be integrated into genomic prediction pipelines in the same manner as a standard SNP‐based dosage matrix. HaploVar was run for three epsilon values (0.6, 0.7, 0.8) three MGmin values (10, 20, 30) and two minFreq values (2 and 9), for a total of 18 parameter combinations. The format parameter was set to 5 and 3 to format the output for GBLUP and all other GP models, respectively. Haplotype parameter combinations where less than 50% of chromosomes identified haplotype variants were discarded, resulting in 14 parameter combinations. The chromosome haplotype variant tables for each 14 parameter combinations were collated using HaploVar's collate_haplotype_variants function. Four additional haplotype variant CSVs were collated with different parameter combinations for the A and C genomes. All 18 parameter combinations used to construct the haplotype variant CSVs used in the GP models are displayed in Table S1. A workflow for preparing the SNP and haplotype genotype data for GP is shown in Figure S1.

Genomic Prediction Models

Genomic prediction was run using the models GBLUP, RF and XGBoost for three phenotypes (flowering time, oil content and oleic acid content) and all 19 forms of genotype data (SNPs and 18 haplotype variant tables). The R package rrBLUP version 4.6.1 (Endelman 2011) was used to implement all GBLUP models. RF was trained using ranger version 0.14.1 (Wright and Ziegler 2017). XGBoost was fit using xgboost version 1.7.11.1 (Chen and Guestrin 2016). Each model incorporated genotype, phenotype and covariate (ecotype and country of origin) data as inputs. In GBLUP framework, covariates were fitted as fixed effects. For RF models, covariates were integrated into the genotype data frame as categorical variables (factors). In contrast, covariates for XGBoost were one‐hot encoded into numerical format and then merged with the genotype matrix. The data was split into 10 equal subsets for 10‐fold cross validation. The same 10 data subsets (folds) were used to test all models. An ensemble model was run for each phenotype and model (GBLUP, RF and XGBoost) combination where the predicted values were the means of the SNP‐based and the best performing haplotype‐based model predictions. Trait prediction accuracy was calculated using the R package Metrics version 0.1.4 (Hamner and Frasco 2018) for mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE). Base R was used to calculate Pearson's correlation coefficient. All R code was run using R version 4.0.3 (R Core Team 2024). Examples of the code used for all GP models are available on GitHub (https://github.com/TessaMacNish/GenomicPredictionModels), including the code used to incorporate the genotype, phenotype and covariate data into the models.

To test the significance of model, trait and genotype effect on Pearson's correlation coefficient, linear mixed effect models were fitted with nlme version 3.1.169 (Pinheiro et al. 2021). Cross‐validation fold was included as a random effect in all linear mixed effect models. All main effect (model, trait and genotype) and interaction combinations were fitted as separate models. The linear mixed effect model with the lowest Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) was selected. To test for the effect of heteroscedasticity on the model, the selected model was updated to allow for group‐specific unequal variance—for each main effect.
