1 Introduction
In this project we introduced an experimental platform that facilitates the study of the two-
way interaction of plasma and flame, and the exploration of the impact of the spatial loca-
tion of the discharge on its kinetics, in a controlled environment. The experimental platform
is a mesoscale CH4 /air premixed flame burner that also acts as a dielectric barrier discharge
(DBD) operated by nanosecond pulsed voltage. A unique feature of this setup is that the
DBD utilizes transparent electrodes, made of Indium Tin Oxide (ITO), to unlock optical ac-
cess along the line-of-sight parallel to the dominant electric field. This platform is suitable
to study both the forward problem (impact of plasma-on-flame), which manifests itself as a
modified flame speed as well as flame wrinkling [1]; and the backward problem (impact of
flame-on-plasma) [2], which includes plasma regime transitions, between microdischarges and
uniform modes, driven by the flame passage [1]. Prior work at MIT has focused on electrical
measurements and direct imaging studies of the plasma-flame interaction through the ITO elec-
trode. This project, which was awarded experimental runtime at the Low-temperature Plasma
Research Facility (PRF) at Sandia National Laboratories, quantifies the flame-plasma interac-
tion through temporally resolved measurement of key radicals (oxygen atom, O) produced by
the nanosecond repetitively pused discharge (NRPD) in an individual microdischarge, using
two-photon absorption laser-induced fluorescence (TALIF).
 Aside from the direct contribution of this study to the field of plasma-assisted combustion
(PAC), the collaboration between MIT and Sandia National Laboratories has demonstrated the
possibility of imaging of TALIF signals through the ITO electrode and has resulted in a new
calibration method using photo-dissociation of NO2 for O-atom measurement that overcomes
some of the challenges of prior work using Xe calibration. This method has enabled probing
the cross-section of individual microdischarges with maximum light collection solid angle,
and the method can be used for future experiments with other DBD setups used for chemical
processing, for example for CO2 conversion or nitrogen fixation.
 This final project report presents the methods and results of this collaboration.

2 Experimental methods

2.1 Plasma-assisted mesoscale burner with single microdischarge
Kinetic studies of PAC have frequently relied on measuring species in low-pressure dielec-
tric barrier discharges (DBDs) to inform and validate chemical kinetic mechanisms. In these
reduced-pressure environments, the discharge manifests as uniform and diffuse, providing a
spatially homogeneous volume to sample with laser diagnostics. In addition, the electric field
distribution along the electrode is well-defined, and the electrical energy deposition, electron
density, electron temperature and emission intensity are spatially near-uniform, simplifying the
comparison between experimental species measurements and 0D kinetic models. However,
practical PAC systems typically operate at atmospheric pressure or higher. In these condi-
tions, the discharge takes the form of many microdischarges with non-uniform energy distribu-
tion and significant gradients in electron density, electron temperature, and emission intensity
along their boundaries. These microdischarges tend to self-organize within the discharge vol-
ume and do not remain fixed in space, but rather respond and get transported by the dynamic
flow environment. The tendency of these filaments to convect with the flow and self-organize
complicates line-of-sight measurements in DBD arrangements at atmospheric pressure.
 To facilitate reliable laser measurements in an atmospheric pressure environment, a spatially-
localized microdischarge needs to be isolated. For the purpose of this work, the microdischarge
needs to be triggered in relevant combustion environments. To that end, a single microdis-
charge was generated in the mesoscale burner shown in Figure 1, which has been presented in
detail in prior work [1, 2].
 The burner is a transparent rectangular quartz channel with a taper, where the height de-
creases linearly from 3.8 mm to 2.2 mm along its 148 mm length. The width of the channel in
the third dimension is 30 mm. The heights were chosen to make the burner mesoscale, where
they approximate the flame quenching distance. The thickness of the quartz walls is 2 mm.
This is a second iteration to the burner presented in [1, 2], where the whole channel is now a
single quartz piece to avoid optical aberrations for the laser, whereas prior versions were made
of separate quartz plates bonded together with high temperature adhesive.
 The burner is operated in a transient flame propagation mode. The fresh fuel/air mixture
is flowed from the narrower to the wider end (right to left), Figure 1. The reactant blend

that a single microdischarge is formed, a technique successfully employed in other work by
Simek et al. [3]. The transient flame propagation mode is advantageous for diagnostics: with
a fixed laser probe volume at the fixed discharge location, the propagating flame naturally
“scans” its entire structure – from the cold reactants, through the preheat and reaction zones,
and into the hot products – across the measurement point. Prior work with a similar setup
[1, 2] characterized the backward problem (the impact of the flame on the plasma), which
drives transitions between filamentary microdischarges and uniform modes depending on the
local gas environment. In this work, we isolate these two environments to provide quantitative
information of the discharge kinetics when operating the plasma in both the burnt and unburnt
gas streams.

2.2 Configurations studied
In any practical system, the discharge will interact with the complex reactive flow environment,
sampling pockets of gas spanning both the unburnt and burnt gas regions. As a result, two
configurations are of interest: the discharge located in the cold reactants, and the discharge
located in the hot products.
 As illustrated in Figure 2, an ideal laminar premixed flame exhibits a structure character-
ized by four distinct zones: the unburnt gas (reactants), the preheat zone dominated by thermal
diffusion transferring heat from the reaction zone backward into the fresh reactants, the reac-
tion zone characterized by steep gradients in species concentration and peak heat release, and
the burnt gas (product) region. It is important to note that the profile in Figure 2 represents
an ideal adiabatic flame where the temperature in the product zone remains constant at the
adiabatic flame temperature (Tad ). In the experimental configuration, however, the flame is
non-adiabatic; the temperature profile in the product region will gradually decrease due to heat
loss to the channel walls and surroundings.
 Because the flame propagates down the channel, the local gas environment at the fixed
discharge location evolves over time. This propagation allows us to sample both the reactant
and product environments sequentially. In the first configuration, the discharge acts in the
reactants. This environment is characterized by low temperature (close to ambient conditions)
and high neutral gas density, consisting of N2 , O2 , and CH4 . In the second configuration,
the discharge is actuated in the product region. This environment is characterized by high
temperature (Tad ≈ 2160 K for the mixture under study) and significantly reduced neutral gas
density. The composition shifts to include concentrations of CO2 and H2 O (and other minor

species) which are efficient collisional quenchers of excited states.

2.3 Electrical measurements
Electrical characterization of the discharge was performed using a voltage and current probe
co-located immediately upstream of the high-voltage ITO electrode. The voltage was measured
using a high-bandwidth Teledyne LeCroy PPE 20kV probe, and the current was monitored wth
a Pearson Electronic Model 6585 Rogowski coil. Data acquisition was performed using a 1
GHz bandwidth oscilloscope (Teledyne LeCroy Waverunner 104MXi-A) with a sampling rate
of 5 GS/s to resolve the fast transient features.
 Figure 3 presents the voltage, current, and cumulative energy deposition for a single pulse
in both the reactant (left) and product (right) streams. To capture the shot-to-shot variability, the
waveforms presented are bounded curves representing one standard deviation (±1σ) above and
below the mean voltage, current, and energy. These statistics were derived from an ensemble
of approximately 10,000 individual waveforms. The specific pulse analyzed corresponds to the
94th pulse within the 200-pulse burst, a point chosen to ensure the pulses represent a quasi-
periodic state of operation. The energy was calculated from the voltage and current waveforms
by shifting them to align in time, multiplying, and then integrating them.
 In both environments, the incident high-voltage pulse exhibits a FWHM of ∼ 20 ns with
a rise time of ∼ 7 ns. Following the primary pulse, subsequent reflections are observed due
to impedance mismatches between the plasma load and the transmission line; these reflections
contribute additional, albeit smaller, energy deposition into the gas. A more detailed mathe-
matical treatment of the waveforms can be found in our prior work [4]. The mean peak voltage

2.4 O-atom TALIF setup
Laser-induced fluorescence (LIF) is a sensitive optical diagnostic technique in which a species
undergoes single-photon excitation and subsequently relaxes into a lower energy state, releas-
ing a fluorescent photon which can be detected to deduce its ground state population. LIF is
commonly used to measure species such as OH, CH, and NO, where the single-photon ex-
citation wavelength is accessible at ultraviolet (UV) wavelengths above 220 nm. However,
for atomic oxygen, single-photon excitation LIF occurs at vacuum UV wavelengths, where
most practical PAC systems, at atmospheric pressure or higher, are optically thick. To address
this challenge, two-photon absorption LIF (TALIF) has been used for sensitive detection of
O-atoms.
 As illustrated in Figure 4, the excitation scheme involves the absorption of two photons
at λ = 225.7 nm to excite ground state oxygen atoms using the 3p3 P ←← 2p3 P transition.
Following excitation, the atoms undergo collisional relaxation and radiative decay from the
3p3 P → 3s3 S transition, emitting a fluorescence photon at λ = 844.6 nm. The fluorescence
intensity is directly proportional to the ground-state number density, provided the quenching
environment and laser fluence are accounted for.
 The optical arrangement for the TALIF measurements is shown in Figure 5. For O-atom

photoionization were negligible so that the LIF signal depended quadratically on laser intensity.
A beam splitter positioned before the burner directed a small fraction of the beam to a beam
profiling camera (WinCamD-LCM with a UV converter) to monitor the laser spatial profile and
energy fluctuations, which were used to correct the quadratic dependence of the two-photon
absorption signal.
 To facilitate optical access, the mesoscale burner was rotated 90 degrees such that the flow
axis was vertical, with the outlet and spark plug facing upward. This orientation allowed the
laser sheet to pass horizontally through the channel while the camera imaged the fluorescence
from the side (which corresponds to the top electrode surface in the standard configuration). It
was verified that this rotation did not significantly alter the flow dynamics or flame stability,
as the Froude number was sufficiently high (F r ≫ 1) that buoyancy effects were negligible
compared to the forced convection.
 The fluorescence signal at 845 nm was collected perpendicular to the laser sheet by an
Intensified CCD (ICCD) camera (Andor iStar DH334T-18F-73) equipped with an f /1.2 58-
mm Nikon camera lens. To isolate the atomic oxygen fluorescence from the broadband plasma
emission and flame chemiluminescence, a narrow bandpass interference filter centered at 845±
2 nm was placed in front of the lens. The spectral rejection of the filter was verified by detuning
the laser wavelength off the 226 nm resonance, upon which the fluorescence signal vanished,
confirming the detection of only the target species. The imaging was done through the ITO

 To determine the local gas temperature for number density corrections, measurements were
performed using a Type K thermocouple (Omega KMQSS-062E-6) inserted directly into the

flame was allowed to propagate down the channel repeatedly to ensure the system reached a

averaged over 200 subsequent flame propagation events, yielding mean gas temperatures of
353 ± 11 K for the reactant stream and 862 ± 12 K for the product stream.

2.5 Data-gathering strategy
Data acquisition was performed over numerous flame propagation events to build sufficient
statistical convergence. The flame propagation frequency was controlled by adjusting the oxy-
gen enrichment to achieve a repeatable propagation event every 1.8 seconds, resulting in the
oxy-rich and fuel lean ϕ = 0.76, XO2 /XN2 = 0.34 mixture. The oxy-rich environment also
facilitated the detection of O-atom signal in the post-combustion gas environment, when using
a lean mixture.

3 O-atom absolute calibration of signals
The methodology described so far yields relative signals, which are sufficient for observing
spatiotemporal trends. However, informing and validating chemical kinetic models requires
quantitative, absolute number densities. Converting the relative TALIF signal into an absolute
O-atom number density (nO ) requires a calibration procedure.

3.1 Xe calibration
Xenon-calibration is the most common procedure used to calibrate O-atom TALIF in the lit-
erature [5, 6]. Xenon is chosen because it has a two-photon resonance (6p′ [3/2]2 ←← 5p 1 S)
at 224.3 nm and fluorescent photon wavelength at 834.9 nm (6p′ [3/2]2 → 6s′ [1/2]2 ), both
spectrally close to the atomic oxygen transitions, shown in Figure 7. As a result, the required
adjustments to the setup are minimal: a different bandpass filter was used (RG780), and the
laser wavelength was shifted to 224.3 nm. To achieve an O atom calibration, the Xe TALIF
signal must be compared to the O atom TALIF signal and corrected for the ratios of their two-
photon absorption cross-sections (σ (2) ), spectral overlap (Γ), laser photon fluences (IXe /νXe ),
fluorescence quantum yields (γ), filter transmission (T ), detector quantum efficiency (η), and
TALIF signal intensities (S), shown in Equation 1.

 A primary advantage of this method over the Xe reference technique is that it produces
the target species itself (O-atom), thereby eliminating uncertainties related to the relative pho-
tophysical properties and collisional quenching rates of dissimilar species. The environment
used is also identical to the reactant environment, reducing uncertainties in the quenching en-
vironment. It is important to note that this calibration is performed under controlled reactant
conditions: applying this calibration to the product region still requires explicit environmental
corrections. Specifically, the result must be corrected for (i) temperature differences (Tcal vs
Tproducts ), which affect the gas density, and (ii) the variation in the collisional quenching envi-
ronment as the background gas composition shifts from reactants to products. Furthermore, this
method requires accurate knowledge of the NO2 absorption cross-section and precise spatial
overlap of the photolysis and probe beams, adding experimental complexity compared to the
single-beam Xe technique. By employing both calibration strategies, we aim to cross-validate
our results in our absolute density measurements.
 The setup for this calibration is shown in Figure 9, where a nanosecond Nd:YAG 355 nm
laser – for photodissociating NO2 – was directed through the side wall, and a femtosecond
Ti:Sapphire 226 nm laser – to execute TALIF on the resulting O atom – was routed through the
top of the channel to prevent the significant laser energy loss that would occur to the channel
walls. The femtosecond laser at 226 nm was chosen over a nanosecond one because the shorter
pulse duration and lower laser energy suppressed the parasitic photolysis of the precursor NO2
by the probe laser itself. Preliminary tests with the nanosecond laser had revealed artificial
inflation of the O-atom signal in this way. Additionally, the broad spectral bandwidth of the
femtosecond pulse facilitates photon pairing, ensuring efficient two-photon excitation without
the need for precise mode-matching or extensive spectral scanning, improving the robustness
of the calibration measurement. This calibration setup was also used to directly measure the
O-atom production from the discharge using the femtosecond probe. This absolute density
measurement established a calibration factor that was applied to the primary dataset collected
with the nanosecond laser system to achieve absolutely calibrated nanosecond laser signals
(presented in Section 4).

3.3 Postprocessing strategy
Although minimizing the grounded electrode’s surface area was intended to localize a singular
microdischarge, the physical implementation introduced some stochastic behavior. Specif-
ically, the extended conductive material on the ITO high-voltage electrode allowed the mi-
crodischarge anchoring point to convect vertically with the bulk flow, resulting in a random
jitter of the microdischarge’s position between shots. The other end of the discharge was more
confined by the cylindrical electrode, resulting in partial tilting of the discharge.
 As quantified in the scatter plot of Figure 10 (left), the centroid of the microdischarge
varied stochastically from shot to shot. If the raw TALIF images are simply averaged with-
out correction, this spatial variation leads to an artificial smearing of the signal (Figure 10,
center), resulting in a blurred profile that drastically underestimates the peak O-atom density.
To correct for this and accurately sample the production from the microdischarge itself rather
than the fixed laboratory frame, a post-processing alignment algorithm was implemented. For
each individual laser shot, the centroid of the plasma emission was calculated, and the coordi-
nate system was shifted to align this centroid with the geometric center of the domain. This
transformation ensures that the ensemble average (Figure 10, right) represents the true spatial
distribution of atomic oxygen relative to the microdischarge core, preserving the peak intensity
and radial gradients that would otherwise be washed out by the jitter.

4 Experimental results

4.1 O-atom measurements for discharge in reactant stream
The spatiotemporal evolution of atomic oxygen production in the reactant stream, within a sin-
gle nanosecond discharge pulse once it has reached a quasi-periodic operation (pulse 94 of 200
in the burst), is presented in Figure 11. The measurements were performed with the microdis-
charge localized in the cold reactant stream, characterized by ϕ = 0.76, XO2 /XN2 = 0.34, and
a local gas temperature of T ≈ 353 ± 11 K via the thermocouple measurements mentioned in
Section 2.4. To resolve the fast kinetics during the discharge phase, the camera gate width was
set to 50 ns. Measurements were performed before (A) and after (B) the incident pulse, as well
as after the first (C), second (D), and third (E) pulse reflections. The data points represented by
the black, blue, green, and red diamonds in Figure 11 (top) correspond to measurements from
four independent experimental trials. The data shown represents an ensemble average of 1000
flame propagation events, with background contributions (plasma emission, laser scattering,
dark noise) subtracted using the interleaved acquisition strategy described in Section 2.5.
 Figure 11 (top) correlates the spatially-averaged O-atom TALIF signal (blue curve/diamonds)

ligible, confirming the absence of residual O-atoms from previous pulses in the burst at this
specific location.

 The decay of the atomic oxygen population during the interpulse region is presented in
Figure 12. The measurements were performed by progressively delaying the 50 ns camera
gate relative to the incident pulse, extending up to 5 µs into the interpulse. The data points
are the spatially-averaged O-atom density within the FWHM of the microdischarge kernel. As
expected, the O-atom density exhibits a monotonic decay throughout the interpulse window.
The decay profile plotted on a semi-logarithmic scale reveals a distinct linear trend, indicating a
first-order exponential decay process of the form nO (t) = n0 exp(−t/τ ). A linear fit to the data
yields a decay rate constant of approximately 5.7 × 105 s−1 , corresponding to a characteristic
lifetime (τ ) of roughly 1.75 µs. In this atmospheric-pressure reactant environment, the loss of
atomic oxygen is likely governed by rapid chemical consumption. The primary mechanism is
expected to be three-body recombination with molecular oxygen to form ozone (O+O2 +M →
O3 +M ), a process known to be efficient at atmospheric pressure. Additionally, in the presence
of methane, rapid oxidation reactions with fuel molecules and radical fragments generated
during the discharge likely contribute to the O-atom consumption rate.

 To quantify the absolute number density in this complex environment, the calibration ob-
tained in the reactant stream (Xe/NO2 method) was transferred to the product stream condi-
tions. This transfer necessitates two corrections: (i) accounting for the change in gas number
density due to the temperature increase (Tprod ≈ 862 K vs. Treact ≈ 353 K) via the ideal gas
law, and (ii) correcting for the change in the collisional quenching environment. While the reac-
tant mixture is well-defined (CH4 /O2 /N2 ), the product stream contains a complex mixture of
species, including high concentrations of H2 O and CO2 , for which high-temperature quench-
ing cross-sections for O(3p3 P ) are not well characterized in the literature. To address this un-
certainty, the O-atom density is reported as a bounded range (shaded blue region). The lower
bound assumes temperature-independent quenching cross-sections, while the upper bound as-
sumes an inverse-temperature dependence (σQ ∝ T −0.5 ). Both bounds are from Frank et al. [9].
 The temporal evolution of the O-atom signal tracks the energy deposition, as with the reac-
tant case. At Point A (t ≈ 120 ns), the signal is negligible prior to the incident pulse. Following
the incident pulse at Point B (t ≈ 250 ns), a rapid rise in O-atom density is observed, coincid-
ing with the deposition of approximately 400 µJ of energy. Despite the similar energy input to
the reactant case, the peak number density is significantly lower (∼50-150 ppm), largely due to
the lower initial gas density, the lower energy density, and the different discharge regime. The
O-atom density continues to rise monotonically through Points C-E (t ≈ 400−700 ns), follow-
ing the step-wise energy addition from the reflected pulses ending at around 600 µJ. Notably,
the signal does not exhibit the sharp roll-off at Point E observed in the reactant case, potentially
suggesting slower recombination kinetics at the lower number densities of the product stream
or differences in diffusive losses given the larger volume of the discharge.
 The 2D images in Figure 13 (bottom) clearly illustrate a transition in the discharge mor-
phology from the reactants to the products. In the cold reactant stream, the discharge manifests
as a constricted filamentary microdischarge, with the O-atom production confined to a narrow
channel approximately 0.8 mm in diameter. Conversely, when the discharge is actuated in the
hot product stream, the O-atom distribution becomes significantly broader and more diffuse,
expanding to fill a larger volume of the inter-electrode gap as time progresses. This observation
is consistent with the regime transitions previously characterized in a similar mesoscale burner
during the passage of a premixed stoichiometric methane/air laminar flame [1].

5 Conclusions
The project resulted in the absolute spatially and temporally resolved measurement of O-atom
concentration in a pulsed nanosecond microdischarge located in both the reactants and prod-
ucts of a premixed lean and oxy-rich flame. The measurements were performed across an ITO
electrode and utilized a novel absolute calibration method based on NO2 photolytic dissocia-
tion. The results are currently being gathered in a Journal Paper that will be shared with the
DOE when published and will also include results using Xe calibration and comparison to a
numerical model.
