Rigorous error bounds for dissipative thermal state preparation from weak system-bath coupling
Christopher Ong, S. A. Parameswaran, Benedikt Placke, Dominik Hahn

Abstract
Thermal state preparation is a central challenge in the simulation of quantum many-body systems. Yet, provably efficient algorithms for this task were only introduced recently [Chen et al. Nature 646 , 561 (2025)]. These algorithms are based on dissipative Lindbladian evolution which exactly fixes the thermal state. Controlled and efficient digital simulation of this evolution, although possible in principle, remains out of reach for present-day quantum hardware. Subsequent work has therefore focused on analog approximations of the proposed Lindbladians via ‘collision models’ with relatively modest requirements — a resettable bath of ancilla qubits whose couplings to the system can be tuned in time-dependent fashion — while still admitting rigorous fixed-point error bounds. Existing rigorous approaches, however, do not exploit the fact that these constructions generically implement not only the desired Lindblad dynamics, but also an additional unitary evolution generated by the system Hamiltonian which may aid convergence to the thermal state [Lloyd and Abanin arXiv:2506.21318 (2025)]. Here, we show that this unitary contribution does indeed tighten the fixed-point error bound and demonstrate that it is rigorously controlled by the system-bath coupling strength J J , scaling as J 2 J^{2} . This demonstrates that the effect of the spurious ‘Lamb shift’ term generated by the system-bath interaction can be controlled by tuning J J . We clarify the role, previously observed, of a randomized implementation in suppressing possible resonances of the drive with the many-body spectrum, and bound the additional variance that this randomization imposes on observables. Finally, we numerically study aspects of the protocol which are relevant for its practical realization, such as the mixing time.



I
Introduction

Thermal state preparation is a central task in the simulation of quantum many-body systems, as it is essential for probing their equilibrium properties. Yet, despite its importance, controlled preparation of the thermal state of a generic local Hamiltonian remains difficult. In the classical setting, Markov chain Monte Carlo methods such as the Metropolis algorithm have been highly successful
29
;
25
. In the quantum setting, however, many existing approaches—such as imaginary-time evolution
30
, variational methods
10
;
13
;
22
, and phase-estimation-based algorithms
32
—continue to face significant challenges involving some subset of their lack of scalability, absence of rigorous convergence guarantees, and inconvenience of implementation on current quantum devices.

Another promising approach for thermal state preparation is provided by dissipative algorithms
42
;
36
;
45
;
26
. The central idea is to engineer an open-system dynamics that drives the system toward the desired state as the fixed point of the evolution.
Recently, a family of dissipative algorithms has been discovered
6
;
7
;
14
, based on the efficient simulation of a family of Lindbladians that satisfy the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) detailed balance conditions
1
;
2
;
24
;
16
. These conditions are a quantum generalization of classical detailed balance, and they imply that the exact fixed point of the Lindbladian is given by the Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}=Z_{\beta}^{-1}e^{-\beta H}
, where
Z_{\beta}=\text{Tr}\,e^{-\beta H}
is the partition function.
These algorithms can therefore be interpreted as a quantum generalization of Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms
18
.
Although Lindbladians satisfying KMS detailed balance have long been known in the form of ‘Davies generators’
11
;
12
, a key new feature of this recently discovered family is that it is generated by quasi-local jump operators. This not only implies their resource-efficient simulability at least in principle, but also makes some aspects of their theoretical analysis much more tractable. For example, it allows one to derive estimates on the mixing time, i.e. the convergence time to equilibrium, in certain regimes, such as at high temperatures
34
;
35
;
3
, for weak interactions
43
;
39
, in one-dimensional systems
4
, and on expander graphs
31
;
33
;
17
.

Despite these major advances, implementing these exact Gibbs samplers remains challenging in practice. Their jump operators are defined in terms of weighted Heisenberg evolutions of local operators and are therefore not straightforward to realize directly. The first theoretical proposals for their implementation
7
;
14
were based on a block-encoding of the Lindbladian, which is beyond the reach of present-day quantum devices and analog platforms. Other implementation strategies have been proposed, including randomized compilation
9
, variational compilation of the jump operators
21
, and approaches that leverage additional properties of the system, such as assumptions motivated by the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis
5
;
38
.

A more physically motivated approach is to implement these Gibbs samplers
approximately
using ‘collision models’ of a type familiar from the open quantum systems literature. In this setting, the system is weakly coupled to bath degrees of freedom represented by ancilla qubits, initially in some unentangled (product) state. After each interaction period, the ancillas are ‘reset’, i.e. reinitialized in the product state.
This repeated reset enables the effective removal of entropy from the system. For a specific choice of time-dependent system-bath dynamics and in a suitably defined weak-coupling limit [cf. Fig.
1
], such models can be designed to approximately realize a Gibbs sampler
20
;
15
;
27
;
37
. For specific choices of parameters, this approximation can even extend beyond the weak-coupling regime
44
.

Although this class of protocols is appealing as it can be implemented using only local interactions, its rigorous analysis is challenging. At leading order in the system-bath coupling strength, the effective dynamics consists of a Lindbladian evolution, followed by a unitary evolution with the system Hamiltonian.
While the dissipative part of the Lindbladian evolution can be chosen to match that of an exact Gibbs sampler, their coherent parts differ due to an effective back-action of the system-bath dynamics, often referred to as the ‘Lamb shift’.
As a consequence, the thermal state is no longer an exact fixed point of the Lindbladian evolution.
Previous analyses
21
;
15
studied the fixed-point error resulting from this Lamb shift, and derived bounds in terms of the mixing time and structural properties of the Lindbladian. However, by neglecting the effect of the unitary system evolution (in effect, by ‘rewinding’ this via backward time-evolution
20
), parts of their bounds were insensitive to the system-bath coupling strength.

Ref.
27
identified another key mechanism which may control the fixed-point error in the weak-coupling limit.
In particular, their analysis suggests that including the unitary evolution under the system Hamiltonian in the error analysis can significantly reduce the fixed-point error, which can be controlled and made arbitrarily small by tuning the system-bath coupling strength. They further introduce a randomization of the evolution time to suppress many-body resonances excited by the periodic system-bath evolution and thus remove possible divergences in the error bound.
While this picture is supported by perturbative arguments, our main contribution is to establish it rigorously and sharpen the resulting analysis and error bounds.
Concretely, we prove that the fixed-point error of the randomized protocol scales quadratically in the system-bath coupling strength, and hence can be made arbitrarily small by tuning this coupling strength. This implies an overall resource scaling of order
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
for preparing the fixed point to accuracy
\epsilon
, which is better than existing protocols that control the fixed-point error by other means.

\mathcal{K}[\rho]
to prepare a thermal state. At each step, a set of ancillas is initialized in the product state

, with the interaction

). The final time
\frac{T}{2}+x

The fixed point
\rho_{\mathrm{fix}}
of the channel
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
is approximately the thermal state
\rho_{\beta}=Z_{\beta}^{-1}e^{-\beta H}
, with a rigorous error bound scaling as
J^{2}
.

Our rigorous bounds are obtained for the fictitious channel that is the average over various realizations.
Practical realizations of the protocol would however be stochastic: in each time step, one evolves for an evolution time sampled at random from a specified distribution. While this procedure is ‘self-averaging’, in that the expectation value over such sequences reproduces the desired averaged channel, the randomization introduces additional variance into measurements of observables, thereby increasing the number of samples required to estimate expectation values. To our knowledge, this additional sampling variance has not been previously noted or studied for quantum Gibbs samplers. Our second main result is to quantify the additional overhead incurred by the randomization step and demonstrate that it does not severely impact the efficiency of the protocol. Specifically, we derive bounds on the extra variance generated by repeated applications of the randomized protocol.
We further support this analysis with perturbative arguments showing that omitting the randomization step worsens the fixed-point error, even in the presence of only sparse resonances.

Our results provide a comprehensive and rigorous analysis of several practically relevant properties of the thermal state preparation protocol, and in particular show how the coupling strength and other parameters can be tuned to achieve a prescribed accuracy. Taken together, these results establish the algorithm firmly as a promising candidate for implementation on near-term digital and analog quantum platforms.

The remainder of this work is organized as follows. In Section
II
, we review the aspects of quantum Gibbs samplers that are most relevant for the present work, before introducing the protocol in Section
III
. In Section
IV
, we highlight several properties of the channel that are used in subsequent derivations of error bounds. In Section
V
, we state our main analytical results concerning the fixed-point error and the variance of observable measurements. In Section
VI
, we present a numerical analysis of the scaling of the spectral gap of the channel with the coupling strength, the role of the randomization step, and the variance of observables. Finally, Section
VII
contains a discussion of the implications of our results, as well as several open questions.

II
Exact Gibbs samplers

We wish to prepare the state
\rho_{\beta}=Z_{\beta}^{-1}e^{-\beta H}
, where
H
is a given local Hamiltonian and
Z_{\beta}=\Tr e^{-\beta H}
is the partition function. This can be done by means of constructing a Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
which fixes this state as its unique steady state
\mathcal{L}[\rho_{\beta}]=0
.

While the general construction of such a ‘quantum Gibbs sampler’ for a long time remained an open problem, following the breakthrough of Ref.
6
there now exist a number of different constructions in the literature
14
;
3
;
6
;
7
;
18
.
Here, we use the construction of Ref.
14
. In the following, we focus on aspects of the construction which are important for our purposes. For a full exposition of KMS detailed balance and quantum Gibbs samplers, we refer the reader for example to Refs.
20
;
14
;
19
.

Consider the following family of Lindbladians:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DB}}[\rho]=-i[G_{\rm{DB}},\rho]+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}L_{a}\rho L^{\dagger}_{a}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{a}^{\dagger}L_{a},\rho\},

(1)

with

\displaystyle L_{a}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}tf(t)e^{iHt}A_{a}e^{-iHt}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}tf(t)A_{a}(t).

(2)

Here,
A_{a}(t)
denotes the Heisenberg evolution
A_{a}(t)=e^{iHt}A_{a}e^{-iHt}
of an operator
A_{a}
,
and the filter function
f(t)
is chosen such that it satisfies a particular symmetry
relation

\displaystyle f(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\mathrm{d}\nu\,q(\nu)e^{\frac{\beta\nu}{4}}\mathrm{e}^{i\nu t},\text{ with }q(-\nu)=q^{*}(\nu).

(3)

Using the Bohr frequency representation,

\displaystyle A_{\nu}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in{\rm Spec}(H)\\
\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}=\nu\end{subarray}}\ket{\omega_{1}}\braket{\omega_{1}|A|\omega_{2}}\bra{\omega_{2}},

(4)

where
\mathrm{Spec}(H)
denotes the set of eigenvalues of
H
, the coherent part can then be expressed as

\displaystyle G_{\rm{DB}}=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\nu\in\mathfrak{B}}\tanh(\frac{\beta\nu}{4})(L^{\dagger}_{a}L_{a})_{\nu},

(5)

with
\mathfrak{B}
denoting the set of Bohr frequencies of the Hamiltonian
\mathfrak{B}=\{\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}:\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathrm{Spec}(H)\}
.

It can be shown
14
that the jump operators in Eq. (
2
) as well as the coherent part in Eq. (
5
) are quasi-local if the filter function
f(t)
decays faster than a power law.

The coherent part and jump operators are carefully constructed such that the Lindbladian in Eq. (
1
) fulfills KMS detailed balance
7
;
1
;
2
;
24
;
16
.
Given the superoperator

\displaystyle\Gamma[\cdot]=\rho_{\beta}^{1/2}(\cdot)\rho_{\beta}^{1/2},

(6)

this relation can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{DB}}=\Gamma^{-1}\circ\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DB}}\circ\Gamma.

(7)

Here, the adjoint operator is defined with respect to the Frobenius inner product

\Tr[\mathcal{L}[\rho]\mathcal{O}]=\Tr[\rho\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\mathcal{O}]]
. Trace preservation under Lindbladian evolution implies
e^{t\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}}[\mathbb{I}]=\mathbb{I}
and therefore
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\mathbb{I}]=0
.

Importantly, KMS detailed balance [Eq. (
7
)], directly implies that
\rho_{\beta}
is a fixed point of the dynamics under
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DB}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DB}}[\rho_{\beta}]=\rho_{\beta}^{1/2}\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{DB}}[\rho_{\beta}^{-1/2}\circ\rho_{\beta}\circ\rho_{\beta}^{-1/2}]\rho_{\beta}^{1/2}=0.

(8)

III
Setup

In this section, we introduce the setup of the thermal state preparation algorithm studied in this work. As shown in Fig.
1
, the protocol is defined on a system of
n_{S}
qubits coupled to a bath of
n_{B}
ancilla qubits. One step of the protocol consists of initialization of the bath qubits to a product state
\rho_{B}^{0}=\outerproduct{0}{0}_{B}
, followed by evolution with a time-dependent, joint system-bath Hamiltonian
H_{SB}(t)
, and then reset of the bath qubits to
\rho_{B}^{0}
.
Note that in principle,
n_{B}=1
is expected to be sufficient for generic Hamiltonians
28
; however, the convergence time of the algorithm is expected to improve using
n_{B}=\mathcal{O}(n_{S})
ancilla qubits [as discussed below, we will aim to approximate a given exact Gibbs sampler, in which case we need one ancilla per jump operator
L_{a}
in Eq. (
1
)].

The system-bath Hamiltonian used during the protocol is given by

\displaystyle H_{SB}(t)=H\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{B}+{\mathbb{I}}_{S}\otimes H_{B}+JV(t).

(9)

Here,
H
is the system Hamiltonian,
H_{B}
is the bath Hamiltonian, and the term
JV(t)
is the interaction between system and bath, where
J
denotes the strength of this coupling.

The interaction term
V(t)
takes the form

\displaystyle V(t)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}f(t)A_{a}\otimes B_{a}^{\dagger}+f^{*}(t)A_{a}^{\dagger}\otimes B_{a}.

(10)

The set of jump operators
\mathcal{A}
can be chosen arbitrarily, as long as they are closed under Hermitian conjugation (
A_{a}\in\mathcal{A}\Rightarrow A^{\dagger}_{a}\in\mathcal{A}
) and guarantee irreducibility of the Lindbladian evolution. (A convenient sufficient choice independent of the Hamiltonian
H
would be, for instance, the single-qubit Pauli operators
X
and
Y
on each site.)

Each jump operator is coupled to a distinct bath qubit, where

B=\frac{1}{2}(X_{B}-iY_{B})
and
B^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{2}(X_{B}+iY_{B})
are the lowering and raising operators of the corresponding bath qubit, respectively.
The filter function
f(t)
is chosen such that it fulfills the relation defined in Eq. (
3
) for exact Gibbs samplers.
For the rest of this work, for concreteness, we use

\displaystyle f(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}(t-\frac{i\beta}{4})^{2}}.

(11)

However, we expect our results to hold more generically, as long as
f(t)
fulfills Eq. (
3
) and decays sufficiently fast.

The dynamics of the bath is chosen to be trivial
H_{B}={\mathbb{I}}_{B}
. Although we do not use it here, an equivalent construction can instead introduce a bath Hamiltonian and choose the interaction amplitude
f(t)
to be real, which may be easier to implement in practice
20
;
27
.

We define the following propagator
U(t)

\displaystyle U\left(t+\frac{T}{2}\right)=\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{-\frac{T}{2}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,H_{SB}(s)},

(12)

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes time ordering, and
T
is an interaction timescale fixed as a parameter of the algorithm.
At each step of the algorithm, the ancilla qubits are initialized in the product state
\rho_{B}^{0}=\ket{0}\bra{0}_{B}
at time
{t=-\frac{T}{2}}
. The combined system is then evolved under Eq. (
9
) until time
t=\frac{T}{2}+x
, where the shift
x
is drawn from a Gaussian distribution with randomization timescale
T_{0}

\displaystyle p(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi T_{0}^{2}}}e^{-\left(\frac{x}{T_{0}}\right)^{2}}.

(13)

At the end of the cycle, the ancilla qubits are reset back to
\rho_{B}^{0}=\ket{0}\bra{0}_{B}
.
For a given
x
, one step of the protocol thus applies a channel
\mathcal{K}_{x}[\rho]
on the system, which takes the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{x}[\rho]=\Tr_{B}\left[U\left(T+x\right)(\rho\otimes\rho_{B}^{0})U^{\dagger}\left(T+x\right)\right].

(14)

We define
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
as the average of
\mathcal{K}_{x}[\rho]
over the distribution of evolution time shifts
x
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[\rho]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,p(x)\mathcal{K}_{x}[\rho].

(15)

As was suggested in Ref.
27
, the averaging over different evolution times acts effectively as a ‘dephaser’ that removes possible resonances of the drive with possible transitions in the system’s many-body spectrum and eliminates potential divergences in the fixed-point error expansion that plague the deterministic limit of the protocol. This point will be discussed more carefully in Sec.
IV.4
.

As mentioned in the introduction, although in practice one implements the randomized protocol, to obtain rigorous bounds it is more convenient to take a two-step approach. First, we invoke the self-averaging property of the randomized evolution to focus on the fixed-point error of the average channel
\mathcal{K}
. However, the real randomized protocol incurs errors in observables relative to
\mathcal{K}
, controlled by the variance of the time-step distribution; our second step is to bound this error. Taken together, these serve to rigorously bound the error of the true randomized protocol.

The convergence of the channel toward the fixed point is characterized by its mixing time
\tau_{\mathcal{K},\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)
. It is defined as the minimum number of iterations of the channel
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
required so that any initial state is guaranteed to be within
\epsilon
of its fixed point
\rho_{\mathrm{fix}}
.

\tau_{\mathcal{K},\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)=\min\left\{\,M\in\mathbb{N}\,\middle|\,\sup_{\rho}\left\|\mathcal{K}^{M}[\rho]-\rho_{\mathrm{fix}}\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon\right\}.

(16)

Here, the distance to the fixed point is quantified using the trace norm,

\displaystyle\norm{\rho}_{1}=\Tr[\sqrt{\rho\rho^{\dagger}}].

(17)

IV
Methods and Heuristic Overview

In this section, we provide some results on the properties of the channel
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
that are necessary for bounding the fixed-point error. We first show, in Sec.
IV.1
, that the channel is well-approximated by a Lindbladian evolution followed by a unitary evolution with the system Hamiltonian. Next, we construct an approximate fixed point of this combined evolution in Sec.
IV.2
and show that its distance to
\rho_{\beta}
is controlled by the coupling strength
J
.
In Sec.
IV.3
, we provide a heuristic explanation for the role of the unitary evolution in controlling the distance of the fixed point to the thermal state.
Finally, in Sec.
IV.4
, we heuristically study the effect of resonances between the driving period of the system-bath coupling and transitions in the many-body spectrum of
H
, which demonstrates the necessity of the randomized evolution time.

IV.1
Approximation of the channel

The average channel
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
almost implements a Lindblad evolution under a Gibbs sampling algorithm, followed by unitary dynamics generated by
H
. Concretely, consider the Lindbladian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]=-i[\Delta G,\rho]+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DB}}[\rho],

(18)

with

\displaystyle\Delta G=G_{\mathrm{LS}}-G_{\mathrm{DB}}.

(19)

The Lamb shift term
G_{\mathrm{LS}}
is given by

G_{\mathrm{LS}}=-\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\,\text{sign}(t_{1}-t_{2})\\
f^{*}(t_{2})f(t_{1})A^{\dagger}_{a}(t_{2})A_{a}(t_{1}).

(20)

This Lindbladian agrees with the exact Gibbs sampler in Eq. (
1
), up to a different coherent term given by
G_{\mathrm{LS}}
.
Furthermore, denote unitary evolution by the system alone by
U_{0}(t)=e^{-iHt}
and consider the channel

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,p(x)U_{0}(T+x)\,\mathrm{e}^{J^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}}\rho\,U_{0}^{\dagger}(T+x).

(21)

The difference between
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]
and the average channel
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
is then bounded by

\norm{\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}}_{1\rightarrow 1}\\
=\mathcal{O}\left(J^{2}n_{B}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{T^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}+4T_{0}^{2}}}+J^{4}n_{B}^{2}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right),

(22)

where the difference between channels is characterized by the induced norm

\displaystyle\norm{\mathcal{M}}_{1\rightarrow 1}=\sup_{\rho}\frac{\norm{\mathcal{M}[\rho]}_{1}}{\norm{\rho}_{1}}.

(23)

The result follows directly from a Dyson series expansion and is derived in App.
C.1
.
The first error term originates from truncating Gaussian tails of the filter function due to the finite-time system-bath evolution in each iteration step [cf. Fig.
1
]. This term is exponentially suppressed in the length of the time evolution
T
.
The second term bounds contributions from higher-order terms of the Dyson series expansion.

From the definition of Eq. (
21
), we see that
J^{2}
plays the role of a time step of the evolution with an effective Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}_{\rm LS}
. Since the effective Lindbladian is the only dissipative part of the protocol, this suggests (although does not show) that the mixing time of the channel
\mathcal{K}_{\rm LS}
,
\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)\equiv\tau_{\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}},\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)
, scales as the inverse of this step size
J^{-2}
.
It is then natural to introduce a rescaled mixing time
{t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)}
of the channel

t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)=J^{2}\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon).

(24)

Although we expect that
t_{\mathrm{mix},J}
approaches a constant for
J\rightarrow 0
, and we provide numerical evidence for this in Sec.
VI
, we stress that we do not have a general proof that
\lim_{J\rightarrow 0}t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)
exists.

There exist some cases where the existence of this limit can be rigorously established, when
2\norm{\rho_\beta^{1/4}\Delta G \rho_\beta^{-1/4}}_{\infty}
is smaller than the spectral gap of the Gibbs sampler
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DB}}

15
, but establishing this limit more generally remains an interesting direction for future work.
In Sec.
VI
, we provide numerical evidence suggesting that this scaling persists even when these existing rigorous results do not apply.

Finally, we note that if
T
is chosen sufficiently large and
J
sufficiently small so that

\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}\!\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\,\norm{\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}}_{1\to 1}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2},

(25)

then the mixing times
\tau_{\mathcal{K},\mathrm{mix}}
of
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
and
\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}
of
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]
are related by [App. D in Ref.
15
and Lemma
2
, App.
A
]

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{K},\mathrm{mix}}(2\epsilon)\leq\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}\!\left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right).

(26)

IV.2
Approximate fixed point of
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]

In this subsection, we show that the channel
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]
has an approximate fixed point
\tilde{\rho}
with the following properties:

1.

\quad\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde{\rho}]=\tilde{\rho}+\mathcal{O}(J^{4})

2.

\quad\norm{\tilde{\rho}-\rho_\beta}_{1}=\mathcal{O}(J^{2})

This fact will be crucial to obtain our rigorous fixed-point error bound in Sec.
V.1
.

An ansatz for the approximate fixed point
\tilde{\rho}
can be motivated by degenerate perturbation theory as done in Ref.
27
. While degenerate perturbation theory is not a controlled order-by-order expansion, the first-order expression is sufficient for our purposes.
We outline the main steps here.

The strategy is to match the relation
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde{\rho}]=\tilde{\rho}
[cf. Eq. (
21
)] with an ansatz
\tilde{\rho}=\rho^{(0)}+J^{2}\sigma
up to order

J^{2}
.
This is done in the eigenbasis of the Hamiltonian (
H\ket{\phi_{a}}=E_{a}\ket{\phi_{a}}
,
A_{ab}=\braket{\phi_{a}|A|\phi_{b}}
,
\omega_{ab}=E_{a}-E_{b}
).

Equating leading-order terms of order
J^{0}
yields

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{4}\omega_{ab}^{2}T_{0}^{2}-i\omega_{ab}T}\rho^{(0)}_{ab}=\rho^{(0)}_{ab},

(27)

which implies that
{\rho}^{(0)}
is diagonal in the eigenbasis of
H
.
To determine
{\rho}^{(0)}
explicitly, it is necessary to consider the
J^{2}
contributions for the diagonal elements.
These give

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho^{(0)}])_{aa}=0.

(28)

It follows that
\rho^{(0)}=\rho_{\beta}
, since
([\Delta G,\rho^{(0)}])_{aa}=0
. This shows that this ansatz satisfies

\norm{\tilde{\rho}-\rho_\beta}_{1}=\mathcal{O}(J^{2})

(29)

as long as
\norm{\sigma}_{1}
is bounded. The relation for the off-diagonal elements
a\neq b
at order
J^{2}
is given by

\displaystyle e^{-i\omega_{ab}T}\left(-i[\Delta G,\rho_{\beta}]_{ab}+e^{-\frac{1}{4}\omega_{ab}^{2}T_{0}^{2}}\sigma_{ab}\right)=\sigma_{ab}.

(30)

Solving for
\sigma_{ab}
yields,

\displaystyle\sigma_{ab}=\frac{-ie^{-i\omega_{ab}T}[\Delta G,\rho_{\beta}]_{ab}}{1-e^{-\frac{1}{4}\omega_{ab}^{2}T_{0}^{2}-i\omega_{ab}T}}.

(31)

We can now substitute the ansatz
\tilde{\rho}=\rho_{\beta}+J^{2}\sigma
as defined above back into
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}
, and rigorously show that (1)

\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde{\rho}]=\tilde{\rho}+\mathcal{O}(J^{4})
, and (2) its distance from
\rho_{\beta}
is bounded by [App.
C.2
]

\displaystyle\norm{\tilde{\rho} - \rho_\beta}_{1}=J^{2}\norm{\sigma}_{1}\leq J^{2}\mathrm{poly}\left(n_{B},\frac{\beta}{\sigma},\frac{T}{\sigma},\frac{T}{T_{0}}\right)e^{\frac{9\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}.

(32)

IV.3
Heuristic picture

The explicit form of
\sigma
in Eq. (
31
) as well as the bound in Eq. (
32
) suggests that the presence of the additional propagator
U_{0}(T+x)
in the channel
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]
is crucial to ensure
\norm{\tilde{\rho}-\rho_{\beta}}_{1}=\mathcal{O}(J^{2})
.
To see this more concretely it is useful to separate the action of the channel into two distinct steps in line with our discussion above, namely ‘fixing the diagonal algebra’ [via Eq. (
27
)] and ‘selecting the Gibbs weights’ [via Eq. (
30
)] within it. When the unitary step is eliminated (as achieved, e.g. by the ‘rewinding’ step in Ref.
20
) the channel at
\mathcal{O}(J^{0})
is just the identity so
every
possible operator is a fixed point. Then, at
\mathcal{O}(J^{2})
the whole Lindbladian correction has to choose the fixed point, and so we have to simply diagonalize the
\mathcal{O}(J^{2})
problem: evidently, there can be no sense in which this is a perturbative procedure in
J^{2}
.

Contrast this to the case when the unitary step is implemented: here the zeroth order map is instead coherent evolution with
H
; ignoring resonances for the moment, the fixed operators of this are, as we have argued, diagonal in the eigenbasis of
H
.
So already at
\mathcal{O}(J^{0})
the unitary part removes the off-diagonal degeneracy of the fixed point, and then within the set of diagonal states, there is a clear difference between the action of the different terms at
\mathcal{O}(J^{2})
. The detailed-balance piece
\mathcal{L}_{\rm DB}
fixes the weights within the diagonal sector to agree with
\rho_{\beta}
, and this happens for
any
finite
J
, even as
J\to 0^{+}
. The coherent perturbation (the difference between the Lamb shift and the ‘correct’ coherent part) is off-diagonal, and, since the off-diagonal elements of
\tilde{\rho}
are fixed to zero at
\order{J^0}
, it can only induce coherences perturbatively in
J^{2}
.

Finally, Eq. (
31
) highlights the role of the probabilistic choice of the final time
t=\frac{T}{2}+x
. In the deterministic case
T_{0}=0
, the denominator can vanish whenever
\omega_{ab}T=2\pi k
with
k
an integer. This characterizes many-body resonances induced by the time-dependent system-bath interaction. The randomized
evolution time eliminates them by dephasing.

We see, therefore, that the additional unitary evolution plays a crucial role in privileging the commutant of the algebra generated by
H
from within the vastly bigger full operator Hilbert space already at
\mathcal{O}(J^{0})
, enabling a controlled treatment in
J^{2}
, and in turn necessitates randomized evolution to eliminate resonances in this procedure.

IV.4
Effect of many-body resonances

As discussed in the previous subsection, resonances cause divergences in Eq. (
31
) if
T_{0}=0
and
\omega_{ab}T=2\pi k
with
k
an integer. It is natural to ask whether these resonances remain isolated, affecting only the (few) matrix elements directly corresponding to the resonant transitions. We show here that they do not: resonances can also modify populations in non-resonant subspaces.

To illustrate this, we construct an approximate fixed point
\rho_{0}
of the channel
\mathcal{K}_{0}[\rho]
corresponding to the deterministic choice
T_{0}=0
. To this end, we follow the same arguments that led to our ansatz for the approximate fixed point of
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]
in Sec.
IV.2
.

As before, the channel
\mathcal{K}_{0}[\rho]
can be approximated by a Lindblad evolution and a unitary evolution,

\mathcal{K}_{0}[\rho]=e^{-iHT}\rho e^{iHT}+J^{2}e^{-iHT}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]e^{iHT}+\mathcal{O}(J^{4}).

(33)

We again match the relation

\mathcal{K}_{0}[\rho_{0}]=\rho_{0}
with the ansatz

\rho_{0}=\rho^{(0)}+J^{2}\rho^{(1)}
. At order
J^{0}
, this gives

e^{-iHT}\rho^{(0)}e^{iHT}=\rho^{(0)}.

(34)

Note that already in this order there is a difference between the resonant and non-resonant case. At a resonance where
\omega_{ab}T=2\pi k
, Eq. (
34
) is satisfied even for non-diagonal
\rho^{(0)}
.

In order to analyze the matrix elements in the eigenbasis of
H
, we split it into a resonant subspace
\{a,b\}
(where
e^{i\omega_{ab}T}=1
) and a non-resonant subspace
\{i,j\}
(where
e^{i\omega_{ij}T}\neq 1
). Eq. (
34
) then implies that
\rho^{(0)}
has zero elements,

(\rho^{(0)})_{ai}=0,\ (\rho^{(0)})_{ij}=0,

(35)

and nonzero elements,

(\rho^{(0)})_{aa}\neq 0,\ (\rho^{(0)})_{ii}\neq 0,\ (\rho^{(0)})_{ab}\neq 0.

(36)

Notably, off-diagonal elements
(\rho^{(0)})_{ab}
in the resonant subspace are now in general allowed to be nonzero. To fully determine
\rho^{(0)}
we have to consider the order
J^{2}
contributions in this subspace.
At second order, it is given by

e^{-iHT}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho^{(0)}]+\rho^{(1)}\big)e^{iHT}=\rho^{(1)}

(37)

which implies that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho^{(0)}]
has zero elements,

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho^{(0)}])_{aa}&=0,\\
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho^{(0)}])_{ii}&=0,\\
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho^{(0)}])_{ab}&=0,\end{split}

(38)

and nonzero elements,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho^{(0)}])_{ai}\neq 0,\ (\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho^{(0)}])_{ij}\neq 0.

(39)

The structures of
\rho^{(0)}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho^{(0)}]
are summarized below for the case of a 4-level system, where the box indicates the resonant space
\{a,b\}
, and matrix elements are represented by ‘0’ if zero or ‘
\times
’ if generally nonzero.

\rho^{(0)}\sim\begin{pmatrix}\boxed{\begin{matrix}\times&\times\\
\times&\times\end{matrix}}&\begin{matrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\\[10.00002pt]
\begin{matrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}&\begin{matrix}\times&0\\
0&\times\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix},\ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho^{(0)}]\sim\begin{pmatrix}\boxed{\begin{matrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}}&\begin{matrix}\times&\times\\
\times&\times\end{matrix}\\[10.00002pt]
\begin{matrix}\times&\times\\
\times&\times\end{matrix}&\begin{matrix}0&\times\\
\times&0\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix}

(40)

The state
\rho^{(0)}
is then determined implicitly as the solution of Eq. (
40
).
Already its structure is different from the non-resonant case, and in particular
\rho^{0}
is not fixed to be diagonal. Furthermore,
in Sec.
VI.2
, we obtain the solution to Eq. (
40
) numerically for a small system. The results show that, at resonance, the matrix elements of the approximate fixed point
\rho_{0}
differ from those of
\rho_{\beta}
even in the limit
J\to 0^{+}
, and that this discrepancy persists even within non-resonant blocks.
Thus, the effects of resonances are not even confined to resonant blocks, but also extend to non-resonant populations. A finite value of
T_{0}
is therefore essential for suppressing these resonance-induced corrections.

V
Rigorous Results

In this section, we present analytical results characterizing the performance of the algorithm. In Sec.
V.1
, we use the approximation of the channel
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
by
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]
and the existence of an approximate fixed point close to
\rho_{\beta}
to obtain a fixed-point error bound. Furthermore, we provide upper bounds for the variance of observables in Sec.
V.2
.

V.1
Fixed-point error bound

Equipped with the observation that the channel
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
effectively implements a Lindbladian evolution followed by a unitary evolution and that this combined evolution has an approximate fixed point close to
\rho_{\beta}
, it is possible to derive the main result of this work: a rigorous bound on the fixed-point error controlled by
J^{2}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\rho_{\rm{fix}}
denote the fixed point of the channel
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
. Denote the parameters of
\mathcal{K}
as the width of the filter function
\sigma
[cf. Eq. (
11
)], the randomization timescale
T_{0}
[cf. Eq. (
13
)], the total evolution time
T
, and the inverse temperature
\beta
. If
\sigma<T
and
T_{0}<T
, then

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\norm{\rho_{\beta}-\rho_{\rm{fix}}}_{1}\leq 2\epsilon+t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)n_{B}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{T^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}+4T_{0}^{2}}}\\
&\qquad+J^{2}t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)\,\mathrm{poly}\left(n_{B},\frac{\beta}{\sigma},\frac{T}{\sigma},\frac{T}{T_{0}}\right)e^{\frac{5\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\end{split}

(41)

The full expression for the fixed-point error (including the explicit form of the
{\rm poly}(\cdots)
term) is given in Eq. (
77
). The second term is a tail error, originating from the channel approximation in Eq. (
22
). This term is exponentially suppressed as a function of
T/\sigma
and can therefore be made small by (mildly) increasing the evolution time between resets.
The third term of this error bound scales quadratically in
J
, and can thus be controlled by tuning the coupling strength
J
.

To obtain this bound, the strategy is to use the approximate fixed point of the channel
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}
, constructed in Sec.
IV.2
. Recall that
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde{\rho}]=\tilde{\rho}+\mathcal{O}(J^{4})
and also that
\tilde{\rho}
is close to the thermal state.
Since, as discussed in Sec.
IV.1
,
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}
is close to the effective channel
\mathcal{K}
implemented by our protocol, a fixed-point bound on
\norm{\rho_\beta-\rho_{\rm{fix}}}_{1}
then follows from a triangle inequality and closeness of fixed points of close channels. In particular (see Lemma
1
, App.
A
for details),

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\norm{\rho_{\mathrm{fix}}-\rho_{\beta}}_{1}\leq\norm{\rho_{\mathrm{fix}}-\tilde{\rho}}_{1}+\norm{\tilde{\rho}-\rho_{\beta}}_{1}\\
&\leq 2\epsilon+\frac{t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)}{J^{2}}\left(\norm{\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}}_{1\rightarrow 1}+\norm{\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde{\rho}]-\tilde{\rho}}_{1}\right)\\
&\phantom{\leq}+\norm{\tilde{\rho}-\rho_{\beta}}_{1}.\end{split}

(42)

Rigorous bounds for
\norm{\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde{\rho}]-\tilde{\rho}}_{1}
and
\norm{\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}}_{1\rightarrow 1}
are obtained in App.
C
.
Substituting these above yields Theorem
1
.

Note that the inequalities in Eq. (
42
) hold for any density matrix

\tilde{\rho}
. The key insight of choosing the approximate fixed point

\tilde{\rho}=\rho_{\beta}+J^{2}\sigma
is that in this case all terms are controlled by a factor of
J^{2}
. Indeed,

\norm{\tilde{\rho}-\rho_\beta}_{1}=J^{2}\norm{\sigma}_{1}
(for a
\sigma
with bounded norm as
J\to 0
) and
\frac{t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)}{J^{2}}\norm{\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde{\rho}]-\tilde{\rho}}_{1}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(J^{2}t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)\right)
.

Provided that
t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)=o(J^{-2})
(or, equivalently,
J^{2}\,t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)\to 0
as
J\to 0
), this result shows that the fixed-point error of the protocol can be made arbitrarily small by decreasing the coupling strength. Note that
t_{\mathrm{mix},J}
here is already the rescaled mixing time; see Eq. (
24
) and the surrounding discussion.
Consequently, to achieve the target accuracy
\epsilon
, one expects the scaling

\displaystyle J^{2}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{t_{\mathrm{mix},J}}\right).

(43)

Here, we have suppressed the dependence on the other protocol parameters and
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}
suppresses logarithmic dependencies on its argument.
The total Hamiltonian evolution time required to prepare an approximation to the thermal state is then given by

\displaystyle T_{\rm{tot}}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{Tt_{\mathrm{mix},J}^{2}}{\epsilon}\right).

(44)

Note that this bound qualitatively improves upon previous results obtained in Refs.
20
;
26
, which estimated the closeness of the fixed point of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}
to
\rho_{\beta}
.

V.2
Bounds on the variance

In practice, the averaged channel
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
is not implemented directly. Rather, at each step a random evolution time
x_{i}
is sampled, giving rise to the channel
\mathcal{K}_{x_{i}}[\rho]
. After
M
steps, this defines the random channel

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{M}[\rho]=\mathcal{K}_{x_{M}}\circ\mathcal{K}_{x_{M-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{K}_{x_{1}}[\rho].

(45)

Expectation values of observables are thus obtained by averaging over repeated realizations of the random channels
\mathcal{E}_{M}
, which reproduces the effect of
M
applications of the same averaged channel
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}_{M}\mathcal{E}_{M}[\rho]&={\mathbb{E}}_{M}\mathcal{K}_{x_{M}}\circ\mathcal{K}_{x_{M-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{K}_{x_{1}}[\rho]\\
&=\mathcal{K}^{M}[\rho].\end{split}

(46)

However, the randomness induces an additional source of variance. To make this explicit, we introduce the following notation.
For an arbitrary initial state
\rho
, denote the expectation value of
O
with respect to this random sequence as
\braket{O}_{M}=\Tr[O\mathcal{E}_{M}[\rho]]
.
The variance of the observable
O
can then be expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{Var}(O)&=\mathbb{E}_{M}\left[\braket{O^{2}}_{M}-\braket{O}_{M}^{2}\right]\\
&+\left[\mathbb{E}_{M}\braket{O}_{M}^{2}-\left(\mathbb{E}_{M}\braket{O}_{M}\right)^{2}\right].\end{split}

(47)

The first term is the usual shot-noise contribution, averaged over sequences, while the second term is the (sequence-to-sequence) variance of the mean
\expectationvalue{O}_{M}
. While the first term remains finite even in the deterministic limit
T_{0}\to 0
, the second contribution is an additional contribution to the variance arising from the randomness in the channel.

The following theorem provides an upper bound on the variance of observables under repeated application of the randomized channel.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{M}
denote the random channel obtained after
M
applications of the protocol. Then, for any observable
O
, the variance satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{Var}(O)&\leq\norm{O}^{2}_{\infty}+\\
&C^{2}T_{0}^{2}\frac{4}{\pi}\norm{H}^{2}_{\infty}\norm{O}^{2}_{\infty}\frac{1-e^{-\frac{2M}{\tau_{\rm{mix}}}}}{1-e^{-\frac{2}{\tau_{\rm{mix}}}}}\end{split}

(48)

The proof of this bound is provided in App.
D
. The constant
C
is determined by the contraction estimate for a primitive channel

\displaystyle\norm{\mathcal{K}^{\dagger(n)}[O-\Tr[\rho_{\mathrm{fix}}O]\mathbb{I}]}_{\infty}<Ce^{-n/\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}}\norm{O}_{\infty}.

(49)

The prefactor
C
depends on structural properties of the channel, such as the size of any Jordan blocks
40
. In general,
C
can be upper-bounded by a factor proportional to the mixing time. In many relevant cases, however,
C=\mathcal{O}(1)
; for example, this is true when the channel is contractive, as at high temperatures
3
.
We provide a numerical analysis of the fluctuations induced by a randomized evolution in Sec.
VI.3
.

VI.1
Scaling of the spectral gap

One important ingredient in the fixed-point error bound is the mixing time
\tau_{\rm mix}
. As shown in Sec.
IV.1

\mathcal{K}[\rho]
implements up to a small error a Lindblad evolution of time
J^{2}
followed by a unitary evolution of the system Hamiltonian. As long as the unitary evolution does not strongly affect the mixing, this suggests a scaling of the channel mixing time with
1/J^{2}

(51)

Let

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm gap}=1-s_{2}.

(52)

Then an upper bound on the mixing time is given by
41

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathcal{K},{\rm mix}}(\epsilon)\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\rm gap}}\log\!\left(\frac{2\norm{\rho_{\rm fix}^{-1/2}}}{\epsilon}\right).

(53)

, leads to additional fluctuations in observables. To this end, we construct 50 realizations of random channel sequences
\mathcal{E}_{M}[\rho]
and measure the expectation value
\braket{O}=\Tr[O\,\mathcal{E}_{M}[\rho]]
for a given observable
O
.

The results for two qubits are shown in Fig.
5
, for the observables
O=H
and
O=Z_{1}Z_{2}
. They indicate that the randomized implementation exhibits substantial fluctuations around the mean-channel result. Consequently, obtaining accurate estimates for observables cannot generally be done using a single random channel, and instead requires averaging over a number of channel realizations in parallel. This introduces an additional sampling overhead depending on the desired accuracy.

It is interesting to observe that the fluctuations decrease with increasing system size, as shown for
n_{S}=6
sites in Fig.
6
. One possible explanation in this case is that the mixed-field Ising model is thermalizing and therefore random trajectories concentrate. Understanding more precisely how the randomized evolution affects the variance would be a useful direction for future study.

As a final note, in addition to randomizing evolution times, other works
15
;
8
have also proposed randomizing the bath Hamiltonian and randomizing jump operators. While these are useful for bounding the mixing time, there is the practical trade-off of an increased variance. We present numerical evidence of this in App.
E
.

(b)].

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we have studied an algorithm for thermal state preparation based on a time-dependent system-bath interaction. The algorithm requires only Hamiltonian evolution with local control over time-dependent interactions together with the ability to reset ancillas. It is therefore readily implementable on digital quantum devices and may also be feasible on analog quantum platforms.

Our first main result is
a rigorous fixed-point error bound that is governed by the mixing time of the channel and scales quadratically with the coupling strength
J
. As a consequence, the error can, in principle, be made arbitrarily small by reducing
J
. This result improves upon previous work on similar protocols, where such fixed-point error bounds did not explicitly account for the dependence on the system-bath coupling strength
20
;
15
, or were based on non-rigorous perturbative arguments
27
.

This favorable scaling in the coupling strength
J
comes at the cost of randomizing the duration of the system-bath interaction, and our analysis shows that this step cannot, in general, be omitted without degrading the accuracy of the thermal state preparation. In practice, the randomization means that the protocol implements evolution with a stochastic series of channels. This induces an additional variance of observables measured during the protocol: our second main contribution is to rigorously bound this resulting statistical error.

There are several important directions for future work. To fully establish practical feasibility of system-bath interaction protocols such as the one studied in this work, it is imperative to develop a more careful understanding of how to optimize the protocol with respect to its many parameters, and how optimal choices depend on features (e.g. the phase) of the system Hamiltonian and of its thermal state.
Recently, Ref.
44
also showed that instead of using the system-bath coupling
J
as a control parameter, a controlled fixed-point error can also be achieved by tuning parameters of the filter function at finite
J
. A natural task is therefore to clarify the differences, and possibly the interplay, between these complementary limits of controlled thermal state preparation using system-bath interactions.

On a more technical level, control of the fixed-point error in our bounds relies on the assumption that the rescaled mixing time of the protocol scales as
o(J^{-2})
as
J\to 0
. Although this assumption seems physically sound, and is supported by numerical results on the mixed-field Ising model, it is clearly desirable to understand its general validity and, if possible, provide rigorous guarantees. We flag this as an important avenue for subsequent investigations.

More generally, it would be valuable to understand the relation between the mixing time of thermal state preparation protocols based on system-bath interactions and
their ‘proximate’ exact Gibbs samplers. For the latter class of Lindbladians, there is an increasing understanding of mixing times in physically relevant settings
34
;
35
;
3
;
43
;
39
;
31
;
33
;
17
.

A priori
, it is not entirely clear how to translate these results to near-term protocols, but obtaining mixing time estimates for the latter will be important to judge their applicability in practice. Here, we note that the related question of quantifying whether a generic system-bath model, possibly upon some suitable coarse-graining, is ‘close’ to an exact sampler has received some recent attention
37
, and may make a convenient starting point for exploring these issues.

Note added:

During the completion of this manuscript, we became aware of Ref.
8
, which studies fixed-point errors for algorithms that prepare thermal states through system-bath interactions. While the channels analyzed in this work differ slightly from the one studied here, the strategies used to derive fixed-point error bounds as a function of
J
agree with our approach.

Appendix A
Closeness of fixed points and mixing times

In this appendix, we present two lemmas adapted from Ref.
15
. Lemma 1 connects the closeness of fixed points to the mixing time and the distance between the corresponding channels. This result is used repeatedly in the derivation of the fixed-point error bounds.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{1}[\rho]
and
\mathcal{K}_{2}[\rho]
be two channels with fixed points
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
respectively. Let
\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)
be the mixing time of
\mathcal{K}_{1}[\rho]
. The trace distance between fixed points
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\norm{\rho_1 - \rho_2}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)\norm{\mathcal{K}_1[\rho_2] - \rho_2}_{1}

(54a)

\displaystyle\norm{\rho_1 - \rho_2}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon+\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)\norm{\mathcal{K}_1 - \mathcal{K}_2}_{1\to 1}

(54b)

Proof of Lemma 1.
The proof of Eq. (
54a
) begins with the following split:

\displaystyle\norm{\rho_1 - \rho_2}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{\rho_1 - \mathcal{K}_1^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)}[\rho_2]}_{1}+\norm{\mathcal{K}_1^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)}[\rho_2] - \rho_2}_{1}.

(55)

The first term is bounded by
\epsilon
by definition of
\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)
[Eq. (
16
)]. The second term can be expressed as a telescoping sum that is simplified using the data processing inequality.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\norm{\mathcal{K}_1^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)}[\rho_2] - \rho_2}_{1}&\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)-1}\norm{\mathcal{K}_1^{n+1}[\rho_2] - \mathcal{K}_1^n[\rho_2]}_{1}\\
&\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)-1}\norm{\mathcal{K}_1[\rho_2] - \rho_2}_{1}\\
&=\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)\norm{\mathcal{K}_1[\rho_2] - \rho_2}_{1}\end{split}

(56)

This completes the proof. The proof of Eq. (
54b
) then follows immediately using the fixed-point relation
\rho_{2}=\mathcal{K}_{2}[\rho_{2}]
,

\displaystyle\norm{\rho_1 - \rho_2}_{1}\leq\epsilon+\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)\norm{\mathcal{K}_1[\rho_2] - \mathcal{K}_2[\rho_2]}_{1}\leq\epsilon+\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)\norm{\mathcal{K}_1 - \mathcal{K}_2}_{1\to 1}

(57)

Lemma 2 shows that two sufficiently close channels have comparable mixing times. This is used to justify that the exact channel
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
has similar mixing times to the approximate channel
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{1}[\rho]
and
\mathcal{K}_{2}[\rho]
be two channels with mixing times
\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon),\tau_{2,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)
respectively. If the two channels are sufficiently close with
\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)\norm{\mathcal{K}_1 - \mathcal{K}_2}_{1\to 1}\leq\epsilon/2
,

\tau_{2,\mathrm{mix}}(2\epsilon)\leq\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)

(58)

Proof of Lemma 2.
Consider
\norm{\mathcal{K}_2^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)}[\rho] - \rho_2}_{1}
for any
\rho
, which can be split as follows.

\norm{\mathcal{K}_2^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)}[\rho] - \rho_2}_{1}\leq\norm{\mathcal{K}_2^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)}[\rho] - \mathcal{K}_1^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)}[\rho]}_{1}+\norm{\mathcal{K}_1^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)}[\rho] - \rho_1}_{1}+\norm{\rho_1 - \rho_2}_{1}

(59)

The second term is bounded by
\epsilon/2
by definition of
\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)
[Eq. (
16
)]. The third term is bounded by
\epsilon/2+\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)\norm{\mathcal{K}_1 - \mathcal{K}_2}_{1\to 1}
[Eq. (
54b
)]. The first term requires a telescoping sum as follows,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\norm{\mathcal{K}_2^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)}[\rho] - \mathcal{K}_1^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)}[\rho]}_{1}&\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)-1}\norm{\mathcal{K}_2^{n+1} \mathcal{K}_1^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)-n-1}[\rho] - \mathcal{K}_2^n \mathcal{K}_1^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)-n}[\rho]}_{1}\\
&=\sum_{n=0}^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)-1}\norm{\mathcal{K}_2^n \!\left( (\mathcal{K}_2-\mathcal{K}_1)\!\left[\mathcal{K}_1^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)-n-1}[\rho]\right] \right)}_{1}\\
&\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)-1}\norm{\mathcal{K}_2-\mathcal{K}_1}_{1\to 1}=\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)\,\norm{\mathcal{K}_2-\mathcal{K}_1}_{1\to 1}.\end{split}

(60)

Collecting these results, the overall bound is given below, where the final inequality uses
\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)\norm{\mathcal{K}_1 - \mathcal{K}_2}_{1\to 1}\leq\epsilon/2
,

\norm{\mathcal{K}_2^{\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)}[\rho] - \rho_2}_{1}\leq\epsilon+2\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)\,\norm{\mathcal{K}_2-\mathcal{K}_1}_{1\to 1}\leq 2\epsilon

(61)

This is the definition of
\tau_{2,\mathrm{mix}}(2\epsilon)
. Thus it is proven that
\tau_{2,\mathrm{mix}}(2\epsilon)\leq\tau_{1,\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon/2)
.

Appendix B
Derivation of the fixed-point error bound

In this section, we provide a detailed explanation of how a trace distance bound between the fixed point of
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
,
\rho_{\rm{fix}}
, and the Gibbs state
\rho_{\beta}
can be derived.
While the overall strategy to bound the fixed-point error is straightforward, some of the calculations are a bit more involved and deferred to Appendix
C
.

B.1
Approximation of the channel

As a first step, the channel
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
is approximated by another channel that is easier to analyze (Sec.
IV.1
).
Denote the propagator of the system dynamics as
U_{0}(t)=e^{-iHt}
.
Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,p(x)U_{0}(T+x)\,\mathrm{e}^{J^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}}\rho\,U_{0}^{\dagger}(T+x).

(62)

The Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]
agrees up to a coherent term with the detailed balance Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DB}}[\rho]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]=-i[\Delta G,\rho]+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DB}}[\rho],

(63)

with

\displaystyle\Delta G=G_{\mathrm{LS}}-G_{\rm{DB}}.

(64)

The Lamb shift term
G_{\mathrm{LS}}
is given by

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{LS}}=-\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\,\text{sign}(t_{1}-t_{2})f^{*}(t_{2})f(t_{1})A^{\dagger}_{a}(t_{2})A_{a}(t_{1}).

(65)

Though
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]
is a good approximation of the exact channel
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
, they differ at order
J^{2}
. Using a Dyson expansion in the interaction picture, it can be shown that [cf. App.
C.1
]

\displaystyle\norm{\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}}_{1\rightarrow 1}=\mathcal{O}\left(J^{2}n_{B}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{T^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}+4T_{0}^{2}}}+J^{4}n_{B}^{2}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(66)

We denote the fixed point of
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]
by
\rho_{\mathrm{LS}}
and the mixing time of the channel
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]
by
\tau_{\rm{mix}}\equiv\frac{t_{\mathrm{mix},J}}{J^{2}}
.
It follows then from Lemma
1
:

\displaystyle\norm{\rho_\mathrm{fix}-\rho_\mathrm{LS}}_{1}\leq\epsilon+\frac{t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)}{J^{2}}\norm{\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}}_{1\rightarrow 1}\leq\epsilon+t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)\mathcal{O}\left(n_{B}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{T^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}+4T_{0}^{2}}}+J^{2}n_{B}^{2}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(67)

B.2
Approximate fixed point of
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}

To bound
\norm{\rho_{\beta}-\rho_\mathrm{LS}}_{1}
, we construct an approximate fixed point of
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}
,

\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde{\rho}]-\tilde{\rho}=\mathcal{O}(J^{4})
and split the bound using the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\norm{\rho_{\beta}-\rho_\mathrm{LS}}_{1}\leq\norm{\rho_{\beta}-\tilde{\rho}}_{1}+\norm{\tilde{\rho}-\rho_\mathrm{LS}}_{1}\leq\epsilon+\norm{\rho_{\beta}-\tilde{\rho}}_{1}+\frac{t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)}{J^{2}}\norm{\tilde{\rho}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde{\rho}]}_{1},

(68)

where the last inequality makes use of Lemma
1
.

An ansatz for the approximate fixed point
\tilde{\rho}
is obtained using degenerate perturbation theory, as outlined in Ref.
27
and Sec.
IV.2
.
While degenerate perturbation theory is not controlled, the obtained expression at first order in
J^{2}
is already sufficient for our purposes.

To obtain an approximate fixed point, we make an ansatz
\tilde{\rho}=\rho^{(0)}+J^{2}\sigma
, expand the channel
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]
to first order in
J^{2}
and match the resulting expression at each order in
J^{2}
.

All expressions are obtained in an eigenbasis of the Hamiltonian
H
:
H\ket{\phi_{a}}=E_{a}\ket{\phi_{a}}
,
A_{ab}=\braket{\phi_{a}|A|\phi_{b}}
and
\omega_{ab}=E_{a}-E_{b}
.

To simplify the expression, we make use of the relation

\displaystyle\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,p(x)\,U_{0}\left(T+x\right)\,\rho\,U_{0}^{\dagger}\left(T+x\right)\right]_{ab}=e^{-\frac{1}{4}\omega_{ab}^{2}T_{0}^{2}-i\omega_{ab}T}\rho_{ab}.

(69)

Matching terms of
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde{\rho}]=\tilde{\rho}
to order
J^{0}
gives

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{4}\omega_{ab}^{2}T_{0}^{2}-i\omega_{ab}T}\rho^{(0)}_{ab}=\rho^{(0)}_{ab},

(70)

and implies that
{\rho}^{(0)}
is diagonal in the eigenbasis of
H
for
T_{0}\neq 0
.

The degeneracy is lifted by the next order
J^{2}
:
The relation for the diagonal terms gives

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho^{(0)}])_{aa}=0,

(71)

and it follows that
\rho^{(0)}=\frac{e^{-\beta E_{a}}}{Z}=\rho_{\beta}
.
The off-diagonal terms
a\neq b
have to satisfy the relation

\displaystyle e^{-i\omega_{ab}T}\left(-i[\Delta G,\rho_{\beta}]_{ab}+e^{-\frac{1}{4}\omega_{ab}^{2}T_{0}^{2}}\sigma_{ab}\right)=\sigma_{ab},

(72)

This relation is satisfied by

\displaystyle\sigma_{ab}=\frac{-ie^{-i\omega_{ab}T}[\Delta G,\rho_{\beta}]_{ab}}{1-e^{-\frac{1}{4}\omega_{ab}^{2}T_{0}^{2}-i\omega_{ab}T}}=\frac{e^{-\beta{\varepsilon}_{ab}}}{Z}\frac{2i\,\sinh\left(\frac{\beta\omega_{ab}}{2}\right)e^{i\omega_{ab}T}}{e^{-\frac{1}{4}\omega_{ab}^{2}T_{0}^{2}}-e^{i\omega_{ab}T}}(\Delta G)_{ab},

(73)

with
\varepsilon_{ab}=\frac{E_{a}+E_{b}}{2}
.
Insertion of the ansatz
\tilde{\rho}
into
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}
shows that indeed
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde{\rho}]-\tilde{\rho}=\mathcal{O}(J^{4})
.

The bounds for
\norm{\sigma}_{1}
and
\norm{\tilde\rho-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde\rho]}_{1}
are a bit tedious, and are obtained in App.
C
.
They are given by [cf. App.
C.2
]

\displaystyle\norm{\rho_\beta-\tilde{\rho}}_{1}=J^{2}\norm{\sigma}_{1}=\mathcal{O}\left(J^{2}\frac{n_{B}\beta}{\sigma^{3}}\frac{T^{6}}{T_{0}^{4}}e^{\frac{9\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}\right),

(74)

and [cf. App.
C.3
]

\displaystyle\norm{\tilde{\rho}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde{\rho}]}_{1}=\mathcal{O}\left(J^{4}n_{B}^{2}\left(1+\frac{\beta}{\sigma^{3}}\frac{T^{6}}{T_{0}^{4}}\right)e^{\frac{5\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(75)

This gives the bound:

\displaystyle\norm{\rho_{\beta}-\rho_\mathrm{LS}}_{1}\leq\epsilon+\mathcal{O}\left(J^{2}\frac{n_{B}\beta}{\sigma^{3}}\frac{T^{6}}{T_{0}^{4}}e^{\frac{9\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}\right)+t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)\mathcal{O}\left(J^{2}n_{B}^{2}\left(1+\frac{\beta}{\sigma^{3}}\frac{T^{6}}{T_{0}^{4}}\right)e^{\frac{5\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(76)

B.3
Combining the bounds

Finally, combining Eq. (
67
) and Eq. (
76
) gives the total fixed-point error:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\norm{\rho_{\beta}-\rho_{\rm{fix}}}_{1}&\leq\norm{\rho_{\beta}-\rho_\mathrm{LS}}_{1}+\norm{\rho_\mathrm{fix}-\rho_\mathrm{LS}}_{1}\\
&\leq 2\epsilon+\mathcal{O}\left(J^{2}\frac{n_{B}\beta}{\sigma^{3}}\frac{T^{6}}{T_{0}^{4}}e^{\frac{9\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}\right)+t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)\mathcal{O}\left(n_{B}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{T^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}+4T_{0}^{2}}}+J^{2}n_{B}^{2}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right)\\
&\qquad+t_{\mathrm{mix},J}(\epsilon)\mathcal{O}\left(J^{2}n_{B}^{2}\left(1+\frac{\beta}{\sigma^{3}}\frac{T^{6}}{T_{0}^{4}}\right)e^{\frac{5\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right).\end{split}

(77)

Eq. (
77
) is the explicit expression for the fixed-point error stated in Theorem
1
.

Appendix C
Explicit calculations for the fixed-point error bound

This appendix presents the detailed derivations underlying the fixed-point error bound summarized in App.
B
. The derivation of the channel-approximation error
\norm{\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}}_{1\rightarrow 1}
[cf. Eq. (
67
)] is given in App.
C.1
. The remaining ingredients entering the bound on
\norm{\rho_\beta-\rho_\mathrm{LS}}_{1}
[Eq. (
68
)] are derived in the subsequent subsections: App.
C.2
treats the term
\norm{\rho_\beta-\tilde{\rho}}_{1}
, while App.
C.3
derives the bound on
\norm{\tilde{\rho}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde{\rho}] }_{1}
.

C.1
Bounds on the channel distance

In this subsection, the bound for the channel distance
\norm{\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}}_{1\rightarrow 1}
stated in Eq. (
66
) is derived.
To do so, observe:

\displaystyle\norm{\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}}_{1\rightarrow 1}\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,p(x)\norm{ \mathcal{K}_x[\cdot] - e^{-iH(T+x)} e^{J^2 \mathcal L_\mathrm{LS}}[\cdot] e^{iH(T+x)} }_{1\rightarrow 1}.

(78)

The channel
\mathcal{K}_{x}[\rho]
can be represented as

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{x}[\rho]=e^{-iH(T+x)}\Tr_{B}\left[\tilde{U}_{x}[\rho\otimes\rho_{B}^{0}]\tilde{U}_{x}^{\dagger}\right]e^{iH(T+x)}\equiv e^{-iH(T+x)}\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}[\rho]e^{iH(T+x)},

(79)

where
\tilde{U}_{x}
is the propagator in the interaction picture

\tilde{U}_{x}=\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{-T/2}^{T/2+x}\mathrm{d}t\,JV(t)\right).

(80)

It follows from the unitary invariance of the norm,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\norm{\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}}_{1\rightarrow 1}&\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,p(x)\norm{e^{-iH(T+x)} \left[\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_x[\cdot]-e^{J^2 \mathcal L_\mathrm{LS}}[\cdot]\right] e^{iH(T+x)}}_{1\rightarrow 1}\\
&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,p(x)\norm{\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_x -e^{J^2 \mathcal L_\mathrm{LS}}}_{1\rightarrow 1},\end{split}

(81)

and thus it suffices to analyze the channels
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}[\rho]
and
\mathrm{e}^{J^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}}[\rho]
.

Our strategy is to expand both channels in powers of
J
. For the channel
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}
, we use a Dyson series for the system-bath propagator, and for the other channel we use the expansion of the exponential function. Keeping terms up to order
J^{2}
, this yields

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}[\rho]

\displaystyle=\rho+J^{2}\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}^{(1)}[\rho]+\mathcal{O}\left(J^{4}n_{B}^{2}\norm{f}_{L^{\infty}}^{4}\right),

(82)

\displaystyle e^{J^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}}[\rho]

\displaystyle=\rho+J^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]+\mathcal{O}(J^{4}\norm{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}}_{1\rightarrow 1}^{2}).

(83)

Comparing these two terms yields a bound on their difference

\|\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}-\mathrm{e}^{J^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}}\|_{1\rightarrow 1}\leq\|\mathrm{e}^{J^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}}-\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}\|_{1\rightarrow 1}=J^{2}\|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}-\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}^{(1)}\|_{1\rightarrow 1}+\mathcal{O}\left(J^{4}n_{B}^{2}(\norm{f}^{4}_{L^{\infty}}+\norm{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}}_{1\rightarrow 1}^{2})\right).

(84)

We will now use the explicit expressions for
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}
and
\tilde{K}_{x}^{(1)}
to bound the difference in their image (the numerator of the rhs above) for arbitrary
\rho
.
To this end, we first derive the expression for
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}^{(1)}[\rho]
, using the Dyson expansion for
\tilde{U}_{x}
given by

\begin{split}\tilde{U}_{x}&=\mathbb{I}-iJ\int_{-T/2}^{T/2+x}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\,V(t_{1})-J^{2}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2+x}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{-T/2}^{t_{1}}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\,V(t_{1})V(t_{2})\\
&+iJ^{3}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2+x}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{-T/2}^{t_{1}}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\int_{-T/2}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t_{3}\,V(t_{1})V(t_{2})V(t_{3})+\mathcal{O}\left(J^{4}\left[\int_{-T/2}^{T/2+x}\mathrm{d}t\,\norm{V(t)}_{\infty}\right]^{4}\right).\end{split}

(85)

Odd orders in
J
vanish after tracing out the bath degrees of freedom.
At order
J^{2}
, there are three contributions:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\text{Tr}_{B}\left[\left(-iJ\int_{-T/2}^{T/2+x}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\,V(t_{1})\right)[\rho\otimes\rho_{B}^{0}]\left(iJ\int_{-T/2}^{T/2+x}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}_{1}\,V^{\dagger}(t^{\prime}_{1})\right)\right]=J^{2}\sum_{a}\tilde{L}_{a}\rho\tilde{L}_{a}^{\dagger},\\
&\text{Tr}_{B}\left[\left(-J^{2}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2+x}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{-T/2}^{t_{1}}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\,V(t_{1})V(t_{2})\right)[\rho\otimes\rho_{B}^{0}]\right]\\
&\qquad=-J^{2}\sum_{a}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2+x}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{-T/2}^{t_{1}}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\,f^{*}(t_{1})f(t_{2})A^{\dagger}_{a}(t_{1})A_{a}(t_{2})\rho,\\
&\text{Tr}_{B}\left[[\rho\otimes\rho_{B}^{0}]\left(-J^{2}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2+x}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}_{1}\int_{-T/2}^{t^{\prime}_{1}}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}_{2}\,V^{\dagger}(t^{\prime}_{2})V^{\dagger}(t^{\prime}_{1})\right)\right]\\
&\qquad=-J^{2}\rho\sum_{a}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2+x}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}_{1}\int_{-T/2}^{t^{\prime}_{1}}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}_{2}\,f(t^{\prime}_{1})f^{*}(t^{\prime}_{2})A^{\dagger}_{a}(t^{\prime}_{2})A_{a}(t^{\prime}_{1}).\end{split}

(86)

It can be shown that these terms sum up to
J^{2}\tilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(1)}_{x}[\rho]
, where

\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}^{(1)}[\rho]=-i[\tilde{G}_{\mathrm{LS}},\rho]+\sum_{a}\tilde{L}_{a}\rho\tilde{L}_{a}^{\dagger}-\left\{\frac{1}{2}\tilde{L}_{a}^{\dagger}\tilde{L}_{a},\rho\right\}.

(87)

Here,
\tilde{G}_{\mathrm{LS}}
and
\tilde{L}_{a}
are nearly identical to
G_{\mathrm{LS}}
[Eq. (
20
)] and
L_{a}
[Eq. (
2
)], but with their integration domains restricted to
(-T/2,\,T/2+x)
instead of
(-\infty,\infty)
. This causes
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(1)}_{x}[\rho]
to differ from
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]
by a finite-time tail error.

This completes the expansion of
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}[\rho]
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}[\rho]=\rho+J^{2}\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}^{(1)}[\rho]+\mathcal{O}\left(n_{B}^{2}\left[\int_{-T/2}^{T/2+x}\mathrm{d}t\,|f(t)|\norm{A_a(t)}_{\infty}\right]^{4}\norm{\rho}_{1}\right)=\rho+J^{2}\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}^{(1)}[\rho]+\mathcal{O}\left(J^{4}n_{B}^{2}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(88)

We continue with bounding
\|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]-\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}^{(1)}[\rho]\|_{1}
, which represents the tail error. We obtain from Eq. (
87
),

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]-\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}^{(1)}[\rho]&=\sum_{a}\iint\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus(-T/2,\,T/2+x)}dt_{1}\,dt_{2}\,f^{*}(t_{1})f(t_{2})\Bigg[-\frac{1}{2}\text{sign}(t_{1}-t_{2})\left[A_{a}^{\dagger}(t_{1})A_{a}(t_{2}),\rho\right]\\
&\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\{A^{\dagger}_{a}(t_{1})A_{a}(t_{2}),\rho\}+A_{a}(t_{2})\rho A^{\dagger}_{a}(t_{1})\Bigg].\end{split}

(89)

Using
\|A_{a}(t)\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, Holder’s inequality gives
\left\|\left[A_{a}(t_{1})A_{a}(t_{2}),\rho\right]\right\|_{1}\leq 2\|\rho\|_{1}
,
\left\|\{A_{a}(t_{1})A_{a}(t_{2}),\rho\}\right\|_{1}\leq 2\|\rho\|_{1}
,
\left\|A_{a}(t_{2})\rho A^{\dagger}_{a}(t_{1})\right\|_{1}\leq\|\rho\|_{1}
. This leads to

\begin{split}\|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]-\tilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(1)}[\rho]\|_{1}&\leq 3n_{B}\|\rho\|_{1}\iint\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus(-T/2,\,T/2+x)}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\,\mathrm{d}t_{2}\,|f(t_{1})||f(t_{2})|\\
&\leq 6n_{B}\|\rho\|_{1}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,|f(t)|\right]\left[\int_{|t|>T/2-|x|}\mathrm{d}t\,|f(t)|\right].\end{split}

(90)

These integrals can be evaluated for the form of
f(t)
chosen in Eq. (
11
)
and thus give

\|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]-\tilde{\mathcal{K}}^{(1)}[\rho]\|_{1}<6n_{B}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}\text{erfc}\left(\frac{T-2|x|}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)\norm{\rho}_{1}.

(91)

To obtain the explicit expression for
\mathcal{O}\left(J^{4}\|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}\|^{2}_{1\rightarrow 1}\right)
, we use

\|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]\|_{1}<3n_{B}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}\|\rho\|_{1},

(92)

and thus get

\mathcal{O}\left(J^{4}\|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}\|^{2}_{1\rightarrow 1}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(J^{4}n_{B}^{2}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(93)

Combining all terms, this gives

\|\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{x}-\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\infty}\|_{1\rightarrow 1}=\mathcal{O}\left(J^{2}n_{B}\,\text{erfc}\left(\frac{T-2|x|}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}+J^{4}n_{B}^{2}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(94)

The final integration over
p(x)
in Eq. (
81
) leads to the final bound

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}\|_{1\rightarrow 1}=\mathcal{O}\left(J^{2}n_{B}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{T^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}+4T_{0}^{2}}}+J^{4}n_{B}^{2}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(95)

C.2
Bounds on the approximate fixed point

In this subsection, the bound for the approximate fixed point
\norm{\rho_\beta-\tilde{\rho}}_{1}=J^{2}\norm{\sigma}_{1}
stated in Eq. (
74
) is derived, where
\tilde{\rho}
is defined by
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde{\rho}]-\tilde{\rho}=\mathcal{O}(J^{4})
.

From the definition in Eq. (
73
), it follows that
\sigma
can be expressed as

\sigma=\rho_{\beta}^{1/2}\Omega\rho_{\beta}^{1/2},

(96)

where
\Omega
is a matrix whose components in the energy eigenbasis are given by

\Omega_{ab}=F(\omega_{ab})(\Delta G)_{ab},

(97)

with

F(\omega_{ab})=\frac{2i\,\sinh\left(\frac{\beta\omega_{ab}}{2}\right)e^{i\omega_{ab}T}}{e^{-\frac{1}{4}\omega_{ab}^{2}T_{0}^{2}}-e^{i\omega_{ab}T}}.

(98)

Using Holder’s inequality, and
\|\rho_{\beta}^{1/2}\|_{2}=1
, this gives

\|\sigma\|_{1}\leq\|\rho_{\beta}^{1/2}\|_{2}\|\Omega\|_{\infty}\|\rho_{\beta}^{1/2}\|_{2}=\|\Omega\|_{\infty}.

(99)

It remains to bound
\|\Omega\|_{\infty}
.

To do this, split
\Omega=\Omega_{\mathrm{LS}}-\Omega_{\mathrm{DB}}
, and consider each of their Bohr frequency representations,

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{DB}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\sum_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathfrak{B}}\frac{1}{2i}F(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})\tanh\left(\frac{\beta(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})}{4}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\,f(t_{1})f^{*}(t_{2})e^{i\nu_{1}t_{1}-i\nu_{2}t_{2}}(A_{\nu_{2}}^{a})^{\dagger}A_{\nu_{1}}^{a},

(100a)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{LS}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\sum_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathfrak{B}}-\frac{1}{2i}F(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{2}\,\text{sign}(t_{1}-t_{2})f(t_{1})f^{*}(t_{2})e^{i\nu_{1}t_{1}-i\nu_{2}t_{2}}(A_{\nu_{2}}^{a})^{\dagger}A_{\nu_{1}}^{a}.

(100b)

C.2.1
Bounding
\Omega_{\mathrm{DB}}

To bound
\|\Omega_{\mathrm{DB}}\|_{\infty}
[Eq. (
100a
)],
make a change of variables
t_{+}=\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{2}
,
t_{-}=\frac{t_{1}-t_{2}}{2}
,
\nu_{+}=\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}
,
\nu_{-}=\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}
and substitute
f(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}\left(t-\frac{i\beta}{4}\right)^{2}}
.
This gives a separable product
\hat{h}_{+}(\nu_{+})\,\hat{h}_{-}(\nu_{-})
,

\Omega_{\mathrm{DB}}=\sum_{a}\sum_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathfrak{B}}-\frac{2i}{\pi\sigma^{2}}\underbrace{F(\nu_{-})\tanh\left(\frac{\beta\nu_{-}}{4}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{-}\,e^{-i\nu_{-}t_{-}}e^{-\frac{4}{\sigma^{2}}t_{-}^{2}}}_{\hat{h}_{-}(\nu_{-})}\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{+}\,e^{-i\nu_{+}t_{+}}e^{-\frac{4}{\sigma^{2}}\left(t_{+}+\frac{i\beta}{4}\right)^{2}}}_{\hat{h}_{+}(\nu_{+})}(A_{\nu_{2}}^{a})^{\dagger}A_{\nu_{1}}^{a}.

(101)

Corollary A.1 of Ref.
7
states that

\sum_{a}\sum_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathfrak{B}}\hat{h}_{+}(\nu_{+})\hat{h}_{-}(\nu_{-})(A_{\nu_{2}}^{a})^{\dagger}A_{\nu_{1}}^{a}=\sum_{a}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{-}\,h_{-}(t_{-})e^{-iHt_{-}}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{+}\,h_{+}(t_{+})A_{a}^{\dagger}(t_{+})A_{a}^{\dagger}(-t_{+})\right)e^{iHt_{-}},

(102)

where the Fourier transform is defined as
\hat{h}(\nu)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,h(t)e^{-i\nu t}\Leftrightarrow h(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\,h(\nu)e^{i\nu t}
.

Using
\|e^{iHt}\|_{\infty}=1
, and
\|A_{a}(t)\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, a bound for
\|\Omega_{\mathrm{DB}}\|_{\infty}
is therefore given by

\begin{split}\|\Omega_{\mathrm{DB}}\|_{\infty}&=\frac{2}{\pi\sigma^{2}}\left\|\sum_{a}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{-}\,h_{-}(t_{-})e^{-iHt_{-}}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{+}\,h_{+}(t_{+})A_{a}^{\dagger}(t_{+})A_{a}^{\dagger}(-t_{+})\right)e^{iHt_{-}}\right\|_{\infty}\\
&\leq\frac{2n_{B}}{\pi\sigma^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{-}\,|h_{-}(t_{-})|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{+}\,|h_{+}(t_{+})|.\end{split}

(103)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{+}\,|h_{+}(t_{+})|
can be found explicitly through an inverse Fourier transform of
\hat{h}_{+}(\nu_{+})
. This leads to

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{+}\,|h_{+}(t_{+})|=\frac{\sigma}{4\sqrt{\pi}}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}.

(104)

It remains to estimate

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{-}\,|h_{-}(t_{-})|
. To do so, consider the explicit expression for
\hat{h}_{-}(\nu)
,

\hat{h}_{-}(\nu)=2i\sqrt{\pi}\sigma\frac{e^{i\nu T}}{e^{-\frac{1}{4}\nu^{2}T_{0}^{2}}-e^{i\nu T}}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{\beta\nu}{4}\right)e^{-\frac{\nu^{2}\sigma^{2}}{16}}.

(105)

Our strategy is to express
\hat{h}_{-}(\nu)
as a product of two appropriately chosen functions
\hat{h}_{-}(\nu)=2i\sqrt{\pi}\sigma\ \hat{p}(\nu)\hat{q}(\nu)
, so that by the convolution theorem,
h_{-}(t)=2i\sqrt{\pi}\sigma\ (p*q)(t)
.
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,|h_{-}(t)|
can then be bounded using Young’s convolution inequality,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,|h_{-}(t)|\equiv\|h_{-}(t)\|_{L^{1}}=2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma\|(p*q)(t)\|_{L^{1}}\leq 2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma\|p(t)\|_{L^{1}}\|q(t)\|_{L^{1}}.

(106)

Here,
p(t),q(t)
denote the inverse Fourier transforms of
\hat{p}(\nu),\,\hat{q}(\nu)
.
Denoting
\alpha=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{32}
, we pick

\hat{p}(\nu)=\frac{1}{\nu}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{\beta\nu}{4}\right)e^{i\nu T-\alpha\nu^{2}},\qquad\hat{q}(\nu)=\frac{\nu e^{-\alpha\nu^{2}}}{e^{-\frac{1}{4}\nu^{2}T_{0}^{2}}-e^{i\nu T}}.

(107)

To determine
\|p(t)\|_{L^{1}}
, the inverse Fourier transform
p(t)
is expressed by substituting
\frac{1}{\nu}\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{\beta\nu}{4}\right)=\int_{0}^{\beta/4}\mathrm{d}x\,\sinh(2\nu x)
and reversing the order of integration.

p(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\beta/4}\mathrm{d}x\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\,\sinh(2\nu x)e^{i\nu(T+t)-\alpha\nu^{2}}=\frac{i}{2\sqrt{\pi\alpha}}e^{-\frac{(T+t)^{2}}{4\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\beta/4}\mathrm{d}x\,e^{\frac{x^{2}}{\alpha}}\sin\left(\frac{T+t}{\alpha}x\right).

(108)

|p(t)|
is now bounded using
\left|\sin\left(\frac{T+t}{\alpha}\right)\right|<1
to obtain
|p(t)|\leq\frac{\beta}{8\sqrt{\pi\alpha}}e^{-\frac{(T+t)^{2}}{4\alpha}}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{16\alpha}}
. It follows that

\norm{p(t)}_{L^{1}}\leq\frac{\beta}{4}e^{\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}.

(109)

To find
\|q(t)\|_{L^{1}}
, we split the
t
-integral into short times
|t|<t_{0}
and long times
|t|>t_{0}
, with
t_{0}
to be determined later:

\|q(t)\|_{L^{1}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-t_{0}}^{t_{0}}\mathrm{d}t\,\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\,e^{i\nu t}\hat{q}(\nu)\right|+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|t|>t_{0}}\mathrm{d}t\,\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\,e^{i\nu t}\hat{q}(\nu)\right|.

(110)

Using partial integration, we obtain

\|q(t)\|_{L^{1}}=\frac{2t_{0}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\,|\hat{q}(\nu)|+\frac{2}{\pi t_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\,\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{q}(\nu)}{\mathrm{d}\nu^{2}}\right|.

(111)

To obtain a bound for
|\hat{q}(\nu)|
, note that
\frac{1}{\left|e^{-\frac{1}{4}\nu^{2}T_{0}^{2}}-e^{i\nu T}\right|}=\Theta(\nu^{-1})
. For a bound of
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{q}(\nu)}{\mathrm{d}\nu^{2}}\right|
, the numerator can be bounded by a term of order
\mathcal{O}(\nu^{3}e^{-\alpha\nu^{2}})
and thus cancels divergences in the denominator.
A tedious but straightforward calculation then yields, with the assumption
T_{0}<T
and
\sigma<T
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\,|\hat{q}(\nu)|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{T^{2}}{\sigma^{2}T_{0}^{2}}\right),\qquad\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\,\left|\frac{d^{2}\hat{q}(\nu)}{d\nu^{2}}\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{T^{10}}{\sigma^{4}T_{0}^{6}}\right).

(112)

Picking
t_{0}=\frac{T^{4}}{T_{0}^{2}\sigma}
, this gives

\begin{split}\|q(t)\|_{L^{1}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{T^{6}}{\sigma^{3}T_{0}^{4}}\right),\end{split}

(113)

and together with Young’s convolution theorem Eq. (
106
) and Eq. (
104
),
\|\Omega_{\mathrm{DB}}\|_{\infty}
is thus bounded by [cf. Eq. (
103
)]

\|\Omega_{\mathrm{DB}}\|_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n_{B}\beta}{\sigma^{3}}\frac{T^{6}}{T_{0}^{4}}e^{\frac{9\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(114)

C.2.2
Bounding
\Omega_{\mathrm{LS}}

The procedure to bound
\|\Omega_{\mathrm{LS}}\|_{\infty}
[Eq. (
100b
)] is similar to before. The same change of variables is first used to obtain a separable expression
\hat{g}_{+}(\nu_{+})\,\hat{g}_{-}(\nu_{-})
,

\Omega_{\mathrm{LS}}=\sum_{a}\sum_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathfrak{B}}\frac{2i}{\pi\sigma^{2}}\underbrace{F(\nu_{-})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{-}\,e^{-i\nu_{-}t_{-}}e^{-\frac{4}{\sigma^{2}}t_{-}^{2}}}_{\hat{g}_{-}(\nu_{-})}\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{+}\,\text{sign}(t_{+})\,e^{-i\nu_{+}t_{+}}e^{-\frac{4}{\sigma^{2}}\left(t_{+}+\frac{i\beta}{4}\right)^{2}}}_{\hat{g}_{+}(\nu_{+})}(A_{\nu_{2}}^{a})^{\dagger}A_{\nu_{1}}^{a}.

(115)

Corollary A.1 of Ref.
7
is again used,

\|\Omega_{\mathrm{LS}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2n_{B}}{\pi\sigma^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{-}\,|g_{-}(t_{-})|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{+}\,|g_{+}(t_{+})|.

(116)

For
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{+}\,|g_{+}(t_{+})|
, it can be integrated explicitly,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{+}\,|g_{+}(t_{+})|=\frac{\sigma}{4\sqrt{\pi}}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}.

(117)

For
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t_{-}\,|g_{-}(t_{-})|
, we first integrate to find
g_{-}(t_{-})
,

\hat{g}_{-}(\nu)=i\sqrt{\pi}\sigma\frac{e^{i\nu T}}{e^{-\frac{1}{4}\nu^{2}T_{0}^{2}}-e^{i\nu T}}\sinh\left(\frac{\beta\nu}{2}\right)e^{-\frac{\nu^{2}\sigma^{2}}{16}}.

(118)

Picking
\hat{g}_{-}(\nu)=i\sqrt{\pi}\sigma\hat{r}(\nu)\hat{q}(\nu)
,

\begin{split}\hat{r}(\nu)&=\frac{1}{\nu}\sinh\left(\frac{\beta\nu}{2}\right)e^{i\nu T-\alpha\nu^{2}},\\
\hat{q}(\nu)&=\frac{\nu e^{-\alpha\nu^{2}}}{e^{-\frac{1}{4}\nu^{2}T_{0}^{2}}-e^{i\nu T}}.\end{split}

(119)

\|g_{-}(t)\|_{L^{1}}
is bounded by the convolution theorem and convolution inequality.

\|g_{-}(t)\|_{L^{1}}\leq\sqrt{\pi}\sigma\|r(t)\|_{L^{1}}\|q(t)\|_{L^{1}}.

(120)

\hat{q}(\nu)
is the same as before, so
\|q(t)\|_{L^{1}}
is bounded by Eq. (
113
).
\hat{r}(\nu)
can be rewritten using
\frac{1}{\nu}\sinh\left(\frac{\beta\nu}{2}\right)=\int_{0}^{\beta/2}\mathrm{d}x\,\cosh(\nu x)
, so
r(t)
can be evaluated by swapping the integrals,

r(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\beta/2}\mathrm{d}x\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\,\cosh(\nu x)e^{i\nu(T+t)-\alpha\nu^{2}}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi\alpha}}e^{-\frac{(T+t)^{2}}{4\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\beta/2}\mathrm{d}x\,e^{\frac{x^{2}}{4\alpha}}\cos\left(\frac{T+t}{2\alpha}x\right).

(121)

Using
\left|\cos\left(\frac{T+t}{2\alpha}x\right)\right|<1
,
|r(t)|<\frac{\beta}{4\sqrt{\pi\alpha}}e^{-\frac{(T+t)^{2}}{4\alpha}}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{16\alpha}}
. The bound for
\|r(t)\|_{L^{1}}
is then given by

\|r(t)\|_{L^{1}}\leq\frac{\beta}{4\sqrt{\pi\alpha}}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{16\alpha}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,e^{-\frac{(T+t)^{2}}{4\alpha}}\leq\frac{\beta}{2}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{16\alpha}}=\frac{\beta}{2}e^{\frac{2\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}.

(122)

Putting these results together, the bound for
\|\Omega_{\mathrm{LS}}\|_{\infty}
is of the same order as
\|\Omega_{\mathrm{DB}}\|_{\infty}
.

\|\Omega_{\mathrm{LS}}\|_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n_{B}\beta}{\sigma^{3}}\frac{T^{6}}{T_{0}^{4}}e^{\frac{9\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(123)

This completes the bound for
\|\sigma\|_{1}
,

\|\sigma\|_{1}=\|\Omega\|_{\infty}\leq\|\Omega_{\mathrm{LS}}\|_{\infty}+\|\Omega_{\mathrm{DB}}\|_{\infty}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n_{B}\beta}{\sigma^{3}}\frac{T^{6}}{T_{0}^{4}}e^{\frac{9\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(124)

C.3
Bounds on
\norm{\tilde\rho-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde\rho]}_{1}

In this subsection, we derive a bound for
\norm{\tilde\rho-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde\rho]}_{1}
, which is the last ingredient needed to obtain the fixed-point error bound in App.
B
.

Using the expansion
\tilde{\rho}=\rho_{\beta}+J^{2}\sigma
and
e^{J^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}}=\rho+J^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]+\mathcal{O}\left(J^{4}n_{B}^{2}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right)
, this gives at order
J^{4}
:

\displaystyle\norm{\tilde\rho-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde\rho]}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq J^{4}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,p(x)\norm{e^{-iH(T+x)} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\sigma] e^{iH(T+x)} }_{1}+\mathcal{O}\left(J^{4}n_{B}^{2}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(125)

Due to the unitary invariance of the norm, the expression can be simplified:

\displaystyle\norm{\tilde\rho-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde\rho]}_{1}\leq J^{4}\norm{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\sigma]}_{1}+\mathcal{O}\left(J^{4}n_{B}^{2}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(126)

The first term is determined by collecting results for
\norm{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\rho]}_{1}
[Eq. (
92
)] and
\norm{\sigma}_{1}
[Eq. (
124
)].

\norm{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\sigma]}_{1}\leq 3n_{B}e^{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}}\norm{\sigma}_{1}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n_{B}^{2}\beta}{\sigma^{3}}\frac{T^{6}}{T_{0}^{4}}e^{\frac{5\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(127)

Thus the complete bound is given by

\norm{\tilde\rho-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{LS}}[\tilde\rho]}_{1}=\mathcal{O}\left(J^{4}n_{B}^{2}\left(1+\frac{\beta}{\sigma^{3}}\frac{T^{6}}{T_{0}^{4}}\right)e^{\frac{5\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\right).

(128)

Appendix D
Bounds on the variance of random channels

In this section, we provide the proof for the variance with respect to the observable
O
after
m
steps.

Consider a random sequence
M=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
, which corresponds to application of the random channel

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{M}[\rho]=\mathcal{K}_{x_{m}}\circ\dots\mathcal{K}_{x_{1}}[\rho].

(129)

For an arbitrary initial state
\rho
, denote the expectation value of
O
with respect to this random sequence as
\braket{O}_{M}=\Tr[O\mathcal{E}_{M}[\rho]]
.
Furthermore, the average over all random sequences is denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{M}
.

The variance of the observable
O
can then be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(O)=\mathbb{E}_{M}\braket{O^{2}}_{M}-\left(\mathbb{E}_{M}\braket{O}_{M}\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{M}\left[\braket{O^{2}}_{M}-\braket{O}_{M}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{M}\left[\braket{O}_{M}^{2}-\left(\mathbb{E}_{M}\braket{O}_{M}\right)^{2}\right].

(130)

In the second equation, the variance is split into two terms.
The first term is the standard fluctuation term, and is bounded by

\left|\mathbb{E}_{M}\left[\braket{O^{2}}_{M}-\braket{O}_{M}^{2}\right]\right|\leq\norm{O}_{\infty}^{2}
.
The second term denotes the additional variance due to the random evolution time of the channel.

To bound the second term, note first that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{M}\left[\braket{O}_{M}^{2}-\left(\mathbb{E}_{M}\braket{O}_{M}\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{M}\left(\braket{O}_{M}-\mathbb{E}_{M}\braket{O}_{M}\right)^{2},

(131)

and
\braket{O}_{M}-\mathbb{E}_{M}\braket{O}_{M}
can be expressed as a telescopic sum:

\displaystyle\left[\braket{O}_{M}-\mathbb{E}_{M}\braket{O}_{M}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}D_{j},

(132)

with

\displaystyle D_{j}=\Tr[\rho\mathcal{K}^{\dagger}_{x_{1}}\circ\dots\mathcal{K}^{\dagger}_{x_{j-1}}(\mathcal{K}^{\dagger}_{x_{j}}-\mathcal{K}^{\dagger})\circ\mathcal{K}^{\dagger(m-j)}[O]].

(133)

Observe that for
i>j
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{M}[D_{i}D_{j}]=0.

(134)

To see this, observe that the average over the
i
-th random number
x_{i}

contains in this case a single factor
(\mathcal{K}^{\dagger}_{x_{i}}-\mathcal{K}^{\dagger})
, which averages to zero.

Furthermore, due to the contractivity of channels, it holds

\displaystyle|D_{j}|\leq\norm{(\mathcal{K}^\dagger_{x_j}-\mathcal{K}^\dagger)\circ\mathcal{K}^{\dagger{(m-j)}}[O]}_{\infty}.

(135)

Note also that
[\mathcal{K}^{\dagger}_{x_{j}}-\mathcal{K}^{\dagger}]\mathbb{I}=0
due to trace preservation. We can thus add a term
-\Tr[\rho_{\rm{fix}}O]\mathbb{I}
to the expression:
This gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{M}\left[\braket{O}_{M}^{2}-\left(\mathbb{E}_{M}\braket{O}_{M}\right)^{2}\right]&\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{M}|D_{j}|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{M}\norm{\mathcal{K}^\dagger_{x_j}-\mathcal{K}^\dagger}^{2}_{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\norm{\mathcal{K}^{\dagger(m-j)}[O-\Tr[\rho_{\rm{fix}}O]\mathbb{I}]}_{\infty}^{2}\\
&\leq C^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{M}\norm{\mathcal{K}^\dagger_{x_j}-\mathcal{K}^\dagger}^{2}_{\infty}\norm{O}^{2}_{\infty}\frac{1-e^{-\frac{2m}{\tau_{\rm{mix}}}}}{1-e^{-\frac{2}{\tau_{\rm{mix}}}}}.\end{split}

(136)

Here we used
\norm{\mathcal{K}^{\dagger(n)}[O-\Tr[\rho_{\rm{fix}}O]\mathbb{I}]}_{\infty}<Ce^{-\frac{n}{\tau_{\rm{mix}}}}\norm{O}_{\infty}
. The prefactor
C
depends on the specific structure of the channel, such as the size of possible Jordan blocks
40
.
An upper bound is given by a factor of the mixing time itself.
However, in many cases, for example, if the channel is contractive as at high temperatures
3
,
C=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

It remains to bound
\mathbb{E}_{M}\norm{\mathcal{K}^\dagger_{x_j}-\mathcal{K}^\dagger}^{2}_{\infty}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{M}\norm{\mathcal{K}^\dagger_{x_j}-\mathcal{K}^\dagger}_{\infty}\leq\int\mathrm{d}x\,p(x)\norm{U(x_j+T)-U(x+T)}_{\infty}\leq T_{0}\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\norm{H}_{\infty}.

(137)

Combining all bounds, this gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(O)\leq\norm{O}^{2}_{\infty}+C^{2}T_{0}^{2}\frac{4}{\pi}\norm{H}^{2}_{\infty}\norm{O}^{2}_{\infty}\frac{1-e^{\frac{2m}{-\tau_{\rm{mix}}}}}{1-e^{-\frac{2}{\tau_{\rm{mix}}}}},

(138)

which is the result in Theorem
2
.

Appendix E
Numerical results on other randomization schemes

Here, we present numerical results on the variance of the randomization scheme introduced in Refs.
15
;
8
. These works propose a randomization of the bath Hamiltonian, and a randomization of jump operators in order to derive a tractable bound for the mixing time of the channel in terms of the exact Gibbs sampler.

For a controlled comparison with our protocol [Fig.
5
], we only test the randomization of the bath Hamiltonian. We use the same protocol parameters
\beta=1,T=10,T_{0}=1,\sigma=1
and the same mixed-field Ising model for
H
[Eq. (
50
)] with
n_{S}=2,g=0.9045,h=0.809
. A randomized evolution time is also used here to suppress resonances, using the same probability distribution
p(x)
from before [Eq. (
13
)].

The bath Hamiltonian is determined by a random parameter
\omega
at every interaction cycle, and is given by

H_{B,\omega}=-\frac{\omega}{2}Z,

(139)

where
\omega
is drawn from the uniform distribution
g(\omega)
on the interval
[0,\omega_{\mathrm{max}}]
, with
\omega_{\mathrm{max}}
set just slightly larger than the greatest spectral gap of the system Hamiltonian
H
.

g(\omega)=\frac{1}{\omega_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\omega\in[0,\omega_{\mathrm{max}}]}.

(140)

The remainder of the Hamiltonian is similar to before,

H_{SB,\omega}(t)=H\otimes\mathbb{I}_{B}+\mathbb{I}_{S}\otimes H_{B,\omega}+Jf(t)\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(A_{a}\otimes B_{a}^{\dagger}+A_{a}^{\dagger}\otimes B_{a}\right),

(141)

with the filter function
f(t)
now chosen to be real,

f(t)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{2t^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}.

(142)

The same jump operator
A_{a}=X_{a}+Z_{a}
is used.

At the start of every interaction cycle, the bath qubits are now initialized to the bath thermal state
\rho_{B}^{0}\propto e^{-\beta H_{B,\omega}}
. Each step of the protocol then implements a channel of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{x,\omega}[\rho]=\Tr_{B}\left[U_{\omega}\left(T+x\right)(\rho\otimes\rho_{B}^{0})U_{\omega}^{\dagger}\left(T+x\right)\right],

(143)

where the propagator
U_{\omega}\left(t+\frac{T}{2}\right)=\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{-\frac{T}{2}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,H_{SB}(s)}
.

The channel
\mathcal{K}[\rho]
is then given by the average of
\mathcal{K}_{x,\omega}[\rho]
over randomized evolution time shifts
x
and also randomized bath Hamiltonians
\omega
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[\rho]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,\int_{0}^{\omega_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}\omega\,p(x)g(\omega)\mathcal{K}_{x,\omega}[\rho],

(144)

whereas in practice, the following random channel is implemented,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{M}[\rho]=\mathcal{K}_{x_{M},\omega_{M}}\circ\mathcal{K}_{x_{M-1},\omega_{M-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{K}_{x_{1},\omega_{1}}[\rho].

(145)
