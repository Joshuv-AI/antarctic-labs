ABSTRACT
The towns of Edgartown, Oak Bluffs and Tisbury, on the island of Martha’s Vineyard, MA, are
concerned about the effect of prolonged grid outages on continuity of their water supply. Water
pumping stations operated by the water utilities that currently serve each of the towns currently have
backup power generation that can support water delivery for up to one week during peak water
demand periods in the summer and early fall. With support from the US DOE Energy Technology
Innovation Partnership Projects, staff at Sandia National Laboratories advised the Martha’s Vineyard
Commission on identifying low-barrier options to extend water delivery in the event of a prolonged
grid outage to two weeks. The team determined that interconnecting the town’s water distribution
systems could be effective only during low demand periods. The option of using the island’s public
electric transit bus infrastructure, operated by the Vineyard Transit Authority, to deliver power to
the pumping stations is possible from the point of view of energy capacity and local generation, but
it is not practically viable at present because the necessary bi-directional electric charging
infrastructure is not available commercially. Mobile electricity storage is a viable off-the-shelf
alternative, that could use local photovoltaic generation as a source of energy, but a business model
that makes this option cost-effective would need to be found. Doubling the capacity of the propane
or diesel storage at the pumping stations is a viable option that could be implemented at low cost.
Water conservation measures can also be effective in extending the duration of water supply.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Background: Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury, located on the island of Martha’s Vineyard, MA,
experience significant seasonal fluctuations in power and water demands due to tourism and
summer population surges. Although the island has not recently faced extended outages, past events
like Hurricane Bob in 1991 and the October 2021 nor’easter highlighted the need for improved
reliability of water and power services. Because Martha’s Vineyard is only accessible by water or air,
deliveries of fuel and other essentials may also be disrupted during severe weather events. Currently,
the towns rely on diesel and propane generators for backup power, raising concerns about potential
fuel shortages during prolonged outages combined with difficulties in fuel deliveries. The Martha’s
Vineyard Energy Commission applied to the U.S. Department of Energy’s Energy Technology
Innovation Partnership Program (ETIPP) to explore alternative solutions.
Problem Statement: Stakeholders wished to ensure a continuous water supply for the towns during
extended power outages. Key questions addressed by ETIPP included:
 • How can the towns provide 14 days of potable water during outages if fuel resupply to the
 island is interrupted?
 • Can the Vineyard Transit Authority (VTA) electric bus fleet and microgrid be leveraged to
 provide temporary mobile power for groundwater pumps during extended power outages?
 • What low-cost opportunities exist to optimize water system operations and promote
 conservation during emergencies?
Stakeholders wished to prioritize affordable solutions that comply with aquifer and wellhead
protection regulations while reducing reliance on potentially interrupted fuel imports.
Approach: Through the ETIPP program, technical staff at Sandia National Laboratories (Sandia)
collaborated with the water utilities, energy committees, fire departments, the Martha’s Vineyard
Commission, and the Massachusetts Department of Environmental Protection to establish criteria
for acceptable solutions. Sandia experts modeled the water distribution systems of Edgartown, Oak
Bluffs, and Tisbury, enhancing existing geographic information system (GIS) datasets and hydraulic
models to assess: 1) risks of material damage to water systems during extended power outages at
pumps and lift stations, and 2) opportunities for low-cost service extensions through operational
modifications, such as interconnecting or shifting pumping between well sites. Sandia also evaluated
the electrical load that the Vineyard Transit Authority (VTA) electric bus fleet could support,
exploring the feasibility of using bus power from the VTA fleet and supporting microgrid to supply
power to the water systems during outages. Sandia surveyed vendors to identify solutions for bus-to-
well interconnections and contacted promising technology providers for technical information to
support further funding applications. Additionally, the ETIPP Regional Partner, Island Institute,
collaborated with the Martha’s Vineyard Commission to create communication materials focused on
emergency water conservation and identified potential funding opportunities to help Edgartown,
Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury water utilities advance implementation efforts.
Findings: Based on the results of Sandia water system modeling and energy demand analysis, the
Sandia team, with the assistance of project stakeholders, determined that:
 • Existing Backup Generator Capacity: Backup generators and associated fuel reserves can
 sustain uninterrupted water supply for at least seven days during summer without

 10

 modifications to consumer behavior, and they can handle multi-week outages in winter
 without changes to the backup system.
 • Time to Failure without Power: In scenarios without interconnects and backup power, the
 time to failure varies significantly between winter and summer due to differences in water
 demand. In winter, the lower demand allows for longer operational durations before the
 system must shut down. For example, Edgartown can sustain water availability for
 approximately 4.66 days under minimum demand in winter. In contrast, during summer,
 particularly at peak demand levels (110% of maximum demand), none of the systems
 (Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, or Tisbury) can sustain delivery for more than a day without power.
 • Interconnects and System Resilience: Opening interconnects between towns decreases
 the time all systems can operate without power, as it allows for faster drainage of tank
 capacity despite improving pressure for firefighting, highlighting that interconnects do not
 enhance resilience in the absence of backup power. The Edgartown water system is most
 sensitive to the opening of the interconnect, while Oak Bluffs and Tisbury are less impacted,
 with time to failure showing little change.
Based on the technoeconomic evaluation conducted by the Sandia team, the Edgartown, Oak Bluffs,
and Tisbury water utilities may have several feasible options for extending the duration of
uninterrupted water supply to approach the stakeholders’ self-identified goal of supplying water for
14 consecutive summer days without grid power.
 • Water demand curtailment: Aside from increasing backup power fuel or availability,
 demand curtailment is the most effective way to extend water availability during a prolonged
 grid outage. Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury utilities report that nearly 30% of summer
 water use is for irrigation, indicating that a 30% reduction in usage could lead to a
 corresponding increase in duration of fuel reserves and associated backup power supply.
 Other methods to reduce water usage during disasters, such as wearing clothes multiple
 times before washing, hotels not laundering sheets daily, and using disposable items instead
 of washing dishes, could also help extend backup power duration. However, these
 suggestions rely on voluntary compliance from the population and cannot be enforced,
 making them unreliable for disaster planning.
 • Other mechanisms: Aside from requesting voluntary demand curtailment in an emergency,
 the water utilities have already implemented most of the available water-domain–as opposed
 to power-domain–approaches for extending the duration of available water. The utilities
 have already begun the process to increase water storage capacity with new tanks and water
 losses are low.
Early in the project, the project team decided that using energy stored in VTA electric buses to
power water pumps would be investigated as a possible low-cost, fast-track solution to augment
backup power. For context, buses, like most other battery-electric vehicles, are charged using power
from the alternating current (AC) grid to the vehicle directly (via L1 and L2 charging, where an
onboard rectifier converts AC to direct current (DC)) or indirectly via conversion to DC using
equipment outside of the vehicle, typically associated with fast charging at high power.
Recently, bidirectional charging technology–commonly referred to as vehicle-to-grid (V2G), vehicle-
to-load (V2L), or vehicle-to-everything (V2X)–has emerged as a potential solution to support the
grid or a facility by leveraging the storage capacity of electric vehicles. Energy from electric bus
batteries is available as direct current (DC). To feed this energy into the grid or to normally grid-

 11

powered loads, it must be converted to alternating current (AC). Additionally, AC electricity must
meet safety and power quality standards, such as the ISO 15118 series [1].
Today, there are two methods for transferring energy from an electric vehicle to an AC system:
V2G-DC and V2G-AC (see Appendix 3 for details). The DC or AC modifier relates to how the
energy crosses the vehicle boundary. In V2G-DC, power conversion from DC to AC occurs outside
the vehicle, while in V2G-AC, this happens onboard.
Despite the potential advantages of the solution, leveraging VTA transit bus assets for emergency
power support is not feasible currently due to technology barriers that are difficult to overcome at
low price with existing off-the-shelf components. However, three next-best possible solutions were
identified, and are described below:
 • DC-V2G: The VTA currently operates seven DC buses, with an additional four expected in
 June 2025 and another 4 in April 2026, which could support a V2G-DC solution. However,
 only two companies, Tellus Power Green and Nuvve, are currently planning to offer
 bidirectional DC charging, but their products are not yet available for sale. BorgWarner, a
 key supplier of bidirectional DC electric vehicle service equipment (EVSE) hardware, has
 announced a pullback from the market. We recommend future project stakeholders, for
 example VTA representatives, contact vendor sales offices (Tellus Power Green, Nuvve, or
 others) for updates on product availability and pricing, noting that for this application,
 chargers could be designed for V2L rather than V2G, which may simplify features. A pilot
 project could cost between $200K and $300K for the installed hardware, making it a
 candidate for state funding opportunities.
 • AC-V2G: Retrofitting the fleet of DC buses as proposed by InventEV is suitable for a pilot
 project. If successful, this solution aligns closely with community expectations due to its low
 production costs. It would utilize on-board power electronics to allow buses to provide AC
 power directly to pumping stations via “camlock” taps mounted on the pump house or tap
 pedestal. Prototype costs range from $100K to $300K, depending on whether 3-phase
 power is provided at 208V or 480V. InventEV is open to partnering with the Martha’s
 Vineyard Commission, VTA, or others to develop this solution, which is recommended if
 funding becomes available.
 • Mobile storage: This solution is currently the only turnkey option that can be delivered
 quickly. Innoversa, an established provider of mobile storage solutions, is interested in
 providing customized equipment, including small trailers to power pumping stations and a
 larger unit to connect with a PV array. The estimated hardware cost for a pilot system is
 around $1M. Finding an island solar array, with suitable charging infrastructure and usage
 agreement for recharging the mobile unit is a necessary next step. Collaborating with a
 partner, such as a construction equipment rental company or the local electric utility
 (Eversource), could enhance the business case. This solution can be implemented
 immediately if funding opportunities arise.
Key Considerations: Demand curtailment can extend backup power supply during emergencies,
regardless of the chosen power solution. These efforts could be initiated alongside other strategies to
increase the likelihood of sustaining water supply for 14 consecutive days during peak demand.
Augmenting the capacity of the existing propane storage tanks could also be considered as a low-risk
solution that could be implemented quickly and at relatively low cost. While this solution is not
directly aligned with the original community goals for this project, one could argue that simply

 12

adding to storage capacity does not produce significant additional environmental concerns, because
the extra fuel would not be used except during emergencies.
Next Steps: The ETIPP project concludes with potential funding opportunities and water
conservation resources developed by Island Institute. The Sandia team has identified industry
contacts for further discussions. If the water departments wish to pursue solutions leveraging the
VTA bus fleet, collaborating with the VTA for assessing bus accessibility to well sites is the next step
that can be taken immediately and at little cost. Further discussions with InventEV and BYD/Ride
could also be productive in preparation for possible funding opportunities at state or federal level in
the mid-term future, as a subsequent step. Alternatively, the MVC or the water departments could
seek collaborations with Eversource or other potential partners for deployment of a turnkey mobile
storage solution, also with potential external funding in mind.

1. INTRODUCTION
The Martha’s Vineyard Commission, a planning and regulatory body created in 1974 to oversee and
coordinate development in the six towns on Martha’s Vineyard, applied to the U.S. Department of
Energy’s Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Program (ETIPP) in 2023 on behalf of the
water departments of Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury on Martha’s Vineyard, Massachusetts.
Selected for the third cohort of ETIPP technical assistance starting in Fall 2023, the Commission
partnered with Sandia National Laboratories, the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), and
Island Institute. Bringing together local stakeholders including the three towns’ water utilities, energy
committees, and fire departments, as well as the island’s transit authority and State Department of
Environmental Protection (DEP), the team developed a project scope that would evaluate options
for ensuring public water supply during extended power outages. This report summarizes Sandia’s
assessment of alternative energy and water system operation options that align with the stakeholders’
definition of adequate resilience.

1.1. Problem Statement
The Martha’s Vineyard Commission applied to the ETIPP program for technical assistance on
behalf of Edgartown’s, Oak Bluffs’, and Tisbury’s water departments to evaluate the
technoeconomic feasibility of using the local municipal electric transit bus fleet to supply power to
groundwater pumping well sites during grid outages. Stakeholders agreed on a design objective to
ensure that the island’s public water systems can provide adequate potable water to residents and
visitors for 14 days during prolonged power failures during the peak summer tourist season when
water and energy demands are highest.

1.2. Site Context
Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury are located on the northeast coast of Martha’s Vineyard, an
island approximately 7 miles south of Massachusetts, accessible only by air or water. As a popular
summer tourist and seasonal second home destination, these towns experience significant seasonal
population fluctuations (Table 1). Recent estimates indicate that the seasonal population increases by
approximately +482% in Edgartown, +358% in Oak Bluffs, and +274% in Tisbury compared to
year-round residents. [2] This influx contributes to seasonal variations in water and power demand,
shown in Table 2.

1.3. Water System
The majority of Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury residents and businesses are served by
centralized municipal water systems that supply public water from groundwater pumping in the
Outwash Plain Aquifer. The water quality is good, with treatment limited to corrosion control at the
wells before distribution, and chlorination for pumping stations in the Oak Bluffs Water District.
Water demand varies seasonally, significantly influenced by the number of visitors and residents on-
island, and landscape irrigation, leading to an up to tenfold increase in demand from February to
August (Table 2)
There are twelve active groundwater wells across the three utilities, described in Table 3, which
includes runtime fractions, nominal flow rates, and pressure heads. Figure 1 shows the pump
locations on the island. While each of the utilities uses a water storage tank located to serve its own
territory, the three water systems are linked and to some extent can cooperate to prevent service
discontinuity.
The majority of energy requirements for water supply in these towns are associated with
groundwater pumping. Due to the favorable groundwater quality, there are no standalone drinking
water treatment plants, so there are no additional high energy demands for treatment. Water storage
tank levels are maintained through system-wide pumping, without separate energy demands for lift
stations.
All pump motors utilize variable speed drives, which include power converters that adjust the
frequency supplied to the motors, allowing for controlled speed regulation. This feature also
prevents the high inrush current typical of non-variable speed motors. In this system, motor speeds
are set to approximately 90% of nominal full speed. Pumps activate when storage tank levels fall
below a specified minimum and deactivate when the maximum setpoint is reached.

1.4. Energy System
Imports from power plants on the mainland via undersea cables originating on Cape Cod, is the
main source of electricity on Martha’s Vineyard, with a 15% share of the total energy generated by
on-island solar arrays. The 806MW Vineyard Wind 1 project, currently nearing completion, will
connect to the transmission infrastructure at Covell’s beach on Cape Cod, and contribute to
Martha’s Vineyard’s energy supply. [3] Electricity on Martha’s Vineyard is supplied by Eversource
Energy, an investor-owned utility that serves New Hampshire, Connecticut, and Massachusetts.
Electric rate negotiations and energy efficiency programs are managed by the Cape Light Compact, a
consumer advocacy group representing 21 towns in Cape Cod, Martha’s Vineyard, and Dukes
County. Currently, four 23-kilovolt submarine cables supply power to the island, with three running
from Falmouth, MA on the mainland to West Chop in Tisbury, and one running to East Chop in
Oak Bluffs. Some of these cables are derated due to their age. The cables are vulnerable and difficult
to repair, and the Vineyard’s 50-megawatt peak electricity usage level is fast approaching their
current 62.5-megawatt capacity. Five 12.5-megawatt mobile diesel generators in Tisbury help serve

 17

peak summer loads and serve as backup should one of the undersea cables be damaged.[4]
[5]Following a cable failure in 2021, Eversource began installing two new undersea cables, each over
5 miles long, at a cost of approximately $310 million, with construction starting in December 2023
and were expected to be operational by March 2026. The project will replace an existing cable and
add a fifth, both capable of handling 23 kilovolts, allowing for the retirement of the mobile diesel
generators. [7] Energy fuels on the island, primarily oil, propane, diesel, and gasoline, are shipped by
ferry or barge.

1.5. Existing and Emerging Risks to Reliable Power Supply
Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury can experience power outages due to interruptions in electric
supply on the mainland, damage to undersea cables, or failures in the on-island distribution system
itself. The electrical infrastructure would be further compromised by disruptions in the delivery of
fuel (diesel, propane), which would hinder backup generator refueling at many island locations.
Historically, the primary causes of large-scale, extended grid interruptions have been natural hazards,
such as summer hurricanes and winter storms. [8] Additionally, heat waves are recognized as an
emerging threat by the North American Reliability Corporation. [9] Wildfires are also an emerging
local concern on Martha’s Vineyard.

1.6. Existing Water Utility Outage Response Plans
The Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury water departments are part of the Massachusetts Water
and Wastewater Agency Response Network (WARN), a mutual aid system that provides resources,
equipment, and manpower to utilities during emergencies. [10] The Massachusetts Department of
Environmental Protection requires utilities to maintain up-to-date emergency response plans.
All pumping stations in Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury are equipped with backup generators
powered by propane-fueled reciprocating engines, except for Tisbury Water Works Well #1, which
uses a diesel generator. All generators are tested monthly, and the propane and diesel tanks are
regularly refueled. Examples of backup generators are shown in Figure 2. During a site visit by
Sandia staff in June 2024, the generators and associated equipment, including tanks and transfer
switches, were found to be well-maintained and functional.

1.7. Other On-Island Resilience Assets
The Martha's Vineyard Transit Authority (VTA) operates a bus transportation system on Martha's
Vineyard, Massachusetts, with 13 fixed routes and a fleet of 35 buses. [11] At the time of writing, 23
of these buses were fully electric, with 18 using AC chargers and 5 using DC fast chargers. VTA
owns a microgrid at its bus depot near Edgartown, featuring a solar panel array (467 kWAC/695
kWDC), a 1.5 MWh battery, and a backup diesel generator. [12]
Under normal conditions, VTA electric buses are charged at the depot with wired chargers and at
various inductive charging stations along the routes. The VTA microgrid is islandable. Under normal
conditions it is connected to the electric grid. During a power outage, the microgrid disconnects and
operates in island mode, generating power through grid-forming inverters and the diesel generator,
with the 1.5 MWh battery managing the balance between generation and load.
The Martha’s Vineyard Commission and VTA were interested in understanding whether the buses
could be used as emergency power sources for the water pumping stations during grid outages for
several reasons. First, by leveraging past VTA investments in its fleet and the microgrid, this option
could potentially be less costly than alternatives. Second, the transit system would likely operate at
reduced capacity during prolonged outages, making some electric buses available for emergency
service. Third, the existing photovoltaic (PV) + battery + generator microgrid can operate off-grid
during outages and recharge the buses daily after they have powered the pumps. The fleet includes
buses with varying battery sizes, averaging between 230 kWh and 455 kWh, while the average pump
power draw is approximately 50 kW, allowing for about 5-6 hours of pump operation at full
capacity. The number of electric buses aligns well with the number of pumps.

2. METHODOLOGY
Stakeholder engagement was central to the ETIPP project’s approach. Prior to visiting Sandia did
extensive preliminary scoping in collaboration with the water departments including:
 • Water network (hydraulic) modeling to identify low-cost operational modifications and
 outreach activities that could be adopted by the water utilities to extend the longevity of
 water supply.
 • Energy analysis to determine cost-effective solutions to maintain pump operation in off-grid
 conditions while adhering to existing state environmental standards and regulations
 governing the water utilities.

2.1. Stakeholder Engagement
The Sandia team visited Martha’s Vineyard in June 2024 to gain local insights and collaborate with
stakeholders to refine the problem statement, objectives, and methodology.
Sandia staff met with Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury water department personnel and toured
the offices, control rooms, well sites, storage tanks, and lift stations of each water department. The
tour also included visiting the utility-scale solar field and backup generators in Tisbury. Discussions
focused on energy options to supplement existing backup systems, and the constraints involved.
Potential low- or zero-emission options presented to the stakeholders and discussed included solar,
batteries, hydrogen fuel cells, wind power, and biofuels. Key constraints included:
 1. Cost: water utilities have pressing priorities greater than increasing system resilience; for
 example, costly lead pipe replacement.
 2. Regulation: State and local regulations restrict battery siting near wells due to
 contamination concerns
 3. Land Availability: Limited cleared land near wells for solar and storage equipment
 installation.
Sandia staff then hosted a stakeholder workshop at the Martha’s Vineyard Commission, attended by
representatives from the Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury water departments, energy
committees, fire departments, and the Massachusetts Department of Environmental Protection.
Sandia presented preliminary energy options for discussion to assess how well each addressed
community concerns. The options spanned all practically viable technologies and included:
 1. Energy storage batteries, potentially combined with PV generation, near the well sites.
 2. Hydrogen storage and fuel cells located near the well sites.
 3. Flow batteries located near the well sites
 4. Transit buses charging at the Vineyard Transit Authority (VTA) depot microgrid and driving
 to deliver power to the well sites.
 5. Mobile storage batteries charging at existing or future large PV arrays and being towed to
 deliver power to the well sites.
After evaluating the pros and cons of each solution, the community stakeholders chose to pursue
two primary options for further analysis: options 4 and 5, while considering other options for future
exploration as technology improves. There were several reasons for this choice, principal among
these was implementation cost, but also included concerns about possible contamination of the well
and land availability for siting large equipment such as PV arrays and storage batteries. Other

 20

reasons considered by the community included technology readiness (for example in the case of
flow batteries) or the sourcing of fuel (for example in the case of hydrogen).
Option 4 involved partnering with the Vineyard Transit Authority, which operates 23 fully electric
buses. Eighteen of these buses use AC chargers, while the newer five use DC chargers. Study of the
idea of using buses as an emergency power source for pumps during grid outages began at the MVC
prior to this project, building on pilot projects in other parts of the country that explored the V2G
use of electric school buses. This option is appealing for several reasons: it could be cost-effective,
the transit system would likely operate at reduced capacity during prolonged outages, making buses
available for emergency service, and the existing PV + battery + generator microgrid at the bus
depot can recharge the buses daily after powering the pumps. Bus battery sizes range between 230
kWh and 455 kWh, while the average pump power draw is about 50 kW, allowing for approximately
5-9 hours of pump operation at full capacity. The number of electric buses aligns well with the
number of pumps, and the the team discussed the merits of this option during the 2024 meeting.
Option 5 involves investing in mobile energy storage, consisting of batteries and power conversion
equipment packaged onto a trailer. Mobile energy storage is an emerging technology with diverse
applications, such as replacing conventional mobile generators at construction sites or events and
providing power during grid upgrades. Several startups, including Nomad Power and Innoversa, are
competing in this space. These solutions offer robust, integrated storage and power conversion
systems, capable of forming a microgrid with power generation equipment like PV arrays or diesel
generators. This option was considered a backup (and more expensive) alternative to using buses.
Sandia also facilitated two subsequent virtual stakeholder meetings in October 2024 and March 2025
to provide interim progress reports and review energy option results. These meetings communicated
technical findings from the water and energy modeling work and confirm the acceptability of the
selected power supply alternatives.

2.2. Water Modeling

2.2.1.     Approach
The Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury water systems were modeled using standard open-source
software tools to assess normal operations and simulate power outage scenarios. The average
minimum and maximum runtimes for each pump were analyzed to determine the energy
requirements for winter and summer operations for each town’s water systems individually and
when interconnected. This data was then integrated into the energy modeling and analysis.
Developing water network models requires input from water utilities regarding both static and
dynamic information. Static information, such as the layout of pipes, tanks, pumps, wells, and valves,
is relatively easy to obtain, often available in map form for maintenance purposes. In contrast,
dynamic information, which reflects the operational characteristics of the system, can be more
challenging to collect. Modelers must make several key decisions.
The first is how to model the demand on the system by consumers. Water demand from domestic
and commercial users is often sporadic and consists of short, unpredictable events, such as flushing
toilets or taking showers. In contrast, landscaping and industrial usage typically has a consistent flow
rate and longer duration. Demand is usually modeled as average usage, often using hourly averages
to balance detail with execution time. Daily usage patterns, adjusted for weekends, are employed for
extended simulations spanning days to months, to calculate average system behaviors.

 21

The second decision is the level of detail that should be included in the network. Models can vary in
detail. Full-pipe models include representations of pipes down to individual homes or businesses,
which can be time-consuming to run and difficult to populate without hourly usage data.
Conversely, highly skeletonized models only include larger water mains, averaging demands from
entire neighborhoods at single points. Previous research has shown that full-pipe models are not
necessarily superior for overall system hydraulic behavior compared to moderately skeletonized
models (though this may not hold true for water quality simulations, which were not included in the
Martha’s Vineyard study). [13] [14]
Accurate source water and pump information are crucial for effective modeling. On Martha’s
Vineyard, all water is sourced from a sole-source aquifer via wells, with water levels varying
throughout the year. The cone of depression affects well water levels during pumping. Accurate
pump curves–pressure head versus flow rate–are often difficult to obtain due to changes with pump
age and the need for testing at multiple flow rates and pressures, which can be costly and time-
consuming. While valves can complicate modeling, the towns on Martha’s Vineyard do not use
valves for pressure management, simplifying the models. Isolation and check valves are
straightforward model elements.
Finally, operational rules are essential for simulating extended network behavior. These rules dictate
when pumps turn on and off, typically in response to pressure conditions or tank levels.

2.2.2. Data and Sources
At the start of the ETIPP project, each of the three towns had network models with varying levels
of detail and formats. The Tisbury model was provided in EPANET INP-file format by the utility’s
contracted consultant. The Oak Bluffs model was delivered as GIS shapefiles, exported from a
commercial format by its consultant. The Edgartown model was created by the Sandia team using
PDF maps of the water network, which were digitized into shapefiles. Additional details were
manually added based on information from the utilities. Elevation data was obtained by extracting
two-foot contours from a U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) digital elevation map of the island, using
the nearest contour elevation for points on the pipe network (Figure 3).

Pump curves for Tisbury were included in the provided EPANET model and used as-is. The Oak
Bluffs model lacked pump curves, but the utility supplied recent well maintenance reports
containing empirical pump curves from the workover contractor. These were used directly, while
curves for two pumps without data were estimated based on the provided curves. Edgartown did
not have pump curves available, but its pumps operate at a fixed flow rate, so dummy curves were
used to enforce behavior at the setpoints.
The Tisbury model is the smallest and most skeletonized; skeletonization is a process of optimizing
a water network model. For example, rather than modeling every house, demands that are along the
same main can be aggregated and treated as a single node. The consultant who developed the model
for Tisbury had performed this skeletonization, and it was used as provided.
The Oak Bluffs model included demand values linked to individual addresses, expressed as a
percentage of total system demand rather than direct water volumes. These demands indicated their
assigned nodes in the network model, with multiple demands assigned to the same node, reflecting
prior skeletonization.
The Edgartown model, developed by the Sandia team, did not have demand information at the per-
home or aggregated level, as full demand generation was outside the project scope. Instead, demands
were assigned stochastically throughout the system to mimic general usage patterns observed in the
other two towns.
None of the towns’ models included time-of-day usage patterns. However, given the island's

2.2.3. Assumptions
The assumptions made in this research provided conservative bounding values for planning
purposes. The following assumptions were applied in developing the model and performance
scenarios:

 26

 • Demands were calculated using the known monthly totals for the minimum and maximum
 usage months from 2022 (February and August, respectively).
 • Daily demand was not adjusted for weekdays and weekends.
 • Wellhead levels were manually adjusted to ensure reasonable system pressures and
 simulation convergence.
 • Stochastic base demands in the Edgartown model were generated using an exponential
 random variable scaled to align with Oak Bluffs and Tisbury.
 • Pump controls for Edgartown were manually modified to ensure simulation convergence
 based on pump curve assumptions.
 • Tanks were isolated from the system when outlet pressure dropped to 20 psi (15 psi for the
 elevated Tisbury tanks) to prevent negative collapse pressure in the surrounding pipes.
 Artificial minimum tank levels were assigned to simulate this response.
Future improvements to this assessment could include developing better demand patterns based on
tank and pump flow mass balance over a week, adjusting base demands for different seasons
(considering many homes are unoccupied in winter), and updating pump curves and well (reservoir)
heads to reflect real-world conditions throughout the year.

2.2.4. Modeling Software
The Water Network Tool for Resilience software (WNTR) was used for the model development,
simulation, and analysis [16]. WNTR uses the EPANET [17] hydraulic model for extended period
simulation. WNTR is a Python® package. Other python packages (Shapely, SciPy, and GeoPandas)
were also used during network model creation to connect junctions and demands, assign elevations
to the Edgartown model, and to connect disconnected pipes. QGIS was used to convert coordinates
to a consistent reference frame (CRS) and to extract the elevation contours from the USGS digital
elevation map. QGIS was also used to check that the models aligned with visible surface elements as
a quality assurance check. The standalone version of the EPANET software was used to debug the
combined model. All development was done in Jupyter Notebooks to provide a documented
process for both development and simulation and to create consolidated results.

2.2.5. Base scenarios
Two base operational scenarios were used: minimum and maximum usage months. February's
monthly demand, shown in Table 2, served as the base demand for all towns, with individual water
usage at each node scaled to match the average hourly usage for that month. Two 24-hour patterns
were created: the first, with an average value of 1.0, was used for the minimum usage month; the
second was tailored for each town to reflect summer demand, also shown in Table 2. The scaling
factors for each town were: Edgartown, 10.04; Oak Bluffs, 5.77; Tisbury, 4.42.
Average runtimes for each pump during the minimum and maximum usage scenarios were
calculated using a 10-day extended period simulation. An extended period simulation was used to
ensure the model had fully calibrated, but the lack of weekend vs. weekday usage meant that it was
not necessary to capture multiple weekends for statistical purposes, so a 10-day simulation was used
to decrease simulation run time. This data was then used in the energy analysis to assess how long
each pump would operate on emergency power.

2.2.6. Backup power failure scenarios
Once power is permanently lost (either through grid outage, or backup power loss, or both), the
systems can only supply water until the tanks are empty. However, a minimum pressure must be
maintained to avoid damage to the system. The simulations do not attempt to isolate the network
beyond tank isolation. The duration of water availability after pumps run out of energy (time to
failure) depends on the level of continued usage and whether the interconnects are opened. The
system is considered to have “failed” when the pumps are out of energy and the tanks are isolated to
protect the infrastructure.
The following simulations were conducted to determine the time-to-failure for tank storage:
 • Base water use:
 o Minimum usage (February demands)
 o 70% of maximum 2023 use (due to voluntary conservation, such as turning off
 irrigation)
 o 110% of maximum 2023 use (August demands, plus larger-than-normal surge of
 tourists)
 • Tank level limitations:
 o 20 psig static pressure leads to tank isolation (via minimum tank level rule)
 • Interconnects:
 o Interconnects remain closed
 o Interconnects opened after 24 hours
 o Interconnects opened after 1 hour (effectively immediately)
Timed rules in EPANET were used to shut off all pumps at simulation time = 36 hours, providing a
calibration period for pressures to stabilize. Five-minutes was used for the hydraulic simulation,
reporting, and rule step sizes.

2.3. Energy Options Analysis

2.3.1.      Approach
The primary goal of the energy analysis was to identify cost-effective options for extending the
number of days that the Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury pumping stations could operate off-
grid while maintaining water service without compromising system integrity or depleting storage.
The first step involved assessing the energy needs of each pumping station and their collective
energy use. The second step analyzed available options to provide the necessary energy for extended
off-grid operations. Options that align with community needs, operational requirements of the water
utilities, and environmental and regulatory constraints were then examined in detail.

 28

Currently, backup power is supplied by propane and diesel generators, which can support pumping
station operations at peak load for about one week off-grid without water-saving measures or
refueling. While increasing the size of the fuel tanks is a straightforward solution, stakeholders
expressed a preference for alternatives that reduce noise and emissions and rely less on off-island
services, including the provision of fuels.
Among the considered solutions, using energy from the Vineyard Transit Authority’s (VTA) bus
depot microgrid and its fleet of electric buses to transfer energy to the pumping stations was favored
for its low cost and potential for quick implementation. An important factor in this decision is that it
was thought that the VTA buses were V2G- or V2L-capable, an assumption that would later prove
not completely accurate. Regardless, based on this initial assumption, the third step in the energy
options analysis was to estimate the energy production capacity of the VTA microgrid during the
peak water consumption month of August to evaluate its adequacy to support the water pumping
needs.
A second constraint was determining how much energy can be transferred to the buses at the depot,
and delivered to the pumping stations daily, given its existing capacity and configuration, and
considering that buses must travel between locations. The fourth step was to create a realistic
schedule for bus dispatch during peak pumping requirements.
Finally, practical considerations included assessing whether the buses and charging infrastructure can
transfer energy to a load at a sufficient rate. If not, options for enabling this capability would have to
be explored. Additionally, alternatives that offer similar benefits to using transit buses, including
cost-effectiveness and compliance with environmental and public safety constraints, were
considered.
Based on the findings, recommendations were made to support future funding applications for pilot
projects or full-scale implementations.

2.3.2. Data and Sources
The data and information resources used in the analysis are summarized below for each step
outlined previously. For the pumping energy needs analysis, peak pumping power was estimated
based on water pressure head, estimated pump efficiencies, and runtime fractions, as described in
section 2.1.
The key assumption is that pumping power is directly proportional to the product of pump head
and flow rate. This is based on the premise that pipe flow head losses are minimal compared to the
total head required to lift water from the water table to the average height of the water column in
the tank. It is also assumed that pumps are well-matched to their head and flow rates, with a
relatively high efficiency of 75%. The total energy requirement for each pump is proportional to its
runtime fraction (the fraction of each day the pump is operational), reported previously in Table 3.
While the individual water demand profile for each well is unknown, a proxy (described in 2.2.2) was
used to estimate hourly discharge patterns for the bus batteries associated with each well and the
necessary charge sessions at the depot to replenish the batteries.
Electricity generation and delivery at the VTA depot relies on solar PV and storage batteries,
supplemented by diesel backup generators. Solar PV output primarily depends on weather
conditions, including solar irradiance, and to a lesser extent, ambient temperature and wind.
Although future weather conditions during a grid outage cannot be predicted, typical meteorological
year (TMY) data (see Appendix A) can be used to estimate likely power generation. The

 29

configuration of the PV array, including area, tilt, and orientation, is also factored into the
calculations. This allows for the estimation of hourly power generation over an entire year under
typical weather conditions.

3.2. Energy Modeling Findings
Given the constraints on siting power generation and storage near water pumping stations, along
with stakeholder priorities, the analysis considered scenarios based on mobile energy solutions to
identify required technologies and estimate associated costs. Two solutions utilize the VTA electric
bus fleet, while one relies on dedicated mobile storage solutions. These solutions are designed to be
implemented before any water conservation measures or system interconnection changes, assuming
the system operates in a business-as-usual mode.

3.2.1. Options for Integrating VTA Buses and Microgrid

3.2.1.1. Power Production and Bus Charging
The VTA fleet and microgrid are relatively well-suited to produce power at rates sufficient to
maintain pumping across the 12 active well sites. The optimal strategy for recharging buses using
solar power at the VTA microgrid is to schedule charging sessions to align with the solar generation

 38

profile. This direct transfer of power from the array to the buses, without intermediary storage,
maximizes efficiency.
Optimizing charging schedules for efficiency involves a mixed-integer optimization process that
considers existing pumping needs and available solar resources, which depend on weather
conditions. Fully determining this optimization was not feasible within the ETIPP project so a
manual version of the process is presented here. The average solar power generation profile is
compared to the bus fleet's charging needs in Figure 11, with data shown in 10-kWh increments.
The subset of the BYD electric fleet includes two 313 kWh buses, two 280 kWh buses, eight 230
kWh buses, and four 180 kWh buses. Assuming 90% utilization of nominal battery capacity and the
bus dispatch schedule outlined in Appendix 2, the total daily charging needs are 3,380 kWh, closely
matching the microgrid's average generation capacity of 3,340 kWh. Any small mismatches can be
covered by the microgrid’s diesel generator.
All buses are assumed to charge at a maximum rate of 80 kW. If the total charge time is not a
multiple of 80 kW, a charging session may stop partway through an hour. Additionally, up to four
charge ports can receive power simultaneously, and each bus charges continuously until the required
amount is reached.

3.2.1.2. Dispatch Schedule for Energy Delivery
After establishing an optimal charging schedule that aligns bus charging loads with solar power
production, the next step is to assign buses to pumping stations. The strategy is to allocate buses
with battery sizes proportional to the energy consumption of specific pumps, meaning that pumps
with higher daily energy use are paired with buses that have larger batteries. In some cases, two
buses may be assigned to a single pumping station, with details provided in Appendix B
When not charging, and assuming they are supporting water pumping rather than transporting
passengers, buses can discharge power to pumps and ancillary devices, travel between the depot and
pumping stations, undergo maintenance, or remain idle. A minimum of two hours is allowed
between the end of a charging session and the start of a pump support session, and vice versa.

 40

Given that trips are generally shorter than 30 minutes, this two-hour buffer accommodates tasks
such as connecting cables to the pumping station's electrical panel or charging station.
Figure 13 presents a sample schedule that meets all constraints and operational needs during a grid
outage, with buses supporting pumping operations. This schedule is illustrative and would require
stakeholder feedback, including an updated bus inventory, agreements on bus availability for water
pumping support, access constraints from water utilities (for example, access to the Lagoon Pond
pumping station with a large vehicle such as a transit bus may be impractical, so alternative solutions
such as not using this station and diverting energy to others), staff roles, and other relevant
considerations. Additionally, because solar resources vary with season and weather, schedules may
need to be dynamic and generated by an optimization tool for several days, updated daily.
For example, at the Edgartown Water Department’s Quenomica station, which consumes 1,019
kWh per day (see Appendix B), two buses are dispatched to reduce the load on the diesel backup
generator. One bus has a capacity of 264 kWh and the other 145 kWh, providing a total of 409 kWh,
or about 40% of the station's energy requirement. The larger bus (orange) charges from 6 a.m. to 10
a.m. and discharges from noon to 7 p.m., while the smaller bus (magenta) charges from p.m. to 6
p.m. (taking advantage of the rapidly diminishing solar generation, augmented by stored microgrid
battery power) and supports pumping from midnight to 8 a.m. the next day. There is at least a two-
hour buffer between charging and discharging sessions, and these sessions do not overlap. Overall,
the buses can supply approximately 36% of the total pumping energy, limited by the energy
generated by the PV array at the VTA depot, extending the water utilities' off-grid operation from 7
days to about 10.5 days under normal conditions.
However, the analysis indicates that the VTA electric bus fleet and microgrid only extend off-grid
operation by 3.5 days, falling short of the desired 7 days. The primary bottleneck is the solar array's
inability to generate enough energy to meet half of the pumping stations' energy needs in business-
as-usual mode. A secondary bottleneck arises from the bus dispatch schedule, which may require
multiple trips to a pumping station, feasible for some but not all stations. These bottlenecks could be
addressed by increasing the size of the depot microgrid, charging buses at alternative self-generating
locations, using faster chargers, and deploying more buses. Some or all these improvements are
likely to occur in the future. Additionally, reducing water demand to 70% of nominal peak would
achieve similar results with existing capacity.

3.2.1.3. Systems Integration
While using transit buses as a resource-efficient solution to enhance the resilience of the water
pumping infrastructure has been demonstrated, this alone is insufficient for full systems integration.
A critical component is the reliable and safe transfer of energy from the buses to the pumps.
As mentioned previously, bidirectional charging technology in the various forms of V2G, V2L or
V2X, has emerged as a potential solution to deliver intermittent renewable energy to the grid by
leveraging the storage capacity of electric vehicles. The BYD buses initially purchased for the VTA
fleet were unique due to their use of high-power AC charging (up to 80 kW), allowing a full charge
in 2-4 hours, depending on battery capacity. Contrary to initial expectations, none of the current
BYD buses can supply sufficient power to the grid or an islanded load to support pumping
operations. Furthermore, BYD has indicated that an original equipment manufacturer retrofit option
is not currently available. The challenge lies not in energy availability, which has been shown to be
sufficient, but in the mechanism for transferring energy from the bus to a load. To overcome this
problem, Sandia reached out to several technology providers to explore feasible solutions that meet
stakeholder criteria.
Moreover, while VTA’s fleet currently consists primarily of AC buses, the authority has recently
purchased DC buses and will transition to a majority DC fleet within 2 to 3 years. However, no
commercially available transit bus in the U.S. currently supports V2G. Therefore, Sandia explored
both DC and AC technology options by engaging with vendors working on V2G demonstration and
commercialization projects.
Leveraging several relevant V2G pilot projects (see Error! Reference source not found.), a
connection was established with the primary solution providers in the West Oakland Public Library
project in California. In this initiative, battery electric and hydrogen fuel cell transit buses will power
HVAC equipment in the library during grid outages, similar to the intended use of VTA buses for
water pumping.
The West Oakland project is a collaboration among AC Transit (transit bus operator), Schneider
Electric (switchgear and microgrid controls), BorgWarner (bidirectional chargers), the City of
Oakland (library building), and New Flyer (the buses), funded by the California Energy Commission
(CEC). New Flyer manufactures various transit technologies, including diesel, hybrid, CNG, trolley,
battery electric, and fuel cell buses, with plans for 50% of their production to be zero-emissions by
2026.
Project participants are working to ensure compliance with IEC 15118-20 standards before testing.
This international standard defines the communication protocol for bidirectional power transfer
(V2G) and wireless power transfer (WPT) between electric vehicles (EVs) and electric vehicle supply
equipment (EVSE), expanding on ISO 15118-2 and enhancing security measures. The CEC will
publish a report documenting the pilot project's results, at project end. Recently, BorgWarner, a
leading provider of DC fast charging solutions, acquired Rhombus to obtain their V2G technology.
A typical Rhombus V2G charging unit is shown in Figure 14.
Unfortunately, over the course of the ETIPP project, BorgWarner announced its exit from the
charging solutions business due to supply chain developments and market uncertainty. This leaves
few options for V2G-DC solutions that can meet Martha’s Vineyard's water pumping requirements.
At the time of writing, Nuvve is expected to announce a product, and Tellus Power Green continues
to list V2G products on their web portal. The coming months will determine the commercial
availability of these solutions. Due to the lack of installations, estimating the cost per unit installed is

challenging, with reported costs ranging from $20,000 to $150,000, depending on location, power
level, and electrical infrastructure upgrades. For water pumping projects, costs are expected to be on
the lower end for equipment and grid connection but may incur higher labor costs.

3.2.2. Other viable solutions
Battery-based mobile energy storage solutions have recently emerged as alternatives to mobile
reciprocating generators for remote sites, construction, and events. These solutions typically consist
of trailer- or skid-mounted batteries with power conversion and control equipment, provided by
companies like Innoversa [20] and Nomad Power [21]. Examples of these configurations are shown
in Figure 17. Smaller trailers, with energy storage capacities between 200 kWh and 300 kWh, can be
matched 1:1 with bus batteries and deployed using similar strategies and schedules. The typical
operation involves towing a fully charged mobile storage unit to a pumping site, using it until
discharged, then towing it to a recharging site, and repeating the cycle until the emergency is
resolved. Unlike the VTA buses, these mobile energy storage units would not have dedicated
charging facilities on the island. However, it may be possible to negotiate charging agreements at the
VTA depot during grid outages, especially if there is excess capacity from underutilized buses.
Alternatively, existing and planned large solar arrays across the island could be leveraged for

 45

charging, as shown in Figure 18. The mobile charging station would consist of a battery energy
storage system sized between 0.5 MWh and 1.0 MWh connected to the PV grid, along with several
DC chargers to charge multiple mobile storage trailers simultaneously. The main disadvantage of
standalone mobile storage solutions is their cost, approximately $1 million per MWh. In contrast,
transit bus-based solutions utilize existing storage and power generation infrastructure. However,
mobile storage trailers could serve other purposes, creating viable business cases for events,
construction sites, maintenance work, and utility operations support. For example, Eversource has
deployed two 500 kWh mobile energy storage units to provide emergency power during distribution
outages (planned or otherwise) at other locations.

3.2.3. Less viable solutions
Locating Li-ion batteries near well sites poses contamination risks to the aquifer due to potential
fires that could release hazardous materials. There are two accepted methods for handling battery
fires: allowing the fire to burn out if the risk of spreading and harm from smoke is low or using large
amounts of water to cool the batteries and prevent thermal runaway. However, the runoff from the
latter method could locally contaminate the aquifer, rendering the well non-viable long-term. While
placing PV generation near well sites could reduce the size and cost of battery storage needed for
resilience, the heavily wooded areas would require significant tree clearing, making this option
impractical despite potentially short capital cost recovery.
Hydrogen storage is an emerging technology that decouples energy storage from power production
capacity. Unlike battery systems, which are typically designed for four-hour power delivery,
hydrogen systems can be sized to meet both maximum power and energy requirements, potentially
lowering costs. Hydrogen can be stored in high-pressure tanks, low-pressure metal hydrides, or as a
liquid. For this study, metal hydride storage is preferred due to its cost, safety, ease of
implementation, and commercial availability [22]. While low-pressure metal hydride storage can
produce toxic chemicals under certain conditions, the risk of ignition is lower than with batteries,
and fires are easier to control. A hydrogen storage system would require a fuel cell and inverter for
AC power production, and if local hydrogen production is desired, a hydrolyzer would be needed to
split water into hydrogen and oxygen. All components can be sourced commercially, for example

 47

from the Italian hydrogen storage system integrator GKN Hydrogen. However, community
stakeholders expressed concerns about the lack of availability of hydrogen from zero-emission
processes and chose not to pursue this option further.
Flow batteries, which allow for decoupling of storage size and power delivery capacity, are
conceptually similar to a hydrogen storage/fuel cell/hydrolyzer system. The most common flow
battery chemistry uses vanadium electrolytes, which can exist in multiple oxidation states, enabling
energy storage and delivery. Flow batteries offer advantages such as long lifespan and safety (the
electrolyte is non-flammable), but they also have disadvantages, including higher costs, lower round-
trip efficiency compared to Li-ion batteries, and the toxicity of vanadium compounds in case of a
spill. Alternatives like organic flow batteries from CMBlu exist [23], but due to their newness and
limited testing, the community decided not to consider this technology at this time.

 48

4. KEY TAKEAWAYS AND POTENTIAL NEXT STEPS
Based on the technoeconomic evaluation by the Sandia technical team, the water utilities in
Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury have several viable options to extend uninterrupted water
supply toward the stakeholders' goal of 14 consecutive summer days without grid power. The more
feasible alternatives, based on strategies to manage existing equipment, technologies that are
commercially available, or that are pre-commercial but with a path to commercialization, are
outlined below. These options cover both water operations and power supply.

4.1. Water Operations
Modifications to water system operations can be implemented at minimal cost and with limited
disruption compared to investing in power sources. However, all but one type of change modeled
for this project, such as opening interconnects or changing pump operations, did not show a
significant improvement and some even decreased runtime. The utilities are already exploring the
addition of storage capacity in the form of new tanks or standpipes. Based on simulations, the water
utilities have already done, or are in the process of doing, nearly all the operational actions they can
to maintain water service during an emergency.
The remaining mechanism to extend water supply that strictly looks at operations is demand
modification. Since modulating demand enhances the duration of backup power supply, these
changes are recommended regardless of the chosen power source solution. They can be enacted
either first or simultaneously with power options. While demand curtailment will not extend water
supply and delivery to the full 14 days during peak demand without additional strategies outlined in
this section, they will improve the likelihood of success.

4.1.1. Demand Curtailment: opportunities and mechanisms
Utilities have noted that nearly 30% of summer water use is for irrigation, as evidenced by decreased
usage on rainy days. With backup power, a 30% reduction in usage translates to 30% longer fuel
availability. Other opportunities for reducing usage during a disaster or power outage are more
personal and would require outreach and education, such as the water saving brochure prepared by
the Island Institute, included in this report. Suggestions include wearing clothes multiple times
before washing, hotels and guests not washing sheets daily, and using disposable items instead of
washing dishes. While these measures can cumulatively extend backup power duration, they rely on
voluntary compliance from the population and may be difficult to ensure during disaster planning.

4.2. Power Source Options
The analysis found that it is currently not feasible to use VTA transit bus assets for emergency
power support to the Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury water utilities on Martha’s Vineyard.
Additionally, there are significant technology barriers that are challenging to address with existing
off-the-shelf solutions at a reasonable cost. The project identified three potential solutions, each
outlined below.

4.2.1.     DC-V2G
At the time of writing, the VTA operated seven DC buses, with four more arriving in June 2025 and
another 4 in April 2026. These buses could fully support a V2G-DC solution. However, only two
companies–Tellus Power Green and Nuvve–currently support bidirectional DC charging, and
neither is selling products yet. BorgWarner, the main supplier of bidirectional DC EVSE hardware,

 49

has recently announced a pullback. The communities may contact the sales offices for updates on
product availability and pricing, noting reduced a V2L rather than V2G use case, which may simplify
the required features. A pilot project with an estimated implementation cost of $200K to $300K for
the installed hardware could be eligible for state-funded financing opportunities.

4.2.2.     AC-V2G
The proposed solution by InventEV, utilizing the same fleet of 7+4+4 DC buses, is suitable for a
pilot project. If successfully implemented, it may closely align with the community's original
expectations due to the low unit cost of production hardware. This solution would use onboard
power electronics to tap into the DC terminals of the bus batteries, enabling the buses to provide
AC power directly to pumping stations via “camlock” taps mounted on the pump house wall or a
tap pedestal. The cost of a prototype for this option ranges from $100K to $300K, depending on
whether 3-phase power is provided at 208V or 480V. InventEV has expressed interest in partnering
with the Martha’s Vineyard Commission, VTA, or others to develop this solution. If funding
opportunities arise, this solution may best fit community needs.

4.2.3. Mobile storage
This solution can be delivered quickly as a turnkey option. Innoversa has expressed interest in
providing a custom product that includes one or more small mobile storage trailers to power the
pumping stations, along with a larger mobile storage unit to interface between a large PV array and
the storage trailers. The estimated hardware cost is around $1 million, and a connection agreement
with a PV array operator would be necessary. Collaborating with a partner, such as a construction
equipment rental company or Eversource, to use the equipment for other purposes during normal
conditions could enhance the business case. This solution could be implemented immediately if
funding opportunities arise.

4.3. Next Steps
The Sandia team has identified industry contacts for further discussions. If the towns wish to pursue
solutions leveraging the VTA bus fleet, collaborating with the VTA for a test of bus accessibility to
well sites (essentially, driving buses to the potential well sites and seeing if they can drive to the
station, considering possible adverse conditions) is the next step that can be taken at little cost.
Further discussions with InventEV and BYD/Ride could also be productive in preparation for
possible funding opportunities at state or federal level in the mid-term future, as a subsequent step.
Alternatively, collaborations with Eversource, such as a utility-funded pilot project such as the one
deployed on the mainland with Innoversa [24], or other potential partners for deployment of a
turnkey mobile storage solution could be sought, also with potential external funding in mind.

5. CONCLUSIONS AND SUMMARY
The main question considered in the energy analysis is how to provide supplemental energy to the
pumps to extend the off-grid runtime from one week to a target of two weeks, at low cost. One
obvious option is to increase the size of the propane/diesel tanks, but there are reasons to consider
alternatives, as follows:
 1. The community wishes to reduce reliance on fuel delivery. Delivery, in the case of
 prolonged grid outages, may be problematic, given that fuel is transported to the island by
 ferry and there may be competing demand from off-island locations for refueling depending
 on the extent of the power outage.
 2. The community prefers to use low-emission technologies for several reasons including
 potential environmental effects like local air pollution and noise.
Possible low- or zero-emissions options include solar, batteries, hydrogen fuel cells, wind power or
biofuels. Constraints that must be considered include:
 1. Cost: water utilities have priorities higher than increasing system resilience, including the
 substitution of lead pipes currently present the existing water distribution system.
 2. State and local regulations limit the siting of batteries in the vicinity of wells.
 3. Availability of land to site equipment near wells.
Several options that could potentially meet the community’s needs were considered for analysis. The
options considered were as follows:
 1. Energy storage batteries, potentially combined with PV generation, located near the
 pumping sites. The batteries and PV could provide grid services to improve the business
 case.
 2. Hydrogen storage and fuel cells, with hydrogen either produced on-site via hydrolysis or
 imported from external suppliers.
 3. Flow batteries located near the pumping sites, with the redox reaction to charge the battery
 powered by the grid. The flow battery could be used to provide grid services to improve the
 business case.
 4. Transit buses charging at the Vineyard Transit Authority depot and delivering energy to the
 pumping stations. In this case, some of the buses in the fleet may be removed from transit
 service and diverted to power wells, but only during emergency conditions.
 5. Mobile storage batteries charged at existing or future large PV arrays and delivering energy to
 the pumping stations. These batteries could be used for other services like construction site
 power or as an alternative to conventional generation at events like concerts or other
 functions where low-noise and low-emission equipment is preferable.
The Sandia team presented each of the solutions to community stakeholders. These were discussed
to establish feasibility and to assess how well each addresses community concerns and wishes.
Ultimately, stakeholders selected to focus on options 4 and 5.
The results of Sandia water system modeling and energy demand analyses showed that generators
can sustain uninterrupted water supply for at least seven days during summer without modifications
to consumer behavior, and they can handle multi-week outages in winter without changes to the
backup system. In scenarios without interconnects and backup power, the time to failure varies

 51

significantly between winter and summer due to differences in water demand. In winter, the lower
demand allows for longer operational durations before the system must shut down. For example,
Edgartown can sustain water availability for approximately 4.66 days under minimum demand in
winter. In contrast, during summer, particularly at peak demand levels (110% of maximum demand),
none of the systems (Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, or Tisbury) can sustain delivery for more than a day
without power.
Opening interconnects between the towns’ water systems decreases the time all systems could
operate without power, as simulations show that it allows for faster drainage of tank capacity despite
improving pressure for firefighting, highlighting that interconnects do not enhance resilience in the
absence of backup power. The Edgartown water system is most sensitive to the opening of the
interconnect, while Oak Bluffs and Tisbury are less impacted, with time to failure showing little
change.
Based on the technoeconomic evaluation conducted by the Sandia team, the Edgartown, Oak Bluffs,
and Tisbury water utilities may have several feasible options for extending the duration of
uninterrupted water supply to approach the stakeholders’ self-identified goal of supplying water for
14 consecutive summer days without grid power.
Currently, leveraging VTA transit bus assets for emergency power support is not feasible due to
technology barriers that are difficult to overcome at low price with existing off-the-shelf solutions.
However, the Sandia team identified the three next-best possible solutions:
 • DC-V2G: At the time of writing, the VTA operated seven DC buses, with an additional four
 expected in June 2025 and another four in April 2026, which could support a V2G-DC
 solution. However, only two companies, Tellus Power Green and Nuvve, currently market
 bidirectional DC charging, but their products are not yet available for sale. BorgWarner, a
 key supplier of bidirectional DC electric vehicle service equipment (EVSE) hardware, has
 announced a pullback from the market. We recommend to contact the sales offices for
 updates on product availability and pricing, noting that the chargers are designed for vehicle
 to load (V2L) rather than vehicle to grid (V2G), which may simplify features. A pilot project
 could cost between $200K and $300K for the installed hardware, making it a candidate for
 state funding opportunities.
 • AC-V2G: The 7+4+4 fleet of DC buses proposed by InventEV is suitable for a pilot
 project. If successful, this solution aligns closely with community expectations due to its low
 production costs. It would utilize on-board power electronics to allow buses to provide AC
 power directly to pumping stations via “camlock” taps mounted on the pump house or tap
 pedestal. Prototype costs range from $100K to $300K, depending on whether 3-phase
 power is provided at 208V or 480V. InventEV is open to partnering with the Martha’s
 Vineyard Commission, VTA, or others to develop this solution, which may be ideal if
 funding becomes available.
 • Mobile storage: This solution is currently the only turnkey option that can be delivered
 quickly. Innoversa is interested in providing custom mobile storage, including small trailers
 to power pumping stations and a larger unit to connect with a PV array. The estimated
 hardware cost for this pilot system is around $1M, and a connection agreement with a PV
 array operator is necessary. Collaborating with a partner, such as a construction equipment
 rental company or the local electric utility (i.e. Eversource), could enhance the business case.
 This solution can be implemented immediately if funding opportunities arise.

 52

Aside from increasing backup power fuel or availability, demand curtailment is the most effective
way to extend water availability during a prolonged grid outage. Edgartown, Oak Bluffs, and Tisbury
utilities indicated report that nearly 30% of summer water use is for irrigation, indicating that a 30%
reduction in usage could lead to a corresponding increase in fuel availability and subsequent power
supply. Other methods to reduce usage during disasters, such as wearing clothes multiple times
before washing, hotels not laundering sheets daily, and using disposable items instead of washing
dishes, could also help extend backup power duration. However, these suggestions rely on voluntary
compliance from the population and cannot be enforced, making them unreliable for disaster
planning.
The ETIPP project concludes with potential funding opportunities and water conservation
resources developed by Island Institute. The Sandia team has identified industry contacts for further
discussions. If the towns wish to pursue solutions leveraging the VTA bus fleet, collaborating with
the VTA for a test of bus accessibility to well sites are the next logical steps, that could be taken
immediately at low cost. Depending on the outcome of this initial step, the community could choose
to pursue the option of using VTA buses as a backup. In this case, a next step would be to continue
to interact with both Inventev and BYD/Ride to determine whether a small pilot project (e.g. with
one bus) is possible and to document the development requirements in preparation for a funding
application. Alternatively, potential partners could be engaged to build a business case for mobile
storage solutions.
Finally, the possibility of augmenting the fuel capacity of the propane tanks (and one diesel tank)
located near each of the pumping stations should be considered, as a low-risk means of achieving
the desired two-week off-grid resilience goals.

APPENDIX A. VTA BUS DEPOT POWER GENERATION
To assess the potential of using transit buses as an auxiliary backup solution to extend the operating
time of backup generators, it is essential to estimate the power generation capacity of the VTA bus
depot microgrid. During normal conditions, VTA electric buses are charged at the depot and various
inductive charging stations along their routes. The microgrid connects to the Vineyard grid during
these times, but operates in island mode during outages, relying on a combination of PV generation,
inverters, and a diesel generator, with a 1.5 MWh battery to balance generator and load.
Figure A-1 provides an aerial view of the VTA microgrid. The nominal DC generation capacity of
the PV array is 700 kW, while the AC capacity is limited to 465 kW due to inverter constraints. Peak
water system load on Martha’s Vineyard occurs in August.
To estimate power generation during a prolonged outage at peak load, the System Advisor Model
(SAM) was used. SAM, developed by the NLR, is a free desktop application for techno-economic
analysis of energy technologies, capable of calculating power output from a PV/inverter system
based on local weather data and system specifications.
