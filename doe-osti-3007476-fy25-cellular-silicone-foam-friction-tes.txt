 Summary of Experiments
This effort was a collaboration between LANL groups MPA-CINT, E-13, and V-14 to characterize
various properties of foam materials for programmatic work. Specifically, MPA-CINT staff members’
contribution to this effort was to perform friction testing of cellular silicone foams of two different
porosities with respect to two engineering materials with predefined surface roughnesses. The selected
foams (henceforth serving as the sled material interface exposed to motion) had porosities of 67% and
70% with respect to fabricated values. The other materials used in the friction testing measurement couple
(static track material) were 304 Stainless Steel (304 SS) and Very High-Temperature Glass-Mica
Ceramic, known as Macor®.

Friction measurements were conducted using an MTS Renew 1125 screw-drive test frame as the primary
testing platform. The system was equipped with a calibrated tension load cell (full scale 500 N) and a
displacement-controlled crosshead, with data acquired at a sampling rate of 20 Hz.

Prior to testing, the 304 SS and Macor tracks were prepared by conventional milling to achieve a nominal
surface roughness of approximately 𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅 = 1.6 µm. Following machining, both track materials were
characterized using a Maruf PS10 surface roughness tester. A total of 15 measurements were performed
of the track materials surface, yielding an average roughness of 𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅 = 1.44µm, standard deviation (𝜎𝜎) =
0.10288 µm for Macor and 𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅 =1.35 µm, 𝜎𝜎 = 0.89618 µm for 304 SS, the distribution of 𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅
measurement values (µm) along the track surfaces are shown in Figure 1.

 Figure 1: Measured 𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅 values (µm) over Macor and SS 304 tracks.

Once characterized, the tracks were rigidly mounted and carefully leveled with respect to the test frame
base to ensure stable and repeatable conditions.

The load cell zero was verified prior to each test. Both the sled and track surfaces were cleaned with
ethanol and dried using compressed air to minimize contamination.

Foam specimens were cut to approximate sled dimensions. Each piece was affixed to the sled block using
rigid double-sided tape, ensuring precise alignment. The foam was trimmed flush with the sled edges, and

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 1

sufficient material was provided to fold smoothly over the sled radius. To preserve surface integrity, the
mounted foam was not brought into contact with any surface other than the cleaned track before testing.

The sled assembly was configured with additional masses to produce nominal stress levels of 2, 8, and 16
psi, relative to the foam contact area. The dimensions of the sled and mass configuration are shown in
Figure 2.

 Figure 2: Sled and mass assembly (left) and sled dimensions (right).

The pulling tether was carefully aligned to remain perpendicular to the sled over the full test stroke.
Calipers and machinist levels were used to minimize vertical force components that could otherwise
affect the measured tangential load.

Once the sled-track couple was assembled, slack was removed from the tether by preloading to
approximately 5 N, then unloading to between 0–2 N before starting the test. Crosshead speed was
programmed to 127 mm/min with a total displacement of 90 mm.

During testing, force–time and force–displacement signals were recorded continuously. After the initial
pristine run to 90 mm, three repeat runs were conducted on the same foam sample, each with a reduced
displacement of 30 mm, while maintaining the applied load. Data were saved after every test cycle.

Following completion of each test series, the track was cleaned with ethanol and dried with compressed
air. The subsequent test sequence was then performed using either a new pristine foam sample under the
same load condition or a different loading condition, according to the test matrix described in a later
section. Once the test matrix (see Table 1 and Table 2) was completed, the data were transferred to E-13
for analysis. Additional lower-compression (~ 0.63 psi) tests were performed later at the behest of E-13.

 Summary of Analysis
The primary goal of the friction tests was to determine the static and kinetic coefficients of friction
(COFs) of the tested foam/substrate pairings as a function of the compressive stress in the foam material.
In the analysis of the test data, the following assumptions were made:

 • For any test, the compressive stress is equivalent to the normal force (the nominal weight) applied
 to the foam divided by the nominal surface area of the foam/substrate interface.

 • The compressive stress in the foam is constant and uniform throughout a test.

 • Friction at the foam/substrate interface follows the standard Coulomb friction model: 𝐹𝐹𝑓𝑓 ≤ 𝜇𝜇𝐹𝐹𝑛𝑛 ,
 where 𝐹𝐹𝑓𝑓 is the tangential force due to friction (parallel to the surface), 𝐹𝐹𝑛𝑛 is the normal force
 applied to the contact interface (normal to the surface), and 𝜇𝜇 is the COF.
 𝐹𝐹𝑓𝑓
 o From this model, both the static COF 𝜇𝜇𝑠𝑠 and kinetic COF 𝜇𝜇𝑘𝑘 are derived as 𝜇𝜇 = 𝐹𝐹 .
 𝑛𝑛

 o To obtain the static COF, the tangential (pull) force at the “breakaway” point, where the
 interface transitions from static to kinetic friction, is used.

 o To obtain the kinetic COF, the tangential force in the kinetic regime following the
 breakaway is used.

The data are not compliance-corrected, therefore insight into the shear characteristics of the foam material
in the static regime of the tests would likely be unreliable. However, it is believed that fixture compliance
does not have a significant effect on the computation of the COF, which relies only on the contact surface
area, the weight applied to the interface, and the magnitude of the pull force.

 Figure 3: Pull load vs. crosshead displacement over multiple runs at varying compressive stresses.

Figure 3 depicts a sample plot of the pull force with respect to the crosshead displacement for multiple
load cases for the 67% porosity foam/304 SS and 67% porosity foam/Macor material pairings. For each
curve, the breakaway point for sample is represented by the first black dot, and the end of the run (when
the pulling ceased) is represented by the second black dot. Notable observations from these plots include:

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 3

 • Initial runs do not exhibit a distinct breakaway point, while subsequent runs typically do.

 • Breakaway becomes less prominent as the compressive load decreases, i.e. the transition between
 static and kinetic friction appears smoother.

 • The breakaway points are generally more prominent for Macor than for SS.

 • The data in the kinetic regime exhibits small fluctuations and a downward trend, which is most
 apparent in the 90-mm initial runs for higher compressive loads.

For each curve, a static COF is calculated from the load at the first black dot, and kinetic COFs are
calculated for all loads between the two black dots, producing a much wider dataset. COF values are
grouped together for each combination of foam porosity, substrate material, and compressive stress, for
which summary statistics (e.g., the mean and standard deviation 𝜎𝜎) are computed. With this information,
the COFs can be plotted with uncertainty as a function of the compressive stress, which is shown in
Figure 4.

 Figure 4: Mean COF ±3𝜎𝜎 as a function of the compressive stress in the foam.

Notable observations from these plots include:

 • As the compressive load increases, the static and kinetic COFs generally decrease. This is
 behavior is consistent with the literature.

 • As the compressive load increases, the 3𝜎𝜎 margins of the COFs typically decrease.

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 4

 • The COFs are generally similar between SS and Macor. The 3𝜎𝜎 margins of the COFs are
 typically wider for Macor than for SS.

 • The kinetic COFs exhibit tighter margins than the static COFs. This can be partially attributed to
 there being far more datapoints for the kinetic COFs than for the static COFs.

 • The 67% porosity foam typically exhibits slightly lower COFs than the 70%, which is consistent
 with the literature. The margins are generally comparable between the porosities.

 Conclusions and Future Work
From these tests, the static and kinetic COFs have been characterized with respect to compressive stress
for 67% and 70% porosity foams on SS and Macor surfaces. Key takeaways from these tests include:

 • The COFs tend to decrease as the compressive stress increases and as the porosity decreases.

 o If an application requires a specific COF at a foam interface, both the porosity of the
 foam and the preload applied to it should be considered as participating variables.

 • The breakaway point appears less pronounced as the compressive load decreases. This may
 indicate that there are other phenomena present, which are more significant at lower compression,
 and which the Coulomb friction model fails to account for. These phenomena may include
 adhesion, stiction, and nonuniform compression.

 o The fact that subsequent runs typically exhibited more prominent breakaways may
 indicate that changes occur at the surfaces in contact between runs, such as damage.

 • The COFs are generally comparable between SS and Macor, however the COFs for Macor
 typically bear wider uncertainty margins.

Although these tests provided valuable insight into the friction properties of cellular silicone foams, they
were host to some limitations. Most notably, during testing the mass on top of the foam samples visibly
tilted in the direction of the pull point when pulling began. Though updates to the test setup mitigated the
tilt, it was still visibly present during the tests analyzed here. As a result, the compression of the foam
samples was likely nonuniform such that the compression decreased as the distance from the pull point
increased, violating the assumption of a uniform compressive stress in the foam. The impact of the tilted
mass on the results is difficult to quantify and not well understood. If the tilting of the mass resulted in the
sample partially lifting off the track material, then the total area in contact would have been reduced,
which would increase the effective compressive stress. If this is true, then the COF values shown here
may correspond to higher effective compressive stresses.

For future work, it would be worthwhile to assess and quantify the impact of the tilt on the compressive
state and COF of the foam samples. To this end, E-13 is currently developing finite element models of the
friction tests to investigate this phenomenon. It would also be valuable to update the existing test fixture
to further mitigate or eliminate the tilt. Alternatively, other tests could be conducted to provide the same
friction information without introducing nonuniform compression in the foam samples. To this end, more
tests, like what has been done historically for similar materials, are planned to be carried out in FY26.
