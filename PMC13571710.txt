Can a Multidisciplinary Team Change the Course of Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension? Changes in Time to Diagnosis After PERT Implementation
Authors: Matías Almeida Souto, Milagros Báez, Marcella Perri, Nazareno Zelaya de León, Federico Devetter, Christian Smith, Lorena Maldonado, Julio Chertcoff, Alejandro Salvado, Martín Bosio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a potentially curable form of pulmonary hypertension, yet diagnostic delay remains a major barrier to timely treatment. Pulmonary embolism response teams (PERT) have emerged as a strategy to optimize follow-up after acute pulmonary embolism (APE), but local evidence on their impact on CTEPH diagnostic timing is scant. We conducted a single-center, retrospective cohort study comparing patients diagnosed with CTEPH before and after PERT implementation in a general hospital in Argentina. Among 29 patients, those diagnosed after PERT implementation had a significantly shorter time from APE to CTEPH diagnosis (median 43 vs. 256 weeks, p = 0.0369) and a non-significant trend toward lower-risk profiles at diagnosis. These findings support the role of structured multidisciplinary follow-up in accelerating CTEPH recognition outside specialized referral centers.

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a treatable form of pulmonary hypertension caused by persistent, organized thrombi obstructing the pulmonary arteries after one or more episodes of acute pulmonary embolism (APE).1, 2 CTEPH is a unique form of pulmonary hypertension that combines mechanical obstruction with variable pulmonary microangiopathy, a process similar to that observed in idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension.3 Persistent obstruction significantly increases pulmonary vascular resistance, leading ultimately to right ventricular failure.3, 4

The reported incidence of CTEPH after an APE episode is variable and has been estimated to be between 0.1% and 9.1% within the first 2 years after a thromboembolic event.3 Many patients may experience undiagnosed or paucisymptomatic thromboembolic episodes.7 Diagnostic delay remains common: intervals between symptom onset and confirmation frequently exceed 12 months, even in specialized healthcare systems,5, 6 and are associated with worse functional and hemodynamic status at diagnosis.4

Pulmonary embolism response teams (PERT) have been proposed to optimize diagnosis, treatment selection, and follow-up after APE,7 but evidence on their specific impact on CTEPH diagnostic timing outside reference centers remains limited. We evaluated the impact of PERT implementation on time to CTEPH diagnosis and clinical risk profile at a general hospital in Argentina.

We conducted a single-center, retrospective, observational cohort study at a tertiary hospital that included adult patients with confirmed CTEPH diagnosed between January 2017 and July 2025, identified from the institutional Pulmonary Vascular Diseases Unit registry. Patients were stratified into a pre-PERT group (diagnosed before October 2021) and a post-PERT group (diagnosed after multidisciplinary PERT implementation). We analyzed age, sex, documented history of APE, time from APE to CTEPH diagnosis (weeks), and ESC/ERS 2022 clinical risk stratification at diagnosis.1 Continuous variables were compared using Student's t-test or Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate; categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test, Fisher's exact test, or chi-squared test for trend. Significance was set at p < 0.05.

Of 30 patients with group 4 pulmonary hypertension, 29 had confirmed CTEPH and were included in the study (1 was excluded for oncologic pulmonary arterial obstruction). Mean age was 65 years; 62.1% were female. A documented history of APE was present in 72% overall, in 56% of the pre-PERT group (n = 16), and in 100% of the post-PERT group (n = 13). Among patients with documented APE, time from the thromboembolic event to CTEPH diagnosis was substantially shorter in the post-PERT group: median 43 weeks (interquartile range [IQR] 22–106) versus 256 weeks (IQR 23–400) in the pre-PERT group (p = 0.0369, Mann–Whitney U test) (Fig. 1). These findings are consistent with international evidence linking structured post-APE follow-up to earlier CTEPH detection. In the ASPIRE registry, a dedicated post-PE follow-up clinic increased CTEPH diagnostic rates and identified disease at an earlier, less severe stage.8, 9 The InShape IV study similarly reported a substantial reduction in time to diagnosis, improved risk profiles using a structured post-discharge detection algorithm, and a low diagnostic failure rate.10 The FOCUS study, a large multicenter cohort of APE survivors, reinforced the rationale for systematic early follow-up by demonstrating a clinically meaningful cumulative incidence of CTEPH.11 The magnitude of the reduction in time to diagnosis observed in our cohort – from a median of 256 to 43 weeks – is broadly comparable to these series, despite differences in design and population.

A distinctive feature of our findings is that they derive from a general hospital setting rather than a specialized pulmonary vascular referral center. Most of the available evidence on structured CTEPH detection originates from highly specialized units with dedicated infrastructure and established referral pathways. However, a substantial proportion of patients with APE are first evaluated and managed outside these centers.12 Our results suggest that a multidisciplinary PERT model can meaningfully shorten the diagnostic interval. This supports the feasibility of PERT-driven follow-up as a scalable strategy applicable to general hospital settings, where most patients at risk for CTEPH are initially identified.

From a clinical standpoint, earlier diagnosis has direct therapeutic relevance. CTEPH is one of the few forms of pulmonary hypertension that is potentially curable.1, 2 Diagnostic delay is associated with more advanced hemodynamic compromise and a narrower therapeutic window at the time of evaluation.4 The trend toward lower-risk profiles observed in our post-PERT group, although not statistically significant, is clinically coherent with this mechanism and suggests that structured follow-up may translate into earlier access to potentially curative interventions.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design carries an inherent risk of information and selection bias. The sample size was small, limiting statistical power, particularly for the risk stratification comparison, and precluding formal sensitivity or multivariable analyses. We did not assess long-term clinical outcomes. Finally, this was a single-center experience, which may limit generalizability to other healthcare settings. Larger prospective multicenter studies are needed to confirm these findings and to evaluate their impact on hard clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, in a general hospital setting, implementation of a PERT team was associated with a significant reduction in time to CTEPH diagnosis, along with a non-significant trend toward earlier-stage disease at diagnosis. These findings support multidisciplinary post-APE follow-up as a strategy to improve early CTEPH detection, with potential implications for timely access to curative-intent therapies, and underscore the relevance of extending such strategies beyond specialized referral centers.
